Neural Network-Based Score Estimation in Diffusion Models: Optimization and Generalization
Yinbin Han, Meisam Razaviyayn & Renyuan Xu

Abstract
Diffusion models have emerged as a powerful tool rivaling GANs in generating high-quality samples with improved fidelity, flexibility, and robustness. A key component of these models is to learn the score function through score matching. Despite empirical success on various tasks, it remains unclear whether gradient-based algorithms can learn the score function with a provable accuracy. As a first step toward answering this question, this paper establishes a mathematical framework for analyzing score estimation using neural networks trained by gradient descent. Our analysis covers both the optimization and the generalization aspects of the learning procedure. In particular, we propose a parametric form to formulate the denoising score-matching problem as a regression with noisy labels. Compared to the standard supervised learning setup, the score-matching problem introduces distinct challenges, including unbounded input, vector-valued output, and an additional time variable, preventing existing techniques from being applied directly. In this paper, we show that with a properly designed neural network architecture, the score function can be accurately approximated by a reproducing kernel Hilbert space induced by neural tangent kernels. Furthermore, by applying an early-stopping rule for gradient descent and leveraging certain coupling arguments between neural network training and kernel regression, we establish the first generalization error (sample complexity) bounds for learning the score function despite the presence of noise in the observations. Our analysis is grounded in a novel parametric form of the neural network and an innovative connection between score matching and regression analysis, facilitating the application of advanced statistical and optimization techniques.



1
Introduction

Diffusion models excel in diverse generative tasks, spanning image, video, and audio generation

(
Song & Ermon 2019
;
Dathathri et al. 2019
;
Song et al. 2020
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
, often outperforming their contemporaries, including GANs, VAEs, normalizing flows, and energy-based models
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
;
Kingma & Welling 2013
;
Rezende & Mohamed 2015
;
Zhao et al. 2016
)
.

A typical diffusion model consists of two diffusion processes
(
Song et al. 2020
;
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
: one moving forward in time and the other moving backward. The forward process transforms a given data sample into white noise in the limit by gradually injecting noise through the diffusion term, while the backward process transforms noise to a sample from the data distribution by sequentially removing the added noise. The implementation of the backward process depends on the score function, defined as the gradient of the logarithmic density, at each timestamp of the forward process.
In practice, however, the score function is unknown and one can only access the true data distribution via finitely many samples. To ensure the fidelity of the backward process in generating realistic samples, it is essential to develop efficient methods to estimate the score function using samples. This estimation is typically achieved through a process known as
score matching
, employing powerful nonlinear functional approximations such as neural networks.

Despite the empirical success, it is theoretically less clear whether a gradient-based algorithm can train a neural network to learn the score function. Existing theoretical work
(
De Bortoli et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Oko et al. 2023
;
Mei & Wu 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Shah et al. 2023
;
Tang & Zhao 2024
;
Gao et al. 2023
)
predominantly focuses on algorithm-agnostic properties of diffusion models such as score approximation, score estimation, and distribution recovery, leaving the theoretical performance of widely-used gradient-based algorithms an open problem.
This paper bridges this gap between theory and practice.
Our contributions are summarized as follows.

Our Work and Contributions.

This work investigates the training of a two-layer fully connected neural network via gradient descent (GD) to learn the score function.
First, we propose a neural network-based parametric form for the score estimator based on the score decomposition (see Lemma
3.1
). This novel design transforms the score-matching objective into a regression with noisy labels. To show the trained neural network minimizes the excess risk of this regression problem, we overcome three main challenges that do not exist in the traditional supervised learning set-ups: 1) unbounded input, 2) vector-valued output,
and 3) an additional time variable. To handle unbounded input, we employ a truncation argument and control the tail behavior using the properties of diffusion processes (see Lemma
3.3
). Next, we establish a universal approximation theorem with respect to the score function using the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS), induced by the neural tangent kernel (NTK); see Theorem
3.6
. In addition, we leverage the recent NTK-based analysis of neural networks to show the equivalence between neural network training and kernel regression (see Theorem
3.9
). Consequently, we transform the score matching into a kernel regression problem. Furthermore, we propose a virtual dataset to address the issue of target shifting caused by the approximation step. In the presence of multi-output labels, a vector-valued localized Rademacher complexity bound is utilized to control the prediction error of two kernel regressions (see Theorem
3.10
). Finally, we employ an early stopping rule for the kernel regression to minimize the score-matching objective and provide the generalization result (see Theorem
3.12
).

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to establish sample complexity bounds of GD-trained neural networks for score matching. Specifically, our paper is the first to utilize NTK for establishing theoretical results for diffusion models.
Although the idea of NTK has been used in many fields, the utilization of existing techniques in the structure of diffusion models brings about its own significant challenges. Our analysis is grounded in a novel parametric form of the neural network and an innovative connection between score matching and regression analysis, facilitating the application of advanced statistical and optimization techniques. In addition, the building blocks of our results can be applied to other supervised learning problems in non-standard forms (such as unbounded input and vector-valued output), which goes beyond score-matching problems.

Related Literature.

Our work is related to three categories of prior work:

First, our framework is closely related to the recent study of diffusion models. A line of work on this topic provides theoretical guarantees of diffusion models for recovering data distribution, assuming access to an accurate score estimator under
L^{2}
or
L^{\infty}
norm
(
De Bortoli et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Lee et al. 2023
;
Shah et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Chen et al. 2023b
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Tang & Zhao 2024
)
. These results offer only a partial understanding of diffusion models as the score estimation part is omitted. To our best knowledge,
Chen et al. 2023a
and
Oko et al. 2023
are the only results that provide score estimation guarantees under
L^{2}
norm, assuming linear data structure or compactly supported data density. However, their emphasis is on algorithm-agnostic analysis without evaluation of any specific algorithms, creating a gap between theory and practical implementation. In contrast, our work offers the first generalization error (sample complexity) bounds for GD-trained neural networks.

Second, our techniques relate to the rich literature of deep learning theory. Inspired by the framework of NTK introduced by
Jacot et al. 2018
, recently
Du et al. 2018
;
Du et al. 2019
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019b
;
Zou et al. 2020
establish linear convergence rate of neural networks for fitting random labels. One key property of GD-trained neural networks is the so-called implicit regularization of parameters. Namely, the minimizer of over-parameterized neural networks is close to the random initialization. Combined with uniform convergence results in statistical learning, this implicit regularization leads to the generalization property of neural networks in the absence of label noise
(
Arora et al. 2019a
)
. However, none of these works delves into the generalization ability of neural networks when confronted with noisy labels.
Kuzborskij & Szepesvári 2022
is the only work that attempts to study the GD-trained neural networks with additive noise. To tackle the challenge posed by the score matching, our approach and, consequently, our theoretical results differ from the existing literature on deep learning theory for supervised learning in three key aspects: 1) handling unbounded input, 2) dealing with vector-valued output, and 3) incorporating an additional time variable.

Lastly, our work is connected to a body of research focused on early stopping rules in kernel regression. See
Celisse & Wahl 2021
for a comprehensive overview of this topic. Our work considers a multi-output extension of the early stopping rule developed in
Raskutti et al. 2014
, which controls the complexity of the predictor class based on empirical distribution.

2
Preliminaries and Problem Statement

In this section, we introduce the mathematical framework of diffusion models
Song et al. 2020
.

Forward Process.

The forward process progressively injects noise into the original data distribution. In the context of data generation, we have the flexibility to work with any forward diffusion process of our choice. For the sake of theoretical convenience, we adhere to the standard convention in the literature
(
Song & Ermon 2020
;
Ho et al. 2020
)
and focus on the Ornstein-Ulhenbeck (OU) process, characterized by the following Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE):

\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}g(t)X_{t}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{g(t)}{\rm d}B_{t},\quad X_{0}\sim p_{0},

(1)

where
g(t)>0
is a deterministic weighting function; and
\left(B_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. Here,
p_{0}
represents the unknown data distribution from which we have access to only a limited number of samples. Our objective is to generate additional samples from this distribution. Denoting the distribution of
X_{t}
at time
t
by
p_{t}
, the explicit solution to (
1
) is given by

\displaystyle X_{t}=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}g(s){\rm d}s}X_{0}+e^{-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}g(s){\rm d}s}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{2}g(u){\rm d}u}\sqrt{g(s)}{\rm d}B_{s}.

Consequently, the conditional distribution
X_{t}|X_{0}
follows a multi-variate Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\alpha(t)X_{0},h(t)I_{d})
with
\alpha(t)\coloneqq\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}g(s){\rm d}s\right)
and
h(t)\coloneqq 1-\alpha^{2}(t)
. Furthermore, under mild assumptions, the OU process converges exponentially to the standard Gaussian distribution
(
Bakry et al. 2014
)
. In practice, the forward process (
1
) will terminate at a sufficiently large timestamp
T>0
such that the distribution
p_{T}
is close to the standard Gaussian distribution.

Backward Process.

By reversing the forward process in time, we obtain a process
\bar{X}_{t}:=X_{T-t}
(well defined under mild assumptions
(
Haussmann & Pardoux 1986
)
) that transforms white noise into samples from the targeted data distribution, fulfilling the purpose of generative modeling. To start, let us first define a backward process (time-reversed SDE) associated with (
1
):

\displaystyle{\rm d}Y_{t}=\left(\frac{1}{2}g(T-t)Y_{t}+g(T-t)\nabla\log p_{T-t}(Y_{t})\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{g(T-t)}{\rm d}\bar{B}_{t},\quad Y_{0}\sim q_{0}

(2)

where
(\bar{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is another
d
-dimensional Brownian motion, the
score function

\nabla\log p_{t}(\cdot)
is defined as the gradient of log density of
X_{t}
, and
q_{0}
is the initial distribution of the backward process. If the score function is known at each time
t
and if
q_{0}=p_{T}
, under mild assumptions, the backward process
(Y_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
has the
same distribution
as the time-reversed process
(X_{T-t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
—see
Föllmer 2005
;
Cattiaux et al. 2021
;
Haussmann & Pardoux 1986
for details.

In practice, however, (
2
) cannot be directly used to generate samples from the targeted data distribution as both the score function and the distribution
p_{T}
are
unknown
. To address this issue, it is common practice to replace
p_{T}
by the standard Gaussian distribution as the initial distribution of the backward process. Then, we replace the ground-truth score
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
by an estimator
s_{\theta}(x,t)
. The estimator
s_{\theta}
is parameterized (and learned) by a neural network. With these modifications, we obtain an approximation of the backward process, which is practically implementable:

\displaystyle{\rm d}Y_{t}=\left(\frac{1}{2}g(T-t)Y_{t}+g(T-t)s_{\theta}(Y_{t},t)\right){\rm d}t+\sqrt{g(T-t)}{\rm d}W_{t},\quad Y_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

(3)

To generate data using (
3
), SDE solvers or discrete-time approximation schemes can be used
(
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Ho et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Song et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2023b
)
.

Score Matching.

To implement the backward process, we need to use samples to estimate the score function. A natural choice is to minimize the
L^{2}
loss between the estimated and actual score:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\;\;\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-\nabla\log p_{t}(X_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]{\rm d}t,

(4)

where
\lambda(t)
is the weight function that captures time inhomogeneity and
s_{\theta}
is the estimator of the score function. Here,
T_{0}>0
is some small value to prevent the score function from blowing up and to stabilize the training procedure
(
Song & Ermon 2019
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Vahdat et al. 2021
)
. A major drawback of the score-matching loss (
4
) is its intractability as
\nabla\log p_{t}
cannot be computed based on the available samples. Thus, instead of minimizing the loss in (
4
), one can equivalently minimize the following denoising score matching as shown by
Vincent 2011
:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\;\;\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|s_{\theta}(X_{t},t)-\nabla\log p_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]{\rm d}t.

(5)

Here,
p_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})
denotes the conditional probability of
X_{t}
given
X_{0}
. It is easy to show that the choice of our forward process in (
1
) implies

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t|0}(X_{t}|X_{0})=\frac{\alpha(t)}{h(t)}X_{0}-\frac{X_{t}}{h(t)}.

(6)

Now, we can plug (
6
) into (
5
) and learn the score function estimator. In practice, however, the score function estimator is parameterized by a neural network. Next, we discuss such a parameterization.

number of samples
N
and a small value
T_{0}>0

do

\left\{\left(t_{j},X_{0,j},X_{t_{j}}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{N}

Neural Network-Based Parameterization.

To parametrize the function
s_{\theta}
, we consider a two-layer ReLU neural network
f_{{\bf W},a}=\left(f^{i}_{{\bf W},a}\right)_{i=1}^{d}
of the following form:

\displaystyle f^{i}_{{\bf W},a}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}\sigma(w_{r}^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})).

(7)

Here,
(x,t)=(x^{1},\dots,x^{d},t)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is the input vector,
w_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
is a weight vector in the first layer,
a_{r}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
is a weight vector in the second layer, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the ReLU activation. The specific bias term
T_{0}
introduced in the architecture plays an important role in the theoretical analysis and also offers valuable insights for practical design. For ease of exposition, we denote
{\bf W}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times m}
and
a=[a_{r}^{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
. We adopt the usual trick in the over-parameterization literature
(
Cai et al. 2019
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019b
)
with
a
fixed throughout the training and only updating
{\bf W}
. This seemingly shallow architecture poses significant challenges when analyzing the convergence of gradient-based algorithms due to its non-convex and non-smooth objective. On the other hand, its ability to effectively approximate a diverse set of functions makes it a promising starting point for advancing theoretical developments.

To train the neural network, we need to have samples measuring the “goodness-of-fit” of the neural network. We use Algorithm
1
to generate
N
i.i.d. data samples. In particular, for each
j=1,\dots,N
, we first sample
X_{0,j}
from
p_{0}
and a timestamp
t_{j}
uniformly over the interval
[T_{0},T]
. Given
X_{0,j}
and
t_{j}
, we then sample
X_{t_{j}}
from the Gaussian distribution
p_{t_{j}|0}({}\cdot{}|X_{0,j})
. Given the output dataset
S:=\left\{(t_{j},X_{0,j},X_{t_{j}})\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
, we train the neural network by minimizing a quadratic loss:

\displaystyle\min_{\bf W}\;\;\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bf W})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|f_{{\bf W},a}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j})-X_{0,j}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(8)

Particularly, we perform the gradience descent (GD) update rule:

\displaystyle w_{r}(\tau+1)-w_{r}(\tau)=-\eta\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(w_{r}(\tau))}{\partial w_{r}(\tau)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(f_{{\bf W},a}^{i}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j})-X^{i}_{0,j})a_{r}^{i}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}-T_{0})\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{r}^{\top}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}-T_{0})\geq 0\right\},

(9)

for
r=1,\ldots,m
. Here,
\eta>0
is the learning rate. We initialize the parameter
\bf W
and
a
according to the following neural tangent kernel (NTK) regime
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
:

\displaystyle w_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d+1}),a_{r}^{i}\sim{\rm Unif}\left\{-1,+1\right\},\quad\forall r\in[m]\text{ and }i\in[d].

One can show that the training loss (
8
) is an empirical version of the denoising score-matching loss defined in (
5
) under a carefully chosen
s_{\theta}
. Correspondingly, the finite sample performance of
s_{\theta}
w.r.t. (
5
) is referred to as
generalization ability
. We would like to remark that the two-layer neural network parameterization has not been explored in the literature for approximating score functions. While the work
Chen et al. 2023a
considered multi-layer neural networks for score approximation, generalization, and distribution recovery; our work is complementary to them as they did not analyze the optimization procedure and no specific learning algorithm is considered in their work.

Neural Tangent Kernels.

For a two-layer ReLU neural network of the form (
7
), we follow
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
to introduce an associated neural tangent kernel

K:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
whose
(i,k)
-th entry is defined as

\displaystyle K^{ik}\left((x,t),\left(\tilde{x},\tilde{t}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}z^{\top}\tilde{z}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}a_{r}^{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{r}(0)^{\top}z\geq 0\right\}\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{r}(0)^{\top}\tilde{z}\geq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=z^{\top}\tilde{z}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{1}^{i}a_{1}^{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{1}(0)^{\top}z\geq 0\right\}\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{1}(0)^{\top}\tilde{z}\geq 0\right\}\right],

where
z=(x,t-T_{0})
and
\tilde{z}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{t}-T_{0})
. Here, the expectation is taken over all the randomness of
a_{1}^{i}
,
a_{1}^{k}
and
w_{1}(0)
. Similarly, we define a scalar-valued NTK
\kappa:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with each coordinate of the neural network:

\displaystyle\kappa\Big((x,t),(\tilde{x},\tilde{t})\Big)\coloneqq z^{\top}\tilde{z}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{1}(0)^{\top}z\geq 0\right\}\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{1}(0)^{\top}\tilde{z}\geq 0\right\}\right],

where
\mathbb{I}
denotes the indicator function. From the definition of the matrix-valued NTK, it is easy to see that
K
is a diagonal matrix and in particular,

K\left((x,t),\left(\tilde{x},\tilde{t}\right)\right)=\kappa((x,t),(\tilde{x},\tilde{t}))I_{d},

where
I_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional identity matrix.
Moreover, we let
\mathcal{H}
be the reproducing Hilbert space (RKHS) induced by the matrix-valued NTK
K
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
be the RKHS induced by the scalar-valued NTK
\kappa

(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Carmeli et al. 2010
)
. Finally, given a dataset
S
and defining
z_{j}=(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}-T_{0})
, the Gram matrix
H
of the kernel
K
is defined as a
dN\times dN
block matrix with

\displaystyle H\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}H_{11}&\cdots&H_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
H_{N1}&\cdots&H_{NN}\end{pmatrix},\quad H^{ik}_{j\ell}\coloneqq z_{j}^{\top}z_{\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[a_{1}^{i}a_{1}^{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{z_{j}^{\top}w_{1}(0)\geq 0,z_{\ell}^{\top}w_{1}(0)\geq 0\right\}\right].

(10)

3
Main Results

This section introduces our main theoretical results. We first propose a parametric form of
s_{\theta}
to simplify the score-matching loss in (
4
). Next, we show that the empirical version of DSM (
5
) is indeed equivalent to the quadratic loss defined in (
8
). Finally, we provide a decomposition of an upper bound on the loss function into four terms: a coupling term, a label mismatch term, a term related to early stopping, and an approximation error. These terms are carefully analyzed later.

To motivate our parametric form of
s_{\theta}
, we start by the following decomposition of the score function:

Lemma 3.1
.

The score function
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
admits the following decomposition:

\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{\alpha(t)}{h(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]-\frac{x}{h(t)}.

(11)

The proof, which follows the Gaussianity of the transition kernel
p_{t|0}
, is deferred to the appendix. A similar decomposition has been proved in
(
Chen et al. 2023a
, Lemma 1)
for data with linear structure, and in
Li et al. 2023
for discrete time analysis and the concurrent work
(
Mei & Wu 2023
)
. Compared to the expression of
\nabla\log p_{t|0}(x_{t}|x_{0})
computed in (
6
), we replace
X_{0}
by
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}\right]
to obtain the ground-truth score function in (
11
). Consequently, we call
X_{0}
the
noisy label
and
\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}\right]
the
true label
. We also make the following assumption on the diffusion models (
1
).

Assumption 3.2
.

The target density function
p_{0}
has a compact support with
\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq D
almost surely, for some constant
D>0
.

Assumption
3.2
is satisfied in most practical settings, including the generation of image, video, or audio. This assumption simplifies the subsequent analysis and can be relaxed to the sub-Gaussian tail assumption. Next, we propose the parametric form of
s_{\theta}
and
\lambda(t)
in the score-matching loss (
4
):

\displaystyle s_{{\bf W},a}(x,t)=\frac{\alpha(t)}{h(t)}\Pi_{D}(f_{{\bf W},a}(x,t))-\frac{x}{h(t)},\quad\textit{with}\,\,\lambda(t)=\frac{h(t)^{2}}{\alpha(t)^{2}},

where
\Pi_{D}
is the projection operator to the
L^{2}
-ball with radius
D
centered at zero. With the choice of
s_{{\bf W},a}
and
\lambda(t)
specified above, the score-matching loss (
4
) becomes

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf W}}\;\;\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}(f_{{\bf W},a}(X_{t},t))-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]{\rm d}t,

(12)

in which we define the target function as
f_{*}(x,t)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]
and the expectation is taken over
X_{t}
. Given that only
{\bf W}
is updated during optimization, in what follows, we omit
a
in the subscript of the neural network. Our loss function (
12
) is also supported by empirical studies
(
Ho et al. 2020
)
. In addition, (
12
) can be viewed as a regression task with noisy labels. In what follows, we will show that neural networks trained on noisy labels generalize well w.r.t. (
12
).

One major challenge in the theoretical analysis, which distinguishes us from the standard supervised learning problems, is the unboundedness of the input
X_{t}
in the objective function. To overcome this challenge, we employ a truncation argument with a threshold
R
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}(f_{\bf W}(X_{t},t))-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}(f_{\bf W}(X_{t},t))-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq R\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

(13)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}(f_{\bf W}(X_{t},t))-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}>R\right\}\right]{\rm d}t.

(14)

The next lemma controls the tail behavior in (
14
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose Assumption
3.2
holds. Then, uniformly over all
\bm{W}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}(f_{\bf W}(X_{t},t))-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}>R\right\}\right]{\rm d}t=\mathcal{O}({R}^{d-2}e^{-R^{2}/4}).

Lemma
3.3
states the term (
14
) is exponentially small in the threshold
R
. Thus, it suffices to focus on the loss (
13
) over the ball with radius
R
. Inspired by

for learning Lipschitz functions, we upper bound (

)
by the following decomposition at each iteration

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4(T-T_{0})}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}\left(f_{\bf W(\tau)}(X_{t},t)\right)-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}\left(f_{\bf W(\tau)}(X_{t},t)\right)-f^{K}_{\tau}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

(coupling)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|f^{K}_{\tau}(X_{t},t)-\tilde{f}^{K}_{\tau}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\tilde{f}^{K}_{\tau}(X_{t},t)-f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t},t)-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t.

(approximation)

The first term is the coupling error between neural networks

and a function

defined as:

\displaystyle f_{\tau}^{K}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}K((X_{t_{j}},t_{j}),(x,t))\gamma_{j}(\tau),\quad\gamma(\tau+1)=\gamma(\tau)-\eta(H\gamma(\tau)-y),

The fourth term is the approximation error of the target function

by a function

in the RKHS

of the same functional form as
{f}_{\tau}^{K}

\tilde{S}=\{(t_{j},\tilde{X}_{0,j},X_{t_{j}})\}_{j=1}^{N}

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{0,j}\coloneqq f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j})+\varepsilon_{j},\quad\varepsilon_{j}\coloneqq X_{0,j}-f_{*}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}).

Finally, we control the third term in the above decomposition by the early stopping rule, which is a classical technique in the statistical learning literature

3.1
Approximation

We start by analyzing the approximation term in our decomposition. This subsection focuses on the approximation error of the target function
f_{*}
by a function in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
induced by the NTK
K
. We start with a regularity assumption on the coefficient
g(t)
in the OU process.

Assumption 3.4
.

The function
g
is almost everywhere continuous and bounded on
[0,\infty)
.

Assumption
3.4
imposes a minimal requirement to guarantee that both
\alpha(t)
and
h(t)
are well defined at each timestamp
t\geq 0
.
In addition, the boundedness assumption of

is used to establish the Lipschitz property of the score function with respect to
t
in the literature
(
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Chen et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
)
.
We also make the following smoothness assumption on the target function

.

Assumption 3.5
.

For all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[T_{0},\infty)

is
\beta_{x}
-Lipschitz in

\left|f_{*}(x,t)-f_{*}(x^{\prime},t)\right|_{2}\leq\beta_{x}\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{2}

Assumption
3.5
implies the score function is Lipschitz w.r.t. the input

. This assumption is standard in the literature
(
Chen et al. 2022a
;
Chen et al. 2022b
;
Chen et al. 2023a
)
. Yet the Lipschitz continuity in Assumption
3.5
is only imposed on the regression function

, which is a consequence of the score decomposition.
To justify Assumption
3.5
, we provide an upper bound of the Lipschitz constant
\beta_{x}
in Lemma
G.1
.
The following theorem states a universal approximation theorem of using RKHS for score functions.

Theorem 3.6

(Universal Approximation of Score Function)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
,
3.4
and
3.5
hold. Let

and

be larger than a constant

1

1

3.2
Coupling

This subsection provides a coupling argument to control the error between the neural network training and the kernel regression. We make the following assumption on the dataset
S
:

Assumption 3.7
.

There exists a
\delta_{1}(\Delta,R)\in[0,1)
such that
\delta_{1}\rightarrow 0
when
R\rightarrow\infty
and
\Delta\to 0
, and we have the following holds with probability at least

t_{j}\in[T_{0}+\Delta,T]\text{ and }\left\|X_{t_{j}}\right\|_{2}\leq R\;\text{ for all sample }j.

Assumption

; see Lemma

in the appendix.
The next assumption is on the minimum eigenvalue of the Gram matrix

Bartlett et al. 2021

Nguyen et al. 2021

Assumption 3.8

There exists a constant

As shown in the literature of deep learning theory

In this subsection, we upper bound the error term induced by the label mismatch. Recall that

3.4
Early Stopping and the final result

Given the function
\tilde{f}_{\tau}^{K}
trained on the data set
\tilde{S}=\left\{\left(t_{j},\tilde{X}_{0,j},X_{t_{j}}\right)\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
and the target function
f_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
that generates the virtual label
\tilde{X}_{0,j}
, we transform the score matching problem to a classical kernel regression problem. The next technical assumption allows us to reduce the excess risk bound for the early-stopped GD learning in RKHS to the excess risk bound for learning Lipschitz functions.

Assumption 3.11
.

Fix any
f_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
with
\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq R_{\mathcal{H}}
and assume labels are generated as
\tilde{X}_{0,j}=f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j})+\varepsilon_{j}
. Suppose
\tilde{f}_{\widehat{T}}^{K}
is obtained by GD-trained kernel regression with the number of iterations
\widehat{T}
. We assume that there exists
\epsilon
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\tilde{f}^{K}_{\widehat{T}}(X_{t},t)-f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t\leq\epsilon(N,\widehat{T}),

and
\epsilon(N,\widehat{T})\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

Here,
\widehat{T}
is a data-dependent
early stopping rule
to control the excess risk of kernel regression.
For supervised learning with noisy labels, early stopping rule for GD is necessary to minimize the excess risk
(
Hu et al. 2021
;
Bartlett & Mendelson 2002
;
Li et al. 2020
)
.
Assumption
3.11
can be satisfied by an extension of classical early stopping rules. For the case of scalar-valued kernel regression, see
(
Raskutti et al. 2014
)
.
Next, we provide a generalization result for the score estimator:

Theorem 3.12

(Score Estimation and Generalization)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
,
3.4
,
3.5
,
3.7
,
3.8
hold and we set
m
and
\eta
as prescribed in Theorem
3.9
. Moreover, suppose
\widehat{T}
satisfies Assumption
3.11
with corresponding
\epsilon(N,\widehat{T})
. Then for any large enough
R
and
R_{\mathcal{H}}
and small enough
\Delta
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\lambda(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|s_{{\bf W}(\widehat{T})}(X_{t},t)-\nabla\log p_{t}(X_{t})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(R^{d-2}e^{-R^{2}/4})+4dA^{2}(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)+\frac{16\Delta D^{2}}{T-T_{0}}+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d^{10}N^{9}C_{\max}^{12}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}^{2}\delta^{4}C_{\min}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+4dA(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)+4C_{0}\left(\sqrt{dA(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)\Gamma_{\delta}}+\Gamma_{\delta}\right)+4\epsilon(N,\widehat{T}),

where
A(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)
is defined in Theorem
3.6
and
\Gamma_{\delta}
is given in Theorem

Theorem
3.12
shows that early-stopped neural network

learns the score function
\nabla\log p_{t}
well in the

sense over the interval
[T_{0},T]
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first
algorithm-based analysis for score estimation with neural network parameterization. Combined with recent findings in the distribution recovery property of diffusion models, we are the first to obtain an end-to-end guarantee with a provably efficient algorithm for diffusion models. The proof of Theorem
3.12
, which relies on Lemma
3.3
and Theorems

,
3.9
and
3.10
, can be found in the appendix.

4
Conclusion and Discussions

In this paper, we establish the
first
algorithm-based analysis of neural network-based score estimation in diffusion models. We demonstrate that GD-trained overparametrized neural networks can learn the ground truth score function with a sufficient number of samples when an early stopping rule is applied. Our work investigates all three aspects of the score estimation task: approximation, optimization, and generalization. The analytical framework laid out in this paper sheds light on the understanding of diffusion models and inspires innovative architecture design.

In addition, our work leaves several interesting questions for future investigation. For instance, the dimension dependency in our convergence results remains sub-optimal. To address this, one approach is to consider the manifold structure of the data distribution, such as the linear subspace assumption as suggested by
Chen et al. 2023a
and
Oko et al. 2023
. Another direction is to understand the role of neural network architectures like U-nets and transformers in the implementation of diffusion models for image tasks. Finally, an extension of training algorithms to stochastic gradient descent (SGD) and Adam would be of independent interest.

Proof of Lemma
3.1

Proof.

Recall that the density function
p_{t}
can be written as

\displaystyle p_{t}(x)=\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0}){\rm d}x_{0},

where the transition kernel
p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})=(2\pi h(t))^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)
.
Utilizing the dominated convergence theorem leads to

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\nabla\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0}){\rm d}x_{0}}{p_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(2\pi h(t)\right)^{-d/2}\int-\frac{x-\alpha(t)x_{0}}{h(t)}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|^{2}}{2h(t)}\right)p_{0}(x_{0}){\rm d}x_{0}}{p_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\int-\frac{x-\alpha(t)x_{0}}{h(t)}\cdot\frac{p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})}{p_{t}(x)}{\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\int-\frac{x-\alpha(t)x_{0}}{h(t)}\cdot p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{\alpha(t)X_{0}-X_{t}}{h(t)}\right|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)}{h(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]-\frac{x}{h(t)},

which completes the proof.

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int x_{0}p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int\frac{x_{0}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})}{\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime}}{\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{x_{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})}{\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime}}{\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int\frac{x_{0}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})\int\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{\left(\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{\rm d}x_{0}.

(17)

To proceed, recall that
X_{t}|X_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\alpha(t)X_{0},h(t)I_{d})
with
\alpha(t)=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{g(s)}{2}{\rm d}s\right)
and
h(t)=1-\alpha^{2}(t)
. We can compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left((2\pi h(t))^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2h(t)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{2}(2\pi h(t))^{-\frac{d}{2}-1}(2\pi)h^{\prime}(t)\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2h(t)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+(2\pi h(t))^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2h(t)}\right)\left(\frac{2(x-\alpha(t)x_{0})^{\top}x_{0}\alpha^{\prime}(t)}{2h(t)}+\frac{\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}h^{\prime}(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})}{2h^{2}(t)}\left(-dh(t)h^{\prime}(t)+2(x-\alpha(t)x_{0})^{\top}x_{0}\alpha^{\prime}(t)h(t)+\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}h^{\prime}(t)\right).

(18)

Since
\alpha^{\prime}(t)=-\alpha(t)g(t)/2
and
h^{\prime}(t)=-2\alpha(t)\alpha^{\prime}(t)=\alpha^{2}(t)g(t)
, we can rewrite (
18
) as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})}{2h^{2}(t)}\left(-dh(t)\alpha^{2}(t)g(t)-(x-\alpha(t)x_{0})^{\top}x_{0}\alpha(t)g(t)h(t)+\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\alpha^{2}(t)g(t)\right)

\displaystyle=p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\left(-dh(t)\alpha(t)-(x-\alpha(t)x_{0})^{\top}x_{0}h(t)+\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\alpha(t)\right)

\displaystyle=p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\left(-dh(t)\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))x_{0}^{\top}x+\alpha(t)\left\|x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

(19)

Plugging (
19
) back into (
17
), we have

\displaystyle\int\frac{x_{0}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})}{\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime}}{\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}\left(-dh(t)\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))X_{0}^{\top}X_{t}+\alpha(t)\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\bigg|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\bigg(-dh(t)\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]+\alpha(t)\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))x\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{0}\right\|^{2}_{2}\big|X_{t}=x\right]+\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}|X_{t}=x\right]\bigg),

and also

\displaystyle\int\frac{x_{0}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})\int\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime\prime}}{\left(\int p_{t|0}(x|x_{0}^{\prime})p_{0}(x_{0}^{\prime}){\rm d}x_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}\mathbb{E}\left[-dh(t)\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))X_{0}^{\top}X_{t}+\alpha(t)\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg|X_{t}\right]\bigg|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[-dh(t)\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))X_{0}^{\top}X_{t}+\alpha(t)\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\bigg|X_{t}=x\right]\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\left(-dh(t)\alpha(t)+\alpha(t)\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))x^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]+\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{0}\right\|^{2}_{2}|X_{t}=x\right]\right)\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right].

Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{*}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\bigg(\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(X_{0}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}\right]\right)\big|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))x\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}|X_{t}=x\right]-\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\bigg(\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(X_{0}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}\right]\right)\big|X_{t}=x\right]-(1+\alpha^{2}(t))x{\rm Cov}(X_{0}|X_{t}=x)\bigg).

The Pythagorean theorem implies that
\left\|X_{0}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}\right]\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}
. Since
\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}\leq D
by Assumption
3.2
, we can apply the triangle inequality to obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[T_{0},\infty)}\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f_{*}(x,t)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha(t)g(t)}{2h^{2}(t)}\bigg[\alpha(t)\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|X_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|X_{0}-\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}\right]\right\|_{2}\bigg|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+(1+\alpha^{2}(t))\left\|x\right\|_{2}\left\|{\rm Cov}(X_{0}|X_{t}=x)\right\|_{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle=O(\sqrt{d}R)\eqqcolon\beta_{t}(R),

where we have used the facts that
\alpha(t)\leq 1
,
h(t)\geq h(T_{0})
and
g(t)
is bounded on
[T_{0},\infty)
.
∎

Next, we define two kernels without the bias term compared to NTKs in Section

be a real-valued RKHS induced by the scalar-valued NTK

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}(z,\tilde{z})\coloneqq z^{\top}\tilde{z}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{1}(0)^{\top}z\geq 0\right\}\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{1}(0)^{\top}\tilde{z}\geq 0\right\}\right].

Similarly, let
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}
be a vector-valued RKHS induced by the matrix-valued NTK
\tilde{K}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(z,\tilde{z})=\tilde{\kappa}(z,\tilde{z})I_{d}.

The next lemma shows the approximation of of a Lipschitz target function over a ball with radius
R
.

Lemma C.2
.

(
Bach 2017
, Proposition 6)

Let
R_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
be larger than a constant
c_{1}
that depends only on
d
. For any function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for any
\left\|z\right\|_{\infty},\left\|z^{\prime}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R
,
\sup_{\left\|z\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\left|f(z)\right|\leq\Lambda
and
\left|f(z)-f(z^{\prime})\right|\leq\frac{\Lambda}{R}\left\|z-z^{\prime}\right\|_{2}
, there exists
f_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
with
\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}^{2}\leq R_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|z\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\left|f(z)-f_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}(z)\right|\leq A(R_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}),\quad A(R_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}})\coloneqq c_{1}\Lambda\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}}}{\Lambda}\right)^{-\frac{2}{d}}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}}}{\Lambda}\right).

Lemma
C.2
comes from
(
Bach 2017
, Proposition 6)
for
d+1
,
\alpha=0
and
q=\infty
.
Now we are prepared to prove that the regression function
f_{*}
can be approximated by a function in the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
induced by
K((x,t),(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))=\tilde{K}((x,t-T_{0}),(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}-T_{0}))

Theorem C.3

(Approximation of the Score Function on the Ball)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
,
3.4
and
3.5
hold. Let
R\geq T-T_{0}
and
R_{\mathcal{H}}
be larger than a constant
c_{1}
that depends only on
d
. There exists a function
f_{\mathcal{H}}\in\mathcal{H}
with
\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq dR_{\mathcal{H}}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\sup_{t\in[T_{0},T]}\left\|f_{*}(x,t)-f_{\mathcal{H}}(x,t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq A(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)\coloneqq c_{1}\Lambda(R)\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{H}}}}{\Lambda(R)}\right)^{-\frac{2}{d}}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{H}}}}{\Lambda(R)}\right),

where
\Lambda(R)=O(\sqrt{d}R^{2})
.

Proof.

We define an auxiliary target function
\tilde{f}_{*}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\tilde{f}_{*}(x,t)\coloneqq f_{*}(x,\left|t\right|+T_{0})
. By Assumption
3.5
and Lemma
C.1
, the function
f_{*}(x,t)
is
\beta_{x}
-Lipschitz in
x
and
\beta_{t}(R)
-Lipschitz in
t
for all
\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq R
and
t\in[T_{0},\infty)
; so is each coordinate map. Since
\sup_{\left\|(x,t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\left\|\tilde{f}_{*}(x,t)\right\|_{2}\leq D
and for all
\left\|(x,t)\right\|_{\infty},\left\|(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\|_{\infty}\leq R
,

\displaystyle\left\|\tilde{f}_{*}(x,t)-\tilde{f}_{*}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\tilde{f}_{*}(x,t)-\tilde{f}_{*}(x^{\prime},t)\right\|_{2}+\left\|\tilde{f_{*}}(x^{\prime},t)-\tilde{f}_{*}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|f_{*}(x,\left|t\right|+T_{0})-f_{*}(x^{\prime},\left|t\right|+T_{0})\right\|_{2}+\left\|f_{*}(x^{\prime},\left|t\right|+T_{0})-f_{*}(x^{\prime},\left|t^{\prime}\right|+T_{0})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{x}\left\|x-x^{\prime}\right\|_{2}+\beta_{t}(R)\left|\left|t\right|-\left|t^{\prime}\right|\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\beta_{x}+\beta_{t}(R)\right)\left\|(x,t)-(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\right\|_{2},

(20)

one can apply Lemma
C.2
by choosing
\Lambda(R)=\max\left\{D,R\left\{\beta_{x}+\beta_{t}(R)\right\}\right\}
to conclude that for each coordinate
i=1,\dots,d
, there exists
\tilde{f}_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}^{i}\in\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}
with
\left\|\tilde{f}^{i}_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\right\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}^{2}\leq R_{\mathcal{H}}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|(x,t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\left|\tilde{f}_{*}^{i}(x,t)-\tilde{f}^{i}_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}(x,t)\right|\leq A(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)=c_{1}\Lambda(R)\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{H}}}}{\Lambda(R)}\right)^{-\frac{2}{d}}\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{R_{\mathcal{H}}}}{\Lambda(R)}\right).

Defining
f^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}(x,t)\coloneqq\tilde{f}_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}^{i}(x,t-T_{0})
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\,\,\sup_{t\in[T_{0},R+T_{0}]}\left|f_{*}^{i}(x,t)-f_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(x,t)\right|\leq A(R_{\mathcal{H}},R).

Note that
f^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}
lies in the RKHS induced by the kernel
{\kappa}((x,t),(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}))=\tilde{\kappa}((x,t-T_{0}),(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}-T_{0}))
and
\left\|f^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\tilde{\kappa}}=\left\|\tilde{f}^{i}_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\right\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}
.
We next show that
f_{\mathcal{H}}=(f^{1}_{\mathcal{H}},\dots,f^{d}_{\mathcal{H}})
is in the RKHS induced by
K
. Since each coordinate of
f^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}
lies in the RKHS induced by
{\kappa}
, by relabeling data points, without loss of generality, it suffices to consider

\displaystyle f^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}(\cdot)=\sum_{p=1}^{P}\alpha_{p}^{i}{\kappa}((x,t)_{p},\cdot),\quad(x,t)_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\alpha_{p}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

It follows

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{H}}(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{f}_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(\cdot)\mathbf{e}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{p}\alpha_{p}^{i}\kappa((x,t)_{p},\cdot)\right)\mathbf{e}_{i}=\sum_{p=1}^{P}{K}((x,t)_{p},\cdot)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{p}^{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\right)\in\mathcal{H}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\sum_{p=1}^{P}{K}((x,t)_{p},\cdot)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{p}^{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}\right),\sum_{q=1}^{P}{K}((x,t)_{q},\cdot)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}\alpha_{q}^{k}\mathbf{e}_{k}\right)\right\rangle_{{K}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q}\sum_{i,k}\alpha_{p}^{i}\alpha_{q}^{k}\mathbf{e}_{i}^{\top}{K}((x,t)_{p},(x,t)_{q})\mathbf{e}_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{p,q}\alpha_{p}^{i}\alpha_{q}^{i}{\kappa}((x,t)_{p},(x,t)_{q})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\langle\sum_{p=1}^{P}\alpha_{p}^{i}{\kappa}((x,t)_{p},\cdot),\sum_{q=1}^{P}\alpha_{q}^{i}{\kappa}((x,t)_{q},\cdot)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|f^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{{\kappa}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\|\tilde{f}^{i}_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}\right\|_{\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{1}}^{2}\leq dR_{\mathcal{H}}.

(21)

Therefore, we have found a function
f_{\mathcal{H}}:\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the RKHS induced by
{K}
such that
\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq dR_{\mathcal{H}}
and

\displaystyle\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq R}\,\,\sup_{t\in[T_{0},T]}\left\|f_{*}(x,t)-f_{\mathcal{H}}(x,t)\right\|_{\infty}\leq A(R_{\mathcal{H}},R).

∎

As a corollary of Theorem
C.3
, we can prove Theorem
3.6
.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

For any
{R}\geq T-T_{0}
and
t\in[T_{0},T]
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t},t)-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq d\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{\infty}\leq{R}}\sup_{t\in[T_{0},T]}\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}(x,t)-f_{*}(x,t)\right\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq dA^{2}(R_{\mathcal{H}},{R}),

which implies that

\displaystyle\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|f_{\mathcal{H}}(X_{t},t)-f_{*}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t\leq d(T-T_{0})A^{2}(R_{\mathcal{H}},{R}).

Dividing both sides by

will complete the proof.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
3.9

To prove Theorem
3.9
, we first show the linear convergence rate of gradient descent over the training dataset
S=\left\{t_{j},X_{0,j},X_{t_{j}}\right\}
.
Define a Gram matrix
H(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{dN\times dN}
at each iteration
\tau
as as block matrix:

\displaystyle H(\tau)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}H_{11}&\dots&H_{1N}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
H_{N1}&\dots&H_{NN}\end{pmatrix},\quad H^{ik}_{j\ell}(\tau)=\frac{1}{m}z_{j}^{\top}z_{\ell}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}a_{r}^{k}\mathbb{I}\left\{z_{j}^{\top}w_{r}(\tau)\geq 0,z_{\ell}^{\top}w_{r}(\tau)\geq 0\right\}.

One can check that
H=\mathbb{E}\left[H(0)\right]
with expectation taken over the random initialization. For ease of presentation, recall that we have set
C_{\min}=\Delta
and
C_{\max}=\sqrt{R^{2}+(T-T_{0})^{2}}
so that
\left\|(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}-T_{0})\right\|_{2}\in[C_{\min},C_{\max}]
by Assumption
3.7
. Moreover, we denote the activation pattern of neural
w_{r}
for sample
j
at iteration
\tau
as
\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\coloneqq\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j}\geq 0\right\}
. The convergence of GD algorithm is given in the next theorem.

Theorem D.1

(Convergence Rate of Gradient Descent)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
,
3.7
and
3.8
hold. If we set
m=\Omega\left(\frac{(dN)^{6}C_{\max}^{6}}{\lambda_{0}^{10}\delta^{3}C_{\min}^{2}}\right)
with i.i.d. initialize for
w_{r}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d+1})
and
a_{r}^{i}\sim{\rm Unif}\left\{-1,1\right\}
, and we set
\eta=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{(dN)^{2}C_{\max}^{4}}\right)
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
\tau\geq 0
and
r=1,\dots,m
simultaneously, we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bf W}(\tau))\leq\left(1-\eta\lambda_{0}\right)^{\tau}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bf W}(0)),

(22)

and

\displaystyle\left\|w_{r}(\tau)-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}\leq R_{w}\coloneqq O\left(\frac{dNC^{2}_{\max}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\sqrt{\delta}}\right).

(23)

Proof.

Following the ideas in
Du et al. 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019a
, we prove the convergence of GD by induction. The induction is to show that (
22
) holds for all
\tau
.
It is straightforward to see the inequality holds for
\tau=0
. Assuming (
22
) holds for
0\leq\tau^{\prime}\leq\tau
, we will show it is also true for
\tau^{\prime}=\tau+1
. Let
u(\tau)={\rm vec}(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})(\tau)
and
y={\rm vec}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})
with
u_{j}(\tau)=f_{\bf W(\tau)}(X_{t_{j}},t_{j})
and
y_{j}=X_{0,j}
. We first need the following result for all
\tau^{\prime}=0,\dots,\tau+1
:

\displaystyle\left\|w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\eta\sum_{\tau^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\tau^{\prime}-1}\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bf W}(\tau^{\prime\prime}))}{\partial w_{r}(\tau^{\prime\prime})}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\sum_{\tau^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\tau^{\prime}-1}\left\|\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}({\bf W}(\tau^{\prime\prime}))}{\partial w_{r}(\tau^{\prime\prime})}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\eta C_{\max}\sum_{\tau^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\tau^{\prime}-1}\frac{\sqrt{dN}\left\|u(\tau^{\prime\prime})-y\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{m}}

(24)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta C_{\max}\sqrt{dN}}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\tau^{\prime}-1}\left(1-\eta\lambda_{0}\right)^{\tau^{\prime\prime}/2}\left\|u(0)-y\right\|_{2}

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta C_{\max}\sqrt{dN}}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{\tau^{\prime\prime}=0}^{\infty}(1-\eta\lambda_{0}/2)^{\tau^{\prime\prime}}\left\|u(0)-y\right\|_{2}

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{2C_{\max}\sqrt{dN}\left\|u(0)-y\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}}.

(27)

Here, we have an upper bound on gradient (
9
) to derive (
24
). Also, (
25
) and (
26
) follow from the induction hypothesis and the fact that
\sqrt{1-x}\leq 1-x/2
.
We further bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|u(0)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|u^{i}_{j}(0)-y_{j}^{i}\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\left[(y_{j}^{i})^{2}-2y_{j}^{i}\mathbb{E}\left[f^{i}({\bf W},a,(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}))\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(f^{i})^{2}({\bf W},a,(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}))\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\left[(y_{j}^{i})^{2}+C_{\max}^{2}\right]=O(dNC_{\max}^{2}),

where we have used the facts that
\mathbb{E}\left[f^{i}({\bf W},a,(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}))\right]=0
,
\mathbb{E}\left[(f^{i})^{2}({\bf W},a,(X_{t_{j}},t_{j}))\right]\leq C_{\max}^{2}
and
\left\|y_{j}\right\|_{2}\leq D
. Thus, the Markov’s inequality yields

\left\|u(0)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(dNC_{\max}^{2}/\delta)
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}\leq R_{w}\coloneqq O\left(\frac{dNC^{2}_{\max}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\sqrt{\delta}}\right),\quad\forall\tau^{\prime}=0,\dots,\tau+1,r=1,\dots,m.

(28)

Define index sets

\displaystyle S_{j}\coloneqq\left\{r\in[m]:\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}=0\right\},\quad\bar{S}_{j}\coloneqq\left\{r\in[m]:\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}\neq 0\right\},

where
A_{j,r}\coloneqq\left\{\left|w_{r}(0)^{\top}z_{j}\right|\leq R_{w}C_{\max}\right\}
. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau^{\prime})\neq\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(0)\right\}\leq\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}>R_{w}\right\}.

(29)

To see this, note that if
\left\|w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}\leq R_{w}
it follows
\left|w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})^{\top}z_{j}-w_{r}(0)^{\top}z_{j}\right|\leq R_{w}C_{\max}
. If
w_{r}(0)^{\top}z_{j}>R_{w}C_{\max}
, then
w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})^{\top}z_{j}>0
. Similarly, if
w_{r}(0)^{\top}z_{j}<-R_{w}C_{\max}
, then
w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})^{\top}z_{j}<0
. Hence, we must have
\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau^{\prime})=\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(0)
. From (
28
) and (
29
), we conclude that with probability at least
1-\delta
, all neurons in
S_{j}
will not change their activation pattern on
z_{j}
during optimization, i.e.,

\displaystyle r\in S_{j}\implies\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau^{\prime})=\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(0),\quad\forall\tau^{\prime}=0,\dots,\tau+1.

(30)

With such a partition, we can write the dynamics of
u_{j}^{i}(\tau)
as

\displaystyle u_{j}^{i}(\tau+1)-u_{j}^{i}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}\left[\sigma(w_{r}(\tau+1)^{\top}z_{j})-\sigma(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in S_{j}}a_{r}^{i}\left[\sigma(w_{r}(\tau+1)^{\top}z_{j})-\sigma(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j})\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}a_{r}^{i}\left[\sigma(w_{r}(\tau+1)^{\top}z_{j})-\sigma(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j})\right].

(31)

By utilizing the condition (
30
), we bound the first term in (
31
) as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in S_{j}}a_{r}^{i}\left[\sigma(w_{r}(\tau+1)^{\top}z_{j})-\sigma(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in S_{j}}a_{r}^{i}\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\left(w_{r}(\tau+1)^{\top}z_{j}-w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in S_{j}}a_{r}^{i}\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\left(-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})a_{r}^{k}z_{\ell}\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(\tau)\right)^{\top}z_{j}

(32)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\eta}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})z_{j}^{\top}z_{\ell}\sum_{r\in S_{j}}a_{r}^{i}a_{r}^{k}\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\eta\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})H_{j\ell}^{ik}(\tau)+\epsilon_{j}^{i}(\tau),

(33)

where we have set
\epsilon_{j}^{i}(\tau)\coloneqq\frac{\eta}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})z_{j}^{\top}z_{\ell}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}a_{r}^{i}a_{r}^{k}(\tau)\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(\tau)
. Here, we have used the GD update rule and the definition of
H_{j\ell}^{ik}(\tau)
to derive (
32
) and (
33
). We can further upper bound the error term

\displaystyle\left|\epsilon_{j}^{i}(\tau)\right|\leq\frac{\eta}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}\left\|z_{\ell}\right\|_{2}\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|\leq\frac{\eta C_{\max}^{2}\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|\sqrt{dN}}{m}\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}.

(34)

Next, we denote the second term in (
31
) by
\bar{\epsilon}_{j}^{i}(\tau)
, which can be bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\bar{\epsilon}_{j}^{i}(\tau)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}a_{r}^{i}\left[\sigma(w_{r}(\tau+1)^{\top}z_{j})-\sigma(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}z_{j})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}\left|a_{r}^{i}\right|\left|(w_{r}(\tau+1)-w_{r}(\tau))^{\top}z_{j}\right|

(35)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\max}}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}\left\|w_{r}(\tau+1)-w_{r}(\tau)\right\|_{2}

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\max}}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}\left\|-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})a_{r}^{k}z_{\ell}\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(\tau)\right\|_{2}

(37)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta C_{\max}}{m}\sum_{r\in\bar{S}_{j}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left|u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k}\right|\left\|z_{\ell}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta C^{2}_{\max}\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|\sqrt{dN}}{m}\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2},

(38)

where we have applied the
1
-Lipschitz property of the ReLU activation function to obtain (
35
). Also, we have employed the facts
\left|a_{r}^{i}\right|\leq 1
and
\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{\max}
in (
36
). The GD update rule has been utilized to reach (
37
). Combining (
31
), (
33
) and (
38
), we have

\displaystyle u_{j}^{i}(\tau+1)-u_{j}^{i}(\tau)=-\eta\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(u_{\ell}^{k}(\tau)-y_{\ell}^{k})H_{j\ell}^{ik}(\tau)+\epsilon_{j}^{i}(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}_{j}^{i}(\tau),

which can be further written in a compact form through vectorization:

\displaystyle u(\tau+1)-u(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\eta H(\tau)(u(\tau)-y)+\epsilon(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\eta H(u(\tau)-y)+\eta(H-H(\tau))(u(\tau)-y)+\epsilon(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}(\tau),

(39)

where
\epsilon(\tau)
and
\bar{\epsilon}(\tau)
are defined as similar to
u(\tau)
by vectorization.

We move on to show
H(\tau)
is close to
H
for sufficiently wide neural networks. First, the Hoeffding’s inequality implies, with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|H_{j\ell}^{ik}(0)-H_{j\ell}^{ik}\right|\leq C_{\max}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2\log(2/\delta^{\prime})}{m}}.

Setting
\delta^{\prime}=\delta/(dN)^{2}
and applying the union bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|H-H(0)\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i,k,j,\ell}\left|H_{j\ell}^{ik}(0)-H_{j\ell}^{ik}\right|^{2}\leq(dN)^{2}C_{\max}^{4}\cdot\frac{2\log(2(dN)^{2}/\delta)}{m},

(40)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Next, note that (
29
) also implies

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau^{\prime})\neq\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(0)\right\}\leq\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|w_{r}(\tau^{\prime})-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}>R_{w}\text{ for some }r\right\}.

It follows

\displaystyle\left|H_{j\ell}^{ik}(\tau)-H_{j\ell}^{ik}(0)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{m}z_{j}^{\top}z_{\ell}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}a_{r}^{k}\left[\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(\tau)-\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(0)\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(0)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\max}^{2}}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(\tau)\neq\mathbb{I}_{j,r}(0)\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(\tau)\neq\mathbb{I}_{\ell,r}(0)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\max}^{2}}{m}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{\ell,r}\right\}\right]+2\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|w_{r}(\tau)-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}>R_{w}\text{ for some }r\right\}\right).

By taking expectation on both sides and applying (
28
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{j\ell}^{ik}(\tau)-H_{j\ell}^{ik}(0)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\max}^{2}}{m}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}+\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{\ell,r}\right\}\right]+\frac{2C_{\max}^{2}}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|w_{r}(\tau)-w_{r}(0)\right\|_{2}>R_{w}\text{ for some }r\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4R_{w}C_{\max}^{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi}C_{\min}}+\frac{2C_{\max}^{2}}{m}\delta,

(41)

where we have used the following anti-concentration inequality for Gaussian random variables:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}\right]=\mathbb{P}_{z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\left\|z_{j}\right\|^{2}_{2})}\left(\left|z\right|\leq R_{w}C_{\max}\right)=\int_{-R_{w}C_{\max}}^{R_{w}C_{\max}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}}}e^{-z^{2}/2\left\|z_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{2R_{w}C_{\max}}{\sqrt{2\pi}C_{\min}}.

(42)

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|H(\tau)-H(0)\right\|_{F}\right]\leq\sum_{i,k,j,\ell}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|H_{j\ell}^{ik}(\tau)-H_{j\ell}^{ik}(0)\right|\right]\leq\frac{4(dN)^{2}R_{w}C_{\max}^{3}}{\sqrt{2\pi}C_{\min}}+\frac{2(dN)^{2}C_{\max}^{2}}{m}\delta.

Finally, from the Markov’s inequality, we know that with probability at least
1-\delta
it holds

\displaystyle\left\|H(\tau)-H(0)\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(dN)^{3}C_{\max}^{4}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\delta^{3/2}C_{\min}}\right).

(43)

Therefore, combining (
40
) and (
43
) leads to

\displaystyle\left\|H-H(\tau)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|H-H(0)\right\|_{2}+\left\|H(0)-H(\tau)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(dN)\sqrt{\log((dN)^{2}/\delta)}}{\sqrt{m}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(dN)^{3}C_{\max}^{4}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\delta^{3/2}C_{\min}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(dN)^{3}C_{\max}^{4}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\delta^{3/2}C_{\min}},

(44)

It remains to bound two error terms in (
39
). From (
34
) and (
38
), we know that

\displaystyle\left\|\epsilon(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}(\tau)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\epsilon(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}(\tau)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\epsilon_{j}^{i}(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}_{j}^{i}\right|(\tau)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\eta\left(C_{\max}+C_{\max}^{2}\right)\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|\sqrt{dN}}{m}\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\eta C_{\max}^{2}d\sqrt{dN}}{m}\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|.

(45)

Furthermore, it follows from (
28
) and (
42
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{r=1}^{m}\mathbb{I}\left\{A_{j,r}\right\}\right]=\frac{2mR_{w}C_{\max}}{\sqrt{2\pi}C_{\min}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{m}(dN)C_{\max}^{3}}{\lambda_{0}\sqrt{\delta}C_{\min}}\right).

Thus, the Markov’s inequality implies
\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left|\bar{S}_{j}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{m}dN^{2}C_{\max}^{3}}{\lambda_{0}\delta^{3/2}C_{\min}}\right)
with probability at least
1-\delta
.

We need the last result before proving the induction hypothesis. Following the same argument as in (
38
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|u(\tau+1)-u(\tau)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\left|u_{j}^{i}(\tau+1)-u_{j}^{i}(\tau)\right|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq(dN)\left(\eta C_{\max}^{2}\sqrt{dN}\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta^{2}(dN)^{2}C_{\max}^{4}\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(46)

With the prediction dynamics (
39
) and all the estimates (
44
), (
45
) and (
46
), we can prove the induction hypothesis:

\displaystyle\left\|u(\tau+1)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|u(\tau+1)-u(\tau)+u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|u(\tau+1)-u(\tau)\right\|_{2}^{2}+2(u(\tau+1)-u(\tau))^{\top}(u(\tau)-y)

\displaystyle=\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|u\left(\tau+1\right)-u(\tau)\right\|_{2}^{2}-2\eta(u(\tau)-y)^{\top}H(u(\tau)-y)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\eta(u(\tau)-y)^{\top}(H-H(\tau))(u(\tau)-y)+2(\epsilon(\tau)+\bar{\epsilon}(\tau))^{\top}(u(\tau)-y)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-2\eta\lambda_{0}-O\left(\eta^{2}(dN)^{2}C_{\max}^{4}\right)+O\left(\frac{\eta(dN)^{3}C_{\max}^{4}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\delta^{3/2}C_{\min}}\right)+O\left(\frac{\eta(dN)^{5/2}C_{\max}^{5}}{\sqrt{m}\lambda_{0}\delta^{3/2}C_{\min}}\right)\right)\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\eta\lambda_{0})\left\|u(\tau)-y\right\|_{2}^{2},

where we have used the assumption
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{\min}(H)>0
and the bounds
m=\Omega\left(\frac{(dN)^{6}C_{\max}^{10}}{\lambda_{0}^{4}\delta^{3}C_{\min}^{2}}\right)
and
\eta=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lambda_{0}}{(dN)^{2}C_{\max}^{4}}\right)
. Therefore, we finish the induction and conclude the proof by scaling
\delta
.

To upper bound the coupling term, the non-expansive property of the projection operator and Assumption

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}\left(f_{\bf W(\tau)}(X_{t},t)\right)-f^{K}_{\tau}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}+\Delta}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\Pi_{D}\left(f_{\bf W(\tau)}(X_{t},t)\right)-f^{K}_{\tau}(X_{t},t)\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq{R}\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

(47)

To upper bound the second term in (

Lemma D.2
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
3.9
. Then with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}+\Delta}^{T}\int_{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq R}\left\|f_{\bf{W}(\tau)}(x,t)-f^{{\rm lin}}_{\bar{\bf W}(\tau)}(x,t)\right\|_{2}^{2}{\rm d}P_{X_{t}}(x){\rm d}t=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d(dN)^{9}C_{\max}^{12}}{\sqrt{m}\delta^{4}\lambda_{0}^{2}C_{\min}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Denote by
\mathbb{I}_{r}(\tau)\coloneqq\mathbb{I}\left\{w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\geq 0\right\}
.
Note that for each
i=1,\dots,d
we have

\displaystyle\left|f_{\bf{W}(\tau)}^{i}(x,t)-f^{{\rm lin},i}_{\bar{\bf W}(\tau)}(x,t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}\sigma\left(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}\bar{w}_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}\sigma\left(w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad+\bigg|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}\bar{w}_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}w_{r}(\tau)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\left(\mathbb{I}_{r}(\tau)-\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right)\right|+\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}a_{r}^{i}(w_{r}(\tau)-\bar{w}_{r}(\tau))^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left|\left(w_{r}(\tau)-w_{r}(0)\right)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\right|\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{r}(\tau)\neq\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left|\left(w_{r}(\tau)-\bar{w}_{r}(\tau)\right)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\right|\mathbb{I}_{r}(0),

(48)

where we have used the fact that

\displaystyle\left|a\right|\mathbb{I}\left\{{\rm sgn}(a)\neq{\rm sgn}(b)\right\}\leq\left|a-b\right|\mathbb{I}\left\{{\rm sgn}(a)\neq{\rm sgn}(b)\right\},\quad\forall a,b\in\mathbb{R}.

Taking square both sides of (

\displaystyle\left|f_{\bf{W}(\tau)}^{i}(x,t)-f^{{\rm lin},i}_{\bar{\bf W}(\tau)}(x,t)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left|\left(w_{r}(\tau)-w_{r}(0)\right)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\right|^{2}\mathbb{I}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{r}(\tau)\neq\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+2\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\left|\left(w_{r}(\tau)-\bar{w}_{r}(\tau)\right)^{\top}(x,t-T_{0})\right|\mathbb{I}_{r}(0)\right)^{2}

We first bound the first term in (

implies that with probability at least

29

Lemma D.3
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
3.9
, if we initialize
\gamma(0)=\bar{\gamma}(0)=H(0)^{-1}u(0)
, then it holds with probability at least
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}+\Delta}^{T}\int_{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq R}\left\|f^{\rm lin}_{\bar{\bf W}(\tau)}(x,t)-f_{\tau}^{K}(x,t)\right\|_{2}^{2}{\rm d}P_{X_{t}}(x){\rm d}t=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d^{5}N^{4}C_{\max}^{8}}{m\delta^{2}\lambda_{0}^{2}}\right).

Proof.

Note that the gradient of the training loss is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{\rm lin}(\bar{\bf W})}{\partial{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W})}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W})}\frac{1}{2}\left\|u^{\rm lin}-y\right\|_{2}^{2}={\bf Z}(0)^{\top}(u^{\rm lin}-y).

We first show that at
\tau=0
, there is a vector
\bar{\gamma}(0)\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
such that
{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W})(0)={\bf Z}(0)
. Note that our choice implies
\bar{\gamma}(0)=\gamma(0)=\left({\bf Z}(0){\bf Z}(0)^{\top}\right)^{-1}u^{\rm lin}(0)
. Let
{\bf Z}(0)=U\Sigma V^{\top}
be the corresponding singular value decomposition. Since
{\bf Z}(0)
has full row rank, we can write the diagonal entries of
\Sigma
as
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{dN}>0
. Noting that
u^{\rm lin}(0)={\bf Z}(0){\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(0))
,

\displaystyle{\bf Z}(0)^{\top}\bar{\gamma}(0)

\displaystyle={\bf Z}(0)^{\top}\left({\bf Z}(0){\bf Z}(0)^{\top}\right)^{-1}u^{\rm lin}(0)

\displaystyle=V\Sigma^{\top}U^{\top}(U\Sigma V^{\top}V\Sigma^{\top}U^{\top})^{-1}U\Sigma V^{\top}{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(0))

\displaystyle=V\Sigma^{\top}U^{\top}(U{\rm diag}(\sigma_{1}^{-2},\dots,\sigma_{dN}^{-2})U^{\top})U\Sigma V^{\top}{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(0))

\displaystyle=V\begin{pmatrix}I_{dN}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{\top}{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(0))

\displaystyle=V\begin{pmatrix}I_{dN}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_{dN}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}V^{\top}{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(0))={\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(0)).

It follows for each
\tau
, there is a vector
\bar{\gamma}(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
such that

\displaystyle{\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(\tau))

\displaystyle={\rm vec}(\bar{\bf W}(\tau-1))-\eta{\bf Z}(0)^{\top}(u^{\rm lin}(\tau-1)-y)={\bf Z}(0)^{\top}\bar{\gamma}(\tau).

Define a matrix
{\bf Z}(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m(d+1)}
such that its
i
-th row is

Also, we utilized the equality condition of Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain (

). Further, (
77
) is a consequence of Jensen’s inequality. Moreover, we apply the facts

to derive (

).

Next, we want to find
\beta
associated with
\mathcal{F}_{\rho}^{R}
. Note that the reproducing property and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality imply that

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\sup_{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[T_{0},T]}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|f^{i}(x,t)\right|\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq R\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq R}\sup_{t\in[T_{0},T]}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left|\left\langle f,K(\cdot,(x,t)){\bf e}_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq R}\sup_{t\in[T_{0},T]}\left\|f\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\|K(\cdot,(x,t)){\bf e}_{i}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho C_{\max}.

It remains to find some
\rho
such that
\left\|f_{\tau}^{K}-\tilde{f}_{\tau}^{K}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\rho
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\tau}^{K}-\tilde{f}_{\tau}^{K}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{N}K((X_{t_{j}},t_{j}),\cdot)(\gamma_{j}(\tau)-\tilde{\gamma}_{j}(\tau))\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}(\gamma_{j}(\tau)-\tilde{\gamma}_{j}(\tau))^{\top}K((X_{t_{j}},t_{j}),(X_{t_{\ell}},t_{\ell}))(\gamma_{j}(\tau)-\tilde{\gamma}_{j}(\tau))

\displaystyle=(\gamma(\tau)-\tilde{\gamma}(\tau))^{\top}H(\gamma(\tau)-\tilde{\gamma}(\tau)).

Note that the GD updating rule implies

\displaystyle\gamma(\tau)-\tilde{\gamma}(\tau)=H^{-1}(I_{dN}-(I_{dN}-\eta H^{2})^{\tau})(y-\tilde{y}).

Therefore, Assumption
3.8
and Theorem
C.3
lead to

\displaystyle\left\|f_{\tau}^{K}-\tilde{f}_{\tau}^{K}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\left\|(I_{dN}-(I_{dN}-\eta H^{2})^{\tau})(y-\tilde{y})\right\|_{H^{-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|H^{-1}\right\|_{2}\left\|I_{dN}-(I_{dN}-\eta H^{2})^{\tau}\right\|_{2}\left\|y-\tilde{y}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\left\|y-\tilde{y}\right\|_{2}}{\lambda_{0}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{dN}A(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)}{\lambda_{0}}=\rho.

Here, we have used the choice of small enough
\eta
and the fact that
\left\|H\right\|_{F}
is finite.
Now we put everything elements above together and apply Lemma
E.1
to conclude that with probability
1-\delta
that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}}^{T}\int_{\left\|x\right\|_{2}\leq R}\left\|f_{\tau}^{K}(x,t)-\tilde{f}_{\tau}^{K}(x,t)\right\|_{2}^{2}{\rm d}P_{X_{t}}(x)dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|u^{K}(\tau)-\tilde{u}^{K}(\tau)\right\|_{2}^{2}+C_{0}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|u^{K}(\tau)-\tilde{u}^{K}(\tau)\right\|_{2}^{2}\cdot\Gamma_{\delta}}+\Gamma_{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq dA(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)+C_{0}\left(\sqrt{dA(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)\Gamma_{\delta}}+\Gamma_{\delta}\right),

where we have defined

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\delta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Gamma_{\delta}(\mathcal{F}_{\rho}^{R})

\displaystyle=\left(2d\left(\sqrt{d}\log^{3/2}\left(e\beta dN\right)\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{dN}(\left(\Pi\circ\mathcal{F}\right))+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)^{2}+\frac{d\beta^{2}}{N}\left(\log(1/\delta)+\log(\log N)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2d\left(\sqrt{d}\log^{3/2}\left(e\rho C_{\max}dN\right)\frac{\rho C_{\max}}{\sqrt{dN}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right)^{2}+\frac{d\rho^{2}C_{\max}^{2}}{N}\left(\log(1/\delta)+\log(\log N)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{d^{2}A^{2}(R_{\mathcal{H}},R)C_{\max}^{2}}{\lambda_{0}^{2}}\left(\log(1/\delta)+\log\left(\log N\right)\right).

∎

Appendix G
Verification of Assumptions

In this section, we verify Assumptions
3.5
,
3.7
and
3.8
. The following lemma provides an upper bound of
\beta_{x}
in Assumption
3.5
.

Lemma G.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
3.2
holds. Then the Lipschitz constant
\beta_{x}
in Assumption
3.5
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\beta_{x}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{D}{h(T_{0})}\right).

Proof.

The proof essentially follows from the Tweedie’s formula. We first observe that

\displaystyle p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2h(t)}\left\|x-\alpha(t)x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2h(t)}\right)\exp\left(\frac{\alpha(t)x^{\top}x_{0}}{h(t)}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}(t)\left\|x_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2h(t)}\right).

Let
\phi(x)=\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2h(t)}\right)
and
T(x_{0})=\alpha(t)x_{0}/h(t)
. We can write

\displaystyle p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})=\phi(x)\exp\left(x^{\top}T(x_{0})\right)\exp\left(\psi(x_{0})\right).

Here,
\psi(\cdot)
is a function such that
p_{t|0}(\cdot|x_{0})
integrates to 1. The Bayes’ rule implies

\displaystyle p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x)=\frac{p_{t|0}(x|x_{0})p_{0}(x_{0})}{p_{t}(x)}=\exp\left(-\nu(x)+x^{\top}T(x_{0})\right)\left[p_{0}(x_{0})e^{\psi(x_{0})}\right],

where we have defined
\nu(x)=\log(p_{t}(x)/\phi(x))
. Since
p_{0|t}
is a probability density, we must have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}\int p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}\left\{e^{-\nu(x)}\int e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\nu(x)e^{-\nu(x)}\int e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-\nu(x)}\int T(x_{0})e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\nu(x)\int p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x){\rm d}x_{0}+\int T(x_{0})p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\nu(x)+\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})|X_{t}=x\right].

It follows that
\nabla\nu(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})|X_{t}=x\right]
. Similarly, we can differentiate one more time to have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}^{2}\int p_{0|t}(x_{0}|x){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{x}\bigg\{-\nabla\nu(x)e^{-\nu(x)}\int e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-\nu(x)}\int T(x_{0})e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla^{2}\nu(x)e^{-\nu(x)}+\nabla\nu(x)(\nabla\nu(x))^{\top}e^{-\nu(x)}\right)\int e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle\qquad-\nabla\nu(x)\left(e^{-\nu(x)}\int T(x_{0})e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\qquad-\nabla\nu(x)\left(e^{-\nu(x)}\int T(x_{0})e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\qquad+e^{-\nu(x)}\int T(x_{0})T(x_{0})^{\top}e^{x^{\top}T(x_{0})}p_{0}(x_{0}e^{\psi(x_{0})}){\rm d}x_{0}

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}\nu(x)-\nabla\nu(x)(\nabla\nu(x))^{\top}-2\nabla\nu(x)\left(\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})|X_{t}=x\right]\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})T(X_{0})^{\top}|X_{t}=x\right]

\displaystyle=-\nabla^{2}\nu(x)+\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})T(X_{0})^{\top}|X_{t}=x\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})|X_{t}=x\right]\left(\mathbb{E}\left[T(X_{0})|X_{t}=x\right]\right)^{\top}.

We can conclude that
\nabla^{2}\nu(x)=\mathrm{Cov}(T(X_{0})|X_{t}=x)
. Substitution the definition of
T(X_{0})
to have

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]=\frac{\alpha(t)}{h(t)}\mathrm{Cov}(X_{0}|X_{t}=x).

Since
\alpha(t)\leq 1
and
h(t)\geq h(T_{0})
, Assumption
3.2
implies

\displaystyle\beta_{x}\leq\left\|\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{0}|X_{t}=x\right]\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{D}{h(T_{0})}\right).

∎

Next, we move on to justify Assumption
3.7
. The next result shows that the input training dataset has a concentration property.

Lemma G.2
.

Let
\left\{(t_{j},X_{0,j},X_{t_{j}})\right\}_{j=1}^{N}
be sampled from Algorithm
1
. With probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle t_{j}\in[T_{0}+\Delta,T],\quad\left\|X_{t_{j}}\right\|_{2}\leq R,

where
\delta=\frac{N\Delta}{T-T_{0}}+\mathcal{O}\left(NR^{d-2}e^{-R^{2}/4}\right)
.

Proof.

Note that in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we have shown that for any
t

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}>R\right\}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(R^{d-2}e^{-R^{2}/4}\right).

It follows

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}+\Delta}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq R\right\}\right]{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}+\Delta}^{T}\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq R\right\}\right]\right){\rm d}t

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{\Delta}{T-T_{0}}-\mathcal{O}\left(R^{d-2}e^{-R^{2}/4}\right).

Set
\delta^{\prime}=\frac{\Delta}{T-T_{0}}+\mathcal{O}\left(R^{d-2}e^{-R^{2}/4}\right)
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T-T_{0}}\int_{T_{0}+\Delta}^{T}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|X_{t}\right\|_{2}\leq R\right){\rm d}t\geq 1-\delta^{\prime}.

To apply the union bound, set
\delta=N\delta^{\prime}
. Therefore, with probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle t_{j}\in[T_{0}+\Delta,T],\quad\left\|X_{t_{j}}\right\|_{2}\leq R.

∎

Finally, we provide a justification of Assumption
3.8
.
Recall that we denote
H
the Gram matrix of
K
and
H^{ii}=[H^{ii}]_{jk}
the Gram matrix of
\kappa
(independent of
i
). For the scalar-valued NTK
\kappa
, we refer the readers to
Nguyen et al. 2021
for a comprehensive analysis of
H^{ii}
. Our next lemma shows
H
and
H^{ii}
share the same smallest eigenvalue for any
i\in[d]
.

Lemma G.3
.

Let
H
and
H^{ii}
be the Gram matrices of matrix-valued NTK
K
and real-valued NTK
\kappa
respectively. Then,
\lambda_{\min}(H)=\lambda_{\min}(H^{ii})
.

Proof.

Let
v=(v_{1}^{\top},\dots,v_{N}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{dN}
, where
v_{j}=(v_{j}^{1},\dots,v_{j}^{d})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can write

\displaystyle v^{\top}Hv=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}v_{j}^{\top}H_{j\ell}v_{\ell}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\ell}^{N}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}v_{j}^{i}H_{j\ell}^{ik}v_{\ell}^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}(v^{i})^{\top}H^{ik}v^{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}(v^{i})^{\top}H^{ii}v^{i}.

We first assume
\lambda_{\min}(H)\geq\lambda_{0}
. Let
i\in[d]
be fixed. Consider
v
with
v^{k}=0
for
k\neq i
. The smallest eigenvalue of
H
implies

\displaystyle v^{\top}Hv=(v^{i})^{\top}H^{ii}v^{i}\geq\lambda_{0}(v^{i})^{\top}v^{i},

which follows
\lambda_{\min}(H^{ii})\geq\lambda_{0}
since
v^{i}
is arbitrary. Conversely, suppose that
\lambda_{\min}(H^{ii})\geq\lambda_{0}
. For any
v
, we must have

\displaystyle v^{\top}Hv\geq\lambda_{0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}(v^{i})^{\top}v^{i}=\lambda_{0}v^{\top}v

Since
v
is arbitrary, we can conclude that
\lambda_{\min}(H)\geq\lambda_{0}
. Therefore, we finish the proof.
∎
