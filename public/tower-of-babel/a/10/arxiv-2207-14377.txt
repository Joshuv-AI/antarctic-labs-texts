1.
Introduction and Main Results

1.1.
Background

Understanding the value distribution of Dirichlet characters is a central theme in analytic number theory. An old and famous conjecture of I.M. Vinogradov predicts that the least quadratic non-residue
n_{p}
modulo a prime
p
satisfies
n_{p}\ll_{\delta}p^{\delta}
as
p\rightarrow\infty
for any
\delta>0
.
This conjecture, relating to negative values of the Legendre symbol
\left(\tfrac{\cdot}{p}\right)
, may be generalized to other primitive Dirichlet characters. One can ask whether the least integer
n_{\chi}
for which a primitive character
\chi\pmod{q}
yields
\chi(n)\neq 0,1
satisfies
n_{\chi}\ll_{\delta}q^{\delta}
as
q\rightarrow\infty
for any
\delta>0
.

These problems may be recast in terms of cancellation in short character sums. For the Legendre symbol modulo
p
it is a folklore conjecture going beyond Vinogradov’s that

\left|\sum_{n\leq x}\left(\frac{n}{p}\right)\right|=o_{p\rightarrow\infty}(x)\text{ whenever $x>p^{\delta}$ for any $\delta>0$,}

and more generally for any primitive Dirichlet character
\chi\pmod{q}
we expect that

(1)

\left|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\right|=o_{q\rightarrow\infty}(x)\text{ whenever $x>q^{\delta}$ for any $\delta>0$.}

Currently, the best general result towards (
1
), due to Burgess
[
3
]
, allows for such cancellation (at least for
q
cube-free) whenever
\delta>1/4
, improved to
\delta\geq 1/4
for prime
q
by Hildebrand
[
17
]
. However, it is a notoriously difficult problem to extend the zero-free regions of Dirichlet
L
-functions for
individual characters
. It is therefore desirable to determine other sufficient criteria that a primitive character might satisfy that guarantees cancellation in its partial sums in a range going beyond Burgess’ theorem.

Both of the above conjectures are well-known to hold (in a much stronger form) under the assumption of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH), but should even hold assuming far less. Indeed, Granville and Soundararajan
[
14
, Cor. 1.2]
showed that (
1
) holds as long as
L(s,\chi)
has sufficiently few zeros in certain small rectangles near the line
\text{Re}(s)=1
, a condition that is easily implied for
typical
characters
\chi
by classical zero density estimates.

As has been elaborated upon in various works (see e.g.,
[
2
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
21
]
and
[
10
]
), there is also a relationship between such questions about short character sums and corresponding estimates for
maximal
character sums, in particular regarding improvements to the Pólya-Vinogradov inequality, which asserts that any non-principal character modulo
q
satisfies

(2)

M(\chi):=\max_{1\leq t\leq q}\left|\sum_{n\leq t}\chi(n)\right|\ll\sqrt{q}\log q.

Montgomery and Vaughan
[
22
]
showed that, assuming GRH, the factor
\log q
in (
2
) may be improved to
\log\log q
. This is sharp (up to the implicit constant) for quadratic characters according to a construction due to Paley
[
25
]
. A general,
unconditional
improvement to the Pólya-Vinogradov inequality has, however, resisted proof for over a century.

Characters of various fixed orders
d\geq 2
have been considered in this connection in a number of works, see e.g.
[
13
]
and
[
8
]
.
Building on a breakthrough by Granville-Soundararajan
[
13
]
, Goldmakher
[
7
]
showed that for characters with
fixed odd
order
d
the estimate (
2
) may be improved unconditionally
to

(3)

M(\chi)\ll\sqrt{q}(\log q)^{1-\delta(d)+o(1)},\text{ with }\delta(d)=1-\frac{d}{\pi}\sin(\pi/d)>0

(see
[
20
]
for more precise results). On the other hand, unconditional improvements to (
2
) for
fixed even
order characters seem to provide a logjam to generally improving (
2
). Once again, it would be valuable to determine other collections of primitive characters for which one may improve the Pólya-Vinogradov inequality.

In this paper, we will study the relationship between cancellation in short and maximal character sums and the size of the
order
of a character
\chi\pmod{q}
, i.e, the minimal positive integer
d
such that
\chi^{d}
is principal.

1.2.
Motivating Questions

In order to obtain improvements in estimates for short and maximal sums of a primitive character
\chi\pmod{q}
, we must rule out the heuristic possibility that

\chi(p)=1\text{ for all but very few }p\leq q^{\delta}.

Since
\chi
is multiplicative, this would further imply that

\chi(n)=1\text{ for many integers }n\leq q^{\delta}.

In order to preclude this possibility, therefore,
we would like to
quantify the frequency with which a character takes the value
1
at integers
n
up to some small threshold
x
. Note that this question is more refined than simply
trying to bound
n_{\chi}
.

In this paper, we shall study the value distribution of characters
\chi
of
large
order (in a sense to be made precise shortly). If
\chi
has modulus
q
then the order
d
of
\chi
divides
\phi(q)
, and most such divisors grow as a function of
q
. Thus, the collection of such characters is rather substantial. Moreover, as the elementary results in Section
2
below show, such characters do exhibit some variation in their values
\chi(n)
for small
n
, which might suggest that their short and maximal sums also exhibit cancellation.

The role of a character’s order in its value distribution has previously been considered in the work of K. K. Norton on small upper bounds for
n_{\chi}
(see
[
23
]
and especially
[
24
]
, which contains a substantial survey on the topic), and of Granville and Soundararajan
[
11
]
on upper bounds for
|L(1,\chi)|
. To our knowledge, however, questions surrounding especially the
paucity
of solutions to
\chi(n)=1
and
distribution
of values
\chi(n)\neq 1
for small
n
have not appeared previously in the literature, in particular when the order of the character is allowed to grow as a function of its modulus.

In this direction we pose the following three motivating questions:

Question 1
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1)
. If
\chi
is a primitive character modulo
q
with order
d=d(q)
growing with
q
, how few solutions
n\leq q^{\delta}
are there to
\chi(n)=1
, or more generally to
\chi(n)=\alpha
when
\alpha^{d}=1
?

Question 2
.

If
\chi
is a character as in the previous question, can it be shown that for any fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\Big|=o_{d\rightarrow\infty}(x)\text{ for all }x>q^{\delta}?

Question 3
.

For a character
\chi
as in the previous two questions, can it be shown that
M(\chi)=o_{d\rightarrow\infty}(\sqrt{q}\log q)
?

The rationale for these questions is the following: if, say,
\chi
is primitive modulo a prime
q
of order
d
then
\{\chi(n)\}_{n<q}
equidistributes among the
d
th order roots of unity (by orthogonality of Dirichlet characters). If
d
is large then one expects that the level sets

\{n\leq x:\chi(n)=\alpha\},\quad\alpha^{d}=1,

should become sparse i.e., of size
o(x)
, even when
x
is rather small relative to
q
. Naturally, we would like to understand how quickly this can occur (i.e. how small can
x
be for this to happen). The variation in the values of
\chi(n)
also suggests the possibility that the short and maximal sums of
\chi
might exhibit cancellation as well.

1.5.
Remarks on the results

Remark 1
.

Underlying the results above is the commonly-exploited strategy that while information about
individual
characters is usually difficult to ascertain, it is often possible to make progress on average over a
family
of characters. When
d
is a large prime, for instance, the characters
\{\chi^{\ell}\}_{1\leq\ell\leq d-1}
all have exact degree
d
, and this collection, though thin, is large enough for averaging techniques to be effective. Fortunately, since these powers are all generated by
\chi
we are able to use this average information to elucidate some properties of
\chi
, e.g., Theorem
1
.

Remark 2
.

The condition that
q
is prime in our main theorems is mainly a convenience that ensures that (
4
) holds, and hence
\chi\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(x_{0};d)
for an appropriate scale
x_{0}
. As such, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
can be applied to
\chi
. However, the bound (
4
) is only used in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, and could be removed at the expense of replacing the quantity
\rho(\tfrac{C_{2}}{\eta\delta})
by
\rho(\tfrac{q}{\phi(q)}\frac{C_{2}}{\eta\delta})
in (
5
).

As a result, we may equally well extend Corollary
1.3
to a collection of moduli
q
with uniformly bounded sums
\sum_{p|q}p^{-1}
.
Note in this connection that the trivial bound

\Big|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\Big|\leq|\{n\leq x:(n,q)=1\}|\ll x\prod_{p|q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right),

valid for any
x>q^{\delta}
and
\delta>0
fixed, shows that if
\sum_{p|q}p^{-1}
is
unbounded
as a function of
q
, in contrast, then we can trivially answer Question
2
in the affirmative.

Remark 3
.

Our requirement that
d
be squarefree in Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.3
is needed
to ensure that when
d
is large so are most of its prime factors. As such, the group
\mu_{d}
does not have “too many” small subgroups.
Morally, this prevents from occurring the situation that
\chi(n)
has order much smaller than
d
for many
n
, which would yield to much repetition in the sequence
(\chi(n))_{n}
. In place of the squarefreeness of
d
, it would be sufficient to assume that

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{k}||d\\
p>z\end{subarray}}p^{k},\text{ with }z\asymp\log(1/\eta),

is large enough in terms of
\eta,\delta
.

Remark 4
.

The savings obtained in Theorem

\left|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\right|\ll_{\varepsilon}x^{1/2+\varepsilon},\quad x>q^{\varepsilon}

\frac{1}{q-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\chi\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\right|\ll\frac{\sqrt{x}}{(\log\log\min\{x,q/x\})^{1/4}}.

We might expect by analogy that

at least in some range of

3.
Background and Proof Strategy

3.1.
A Pretentious Primer

The arguments used towards the proof of our main theorems are grounded in notions of pretentious number theory, as developed by Granville and Soundararajan. Here, we give a brief overview of those ideas from that subject that will be relevant in this paper.

Let
\mathbb{U}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq 1\}
. Given arithmetic functions
f,g:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}
and
x\geq 2
we define the
pretentious distance
between
f
and
g
(at scale
x
) as

\mathbb{D}(f,g;x):=\left(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{1-\text{Re}(f(p)\overline{g}(p))}{p}\right)^{1/2}.

Note that
\mathbb{D}(f,g;x)=\mathbb{D}(f\overline{g},1;x)
, and by Mertens’ theorem,
0\leq\mathbb{D}(f,g;x)^{2}\leq 2\log\log x
. This distance function also satisfies a triangle inequality: given
f,g,h:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}
we have

(9)

\mathbb{D}(f,h;x)\leq\mathbb{D}(f,g;x)+\mathbb{D}(g,h;x),

which implies the useful inequality (see
[
13
, Lem. 3.1]
)

(10)

\mathbb{D}(f_{1}f_{2},g_{1}g_{2};x)=\mathbb{D}(f_{1}\overline{g}_{1},f_{2}\overline{g}_{2};x)\leq\mathbb{D}(f_{1}\overline{g}_{1},1;x)+\mathbb{D}(f_{2}\overline{g}_{2},1;x)=\mathbb{D}(f_{1},g_{1};x)+\mathbb{D}(f_{2},g_{2};x).

If
f
and
g
are multiplicative functions for which
\mathbb{D}(f,g;x)^{2}=o(\log\log x)
then
f(p)\approx g(p)
for most
p
(in a suitable average sense), and we think of
f
and
g
as approximating one another. In the particular case that
\mathbb{D}(f,g;x)
is
bounded
as a function of
x
we say
that
f
is
g
-pretentious
(or, symmetrically, that
g
is
f
-pretentious
).

The pretentious distance can be used to express upper bounds for Césaro averages of bounded multiplicative functions. The Halász-Montgomery-Tenenbaum inequality
[
27
, Cor. III.4.12]
, a quantitative refinement of fundamental work of Halász, states that for a multiplicative function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}
and parameters
x\geq 3
and
T\geq 1
,

(11)

\frac{1}{x}\left|\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\right|\ll(M+1)e^{-M}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\log\log x}{\log x},

where
M:=\min_{|t|\leq T}\mathbb{D}(f,n^{it};x)^{2}
. Thus, if
f
is not
n^{it}
-pretentious for all
|t|\leq T
, and
T
is large enough, then the partial sums of
f
are small. This result will be used several times in the sequel.

\max\{|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|\}\geq P^{-}(d)
. Since
(r_{1}r_{2},d)>1
if and only if

from

with

\ll\sum_{1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d-1}\sigma_{j_{1}}\sigma_{j_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},r_{2}\neq 0\\
\max\{|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|\}\geq P^{-}(d)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(r_{1}r_{2})^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{P^{-}(d)}S_{f}(x)^{2}.

We next consider the contribution from those
r_{1},r_{2}\neq 0
with
(r_{1}r_{2},d)=1
.
Fix
1\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq d-1
for the moment, and suppose

(j_{1},d)=(j_{2},d)=:\lambda
. Since
\lambda<d
and
\lambda|d
we have

. Setting
J_{i}:=j_{i}/(j_{1},j_{2})
for

(J_{1},J_{2})=(J_{1}J_{2},d/\lambda)=1

Separating the contribution of

from the remainder, we find

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda|d\\
\lambda<d\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq J_{1},J_{2}\leq d/\lambda\\
(J_{1},J_{2})=1\\
(J_{1}J_{2},d/\lambda)=1\end{subarray}}\sigma_{\lambda J_{1}}\sigma_{\lambda J_{2}}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|<d/\lambda\\

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi^{4}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda|d\\
\lambda<d\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|<d/\lambda\\
(r_{1}r_{2},d)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{(-1)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}}{(r_{1}r_{2})^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq J_{1},J_{2}\leq d/\lambda\\
(J_{1},J_{2})=1\\
(J_{1}J_{2},d/\lambda)=1\\
J_{1}r_{1}\equiv J_{2}r_{2}\pmod{d/\lambda}\end{subarray}}\sigma_{\lambda J_{1}}\sigma_{\lambda J_{2}}+O\left(\frac{1}{P^{-}(d)}S_{f}(x)^{2}\right).

Note that, fixing
1\leq|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|,J_{1}<d/\lambda
, any possible choice of
J_{2}
is unique and fixed by the congruence in the inner sum.
By hypothesis
\sigma_{\lambda J_{2}}\ll\varepsilon S_{f}(x)
, and therefore the first expression in (

\ll\varepsilon S_{f}(x)\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|<d\\

\lambda<d\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq J\leq d/\lambda\\
(J,d/\lambda)=1\end{subarray}}\sigma_{\lambda J}\ll\varepsilon S_{f}(x)^{2}.

It follows, therefore, that

\Delta\ll\left(\varepsilon+\frac{1}{P^{-}(d)}\right)S_{f}(x)^{2}\ll\varepsilon S_{f}(x)^{2}.

By Chebyshev’s inequality, we therefore deduce that for all but
O(\varepsilon^{1/2}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)d)
choices of
1\leq\ell\leq d-1
,

\sum_{1\leq j\leq d-1}\|\frac{\ell j}{d}\|^{2}\sigma_{j}=\left(\frac{1}{12}+O(\log(1/\varepsilon)^{-1})\right)S_{f}(x).

Therefore, invoking (
20
), we deduce that for all but
O(\varepsilon^{1/2}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)d)
choices of
1\leq\ell\leq d-1

\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},1;x)^{2}\geq 8\cdot\left(\frac{1}{12}+O(\log(1/\varepsilon)^{-1})\right)S_{f}(x)=\left(\frac{2}{3}+O(\log(1/\varepsilon)^{-1})\right)S_{f}(x),

as claimed.
∎

Case 2: some

We next consider the case in which

\sigma_{j_{0}}

S_{f}(x)
. When

is large the following proposition will provide an alternative bound for the partial sums of

when

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
3\leq x\leq x_{0}
.
Let
c>0
satisfy
P^{-}(d)>1/c
, and assume that
|\mathcal{C}_{d}(\varepsilon)|\geq cd

and suppose that

Then for any
1\leq j,\ell\leq d-1
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
at least one of the following bounds hold:

\frac{1}{x}\left|\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)\right|\leq\varepsilon,

or else

\frac{1}{x}\left|\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)\right|\ll\|\ell j/d\|^{-1}\left(\lambda\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}+\log(e/\lambda)\left(\lambda^{1-2/\pi}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\right)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}}}+\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1-2/\pi+o(1)}}\right).

Proof.

We may assume that
x
is as large (and
\varepsilon
as small) as desired, otherwise at least one of the alternatives is trivial. We may also assume that
\sigma_{j}\geq 1
and
\lambda\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}<1/2
, since otherwise the second alternative is trivial.

Suppose the first alternative fails. Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
4.1
,

\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{it_{\ell}};x)^{2}\leq 2\log(1/\varepsilon)<\frac{1}{16m^{2}}\log\log x.

for
\varepsilon
small enough.

Seeking to apply
[
12
, Thm. 4]
below, we must introduce some notation. For each
1\leq\ell\leq d-1
define

F_{\ell}(s):=\prod_{p\leq x}\left(1+\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{f^{\ell}(p^{j})}{p^{js}}\right),\quad s\in\mathbb{C}.

Let
y_{\ell,0}\in[-2\log x,2\log x]
be chosen so that
|F_{\ell}(1+iy_{\ell,0})|=\max_{|y|\leq 2\log x}|F_{\ell}(1+iy)|
, and set

y_{\ell}:=\begin{cases}y_{\ell,0}&\text{ if }|y_{\ell,0}|<\tfrac{1}{2}\log x\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We will need an upper bound for
|y_{\ell}|
whenever
y_{\ell}\neq 0
, so assume for the moment that this is the case. By Mertens’ theorem, given any
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

\frac{|F_{\ell}(1+iy)|}{\log x}\asymp\frac{1}{\log x}\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\text{Re}(f^{\ell}(p)p^{-iy})}{p}\right)\asymp e^{-\mathbb{D}(f,n^{iy};x)^{2}}.

Thus, if
y_{\ell}\neq 0
then

\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{iy_{\ell}};x)^{2}=\min_{|y|\leq 2\log x}\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{iy};x)^{2}+O(1)\leq\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{it_{\ell}};x)^{2}+O(1)\leq 2\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1).

By (
10
) and the crude bound
|t_{\ell}-y_{\ell}|\leq 2\log x
we thus obtain

\mathbb{D}(1,n^{i(t_{\ell}-y_{\ell})};x)\leq\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{iy_{\ell}};x)+\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{it_{\ell}};x)\leq 2\sqrt{2\log(1/\varepsilon)}+O(1).

In light of (
18
) and the hypothesis
\log(1/\varepsilon)<\frac{1}{32}\log\log x
, we see that
|t_{\ell}-y_{\ell}|\leq 100
. Thus, (
19
) delivers

\log(1+|t_{\ell}-y_{\ell}|\log x)\leq 10\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1).

Using Proposition
4.1
, we therefore conclude that

(22)

|y_{\ell}|\leq|y_{\ell}-t_{\ell}|+|t_{\ell}|\ll\frac{\varepsilon^{-10}+\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}}{\log x}\ll\frac{\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}}{\log x},

a bound that we shall employ momentarily.

With this setup complete we may now proceed with the proof of the proposition. By
[
12
, Lem. 7.1]
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{iy_{\ell}}}{1+iy_{\ell}}\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}+O\left(\frac{e^{\mathbb{D}(f^{\ell},n^{iy_{\ell}};x)\sqrt{(2+o(1))\log\log x}}}{\log x}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{iy_{\ell}}}{1+iy_{\ell}}\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}+O\left((\log x)^{\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}-1+o(1)}\right).

Let now
S_{j}:=\{p\leq q:f(p)=e(j/d)\}
and define the completely additive function

\Omega_{S_{j}}(n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{k}||n\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}k=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mp=n\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}1,

whose mean-value over
n\leq x
is asymptotically

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{k}\leq x\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p^{k}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}}\right)=\sigma_{j}+O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}}\right).

By the Turán-Kubilius inequality
[
27
, Thm. III.3.1]
and complete multiplicativity, we deduce that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}\cdot\frac{\Omega_{S_{j}}(n)+(\sigma_{j}-\Omega_{S_{j}}(n))}{\sigma_{j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mp\leq x\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}f^{\ell}(p)p^{-iy_{\ell}}f^{\ell}(m)m^{-iy_{\ell}}+O\left(\frac{1}{x\sqrt{\sigma_{j}}}\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{|\Omega_{S_{j}}(n)-\sigma_{j}|}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{\ell}(p)p^{-iy_{\ell}}}{p}\frac{p}{x}\sum_{m\leq x/p}f^{\ell}(m)m^{-iy_{\ell}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}}}\right).

We split the sum over
p\leq x
into the segments
p\leq x^{\lambda}
and
x^{\lambda}<p\leq x
. The contribution from the second segment to the above is

\ll\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\sum_{x^{\lambda}<p\leq x}\frac{1}{p}\ll\frac{\log(e/\lambda)}{\sigma_{j}}.

Now, for each
p\leq x^{\lambda}
we apply the Lipschitz bound
[
12
, Thm. 4]
, which yields

\frac{p}{x}\sum_{n\leq x/p}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}=\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}+O\left(\lambda^{1-2/\pi}\log(e/\lambda)+\frac{1}{(\log x)^{1-2/\pi+o(1)}}\right).

We thus conclude that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x^{\lambda}\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{\ell}(p)p^{-iy_{\ell}}}{p}\right)\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)n^{-iy_{\ell}}

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}}}+\log(e/\lambda)\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}+\lambda^{1-2/\pi}\right)+(\log x)^{\tfrac{2}{\pi}-1+o(1)}\right).

For each
p\leq x^{\lambda}
, (
22
) yields

p^{-iy_{\ell}}=1+O(\lambda\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}),

and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x^{\lambda}\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{\ell}(p)p^{-iy_{\ell}}}{p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\
p\in S_{j}\end{subarray}}\frac{f^{\ell}(p)}{p}+O\left(\lambda\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}+\frac{\log(e/\lambda)}{\sigma_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=e(\ell j/d)+O\left(\lambda\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}+\frac{\log(e/\lambda)}{\sigma_{j}}\right).

Hence, appealing once again to
[
12
, Lem. 7.1]
, we find

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)=e(\ell j/d)\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leq x}f^{\ell}(n)+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma_{j}}}+\log(e/\lambda)\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{j}}+\lambda^{1-2/\pi}\right)+\lambda\varepsilon^{-32m^{2}}+(\log x)^{\tfrac{2}{\pi}-1+o(1)}\right).

Rearranging the above and using
|1-e(\ell j/d)|\gg\|\ell j/d\|
, the claim follows.
∎

.

Proof of Theorem

We now turn to the proof of Theorem

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq x\\

We will analyze the
prime

Our general argument will allow us to handle

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
c\in(0,1)
. If
\chi

then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq q\\

Proof.

We will estimate the product

(30)

\prod_{1\leq\ell\leq d}|L(1+1/\log q,\chi^{\ell})|

For the first, note that if
\chi^{\ell}
is a non-exceptional, non-principal character (see e.g.
[
10

for a precise definition) then by e.g.
[
10

|L(1+1/\log q,\chi^{\ell})|=\exp\left(\text{Re}\left(-\sum_{p\leq q}\log\Big(1-\frac{\chi^{\ell}(p)}{p^{1+1/\log q}}\Big)+O(1)\right)\right)=\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq q}\frac{\chi^{\ell}(p)}{p}+O(1)\right).

This estimate also holds when
\ell=d
using properties of the Riemann zeta function. This bound thus holds for all but at most one

, and if such an exception exists then the corresponding character must be real.

If
d
is even then let
\ell_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
be such that
\chi^{\ell_{0}}

Lemma 6.2
