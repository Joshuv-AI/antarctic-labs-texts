Natural Gradient Hybrid Variational Inference with Application to Deep Mixed Models

Abstract

Stochastic models with global parameters and latent variables are common, and for which variational inference (VI) is popular. However, existing methods are often either slow or inaccurate in high dimensions. We suggest a fast and accurate VI method for this case that employs a well-defined natural gradient variational optimization that targets the joint posterior of the global parameters and latent variables. It is a hybrid method, where at each step the global parameters are updated using the natural gradient and the latent variables are generated from their conditional posterior. A fast to compute expression for the Tikhonov damped Fisher information matrix is used, along with the re-parameterization trick, to provide a stable natural gradient. We apply the approach to deep mixed models, which are an emerging class of Bayesian neural networks with random output layer coefficients to allow for heterogeneity. A range of simulations show that using the natural gradient is substantially more efficient than using the ordinary gradient, and that the approach is faster and more accurate than two cutting-edge natural gradient VI methods. In a financial application we show that accounting for industry level heterogeneity using the deep mixed model improves the accuracy of asset pricing models.
MATLAB code to implement the method can be found at:

https://github.com/WeibenZhang07/NG-HVI
.

Keywords
: Asset Pricing; Bayesian Neural Networks; Natural Gradient Optimization; Random Coefficients; Re-parameterization trick; Variational Bayes.

Hybrid Variational Inference

2.1

Variational inference

We consider a stochastic model with data
y
and unknowns
\psi
.
Bayesian inference for
\psi
employs its posterior density
p(\text{\boldmath$\psi$}|\text{\boldmath$y$})\propto p(\text{\boldmath$y$}|\text{\boldmath$\psi$})p(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})\equiv g(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})
, where
p(\text{\boldmath$y$}|\text{\boldmath$\psi$})
is the likelihood and
p(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})
is the prior.
The posterior is often difficult to evaluate, and in
VI it is approximated using a density
q(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})\in{\cal Q}
, with
{\cal Q}
a
family of flexible but tractable densities. The density
q
is called the “variational approximation” (VA) and obtained by minimizing a distance metric between
p(\text{\boldmath$\psi$}|\text{\boldmath$y$})
and
q(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})
.
The Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) is the most popular choice, and it is easily shown that minimizing
the KLD corresponds to maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=E_{q}\left[\log g(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})-\log q(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})\right]

over
q\in{\cal Q}
, where the expectation above is with respect to
\text{\boldmath$\psi$}\sim q
. This optimization
problem is called “variational optimization” and the method used for its solution determines
the speed of the VI method; for overviews of VI see
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
,

Blei et al., 2017
, and
Zhang et al., 2019a
.

2.2

Variational inference for latent variable models

Stochastic models that have both unknown
parameters
\theta
and latent
variables
z
are popular.
Examples include mixed models
where
z
are random coefficients
(
Tran et al., 2020
)
, tobit models where
z
are uncensored data
(
Danaher et al., 2020
)
,
state space models where
z
are latent states
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
,
and topic models where
z
are topics
(
Blei et al., 2003
)
. There are a number
of ways to apply
VI to this case. The most popular strategy is to set
\text{\boldmath$\psi$}=(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\top})^{\top}
and approximate
the “augmented posterior”
p(\text{\boldmath$\psi$}|\text{\boldmath$y$})
; for recent examples, see
Hoffman and Blei, 2015
,
Loaiza-Maya and Smith, 2019

and
Tan, 2021
.
However, because
\mbox{dim}(\text{\boldmath$z$})
is often very large, approximating the augmented posterior can introduce
substantial cumulative error. One alternative is to integrate out
z
using
numerical or Monte Carlo methods, such as importance sampling
(
Gunawan et al., 2017
,
Tran et al., 2020
)
. However, this can prove slow or even computationally infeasible for large models. An alternative that is both more
accurate and scalable was suggested by
Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
, which we
briefly outline below.

2.3

Hybrid variational inference

For latent variable models consider the VA

\displaystyle q_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})=p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})q^{0}_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\,,

(1)

where
q^{0}_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
is the density of a fixed form VA with
parameters
\lambda
.
Then it is possible to
show that the ELBO for the augmented posterior of
\text{\boldmath$\psi$}=(\text{\boldmath$\theta$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\top})^{\top}
is equal to the ELBO of the marginal
posterior of
\theta
; that is,

{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\equiv E_{q_{\lambda}}\left[\log g(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})-\log q_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})\right]=E_{q^{0}_{\lambda}}\left[\log\left(p(\text{\boldmath$y$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$})p(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\right)-\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\right]\equiv{\cal L}^{0}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\,.

(2)

Here, both ELBO’s are denoted as functions of
\lambda
because this vector parameterizes both
q^{0}_{\lambda}
and

q_{\lambda}\in{\cal Q}
. The equality at (
2
) means that solving
the variational optimization for
\psi
with respect to
\lambda
also solves the variational optimization problem
for
\theta
with
z
integrated out
exactly
. We stress this point because it is the source
of the increased accuracy of this VI method.

Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
use a stochastic gradient ascent (SGA) algorithm
(
Bottou, 2010
)

to solve the variational
optimization. The key input to SGA is an unbiased estimate of the
ordinary gradient
\nabla_{\lambda}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
, and these
authors show
how the re-parameterization trick
(
Kingma and Welling, 2014
,
Rezende et al., 2014
)

combined with the VA at (
1
) gives an efficient
gradient estimate. The re-parameterization is of the model parameters

\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\sim q^{0}_{\lambda}

in terms of a random vector
\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}\sim f_{\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}}

that is invariant to

\lambda
, and
a deterministic function
h
. The latent variables
z
are not
re-parameterized.
If the joint density of
\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}=\left((\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0})^{\top},\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\top}\right)^{\top}
is denoted as

f_{\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}}(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$})=f_{\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}}(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$z$})=f_{\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}}(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0})p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}),\text{\boldmath$y$})\,,

then it is possible to show that
the gradient can be
written as an expectation with respect to
\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}\sim f_{\varepsilon}
as

\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})=E_{f_{\varepsilon}}\left[\frac{\partial h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{\top}}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}}\left(\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})-\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\lambda}^{0}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\right)\right]\,.

(3)

A key observation is that to evaluate (
3
) only
\nabla_{\theta}\log q_{\lambda}^{0}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
is required, rather
the higher dimensional
\nabla_{\text{\boldmath$\psi$}}\log q_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})
which would be necessary for other choices of the VA at (
1
).
An unbiased approximation to the expectation above is evaluated by plugging in a single draw from
f_{\varepsilon}
obtained by first drawing from
f_{\varepsilon^{0}}
, and then
from
p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})
with
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
.
For many stochastic models, generation from

p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})
can be done either exactly or approximately using MCMC or other methods.
This provides a framework where MCMC can be used within a well-defined SGA variational optimization, so that these authors
refer to the method as hybrid VI. In the next section we extend the approach
of these authors
to use the natural gradient to both increase the effectiveness and further reduce the computational burden of hybrid VI.

Natural Gradient Hybrid Variational Inference

3.1

Natural gradient ascent

Given a starting value
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(0)}
, SGA recursively updates the
variational parameters as

\bm{\lambda}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\lambda}^{(t)}+\bm{\rho}^{(t)}\circ\left.\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}\right|_{\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}=\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(t)}},\;t=0,1,\ldots

(4)

until reaching convergence. Here,
\bm{\rho}^{(t)}
are adaptive learning rates, ‘
\circ
’ is the Hadamard (element-wise) product,
and
\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}
is an unbiased
estimator of the gradient that is evaluated at
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}=\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{(t)}
. While convergence of SGA follows from the conditions in
Robbins and Monro, 1951
, the algorithm does not exploit the information
geometry of
q_{\lambda}(\bm{\psi})
that can increase the speed of convergence in variational optimization
(
Khan and Nielsen, 2018
)
. In contrast,
using
the natural gradient does so
(
Amari, 1998
,
Honkela et al., 2010
)
.
The main idea is that
\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}
is replaced in (
4
) by the unbiased estimate
3
3

3

The notation

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})
is also often
used for the exact natural gradient, although here we do not employ
additional notation to distinguish between it and its unbiased approximation.

of the natural gradient

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})\equiv F(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})},

(5)

where the Fisher information matrix (FIM)

F(\bm{\lambda})=E_{q_{\lambda}}\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\log q_{\lambda}(\bm{\psi})\nabla_{\lambda}\log q_{\lambda}(\bm{\psi})^{\top}\right]=-E_{q_{\lambda}}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\log q_{\lambda}(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})}{\partial\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}\partial\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{\top}}\right]\,,

points
\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}
towards the steepest direction of the objective function. This approach, known as stochastic natural gradient ascent (SNGA), typically requires far fewer iterations to reach convergence than SGA; see
Martens, 2020
for a recent overview of natural gradient optimization.

The main requirement of NGA is that a tractable expression for
F(\bm{\lambda})
is available. Its
derivation for the VA at (
1
) may appear challenging, but Theorem
1
shows that
F(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})
for this
VA is tractable whenever the FIM for
q^{0}_{\lambda}
is also.

(

and denote the Fisher information matrix of the marginal approximation
q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})
as
F^{0}(\bm{\lambda})=E_{q_{\lambda}^{0}}\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\log q_{\lambda}^{0}(\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\lambda}\log q_{\lambda}^{0}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\right]
. Then it holds that

F(\bm{\lambda})=F^{0}(\bm{\lambda})

Corollary 1.1

(
Natural gradient for hybrid VI)
.

If
\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}
is an unbiased estimate
of the gradient of the evidence lower bound, then an unbiased estimate
of the natural gradient is given by

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})=F^{0}(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}\,.

(6)

Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.1
have three important implications
for the use of SNGA to
solve the variational optimization problem with
the VA at (
1
). First, the natural gradient can be constructed for a wide range of latent variable models because it does
not require evaluation of the density

p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})
or its derivative, but only the ability
to generate from it. Second,
evaluation of the natural gradient is unaffected
by the dimension of
z
.
In contrast,
other VI methods for latent variables can scale poorly; for example, the
computational complexity of the FIM increases with the dimension of
\bm{z}
for the VA in
Tan, 2022
.
A third implication is that in Corollary
1.1
the
computation of the FIM and
\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}
can
be done separately.
This allows variance reduction techniques when estimating the latter,
such as the
use of control variates
(
Paisley et al., 2012
,
Ranganath et al., 2014
,
Tran et al., 2017
)
or
the re-parametrization trick as adopted here.

The natural gradient can provide an unreliable update in regions where the ELBO function is flat, as the FIM becomes singular in these regions. As discussed in
Lin et al., 2021
, this is in fact the case for Gaussian variational approximations with factor covariance structures, such as that of
Ong et al., 2018a
considered in this paper. Damping of the FIM has been proposed as an approach to tackle this type of problems inside variational inference
(
Zhang et al., 2018
)
. Here, we follow suit and employ the damping of the form

\widetilde{F}^{0}(\bm{\lambda})=F^{0}(\bm{\lambda})+\delta\text{diag}\left(F^{0}(\bm{\lambda})\right)\,,

(7)

where
\diag(F^{0})
is a diagonal matrix comprising the leading diagonal elements of
F^{0}
, and
\delta>0
is a damping factor.
Martens and Sutskever, 2012
suggest that unlike damping that uses the identity matrix, this type of damping takes into consideration the scale in which the variational parameters lie and would preserve the self-rescaling property of the natural gradient update. We then construct an update by using the normalised damped natural gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})/||\widetilde{\nabla}_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})||

the combination of a normalised natural gradient and an adaptive learning rate method leads to a stable updating algorithm. Algorithm

to add momentum in the evaluation of the natural gradient.

3.2

Fixed form approximation

For the marginal VA of
\theta
, we use Gaussian VAs
q_{\lambda}^{0}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})=\phi_{m}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$},\Sigma)
with a factor model
covariance matrix, also called “low rank plus diagonal”, as
suggested by
Miller et al., 2017
,
Ong et al., 2018a

for the covariance matrix and
Mishkin et al., 2018
for the precision matrix.
If
m=\mbox{dim}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
, then

\Sigma=BB^{\top}+D^{2}
, with
D
a diagonal matrix and

B
an
(m\times p)
matrix where
p<<m
, so that
the number of
variational parameters scales linearly with
m
.
We set the upper triangular elements of
B
to zero, while leaving the leading diagonal elements unconstrained. Although the factor decomposition can be made unique by restricting the diagonal elements of
B
and
D
, we do not do so because the unrestricted parameterization can expedite the optimization process as noted by
Ong et al., 2018a
and others when using ordinary gradients.
The variational parameters are
\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}=(\text{\boldmath$\mu$}^{\top},\mbox{vech}(B)^{\top},\text{\boldmath$d$}^{\top})^{\top}
,
where “vech” is the half-vectorization operator applied to a rectangular matrix, and

d
is a vector containing the non-zero entries of
D
.

An advantage
of this factorization is that it has a convenient generative representation
given by

\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=\text{\boldmath$\mu$}+B\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0}+\text{\boldmath$d$}\circ\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0}\,,

that defines the transformation
h
with
\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}=((\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0})^{\top},(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})^{\top})^{\top}\sim N(\bm{0},I_{m+p})
.
A second advantage is that the derivatives of
q^{0}_{\lambda}

required to evaluate the re-parameterized gradient at (
3
)
are given in closed form in
Ong et al., 2018a

and are fast to compute. A third advantage is that the damped natural
gradient
\widetilde{F}^{0}(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}
can also be computed efficiently
as follows.

Ong et al., 2018b

show that the FIM is sparse with

F^{0}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}F_{11}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&\bm{0}&\bm{0}\\
\bm{0}&F_{22}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&F_{32}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{\top}\\
\bm{0}&F_{32}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&F_{33}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\\
\end{array}\right]\,,

(8)

where the block matrices in
F^{0}
follow the partition of
\lambda
, and
\bm{0}
denotes a conformable matrix of zeros.
These authors derive closed form expressions for
F_{11},F_{22},F_{32}

and
F_{33}
. The damped FIM is equal to (
8
)
but with the leading diagonal blocks replaced by

\widetilde{F}_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=F_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})+\delta\mbox{diag}\left(F_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\right)
for
j=1,2,3
.
The first elements of the damped natural gradient can be computed as

\widetilde{F}_{11}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{-1}\widehat{\nabla_{\mu}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}
, using the closed form expression for
\widetilde{F}_{11}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{-1}
given in Appendix
B
.
The remaining
elements

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{F}_{22}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&F_{32}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{\top}\\
F_{23}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&\widetilde{F}_{33}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\end{array}\right]^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\nabla_{\rm{vech}(B)}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}\\
\widehat{\nabla_{d}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}\end{array}\right)\,,

(9)

are obtained using a conjugate gradient solver.
Full details on the efficient calculation of the damped natural gradient
are given in Part B of the Web Appendix.

To speed computation of the (un-damped) natural gradient,
Tran et al., 2020
approximate the FIM as
block diagonal, in which case there is no need to solve (
9
) because
\widetilde{F}_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{-1}
for
j=2,3
can also be computed in closed form.
However, this loses information about the curvature of
q_{\lambda}
. In addition, these authors set
p=1
to speed computations,
although a higher number of factors is sometimes necessary to improve accuracy of the VA. In contrast, we do not adopt these simplifications and found that
employing the damped FIM stabilizes the natural gradient update step, allowing us to apply our algorithm
in reasonable time using natural gradients with dimension up to 13,895; see Table
7
for a summary of the size and characteristics of our examples.
Finally, we note that other VAs may also be considered for
q^{0}
, although restricting the choice to approximations where

F^{0}
is tractable (as with the choice here) is necessary for a fast NG-HVI method in practice.

Hybrid Variational Inference for Deep Mixed Models

4.1

Deep mixed models

Bayesian neural networks are probabilistic neural networks estimated using
Bayesian inference; see
Jospin et al., 2022
for an
introduction. Following
Tran et al., 2020
,
we consider a Bayesian neural network with random output layer coefficients to allow for group-level heterogeneity, which these authors call
a deep mixed model (DMM).

For a feed forward
network with
L
layers, the DMM we consider draws observation

i
, belonging to group
k
, from

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle{\cal D}\left(f_{L+1}\left((\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(L)}_{i}\right)\right)\;\mbox{ for }i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(l)}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{l}\left(W_{l}\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(l-1)}_{i}\right)\;\mbox{ for }l=1,\ldots,L\,,

(11)

where
f_{l}
is an activation function,
\text{\boldmath$h$}_{i}^{(l)}
for
l\geq 1
is
the vector of
l^{th}
layer values for observation
i
with the first element a
constant off-set,

\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(0)}_{i}=\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
is the input vector, and
W_{l}
is the weight matrix at layer
l
.
The output value
y_{i}
has probability
distribution
{\cal D}
with parameters that are a function of

h^{(L+1)}_{i}=f_{L+1}\left((\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k})^{\top}\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(L)}_{i}\right)
, where
we assume a linear activation function
f_{L+1}
in our examples.
The output layer coefficients are
decomposed into a fixed effect term
\beta
and a random effects term
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}\sim N(\bm{0},\Omega_{\alpha})
that varies
over group
k=1,\ldots,K
.
The random effects allow translation of the nodes from layer
L
into the output layer to be
heterogeneous over the
K
groups.
The introduction of random coefficients into the output layer
of other deep neural networks is similar.

4.2

NG-HVI for DMM

The unknowns in the DMM model at (
11
) include
weights
\text{\boldmath$w$}=(\mbox{vec}(W_{1})^{\top},\ldots,\mbox{vec}(W_{L})^{\top})^{\top}
,
fixed effects
\beta
, random effects values

\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{1},\ldots,\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{K}
, covariance matrix
\Omega_{\alpha}

and any other parameters used to specify the distribution
{\cal D}
.
Bayesian estimation involves selecting priors for all unknowns
and then evaluating their joint posterior distribution.
The selection
of appropriate priors for weights in Bayesian neural networks is an area of current research; see
Tran et al., 2022
for a discussion. Here, we adopt the simple
priors
\text{\boldmath$w$}\sim N(\bm{0},\sigma_{w}^{2}I)
and
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}\sim N(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}_{\beta}I)
with
\sigma^{2}_{w}=\sigma^{2}_{\beta}=100
. If
\mbox{dim}(\text{\boldmath$h$}_{i}^{(l)})=m_{l}
, then
following
Tan, 2022
a prior

\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1}\sim\mbox{Wishart}(0.01I,\nu)
is adopted for the
precision matrix, so that the prior for

\Omega_{\alpha}
is an inverse Wishart, with
\nu=m_{L}+1
. We stress that other priors for
\text{\boldmath$w$},\text{\boldmath$\beta$},\Omega_{\alpha}
can also be
adopted just as readily when using our black box VI method.

Because
K
is often large in practice, computation
of the posterior can be challenging. However,
setting
\text{\boldmath$z$}=(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{K}^{\top})^{\top}
and
\theta
to
a vector comprising all the other unknowns,
the proposed NG-HVI method is well-suited to train such DMMs. To implement step (c) of Algorithm
1
, evaluation of the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})
is required.
The gradient with respect to
w
can be evaluated using back-propagation
(
Rumelhart et al., 1986
)
.
We follow
Tan, 2021
and re-parameterize

\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1}=LL^{\top}
using its Cholesky factor
L=\{l_{ij}\}
and
set
l
to a vector of the elements
\{l_{ij};j<i\}\cup\{\log(l_{ii});i=1,\ldots,m_{L}\}
. These elements are
on the real line, and both the prior for
l
and the gradient

\nabla_{l}\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})
are available in closed form; see
Appendix
C
.
The gradients with
respect to the other elements of
\theta
depend on the choice for
{\cal D}
, but are usually available
either in closed
form or using numerical differentiation.
Another requirement
to implement the algorithm is for generation from
p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})
at step (b) to be feasible, which is also contingent on choice
{\cal D}
.

The implementation of NG-HVI is outlined below for two DMMs with Gaussian and
Bernoulli distributions
{\cal D}
for the output variable. The performance of the method
is assessed using data simulated from
two examples, where the first DGP matches the DMM fit, while the second DGP does not match the DMM fit (so that there is model mis-specification).
SG-HVI and two existing natural gradient VI methods act as benchmarks.
Performance is evaluated using the posterior predictive distribution,
computed as outlined in Part D of the Web Appendix.

DMM for Financial Asset Pricing

There is growing interest in the use of machine learning methods
for asset pricing in the financial literature

(
Fang and Taylor, 2021
,
Feng et al., 2022
)
. Compared to traditional linear models,
there is evidence that neural networks can increase the predictive accuracy for asset returns
(
Gu et al., 2020
)
, as well as there being heterogeneity across industries
(
Diallo et al., 2019
,
Gu et al., 2020
)
.

To explore both features, we
estimate Gaussian DMMs with the output layer given at (
12
),
and either the three factors of
Fama and French, 1993
(hereafter “FF3”), or the five factors of
Fama and French, 2015
(hereafter “FF5”), as inputs.
The three factors in FF3 are
the excess market return (ERM), a firm size factor (SMB) and a value premium (HML).
The five factors in FF5 extends these to include a firm profitability factor (RMW) and an firm investment strategy factor (CMA). The random effect groups are defined by the 4-digit Security Industry Code (SIC),
so that the DMM captures industry variation in the factor risk premia.
Two architectures are used. The first has one hidden layer of 8 neurons,
and the second has 3 hidden layers of 32, 16 and 8 neurons as used by
Gu et al., 2020
. In this example we use NG-HVI with a factor covariance structure for
q^{0}
with
p=3
for a DMM with one hidden layer, and
p=5
for a DMM with three hidden layers.
We do not identify an optimal architecture, and are unaware of other studies that do so for asset pricing models.

Discussion

While natural gradient stochastic optimization methods have strong potential in
variational inference, their use is limited due to the computational
complexity of implementation for choices of VAs outside the exponential family.
In this paper, we propose a natural gradient method (NG-HVI) that employs the VA at (
1
), which is outside the exponential family.
Doing so has three advantages which we list here.
First, Theorem
1
and Corollary
1.1
show that the computational complexity of evaluating the natural gradient grows only with

\mbox{dim}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
, not the dimension of the target
density
p(\text{\boldmath$\psi$}|\text{\boldmath$y$})
. When
\mbox{dim}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})<<\mbox{dim}(\text{\boldmath$\psi$})
(which is the case with most
latent variable models) this reduces the computational complexity of evaluating
the natural gradient greatly. Second, the proposed VA may be far from Gaussian
because the conditional posterior

p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})
may be also. This density, nor
its derivatives, are not required to implement
Algorithm
1
. Instead, it is only necessary to draw from this conditional posterior,
which can be achieved using a wide array of existing methods.
Last, that approximation at (
1
) is necessarily more
accurate than any other VA for
\psi
that shares the same marginal
q^{0}(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
.
In practice, the importance of this feature grows with the dimension of
z
, because
the cumulative error of even well-calibrated fixed form VAs for
z
grows as well.
We demonstrate these advantages in our empirical work. The results suggest
that learning the
VA using SNGA proves more efficient and stable than using SGA as originally
suggested by
Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
. The proposed
method also out-performed the two natural gradient benchmark methods for
our examples. While these examples are all latent variable models, NG-HVI can
also be used with
other stochastic models where
\psi
is partitioned in other ways, as long as generation of
z

from its conditional posterior is
viable. For example,
z
may contain discrete-valued
parameters, which is a case that is difficult to deal with using standard VI methods
(
Tran et al., 2019
,
Ji et al., 2021
)
.

a summary of these examples, along with details on the application of NG-HVI for their estimation. We focus on DMMs
that employ
random coefficients on the output layer of a probabilistic Bayesian
neural network because they have strong applied potential.
They allow for heterogeneity, which is important in many studies, and capturing it
using random coefficients in other stochastic models is a well-established approach
(
Gelman and Hill, 2006
)
.
However, DMMs are hard to train because
the posterior is complex and
the dimension of
z
is often high. Our empirical work demonstrates
that NG-HVI is an effective approach for doing so, and that it improves substantially on SG-HVI.
One alternative is to use importance sampling to integrate out the random
coefficients, as suggested in the
original study of
Tran et al., 2020
. However, this is more computationally
demanding than NG-HVI, and the method scales poorly with the number of
output layer nodes. The potential of DMMs is demonstrated in
the financial study, where
the combination of a feed forward neural network and industry-based
heterogeneity produces a significant
increase in predictive accuracy using the Fama and French factors as inputs.

We finish by noting two directions for further work. First, while we employ
the Gaussian factor covariance VA for
q^{0}
,
other VAs for which fast natural gradient updates can be computed
may also be used. This includes a Gaussian VA
with fast Cholesky updates as suggested by
Tan, 2022
,
the
implicit copula VAs of
Han et al., 2016
,
Smith et al., 2020
, the
mixture VAs of
Lin et al., 2019
or the tractable
fixed forms
suggested by
Lin et al., 2021
.
Second, the promising results of the financial study encourage the further exploration of the
use of DMMs in asset pricing.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
1

Observe that because
q_{\lambda}(\bm{\psi})=q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})p(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})
, where
p(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})
is not a function of
\lambda
, then

\displaystyle F(\bm{\lambda})

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\lambda}}\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\log q_{\lambda}(\bm{\psi})\nabla_{\lambda}\log q_{\lambda}(\bm{\psi})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\lambda}}\left[\left(\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})+\nabla_{\lambda}\log p(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})\right)\left(\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})+\nabla_{\lambda}\log p(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})\right)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\lambda}}\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\lambda}^{0}}\left[E_{p(\bm{z}|\bm{\theta},\bm{y})}\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=E_{q_{\lambda}^{0}}\left[\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})\nabla_{\lambda}\log q^{0}_{\lambda}(\bm{\theta})^{\top}\right]=F^{0}(\bm{\lambda})

which proves the result.

Part A: Notational conventions and matrix differentiation rules used

We outline the notational conventions that we adopt in computing
derivatives throughout the paper, which are the same as adopted in
Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
. For a
d
-dimensional vector valued function
g(\bm{x})
of an
n
-dimensional
argument
\bm{x}
,
\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bm{x}}
is the
d\times n
matrix with element
(i,j)

\frac{\partial g_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}
. This means for a scalar
g(\bm{x})
,
\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bm{x}}
is
a row vector. When discussing the SGA algorithm we also sometimes write
\nabla_{x}g(\bm{x})=\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bm{x}}^{\top}
, which is a column vector.
When the function
g(\bm{x})
or the argument
\bm{x}
are matrix valued, then
\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bm{x}}
is taken to
mean
\frac{\partial\text{vec}(g(\bm{x}))}{\partial\text{vec}(\bm{x})}
, where
\text{vec}(A)
denotes the vectorization of a matrix
A
obtained by stacking its columns one
underneath another. If
g(x)
and
h(x)
are matrix valued functions, say
g(x)
takes values which are
d\times r
and
h(x)
takes values which are
r\times n
,
then a matrix valued product rule is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(x)h(x)}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=(h(x)^{\top}\otimes I_{d})\frac{\partial g(x)}{\partial x}+(I_{n}\otimes g(x))\frac{\partial h(x)}{\partial x}

where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product and
I_{a}
denotes the
a\times a
identity matrix for a positive integer
a
.

Some other useful results used repeatedly throughout the derivations below are

\text{vec}(ABC)=(C^{\top}\otimes A)\text{vec}(B),

for conformable matrices
A
,
B
and
C

the derivative

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A^{-1}}{\partial A}

\displaystyle=-(A^{-\top}\otimes A^{-1}).

We also write
K_{m,n}
for the commutation matrix (see, for example, Magnus and Neudecker, 1999).

Last, for scalar function
g(x)
of scalar-valued argument
x
, we
sometimes write
g^{\prime}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}g(x)
and
g^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}g(x)
for the first and second derivatives with respect
to
x
whenever it appears clearer to do so.

Part B: Additional details on the efficient evaluation of the damped natural gradient

Here we provide further details on the efficient evaluation of
the damped natural gradient

\widetilde{\nabla}_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})\equiv\widetilde{F}^{0}(\bm{\lambda})^{-1}\widehat{\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal{L}(\bm{\lambda})}\,,

for the VA at (1) with the Gaussian VA with factor covariance matrix for
q^{0}_{\lambda}
given in Section 3.4.

First, restating the closed form expressions given in
Ong et al., 2018a
for the re-parameterization gradient at (7) gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{f_{\varepsilon}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})+(BB^{\top}+D^{2})^{-1}(B\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0}+\text{\boldmath$d$}\circ\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})\right]

\displaystyle\nabla_{\rm{vech}(B)}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{f_{\varepsilon}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0})^{\top}+(BB^{\top}+D^{2})^{-1}(B\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0}+\text{\boldmath$d$}\circ\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\nabla_{d}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{f_{\varepsilon}}\left[\mbox{diag}\left(\nabla_{\theta}\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})^{\top}+(BB^{\top}+D^{2})^{-1}(B\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0}+\text{\boldmath$d$}\circ\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})^{\top}\right)\right]\,,

where
{\rm diag}(A)
is the vector of diagonal elements of the square matrix
A
.
The expectations are evaluated at a single draw from the density

\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}=\left((\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}_{1})^{\top},(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}_{2})^{\top},\text{\boldmath$z$}^{\top}\right)^{\top}\sim f_{\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}}(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$z$})=f_{\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}}(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0})p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}),\text{\boldmath$y$})\,,

with
\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0}=((\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0})^{\top},(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0})^{\top})^{\top}
,
the transformation
h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=\text{\boldmath$\mu$}+B\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{1}^{0}+\text{\boldmath$d$}\circ\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}_{2}^{0}
and
f_{\varepsilon_{0}}
is the density of a
N(\bm{0},I_{m+p})
distribution.
Generating from
p(\text{\boldmath$z$}|h(\text{\boldmath$\varepsilon$}^{0},\text{\boldmath$\lambda$}),\text{\boldmath$y$})
is undertaken
either directly using a Monte Carlo method, or approximately using a few steps
of an MCMC scheme initialized at the draw of
z
obtained at the previous step of the NGA algorithm.
In the computations above, the
m\times m
matrix
(BB^{\top}+D^{2})^{-1}
is not evaluated or stored directly, but is replaced by
the Woodbury formula
(BB^{\top}+D^{2})^{-1}=D^{-2}-D^{-2}B(I+B^{\top}D^{-2}B)^{-1}B^{\top}D^{-2}
in the equations. Multiplying through enables efficient evaluation.

Second, to evaluate the damped FIM, we first note that for the Gaussian factor
covariance VA for
q^{0}_{\lambda}
, the FIM
F^{0}
is given by the patterned matrix at (8).
Ong et al., 2018b
give the following closed form expressions for the block
matrices:

\displaystyle F_{11}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(BB^{\top}+D^{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle F_{22}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(B^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}B\otimes\Sigma^{-1})

\displaystyle F_{33}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(D\Sigma^{-1}\circ\Sigma^{-1}D)

\displaystyle F_{32}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(B^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}D\otimes\Sigma^{-1})E_{m}^{\top}

where
E_{m}
is an
m\times m^{2}
permutation matrix such that for an
m\times m
matrix
A
,
E_{m}\mbox{vec}(A)=\mbox{diag}(A)
.

The damped FIM is equal to (8), but with the leading diagonal blocks replaced by

\widetilde{F}_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})=F_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})+\delta\mbox{diag}\left(F_{jj}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\right)
for
j=1,2,3
. The
elements

\widetilde{F}_{11}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{-1}\widehat{\nabla_{\mu}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}
are computed using the analytical
expression for
\widetilde{F}_{11}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{-1}
given in Appendix B
of the paper.
The remaining elements
of the damped natural gradient are
obtained by using a pre-conditioned conjugate gradient solver (such as “
pcg
” in MATLAB) to solve the system

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widetilde{F}_{22}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&F_{32}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})^{\top}\\
F_{23}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})&\widetilde{F}_{33}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})\end{array}\right]^{-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\widehat{\nabla_{\rm{vech}(B)}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}\\
\widehat{\nabla_{d}{\cal L}(\text{\boldmath$\lambda$})}\end{array}\right)\,.

C.1: Linear random effects model

In the random effects model we set the fixed effect vector
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}=(0.8292,-1.3250,0.9909,1.6823,-1.7564,0.0580)
, and the
covariate vector
\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
consists of an intercept and
five values generated from a
correlated Gaussian. The error variance
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}=1
throughout,
and the random effect variance
\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}
is varied as outlined
in Section 3.

To generate the latent variables in the HVI methods,
it is straightforward to show that
p(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}p(\alpha_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k})
,
where
\alpha_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}\sim N(\mu_{\alpha_{k}},\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{k}})
with

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{k}}\frac{\text{\boldmath$\iota$}_{k}^{\top}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}-X_{k}\text{\boldmath$\beta$})}{\sigma^{2}_{e}}

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{\alpha}}+\frac{\text{\boldmath$\iota$}_{k}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$\iota$}_{k}}{\sigma^{2}_{e}}\right)^{-1}

Here
X_{k}
and
\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}
are the covariate matrix and response variable corresponding to group
k
, respectively. The vector

C.2: Probit random effects model

We outline an additional example that extends
the linear random effects model in Section 3.4 (Example 1)
to a probit model with random effect. This further illustrates
the increase in optimization efficiency using SNGA over SGA.
Consider a probit model with a scalar random effect:

\displaystyle y_{i}^{*}

\displaystyle=\text{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i}+\alpha_{k}+\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}(y_{i}^{*}>0)\,,

where the indicator function

\mathds{1}(X)=1
if
X
is true, and zero otherwise, and

\epsilon_{i}\sim N(0,1)
. Following much of the random effects literature, index
k
denotes the group membership of observation
i
.

We generate
n=2000
observations from a DGP
that has
K=100
groups and
3
covariates as follows:

(i)

Generate
\text{\boldmath$x$}^{0}_{i}\sim N_{3}(\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{x},\Sigma_{x})
with
\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{x}=\mathbf{0}_{3},\Sigma_{x}=\L\L^{\prime}+diag(\text{\boldmath$d$})
, and set
\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i}=(1,\text{\boldmath$x$}^{0,\top}_{i})^{\top}
.

(ii)

Generate
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}\sim N(\mathbf{0}_{4},10I_{4})
.

(iii)

Generate
\alpha_{k}\sim N(0,1)
for
k=1,\dots,100
.

(iv)

Generate
y_{i}^{*}\sim N(\text{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top}\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i}+\alpha_{k},1)
and set
y_{i}=\mathds{1}(y_{i}^{*}>0)
for
i=1,\ldots,2000
.

In this example
y_{i}^{*}
and
\alpha_{k}
are both latent variables, so for implementing the HVI methods we set

\text{\boldmath$z$}=(\text{\boldmath$y$}^{*\top},\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}^{\top})^{\top}
where
is
\text{\boldmath$y$}^{*}=(y_{1}^{*},y_{2}^{*},\dots,y_{n}^{*})^{\top},\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{K})^{\top}
. To generate from the distribution
\text{\boldmath$z$}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$}
we use MCMC to generate alternately from
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}|\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\star},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}
and then from
\text{\boldmath$y$}^{\star}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$},\text{\boldmath$y$}
.
Generation from each is very fast because their densities are products of
independent univariate Gaussian densities. We find that just five sweeps of this
MCMC scheme, initialized at the values of
z
from the last step of
the stochastic optimization algorithm, provides strong results.
The approach of using MCMC within SGA was explored
by
Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
for more complex models
and found to work well.

In this simulation, the values
\text{\boldmath$y$}^{*}
are known. Therefore, to monitor convergence, the
root mean squared error

\displaystyle RMSE=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{2000}(\hat{y}_{i}^{*}-y_{i}^{*})^{2}}{2000}}

is computed,
where
\hat{y}_{i}^{*}
is the variational mean of the latent variable
y_{i}^{*}
.

By this measure, NG-HVI convergences in less than

Part D: Additional Details for the Examples in Section 4

D.1: Parameterization, priors and gradients

This section provides further details on the parameterization and choice of priors, then provides the required gradients and derivatives to implement
the HVI method.

The model parameters of the Gaussian DMM in Section 4 are
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\text{\boldmath$w$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top},\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon},\text{\boldmath$l$}^{\top})^{\top}
. For variational inference, we need to transform
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
to the real line. Thus we introduce
\theta_{\epsilon}=\log(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})
and the updated model parameters are
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\text{\boldmath$w$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top},\theta_{\epsilon},\text{\boldmath$l$}^{\top})^{\top}
. Then we have
g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})p(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\bigr)p(\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
with

\displaystyle p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle=\phi(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k};H_{k}(\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}),\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle P(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle=\phi(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k};\boldmath{0},\Omega_{\alpha})

\displaystyle p(\text{\boldmath$w$})

\displaystyle=\phi(\text{\boldmath$w$};\boldmath{0},\sigma^{2}_{w}I)

\displaystyle p(\text{\boldmath$\beta$})

\displaystyle=\phi(\text{\boldmath$\beta$};\boldmath{0},\sigma^{2}_{\beta}I)

\displaystyle p(\theta_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=\frac{b^{a}}{\Gamma(a)}\exp(\theta_{\epsilon})^{-a}\exp\bigl(-b\,\exp(-\theta_{\epsilon})\bigr)

\displaystyle p(\text{\boldmath$l$})

\displaystyle=2^{m_{L}}p(\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1})\prod_{i=1}^{m_{L}}l_{ii}^{(m_{L}-i+2)}

Here,
p(\theta_{\epsilon})
was constructed by using the prior
p(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})=p_{IG}(\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon};a,b)
and deriving the corresponding prior on
\theta_{\epsilon}
. The density of a
N(\text{\boldmath$\mu$},\Sigma)
distribution is denoted as
\phi(\text{\boldmath$x$};\text{\boldmath$\mu$},\Sigma)
, and
p(\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1})
has is the density
of a Wishart distribution with degrees of freedom
\nu
and scale matrix
0.01I
.
For this example we set
\sigma^{2}_{w}=100
and
\sigma^{2}_{\beta}=100
.
H_{k}
is the
n_{k}\times(m_{L}+1)
matrix with each row containing
\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(L)}_{i}
for
i\in k
.

Given above definitions, the function
\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})
with
\text{\boldmath$z$}=(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{K}^{\top})^{\top}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl(\log p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\bigr)+\log p(\text{\boldmath$w$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\beta$})+\log p(\theta_{\epsilon})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$l$})

The gradient of
\log p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
with respect to
w
and
\beta
can be evaluated jointly. Denote
\text{\boldmath$c$}=(\text{\boldmath$w$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top})
, then

\displaystyle\nabla_{c}\log p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}_{\epsilon}\nabla_{c}\text{\boldmath$\eta$}_{k}

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$\eta$}_{k}

\displaystyle=H_{k}(\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k})

\nabla_{c}\text{\boldmath$\eta$}_{k}
can be evaluated using standard back-propagation algorithm. The gradient of
\log p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
with respect to
\theta_{\epsilon}
can be computed as:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta_{\epsilon}}\log p(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}n_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\exp(-\theta_{\epsilon})(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}-\text{\boldmath$\eta$}_{k})^{\top}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}-\text{\boldmath$\eta$}_{k})

See Appendix C in the manuscript for the gradient of
\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})
with respect to
l
. It is straight forward to show the following:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{\boldmath$w$}}\log p(\text{\boldmath$w$})

\displaystyle=-1/\sigma^{2}_{w}\text{\boldmath$w$}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\text{\boldmath$\beta$}}\log p(\text{\boldmath$\beta$})

\displaystyle=-1/\sigma^{2}_{\beta}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta_{\epsilon}}\log p(\theta_{\epsilon})

\displaystyle=-a+b\exp(-\theta_{\epsilon})

The model parameters of the Bernoulli DMM in Section 4 are
\text{\boldmath$\theta$}=(\text{\boldmath$w$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\top},\text{\boldmath$\omega$}^{\top})^{\top}
, where
\omega
is the non-zero elements of diagonal matrix
\Omega_{\alpha}
with each element follows an
IG(0.1,0.1)
distribution. Again, for variational inference we use log-transformation to transform
\omega
to real line. The prior for
w
and
\beta
are normal distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and
\sigma^{2}_{w}=50
,
\sigma^{2}_{\beta}=5
. The function
\log g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})
with
\text{\boldmath$z$}=(y_{1}^{*},\dots,y_{n}^{*},\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{K}^{\top})^{\top}
is now:

\displaystyle g(\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$z$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\bigl((\mathds{1}(y^{*}_{j}\leq 0,y_{j}=0)+\mathds{1}(y^{*}_{j}>0,y_{j}=1))\log p(y^{*}_{j}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\log p(\text{\boldmath$w$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\beta$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\omega$})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{k}}\bigl(\log p(y^{*}_{j}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$})\bigr)+\log p(\text{\boldmath$w$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\beta$})+\log p(\text{\boldmath$\omega$})

\mathds{1}(y^{*}_{j}\leq 0,y_{j}=0)+\mathds{1}(y^{*}_{j}>0,y_{j}=1)
always sum to
1
as
y_{i}^{*}
is drawn from truncated normal using HVI methods, thus the second equation follows. The gradient of
\log p(y^{*}_{j}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$})
with respect to
w
and
\beta
again can be evaluate using back-propagation algorithm in a similar way as above, with
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}=1
.

D.2: Additional details on Example 2

In the data generating process (DGP) in Section 4.3, we simulated data from a DMM with two hidden layers, each with 5 neurons. The inputs
\text{\boldmath$x$}_{i}\sim N(\bm{0},V_{x})
with

\displaystyle V_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-0.5&0.2&0\\
0&1&0&-0.5&0.2\\
-0.5&0&1&0&-0.5\\
0.2&-0.5&0&1&0\\
0&0.2&-0.5&0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,

and
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}\sim N(0,\Omega_{\alpha})
with
\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1}=diag(1.0443,9.0498,0.4569,0.5190,0.2857,2.7548)
.
The output layer fixed effect coefficient vector
\text{\boldmath$\beta$}=(0.8292,-1.3250,3.9909,1.6823,-1.7564,0.5580)^{\top}
, where the first element is the offset.
The hidden layer weights are fixed across simulations, with their values derived
by fitting NAGVAC to the simulated dataset provided in
Tran et al., 2020
and use the weighting matrices in the last step of this algorithm.
We use
6
observations from each group for training,
2
observations
from each group for testing, and another
2
observations from each group for implementing the NAGVAC stopping rule.

To implement step (b) of Algorithm 1, the conditional posterior
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$}\sim N(\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{k},\Sigma_{k})
with

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$\mu$}_{k}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{k}H_{k}^{\top}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}-H_{k}\text{\boldmath$\beta$})/\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}

\displaystyle=(\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1}+H_{k}^{\top}H_{k}/\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon})^{-1}

We also use NAGVAC to fit the data using the code provided by the authors with default values. This approach assumes diagonal
\Omega_{\alpha}
, which matches the DGP. It also employs a stopping rule based on improvements in predictive cross entropy (PCE) for validation data, for which we draw a further 2 observations per group from the DGP. However, we find that NAGVAC does not work well with the simulated data in this example in terms of predictive accuracy, producing negative
R^{2}
for both in-sample and out-of-sample prediction. Thus we do not include the results of NAGVAC in this example.

D.3: Additional details on Example 3

The DGP in Example 3 in Section 4 generates
y_{i}\sim\mbox{Bernoulli}(p_{i})
where

\displaystyle p_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+exp(-\eta_{i})}

\displaystyle\eta_{i}

\displaystyle=2+3(x_{i,1}-2x_{i,2})^{2}-5\left(\frac{x_{i,3}}{(1+x_{i,4})^{2}}\right)-5x_{i,5}+a_{k}\,.

Following
Tran et al., 2020
, the five covariates

x_{i,j},j=1,\dots,5
are generated from uniform distributions
\mathcal{U}(-1,1)
and
a_{k}\sim N(0,1)
. This DGP is the same as that
suggested by these authors, except that we employ a logistic function for
determining
p_{i}
, rather than a probit function. The reason for the difference is to advantage
the NAGVAC algorithm which also uses a logistic link.

Both HVI methods employ an MCMC step within the SNGA optimization.
For both HVI methods, we draw alternately from the conditional posterior
of
\alpha
and
y^{*}
. These both factorize into products over the groups
and observations, respectively, with

\displaystyle\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}|\text{\boldmath$\theta$},\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left((\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1}+H_{k}^{\prime}H_{k})^{-1}H_{k}^{\prime}(\text{\boldmath$y$}_{k}^{*}-H_{k}^{\prime}\text{\boldmath$\beta$}),(\Omega_{\alpha}^{-1}+H_{k}^{\prime}H_{k})^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle y_{i}^{*}|\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k},\text{\boldmath$\theta$},y_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\begin{cases}TN_{(0,\infty)}(\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(L)\top}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}),1)\quad\text{ if }y_{i}=1\\
TN_{(-\infty,0)}(\text{\boldmath$h$}^{(L)\top}_{i}(\text{\boldmath$\beta$}+\text{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}),1)\quad\text{ if }y_{i}=0\end{cases}

Here
H_{k}
has the same definition as above, while

TN_{(a,b)}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
represents truncated normal distribution with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
and support
[a,b]
. We run this MCMC scheme for 5 sweeps, after initializing
\text{\boldmath$\alpha$},\text{\boldmath$y$}^{*}
at their values from the last step of the optimization
algorithm. The approach of using MCMC within SGA was explored
by
Loaiza-Maya et al., 2022
for more complex models
and found to work well.
