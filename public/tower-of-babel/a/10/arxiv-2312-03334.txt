Automorphisms of self-similar trees
Tobias Hartnick, Merlin Incerti–Medici

Abstract
. We explicitly determine the automorphism groups of all self-similar trees (a.k.a. trees with finitely many cone types). We show that any such automorphism group is a direct limit of certain finite products of finite symmetric groups, which are parametrized by a certain deterministic finite automaton.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
History and background

The study of rooted automorphisms of locally finite rooted trees is a central subject in group theory, geometry and dynamics.
If
T
is a
q
-regular rooted tree, then its rooted automorphism group
G=\mathrm{Aut}(T)
satisfies the
wreath recursion

G\cong\mathrm{Sym}_{q}\wr G
, and this recursion determines the algebraic structure of
G
uniquely. Namely, if we denote by
\Rist(n)
the rigid stabilizer of the
n
th level of
T
, then

G\cong\mathrm{Sym}_{q}\ltimes\Rist(1)\cong\mathrm{Sym}_{q}\ltimes(\mathrm{Sym}_{q}\ltimes\Rist(2))\cong\dots,

and
G
is isomorphic to the direct limit of the iterated semidirect products

\mathrm{Sym}_{q}\ltimes(\mathrm{Sym}_{q}\ltimes(\dots\ltimes\mathrm{Sym}_{q})).

A similar presentation of
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is still available if
T
is spherically homogeneous; in this case,
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is the direct limit of iterated semidirect products of finite symmetric groups of the appropriate cardinality. Beyond the spherically homogeneous case, a full description of the algebraic structure of the group
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
seems to be missing from the literature.

The goal of the present article is to fill this gap by describing the structure of
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
for
T
in a large class of locally finite trees. The class of trees we will consider has appeared under various names in different areas of mathematics, in particular as
periodic trees

[
Lyo90
]
or
trees with finitely many cone types

[
NW02
]
in probability theory, as
trees of finite cone type
in spectral graph theory
[
KLW12
,
KLW14
]
and, most recently, as
self-similar trees
in geometric group theory
[
BBM21
]
. We prefer the latter term. We are going to show that, as in the spherically homogeneous case, the automorphism group of a self-similar tree is isomorphic to a certain direct limit of iterated semidirect products of symmetric groups which are parametrized by a finite automaton.

From a dynamical perspective, the class of self-similar trees is closely related to subshifts of finite type, and from the perspective of theoretical computer science it is closely related to regular languages and deterministic finite automata (DFAs). It turns out that this latter point of view is particularly beneficial for the study of the automorphism group.

1.3.
Portraits of automorphisms

From now on let
T=T_{\mathcal{A}}
be the path language tree of a DFA
\mathcal{A}
. Given a vertex
v
of
T
we denote by
\Sigma(v)\subset\Sigma
the set of labels of the outgoing edges from
v
. Then for every
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
there exists a unique injection
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g):\Sigma(v)\to\Sigma
such that

g(v\alpha)=g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\alpha)\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in\Sigma(v).

Definition 1.3
.

The collection
\sigma(g)=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g))_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
of maps is called the
portrait
of
g
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
.

While every automorphism is uniquely determined by its portrait, it is in general hard to decide whether a given collection of maps is the portrait of an automorphism. However, if
\mathcal{A}
happens to be geometrically minimal, then there is an easy criterion.

Notation 1.4
.

Given states
q,q^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{A}=(Q,\delta,\epsilon)
we denote

\Sigma(q,q^{\prime}):=\{\alpha\in\Sigma\mid\delta(q,\alpha)=q^{\prime}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma(q):=\bigsqcup_{q^{\prime}}\Sigma(q,q^{\prime}).

One can show that
\Sigma(v)=\Sigma(q(v))
for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
. We then define the group of
admissible permutations
at
q
as

\mathrm{Sym}(q):=\prod_{q^{\prime}}\mathrm{Sym}(\Sigma(q,q^{\prime}))<\mathrm{Sym}(\Sigma(q)).

We note that this group acts by automorphisms on the underlying directed multigraph
A
of
\mathcal{A}
by fixing all vertices and permuting the edges which emanate from
q
.

Theorem 1.5

(Portraits of automorphisms)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is geometrically minimal, then
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v}
is the portrait of an automorphism if and only if
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Sym}(q(v))
for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
.

As an immediate application of Theorem
1.5
we see that the automorphism group of a self-similar tree is either finite or uncountable. To be more precise, let us refer to a pair
(e_{1},e_{2})
of oriented edges in the underlying directed multigraph graph
A
of
\mathcal{A}
with the same source and target as a
double edge
. We say that a double edge
(e_{1},e_{2})
in
A
is
recurrent
if there is an oriented loop in
A
that either contains
e_{1}
or
e_{2}
, or from which the common source of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
can be reached. We then have the following result:

Corollary 1.6

(Size of the automorphism group)
.

Let
T=T_{\mathcal{A}}
be the path language tree of a geometrically minimal DFA
\mathcal{A}
. Then
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is infinite (equivalently, uncountable) if and only if the underlying directed multigraph of
\mathcal{A}
contains a recurrent double edge.

1.4.
Structure of the automorphism group

We can now finally describe the structure of the automorphism group of a self-similar tree
T
. We may identify
T
with the path language tree of a geometrically minimal DFA
\mathcal{A}
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{n}
the words of length
n
in the regular language
\mathcal{L}
of
\mathcal{A}
and define

\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n):=\prod_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\mathrm{Sym}(q(w)).

By definition,
\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n)
is a finite product of finite symmetric groups, which depends only on
\mathcal{A}
. The group
\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n)
acts on
\mathcal{L}_{n+1}
by

(\sigma_{w})_{w\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}\cdot u\alpha=u\sigma_{u}(\alpha)\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathcal{L}_{n}\text{ and }\alpha\in\Sigma(u),

and hence on
\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n+1)
by permuting the indices. It also acts on
\mathcal{L}_{n+k}
for all
k\geq 2
by acting only on the
(k+1)
th letter, and hence on
\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}({n+k})
. We can thus form the iterated semidirect products

\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq n}:=\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(0)\ltimes(\cdots(\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(1)\ltimes(\dots\ltimes\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n))\cdots)),

and we have canonical projections
p_{n}:\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq n}\to\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq(n-1)}
.

Definition 1.7
.

The inverse limit of the inverse system

\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq 0}\;\xleftarrow{p_{1}}\;\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq 1}\;\xleftarrow{p_{2}}\;\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq 2}\;\xleftarrow{p_{3}}\;\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq 3}\;\xleftarrow{p_{4}}\;\dots

is called the
symmetric group
of the DFA
\mathcal{A}
and denoted
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A})
.

Note that, since the underlying set of
\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq 0}
is
\prod_{|v|_{\Sigma}\leq n}\mathrm{Sym}(q(v))
, we can consider
\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A})
as a subset of
\prod_{v\in\mathcal{L}}\mathrm{Sym}(q(v))
.

Theorem 1.8

(Structure of the automorphism group)
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a geometrically minimal DFA for a self-similar tree
T
, then the portrait map defines an isomorphism of topological groups

\sigma:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{A}),\quad g\mapsto(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g))_{v\in\mathcal{L}}.

Theorem
1.8
will be established in Section
5
below. In fact we will establish a more precise variant of the theorem, which also shows for example that the rigid stabilizer
\Rist(n)
of the
n
th level of
T
restricts to an isomorphism

\Rist(n)\cong{\lim_{\leftarrow}}(\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n)\ltimes(\cdots(\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n+1)\ltimes(\dots\ltimes\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}(n+k))\cdots)).

1.5.
Topological generators

Theorem
1.8
allows us to provide an explicit countable set of topological generators for the group
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
: In the situation of the theorem, the group
\mathrm{Sym}(q)
acts by automorphisms on the underlying directed multigraph

of
\mathcal{A}
for every
q\in Q
, fixing all vertices and permuting only the edges emanating from
q
. Since
T
is the universal covering tree of

, this automorphism lifts uniquely to an automorphism
g_{\sigma}
of
T
. For every vertex
w
of

there is a canonical retraction
\ret_{w}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\Rist(w)
onto the rigid stabilizer of

g_{\sigma}^{w}:=\mathrm{ret}_{w}(g_{\sigma})\in\Rist(w)<\mathrm{Aut}(T).

We refer to Constructions
5.12
and
5.17
for detailed definitions of
\ret_{w}
and
g_{\sigma}^{w}
.

Corollary 1.9
.

The group
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is topologically generated by the automorphisms
g_{\sigma}^{w}
with
w\in\mathcal{L}
and
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(q)\setminus\{\mathrm{Id}\}
for some state
q
of
\mathcal{A}[q(w)]
.

1.6.
Action of
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
on regular languages

Now let
\mathcal{A}
be an arbitrary (i.e. not necessarily geometrically minimal) DFA with regular language
\mathcal{L}
. If
T
denotes the regular language tree of
\mathcal{L}
, then
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
acts on
T
and hence on
\mathcal{L}
.

The group
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
can be expressed in terms of portraits with respect to a geometrically minimal DFA
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
whose regular language coincides with
\mathcal{L}
. It is always possible to choose
\overline{\mathcal{A}}
as a geometric covering quotient of
\mathcal{A}
in a suitable sense and thereby make the action of

on

explicit. This is illustrated in a concrete example in Section
6
below.

1.7.
Structure of this article

This article is organized as follows. Section
2
fixes our notation concerning graphs and automata and recalls basic facts about covering of rooted, directed multigraphs. Section
3
introduces portraits of automorphisms of path language trees. Section
4
discusses properties and constructions of geometrically minimal DFAs in close analogy to the labelled case (a.k.a. Myhill-Nerode theory). The main new result is Proposition
4.24
which ensures that minimal graphs (i.e. the underlying graphs of geometrically minimal DFAs) are compatible with automorphisms of their universal covering trees. This is then applied in
Section
5
to determine the automorphism groups of self-similar trees. In Section
6
we show how to switch back and forth between the descriptions of automorphisms obtained in Sections
3
and
5
and illustrate this on a concrete example.

2.
Covering theory of graphs and automata

For lack of a convenient reference we recall the basic covering theory of graphs and automata. Our graphs will be rooted, directed, oriented multigraphs, and we will develop a theory of rooted, directed, oriented coverings for such graphs. Our automata will be deterministic finite automata (with a single initial state and all states accepting) which we consider as finite labelled graphs, and their covering theory will be a labelled version of our covering theory for graphs.

p_{0}

Remark 2.25

3.
Portraits of automorphisms

Throughout this section let
\mathcal{A}
be a DFA over some alphabet
\Sigma
with underlying graph
A=(Q,\mathcal{E},s,t,\epsilon)
and labelling
\ell_{A}:\mathcal{E}\to\Sigma
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}\subset\Sigma^{*}
the regular language associated with
\mathcal{A}
and by

the associated path language tree with vertex set
\mathcal{L}
and edge set

as in Example
2.28
. We recall that the lifted labelling
\ell_{T}:E(T)\to\Sigma
is given by the projection to the second coordinate. We denote by
\pi:T\to A
the universal covering projection which maps every vertex

to the state of
\mathcal{A}
which is reached after reading the word

.

Notation 3.1
.

For every vertex
q\in Q
(respectively
v\in\mathcal{L}
) we denote by
\Sigma(q):=\ell_{A}(q^{+})
(respectively
\Sigma(v):=\ell_{T}(v^{+})
) the labels of the outgoing edges. Since
\pi
is labelling preserving we have
\Sigma(v)=\Sigma(\pi_{0}(v))
for all

.

Definition 3.2
.

A collection
\sigma=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
of injections
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}:\Sigma(v)\to\Sigma
is called a
portrait
.

Construction 3.3

(Portrait of an automorphism)
.

If

is an automorphism of
T
and

, then
g
maps the children of
v
to the children of

. There thus exists a unique injection
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g):\Sigma(v)\to\Sigma
such that

(6)

g(v\alpha)=g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\alpha)\quad\text{for all }\alpha\in\Sigma(v).

Then
\sigma(g)=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g))_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
is called the
portrait of the automorphism

. We emphasize that this portrait depends
on the choice of admissible labelling
\ell_{A}
.
We say that a portrait
\sigma

defines an automorphism of

if there exists

with
\sigma(g)=\sigma
.

Remark 3.4

(Automorphisms from portraits)
.

It is immediate from the definition of the portrait that for all
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\in\Sigma
with
\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}
we have

(7)

g(\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n})=\prescript{}{e\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\alpha_{1})\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\alpha_{2})\cdots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{.}\sigma(g)(\alpha_{n})

In particular, every automorphism is uniquely determined by its portrait. To describe all automorphisms of

it thus suffices to characterize all of their portraits.

Construction 3.5
.

Given a portrait
\sigma=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
and a word
\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}
we define

(8)

\sigma(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}):=\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{i})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n})\in\Sigma^{*}.

Note that if
\sigma=\sigma(g)
for some

, then for all

we have
\sigma(v)=g(v)\in\mathcal{L}
, whereas in general we only have
\sigma(v)\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\sigma=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
be an abstract portrait. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\sigma
defines an automorphism of
T
.

(ii)

\sigma(\mathcal{L})=\mathcal{L}
and for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
we have
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\Sigma_{v})=\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}
.

(iii)

\sigma(\mathcal{L})\subset\mathcal{L}
and for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
we have
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\Sigma_{v})\subset\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}
.

The implications (i)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)
\Longrightarrow
(iii) are obvious. For the non-trivial implication (iii)
\Longrightarrow
(i) it suffices to show that for
\sigma
as in (iii) the map
\sigma:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}
defines an automorphism of
T
. This follows by induction from the following lemma:

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that
\sigma(v)\in\mathcal{L}
for all words
v
of length
\leq n
and suppose that for all
v\in\mathcal{L}
of length
n
we have

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}:\Sigma(v)\rightarrow\Sigma(\sigma(v)).

Then for all
w\in\mathcal{L}
of length
n+1
we have
\sigma(w)\in\mathcal{L}
and
\sigma(w)
is a child of
\sigma(v)
.

Proof.

Let
w=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n+1}
. Then

\sigma(w)=\sigma(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n+1}).

By assumption,
\sigma(v)\in V(T)
and
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n+1})
is the label of an outgoing edge at the vertex
\sigma(v)
. Thus
\sigma(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n+1})\in\mathcal{C}(\sigma(v))\subset\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

In view of Lemma
3.7
we can check that a given portrait
\sigma=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
defines an automorphism as follows:

•

Check that
\prescript{}{e\cdot}{\sigma}
maps
\Sigma(e)
to
\Sigma(e)
. Use this to compute
\sigma(\alpha)
for every
\alpha\in\Sigma(e)
.

•

Continue inductively to check for all words
v
of length
n\geq 1
that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
maps
\Sigma(v)
to
\Sigma(\sigma(v))
, and use this to compute
\sigma
on all words of length
n+1
.

In theory, this procedure provides a description of all automorphism of the tree
T
through their portraits (which is particularly useful when one is interested in the specific labelling
\ell_{T}
), but there are several problems. Firstly, there are infinitely many conditions to check, although this cannot be avoided. More importantly, the conditions we need to check are not local: In order to check the condition for
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
we need to compute
\sigma(v)
, which involves
\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}
for all prefixes

of

. As a consequence, while we can check whether a given portrait defines an automorphism, it can be difficult to construct automorphisms, or to understand the structure (or even the size) of
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
.

In order to deal with these problems we are going to describe a special class of labellings for the tree
T
such that the corresponding portraits take a particular simple form. This will allow us to describe the group structure of
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
. Using this description of
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
we will then discuss how automorphisms of
T
interact with the original labelling.

4.
Geometric Myhill-Nerode theory

4.1.
Classical vs. geometric Myhill-Nerode theory

Classical Myhill-Nerode theory is concerned with the most efficient description of regular languages by automata; a convenient reference is
[
BBCF21
]
.
Recall that two states
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
of a DFA
\mathcal{A}
are called
Nerode congruent
if their reachable closures
\mathcal{A}[q_{1}]
and
\mathcal{A}[q_{2}]
define the same regular language; this defines an equivalence relation on the set of states of
\mathcal{A}
. A DFA
\mathcal{A}
is called
minimal
if its Nerode congruence is trivial. One can show that every DFA covers a unique minimal DFA, whose sets of states is given by Nerode congruence classes of states of the original DFA. The problem of algorithmically computing the minimal DFA (or equivalently the Nerode congruence) for a given DFA is known as the
minimization problem
for DFAs; a classical algorithm which achieves this is
Moore’s algorithm
(see
[
BBCF21
, Section 4]
). We emphasize that both the definition of the Nerode congruence and of the minimal DFA depend on the labelling of
\mathcal{A}
, not just the underlying graph.

Here we will be interested in a variant of Myhill-Nerode theory (which we call
geometric Myhill-Nerode theory
), which applies to finite
unlabelled
graphs rather than DFAs. In fact, we will be interested in trees which arise as universal covering trees of finite connected graphs and more specifically in the smallest possible covering quotients (in terms of the number of vertices, say) of such trees. By analogy with the case of automata we will refer to such graphs as
minimal graphs
. It turns out that the geometric theory closely parallels the classical one. In particular, we will show that every finite graph admits a unique minimal graph as a covering quotient, and this graph can be constructed by a variant of Moore’s algorithm. As an application we will show that every self-similar tree admits a self-similar structure which is well-adapted to the action of the automorphism group. This will then allow us to compute the automorphism groups of such trees in Section
5
below.

Remark 4.1
.

We warn the reader that while geometric Myhill-Nerode theory parallels the classical theory, it is not a formal consequence of the latter. In particular, the underlying graph of the minimal DFA of a DFA is
not
the minimal graph covered by the underlying graph in general; see Remark
and Example
4.11
below.

4.2.
Self-similar trees

To motivate our geometric theory, we reprove a characterization of trees with finitely many cone type due originally to Belk, Bleak and Matucci (
[

]
). For this let

be a locally finite rooted tree with vertex set

and edge set

. We recall the following terminology from

]

Definition 4.2

\hat{\psi}
its unique lift to an isomorphism
T_{v}\to T_{w}
. Then the
maximal self-similar structure
on
T_{A}

induced
by
A
is defined by

M:=Q,\quad\texttt{type}:=\pi_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{A}^{\max}(v,w):=\{\hat{\psi}\mid\psi\in\mathrm{Aut}(A_{\pi_{0}(v)})\}.

This establishes the implication (v)
\Rightarrow
(iii), and it remains only to show the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(v). This is where our variant of the Myhill-Nerode construction enters the picture:

Construction 4.5

(Geometric Myhill-Nerode construction)
.

Let
T
be a tree with finitely many cone types. We define the
geometric Nerode congruence
on the vertex set
V(T)
of
T
by

v\equiv w\iff T_{v}\cong T_{w}.

We then denote by
\overline{Q}
the quotient of
V(T)
by
\equiv
. Since
T
has finitely many cone types,
\overline{Q}
is finite. We denote by
\overline{\pi}_{0}:V(T)\to\overline{Q}
the canonical projection and by
\overline{\epsilon}
the equivalence class of the root of
T
.

We now define a finite graph
\overline{A}
with vertex set
\overline{Q}
as follows: We choose once and for all a section
\sigma:\overline{Q}\to V(T)
of
\overline{\pi}_{0}
and, given vertices
\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2}\in\overline{Q}
, we connect
\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2}\in\overline{Q}
by
n_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})
edges, where
n_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})
is the number of edges from
\sigma(\overline{q}_{1})
to
some
representative of
\overline{q}_{2}
. Note that
since
T
is locally finite, the graph
\overline{A}
is finite. In the sequel we refer to
\overline{A}
as the
minimal covering quotient
of
T
and say that
\overline{A}
is a
minimal graph
.

We now claim that
n_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q_{2}})
is independent of the choice of section
\sigma
. Indeed, if
\sigma^{\prime}
is a different section, then
\sigma(\overline{q}_{1})\sim\sigma^{\prime}(\overline{q}_{1})
, hence there is an isomorphism
\varphi:T_{\sigma(\overline{q}_{1})}\to T_{\sigma^{\prime}(\overline{q}_{1})}
. We have

n_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})=|M_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})|,\;\text{where}\;M_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2}):=\{v\in\mathcal{C}(\sigma(\overline{q}_{1}))\mid\overline{\pi}_{0}(v)=\overline{q}_{2}\}

and
\varphi
restricts to a bijection
\mathcal{C}(\sigma(\overline{q}_{1}))\to\mathcal{C}(\sigma^{\prime}(\overline{q}_{1}))
which preserves cone types, hence to a bijection
M_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})\to M_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})
. This shows that
n_{\sigma}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})=n_{\sigma^{\prime}}(\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2})
and proves the claim.

There are now many ways to complete the map
\overline{\pi}_{0}
into a covering morphism
\overline{\pi}:T\to\overline{A}
. Given
\overline{q}_{1},\overline{q}_{2}\in\overline{Q}
and a lift
v_{1}\in V(T)
of
\overline{q}_{1}
, then by the previous claim

|\overline{q}_{1}^{+}\cap\overline{q}_{2}^{-}|=\left|\bigsqcup_{v_{2}\in\overline{\pi}_{0}^{-1}(\overline{q}_{2})}v_{1}^{+}\cap v_{2}^{-}\right|.

Consequently we can map the edges connecting
v_{1}
with lifts of
\overline{q}_{2}
bijectively onto the edges in
\overline{q}_{1}^{+}\cap\overline{q}_{2}^{-}
in any way we want. This will extend
\overline{\pi}_{0}
to a morphism
\overline{\pi}:T\to\overline{A}
, which is then a covering morphism by Corollary
2.15
. In particular,
\overline{A}
is a finite covering quotient of
T
, and thus
T
is isomorphic to the universal covering tree of the finite graph
\overline{A}
.

At this point we have established Theorem
4.3
.

4.3.
Minimal covering quotients

The following proposition justifies the name “minimal covering quotients”:

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
T
be a self-similar tree, let
A
be a covering quotient of
T
and let
\overline{A}
be the minimal covering quotient of
T
.
Then there exists a covering morphism
p:A\to\overline{A}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi:T\to A
be a covering morphism and let
\overline{\pi}:T\to\overline{A}
be as in Construction
4.4
. If
v
and

are vertices of

and
\pi_{0}(v)=\pi_{0}(w)
, then by Corollary
2.22
.(ii) we have

and hence
\overline{\pi}_{0}(v)=\overline{\pi}_{1}(w)
. We thus find
p_{0}:Q\to\overline{Q}
such that

\overline{\pi}_{0}=p_{0}\circ\pi_{0}.

Now let
q_{1}\in Q
, let
v_{1}\in V(T)
be a lift of
q_{1}
and set
\overline{q}_{1}:=p_{0}(q_{1})
. Since

are covering morphisms we then have

|\overline{q}_{1}^{+}\cap\overline{q}_{2}^{+}|=\left|\bigsqcup_{v_{2}\in\overline{\pi}_{0}^{-1}(\overline{q}_{2})}v_{1}^{+}\cap v_{2}^{-}\right|=\left|\bigsqcup_{q_{2}\in\pi_{0}(\overline{\pi}_{0}^{-1}(\overline{q}_{2}))}q_{1}^{+}\cap q_{2}^{-}\right|=\left|\bigsqcup_{q_{2}\in p_{0}^{-1}(\overline{q}_{2}))}q_{1}^{+}\cap q_{2}^{-}\right|.

for every

. We can thus extend

by the same argument as used in Construction

∎

In the situation of Proposition

.
We emphasize that the covering morphism

Proposition 4.7

Let

be two coverings. Then
\pi_{0}=\pi_{0}^{\prime}

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality that

is a tree. We may then assume that
\pi:T\to\overline{A}

. By Remark

. Now if

\pi_{0}(v)=\pi_{0}(p_{0}(v))=\pi_{0}^{\prime}(v).\qed

If we
denote by

and

the number of vertices and edges of a finite graph

Corollary 4.8
.

Let

T
, and let
\overline{A}
be the associated minimal covering quotient.

(i)

and

(ii)

If

Proof.

By Proposition

Remark 4.10

(Geometrically minimal DFAs)

If

From Proposition

Remark 4.12

(Edge automorphism group)
.

Given a graph

If

Every automorphism is a covering, hence all automorphisms of

Example 4.14
.

The converse of Corollary
4.13
is not true, i.e. there exists finite connected graphs whose automorphism group acts trivially on vertices, but which are not minimal.

Figure
1
depicts a graph
A
and its universal covering tree
T
. The minimal covering quotient of
T
has two vertices, since all vertices of
T
except for the root are geometrically Nerode equivalent. Consequently,
A
is not minimal.

On the other hand, every automorphism of
A
has to fix
\epsilon
as no other vertex has four outgoing edges. The vertex
B
is the unique one with exactly two incoming edges and so it has to be fixed as well.
B
‘points’ to

, which thus has to be fixed.
A
‘points’ to

, so

has to be fixed. Now

has to be fixed as well, and hence every automorphism of

is an edge automorphism.

This means that we cannot construct the minimal covering quotient of a finite connected graph

by simply identifying vertices in a common orbit of the automorphism group. This raises the question on how to algorithmically construct minimal covering quotients. We will adress this in the next subsection.

4.4.
Moore’s algorithm revisited

Moore’s algorithm is a famous algorithm which constructs the minimal DFA of a regular language out of a given accepting automaton. In this subsection we explain a
geometric
version of Moore’s algorithm which works in the unlabelled setting.

Thus let
A=(Q,\mathcal{E},s,t,\epsilon)
be a finite connected graph with universal covering tree
T
and let
\overline{A}
be its minimal covering quotient.

Remark 4.15

(Geometric Nerode equivalence)
.

We explain how the graph
\overline{A}
can be constructed (up to isomorphism) from
A
. For this we fix covering morphisms

T\xrightarrow{\pi}A\xrightarrow{p}\overline{A}.

and set
\overline{\pi}:=p\circ\pi
.
By Proposition
4.7
we then have
\overline{\pi}_{0}(v)=[v]
, where
[v]
denotes the geometric Nerode equivalence class of
v
. In particular, if
v,w\in V(T)
with
\pi_{0}(v)=\pi_{0}(w)
, then
v\equiv w
. We deduce that if
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are vertices of
A
and
v_{j},w_{j}\in\pi_{0}^{-1}(q_{j})
, then
v_{1}\equiv v_{2}\Leftrightarrow w_{1}\equiv w_{2}
. We thus obtain an equivalence relation (also denoted
\equiv
) on
q
by setting

(9)

q_{1}\equiv q_{2}\iff v_{1}\equiv v_{2}\text{ for some (hence any) }v_{j}\in\pi_{0}^{-1}(q_{j}).

We refer to
\equiv
as the
geometric Nerode equivalence relation
on
A
.

Note that
\overline{A}
is determined up to isomorphism by the pair
(A,\equiv)
: The vertex set of
\overline{A}
is given by
Q/\equiv
, and two vertices
[q_{1}]
and
[q_{2}]
are connected by as many oriented edges as there are oriented edges from
q_{1}
to some
q_{2}^{\prime}
with
q_{2}^{\prime}\equiv q_{2}
. Thus in order to construct
\overline{A}
, we need to determine the geometric Nerode equivalence relation on
A
.

To compute the geometric Nerode equivalence relation in practice, it suffices to compute certain approximations, which we now define. We fix a section

\sigma:Q\to V(T),\quad q\mapsto\widetilde{q}

of
\pi_{0}
; then the isomorphism class of
T_{\widetilde{q}}
depends only on
q
, and

(10)

q_{1}\equiv q_{2}\iff T_{\widetilde{q}_{1}}\cong T_{\widetilde{q}_{2}}.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we now define a finite subtree
T_{\widetilde{q}}^{(d)}
of
T_{\widetilde{q}}
as the full subtree on those vertices of
T_{\widetilde{q}}
of distance at most
d
from the root. We then define a family of equivalence relations by

(11)

q_{1}\equiv_{d}q_{2}\iff T_{\widetilde{q}_{1}}^{(d)}\cong T_{\widetilde{q}_{2}}^{(d)}.

A schematic depicting how we extend
\varphi
to the children of the leaf
\ell
. The dashed triangles depict the subtrees
(T_{i})_{\gamma_{i}(t_{j})}^{d-t_{2}+1}
of depth

, which are all isomorphic.

Lemma 4.16
.

For vertices
q_{1},q_{2}\in Q
the following are equivalent:

(i)

q_{1}\equiv q_{2}
.

(ii)

q_{1}\equiv_{d}q_{2}
for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

q_{1}\equiv_{d}q_{2}
for some
d\geq(|Q|+1)^{2}
.

Proof.

The equivalence (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) is immediate from (
10
) and (
11
), and the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) is obvious.

To prove (iii)
\implies
(i) we denote
v_{1}:=\widetilde{q}_{1}
and
v_{2}:=\widetilde{q}_{2}
and abbreviate
T_{1}:=T_{v_{1}}
and
T_{2}:=T_{v_{2}}
. We assume that for some
d\geq(|Q|+1)^{2}
we are given an isomorphism
\varphi_{d}:T_{1}^{(d)}\to T_{2}^{(d)}
.
We then construct an isomorphism
\varphi_{d+1}:T_{1}^{(d+1)}\to T_{2}^{(d+1)}

as follows:

We pick a leaf
l
of
T_{v_{1}}^{(d)}
and denote by
\gamma_{1}
the unique geodesic from
v_{1}
to
l
, so that
\gamma_{1}(0)=v_{1}
and
\gamma_{1}(d)=l
. Then
\gamma_{2}:=\varphi\circ\gamma_{1}
is the unique geodesic from
v_{2}
to
\varphi(l)
. We now consider the geodesic
\gamma(t)=(\gamma_{1}(t),\gamma_{2}(t))
and its image
\pi\circ\gamma
in
A\times A
. Since
A\times A
has
|Q|^{2}
vertices and
d\geq(|Q|+1)^{2}
, there exist
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq d-1
such that

\pi(\gamma_{1}(t_{1}))=\pi(\gamma_{1}(t_{2}))\quad\text{and}\quad\pi(\gamma_{2}(t_{1}))=\pi(\gamma_{2}(t_{2})).

This means that we have isomorphisms

(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{1})}\cong(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{2})}\quad\text{and}\quad(T_{2})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{1})}\cong(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{2})}.

Now we observe that
\varphi_{d}
induces isomorphisms

(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{1})}^{(d-t_{1})}\to(T_{2})_{\gamma_{2}(t_{1})}^{(d-t_{1})}\quad\text{and}\quad(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{2})}^{(d-t_{2})}\to(T_{2})_{\gamma_{2}(t_{2})}^{(d-t_{2})}

Since
d-t_{1}\geq d-t_{2}+1
, we can replace
\varphi_{d}
on the subtree
(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{2})}^{(d-t_{2}+1)}
by the map

\widetilde{\varphi}_{d}:(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{2})}^{(d-t_{2}+1)}\to(T_{1})_{\gamma_{1}(t_{1})}^{(d-t_{2}+1)}\xrightarrow{\varphi_{d}}(T_{2})_{\gamma_{2}(t_{1})}^{(d-t_{2}+1)}\to(T_{2})_{\gamma_{2}(t_{2})}^{(d-t_{2}+1)}.

This extends the isomorphism
\varphi_{d}
to the children of the leaf
\ell
. (In fact, it extends
\varphi_{d}
to the level
d+1
intersected with the subtree
T_{\gamma_{1}(t_{2})}
.) After finitely many such modifications we have extended
\varphi_{d}
to the entire level
d+1
, i.e. we have constructed
\varphi_{d+1}
.
∎

Corollary 4.17
.

There exists an algorithm which computes
\overline{A}
from
A
.

Proof.

For all
q_{1},q_{2}\in Q
we can compute the trees
T^{(d)}_{\widetilde{q}_{1}}
and
T^{(d)}_{\widetilde{q}_{2}}
for all
d\leq(|Q|+1)^{2}
. We can then decide in finite time (e.g. by brute force) if these finite trees are isomorphic, and hence if
q_{1}\equiv q_{2}
. This is sufficient to construct
\overline{A}
.
∎

Of course the algorithm outlined in the proof of Corollary
4.17
is not efficient. We briefly indicate what a more efficient algorithm could look like. As we do so, we closely follow the presentation of the classical Moore algorithm in
[
BBCF21
]
.

Notation 4.18
.

We are going to work with partitions of
Q
rather than equivalence relations on
Q
. Given a finite set of partitions
\mathcal{P}_{1},\dots\mathcal{P}_{n}
of
Q
, we denote by

\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{P}_{i}:=\left\{\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}|P_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{i}\right\}\setminus\{\emptyset\},

their coarsest common refinement. Given a subset
P\subset Q
and a natural number
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we define a partition

(P,n):=\left\{\left\{q\in Q\mid\sum_{p\in P}|q^{+}\cap p^{-}|=n\right\},\left\{q\in Q\mid\sum_{p\in P}|q^{+}\cap p^{-}|\neq n\right\}\right\}.

Note that this partition is only non-trivial if
n\leq|Q|
.

Definition 4.19
.

The
geometric Moore algorithm
is defined as follows:

(1)

Set
\mathcal{P}_{0}
to be the partition of
Q
consisting of the single element
Q
.

(2)

For
d=1
to
(|Q|+1)^{2}
do the following.

(3)

For all
n\leq|Q|
define

\mathcal{P}_{n,d}:=\bigwedge_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{d-1}}(P,n).

(4)

Define

\mathcal{P}_{d}:=\mathcal{P}_{d-1}\wedge\bigwedge_{n=0}^{|Q|}\mathcal{P}_{n,d}.

(5)

Return
\mathcal{P}_{(|Q|+1)^{2}}
.

Proposition 4.20
.

The geometric Moore algorithm computes the partition induced by the geometric Nerode equivalence relation on
Q
.

Proof.

We will show by induction that
\mathcal{P}_{d}
is the partition induced by the relation
\equiv_{d}
on
Q
; this is clear for
d=0
.

Thus assume that
\mathcal{P}_{d}
defines the partition associated with
\equiv_{d}
and consider
\mathcal{P}_{d+1}
. By definition, two vertices
q_{1},q_{2}\in Q
are in the same class of
\mathcal{P}_{d+1}
if and only if they are in the same class of
\mathcal{P}_{d}
(i.e. in the same class of
\equiv_{d}
) and for every equivalence class of
\equiv_{d}
, they have the same number of children in that equivalence class. This condition is clearly necessary for
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
to be equivalent with respect to
\equiv_{d+1}
. On the other hand, if the condition is satisfied, then we can
construct an isomorphism

\varphi_{d+1}:T_{\widetilde{q}_{1}}^{(d+1)}\to T_{\widetilde{q}_{2}}^{(d+1)}

as follows: Firstly, we map
\widetilde{q}_{1}
to
\widetilde{q}_{2}
. Secondly, we can send every child
v_{1}
of
\widetilde{q}_{1}
to a child
v_{2}
of
\widetilde{q}_{2}
with
v_{1}\equiv_{d}v_{2}
. The latter implies that we can extend this isomorphism from
T_{\widetilde{q}_{1}}^{(1)}
to
T_{\widetilde{q}_{1}}^{(d+1)}
by mapping
T_{v_{1}}^{(d)}
isomorphically to
T_{v_{2}}^{(d)}
.
∎

This provides an efficient algorithm to compute the minimal covering quotient of a given finite connected graph
A
.

Remark 4.21
.

We have constructed the minimal covering quotient of a self-similar tree using a modification of Moore’s algorithm. Let us briefly outline a different approach, which works by reduction to the classical Moore algorithm.

We claim that on any self-similar tree
T
there exists a labelling (induced from some finite quotient
A
of
T
) such that the corresponding minimal DFA (which can be computed by the classical Moore algorithm) is geometrically minimal. The undelying graph is the then desired minimal covering quotient.

To prove the claim it suffices (by Lemma
4.16
) to construct a labelling which for every edge
e
fully describes the geometry of the space of all paths of length
\leq(|Q|+1)^{2}
that start with
e
. We leave this to the reader.

4.5.

Throughout this subsection,
T

.

Construction 4.22

(Minimal rigid self-similar structures)
.

Let

Note that, by definition, we have

if and only if
v\equiv w
. In other words, our choice of covering morphism
\pi
amounts to choosing a
canonical
isomorphism for every pair of isomorphic subtrees of
T
such that these choices are compatible.

The self-similar structures
S_{\pi}
arising from covering morphisms
\pi:T\to\overline{A}
have the following special property, which makes them particularly adapted to the study of automorphisms of
T
.

Definition 4.23
.

If
S
is a self-similar structure on
T
with type function
type
, then an automorphism
g
of
T
is called
type-preserving
with respect to
S
if
\texttt{type}\circ g=\texttt{type}
. We say that a self-similar structure is
Aut-rigid
if every automorphism of
T
is type-preserving with respect to
S
.

Proposition 4.24
.

For every covering morphism
\pi:T\to\overline{A}
the associated rigid self-similar structure
S_{\pi}
is Aut-rigid.

Proof.

Let
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
and let
v
be a vertex of
T
. Then
g
induces an isomorphism between
T_{v}
and
T_{g(v)}
and hence
\pi_{0}(v)=\pi_{0}(g(v))
. Since
\pi_{0}
is precisely the type function of
S_{\pi}
the proposition follows.
∎

Corollary 4.25
.

Every self-similar tree admits a rigid and Aut-rigid self-similar structure.∎

In general, rigid self-similar structures arising from covering morphisms may fail to be Aut-rigid:

Example 4.26
.

Consider again the finite graph
A
and its universal covering tree
T
from Figure
1
. The labels indicate a specific choice of covering morphism
\pi:T\to A
, which defines a rigid self-similar structure on
T
. We claim that this self-similar structure is not Aut-rigid.

Indeed, since all children of the root are of different types, every type-preserving automorphism of
T
has to fix the first level. However, there are automorphisms of
T
which permute this level arbitrarily, and hence not every automorphism is type-preserving.

5.
Automorphism groups of self-similar trees

In this subsection we describe the automorphism group of an arbitrary self-similar tree
T
in terms of permutation groups. The key idea is to equip
T
with an Aut-rigid self-similar structure and thereby to reduce to the case of type-preserving automorphism. More precisely we will work in the following setting:

Notation 5.1
.

Let
T
be a self-similar tree with vertex set
V(T)
and edge set
E(T)
.

•

We denote by
A=(Q,\mathcal{E},s,t,\epsilon)
the minimal covering quotient of
T
.

•

We fix once and for all a covering morphism
\pi:T\to A
and denote by
S_{\pi}
the associated Aut-rigid self-similar structure as in
4.22
. This determines for all
vertices
v,w
of
T
with
T_{v}\cong T_{w}
a canonical isomorphism
\varphi_{vw}:T_{v}\to T_{w}
as in (
12
).

•

We pick an admissible labelling
\ell:A\to\Sigma
which induces a labelling
\ell_{T}:E(T)\to\Sigma
via
\pi
; this allows us to identify
T
with the path language tree
T_{\mathcal{A}}
for the corresponding geometrically minimal automaton
\mathcal{A}
as in Notation
2.28
. We may thus assume that
V(T)=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
and that edges are given as in (
5
).

•

Since every vertex of
T
except for the root has a unique incoming edge, we can extend
\ell_{T}
to non-root vertices and set

\ell_{T}:V(T)\setminus\{\epsilon\}\to\Sigma,\quad\alpha_{1}\cdots\alpha_{n}\mapsto\alpha_{n}.

•

Let
v\in V(T)
and
q=\pi_{0}(v)\in Q
. Recall that we denote by
\mathcal{C}(q)=t(q^{+})
and
\mathcal{C}(v)=t(v^{+})
the respective children and by
\Sigma(q)=\ell(q^{+})
and
\Sigma(v)=\ell_{T}(v^{+})
the respective labels of the outgoing edges.

5.1.
Admissible portraits

We have seen in Remark
3.4
that every automorphism
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is uniquely determined by its portrait
\sigma(g)
. Under our standing assumption that the labelling on
T
is induded by a
geometrically minimal
DFA, we can characterize the resulting portraits in purely local terms. For this we recall from the introduction that for a state
q\in Q
the group of
admissible permutations
of
\Sigma(q)
is defined as

\mathrm{Sym}(q):=\prod_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(q)}\mathrm{Sym}(\Sigma(q,q^{\prime}))<\mathrm{Sym}(\Sigma(q)).

Here,
\Sigma(q)=\bigsqcup_{q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(q)}\Sigma(q,q^{\prime})
, where
\Sigma(q,q^{\prime}):=\ell(q^{+}\cap(q^{\prime})^{-})
. We say that a portrait
\sigma=(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T)}
is
admissible
if

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
is an admissible permutation of
\Sigma(\pi_{0}(v))
for every
v\in V(T)
. In this case we refer to
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
as the
local permutation
of
\sigma
at
v
.

Theorem 5.2
.

If a portrait
\sigma
defines an automorphism of
T
, then it is admissible.

We will see in Theorem
5.15
below that the converse is also true, i.e. automorphisms of
T
correspond precisely to admissible portraits.

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to the proof of Theorem
5.2
. Our first goal is to describe isomorphisms between rooted subtrees of
T
in terms of our fixed minimal automaton. Note that every rooted isomorphism
\varphi
of trees is uniquely determined by its vertex part
\varphi_{0}
, and to simplify notation we will often just write
\varphi
instead of
\varphi_{0}
.

Remark 5.3

(Identifying subtrees with sublanguages)
.

Let
v\in V(T)
and
q:=\pi_{0}(v)\in Q
. The vertices of
T_{v}
are words of the form
vv^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
with
v^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{*}
. Since the automaton
\mathcal{A}
is in the state
q
after reading
v
, the condition
vv^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
means precisely that
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}[q]}
. We thus obtain a bijection

(13)

\iota_{v}:\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}[q]}\to V(T_{v}),\quad v^{\prime}\mapsto vv^{\prime}.

Identifying
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}[q]}
with the set of vertices of its path language tree
T_{\mathcal{A}[q]}
(cf. Notation
2.28
), we observe that
\iota_{v}
is in fact the vertex-part of an isomorphism
\iota_{v}:T_{\mathcal{A}[q]}\rightarrow T_{v}
of rooted trees.

In the sequel, given two graphs
A
and
B
we denote by
\mathrm{Iso}(A,B)
the set of isomorphisms from
A
to
B
.

Proposition 5.4

(Local isomorphisms)
.

Let
v,w\in V(T)
and assume that
\mathrm{Iso}(T_{v},T_{w})\neq\emptyset
. Then the following hold:

(i)

v\equiv w
, hence
\pi_{0}(v)=\pi_{0}(w)=q
for some
q\in Q
.

(ii)

There is a bijection

\mathrm{Ad}_{v,w}:\mathrm{Iso}(T_{v},T_{w})\to\mathrm{Aut}(T_{\mathcal{A}}[q]),\quad\varphi\mapsto\iota_{w}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\iota_{v}.

which maps
\varphi_{vw}
to the identity.

(iii)

If
\varphi\in\mathrm{Iso}(T_{v},T_{w})
, then
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\varphi}:=\mathrm{Ad}_{v,w}(\varphi)
is the unique element of
\mathrm{Aut}(T_{\mathcal{A}[q]})
such that
\varphi(vv^{\prime})=w\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\varphi}(v^{\prime})
.

Proof.

(i) is immediate from the definition of
\equiv
.

(ii) Since
\iota_{v}
and
\iota_{w}
are isomorphisms, so is
\iota_{w}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\iota_{v}
for every
\varphi\in\mathrm{Iso}(T_{v},T_{w})
. This shows that
\mathrm{Ad}_{v,w}
is well-defined, and by definition it is bijective with inverse
\psi\mapsto\iota_{w}\circ\psi\circ\iota_{v}^{-1}
.

To show the statement on
\varphi_{vw}
, recall that
\varphi_{vw}:T_{v}\rightarrow T_{w}
was defined to be the unique lift of the identity on
A_{q}
. Specifically, if
\gamma
is a path in
A_{q}
that starts at
q
, it has a unique lift
\gamma_{v}
in
T_{v}
that starts at
v
and a unique lift
\gamma_{w}
in
T_{w}
that starts at
w
. The map
\varphi_{vw}
is defined by sending the endpoint of
\gamma_{v}
to
\gamma_{w}
. (In fact,
\varphi_{vw}
sends the entire path
\gamma_{v}
isomorphically to
\gamma_{w}
.) Because we are working with a labelled graph
(A,\ell)
and have lifted this labelling to the universal covering tree, we can use the labeling to describe the map
\varphi_{vw}
and obtain

\varphi_{vw}:T_{v}\rightarrow T_{w},\quad vu\mapsto wu.

Therefore,

(\varphi_{vw})_{0}=\iota_{w}\circ\iota_{v}^{-1}

and we compute

\mathrm{Ad}_{v,w}((\varphi_{vw})_{0})=\iota_{w}^{-1}\circ\iota_{w}\circ\iota_{v}^{-1}\iota_{v}=\mathrm{Id}.

(iii) The formula follows immediately from

\varphi(vv^{\prime})=\varphi\circ\iota_{v}\circ\iota_{v}^{-1}(vv^{\prime})=\iota_{w}\circ\iota_{w}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\iota_{v}(v^{\prime})=w(\iota_{w}^{-1}\circ\varphi\circ\iota_{v})(v^{\prime}),

and it clearly determines
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\varphi}
.
∎

Example 5.5

(Restrictions of global automorphisms)
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
be a global automorphism and
v\in V(T)
with
\pi_{0}(v)=q
. Then
g
restricts to an isomorphism
T_{v}\rightarrow T_{g.v}
to which Proposition
5.4
can be applied. In particular,

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}:=\mathrm{Ad}_{v,g.v}(g|_{T_{v}})\in\mathrm{Aut}(T_{\mathcal{A}[q]})

is the unique automorphism of
\mathrm{Aut}(T_{\mathcal{A}[q]})
such that

(14)

g(vw)=g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}(w)\text{ for all }w\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}[q]}.

Definition 5.6
.

If
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
and
v\in V(T)
, then
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}:=\mathrm{Ad}_{v,g.v}(g|_{T_{v}})
is called the
local action
of
g
at
v
.

Comparing Formulas (
14
) and (
6
) we see that the portrait of
g
is given by the restrictions of the local actions, i.e.

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}|_{\Sigma(\pi_{0}(v))}.

Since
g
is type-preserving and bijective, we deduce from (
6
) that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)\in\mathrm{Sym}(\Sigma(\pi_{0}(v)))
. Theorem
5.2
thus follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 5.7

(Admissibility)
.

Let
\delta
be the transition function of the geometrically minimal DFA
\mathcal{A}
. If
v\in V(T)
is a vertex of type
q
and
\alpha\in\Sigma(q)
, then for every
g\in Aut(T)
, we have

(15)

\delta(q,\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\alpha))=\delta(q,\alpha).

In particular,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)
is an admissible permutation.

Proof.

We observe that
v\alpha
is the unique child of
v
in
T
with
\ell_{T}(v\alpha)=\alpha
. Since
\pi
is label-preserving this means that

q^{\prime}:=\pi_{0}(v\alpha)=\delta(q,\alpha).

Since
g
is type preserving (i.e.
\pi_{0}\circ g=\pi_{0}
) we have
\pi_{0}(g(v\alpha))=q^{\prime}
and Equation (
6
) implies that

q^{\prime}=\pi_{0}(g(v\alpha))=\pi_{0}(g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\alpha))=\delta(q,\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha)).

This shows that

\delta(q,\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha))=q^{\prime}=\delta(q,\alpha),

which implies that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha)
preserves
\Sigma(q,q^{\prime})
for every
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(q)
and thereby shows that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}
is admissible.
∎

This finishes the proof of Theorem
5.2
.

5.2.
Rigid stabilizers

Let
v\in V(T)
; we denote by
|v|_{\Sigma}
the word length of
v
and by
\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v}
the stabilizer of
v
under
V(T)
.

Definition 5.8
.

The
rigid stabilizer

\Rist(v)<\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v}
is the subgroup of all automorphisms which restrict to the identity on
T\setminus T_{v}
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we also denote by
V(T)_{n}\subset\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
the set of words of length
n
in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
and define the
n
th rigid level stabilizer
as

\Rist(n):=\langle\Rist(v)\mid v\in V(T)_{n}\rangle.

Furthermore, we define

\mathrm{Sym}(n):=\prod_{v\in V(T)_{n}}\mathrm{Sym}(\pi(v)),

where we recall that
\pi:T\rightarrow A
is the projection to the Myhill-Nerode graph of
T
and
\mathrm{Sym}(q)
was defined in Definition
. We call elements of
\mathrm{Sym}(n)

admissible permutations at level
n
.

Proposition 5.9
.

The chain
\mathrm{Aut}(T)=\Rist(0)>\Rist(1)>\Rist(2)>\dots
has the following properties:

(i)

\Rist(n)\lhd\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is normal for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

(ii)

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have an isomorphism

i_{n}:\prod_{v\in V(T)_{n}}\Rist(v)\to\Rist(n),\quad(g_{v})_{v\in V(T)_{n}}\to\prod_{v\in V(T)_{n}}g_{v}.

(iii)

\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\Rist(n)=\{e\}
.

(iv)

If
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\Rist(n)
, then
\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}g_{n}
converges in
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
.

Proof.

(i) is immediate from the fact that the
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
-action preserves levels, and (ii) follows from the fact that the trees
T_{v}
with
v\in V(T)_{n}
are pairwise disjoint, hence their rigid stabilizers commute. (iii) holds by definition, and (iv) follows from the fact that the sequence of partial products stabilizes on each level.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
and
h\in\mathrm{Rist}(n)
. Then

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(gh)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g),&\text{if }|v|_{\Sigma}<n\\
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)\circ\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(h),&\text{if }|v|_{\Sigma}=n\end{array}\right.

Proof.

Let
w=v\beta\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}}
with final letter
\beta
. If
|v|_{\Sigma}<n
, then
gh(v)=g(v)
and
gh(w)=g(w)
, and hence (
14
) yields

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{(}gh)(\beta)=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}(\beta)\implies\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(gh)(\beta)=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)(\beta).

If
|v|_{\Sigma}=n
, then we still have
h(v)=v
and thus (
14
) yields

\displaystyle g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{(}gh)(\beta)

\displaystyle=(gh)(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{(}gh)(\beta)=gh(w)=g(h(w))

\displaystyle=g(h(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{h}(\beta))=g(v\prescript{}{v\cdot}{h}(\beta))=g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{h}(\beta)),

which yields
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(gh)(\beta)=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)\circ\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(h)(\beta).

∎

This allows us to compute portraits of infinite products of elements from rigid stabilizers. For
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we introduce the notation

\sigma_{N}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\mathrm{Sym}(N),\quad g\mapsto(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g))_{v\in V(T)_{N}}.

Corollary 5.11
.

If
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\Rist(n)
, then

\sigma_{N}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}g_{n}\right)=\sigma_{N}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N}g_{n}\right)=\sigma_{N}\left(\prod_{n=1}^{N-1}g_{n}\right)\sigma_{N}(g_{N}).\qed

Next we observe that rigid (level) stabilizers are retracts of the automorphism group:

Construction 5.12

(Retractions onto rigid stabilizers)
.

We have natural maps

\iota_{v}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v}\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(T),\;g\mapsto g\quad\text{and}\quad p_{v}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v}\to\mathrm{Aut}(T_{v}),\;g\mapsto g|_{T_{v}}.

The map
p_{v}
restricts to an isomorphism
\Rist(v)\to\mathrm{Aut}(T_{v})
and hence there is a unique
s_{v}:\mathrm{Aut}(T_{v})\to\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v}
such that

p_{v}\circ s_{v}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathrm{Aut}(T_{v})}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{v}(\mathrm{Aut}(T_{v}))=\Rist(v).

We may use the isomorphisms
\varphi_{g(v)v}:T_{g(v)}\rightarrow T_{v}
(see Notation
5.1
) to define

q_{v}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\rightarrow\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v},\quad g\mapsto s_{v}(\varphi_{g(v)v}\circ g|_{T_{v}}).

Note that
q_{v}\circ\iota_{v}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathrm{Aut}(T)_{v}}
, the image of the map
\iota_{v}\circ q_{v}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is precisely
\Rist(v)
, and the map

\mathrm{ret}_{v}:=\iota_{v}\circ q_{v}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\rightarrow\Rist(v)

is a retraction Given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we also obtain a retraction

\mathrm{ret}_{n}:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\Rist(n),\quad g\mapsto\prod_{v\in V(T)_{n}}\mathrm{ret}_{v}(g).

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
v\in T(V)
be of type
q
and let
w\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{A}[q]}
. Then for all
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(T)
we have

(16)

\mathrm{ret}_{v}(g)(vw)=v\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}(w).

Proof.

Let us abbreviate
h:=\varphi_{g(v)v}\circ g
, then

\mathrm{ret}_{v}(g)(vw)=(s_{v}(\varphi_{g(v)v}\circ g)|_{T_{v}}))(vw)=s_{v}(h)(vw)=h(vw)=h(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{h}(w).

Now
h(v)=(\varphi_{g(v)v}\circ g)(v)=v
and
\varphi_{g(v)v}=\iota_{v}\circ\iota_{g(v)}^{-1}
. Thus

h(vw)=\varphi_{g(v)v}(g(vw))=\varphi_{g(v)v}(g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}(w))=v\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}(w).\qed

Corollary 5.14
.

Let
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(V)
and
h=\mathrm{ret}_{v}(g)
. Then for all
w\in V(T)
we have

(17)

\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}(h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}(g),&\text{if }w\in V(T_{v}),\\
\mathrm{Id},&\text{else}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

By (
16
) we have
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{h}=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{g}
and thus
\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}(h)=\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}(h)
for all
w\in T_{v}
. If
w\in V(T)\setminus V(T_{v})
and
\alpha\in\Sigma(w)
, then either
w\alpha\not\in V(T_{v})
or
w\alpha=v
. Either way we have
h(w\alpha)=w\alpha
(and
h(w)=w
) since
h\in\Rist(v)
. We thus deduce that

w\alpha=h(w\alpha)=h(w)\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma(h)}(\alpha)=w\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma(h)}(\alpha),

and hence
\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma(h)}=\mathrm{Id}
.
∎

Consequently, if
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(V)
and
h=\mathrm{ret}_{n}(g)
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then

(18)

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g),&\text{if }|v|_{\Sigma}\geq n,\\
\mathrm{Id},&\text{if }|v|_{\Sigma}<n.\end{array}\right.

5.3.
Basic automorphisms

We can now prove the converse of Theorem
5.2
:

Theorem 5.15
.

A portrait defines an automorphism if and only if it is admissible.

Remark 5.16
.

We have already characterized portraits which define an automorphism in Theorem
3.6
and Remark
3.8
. Compared to these previous characterizations, the characterization from Theorem
5.15
is local (i.e. the condition on
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g)
can be checked without any reference to the other permutation
\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}(g)
), and this will allow us to determine the structure of the automorphism group completely.

More explicitly, Theorem
5.15
say that the portrait map defines a bijection

(19)

\sigma:\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\prod_{v\in V(T)}\mathrm{Sym}(\pi_{0}(v)),\quad g\mapsto\sigma(g).

Note that
\sigma
is well-defined by Theorem
5.2
and injective by Remark
3.4
. To prove surjectivity we need to construct explicit automorphisms of
T
and to compute their portraits.

Construction 5.17

(Basic automorphisms)
.

For every
q\in Q
, every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(q)
acts by rooted automorphisms on
A
by fixing all vertices and permuting the edges emanating from
q
, inducing the permutation
\sigma
on labels. This provides an embedding
\mathrm{Sym}(q)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{Aut}_{0}(A)
. We can thus lift every
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(q)
to a unique automorphism
g_{\sigma}
of
T
whose portrait is given by

(20)

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g_{\sigma})}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\sigma,&\text{if }\pi_{0}(v)=q,\\
\mathrm{id},&\text{else}.\end{array}\right.

More generally, given
q\in Q
,
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(q)
and
w\in V(T)
we define

(21)

g_{\sigma}^{w}:=\mathrm{ret}_{w}(g_{\sigma})\in\Rist(w).

By (
20
) and (
17
), the portrait of
g_{\sigma}^{w}
is given by

(22)

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g_{\sigma}^{w})}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\sigma,&\text{if }v\in T_{w}\text{ and }\pi_{0}(v)=q,\\
\mathrm{id},&\text{else}.\end{array}\right.

Note that
g_{\sigma}^{w}
is non-trivial if and only if
\sigma
is non-trivial and
q
is a state of
\mathcal{A}[\pi_{0}(w)]
.

Theorem 5.18
.

Let
G<\mathrm{Aut}(T)
denote the closure of the subgroup generated by the elements
g_{\sigma}^{w}
, where
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(q)\setminus\{\mathrm{Id}\}
for some state
q
of
\mathcal{A}[\pi_{0}(w)]
. Then
\sigma|_{G}
is surjective.

Note that Theorem
5.15
is an immediate consequence. Furthermore, injectivity of the portrait map on
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 5.19
.

The group
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is topologically generated by the elements
g_{\sigma}^{w}
from (
21
), where
\sigma\in\mathrm{Sym}(q)\setminus\{\mathrm{Id}\}
for some state
q
of
\mathcal{A}[\pi_{0}(w)]
.

Concerning the proof of Theorem
5.18
we observe:

Lemma 5.20
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the map

\sigma_{n}:\Rist(n)\cap G\to\mathrm{Sym}(n),\quad g\mapsto(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(g))_{v\in V(T)_{n}}

is surjective.

Proof.

Given
\sigma=(\sigma_{v})_{v\in V(T)_{n}}\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)
we define

g:=\prod_{v\in V(T)_{n}}g_{\sigma_{v}}^{v}\in\Rist(n)\cap G.

If
v\in V(T)_{n}
, then
g
acts on
T_{v}
by
g_{\sigma_{v}}^{v}
, and hence we deduce from (
22
) that

Proof of Theorem

Remark 5.21

Let

Given

We have a group isomorphism

(Structure of the automorphism group)
.

The natural map

\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\lim_{\leftarrow}\mathrm{Aut}(T)/\Rist(n+1),\quad g\mapsto(g\Rist(n+1))_{n\geq 0}

is an isomorphism of topological groups. In particular, as topological groups,

\mathrm{Aut}(T)\cong\lim_{\leftarrow}\mathrm{Sym}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\leq n}.

Proof.

There is a natural bijection

\iota:\lim_{\leftarrow}\mathrm{Sym}_{\leq n}\to\prod_{v\in V(T)}\mathrm{Sym}(\pi_{0}(v)),

such that the composition

\mathrm{Aut}(T)\to\lim_{\leftarrow}\mathrm{Aut}(T)/\Rist(n+1)\to\lim_{\leftarrow}\mathrm{Sym}_{\leq n}\xrightarrow{\iota}\prod_{v\in V(T)}\mathrm{Sym}(\pi_{0}(v))

is just the portrait map. By Theorem

This composition is bijective, as are the second and third map. This implies that also the first map is a bijection, which shows that the maps in the theorem are continuous group isomorphisms. They are then even isomorphisms of topological groups by the open mapping theorem.

\Rist(n)\cong{\lim_{\leftarrow}}(\mathrm{Sym}(n)\ltimes(\cdots(\mathrm{Sym}(n+1)\ltimes(\dots\ltimes\mathrm{Sym}(n+k))\cdots)).\qed

5.5.
Size of the automorphism group

Let
A
be a graph. If
e_{1},e_{2}
are two distinct edges in
A
with the same source and target, then we refer to
(e_{1},e_{2})
as a
double edge
of
A
. (There might be more than two edges between the same source and target. We only ever need to choose a pair of them and call it our double edge.)

Definition 5.27
.

A double edge
(e_{1},e_{2})
in
A
is
recurrent
if there is a loop in
A
that either contains
e_{1}
or
e_{2}
, or from which the common source of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
can be reached.

Theorem 5.28
.

The following are equivalent for a self-similar tree
T
:

(i)

Some covering quotient of
T
contains a recurrent double edge.

(ii)

The Myhill-Nerode graphs of
T
contains a recurrent double edge.

(iii)

\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is infinite.

(iv)

\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is uncountable.

Proof.

Since (i)
\Leftrightarrow
(ii) holds by Proposition
4.6
, it suffices to consider the Myhill-Nerode graph
A
of
T
; we fix a universal covering
\pi:T\to A
and call a vertex
v\in V(T)_{n}

permutable
if
\pi_{0}(v)
is the source of a double edge in
A
.

The portrait map defines a bijection
\mathrm{Aut}(T)\rightarrow\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Sym}(n)
(Theorem
5.15
), and the group
\mathrm{Sym}(n)
is only non-trivial if there exists a permutable vertex
v\in V(T)_{n}
. Thus
\mathrm{Aut}(T)
is finite if there are only finitely many permutable vertices, and uncountable otherwise. This shows that (iii)
\Leftrightarrow
(iv).

If there is a recurrent double edge
(e_{1},e_{2})
emanating from a vertex
q
of
A
, then there are infinitely many paths from the root to
q
, hence
q
has infinitely many preimages under
\pi_{0}
, which are permutable by definition, hence (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iv).
If there is no such edge, then there are only finitely paths to the initial vertex of each double edge, and hence there are only finitely many permutable vertices, which shows by contraposition that (iii)
\Rightarrow
(ii).
∎

Corollary 5.29
.

The cardinality of the automorphism group of a self-sinilar tree is either finite or the cardinality of the continuum.
