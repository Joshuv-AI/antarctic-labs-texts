An Algebraic Approach to Learning and Grounding
Johanna Björklund, Adam Dahlgren Lindström, Frank Drewes

Abstract
We consider the problem of learning the semantics of composite algebraic expressions from examples. The outcome is a versatile framework for studying learning tasks that can be put into the following abstract form: The input is a partial algebra 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} and a finite set of examples ( φ 1 , O 1 ) , ( φ 2 , O 2 ) , … (\varphi_{1},O_{1}),(\varphi_{2},O_{2}),\ldots , each consisting of an algebraic term φ i \varphi_{i} and a set of objects O i O_{i} . The objective is to simultaneously fill in the missing algebraic operations in 𝒜 {\mathcal{A}} and ground the variables of every φ i \varphi_{i} in O i O_{i} , so that the combined value of the terms is optimised. We demonstrate the applicability of this framework through case studies in grammatical inference, picture-language learning, and the grounding of logic scene descriptions.

Introduction

Modern natural language processing literature, and deep learning-based methods in particular, is critiqued for using terms like learning, grounding, meaning, and understanding loosely without clear definitions
(
Bender and Koller 2020
)
.
Large language models do not learn representations that are sufficient for the applications they are used for, resulting in, e.g., perpetuation of discriminatory bias
(
Bender et al. 2021
)
.
Successful representational systems are usually modular, in that they describe complex concepts by combining representations for simpler ones. Examples include first-order predicate logic, where predicates, variables, and connectives are assembled into logic expressions, or natural language (NL), where lexical and grammatical constructs are the building blocks of sentences, which in turn combine to paragraphs, and so on.
When learning the components of a representational system from real-world observations, we seldom observe instances of a concept in isolation. Even in picture books for children, an image that is intended to convey the notion of a shape may also be understood to stand for, e.g., a colour or a size, so the child may need several observations to grasp the concept. We are therefore interested in exploring the conditions under which composite semantics can be learnt by example.

To this end, we introduce the notion of a
template algebra
. This is an algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
that is defined with respect to a set of typed operator symbols
\Sigma
. The algebra is non-standard in that (i) there is a dedicated
evaluation type

\tau
whose domain in
{\mathcal{A}}
is a linearly ordered set, and (ii) a subset of the operators for the symbols in
\Sigma
have been left undefined. Given a set of terms over
\Sigma
of type
\tau
, the learning task is to choose the missing operators (from families of candidate ones) so that the combined value of the terms is optimal.

To integrate domains that are not finitely generated, the terms may contain variables which are assigned values from a target domain during evaluation. With this addition, a learning example is a pair
(\varphi,O)
, where
\varphi
is a
\Sigma
-term with variables, and
O
is a set of suitable typed elements that can be substituted for the variables. Again, the goal is to find an optimal solution over the entire set of examples, but the task has become more complicated, as the learning algorithm must simultaneously decide what object to map to what variable, and what operators to choose for those missing. Coming back to the picture book example, this correspond to simultaneously inferring what ‘red’ means in a phrase like “Look at the red ball” and what object in the picture constitutes the red ball.

This second subtask, which can be described as linking abstract representations to target domains, is known as
grounding
. Other examples of grounding are connecting symbols on a map to features of the terrain, and to link words in a natural-language search query to tables, columns, and threshold values in a database. Also when training is done and the algebra complete, grounding can still remain challenging for unseen examples. However, it is likely that the more accurately the system has learnt the target operations, the better it is positioned also to solve the remaining problem of grounding, because it can more efficiently limit the search space when mapping objects to variables.

The purpose of our framework is to provide flexible means for setting up formally defined symbolic, neural, or neural-symbolic tasks for learning from examples (where the emphasis is on tasks involving neural components). Moreover, this is done in such a way that corpora – sets of examples of the type defined by the task – can be generated automatically using grammars and automata. In summary, our approach provides a unified way to

•

formalize learning tasks that involve mixtures of symbolic and neural components,

•

study the problem of grounding as an integral part of other learning tasks, and

•

automatically generate sets of examples for studying proposed learning algorithms.

To demonstrate the framework’s ability to support a variety of classical and modern learning approaches, we discuss instantiations of the framework in three learning scenarios. The first
is the grammatical inference of regular string language from characteristic sets. The second considers picture-language learning and involves the estimation of affine transformations with neural networks. The final instantiation serves to ground the variables of a logic scene description in geometric objects, by training neural networks to recognise atomic properties of the objects such as colour, shape, and size. This ability to integrate finite and infinite-state methods makes the proposed framework a contribution to the field of neuro-symbolic reasoning.

1.1
Related work

Learning and grounding is addressed in symbolic, neural, and neuro-symbolic settings throughout the literature.
Our framework provides a way to systematically study the different methods and how they approach the grounding problem.
In this section we outline important work in the respective fields.

Visual grounding has been addressed in neuro-symbolic machine learning in various ways.
Inductive Logic Programming (ILP,
Muggleton and De Raedt 1994
) is a form of symbolic learning and reasoning with first-order clauses for learning relations in data.
Background knowledge is used together with examples to induce a hypothesis, in the form of a logic program, describing positive and negative examples in given data.
ILP is applied to, e.g., scientific discovery, robotics, program analysis
(
Cropper et al. 2022
)
, with neuro-symbolic approaches to machine vision problems
(
Dai et al. 2015
;
Varghese et al. 2021
)
.

Probabilistic logic programming (PLP
Dantsin 1992
;
Ng and Subrahmanian 1992
), provides reasoning under uncertainty, with methods such as
De Raedt et al. 2007
.

Raedt and Kersting 2008
provides an overview of the combination of PLP and ILP, Probabilistic ILP.
Neuro-symbolic PLP, such as DeepProbLog, introduces predicates that are realised by neural networks to be trained, as a way to ground and reason about visual concepts
(
Manhaeve et al. 2018
;
Weber et al. 2019
;
Winters et al. 2021
)
.
In the Neuro-Symbolic Concept Learner by
Mao et al. 2019
, programs are taught to perform visual question answering by combining a neural perception module, a semantic parsing module to construct programs from language, and a program executor which bridges these components to provide answers.
The idea of Neural State Machines by
Hudson and Manning 2019
builds on probabilistic graphs to learn and reason about concepts for visual question answering.
Logic Tensor Networks
(
Serafini and Garcez 2016
;
Badreddine et al. 2022
)
have been used for semantic image interpretation as a partial grounding problem
(
Donadello et al. 2017
)
.

Garcez et al. 2019
gives an overview of how learning and reasoning can be integrated, and how logical representations are used for that purpose.

Answer Set Programming (ASP) is another approach to knowledge representation and reasoning based on searching for stable models, answer sets, as solutions to given logic programs
(
Niemelä 1999
;
Marek and Truszczyński 1999
;
Lifschitz 2002
)
.
ASP is used in decision support systems, planning robotics, and many other domains
(
Erdem et al. 2016
)
, with neuro-symbolic examples in reasoning about objects in video
(
Suchan et al. 2018
)
.

Finally, grounding in language and vision with neural networks is extensively researched for tasks such as Visual Question Answering (VQA)
(
Antol et al. 2015
;
Fukui et al. 2016
;
Chaplot et al. 2018
)
.
Neuro-symbolic approaches to VQA include
(
Yi et al. 2018
)
.

The above domains all have different perspectives on learning and grounding, and there are overlaps, such as between ILP and PLP.
We can formalise the described methods and the problems they try to solve, to examine the similarities, challenges, and opportunities.

An alphabet is a finite nonempty set. Given an alphabet

A string language is a subset of

We denote a (partial)

is defined. The language

is defined in the standard way:

. A string language is

Let

be a string language and

. To simplify notation, may use

to denote the statement

. The

into a set of congruence classes

is finite if and only if

is regular. A string

is

(with respect to

. A congruence class in

is live if it contains a live string. If

is regular, the canonical DFA recognizing

is

where

and

. (Note that we consider the partial canonical DFA

that contains only the live congruence classes as states.)

Terms and Algebras

For a set
\Gamma

such that
\Sigma
is a finite set of symbols and
\mathit{type}_{\Sigma}\colon\Sigma\to\Gamma^{+}

. If

.

We extend the evaluation of terms to terms with variables in a standard manner. For this, let

(where

.

2.

If

\varphi(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})=f(\mathrm{val}_{\mathcal{A}}^{X_{\ell}}(t_{1})(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell}),\dots,\mathrm{val}_{\mathcal{A}}^{X_{\ell}}(t_{k})(a_{1},\dots,a_{\ell})

Note that this definition is consistent with the evaluation of terms without variables when

3
Learning from Algebraic Expressions

We now formalise an algebraic framework for learning and grounding.
Let
\Sigma
be a
\gamma
-typed alphabet. A
template
\Sigma
-algebra (over
\Gamma
)
is defined just like a
\Sigma
-algebra over
\Gamma
, with the exception that
f_{\mathcal{A}}
is undefined for some of the symbols
f\in\Sigma
. These

represent the functions to be learned. An
instance
of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is a
\Sigma
-algebra such that
f_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}=f_{\mathcal{A}}
for all
f\in\Sigma
for which
f_{\mathcal{A}}
is defined.

Consider a template
\Sigma
-algebra as above, and assume that a type
\tau\in\Gamma
is designated as the
evaluation type
, for which an additional linear order
\leq
on
{\mathbb{A}}_{\tau}
is specified, as well as a summation operation
\bigoplus
that is well-defined on every finite subset of
{\mathbb{A}}_{\tau}
. Thus, every finite subset of
{\mathbb{A}}_{\tau}
has uniquely defined maxima and minima. An
example
is a pair
(\varphi,O)
consisting of a term
\varphi\in X_{\ell}]^{\tau}
and a set
O\subseteq\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}{\mathbb{A}}_{\gamma}
. Suppose that
\mathit{type}_{X_{\ell}}(x_{i})=\gamma_{i}
. Then the set
[O\to X_{\ell}]
of all
groundings of
O
is the set of all injective mappings
g\colon X_{\ell}\to O
such that
g(x_{i})\in{\mathbb{A}}_{\gamma_{i}}
for all
i\in[\ell]
. Given an instance
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
, the
value
of an example
S=(\varphi,O)
is

\mathrm{val}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(S)=\opt_{g\in[O\to X_{l}]}\mathrm{val}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(\varphi)(g(x_{1}),\dots,g(x_{\ell}))\kern 5.0pt,

(1)

where
\opt\in\{\min,\max\}
is a specified optimisation criterion.

Given a finite set
\mathcal{S}
of examples, the learning goal is to find an instance
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
that optimises the total value

\bigoplus_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{val}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(S)

(2)

of all examples. Since the desired outcome
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
depend on which algebras
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
are admissible solutions, these must be made explicit when formalising the learning task. In particular, for every
f\colon\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{k}\to\gamma
in
\Sigma
such that
f_{{\mathcal{A}}}
is undefined, we specify a set
\mathcal{F}_{f}
of candidate functions
g\colon{\mathbb{A}}_{\gamma_{1}}\times\cdots\times{\mathbb{A}}_{\gamma_{k}}\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\gamma}
for
f_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
.

4
Example Instantiations of the Framework

To illustrate how the algebraic framework of Section
3
can be instantiated to capture various scenarios for algorithmic learning, we now describe three application domains.

4.1
Regular languages

The first instance we consider is the grammatical inference of regular languages from finite data. If the data constitutes a
characteristic set

(
de la Higuera 1997
)
, then a finite automaton for the target language can be computed in polynomial time in the size of that set
(
Oncina and Garcia 1992
)
.
Intuitively, a characteristic set contains strings that represent every state and every transition of the unique minimal DFA for the target language. We discuss a similar setting using our framework, in order to illustrate how traditional automata learning fits into it.

Throughout the remainder of this subsection, let
\Xi
be a finite, totally ordered, set of symbols, and
L\subseteq\Xi^{*}
be a fixed but arbitrary regular language.
Let the set of types be
\Gamma=\{\alpha,\beta\}
, the
\Gamma
-typed alphabet be

, where
{\mathbb{A}}_{\alpha}=\Xi^{*}
and
{\mathbb{A}}_{\beta}=\{\mathrm{false},\mathrm{true}\}
. The operation
\neg_{\mathcal{A}}
is logic negation. The operations
\mathit{accept}_{\mathcal{A}}
and
\mathit{equiv}_{\mathcal{A}}
are both undefined: The characteristic function

for the language

is our learning target, and

will be instantiated to model the Nerode congruence

of

. Thus, admissible instantiations are algebras

in which
\mathit{accept}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}
is the characteristic function of a regular language

and
\mathit{equiv}_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}

Definition 2
.

Let

, and let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on

of

is the smallest equivalence relation
{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}}\supseteq{\sim}
on

such that, for all

(where

and

Note that, for finite

We have the following lemma:

Lemma 3
.

{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}}={\sim_{L}}\cap(\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})\times\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S}))

Proof.

Since right completion is monotone and

{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\subseteq{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{L}}={\sim_{L}}
, where the equality holds because

is a right congruence. Here,
{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\subseteq\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})\times\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})

{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}}={\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\cap(\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})\times\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S}))\subseteq{\sim_{L}}\cap(\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})\times\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S}))
.

For the other inclusion, assume that

are such that

. Due to Condition

and

must either both be in

or both be in

with

, or else

would constitute a convergence and thus

. Assume that

are of minimal length with this property. Then

, but this cannot be because

Definition 4

(DFA construction)
.

Let

, let
\sim
be an equivalence relation on

, and let

. The DFA
\textsc{Aut}(S,\sim,F)
is given by
(\Xi,Q,\delta,q_{0},Q_{F})
where

•

,

•

\delta
is the partial transition function such that
\delta(B,\xi)
is defined and equal to

if

for some

,

•

, and

•

.

With the preceding definition, we can now use
\mathcal{S}
to build a DFA that recognises

Lemma 5
.

Let
F_{\mathcal{S}}=\{w\in\mathit{str}(\mathcal{S})\mid(\mathit{accept}[x],\{w\})\in\mathcal{S}\}
. Then the DFA
M_{\mathcal{S}}=\textsc{Aut}(\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S}),\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}},F_{\mathcal{S}})
is isomorphic to

.

Proof.

Let
M_{L}=(\Xi,Q^{\prime},\delta^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime},F^{\prime})
and
M_{\mathcal{S}}=(\Xi,Q,\delta,q_{0},F)
. By Lemma
3
,
{\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}}\subseteq{\sim_{L}}
, so every state
q\in Q
is a subset of some (by Lemma
1
live) congruence class in
(\Xi^{*}/{\sim_{L}})
, i.e., of a state
q^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
. Also by Lemma
3
,
w\not\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}w^{\prime}
implies
w\not\sim_{L}w^{\prime}
. Hence, the mapping
\pi\colon Q\to Q^{\prime}
such that
q\subseteq\pi(q)
for all

is well defined and injective.

Lemma
1
together with the constructions of
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
(via Definition
4
and the Myhill-Nerode construction, respectively), yield the following for all
q\in Q
and
\xi\in\Xi

\delta(q,\xi)
is defined if and only if
\delta^{\prime}(\pi(q),\xi)
is defined, and in this case it holds that
\delta^{\prime}(\pi(q),\xi)=\pi(\delta(q,\xi))
.
Moreover,
\pi(q_{0})=q_{0}^{\prime}
because
\varepsilon\in q_{0}\cap q_{0}^{\prime}
. Finally, a state

is in

if and only if

, which by the definition of

and Condition

is the case if and only if

results from the Myhill-Nerode construction, the latter is equivalent to

. Hence, altogether,

and

are isomorphic.
∎

Note that the construction of

does not depend on the existence of distinguishing examples according to Condition

would stay unchanged even if those examples would be absent, the theorem we prove now would fail.

Theorem 6
.

Let
L
be a regular language and
M_{L}=(\Xi,Q,\delta,q_{0},F)
. Then

{\mathcal{A}}_{L}
maximizes (
2
). Furthermore, it is minimal in the following sense: if
M=(\Xi,Q^{\prime},\delta^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime},F^{\prime})
is another DFA such that
{\mathcal{A}}_{L(M)}
maximizes (
2
), then there is an injective mapping
\pi\colon Q\to Q^{\prime}
such that
\pi(q_{0})=q_{0}^{\prime}
,
\delta^{\prime}(\pi(q),\xi)=\pi(\delta(q,\xi))
for all
(q,\xi)\in\mathrm{dom}(\delta)
, and
\pi(F)\subseteq F^{\prime}
. In particular,

.

Proof.

It is straightforward to verify that the instantiation
{\mathcal{A}}_{L}
ensures that all examples attain the value
\mathrm{true}
. Hence,
{\mathcal{A}}_{L}
maximizes (
2
).

For the rest of the proof, let
M_{\mathcal{S}}=\textsc{Aut}(\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S}),\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}},F_{\mathcal{S}})
. By Lemma
5
we prove the statement of the theorem for

instead of
M_{L}
, i.e., the states are subsets of
\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})
instead of congruence classes of
(\Xi^{*}/{\sim_{L}})
. Let
M
be another DFA, as in the statement of the theorem. Instead of
M
, we shall for the time being consider the canonical
total
DFA
M_{0}=(\Xi,Q_{0}^{\prime},\delta_{0}^{\prime},q_{0}^{\prime},F^{\prime})
recognising
L(M)
. Thus, we can assume that
M
is obtained from
M_{0}
by removing the sink state if there is one, and
M=M_{0}
otherwise.

Every state
q\in Q
is a subset of a state in
Q_{0}^{\prime}
because

(a)

for all
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})
the implications
w\sim_{\mathcal{S}}w^{\prime}\Rightarrow(\mathit{equiv}(x,y),\{w,w^{\prime}\})\in\mathcal{S}\Rightarrow w\sim_{L(M_{0})}w^{\prime}
hold (the latter since
M
maximises (
2
)),

(b)

\sim_{L(M_{0})}
is a right congruence and

(c)

\sim^{\mathrm{rc}}_{\mathcal{S}}
is the smallest right equivalence on
\mathit{prefixes}(\mathcal{S})
that contains
\sim_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Let thus
\pi\colon Q\to Q_{0}^{\prime}
be the mapping such that
q\subseteq\pi(q)
for all
q\in Q
. Then
\pi
is injective: if there would be two distinct states
q,q^{\prime}\in Q
such that
q^{\prime}\subseteq\pi(q)
then
M_{0}
(and thus

) would fail to satisfy one of the examples
(\neg\mathit{equiv}(x,y),O)
contained in
\mathcal{S}
owing to Condition

, and would thus not maximise (
2
). Furthermore, by Condition

we have
\pi(q)\in F_{\mathcal{S}}
for all
q\in F
, and since
\varepsilon\in q_{0}\cap q_{0}^{\prime}
, we also have
\pi(q_{0})=q_{0}^{\prime}
.

Now recall that
\delta_{0}^{\prime}
is the transition function defined by the Myhill-Nerode construction. Thus, for all
q\in Q_{0}
,

, and

it holds that
\delta_{0}^{\prime}(q,a)=q^{\prime}
if

. Since this is just how

is defined, it follows that
\delta_{0}(\pi(q),\xi)=\pi(\delta(q,\xi))
for all

. In particular, all states of the form

,

, are live in

∎

4.2
Picture Languages

The second example instantiation of the algebraic framework allows us to infer picture languages of the type described by
Drewes 2006
.
These language are generated by grammatical systems whose rules transform and combine images to more complex ones: A
collage grammar
is a pair
G=(g,\mathcal{C})
consisting of

(1)

a regular tree grammar
g
such that
L(g)\subseteq p
for some type
p
and a
\{p\}
-typed alphabet
\Sigma
, and

(2)

a so-called collage algebra
\mathcal{C}=(\mathbb{C},(F_{\mathcal{C}})_{F\in\Sigma})
.

The components of such a collage algebra
\mathcal{C}
are given as follows:

•

\mathbb{C}
is the set of all compact subsets of
2
, here referred to as
pictures
.
2
2

2

A subset of
2
is compact if it is bounded (a subset of a disc) and contains all limits of Cauchy sequences in it.

•

The interpretation of every
F\colon p
in
\Sigma
is an element
F_{\mathcal{C}}
of
\mathbb{C}
.

•

The interpretation of every
F\colon p^{k}\to p
with
k>1
, is given by
k
affine transformation
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
of
2
, as follows:
F_{\mathcal{C}}(C_{1},\dots,C_{k})=\bigcup_{i\in[k]}f_{i}(C_{i})
. Thus,
F
transforms its
i
th argument by
f_{i}
and returns the union of the resulting
k
pictures. We also denote such a function
F_{\mathcal{C}}
by
\langle f_{1}\cdots f_{k}\rangle
.

The language generated by
G
is
L(G)=\{\mathrm{val}_{\mathcal{C}}(t)\mid t\in L(g)\}
.

is depicted in Figure

Suppose we want to learn
G
from examples. We obviously have to learn both
g
and the interpretation of the symbols appearing in the terms in
L(g)
, i.e., we have to learn
\mathcal{C}
. Learning
g
is an interesting task that has been considered in many research articles. However, since this problem is similar to Section
4.1
, we do not consider it here, focusing instead on the second component. We discuss one way in which our framework can be used to formalise and study this learning task.

Consider the operation
F_{\mathcal{C}}=\langle f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}\rangle
in our example. To learn it, we need to learn each of the affine transformations
f_{i}
defining it. By the definition of affine transformations,
f_{i}(x)=M_{i}x+b_{i}
where
M_{i}
is a
2\times 2
matrix with real coefficients and
b\in\real^{2}
. This is just the type of mapping a single linear layer of a neural network
\mathcal{N}
can implement. Hence, we propose to use our framework to study how this can be done. For the learning, we may assume that all operations except
F_{\mathcal{A}}
are known, i.e., we consider the template algebra
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
which is equal to
\mathcal{C}
with the exception that
F_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
is undefined. We can use the regular tree grammar
g
to generate examples from which the network is supposed to learn
F_{\mathcal{A}}
. Intuitively, each example is a pair consisting of a term and its evaluation according to
\mathcal{C}
. However, this does not yet fulfil the requirements of our general definition. What is missing is a way to measure how good a job
\mathcal{N}
does. In other words, we need a distance measure on
\mathbb{C}
. For this, we may for example use the well-known Hausdorff measure
(
Hausdorff 1918
)
, the more recent Earth Mover’s distance by
Rubner et al. 2000
, or, if we restrict ourselves to pictures consisting of finite unions of simple geometric shapes, the area of the symmetric difference between pictures. Let
\delta
denote such a distance measure, and let
\mathcal{C}_{\delta}
be
\mathcal{C}
extended by this operation, which is of type
\delta\colon\mathbb{C}\mathbb{C}\to\real
. (For simplicity, we blur the distinction between the symbol
\delta
and its interpretation.) Now, to stay within our framework, we can use examples of the form
(\delta[x_{1},t],\mathrm{val}_{\mathcal{C}}(t))
, where

. In fact, as long as we are not concerned with learning
g
as well, the requirement that

is superfluous. Furthermore, we can enrich
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
with further shapes than only
C
, making it possible to use examples that simplify learning.

can learn

\mathcal{N}
, using as a loss function the average of the losses of individual examples. Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{N}}

by interpreting

according to
\mathcal{N}
. The loss of a single example

The final instantiation of the algebraic framework serves to learn (predicates representing) properties of physical objects, so as to be able to ground the variables of a logic scene description in a scene. For simplicity, we limit the discussion to the type of synthetic scenes provided by
Johnson et al. 2017
through the CLEVR dataset. In this dataset, a scene is a configuration of a limited number of geometrical objects of different shapes, colors, materials, and sizes. For the present, we also disregard the relative positioning of objects within the scene, even though such relations could be modelled by an extension of the logic described in the following. A final simplification is the assumption that the scene has already been segmented and every object found has been translated into a real-valued vector
o
by a pretrained visual embedding model. A scene is thus represented as a multiset of such vectors, and these multisets take the place of the objects in examples.

5
Conclusion and future work

We have introduced an algebraic framework that can be instantiated for a variety of learning settings, allowing us to integrate grammatical inference and deep-learning approaches. As indicated throughout the paper, this is an initial theoretical effort, but framework lends itself well to empirical experiments, and this is also the the natural next step. One of the main advantages of the algebraic approach is that it allows us to look at a learning setting through a layer of abstraction. This makes it possible to treat various aspects in isolation, and opens for a wealth of research directions.

In general, we may ask what properties of the algebraic domains and operations promote learning. We may also consider the effect of different valuation schemes, e.g., should we allow that two variables are assigned the same logic object, or require that every object is assigned to some variable. We may also study the effect of adding universal and existential quantifiers, and so requiring that certain properties hold for all or some of the elements in an example. Another question is the trade-off between computational complexity and expressiveness that comes from learning predicates of arity greater than one. Such an addition would allow us to learn relations, e.g., whether one object is smaller than another, but would doubtlessly also require greater computational effort.

In the cases where we apply deep learning, it is natural to explore different forms of object representations and network architectures, but also the realisation of logic connectives in a numerical domain. Finally, we may also consider how we can manage a situation where the number of elements in an example is very large (consider, e.g., a painting of a city scene in great detail). Here, it may not be feasible to try every possible way of linking the variables in the accompanying formula to a segment of the scene, so one may devise various heuristics of limiting the number of combinations to evaluate.
