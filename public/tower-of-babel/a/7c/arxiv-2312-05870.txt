Critical dynamics within the real-time fRG approach
Yong-rui Chen, Yang-yang Tan, Wei-jie Fu

Abstract
The Schwinger-Keldysh functional renormalization group (fRG) developed in Tan et al. 2022a is employed to investigate critical dynamics related to a second-order phase transition. The effective action of model A is expanded to the order of O ( ∂ 2 ) O(\partial^{2}) in the derivative expansion for the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) symmetry. By solving the fixed-point equations of effective potential and wave function, we obtain static and dynamic critical exponents for different values of the spatial dimension d d and the field component number N N . It is found that one has z ≥ 2 z\geq 2 in the whole range of 2 ≤ d ≤ 4 2\leq d\leq 4 for the case of N = 1 N=1 , while in the case of N = 4 N=4 the dynamic critical exponent turns to z < 2 z<2 when the dimension approach towards d = 2 d=2 .



I
Introduction

Non-equilibrium critical dynamics might play a significant role, when quark-gluon plasma (QGP) produced in relativistic heavy ion collisions evolves into the critical region of the critical end point (CEP) in the QCD phase diagram
Son and Stephanov 2004
;
Fu 2022
;
Luo et al. 2022
;
Arslandok et al. 2023
, though recently it has been found the critical region of QCD is extremely small
Braun et al. 2023a
. This is quite relevant for the search of CEP in experiments under way currently at, e.g., Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC)
Luo and Xu 2017
;
Adam et al. 2021
and other facilities. In the critical region, dynamics is dominated by the massless modes, and the significantly increased correlation length results in the well-known critical slowing down
Rajagopal and Wilczek 1993
;
Berdnikov and Rajagopal 2000
. On the other hand, the dynamics in the critical region is simplified, since it does not depend on the details of interactions of different systems, but is rather governed by some universal properties, which has been discussed in detail in the seminal paper
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
.

In early studies of critical dynamics, perturbation techniques are usually adopted, for example the
\epsilon
-expansion
Halperin et al. 1972
;
Halperin et al. 1974
;
Halperin et al. 1976
or the
1/N
expansion
Ma 1972
. In recent years many nonperturbative methods have been utilized. Real-time lattice simulations in classical-statistical field theory are used to calculate spectral functions and critical dynamics
Berges et al. 2010
;
Schlichting et al. 2020
;
Schweitzer et al. 2020
;
Schweitzer et al. 2022
;
Florio et al. 2022
;
Florio et al. 2023
. Real-time correlation functions, spectral functions, dynamic critical exponent, dissipation dynamics, etc., are investigated within the real-time functional renormalization group (fRG) approach
Tan et al. 2022a
;
Huelsmann et al. 2020
;
Roth et al. 2022
;
Roth and von
Smekal 2023
;
Batini et al. 2023
, or based on analytically continued fRG flows
Jung et al. 2021
. Moreover, spectral representations of correlation functions have recently been combined with the fRG and Dyson-Schwinger Equations (DSE), which are now known as the spectral fRG
Braun et al. 2023b
;
Horak et al. 2023a
or spectral DSE
Horak et al. 2020
;
Horak et al. 2021
;
Horak et al. 2022
;
Horak et al. 2023b
.

In our former work
Tan et al. 2022a
, we have developed the formalism of fRG formulated on the Schwinger-Keldysh closed time path
Schwinger 1961
;
Keldysh 1964
, see also, e.g.,
Chou et al. 1985
;
Blaizot and Iancu 2002
;
Berges 2004
;
Sieberer et al. 2016
for some relevant reviews about the Schwinger-Keldysh path integral. By the use of the Keldysh rotation, one is able to express the real-time effective action in terms of two different fields: One is referred to as the “classical” field and the other “quantum” field. The relevant diagram techniques were also devised there
Tan et al. 2022a
.

In this work we would like to employ the Schwinger-Keldysh fRG in
Tan et al. 2022a
to study the critical dynamics of a dissipative relaxation model, that is classified as model A in
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
. We will employ the method of derivative expansion to make truncation for the real-time effective action, which is usually used in the studies of static critical properties, cf.
Balog et al. 2019
;
De Polsi et al. 2020
. The action is expanded to the order of
O(\partial^{2})
for the
O(N)
symmetry. Fixed-point equations will be utilized to investigate the critical dynamics of the relaxation model.

This paper is organized as follows: In Sec.
II
the dynamic model and its representation in the Schwinger-Keldysh fRG are presented. In Sec.
III
we discuss the flow equations of the effective potential, wave function and the kinetic coefficient. Numerical results are presented and discussed in Sec.
IV
. In Sec.
V
we show our summary and conclusions. In App.
A
we show the Feynman rules for the propagators and vertices in the Schwinger-Keldysh fRG. In App.
B
the flow of effective potential in the mesoscopic relaxation model and that in the microscopic theory are compared.

II
Dynamic model within the real-time fRG approach

We begin with the dissipative relaxation model with no conservation laws, which is classified as model A in the seminal paper
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
. The equation of motion for the scalar field of
N
components
\phi_{a}
with
a=0,1,\cdots N-1
is described by the Langevin equation, viz.,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\phi_{a}(x,t)}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle-\Gamma\frac{\delta F[\phi]}{\delta\phi_{a}}+\eta_{a}(x,t)\,,

(1)

with a functional of fields for the free energy

\displaystyle F[\phi]=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}^{d}x\left(\frac{1}{2}Z_{\phi}(\rho)(\partial_{i}\phi_{a})(\partial_{i}\phi_{a})+V(\rho)-c\sigma\right)\,.

(2)

where
Z_{\phi}(\rho)
and
V(\rho)
are the field-dependent wave function and effective potential, respectively. The notations
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial x^{i}
,
\rho=\phi^{2}/2
with
\phi^{2}=\phi_{a}\phi_{a}
are used, and summation is assumed for repeated indices. Note that the
O(N)
symmetry in Eq.
2
is explicitly broken by the last linear term in
\sigma\equiv\phi_{a=0}
, with the breaking strength
c
. In Eq.
1
the diffusion constant
\Gamma
describes the relaxation rate and the last term denotes the Gaussian white noises with vanishing mean value, i.e.,
\langle\eta_{a}(x,t)\rangle=0
, and nonzero two-point correlations, as follows

\displaystyle\langle\eta_{a}(x,t)\eta_{a^{\prime}}(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=

\displaystyle\,2\Gamma\,T\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta(t-t^{\prime})\delta_{aa^{\prime}}\,,

(3)

with the temperature
T
.

In this work, we would like to study the critical dynamics of model A within the functional renormalization group formulated on the Schwinger-Keldysh closed time path. The real-time fRG with the Schwinger-Keldysh path integral and the relevant techniques thereof have been discussed in detail in our former work
Tan et al. 2022a
, see also
Martin et al. 1973
;
Canet and Chaté 2007
. Following the approach there, one is able to arrive at the renormalization group (RG) scale dependent effective action corresponding to Eq.
1
with Eq.
2
, that is,

\displaystyle\Gamma_{k}[\phi_{c},\phi_{q}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}^{d}x\,\bigg\{Z_{t,k}\phi_{a,q}i(\partial_{t}\phi_{a,c})+iZ_{\phi,k}(\rho_{c})(\partial_{i}\phi_{a,q})(\partial_{i}\phi_{a,c})

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{4}Z^{\prime}_{\phi,k}(\rho_{c})\phi_{a,q}\phi_{a,c}(\partial_{i}\phi_{b,c})(\partial_{i}\phi_{b,c})+iV^{\prime}_{k}(\rho_{c})\phi_{a,q}\phi_{a,c}

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2}ic\sigma_{q}-2i\,Z_{t,k}T\phi_{a,q}^{2}\bigg\}\,,

(4)

with
\partial_{t}=\partial/\partial t
, where
\phi_{c}
and
\phi_{q}
stand for the “classical” and “quantum” fields, respectively. Note that in Eq.
4
there are derivatives of the wave function and the effective potential, i.e.,

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}_{\phi,k}(\rho_{c})=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial Z_{\phi,k}(\rho_{c})}{\partial\rho_{c}}\,,\qquad V^{\prime}_{k}(\rho_{c})=\frac{\partial V_{k}(\rho_{c})}{\partial\rho_{c}}\,,

(5)

with
\rho_{c}=\phi_{c}^{2}/4
. The action here is expanded to the order of
O(\partial^{2}_{i})
in the derivative expansion. The kinetic coefficient
Z_{t,k}
in Eq.
4
is related to the relaxation rate in Eq.
1
through
Z_{t,k}\sim 1/\Gamma
. Quantities with suffix
k
indicate their dependence on the RG scale. From the effective action in the Schwinger-Keldysh field theory in Eq.
4
, one is able to obtain the retarded, advanced, and Keldysh propagators, three- and four-point vertices, etc., which are discussed in detail in
Appendix

A
.

The flow equation of effective potential can be obtained from the flow of one-point function as shown in Fig.
1
. By employing the Feynman rules for the propagators and vertices in
Appendix

A
, one is able to obtain the flow equation of effective potential (first-order derivative w.r.t. the field), to wit,

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}V^{\prime}_{k}(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\nu_{d}}{2}Tk^{d}\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}xx^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{2-\eta(1-x)}{\big(z_{\phi}(\rho)-1\big)x+1+\bar{m}_{\sigma}^{2}}

\displaystyle+(N-1)\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}xx^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{2-\eta(1-x)}{\big(z_{\phi}(\rho)-1\big)x+1+\bar{m}_{\pi}^{2}}\bigg\}\,,

(6)

where we have used
\rho
in replace of
\rho_{c}
without ambiguity. The RG time is
\tau=\ln(k/\Lambda)
with
\Lambda
being some reference scale, e.g., an ultraviolet cutoff. The angular integral in
d
dimension gives rise to a constant
\nu_{d}=1/[(4\pi)^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)]
, where
\Gamma(d/2)
is the gamma function. In Eq.
6
one also has

\displaystyle z_{\phi}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\phi,k}(\rho)}{Z_{\phi,k}}\,,

(7)

with
Z_{\phi,k}=Z_{\phi,k}(\rho_{0})
, that is field-independent, and
\rho_{0}
is usually chosen to be the position of the minimum of potential, i.e.,
V^{\prime}_{k}(\rho_{0})=0
. The anomalous dimension reads

\displaystyle\eta=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial_{\tau}Z_{\phi,k}}{Z_{\phi,k}}\,.

(8)

The dimensionless renormalized meson masses in Eq.
6
read

\displaystyle\bar{m}_{\sigma}^{2}=

\displaystyle Z_{\phi,k}^{-1}k^{-2}m_{\sigma}^{2}\,,\qquad\bar{m}_{\pi}^{2}=Z_{\phi,k}^{-1}k^{-2}m_{\pi}^{2}\,,

(9)

where the bare masses are shown in Eq.
A
. In
Appendix

B
it has been demonstrated that the flow of effective potential in the mesoscopic relaxation model in Eq.
6
corresponds to the high temperature limit of the flow of effective potential in the microscopic Klein-Gordon theory
Tan et al. 2022a
.

In fact in order to investigate scaling properties of Eq.
6
, it is more convenient to adopt dimensionless renormalized variables, such as

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}=

\displaystyle Z_{\phi,k}T^{-1}k^{2-d}\rho\,,\qquad u(\bar{\rho})=T^{-1}k^{-d}V_{k}(\rho)\,.

(10)

Then, one is left with

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-2+\eta)u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})+(-2+d+\eta)\bar{\rho}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})-\frac{\nu_{d}}{2}

\displaystyle\times\bigg\{\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\big[2-\eta(1-x)\big]\big[z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})x+3u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})

\displaystyle+2\bar{\rho}u^{(3)}(\bar{\rho})\big]\big[\big(z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})-1\big)x+1+u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})+2\bar{\rho}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\big]^{-2}

\displaystyle+(N-1)\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\big[2-\eta(1-x)\big]\big[z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})x+u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\big]

\displaystyle\times\big[\big(z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})-1\big)x+1+u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\big]^{-2}\bigg\}\,.

(11)

The flows of the wave function renormalization
Z_{\phi,k}(\rho_{c})
and the kinetic coefficient
Z_{t,k}
in the effective action in Eq.
4
can be extracted from the flow equation of the two-point function, e.g., the inverse retarded propagator in Eq.
27
. The flow of the inverse retarded propagator is shown in Fig.
2
. Inserting the different propagators, three- and four-point vertices in App.
A
into the flow equation, one is able to close the equations. The computation is straightforward, though a bit tedious. It is obvious from Eq.
27
that the flow of the wave function renormalization can be obtained by performing the projection as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Z_{\phi,k}(\rho)=

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0}\rightarrow 0\\
\bm{p}\rightarrow 0\end{subarray}}(-i)\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{p}^{2}}\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]}{\delta\phi_{a,q}(-p)\delta\phi_{a,c}(p)}\bigg|_{\Phi_{\mathrm{EoM}}}\,.

(12)

Note that there is no summation for the index of field component
a
. In the same way, one finds for the kinetic coefficient

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Z_{t,k}=

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{0}\rightarrow 0\\
\bm{p}\rightarrow 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{0}}\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma_{k}[\Phi]}{\delta\phi_{a,q}(-p)\delta\phi_{a,c}(p)}\bigg|_{\Phi_{\mathrm{EoM}}}\,.

(13)

In this work the projections in Eq.
12
and Eq.
16
are made on the pion field, i.e., the field component
a\neq 0
. Then, one arrives at

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})+(-2+d+\eta)\bar{\rho}z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})+\frac{2}{d}\bar{\rho}\,\big(z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})\big)^{2}\nu_{d}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}}s(x)\bigg(\frac{1}{L_{\pi}(x)L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}+\frac{1}{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)L_{\sigma}(x)}\bigg)

\displaystyle+4\bar{\rho}z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{s(x)}{L_{\pi}(x)L_{\sigma}(x)^{2}}

\displaystyle-4\bar{\rho}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\big)^{2}\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{s(x)}{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}\big(\partial_{x}L_{\pi}(x)\big)

\displaystyle+\frac{8}{d}\bar{\rho}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\big)^{2}\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}}\bigg(\frac{1}{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)L^{3}_{\sigma}(x)}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{L^{3}_{\pi}(x)L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}\bigg)\big(\partial_{x}L_{\pi}(x)\big)^{2}s(x)

\displaystyle-\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{8}{d}\bar{\rho}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\big)^{2}\frac{1}{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}\big(\partial^{2}_{x}L_{\pi}(x)\big)s(x)

\displaystyle-\big(z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})+2\bar{\rho}z^{(2)}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})\big)\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{1}{L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}s(x)

\displaystyle-(N-1)z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\frac{1}{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)}s(x)\,,

(14)

with

\displaystyle s(x)=

\displaystyle\big[2-\eta(1-x)\big]\Theta(1-x)\,,

\displaystyle L_{\pi}(x)=

\displaystyle z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})x+(1-x)\Theta(1-x)+u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})\,,

\displaystyle L_{\sigma}(x)=

\displaystyle z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})x+(1-x)\Theta(1-x)+u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})+2\bar{\rho}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\,,

(15)

where the flat regulator, cf. Eq.
36
, is used, and
\Theta(x)
denotes the Heaviside step function.

The flow of the kinetic coefficient reads

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Z_{t,k}=

\displaystyle-Z_{t,k}2\bar{\rho}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho})\big)^{2}\nu_{d}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\frac{d}{2}-1}s(x)

\displaystyle\times\frac{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)+4L_{\pi}(x)L_{\sigma}(x)+L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)}{L^{2}_{\pi}(x)L^{2}_{\sigma}(x)\big[L_{\pi}(x)+L_{\sigma}(x)\big]^{2}}\,,

(16)

which allows us to define the dynamic anomalous dimension as follows

\displaystyle\eta_{t}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial_{\tau}Z_{t,k}}{Z_{t,k}}\,.

(17)

The static anomalous dimension in Eq.
8
can be obtained by evaluating Eq.
14
at the minimum of potential
\bar{\rho}_{0}
, and one arrives at

\displaystyle\eta=

\displaystyle-\partial_{\tau}z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho}_{0})+\eta z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho}_{0})-(\partial_{\tau}\bar{\rho}_{0})z^{\prime}_{\phi}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\,,

(18)

with

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\bar{\rho}_{0}=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial_{\tau}u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0})}{u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})}\,,

(19)

where the last equation follows from the requirement

\displaystyle\frac{du^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0})}{d\tau}=

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0})+(\partial_{\tau}\bar{\rho}_{0})u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})=0\,.

(20)

Note that since the field dependence of the kinetic coefficient is neglected, it is a natural choice to compute its flow in Eq.
16
at the physical point
\bar{\rho}_{0}
. With the static anomalous dimension
\eta
in Eq.
8
or Eq.
18
and dynamic anomalous dimension
\eta_{t}
in Eq.
17
, one is able to calculate the dynamic critical exponent
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977

\displaystyle z=

\displaystyle 2-\eta+\eta_{t}\,

(21)

It is interesting to find that the flows of the effective potential in Eq.
11
and the wave function in Eq.
14
do not receive contributions from the dynamical variable
\eta_{t}
, indicating that the dynamics is decoupled from the static properties in the truncation as shown in the effective action in Eq.
4
. In fact, in more sophisticated truncations, for instance, when the momentum or frequency dependence of the kinetic coefficient
Z_{t,k}
is taken into account, there is no decoupling any more. Moreover, if the field dependence of the wave function is ignored, the truncation then is reduced to the modified local potential approximation, usually denoted by
\mathrm{LPA}^{\prime}
. Then the static anomalous dimension reads

\displaystyle\eta=

\displaystyle\frac{8}{d}\frac{1}{2^{d}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)}\frac{\bar{\rho}_{0}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\big)^{2}}{\big(1+2\bar{\rho}_{0}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\big)^{2}}\,,

(22)

and the dynamic anomalous dimension is given by

\displaystyle\eta_{t}=

\displaystyle\frac{4(2+d-\eta)}{d(2+d)}\frac{1}{2^{d}\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d/2)}\bar{\rho}_{0}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\big)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\frac{3+6\bar{\rho}_{0}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})+2\bar{\rho}_{0}^{2}\big(u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\big)^{2}}{\big(1+\bar{\rho}_{0}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\big)^{2}\big(1+2\bar{\rho}_{0}u^{(2)}(\bar{\rho}_{0})\big)^{2}}\,.

(23)

If the static anomalous dimension in Eq.
22
is assumed to be vanishing, i.e.,
\eta=0
, the truncation then returns to the local potential approximation (LPA).

In this section we solve fixed-point solutions of the flow equations of the effective potential and wave function numerically, that is
\partial_{\tau}u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})=0
and
\partial_{\tau}z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})=0
. In this work we focus on the Wilson-Fisher fixed point which is characterized by just one relevant eigenvalue of eigenperturbations around the fixed point
Wilson and Fisher 1972
;
Ma 2000
, and this relevant eigenvalue is usually denoted by
1/\nu
, where
\nu
is one of two static critical exponents besides the anomalous dimension
\eta
.

In this work we employ two different numerical methods to solve the fixed-point equations. One is the conventional grid method where the potential
u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})
and wave function
z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})
are discretized on a grid of
\bar{\rho}
. The other one is the high-precision direct integral of the fixed-point equation, that is recently proposed in
Tan et al. 2022b
, and more details can be found there. We find that these two different numerical methods produce identical results.

In Fig.
3
we show the fixed-point solutions of the global potential and wave function. It is found that with the decrease of dimension
d
, the zero crossing point
\bar{\rho}_{0}
of
u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho})
, i.e.,
u^{\prime}(\bar{\rho}_{0})=0
, moves right towards the direction of larger
\bar{\rho}
. One can also find that with the decrease of dimension
d
, the dependence of the wave function
z_{\phi}(\bar{\rho})
on the field
\bar{\rho}
becomes stronger, which indicates that when the dimension is small, say
d\lesssim 3
, the field dependence of the wave function should be taken into account. In Fig.
4
we show the critical exponent
\nu
as a function of the dimension
d
. Obviously, results obtained from the three truncations are convergent when the dimension is
d\gtrsim 3
. Deviations are observed in the region of small
d
, in particular in the vicinity of
d=2
. In fact, the numerical calculations become more and more difficult as the dimension is approaching
d=2
. This is already indicated in the results of the potential in the left panel of Fig.
3
. The zero crossing point
\bar{\rho}_{0}
is divergent when one has
d=2
and
N\geq 2
. Therefore, the calculation of derivative expansion ceases at a value of
d
, where the computation is quite time-consuming.

The results of static anomalous dimension
\eta
are presented in Fig.
5
. Since one has
\eta=0
in LPA, only two truncations are compared. One finds that the static anomalous dimension as a function of the dimension is monotonic for
N=1
, whereas there is a non-monotonic dependence in the case of
N=4
. This is closely related to the Mermin-Wagner-Hohenberg theorem
Mermin and Wagner 1966
;
Hohenberg 1967
;
Coleman 1973
, i.e., there is no phase transition in
d=2
dimension for the
O(N)
symmetry with
N\geq 2
. In Fig.
6
we show the dynamic anomalous dimension
\eta_{t}
. Similar with the static one, the dependence of
\eta_{t}
on the dimension is monotonic in
N=1
but non-monotonic for
N\geq 2
. Moreover, one can see
\eta_{t}=0
at
d=4
, indicating there is no critical fluctuation or critical slowing down at the Gaussian fixed point.

With the static and dynamic anomalous dimensions, one can obtain the dynamic critical exponent through Eq.
21
. The relevant results are presented in Fig.
7
. In the same way, we use three different truncations. Moreover, we also compare with the result of
\epsilon=4-d
expansion in the order of three loops
Halperin et al. 1972
;
Folk and Moser 2006
, which reads

\displaystyle z=2+c\eta\,.

(24)

with the constant
c

\displaystyle c=0.726(1-0.1885\epsilon)\,,

(25)

and the static anomalous dimension in the three-loop order
Wilson 1972

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{N+2}{2(N+8)^{2}}\epsilon^{2}+\frac{N+2}{2(N+8)^{2}}\left[\frac{6(3N+14)}{(N+8)^{2}}-\frac{1}{4}\right]\epsilon^{3}\,.

(26)

One can see that when the dimension is very close to
d=4
, say
d\gtrsim 3.5
, where the
\epsilon
expansion should work well, our results obtained with the
\mathrm{LPA}^{\prime}
or the derivative expansion are comparable with the
\epsilon
expansion. However, the LPA computation overestimates the dynamic critical exponent apparently, since the static anomalous dimension in LPA is neglected. Furthermore, in the whole range of
2\leq d\leq 4
, our results prefer
z\geq 2
for the case of
N=1
. The case of
N=4
is, however, more intricate, and one can see that the dynamic critical exponent calculated with the derivative expansion turns to
z<2
when the dimension is below
d=2.5
.

V
Summary and conclusions

In this work, we have used the Schwinger-Keldysh fRG developed in
Tan et al. 2022a
to study critical dynamics related to a second-order phase transition. As a concrete example, a dissipative relaxation model classified as model A is employed. In the formalism of Schwinger-Keldysh fRG, the RG scale dependent effective action is expressed in terms of two different kinds of fields. One is similar with the physical classical field, i.e. the “classical” field, and the other plays the role of fluctuations, called the “quantum” field. This formalism of double fields provides us with a very powerful approach to study real-time critical dynamics by employing systematic expansions for the truncation. For example, the derivative expansion can be applied to the sector of classical fields. For the sector of quantum fields, one is able to study the transition from a microscopic theory to a mesoscopic model, e.g., the semiclassical limit of a quantum action, see e.g.
Sieberer et al. 2016
for more relevant discussions.

We expand the effective action of
O(N)
symmetry to the order of
O(\partial^{2})
in the derivative expansion. The flow equations of the effective potential, wave function and the kinetic coefficient are obtained. By solving the fixed-point equations of the dimensionless renormalized potential and wave function, one is able to find the solution of the Wilson-Fisher fixed point and the relevant static and dynamic critical exponents. It is found that both the static anomalous dimension
\eta
and the dynamic anomalous dimension
\eta_{t}
behave as monotonic functions of the spatial dimension
d
in the range of
2\leq d\leq 4
in the case of
N=1
, whereas they are both non-monotonic when
N\geq 2
.

The dynamic critical exponent
z
is obtained as a function of the spatial dimension
d
for different values of
N
. Our results are also compared with those of
\epsilon
expansion in the order of three loops. It is found that results obtained from derivative expansion and
\mathrm{LPA}^{\prime}
are consistent with that from the
\epsilon
expansion when the dimension is close to
d=4
, while LPA overestimates the dynamic critical exponent. Furthermore, we find that
z\geq 2
in the whole range of
2\leq d\leq 4
for the case of
N=1
, while in the case of
N=4
the dynamic critical exponent turns to
z<2
when the dimension approach towards
d=2
.
