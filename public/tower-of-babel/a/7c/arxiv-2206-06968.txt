On the necessity of the inf-sup condition for a mixed finite element formulation
Fleurianne Bertrand, Daniele Boffi

Abstract
. We study a non standard mixed formulation of the Poisson problem, sometimes known as dual mixed formulation. For reasons related to the equilibration of the flux, we use finite elements that are conforming in 𝐇 ⁡ ( div , Ω ) \mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega) for the approximation of the gradients, even if the formulation would allow for discontinuous finite elements. The scheme is not uniformly inf-sup stable, but we can show existence and uniqueness of the solution, as well as optimal error estimates for the gradient variable when suitable regularity assumptions are made. Several additional remarks complete the paper, shedding some light on the sources of instability for mixed formulations.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we discuss the numerical approximation of saddle point problems
of the following form: given two Hilbert spaces
V
and
Q
, two continuous
bilinear forms
a(\cdot,\cdot):V\times V\to\mathbb{R}
and
b(\cdot,\cdot):V\times Q\to\mathbb{R}
, and two functionals
f\in V^{\prime}
and
g\in Q^{\prime}
, find
u\in V
and
p\in Q
such that

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a(u,v)+b(v,p)={}_{V^{\prime}}\langle f,v\rangle_{V}&&\forall v\in V\\
&b(u,q)={}_{Q^{\prime}}\langle g,q\rangle_{Q}&&\forall q\in Q.\end{aligned}\right.

It is well-known that a conforming approximation of the problem relies on
suitable stability conditions, which are usually referred to as
inf-sup
conditions
(see Section
2
).
While it is universally understood that the inf-sup conditions are sufficient
for the quasi-optimal convergence of any Galerkin discretization, the question
whether such conditions are also necessary is less studied; nevertheless it is
a common belief that in general the inf-sup conditions are essentially
sufficient and necessary for the optimal behavior of a numerical scheme and
everybody agrees that inf-sup unstable formulations should be avoided unless
special tricks are adopted (stabilizations, filtering of spurious modes,
special meshes, etc.).

In this paper we discuss the necessity of the inf-sup conditions by studying
the approximation of a non standard mixed formulation for the Poisson
equation. In particular, we present a scheme which, under suitable
conditions, is optimally convergent even if the inf-sup constant goes to zero
as the mesh is refined. This (counter-)example can be consider as an extension
of a one dimensional toy problem that has been studied in
[
1
]
. The
interested reader is also referred to the abstract setting sketched
in
[
6
, Section 5.6.2]
where it is shown what can happen when the
inf-sup condition goes wrong.

After an introductory section about the inf-sup conditions, in
Section
3
we present the dual mixed formulation of the
Poisson equation. Section
4
deals with discrete inf-sup
constant, including some numerical tests showing the mesh dependent behavior
of the stability condition. In Section
5
we show how the
finite element spaces can be spit into a stable part and an unstable one.
Sections
6
and
7
present the convergence
theoretical results and some numerical tests confirming the theory. Finally,
two appendices conclude the paper with some links between the considered
problem and a flux equilibration strategy.

2.
Generalities about the inf-sup conditions

In this section and in the sequel of this paper we follow the framework
of
[
6
]
; we recall some relevant and well known results for completeness
and for setting our notation.

The conforming Galerkin approximation of the mixed formulation presented
in (
1
) consists in choosing appropriate finite element subspaces

V_{h}\subset V
and
Q_{h}\subset Q
, and in finding
u_{h}\in V_{h}
and
p_{h}\in Q_{h}
such that

(2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &a(u_{h},v)+b(v,p_{h})={}_{V^{\prime}}\langle f,v\rangle_{V}&&\forall v\in V_{h}\\
&b(u_{h},q)={}_{Q^{\prime}}\langle g,q\rangle_{Q}&&\forall q\in Q_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

Let
N_{V}
be the dimension of
V_{h}
and
N_{Q}
the one of
Q_{h}
, the matrix
form of the discrete problem expressed in (
2
) is given by

(3)

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}&\mathsf{B}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{B}&\mathsf{0}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{u}\\
\mathsf{p}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{f}\\
\mathsf{g}\end{matrix}\right),

where
\mathsf{A}
is a square matrix of size
N_{V}\times N_{V}
,
\mathsf{B}
is a
rectangular matrix of size
N_{Q}\times N_{V}
,
\mathsf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{V}}
and

\mathsf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{Q}}
are column vector representations of
u\in V_{h}
and
p\in Q_{h}
, respectively, and
\mathsf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{V}}
and
\mathsf{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{Q}}
are column
vector realizations of the right hand sides
f
and
g
, respectively.

The necessary and sufficient conditions for the solvability
of (
3
) are summarized in
[
6
, Theorem 3.2.1]
, which we now
recall for the reader’s convenience.
We denote by
\mathsf{K}
the kernel of
\mathsf{B}

\mathsf{K}=\ker\mathsf{B}.

The restriction of
\mathsf{A}
to
\mathsf{K}
is denoted by
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}}

\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}}=\Pi_{\mathsf{K}}\mathsf{A}E_{\mathsf{K}},

where
\Pi_{\mathsf{K}}
and
E_{\mathsf{K}}
are the projection from
\mathbb{R}^{N_{V}}
onto
\mathsf{K}

and the embedding from
\mathsf{K}
into
\mathbb{R}^{N_{V}}
, respectively. The two
conditions equivalent to the solvability of (
3
) (for all possible
right hand sides) are the following ones.

M1:

The matrix
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}}
is invertible.

M2:

N_{V}\geq N_{Q}
and the matrix
\mathsf{B}
is full rank.

M1
can be expressed by saying that the operator
associated with
\mathsf{A}_{\mathsf{K}\mathsf{K}}
is surjective or, equivalently, injective;
analogously,
M2
states that the operator associated with

\mathsf{B}
is surjective or, equivalently, that the one associated with

\mathsf{B}^{\top}
is injective.

The essential ideas behind the inf-sup theory is that a uniform stability of
problem (
2
) with respect to the parameter
h
requires that
conditions
M1
and
M2
are made explicit and uniform with
respect to
h
. This is done by introducing suitable inf-sup conditions.

Let
\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}_{h}}
be the subspace of
V_{h}
associated with the kernel of the matrix

\mathsf{B}

\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}_{h}}=\{v_{h}\in V_{h}:b(v_{h},q)=0\ \forall q\in Q_{h}\}.

Then hypotheses
M1
and
M2
correspond to the following two
inf-sup conditions, respectively (where, as usual, when there is an inf-sup
involving fractions, we understand that the infimum and the supremum are taken
over non vanishing functions).

IS1:

There exists a constant
\alpha_{h}>0
such that

(4)

\inf_{v_{h}\in\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}_{h}}}\sup_{w_{h}\in\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}_{h}}}\frac{a(v_{h},w_{h})}{\|v_{h}\|_{V}\|w_{h}\|_{V}}\geq\alpha_{h}.

IS2:

There exists a constant
\beta_{h}>0
such that

(5)

\inf_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{b(v_{h},q_{h})}{\|v_{h}\|_{V}\|q_{h}\|_{Q}}\geq\beta_{h}.

We assume that the continuous problem (
1
) is stable; in
particular, the following conditions analogue to
IS1
and
IS2

guarantee this property, where the continuous kernel is defined as

\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}}=\{v\in V:b(v,q)=0\ \forall q\in Q\}.

•

There exists a constant
\alpha>0
such that

(6)

\inf_{v\in\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}}}\sup_{w\in\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}}}\frac{a(v,w)}{\|v\|_{V}\|w\|_{V}}\geq\alpha

and

(7)

\inf_{w\in\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}}}\sup_{v\in\mathsf{K}_{\mathsf{B}}}\frac{a(v,w)}{\|v\|_{V}\|w\|_{V}}\geq\alpha.

•

There exists a constant
\beta>0
such that

(8)

\inf_{q\in Q}\sup_{v\in V}\frac{b(v,q)}{\|v\|_{V}\|q\|_{Q}}\geq\beta.

The following theorem summarizes the stability and convergence result that is
obtained when the inf-sup conditions are uniform with respect to the parameter

h
.

Theorem 1
.

If there exist
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\beta_{0}>0
such that the constants in

IS1
and
IS2
are uniformly bounded below, that is,

\alpha_{h}\geq\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{h}\geq\beta_{0}
for all
h
, then the following
quasi-optimal error estimate holds true

(9)

\|u-u_{h}\|_{V}+\|p-p_{h}\|_{Q}\leq C\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}v_{h}\in V_{h}\\
q_{h}\in Q_{h}\end{subarray}}\left(\|u-v_{h}\|_{V}+\|p-q_{h}\|_{Q}\right),

where
(u,p)
and
(u_{h},p_{h})
are the solutions of (
1
)
and (
2
), respectively.

Remark 2
.

The constant
C
in (
9
) could be made explicit in terms of

\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
. The interested reader is referred
to
[
6
, Theorem 5.2.1]
.

3.
A non-standard mixed formulation for the Laplace equation

In this paper we consider the homogeneous Dirichlet problem for the Laplace
equation on a polygonal domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
: given
f
find
u
such that

(10)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\Delta u=f&&\text{in }\Omega\\
&u=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

More general elliptic equations might be considered, but we believe that this
is the simplest and most effective setting for the presentation of our
results.

We split the second order equation as a system of two first order equations by
introducing the variable
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=-\operatorname{\nabla}u
. As opposed to the standard mixed
formulation, we integrate by parts the equilibrium equation and not the
equation defining
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and obtain the following problem: given
f
in

H^{-1}(\Omega)
, find
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
and
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

such that

(11)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}u)=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}\\
&(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=-\langle f,v\rangle&&\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).\end{aligned}\right.

Sometimes this formulation is called dual mixed formulation to differentiate
it from the standard primal mixed formulation.

Remark 3
.

Our interest in problem (
11
) is related to an analogue
formulation used in elasticity (see
[
12
]
). A numerical study of the
inf-sup condition in the case of the Laplace equation was presented
in
[
4
]
.

The mixed formulation is well posed in the chosen functional spaces. For
completeness, this is proved in the next theorem.

Theorem 4
.

The mixed formulation presented in (
11
) is well posed in
the sense that the inf-sup conditions (
6
),
(
7
), and (
8
) are satisfied.

Proof.

The inf-sup conditions (
6
) and (
7
) follow from
the fact that the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
corresponds to the identity
operator in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
, which is clearly invertible on the whole space.
The inf-sup condition (
8
) for the bilinear form

b(\cdot,\cdot)
follows from the inclusion
\operatorname{\nabla}H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\subset L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
: given
v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, the vectorfield
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\operatorname{\nabla}v

satisfies
b(\boldsymbol{\tau},v)=\|\operatorname{\nabla}v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq C_{1}\|v\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
and
\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\|\operatorname{\nabla}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2}\|v\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}
, that is (
8
)
with
\beta=C_{1}/C_{2}
.

∎

Given two discrete subspaces
\Sigma_{h}\subset L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
and
U_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, the discretization or problem (
11
) reads: find

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
and
u_{h}\in U_{h}
such that

(12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h})=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}\\
&(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=-\langle f,v\rangle&&\forall v\in U_{h}.\end{aligned}\right.

Remark 5
.

Since the inf-sup conditions (
6
) and (
7
) are
satisfied on the entire space
L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
, any choice of discrete spaces

\Sigma_{h}
and
U_{h}
will satisfy uniformly the discrete inf-sup
condition (
4
). It follows that the only condition to be shown
for the stability of the discretization, is the uniform bound of the discrete
inf-sup constant in (
5
) associated with the bilinear form

b(\cdot,\cdot)
.

A natural choice for the discrete spaces is given by discontinuous piecewise
polynomials of degree
k
(in each component) for
\Sigma_{h}
and continuous
piecewise polynomials of degree
k+1
for
U_{h}
. In the next proposition we
state the stability and the quasi-optimal convergence of the resulting scheme.

Proposition 6
.

For
k\geq 0
let
\Sigma_{h}
be the space of discontinuous piecewise polynomials of
degree
k
in each component and
U_{h}
be the space of continuous piecewise
polynomials of degree
k+1
with zero boundary conditions. Then the
approximation (
12
) of the mixed
formulation (
11
) is uniformly stable in the sense of
Theorem
1
and the following quasi-optimal error estimate holds
true

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u-u_{h}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}\\
v_{h}\in U_{h}\end{subarray}}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u-v\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\right).

In particular, if the solution
(\boldsymbol{\sigma},u)
is smooth enough, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u-u_{h}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+1}\left(\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{H^{k+1}(\Omega)}+\|u\|_{H^{k+2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ch^{k+1}\|u\|_{H^{k+2}(\Omega)}.

In general, if
u\in H^{1+s}(\Omega)
with
s\leq k+1
, we obtain

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u-u_{h}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{s}\|u\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The stability proof follows the same lines as in Theorem
4
.
In particular, the uniform inf-sup condition (
4
) is guaranteed
by the global invertibility of the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
which
corresponds to the identity in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. The uniform inf-sup
condition (
5
) for the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
follows
from the inclusion
\operatorname{\nabla}U_{h}\subset\Sigma_{h}
.

∎

It can actually be easily observed that if
\operatorname{\nabla}U_{h}\subset\Sigma_{h}
then the mixed
formulation (
12
) is equivalent to the standard Galerkin
formulation where the space
U_{h}
is used. We state this result in the
following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

If the inclusion
\operatorname{\nabla}U_{h}\subset\Sigma_{h}
is satisfied, then the mixed
formulation (
12
) is well posed in the sense of
Theorem
1
and the component
u_{h}
of its solution solves the
standard Galerkin formulation

(13)

(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=\langle f,v\rangle\qquad\forall v\in U_{h}.

The other component of the solution is given by

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}
.

Proof.

It is easily seen that inserting
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}

into (
12
), the first equation is an identity and the second
equation corresponds precisely to (
13
).
∎

The lowest-order element presented in Proposition
6
will be
referred to as the
P_{0}-P_{1}
scheme.

In a more general context,
[
12
]
discusses the approximation of a
linear elasticity problem with a mixed scheme which has some analogies with
our formulation (
11
). For particular reasons related to some
equilibration properties that will be made more precise later on, it is
proposed the use of Raviart–Thomas elements
RT_{0}
for the definition of

\Sigma_{h}
. If
discontinuous

RT_{0}
elements are used, then the same
stability proof as for the
P_{0}-P_{1}
scheme applies. This follows from the
fact that
\Sigma_{h}
contains the space of piecewise constants. We state this
result in the following corollary for any degree
k
.

Corollary 8
.

Let
\Sigma_{h}
be the space of discontinuous Raviart–Thomas finite elements of
degree
k\geq 0
and
U_{h}
be the space of continuous piecewise polynomials of
degree
k+1
with homogeneous boundary conditions.
Then the formulation (
12
) is uniformly stable and the
following error estimate holds true if the solution
u
is smooth enough

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u-u_{h}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{s}\|u\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)}

with
s\leq k+1
.
Moreover, the mixed problem is equivalent to the standard Galerkin
approximation (
13
) with continuous polynomials of degree
k+1
and

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}
.

We now consider the lowest order case, that is
k=0
, so that the
approximation of
u
is obtained by standard piecewise linear elements.

For reasons related to the equilibration property
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=f
,
in
[
12
]
it is proposed to use
\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
-conforming
RT_{0}
elements for
the definition of
\Sigma_{h}
. We are going to denote this element by
RT_{0}-P_{1}
.
Numerical evidence seems to indicate that for the elasticity problem this
choice provides a uniformly stable scheme
[
12
]
, while this is not the
case for the Poisson problem
[
4
]
. We are going to analyze in more
detail this element in the next sections.

4.
The inf-sup condition for the
RT_{0}-P_{1}
scheme

The first inf-sup condition
IS1
(
4
) is automatically
satisfied for our mixed formulation (see Remark
2
), so that we
are only discussing the second inf-sup
IS2
(
5
) which
reads:

(14)

\inf_{v\in U_{h}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\geq\beta_{h}.

We start with a positive result, showing that for all
h
the constant

\beta_{h}
is strictly greater than zero.

Theorem 9
.

For all
v\in U_{h}
with
v\neq 0
there exists
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}
such that

(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)>0,

that is the inf-sup constant in (
14
) satisfies
\beta_{h}>0
for all

h
.

Proof.

After integration by parts and taking into account the boundary conditions, we
have

(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=-(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau},v).

We denote by
\Pi_{0}
the
L^{2}(\Omega)
projection onto the space of
piecewise constant functions; the term
(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)
is maximized by
taking
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}
with
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau}=\Pi_{0}v
, so that we have

(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=-\|\Pi_{0}v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Hence the result follows by observing that
\Pi_{0}v=0
implies
v=0
if

v
is vanishing on
\partial\Omega
.
Indeed, it is not possible to construct a function
v\in U_{h}

that is zero mean valued in each element. This is easily seen by starting from
a boundary element
T
(with two vertices on
\partial\Omega
): if
v
is zero
mean valued on
T
, then necessarily it vanishes on
T
; the same argument can
then be applied to the neighboring elements sharing an edge with
T
and so on
until it is seen that
v
must vanish on all elements of the triangulation.
∎

The immediate consequence of the previous theorem is that
problem (
12
) is solvable.

Corollary 10
.

For all
f\in H^{-1}(\Omega)
and all
h
there exists a unique solution to
problem (
12
).

We postpone to Appendix
B
further theoretical investigations
about the behavior of the inf-sup constant. Here we continue this study
numerically.

It is well known that an estimate of the inf-sup constant
\beta_{h}
appearing
in (
14
) can be obtained by solving an algebraic problem.
In
[
6
]
a singular value decomposition is used. An essentially
equivalent approach was described in
[
9
]
(see
Remark
12
), based on an idea from
[
10
]
.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\Sigma_{h}
and
U_{h}
be finite element spaces and consider the discrete saddle
point problem (
12
).
Let
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
be the corresponding matrices appearing
in (
3
) and introduce the matrix
\mathsf{M}
corresponding to the

H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
inner product
(\operatorname{\nabla}\cdot,\operatorname{\nabla}\cdot)
in
U_{h}
. Then the
inf-sup constant
\beta_{h}
in (
14
) is equal to
\sqrt{\mu_{min}}
,
where
\mu_{min}
is the smallest eigenvalue
\mu
of the
generalized eigenvalue problem

(15)

\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}^{-1}\mathsf{B}^{\top}\mathsf{x}=\mu\mathsf{M}\mathsf{x}.

Remark 12
.

In
[
6
, Section 3.4.3]
it is shown that the constant
\beta_{h}
is equal
to the smallest singular value of the matrix

\mathsf{M}^{-1/2}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}^{-1/2}
. This statement is equivalent to
Proposition
15
since the singular values of

\mathcal{A}=\mathsf{M}^{-1/2}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}^{-1/2}
are the square roots of the
eigenvalues of

\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}^{\top}=\mathsf{M}^{-1/2}\mathsf{B}\mathsf{A}^{-1}\mathsf{B}^{\top}\mathsf{M}^{-1/2}
.
Please note the typo in
[
6
, Proposition 3.4.5]
, where the involved
matrix should read
S_{Y}^{-1}BS_{X}^{-1}
instead of
S_{Y}BS_{X}
.

The eigenvalue problem (
15
) has
N(h)=\dim(U_{h})
eigensolutions
(taking into account possibly repeated eigenvalues); we number the eigenvalues
starting from the largest one, so that
\mu_{min}=\mu_{N(h)}
,

\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\dots\geq\mu_{N(h)}\geq 0

and we denote the corresponding eigenvectors by
\{\mathsf{x}_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N(h)}
.
Each eigenvector
\mathsf{x}_{i}
represents an element
g_{i}
of
U_{h}
and we have

U_{h}=\text{span}\{g_{1},\dots,g_{N(h)}\}.

The eigenvalue problem (
15
) associated with the inf-sup condition
has also a mixed equivalent formulation:

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}&\mathsf{B}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{B}&\mathsf{0}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}\\
\mathsf{x}\end{matrix}\right)=\mu\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}&-M\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}\\
\mathsf{x}\end{matrix}\right).

The two formulations are easily shown equivalent to each other by solving for

\mathsf{y}
the first equation
\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}+\mathsf{B}^{\top}\mathsf{x}=0
and substituting into the
second equation.

This allows the definition of associated eigenvectors

\{\mathsf{y}_{i}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N(h)}
in addition to the
\{\mathsf{x}_{i}\}
’s satisfying the
relation

\mathsf{A}\mathsf{y}_{i}+\mathsf{B}^{\top}\mathsf{x}_{i}=0\qquad(i=1,\dots,N(h)).

Translating into the finite element notation, we have constructed two sets of
finite element functions
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i,h}\}\subset\Sigma_{h}
and

\{u_{i,h}\}\subset U_{h}
that satisfy the following variational problem

(16)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i,h},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}u_{i,h})=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}\\
&(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i,h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=-\mu_{i}(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{i,h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)&&\forall v\in U_{h}\end{aligned}\right.

and such that

\displaystyle\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1,h},\dots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N(h),h}\}\subset\Sigma_{h}

\displaystyle\text{span}\{u_{1,h},\dots,u_{N(h),h}\}=U_{h}.

The eigenvectors
\{u_{i,h}\}
can be chosen so that

\displaystyle(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{i,h},\operatorname{\nabla}u_{j,h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0

\displaystyle\text{if }i\neq j

\displaystyle\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{i,h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1.

It follows that also the
\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i,h}\}
are orthogonal, since

5.
Stable and unstable subspaces

The following discussion identifies special subspaces of
U_{h}
for which a
uniform inf-sup condition holds true.
According to what we have seen in the previous section, we are expecting that
the degeneracy of the inf-sup constant is associated with highly oscillatory
eigenfunctions of problem (
16
).

Let
w_{k}\in U_{h}
,
k=1,\dots,N(h)
, be the
k
-th discrete eigenfunction of the
Laplace operator approximated by the standard Galerkin method, that
is

(\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=\lambda_{k}(w_{k},v)\quad\forall v\in U_{h}

with
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
.

Our first result shows that if we fix
\bar{N}
and restrict
U_{h}
to the space
spanned by
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{\bar{N}}\}
, then the inf-sup condition holds
with a constant uniform in
h
.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\bar{W}_{h}
be the subspace of
U_{h}
spanned by
\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{\bar{N}}\}
. Then there exists
\bar{\beta}>0
independent of
h
such that

(17)

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}w)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq\bar{\beta}\qquad\forall w\in\bar{W}_{h}.

Proof.

First we show that (
17
) is satisfied when
w=w_{k}
for a fixed

k
. We define
\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}
as the solution of the following mixed
problem: find
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{k}\in\Sigma_{h}
and
p
in the space of piecewise constant
functions
\mathcal{P}_{0}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau},p)=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}\\
&(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k},q)=-\lambda_{k}(w_{k},q)&&\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

We then have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\sigma}\lambda_{k}\|w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C_{\sigma}\lambda_{k}^{1/2}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}=-\lambda_{k}\Pi_{0}w_{k},

where
\Pi_{0}
is the
L^{2}
projection onto
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

By choosing
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}
in (
17
) we can conclude

(18)

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k})}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k},\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k})}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}=-\frac{(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k},w_{k})}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}=\frac{\lambda_{k}(\Pi_{0}w_{k},w_{k})}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda_{k}(w_{k},w_{k})-\lambda_{k}(w_{k}-\Pi_{0}w_{k},w_{k})}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\lambda_{k}(w_{k}-\Pi_{0}w_{k},w_{k}-\Pi_{0}w_{k})}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\lambda_{k}\|w_{k}-\Pi_{0}w_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{C_{\sigma}\lambda_{k}^{1/2}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\lambda_{k}C^{2}_{\Pi}h^{2}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{C_{\sigma}\lambda_{k}^{1/2}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-C^{2}_{\Pi}h^{2}\lambda_{k}}{C_{\sigma}\lambda_{k}^{1/2}}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=\beta_{k}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
C_{\Pi}
denotes the constant appearing in the approximation property of

\Pi_{0}

\|f-\Pi_{0}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Pi}h\|\operatorname{\nabla}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

It follows that
\beta_{k}
is bounded below uniformly in
h
for
k
fixed and

h
small enough.

In order to complete the proof it remains to extend the result to a generic

w\in\bar{W}_{h}
; the restriction of
h
small enough is removed by comparing
with Theorem
9
.

We detail how to deal with
w=w_{i}+w_{j}
; the generic result follows with
similar arguments by considering a finite linear combination of discrete
eigenfunctions.
We define
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\varsigma}=\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{j}

in (
17
), where
\boldsymbol{\varsigma}_{k}
(
k=i,j
) is defined in the
previous step. We have

(19)

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}w)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(\boldsymbol{\varsigma},\operatorname{\nabla}w)}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}=\frac{(\lambda_{i}\Pi_{0}w_{i}+\lambda_{j}\Pi_{0}w_{j},w)}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(\lambda_{i}(w_{i}-\Pi_{0}w_{i})+\lambda_{j}(w_{j}-\Pi_{0}w_{j}),w)}{C_{\sigma}\|\lambda_{i}w_{i}+\lambda_{j}w_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}.

Let us study separately the numerator and the denominator of the last
expression by starting with the scalar product appearing in the numerator. In
order to make the notation shorted, we denote by
f_{k}
(
k=i,j
) the term

\lambda_{k}w_{k}
.

\displaystyle(f_{i}-\Pi_{0}f_{i}

\displaystyle+f_{j}-\Pi_{0}f_{j},w_{i}+w_{j})

\displaystyle=(f_{i}-\Pi_{0}f_{i},w_{i})+(f_{j}-\Pi_{0}f_{j},w_{j})

\displaystyle+(f_{i}-\Pi_{0}f_{i},w_{j})+(f_{j}-\Pi_{0}f_{j},w_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi}h\left(\|\operatorname{\nabla}f_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|w_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\operatorname{\nabla}f_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|w_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+(f_{i}-\Pi_{0}f_{i},w_{j}-\Pi_{0}w_{j})+(f_{j}-\Pi_{0}f_{j},w_{i}-\Pi_{0}w_{i})

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi}h\left(\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})^{1/2}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+C^{2}_{\Pi}h^{2}\left(\lambda_{i}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda_{j}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{i}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\Pi}h\left(\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})^{1/2}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)

\displaystyle+(C^{2}_{\Pi}h^{2}/2)\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where we used twice the orthogonality of
\operatorname{\nabla}w_{i}
and
\operatorname{\nabla}w_{j}
.
It follows that the numerator in (
19
) can be bounded below by a
positive constant times
\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for
h
small enough.
For the denominator, we have

\displaystyle\|\lambda_{i}w_{i}+\lambda_{j}w_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}^{2}\|w_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda_{j}^{2}\|w_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{i}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\lambda_{j}\|\operatorname{\nabla}w_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j})\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where we used again the orthogonality of
\operatorname{\nabla}w_{i}
and
\operatorname{\nabla}w_{j}
together
with the orthogonality of
w_{i}
and
w_{j}
. It follows that for
h
small
enough there exists a constant
C
independent of
h
, but dependent on
i

and
j
, such that

\frac{(\boldsymbol{\varsigma},\operatorname{\nabla}w)}{\|\boldsymbol{\varsigma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq C\|\operatorname{\nabla}w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

∎

Remark 14
.

The inf-sup constant of the previous theorem depends on the dimension of
\bar{W}_{h}
. In particular, it gives a confirmation that the components of
U_{h}
that
are source of instability are associated with highly oscillating functions.
We now discuss how it is possible to split the solution of
problem (
12
) into a stable part and an unstable one in a more
abstract way.

It can be easily seen that all eigenvalues of (
15
) are not larger
than
1
, so that we fix a threshold value
\underline{\mu}
between
0
and
1
and
define an index
N_{\underline{\mu}}
(depending on
h
) so that

\displaystyle\mu_{i}\geq\underline{\mu}

\displaystyle\forall i\leq N_{\underline{\mu}}

\displaystyle\mu_{i}<\underline{\mu}

\displaystyle\forall i>N_{\underline{\mu}}.

We can then introduce the following spaces

(20)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{1,h},\dots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N_{\underline{\mu}},h}\}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}=\text{span}\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N_{\underline{\mu}}+1,h},\dots,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{N(h),h}\}

\displaystyle U_{1}=\text{span}\{u_{1,h},\dots,u_{N_{\underline{\mu}},h}\}

\displaystyle U_{2}=\text{span}\{u_{N_{\underline{\mu}}+1,h},\dots,u_{N(h),h}\}.

It follows that
\Sigma_{h}=\Sigma_{1}\oplus\Sigma_{2}\oplus\tilde{\Sigma}
and that

U_{h}=U_{1}\oplus U_{2}
, where
\tilde{\Sigma}
is a remainder space whose dimension is
equal to
\dim\Sigma_{h}-N(h)\geq 0
. This space satisfies the orthogonality

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i,h},\boldsymbol{\tau})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0\quad\forall i\ \forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\tilde{\Sigma}.

Remark 15
.

It turns out that the subspaces
\Sigma_{1}-U_{1}
provide a stable discretization
of (
12
) since the corresponding inf-sup constant is
associated with
(\underline{\mu})^{1/2}>0
which is independent of
h
.

We can now look at the matrix form of our discrete
problem (
12
)

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}&\mathsf{B}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{B}&\mathsf{0}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}\\
\mathsf{x}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{0}\\
-\mathsf{f}\end{matrix}\right).

The above problem can be written in the following form by using the splitting
of the spaces
\Sigma_{h}
and
U_{h}
:

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}_{11}&\mathsf{A}_{21}^{\top}&\mathsf{A}_{31}^{\top}&\mathsf{B}_{11}^{\top}&\mathsf{B}_{21}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{A}_{21}&\mathsf{A}_{22}&\mathsf{A}_{32}^{\top}&\mathsf{B}_{12}^{\top}&\mathsf{B}_{22}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{A}_{31}&\mathsf{A}_{32}&\mathsf{A}_{33}&\mathsf{B}_{13}^{\top}&\mathsf{B}_{23}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{B}_{11}&\mathsf{B}_{12}&\mathsf{B}_{13}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{B}_{21}&\mathsf{B}_{22}&\mathsf{B}_{23}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}_{1}\\
\mathsf{y}_{2}\\
\mathsf{y}_{3}\\
\mathsf{x}_{1}\\
\mathsf{x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{1}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

Some of the matrices involved with this formulation are vanishing. In
particular, it is easy to see that
\mathsf{A}_{21}=\mathsf{0}
due to the
orthogonalities of the
\operatorname{\nabla}u_{i}
’s; analogously,
\mathsf{B}_{12}=\mathsf{0}
and

\mathsf{B}_{21}=\mathsf{0}
.
Moreover, due to the orthogonality of
\tilde{\Sigma}
with the rest of
\Sigma_{h}
, it follows
that
\mathsf{A}_{31}=\mathsf{0}
and
\mathsf{A}_{32}=\mathsf{0}
, and also that

\mathsf{B}_{13}=\mathsf{0}
and
\mathsf{B}_{23}=\mathsf{0}
.

Hence the matrix problem is reduced to

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}_{11}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{B}_{11}^{\top}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{A}_{22}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{B}_{22}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{A}_{33}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{B}_{11}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{B}_{22}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}_{1}\\
\mathsf{y}_{2}\\
\mathsf{y}_{3}\\
\mathsf{x}_{1}\\
\mathsf{x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{1}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{2}\end{matrix}\right).

Since
\mathsf{A}_{33}
is invertible, it follows that
\mathsf{y}_{3}=0
so that the

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}_{11}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{B}_{11}^{\top}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{A}_{22}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{B}_{22}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{B}_{11}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{B}_{22}&\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}_{1}\\
\mathsf{y}_{2}\\

\mathsf{x}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{1}\\
-\mathsf{f}_{2}\end{matrix}\right)

where all the non-vanishing blocks are square and diagonal.

It follows that the system decouples into the following two systems:

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}_{11}&\mathsf{B}_{11}^{\top}\\
\mathsf{B}_{11}&\mathsf{0}\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{y}_{1}\\
\mathsf{x}_{1}\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{0}\\

\left(\begin{matrix}\mathsf{A}_{22}&\mathsf{B}_{22}^{\top}\\

This corresponds to the following two variational problems. Find

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1,h}\in\Sigma_{1}
and

such that

(21)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1,h},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}w_{1,h})=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{1}\\

and find
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2,h}\in\Sigma_{2}
and

such that

(22)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2,h},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}w_{2,h})=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{2}\\
&(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2,h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=-\langle f,v\rangle&&\forall v\in U_{2}\end{aligned}\right.

The solution of (
12
) is then given simply by

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1,h}+\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2,h}\qquad u_{h}=w_{1,h}+w_{2,h}.

) corresponds to a

, while (

goes to zero.

Proposition 16
.

Let us consider the solution

be a constant between

,

, and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{1,h}+\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2,h}

\displaystyle u_{h}=w_{1,h}+w_{2,h},

where

and

(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{2,h},w_{2,h})\in\Sigma_{2}\times U_{2}
solve (

)
and (

), respectively.

Theorem 17
.

The second component

6.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{0}
be the finite element space of piecewise constant functions. We
introduce the following two subspaces of
\Sigma_{h}
:

\displaystyle Z_{h}^{m}(f)=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}:(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},q_{h})=(f,q_{h})\ \forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\}

\displaystyle Z_{h}(f)=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}:(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v_{h})=-(f,v_{h})\ \forall v_{h}\in U_{h}\}.

We have the following crucial result.

Lemma 19
.

If
f
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, then
Z_{h}^{m}(f)\subset Z_{h}(f)
. Moreover, if

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
satisfies
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}=f
, then

\inf_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in Z_{h}(f)}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}\in\Sigma_{h}
is the interpolant of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is in
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, then
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is smooth enough so that its
interpolant
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}\in\Sigma_{h}
is well defined.
In this setting the definition of
Z_{h}(f)
reads

Z_{h}(f)=\{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}:(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\Pi_{0}v_{h})=(f,\Pi_{0}v_{h})\ \forall v_{h}\in U_{h}\},

where
\Pi_{0}
denotes the
L^{2}(\Omega)
projection onto
\mathcal{P}_{0}
.
It follows that if
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}
belongs to
Z_{h}^{m}(f)
then it is also in

Z_{h}(f)
.

From the standard inf-sup condition that is valid for the spaces
\Sigma_{h}
and

\mathcal{P}_{0}
, Proposition 5.1.3 of
[
6
]
gives

\inf_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in Z_{h}^{m}(f)}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)}\leq C\inf_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)},

so that the inclusion
Z_{h}^{m}(f)\subset Z_{h}(f)
gives, in particular,

\inf_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in Z_{h}(f)}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\inf_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}}\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)}.

From the commuting diagram property
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}=\Pi_{0}\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
,
observing that
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{0}
, we have that

\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I})=0
, so that we can conclude that the right
hand side can be estimated by
\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
∎

The following theorem shows the convergence of the approximation of

\boldsymbol{\sigma}
given by (
12
) in the case when
f
belongs to

\mathcal{P}_{0}
.

Theorem 20
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
be the first component of the solution
of (
11
) for
f\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
the corresponding
approximation given by (
12
). Then we have the estimate

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\inf_{v_{h}\in U_{h}}\|\operatorname{\nabla}(u-v_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{I}\in\Sigma_{h}
is the interpolant of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.

Proof.

We estimate
\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in Z_{h}(f)
.
The result will then follow from the triangular inequality and
Lemma
19
.

From the error equation and the properties of
Z_{h}(f)
we have for all

v_{h}\in U_{h}

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}(u-u_{h}))+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})

\displaystyle=(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}(u-v_{h}))+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left(\|\operatorname{\nabla}(u-v_{h})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right),

from which we obtain the required estimate.
∎

Corollary 21
.

If
f\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and if the Poisson problem has
H^{1+s}(\Omega)

regularity for some
s\in(0,1]
, then the following optimal error estimate
holds true

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch^{s}\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

The result follows by approximating
f
with
\Pi_{0}f
and using the previous
theorem together with the standard estimate

\|f-\Pi_{0}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq Ch\|\operatorname{\nabla}f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

∎

Appendix A
The asymptotic behavior of the inf-sup constant

In this section we discuss theoretically the asymptotic behavior of the
inf-sup constant that has been studied numerically in Section
4
.

We start by some remarks related to the continuous inf-sup condition: given

u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
there exists
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{2}
such that

(26)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\operatorname{\nabla}u)=\|\operatorname{\nabla}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\operatorname{\nabla}u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

This can be easily achieved by defining
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\operatorname{\nabla}u
and the constant

C
is equal to one in this case. The same consideration could lead to a
uniform discrete inf-sup condition if we had the inclusion

\operatorname{\nabla}U_{h}\subset\Sigma_{h}
. In our case, however, the inclusion is not satisfied
because the space
\Sigma_{h}
is in
\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
. The natural question is then if it is
possible, at the continuous level, to find
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)

satisfying (
26
). This is certainly true if
\operatorname{\nabla}u
belongs to

\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
which is the case, for instance, when
u
is the solution of a Poisson
problem
-\Delta u=g
for some
g
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. We state this result in
the following proposition.

Proposition 22
.

Let
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
be the solution of the problem

(\operatorname{\nabla}u,\operatorname{\nabla}v)=(g,v)\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

for some
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\operatorname{\nabla}u
belongs to
\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
and
satisfies (
26
) with
C=1
.

We now try to mimic this proposition at the discrete level. Since the
divergence of
\Sigma_{h}
is piecewise constant, it is natural to start by
considering
u_{h}\in U_{h}
that solves

(27)

(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=(g_{h},v)\quad\forall v\in U_{h}

for some piecewise constant right hand side
g_{h}
. If we define

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}
we have that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
is not in
\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
since

\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}
has only the tangential component continuous across the elements
while we would need the normal one. We are then led to some sort of
equilibration strategy, in the spirit
of
[
11
,
8
,
7
,
13
,
5
]
. The optimal way to achieve the
equilibration is to solve a global problem and to define
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
as
the solution of the mixed problem: find
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
and
p_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}

such that

(28)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau})+(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau},p_{h})=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}\\
&(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},q)=-(g_{h},q)&&\forall q\in\mathcal{P}_{0}\end{aligned}\right.

Then we have
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=-g_{h}
which implies

(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h})=(g_{h},u_{h})=\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

Hence, we get a bound for the discrete inf-sup condition if we can estimate
the constant
C(h)
for which it holds

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(h)\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Clearly, from (
28
) we have

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|g_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
so that we need a
bound of
\|g_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
in terms of
\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
.
Since
u_{h}
solves (
27
), this bound is related to an
inf-sup condition between the spaces
U_{h}
and
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. More precisely, the
following lemma holds true.

Lemma 23
.

Let
U_{0}
be the subspace of
U_{h}
defined as the solutions
of (
27
) for some
g_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
. Let
\zeta(h)>0
be such
that the following inf-sup condition holds true

(29)

\inf_{w_{h}\in U_{h}}\sup_{g_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}}\frac{(g_{h},w_{h})}{\|g_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|w_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq\zeta(h).

Then the inf-sup condition holds true for the space
U_{0}

\inf_{v\in U_{0}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\geq C\zeta(h).

The inf-sup constant
\zeta(h)
in (
29
) can be investigated
theoretically on special meshes or in the case of a one dimensional domain. It
turns out that its behavior is similar to the one of
\beta_{h}

in (
14
). For instance, Table
4
shows the value of the

Section
4
). It turns out that with a uniform mesh
\zeta(h)

decays as O(h) while with a non structured mesh the decay is less pronounced.

Appendix B
Some connections with flux equilibration

We have seen that the inf-sup constant
\beta_{h}
in general is not bounded
below independently of
h
. We have also seen in several parts of this paper
some analogies between the inf-sup condition and the well known flux
equilibration strategy used, for instance, in the a posteriori analysis of
standard finite elements (see, for
instance,
[
11
,
8
,
7
,
13
,
5
]
).
In this appendix we explore this connections in more detail.

To this aim, we make use of the following natural modification of the usual
Fortin trick
[
6
]
.

Proposition 24
.

Let
U_{0}
be a subspace of
U_{h}
and assume that there exists a linear
projection
\Pi:\operatorname{\nabla}(U_{0})\to\Sigma_{h}
such that for any

\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\operatorname{\nabla}(U_{0})

(30)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\tau}-\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=0

\displaystyle\forall v\in U_{0}

\displaystyle\|\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\Pi}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Then the following inf-sup condition holds true

(31)

\inf_{v\in U_{0}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\||\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}}\geq\beta

with
\beta=1/(C_{P}C_{\Pi})
, being
C_{P}
the Poincaré constant.

Proof.

Take
v\in U_{0}
, then we have

\displaystyle\|v\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{P}\|\operatorname{\nabla}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C_{P}\frac{(\operatorname{\nabla}v,\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|\operatorname{\nabla}v\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{P}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\operatorname{\nabla}(U_{0})}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle=C_{P}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\operatorname{\nabla}(U_{0})}\frac{(\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}\leq{C_{P}}{C_{\Pi}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\operatorname{\nabla}(U_{0})}\frac{(\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\leq{C_{P}}{C_{\Pi}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{(\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v)}{\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}},

which implies the inf-sup condition (
31
) with

\beta=1/(C_{P}C_{\Pi})
.

∎

Looking carefully at the properties of the Fortin projector
in (
30
), we can see that the construction of
\Pi
consists in
finding a mapping from gradients of continuous piecewise polynomials into the
lowest order Raviart–Thomas space in
\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
. This problem has been widely
studied, for instance, in the framework of flux equilibration of finite
element spaces.

In this appendix we try to identify a suitable subspace
W_{1}
of
U_{h}
that
could be used as
U_{0}
in Proposition
24
.
We start with the following heuristic reasoning. Let us take a function

f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and for each
h
consider the solution
u_{h}\in U_{h}
of the
following standard Galerkin problem

(32)

(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v_{h})=(\Pi_{0}f,v_{h})\qquad\forall v_{h}\in U_{h}.

Then the most natural way to find an equilibrated
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
is to
solve the following global mixed problem: find

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},p_{h})\in\Sigma_{h}\times\mathcal{P}_{0}
such that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})+(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h},p_{h})=0&&\forall\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}\\
&(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},q_{h})=-(f,q_{h})&&\forall q_{h}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

By considering
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}
and
\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
and observing
that
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=-\Pi_{0}f
, we then have

(\boldsymbol{\tau}-\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau},\operatorname{\nabla}v_{h})=(\Pi_{0}f,v)+(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},v)=0\qquad\forall v\in U_{h},

that is, the first Fortin condition in (
30
) is satisfied.
Moreover, we can bound
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
by triangular inequality as follows

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{0}\leq\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}+\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}.

The first term on the right hand side is going to zero and can be bounded by

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}-\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}\leq h^{s}\|\Pi_{0}f\|_{0}

if the solution of the continuous problem corresponding to (
32
) has
regularity
H^{1+s}(\Omega)
.
In order to estimate
\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{0}
uniformly in terms of

\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}
we then need to bound
\|\Pi_{0}f\|_{0}
uniformly by

h^{-s}\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}
. Unfortunately this cannot be done in general.

The equilibration technique is used in the next theorem to show how the
Fortin operator restricted to a suitable subspace of
U_{h}
behaves
asymptotically in
h
.

Theorem 25
.

Let
W_{1}
be the following subspace of
U_{h}
:

W_{1}=\{w\in U_{h}\,|\,\exists g_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}:(\operatorname{\nabla}w,\operatorname{\nabla}v)=(g_{0},v)\ \forall v\in U_{h}\},

where
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is the space of piecewise constant functions. Let
\zeta(h)
be
the inf-sup constant introduced in (
29
) and
\rho(h)
a function of

h
so that the inverse inequality
\|\operatorname{\nabla}v\|_{0}\leq\rho(h)\|v\|_{0}
holds
true. Then there exists a
Fortin operator
\Pi
as in Proposition
24
with

U_{0}=W_{1}
and
C_{\Pi}\leq Ch\rho(h)/\zeta(h)
.

Proof.

We denote by
\mathcal{T}_{h}
our triangulation of
\Omega
and by

\mathcal{E}_{h}
the skeleton of the edges.
Given
u_{h}\in W_{1}
, we consider
\boldsymbol{\tau}=\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}
so that the flux
reconstruction procedure provided in
[
7
]
and described with
explicit formulas in
[
3
]
will give a function

\Pi\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
.
The construction is local and is performed as

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=\nabla u_{h}+\sum\limits_{z\in\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}
is a
discontinuous

RT_{0}
function (
DRT_{0}
)
supported on the patch

\omega_{z}=\{T\in\mathcal{T}:z\text{ is a vertex of }T\}.

As the space
DRT_{0}
consists of edge basis functions, we will also consider
the set

\mathcal{E}_{z}=\{E\in\mathcal{E}:z\text{ is a vertex of }E\}.

Let
g_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
be such that
(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=(g_{0},v)

\forall v\in U_{h}

as in the definition of
W_{1}
.
Following the standard procedure of flux reconstruction we have that

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
belongs to
\Sigma_{h}
and that the Fortin property

(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=0
(
\forall v\in W_{1}
) is satisfied if the

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}
are chosen such that

(33)

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}|_{T}=-\frac{1}{|T|}(g_{0},\phi_{z})_{T}

\displaystyle\forall T\in\omega_{z}

\displaystyle\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket_{E}=-\frac{1}{2}\llbracket\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket_{E}

\displaystyle\forall E\in\mathcal{E}_{z}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\omega_{z}

where
\phi_{z}
is the linear nodal basis function corresponding to the
node
z
and vanishing on the boundary of the patch
\omega_{z}
.

Indeed, the second equation in (
33
) guarantees that

\llbracket\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\rrbracket_{E}=0
for all internal edges
E
, since each edge belongs
to exactly two patches. Hence
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}
belongs to
\mathbf{H}(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}};\Omega)
and we can
evaluate its divergence element by element. From the first equation
in (
33
) we have

\left(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\sum\limits_{z\in\mathcal{V}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\right)\Bigg|_{T}=-\frac{1}{|T|}\sum_{i=1}^{3}(g_{0},\phi_{z_{i}})_{T}=-\frac{1}{|T|}(g_{0},1)_{T}=g_{0}|_{T},

where
z_{i}
(
i=1,2,3
) are the three vertices of
T
. From

(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h})|_{T}=0
it follows
\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}=-g_{0}
, so that we get the
Fortin property

(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)=(g_{0},v)=(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v)\qquad\forall v\in U_{h}.

The edge basis functions considered for the space
\Sigma_{h}
are supported in the
two adjacent triangles of the edge
E
.
We denote by
T^{-}_{E}
and
T^{+}_{E}
the two triangles adjacent to
E
and define
an edge oriented basis

\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{-}\}_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{h}}\cup\{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{+}\}_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{h},E\not\subset\partial\Omega}

for
DRT_{0}
, using the basis functions

\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{-}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2|T^{-}_{E}|}(\mathbf{x}-P^{-}_{E})&\text{on }T^{-}_{E}\\
0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}\text{ and }\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{+}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2|T^{+}_{E}|}(\mathbf{x}-P^{+}_{E})&\text{on }T^{+}_{E}\\
0&\text{elsewhere}\end{cases}

where
P^{-}_{E}
and
P^{+}_{E}
are the vertices of
T^{-}_{E}
and
T^{+}_{E}
,
respectively, not shared by the two triangles.
This basis uses a similar representation of the one presented
in
[
2
]
although here

\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{\pm}=\pm\frac{1}{|T^{\pm}_{E}|}

and thus

\int_{E}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\pm}_{E}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{E}=\pm\left(\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{\pm},1\right)_{T^{\pm}_{E}}=1,

where the normal
\mathbf{n}_{E}
is pointing from
T^{+}_{E}
to
T^{-}_{E}
. It follows

\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\pm}_{E}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{E}=\frac{1}{|E|}.

These basis functions allow for the following construction:

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}=\sum\limits_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{z}}(\tau_{E,z}^{-}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{-}+\tau_{E,z}^{+}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{+})

where the coefficients
\tau_{E,z}^{+}
and
\tau_{E,z}^{-}
will be chosen so
that the equilibration conditions (
33
) hold.

For the computation of the coefficients, we index the triangles in
the patch from
1
to
n_{z}
and define
T_{0}:=T_{n_{z}}
and
T_{n_{z}+1}:=T_{1}
.
Further, we define
E_{i}=T_{i}\cap T_{i+1}
and
T^{+}_{E_{i}}=T_{i+1}
. Then,

\operatorname{\mathrm{div}}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}|_{T_{i}}=\tau_{E_{i-1},z}^{+}\frac{1}{|T_{i}|}-\tau_{E_{i},z}^{-}\frac{1}{|T_{i}|}

and the first condition in (
33
) reads

\tau_{E_{i},z}^{-}=(g_{0},\phi_{z})_{T_{i}}+\tau^{+}_{E_{i-1},z}\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n_{z}.

The second condition in (
33
) implies

\tau_{E_{i},z}^{+}-\tau_{E_{i},z}^{-}=-\frac{|E_{i}|}{2}\llbracket\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket_{E_{i}}\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n_{z}.

This leads to

\tau_{E_{i},z}^{+}-\tau^{+}_{E_{i-1},z}=-\frac{|E_{i}|}{2}\llbracket\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket_{E_{i}}+(g_{0},\phi_{z})_{T_{i}}\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n_{z}

and thus to

\tau_{E_{i},z}^{+}=\tau^{+}_{E_{0},z}+\sum\limits_{j=1}^{i}\left(-\frac{|E_{j}|}{2}\llbracket\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket_{E_{j}}+(g_{0},\phi_{z})_{T_{j}}\right)\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n_{z}.

We can bound the two terms on the right hand side that are involved in the
summation as follows:

\left|\frac{|E_{j}|}{2}\llbracket\nabla u_{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\rrbracket_{E_{j}}\right|\leq C\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0,\omega_{z}}

and

|(g_{0},\phi_{z})_{T_{j}}|\leq\|g_{0}\|_{0,T_{j}}\|\phi_{z}\|_{0,T_{j}}\leq Ch\|g_{0}\|_{0,T_{j}}.

Choosing
\tau^{+}_{E_{0},z}=0
we then have

\displaystyle|\tau_{E_{i},z}^{+}|\leq C(\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0,\omega_{z}}+h\|g_{0}\|_{0,\omega_{z}})

\displaystyle|\tau_{E_{i},z}^{-}|\leq C(\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0,\omega_{z}}+h\|g_{0}\|_{0,\omega_{z}})

\displaystyle\forall i=1,\dots,n_{z}.

By a scaling argument or by using a suitable quadrature rule we see that

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}_{E}^{\pm}\|_{0}^{2}=\frac{1}{4|T_{E}^{\pm}|^{2}}\|\mathbf{x}-P_{E}^{\pm}\|_{0}^{2}\leq Ch^{2}/|T_{E}^{\pm}|\leq C

so that it holds

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\|_{0,\omega_{z}}\leq C(\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0,\omega_{z}}+h\|g_{0}\|_{0,\omega_{z}})
.
By putting all the patches together and considering that the intersections
between patches contain a bounded number of elements and that each element
belongs to a bounded number of patches we get

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+h\|g_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}).

It remains to estimate
g_{h}
, which can be done by considering the inf-sup
constant discussed in Appendix
A
concerning the
P_{0}-P_{1}

element (
29
). By the definition of
g_{0}
we have

(g_{0},v_{h})=(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v_{h})\quad\forall v_{h}\in U_{h}

and hence

\displaystyle\zeta(h)\|g_{0}\|_{0}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v_{h}\in U_{h}}\frac{(g_{0},v_{h})}{\|v\|_{0}}=\sup_{v_{h}\in U_{h}}\frac{(\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h},\operatorname{\nabla}v_{h})}{\|v\|_{0}}\leq\sup_{v_{h}\in U_{h}}\frac{\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}\|\operatorname{\nabla}v_{h}\|_{0}}{\|v_{h}\|_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\rho(h)\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{0}.

Finally, we arrive at the final estimate

\|\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\operatorname{\nabla}u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(1+h\rho(h)/\zeta(h)),

which leads to the bound
C_{\Pi}\leq Ch\rho(h)/\zeta(h)
.
∎

Although the above considerations do not provide a rigorous proof that the
inf-sup constant is vanishing with
h
, they give a clear indication that we
should not expect the constant
\beta_{h}
to be uniformly bounded away from
zero. Indeed, the behavior of the inf-sup constant depends on the chosen mesh
as shown in Section
4
: if the mesh is quasiuniform then

h\rho(h)
is bounded from above and below so that we have a confirmation that
the inf-sup constant cannot be better than
\zeta(h)
.
