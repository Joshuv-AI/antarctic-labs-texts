An Introduction to the Bernoulli Function
Peter H. N. Luschny

Abstract




[title=Index]

Abstract.

We explore a variant of the zeta function interpolating the Bernoulli numbers based on an integral
representation suggested by J. Jensen. The
Bernoulli function

\operatorname{B}(s,v)=-s\,\zeta(1-s,v)

can be introduced independently of the zeta function if it is based on a formula first
given by Jensen in 1895. We examine the functional equation of
\operatorname{B}(s,v)
and
its representation by the Riemann
\zeta
and
\xi
function, and
recast classical results of Hadamard, Worpitzky, and Hasse in terms of
\operatorname{B}(s,v).

The
extended Bernoulli function
defines the Bernoulli numbers
for odd indices basing them on rational numbers studied by Euler in 1735
that underlie the
Euler and André numbers
.
The
Euler function
is introduced as the difference between values of the
Hurwitz-Bernoulli function. The
André function
and the
Seki function

are the unsigned versions of the extended Euler resp. Bernoulli function.

1 – Prologue: extension by interpolation

The question

André Weil recounts the origin of the gamma function
in his historical exposition of number theory
[
54
, p. 275]
:

“Ever since his early days in Petersburg Euler had been interested in the interpolation
of functions and formulas given at first only for integral values of the argument;
that is how he had created the theory of the gamma function.”

The three hundred year success story of Euler’s gamma function shows how
fruitful this question is. The usefulness of such an investigation is not limited
by the fact that there are infinitely many ways to interpolate a sequence of numbers.

This essay will explore the question: how can the Bernoulli numbers be interpolated most meaningfully?

The method

The Bernoulli numbers had been known for some time at the beginning of the 18th century
and used in the (now Euler–Maclaurin called) summation formula in analysis, first
without realizing that these numbers are the same each case.

Then, in 1755, Euler baptized these numbers
Bernoulli numbers

in his
Institutiones calculi differentialis

(following the lead of de Moivre).
After that, things changed, as Sandifer
[
46
]
explains:

“… for once the Bernoulli numbers had a name,
their diverse occurrences could be recognized, organized, manipulated and
understood. Having a name, they made sense.”

The function we are going to talk about is not new.
However, it is not treated as a function in its own right and with its own name.
So we will give the beast a name. We will call the interpolating function the

Bernoulli function
. Since, as Mazur
[
41
]
asserts,
the Bernoulli numbers “act as a unifying force, holding together seemingly
disparate fields of mathematics,” this should all the more
be manifest in this function.

What to expect

This note is best read as an annotated formula collection; we refer to the cited literature for the proofs.

The Bernoulli function and the Riemann zeta function can be understood as complementary pair.
One can derive the properties of one from those of the other. For instance, all the questions Riemann
associated with the zeta function can just as easily be discussed with the Bernoulli function.
In addition, the introduction of the Bernoulli function leads to a better understanding which
numbers the
generatingfunctionologists

[
55
]
should hang up on their clothesline
(see the discussion in
[
33
]
).

Seemingly the first to treat the Bernoulli function in our sense was Jensen
[
25
]
,
who gave an integral formula for the Bernoulli function of remarkable simplicity.
Except for referencing Cauchy’s theorem, he did not develop the proof. The proof
is worked out in Johansson and Blagouchine
[
28
]
.

A table of contents (
An Introduction to the

Bernoulli Function
) can be found at the end
of the paper.

3 – Stieltjes constants and Zeta function

The generalized Euler constants, also called
Stieltjes constants
,
are the real numbers
\gamma_{n}
defined by the Laurent series in a neighborhood of
s=1

of the Riemann zeta function (
[
1
,
45
]
),

\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\gamma_{n}(s-1)^{n},\qquad(s\neq 1).

(1)

As a particular case they include the Euler constant

\gamma_{0}=\gamma\ \,(\approx\frac{228}{395})
.
Blagouchine
[
7
]

gives a detailed discussion with many historical notes.
Following Franel
[
17
]
, Blagouchine
[
6
]
shows that

\gamma_{n}\,=\,-\frac{2\,\pi}{n+1}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\,\frac{\log\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+iz\right)^{n+1}}{\left({{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi\,z}}+{{\mathrm{e}}^{\pi\,z}}\right)^{2}}\,{dz}.

(2)

Here, and in all later similar formulas, we write
f(x)^{n}
for
(f(x))^{n}

and take the principal branch of the logarithm implicit in the exponential.

Recently Johansson
[
26
]
observed that one can employ the integral representation
(
2
) in a particularly efficient way to approximate the Stieltjes constants with prescribed precision numerically.

Figure 2:
The Bernoulli constants seen as values of a real function.

4 – Bernoulli constants and Bernoulli function

The
Bernoulli constants

\beta_{n}
are defined for
n\geq 0
as

{\beta_{n}\,=\,2\pi\!\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}{\frac{\log\!\left(\frac{1}{2}+iz\right)^{n}}{\left({{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi\,z}}+{{\mathrm{e}}^{\pi\,z}}\right)^{2}}}\,{dz}.}

(3)

The
Bernoulli function
is defined as

\operatorname{B}(s)\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}\,.

(4)

Since
\beta_{0}=1
we have in particular
\operatorname{B}(0)=1
.

Comparing the definitions of the constants
\gamma_{n}
and
\beta_{n}
,
we see that in (
3
) the exponent
of the power is synchronous to the index and the factor
-1/(n+1)
in (
2
)
has disappeared. In other words,
\gamma_{n}=-\frac{\beta_{n+1}}{n+1}
,
and the
Riemann zeta representation
of the Bernoulli function
becomes

\displaystyle\operatorname{B}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n+1}\frac{s^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\frac{s^{n+1}}{n!}

\displaystyle=1-s\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=-s\zeta(1-s).

(5)

The singularity of
-s\zeta(1-s)
at
s=0
is removed by

\operatorname{B}(0)=1
. Thus
\operatorname{B}(s)
is an entire
function with its defining series converging everywhere in
\mathbb{C}
.

5 – The Bernoulli numbers

We define the
Bernoulli numbers
as the values of the
Bernoulli function at the nonnegative integers. According to (
4
) this means

\operatorname{B}_{n}\,=\,\operatorname{B}(n)\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta_{j}\frac{n^{j}}{j!}.

(6)

Since for
n>1
an odd integer
-n\zeta(1-n)=0
, the Bernoulli numbers
vanish at these integers and

\operatorname{B}_{n}=1-n\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\frac{n^{j}}{j!}
implies

\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\frac{n^{j}}{j!}=\frac{1}{n}\text{ and }\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta_{j}\frac{n^{j}}{j!}=0\qquad(n>1\text{ odd}).

(7)

\operatorname{B}(s)=-s\zeta(1-s)

(8)

{\operatorname{B}(s)\,=\,1+\beta_{1}s+\frac{\beta_{2}}{2}s^{2}+\frac{\beta_{3}}{6}s^{3}+\frac{\beta_{4}}{24}s^{4}\,\ldots\ .}

7 – Integral formulas for the Bernoulli constants

Let us come back to the definition of
\beta_{n}
as given in (
3
).
The appearance of the imaginary unit forces complex integration; on the
other hand, only the real part of the result is used. Fortunately, the definition
can be simplified such that the computation stays in the realm of reals
provided
n
is a nonnegative integer.

Using the symmetry of the integrand with respect to the
y
-axis and

\left({{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi\,z}}+{{\mathrm{e}}^{\pi\,z}}\right)^{2}=4\cosh(\pi z)^{2}

we get from the definition (
3
)

\beta_{n}\ =\ \pi\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\,{\frac{\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\!\left(\log(\frac{1}{2}+iz)^{n}\right)}{\cosh(\pi z)^{2}}}dz\,.

(10)

For the numerator of the integrand we set for
n\geq 0

\sigma_{n}(z)\,=\,\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(\log(\tfrac{1}{2}+iz)^{n}).

(11)

By induction we see that

\sigma_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{2k}a(z)^{n-2k}\,b(z)^{2k},

(12)

where
a(z)=\log(z^{2}+\frac{1}{4})/2
and
b(z)=\arctan(2z)
.

Figure 3:
f(z)=-\log((z^{2}+\frac{1}{4})^{\frac{1}{4}})\sech(\pi z)^{2}

with

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z)\,dz=\frac{\gamma}{\pi}.

Therefore the constants can be computed by real integrals,

\beta_{n}\,=\,\pi\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n}(z)}{\cosh(\pi z)^{2}}\,dz\,.

(13)

Similarly we have for the Stieltjes constants

\gamma_{n}\,=\,-\frac{\pi}{n+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{n+1}(z)}{\cosh(\pi z)^{2}}\,dz\,.

(14)

8 – Some special integral formulas

Let us consider some special cases of these integral formulas.
Formula (
14
) reads for
n=0

\gamma\,=\,-\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\log(z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4})}{2\cosh(\pi z)^{2}}\,dz.

(15)

This follows since for real
z

\sigma_{1}(z)=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\log\left(\frac{1}{2}+iz\right)=\log\left(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{4z^{2}+1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(z^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right).

Using the symmetry of the integrand with respect to the
y
-axis this can be
rephrased as: Euler’s gamma is
\pi
times the integral of

-\log((z^{2}+\tfrac{1}{4})^{\tfrac{1}{4}})\sech(\pi z)^{2}
over the real line.
See figure
3
.

With the abbreviations

a=\log(z^{2}+\frac{1}{4})/2,\,b=\arctan(2z)
and
c=\cosh(\pi z)

the first few Bernoulli constants are by (
12
):

\displaystyle\beta_{1}\,

\displaystyle=\,\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a}{c^{2}}\,dz\,,

(16)

\displaystyle\beta_{2}\,

\displaystyle=\,\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{2}-b^{2}}{c^{2}}\,dz\,,

(17)

\displaystyle\beta_{3}\,

\displaystyle=\,\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{3}-3ab^{2}}{c^{2}}\,dz\,,

(18)

\displaystyle\beta_{4}\,

\displaystyle=\,\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{4}-6a^{2}b^{2}+b^{4}}{c^{2}}\,dz\,,

(19)

\displaystyle\beta_{5}\,

\displaystyle=\,\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{5}-10a^{3}b^{2}+5ab^{4}}{c^{2}}\,dz\,.

(20)

9 – Generalized Bernoulli constants

We recall that
\gamma_{n}/n!
is the coefficient of
(1-s)^{n}

in the Laurent expansion of
\zeta(s)
about
s=1
and

\gamma_{n}(v)/n!
is the coefficient of
(1-s)^{n}
in the Laurent
expansion of
\zeta(s,v)
about
s=1
. In other words, with the

generalized Stieltjes constants

\gamma_{n}(v)

we have the
Hurwitz zeta function

\zeta(s,v)
in the form

\zeta(s,v)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\gamma_{n}(v)(s-1)^{n},\quad(s\neq 1).

(21)

The generalized Stieltjes constants may be computed for
n\geq 0
and
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(v)>\frac{1}{2}
by an extension
of the integral representation (
2
), see Johansson and Blagouchine
[
28
, formulas 2, 32, 42]
.

\gamma_{n}(v)\,=\,-\frac{\pi}{2(n+1)}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\log\!\left(v-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz\right)^{n+1}}{\cosh(\pi x)^{2}}\,dz.

(22)

The
generalized Bernoulli constants
are defined as

{\beta_{s}(v)=2\pi\!\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\!\frac{\,\log\!\big(v-\frac{1}{2}+ix\big)^{s}\,}{\left({\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi x}+{\mathrm{e}}^{\pi x}\right)^{2}}\,dx.}

(23)

We see that
\beta_{0}(v)=1
for all
v
and

\beta_{n}(v)=-n\gamma_{n-1}(v)
for
n\geq 1
.

10 – The generalized Bernoulli function

We introduce the generalized Bernoulli function
\operatorname{B}(s,v)

analogous to the Hurwitz zeta function. The new parameter
v
can be
any complex number that is not a nonpositive integer. Then we define the

generalized Bernoulli function
as

{\operatorname{B}(s,v)\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(v)\frac{s^{n}}{n!}\,.}

(24)

For
v=1,
this is the ordinary Bernoulli function (
4
).
Using the identities from the last section, we get

\operatorname{B}(s,v)=1-s\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}(v)\frac{s^{n}}{n!}.

(25)

Thus the generalized Bernoulli function can be represented by

\operatorname{B}(s,v)=-s\,\zeta(1-s,v),\qquad(s\neq 1).

(26)

This identity also embeds the
Bernoulli polynomials
as

\operatorname{B}_{n}(x)\,=\,\operatorname{B}(n,x)\quad(n\geq 0,n\text{ integer}).

(27)

This follows from (
26
) (see for instance Apostol
[
4
, th. 12.13]
)
and the fact that
\operatorname{B}(0,x)\,=\,1.

11 – Integral formulas for the Bernoulli function

We can transfer the integral formulas for the Bernoulli constants to the
Bernoulli function itself. First we reproduce a formula by Jensen
[
25
]
,
which he gave in reply to Cesàro in
L’Intermédiaire des mathématiciens
.

{(s-1)\zeta(s)\,=\,2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2}+iy)^{1-s}}{({\mathrm{e}}^{\pi y}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi y})^{2}}\,dy.}

(28)

Jensen comments:

“… [this formula] is remarkable because of its simplicity
and can easily be demonstrated with the help of Cauchy’s theorem.”

How Jensen actually computed
(s-1)\zeta(s)
is unclear, since the formula for
the coefficients
\operatorname{c}_{v}
, which he states, rapidly diverges.
This was observed by Kotěšovec (personal communication).

In a numerical example Jensen uses the Bernoulli constants in the form

\operatorname{c}_{v}\,=\,(-1)^{v}\beta_{v}/{v!}.

Applied to the Bernoulli function,
Jensen’s formula
is written as

{\operatorname{B}(s)\,=\,2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2}+iz)^{s}}{({\mathrm{e}}^{\pi z}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi z})^{2}}\,dz.}

(29)

In Johansson and Blagouchine
[
28
]
this is a particular case of the first
formula of theorem 1. (See also Srivastava and Choi
[
50
, p. 92]

and the discussion
[
49
]
.)

Hadjicostas
[
20
]
remarks that from this theorem also the
representation for the generalized Bernoulli function follows:

For all

s\in\mathbb{C}

and

v\in\mathbb{C}

with

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(v)\geq 1/2

{\operatorname{B}(s,v)\,=\,2\pi\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(v-\tfrac{1}{2}+iz)^{s}}{({\mathrm{e}}^{\pi z}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi z})^{2}}\,dz.}

(30)

This formula is the central formula in this paper.

12 – The Hurwitz–Bernoulli function

The
Hurwitz–Bernoulli function
is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{H}(s,v)&={\mathrm{e}}^{-i\pi s/2}\,\operatorname{L}(s,v)+{\mathrm{e}}^{i\pi s/2}\,\operatorname{L}(s,1-v),\\
\operatorname{L}(s,v)&=-\frac{s\,!}{(2\pi)^{s}}\operatorname{Li}_{s}({\mathrm{e}}^{2\pi iv}).\end{split}

(31)

Here
\operatorname{Li}_{s}(v)
denotes the polylogarithm.
The proposition that

\operatorname{B}_{s}(v)=\operatorname{B}(s,v)=\operatorname{H}(s,v),\ \ \text{for }0\leq v\leq 1\text{ and }s>1,

(32)

goes back to Hurwitz. The corresponding case for the zeta function (
32
)
is known as the
Hurwitz formula

[
4
, p. 71]
.
With the Hurwitz–Bernoulli function the Bernoulli polynomials can be
continuously deformed into each other (see figure
4
).

Figure 5:
The Bernoulli function and the central Bernoulli function

13 – The central Bernoulli function

Setting
v=1
in (
32
) the Hurwitz–Bernoulli function simplifies to

{\operatorname{B}_{s}(1)\,=\,-2\,s!\operatorname{Li}_{s}(1)\cos(s\pi/2)/(2\pi)^{s},\quad(s>1).}

(33)

For
s>1
one can replace the polylogarithm with the zeta function and then apply
the functional equation of the zeta function to get

\operatorname{B}_{s}(1)\,=\,-s\zeta(1-s),\qquad(s>1).

(34)

Thus the Bernoulli function is a vertical section of the Hurwitz–Bernoulli function,

\operatorname{B}(s)=\operatorname{B}_{s}(1),
similarly as the Bernoulli numbers
are special cases of the Bernoulli polynomials,
\operatorname{B}_{n}=\operatorname{B}_{n}(1)
.

Setting
v=1/2
in the Hurwitz–Bernoulli function leads to a second noteworthy case.
Then (
32

15 – The Genocchi function

How much does the central Bernoulli function deviate from the Bernoulli function?
The
Genocchi function
answers this question (up to a normalization factor).

\operatorname{G}(s)\,=\,2^{s}\left(\operatorname{B}^{c}(s)-\operatorname{B}(s)\right)=\,2^{s}\left(\operatorname{B}_{s}(\tfrac{1}{2})-\operatorname{B}_{s}(1)\right).

(42)

From the identities (
34

\operatorname{G}(s)\,=\,2(1-2^{s})\operatorname{B}(s).

(43)

The Genocchi function takes integer values for nonnegative integer arguments.
The
Genocchi numbers

\operatorname{G}_{n}=\operatorname{G}(n)

\operatorname{G}_{n}=0,\,-1,\,-1,\,0,\,1,\,0,\,-3,\,0,\,17,\,0,\,-155,\,0,\,2073,\,\dots

(44)

The plain Genocchi function is recovered by

For

21 – The Hasse representation

In 1930 Hasse
[
21
]
took the next step
in the development of formula (

{\operatorname{B}(s,v)\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\,n+1\,}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}(k+v)^{s}}

(64)

is an infinite series

that converges for all complex
s
and represents the entire
function
-s\zeta(1-s,v)
, the Bernoulli function.

For instance the Hasse series of the central
Bernoulli function is

{\operatorname{B}^{c}(s)\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\,n+1\,}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\left(k+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{s}}.

(65)

This representation in turn provides an explicit formula for the central Bernoulli numbers

(
39

{\operatorname{B}^{c}_{n}\,=\,\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\,j+1\,}\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}\binom{j}{k}(2k+1)^{n}}.

(66)

An important corollary to Hasse’s formula is: The generalized Bernoulli constants
\beta_{s}(v)
(
23
)
are given by the infinite series

\beta_{s}(v)\,=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\,n+1\,}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\binom{n}{k}\ln(k+v)^{s}.

(67)

For proofs see Blagouchine
[
8
, Cor. 1, formula 123]
and the references given there.

22 – The functional equation

The functional equation of the Bernoulli function
generalizes a formula of Euler which Graham et al.
[
18
, eq. 6.89]

call
almost miraculous
. But before we discuss it we introduce yet another
function and quote a remark from Tao
[
51
]
.

“It may be that
2\pi i
is an even more fundamental constant than

2\pi
or
\pi
. It is, after all, the generator of
\log(1)
. The fact
that so many formulas involving
\pi^{n}
depend on the parity of
n

is another clue in this regard.”

Taking up this remark we will use the notation
\tau=2\pi i
and
the function

{\tau(s)}=\tau^{-s}+(-\tau)^{-s}.

(68)

We use the principal branch of the logarithm when taking powers of
\tau
.
If
s
is real we can also write
\tau(s)=2^{1-s}\pi^{-s}\cos(\pi s/2)
.

This notation allows us to express the functional equation of
the Riemann zeta function
[
45
]
as the product of three functions,

\zeta\!\left(1-s\right)=\zeta\!\left(s\right)\tau(s)\,\Gamma(s)\qquad(s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,1\}).

(69)

Using (
69
) and
\operatorname{B}(s)=-s\zeta(1-s)
we get the representation

{\operatorname{B}(s)=-\zeta(s)\,\tau(s)\,s!.}

(70)

From
\operatorname{B}(1-s)=(s-1)\zeta(s)
we obtain a self-referential
representation of the Bernoulli function, the
functional equation

{\operatorname{B}(s)=\frac{\operatorname{B}(1-s)}{1-s}\tau(s)\,s!\,.}

(71)

This functional equation also has a
symmetric variant
,
which means that the left side of (
72
) is unchanged by
the substitution
s\leftarrow 1-s
.

\operatorname{B}(1-s)\,\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)!\ \pi^{-s/2}\,=\,\operatorname{B}(s)\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)!\ \pi^{-(1-s)/2}.

(72)

The Riemann decomposition of the Bernoulli function (
70
).

23 – Representation by the Riemann
\xi
function

The right (or the left) side of (
72
) turns out to be the

Riemann
\xi
function
,

\xi(s)\,=\,\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)!\ \pi^{-s/2}\,(s-1)\zeta(s).

(73)

For a discussion of this function see for instance Edwards
[
14
]
.
Thus we get a second representation of the Bernoulli function
in terms of a Riemann function:

{\operatorname{B}(s)\,=\,\frac{\pi^{(1-s)/2}}{\left((1-s)/2\right)!}\ \xi(s)\,.}

(74)

By the functional equation of
\xi
,
\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)
, we also get

{\operatorname{B}(1-s)\,=\,\frac{\pi^{s/2}}{\left(s/2\right)!}\ \xi(s)\,.}

(75)

This is a good opportunity to check the value of
\operatorname{B}(-1)
.

{\operatorname{B}(-1)\,=\,\pi\,\xi(-1)=\,\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}=\pi\,\xi(2)\,.}

(76)

In this row of identities, the names Bernoulli, Euler (solving the Basel problem in 1734),
and Riemann join together.

24 – The Hadamard decomposition

We denote Hadamard’s infinite product over the zeros of
\zeta(s)
by

\operatorname{H}_{\zeta}(s)\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\prod_{\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits{\rho}>0}\left(1-\frac{s}{\rho}\right)\left(1-\frac{s}{1-\rho}\right).

(77)

The product runs over the zeros with
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\rho)>0
.
The absolute convergence of the product is guaranteed as the terms
are taken in pairs
(\rho,1-\rho)
.
Hadamard’s infinite product expansion of
\zeta(s)
is

\zeta(s)\,=\,\frac{\pi^{s/2}}{\left(s/2\right)!}\,\frac{\operatorname{H}_{\zeta}(s)}{s-1}\,,\quad s\notin\{1,-2,-4,\dots\}.

(78)

Since the zeros of
\zeta(s)
and
\operatorname{B}(s)
are
identical in the critical strip by (
70
), this representation
carries directly over to the Bernoulli case. Writing
\sigma=(1-s)/2
we get
(see figure
15
)

{\operatorname{B}(s)\,=\,\frac{\pi^{\sigma}}{\sigma!}\,\operatorname{H}_{\zeta}(s)\,,\quad s\notin\{-2,-4,-6,\dots\}.}

(79)

This is the
Hadamard decomposition of the Bernoulli function.

The zeros of
\operatorname{B}(s)
with
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\rho)=0
are
at
3,5,7,\ldots
(making the Bernoulli numbers vanish at these indices),
due to the factorial term in the denominator. This representation separates
the nontrivial zeros on the critical line from the trivial zeros on the real axes.

Here we see one more reason why
\operatorname{B}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}
.
The oscillating factor has the value
\frac{\pi^{0}}{0!}=1,

and the Hadamard factor has the value

\operatorname{H}_{\zeta}(1)=-\zeta(0)\cdot 1
.
The Bernoulli value follows from
\zeta(0)=-\frac{1}{2}
.

If we compare the identities
(\ref{q554})
and
(\ref{q56})
, we get as
a corollary
{\xi=\operatorname{H}_{\zeta}}.

This is precisely the proposition that Hadamard proves in his 1893 paper
[
19
]
.
Thus applying (
28
) to (
78
) leads
to Jensen’s formula for the Riemann
\xi
function,

\xi(s)\,=\,\frac{2(s/2)!}{\pi^{s/2-1}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{(\tfrac{1}{2}+ix)^{1-s}}{({\mathrm{e}}^{\pi x}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-\pi x})^{2}}\,dx.

(80)

32 – The André function

Many of the traditional integer sequences considered here are
signed, like the Bernoulli numbers and the Euler numbers.
However, under the influence of combinatorics, more and more the
unsigned versions of these numbers have come into focus.
The paradigmatic example are the absolute Euler numbers,
which we call André numbers.

The unsigned versions of the Euler secant and Euler tangent functions are defined as

\displaystyle\operatorname{|E|}_{\sigma}(s)\,=\,\cos(\pi s/2)\operatorname{E}_{\sigma}(s);

(108)

\displaystyle\operatorname{|E|}_{\tau}(s)\,=\,\sin(\pi s/2)\operatorname{E}_{\tau}(s).

(109)

The
André function
is defined as the sum of these two
unsigned Euler functions,

{\mathcal{A}(s)\,=\,\operatorname{|E|}_{\sigma}(s)+\operatorname{|E|}_{\tau}(s).}

(110)

Considering the long chain of definitions on which (
110
) is based,
it is astonishing how easily it can be represented by a single function.

{\mathcal{A}(s)\,=\,(-i)^{s+1}\operatorname{Li}_{-s}(i)+i^{s+1}\operatorname{Li}_{-s}(-i).}

(111)

Here
i
is the imaginary unit,
\operatorname{Li}_{s}(v)
is the polylogarithm,
and the principal branch of the logarithm is used for the powers.

For integer

\mathcal{A}_{n}=2\,(-i)^{n+1}\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(i).

(113)

(114)

35 – Asymptotics for the Bernoulli function

An asymptotic expansion of the Bernoulli function follows directly from

\operatorname{B}(s)=-\zeta(s)\,\tau(s)\,s!
by using
Stirling’s formula and the generalized harmonic numbers.

For the remainder term we will use the notation

\operatorname{R_{K}}(s)\,=\,\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\operatorname{K}}\frac{\operatorname{B}(n+1)}{n+1}\frac{s^{-n}}{n}\right).

(127)

For an
even positive integer

n
, the Bernoulli function has an
efficient asymptotic approximation
[
34
]
,
which reads with
\operatorname{R_{5}}(s)

{|\operatorname{B}(n)|\,\sim\,4\,\pi\,\left(\frac{n}{2\pi\mathrm{e}}\right)^{n+1/2}\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{n^{-1}}{12}-\frac{n^{-3}}{360}+\frac{n^{-5}}{1260}\right).}

(128)

The coefficients originate from the Stirling expansion of
\log(\Gamma(s))

(see
A046969
).

The number of exact decimal digits guaranteed
by (
128
) is
3\log(3\,n)
if
n\geq 50
.
The Boost
\operatorname{C}^{++}
library
[
10
]
uses
this approximation for huge arguments
n
.

Different asymptotic developments can be based on other expansions of
the Gamma function, for instance, on Binet’s formula
[
15
, p. 48,
A122252
]

generalized by Nemes
[
42
, 4.2]
. More general asymptotic
expansions and error bounds follow from those of the Hurwitz zeta
function established in Nemes
[
43
]
.

But much more is true: The assumption made for (
128
) that
n
is an even positive
integer can be dropped if one adds the factor
-\cos\left(\frac{s\pi}{2}\right)
to the right side.
This gives the asymptotic expansion of the Bernoulli function for
real
s>0
, with
\operatorname{K}
suitably chosen,

\operatorname{B}(s)\,\sim\,4\,\pi\,\left({\frac{s}{{{2\pi\mathrm{e}}}}}\right)^{s+1/2}\left(-\cos\left(\frac{s\pi}{2}\right)\right)\operatorname{R}_{\operatorname{K}}(s).

(129)

Moreover, the Seki
\mathcal{S}(s)
has the
corresponding expansion without the factor
-\cos\left(\frac{s\pi}{2}\right)
.

The close connection between the Bernoulli and the Euler numbers
is also reflected in the fact that the asymptotic development
of the Euler function differs formally only slightly from (
129
).

\operatorname{E}(s)\,\simeq\,-4\,\left({\frac{2s}{{{\pi\mathrm{e}}}}}\right)^{s+1/2}\left(-\cos\left(\frac{s\pi}{2}\right)\right)\operatorname{R}_{\operatorname{K}}(s).

(130)

From (
129
) and (
130
) asymptotic expansions for other functions can be
easily derived. As an example we show an asymptotic expansion of the logarithm of
the André function of order
O(s^{-7})
.

\log\mathcal{A}(s)\ \simeq\ \log(4)+\left(\frac{1}{2}+s\right)\log\left(\frac{2s}{\pi}\right)+\frac{(2/7)-s^{2}+30s^{4}-360s^{6}}{360s^{5}}

(131)

37 – Epilogue: Generating functions

The value of
\operatorname{B}(1)
deserves special attention. Since it is well known
that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{\gamma_{j}}/{j!}=1/2
, it follows from (
8
) that

\operatorname{B}(1)=1/2
. Unfortunately, the popular generating
function
z/(\mathrm{e}^{z}-1)
misses this value
and disrupts at this point the connection between the Bernoulli numbers
and the
\zeta
function.

For those who do not care about the connection with the zeta function, we add:
Even the most elementary relations between
the Bernoulli numbers and the Bernoulli polynomials break with this choice.
For instance, consider the basic identity (
41
).
It applies to all Bernoulli numbers if

\operatorname{B}_{n}=\operatorname{B}_{n}(1)

but not if
\operatorname{B}_{n}=\operatorname{B}_{n}(0)
is set.

Many other relations get restricted in their range of validity if the wrong choice is made,
for example, the relation between the Bernoulli numbers and the Eulerian numbers.
Such examples are described in the discussion
[
33
]
.

Instead, use the power series
f(z)
with the constant term
1
such that the
coefficient of
x^{n}
in
(f(x))^{n+1}
equals
1
for all
n
.
There is only one power series satisfying this condition, as Hirzebruch
[
23
]
observes.

This series is the
Todd function

(called after John Arthur Todd)

{\operatorname{T}(z)\,=\,\frac{z}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-z}}\,=\,1+\frac{1}{2}\frac{z}{1!}+\frac{1}{6}\frac{z^{2}}{2!}-\frac{1}{30}\frac{z^{4}}{4!}+\ldots}.

(132)

It generates the Bernoulli numbers matching the values of
the Bernoulli function at the nonnegative integers.

A modern exposition based on the Todd series is the monograph of
Arakawa, Ibukiyama, and Kaneko
[
5
]
. The authors adopt this
definition “because it is the original definition of Seki and Bernoulli
for one thing, and it is better suited to the special values of the
Riemann zeta function for another.”

Similarly, Neukirch in
Algebraic Number Theory

[
44
]
calls
the definition
f(z)=z/(1-\mathrm{e}^{-z})
“more natural and better
suited for the further development of the theory.”
One might hope that all mathematicians will support this simple step towards
greater consistency someday.
