A Survey on Decentralized Identifiers and Verifiable Credentials
Carlo Mazzocca, Abbas Acar, Selcuk Uluagac, Rebecca Montanari, Paolo Bellavista, Mauro Conti

Abstract
Digital identity has always been considered the keystone for implementing secure and trustworthy communications among parties. The ever-evolving digital landscape has gone through many technological transformations that have also affected the way entities are digitally identified. During this digital evolution, identity management has shifted from centralized to decentralized approaches. The last era of this journey is represented by the emerging Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI), which gives users full control over their data. SSI leverages decentralized identifiers (DIDs) and verifiable credentials (VCs), which have been recently standardized by the World Wide Web Community (W3C). These technologies have the potential to build more secure and decentralized digital identity systems, remarkably contributing to strengthening the security of communications that typically involve many distributed participants. It is worth noting that the scope of DIDs and VCs extends beyond individuals, encompassing a broad range of entities including cloud, edge, and Internet of Things (IoT) resources. However, due to their novelty, existing literature lacks a comprehensive survey on how DIDs and VCs have been employed in different application domains, which go beyond SSI systems. This paper provides readers with a comprehensive overview of such technologies from different perspectives. Specifically, we first provide the background on DIDs and VCs. Then, we analyze available implementations and offer an in-depth review of how these technologies have been employed across different use-case scenarios. Furthermore, we examine recent regulations and initiatives that have been emerging worldwide. Finally, we present some challenges that hinder their adoption in real-world scenarios and future research directions.

Introduction

The recent proliferation of digital services that can be accessed through a network connection has resulted in an unprecedented increase in the number of digital identities
[
1
]
. As a result, the vast majority of the world’s population owns at least one digital identity, which serves as the key to unlocking a multitude of online capabilities and services. However, the concept of digital identity extends far beyond the identification of human beings
[
2
]
. With the widespread adoption of the Internet of Things (IoT) and the power of the 5th generation (5G) networks, coupled with the upcoming advent of the 6th generation (6G), there has been a remarkable increase in the number of connected devices
[
3
]
. These devices require unique digital identities that enable their participation in the digital ecosystem, i.e., establishing secure communications.

How to identify entities in the digital landscape has always been a primary concern as also witnessed by the number of solutions that have occurred over the years. Indeed, the way an entity proves to be the owner of a digital identity can be affected by a wide range of vulnerabilities
[
4
]
.
One of the main challenges of traditional identification methods lies in the way they are managed, which usually involves centralized identity providers. Information is stored in centralized data repositories, potentially leading to serious data breaches that cause significant loss of personal data and disrupt the stakeholders’ reputation
[
5
,
6
]
. Moreover, in most cases, authentication still relies on the use of usernames and passwords, which is vulnerable to several attacks
[
7
]
(e.g., dictionary or phishing attacks). Storing credentials in centralized databases increases the risk to user security, especially when individuals reuse the same login information across multiple services
[
8
]
.
Finally, centralized identity management systems depend on specific identity service nodes, thus being potentially affected by the single point of failure problem
[
9
]
.

Some of these challenges have been partially addressed with the advent of federated identity systems
[
10
]
. The principal advantage of this identity model lies in empowering users to seamlessly access multiple services using the same identity. This identification paradigm relies on mutual trust among various parties, wherein verification is distributed across all identification systems or parties mutually accept the standards employed by each system. In a typical federated scenario, users authenticate themselves against a singular authority, referred to as the identity provider, granting them to access all other applications on its behalf. Despite representing progress toward decentralization, federated identity still raises significant concerns regarding user privacy. Specifically, this model hinges on a certain level of trust in identity providers, and individuals retain limited control over their data.

Therefore, the increasing use of online services, the growing decentralization, as well as the increasing awareness of the drawbacks of traditional methods are paving the way to more secure and privacy-preserving methods. In this direction, due to the current laws and regulations such as the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)
[
11
]
, the concept of
self-sovereign identity
(SSI)
[
12
]
is gaining much interest from both the academic and industrial worlds.

SSI is based on the idea that an individual must have full control of their information
[
13
]
without being forced to outsource data to any centralized authority or third party. With an SSI system, users can store their identity data and determine how much of it they wish to share. Thus, they have the autonomy to decide with whom they share their data. A fundamental achievement of SSI is the capability of a user to present their trusted credentials to a third party without having to engage an intermediary. This process is enabled through the ownership and control of decentralized identifiers (DIDs)
[
14
]
that define a globally unique and cryptographic identifier scheme. Each DID is associated with a DID Document, which contains publicly available information (e.g., public keys), and is stored on shared data sources such as distributed ledger technologies (DLTs). By presenting a DID, an individual can obtain verifiable credentials (VCs)
[
15
]
. These VCs constitute claims regarding the DID, commonly known as the subject, and can be verified by an external party without necessitating direct engagement with the VC issuer. For example, an individual applying for a job online could issue a digitally signed credential from their university, attesting to their acquisition of a bachelor’s degree and residency in the relevant country in which they are applying.

II
Fundamentals of Digital Identity

This section offers an overview of the development of digital identities with a particular focus on the standards and technologies that enable SSI systems.

II-A

Digital Identity Evolution

Digital identity development has gone through many eras, which have gradually shifted digital identification from centralized to decentralized models
[
26
]
. Figure
2
shows the digital identity evolution over time.

II-A
1
Centralized Identity

In centralized approaches, identities are managed by a central authority
[
27
]
.
One of the first forms of digital identity dates back to 1988 when the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority (IANA) was responsible for determining the validity of IP. Later, the scientific community witnessed the birth of other organizations such as the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN) for arbitrating the validity of domain names.

The core of the era of centralized identities is represented by users who are identified through a combination of usernames and passwords.
However, centralized approaches suffer from a single point of failure and the information related to users, including those sensitive, is managed by centralized entities, which represents a major concern due to the emerging privacy and law regulations. Password-based approaches also introduce further concerns
[
28
]
since users tend to prefer simplicity over security when choosing their password, making them vulnerable to several attacks, i.e. dictionary or phishing attacks. Additionally, individuals are required to have as many identities as the number of services, creating a fragmented landscape.

The widespread adoption of centralized models raises concerns also for service providers
[
29
]
, necessitating substantial investments in securely storing, preserving, and safeguarding user data in compliance with existing regulations.

II-A
2
Federated Identity

Federated identity represents the second era of digital identification, capable of overcoming some of the issues of centralized identification. Federated identity allows users to utilize the same identity across multiple sites and applications
[
30
]
. By logging in once, users can access various resources without the hassle of creating separate accounts for each system. This approach relies on trusted relationships between different parties to authenticate and authorize users across systems securely. Indeed, such a federation is achieved by distributing identification and verification components across all identification systems or mutually accepting standards used by different systems

One of the pioneers in proposing federated identity was Microsoft with its Passport program, which allows users to access different websites through a unique login. In 2001, Sun Microsystems formed the Liberty Alliance, also known as the Liberty Federation, a consortium aimed to develop open standards for federated identity and identity-based web services. Recently, major companies like Google and Meta have embraced single sign-on (SSO) support
[
31
]
. A typical federated scenario involves a user authenticating against a singular authority, referred to as the identity provider (e.g., Google or Meta identity providers), granting them to access all
other applications on its behalf.

While federated models have made notable progress in mitigating identity fragmentation concerns, they still pose considerable challenges to individual privacy. Despite a decrease in the number of centralized entities involved, users must place a certain level of trust in identity providers, which remain centralized and manage their data. Consequently, individuals experience limited control over their information. Furthermore, federated identity relies on mutual recognition between two or more parties, a process that is not always straightforward, particularly within large-scale systems
[
32
]
.

II-A
3
User-centric Identity

The growing demand for increased control over personal data has given rise to the concept of user-centric identity, where individuals have the power to manage their identities individually or across multiple authorities without relying on a centralized federation
[
33
,
34
]
. In this model, users retain complete autonomy and must explicitly grant consent before sharing or modifying their data, ensuring a heightened sense of privacy and security.

In 2005, the Identity Commons, an influential American organization with a mission to support, facilitate, and promote the creation of an open identity layer for the Internet, played a pivotal role in advocating for the Internet Identity Workshop (IIW). The IIW places users at the core of identity management, aiming to empower individuals in shaping their online identities. Moreover, it has been instrumental in fostering numerous renowned projects that embody user-centric principles, including OpenID, OpenID 2.0, OpenID Connect (OIDC), Open Authorization (OAuth), and Fast IDentity Online (FIDO)
[
35
,
34
,
36
]
. User-centric approaches grant individuals the authority to control their data through various authenticators (e.g., JSON Web Tokens) and certificates issued by different service providers, all securely stored in their devices.

Despite the notable progress in enabling users to directly manage their data, user-centric identity systems still depend on trust relationships with service providers and authorities. As a result, users must place their trust in these entities, hoping that they will handle their information responsibly and not misuse it
[
37
]
.

Overview of a DID-based architecture and the relationship of the main components.

II-A
4
Self-Sovereign Identity

SSI represents the last era of the long and evolving journey of digital identity development. The concept was first introduced in 2012 and stands as a significant advancement over user-centric identity systems
[
38
]
.

In contrast to conventional identity management systems where the service provider is at the center of the identity model, SSI places the individual at the core. Users are granted full control over their identities, enabling them to decide when, if, and how they wish to disclose or modify their data. The robustness of a secure SSI lies in its reliance on cutting-edge technologies, with a decentralized infrastructure serving as the foundation of its implementation.

The architecture of this identity model is enabled by emerging technologies such as DLTs, DIDs, and VCs. By leveraging DLTs like blockchain, SSI systems effectively eliminate the need for central authorities and foster a trustless environment where users can interact with services and applications without providing their sensitive information to a single entity
[
39
]
. This decentralized architecture ensures that data is cryptographically secured and verifiable, establishing a foundation of transparency and immutability for identity management.

In an SSI ecosystem, every individual is uniquely identified by DID, linked to a key pair that is under the direct management of the user. The public key bound to the identifier is shared on the DLT, ensuring accessibility for users to access such information and independently verify identities
[
12
]
. In this context, users can prove attributes or claims about themselves through VCs that can be cryptographically verified by any other parties without needing to consult a centralized authority. Such VCs are typically transmitted off-chain due to privacy considerations. Moreover, to verify the legitimacy of these credentials, the verifier leverages publicly available information associated with the identifier and the credential issuer.

The adoption of SSI marks a paradigm shift in how digital identity is conceptualized and implemented. This decentralized not only enhances security but also underscores the shift towards a trustless environment. In this landscape, individuals maintain control over their identity, determining how and when to share information.

II-B

Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs)

DIDs have emerged as a groundbreaking form of identifier widely embraced within decentralized identity systems. They were formalized and standardized by the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C)
[
40
]
that dedicated substantial resources and collaborative efforts from 2017 to 2019, ultimately culminating in the publication of the DID specification as an official W3C Recommendation. Fig.
3
reports a basic overview of the major components of a DID-based architecture.

A DID uniquely identifies a
DID Subject
, whether it is a human or non-human entity. It is a textual string comprising three essential components: the Uniform Resource Identifier, the identifier for the specific DID method, and the method-specific identifier for the DID. A DID method specifies the precise operation by which DIDs and
DID Document
are created, resolved, updated, and deactivated. A
DID URL
enhances a basic DID by including additional standard URI components like path, query, and fragment. This extension allows for precise resource location, such as a cryptographic public key within a DID Document or an external resource linked to the DID Document.

Each DID resolves to a DID Document, a machine-readable JSON-LD document, that contains various information about the DID Subject. In particular, it comprises cryptographic public keys, service endpoints, authentication parameters, timestamps, and additional metadata. DIDs have been designed to overcome identity providers and centralized authorities. An entity can prove the ownership of a DID by leveraging the private key corresponding to the public one embedded in the DID document. To verify the proof, the verifier will only need to access the DID Document, which is public and shared through a
Verifiable Data Registry
(VDR). Fig.
4
shows an example of DID and its corresponding DID Document.

The
DID Controller
refers to the entity with the authority to modify the DID Document. A DID might have more than one controller and, as shown in the example, it can coincide with the DID Subject.

Fig. 5:
Example of a VC.

Additionally, the DID specification
[
41
]
introduces three types of DIDs:

•

Anywise
: A DID can be utilized with an unspecified number of parties, typically strangers. It allows for broad usage without limiting the number of relationships that can be established.

•

Pairwise
: A DID is only known by its subject and precisely one other party, such as a service provider. Pairwise DIDs are employed to address privacy concerns by ensuring that each relationship has a unique DID, minimizing the risk of correlation between different interactions.

•

N-wise
: A DID designed to be known by strictly N parties, including the subject. It is a more general concept that encompasses pairwise DIDs as a special case when N equals 2.

DIDs are resolved through a universal resolver
[
42
]
that supports multiple DID systems. For a DID system to be compatible with the universal resolver, it needs to implement a DID adaptor that acts as an interface between the system-specific DID methods and the universal resolver.

The growing interest in DID has also resulted in novel DID-based communication protocols
[
43
,
44
]
to enable private and secure communication between two or more SSI entities in a peer-to-peer fashion. This protocol relies on DIDs and facilitates mutual authentication between the participating parties. The DID Auth protocol allows an identity owner to use their client application, such as a mobile device or browser, to prove their control over a DID to a service provider. This protocol utilizes a challenge-response cycle that can be customized based on the specific situation. By using the DID Auth protocol, traditional forms of authentication like usernames and passwords can be replaced, creating an authenticated communication channel between the identity owner and the service provider.

II-C

Verifiable Credentials (VCs)

VC is another specification developed by the W3C, aimed at creating an interoperable data structure capable of representing claims that are cryptographically verifiable and tamper-proof. Fig.
5
shows an example of VC that enables all the alumni of ”Example University” to get a discount on season tickets to sporting events.

Fig. 6:
Overview of VC main actors.

In the VC ecosystem, a holder refers to an entity exercising control over one or more VCs. On the other hand, an issuer (e.g., banks and government agencies) is responsible for creating and issuing new VCs. Finally, a verifier is an entity that obtains one or more VCs to perform verification. For instance, an e-commerce website that requires credentials from its customers acts as a verifier. Fig.
6
illustrates the key roles within the ecosystem of verifiable credentials.

To facilitate the creation and verification of identifiers, keys, verifiable credentials schemas, and other relevant data essential for using VCs, a VDR plays a crucial role. This registry acts as a mediator within the ecosystem. As for DID, VCs also consist of several elements. These include the URI of the subject, the URI of the issuer responsible for the claims, and URIs that uniquely identify the credential. The URI associated with the subject serves the purpose of retrieving the subject’s public key, ensuring the verification of the credential’s ownership. Simultaneously, the URI linked to the issuer is essential for obtaining their public key and verifying that the credential originates from a trusted entity. Notably, the URI can also take the form of a DID.

Furthermore, a VC includes claim expiration conditions and cryptographic signatures. This inclusion ensures that the credential’s validity is maintained over time and that the integrity of the information is secured through cryptographic verification.

It is worth noting that the W3C Verifiable Credentials Working Group has also defined the concept of Verifiable Presentations (VPs), which specify the methods for signing and presenting VCs by the holder. VCs or VPs can be described using JSON-LD, JSON, or JSON Web Token formats. Fig.
7
shows the VP obtained from the VC of the previous example. The standard related to expressing verifiable information on the Web has been published to provide a regulation specification for expressing credentials on the Internet; so, they are machine-verifiable, respect privacy, and, most importantly, are cryptographically secure.

Example of VP.

II-D

Verifiable Data Registry (VDR)

In the context of DIDs and VCs, a VDR plays a crucial role in facilitating the creation, management, and verification of identifiers, keys, credential schemas, and other relevant data required to utilize these decentralized identity technologies
[
45
]
.

A VDR acts as a trusted intermediary or mediator within the ecosystem of DIDs and VCs. It serves as a repository or database that stores and provides access to essential information related to DIDs and VCs. This includes the storage of DID Documents, which contain information about DIDs, such as cryptographic public keys, service endpoints, authentication parameters, timestamps, and metadata. The VDR ensures the availability and accessibility of these DID Documents to support the resolution and verification processes.

The VDR enables the verification of DIDs and VCs by providing a trusted and reliable source of information. When a verifier needs to verify a DID or a VC, it can retrieve the relevant information from the VDR. By accessing the public information stored in the registry, the verifier can validate the authenticity, integrity, and validity of the DIDs and VCs involved in the verification process. This allows for the establishment of trust and confidence in the decentralized identity ecosystem.

The VDR also plays a role in managing and maintaining the lifecycle of DIDs and VCs. It facilitates the creation, registration, and revocation of DIDs and VCs by acting as a centralized point of control or coordination. For example, when an entity wants to create a new DID or issue a new VC, it can interact with the VDR to ensure the proper registration and management of the associated information. Similarly, when a DID or VC needs to be revoked or updated, the VDR can handle the necessary operations to reflect the changes in the system. Additionally, the VDR can support interoperability and standardization within the decentralized identity ecosystem. It can enforce consistent data formats, validation rules, and data-sharing protocols, ensuring that the information stored in the registry adheres to the established standards and specifications. This promotes compatibility and seamless integration across different DID methods, VC issuers, and verifiers.

The W3C’s standards currently lack explicit specifications regarding how the VDR is realized. Most of the proposed approaches are based on DLTs
[
46
]
, decentralized ledger networks leveraging the computational resources of numerous nodes to ensure the security and transparency of data. Among these,
blockchain
stands out as the most popular type of DLT, characterized by interconnected blocks
[
47
]
. Each block has a reference to the previous block’s hash, thus building a secure chain. Any tampering would lead to a different hash that can be easily detected. This feature is fundamental to enable the secure sharing of information such as DID Documents. Additionally, many blockchains also support
smart contract

[
48
]
, which are self-executing programs stored directly on the blockchain and triggered when specific conditions are met. This inherent design ensures fault tolerance, tamper-proofing, and traceability. In the subsequent sections, we will show how these features make smart contracts a valuable technology in various application domains utilizing DIDs and VCs.

It is worth noting that alternative solutions have also emerged. For example, Information-Centric Networking (ICN) is a novel networking paradigm that can be adopted to implement the registry
[
49
]
. Users interact with edge nodes to manage DIDs through HTTP APIs, which are then translated into appropriate ICN flows.

III
Implementations

The successful implementation of DIDs and VCs plays a crucial role in the adoption of SSI systems and the development of next-generation services. Developers require efficient implementations that allow them to leverage these technologies with minimal complexity while adhering to standard specifications.

While initially conceived to empower individuals with comprehensive control over their data, the applicability of DIDs and VCs extends beyond human identities. These standards hold the potential to be extended to anything, including cloud, edge, and IoT devices, which feature heterogeneous storage and computing capabilities. Given this diversity in
resources, the broad adoption of these technologies demands implementations that efficiently minimize storage and computing overhead.

Currently, the development of DIDs and VCs is still in its early stages, resulting in a limited number of available implementations. However, our examination of the existing frameworks offers developers and stakeholders valuable insights into the key differences among the main available solutions, guiding them in selecting the implementation more suitable for their use cases.

Notably, efforts to enhance the efficiency and usability of these implementations are ongoing, including optimization techniques, interoperability frameworks, and standardization efforts to ensure seamless integration and adoption.
Furthermore, as technology advances and the ecosystem matures, we can expect a broader range of efficient and scalable implementations of DIDs and VCs. Table

IOTA Identity Framework

The IOTA Identity framework
[
51
]
implements the most common standards and patterns for managing decentralized identity in both a DLT agnostic and
iota
method specification manner. It is designed by IOTA, a next-generation DLT engineered for the IoT world. The IOTA Identity Framework enables the creation of new digital identities for anyone or anything (including IoT devices) at any time. The functionalities are offered both in Rust and Web Assembly (WASM). The IOTA identity framework has been designed to implement SSI on IOTA, whose data structure is a direct acyclic graph (DAG) that provides some unique features:

•

Feeless
: IOTA does not foresee any miners or validators; therefore, messages are stored without incurring any cost. Thus, SSI applications can be directly deployed on the main network, enabling anyone to create identities at no cost and favoring worldwide inclusion.

•

Ease-of-use
: IOTA Identity can be used without any cryptocurrency token. Furthermore, the framework offers simple APIs that allow standardized and flexible behavior. Finally, IOTA Identity implements a solution named
stronghold
to securely manage secrets.

•

General Purpose DLT
: IOTA is a general-purpose DLT, which means that SSI can integrated with other DLT features such as payments, data streams, smart contracts, and access control.

Veramo

Veramo
[
54
]
is a JavaScript Framework designed for managing DIDs and VCs. It is flexible and modular, making it suitable for adapting to complex workflows. A Veramo Agent embodies an Agent implementation that leverages a plugin-driven architecture. This architecture allows Veramo to be modular, scalable, and potentially integrate with the extensive spectrum of emerging standards in the landscape of verifiable data. Veramo Agent is responsible for creating identifiers, resolving identifiers, credential issuance, credential revocation, credential exchange, and secret application hot sauce. Furthermore, it is multi-platform since it runs on Node, Browsers, and React Native straight out of the box.

The architecture of Veramo comprises a core component and a suite of plugins. The core serves as the gateway to the API, seamlessly binding the plugins together and fostering their interoperability. Depending on the selection of plugins, the Veramo Agent can assume various roles, adapting to diverse use cases. The available plugins encompass many functionalities, including DID Methods, messaging protocols, storage solutions, key management, and many others.

Finally, Veramo extends its versatility by providing a Command Line Interface (CLI) tool. This tool offers the convenience of swiftly creating DIDs and VCs directly from a terminal or running a local cloud agent, streamlining development and testing processes.

III-F

Lessons Learned

The exploration of the most popular implementations and frameworks highlights their adherence to the W3C standard, open-source nature, and readiness for deployment - three key requirements to favor the widespread adoption of DIDs and VCs. In particular, this adherence ensures seamless interoperability across different systems, a crucial aspect for the successful integration of these identity technologies. By reviewing implementations, we observe that some of them are more generic and web-centric (i.e., DIDKit, Hyperledger Aires, and Veramo), while others are more tailored for specific application domains. Consequently, developers must select a framework based on their needs and application scenarios.

Among the existing implementations, DIDKit offers developers higher flexibility by supporting a variety of popular programming languages. Its inclusion of a ready-to-deploy HTTP/HTTPS server makes it particularly valuable for developers to approach these standards for the first time. Similarly, Hyperledger Aires and Veramo are designed to simplify the creation of decentralized identity applications. The choice between DIDKit and Hyperledger may hinge on the developer’s language preferences, given their support for different programming languages. Conversely, while Veramo is modular and adaptable to complex workflows, its limitation to JavaScript may influence developers’ decisions.

As outlined throughout this survey, the scope of DIDs and VCs does not encompass only individuals, being potentially extensible to anyone and anything. In this context, the IOTA Identity framework emerges as a natural and promising solution to empower the use of these technologies in real-world scenarios, involving humans and machines (e.g., IoT devices). Since IOTA is a distributed ledger, it not only facilitates the use of these technologies in diverse scenarios but also inherently provides a working VDR. This eliminates the need for additional configuration of another DLT to disseminate publicly available information like DID Documents.

For developers focusing on mobile-centric solutions, the Microsoft Entra Wallet Library stands out as it is specifically designed for managing DIDs and VCs on various mobile devices (e.g., smartphones and smartwatches), featuring iOS and Android operating systems. This implementation empowers developers to provide end-users with comprehensive control over their data through their preferred mobile devices. The active involvement of major technology companies is crucial in advancing these technologies and delivering solutions seamlessly integrated into the modern digital landscape.

Vehicular Communications

In V2V communications, DIDs and VCs ensure integrity, authentication, confidentiality, and privacy without any trusted authority. D-V2X
[
55
]
is a blockchain-based decentralized vehicle-to-anything (V2X) protocol that does not require any trusted party and can be implemented on top of any communication protocol. The key element is represented by the decentralized vehicular PKI (D-VPKI), which replaces the vehicular public-key infrastructure (VPKI) in V2X.
To link a VIN to the vehicle using a blockchain, the vehicle registers a random DID into the D-VPKI. Then, the original equipment manufacturer (OEM) issues a VC embedding the VIN and any other relevant information. Considering the most decentralized configuration, the vehicle holds the subject and holder. Thus, every time the vehicle needs to prove that its DID is associated with its VIN, it will provide the VC. To preserve privacy, a vehicle registers many different DIDs, which are linked to the master DID through zero-knowledge protocol (ZKP)-enabled VCs issued by the OEM that will remove them after their creation. Alternatively, to prevent the OEM from inferring the original DID, VCs can be issued by leveraging secure multiparty computation. To use pseudonyms, a vehicle creates two VPs: one demonstrating that the new pseudonym and the previous one belong to the same master DID and a second to prove the validity of the master DID without revealing it. In addition, to prove location and avoid GPS spoofing attacks, when a vehicle passes nearby to an infrastructure element (e.g., a traffic light) it is provided with a VC that comprises information about its location.

Privacy-preserving computation is one of the major concerns in communications. In this direction, another approach to avoid tracking participants in V2X communications consists of changing the blockchain address while transferring current reputations
[
56
]
. This mechanism prevents an external observer from inferring that the two addresses belong to the same vehicle. A new address is locally generated by the vehicle using a secret
K
, derived from the private key of its DID. The addresses form a deterministic chain that can be reconstructed for auditing or investigation purposes. To transfer reputation, the vehicle produces a promise comprising the novel address, the reputation value, and a random nonce. Such information is hashed and sent, along with a ZKP, to a smart contract that verifies the proof, checks the correctness of the reputation balance, and stores the hash in a Merkle Tree. In addition, the old address is removed from the reputation mapping. Finally, the reputation is released by the smart contract to whoever can prove the control of the note.

Furthermore, they enable authenticated communications with other vehicles. When a vehicle receives a message, it authenticates the sender and verifies if the sender’s DID is included in the list of users authorized in the underlying layer blockchain, as well as its reputation that must exceed a predetermined threshold. Once the receiver evaluates the veracity of the event described in the message, it reports feedback to the RSU, which updates the sender’s reputation in the underlying blockchain.

The integration of DIDs, VCs, and DLTs holds significant promise for the industrial supply chain within the industry ecosystem. By leveraging these technologies, various aspects of supply chain and logistics activities can be improved, enhancing transparency, traceability, and interoperability.

Subsequently, a verifier can retrieve the VC by collecting the hash from the blockchain and accessing the corresponding data on IPFS. This process ensures the integrity and authenticity of the certifications, providing a robust mechanism for trust within the supply chain. Fig.

Users and Devices

CanDID
[
113
]
is a decentralized identity system that enables issuing credentials in a user-friendly manner. The identity subsystem ports identities and credentials securely from existing web services (e.g., social media platforms and online bank accounts), thus it can create trustworthy credentials without the need to explicitly create DID-compatible credentials. CanDID also offers a key recovery service that allows users to effectively retrieve their keys.

BIdM
[
114
]
is a decentralized cross-domain identity management system that operates on blockchain technology. The system uses DIDs to identify identities and remove any single-point dependencies. The public key corresponding to each identity is retrieved via a blockchain-based identity provider, ensuring secure and trustworthy identity management. BIdM’s design includes a one-way accumulator that accumulates valid key-value pairs, composed of DIDs and public keys and enables efficient proof of membership. These accumulator states are stored on a blockchain consortium system, which prevents a single point of failure and ensures synchronization across multiple domains.
For cross-domain authentication, the identity-relying party retrieves the authenticated entity’s information and confirms the identity’s authenticity via the accumulator. Additionally, to better represent the user’s identity and attributes, BIdM divides identities into master and shadow. The master identity corresponds to the DID used to identify users uniquely in blockchain networks, while the shadow identity is issued by the identity provider and stored offline. SmartDID
[
115
]
is another blockchain-based distributed identity system for IoT. Beyond the identity system, it also comprises a dual-credential mode and a distributed proof system. The dual-credential mode is based on commitment and zero-knowledge proofs to protect the privacy of sensitive attributes, on-chain identity data, and linkage of credentials.

SSIBAC
[
116
]
is an access control model based on the XACML specification that leverages DIDs, VCs, and blockchain technologies to provide privacy and data sovereignty. VCs are encoded in VPs and mapped to access control policies, which are stored in a policy retrieval point. Access requests are regulated through permission validators that bind VPs to attributes, roles, or other data abstractions. VCs are associated with different validators according to their input. Thus, each VC corresponds to an attribute or role specified by an access control policy.

To add an extra layer of security, SSI identity frameworks can be integrated with additional authentication protocols
[
117
]
like FIDO
[
35
]
. This strategic combination provides an added layer of protection, mitigating the risk of personal data compromise and preventing unauthorized access to resources, even in the event of identity theft. Each individual uses a FIDO authenticator that generates a locally stored key pair serving as their credentials. This key pair is linked to the user’s DID, facilitating identification and verification of ownership of VCs. In cases where users lack external authentication devices, such as USB tokens, they can opt for a Trusted Execution Environment (TEE) to function as a FIDO authenticator.

IV-E
2
Services and Data

IPFS is a decentralized protocol for storing and sharing data, where each item is identified by a content identifier (CID) obtained by applying a hash function. However, any modification to the data would result in a new CID, which can cause issues in managing the identifiers at scale. The integration of DID and DNS can help to address this concern
[
118
]
. DIDs are used as content names, and DNSlink enables the binding of a DID to a CID. The content owner publishes a self-verifiable content item on IPFS, which includes the DID document, proof, signed metadata, and the item itself. The generated DID is associated with the CID through DNSlink, allowing the location of a content item to know its DID. Thus, every time the CID returned by IPFS is modified, the corresponding DNS record is updated.

Beyond data storage, DIDs can also be extended to service discovery in peer-to-peer networks by using them to identify threads as global resources
[
119
]
. DID and VCs are enabling technologies to allow identity verification among multiple entities that belong to different organizations. However, they are usually employed to verify identities within a single blockchain. Enabling cross-chain verification of DIDs belonging to different blockchains can favor communication and collaboration among entities that need to establish trust. For example, in JointCloud, a novel cloud computing paradigm that connects different clouds, to manage the heterogeneity of VCs issued by multiple organizations, it is possible to leverage a novel data structure signed by any entity, called verifiable claim
[
120
]
. To conduct verification of signed claims on other blockchains, the verifier executes cross-chain contracts and, if allowed, acquires and verifies the signed claims. To enhance the trustworthiness of this approach, verifiable claims, signers, and verifiers are assigned a credibility value.

The use of DIDs can be also extended to build self-verifiable content advertisement in named data networking (NDN)
[
121
]
, which enables routing information through content identifiers instead of network location identifiers. Content owners use hierarchical DIDs as content names and authorize a publisher to advertise a content name prefix to a specific router. To secure content advertisement, the content owner generates a DID document including the JWK representation of a key. Thus, the publisher with that key will be authorized to advertise that content.

IV-F

Lessons Learned

The review of the existing body of research outlines that DIDs and VCs have found applications across seemingly unrelated fields, including smart transportation and smart healthcare. Despite the dissimilarity, in most of the considered domains, these technologies are used as integral components in realizing SSI systems.

A key feature of DIDs and VCs is their capacity to empower individuals with complete control over their data, enabling selective information sharing. Consequently, communication becomes more secure and the risk of potential data breaches is reduced, contributing to building a decentralized and safer digital landscape. Moreover, as underscored by the differences between application domains, these digital identities are utilized across a multitude of different platforms and applications, thereby favoring interoperability and improving user experience. Notably, VCs can seamlessly enable access to both educational and healthcare services.

While existing literature primarily focuses on DIDs and VCs as building blocks for SSI,
their ability to provide decentralized verification and the use of DLTs extends beyond, paving the way for fulfilling the objectives of various applications. As highlighted in the literature review, DIDs and VCs can be leveraged in many scenarios where decentralized verification and/or immutability are required, and human involvement is not the primary focus. For instance, in vehicular scenarios, these technologies are used to enable secure and verifiable communications between vehicles and roadside units
[
55
]
. Similarly, the Gnomon framework
[
83
]
employs DIDs and VCs to securely update firmware on devices.

Finally, as discussed in Section
III
, there is a connection between the chosen implementation and the application domain. For example, referring to the frameworks reported in Table
III
, in contexts such as smart agriculture and smart transportation, where entities like smart sensors and vehicles require digital identities, IOTA Identity stands as a natural solution. On the other hand, for individuals who tend to prefer managing their digital credentials through mobile applications, e.g., storing medical certificates or travel information, the Microsoft Entra Wallet offers all the necessary functionalities.

The eIDAS regulation is based on the federated identities model, wherein users are registered with an identity provider. In this ecosystem, each EUDIW is associated with a DID, and the credentials stored in the EUDIW are in the form of VCs issued by eIDAS issuers. The verification keys of these credentials need to be publicly accessible by anyone for any use case, ensuring transparency and interoperability.

Lessons Learned

The analysis of emerging regulations, projects, and organizations underscores the global momentum behind DIDs and VCs. These technologies are not only advancing digital identity but also empowering individuals, communities, and organizations across continents. Europe is undergoing a remarkable digital transformation, moving towards the SSI model, as exemplified by eIDAS 2.0. Moreover, many countries are actively working on national SSI frameworks, recognizing the importance of tailoring digital identity solutions to local needs and regulations.

In North America, government commitment to promoting the adoption of DIDs and VCs is evident. The US DHS is currently seeking innovative solutions for the privacy-preserving digital credential ecosystem, highlighting the potential of such technologies. The Canadian Digital Identity and Authentication Council is shaping the Pan-Canadian Trust Framework, emphasizing trust and interoperability in the digital identity ecosystem.

Argentina is the most active country in the South America. The DIDI project exemplifies how DIDs and VCs can allow vulnerable populations to break socioeconomic and financial barriers. Among Asian countries, South Korea stands as one of the leaders in adopting DIDs and VCs in digital identity solutions. These initiatives reflect the forward-thinking approach to digital identity and its potential to transform various sectors, including finance and public services. In late 2023, China unveiled its plans for the introduction of a new digital identity service based on DIDs and VCs, supporting the vision that these technologies will experience a significant boost shortly.

DIDs and VCs are also envisioned as valuable technologies to promote inclusivity and face the unique challenges that affect Africa. Cross-jurisdictional use cases demonstrate the potential of VCs to enhance digital identity across borders.

VI
Main Features of DIDs and VCs

The comprehensive examination of existing literature on DIDs and VCs, coupled with the analysis of emerging regulations and initiatives worldwide, underscores the crucial role these emerging standards play in shaping both the current and future digital identity landscapes. This section presents the main features of DIDs and VCs.

VI-A

DID Features

1.

Self-Sovereignty
: Users have control and ownership over their DIDs, which can be created and managed by themselves. This self-sovereignty makes individuals independent from central authorities or third-party entities.

2.

Inclusivity
: DIDs facilitate inclusivity by providing digital access to individuals in least-developed countries lacking traditional forms of identification such as government-issued IDs.

3.

Privacy and Security
: DIDs allow users to selectively disclose information, preventing the oversharing of personal data. Employing cryptographic techniques like PKI for key generation, digital signature, and encryption, DIDs ensure robust defenses against unauthorized access and data breaches. Additionally, the use of decentralized networks such as DLT reduces the risk of a single point of failure, enhancing overall security.

4.

Interoperability and Portability
: DIDs are designed to be compatible with various platforms, fostering seamless integration and communication across different systems. Moreover, they promote portability by enabling users to easily transfer their identifiers and associated data across various platforms.

5.

Persistence
: DIDs are consistent and permanent, offering a reliable means of identification even as individuals switch service providers or platforms.

VI-B

VC Features

1.

Decentralized Verification
: Entities issuing VCs are inherently trusted, ensuring reliable verification of credential authenticity and contained information. VCs can be verified independently at any location and time without direct interaction with the issuer.

2.

Privacy and Security
: VCs support ZKP and selective disclosure, granting users the autonomy to share only necessary information with verifiers, without compromising entire credentials. Personal information is not stored on Distributed Ledger Technologies DLTs or centralized systems, minimizing the risk of data breaches. Moreover, by providing data owners full control over their data, VCs can help organizations become compliant with existing regulations such as the GDPR.

3.

Interoperability and Portability
: W3C standards promote a standardized approach to VC implementation, ensuring high interoperability and consistency. VCs are designed to seamlessly operate across different platforms and applications. Individuals can store VCs in their digital wallet and take them anywhere while still being verifiable, offering users flexibility and convenience in presenting their credentials.

4.

Temper-Resistance
: VCs are cryptographically signed, making them resistant to tampering or unauthorized alterations, thereby ensuring the integrity of the information included.

VII
Challenges & Future Research Directions

From the extensive review of the applications of DIDs and VCs, we identify possible challenges and future research directions.

VII-A

Standardization

Although DIDS and VCs have been standardized by the W3C,
there are several standardization challenges related to their content and protocols at different levels. In this direction, one of the primary concerns is related to the fact that DID method specifications present differences in format, completeness of information, information density, and even versioning of the specifications
[
149
]
. This issue also affects VCs since there are just a few attempts of standardization that are related to specific application domains, such as EBSI which is actively working to define an education credential accepted by different European universities
[
102
]
. On the other hand, there are some other contexts where the use of VCs is in the very early stages and a standardized data structure would be valuable to favor their adoption. For example, IoT lacks standardization for VCs shared among devices.

It is worth noting that standardization challenges go beyond the content and data structure of DIDs and VCs, as they also affect the related protocols from different perspectives. At the state-of-the-art, many aspects are still non-normative. For example, from a security point of view, building a consensus on these security standards can be complex. Currently, there are no specifications available on cryptographic algorithms or key management practices. These considerations can be extended to other levels including lifecycle management, interoperability, laws, regulations, etc.

VII-B

Seamless Integration

The implementation of DIDs and VCs faces significant challenges when attempting to seamlessly integrate with legacy systems. Current solutions that adopt these technologies usually consider a collaborative environment where a community of issuers of VCs actively participates. However, this assumption does not always align with reality, as only a limited number of entities are ready to issue VCs. Traditional issuers of digital credentials may be unwilling to issue signed variants like VCs
[
113
]
.

In practice, the broad-scale adoption of these technologies demands the usage of VCs constructed from data available in existing and unmodified services
[
150
,
151
]
. Overcoming the inertia associated with legacy systems and encouraging seamless interoperability between traditional and decentralized credentialing processes emerge as critical challenges in ensuring the effective integration and widespread acceptance of DIDs and VCs. Addressing these challenges requires innovative approaches that facilitate a smooth transition for legacy systems, fostering an environment where both traditional and decentralized credential issuers can coexist and collaborate seamlessly.

VII-C

Digital Wallet

The practical use of digital identities relies heavily on the existence of a control system that empowers users to manage them efficiently. This involves inspecting the status of their entitlements, overseeing data, and granting consent for, as well as initiating, information disclosure—similar to the handling of physical credentials. To achieve this in the decentralized identity landscape, individuals should have the capability to securely store their DIDs and VCS in a user-friendly and secure manner. In the decentralized identity context, the tool offering users control over their data is known as a digital wallet
[
152
]
. Unlike conventional storage in large databases, which raises ethical, security, and privacy concerns
[
153
]
, the use of a digital wallet provides a more personalized and secure approach. Such instruments Digital wallets should possess the functionality to store both VCs and the associated cryptographic key pairs of DIDs. This information enables users to respond to various service providers, even when an internet connection is unavailable (e.g., using Bluetooth or NFC). Additionally, the independence of wallet software from any control by identity providers or relying parties is crucial to ensure user autonomy and security.

The interest in digital wallets is remarkably growing all over the world. For instance, by 2024, all EU member states are mandated to make an EUDIW available to every interested citizen
[
154
]
. Consequently, a hot research avenue involves not only the design and development of digital wallets but also the exploration and evaluation of worthwhile applications that support and enhance their functionality.

VII-D

Application Domains

As outlined in Section
IV
, DIDs and VCs have demonstrated their versatility and applicability across a wide array of domains for distinct purposes. Nevertheless, upon a comprehensive review of the existing literature, it becomes evident that there exists a notable disparity in the adoption of these technologies across various application domains. In some domains, we find a robust body of literature and practical implementations, reflecting a consistent and well-established presence of DIDs and VCs. Conversely, in certain other fields, the utilization of DIDs and VCs remains comparatively limited, despite the potential these technologies hold to effectively address prevailing privacy and security challenges. To bridge this gap and fully unlock their potential, future research should also focus on under-considered domains. In the following, we discuss some of them.

VII-D
1
E-voting

We claim that DIDs and VCs are very promising in the development of novel e-voting systems that securely store all the preferences without leaking any information about the individuals
[
155
]
. In this context, DIDs are used to uniquely identify voters, while VCs are issued by trusted entities, such as government agencies or identity providers, confirming a voter’s eligibility without exposing their details. DIDs and VCs, however, allow for the creation of pseudonymous, unlinkable digital identities. Voters can cast their ballots without revealing their real-world identity, and these votes can be securely recorded on the blockchain, ensuring privacy. Furthermore, the use of DIDs and VCs opens up opportunities for remote and mobile voting. Indeed, voters can potentially participate from anywhere, reducing geographical barriers and increasing overall voter turnout. These technologies provide a strong foundation for end-to-end verifiable e-voting systems, ensuring that votes are accurately counted and users can verify to have been included in the count
[
156
]
.

However, it is important to note that the adoption of DIDs and VCs in e-voting systems also brings challenges. Standardization, trust frameworks, and robust authentication mechanisms must be established to create a resilient and trustworthy e-voting ecosystem.

VII-D
2
Digital Twin

In the context of DTs, there is only a work that proposes the use of such technologies despite their capacity to establish trustworthiness among entities, which is a crucial concern in implementing digital replicas of objects and environments
[
157
]
. DIDs and VCs provide a standardized and secure way to verify the authenticity of data sources, ensuring that the data used to create and update the digital twin is accurate and reliable. Trustworthiness also encompasses the assurance that the data collected from the physical entity and mirrored in the digital twin remains unaltered and secure throughout its lifecycle. DIDs provide immutable linking of data sources to DTs, reducing the risk of unauthorized tampering or data breaches. On the other hand, VCs offer a mechanism to ensure that the entities providing data to the digital twin can be authenticated, enhancing the overall security of the system. Moreover, DTs often interact with a multitude of systems and entities, making interoperability and cross-domain trust essential. DIDs and VCs offer a standardized way to establish trust and data exchange across domains.

VII-D
3
Smart Grid

Furthermore, there are also suitable to enhance next-generation services such as Grid-to-Go
[
158
]
, which consider a scenario where a landlord of an Airbnb has to pay the energy bill for their tenants although they may consume an excessive amount of energy (e.g., charging electric vehicles). To avoid such a concern, smart meters can be programmed to charge the tenant by routing consumption measurements from the landlord’s utility to the tenant’s utility. The use of DIDs can be utilized to provide among participants in a decentralized way, avoiding placing trust in specific entities.

VII-E

Security and Privacy

Although DIDs and VCs offer many security benefits especially in bolstering privacy and providing users greater control over their data, some security issues need to be further explored.

VII-E
1
DID Management

Granting third parties access to manage DID documents introduces potential vulnerabilities that must be approached with caution. One such risk is the possibility of the DID controller impersonating the DID owner by generating a new key pair and modifying the public key in the DID document. Authorization and auditing mechanisms are needed to detect and prevent unauthorized modifications. The public key associated with a DID is usually registered to a blockchain enabling access to other nodes of the network. The compromising of the corresponding private key is then followed by key rotation that generates and registers a new key pair. To replace the current key pair, the DID owner publishes the novel public key signed with the previous one
[
159
]
. Since the moment at which the private key cannot be known in advance, to avoid malicious attacks based on the compromised private key, entities can only update the key pair regularly to reduce the vulnerability window. This is a primary concern for a DID-based system since compromising the private key will lead to complete control of the attacker over that identity. Furthermore, the continuous generation of novel key pairs also exhibits usability issues. To tackle such challenges, DIDs should support flexible key rotation
[
160
]
. The security credentials of the DID can be enriched with a one-time password (OTP) based on the hash chain that serves as nonce and second-factor authentication for key rotation. In this way, the attacker must obtain the OTP and the private key to steal the victim’s digital identity.

VII-E
2
VC Revocation

Despite the growing interest in VCs, the current landscape lacks a substantial body of research focused on developing novel and efficient approaches for their revocation. At the time of this paper, there is only one specification proposed, which does not hold the status of a W3C standard, nor is it on the W3C standards track. This specification, known as ”Revocation List 2020”
[
161
]
, revolves around a bitstring-based mechanism for managing revocations. In this arrangement, each bit within the bitstring corresponds to an index stored in the VCs. When the bit is set to 1 the VC is revoked, otherwise it is still valid. It is worth noting that the degree of privacy achievable through this approach is directly linked to the size of the revocation list. A large number of VCs issued makes it more difficult to correlate a VC to the owner. Another favorable facet of this strategy is its potential for compression.

Given that the majority of credentials remain valid, lengthy stretches of bits often retain identical values. This trait lends itself to compression techniques like ZLIB
[
162
]
, allowing for the reduction of the bitstring’s size. For instance, a 16KB bitstring housing only a handful of revoked VCs could potentially be compressed down to a mere few hundred bytes. To visualize this, refer to Fig.
15
for an illustrative representation of Revocation List 2020 in action.

VII-E
3
Accountability

Ensuring privacy while simultaneously adhering to existing regulations, such as Know-Your-Customer (KYC)
[
163
]
and Anti-Money-Laundering (AML)
[
164
]
, poses a significant challenge for DIDs. DIDs are designed with privacy preservation as a core objective, but reconciling this feature with the need to screen users presents several complexities. Modern systems leveraging DIDs should meet these regulations and be able to screen users of the system. Specifically, they should be able to identify and validate credentials associated with users. This becomes crucial for screening individuals against various lists, such as sanctions lists, to determine if any preventative actions, like blacklisting, are warranted.

VII-E
4
Privacy

Research in this domain should explore novel methods and technologies that can seamlessly integrate privacy-preserving features within DIDs while concurrently enabling compliance with regulatory measures. This involves developing cryptographic techniques or privacy-enhancing protocols that empower DIDs to provide verifiable information without compromising the sensitive details of users.

VIII
Conclusions

Digital identification has always been a persistent concern since the first inception of the Internet. Over the years, a multitude of solutions have been proposed, and the contemporary landscape reflects a shift towards decentralization, prioritizing end-user control over their data and aligning with evolving privacy regulations. However, the concept of digital identification is not limited to individuals, being potentially extendible to anyone and anything, including cloud, edge, and IoT resources.

In this article, we present a comprehensive survey on DIDs and VCs, two building blocks to authenticate and authorize entities in the modern digital landscape, enabling secure and trustworthy communication. This survey aims to comprehensively analyze DIDs and VCs in terms of implementations, application domains, and regulations. Through the investigation of the available implementations available, we provide developers with valuable insights into the features of existing libraries, facilitating informed choices across different projects. While the analysis of the application DIDs and VCs demonstrates that their utility goes far beyond SSI systems. Furthermore, our survey also explores global initiatives and projects spearheaded by governments and political institutions, offering a tangible glimpse into the trajectory DIDs and VCs are taking on a global scale. Finally, as these technologies are relatively novel, we outline emerging challenges and highlight avenues for future research, serving as a valuable starting point for ongoing and future research endeavors in this field.
