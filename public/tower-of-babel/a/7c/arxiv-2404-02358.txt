2
Symmetric spaces and KP time-optimal control

2.1
Overview - setting the scene

Unitary motion is defined by the Schrödinger equation:

\displaystyle\frac{dU}{dt}=-iHU

(1)

whose solution is often formally written as the time-ordered exponential:

\displaystyle U(T)=\mathcal{T}e^{-i\int_{0}^{T}H(t)dt}U_{0}.

(2)

Usually, these kind of equations are considered as matrix or operator equations in a given representation. However, such equations can be considered more fundamentally differential geometric in nature. This is because time-ordered exponentials can be expanded in commutators which themselves have a structure that is often universal. Universal in this context refers to the fact that a matrix representation can be one of an infinite number of otherwise inequivalent representations. Perhaps the most useful and well-known example is angular momentum, with commutators that define the group of virtual rotations. To emphasize angular momenta and rotations as universal is to appreciate that they are concepts which precede or transcend any particular matrix representations, and this universality serves as a platform for understanding details such as energy quantization.

The differential geometry of universal structures was a famous passion of Poincaré, who originated the ideas of “universal covering group” and “universal enveloping algebra”, and Klein, for whom the “universal properties” of category theory are an homage. In quantum physics however, although it is certainly useful to have an intuitive understanding that rotation is universal, there is usually no need to work directly with things like universal covers. Instead, the standard is to assume a Hilbert space and immediately consider angular momenta as acting on states to logically derive the spin quantum numbers and consequent matrix representations. Indeed, this quantum standard to act on states is why equation (
1
) is often called “Schrödinger.” Followed by the basic fact that the matrix group
SU(2)
is the fundamental representation of the universal cover of 3-dimensional rotations, one can then just use this matrix group (and perhaps a little good faith) to come to understand the universal. However, if more general transformation groups are considered, calculations with matrix representations can make the universal geometry they are representing far less apparent.

This article demonstrates that the so-called “K-P” problems, can be solved completely with the help of some universal techniques. The “K-P” problems are a family of unitary control problems where the Hamiltonian control space
\mathfrak{p}
is a Lie triple system, defined by the property
[\mathfrak{p},[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}]]\subseteq\mathfrak{p}
, and the target Hamiltonian is in
\mathfrak{k}=[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}]
. We expand on this below.

\displaystyle dUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=k\bigg[k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}dk+\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})+i\Big(d\Theta+\sin\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})\Big)\bigg]k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

The latter two sets of parameters are the coordinates of a symmetric space. Presented in this way, the Schrödinger equation represents a Maurer-Cartan (differential) form

For our purposes, it allows us to interpret the right-hand side in terms of the relevant principal bundle connection. Intuitively, a connection provides a way to differentiate sections of the bundle and to parallel transport along curves in the base manifold
G

\displaystyle i\Big(d\Theta+\sin\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})\Big)

(11)

This path is a sequence of quantum gates or the circuit. In quantum mechanics, we are interested in minimising the external time parameter. For this, we typically minimise the action such that the total time of a circuit evolving is:

\displaystyle\Omega T=\int_{\gamma}\!\sqrt{(idUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1},idUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})}

2.5
Holonomy targets

The local frame has two coordinate systems that are particular, the cardinal (basis) frame “
k
” and the geodesic frame “
q
.” The cardinal frame describes the local, compact part of the group, while the geodesic frame describes the global, non-compact part of the group.
Define a target in the holonomy group
K
to be one such that:

\displaystyle U(T)U(0)^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=e^{iX}\in K.

(15)

Holonomy group refers to the set of group transformations a vector undergoes when it is parallel transported around a closed loop in a manifold. For symmetric spaces, the choice of connection gives rise to an implicit subgroup
K<G
which acts as a holonomy group. Defining the connection for the geodesic frame:

\displaystyle q^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}dq=c^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\Big[(1-\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta})(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})\Big]c.

(16)

such that the Hamiltonian does not contain elements of
\mathfrak{k}
. The intuition for a qubit is that rotations achieved via

are by construction parallel transporting vectors via the action of
[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}]\subset\mathfrak{k}
. Where
J_{z}
is not in the control subalgebra, a way to parallel transport such vectors, achieving a
J_{z}
rotation, but using other generators not in
K
is needed. For constant-
\theta
paths, equation (
15

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}_{\Phi}(\Theta)=\Theta\hskip 50.0pt\text{and therefore}\hskip 50.0ptX=(1-\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta})\Phi.

(17)

Note the first of these conditions is explicated as a condition that the generators comprising
\Phi\in\mathfrak{k}
belong to the commutant (see Appendix for discussion). Holonomic targets may be, under certain assumptions, generated via unitaries

which in a Riemannian context are paths with zero geodesic curvature, indicating parallel translation in a geometric sense. By contrast, where the manifold is subRiemannian (i.e. where
\mathfrak{p}<\mathfrak{g}
) then subRiemannian geodesics may exhibit non-zero geodesic curvature by comparison to

\mathfrak{g}

Symmetric space controls in
\mathfrak{p}

Summarising the above, our conjecture is that for symmetric space controls in

For such constant-

\displaystyle\Omega T=\min_{\Theta,\Phi}\Big|\sin\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(\Phi)\Big|

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}_{\Phi}(\Theta)=\Theta

\displaystyle X=(1-\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta})\Phi.

K=S(U(1)\times U(1))=\text{span}\{e^{i\eta\sigma_{z}}\}
. Further define Pauli matrices
\{\sigma_{k}\}
with angular momenta
J_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{k}
. Define the Cartan conjugation
\chi
as:

\displaystyle U^{\chi}=e^{-iJ_{z}\pi}U^{\dagger}e^{iJ_{z}\pi}

(29)

together with a Cartan projection
\pi(U)=U^{\chi}U
. To define the relevant Cartan decomposition, we note the quotient group
G/K
corresponds to the symmetric space:

\displaystyle S^{2}\approx\frac{SU(2)}{S(U(1)\times U(1))}\equiv\text{AIII(1,1)}

(30)

where
S^{2}=\pi(G)=K/G
. As distinct from the projection in
[
42
]
, the projection is representation independent. The Cartan projection above defines the relevant
G=KAK
decomposition. The corresponding Cartan decomposition of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
satisfying equations (
6
) is:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(2)=\underbrace{\braket{-iJ_{z}}}_{\mathfrak{k}}\oplus\underbrace{\braket{-iJ_{x},-iJ_{y}}}_{\mathfrak{p}}

(31)

with Cartan subalgebra chosen to be
\mathfrak{a}=\braket{-iJ_{y}}
. We can easily see the Cartan commutation relations (equation (
6
)) hold. For
S^{2}
above this corresponds to the Euler decomposition:

\displaystyle U=e^{iJ_{z}\psi}e^{iJ_{y}\theta}e^{iJ_{z}\phi}

(32)

Define:

\displaystyle k=e^{iJ_{z}\psi}\qquad i\Theta=iJ_{y}\theta\qquad c=e^{iJ_{z}\phi}\qquad q=kc=e^{iJ_{z}\chi}

(33)

to represent the unitary as:

\displaystyle U=ke^{i\Theta}c=qe^{ic^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\Theta c}

(34)

Under this change of variables, the Cartan conjugation becomes:

\displaystyle k^{\chi}=k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\qquad(e^{i\Theta})^{\chi}=e^{i\Theta}\qquad(UV)^{\chi}=V^{\chi}U^{\chi}

(35)

with (using the adjoint action):

\displaystyle\pi(ke^{i\Theta}c)=e^{2c^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}i\Theta c}

(36)

The Schrodinger equation then becomes:

\displaystyle dUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

\displaystyle=k\left(k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}dk+\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})+id\Theta+i\sin\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})\right)k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(37)

\displaystyle=k\Big(iJ_{z}(d\psi+d\phi\cos(\theta))+iJ_{y}d\theta-iJ_{x}d\phi\sin(\theta)\Big)k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(38)

by taking the relevant differentials and inverses in equation (
33
), using equation (
241
) and calculating the adjoint action on
c
. Under this Cartan decomposition, we see equation (
38
) partitioned into symmetric
\mathfrak{k}
and antisymmetric
\mathfrak{p}
part:

\displaystyle dUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=k\Big(\underbrace{iJ_{z}(d\psi+d\phi\cos(\theta)}_{\in\mathfrak{k}})+\underbrace{iJ_{y}d\theta-iJ_{x}d\phi\sin(\theta)}_{\in\mathfrak{p}}\Big)k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(39)

Restricting
dUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\in\mathfrak{p}
is equivalent to defining a minimal connection
d\psi=-d\phi\cos\theta
. That is, effectively setting the symmetric part of the Hamiltonian to zero (or in geometric parlance, restricting to the horizontal subspace corresponding to the underlying subRiemannian manifold). The Hamiltonian becomes:

\displaystyle H=i\frac{dU}{dt}U^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=k\Big(J_{x}\dot{\phi}\sin(\theta)-J_{y}\dot{\theta}\Big)k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}

(40)

where we have defined conjugate momenta
\dot{\phi},\dot{\theta}
for extremisation below. To calculate the optimal time, we first define the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
for which we require normalisation of the extremised action in (such that we can define an appropriately scaled norm and metric). For a subRiemannian space of interest in the adjoint representation, the Euclidean norm is then simply defined in terms of the Killing form as
|X|=\sqrt{(X,X)}
such that:

\displaystyle|idUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|^{2}=(d\psi+d\phi\cos(\theta))^{2}+d\theta^{2}+(d\phi\sin(\theta))^{2}

(41)

where
|J_{z}|^{2}=\mathbb{I}
. Define the energy of the Hamiltonian as
|H|=\Omega
such that:

\displaystyle\Omega t=\Omega\int_{\gamma}dt=\int_{\gamma}|idUU^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}|=\int_{\gamma}\left|i\frac{dU}{ds}U^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\right|ds

(42)

Here
\gamma
defines the curve along the manifold generated by the Hamiltonian and
t
the time elapsed. Calculating path length here is equivalent to approximating time elapsed modulo
\Omega
. Consistent with typical differential geometric methods, we parametrise by arc length
ds
[
43
]
. Define:

\displaystyle\dot{t}=\left|i\frac{dU}{ds}U^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\right|=\frac{dt}{ds}

(43)

setting optimal (minimal) time as:

\displaystyle T=\min_{\gamma}t=\min\left|\int_{\gamma}\dot{t}ds\right|

(44)

Extremisation can be performed using the method of Lagrange multipliers and the minimal connection above. As we demonstrate below, doing so in conjunction with the constant-
\theta
assumption simplifies the variational problem of estimating minimal time. The relevant action is given by:

\displaystyle S=\Omega t=\int_{\gamma}\Big(\dot{t}+\lambda J_{z}(\dot{\psi}+\dot{\phi}\cos(\theta))\Big)ds

(45)

noting again the role of the connection. Parametrising by arc length
s
we have:

\displaystyle\left|i\frac{dU}{ds}U^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\right|^{2}=(\dot{\psi}+\dot{\phi}\cos(\theta))^{2}+\dot{\theta}^{2}+(\dot{\phi}\sin(\theta))^{2}

(46)

Extremising the action
\delta S=0
resolves the canonical position and momenta via the equation above as:

\displaystyle\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\dot{\psi}}=\frac{1}{\dot{t}}\big(\dot{\psi}+\dot{\phi}\cos(\theta)\big)+\lambda

(47)

\displaystyle\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\dot{\phi}}=\frac{\dot{\phi}}{\dot{t}}+\left(\frac{\dot{\psi}}{\dot{t}}+\lambda\right)\cos(\theta)

(48)

\displaystyle\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\dot{\theta}}=\frac{\dot{\theta}}{\dot{t}}

(49)

\displaystyle\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\theta}=-\left(\frac{\dot{\psi}}{\dot{t}}+\lambda\right)\dot{\phi}\sin(\theta)

(50)

\displaystyle\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\lambda}=\dot{\psi}+\dot{\phi}\cos(\theta)

(51)

where we have assumed vanishing quadratic infinitesimals to first order e.g.
d\phi^{2}=0
. We note that given
\lambda
is constant, it does not affect the total time
T
. The choice of
\lambda
can be considered a global gauge degree of freedom i.e.
\frac{\partial T}{\partial\lambda}=0
(i.e. regardless of
\lambda
minimal time
T
remains the same). The minimal connection constraint (see Appendix (
52
)):

\displaystyle k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}dk=-\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

(52)

can be written as:

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}=-\dot{\phi}\cos(\theta)

(53)

as we have specified
k
. The connection and equation (
51
) imply that
\dot{\psi}(s)
becomes a local gauge degree of freedom in that it can vary from point to point along the path parameter
s
without affecting the physics of the system (i.e. the rate of change of
\psi
can vary from point to point without affecting the energy
\Omega
or time
T
of the system). That is:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta T}{\delta\dot{\psi}}=0

(54)

We can simplify the equations of motion by setting a gauge fixing condition (sometimes called a gauge trajectory). Thus we select:

\displaystyle\dot{\psi}/\dot{t}+\lambda=0

(55)

Recalling extremisation via
dS=0
, we find equations (
48
) and (
49
) become:

\displaystyle dS=\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\dot{\phi}}+\Omega\frac{\delta t}{\delta\dot{\theta}}=\frac{\dot{\phi}}{\dot{t}}+\frac{\dot{\theta}}{\dot{t}}=0

(56)

Thus
\dot{\phi}/\dot{t}
and
\dot{\theta}/\dot{t}
are constant by the constant-
\theta
assumption i.e.
\dot{\theta}=0
. Minimising over constant
\dot{\theta}
:

\displaystyle\Omega\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot{\theta}}=(\dot{\theta}/\dot{t})\int_{\gamma}ds=0

(57)

confirms the independence of
T
from
\theta
(i.e. as
\dot{t}
and the path-length
\int_{\gamma}ds\neq 0
). Combining the above results reduces the integrand in equation (
41
) to dependence on the
d\phi\sin\theta
term (as the minimal connection condition and constant
\theta
condition cause the first two terms to vanish).
Such simplifications then mean optimal time is found via minimisation over initial conditions in equation (
44
):

\displaystyle T=\min_{\theta,\phi}\left|\int_{\gamma}d\phi\sin\theta\right|=\min|\phi\sin\theta|\qquad\phi=\int_{\gamma}d\phi

(58)

Note by comparison with the general form of equation (
218
), here the
\phi\sin\theta
term represents
\sin\text{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})=\sin\text{ad}_{\Theta}(\Phi)
. The above method shows how the constant-
\theta
method simplifies the overall extremisation making the minimisation for
T
manageable.

Now consider holonomy targets of the form:

\displaystyle U(T)U_{0}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=e^{-iX}=e^{iJ_{z}2\eta}\in K

(59)

with controls only in
\mathfrak{p}=\{iJ_{x},iJ_{y}\}
. By assumption
U
is of
KAK
form (equation (
34
)):

\displaystyle U(T)U_{0}^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=e^{-iX}=e^{iJ_{z}2\eta}=ke^{i\Theta}c=qe^{ic^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}\Theta c}

(60)

Choosing the initial condition as:

\displaystyle U_{0}=e^{i\Theta}

(61)

The simplest form is when
c
resolves to identity. This in turn requires
c\in K
to resolve to the identity:

\displaystyle M\equiv\Big\{c\in K:c\Theta c^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}=\Theta\Big\}=\{\pm\mathbb{I}\}

(62)

Given we have a single element in
\mathfrak{k}
, this is equivalent
c=\exp(iJ_{z}\phi)
where:

\displaystyle\phi=2\pi n

(63)

for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. In general we must optimise over choices of
n
, which in this case is simply
n=1
. From this choice of initial condition we have:

\displaystyle q(T)

\displaystyle=k(T)c(T)=e^{-iX}

(64)

\displaystyle q(T)

\displaystyle=e^{iJ_{z}\psi}e^{iJ_{z}\phi}=e^{iJ_{z}(\psi+\phi)}

(65)

Note this is a relatively simple form of

as
\Phi
comprises only a single generator
J_{z}
.
This condition is equivalent to:

\displaystyle 2\eta=\int_{\gamma}d\chi=\int_{\gamma}d\psi+d\phi=2\pi n(1-\cos(\theta))

(66)

Here we have again used the minimal connection constraint (equation (
53
)) for substitution of variables. Thus:

\displaystyle\cos(\theta)=1-\frac{\eta}{n\pi}

(67)

Substituting into equation (
58
) we have that:

\displaystyle\Omega T

\displaystyle=\min_{n}2\pi n\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{n\pi}-\left(\frac{\eta}{n\pi}\right)^{2}}

(68)

\displaystyle=2\min_{n}\sqrt{\eta(2\pi n-\eta)}

(69)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\eta(2\pi-\eta)}

(70)

where we have used:

\displaystyle\sin^{2}\theta=\left(1-\frac{\eta}{n\pi}\right)^{2}=\frac{2\eta}{n\pi}-\left(\frac{\eta}{n\pi}\right)^{2}

(71)

using trigonometric identities and setting
n=1
. Note the time optimality is consistent with
[
42
]
, namely:

\displaystyle T=\frac{2\sqrt{\eta(2\pi-\eta)}}{\Omega}

We now have the optimal time in terms of the parametrised angle of rotation for

\psi/t+\lambda=0
) such that:

\displaystyle=-\psi(T)/T

and per time interval

4
Discussion

We have demonstrated that for specific categories of quantum control problems, particularly those where the antisymmetric centralizer generators parameterised by angle
\theta
remain constant, it is possible to obtain analytic solutions for time-optimal circuit synthesis for non-exceptional symmetric spaces using a global Cartan decomposition. This is particularly true when the control subalgebras are restricted to cases where the Hamiltonian consists of generators from a horizontal distribution (bracket-generating
[
1
,
37
]
)
\mathfrak{p}
with
\mathfrak{p}\neq\mathfrak{g}
(where the vertical subspace is not null). Direct access is only available to subalgebras
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathfrak{g}
. However, we have shown that if the assumptions
[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}]\subset\mathfrak{k}
and
d\Theta=0
hold, arbitrary generators in
\mathfrak{k}
can be indirectly synthesised (via application of Lie brackets), which in turn makes the entirety of
\mathfrak{g}
available in optimal time. Geometrically, we have demonstrated a method for synthesis of time-optimal subRiemannian geodesics using
\mathfrak{p}
. Note that, as mentioned in the example for
SU(2)
above, subRiemannian geodesics may exhibit non-zero curvature with respect to the entire manifold, but this is to be expected where we are limited to a control subalgebra. Consequently, in principle, arbitrary
U_{T}\in G
(rather than just)
U_{T}\in G/K
becomes reachable in a control sense.

Appendix B
Adjoint action and commutation relations

The adjoint action of group elements can be expressed via conjugation as
\text{Ad}_{h}(g)=hgh^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}
where
g,h\in G
. The adjoint action of a Lie algebra upon itself, which we focus on, is via the commutator namely
\mathrm{ad}_{X}(Y)=[X,Y]

X,Y\in\mathfrak{g}
. Thus terms such as
\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(\Phi)

\displaystyle e^{i\Theta}Xe^{-i\Theta}=e^{i\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}}(X)=\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(X)+i\sin\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(X)

(241)

The
\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(X)
term can be understood in terms of the cosine expansion:

\displaystyle\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(X)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n)!}(\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}))^{2n}(X)

(242)

Thus each term is a multiple of
\mathrm{ad}_{\theta}^{2}
. For [certain] choices of generators
\Theta
and

this effectively means that [acts as an involution up to coefficients]. Thus in the
SU(2)
case,
\mathrm{ad}^{2n}_{J_{y}}(J_{z})\propto J_{z}
(with
n=0,1,2,...
). Each application of the adjoint action acquires a
\theta^{2}
term (from
\Theta=i\theta J_{y}
), such that the series can be written:

\displaystyle\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(-iJ_{z})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{(2n)!}(\theta))^{2n}(J_{z})=\cos(\theta)(-iJ_{z})

(243)

More generally, the Cartan commutation relations exhibit such an [involutive] property given that for
\Theta\in\mathfrak{k}

\displaystyle\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(\mathfrak{k})=[\Theta,\mathfrak{k}]\in\mathfrak{p}\qquad\mathrm{ad}^{2}_{\Theta}(\mathfrak{k})=[\Theta,[\Theta,\mathfrak{k}]]\in\mathfrak{k}

(244)

In the general case, assuming an appropriately chosen representation, each application of the adjoint action by even
n
or odd

A simple application of the product rule and the Euler formula:

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{p},[\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}]]\subset\mathfrak{p}

\displaystyle k^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1}dk=-\cos\mathrm{ad}_{\Theta}(dcc^{\,\text{-}\hskip-1.0pt1})

k(t)
