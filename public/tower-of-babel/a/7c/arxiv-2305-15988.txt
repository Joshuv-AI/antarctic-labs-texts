Non-Log-Concave and Nonsmooth Sampling via Langevin Monte Carlo Algorithms
Tim Tsz-Kit Lau, Han Liu, Thomas Pock

Abstract
We study the problem of approximate sampling from non-log-concave distributions, e.g., Gaussian mixtures, which is often challenging even in low dimensions due to their multimodality. We focus on performing this task via Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods derived from discretizations of the overdamped Langevin diffusions, which are commonly known as Langevin Monte Carlo algorithms. Furthermore, we are also interested in two nonsmooth cases for which a large class of proximal MCMC methods have been developed: (i) a nonsmooth prior is considered with a Gaussian mixture likelihood; (ii) a Laplacian mixture distribution. Such nonsmooth and non-log-concave sampling tasks arise from a wide range of applications to Bayesian inference and imaging inverse problems such as image deconvolution. We perform numerical simulations to compare the performance of most commonly used Langevin Monte Carlo algorithms.

Introduction

Sampling efficiently from a high-dimensional target distribution
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-U}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has been a long-standing problem in various scientific and engineering disciplines, including statistics, applied probability, physics, machine learning and signal processing.
The task of drawing samples efficiently from high-dimensional complex probability distributions enables us to perform inference using complex statistical models from large amounts of data, where uncertainty quantification is of paramount importance to understand the intrinsic risk associated with every decision made with models learned from data. The ability to quantify uncertainty when comparing a theoretical or computational model to observations is critical to conducting a sound scientific investigation, particularly in machine-learned models and in the physical sciences like physics
[
96

. More specifically, Bayesian inference

[

While Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods

In particular, in modern large-scale applications such as Bayesian deep learning in the overparameterized regime in which we want to make posterior inference on the neural network weights, traditional MCMC algorithms become computationally prohibitive in such high dimensions and alternative approaches such as variational inference (VI; see e.g.,

Preliminaries

In this section, we introduce the formulation of our problem of interest and necessary technical tools to facilitate the discussion of various LMC algorithms.

2.1
Problem Formulation

We are concerned with the problem of generating samples from a probability distribution
\pi
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
which admits a density, with slight abuse of notation, also denoted by
\pi
, with respect to the Lebesgue measure

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\pi(\bm{x})=\left.\mathrm{e}^{-U(\bm{x})}\,\middle/\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(\bm{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y}\right.,

(2.1)

where the
potential

U\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is measurable and we assume that
0<\int_{\mathcal{U}}\mathrm{e}^{-U(\bm{y})}\,\mathrm{d}\bm{y}<+\infty
for
\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\dom U
. We also write
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-U}
for (
2.1
). Usually, the number of dimensions
d\gg 1
.

Specifically, we consider mixture distributions, e.g., Gaussian mixtures, which are weighted averages of distributions of the same class but with possibly different parameters. For examples, Gaussian mixtures involve Gaussians with possibly different means and covariance matrices. More specifically, for
\bm{\omega}=(\omega_{k})_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}\in\Delta^{K}
, the density of a
\bm{\omega}
-mixture is given by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad p(\bm{x})\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}p_{k}(\bm{x}),

where, for a
\bm{\omega}
-Gaussian mixture, we have, for each
k\in\llbracket K\rrbracket
,

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad p_{k}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det(2\uppi\bm{\Sigma}_{k})}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k})\right\rangle\right\},

which is the density of a
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution with mean
\bm{\mu}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
.
The corresponding
potential
is defined by
U(\bm{x})\coloneqq-\log p(\bm{x})
, which is nonconvex.

In various Bayesian imaging inverse problems and high-dimensional Bayesian sparse regression problems, in order to exploit prior knowledge available, prior distributions are often introduced. Here we consider priors
\varrho\propto\mathrm{e}^{-g}
with
g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and possibly nonsmooth, i.e., priors which are log-concave yet not necessarily log-smooth. More notable examples include the
\ell_{1}
-norm (i.e., Laplacian prior), and the isotropic/1D (e.g., for trend filtering
[
115
]
and fused lasso
[
187
,
188
]
) and anisotropic/2D (for images) total-variation (TV) pseudonorms
[
37
]
:

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad g_{1}(\bm{x})=\tau\left\|\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\|_{{1}}\quad\text{or}\quad g_{2}(\bm{x})=\tau\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{1-2},

(2.2)

where
\tau>0
is a regularization parameter,
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1-2}
is the composite
\ell_{1}
-
\ell_{2}
norm and
\bm{D}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
is a linear operator. For instance,
\bm{D}=\bm{I}_{d}
in
g_{1}
corresponds to the
\ell_{1}
-norm, and
\bm{D}
being the
first-order differencing matrix
in
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
corresponds to the 1D-TV and 2D-TV pseudonorms respectively. For Bayesian posterior inference, we then want to sample from the posterior distribution
\pi(\bm{x})\propto p(\bm{x})\times\varrho(\bm{x})
. Notice that, in various Bayesian imaging inverse problems such as denoising, deconvolution and reconstruction, the likelihood term
p(\bm{x})
is usually a less complicated Gaussian likelihood. We consider Gaussian mixtures instead for generality.

On the other hand, in numerical simulations, we are also interested in generating samples from a mixture of non-log-smooth distributions. In particular, we consider a
\bm{\lambda}
-(isotropic) Laplacian mixture, where, for
k\in\llbracket K\rrbracket
, each of the
K
Laplacian distributions has a density:

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad p_{k}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\frac{\alpha_{k}^{d}}{2^{d}}\exp\left\{-\alpha_{k}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k}\right\|_{{1}}\right\},

(2.3)

where
\bm{\mu}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a location parameter and
\alpha_{k}>0
is a scale parameter.

Definition 2.1

(Proximity operator)
.

For
\lambda>0
, the
proximity operator
of a function

is defined by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\prox_{\lambda h}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}.

Definition 2.2

(Moreau envelope)
.

For
\lambda>0
, the
Moreau envelope
, also known as the
Moreau–Yosida regularization
of a function
h\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, is defined by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad h_{\lambda}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\}.

Since
h
is convex, by
[
175
, Theorem 2.26]
, the Moreau envelope is well-defined, convex and continuously differentiable with its gradient given by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla h_{\lambda}(\bm{x})=\lambda^{-1}\left(\bm{x}-\prox_{\lambda h}(\bm{x})\right).

The notions of Moreau envelope and proximity operator can be extended with Bregman divergences
[
29
]
. We will need to introduce the notions of Legendre functions and Fenchel conjugate before formally defining Bregman divergences.

Definition 2.3

(Legendre functions)
.

Let
\varphi\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
A function
\varphi
is called (i)
essentially smooth
, if it is differentiable on
\interior\dom\varphi\neq\varnothing
and
\|\nabla\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})\|\to+\infty
whenever
\bm{x}_{n}\to\bm{x}\in\operatorname{bdry}\dom\varphi
; (ii)
essentially strictly convex
, if it is strictly convex on
\interior\dom\varphi
; (iii)

, if it is both essentially smooth and essentially strictly convex.

Definition 2.4

(Fenchel conjugate)
.

The
Fenchel conjugate
of a proper function
h
is defined by

h^{*}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\sup_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\left\{\left\langle\bm{y},\bm{x}\right\rangle-h(\bm{y})\right\}
.
For a Legendre function
\varphi
, it is well known that
\nabla\varphi\colon\interior\dom\varphi\to\interior\dom\varphi^{*}
with
(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}=\nabla\varphi^{*}
.

Definition 2.5

(Bregman divergence)
.

The
Bregman divergence
between
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
associated with a Legendre function
\varphi
is defined through

D_{\varphi}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,+\infty]\colon(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mapsto\begin{cases}\varphi(\bm{x})-\varphi(\bm{y})-\left\langle\nabla\varphi(\bm{y}),\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\rangle,&if $\bm{y}\in\interior\dom\varphi$,\\
+\infty,&otherwise.\end{cases}

[

,

,

,
14
]

Definition 2.6

(Bregman proximity operators)
.

For
\lambda>0
, the

and

of

associated with a Legendre function
\varphi
are respectively defined by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\interior\dom\varphi)\quad\begin{aligned} \overleftarrow{\operatorname{P}}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace\lambda,h}^{\varphi}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{\lambda}D_{\varphi}(\bm{y},\bm{x})\right\},\\
\overrightarrow{\operatorname{P}}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace\lambda,h}^{\varphi}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{\lambda}D_{\varphi}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\right\}.\end{aligned}

Definition 2.7

(Bregman–Moreau envelopes)
.

For
\lambda>0
, the
left
and
right Bregman–Moreau envelopes
of

associated with a Legendre function
\varphi
are respectively defined by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\begin{aligned} \overleftarrow{\mathrm{env}}_{\lambda,h}^{\varphi}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq\inf_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{\lambda}D_{\varphi}(\bm{y},\bm{x})\right\},\\
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{env}}_{\lambda,h}^{\varphi}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq\inf_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{\lambda}D_{\varphi}(\bm{x},\bm{y})\right\}.\end{aligned}

When

Under some rather technical conditions

(\forall\bm{x}\in\interior\dom\varphi)\quad\begin{aligned} \nabla\overleftarrow{\mathrm{env}}_{\lambda,h}^{\varphi}(\bm{x})&=\frac{1}{\lambda}\nabla^{2}\varphi(\bm{x})\left(\bm{x}-\overleftarrow{\operatorname{P}}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace\lambda,h}^{\varphi}(\bm{x})\right),\\

3
Langevin Monte Carlo Algorithms

The Langevin Monte Carlo (LMC) algorithm is based on the Euler–Maruyama (EM) discretization of the overdamped Langevin diffusion. Lots of variants of the LMC algorithm have also been proposed in the literature to improve sampling efficiency and handle structural properties of target distributions. In this section, we give a brief review on LMC algorithms, which are used as an umbrella term to refer to as the class of MCMC algorithms motivated by the overdamped Langevin diffusion.

3.1
Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm

Let us recall that we are interested in sampling from the target distribution
\pi
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose density is only known up to a constant. With slight abuse of notation,
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-U}
also denotes the density of the target distribution with respect to the Lebesgue measure, where
U\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is called the
potential
of the distribution
\pi
. We also assume that
U
is Lipschitz smooth, i.e., there exists a Lipschitz constant
L>0
such that
\left\lVert\nabla U(\bm{x})-\nabla U(\bm{y})\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant L\left\lVert\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\rVert_{2}
for any
(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and that
U\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
The
overdamped Langevin diffusion
is a stochastic differential equation given by, for
t\geqslant 0
,

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\nabla U(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

(3.1)

where
(W_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion. It is well-known that under mild appropriate conditions on
U
, this equation has a unique strong solution. Furthermore, if
\mathrm{e}^{-U}
is integrable, then
\pi\propto\mathrm{e}^{-U}
is the unique invariant distribution of the semigroup associated with (
3.1
).

Using the first-order Euler–Maruyama (EM) discretization of the overdamped Langevin diffusion, we obtain the
Langevin Monte Carlo
(LMC) algorithm (see e.g.,
[
158
,
153
]
), which is arguably the most widely-studied gradient-based MCMC algorithm:

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\bm{x}_{n}-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n},

(3.2)

where
(\bm{\xi}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of i.i.d.
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random variables and
\gamma>0
is a given step size. Possibly replaced with a varying step size
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the LMC algorithm is also referred to as the
unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(ULA)
[
82
,
173
]
. Notice that we use constant step sizes
\gamma>0
when describing the LMC algorithms but nonincreasing step size sequences
(\gamma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be used in practice.

3.2
Metropolis-Adjusted Langevin Algorithm

Due to the discretization, ULA gives biased samples depending on the step size. To adjust for the bias and ensure that the resulting algorithm has exactly the correct invariant distribution, the Metropolis–Hastings (MH) correction can be used. Applying a Metropolis–Hastings (MH) correction step at each iteration (as a proposal) of (
3.2
) yields the
Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm
(MALA)
[
173
,
170
,
89
]
.

To be specific, the MH correction step operates as follows.
Given a proposal
\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1}
and the current sample
\bm{x}_{n}
, the proposal is accepted with the acceptance probability

\alpha=\alpha(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1},\bm{x}_{n})\coloneqq\frac{p(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1})q_{\gamma}(\bm{x}_{n}\,|\,\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1})}{p(\bm{x}_{n})q_{\gamma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1}\,|\,\bm{x}_{n})}\wedge 1,

(3.3)

with

q_{\gamma}(\widetilde{\bm{x}}\,|\,\bm{x})=\phi_{d}(\widetilde{\bm{x}};\bm{x}-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}),2\gamma\bm{I}_{d})\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4\gamma}\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{x}}-(\bm{x}-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\},

where
\phi_{d}(\cdot;\bm{\mu},\bm{\Sigma})
denotes the
d
-dimensional Gaussian density with mean
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}
. In general, the MH correction step can be applied to any proposal.
Now, defining
b_{n+1}\coloneqq\mathbb{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(\alpha(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1},\bm{x}_{n})-u_{n}\right)
with a sequence of
\mathsf{U}(0,1)
random variables
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, MALA then iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\begin{aligned} \widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1}&=\bm{x}_{n}-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n},\\
\bm{x}_{n+1}&=b_{n+1}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1}+(1-b_{n+1})\bm{x}_{n}.\end{aligned}

(3.4)

3.3
Preconditioned Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm

In convex optimization algorithms, to accelerate convergence, usually a preconditioning matrix can be used in order to exploit the local geometry of the objective function (cf. Newton’s method). In a similar vein, we can also apply a preconditioning matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
in ULA. This algorithm is called the
preconditioned unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(PULA), which iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\bm{x}_{n}-\gamma\bm{M}\nabla U(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\bm{M}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}_{n}.

(3.5)

Indeed, the preconditioning matrix can be either state-dependent or state-independent. Usually, state-dependent preconditioning matrices can help exploit the local geometry of the current sample, but would incur more computations. A common choice of the preconditioning matrix is
\bm{M}(\bm{x})=[\nabla^{2}U(\bm{x})]^{-1}
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the inverse Hessian of the potential (which is indeed the
Fisher metric
; see e.g.,
[
102
,
135
]
). Under this context, PULA can be interpreted as ULA directly on the natural Riemannian manifold where the parameters live. There are also alternative strategies to choose the metric
\bm{M}(\bm{x})
, see e.g., the SoftAbs metric proposed in
[
19
]
and a Majorization-Minimization (MM) strategy proposed in
[
144
]
with lower computational complexity.

3.4
Mirror-Langevin Algorithm

The mirror-Langevin algorithm is the sampling counterpart of mirror descent
[
15
]
. Introduced in
[
209
]
, under certain assumptions on
U
, the mirror-Langevin diffusion (MLD) takes the form: for
t\geqslant 0
,

\begin{cases}X_{t}=\nabla\varphi^{*}(Y_{t}),\\
\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=-\nabla U(X_{t})\,\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\left[\nabla^{2}\varphi(X_{t})\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\end{cases}

where
\varphi
is a Legendre function and
\varphi^{*}
is the Fenchel conjugate of
\varphi
.
Its EM discretization yields a
Hessian Riemannian LMC
(HRLMC) algorithm:

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\nabla\varphi^{*}\left(\nabla\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\left[\nabla^{2}\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}_{n}\right).

(3.6)

This is the main discretization scheme considered in
[
209
]
and an earlier draft of
[
110
]
, and further studied in
[
132
]
, which is a specific instance of the Riemannian LMC reminiscent of the mirror descent algorithm.
[
4
]
consider an alternative discretization scheme motivated by the mirrorless mirror descent
[
106
]
, called the
mirror-Langevin algorithm
(MLA):

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\begin{aligned} \bm{x}_{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}&=\nabla\varphi^{*}\left(\nabla\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}_{n})\right),\\
\bm{x}_{n+1}&=\nabla\varphi^{*}(Y_{\gamma_{n}}),\end{aligned}

(3.7)

where

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\sqrt{2}\left[\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(Y_{t})\right]^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},\\
Y_{0}=\nabla\varphi\left(\bm{x}_{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)=\nabla\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla U(\bm{x}_{n}).\end{cases}

(3.8)

MLA (
3.7
) is harder to implement than (
3.6
) in practice since a continuous diffusion (
3.8
) has to be solved.
See also
[
132
,
113
]
for related theoretical results of MLA. With slight ambiguity, we will also refer (
3.6
) as MLA in simulations as it is easier to solve. Note that PULA is a particular instance of MLA when
\varphi(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{\bm{M}^{-1}}^{2}
.

4
Proximal Langevin Monte Carlo Algorithms

We now move to the case where the potential
U
takes a composite form, i.e.,
U=f+g
, where
f
is Lipschitz smooth and
g
is possibly nonsmooth.
Since the LMC algorithms require the smoothness of the potential, one important idea proposed in
[
161
,
85
]
is to approximate the nonsmooth part
g
of the potential
U
using its Moreau envelope
g_{\lambda}
with a smoothing parameter
\lambda>0
, followed by sampling from the smooth surrogate density
\pi_{\lambda}\propto\mathrm{e}^{-U_{\lambda}}
, where
U_{\lambda}\coloneqq f+g_{\lambda}
(with slight abuse of notation of
U_{\lambda}
for not representing its Moreau envelope), which converges weakly to the target density
\pi
as
\lambda\to 0
.
This idea is reminiscent of its convex optimization counterpart: when minimizing composite functions in the form of
U=f+g
, a large class of proximal splitting algorithms
[
157
,
60
,
39
,
65
,
62
]
were proposed and have been used widely.
In the following, we introduce such proximal LMC algorithms and discuss their similarities to proximal optimization algorithms in more detail.
Note that we use constant smoothing parameters
\lambda>0
when describing the proximal LMC algorithms but nonincreasing smoothing parameter sequences
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be used in practice.

4.1
Moreau–Yosida Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm

Proposed in
[
161
,
85
]
, approximating the nonsmooth part
g
by its Moreau envelope (Moreau–Yosida regularization), the
Moreau–Yosida unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(MYULA) iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\right)\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla f(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n}.

(4.1)

A special case is when the step size is equal to the smoothing parameter, i.e.,
\lambda=\gamma
, so that we have

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\prox_{\gamma g}(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla f(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n}.

Furthermore, if
f=0
, then we recover the
proximal unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(P-ULA), which can be viewed as the sampling analogue of the
proximal point algorithm

[
176
]
in convex optimization.

4.2
Proximal Gradient Langevin Dynamics

Another different yet similar proximal LMC algorithm proposed in
[
86
]
(with stochastic gradient estimates) is the
proximal gradient Langevin dynamics
(PGLD), which iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla f(\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n}))+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n}.

(4.2)

Note that, if we write
\bm{y}_{n}=\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then we get an equivalent update:

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{y}_{n+1}=\prox_{\lambda g}\left(\bm{y}_{n}-\gamma\nabla f(\bm{y}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n}\right),

which can be viewed as the sampling analogue (in
\bm{y}_{n}
) of the
proximal gradient algorithm

[
60
]
(a.k.a. the
forward-backward algorithm
) in convex optimization when
\lambda=\gamma
. Furthermore, when
f=0
and
\lambda=\gamma
, PGLD is equivalent to P-ULA.

4.3
Proximal MALA and Moreau–Yosida Regularized MALA

With the MH correction step,
Moreau–Yosida regularized MALA
(MYMALA) iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\;\begin{aligned} \widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1}&=\left(1-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\right)\bm{x}_{n}+\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla f(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n},\\
\bm{x}_{n+1}&=b_{n+1}\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1}+(1-b_{n+1})\bm{x}_{n},\,b_{n+1}\coloneqq\mathbb{1}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(\alpha(\widetilde{\bm{x}}_{n+1},\bm{x}_{n})-u_{n}\right),\end{aligned}

(4.3)

where
\alpha
is defined in (
3.3
) and
u_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathsf{U}(0,1)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Again, letting
\lambda=\gamma
and
f=0
, we recover
proximal MALA
(P-MALA) proposed and studied in
[
161
,
163
]
.

The full MYMALA was mentioned briefly in
[
85
]
, but was not theoretically studied in detail with convergence guarantees in the literature until recently for the special case of
f=0
:
[
163
]
studied the case where
\lambda=\gamma
and
[
68
]
for the general case where
\lambda\neq\gamma
. Notice that, as mentioned in
[
68
]
, the proximal MALA can also be used for sampling smooth distributions. In this context, the proximity operators associated with log-concave densities can be found in
[
42
]
.

4.4
Preconditioned Proximal Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm

Incorporating the geometric idea with proximal LMC algorithms, an accelerated preconditioned version of P-ULA (PP-ULA) was proposed in
[
66
]
. This involves a preconditioning matrix
\bm{Q}^{-1}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
in the Moreau envelope and the proximity operator, or equivalently, a Bregman–Moreau envelope and a Bregman proximity operator with a Legendre function
\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\bm{Q}^{-1}}^{2}
: for
\lambda>0
, the Moreau envelope and proximity operator of
h\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with respect to the norm induced by
\bm{Q}^{-1}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
are respectively defined as

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\begin{aligned} h_{\lambda}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rVert_{\bm{Q}^{-1}}^{2}\right\},\\
\prox_{\lambda h}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq\argmin_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \left\{h(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rVert_{\bm{Q}^{-1}}^{2}\right\}.\end{aligned}

Note that, similar to the Moreau envelope
h_{\lambda}
, the preconditioned Moreau envelope
h_{\lambda}^{\bm{Q}}
is also differentiable and its gradient (cf. (
2.4
)) is given by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla h_{\lambda}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\bm{Q}^{-1}\left(\bm{x}-\prox_{\lambda h}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})\right).

Consequently, with a preconditioning matrix
\bm{M}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
for the Langevin diffusion, PP-ULA iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\bm{x}_{n}-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\bm{M}\bm{Q}^{-1}\left(\bm{x}_{n}-\prox_{\lambda U}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x}_{n})\right)+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{M}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}_{n},

(4.4)

where
\bm{Q}\in\mathbb{S}_{++}^{d}
. Again, for simplicity, letting
\lambda=\gamma
and
\bm{M}=\bm{Q}
yields

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\prox_{\lambda U}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{Q}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}_{n}.

Let us recall that
U=f+g
where
f
is Lipschitz differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. While the proximity operator of the sum of two functions is generally intractable
[
164
]
, but when
\lambda
is small,
\prox_{\lambda g}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x}-\lambda\bm{Q}\nabla f(\bm{x}))
is a good approximation of
\prox_{\lambda U}^{\bm{Q}}(\bm{x})

[
66
]
. Thus, PP-ULA indeed iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\prox_{\lambda g}^{\bm{Q}}\left(\bm{x}_{n}-\lambda\bm{Q}\nabla f(\bm{x}_{n})\right)+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{Q}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}_{n},

which can be viewed as the sampling analogue of the
variable metric forward-backward algorithm

[
59
]
.
In addition, if
\prox_{\lambda g}
is easy to compute, then
\prox_{\lambda g}^{\bm{Q}}
can be obtained using the dual forward-backward (DFB) algorithm
[
63
]
; see also
[
66
]
for details.

4.5
Forward-Backward Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm

A potential drawback of using the Moreau envelope in MYULA as a smooth approximation of only the possibly nonsmooth part
g
is that it often does not maintain the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimator of the original target distribution. Concurrent works
[
90
,
101
]
pointed out such a limitation and proposed the use of the forward-backward envelope instead to address this issue, which leads to the
forward-backward unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(FBULA) and the
smoothing unadjusted Langevin algorithm
(SULA) .
For
\lambda>0
, the forward-backward envelope of
U=f+g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
184
,
186
]
is defined by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad U^{f,g}_{\lambda}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ \left\{f(\bm{x})+\left\langle\nabla f(\bm{x}),\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle+g(\bm{y})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right\},

which has an explicit expression involving the gradient of
f
and the Moreau envelope of
g
:

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad U^{f,g}_{\lambda}(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert\nabla f(\bm{x})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+g_{\lambda}(\bm{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bm{x})).

For
\lambda\in(0,1/L)
, where
L>0
is the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla f
, the forward-backward envelope is continuously differentiable and its gradient is given by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla U^{f,g}_{\lambda}(\bm{x})=\lambda^{-1}\left(\bm{I}_{d}-\lambda\nabla^{2}f(\bm{x})\right)\left(\bm{x}-\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bm{x}))\right).

Here we assume the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f
is continuous. Then, FBULA iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\bm{x}_{n}-\gamma\nabla U^{f,g}_{\lambda}(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n}.

(4.5)

While motivated by a tool from convex optimization, we are not aware of a corresponding optimization algorithm in the literature except for the case of
\lambda=\gamma
and
f=0
in which FBULA is equivalent to P-ULA.

4.6
Bregman–Moreau Unadjusted Mirror-Langevin Algorithm

Extending the Moreau envelope using the Bregman–Moreau envelope along with the mirror-Langevin algorithm,
[
121
]
proposed the
Bregman–Moreau unadjusted mirror-Langevin algorithm
(BMUMLA).
Using two possibly different Legendre functions
\varphi
and
\psi
, BMUMLA iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\nabla\varphi^{*}\left(\nabla\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\nabla U_{\lambda}^{\psi}(\bm{x}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\left[\nabla^{2}\varphi(\bm{x}_{n})\right]^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bm{\xi}_{n}\right),

(4.6)

where
U_{\lambda}^{\psi}
represents either of

\overleftarrow{U}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace\lambda}^{\psi}\coloneqq f+\overleftarrow{\mathrm{env}}_{\lambda,g}^{\psi}\quad\text{and}\quad\overrightarrow{U}_{\negthinspace\negthinspace\lambda}^{\psi}\coloneqq f+\overrightarrow{\mathrm{env}}_{\lambda,g}^{\psi}.

(4.7)

In particular, when choosing
\lambda=\gamma
,
\varphi=\psi
and the right Bregman–Moreau envelope, we have

4.7
Primal-Dual Langevin Algorithms

Motivated by Chambolle–Pock’s
Primal-Dual Hybrid Gradient
(PDHG) algorithm
[
38
]
, a first-order primal-dual splitting algorithm, very recently
[
152
]
proposed a primal-dual Langevin algorithm, called the
unadjusted Langevin primal-dual algorithm
(ULPDA). In particular, the nonsmooth part of the target potential is now a composition of a nonsmooth function
g
and a linear operator
\bm{D}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, i.e.,
U=f+g\circ\bm{D}
, which widely appears in TV pseudonorms (
2.2
). Alternatively, the target potential
U
can be written as

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad U(\bm{x})=f(\bm{x})+\sup_{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}}\,\left\{\left\langle\bm{D}\bm{x},\bm{u}\right\rangle-g^{*}(\bm{u})\right\}.

It can be shown that
\bm{u}\in\partial g(\bm{D}\bm{x})
satisfies the supremum, where
\partial g
is the subdifferential of
g
defined later in (
5.2
).
Then, choosing
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\bm{u}_{0}=\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}
, ULPDA iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\begin{aligned} \bm{x}_{n+1}&=\prox_{\gamma f}(\bm{x}_{n}-\gamma\bm{D}^{*}\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n},\\
\bm{u}_{n+1}&=\prox_{\lambda g^{*}}(\bm{u}_{n}+\lambda\bm{D}(2\bm{x}_{n+1}-\bm{x}_{n})),\\
\widetilde{\bm{u}}_{n+1}&=\bm{u}_{n}+\tau(\bm{u}_{n+1}-\bm{u}_{n}),\end{aligned}

(4.8)

where the step sizes
(\gamma,\lambda,\tau)
satisfy
\gamma\lambda\leqslant|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{D}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{{\mathrm{S}}}^{-2}
and
\tau\in[0,1]
,
|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|\bm{D}|\kern-1.07639pt|\kern-1.07639pt|_{{\mathrm{S}}}\coloneqq\sup_{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\bm{x}\neq\bm{0}}\{\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}/\left\lVert\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}\}
is the spectral norm of
\bm{D}
, and
\bm{D}^{*}
is the adjoint of
\bm{D}
. Note that theoretical convergence properties of ULPDA remain open.

Another line of recent works regarding proximal LMC algorithms for target potentials with a linear operator in their nonsmooth part is
[
8
,
2
,
3
,
94
]
. It is worth mentioning that primal-dual Langevin algorithms reminiscent of other primal-dual splitting algorithms such as the Loris–Verhoeven
[

[

and Combettes–Pesquet
[

algorithms have yet been explored in the literature.

5
Stochastic Gradient Langevin Monte Carlo Algorithms

In large-scale applications involving very large datasets such as Bayesian deep learning, the exact gradient of the smooth part of the potential would usually be very computationally expensive. In a similar vein to stochastic gradient methods in optimization, the gradient

\nabla f
of the Lipschitz smooth part
f\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is usually replaced by its unbiased estimate
\widehat{\nabla}f
using stochastic approximation
[
169
]
. Suppose that there exists a measurable space
(\mathsf{Z},\mathcal{Z})
and a probability measure
\mathcal{D}
on
(\mathsf{Z},\mathcal{Z})
, where
\mathsf{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. A stochastic gradient
\widehat{\nabla}f\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathsf{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of
f
is a measurable function which satisfies

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\widehat{\nabla}f(\bm{x},\bm{z})\right]=\nabla f(\bm{x}).

ULA with stochastic gradients is known as
stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics
(SGLD)
[
201
]
, which is also used in the context of optimization for nonconvex optimization
[
165
]
. Developing extensions of SGLD for sampling multimodal distributions or nonconvex optimization is an active area of research, see e.g.,
cyclical SGLD

[
211
]
and
contour SGLD

[
75
,
77
,
76
]
.

For nonsmooth composite target potentials,
[
86
]
proposed the use of stochastic gradients in PGLD, leading to
stochastic proximal gradient Langevin dynamics
(SPGLD), which iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n})-\gamma\widehat{\nabla}f(\prox_{\lambda g}(\bm{x}_{n}),\bm{z}_{n})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n},

(5.1)

where
(\bm{z}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables distributed according to
\mathcal{D}
.

Other than SPGLD,
[
86
]
also proposed to use a subgradient of the nonsmooth part
g
instead of its proximity operator. In particular, if
g\in\Gamma_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, its
subdifferential

\partial g
is defined by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\partial g(\bm{x})\coloneqq\left\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:(\forall\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\;\;g(\bm{x})+\left\langle\bm{u},\bm{y}-\bm{x}\right\rangle\leqslant g(\bm{y})\right\},

(5.2)

which is nonempty. For
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, any element of
\partial g(\bm{x})
is referred to as a
subgradient
of
g
at
\bm{x}
. Then, the
stochastic subgradient Langevin dynamics
(SSGLD) iterates

(\forall n\in\mathbb{N})\quad\bm{x}_{n+1}=\bm{x}_{n}-\gamma\left(\widehat{\nabla}f(\bm{x}_{n},\bm{z}_{n})+\partial g(\bm{x}_{n})\right)+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,\bm{\xi}_{n}.

(5.3)

We also refer readers to
[
178
,
177
,
194
,
73
]
for further works on stochastic gradient proximal Langevin algorithms.

6.4
Bayesian Image Deconvolution

In addition to the above simulations, we also want to demonstrate the LMC algorithms with real data for high-dimensional Bayesian imaging inverse problems which have non-log-concave posterior densities. We consider an image deconvolution task with a non-log-concave TV prior induced by the nonconvex TV regularizer
[
182
,
181
,
120
]
. The (isotropic) TV pseudonorm involves a linear operator (the first-order differencing matrix), hence allowing us to invoke a primal-dual Langevin algorithm.

We now briefly describe the problem setting, following §4.1.2 of
[
85
]
. In image deconvolution, the goal is to recover a high-resolution image
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from a blurred and noisy observation
\bm{y}=\bm{H}\bm{x}+\bm{w}
, where
\bm{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a circulant blurring matrix and
\bm{w}\sim\mathsf{N}(\bm{0}_{d},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{d})
. The posterior distribution is given by

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\pi(\bm{x})=p(\bm{x}\,|\,\bm{y})\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{H}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\tau\mathrm{TV}(\bm{x})\right\},

where
\mathrm{TV}
denotes the total-variation pseudonorm or its nonconvex variant such as the MC-TV
[
182
,
181
,
80
]
and ME-TV
[
180
]
penalties,
\sigma^{2}>0
is the noise variance and
\tau>0
is a regularization parameter. Both
\sigma^{2}
and
\tau
are assumed to be fixed in this context. We note that the MC-TV and ME-TV priors are motivated by the MC-TV and ME-TV penalties, which might lead to better recovered signals than the original convex TV penalty. Now we let
g(\bm{x})\coloneqq\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{1-2}
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the isotropic TV pseudonorm, where
\bm{D}\coloneqq(\bm{D}_{h}^{\top},\bm{D}_{v}^{\top})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times d}
is the two-dimensional discrete gradient operator with
\bm{D}_{h},\bm{D}_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
the first-order horizontal and vertical difference matrices. We also denote
\widetilde{g}\coloneqq\left\|\cdot\right\|_{{1}}
. We consider the isotropic MC-TV (minimax-concave) and the ME-TV (Moreau envelope) priors
[
120
]
with their corresponding potentials

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\begin{aligned} \mathrm{TV}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{MC}}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq g(\bm{x})-\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}),\\
\mathrm{TV}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{ME}}(\bm{x})&\coloneqq g(\bm{x})-g_{\gamma}(\bm{x}),\end{aligned}

where
\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}
and
g_{\gamma}
denote the Moreau envelopes of
\widetilde{g}
and
g
with a smoothing parameter
\gamma>0
respectively.
Note that we have the (in general) nonconvex posterior potential
U=f+\tau g
with
f^{\mathrm{MC}}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{H}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\tau\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2})
and
f^{\mathrm{ME}}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{H}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-\tau g_{\gamma}(\bm{x})
. However, if
\gamma
is well chosen according to the values of
\sigma^{2}
and
\tau
, then
U
could be convex
[
120
, Theorems 1 and 2]
.

We consider the following two LMC algorithms, MYULA (
4.1
) and ULPDA (
4.8
). In MYULA, as we replace the nonsmooth part
\tau g
by its Moreau envelope
\tau g_{\tau\lambda}
with a smoothing parameter
\lambda>0
, the (smooth) surrogate posterior potential is thus

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\begin{aligned} U_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{MC}}(\bm{x})&=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{H}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\tau[g_{\tau\lambda}(\bm{x})-\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2})],\\
U_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{ME}}(\bm{x})&=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{H}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+\tau[g_{\tau\lambda}(\bm{x})-g_{\gamma}(\bm{x})],\end{aligned}

in which we have a difference-of-Moreau-envelopes smoothing
[
185
]
. The difference of Moreau envelopes are both differentiable. In particular, the gradient of
\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\cdot\right\rVert_{2})
is given by (see
[
120
, Proposition 4]
for details)

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2})=\bm{D}^{\top}\min\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma},\frac{1}{\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}}\right\}\bm{D}\bm{x}.

(6.2)

In ULPDA, we need to compute
\prox_{\lambda f}
for some
\lambda>0
. Letting
\varphi(\bm{x})\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\bm{y}-\bm{H}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
, then we have
f^{\mathrm{MC}}=\varphi-\tau\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\cdot\right\rVert_{2})
and
f^{\mathrm{ME}}(\bm{x})=\varphi-\tau g_{\gamma}
. Although the computation of the proximity operator of the sum of two functions is generally intractable
[
164
]
, we use a first-order Taylor expansion for the latter term to approximate the proximity operator of
f
and introduce a forward step as follows:

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\begin{aligned} \prox_{\lambda f^{\mathrm{MC}}}(\bm{x})&\approx\prox_{\lambda\varphi}\left(\bm{x}+\lambda\tau\nabla\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2})\right),\\
\prox_{\lambda f^{\mathrm{ME}}}(\bm{x})&\approx\prox_{\lambda\varphi}\left(\left(1+\frac{\lambda\tau}{\gamma}\right)\bm{x}-\frac{\lambda\tau}{\gamma}\prox_{\gamma g}(\bm{x})\right).\end{aligned}

Here
\nabla\widetilde{g}_{\gamma}(\left\lVert\bm{D}\bm{x}\right\rVert_{2})
is given in (
6.2
),
\prox_{\gamma g}=\soft_{\gamma}
is the proximity operator of the isotropic TV pseudonorm, and

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\prox_{\lambda\varphi}(\bm{x})=\left(\mathrm{Id}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma^{2}}\bm{H}^{*}\bm{H}\right)^{-1}\left(\bm{x}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sigma^{2}}\bm{H}^{*}\bm{y}\right),

where
\mathrm{Id}
is the identity operator and
\bm{H}^{*}
is the adjoint of
\bm{H}
.

7
Conclusion

In this chapter, we focus on understanding the convergence behavior of MCMC algorithms motivated by the overdamped Langevin diffusion for non-log-concave and non-log-smooth sampling through numerical simulations. Similar analysis can also be performed using higher-order algorithms such as the underdamped Langevin Monte Carlo (ULMC) algorithm and the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm, and their variants.

Other than the considered formulation in which smoothing and regularization parameters are usually pre-specified, recently the proximal MCMC methods are studied through a fully Bayesian framework with the use of epigraph priors
[
214
]
, avoiding the need of tuning regularization parameters. An empirical Bayesian approach is also studied in
[
194
,
73
]
. On the other hand, there is also recent interest in other MCMC algorithms based on splitting methods, e.g., ADMM-type splitting and splitting in higher-order algorithms
[
197
,
145
,
18
,
183
,
35
]
.
Note that the success of LMC algorithms hinges heavily on efficient sampling from a high-dimensional Gaussian distribution; see
[
196
]
for a recent review on the topic and its connection with the proximal point algorithm.
Recent works also concern the development of LMC algorithms with the use of Plug & Play (PnP) priors in the Bayesian statistical framework
[
122
,
123
]
, which are implicit priors based on highly nonconvex neural networks with more flexibility than explicit priors and have gained popularity in Bayesian imaging problems.

While LMC algorithms are designed mainly for continuous and unconstrained domains, recent works also attempt to adapt them for sampling from discrete distributions
[
207
,
105
,
168
,
212
,
213
,
160
]
.
We also note the intriguing connection of LMC algorithms to nonconvex optimization algorithms, in a sense that SGLD and its variants are used as global nonconvex optimization algorithms
[
165
,
91
,
205
]
.

We give the expressions of the gradient and the Hessian of the potential
U
for the Gaussian mixture density
p
:

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla U(\bm{x})=-\dfrac{\nabla p(\bm{x})}{p(\bm{x})},\quad\nabla^{2}U(\bm{x})=\dfrac{\nabla p(\bm{x})\nabla p(\bm{x})^{\top}}{p(\bm{x})^{2}}-\dfrac{\nabla^{2}p(\bm{x})}{p(\bm{x})},

where, for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the gradient and the Hessian of the density
p
of the Gaussian mixture are

\displaystyle\nabla p(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\nabla p_{k}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}}{\sqrt{\det(2\uppi\bm{\Sigma}_{k})}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k})\right\rangle\right\}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\bm{\mu}_{k}-\bm{x}),

and

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}p(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\nabla^{2}p_{k}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}}{\sqrt{\det(2\uppi\bm{\Sigma}_{k})}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k},\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k})\right\rangle\right\}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k})(\bm{x}-\bm{\mu}_{k})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{-1}\right).

On the other hand, the gradient of the surrogate potential
U^{\lambda}
for the Laplacian mixture surrogate density
p^{\lambda}
take the expressions:

(\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d})\quad\nabla U^{\lambda}(\bm{x})=-\nabla p^{\lambda}(\bm{x})/p^{\lambda}(\bm{x}),

where, for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\nabla p^{\lambda}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\omega_{k}\nabla p_{k}^{\lambda}(\bm{x})=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}\alpha_{k}^{d}}{2^{d}}\exp\left\{-g_{k}^{\lambda}(\bm{x})\right\}\nabla g_{k}^{\lambda}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\omega_{k}\alpha_{k}^{d}}{2^{d}\lambda}\exp\left\{-g_{k}^{\lambda}(\bm{x})\right\}\left(\prox_{\lambda g_{k}}(\bm{x})-\bm{x}\right).
