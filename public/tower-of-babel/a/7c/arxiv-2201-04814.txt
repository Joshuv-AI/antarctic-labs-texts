Introduction

In this paper, we aim to study the compact support property of nonnegative solution to the following stochastic partial differential equations

\partial_{t}u(\omega,t,x)=\mathcal{L}u(\omega,t,x)\,+h(\omega,t,x,u(t,x))\dot{F}(t,x),\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathds{R}^{d};\quad u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the random differential operator defined as

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{L}u(\omega,t,x)=a^{ij}(\omega,t,x)u_{x^{i}x^{j}}+b^{i}(\omega,t,x)u_{x^{i}}+c(\omega,t,x)u\end{gathered}

and
h(\omega,t,x,u)
is a function such that for some nonrandom constants
K\geq 1
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

K^{-1}u^{\lambda}\leq h(\omega,t,x,u)\leq K(1+u)\quad\quad\text{for all \quad\quad$\omega\in\Omega,\,\,t>0,\,\,x\in\mathds{R}^{d},\,\,u\geq 0$}.

(1.2)

We assume Einstein’s summation convention on
i
and
j
, and
i
and
j
go from 1 to
d
.
The detailed conditions on
a,b,c
, and
h
are specified below in Assumptions
2.3
and
2.4
.
The nonrandom initial data
u_{0}
is a nonnegative Hölder continuous function with compact support. The noise
\dot{F}
is a centered generalized Gaussian random field whose covariance is given by

\mathds{E}[F(t,x)F(s,y)]=\delta_{0}(t-s)f(x-y),

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta distribution and
f
is a
correlation function (or measure)
. In other words,
f
is a nonnegative and nonnegative definite function/measure. We denote that the spectral measure
\mu
of
f
is a nonnegative tempered measure defined by

\mu(\xi):=\mathcal{F}(f)(\xi):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}f(dx)

(1.3)

which is the Fourier transform of
f
.

The
compact support property (CSP)
for (
1.1
) means that if the initial function
u_{0}
has a compact support, then so does the solution
u(t,\cdot)
for all
t>0
with probability 1, and this property has been studied extensively for one-dimensional stochastic heat equations driven by space-time white noise. More precisely, consider the following stochastic heat equations:

\partial_{t}u=u_{xx}+u^{\lambda}\,\dot{W},\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathds{R};\quad u(0,\cdot)=u_{0},

(1.4)

where
\dot{W}
is space-time white noise (i.e.
f=\delta_{0}
) and
\lambda>0
is a fixed constant.

When
\lambda=1/2
in (
1.4
), the solution to (
1.4
) can be regarded as a density of super-Brownian motion, and Iscoe (
[
11
]
) showed that CSP holds for (
1.4
) by using some singular elliptic boundary value problem related to properties of super-Brownian motion. Shiga (
[
26
]
) extended Iscoe’s technique to show CSP for (
1.4
) when
\lambda\in(0\,,1/2)
. For
\lambda\in(1/2\,,1)
, Mueller and Perkins (
[
21
]
) showed among other things that CSP holds for (
1.4
) by constructing and then applying a
historical process
that represents the ancestry of particles. In fact, the case where
\lambda\in(1/2,1)
is more delicate, as explained in
[
21
]
, since this case can be regarded as a super-Brownian motion with birth and death rates slowed down (that is, the coefficient of the noise
u^{\lambda}
is smaller than
u^{1/2}
when
u
is close to 0), but still the effect of noise is strong enough for (
1.4
) to have CSP. On the other hand, when
\lambda\geq 1
, Mueller (
[
20
]
) showed that CSP does not hold. In this case, when
u
is close to 0,
u^{\lambda}
for
\lambda\geq 1
becomes quite small so that the positivity of the heat semigroup wins against the effect of noise. Hence, for (
1.4
), the critical exponent
\lambda
that does not guarantee CSP is
1
.

Regarding the one-dimensional SPDE (
1.1
) with the random second order differential operator
\mathcal{L}
and with space-time white noise, using completely different methods that are based on the weak solution to (
1.1
), Krylov (
[
15
]
) showed that CSP holds when
h(u)=u^{\lambda}
for any
\lambda\in(0\,,1)
under the hypothesis of the existence of a suitable solution. Following the same argument by Krylov (
[
15
]
), Rippl (
[
23
]
) showed that CSP holds for (
1.4
) when
\dot{W}
is replaced by
\dot{F}
whose spatial covariance is of Riesz type, i.e.,
f(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
x\in\mathds{R}
. The proof therein strongly depends on the scaling property of the specific correlation function
|x|^{-\alpha}
. Moreover, both authors in
[
15
]
and
[
23
]
only considered the one-dimensional SPDEs, and the generalization of their techniques to higher dimensions is highly nontrivial, which is one of our main contributions in this paper.

When
d\geq 2
, in order to have a function-valued solution to (
1.1
), noise should be colored in space. In particular, when
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
and
h(u)
is globally Lipschitz, Dalang (
[
6
]
) showed that if the spectral measure
\mu
of
f
satisfies

\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\frac{\mu(d\xi)}{1+|\xi|^{2}}<\infty,

(1.5)

there exists a unique random field solution. However, this condition itself does not guarantee that the solution is continuous in
t
and
x
(see
[
3
,
9
]
), and one sufficient condition that guarantees the Hölder continuity of the solution is the following:

\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\frac{\mu(d\xi)}{(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1-\eta}}<+\infty\quad\text{for some $\eta\in(0,1]$,}

(1.6)

which is called the
reinforced Dalang’s condition
(see
[
25
,
8
,
2
]
). Under the condition (
1.6
), when
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
and
h(u)\approx u
in (
1.1
), following Mueller’s argument (
[
20
]
), Chen and Huang (
[
2
]
) showed that CSP does not hold. In addition, when
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
and
h(u)=u^{\lambda}
for
\lambda>1
in (
1.1
), following Mueller’s argument (
[
20
]
), one can show that CSP does not hold under the condition (
1.6
). Thus, a natural question arises whether CSP holds for (
1.1
) when
h
satisfies (
1.2
) for
\lambda\in(0\,,1)
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no literature on CSP for (
1.1
) even when
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
on
\mathds{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
, and we provide an answer in the affirmative in this paper.

Our main result of this paper is that when
h
satisfies (
1.2
) and the random second order differential operator
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.1
) satisfies some natural condition (see Assumption
2.3
), CSP holds for (
1.1
) only under the condition (
1.6
), which includes
f(x)=\delta_{0}(x)
,
f(x)=|x|^{-\alpha}
for
\alpha\in(0,2\wedge d)
,
f\in C_{c}(\mathds{R}^{d})
, and
f(x)=1
for
x\in\mathds{R}^{d}
. Note that
f\equiv 1
means the noise is just white noise in time only, i.e.,
\dot{F}(t,x)=\dot{B}_{t}
where
B_{t}
is the standard one-dimensional Brownian motion. Therefore, our result highlights that CSP is essentially due to the sub-linearity
u^{\lambda}
with
\lambda\in(0\,,1)
, not to the choice of noise. In addition, it also suggests that, as for one-dimensional stochastic heat equations with space-time white noise, the critical exponent
\lambda
that does not guarantee CSP for (
1.1
) is 1.

Let us now mention a few words about the proof. Since (
1.1
) includes the random second order differential operator
\mathcal{L}
, we use the approach given by Krylov
[
15
]
. Krylov
[
15
]
assumed the existence of a nonnegative solution. From this assumption, he obtained Lemma 2.1 in
[
15
]
, which is the crucial lemma that basically produces CSP. In this paper, on the other hand, we first show by using a compactness argument and the
L_{p}
theory (
[
16
,
14
]
) that a solution exists and is also nonnegative (see Theorem
2.9
), and then obtain Lemma
3.2
that corresponds to Lemma 2.1 in
[
15
]
. However, since our spatial space can be
\mathds{R}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
, which makes the boundary of a ball in
\mathds{R}^{d}
much more complex than the boundary of a ball in
\mathds{R}
(i.e., just single points), we need another lemma (Lemma
3.1
) for the proof of CSP in order to control the boundary behavior of the solution. In addition, because our noise is spatially correlated, some new ideas should be developed to obtain Lemma
3.2
. The first idea is to use some comparison argument that can compare the given nonnegative definite measure
f
with another nonnegative definite function that can be written as a convolution of some other functions (see Lemma
4.3
). This comparison allows us to bound the integral of a function by the integral of a function convoluted with the nonnegative definite measure
f
(see (
4.69
)). Another one is Lemma
4.2
, which is used to adjust the exponent of integrals in Lemma
3.2
. In order to use Lemma
4.2
, the solution
u
should be Hölder continuous in
t
and
x
, and we show Hölder continuity of the solution by using the
L_{p}
-theory (see
[
16
,
14
]
).

\displaystyle\left(\int_{R+ar<|x|<R+br}g(x)dx\right)^{\frac{\gamma\lambda+d}{\gamma+d}}\leq NR^{\frac{d(d-1)}{\gamma+d}}(r(b-a))^{\frac{-d(\gamma+d-1)}{\gamma+d}}\int_{R+ar<|x|<R+br}(g(x))^{\lambda}dx,

(4.40)

where

(ii)

Let

where

Proof.

The idea of proof follows from

To prove Lemma

(

(Step 1)
In this step, we consider the case

(4.42)

First we assume

. Notice that

(4.43)

Now we consider the case where

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\int_{R+ar<|x|<R+br}(g(x))^{\lambda}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\int_{R+ar<|x|<R+br}(g(x))^{\lambda}\mathbb{1}_{\{|x-x_{0}|<(4H)^{-1/\gamma}\}}\,dx

\displaystyle\geq 4^{-\lambda}\int_{R+ar<|x|<R+br}\mathbb{1}_{\{|x-x_{0}|<(4H)^{-1/\gamma}\}}\,dx

(4.45)

(Step 2)

where

Lemma 4.3

For the given correlation measure

Let us first construct
\varphi
by approximating

is a continuous, nonnegative and nonnegative definite function on

For

is a nonnegative and nonnegative definite

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}g(x)g(y)f_{\varepsilon}(x-y)dxdy

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}g^{(\delta)}(x)g^{(\delta)}(y)f_{\varepsilon}(x-y)dxdy

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left(g^{(\delta)}\ast\tilde{g}^{(\delta)}\right)(x)f_{\varepsilon}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left|\hat{g}^{(\delta)}(\xi)\right|^{2}\hat{f}_{\varepsilon}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left|\hat{g}^{(\delta)}(\xi)\right|^{2}\left|\hat{\psi}_{\varepsilon}(\xi)\right|^{2}\hat{f}(d\xi).

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left|\hat{g}^{(\delta)}(\xi)\right|^{2}\hat{f}(d\xi)

\displaystyle=\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left(g^{(\delta)}\ast\tilde{g}^{(\delta)}\right)(x)f(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}(g\ast\tilde{g})(x)f(dx),

where the last equality is justified by the bounded convergence theorem. This completes the proof.

\tau\leq T
be a bounded stopping time and
u\in\mathds{H}_{p}^{\gamma}(\tau)
.

(i)

We write
u\in\mathcal{H}^{\gamma}_{p}(\tau)
if
u_{0}\in U_{p}^{\gamma}
and there exists

in the sense of distributions, i.e., for any

, the equality

(u(t,\cdot),\phi)=(u_{0},\phi)+\int_{0}^{t}(f(s,\cdot),\phi)ds+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{t}(g^{k}(s,\cdot),\phi)dw_{s}^{k}

(A.3)

holds for all

almost surely.
In this case, we write

\mathds{D}u:=f,\quad\mathds{S}u:=g.

(ii)

The norm of
\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\gamma}(\tau)
is defined by

\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\gamma}(\tau)}:=\|u\|_{\mathds{H}_{p}^{\gamma}(\tau)}+\|\mathds{D}u\|_{\mathds{H}_{p}^{\gamma-2}(\tau)}+\|\mathds{S}u\|_{\mathds{H}_{p}^{\gamma-1}(\tau,\ell_{2})}+\|u(0,\cdot)\|_{U_{p}^{\gamma}}.

Next, we introduce the Hölder embedding theorem for
\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\gamma}(\tau)

Theorem A.6
.

Note that if

Let us take a symmetric function

. 2 Let

By Assumption

(A.5)

|h_{n}(t,x,u)|\leq N(1+u),

Lemma A.7

Proof.

We first note that the stochastic integral part in (

We now show non-negativity of

. Define

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\bar{u}_{n}(\omega,t,x)\,+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bar{h}_{n}(\omega,t,x,\bar{u}_{n}(t,x))(f*e_{k})(x)dw_{t}^{k}\quad(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathds{R}^{d}

Since

Now, for the non-negativity of

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

We first show that there is a nonnegative solution
u
to (
1.1
) and then show that any solution to (
1.1
) is Hölder continuous almost surely.

(Step 1) (Existence)

Since we use the standard compactness argument for the existence of a stochastically weak solution (see e.g.
[
22
, Theorem 1.2]
and
[
26
, Theorem 2.6]
), we only outline the proof.

From the bounds (
A.8
) and (
A.9
), the Kolmogorov type tightness criterion (see
[
26
, Lemma 6.3]
) implies that
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathds{N}}
is tight in
C(\mathds{R}_{+},C_{tem})
. Then, by Prokhorov’s theorem combined with Skorokhod’s representation theorem, we have an appropriate probability space and the sequence of solutions
\tilde{u}_{n}
on it, which is identical in law to
\{u_{n}\}
and converges to some nonnegative
u
almost surely in
C(\mathds{R}_{+},C_{tem})
. Since
h_{n}
converges to
h
uniformly on compact sets and
a^{ij},b^{i},c
are bounded functions, it can be shown that the limit
u\in C(\mathds{R}_{+},C_{tem})
is indeed a nonnegative solution of (
2.7
).

(Step 2) (Hölder regularity)
We assume
u
is a solution to (
1.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.7
. Let
p>\frac{d+2}{\eta}
. For
n\in\mathds{N}
and
a>0
, set

\tau_{n}:=\tau\wedge n\wedge\inf\left\{t\geq 0:\sup_{x\in\mathds{R}^{d}}u(t,x)e^{-a|x|}\geq n\right\}.

Fix
t\leq\tau_{n}
and take
\Psi(x)=\Psi_{2a}(x)=\frac{1}{\cosh(2a|x|)}
.
Then, since we assume (
2.5
), as in (
A.21
), we have

\displaystyle\|h(u)\Psi\bm{\nu}\|^{p}_{\mathds{H}_{p}^{\eta-1}(\ell_{2},\tau_{n})}\leq N\|h(u)\Psi\|_{\mathds{L}_{p}(\tau_{n})}^{p}\leq N\int_{\mathds{R}^{d}}\left(1+e^{pa|x|}\right)|\Psi(x)|^{p}dx<\infty,

In addition, if we define

g:=u\left(a^{ij}\Psi_{x^{i}x^{j}}+2a^{ij}_{x^{i}}\Psi_{x^{j}}-b^{i}\Psi_{x^{i}}\right)-\left(2a^{ij}\Psi_{x^{i}}(x)u\right)_{x^{j}},

then, similar to (
A.20
), we have
\|g\|_{\mathds{H}_{p}^{\eta-2}(\tau_{n})}^{p}<\infty.

Since
\Psi u_{0}\in L_{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F}_{0},H_{p}^{\eta-2/p})
,
[
16
, Theorem 5.1]
yields that there exists a unique
v\in\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\eta}(\tau_{n})
such that

\partial_{t}v=a^{ij}v_{x^{i}x^{j}}+b^{i}v_{x^{i}}+cv+g+h(u)\Psi\dot{F},\quad t\leq\tau;\quad v(0,\cdot)=\Psi u_{0},

in the sense of Definition
2.7
(see also
[
16
, Remark 5.3]
). Note that
h(u)\Psi
is used in the stochastic integral part, not
h(v)\Psi
.
Observe that for fixed
\omega\in\Omega
,
\bar{u}:=v-\Psi u
satisfies

\partial_{t}\bar{u}=a^{ij}\bar{u}_{x^{i}x^{j}}+b^{i}\bar{u}_{x^{i}}+c\bar{u},\quad 0<t<\tau_{n};\quad\bar{u}(0,\cdot)=0.

Since
\Psi u,v\in L_{p}((0,\tau_{n})\times\mathds{R}^{d})
, we have
\bar{u}\in L_{p}((0,\tau_{n})\times\mathds{R}^{d})
. Thus, by the deterministic version of
L_{p}
theory (e.g.
[
19
]
), we have
\bar{u}(t,\cdot)=0
in
L_{p}(\mathds{R}^{d})
for all
t\leq\tau_{n}
almost every
\omega
. Thus,
\Psi u=v\in\mathcal{H}_{p}^{\eta}(\tau_{n})
. Since
p>\frac{d+2}{\eta}
, by Theorem
A.6
(
ii
), for any
\gamma<\eta/2
, we have

\Psi u\in C_{t,x}^{\gamma,2\gamma}([0,\tau_{n}]\times\mathds{R}^{d}),

almost surely. The theorem is proved.
