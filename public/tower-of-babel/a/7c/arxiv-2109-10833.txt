Bounds on approximating Max k k XOR with quantum and classical local algorithms
Kunal Marwaha, Stuart Hadfield

Abstract
We consider the power of local algorithms for approximately solving Max k k XOR, a generalization of two constraint satisfaction problems previously studied with classical and quantum algorithms (MaxCut and Max E3LIN2). In Max k k XOR each constraint is the XOR of exactly k k variables and a parity bit. On instances with either random signs (parities) or no overlapping clauses and D + 1 D+1 clauses per variable, we calculate the expected satisfying fraction of the depth-1 QAOA from [ FGG14 ] and compare with a generalization of the local threshold algorithm from [ HRSS17 ] . Notably, the quantum algorithm outperforms the threshold algorithm for k > 4 k>4 . On the other hand, we highlight potential difficulties for the QAOA to achieve computational quantum advantage on this problem. We first compute a tight upper bound on the maximum satisfying fraction of nearly all large random regular Max k k XOR instances by numerically calculating the ground state energy density P ⁡ ( k ) P(k) of a mean-field k k -spin glass [ Sen17 ] . The upper bound grows with k k much faster than the performance of both one-local algorithms. We also identify a new obstruction result for low-depth quantum circuits (including the QAOA) when k = 3 k=3 , generalizing a result of [ BKKT19 ] when k = 2 k=2 . We conjecture that a similar obstruction exists for all k k .

The standard way to study Max

Since the QAOA’s output is not deterministic, a common performance measure is the expected value,
\langle C\rangle
. (One can repeat the algorithm in order to achieve an outcome of at least
\langle C\rangle
with high probability.)
So we consider the expected value of the depth-1 QAOA on each

d_{n_{1}...n_{k}}Z_{n_{1}}...Z_{n_{k}}
term, as functions of the algorithm parameters. Previous analyses obtained exact formulas for
\langle C\rangle
for the depth-1 QAOA for MaxCut
[

]

bounds to the parameter-optimized

for Max 3XOR
[

]

We simplify the performance of the QAOA at depth-1 on triangle-free Max

In order to achieve this, we use the proof technique of

The main idea is that the expected value of each QAOA term can be written

\displaystyle\langle d_{n_{1}...n_{k}}Z_{n_{1}}...Z_{n_{k}}\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=d_{n_{1}...n_{k}}\langle\psi_{0}|\left(U_{C}^{\dagger}\left(U_{B}^{\dagger}Z_{n_{1}}...Z_{n_{k}}U_{B}\right)U_{C}\right)|\psi_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=d_{n_{1}...n_{k}}\Tr[\rho_{0}\left(U_{C}^{\dagger}\left(U_{B}^{\dagger}Z_{n_{1}}...Z_{n_{k}}U_{B}\right)U_{C}\right)],

which, as suggested by the parentheses, can be computed using a sequence of operator conjugations, followed by taking the initial state expectation value.
Here
U_{C}=e^{-i\gamma C}
,
U_{B}=e^{-i\beta\sum_{j=1}^{n}X_{j}}

For this standard initial state, operator terms containing any
Y

Z

This trick is particularly convenient for directly incorporating the effects of problem and algorithm locality.

A local threshold algorithm on Max
k
XOR

Recall the threshold algorithm of
[
HRSS17
]
for MaxCut.
This is also a one-local algorithm; given an initial assignment, the output assignment for each vertex depends only on the initial values assigned to its neighbors.

[
Has19
]
showed that
a generalization of this algorithm outperformed the depth-1 QAOA on MaxCut for all regular triangle-free graphs.

The threshold algorithm naturally generalizes to
Max
k
XOR as follows. Each node makes an update decision based on its
and its neighbors’ current assignments.
7
7

7

Each node also needs the values
d_{n_{1}...n_{k}}=\pm 1/2
associated with the clauses it is involved in. This is distinct from MaxCut, where the
d_{ab}
are all the same.
Given a parameter
\mu
:

1.

Assign every node a value
\pm 1
with equal probability, independently.

2.

Simultaneously, every node checks the number of clauses it is involved in that are satisfied; if it is at most
\mu
then the node’s value is switched.
8
8

8

[
HRSS17
]
considers a threshold
\tau=D+1-\mu
with respect to our notation.

We extend the analysis of
[
HRSS17
]
to find an expression for the performance of this algorithm, given any
(D+1)
-regular, triangle-free instance. We numerically optimize this expression at every
k<200
, for each
D<300
and in the large-degree limit.

Consider a fixed clause. As it’s triangle-free, each of the neighbor clauses are i.i.d. satisfied with probability
1/2
after Step 1. This is because in each adjacent clause there are
k-1
i.i.d remaining variables that could be
\pm 1
.
9
9

9

The adjacent clauses are also i.i.d. when the clauses are arbitrary but the clause signs are random. As a result, this analysis is exactly the same if the instance is
not
triangle-free but the clause signs are randomly chosen.
Let’s say the fixed clause is unsatisfied. Then the chance one of its nodes (a
member node
) will flip is the chance that at most
\mu
of the clauses containing that node are satisfied. If instead the fixed clause is satisfied, then a member node will flip if at most
\mu-1
neighbor clauses are satisfied (because one is already satisfied). This difference is the secret to the algorithm: the flip operation has a slight bias towards satisfying more clauses. Hence we have the quantities:

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\Pr[flip|\text{UNSAT}]=\frac{1}{2^{D}}\sum_{i=0}^{\mu}{D\choose i}

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=1-g

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\Pr[flip|\text{SAT}]=\frac{1}{2^{D}}\sum_{i=0}^{\mu-1}{D\choose i}

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle=1-r

\displaystyle g-r=\Delta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{D}}{D\choose\mu}

If a clause is initially unsatisfied, it needs an odd number of variables to flip to become satisfied (these correspond to odd terms of a binomial with probability
g
). If it is initially satisfied, it needs an even number of flips out of
k
to remain so (these correspond to even terms of binomial with probability
r=g-\Delta
). Altogether, the chance
F=1/2+C_{k,D}/\sqrt{D}
of satisfying the clause is:

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{j=1,3,5,...}^{k}{k\choose j}g^{j}h^{k-j}+\sum_{j=0,2,4,...}^{k}{k\choose j}r^{j}s^{k-j}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(1-(h-g)^{k})+\frac{1}{4}(1+(s-r)^{k})=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(1-2g+2\Delta)^{k}-(1-2g)^{k}}{4}

Then,
\mu
can be optimized at each
k
and
D
to find the best threshold value. When
k=2
, the expression simplifies to
1/2+\Delta(1-2g+\Delta)
. This matches the expression given in Lemma 3 of
[
HRSS17
]
for
k=2
.
When
k
is odd,
F
is unchanged when switching

and
r

D/2
.

At large degree, the asymptotic value of the constant
C_{k}=\lim_{D\to\infty}C_{k,D}
and optimal
\mu=D/2+\alpha\sqrt{D}
can also be numerically computed:

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi D}}e^{-2\alpha^{2}}

\displaystyle g\approx\Phi(2\alpha)+\frac{\Delta}{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\erf(\alpha\sqrt{2})+1+\Delta)

\displaystyle F-\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(\erf(-\alpha\sqrt{2})+\Delta)^{k}-(\erf(-\alpha\sqrt{2})-\Delta)^{k}}{4}\approx\frac{k\Delta}{2}\erf^{k-1}(-\alpha\sqrt{2}).

The performance is maximized when
(k-1)\Delta\sqrt{D}=2\alpha\erf(\alpha\sqrt{2})
. A numerical computation can be done for each
k

As noted in

5.1
P(k)
is upper-bounded by its limiting value

We use the same definitions to solve the Parisi functional as in many papers
[
AC13
,
Pan14
,
AC14
,
AC16
,
Pan17
]
. Consider a step function on
[0,1)
with two pieces:
0
on
[0,\epsilon)
and
m_{1}
on
[\epsilon,1)
where
\epsilon\ll 1/k\leq 1
. For a pure
k
-spin glass,
\xi(s)=s^{k}
. From Equation 4 of
[
AC16
]
, the Parisi functional is then:

P(k)=\Psi_{0}(0)-B=\Psi_{0}(0)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\xi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(t)tdt

The penalty term
B
can be simplified:

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}\int_{\epsilon}^{1}k(k-1)t^{k-1}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)m_{1}}{2}(1-\epsilon^{k})\approx\frac{m_{1}(k-1)}{2}

Let’s define
a_{i}=\sqrt{\xi^{{}^{\prime}}(q_{i+1})-\xi^{{}^{\prime}}(q_{i})}
, where
q_{0}=0,q_{1}=\epsilon,q_{2}=1
. So
a_{1}=\sqrt{k}\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{k-1}}\approx\sqrt{k}
, and
a_{0}=\sqrt{k}\sqrt{\epsilon^{k-1}}\approx 0
. We use these variables to recursively calculate
\Psi_{i}
, as in Equation 12 of
[
AC13
]
.

Define
\Psi_{2}(x)=|x|
as the boundary condition as in
[
AC16
]
, and
\Psi_{0}(x)=\E[\Psi_{1}(x+a_{0}z)]
, averaging across a standard normal variable
z
.
But since
a_{0}\approx 0
we have
\Psi_{0}(x)\approx\Psi_{1}(x)
.

We define
\Psi_{1}(x)
as the following:

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\log\Bigg(
\E\Big[ e^{m_1 \Psi_2(x + a_1 z)} \Big]
\Bigg)\Big[e^{m_{1}\Psi_{2}(x+a_{1}z)}\Big]\Bigg.

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{m_{1}}\log\Bigg(
\E\Big[ e^{m_1 |x + \sqrt{k} z|} \Big]
\Bigg)\Big[e^{m_{1}|x+\sqrt{k}z|}\Big]\Bigg.

Then we can solve for
\Psi_{0}(0)
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{0}(0)\approx\Psi_{1}(0)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{m_{1}}\log\Bigg(
\E\Big[ e^{m_1 \sqrt{k} |z|} \Big]
\Bigg)\Big[e^{m_{1}\sqrt{k}|z|}\Big]\Bigg.=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\log\Bigg(
e^{m_1^2 k / 2} \Big( \erf(m_1 \sqrt{k/2}) + 1 \Big)
\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{1}k}{2}+\frac{1}{m_{1}}\log\Big(
\erf(m_1 \sqrt{k/2}) + 1
\Big)\leq\frac{m_{1}k}{2}+\frac{\log{2}}{m_{1}}

This upper bounds the Parisi functional:

\displaystyle P(k)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{0}(0)-B\lesssim\frac{m_{1}}{2}+\frac{\log{2}}{m_{1}}

The upper bound is minimized at
m_{1}=\sqrt{2\log{2}}
with
P=\sqrt{2\log{2}}
. So this means that
P(k)
is upper bounded at approximately
\sqrt{2\log{2}}
. We also observe this in our numerical calculations; see Figure
5

k
-spin glass becomes the random energy model where

6
An NLTS-based obstruction for QAOA on Max
3
XOR

Here we construct Hamiltonians for Max
3
XOR that satisfy the No Low-energy Trivial States (NLTS) property for quantum circuits with a particular symmetry property. Our motivation is that QAOA circuits at odd
k
do not have the global bit-flip
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry considered in
[
BKKT19
]
; rather, we explain how QAOA circuits in this case satisfy a property we call partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. We use this to show a constant-fraction obstruction for low-depth QAOA on Max 3XOR, analogous to the previous results obtained for MaxCut.

Given a unitary operator
A
, a family of quantum circuits
\{U_{\alpha}\}
is said to have
A
as a symmetry if each circuit satisfies
A^{\dagger}U_{\alpha}A=U_{\alpha}
(see e.g.
[
SHHS21
]
).
For Max
k
XOR with even
k
, QAOA circuits have a so-called
11
11

11

As
A^{2}=I
,
{I,A} gives a representation of the two-element group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry for
A=X_{1}X_{2}\dots X_{n}
, which corresponds to the classical operation of flipping all bits. Since the standard initial state satisfies
A|+\rangle^{\otimes n}=|+\rangle^{\otimes n}
, the probability of measuring any particular bit string after running one of these QAOA circuits is the same as that of its complement.

The NLTS property holds for a family of Hamiltonians when the
quantum circuits and product states have
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
[
BKKT19
]
. These Hamiltonians represent MaxCut instances on expander graphs. We generalize this result to construct a family of Max 3XOR instances, which have a slightly more general symmetry that also applies to the corresponding QAOA circuits. Let’s call this
partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
. This refers to when the unitary has
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
bit-flip symmetry
with respect to some large, fixed subset
V_{+}\subsetneq V
, where both
V/V_{+}
and
V_{+}
have cardinality
\Theta(|V|)
, and
|V/V_{+}|\ll|V_{+}|
. To be explicit, let’s suppose
|V/V_{+}|<|V_{+}|/r
for some constant
r>4
. Importantly, the QAOA circuit for Max 3XOR has the same
partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
on these instances, and so is obstructed from optimally solving these instances at sub-logarithmic depth.

Theorem 3

(Formal version of Theorem
2
)
.

There exists an
\epsilon>0
and a family of Hamiltonians
\{H_{n}\}
with partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, each with ground state energy
0
, such that when
n>5184\cdot 8^{d}
, every depth-
d
unitary
U
, composed of 2-qubit gates and with the same partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry, gives
\langle+^{\otimes n}|U^{\dagger}H_{n}U|+^{\otimes n}\rangle>\epsilon n
.

At a high level, we choose a large random MaxCut instance (with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry). We then add a small number of nodes, and convert every edge into hyperedges including one of the added nodes. The Hamiltonian then has a large “core” of nodes that obey
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry. The QAOA circuits that encode these Hamiltonians also have this generalized symmetry:

Corollary 2

(Formal version of Corollary
1
)
.

There exists a family of Hamiltonians
\{H_{n}\}
representing Max
3
XOR instances that are fully satisfiable, but the QAOA can only achieve a satisfying fraction at most

\displaystyle 0.99+\frac{\sqrt{D-1}}{50D}+o\Big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{D}}\Big)

when the depth
p<\log_{2}(n/5184)(648D)^{-1}
.

This is a constant-fraction obstruction to QAOA on Max 3XOR. It may be possible to improve the constant; we did not optimize it. See the following subsection (
6.1
) for the proofs. They proceed similarly to that of Theorem 1 and Corollary 1 in
[
BKKT19
]
using our more general notion of symmetry.

It may be possible to use our Hamiltonian construction to create
k
-uniform hyperedges of
k>3
, while keeping the
partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
. Because of this, we expect a constant-fraction obstruction of QAOA for some instances of Max
k
XOR, at every fixed
k
, until the depth is logarithmic with
n
.

It is also possible that simple extensions to QAOA can avoid this obstruction, notably the so-called recursive approach (RQAOA) of
[
BKKT19
]
, given a suitable generalization of its variable elimination procedure to
k>2
.
We leave this as an open research direction.

6.1
Proof of obstruction results

We first show the proof of Theorem
3
.
Let’s define the following family of Hamiltonians
H_{n}
. Given the subset
V_{+}
with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry (such that
|V_{+}|=n^{\prime}
), construct a sparse random
D
-regular graph
G_{n^{\prime}}(V_{+},E)
. We call
G_{n^{\prime}}
the “inner graph”. We take
G
and add
\lfloor n^{\prime}/r\rfloor-O(1)\leq n^{\prime}/r
nodes for some constant
r>4
, split evenly into two sets:
A
(the sources) and
B
(the sinks). We replace every edge
e_{uv}
with two hyperedges: one that includes a node
a(e_{uv})\in A
and one that includes a node
b(e_{uv})\in B
. We assign hyperedges so that the maximum degree of each new node is at most
2Dr
. Then each Hamiltonian is defined as follows:

\displaystyle H_{n}

\displaystyle=H_{n,A}-H_{n,B}+|E|

\displaystyle H_{n,A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e_{uv}\in E}Z_{a(e_{uv})}Z_{u}Z_{v}

\displaystyle H_{n,B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e_{uv}\in E}Z_{b(e_{uv})}Z_{u}Z_{v}

This Hamiltonian is local, because each interaction term operates on
3=O(1)
qubits, and each qubit is involved in at most
2Dr=O(1)
terms.

7
Discussion

This paper calculates numerical upper and lower bounds of local classical and quantum algorithms approximately solving instances of Max
k
XOR. At
k>4
on triangle-free instances, the depth-1 QAOA is the best known local algorithm to our knowledge; however, both algorithms studied perform suboptimally on random instances at high
k
. The optimal value of a random instance is determined via its relationship to the Parisi constant of a spin glass; the numerical Parisi calculator is of independent interest. We also show that in the worst case, some low-depth quantum local circuits, including the QAOA, are obstructed on this problem for
k=3
. This is true even if the overlap gap property is not known to hold.

This work raises many open questions, some of which are quite general. What are the best algorithms, quantum or classical, for approximately solving Max
k
XOR at higher
k
? Is there a connection between the average performance of all one-local algorithms on triangle-free instances and instances with random signs? Is there a method to compute the maximum satisfying fraction of any constraint satisfaction problem using spin glass theory? Further study is needed here.

When
k=2
, we show an obstruction to sub-logarithmic-depth QAOA by converting obstructed Max
k
XOR instances with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
to Max
(k+1)
XOR instances with
partial
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry
. It is possible this procedure works for all even
k
, and extending the proof in
[
BKKT19
]
will lead to an obstruction for all
k\geq 2
. However, it is unknown whether any efficient algorithms, even nonlocal quantum algorithms such as RQAOA
[
BKKT19
]
, can outperform QAOA on the obstructed instances for
k>2
.

Ultimately it is important to better characterize the power of quantum circuits for exact and approximate optimization.
A natural extension of this work is to compare depth-
p
QAOA and depth-
p
local classical algorithms on Max
k
XOR for
p>1
.
However, quantum algorithms for optimization appear challenging to analyze beyond relatively simple cases. Even the QAOA proves difficult to analyze at constant-depth, let alone the more exciting
p=\Theta(\log n)
or
p=\textrm{poly}(n)
regimes.

In the meantime, the search continues for NISQ optimization advantage. Our results suggest that QAOA at depth-1 is only weakly advantageous on this problem, and that local quantum circuits are obstructed on some instances of this problem. But the QAOA or other quantum algorithms may be especially effective on certain families of Max
k
XOR instances. It remains open if there is a setting where near-term quantum computers solve an optimization problem definitively better than all classical computers; or, conversely, how much results obstructing quantum advantage can be generalized and strengthened.
