Abstract
. We introduce a convergent finite difference method for solving the optimal transportation problem on the sphere. The method applies to both the traditional squared geodesic cost (arising in mesh generation) and a logarithmic cost (arising in the reflector antenna design problem). At each point on the sphere, we replace the surface PDE with a Generated Jacobian equation posed on the local tangent plane using geodesic normal coordinates. The discretization is inspired by recent monotone methods for the Monge-Ampère equation, but requires significant adaptations in order to correctly handle the mix of gradient and Hessian terms appearing inside the nonlinear determinant operator, as well as the singular logarithmic cost function. Numerical results demonstrate the success of this method on a wide range of challenging problems involving both the squared geodesic and the logarithmic cost functions.



We consider the problem of optimal transportation on the sphere. That is, given two prescribed density functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, we seek a mapping
T:\mathbb{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{2}
such that

(1)

T=\argmin\limits_{T_{\#}f_{1}=f_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}c(x,T(x))f_{1}(x)dS(x).

Here
c(x,y)
is the cost of transporting a unit of mass from
x
to
y
and
T_{\#}f_{1}=f_{2}
indicates that

\int_{A}f_{1}(x)\,dS(x)=\int_{T(A)}f_{2}(y)\,dS(y)

for every measurable
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

In recent years, optimal transport has emerged as an important component in many different applications including image registration
[
13
]
,
astrophysics (estimating the shape of the early universe)
[
9
]
,
meteorology
[
7
]
, machine learning
[
21
]
, and geophysical inversion problems
[
8
]
, among others. This has led to the development of a number of different numerical methods and convergence results for solving the optimal transport problem in Euclidean geometries
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
14
,
16
,
22
,
25
,
29
]
.

A less well studied but equally important setting is optimal transport between density functions on the sphere.
Perhaps the simplest cost is the squared geodesic distance

c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)^{2},

where
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)
denotes the geodesic distance between
x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. This cost function has recently been applied to the problem of mesh generation on the sphere in the context of meteorology
[
19
,
28
]
.

A second cost of particular interest is the log cost

c(x,y)=-\log\left\|x-y\right\|,

which arises in the reflector antenna design problem
[
12
,
27
]
. The notation
\left\|\cdot\right\|
denotes the Euclidean distance in the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Recently, some progress has been made in the numerical solution of the Optimal Transport problem on the sphere in the case of the squared geodesic cost. The work of
[
28
]
used a geometric interpretation of a Monge-Ampère type equation on the sphere to produce the first such method. A finite element solution of this Monge-Ampère type equation was produced in
[
19
]
. For problems posed on a subset of the sphere, the stereographic projection can be used to reframe the problem as an optimal transport problem on the plane (with non-quadratic cost); this was the approach of
[
23
]
. The authors of the present article recently introduced a simple framework for proving the convergence of numerical methods for optimal transport on the sphere
[
15
]
. However, to date we are not aware of any methods that fit within this framework.

In this article, we produce the first convergent PDE-based method for solving the Optimal Transport problem on the sphere. This method is based on an approximation of a Generated Jacobian equation on local tangent planes, using carefully constructed local coordinates. The discretization is inspired by recent monotone methods that have been proposed for the Monge-Ampère equation. However, a complicating factor is the presence of gradient terms mixed with the Hessian terms inside of a nonlinear operator. This requires the introduction of new techniques for approximating both first- and second-order terms in order to preserve both the consistency and the monotonicity of our scheme. Additionally, the logarithmic cost function requires a careful regularization in order to preserve the well-posedness of the method. We produce an implementation and present computational results that demonstrate the success of this method for both the squared geodesic cost and the logarithmic cost.

1.
Background

1.1.
Optimal transport on the sphere

We consider points
x,y
lying on a unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
centered at the origin. We are interested in two different cost functions
c(x,y)
: the squared geodesic distance on the sphere,

(2)

c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(2\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\left\|x-y\right\|}{2}\right)\right)^{2},

and the log-cost arising in the reflector antenna problem,

(3)

c(x,y)=-\log\left\|x-y\right\|.

The optimal map corresponding to each cost function is determined from the conditions

(4)

\begin{cases}\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2},x}c\left(x,T(x,p)\right)=-p,&x\in\mathbb{S}^{2},p\in\mathcal{T}_{x}\\
T(x,p)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\end{cases}\vskip-4.0pt

where
\mathcal{T}_{x}
denotes the tangent plane at
x
. These mappings can be found explicitly for the cost functions we are interested in. In the case of the squared geodesic cost
[
19
]
, the map is

(5)

T(x,p)=\cos\left(\left\|p\right\|\right)x+\sin\left(\left\|p\right\|\right)\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}.

In the case of the logarithmic cost
[
15
]
, the map is

(6)

T(x,p)=x\frac{\left\|p\right\|^{2}-1/4}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}-\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}.

The solution to the optimal transport problem is then given by

(7)

F\left(x,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u(x),D_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}u(x)\right)=0

where

(8)

F(x,p,M)\equiv-\det\left(M+A(x,p)\right)+H(x,p)

subject to the
c
-convexity (ellipticity) condition, which requires

(9)

D_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}^{2}u(x)+A(x,\nabla_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}u(x))\geq 0.

Here

\displaystyle A(x,p)

\displaystyle=D_{\mathbb{S}^{2},xx}^{2}c\left(x,T(x,p)\right)

\displaystyle H(x,p)

\displaystyle=\left|\det{D_{\mathbb{S}^{2},xy}^{2}c\left(x,T(x,p)\right)}\right|f_{1}(x)/f_{2}\left(T(x,p)\right),

and the PDE now describes a nonlinear relationship between the surface gradient and Hessian on the sphere.

This PDE belongs to the class of Generated Jacobian equations
[
26
]
, and places constraints on the Jacobian of the mapping
T(x,\nabla u(x))
in order to force the density
f_{1}
to be transported into the density
f_{2}
. It is similar to the Monge-Ampère equation that is seen in optimal transport in Euclidean space (with quadratic cost
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
). However, the introduction of more complicated geometries and cost functions now leads to a mix of gradient and Hessian terms inside of the nonlinear determinant operator.

This problem was studied by Loeper
[
18
]
, who showed that under mild regularity requirements on the data, the optimal transport problem on the sphere admits a smooth (
C^{3}
) solution
u
.

Hypothesis 1

(Conditions on data)
.

We require problem data to satisfy the following conditions:

(a)

There exists some
m>0
such that
f_{2}(x)\geq m
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

(b)

The mass balance condition holds,
\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f_{1}(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f_{2}(y)\,dy
.

(c)

The cost function is either
c(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)^{2}
or
c(x,y)=-\log\left\|x-y\right\|
.

(d)

The data satisfies the regularity requirements
f_{1},f_{2}\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
.

Weak (
C^{1}
) solutions are also possible for discontinuous density functions that are only in
L^{p}
(
1\leq p<\infty
). Moreover, the solution is unique up to additive constants.

1.2.
Approximation of elliptic PDEs

The PDE operators we consider in this work are degenerate elliptic.

(10)

F\left(x,u(x),\nabla u(x),D^{2}u(x)\right)=0,\quad x\in\bar{\mathbb{S}^{2}}.

Definition 2

(Degenerate elliptic)
.

The operator

F:\bar{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{S}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

is
degenerate elliptic
if

F(x,u,X)\leq F(x,v,Y)

whenever
u\leq v
and
X\geq Y
.

The PDE operators that we consider in this work are degenerate elliptic if they are non-decreasing functions of the argument
u
and non-increasing functions of all subsequent arguments (which involve second directional derivatives).

Since degenerate elliptic equations need not have classical solutions, solutions may need to be interpreted in a weak sense. Moreover, even when smooth solutions exist, the use of weak solutions often provides an easier path to convergent numerical methods for fully nonlinear elliptic equations. The numerical methods developed in this article are guided by the very powerful concept of the viscosity solution
[
6
]
.

Definition 3

(Upper and lower semi-continuous envelopes)
.

The
upper and lower semi-continuous envelopes
of a function
u(x)
are defined, respectively, by

u^{*}(x)=\limsup_{\mathbf{y}\to x}u(\mathbf{y}),\quad u_{*}(x)=\liminf_{\mathbf{y}\to x}u(\mathbf{y}).

Definition 4

(Viscosity solution)
.

An upper (lower) semi-continuous function
u
is a
viscosity subsolution (supersolution)
of (
10
) if for every
\phi\in C^{2}(\bar{\Omega})
, whenever
u-\phi
has a local maximum (minimum) at
x\in\bar{\Omega}
, then

F_{*}^{(*)}(x,u(x),\nabla\phi(x),D^{2}\phi(x))\leq(\geq)0.

A continuous function
u
is a
viscosity solution
of (
10
) if it is both a subsolution and a supersolution.

The first steps towards constructing convergent methods for fully nonlinear elliptic equations where provided by a powerful framework introduced by Barles and Souganidis
[
1
]
and further developed by Oberman
[
20
]
.

We consider finite difference schemes that have the form

(11)

F^{h}\left(x,u(x),u(x)-u(\cdot)\right)=0

where
h
is a small parameter relating to the grid resolution.

The convergence framework requires notions of consistency, monotonicity and stability.

Definition 5

(Consistency)
.

The scheme (
11
) is
consistent
with the equation (
10
) if for any smooth function
\phi
and
x\in\bar{\Omega}
,

\limsup_{\epsilon\to 0^{+},\mathbf{y}\to x,\xi\to 0}F^{h}\left(\mathbf{y},\phi(\mathbf{y})+\xi,\phi(\mathbf{y})-\phi(\cdot)\right)\leq F^{*}(x,\phi(x),\nabla\phi(x),D^{2}\phi(x)),

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0^{+},\mathbf{y}\to x,\xi\to 0}F^{h}\left(\mathbf{y},\phi(\mathbf{y})+\xi,\phi(\mathbf{y})-\phi(\cdot)\right)\geq F_{*}(x,\phi(x),\nabla\phi(x),D^{2}\phi(x)).

Definition 6

(Monotonicity)
.

The scheme (
11
) is monotone if
F^{h}
is a non-decreasing function of its final two arguments.

Definition 7

(Stability)
.

The scheme (
11
) is stable if there exists some
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that if
u^{h}
is any solution of (
11
) then
\|u^{h}\|_{\infty}\leq M
.

To consistent schemes, we can also associate a local truncation error.

Definition 8

(Truncation error)
.

The truncation error
\tau(h)>0
of the scheme (
11
) is a quantity chosen so that for every smooth function
\phi

\limsup\limits_{h\to 0}\max\limits_{x\in\mathcal{G}^{h}}\frac{\left|F^{h}(x,\phi(x),\phi(x)-\phi(\cdot))-F(x,\nabla\phi(x),D^{2}\phi(x))\right|}{\tau(h)}<\infty.

Schemes that satisfy these three properties respect the notion of the viscosity solution at the discrete level. In particular, these schemes preserve the maximum principle and are guaranteed to converge to the solution of the underlying PDE if that equation satisfies a comparison principle.

Definition 9

(Comparison principle)
.

A PDE has a
comparison principle
if whenever
u
is an upper semi-continuous subsolution and
v
a lower semi-continuous supersolution of the equation, then
u\leq v
on
\bar{\Omega}
.

Theorem 10

(Convergence
[
20
]
)
.

Let
u
be the unique viscosity solution of the PDE (
10
), where
F
is a degenerate elliptic operator with a comparison principle. Let the approximation
F^{h}
be consistent, monotone, and stable and
u^{h}
any solution of the scheme (
11
). Then
u^{h}
converges uniformly to
u
as
h\to 0
.

1.3.
Convergence results for Optimal Transport on the sphere

The nonlinear PDE (
8
) that we are interested in does not satisfy a comparison principle, and thus the Barles-Souganidis convergence results do not immediately apply. However, the authors of this article have recently demonstrated that this framework could be extended to (
8
) through a careful consideration of the geometry and the introduction of a term that enforces a strong form of stability
[
15
]
.

1.3.1.
Reformulation of PDE

The first step in constructing convergent methods is to translate the surface PDE (
8
) at the point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
to an equation posed on the local tangent plane
\mathcal{T}_{x_{0}}
. This requires introducing local coordinates on the tangent plane. In general, local coordinates will introduce distortions to the Hessian that require the introduction of additional gradient terms. However, this problem can be avoided with the use of
geodesic normal coordinates
, which preserve distance from the reference point
x_{0}
.
An explicit expression for these coordinates
v_{x_{0}}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\setminus\{-x_{0}\}\to\mathcal{T}_{x_{0}}
is given by

(12)

v_{x_{0}}(x)=x_{0}\left(1-d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x)\cot d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x)\right)+x\left(d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x)\csc d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x)\right).

For each point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
we can now define a function
\tilde{u}_{x_{0}}(z)
on the relevant tangent plane
\mathcal{T}_{x_{0}}
in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
by

(13)

\tilde{u}_{x_{0}}(z)=u\left(v_{x_{0}}^{-1}(z)\right).

This choice of coordinates allows us to express the PDE (
8
) at the point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
as a generalized Monge-Ampère equation

(14)

F(x_{0},\nabla\tilde{u}(x_{0}),D^{2}\tilde{u}(x_{0}))\equiv-\det\left(D^{2}\tilde{u}(x_{0})+A(x_{0},\nabla\tilde{u}(x_{0}))\right)+H(x_{0},\nabla\tilde{u}(x_{0}))=0,

where all derivatives are now interpreted in the usual sense on a two-dimensional plane.

Remark 11
.

From this point on, we will refer to the surface PDE (
8
) and the tangent plane representation (
14
) interchangeably, with the implicit understanding that coordinates in the tangent plane are given by the geodesic local coordinates.

The problem of approximating the Optimal Transport PDE on the sphere can be further simplified by embedding the
c
-convexity (ellipticity) constraint into the equation. This is accomplished through the introduction of a modified determinant operator satisfying

(15)

\text{det}^{+}(M)=\begin{cases}\text{det}(M),\ \ \ M\geq 0\\
<0,\ \ \ \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then we can absorb the constraint into the PDE (
14
) through the modification

(16)

F^{+}(x,\nabla u(x),D^{2}u(x))\equiv-{\det}^{+}(D^{2}{u}(x)+A(x,\nabla u(x)))+H(x,\nabla u(x))=0.

Finally, the solutions of the Optimal Transport PDE satisfy
a priori
Lipschitz bounds
\left\|\nabla u\right\|<R
for any
R>\pi
(squared geodesic cost) or
R>C
(logarithmic cost; see
[
18
, Proposition 6.1]
for details). These bounds can be explicitly built into the PDE through a further modification

(17)

G(x,\nabla u(x),D^{2}u(x))\equiv\max\left\{F^{+}(x,\nabla u(x),D^{2}u(x)),\left\|\nabla u(x)\right\|-R\right\}=0.

While this modification enforces a condition that is automatically satisfied by solutions at the continuous level, it also improves the stability of approximation schemes.

1.3.2.
Convergence results

The convergence results of
[
15
]
require a mesh or point cloud
\mathcal{G}^{h}\subset\mathbb{S}^{2}
on the sphere that satisfies very mild structural regularity conditions.
We define the discretization parameter
h
as

(18)

h=\sup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\min\limits_{y\in\mathcal{G}^{h}}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y).

In particular, this guarantees that any ball of radius
h
on the sphere will contain at least one discretization point.

Then we require a grid that admits a triangulation without any long, skinny triangles. Specifically:

Hypothesis 12

(Conditions on point cloud)
.

There exists a triangulation
T^{h}
of
\mathcal{G}^{h}
with the following properties:

(a)

The diameter of the triangulation, defined as

(19)

\text{diam}(T^{h})=\max\limits_{t\in T^{h}}\text{diam}(t),

satisfies
\text{diam}(T^{h})\to 0
as
h\to 0
.

(b)

There exists some
\gamma<\pi
(independent of
h
) such that whenever
\theta
is an interior angle of any triangle
t\in T^{h}
then
\theta\leq\gamma
.

We remark that there is no need to construct this triangulation in practice, it need only exist in theory.

We also associate to each point cloud
\mathcal{G}^{h}
a search radius
r(h)
chosen to satisfy

(20)

r(h)\to 0,\,\frac{h}{r(h)}\to 0\text{ as }h\to 0,\quad\text{diam}(T^{h})<r(h).

Next, we project nearby grid points onto the local tangent plan
\mathcal{T}_{x_{0}}
, which is spanned by the orthonormal vectors
\left(\hat{\theta},\hat{\phi}\right)
. For all points
x_{i}\in\mathcal{G}^{h}\cap B\left(x_{0},r(h)\right)
, we define their projection onto the tangent plane through geodesic normal coordinates via

(21)

z_{i}=x_{0}\left(1-d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x_{i})\cot d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x_{i})\right)+x_{i}\left(d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x_{i})\csc d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x_{0},x_{i})\right).

Let
\mathcal{Z}^{h}(x_{0})\subset\mathcal{T}_{x_{0}}
be the resulting collection of points.

1
.

These are now the discretization points available to use for the approximation of (

) at
x_{0}
; recall that this PDE is posed on the two-dimensional tangent plane. The convergence result of
[
15
]
proposed a specific, simple discretization
E^{h}
of the Eikonal term
\|\nabla u(x_{0})\|
that is valid on any grid or point cloud.

(22)

E^{h}(z,u(z)-u(\cdot))=\max\limits_{y\in\mathcal{Z}^{h}(z)}\frac{u(z)-u(y)}{\left\|z-y\right\|}.

Then letting
F^{h}
be an approximation of the convexified PDE (
16
), we can define an approximation of the modified PDE (
17
) by

(23)

G^{h}(z,u(z)-u(\cdot))=\max\left\{F^{h}(z,u(z)-u(\cdot)),E^{h}(z,u(z)-u(\cdot))-R\right\},\quad z\in\mathcal{G}^{h}.

Fixing a point
x^{*}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and a sequence
x^{*}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}^{h}\to x^{*}
, we arrive at the following two-step approach for obtaining a numerical solution
u^{h}
of the Optimal Transport problem on the sphere.

1.

Solve the discrete system

(24)

G^{h}\left(z,v^{h}(z)-v^{h}(\cdot)\right)+\tau(h)v^{h}(z)=0,\quad z\in\mathcal{G}^{h}

for the grid function
v^{h}
.

2.

Define the candidate solution

(25)

u^{h}(z)=v^{h}(z)-v^{h}(x^{*}_{h}),\quad z\in\mathcal{G}^{h}.

Under appropriate assumptions on the density functions
f_{1},f_{2}
and the approximation scheme
F^{h}
, this is guaranteed to converge to the solution of the Optimal Transport problem on the sphere.

Theorem 13

(Convergence of approximation
[
15
]
)
.

Under the assumptions of Hypothesis
1
, let
u\in C^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
be the unique solution of (
8
) satisfying
u(x^{*})=0
. Let
\mathcal{G}^{h}
be a grid satisfying Hypothesis
12
and let
F^{h}
be any consistent, monotone approximation of (
17
). Then for each sufficiently small
h>0
, the grid function
u^{h}
defined in (
25
) is uniquely defined. Moreover,
u^{h}
converges uniformly to
u
as
h\to 0
.

Remark 14
.

The above convergence theorem can be extended to weak (
C^{1}
) solutions by additionally requiring the approximation scheme to underestimate the value of the PDE.

2.
Formulation of the PDE

The PDE (
8
)-(
9
) can be formulated in different equivalent ways. We begin by briefly describing a reformulation that lends itself to the construction of a discretization that fits within the framework of Theorem
13
.

2.1.
Regularization of logarithmic cost

One modification of the PDE that we find is necessary to build monotone schemes is to make the logarithmic cost Lipschitz by using a cutoff function. We recall (as discussed in
subsubsection 1.3.1
) that the solution
u
to the optimal transport satisfies an
a priori
Lipschitz bound
[
18
]

\|\nabla u\|<R
, which also yields the following lower bound on the distance mass can be transported.

(26)

\left\|x-y\right\|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1/4}}

when
y=T\left(x,\nabla u(x)\right)
is the exact transport map.

However, the process of solving a discrete version of (
8
) may evolve through values of
u
(and consequently
y
) that do not satisfy this bound. This loss of Lipschitz continuity can lead to a breakdown in monotonicity. We thus propose the following
C^{3}
regularization of the logarithmic cost function, which agrees with the true logarithmic cost when the bound (
26
) is satisfied.

(27)

\tilde{c}(x,y)=\begin{cases}-\log\left\|x-y\right\|,\ \ \ \left\|x-y\right\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1/4}}\\
\Psi(\left\|x-y\right\|),\ \ \ \left\|x-y\right\|<\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1/4}}\end{cases}

where
z_{*}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1/4}}
and

\Psi(z)=-\log(z_{*})-\frac{1}{z_{*}}(z-z_{*})+\frac{1}{2z_{*}^{2}}(z-z_{*})^{2}-\frac{1}{3z_{*}^{3}}(z-z_{*})^{3}.

Because this regularization does not change the solutions of the PDE, the analysis of
[
15
]
and the ultimate convergence result (Theorem
13
) will also apply to discretizations involving this smoother cost function.

Lemma 15

(Equivalence of solution for modified cost function)
.

Under the assumptions of Hypothesis
1
, a function
u\in C^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
is a solution of (
8
) with the logarithmic cost (
3
) if and only if it is a solution of (
8
) with the regularized cost (
27
).

Proof.

First let
u
be a solution using the original cost function
c(x,y)=-\log\left\|x-y\right\|
. Because of the
a priori
bounds on the gradient, this automatically satisfies the PDE (
8
) with the regularized cost function.

Next let
v
be any solution of (
8
) using the regularized cost function
\tilde{c}(x,y)
from (
27
). Notice that for

\left\|x-y\right\|\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1/4}}
, we have

(28)

\tilde{c}(x,y)=-\log\left(2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)\right)\right).

It is easily verified via differentiation that this is convex in
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)
for
\|x-y\|<\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{R^{2}+1/4}}
, and the original logarithmic cost is also convex in
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)
. The modified cost is
C^{3}
, so verifying that the second derivative in the variable
d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}(x,y)
is everywhere positive is sufficient to guarantee convexity in this variable. Since this new cost function is Lipschitz and convex in the Riemannian distance, the
\tilde{c}
-convex solution of (
8
) for the regularized cost
\tilde{c}(x,y)
is guaranteed to be unique by McCann as noted in
[
17
]
. Since the solution
u
of the original equation solves this modified equation, uniqueness requires that
v=u
.
∎

Remark 16
.

Because this regularization transforms the Optimal Transport problem with a singular cost function into an Optimal Transport problem with a smooth cost function, the techniques of
[
15
]
(which were introduced for the squared geodesic cost) can be extended to show that weak (
C^{0,1}
) solutions of this modified problem are also unique. This assumption of uniqueness of
C^{0,1}
viscosity solutions was needed to prove convergence even in the smooth setting. With minor modification, Theorem
13
can also be extended to handle convergence to weak solutions under much milder regularity requirements on the data; see
[
15
, Theorem 36]
. These modifications are addressed in
subsection 4.5
.

In the development and analysis of our numerical method in the following sections, we will often refer to the cost function
\tilde{c}
. In the case of the logarithmic cost, this refers to the regularized cost (
27
). In the case of the squared geodesic cost,
\tilde{c}
will refer to the original cost function
c
, which is automatically smooth.

2.2.
Variational formulation of the determinant of a Hessian

Since our PDE involves computing the determinant of a Hessian, here we show how to do this so as to later build a monotone discretization of the second derivatives. As utilized in
[
11
]
, Hadamard’s inequality allows the determinant of a positive definite matrix
M
to be computed via the minimization problem

(29)

\det M=\min_{v_{i}\in V}\prod_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}^{T}Mv_{i}

where
V
is the set of all orthogonal bases for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

We require a formulation satisfying (
15
), which modifies this formulation to ensure that no negative terms appear when the symmetric matrix
M
is not positive definite. A simple approach is to use

(30)

{\det}^{+}M=\min_{v_{i}\in V}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\max\left\{v_{i}^{T}Mv_{i},0\right\}.

If our matrix
M=D^{2}\phi(x)
is a Hessian matrix, this becomes:

\displaystyle{\det}^{+}(D^{2}\phi(x))

\displaystyle=\min_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in V}\left\{\prod_{j=1}^{2}\nu_{j}^{T}(D^{2}\phi(x))\nu_{j},0\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in V}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\max\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\nu_{j}^{2}},0\right\}.

In particular, this allows us to replace the determinant in (
8
) with

(31)

{\det}^{+}(D^{2}u(x)+A(x,\nabla u(x)))=\min_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in V}\prod_{j=1}^{2}\max\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}u(x)}{\partial\nu_{j}^{2}}+\left.\frac{\partial^{2}c(x,y)}{\partial\nu_{j}^{2}}\right|_{y=T(x,\nabla u(x))},0\right\}.

2.3.
Mixed Hessian

The framework developed by the authors of this article in
[
15
]
only requires the construction of consistent approximations of derivatives with respect to
x
, expressed in the geodesic normal coordinate system. For the mixed Hessian term,
\det D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y)
it is not immediately clear how to do this. In fact, the relative simplicity of the notation obfuscates the actual complexity of the object. In
[
17
]
, Loeper outlines a clearer representation of this quantity in curved geometries and for different cost functions.

We recall that the optimal map
T(x,p)
(also known as the
c
-exponential map) satisfies (
4
) and can be constructed explicitly for the cost function of interest to us via (
5
)-(
6
). Then from
[
17
]
, the mixed Hessian satisfies

(32)

[D^{2}_{xy}c]^{-1}=-D_{p}T(x,p)|_{x,p=-\nabla_{x}c(x,y)}.

This representation formula (
32
) shows that the inverse of the mixed Hessian is simply the Jacobian of the map
T(x,p)
with respect to
p
. Since we will be taking the determinant, this is actually a change of area formula for the transformation
T(x,p)
. See Figure
2
.

Figure 2.
The determinant of the mixed Hessian is the change in area formula from the set
T(x,E)
on the sphere sphere to the set
E
on the local tangent plane

We will compute this change of area by computing the linear differential map
dT
for both cost functions. That is, the change in area will be computed using orthogonal perturbations
\Delta p_{1}
,
\Delta p_{2}
(so
\Delta p_{1}\cdot\Delta p_{2}=0
) in the tangent plane
\mathcal{T}_{x}
.

\displaystyle\left|\det\right.

\displaystyle\left.(D_{p}T(x,p))\right|=

\displaystyle\left|\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})-T(x,p)}{\|\Delta p_{1}\|}\times\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})-T(x,p)}{\|\Delta p_{2}\|}\right|.

In general, the area element on a manifold is a function of the wedge product of two covectors, which need not be orthogonal. This has the interpretation of the area of a parallelogram on the manifold. However, in the special case where the vectors are orthogonal (or are orthogonal to leading order), this reduces to an ordinary product. This is indeed the case for both the squared geodesic and logarithmic cost functions. Thus the change of area formula reduces to the simpler expression

\displaystyle\left|\det\right.

\displaystyle\left.(D_{p}T(x,p))\right|=

\displaystyle\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0,\Delta p_{1}\cdot\Delta p_{2}=0}\frac{d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left(T(x,p),T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\right)d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left(T(x,p),T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\right)}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

and the determinant of the mixed Hessian is given by

(33)

\left|\det(D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y))\right|=\left.\frac{1}{\left|D_{p}T(x,p)\right|}\right|_{p=-\nabla_{x}c(x,y)}.

This can be computed explicitly for both the squared geodesic cost,

(34)

\left|\det(D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y))\right|=\frac{\left\|p\right\|}{\sin\left\|p\right\|},

and for the logarithmic cost,

(35)

\left|\det(D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y))\right|=\left(\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4\right)^{2}.

See Appendix
Appendix A: Derivation of the Mixed Hessian
for details.

We note also that these formulas coincide with the formulas that can be derived via standard change of variables formulas by requiring

\int_{T(x,E)}dS=\int_{E}\left|\det(D_{p}T(x,p))\right|\,dp

for every measurable
E\in\mathcal{T}(x)
.

).

is the search radius used to define the neighborhood

x_{\nu,j}-x_{i}

\nu

•

is the minimal absolute angle between

•

Each

•

Components of

3.2.
Approximation of second derivatives

Our overall approximation of (
8
) will hinge on the construction of (negative) monotone schemes for second directional derivatives
\dfrac{\partial^{2}\phi}{\partial\nu^{2}}
. We introduce approximations of the form

(37)

\mathcal{D}_{\nu\nu}\phi(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{4}a_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right).

As in
[
10
]
, consistency and negative monotonicity can be achieved by finding a solution of the system

(38)

\begin{cases}\sum a_{\nu,j}h_{\nu,j}\cos\theta_{\nu,j}=0\\
\sum a_{\nu,j}h_{\nu,j}\sin\theta_{\nu,j}=0\\
\sum\frac{1}{2}a_{\nu,j}h_{\nu,j}^{2}\cos^{2}\theta_{\nu,j}=1\\
a_{\nu,j}\geq 0\end{cases}

An explicit solution is given by

(39)

\begin{split}a_{\nu,1}&=\frac{2S_{\nu,4}(C_{\nu,3}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}S_{\nu,3})}{\det(A)}\\
a_{\nu,2}&=\frac{2S_{\nu,3}(C_{\nu,1}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}S_{\nu,1})}{\det(A)}\\
a_{\nu,3}&=\frac{-2S_{\nu,2}(C_{\nu,1}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}S_{\nu,1})}{\det(A)}\\
a_{\nu,4}&=\frac{-2S_{\nu,1}(C_{\nu,3}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}S_{\nu,3})}{\det(A)}\end{split}

where

(40)

\begin{split}\det(A)=&(C_{\nu,3}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}S_{\nu,3})(C_{\nu,1}^{2}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}^{2}S_{\nu,1})\\
&-(C_{\nu,1}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}S_{\nu,1})(C_{\nu,3}^{2}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}^{2}S_{\nu,3}).\end{split}

3.3.
Approximation of functions of the gradient

The PDE (
8
) involves several terms of the form
g(\nabla u)
. Importantly, either automatically or through appropriate regularization (see
subsection 2.1
), each of these functions
g
has a bounded Lipschitz constant
L_{g}
. This allows us to pursue a generalized Lax-Friedrichs type discretization of the form

(41)

\begin{split}\tilde{g}^{\pm}(\mathcal{D}\phi(x_{i}))=&g\left(\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\nu\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}b_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right)\right)\\
&\mp\epsilon_{g}\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}a_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right).\end{split}

Above, the coefficients
a_{\nu,j}
are identical to the coefficients that arise in the approximation of second directional derivatives. This introduces a Laplacian regularization term, which is carefully chosen to enforce monotonicity (or negative monotonicity) even if the coefficients
b_{\nu,j}
do not on their own produce a monotone scheme.

We first require coefficients
b_{\nu,j}
that ensure that

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\phi(x_{i})=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}b_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right)

is a consistent approximation of the first directional derivative
\dfrac{\partial\phi(x_{i})}{\partial\nu}
.

Taylor expanding, we obtain

\mathcal{D}_{\nu}\phi(x_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{4}b_{\nu,j}\left(h_{\nu,j}\cos\theta_{\nu,j}\frac{\partial\phi(x_{i})}{\partial\nu}+h_{\nu,j}\sin\theta_{\nu,j}\frac{\partial\phi(x_{i})}{\partial\nu^{\perp}}+\mathcal{O}(r^{2})\right).

Consistency then requires a solution of the system

(42)

\begin{cases}\sum b_{\nu,j}h_{\nu,j}\cos\theta_{\nu,j}=1\\
\sum b_{\nu,j}h_{\nu,j}\sin\theta_{\nu,j}=0.\end{cases}

An explicit solution is

(43)

\begin{split}b_{\nu,1}=\frac{S_{\nu,4}(S_{\nu,3}C_{\nu,2}^{2}-S_{\nu,2}C_{\nu,3}^{2})}{\det(A)}\\
b_{\nu,2}=-\frac{S_{\nu,3}(S_{\nu,4}C_{\nu,1}^{2}-S_{\nu,1}C_{\nu,4}^{2})}{\det(A)}\\
b_{\nu,3}=\frac{S_{\nu,2}(S_{\nu,4}C_{\nu,1}^{2}-S_{\nu,1}C_{\nu,4}^{2})}{\det(A)}\\
b_{\nu,4}=-\frac{S_{\nu,1}(S_{\nu,3}C_{\nu,2}^{2}-S_{\nu,2}C_{\nu,3}^{2})}{\det(A)}\end{split}

where
\det(A)
is again given by (
40
).

We then substitute these coefficients into (
41
) and define a regularization factor satisfying

(44)

\epsilon_{g}=\max\left\{\frac{L_{g}\left|b_{\nu,j}\right|}{a_{\nu,j}}\mid j\in\{1,2,3,4\},\,\nu\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}\right\}.

We will verify that this is finite and bounded in
section 4
.

3.4.
Approximation of the nonlinear operator

We now have the building blocks in place to describe a discretization of the full nonlinear operator (
8
) at the point
x_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
.

The variational formulation of the modified determinant (
30
) requires performing a minimization over the set
V
of orthogonal bases for
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the discrete version, we consider a finite subset of
V
that ensures that all directions are resolved in the limit
h\to 0
. A simple choice is given by

(45)

\tilde{V}=\left\{(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\mid\theta=jd\theta,\,j=0,\ldots,\frac{\pi}{2d\theta}\right\}

where
d\theta=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{h}\right)
is the angular resolution of the stencil described in
subsection 3.1
.

The PDE involves several different functions of the gradient. For compactness, we introduce the shorthand notation

(46)

\begin{split}g_{1,\nu}(x_{i},p)=\mathcal{D}_{\nu\nu}\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},p))\end{split}

where the differencing is performed only in the first argument of
\tilde{c}
. This involves the explicit formulas for the optimal map given in (
5
)-(
6
).

We also define

(47)

\begin{split}g_{2}(x_{i},p)&=\frac{\left|\det D^{2}_{xy}c\left(x_{i},T(x_{i},p)\right)\right|}{f_{2}\left(T(x_{i},p)\right)}\end{split}

recalling that the determinant of the mixed Hessian can be replaced with the simple explicit representations obtained in
subsection 2.3
, which we here denote by
H(p)
.

The discretization of these functions of the gradient require a regularization parameter (
44
), which involves Lipschitz bounds on these functions. We select bounds satisfying

(48)

L_{g_{1}}>L_{\tilde{c}}L_{T}

and

(49)

L_{g_{2}}\geq\left\|\frac{1}{f_{2}}\right\|^{2}\left(\|f_{2}\|L_{H}+\|H\|L_{f_{2}}L_{T}\right)

where
L_{T}
is the Lipschitz constant of the optimal map
T(x,p)
with respect to the variable
p
,
L_{H}
and
L_{f_{2}}
are the Lipschitz constants of the functions
H
and
f_{2}
respectively, and

(50)

L_{\tilde{c}}=\max\limits_{\left|\nu\right|=1,x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left\|\nabla_{y}\left(\nu^{T}D_{xx}^{2}\tilde{c}(x,y)\nu\right)\right\|.

We remark that while these constants
L_{g_{1}}
and
L_{g_{2}}
depend on the problem data and particular cost function, they are all guaranteed to be bounded under the assumptions of Hypothesis
1
and can be computed explicitly using the formulas in (
5
),(
6
), (
27
), (
34
), and (
35
).

We can then write down the full discretization of (
8
) as

(51)

\begin{split}F^{h}(x,u(x)-u(\cdot))=&-\min\limits_{(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\in\tilde{V}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{2}\max\left\{\mathcal{D}_{\nu_{j}\nu_{j}}u(x_{i})+\tilde{g}_{1,\nu_{j}}^{-}(\mathcal{D}u(x_{i})),0\right\}\\
&+f_{1}(x_{i})\tilde{g}_{2}^{+}\left(x_{i},\mathcal{D}u(x_{i})\right).\end{split}

3.5.
Solution method

In order to efficiently obtain a convergent approximation to (
8
), we will slightly modify the two-step procedure described
in (
52
)-(
25
). We propose instead the following solution and verification process, which is equivalent.

1.

Solve the discrete system

(52)

F^{h}(x,v^{h}(x)-v^{h}(\cdot))+\sqrt{h}v^{h}(x)=0,\quad x\in\mathcal{G}

for the grid function
v^{h}
.

2.

Verify that the grid function
v^{h}
satisfies the bounds

E^{h}(x,v^{h}(x)-v^{h}(\cdot))\leq R,\quad x\in\mathcal{G}.

3.

If the verification step fails, redefine
v^{h}
by solving the modified system (
52
)

G^{h}\left(x,v^{h}(x)-v^{h}(\cdot)\right)+\sqrt{h}v^{h}(x)=0,\quad x\in\mathcal{G}

using the solution obtained in Step 1 as an initial guess.

4.

Define the discrete solution

(53)

u^{h}(x)=v^{h}(x)-v^{h}(x^{*}_{h}),\quad x\in\mathcal{G}.

We note that in practice, we have never found Step 3 above to be necessary. Thus although this procedure appears to be longer than simply performing Steps 3-4, it actually allows us to obtain the same solution by solving a simpler system.

The strong nonlinearity in the PDE (
8
), particularly in that it involves nonlinear gradient terms that have very little required structure, makes the construction of a nonlinear Gauss-Jacobi, algebraic multigrid, and/or approximate Newton-type method highly nontrivial. In the present work, we perform all our computations using explicit parabolic schemes of the form

v_{n+1}^{h}(x_{i})=v_{n}^{h}(x_{i})-\Delta tF^{h}(v_{n}^{h}(x_{i}),v_{n}^{h}(x_{i})-v_{n}^{h}(\cdot)).

As discussed in
[
20
]
,
\Delta t
has to satisfy a nonlinear CFL condition in order to guarantee convergence. In particular, we require
\Delta t<1/L_{F^{h}}
, where
L_{F^{h}}
is the Lipschitz constant of
F^{h}
with respect to the arguments
u^{h}_{i}
. This Lipschitz constant scales like
L_{F^{h}}=\mathcal{O}(h^{-2})
and can either be determined explicitly
a priori
or adaptively under a requirement that the residual should decrease. In some cases, acceleration of this process is possible using the approach of
[
24
]
.

Faster solvers for these kinds of systems are, of course, desirable and will be explored in future work.

4.
Convergence

We are now prepared to prove that the numerical method defined by (
51

Theorem 18

(Convergence)
.

Under the assumptions of Hypothesis
1
, let
u\in C^{3}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
be the unique solution of (
8
) satisfying
u(x^{*})=0
. Let
\mathcal{G}^{h}
be a grid satisfying Hypothesis
12
and let
F^{h}
be defined as in (
51
). Then for each sufficiently small
h>0
, the grid function
u^{h}
defined in (
53
) is uniquely defined. Moreover,
u^{h}
converges uniformly to
u
as

.

This result follows immediately from the framework described in Theorem
13
provided we can verify that our approximation scheme
F^{h}
is consistent (Lemma
25

4.1.
Bounds on coefficients

We begin by demonstrating that the coefficients
a_{\nu,j},b_{\nu,j}
appearing in the approximation of the second directional derivatives can be bounded.

Lemma 19

(Bounds on coefficients (second derivatives))
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all sufficiently small
h>0
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the coefficients defined by (
39
) satisfy

a_{\nu,j}\geq\frac{C}{h}.

Proof.

We establish a bound for the coefficient
a_{\nu,1}
; the remaining coefficients are similar.

Recall the notation
C_{\nu,j}=h_{\nu,j}\cos\theta_{\nu,j}
,
S_{\nu,j}=h_{\nu,j}\sin\theta_{\nu,j}
. Since each
x_{\nu,j}
lies in the
jth
quadrant, each of these terms has a definite sign. Based on the requirements on
h_{\nu,j},d\theta_{\nu,j}
established in Lemma
17
, we can record the asymptotic bounds

\displaystyle(r-2h)\left(1-\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left|C_{\nu,j}\right|\leq r

\displaystyle(r-2h)\left(d\theta-\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left|S_{\nu,j}\right|\leq r\left(2d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3})\right).

We recall also that
r,d\theta=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})
.

These observations allow us to establish the following bounds:

\begin{split}\det(A)=&(C_{\nu,3}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}S_{\nu,3})(C_{\nu,1}^{2}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}^{2}S_{\nu,1})\\
&-(C_{\nu,1}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}S_{\nu,1})(C_{\nu,3}^{2}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}^{2}S_{\nu,3})\\
&\leq r^{5}(4d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}))(4d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}))+r^{5}(4d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}))(4d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}))\\
&=32r^{5}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{9/2})\end{split}

and

\begin{split}-2S_{\nu,4}(C_{\nu,2}S_{\nu,3}-C_{\nu,3}S_{\nu,2})&\geq 2(r-2h)^{3}(d\theta-\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}))\left(2d\theta-\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3})\right)\\
&=4r^{3}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{3}).\end{split}

Combining these bounds, we obtain

a_{\nu,1}\geq\frac{4r^{3}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{3})}{32r^{5}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{9/2})}=\frac{1}{8r^{2}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})\right)

where
r^{2}=\mathcal{O}(h)
.
∎

Lemma 20

(Bounds on coefficients (first derivatives))
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all sufficiently small
h>0
and
\nu\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the coefficients defined by (
42
) satisfy

\left|b_{\nu,j}\right|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{h}}.

Proof.

We proceed as in the proof of Lemma
19
and compute the bounds

\begin{split}\det(A)=&(C_{\nu,3}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}S_{\nu,3})(C_{\nu,1}^{2}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}^{2}S_{\nu,1})\\
&-(C_{\nu,1}S_{\nu,4}-C_{\nu,4}S_{\nu,1})(C_{\nu,3}^{2}S_{\nu,2}-C_{\nu,2}^{2}S_{\nu,3})\\
&\geq 8(r-2h)^{5}\left(1-\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{2})\right)^{3}\left(d\theta-\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3})\right)^{2}\\
&=8r^{5}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{4})\end{split}

and

0\leq S_{\nu,4}(S_{\nu,3}C_{\nu,2}^{2}-S_{\nu,2}C_{\nu,3}^{2})\leq 2r^{4}(2d\theta+\mathcal{O}(d\theta^{3}))^{2}=8r^{4}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{4}).

Combining these, we find that

0\leq b_{\nu,1}\leq\frac{8r^{4}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{4})}{8r^{5}d\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{4})}=\frac{1}{r}\left(1+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})\right)

with
r=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{h}\right)
.

The other coefficients are similar, though some are positive and some are negative.
∎

4.2.
Bounds on Lipschitz constants

We next establish Lipschitz bounds on the functions
g_{1,\nu},g_{2}
defined in (
46
) and (
47
), which play an important role in the discretization of functions of the gradient.

Lemma 21

(Lipschitz bound on
g_{1,\nu}
)
.

Let
x_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
be fixed. Then for
p\in\mathcal{T}_{x_{i}}
, the function

g_{1,\nu}(p)=\mathcal{D}_{\nu\nu}\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},p)).

has a Lipschitz constant
L
satisfying

L\leq L_{\tilde{c}}L_{T}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h}).

Proof.

We first recall that
g_{1,\nu}(p)
involves a finite difference discretization. By consistency, we have

g_{1,\nu}(p)=\nu^{T}\left(D_{xx}^{2}\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},p))\right)\nu+C(p)\sqrt{h}

where the coefficient
C(p)
arising in the discretization error is at least Lipschitz continuous in
p
. Then using regularity, we can calculate

\displaystyle g_{1,\nu}(p)-g_{1,\nu}(q)

\displaystyle=\nu^{T}D_{xx}^{2}\left(\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},p))-\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},q))\right)\nu+(C(p)-C(q))\sqrt{h}

\displaystyle\leq\max\limits_{\left|\nu\right|=1,x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left\|\nabla_{y}\left(\nu^{T}D_{xx}^{2}\tilde{c}(x,y)\nu\right)\right\|\|T(x_{i},p)-T(x_{i},q)\|+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})\|p-q\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(L_{\tilde{c}}L_{T}+\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})\right)\|p-q\|.\qed

Lemma 22

(Lipschitz bound on
g_{2}
)
.

Let
x_{i}\in\mathcal{G}
be fixed. Then for
p\in\mathcal{T}_{x_{i}}
, the function

g_{2}(p)=\frac{\left|\det D^{2}_{xy}\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},p))\right|}{f_{2}(T(x_{i},p))}

has a Lipschitz constant
L
satisfying

L\leq\left\|\frac{1}{f_{2}}\right\|^{2}\left(\|f_{2}\|L_{H}+\|H\|L_{f_{2}}L_{T}\right).

Proof.

Using the notation
H(p)=\left|\det D^{2}_{xy}\tilde{c}(x_{i},T(x_{i},p))\right|
, we have

\displaystyle g_{2}(p)-g_{2}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{H(p)}{f_{2}(T(x_{i},p))}-\frac{H(q)}{f_{2}(T(x_{i},q))}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}(T(x_{i},q))H(p)-f_{2}(T(x_{i},p))H(q)}{f_{2}(T(x_{i},p))f_{2}(T(x_{i},q))}

\displaystyle=\frac{f_{2}(T(x_{i},q))(H(p)-H(q))+H(q)(f_{2}(T(x_{i},q))-f_{1}(T(x_{i},p)))}{f_{2}(T(x_{i},p))f_{2}(T(x_{i},q))}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{f_{2}}\right\|^{2}\left(\|f_{2}\|L_{H}+\|H\|L_{f_{2}}L_{T}\right)\|p-q\|.\qed

4.3.
Lax-Friedrichs approximations

We now verify that the Lax-Friedrichs type approximations for functions of the gradient defined in (
41
) are both consistent and monotone.

Lemma 23

(Consistency of functions of gradient)
.

Let
g
be Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{g}
and
\phi\in C^{2}
. Then

\begin{split}\tilde{g}^{\pm}(\mathcal{D}\phi(x_{i}))=&g\left(\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\nu\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}b_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right)\right)\\
&\mp\epsilon_{g}\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}a_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right).\end{split}

is a consistent approximation of
g(\nabla\phi)
.

Proof.

We note that by construction, we have

\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\nu\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}b_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right)=\nabla\phi(x_{i})

and

\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}a_{\nu,j}\left(\phi(x_{\nu,j})-\phi(x_{i})\right)=\Delta\phi(x_{i}).

Using Lemmas
19
-
20
, we can also bound
\epsilon_{g}
via

\displaystyle\epsilon_{g}

\displaystyle=\max\left\{\frac{L_{g}\left|b_{\nu,j}\right|}{a_{\nu,j}}\mid j\in\{1,2,3,4\},\,\nu\in\{(0,1),(1,0)\}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{g}(C_{b}/\sqrt{h})}{C_{a}/h}

\displaystyle=C\sqrt{h}

where the constant
C
is independent of
h
.

Combining these results, we obtain

\lim\limits_{h\to 0}\tilde{g}^{\pm}(\mathcal{D}\phi(x_{i}))=g(\nabla\phi(x_{i})),

with a truncation error of
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{h})
.
∎

Lemma 24

(Monotonicity of functions of the gradient)
.

Let
g
be Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
L_{g}
and
\phi\in C^{2}
. Then the schemes
\tilde{g}^{+}(\mathcal{D}\phi(x_{i}))
and
\tilde{g}^{-}(\mathcal{D}\phi(x_{i}))
are monotone and negative monotone respectively.

Proof.

We verify monotonicity of
\tilde{g}^{+}(\mathcal{D}\phi(x_{i}))
; the other part of the argument is identical.

Denoting by
d_{j}
the differences
\phi(x_{i})-\phi(x_{\nu,j})
allows us to express the scheme more compactly as

G(d)=g\left(-\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\nu\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}b_{\nu,j}d_{j}\right)+\epsilon_{g}\sum\limits_{\nu\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{4}a_{\nu,j}d_{j}.

We now introduce a perturbation
\delta>0
into the argument
d_{k}
to obtain

\displaystyle G(d+\delta\hat{d}_{k})-G(d)

\displaystyle\geq-L_{g}\left|b_{\nu,k}\right|\delta+\epsilon_{g}a_{\nu,k}\delta

\displaystyle\geq 0

since
\epsilon_{g}\geq L\left|b_{\nu,k}\right|/a_{\nu,k}
. Therefore the scheme is monotone.
∎

4.5.
Extensions to nonsmooth problems

The results of
[
18
]
ensure existence of weak solutions to the optimal transport PDE (
8
) in the relaxed setting where
f_{1},f_{2}\in L^{1}
with
f_{2}
bounded away from zero and
f_{1}
bounded away from infinity.
In
[
15
]
, the authors of this article showed that these solutions can be computed for the squared geodesic cost as in Theorem
13
if the scheme
F^{h}
is additionally required to underestimate when applied to the true solution. This result has now been extended to the regularized logarithmic cost via the remark
16
.

Naturally underestimating schemes can be constructed for Monge-Ampère type equations in some cases
[
3
,
14
]
. An alternate approach is to utilize a scheme of the form

\tilde{F}^{h}(x,u(x)-u(\cdot))=F^{h}(x,u(x)-u(\cdot))-h^{\alpha}

for a sufficiently small
\alpha>0
. This preserves the consistency and monotonicity of the original scheme, while decreasing the value of the scheme and forcing it to be negative when applied to the true solution of the PDE.

In addition, definition of consistency (Definition
5
) in terms of upper and lower semicontinuous envelopes of the PDE operator allows us to accommodate discontinuous data
f_{1},f_{2}
as in
[
14
]
. This does not require any change in the way
f_{1}(x)
is handled. However, functions
f_{2}\notin C^{0,1}
must be carefully regularized to preserve consistency and monotonicity since it takes as its argument terms
T(x,\nabla u(x))
that involve gradients.

The approach we propose is to introduce a discrete version of the target density function

(54)

f_{2}^{h}(y)=(K_{h^{1/4}}*f_{2})(y)

where
K_{h^{1/4}}
is a mollifier that ensures that the Lipschitz constant of
f_{2}^{h}
satisfies

L_{f_{2}^{h}}\leq h^{-1/4}.

From here, we use the same discretization of
f_{2}(T(x,\nabla u(x))
introduced in (
41
).
We note that in this case (following Lemma
23
), the regularization parameter will satisfy

\epsilon_{g_{2}^{h}}=\mathcal{O}\left(L_{f_{2}^{h}}\sqrt{h}\right)=\mathcal{O}(h^{1/4}).

Since this parameter converges to zero as
h\to 0
, the resulting scheme will still be consistent in the sense of Definition
5
. The monotonicity result (Lemma
24
) is unchanged.

5.1.
Structured and Unstructured Grids

The scheme we have built works well on both structured and unstructured grids, provided that they satisfy the mild conditions of Hypothesis
12
. By structured we mean that there exists a deterministic way of building the grid. Likewise, unstructured here means there is a stochastic element in the construction of the grid.

Here, we describe four types of grids that satisfy these hypotheses and that we make use of in practice: the cube grid (structured), the random grid (fully unstructured), the latitude-longitude grid (unstructured), and the layered grid (structured).

The structured cube grid is constructed as follows. First, a grid of evenly-spaced points is generated on the faces of a cube which contains the sphere. Then, the points on such a grid on the cube are projected onto the sphere. See Figure
5
for an example of the resulting grid.

The semi-unstructured latitude-longitude grid is constructed as follows. We begin with a structured grid composed of points equally spaced in both latitude
\theta
and longitude
\phi
. However, this produces a highly over-resolved grid near the poles, which does not satisfy the required structure conditions. In order to get rid of this redundancy, we stochastically remove grid points within a geodesic distance
\mathcal{O}(h)
of both poles. That is, for each grid point
x_{i}=(\theta_{i},\phi_{i})
, we compute
\xi_{i}=\sin\theta_{i}
and generate a value using the random variable
\Xi\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
. If

, then we remove the point

from the grid. The removal of these points creates a new unstructured grid that almost surely satisfies Hypothesis
12
. See Figure
5
for an example of such a grid.

To construct the structured layered grid, we take an integer
n
and
\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(h)
and define the rows:

. For each row, we have

, where

6.
Conclusion

We have introduced a provably convergent numerical method for solving the Optimal Transport problem on the sphere. The method applies to both the squared geodesic cost function (important in mesh generation) and the logarithmic cost function (arising in the reflector antenna problem). This method involves replacing the PDE on the manifold with equivalent PDEs on local tangent planes using a careful choice of geodesic normal coordinates. The resulting PDE was approximated using monotone schemes inspired by recent methods for solving the Monge-Ampère equation. However, significant new techniques were introduced to handle the complicated nonlinear combination of gradient and Hessian terms. Moreover, we introduced a smooth cutoff for the logarithmic cost function to enable well-posed, consistent, and monotone discretizations even in the presence of this potentially unbounded cost function.

We produced an implementation of this method and demonstrated via computations how our numerical method is able to address issues heretofore unresolved. Notably, the method can handle both structured and unstructured grids, non-smooth data, and produces compelling results in examples closely related to both the moving mesh and reflector antenna design problems.

Appendix A: Derivation of the Mixed Hessian

In this appendix, we fill in the details of the derivation of simple expressions for the determinant of the mixed Hessian. For each cost function, we take the following approach:

(1)

Introduce orthogonal perturbations
\Delta p_{1},\Delta p_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{x}
such that
\Delta p_{1}\cdot p=0
and
\Delta p_{2}=\hat{p}\|\Delta p_{2}\|
.

(2)

Establish that
T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})
and
T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})
are orthogonal to leading order.

(3)

Compute the change of area formula

\displaystyle\left|\det\right.

\displaystyle\left.(D_{p}T(x,p))\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left(T(x,p),T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\right)d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left(T(x,p),T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\right)}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\|\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\|}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

where we can simplify the formulas by using the fact that

\left\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p)\right\|=d_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\left(T(x,p),T(x,p+\Delta p)\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\|\Delta p\right\|^{2}\right).

A.1.
Squared geodesic cost

We begin with the squared geodesic cost, recalling that the mapping
T
has the explicit form

T(x,p)=\cos\left(\left\|p\right\|\right)x+\sin\left(\left\|p\right\|\right)\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}.

First consider a perturbation satisfying
\Delta p_{1}\cdot p=0
.

\displaystyle T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})=

\displaystyle x\left(\cos\left\|p\right\|-\cos\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}\sin\left\|p\right\|-\frac{p+\Delta p_{1}}{\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|}\sin\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|

Now, since
p
and
\Delta p_{1}
are orthogonal,

\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|=\sqrt{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|^{2}}=\|p\|+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|^{2}\right).

Thus to leading order we obtain

T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})=\Delta p_{1}\frac{\sin\left\|p\right\|}{\left\|p\right\|}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|^{2}\right).

Now, suppose that
\Delta p_{2}=\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}
. In this case,
\left\|p+\Delta p_{2}\right\|=\left\|p\right\|+\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|
. As before, we compute to leading order:

\displaystyle T(x,p)

\displaystyle-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})

\displaystyle=x\left(\cos\left\|p\right\|-\cos\left\|p+\Delta p_{2}\right\|\right)+\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}\sin\left\|p\right\|-\frac{p+\Delta p_{2}}{\left\|p+\Delta p_{2}\right\|}\sin\left\|p+\Delta p_{2}\right\|

\displaystyle=x\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\sin\left\|p\right\|-\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\cos\left\|p\right\|+\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}}\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\sin\left\|p\right\|-\frac{\Delta p_{2}}{\left\|p\right\|}\sin\left\|p\right\|

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=x\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\sin\left\|p\right\|-\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\cos\left\|p\right\|+\mathcal{O}\left(\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|^{2}\right).

Now since
x\cdot\Delta p_{1}=p\cdot\Delta p_{1}=\Delta p_{1}\cdot\Delta p_{2}=0
, we can easily verify that

(T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1}))\cdot(T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2}))=o\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|+\|\Delta p_{2}\|\right)

so that the perturbations in the map are indeed orthogonal to leading order.

Now we can use orthogonality to easily compute the magnitudes of these perturbations via

\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\|^{2}=\|\Delta p_{1}\|^{2}\frac{\sin^{2}\left\|p\right\|}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}}+o\left(\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|^{2}\right)

and

\displaystyle\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\sin^{2}\|p\|+\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\cos^{2}\|p\|+o\left(\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}+o\left(\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\right).

where we have used the fact that
x
and
p/\|p\|
are unit vectors.

Then we can compute the change of area formula as

\displaystyle\left|\det\right.

\displaystyle\left.(D_{p}T(x,p))\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\|\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\|}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|\Delta p_{1}\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|\sin\|p\|/\|p\|+o\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|\right)}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\|p\|}{\|p\|}.

Hence, the determinant of the mixed Hessian for the squared geodesic cost is

(59)

\left|\det D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y)\right|=\frac{\left\|p\right\|}{\sin\left\|p\right\|}.

A.2.
Logarithmic cost

Now we perform the same procedure for the logarithmic map, which has the explicit form

T(x,p)=x\frac{\left\|p\right\|^{2}-1/4}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}-\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}.

We again begin with a perturbation satisfying
p\cdot\Delta p_{1}=0
so that

\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|=\|p\|+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|^{2}\right).

To leading order, we can compute

\displaystyle T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})

\displaystyle=x\frac{\left\|p\right\|^{2}-1/4}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}-x\frac{\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|^{2}-1/4}{\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|^{2}+1/4}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}+\frac{p+\Delta p_{1}}{\left\|p+\Delta p_{1}\right\|^{2}+1/4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta p_{1}}{\|p^{2}\|+1/4}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|^{2}\right).

Next we consider an orthogonal perturbation
\Delta p_{2}=\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|}
so that

\|p+\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}=\|p\|^{2}+2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\right).

Now we can compute to leading order

\displaystyle\|T(x,p)

\displaystyle-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\|=x\frac{\left\|p\right\|^{2}-1/4}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}-x\frac{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|-1/4}{\left\|p\right\|+2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}+1/4}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\frac{p}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}+\frac{p+\Delta p_{2}}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|+1/4}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-x\frac{2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|}{\|p\|^{2}+1/4}+x\frac{2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|(\|p\|^{2}-1/4)}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{\Delta p_{2}}{\|p\|^{2}+1/4}-p\frac{2\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-x\frac{\|p\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}}+\hat{p}\frac{\|\Delta p_{2}\|(1/4-\|p\|^{2})}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}\right).

Since
x
,
p
, and
\Delta p_{1}
are mutually orthogonal, we can immediately verify that

(T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1}))\cdot(T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2}))=o\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|+\|\Delta p_{2}\|\right)

so that the perturbations in the mapping are again orthogonal to leading order.

Next we compute the lengths of the perturbations using orthogonality:

\left\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\right\|=\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\frac{1}{\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4}+o(\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|)

and

\displaystyle\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|p\|^{2}\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}+\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}(1/4-\|p\|^{2})^{2}}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{4}}+o(\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{4}}\left(\|p\|^{4}+\frac{1}{2}\|p\|^{2}+\frac{1}{16}\right)+o(\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\Delta p_{2}\|^{2}}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}}+o(\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|^{2}).

Then we can again compute the change of area formula as

\displaystyle\left|\det\right.

\displaystyle\left.(D_{p}T(x,p))\right|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{1})\|\|T(x,p)-T(x,p+\Delta p_{2})\|}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|,\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|\rightarrow 0}\frac{\|\Delta p_{1}\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|/(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}+o\left(\|\Delta p_{1}\|\|\Delta p_{2}\|\right)}{\left\|\Delta p_{1}\right\|\left\|\Delta p_{2}\right\|}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\|p\|^{2}+1/4)^{2}}.

Hence, the determinant of the mixed Hessian for the logarithmic cost is

(60)

\left|\det D^{2}_{xy}c(x,y)\right|=\left(\left\|p\right\|^{2}+1/4\right)^{2}.
