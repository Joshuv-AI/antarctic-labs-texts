The Three Stages of Learning Dynamics in High-dimensional Kernel Methods
Nikhil Ghosh, Song Mei, Bin Yu

Abstract
To understand how deep learning works, it is crucial to understand the training dynamics of neural networks. Several interesting hypotheses about these dynamics have been made based on empirically observed phenomena, but there exists a limited theoretical understanding of when and why such phenomena occur. In this paper, we consider the training dynamics of gradient flow on kernel least-squares objectives, which is a limiting dynamics of SGD trained neural networks. Using precise high-dimensional asymptotics, we characterize the dynamics of the fitted model in two “worlds”: in the Oracle World the model is trained on the population distribution and in the Empirical World the model is trained on a sampled dataset. We show that under mild conditions on the kernel and L 2 L^{2} target regression function the training dynamics undergo three stages characterized by the behaviors of the models in the two worlds. Our theoretical results also mathematically formalize some interesting deep learning phenomena. Specifically, in our setting we show that SGD progressively learns more complex functions and that there is a “deep bootstrap” phenomenon: during the second stage, the test error of both worlds remain close despite the empirical training error being much smaller. Finally, we give a concrete example comparing the dynamics of two different kernels which shows that faster training is not necessary for better generalization.

Introduction

In order to fundamentally understand how and why deep learning works, there has been much effort to understand the learning dynamics of neural networks trained by gradient descent based algorithms. This effort has led to the discovery of many intriguing empirical phenomena (e.g.
Frankle et al. 2020
;
Fort et al. 2020
;
Nakkiran et al. 2019a
;
Nakkiran et al. 2019b
;
Nakkiran et al. 2020
) that help shape our conceptual framework for understanding the learning process in neural networks.
Nakkiran et al. 2019b
provides evidence that SGD starts by first learning a linear classifier and over time learns increasingly functionally complex classifiers.
Nakkiran et al. 2020
introduces the ‘‘deep bootstrap’’ phenomenon: for some deep learning tasks the empirical world test error remains close to the oracle world error
1
1

1

Their paper uses “Ideal World” for “Oracle World” and “Real World” for “Empirical World”.
for many SGD iterations, even if the empirical training and test errors display a large gap. To better understand such phenomena, it is useful to study training dynamics in relevant but mathematically tractable settings.

One approach for theoretical investigation is to study kernel methods, which were recently shown to have a tight connection with over-parameterized neural networks
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Du et al. 2018
)
. Indeed, consider a sequence of neural networks
(f_{N}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
with the widths of the layers going to infinity as
N\to\infty
. Assuming proper parametrization and initialization, for large
N
the SGD dynamics on
f_{N}

is known to be well approximated by the corresponding dynamics on the first-order Taylor expansion of
f_{N}
around its initialization
{\bm{\theta}}^{0}
,

f_{N,\rm{lin}}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}})=f_{N}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{0})+\langle\gradient_{{\bm{\theta}}}f_{N}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{0}),{\bm{\theta}}-{\bm{\theta}}^{0}\rangle.

Thus, in the large width limit it suffices to study the dynamics on the linearization
f_{N,\rm{lin}}
.
When using the squared loss, these dynamics correspond to optimizing a kernel least-squares objective with the neural tangent kernel
K_{N}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\langle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{N}({\bm{x}};{\bm{\theta}}^{0}),\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f_{N}({\bm{x}}^{\prime};{\bm{\theta}}^{0})\rangle
.

Over the past few years, researchers have used kernel machines as a tractable model to investigate many neural network phenomena including benign overfitting, i.e., generalization despite the interpolation of noisy data
(
Bartlett et al. 2020
;
Liang & Rakhlin 2020
)
and double-descent, i.e., risk curves that are not classically U-shaped
(
Belkin et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
. Kernels have also been studied to better understand certain aspects of neural network architectures such as invariance and stability
(
Bietti & Mairal 2017
;
Mei et al. 2021b
)
. Although kernel methods cannot be used to explain some phenomena such as feature learning, they can still be conceptually useful for understanding other neural networks properties.

Informally, our main results show that under reasonable conditions on the regression function and the kernel the training dynamics undergo the following three stages:

•

Stage one
: the empirical training, the empirical test, and the oracle errors are all close.

•

Stage two
: the empirical training error decays to zero, but the empirical test error and the oracle error stay close and keep
approximately
constant.

•

Stage three
: the empirical training error is still zero, the empirical test error stays
approximately
constant, but the oracle test error decays to the approximation error.

The existence of the first and third stages are not unexpected: at the beginning of training the model has not fit the dataset enough to distinguish the oracle and empirical world and at the end of training an expressive enough model with infinite samples will outperform one with finitely many. The most interesting stage is the second one where the empirical model begins to “overfit” the training set while still remaining close to the non-interpolating oracle model in the
L^{2}

Related Literature

The generalization error of the kernel ridge regression (KRR) solution has been well-studied in both the fixed dimension regime
(
Wainwright 2019
, Chap. 13)
,
(
Caponnetto & De Vito 2007
)
and the high-dimensional regime
(
El Karoui 2010
;
Liang & Rakhlin 2020
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Ghorbani et al. 2020
;
Ghorbani et al. 2021
;
Mei et al. 2021a
;
Mei et al. 2021b
)
. Most closely related to our results is the setting of
(
Ghorbani et al. 2021
;
Mei et al. 2021a
;
Mei et al. 2021b
)
. Analysis of the entire KRR training trajectory has also been done
(
Yao et al. 2007
;
Raskutti et al. 2014
;
Cao et al. 2019
)
but only for the fixed dimensional setting. Classical non-parametric rates are often obtained by specifying a strong regularity assumption on the target function (e.g. the source condition in
Fischer & Steinwart 2020
), whereas in our work the assumption on the target function is mild.

Another line of work directly studies the dynamics of learning in linear neural networks
(
Saxe et al. 2013
;
Li et al. 2018
;
Arora et al. 2019
;
Vaskevicius et al. 2019
)
. Similar to us, these works show that some notion of complexity (typically effective rank or sparsity) increases in the linear network over the course of optimization.

The relationship between the speed of iterative optimization and gap between population and empirical quantities has been studied before in the context of algorithmic stability
(
Bousquet & Elisseeff 2002
;
Hardt et al. 2016
;
Chen et al. 2018
)
. These analyses certify good empirical generalization by using stability in the first few iterations to upper bound the gap between train and test error. In contrast, our analysis directly computes the errors at an arbitrary time
t
(c.f. Remark
2
). The relationship between oracle and empirical training dynamics has been considered before in
Bottou & LeCun 2004
and
Pillaud-Vivien et al. 2018
.

Results

In this section we introduce the problem and present a specialization of our results to two concrete settings:
dot product and group invariant kernels on the sphere
(Theorems
1
and
2
respectively). The more general version of our results is described in Appendix

3.1
Problem setup

We consider the supervised learning problem where we are given i.i.d. data
({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})_{i\leq n}
. The covariate vectors
({\bm{x}}_{i})_{i\leq n}\sim_{iid}{\rm Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
and the real-valued noisy responses
y_{i}=f_{d}({\bm{x}}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
for some unknown target function
f_{d}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
and
(\varepsilon_{i})_{i\leq n}\sim_{iid}{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
.

Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
, we define its test error
R(f)
and its training error
\widehat{R}_{n}(f)
as

R(f)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}}_{\rm{new}},y_{\rm{new}})}\{(y_{\rm{new}}-f({\bm{x}}_{\rm{new}}))^{2}\},~~~~~~~\widehat{R}_{n}(f)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f({\bm{x}}_{i}))^{2},

(1)

where
({\bm{x}}_{\rm{new}},y_{\rm{new}})
is i.i.d. with
({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})_{i\leq n}
. The test error
R(f)
measures the fit of
f
on the population distribution and the training error
\widehat{R}_{n}(f)
measures the fit of
f
to the training set.

For a kernel function
H_{d}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})\to\mathbb{R}
, we analyse the dynamics of the following two fitted models indexed by time
t
: the
oracle model

f^{\rm{or}}_{t}
and the
empirical model

\hat{f}_{t}
, which are given by the gradient flow on
R
and
\widehat{R}_{n}
over the associated RKHS
{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
respectively

\displaystyle\derivative{t}f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=-\gradient R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{x}}))=\mathbb{E}[H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})(f_{d}({\bm{z}})-f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{z}}))],

(2)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=-\gradient\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}))=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{i})(y_{i}-\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{i})),

(3)

with zero initialization
f^{\rm{or}}_{0}\equiv\hat{f}_{0}\equiv 0
. These dynamics are motivated from the neural tangent kernel perspective of over-parameterized neural networks
(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Du et al. 2018
)
. A precise mathematical definition and derivation of these two dynamics are provided in Appendix
E.1
.

For our results we make some assumptions on the spectral properties of the kernels
H_{d}
similar to those in
Mei et al. 2021a
that are discussed in detail in Appendix
A.2
.
At a high-level we require that the diagonal elements of the kernel concentrate, that the kernel eigenvalues obey certain spectral gap conditions, and that the top eigenfunctions obey a hyperconctractivity condition which says they are “delocalized”
. For the specific settings of Theorems
1
and
2
we give more specific conditions on the kernels that are more easily verified and imply the required spectral properties.

3.2
Dot Product Kernels

In our first example, we consider dot product kernels
H_{d}
of the form

H_{d}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})=h_{d}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\rangle/d),\,~~~~~~~\forall{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),

(4)

for some function
h_{d}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
.
Our results apply to general dot product kernels under weak conditions on
h_{d}
given in Appendix
C.2
. In particular they apply to the random feature and neural tangent kernels associated to certain fully connected neural networks
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
.

Before presenting our results for this setting we introduce some notation. Denote by
\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\leq\,\ell}
the orthogonal projection onto the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
spanned by polynomials of degree less than or equal to
\ell
. The projectors
\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\ell}
and
\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\ell}
are defined analogously (see Appendix
G
for details). We use
o_{d}(\cdot)
for standard little-o relations, where the subscript
d
emphasizes the asymptotic variable. The statement
f(d)=\omega_{d}(g(d))
is equivalent to
g(d)=o_{d}(f(d))
. We use
o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
in probability relations. Namely for two sequences of random variables
Z_{1}(d)
and
Z_{2}(d)
,
Z_{1}(d)=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(Z_{2}(d))
if for any
\varepsilon,C_{\varepsilon}>0
there exists
d_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, such that
\mathbb{P}(|Z_{1}(d)/Z_{2}(d)|<C_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon
for all
d\geq d_{\varepsilon}
. The asymptotic notations
O_{d},O_{d,\mathbb{P}}
etc. are defined analogously.

Theorem 1

(Dot Product Kernels)
.

Let
\{f_{d}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions such that for some
\eta>0
,
\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}=O_{d}(1)
, and let
\{H_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of dot product kernels satisfying Assumption
4
. Assume that for some fixed integers
\mathsf{j},\mathsf{s}\geq 0
and some
\delta>0
that

d^{\mathsf{j}+\delta}\leq t\leq d^{\mathsf{j}+1-\delta},~~~~~d^{\mathsf{s}+\delta}\leq n\leq d^{\mathsf{s}+1-\delta}.

Then we have the following characterizations,

(a)

(Oracle World) The oracle model learns every degree component of
f_{d}
as time progresses

R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t})=\left\lVert\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\mathsf{j}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d}(1),~~~~~\left\lVert f^{\rm{or}}_{t}-\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\leq\,\mathsf{j}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d}(1).

(b)

(Empirical World – Train) Empirical training error follows oracle error then goes to zero

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\mathsf{j}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\text{ if }t/n=o_{d}(1),

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\text{ if }t/n=\omega_{d}(1).

(c)

(Empirical World – Test) Empirical test error follows oracle error until the empirical model learns the degree-
\mathsf{s}
component of
f_{d}

R(\hat{f}_{t})=\left\lVert\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\min\{\mathsf{j},\mathsf{s}\}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1),~~~~~\left\lVert\hat{f}_{t}-\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\leq\,\min\{\mathsf{j},\mathsf{s}\}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1).

The results are conceptually illustrated in an example in Fig.
3(a)
which shows the stair-case phenomenon in high-dimensions and the three learning stages. We see that in both the oracle world and the empirical world, the prediction model increases in complexity over time. More precisely, the model learns the best polynomial fit to the target function (in an
L^{2}
sense) of increasingly higher degree. In the empirical world the maximum complexity is determined by the sample size
n
, which is in contrast to the oracle world where there are effectively infinite samples.

The results imply that generally (but not always c.f. Remark
1
) there will be three stages of learning. In the first stage the oracle and empirical world models are close in
L^{2}
and fit a polynomial with degree determined by
t
. The first stage lasts from
t=0
to
t=nd^{-\varepsilon}\ll n
for some small
\varepsilon>0
. As
t
approaches
n
, there is a phase transition and the empirical world training error goes to zero at
t=nd^{\varepsilon}\gg n
. From time
nd^{-\varepsilon}
till at least
d^{\mathsf{s}+1}
is the second stage where the empirical and oracle models remain close in
L^{2}
but the gap between test and train error can be large. If the sample size
n
is not large enough for
\hat{f}_{t}
to learn the target function, then at some large enough
t
we will enter a third stage where
f^{\rm{or}}_{t}
improves in performance, outperforming
\hat{f}_{t}
which remains the same. On synthetic data in finite dimensions we can see a resemblance of the staircase shape which becomes sharper with increasing
d
(c.f. Appendix
F
).

3.3
Group Invariant Kernels

We now consider our second setting which concerns the invariant function estimation problem introduced in
Mei et al. 2021b
. As before, we are given i.i.d. data
({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})_{i\leq n}
where the feature vectors
({\bm{x}}_{i})_{i\leq n}\sim_{iid}{\rm Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
and noisy responses
y_{i}=f_{d}({\bm{x}}_{i})+\varepsilon_{i}
. We now assume that the target function
f_{d}
satisfies an invariant property. We consider a general type of invariance, defined by a group
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
that is represented as a subgroup of the orthogonal group in
d
dimensions. The group element
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
acts on a vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
via
{\bm{x}}\mapsto g\cdot{\bm{x}}
. We say that
f_{\star}
is
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
-invariant if
f_{\star}({\bm{x}})=f_{\star}(g\cdot{\bm{x}})
for all
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
. We denote the space of square integrable
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
-invariant functions by
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})
. We focus on groups
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
that are
groups of degeneracy
\alpha
as defined below. As an example, we consider the cyclic group
\Cyc_{d}=\{g_{0},g_{1},\ldots,g_{d-1}\}
where for any
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
, the group action is defined by
g_{i}\cdot{\bm{x}}=(x_{i+1},x_{i+2},\ldots,x_{d},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{i})^{\mathsf{T}}
. The cyclic group has degeneracy
1
.

Definition 1

(Groups of degeneracy
\alpha
)
.

Let
V_{d,k}
be the subspace of degree-k polynomials that are orthogonal to polynomials of degree at most
(k-1)
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
, and denote by
V_{d,k}({\mathcal{G}}_{d})
the subspace of
V_{d,k}
formed by polynomials that are
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
-invariant. We say that
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
has degeneracy
\alpha
if for any integer
k\geq\alpha
we have
\dim(V_{d,k}/V_{d,k}({\mathcal{G}}_{d}))\asymp d^{\alpha}
(i.e., there exists
0<c_{k}\leq C_{k}<+\infty
such that
c_{k}\leq\dim(V_{d,k}/V_{d,k}({\mathcal{G}}_{d}))\leq C_{k}
for any
d\geq 2
).

To encode invariance in our kernel we consider
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
-invariant kernels
H_{d}
of the form

H_{d,\text{inv}}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})=\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{d}}h(\langle{\bm{x}}_{1},g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{2}\rangle/d)\pi_{d}(\differential{g}),

(5)

where
\pi_{d}
is the Haar measuare on
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
. Such kernels satisfy the following invariance property: for all
g,g^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
and for
H_{d}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})=H_{d}(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{1},g^{\prime}\cdot{\bm{x}}_{2})
for every
{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}
. For the cyclic group,
\pi_{d}
is the uniform measure. We now present our results for the group invariant setting.

(Group Invariant Kernels)
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
be a group of degeneracy
\alpha\leq 1
according to Definition
1
. Let
\{f_{d}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})\}_{d\geq 1}
a sequence of
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
-invariant functions such that for some
\eta>0
,
\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}=O_{d}(1)
, and let
\{H_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
-invariant kernels satisfying Assumption
5
. Assume that for some fixed integers
\mathsf{j}\geq 0,\mathsf{s}\geq 1
and some
\delta>0
that

d^{\mathsf{j}+\delta}\leq t\leq d^{\mathsf{j}+1-\delta},~~~~~d^{\mathsf{s}-\alpha+\delta}\leq n\leq d^{\mathsf{s}+1-\alpha-\delta}.

Then we have the following characterizations,

(a)

(Oracle World) The oracle model learns every degree component of
f_{d}
as time progresses

R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t})=\left\lVert\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\mathsf{j}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d}(1),~~~~~\left\lVert f^{\rm{or}}_{t}-\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\leq\,\mathsf{j}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d}(1).

(b)

(Empirical World – Train) Empirical training error follows oracle error then goes to zero

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\mathsf{j}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\text{ if }t/n=o_{d}(d^{\alpha}),

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\text{ if }t/n=\omega_{d}(d^{\alpha}).

(c)

(Empirical World – Test) Empirical test error follows oracle error until the empirical model learns the degree-
\mathsf{s}
component of
f_{d}

R(\hat{f}_{t})=\left\lVert\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{>\min\{\mathsf{j},\mathsf{s}\}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1),~~~~~\left\lVert\hat{f}_{t}-\overline{\mathsf{P}}_{\leq\,\min\{\mathsf{j},\mathsf{s}\}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1).

With respect to the dot product kernel setting (c.f. Theorem
1
), in this setting the behavior of the oracle world is unchanged, but the empirical world behaves as if it has
d^{\alpha}
times as many samples. This is illustrated graphically in Fig.

(c.f. Appendix

d^{\alpha}=d
, we reach the following intriguing conclusion: if the target function is cyclically invariant, then using a dot product kernel and augmenting a training set of
n
i.i.d. samples to
nd
many samples is asymptotically equivalent to training with
nd
i.i.d. samples.

Remark 2

(Optimization Speed versus Generalization)
.

Interestingly
, training with an invariant kernel
is

slower than with a dot product kernel and takes longer to interpolate the dataset despite eventually generalizing better on invariant function estimation tasks (c.f. Fig.
3(c)
).
This conclusion is
not an artifact of the continuous time analysis (c.f. Appendix
E.3
) and is observed empirically in Section
4.2
for discrete-time SGD
.
This example highlights the limitation of stability based analyses (e.g.
Hardt et al. 2016
) which argue that faster SGD training leads to better generalization. While a faster rate leads to better generalization in the first stage when stability can control the gap between train and test error, our analysis shows that the duration length of the first stage also impacts the final generalization error.

Remark 3

(Connection with Deep Phenomena)
.

The dynamics of high-dimensional kernel regression display behaviors
that parallel

some
empirically observed phenomena in deep learning. For kernel regression,
we have shown that
the complexity of the empirical model
, measured as the number of learned eigenfunctions,

depends on the time optimized when
t\ll n
and the sample size when
t\gg n
.
At a high-level, we also expect a similar story for neural networks
but for some other notion of complexity
. It is believed that neural networks first learn simple functions and then progressively more complex ones,
until the complexity saturates after interpolating at some time proportional to
n

(
Nakkiran et al. 2019b
)
.

We have also shown that in kernel regression there is a non-trivial “deep boostrap” phenomenon
(
Nakkiran et al. 2020
)
during the second learning stage:

the gap between the oracle world and empirical world test errors is negligible whereas the train and test errors exhibit a substantial gap.
The
gradient flow
results
for kernel regression
can also provide insight into the deep bootstrap for random feature networks trained with discrete-time SGD
as these results
can approximately predict their behavior
(see Section
4.2
).

Remark 4

(Connection with Deep Learning Practice)
.

Although we believe our results conceptually shed light on some of the interesting behaviors observed in the training dynamics of deep learning,
due to our stylized setting we may not exactly see the predicted phenomena in practice.

Accurately observing the three stages
of kernel regression

requires sufficiently high-dimensional data in order for the kernel eigenvalues to obey a staircase-like decay and for training to be sufficiently long as the time axis should be in log-scale. Our results hold for regression whereas for classification the empirical model may continue improving after classifying the train set correctly. Despite these caveats, certain conclusions can be observed in some realistic settings (c.f. Appendix
E.4
).

5
Summary and Discussion

In this paper, we used precise asymptotics to study the oracle world and empirical world dynamics of gradient flow on kernel least-squares objectives for high-dimensional regression problems. Under reasonable conditions on the target function and kernel, we showed that in this setting there are three learning stages based on the behaviors of the empirical and oracle models and also connected our results to some empirical deep learning phenomena.

Although our setting already captures some interesting aspects of deep learning training dynamics, there are some limitations which would be interesting to resolve in future work. We require very high-dimensional data in order for the asymptotics to be accurate, but real data distributions have low-dimensional structure.
We work in a limiting regime of neural network training where the dynamics are linear and the step-size is infinitesimal. It is an important direction to extend this analysis to the non-linear feature learning regime and to consider discrete step-size minibatch SGD, as these are considered important aspects of network training.

Our results hold for the square-loss in regression problems, but many deep learning problems involve classification using cross-entropy loss. Lastly, our analysis holds specifically for gradient flow, so it would be also interesting to consider other iterative learning algorithms such as boosting.

A.1

Consider a sequence of Polish probability spaces
({\mathcal{X}}_{d},\nu_{d})

\norm{f}_{L^{p}({\mathcal{X}}_{d})}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\nu_{d}}[|f({\bm{x}})^{p}|]^{1/p}

L^{p}

We consider a general RKHS defined on

\mathbb{H}_{d}g({\bm{x}})=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{d}}H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})g({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\nu_{d}(\differential{\bx'}),

\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{d}}H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})g({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\nu_{d}(\differential{\bx'})=0

\Span(\psi_{j},j\geq 1)={\mathcal{D}}_{d}\subseteq L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}_{d})

(\lambda_{d,j})_{j\geq 1}

L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}_{d}\times{\mathcal{X}}_{d})
.

\mathbb{H}_{d,S}

If
S=\{j\in\mathbb{N}:j\leq\ell\}

\mathbb{H}_{d,\leq\ell}

S=\{j\in\mathbb{N}:j>\ell\}
. The trace of this operator is given by

\Tr(\kernelop_{d,S})\equiv\sum\limits_{j\in S}\lambda_{d,j}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\nu_{d}}[H_{d,S}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})]<\infty.

\widehat{R}_{n}:L^{2}({\mathcal{X}}_{d})\to\mathbb{R}

R(f)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}}_{\rm{new}},y_{\rm{new}})}\{(y_{\rm{new}}-f({\bm{x}}_{\rm{new}}))^{2}\},~~~~~~~\widehat{R}_{n}(f)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-f({\bm{x}}_{i}))^{2},

(9)

where
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n}),({\bm{x}}_{\rm{new}},y_{\rm{new}})

A.2
General Assumptions

We now state our assumptions on the kernel and the sequence of probability spaces
({\mathcal{X}}_{d},\nu_{d})
.

Assumption 1

(
\{n(d),\mathsf{m}(d)\}_{d\geq 1}
-Kernel Concentration Property)
.

We say that the sequence of operators
\{\mathbb{H}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
satisfies the Kernel Concentration Property (KCP) with respect to the sequence
\{n(d),\mathsf{m}(d)\}_{d\geq 1}
if there exists a sequence of integers
\{r(d)\}_{d\geq 1}
with
r(d)\geq\mathsf{m}(d)
, such that the following conditions hold.

(a)

(Hypercontractivity of finite eigenspaces.) For any fixed
q\geq 1
, there exists a constant
C
such that for any
h\in{\mathcal{D}}_{d,\leq r(d)}=\Span(\psi_{s},1\leq s\leq r(d))
, we have

\norm{h}_{L^{2q}}\leq C\norm{h}_{L^{2}}.

(b)

(Properly decaying eigenvalues) There exists fixed
\delta_{0}>0
, such that, for all
d
large enough,

\displaystyle n(d)^{2+\delta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\sum_{j=r(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,j}^{4})^{2}}{\sum_{j=r(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,j}^{8}},

\displaystyle n(d)^{2+\delta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\sum_{j=r(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,j}^{2})^{2}}{\sum_{j=r(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,j}^{4}}.

(c)

(Concentration of diagonal elements of kernel) For
({\bm{x}}_{i})_{i\in[n(d)]}\sim_{iid}\nu_{d}
, we have:

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n(d)]}\quantity|\E_{\bx\sim\nu_d}[H_{d, >\nind(d)}(\bx_i, \bx)^2] - \E_{\bx, \bx' \sim\nu_d}[H_{d, >\nind(d)}(\bx, \bx')^2]|

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\sim\nu_{d}}[H_{d,>\mathsf{m}(d)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})^{2}],

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[n(d)]}\quantity|H_{d, >\nind(d)}(\bx_i, \bx_i) - \E_{\bx}[H_{d, >\nind(d)}(\bx, \bx)]|

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}}[H_{d,>\mathsf{m}(d)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})].

Assumption
1
(a)
can be interpreted as requiring that the top eigenfunctions of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
are delocalized. Assumption
1
(b)
concerns the tail of eigenvalues of
\mathbb{H}_{d}
and is a mild assumption in high-dimensions. Lastly, Assumption
1
(c)
essentially requires that “most points” in
{\mathcal{X}}_{d}
behave similarly in the sense of having similar values of the kernel diagonal
H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}})
.

Assumption 2

(Eigenvalue condition at level
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
)
.

We say that the sequence of kernel operators
\{\mathbb{H}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
satisfies the Eigenvalue Condition at level
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
if the following conditions hold for all
d
large enough

(a)

There exists a fixed
\delta_{0}>0
, such that

\displaystyle n(d)^{1+\delta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{d,\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{4}}\sum_{k=\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,k}^{4},

(12)

\displaystyle n(d)^{1+\delta_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{d,\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{2}}\sum_{k=\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,k}^{2}.

(13)

(b)

There exists a fixed
\delta_{0}>0
, such that

n(d)^{1-\delta_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{\lambda_{d,\mathsf{m}(d)}^{2}}\sum_{k=\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,k}^{2}.

(c)

There exists a fixed
\delta_{0}>0
, such that

\mathsf{m}(d)\leq n(d)^{1-\delta_{0}}.

Assumptions
2
(a)
and
2
(b)
can be seen as a spectral gap assumption. This ensures a clear separation between the eigenvalues in the subspace
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,\leq\mathsf{m}(d)}
and the subspace
{\mathcal{D}}_{d,>\mathsf{m}(d)}
. The technical requirement in Assumption
2
(c)
is mild.

In the asymptotic setting, we will be interested in the model learned at a time
t=t(d)
scaling with the dimension. The following assumptions give requirements for a valid scaling.

Assumption 3

(Admissible Time at
\{(t(d),\mathsf{u}(d),n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
)
.

We say that the sequence of tuples
\{(t(d),\mathsf{u}(d),n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
is an Admissible Time
for the sequence of kernel operators
\{\mathbb{H}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
if the following conditions hold

(a)

There exists a fixed
\delta_{0}>0
, such that for
d

\frac{1}{\lambda_{d,\mathsf{u}(d)}^{2}}\leq t(d)^{1-\delta_{0}}\leq t(d)^{1+\delta_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{d,\mathsf{u}(d)+1}^{2}}.

(b)

If
\mathsf{u}(d)<\mathsf{m}(d)
for infinitely many

\frac{t(d)}{n(d)}\sum_{k=\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,k}^{2}=o_{d}(1).

(c)

There exists a constant
C
such that

\sum_{k=1}^{\mathsf{m}(d)}\lambda_{d,k}^{2}\leq C\sum_{k=\mathsf{m}(d)+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{d,k}^{2}.

Assumption

is similar to the spectral gap condition Assumption

(a)
,
2

for
(t(d),\mathsf{u}(d))_{d\geq 1}

Theorem 3

\displaystyle R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t})=\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\mathsf{u}(d)}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d}(1)\cdot\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

The next results describe the empirical model. First we characterize the training error.

Theorem 4

(Empirical World - Train)
.

Let
\{f_{d}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions, let the covariates
({\bm{x}}_{i})_{i\in[n(d)]}\sim\nu_{d}
independently, and let
\{\mathbb{H}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of kernel operators such that
\{(\mathbb{H}_{d},n(d),\mathsf{m}(d),t(d),\mathsf{u}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
satisfies
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
-KPCP (Assumption
1
), eigenvalue condition at level
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
(Assumption
2
), and
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d),t(d),\mathsf{u}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
is a valid time (Assumption
3
). Define
\kappa_{H}=\Tr(\kernelop_{d, >\nind(d)})
and
\ell(d)=\min\{\mathsf{u}(d),\mathsf{m}(d)\}
. Then for any
\eta>0
we have,

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell(d)}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot(\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})

\displaystyle\text{if }t=o_{d}(n/\kappa_{H}),

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot(\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})

\displaystyle\text{if }t=\omega_{d}(n/\kappa_{H}).

In the early-time regime
t\ll n/\kappa_{H}
the training error may be non-zero and matches the oracle world error if also
\mathsf{u}(d)\leq\mathsf{m}(d)
. In the late-time regime
t\gg n/\kappa_{H}
the training error is negligible and the model interpolates the training set. The quantity
\kappa_{H}
arises since the empirical kernel matrix can be decomposed as
{\bm{H}}={\bm{H}}_{\leq\mathsf{m}}+{\bm{H}}_{>\mathsf{m}}
and the second component is approximately a multiple of the identity:
{\bm{H}}_{>\mathsf{m}}\approx\Tr(\bH_{> \nind})\cdot{\mathbf{I}}_{n}=\kappa_{H}\cdot{\mathbf{I}}_{n}
. This term acts as a self-induced ridge-regularizer.

Our final result characterizes the test error of the empirical model.

Theorem 5

(Empirical World - Test)
.

Let
\{f_{d}\in{\mathcal{D}}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of functions, let the covariates
({\bm{x}}_{i})_{i\in[n(d)]}\sim\nu_{d}
independently, and let
\{\mathbb{H}_{d}\}_{d\geq 1}
be a sequence of kernel operators such that
\{(\mathbb{H}_{d},n(d),\mathsf{m}(d),t(d),\mathsf{u}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
satisfies
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
-KPCP (Assumption
1
), eigenvalue condition at level
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
(Assumption
2
), and
\{(n(d),\mathsf{m}(d),t(d),\mathsf{u}(d))\}_{d\geq 1}
is a valid time (Assumption
3
). Define
\ell(d)=\min\{\mathsf{u}(d),\mathsf{m}(d)\}
. Then for any
\eta>0
we have,

\displaystyle R(\hat{f}_{t})=\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell(d)}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot(\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\hat{f}_{t}-\mathsf{P}_{\leq\ell(d)}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot(\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}).

The result shows that the empirical model is essentially the projection of the regression function onto the first
\ell(d)
eigenfunctions. The quantity

B.1
Oracle World - Proof of Theorem
3

The oracle model ODE Eq. (
10
) with initialization
f^{\rm{or}}_{0}\equiv 0
can be solved (c.f. Appendix
E.1
) to yield the solution

f^{\rm{or}}_{t}=f_{d}-e^{-t\mathbb{H}_{d}}f_{d}.

Therefore the excess risk is given by

R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t})-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim\nu_{d}}(f_{d}({\bm{x}})-f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{x}}))^{2}=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{d}}f_{d}({\bm{x}})e^{-2t\mathbb{H}_{d}}f_{d}({\bm{x}})\nu_{d}(\differential{\bx})=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp(-2t \lambda_{d,k}^2)\hat{f}_{k}^{2},

where
\lambda_{d,k}^{2}
are the kernel eigenvalues and
\hat{f}_{k}:=\langle f_{d},\psi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}}
are the Fourier coefficients of
f_{d}
in the kernel eigenbasis (c.f. Appendix
A.1
).
We can control this quantity as follows,

\displaystyle|R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t})-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\mathsf{u}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}|/\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\quantity{\max_{k \leq\tind} \exp(-2t \lambda_{d,k}^2), \max_{k \geq\tind+ 1} 1 - \exp(-2t \lambda_{d,k}^2)}

\displaystyle\leq\max\quantity{\max_{k \leq\tind} \exp(-\Omega(t \lambda_{d,k}^2)), \max_{k \geq\tind+ 1} O(t \lambda_{d,k}^2)}

\displaystyle\leq\max\quantity{\exp(-\Omega(t^{\delta_0})), O(t^{-\delta_0})}=o_{d}(1),

where the last inequality follows from Assumption
3
(a)
. This shows the first theorem statement,

\left\lVert f^{\rm{or}}_{t}-\mathsf{P}_{\leq\mathsf{u}}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d}(1)\cdot\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Now observe that

\mathsf{P}_{\leq u}f_{d}-f^{\rm{or}}_{t}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\mathsf{u}}e^{-t\lambda_{d,k}^{2}}\hat{f}_{k}\psi_{k}-\sum\limits_{k\geq\mathsf{u}+1}(1-e^{-t\lambda_{d,k}^{2}})\hat{f}_{k}\psi_{k},

hence

\displaystyle\left\lVert f^{\rm{or}}_{t}-\mathsf{P}_{\leq u}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}/\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\quantity{\max_{k \leq\tind} \exp(-2t \lambda_{d,k}^2), \max_{k \geq\tind+ 1} (1 - e^{-t \lambda_{d,k}^2})^2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\quantity{\max_{k \leq\tind} \exp(-2t \lambda_{d,k}^2), \max_{k \geq\tind+ 1} 1 - \exp(-2t \lambda_{d,k}^2)}=o_{d}(1),

where the second inequality follows from the fact that
(1-e^{-x})^{2}\leq 1-e^{-2x}
and the final equality is from the proof of the first part of the theorem. Thus,

\left\lVert f^{\rm{or}}_{t}-\mathsf{P}_{\leq u}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=o_{d}(1)\cdot\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2},

completing the proof.

\left\lVert e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)
, hence

\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

From now on we focus on the case that
t=o_{d}(n/\kappa_{H})
. Let us decompose the training error as

\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})=R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3},

where

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{f}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}},

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}},

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{\varepsilon}}.

B.3.1
Term
R_{1}

Let us start by analysing
R_{1}
. We can write

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}({\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell}+{\bm{f}}_{>\ell})^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}({\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell}+{\bm{f}}_{>\ell})

\displaystyle=T_{1}+2T_{2}+T_{3}

where

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell},

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{f}}_{>\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell},

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{f}}_{>\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}}_{>\ell}.

Let us analyse the term
T_{1}
,

T_{1}\leq\frac{1}{n}{\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}}{\bm{f}}_{\leq\ell}.

By Lemma
2
, denoting
{\bm{B}}={\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}/n
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}={\bm{B}}e^{-2t{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}}.

We will show that

\left\lVert{\bm{B}}e^{-2t{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1).

(22)

By Lemma
4
, since
\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}=\Theta_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)
, for
d
large
{\bm{B}}
has a positive-definite square root
{\bm{B}}^{1/2}
w.h.p. Therefore we can write

{\bm{B}}e^{-2t{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}}={\bm{B}}^{1/2}e^{-2t{\bm{B}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}^{1/2}}{\bm{B}}^{1/2},

and bound the operator norm

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\bm{B}}e^{-2t{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}\left\lVert e^{-2t{\bm{B}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}^{1/2}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}e^{-2t\lambda_{\min}({\bm{B}}^{1/2}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{B}}^{1/2})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}e^{-2t\lambda_{\max}({\bm{B}})\lambda_{\min}({\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2})}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1),

since by Assumption
3
(a)
,
t\lambda_{\min}({{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}})=\Omega(t^{\delta_{0}})
. Therefore
T_{1}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{\leq\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}
.

Now we analyse the term
T_{3}
. Observe that by the inequality
1-x\leq e^{-x}\leq 1
,

\frac{1}{n}\norm{\bfg{\cut}}_{2}^{2}-(2t/n)\frac{1}{n}{\bm{f}}_{>\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}({\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}+\kappa_{H}({\mathbf{I}}_{n}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{H})){\bm{f}}_{>\ell}\leq T_{3}\leq\frac{1}{n}\norm{\bfg{\cut}}_{2}^{2}.

We have by Lemma
7
,

t\mathbb{E}[\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{>\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Psi}}_{>\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}{\bm{D}}_{\leq\ell}^{2}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Psi}}_{>\ell}\widehat{{\bm{f}}}_{>\ell}]/n^{2}\leq C(\eta)\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}\quantity(\frac{t}{n} \sum\limits_{s=1}^\cut\lambda_{d, s}^2),

where the last quantity is
o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}
by Assumption
3
(c)
. Thus we see that

T_{3}=\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}.

Observe that by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle T_{2}\leq(T_{1}T_{3})^{1/2}\leq o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{\leq\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}.

Putting everything together we see that,

R_{1}=\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot(\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}).

B.3.2
Term
R_{2}

Turning to
R_{2}
, we take the second-moment with respect to
{\bm{\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[R_{2}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[{\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}}{\bm{f}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{\varepsilon}}]/\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{n^{2}}{\bm{f}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-4(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{f}}\leq\frac{4}{n}(\norm{\boldf}_{2}^{2}/n)

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}.

By Markov’s inequality
R_{2}=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}
.

B.3.3
Term
R_{3}

Now let us analyse
R_{3}
. Recalling the definition
{\bm{B}}={\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\Psi}}_{\leq\ell}/n
, we can compute the expectation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}(1-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[R_{3}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Tr(\id_n - e^{-2(t/n)\bH})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}e^{-\kappa_{H}t/n}\Tr(\id_n - e^{-2(t/n)(\bPsil{\cut} \bDl{\cut}^2 \bPsil{\cut}^\sT)})+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{n}e^{-\kappa_{H}t/n}\Tr(2(t/n)(\bPsil{\cut} \bDl{\cut}^2 \bPsil{\cut}^\sT))+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}e^{-\kappa_{H}t/n}\Tr(2t\bB\bDl{\cut}^2)+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2t}{n}e^{-\kappa_{H}t/n}\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}\Tr(\bDl{\cut}^2)+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1).

Equality
(a)
follows from Lemma
8
. For
(b)
we used the inequality
1-e^{-x}\leq x
and that trace is the sum of eigenvalues. Equality
(c)
uses the cyclic property of trace. In the last equality we used that by Lemma
4
,
\left\lVert{\bm{B}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}=O_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)
and Assumption
3
(c)
implies that

\frac{t}{n}\Tr(\bDl{\cut}^2)=O_{d}\quantity(\frac{t}{n} \kappa_H)=o_{d}(1),

since by assumption we consider
t=o_{d}(n/\kappa_{H})
. Turning to the variance

\displaystyle\text{Var}_{\bm{\varepsilon}}[R_{3}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[R_{3}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[R_{3}]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[({\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathsf{T}}e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}{\bm{\varepsilon}})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[R_{3}]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\varepsilon}}}[({\bm{\varepsilon}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bm{\varepsilon}})^{2}]-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}(1+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1))

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}O(1/n)\cdot\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}-\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}(1+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1))

\displaystyle=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4},

where the first inequality uses that
\left\lVert e^{-2(t/n){\bm{H}}}\right\rVert_{\rm{op}}\leq 1
and
(a)
holds because
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{i}^{4}]=3\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{4}
for
\varepsilon_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2})
. Therefore by Chebyshev’s inequality,
R_{3}=\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}(1+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1))
.

Putting everything together yields

\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})=R_{1}+R_{2}+R_{3}=\left\lVert\mathsf{P}_{>\ell}\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(1)\cdot(\left\lVert f_{d}\right\rVert_{L^{2+\eta}}^{2}+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}).

E.1

Recall that we are interested in the following dynamics given in Eqs. (
2

3

\displaystyle\derivative{t}f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{z}})(f_{d}({\bm{z}})-f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{z}}))],

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{i})(y_{i}-\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{i})),

with zero initialization
f^{\rm{or}}_{0}\equiv\hat{f}_{0}\equiv 0
. In this section we clarify the derivation, validity, and solution of these dynamics. Let us consider the maps
R:{\mathcal{H}}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\widehat{R}_{n}:{\mathcal{H}}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined in Eq. (
1
).

\displaystyle R(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{X}}_{d}}(f({\bm{x}})-f_{d}({\bm{x}}))^{2}\differential{\nu_d}({\bm{x}})+\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{R}_{n}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(f({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i})^{2}.

First, we recall the definition of the Fréchet derivative of a functional
V:{\mathcal{H}}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}
at
f
. The Fréchet derivative
DV(f)
is the linear functional such that for
g\in{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
,

\lim_{\norm{g}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{d}}\to 0}\frac{|V(f+g)-V(f)-DV(f)(g)|}{\norm{g}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{d}}}=0.

The gradient
\gradient V(f)\in{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
is defined such that

\langle\gradient V(f),g\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{d}}=DV(f)(g)

exists uniquely by the Riesz representation theorem. The gradients of the risk functionals are

\displaystyle\gradient R(f)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{H}_{d}(f-f_{d}),

\displaystyle\gradient\widehat{R}_{n}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(f({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i})H_{{\bm{x}}_{i}},

where
H_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}({\bm{x}}):=H_{d}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}})
and
\mathbb{H}_{d}
is the kernel operator as in Appendix
A.1
. A proof of this fact is given in Proposition 2.1 of
Yao et al. 2007
. Taking
f^{\rm{or}}_{t}(x)
as shorthand for
f^{\rm{or}}(t,x)
where
f^{\rm{or}}(t,\cdot)\in{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
is the oracle model at time
t
and similarly for
\hat{f}_{t}(x)
, the following gradient flows with zero initialization are well-defined for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}f^{\rm{or}}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\gradient R(f^{\rm{or}}_{t})=-\mathbb{H}_{d}(f^{\rm{or}}_{t}-f_{d})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}}[H_{d}(\cdot,{\bm{z}})(f_{d}({\bm{z}})-f^{\rm{or}}_{t}({\bm{z}})],

(41)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\gradient\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i})H_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}H_{d}(\cdot,{\bm{x}}_{i})(y_{i}-\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{i})).

(42)

The oracle model ODE Eq. (

\exp(\bA):=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}{\bm{A}}^{k}/k!

f^{\rm{or}}_{t}=f_{d}+\exp(-t \kernelop_d)(f^{\rm{or}}_{0}-f_{d})=f_{d}-\exp(-t \kernelop_d)f_{d}.

(43)

For the empirical model ODE Eq. (
42

\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i\in[n]}
. Letting
{\bm{u}}(t)=(\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}
,
{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}
, and

\derivative{t}{\bm{u}}(t)=-\frac{1}{n}{\bm{H}}({\bm{u}}(t)-{\bm{y}}),

with initial condition

. As this a linear ODE, the solution is given by

{\bm{u}}(t)={\bm{y}}+e^{-t{\bm{H}}/n}({\bm{u}}(0)-{\bm{y}})=({\mathbf{I}}_{n}-e^{-t{\bm{H}}/n}){\bm{y}}.

(44)

For
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define
{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}})=(H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
{\bm{a}}(t)={\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We will show that the function
\hat{f}_{t}(\cdot):=\langle{\bm{h}}(\cdot),{\bm{a}}(t)\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
, which satisfies
(\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}}_{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}={\bm{u}}(t)
, satisfies the following equation

\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{n}\langle{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{y}}-{\bm{u}}(t)\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\langle{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}}),e^{-t{\bm{H}}/n}{\bm{y}}\rangle,

which is Eq. (
42
) at point
{\bm{x}}
. Indeed, by the chain rule

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\derivative{t}\langle{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{a}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}}),\derivative{t}{\bm{a}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}}),{\bm{H}}^{-1}\frac{1}{n}{\bm{H}}e^{-t{\bm{H}}/n}{\bm{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\langle{\bm{h}}({\bm{x}}),e^{-t{\bm{H}}/n}{\bm{y}}\rangle,

which is what we wanted to show.

E.2
Equivalence between Invariant Kernels and Data Augmentation

In this section we will show an equivalence between the (time rescaled) gradient flows for training invariant kernels and using an augmented dataset. Specifically consider a group
{\mathcal{G}}
and a kernel
H
that is
{\mathcal{G}}
-equivariant, that is

H(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{1},g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{2})=H({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})\quad\forall g\in{\mathcal{G}},\,\forall{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}.

Given a
{\mathcal{G}}
-equivariant kernel
H
, we define a
{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant kernel
H_{\rm{inv}}
as the group averaged kernel

H_{\rm{inv}}=\int_{{\mathcal{G}}}H({\bm{x}}_{1},g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{2})\pi(\differential{g})

for the Haar measure
\pi
on
{\mathcal{G}}
(c.f. Eq. (
5
)). Note that any dot product kernel is
{\mathcal{G}}
-equivariant for
{\mathcal{G}}
a subgroup the orthogonal group e.g. the cyclic group
\Cyc
(c.f. Appendix
3.3
).

Given a dataset
({\bm{X}},{\bm{y}})=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}):i\in[n]\}
consider the augmented dataset

({\bm{X}}_{{\mathcal{G}}},{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{G}}})=\{(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}):g\in{\mathcal{G}},i\in[n]\}.

We consider the (rescaled c.f. Remark
5
) empirical dynamics Eq. (
3
) of the gradient flow on
({\bm{X}},{\bm{y}})
using
H_{\rm{inv}}
which we denote
\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}

\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}({\bm{x}})=-m\gradient\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}})=-\frac{m}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i})H_{\rm{inv}}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}})

and the empirical dynamics of the gradient flow on
({\bm{X}}_{{\mathcal{G}}},{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{G}}})
using
H
which we denote
\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}

\derivative{t}\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}({\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{g\in{\mathcal{G}}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i})H(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}).

Proposition 3
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be a finite group with
m
elements. Given a
{\mathcal{G}}
-equivariant kernel
H
, if
\pi
is the uniform measure on
{\mathcal{G}}
then

\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}\equiv\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}},~~~~\forall t\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\}
where
g_{1}
is the identity. Define the output vectors

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{\rm{inv}}(t)

\displaystyle:=(\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}({\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}({\bm{x}}_{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{g}(t)

\displaystyle:=(\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{1}),\ldots,\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{n}))^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{\rm{aug}}(t)

\displaystyle:=({\bm{u}}_{g_{1}}(t),\ldots,{\bm{u}}_{g_{m}}(t))^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}.

Furthermore, define the kernel matrices

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{g,g^{\prime}}

\displaystyle:=[H(g\cdot{\bm{x}}_{i},g^{\prime}\cdot{\bm{x}}_{j})]_{i,j\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\text{ for }g,g^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}},

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{\rm{aug}}

\displaystyle:=[{\bm{H}}_{g,g^{\prime}}]_{g,g^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{G}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times mn},

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{\rm{inv}}

\displaystyle=[H_{\rm{inv}}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})]_{i,j\in[n]}.

Note that by definition
{\bm{H}}_{\rm{inv}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}{\bm{H}}_{g_{1},g_{j}}
. We will show that

{\bm{u}}_{\rm{aug}}(t)=({\bm{u}}_{\rm{inv}}(t),{\bm{u}}_{\rm{inv}}(t),\ldots,{\bm{u}}_{\rm{inv}}(t))\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

(45)

From this the result follows by Theorem 4.1 in
Li et al. 2019
since
\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}},\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}
are given by kernel regressions with targets
{\bm{u}}_{\rm{inv}}(t),{\bm{u}}_{\rm{aug}}(t)
and kernels
H_{\rm{inv}},H
respectively.

By Eq. (
42
), we can write

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{\rm{inv}}(t)

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{I}}-\exp(-t m \bH_{\rm{inv}} /n )){\bm{y}},

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}_{\rm{aug}}(t)

\displaystyle=({\mathbf{I}}-\exp(-t \bH_{\rm{aug}} /n )){\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{G}}},

where
{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}=({\bm{y}},\ldots,{\bm{y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
. By expanding the matrix exponential series and using linearity, it suffices to show that

{\bm{H}}_{\rm{aug}}^{k}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{G}}=(m^{k}H_{\rm{inv}}^{k}{\bm{y}},m^{k}H_{\rm{inv}}^{k}{\bm{y}},\ldots,m^{k}H_{\rm{inv}}^{k}{\bm{y}})\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

in order to show Eq. (
45
) holds. We prove the above by induction on
k
. For
k=1
, observe that

\displaystyle H_{\rm{aug}}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\sum\limits_{j = 1}^m \bH_{g_i, g_j} \by)_{i=1}^{m}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\sum\limits_{j = 1}^m \bH_{g_1, g_j} \by)_{i=1}^{m}

\displaystyle=(m{\bm{H}}_{\rm{inv}}{\bm{y}})_{i=1}^{m},

where the second equality follows from
{\mathcal{G}}
-equivariance of
H
. Assume the inductive hypothesis holds for
k
. Then

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{\rm{aug}}^{k+1}{\bm{y}}_{\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle={\bm{H}}_{\rm{aug}}{\bm{H}}_{\rm{aug}}^{k}{\bm{y}}_{{\mathcal{G}}}

\displaystyle=\quantity(m^k \sum\limits_{j = 1}^m \bH_{g_i, g_j} \bH_{\rm{inv}}^k \by)_{i=1}^{m}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\sum\limits_{j = 1}^m \bH_{g_1, g_j} \qty(\sum\limits_{j' = 1}^m \bH_{g_1, g_{j'}})^k \by)_{i=1}^{m}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\qty(\sum\limits_{j = 1}^m \bH_{g_1, g_{j}})^{k+1} \by)_{i=1}^{m}

\displaystyle=(m^{k+1}{\bm{H}}_{\rm{inv}}^{k+1}{\bm{y}})_{i=1}^{m},

where the second equality applies the induction hypothesis and the third equality uses equivariance. Thus the inductive claim is proved and the proof is complete.

∎

Remark 5
.

The scaling factor
m
in the gradient flow for
\hat{f}_{t,\rm{inv}}
, leads to a natural comparison with
\hat{f}_{t,\rm{aug}}
as elaborated in Appendix
E.3
. As argued in that section, in the gradient descent discretization, it is natural to take a step-size inversely proportional to the maximum kernel eigenvalue. In the case of high-dimensional invariant kernels, note that

\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}}_{\rm{aug}})=m\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}})\sim m\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}}_{\rm{inv}}),

hence the step-size for the invariant kernel flow should be
m
times larger.

E.3
Discretizing Time

Comparing different “speeds” of optimization algorithms only makes sense for discrete-time algorithms. Consider the following gradient descent dynamics with step-size
\eta
, obtained as the discretization of the empirical gradient flow Eq. (
42
)

\hat{f}_{k+1}=\hat{f}_{k}-\eta\gradient\widehat{R}_{n}(\hat{f}_{k})=\hat{f}_{k}-\eta\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{f}_{k}({\bm{x}}_{i})-y_{i})H(\cdot,{\bm{x}}_{i}),~~~~k=0,1,\ldots

(46)

We will argue that it is natural to take
\eta\sim n/\lambda_{\max}({\bm{H}})
where
{\bm{H}}
is the kernel matrix.

Define the sampling operator
S:{\mathcal{H}}_{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
S(f)=(f({\bm{x}}_{i}))_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
S^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
be its adjoint, defined by
S^{*}({\bm{y}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}H_{{\bm{x}}_{i}}
(see
Yao et al. 2007
Appendix B for more details). Then we can rewrite the gradient descent equation Eq. (
46
) as

\hat{f}_{k+1}=\hat{f}_{k}-\eta(S^{*}S\hat{f}_{t}-S^{*}{\bm{y}}),~~~~k=0,1,\ldots

(47)

Let
T=S^{*}S
and define
\overline{{\bm{H}}}:=SS^{*}=\frac{1}{n}{\bm{H}}
to be the normalized kernel matrix. Let
b:=S^{*}\overline{{\bm{H}}}^{-1}{\bm{y}}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
and note that since
Tb=S^{*}{\bm{y}}
, we can rewrite Eq. (
47
) as

\hat{f}_{k+1}=\hat{f}_{k}-\eta T(\hat{f}_{k}-b),~~~~k=0,1,\ldots

(48)

Denote the eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bm{H}}}
as
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{n}>0
. By the spectral theorem, there exists a set of orthornomal eigenvectors
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
such that
T=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\phi_{i}\phi_{i}^{*}
. Define
\alpha_{i}(k)=\langle\hat{f}_{k}-b,\phi_{i}\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
. By taking Eq. (
48
) then subtracting
b
and taking the inner product with
\phi_{i}
on both sides, we get the coordinate evolution equations for
i=1,\ldots,n

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}(k+1)

\displaystyle=\langle(\text{id}-\eta T)(\hat{f}_{k}-b),\phi_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\hat{f}_{k}-b),(\text{id}-\eta T)\phi_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\eta\lambda_{i})\alpha_{i}(k),

where the second equality holds since
T
is self-adjoint and the last equality is since
\phi_{i}
is an eigenvector of
T
. It is easy to see that
\alpha_{i}(k)=(1-\eta\lambda_{i})^{k}\alpha_{i}(0)
. Therefore we see that gradient descent Eq. (
46
) is guaranteed to converge if
\eta<1/(2\lambda_{1})
and may not otherwise. Therefore it is natural to choose the step-size
\eta
to scale asymptotically as
\eta\sim 1/\lambda_{\max}(\overline{{\bm{H}}})
.

For a dot product kernel
H
and its corresponding invariant kernel
H_{\rm{inv}}
the kernel matrices have operator norms of the same order

\lambda_{\max}({{\bm{H}}})\sim\lambda_{\max}({{\bm{H}}_{\rm{inv}}}),

hence no time rescaling is need to compare the corresponding optimization speeds asymptotically.

Appendix G
Technical background

G.1
Notations

For a positive integer, we denote by
[n]
the set
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. For vectors
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we denote
\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle=u_{1}v_{1}+\ldots+u_{d}v_{d}
their scalar product, and
\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}=\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{u}}\rangle^{1/2}
the
\ell_{2}
norm. Given a matrix
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, we denote
\|{\bm{A}}\|_{{\rm op}}=\max_{\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}=1}\|{\bm{A}}{\bm{u}}\|_{2}
its operator norm and by
\|{\bm{A}}\|_{F}=\big(\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}^{2}\big)^{1/2}
its Frobenius norm. If
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a square matrix, the trace of
{\bm{A}}
is denoted by
\Tr(\bA)=\sum_{i\in[n]}A_{ii}
.

We use
O_{d}(\,\cdot\,)
(resp.
o_{d}(\,\cdot\,)
) for the standard big-O (resp. little-o) relations, where the subscript
d
emphasizes the asymptotic variable. Furthermore, we write
f=\Omega_{d}(g)
if
g(d)=O_{d}(f(d))
, and
f=\omega_{d}(g)
if
g(d)=o_{d}(f(d))
. Finally,
f=\Theta_{d}(g)
if we have both
f=O_{d}(g)
and
f=\Omega_{d}(g)
.

We use
O_{d,\mathbb{P}}(\,\cdot\,)
(resp.
o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(\,\cdot\,)
) the big-O (resp. little-o) in probability relations. Namely, for
h_{1}(d)
and
h_{2}(d)
two sequences of random variables,
h_{1}(d)=O_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
if for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
and
d_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|h_{1}(d)/h_{2}(d)|>C_{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon,\qquad\forall d\geq d_{\varepsilon},

and respectively:
h_{1}(d)=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
, if
h_{1}(d)/h_{2}(d)
converges to
0
in probability. Similarly, we will denote
h_{1}(d)=\Omega_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
if
h_{2}(d)=O_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{1}(d))
, and
h_{1}(d)=\omega_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
if
h_{2}(d)=o_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{1}(d))
. Finally,
h_{1}(d)=\Theta_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
if we have both
h_{1}(d)=O_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
and
h_{1}(d)=\Omega_{d,\mathbb{P}}(h_{2}(d))
.

G.2
Functional spaces over the sphere

For
d\geq 3
, we let
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(r)=\{{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|{\bm{x}}\|_{2}=r\}
denote the sphere with radius
r
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
We will mostly work with the sphere of radius
\sqrt{d}
,
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
and will denote by
\tau_{d}
the uniform probability measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
.
All functions in this section are assumed to be elements of
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),\tau_{d})
, with scalar product and norm denoted as
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{L^{2}}

and
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{L^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{L^{2}}\equiv\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})}f({\bm{x}})\,g({\bm{x}})\,\tau_{d}({\rm d}{\bm{x}})\,.

(49)

For
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, let
\tilde{V}_{d,\ell}
be the space of homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
\ell
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(i.e. homogeneous
polynomials
q({\bm{x}})
satisfying
\Delta q({\bm{x}})=0
), and denote by
V_{d,\ell}
the linear space of functions obtained by restricting the polynomials in
\tilde{V}_{d,\ell}

to
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
. With these definitions, we have the following orthogonal decomposition

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),\tau_{d})=\bigoplus_{\ell=0}^{\infty}V_{d,\ell}\,.

(50)

The dimension of each subspace is given by

\displaystyle\dim(V_{d,\ell})=B(d,\ell)=\frac{2\ell+d-2}{d-2}\binom{\ell+d-3}{\ell}\,.

(51)

For each
\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, the spherical harmonics
\{Y_{\ell,j}^{(d)}\}_{1\leq j\leq B(d,\ell)}
form an orthonormal basis of
V_{d,\ell}
:

\langle Y^{(d)}_{ki},Y^{(d)}_{sj}\rangle_{L^{2}}=\delta_{ij}\delta_{ks}.

Note that our convention is different from the more standard one, that defines the spherical harmonics as functions on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(1)
.
It is immediate to pass from one convention to the other by a simple scaling. We will drop the superscript
d
and write
Y_{\ell,j}=Y_{\ell,j}^{(d)}
whenever clear from the context.

We denote by
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{k}
the orthogonal projections to
V_{d,k}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),\tau_{d})
. This can be written in terms of spherical harmonics as

\displaystyle{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{k}f({\bm{x}})\equiv

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{B(d,k)}\langle f,Y_{kl}\rangle_{L^{2}}Y_{kl}({\bm{x}}).

(52)

We also define

{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{k}
,
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{>\ell}\equiv{\mathbf{I}}-{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell}=\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{\infty}{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{k}
,
and
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{<\ell}\equiv{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell-1}
,
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\geq\ell}\equiv{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{>\ell-1}
.

G.3
Gegenbauer polynomials

The
\ell
-th Gegenbauer polynomial
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}
is a polynomial of degree
\ell
. Consistently
with our convention for spherical harmonics, we view
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}
as a function
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}:[-d,d]\to{\mathbb{R}}
. The set
\{Q_{\ell}^{(d)}\}_{\ell\geq 0}

forms an orthogonal basis on
L^{2}([-d,d],\tilde{\tau}^{1}_{d})
, where
\tilde{\tau}^{1}_{d}
is the distribution of
\sqrt{d}\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{e}}_{1}\rangle
when
{\bm{x}}\sim\tau_{d}
,
satisfying the normalization condition:

\displaystyle\langle Q^{(d)}_{k}(\sqrt{d}\langle{\bm{e}}_{1},\cdot\rangle),Q^{(d)}_{j}(\sqrt{d}\langle{\bm{e}}_{1},\cdot\rangle)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))}=\frac{1}{B(d,k)}\,\delta_{jk}\,.

(53)

In particular, these polynomials are normalized so that
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(d)=1
.
As above, we will omit the superscript
(d)
in
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}
when clear from the context.

Gegenbauer polynomials are directly related to spherical harmonics as follows. Fix some vector
{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
and
consider the subspace of
V_{\ell}
formed by all functions that are invariant under rotations in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that keep
{\bm{v}}
unchanged.
It is not hard to see that this subspace has dimension one, and coincides with the span of the function
Q_{\ell}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{v}},\,\cdot\,\rangle)
.

We will use the following properties of Gegenbauer polynomials

1.

For
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})

\displaystyle\langle Q_{j}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{x}},\cdot\rangle),Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{y}},\cdot\rangle)\rangle_{L^{2}}=\frac{1}{B(d,k)}\delta_{jk}Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle).

(54)

2.

For
{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})

\displaystyle Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)=\frac{1}{B(d,k)}\sum_{i=1}^{B(d,k)}Y_{ki}^{(d)}({\bm{x}})Y_{ki}^{(d)}({\bm{y}}).

(55)

These properties imply that, up to a constant,
Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)
is a representation of the projector onto
the subspace of degree-
k
spherical harmonics

\displaystyle({\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{k}f)({\bm{x}})=B(d,k)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})}\,Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)\,f({\bm{y}})\,\tau_{d}({\rm d}{\bm{y}})\,.

(56)

For a function
\sigma\in L^{2}([-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}],\tau^{1}_{d})
(where
\tau^{1}_{d}
is the distribution of
\langle{\bm{e}}_{1},{\bm{x}}\rangle
when
{\bm{x}}\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
), denoting its spherical harmonics coefficients
\xi_{d,k}(\sigma)
to be

\displaystyle\xi_{d,k}(\sigma)=\int_{[-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}]}\sigma(x)Q_{k}^{(d)}(\sqrt{d}x)\tau^{1}_{d}({\rm d}x),

(57)

then we have the following equation holds in
L^{2}([-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}],\tau^{1}_{d})
sense

\sigma(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{d,k}(\sigma)B(d,k)Q_{k}^{(d)}(\sqrt{d}x).

For any dot product kernel
H_{d}({\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2})=h_{d}(\langle{\bm{x}}_{1},{\bm{x}}_{2}\rangle/d)
,
with
h_{d}(\sqrt{d}\,\cdot\,)\in L^{2}([-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}],\tau^{1}_{d})
,
we can associate a self adjoint operator
{\mathcal{H}}_{d}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}_{d}f({\bm{x}})\equiv\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})}h_{d}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{1}\rangle/d)\,f({\bm{x}}_{1})\,\tau_{d}({\rm d}{\bm{x}}_{1})\,.

(58)

By rotational invariance, the space
V_{k}
of homogeneous polynomials of degree
k
is an eigenspace of

{\mathcal{H}}_{d}
, and we will denote the corresponding eigenvalue by
\xi_{d,k}(h_{d})
. In other words

{\mathcal{H}}_{d}f({\bm{x}})\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{d,k}(h_{d}){\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{k}f
. The eigenvalues can be computed via

\displaystyle\xi_{d,k}(h_{d})=\int_{[-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}]}h_{d}\big(x/\sqrt{d}\big)Q_{k}^{(d)}(\sqrt{d}x)\tau^{1}_{d}({\rm d}x)\,.

(59)

For a dot product kernel
H_{d}({\bm{x}},{\bm{y}})=h_{d}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/d)
consider the Gegenbauer expansion of
h_{d}
in
L^{2}([-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}],\tau_{d}^{1})

h_{d}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/d)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{k,d}(h_{d})B(d,k)Q_{k}^{(d)}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle).

(60)

Using Eq. (
54
) we can equivalently write the kernel as an expectation over random features for some activation
\sigma_{d}

h_{d}(\langle{\bm{x}},{\bm{y}}\rangle/d)=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{w}}\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}[\sigma_{d}(\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{x}}\rangle)\sigma_{d}(\langle{\bm{w}},{\bm{y}}\rangle)]

(61)

by taking

\sigma_{d}(x)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\xi_{d,k}(h_{d})^{1/2}B(d,k)Q_{k}^{(d)}(\sqrt{d}x).

(62)

Note that
\sigma_{d}\in L^{2}([-\sqrt{d},\sqrt{d}],\tau_{d}^{1})
as long as
h(1)<\infty
.

G.4
Hermite polynomials

The Hermite polynomials
\{{\rm He}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
form an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\gamma)
, where

\gamma({\rm d}x)=e^{-x^{2}/2}{\rm d}x/\sqrt{2\pi}

is the standard Gaussian measure, and
{\rm He}_{k}
has degree
k
. We will follow the classical normalization (here and below, expectation is with respect to

G\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big\{{\rm He}_{j}(G)\,{\rm He}_{k}(G)\big\}=k!\,\delta_{jk}\,.

(63)

As a consequence, for any function
g\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}},\gamma)
, we have the decomposition

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}(g)}{k!}\,{\rm He}_{k}(x)\,,\;\;\;\;\;\;\mu_{k}(g)\equiv\mathbb{E}\big\{g(G)\,{\rm He}_{k}(G)\}\,.

(64)

The Hermite polynomials can be obtained as high-dimensional limits of the Gegenbauer polynomials introduced in the previous section. Indeed, the Gegenbauer polynomials (up to a
\sqrt{d}
scaling in domain) are constructed by Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization of the monomials
\{x^{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
with respect to the measure

\tilde{\tau}^{1}_{d}
, while Hermite polynomial are obtained by Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization with respect to
\gamma
. Since
\tilde{\tau}^{1}_{d}\Rightarrow\gamma

(here
\Rightarrow
denotes weak convergence),
it is immediate to show that, for any fixed integer
k
,

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}{\rm Coeff}\{Q_{k}^{(d)}(\sqrt{d}x)\,B(d,k)^{1/2}\}={\rm Coeff}\left\{\frac{1}{(k!)^{1/2}}\,{\rm He}_{k}(x)\right\}\,.

(65)

Here and below, for
P
a polynomial,
{\rm Coeff}\{P(x)\}
is the vector of the coefficients of
P
. As a consequence,
for any fixed integer
k
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(\sigma)=\lim_{d\to\infty}\xi_{d,k}(\sigma)(B(d,k)k!)^{1/2},

(66)

where
\mu_{k}(\sigma)
and
\xi_{d,k}(\sigma)
are given in Eq. (
64
) and Eq. (
57
).

G.5
The invariant function class and the symmetrization operator

Let
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
be a group that is isomorphic to a subgroup of
{\mathcal{O}}(d)
, the orthogonal group in
d
dimension. That means, each element of
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
can be identified with a matrix in
{\mathcal{O}}(d)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and the group addition operation in
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
can be regarded as matrix multiplications in
{\mathcal{O}}(d)
. For any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
and
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
, we define group action
g\cdot{\bm{x}}
to be the multiplication of matrix representation of
g
with the vector
{\bm{x}}
. We equip
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
with a probability measure
\pi_{d}
, which is the uniform probability measure on
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
. More specifically, the Borel sigma algebra on
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
is defined as the Borel sigma algebra of
{\mathcal{O}}(d)
restricted on
{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
. The uniform probability measure
\pi_{d}
satisfies the property that, for any Borel-measurable set
B\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
and any
g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}
, we have

\pi_{d}(B)=\pi_{d}(gB).

Let
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
be the class of
L^{2}
functions on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})
equipped with uniform probability measure
{\rm Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
. We define the invariant function class to be

L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})=\Big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d})):f({\bm{x}})=f(g\cdot{\bm{x}}),~~\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),~~\forall g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}\Big\}.

We define the symmetrization operator
{\mathcal{S}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})
to be

({\mathcal{S}}f)({\bm{x}})=\int_{{\mathcal{G}}_{d}}f(g\cdot{\bm{x}})\pi_{d}({\rm d}g).

G.6
Orthogonal polynomials on invariant function class

We define
V_{d,\leq k}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
to be the subspace spanned by all the degree
\ell
polynomials,
V_{d,>k}\equiv V_{d,\leq k}^{\perp}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
to be the orthogonal complement of
V_{d,\leq k}
, and
V_{d,k}=V_{d,\leq k}\cap V_{d,\leq k-1}^{\perp}
. In words,
V_{d,k}
contains all degree
k
polynomials that orthogonal to all polynomials of degree at most
k-1
. We further define
V_{d,<k}=V_{d,\leq k-1}
and
V_{d,\geq k}=V_{d,>k-1}
.

Let
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell}
to be the projection operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\rm Unif})
that project a function onto
V_{d,\leq\ell}
, the space spanned by all the degree
\ell
polynomials. Then it is easy to see that
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell}
and
{\mathcal{S}}
operator commute. This means, for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))
, we have

{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell}[{\mathcal{S}}(f)]={\mathcal{S}}[{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\leq\ell}(f)].

Similarly, we can define
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\ell}
,
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{<\ell}
,
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{>\ell}
,
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\geq\ell}
, which commute with
{\mathcal{S}}
. We denote
V_{d,\ell}({\mathcal{G}}_{d})\equiv{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})
to be the space of polynomials in the images of
{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\ell}{\mathcal{S}}
. Then we have

{\mathcal{P}}_{\ell}({\mathcal{A}}_{d},{\mathcal{G}}_{d})={\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\ell}(L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d}))={\mathcal{S}}[{\overline{\mathsf{P}}}_{\ell}(L^{2}({\mathcal{A}}_{d}))].

We denote
D(d,k)\equiv\dim({\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d}))
to be the dimension of
{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})
. We denote
\{\overline{Y}_{kl}^{(d)}\}_{l\in[D(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d});k)]}
to be a set of orthonormal polynomial basis in
{\mathcal{P}}_{k}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),{\mathcal{G}}_{d})
. That means

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}\sim{\rm Unif}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}))}[\overline{Y}_{k_{1}l_{1}}^{(d)}({\bm{x}})\overline{Y}_{k_{2}l_{2}}^{(d)}({\bm{x}})]=\bm{1}\{k_{1}=k_{2},l_{1}=l_{2}\},

and

\overline{Y}_{kl}^{(d)}({\bm{x}})=\overline{Y}_{kl}^{(d)}(g\cdot{\bm{x}}),~~\forall{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}(\sqrt{d}),~~\forall g\in{\mathcal{G}}_{d}.
