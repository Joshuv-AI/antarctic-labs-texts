Expected nodal volume for non-Gaussian random band-limited functions
Zakhar Kabluchko, Andrea Sartori, Igor Wigman

Abstract
. The asymptotic law for the expected nodal volume of random non-Gaussian monochromatic band-limited functions is determined in vast generality. Our methods combine microlocal analytic techniques and modern probability theory. A particularly challenging obstacle needed to overcome is the possible concentration of nodal volume on a small proportion of the manifold, requiring solutions in both disciplines. As for the fine aspects of the distribution of nodal volume, such as its variance, it is expected that the non-Gaussian monochromatic functions behave qualitatively differently compared to their Gaussian counterpart, with some conjectures been put forward.

In the recent years a lot of effort has been put into understanding

2.
Discussion

2.1.
Survey of non-Gaussian literature

To our best knowledge, Theorem
1.1
is the first universality result applicable in the asserted vastly general scenario, in terms of both the underlying manifold
M
and the random coefficients
\{a_{i}\}
. Our approach is based on a blend of microlocal analytic techniques,
missing from the existing non-Gaussian literature, and purely probabilistic methods. The closest analogue to Theorem
1.1
we are aware of in the existing literature is
[
1
]
, dealing with
2
d random non-Gaussian trigonometric polynomials: these are related to the
random band-limited Laplace eigenfunctions on the standard
2
d torus, corresponding to the long energy window
\rho(T)=T
(here, the energies ordering
is somewhat different, to allow for separation of variables). The asymptotics for the expected nodal length was asserted for centred unit variance random variables, in perfect harmony to (
1.6
) (though with a different leading constant, a by-product of a non-monochromatic scaling limit).

Even though we didn’t meticulously validate all the details, we believe that their arguments translate verbatim for the “pure”
2
d toral Laplace eigenfunctions

(2.1)

g_{n}(x)=\sum\limits_{\underset{\|\mu\|^{2}=n}{\mu\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}}a_{\mu}\cdot e(\langle\mu,x\rangle),

where the
a_{\mu}
are i.i.d., save for the relation
a_{-\mu}=\overline{a_{\mu}}
making
g_{n}
real-valued, and the summation on the r.h.s. of (
2.1
) is w.r.t. to all standard lattice points lying on the radius-
\sqrt{n}
centred circle. In the Gaussian context
the
g_{n}
are usually referred to as “arithmetic random waves” (ARW), see e.g.
[
24
,
43
,
47
]
; they are the
band-limited functions for the standard flat torus corresponding to the “short energy window”
\rho(T)=1

(in fact, in this case, the energy window width could be made infinitesimal).
Other than the result for
2
d random trigonometric polynomials all the literature concerning real zeros of non-Gaussian ensembles is
1
-dimensional in essence: real zeros of random algebraic polynomials or Taylor series, see e.g.
[
22
,
23
,
42
]
and the references therein,
random trigonometric polynomials on the circle
[
3
]
,
and the restrictions of
2
d random toral Laplace eigenfunctions (
2.1
) to a smooth curve
[
14
]
.

2.2.
Gaussian vs. non-Gaussian monochromatic functions: cases of study

Unlike the non-Gaussian state of art concerning the zeros of the band-limited functions, the Gaussian literature is vast and rapidly expanding, thanks to the powerful Kac-Rice method tailored to this case, at times, combined with the Wiener chaos expansion. Here the literature varies
from the very precise and detailed results concerning the zero volume distribution (its expectation, variance and limit law),
restricted to some particularly important ensembles, such as random spherical harmonics
[
35
,
55
]
or
the arithmetic random waves
[
24
,
34
]
, to somewhat less detailed results, but of far more general nature
[
13
,
59
]
,
to almost sure asymptotic result
[
19
]
w.r.t. a randomly independently drawn sequence of functions
\{f_{T}\}_{T}
.

It is plausible, if not likely, that, under a slightly more restrictive assumptions on the random variables, our techniques yield a power saving upper bound for the nodal length variance of the type

\Var\left(\frac{f_{T}}{T}\right)=O\left(T^{-\delta}\right)

for some

, but certainly not a
precise
asymptotic law for the variance, even for the particular cases of non-Gaussian random spherical harmonics or the non-Gaussian Arithmetic Random Waves. In the Gaussian case even some important
non-local
properties of the nodal set were addressed: the expected number of nodal components
[
38
,
39
]
, their fluctuations
[
16
,
40
]
, their fine topology and geometry, and their relative position
[
4
,
50
]
.

The aforementioned random ensemble of Gaussian spherical harmonics is the sequence of functions
f_{\ell}:\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\ell\geq 1
, where

(2.2)

f_{\ell}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\ell+1}}\sum\limits_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell,m}Y_{\ell,m}(x),

with

whereas a significantly heavier machinery, also appealing to the Kac-Rice method, yields
[
55
]
a precise asymptotic law

\Var(\mathcal{V}(f_{\ell}))\underset{\ell\rightarrow\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{32}\log{\ell},

smaller than what would have been thought the natural scaling

would be (“Berry’s cancellation phenomenon”).

For the non-Gaussian random spherical harmonics, Theorem
1.1
is not directly applicable, because of the extra condition

\rho(T)\geq\frac{T}{\log{T}}

However, in Appendix

In light of the non-universality result of
[

, it is

that in the non-Gaussian case (i.e. the

are centred unit variance i.i.d. random variable), the variance satisfies the

with

It seems less likely, though

For the
2

2.1
), it was found that the expected nodal length is given precisely by
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(g_{n})]=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\sqrt{n}

where

Using the same reasoning as for the spherical harmonics, for the non-Gaussian case (i.e.

are centred unit variance i.i.d. random variables), it is

that the

\Var(\mathcal{V}(g_{n}))\sim\widetilde{c_{1}}\frac{n}{r_{2}(n)}+\widetilde{c_{2}}\frac{n}{r_{2}(n)^{2}}

holds with

possibly depending on both the law of

and the angular distribution of the lattice points

in (

),
with
\widetilde{c_{1}}
vanishing for

a peculiar class of distribution laws, including the Gaussian (whence
\widetilde{c_{1}}
vanishes independent of the angular distribution of the lattice points
\{\mu\}
). The dependence of
\widetilde{c_{1}}
and
\widetilde{c_{2}}
on both the distribution law of
a_{\mu}
and the angular distribution of
\{\mu\}
is of interest, in particular, whether the vanishing of
\widetilde{c_{1}}
depends on the angular distribution of

at all (which is not the case if

2.3.
Self-focal points

An earlier version of this manuscript contained a version of Theorem
1.1
, applicable under a seemingly somewhat restrictive
(though very mild) assumption on
M
, rather than its mere analyticity, concerning its so-called

is redundant, as explained below, after a few necessary definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(M,g)
be a smooth compact manifold without boundaries,
S^{*}M
the cotangent sphere bundle on
M
,
and
G^{t}:S^{*}M\rightarrow S^{*}M

.

(1)

The set of loop directions based at

is

\mathcal{L}_{x}=\{\xi\in S^{*}_{x}M:\>\exists t>0.\,\exp_{x}(t\xi)=x\}.

(2)

The set of closed geodesics based at
x
is

\mathcal{CL}_{x}=\{\xi\in S^{*}_{x}M:\>\exists t>0.\,G^{t}(x,\xi)=(x,\xi)\}.

(3)

A point

is

,
if
|\mathcal{L}_{x}|>0
, where

is the natural measure on

induced by
the metric

.

(4)

The geodesic flow on
M
is

, if the set of its closed geodesics is of full Liouville measure
in

. The geodesic flow on
M
is

if the set of its periodic closed geodesics is of Liouville measure

.

We observe that for

0

(see either
[

or Lemma

Originally, Theorem

assumed that if

. However, it was demonstrated
[

that if

4.
Asymptotic Gaussianity

Before stating the main result of this section, we need to introduce some notation. We denote

to be the monochromatic isotropic Gaussian field on

. In what follows we will use the shorthands

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(F_{x}):=\mathcal{V}\left(F_{x},\frac{1}{2}B_{0}\right)\quad\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu}):=\mathcal{V}\left(F_{\mu},\frac{1}{2}B_{0}\right).

The aim of this section is to prove the following proposition:

.

Let

be as in (

be as above. Under the assumptions of Theorem

, there exists a subset

such that, uniformly for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(F_{x})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})

\displaystyle T\rightarrow\infty,

the convergence in distribution.

To ease the exposition, we split the proof of Proposition

into a series of steps: first we prove that

, as in (

), converges to

in distribution in the appropriate space of functions, then we deduce Proposition
4.1
using a stability notion for the nodal set introduced in
[

. Before beginning the proof, we need the following consequence of Sogge’s bound (

4.1.

In this section we prove the following consequence of (

To state the next result, given a Laplace eigenfunction

Lemma 4.3

Proof of Lemma

.

First, we observe that, given

4.3.
Tightness

A sequence of probability measures

on some topological space

\epsilon>0

such that

\nu_{n}(X\backslash K)\leq\epsilon,

[

(1)

For any

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(g\in C^{2}(V):|D^{\alpha}g(y)|>M)\leq\varepsilon.

(2)

For any
|\alpha|\leq 2
and

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\nu_{n}\left(g\in C^{2}(V):\sup_{|y-y^{\prime}|\leq\delta}|D^{\alpha}g(y)-D^{\alpha}g(y^{\prime})|>\varepsilon\right)=0.

The aim of this section is to show that we can bound

on

25

It will be useful to also introduce an auxiliary function

\displaystyle(\Delta+\partial_{t}^{2})f^{H}(x,t)=0\hskip 28.45274ptf^{H}(x,0)=0\hskip 28.45274pt\partial_{t}f^{H}(x,0)=f.

Note that we can write explicitly

\displaystyle f^{H}(x,t)=v^{-1/2}(T)\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in[T-\rho(T),T]}a_{i}\frac{\sinh(\lambda_{i}t)}{\lambda_{i}}\phi_{i}(x),

Our main tool to prove Proposition

and

Lemma 5.2

Let

To ease the exposition, we split the proof of Proposition

6.
Anti-concentration

The aim of this section is to show that
\mathcal{V}(F_{x}(\cdot,\omega))
is uniformly integrable in
\omega\in\Omega
, that is we prove the following Proposition:

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
F_{x}
be as in (
3.3
),

be as in (
1.3
), and
g(t)=t\log t
. Suppose that

, then there exists constants
C>0
,
\kappa=\kappa(n)>0
, independent of

, and a subset
\tilde{A}\subset M
of volume at most

, such that

for all

For the sake of clarity, we divide the proof of Proposition

, into lemmas: we first estimate the probability that
F_{x}
attains small values, and then show how this can be used to bound the nodal volume.

6.1.
Small values of

To estimate the probability of occurrence of small of
f
, we need the following lemma of Halasz, see for example
[
20
]
and
[
41

.

Lemma 6.2

(Halasz’ inequality)
.

Let
X
be a real-valued random variable, and let
\psi(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(itX)]
be its characteristic function. Then there exists some absolute constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|X|\leq 1)\leq C\int_{|t|\leq 1}|\psi(t)|dt.

We then prove the following Lemma:

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
f
be as in (
1.2
) and
v=v(T)=c_{M}\rho(T)T^{n-1}
as in (
1.3
). Then, uniformly for
x\in M
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|f(x)|\leq\tau\right)\lesssim\tau+v(T)^{-1/2}

where the constant implied in the
\lesssim
-notation is absolute.

Proof.

By Lemma
6.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|f(x)|/\tau\leq 1)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{|t|\leq 1}|\mathbb{E}[\exp(it|f(x)|/\tau)]|dt

\displaystyle=C\tau\int_{|t|\leq 1/\tau}|\mathbb{E}[\exp(it|f(x)|)]|dt

(6.1)

\displaystyle=C\tau\int_{|t|\leq 1/\tau}\left|\prod_{\lambda_{i}}\psi_{i}(t)\right|dt

where
\psi_{i}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\exp(ita_{i}\phi_{i}(x)/v^{1/2})]
and
C
is as in Lemma
6.2
.

Now, let
K>1
be some parameter to be chosen later, and observe that, in light of Lemma
3.2
, we have

v^{-1}\sum_{\lambda_{i}}|\phi_{i}(x)|^{2}\lesssim 2,

thus there are at most
O(v/K^{2})

\lambda_{i}
’s such that
|\phi_{i}(x)|>K
. We claim that, if
|\phi_{i}(x)|\leq K
, there exists some
c>0
such that

(6.2)

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{i}(t)\right|\leq e^{-t^{2}v^{-1}}

for all
t\in[-cv^{1/2}/K,cv^{1/2}/K]
. Indeed, if
|\phi_{i}(x)|\leq K
, then
a_{i}\phi_{i}(x)v^{-1/2}
is a random variable with mean zero and second moment bounded by
K^{2}/v
. Thus, using the fact that
|\exp(iy)-(1+iy-2^{-1}y^{2})|\leq\min(6^{-1}y^{3},y^{2})
, we can write

(6.3)

\displaystyle\left|\psi_{i}(t)-\left(1-\frac{K^{2}}{2v}t^{2}\right)\right|\leq\frac{K^{2}}{v}t^{2}

\displaystyle\left|e^{-t^{2}v^{-1}}-\left(1-\frac{K^{2}}{2v}t^{2}\right)\right|\leq\frac{K^{2}}{v}t^{2},

provided
t\in[-cv^{1/2}/K,cv^{1/2}/K]
for some sufficiently small
c>0
. Therefore, (
6.2
) follows from (
6.3
), choosing
c>0
appropriately.

Using the claim (
6.2
), and since there are at least
v\left(1-O\left(K^{-2}\right)\right)
values of

such that
|\phi_{i}(x)|\leq K
, for all
t\in[-cv^{1/2}/K,cv^{1/2}/K]
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{|\phi_{i}(x)|\leq K}\psi_{i}(t)\right|\lesssim\exp\left(-t^{2}\left(1-O(K^{-2})\right)\right).

Thus, using the trivial bound
|\psi_{i}(t)|\leq 1
if
|\phi_{i}(x)|\geq K
, and picking

, for all

\left|\prod_{i}\psi_{i}(t)\right|\lesssim\exp\left(-c_{2}t^{2}\right),

for some absolute constant

Hence, if

, that is

) is bounded, thus

If

\mathbb{P}(|f(x)|\leq\tau)\leq\mathbb{P}(|f(x)|\leq c_{1}^{-1}v^{-1/2})\lesssim v^{-1/2}.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
6.3
.
∎

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[g(\mathcal{V}(F_{x}))]\lesssim\int_{1}^{h(T)}g^{\prime}(t)\frac{1}{tK(t)}dt+\int_{1}^{h(T)}g^{\prime}(t)\frac{tK(t)}{e^{2t}}dt+O(1),

provided
h(T)\leq v^{1/2}(T)/K(h(T))
. Taking
K(t)=\log(t)^{3}
, we have
K(h(T))\gtrsim(\log T)^{3}
, thus, using (
1.3
) and the assumption
v(T)\geq T^{2}/\log T
, we have

h(T)\lesssim\frac{T^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}{(\log T)^{3}}\leq v(T)^{1/2}/K(h(T)).

Hence, Proposition
6.1
follow from (
6.10
).
∎

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Before concluding the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need two standard lemmas.

7.1.
Preliminary lemmas

For the reader’s convenience, we include the proofs of the following two lemmas.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
F_{x}
be as in (
3.3
) and
\omega_{n}
be the volume of the ball of radius
1
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(f)=\frac{2^{n}T}{\omega_{n}}\left(1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1)\right)\int_{M}\mathcal{V}(F_{x})dV_{g}(x).

Proof.

First, we observe that we may write

(7.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}\left(f,B_{g}\left(x,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)=\int_{f^{-1}(0)}\mathds{1}_{y\in B_{g}(x,1/(2T))}d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Then, integrating both sides of (
7.1
) and using Fubini’s Theorem, we have

(7.2)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\mathcal{V}\left(f,B_{g}\left(x,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)dV_{g}(x)=\int_{f^{-1}(0)}\vol_{g}\left(B_{g}\left(y,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y).

Now, we observe that, in light of the definition of
F_{x}
in section
4
, we have

\mathcal{V}\left(f,B_{g}\left(x,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)\cdot T^{n-1}(1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1))=\mathcal{V}(F_{x}).

Thus, the LHS of (
7.2
) is

(7.3)

\displaystyle\int_{M}\mathcal{V}\left(f,B_{g}\left(x,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)dV_{g}(x)=\frac{1}{T^{n-1}}\left(1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1)\right)\int_{M}\mathcal{V}(F_{x})dx

Moreover, for all
y\in M
, we also have

\vol_{g}\left(B_{g}\left(y,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)=\vol_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(B\left(0,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)\left(1+O\left(T^{-1}\right)\right)=\frac{\omega_{n}}{(2T)^{n}}(1+O(T^{-1})).

Thus, the r.h.s. of (
7.2
) is

(7.4)

\displaystyle\int_{f^{-1}(0)}\vol_{g}\left(B_{g}\left(y,\frac{1}{2T}\right)\right)d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(y)=\frac{\omega_{n}}{(2T)^{n}}(1+O(T^{-1}))\mathcal{V}(f)

Hence, Lemma
7.1
follows from inserting (
7.3
) and (
7.4
) into (
7.2
).
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
F_{\mu}
be as in section
4
, and
\omega_{n}
be the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})]=2^{-n}\omega_{n}\left(\frac{4\pi}{n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}.

Proof.

Since the support of
\mu
, being the unit sphere, is not contained in an hyperplane, the distribution of
(F_{\mu},\nabla F_{\mu})
is non-degenerate. Thus, we may apply the Kac-Rice formula
[
2
, Theorem 6.1]
, to see that

(7.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})]=\int_{2^{-1}B_{0}}\mathbb{E}[|\nabla F_{\mu}(y)||F_{\mu}(y)=0]\varphi_{F_{\mu}(y)}(0)dy,

where
\varphi_{F_{\mu}(y)}(0)
is the density of
F_{\mu}(y)
at the point
0
. Since
\mathbb{E}[|F_{\mu}(y)|^{2}]=1
,
\nabla F_{\mu}
and
F_{\mu}
are independent, and bearing in mind that
F_{\mu}
is stationary (that is,
F_{\mu}(y)
has the same distribution as
F_{\mu}(0)
), we have

(7.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\nabla F_{\mu}(y)||F_{\mu}(y)=0]\varphi_{F_{\mu}(y)}(0)=\mathbb{E}[\nabla F_{\mu}(0)]\varphi_{F_{\mu}(0)}(0).

The latter can be computed explicitly, see for example
[
47
, Proposition 4.1]
, to be

(7.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\nabla F_{\mu}(0)|]\varphi_{F_{\mu}(0)}(0)=\left(\frac{4\pi}{n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}.

Hence, Lemma
7.2
follows by inserting (
7.7
) into (
7.5
) via (
7.6
).
∎

7.2.
Concluding the proof of Theorem
1.1

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Thanks to Lemma
7.1
and Fubini’s Theorem, we have

(7.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(f)]=\frac{2^{n}T}{\omega_{n}}\left(1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1))\right)\int_{M}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{x})]dV_{g}(x)

Thanks to Propositions
4.1
, Proposition
6.1
, whose assumptions are satisfied thanks to the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
, and the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we have

(7.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{x})]=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})](1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1)),

uniformly for all
x\in M
outside a subset
A\subset M
of volume at most
O(\log T/T)
.
Moreover, thanks to Corollary
5.8
, we have

\mathcal{V}(F_{x})\lesssim T2^{-\log T/\log\log T},

uniformly for all
x
outside a subset
\tilde{A}\subset M
of volume at most
O(T^{-1-\kappa})
with
\kappa>0
independent of
T
.

Thus, using (
7.9
) and Lemma
5.6
, we may rewrite the integral in (
7.8
) as

\displaystyle\int_{M}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{x})]dV_{g}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{M\backslash A}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{x})]dV_{g}(x)+\int_{A\cap\tilde{A}}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{x})]dV_{g}(x)+\int_{A\backslash\tilde{A}}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{x})]dV_{g}(x)

\displaystyle=\vol(M\backslash A)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})](1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1))+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1)

(7.10)

\displaystyle=\vol(M)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})](1+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1))+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(1).

Inserting (
7.2
) into (
7.8
) and using Lemma
7.2
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(f)]

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}}{\omega_{n}}\vol(M)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})]\cdot\left(T+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(T)\right)

\displaystyle=\vol(M)\left(\frac{4\pi}{n}\right)^{1/2}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)}T+o_{T\rightarrow\infty}(T),

as required.
∎

Appendix A
Important case: random non-Gaussian spherical harmonics

The sequence of Laplace eigenvalues on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, the two dimensional round unit sphere, is given by
\{\ell(\ell+1)\}
, where
\ell>0
runs through the integers. Each eigenvalue has multiplicity
2\ell+1
and the corresponding Laplace eigenfunctions are the restrictions of homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
\ell
to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, also known as spherical harmonics.

There exists a “distinguished”orthonormal base for the space of spherical harmonics of degree
\ell
, see for example
[
60
, Theorem 4.9]
,

(A.1)

\displaystyle Y_{\ell,m}(\theta,\varphi)=\left((2\ell+1)\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}\right)^{1/2}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos(\theta))e^{im\varphi},

where
m=-\ell,...,\ell
,
(\theta,\varphi)
are polar-coordinates on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
P_{\ell}^{m}(x)
are the associated Legendre polynomials. Therefore, the band-limited functions on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
with spectral parameter
T=\ell(\ell+1)
and width of the energy window
\rho(T)=1
, such as
f_{\ell}
in (
2.2
), can be expressed as a linear combinations of the functions in (
A.1
).

We observe that, under the notation (
3.3
) of section
3.2
, thanks to the Funk-Hecke formula and the Hilb’s asymptotics for Legendre polynomials (see e.g.
[
38
, Claim 2.7 and Claim 2.9]
and references therein), one has

\mathbb{E}[F_{x,\ell}(y)F_{x,\ell}(y^{\prime})]=J_{0}(|y-y^{\prime}|)+o_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(1),

and we can also differentiate both sides any arbitrary finite number of times.

In this section, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem A.1
.

Let

f_{\ell}(x)=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{m}Y_{\ell,m}(x),

where the
Y_{\ell,m}
are given in (
A.1
) and assume that
a_{\ell,m}
are i.i.d.
\pm 1
Bernoulli random variables. Then

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(f_{\ell})]=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}}\pi\ell+o_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(\ell).

The main crux of extending the proof of Theorem
1.1
to the
2
d-case is the failure of Proposition
6.1
in
2
d. However, in the setting of Theorem
A.1
, it is possible to overcome the said obstacle by invoking the following result due to Nazarov, Nishry and Sodin
[
37
, Corollary 2.2]
on the log-integrability of random Fourier series with Bernoulli coefficients:

Lemma A.2

(
[
37
, Corollary 2.2]
)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{\mathbb{C}}
be a random Fourier series given by

g(\varphi)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{m}b_{m}e(m\varphi),

where
\{a_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
are i.i.d.
\pm 1
Bernoulli random variables defined on the probability space
\Omega
and

\{b_{m}\}_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}
is a sequence of (deterministic) complex-valued coefficients
so that

||g||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\Omega)}=1.

Then, for every integer
p>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(p)>0
such that

\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\Omega}\left|\log|g|\right|^{p}d\nu\leq C,

where
\nu
is the product measure of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)
on
\Omega
.

In fact, Lemma
A.2
implies the following:

Corollary A.3
.

Let
f_{\ell}
be as in Theorem
A.1
,
x\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and
F_{x}=F_{x,\ell}
the scaled version of
f_{\ell}

as in (
3.3
). Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}\mathcal{V}(F_{x})^{2}d\sigma<C,

where
\sigma
is the product measure of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)
on
\Omega
.

Proof.

The proof of Corollary
A.3
is very similar to the proof of Lemma
6.4
, so we omit some details while retaining same notation. Thanks to the inequality (
6.6
) and the fact that
(X+Y)^{2}\lesssim X^{2}+Y^{2},

we have

(A.2)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}\mathcal{V}(F_{x})^{2}d\sigma\lesssim\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}\left(\log\frac{||f^{H}_{\ell}||_{L^{\infty}(4B^{+})}}{||f^{H}_{\ell}||_{L^{\infty}(2B^{+})}}\right)^{2}d\sigma\lesssim O(1)+\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}|\log|f_{\ell}||^{2}d\sigma,

where
\sigma
is the product measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega
. Now, we wish to use Lemma
A.2
to estimate the second term on the r.h.s. of (
A.2
).

First, we observe that, writing
f_{\ell}=f_{\ell}(\varphi,\theta)
, where
(\varphi,\theta)
are spherical coordinates, and using Fubini’s Theorem, we have

(A.3)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}|\log|f_{\ell}||^{2}d\sigma\leq\int\limits_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\Omega}|\log|f_{\ell}(\varphi,\theta)||^{2}d\nu,

where
d\nu=d\varphi\otimes d\mathbb{P}
is the product measure on
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\Omega
.
Writing
f_{\ell}(x)=f_{\ell}(\varphi,\theta)
and bearing in mind (
A.1
), we have

f_{\ell}(\varphi,\theta)=\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}a_{\ell,m}\left(\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}\right)^{1/2}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos(\theta))e^{im\varphi}.

Therefore, fixing
\theta
, thanks to the normalisation
\mathbb{E}[|f_{\ell}(\varphi,\theta)|^{2}]=1
, we may apply Lemma
A.2
with
b_{m}=\frac{(\ell-m)!}{(\ell+m)!}^{1/2}P_{\ell}^{m}(\cos(\theta))
, to see that

\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\Omega}|\log|f_{\ell}||^{2}d\nu\leq C

for some absolute constant
C>0
. Hence, thanks to (
A.3
), the r.h.s. of (
A.2
) is bounded and this concludes the proof of Corollary
A.3
.
∎

We are finally in the position to prove Theorem
A.1
:

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

As in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, Lemma
7.1
, Fubini’s Theorem and Corollary
5.6
give

(A.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(f)]=\frac{4}{\pi}\ell\cdot\left(1+o_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(1)\right)\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}\mathcal{V}(F_{x})d\sigma,

where
\sigma
is the product measure on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega
. Now, Proposition
4.1
implies that

\mathcal{V}(F_{x})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})\hskip 22.76219pt\ell\rightarrow\infty,

in the product space
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega
. Thus, Corollary
A.3
together with the Dominated Convergence Theorem give

\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}\mathcal{V}(F_{x})d\sigma\longrightarrow\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\Omega}\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})d\sigma\hskip 22.76219pt\ell\rightarrow\infty.

Therefore, another application of Fubini’s Theorem together with (
A.4
) give

(A.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(f)]=\frac{4}{\pi}\ell\cdot\left(1+o_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}(1)\right)\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}(F_{\mu})]dx

Finally, the right hand side of (
A.5
) can be computed via Lemma
7.2
and Theorem
A.1
follows.
∎
