Power-law bounds for increasing subsequences in Brownian separable permutons and homogeneous sets in Brownian cographons
Jacopo Borga Stanford University William Da Silva University of Vienna Ewain Gwynne University of Chicago

Abstract
The Brownian separable permutons are a one-parameter family – indexed by p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) – of universal limits of random constrained permutations. We show that for each p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) , there are explicit constants 1 / 2 < α ∗ ​ ( p ) ≤ β ∗ ​ ( p ) < 1 1/2<\alpha_{*}(p)\leq\beta^{*}(p)<1 such that the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a random permutation of size n n sampled from the Brownian separable permuton is between n α ∗ ​ ( p ) − o ​ ( 1 ) n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-o(1)} and n β ∗ ​ ( p ) + o ​ ( 1 ) n^{\beta^{*}(p)+o(1)} with probability tending to 1 as n → ∞ n\to\infty . In the symmetric case p = 1 / 2 p=1/2 , we have α ∗ ​ ( p ) ≈ 0.812 \alpha_{*}(p)\approx 0.812 and β ∗ ​ ( p ) ≈ 0.975 \beta^{*}(p)\approx 0.975 . We present numerical simulations which suggest that the lower bound α ∗ ​ ( p ) \alpha_{*}(p) is close to optimal in the whole range p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) . Our results work equally well for the closely related Brownian cographons. In this setting, we show that for each p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) , the size of the largest clique (resp. independent set) in a random graph on n n vertices sampled from the Brownian cographon is between n α ∗ ​ ( p ) − o ​ ( 1 ) n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-o(1)} and n β ∗ ​ ( p ) + o ​ ( 1 ) n^{\beta^{*}(p)+o(1)} (resp. n α ∗ ​ ( 1 − p ) − o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{\alpha_{*}(1-p)-o(1)} and n β ∗ ​ ( 1 − p ) + o ⁡ ( 1 ) n^{\beta^{*}(1-p)+o(1)} ) with probability tending to 1 as n → ∞ n\to\infty . Our proofs are based on the analysis of a fragmentation process embedded in a Brownian excursion introduced by Bertoin (2002). We expect that our techniques can be extended to prove similar bounds for uniform separable permutations and uniform cographs.

Introduction

The length of the longest increasing subsequence in a random permutation and the size of the largest homogeneous set (
i.e.
a clique or an independent set) in a random graph are two of the classical problems at the interface of combinatorics and probability theory, with connections to several other areas of mathematics.

In this paper, we investigate these classical problems in the setting of universal Brownian-type permutations and graphs,
i.e.
for the
Brownian separable permutons

[
BBF+18
,
BBF+20
]
and the
Brownian cographons

[
BBF+22a
,
Stu21
]
. These objects are the universal limits of various random permutations and graph families.

In the following sections, we first discuss our results on permutations (
Section

1.1
) and then on graphs (
Section

1.2
). In both sections, we briefly review the literature around the questions addressed in this paper. We then present two conjectures and some potential extensions of our work (
Section

1.3
). Here, we also discuss a few open problems and additional motivation for our work coming from random geometry and the study of planar maps.
Finally, we give an overview of the techniques used to establish our main results, explaining how they can potentially be used to answer similar questions in the continuum setting (
Section

1.4
).

1.1
Brownian separable permuton results

1.1.1
Permutons and the Brownian separable permutons

A Borel probability measure
\mu
on the unit square
[0,1]^{2}
is a
permuton
if both of its marginals are uniform, that is,
\mu([a,b]\times[0,1])=\mu([0,1]\times[a,b])=b-a
for all
0\leq a<b\leq 1
.
To a permutation
\sigma
, we can associate a permuton
\mu_{\sigma}
which is equal to
n

\{[\frac{i-1}{n},\frac{i}{n}]\times[\frac{\sigma(i)-1}{n},\frac{\sigma(i)}{n}]:i\in[n]\}.

For a sequence of permutations

\sigma_{n}

converges in the permuton sense
to a limiting permuton

\mu_{\sigma_{n}}
converge weakly to
\mu

[
Grü22
]
for a survey.

One should think of the sequence

The Brownian separable permutons were first introduced while studying random separable permutations

1.1.2
The length of the longest increasing subsequence

There is a vast literature devoted to the asymptotic behavior of the length of the longest increasing subsequence

For uniform random permutations

1.1.3
Main results

Given a permuton
\mu
, sample
n
independent points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
in the unit
square
[0,1]^{2}
according to
\mu

n

\sigma

i

This definition can be naturally extended to the case of random permutons. For more details, see

[
Bor21

. It is important to note that

converges in distribution in the permuton sense to

tends to infinity, as shown in
[

, Lemma 2.3]

Recall that
\LIS(\cdot)
denotes the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a permutation. Let
p\in(0,1)
and recall that
\bm{\mu}_{p}
is the Brownian separable permuton of parameter
p
. We restrict our analysis to the case

, since the cases when
p=0
and
p=1
are degenerate: the Brownian separable permutons

(resp.

We are interested in studying

The results of

show that the following convergence holds in probability for all

\frac{\LIS(\Perm(\bm{\mu}_{p},n))}{n}\to 0.

It is simple to show that

1.2
Brownian cographon results

1.2.1
Graphons and the Brownian cographons

A
graphon
is an equivalence class of measurable functions
W:[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\}
which are symmetric (
i.e.

W(x,y)=W(y,x)
for all
x,y\in[0,1]
), under the equivalence relation
\sim
, where
W\sim U
if there exists an invertible, measurable, Lebesgue measure preserving function
\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
such that
W(\phi(x),\phi(y))=U(x,y)
for almost every
x,y\in[0,1].

Intuitively, a graphon is a continuous analog of the adjacency matrix of a graph, viewed up to relabeling its continuous vertex set. To every graph
G
with
n
labeled vertices, one can naturally associate a corresponding graphon:

\displaystyle W_{G}:

\displaystyle[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\},

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto\,\,\,A_{\lceil nx\rceil,\lceil ny\rceil},

where
A
is the adjacency matrix of the graph
G
. Note that any relabeling of the vertices of
G
gives the same graphon
W_{G}
, and so the definition extends to unlabeled graphs.
It is possible to define the so-called
cut metric
, first on functions and then on graphons. The cut metric induces a notion of convergence for graphons (and so for graphs). Roughly speaking, the graphon convergence is the convergence of the rescaled adjacency matrix with respect to the cut metric. Graphon convergence has been first studied in
[
BCL+08
]
and developed into a vast topic in graph combinatorics, see

[
Lov12
]
for an overview of this field of research.

Given the signed excursion
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
introduced in
Section

, the

\bm{W}_{p}
of parameter

is defined (following
[

]
) as the equivalence class of random functions

\displaystyle\bm{W}_{p}:

\displaystyle[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\},

(1.5)

\displaystyle(x,y)\mapsto\,\,\,\mathds{1}_{\mathfrak{s}(\ell_{x,y})=\oplus},

where, as in

and

. If

then we arbitrarily set

. This choice does not change the law of

because a.s.

A

The Brownian cographon

has been proven to be the limit in the graphon sense of uniform random cographs when the number of vertices tends to infinity

1.2.2
The size of the largest homogeneous set

An

is a subset of its vertices such that every two distinct vertices in the subset are adjacent. For a graph

]

3

We emphasize that cographs play a key role in this conjecture. Indeed, since a homogeneous set in a graph induces a cograph, and all cographs of size

1.3
Conjectures and potential extensions

The results in Theorems
1.1

and
1.3
establish upper and lower bounds for the polynomial growth of the longest increasing subsequence in permutations sampled from the Brownian separable permutons and for the largest clique and independent set in graphs sampled from the Brownian cographons. We expect the existence of a deterministic

for these quantities (
c.f.

2.2

Conjecture 1.5
.

For all
p\in(0,1)
there exists

such that
with probability tending to 1 as

\LIS(\Perm(\bm{\mu}_{p},n))=n^{d(p)+o(1)},

\LCL(\Graph(\bm{W}_{p},n))=n^{d(p)+o(1)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\LIN(\Graph(\bm{W}_{p},n))=n^{d(1-p)+o(1)}.

As already mentioned, numerical simulations suggest that

should be very close to
\alpha_{*}(p)
for all
p\in(0,1)
. See
Appendix

A
for more details. Various heuristic arguments indicate that our lower bound
\alpha_{*}(p)
should
not
be sharp for all
p\in(0,1)
,
i.e.
it should hold that
\alpha_{*}(p)<d(p)
for all

We expect that our estimates for longest increasing subsequences and largest homogeneous sets from Theorems
1.1
and
1.3
in the case when
p=1/2
can be transferred to the setting of uniform separable permutations and uniform cographs, respectively. To explain how this might be accomplished, we recall that both uniform separable permutations and uniform cographs can be encoded by random walk excursions with i.i.d. steps and some collections of random signs
\oplus/\ominus
, in a similar way as the Brownian separable permutons and the Brownian cographons can be encoded by a signed Brownian excursion.

The
KMT coupling theorem

[
KMT75
,
Zai98
]
states that one can construct a random walk with i.i.d. steps and a standard Brownian motion on the same probability space in such a way that with high probability, the difference of their values at each time
n
is
O(\log n)
. To transfer our bounds to the setting of uniform separable permutons and uniform graphons, a natural approach would be to couple the encoding walks for (the infinite-volume version of) the discrete models with the encoding Brownian motion for (the infinite-volume version of) the Brownian models via KMT. One could then use the coupling to transfer estimates for Brownian motion to estimates for random walk, and finally use local absolute continuity arguments to transfer from an unconditioned walk to a random walk excursion. We point out that a similar approach has been used to prove estimates for random planar maps in
[
GHS20
]
.
However, we expect the above KMT coupling argument to require a fair amount of technical work, so we do not carry it out in this paper.

It would also be interesting to deal with the discrete models themselves to come up with power-law bounds (or, even better, with the exact exponents) for the length of the longest increasing sequence in uniform separable permutations and the size of the largest homogeneous set in uniform cographs.

In fact, we expect an even stronger relationship between discrete and continuum models.

Conjecture 1.6
.

Let
d(1/2)\in[\alpha_{*}(1/2),\beta^{*}(1/2)]
be as in
1.5
. Let
\sigma_{n}
and
G_{n}
be a uniform separable permutation and a uniform separable cograph of size

\LIS(\sigma_{n})=n^{d(1/2)+o(1)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\LHS(G_{n})=n^{d(1/2)+o(1)}.

It also seems possible that our methods could be extended to some models of uniform permutations in substitution-closed classes which are well-behaved with respect to the substitution decomposition
[
BBF+20
,
BBFS20
]
and to uniform graphs in some classes of graphs which are closed under the substitution operation at the core of the modular decomposition

1.4
Proof techniques and ordered subsets of the signed Brownian excursion

Recall that we will derive
1.3
from
1.1
, so here we focus on the latter theorem. Our results in
1.1
follow – after some non-trivial arguments in Sections
4

(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
.

1.4.1
Strategy for the proof of the lower bound

We say that a subset
O\subset[0,1]
is
ordered w.r.t.

\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
if the usual order on
O
coincides with
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
on
O\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}}
.

To obtain the lower bound in
1.1
, we first define in (
1.6
) a selection rule
\mathsf{S}
which determines a large subset
\mathcal{S}\subset[0,1]
ordered w.r.t.
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
. Here,
large
refers to a good notion of size, which we could,
e.g.
, take to be Hausdorff dimension.

To define the selection rule
\mathsf{S}
, we explore the signed excursion
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
by heights.
We call
branching height
a height which corresponds to a local minimum of
\mathfrak{e}
: a branching height is said to be
positive
or
negative
according to the sign
\oplus
or
\ominus
of the corresponding symbol in
\mathfrak{s}
. We denote by
\mathcal{B}_{\oplus}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
the set of positive and negative branching heights respectively, and we set
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{\oplus}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
. Each branching height
b=\mathfrak{e}(t_{b})
results in one sub-excursion of
\mathfrak{e}
over an interval
(a,c)\subset[0,1]
splitting into two sub-excursions over the intervals
(a,t_{b})
and
(t_{b},c)
for some
t_{b}\in(a,c)
. See the red excursions and red intervals in
Figure

4
.

Figure 4:
A sketch for the notation introduced in
Section

1.4.1
.

Note that the
optimal
selection rule to find the largest ordered subset of
[0,1]
w.r.t.
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
can be informally read as follows. Whenever we hit a positive branching height, we keep both intervals
(a,t_{b})
and
(t_{b},c)
and carry on the exploration in both components of
\mathfrak{e}
. On the other hand, when reaching a negative branching height, one needs to discard one of the intervals
(a,t_{b})
and
(t_{b},c)
(because in this case, by definition (
1.1
),
y\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}x
for almost all
x\in(a,t_{b})
and
y\in(t_{b},c)
), keeping only the one with the largest ordered subset.

The main issue with the previous considerations is the heavily non-Markovian nature of the selection rule at negative branching heights: in order to pick one of
(a,t_{b})
or
(t_{b},c)
over the other, one
a priori
needs to look into the whole future and see which one of them has the largest ordered subset. Our approach is to replace this selection rule by a Markovian rule. More precisely, we consider the following
selection rule

\mathsf{S}
:

“Whenever reaching a negative branching height, we discard the smaller

(1.6)

(in terms of Lebesgue measure) interval between
(a,t_{b})
and
(t_{b},c)
.”

We then want to show that the selection rule
\mathsf{S}
does not discard too many points, in a sense made precise in the next two paragraphs.

One essential tool in our proof is the analysis of a natural
fragmentation process

[
Ber06
]
, or more precisely of a
self-similar interval fragmentation process
, embedded in the Brownian excursion
\mathfrak{e}
(see (
2.1
) below for a precise definition). A key player in the description of such processes is the so-called
tagged fragment
, which in our case, consists in looking at the duration of the excursion at height
h\geq 0
straddling a uniform point
U
in
(0,1)
. See the green excursion and green interval in
Figure

4
.
[
Ber02
, Section 4]
describes the law of the tagged fragment as a stochastic process with time parameter
h\geq 0
: it is an explicit
positive self-similar Markov process with index
-\frac{1}{2}
, see
2.3
for a precise statement.

Remark 1.8
.

The specific expressions for our bounds
\alpha_{*}(p)
and
\beta^{*}(p)
in
Remark

1.2
are related to the expression of some Laplace exponents of the fragmentation process mentioned above, see for instance (
3.3
) below.

In order to show that the selection rule
\mathsf{S}
does not discard too many points, we proceed as follows:

(a)

we first estimate the probability that the tagged fragment corresponding to
U
survives
long enough
, in the sense that it reaches some small value
\varepsilon
before it (possibly) gets discarded by
\mathsf{S}
. In
3.1
, we show that the latter probability is asymptotically
c\,\varepsilon^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)}
, for some positive constant
c>0
(recall from
Remark

1.2
that
\lambda_{*}(p)=1-\alpha_{*}(p)
).

(b)

Then in
3.4
, we extend this estimate to a so-called
two-point function estimate
: we show that the probability that two tagged fragments corresponding to two independent uniform points
U
and
V
in
(0,1)
both survive until they get smaller than
\varepsilon
is asymptotically
c^{\prime}\,\varepsilon^{2\lambda_{\ast}(p)}
, for some (other) positive constant
c^{\prime}>0
.

The above two estimates turn out to be enough to then deduce the lower bound in
1.1
, as shown in
Section

4
.

Remark 1.9
.

One can also introduce the set of survival times

\mathcal{S}:=\{t\in[0,1],\;t\;\text{is not in any sub-interval which is discarded by }\mathsf{S}\}.

Plainly,
\mathcal{S}
is a random set of fractal type and whose law depends on the parameter
p\in(0,1)
of the signs. Moreover, by construction, we have the following immediate result: Almost surely,
\mathcal{S}
is totally ordered w.r.t.
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
.

We believe that the first moment estimate in Item
(a)
above, together with an upgraded version of the two-point function estimate in Item
(b)
above and some energy method arguments (see for instance
[
MP10
, Theorem 4.27]
), would be enough to prove the following result:

For all
p\in(0,1)
, almost surely, the Hausdorff dimension of
\mathcal{S}
is

\dim(\mathcal{S})=\dim(\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}})=\alpha_{*}(p),

where
\alpha_{*}(p)
is as in
Remark

1.2
.

We expect the upgraded version of the two-point function estimate mentioned above to require a fair amount of technical work, so we do not carry it out in this paper.

1.4.2
Strategy for the proof of the upper-bound

Our strategy to prove the upper bound in
1.1
is in some sense to analyze the
worst-case scenario
.

Recall the notation from the previous section. We pick (in a manner which is allowed to depend on the signed excursion
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
, plus possibly some additional independent information) an arbitrary subset
O\subset[0,1]^{2}
which is ordered w.r.t.
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
.
For each branching height
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
, let
\eta_{b}
be the
discarding rule
corresponding to the set
O
, that is,
\eta_{b}
is equal to
L
(for
left
) if
O
intersects
(t_{b},c)
and
\eta_{b}
is equal to
R
(for
right
) if
O
intersects
(a,t_{b})
(we make an arbitrary choice if
O
intersects neither of the two intervals). Our goal is to show that
(\eta_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}
discards a large subset of
[0,1]
so that the complement is small. To do that, we upper-bound as
\varepsilon\to 0
the following probability

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((\eta_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}\text{ does not discard the fragment corresponding to }U\text{ before it gets smaller than }\varepsilon}}\right),

where
U
is a uniform point in
[0,1]
independent from everything else. In
5.1
, we show that the above probability is upper-bounded by
c\cdot\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
(recall from
Remark

1.2
that
\lambda^{*}(p)=1-\beta^{*}(p)
). This is done in two main steps:

•

Fix
r\in(1/2,1)
. We say that a branching height
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
whose corresponding excursion interval
(a,c)
contains
U
is
balanced
(at scale
r
) if
\max\{t_{b}-a,c-t_{b}\}\leq r(c-a)
,
i.e.
the sub-excursions to the left and right of the local minimum time
t_{b}
are of comparable time duration (recall
Figure

4
). The first step, which is carried out in
5.4
, is to upper-bound the probability that
(\eta_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}
does not discard
U
before reaching the
m
-th smallest balanced branching height. In particular, we will show that this probability is upper-bounded by
r^{m}
.

•

The second step, which is carried out in
5.5
, is to upper-bound the probability that the fragment corresponding to
U
at the
m
-th balanced branching height has duration smaller than
\varepsilon
.

We then optimize over the parameters
r
and
m
.

Finally, we transfer this estimate to the discrete setting in
Section

6
(obtaining the upper bound in
1.1
), as follows.
First, we sample a permutation
\Perm(\bm{\mu}_{p},n)
from the Brownian separable permuton
\bm{\mu}_{p}
. Then, we consider the discarding rule
(\eta_{b})_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}
corresponding to the points of the longest increasing subsequence in
\Perm(\bm{\mu}_{p},n)
(if there are multiple ones, we choose one arbitrarily). That is, at each branching height
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
we discard the interval not containing points of the longest increasing subsequence (we make an arbitrary choice if the longest increasing subsequence intersects neither of the two intervals). We then conclude using the estimates above.

Remark 1.10
.

As in Remark
1.9
, we expect that one can extend the arguments discussed above to obtain the following result:

Fix

. Almost surely, every set

such that

is totally ordered w.r.t.

satisfies

where

is as in
Remark

1.2
.

However, there are some technical arguments involved in transferring from an estimate for a uniform time

to a bound for Hausdorff dimension, so for the sake of brevity we do not prove the above statement in this paper.

2.1
Brownian excursions

We collect some standard properties of a Brownian excursion
\mathfrak{e}
and the order
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
introduced in (
1.1
) that will be used frequently (and sometimes tacitly) in the paper.

Conditioning on
\mathfrak{e}
, we say that
t\in[0,1]
is
regular
for
\mathfrak{e}
if it is
not
a one-sided local minimum of
\mathfrak{e}
, that is,
t\in[0,1]
is regular for
\mathfrak{e}
if

\forall\varepsilon>0,\,\exists\,t_{1}\in(t-\varepsilon,t),t_{2}\in(t,t+\varepsilon)\text{ such that }\mathfrak{e}(t_{1})<\mathfrak{e}(t)\;\text{and}\;\mathfrak{e}(t_{2})<\mathfrak{e}(t).

We denote by
\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}}
the (random) set of regular points of
\mathfrak{e}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. Let
\mathfrak{e}
be a (normalized) Brownian excursion and
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
be the relation introduced in (
1.1
). Then, almost surely, the following assertions hold.

(a)

All local minima of
\mathfrak{e}
are strict local minima (hence countable), they all have different heights, and they are dense in
[0,1]
.

(b)

Local minima never split the excursion into two sub-excursions with equal durations.

(c)

For every
x,y\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}}
with
x<y
, it holds that
\min_{[x,y]}\mathfrak{e}
is reached at a unique point which is a strict local minimum.

(d)

The relation
\vartriangleleft_{\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p}
restricted to
\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}}
is a total order.

(e)

\dim([0,1]\setminus\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}})=1/2
, where
\dim(\cdot)
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of a set.

Proof.

The first two items are classical properties of Brownian excursions (see for instance
[
RY13
, Chapter XII]
). Then Item (c) follows from the previous properties and the definition of regular points.

Item (d) is
[
Maa20
, Lemma 2.5]
. Note that the latter lemma states our claim only for a set of Lebesgue measure one (instead of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathfrak{e}}
), but the proof of the lemma proves exactly our claim.

Finally, we prove Item (e). This result is classical in the probabilistic literature, and we include a proof only for the sake of completeness. Let
t
be a time which is not regular for
\mathfrak{e}
. Then either there is a rational
q<t
such that
\mathfrak{e}
attains a running minimum at time
t
when run forward started from time
q
; or there exists a rational
q>t
such that
\mathfrak{e}
attains a running minimum at time
t
when run backward started from time
q
. For each rational time
q
, the set of times at which
\mathfrak{e}
attains a running minimum when run forward (resp. backward) from time
q
has a.s. Hausdorff dimension 1/2 (by local absolute continuity between the Brownian excursion and Brownian motion). Therefore the result follows from the countable stability of Hausdorff dimension.
∎

(2.9)

For

b\geq 0

First moment estimate

Our first estimate in this subsection concerns the probability that the fragment targeted at the uniform point
U
survives
long enough
, in the sense that it reaches some small value before it gets (possibly) discarded. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and

H^{U}_{\varepsilon}:=\inf\{h>0,\;F^{U}(h)<\varepsilon\}.

(3.5)

Recalling the height
H_{\mathsf{S}}^{U}
from (
3.1
), we also introduce the event

\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{H^{U}_{\varepsilon}<H^{U}_{\mathsf{S}}}}\right\},

(3.6)

i.e.
, the event that the fragment targeted at the uniform point
U
gets smaller than
\varepsilon
before it gets (possibly) discarded.

We fix

for the rest of the section. All the constants appearing in the next propositions depend on
p
, even if not explicitly stated.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{\ast}(p)
be the only positive solution to the equation
\Phi^{\mathsf{S}}(-\lambda_{\ast}(p))=-\lambda(p)
, with
\Phi^{\mathsf{S}}
as in (
3.3
) and

as in (

).
The probability of

(3.7)

Moreover, there exists a constant

such that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}\big)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c\,\varepsilon^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)}.

(3.8)

Remark 3.2
.

We emphasize that the exponent
\lambda_{\ast}(p)
is the same as the one appearing in
Remark

1.2
(see also that remark for some particular values of

).
Since

is continuous on

with
\Phi^{\mathsf{S}}(0)=0
and
\Phi^{\mathsf{S}}(q)\to-\infty
as

, it is plain that

. See the left-hand side of
Figure

for

, obtained by solving numerically the equation

.

Proof of
3.1
.

The Lamperti representation provides a natural point of view to address this question (see for instance
[

, Section 2.3]
where a similar approach was used in a different context).
Recall the subordinators
\xi^{\mathsf{S}}
and
\xi^{\mathsf{K}}
and the time

introduced just above (

). Note that

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(H^{U}_{\varepsilon}<H^{U}_{\mathsf{S}}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(T_{-\log\varepsilon}<T\big),

(3.9)

where

is the first passage time of

above

(see (

)), by the independence of

and

(and so of

and

),

(3.10)

3.1
is then a consequence of

. First, note that for all

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\zeta\geq 0
. There exists a constant

such that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F^{U}(H^{U}_{\varepsilon})^{\zeta}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}}}}\right]\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c\,\varepsilon^{\zeta+\lambda_{\ast}(p)},

where

is the positive solution to

.
Moreover, if

,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F^{U}(H^{U}_{\varepsilon})^{\zeta}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}}}}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{\zeta+\lambda_{\ast}(p)},\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon>0.

Proof.

Using the description in

, together with the notation at the beginning of this subsection, we have that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F^{U}(H^{U}_{\varepsilon})^{\zeta}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\xi^{\mathsf{S}}_{T_{-\log\varepsilon}}}\cdot\mathds{1}_{T_{-\log\varepsilon}<T}}}\right],

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[F^{U}(H^{U}_{\varepsilon})^{\zeta}\cdot\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\zeta\xi^{\mathsf{S}}_{T_{-\log\varepsilon}}-\lambda(p)T_{-\log\varepsilon}}}}\right].

3.3
Two-point function estimate

We now consider two independent uniform points
U
and
V
in
(0,1)
also independent from all the other random quantities. Recall from (
3.6
) the notation
\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}
respectively for the events that the fragments containing
U
and
V
survive in the strategy
\mathsf{S}
(defined in (
1.6
)) until getting smaller than
\varepsilon>0
. We are interested in the correlation between the two events
\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}
. The aim of this section is to prove the following second moment estimate, which should be compared to
3.1
.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}}}\right)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c\,\varepsilon^{2\lambda_{\ast}(p)},

where
\lambda_{\ast}(p)
is the only positive solution to the equation
\Phi^{\mathsf{S}}(-\lambda_{\ast}(p))=-\lambda(p)
, with
\Phi^{\mathsf{S}}
as in (
3.3
).

Proof.

Introduce, for
\varepsilon>0
, the event that
U
and
V
split before reaching
\varepsilon
, namely

\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{U}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U})\neq I^{V}(H_{\varepsilon}^{V})}}\right\}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. We split the event
\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}
according to
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V}
and its complement.

We first deal with the two-point function on the event
(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V})^{c}
. In this case, we condition on
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},U)
to obtain

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}\cap(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V})^{c}}}\right)=\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}\cap\{V\in I^{U}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U})\}}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}}\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\in I^{U}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U})|(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},U)}}\right)}}\right].

(3.12)

We now argue conditionally on
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},U)
. Since
H_{\varepsilon}^{U}
is a stopping time for the filtration
\sigma(I^{U}(h^{\prime}),h^{\prime}\leq h)
, defined for all
h>0
, and
V
is independent of
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},U)
, we a.s. have
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V\in I^{U}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U})|(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},U)}}\right)=F^{U}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U})
. Plugging this identity into (
3.12
), we infer that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}\cap(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V})^{c}}}\right)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}}\cdot F^{U}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U})}}\right].

Using
3.3
, we therefore conclude that there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{E}^{V}_{\varepsilon}\cap(\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V})^{c}}}\right)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c_{1}\varepsilon^{1+\lambda_{\ast}(p)}.

(3.13)

We next deal with the two-point function on the event
\mathcal{G}_{\varepsilon}^{U,V}
. Remark that this event can be rephrased as the existence of a branching height
a\leq H_{\varepsilon}^{U}\wedge H_{\varepsilon}^{V}
separating
U
and
V
. We note that
a
is a stopping time with respect to the filtration
\sigma(I^{U}(h^{\prime}),I^{V}(h^{\prime})\,,\,h^{\prime}\leq h)
, defined for all
h>0
. Moreover, given
I^{U}(a)
, the law of
U
is uniform in
I^{U}(a)
(and the same applies to
V
). Therefore, by conditioning at height
a
and using the branching property of excursions above
a
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{V}\cap\mathcal{G}^{U,V}_{\varepsilon}\Big)\\
=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\{a<H^{U}_{\mathsf{S}}\wedge H^{V}_{\mathsf{S}}\wedge H^{U}_{\varepsilon}\wedge H^{V}_{\varepsilon}\}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{F^{U}(a)}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\widetilde{U}}\big)\cdot\mathbb{P}_{F^{V}(a)}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\widetilde{V}}\big)}}\right],

(3.14)

where
\mathbb{P}_{\ell}
describes the law of a Brownian excursion with duration
\ell>0
, and conditionally on
F^{U}(a)
and
F^{V}(a)
,
\widetilde{U},\widetilde{V}
are independent and uniform in
(0,F^{U}(a))
and
(0,F^{V}(a))
respectively. By Brownian scaling, for
\ell>0
we have

\mathbb{P}_{\ell}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\widetilde{U}}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/\ell}^{U^{\prime}}\big)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{\ell}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{\widetilde{V}}\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/\ell}^{V^{\prime}}\big),

with
U^{\prime},V^{\prime}
uniform in
(0,1)
. Equation (
3.14
) now boils down to

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{V}\cap\mathcal{G}^{U,V}_{\varepsilon}\Big)=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\{a<H^{U}_{\mathsf{S}}\wedge H^{V}_{\mathsf{S}}\wedge H^{U}_{\varepsilon}\wedge H^{V}_{\varepsilon}\}}\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/F^{U}(a)}^{U^{\prime}}\,\middle|\,F^{U}(a)}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/F^{V}(a)}^{V^{\prime}}\,\middle|\,F^{V}(a)}}\right)}}\right].

(3.15)

We now take
\varepsilon\to 0
. We claim that for some constant
c_{2}>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{V}\cap\mathcal{G}^{U,V}_{\varepsilon}\Big)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c_{2}\,\varepsilon^{2\lambda_{\ast}(p)}\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\{a<H^{U}_{\mathsf{S}}\wedge H^{V}_{\mathsf{S}}\}}F^{U}(a)^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}F^{V}(a)^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right].

(3.16)

Indeed, from (
3.8
) in
3.1
, we know that there exists a constant
c>0
such that
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/F^{U}(a)}^{U^{\prime}}\big|F^{U}(a)\big)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c\cdot(\varepsilon/F^{U}(a))^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)}
and likewise
\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/F^{V}(a)}^{V^{\prime}}\big|F^{V}(a)\big)\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\sim}c\cdot(\varepsilon/F^{V}(a))^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)}
. The result then follows from (
3.15
) provided we can apply dominated convergence. We now justify that we can apply it.

First of all, recall from (
3.7
) that we have, for all
\varepsilon>0
, the upper-bounds

\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/F^{U}(a)}^{U^{\prime}}\big|F^{U}(a)\big)\leq(\varepsilon/F^{U}(a))^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon/F^{V}(a)}^{V^{\prime}}\big|F^{V}(a)\big)\leq(\varepsilon/F^{V}(a))^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)}.

The domination will therefore follow if we prove that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\{a<H^{U}_{\mathsf{S}}\wedge H^{V}_{\mathsf{S}}\}}F^{U}(a)^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}F^{V}(a)^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right]<\infty.

(3.17)

To do so, we argue conditionally on
F^{U}
, by first noting that one can construct
F^{V}(a)
from
F^{U}
as follows. Recall that
a
corresponds to the branching height separating
U
and
V
, hence
F^{V}(a)
is equal to the length of the interval not containing
U
split by the jump of
F^{U}
at height
a
, that is
F^{V}(a)=-\Delta F^{U}(a)
(recall
Figure

6
, p.
6
). Now observe that given some fixed jump time
h
of
F^{U}
, the probability that the branching height
a
is equal to
h
is given by the probability that
V
belongs to the interval corresponding to the fragment
-\Delta F^{U}(h)
. In other words, conditionally on
F^{U}
, using the independence of
V
and
U
, one can build
F^{V}(a)
by selecting the jump
-\Delta F^{U}(h)
of
F^{U}
at time
h
with probability
-\Delta F^{U}(h)
. By removing the indicator and conditioning (
3.17
) on
F^{U}
, one therefore gets

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{a<H_{\mathsf{S}}^{U}\wedge H_{\mathsf{S}}^{V}}(F^{U}(a)F^{V}(a))^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{0<h<h^{U}}(F^{U}(h))^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}(-\Delta F^{U}(h))^{1-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right].

Using the Lamperti representation from
2.3
, and noting that
-\Delta F^{U}(h)=\mathrm{e}^{-\xi_{b^{-}}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\xi_{b}})
with
b=\rho(h)
, this becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{a<H_{\mathsf{S}}^{U}\wedge H_{\mathsf{S}}^{V}}(F^{U}(a)F^{V}(a))^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{b>0}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)\xi_{b}}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\lambda_{\ast}(p))\xi_{b^{-}}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\xi_{b}})^{1-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\sum_{b>0}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-2\lambda_{\ast}(p))\xi_{b^{-}}}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)\Delta\xi_{b}}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\Delta\xi_{b}})^{1-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right].

Then an application of the compensation formula for
\xi
(see
e.g.

[
Kyp14
, Theorem 4.4]
) provides

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{a<H_{\mathsf{S}}^{U}\wedge H_{\mathsf{S}}^{V}}(F^{U}(a)F^{V}(a))^{-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}}}\right]\\
\leq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-2\lambda_{\ast}(p))\xi_{b}}\mathrm{d}b}}\right]\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda_{\ast}(p)x}(1-\mathrm{e}^{-x})^{1-\lambda_{\ast}(p)}.

On the one hand, since
\lambda_{\ast}(p)<1/2
, it is clear from the expression of
\Lambda
in
2.3
that the second integral is finite. On the other hand, the first expectation is simply

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-(1-2\lambda_{\ast}(p))\xi_{b}}\mathrm{d}b}}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-b\Phi(1-2\lambda_{\ast}(p))}\mathrm{d}b.

As
\lambda_{\ast}(p)<1/2
, the observation that
\Phi(1-2\lambda_{\ast}(p))>0
concludes the proof of (
3.17
). The domination is thus established, which proves (
3.16
).

3.4
finally follows from the two asymptotics (
3.13
) and (
3.16
).
∎

4
Lower bound for sequences sampled from the Brownian separable permutons

The main goal of this section is to prove the lower bound in
1.1
. As already pointed out in
Remark

1.2
,
\alpha_{*}(p)>1/2
for all
p\in(0,1)
(indeed,
\alpha_{*}(0^{+})=1/2
and
\alpha_{*}(1^{-})=1
, and
\alpha_{*}(p)
is strictly increasing in
p\in(0,1)
). Hence, it is enough to prove the lower bound in the second item in the theorem statement. This is done in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
and let
\alpha_{*}(p)=1-\lambda_{*}(p)
be as in
Remark

1.2
. Let
\sigma_{n}
be a random permutation of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
sampled from the Brownian separable permuton
\bm{\mu}_{p}
. Then for all
\alpha<\alpha_{*}(p)
, the following convergence in probability holds

\frac{\LIS(\sigma_{n})}{n^{\alpha}}\to\infty.

(4.1)

Proof.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and set
\varepsilon=1/n
. Let
(U_{i})_{i\leq n}
be a sequence of i.i.d. uniform random variables in
(0,1)
, independent of all the other random quantities, and recall from
Section

2.2
that
\sigma_{n}=\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{i})_{i\leq n})
.
Recall also the event
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}
from (
3.6
), and introduce
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U_{i}}}
. Thanks to
3.1
, there exist two constants
c,C>0
such that, for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{E}[S_{n}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U_{i}})\in(c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)},C\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)}),

(4.2)

where we used that
\varepsilon=1/n
and
\alpha_{*}(p)=1-\lambda_{\ast}(p)
. Moreover, from
3.4
, if
U
and
V
are independent uniform random variables in
(0,1)
also independent of all the other random quantities, there exists another constant
c>0
such that, for
\varepsilon=1/n
small enough,

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{V}\bigg)\leq c\cdot\varepsilon^{2\lambda_{\ast}(p)}=c\cdot n^{2\alpha_{*}(p)-2}.

(4.3)

Using (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) we deduce that for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{E}[S_{n}^{2}]=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U_{i}}\cap\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U_{j}})\leq c\cdot n^{2\alpha_{*}(p)},

(4.4)

for some (other) constant
c>0
.
The Paley–Zygmund inequality with the bounds in (
4.2
) and (
4.4
) implies that there exist two (other) constants
c,q>0
such that for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{P}(S_{n}\geq c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)})\geq q.

(4.5)

Now, recalling that
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U_{i}}}
, denote by
\{U^{*}_{\ell},\ell\in[S_{n}]\}
the
S_{n}
random variables among
\{U_{i},i\leq n\}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{\varepsilon}^{U^{*}_{\ell}}
occurs.
We now want to extract a large subset
\{U^{*}_{\ell},\ell\in\Lambda\}
of
\{U^{*}_{\ell},\ell\in[S_{n}]\}
such that the corresponding intervals
\{I^{U^{*}_{\ell}}_{\varepsilon},\ell\in\Lambda\}
are disjoint (recall that
I^{t}_{\varepsilon}:=I^{t}(H^{t}_{\varepsilon})
denotes the largest interval containing
t
of size smaller than
\varepsilon
in the fragmentation process). The reason to be interested in such a large subset resides in the following result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Almost surely, if
\{I^{U^{*}_{\ell}}_{\varepsilon},\ell\in\Lambda\}
are disjoint, then the permutation
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U^{*}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Lambda})
is increasing.

Proof of
4.2
.

Assume that
\{I^{U^{*}_{\ell}}_{\varepsilon},\ell\in\Lambda\}
are disjoint and recall that for all
\ell
, the variable
U^{*}_{\ell}
is chosen so that
\mathcal{E}^{U^{*}_{\ell}}_{\varepsilon}
occurs. By definition of
\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.6
), if
\mathcal{E}^{U}_{\varepsilon}
occurs then the interval
I^{U}_{\varepsilon}
containing
U
is not discarded by the selection rule
\mathsf{S}
. Now suppose for a contradiction that there exist two variables
U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}
and
U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}
, with
U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}<U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}
and
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\Lambda
, such that the two corresponding values in
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U^{*}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Lambda})
form an inversion.
Since
I^{U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}}_{\varepsilon}
and
I^{U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}}_{\varepsilon}
are disjoint, there must exist (by
2.1
) a local minimum of
\mathfrak{e}
at some height
h=\mathfrak{e}(t)
which separates
U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}
and
U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}
, in particular
t\notin I^{U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}}_{\varepsilon}\cup I^{U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}}_{\varepsilon}
,

F^{U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}}(h^{-})\geq\varepsilon
and
F^{U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}}(h^{-})\geq\varepsilon
. See
Figure

7
. Moreover, the sign corresponding to such a local minimum must be a
\ominus
(since we assumed that the two elements corresponding to
U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}
and
U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}
in
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U^{*}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Lambda})
form an inversion). We deduce that either
U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}
or
U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}
must be discarded by
\mathsf{S}
at height
h
. Since
F^{U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}}(h^{-})\geq\varepsilon
and
F^{U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}}(h^{-})\geq\varepsilon
, this contradicts the fact that
\mathcal{E}^{U^{*}_{\ell_{1}}}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{E}^{U^{*}_{\ell_{2}}}_{\varepsilon}
occur.
∎

An illustration explaining the proof of
4.2
.

In order to guarantee that the size of
\Lambda
(and hence the size of the increasing permutation
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U^{*}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Lambda})
) is large, we also need the following estimate.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\varepsilon=1/n
and fix
\zeta>0
. There exist two constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
(which may depend on
\zeta
but not on
n
) such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\in[n],\;\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I^{U_{i}}_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{\zeta}}}\right)\geq 1-c_{1}n\cdot\exp(-c_{2}n^{\zeta}),\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

Proof of
4.3
.

This is a standard binomial concentration argument. Let
\varepsilon=1/n
and fix
\zeta>0
.
We recall that the number of uniform variables among
(U_{i})_{i\leq n}
which fall in some prescribed interval
A
follows a binomial distribution
\text{Bin}(n,|A|)
with success probability
|A|
. Note that for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\in[n],\,\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I^{U_{i}}_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{\zeta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\big(\#\{j\in[n]\setminus\{i\},\,U_{j}\in I^{U_{i}}_{\varepsilon}\}\geq n^{\zeta}-1\big)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(n-1,\varepsilon)\geq n^{\zeta}-1),

where in the last inequality we used that by definition
|I^{U_{i}}_{\varepsilon}|=F^{U_{i}}(H_{\varepsilon}^{U_{i}})\leq\varepsilon
. Now recalling that
\varepsilon=1/n
, we get by Chernov’s bound that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\in[n],\,\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I^{U_{i}}_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{\zeta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-c_{1}n\cdot\min_{\gamma>0}\{\exp(-\gamma n^{\zeta})\mathbb{E}[\exp(\gamma\text{Bin}(n-1,1/n))]\}

\displaystyle=1-c_{1}n\cdot\min_{\gamma>0}\{\exp(-\gamma n^{\zeta})(1-1/n+\exp(\gamma)/n)^{n-1}\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-c_{1}n\cdot\exp(-c_{2}n^{\zeta}),

(4.6)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are two constants.
∎

Note that on the event
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S_{n}\geq c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)}}}\right\}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\forall i\in[n],\;\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I^{U_{i}}_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{\zeta}}}\right\}
, we can extract a subset
\{U^{*}_{\ell},\ell\in\Lambda\}
of
\{U^{*}_{\ell},\ell\in[S_{n}]\}
such that the corresponding intervals
\{I^{U^{*}_{\ell}}_{\varepsilon},\ell\in\Lambda\}
are disjoint and the size of
\Lambda
is large, in the sense that
\#\Lambda\geq c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}
. Hence, on this event, thanks to
4.2
, we have that
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U^{*}_{\ell})_{\ell\in\Lambda})
is an increasing subsequence in
\sigma_{n}=\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{i})_{i\leq n})
of size at least
c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}
.

Using (
4.5
) and
4.3
, we deduce that for all
\zeta>0
there exist two (other) constants
c,q>0
(which may depend on
\zeta
) such that,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma_{n})\geq c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}}}\right)\geq q,\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

(4.7)

Note that this estimate holds for all
n\geq 1
since we are allowed to choose a (possibly smaller) constant
c>0
.

The rest of the proof is devoted to upgrading (
4.7
) by proving that for all
\zeta>0
there exists (another) constant
c>0
(which may depend on
\zeta
) such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma_{n})\geq c\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}}}\right)=1.

(4.8)

To do this, we use a zero-one law type argument.
Recall the signed excursion
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
and the definition of the tagged fragment from
2.3
.
Given a branching height
h>0
, recall that
\mathfrak{s}(h)
is the corresponding sign.
For
\delta>0
, let

H=H(\delta):=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{h>0\,\middle|\;\mathfrak{s}(h)=\oplus,\,\,\min\{F^{U}(h^{-})-F^{U}(h),F^{U}(h)\}\geq\delta,\,\,F^{U}(h)>1/2}}\right\},

(4.9)

with the convention that
\inf\emptyset=\infty
. We claim the following.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(H<\infty)\geq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

Proof of
4.4
.

Fix
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
.
It suffices to show that a.s. there exists a random
\delta>0
such that
H(\delta)<\infty
. Indeed, by the continuity of the probability for increasing families of events, this implies that there is some deterministic
\delta
such that
\mathbb{P}(H(\delta)<\infty)\geq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
.

Recall that from
2.3
we have that
F^{[U]}(h)=\exp(-\xi_{s})
with
s=\rho(h)
. Since
\xi
is a subordinator with Levy measure
\Lambda(0,\infty)=\infty
, then
F^{U}(h)
does not immediately jump below
1/2
(by right-continuity of
F^{U}(h)
), and it has infinitely many downward jumps in every non-trivial interval of times (because
\Lambda(0,\infty)=\infty
; see
e.g.

[
Ber96
, Section I.5]
). Hence there are infinitely many
h>0
such that
\min\{F^{U}(h^{-})-F^{U}(h),F^{U}(h)\}>0
and
F^{U}(h)>1/2
. Since the signs
\mathfrak{s}
at different branching heights are i.i.d., a.s. there exists
h>0
satisfying the conditions of the previous sentence such that
\mathfrak{s}(h)=\oplus
. We then take
\delta:=\min\{F^{U}(h^{-})-F^{U}(h),F^{U}(h)\}
for the latter choice of
h
.
∎

We now fix
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
and take
\delta>0
so that
\mathbb{P}(H<\infty)\geq 1-\varepsilon^{\prime}
. When
H<\infty
, by the definition of
H
in (
4.9
), the sign of the branching height
H
is
\mathfrak{s}(H)=\oplus
and
H
splits
I^{U}(H)
into the two intervals
L
and
R
with
\min\{|L|,|R|\}=\min\{F^{U}(H^{-})-F^{U}(H),F^{U}(H)\}\geq\delta
, and none of them is discarded by the selection rule
\mathsf{S}
(recall how the selection rule
\mathsf{S}
works from (
1.6
)). When
H=\infty
, we set
L=R=\emptyset
. With the convention that
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},\emptyset)
is the empty permutation, we set

\sigma^{L}_{n}:=\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{i})_{i\leq n}\cap L)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sigma^{R}_{n}:=\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{i})_{i\leq n}\cap R),

and observe that
|\sigma^{L}_{n}|=\#\big((U_{i})_{i\leq n}\cap L\big)
and
|\sigma^{R}_{n}|=\#\big((U_{i})_{i\leq n}\cap R\big)
. We have the following bound on the size of the two permutations.

Lemma 4.5
.

For all
\delta>0
, there exists two constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
(which may depend on
\delta
but not on
n
) such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|\}\geq\delta n/3\,\middle|\,H<\infty}}\right)\geq 1-c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}n),\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

Proof of
4.5
.

The proof uses standard binomial concentration arguments. Fix
\delta>0
.
We have that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|\}\geq\delta n/3\,\middle|\,H<\infty}}\right)=1-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|\}<\delta n/3\,\middle|\,H<\infty}}\right)\\
\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\sigma^{L}_{n}|<\delta n/3\,\middle|\,H<\infty}}\right)-\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\sigma^{R}_{n}|<\delta n/3\,\middle|\,H<\infty}}\right)\geq 1-2\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\text{Bin}(n,\delta)<\delta n/3}}\right),

where the first inequality is a union bound, and the last inequality comes from the fact that
\min\{|L|,|R|\}\geq\delta
when
H<\infty
.
Standard binomial concentration bounds (as in (
4
)) then provide the existence of two constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\min\{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|\}\geq\delta n/3\,\middle|\,H<\infty}}\right)\geq 1-c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}n),\quad\text{for all }n\geq 1,

which is our claim.
∎

We now conclude the proof of
4.1
. By standard self-similarity properties of the Brownian excursion
\mathfrak{e}
, under the conditional law given
(|L|,|R|,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|)
, the permutations
\sigma^{L}_{n}
and
\sigma^{R}_{n}
have the same law as two independent copies of
\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{i})_{i\leq n})
but of size
|\sigma^{L}_{n}|
and
|\sigma^{R}_{n}|
and defined in terms of two independent excursions of duration
|L|
and
|R|
, respectively.
Taking
c>0
as in (
4.7
), and setting

G:=\{H<\infty,\,\min\{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|\}\geq\delta n/3\},

we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})\geq c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\}\cup\{\LIS(\sigma^{R}_{n})\geq c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\}}}\right)

(4.10)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})<c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\,\middle|\,|L|,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma^{R}_{n})<c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\,\middle|\,|R|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})<c|\sigma^{L}_{n}|^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\,\middle|\,|L|,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma^{R}_{n})<c|\sigma^{R}_{n}|^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\,\middle|\,|R|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|}}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}_{G}}}\right]-\mathbb{P}(G^{c}),

where in the last inequality we used that on the event
G
we have that
\min\{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|,|\sigma^{R}_{n}|\}\geq\delta n/3
.
Now, observe that by Brownian scaling,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sigma_{n}^{L}\,\middle|\,|L|,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Perm\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(\overline{U}_{i})_{i\leq|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}}}\right)\,\middle|\,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}}\right),

where
(\overline{U}_{i})_{i}
is a new sequence of i.i.d. uniform random variables in
(0,1)
, independent of all other quantities.
Recalling that
\sigma_{n}=\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{i})_{i\leq n})
, we get that almost surely, for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})<c\cdot|\sigma^{L}_{n}|^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\,\middle|\,|L|,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}}\right)\cdot\mathds{1}_{G}=\mathbb{P}\Big(\LIS(\sigma_{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|})<c\cdot{|\sigma^{L}_{n}|}^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\,\Big|\,|\sigma^{L}_{n}|\Big)\cdot\mathds{1}_{G}\leq 1-q,

where the last inequality follows from the bound in (
4.7
) (recall that we chose the same constant
c>0
as in (
4.7
)).
Noting that the same argument holds with
R
instead of
L
, we conclude from (
4.10
) that, for all
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})\geq c\cdot(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\}\cup\{\LIS(\sigma^{R}_{n})\geq c\cdot(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\}}}\right)\geq 1-(1-q)^{2}-\mathbb{P}(G^{c}).

Claims
4.4
and
4.5
imply that, for each large enough
n
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\{\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})\geq c\cdot(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\}\cup\{\LIS(\sigma^{R}_{n})\geq c\cdot(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\}}}\right)\geq 1-(1-q)^{2}-2\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Since if
\LIS(\sigma^{L}_{n})\geq c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}
or
\LIS(\sigma^{R}_{n})\geq c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}
then
\LIS(\sigma_{n})\geq c(\delta n/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}
, the last estimate implies that for each large enough
n
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma_{n})\geq c\cdot(\delta/3)^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}}}\right)\geq 1-(1-q)^{2}-2\varepsilon^{\prime},

where we recall that
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\delta
were fixed right after the statement of
4.4
.
By possibly choosing a smaller constant
c
, we deduce that for each
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
there exists a constant
c(\varepsilon^{\prime})>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\LIS(\sigma_{n})\geq c(\varepsilon^{\prime})\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta}}}\right)\geq 1-(1-q)^{2}-2\varepsilon^{\prime},\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1.

(4.11)

Now, set

q^{*}:=\sup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{r\in[0,1]\,\middle|\,\text{there exists }c(r)>0\text{ s.t. }\mathbb{P}(\LIS(\sigma_{n})\geq c(r)\cdot n^{\alpha_{*}(p)-\zeta})\geq r\text{ for all }n\geq 1}}\right\},

and note that
q^{*}>0
thanks to (
4.7
). The two bounds in (
4.7
) and (
4.11
) and our definition of
q^{*}
show that if
q<q^{*}
, then
1-(1-q)^{2}\leq q^{*}
. Taking
q\to q^{*}
, we get
1-q^{*}\leq(1-q^{*})^{2}
. Noting that the only strictly positive solution of the latter inequality is
q^{*}=1
, we conclude the proof of (
4.8
).

Since (
4.8
) holds for any arbitrary
\zeta>0
, we deduce the convergence in (
4.1
).
∎

5
Estimates for the upper bound

In this section we prove two fundamental estimates (see Propositions
5.4
and
5.5
below) that will be later used in
Section

6
to upper bound the length of the longest increasing subsequence in permutations sampled from the Brownian separable permutons.

5.1
The two main estimates

Recall from
Section

1.4.1
that
\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
and
\mathcal{B}_{\oplus}
stand for the collections of negative and positive branching heights in the signed excursion
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
respectively,
i.e.
the collection of heights corresponding to the local minima of
\mathfrak{e}
decorated by a
\ominus
–sign and a
\oplus
–sign respectively. Moreover
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}_{\oplus}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
.
Recall also that
I^{t}(h)
,
h\geq 0
, denotes the open interval which contains
t
in the interval fragmentation
\mathfrak{F}_{0}(h)
introduced in (
2.1
). We set
I^{t}(h^{-})
to be equal to the interior of
\bigcap_{\ell<h}I^{t}(\ell)
.

Of particular importance will be the case of negative branching heights
\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
, for which we introduce the following additional notation. Since
\mathfrak{e}
has almost surely distinct local minima (
2.1
), almost surely, for all
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
, there is a unique time
t_{b}\in[0,1]
when
\mathfrak{e}
has a local minimum with
b=\mathfrak{e}(t_{b})
.
We define

(\ell_{b},r_{b}):=I^{t_{b}}(b^{-}),\qquad L_{b}:=(\ell_{b},t_{b}),\qquad\text{and}\qquad R_{b}:=(t_{b},r_{b}),

(5.1)

i.e.

L_{b}
and
R_{b}
are the two intervals in which the interval
I^{t_{b}}(b^{-})
is split at the branching height
b
. Finally, given
t\in[0,1]
, we also denote by
\mathcal{B}^{t}_{\ominus}
the collection of negative branching heights
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
such that
t\in(\ell_{b},r_{b})
. See
Figure

8
for an illustration.

Figure 8:
A sketch for the notation introduced in
Section

5.1
.

We now introduce a particular type of sequences; the motivation will be clarified right after their definition. Fix
p\in(0,1)
,
i.e.
fix the parameter for the signs of the signed excursion
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},p)
. Conditioning on
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s})
, let
\mathcal{D}
(for
discarding rules
, see explanations below) be the set of sequences
\eta=\{\eta_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}
, with
\eta_{b}\in\{L_{b},R_{b},\diamond\}
, such that
\eta_{b}=\diamond
if and only if there exists
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
with
b^{\prime}<b
such that
t_{b}\in\eta_{b^{\prime}}
.

Note that given
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s})
, we can think of
\eta=\{\eta_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}

as a deterministic
discarding rule
, where each
\eta_{b}
determines which side of the interval
(\ell_{b},r_{b})
we are going to discard (the case
\eta_{b}=\diamond
corresponds to the case when the interval
(\ell_{b},r_{b})
is contained in an interval that was already discarded at some smaller negative branching height).

We introduce some notation. Given
t\in[0,1]
and
b\in\mathcal{B}^{t}_{\ominus}
, we say that
\eta_{b}
does not discard
t
if
t\notin\eta_{b}
.
We want to estimate the probability of the following events defined for all
t\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

E_{\varepsilon}^{t}=E_{\varepsilon}^{t}(\eta):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\eta_{b}\text{ does not discard }t\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}^{t}_{\ominus}\text{ such that }F^{t}(b)\geq\varepsilon}}\right\}.

(5.2)

More precisely, we are interested in the case when
t
is a uniform point
U
in
(0,1)
independent of all other random quantities. The ideas used in this section to estimate the probability of the above event will then play a key role in
Section

6
.

Recall the subordinator
\xi
introduced in
2.3
. In this section we use the decomposition
\xi=\xi^{\oplus}+\xi^{\ominus}
, where
\xi^{\oplus}
(resp.
\xi^{\ominus}
) is the process determined by the jumps of
\xi
corresponding to the local minima of
\mathfrak{e}
decorated by
\oplus
–signs (resp.
\ominus
–signs).
Then, arguing as in (
3.2
), the process
\xi^{\ominus}
is a Lévy process with intensity measure on
(0,+\infty)

\Lambda^{\ominus}(\mathrm{d}x):=(1-p)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{2(1-p)\,\mathrm{e}^{x}\mathrm{d}x}{\sqrt{2\pi(\mathrm{e}^{x}-1)^{3}}}.

(5.3)

Now recall from
Remark

1.2
the definition of
\lambda^{*}(p)=1-\beta^{*}(p)
as

\lambda^{*}(p)=\sup_{\beta\in(0,\log(2)),\delta>0}\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\beta\delta\,,\,\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}(p)\}}}\right\},

(5.4)

and note that for all
\gamma<0
and all
r\in(1/2,1)
, by simple calculations, the equation (
1.4
) satisfied by
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)
can be rephrased more conveniently as

\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p))=-(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma})\Lambda^{\ominus}\big(-\log(r),-\log(1-r)\big),

(5.5)

where we recall from
Remark

2.4
that
\Phi(q)=2\sqrt{2}\frac{\Gamma(q+1/2)}{\Gamma(q)}
. Note that the above equation has a unique positive solution because as explained in
Remark

2.4
, the function
\Phi(\cdot)
is increasing,
\Phi(0)=0
,
\lim_{q\to(-1/2)^{+}}\Phi(q)=-\infty
and
-(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma})\Lambda^{\ominus}\big(-\log(r),-\log(1-r)\big)<0
for all
\gamma<0
and all
r\in(1/2,1)
.

We are going to prove (after stating and proving two additional complementary lemmas) the following upper bound for the probability of the event
E_{\varepsilon}^{U}
introduced in (
5.2
).

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. Let
\eta\in\mathcal{D}
be a discarding rule chosen in a
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s})
–measurable manner. Then,

\mathbb{P}(E_{\varepsilon}^{U})\leq 2\,\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)},\quad\text{for all }\varepsilon\in(0,1).

The following two results complement the bound obtained in the above proposition.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
p\in(0,1)
, we have that
\lambda^{*}(p)>0
.

Proof of
5.2
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
. Recall the definition of
\lambda^{*}(p)
from (
5.4
).
We show that there exist
\beta\in(0,\log(2))
and
\delta>0
such that
\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}(p)\}>0
, then the desired result follows. Fix any
\gamma_{0}<0
and
\beta_{0}\in(0,\log(2))
. Note that since
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}(p)
is defined as the only positive solution to (
5.5
) with
r=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}
, we have that
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{0},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{0}}}(p)>0
. Therefore there always exists
\delta>0
small enough such that
\gamma_{0}\delta+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{0},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{0}}}(p)>0
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

The supremum in (
5.4
) is attained, i.e. there exist
\beta=\beta(p)\in(0,\log(2))
and
\delta=\delta(p)>0
such that

\lambda^{*}(p)=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\beta\delta\,,\,\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}(p)\}}}\right\}.

Proof.

To see this, let

\mu(\delta,\beta):=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\beta\delta\,,\,\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}}(p)\}}}\right\},\quad\delta>0,\;\beta\in(0,\log(2)),

and
\delta_{n}>0,\beta_{n}\in(0,\log 2)
define two sequences such that
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\to\lambda^{*}(p)
as
n\to\infty
. Our argument is to show that we can extract from
(\delta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
and
(\beta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
converging subsequences with limits in
(0,\infty)
and
(0,\log(2))
respectively (note that the extremities of the intervals are excluded).

First of all,
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\beta_{n}\delta_{n}\leq\delta_{n}\log(2)
, and since
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\to\lambda^{*}(p)>0
(see
5.2
), it must be the case that
(\delta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is bounded away from
0
. Secondly, it is fairly easy to see that
(\beta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is bounded away from
\log(2)
. In fact, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\}\leq\sup_{\gamma<0}\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)
. But note that for all
r\in(1/2,1)
,
\sup_{\gamma<0}\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)=\kappa^{*}_{\infty,r}(p)
, where
\kappa^{*}_{\infty,r}(p)
is the unique positive solution to

\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\infty,r}(p))=-\Lambda^{\ominus}\big(-\log(r),-\log(1-r)\big).

It is clear that
\kappa^{*}_{\infty,r}(p)\to 0
as
r\to 1/2
. Therefore,
\beta_{n}\to\log(2)
(up to extraction) would imply
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\kappa^{*}_{\infty,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. This contradicts
\lambda^{*}(p)>0
.

Next, we claim that
(\beta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is bounded away from
0
. Suppose that this is not the case; without loss of generality we can assume that
\beta_{n}\to 0
. Take
n
large enough so that
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\geq\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}
. The bound
\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}\leq\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\beta_{n}\delta_{n}
shows that
\delta_{n}\to\infty
. Then, for all
n
, we pick
\gamma_{n}<0
such that,

\gamma_{n}\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\}.

(5.6)

This is possible since
\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\}\geq\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\geq\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}>0
. We first remark that, since
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\leq\frac{1}{2}
(recall that
\lim_{q\to(-1/2)^{+}}\Phi(q)=-\infty
),

\gamma_{n}\delta_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: def gamma_n}}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{2}\Big(\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\}-1\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}-1\Big),

(5.7)

and hence
\gamma_{n}\to 0
because
\gamma_{n}<0
,
\delta_{n}\to\infty
and
\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}-1\Big)\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Now by definition of
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)
,

\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p))=-(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{n}})\Lambda^{\ominus}\big(\beta_{n},-\log(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}})\big).

A simple integral estimate shows that, as
n\to\infty
,
\Lambda^{\ominus}\big(\beta_{n},-\log(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}})\big)\sim c/\sqrt{\beta_{n}}
for some positive constant
c
, whence
\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p))\sim c\gamma_{n}/\sqrt{\beta_{n}}
as
n\to\infty
. Note that by the inequality
\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}\leq\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\beta_{n}\delta_{n}
and the fact that
\gamma_{n}<0
,

\frac{\gamma_{n}}{\sqrt{\beta_{n}}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda^{*}(p)}}\cdot\gamma_{n}\sqrt{\delta_{n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: lb gammadelta}}}{{\geq}}\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda^{*}(p)}}\cdot\frac{\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}-1}{\sqrt{\delta_{n}}}.

Since
\gamma_{n}<0
, this entails
\gamma_{n}/\sqrt{\beta_{n}}\to 0
and hence
\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p))\to 0
. Therefore
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\to 0
. But this is a contradiction: indeed,

\frac{\lambda^{*}(p)}{2}\leq\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: def gamma_n}}}{{\leq}}2\gamma_{n}\delta_{n}+2\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\leq 2\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p),

(5.8)

so that we would have
\lambda^{*}(p)=0
.

Finally, we prove that
(\delta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is upper bounded using a similar argument. We argue by contradiction, assuming that
\delta_{n}\to\infty
(this is possible up to extraction). We first use the inequality
\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})\leq\sup_{\gamma<0}\{\gamma\delta_{n}+\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p)\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and we consider
\gamma_{n}<0
as in (
5.6
). Now the same argument as in (
5.7
) shows that
\gamma_{n}\to 0
. Since we know from the previous paragraph that
(\beta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is bounded away from
0
, we deduce that, as
n\to\infty
,

\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}(p))=-(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma_{n}})\Lambda^{\ominus}\big(\beta_{n},-\log(1-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}})\big)\longrightarrow 0.

Therefore
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma_{n},\mathrm{e}^{-\beta_{n}}}\to 0
and we conclude as in (
5.8
) that this is impossible.

We have proved that
(\delta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is upper bounded and bounded away from
0
, and that
(\beta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
is bounded away both from
\log(2)
and
0
. We may now conclude the proof. Up to extraction, we can assume that
(\delta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
and
(\beta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N})
converge to
\delta_{\infty}\in(0,\infty)
and
\beta_{\infty}\in(0,\log(2))
respectively. Taking a limit, we finally get
\lambda^{*}(p)=\underset{n\to\infty}{\lim}\mu(\delta_{n},\beta_{n})=\mu(\delta_{\infty},\beta_{\infty})
, which proves our statement.
∎

Proof of

.

For a constant

, we consider the sequence of branching heights defined for all

The random variables

\xi^{\ominus}
by time

(see
e.g.

[

We also set for all

A^{U}_{m}=A^{U}_{m}(r):=\{\eta_{b}\text{ does not discard }U\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}^{U}_{\ominus}\text{ such that }b\leq\tau^{U}_{m}\},

(5.10)

and we claim that for all
r\in(1/2,1)
,

and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

E_{\varepsilon}^{U}\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A^{U}_{m}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{U}(\tau_{m}^{U})\geq\varepsilon}}\right\}}}\right)\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{F^{U}(\tau_{m}^{U})<\varepsilon}}\right\}.

Indeed, if

occurs –
i.e.

, then

for all

such that

.
Hence
\eta_{b}
must not discard

for all for all

such that

.

As a consequence, for all

,

and

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{\varepsilon}^{U}}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A^{U}_{m}}}\right)+\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{U}\big(\tau_{m}^{U}\big)<\varepsilon}}\right).

In the next two crucial propositions (whose proofs are postponed to

Proposition 5.4
.

For all
r\in(1/2,1)
,
m>0

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A^{U}_{m}}}\right)\leq r^{m}.

Proposition 5.5
.

For all
r\in(1/2,1)
,
m>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{U}\big(\tau^{U}_{m}\big)<\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\inf_{\gamma<0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma m}\cdot\varepsilon^{\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)}}}\right\},

where
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)
was defined in (
5.5
).

We now conclude the proof of
5.1
. Combining the estimates in the last two propositions with (

), we get that for all
r\in(1/2,1)
,
m>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{\varepsilon}^{U}}}\right)\leq r^{m}+\inf_{\gamma<0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma m}\cdot\varepsilon^{\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)}}}\right\}.

Setting

In particular, for all

.

Proof of
5.4
.

Recall that
\eta\in\mathcal{D}
is a discarding rule chosen in a
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s})
–measurable manner.
Also recall from (
5.10
) that for all
m>0
,

A^{U}_{m}=A^{U}_{m}(r):=\{\eta_{b}\text{ does not discard }U\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}^{U}_{\ominus}\text{ such that }b\leq\tau^{U}_{m}\},

(5.13)

where
\tau_{m}^{U}=\tau_{m}^{U}(r)
was defined in (
5.9
).
We want to prove that for all
r\in(1/2,1)
,
m>0
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A^{U}_{m}}}\right)\leq r^{m}.

(5.14)

Setting

B^{U}_{m}=B^{U}_{m}(r):=\{\eta_{b}\text{ does not discard }U\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}^{U}_{\ominus}\text{ such that }b<\tau^{U}_{m}\},

(note that the inequality is strict here), we have that

A^{U}_{m}=B^{U}_{m}\cap\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\text{ does not discard }U}}\right\}.

(5.15)

We consider the following filtration, defined for all
m>0
by

\mathcal{F}_{m}:=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(I^{U}(h))_{h<\tau^{U}_{m+1}},(\tau^{U}_{s})_{s\leq m+1}}}\right).

We emphasize that, importantly, the interval
I^{U}(h)
at level
h=\tau_{m+1}^{U}
is not included in the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{m}
. Fix
m>0
. Note that:

•

B^{U}_{m}
is
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
–measurable.

•

I^{U}((\tau^{U}_{m})^{-})
is
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
–measurable because by definition it is equal to the interior of the decreasing intersection
\bigcap_{h<\tau^{U}_{m}}I^{U}(h)
.

•

L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}
and
R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}
are
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
–measurable because
\tau_{m}^{U}
is
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
–measurable and
L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}
and
R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}
are a deterministic function of
\tau_{m}^{U}
and
\mathfrak{e}
.

•

|I^{U}((\tau^{U}_{m})^{-})|=|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|+|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|
.

•

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
,
U
is uniform in
I^{U}((\tau^{U}_{m})^{-})
because
I^{U}(h)
at level
h=\tau_{m}^{U}
is not included in the definition of
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
.

•

Conditioning on
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
, the probability that
U
falls in
L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}
(resp.
R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}
) is therefore
\frac{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|+|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}
(resp.
\frac{|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|+|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}
).

The above observations entail that, almost surely, for all
m>0
,

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\,\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{\{\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\text{ does not discard }U\}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{m-1}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\,\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(U\text{ is not in the interval discarded by }\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\middle|\mathcal{F}_{m-1}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|+|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}\delta_{\{\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}=R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\}}+\frac{|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|+|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}\delta_{\{\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}=L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\,\frac{\max\{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|,|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\}|}{|L_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|+|R_{\tau_{m}^{U}}|}\leq\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\,r,

(5.16)

where the last inequality follows by definition of
\tau_{m}^{U}
.
Note also that in the second equality of the above equation we used that if
\eta_{b}
does not discard
U
for all
b\in\mathcal{B}^{U}_{\ominus}
such that
b<\tau_{m}^{U}
(which is the event
B^{U}_{m}
) then
\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\neq\diamond
by the definition of discarding rule.
Therefore, we get that for all
m>0
,

\mathbb{P}(A^{U}_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:simply_event}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\text{ does not discard }U\}}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\mathds{1}_{\{\eta_{\tau_{m}^{U}}\text{ does not discard }U\}}\Big|\mathcal{F}_{m-1}\Big)}}\right]\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq: sec 4 uniform re-sampling}}}{{\leq}}r\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m}}}}\right]\leq r\cdot\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathds{1}_{B^{U}_{m-1}}\mathds{1}_{\{\eta_{\tau_{m-1}^{U}}\text{ does not discard }U\}}}}\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:simply_event}}}{{=}}r\cdot\mathbb{P}(A^{U}_{m-1}),

where in the second equality we used that
\mathds{1}_{B_{m}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{m-1}
–measurable.
Iterating the same argument, we retrieve (
5.14
).
∎

Proof of
5.5
.

We want to prove that for all
r\in(1/2,1)
,
m>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{U}\big(\tau^{U}_{m}\big)<\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\inf_{\gamma<0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma m}\cdot\varepsilon^{\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)}}}\right\},

where
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)
was defined in (
5.5
).
We fix
r\in(1/2,1)
,
m>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Let

\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{b\in\mathcal{B}^{U}_{\ominus}\;\middle|\;F^{U}(b^{-})\geq\varepsilon\text{ and }\frac{\max\{|L_{b}|,|R_{b}|\}}{|L_{b}|+|R_{b}|}\leq r}}\right\}.

We start by noticing that
\{F^{U}(\tau^{U}_{m})<\varepsilon\}\subseteq\{\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}\leq m\}
. By Chernoff bound, for all
\gamma<0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{U}\big(\tau^{U}_{m}\big)<\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}\leq m}}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma m}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}}}}\right].

(5.17)

Fix
\gamma<0
. Recall the notation
\Delta\xi^{\ominus}_{s}=\xi^{\ominus}_{s}-\xi^{\ominus}_{s^{-}}
, where the process
\xi^{\ominus}_{s}
was introduced above (
5.3
).
We claim that

\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}=\sum_{s>0}\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi^{\ominus}_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})},

(5.18)

where
r_{0}=-\log(r)
and
r_{1}=-\log(1-r)
.
Indeed, note that for all
b\in\mathcal{B}^{U}_{\ominus}
,
F^{U}(b^{-})\geq\varepsilon
if and only if
\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)
, with
s=\rho(b)
.
Moreover, we have that
\max\{|L_{b}|,|R_{b}|\}\leq r(|L_{b}|+|R_{b}|)
if and only if
\Delta\xi^{\ominus}_{s}\in(-\log(r),-\log(1-r))
, as already explained below (
5.9
).
Hence we proved the equality (
5.18
). The latter implies that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma\sum_{s>0}\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi^{\ominus}_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})}}}\right\}}}\right].

(5.19)

By definition of
\xi^{\ominus}
, the law of
\xi^{\ominus}
is obtained from the law of
\xi
by keeping the jumps of
\xi
according to i.i.d. coin tosses with success probability
1-p
. More precisely, if
(\chi_{s},s>0)
denotes a collection of i.i.d. coin flips, with
\mathbb{P}(\chi=1)=1-p=1-\mathbb{P}(\chi=0)
, and that is further independent of
\xi
, we have the identity in law

\sum_{s>0}\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi^{\ominus}_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})}\overset{d}{=}\sum_{s>0}\chi_{s}\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})}.

(5.20)

We combine equations (
5.19
) and (
5.20
) into

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma\sum_{s>0}\chi_{s}\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})}}}\right\}}}\right].

Using the independence of
\chi
and
\xi
, and the fact that
\chi
is a collection of i.i.d. Bernoulli variables, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{s>0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+(1-p)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})}}}\right\}}}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\sum_{s>0}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+(1-p)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{\Delta\xi_{s}\in(r_{0},r_{1})}}}\right\}}}\right)}}\right\}}}\right].

Using the exponential formula for
\xi
(see for instance
[
RY13
, Proposition XII.1.12]
), the previous display reduces to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda(dx)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p+(1-p)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma\mathds{1}_{\xi_{s^{-}}\leq\log(1/\varepsilon)}\mathds{1}_{x\in(r_{0},r_{1})}}}\right\}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right\}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-T_{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Lambda(dx)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-p-(1-p)\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\gamma\mathds{1}_{x\in(r_{0},r_{1})}}}\right\}}}\right)}}\right\}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-T_{\log(1/\varepsilon)}(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma})\Lambda^{\ominus}(r_{0},r_{1})}}\right\}}}\right],

where in the last equality we used that for an indicator
\mathds{1}_{A}
,
1-p-(1-p)\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\mathds{1}_{A}}=(1-p)(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma})\mathds{1}_{A}
and that
\Lambda^{\ominus}(\mathrm{d}x)=(1-p)\Lambda(\mathrm{d}x)
from (
5.3
).
Now let
\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)
be defined as in (
5.5
),
i.e.
as the only positive solution to the equation
\Phi(-\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p))=-(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma})\Lambda^{\ominus}(r_{0},r_{1})
. Using the second claim (
2.6
) in
2.5
with
a=(1-\mathrm{e}^{\gamma})\Lambda^{\ominus}(r_{0},r_{1})
and
b=0
, we deduce the upper bound
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathrm{e}^{\gamma\#\mathcal{J}^{U}_{\varepsilon,r}}}}\right]\leq\varepsilon^{\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p)}
.
Going back to our Chernoff estimate in (
5.17
), we deduce that
\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{U}\big(\tau^{U}_{m}\big)<\varepsilon}}\right)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma m}\varepsilon^{\kappa^{*}_{\gamma,r}(p,q)}
.
Optimizing over
\gamma<0
yields the desired claim.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

Our estimate in
5.5
can be slightly improved by dealing directly with the left-hand side of (
5.17
). The idea is to use the Lamperti representation (
2.3
) to rephrase the event
\{F^{U}(\tau_{m}^{U})<\varepsilon\}
in terms of the underlying Lévy process, and then use a thinning argument to select the special jumps corresponding to the times
\tau_{i}^{U}
. This would lead to a slightly better (but uglier) upper bound for the exponent for
\LIS(\Perm(\bm{\mu}_{p},n))
.

We did not follow this other route, since we found the current argument slightly cleaner and in any case, both arguments provide bounds which are quite far from the actual behavior of
\LIS(\Perm(\bm{\mu}_{p},n))
.

6
Upper bound for sequences sampled from the Brownian separable permutons

The main goal of this section is to prove the upper bound in
1.1
, completing the proof of the theorem. Note that in
5.2
we proved that
\lambda^{*}(p)>0
, and so that
\beta^{*}(p)=1-\lambda^{*}(p)<1
for all
p\in(0,1)
. Hence, in order to complete the proof of
1.1
it remains to prove the upper bound in the second item in the theorem statement. This is done in the next proposition.

Proposition 6.1
.

Fix
p\in(0,1)
and let
\beta^{*}(p)=1-\lambda^{*}(p)
be as in
Remark

1.2
. Let
\sigma_{n}
be a random permutation of size
n\in\mathbb{N}
sampled from the Brownian separable permuton
\bm{\mu}_{p}
. Then for all
\beta>\beta^{*}(p)
, the following convergence in probability holds

\frac{\LIS(\sigma_{n})}{n^{\beta}}\to 0.

Proof.

We split the proof in six steps. In what follows,
w.h.p.
means
with probability tending to one when
n\to\infty
.

Step 0
: Fixing the notation and setting our goal.

(a)

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
(U_{j})_{j\leq n}
be a sequence of i.i.d. uniform random variables on
(0,1)
and recall from
Section

2.2
that
\sigma_{n}=\Perm(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{j})_{j\leq n})
;

(b)

Fix
\zeta>0
(small), and set
\varepsilon=n^{-1+\zeta}
;

(c)

Let
r=r(p)\in(1/2,1)
and
\delta=\delta(p)>0
be as in
5.6
;

(d)

Finally, with the above choice of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
, set
m=\delta\log(1/\varepsilon)
.

We will show that w.h.p.
\LIS(\sigma_{n})
is at most
4\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+3\zeta}
. Note that this would be enough to conclude the proof.

Step 1
: Introducing the discarding rule
\eta
.

Recall the definition of the set of possible discarding rules
\mathcal{D}
from the beginning of
Section

5.1
and the notation in
Figure

8
.
Let
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}
be the set of points in
(U_{j})_{j\leq n}
corresponding to the longest increasing subsequence in
\sigma_{n}
(if there are multiple ones, we choose one arbitrarily).
Let
\eta=\{\eta_{b}\}_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}}
be the selection rule corresponding to this longest increasing subsequence. More precisely, let us choose
\eta\in\mathcal{D}
in the following way for all
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
:

•

if
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}\cap L_{b}\neq\emptyset
then set
\eta_{b}=R_{b}
;

•

if
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}\cap R_{b}\neq\emptyset
then set
\eta_{b}=L_{b}
;

•

if
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}\cap L_{b}=\emptyset
and
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}\cap R_{b}=\emptyset
and there exists
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
with
b^{\prime}\leq b
such that
b\in\eta_{b^{\prime}}
then set
\eta_{b}=\diamond
, otherwise set
\eta_{b}=L_{b}
(note that this is an arbitrary choice).

Here we emphasize that
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}\cap L_{b}
and
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}\cap R_{b}
cannot both be non-empty, since
\{U^{*}_{\ell}\}
is increasing and
b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ominus}
. Note also that
\eta
is chosen in a
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s},(U_{j})_{j\leq n})
–measurable manner.

Recall now the definition of
\tau_{m}^{U}=\tau_{m}^{U}(r)
from (
5.9
) and recall that
I^{U}(\tau_{m}^{U})
denotes the interval containing
U
at height
\tau_{m}^{U}
in the interval fragmentation introduce in (
2.1
). For ease of notation, we introduce the more compact notation
I^{j}_{m}:=I^{U_{j}}(\tau_{m}^{U_{j}})
for intervals and
F^{j}_{m}:=F^{U_{j}}(\tau_{m}^{U_{j}})
for their lengths.

Step 2
: W.h.p., at most
n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
of the intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in[n]}}\right\}
have length smaller than
\varepsilon
.

From
5.6
and our choice of the constants in Step 0, we have that for all
j\leq n
,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(F^{j}_{m}<\varepsilon}}\right)\leq 2\,\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)}.

(6.1)

Now set
S_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{F^{j}_{m}<\varepsilon}
,
i.e.

S_{n}
counts the number of intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in[n]}}\right\}
having length smaller than
\varepsilon
. By Markov’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S_{n}>n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}}}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[S_{n}}}\right]}{n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bound_2}}}{{\leq}}\frac{n\cdot 2\,\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)}}{n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}}=2\,n^{-\zeta(1-\lambda^{*}(p))},

(6.2)

where in the last equality we used that
\varepsilon=n^{-1+\zeta}
.

Step 3
: W.h.p., the total length of the non-discarded intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in[n]}}\right\}
is at most
\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)-\zeta}
.

Let
J
be the set of indexes

J:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{j\in[n]\,\middle|\,\eta_{b}\text{ does not discard }U_{j}\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}^{U_{j}}_{\ominus}\text{ such that }b\leq\tau^{U_{j}}_{m}}}\right\},

and
\mathcal{J}
be a subset of
J
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in\mathcal{J}}}\right\}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in J}}\right\},

and all the intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in\mathcal{J}}}\right\}
are pairwise disjoint (note that by definition if
U_{i}\in I^{j}_{m}
then
I^{i}_{m}=I^{j}_{m}
). Note also that by definition,
\LIS(\sigma_{n})
is upper bounded by the cardinality of
J
.

Let now
V
be an additional uniform random variables on
(0,1)
sampled independently from all other random quantities.
Note that if
V
is contained in
I^{j}_{m}
for some
j\in\mathcal{J}
, then the event

A^{V}_{m}=\{\eta_{b}\text{ does not discard }V\text{ for all }b\in\mathcal{B}^{V}_{\ominus}\text{ such that }b\leq\tau^{V}_{m}\},

occurs.
Hence, since
V
is uniform and independent of everything else, setting
\mathcal{L}_{m}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}F^{j}_{m}
,
i.e.

\mathcal{L}_{m}
is the total length of the non-discarded intervals among
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in[n]}}\right\}
, we get

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{L}_{m}}}\right]=\mathbb{P}\Bigg(V\in\bigcup_{j\in\mathcal{J}}I^{j}_{m}\Bigg)\leq\mathbb{P}(A^{V}_{m}).

Recall that the discarding rule
\eta
from Step 1 depends only on
(\mathfrak{e},\mathfrak{s})
and
(U_{j})_{j\leq n}
, and
V
is uniform and independent from everything else. By
5.6
(applied under the conditional law given
(U_{j})_{j\leq n}
) and our choice of the constants in Step 0,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{L}_{m}}}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}(A^{V}_{m})=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A^{V}_{m}\,\middle|\,(U_{j})_{j\leq n}}}\right)}}\right]\leq 2\,\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)}.

Hence, using Markov’s inequality, we get that

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}_{m}>\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)-\zeta})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathcal{L}_{m}}}\right]}{\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)-\zeta}}\leq 2\,{\varepsilon^{\zeta}}.

Step 4
: W.h.p., the cardinality of
\mathcal{J}
is at most
2\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
. Moreover, w.h.p. the union of the intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in\mathcal{J}}}\right\}
can be covered by at most
4\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
intervals of length
\varepsilon
with endpoints in
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}
.

By Step 2, w.h.p., at most
n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
of the intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in\mathcal{J}}}\right\}
have length smaller than
\varepsilon
. And so, the union of all these intervals can be covered by at most
2\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
intervals of length
\varepsilon
with endpoints in
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}
.

By Step 3, w.h.p., the total size
\mathcal{L}_{m}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}F^{j}_{m}
of the non-discarded intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in\mathcal{J}}}\right\}
is at most
\varepsilon^{\lambda^{*}(p)-\zeta}
. Hence, recalling that
\varepsilon=n^{-1+\zeta}
, there are at most
\varepsilon^{-1+\lambda^{*}(p)-\zeta}\leq n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
intervals in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{I^{j}_{m},j\in\mathcal{J}}}\right\}
having length bigger than
\varepsilon
and the union of all these intervals can be covered by at most
2\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
intervals of length
\varepsilon
with endpoints in
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}
.

Step 5
: W.h.p., the cardinality of
J
(which is an upper bound for
\LIS(\sigma_{n})
) is at most
4\cdot n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+3\,\zeta}
.

It remains to deal with the possible discrepancy between the cardinality of
\mathcal{J}
and
J
.
Let
I_{\varepsilon}
be a deterministic interval of size
\varepsilon
and with endpoints in
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}
. Note that the number of uniform variables among
(U_{j})_{j\in[n]}
which fall in
I_{\varepsilon}
follows a binomial distribution
\text{Bin}(n,\varepsilon)
. Therefore, for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{2\zeta}}}\right)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}\big(\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I_{\varepsilon}\}\geq n^{2\zeta}\big)

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(n,\varepsilon)\geq n^{2\zeta}).

Now recalling that
\varepsilon=n^{-1+\zeta}
, we get by Chernov’s bound that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{2\zeta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\min_{\gamma>0}\{\exp(-\gamma n^{2\zeta})\mathbb{E}[\exp(\gamma\text{Bin}(n,n^{-1+\zeta}))]\}

\displaystyle=1-\min_{\gamma>0}\{\exp(-\gamma n^{2\zeta})(1-n^{-1+\zeta}+\exp(\gamma)n^{-1+\zeta})^{n}\}

\displaystyle\geq 1-c_{1}\cdot\exp(-c_{2}n^{\zeta}),

(6.3)

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are two constants. Letting
(I^{i}_{\varepsilon})_{i\in[\varepsilon^{-1}]}
denote the collection of
\lceil\varepsilon^{-1}\rceil
intervals of size
\varepsilon
with endpoints in
\varepsilon\mathbb{Z}
covering
(0,1)
, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\forall i\in[\varepsilon^{-1}],\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I^{i}_{\varepsilon}\}<n^{2\zeta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\,\,\geq\,\,1-\lceil\varepsilon^{-1}\rceil\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\#\{j\in[n],\,U_{j}\in I_{\varepsilon}\}\geq n^{2\zeta}}}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:chern_bnd}}}{{\geq}}1-c_{1}n^{1-\zeta}\cdot\exp(-c_{2}n^{\zeta}),

where in the last inequality we also used that
\varepsilon=n^{-1+\zeta}
.

By this estimate and the estimate in Step 4, combined with a union bound, we get that w.h.p. the total number of points
(U_{j})_{j\leq n}
in the union of the intervals
\{I^{j}_{m}:j\in\mathcal{J}\}
is at most
n^{2\zeta}\cdot 4\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+\zeta}
. In particular, w.h.p., the cardinality of
J
(which is an upper bound for
\LIS(\sigma_{n})
) is at most
4\,n^{1-\lambda^{*}(p)+3\zeta}
.
∎

A natural question in light of our results in

2.2

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k\log(2),\log(\overline{\LIS_{k}(p)})}}\right)
for

1.5
