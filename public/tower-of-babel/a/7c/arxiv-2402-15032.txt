Analysis of Conformally Invariant Energies on four-Dimensional Hypersurfaces
Yann Bernard

Abstract




Abstract:

We discuss a large class of conformally invariant curvature energies for immersed hypersurfaces of dimension 4. The class under study includes various examples that have appeared in the recent literature and which arise from different contexts. We show that under natural small-energy hypotheses, critical points satisfy improved energy estimates. Nearly all the PDEs which we consider are quasilinear and fourth-order in the mean curvature. We approach the problem à la T. Rivière by generating first from Noether’s theorem divergence-free “potentials”, and then by exhibiting an underlying analytically favourable algebraic structure relating them. Similar ideas are used to obtain energy estimates for the Weyl tensor of a Bach-flat immersion. Finally, we show how to use Noether’s theorem and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem to construct on a hypersurface non-trivial divergence-free and Codazzi symmetric two-tensors of order
\mathcal{O}(|h|^{3})
, where
h
denotes the second fundamental form.

I
Introduction and Main Results

I.1
Divergence-free and Codazzi Tensors in Dimension Four

Noether’s theorem is a powerful tool that associates divergence-free quantities to the invariance of some energy. In two-dimensional submanifold geometry, for example, the Codazzi identity may be seen as an application of Noether’s principle. Let us illustrate this point. Let
\vec{\Phi}:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a smooth immersion of a 2-dimensional immersed oriented surface into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We let
\vec{n}
be the corresponding outward unit-normal vector we let
\nabla
be the covariant derivative associated with the pullback metric
g:=\vec{\Phi}^{*}g_{\text{Eucl}}
. In local coordinates, the second fundamental form and mean curvature satisfy

h^{ij}\>=\;\vec{n}\cdot\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}\vec{\Phi}\qquad\text{and}\qquad H\;:=\;\dfrac{1}{2}g^{ij}h_{ij}\>.

An important quantity is the Gauss curvature
2K:=4H^{2}-|h|^{2}
. It is in fact proportional to the scalar curvature and is thus an intrinsic quantity. A remarkable property of
K
is that if
\Sigma
is compact and without boundary, it holds

\int_{\Sigma}K\,d\text{vol}_{g}\;=\;\text{constant}\>.

The constant is precisely determined by the topology of
\Sigma
. This is known as the Gauss-Bonnet theorem. In accordance with this theorem, any reasonable immersion of
\Sigma
will be a critical point of the energy
\int_{\Sigma}K
. As we will show, applying an infinitesimal translation of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
on the Gauss-Bonnet energy yields the existence of a divergence-free symmetric two-tensor

F^{ab}\;=\;h^{ab}-2Hg^{ab}\>.

Of course
\nabla_{b}F^{ab}=0
is equivalent to the Codazzi identity. One interesting feature of the tensor
F
is that it satisfies

F\;=\;T-g\text{Tr}_{g}T

where
T\equiv h
is a Codazzi tensor, namely its derivative is symmetric:
\nabla^{a}h^{bc}=\nabla^{b}h^{ac}
. In fact, as a generic trivial observation, let us note that a Codazzi tensor
T
always generates a divergence-free tensor:

\nabla_{b}(T^{ab}-g^{ab}\text{Tr}_{g}T\big)\;=\;0\>.

However, from a divergence-free tensor, there is no recipe to devise a Codazzi tensor.

What happens in higher dimension? This paper is concerned with four-dimensional manifolds immersed into
\mathbb{R}^{5}
with the Euclidean metric. In this situation, the Gauss-Bonnet theorem subsists with
K
replaced by
\text{det}_{g}h
. The same procedure as in 2d may be applied to obtain a divergence-free tensor of order
\mathcal{O}(|h|^{3})
. In that case however, there is no generic Codazzi tensor of order
\mathcal{O}(|h|^{3})
, except in a very special case: when the manifold is locally conformally flat. We will in due time elaborate further on this condition. It can be summarized as follows. The expressions for the Riemann, Ricci, and scalar curvature tensors are well-known
[
H
]
:

R_{ijkl}\,=\,h_{ik}h_{jl}-h_{il}h_{jk}\quad,\quad R_{ik}\,=\,R_{ijk}^{\>\>\>\>\>\>j}\,=\,4Hh_{ik}-(h^{2})_{ik}\quad,\quad R\,=\,R^{i}_{i}\,=\,16H^{2}-|h|^{2}\>.

The Schouten tensor is

P_{ik}\;:=\;R_{ik}-\dfrac{R}{6}g_{ik}\>,

while the Weyl tensor is

W_{ijkl}\;=\;R_{ijkl}+\dfrac{1}{2}\big(P_{ik}g_{jl}+P_{jl}P_{ik}-P_{jk}g_{il}-P_{il}g_{jk}\big)\>.

Two-dimensional surfaces can always be covered by local conformal charts (i.e. coordinates in which the metric becomes proportional to the identity). In higher dimensions however, a constraint emerges. A four-dimensional manifold is locally conformally flat if and only if its Weyl tensor vanishes. One shows that this implies that the Schouten tensor is a Codazzi tensor:

\nabla^{a}P^{bc}\;=\;\nabla^{b}P^{ac}\>.

In that case, the corresponding divergence-free tensor is the Einstein tensor:

E^{ab}\;:=\;P^{ab}-g^{ab}\text{Tr}_{g}P\>.

These tensors are of order
\mathcal{O}(|h|^{2})
.

III.1
Notational Interlude

We fix a basis
\{e^{i}\}_{i=1,\cdots,N}
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and an orientation. Suppose
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is equipped with a metric
\mathfrak{g}
. Let
\Lambda^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
be the corresponding vector space of
k
-forms. We will need component expansions of certain operations involving forms. A typical element
A\in\Lambda^{p}
may be expanded in tensorial notation as

A\;=\;\dfrac{1}{p!}\sum_{\text{all indices}}A_{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}\,e^{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e^{i_{p}}\>,

where
A_{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}
is totally antisymmetric. For notational convenience, we will render this as

A\;\equiv\;A_{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}\>.

Forms are tensors: we raise and lower indices as usual using the metric
\mathfrak{g}
. Of course, as forms are anti-symmetric, the relative position of indices is essential: we follow standard conventions.

The inner product on forms will be denoted

\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathfrak{g}}\;=\;\dfrac{1}{p!}A^{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}B_{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}\qquad\text{for}\quad A\in\Lambda^{p}\>\>,\>\>B\in\Lambda^{p}\>.

The bracket symbol
[\ldots]
will denote scaled total permutations. We conventionally set the wedge product to be

A\wedge B\;\equiv\;\dfrac{(p+q)!}{p!q!}A_{[i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}B_{j_{1}\ldots j_{q}]}\qquad\text{for}\quad A\in\Lambda^{p}\>\>,\>\>B\in\Lambda^{q}\>.

The
interior multiplication
(restricted to our interest) is the bilinear operation
\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\;:\;\Lambda^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times\Lambda^{p-1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\rightarrow\Lambda^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
which satisfies in particular

A\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits B\;\equiv\;\dfrac{1}{(p-1)!}A^{i_{1}\ldots i_{p}}B_{i_{1}\ldots i_{p-1}}\qquad\text{for}\quad A\in\Lambda^{p}\>\>,\>\>B\in\Lambda^{p-1}\>.

The
first-order contraction operator

\bullet:\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\times\Lambda^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\rightarrow\Lambda^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
is defined as follows. For a
2
-form
{A}
and a
1
-form
{B}
, we set

{A}\bullet{B}:={A}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits B\>.

For a
p
-form
{B}
and a
q
-form
{C}
, we have

{A}\bullet({B}\wedge{C}):=({A}\bullet{B})\wedge{C}+(-1)^{pq}({A}\bullet{C})\wedge{B}\>.

This operation will be used for forms with values in
\Lambda^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{5})
.

We will also need an operation taking a pair a two-forms to a two-form, namely:

A\odot B\;\equiv\;A^{i_{2}j}B_{j}^{\>\>i_{1}}-A^{i_{1}j}B_{j}^{\>\>i_{2}}\qquad\text{for}\quad A,B\in\Lambda^{2}\>.

Being given an orientation and a metric, we can equip our manifold with the volume form
\epsilon\in\Lambda^{N}
via
\epsilon^{i_{1}\ldots i_{N}}:=|\mathfrak{g}|^{1/2}\mbox{sign}\begin{pmatrix}1&2&\cdots&N\\
i_{1}&i_{2}&\cdots&i_{N}\end{pmatrix}
. With it, we define the Hodge star operator
\star_{\mathfrak{g}}:\Lambda^{p}\rightarrow\Lambda^{N-p}
as usual. In components, we have

\star_{\mathfrak{g}}A\;\equiv\;\dfrac{1}{p!}\,\epsilon^{i_{1}\ldots i_{N-p}j_{1}\ldots j_{p}}A_{j_{1}\ldots j_{p}}\qquad\text{for}\quad A\in\Lambda^{p}\>.

Note that

\star_{\mathfrak{g}}\star_{\mathfrak{g}}A=(-1)^{p(N-p)}A\>.

If a connection
\nabla
compatible with the metric
\mathfrak{g}
is provided, we define in accordance with the Leibniz rule, the covariant exterior derivative
d
of a
p
-form
A
in components as

dA\;:=\;(p+1)\nabla_{[i_{0}}A_{i_{1}...i_{p}]}\;\in\;\Lambda^{p+1}\>.

We also define the codifferential
d^{\star_{\mathfrak{g}}}:=\star_{\mathfrak{g}}d\star_{\mathfrak{g}}
, whose components take the expression

d^{\star_{\mathfrak{g}}}A=\nabla^{j}A_{ji_{1}...i_{p-1}}\;\in\;\Lambda^{p-1}\>.

For any
p
-form
A
, it holds

d^{2}A\;=\;0\;=\;(d^{\star_{\mathfrak{g}}})^{2}A\>.

We will work within the context of multivector-valued forms. Our objects will be elements of
\Lambda^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{4},\Lambda^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{5}))
. To distinguish operations taking place in the parameter space
(\mathbb{R}^{4},g)
from operations taking place in the ambient space
(\mathbb{R}^{5},g_{\text{Eucl}})
, we will superimpose the operators defined above with the convention that the one on the bottom acts on parameter space, while the one at the top acts on ambient space.
Typically, consider the two-vector-valued two-form
\vec{\eta}\in\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{4},\Lambda^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{5}))
whose components are given by

\vec{\eta}_{ij}\;=\;\nabla_{i}\vec{\Phi}\wedge\nabla_{j}\vec{\Phi}\>.

We will write more succinctly

\vec{\eta}\;=:\;\dfrac{1}{2}d\vec{\Phi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\wedge}}{{\wedge}}d\vec{\Phi}\>.

Similar quantities are defined following the same logic. Naturally, for multivector 0-forms, the bottom symbol will be omitted, while for 0-vector-valued forms, the symbol on the top will be omitted instead. The notation
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle
is reserved for
\langle\cdot\,,\cdot\rangle_{g}
. The symbol
\star
is reserved for
\star_{0}
. In other words, the Hodge star operator used in the present article is intrinsic. This shall ease notation greatly and hopefully awake no confusion.

A.3
The case
\mathcal{F}={det}_{g}h
and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem: proof of Theorem
I.1

This case is of course instructive since by Gauss-Bonnet, our energy is a constant (we assume no boundary) and we therefore would expect the corresponding stress-energy tensor to vanish identically. In other words, since
\int\text{det}_{g}h
is a characteristic class, its variations should be identically vanishing. This is easily verified by brute force in dimension 2, but, as we will see, the four-dimensional case is much less evident.

A.3.1
Dimension 2

In dimension 2, the Cayley-Hamilton theorem (or Gauss equation for instance) yields that

-2\text{det}_{g}h\;=\;|h|^{2}-4H^{2}\>,

where
\text{det}_{g}h\equiv K
is the Gauss curvature.

The variation of the functional
\int_{\Sigma}\mathcal{F}d\text{vol}_{g}
with
\mathcal{F}:=|h|^{2}-4H^{2}
is found using M.M. Müller’s procedure.
We have easily that

\dfrac{\partial{\mathcal{F}}}{\partial g_{ab}}\;=\;-2(h^{2})^{ab}+4Hh^{ab}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dfrac{\partial{\mathcal{F}}}{\partial h_{ab}}\;=\;2h^{ab}-4Hg^{ab}\>.

G^{ab}\;=\;2(\text{det}_{g}h)g^{ab}+2(h^{2})^{ab}-4Hh^{ab}\;\equiv\;0\>,

by the Cayley-Hamilton theorem. Moreover,

F^{ab}\;=\;2h^{ab}-4Hg^{ab}\>,

so that by the Codazzi equation we find

\nabla_{a}F^{ab}\;=\;0\>.

The Noether field associated with the characteristic class
\int_{\Sigma}K\,d\text{vol}_{g}

G^{ab}\nabla_{b}\vec{\Phi}-\vec{n}\nabla_{b}F^{ab}\>,

is indeed identically vanishing.

We have seen in Section

.

The stress-energy vector field is

\vec{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{E}}\;:=\;G_{\mathcal{E}}^{ab}\nabla_{b}\vec{\Phi}-\vec{n}\nabla_{b}F^{ab}_{\mathcal{E}}\>.

For a critical point, we have
