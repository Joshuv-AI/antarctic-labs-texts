Reversible Target-Binding Kinetics of Multiple Impatient Particles
Denis S. Grebenkov, Aanjaneya Kumar

Abstract
Certain biochemical reactions can only be triggered after binding of a sufficient number of particles to a specific target region such as an enzyme or a protein sensor. We investigate the distribution of the reaction time, i.e., the first instance when all independently diffusing particles are bound to the target. When each particle binds irreversibly, this is equivalent to the first-passage time of the slowest (last) particle. In turn, reversible binding to the target renders the problem much more challenging and drastically changes the distribution of the reaction time. We derive the exact solution of this problem and investigate the short-time and long-time asymptotic behaviors of the reaction time probability density. We also analyze how the mean reaction time depends on the unbinding rate and the number of particles. Our exact and asymptotic solutions are compared to Monte Carlo simulations.

In spite of numerous advantages of the LMA, it relies on a rough

the problem of impatient particles in the case

. Using the
renewal approach, we derive the exact solution for this problem. We
deduce the short-time and long-time behavior of this solution and
compare it with the LMA predictions, as well as to Monte Carlo
simulations. The short-time asymptotic analysis is also extended to
arbitrary

of the reaction time distribution at moderate and long times.
However, the LMA overestimates the mean reaction time and the decay
time, and fails at short times. From the practical point of view, the
LMA can thus be used for qualitative estimations but further
improvements are necessary for getting more accurate results.

II.2
Exact solution

To proceed, we introduce the probability
\mathcal{P}_{t}(n|m)
that starting
from
m
particles bound to the target at time
0
, there are
n

particles bound to the target at time
t
. This probability is hard
to compute in general due to unbinding events. However, there are two
particular cases for which
\mathcal{P}_{t}(n|m)
can be expressed in terms of a
single particle dynamics. Let
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
denote the occupancy
probability that an initially free particle that started from a point

\bm{x}_{0}
is bound to the target at time
t
. Since all particles are
independent, the probability of finding
m
bound particles on the
target at time
t
is

\mathcal{P}_{t}(m|0)={N\choose m}[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{m}\big[1-P(t|\bm{x}_{0})\big]^{N-m},

(11)

where we have chosen the initial condition that all particles are
initially free. Similarly, if
Q(t)
denotes the probability that,
starting from the bound state at time
0
, the particle is bound to
the target at time
t
, then

\mathcal{P}_{t}(m|N)={N\choose m}[Q(t)]^{m}\big[1-Q(t)\big]^{N-m}.

(12)

The probability density of the reaction time
\mathcal{T}_{N}
can then be
obtained from a standard renewal equation:

\mathcal{P}_{t}(N|0)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,H_{N}(t^{\prime})\,\mathcal{P}_{t-t^{\prime}}(N|N).

(13)

Switching to Laplace space allows us to get

\tilde{H}_{N}(p|\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{\mathcal{L}\{[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}}{\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}}\,,

(14)

where both
\mathcal{L}
and tilde denote the Laplace transform, e.g.

\tilde{f}(p)=\mathcal{L}[f(t)]=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-pt}\,f(t).

The inversion of the Laplace transform gives the probability density
in time domain:

H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\biggr\{\frac{\mathcal{L}\{[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}}{\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}}\biggr\}.

(15)

The last step consists in relating the probabilities
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

Q(t)
to the first-passage time statistics of a single particle.
This can be done in a standard way by summing contributions according
to the number of unbinding events (see, e.g.,
Reva21
). For
instance, one finds

Q(t)=\Psi(t)+\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\,\psi(t_{1})\int\limits_{t_{1}}^{t}dt^{\prime}_{1}\,H(t^{\prime}_{1}-t_{1})\,\Psi(t-t^{\prime}_{1})+\ldots,

where
\Psi(t)=e^{-k_{\rm off}t}
is the probability of staying in the
bound state up to time
t
,
\psi(t)=-d\Psi(t)/dt=k_{\rm off}e^{-k_{\rm off}t}
is the probability density of the associated waiting time, and

H(t)
is the probability density of the rebinding time. The first
term in the above equation is the contribution without unbinding. In
the second term, the particle unbinds at time
t_{1}
, diffuses in the
bulk until the next rebinding at time
t^{\prime}_{1}
, and remains bound until
time
t
. The third and next terms correspond to 2, 3, etc. unbinding
events. In Laplace domain, one simply gets

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{\Psi}(p)+\tilde{\psi}(p)\tilde{H}(p)\tilde{\Psi}(p)+\ldots=\frac{\tilde{\Psi}(p)}{1-\tilde{\psi}(p)\tilde{H}(p)}

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p+k_{\rm off}(1-\tilde{H}(p))}\,.

In turn, the occupancy probability
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
includes an additional
step of the first-passage to the target that yields:

\displaystyle\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})\,\tilde{Q}(p)

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})}{p+k_{\rm off}(1-\tilde{H}(p))}\,,

(18)

where
\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
is the Laplace transform of the probability
density
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of the first-passage time to the target when the
particle started from a point
\bm{x}_{0}
.
Note that as the particle is released after unbinding from a uniformly
distributed point on the target boundary
\Gamma
, one also gets

H(t)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\int\limits_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}_{0}\,H(t|\bm{x}_{0}),

(19)

where
|\Gamma|
is the Lebesgue measure of
\Gamma
(e.g., the area of
\Gamma

in the three-dimensional case). In this way, both probabilities

P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
Q(t)
are expressed in terms of the first-passage
time probability density
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
for a single particle.
In the case
N=1
, comparison of Eqs. (
14
,

17
) yields immediately that
H_{1}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, as
expected.

II.3
Spectral decompositions

As we deal with restricted diffusion in a bounded domain, the
probabilities
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
Q(t)
can be formally deduced from
their Laplace transforms by applying the residue theorem. Let

\{p_{n}\}
be the poles of
\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
that lie on the
negative
real axis:
0=p_{0}>p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\searrow-\infty
.
According to Eq. (
18
), these poles satisfy the
equation:

p_{n}+k_{\rm off}(1-\tilde{H}(p_{n}))=0.

(20)

Note that since the poles of
\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
and
\tilde{H}(p)

are the same, they cancel each other in Eq. (
18
) and
thus are not included in the set of poles of
\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
.
If all the poles are simple, the inverse Laplace transform yields

P(t|\bm{x}_{0})=P_{\infty}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}v_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})\,e^{p_{n}t},

(21)

where
v_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})
is the residue of
\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
evaluated at
the pole
p_{n}
. The steady-state limit
P_{\infty}
corresponds to the
pole at
0
, which can be obtained by using the Taylor expansion

\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})=\langle e^{-p\tau}\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}=1-p\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}+O(p^{2}),

(22)

where
\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}
is the mean FPT to the target for a
single particle started from
\bm{x}_{0}
. Similarly,
\tilde{H}(p)=1-p\langle\tau\rangle+O(p^{2})
, where
\langle\tau\rangle
is the mean
rebinding time:

\langle\tau\rangle=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\int\limits_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}_{0}\,\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}.

(23)

As a consequence, Eq. (
18

P_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1+k_{\rm off}\langle\tau\rangle}\,.

(24)

The mean rebinding time
\langle\tau\rangle
can be found explicitly
by writing the boundary value problem for the mean FPT:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}D\Delta\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}&=-1&(\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega),\\
-D\partial_{n}\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}&=\kappa\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}(\bm{x}_{0})\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}&(\bm{x}_{0}\in\partial\Omega),\\
\end{array}\right.

(25)

where
\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}(\bm{x}_{0})
is the indicator function of
\Gamma
:

\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}(\bm{x}_{0})=1
if
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Gamma
, and
0
otherwise. Integrating
the first relation over
\bm{x}_{0}\in\Omega
and applying the Green’s
formula, one gets

\displaystyle-|\Omega|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}_{0}\,D\Delta\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}=\int\limits_{\partial\Omega}d\bm{x}_{0}\,D\partial_{n}\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}_{0}\,\kappa\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}=-\kappa|\Gamma|\langle\tau\rangle,

from which

\langle\tau\rangle=\frac{|\Omega|}{\kappa|\Gamma|}\,,

(26)

where
|\Omega|
is the volume of the domain. For a spherical target
of radius
\rho
, the reactivity can be expressed in terms of the
forward constant
k_{\rm on}=\kappa(4\pi\rho^{2}N_{A})
(with
N_{A}\approx 6.02\cdot 10^{23}~\rm{mol}^{-1}
being the Avogadro number)

Shoup82
;
Lauffenburger
so that the mean rebinding time also reads
as
\langle\tau\rangle=N_{A}|\Omega|/k_{\rm on}
. Defining the
dimensionless quantity

\eta=k_{\rm off}\langle\tau\rangle=\frac{k_{\rm off}|\Omega|}{\kappa|\Gamma|}=\frac{k_{\rm off}|\Omega|N_{A}}{k_{\rm on}}\,,

(27)

we simply get
P_{\infty}=1/(1+\eta)
.

In general, the poles are not necessarily simple. In particular, if
the unbinding rate
k_{\rm off}
is such that
\tilde{H}(-k_{\rm off})=0
, then

-k_{\rm off}
is the pole of
\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
of higher order than
1
.
For instance, if
-k_{\rm off}
is the pole of order
2
, the corresponding
term in the spectral expansion (
21
) is of the form
te^{-k_{\rm off}t}
. As the set of zeros of the function
\tilde{H}(p)
is
discrete, we will ignore such specific values of the unbinding rate

k_{\rm off}
.

Introducing

\tilde{P}(p)=\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\int\limits_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}_{0}\,\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})=\tilde{H}(p)\,\tilde{Q}(p),

(28)

we can express
\tilde{Q}(p)
from Eq. (
16
) as

\tilde{Q}(p)=\frac{1}{p+k_{\rm off}}+\frac{k_{\rm off}}{p+k_{\rm off}}\tilde{P}(p),

(29)

which in time domain reads

Q(t)=e^{-k_{\rm off}t}+k_{\rm off}\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,e^{-k_{\rm off}t^{\prime}}\,P(t-t^{\prime}).

(30)

This relation implies that
Q(t)
monotonously decreases from
Q(0)=1
to
Q(\infty)=P_{\infty}
(see Appendix
A.4
).
Substituting Eq. (
21
) into this relation, we get

Q(t)=P_{\infty}+Q_{0}e^{-k_{\rm off}t}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}e^{p_{n}t},

(31)

where

\displaystyle Q_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-P_{\infty}-\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}\,,

\displaystyle q_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{v_{n}}{1+p_{n}/k_{\rm off}}\quad(n=1,2,\ldots),

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\int\limits_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}_{0}v_{n}(\bm{x}_{0}),

and we assumed that
-k_{\rm off}
is not the pole. On one hand, evaluating

\tilde{P}(p)
at
p=-k_{\rm off}
, one finds

\tilde{P}(-k_{\rm off})=\frac{P_{\infty}}{-k_{\rm off}}-\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{v_{n}}{k_{\rm off}+p_{n}}\,.

On the other hand, Eq. (
18
) implies

\tilde{P}(-k_{\rm off})=\frac{\tilde{H}(-k_{\rm off})}{-k_{\rm off}+k_{\rm off}(1-\tilde{H}(-k_{\rm off}))}=-\frac{1}{k_{\rm off}}\,,

yielding
Q_{0}\equiv 0
, and thus

Q(t)=P_{\infty}+\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}e^{p_{n}t}.

(32)

In summary, Eqs. (
15
,
21
,

32
) fully determine the exact form of the
probability density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in terms of the first-passage time
statistics
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of a single particle. Even if
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is
known explicitly (see an example in Appendix
B
), a
numerical implementation of this exact solution remains challenging
because it involves: finding zeros
\{p_{n}\}
of Eq. (
20
),
evaluation of the residues at these poles, computation of spectral
expansions (
21
,
32
) and finally the
inverse Laplace transform in Eq. (
15
). The practical
details of this computation are discussed in Appendix

C
. At the same time, our exact solution opens a
way to investigate the asymptotic behavior of the exact probability
density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in a rather general setting. Before turning to
this analysis, we discuss the mean reaction time.

II.4
Mean reaction time

The relation (
15
) allows one to access the moments of the
reaction time:

\langle\mathcal{T}_{N}^{k}\rangle=(-1)^{k}\lim_{p\to 0}\frac{\partial^{k}}{\partial p^{k}}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}\{[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}}{\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}}\,.

(33)

In particular, the mean reaction time is

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}_{N}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\biggl(\frac{\mathcal{L}\{t[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}}{\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}}

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}\{[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}\,\mathcal{L}\{t[Q(t)]^{N}\}}{(\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\})^{2}}\biggr).

As both
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
Q(t)
tend to
P_{\infty}
in the long-time
limit, setting
p=0
in the above Laplace transforms would yield
divergence. To overcome this issue, one can add and subtract the term

P_{\infty}^{N}
to each Laplace transform, e.g.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\{t[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t\,e^{-pt}\bigl([P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}-P_{\infty}^{N}+P_{\infty}^{N}\bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{\infty}^{N}/p^{2}+a_{1}+o(1)\qquad(p\to 0),

where

a_{k}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{k}\,\bigl([P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}-P_{\infty}^{N}\bigr).

(34)

Introducing also

b_{k}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{k}\,\bigl([Q(t)]^{N}-P_{\infty}^{N}\bigr),

(35)

we compute the above limit as

\langle\mathcal{T}_{N}\rangle=\frac{b_{0}-a_{0}}{P_{\infty}^{N}}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\frac{[Q(t)]^{N}-[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}}{P_{\infty}^{N}}\,.

(36)

Higher-order moments of
\mathcal{T}_{N}
can be expressed in a similar way.
For
N=1
, this relation implies that
\langle\mathcal{T}_{1}\rangle=\langle\tau\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}
, as expected. Equation (
36
) is a
generalization of the expression for the mean slowest FPT governed by
the probability density in Eq. (
3
):

\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}\rangle=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl(1-[1-S(t)]^{N}\bigr).

(37)

In fact, if there is no unbinding (
k_{\rm off}=0
), one gets
Q(t)=1
,

P_{\infty}=1
, and
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})=1-S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. In Appendix

A.1
, we derive the large-
N
asymptotic behavior of
this mean time:

\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}\rangle\propto\langle\tau\rangle\ln N\qquad(N\to\infty).

(38)

The unbinding mechanism drastically changes this asymptotic behavior
into

\langle\mathcal{T}_{N}\rangle\propto\frac{(1+k_{\rm off}\langle\tau\rangle)^{N}}{k_{\rm off}N}\qquad(N\to\infty),

(39)

i.e., a very slow logarithmic increase turns into exponential growth
controlled by the unbinding rate
k_{\rm off}
(see Appendix

A.2
). As a consequence, when many particles are needed
to trigger the reaction, even a small unbinding rate can considerably
alter predictions of the irreversible setting. We emphasize however,
that Eq. (
39
) captures only the large-
N

asymptotic behavior and is not applicable at small
N
. In
particular, an non-monotonous dependence of the right-hand side of
Eq. (
39
) on
k_{\rm off}
and
N
is not reproduced for
the mean reaction time (see further discussion in Appendix

A.2
).

II.5
Long-time behavior

The probability density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
can be formally obtained via the
inverse Laplace transform in Eq. (
14
) by finding the poles

p_{n,N}
of the function
\tilde{H}_{N}(p|\bm{x}_{0})
in the complex plane
and applying the residue theorem. This is a difficult task, even
numerically, especially for large
N
. We focus therefore on the pole

p_{1,N}
with the smallest absolute value that determines the decay
time
T_{N}=1/|p_{1,N}|
of the probability density at long times:

H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{-t/T_{N}}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(40)

As
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
admits the spectral decomposition (
21
)
with the poles
p_{n}
, the poles of the numerator
\mathcal{L}\{[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}

of Eq. (
14
) are obtained as all linear combinations of the
form
p_{n_{1}}+p_{n_{2}}+\ldots+p_{n_{N}}
. In particular, the pole
with the smallest absolute value is still
p_{1}
(apart from the pole
at
0
). The situation is more difficult for the denominator

\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}
, for which we are looking not for its poles, but for
zeros.
Let us search for a zero of this function:

\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}(p)=\sum\limits_{n_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum\limits_{n_{N}=0}^{\infty}\frac{q_{n_{1}}\cdots q_{n_{N}}}{p-p_{n_{1}}-\ldots-p_{n_{N}}}\,,

(41)

where we included the pole at
0
by setting
p_{0}=0
and
q_{0}=P_{\infty}
. As
p\to 0
, the leading term of this expression is

q_{0}^{N}/p
, which can be separated from the other terms. In the
leading-order approximation, one can set
p=0
in the remaining
terms:

0=\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}(p_{1,N})\approx\frac{q_{0}^{N}}{p_{1,N}}-\hskip-14.22636pt\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}=0,\ldots\\
\ldots,n_{N}=0,\\
n_{1}+\ldots+n_{N}>0\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\hskip-8.53581pt\frac{q_{n_{1}}\cdots q_{n_{N}}}{p_{n_{1}}+\ldots+p_{n_{N}}}.

(42)

The multiple sum, from which the term with
n_{1}=n_{2}=\ldots=n_{N}=0
was subtracted, can be expressed in terms of an integral, yielding
an approximation for the pole
p_{1,N}
:

p_{1,N}\approx-P_{\infty}^{N}\biggl(\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl([Q(t)]^{N}-P_{\infty}^{N}\bigr)\biggr)^{-1}.

(43)

As a consequence, the decay rate is

T_{N}\approx P_{\infty}^{-N}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl([Q(t)]^{N}-P_{\infty}^{N}\bigr).

(44)

Curiously, this expression is very similar to the expression
(
36
) for the mean reaction time.

The accuracy of this approximation depends on various parameters such
as
k_{\rm off}
and
N
. In fact, in order to get the sum in
Eq. (
42
), we neglected
p_{1,N}
under the assumption
that
|p_{1,N}|
is much smaller than
|p_{1}|
. As
N
increases, the
reaction event occurs at longer times, i.e., the decay time increases,
and the approximation gets more accurate. In turn, the case
N=1

is the worst for this approximation (see discussion in Appendix

A.3
). Similarly, as
k_{\rm off}
increases, the particles
unbind more often, the decay time increases, yielding a more accurate
approximation. Note that the approximation (
43
) can
be improved by accounting perturbatively for next-order corrections.

II.6
Short-time behavior

At short times, the main contribution to the probability density of
the first-passage time comes from the particles that follow almost
“direct trajectories” to the target

Godec16b
;
Basnayake18
;
Grebenkov18
. As a consequence, the
short-time behavior is generally

H(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx C_{\bm{x}_{0}}\,t^{\alpha}\,e^{-\delta^{2}/(4Dt)}\qquad(t\to 0),

(45)

where
\delta
is the distance between the starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
and
the target region
\Gamma
,
t^{\alpha}
is a power-law correction, and

C_{\bm{x}_{0}}
is the prefactor depending on the starting point, the shape
of the domain, and the reactivity
\kappa
. Note that
\delta
is
either the Euclidean distance (i.e., the length of the shortest
interval connecting
\bm{x}_{0}
and
\Gamma
), or the geodesic distance along
the shortest curvilinear path from
\bm{x}_{0}
and
\Gamma
that bypasses
eventual obstacles. As a rigorous derivation of this relation is
beyond the scope of the paper, we use it as an assumption, under which
the following results are valid (see an example in Appendix

B.3
).

When the starting point
\bm{x}_{0}
lies on the target,
\delta=0
and
Eq. (
45
) implies
H(t)\approx Ct^{\alpha}
with
C=(1/|\Gamma|)\int\nolimits_{\Gamma}d\bm{x}_{0}\,C_{\bm{x}_{0}}
, from which

\tilde{H}(p)\approx C\Gamma(\alpha+1)p^{-1-\alpha}
as

p\to\infty
. If
\alpha>-2
, the leading term in the denominator
of Eq. (
18
) is
p
that implies for any
\bm{x}_{0}
:

\tilde{P}(p|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\frac{\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})}{p}\,,

(46)

which in time domain gives

P(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,H(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})=1-S(t|\bm{x}_{0}).

(47)

When
\bm{x}_{0}\notin\Gamma
, the integral of Eq. (
45
) yields in
the lowest order

P(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\frac{4DC_{\bm{x}_{0}}}{\delta^{2}}\,t^{\alpha+2}\,e^{-\delta^{2}/(4Dt)}\,,

(48)

from which Eq. (
11
) implies

\mathcal{P}_{t}(N|0)\approx\left(\frac{4DC_{\bm{x}_{0}}}{\delta^{2}}\right)^{N}\,t^{N(\alpha+2)}\,e^{-N\delta^{2}/(4Dt)}.

(49)

At short times, one has
Q(t)\approx 1
, and thus
\mathcal{P}_{t}(N|N)\approx 1
, so that

\tilde{H}_{N}(p|\bm{x}_{0})\approx p\mathcal{L}\big\{\mathcal{P}_{t}(N|0)\big\}\,,

(50)

which in turn gives us the short-time behavior

\displaystyle H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\frac{\partial{\mathcal{P}_{t}(N|0)}}{\partial{t}}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{N\delta^{2}}{4D}\left(\frac{4DC_{\bm{x}_{0}}}{\delta^{2}}\right)^{N}\,t^{N(\alpha+2)-2}\,e^{-N\delta^{2}/(4Dt)}.

(51)

This leading-order asymptotic relation can be improved by computing
the next-order term in the integral of Eq. (
45
) that
yields the correction
O(t)
to Eq. (
51
), which is
still independent of the unbinding rate
k_{\rm off}
. In turn,
k_{\rm off}

appears in the correction
O(t^{2})
by using
Q(t)\approx 1-k_{\rm off}t

instead of
Q(t)\approx 1
in the above derivation. The integral of
this expression yields, in the leading order:

S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx 1-\left(\frac{4DC_{\bm{x}_{0}}}{\delta^{2}}\right)^{N}t^{N(\alpha+2)}\,e^{-N\delta^{2}/(4Dt)}.

(52)

It is easy to see that for
N=1
, the leading term of the short-time
behavior in Eq. (
45
) is recovered. We note that the
short-time behavior of the reaction time density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is
identical to that of the probability density
H^{0}_{N,N}(t)
of the
first-passage time
\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}
. This result is independent of the
unbinding rate
k_{\rm off}
because the probability of an unbinding event
is small at times
t\ll k_{\rm off}^{-1}
. As a consequence, for any
K
,
one can approximate the short-time behavior of
\mathcal{T}_{K,N}
by that of

\mathcal{T}^{0}_{K,N}
, for which the probability density is given explicitly by
Eq. (
3
). Substituting here the short-time asymptotic
relations (
45
,
47
,
48
), we get
then

\displaystyle H_{K,N}(t)\approx H^{0}_{K,N}(t)

(53)

\displaystyle\approx{N\choose K}\frac{K\delta^{2}}{4D}\left(\frac{4DC_{\bm{x}_{0}}}{\delta^{2}}\right)^{K}t^{K(\alpha+2)-2}\,e^{-K\delta^{2}/(4Dt)},

which generalizes Eq. (
51
).

III.1
Comparison with irreversible binding case

First, we note that the limit of irreversible binding can be achieved
by setting either
k_{\rm off}=0
or
\kappa=\infty
. In fact, in the
latter case, any particle that unbinds from the target, immediately
rebinds and thus never leaves the target. As a consequence, the
natural parameter characterizing the unbinding kinetics is the
dimensionless quantity
\eta
defined by Eq. (
27
). When

\eta
is small, unbinding kinetics is usually considered as
irrelevant. In the following, we consider the irreversible binding
limit by keeping
\kappa
fixed and setting
k_{\rm off}\to 0
.

For irreversible binding, the short-time behavior of the probability
density
H_{K,N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is given by Eq. (
53
). In
turn, the long-time behavior follows from the spectral expansion of
the probability density
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
of the first-passage time. In
fact, as restricted diffusion occurs in a bounded domain, the
governing Laplace operator,
-\Delta
, has a discrete spectrum, i.e.,
a countable set of eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\ldots\nearrow\infty
that are associated to

L_{2}(\Omega)
-normalized eigenfunctions
\{u_{n}(\bm{x})\}
forming a
complete orthonormal basis in
L_{2}(\Omega)

Grebenkov13
. As a
consequence, the survival probability admits the standard spectral
decomposition
Gardiner

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\,u_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{-Dt\lambda_{n}},\quad c_{n}=\int\limits_{\Omega}d\bm{x}\,u_{n}(\bm{x}),

(54)

from which Eq. (
3
) implies

H^{0}_{N,N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\approx N\,D\lambda_{1}\,c_{1}u_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{-Dt\lambda_{1}}\,.

(55)

When the target is small, one has

\lambda_{1}\approx\frac{\kappa|\Gamma|}{D|\Omega|}=\frac{1}{D\langle\tau\rangle}\,,

(56)

where we used Eq. (
26
) for the mean rebinding
time. We get therefore

H^{0}_{N,N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})\propto e^{-t/\langle\tau\rangle}\qquad(t\to\infty).

(57)

One sees that the decay time here,
\langle\tau\rangle
, does not
depend on
N
, in sharp contrast to the exponential growth of
T_{N}
in
Eq. (
44
) for reversible binding.

Lawley found that the mean of the FPT
\mathcal{T}^{0}_{K,N}
was determined for
any
fixed

K
as
Lawley20b

\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{K,N}\rangle\approx\frac{C}{\ln N}\qquad(N\to\infty),

(58)

with some constant
C
and higher-order corrections
1/(\ln N)^{2}

depending on
K
. However, this behavior cannot be applied to
K=N
. In Appendix
A.1
, we show that

\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}\rangle\approx C^{\prime}\ln N\qquad(N\to\infty),

(59)

with another constant
C^{\prime}
determined by the decay time of the
survival probability for a single particle. Even though the mean
arrival time of the slowest particle differs by a factor
(\ln N)^{2}

from that of the fastest particle, the need for
N
particles to
trigger the reaction event does not considerably slow down the
irreversible reaction kinetics. This observation is totally different
in the case of reversible binding, for which the mean reaction time

\langle\mathcal{T}_{N}\rangle
in Eq. (
39
) exhibits an
exponential growth with
N
.

III.2
Comparison with the LMA

Now, we compare our exact results to the Lawley-Madrid approximation.
This approximation was designed under assumption that the rebinding
time distribution can be approximated by an exponential law:
S(t)\approx\bar{S}(t)=e^{-\nu t}
, with an appropriate rate
\nu
.
There are two natural choices for this rate. In order to get the
correct long-time behavior of the survival probability, one can set

\nu=D\lambda_{1}
to match the leading term of the exact spectral
expansion (
54
). Alternatively, as the rebinding time

\tau
is approximated by an exponential law, one can set
\nu=1/\langle\tau\rangle
. When the target is small and weakly reactive,
Eq. (
56
) indicates that
1/\langle\tau\rangle

is close to
D\lambda_{1}
, and both choices yield the same result. One
sees that the approximate equality
1/\langle\tau\rangle\approx D\lambda_{1}
ensures the self-consistence of the Lawley-Madrid
approximation and can thus serve as a practical indicator of its
validity. As a consequence, the LMA is expected to capture the
long-time behavior of the probability density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in the
limit of small targets. In the remaining part of this section, we
assume that the validity conditions of the LMA are fulfilled and set

\nu=1/\langle\tau\rangle
.

First, we look at the mean reaction time. Lawley and Madrid analyzed
the asymptotic behavior of their Eq. (
7
) in two
limits: (i) when
K
is fixed and
N\to\infty
, in which case

\langle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{K,N}\rangle\sim\langle\tau\rangle K/N
, i.e.,
essentially a linear growth with
K
; (ii) when
1/(1+k_{\rm off}\langle\tau\rangle)<K/N<1
is fixed, in which case
\langle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{K,N}\rangle
exhibits a very rapid growth
Lawley19
.
While the limiting case
K=N
was not discussed, we deduced the
asymptotic behavior of Eq. (
7
) by using similar tools:

\langle\bar{\mathcal{T}}_{N,N}\rangle\approx\frac{(1+k_{\rm off}\langle\tau\rangle)^{N}}{k_{\rm off}N}\quad(N\gg 1).

(60)

This expression coincides with Eq. (
39
) that we
obtained from the exact solution (
36
). This highlights
that the LMA captures qualitatively the long-time behavior. In turn,
as discussed earlier and illustrated in Table
1
, both
Eq. (
7
) and its asymptotic form (
60
)
overestimate the mean reaction time.

Let us now turn to the approximation (
6
) of the
probability density. Denoting by
0>\nu_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\nu_{N}
the negative eigenvalues of the matrix
W^{(N)}
, one sees that
the long-time asymptotic behavior is determined by the largest
eigenvalue
\nu_{1}
,

\bar{H}_{N,N}(t)\propto e^{-t/\bar{T}_{N}}\qquad(t\to\infty),

(61)

with
\bar{T}_{N}=-1/\nu_{1}
. In turn, the short-time approximation
reads

\bar{H}_{N,N}(t)\approx\frac{N}{\langle\tau\rangle}\,(t/\langle\tau\rangle)^{N-1}+O(t^{N})\quad(t\to 0),

(62)

where the lower-order powers of
t
vanish because of the
three-diagonal structure of the matrix
W^{(N)}
, whereas the
prefactor in front of the leading term
t^{N-1}
is

\frac{[(W^{(N)})^{N-1}]_{N,1}}{(N-1)!}=N/\langle\tau\rangle^{N-1}
. Expectedly, this asymptotic behavior is different
from relation (
51
) derived from our exact solution.
Note that in the limit
k_{\rm off}\to 0
, the assumed exponential law for
the rebinding time implies

\lim\limits_{k_{\rm off}\to 0}\bar{H}_{N,N}(t)=\frac{N}{\langle\tau\rangle}e^{-t/\langle\tau\rangle}(1-e^{-t/\langle\tau\rangle})^{N-1}.

(63)

Figure
2
illustrates the behavior of the probability
density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
for two values
N=2
and
N=3
, as well as
two starting positions
|\bm{x}_{0}|=5
and
|\bm{x}_{0}|=2
. First of all,
we note that the probability density is broad, spanning over 4 to 6
orders of magnitude in time. At short times, the probability density

H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
does not depend on the unbinding rate
k_{\rm off}
, yielding
the universal behavior of the left tail of the distribution given by
Eq. (
3
). Note that the short-time asymptotic relation
(
51
) is not accurate on the considered range of times
but captures correctly the leading-order term. This relation can be
improved by including next-order corrections. At the timescale

1/k_{\rm off}
, the unbinding mechanism starts to play a role, yielding
deviations from the irreversible binding case. These deviations are
actually visible already at
t\gtrsim 5
for
k_{\rm off}=0.03
and
t\gtrsim 50
for
k_{\rm off}=0.003
. As unbinding events slow down the
reaction, the right tail of the distribution is shifted towards longer
times as
k_{\rm off}
increases. In fact, the long-time decay
(
40
) is determined by the exponential function with the
decay time
T_{N}
increasing with
k_{\rm off}
. Note that Monte Carlo
simulations are in perfect agreement with the exact solution.

The comparison with the LMA reveals its advantages and limitations.
The LMA captures correctly the behavior of the probability density for
moderate and long times, the agreement being better as
k_{\rm off}
is
smaller. One sees that the LMA systematically overestimates the decay
time that controls the long-time behavior (see Table
1
).
Deviations become larger as
N
and
k_{\rm off}
increase. Expectedly, the
LMA totally fails at short times. Deviations are stronger when the
particles start closer to the target. In fact, when
|\bm{x}_{0}|=2
,
there is a notable maximum around
t\sim 1
that is not captured by
the LMA. The most probable time determining the position of this
maximum is several orders of magnitude smaller than the mean reaction
time. This maximum can be relevant for applications when the source
of particles is close to the target (see
Reva21
for further
discussions).
Nevertheless, the explicit character of the Lawley-Madrid
approximation and a much simpler computation of the probability
density via Eq. (
6
) make it a valuable tool for a
first-step analysis of reversible reactions with multiple particles.
Further improvements of the LMA present an important perspective.

IV
Conclusions and perspectives

Diffusion-controlled reactions involving multiple particles are
abundant and particularly relevant in biochemistry. The need for a
sufficient number of bound particles can be considered as a sort of
protection mechanism against spontaneous triggering, as well as a mean
for reliable control of reactions.
The overwhelming majority of former studies in this field were focused
on first-passage times of a single particle, with a straightforward
extension to the extreme statistics of many particles with
irreversible binding to the target. In turn, the problem of impatient
particles with reversible binding seems to remain unnoticed, in spite
of its practical relevance
Grebenkov17
. For instance, five
calcium ions have to bind to a calcium-sensing protein to initiate the
release of neurotransmitters for signalling between neurons

Berridge03
;
Eggermann12
;
Nakamura15
;
Dittrich13
;
Guerrier16
;
Reva21
.
To outline the role of unbinding kinetics onto this process, we take
the following estimates from Ref.
Reva21
:
R=300
nm,
k_{\rm on}=6.35\cdot 10^{8}~\rm{M}^{-1}~\rm{s}^{-1}=6.35\cdot 10^{5}~\rm{mol}~\rm{m}^{-3}~\rm{s}^{-1}
and
k_{\rm off}=1.57\cdot 10^{4}~\rm{s}^{-1}
, from which the mean rebinding time is
\langle\tau\rangle\approx 0.1
s, see Eqs. (
26
,

27
). As a consequence,
\eta=k_{\rm off}\langle\tau\rangle\approx 1.57\cdot 10^{3}\gg 1
, so that one cannot simply ignore
reversible binding that drastically changes the distribution of the
reaction time.

Even if the particles diffuse independently, their randomly
“asynchronized” waiting times on the target render the problem of
exact characterization of the reaction times
\mathcal{T}_{K,N}
mathematically
challenging. The remarkable work by Lawley and Madrid brought an
elegant approximate solution to this problem
Lawley19
. The
good accuracy of this approximation, as reported by its authors, might
seem to suggest that this challenging problem is fully solved. In
this paper, we showed that this is far from being the end of the
story.

We focused on the particular case of the first time
\mathcal{T}_{N}=\mathcal{T}_{N,N}
when all
N
particles are bound to the target. This choice
allowed us to derive, for the first time, the exact complete solution
of the problem of impatient particles, i.e., to express the
probability density of the random variable
\mathcal{T}_{N}
in terms of the
first-passage time distribution of a single particle. This exact
solution revealed some limitations and deficiencies of the LMA. In
particular, we showed that the approximate solution captures the
qualitative behavior at moderate and long times but fails at short
times. Moreover, the LMA overestimates the mean reaction time and the
decay time so that its predictions are inaccurate in some settings.
At the same time, the complexity of the exact solution for
\mathcal{T}_{N,N}

and yet a fully open problem of finding the exact solution in the
general case
\mathcal{T}_{K,N}
make the LMA a valuable tool for the
qualitative analysis and preliminary estimations. Moreover, the
accuracy of the LMA is expected to be much higher in the limit of very
small targets. We believe that further improvements of the LMA or
development of alternative methods can bring important insights on the
problem of impatient particles. This is an interesting perspective of
the present work.

We also emphasize that impatient particles offer an excellent example
of a physical problem, for which standard numerical methods may be
insufficient for getting the whole picture. In particular, as the
mean reaction time and the decay time grow exponentially fast with the
number of particles, getting the whole distribution of the reaction
time
\mathcal{T}_{N}
was not possible even for moderate
N
. For instance, a
Monte Carlo simulation with
10^{6}
realizations used to plot the
empirical probability density in Fig.
2
took one day on a
laptop. However, this simulation allowed to get the behavior of

H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
only for a limited range of time scales (e.g., from

10^{2}
to
10^{4}
for
N=2
). Even though parallelization can easily
increase the number of realizations (say, by a factor 100 or 1000), it
would not be enough to get the short-time behavior. As the
computational time explodes with
N
, we could not complete Monte
Carlo simulations even for moderate values of
N
such as
N=5
or

N=10
. Here, analytical tools and approximations are
indispensable.

While we focused on the setting when all particles start from the same
fixed point
\bm{x}_{0}
, our exact solution can be easily extended to a
more general case with distinct starting points. Moreover, the
starting point of each particle can also be random. In the case of a
uniform distribution of the starting points, the properties of the
fastest FPT
\mathcal{T}^{0}_{1,N}
were studied in
Grebenkov20
. An
extension to the reaction time
\mathcal{T}_{N}
is straightforward.

The exact expression (
36
) for the mean reaction time

\langle\mathcal{T}_{N}\rangle
opens a way to investigate the role of different
parameters onto the reaction kinetics. A rough approximation allowed
us to access the large-
N
asymptotic behavior of this quantity.
However, the asymptotic formula (
39
) lacks an exact
prefactor and also fails at small
N
. More accurate analysis of
Eq. (
36
) could hopefully improve this formula to get a
quantitatively accurate description of the mean reaction time. Its
extension to other reaction times
\langle\mathcal{T}_{K,N}\rangle
presents
an exciting perspective.

A.1
Mean reaction time for irreversible binding

The mean fastest FPT
\mathcal{T}^{0}_{1,N}
and, more generally, the mean
K
-th
fastest FPT
\mathcal{T}^{0}_{K,N}
, were thoroughly investigated in the
irreversible binding case

Weiss83
;
Basnayake18
;
Basnayake19
;
Lawley20a
;
Lawley20b
. For any
fixed
K
, the mean value
\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{K,N}\rangle
behaves
universally as
1/\ln N
in the large
N
limit, whereas the
higher-order corrections
O(1/(\ln N)^{2})
depend on
K
. In turn, the
asymptotic behavior of the slowest FPT
\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}
was not discussed,
to our knowledge. In particular, the former result for any fixed
K

cannot be applied to the case
K=N
. Here, we sketch the main steps
of this analysis, more rigorous derivations being beyond the scope of
this paper.

As the mean time
\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}\rangle
is given by
Eq. (
37
), its asymptotic analysis is reduced to that
of the survival probability
S(t|\bm{x}_{0})
for a single particle. It is
easy to check that the function
f(t)=1-(1-S(t|\bm{x}_{0}))^{N}

monotonously decreases from
1
at
t=0
to
0
as
t\to\infty
. The
integral in Eq. (
37
) can be evaluated by
approximating
f(t)
by the Heaviside step function
\Theta(t_{N}-t)
,
where
t_{N}
is chosen by setting
f(t_{N})=\zeta
, with
\zeta
being
around
1/2
(see below). This equation yields
S(t_{N}|\bm{x}_{0})=1-(1-\zeta)^{1/N}
. When
N
is large, the right-hand side of this
relation is close to
0
. In other words, the limit
N\to\infty

corresponds to large
t_{N}
, for which the spectral expansion
(
54
) can be truncated to a single term,
S(t_{N}|\bm{x}_{0})\approx c_{1}u_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})e^{-D\lambda_{1}t_{N}}
. As a consequence, one gets

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}\rangle

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle t_{N}\approx-\frac{\ln(1-(1-\zeta)^{1/N})-\ln(c_{1}u_{1}(\bm{x}_{0}))}{D\lambda_{1}}

(64)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\ln N+\ln(c_{1}u_{1}(\bm{x}_{0}))-\ln\ln\frac{1}{1-\xi}}{D\lambda_{1}}\,.

Even though this approximate relation depends on a somewhat arbitrary
choice of
\zeta
around
1/2
, this dependence is weak and
corresponds to the sub-leading (constant) term, as compared to the
leading term
\ln N
. Note that
1/(D\lambda_{1})
is the decay time
for a single particle which determines the natural timescale of the
problem. Figure
4
illustrates the dependence of

\langle\mathcal{T}^{0}_{N,N}\rangle
on
N
and its large-
N
asymptotic
behavior (
64
).

A.3
The decay time for
N=1

As mentioned in Sec.
II.5
, the approximation
(
44
) of the decay time
T_{N}
is least accurate in the

. To illustrate this point, we note that the integral in
Eq. (
44
) can be found explicitly for
N=1
. In fact,
one has

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}dt\bigl(Q(t)-P_{\infty}\bigr)=\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\biggl(\tilde{Q}(p)-\frac{P_{\infty}}{p}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\biggl(\frac{1}{p+k_{\rm off}(1-\tilde{H}(p))}-\frac{P_{\infty}}{p}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\biggl(\frac{1}{p+k_{\rm off}(p\langle\tau\rangle-p^{2}\langle\tau^{2}/2\rangle+O(p^{3}))}-\frac{P_{\infty}}{p}\biggr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}P_{\infty}^{2}k_{\rm off}\langle\tau^{2}\rangle,

where we used Eq. (
24
). As a consequence, the
approximation (
44
) reads

p_{1,1}\approx-\frac{2}{P_{\infty}k_{\rm off}\langle\tau^{2}\rangle}\,.

(68)

Note that for restricted diffusion between concentric spheres, the
second moment of the rebinding time is known explicitly, see
Eq. (
76
). However, a similar approximation can be used to
estimate the first pole of
\tilde{Q}(p)
, by expanding
\tilde{H}(p)

in Eq. (

p_{1}\approx-2/(P_{\infty}k_{\rm off}\langle\tau^{2}\rangle)
. In other

turns out to be identical to

, thus invalidating
the approximation in the case

.

A.4
Behavior of the function
Q(t)

According to Eq. (
30

is a monotonously decreasing
function. In fact, the time derivative of Eq. (
30

Q^{\prime}(t)=-k_{\rm off}\bigl(Q(t)-P(t)\bigr).

(69)

Comparing the probabilities
P(t)
and
Q(t)
of finding the particle
bound to the target, one realizes that the former includes an
additional step of binding to the target and thus
P(t)\leq Q(t)
,
implying
Q^{\prime}(t)\leq 0
. This property can also be deduced in a more
formal way. In fact, as
P(t)
is the convolution of
Q(t)
and

H(t)
(see Eq. (
28
)), its integration by parts
yields

\displaystyle P(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,Q(t-t^{\prime})H(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\bigl[Q(0)S(t)-S(0)Q(t)\bigr]+\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,Q^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})S(t^{\prime}).

Since
S(0)=Q(0)=1
, one deduces

Q^{\prime}(t)=-k_{\rm off}\biggl(S(t)-\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,S(t^{\prime})Q^{\prime}(t-t^{\prime})\biggr),

(70)

where
S(t)\geq 0
is the survival probability. Applying a sort of
induction argument, one can check that the right-hand side is
negative. Note also that this relation implies
Q^{\prime}(0)=-k_{\rm off}
and
thus
Q(t)\approx 1-k_{\rm off}t+O(t^{2})
as
t\to 0
.

Appendix B
Diffusion between concentric spheres

In this Appendix, we summarize former results needed for evaluating
the probability density of the reaction time
\mathcal{T}_{N}
for the
practically relevant scenario of particles diffusing in a shell-like
domain
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\rho<|\bm{x}|<R\}
bounded between two
concentric spheres of radii
\rho
and
R
. The inner sphere is a
partially reactive target with reactivity
\kappa
, whereas the outer
sphere is reflecting. The rotational symmetry of the problem allows
for an explicit solution by separation of variables

Redner
;
Gardiner
;
Carslaw
. The first-passage time distribution
was discussed in
Grebenkov18
, whereas the exact solution for
the probability
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
was given in
Reva21
.

B.1
First-passage time density

The probability density of the first-passage time can be found by
separation of variables in a standard way (see
Grebenkov18
for
details). The rotational symmetry implies that
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and other
related quantities depend only on time
t
and the radial coordinate

r=|\bm{x}_{0}|
. In Laplace domain, one has

\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{g(r)}{g(\rho)-g^{\prime}(\rho)\frac{D}{\kappa}}\,,

(71)

where

g(r)=\frac{R\sqrt{p/D}\cosh\xi-\sinh\xi}{r\sqrt{p/D}}\,,

(72)

with
\xi=(R-r)\sqrt{p/D}
, and
g^{\prime}(r)
is given by

g^{\prime}(r)=\frac{(1-Rrp/D)\sinh\xi-\xi\cosh\xi}{r^{2}\sqrt{p/D}}\,.

(73)

The moments of the first-passage time can be found as

\langle\tau^{k}\rangle_{\bm{x}_{0}}=(-1)^{k}\lim\limits_{p\to 0}\frac{\partial^{k}\tilde{H}(p|\bm{x}_{0})}{\partial p^{k}}\,.

(74)

Setting
|\bm{x}_{0}|=\rho
, one also determines the moments of the
rebinding time, e.g.,

\langle\tau\rangle=\frac{R^{3}-\rho^{3}}{3\kappa\rho^{2}}

(75)

and

\displaystyle\langle\tau^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2(R^{3}-\rho^{3})^{2}}{9\kappa^{2}\rho^{4}}

(76)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{2(5R^{6}-9R^{5}\rho+5R^{3}\rho^{3}-\rho^{6})}{45D\kappa\rho^{3}}\,.

The inversion of the Laplace transform in Eq. (
71
) by means
of the residue theorem yields:

H(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\frac{D}{\rho^{2}}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{2}\,\hat{c}_{n}\,u\bigl(\hat{\alpha}_{n},|\bm{x}_{0}|\bigr)\,e^{-Dt\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{2}/\rho^{2}},

(77)

with

\displaystyle u(\alpha,r)=\frac{\rho\,\sin\bigl(\alpha\frac{R-r}{\rho}\bigr)-R\alpha\cos\bigl(\alpha\frac{R-r}{\rho}\bigr)}{r}\,,

(78)

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}=-\frac{2\mu\rho^{2}}{\hat{\alpha}_{n}}\biggl[(\mu R(R-\rho)+R^{2}+\rho^{2})

\displaystyle\times\hat{\alpha}_{n}\sin(\hat{\alpha}_{n}\beta)+(R(R-\rho)\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{2}-\mu\rho^{2})\cos(\hat{\alpha}_{n}\beta)\biggr]^{-1}\,,

and
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
(with
n=1,2,\ldots
) denoting the positive solutions
of the trigonometric equation

\tan(\alpha\beta)=\frac{\alpha\bigl(\beta+(1+\beta)\mu\bigr)}{1+\mu+(1+\beta)\alpha^{2}}\,,

(79)

with

\mu=\kappa\rho/D,\qquad\beta=(R-\rho)/\rho.

(80)

Note that the survival probability is obtained by integrating
Eq. (
77
):

S(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\sum\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\hat{c}_{n}\,u\bigl(\hat{\alpha}_{n},|\bm{x}_{0}|\bigr)\,e^{-Dt\hat{\alpha}_{n}^{2}/\rho^{2}}.

(81)

B.2
The occupancy probability

In turn, the spectral expansion (
21
) of the occupancy
probability
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
was derived in
Reva21
, with
v_{n}(\bm{x}_{0})=c_{n}u(\alpha_{n},|\bm{x}_{0}|)
, where
u(\alpha,r)
is given by
Eq. (
78
),
p_{n}=-\alpha_{n}^{2}D/\rho^{2}
, and

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\mu}{\sin(\alpha_{n}\beta)\bigl(\alpha_{n}^{2}w_{1}+w_{2}\bigr)+\alpha_{n}\cos(\alpha_{n}\beta)\bigl(\alpha_{n}^{2}w_{3}+w_{4}\bigr)},

with

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=4(1+\beta)+\beta(\beta+\mu(1+\beta)),

(82a)

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=2(1+\mu-\lambda(1+\beta))-\lambda\beta^{2},

(82b)

\displaystyle w_{3}

\displaystyle=\beta(1+\beta),

(82c)

\displaystyle w_{4}

\displaystyle=\beta(1+\mu-\lambda(1+\beta))-3(\beta+\mu(1+\beta)),

(82d)

\lambda=k_{\rm off}\rho^{2}/D
, and
\alpha_{n}
are strictly positive
solutions of the trigonometric equation

\sin(\alpha_{n}\beta)=\frac{\bigl[\alpha_{n}^{2}(\beta+\mu(1+\beta))-\lambda\beta\bigr]\alpha_{n}\cos(\alpha_{n}\beta)}{\alpha_{n}^{4}(1+\beta)+\alpha_{n}^{2}(1+\mu-\lambda(1+\beta))-\lambda}\,.

(83)

enumerated by
n=1,2,\ldots
. Note that the coefficients
v_{n}

determining
Q(t)
in Eq. (
32
) are simply
v_{n}=c_{n}\,u(\alpha_{n},\rho)
.

B.3
Short-time asymptotic behavior

Here we focus on the short-time behavior of
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. As the
solution in Eq. (
77
) depends only on the radial coordinate
r=|\bm{x}_{0}|
, we replace
\bm{x}_{0}
by
r
in the following expressions.
Setting
s=(R-\rho)^{2}p/D
and
\nu=D/(\kappa\rho)
, we can
rewrite
\tilde{H}(p|r)
as

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(p|r)=\frac{\beta\sqrt{s}\cosh{\big(\sqrt{s}\frac{R-r}{R-\rho}\big)}-\sinh{\big(\sqrt{s}\frac{R-r}{R-\rho}\big)}}{r/\rho}

(84)

\displaystyle\times\bigg((\nu+\beta)\sqrt{s}\cosh(\sqrt{s})-(1+\nu-\gamma s)\sinh(\sqrt{s})\bigg)^{-1},

where
\gamma=\nu\beta^{2}\rho/R
. We first study the case

r=\rho
, for which

\tilde{H}(p|\rho)=\frac{1-\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{s}}\tanh(\sqrt{s})}{1+\nu/\beta-\frac{1+\nu-\gamma s}{\beta\sqrt{s}}\tanh(\sqrt{s})}\,.

(85)

For large
s
,
\tanh(\sqrt{s})=1+O(e^{-2\sqrt{s}})
, which further
implies

\displaystyle\tilde{H}(p|\rho)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\beta\sqrt{s}-1}{(\nu+\beta)\sqrt{s}-(1+\nu)+\gamma s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}\biggr(\frac{A_{1}}{\sqrt{s}+x_{1}}+\frac{A_{2}}{\sqrt{s}+x_{2}}\bigg),

where

x_{1,2}=\frac{(\nu+\beta)\pm\sqrt{(\nu+\beta)^{2}+4\gamma(1+\nu)}}{2\gamma}\,,

(86)

and

A_{1}=\frac{1+\beta x_{1}}{x_{1}-x_{2}}\,,\qquad A_{2}=\frac{1+\beta x_{2}}{x_{2}-x_{1}}\,.

(87)

Using the following inverse Laplace transform,

\mathcal{L}\biggr\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}+a}\biggr\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\tau}}-ae^{a^{2}\tau}\text{erfc}(a\sqrt{\tau})=f_{a}(\tau)

(88)

(where
\textrm{erfc}(z)
is the complementary error function), we
find the short-time approximation

H(t|\rho)\approx D\frac{A_{1}f_{x_{1}}\big(\frac{Dt}{(R-\rho)^{2}}\big)+A_{2}f_{x_{2}}\big(\frac{Dt}{(R-\rho)^{2}}\big)}{(R-\rho)^{2}\gamma}\,.

(89)

Using
f_{a}(\tau)\approx 1/\sqrt{\pi\tau}-a+2a^{2}\sqrt{\tau/\pi}+O(\tau)
, one gets

\displaystyle H(t|\rho)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\kappa}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}+\kappa(1/\rho+\kappa/D)

(90)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2\frac{\kappa(1/\rho+\kappa/D)^{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{Dt}+O(t).

For the case of
\rho<r<R
, we have

\tilde{H}(p|r)\approx\frac{\rho}{r}e^{-\sqrt{s}(r-\rho)/(R-\rho)}\tilde{H}(p|\rho),

(91)

where we neglected the terms of the order
e^{-2\sqrt{s}}
and

e^{-2\sqrt{s}\delta}
, with
\delta=(R-r)/(R-\rho)
. Note that if

r
is close to
R
(i.e., if
\delta
is very small), the above
approximation would be slightly modified. Since the Laplace transform
is expressed as a product of two terms, the inverse Laplace transform
yields the convolution

H(t|r)\approx\frac{\rho}{r}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}H(t-t^{\prime}|\rho)\frac{(r-\rho)e^{-(r-\rho)^{2}/(4Dt^{\prime})}}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt^{\prime 3}}}\,,

(92)

which can be evaluated using the asymptotic expression for

H(t|\rho)
to give

H(t|r)=\frac{\rho\kappa e^{-(r-\rho)^{2}/(4Dt)}}{r\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\bigg(1+\frac{2Dt(1+\kappa\rho/D)}{\rho(r-\rho)}+\cdots\bigg).

(93)

One can recognize Eq. (
45
) in the leading term, with

\alpha=-1/2
and

C_{\bm{x}_{0}}=\frac{\rho\kappa}{r\sqrt{\pi D}}\,.

(94)

Appendix C
Numerical implementation

Our central formula (
15
) expresses the probability density

H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
in terms of the accessible probabilities
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and

Q(t)
. However, its practical implementation requires the
computation of two Laplace transforms,
\mathcal{L}\{[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\}
and

\mathcal{L}\{[Q(t)]^{N}\}
, and then the evaluation of the inverse Laplace
transform of their ratio. Since both
P(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
Q(t)
are given
as spectral expansions, such a computation becomes numerically
difficult, especially at small and large times when
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

rapidly decays. We also attempted a direct solution of the related
deconvolution problem:

[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}=\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,H_{N}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,[Q(t-t^{\prime})]^{N},

(95)

but it was numerically unstable.

To resolve this difficulty, one can integrate Eq. (
95
)
by parts to transform it into an integral equation on the survival
probability
S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\mathbb{P}\{\mathcal{T}_{N}>t\}
:

\displaystyle[Q(t)]^{N}-[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})

(96)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\,S_{N}(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})\,q_{N}(t-t^{\prime}),

where we used
Q(0)=S_{N}(0|\bm{x}_{0})=1
, and defined

q_{N}(t)=-\frac{d}{dt}[Q(t)]^{N}=-N[Q(t)]^{N-1}\frac{dQ}{dt}\,.

(97)

Considering the last term in Eq. (
96
) as the application
of an integral operator
\mathcal{Q}
to the function
S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
, one can
formally invert this relation to get

S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=(I-\mathcal{Q})^{-1}\bigl([Q(t)]^{N}-[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\bigr),

(98)

where
I
is the identity operator. Expanding the operator

(I-\mathcal{Q})^{-1}
into the geometric series, one finally expresses the
survival probability as

S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})=\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}\bigl([Q(t^{\prime})]^{N}-[P(t^{\prime}|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\bigr)R(t-t^{\prime}),

(99)

where

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(I-\mathcal{Q})^{-1}\delta(t)

(100)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(t)+q_{N}(t)+\int\limits_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\,q_{N}(t_{1})\,q_{N}(t-t_{1})+\ldots,

i.e., the sum of convolutions of
q_{N}(t)
with itself of all orders.

In practice, we compute both
[Q(t)]^{N}-[P(t|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}
and
q_{N}(t)

over a linear grid of points
\{0,\delta,2\delta,\ldots,\delta(K-1)\}
and then evaluate convolutions by fast Fourier transform
(FFT). In this way, one gets the survival probability evaluated at
grid points:

S_{N}(j\delta|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\frac{\mathcal{F}\{p_{k}\}}{1-\mathcal{F}\{q_{k}\}}\right\}\,,

(101)

with
j=0,1,\ldots,K-1
, where
p_{k}=c_{k}\bigl([Q(k\delta)]^{N}-[P(k\delta|\bm{x}_{0})]^{N}\bigr)
,
q_{k}=\delta c_{k}q_{N}(k\delta)
,
c_{0}=1/2
,
c_{k}=1
for
0<k<K
, and
c_{k}=0
for
K\leq k<2K
.
Here the coefficient
c_{0}
accounts for the integration weight
1/2

of the first point, whereas
c_{k}
for
k\geq K
allow one to pad the
vectors by
0
for the proper computation of linear convolutions via
direct (
\mathcal{F}
) and inverse (
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
) FFTs applied to vectors of
length
2K
. Note that the probability density
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
can also
be found via FFT as

H_{N}(j\delta|\bm{x}_{0})\approx\frac{1}{\delta}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{\bigl(e^{2\pi ik/(2K)}-1\bigr)\frac{\mathcal{F}\{p_{k}\}}{1-\mathcal{F}\{q_{k}\}}\right\}\,,

(102)

in analogy with the evaluation of a derivative via standard Fourier
transform:
f^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{ik\mathcal{F}\{f(x)\}\}
.

The time step
\delta
sets the minimal time at which both

S_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
and
H_{N}(t|\bm{x}_{0})
are available, and controls the
accuracy of the whole computation. In fact, it determines how
accurately discrete sums approximate convolution integrals. This is
particularly important for the evaluation of
\mathcal{F}\{q_{k}\}
, whose
maximal value is achieved at the zero frequency:

\displaystyle\max\{|\mathcal{F}\{q_{k}\}|\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{0}\{q_{k}\}=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{2K-1}q_{k}\approx\int\limits_{0}^{t_{\rm max}}dt\,q_{N}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-[Q(t_{\rm max})]^{N}\approx 1-P_{\infty}^{N}<1.

As a consequence,
1/(1-\mathcal{F}\{q_{k}\})
is well defined. However,
when
N
or
k_{\rm off}
increase, the maximum approaches to
1
. If

\delta
is not small enough, inaccurate discretization may result in

\mathcal{F}_{0}\{q_{k}\}
exceeding
1
and thus strong instabilities in the above
computation. For the computation of theoretical curves in
Fig.
2
, we used
\delta=0.01
in all cases, except for
the case
k_{\rm off}=0.03
and
N=3
, for which
\delta=0.005
was
needed.

Appendix D
Monte Carlo simulations

Monte Carlo simulations were realized via a standard event-driven
scheme. Each particle was equipped by its internal “clock”
t_{i}

and the binary state variable
s_{i}
indicating whether the particle is
bound or not. At time
0
, all particles are free (
s_{i}=1
) and
released from a fixed point
\bm{x}_{0}
, with their clocks being set to

0
. The particles diffuse independently and bind the target at
random times sampled from the probability density
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
. The
internal clock of each particle is thus set to its (individual)
first-binding time, while their states are set to
0
(bound). We
emphasize that these FPTs account for partial reactivity of the
target, i.e., for eventual failed binding attempts and reflections
from the target, until the successful binding. Selecting the particle
with the minimal internal clock (say,
t_{i}
), one updates this clock
by adding a random waiting time
\delta_{i}
generated from the
exponential law with the rate
k_{\rm off}
, and sets its state variable

s_{i}
to
1
(free). In other words,
t_{i}
is replaced by

t_{i}+\delta_{i}
, which is the instance when the
i
-th particle unbinds
from the target and resumes its diffusion. From now on, the following
step is repeated: one selects the particle with the minimal internal
time (say,
t_{j}
); if
s_{j}=0
(i.e., at the instance
t_{j}
the
particle binds to the target), we evaluate the number of bound
particles at time
t_{j}
, and the simulation is stopped if all
particles are bound; if the simulation is not stopped, the clock
t_{j}

is updated by adding either a newly generated random waiting time

\delta_{j}
(if
s_{j}=0
), or a random rebinding time
\tau
sampled
from the probability density
H(t)
(if
s_{j}=1
). This step is
repeated until the simulation is stopped (see Fig.
1
).

The first-binding times are generated from the known probability
density
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
given by Eq. (
77
). To sample from a
broad distribution
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
spanning several orders of magnitude in
time, we first perform a change of variable
\zeta=\ln t
, and
obtain the associated probability density
H_{1}(\zeta|\bm{x}_{0})
. Prior to
running simulations, we create a linear grid of possible values

\zeta_{k}
, ranging from
\zeta_{\min}
to
\zeta_{\rm max}
, with a
step
d\zeta=0.01
, and a grid containing the probability weight

H_{1}(\zeta_{k}|\bm{x}_{0})d\zeta
of each value
\zeta_{k}
. Using these
probability weights, a (pseudo)-random value of
\zeta
is generated
by using the Matlab function
randsample
, and the corresponding
first-binding time is obtained as
e^{\zeta}
. The same method is used
for generating rebinding times from the known probability density

H(t)
.

For the considered example of restricted diffusion between two
spheres, the explicit form of the survival probability
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
is
provided in Appendix
B
. The spectral decomposition
(
81
) was truncated at a large order
n=10000
in
order to access accurately the short-time behavior of
H(t|\bm{x}_{0})
.
The zeros
\hat{\alpha}_{n}
of Eq. (
79
) were found by
the bisection method (see
Grebenkov18
;
Reva21
for details). The
grid bounds
\zeta_{\rm min}
and
\zeta_{\rm max}
depend on the
parameters and were chosen manually to cover a broad range of times
whose probability density is not negligible (e.g., we used
\zeta_{\rm min}=-15
and
\zeta_{\rm max}=10
for computing
H(t)
for

\kappa=1
).
