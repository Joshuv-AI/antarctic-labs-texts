Enriched categories and tropical mathematics
Soichiro Fujii

Abstract
. This is a survey paper on the connection of enriched category theory over a quantale and tropical mathematics. Quantales or complete idempotent semirings, as well as matrices with coefficients in them, are fundamental objects in both fields. We first explain standard category-theoretic constructions on matrices, namely composition, right extension, right lifting and the Isbell hull. Along the way, we review known reformulations (due to Elliott and Willerton) of tropical polytopes, directed tight spans and the Legendre–Fenchel transform by means of these constructions, illustrating their ubiquity in tropical mathematics and related fields. We then consider complete semimodules over a quantale 𝒬 {\mathcal{Q}} , a tropical analogue of vector spaces over a field, and mention Stubbe’s result identifying them with skeletal and complete 𝒬 {\mathcal{Q}} -categories. With the aim to bridge a gap between enriched category theory and tropical mathematics, we assume no knowledge in either field.

1.
Introduction

Tropical mathematics
(also called idempotent mathematics) is mathematics over an idempotent semiring, instead of over the fields of real or complex numbers as in many branches of ordinary mathematics.
A prototypical example of idempotent semirings is the so-called
min-plus semiring

([-\infty,\infty],\infty,0,\min,+)
, defined as the set
[-\infty,\infty]
of extended real numbers equipped with the minimum operation as (idempotent) addition and the ordinary addition
+
as multiplication.
Such structures naturally arise in many branches of mathematical sciences, including operations research
[
CG91
]
, formal language theory
[
Sim94
,
Pin98
]
, mathematical physics
[
LMS01
]
and algebraic geometry
[
Vir01
]
.

Enriched categories
, on the other hand, are a generalisation of categories
[
ML98
]
defined relative to a
base
.
We can choose arbitrary
monoidal categories
as the base, of which idempotent semirings are a special case. The theory works best when the base enjoys certain completeness properties, and in the case of idempotent semiring, imposing these additional completeness assumptions results in the notion of (unital)
quantale

[
Mul86
]
.
Preordered sets, metric spaces and ultrametric spaces are all instances of enriched categories over a quantale
[
Law73
]
.

In this paper we take a look at a branch of tropical mathematics, namely
linear algebra over a quantale
(cf.
[
CGQ04
]
), from the enriched categorical perspective.
We believe that enriched category theory can provide an abstract framework to tropical mathematics, which would help us to recognise various easy facts that hold for purely formal reasons, and focus on genuine problems.
On the other hand, tropical mathematics can offer novel interpretation to category theoretic concepts, which might eventually lead to discovery of useful categorical notions of tropical origin, along the lines of Lawvere’s notion of Cauchy completeness generalised from metric spaces to enriched categories
[
Law73
]
.
The purpose of this paper is to lay the foundations for a further fruitful interplay between enriched categories and tropical mathematics, by collecting basic notions and surveying known results which suggest various links between the two fields.
Accordingly, we have tried to make this paper mostly self-contained and accessible to both category theorists and tropical mathematicians.

The outline of this paper is as follows.
After reviewing the notion of quantale (Section
2
), we move on to study matrices over a quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}
(called
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrices) in Section
3
.
Matrices over a quantale admit not only composition, but also the operations of
right extension
and
right lifting
, which may be computed by the formulas dual to the one for composition. Right extensions and right liftings of
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrices enable us to define the
Isbell hull
of a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix (Section
4
), which is known to unify such constructions as directed tight spans of metric spaces
[
Wil13
]
, lower semicontinuous convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
Wil15
]
and tropical polytopes
[
Ell17
]
; we also provide brief expositions of these results.

In Section
5
we study complete semimodules over a quantale (cf.
[
CGQ04
,
BK12
]
), a tropical analogue of vector spaces over a field.
It turns out that the Isbell hull of a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix naturally admits the structure of a complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
, and conversely, any complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
can be realised as the Isbell hull of some
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix.
In Section
6
we introduce categories enriched over a quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}
(called
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories). Any complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
naturally induces a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category, and those
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories arising from a complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
are characterised by the intrinsic properties of
skeletality
and
completeness

[
Stu06
]
.

2.
Quantales

We first introduce
quantales
, also known as
complete
idempotent semirings
.
They are the enriching (or base) categories
in the portion of enriched category theory we shall be concerned with;
in tropical mathematics, elements of a quantale are often considered as

scalars
, and play the role similar to that of real or complex numbers in ordinary mathematics.

Definition 2.1

(
[
Mul86
]
)
.

A (unital)
quantale

{\mathcal{Q}}

is a complete lattice
(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}})

equipped with a monoid structure
(Q,I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{\mathcal{Q}})

such that the multiplication
\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}
preserves arbitrary suprema each
variable:

(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i})\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x=\bigvee_{i\in I}(y_{i}\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x)
and

y\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i})=\bigvee_{i\in I}(y\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x_{i})
.
We often omit the subscript
{\mathcal{Q}}
from the data of a quantale,
writing it simply as
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq,I,\circ)
.

Though we have introduced quantales in an order-theoretic manner, we could have
introduced them in a more algebraic manner.
In tropical mathematics, it is customary to start from the notion of
idempotent semiring
, namely a semiring
(Q,\mathbf{0},I,\oplus,\circ)
with an idempotent addition:

x\oplus x=x
.
Given such an idempotent semiring,
its additive part
(Q,\mathbf{0},\oplus)
is an idempotent commutative monoid, and thus
induces a partial order
\preceq
on
Q

by
x\preceq y
if and only if
x\oplus y=y
.
The resulting poset
(Q,\preceq)
is an
(upper) semilattice (= poset with all finite suprema), with the least element
\mathbf{0}
and the binary supremum operation
\oplus
.
Therefore quantales may also be construed as idempotent semirings with
an additional
completeness
property.

Notice that in the definition of quantale,
we do not assume commutativity of the multiplication
\circ
by default;
quantales with commutative multiplication are said to be

commutative
.
Although many examples of quantales we shall consider below are
in fact commutative, we work within the more general noncommutative
setting in order to underline a certain parallelism between the structure of a
quantale and that of
matrices
(of arbitrary dimensions) with
coefficients in a quantale (Section
3
).
For matrices the multiplication (or composition) is not commutative; indeed,
commutativity of multiplication does not even make sense.

The notion of
adjunction
between posets is central to our approach (see also
[
CGQ04
,
BK12
]
).
Recall that given posets
(L,\preceq)
and
(L^{\prime},\preceq^{\prime})
,
two functions
f\colon L\longrightarrow L^{\prime}

and
u\colon L^{\prime}\longrightarrow L
are said to form an
adjunction

(or
Galois connection
)
if for any
l\in L
and
l^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}
,

(1)

f(l)\preceq^{\prime}l^{\prime}\iff l\preceq u(l^{\prime})

holds.
We call
f
the
left adjoint
of
u
and
u
the
right
adjoint
of
f
,
and write them as
f\dashv u
.
The adjointness relation (
1
) is powerful enough to
determine each of the functions
f
and
u
from the other, and force both of
them to be
monotone
functions (see
[
Str12
]
).

We record the following well-known fact, to which we shall resort frequently in
the sequel.

Proposition 2.2

(See e.g.,
[
DP02
, Proposition 7.34]
)
.

Let
(L,\preceq_{L})
be a complete lattice and
(P,\preceq_{P})
be a poset.
A function
f\colon L\longrightarrow P
preserves arbitrary suprema
if and only if there exists a function
u\colon P\longrightarrow L

such that
f\dashv u
.

As the first application of Proposition
2.2
,
observe that in any quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq,

I,\circ)

there are two
residuation
operations:
for any
x\in Q
, the function
(-)\circ x\colon Q\longrightarrow Q

preserves arbitrary suprema, and hence has a right adjoint

(-)\mathbin{\swarrow}x\colon Q\longrightarrow Q

called the
right extension along
x
;
similarly, for any
y\in Q
the function

y\circ(-)
has a right adjoint
y\mathbin{\searrow}(-)
,
called the
right lifting along
y
.
Of course, in a commutative quantale the right extensions and right liftings
coincide.
The defining adjointness relations are:

(2)

y\preceq z\mathbin{\swarrow}x\iff y\circ x\preceq z\iff x\preceq y\mathbin{\searrow}z.

Focusing on the leftmost and rightmost formulas of
(
2
), we obtain

z\mathbin{\swarrow}x\succeq y\iff x\preceq y\mathbin{\searrow}z.

That is,
z\mathbin{\swarrow}(-)\colon Q\longrightarrow Q
(regarded as a
function from the poset
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq)
to its
dual
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}=(Q,\succeq)
) is the left
adjoint of
(-)\mathbin{\searrow}z\colon Q\longrightarrow{Q}
(from
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}
to
{\mathcal{Q}}
)
for any
z\in Q
.
The three types of adjunctions

{\mathcal{Q}}
{\mathcal{Q}}
(-)\circ x
(-)\mathbin{\swarrow}x
\dashv

{\mathcal{Q}}
{\mathcal{Q}}
y\circ(-)
y\mathbin{\searrow}(-)
\dashv

{\mathcal{Q}}
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}
z\mathbin{\swarrow}(-)
(-)\mathbin{\searrow}z
\dashv

are fundamental in theory of quantales.
In Sections
3
and
5
, we shall see that similar structures arise from matrices with coefficients in a quantale, and complete semimodules over a quantale, respectively.

We conclude this section with some examples of quantales.

.

The

.
Here,
([-\infty,\infty],\leq)

\neq-\infty

x\mapsto-x

Example 2.4
.

The
min-plus quantale

{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}=([-\infty,\infty],\geq,0,+)
.
The underlying poset
([-\infty,\infty],\geq)
is the poset
(\mathbb{R},\geq)
of all real numbers ordered by the
opposite
of the usual order, extended with the
greatest
element
-\infty
and the
least
element
\infty
.
The extension of
+
is again uniquely determined by the requirement that it should be part of a quantale structure.

In fact, the quantale
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
is isomorphic to
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{max},+}}
under the mapping
x\mapsto-x
.
So they are just different ways of presenting the same structure.
However, note that this does not mean that the operation tables are the
same
for
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{max},+}}
and
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
; for instance, we have
-\infty+\infty=-\infty

in
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{max},+}}
, but have
-\infty+\infty=\infty
in
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
.

Example 2.5
.

The
nonnegative min-plus quantale
(also called the
Lawvere quantale
)
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}=([0,\infty],\geq,0,+)
.
This is
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
restricted to the nonnegative (i.e.,
\geq 0
) part.
The
+
operation is the restriction of
+
for
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
to
[0,\infty]
, which is anyway the only sensible extension of the ordinary addition.
The right extension/right lifting is given by an extension of the

truncated subtraction
\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}
, defined by

u\mathbin{\text{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 4.30554pt\hbox{.}\cr$-$\cr}}}}t=\max\{u-t,0\}
for nonnegative real numbers
t
and
u
.
This quantale is introduced in
[
Law73
]
for a category-theoretic approach to the theory of metric spaces.

Note that by restricting the isomorphism between
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{max},+}}
and
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
,
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
is isomorphic to the
nonpositive max-plus quantale
(in the obvious sense), but it is not isomorphic to “the nonnegative max-plus quantale”; indeed, there is no quantale that deserves this name.
It is impossible to endow a quantale structure on the poset
([0,\infty],\leq)
in such a way that its multiplication is given by an extension of the ordinary addition
+
, because the quantale axioms would force
0+x=0
for all
x\in[0,\infty]
.

Example 2.6
.

One can also consider the “discrete” variants of the quantales
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{max},+}}
(
\cong{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
) or
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
, replacing the real numbers by the integers.
The discrete variant of
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
is used in
[
Fuj14
]
in connection to discrete convex analysis
[
Mur03
]
.

Example 2.7
.

The
nonnegative min-max quantale

{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min,max}}}=([0,\infty],\geq,0,\max)
.
Its underlying poset
([0,\infty],\geq)
is the same as that of
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
. We take the binary max operation with respect to the usual ordering
\leq
, namely the binary infimum operation with respect to
\geq
, as the multiplication. The right extension/right lifting is given by

z\mathbin{\swarrow}y=y\mathbin{\searrow}z=\begin{cases}0\ \text{ if }y\geq z\\
z\ \text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

This quantale is related to (a generalisation of) ultrametric spaces.

We remark that more generally, any
locale
, i.e., a complete lattice in which the binary infimum operation
\wedge
satisfies the infinitary distributive law
(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i})\wedge x=\bigvee_{i\in I}(y_{i}\wedge x)
, acquires a quantale structure with
\wedge
as the multiplication;
indeed quantales were first introduced as a quantum theoretic generalisation of locales
[
Mul86
]
.
The poset
([0,\infty],\geq)
, or more generally any totally ordered complete lattice, is a locale.

Example 2.8
.

The
truth value quantale
(or the
two-element quantale
)
{\mathbf{2}}=(\{\bot,\top\},\vdash,\top,\wedge)
.
The underlying poset of this quantale consists of
\top
for “truth” and
\bot
for “falsity”, ordered by the entailment relation
\vdash
, so that
\bot\vdash\top
.
The monoid structure is given by conjunction
\wedge
.
This is also used in
[
Law73
]
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,e,\cdot)
be a monoid. The

free quantale generated by
{\mathcal{M}}

is
\mathcal{P}{\mathcal{M}}=(\mathcal{P}M,\subseteq,\{e\},\cdot)
,
where
\mathcal{P}M
is the power set of
M
and the multiplication
\cdot
on
\mathcal{P}M
is the unique supremum-preserving extension of the original multiplication on
M
, which is given by

A\cdot B=\{\,a\cdot b\mid a\in A,b\in B\,\}

for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}M
.
Unlike the previous examples, this quantale is not commutative unless
{\mathcal{M}}
is.
It has been used in connection to formal language theory, both from the categorical side
[
Ros95
]
as well as from the tropical side
[
Pin98
]
.

Example 2.10
.

Let
A
be a set. The poset
(\mathcal{P}(A\times A),\subseteq)

of all binary relations on
A
admits a quantale structure

(\mathcal{P}(A\times A),\subseteq,I_{A},\circ)
, where
I_{A}
denotes the diagonal relation on
A
and
\circ
denotes composition of relations.
This quantale is not commutative in general.
In fact, this is obtained from
{\mathbf{2}}
via a general construction of
matrix quantale
; see the next section.

3.
Matrices

In this section we study matrices over a quantale.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quantale, and
A
and
B
be (possibly infinite) sets.
A
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix
(or simply a
matrix
)
from
A
to
B
is a
(B\times A)
-indexed
family of elements of
Q
.
If
X=(X_{b,a})_{b\in B,a\in A}
is a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix from
A
to
B
,
we denote it by
X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}B
.

In particular, if
A=\{\,1,\dots,m\,\}
and
B=\{\,1,\dots,n\,\}

for some natural numbers
m
and
n
, then a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix

X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}B
is nothing but an
(n\times m)
-matrix with coefficients in
{\mathcal{Q}}
, which we may write as

(3)

X=\left[\begin{tabular}[]{c c c c}$X_{1,1}$&$X_{1,2}$&$\cdots$&$X_{1,m}$\\
$X_{2,1}$&$X_{2,2}$&$\cdots$&$X_{2,m}$\\
$\vdots$&$\vdots$&$\ddots$&$\vdots$\\
$X_{n,1}$&$X_{n,2}$&$\cdots$&$X_{n,m}$\\
\end{tabular}\right]

as usual.

For any set
A
we have the
identity matrix

I_{A}

(I_{A})_{a,a^{\prime}}=\begin{cases}I_{\mathcal{Q}}&\text{ if }a=a^{\prime},\\
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{Q}}&\text{ otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{Q}}
denotes the least element in
(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}})
.
Given matrices
X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}B
and
Y\colon B\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}C
,
we may define their
composition

Y\circ X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}C
as follows:

(4)

(Y\circ X)_{c,a}=\bigvee_{b\in B}(Y_{c,b}\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}X_{b,a}).

Since the supremum
\bigvee
corresponds to (possibly infinitary) addition in a quantale,
(
4
) may be seen as the tropical analogue of the usual matrix multiplication formula.
It can be easily checked that the composition operation satisfies the familiar laws, namely

(5)

X\circ I_{A}=X,\quad I_{B}\circ X=X\quad\text{and}\quad Z\circ(Y\circ X)=(Z\circ Y)\circ X

for all
X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}B
,
Y\colon B\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}C
and
Z\colon C\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}D
.

We put a partial order
\preceq_{A,B}
on the set
{{{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbf{Mat}}(A,B)

of all
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrices from
A
to
B
in an entrywise manner, namely

X\preceq_{A,B}X^{\prime}
if and only if for all
b\in B
and
a\in A
,
X_{b,a}\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}X^{\prime}_{b,a}
.
The poset
({{{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbf{Mat}}(A,B),\preceq_{A,B})
is isomorphic
to the
(B\times A)
-fold product of
(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}})
, and hence is again
a complete lattice.
We sometimes omit the subscripts
A,B
in
\preceq_{A,B}
.
One easily verifies that the composition operation
preserves arbitrary suprema in each variable:

(6)

(\bigvee_{i\in I}Y_{i})\circ X=\bigvee_{i\in I}(Y_{i}\circ X)\quad\text{and}\quad Y\circ(\bigvee_{i\in I}X_{i})=\bigvee_{i\in I}(Y\circ X_{i})

Example 3.2
.

Willerton
[

]
observes that the
Legendre–Fenchel transform (see e.g.,
[
Roc70
, Section 12]
) is an instance of right extension/right lifting of matrices.
Recall that the Legendre–Fenchel transform on a finite-dimensional real vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
maps any function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
to its
conjugate
, which is a function
f^{\ast}
of type
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}\longrightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
,
where
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the dual vector space of the original
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Using the
pairing function

{\langle-,-\rangle}
defined by
{\langle p,v\rangle}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}v_{i}
for each
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}
and

v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the formula for the conjugate
f^{\ast}
is given by

(12)

f^{\ast}(p)=\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{{\langle p,v\rangle}-f(v)\}

for each
p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}
.

To understand this construction via matrices, we first regard the pairing function
{\langle-,-\rangle}
as an
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-matrix
{\langle-,-\rangle}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}
. A function
f
of type
\mathbb{R}^{n}\longrightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
may be identified with an
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-matrix
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}1
, and likewise a function
g
of type
(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}\longrightarrow[-\infty,\infty]
with an
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-matrix
g\colon 1\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}
. Then we have

; see (
10
). Note that infima in

(denoted by

in (

in (

The Legendre–Fenchel transform works in the converse direction as well, mapping a function

to its conjugate

given by essentially the same formula:

g^{\ast}(v)=\sup_{p\in(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\ast}}\{{\langle p,v\rangle}-g(p)\}.

Of course, this can be expressed as
g^{\ast}=g\mathbin{\searrow}{\langle-,-\rangle}
using matrices.

The Legendre–Fenchel transform is an important tool in convex analysis.
One reason for this importance is that for any function

,
(f^{\ast})^{\ast}
is its

, i.e., the least (with respect to the pointwise order
\leq
) lower-semicontinuous convex function above

In particular, lower-semicontinuous convex functions can be characterised as the “fixed points” of the Legendre–Fenchel transform; we shall revisit this point in the next section.

4.
The Isbell hull of a matrix

In this section we introduce the notion of Isbell hull of a matrix.
It incorporates many important constructions in tropical mathematics and related fields, such as the tropical polytope generated by a set of points
[

]
, the directed tight span of a (generalised) metric space
[

,
KKO12
]
, and the set of lower-semicontinuous convex functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Definition 4.1

(
[
SZ13
, Section 4]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}
be a quantale and

be a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix.
The
Isbell hull of
Z
, denoted by
\mathrm{Isb}(Z)
,
is the set of all pairs
(X,Y)
of
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrices
X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}1
and

Y\colon 1\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}C
such that both
Y=Z\mathbin{\swarrow}X
and

hold.

In this section we treat the Isbell hull of a matrix only as
a

of certain pairs of matrices, but in fact it has rich structure, as we shall see in Section

The term “Isbell hull” is already used in

The Isbell hull construction, like many important constructions, may be viewed from several different angles.
The first alternative view is provided by the following.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
Z\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}C
be a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix. A pair
(X,Y)\in{{{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbf{Mat}}(A,1)\times{{{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbf{Mat}}(1,C)
belongs to
\mathrm{Isb}(Z)

if and only if it is a maximal pair under-approximating
Z
,
in the sense that (i)
Y\circ X\preceq Z
, and (ii) if
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in{{{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbf{Mat}}(A,1)\times{{{\mathcal{Q}}}\mathchar 45\relax\mathbf{Mat}}(1,C)

X\preceq X^{\prime}

and
Y\preceq Y^{\prime}

X=X^{\prime}
and
Y=Y^{\prime}

Proof.

Suppose

Conversely, suppose that

Complete semimodules

In this section we study the notion of complete semimodule over a quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}
. It will turn out that the Isbell hull of any
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix acquires such structure and conversely, any complete semimodule is isomorphic to the Isbell hull of itself (regarded as a

Definition 5.1

(
[
CGQ04
, Section 2.2]
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}},I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{{\mathcal{Q}}})
be a quantale.
A
(left) complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a
complete lattice
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,\preceq_{{\mathcal{M}}})
equipped with a left action
\ast_{{\mathcal{M}}}\colon Q\times M\longrightarrow M

of the monoid
(Q,I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{\mathcal{Q}})
on the set

,
such that
\ast_{\mathcal{M}}
preserves arbitrary suprema in each argument:

(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i})\ast_{\mathcal{M}}m=\bigvee_{i\in I}(x_{i}\ast_{\mathcal{M}}m)
and

x\ast_{\mathcal{M}}(\bigvee_{i\in I}m_{i})=\bigvee_{i\in I}(x\ast_{\mathcal{M}}m_{i})
.

A
right complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a left complete semimodule over the quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{rev}}=(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}},I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ^{\mathrm{rev}}_{\mathcal{Q}})
, where
x\circ^{\mathrm{rev}}_{\mathcal{Q}}y=y\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}x
.

Analogously to the case of quantale, there is an algebraic variant of the above order-theoretic definition.
In general, a
semimodule over a semiring
{\mathcal{Q}}
is a commutative monoid
{\mathcal{M}}
equipped with an action of
{\mathcal{Q}}
, which may be succinctly expressed as a semiring homomorphism from
{\mathcal{Q}}
to the semiring
\mathrm{End}({\mathcal{M}})
of all endomorphisms on
{\mathcal{M}}
.
If
{\mathcal{Q}}
is an idempotent semiring then the underlying commutative monoid
{\mathcal{M}}
of any semimodule over it is necessarily idempotent, since the existence of a semiring homomorphism
{\mathcal{Q}}\longrightarrow\mathrm{End}({\mathcal{M}})
forces
{\mathrm{id}_{{\mathcal{M}}}}\in\mathrm{End}({\mathcal{M}})
to be idempotent (with respect to addition).
So a semimodule over an idempotent semiring can be equivalently given as an (upper) semilattice with an appropriate action, and Definition
5.1
may be viewed as a
complete
version of it.

Applying Proposition
2.2
, we obtain two residuation
operations associated with a complete semimodule

{\mathcal{M}}=(M,\preceq_{\mathcal{M}},\ast_{\mathcal{M}})
:
for each
m\in M
, the right adjoint
(-)\mathbin{\swarrow}_{\mathcal{M}}m\colon M\longrightarrow Q
of
(-)\ast_{\mathcal{M}}m\colon Q\longrightarrow M
, and for each
x\in Q
,
the right adjoint
x\mathbin{\searrow}_{\mathcal{M}}(-)\colon M\longrightarrow M
of

x\ast_{\mathcal{M}}(-)\colon M\longrightarrow M
.
We again have the adjointness relation

(14)

x\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}n\mathbin{\swarrow}_{\mathcal{M}}m\iff x\ast_{\mathcal{M}}m\preceq_{\mathcal{M}}n\iff m\preceq_{\mathcal{M}}x\mathbin{\searrow}_{\mathcal{M}}n

for each

and

.
Adopting terminology from enriched category theory, we call the function

\ast_{\mathcal{M}}\colon{\mathcal{Q}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}

the

, the function

\mathbin{\swarrow}_{{\mathcal{M}}}\colon{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathrm{op}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{Q}}

, and the function

\mathbin{\searrow}_{{\mathcal{M}}}\colon{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}

[

Example 5.3

Here are some immediate examples of (left or right) complete semimodules over a quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}},I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{\mathcal{Q}})

{\mathcal{Q}}
unless otherwise stated.
First, clearly the underlying complete lattice
(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}})
of

The dual right complete semimodule

corresponding to it (Proposition

On the other hand, the complete lattice

Because left or right complete semimodules are closed under arbitrary products, for any pair of sets

This is indeed possible, by means of the hom-functor.
The resulting notion is

-category

Y\colon 1\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}C
.
The key observation is that the equation
X=Y\mathbin{\searrow}Z
may be regarded as an expression of
X
by a “tropical linear combination (in
({\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{op}})^{A}
)” of the row vectors
Z_{c,-}
.
More precisely, we read from the formula (
11
) for right lifting that we have

(15)

X=Y\mathbin{\searrow}Z=\bigwedge_{c\in C}(Y_{c,\ast}\mathbin{\searrow}Z_{c,-}).

Observe that the column vector
Y
plays the role of “coefficients” of the above tropical linear combination.
Any row vector
X
as in (
15
), expressible as the infimum of powers of
Z_{c,-}
, must lie in any right complete subsemimodule containing the row vectors
Z_{c,-}
.
Also we see that for any
c\in C
, the row vector
Z_{c,-}

itself may be expressed as a trivial linear combination, upon taking
Y
to be

Y_{c^{\prime},\ast}=\begin{cases}I_{Q}&\text{if }c=c^{\prime},\\
\mathbf{0}_{\mathcal{Q}}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

That the set
\mathrm{Isb}(Z)
of all row vectors expressible as (
15
) is closed under infima and the power
x\mathbin{\searrow}(-)
by an element
x
of
{\mathcal{Q}}
may be checked easily.
Precisely, given a family
X_{i}=Y_{i}\mathbin{\searrow}Z
, we may take new coefficient
\bigvee_{i\in I}Y_{i}
to express their infimum:
\bigwedge_{i\in I}X_{i}=(\bigvee_{i\in I}Y_{i})\mathbin{\searrow}Z
.
Given
X=Y\mathbin{\searrow}Z
, we have
x\mathbin{\searrow}X=(Y\circ x)\mathbin{\searrow}Z
.
∎

As a consequence of this proposition, the Isbell hull of any
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix acquires the structure of a left complete
{\mathcal{Q}}
-semimodule.

6.
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories

In this section, we introduce
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories for a quantale
{\mathcal{Q}}
. They are instances of the well-studied notion of enriched category
[
Kel82
]
.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}=(Q,\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}},I_{\mathcal{Q}},\circ_{\mathcal{Q}})
be a quantale.
A
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category

{\mathcal{C}}
consists of:

(CD1):

a set
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
of
objects
;

(CD2):

for each
c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
, an element
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\in{Q}

satisfying the following axioms:

(CA1):

for each
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
I_{\mathcal{Q}}\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c)
;

(CA2):

for each
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})\circ_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime\prime})
.

We also write
c\in{\mathcal{C}}
for
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
.

Note that a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}
can be identified with a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix
{\mathcal{C}}\colon{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}

with
{\mathcal{C}}_{c^{\prime},c}={\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})
.
In fact, a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category may be defined more succinctly as a set
A
(corresponding to (CD1)) together with a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-matrix
X\colon A\mathrel{\mathchoice{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\displaystyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\displaystyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\textstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\textstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\mapstochar\mkern 5.0mu$\cr$\scriptscriptstyle\longrightarrow$\cr}}}}A
(CD2) on it such that
I_{A}\preceq_{A,A}X
(CA1) and
X\circ X\preceq_{A,A}X
(CA2) hold
[
BCSW83
]
.

Example 6.2
.

In the case
{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathbf{2}}
, we may identify the data of a
{\mathbf{2}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}=({\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})},({\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime}))_{c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}})
with a set
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
equipped with a binary relation
\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}
on it (defined as the set of all pairs
(c,c^{\prime})\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}\times{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
with
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})=\top
).
The axioms (CA1) and (CA2) for a
{\mathbf{2}}
-category then translate to reflexivity and transitivity of
\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}
respectively, hence a
{\mathbf{2}}
-category is nothing but a
preordered set
.

Example 6.3
.

In the case
{\mathcal{Q}}={\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
, we may regard
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-categories as
generalised metric spaces

[
Law73
]
(cf. Example
4.6
);
objects of an
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}
are thought of as
points
and the element
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\in[0,\infty]
as the
distance from
c
to
c^{\prime}
.
Notice that the axioms for
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-category indeed translate to some of the axioms for metric spaces:

(CA1):

for each
c\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
0\geq{\mathcal{C}}(c,c)
(that is,
{\mathcal{C}}(c,c)=0
); and

(CA2):

for each
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime})+{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\geq{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime\prime})

Every metric space is an

-category, but not conversely.
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min},+}}
-categories are more general than metric spaces in the following three aspects:

•

distance may attain
\infty
;

•

distance is non-symmetric (or

{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})
may not be equal to

; and

•

{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})={\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c)=0

.

Example 6.4
.

Similarly,
{\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{\geq 0}_{\mathrm{min,max}}}
-categories may be regarded as
generalised ultrametric spaces
; note that axiom (CA2) now reads:

(CA2):

for each
c,c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
,
\max\{\,{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},c^{\prime\prime}),{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime})\,\}\geq{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime\prime})
.

As we have already mentioned, every left complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
gives rise to a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category. The precise construction is as follows.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,\preceq_{\mathcal{M}},\ast_{{\mathcal{M}}})
be a left complete semimodule over
{\mathcal{Q}}
. The following data defines a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category, again called
{\mathcal{M}}
:

(CD1):

the set of objects is
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{M}})}=M
; and

(CD2):

for each
m,m^{\prime}\in M
, the element
{\mathcal{M}}(m,m^{\prime})\in Q
is defined as
m^{\prime}\mathbin{\swarrow}_{\mathcal{M}}m
(note the order of the arguments
m
and
m^{\prime}
).

The axioms can be checked easily by means of the adjointness relations (
14
).

The structure of a left complete semimodule
{\mathcal{M}}
, namely the order relation
\preceq_{\mathcal{M}}
and the action
\ast_{\mathcal{M}}
, can be recovered from the induced
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category
{\mathcal{M}}=(M,({\mathcal{M}}(m,m^{\prime}))_{m,m^{\prime}\in M})
.
To see this, first note that any
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}=({\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})},({\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime}))_{c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}})
determines a preorder relation
\preceq_{{\mathcal{C}}}
on
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
by

(17)

c\preceq_{{\mathcal{C}}}c^{\prime}\ \text{ if and only if }\ I_{\mathcal{Q}}\preceq_{\mathcal{Q}}{\mathcal{C}}(c,c^{\prime}).

Two objects
c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
are said to be
isomorphic
if both
c\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}c^{\prime}
and
c^{\prime}\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}c
hold.
Isomorphic objects behave exactly in the same manner, namely if
c,c^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
are isomorphic then for every object
d\in{\mathcal{C}}
, we have
{\mathcal{C}}(c,d)={\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},d)
and
{\mathcal{C}}(d,c)={\mathcal{C}}(d,c^{\prime})
.
We call a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}

skeletal
if isomorphic objects in
{\mathcal{C}}
are equal, i.e., if the induced preorder relation
\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}
on
{\mathrm{ob}({\mathcal{C}})}
is antisymmetric and is a partial order relation.
Now, if a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category
{\mathcal{M}}
is derived from a left complete semimodule
{\mathcal{M}}
by the construction of Definition
6.5
, then we actually recover the original order relation on the complete semimodule by (
17
) (see (
14
)).
The action
\ast_{\mathcal{M}}
is then uniquely determined from the hom-functor
{\mathcal{M}}(-,-)
via the adjunction
(-)\ast_{\mathcal{M}}m\dashv{\mathcal{M}}(m,-)
.

In fact, it is also possible to define the counterpart of
\ast_{\mathcal{M}}
for general
{\mathcal{Q}}
-categories.
Given a
{\mathcal{Q}}
-category
{\mathcal{C}}

is said to be a

{\mathcal{C}}(c^{\prime},d)={\mathcal{C}}(c,d)\mathbin{\swarrow}x

Copowers of

may or may not exist in

is a copower of
c
by

is also a copower of

and

In particular, in a sketetal

There is also a dual notion of

such that for any

A

•

powered
