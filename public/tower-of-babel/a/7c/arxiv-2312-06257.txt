On Some Extensions of π \pi -Regular Rings
Peter Danchev, Arash Javan, Ahmad Moussavi

Abstract
. Some variations of π \pi -regular and nil clean rings were recently introduced in [ 5 , 7 , 6 ] , respectively. In this paper, we examine the structure and relationships between these classes of rings. Specifically, we prove that ( m , n ) (m,n) -regularly nil clean rings are left-right symmetric and also show that the inclusions ( D D -regularly nil clean) ⊆ \subseteq (regularly nil clean) ⊆ \subseteq ( ( m , n ) (m,n) -regularly nil clean) hold, as well as we answer Questions 1, 2 and 3 posed in [ 7 ] . Moreover, some other analogous questions concerning the symmetric properties of certain classes of rings are treated as well by proving that centrally Utumi rings are always strongly π \pi -regular.

2.
The relationship between regularly nil clean and
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean rings

We begin our work with the following technicality.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean rings
R
are left-right symmetric in the sense that, for any
a\in R
, there exists
f\in Id(R)\cap a^{n}Ra^{m}
with
a^{n}(1-f)a^{m}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
.

Proof.

In view of Definition
1.1
above, given that
e\in a^{m}Ra^{n}
such that
a^{n}(1-e)a^{m}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
m<n
. Write
e=a^{m}ra^{n}
, and set
f:=(a^{n}ra^{m})^{2}
. Thus,

\displaystyle f^{2}

\displaystyle=(a^{n}ra^{m})(a^{n}ra^{m})(a^{n}ra^{m})(a^{n}ra^{m})

\displaystyle=a^{n-m}(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{m})

\displaystyle=a^{n-m}(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{m})=(a^{n}ra^{m})(a^{n}ra^{m})=f.

Also,

fa^{n}=(a^{n}ra^{m})(a^{n}ra^{m})a^{n}=a^{n-m}(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{n})a^{m}=a^{n-m}ea^{m}.

This allows us to infer that

a^{m}(1-f)a^{n}=a^{m}(a^{n}-fa^{n})=a^{m}(a^{n}-a^{n-m}ea^{m})=

=a^{n}a^{m}-a^{n}ea^{m}=a^{n}(1-e)a^{m}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Therefore, the relation
a^{n}(1-f)a^{m}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
follows now immediately.
∎

Our next statement we are planning to prove is the following.

Proposition 2.2
.

Regularly nil clean rings are
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean for every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
R
is a regularly nil clean ring. Let
a\in R
be an arbitrary element and set
k:=n+m
, so
a^{k}\in R
. By definition, there is
e\in a^{k}R
such that
(1-e)a^{k}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Write
e=a^{k}r
for some
r\in R
, and put
r^{\prime}:=re
and
f:=a^{m}r^{\prime}a^{n}
. Thus,
e=a^{k}r^{\prime}
,
r^{\prime}=r^{\prime}e
and

f^{2}=a^{m}r^{\prime}a^{k}r^{\prime}a^{n}=a^{m}r^{\prime}ea^{n}=a^{m}r^{\prime}a^{n}=f.

Also,

a^{n}f=a^{n}a^{m}r^{\prime}a^{n}=a^{k}r^{\prime}a^{n}=ea^{n}.

Then, we have

a^{n}(1-f)a^{m}=(a^{n}-a^{n}f)a^{m}=(a^{n}-ea^{n})a^{m}=(1-e)a^{k}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Therefore,
a^{m}(1-f)a^{n}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, and so the result follows.
∎

We are now prepared to establish the following assertion.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
R
be a ring and
m,n,p,q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
m+n=p+q
. Then,
R
is an
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean ring if, and only if,
R
is a
(p,q)
-regularly nil clean.

Proof.

Suppose that
e\in a^{m}Ra^{n}
such that
(1-e)a^{m+n}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
m>p
. Write
e=a^{m}ra^{n}
, and define
f:=a^{p}ra^{p+q}ra^{q}\in a^{p}Ra^{q}
. Then,

\displaystyle f^{2}

\displaystyle=(a^{p}ra^{p+q}ra^{q})(a^{p}ra^{p+q}ra^{q})=(a^{p}ra^{m+n}ra^{q})(a^{p}ra^{m+n}ra^{q})

\displaystyle=a^{p}ra^{n}(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{n})(a^{m}ra^{n})a^{q-n}=a^{p}ra^{n}(a^{m}ra^{n})a^{q-n}

\displaystyle=a^{p}ra^{n+m}ra^{q}=a^{p}ra^{p+q}ra^{q}=f.

However, we can see that
a^{m-p}f=ea^{q-n}
. Also,

a^{m-p}(1-f)a^{n+p}=(a^{m-p}-a^{m-p}f)a^{n+p}=(a^{m-p}-ea^{q-n})a^{n+p}=

=a^{m+n}-ea^{p+q}=a^{m+n}-ea^{m+n}=(1-e)a^{m+n}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

So,

a^{m-p}(1-f)a^{n+p}\in{\rm Nil}(R),

but

a^{p}(1-f)a^{q}\in{\rm Nil}(R)

if, and only if,

a^{p+q}(1-f)\in{\rm Nil}(R)

if, and only if,

a^{m+n}(1-f)\in{\rm Nil}(R)

if, and only if,

a^{m+n+p-p}(1-f)\in{\rm Nil}(R)

if, and only if,

a^{m-p}(1-f)a^{n+p}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Consequently,
R
is a
(p,q)
-regularly nil clean ring, as asserted.
∎

The following technical claim is useful. Recall that a ring

Lemma 2.5

Let

-regularly nil clean ring such that

Assume

We now can attack the following basic statement which focuses on some common extensions of commutative rings. In it, in defining a
2
-primal ring, one usually means a ring

such that the set of nilpotent elements

equals the prime radical (that is, the intersection of prime ideals). In this direction, the symbol

stands for the

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
R
be a
2
-primal
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean ring for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
such that
(m,n)\neq(0,0)
. Then,
{\rm M}_{k}(R)
is a
(p,q)
-regularly nil clean ring for every
p,q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
and

.

Proof.

We know that (see, for more details,
[

19
]

{\rm M}_{k}(R)/J({\rm M}_{k}(R))\cong{\rm M}_{k}(R/J(R))={\rm M}_{k}(R/\text{Nil}_{*}(R)).

Since the quotient
\overline{R}=R/\text{Nil}_{*}(R)
is simultaneously reduced and
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean, Lemma
2.5
ensures that
\overline{R}
is regular. Therefore,
[
18
]
assures that
{\rm M}_{k}(\overline{R})
is regular. Furthermore, combining
[

and Proposition

, one sees that
{\rm M}_{k}(\overline{R})
is a
(p,q)
-regularly nil clean ring.

On the other hand, since

is a nil-ideal and

2

J({\rm M}_{k}(R))={\rm M}_{k}(J(R))={\rm M}_{k}(\text{Nil}_{*}(R))=\text{Nil}_{*}({\rm M}_{k}(R))

is nil. Consequently,
[

insures that

is
(p,q)
-regularly nil clean, as claimed.

The next two technical claims are helpful.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
R
be an abelian
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean ring for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
such that
(m,n)\neq(0,0)
. Then,

is
(p,q)
-regularly nil clean for every

If, for a moment,

\displaystyle(a^{p+q}(1-e))^{k(n+m)}=((a(1-e))^{p+q})^{k(m+n)}=((a(1-e))^{k(m+n)})^{p+q}

\displaystyle=(a^{k(n+m)}(1-e))^{p+q}\in\text{Nil}(R).

Hence,
a^{p+q}(1-e)\in\text{Nil}(R)
, as needed.

If, however,

, then, for each

, there is
e\in a^{n+m}R\subseteq a^{p+q}R
such that
a^{n+m}(1-e)\in\text{Nil}(R)

\displaystyle(a^{p+q}(1-e))^{n+m}=((a(1-e))^{p+q})^{n+m}=((a(1-e))^{n+m})^{p+q}

\displaystyle=(a^{n+m}(1-e))^{p+q}\in\text{Nil}(R).

Hence,

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
R
be an NI ring which is
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
such that
(m,n)\neq(0,0)
. Then,

(p,q)
-regularly nil clean for every
p,q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}

Proof.

Firstly, if
m+n\leq p+q
, then there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
p+q\leq k(n+m)
. One inspects that, for each
a\in R
, there is

e=e^{2}\in(a^{k})^{n+m}R\subseteq a^{p+q}R,\text{ such that }(a^{k})^{n+m}(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Letting

be a remainder of

modulo

(

), there will exist
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

a^{k^{\prime}(p+q)}a^{x}(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Therefore,

a^{k^{\prime}(p+q)}(1-e)a^{x}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

But, as

is an NI ring, we find that

a^{k^{\prime}(p+q)}(1-e)a^{p+q}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

Furthermore, using the same property of

, namely that
{\rm Nil}(R)
is an ideal of

\displaystyle a^{k^{\prime}(p+q)}(1-e)a^{p+q}\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Rightarrow a^{k^{\prime}(p+q)}(1-e)a^{p+q}(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle a^{(k^{\prime}-1)(p+q)}a^{p+q}(1-e)a^{p+q}(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Rightarrow a^{(k^{\prime}-1)(p+q)}[a^{p+q}(1-e)]^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\cdots\Rightarrow[a^{p+q}(1-e)]^{k^{\prime}+1}\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Rightarrow a^{p+q}(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R).

In fact, a critical step is that

a^{(k^{\prime}-1)(p+q)}[a^{p+q}(1-e)]^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)=a^{p+q}a^{(k^{\prime}-2)(p+q)}[a^{p+q}(1-e)]^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)

and thus, as above illustrated, multiplying both sides from the right by

, we can get the final result, as pursued.

Hence, one concludes also that
(1-e)a^{p+q}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, as expected.

Reciprocally, if

, as asked for.

We now continue our work on another vein. In fact, it was asked in
[
5
]
whether Utumi’s rings possess the left-right symmetric property. It was shown in
[

, Theorem 2.13]
that the answer to this question is positive. Nevertheless, in the next technical claim, we shall show in a different and more transparent way that this is really true.

Lemma 2.13
.

For any ring R, the following are equivalent:

1.

R
is an Utumi ring.

2.

For every
x\in R
, there exists
y\in R
such that
x-x^{2}y\in{\rm Nil}(R)
.

3.

For every
x\in R
, there exists
y\in R
such that
x-xyx\in{\rm Nil}(R)
.

4.

For every
x\in R
, there exists
y\in R
such that
x-yx^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)
.

Proof.

(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)
is clear from the definition. It is now enough to show that the implications
(2)\Rightarrow(3)\Rightarrow(4)\Rightarrow(2)
are valid. To that goal, for any two elements
x,y
of a ring
R
, it follows that
xy\in{\rm Nil}(R)
if, only if,
yx\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, whence

\displaystyle x-x^{2}y\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow x(1-xy)\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Leftrightarrow(1-xy)x\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\,\,x-xyx\in{\rm Nil}(R)\,\,\Leftrightarrow x(1-yx)\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(1-yx)x\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Leftrightarrow\>\>x-yx^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R).

This automatically completes the proof.
∎

Besides, it was asked in
[
5
, Problem 3.3]
whether centrally Utumi’s rings are exchange. We now will concentrate on this and will prove even something more, namely that each centrally Utumi’s ring is strongly
\pi
-regular, and since it was demonstrated in
[
24
, Example 23]
that strongly
\pi
-regular rings are exchange, we will be done.

Theorem 2.14
.

Any centrally Utimi ring is a strongly
\pi
-regular ring.

Proof.

Let
R
be a centrally Utimi ring and let
x\in R
be an arbitrary element. Hence, there is
y\in R
such that
x-x^{2}y\in{\rm Nil}(R)\cap C(R)
. Therefore, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we derive:

\displaystyle(x-x^{2}y)^{n}

\displaystyle=(x-x^{2}y)^{n-1}(x-x^{2}y)=(x-x^{2}y)^{n-1}x(1-xy)

\displaystyle=x(x-x^{2}y)^{n-1}(1-xy)=x(x-x^{2}y)^{n-2}(x-x^{2}y)(1-xy)

\displaystyle=x(x-x^{2}y)^{n-2}x(1-xy)^{2}=x^{2}(x-x^{2}y)^{n-2}(1-xy)^{2}

\displaystyle=\cdots=x^{n}(1-xy)^{n}.

On the other hand, there is
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(x-x^{2}y)^{m}=0
. So,
x^{m}(1-xy)^{m}=0
and expanding this by the classical Newton’s binomial formula, we deduce that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=x^{m}(1-xy)^{m}=x^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{m}(-1)^{i}\binom{m}{i}(xy)^{i}

\displaystyle=x^{m}(1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{i}\binom{m}{i}x(yx)^{i-1}y)=x^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(-1)^{i}\binom{m}{i}x^{m+1}(yx)^{i-1}y,

which gives that

x^{m}=k_{1}x^{m+1}y+\cdots+k_{m}x^{m+1}(yx)^{m-1}y,

where we put
k_{i}=(-1)^{i+1}\binom{m}{i}\in\mathbb{N}
whenever
i=1,\ldots,m
. Finally, one detects that
x^{m}=x^{m+1}r
, where
r\in R
. This shows that the ring
R
is strongly
\pi
-regular, as promised.
∎

Meanwhile, in order to illustrate that the reverse implication is untrue, it is easy to see that the ring
\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}
consisting of all rational numbers with odd denominators is a commutative (local) exchange ring which is, manifestly, not centrally Utimi.

The next construction is worthy of documentation.

Example 2.15
.

For any ring
R
, both the polynomial ring
R[x]
and the power series ring
R[[x]]
are
not

(m,n)
-regularly nil clean for every
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

If we assume, by way of a contradiction, that the ring
R[[x]]
is
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean, then according to
[
19
, Exercise 5.6]
we know that

J(R[[x]])=\{a+xf(x)|a\in J(R)\>\textrm{and}\>f(x)\in R[[x]]\}.

So,
x\in J(R[[x]])
. Therefore,
J(R[[x]])
is not nil, but by
[
6
, Lemma 2.1]
,
J(R[[x]])
is known to be nil. This is, indeed, a contradiction, as required.

Now, assume, in a way of a contradiction, that
R[x]
is
(m,n)
-regularly nil clean. Then, there exists
e\in x^{m}R[x]x^{n}\cap Id(R[x])
with
x^{n+m}(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R[x])
. Consequently, there is
f\in R[x]
such that

. With a simple calculation at hand, we verify that

x^{n+m}(1-e)=x^{n+m}\notin{\rm Nil}(R[x]),

which is the desired contrary to our assumption.
∎

3.
The relationship between regularly nil clean and D-regularly nil clean rings

According to the corresponding definitions alluded to above, every D-regularly nil clean is a regularly nil clean ring. In this section, we examine under what additional conditions and circumstances the opposite is true, that is, D-regularly nil clean rings and regularly nil clean rings do coincide.

However, first and foremost, we provide some basic examples.

Example 3.1
.

1.

Each invertible element in a ring is D-regularly nil clean. In fact, if
a
is invertible in
R
, we can write
e=1=aa^{-2}a\in aRa
. In particular, every division ring is D-regularly nil clean.

2.

Each idempotent element in a ring is D-regularly nil clean. In fact, if
a
is an idempotent in
R
, we can write
e=a=aa\in aRa
. In particular, any Boolean ring is D-regularly nil clean.

3.

Each nilpotent element in a ring is D-regularly nil clean. In fact, if
a
is a nilpotent in
R
and
a^{n}=0
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can write
e=0=aa^{n-2}a\in aRa
.

4.

All D-regularly nil clean rings are closed under finite direct products.

With the first and third parts of the above example at hand, we can conclude that
\mathbb{Z}_{p^{n}}
is a D-regularly nil clean ring for every prime number
p
and positive integer
n
. Also, from the fourth part, we have that
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
is a D-regularly nil clean ring for every positive integer
m
.

Besides, it was asked in
[
7
, Problem 3.1]
whether an infinite direct product of (strongly)
\pi
-regular rings is a D-regularly nil clean ring. Owing to the next result, we answer this question in the negative.

Example 3.2
.

An infinite product of (strongly)
\pi
-regular rings is
not
D-regularly nil clean. Even more, we can construct an infinite direct product of D-regularly nil clean rings that is
not
D-regularly nil clean itself. Indeed, consider

R=\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{4}\times\mathbb{Z}_{8}\times\cdots.

We claim that the element
a=(0,2,2,2,\ldots)\in J(R)\subseteq R
is
not
D-regularly nil clean as for otherwise it must be that
e\in aRa\subseteq J(R)
. So, it immediately follows that
e=0
. But, this forces that
a(1-e)=a\notin{\rm Nil}(R)
, as asked for.

We now have all the ingredients necessary to prove the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
R
be an NI ring. Then,
R
is regularly nil clean if, and only if,
R
is D-regularly nil clean.

Proof.

One elementarily sees that, it is enough to show only that every regularly nil clean ring is
D
-regularly nil clean. Invoking Proposition
2.2
, every regularly nil clean ring is
(1,1)
-regularly nil clean. Letting
a\in R
be an arbitrary element, there exists
e=e^{2}\in aRa
such that
a(1-e)a\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Since
R
is an NI ring, we easily see that
a(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, as required.
∎

Having the needed machinery at hand, we are now in a position to prove the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
R
be an abelian ring. Then,
R
is regularly nil clean if, and only if,
R
is D-regularly nil clean.

Proof.

As above, it suffices to show only that every regularly nil clean ring is
D
-regularly nil clean. Exploiting Proposition
2.2
, each regularly nil clean ring is
(1,1)
-regularly nil clean. Therefore, for every
a\in R
, there exists
e=e^{2}\in aRa
such that
a(1-e)a\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Since
R
is an abelian ring, we have

\displaystyle a(1-e)a\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow a(1-e)^{2}a\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Rightarrow a(1-e)a(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow(a(1-e))^{2}\in{\rm Nil}(R)\Rightarrow a(1-e)\in{\rm Nil}(R),

as needed.
∎

We next proceed by showing the following.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
3
, Lemma 1.5]

Let
R
be a commutative domain and
A\in{\rm M}_{2}(R)
. Then,
A
is an idempotent if, and only if, either
A=0
, or
A=I
, or

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&1-a\end{pmatrix},

where
bc=a-a^{2}
in
R
.

Taking into account
[
7
, Theorem 2.7]
and
[
7
, Theorem 2.10]
, regular and
\pi
-regular rings are both D-regularly nil clean. However, it was asked in
[
7

if there is a (
\pi
-)regular element that is
not
D-regularly nil clean.

The following example manifestly illustrates that the answer to this question is absolutely positive (for results of a similar aspect, we refer to
[
14
]
and
[
17
]
, respectively).

Example 3.6
.

There is a (
\pi
-)regular element which is
not
D-regularly nil clean.

Proof.

Let
R={\rm M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, and take

A=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
3&-3\end{pmatrix}\in R

and
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\in R

It is not so hard to inspect that
A=ABA
and
A^{n}=(-2)^{n-1}A
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If, however, we assume the contrary that
A
is a D-regularly nil clean element, then there exists an element
E\in ARA\cap Id(R)
such that
A(I-E)\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Since
A
is not nilpotent, we have
E\neq 0
and write

E=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
3&-3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\
z&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
3&-3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}h&-h\\
3h&-3h\end{pmatrix},

where
h=(x-z)+3(y-d)\in\mathbb{Z}
. In accordance with Lemma
3.5
, we deduce that
E
is not idempotent, which is an obvious contradiction with our assumption, thus giving the wanted claim.
∎

not
D-regularly nil clean.

We are now ready to state and prove the following claim.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
R
be an NI ring and
a\in R
. If
a=b+q
, where
b
is regularly nil clean and
q\in{\rm Nil}(R)
, then
a
is regularly nil clean.

Proof.

Choose an idempotent
g\in bR
such that
(1-g)b\in{\rm Nil}(R)
. Write
g=br
and set
\bar{g}:=br+{\rm Nil}(R)
. Since
a\equiv b
modulo
{\rm Nil}(R)
, we obtain
\bar{g}=ar+{\rm Nil}(R)\in R/{\rm Nil}(R)
. Now, utilizing
[
18

, there will exist an idempotent
e\in arR
with
\bar{e}=\bar{g}
. But, one readily checks that
(1-e)a\equiv(1-g)b=0
modulo
{\rm Nil}(R)
, as expected.
∎
