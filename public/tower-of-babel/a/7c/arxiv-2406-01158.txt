Profile Reconstruction from Private Sketches
Hao WU, Rasmus Pagh

Abstract
Given a multiset of n n items from 𝒟 \mathcal{D} , the profile reconstruction problem is to estimate, for t = 0 , 1 , … , n t=0,1,\dots,n , the fraction f → ​ [ t ] \vec{f}[t] of items in 𝒟 \mathcal{D} that appear exactly t t times. We consider differentially private profile estimation in a distributed, space-constrained setting where we wish to maintain an updatable, private sketch of the multiset that allows us to compute an approximation of f → = ( f → ​ [ 0 ] , … , f → ​ [ n ] ) \vec{f}=(\vec{f}[0],\dots,\vec{f}[n]) . Using a histogram privatized using discrete Laplace noise, we show how to “reverse” the noise, using an approach of Dwork et al. (ITCS ’10). We show how to speed up their LP-based technique from polynomial time to O ⁡ ( d + n ​ log ⁡ n ) O(d+n\log n) , where d = | 𝒟 | d=|\mathcal{D}| , and analyze the achievable error in the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} , ℓ 2 \ell_{2} and ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} norms. In all cases the dependency of the error on d d is O ⁡ ( 1 / d ) O(1/\sqrt{d}) — we give an information-theoretic lower bound showing that this dependence on d d is asymptotically optimal among all private, updatable sketches for the profile reconstruction problem with a high-probability error guarantee.



1
Introduction

The
profile
is a fundamental statistic of a data set.
Given a multiset of
n
items from a finite domain
\mathcal{D}
, the profile is a vector
\vec{f}
whose
t^{(th)}
entry equals the fraction
\vec{f}[t]
of items in
\mathcal{D}
that appear exactly
t
times.
The concept has arisen in many contexts under various names, including
rarity
,
incidence
,
fingerprint
,
prevalence
,
collision statistics
,
anonymous histogram
,
unattributed histogram
, and
histogram of histograms
.
Besides succinctly summarizing frequency information in a dataset, for example the degree distribution in a graph, a profile is sufficient to compute any
symmetric
function of the dataset histogram
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
.

In settings where space, communication or privacy constraints prevents us from computing the exact profile it is of interest to
approximate
the profile
\vec{f}
by a vector
\vec{r}
such that
\vec{r}-\vec{f}
has small
\ell_{p}
norm for some parameter
p
.

Streaming algorithms.

Datar & Muthukrishnan (2002)
considered estimating the profile in a streaming setting, showing that a small sample of the domain
\mathcal{D}
suffices to ensure small error (they focus on
\ell_{1}
error, but the technique applies to general
\ell_{p}
norms).
The sample can be described by a compact hash function, making it suitable for use in distributed settings.
More recently,
Chen, Indyk, and Woodruff (2024)
improved the error bound, and showed matching space lower bounds for streaming algorithms.
General estimators for symmetric functions based on samples were presented and analyzed by
Acharya, Das, Orlitsky, and Suresh (2017)
.

Differentially private algorithms.

Differential privacy
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
is a leading approach to ensure that the output of an algorithm, from simple statistics to machine learning models, does not reveal too much about the inputs.
It is one of the central techniques for enabling collection of statistics, training of machine learning models, and other computations on data that may hold sensitive information.
We will focus on the privacy of individual elements in the multiset, that is, our differential privacy guarantee is defined in terms of pairs of neighboring datasets where the number of occurrences of a particular element differs by 1, and that are otherwise identical.

In the
central model
of differential privacy (where a trusted curator holds the input data), privately releasing a
profile
with small
\ell_{2}
error was considered by
Hay, Li, Miklau, and Jensen (2009)
;
Hay, Rastogi, Miklau, and Suciu (2010)
, the latter focusing on the application of graph degree distributions. Private synthetic graphs with specific degree distribution were also studied by
Karwa and Slavkovic (2012)
.

Motivated by estimating repetitions in password datasets,
Blocki, Datta, and Bonneau (2016)
studied differentially private protocols for profile estimation, focusing on
\ell_{2}
error.

Suresh (2019)
considered an alternative (incomparable) error metric, where the profile is represented in the “verbatim” form of a vector encoding all frequencies in sorted order. They presented an algorithm to release verbatim profiles with a small
\ell_{1}
error. Recently
Manurangsi (2022)
showed improved tight upper and lower bounds on the privacy/
\ell_{1}
error trade-off for verbatim profiles.

Differentially private distributed/streaming algorithms

Dwork, Naor, Pitassi, Rothblum, and Yekhanin (2010)
studied profile estimation (referred to as
t
-incidence
) in the pan-private streaming setting, where the internal state of the streaming algorithm must satisfy a differential privacy guarantee.
Pan-private algorithms protect against
intrusions
, i.e., unintended release of the internal state of the algorithm.
Unlike the other works on private profile estimation mentioned above, the neighboring relation considered by
Dwork et al. (2010)
is defined on the profile itself, i.e., datasets are neighboring if they differ
by any amount
on the count of one item (so the profiles differ in two entries).
In a nutshell

Since there is no good bound on the difference between histograms with their notion of neighboring datasets,
Dwork et al. (2010)
actually work with histograms of counts modulo several small random primes

, and use these to estimate profiles on the counts modulo

Since the noisy histogram is a linear function of the histogram it is

Dwork et al. (2010)
finally show how to derive an estimate of the profile from the noisy histogram by solving a linear programming problem.
The size of the sketch required for achieving
\ell_{\infty}

\text{poly}(\log n,1/\varepsilon,1/\alpha)
with unspecified exponents.
Unfortunately, the paper does not analyze for what values of

1.1
Our contributions

In this paper we study how to approximate the profile from a noisy histogram of the data.
Specifically, we consider the setting where
discrete Laplace
noise has been added to the counts in the histogram.
(This is different from the noise distribution used by
Dwork et al. (2010)
, which is nearly uniform modulo a small prime
p_{i}
.)
Computing the empirical profile directly from the noisy histogram can lead to large errors.
For example, consider the situation where
\mathcal{D}
has
n
items that each appear once in the dataset and let
\varepsilon>0
be a small constant.
After adding noise to the histogram to make it
\varepsilon
-differentially private (and possibly rounding up negative values to 0), the fraction of items with a count of 1 will be
\approx\varepsilon
, far from the true fraction of
1
.

Instead we use an approach similar to that of
Dwork et al. (2010)
to approximately invert the effect of the noise.
Since we use a known noise distribution, the
expected
value of the profile computed from the noisy histogram is a linear function of the true profile.
Due to concentration we can argue that the observed values in the profile based on the noisy histogram are in fact close to the expected ones, and thus it makes sense to find a histogram whose expected profile after noise would be close to the observed noisy profile.
Unlike
Dwork et al. (2010)
who formulate this as a feasibility question of a linear program, we show that it can be reduced to solving a linear
\ell_{p}
-regression problem with a circulant matrix, paving the way for a near-linear time algorithm.
We also give a careful analysis of the error
\mathcal{E}\textit{rr}_{p}
in
\ell_{p}
norm, for
p\in\{1,2,\infty\}
, showing the following upper bound:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

,
\varepsilon>0
.
Denote

, and assume that

.
There is an algorithm that, given a private version

\varepsilon
-DP discrete Laplace mechanism and a parameter

or

for the profile

of

Moreover, with probability at least

satisfies

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\textit{rr}_{1}

\displaystyle\in O{\left({\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}+1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\right)}^{2}{\left(\frac{\sum_{t=-B}^{n+B}\sqrt{\Expectation\left[\tilde{f}[t]\right]}}{\sqrt{d}}+\sqrt{\frac{2\ln\frac{1}{\eta}}{d}}\right)}\right)}

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\textit{rr}_{2}

\displaystyle\in O{\left({\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}+1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\right)}^{2}\sqrt{\frac{\ln\frac{1}{\eta}}{d}}\right)}

(2)

\displaystyle\in{\left({\left(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}+1}{e^{\varepsilon}-1}\right)}^{2}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{2\,{\left(e^{\varepsilon}-1\right)}}{d\,{\left(e^{\varepsilon}+1\right)}}\ln\frac{n}{\eta}}+\frac{\ln\frac{n}{\eta}}{3\,d}\right)}\right)}

where

Notice that the error bounds in all three norms are proportional to

As we will discuss below, this dependence is optimal under

On the other hand,

We note that the bound on

For every

(4)

Instead, it applies to a broader class of algorithms that attempt to reconstruct the profile from outputs of

\varepsilon

Informally, the former denotes the property that when the input histogram

1.2
Technical overview

As noted by
Datar & Muthukrishnan (2002)
and
Dwork et al. (2010)
it suffices to estimate the profile of a sample of elements from the domain, since the profile of the sample will closely resemble the true profile.
Thus we will assume that this sampling step has already been done, and consider the full histogram of (sampled) items.
The final error bounds add the sampling error and the error from privacy using the triangle inequality, but here we focus on the latter.

Given a noisy histogram
\tilde{h}
, we can compute an empirical profile
\tilde{f}
.
As the noisy histogram is obtained by adding independent noise to each coordinate of the full histogram
\vec{h}
, the empirical profile can be viewed as a function of a set of independent random variables and should concentrate around its expectation
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}]
.
Hence, we regard the empirical profile as a reliable representative of its expectation.

Although the expectation
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}]
itself may not be a good approximation of the original profile
\vec{f}
of
\vec{h}
, there is a subtle connection between them:
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}]
can be computed via a linear transform of the original profile
\vec{f}
.
Due the symmetry of discrete Laplace noise, we can write the linear transform as
\boldsymbol{A}\vec{f}
for a circulant matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
with non-zero eigenvalues.

Since the inversion for such linear transforms can be computed in
O(n\log n)
time via Fast Fourier transform (FFT), it motivates us to compute
\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\tilde{f}
as an approximation of the true profile.
However, such an approximation may not be a valid profile: it may contain coordinates outside
[0,1]
, and its coordinates do not sum up to
1
.
We will design a systematic procedure for fixing these violations, which also relies on FFT and runs in
O(n\log n)
time.

The approximation error comes from three sources: the deviation of
\tilde{f}
from its expectation, the inverse linear transform, and the violation fixing procedure.
Each part will be analyzed separately.
The second part, in particular, might be of independent interest as it involves examining various matrix norms of
\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}
, which naturally arises from the discrete Laplace mechanism.

To establish a lower bound, we employ a reduction from the
d
-dimensional inner product estimation problem in the framework of two-party differential privacy where an error of
\Omega(\sqrt{d})
is inevitable
(
McGregor et al., 2010
;
Haitner et al., 2022
)
.

Problem Description

Let
\mathcal{D}\doteq\left\{{1,\ldots,d}\right\}
be a set of
d
items.
Given a multiset of
n
items from
\mathcal{D}
,
for each item
\ell\in\mathcal{D}
, let
\vec{h}[\ell]
be the number of times it appears in the multiset.
The
histogram
is a vector
\vec{h}\doteq{({\vec{h}[1],\ldots,\vec{h}[d]})}\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}
.
The
profile
of the histogram
\vec{h}
is a frequency vector
\vec{f}\doteq(\vec{f}[0],\ldots,\vec{f}[n])\in[0,1]^{n+1},

where

\vec{f}[t]={\left(1/d\right)}\cdot|\{\ell\in\mathcal{D}:\vec{h}[\ell]=t\}|

is the fraction of elements in
\mathcal{D}
which appears
t
times in the multiset, for each
t\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]
.

Our goal is to design an efficient and accurate algorithm for estimating the profile
\vec{f}
from a private histogram
\tilde{h}
output by the
discrete Laplace mechanism
(or geometric mechanism) due to
Ghosh, Roughgarden, and Sundararajan (2009)
.

Discrete Laplace Mechanism.

Given an input histogram
\vec{h}
, the mechanism aims at outputting a perturbed version
\tilde{h}
of
\vec{h}
that is insensitive to
the addition/removal of an arbitrary item in the multiset.
Specifically, the mechanism creates
\tilde{h}
by adding independent noises to each coordinate of
\vec{h}
, such that

\tilde{h}[\ell]=\vec{h}[\ell]+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right),\,\forall\ell\in\mathcal{D},

where
\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)
denotes a random variable following discrete Laplace distribution:

\Pr\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)=t\right]=\frac{1-e^{-\varepsilon}}{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}\cdot e^{-\varepsilon\cdot\left|{t}\right|},\,\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}.

In practice, a clipping step can be applied after coordinate perturbation:

\tilde{h}[\ell]=\text{clip}{\left[{{\vec{h}[\ell]+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)},{0},{n}}\right]},\,\forall\ell\in\mathcal{D}.

where
\text{clip}[\vec{h}[\ell]+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right),0,n]\doteq\max\{0,\min\{\vec{h}[\ell]

+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right),n\}\}
ensures the returned value is in
[0\,.\,.\,n]
.

Privacy Guarantee.

The privacy guarantee provided by the mechanism is measured by the framework of
differential privacy
.
Formally, we call two histograms
\vec{h}
and
\vec{h}^{\prime}

neighboring
, written as
\vec{h}\sim\vec{h}^{\prime}
,
if

\exists\ell\in\mathcal{D}
, s.t.,
|\vec{h}[\ell]-\vec{h}^{\prime}[\ell]|\leq 1
, and
\forall\ell^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}\setminus\left\{{\ell}\right\}
, it holds that
\vec{h}[\ell^{\prime}]=\vec{h}^{\prime}[\ell^{\prime}]
.
The discrete Laplace mechanism ensures that the output histograms have similar distributions for neighboring inputs.

Definition 2.1

(
{\varepsilon}
-Differentially Privacy
(
Dwork & Roth, 2014
)
)
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, a randomized algorithm

\mathcal{A}:\mathbb{N}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}

is called
{\varepsilon}
-differentially private (DP),
if for every
\vec{h},\vec{h}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
\vec{h}\sim\vec{h}^{\prime}
,
and
all (measurable)
Z\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Pr\left[\mathcal{A}(\vec{h})\in Z\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr[\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}^{\prime})\in Z]\,.\end{array}

(5)

Ghosh et al. (2009)
showed that the discrete Laplace mechanism is
\varepsilon
-DP (with or without the clipping step).

4
Near-linear Time Algorithm

In this section, we introduce an algorithm that achieves the properties stated in Theorem
1.1
.
Our presentation is structured as follows.
In Section
4.1
, we demonstrate that we can transform the histograms published by the discrete Laplace mechanisms with the clipping step into ones without clipping steps, ensuring they follow the same distribution.
This allows us to address perturbed histograms with and without clipping in a unified approach.
Subsequently, in Section
4.2
, we formulate the task of searching for a good estimate of the profile as an optimization problem.
Section
4.3
presents a near-linear algorithm for systematically finding an approximate solution to the optimization problem.
Sections
4.4
and
4.5
analyze the time complexity and the approximation error of the proposed algorithms, respectively.
Finally, Section
4.6
provides a more detailed comparison of our algorithm with the previous approach.

4.1
Unfolding Clipped Histograms

Assume that
\tilde{h}
has been clipped, so that each entry has value between
0
and
n
.
Based on the clipped histogram, we can construct a new histogram that has the same distribution as the an unclipped one.
Consider an entry
\tilde{h}[\ell]=\text{clip}{\left[{{\vec{h}[\ell]+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)},{0},{n}}\right]}
for some
\ell\in\mathcal{D}
, and the recovery procedure:

\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{h}^{\prime}[\ell]\doteq\begin{cases}\tilde{h}[\ell],&\text{ if }0<\tilde{h}[\ell]<n,\\
\tilde{h}[\ell]-\operatorname{\mathbb{DE}xp}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right),&\text{ if }\tilde{h}[\ell]=0,\\
\tilde{h}[\ell]+\operatorname{\mathbb{DE}xp}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right),&\text{ if }n=\tilde{h}[\ell],\end{cases}\end{array}

(8)

where
\operatorname{\mathbb{DE}xp}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)
denotes a random variable following the geometric distribution:

\Pr\left[\operatorname{\mathbb{DE}xp}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)=t\right]={\left(1-e^{-\varepsilon}\right)}\cdot e^{-\varepsilon\cdot{t}},\,\forall t\in\mathbb{N}.

Lemma 4.1
.

\tilde{h}^{\prime}[\ell]
has the same distribution as
\vec{h}[\ell]+Z
, where
Z
is an independent copy of
\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)
.

Appendix
B
contains the detailed proof of the theorem.
Here, we present a high-level overview of the proof’s intuition: when restricting the two-directional discrete Laplace random variable
\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)
to a single direction, it reduces to a random variable that follows the geometric distribution.
It is a well-known fact that for a geometric random variable, truncating it at a certain value and adding an independent copy of the random variable at the truncated value preserves its geometric distribution.
This property is commonly referred to as the ”memorylessness” property of the geometric distribution.

4.2
Searching for Profiles

A natural attempt would be estimating the profile
\vec{f}
with
\tilde{f}[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]\doteq{({\tilde{f}[0],\ldots,\tilde{f}[n]})}
, where
\tilde{f}[t]={\left(1/d\right)}\cdot|\{\ell\in\mathcal{D}:\tilde{h}[\ell]=t\}|,\forall t\in\mathbb{Z}.

There are several issues with such an estimate. First,
\tilde{f}[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]
is not an empirical profile, as its entries do not sum up to
1
.
An even more significant concern is that such an estimate can incur an error of
\Omega(1)
.

Example.

Consider the example where
\vec{h}[\ell]=1,\forall\ell\in\mathcal{D}.

Hence,
\vec{f}[1]=1
and
\vec{f}[t]=0
for all
t\neq 1
.
But, it can be shown that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}[1]]=\frac{1-e^{-\varepsilon}}{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}
and
\mathbb{V}ar[\tilde{f}[1]]\leq\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-\varepsilon}}{1+e^{-\varepsilon}}.

By Chebyshev’s inequality,
when
\varepsilon\in\Theta(1)
, with constant probability,
\|\tilde{f}[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]-\vec{f}\|_{p}\geq|\tilde{f}[1]-\vec{f}[1]|\in\Omega(1),
for
p=1,2,\infty.

To deepen our understanding of the relationship between
\tilde{f}
and
\vec{f}
, let us consider a fixed

.
There are

\mathcal{D}
such that
\vec{h}[\ell]=t
. For each of these items, the random variable
\tilde{h}[\ell]=\vec{h}[\ell]+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)
is independent and identically distributed.
Our
analysis
considers an algorithm, outlined in Algorithm

, for generating
\tilde{f}
directly from
\vec{f}
.
We emphasize that our final algorithm is the combination of Algorithms

Conditioning on

Taking the Expectation.

\hskip-2.84526pt\begin{array}[]{c}\left\|{\Expectation_{\mathcal{A}}\left[\mathcal{A}{\left(\vec{r}\right)}\mid\mathcal{E}_{B}\right]-\tilde{f}}\right\|_{p}\leq\Expectation_{\mathcal{A}}\left[\left\|{{\mathcal{A}{\left(\vec{r}\right)}}-\tilde{f}}\right\|_{p}\mid\mathcal{E}_{B}\right].\end{array}

\ell\in[d]
, their requirement was that

\big(\max_{t\in[0..n]}\Pr[\tilde{h}[\ell]=t]\big)\big/\big(\min_{t\in[0..n]}\Pr[\tilde{h}[\ell]=t]\big)\leq e^{\varepsilon}.

Specifically, they used noise scaling with
n
:
\tilde{h}[\ell]\leftarrow\vec{h}[\ell]+Z\pmod{n+1}
, where
Z
is a noise variable such that
\Pr[Z=t]\propto e^{-\varepsilon\cdot t/(n+1)}
.

In contrast, our paper considers adding symmetric discrete Laplace noise
\mathbb{DL}ap(e^{-\varepsilon})
to each entry, which is fundamental and widely used for publishing privatized histograms. Our technique is applicable even in the pan-private setting, with appropriately scaled noise.

Another minor difference lies in the optimization problem: their linear program (LP) aims to achieve solutions with
\ell_{\infty}
error, whereas our algorithm supports
\ell_{1}/\ell_{2}/\ell_{\infty}
errors. Moreover, they did not provide an almost linear-time algorithm for solving their LP. However, the almost linear-time algorithm developed in our paper can solve their LP as well, as it also involves a circulant matrix.

5
Lower Bound

In this section, we discuss the lower bound in Theorem
1.2
.
We will begin by providing the formal definition of
updatable
histogram publishing algorithms and the
sensitivity
of the profile reconstruction algorithms when composed with the private histogram publishing algorithm.
To establish the lower bound, we will then present a reduction from the inner product estimation problem in the two-party setting.

Our lower bound applies to an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that is updatable, meaning that there exists an update algorithm
\mathcal{U}
, s.t., given the output
\mathcal{A}(\vec{h})
and some other histogram
\vec{h}^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{U}
can adjust the output directly (by computing
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}),\tilde{h})
), as if the input to
\mathcal{A}
is
\vec{h}+\vec{h}^{\prime}.

Formally,

Definition 5.1

(Updatable Algorithm)
.

A (randomized) algorithm
\mathcal{A}:[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is called updatable, if there exists another (randomized) algorithm
\mathcal{U}:\mathcal{Y}\times[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, the
update algorithm
, such that for every
\vec{h},\vec{h}^{\prime}\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}
,
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}),\vec{h}^{\prime})
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}+\vec{h}^{\prime})
.

Example 5.2
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is the discrete Laplace mechanism, then it is updatable.
Let
\mathcal{U}
be the vector addition operation:

\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}),\vec{h}^{\prime})=\vec{h}+\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)^{d}+\vec{h}^{\prime}=\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}+\vec{h}^{\prime}),

where
{\operatorname{\mathbb{DL}ap}\left({e^{-\varepsilon}}\right)}^{d}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is a vector composed of independent discrete Laplace random variables.

When
\mathcal{A}
represents a randomized algorithm, it can be conceptualized as a function, denoted as
F_{A}
, acting on both the input of
\mathcal{A}
and the randomness inherent in its execution (commonly referred to as ”coin tosses” in the literature).
This randomness is formally represented by the random variable
C_{A}
.
In what follows, denote
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,1]^{n+1}
as an algorithm trying to reconstruct the profile of
\vec{h}
when given
\mathcal{A}(\vec{h}).

Definition 5.3
.

The sensitivity of
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}
is defined as the maximum deviation of
\mathcal{R}\circ F_{A}
, over all possible realization
c_{A}
of
C_{A}
and all possible pair of neighboring inputs of
\mathcal{A}:

\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\doteq\max_{c_{A}}\max_{\vec{h}\sim\vec{h}^{\prime}}\left\|{\mathcal{R}{\left(F_{A}{\left(\vec{h},c_{A}\right)}\right)}-\mathcal{R}{\left(F_{A}{\left(\vec{h}^{\prime},c_{A}\right)}\right)}}\right\|_{\infty},

where the second maximum is over all
\vec{h},\vec{h}^{\prime}\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}
such that
\|\vec{h}-\vec{h}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 1

In Appendix
G
, we show that when
\mathcal{A}
is the discrete Laplace mechanism,
\mathcal{R}
is the profile reconstruction algorithm stated in Theorem
1.1
, and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\in o{\big({\big(\frac{e^{\varepsilon}-1}{e^{\varepsilon}+1}\big)^{2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}}\big)}
,
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}
satisfies
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\in o\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\big)
. Hence, the lower bound in Theorem
1.2
applies to our algorithms.

Two Party Differential Privacy.

We will prove the lower bound by establishing a connection between the profile estimation problem and the inner product estimation problem in the two party differential privacy setting
(
McGregor et al., 2010
)
.

Two players, Alice and Bob, equipped with unrestricted computational capabilities, collaborate to compute the inner product
\left\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\right\rangle
,
where
\vec{x}\in\left\{{-1,1}\right\}^{d}
is the binary string held by Alice, and
\vec{y}\in\left\{{-1,1}\right\}^{d}
is one held by Bob.
The collaboration follows a pre-agreed protocol, denoted as
\Pi
.
In this protocol, they alternate turns to communicate with each other.
During each player’s turn, the communicated information is determined by their own vector, the cumulative communication up to that point, and possibly their randomness.
The sequences of messages, which collectively form the protocol’s transcript, are denoted as
\Pi
.
To protect the data of both parties, it is required that each party’s view of
\Pi
, as a function of the other party’s data, should be differentially private.

Formally, the view of Alice,
denoted as
\Pi_{\vec{x}}:\left\{{-1,1}\right\}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}

(where
\mathcal{Z}
is the set of all possible sequences of messages), is regarded as a (randomized) algorithm with input string
\vec{y}
when
\vec{x}
is fixed.
The view
\Pi_{\vec{x}}
is
\varepsilon
-DP, if for each measurable
Z\subseteq\mathcal{Z}
and every pair of neighboring vector
\vec{y}
and
\vec{y}^{\prime}
s.t.
\left\|{\vec{y}-\vec{y}^{\prime}}\right\|=1
, it holds that

\begin{array}[]{c}\Pr\left[\Pi_{\vec{x}}(\vec{y})\in Z\right]\leq e^{\varepsilon}\cdot\Pr\left[\Pi_{\vec{x}}(\vec{y}^{\prime})\in Z\right].\end{array}

We can define the view of Bob

\Pi_{\vec{y}}:\left\{{-1,1}\right\}^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}

and the
\varepsilon
-DP property of
\Pi_{\vec{y}}
in a similar manner.
The protocol
\Pi
is called
\varepsilon
-DP, if
\Pi_{\vec{x}}
is
\varepsilon
-DP for all possible
\vec{x}
, and
\Pi_{\vec{y}}
is
\varepsilon
-DP for all possible
\vec{y}
.

McGregor et al. (2010)
established a lower bound on inner product estimation, which was further refined by
Haitner et al. (2022)
.

Fact 5.4

(The Two Party Lower Bound
(
Haitner et al., 2022
)
)
.

An
\varepsilon
-DP two-party protocol that, for some
\ell\geq\log d
, estimate the inner product over
\left\{{\text{-1},1}\right\}^{d}\times\left\{{\text{-1},1}\right\}^{d}
up to an additive error
\ell
with probability
c\cdot e^{c\cdot\epsilon}\cdot\ell/\sqrt{d}
(for some universal constant
c>0
), can be used to construct a key-agreement protocol.

Since an information theoretically secure key agreement does not exist
(
Haitner et al., 2022
)
, Fact
5.4
implies that such protocols do not exist either.

1.2
.

The complete proof is included in the Appendix

We provide some further comments on the lower bound.
First, it is also possible to prove a lower bound of

\begin{array}[]{c}\Expectation\left[\left\|{\vec{f}-\mathcal{R}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{h})})}}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\in o{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}\cdot\log d}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{\varepsilon}}\right)},\end{array}

(11)

for Theorem
1.2
, via a similar reduction to the two-party inner product lower bound accuracy lower bound by
McGregor et al. (2010)
.
In particular, they showed that with probability
1-\delta
, inner product estimation in a two-party setting incurs an additive error of

\Omega{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\log d}\cdot\frac{\delta}{e^{\varepsilon}}\right)}
.
Compared to Equation (
4
), Equation (
11
) avoids a constant
c
blow up in the exponent, at the cost of an addition factor of
\log d
in the denominator.

Second, Theorem
1.2
assumes
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\in o\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\big)
. We considered removing this assumption because it appears that if
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\in\Omega\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\big)
, it would imply that

\mathbb{E}[\|\vec{f}-\mathcal{R}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{h})})}\|_{\infty}]\in\Omega\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}\cdot e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\big)

for some
\vec{h}
. However, this is not the case. Since
\mathcal{A}
is a randomized algorithm,
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}
is defined over all randomness
c_{A}
of
\mathcal{A}
as per Definition
5.3
. It is possible that
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\in\Omega\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\big)
for the
c_{A}
’s with very small probabilities. Therefore, we cannot derive a lower bound for

\mathbb{E}[\|\vec{f}-\mathcal{R}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{h})})}\|_{\infty}].

Finally, our current lower bound applies to an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that protects the number of occurrences of a single element by 1.
It extends naturally to an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that protects the number of occurrences of a single element by an arbitrary amount, as long as it satisfies the other assumptions in Theorem
1.2
.

Definition A.1

(Lipschitz Condition)
.

Consider an arbitrary set
\mathcal{Z}
.
A function
\Delta:\mathcal{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the Lipschitz condition with bound
c\in\mathbb{R}
if, for every
i\in[d]
and for every sequence of values
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\in\mathcal{Z}
and

|\Delta(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{d})-\Delta(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{i-1},y_{i},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{d})|\leq c.

Fact A.2

(McDiarmid’s Inequality
(
Mitzenmacher & Upfal, 2017
;
Tolstikhin, 2017
)
)
.

Let
\Delta:\mathcal{Z}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function that satisfies the Lipschitz condition with bound

.
Let

be independent random variables from the set
\mathcal{Z}

Then for all

\Pr\left[\Delta(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})-\Expectation\left[\Delta(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})\right]\geq\lambda\right]\leq\exp{\left(-\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{dc^{2}}\right)}\,.

Fact A.3

(Bernstein’s Inequality
(
Chung & Lu, 2006
;
Audibert et al., 2009
)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{d}
be independent real-valued random variables such that
|X_{i}|\leq c
with probability one.
Let

and
\Variance\left[S_{d}\right]=\sum_{i\in[d]}\Variance\left[X_{i}\right]
. Then for all
\eta\in(0,1)

\left|S_{d}-\Expectation\left[S_{d}\right]\right|\leq\sqrt{2\Variance\left[S_{d}\right]\ln\frac{2}{\eta}}+\frac{2c\ln\frac{2}{\eta}}{3}\,,

with probability at least

.

,

\displaystyle\left|{\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\times\vec{a}\right\rangle}\right|

\displaystyle\geq\max_{t}\left|{\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\times\vec{e}_{t}\right\rangle}\right|=\max_{t}\left|{\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}[\cdot,t]\right\rangle}\right|.

It remains to verify that
\max_{t}\left|{\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}[\cdot,t]\right\rangle}\right|\neq 0
.
This is true, as

\displaystyle\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\sum_{t}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}[\cdot,t]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\times\vec{\mathbbm{1}}\right\rangle=\left\langle\vec{\mathbbm{1}}_{{0:n}},\vec{\mathbbm{1}}\right\rangle=n+1,

where the equation
\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\times\vec{\mathbbm{1}}=\vec{\mathbbm{1}}
holds since
\boldsymbol{A}\times\vec{\mathbbm{1}}=\vec{\mathbbm{1}}
.

Combined, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{A}_{\textit{fst-inv}}(\tilde{f})-\mathcal{A}_{\textit{fst-inv}}(\tilde{f}^{\prime})}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\in O{\left(\frac{1}{d}\cdot\left\|{\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}}\right\|_{\infty}\right)}.

To ease the notation, from now on, denote
\vec{r}=\mathcal{A}_{\textit{fst-inv}}(\tilde{f})
and
\vec{r}\,^{\prime}\doteq\mathcal{A}_{\textit{fst-inv}}(\tilde{f}\,^{\prime})
.
We will finally show that, Applying
\mathcal{A}_{\textit{rnd}}
(Algorithm
3
) to
\vec{r}
and
\vec{r}\,^{\prime}
the amplify their
\ell_{\infty}
distance only by some constant:

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathcal{A}_{\textit{rnd}}{\left(\vec{r}\right)}-\mathcal{A}_{\textit{rnd}}{\left(\vec{r}\,^{\prime}\right)}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\in O{\left(\left\|{\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\right)}.

We investigate the steps of Algorithm
3
.
First, rounding the coordinates of
\vec{r}
and
\vec{r}\,^{\prime}
to the interval
[0,1]
only decreases their
\ell_{\infty}
distance.

Next, the algorithm searches for some thresholds
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]}{\left(\vec{r}[t]-\min\left\{{\tau,\vec{r}[t]}\right\}\right)}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]}{\left(\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]-\min\left\{{\tau^{\prime},\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]}\right\}\right)}

\displaystyle=1.

We claim that
\tau\,^{\prime}\leq\tau+\left\|{\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}
,
as

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]}{\left(\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]-\min\left\{{\tau+\left\|{\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty},\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]}\right\}\right)}\leq\sum_{t\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]}{\left(\vec{r}[t]-\min\left\{{\tau,\vec{r}[t]}\right\}\right)}=1.

Via symmetry, we can prove that
\tau\leq\tau^{\prime}+\left\|{\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}
.
As Algorithm
3
eventually update each
\vec{r}[t]
and
\vec{r}^{\prime}[t]
by

\displaystyle\vec{r}[t]\leftarrow\vec{r}[t]-\min\left\{{\tau,\vec{r}[t]}\right\},\quad\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]\leftarrow\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]-\min\left\{{\tau^{\prime},\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]}\right\},

after the update, it still holds that
\left|{\vec{r}[t]-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}[t]}\right|\in O(\left\|{\vec{r}-\vec{r}\,^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty})
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

We will prove the theorem by contraction.
Assume there exists
\varepsilon
-DP and updatable algorithm
\mathcal{A}:[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, profile estimation algorithm
\mathcal{R}:\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,1]^{n+1}
,
such that, for every input
\vec{h}\in[{0}\,.\,.\,{n}]^{d}
, s.t.

\mathbb{E}[\|\vec{f}-\mathcal{R}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{h})})}\|_{\infty}]\in o{\big({{1}/{({\sqrt{d}\cdot e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}})}}\big)}.

We will construct an
\varepsilon
-DP protocol
P
such that such that for every
\vec{x}
and
\vec{y}
,
Bob’s output (denoted by
m_{B}
) satisfies

\Pr\left[\left|{m_{B}-\left\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\right\rangle}\right|\geq\mathcal{E}\textit{rr}\right]\in o(1),

(17)

where
\mathcal{E}\textit{rr}=\sqrt{d}/{({2\cdot c\cdot e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}})}
,
contradicting Fact
5.4
.

The protocol is given by Algorithm
4
.
Alice initializes the computation by sending
\mathcal{A}{({\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}})}
to Bob.
On receiving
\mathcal{A}{({\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}})}
, Bob updates it with their own data
\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}
by running algorithm
\mathcal{U}
, and the result is then passed to the profile estimation algorithm
\mathcal{R}
.
Observe that by the property of updatable algorithm,
\mathcal{U}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}),\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})})}=\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})
.
Hence,
\mathcal{R}{\left(\mathcal{U}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}),\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})})}\right)}
is essentially an estimate of the profile
\vec{f}
of
\vec{h}\doteq\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}
.
Finally, Bob publishes
d\cdot{\left(\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\right)}
.
where
\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\in\mathbb{R}
is a random variable following the Laplace distribution whose probability density function is given by
p(z)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\cdot\exp{\left(-\varepsilon\cdot\left|{z}\right|\right)},

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}.

It remains to analyze the privacy and utility guarantee of
P
respectively.

Privacy Guarantee.

The view of Bob is
\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})
, which is
\varepsilon
-DP by the assumptions of
\mathcal{A}
.
The view of Alice is
d\cdot{\left(\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\right)}
.
To prove this is
\varepsilon
-DP, it suffices to show that
\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]
has sensitivity
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}
.

Assume the randomness
C_{A}
of
\mathcal{A}
has realization
c_{A}
.
The message
\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})
received by Bob therefore can be written as
F_{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}},c_{A}).

After performing the update
\mathcal{U}{({\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}),\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})})}=\mathcal{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}})
, the message becomes
F_{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}},c_{A})
.
Suppose that
\vec{y}
is replaced by a neighbor binary vector
\vec{y}\,^{\prime}
such that
\left\|{\vec{y}-\vec{y}\,^{\prime}}\right\|_{1}\leq 1
.
Then
\left\|{\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}-(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}\,^{\prime})}\right\|_{1}\leq 1
,
and via the definition of
\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}
, we have
\left\|{\mathcal{R}{\left(F_{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}},c_{A})\right)}-\mathcal{R}{\left(F_{A}(\vec{x}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}}+\vec{y}^{\prime}+\vec{\mathbbm{1}},c_{A})\right)}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}
.

Accuracy Guarantee.

Since
\vec{x}
and
\vec{y}
are binary vectors,
\left\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\right\rangle=\left|{\left\{{\ell\in[d]:{({\vec{x}+\vec{y}})}[\ell]=2}\right\}}\right|=d\cdot\vec{f}[2]
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\left|{d\cdot{\left(\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\right)}-d\cdot{\left(\vec{f}[2]+\vec{f}[0]-\vec{f}[2]\right)}}\right|

(18)

\displaystyle\leq d\cdot{\left(\left|{\vec{r}[4]-\vec{f}[4]}\right|+\left|{\vec{r}[2]-\vec{f}[2]}\right|+\left|{\vec{r}[0]-\vec{f}[0]}\right|+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\left|{\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)}\right|\right)}.

(19)

Since
\Expectation\left[\left|{\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)}\right|\right]\in O(1/\varepsilon)
, and by assumption,

\Expectation\left[\left\|{\vec{r}-\vec{f}}\right\|_{\infty}\right]\in o{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{1}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\right)},

and

\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\in o{\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\right)},

it concludes that

\Expectation\left[\left|{d\cdot{\left(\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\right)}-d\cdot{\left(\vec{f}[2]+\vec{f}[0]-\vec{f}[2]\right)}}\right|\right]\in o{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\right)}.

Combing
d\cdot{({\vec{f}[4]+\vec{f}[0]-\vec{f}[2]})}=\left\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\right\rangle
with Markov inequality, we see

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\left|{d\cdot{\left(\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\right)}-\left\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\right\rangle}\right|\geq\frac{2\cdot c_{1}\cdot\sqrt{d}}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\right]

(20)

\displaystyle\leq{\Expectation\left[\left|{d\cdot{\left(\vec{r}[4]+\vec{r}[0]-\vec{r}[2]+3\cdot\Delta_{\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{A}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbb{L}ap}\left({1/\varepsilon}\right)\right)}-\left\langle\vec{x},\vec{y}\right\rangle}\right|\right]}\Bigg/{{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2\cdot c\cdot e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\right)}}

(21)

\displaystyle\in o{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\middle/{\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{2\cdot c\cdot e^{c\cdot\varepsilon}}\right)}\right)}

(22)

\displaystyle=o(1).

(23)

∎
