Exact Convergence Analysis for Metropolis-Hastings Independence Samplers in Wasserstein Distances
Austin Brown, Galin L. Jones

Abstract
Under mild assumptions, we show the exact convergence rate in total variation is also exact in weaker Wasserstein distances for the Metropolis-Hastings independence sampler. We develop a new upper and lower bound on the worst-case Wasserstein distance when initialized from points. For an arbitrary point initialization, we show the convergence rate is the same and matches the convergence rate in total variation. We derive exact convergence expressions for more general Wasserstein distances when initialization is at a specific point. Using optimization, we construct a novel centered independent proposal to develop exact convergence rates in Bayesian quantile regression and many generalized linear model settings. We show the exact convergence rate can be upper bounded in Bayesian binary response regression (e.g. logistic and probit) when the sample size and dimension grow together.

Exact convergence rates in total variation in the general state space setting have been nonexistent until very recently, and, again, these are for MHI samplers
[

,
51
]

However, exact convergence rates in total variation have been studied only in trivial examples.
This leaves practitioners with little guidance on the convergence behavior and reliability of MHI samplers in practical applications, especially in high dimensions.

We study exact convergence rates of the MHI sampler in

-Wasserstein distances, which we refer to as only Wasserstein distances.
The exact convergence behavior of Metropolis-Hastings algorithms across various Wasserstein distances has not been previously studied.
Only in specific Wasserstein distances, have upper bounds been developed

,

.
We develop exact convergence rates which are universal across Wasserstein distances for the MHI sampler.
Surprisingly, we show the exact convergence rate in total variation

We develop a new upper and lower bound on the worst-case Wasserstein distance when initialized from points.

Compared to methods used to approximate integrals such as importance sampling, MHI samplers can generate samples from the target distribution which is often of interest for practitioners.

Connections between the MHI sampler and rejection sampling are also well-known

2
MHI samplers and Wasserstein distances

As they will be considered here, MHI samplers
simulate a Markov chain with invariant distribution
\Pi
supported on a
nonempty set
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
using a proposal distribution

Q
which, to avoid trivialities, is assumed throughout to be
different than
\Pi
.
We also assume throughout that
\Pi
has Lebesgue density
\pi
with support
\Theta
and
Q
has Lebesgue density
q
with support
\Theta
.
Define

a(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\begin{cases}\min\left\{\frac{\pi(\theta^{\prime})q(\theta)}{\pi(\theta)q(\theta^{\prime})},1\right\},&\text{ if }\pi(\theta)q(\theta^{\prime})>0\\
1,&\text{ if }\pi(\theta)q(\theta^{\prime})=0\end{cases}.

We will consider MHI samplers initialized at a point
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
. MHI proceeds as follows: for
t\in\{1,2,\ldots\}
, given
\theta_{t-1}
, draw
\theta^{\prime}_{t}\sim Q(\cdot)
and
U_{t}\sim\text{Unif}(0,1)
independently so that

\theta_{t}=\begin{cases}\theta_{t}^{\prime},\hskip 14.22636pt\text{if }U_{t}\leq a\left(\theta_{t-1},\theta^{\prime}_{t}\right)\\
\theta_{t-1},\hskip 4.26773pt\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

If
\delta_{\theta}
denotes the Dirac measure at the point
\theta
,
the MHI Markov kernel
P
is defined for

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

P(\theta,B)=\int_{B}a(\theta,\theta^{\prime})q(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}+\delta_{\theta}(B)\left(1-\int a(\theta,\theta^{\prime})q(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}\right).

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the Markov kernel at iteration time
t\geq 2
recursively by

P^{t}(\theta,B)=\int P(\theta,d\theta^{\prime})P^{t-1}(\theta^{\prime},B).

Let
\mathcal{C}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi)
be the set of all joint probability
measures with marginals
P^{t}(\theta,\cdot)

and
\Pi
and
\rho
be a lower semicontinuous metric. The
L_{1}
-Wasserstein distance
[
23
,
49
,
50
]
, which we will call simply the Wasserstein distance, is

\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi\right)=\inf_{\xi\in\mathcal{C}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi)}\int\rho(\theta,\omega)d\xi(\theta,\omega).

Notice that when the metric
\rho
is
\rho(\theta,\omega)=I_{\theta\not=\omega}
, then the Wasserstein distance is the total variation distance
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\text{TV}}
.
More generally for a lower semicontinuous function
V\geq 1
,
\rho(\theta,\omega)=[V(\theta)+V(\omega)]I_{\theta\not=\omega}
defines a weighted total variation distance.
Another example is
\rho(\theta,\omega)=\min\{\left\lVert\theta-\omega\right\rVert,1\}
which is always less than the Hamming metric used in total variation.
More general
L_{p}
-Wasserstein distances with
p\geq 2
are not studied in this work.

3
Exact convergence rates for MHI samplers

When the ratio of the proposal and target densities is bounded below by a positive number, that is,

\epsilon^{*}=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\{q(\theta)/\pi(\theta)\}>0,

the MHI sampler is uniformly ergodic in total variation with convergence rate upper bounded by
1-\epsilon^{*}

[
47
, Corollary 4]
.
Unlike in accept-reject sampling,
\epsilon^{*}
does not need to be known explicitly or computed in order to implement MHI.
However, this requirement was shown to be necessary for uniform ergodicity in total variation
[
28
, Theorem 2.1]
. More recently, it was shown the convergence rate cannot be improved
[
51
, Theorem 1]
.
We show this is the case even in weaker Wasserstein distances where the lower bound does not follow trivially from that of the total variation lower bound
[
51
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\rho(\cdot,\cdot)\leq 1
.
Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi)\leq(1-\epsilon^{*})^{t}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\rho(\cdot,\theta)d\Pi.

If in addition,
q
is lower semicontinuous on
\Theta
,
\pi
is upper semicontinuous on
\Theta
, and

\Theta
can be expressed as a countable union of compact sets, then

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon^{*})^{t}\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\rho(\cdot,\theta)d\Pi\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi)\leq(1-\epsilon^{*})^{t}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\rho(\cdot,\theta)d\Pi.

Proof.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
∎

The semicontinuity assumption is not required when working with the total variation distance
[
51
, Theorem 1]
, but it is a mild assumption that holds in many practical applications.
The upper bound constant can improve upon upper bounds in total variation
[
47
]
if for example,
\rho
is continuous and
\Theta
is compact.
If
\epsilon^{*}=0
, Theorem
1
also gives the lower bound

\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\rho(\cdot,\theta)d\Pi\leq\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi),

which shows MHI cannot converge uniformly from any starting point for many Wasserstein distances.
Thus, under mild assumptions, Theorem
1
gives a complete characterization of the worst-case convergence of the MHI sampler in many Wasserstein distances.

Exact convergence expressions are available when the Markov chain is initialized at
\theta^{*}=\text{argmin}\left\{q(\theta)/\pi(\theta):\theta\in\Theta\right\}
using techniques from
[
51
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose there exists a solution

\theta^{*}=\text{argmin}\left\{q(\theta)/\pi(\theta):\theta\in\Theta\right\}.

Then

\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot),\Pi\right)=\left(1-q(\theta^{*})/\pi(\theta^{*})\right)^{t}\int\rho(\theta,\theta^{*})d\Pi(\theta).

Proof.

Define
\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}=q(\theta^{*})/\pi(\theta^{*})=\epsilon^{*}
.
Under our assumptions
P^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot)
can be represented as a convex combination of the target distribution and the Dirac measure at the point
\theta^{*}

[
51
, Remark 1, Theorem 2]
, that is,

\displaystyle P^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\left(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}\right)^{t}\right)\Pi+\left(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}\right)^{t}\delta_{\theta^{*}}.

(1)

Let
\psi:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
be a function such that
\int_{\Theta}|\psi|d\Pi<\infty
.
We have
the identity,

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\psi dP^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot)=\left(1-\left(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}\right)^{t}\right)\int_{\Theta}\psi d\Pi(B)+\left(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}\right)^{t}\psi(\theta^{*}).

(2)

Since the only coupling between
\Pi^{*}
and the Dirac measure
\delta_{\theta^{*}}
is the independent coupling
[
13
]
, the Wasserstein distance takes the simple form
\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(\delta_{\theta^{*}},\Pi\right)=\int\rho(\theta,\theta^{*})d\Pi(\theta)
.

Since
q
is not exactly
\pi
, then
\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}\in(0,1)
.
Let
M_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the set of bounded measurable functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for real-valued functions
\varphi
, let
\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\text{Lip}(\rho)}=\sup_{x,y,x\not=y}\{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|/\rho(x,y)\}
denote the Lipschitz norm with respect to the distance
\rho
.
Applying the Kantorovich-Rubinstein theorem
[
49
, Theorem 1.14]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot),\Pi\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in M_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\text{Lip}(\rho)}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Theta}\varphi d\left(P^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot)-\Pi\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in M_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\text{Lip}(\rho)}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\left\{(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}})^{t}\int_{\Theta}\varphi d\left(\delta_{\theta^{*}}-\Pi\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}})^{t}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in M_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\text{Lip}(\rho)}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\int_{\Theta}\varphi d\left(\delta_{\theta^{*}}-\Pi\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}})^{t}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(\delta_{\theta^{*}},\Pi\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}})^{t}\int_{\Theta}\rho(\theta,\theta^{*})d\Pi(\theta).

∎

.

3.1
Application: Bayesian quantile regression

Fix
r\in(0,1)
and suppose, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
\epsilon_{i}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with density

p_{r}(\epsilon)=r(1-r)\left(\exp((1-r)\epsilon)I_{\epsilon<0}+\exp(-r\epsilon)I_{\epsilon\geq 0}\right).

Let
v_{0},s_{0}\in(0,\infty)
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be symmetric positive-definite.
We paramaterize the inverse gamma distribution so that if
\sigma\sim\text{IG}(v,s)
for some
v,c\in(0,\infty)
, then it has a density proportional to
\sigma^{-v-1}\exp(-s/\sigma)
.
Assume the Bayesian quantile regression model for
i\in 1,\ldots,n
where
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is fixed and

\displaystyle\sigma\sim\text{IG}(v_{0},s_{0})

\displaystyle\beta|\sigma\sim\text{N}_{d}(0,\sigma C)

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\beta^{T}X_{i}+\sigma\epsilon_{i}.

Let
\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)
denote the posterior and
\pi(\cdot|X,Y)
denote the density for this Bayesian model with normalizing constant
Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}
.

Upper bounds on the convergence rate were previously investigated for Gibbs samplers
[
24
]
in this setting. We will study the MHI sampler with a normal-inverse-gamma proposal constructed as follows. Define
\ell_{r}(u)=u(r-I_{u<0})
and
s_{n,r}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by
s_{n,r}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{r}(Y_{i}-\beta^{T}X_{i})+\beta^{T}C^{-1}\beta/2
. Since
s_{n,r}
is strongly convex, let
\beta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the unique minimum of the function
s_{n,r}
. Now the MHI proposal is given by

\displaystyle\sigma\sim\text{IG}(n+v_{0},s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta^{*}))

\displaystyle\beta|\sigma\sim\text{N}_{d}(\beta^{*},\sigma C).

Let
\Gamma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the usual Gamma function and define

\epsilon_{\beta^{*}}=Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}\left(2\pi\right)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\det\left(C\right)^{-1/2}\left(s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta^{*})\right)^{n+v_{0}}\Gamma(n+v_{0})^{-1}.

The following gives an exact convergence rate of this algorithm which completely characterizes its convergence from a specific initialization.

Theorem 2
.

For any
\sigma_{0}\in(0,\infty)

\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}((\beta^{*},\sigma_{0}),\cdot),\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)\right)=\left(1-\epsilon_{\beta^{*}}\right)^{t}\int\rho((\beta,\sigma),(\beta^{*},\sigma_{0}))d\Pi(\beta,\sigma|X,Y).

Proof.

We may define the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle f(\beta,\sigma)=\frac{s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta)}{\sigma}+(n+v_{0}+1+d/2)\log(\sigma)

and write the posterior density as
\pi(\beta,\sigma|X,Y)=Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}^{-1}\exp\left(-f(\beta,\sigma)\right)
. Since the function
\beta\mapsto s_{n,r}(\beta)-\beta^{T}C^{-1}\beta/2
is a convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then by Lemma
4
for every
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

s_{n,r}(\beta)\geq s_{n,r}(\beta^{*})+\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right).

For any
\left(\beta,\sigma\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
, we then have the lower bound

\displaystyle f(\beta,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta)}{\sigma}+(n+v_{0}+1+d/2)\log(\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta^{*})}{\sigma}+(n+v_{0}+1+d/2)\log(\sigma)+\frac{1}{2\sigma}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right).

This implies

\displaystyle f(\beta,\sigma)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\sigma}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}\left(\beta-\beta^{*}\right)-\frac{s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta^{*})}{\sigma}-(n+v_{0}+1+d/2)\log(\sigma)

\displaystyle\geq 0.

Let
q
denote the proposal’s normal-inverse-gamma density.
For any
\sigma_{0}\in(0,\infty)
and for every
\left(\beta,\sigma\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)
, we have shown

\displaystyle\frac{q(\beta,\sigma)}{\pi(\beta,\sigma)}

\displaystyle\geq Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}\left(2\pi\right)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\det\left(C\right)^{-1/2}\left(s_{0}+s_{n,r}(\beta^{*})\right)^{n+v_{0}}\Gamma(n+v_{0})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{q(\beta^{*},\sigma_{0})}{\pi(\beta^{*},\sigma_{0})}.

An application of Proposition
1
completes the proof.
∎

Note that
\epsilon_{\beta^{*}}
is difficult to compute since it depends on the normalizing constant, but we give an example later where upper bounding the convergence rate is possible in Bayesian logistic regression.

4
The convergence rate at arbitrary initializations

The previous section studies the worst-case convergence rate and the convergence rate at an individual point for the MHI sampler.
We can study the convergence rate at every point as was done for total variation
[
51
]
.
The technique needed to prove this relies on the exact representation of the MHI sampler
[
45
, Theorem 1]
but new techniques are needed to show this in the Wasserstein distance.
Similar to the convergence rate in total variation
[
51
]
, we define the Wasserstein convergence rate for a point
\theta\in\Theta
as

r_{\rho}(\theta)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi)^{1/t}.

When the distance metric is
\min\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert,1\}
where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
can be any norm, Theorem
3
shows we can obtain the convergence rate at every point under mild conditions.
We require
\pi,q
to be locally Lipschitz continuous and bounded which is stronger than only locally Lipschitz as in total variation
[
51
]
.
However, this additional condition is satisfied in many practical applications in statistics.
Theorem
3
also lower bounds the convergence rate for Wasserstein
L_{p}
-distances and the rate of convergence for these distances cannot be improved.

Theorem 3
.

If
\pi,q
are locally Lipschitz continuous and bounded on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and there exists a solution

\theta^{*}=\text{argmin}\left\{q(\theta)/\pi(\theta):\theta\in\Theta\right\},

then for any
\theta\in\Theta
, the Wasserstein convergence rate is the same with

r_{\min\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert,1\}}(\theta)=1-q(\theta^{*})/\pi(\theta^{*}).

Proof.

If the initialization is at
\theta^{*}
, then the result follows from Proposition
1
.
Fix a point
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
such that
\theta_{0}\not=\theta^{*}
.
Using Lemma
1
, we have an upper bound on the convergence rate by

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{W}_{\min\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert,1\}}(P^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot),\Pi)^{1/t}\leq 1-q(\theta^{*})/\pi(\theta^{*}).

It remains to lower bound the limit inferior.

For
h\in(0,1]
, define the function

\varphi_{h}(\theta)=\frac{1}{(2h)^{d}}\exp(-h^{-1}\left\lVert\theta-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{1}).

This is the probability density function for a Laplace distribution and
\varphi_{h}
is
2^{-d}h^{-d-1}
Lipschitz, and so
2^{d}h^{d+1}\varphi_{h}(\theta)=h\exp(-h^{-1}\left\lVert\theta-\theta^{*}\right\rVert_{1})
is nonnegative,
1-
Lipschitz and bounded by
1
.
In particular, it is readily shown that
2^{d}h^{d+1}\varphi_{h}(\theta)
is
\min\{1,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}\}
Lipschitz.
Using Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality
[
49
, Theorem 1.14]
, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\min\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1},1\}}(P^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot),\Pi)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d}h^{d+1}\left[\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi-\int\varphi_{h}dP^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot)\right].

(3)

We will develop some approximation properties of
\varphi_{h}
.
Since
\theta_{0}\not=\theta^{*}
, then it is readily shown that
\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\varphi_{h}(\theta_{0})=0
.
Using change of variables and since we have assumed
\sup_{\theta}\pi(\theta)<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\left|\int\varphi_{h}(\theta^{\prime})\pi(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}-\pi(\theta^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}\leq t}\left|\pi(\theta^{*}+h\theta^{\prime})-\pi(\theta^{*})\right|2^{-d}\exp(-\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1})d\theta^{\prime}

(4)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt+2\sup_{\theta}\pi(\theta)\int_{\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}>t}2^{-d}\exp(-\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}\right\rVert_{1})d\theta^{\prime}.

(5)

If
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{d}
i.i.d. Laplace, then we have the tail bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\left\lVert Y\right\rVert_{2}\geq t)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i}|Y_{i}|\geq\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(|Y_{i}|\geq\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\exp\left(-\frac{t}{\sqrt{d}}\right).

Since norms are equivalent on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
\pi,q
are locally Lipschitz with respect to any norm.
Choosing
h=h_{0}/t^{2}
for some
h_{0}\in(0,1)
, since
\pi
is locally Lipschitz, we can find a universal constant
L\in(0,\infty)
so that for any
\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}\right\rVert_{2}\leq t
.

\left|\pi(\theta^{*}+h\theta^{\prime})-\pi(\theta^{*})\right|\leq\frac{h_{0}L}{t}.

Applying these upper bounds to (
4
) and (
5
), then for large enough
t
,

\displaystyle\left|\int\varphi_{h}(\theta^{\prime})\pi(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}-\pi(\theta^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2h_{0}L}{t}.

(6)

A similar argument with the assumptions on
q
yields for large enough
t
,

\displaystyle\left|\int\varphi_{h}(\theta^{\prime})q(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}-q(\theta^{*})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2h_{0}L}{t}.

(7)

It remains to lower bound (
3
).
We will use the exact representation of the independence sampler
[
45
, Theorem 1, Lemma 3]
.
Define the importance sampling weight by
w(\theta)=\pi(\theta)/q(\theta)
and its maximum
w^{*}=w(\theta^{*})=\pi(\theta^{*})/q(\theta^{*})
.
The rejection probability can be represented for
\theta\in\Theta
by

\lambda(w(\theta))=\int_{w(\theta^{\prime})\leq w(\theta)}\left[1-\frac{w(\theta^{\prime})}{w(\theta)}\right]q(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}.

For
w\in(0,\infty)
, define

T_{t}(w)=\int_{w}^{\infty}\frac{t\lambda^{t-1}(v)}{v^{2}}dv,

and using the exact representation of the independence sampler
[
45
, Theorem 1, Lemma 3]
, for measurable sets
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

P^{t}(\theta_{0},B)=\int_{B}T_{t}(\max\{w(\theta_{0}),w(\theta^{\prime})\})\pi(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}+\lambda^{t}(w(\theta_{0}))\delta_{\theta_{0}}(B).

The proof of existing results
[
51
, Theorem 4]
shows that

T_{t}(w)\leq 1+(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\left[\frac{t}{w^{*}-1}\left(\frac{w^{*}}{w}-1\right)-1\right].

We now use the exact representation of the independence sampler to lower bound (
3
).
We have
\lambda^{t}(w(\theta_{0}))\leq(1-1/w^{*})^{t}
and so we then have the upper bound

\displaystyle\int\varphi_{h}dP^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot)-\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi

\displaystyle\leq-(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi+\lambda^{t}(w(\theta_{0}))\varphi_{h}(\theta_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt+(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\frac{t}{w^{*}-1}\int\left[\frac{w^{*}}{\max\{w(\theta_{0}),w(\theta^{\prime})\}}-1\right]\varphi_{h}(\theta^{\prime})\pi(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}

\displaystyle\leq(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\left[\varphi_{h}(\theta_{0})-\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt+(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\frac{t}{w^{*}-1}\left[w^{*}\int\varphi_{h}(\theta^{\prime})q(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}-\int\varphi_{h}(\theta^{\prime})\pi(\theta^{\prime})d\theta^{\prime}\right].

(8)

Using the upper bound (
8
) with the approximations (
6
) and (
7
) yields

\displaystyle\int\varphi_{h}dP^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot)-\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi

\displaystyle\leq(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\left[\varphi_{h}(\theta_{0})-\pi(\theta^{*})+\frac{2h_{0}L}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt+(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\frac{t}{w^{*}-1}\left[w^{*}q(\theta^{*})-\pi(\theta^{*})+(w^{*}+1)\frac{2h_{0}L}{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-1/w^{*})^{t}\left[\varphi_{h}(\theta_{0})-\pi(\theta^{*})+\frac{2h_{0}L}{t}+\frac{w^{*}+1}{w^{*}-1}2h_{0}L\right].

Therefore, we can choose a small enough
h_{0}\in(0,1)
independently of
t
so that we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\int\varphi_{h}dP^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot)-\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\pi(\theta^{*})}{4}(1-1/w^{*})^{t}.

Applying these bounds to (
3
) and using that we have chosen
h=h_{0}/t^{2}
, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\min\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1},1\}}(P^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot),\Pi)

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d}h^{d+1}\left[\int\varphi_{h}d\Pi-\int\varphi_{h}dP^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot)\right]

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d-2}h^{d+1}\pi(\theta^{*})(1-1/w^{*})^{t}

\displaystyle\geq 2^{d-2}\left(\frac{h_{0}}{t^{2}}\right)^{d+1}\pi(\theta^{*})(1-1/w^{*})^{t}.

Taking the limit

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{W}_{\min\{\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1},1\}}(P^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot),\Pi)^{1/t}\geq 1-1/w^{*}=1-q(\theta^{*})/\pi(\theta^{*}).

Since all norms are equivalent on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, this can be extended to any norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
.
∎

5
MHI samplers with centered Gaussian proposals

We look to apply the exact convergence expression from Proposition
1
to practical applications since the convergence rate is the same at every initialization under mild assumptions.
Recently, centered drift functions have been used to improve convergence analyses of some Monte Carlo Markov chains
[
10
,
36
,
39
]
. Our focus is instead on centering the proposal distribution, that is, matching the optimal points of the proposal and target densities similar to Laplace approximations.

We shall see in the next section that by centering a Gaussian proposal, we may satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
1
for a general class of target distributions with
\theta^{*}
being the optimum of the target’s density. While we focus on Gaussian proposals,
the technique of centering proposals is in fact more general.

We will assume the target
distribution
\Pi
is a probability distribution supported on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
With
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and normalizing constant
Z_{\Pi}
, define the density
\pi
by

\pi(\theta)=Z_{\Pi}^{-1}\exp\left(-f(\theta)\right)
.
Let
\theta^{*}
be the unique maximum of
\pi
,
\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
, and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a symmetric, positive-definite matrix.
Let the proposal distribution
Q
with density
q
correspond to a
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution,
\text{N}_{d}(\theta^{*},\alpha^{-1}C)
. In this case, the ratio of the proposal density and target density is
\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\alpha^{d/2}\det\left(C\right)^{-1/2}Z_{\Pi}\exp(f(\theta^{*}))
.

Proposition 2
.

If
\theta^{*}
exists and, for
any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f(\theta)\geq f(\theta^{*})+\alpha\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)/2,

then

\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\theta^{*},\cdot),\Pi\right)=\left(1-\epsilon_{\theta^{*}}\right)^{t}\int\rho(\theta,\theta^{*})d\Pi(\theta).

Proof.

Since the proposal density has been centered at the point

\theta^{*}
, it then satisfies

q(\theta^{*})=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\alpha^{d/2}\det\left(C\right)^{-1/2}
. For every

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\frac{q(\theta)}{\pi(\theta)}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\alpha^{d/2}\det\left(C\right)^{-1/2}Z_{\Pi}\exp\left(f(\theta)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq(2\pi)^{-d/2}\alpha^{d/2}\det\left(C\right)^{-1/2}Z_{\Pi}\exp\left(f(\theta^{*})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q(\theta^{*})}{\pi(\theta^{*})}.

Since both densities are positive and the proposal is independent of
the previous iteration, we have shown that the conditions for Proposition
1
are satisfied and an application of Proposition

1
with the proposal and target distribution
Q
and
\Pi
as we have defined them in this section completes the proof.
∎

The point
\theta^{*}
is guaranteed to exist if the function
f
satisfies
a convexity property.
A function
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is strongly convex with parameter
\mu
if there is an
\mu\in(0,+\infty)
so that

h(\cdot)-\mu\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert^{2}/2
is convex

[
16
,
31
]
. The norm in this definition is
often taken to be the Euclidean norm, but we will use the
norm induced by the matrix
C^{-1}
.
We consider using a Gaussian proposal centered at a point
\theta_{0}
which is not necessarily the optimum of the target density.
Let
g_{f(\theta_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a subgradient of
f
at
\theta_{0}

[
31
]
.
For a point
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we consider the proposal corresponding to a
d
-dimensional Gaussian distribution,
\text{N}_{d}(\theta_{0}-\alpha^{-1}Cg_{f(\theta_{0})},\alpha^{-1}C)
. When
f
is differentiable, this construction of the proposal uses the gradient of
f
in a similar way as MALA.
The ratio of the proposal and target density evaluated at
\theta_{0}
is
\epsilon_{\theta_{0}}=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\det\left(\alpha^{-1}C\right)^{-1/2}Z_{\Pi}\exp(f(\theta_{0})-{g_{f(\theta_{0})}}^{T}Cg_{f(\theta_{0})}/(2\alpha))
.
Choosing
\theta_{0}\equiv\theta^{*}
yields the centered Gaussian proposal, but we also have an exact convergence expression in other cases.

Proposition 3
.

If the function

\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-\alpha\theta^{T}C^{-1}\theta/2
is convex for
all points on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then

\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\theta_{0},\cdot),\Pi\right)=(1-\epsilon_{\theta_{0}})^{t}\int\rho(\theta,\theta_{0})d\Pi(\theta).

Proof.

Since the function

f(\theta)-\alpha\theta^{T}C^{-1}\theta/2
is convex for
all points on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then for each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle f(\theta)

\displaystyle\geq f(\theta_{0})+{g_{f(\theta_{0})}}^{T}(\theta-\theta_{0})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta_{0}\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta-\theta_{0})

\displaystyle=f(\theta_{0})-\frac{1}{2\alpha}(Cg_{f(\theta_{0})})^{T}g_{f(\theta_{0})}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta_{0}+\alpha^{-1}Cg_{f(\theta_{0})}\right)^{T}C^{-1}\left(\theta-\theta_{0}+\alpha^{-1}Cg_{f(\theta_{0})}\right).

This implies for every
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the ratio of the proposal density
q
corresponding to the distribution
\text{N}_{d}(\theta_{0}-\alpha^{-1}Cg_{f(\theta_{0})},\alpha^{-1}C)
and target density
\pi
satisfies

\frac{q(\theta)}{\pi(\theta)}\geq\frac{q(\theta_{0})}{\pi(\theta_{0})}=\epsilon_{\theta_{0}}.

An application of Proposition

1
completes the proof.
∎

5.1
Application: Bayesian generalized linear models

We consider Bayesian Poisson and negative-binomial regression for count response regression and Bayesian logistic and probit regression for binary response regression (e.g. logistic and probit).
Suppose there are
n
discrete-valued responses
Y_{i}

with features
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a parameter
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For Poisson regression, assume the
Y_{i}
’s are conditionally independent with

Y_{i}\big|X_{i},\beta\sim\text{Poisson}\left(\exp\left(\beta^{T}X_{i}\right)\right).

Similarly, for negative-binomial regression, if
\xi\in(0,+\infty)
, assume

Y_{i}\big|X_{i},\beta\sim\text{Negative-Binomial}\left(\xi,\left(1+\exp\left(-\beta^{T}X_{i}\right)\right)^{-1}\right).

For binary response regression, if
S:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
, assume

Y_{i}|X_{i},\beta\sim\text{Bernoulli}\left(S\left(\beta^{T}X_{i}\right)\right).

For logistic regression, we will consider
S(x)=\left(1+\exp\left(x\right)\right)^{-1}
and for probit regression, we will consider
S(x)
to be the cumulative distribution function of a standard Gaussian random variable.
Suppose
\beta\sim\text{N}_{d}(0,\alpha^{-1}C)
where

\alpha\in(0,+\infty)
and
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a symmetric,
positive-definite matrix. Both
\alpha
and
C
are assumed to be known.
Define the vector
Y=\left(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\right)^{T}
and the matrix
X=\left(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\right)^{T}
.
Let
\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)
denote the posterior with density
\pi(\cdot|X,Y)
.
If
\ell_{n}
denotes the negative log-likelihood for each model, the posterior density
is characterized by

\pi(\beta|X,Y)=Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y))}^{-1}\exp\left(-\ell_{n}(\beta)-\frac{\alpha}{2}\beta^{T}C^{-1}\beta\right).

Observe that the function
\ell_{n}
is convex in all four models we consider.
Let
\beta^{*}
denote the unique maximum of
\pi(\cdot|X,Y)
.
For the MHI
algorithm, we use a
\text{N}_{d}(\beta^{*},\alpha^{-1}C)
proposal
distribution, and Proposition
3
immediately yields the following for each posterior.

Corollary 1
.

We have

\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\beta^{*},\cdot),\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)\right)=\left(1-\epsilon_{\beta^{*}}\right)^{t}\int\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi\left(\beta|X,Y\right)

where
\epsilon_{\beta^{*}}=\exp(\ell_{n}(\beta^{*})+\frac{\alpha}{2}{\beta^{*}}^{T}C^{-1}\beta^{*})Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}\left((2\pi)^{d/2}\det\left(\alpha^{-1}C\right)^{1/2}\right)^{-1}
.

5.2
Convergence complexity analysis in binary response regression

Our goal now is to obtain an upper bound on the rate of convergence established in Corollary
1

in high dimensions for binary response regression. In this context, it is natural to treat the

(Y_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
as stochastic; each time the sample size increases, the additional observation is randomly generated. Specifically, we will assume that
(Y_{i},X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are independent with

Y_{i}|X_{i},\beta\sim\text{Bernoulli}\left(S\left(\beta^{T}X_{i}\right)\right)
and
X_{i}\sim N_{d}(0,\sigma^{2}n^{-1}I_{d})
with

\sigma^{2}\in(0,+\infty)
.
Similar scaling assumptions on the data are used for high-dimensional
maximum-likelihood theory in logistic regression
[
46
]
. We
will also assume the limit of the trace of the covariance matrix used
in our prior is finite, that is,
tr(C)\to s_{0}\in(0,+\infty)
as
d\to+\infty
. Note that this is a necessary condition that the trace of the covariance is finite for Gaussian distributions to exist in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
[
3
]
.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that the following conditions hold.

1.

The negative log-likelihood
\ell_{n}
is a twice continuously differentiable convex function.

2.

There is a universal constant
r_{0}\in(0,+\infty)
such that the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood
H_{\ell_{n}}
satisfies for every
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\lambda_{max}\left(H_{\ell_{n}}(\beta)\right)\leq r_{0}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right).

Let

a_{0}=r_{0}(1+\gamma^{1/2})^{2}\sigma^{2}s_{0}/(2\alpha)
. If

d,n\to+\infty
in such a way that
d/n\to\gamma\in(0,+\infty)
,
then, almost surely

\limsup_{d/n\to\gamma}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\beta^{*},\cdot),\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)\right)\leq M_{0}(1-\exp\left(-a_{0}\right))^{t}

where
M_{0}=\limsup_{d/n\to\gamma}\int\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y)
.

Proof.

Under our assumption, we may write the matrix

X=n^{-1/2}G
where
G
is a matrix with i.i.d Gaussian
entries with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Denote the
largest eigenvalue of the matrix
X^{T}X
by
\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)
.
Therefore, as
d,n\to\infty
in such a way that

d/n\to\gamma\in(0,+\infty)
,

\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)=\lambda_{max}\left(\frac{1}{n}G^{T}G\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}=1}\left\lVert G^{T}Gv\right\rVert_{2}\to(1+\gamma^{1/2})^{2}\sigma^{2}

almost surely
[
11
, Theorem 1]
.

Define the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f(\beta)=\ell_{n}(\beta)+\alpha/2\beta^{T}C^{-1}\beta
where

\ell_{n}
is the negative log-likelihood loss function and define
Z_{Q}=(2\pi)^{d/2}\det\left(\alpha^{-1}C\right)^{1/2}
. We will first
lower bound the quantity

\exp(f(\beta^{*}))Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}/Z_{Q}
.
We have that for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

v^{T}H_{\ell_{n}}(\beta)v\leq r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

This implies that for any
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Hessian of
f
, denoted by
H_{f}
, satisfies

v^{T}H_{f}(\beta)v\leq v^{T}\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)I_{d}+\alpha C^{-1}\right)v.

Since the function
\ell_{n}
is twice continuously differentiable, then
f
is also twice continuously differentiable.
Since both the gradient
\nabla f
and Hessian
H_{f}
are continuous and
\nabla f(\beta^{*})=0
, we use a Taylor expansion to obtain the upper bound

\displaystyle f(\beta)

\displaystyle=f(\beta^{*})+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{t}(\beta-\beta^{*})^{T}H_{f}(\beta^{*}+s(\beta-\beta^{*}))(\beta-\beta^{*})dsdt

\displaystyle\leq f(\beta^{*})+\frac{1}{2}(\beta-\beta^{*})^{T}\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)I_{d}+\alpha C^{-1}\right)(\beta-\beta^{*}).

We then have a lower bound on the normalizing constant of the target posterior

Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp\left(-f(\beta)\right)d\beta\geq\frac{\exp\left(-f(\beta^{*})\right)(2\pi)^{d/2}}{\det\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)I_{d}+\alpha C^{-1}\right)^{1/2}}.

This in turn yields a lower bound on the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}}{Z_{Q}}\exp\left(f(\beta^{*})\right)\geq\frac{\det\left(\alpha C^{-1}\right)^{1/2}}{\det\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)I_{d}+\alpha C^{-1}\right)^{1/2}}.

(9)

The matrix
C
is symmetric and positive-definite and so its
eigenvalues exist and are positive. Let

\left(\lambda_{i}(C)\right)_{i=1}^{d}
be the eigenvalues of
C
. It
is readily verified that the eigenvalues of the matrix

r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)I_{d}+\alpha C^{-1}
exist and are

\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)+\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{i}(C)}\right)_{i=1}^{d}
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\det\left(\alpha C^{-1}\right)}{\det\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}(X^{T}X)I_{d}+\alpha C^{-1}\right)}

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{i}(C)}}{\prod_{i=1}^{d}\left(r_{0}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{i}(C)}\right)}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{i}(C)}}{r_{0}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)+\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_{i}(C)}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)\lambda_{i}(C)+1}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)\lambda_{i}(C)+1\right)\right).

(11)

We have the basic inequality
\log(x+1)\leq x
for any
x\in[0,+\infty)
.
Since the eigenvalues of
C
are positive and
\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)
is nonnegative, we have the upper bound

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)\lambda_{i}(C)+1\right)\leq\frac{r_{0}}{\alpha}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(C).

(12)

This yields a lower bound on (
11
). Define the doubly-indexed
sequence
(a_{d,n})
by

a_{d,n}=\frac{r_{0}}{2\alpha}\lambda_{max}\left(X^{T}X\right)\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(C).

We have then shown that

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}}{Z_{Q}}\exp\left(f(\beta^{*})\right)\geq\exp\left(-a_{d,n}\right).

(13)

By our assumption,
tr(C)\to s_{0}
as
d\to\infty
.
That is to say that

\lim_{d\to+\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}(C)=s_{0}.

Then as
d,n\to\infty
in such a way that
d/n\to\gamma\in(0,+\infty)
, by continuity

a_{d,n}\to\frac{r_{0}}{2\alpha}(1+\gamma^{1/2})^{2}\sigma^{2}s_{0}

almost surely.
This implies using continuity that almost surely,

\displaystyle\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}(1-\exp\left(-a_{n,d}\right))^{t}

\displaystyle=(1-\exp\left(-a_{0}\right))^{t}.

By Corollary

1
, we have the upper bound on
the Wasserstein distance for each
d
and each
n
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\beta^{*},\cdot),\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\exp(f(\beta^{*}))\frac{Z_{\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)}}{Z_{Q}}\right)^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\exp\left(-a_{n,d}\right))^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y).

Suppose that
\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y)<\infty
.
Using properties of the limit superior,

\displaystyle\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\beta^{*},\cdot),\Pi(\cdot|X,Y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}(1-\exp\left(-a_{n,d}\right))^{t}\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}(1-\exp\left(-a_{n,d}\right))^{t}\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y)

\displaystyle=(1-\exp\left(-a_{0}\right))^{t}\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y).

The other case when
\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d,n\to\infty\\
\frac{d}{n}\to\gamma\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(\beta,\beta^{*})d\Pi(\beta|X,Y)=+\infty
is trivial.
∎

.

Final remarks

We studied the exact convergence behavior of the MHI sampler across general Wasserstein distances.
We showed upper and lower bounds on the worst-case convergence rate for Wasserstein distances weaker than the total variation distance.
We showed the exact convergence rate at every initialization for Wasserstein distances weaker than the total variation distance is the same and matches that of the total variation convergence rate
[
51
]
.
When starting at a certain point, we gave exact convergence expressions.
By centering an independent proposal, we directly imbue the Markov chain with a strong attraction to a set where the target distribution has high probability. We showed this technique can provide uniform control over acceptance probability yielding exact convergence rates in Bayesian quantile regression.
The centered MHI sampler turns out to have many applications for posteriors that arise in Bayesian generalized linear models where more sophisticated proposals are often used. With additional assumptions on the data and prior, we also showed this exact convergence rate may be upper bounded when sampling high-dimensional posteriors in Bayesian binary response regression.

Lemma 1

Let
\epsilon^{*}=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\{q(\theta)/\pi(\theta)\}

Then

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi)\leq(1-\epsilon^{*})^{t}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\rho(\theta,\cdot)d\Pi.

Proof.

Let
\theta\in\Theta

[

is a Markov kernel with invariant distribution

It then follows that

\displaystyle\int\varphi dP^{t}(\theta,\cdot)-\int\varphi d\Pi

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon^{*})\left[\int R\varphi dP^{t-1}(\theta,\cdot)-\int\varphi d\Pi\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon^{*})\left[\int R\varphi dP^{t-1}(\theta,\cdot)-\int R\varphi d\Pi\right]

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon^{*})^{t}\left[\int\varphi dR^{t}(\theta,\cdot)-\int\varphi d\Pi\right].

Since

\displaystyle\left|\int\varphi dR^{t}(\theta,\cdot)-\int\varphi d\Pi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int\int\left[\varphi(\theta^{\prime})-\varphi(\omega)\right]d\Pi(\omega)dR^{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int\int\rho(\theta^{\prime},\omega)d\Pi(\omega)dR^{t}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta}\int\rho(\theta^{\prime},\cdot)d\Pi.

Taking the supremum with respect to

\displaystyle\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{W}_{\rho}\left(P^{t}(\theta,\cdot),\Pi\right)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\sup_{\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{\text{Lip}(\rho)}\leq 1}\left[\int\varphi dP^{t}(\theta,\cdot)-\int\varphi d\Pi\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\epsilon^{*})^{t}\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\int\rho(\theta,\cdot)d\Pi.

We now turn our attention to establishing the lower bound.

Then define the sequence

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38092pt+\varphi(\theta_{n})\left(1-\int_{B_{n}}\min\left\{\frac{\pi(y)q(\theta_{n})}{\pi(\theta_{n})q(y)},1\right\}q(y)dy\right).

Lemma 3
.

Let
\epsilon^{*}=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\{q(\theta)/\pi(\theta)\}
.
Suppose there is a sequence of compact sets
B_{n}\subseteq B_{n+1}\subseteq\Theta
increasing to
\Theta=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}B_{n}
.
Define the sequence
\epsilon_{n}=\inf_{\theta\in B_{n}}q(\theta)/\pi(\theta)
.
Then
\epsilon_{n}
is monotonically non-increasing to its limit
\epsilon^{*}
.

Proof.

By definition of the infimum,
\epsilon_{n}\geq\epsilon_{n+1}
and
\epsilon_{n}\geq\epsilon^{*}
. Hence the sequence
\epsilon_{n}
converges.
Let
\delta\in(0,\infty)
. By the definition of the infimum, we can choose
\theta_{\delta}\in\Theta
with
\pi(\theta_{\delta})>0
such that

q(\theta_{\delta})/\pi(\theta_{\delta})-\delta\leq\epsilon^{*}.

We can choose
B_{n_{\delta}}
such that
\theta_{\delta}\in B_{n_{\delta}}
.
Then

\epsilon_{n_{\delta}}-\delta\leq q(\theta_{\delta})/\pi(\theta_{\delta})-\delta\leq\epsilon^{*}.

It follows that for any
n\geq n_{\delta}

|\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon^{*}|=\epsilon_{n}-\epsilon^{*}\leq\epsilon_{n_{\delta}}-\epsilon^{*}\leq\delta.

Therefore,
\lim_{n}\epsilon_{n}=\epsilon^{*}
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a positive-definite, symmetric matrix and
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and suppose
\theta\mapsto f(\theta)-\alpha\theta^{T}C^{-1}\theta/2
is convex for all points on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then there exists
\theta^{*}=\text{argmin}_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(\theta)
and

f(\theta)\geq f(\theta^{*})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta-\theta^{*}).

Proof.

Since the function

f(\theta)-\alpha\theta^{T}C^{-1}\theta/2
is convex for
all points on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it follows that for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
and any
(\theta,\theta^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle f(\lambda\theta+(1-\lambda)\theta^{\prime})\leq\lambda f(\theta)+(1-\lambda)f(\theta^{\prime})-\frac{\alpha}{2}\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta^{\prime}-\theta).

Since

C^{-1}
is positive-definite, then

\alpha\lambda(1-\lambda)\left(\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta^{\prime}-\theta)/2
is nonnegative and this implies
that
f
is a convex function.
It can also be shown that
\lim_{\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert\to+\infty}f(\theta)=+\infty
and since
f
is lower semicontinuous, then
f
attains its minimum
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The right directional derivative

f^{\prime}(\theta^{*};\theta)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}t^{-1}\left[f(\theta^{*}+t\theta)-f(\theta^{*})\right]

exists for all points

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
31
, Theorem 3.1.12]
. For

\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)}\frac{1}{\lambda}\left[f(\theta^{*}+\lambda\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right))-f(\theta^{*})\right]-\frac{1}{(1-\lambda)}\left(f(\theta)-f(\theta^{*})\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta-\theta^{*}).

Taking the limit with
\lambda\downarrow 0
, we have that

f^{\prime}(\theta^{*};\theta-\theta^{*})-f(\theta)+f(\theta^{*})\leq-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta-\theta^{*}).

Since
\theta^{*}
is the minimum of
f
, then the right directional derivative satisfies
f^{\prime}(\theta^{*};\theta-\theta^{*})\geq 0
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Therefore for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

f(\theta)\geq f(\theta^{*})+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\theta-\theta^{*}\right)^{T}C^{-1}(\theta-\theta^{*}).

∎
