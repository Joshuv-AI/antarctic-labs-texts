Long-term Causal Inference with Continuous Treatment: Generalization Bound via Optimal Transport Weights
Zeqin Yang, Weilin Chen, Ruichu Cai, Yuguang Yan, Zhifeng Hao, Zhipeng Yu, Zhichao Zou, Jixing Xu, Peng Zhen, Jiecheng Guo

Abstract
. Estimating long-term treatment effect is difficult since long-term outcomes are typically available only in observational data where unobserved confounders biases causal estimation, while experimental data is often limited to short-term outcomes. The problem is further complicated by continuous treatments and individual-level heterogeneity, which require recovering an entire personalized response curve rather than a single average treatment effect. To address these challenges, we formulate a framework that combines these complementary data sources to estimate long-term Heterogeneous Dose-Response Curve (HDRC). First, to remove the unobserved confounders in the long-term observational data, we construct an optimal transport-based reweighting scheme that aligns the long-term observational data to an auxiliary short-term experimental data, thereby identifying the long-term HDRC. Second, under the induced weighted distribution, we analyze counterfactual prediction with continuous treatments and derive a generalization bound for long-term estimation error. Guided by the resulting theory, we develop a practical long-term HDRC estimator. Empirical studies across synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets highlight the effectiveness of the proposed method.

1.
Introduction

Long-term treatment effect estimation is practically significant in various domains
(
Hohnhold et al., 2015
;
Fleming et al., 1994
;
Chetty et al., 2011
)
.
While Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for estimating causal effects, collecting long-term outcomes through RCTs is often infeasible due to high costs, e.g., it may take years or even decades to collect mortality outcomes in clinical trials.
As a result, only short-term experimental data are typically available.
In contrast, long-term observational data are often more accessible and cost-effective, and thus are commonly taken as a complement to short-term experimental data.

Under such data combination setting,
there has been growing interest in leveraging short-term outcomes to assist in inferring long-term effects.
With all observed confounders,
to estimate long-term average effects,

(
Kallus and Mao, 2020
)
studies the efficiency gains of incorporating the short-term outcomes from experimental data with only limited long-term outcomes under binary treatments, while
(
Zeng et al., 2024
)
extends this to continuous treatments.
As a counterpart, allowing for unobserved confounders,
(
Athey et al., 2020
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2025
;
Imbens et al., 2024
)
propose different assumptions to use short-term outcomes from experimental data to remove confounding in long-term observational data.
Nevertheless, they still focus on long-term average effects of binary treatment.

Therefore, previous works lack practical applicability,
since they have largely focused on estimating long-term average effects under ideal assumptions, e.g.,
no unobserved confounders in observational data or binary treatment setting.
In practice,
to develop personalized policies, decision-makers usually require long-term individual effects estimated with unobserved confounders and continuous treatment,
where the average treatment effects are insufficiently informative and the above assumptions are easily violated.
Consider a vocational training example where a company wants to evaluate the effect of training hours (
continuous treatment
) on one-year employment income (
long-term outcome
) of different participants (
heterogeneity
).
To support this, two types of data can be collected:
short-term experimental data that only collect multiple post-training skill assessment scores (
short-term outcomes
),
and long-term historical observational data that contain both short- and long-term outcomes but suffer from unobserved confounders, such as participants’ learning aptitude.
In such a scenario, how to estimate the individual treatment effect of training hours on the long-term income of a specific participant, i.e., long-term Heterogeneous Dose-Response Curve (HDRC), still remains challenging.

Motivated by the example, this paper focuses on estimating long-term HDRC with unobserved confounders and continuous treatment via data combination,
which presents two key challenges.
First, the long-term outcome only exists in the observational data, suffering from unobserved confounders, and is missing in the experimental data, causing the identification problem.
Second, the spaces of short- and long-term counterfactual outcomes are infinite since the treatment is continuous, causing large counterfactual prediction error of the long-term HDRC with only the actual outcome.

To address the first challenge of identifiability,
we theoretically demonstrate that
unobserved confounders can be eliminated by aligning the conditional distributions of short-term outcomes between observational and experimental data.
To achieve such alignment, under the Optimal Transport (OT) reweighting framework, we establish a mini-batch and joint distribution-based upper bounds on the discrepancy of conditional distributions,
which is computationally efficient and can be easily embedded into deep learning.
Further, to address the second challenge of lacking counterfactual outcomes,
we derive a generalization bound of counterfactual regression based on the reweighted distribution induced by OT,
showing the counterfactual prediction error of the long-term HDRC can be bounded by the observed weighted factual error with a weighted IPM term.
Based on the above theoretical results,
we propose our model called
L
ong-term h
E
terogeneous dose-response curve estim
A
tor with
R
eweighting and represe
N
tation learning (LEARN),
capable of reducing the observed and unobserved confounding bias.

Our contributions are summarized as follows.

First
, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to address the problem of estimating long-term HDRC with unobserved confounders and continuous treatment via data combination.

Second
, we theoretically show that the unobserved confounders can be removed by aligning the conditional distributions between observational and experimental data,
and propose a practical and computationally OT weighting method to achieve such alignment.

Third
, based on the OT-induced reweighted distribution, we derive the generalization bound on the counterfactual prediction error in long-term HDRC estimation, which inspires our model.

Finally
, we conduct extensive experiments on multiple synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets to demonstrate the effectiveness of our proposed method.

2.
Related Work

Long-term Causal Inference.

A common strategy for long-term causal inference is to leverage short-term outcomes via data combination.
Under the surrogacy assumption that short-term outcomes fully mediate the effect of treatment,

(
Athey et al., 2019
)
combines multiple short-term outcomes to robustly estimate the long-term average effects,

(
Cheng et al., 2021
)
models the relationship between the short- and long-term outcomes via deep learning,

(
Singh, 2022
)
proposes kernel estimator for more complex average causal estimands without linearity and separability assumptions.
However,
the surrogacy assumption is often too strong in practice.
With all observed confounders,

(
Kallus and Mao, 2020
)
studies the role of short-term outcomes for long-term causal inference under binary treatment,
while
(
Zeng et al., 2024
)
serves as a counterpart for continuous treatment.

(
Cai et al., 2024
)
consider settings with partially unobserved surrogates and employ identifiable variational autoencoder for recovery.

(
Tran et al.,
)
address dynamic treatments through reinforcement learning,
and
(
Yang et al., 2024
;
Wu et al., 2024
)
develop policy learning method for balancing short- and long-term rewards.
Further,
to address the unobserved confounders,

(
Athey et al., 2020
)
introduce relaxed latent unconfoundedness for identification,
which is further leveraged by
(
Chen and Ritzwoller, 2023
)
to develop a doubly robust estimator.
Alternative identifiability assumptions are proposed by

(
Ghassami et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2025
;
Obradović, 2024
)
.
Interestingly,
(
Park and Sasaki, 2024
)
studies the bracketing relationship between identifiability assumptions in
(
Athey et al., 2020
)
and
(
Ghassami et al., 2022
)
.
Based on proximal methods
(
Tchetgen Tchetgen et al., 2024
)
,

(
Imbens et al., 2024
)
exploits the sequential structure of short-term outcomes.

(
Van Goffrier et al., 2023
)
performs instrumental variable regression to estimate long-term effects.
Despite these advances across various settings,
to the best of our knowledge,
we are the first to focus on the long-term HDRC estimation problem with unobserved confounders and continuous treatment.

Dose-Response Curve Estimation.

Dose-response curve estimation is broadly studied through statistical methods
(
Vegetabile et al., 2021
;
Kennedy et al., 2017
;
Zhu et al., 2015
)
,
and most of them are based on the generalized propensity score
(
Imbens, 2000
)
or entropy balancing
(
Tübbicke, 2022
)
.
Recently,
deep learning-based methods also attract the attention of the research community.
DRNet
(
Schwab et al., 2020
)
divides the continuous treatment into several intervals, and trains one separate head for each interval.
In order to produce a continuous curve,
VCNet
(
Nie et al., 2021
)
adapts a varying coefficient model into neural networks.
SCIGAN
(
Bica et al., 2020
)
generates counterfactual outcomes based on the generative adversarial network.
ADMIT
(
Wang et al., 2022
)
derives a counterfactual bound of estimating the average dose-response curve with a discretized approximation of the IPM distance,
and ACFR
(
Kazemi and Ester, 2024
)
minimizes the KL-divergence using an adversarial game to extract balanced representations for continuous treatment.
Different from the existing works above,
we aim to estimate the HDRC under the long-term estimation scenario with unobserved confounders in this paper, which is more complex.

Optimal Transport.

Optimal transport seeks a minimum-cost plan for moving mass between distributions

(
Monge, 1781
;
Kantorovitch, 1958
;
Villani, 2008
)
,
and has recently been widely used in various applications

(
Peyré et al., 2019
;
Yan et al., 2019
;
Zhao and Zhou, 2018
)
.
There are some works trying to introduce optimal transport into causal inference.

(
Gunsilius and Xu, 2021
)
employs unbalanced optimal transport
for matching.

(
Li et al., 2021
)
infers counterfactual outcomes via transporting the factual distribution to the counterfactual one.

(
Dunipace, 2021
;
Yan et al.,
;
Yan et al., 2024
)
learn balancing weights via optimal transport.

(
Wang et al., 2024
)
use optimal transport to address mini-batch sampling and unobserved confounders under CFR
(
Shalit et al., 2017
)
framework.
To the best of our knowledge,
this is the first work exploiting optimal transport to solve the unobserved confounder problem via data combination for long-term effect estimation.

3.
Preliminary

3.1.
Optimal Transport Weighting

Optimal Transport (OT)
(
Villani, 2021
)
is widely used to quantify distribution discrepancy as the minimum cost of transporting one distribution to another.
Among the rich theory of OT, we focus on the
Kantorovich Problem

(
Kantorovich, 2006
)
for discrete distribution in this paper.
Recently,
thanks to its many advantages,
such as geometric sensitivity and stability,
OT has been explored for learning weights to align distribution
(
Dunipace, 2021
;
Guo et al., 2022
;
Yan et al., 2024
)
.
Specifically,
suppose we want to learn weights
\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}
for samples in distribution
\alpha
,
such that the reweighted distribution is aligned with another distribution
\beta
.
To achieve this goal,
we view the to-be-learned weights
\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}
as the probability measure of distribution
\alpha
,
and represent the distribution
\beta
with a uniform probability measure
\bm{\mu}
.
By doing so,
the learning of
\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}
can be formulated as the process of minimizing the OT distance between distributions
\alpha
and
\beta
:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}}OT(\alpha,\beta)=\min_{\mathbf{w}_{\alpha}}\min_{\Gamma\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)}\langle\Gamma,C\rangle,

(1)

\displaystyle s.t.~~\Pi(\alpha,\beta)=\{\Gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times m}|\Gamma\mathbf{1}_{m}=\mathbf{w}_{\alpha},\Gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\bm{\mu},\Gamma_{ij}\in\left[0,1\right]\},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the Frobenius inner product,

n
and
m
are the sample numbers of
\alpha
and
\beta
,
the transport cost matrix
C
is the unit-wise distance between
\alpha
and
\beta
,
the transport probability matrix
\Gamma\in\Pi(\alpha,\beta)
is learned by minimizing
OT(\alpha,\beta)
,
which reflects how to transport samples from
\alpha
to
\beta
.

3.2.
Long-term HDRC Estimation

Let

A\in{\mathcal{A}}
be the 1-dimensional continuous treatment,

\mathbf{X}\in{\mathcal{X}}
be the observed confounders,

\mathbf{U}\in{\mathcal{U}}
be the unobserved confounders,

\mathbf{S}(a)\in{\mathcal{S}}
be the potential short-term outcomes measured at timesteps
1,2,...,t_{0}
,
and
Y(a)\in{\mathcal{Y}}
be the potential long-term outcome at timestep
T
.
Let lowercase letters (e.g.,
a,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u},\mathbf{s}(a),y(a)
) denote the value of the random variables.
Following the potential outcome framework
(
Rubin, 1974
)
,
the observed short- and long-term outcomes
\mathbf{S},Y
are the potential outcomes
\mathbf{S}(a),Y(a)
corresponding to the actually treatment
a
.
Besides, we use
P,\hat{P}
to represent the population and empirical distributions, respectively.

Following
(
Athey et al., 2020
;
Chen and Ritzwoller, 2023
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
)
,
we consider a data combination setting where
G=\{o,e\}
distinguishes between two types of data:
a large observational data
O=\{a_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i},y_{i},G_{i}=o\}_{i=1}^{n_{o}}

and
a small experimental data
E=\{a_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i},G_{i}=e\}_{i=n_{o}+1}^{n_{o}+n_{e}}
(
n_{e}\ll n_{o}
).
That is,
the treatment
A
, covariates
\mathbf{X}
and short-term outcomes
\mathbf{S}
are available in both datasets,
while long-term outcome
Y
is only available in observational data.
Our goal is to estimate the long-term Heterogeneous Dose-Response Curve (HDRC),
which is defined as:

(2)

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{x},a)={\mathbb{E}}[Y(a)|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}].

Notably,
the long-term HDRC cannot be estimated solely from the experimental data due to the missingness of long-term outcome
Y
,
and
also cannot be estimated solely from the observational data due to the possible unobserved confounders.
To overcome these challenges,
we learn long-term HDRC via data combination with the following assumptions:

Assumption 1 (Identification Assumptions).

A1

[Consistency]

If
A=a
, then
Y=Y(a),~\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}(a)
.

A2

[Positivity]

\forall a,\mathbf{x}
, we have

0<P(A=a|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})<1
and
0<P(G=o|A=a,\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})<1
.

A3

[Weak Internal validity of observational data]

A\perp\!\!\!\perp\{Y(a),\mathbf{S}(a)\}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{U},G=o
;
A\not\!\perp\!\!\!\perp\{Y(a),\mathbf{S}(a)\}|\mathbf{X},G=o
.

A4

[Internal validity of experimental data]

A\perp\!\!\!\perp\{Y(a),\mathbf{S}(a)\}|\mathbf{X},G=e
.

A5

[Unconfounded selection]

G\perp\!\!\!\perp\{Y(a),\mathbf{S}(a)\}|\mathbf{X}
.

A6

[Latent Unconfoundedness]

A\perp\!\!\!\perp Y(a)|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{S}(a),G=o
.

The assumptions above are mild and widely used, e.g.,
(
Athey et al., 2020
;
Ghassami et al., 2022
)
.
Assumption
A1
and
A2
are standard assumptions in causal inference.
Assumption
A3
allows unobserved confounders to exist in observational data,
which is much weaker than the classic unconfoundedness assumption.
However, this assumption also poses challenges for identification
Different from Assumption
A3
,
Assumption
A4
does not allow for the presence of unobserved confounders in experimental data.
Assumption
A5

allows us to generalize the distribution of potential outcomes between different types of data
G
,
and this assumption is reasonable because the difference between experimental and observational data is usually the treatment mechanism, while the potential outcome distributions remain unchanged and stable.
Finally,
Assumption
A6
is the key assumption in our paper,
which is first proposed by
(
Athey et al., 2020
)
to achieve long-term average effect identification under the binary-treatment setting.
Different from
(
Athey et al., 2020
)
, we reuse this assumption to identify long-term HDRC by a novel reweighting schema.
This assumption means that unobserved confounders in the observational data is mediated through the short-term outcomes,
effectively blocking the path
\mathbf{U}\rightarrow Y
.
In our vocational training example,
this assumption means that the short-term skill assessment scores fully reflect the participants’ learning aptitude.
The causal graphs that satisfy the above assumptions are shown in Fig.
1
.

Remark 1.

The plausibility of Assumption
A6
can be assessed by sensitivity analysis.

(
Kallus et al., 2019
;
Frauen et al., 2023
)
provide functional interval method under marginal sensitivity models.
Since Assumption 6,
A\perp Y(a)\mid X,S(a)
, only augments standard unconfoundedness
A\perp Y(a)\mid X
by conditioning on
S(a)
, so these methods can be naturally adapted here by treating
S
as an additional conditioning variable.

4.
Proposed Method

To estimate long-term HDRC unbiasedly,
we address unobserved confounders via reweighting and observed confounders via representation learning, as detailed in Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, respectively.

4.1.
Optimal Transport Weights for Unobserved Confounders via Data Combination

We now present our method for handling the unobserved confounders in observational data.
Firstly,
we propose a novel reweighting schema dealing with the unobserved confounders in observational data, making long-term HDRC identifiable.
Then we provide a practical and computationally OT method to learn such weights via data combination.

4.1.1.
Identifiable Long-term HDRC via Reweighting

Since
Y
is only observed in observational data,
identifying long-term HDRC requires the unconfoundedness assumption of observational data, i.e.,
A\perp\!\!\!\perp\{\mathbf{S}(a),Y(a)\}|\mathbf{X},G=o
,
which does not hold due to Assumption
A3
.
Fortunately,
A\perp\!\!\!\perp\{\mathbf{S}(a),Y(a)\}|\mathbf{X},G=e
holds,
though
Y
is missing in the experimental data.
It raises a question:
can we remove the influence of unobserved confounders in the observational data to achieve identification,
by utilizing experimental data that only includes short-term outcomes?
We find that,
although
\mathbf{U}
is unobserved, its influence can be captured by the (in)dependence between
\mathbf{S}
and
G
conditional on
\mathbf{X}
and
A
,
as illustrated in the Proposition
1
.

Proposition 0.

Suppose Assumptions
1
holds, and
consider weights
\mathbf{w}=\{\mathbf{w}_{o},\bm{\mu}\}
, where

\mathbf{w}_{o}
are learnable weights for observational units (
G=o
) and

\bm{\mu}
are uniform constant weights for experimental units (
G=e
).
Let
P^{\mathbf{w}}
denote the induced reweighted distribution,
under which we assume the following conditional independence holds:

(3)

\displaystyle P^{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{S},G\mid\mathbf{X},A)=P^{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{S}\mid\mathbf{X},A)\,P^{\mathbf{w}}(G\mid\mathbf{X},A),

i.e.,
\mathbf{S}\perp\!\!\!\perp G\mid\mathbf{X},A
.
Then we have:

\displaystyle P^{\mathbf{w}}\big(\mathbf{S}(a)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=a,G=o\big)=P^{\mathbf{w}}\big(\mathbf{S}(a)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},G=o\big).

The proof is given in Appendix
A.1
. Proposition
1
is intuitive. Since unconfoundedness holds in
G=e
,
then
P(\mathbf{S}(a)|\mathbf{X},G=e)=P(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A=a,G=e)
, but it does not hold when
G=o
.
If we can learn weight
\mathbf{w}
aligning short-term outcomes between observational data and experimental data,
i.e., making
P(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=e)=P(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=o)
,
the unconfoundedness will also hold in
G=o
based on Assumption
A5
.
Based on Proposition
1
, we further show the long-term HDRC is identifiable as follows.

Theorem 2.

Suppose Assumptions
1
holds,
and consider weights
\mathbf{w}=\{\mathbf{w}_{o},\bm{\mu}\}
such that the conditions of Proposition
1
are satisfied.
Let
P^{\mathbf{w}}
denote the corresponding reweighted distribution.
Then, for all
\mathbf{x},a
, the following weighted (mean) unconfoundedness for the long-term potential outcome holds:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{P^{\mathbf{w}}}\big[Y(a)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=a,G=o\big]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\mathbf{w}}}\big[Y(a)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},G=o\big],

and the long-term heterogeneous dose-response curve (HDRC)

\mu(\mathbf{x},a)=\mathbb{E}\big[Y(a)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\big]

is identified by

(5)

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbf{x},a)=\mathbb{E}\big[Y(a)\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{P^{\mathbf{w}}}\big[Y\mid\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=a,G=o\big].

The proof is given in Appendix
A.2
.
Theorem
2
is built on Proposition
1
, indicating that if we could learn suitable weights making
\mathbf{S}\perp\!\!\!\perp G|\mathbf{X},A
,
the influence of unobserved confounders will be removed,
and then long-term HDRC can be identified.
Therefore,
our goal now is to learn a set of weights
\mathbf{w}_{o}
for observational samples such that
\mathbf{S}\perp\!\!\!\perp G|\mathbf{X},A
holds based on the reweighted distribution,
i.e.,

P^{\mathbf{w}_{o}}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=o)=P^{\bm{\mu}}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=e)
.

4.1.2.
Learning Optimal Transport Weights

Based on the above theoretical analyses,
we need to learn weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o}
for observational samples to minimize the distances between the empirical distributions
\hat{P}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=o)
and
\hat{P}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=e)
.
However, most existing works only study how to measure the distances between joint distributions, instead of conditional distributions.
Surprisingly, we find that under the optimal transport framework, the distance between conditional distributions can be bounded by that of joint distributions.
As a result, we propose a mini-batch joint distribution-based OT method that not only helps align the conditional distributions but also can be easily embedded into the training of deep learning with theoretical guarantees.

Following Eq.(
1
),
our goal is estimating weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o}
for observational samples via minimizing the
conditional OT distance
,
i.e.,

OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X},A,G=e))
.
The intuitive approach to solve this problem is to minimize every sub-problem for every realization
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=a
as follows:

OT^{con}_{\mathbf{X},A}=\sum_{\mathbf{x},a}OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=a,G=o),\;\hat{P}(\mathbf{S}|\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x},A=a,G=e))\hat{P}(\mathbf{x},a)
.

The First Challenge.

However,
solving the above conditional OT problem is infeasible.
The reason is that the conditional set
(\mathbf{X},A)
are multi-dimensional continuous variables,
leading to the existence of infinitely sub-problems.
To overcome this challenge,
we establish a connection between the OT distance regarding conditional distributions and that regarding joint distributions:

Theorem 3.

Under mild conditions in Appendix
A.3
,
we have:

(6)

\displaystyle OT^{con}_{\mathbf{x},a}\leq OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e)).

The proof is given in Appendix
A.3
.
Theorem
3
shows that the conditional OT distance can be bounded by the OT distance between the corresponding joint distributions,
which motivates us to learn weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o}
by minimizing the upper bound
OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))
instead of the infeasible
OT^{con}_{\mathbf{x},a}
.

The Second Challenge.

While we solve the problem of conditional OT,
another challenge about intensive computation emerges.
Specifically,
solving
OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))
will cause a high computational cost since the whole optimized transport matrix
\Gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{o}\times n_{e}}
can be large with the large size of whole observational data
n_{o}
.
Additionally, optimizing the whole matrix at each iteration is also not suitable for the mini-batch training manner of deep learning.
To tackle this challenge,
inspired by
(
Yang et al., 2023
)
,
we propose a mini-batch OT based on randomly sub-sampled observational data and provide its theoretical analysis as follows:

Definition 0.

Consider two empirical distributions
\alpha
and
\beta
with
n
and
m
units,
we assume the batch size
b
of the first distribution
\alpha
satisfy
b\mid n
and let
k=n/b
be the number of batches.
Let
\mathcal{B}_{i}
be index set of the
i
-th batch in
\alpha
and the corresponding empirical distribution is
\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}
,
then the mini-batch OT problem is defined as:

(7)

\displaystyle m\mbox{-}OT(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}OT(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}_{i}},\beta).

Theorem 5.

Let
\gamma_{i}
be the optimal transport probability matrix of the
i
-th batch OT problem of
m\mbox{-}OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))
.
With extending
\gamma_{i}
to a
n_{o}\times n_{e}
matrix
\Gamma_{i}
that pads zero entries to the row whose index does not belong to
\mathcal{B}_{i}
,
we have:

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma_{i}\in\Pi(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e)),

and

\displaystyle~~~~~OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))

(9)

\displaystyle\leq m\mbox{-}OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e)).

The proof is in Appendix
A.4
.
Theorem
5
implies that the OT distance of the joint distribution
P(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A)
between observational and experimental data is upper-bounded by its corresponding
m\mbox{-}OT
problem.
Solving the
m\mbox{-}OT
problem not only significantly reduces the high optimization cost,
but also allows us to embed the OT distance into deep learning,
where only batch observational units and full experimental units are considered at each iteration.

Conclusion.

We have overcome two challenges about the conditional OT distance and high computational cost via Theorem
3
and Theorem
5
.
Combining these theorems,
we could learn the weights
\mathbf{w}_{o}
by minimizing
m\mbox{-}OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))
,
which is the upper bound of the original
OT^{con}_{\mathbf{x},a}
.
As a result,
our estimated weights
\mathbf{w}_{o}
could achieve Proposition
1
approximately,
which leads to the identification of HDRC according to Theorem
2
.

4.2.
Generalization Bound on Long-term HDCR

Although the OT-induced weights
\mathbf{w}_{o}
could remove the unobserved confounders and makes HDRC identifiable,
the counterfactual prediction error about long-term HDRC is still large.
The reason is that the regression can only be trained with the factual outcomes
while the counterfactual ones are infinite due to the continuous treatment,
and the confounding bias exists since
\mathbf{X}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp A
.
To address it,
we now derive the generalization bound about the counterfactual prediction error based on the OT-based reweighted distribution,
where the unobserved confounding bias has been removed.

We first define some notations.
Let
\phi:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{Z}}
be a representation function with inverse
\psi
,
where
{\mathcal{Z}}
is the representation space.
Let
g:{\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}
and
h:{\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{Y}}
be the hypotheses that predict the potential short- and long-term outcomes.
Since we aims to predict potential long-term outcome
Y(a)
for all possible treatments
a
on sample
\mathbf{x}
,
which requires that we could already predict the potential short-term outcomes
\mathbf{S}(a)
accurately.
As a result,
we define the combined loss for learning these two hypotheses
g,h
as
\ell_{\phi,g,h}(\mathbf{x},a)=\ell^{s}_{\phi,g}(\mathbf{x},a)+\ell^{y}_{\phi,h}(\mathbf{x},a)
,
where

\ell_{\phi,g}^{s}(\mathbf{x},a)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}L(\mathbf{s}(a),g(\phi(\mathbf{x}),a))P(\mathbf{s}(a)|\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{s}(a)

and

\ell_{\phi,h}^{y}(\mathbf{x},a)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}}L(y(a),h(\phi(\mathbf{x}),a,\mathbf{s}(a)))P(y(a),\mathbf{s}(a)|\mathbf{x})d\mathbf{s}(a)dy(a)

(
L(\cdot,\cdot)
is the error function).
The ideal prediction error over all treatment
a
for
\mathbf{x}
is defined as
{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{a\sim P(A)}[\ell_{\phi,g,h}(\mathbf{x},a)]
,
and the target of counterfactual prediction is minimizing
{\mathcal{E}}_{cf}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x}\sim P(\mathbf{X})}[{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{x})]
.

However,

{\mathcal{E}}_{cf}
is practically incomputable,
because the factual data we access lack the counterfactual outcomes.
As a result,
we can only obtain the prediction error on the reweighted factual distribution as
{\mathcal{E}}_{f}^{\mathbf{w}_{o}}={\mathbb{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a\sim P^{\mathbf{w}_{o}}(\mathbf{X},A)}[\ell_{\phi,g,h}(\mathbf{x},a)]
.
Next,
following
(
Shalit et al., 2017
;
Bellot et al., 2022
)
,
we derive a generalization bound to bridge a connection between
{\mathcal{E}}_{cf}
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{f}^{\mathbf{w}_{o}}
in the long-term HRDC estimation scenario:

Theorem 6.

Assuming a family
{\mathcal{M}}
of function
m:{\mathcal{Z}}\times{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
and there exists a constant
B_{\phi}>0
such that
\frac{1}{B_{\Phi}}\cdot\ell_{\phi,g,h}(\mathbf{x},a)\in{\mathcal{M}}
,
then we have:

(10)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{cf}\leq{\mathcal{E}}_{f}^{\mathbf{w}_{o}}+IPM_{\mathcal{M}}(P(\mathbf{Z})P(A),~\mathbf{w}_{o}\cdot P(\mathbf{Z},A)),

where
IPM_{{\mathcal{M}}}(p,q)=\sup_{m\in{\mathcal{M}}}\left|\int_{\mathcal{X}}g(x)(p(x)-q(x))dx\right|
is integral probability metric for a chosen
{\mathcal{M}}
,

P(\mathbf{Z})
and
P(\mathbf{Z},A)
are distributions induced by the map
\phi
from
P(\mathbf{X})
and
P(\mathbf{X},A)
.

The proof is given in Appendix
A.5
.
Theorem
6
states that the counterfactual error
{\mathcal{E}}_{cf}
is upper-bounded by the factual error
{\mathcal{E}}_{f}^{\mathbf{w}_{o}}
plus a reweighted IPM term that measures the dependence between treatment
A
and observed confounders
\mathbf{X}
.
The theorem inspires a classical balanced representation method
(
Shalit et al., 2017
)
,
which handles the observed confounding bias by learning the balanced representation
\mathbf{Z}=\phi(\mathbf{X})
that is independent of treatment
A
, i.e.,
\mathbf{Z}\perp\!\!\!\perp A
.

5.
Model Architecture

We have presented the theoretical results,
which handle the unobserved and observed confounders by reweighting and representation learning,
respectively.
In this section,
we summarize the model architecture inspired by the theoretical results.
As shown in Fig.
2
,
LEARN can be divided into three modules:
OT weight module,
balanced representation module
and long-term estimation module.

OT Weight Module.

Based on Theorems
3
and
5
, this module estimates weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o}
to remove unobserved confounding bias.
To embed the
m\mbox{-}OT(\alpha,\beta)
problem into deep learning,
following
(
Yang et al., 2023
)
,
we approximate it by solving
OT(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)
over each mini-batch
\mathcal{B}
.
At each iteration,
batch weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,\mathcal{B}}=\{\hat{w}_{o,1},...,\hat{w}_{o,b}\}
of observational data are learned by minimizing
OT(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{B}}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))
between the batch observational data and full experimental data.
However,
optimizing the OT problem directly usually induces a sparse solution,
leading to low data efficiency

(
Blondel et al., 2018
;
Vincent-Cuaz et al., 2022
)
.
Motivated by
(
Yan et al., 2024
)
,
we apply a negative entropy regularization on the marginal distributions
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,\mathcal{B}}
,
i.e.,

\Omega(\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,\mathcal{B}})=\sum_{j=1}^{b}\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,j}(\log\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,j}-1)
,
to encourage more observational units to be transported,
and avoid expensive linear programming
(
Cuturi, 2013
)
.
Finally,
the weights
\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,\mathcal{B}}
are learned by:

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,\mathcal{B}}}\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)}

\displaystyle\langle\gamma,C\rangle+\lambda_{e}\Omega(\gamma),

\displaystyle s.t.~\Pi(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)

\displaystyle=\{\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{b\times n_{e}}|\gamma\mathbf{1}_{n_{e}}=\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{o,\mathcal{B}},\;\gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}_{b}=\hat{\bm{\mu}},\;\gamma_{ij}\in\left[0,1\right]\}.

Here
\lambda_{e}
is a hyperparameter, the cost matrix
\mathbf{C}
is constructed as

C_{ij}=\beta_{r}d(\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{i},\bar{\mathbf{r}}_{j})+\beta_{x}d(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})+\beta_{a}d(a_{i},a_{j})
,
where
\beta_{r},\beta_{x},\beta_{a}
are hyperparameters,
d(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Euclidean distance,
i,j
denote the indices of two units.
The distance of short-term outcomes
\mathbf{s}
is computed from the GRU mean embedding in the long-term estimation module (details as below) as
\bar{\mathbf{r}}=\frac{1}{t_{0}}\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}\mathbf{r}_{t}
.
Therefore,
we need pre-train the GRU firstly to ensure that its embedding is meaningful.

Following
(
Yan et al., 2024
)
,
we develop a projected mirror descent
(
Nemirovskij and Yudin, 1983
;
Raskutti and Mukherjee, 2015
)
to solve the above problem,
which firstly performs proximal gradient descent with the Bregman divergence
(
Banerjee et al., 2005
)
,
and then obtains a feasible solution in the set
\Pi(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)
by projection.
The details can be found in Appendix
A.6
.

Balanced Representation Module.

Based on Theorem
6
,
we design this module to correct the observed confounding bias.
We transform the observed confounders
\mathbf{X}
into balanced representation
\mathbf{Z}
through MLP
\phi(\cdot)
via minimizing
IPM(P(\mathbf{Z})P(A),\mathbf{w}_{o,\mathcal{B}}P(\mathbf{Z},A))
.
In practice,
we use the Wasserstein distance
(
Villani and others, 2009
)
as the implementation of the IPM.
To calculate it,
we simulate samples standing for the product of marginal distributions
P(\mathbf{Z})P(A)
by randomly permuting the observed treatment
A
,
and the original samples are drawn from the joint distribution
P(\mathbf{Z},A)
.

.

Long-Term Estimation Module.
This module aims to estimate the potential long-term outcome
Y(a)
based on potential short-term outcomes
\mathbf{S}(a)
for different units.
We follow LTEE
(
Cheng et al., 2021
)
to model the relationship between the short- and long-term outcomes.
Specifically,
the balanced representations
\mathbf{Z}
are firstly fed into the GRU
q(\cdot)
to obtain the short-term representations
\mathbf{R}_{t}
at each timestep
t
,
which is then used to predict the corresponding short-term outcome
\hat{S}_{t}
with a shared MLP
g(\cdot)
.
After that,
we leverage the attention mechanism
f(\cdot)

(
Bahdanau et al., 2014
)
to construct the long-term representation
\mathbf{R}_{T}
from
\{\mathbf{R}_{1},\mathbf{R}_{2},...,\mathbf{R}_{t_{0}}\}
as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{t}=\tanh{(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{R}_{t}+\mathbf{b})},~~~\alpha_{t}=\frac{\exp{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{V}}}{\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}\exp{\tilde{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{\top}\mathbf{V}}},~~~\mathbf{R}_{T}=\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}\alpha_{t}\mathbf{R}_{t},

where
\mathbf{W},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{V}
are the learnable parameters.
Then base on
\mathbf{R}_{T}
,
the long-term outcome
\hat{Y}_{T}
is through an MLP
h(\cdot)
.

However, different from the original LTEE which only works with binary treatment,
we employ the varying coefficient structure
(
Nie et al., 2021
)
to extend it to continuous treatment.
In particular,
we use splines to model the parameters of the above four network structures
q(\cdot),g(\cdot),f(\cdot),h(\cdot)
.
As a result,
the influence of the treatment is not lost in high-dimensional representations, which in practice has been shown to lead to better performance.

Loss Function.
The total training loss at each iteration is:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\hat{w}_{o,i}(y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i})^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{o}}{b}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}\hat{w}_{o,i}(s_{i,t}-\hat{s}_{i,t})^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1-\lambda_{o}}{n_{e}}\sum_{i=n_{o}+1}^{n_{o}+n_{e}}\sum_{t=1}^{t_{0}}(s_{i,t}-\hat{s}_{i,t})^{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{b}IPM(\{\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\tilde{a}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b},\{\hat{w}_{o,i}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\hat{w}_{o,i}a_{i}\}_{i=0}^{b}),

where
b
is the batch size of observational data,
\lambda_{b}
is the strength of IPM, and
\tilde{a}_{i}
is the samples obtained by randomly permuting original treatment samples
a_{i}
.
To make full use of the datasets,
besides the observational data,
we also train the model based on the prediction error of the short-term outcomes on experimental data,
and use
\lambda_{o}
to control its strength.

In this section,

.

: How does LEARN perform in long-term HDRC estimation compared to existing methods?

6.1.
Datasets

Since the true long-term HDRC is unavailable for real-world datasets,
following prior works
(
Nie et al., 2021
;
Wang et al., 2022
;
Kazemi and Ester, 2024
)
,
we use five synthetic datasets with varying levels of unobserved confounding bias controlled by
\beta_{U}\in\{1,1.5,2,2.5,3\}
,
and two semi-synthetic datasets,
News
(
Schwab et al., 2020
)
and TCGA
(
Weinstein et al., 2013
)
,
to evaluate our model.

Synthetic Data Generation.

Following Fig.
1
,
we first sample
n_{o}
observational units as

X^{o}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0.1}_{p},\mathbf{I}_{p}),U\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0.25}_{q},\mathbf{I}_{q})
,
and
n_{e}
experimental units as

X^{e}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0.2}_{p},\mathbf{I}_{p}),U\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0.25}_{q},\mathbf{I}_{q})
.
With
W_{j}^{x}\sim\mathcal{U}(0,0.5),W_{j}^{u}\sim\mathcal{U}(0.2,0.5)
.
we then generate treatment as
A=\sigma\left(\sum_{j=1}^{p/3}W_{j}^{x}\sin{(X_{j})}+\sum_{j=p/3}^{2p/3}W_{j}^{x}X_{j}^{2}+\beta_{U}\bar{U}\mathbb{I}(G=o)\right)
,
generate short-term outcomes (
t=0,\ldots,7
) as
S_{t}=\sum_{j=p/3}^{2p/3}W_{j}^{x}X_{j}^{2}+(t+5)A(\sum_{j=2p/3}^{p}W_{j}^{x}X_{j})+\beta_{U}tA\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}W_{j}^{u}\cos{(U_{j})}\right)+0.5\bar{S}_{1:t-1}+\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)
,
generate long-term outcome
Y=0.25\sum_{j=1}^{p}W_{j}^{x}X_{j}^{2}+\frac{A}{7}\sum_{t=1}^{7}e^{1/t}S_{t}+\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)
.
We set
n_{o}=10000
,
n_{e}=500
,
p=15
, and
q=5
.
We construct 5 datasets with
\beta_{U}\in\{1,1.5,2,2.5,3\}
to control the strength of unobserved confounding.

Semi-synthetic Data Generation.

We use News
(
Schwab et al., 2020
)
and TCGA
(
Weinstein et al., 2013
)
, containing 5,000 NY Times articles and gene-expression data from 9,659 cancer patients, respectively.
For each dataset, we reuse the original covariates, split samples into observational and experimental subsets at 9:1, and split covariates into observed
X
and unobserved
U
at 8:2.
Following
(
Nie et al., 2021
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
, let
\bm{v}_{i,x}=\bm{u}_{i,x}/\|\bm{u}_{i,x}\|
and
\bm{v}_{i,u}=\bm{u}_{i,u}/\|\bm{u}_{i,u}\|
, where
\bm{u}_{i,x},\bm{u}_{i,u}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{1})
for
i=1,2,3
.
We sample
A\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\gamma,\beta)
with
\gamma=2
,
\beta=(1)/d^{*}
, and
d^{*}=\left|\frac{\bm{v}_{3,x}^{T}X}{2\bm{v}_{2,x}^{T}X}+\frac{\bm{v}_{3,u}^{T}U}{2\bm{v}_{2,u}^{T}U}\mathbb{I}(G=o)\right|
.
For
t=0,\ldots,7
,
we first generate

S_{t}^{\prime}=\max\{-2,\min[2,\exp(\frac{\bm{v}_{2,x}^{T}X}{\bm{v}_{3,x}^{T}X}+\frac{\bm{v}_{2,u}^{T}U}{\bm{v}_{3,u}^{T}U}-0.3)]\}+\alpha t(\bm{v}_{1,x}^{T}X+\bm{v}_{1,u}^{T}U)
,

S_{t}=8(A-0.5)^{2}\sin(\frac{\pi}{2}A)S_{t}^{\prime}+0.5\bar{S}_{1:t-1}+\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)
, and
Y=\lambda(\bm{v}_{1,x}^{T}X+\bm{v}_{2,x}^{T}X)+\frac{A}{7}\sum_{t=1}^{7}e^{1/t}S_{t}+\mathcal{N}(0,0.5)
.
For News, we select
\alpha=1,\lambda=5
, and for TCGA, we select
\alpha=20,\lambda=50
.

Result and Analysis

Overall Performance (RQ1).

Table
1
presents the results on synthetic and semi-synthetic datasets.
First,
LEARN achieves the highest accuracy across all datasets, outperforming baselines only designed for continuous treatments.
This gains mainly come from the long-term estimation module to model the relationship between short- and long-term outcomes,
which also benefit LTEE(VCNet).
Second,
LEARN(IPM) outperforms LEARN(None), indicating that IPM-based balanced representations reduce observed confounding bias, thereby lowering MISE.
Finally,
LEARN further surpasses LEARN(IPM), verifying the benefit of correcting unobserved confounding with the OT weight module.

Confounding Bias Correction (RQ2).

We assess whether the proposed modules reduce both observed and unobserved confounders on the synthetic dataset (
\beta_{U}=1
).
For observed confounders, we assess the balanced representation module using the Hilbert-Schmidt Independence Criterion (HSIC,
(
Gretton et al., 2005
)
).
The dependence between
\phi(\mathbf{X})
and
A
is reduced by
83.45%
.
For unobserved confounders, we evaluate the OT weight module using the Hilbert–Schmidt Conditional Independence Criterion (HSCONIC,
(
Fukumizu et al., 2007
;
Sun et al., 2007
)
) to assess the post-reweighting unconfoundedness condition in observational data,
i.e.,
A\perp\!\!\!\perp\{Y(a),\mathbf{S}(a)\}\mid\mathbf{X},G=o
.
Reweighting reduces the conditional dependence between
A
and
Y(a)
,
\mathbf{S}(a)
given
\mathbf{X}
by
67.71%
and
71.55%
, respectively.

Sensitivity of Batch Size (RQ3).

Following
(
Sommerfeld et al., 2019
)
, we analyze the sensitivity of batch size in the
m
-OT problem by computing the relative approximation error:

\frac{|m\mbox{-}OT_{B}(\alpha,\beta)-OT^{*}(\alpha,\beta)|}{OT^{*}(\alpha,\beta)}
,
where
OT^{*}(\alpha,\beta)=\langle\Gamma^{*},C\rangle
is the optimal transport cost optimized using full observational data,
and
m\mbox{-}OT_{B}(\alpha,\beta)
is the approximate cost with batch size
B
.
Table
2
reports the results on synthetic dataset (
\beta_{U}=1
),
which shows that smaller batches lead to higher relative approximation error, reflecting a trade-off between computational efficiency and accuracy about
m\mbox{-}OT
problem.
However, even with the smallest batch size of 512, the relative error 14.51% is acceptable.

Impact of Sample Size on experimental data (RQ4).

Since LEARN relies on data combination,
we evaluate the impact of the experimental sample size using the synthetic dataset (
\beta_{U}=1
).
Table
3
shows that MISE decreases with larger sample sizes,
as a larger experimental data provides a more reliable distribution for aligning the observational data,
which allows us to learn more effective weights for eliminating unobserved confounding bias.

Hyperparameter Sensitivity Study (RQ5).

We study the sensitivity to
\lambda_{b},\lambda_{o},\lambda_{e}
in Fig.
3
.
Firstly,
we observe that MISE increases when
\lambda_{b}
becomes small or large,
showing a trade-off between representation balancing and outcome estimation.
Secondly,
the results of varying
\lambda_{o}
shows that jointly using short-term outcomes from observational and experimental data improves performance.
Finally,
the negative entropy regularization in OT is beneficial,
but large
\lambda_{e}
will push the learned weights close to the uniform distribution, resulting in a failure of alignment.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper,
we provide a practical solution to estimate the long-term HDRC with unobserved confounders and continuous treatment via data combination,
which is not well-studied in existing work.
Specifically,
to remove unobserved confounders and make long-term HDRC identifiable,
we first propose a novel weighting schema aligning the conditional distribution of short-term outcomes between observational and experimental groups.
We solve the reweighting problem under an optimal transport framework,
showing that the conditional distribution discrepancy can be bounded by mini-batch joint distribution discrepancy,
which is computationally efficient.
Further, to handle the observed confounders,
we derive a generalization bound on the counterfactual outcome regression error
based on the OT-induced reweighted distribution.
Building upon the above theoretical results,
we develop our model LEARN to estimate long-term HDRC accurately.
Extensive experimental results verify the correctness of our theory and the effectiveness of LEARN.

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma_{i})^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n_{o}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\Gamma_{i}^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n_{o}})=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\beta=\beta,

Combining Eq. (
A.23
) with conditions
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cap\mathcal{B}_{j}=\emptyset
and
\cup_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{B}_{i}=\{1,2,...,n_{o}\}
,
we obtain that:

\displaystyle(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i})^{j}=k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{I}(j\in\mathcal{B}_{i})=k,

which means that the term
\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}

\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\eta_{i}=\mathbf{1}_{n_{o}}
.
As a result,
we have already proved Eq. (

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma_{i}\right)\mathbf{1}_{n_{e}}=\alpha,~~~\left(\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma_{i}\right)^{T}\mathbf{1}_{n_{o}}=\beta.

By denoting the cost matrix between

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\;\;OT(\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}OT(\hat{P}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=o),\hat{P}(\mathbf{S},\mathbf{X},A|G=e))

A.6.
Derivation about Projected Mirror Descent

In this section,
following
(
Yan et al., 2024
)
,
we develop a projected mirror descent
(
Nemirovskij and Yudin, 1983
;
Raskutti and Mukherjee, 2015
)
based on the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to solve the following problem,
which is non-trivial to address because of the equality constrints:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{w}_{o,\mathcal{B}}}\min_{\gamma\in\Pi(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)}\langle\gamma,\mathbf{C}\rangle+\lambda_{e}\Omega(\gamma),

\displaystyle s.t.~

\displaystyle\Pi(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)=\{\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{b\times n_{e}}|\gamma\mathbf{1}_{n_{e}}=\mathbf{w}_{o,\mathcal{B}},\gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}_{b}=\bm{\mu},\gamma_{ij}\in\left[0,1\right]\},

Firstly,
we rewrite the constrain about
\gamma
as

(A.29)

\displaystyle\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)=\{\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}^{b\times n_{e}}|\gamma^{T}\mathbf{1}_{b}=\bm{\mu},\gamma_{ij}\in\left[0,1\right]\},

which does not consider the constraint
\gamma\mathbf{1}_{n_{e}}=\mathbf{w}_{o,\mathcal{B}}

since
\mathbf{w}_{o,\mathcal{B}}
are also parameters to be optimized.
Based on this,
the OT problem with a negative entropy regularization is given as follows:

(A.30)

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma}\langle\gamma,\mathbf{C}\rangle+\lambda_{e}\Omega(\gamma),~~~s.t.~\gamma\in\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta).

For simplicity,
we define the objective function in Problem (
A.30
) as

f(\gamma)=\langle\gamma,\mathbf{C}\rangle+\lambda_{e}\Omega(\gamma)
,
and the
(i,j)
-th element of the gradient
\nabla f(\gamma)
is denoted by
\nabla_{ij}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{ij}=C_{ij}+\lambda_{e}\log w_{i}=C_{ij}+\lambda_{e}\log\gamma_{i\cdot},

where
\gamma_{i\cdot}
is the sum of
i
-th row of
\gamma
.
Then at each iteration,
we solve the following problem:

(A.31)

\displaystyle\gamma^{k}=\min_{\gamma\in\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)}\eta\langle\nabla f(\gamma^{k-1}),\gamma\rangle+\mathcal{D}(\gamma||\gamma^{k-1}),

which firstly performs proximal gradient descent with the Bregman divergence and the stepsize
\eta
,
and then obtains a feasible solution in the set
\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)
by projection.
Next, we present the details of these two operations.

Proximal Gradient Descent

Let
\upsilon^{k}
be the solution to Problem (
A.31
) without considering the constraint
\gamma\in\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)
,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\upsilon^{k}=\min_{\gamma}~~\eta\langle\nabla f(\gamma^{k-1}),\gamma\rangle+\mathcal{D}(\gamma||\gamma^{k-1}).

We adopt the KL divergence between two distributions
\gamma
and
\gamma^{k-1}

as the Bregman divergence
\mathcal{D}(\gamma||\gamma^{k})
,
then the closed-form solution to the above problem is given as:

(A.32)

\displaystyle\upsilon^{k}=\gamma^{k-1}\odot\exp(-\eta\nabla f(\gamma^{k-1})).

Projection Operation

To make sure
\gamma^{k}
satisfies the constraints in Eq. (
A.29
),
we update
\gamma^{k}
by finding
\gamma\in\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\alpha_{\mathcal{B}},\beta)
which is most close to
\upsilon^{k}

under the KL metric.
This is achieved by solving the projection problem (ignore constraint
\gamma_{ij}\in\left[0,1\right]
for now):

\displaystyle\min_{\gamma}\quad\mathcal{D}(\gamma||\upsilon^{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{e}}\gamma_{ij}\log(\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{\upsilon^{k}_{ij}})-\gamma_{ij}+\upsilon^{k}_{ij},

(A.33)

\displaystyle s.t.~~\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{b}=\bm{\mu}.

By introducing the Lagrangian multipliers
\bm{\lambda}=[\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n_{t}}]^{\top}
for the constraint
\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{b}=\bm{\mu}
,
we obtain the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}(\gamma,\bm{\lambda})
as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\gamma,\bm{\lambda})=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{e}}\gamma_{ij}\log(\frac{\gamma_{ij}}{\upsilon^{k}_{ij}})-\gamma_{ij}+\upsilon^{k}_{ij}+\bm{\lambda}^{\top}(\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{b}-\bm{\mu}).

By taking the partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}(\gamma,\bm{\lambda})
with respect to
\gamma_{ij}
to zero,
we obtain:

(A.34)

\displaystyle\log\gamma_{ij}=\log\upsilon^{k}_{ij}-\lambda_{j}\Rightarrow\gamma_{ij}=\upsilon^{k}_{ij}\exp(-\lambda_{j}).

According to the equality constraint
\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{b}=\bm{\mu}
,
we have
\sum_{i=1}^{b}\gamma_{ij}=1/n_{e}
.
Combining it with the above result,
we further obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{b}\gamma_{ij}=\sum_{i=1}^{b}\upsilon^{k}_{ij}\exp(-\lambda_{j})

\displaystyle=\exp(-\lambda_{j})\sum_{i=1}^{b}\upsilon^{k}_{ij}=\frac{1}{n_{e}}

(A.35)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\exp(-\lambda_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{e}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\upsilon^{k}_{ij}}.

Combining Eq. (
A.34
) and (
A.35
),
we obtain the solution:

(A.36)

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}=\upsilon^{k}_{ij}\Big/\Big(n_{e}\sum_{i=1}^{b}\upsilon^{k}_{ij}\Big).

For an initial value
\gamma_{ij}^{0}\geq 0
,
given
\upsilon^{k}_{ij}>0
which is guaranteed by the update rule in Eq. (
A.29
),
it is obvious that the solution obtained by Eq. (
A.36
)
satisfies the box constraint

\gamma_{ij}\in[0,1]
.
Therefore,
Problem (
A.33
) does not consider this constraint explicitly.

For now,
we have derived how to solve the Problem Eq. (
A.30
).
That is,
we repeat steps Eq. (
A.32
) and (
A.36
) until converging.
After that,
the weights of observational samples could be obtained as
w_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{e}}\gamma^{*}_{ij}
,
where
w_{i}
is the sum of the
i
-th row of
\gamma^{*}
.
