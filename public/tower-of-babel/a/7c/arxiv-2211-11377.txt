Lifespan estimates for the compressible Euler equations with damping via Orlicz spaces techniques
Ning-An Lai, Nico Michele Schiavone

Abstract
In this paper we are interested in the upper bound of the lifespan estimate for the compressible Euler system with time dependent damping and small initial perturbations. We employ some techniques from the blow-up study of nonlinear wave equations. The novelty consists in the introduction of tools from the Orlicz spaces theory to handle the nonlinear term emerging from the pressure p ≡ p ⁡ ( ρ ) p\equiv p(\rho) , which admits different asymptotic behavior for large and small values of ρ − 1 \rho-1 , being ρ \rho the density. Hence we can establish, in high dimensions n ∈ { 2 , 3 } n\in\{2,3\} , unified upper bounds of the lifespan estimate depending only on the dimension n n and on the damping strength, and independent of the adiabatic index γ > 1 \gamma>1 . We conjecture our results to be optimal. The method employed here not only improves the known upper bounds of the lifespan for n ∈ { 2 , 3 } n\in\{2,3\} , but has potential application in the study of related problems.

Introduction

In this work, we will consider the compressible isentropic Euler equations with time dependent damping

\displaystyle\rho_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho u)=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.1a)

\displaystyle(\rho u)_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u)+\nabla p+\frac{\mu\,\rho u}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}=0,\quad(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1.1b)

\displaystyle u(0,x)=\varepsilon u_{0}(x),\quad\rho(0,x)=\overline{\rho}+\varepsilon\rho_{0}(x),

(1.1c)

where
\rho\colon[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
u\colon[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\colon[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
stand for the density, velocity and pressure of the fluid respectively,
n\in\{1,2,3\}
is the spatial dimension, while
\tfrac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}
is a time dependent frictional coefficient, with
\mu\geq 0
and
\lambda\geq 1
.
The initial values are a small perturbation of constant states (with
\overline{\rho}>0
), where the “smallness” is quantified by the parameter
\varepsilon>0
, and the perturbations
\rho_{0},u_{0}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfy

\text{supp}\,(\rho_{0}-\overline{\rho}),\,\text{supp}\,u_{0}\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x|\leq R\}

(1.2)

for some positive constant
R
. We assume that the pressure satisfies the state equation for the gas

p\equiv p(\rho)=A\rho^{\gamma},

where
A>0
is a constant and
\gamma>1
is the adiabatic index.
However, it is not restrictive in the following to assume
R=\overline{\rho}=1
and
A=\frac{1}{\gamma}
.
Our study concerns the estimates for the
lifespan

T_{\varepsilon}
of the solution, defined as the largest time such that the solution exists and is
C^{1}
on
[0,T_{\varepsilon})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

1.1
The undamped case

Setting
\mu=0
, the system (
1.1
) reduces to the classic Euler equations, a fundamental system in fluid dynamics consisting in a set of quasilinear hyperbolic equations governing the adiabatic and inviscid flow for an ideal fluid. Generally speaking, the compressible Euler equations develop shock waves in finite time for general initial data (see
[
40
]
and references therein).

The study of formation of singularity for compressible Euler equations was initiated by Sideris in
[
36
]
, where several blow-up results were established for the
3
-dimensional Euler equations modeling a polytropic, ideal fluid with both large data and small initial perturbations.
In the subsequent works
[
37
,
38
]
by the same author, the following lifespan estimate

\exp\left(C_{1}\varepsilon^{-1}\right)\leq T_{\varepsilon}\leq\exp\left(C_{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\right)

(1.3)

was established in
3D
, where the lower bound was obtained under the assumption that the initial velocity is irrotational and the upper bound holds for
\gamma=2
. In particular, Sideris extended the generic lower bound

T_{\varepsilon}\geq C\varepsilon^{-1}

(1.4)

which typically holds for symmetric hyperbolic systems in any number of space dimensions (see
[
18
]
and
[
30
]
for the general theory).
Here and in the following, we will use
C,C_{1},C_{2}
to denote some generic positive constants independent of
\varepsilon
, the value of which may change in different places.

Rammaha proved in
[
34
]
the formation of singularity in finite time for the
2D
case, and furthermore, for
\gamma=2
, obtained the following upper bound of the lifespan for small perturbations:

T_{\varepsilon}\leq C\varepsilon^{-2}.

Later, Alinhac showed in
[
2
]
that the lifespan for rotationnally invariant data in
2D
satisfies

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\varepsilon^{2}T_{\varepsilon}=C.

The compressible Euler equations in two space dimensions is reconsidered by Sideris in
[
39
]
, where different lower bounds for the lifespan are established under different assumptions on the initial data. In
[
25
]
, Lai, Xiang and Zhou studied the generalized Riemann problem governed by compressible isothermal (
p=\rho
) Euler equations in
2D
and
3D
, establishing blow-up results for the fan-shaped wave structure solution. Recently, Jin and Zhou in
[
15
]
derived, for
\gamma=2
, the following upper bound for the lifespan estimate:

T_{\varepsilon}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &C\varepsilon^{-1}&&\text{if $n=1$,}\\
&C\varepsilon^{-2}&&\text{if $n=2$,}\\
&\exp(C\varepsilon^{-1})&&\text{if $n=3$,}\end{aligned}\right.

(1.5)

which in particular improves the upper bound in (
1.3
), and shows the optimality of the lifespan estimate in
3
-dimensions when
\gamma=2
. To be precise, in their work only the
3D
case is explicated, but their method and Lemma 2.1 therein work also in lower dimensions.

On the other side, putting together (
1.4
), which holds from the general theory, and the results in
[
2
,
39
]
and in
[
37
]
, we find that the lower bounds corresponding to (
1.5
) hold for any
\gamma>1
, namely

T_{\varepsilon}\geq\left\{\begin{aligned} &C\varepsilon^{-1}&&\text{if $n=1$,}\\
&C\varepsilon^{-2}&&\text{if $n=2$,}\\
&\exp(C\varepsilon^{-1})&&\text{if $n=3$.}\end{aligned}\right.

In particular, we can see that the lifespan estimates in (
1.5
) are optimal at least for
\gamma=2
in
1D
and
3D
, and for any
\gamma>1
if
n=2
(due to
[
2
]
). Actually, we anticipate that they are optimal for any
\gamma>1
also in the
1D
case thanks to the results in
[
41
]
, but we will properly discuss them in the next subsection.

1.2
The damped case

Let us turn our attention to the problem in the presence of a time dependent damping term.
Wang and Yang in
[
45
]
proved global existence in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the system (
1.1
) with a positive constant damping (i.e.
\mu>0
,
\lambda=0
) and small initial perturbation of some constant state. Sideris, Thomases and Wang
[
40
]
restudied the same case in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, showing that the damping term can prevent the development of singularities in finite time if the initial perturbations are small, but on the contrary it is not strong enough to prevent the blow-up for large initial data, even if they are smooth.

Hou and Yin in
[
12
]
, and Hou, Witt and Yin in
[
11
]
studied the system (
1.1
) in
2D
and
3D
, proving: global existence for
0\leq\lambda<1
,
\mu>0
and for
\lambda=1
,
\mu>3-n
; finite time blow-up for
\lambda>1
,
\mu>0
and for
\lambda=1
,
0<\mu\leq 1
,
n=2
. Also, they established the following upper bound for the lifespan estimate:

T_{\varepsilon}\leq\exp\left(C\varepsilon^{-2}\right)

(1.6)

for
\gamma=2
(see the details in
[
12
, p. 2511]
for
n=2
and in
[
11
, p. 416]
for
n=3
). In
[
32
,
33
]
, Pan studied the corresponding problem in
1
-dimension, showing that: if
0\leq\lambda<1
,
\mu>0
or if
\lambda=1
,
\mu>2
, there exists a global solution; if
\lambda=1
,
0\leq\mu\leq 2
or if
\lambda>1
,
\mu\geq 0
, the
C^{1}
-solutions will blow up in finite time. Moreover, in the latter case, the same lifespan estimate as (
1.6
) was established in
[
32
]
for
\gamma=2
.
From these results, one infers that the point
(\lambda,\mu)=(1,n-3)
is critical for the problem.
Notice that both the blow-up results in
[
32
,
33
]
and
[
12
,
11
]
are established exploiting the method developed by Sideris in
[
36
]
: a
1D
semilinear-type wave equation is constructed for some average quantity related to the density, and then the d’Alembert’s formula is used to establish an ordinary differential inequality.

In the paper
[
41
]
, Sugiyama studied the system (
1.1
) in
1D
, in its equivalent form obtained by changing the Eulerian coordinates into Lagrangian ones. Explicitly, in
[
41
]
it is considered the so-called
p
-system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{t}-u_{x}=0,\\
&u_{t}+p_{x}+a(t,x)v=0,\\
&u(0,x)=\varepsilon u_{0}(x),\quad v(0,x)=1+\varepsilon v_{0}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(1.7)

where
p\equiv p(v)=\tfrac{v^{-\gamma}}{\gamma}
and
v=1/\rho
is the specific volume.
For the space-independent damping
a(t,x)=\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}
, making use of Riemann invariants, the author was able to establish the estimates

T_{\varepsilon}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &C\varepsilon^{-1}&&\text{for $\lambda>1$ and $\mu\geq 0$,}\\
&C\varepsilon^{-\tfrac{2}{2-\mu}}&&\text{for $\lambda=1$ and $0\leq\mu<2$,}\\
&\exp(C\varepsilon^{-1})&&\text{for $\lambda=1$ and $\mu=2$,}\end{aligned}\right.

(1.8)

not only from above, but also from below, namely obtaining the sharp lifespan estimates for small
\varepsilon
, provided that
\lim_{x\to-\infty}(u_{0}(x),v_{0}(x))=(u_{-},v_{-})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For the study of problem (
1.7
), see also
[
4
]
and the references therein.

At the best of our knowledge, except for the above nice results in
1D
by Sugiyama, the lifespan estimate in
2D
and
3D
for (
1.1
) seems to be still unclear, especially when
\gamma\neq 2
, even in the undamped case for
n=3
.
The method in
[
32
,
33
,
12
,
11
]
, successfully proves the blow-up of the solutions, but provides, as upper bound of the lifespan, the estimate (
1.6
) for
\gamma\geq 2
, which unfortunately seems to be not the optimal one: indeed, it is reasonable to think that this should be closely related to the dimension
n
and to the damping strength.

Furthermore, in the literature cited until now often only the case
\gamma=2
is explicitly considered. The modifications necessary to generalize the proof for any
\gamma>1
are then indicated, pointing to the fact that the lifespan estimates may be modified, but it is not clear how. This happens in the works
[
36
,
15
]
for
n=3
and
[
34
]
for
n=2
in the undamped case, and similarly in the works
[
12
]
and
[
32
]
for the case with the time dependent damping term.

Nevertheless, the lower bounds of the lifespan for (
1.1
) are independent of
\gamma
in the undamped case, and they are optimal for
n\in\{1,2\}
and any
\gamma>1
, as we observed at the end of the previous subsection.
Thus, it is natural to believe that this phenomena should be true also for the bounds of the lifespan in the damped case, as actually verified in
[
41
]
for
n=1

2.3
.

2
Aims and results

In this paper, we are going to employ an argument based on the manipulation of suitable multipliers to absorb the damping term, and a variation of a lemma on differential inequality established in Li and Zhou
[
26
]
.
Similar techniques were applied in the context of the blow-up study for semilinear damped wave equations with small initial data — as we will show, the density satisfies this kind of equation (see Section
3
).
About the damped wave equation, there exist a huge and extensive literatures. Since the case treated in this work can be correspondent to the scattering case (when
\lambda>1
) and to the scale-invariant case (when
\lambda=1
) of the damped wave equation according to the Wirth’s classification (see
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
), we cite only the works
[
28
,
22
,
43
]
and

[
6
,
8
,
7
,
31
,
16
,
44
,
17
,
14
,
24
,
13
,
42
,
27
]
for the two cases respectively, but we refer the reader to the Introduction of
[
21
]
and references therein for a comprehensive presentation.

The novelty in our work consists in mixing these techniques, which manage the linear part of the equation, with tools from the Orlicz spaces theory, which seems to be a suitable setting to deal with the non-power-like nonlinearity appearing in (
3.9
) below in the case of
\gamma\neq 2
. In this way, we are able to derive upper bounds of the lifespan not only merely in the case
\gamma=2
, improving the existing ones, but also in the general case
\gamma\neq 2
, obtaining the
papabili
optimal results (see Remark
2.2
).
To the best of our knowledge, the use of Orlicz space theory in the context of blow-up and lifespan estimates for nonlinear wave equations seems to be new, and we are confident they can be potentially applied to other problems (see Remark
2.5
).

Let us present now our main results.

Theorem 1
.

Let
n\in\{1,2,3\}
and
\gamma>1
. Suppose
\lambda>1
and
\mu\geq 0
.
Assume that the initial data satisfy (
1.2
) and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho_{0}\phi\text{\,d}x>0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u_{0}\cdot\nabla\phi\text{\,d}x>0,

(2.1)

where
\phi
is defined in (
5.1
) below.
Then the system (
1.1
) has no global solutions, and the lifespan estimate satisfies

T_{\varepsilon}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &C\varepsilon^{-\mbox{\large$\frac{2}{3-n}$}}&&\text{if $n\in\{1,2\}$,}\\
&\exp\left(C\varepsilon^{-1}\right)&&\text{if $n=3$,}\end{aligned}\right.

provided
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for some positive constant
\varepsilon_{0}\equiv\varepsilon_{0}(n,\lambda,\mu,\gamma,u_{0},\rho_{0})
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
n\in\{1,2\}
and
\gamma>1
. Suppose
\lambda=1
and
0\leq\mu\leq 3-n
.
Assume that the initial data satisfy (
1.2
) and (
2.1
).
Then the system (
1.1
) has no global solutions, and the lifespan estimate satisfies

T_{\varepsilon}\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &C\varepsilon^{-\mbox{\large$\frac{2}{3-n-\mu}$}}&&\text{if $\mu<3-n$,}\\
&\exp\left(C\varepsilon^{-1}\right)&&\text{if $\mu=3-n$,}\end{aligned}\right.

(2.2)

provided
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
for some positive constant
\varepsilon_{0}\equiv\varepsilon_{0}(n,\mu,\gamma,u_{0},\rho_{0})
.

Remark 2.1

(
About optimality in the undamped case
)
.

The results in Theorems
1
and
2
hold also for the compressible Euler system without damping, setting
\mu=0
. Together with the lower bound in (
1.3
) by Sideris, and recalling the discussion at the end of Subsection
1.1
, we completely close the problem of the optimality of the lifespan estimates in the undamped case for any
\gamma>1
.

Remark 2.2

(
About optimality in the damped case
)
.

Compared to the result (
1.6
) obtained in
[
11
,
12
,
32
]
, we improve the lifespan estimates for
n=2
and
n=3
, whereas for
n=1
we re-demonstrate the upper bound in (
1.8
) with a completely different approach.
Since when
\lambda=1
it is the size of the positive constant
\mu
that determines whether there is global solution or blow-up in finite time, it is reasonable to believe that the upper bound of the lifespan depends also on
\mu
, as the results in
1D
(
1.8
) obtained in
[
41
]
by Sugiyama.
And precisely in view of these results, it is natural to conjecture that the lifespan estimates in Theorem
1
and
2
should be indeed optimal, because we already know they are for
n=1
.

Remark 2.3

(
Independence of
\gamma
)
.

In light of Remark
2.1
, we can provide a negative answer to the statements at the end of
[
36
]
and
[
34
]
: in the undamped case, the lifespan estimates are actually independent of
\gamma
, or better, for any
\gamma>1
they coincide with the ones in the case
\gamma=2
. This should be true also in the damped case, on the basis of Remark
2.2
.

Remark 2.4

(
Relations with the Glassey’s conjecture
)
.

As we anticipated before, we will reduce ourselves to the study of the damped wave equation (
3.9
), and hence, roughly speaking, to the inequality

\widetilde{\rho}_{tt}-\Delta\widetilde{\rho}+\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}\widetilde{\rho}_{t}\geq\Delta R(\widetilde{\rho})

(2.3)

where
\lim_{p\to 0}\frac{R(p)}{|p|^{2}}=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}
and
\lim_{p\to+\infty}\frac{R(p)}{p^{\gamma}}=\frac{1}{\gamma}
. But we can be more precise.
Indeed, it is interesting to observe that our problem seems to be related to the Glassey’s conjecture for the wave equation with derivative-type non-linearity, which affirms that the critical exponent for the equation

u_{tt}-\Delta u+\frac{\mu}{1+t}u_{t}=|u_{t}|^{p}

with small initial data is given by the Glassey exponent

p_{G}(n):=1+\frac{2}{n-1}.

Let us consider the corresponding problem with damping term, namely

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u_{tt}-\Delta u+\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}u_{t}=|u_{t}|^{p},\\
&u(0,x)=\varepsilon u_{0}(x),\quad u_{t}(0,x)=\varepsilon u_{1}(x),\end{aligned}\right.

(2.4)

for which the possibly optimal blow-up results in the case
\lambda>1
and
\lambda=1
are given in
[
23
]
and
[
10
]
respectively (in the latter reference a more general combined nonlinearity is considered, see also
[
29
,
20
,
5
,
9
]
for related problems). For the model with
\lambda=1
the critical exponent seems to be
p_{G}(n+\mu)
, in the sense that we have blow-up for any
1<p\leq p_{G}(n+\mu)
and global existence for
p>p_{G}(n+\mu)
. But if we set
p=2
, this requirement is equivalent to
\mu\leq 3-n
, which is exactly the condition on
\mu
in Theorem
2
.
Similarly, if
\lambda>1
the critical exponent should be
p_{G}(n)
, experiencing blow-up for
1<p\leq p_{G}(n)
. Setting
p=2
this condition reduce to
n\in\{1,2,3\}
.
This analogy holds true also for the lifespan estimates: the corresponding ones proved in
[
23
]
and
[
10
]
, setting
p=2
, match with the ones provided in Theorem
1
and
2
.

In other words, our problem (
1.1
) seems to share the same blow-up dynamics of the problem (
2.4
) with the exponent fixed to be
p=2
. The rising of this particular number is not surprising, in view of Remark
2.3
and our particular nonlinearity. Indeed, even if the function
R
defined in (
3.8
) and appearing in (
2.3
) behaves like
|\!\cdot\!|^{2}
for small argument and like
|\!\cdot\!|^{\gamma}
for large argument, it will emerge from the proof of the theorems that it is actually the power
|\!\cdot\!|^{2}
what essentially influence the behavior of the problem.

Remark 2.5

(
Other problems
)
.

Let us list here some other related problems:

•

The method exploited in this work, based on the combination of techniques from the wave equations blow-up theory with Orlicz space tools, could be employed also in the study of blow-up phenomena for wave equation with general non-homogeneous convex non-linearities. For example, the computations we perform can be adapted for an equation like

u_{tt}-\Delta u+d(t)u_{t}+m(t)u=\min\{|u|^{p},|u|^{q}\}

with some constants
p,q>1
and damping and mass terms
d(t)
,
m(t)
.

•

Another potential application of our methods is to the problem corresponding to (
1.1
) in exterior domain with appropriate boundary conditions.

•

For
\gamma=2
the problem (
1.1
) is closely related to the inviscid shallow-water equations: they indeed coincide if the pressure satisfies the law
p(\rho)=\frac{\rho^{2}}{2F^{2}}
, being
F
the Froude number (see e.g.
[
3
,
30
]
).

3
Local existence, finite speed of propagation, reformulation

In this section we are going to recall the local existence and finite speed of propagation property of the solution for (
1.1
), after reformulating it in a symmetric hyperbolic system. Then, we will deduce the damped wave equation satisfied by the density, the energy and weak formulations of which will be the starting point of our argument.

Denote the sound speed by

\sigma(\rho)=\sqrt{p^{\prime}(\rho)}=\rho^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}

and set
\overline{\sigma}=\sigma(1)=1
, which corresponds to the sound speed at the background density
\overline{\rho}=1
. Moreover, let

\theta(t,x)=\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left(\sigma(\rho)-1\right)=\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left(\rho^{\frac{\gamma-1}{2}}-1\right).

Then the system (
1.1
) can be reformulated as

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\theta+u\cdot\nabla\theta+\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\theta\right)\nabla\cdot u=0,\\
&\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\left(1+\frac{\gamma-1}{2}\theta\right)\nabla\theta+\frac{\mu u}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}=0,\\
&\theta(0,x)=\varepsilon\theta_{0}(x),\qquad u(0,x)=\varepsilon u_{0}(x)\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

where

\theta_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{2}{\gamma-1}\left(\sigma(1+\varepsilon\rho_{0})-1\right)

satisfies

\theta_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\quad\text{for $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$,}\qquad\text{supp}\,\theta_{0}\subseteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x|\leq 1\right\}.

It is easy to see that (
3.1
) is a symmetric hyperbolic system of the form

V_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}(V)V_{x_{i}}=f(t,V)

with
V=(\theta,u)^{T}
. According to the general theory in
[
18
,
30
]
, the above hyperbolic system of conservation laws admits a local
C^{1}
-solution on the time interval
[0,T)
for some
T>0
, provided the initial data are sufficiently regular. It holds also that

\rho(t,x)>0\qquad\text{for $(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$,}

if the initial density satisfies
\rho(0,x)>0
(which in our case is surely true for
\varepsilon
small enough). Moreover, it can be proved that for any local
C^{1}
-solution
(\rho,u)
of the system (
1.1
), with initial data satisfying (
1.2
), the following finite speed of propagation result holds:

\text{supp}\,(\rho-1),\,\,\text{supp}\,u\subseteq\{(t,x)\in(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\colon|x|\leq 1+t\}.

(3.2)

This can be showed by exploiting a parallel method as in the proof of Lemma 3.2 in
[
40
]
. We omit the details here, and we refer to
[
36
,
37
,
40
]
for a more exhaustive discussion.

With the local existence and the finite speed of propagation in hands, we can proceed reformulating our equations in a integral form, multiplying by a test function.

3.1
The damped wave equation

Suppose
(\rho,u)
is a
C^{1}
-solution to (
1.1
), satisfying (
3.2
).
Let
\Phi(t,x)
be a real smooth function with compact support on
[0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Multiplying equation (
1.1b
) by
\nabla\Phi
, integrating on the strip
[0,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the space-time, with
t\in[0,T)
, and then by parts with respect to the time,
we reach

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho u\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho u\cdot\nabla\Phi_{s}-d(s)\rho u\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s\\
=&\,\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+\varepsilon\rho_{0})u_{0}\cdot\nabla\Phi(0,x)\text{\,d}x\\
&-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u)+\nabla\frac{\rho^{\gamma}-1}{\gamma}\right]\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s.\end{split}

(3.3)

where for short we set

d(s):=\frac{\mu}{(1+s)^{\lambda}}.

Consider now equation (
1.1a
). After a multiplication by
\Phi_{s}(s,x)
, integrating on
[0,t)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and then by parts with respect to the space, we have

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho u\cdot\nabla\Phi_{s}\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\rho-1)_{s}\Phi_{s}\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s.

(3.4)

Multiplying (
1.1a
) by
\Phi
instead, integrating only on the space and then by parts, we obtain that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho u\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\rho-1)_{t}\Phi\text{\,d}x.

(3.5)

Finally, multiplying (
1.1a
) by
d(s)\Phi(s,x)
, integrating on the space-time and then by parts, we get

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d(s)\rho u\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d(s)(\rho-1)_{s}\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s.\end{split}

(3.6)

Now, inserting (
3.4
), (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) into (
3.3
), we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\rho-1)_{t}\Phi\text{\,d}x-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\rho-1)_{s}\Phi_{s}\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla(\rho-1)\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}d(s)(\rho-1)_{s}\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s\\
=&\,-\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\cdot((1+\varepsilon\rho_{0})u_{0})\Phi(0,x)\text{\,d}x\\
&-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[\nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u)\right]\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla R(\rho-1)\cdot\nabla\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}s\end{split}

(3.7)

where
R\colon[-1,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
is defined by

R(p):=\frac{(p+1)^{\gamma}-1}{\gamma}-p.

(3.8)

Notice that equation (
3.7
) can be regarded as the energy solution formulation of the nonlinear damped wave equation

(\rho-1)_{tt}-\Delta(\rho-1)+d(t)(\rho-1)_{t}=\nabla\cdot\left[\nabla\cdot(\rho u\otimes u)\right]+\Delta R(\rho-1)

(3.9)

with initial data

\displaystyle[\rho-1]_{t=0}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\rho_{0},

\displaystyle[(\rho-1)_{t}]_{t=0}

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\nabla\cdot((1+\varepsilon\rho_{0})u_{0}).

Finally, we will need also the weak solution formulation of (
3.9
). Applying the integrations by parts several times in (
3.7
) and letting
t\to T
, we obtain

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\rho-1)\left[\Phi_{tt}-\Delta\Phi-d(t)\Phi_{t}-d_{t}(t)\Phi\right]\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}t\\
=&\,\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[d(0)\rho_{0}\Phi(0,x)+(1+\varepsilon\rho_{0})u_{0}\cdot\nabla\Phi(0,x)-\rho_{0}\Phi_{t}(0,x)\right]\text{\,d}x\\
&+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\text{tr}[(\rho u\otimes u)\nabla^{2}\Phi]\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}t+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}R(\rho-1)\Delta\Phi\text{\,d}x\text{\,d}t\end{split}

(3.10)

where
\nabla^{2}\Phi
is the Hessian matrix of
\Phi
.

4
Some pills of Orlicz spaces theory

Here we briefly recap some tools from the Orlicz space theory, together with some properties we will largely employ across our work. As references on the Orlicz spaces, see for example the classic books
[
19
]
and
[
35
]
.

Let us consider a continuous, even, convex function
\Upupsilon\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying the conditions

\lim_{p\to 0}\frac{\Upupsilon(p)}{p}=0,\qquad\lim_{|p|\to+\infty}\frac{\Upupsilon(p)}{|p|}=+\infty.

By definition (see
[
19
, §I.1.5]
),
\Upupsilon
is a
N
-function. The
N
-function complementary to
\Upupsilon
, denoted by
\Upupsilon^{*}
, is obtained by taking the Legendre transform of
\Upupsilon
(
[
19
, Equation (2.9)]
), namely

\displaystyle\Upupsilon^{*}(q):=\sup_{p\geq 0}\,\{p|q|-\Upupsilon(p)\}.

(4.1)

The following useful inequalities (
[
19
, Equation (2.10)]
) hold for any
p\geq 0
:

p\leq\Upupsilon^{-1}(p)\cdot(\Upupsilon^{*})^{-1}(p)\leq 2p.

(4.2)

We are in the position now to define the Orlicz space associated to
\Upupsilon
. Let us denote with
\widetilde{L^{\Upupsilon}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the Orlicz class of functions
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which

\rho(u;\Upupsilon):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon(u(x))\text{\,d}x<\infty.

Then, we denote by
L^{\Upupsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the set of all functions
u
satisfying the condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)v(x)\text{\,d}x<\infty

for all
v\in\widetilde{L^{\Upupsilon^{*}}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The set
L^{\Upupsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a complete normed linear space, called Orlicz space, when equipped with the Orlicz norm

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{(L^{\Upupsilon})}:=\sup_{\rho(v;\Upupsilon^{*})\leq 1}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)v(x)\text{\,d}x\right|.

The set
L^{\Upupsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can also be transformed into a Banach space by using the Luxemburg norm, defined as

1
1

1

A small remark on the notation: compared with the monography
[
19
]
by Krasnosel’skii and Rutickii, for cosmetic reasons we reverse the symbols used for the Orlicz and Luxemburg norms, since we will employ only the latter norm in our computations.

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon}}:=\inf\left\{k>0\colon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon\left(\frac{u(x)}{k}\right)\text{\,d}x\leq 1\right\},

which is equivalent to the Orlicz norm, since (
[
19
, Equation (9.24)]
)

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon}}\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{(L^{\Upupsilon})}\leq 2\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon}}.

(4.3)

Last but least, a key tool we need from the Orlicz spaces theory is the following Hölder inequality.

Lemma 1

(
[
19
, Theorem 9.3]
)
.

The inequality

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}u(x)v(x)\text{\,d}x\right|\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{(L^{\Upupsilon})}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{(L^{\Upupsilon^{*}})}

holds for any pair of functions
u\in L^{\Upupsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
v\in L^{\Upupsilon^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

The above is true for any
N
-function, but it is time to specialize the definition of
\Upupsilon
to adapt it to our purposes.
For us, the role of
\Upupsilon\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
will be held by

\Upupsilon(p):=\frac{(|p|+1)^{\gamma}-1}{\gamma}-|p|,

(4.4)

whose complementary function, as one can check from formula (
4.1
), is

\Upupsilon^{*}(q)=\frac{(|q|+1)^{\gamma^{\prime}}-1}{\gamma^{\prime}}-|q|,

where
\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}
is the conjugate exponent of
\gamma
, i.e.
\frac{1}{\gamma}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}}=1
.

Note that, in the simplest case
\gamma=2
, we have
\Upupsilon(p)=\frac{|p|^{2}}{2}
. In the general case
\gamma>1
, it is clearly seen that

\Upupsilon(p)\sim\frac{|p|^{\gamma}}{\gamma}

when
|p|\to+\infty
.
On the other side, when
p\to 0
, by Taylor’s theorem it holds

\Upupsilon(p)=\frac{\gamma-1}{2}|p|^{2}+o(|p|^{2}).

Therefore we can deduce

\Upupsilon(p)\approx\left\{\begin{aligned} &|p|^{2}&&\text{if $|p|\leq 1$,}\\
&|p|^{\gamma}&&\text{if $|p|>1$,}\end{aligned}\right.

(4.5)

where the implicit constants in the symbol “
\approx
” depend only on
\gamma
. This relation is helpful in order to highlight the asymptotic behavior of
\Upupsilon
, and we will repeatedly use it to approximate the value of
\Upupsilon
.

We need to introduce also the following function
\Upxi\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
, which roughly speaking inverts the asymptotic behaviors of
\Upupsilon(p)
near
p=0
and near
p=\infty
:

\Upxi(p):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{\Upupsilon(1/p)}&&\text{if $p\neq 0$,}\\
&0&&\text{if $p=0$,}\end{aligned}\right.\approx\begin{cases}|p|^{\gamma}&\text{if $|p|\leq 1$,}\\
|p|^{2}&\text{if $|p|>1$.}\end{cases}

(4.6)

It is also useful to observe that, for
1<\gamma\leq 2
,
\Upupsilon
is super-multiplicative (apart from a multiplicative constant), while on the contrary it is sub-multiplicative (again apart from a multiplicative constant) when
\gamma\geq 2
.

Lemma 2
.

Consider
\Upupsilon
defined in (
4.4
).
If
1<\gamma\leq 2
, then

\Upupsilon(pq)\gtrsim\Upupsilon(p)\,\Upupsilon(q)

(4.7)

for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
, from which in particular

\Upupsilon(pq)\lesssim\Upupsilon(p)\,\Upxi(q),\qquad\Upxi(pq)\lesssim\Upxi(p)\,\Upxi(q),

(4.8)

for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
.

If
\gamma\geq 2
, then

\Upupsilon(pq)\lesssim\Upupsilon(p)\,\Upupsilon(q)

(4.9)

for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
, from which in particular

\Upupsilon(pq)\gtrsim\Upupsilon(p)\,\Upxi(q),\qquad\Upxi(pq)\gtrsim\Upxi(p)\,\Upxi(q),

for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
.

All the implicit constants depend only on
\gamma
.

Proof.

The proof is naïf: we just need to use (
4.5
). Without loss of generality we can assume
|p|\leq|q|
. Firstly suppose
1<\gamma\leq 2
. There are four cases:

(i)

if
|p|\leq|q|\leq 1
, then in particular
|pq|\leq 1
, thus

\Upupsilon(p)\Upupsilon(q)\approx|p|^{2}|q|^{2}\approx\Upupsilon(pq);

(ii)

if
|p|\leq 1<|q|
and
|pq|\leq 1
, then

\Upupsilon(p)\Upupsilon(q)\approx|p|^{2}|q|^{\gamma}\approx\Upupsilon(pq)|q|^{-(2-\gamma)}\leq\Upupsilon(pq);

(iii)

if
|p|\leq 1<|q|
and
|pq|>1
, then

\Upupsilon(p)\Upupsilon(q)\approx|p|^{2}|q|^{\gamma}\approx\Upupsilon(pq)|p|^{2-\gamma}\leq\Upupsilon(pq);

(iv)

if
1<|p|\leq|q|
, then in particular
|pq|>1
, thus

\Upupsilon(p)\Upupsilon(q)\approx|p|^{\gamma}|q|^{\gamma}\approx\Upupsilon(pq).

The relation on the right of (
4.8
) follows directly from (
4.7
) and the definition (
4.6
) of
\Upxi
, whereas the relation on the left follows by substituting
p
and
q
with
pq
and
1/q
respectively in (
4.7
). The case
\gamma>2
is completely analogous.
∎

Remark 4.1
.

Note that the super-/sub-multiplicative relations above in general can not be inverted if
\gamma\neq 2
, in the sense that (
4.9
) does not hold if
1<\gamma<2
, whereas (
4.7
) does not hold if
\gamma>2
. In the special case
\gamma=2
instead, we have
\Upupsilon(pq)=2\Upupsilon(p)\Upupsilon(q)
for any
p,q\in\mathbb{R}
.

An immediate implication of Lemma
2
is that, if
p\lesssim q
, then
\Upupsilon(p)\lesssim\Upupsilon(q)
and
\Upxi(p)\lesssim\Upxi(q)
.
Another consequence is the following observation (which we upgrade at the status of lemma for convenience).

Lemma 3
.

Consider
\Upupsilon
defined in (
4.4
) and let
u\in L^{\Upupsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon\left(\frac{u(x)}{k}\right)\text{\,d}x\leq\kappa_{0}

for some
k>0
and
\kappa_{0}>0
, then

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon}}\leq c_{\gamma,\kappa_{0}}k,

where
c_{\gamma,\kappa_{0}}
is a positive constant depending only on
\gamma
and
\kappa_{0}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
2
there exists some positive constant
d_{\gamma}
, depending only on
\gamma
, such that
\Upupsilon(pq)\leq d_{\gamma}\Upupsilon(p)\,\Upxi(q)
if
1<\gamma\leq 2
. Set

c_{\gamma,\kappa_{0}}:=\left[\Upxi^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{d_{\gamma}\kappa_{0}}\right)\right]^{-1}.

Then

1\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon\left(\frac{u}{k}\right)\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}}\text{\,d}x=d_{\gamma}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon\left(\frac{u}{k}\right)\Upxi\left(\frac{1}{c_{\gamma,\kappa_{0}}}\right)\text{\,d}x\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon\left(\frac{u}{c_{\gamma,\kappa_{0}}k}\right)\text{\,d}x

and hence, by definition of Luxemburg norm, we have that
c_{\gamma,\kappa_{0}}k
is an upper bound for
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon}}
. Employing instead
\Upupsilon(pq)\leq\widetilde{d_{\gamma}}\Upupsilon(p)\,\Upupsilon(q)
, one can prove the case
\gamma\geq 2
in the same way.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Here and in the following, when we write
\Upupsilon^{-1}
,
(\Upupsilon^{*})^{-1}
or
\Upxi^{-1}
, clearly we mean the inverse function of
\Upupsilon
,
\Upupsilon^{*}
or
\Upxi
respectively, when restricted on the non-negative interval
[0,+\infty)
.

We are now ready to prove our theorems.

Proof of Theorem

5.1
The test function

Let us define the positive function

\phi(x):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}e^{x\cdot\sigma}\text{d}\sigma&\text{if $n\geq 2$},\\
&\frac{e^{x}+e^{-x}}{2}&\text{if $n=1$},\end{aligned}\right.

(5.1)

where
\omega_{n}:=\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)}
is the volume of the
n
-dimensional unit ball, so that
n\omega_{n}
is the volume of the unit
(n-1)
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. This function was firstly introduced to prove the blow-up of wave equations by Yordanov and Zhang in
[
49
]
.
The function
\phi
is radial, strictly increasing with respect to
|x|
, solves
\Delta\phi=\phi
, and moreover

\phi(0)=1,\qquad\phi(x)\sim\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}{n\omega_{n}}|x|^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{|x|}\quad\text{when $|x|\to+\infty$,}

therefore
\phi
satisfies, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the relation

\phi(x)\approx\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}e^{|x|}.

(5.2)

Here and in the following
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle:=(1+|\!\cdot\!|^{2})^{1/2}
stands as customary for the Japanese bracket notation.

All the above properties of
\phi
can be easily deduced rewriting this function in a closed form. Indeed, using
n
-dimensional spherical coordinates and choosing the polar axis parallel to
x
, one can prove that actually

\phi(x)=\frac{(2\pi)^{\frac{n}{2}}}{n\omega_{n}}|x|^{1-\frac{n}{2}}I_{\frac{n}{2}-1}(|x|),

where
I_{\nu}(z)
is the modified Bessel function of the first kind.
2
2

2

Curiously, as far as we know, this closed expression seems to be never reported in the related literature.

From the properties of the latter (see e.g.
[
1
, Sections 9.6 and 9.7]
) follow those of
\phi
.

Let us consider then the positive function

\psi(t,x):=e^{-t}\phi(x),

which satisfies
\psi_{t}=-\psi
,
\Delta\psi=\psi
,
and the following bounds we will use later:

\psi(t,x)\lesssim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}

(5.3)

for
|x|\leq 1+t
, and

\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\psi^{b}(x)\text{\,d}x\approx\left\langle t\right\rangle^{\frac{n-1}{2}(2-b)}

(5.4)

for any
b\in[0,2]
and
t\geq 0
.
The estimate (
5.3
) comes directly from (
5.2
).
For (
5.4
), observe that from one side we have

\begin{split}\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\psi^{b}(x)\text{\,d}x&\gtrsim\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}b}e^{b|x|-bt}\text{\,d}x\\
&\gtrsim\int_{\frac{1+t}{2}}^{1+t}\left\langle r\right\rangle^{\frac{n-1}{2}(2-b)}e^{br-bt}\text{\,d}r\\
&\gtrsim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{\frac{n-1}{2}(2-b)},\end{split}

while on the other side

\begin{split}\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\psi^{b}(x)\text{\,d}x&\lesssim\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\left\langle x\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}b}e^{b|x|-bt}\text{\,d}x\\
&\lesssim\int_{0}^{1+t}\left\langle r\right\rangle^{\frac{n-1}{2}(2-b)}e^{br-bt}\text{\,d}r\\
&\lesssim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{\frac{n-1}{2}(2-b)}.\end{split}

Now, let
\chi
a smooth compactly supported cut-off function such that
\chi(t,x)\equiv 1
on the support of
\rho-1
. Choosing
\Phi=\psi\chi
as test function in (
3.7

\begin{multlined}F^{\prime\prime}+2F^{\prime}+\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}(F^{\prime}+F)\\

(5.5)

where we define the functional
F\equiv F(t)
as

F(t):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(\rho-1)\psi\text{\,d}x.

Observe that from the conditions (

\displaystyle\begin{split}F(0)&=\varepsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho_{0}\phi\text{\,d}x>0,\\

(5.6)

The last inequality holds true for

5.2
Estimate for the nonlinear term

First of all, we can get rid of the first term on the right-hand side of (

\begin{split}\text{tr}\left[(\rho u\otimes u)\nabla^{2}\psi\right]&=\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\rho\,\text{tr}\left[(u\otimes u)(\sigma\otimes\sigma)\right]\,e^{x\cdot\sigma-t}\text{\,d}\sigma\\
&=\frac{1}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\rho(u\cdot\sigma)^{2}e^{x\cdot\sigma-t}\text{\,d}\sigma\\
&\geq 0\end{split}

whereas of course

\text{tr}\left[(\rho u\otimes u)\nabla^{2}\psi\right]=\rho u^{2}\,\frac{e^{x-t}+e^{-x-t}}{2}\geq 0

in the
1
-dimensional case.
Moreover, the functions
\Upupsilon
and
R

4.4
) and (
3.8

[0,+\infty)
, but are different on
[-1,0)

for

R(\rho-1)\approx\Upupsilon(\rho-1)

\rho\geq 0
.
Therefore, from (
5.5

F^{\prime\prime}+2F^{\prime}+\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}(F^{\prime}+F)\gtrsim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon(\rho-1)\,\psi\text{\,d}x.

(5.7)

If
\gamma>2

4.5

\Upupsilon(p)\gtrsim|p|^{2}
. Thus, the case
\gamma>2
can be reduced to the case
\gamma=2

1<\gamma\leq 2
.

We want to prove now that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon(\rho-1)\psi\text{\,d}x\gtrsim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\Upupsilon(F),

(5.8)

which inserted in (
5.7
) would imply

F^{\prime\prime}+2F^{\prime}+\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}(F^{\prime}+F)\gtrsim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\Upupsilon(F).

(5.9)

Now the Orlicz spaces properties comes into play.
From the inequality (
4.2
), the support property (
3.2
), the equivalence relation (
4.3
) and the Hölder inequality in Lemma

, we obtain, for a fixed positive

\begin{split}|F(t)|&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\rho-1|\psi\text{\,d}x\\

&\lesssim\left\lVert\psi^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon^{*}}(|x|\leq 1+t)}\left\lVert|\rho-1|\Upupsilon^{-1}\left(\psi^{\alpha}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\end{split}

(5.10)

In the penultimate relation we used

\psi^{1-\alpha}(\Upupsilon^{*})^{-1}\left(\psi^{\alpha}\right)\approx\psi^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}

which follows from
\psi\lesssim 1
(due to (
5.3
)) and the fact that
(\Upupsilon^{*})^{-1}(p)\approx|p|^{1/2}
if

is small.

Let us consider
\left\lVert\psi^{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\rVert_{L^{\Upupsilon^{*}}(|x|\leq 1+t)}
. Note that, thanks to (

\frac{\psi^{1-\alpha/2}}{\left(\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\psi^{2-\alpha}\text{\,d}x\right)^{1/2}}\lesssim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\lesssim 1

for
|x|\leq 1+t
,
and therefore, since
\Upupsilon^{*}(p)\approx|p|^{2}
for small
|p|

where we used the homogeneity of the norm. From (

) and the compactness of the support of

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Upupsilon\left(\frac{|\rho-1|\Psi}{k}\right)\text{\,d}x&\lesssim\int_{|x|\leq 1+t}\Upupsilon(\rho-1)\,\Upxi\left(\frac{\Psi}{k}\right)\text{\,d}x\\

inserting which into (

Remark 5.1
.

The choice of

employed in (

Remark 5.2

In (

and, proceeding as above, with

with again the choice

. At this point, since

is not sub-multiplicative for

The multiplier

Now that we have (
5.9
) at our disposal, let us consider the multiplier

introduced in
[
22
]
, which solves the ordinary differential equation

\frac{\mathcal{m}^{\prime}(t)}{\mathcal{m}(t)}=\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}

and satisfies the bounds

for

and

. Its role is to “absorb” the damping term. Indeed, adding
\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}F
on both side of (

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left\{\mathcal{m}(t)\,[F^{\prime}(t)+2F(t)]\right\}\gtrsim\frac{\mu}{(1+t)^{\lambda}}\mathcal{m}(t)F(t)+\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathcal{m}\Upupsilon(F(t)).

We will soon prove that actually

is non-negative, so we can get rid also of the additional first term on the right-hand side.
After an integration with respect to the time we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{m}(t)[F^{\prime}(t)+2F(t)]\gtrsim&\,\mathcal{m}(0)[F^{\prime}(0)+2F(0)]\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\mu}{(1+s)^{\lambda}}\mathcal{m}(s)F(s)\text{\,d}s\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle s\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathcal{m}(s)\Upupsilon(F(s))\text{\,d}s.\end{split}

Multiplying now by
\frac{e^{2t}}{\mathcal{m}(t)}
, integrating again with respect to the time, and then multiplying by
e^{-2t}
, we aim to

\begin{split}F(t)\gtrsim&\,F(0)e^{-2t}+\mathcal{m}(0)[F^{\prime}(0)+2F(0)]e^{-2t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{2s}}{\mathcal{m}(s)}\text{\,d}s\\
&+e^{-2t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e^{2s}}{\mathcal{m}(s)}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\mu}{(1+r)^{\lambda}}\mathcal{m}(r)F(r)\text{\,d}r\text{\,d}s\\

From this expression we can prove that
F(t)>0
for

, employing a standard comparison argument. Indeed, due to the fact that
F(0)>0
by our assumption on the initial data and that
F
is continuous, we know that
F(t)>0
at least for small

. Assume by contradiction that

is the smallest zero point of

; therefore, setting

in (

) and using also that
F^{\prime}(0)+2F(0)\geq 0
, we get
0=F(t_{0})\gtrsim F(0)e^{-2t_{0}}
— a contradiction. Thus
F(t)>0
for any
t\geq 0
.

Thanks to this information we can suppress the third term in (

), and using also

(due to (

\begin{split}F(t)\gtrsim&\,F(0)e^{-2t}+[F^{\prime}(0)+2F(0)]\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}\\
&+e^{-2t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2s}\int_{0}^{s}\left\langle r\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\Upupsilon(F(r))\text{\,d}r\text{\,d}s.\end{split}

Morally, we would like now to “differentiate” the above estimate to obtain a differential inequality like (

) but without the damping term. Let us introduce the auxiliary function
\overline{F}\equiv\overline{F}(t)

\begin{split}\overline{F}(t):=&\,\frac{F(0)}{2}e^{-2t}+[F^{\prime}(0)+2F(0)]\frac{1-e^{-2t}}{2}\\

From its definition and (

), it holds

F(t)\gtrsim\frac{F(0)}{2}e^{-2t}+\overline{F}(t)\geq\overline{F}(t)>0.

It is easy to check, multiplying firstly by

and deriving, and then multiplying by

and deriving again, that

\overline{F}^{\prime\prime}(t)+2\overline{F}^{\prime}(t)=\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\Upupsilon(F(t))\gtrsim\left\langle t\right\rangle^{-\frac{n-1}{2}}\Upupsilon\left(\overline{F}(t)\right).

Moreover

\displaystyle\overline{F}(0)=\frac{F(0)}{2}>0,

\displaystyle\overline{F}^{\prime}(0)=F^{\prime}(0)+F(0)>0.

), the conclusion of the proof follows from a straightforward application of the next lemma, which is a variation of Theorem 3.1 in
[

, with a non-linear term which is allowed to behave like two different powers when its argument is respectively small or large. Since the proof of the lemma follows step by step the one in
[

]

Lemma 4

Let

. Assume that
