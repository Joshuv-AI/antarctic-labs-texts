Abstract
Attention has recently been focused on the possibility of artificially intelligent sellers on platforms colluding to limit output and raise prices. Such arrangements (cartels), however, feature an incentive for individual sellers to deviate to a lower price (cheat) to increase their own profits. Stabilizing such cartels therefore requires credible threats of punishments, such as price wars. In this paper, I propose a mechanism to destabilize cartels by protecting any cheaters from a price war by guaranteeing a stream of profits which is unaffected by arbitrary punishments, only if such punishments actually occur. Equilibrium analysis of the induced game predicts a reversion to repeated static Nash pricing. When implemented in a reinforcement learning framework, it provides substantial reductions in prices (reducing markups by 40% or more), without affecting product variety or requiring the platform to make any payments on path. This mechanism applies to both the sale of differentiated goods on platforms, and the sale of homogeneous goods through direct sales. The mechanism operates purely off-path, thereby inducing no welfare losses in practice, and does not depend on the choice of discount factors.

Introduction

Concern with cartels is as old as the field of economic inquiry. While
cartels were not always considered as being a failure of the market (see for
instance the work in classical cartel theory by early German economists such
as Kleinwächter), by the 1960s, economic theory had accepted cartels as
being harmful, and studies such as
Stigler 1964
were beginning
to study the problem formally with the tools of modern economics.
Legislative action in the form of the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 had
indeed preceded such studies.

Interest in this classical problem has been rejuvenated by emerging theories
of algorithmic collusion by artificially intelligent pricing algorithms,
which are often used by sellers on platforms to price their products. While

OECD 2017
raised the question, recent work in
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020
has confirmed that this is indeed a very real
possibility. This is a major concern in this context as the communication
records which are usually at the heart of the prosecution’s case against a
cartel do not exist when AI agents learn to collude without communication.
Some solutions have also been posed in the context of a monopoly platform in

Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, which I discuss later.

In this paper, I propose a mechanism which destabilizes cartels both on
platforms and (with suitable alterations) in markets featuring direct
interactions between buyers and sellers (termed direct markets hereafter).
The mechanism is in the vein of the principle of “helping
the cheater” presented in
Marx et al. 2017
.
1
1

1

In
Marx et al. 2017
, it is argued that if a buyer is in receipt of a lower bid
from a member of the cartel (i.e. cheating), it should not reveal this to
the other members in order to “help the
cheater” since letting the cheating happen is in the
interest of the buyer.
It protects any seller who cheats a cartel from
being effectively punished by other members of the cartel using a (platform
provided) price topup, only if such punishment is actually observed. When
punishment is ineffective because the agent being punished is protected
adequately, the act of punishing is suboptimal, as it reduces profits for
the punisher as well, so that in theory, collusion is not sustainable, and
prices should drop, bringing them closer to repeated static Nash pricing.
Further, since no punishments are predicted, no topups should occur on path
either, so that the reductions in prices should be, in effect,
“free”. These results are captured in
Theorem
1
.

In the context of algorithmic collusion by artificially intelligent sellers
on platforms, I test this mechanism in a Multi Agent Reinforcement Learning
(MARL) framework, such as the one introduced in
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020

and subsequently used in
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, and show that the
predictions made by the theory are indeed observed in the actions of the AI
agents.
2
2

2

This framework is different from the one in
Klein 2021
,
which involves sequential pricing. Here, sellers price simultaneously.
The
price does drop substantially, so that the markups above the static Nash
prices are often reduced by 40% or more. Further, this comes at no actual
cost to the platform or consumers, as the top-ups remain off the path,
operating as a threat which destabilizes the cartel, but is never deployed,
which is precisely the theoretical prediction.

In the second case, that is, when the mechanism is used in direct markets
(markets where sellers and buyers interact directly), if the competition
remains of the Bertrand variety, then the buyer (or a group of buyers) can
take over the role of providing the above top-ups and the arguments carry
over directly. If, on the other hand, the market features quantity
competition on homogeneous goods
3
3

3

A common type of collusion. OPEC and the Lysine cartel are examples of
collusion on a homogeneous good.
, then the buyer (or a group of buyers) can
offer to purchase the quantities produced in the period when cheating first
occurs at the prevailing price in that period from each seller who was a
part of the first stage price drop, conditional on there being a second
price drop owing to punishments in some later period. This again has the
effect of insulating the cheaters from the punishments by the cartel and
destabilizing all cartels (Theorem
2
).
4
4

4

In the analysis of this context, we limit ourselves to the study of Subgame
Perfect Nash equilibria of the game induced by the mechanism.
Further, it
involves no welfare losses, such as those stemming from the use of
inventories (
Snyder 1996
) or buyer groups (
Dana Jr 2012
).

This approach has several attractive properties. First, the mechanism
operates purely off path, and thus avoids distorting the market outcome.
This contrasts sharply with existing solutions in the literature, such as
the DPDP algorithm proposed in
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, which involves
steering most of the demand to a single seller on path. In markets which
operate as oligopolies, where several sellers jointly supply the quantity
demanded, this approach may be incompatible with supply constraints, as a
single seller may not be able to meet the demand being steered towards it.
5
5

5

The market for aerated beverages in the US is an example, where demand is
split fairly evenly, and logistical concerns may prevent the quick addition
of supply capacity.
The two stage price drop rule proposed in this paper
allows all the sellers to continue supplying the market, thereby
circumventing this limitation. Further, as noted in
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, depending on the elasticity of market size to prices,
platforms might not have the incentive to prevent cartelization in some
settings, requiring competition authorities to step in with legislation.
When authorities are unsure about whether a market is cartelized or not,
requiring the use of the two stage price drop rule might be more acceptable
owing to its off-path nature, as it avoids distorting a potentially
uncartelized market.
6
6

6

Indeed, further complications may emerge from on path distortions. Another
subtle issue involves official and unofficial sellers for the same product.
In several markets, quality concerns around products sold by unofficial
sellers mean that a substantial volume of the sales made on the platform are
made by official sellers, while unofficial sellers price somewhat lower. A
steering algorithm based on selecting for lower prices (such as DPDP) would
then select unofficial sellers with a higher likelihood. Then, the platform
may be unwilling to use such steering algorithms in practice as buyers may
be less willing to purchase from the unofficial seller, and platform profits
might fall.

Second, the mechanism applies to a wide variety of markets, including those
where the marginal costs of the sellers are heterogeneous, or those with
multiple platforms as well. This is of significant practical importance,
since most real world markets involve different marginal costs and are
served by multiple platforms.
7
7

7

For instance, in the e-commerce industry, Amazon and eBay operate parallely
and flight tickets can be booked on one of many platforms, such as
booking.com, kayak.com etc,
The major challenge with multiple platforms
stems from the following subtle issue - it is possible for a cartel to wage
a price war on a platform while only selling products on a different
platform, as long as switching costs (across platforms) are not too high for
buyers. This is because buyers are lured away into the other platform by the
lower prices which such a price war yields. Other recent solutions to the
problem of collusion on platforms, such as
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, by
contrast, focus on monopoly platforms and require sellers to have identical
marginal costs.

Third, the mechanism, by design, does not require the platform (or the buyer
in direct markets) to have any knowledge of the market structure (including
the duration and intensity of punishment strategies), whether (or by how
much) sellers discount the future, cost structure, or the demand functions
of the buyers on the platform.
8
8

8

While the thheoretical treatment does not need knowledge of the cost
structure when considering subgame perfect equilibria, the MARL
implementation requires knowledge of the marginal cost of the agents.
However fairly imprecise estimates, such as those with 25% errors suffice.
In principle, even the MARL implementation can be made to work without
knowledge of the cost structure, but this would require a much larger state
space, which is impractical for training on a personal computer.
Indeed, it
is not necessary to know whether the market is cartelized or not. Low
informational requirements make the mechanism suitable for use in a variety
of scenarios.

2.4

discusses the treatment involving AI sellers; Section
3
extends
the mechanism to destabilize cartels in direct markets; Section
4
reviews the relevant literature, and Section
5

Destabilizing Cartels on Platforms

2.1
Model

In this section, I consider a setting with
I
sellers, identified with
i\in\{1,2,...,I\}=\mathcal{I}
, each of which sells differentiated products
on a monopoly platform.
9
9

9

I will discuss the case of multiple platforms in Section
2.5
.
In
each period
t
, seller
i
sells a quantity
q_{i,t}
at a price
p_{i,t}
.
The sellers have a (constant) marginal cost of
c_{i}
.

There is a unit mass of consumers each with one unit of demand. A
representative consumer who buys product
i
in period
t
obtains utility

u_{i}^{t}=a_{i}-p_{i,t}+\zeta_{i}\text{.}

If she buys no products, she obtains the outside option utility

u_{i}^{t}=a_{0}+\zeta_{0}\text{.}

\zeta_{0}
and
\zeta_{i}
are independent random variables with a type
I extreme value distribution that has a common scale parameter
\mu
. These assumptions are essentially identical to those in
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020
. With these assumptions, each seller receives the
standard logit demand given by

D_{i}(p_{t})=\frac{e^{(\frac{a_{i}-p_{i,t}}{\mu})}}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{I}}e^{(\frac{a_{j}-p_{j,t}}{\mu})}+e^{a_{0}}}\text{,}

where
p_{t}=(p_{i,t})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
represents the set of
prices at time
t
.

In this model, it is well established that there is a unique Nash
equilibrium of the static game, and it is an interior optimum, satisfying
the first order conditions for profit maximization by each seller (given the
prices set by the others). I denote the set of Nash equilibrium prices by
p^{\ast}=(p_{i}^{\ast})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
. In the following result, I establish that in any effective cartel, at least one seller has an incentive
to reduce its prices.

Lemma 1

If at least one seller
i
sets a price strictly above
p_{i}^{\ast}
, then there is a seller who has the contemporaneous incentive
to reduce its price. Conversely, if a seller sets a price strictly below
p_{i}^{\ast}
, then there is a seller who has the contemporaneous incentive
to raise its price.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.

2.2
Cartels

A cartel is described by the tuple
\mathcal{C}=\left\langle(p_{i}^{c})_{i\in\mathcal{I}},T,(\bar{p}_{i,t}((p_{j,\tau})_{j\in I,\tau=1,..,t-1}))_{i\in\mathcal{I},t=1,..,T}\right\rangle
, which refers to the
following strategy: If each seller has chosen the cartel prescribed prices
p_{i}^{c}
in the previous periods, then each seller continues to price at
p_{i}^{c}
. But, if any seller deviates to any other price, then in the
T

periods following this deviation, sellers price at
\bar{p}_{i,t}((p_{j,t})_{j\in I,t=1,..,T})
which are a function of the history of
the game since the period in which the first deviation occurred. Then, they
revert to pricing at
p_{i}^{c}
. That is, I allow for
T
periods of
punishment, with quantities allowed to vary as a function of the production
of other sellers.
T
is not fixed, and can be chosen by the cartel
depending on various parameters. Further, the platform does not need to know

T\,
. Notice that this allows for essentially any cartel punishment
strategy so long as it depends only on the history of the game before period

t
, and not on the prices being chosen for period
t
. Further, this set of
cartel strategies allows for the noncooperative strategy of repeated Nash
pricing as well. In particular, this is obtained by setting
p_{i}^{c}=p_{i}^{\ast}
, and the punishment quantities to
p_{i}^{\ast}

for each seller
i
regardless of the history of the game.
10
10

10

While it is fairly permissive, this structure does rule out some cartel
structures. See Appendix
B
for a description.

2.3
Destabilizing Mechanism

In this section, I will describe the mechanism used to destabilize cartels
on platforms. The mechanism requires the platform to announce (and commit
to) the following rule at some time
\tau
.

Definition 1

(
Two stage price drop rule
) If a seller
i
reduces its price at
time
\tau_{1}>\tau+1
relative to
p_{i,\tau+1}
and is followed by one
or more sellers
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
reducing
their prices in period
\tau_{2}>\tau_{1}
, then the platform will provide
a price top-up
\hat{p}_{i,t}>0
to seller
i
if it

•

sets price
p_{i,\tau_{1}}
at period
\tau_{2}
, and

•

sets price
p_{i,t}\in[\min_{\tilde{\imath}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}}p_{\tilde{\imath},t-1},\max_{\tilde{\imath}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}}p_{\tilde{\imath},t-1}]
after period
\tau_{2}
, and

•

sells no more than
q_{i,\tau_{1}}
in any period on or after
\tau_{2}

where
\hat{p}_{i,t}
satisfies
(\hat{p}_{i,t}+p_{i,t})q_{i,t}=p_{i,\tau_{1}}q_{i,\tau_{1}}
at any time
t\geq\tau_{2}
.

Note that the platform must allow one period to elapse after
\tau
before
monitoring for price drops. This sets up a baseline against which drops are
measured.

Before proving that this mechanism destabilizes cartels on the platform, I first provide some intuition for its design. The mechanism shares the spirit
of the “help the cheater” principle from

Marx et al. 2017
. The seller
i
in the definition above is the one which cheats
the cartel in period
\tau_{1}
. The set of sellers
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
punish the cheaters starting from period
\tau_{2}
. However, it is
possible that some sellers who cheat the cartel at
\tau_{1}
also join in
the punishments at
\tau_{2}
, but such sellers are not helped (via the
top-ups) by the platform.
11
11

11

Notice that the platform may have no incentive to offer such top-ups once
the cheating has happened. Therefore, this requires commitment. This is a
common assumption shared by most papers in this literature though.

The platform helps the cheaters by effectively insulating them from
punishment by the cartel. To see how, it is helpful to ask - why is the
punishment effective? When the sellers in
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
reduce
prices, they shift demand towards themselves (and away from seller
i
) if
seller
i
does not also make a corresponding reduction in prices. Even if
seller
i
best responds to the punishment, its profit is less than that
under the cartel. Accordingly, a cartel is stable if for sufficiently long
punishment strategies, and sufficiently patient sellers, following the
cartel prescribed prices yields higher utility than cheating the cartel and
best responding to the punishment strategy. By providing top-ups however,
the above mechanism brings up the profit of seller
i
to the level it was
at when it cheated the cartel (which is even higher than the profit under
the cartel). The top-ups are determined by the equation
(\hat{p}_{i,t}+p_{i,t})q_{i,t}=p_{i,\tau_{1}}q_{i,\tau_{1}}
, which effectively
matches the
revenue
from period
\tau^{1}
at any period on or after
the punishment has started, i.e.
\tau_{2}
. Since the seller is limited to
a quantity
q_{i}^{\tau_{1}}
, revenue matching implies profit matching, so
that the profit of seller
i
is not affected by the punishments.

Such profit matching however creates an incorrect incentive for the sellers
who receive protection. They have an incentive to produce exactly one unit
of the product (thereby saving production costs) and obtain a very high
top-up from the platform (since they are guaranteed a certain level of
revenue). While even with this caveat the mechanism destabilizes cartels,
this leaves the platform with a major liability. Setting the price between
the punishment prices (in the range
[\min_{\tilde{\imath}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}}p_{\tilde{\imath},t-1},\max_{\tilde{\imath}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}}p_{\tilde{\imath},t-1}]
) set by sellers in
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
from the previous period reduces this liability, as the seller
is likely to sell a reasonable amount when (approximately) matching prices
with the punishing sellers (
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
).

The reason for conditioning these top-ups on a two stage price drop (indeed
we could destabilize cartels with unconditional top-ups) is somewhat more
technical - it allows us to move the destabilization off the equilibrium
path. In essence, the rule creates a “race” to avail of the top-ups, since any seller who can
gain the profit guarantee is better off than they would be if they followed
the cartel strategy.
12
12

12

This point is formally demonstrated in the proof of Theorem
1
in the appendix.
This leads to a one-stage drop to the
noncooperative prices since no seller is willing to maintain higher prices
and allow the others to cheat first. There is no further incentive to drop
prices, so that a two stage price drop never occurs. Thus, the rule yields
repeated Nash pricing without leaving the platform liable for top-ups.
13
13

13

A close analogy to the theory of implementation of social choice functions
is the notion that the mechanism
fully implements
the noncooperative
equilibrium within the set of acceptable cartel strategies with no
transfers
on the equilibrium path, where the top-ups are interpreted as
transfers from the platform (mechanism designer) to the sellers (agents).

The reader may also consider the possibility that sellers may cooperate to
take advantage of these top-ups, for instance, by causing two very small
price drops, and then asking the recipients of the top-ups to share the
additional revenue with the cartel. The reason this is infeasible is that
such an agreement is not enforceable, as it violates antitrust laws. Since
it is not enforceable, the recipients of the top-ups will not share the
additional revenues, nullifying the agreement.

Now, we can state the main result of this section.

Theorem 1

The game induced by the two stage price drop rule has a
unique subgame perfect Nash equilibrium in the class of cartel strategies
\mathcal{C}
involving each seller pricing at the Nash price
p_{i}^{\ast}

in each period after the rule is announced. Further, the platform provides
no top-ups in equilibrium.

The proof is provided in Appendix
C
.

If we consider a substantially different model, where sellers engage in
price competition and sell directly (rather than on a platform) either to
one large buyer, or to many small buyers (each of whom has the same demand
function as in the base model), the flexibility of the two stage price drop
rule can be seen in the following result.

Corollary 1

In markets featuring direct interactions between parties
(i.e. without platforms) alongside price competition, the two stage price
drop rule can also be used by a sufficiently large buyer to destabilize
cartels. Further, if the buyers can contract amongst themselves, then a
sufficiently large group of small buyers can destabilize cartels using the
two stage price drop rule as well.

Proof.
For the case with one large buyer, it can announce (and commit to) the two
stage price drop rule. From Theorem
1
then, repeated Nash
pricing would obtain. If small buyers can contract amongst themselves, then
a large enough group of small buyers can credibly commit to provide top-ups
as well. Once again, from Theorem
1
, repeated Nash pricing
obtains.

Multi Agent Reinforcement Learning

In this section, I discuss how the above mechanism, with suitable
modifications, is implemented in the MARL framework, and the results which
obtain. We begin with a simplified informal discussion of Q-learning, which
shall suffice for our purposes.
14
14

14

For a more formal treatment, the reader is well served by
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020
and

.

2.4.1
An Introduction to Q-learning

Q-learning is a model free learning algorithm to train agents to address the
problem of finding the best strategies to interact with a given environment
to maximize some objective. This is usually achieved by starting with what
is called a Q-table, the rows and columns of which correspond to states of
the environment and actions respectively. The values in the table are the

quality
of an action, which is the origin of the term Q-learning. In
general, the Q-value associated with an action
a
at a state
s
is the
contemporaneous utility obtained by taking action
a
in the given state,
and the implicit value of the state
s^{\prime}
that results in the next
period, discounted by
\delta
. This latter component is modeled by the
value of the action in the state
s^{\prime}
with the highest Q-value.
Hypothetically, if the correct Q-values were known, the agent would simply
observe the state of the world, and then choose the action with the highest
Q-value, and this would be the best strategy.

The typical way to learn the correct Q-values is a form of value function
iteration where agents try each action at each state several times and learn
the resulting value. This process must balance exploration with exploitation
- if the agent attempts to explore as much as possible (say for instance by
always choosing a random action), then it will fail to learn because the
value of the best action at
s^{\prime}
is not valued correctly since it
is derived (in turn) from purely random actions. Thus, commonly, an
\varepsilon
-greedy strategy is used, where, with probability
\varepsilon
, the agent chooses a random action, while with probability
(1-\varepsilon)
, the agent chooses the best action available to it. Further,
\varepsilon
is usually decayed over time, so that in the beginning, when
the knowledge of the agent is limited, it is biased more towards
exploration, and over time, as it improves, it is biased more towards
exploitation. This is usually achieved by setting
\varepsilon=e^{-\beta t}
, and in this case,
\beta
is chosen to be
4\times 10^{-6}
, following

Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020
. Thereafter, the Q-value of a state action pair
is updated to a convex combination of the old Q-value and the payoff
obtained as a result of the action chosen with weights
(1-\alpha)
and
\alpha
respectively. The weight
\alpha
is usually heavily balanced
towards the old Q-value, so that typically values such as
0.1
or
0.15

are used. This is to prevent very sharp changes in the Q-value table, so
that if the optimal strategy is learnt, it remains stable. These steps are
then iterated till convergence, which is defined by the requirement that the
best action for any state has not changed in
100,000
iterations.

To summarize, the following steps are performed in sequence.

1.

Initialize the Q-value of each state action combination to
0
.
15
15

15

In both
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020
and
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, the
Q-values are initialized to the contemporaneous utilities assuming the other
agent plays random actions. However, much the same set of outcomes is
observed with this simpler initialization, with the collusive price being
identical up to two decimal spaces, so I choose this approach for
simplicity.

2.

Observe the state of the environment. Suppose it is
s
.

3.

With probability
\varepsilon
, choose a random action, or with
probability
(1-\varepsilon)
choose the action with the highest Q-value in
state
s
. Suppose the chosen action is
a
.

4.

Take action
a
, observe the utility obtained,
u(a,s)
, and the next
state
s^{\prime}
.

5.

Define the new Q-value
Q^{\prime}(a,s)=u(a,s)+\delta\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(a^{\prime},s^{\prime})

6.

Update the Q-table:
Q(a,s)\leftarrow(1-\alpha)Q(a,s)+\alpha Q^{\prime}(a,s)

7.

Iterate to convergence.

2.4.2
Implementing the Two Stage Price Drop Rule with AI Sellers

Turning to the case of multiagent collusion on platforms, the state space is
typically modeled to include only one past period of memory. That is, the
state is a vector of tuples like
(p_{1},p_{2})
, where
p_{1}
and
p_{2}

are the prices set by agent 1 and agent 2 in the previous period.
16
16

16

Q-learning is fairly computationally demanding, so we study a two agent
case. However, in principle, the two stage price drop rule could be applied
to any number of agents.
Further, following
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, the
space of prices is discretized to use
15
points between
1
and
2.1
.
There are thus 225 states and 15 actions in each state. With the parameter
choices in
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
, i.e.
a_{0}=0
,
a_{1}=2
,
a_{2}=2
,

c_{1}=1
,
c_{2}=1
,
\delta=0.95
and following averaging over 108
simulations, the static Nash prices are
p_{1}=p_{2}=1.47
and the prices
observed in the Q-learning simulation are
p_{1}=p_{2}=1.6886
, resulting in
a markup of
14.87\%
.
17
17

17

In some small number of cases, price cycles are observed. The numbers
presented here exclude these cycles.

When implementing the two stage price drop rule under Q-learning, the
following slightly modified version of the rule is used.

Definition 2

(
Simplified Two stage price drop rule for AI sellers
) If a seller
i
reduces its price at time
\tau_{1}>\tau+1
relative to
p_{i,\tau+1}
, leading to higher profits than at period
\tau
, and is followed by one
or more sellers
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
reducing
their prices in period
\tau_{2}>\tau_{1}
, then the platform will provide
a price top-up
\hat{p}_{i,t}>0
to seller
i
if it sets price
p_{i,\tau_{1}}
from period
\tau_{1}
onwards, where
\hat{p}_{i,t}

satisfies
(\hat{p}_{i,t}+p_{i,t}-c_{i})q_{i,t}=q_{i,\tau_{1}}(p_{i,\tau_{1}}-c_{i})
at any time
t\geq\tau_{2}
.

The main modifications are the following. First, the agent who performs the
first stage price drop is asked to set the same price as it did when it
first dropped the price. This is intended to let it make some sales in the
post price drop regime, as otherwise it has the incentive to raise its price
and sell a small quantity in order to reduce production costs, as it is
guaranteed a certain profit. This increases the (off path) burden from
top-ups to the platform. Second, the quantity of sale limitation is removed,
as the number of possible quantities is 225, one for each combination of
prices. This would yield a state space that is too large for training to be
practical. Finally, the inability to remember the quantity means that
instead of matching revenues, the mechanism now matches profits, and this,
in turn, necessitates knowledge of the costs. As I will show however, the
mechanism is fairly robust to cost misspecification, functioning reasonably
well up to a
10\%
underspecification of the cost, and a
25\%

100

2.5
Multiple Platforms

In this section, I consider a scenario where the market is served by
multiple platforms. This is indeed quite common in practice, with Amazon and
Best Buy selling electronics in the United States, Flipkart and Amazon
selling mobile phones in India and so on. For simplicity, I consider a
setting where the platforms are identical and consumers switch between
platforms costlessly. Accordingly, consider the base model, with
J

platforms, denoted by the set
\mathcal{J}=\{1,2,...,J\}
, and let a typical
platform be denoted by
j
. A representative consumer who buys product
i

in period
t
from platform
j
obtains utility

u_{i}^{t}=a_{i}-p_{i,j,t}+\zeta_{i}\text{,}

where
p_{i,j,t}
represents the price of product
i
on platform
j
in
period
t
. The utility from purchasing the outside option remains
unaffected.

With these assumptions, each firm receives the standard logit demand given by

D_{i}(p_{t})=\frac{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}e^{(\frac{a_{i}-p_{i,j,t}}{\mu})}}{\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I\times J}}e^{(\frac{a_{i}-p_{i,j,t}}{\mu})}+e^{a_{0}}}\text{,}

where
p_{t}=(p_{i,j,t})_{i,j}
represents the set of prices at time
t
.

To illustrate the main challenge that is posed by the presence of multiple
platforms, notice that if a seller cheats the cartel on one platform, the
rest of the cartel can punish on all the other platforms as well. Then, if
one of the platforms offers protection to the seller using the two stage
price drop rule, it may insulate the seller from the losses on that
platform, but the seller may still suffer substantial losses on the other
platforms which may be a sufficient deterrent to cheating. Thus, a single
platform deploying the mechanism may be insufficient to yield noncooperative
pricing unless it represents the majority of sales in the market.

In principle, if a platform (say Platform A) monitors the prices within a
certain market segment across all the other platforms and then provides
protection (via top-ups) to any seller which reduces prices on Platform A
and is punished either on Platform A or on other platforms, then it could
still break cartels, but off the equilibrium, it is possible that a single
platform may not have the resources to finance the necessary top-ups. If the
protection is not considered credible by the sellers, then the cartel may in
fact be sustainable.

The issue is further compounded by the fact that platforms may or may not
have the incentive to break cartels. Indeed
Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
note
that platforms may or may not benefit from breaking cartels, on account of
the following trade-off - lower prices resulting from breaking cartels cause
the market size to increase, increasing quantities sold, but also cause a
direct downward pressure on revenues. Depending on the elasticity of
revenues, platforms may or may not prefer cartelization.
23
23

23

This assumes that platform profits are a share of seller revenues. While
other pricing strategies exist, this is a relatively common model.
I argue
that this is not of material concern in either of the two possible market
structures - one where the market size increases sufficiently from the
reduction in prices resulting from breaking the cartel so that platform
profits rise, and another where it does not.

First, I claim that if the platforms have an incentive to break cartels,
they could contractually agree to jointly follow a modified version of the
two stage price drop rule (in the spirit of Corollary
1
).
The formal rule implemented on platform
j
is presented below, and each
platform
j\in\mathcal{J}
contractually agrees to implement this rule for
itself.

Definition 3

(
Two stage price drop rule for multiple platforms
) If a seller
i

reduces its price at time
\tau_{1}>\tau+1
relative to
p_{i,j,\tau+1}

and is followed by one or more sellers
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
reducing their prices on any platform in period
\tau_{2}>\tau_{1}
, then the platform
j
will provide a price top-up
\hat{p}_{i,j,t}>0

to seller
i
at any time period on or after
\tau_{2}
if it

•

sets price
p_{i,j,\tau_{1}}
on platform
j
at period
\tau_{2}
,
and

•

sets price
p_{i,j,t}\in[\min_{\tilde{\imath}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}}\min_{j}p_{\tilde{\imath},j,t-1},\max_{\tilde{\imath}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}}\max_{j}p_{\tilde{\imath},j,t-1}]
on platform
j

after period
\tau_{2}
, and

•

sells no more than
q_{i,j,\tau_{1}}
on platform
j
in any period
on or after
\tau_{2}

where
\hat{p}_{i,j,t}
satisfies
(\hat{p}_{i,j,t}+p_{i,j,t})q_{i,j,t}=p_{i,j,\tau_{1}}q_{i,j,\tau_{1}}
at any time

t\geq\tau_{2}
.

Aside from being a “per platform” version
of the basic two stage price drop rule, the main modification to the rule
involves making the top-up contingent on any “punishing” price drops, including those on other platforms.
24
24

24

While this assumes that a platform has visibility into the prices on
another, this is typically true in the settings considered in this paper.

When platforms have an incentive to break cartels, jointly committing to
follow the above rule ensures that each platform provides protection to
sellers who cheat the cartel. The cost of top-ups is also well distributed,
which can be crucial when platforms have limited budgets for top-ups. The
reasoning behind the rule remains largely the same - if there is an attempt
at forming a cartel, then there is a seller who can cheat on all the
platforms in period
\tau_{1}
, and ensure a higher profit than under the
cartel in the following periods using the top-ups if necessary.
25
25

25

A formal proof is omitted since the arguments are very similar to those in
the proof of Theorem
1
.

In the second case, that is, if the market size does not grow sufficiently
from destabilizing cartels, then neither the sellers nor the platform have
any incentive to destabilize cartels. Indeed, they benefit from the
existence of cartels. Buyers on platforms are typically not well organized,
so that in practice the responsibility of maintaining a competitive market
may fall to regulatory authorities. In such a setting, the two stage price
drop rule provides a way for regulators to destabilize cartels through
legislation. Indeed there are benefits to be obtained from legislating the
use of the two stage price drop rule across platforms. Without cartels,
consumer surplus increases, and markets operate closer to the competitive
benchmark, both of which are among the goals for a typical competition
policy. Further, this is reasonably practical as well, due to the low
informational requirements of this approach.
26
26

26

To verify whether a platform followed the two-stage price drop rule or not,
regulators only need access to the history of prices, quantities, and
top-ups (if any). Each of these should be relatively simple for the platform
to provide.
Finally, there is no loss from deploying the two stage price
drop rule in a market without cartels - the market continues to function as
it did. In contrast, existing solutions such as the DPDP method presented in

Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
affect the normal functioning of the market by
steering a majority of the demand toward a single seller even when no
cartels exist.

3
Destabilizing Cartels with Direct Interactions

3.1
Model

Having discussed price competition over differentiated goods on platforms
above, in this section, I consider a direct market for a homogeneous
product, with a buyer (the case with multiple buyers is discussed later),
and a set of sellers
\mathcal{I}
. A typical seller is denoted by
i\in\{1,2,...I\}=\mathcal{I}
. The buyer must obtain a quantity
Q
of the
product and has two ways of doing so - it can either procure the product
from the sellers, or it can produce it on its own. In general, it buys a
quantity
q
from the aforementioned sellers at a price
p
, and produces a
quantity
Q-q
itself, using a different technology. The total cost of the
quantity
Q
is given by
pq+c(Q-q)^{2}
, where
c>0
is a constant.
27
27

27

The intuition for the functional form is as follows - we assume that the
sellers have invested in a high quality production technology for this
market, and are therefore able to produce at a linear cost. The buyer on the
other hand, uses a low quality production technology (or relies on imports)
so that her costs scale quadratically with the quantity she needs to produce.
Then, given a price
p
, the buyer buys the quantity
q=Q-\frac{p}{2c}
,
which yields the following inverse demand function:
p=2cQ-2cq
.
Normalizing the price by
2c
, we obtain the classical Cournot demand
function
p=Q-q
, where
q
is the quantity produced by all the sellers
combined. Denote by
c_{i}
the (constant) marginal cost of seller
i
, and
by
q_{t}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}q_{i,t}
, the total quantity produced (and
sold) at period
t
. At each period, sellers can observe the quantities sold
by all the other sellers in the market. However, they cannot observe the
quantities being produced for the current period.

The (contemporaneous) utility of the buyer given a vector of quantities
(q_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
is given by
u_{b}(q)=-pq-c(Q-q)^{2}
. Then, the
overall utility of the buyer (at period
\tau
) is given by
u_{b,\tau}(q_{t})=-\sum_{t=\tau}^{\infty}\beta_{b}^{t-\tau}(p_{t}q_{t}+c(Q-q_{t})^{2})
, and the utility of seller
i
is given by
u_{i}=\sum_{t=\tau}^{\infty}\beta_{i}^{t-\tau}(p_{t}-c_{i})q_{i,t}
, given
p_{t}=Q-q_{t}
.

In this section, I consider the following price formation process. First,
the sellers make price quantity offers to the buyer, that is, each seller
i\in I
makes an offer to sell the quantity
q_{i}
at the price
p_{i}
to
the buyer. Since these are formal offers, if a cartel of sellers makes
offers at the same exact price to a buyer (with cartel prescribed
quantities), this leaves them vulnerable to litigation, since the buyer can
then make a strong case for collusive behavior. Therefore, it is likely that
there is some heterogeneity in the offered prices.
28
28

28

It is possible, for instance, that the cartel has some system of payouts
within itself to compensate those sellers who have to accept the lower
prices. In particular, this could take the form of a higher production quota.
Then, the buyer ascends the price offer chart, buying out the quantities
from the sellers at the lowest prices first. This leads to a rising average
price for the buyer as it climbs up the offered prices. However, as the
average price increases, the demand of the buyer also decreases, so that at
some price, the buyer no longer wishes to purchase any more of the product.
This leads to the formation of the equilibrium price in this model. Denote
this average price in period
t
as
p_{t}
.

The non-cooperative equilibrium quantities (denoted by
q_{i,t}^{nc}
) are
given by the solution to the following system of equations, which are
obtained from the first order profit maximization condition for each seller.

q_{i,t}^{nc}=\frac{Q-\sum_{j\neq i}q_{j,t}^{nc}-c_{i}}{2}

Notice that given any vector of
opposing
quantities,
q_{-i}
, the
expression above, i.e.
q_{i}=\frac{Q-\sum_{j\neq i}q_{j}-c_{i}}{2}
gives
the optimal quantity for seller
i
. Denote this as
q_{i}^{\ast}(q_{-i})
.
With this, we define the following notation. Let
\pi_{i}(q)=(Q-\sum_{j}q_{j})q_{i}-c_{i}q_{i}
denote the profit of seller
i

under the vector of quantities
q
. Also, denote by
\pi_{i}^{\ast}(q_{-i})=\pi_{i}(q_{-i},q_{i}^{\ast}(q_{-i}))
the maximum profit seller
i
can make when the other sellers produce quantities
q_{-i}
.

3.2
Cartels

Analogously to the setup described in connection to platforms earlier, a
cartel is described by the tuple
\mathcal{C}=\left\langle(q_{i}^{c})_{i\in\mathcal{I}},T,(\bar{q}_{i,t}((q_{j,\tau})_{j\in I,\tau=1,..,t-1}))_{i\in\mathcal{I},t=1,..,T}\right\rangle
, which refers to the following
strategy: If each seller has produced the cartel prescribed quantities
q_{i}^{c}
in the previous periods, then each seller continues to produce
q_{i}^{c}
. But, if any seller deviates to any other quantity, then in the
T
periods following this deviation, sellers produce the quantities
\bar{q}_{i,t}((q_{j,t})_{j\in I,t=1,..,T})
which are a function of the history of
the game since the period in which the first deviation occurred. Then, they
revert to producing
q_{i}^{c}
. That is, as earlier, I allow for
T

periods of punishment, with quantities allowed to vary as a function of the
production of other sellers. Once again, this restricts the cartel to not
prescribe time varying production quantities for the reasons detailed
earlier, but allows for a wide variety of punishment strategies. The
noncooperative strategy is also allowed, with sellers producing
q_{i}^{nc}

in each period, and effectively not punishing deviations.

In the claim below, I establish that any set of cartel suggested quantities
(q_{i}^{c})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
which reduces the quantity supplied to the
market relative to the noncooperative equilibrium must leave at least one
seller with the contemporaneous incentive to deviate to a higher quantity.

Lemma 2

For any vector of quantities
(q_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{I}}
, with
q=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}q_{i}
, if
q<q^{nc}
, then there is a
seller
i
for whom
Q-\sum_{j\neq i}q_{j}-c_{i}-2q_{i}>0
. Conversely, if
q>q^{nc}
, then there is a seller
i
for whom
Q-\sum_{j\neq i}q_{j}-c_{i}-2q_{i}<0
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
D
.

Therefore, whenever there is a cartel which strictly reduces the utility of
the buyer, there is a member of the cartel who has the incentive to ”cheat”
the cartel and deviate to increase output. In doing so, it increases its
profit over the cartel setting as well. If this seller increases its output
above the cartel prescribed quantity, then the overall quantity in the
market increases, the price decreases, and therefore the buyer is strictly
better off. Therefore, such cheating is in the interest of the buyer. Then,
it is natural to ask if the buyer could incentivize the seller to cheat. It
turns out that the answer is yes, if the buyer uses the mechanism proposed
below.

3.3
Destabilizing Mechanism

The mechanism requires the buyer to commit to the following rule at some
time
\tau>1
.

Definition 4

(
Two stage price drop rule for direct markets
) If any seller
i

increases their quantities at time
\tau_{1}>\tau
, and is followed by one
or more sellers
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
increasing
their quantities in period
\tau_{2}>\tau_{1}
, then the buyer offers to
purchase a quantity
q_{i,\tau_{1}}
from seller
i
at a price
p_{\tau_{1}}
on or after period
\tau_{2}
if
i\notin\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
.

The intuition for this rule is very similar to the version for platforms,
and continues to share some similarities with the “help the
cheater” principle from
Marx et al. 2017
. However, the
homogeneity of products allows for a considerable simplification of the
mechanism. The seller
i
is among the ones who cheat the cartel first (in
period
\tau_{1}
), and the set
\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}
is the set of
sellers who punish the cheaters. As earlier, it is possible that some
sellers who cheat the cartel at
\tau_{1}
also join in the punishments at
\tau_{2}
, but such sellers are not offered purchases by the buyer. The
buyer, by offering to purchase from seller
i
the quantity produced by it
at
\tau_{1}
, at the prevailing price in that period, offers a revenue
(and owing to the quantity limit also a profit) guarantee. This guarantee is
at a profit level higher than that under the cartel, effectively insulating
these sellers from the punishment being meted out by the cartel.

As before, the purpose of conditioning these guarantees on a two stage price
drop is to leave the buyer free of liabilities on the equilibrium path. The
rule described above creates a “race” towards the guarantee, which yields a one-stage drop to noncooperative
pricing.

The reason that this mechanism is simpler than the one used in the platforms
context is that the buyer can directly offer to buy certain quantities of
the product. A platform is not (presumably) also a consumer of the products
sold on it, and thus cannot directly protect sellers. Instead, it has to
rely on being able to make sales to the buyers on it. Another notable point
of difference between the direct market version and the platform version is
that the commitment assumption is less significant in this context. This is
because without any intervention, the buyer continues to purchase at the
cartel price, which is higher than the price at which it provides the
guarantee.
29
29

29

The guarantee is provided at
p_{\tau_{1}}
which is lower than the cartel
price due to the first stage drop owing to seller
i
(and possibly others)
cheating the cartel by increasing production.
Therefore, even
off-equilibrium, the buyer is better off than it would have been without the
intervention.

With these concepts in place, we can now state the main result of this
section.

Theorem 2

The game induced by the two stage price drop rule has a
unique subgame perfect Nash equilibrium in the class of cartel strategies
\mathcal{C}
involving each seller producing the noncooperative quantity
q_{i,t}^{nc}
in each period
t
after the rule is announced. Further, the
buyer makes no purchase offers in equilibrium.

The proof is provided in Appendix
E
.

Much in the same as vein as Theorem
1
, this result also
carries through to an environment with multiple small buyers who can
contract amongst themselves, by agreeing to provide parts of the guarantee,
so that the cheating sellers see it as a credible form of protection. This
is beneficial both on and off the equilibrium path, as discussed above. This
is in fact, closely related to the notion of
Countervailing Power
(see for instance
Loertscher and Marx 2022
and
Snyder 1996
), which
refers to the claim that mergers on one side of the market yield better
outcomes due to equalization of bargaining power. When analyzed from that
standpoint, this mechanism yields a way for the “merged” set of buyers to
operationalize
the
additional bargaining power they obtain by contracting together to
destabilize cartels.

4
Literature Review

Stigler 1964
is one of the earliest papers studying the question
of collusion formally, and lays out the fundamental challenge to practical
collusion as being secret price cutting. Also among the early literature is

Green and Porter 1984
which studies collusion under demand
uncertainty.

One of the seminal papers in the literature on countervailing power is
Snyder 1996
, which shows that a large buyer, when facing a group of sellers who
can form a cartel can make collusion harder by creating booms and busts in
the demand by the use of its inventory. Collusion is harder to sustain on a
boom since the sellers know that a bust is to follow, and are less concerned
with any punishments that the cartel may execute since the demand will be
low anyway. This method increases the required discount factor for
sustaining collusion, but may be impractical in the markets for many goods
where a sustained supply is required. In addition, such booms and busts may
cause significant loss of welfare on account of inventory costs and the
temporal preferences of agents.

Marx et al. 2017
discusses possible methods to guard against collusion for a
downstream firm when faced with suppliers who might form cartels. Among the
various methods presented is the principle of “help the
cheater”, with which the method presented in this paper
shares some similarities. The principle has other implications, such as not
revealing the identity of low bidders to the rest of the (presumed) cartel.

In
Loertscher and Marx 2022
, the authors lay out a theory for
countervailing power with incomplete information bargaining. They show that
vertical integration may not enhance (equally weighted) social surplus, but
horizontal mergers may do so, by equalizing bargaining weights between the
buyers and sellers. This is somewhat close in spirit to results in the class
of Corollary
1
where buyers cooperate to provide top-ups.

Turning to the recent literature around algorithmic collusion on platforms,
the OECD raised the concern in
OECD 2017
and
Calvano, Calzolari, Denicolo, and Pastorello 2020
showed that this is indeed possible. Using a
Q-learning approach, they established that pricing algorithms could learn to
collude by raising prices and evolving sophisticated punishment strategies
where they punish deviations for a finite number of periods and gradually
return to collusive pricing.

Johnson, Rhodes, and Wildenbeest 2020
study solutions to the problem of algorithmic
collusion when sellers on a monopoly platform engage in price competition
over differentiated products. For cartelized markets, they suggest an
algorithm for destabilizing cartels called Dynamic Price Directed Prominence
(DPDP). In essence, it involves steering demand towards a seller which
cheats the cartel using the recommendation system associated with the
platform unless the seller raises its prices in the interim or is undercut
by a significant margin. DPDP has excellent theoretical and empirical
performance, yielding marginal cost pricing in theoretical analyses and
substantial reductions in price in the Q-learning simulations. However, it
is limited to settings with homogeneous marginal cost, a monopoly platform,
and causes a loss of product variety on path, each of which somewhat limits
its applicability in practical settings. The two stage price drop rule
presented here can be applied with heterogenous marginal costs, on multiple
platforms and without any distortions on path. While some knowledge of the
cost structure is required for the Q-learning simulations, relatively coarse
knowledge suffices.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, I proposed several variations of the
two stage price
drop rule
which constitutes a mechanism to destabilize cartels on platforms
as well as in direct markets. It improves upon the existing literature in
that it causes no welfare losses on path, allows for heterogeneous marginal
costs, multiple platforms (possibly with legislative support), and requires
very little information to deploy.

There are several directions for future research. First, in practice, prices
may fluctuate slightly for various reasons, such as promotions, variations
in demand, availability of inputs etc. However, price variations owing to
cheating or the destabilization of cartels (and reversion to competitive
oligopolistic markets) are likely to dominate day to day fluctuations. An
interesting avenue for future research is to characterize the levels of
noise in prices so that the rule presented here can be applied to a given
market by requiring that the price drops be of a certain magnitude to
qualify as the first or the second stage drop. An alternate approach to this
is to consider a reporting mechanism which allows sellers to alert the
platform or the buyer that it intends to cheat the cartel, and request
protection. The major challenge in this approach would be to prevent sellers
from abusing this mechanism in order to make profits from the protection.

Second, as more computational power becomes available, it should be
interesting to see if the original variant of the two stage price drop rule
involving revenue matching is effective in the MARL framework. If this
becomes possible, then it would be possible to entirely eliminate the
requirement for estimating the marginal cost.

Appendix B
The Structure of Cartels

The specification
\mathcal{C}=\left\langle(p_{i}^{c})_{i\in\mathcal{I}},T,(\bar{p}_{i,t}((p_{j,\tau})_{j\in I,\tau=1,..,t-1}))_{i\in\mathcal{I},t=1,..,T}\right\rangle
for cartels excludes the strategic possibility of
a cartel which prescribes a time varying price profile (
p_{i,t}^{c}
for
instance). Most cartels in practice do however satisfy this restriction. For
instance,
Harrington and Skrzypacz 2011
covers several examples including the Lysine cartel
(where quantities were fixed over a certain number of periods), the Citric
Acid cartel (where market shares were fixed, effectively fixing prices in
the model discussed above), and the vitamins cartel, where (again) market
shares, and hence prices were fixed. Further, coordinating time varying
price prescriptions is likely to lead to a fair volume of communication
records, which, given antitrust laws, firms maybe unwilling to allow.
30
30

30

The Lysine cartel, for instance was successfully prosecuted owing to the
large volume of communication records that were available to the prosecutors.
Finally, erratic production schedules may be unsustainable in practice
because the typical procurement contract for inputs in a repeated production
setup is likely to involve a constant quantity over a certain number of
periods.

The specification however, does allow for time varying punishment
strategies. This is because it is a well known result in the theory of
repeated games that time varying punishment strategies can be used to
stabilize collusive arrangements which simpler repetitive punishment
strategies can not (see for instance
Abreu 1988
).
