Regularization by inexact Krylov methods with applications to blind deblurring Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Silvia Gazzola, Malena Sabaté Landman

Abstract
This paper is concerned with the regularization of large-scale discrete inverse problems by means of inexact Krylov methods. Specifically, we derive two new inexact Krylov methods that can be efficiently applied to unregularized or Tikhonov-regularized least squares problems, and we study their theoretical properties, including links with their exact counterparts and strategies to monitor the amount of inexactness. We then apply the new methods to separable nonlinear inverse problems arising in blind deblurring. In this setting inexactness stems from the uncertainty in the parameters defining the blur, which may be recovered using a variable projection method leading to an inner-outer iteration scheme (i.e., one cycle of inner iterations is performed to solve one linear deblurring subproblem for any intermediate values of the blurring parameters computed by a nonlinear least squares solver). The new inexact solvers can naturally handle varying inexact blurring parameters while solving the linear deblurring subproblems, allowing for a much reduced number of total iterations and substantial computational savings with respect to their exact counterparts.



keywords

Inexact Krylov methods, separable nonlinear inverse problems, variable projection method, Tikhonov regularization,

Introduction

Linear discrete inverse problems of the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|Ax-b\|\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad b=b_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}+e=Ax_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}+e\,,\;\quad\frac{\|e\|}{\|b_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}\|}\ll 1\,,

(1)

and where
e
is an unknown perturbation affecting the data
b
, arise in many engineering and scientific applications; see
[
4
,
5
,
18
,
17
]
. Here and in the following,
\|\cdot\|
denotes the vector 2-norm and the induced matrix 2-norm. We assume that the discretized forward operator
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, with
m\geq n
, has full column rank with singular values quickly decaying and clustering at zero, so that
A
is ill-conditioned. Although, under these assumptions, problem (
1
) has a unique solution, the ill-conditioning of
A
and the presence of the noise
e
in
b
prompt the use of some regularization to recover a meaningful approximation of
x_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}
.

In this paper we consider Tikhonov regularization, which computes

x_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}\approx x_{\lambda}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\>\|Ax-b\|^{2}\,+\,\lambda^{2}\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}\,,

(2)

where an initial estimate
x_{0}
for
x_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}
is included. Basically, Tikhonov regularization replaces the original least squares (LS) problem (
1
) by a
penalized LS problem
, where a ‘small’ value of the regularization term
\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}
, weighted by a regularization parameter
\lambda>0
, is enforced.
Prescribing a suitable value of
\lambda
is crucial to compute solutions that are neither under-regularized (
\lambda
too small) nor over-regularized (
\lambda
too big).
Equivalently, with a simple change of variable, problem (
2
) can be reformulated as

z_{\lambda}=\arg\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\>\|Az-r_{0}\|^{2}\,+\,\lambda^{2}\|z\|^{2}\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\begin{array}[]{lcl}r_{0}&=&b-Ax_{0}\\
x_{\lambda}&=&x_{0}+z_{\lambda}\end{array}.

(3)

Although (
3
) has a closed-form solution, when dealing with a large-scale and unstructured
A
, and without prior knowledge of a suitable value of
\lambda
, computing a good
z_{\lambda}
would potentially involve repeatedly applying a (matrix-free) iterative solver for LS problems, one for each considered value of
\lambda
. In this framework, many Krylov methods are successfully applied to either
(
1
) (i.e., as stand-alone solvers that regularize by early termination of the iterations), or (
3
) (in a so-called
hybrid
fashion, i.e., by combining projection onto Krylov subspaces of increasing dimensions and Tikhonov regularization, with the possibility of efficiently and adaptively choosing
\lambda
as the iterations progress); see, for instance,
[
1
,
4
,
13
,
14
]
and the references therein. Among the Krylov methods routinely used for regularization, the mathematically equivalent LSQR and CGLS methods are arguably among the the most popular ones, as their theoretical properties and practical performance are generally well-understood; see
[
16
,
20
]
.

In many situations the (discretized) forward operator
A
is not fully known and, if we assume that
A
depends on a few unknown parameters
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, with
p\ll n
, then these should be recovered replacing (
1
) by a problem of the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\|A(y)x-b\|\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad b=b_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}+e=A(y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}})x_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}+e\,.

(4)

This simple generalisation of (
1
) results in a much more difficult problem to solve: indeed, problem (
4
) is jointly nonlinear and nonconvex in
(x,y)
, so that, in particular, it may not have a unique solution. Moreover, since (
4
) is ill-posed, one should apply some regularization and consider, for instance,

z_{\lambda}=\arg\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\>\|A(y)z-r_{0}\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\|z\|^{2},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\begin{array}[]{lcl}r_{0}&=&b-A(y)x_{0}\\
x_{\lambda}&=&x_{0}+z_{\lambda}\end{array}.

(5)

The above problem generalizes (
3
); note that Tikhonov regularization is applied to
z
only, while regularization on
y
is implicitly enforced by assuming
p\ll n
(i.e., by considering a so-called reduced parameter space).
Problems like (
4
), (
5
) arise in a variety of signal and image processing tasks, such as instrument calibration
[
5
,
15
]
and super-resolution
[
3
]
, just to name a few. In this paper we are particularly interested in spatially invariant blind deblurring
[
4
]
, where
b
is a blurred and noisy image (reshaped as a vector) and
A(y)
encodes information about a parametric blur (i.e., defined by the unknown parameters
y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}
) that corrupts every pixel of the unperturbed unknown image
x_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}
(reshaped as a vector). To mitigate the complexity of problem (
4
) one can take advantage of separability, as the objective function in (
4
) is linear in
x
. For instance, the variable projection method
[
15
]
applied to (
4
) implicitly eliminates
x
, obtaining a reduced cost functional that depends only on
y
and that is optimized using a nonlinear LS solver. Note that, in a large-scale setting, to recover
x
one should still employ an iterative linear LS solver; i.e., one should adopt the strategies mentioned above for problems (
1
), (
3
).
In particular,
[
5
]
proposes a very efficient inner-outer iteration scheme that, while computing
x
for a fixed
y
using a LSQR-based hybrid method, determines a suitable regularization parameter
\lambda
on the fly;
y
is updated using a Gauss–Newton method.

The main goals of this paper are to introduce inexact Krylov methods for regularizing inverse problems, and to apply them to
compute a solution of
(
4
), (
5
). Indeed, our investigation is prompted by the fact that, when exploiting the separability of the objective function in (
4
), (
5
) and applying a traditional Krylov method to recover
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for a fixed
y\neq y_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, one is actually using an inexact Krylov method, in that one is generating the approximation subspace for
x
by performing matrix-vector products with inexact
A(y)
and
A^{T}(y)
rather than the exact
A(y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}})
and
A^{T}(y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}})
, respectively.

Starting from a reformulation of the so-called inexact Golub-Kahan decomposition
[
11
]
, we derive two new inexact Krylov solvers for LS problems (
1
)
that can be regarded as the inexact counterparts of LSQR and CGLS, respectively; also, we study bounds for the amount of inexactness,
so that exact and inexact solvers have comparable performances. We then explain how inexact LSQR and CGLS can be used in a hybrid fashion, i.e., to solve a Tikhonov-regularized LS problem (
3
). Exact hybrid methods are underpinned by a solid theory guaranteeing that first regularizing and then projecting (
3
) is equivalent to first projecting (
1
) and then regularizing; moreover, an adaptive choice of the regularization parameter is possible thanks to the shift invariance of Krylov subspaces. When deriving inexact hybrid methods, we study conditions under which the new solvers keep enjoying such properties. Finally, focussing on the variable projection method applied to (
4
), (
5
), we explain how the new inexact Krylov methods potentially allow updates of the parameters
y
as soon as a new approximation of
x
is available, i.e., at each iteration of the inexact linear solver, rather than after a suitable number of exact iterations
(as prescribed by the inner-outer schemes mentioned above). As far as specific conditions bounding the amount of inexactness are satisfied, this strategy will result in great computational savings.

Although we are aware of some research about the use of an inexact
A^{T}
in iterative regularization methods for linear inverse problems in
image deblurring
[
7
]
and computed tomography
[
10
]
, to the best of our knowledge the use of inexact Krylov methods for regularization and, in particular, for solving separable nonlinear inverse problems (
4
), (
5
), is novel.
Inexact Krylov methods are however ubiquitous in a number of numerical linear algebra tasks, typically involving well-conditioned matrices:
we refer to
[
24
]
for possible applications and a literature review, as well as for a comprehensive theoretical treatment and understanding of inexact Krylov methods.
Even if the investigations in
[
24
]
may appear similar to some of the ones proposed here, our approach is significantly different in that:
(i) we consider solvers based on the inexact Golub-Kahan
decomposition rather than on the inexact Arnoldi algorithm;
(ii) since
A
and
A(y)
are ill-conditioned, assuring that the exact and the inexact residuals are close
is not enough to guarantee that the exact and inexact solutions are close, and alternative conditions should be derived;
(iii) when considering (
4
), (
5
), the exact parameters
y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}
are unknown and, as a consequence, the amount of inexactness in applying
A(y)
and
A^{T}(y)
is unknown and some heuristics should be adopted to monitor it. Moreover, although
[
25
]
already considers inexact versions of the CG method based on either three-term recurrences or an inexact version of the Lanczos process, the new inexact CGLS method cannot be regarded as a straightforward generalization of inexact CG because of the challenges in handling inexactness in both
A
and
A^{T}
.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
reviews some background material about Krylov methods based on Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization, applied to (
1
) or to (
3
) in a hybrid fashion. Section
3
introduces the new inexact solvers based on the inexact Golub-Kahan decomposition, and develops some theory (including bounds for the amount of inexactness) that relates them to their exact counterparts. Section
4
describes how the new inexact methods can be applied to the blind deblurring problem, including computable strategies to define and bound inexactness; the results of some numerical tests are also displayed. Finally, Section
5
outlines some concluding remarks.

2
Combining Krylov methods and Tikhonov regularization

In this section we assume that there is no inexactness.
We first recall the Golub-Kahan (GK) algorithm and its relations with the symmetric Lanczos algorithm, and we then explain how both algorithms can be employed to approximate the solution of problems (
1
) and (
3
).

Starting from

u_{1}=r_{0}/\|r_{0}\|=r_{0}/\beta,\qquad\|v\|v=A^{T}u_{1}\,,\;v_{1}=v/\|v\|=v/\alpha_{1}\,,

(6)

the
i
th GK iteration computes

\beta_{i+1}u_{i+1}=Av_{i}-\alpha_{i}u_{i}\,,\qquad\alpha_{i+1}v_{i+1}=A^{T}u_{i+1}-\beta_{i+1}v_{i},\quad i=1,2,\dots,n-1,

(7)

with
\alpha_{i+1}
and
\beta_{i+1}
chosen so that
\|v_{i+1}\|=1
and
\|u_{i+1}\|=1
, respectively. In the following we assume that
\alpha_{i+1}\neq 0
or
\beta_{i+1}\neq 0
,
i=1,\dots,n-1
(i.e., GK does not break down), so that relations (
7
) are always well-defined; we also note that, in practice, GK applied to (
1
) is feasible only if
i\ll n
iterations are computed. After
k
GK iterations are performed, one can write partial matrix factorizations of the form

\displaystyle AV_{k}=U_{k+1}\bar{B}_{k}\,,\qquad A^{T}U_{k+1}=V_{k+1}B_{k+1}^{T}\,,

(8)

where
V_{k+1}=[v_{1},\dots,v_{k},v_{k+1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(k+1)}
and
U_{k+1}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{k+1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times(k+1)}
,
are matrices whose orthonormal columns span the Krylov subspaces
\mathcal{K}_{k+1}(A^{T}A,A^{T}r_{0})
and
\mathcal{K}_{k+1}(AA^{T},r_{0})
, respectively;

B_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
is the lower bidiagonal matrix having
[B_{k}]_{i,i}=\alpha_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,k+1
and
[B_{k}]_{i+1,i}=\beta_{i+1}
,
i=1,\dots,k
;
\bar{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}
is obtained by removing the last column of
B_{k+1}
.

The symmetric Lanczos
[
23
, Chapter 6]
and the GK algorithms are closely related: indeed,
multiplying the first expression in (
8
) from the left by
A^{T}
, and using again the second equation in (
8
), one obtains

\underbrace{A^{T}A}_{=:\widehat{A}}V_{k}=A^{T}U_{k+1}\bar{B}_{k}=V_{k+1}\underbrace{B_{k+1}^{T}\bar{B}_{k}}_{=:\bar{T}_{k}}=V_{k}\underbrace{\bar{B}_{k}^{T}\bar{B}_{k}}_{=:{T}_{k}}+\alpha_{k+1}\beta_{k+1}v_{k+1}e^{T}_{k},\vskip-5.69046pt

(9)

so that
V_{k}
can be regarded as the matrix generated by performing
k

A^{T}A
, with initial vector
A^{T}r_{0}
. We remark that the symmetric Lanczos algorithm is mathematically equivalent to the Arnoldi algorithm, the symmetric tridiagonal matrix
{T}_{k}
being linked to the fact that
A^{T}A
is symmetric.

LSQR is an iterative solver for (
1
) based on GK bidiagonalization (
8
). The
k
th iteration of LSQR computes

s_{k}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|\bar{B}_{k}s-\beta e_{1}\|\,,\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad(\bar{B}_{k}^{T}\bar{B}_{k})s_{k}=\bar{B}_{k}^{T}(\beta e_{1})\,,

(10)

and takes
x_{k}=x_{0}+z_{k}=x_{0}+V_{k}s_{k}
. Equivalently, using (
6
) and (
6
), LSQR computes

q_{k}=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{R}(U_{k+1}\bar{B}_{k})=\mathcal{R}(AV_{k})}\|q-r_{0}\|\,.

(11)

In other words, the
k
th iteration of LSQR projects the solution of (
1
) onto
\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
orthogonally to
\mathcal{R}(AV_{k})
. LSQR is also mathematically equivalent to CGLS, i.e., CG method applied to the normal equations associated to (
1
). Namely, (
10
) can be also expressed as

\overbrace{V_{k}^{T}(A^{T}A)V_{k}}^{=\bar{B}_{k}^{T}\bar{B}_{k}}s_{k}\quad=\overbrace{V_{k}^{T}A^{T}r_{0}}^{\bar{B}_{k}^{T}U_{k+1}^{T}r_{0}=\bar{B}_{k}^{T}\|r_{0}\|e_{1}}\,,

(12)

where equation (
9

) and (
8
) have been used to get the rightmost equalities. This essentially means that CGLS computes

q_{k}\in\mathcal{R}(A^{T}AV_{k})=\mathcal{R}(V_{k+1}\bar{T}_{k})\quad\mbox{such that}\quad\overbrace{A^{T}r_{0}}^{=:\widehat{r}_{0}}-q_{k}\perp\mathcal{R}(V_{k})\,,

(13)

i.e., CGLS projects the solution of (
1
) onto
\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
orthogonally to
\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
.

LSQR and CGLS can be adopted in a hybrid fashion to solve (
3
), too.
In this setting, assuming for now that
\lambda
is fixed, we are faced with many equivalences that involve the interplay of regularization and projection, and leverage properties of Krylov basis vectors (such as orthonormality or shift invariance). Despite the specific formulation, the
k
th iteration of all the methods computes

x_{{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}}\approx x_{\lambda,k}=x_{0}+V_{k}s_{\lambda,k}\in\mathcal{K}_{k}(A^{T}A,A^{T}r_{0})=\mathcal{K}_{k}(A^{T}A+\lambda^{2}I,A^{T}r_{0})\,.

(14)

Starting from (
3
), and exploiting (
6
) and (
8
),
we can write

s_{\lambda,k}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\|AV_{k}s-r_{0}\right\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\left\|V_{k}s\right\|^{2}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\|\bar{B}_{k}s-\beta e_{1}\right\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\left\|s\right\|^{2}\,.

(15)

Alternatively, starting from the reformulation of (
3
) as an
augmented LS problem
, and exploiting similar properties,
we can write

s_{\lambda,k}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\>\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]V_{k}s-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{0}\\
0\end{array}\right]\right\|^{2}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\>\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\bar{B}_{k}\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]s-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\beta e_{1}\\
0\end{array}\right]\right\|^{2}\,.

(16)

Obviously (
15
) and (
16
) are equivalent and, in particular, the leftmost quantities in both equations can be regarded as Tikhonov-regularized versions of the projected LS problem (

) solved by LSQR. Therefore, first regularizing (considering a penalized or augmented LS problem) and then projecting is equivalent to first projecting and then regularizing (considering a penalized or augmented LS problem, respectively). Note that, since
V_{k}
is generated with respect to
A
and
r_{0}
(i.e., it is independent of
\lambda
), one can potentially change
\lambda
at each GK iteration so that, if
\lambda=\lambda_{k}
at the
k
th iteration
of the hybrid methods (

) and (

), an instance of problems (
3
)
with

is approximated. This proves strategic in case a suitable value of
\lambda
is not known a priori (see
[
6
]
), and to adapt the amount of regularization to the

th projected problem.
Finally, we can consider the normal equations formulation associated to the Tikhonov augmented LS problem
and, similarly to (

), we compute

\displaystyle s_{\lambda,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(V_{k}^{T}(A^{T}A+\lambda^{2}I)V_{k}\right)^{-1}V_{k}^{T}(A^{T}r_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({T}_{k}+\lambda^{2}I)^{-1}\|A^{T}r_{0}\|e_{1}=\left(\bar{B}_{k}^{T}\bar{B}_{k}+\lambda^{2}I\right)^{-1}\bar{B}_{k}^{T}(\beta e_{1})\,,

where we have exploited (

) and (

).
Note that
s_{\lambda,k}

) expresses the solution to the normal equations associated to (

), so that (

) is equivalent to (

). Also in this case, by exploiting the shift-invariance of Krylov subspaces (

3
Combining inexact Krylov methods and Tikhonov regularization

In this section we first present an inexact Golub-Kahan decomposition, and we show how it can be employed to solve linear systems of the form (
1
).
We then derive a couple of strategies to combine the inexact Golub-Kahan decomposition and Tikhonov regularization. Unfortunately, only a few of the equivalences presented in Section
2
for the exact case extend to the inexact case.

3.1
Inexact Golub-Kahan (iGK) decomposition

Assume that the actions of
A
and
A^{T}
are just approximately available. Then the solution of linear systems of the form (
1
) can be efficiently approximated using methods based on the inexact Golub-Kahan (iGK) algorithm that,
at the
i
th iteration,
only uses the available actions of
(A+E_{i})
and
(A+F_{i})^{T}
.
Starting from

u_{1}=r_{0}/\|r_{0}\|=r_{0}/\beta,\quad\|v\|v=(A+F_{1})^{T}u_{1}\,,\quad v_{1}=v/\|v\|=v/[L]_{1,1}\,,

(18)

the
i
th iteration of the inexact Golub-Kahan (iGK) algorithm computes

\begin{array}[]{lll}u=(A+E_{i})v_{i},&u=(I-U_{i}U_{i}^{T})u,&u_{i+1}=u/\|u\|\,,\\
v=(A+F_{i+1})^{T}u_{i+1},&v=(I-V_{i}V_{i}^{T})v,&v_{i+1}=v/\|v\|\,,\end{array}

(19)

where
U_{i}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times i}
,
V_{i}=[v_{1},\dots,v_{i}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times i}
are matrices with orthonormal columns. Note that, if
x_{0}\neq 0
, the starting vector
r_{0}
in (
18
) may already be affected by some inexactness, as
r_{0}=b-(A+E_{0})x_{0}
; by committing a slight abuse of notation, here and in the following we will still denote such quantity by
r_{0}
, even if it may be different from the one appearing in (
3
), (
5
), and (
6
).
After
k
iGK iterations are performed, one can write partial matrix factorizations of the form

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left[(A+E_{1})v_{1},...,(A+E_{k})v_{k}\right]&=&U_{k+1}M_{k}\,,\\
\left[(A+F_{1})^{T}u_{1},...,(A+F_{k+1})^{T}u_{k+1}\right]&=&V_{k+1}L_{k+1}^{T}\,,\end{array}

(20)

where
M_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}
is upper Hessenberg with
[M_{k}]_{j,i}=u_{j}^{T}u
and
[M_{k}]_{i+1,i}=\|u\|
for
j\leq i\leq k
, and
L_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times(k+1)}
is lower triangular with
[L_{k+1}]_{i+1,j}=v_{j}^{T}v
and
[L_{k+1}]_{i+1,i+1}=\|v\|
for
j\leq i\leq k
. Note that, to impose orthogonality,
M_{k}
and
L_{k+1}
have to be considered rather than the simpler bidiagonal
B_{k}
(and its variant) as in (
8
).
The above relations can be re-written in the following even more compact form

\begin{array}[]{lcl}(A+\mathcal{E}_{k})V_{k}&=&U_{k+1}M_{k}\\
(A+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1})^{T}U_{k+1}&=&V_{k+1}L_{k+1}^{T}\end{array},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{E}_{k}&=&\sum_{i=1}^{k}E_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\\
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}&=&\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}F_{i}u_{i}u_{i}^{T}\end{array}.

(21)

Such partial decompositions involving the matrices
A
and
A^{T}
were first introduced in
[
11
]
in the framework of matrix function computations; also, the iGK decomposition bears similarities to the flexible Golub-Kahan decomposition introduced in
[
2
]
.

Similarly to relation (
9
) in the exact case, one can use (
21
) to devise an inexact relation involving the matrix
A^{T}A
by considering

\displaystyle(\overbrace{A^{T}A}^{=\widehat{A}}+\overbrace{{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1}^{T}A+A^{T}\mathcal{E}_{k}+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1}^{T}\mathcal{E}_{k}}^{=:\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}})V_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1})^{T}(A+\mathcal{E}_{k})V_{k}

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(A+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1})^{T}U_{k+1}M_{k}=V_{k+1}\overbrace{L_{k+1}^{T}M_{k}}^{=:\widehat{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{k}\bar{L}_{k}^{T}M_{k}+[\widehat{H}_{k}]_{k+1,k}v_{k+1}e_{k}^{T},

where
\bar{L}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}
is the matrix obtained by removing the last column of
L_{k+1}
. With respect to the exact case (
9
), we note that, in the fourth and fifth of the above equalities, an upper Hessenberg matrix
\widehat{H}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}
appears (instead of a symmetric tridiagonal matrix
T_{k}
). This is a consequence of the fact that
(\widehat{A}+{\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}})
is (in general) non symmetric (unless the matrices
A
,
\mathcal{E}_{k}
,
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
all commute). Moreover, while (
9
) was a particular case of the Arnoldi decomposition (applied to
\widehat{A}
), the inexact Lanczos (iLanczos) decomposition (
22
) cannot generally
be regarded as a particular case of an inexact Arnoldi (iArnoldi) decomposition (associated to
\widehat{A}
). Indeed, starting from
v_{1}^{iA}=v_{1}
as in (
18
), the
i
th iArnoldi iteration computes

v=(A+F_{i+1})^{T}(A+E_{i})v_{i}^{iA},\quad v=(I-V_{i}^{iA}(V_{i}^{iA})^{T})v,\quad v_{i+1}^{iA}=v/\|v\|\,,

where
V_{i}^{iA}=[v_{1}^{iA},\dots,v_{i}^{iA}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times i}
has orthonormal columns. After
k
iArnoldi iterations are performed, one can write a partial matrix factorization of the form

\left[(A+F_{2})^{T}(A+E_{1})v_{1}^{iA},...,(A+F_{k+1})^{T}(A+E_{k})v_{k}^{iA}\right]=V_{k+1}^{iA}H_{k}^{iA}\,,

(23)

where
H_{k}^{iA}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}
is upper Hessenberg with
[H_{k}^{iA}]_{j,i}=v_{j}^{T}v
and
[H_{k}^{iA}]_{i+1,i}=\|v\|
for
j\leq i\leq k
. More compactly,

(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{iA})V_{k}^{iA}=V_{k+1}^{iA}H_{k}^{iA},\;\mbox{where}\;\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}^{iA}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(A^{T}E_{i}+F_{i+1}^{T}A+F_{i+1}^{T}E_{i})v_{i}^{iA}(v_{i}^{iA})^{T}.

(24)

By comparing (
22
) and (
24
),
one can see that, for
j=1,\dots,k
, iLanczos computes

A^{T}Av_{j}+\underbrace{A^{T}E_{j}v_{j}+\mathcal{F}_{k+1}U_{k+1}M_{k}e_{j}}_{=A^{T}E_{j}v_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}[M_{k}]_{i,j}F_{i}^{T}u_{i}=:\,\widehat{e}_{j}}=\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}[\widehat{H}_{k}]_{j,i}v_{i}\,,

(25)

while iArnoldi computes

A^{T}Av_{j}^{iA}+A^{T}E_{j}v_{j}^{iA}+F_{j+1}^{T}Av_{j}^{iA}+F_{j+1}^{T}E_{j}v_{j}^{iA}=\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}[H_{k}^{iA}]_{j,i}v_{i}^{iA}\,.

It is clear that the two expressions above are generally different. They however coincide in specific instances, e.g., when
F_{j+1}=0
,
j=1,\dots,k
, i.e., when the matrix-vector products with
A^{T}
are computed exactly.

3.2
Linear solvers based on the iGK decomposition

We define the inexact LSQR (iLSQR) method to be an iterative solver for (
1
) that, at the

k
th iteration, computes

q_{k}=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{R}(U_{k+1}M_{k})}\|q-r_{0}\|\,.

(26)

Thanks to the orthonormality of the columns of
U_{k+1}
and (
21
), the equalities

\|M_{k}s-\beta e_{1}\|=\|U_{k+1}M_{k}s-r_{0}\|=\|(A+\mathcal{E}_{k})V_{k}s-r_{0}\|

hold, so that iLSQR equivalently computes

s_{k}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|M_{k}s-\beta e_{1}\|\,,\quad\mbox{i.e.,}\quad(M_{k}^{T}M_{k})s_{k}=M_{k}^{T}(\beta e_{1})\,,

(27)

and takes
x_{k}=x_{0}+z_{k}=x_{0}+V_{k}s_{k}
. Although (
27
) and (
26
) are formally equivalent to (
10
) and (
11
), respectively (i.e., the analogous relations written for LSQR), because of the presence of
\mathcal{E}_{k}
in the first equation in (
21
), the
k
th iLSQR iteration does not minimize the exact residual
\|r_{0}-Az\|
among the vectors
z\in\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
, and
\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
is not a Krylov subspace anymore. Note that this is analogous to what happens in the case of GMRES and inexact GMRES; see
[
24
]
for more details.

We define the inexact CGLS (iCGLS) method to be an iterative solver for (
1
) that, at the
k
th iteration, computes

q_{k}\in\mathcal{R}(V_{k+1}\widehat{H}_{k})\quad\mbox{such that}\quad(A+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1})^{T}r_{0}-q_{k}\perp\mathcal{R}(V_{k})\,.

(28)

Note that, contrarily to exact case (
13
), iCGLS does not impose an orthogonality condition on the exact normal equation residual
A^{T}r_{k}
, and additional care should be taken because
(A+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1})^{T}r_{0}=(A+F_{1})^{T}r_{0}
is also potentially affected by some errors. Equivalently, instead of imposing (
28
), one may impose

V_{k}^{T}(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k})V_{k}s_{k}=V_{k}^{T}(A+{\mathcal{F}}_{k+1})^{T}r_{0}\,,

(29)

directly, so that iCGLS solves

\bar{L}_{k}^{T}M_{k}s_{k}=[\bar{L}_{k}]_{1,1}\beta e_{1}

(30)

and takes
x_{k}=x_{0}+z_{k}=x_{0}+V_{k}s_{k}
.

We must stress that iLSQR is not equivalent to iCGLS anymore (this is evident comparing equations (
27
) and (
30
)); also, differently to the exact case,
M^{T}_{k}\beta e_{1}\neq\|A^{T}r_{0}\|e_{1}
. However, the
k
th iteration of both iLSQR and iCGLS computes

x_{k}=x_{0}+V_{k}s_{k}\,,\quad\mbox{where $V_{k}$ is defined in (\ref{eq:inex_gkb_2}) or (\ref{eq:iLanczos}).}

(31)

In the following we will introduce two different strategies to combine Tikhonov regularization and inexact Krylov methods, based on iLSQR and iCGLS, respectively.

3.3
A hybrid method based on iLSQR

A first way of combining iGK and Tikhonov regularization consists in applying the iLSQR condition (
26
)
to the augmented LS problem formulation of Tikhonov regularization (
3
) assuming, for the moment, that
\lambda
is fixed. Namely, we extend relations (
16
) to the inexact case by computing, at the
k
th iteration,

\displaystyle q_{\lambda,k}=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{R}(W_{\lambda,k})}\left\|q-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{0}\\
0\end{array}\right]\right\|,\quad\text{where}\quad W_{\lambda,k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}U_{k+1}M_{k}\\
\lambda V_{k}\end{array}\right].

Exploiting the relation

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A+\mathcal{E}_{k}\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]V_{k}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}U_{k+1}&0\\
0&V_{k}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}M_{k}\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}U_{k+1}M_{k}\\
\lambda V_{k}\end{array}\right]\,,

(37)

which is a trivial extension of (
21
), and recalling the definition of
u_{1}
in (
18
), it is easy to see that solving problem (
3.3
) is equivalent to computing
q_{\lambda,k}=W_{\lambda,k}s_{\lambda,k}
, where

\displaystyle s_{\lambda,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}M_{k}\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]s-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\beta e_{1}\\
0\end{array}\right]\right\|^{2}\!\!\!\!=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\|M_{k}s-\beta e_{1}\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\|s\|^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(M^{T}_{k}M_{k}+\lambda^{2}I)^{-1}M^{T}_{k}(\beta e_{1}).

In the following we refer to method in (
3.3
) or (
3.3
)-(
43
) as hybrid-iLSQR. Looking at the above equations, it is evident that first regularizing and then projecting (i.e., the path that we just followed) is equivalent to first projecting and then regularizing. Indeed, the same problem (
3.3
) may be obtained by first applying iLSQR to (
1
) and then regularizing the projected LS problem (
27
) (using the augmented LS formulation of Tikhonov regularization). Note that, taking inexactness into account and using relations (
21
) and (
37
), one can link (
3.3
) to the full-dimensional problem

\displaystyle s_{\lambda,k}=\arg\min_{s\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\left\|\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A+\mathcal{E}_{k}\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]V_{k}s-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{0}\\
0\end{array}\right]\right\|^{2}.

Looking at the above formulation it is evident that the optimality properties of hybrid LSQR (
15
) and hybrid-iLSQR are different, as the functional minimized by the latter is an error-corrupted version of the augmented-LS Tikhonov functional.

3.4
A hybrid method based on iCGLS

A second way of combining iGK and Tikhonov regularization consists in
extending condition (
28
) to the normal equations associated to the augmented LS problem formulation of Tikhonov regularization (
3
) assuming, for the moment, that
\lambda
is fixed. Namely, at the
k
th iteration, we impose

q_{\lambda,k}\in\mathcal{R}(W_{\lambda,k})=\mathcal{R}(V_{k+1}(\widehat{H}_{k}+\lambda^{2}\bar{I}))\,,\quad(A+\mathcal{F}_{k+1})^{T}r_{0}-q_{\lambda,k}\perp\mathcal{R}(V_{k})\,,

(49)

where
\bar{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{(k+1)\times k}
denotes the identity matrix of order
(k+1)
without its last column. Equivalently, we can apply
the iCGLS condition (
29
)
to the normal equations associated to the augmented LS problem formulation of Tikhonov regularization (
3
) assuming, for the moment, that
\lambda
is fixed. That is,
we compute

\displaystyle s_{\lambda,k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(V_{k}^{T}(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}+\lambda^{2}I)V_{k}\right)^{-1}V_{k}^{T}(A+\mathcal{F}_{k+1})^{T}r_{0}

(50)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\bar{L}_{k}^{T}M_{k}+\lambda^{2}I)^{-1}[\bar{L}_{k}]_{1,1}\beta e_{1}\,,

and then take
x_{\lambda,k}=x_{0}+V_{k}s_{\lambda,k}
. In the following we refer to the method in (
49
) or (
50
) as hybrid-iCGLS method.
Looking at equation (
50
), it is evident that applying iCGLS to the shifted normal equations (i.e., the path that we just followed) is equivalent to shifting the projected normal equations (so that, similarly to hybrid LSQR, regularization and projection are interchangeable). However note that, differently from hybrid LSQR (
17
), hybrid-iCGLS projects the error-corrupted normal equations.

3.5
Remarks about iLSQR, iCGLS, and their hybrid counterparts

As already mentioned in the previous sections, one of the upsides of using hybrid methods consists in the fact that they allow efficient and adaptive regularization parameter choice on the fly. This is essentially linked to the shift-invariance property of the approximation subspace for the solution, which is the same for LSQR-based and CGLS-based methods; see (
14
). Before considering possible parameter choice strategies for hybrid methods based on iGK, it is therefore natural to assess if shift invariance is still enjoyed by the inexact approximation subspace for the solution; see (
31
).
In the iCGLS case we can state the following.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
k
iLanczos iterations (
22
) have been performed, so that the partial decomposition (
22
) can be written. Assume that
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1,\dots,k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}
is such that
\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j}
if
i\neq j
. If
\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}
is independent of
\lambda_{i}
(or, equivalently, both
\mathcal{E}_{k}
and
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}
are independent of
\lambda_{i}
),
i=1,\dots,k
, then
the subspace
\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
is shift-invariant. Moreover, if
\mathcal{F}_{k+1}=0
and

\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{i,\tilde{\Lambda}_{k}}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}(E_{j}+\tilde{\lambda}_{j}^{2}I)v_{j}v_{j}^{T}=\mathcal{E}_{i}+V_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}^{2}V_{i}^{T},\quad\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}=\mbox{\emph{diag}}(\tilde{\lambda}_{1},\dots,\tilde{\lambda}_{i})\,,

(51)

then
the subspace
\mathcal{R}(V_{k})
is shift-invariant.

Proof.

The proof follows directly from (
22
).
Indeed, for the first statement,

(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{i})V_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}V_{i}=V_{i+1}\widehat{H}_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}V_{i+1}\bar{I}=V_{i+1}(\widehat{H}_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}\bar{I})\,,\quad i=1,\dots,k\,.

For the second statement,

\displaystyle(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{i,\tilde{\Lambda}_{k}}+\lambda_{i}^{2}I)V_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{i})V_{i}+V_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}^{2}_{i}V_{i}^{T}V_{i}+\lambda_{i}^{2}IV_{i}=V_{i+1}\widehat{H}_{i}+V_{i}\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{i}^{2}V_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V_{i+1}\left(\widehat{H}_{i}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\Lambda}_{i}^{2}\\
0\end{array}\right]+\lambda_{i}^{2}\bar{I}\right)\,,\quad i=1,\dots,k\,.

We now derive bounds on norms of relevant quantities computed by the exact and inexact solvers.
We start by studying the relationship between the exact residual
r_{k}^{e}
and the inexact residual
r_{k}
,
k=1,2,\dots
,
extending to iLSQR and iCGLS
the relations derived in
[
24
]
for inexact FOM and GMRES.
When considering iLSQR (
27
), it is natural to bound the distance between the exact and the inexact residuals as follows

\displaystyle\|r_{k}^{e}-r_{k}\|=\|r_{0}^{e}-Ax_{k}-(r_{0}-(A+\mathcal{E}_{k})x_{k})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|E_{0}x_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{k}V_{k}s_{k}\|

(53)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|E_{0}x_{0}\|+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|E_{l}\|\,|[s_{k}]_{l}|

Directly from the relation above, the following bound for the norm of exact residual can be derived

\displaystyle\|r_{k}^{e}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|r_{k}\|+\|E_{0}x_{0}\|+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|E_{l}\|\,|[s_{k}]_{l}|\,.

(54)

Note that the residuals
r_{k}
,
k=1,2\dots
are the only ones that we can assume available (and whose norms can be efficiently computed, as
\|r_{k}\|=\|M_{k}s_{k}-\beta e_{1}\|
). In the following we focus on bounds for the norm of the exact residuals only.

Similar but somewhat more complicated estimates hold when considering iCGLS, as both errors in
A
and
A^{T}
must be included; in particular, the right-hand-side vector in equation (
29
) is affected by errors both in
r_{0}
and in
A^{T}
. Indeed,

\widehat{r}_{0}=(A+F_{1})^{T}r_{0}=\underbrace{A^{T}(b-Ax_{0})}_{=:\widehat{r}_{0}^{e}}+\underbrace{F_{1}^{T}b}_{=:\widehat{b}_{0}}-\underbrace{(F_{1}^{T}A+A^{T}E_{0}+F_{1}^{T}E_{0})x_{0}}_{=:\widehat{E}_{0}x_{0}}\,.

When bounding the normal equations residual norm, one should then consider

\displaystyle\|\underbrace{\widehat{r}_{0}^{e}-\widehat{A}V_{k}s_{k}}_{=:\widehat{r}_{k}^{e}}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\widehat{r}_{0}-\widehat{b}_{0}+\widehat{E}_{0}x_{0}-(\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k})V_{k}s_{k}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k}V_{k}s_{k}\|

(55)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|[L_{k}]_{1,1}\beta e_{1}-\widehat{H}_{k}s_{k}\|+\|\widehat{b}_{0}\|+\|\widehat{E}_{0}x_{0}\|

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|A^{T}E_{j}\||[s_{k}]_{j}|+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}\left|[M_{k}]_{i,j}\right|\|F_{i}^{T}\|\right)|[s_{k}]_{j}|\,,

where the last two sums are obtained applying standard bounds to
\widehat{e}_{j}
in (
25
).

Contrarily to the well-posed case, when solving ill-posed problems using iLSQR and iCGLS, one should not expect
r_{k}^{e}
and
\widehat{r}_{k}^{e}
to be close to zero, as this would imply data overfitting (recall the discussion in Section
2
). As a consequence, one can allow more inexactness. The above relations extend to iLSQR and iCGLS used in a hybrid fashion.

When considering hybrid-iLSQR (
3.3
) with a fixed
\lambda
, let us define

r_{\lambda,k}^{e}=:\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{0}^{e}\\
0\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]V_{k}s_{\lambda,k},\quad r_{\lambda,k}=:\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r_{0}^{e}\\
0\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A+\mathcal{E}_{k}\\
\lambda I\end{array}\right]V_{k}s_{\lambda,k}\,,

so that

\displaystyle\left\|r_{\lambda,k}^{e}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|r_{\lambda,k}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathcal{E}_{k}\\
0\end{array}\right]V_{k}s_{\lambda,k}+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}E_{0}\\
0\end{array}\right]x_{0}\right\|^{2}

(60)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|r_{\lambda,k}\|^{2}+\|E_{0}x_{0}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|E_{l}\|^{2}|[s_{\lambda,k}]_{l}|^{2}\,.

In order for the exact and inexact residual norms to be close,
one should estimate the desired magnitude of
\|r_{\lambda,k}^{e}\|^{2}
. Contrarily to
\|r_{k}^{e}\|
, even running
n
iterations of the (exact) hybrid LSQR would not deliver a value of
\|r_{{}_{\lambda,k}}^{e}\|
close to zero. We have that, ideally, if
x_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}-x_{0}=:z_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}
and
e
were known, the most desirable value of the objective function in (
3
) would be

\|Az_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}-r_{0}^{e}\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\|z_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}\|^{2}=\|e\|^{2}+\lambda^{2}\|z_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}\|^{2}\,.

Now, depending on the application, estimates for
\|e\|^{2}
and
\|z_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}\|^{2}
may be available. If not, one may use a noise estimator for the former (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
) and, recalling that

\|r_{0}^{e}\|^{2}\leq\|A\|^{2}\|z_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}\|^{2}+\|e\|^{2}\quad\mbox{(directly from (\ref{eq:linsys}))},\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|r_{0}\|\leq\|r_{0}^{e}\|+\|E_{0}x_{0}\|\,,

take the lower bound

\displaystyle\|z_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|A\|^{2}}(\|r_{0}\|^{2}-\|e\|^{2}-\|E_{0}x_{0}\|^{2})

(61)

for the latter, where
\|A\|
should be estimated as well.

When considering hybrid-iCGLS (
50
) with a fixed
\lambda
, the estimates are very similar to the ones written above in the iCGLS case. Namely,

\displaystyle\|\widehat{r}_{\lambda,k}^{e}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\widehat{r}_{\lambda,k}\|+\|\widehat{b}_{0}\|+\|\widehat{E}_{0}x_{0}\|

(62)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|A^{T}E_{j}\||[s_{{}_{\lambda,k}}]_{j}|+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}\left|[M_{k}]_{i,j}\right|\|F_{i}^{T}\|\right)|[s_{{}_{\lambda,k}}]_{j}|

where
\widehat{r}_{\lambda,k}^{e}:=\widehat{r}_{0}^{e}-(\widehat{A}+\lambda^{2}I)x_{\lambda,k}
and
\widehat{r}_{\lambda,k}=\widehat{r}_{0}-((\widehat{A}+\widehat{\mathcal{E}}_{k})+\lambda^{2}I)x_{\lambda,k}
.
We should however stress that, differently from all the other estimates so far derived,
\|\widehat{r}_{\lambda,k}^{e}\|
should be close to zero when, given a suitable value of
\lambda
, a good regularized solution is computed (these are indeed the optimality conditions for problem (
3
)); therefore, a strict monitoring of the inexactness in
\widehat{r}_{\lambda,k}
may be necessary.

We conclude this section by mentioning that inequalities (
54
), (
55
), (
60
) and (
62
), being expressed with respect to the
k
th projected solutions
s_{k}
or
s_{{}_{\lambda,k}}
, cannot be straightforwardly employed when, at the
j
th iteration of the inexact solvers, one may need to bound every
\|E_{j}\|
,
1\leq j\leq k
, to guarantee that
\|r_{k}\|
,
\|\widehat{r}_{k}\|
,
\|r{{}_{\lambda,k}}\|
and
\|\widehat{r}{{}_{\lambda,k}}\|
are close enough to their exact counterparts. To allow this,
[
24
]
considers upper bounds for the magnitude of the components of the
k
th projected solution depending on the norm of the
j
th inexact residual and the smallest singular value of the
k
th projected coefficient matrix: if the latter can be easily estimated ahead of the iterations,
\|E_{j}\|
can then be adaptively bounded. This approach can be straightforwardly extended to the new methods introduced in this section.
More precisely, denoting by
\sigma_{k}(C)
the
k
th singular value of a matrix
C
, one can state that, if

\|E_{j}\|\leq\frac{\sigma_{k}(M_{k})}{k}\frac{1}{\|r_{j-1}\|}\varepsilon\quad\mbox{and}\quad\|E_{j}\|\leq\frac{(\sigma_{k}(M_{k}^{T}M_{k}+\lambda^{2}I))^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}{k}\frac{1}{\|r_{\lambda,j-1}\|}\varepsilon\,,

(63)

then the last term in the last inequality in (
54
) and (
60
), respectively, is bounded by
\varepsilon
.
Similar bounds can be derived for the iCGLS-based solvers (
55
), (
62
). Although a careful analysis of the behavior of
\sigma_{k}(M_{k})
and
\|r_{j-1}\|
still has to be performed for iLSQR, it is well known that, if the (exact) GK algorithm were adopted in the framework of (
1
), then
\sigma_{k}(M_{k})
would eventually be numerically zero, while
\|r_{j-1}\|
would eventually stabilize around
\|e\|
; see
[
12
,
13
,
19
]
. This trend is enhanced in the case of severely ill-posed problems. If such a behavior is also assumed when using iGK, the first bound in (
63
) would eventually prescribe a numerically zero
\|E_{j}\|
, while the second bound in (
63
) would be more permissive, in that
(\sigma_{k}(M_{k}^{T}M_{k}+\lambda^{2}I))^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
would eventually stabilize around
\lambda
. A numerical illustration is given in Section
4.2
.

4
Inexact Krylov methods for blind deblurring

In this section we explain how the inexact solvers presented in Section
3
can be adopted to solve separable nonlinear inverse problems of the form (
4
), which we compactly rewrite as

\displaystyle z_{\lambda}=\arg\!\!\!\!\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n},y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}g(z,y)\,,\;\mbox{where}\;\begin{array}[]{lcl}g(z,y)&=&\!\!\!\!\|F(z,y)\|^{2}\\
F(z,y)&=&\!\!\!\!\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y)z-\widetilde{r}_{0}\\
\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y)&=&\!\!\!\![\,A^{T}(y),\lambda I\,]^{T},\;\widetilde{r}_{0}=[r_{0}^{T},0^{T}]^{T}\\
x_{\lambda}&=&x_{0}+z_{\lambda}\end{array}.

In particular, we will target blind image deblurring problems using a variable projection method,
and we will display the results of some numerical tests, including comparisons with other Krylov-based approaches for blind deconvolution.

4.1
Problem formulation

Here and in the following, the unknown
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
appearing in (
1
) is a vectorialized image obtained by stacking the columns of the 2D image
X\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
, with
n=N^{2}
. The matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
models a spatially invariant blurring process, i.e., a convolution process defined assigning a point spread function (PSF) that describes the deformation undergone by each entry (pixel) of
X
, and boundary conditions that prescribe the behavior of the pixels at the boundaries of
X
. Conventionally (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
),
a PSF
P\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a sparse image with only a few nonzero pixels located at the center of
P
. In the parametric model adopted here, the entries of the PSF are assigned an analytical expression depending on some parameters
y
: such a parametric PSF is denoted by
P(y)
.
A parameter-dependent blurring matrix may be denoted by
A(P(y))
, using an alternative notation to
A(y)
appearing in
(
4
);
moreover, committing a slight abuse of notation, we may write matrix-vector products with
A
as either
A(y)x
or
A(y)X
. In the following we consider Gaussian blurs, where
y=[\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho]^{T}
and the
(i,j)
th entry of the PSF centred at pixel
(\chi_{1},\chi_{2})
reads

[P(y)]_{i,j}={c(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho)}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}i-\chi_{1}\\
j-\chi_{2}\end{array}\right]^{T}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\sigma_{1}^{2}&\rho^{2}\\
\rho^{2}&\sigma_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right]^{-1}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}i-\chi_{1}\\
j-\chi_{2}\end{array}\right]\right)\,.

(69)

Here
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
determine the spread of the Gaussian, and
\rho
determines its orientation;

c(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho)
is a scaling factor introduced so that
\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}[P(y)]_{i,j}=1
. Note that

\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}-\rho^{4}>0

(70)

should also be imposed for (
69
) to be meaningful.
An important property of the blurring matrix is that, thanks to the particular structure of a blurring matrix
A
,

A(y)X=A(y)x=A(P(y))x=A(X)P(y)\,.

(71)

When considering blind deblurring within this framework, the parameters
y=y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}
defining the blur are unknown and should be recovered alongside an approximation of
x
. Without any loss of generality, reflexive (or Neumann) boundary conditions (fixed during the iterative solver) are assumed from now on.

4.2
Solution by inexact Krylov methods and error control

The method outlined in Algorithm
1
involves an inner-outer iteration scheme, where
a hybrid-LSQR method fully runs for each value of the blurring parameters determined within the Gauss-Newton outer iterations. The basic idea leading to the use of inexact Krylov methods in the setting of blind deblurring is to allow Gauss-Newton updates of the blurring parameters at each iteration of the hybrid method used to approximate the deblurred image. This implies that the coefficient matrix for the computation of
z_{\lambda}(y)
in (
4
) is applied with varying amount of inexactness, using the hybrid-iLSQR or the hybrid-iCGLS methods (Section
3.3
and
3.4
, respectively) rather than the hybrid-LSQR.
The bounds derived in Section
3.5
should be employed to monitor the quality of the solution: when exceeding the tolerated amount of inexactness, the hybrid inexact methods should be restarted.
In the following we explain how inexactness is defined in the blind deblurring setting, and we tailor the iGK algorithm to this application; a sketch is provided in Algorithm
2
. Note that only the hybrid-iLSQR method will be considered from now on: the derivations below can be easily extended to hybrid-iCGLS, which performs very similarly to hybrid-iLSQR on the tested problems.

Defining inexactness in the setting of blind deblurring is not straightforward: since
y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}}
is unknown,
A(y_{\text{\footnotesize{{true}}}})
is unavailable and an absolute concept of error cannot be defined. Our pragmatical point of view is to consider as exact blurring matrix the latest computed approximation of
A(y)
. That is, if
j-1
iterations of Algorithm
2
(lines 3 to 9) are performed,
A(y_{j-1})
is regarded as the exact coefficient matrix for the
j
th iteration, so that

A(y_{i-1})=A(y_{j-1})+E_{i}^{j}\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad E_{i}^{j}:=A(y_{i-1})-A(y_{j-1})\,,

(76)

is the inexact matrix applied at iteration
i\leq j
. A few remarks are in order here. First of all, such a definition of inexactness is iteration-dependent, i.e., it is valid at the
j
th iteration of Algorithm
2
only, and should be updated as the iterations proceed; hence the notation
E_{i}^{j}
for the error in
A(y_{i-1})
. Second, according to (
76
), when computing the
j
th product at the
j
th iGK iteration,
E_{j}^{j}=0
: this is somewhat opposite to the situation described in
[
24
]
, where an increasing amount of inexactness is generally allowed as the iterations of the inexact solver proceed.
The inexactness defined in (
76
) can anyway be tolerated, as far as the bounds derived in Section
3.5
can be satisfied.
Finally, the definition of inexactness (
76
) well-matches with the approach adopted in Algorithm
1
: indeed, given a current value
y_{j-1}
of the blurring parameters,
E_{i}^{j}
quantifies how much the previous
y_{i-1}
,
i\leq j
, are allowed differ, so that the performance of hybrid-iLSQR with varying
y
in Algorithm
2
is similar to the performance of hybrid-LSQR with fixed
y=y_{j-1}
in Algorithm
1
.

According to the definition (
76
), after
j
iGK iterations are performed
(leading to the blurring parameter
y_{j}
),
the partial decomposition in (
21
) reads

(A(y_{j-1})+\mathcal{E}_{j}^{j})V_{j}=U_{j+1}M_{j}\,,\quad(A(y_{j-1})+{\mathcal{F}}_{j+1}^{j})^{T}U_{j+1}=V_{j+1}L_{j+1}^{T}\,,

where

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{E}_{j}^{j}&=&\sum_{i=1}^{j}E_{i}^{j}v_{i}v_{i}^{T}\\
\mathcal{F}_{j+1}^{j}&=&\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}(E_{i-1}^{j})^{T}u_{i}u_{i}^{T}\\
\end{array}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\begin{array}[]{l}E_{0}^{j}=A(y_{0})-A(y_{j-1})\\
E_{i}^{j}\;\mbox{ is as in (\ref{def:error}), $i=1,\dots,j$}\end{array}.

(77)

The specific expressions (
77
) are linked to the fact that the updated
y_{j}
is computed after both
u_{j+1}
and
v_{j+1}
are computed (in this order).

To guarantee that, at the
j
th iteration of Algorithm
2
with a fixed regularization parameter
\lambda
, the norm of the ‘exact’ residual (which would have been obtained applying hybrid-iLSQR with coefficient matrix
A(y_{j-1})
) is sufficiently close to the norm of the computed residual, the bound (
60
) should be checked, using the iteration-specific definition of
E_{i}^{j}
,
i=1,\dots,j
in (
76
): the
(j+1)
th iGK iteration is performed only if such a condition is satisfied (and condition (

) is then checked with the updated
E_{i}^{j}
,
i=1,\dots,j+1
); otherwise iGK should be restarted, possibly taking as initial guess
x_{0}
for the deblurred image the last valid approximation of

x_{0}=x_{\lambda,j-1}
, and as an initial guess
y_{0}
for the blurring parameters their last computed value, i.e.,
y_{0}=y_{j}
. Alternatively, to guarantee that ‘exact’ and computed residuals are sufficiently close, one can employ the second bound in (
63
). It should be stressed that, in the blind deblurring setting, the amount of inexactness is dictated by the Gauss-Newton updates and, therefore, cannot be adaptively set.

\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})z_{\lambda,j+1}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|\leq\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j-1})z_{\lambda,j}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|\,.

(78)

Assuming that the regularization parameter
\lambda
is fixed and using the notations

\widetilde{E}^{j}_{i}=[\,(E^{j}_{i})^{T},\,0^{T}\,]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n},\;i=0,\dots,j,\quad\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}^{j}_{j}=[\,(\mathcal{E}^{j}_{j})^{T},\,0^{T}\,]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n}\,,

where
E^{j}_{i}
and
\mathcal{E}_{i}^{j}
are defined as in (
77
), let us assume that

\|\widetilde{E}_{0}^{j+1}x_{0}\|+\|\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{j+1}^{j+1}\underbrace{V_{j}s_{\lambda,j}}_{=z_{\lambda,j}}\|\leq\widetilde{\varepsilon}\,,\quad\|\widetilde{E}_{0}^{j+1}x_{0}\|+\|\mathcal{E}_{j+1}^{j+1}\underbrace{V_{j+1}s_{\lambda,j+1}}_{=z_{\lambda,j+1}}\|\leq\widetilde{\varepsilon}\,.

(79)

Denoting by
\breve{s}_{\lambda,j+1}=[s_{\lambda,j}^{T},0]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{j+1}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})V_{j+1}s_{j+1}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|-\widetilde{\varepsilon}\leq\|(\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{j+1}^{j+1})V_{j+1}s_{j+1}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{j+1}^{j+1})V_{j+1}\breve{s}_{\lambda,j+1}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|=\|(\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})+\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{j+1}^{j+1})V_{j}s_{\lambda,j}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})V_{j}s_{\lambda,j}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|+\widetilde{\varepsilon}\leq\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j-1})V_{j}s_{\lambda,j}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|+\widetilde{\varepsilon}\,.

In the above chain of inequalities, the first one comes from the triangular inequality and (
79
), the second one comes from the hybrid-iLSQR optimality property (
3.3
), the third equality holds because
E_{k+1}^{k+1}=0
, the fourth inequality comes from the triangular inequality and (

) and, finally, the fifth inequality holds because of the Gauss-Newton step (lines 7 and 8 of Algorithm
2
, with

defined as in (

Therefore, instead of (

\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j})z_{\lambda,j+1}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|\leq\|\widetilde{A}_{\lambda}(y_{j-1})z_{\lambda,j}-\widetilde{r}_{0}\|+2\widetilde{\varepsilon}\,,

(81)

This can be achieved using a numerical optimizer, such as MATLAB’s

\gamma=1

4.3
Computational strategies

In this section we discuss some implementation details, which were just briefly mentioned in the previous sections. In particular, we focus on the computation of error bounds for controlling inexactness of the solvers, adaptive regularization parameter choice, and stopping criteria.

Computable bounds for
\|E_{j}\|

As mentioned in the previous sections and illustrated in frame (a) of Figure
4
, error control must be implemented to expect meaningful approximations when using inexact methods.
In this section we describe how bounds for the errors can be cheaply obtained when considering blurring matrices.

Under specific assumptions on the PSF and the boundary conditions, the coefficient matrix in (
1
) has a special structure that can be exploited for computing its eigendecomposition or its SVD: we refer to
[
18
, Chapter 4]
for an overview. We remark that, within the blind deconvolution framework described in Section
4.1
, such assumptions (e.g., rank-1 or symmetric PSF, circulant boundary conditions) cannot generally be made, as they will result in corrupted reconstructions; see, for instance,
[
9
]
and the references therein. In the following, and in agreement with Section
4.1
, we provide details for the reflexive boundary conditions case.

It is well known that, when the PSF is doubly symmetric and reflexive boundary conditions are imposed, the blurring matrix is a block Toeplitz-plus-Hankel matrix with Toeplitz-plus-Hankel blocks and can be diagonalized using the discrete cosine transform (DCT), which can be implemented as fast cosine transform (FCT); see
[
22
]
. More precisely, at the
j
th iteration of Algorithm
2
, one can write

A(y_{j-1})=C^{T}\Lambda_{j}C=C^{T}\text{diag}(\lambda^{(j)}_{1},...,\lambda^{(j)}_{n})C\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\lambda^{(j)}_{l}=\,[CA(y_{j-1})e_{1}]_{l}/[C]_{l,1}

and
C
is the two-dimensional orthogonal discrete cosine transform matrix. Therefore, according to the definition in (
76
), for
i\leq j
,

\displaystyle\|E_{i}^{j}\|=\|A(y_{i-1})-A(y_{j-1})\|=\|C^{T}(\Lambda_{i}-\Lambda_{j})C\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\Lambda_{i}-\Lambda_{j}\|

(82)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{k=1,\dots,n}\left|\lambda^{(i)}_{k}-\lambda^{(j)}_{k}\right|\,.

Moreover, thanks to the normalization condition for the PSF in (
69
), it follows that, in this case,
\|A(y_{i-1})\|=1
,
i\leq j
: this is useful if the bound (
60
), involving the estimate (
61
) is employed.

If the PSF is not doubly symmetric, then one can replace the blurring matrix with its optimal (in the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
) approximation obtained through cosine transformation. Namely, at the
j
th iteration of Algorithm
2
, one approximates
A(y_{j-1})
by

\bar{A}(y_{j-1})=C^{T}\bar{\Lambda}_{j}C,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\bar{\Lambda}_{j}=\mbox{diag}\left(\frac{1}{2}C(A(y_{j-1})+A^{T}(y_{j-1}))C^{T}\right)\,.

(83)

In other words, one should just take the symmetric part of the PSF and form a structured matrix with respect to it. The error associated to such optimal approximation is
\|\bar{A}(y_{j-1})-A(y_{j-1})\|_{F}^{2}=1/2\|C(A(y_{j-1})-A^{T}(y_{j-1}))C^{T}\|_{F}^{2}
, i.e.,
\bar{A}(y_{j-1})
is a good approximation of
A(y_{j-1})
if the blurring matrix (or the PSF
P(y_{j-1})
) is close to symmetric. The approximation (
83
) is typically used when devising preconditioners for image deblurring problems (
1
); see again
[
22
]
. Within framework described in Section
4.2
we propose to use approximation (
83
) to efficiently control the inexactness of the hybird-iLSQR methods, i.e., for
i\leq j
, we take

\|{E}_{i}^{j}\|\simeq\|\bar{E}_{i}^{j}\|:=\|\bar{A}(y_{i-1})-\bar{A}(y_{j-1})\|=\|\bar{\Lambda}_{i}-\bar{\Lambda}_{j}\|\,.

(84)

Parameter choice

As already remarked in
[
5
]
, being able to adaptively set the regularization parameter
\lambda
in (
4
) is of pivotal importance when performing blind deconvolution, since the amount of regularization should be linked to the amount of blur, as specified by the varying unknown blur parameters
y
.
When using inexact hybrid methods in this setting, since
y
depends on the current
\lambda
through the current approximate solution
x
, it cannot generally be guaranteed that the assumptions of Proposition
1
hold, i.e., the approximation subspace for the solution
x
may not be invariant with respect to
\lambda
. For instance, for the illustrative example described in Section
4.1
, all the possible cross products of the orthonormal basis vectors obtained after 10 hybrid-iLSQR iterations performed with different values of
\lambda
are nonzero, implying that such basis vectors span two different spaces. These quantities are displayed in frames (c) and (f) of Figure
3
.
As a consequence, although one can successfully regularize the projected inexact problem (as described below), this may not be equivalent to regularizing the exact full-dimensional problem.

If a good estimate of the magnitude of the noise
\|e\|
is available, we can apply the discrepancy principle to the projected problem (
3.3
), i.e., at the
k
th hybrid-iLSQR iteration we compute
\lambda=\lambda_{k}
such that

\|M_{k}s_{\lambda,k}-\beta e_{1}\|^{2}=\tau\|e\|^{2}\quad\mbox{where $\tau$ is a safety threshold (typically $\tau>1$, $\tau\simeq 1$)}.

We note that satisfying the above condition does not guarantee that the ‘exact’ discrepancy principle is satisfied. Indeed, similarly to (
54
), we get

\displaystyle\tau\|e\|^{2}-\varepsilon\leq\|Ax_{\lambda,k}-b\|^{2}\leq\tau\|e\|^{2}+\varepsilon\,,\quad\mbox{where}\quad\varepsilon=\|E_{0}x_{0}\|^{2}+\sum_{l=1}^{k}\|E_{l}\|^{2}|[s_{\lambda,k}]_{l}|^{2}

is controlled through the inexactness bounds derived in Section
3.5
. Recalling that
\|Ax_{\lambda,k}-b\|
is an increasing function of
\lambda
, and depending on the above bounds being quite strict or loose,
x_{\lambda,k}
may be under- or over- regularized for the original problem.

Alternatively, following
[
5
]
, we may use the weighted GCV (wGCV) criterion applied to the projected problem (
3.3
), i.e., at the
k
th hybrid-iLSQR iteration we compute

\lambda_{k}=\arg\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\frac{k\|(I-M_{k}(M_{k}^{T}M_{k}+\lambda^{2}I)^{-1}M_{k}^{T})\beta e_{1}\|^{2}}{(\mbox{trace}(I-\omega M_{k}(M_{k}^{T}M_{k}+\lambda^{2}I)^{-1}M_{k}^{T}))^{2}}\,.

We remark that other parameter choice rules typically employed within (exact) hybrid methods (see, for instance,
[
13
,
14
]
), can be adapted to work with inexactness; moreover, other strategies that rely on structured approximations of the blurring matrix can be exploited, too (as suggested by
[
6
]
).

Stopping criteria

When solving the blind deconvolution problem, one is recovering the unknown sharp image
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as well as the unknown blurring parameters
y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
: for this reason, effective stopping criteria should be devised, based on the behavior of both variables. Moreover, when inexact methods are applied as described in Section
4.2
, the effect of the errors in the estimated blurring matrix has to be considered: indeed, as specified in line 3 of Algorithm
2
, one should restart the hybrid-iLSQR method as soon as the difference between exact and inexact residual (bounded as in (
60
) or (
63
)) exceeds a pre-specified or adaptively estimated tolerance
\varepsilon
.
Looking at the progress of both
x
and
y
it would be natural to stop the iterations of Algorithms
2
as soon as the relative gradient norm
\|\nabla_{y}h(y)\|/\|\nabla_{y}h(y_{0})\|
of the objective function is approximately zero, which means that a stationary point for the objective function
h
(defined in (
73
)) has been reached.
In addition to this, one may monitor the (relative) stabilization of some relevant quantities, e.g., stop when

\frac{|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{k-1}|}{\lambda_{k-1}}\leq\theta_{1}\,,\quad\frac{\|x_{\lambda,k}-x_{\lambda,k-1}\|}{\|x_{\lambda,k-1}\|}\leq\theta_{2}\,,\quad\frac{\|y_{k}-y_{k-1}\|}{\|y_{k-1}\|}\leq\theta_{3}\,,\quad k=2,3,\dots,

where
\theta_{1},\,\theta_{2},\,\theta_{3}>0
are user-specified tolerances. If wGCV is employed to set
\lambda
, it can be also (simultaneously) used as a stopping criterion; we refer to
[
5
]
for additional details.

Further comments on hybrid-iLSQR for the illustrative test problem in Figure
1

We conclude this section by providing some comments about the performance of the hybrid-iLSQR method, especially in comparison with the well-established Algorithm
1
, which is implemented with ‘cold’ restarts (i.e., taking
x_{0}=0
at line 10); Algorithm
2
is instead implemented with ‘warm’ restarts, i.e., taking
x_{0}=x_{\lambda,k}
at line 10. Note that, to enforce that
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
(and
\rho=0
) in (
69
) and keep the illustrative example simple, the solvers are coded in such a way that only one blurring parameter, i.e.,
y=\sigma_{1}
, has to be computed. Looking at Figures
4
and
5
, it is evident that both Algorithms
1
and
2
eventually compute reconstructions of the same quality, as the values of the relative errors and the blurring parameter are quite similar; in particular, since
\sigma_{1}=2.5
,
y
is better approximated using Algorithm
2
). All the graphs in Figure
4
display the behavior of the methods versus the total number of iterations. In particular, the first inner loops of Algorithm
1
are affected by the so-called semi-convergence phenomenon (i.e., permanent increase of the error after only a few iterations): this is evident looking at frame (b), and can probably be mitigated by a more accurate tuning of the inner stopping criteria; nevertheless, the values at the outer iterations are generally decreasing. Some oscillations in the reconstruction quality also affect Algorithm
2
implemented with the wGCV criterion, while we note that, for this test problem, the behavior versus the number of iterations seems more stable when a fixed regularization parameter is employed (see Figure
1
, frames (c) and (f)). Algorithm
2
is implemented with error control as described in (
82
): note that the FCT-based decomposition appearing therein is exact for this test problem, as
\sigma_{1}=\sigma_{2}
is enforced at each iteration, implying that the PSF is doubly symmetric. Finally, we remark that the performance of both Algorithms
1
and
2
depends on the initial guess for the blurring parameters (for the examples shown here,
y_{0}=7
).

The cost of
k
iterations of Algorithms
1
and
2
is comparable when
k\ll n
. Indeed, both algorithms have to compute
k
matrix-vector products with
A
and
A^{T}
to generate the approximation subspace for
x
: while this in general costs
O(kn^{2})
flops, it can be reduced to
O(kn\log n)
if additional assumptions on the PSF (and the boundary conditions) hold; see
[
18
, Chapter 4]
for an overview. Generating an orthonormal basis for the solution subspace amounts to
O(n)
flops for Algorithm
1
(thanks to short recurrences) and
O(k^{2}n)
flops for Algorithm
2
(because of full orthonormalization). Solving the projected problem costs
O(k)
flops for Algorithm
1
(exploiting the bidiagonal structure of the matrix in (
15
)) and
O(k^{2})
for Algorithm
2
. Following the startegy in
[
5
]
, the cost of updating the blurring parameters for both algorithms amounts to
O(pn)
flops for computing the Jacobian (
75
) and
O(p^{2})
for performing a Gauss-Newton step (
81
): these are negligible if
p\ll n
.

5
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper we introduced and analysed the new iLSQR and iCGLS methods: these are inexact Krylov methods based on the inexact Golub-Kahan decomposition that, when used as purely iterative methods, or in combination with Tikhonov regularization, can be efficiently employed to regularize large-scale ill-posed problems, provided that the amount of inexactness is monitored at each iteration. We tested the new methods on separable nonlinear inverse problems arising in blind deblurring, handled with a variable projection approach.

Future work will be performed with the goals of: (i) deriving new inexact solvers other than iLSQR and iCGLS, combined with methods other than standard form Tikhonov; (ii) handling nonlinear separable inverse problems other than blind deblurring. Regarding (i): still leveraging the inexact Golub-Kahan decomposition, one may devise an inexact version of LSMR, similarly to what was done for flexible solvers; see
[
2
]
. Alternatively, one may employ the inexact GMRES and FOM solvers based on the inexact Arnoldi decomposition described in
[
24
]
. Moreover, one may consider the combination of inexact methods and general-form Tikhonov regularization, where the penality term
\lambda^{2}\|Lz\|_{2}^{2}
replaces
\lambda^{2}\|z\|_{2}^{2}
in (
3
), and where the regularization matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}\times n}
enforces some prior information about the solution.
Regarding (ii): the survey paper
[
15
]
describes a number of applications where the variable projection method is routinely used and that, therefore, may be potentially adapted to work with inexact Krylov methods. These include many inverse problems, such as magnetic resonance imaging in medicine, superresolution of images, instrumental calibration, but also machine learning tasks such as the training of neural networks.
