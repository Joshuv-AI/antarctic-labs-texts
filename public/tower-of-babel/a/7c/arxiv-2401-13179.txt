Abstract
Forecasting volatility and quantiles of financial returns is essential for accurately measuring financial tail risks, such as value-at-risk and expected shortfall. The critical elements in these forecasts involve understanding the distribution of financial returns and accurately estimating volatility. This paper introduces an advancement to the traditional stochastic volatility model, termed the realized stochastic volatility model, which integrates realized volatility as a precise estimator of volatility. To capture the well-known characteristics of return distribution, namely skewness and heavy tails, we incorporate three types of skew- t t distributions. Among these, two distributions include the skew-normal feature, offering enhanced flexibility in modeling the return distribution. We employ a Bayesian estimation approach using the Markov chain Monte Carlo method and apply it to major stock indices. Our empirical analysis, utilizing data from US and Japanese stock indices, indicates that the inclusion of both skewness and heavy tails in daily returns significantly improves the accuracy of volatility and quantile forecasts.



Keywords:
Bayesian estimation, Markov chain Monte Carlo, Realized volatility, Skew-t distribution, Stochastic volatility

1
Introduction

Financial volatility, defined as the standard deviation or variance of asset returns, exhibits stochastic variation over time, making its accurate forecasting crucial for financial risk management. Historically, time-varying volatility estimation and forecasting relied on two classes of time-series models using financial returns: the generalized autoregressive conditional heteroskedasticity (GARCH) methodologies, as per
Engle 1982
and
Bollerslev 1986
, and the stochastic volatility (SV) model, introduced by
Taylor 1994
. These models, known for capturing volatility clustering and high volatility persistence, have evolved to accommodate volatility asymmetry or the leverage effect – the observed negative correlation between today’s return and tomorrow’s volatility in stock markets. One notable development is the exponential GARCH (EGARCH) model by
Nelson 1991
.

In more recent years, realized volatility (RV) has emerged as a predominant method for volatility estimation, replacing traditional models. RV, computed daily as the sum of squared intraday returns, is thoroughly reviewed by
Andersen and Benzoni 2009
and
McAleer and Medeiros 2008
. To model RV dynamics, various studies, such as
Andersen et al. 2003
, have shown that daily RV may follow a long-memory process, leading to the use of autoregressive fractionally integrated moving average (ARFIMA) models (refer to
Beran 1994
for a detailed discussion on long-memory and ARFIMA models). Additionally, the heterogeneous autoregressive (HAR) model, proposed by
Corsi 2009
, is widely used for modeling the dynamics of RV. Although not a long-memory model per se, the HAR model effectively approximates long-memory processes.

Despite the improved volatility forecasts offered by ARFIMA and HAR models over GARCH and SV models, RV is susceptible to biases from market microstructure noise and non-trading hours. Techniques such as the multiscale estimators by
Zhang et al. 2005
and
lanzhang_efficient_2006
, the pre-averaging approach by
Jacod et al. 2009
, and the realized kernel (RK) method by
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. 2008
;
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. 2009
have been developed to mitigate these biases. For further details, see
Aït-sahalia and Mykland 2009
,
Ubukata and Watanabe 2014
, and
Liu et al. 2015
.

Addressing the bias in RV, two hybrid model classes have been introduced: the realized stochastic volatility (RSV) model, as proposed by
Takahashi et al. 2009
,
Dobrev and Szerszen 2010
, and
Koopman and Scharth 2013
, and the realized GARCH (RGARCH) and realized EGARCH (REGARCH) models, introduced by
Hansen et al. 2012
and
Hansen and Huang 2016
, respectively. Although previous studies have compared the volatility forecasting abilities of these models within their respective classes, a comprehensive cross-class comparison has been limited. Notable exceptions are
Takahashi et al. 2021
and
Takahashi et al. 2023
, which evaluate the forecasting abilities of various models, including SV, EGARCH, RSV, and REGARCH models.

In addition to volatility forecasting, accurately predicting financial tail risks, such as value-at-risk (VaR) and expected shortfall (ES), is essential. Financial return distributions often exhibit heavier tails and more peakedness than the normal distribution, a phenomenon known as leptokurtosis. While time-varying volatility partly explains this heavy-tailed nature, the conditional return distribution on volatility may still exhibit leptokurtic characteristics and skewness. Consequently, various skew Student’s
t
distributions have been incorporated into volatility models
(
Abanto-Valle et al. 2015
;
Kobayashi 2016
)
. Notably, the generalized hyperbolic (GH) skew Student’s
t
distribution, as discussed in
Aas and Haff 2006
, has been applied to both SV
(
Nakajima and Omori 2012
;
Leão et al. 2017
)
and RSV models
(
Trojan 2013
;
Nugroho and Morimoto 2014
;
Nugroho and Morimoto 2016
;
Takahashi et al. 2016
)
.

Building on this framework, the RSV model is extended to include three types of skew-
t
distributions, and a Bayesian estimation scheme using a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method is developed. These skew-
t
distributions are those proposed by
Azzalini 1985
and
Fernández and Steel 1995
, along with the GH skew-
t
distribution. In our empirical analysis using the Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) and the Nikkei 225 (N225) data, we evaluate the volatility, VaR, and ES forecasts of RSV models as well as SV, EGARCH, and REGARCH models. Forecasts are assessed using mean squared error and quasi likelihood loss functions for volatility, and the joint loss function proposed by
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
, with specifications suggested by
Patton et al. 2019
, for VaR and ES. Implementing the predictive ability test by
Giacomini and White 2006
and the model confidence set by
Hansen et al. 2011
, our results indicate that RV significantly improves forecasting performance. The RSV models, especially those incorporating skew-
t
distributions, demonstrate superior performance, corroborating and extending findings from previous studies.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces the basic SV model and the RSV models, which incorporate skew-
t
distributions, along with a brief overview of RV. Section
3
delves into the Bayesian estimation methodology, utilizing MCMC simulations, and discusses various measures for evaluating forecast performance. Section
4
presents an empirical study employing financial stock indices data. Finally, Section
5
provides a conclusion, summarizing key findings and implications of the study.

2
Model

2.1
Stochastic Volatility Model

First, let’s define the daily return of an asset as

y_{t}=\log p_{t}-\log p_{t-1},

where
p_{t}
represents the closing price on day
t
. We further define the volatility as a variance of
y_{t}
as

\text{E}[y_{t}^{2}]=\sigma_{t}^{2}=\exp(h_{t}).

The term
h_{t}
denotes the logarithm of volatility. This logarithmic volatility,
h_{t}
, is considered a latent variable. It is presumed to follow a stationary first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) process characterized by the autoregressive parameter
\phi
and a mean of
\mu
. Additionally, the absolute value of
\phi
should be less than one to ensure stationarity. With
h_{t}
given at time
t
, the SV model can be detailed as:

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{t}\exp\left(\frac{h_{t}}{2}\right),\quad t=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

\displaystyle h_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu+\phi(h_{t}-\mu)+\eta_{t},\quad t=1,\ldots,n-1,\quad|\phi|<1,

(2)

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle\sim N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}{1-\phi^{2}}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(0,1),

(4)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}|\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(\rho\sigma_{\eta}\epsilon_{t},(1-\rho^{2})\sigma_{\eta}^{2}).

(5)

Here, we assume a stationary distribution for the initial log latent volatility,
h_{1}
. The AR(1) process for
h_{t}
is intended to describe volatility clustering. This is the phenomenon where significant price fluctuations tend to occur in close succession. The disturbance vector
(\epsilon_{t},\eta_{t})^{\prime}
is believed to obey a bivariate normal distribution with a correlation
\rho
between
\epsilon_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
(or equivalently, between
y_{t}
and
h_{t+1}
). A negative correlation, where
\rho<0
, indicates an asymmetry or a leverage effect. This suggests that a decline in
y_{t}
is often followed by an increase in
h_{t+1}
.

2.2
Realized Volatility

In the SV model, the volatility
\exp(h_{t})
is considered a latent variable since
h_{t}
is unobserved. Recently, due to the availability of high-frequency data, RV has been proposed as an estimator for true volatility using intraday data. We first define the true volatility at time
t
. Let
p(s)
denote the logarithm of the price of the asset at time
s
, and assume it follows the continuous time diffusion process given by

dp(s)=\mu(s)ds+\sigma(s)dW(s),

(6)

where
\mu(s)
,
\sigma^{2}(s)
, and
W(s)
represent the drift, the volatility, and the Wiener process, respectively. The true volatility for day
t
is defined as

IV_{t}=\int_{t}^{t+1}\sigma^{2}(s)ds,

(7)

which is also referred to as the integrated volatility. Suppose we have
n
intraday observations for day
t
, given by
\left\{r_{t},r_{t+1/n},\ldots,r_{t+(n-1)/n}\right\}
. Then the RV, as an estimator of
IV_{t}
, is defined by

RV_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}r_{t+i/n}^{2}.

(8)

Under certain conditions, it can be shown that
RV_{t}
converges to
IV_{t}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. However, these conditions are often not satisfied in practice with high-frequency data, resulting in a bias in the estimator.

One source of this bias arises from non-trading hours during which we lack intraday observations. If we ignore the non-trading hours to compute the RV, we underestimate the true volatility for a whole day (24 hours). To address the non-trading hours,
Hansen and Lunde 2005
proposed adjusting
RV_{t}
as follows:

\displaystyle RV_{t}^{HL}=c_{HL}RV_{t},\quad c_{HL}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(y_{t}-\overline{y})^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}RV_{t}}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}y_{t},

(9)

where
RV_{t}
represents the RV when non-trading hours are ignored. The average of
RV_{t}^{HL}
is set equal to the sample variance of the daily returns.

Another source of bias is the microstructure noise. The actual asset prices are observed with microstructure noise, which results in autocorrelations in the asset returns and violates these assumptions. The effect of microstructure noise is known to become larger as the time interval between observed prices becomes smaller. The optimal time intervals to compute the RV have been investigated in the literature
(
Aït-Sahalia et al. 2005
;
Bandi and Russell 2006
;
Bandi and Russell 2008
;
Liu et al. 2015
)
. Alternative estimators, such as the two or multi-scale estimator
(
Zhang et al. 2005
;
Zhang 2006
)
and the preaveraging estimator
(
Jacod et al. 2009
)
, have also been proposed. In the empirical study in Section
4
, we use the RK
(
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. 2008
)
as an unbiased volatility estimator. The RK for day
t
is defined as

\displaystyle RK_{t}=\sum_{h=-H}^{H}k\left(\frac{h}{H+1}\right)\gamma_{h},

(10)

where
H
is the bandwidth,
k(\cdot)\in[0,1]
is a non-stochastic weight function, and

\displaystyle\gamma_{h}=\sum_{i=|h|+1}^{m}r_{t-1+i/m}r_{t-1+(i-|h|)/m}.

(11)

For the choice of
k(\cdot)
,
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. 2009
have suggested the Parzen kernel function and provided a detailed procedure for the choice of
H
.

Alternatively, these biases can be incorporated into two classes of hybrid models: the RSV and RGARCH models. In particular, the RSV model proposed by
Takahashi et al. 2009
adjusts the bias of the RV by estimating the model parameter as discussed in Section
2.3
.

2.3
Realized Stochastic Volatility Model

Takahashi et al. 2009
introduced an additional source of information regarding the true volatility. Besides the measurement equation for the daily return
y_{t}
, they also considered that of the log RV,
x_{t}=\log RV_{t}
. Let
h_{t}
denote the logarithm of the true volatility,
h_{t}=\log IV_{t}
. The RSV model is defined as follows:

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=\xi+h_{t}+u_{t},\quad t=1,\ldots,n,

(12)

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{t}\exp\left(\frac{h_{t}}{2}\right),\quad t=1,\ldots,n,

(13)

\displaystyle h_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mu+\phi(h_{t}-\mu)+\eta_{t},\quad t=1,\ldots,n-1,\quad|\phi|<1,

(14)

\displaystyle h_{1}

\displaystyle\sim N\left(\mu,\frac{\sigma_{\eta}^{2}}{1-\phi^{2}}\right),

(15)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(0,\sigma_{u}^{2}),

(16)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(0,1),

(17)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}|\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(\rho\sigma_{\eta}\epsilon_{t},(1-\rho^{2})\sigma_{\eta}^{2}),

(18)

where
u_{t}
is assumed to be independent of
(\epsilon_{t},\eta_{t})
for simplicity. In general, equation (
12
) could be replaced by

x_{t}=\xi+\psi h_{t}+u_{t}.

However, empirical studies have found that this extension does not improve the forecasting performance of the volatilities, leading to the assumption
\psi=1
as in
Takahashi et al. 2009
.

The parameter
\xi
serves as a bias correction factor in
\log RV_{t}
, where
\xi=0
implies that there is no bias. Ignoring the non-trading hours while computing the RV leads to an underestimation of
IV_{t}
, expecting a negative bias.
Hansen and Lunde 2006
demonstrated that microstructure noise may induce either a positive or negative bias. Therefore,
\xi>0
if the positive bias caused by microstructure noise dominates the negative bias from ignoring non-trading hours, and
\xi<0
otherwise.

The distribution of
\epsilon_{t}
corresponds to the return distribution conditional on the effect of the time-varying volatility. Although the inclusion of stochastic volatility
h_{t}
captures the heavy tails of financial returns, the distribution of
\epsilon_{t}
can be further assumed to have heavy tails, such as a Student’s
t
distribution. Moreover, financial returns may also exhibit skewness. The excess kurtosis and negative skewness can be accounted for by adopting an asymmetric heavy-tailed distribution for daily returns.

For the SV model,
Nakajima and Omori 2012
and
Leão et al. 2017
adapted the GH skew Student’s
t
distribution proposed by
Aas and Haff 2006
. Additionally,
Abanto-Valle et al. 2015
employed the skew Student’s
t
distribution proposed by
Azzalini and Capitanio 2003
, while
Kobayashi 2016
utilized the skew exponential power distribution. Furthermore,
Steel 1998
extended the SV model using the method proposed by
Fernández and Steel 1995
. For the RSV model,
Trojan 2013
,
Nugroho and Morimoto 2014
;
Nugroho and Morimoto 2016
, and
Takahashi et al. 2016
adapted the GH skew Student’s
t
distribution.
In the following, we extend the RSV model in equations (
12
)–(
18
), henceforth referred to as the RSV-N model, by employing such distributions for
\epsilon_{t}
.

2.4
RSV Model with Student’s
t
Distribution

The RSV model, when augmented with the Student’s
t
distribution, will henceforth be referred to as the RSV-T model. This model is derived by modifying the distributions of
\epsilon_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
as described in equations (
17
)–(
18
), to:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle=z_{t}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\mu_{\lambda}}},

(19)

\displaystyle z_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(0,1),

(20)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}|z_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(\rho\sigma_{\eta}z_{t},(1-\rho^{2})\sigma_{\eta}^{2}),

(21)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}IG\left(\frac{\nu}{2},\frac{\nu}{2}\right),\quad\mu_{\lambda}=E[\lambda_{t}]=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2},\quad\nu>2.

(22)

The parameter
\nu
is the degrees of freedom, which governs the excess kurtosis not captured by the stochastic volatility
h_{t}
.
As
\nu
approaches infinity, the model reverts to the RSV-N model described in equations (
12
)–(
18
).
It’s worth noting that the correlation between
\epsilon_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
now becomes a function of both
\rho
and
\nu
, expressed as:

\displaystyle\text{Corr}[\epsilon_{t},\eta_{t}]=\frac{E\left[\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\right]}{\sqrt{\mu_{\lambda}}}\rho,

(23)

where

\displaystyle E\left[\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\right]=\sqrt{\frac{\nu}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu-m}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)}.

(24)

2.5
RSV Model with GH Skew-
t
Distribution

Takahashi et al. 2016
extend the RSV-N model delineated in (
12
)–(
18
) by incorporating the GH skew-
t
distribution, hereafter referred to as the RSV-GH-ST model. This model is attained by substituting
\epsilon_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
in (
17
)–(
18
) with the ensuing mixture representation:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta(\lambda_{t}-\mu_{\lambda})+\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}z_{t}}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}+\mu_{\lambda}}},

(25)

\displaystyle z_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(0,1),

(26)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}|z_{t}

\displaystyle\sim N(\rho\sigma_{\eta}z_{t},(1-\rho^{2})\sigma_{\eta}^{2}),

(27)

where
\lambda_{t}
and
\mu_{\lambda}
are defined as in (
22
), and

\displaystyle\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}=V[\lambda_{t}]=E\left[\lambda_{t}^{2}\right]-\{E[\lambda_{t}]\}^{2}=\frac{2\nu^{2}}{(\nu-2)^{2}(\nu-4)},\quad\nu>4.

(28)

The term
\sqrt{\beta^{2}\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}+\mu_{\lambda}}
standardizes the return ensuring that the variance of the return remains
\exp(h_{t})
.
It is noteworthy that the correlation between
\epsilon_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
is now a function of
\rho
,
\nu
, and
\beta
, expressed by:

\displaystyle\text{Corr}[\epsilon_{t},\eta_{t}]=\frac{E\left[\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\right]}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}\sigma_{\lambda}^{2}+\mu_{\lambda}}}\rho,

(29)

where
E\left[\sqrt{\lambda_{t}}\right]
is specified in (
24
).
When
\beta=0
, this model reverts to the RSV-T model.

The skewness and tail thickness of
\epsilon_{t}
are concurrently determined by the parameter values of
\beta
and
\nu
. To exhibit how the distribution shape varies with the values, we render the simulated density function of
\epsilon_{t}
in Figure
1
. We discern from (i) of the figure that a lower value of
\beta
yields a more negative or left-skewed distribution with heavier tails. As stipulated,
\beta=0
corresponds to a symmetric Student’s
t
density. Conversely, from (ii), the density appears less skewed and has lighter tails as
\nu
increases. Additionally, as
\nu\to\infty
or
\lambda_{t}=1
for all
t
, the distribution morphs into a normal distribution regardless of the value of
\beta
. Therefore,
\nu
and
\beta
can be interpreted as parameters regarding the tail thickness and skewness, respectively, albeit the overall shape of the distribution is determined by their joint values.

\displaystyle p_{T}(w|\gamma,\nu)=\frac{2}{\gamma+\gamma^{-1}}\left(f_{T}\left(\frac{w}{\gamma}\bigg|\nu\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{w\geq 0\right\}+f_{T}\left(\gamma w|\nu\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{w<0\right\}\right),\quad\gamma>0,

\displaystyle=E[w]=M_{1}(\gamma-\gamma^{-1}),

\sigma_{*}^{2}

\gamma
and
\nu
.
To standardize the density, we let
w_{*}=(w-\mu_{*})/\sigma_{*}
. Subsequently, we have
E[z]=0
,
V[z]=E[z^{2}]=1
, and its probability density function is

\displaystyle q_{T}(w_{*}|\gamma,\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sigma_{*}}{\gamma+\gamma^{-1}}\left(f_{T}\left(\frac{\sigma_{*}w_{*}+\mu_{*}}{\gamma}\bigg|\nu\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{w_{*}\geq-\frac{\mu_{*}}{\sigma_{*}}\right\}\right.

(45)

\displaystyle\hskip 55.00008pt\left.+f_{T}\left(\gamma(\sigma_{*}w_{*}+\mu_{*})\bigg|\nu\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{w_{*}<-\frac{\mu_{*}}{\sigma_{*}}\right\}\right)

(46)

Thus,

is obtained as the standardized FS skew-
t
density.
By replacing

with the standard normal density

.

Now, by employing the FS skew-

and
\eta_{t}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{t}\sim q_{T}(\epsilon_{t}|\gamma,\nu),

(47)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}|\epsilon_{t}\sim N(\rho\sigma_{\eta}\epsilon_{t},(1-\rho^{2})\sigma_{\eta}^{2}),

(48)

where
q_{T}(\cdot|\gamma,\nu)
is the standardized FS skew-
t
density defined in (
46
).
By replacing
q_{T}(\cdot|\gamma,\nu)
with
q_{N}(\cdot|\gamma)
, we derive the RSV model with skew normal distribution, referred to as the RSV-FS-SN henceforth.
Note that
\rho
symbolizes the correlation between
\epsilon_{t}
and
\eta_{t}
, akin to the RSV-N model.

The parameter

When

Bayesian Estimation Methodology

Each of the RSV models defined in Section
2
is characterized as a nonlinear Gaussian state space model with numerous latent variables. This complexity makes the evaluation of the likelihood function, given the parameters, a challenging task, as is the implementation of maximum likelihood estimation. To circumvent these challenges, we adopt a Bayesian approach for parameter estimation using MCMC simulation, as has been done in previous literature.

For the prior distribution of
(\mu,\phi,\sigma_{\eta}^{2},\rho,\xi,\sigma_{u}^{2})

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\sim N(m_{\mu},s_{\mu}^{2}),\quad\frac{\phi+1}{2}\sim B(a_{\phi 0},b_{\phi 0}),

(49)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\rho+1}{2}

\displaystyle\sim B(a_{\rho 0},b_{\rho 0}),\quad\sigma_{\eta}^{2}\sim IG\left(\frac{n_{\eta}}{2},\frac{S_{\eta}}{2}\right)

(50)

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\sim N(m_{\xi},s_{\xi}^{2}),\quad\sigma_{u}^{2}\sim IG\left(\frac{n_{u}}{2},\frac{S_{u}}{2}\right)

(51)

3.3
One-day-ahead Forecast

To obtain one-day-ahead forecasts of financial returns and volatilities, we utilize the predictive distribution within each state-space model.
Let
\bm{\theta}^{(i)}
and
\bm{h}^{(i)}
represent the
i
th sample of parameters and latent log-volatilities in the MCMC simulation, respectively.
We can then generate the one-step-ahead predictive samples as follows.

1.

Generate
h_{n+1}^{(i)}|\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{h}^{(i)},\bm{\theta}^{(i)}\sim N(\mu_{n+1}^{(i)},(\sigma_{n+1}^{(i)})^{2})
where

\displaystyle\mu_{n+1}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\mu^{(i)}+\phi^{(i)}h_{n}^{(i)}-\mu^{(i)}+\rho^{(i)}\sigma_{\eta}^{(i)}y_{n}\exp\left(-\frac{h_{n}^{(i)}}{2}\right),

(54)

\displaystyle(\sigma_{n+1}^{(i)})^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-(\rho^{(i)})^{2})(\sigma_{\eta}^{(i)})^{2}.

(55)

2.

Generate
\epsilon_{n+1}^{(i)}
from the distribution corresponding to the chosen RSV model given
\bm{\theta}^{(i)}
and
\bm{h}^{(i)}
.

3.

Compute
y_{n+1}^{(i)}
as

\displaystyle y_{n+1}^{(i)}=\epsilon_{n+1}^{(i)}\exp\left(-\frac{h_{n+1}^{(i)}}{2}\right).

(56)

By repeating the above procedure for
M
times, we obtain the generated samples
\{h_{n+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and
\{y_{n+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
.

One-day-ahead quantile forecasts, such as VaR and ES, can be derived from the distribution of
\{y_{n+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
for each model.
The one-day-ahead VaR forecasts with probability
\alpha
at time
n+1
, denoted by
\mbox{VaR}_{n+1}(\alpha)
, are defined as

\displaystyle\mbox{Pr}(y_{n+1}<\mbox{VaR}_{n+1}(\alpha)|\mathcal{I}_{n})=\alpha,

(57)

where
\mathcal{I}_{n}
denotes the information set available at time
n
.
The corresponding ES forecasts, denoted by
\mbox{ES}_{n+1}(\alpha)
, are then given as

\displaystyle\mbox{ES}_{n+1}(\alpha)=E[y_{n+1}|y_{n+1}<\mbox{VaR}_{n+1}(\alpha),\mathcal{I}_{n}]

(58)

The one-day-ahead VaR and ES can be obtained as the
(1-\alpha)
th quantile and conditional average of
\{y_{n+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{M}
, respectively.

We evaluate the obtained volatility, VaR, and ES forecasts by employing suitable loss or scoring functions.
Specifically, following
Takahashi et al. 2023
, we compute the Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Gaussian Quasi-Likelihood (QLIKE) loss functions for volatility forecasts, and the
Fissler and Ziegel 2016
(FZ) joint loss function for VaR and ES forecasts.
To utilize the FZ loss function for forecast evaluation, we adhere to
Patton et al. 2019
and employ the following form, referred to as the FZ0 loss function hereafter,

\displaystyle L_{FZ0}(y,v,e;\alpha)=-\frac{1}{\alpha e}\mathbbm{1}\{y\leq v\}(v-y)+\frac{v}{e}+\log(-e)-1,

(59)

where
y
,
v
, and
e
denote a return, VaR, and ES, respectively.
Patton et al. 2019
demonstrate that the FZ0 loss function is the only FZ loss function generating loss differences that are homogeneous of degree zero.

While average losses derived from the aforementioned loss functions offer initial insights into the forecast performance of the models in contention, they do not indicate if the differences in losses are statistically significant.
To ascertain this, we utilize the conditional and unconditional predictive ability tests by
Giacomini and White 2006
, hereinafter referred to as GW tests, on the loss differences.
The GW tests are particularly pertinent to this paper’s objectives because they emphasize the rolling window methods we adopt in Section
4
. This allows for a cohesive assessment of both nested and non-nested models, including the RSV models described in Section
2
.

For the
unconditional
predictive ability, the GW test statistic conforms to that proposed by
Diebold and Mariano 2002
and is, under the null hypothesis, asymptotically standard normally distributed. For the
conditional
predictive ability, the GW test defines the null hypothesis as

\displaystyle H_{0,h}:E[\Delta L_{t+1}|\mathcal{I}_{t}]=0,\quad t=n,n+1,\ldots,n+n_{f}-1,

(60)

where
\Delta L_{t}
is the loss difference between two models at forecast date
t
, and
\mathcal{I}_{t}
is the information set at time
t
.

Given a test function
\mathbbm{h}_{t}
, a
q\times 1
vector measurable with respect to
\mathcal{I}_{t}

(
Stinchcombe and White 1998
)
, the null hypothesis translates to

\displaystyle H_{0,h}:E[\mathbbm{h}_{t}\Delta L_{t+1}]=0,\quad t=n,n+1,\ldots,n+n_{f}-1.

(61)

Defining
Z_{t+1}=\mathbbm{h}_{t}\Delta L_{t+1}
and
\bar{Z}_{n_{f}}=n_{f}^{-1}\sum_{t=n}^{n+n_{f}-1}Z_{t+1}
, the alternative hypothesis becomes

\displaystyle H_{A,h}:E[\bar{Z}_{n_{f}}^{\prime}]E[\bar{Z}_{n_{f}}]\geq d>0,\quad\mbox{for sufficiently large $n_{f}$}.

(62)

We employ the Wald-type test statistic

\displaystyle T_{n_{f}}=n\bar{Z}_{n_{f}}^{\prime}\hat{\Omega}_{n_{f}}^{-1}\bar{Z}_{n_{f}},

(63)

where
\hat{\Omega}_{n_{f}}=n_{f}^{-1}\sum_{t=n}^{n+n_{f}-1}Z_{t+1}Z_{t+1}^{\prime}
consistently estimates the variance of
Z_{t+1}
. Under the null,
T_{n_{f}}
follows a chi-square distribution with
q
degrees of freedom,
\chi_{q}^{2}
. For significance level
p
, the null hypothesis is rejected when
T_{n_{f}}>\chi_{q,1-p}^{2}
, where
\chi_{q,1-p}^{2}
is the
(1-p)
quantile of
\chi_{q}^{2}
.

In practice, the selection of
\mathbbm{h}_{t}
should discern between the forecast performances of the models. Focusing on one-day-ahead forecasts, we follow the specifications of
Takahashi et al. 2023
and define
\mathbbm{h}_{t}=(1,\Delta L_{t})^{\prime}
for
t=n,n+1,\ldots,n+n_{f}-1
. If the null is rejected, it implies that the lagged loss differences,
\Delta L_{t}
, can forecast the subsequent loss differences,
\Delta L_{t+1}
. Using
\hat{b}
to represent the regression coefficient of
\Delta L_{t+1}
on
\mathbbm{h}_{t}
, the predicted loss differences
\{\hat{b}^{\prime}\mathbbm{h}_{t}\}_{t=n}^{n+n_{f}-1}
serve as indicators for assessing model performances across different time points. In Section
4
, we measure relative performance by the frequency with which one model forecasts larger losses than the other, as expressed by

\displaystyle I_{n_{f},0}=\frac{1}{n_{f}}\sum_{t=n}^{n+n_{f}-1}I(\hat{b}^{\prime}\mathbbm{h}_{t}>0).

(64)

4.3
Out-of-sample Forecasting

Adhering to the approach of
Takahashi et al. 2016

For the DJIA, this size is set at 1,993 observations, with the last observation dates spanning from April 28, 2017, to September 26, 2019. For the N225, the window encompasses 1,942 observations, and the final observation dates range from April 28, 2017, to September 27, 2019. We produce one-day ahead forecasts after each estimation. Specifically, we generate 15,000 prediction samples, then calculate the means and medians of volatility forecasts for the SV and RSV models.
7
7

7

4.3.1

To appraise the accuracy of the volatility forecasts, we compute their average loss using the MSE and QLIKE loss metrics, as elaborated in Section

. Both the MSE and QLIKE losses are robust functions and offer rankings congruent with those derived using the true volatility, provided the volatility proxy serves as a conditionally unbiased estimator of the actual volatility. In order to counteract the bias introduced by market microstructure noise, we employ the RK utilizing the Parzen kernel function, accompanied by a bandwidth as recommended by
Barndorff-Nielsen et al. 2009
, to approximate the underlying volatility. Additionally, to correct for biases arising from non-trading hours, we adopt the method proposed by
Hansen and Lunde 2005

\displaystyle c_{HL}=\frac{\sum_{s=t-n+1}^{t}(y_{s}-\bar{y})^{2}}{\sum_{s=t-n+1}^{t}RK_{s}},\quad\bar{y}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=t-n+1}^{t}y_{s},

(68)

where

signifies the fixed window size.

5
Conclusion

This study has systematically analyzed the performance of various models in forecasting volatility, VaR, and ES for financial indices, specifically focusing on the DJIA and N225 indices. Our comprehensive evaluation, as presented in Table
8
, demonstrates that the RSV models consistently outperform other competing models in terms of average ranking losses for volatility, VaR, and ES forecasts.

Particularly noteworthy is the superior performance of the RSV-FS-SN model, which ranks as the best among all evaluated models. This finding underscores the effectiveness of incorporating RV into forecasting models. Moreover, the comparative analysis between RSV and REGARCH models reveals that the RSV models exhibit enhanced predictive capabilities.

An important contribution of our study is the validation of the benefit of integrating skewed
t
-distributions into these forecasting models. This integration has shown a marked improvement in forecast accuracy, thereby providing a significant insight into the methodology of financial forecasting.

In conclusion, our results suggest that for accurate and reliable forecasts of volatility, VaR, and ES, the RSV models, particularly when augmented with skewed
t
-distributions, offer a robust and effective approach. These findings have important implications for risk management and financial decision-making, highlighting the critical role of model selection and the incorporation of appropriate statistical distributions in enhancing forecast precision.

\displaystyle\exp\left\{(n_{\gamma 0}-1)\log\gamma-S_{\gamma 0}\gamma+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q_{T}\left(y_{t}\exp(-h_{t}/2)|\gamma,\nu\right)\right\}.

We consider a transformation
\tilde{\gamma}=\log\gamma
, and conduct a random walk MH algorithm for
\tilde{\gamma}
(so that we add a Jacobian term
\tilde{\gamma}
to the log conditional posterior density).

A.2.8
Generation of
\nu

The conditional probability density function of
\nu
is

\displaystyle\pi(\nu|\cdot)\propto\exp\left\{(n_{\nu 0}-1)\log\nu-S_{\nu 0}\nu+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log q_{T}\left(y_{t}\exp(-h_{t}/2)|\gamma,\nu\right)\right\}.

We consider a transformation
\tilde{\nu}=\log(\nu-2)
, and conduct a random walk MH algorithm for
\tilde{\nu}
(so that we add a Jacobian term
\tilde{\nu}
to the log conditional posterior density).

A.2.9
Generation of
\bm{h}

First we define

\displaystyle g(h_{t}|\gamma,\nu)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nu+1}{2}\log\left[1+\frac{\left(\sigma_{*}y_{t}\exp(-h_{t}/2)+\mu_{*}\right)^{2}}{\nu}\left\{\gamma^{-2}I\left(y_{t}\exp(-h_{t}/2)\geq-\frac{\mu_{*}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 170.71652pt\left.\left.+\gamma^{2}I\left(y_{t}\exp(-h_{t}/2)<-\frac{\mu_{*}}{\sigma_{*}}\right)\right\}\right].

(1) For
t=1
, the conditional posterior density of
h_{1}
is

\displaystyle\pi(h_{1}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[g(h_{1}|\gamma,\nu)-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}}\left\{h_{2}-\mu-\phi(h_{1}-\mu)-\rho\sigma\exp(-h_{1}/2)y_{1}\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}h_{1}-\frac{(1-\phi^{2})}{2\sigma^{2}}(h_{1}-\mu)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}(x_{1}-\xi-h_{1})^{2}\right].

Define

\displaystyle\sigma_{h1}^{-2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}\left\{1-\phi^{2}+\frac{\phi^{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}\right\}+\sigma_{u}^{-2},

\displaystyle\mu_{h1}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{h1}^{2}\left[-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\phi\{h_{2}-(1-\phi)\mu\}}{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{(1-\phi^{2})\mu}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{x_{1}-\xi}{\sigma_{u}^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle k(h_{1})

\displaystyle=\exp\left[g(h_{1}|\gamma,\nu)+\frac{\rho y_{1}\exp(-h_{1}/2)}{(1-\rho^{2})\sigma}\left\{h_{2}-\mu-\phi(h_{1}-\mu)\right\}-\frac{\rho^{2}y_{1}^{2}\exp(-h_{1})}{2(1-\rho^{2})}\right].

Thus we propose a candidate
h_{1}^{\dagger}\sim N(\mu_{h1},\sigma_{h1}^{2})
and accept it with probability
\min\{1,k(h_{1}^{\dagger})/k(h_{1})\}
.

(2) For
t=2,\ldots,T-1
, the conditional posterior density of
h_{t}
is

\displaystyle\pi(h_{t}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[g(h_{t}|\gamma,\nu)-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}}\left\{h_{t+1}-\mu-\phi(h_{t}-\mu)-\rho\sigma\exp(-h_{t}/2)y_{t}\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}}\left\{h_{t}-\mu-\phi(h_{t-1}-\mu)-\rho\sigma\exp(-h_{t-1}/2)y_{t-1}\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}h_{t}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}(x_{t}-\xi-h_{t})^{2}\right].

Define

\displaystyle\sigma_{ht}^{-2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}\left(\frac{1+\phi^{2}}{1-\rho^{2}}\right)+\sigma_{u}^{-2},

\displaystyle\mu_{ht}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{ht}^{2}\left[-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\phi\{h_{t+1}-(1-\phi)\mu\}}{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{\{(1-\phi)\mu+\phi h_{t-1}\}}{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{\rho y_{t-1}\exp(-h_{t-1}/2)}{(1-\rho^{2})\sigma}+\frac{x_{t}-\xi}{\sigma_{u}^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle k(h_{t})

\displaystyle=\exp\left[g(h_{t}|\gamma,\nu)-\frac{\rho^{2}y_{t}^{2}\exp(-h_{t})}{2(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{\rho y_{t}\exp(-h_{t}/2)}{(1-\rho^{2})\sigma}\left\{h_{t+1}-\mu-\phi(h_{t}-\mu)\right\}\right].

Thus we propose a candidate
h_{t}^{\dagger}\sim N(\mu_{ht},\sigma_{ht}^{2})
and accept it with probability
\min\{1,k(h_{t}^{\dagger})/g(h_{t})\}
.

(3) For
t=T
, the conditional posterior density of
h_{T}
is

\displaystyle\pi(h_{T}|\cdot)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left[g(h_{T}|\gamma,\nu)-\frac{1}{2(1-\rho^{2})\sigma^{2}}\left\{h_{T}-\mu-\phi(h_{T-1}-\mu)-\rho\sigma\exp(-h_{T-1}/2)y_{T-1}\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\times\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}h_{T}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{u}^{2}}(x_{T}-\xi-h_{T})^{2}\right].

Define

\displaystyle\sigma_{hT}^{-2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-2}(1-\rho^{2})^{-1}+\sigma_{u}^{-2},

\displaystyle\mu_{hT}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{hT}^{2}\left[-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(1-\phi)\mu+\phi h_{T-1}}{\sigma^{2}(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{\rho y_{T-1}\exp(-h_{T-1}/2)}{(1-\rho^{2})\sigma}+\frac{x_{T}-\xi}{\sigma_{u}^{2}}\right],

\displaystyle k(h_{T})

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{g(h_{T}|\gamma,\nu)\right\}.

Thus we propose a candidate
h_{T}^{\dagger}\sim N(\mu_{hT},\sigma_{hT}^{2})
and accept it with probability
\min\{1,k(h_{T}^{\dagger})/k(h_{T})\}
.
