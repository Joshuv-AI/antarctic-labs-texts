Global existence and lower bounds in a class of tumor-immune cell interactions chemotaxis systems
♯ Shanmugasundaram Gnanasekaran, ♮ Alessandro Columbu, ♮ Rafael Díaz Fuentes, ♭ Nagarajan Nithyadevi

Abstract
. This paper investigates the properties of classical solutions to a class of chemotaxis systems that model interactions between tumor and immune cells. Our focus is on examining the global existence and explosion of such solutions in bounded domains of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 , under Neumann boundary conditions. We distinguish between two scenarios: one where all equations are parabolic and another where only one equation is parabolic while the rest are elliptic. Boundedness is demonstrated under smallness assumptions on the initial data in the former scenario, while no such constraints are necessary in the latter. Additionally, we provide estimates for the blow-up time of unbounded solutions in three dimensions, supported by numerical simulations. Keywords: Blow–up, Boundedness, Classical solutions, Numerical Simulations, Tumor–immune cell interaction. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification: 35A01; 35A09; 35B44; 92C17; 65N06.

1.
Introduction and motivations

Chemotaxis refers to the movement of microorganisms in response to chemical signals. To gain a deeper understanding of this phenomenon in mathematical terms, one can explore the classical Keller-Segel system. Over the past few decades, researchers have extensively studied the classical Keller-Segel model and its various modified versions, recognizing its significance in the fields of mathematics and biology. For the latest advancements in this area, one can refer to the work by Lankeit and Winkler
[
14
]
. It is noteworthy that Keller and Segel
[
13
]
proposed the consumption model in 1971 to describe the motion of
E-coli
bacteria in response to oxygen gradient and consumption. The model is represented by the following system of partial differential equations

(1.1)

\begin{cases}{u}_{t}=\Delta{u}-\chi\nabla\cdot({u}{\nabla{v}}),\\
{v}_{t}=\Delta{v}-u{v}.\end{cases}

In this system,
{u}
represents the density of the bacteria,
{v}
represents the oxygen concentration, and
\chi>0
is the chemotaxis coefficient. The model predicts the formation of a traveling band of bacteria when the chemotaxis coefficient exceeds a critical value, which can be confirmed through practical observations. Tao
[
17
]
derived the condition for the global existence of a classical solution to the system (
1.1
) for
n\geq 2
. Later a weak global solution to the system (
1.1
) is investigated by Tao and Winkler
[
19
]
for
n\geq 3
. They also demonstrated that the solution converges towards constant equilibria as
t\to\infty
. Zhang and Li
[
27
]
have improved the condition for the global existence of a classical solution in two spatial dimensions. They showed that the condition stated in
[
17
]
is needed for
n\geq 3
and also showed that the convergence of the solution is exponential as time increases. Jiang et al.
[
12
]
studied the blow-up properties of the above system (
1.1
) in three spatial dimensions based on the kinetic reformulation technique under the unproved assumption that blow-up occurs. These blow-up results generalized the earlier results available in the literature. In addition, the local non-degeneracy for blow-up points is also indicated. The global existence of the classical solution of the system (
1.1
), under more relaxed assumptions, is proven by Baghaei and Khelghati
[
3
]
.

Fuest
[
9
]
studied the existence of solution of the following system

\begin{cases}{u}_{t}=\Delta{u}-\chi\nabla\cdot({u}{\nabla{v}}),\\
{v}_{t}=\Delta{v}-u{v},\\
w_{t}=-\delta w+u.\end{cases}

It has been demonstrated that when
n\leq 2
or
\|v_{0}\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{3n}
, the system has a global bounded solution. In addition, it is also shown that the solution converges towards equilibrium point as
t\to\infty
. A similar result was subsequently studied by Liu et al.
[
15
]
with the introduction of a source term
f(u)\leq\mu(u-u^{r})
for
n\geq 2
in the first equation. Recently both the above two results have been partially improved by Frassu and Viglialoro in
[
8
]
for
n\geq 5
. For more details related to those models, see
[
26
,
29
]
.

Wang et al.
[
22
]
examined the global existence and asymptotic behaviour of the following predator-prey system with indirect prey–taxis (with positive constants and
f,g,h
sufficiently regular)

(1.2)

\begin{cases}u_{t}=d_{1}\Delta u-\nabla\cdot(\chi(w)u\nabla w)+bug(v)-uh(u),\\
w_{t}=d_{2}\Delta w-\mu w+rv,\\
v_{t}=d_{3}\Delta v+f(v)-ug(v),\end{cases}

providing the convergence rates of the solution. Rather than the prey–taxis, Ahn and Yoon
[
1
]
analyzed the system, similar to (
1.2
) with indirect predator–taxis. In addition, by constructing an appropriate Lyapunov functional, the stability results are also given for
n\leq 2
. Recently with respect to the nonlinear diffusion, the existence of a classical solution was established in
[
25
]
under some parameter assumptions. The authors also discussed in other scenarios the asymptotic behaviour of solutions.

Chemotaxis models offer valuable insights into immune cell migration mechanisms, enhancing our understanding of immune responses, inflammation, and related diseases. These models are connected to immunology research, drug development, and immunotherapy. Lymphocytes are one of the major immune cells, play a vital role in the immune system. They are produced from bone marrow stem cells and are present in blood and lymph tissues. Lymphocytes work together with other immune cells to protect the body against various microorganisms. They include B cells, which produce antibodies to fight foreign microorganisms, and T cells, which regulate immune responses. Most of the T cells require assistance from another immune cell to be activated. Activated T cells, such as Natural Killer cells (NK cells), specialize in destroying cancer cells and virus-infected cells. The dynamics of the above phenomena are mathematically described by Hu and Tao
[
11
]
with the help of nonlinear parabolic partial differential equations similar to (
1.2
)

(1.3)

\begin{cases}{u}_{t}=\Delta{u}-\chi\nabla\cdot({u}{\nabla{v}}),\\
{v}_{t}=\Delta{v}+{w}-{v}-u{v},\\
{w}_{t}=\Delta{w}-u{w}+{w}(1-{w}).\end{cases}

The authors studied well-posedness, solvability and properties of classical solutions to system (
1.3
) in the situation where
\Omega
is a two-dimensional domain; more precisely, for any
\chi>0
and any sufficiently regular initial data solutions are globally bounded. Furthermore, for
\chi
is small enough and
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}<1
, convergence to equilibrium points as
t\to\infty
is proved. On the other hand, also the asymptotic behaviour when
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\geq 1
is discussed.

1.1.
Aim of the research

Exactly inspired by
[
11
]
, in this work we aim at investigating model (
1.3
) in higher dimensions, in more simplified cases, introducing parameters in the equations to circumvent the nondimensionalization of the model. More precisely, we consider the following initial-boundary value problem that describes the tumor-immune cell interactions given by

(1.4)

\begin{cases}{u}_{t}=\Delta{u}-\chi\nabla\cdot({u}{\nabla{v}}),\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),\\
\tau{v}_{t}=\Delta{v}+\alpha{w}-\beta{v}-\gamma u{v},&\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),\\
\tau{w}_{t}=\Delta{w}-\delta u{w}+\mu{w}(1-{w}),&\text{in}\;\Omega\times(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),\\
\frac{\partial{u}}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial{v}}{\partial\nu}=\frac{\partial{w}}{\partial\nu}=0,&\text{on}\;\partial\Omega\times(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),\\
{u}(x,0)={u}_{0}(x),\quad\tau{v}(x,0)=\tau{v}_{0}(x),\quad\tau{w}(x,0)=\tau{w}_{0}(x),&x\in\overline{\Omega}.\end{cases}

Herein
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded domain,
n\geq 3
, with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
,
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal on
\partial\Omega
and
\tau\in\{0,1\}
. Moreover, with
T_{\mathrm{max}}
we are indicating a positive value, possibly equal to infinity, representing the maximum instant of time up to which solutions are defined. The unknown function
{u}={u}(x,t)
represents the density of lymphocytes,
{v}={v}(x,t)
describes the chemical signal secreted by tumor cells and
{w}={w}(x,t)
denotes the density of tumor cells, initially distributed as
u_{0}(x)
,
\tau v_{0}(x)
, and
\tau w_{0}(x)
.
Additionally, the parameters
\chi
,
\alpha
,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
\delta
,
\mu
are assumed to be positive constants.

The system (
1.4
) describes the interaction of two cells with one chemical secreted by the tumor cells. The term
-\chi\nabla\cdot({u}\nabla{v})
represents the movement of the lymphocytes towards the chemical signal and assume that there is a natural logistic growth term
\mu{w}(1-{w})
for the cancer cells with growth coefficient
\mu
. The constant
\alpha
is the production rate of the chemical by the tumor cells,
\beta
is the decay rate of the chemical and
\gamma
is the consumption rate of the chemical by the lymphocytes. The parameter
\delta
is known as the destruction rate of the tumor cells by the lymphocytes.

Motivated by the above research works and biological applications which were mentioned earlier, we focus our analysis concerning system (
1.4
) discussing the following scenarios.

\triangleright

In the fully parabolic case (i.e.,
\tau=1
), we prove global existence and boundedness of classical solutions, under smallness conditions involving the parameters and the initial data (see Theorem
1.1
).

\triangleright

In the parabolic-elliptic-elliptic case (i.e.,
\tau=0
), we establish the same results as above without any restriction on the parameters and the initial data (see Theorem
1.1
).

\triangleright

In 3D domains, lower bounds for the maximal existence time of blowing-up solutions are derived (see Theorem
1.2
).

\triangleright

Some numerical simulations in three dimensions are performed (see Section
\lx@sectionsign
5
).

1.2.
Presentation of the main results

In order to formally present the aforementioned analysis, let us suppose that initial values
{u}_{0}
,
\tau{v}_{0}
and
\tau{w}_{0}
satisfy

(1.5)

\begin{cases}{u}_{0}\in{C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})},\quad\mbox{with}\quad{u}_{0}\geq 0\quad\mbox{in}\>\Omega,\\
\tau{v}_{0},\tau{w}_{0}\in{W^{1,q}}(\Omega),\quad\mbox{for some}\,\,{q}>n,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\tau{v}_{0},\tau{w}_{0}\geq 0\quad\mbox{in}\>\Omega.\end{cases}

With such a preparation, the main theorems are stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
n\geq 3
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary,
q>n
and
\tau\in\{0,1\}
. Then for any initial data
({u}_{0},\tau{v}_{0},\tau{w}_{0})
satisfying (
1.5
) and additionally

(1.6)

\displaystyle\tau\max\left\{\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\|{w}_{0}\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)},\|{v}_{0}\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\right\}<\frac{\pi}{\chi}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}},

system (
1.4
) admits a unique nonnegative global classical solution
({u},{v},{w})
which is uniformly bounded-in-time, in the sense that
for some
C>0

\displaystyle\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}+\big\|{v}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}+\big\|{w}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}\leq C,\qquad\qquad\forall\,t>0.

Remark 1.1
.

Conversely to the case
\tau=1
, when
\tau=0
we see that (
1.6
) in Theorem
1.1
is automatically fulfilled, independently by the choice of the data of the problem. This stresses how dealing with simplified elliptic versions of chemotaxis models is, in general, more straightforward than for the fully parabolic ones. In this sense, it appears natural to investigate whether the introduction of damping terms (as for instance logistic sources; see
[
20
]
,
[
23
]
) in the equation for
u
in problem (
1.4
), naturally for
\tau=1
, enables to relax restriction (
1.6
).

As to the estimate of the blow-up time for solutions to model system (
1.4
), we will make use of the following functional:

(1.7)

\Psi_{\tau}=\Psi_{\tau}(t):=\int_{\Omega}u^{2}+\tau\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{4}+\tau\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w\rvert^{2}\quad\textrm{for all }t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),

with initial value

(1.8)

\Psi_{\tau}(0)=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{2}+\tau\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{0}\rvert^{4}+\tau\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w_{0}\rvert^{2}.

For the upcoming analysis, it is important to note that we must consider an additional restriction on the domain.

Theorem 1.2
.

For
n=3
,
q\geq 4
, and
\tau\in\{0,1\}
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded convex domain. Moreover, let
(u,v,w)
be a nonnegative solution to model (
1.4
), emanating from data as in (
1.5
), blowing-up at finite time
T_{\mathrm{max}}
, in the sense that
\limsup_{t\to T_{\mathrm{max}}}\lVert u(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=+\infty
.
Then, there exist computable constants
\mathcal{A}_{\tau},\mathcal{B}_{\tau},\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
such that

(1.9)

T_{\text{max}}\geq\int_{\Psi_{\tau}(0)}^{+\infty}\frac{d\Psi_{\tau}(t)}{\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\Psi_{\tau}(t)^{3}+\mathcal{B}_{\tau}\Psi_{\tau}(t)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\Psi_{\tau}(t)^{\tau}},

where
\Psi_{\tau}
is defined in (
1.7
).

Our article is organized as follows: In Section
\lx@sectionsign
2
, we present some basic inequalities, key lemmas and we prove the local existence of classical solutions to system (
1.4
). Section
\lx@sectionsign
3
deals with the boundedness and global existence of such solutions. Section
\lx@sectionsign

is devoted to blow-up analysis.
Section
\lx@sectionsign

presents the numerical examples of our considered system that validates such blow-up result.

2.
Preliminaries, local existence and extensibility criterion

We recall a few useful inequalities and key lemmas that we are going to use in the next sections.
The following proof of the local existence lemma is adopted from well-established arguments proved in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Local Existence)
.

Suppose that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geq 2,
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary and
q>n
. Then for each nonnegative initial data satisfying (
1.5
), there exists
T_{\mathrm{max}}\in(0,\infty]
such that the system (
1.4
) admits a unique nonnegative solution
({u},{v},{w})
belonging to

\displaystyle{u}

\displaystyle\in{C^{0}}\left(\overline{\Omega}\times\left.\left[0,T_{\mathrm{max}}\right.\right)\right)\cap{C^{2,1}}\left(\overline{\Omega}\times\left(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}\right)\right),

\displaystyle{v},{w}

\displaystyle\in{C^{0}}\left(\overline{\Omega}\times\left.\left[0,T_{\mathrm{max}}\right.\right)\right)\cap{C^{2,1}}\left(\overline{\Omega}\times\left(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}\right)\right)\cap{L^{\infty}_{loc}}\left(\left.\left[0,T_{\mathrm{max}}\right.\right);{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)\right).

Furthermore, if
T_{\mathrm{max}}<\infty
, then

(2.1)

\begin{split}\limsup_{t\to T_{\mathrm{max}}}\Big(\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}+\big\|{v}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}+\big\|{w}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}\Big)=\infty.\end{split}

Proof.

The proof can be derived by standard arguments involving the Banach fixed point theorem and the parabolic and elliptic regularity theories. (See for instance
[
10
]
.)
Moreover, for
\tau=\{0,1\}
,
\underline{u}\equiv 0
is a subsolution of the equation for
u
and along to (
1.5
) this implies that
u\geq 0
for all
(x,t)\in\bar{\Omega}\times(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
, see
[
7
,
\lx@sectionsign
6.4 and
\lx@sectionsign
7.1.4]
. An analogous argument holds for
v
and
w
.
∎

From now on, with
(u,v,w)
we will refer to the local-in-time solution to our model (
1.4
) emanating from initial data (
1.5
), provided by Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The solution
(u,v,w)
satisfies

(2.2)

\begin{split}\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{1}}(\Omega)}=M_{1}:=\|{u}_{0}\|_{{L^{1}}(\Omega)},\hskip 99.58464pt\forall t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})\end{split}

and

(2.3)

\displaystyle\big\|{w}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq M_{2}:=\max\Big\{1,\tau\|{w}_{0}\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\Big\},\hskip 56.9055pt

\displaystyle\forall t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),

(2.4)

\displaystyle\big\|{v}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq M_{3}:=\max\Big\{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}M_{2},\tau\|{v}_{0}\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\Big\},

\displaystyle\forall t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}).

Proof.

Integration of the first equation in (
1.4
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega}{u}=0\quad\text{for all $t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})$}.

Therefore, this problem preserves mass for
u
, in the sense that (
2.2
) holds.
Further (
2.3
) and (
2.4
) are consequences of the elliptic and parabolic maximum principle (we refer again to
[
7
,
\lx@sectionsign
6.4 and
\lx@sectionsign
7.1.4]
).
Indeed, if
\tau=0
the third equation makes that
\overline{w}\equiv 1
is a supersolution, so that
0\leq w\leq 1
for all
(x,t)\in\bar{\Omega}\times(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
. We exploit these information to observe that
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}M_{2}
is a supersolution for the second equation. The case
\tau=1
is similar but also the initial condition has to be taken into consideration.
∎

The following result allows us to establish boundedness of solutions once some uniform-in-time estimates of these in appropriate Lebesgue spaces are derived.

(If not specified, all the constants are positive and the small-letter constants
c_{i}
are local to each proof.)

Lemma 2.3

(Extensibility criterion)
.

Suppose there exists
p>\frac{n}{2}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\sup\limits_{t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})}\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{p}}(\Omega)}<\infty.

Then we have

\sup\limits_{t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})}\Big(\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}+\big\|{v}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}+\big\|{w}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}\Big)<\infty.

In particular
T_{\mathrm{max}}=\infty
and
u\in L^{\infty}((0,\infty),L^{\infty}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

Let
q>n
and for each fixed
p>\frac{n}{2}
, let us define

\frac{np}{(n-p)_{+}}=\begin{cases}\infty,&\quad\mbox{if}\quad p\geq n,\\
\frac{np}{n-p},&\quad\mbox{if}\quad\frac{n}{2}<p<n,\end{cases}

and choose
q<\frac{np}{(n-p)_{+}}
and
1<q_{0}<q
fulfilling
n<q_{0}<\frac{np}{(n-p)_{+}}

which enables to take
\zeta>1
,
n<\zeta q_{0}<\frac{np}{(n-p)_{+}}
and
\zeta q_{0}\leq q
. We fix arbitrary
t\in(0,\>T_{\mathrm{max}})
. Applying the variation of constants formula to the second equation of
(
1.4
)
, we get

\displaystyle{v}(\cdot,t)=e^{-\beta t}e^{t\Delta}{v}_{0}+\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\beta(t-s)}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\Big(\alpha{w}(\cdot,s)-\gamma u(\cdot,s){v}(\cdot,s)\Big)\mathrm{d}s.

From the above, we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|\nabla{v}(\cdot,t)\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\leq

\displaystyle\>e^{-\beta t}\|\nabla e^{t\Delta}{v}_{0}\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle+\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\Big\|\nabla e^{(t-s)\Delta}\left(\alpha{w}(\cdot,s)-\gamma{u}(\cdot,s){v}(\cdot,s)\right)\Big\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\mathrm{d}s.

By using the estimates for the Neumann heat semigroup
[
24
]
and
q_{0}\zeta\leq q
, we obtain

\begin{split}\|\nabla{v}(\cdot,t)\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\leq&\>c_{1}e^{-\beta t}\|{v}_{0}\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}\\
&+c_{2}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\left(1+\left(t-s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta}\right)}\right)e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\\
&\times\Big\|\alpha{w}(\cdot,s)-\gamma{u}(\cdot,s){v}(\cdot,s)\Big\|_{{L^{p}}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}s\\
\leq&\>c_{1}\|{v}_{0}\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}\\
&+\alpha c_{2}M_{2}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\left(1+\left(t-s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta}\right)}\right)e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\\
&+\gamma c_{2}M_{3}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\beta(t-s)}\left(1+\left(t-s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta}\right)}\right)e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\\
&\times\Big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\Big\|_{{L^{p}}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}s,\end{split}

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are positive constants, and were we invoked Lemma
2.2
. Because of our assumptions
q_{0}\zeta<\frac{np}{(n-p)}
and
q_{0}\zeta\leq q
, we can ensure that
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta}\right)<1
.
Since by hypotheses
\lVert u(\cdot,t)\rVert_{L^{p}(\Omega)}<c_{3}
, for certain
c_{3}>0
, in view of the fact that the Gamma function gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha\psi}\left(1+\psi^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta})}\right)e^{-\lambda\psi}<\infty
, we can conclude that

(2.5)

\|\nabla{v}(\cdot,t)\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\leq c_{4},\qquad\qquad\forall\,t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),

where
c_{4}>0
. Next let
t_{0}=\mathrm{max}\{0,\>t-1\}
and use the variation of constants formula to the first equation of
(
1.4
)
, to get

(2.6)

\begin{split}u(\cdot,t)=&e^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}{u}(\cdot,t_{0})-\chi\int^{t}_{t_{0}}e^{(t-s)\Delta}\nabla\cdot\Big({u}(\cdot,s){\nabla{v}(\cdot,s)}\Big)\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

By taking
{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)
norm on both sides of (
2.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq

\displaystyle\>\big\|e^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}+\chi\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\Big\|e^{(t-s)\Delta}\nabla\cdot\Big({u}(\cdot,s){\nabla{v}(\cdot,s)}\Big)\Big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}s

for all
t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
. If
t\leq 1
, then
t_{0}=0
and we can use the maximum principle to get

\displaystyle\big\|e^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}=\big\|e^{(t-s_{0})\Delta}{u}(\cdot,0)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq\big\|{u}(\cdot,0)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}.

If
t>1
, again using the Neumann heat semigroup properties and Lemma
2.2
, with
c_{5}>0
,

\displaystyle\big\|e^{(t-t_{0})\Delta}{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq c_{5}(t-t_{0})^{-\frac{n}{2}}\big\|{u}(\cdot,t_{0})\big\|_{{L^{1}}(\Omega)}\leq c_{5}M_{1},

because
t-t_{0}=1
.
Similarly, for some
c_{6}>0
we have

(2.7)

\begin{split}\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq&\>\max\Big\{\|{u}(\cdot,0)\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)},c_{5}M_{1}\Big\}\\
&+c_{6}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\left(1+\left(t-s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{q_{0}}-\frac{1}{\infty}\right)}\right)e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\Big\|{u}(\cdot,s){\nabla{v}(\cdot,s)}\Big\|_{{L^{q_{0}}}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Here by using the Hölder inequality and the Interpolation inequalities, (
2.2
) and (
2.5
) provide

(2.8)

\begin{split}\big\|{u}(\cdot,s){\nabla{v}(\cdot,s)}\big\|_{{L^{q_{0}}}(\Omega)}\leq&\>\>\big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\big\|_{{L^{\widehat{\zeta}q_{0}}}(\Omega)}\>\big\|{\nabla{v}(\cdot,s)}\big\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\\
\leq&\>\>\big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}^{\sigma_{2}}\>\big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\big\|_{{L^{1}}(\Omega)}^{1-\sigma_{2}}\>\big\|{\nabla{v}(\cdot,s)}\big\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\\
\leq&\>c_{7}\big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}^{\sigma_{2}},\end{split}

where
\widehat{\zeta}
is the dual exponent of
\zeta
and
\sigma_{2}=1-\frac{1}{\widehat{\zeta}q_{0}}\in(0,1)
, for all

and

. Inserting (
2.8
) in (
2.7
), with the Gamma function we obtain

, where

. This gives

\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq\>\max\Big\{\|{u}(\cdot,0)\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)},c_{5}M_{1}\Big\}+c_{8}\big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}^{\sigma_{2}}.

Finally, using the Young inequality, we obtain

(2.9)

\begin{split}\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\leq&c_{9},\hskip 56.9055pt\forall t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),\end{split}

where
c_{9}>0
.
Similarly applying the variation of constants formula to the third equation of
(
1.4
)
, we get

\displaystyle\|\nabla{w}(\cdot,t)\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\leq

\displaystyle\>\|\nabla e^{t\Delta}{w}_{0}\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}+\delta\int^{t}_{0}\Big\|\nabla e^{(t-s)\Delta}{u}(\cdot,s){w}(\cdot,s)\Big\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\mu\int^{t}_{0}\Big\|\nabla e^{(t-s)\Delta}{w}(\cdot,s)\big(1-{w}(\cdot,s)\big)\Big\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\mathrm{d}s,

and we obtain

\displaystyle\|\nabla{w}(\cdot,t)\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}\leq

\displaystyle\>c_{10}\|{w}_{0}\|_{{W^{1,q}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+c_{11}\big\|{w}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{\infty}}(\Omega)}\int^{t}_{0}\left(1+\left(t-s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta}\right)}\right)e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\Big\|{u}(\cdot,s)\Big\|_{{L^{p}}(\Omega)}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+c_{12}\int^{t}_{0}\left(1+\left(t-s\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q_{0}\zeta}\right)}\right)e^{-\lambda(t-s)}\Big\|\mu{w}(\cdot,s)(1-{w}(\cdot,s))\Big\|_{{L^{p}}(\Omega)}.

In turn, since

is bounded we obtain

(2.10)

\begin{split}\|\nabla{w}(\cdot,t)\|_{{L^{q_{0}\zeta}(\Omega)}}&\leq c_{13},\hskip 56.9055ptt\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),\end{split}

where

. Finally, if by contradiction

) and (

) would provide an inconsistency to the blow-up criterion (

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and
p>1
. Define the function

\displaystyle\phi(x):=e^{\zeta(x)},\quad 0\leq x\leq K,

where

\displaystyle\zeta(x):=-\frac{l}{2m}x+\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2m}\int^{x}_{0}\tan\left(\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}s+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\right)\mathrm{d}s

with
k=(p-1)^{2},l=-4(p-1)\epsilon,m=\frac{4}{p}(1+(p-1)\epsilon)
and
r=\frac{4}{p}(p-1)(1-\epsilon)
. If

(2.11)

\begin{split}K<\frac{2}{\sqrt{p}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+p\epsilon}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\arctan\sqrt{\frac{p}{1+(p-1)\epsilon-p\epsilon^{2}}}\epsilon\right),\end{split}

then the function
\phi(x)
is well defined and satisfies the following properties

(2.12)

1\leq\phi(x)\leq\phi(K),\qquad 0\leq\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)<\infty

and

(2.13)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{p}\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)-\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\geq 0.\end{split}

Moreover we have

(2.14)

\begin{split}|(p-1)\phi(x)-2\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)|-2\sqrt{(p-1)(1-\epsilon)\phi(x)\left(\frac{1}{p}\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)-\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\right)}=0\end{split}

for all
0\leq x\leq K
.

Proof.

First we prove that the function
\phi(x)
is well defined. For any
p>1
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we attain

\displaystyle 4km-l^{2}=\frac{16(p-1)^{2}}{p}\Big(1+(p-1)\epsilon-p\epsilon^{2}\Big)=\frac{16(p-1)^{2}}{p}(1-\epsilon)(1+p\epsilon)>0

and

(2.15)

\begin{split}\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}s+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\geq&\arctan\left(\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\right)\\
\geq&-\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{(1-\epsilon)(1+p\epsilon)}}\epsilon\right)>-\frac{\pi}{2}\end{split}

due to
4km-l^{2}>0
. Similarly we arrive at

(2.16)

\begin{split}\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}s+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\leq&\frac{\sqrt{p}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1+p\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}K-\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{(1-\epsilon)(1+p\epsilon)}}\epsilon\right)\\
<&\frac{\sqrt{p}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1+p\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}}\\
&\times\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{p}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+p\epsilon}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\arctan\sqrt{\frac{p}{1+(p-1)\epsilon-p\epsilon^{2}}}\epsilon\right)\right)\\
&-\arctan\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{(1-\epsilon)(1+p\epsilon)}}\epsilon\right)<\frac{\pi}{2}.\end{split}

From (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), we conclude that the function
\zeta(x)
is bounded. This gives
\phi(x)
is well defined for
0\leq x\leq K
. Next we attain

\displaystyle\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)=

\displaystyle\phi(x)\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi(x)\left(-\frac{l}{2m}+\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2m}\tan\left(\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}x+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\phi(x)\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(0)\geq 0,

for all
0\leq x\leq K
. Since
\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)
is continuous and nonnegative, we achieve
1\leq\phi(x)\leq\phi(K)
. Moreover, using (
2.15
), we have

\displaystyle\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)=

\displaystyle\phi(x)\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)<\infty,

for all
0\leq x\leq K
; these last relations prove (
2.12
). Now we calculate

(2.17)

\begin{split}\zeta^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)=&\frac{4km-l^{2}}{4mr}\sec^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}x+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\right)\\
=&\frac{4km-l^{2}}{4mr}\left(1+\tan^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}x+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\right)\right).\end{split}

From
\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)
, we arrive at

(2.18)

\begin{split}\frac{4m^{2}}{4km-l^{2}}\left(\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)+\frac{l}{2m}\right)^{2}=\tan^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}{2r}x+\arctan\frac{l}{\sqrt{4km-l^{2}}}\right).\end{split}

By substituting (
2.18
) in (

) we get

(2.19)

\begin{split}\zeta^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)=&\frac{4km-l^{2}}{4mr}\left(1+\frac{4m^{2}}{4km-l^{2}}\left(\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)+\frac{l}{2m}\right)^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{r}\Big(m\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)^{2}+l\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)+k\Big).\end{split}

Next, with the help of (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p}\phi^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)-\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)=

\displaystyle\phi(x)\left(\frac{1}{p}\Big(\zeta^{{}^{\prime\prime}}(x)+\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)^{2}\Big)-\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\phi(x)}{p}\left(\left(\frac{m}{r}+1\right)\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)^{2}+\left(\frac{l}{r}-p\right)\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)+\frac{k}{r}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\phi(x)}{p}\left(\frac{p}{(p-1)(1-\epsilon)}\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)^{2}-\frac{p}{1-\epsilon}\zeta^{{}^{\prime}}(x)+\frac{p(p-1)}{4(1-\epsilon)}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4(p-1)(1-\epsilon)}\frac{1}{\phi(x)}\left((p-1)\phi(x)-2\phi^{{}^{\prime}}(x)\right)^{2}.

2.13

{u}(x,0)={u}_{0}(x),&x\in\overline{\Omega}.\end{cases}

In the specific let us establish this result.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
p>1
there exists
C_{2}>0
such that the local solution
(u,v,w)
of (
3.7
) satisfies

\displaystyle\big\|{u}(\cdot,t)\big\|_{{L^{p}}(\Omega)}\leq C_{2},\qquad\text{for all $t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})$}.

Proof.

We begin with the differentiation of the functional
\Phi(t)=\int_{\Omega}u^{p}
for which we apply the divergence theorem and obtain

\begin{split}\Phi^{\prime}(t)=&p\int_{\Omega}{u}^{p-1}\Delta{u}-p\chi\int_{\Omega}{u}^{p-1}\nabla\cdot\left({u}\nabla{v}\right)\\
=&-p(p-1)\int_{\Omega}{u}^{p-2}\left|\nabla{u}\right|^{2}+p(p-1)\chi\int_{\Omega}{u}^{p-1}\nabla{u}\cdot\nabla{v}\\
=&-\frac{4(p-1)}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\right|^{2}-(p-1)\chi\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\Delta v\quad\text{on $(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})$.}\end{split}

We now make use of the second equation in conjunction with the fact that
u,v
and
w
are positive and
w
is bounded as in (
2.3
), so to write

(3.8)

\begin{split}\Phi^{\prime}(t)=&-\frac{4(p-1)}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\right|^{2}-(p-1)\chi\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\left(-\alpha w+\beta v+\gamma uv\right)\\
\leq&-\frac{4(p-1)}{p}\int_{\Omega}\left|\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\right|^{2}+\alpha(p-1)\chi M_{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\quad\text{on $(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})$.}\end{split}

Exactly as in (
3.2
), we can say that for any
c_{1}>0
there is a proper
c_{2}>0
such that

(3.9)

c_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{p}\leq\frac{2(p-1)}{p}\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla u^{\frac{p}{2}}\rvert^{2}+c_{2}\quad\text{on $(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})$}.

In this way (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) lead to this initial problem:

\begin{cases}\Phi^{\prime}(t)\leq 2c_{2}-c_{1}\Phi(t)&\text{on $(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})$},\\
\Phi(0)=\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{p}.\end{cases}

Naturally, the above problem is solvable and
\Phi(t)\leq\max{\left\{\int_{\Omega}u_{0}^{p},\frac{2c_{2}}{c_{1}}\right\}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For the case
\tau=0
, we directly consider Lemmas
3.2
and
2.3
. For
\tau=1
, with the aim of exploiting Lemma
3.1
(and successively again Lemma
2.3
), it is sufficient to
verify (
2.11
) with
K:=\chi M_{3}<\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\pi
. We can choose

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{\pi^{2}-\frac{n}{2}K^{2}}{2\left(\pi^{2}+(\frac{n}{2})^{2}K^{2}\right)}\in(0,1)

such that

\displaystyle K=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1+2\epsilon\frac{n}{2}}}\frac{\pi}{2}<\frac{2}{\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+\epsilon\frac{n}{2}}}\frac{\pi}{2},

for some
p>\frac{n}{2}
. Then we have

\displaystyle K<\frac{2}{\sqrt{p}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+p\epsilon}}\frac{\pi}{2}<\frac{2}{\sqrt{p}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\epsilon}{1+p\epsilon}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}+\arctan\sqrt{\frac{p}{1+(p-1)\epsilon-p\epsilon^{2}}}\epsilon\right).\hfill\qed

Lemma 4.1
.

For

(4.1)

\Psi^{\prime}_{\tau}(t)\leq\mathcal{A}_{\tau}\Psi_{\tau}(t)^{3}+\mathcal{B}_{\tau}\Psi_{\tau}(t)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\Psi_{\tau}(t)^{\tau}\quad\text{on}\;(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}),

Proof.

We will distinguish the fully parabolic case, i.e.,
\tau=1
, and the parabolic-elliptic-elliptic case, i.e.,
\tau=0

The case
\tau=1

We have to deal with the following energy function

(4.2)

\Psi_{1}(t):=\int_{\Omega}u^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{4}+\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla w\rvert^{2}\qquad\text{for all}\;t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}).

Let us denote the integrals in (
4.2
) by
I_{1}(t)
,
I_{2}(t)
, and
I_{3}(t)
respectively. Let us then compute their derivatives one at a time.

For the first integral
I_{1}(t)
we have thanks to the Young inequality

(4.3)

\begin{split}\frac{dI_{1}(t)}{dt}&=2\int_{\Omega}u\left[\Delta{u}-\chi\nabla\cdot({u}{\nabla{v}})\right]=-2\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{u}}\rvert^{2}+2\chi\int_{\Omega}{\nabla{u}}\cdot u{\nabla{v}}\\
&\leq-\frac{3}{2}\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{u}}\rvert^{2}+2\chi^{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\quad\text{on}\;(0,T_{\mathrm{max}}).\end{split}

The Young inequality applied again to
u^{2}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}
and successively the inequality in (
2.20
), with
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{4A_{3}\chi^{2}}
for
\int_{\Omega}u^{3}
and
\varepsilon=\frac{27}{16A_{3}\chi^{2}}
for
\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{6}
, provide for all
t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})

(4.4)

\begin{split}\frac{dI_{1}(t)}{dt}&\leq-\frac{3}{2}\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{u}}\rvert^{2}+2\chi^{2}\int_{\Omega}u^{3}+\frac{8\chi^{2}}{27}\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{6}\\
&\leq-\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{u}}\rvert^{2}+2A_{1}\chi^{2}\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}+2^{7}A_{2}A_{3}^{3}\chi^{8}\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\right)^{3}\\
&\quad+\frac{8A_{1}\chi^{2}}{27}\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}+\frac{2^{15}A_{2}A_{3}^{3}\chi^{8}}{3^{12}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}\right)^{3}+2\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}.\end{split}

In the last step we invoked as well the inequality
\lvert\nabla\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\rvert^{2}\leq 4\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}
, applied to the integral term
\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla|\nabla v|^{2}|^{2}
.

With the second term
I_{2}(t)
we obtain, once again by relying on the divergence theorem and moreover by virtue of the identity
\Delta(\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2})=2{\nabla{v}}\cdot\nabla{\Delta{v}}+2\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}
, for
t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
:

\begin{split}\frac{dI_{2}(t)}{dt}&=4\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}{\nabla{v}}\cdot\left[\nabla{\Delta{v}}+\alpha{\nabla{w}}-\beta{\nabla{v}}-\gamma v{\nabla{u}}-\gamma u{\nabla{v}}\right]\\
&=4\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}{\nabla{v}}\cdot\nabla{\Delta{v}}+4\alpha\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{w}}-4\beta\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}\\
&\quad-4\gamma\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}v{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{u}}-4\gamma\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}u\\
&=2\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\Delta(\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2})-4\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}+4\alpha\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{w}}-4\beta\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}\\
&\quad-4\gamma\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}v{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{u}}-4\gamma\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}u\\
&=2\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\nabla(\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2})\cdot\nu-2\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\rvert^{2}-4\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}\\
&\quad+4\alpha\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{w}}-4\beta\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}-4\gamma\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}v{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{u}}-4\gamma\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{4}u.\end{split}

In turn, the inequality
\nabla(\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2})\cdot\nu\leq 0
on
\partial\Omega
proved in
[
18
]
, after ignoring nonpositive terms, leads to

\begin{split}\frac{dI_{2}(t)}{dt}&\leq-2\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\rvert^{2}-4\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}+4\alpha\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}{\nabla{v}}\cdot{\nabla{w}}\\

&\leq-2\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\rvert^{2}-4\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{2}\lvert D^{2}v\rvert^{2}+4\left(\alpha+4(\gamma M_{3})^{2}\right)\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{v}}\rvert^{6}\\
&\quad+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{w}}\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}\lvert{\nabla{u}}\rvert^{2},\quad\text{with}\;t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})\end{split}

having exploited the Young inequalities, as well as
\lVert v\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq M_{3}
(recall (
2.4

The inequality in (
2.20
), with the choice

(4.5)

on
(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
.

With analogous steps, last integral
I_{3}(t)
leads, for
t\in(0,T_{\mathrm{max}})
, to

(4.6)

\begin{split}\frac{dI_{3}(t)}{dt}&=2\int_{\Omega}{\nabla{w}}\cdot\left[\nabla{\Delta{w}}-\delta w{\nabla{u}}-\delta u{\nabla{w}}+\mu{\nabla{w}}-2\mu w{\nabla{w}}\right]\\

recalling that
