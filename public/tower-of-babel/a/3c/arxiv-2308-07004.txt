Introduction

Knapsack
is one of the most fundamental problems in
computer science and mathematical optimization, and is actively being studied in fields such as integer programming and fine-grained complexity.
In the Knapsack problem (sometimes also called 0-1 Knapsack), we are given a set
I
of
n

W

J\subseteq I

\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}\leq W
such that the total profit
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}
is maximized.

Knapsack is well-known to be hard: it appeared in Karp’s original list of 21 NP-hard problems

(1-\varepsilon)

\mathrm{SOL}

(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\leq\mathrm{SOL}\leq\mathrm{OPT}
.

fully polynomial-time approximation schemes (FPTASes)

(1-\varepsilon)

Preliminaries

We write
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\dots\}
and
\mathbb{N}^{+}=\{1,2,\dots\}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we write
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
. For a finite set
S
we let
\left\lvert S\right\rvert
be the size of
S
(i.e. the number of elements in
S
). For a sequence
A=\{a_{i}\}
, we let
\left\lvert A\right\rvert
be the length of

and for an interval
[l,r]\in[1,\left\lvert A\right\rvert]

2.1
Problem Statements

In the Knapsack problem, the input is a list of
n
items
(p_{1},w_{1}),\dots,(p_{n},w_{n})\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
together with a knapsack capacity
W\in\mathbb{N}
, and the optimal value is

\mathrm{OPT}:=\max_{J\subseteq[n]}\Big\{\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}\,\Big|\,\sum_{j\in J}w_{j}\leq W\Big\}.

Given a Knapsack instance and a parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, an
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm
is required to output a number
\mathrm{SOL}
such that
(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}\leq\mathrm{SOL}\leq\mathrm{OPT}
.

In this problem, we can assume
n=O(\varepsilon^{-4})
and hence
\log n=O(\log\varepsilon^{-1})
. For larger
n
, Lawler’s algorithm
[
Law79
]
for Knapsack in
O(n\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{4})
time is already near-optimal.

We will sometimes describe algorithms with approximation ratio
1-O(\varepsilon)
(or
1-\tilde{O}(\varepsilon)
), which can be made
1-\varepsilon
by scaling down
\varepsilon
by a constant factor (or a logarithmic factor) at the beginning.

2.2
Knapsack Problem and Profit functions

In the knapsack problem, assume
0<w_{i}\leq W
and
p_{i}>0
for every item
i
. Then a trivial lower bound of the maximum total profit is
\max_{j}p_{j}
. At the beginning, we can discard all items
i
with
p_{i}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{n}\max_{j}p_{j}
, reducing the total profit by at most
\varepsilon\max_{j}p_{j}
, which is only an
O(\varepsilon)
fraction of the optimal total profit. Therefore, we can assume
\frac{\max_{j}p_{j}}{\min_{j}p_{j}}\leq\frac{n}{\varepsilon}
.

We adopt the terminology of Chan
[
Cha18
]
with some modification. We use the term
profit function
to refer to any monotone non-decreasing step function over some domain range
x\in[l,r]
. The
complexity
of a monotone step function refers to the number of its steps. The
(\max,+)
-convolution of two functions
f
with domain
x\in[l_{f},r_{f}]
and
g
with domain
x\in[l_{g},r_{g}]
is a function with domain
x\in[l_{f}+l_{g},r_{f}+r_{g}]
and is defined to be:

(f\oplus g)(x)=\max_{x_{f}\in[l_{f},r_{f}],x_{g}\in[l_{g},r_{g}],x_{f}+x_{g}=x}f(x_{f})+g(x_{g}).

The contour of a profit function with domain
x\in[x_{1},x_{k}]
can be described by a set
P(f)
of
(2k-1)
2-D points

\{(x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2}),(x_{3},y_{2}),\cdots,(x_{k},y_{k-1}),(x_{k},y_{k})\}

with
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{k}
and
y_{1}<y_{2}<\cdots<y_{k}
, where
f(x)=y_{i}
for
x_{i}\leq x<x_{i+1}(1\leq i\leq n)
.

For a set
I
of items, we use
f_{I}
to denote the profit function defined as:

f_{I}(x)=\max\Bigg\{\sum_{i\in J}p_{i}:\sum_{i\in J}w_{i}\leq x,\;\;J\subseteq I\Bigg\}

over
x\in[0,+\infty)
.

Let
I_{1},I_{2}
be two disjoint subsets of items, and
I=I_{1}\cup I_{2}
. It is straightforward to see that
f_{I}=f_{I_{1}}\oplus f_{I_{2}}
.

For each item
i\in I
, we let its
unit profit
be
p_{i}/w_{i}
.

2.3
(1-\delta,\Delta)
approximation up to
t

We sometimes uses the notion of
(1-\delta,\Delta)
-approximation up to
t
, as stated below.

Definition 2.1

(Approximation for Profit Functions)
.

For functions
\tilde{f},f
with identical domains and real numbers
t,\Delta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\delta\in[0,1)
, we say that
\tilde{f}
is a
(1-\delta,\Delta)
approximation of
f
up to
t
, if for all
x
in their domain,

\tilde{f}(x)\leq f(x)

holds, and

\tilde{f}(x)\geq(1-\delta)f(x)-\Delta

holds whenever
f(x)\leq t
.

For the case of
t=+\infty
, we simply omit the phrase “up to
t
.”

We also refer to
(1,\Delta)
approximation as
\Delta
-additive approximation
, and refer to
(1-\delta,0)
approximation as
(1-\delta)
-multiplicative approximation
, or simply
(1-\delta)
approximation
.

We now prove the following fact:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
I_{1},I_{2}
be two disjoint subsets of items.
For
i\in\{1,2\}
, suppose
\tilde{f}_{i}
is a
(1-\delta,\Delta_{i})
approximation of the profit function
f_{I_{i}}
up to
t
.
Then,
f=\tilde{f}_{1}\oplus\tilde{f}_{2}
is a
(1-\delta,\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2})
approximation of
f_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}
up to
t
.

Proof.

For any
b\in[0,t]
where
f(b)=f_{1}(b_{1})+f_{2}(b_{2})
for
b_{1}+b_{2}=b
and
b_{1},b_{2}\in[0,t]
, we have for
i\in[1,2]
,
(1-\delta)f_{i}(b_{i})-\Delta_{i}\leq f_{I_{i}}(b_{i})
. Since
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
are disjoint,
f_{I_{1}\cup I_{2}}(b)\geq f_{I_{1}}(b_{1})+f_{I_{2}}(b_{2})
. Hence,
f_{I_{1}}(b_{1})+f_{I_{2}}(b_{2})\leq(1-\delta)f_{1}(b_{1})-\Delta_{1}+(1-\delta)f_{2}(b_{2})-\Delta_{2}=(1-\delta)(f_{1}(b_{1})+f_{2}(b_{2}))-(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2})=(1-\Delta)f(b)-(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{2})
.

The converse direction can be verified similarly.
∎

Following Chan
[
Cha18
]
and Jin
[
Jin19
]
, given a monotone step function
f
with range contained in
\{-\infty,0\}\cup[A,B]
, one can
round the positive values of
f
down to powers of
1/(1-\varepsilon)
, and obtain another profit function
\tilde{f}
which has complexity only
O(\varepsilon^{-1}\log(B/A))
, and
(1-\varepsilon)
-approximates
f
.
In our algorithm we will always have
B/A\leq\operatorname{\mathrm{poly}}(n/\varepsilon)
, so we may always assume that the intermediate profit functions computed during our algorithm are monotone step functions with complexity
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
after rounding.

3.3.1
Chan’s Framework

We can easily see that the extra running time incurred by the reduction in

Improving the running time for
1
and for Knapsack have been very challenging tasks in the recent years. This is mainly due to the inability to go around the trade-off between two different approaches: the sequential approach that is efficient at the cost of precision, and the divide-and-conquer approach that is precise at the cost of efficiency. We now briefly introduce them and will limit our discussion on
1
for now for simplicity.

In the sequential approach, we go through items in
I
one by one and update the profit function. The classical dynamic programming solution to
knapsack
can be seen as an analog in the deterministic case. Suppose the total profit value of items seen so far is
P_{i}:=\sum_{j\leq i}{p_{j}}
. The profit function for the first
i
items has a complexity of
O(P_{i})
and updating the profit function with the addition of a single item can easily be done in
O(P_{i})
time. In the approximate case, we perform rounding on the profit function after each addition. Since errors from sequential rounding add up, to guarantee a final additive error of
n\varepsilon
, one has to round to multiples of
\varepsilon
. Since
P_{i}=O(n)
, this unfortunately makes the complexities of profit functions
O(n\varepsilon^{-1})
and the total running time becomes
O(n^{2}\varepsilon^{-1})=O(\varepsilon^{-3})
.

An alternative method is to use divide and conquer. Without loss of generality assume that
\left\lvert I\right\rvert
is a power of
2
, We divide
I
into two equal parts
I=X\cup Y
. We approximate the profit functions
f_{X}
and
f_{Y}
recursively and approximate
f_{I}=f_{X}\oplus f_{Y}
. Every time we are in a sub-problem with total profit value
P
, we round to multiples of
P\varepsilon
, and we can show that the total additive error is
O(n\varepsilon\log n)=\tilde{O}(n\varepsilon)
. Thus we can keep the complexities of profit functions
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
. However, we have to merge two profit functions of complexity
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
for each of the
O(n)
sub-problems in the entire recursion tree, which is a
(\max,+)
-convolution that generally takes quadratic time. Therefore we still only have a running time of
\tilde{O}(n\varepsilon^{-2})=\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-3})
.

As shown by Chan
[
Cha18
]
, assuming that
n=O(\varepsilon^{-1})
, one can achieve a running time of
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-2.5})
by combining these two ideas: divide
I
into
\varepsilon^{-0.5}
chunks of size
O(\varepsilon^{-0.5})
, solve for each chunk sequentially and use divide-and-conquer to merge the answers for chunks. Chan’s original version was a slightly more complicated algorithm directly for Knapsack with a running time of
\tilde{O}(n+\varepsilon^{-2.5})
as the reduction of the form of
Lemma

3.12
did not exist back then.

Obtaining further improvements for either
1
or Knapsack has been an arduous endeavor, and such improvements have all been based on Chan’s framework. In the same work, Chan observed that in the sequential method, instead of repeated rounding at each item, if all
p_{i}
’s are close to multiples of some large number

, we can simply round everything to multiples of
p^{\prime}
at the beginning. Chan employed a number theoretical lemma stating that it is possible to group items into groups where items in each group is close to multiples of some large number, and achieved a running time of
\tilde{O}(n+\varepsilon^{-12/5})
for Knapsack and
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-7/3})
for
1
. Jin
[
Jin19
]
extended the technique to multiple layers and combined it with greedy, resulting in a running time of
\tilde{O}(n+\varepsilon^{-9/4})
for Knapsack and
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-9/4})
for
1
. The reduction in
Lemma

3.12
was proposed in the algorithm by Deng, Jin and Mao
[
DJM23
]
, which used Jin’s previous results as ingredients. They also used a randomized partitioning technique for limiting the scope of
(\max,+)
-convolution to achieve a running time of
\tilde{O}(\varepsilon^{-11/5})
for
1
and a running time of
\tilde{O}(n+\varepsilon^{-11/5})
for Knapsack. As we can see, not only have improvements been more and more marginal, but the algorithms that achieve them have also increased in complexity.

3.3.2
Sparsification for Subset Sum and Its Potential on Knapsack

Researchers have struggled much less on the related problem of approximating subset-sum. The first algorithm with a running time of
\tilde{O}(n/\varepsilon)
was known in the 70s
[
GL79
]
, and the first algorithm that achieves a running time of
\tilde{O}(n+\varepsilon^{-2})
was known in 1997
[

]

improved the running time to

-convolution hypothesis. Bringmann and Nakos showed the sparsification technique for reducing the size of sets of subset-sums we need to store without losing any precision, which, if employed to knapsack, could give us a best-of-both-worlds solution that achieves efficiency and precision at the same time.

We will give a brief introduction of this technique. For simplicity, we only discuss additive approximation. A
\Delta
-additive approximation of a set
S
is a set
S^{\prime}
such that
\forall x\in S,\exists x^{\prime}\in S^{\prime},x-\Delta\leq x^{\prime}\leq x

\forall x^{\prime}\in S^{\prime},\exists x\in S,x-\Delta\leq x^{\prime}\leq x

S
be a set of numbers. To “sparsify”

, we recursively do the following: if there exists

such that

, remove
v
from
S
. In the resulting set, for every three consecutive numbers

, we have

. Thus if

, then the sparsification
S^{\prime}
has a size of
O(B/\Delta)
. Suppose we have two sets
S_{X}
and
S_{Y}
representing possible subset sums of two disjoint sets
X
and
Y
, and we want to approximate

which is the set of possible subset sums of

. Suppose the sparsifications of
S_{X}
and
S_{y}
are

and
\hat{S}_{Y}
. It is proven in

]
that the set

\Delta
-approximation (rather than

-approximation) of the set

. In other words, individual rounding errors do not accumulate.

Unfortunately, the simplest analog of sparsification for profit function fails to prevent accumulation of rounding errors. Suppose that to “sparsify” a profit function

The core idea in this paper is a sophisticated way of sparsifying profit functions without accumulation of rounding errors. The sparsified results can be described by a small amount of convex hulls on a 2D plane. We will use techniques in computational geometry to merge these convex hulls, resulting in the final quadratic running time.

Open Problems

Although this paper closes the gap between the best algorithm and the conditional lower bound for the Knapsack problem, there are still some open problems in this field.

•

Can we make our algorithm simple? Intuitively, a “simple” algorithm should not use any results from additive combinatorics, or a technique analogous to Chan’s number theoretical lemma. Despite the fact that our algorithm for

2.3

•

Can we shave an

factor from our algorithm? The previous algorithm from
[

]
has a running time of
\tilde{O}\left(n+(\frac{1}{\varepsilon})^{11/5}/2^{\Omega(\sqrt{\log(1/\varepsilon)})}\right)
