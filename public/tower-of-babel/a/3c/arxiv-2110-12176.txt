New Methods for MLE of Toeplitz Structured Covariance Matrices with Applications to RADAR Problems
Augusto Aubry, Prabhu Babu, Antonio De Maio, Rikhabchand Jyothi

Abstract
This work considers Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) of a Toeplitz structured covariance matrix. In this regard, an equivalent reformulation of the MLE problem is introduced and two iterative algorithms are proposed for the optimization of the equivalent problem. Both the strategies are based on the Majorization Minimization (MM) framework and hence enjoy nice properties such as monotonicity and ensured convergence to a stationary point of the equivalent MLE problem. The proposed algorithms are also extended to deal with MLE of other related covariance structures, namely, the banded Toeplitz, Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz, low rank Toeplitz structure plus a scalar matrix (accounting for white noise), and finally Toeplitz matrices satisfying a condition number constraint. Through numerical simulations, it is shown that new methods provide satisfactory performance levels in terms of both mean square estimation error and signal-to-interference-plus-noise ratio.

Both ATOM1 and ATOM2 involve the construction of a surrogate function together with its optimization. Specifically, the two procedures construct distinct surrogate functions and therefore solve different surrogate minimization problems. While ATOM1 addresses the surrogate minimization problem using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM), ATOM2 handles it via alternating projection or Dykstra’s algorithm. ATOM2 is also extended to include other constraints, such as an upper bound to the condition number and a low rank plus white noise covariance structure. Finally, ATOM2 is modified to deal with banded Toeplitz and Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz structures. The major contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

1.

Two iterative algorithms ATOM1 and ATOM2 are proposed based on the MM framework to address MLE of a Toeplitz covariance matrix. Their computational complexities are thoroughly discussed. Also, the convergence of the procedures to a stationary point of the MLE problem is established.

2.

The extensions of ATOM2 to handle additional covariance structures, such as banded Toeplitz, Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz, and low rank Toeplitz term plus a scalar matrix are discussed, together with the possibility to account for a condition number constraint.

3.

The Cramer-Rao lower bounds for the MLE estimation of Toeplitz, banded Toeplitz, and Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz covariance matrices are derived.

4.

The proposed algorithms and their extensions are compared with some state-of-the-art procedures via numerical simulations using the Mean Square Error (MSE) and Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR) (for case studies related to radar application) as performance metrics.

Let us assume the availability of

III
Algorithms for Toeplitz covariance matrix estimation

In this section, ATOM1 and ATOM2 are proposed to tackle the MLE problem of TSC matrix. Both leverage the MM principle (applied to an equivalent reformulation of the MLE problem) and differ in the way they construct and handle the surrogate minimization problem. ATOM1 solves the surrogate optimization using ADMM while ATOM2 tackles it using alternating projection or Dykstra’s algorithm. Hence the computational complexity and proof of convergence of the procedures are established. Finally, the extension of ATOM2 to deal with additional covariance constraints along with the Toeplitz structure is provided.

{\boldsymbol{V}}
are drawn randomly from a uniform distribution over
[0,1]^{mn}
. For
t\geq 1
, the matrices
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{t}_{0}
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}^{t}_{0}
can be initialized with their last value after convergence at the previous ADMM iteration, respectively. Another input parameter required by ATOM1 is the penalty parameter
\rho
, introduced during the formation of the Augmented Lagrangian of the ADMM algorithm. It is shown in
[
32
]
, that the ADMM algorithm converges for any value of
\rho>0
. However, the numerical stability and the convergence rate of the algorithm depends on the choice of
\rho
. Simulation results have highlighted that for
\rho=m
, the ADMM algorithm is stable for different values of
n
and
m
. Hence, unless otherwise stated, in all the numerical analysis the penalty parameter
\rho=m
is used.

III-B

Computational complexity of ATOM1

ATOM1 is iterative in nature with two loops - the outer loop updates the Toeplitz matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
while the inner loop solves the surrogate minimization problem using ADMM. Note that in the inner loop, one requires to compute the matrix
\dfrac{1}{n}\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}^{H}
- which is iteration independent and hence can be pre-computed. Moreover, this matrix can be evaluated efficiently leveraging an equivalent formulation of Problems (
10
) and (
11
). In this respect, first obtain the Cholesky decomposition of the matrix
{\mathbf{R}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
, i.e., compute
{\mathbf{R}}_{\textrm{SCM}}=\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}^{H}
(where
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}
is a lower triangular matrix) with a complexity of
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})
. Then, Problem (
11
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}=\underset{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in Toep,{\boldsymbol{X}}\succeq 0}{\rm arg\>min}\>\textrm{Tr}\left(({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t})^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right)\>\>{\rm s.t.}\quad\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}]_{i}^{H}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{-1}[\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}]_{i}\leq 1\end{array}

(19)

Casting the above problem as an SDP it follows that

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}=\underset{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in Toep,\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}}{\rm arg\>min}\>\textrm{Tr}\left(({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t})^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right)\>\>{\rm s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}\geq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\end{array}

(20)

where
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}=\bar{{\mathbf{r}}}\bar{{\mathbf{r}}}^{H}
and the vector
\bar{{\mathbf{r}}}\in\mathbb{C}^{m^{2}\times 1}
is obtained by vectorizing the matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}
. Comparing the constraint of Problem (
20
) and (
11
), it can be observed that instead of computing the matrix
\dfrac{1}{n}\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}\bar{{\mathbf{y}}}^{H}
, one needs to evaluate
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
. Exploiting the sparseness of the vector
\bar{{\mathbf{r}}}
, the matrix
\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
can be determined with a cost
of
\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\dfrac{m(m+1)}{2}\right)^{2}\right)

[
34
]
. Let us now discuss the complexity related to the outer and inner loops of ATOM1. In this regard, note that the inner loop of ATOM1 requires the computation of the matrix
({{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}})^{-1}
- which is outer loop iteration dependent. Therefore, this matrix can be computed once in the outer loop. Consequently, apart from the computations of the inner loop, an outer loop cycle requires only the estimation of the matrix
({{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}})^{-1}
. This in-turn can be efficiently computed using Gohberg–Semencul formula with a complexity
\mathcal{O}(m\>{\rm{log}}m)

[
2
]
. The computational complexity of an inner loop cycle is related to the projection of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
onto the set of PSD matrices and projection of
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
onto the set of Toeplitz matrices.
The cost of projection of
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
onto the set of Toeplitz matrices is mainly dictated by the computation of average of the elements along the respective diagonals of
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}
. Hence, the cost of matrix projection onto the Toepltiz matrices set is
\mathcal{O}(m^{2})
. Next, the projection of
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
onto the set of PSD matrices mainly involves the computation of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
- whose corresponding complexity is
\mathcal{O}({m^{6}})

[
34
]
. Therefore, the computational complexity of ATOM1 is
\mathcal{O}(\eta{m^{6}})
where
\eta
is the total number of inner loop iterations required by the algorithm to converge.
A drawback of ATOM1 is that it has a restriction in handling additional constraints on the covariance matrix. This is because ATOM1 implements ADMM algorithm at every inner iteration which requires the optimization problem to exhibit the standard form
[
32
,
35
]

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\boldsymbol{Z}}},{\boldsymbol{E}}}{\rm minimize}&f({\boldsymbol{Z}})+g({\boldsymbol{E}})\\
{\rm subject\ to}&\>\>{\mathbf{A}}_{1}{\boldsymbol{Z}}+{\mathbf{A}}_{2}{\boldsymbol{E}}={\boldsymbol{C}}\end{array}

(21)

where
f({\boldsymbol{Z}})
,
g({\boldsymbol{E}})
are convex functions and
{\mathbf{A}}_{1}
,
{\mathbf{A}}_{2}
,
{\boldsymbol{C}}
are matrices of appropriate dimensions, respectively. Therefore, to incorporate additional inequality constraints (such as an upper bound on the condition number of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
), one needs to replace the inequality constraint with an equality constraint. This can be done by introducing a slack variable to the existing optimization variables
{\boldsymbol{Z}}
and
{\boldsymbol{E}}
. However, there is no convergence guarantee of ADMM when there are more than two optimization variables
[
36
]
. This issue is addressed in the following by proposing another low complexity algorithm, referred to as ATOM2, to solve Problem (
11
).

III-C

ATOM 2

To develop a computationally efficient and flexible estimation procedure capable of including additional constraints, a different surrogate function is constructed for the objective function in Problem (
P_{2}
). To this end, let us once again refer to Problem (
11
) cast in the equivalent (but computationally convenient) form

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\boldsymbol{X}}\in Toep,{\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}}}}{\rm minimize}\>\textrm{Tr}\left(({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t})^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right)\\
{\rm subject\ to}\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\end{array}

(22)

and suitably constructing its surrogate function. In this respect, adding and subtracting
\textrm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{2})
in the objective function yields

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in Toep,\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}}{\rm minimize}\>\textrm{Tr}\left(({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t})^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right)+\textrm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{2})-\textrm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{2})\\
{\rm subject\ to}\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\end{array}

(23)

Note that
-\textrm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{2})
is a concave function of
{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
and hence given a feasible solution
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
, it is possible to construct the surrogate function
\tilde{g}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})
for the above problem using Lemma
III.2

\begin{array}[]{ll}\tilde{g}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})=\textrm{Tr}\left(({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t})^{-1}{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\right)+\textrm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}^{2})-2\textrm{Tr}({{\boldsymbol{X}}}{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{t})\end{array}

(24)

Note that
\tilde{g}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})
is a surrogate to a surrogate function. Nonetheless, since
\tilde{g}({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})
is a tighter surrogate for
g({\boldsymbol{X}}|{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t})
, it can be viewed as a direct surrogate for the objective function in Problem (
P_{2}
). Then, at any iteration
t
, given
{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}
, an optimal solution to the following surrogate minimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t+1}=\underset{{{\boldsymbol{X}}}\in Toep}{\rm arg\>min}\>\|{\boldsymbol{X}}-{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
\hskip 42.67912pt{\rm subject\ to}\quad\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}={\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{X}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\end{array}

(25)

is required, where
{\boldsymbol{B}}={{{\boldsymbol{X}}}}_{t}-0.5({{\boldsymbol{X}}}_{t})^{-1}
.
An efficient way to solve Problem (
25
) is now devised. To this end, let us cast (
25
) in terms of a single block diagonal matrix
{\mathbf{T}}
as follows

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\mathbf{T}}}}{\rm minimize}\>\|{\mathbf{T}}-{\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
{\rm subject\ to}\>\>{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\\
\hskip 49.79231pt{{\mathbf{T}}\>\textrm{is a block diagonal matrix with the same diagonal Toeplitz blocks}}\end{array}

(26)

Problem (
26
) seeks for the point
{\mathbf{T}}
belonging to the intersection of the two sets - the set of block diagonal matrices with the same diagonal Toeplitz blocks and the set defined by the Linear Matrix Inequality (LMI)
{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
- closest to
{\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}
. Since both the sets are convex, one can use the technique of alternating projection or Projection Onto the Convex Sets (POCS) to solve Problem (
26
)
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. To describe this algorithm, let us denote by
{\mathcal{P}_{Toep}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
and
{\mathcal{P}_{LMI}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
the orthogonal projection of a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
onto the set of block diagonal matrices with the same diagonal Toeplitz blocks and onto the set defined by
{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
, respectively. Both
{\mathcal{P}_{Toep}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
and
{\mathcal{P}_{LMI}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
can be obtained as follows:

1.

Calculation of
{\mathcal{P}_{Toep}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
:

Given a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
, the orthogonal projection of the matrix onto the set of block diagonal matrices with the same diagonal Toeplitz blocks can be obtained in two steps. First the averaging of the diagonal blocks of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
is performed. Then, the elements along each diagonal of the matrix obtained in the previous step are averaged.

2.

Calculation of
{\mathcal{P}_{LMI}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
:

Given a matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}
, its orthogonal projection onto the set defined by LMI
{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
can be obtained as follows. First, calculate the EigenValue Decomposition (EVD) of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}-\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
, i.e., obtain
[\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}},\bar{{\boldsymbol{V}}}]={\rm{eig}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}}-\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}})
, where
\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}}
and
\bar{{\boldsymbol{V}}}
are matrices containing the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the spectral decomposition, respectively. Then, the orthogonal projection
{\mathcal{P}_{LMI}}({\boldsymbol{\Psi}})
is given by
\bar{{\boldsymbol{V}}}{\rm{max}}(\bar{{\boldsymbol{U}}},{\boldsymbol{0}})\bar{{\boldsymbol{V}}}^{H}+\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
.

According to POCS method, given an initial value
{\mathbf{T}}_{0}={\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}
, first compute
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{k+1}={\mathcal{P}_{Toep}}({\mathbf{T}}_{k})
and then, using
{\boldsymbol{Y}}_{k+1}
, determine
{\mathbf{T}}_{k+1}={\mathcal{P}_{LMI}}({\boldsymbol{Y}}_{k+1})
, i.e., the starting point of the next iteration. Hence, POCS finds a sequence of iterates
{\mathbf{T}}_{k}
by alternatingly projecting between the two convex sets. However, as reported in
[
40
]
, POCS may suffer from slow convergence. A refinement of POCS is Dykstra’s projection
[
41
,
42
]
which finds a point closest to
{\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}
by adding correction vectors
{\mathbf{P}}_{k}
and
{\boldsymbol{Q}}_{k}
before every projection, which in-turn ensures convergence of sequence
{\mathbf{T}}_{k+1}
to the optimal solution
{{\mathbf{T}}}^{*}

[
41
]
. The pseudocode of Dykstra’s algorithm is shown in

III-D

Proof of convergence

In this subsection, the proof of convergence of ATOM1 and ATOM2 is established. In this regard, it is worth pointing out that both the algorithms differ in the way they construct and optimize the surrogate function for the Problem (

Before stating the lemma, let us first introduce the first-order optimality condition for minimizing a function over a convex constraint set. A point
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is a stationary point of
f(\cdot)
if
f^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{X}};{\boldsymbol{D}})\geq 0
for all
{\boldsymbol{D}}
such that
{\boldsymbol{X}}+{\boldsymbol{D}}\in\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the convex constraint set and
f^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{X}};{\boldsymbol{D}})
is the directional derivative of
f(\cdot)
at point
{\boldsymbol{X}}
in direction
{\boldsymbol{D}}
and is defined as
[
26
]

\begin{array}[]{ll}f^{\prime}({\boldsymbol{X}};{\boldsymbol{D}})=\underset{\lambda\downarrow 0}{\lim}\>\textrm{inf}\>\dfrac{f({\boldsymbol{X}}+\lambda{\boldsymbol{D}})-f({\boldsymbol{X}})}{\lambda}\end{array}

(27)

Based on the following lemma, both ATOM1 and ATOM2 are guaranteed to converge to a stationary point of Problem (
P_{2}
)

Denoting by
\{{\boldsymbol{X}}_{t}\}

III-F

Extensions of ATOM2

The extension of ATOM2 to handle additional constraints other than the Toeplitz structure in the covariance estimation process is now addressed. In particular, it is shown that ATOM2 can be generalized to account for the following scenarios: Banded Toeplitz matrices, Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz matrices, low rank matrices with Toeplitz structure plus a scalar matrix, and finally, matrices with Toeplitz structure satisfying a condition number constraint. However, as discussed at the end of Subsection
III-B
, ATOM1 cannot be directly extended to tackle the general constraints as for instance an upper bound requirement to the condition number.

1.

MLE of banded Toeplitz covariance matrix

The covariance matrix is constrained to exhibit a banded Toeplitz structure of bandwidth
b
(see
[
17
,
48
]
for relevant applications). For instance, assuming a bandwidth
b=2
and dimension
m=5
the covariance matrix enjoys the following structure

{\mathbf{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}&r_{2}&r_{3}&0&0\\
r^{*}_{2}&r_{1}&r_{2}&r_{3}&0\\
r^{*}_{3}&r^{*}_{2}&r_{1}&r_{2}&r_{3}\\
0&r^{*}_{3}&r^{*}_{2}&r_{1}&r_{2}\\
0&0&r^{*}_{3}&r^{*}_{2}&r_{1}\end{bmatrix}

Then, the MLE problem for banded Toeplitz covariance matrix can be formulated as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\mathbf{R}}\in Band-Toep,\>{\mathbf{R}}\succ 0}{\rm minimize}\>\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{H}{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+\log|{\mathbf{R}}|\end{array}

(36)

where
Band-Toep
is used to denote the set of banded Toeplitz matrices. Exploiting again Lemma
III.1
, the above problem can be cast in the following equivalent form

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\boldsymbol{X}}\in Band-Toep,\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}}}{\rm minimize}\>\log|{\boldsymbol{X}}|\\
\hskip 17.07164pt{\rm subject\ to}\quad\>\boldsymbol{\hat{X}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\end{array}

(37)

Hence (
37
) is handled via MM framework solving the following surrogate minimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\mathbf{T}}}}{\rm minimize}\>\|{\mathbf{T}}-{\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
{\rm subject\ to}\>\>{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\\
\hskip 49.79231pt{{\mathbf{T}}\>\textrm{is a block diagonal matrix with the same diagonal banded Toeplitz blocks}}\end{array}

(38)

The above problem involves two convex sets: the set defined by the LMI
{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}
and the set of block diagonal matrices with each block being equal and having a banded Toeplitz structure with bandwidth
b
. Consequently, Dykstra’s projection algorithm or POCS can be used to solve Problem (
38
). The projection of a matrix onto the LMI set can be calculated as discussed earlier in Subsection
III-C
. The projection of a matrix
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
onto the set of block banded Toeplitz matrices can be obtained in two steps. In the first step, averaging the diagonal blocks of the matrix
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
is performed. In the next step, the elements outside the bandwidth are replaced with zeros. In contrast, the elements within the bandwidth are replaced with the average of the respective diagonal elements of the matrix obtained in the first step
[
49
]
.

3.
MLE of Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz covariance matrix

In space-time adaptive processing radar applications, the covariance matrix exhibits a Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz (TBT) structure. This refers to a block Toeplitz matrix with each block having a Toeplitz structure
[
50
,
51
]
. An example of a TBT-structured covariance matrix with
p
blocks is shown below

{\mathbf{R}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathbf{R}}_{1}&{\mathbf{R}}_{2}&\dots&{\mathbf{R}}_{p}\\
{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{2}&{\mathbf{R}}_{1}&\dots&{\mathbf{R}}_{2}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{p}&\dots&{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{2}&{\mathbf{R}}_{1}\end{bmatrix}

The MLE problem of TBT covariance matrix is formulated as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\mathbf{R}}\in TBT,{\mathbf{R}}\succ 0}{\rm minimize}\>\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{H}{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+\log|{\mathbf{R}}|\end{array}

(39)

where the notation
TBT
is used to indicate the set of Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz matrices. The minimizer of (
39
) is obtained by solving at any given step the following surrogate optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\mathbf{T}}}}{\rm minimize}\>\|{\mathbf{T}}-{\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
{\rm subject\ to}\>\>{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\\
\hskip 49.79231pt{{\mathbf{T}}\>\textrm{is a block diagonal matrix with the same diagonal TBT blocks}}\end{array}

(40)

Problem (
40
) exhibits two constraints -
1
) a LMI constraint and
2
) a structural constraint - where the optimization variable
{\mathbf{T}}
is confined to be a block diagonal matrix with each block being equal and having a TBT structure. Since both the constraints are convex, Dykstra’s projection or POCS can be applied to solve Problem (
40
). The projection of a matrix onto the LMI set can be calculated as discussed earlier in Section
III
B. The projection of a given matrix
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
onto the set of matrices having the TBT constraint can be obtained as follows. First, a matrix
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}_{\rm{Avg}}
is obtained by averaging the diagonal blocks of the matrix
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
. Next
p
matrices are obtained by averaging the diagonal blocks of the matrix
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}_{\rm{Avg}}
from the preceding step. Finally, each of the
p
matrices are projected onto the Toeplitz set as described in Section
III
C
[
49
]
.

4.
MLE of Toeplitz covariance matrix with condition number constraint

In this extension, a condition number constraint
\kappa
(assumed to be known) in addition to the Toeplitz structural constraint on the covariance matrix (see for applications
[
52
,
53
]
) is considered. The MLE problem can be formulated as

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{\mathbf{R}}\in Toep,{\mathbf{R}}\succ 0}{\rm minimize}\>\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{H}{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+\log|{\mathbf{R}}|\\
{\rm subject\ to}\>\>\>\dfrac{\lambda_{max}({\mathbf{R}})}{\lambda_{min}({\mathbf{R}})}\leq\kappa\end{array}

(41)

To get a minimizer of the above problem, the steps used in ATOM2 are borrowed to arrive at the following surrogate optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{ll}\underset{{{\mathbf{T}}}}{\rm minimize}\>\|{\mathbf{T}}-{\mathbf{I}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{F}^{2}\\
{\rm subject\ to}\>\>{\mathbf{T}}\succeq\bar{{\mathbf{R}}}_{\textrm{SCM}}\\
\hskip 49.79231pt{{\mathbf{T}}\>\textrm{is a block diagonal matrix with the same diagonal Toeplitz blocks}}\\
\hskip 49.79231pt\textrm{whose condition number is upper bounded by\>}\kappa\end{array}

(42)

The above problem includes three constraints: the LMI constraint, the structural constraint which confines the covariance matrix to follow a Toeplitz structure, and the condition number constraint. All the three constraints are convex and therefore, Dykstra’s projection or POCS can be applied to solve Problem (
42
). The projection of a given matrix
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
onto
\dfrac{\lambda_{max}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}})}{\lambda_{min}(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}})}\leq\kappa
is now discussed. Given a matrix
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
, let
{\boldsymbol{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}{\boldsymbol{V}}^{H}
denote its EVD with
{\boldsymbol{\Gamma}}=\rm{diag}([\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\cdots,\gamma_{m}]^{T})
where
\gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\gamma_{m}
and the columns of
{\boldsymbol{V}}
contain the corresponding eigenvectors. Then, the projection of
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}
onto the condition number constraint is given by
[
21
]
:
{\boldsymbol{V}}{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}{\boldsymbol{V}}^{H}
where
{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}={\rm{diag}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(u^{*}))
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(u)=[\lambda_{1}(u),\lambda_{2}(u),\cdots,\lambda_{n}(u)]^{T}
with

\lambda_{i}(u)=\min\left(\kappa u,\max\left(\gamma_{i},\max\left(0,u\right)\right)\right)\>\>i=1,2,\cdots,m

(43)

and
u^{*}
can be obtained by solving a convex optimization problem with a linear computational complexity, see
[
52
]
for more details. The projection onto the convex sets defined by the LMI and Toeplitz structure can be evidently handled as discussed in Subsection
III-C
.

IV
Cramer-Rao Lower bound calculation

In this section, the Cramer-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) is derived for three structured covariance matrices, namely, the Toeplitz, the banded Toeplitz, and the TBT structures. The CRLB provides a lower bound on the variance of any unbiased estimator
[
54
]
. To proceed further, let
\theta
represent the real value vector parametrizing a given covariance matrix structure of interest. The specific definition of
\theta
is provided in the next subsections for each case study. Then, the CRLB is the inverse of the Fisher Information matrix (FIM) whose
(i,k)^{th}
element is

\begin{array}[]{ll}[{\mathbf{F}}]_{i,k}=\textbf{E}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\log f({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{R}})}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{k}}\right]\end{array}

(44)

where
f({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{R}})
is the likelihood function i.e.,
f({\mathbf{y}};{\mathbf{R}})=\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}^{H}{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}{\mathbf{y}}_{i}+\log|{\mathbf{R}}|
. The
(i,k)^{th}
element of the FIM can be computed using the
Slepian–Bangs
formula

[
2
]

[{\mathbf{F}}]_{i,k}=n\textrm{Tr}\left({\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{k}}\right)

(45)

In the following subsections, the lower bounds on
{\mathbf{R}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for some covariance structures considered in the manuscript are derived.

IV-A

Toeplitz matrix

As the entries of the TSC matrix are completely characterized by its first row, i.e.,
[r_{1},r_{2},\cdots r_{m}]^{T}
, the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{H}^{m\times m}
can be parameterized by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[r_{1},\Re(r_{2}),\cdots\Re(r_{m}),\\
\Im(r_{2}),...,\Im(r_{m})]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m-1}
where
\Re(r_{i})
and
\Im(r_{i})
denotes the real and imaginary parts of
r_{i}
, respectively. Then, the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{R}}
can be expressed in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and basis matrices
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g}
(defined as in (
47
)),
g=1,2,\cdots,m

[
16
]

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{R}}=\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{m}\theta_{g}{\Re}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g})+j\displaystyle\sum_{g=m+1}^{2m-1}\theta_{g}{\Im}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g-m+1})\end{array}

(46)

The
(i,k)^{th}
element of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g}
is given as

\begin{array}[]{ll}[{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g}]_{i,k}=\begin{cases}1&i-k=g-1=0\\
1+j&k-i=g-1\neq 0\\
1-j&i-k=g-1\neq 0\\
0&\rm{otherwise}\end{cases}\end{array}

(47)

Using (
46
),
\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}
can be obtained as

\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\begin{cases}\Re({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i})&1\leq i\leq m\\
j\Im({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i-m+1})&m+1\leq i\leq 2m-1\end{cases}

Substituting
\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}
in (
45
), yields the FIM for Toeplitz covariance matrix. Then, the CRLB on
r_{i}
is

\begin{cases}[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i,i}&i=1\\
[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i,i}+[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i+m-1,i+m-1}&i\neq 1\end{cases}

IV-B

Banded Toepltiz matrix

In the case of banded Toeplitz matrix with bandwidth
b
, the first row of the covariance matrix
{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{H}^{m\times m}
has only
b+1
non-zero terms. Therefore,
{\mathbf{R}}
can be parameterized via
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[r_{1},\Re(r_{2}),\cdots\Re(r_{b+1})\\
,\Im(r_{2}),...,\Im(r_{b+1})]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2b+1}
. Besides
{\mathbf{R}}
can be expressed in terms of basis matrices
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g}
and real coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{R}}=\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{b+1}\theta_{g}{\Re}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g})+j\displaystyle\sum_{g=b+2}^{2b+1}\theta_{g}{\Im}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{Toep}_{g-b})\end{array}

(48)

and consequently

\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\begin{cases}{\Re}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i})&1\leq i\leq b+1\\
j{\Im}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i-b})&b+2\leq i\leq 2b+1\end{cases}

Substituting
\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}
in (
45
), yields the FIM for banded Toeplitz covariance matrix and the CRLB on
r_{i}
can be obtained as

\begin{cases}[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i,i}&i=1\\
[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i,i}+[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i+b,i+b}&i\neq 1\end{cases}

IV-C

Toeplitz-block-Toeplitz matrix

A TBT matrix with
p
blocks of size
l
can be specified by its first row i.e.,
[r_{1},{r_{2}},\cdots,{r_{l}},\cdots,r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+1},\\
{r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+2}},\cdots,{r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+l}}]^{T}
. As a consequence, a TBT matrix can be expressed as follows

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{R}}^{\rm{TBT}}={\boldsymbol{C}}_{0}\otimes{\mathbf{R}}_{0}+\displaystyle\sum_{w=1}^{{p-1}}\left(\left({\boldsymbol{C}}_{w}\otimes{\mathbf{R}}_{w}\right)+\left({\boldsymbol{C}}_{w}^{T}\otimes{\mathbf{R}}^{*}_{w}\right)\right)\end{array}

(49)

where
(i,k)^{th}
element of the matrix
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times l}
is given by

[{\boldsymbol{C}}_{w}]_{i,k}=\begin{cases}1&i-k=w\\
0&\rm{otherwise}\end{cases}

and
{\mathbf{R}}_{w}
is

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathbf{R}}_{w}=\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{l}\theta_{(g+qw)}{\Re}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g})+j\displaystyle\sum_{g={l+1}}^{2l-1}\theta_{(g+qw)}{\Im}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{Toep}_{g-l+1})\end{array}

(50)

where
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=[r_{1},\Re({r_{2}}),\cdots,\Re({r_{l}}),\Im({r_{2}}),\cdots,\Im({r_{l}}),\cdots,r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+1},\Re({r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+2}}),\cdots,\Re({r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+l}}),\\
\Im({r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+2}}),\cdots,\Im({r_{(p-1)(2l-1)+l}})]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{(2l-1)p}
and
{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{g}
of size
\l\times l
is defined in (
47
). Let
\theta_{i}
belong to the
z^{th}
block and
q=2l-1
, then
\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}
is given by

\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}=\begin{cases}{\boldsymbol{C}}_{0}\otimes\Re({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i})&1\leq i\leq{l},z=0\\
j{\boldsymbol{C}}_{0}\otimes\Im({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i-l+1})&{l}+1\leq i\leq q,z=0\\
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{z}\otimes\Re({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i-qz})+{\boldsymbol{C}}_{z}^{T}\otimes{\Re}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i-qz})&1+qz\leq i\leq{l}+qz,z\neq 0\\
j{\boldsymbol{C}}_{z}\otimes{\Im}({\boldsymbol{B}}^{\rm{Toep}}_{i-qz-l+1})-j{\boldsymbol{C}}_{z}^{T}\otimes{\Im}({{\boldsymbol{B}}^{*}}^{{\rm{Toep}}}_{i-qz-l+1})&{l}+1+qz\leq i\leq q+qz,z\neq 0\\
\end{cases}

Substituting
\frac{\partial{\mathbf{R}}}{\partial\theta_{i}}
in (
45
), the FIM for TBT covariance matrix is obtained. Then, the CRLB on
r_{i}
is

\begin{cases}[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{2i-(z+1),2i-(z+1)}&i=1,l+1,\cdots,(p-1)l+1\\
[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i+2z,i+2z}+[{\mathbf{F}}^{-1}]_{i+l-1+2z,i+l-1+2z}&i\neq 1,l+1,\cdots,(p-1)l+1\\
\end{cases}

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, MLE of TSC matrices is considered. Precisely leveraging the MM framework, two iterative algorithms ATOM1 and ATOM2 are developed. Both inherit the key properties of MM i.e., they monotonically decrease the underlying cost function with guaranteed convergence to a stationary point of the equivalent MLE problem. Subsequently, ATOM2 is extended to handle covariance matrix MLE forcing other Toeplitz-related structures, such as: banded Toeplitz, TBT, low rank Toeplitz plus a scalar matrix, and Toeplitz structure satisfying a condition number constraint. Simulation results indicate that the proposed algorithms can perform better than some state-of-the-art techniques in terms of MSE and the SINR metrics.

Some of the possible future research directions are now outlined. In the present work, for low rank TSC matrix estimation, the rank (
r
) of the covariance matrix is assumed to be known. However, in many scenarios a perfect prior knowledge of
r
could not be available. Therefore, the proposed algorithm could be extended and analyzed to the case of unknown
r
. Another possible extension of the proposed technique could be MLE of a Toeplitz covariance matrix assuming a compound Gaussian distribution for the underlining data which has a significant application in low-grazing angle target detection
[
55
,
56
]
.
