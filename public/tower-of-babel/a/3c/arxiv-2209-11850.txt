Griffiths inequalities for non-interacting rotors
Ira Herbst

Abstract




Mathematics Subject Classification: 82D99, 60E15.

ABSTRACT: We prove Griffiths inequalities for spins in
n\geq 2
dimensions with no interaction.

1
Introduction

An old open problem is to prove correlation inequalities
Efg\geq(Ef)(Eg)
where the expectation is in the ferromagnetic measure

Ef=\int f(\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{N})e^{\sum_{i,j}f_{i,j}\sigma_{i}\cdot\sigma_{j}}d\sigma_{1}\cdots d\sigma_{N}/Z

.

Here
\sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},n\geq 2
,
d\sigma_{i}
is normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and
Z
is chosen so that
E
is expectation in a probability measure. The fact that the measure is ferromagnetic means
f_{i,j}\geq 0
. The open problem is to show (or disprove by example) that
Efg\geq(Ef)(Eg)
if both
f
and
g
are of the form
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(\sigma_{i}\cdot\sigma_{j})^{n_{i,j}}
. Ferromagnetic spin inequalities were proved for
n=1
in special cases by Robert Griffiths
[
1
,
2
]
and more generally by D. Kelly and Seymour Sherman
[
3
]
. In
n=2
they were proved by Jean Ginibre
[
4
]
. Ginibre’s proof relied on even stronger inequalities which he proved for
n=2
. But these were shown to be false in general for
n>2
by Garrett Sylvester
[
12
]
with explicit counterexamples. There are correlation inequalities known in dimensions three and four with ferromagnetic interaction but to my knowledge not any of the correlation inequalities mentioned above. See the review article
[
5
]
of James Monroe and Paul Pearce.

In the next section we prove the correlation inequalities in the case
f_{i,j}=0
all
i,j
. This is of course physically uninteresting but mathematically non-trivial. They have been proved by a completely different method by Bernhard Baumgartner
[
11
]
.

I proved these results in 1979-80 but never published the proof. Now in my retirement years I thought this method of proof should see the light of day to perhaps spur additional research.

In the last section using the same semigroup technique we prove Griffiths inequalities for Gaussian spins with a ferromagnetic interaction. This actually has a trivial proof (see for example
[
12
]
) using a change of variable and Isserlis’ theorem
[
9
]
(or Wick’s theorem
[
10
]
) for Gaussian integrals. But we give a semigroup proof, again to possibly spur additional research.

2
The inequalities

Let
\mathcal{E}
be the set of linear combinations of monomials of the form
\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq N}(\sigma_{i}\cdot\sigma_{j})^{n_{i,j}}
with positive coefficients. Here
\sigma_{i}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1},n\geq 2
,
n_{i,j}\geq 0
. Define

Ef=\int f(\sigma_{1},\cdots,\sigma_{N})d\sigma_{1}\cdots d\sigma_{N}

(2.1)

where
d\sigma_{j}
is normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Theorem 2.1

Suppose
f,g\in\mathcal{E}
. Then the first and second Griffiths inequalities are valid:
Ef\geq 0
and
Efg\geq EfEg
, where
E
is given by (
2.1
).

We use a method devised by Loren Pitt
[
6
]
and used by Herbst and Pitt
[
7
]
to prove results related to the FKG inequalities. The idea is to find a self adjoint operator
A\leq 0
with the function
1
the non-degenerate normalized ground state of
-A
with
A1=0
so that for real
f
and
g
in
L^{2}
,
Efe^{tA}g=(f,e^{tA}g)\rightarrow(f,1)(1,g)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Thus if one can show
(d/dt)Efe^{tA}g\leq 0
for particular functions
f
and
g
we have
Efg\geq(Ef)(Eg)
.

We take
A=\Delta=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Delta_{i}
where
\Delta_{i}
is the spherical Laplacian on the
i
th sphere. The main step is to show that if
f\in\mathcal{E}
then
e^{t\Delta}f
is a limit of functions in
\mathcal{E}
. Since with
f,g\in\mathcal{E}
,

(d/dt)Efe^{t\Delta}g=-\int\nabla f\cdot\nabla e^{t\Delta}gd\sigma_{1}\cdots d\sigma_{N},

it then remains to show that
\int(\nabla f\cdot\nabla h)\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\sigma_{i}\geq 0
for
f,h\in\mathcal{E}
. Here
\nabla f\cdot\nabla h=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{i}f\cdot\nabla_{i}h
and
\nabla_{i}
is the gradient on the
i
th sphere. Thus for example
\nabla_{i}\sigma_{i}\cdot a=a-(a\cdot\sigma_{i})\sigma_{i}=(I-P_{\sigma_{i}})a
where
P_{\sigma_{i}}
is the orthogonal projection onto multiples of
\sigma_{i}
. We find

\nabla_{1}(\sigma_{1}\cdot\sigma_{i})\cdot\nabla_{1}(\sigma_{1}\cdot\sigma_{j})=\sigma_{i}\cdot(I-P_{\sigma_{1}})\sigma_{j}.

Thus to show
\int(\nabla f\cdot\nabla h)\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\sigma_{i}\geq 0
for
f,h\in\mathcal{E}
it suffices to show for non-negative integers
n_{lk}
that the integral

J=\int[\sigma_{i}\cdot(I-P_{\sigma_{1}})\sigma_{j}]\prod_{l,k}(\sigma_{l}\cdot\sigma_{k})^{n_{lk}}d\sigma_{1}d\sigma_{2}\cdots d\sigma_{N}

(2.2)

is non-negative.
To see that
J\geq 0
, we first note that by rotation invariance we can omit the integral over
\sigma_{1}
and take
\sigma_{1}=(1,\cdots,0)
without changing the value of (
2.2
). Thus we are left with sums with positive coefficients of integrals of the form (
(\sigma_{i})_{m}
is the
m
th component of the vector
\sigma_{i}
)

\int\sum_{m=2}^{n}(\sigma_{i})_{m}(\sigma_{j})_{m}(\sigma_{l_{1}})_{m_{l_{1}}}(\sigma_{l_{2}})_{m_{l_{2}}}\cdots(\sigma_{l_{M}})_{m_{l_{M}}}d\sigma_{2}\cdots d\sigma_{N}

where
l_{p}>1
or more generally sums with positive coefficients of integrals of the form

I=\int(\sigma_{l_{1}})_{m_{l_{1}}}(\sigma_{l_{2}})_{m_{l_{2}}}\cdots(\sigma_{l_{M}})_{m_{l_{M}}}d\sigma_{2}\cdots d\sigma_{N}

where again
l_{p}>1
. Note that
\int\prod_{i=1}^{n}[(\sigma_{l_{p}})_{i}^{m_{i}}]d\sigma_{l_{p}}=0
unless all the integers
m_{i}
are even and thus
I=0
unless the integrand is a product of squares. The fact that
I\geq 0
shows the very simple Griffiths first inequality,
Ef\geq 0
, even with ferromagnetic interaction.

It remains to show that for
f\in\mathcal{E}
,
e^{t\Delta}f
is a limit of functions in
\mathcal{E}
.
To show this we use a theorem of Paul Chernoff
[
8
]
to approximate
e^{t\Delta_{i}}
. The operator
e^{t\Delta}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an integral operator with kernel
K_{t}(x,y)=(4\pi t)^{-n/2}e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4t}
. Intuition tells us that a good small time approximation to
e^{t\Delta_{0}}
where
\Delta_{0}
is the spherical Laplacian on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
should be the operator
U(t)
given by

U(t)f(\sigma)=c(t)\int e^{-|\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}|^{2}/4t}f(\sigma^{\prime})d\sigma^{\prime}

where
d\sigma^{\prime}
is normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
and
c(t)
is chosen so that
U(t)1=1
.

We can calculate
c(t)
for
t>0
and small. We find

c(t)=A_{n-1}(4\pi t)^{-(n-1)/2}(1+O(t))

where
A_{n-1}
is the area of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
(
A_{n-1}=2\pi^{n/2}/\Gamma(n/2)
).
Clearly
||U(t)||_{p}=1
for
p=1,\infty
which gives
||U(t)||_{p}=1
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
by the Riesz convexity theorem. Here we also use
U(t)1=1
to get equality.

We use Chernoff’s theorem:

Theorem 2.2

[
8
]

Suppose
U(t)
is a strongly continuous family of contractions for
t\geq 0
on a Banach space
B
with
U(0)=I
. Suppose
A
is the generator of a strongly continuous contraction semigroup on
B
such
that
(U(t)-I)t^{-1}f
converges strongly to
Af
on a core for
A
. Then
U(t/m)^{m}
converges strongly to
e^{tA}
as
m\to\infty
.

We use this for
A=\Delta_{0}
,
B=L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
. Note that the set of all
f=e^{s\Delta_{0}}\psi
for some
s>0
and some
\psi\in L^{2}
is a core for
\Delta_{0}
and thus we need only prove convergence on smooth functions. Note also that one need not show
\nabla U(t/n)^{n}f\rightarrow\nabla e^{t\Delta_{0}}f
since we have
d/dt(g,e^{t\Delta}f)=(\Delta g,e^{t\Delta}f)
. Thus consider

((U(t)-I)t^{-1}f)(\sigma)=c(t)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}e^{-|\sigma-\sigma^{\prime}|^{2}/4t}(f(\sigma^{\prime})-f(\sigma))t^{-1}d\sigma^{\prime}

for smooth
f
. Fix
\sigma
and note that integration over
|\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma|>\delta
for any positive
\delta
gives a negligible contribution so we can assume that
f
has small support around
\sigma
and that we integrate only over a small region of the sphere around
\sigma
. We use coordinates on the sphere around
\sigma
so that for
\sigma^{\prime}\cdot\sigma>0,\sigma^{\prime}=\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}\sigma+x
with
x
in the
(n-1)
- plane perpendicular to
\sigma
. We see that

|\sigma^{\prime}-\sigma|^{2}=|x|^{2}(1+|x|^{4}(\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}+1)^{-2})

Without loss of generality (using the fact that
U(t)
commutes with rotations) we take the
n
th component of
\sigma
equal to
1
so that we can write
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n-1})
, dropping
x_{n}=0.

In this coordinate system, at the point
\sigma
we have
d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot d\sigma^{\prime}_{2}=\sum_{i,j}g_{i,j}(dx_{i})_{1}(dx_{j})_{2}
with
g_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}+x_{i}x_{j}/(1-|x|^{2})
. Thus
\det(g_{i,j})=(1-|x|^{2})^{-1}
and

I=((U(t)-I)t^{-1}f)(\sigma)=

c(t)\int_{|x|<1/2}e^{-|x|^{2}(1+|x|^{4}(\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}+1)^{-2})/4t})(\tilde{f}(x)-\tilde{f}(0))t^{-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}+O(e^{-1/20t})

where
dx
is Lebesgue measure on the plane perpendicular to
\sigma
and
\tilde{f}(x)=f(\sigma^{\prime})
. It is easy to see that
c(t)\int_{|x|<1/2}|x|^{3}e^{-|x|^{2}/4t}t^{-1}dx\leq C\sqrt{t}
so that since odd functions of
x
give zero integrals

I=c(t)\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\tilde{f}(0)\int_{|x|<1/2}e^{-|x|^{2}(1+|x|^{4}(\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}+1)^{-2})/4t})x_{i}x_{j}t^{-1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-|x|^{2}}}+O(\sqrt{t})=

c(t)\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\tilde{f}(0)\int_{|x|<1/2}e^{-|x|^{2}/4t}x_{i}x_{j}t^{-1}dx+O(\sqrt{t})=

c(t)\frac{1}{2}\partial\cdot\partial\tilde{f}(0)\int_{|x|<1/2}e^{-|x|^{2}/4t}(|x|^{2}/t(n-1))dx+O(\sqrt{t}).

We can integrate over all of
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with a negligible error. Substituting
y=x/\sqrt{2t}
we obtain

I=\frac{1}{2}\partial\cdot\partial\tilde{f}(0)c(t)(\sqrt{2t})^{n-1}\int e^{-|y|^{2}/2}(2|y|^{2}/(n-1))dy+O(\sqrt{t})=

\partial\cdot\partial\tilde{f}(0)c(t)(\sqrt{4\pi t})^{n-1}+O(\sqrt{t}).

Since
c(t)(\sqrt{4\pi t})^{n-1}=1+O(t)

I=\partial\cdot\partial\tilde{f}(0)+O(\sqrt{t}).

It is easy to calculate the inverse of
g_{i,j}
. We obtain
g^{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}-x_{i}x_{j}
, so that in this coordinate system
\Delta_{0}=\sum_{i,j}(\sqrt{g})^{-1}\partial_{i}\sqrt{g}g^{i,j}\partial_{j}
(
g=\det(g_{i,j})=(1-|x|^{2})^{-1}
) or

\Delta_{0}=-(n-1)x\cdot\partial+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n-1}(\delta_{i,j}-x_{i}x_{j})\partial_{i}\partial_{j}.

Thus according to Theorem
2.2
we have
U(t/n)^{n}\rightarrow e^{t\Delta_{0}}
. It remains to show that for
f\in\mathcal{E},U(t)f
is a limit of functions in
\mathcal{E}

or more explicitly we must show that

\int e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{\prime}|^{2}/4t}f(\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,\sigma^{\prime}_{N})d\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,d\sigma^{\prime}_{N}

is a limit of functions in
\mathcal{E}
. Since
e^{-|\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{i}^{\prime}|^{2}/4t}=e^{-1/2t}e^{\sigma_{i}\cdot\sigma_{i}^{\prime}/2t}
, expanding the second exponential in a power series we see that if suffices to show that

\int(\sigma_{1}\cdot\sigma_{1}^{\prime})^{n_{1}}\cdots(\sigma_{N}\cdot\sigma_{N}^{\prime})^{n_{N}}f(\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,\sigma^{\prime}_{N})d\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,d\sigma^{\prime}_{N}\in\mathcal{E}.

If
f
is a sum of terms of the form
c\prod_{i<j}(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{j})^{n_{i,j}}
with
c>0
it thus suffices to show that the more general integral

J=\int\prod_{j_{1}}(\sigma_{1}\cdot\sigma_{j_{1}}^{\prime})^{m_{j_{1}}}\cdots\prod_{j_{N}}(\sigma_{N}\cdot\sigma_{j_{N}}^{\prime})^{m_{j_{N}}}\prod_{i<j}(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{j})^{n_{i,j}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1},\cdots,d\sigma^{\prime}_{N}\in\mathcal{E}.

Let us do the integral over
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
, namely an integral of the form

I=\int\prod_{1\leq k}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\sigma_{k})^{n_{k}}\prod_{1<j}(\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{j})^{n_{1,j}}d\sigma^{\prime}_{1}.

We convert this to a Gaussian integral

I=C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\prod_{1\leq k}(\sigma_{k}\cdot x)^{n_{k}}\prod_{1<j}(x\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{j})^{n_{1,j}}dx/(\sqrt{2\pi})^{n}

where
C^{-1}=A_{n-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{(\sum n_{k}+n-1+\sum_{j}n_{1,j})}e^{-s^{2}/2}ds/(\sqrt{2\pi})^{-n}
and
A_{n-1}
is the area of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. We now use Leon Isserlis’ theorem
[
9
]
(better known as Wick’s theorem
[
10
]
in the physics literature) to compute this integral. The theorem states in particular that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\prod_{i=1}^{L}(a_{i}\cdot x)dx/(\sqrt{2\pi})^{n}=\sum_{p\in P_{L}}\prod_{(i,j)\in p}(a_{i}\cdot a_{j})

where
P_{L}
is the set of pairings of
\{1,\cdots,L\}
, i.e. the distinct ways of partitioning
\{1,\cdots,L\}
into pairs
(i,j)
. The product is over the pairs contained in the partition
p\in P_{L}
. If
L
is odd, the integral is
0
. Thus either
I=0
or
I
is a sum of terms, each a product of a function in
\mathcal{E}
and a product
\prod_{1<i<j}(\sigma^{\prime}_{i}\cdot\sigma^{\prime}_{j})^{l_{i,j}}
. Thus in the integral
J
, after doing the
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
integral we have a sum of terms involving a factor from
\mathcal{E}
times an integral of the same form as
J
except
\sigma^{\prime}_{1}
is omitted. Iteration shows that
J\in\mathcal{E}
and completes the demonstration.

\blacksquare

3
Gaussian ferromagnetic spins

In this section, using semigroup techniques, we prove the Griffiths inequalities for
n
dimensional spins in a ferromagnetic Gaussian measure
Z^{-1}e^{-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}f_{ij}x_{i}\cdot x_{j}/2}
where the matrix
F=(f_{ij})
is positive definite with
f_{ij}\leq 0
for
i\neq j
. There is a much simpler proof (see
[
12
]
for example) which proves the stronger Ginibre inequalities
[
4
]
by a simple change of variable. But again we give a brief outline of a (longer) proof which we hope might spur additional research.

Proposition 3.1

Define the expectation

Ef=\int f(x_{1},x_{2},\cdots.x_{N})d\mu(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N}),

d\mu(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})=Z^{-1}e^{-\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}f_{ij}x_{i}\cdot x_{j}/2}dx_{1}\cdots dx_{N}

where the matrix
F=(f_{ij})
is symmetric and positive definite with
f_{ij}\leq 0
for
i\neq j
.
Z
is defined so that
\mu
is a probability measure. The variables
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{E}
be the cone of non-negative linear combinations of multinomials
\prod_{i\leq j}(x_{i}\cdot x_{j})^{n_{i,j}}
with integers
n_{i,j}\geq 0
. Then if
f,g\in\mathcal{E}

Ef\geq 0,Efg\geq EfEg.

We give a brief outline of the semigroup proof: We define the self adjoint operator
A
in
L^{2}(e^{-Q}dx)
with
Q=\sum f_{ij}x_{i}\cdot x_{j}/2
by the formula

\int fAge^{-Q}dx=-\int\nabla f\cdot\nabla ge^{-Q}dx.

We calculate
A=\Delta-\nabla Q\cdot\nabla
. (
A
is related to the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. The unitary transform of
A
,
e^{-Q/2}Ae^{Q/2}
, is the negative of a harmonic oscillator Hamiltonian,
H=-\Delta+|Fx|^{2}/4-n\text{tr}F
. ) We use the Trotter formula
[
13
]
which gives
\lim_{m\to\infty}(e^{t\Delta/m}e^{-t\nabla Q\cdot\nabla/m})^{m}=e^{tA}
.
e^{t\Delta}
preserves
\mathcal{E}
as does
e^{-t\nabla Q\cdot\nabla}
which just takes
f(x)
to
f(e^{-tF}x)
. The ferromagnetic nature of
F
guarantees that the matrix elements of
e^{-tF}
are non-negative (proof:
e^{-tF}=\lim_{m\to\infty}(I-tF/m)^{m}
) and thus
e^{-tF}
preserves
\mathcal{E}
. Finally Isserlis’ theorem shows that the resulting integrals are non-negative (here we use that
F^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tF}dt
has non-negative entries).
