Efficient Computation of Periods and Covers Using Sampling
Thierry Lecroq, Francesco Pio Marino

Abstract
Identifying regularities in strings, such as periods and covers , is crucial for applications in text compression, computational biology, and pattern recognition. Characters-Distance-Sampling ( CDS ) is an efficient technique that encodes a string by storing distances between selected pivot characters, accelerating string-processing tasks. We apply CDS to compute periods and shortest covers, selecting only the first character as the pivot. This strategy yields optimized computations, achieving speedups of 38 % 38\% – 43 % 43\% for period computation and 63 % 63\% – 72 % 72\% for cover detection. These results demonstrate the potential of CDS -based representations for efficient string analysis and broader applications.



1
Introduction

String analysis is fundamental in various domains, including literature, linguistics, computer science, and computational biology, where DNA, RNA, and protein sequences are represented as character chains. Identifying structural regularities in strings is essential for efficient data processing, pattern recognition, and compression. Among these,
periods

[
14
]
and
quasiperiodicities
(
covers
)
[
1
,
2
,
12
]
are widely studied in combinatorial pattern matching, with applications in text indexing, bioinformatics, and data compression.

In recent years,
Characters-Distance-Sampling
(
CDS
) has been successfully applied in string processing tasks such as exact pattern matching
[
5
,
9
,
10
]
.
CDS
represents a string by storing the distances between occurrences of selected
pivot characters
, typically leading to a more compact representation of the string. However, despite its effectiveness in searching and indexing,
CDS
has not yet been leveraged for computing string regularities.

In this paper, we present the first application of
CDS
to the computation of fundamental string regularities, specifically the
period
and the
shortest cover
of a string. We develop efficient algorithms that operate directly on the
CDS
representation of a string, significantly improving computational efficiency compared to classical methods. Our experimental results demonstrate that these methods achieve substantial speedups over traditional approaches.

Preliminaries

A
string
is a sequence of zero or more characters drawn from a finite
alphabet

\Sigma
, with
\sigma=|\Sigma|
denoting the number of distinct characters. The empty string, containing zero characters, is denoted by
\varepsilon
, and the set of all finite strings over
\Sigma
is
\Sigma^{*}
. A string
x
of length
m
is represented as
x[0\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}m-1]
, where
x[i]\in\Sigma
for
0\leq i\leq m-1
, where
|x|=m
is the length of the string.

A string
u
is a
prefix
of
x
if
x=uw
for some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and a
suffix
if
x=wu
. If
u
is both a prefix and a suffix of
x
but
u\neq x
, it is called a
border
. By definition, the empty string is a border of any non-empty string. A string may have multiple borders, with the longest referred to as
the border
of
x
, denoted by
\textit{Border}(x)
.

An integer
p
(
1\leq p\leq|x|
) is a
period
of
x
if
x[i]=x[i+p]
for all
0\leq i<|x|-p
. The smallest such
p
is called
the period
of
x
, denoted by
\textit{per}(x)
. It is well known that
x
has period
p
if and only if it has a border of length
|x|-p

[
13
]
. Specifically, for a non-empty string
x
:

|x|=\textit{per}(x)+|\textit{Border}(x)|.

Borders and periods are dual concepts since each period corresponds to a border, and vice versa. All borders of a non-empty string
x
can be found recursively through the borders of its longest border. In online string matching, a string’s period determines the optimal shift length when an occurrence is found.

A related concept is that of
covers
. A string
w
of length
\ell
is a
cover
(or
quasiperiod
) of a string
x
of length
m
if every position in
x
is included in some occurrence of
w
.
Let
\textit{pos}(u,x)
be the ordered list of all starting positions where
u
appears in
x
:

\textit{pos}(u,x)=(i\mid x[i\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}i+|u|-1]=u)
. Then

\{0,|x|-|w|\}\subseteq\textit{pos}(w,x)

if
w
is a cover of
x
.

This ensures that every character of
x
is contained within at least one occurrence of
w
, though occurrences may overlap. A string can have multiple covers, with the shortest among them called the
shortest cover
. If a string
x
has no proper cover (i.e., its only cover is itself), it is called
superprimitive
; otherwise, it is
quasiperiodic
.

Characters-Distance-Sampling in Brief

In this section, we provide a concise overview of the methodology used to construct a partial index in the
CDS
setting.

Let
x
be a string of length
m
over an alphabet
\Sigma
of size
\sigma
. Assuming that all strings are treated as vectors starting at position
0
, we denote
x[i]
as the
(i+1)
-th character of
x
, for
0\leq i<m
.

To build the partial index, a subset
C\subseteq\Sigma
is chosen as the
set of pivot characters
. The input string
x
is then sampled by computing the distances between consecutive occurrences of any pivot character
c\in C
.

Formally, given the function
\delta:\{0,\dots,\dot{m}-1\}\rightarrow\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
, where
\delta(i)
is the position of the
(i+1)
-th occurrence a pivot character in
x
, we first obtain the
position-sampled
version of
x
, indicated by
\dot{x}
, as the numeric sequence of length
\dot{m}
, defined as

\dot{x}=\langle\delta(0),\delta(1),\ldots,\delta(\dot{m}-1)\rangle
.

Then, given the function
\Delta
, where
\Delta(i)=\delta(i+1)-\delta(i)
for
0\leq i<\dot{m}-1
, is the distance between two consecutive occurrences of any pivot character in
x
, we get the
character-distance sampled
version of the text
x
, indicated by
\bar{x}
, as the numeric sequence of length
\bar{m}=\dot{m}-1
, defined as

\bar{x}=\langle\Delta(0),\Delta(1),\ldots,\Delta(\bar{m}-1)\rangle=\langle\delta(1)-\delta(0),\delta(2)-\delta(1),\ldots,\delta(\dot{m}-1)-\delta(\dot{m}-2)\rangle
.

For example, let
x=

agaacgcagtata
be a
DNA
sequence of length 13, over the alphabet
\Sigma=\{
a
,
c
,
g
,
t
\}
. Let
C=\{
a
\}
be the set of pivot characters. Thus, the position-sampled version of
x
is
\dot{x}=\langle 0,2,3,7,10,12\rangle
. The character-distance sampling version of
x
is
\bar{x}=\langle 2,1,4,3,2\rangle
. Specifically,
\bar{x}[0]=\Delta(0)=\delta(1)-\delta(0)=2-0=2
, while
\bar{x}[2]=\Delta(2)=\delta(3)-\delta(2)=7-3=4
, and so on.

When searching for a pattern
p
, in a larger text
t
, a preprocessing step computes their sampled versions
\bar{p}
and
\bar{t}
, after which any string matching algorithm can be applied to find occurrences of
\bar{p}
in
\bar{t}
(see
[
8
]
for details).
In its original design, the algorithm maintains the position-sampled version
\dot{t}
and dynamically computes
\bar{t}
during the search. This approach balances memory usage, as storing distances or positions requires the same space, but retaining positions allows direct access to the original text for verification.

However, in this formulation, each element of the sampled text requires 4 bytes of memory. An alternative implementation
[
6
]
reduces memory usage by shifting from position-based to distance-based representation, introducing the concept of
fake samples
. Here, distances exceeding a fixed threshold are decomposed into a sequence of smaller distances with inserted fake samples. This method reduces memory consumption, allowing representation with just 8 bits per element (for a threshold of 256).

Experimental results indicate that the additional space required to store the sampled text ranges from
2.5\%
to
20\%
, depending on the pattern length
[
6
]
. Furthermore, recent studies demonstrate that the Character Distance Sampling algorithm enables any exact string matching procedure to be used as the search method
[
7
]
. Notably, search times are reduced by up to
96\%
compared to applying the same procedure to the non-sampled version of the text.

3.1
Classical Computation of the Period of a String

A fundamental approach for computing the period of a string
x
relies on determining the length of the border of all its prefixes. The key observation is that the length of the longest border of
x
directly determines its period.

The
border array
of a non-empty string
x
of length
m
is an array of size
m+1
defined as follows:

\textit{border}_{x}[0]=-1,\quad\textit{border}_{x}[i]=|\textit{Border}(x[0\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}i-1])|\quad\text{for }1\leq i\leq|x|.

The border array can be computed in linear time using the classical algorithm presented in
[
3
]
.
This structure allows the period of the string to be determined efficiently using the relation
\textit{per}(x)=m-\textit{border}_{x}[m]
, where
m
is the length of
x
.

Each entry

. This means that at position

, the prefix

For instance, the prefix

3.2
Classical Computation of the Shortest Cover of a String

Since a cover is inherently a border, computing the shortest cover of a string requires first identifying all its non-empty borders. For a string
x
of length
m
let us define the sequence of borders recursively as follows:

\textit{Border}^{0}(x)=x,\quad\textit{Border}^{i}(x)=\textit{Border}(\textit{Border}^{i-1}(x))\text{ for }i>0.

The sequence of non-empty borders is then:

\textit{Border}^{1}(x),\textit{Border}^{2}(x),\dots,\textit{Border}^{k}(x)\neq\varepsilon,\quad\text{such that }\textit{Border}^{k+1}(x)=\varepsilon.

The shortest cover of
x
is the smallest non-empty border
\textit{Border}^{j}(x)
that covers all the borders
\textit{Border}^{g}(x)
for
0\leq g<j
.

The algorithm starts by computing the shortest border and iteratively checks whether each border covers longer ones. If a border
\textit{Border}^{j}(x)
fails to cover some
\textit{Border}^{h}(x)
with
h<j
, the process resumes with
j=h
. To ensure a linear-time complexity, when a border
w
of a prefix
x[0\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}n-1]
has length
\ell>m/2
, its length is adjusted as:

\ell=n-\ell+m\bmod(n-\ell).

This effectively reduces
w
to
uu^{\prime}
, where
w=u^{r}u^{\prime}
and
u^{\prime}
is a prefix of
u
.

For instance, to compute the shortest cover, we first extract all the non-empty borders of
x
. The decreasing sequence of the non-empty border lengths for
x[0\mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp}11]=\mbox{\tt abaababaaba}
is:

(\textit{border}_{x}^{1}[|x|]=6,\textit{border}_{x}^{2}[|x|]=3,\textit{border}_{x}^{3}[|x|]=1).

This means that sequence of borders in increasing length of
x
is:
(\mbox{\tt a},\mbox{\tt aba},\mbox{\tt abaaba}).

For a string to be a cover, every occurrence of the border must occur in a way that ensures complete coverage of
x
. Here, the smallest border,
a
, is not sufficient to cover
aba
. However, the border
aba
does cover both
abaaba
and the full string
x
.
Thus, the shortest cover of
x
is:
\mbox{\tt aba}.

This result confirms that the minimal repeating unit that effectively covers
x
is of length
3
.

Computing the period of a string from its
CDS
representation

This section presents an efficient method for computing the period of a string using its
CDS
representation. We first consider the case where the alphabet consists of only two distinct characters, meaning
\sigma=|\Sigma|=2
, thus
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
.
Let
a
be the only pivot character.
Let
x
be a string of length
m
. The function
\delta
denotes the position of occurrences of a designated pivot character in
x
. The
CDS
representation of
x
is denoted by
\bar{x}
, and its length is given by
\bar{m}=|\bar{x}|
.

The key idea is that the periodic structure of
\bar{x}
provides enough information to determine the period of
x
. Before proving this, we present two auxiliary results that establish the fundamental relationships between
x
and its
CDS
representation.

From any position
\delta(i)
of the pivot character
a
, all positions
in
\delta
greater than
\delta(i)
correspond to
\delta(i)
plus a sum of elements from
\bar{x}
. These positions also contain
a
.

Lemma 1

Let
x[\delta(i)]=a
for
0\leq i\leq\bar{m}-1
. Then:

x[\delta(i)+\sum_{j=i}^{k}\bar{x}[j]]=a,\quad\text{for }i\leq k\leq\bar{m}-1.

Proof

Expanding the sum of distances between consecutive pivot occurrences, we obtain:

x[\delta(i)+\sum_{j=i}^{k}\bar{x}[j]]=x[\delta(i)+(\delta(i+1)-\delta(i))+\dots+(\delta(k+1)-\delta(k))].

Since
\delta(k+1)
corresponds to the position of the pivot character, it follows that:

x[\delta(k+1)]=a.

A similar result holds for positions preceding a pivot occurrence.

Lemma 2

Let
x[\delta(i)]=a
for
0\leq i\leq\bar{m}-1
. Then:

x[\delta(i)-\sum_{j=k}^{i-1}\bar{x}[j]]=a,\quad\text{for }0\leq k\leq i-1.

Proof

By summing the distances between previous occurrences of the pivot character, we obtain:

x[\delta(i)-(\delta(k+1)-\delta(k)+\dots+\delta(i)-\delta(i+1))]=x[\delta(k)].

Since
\delta(k)
corresponds to an occurrence of the pivot character, we conclude that:

x[\delta(k)]=a.

5
Computing the shortest cover of a string from its
CDS
representation

The classical computation of the shortest cover of a string
x
relies on its border array. Since borders play a fundamental role in determining covers, the approach used to compute borders in the
CDS
representation can be adapted to efficiently determine the shortest cover as well.

The key idea remains the same: covers, like periods, are closely tied to the border structure of a string.
The shortest cover of
x
must be a border that also satisfies the covering condition, ensuring that every position in
x
is part of at least one occurrence of the cover.

The algorithm consists of merely identifying borders and verifying which of these borders serve as valid cover for
x
. This is accomplished by leveraging the border array of the
CDS
representation of
x
, efficiently determining the shortest cover without needing to explicitly reconstruct the full string.

By utilizing the properties of the
CDS
representation, the adapted algorithm ensures that the computation of the shortest cover remains efficient. The method minimizes redundant checks and avoids unnecessary recomputation by systematically examining the borders derived from the
CDS
representation. In case of binary alphabets this can be done in linear worst-case time complexity, while for larger alphabets this achieves quadratic worst-case time complexity, however experimental results demonstrate that this solution is faster in practice compared to the classical computation.

7
Conclusion

In this paper, we demonstrated that the partial index built using the
Characters-Distance-Sampling
(
CDS
) algorithm, originally designed to enhance the efficiency of string matching algorithms, can also be effectively utilized to compute regularities in strings. This novel approach leverages the compact and structured nature of the
CDS
representation to extract fundamental properties of strings in a computationally efficient manner.

Specifically, we introduced optimized methods for solving two well-studied problems in the field of string regularities: the computation of the
period
and the detection of
quasiperiodicity
or
covers
. By adapting classical techniques to work with the
CDS
representation, we developed algorithms that significantly outperform traditional approaches. Experimental results confirm the effectiveness of our methods, showing speedups ranging from
38\%
to
43\%
for period computation and from
63\%
to
72\%
for shortest cover detection. These improvements demonstrate the potential of the
CDS
representation not only for accelerating string matching but also for efficiently identifying structural properties of strings.

Our findings open the door for further research into the use of
CDS
-based methods for detecting other types of regularities in strings. Future work could explore the application of this approach to problems such as the computation of
seeds
,
k-covers
, or other forms of repetitive structures. Investigating the theoretical bounds of this technique, as well as its practical implications in bioinformatics, data compression, and text processing, could lead to new advancements in the field.

The same techniques can be easily adapted to strings given in other kind of representation such as the run-length encoding.

By demonstrating that a representation initially designed for string matching can be repurposed to analyze structural properties of strings, this work provides a new perspective on the versatility of distance-based indexing techniques. The promising results obtained in this study suggest that
CDS
-based methods could be further developed to enhance the efficiency of a wide range of string-processing applications.
