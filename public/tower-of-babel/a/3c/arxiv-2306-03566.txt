Memory-Based Dual Gaussian Processes for Sequential Learning
Paul E. Chang, Prakhar Verma, S.T. John, Arno Solin, Mohammad Emtiyaz Khan

Abstract
Sequential learning with Gaussian processes (GPs) is challenging when access to past data is limited, for example, in continual and active learning. In such cases, errors can accumulate over time due to inaccuracies in the posterior, hyperparameters, and inducing points, making accurate learning challenging. Here, we present a method to keep all such errors in check using the recently proposed dual sparse variational GP. Our method enables accurate inference for generic likelihoods and improves learning by actively building and updating a memory of past data. We demonstrate its effectiveness in several applications involving Bayesian optimization, active learning, and continual learning.

Introduction

Sequential decision-making requires uncertainty estimates that can be used to plan for the future. For this reason, Gaussian process (GP) models are popular for sequential problems in applications such as model-based reinforcement learning
(
Deisenroth & Rasmussen 2011
)
and Bayesian optimization
(
Garnett 2023
)
.
However, exact online inference in GPs requires access to all past data, which becomes infeasible over time as the amount of data grows
(
Csató & Opper 2002
)
.
Sparse GP methods can reduce this cost, but they too assume access to all past data. For example, the popular sparse variational GP (SVGP) and variational free energy
(
Titsias 2009
, VFE,)
methods require multiple passes through the data during the stochastic training
(
Hensman et al. 2013
)
. This can lead to inaccuracies for continual or active learning, where access to past data is limited and errors can accumulate over time.

Errors can arise from multiple sources: inaccurate posteriors, wrong hyperparameter values, or poor sparse representations. Various techniques can be used to control such errors, but past attempts struggled to find a coherent solution to the problem.
Initially,
Csató & Opper 2002
used expectation propagation (EP) for inference and a projection method for sparsity but did not estimate hyperparameters.
More recently,
Bui et al. 2017
did estimate hyperparameters but found EP to perform worse than variational inference.
Maddox et al. 2021
did not optimize hyperparameters or use the evidence lower bound (ELBO) objective but instead resorted to a Laplace approximation for non-Gaussian likelihoods. Their method to obtain sparse representation uses a gradient method combined with pivoted Cholesky.
Kapoor et al. 2021
attempt to improve performance by using a structured
covariance during inference, but the cost grows with tasks which is due to the increasing size of the sparse representation. These attempts use a mix of methods to control various errors, which all lead to complications. We aim to build a single coherent method by tackling various errors simultaneously; see
Fig.

1
for an overview.

We adapt the dual-SVGP method of
Adam et al. 2021
to perform sequential learning with generic likelihoods.
A key contribution of our work is to improve performance by adding a memory set of past examples. A sufficiently large memory can achieve arbitrarily close performance to the batch case, and the performance loss can be minimized by choosing the set carefully. For such selection, we propose a new score called the
Bayesian leverage score
which uses the dual parameters of the dual-SVGP posterior to characterize relative importance of past examples.
Overall, our memory-based SVGP enables us to tackle the various errors arising in sequential learning. It also avoids the complications arising in previous work on sequential learning, as discussed below:

1.

Unlike
Csató & Opper 2002
, we aim to minimize the ELBO which aligns better with
Bui et al. 2017
who also found the ELBO to give better results than EP.
Our dual parameters can be seen as an estimate of those used in
Csató & Opper 2002
.

2.

Similarly to
Bui et al. 2017
, we obtain a pseudo-data interpretation but it is derived from the dual parameterization. Our approach directly addresses the issues they aim to solve and is more straightforward.

3.

Unlike
Maddox et al. 2021
, we estimate hyperparameters and use a variational method for inference.

4.

Unlike
Kapoor et al. 2021
, we use a fixed number of inducing inputs and instead increase the memory size. The cost grows linearly as opposed to a cubically increasing cost of their method. We also do not need any regularization for hyperparameters.

Our use of memory is similar to recent continual learning methods
(
Nguyen et al. 2018
;
Titsias et al. 2020
;
Pan et al. 2020
;
Khan & Swaroop 2021
)
, but we select the memory via an extension of leverage scores
(
Cook & Weisberg 1980
;
Alaoui & Mahoney 2015
)
. We demonstrate effectiveness of our approach on several applications involving Bayesian optimization, active learning, and continual learning.

2
Sequential Learning with a GP

We consider sequential learning in models with a GP prior over functions,
f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\kappa_{\bm{\theta}})
. The prior is characterized by a covariance (kernel) function
\kappa_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, where
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
are input vectors and
\bm{\theta}
denotes the hyperparameters. Observations
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})
are modeled by the likelihood
p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})

given a function value
f_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
In the sequential setting, we first compute the posterior on
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\textrm{old}},\mathbf{y}_{\textrm{old}})
where
\mathbf{X}_{\text{old}}
is a matrix containing all the past inputs
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}
as rows and
\mathbf{y}_{\text{old}}
is a vector containing all the past outputs
y_{i}
. Then, when new data
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{new}}=(\mathbf{X}_{\textrm{new}},\mathbf{y}_{\textrm{new}})
is observed, our goal is to update the posterior and hyperparameters.

The posterior inference in the sequential setting is challenging because the cost grows cubically in the size of data (denoted by
n
).
We can see this by expressing the posterior
p(f_{i}|\mathbf{y})
as follows
(
Csató & Opper 2002
, Lemma 1)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y})}[f_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{x}i}^{\top}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$},

(1)

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}_{p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y})}[f_{i}]

\displaystyle=\kappa_{ii}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{x}i}^{\top}(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}+\text{{diag}}(\text{$\bm{\beta}$})^{-1})^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{x}i},

where
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
are vectors of
n
dual parameters,

\begin{split}\alpha_{i}&=\mathbb{E}_{p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y})}[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})],\\
\beta_{i}&=\mathbb{E}_{p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y})}[-\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})],\end{split}

(2)

respectively; a derivation is given in
Section

A.1
.
Here, we use
\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
to denote the
n\times n
matrix with
\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
as the
ij
th
entry. Similarly,
\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{x}i}
denotes a vector where each
j
th
element is
\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
, and
\kappa_{ii}=\kappa(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
The parameters
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
are the dual parameters that arise in the dual formulations used in, for example, support vector machines
(
Cortes & Vapnik 1995
)
, whose origins are found in work by
Kimeldorf & Wahba 1971
.
The posterior holds for generic likelihoods, even a non-Gaussian one, and no approximations are involved.

More importantly, the expressions in
Eq.

1
and
Eq.

2
clearly show the challenge of sequential learning, where as data grows, so do
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$},\text{$\bm{\beta}$})
, and naïve computation of the posterior is now
O((n_{\textrm{old}}+n_{\textrm{new}})^{3})
due to the matrix inversion, quickly making inference infeasible.
Csató & Opper 2002
suggested storing and using only a subset of past data to reduce the computation. They estimate
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
using a Gaussian approximation to
p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y})
, obtained by EP, but did not consider updating the hyperparameters.

In the full-batch case, scaling can be improved using the SVGP method
(
Titsias 2009
;
Hensman et al. 2013
)
, which optimizes an ELBO to select hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}
, inducing inputs
\mathbf{Z}=(\text{$\bm{z}$}_{1},\ldots,\text{$\bm{z}$}_{m})
with
m\ll n
, and the posterior
q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}\,|\,\mathbf{m},\mathbf{V})
defined over functions
\mathbf{u}_{i}=f(\text{$\bm{z}$}_{i})
at
\text{$\bm{z}$}_{i}
. The ELBO is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}\cup\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{new}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}],

(3)

which uses the following posterior predictive distribution,

q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})=\mathrm{N}(f_{i}\,|\,\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{m},\kappa_{ii}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}+\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}),

(4)

where
\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}
, and
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u})
is the GP prior over
\mathbf{u}
. The last term in the bound is the Kullback–Leibler divergence (KLD). The method assumes that all data is available throughout training, so it does not directly apply in the sequential setting.

Bui et al. 2017
extend the SVGP method in an ad-hoc way by adding two additional KLD terms to match the previous posterior and prior at the old inducing inputs.
Let
\mathbf{Z}_{\text{old}}
denote the old inducing inputs with
\mathbf{u}_{\text{old}}=f(\mathbf{Z}_{\text{old}})
and posterior predictive
q_{\mathbf{u}_{\text{old}}}(f_{i})
. Also, let
p_{\bm{\theta}_{\text{old}}}(f_{i})
denote the prior for the old hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}_{\text{old}}
.
Given the new data
\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}
,
Bui et al. 2017
modify the ELBO as follows,

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{new}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}_{\textrm{old}}}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}}))]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}})\,\|\,q_{\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}}}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}})],

(5)

where the last two KLD terms are the newly added terms which are meant to penalize deviations from both the old prior and posterior. This is an indirect way to keep the solutions close to the full-batch case, but it does not always work well (we give empirical evidence in
Section

4
).

Maddox et al. 2021
revisit the approach of
Bui et al. 2017
in the context of Bayesian optimization and active learning. They simplify the derivation for the Gaussian case via pseudo-data. They resort to a Laplace approximation to handle non-Gaussian likelihoods. Laplace approximation is a more ‘local’ one than those obtained by variational methods and it can give worse results
(
Opper & Archambeau 2009
)
. In addition, they do not optimize hyperparameters.

We will now show that the above difficulties can be alleviated by using the method of
Adam et al. 2021
: we can estimate the parameterization of
Csató & Opper 2002
, improve the ELBO of
Bui et al. 2017
, improve over Laplace approximation of
Maddox et al. 2021
, and, unlike
Kapoor et al. 2021
, keep the computational cost from exploding.

3
A Memory-based Dual-SVGP Method

We adapt the dual-SVGP method of
Adam et al. 2021
to enable accurate inference in the sequential case. We first describe the dual form of the SVGP solution and then use it to derive a new memory-based objective to perform sequential learning. Then, we optimize the new objective by using the dual-SVGP algorithm to update the posterior, hyperparameters, and inducing inputs. Finally, we present the new Bayesian leverage score to select the memory.

3.1
The Dual Form of the SVGP Solution

The dual form of the sequential SVGP solution has a strikingly similar form to the full-GP case given in
Eq.

1
. Consider the following ELBO defined over the old data,

\mathcal{L}_{\text{old}}=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}].

(6)

Adam et al. 2021
show that a stationary point
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}\,|\,\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}},\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})
has a dual form for its natural parameters:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\textrm{old}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{old}},

(7)

\displaystyle(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\textrm{old}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1},

(8)

where
\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{old}}=\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}}/\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}+\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}}
is a ‘pseudo’ output, and
(\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}},\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}})
are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q^{\text{old}}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})],

(9)

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q^{\text{old}}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[-\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})].

The result follows from Eq. (21) in
Adam et al. 2021
. A proof is given in
Section

A.2
where we also derive a dual-form for the predictive distribution
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(f_{i})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(f)}[f_{i}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}},

(10)

\displaystyle\textrm{Var}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(f)}[f_{i}]

\displaystyle=\kappa_{ii}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}-\left(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}\right)^{-1}\right]\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}.

The form is strikingly similar to
Eq.

1
but it uses two dual parameters consisting of an
m
-length vector
\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}
and
m\times m
-size matrix
\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}
, defined as

\begin{split}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}&=\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\,\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}},\\
\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}&=\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\,\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\,\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}.\end{split}

(11)

The pair
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}},\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}})
can be seen as ‘amortized’ dual parameters which do not depend on the data size
n
, but rather provide a distilled compact summary through
m
inducing inputs.
Similarly, the pair
(\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}},\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}})
can be seen as an estimate of
(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i})
in
Eq.

2
obtained by using
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(f_{i})
instead of the exact posterior
p(f_{i}|\mathbf{y}^{\text{old}})
.

The posterior can also be expressed in a form where likelihoods are replaced by their Gaussian approximations,

\displaystyle q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle\propto e^{\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}}-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\propto p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}\,\,e^{\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}}-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\left((\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\right)\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle\propto p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}\textstyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}}e^{-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}(\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{old}}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}},

(12)

where the second line is obtained by adding and subtracting
\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{u}/2
and the third line is obtained by using
Eqs.

7
and
8
and then completing the squares.
Previous works used EP to obtain such site parameters
(
Csató & Opper 2002
;
Bui et al. 2017
)
, but we obtain them with SVGP too.

Adam et al. 2021
further show that the dual form can be used to improve hyperparameter optimization. The idea is to fix the dual parameters in
Eq.

12
and treat both
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u})
and
\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}
as functions of
\bm{\theta}
. Then, plugging
Eq.

12
into
Eq.

6
gives rise to an objective which, compared to the ELBO, is better aligned with the marginal likelihood; see App. B of
Adam et al. 2021
.
They propose a stochastic expectation-maximization procedure in which the posterior is updated by maximizing
Eq.

6
and hyperparameters are updated using the new objective.

In the next section, we will derive a new objective for sequential learning where memory is used to mimic the batch-SVGP of
Eq.

3
. Similarly to
Bui et al. 2017
, we also use the pseudo-data idea, but our approach is more straightforward and directly addresses the issues they aim to solve.

3.2
Memory-based Objective for Sequential Learning

Our goal is to closely mimic the batch-SVGP objective given in
Eq.

3
. We make two modifications to the batch objective. First, instead of
\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}
, we use a subset of examples stored in a memory set
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{new}}\cup\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}].

(13)

For
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
, we exactly recover
\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}
and, for small memory size, the error can be minimized by carefully choosing
\mathcal{M}
as a representative set of
\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}
.

Second, we reuse the old posterior
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})
which contains a distilled summary of the old data in its dual parameters.
Our key idea is to express the prior in terms of the old posterior by using
Eq.

12
,

\displaystyle p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}

\displaystyle\propto\frac{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})}{\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}}e^{-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}(\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{old}}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}}}\propto\frac{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})}{\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}}(\mathbf{u})},

(14)

where we rewrite the denominator as a Gaussian:

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}\,|\,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{old}},\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\text{old}}),

(15)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}(\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}+\mathbf{m}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}},

(16)

\displaystyle\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}(\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}.

(17)

A derivation is in
Section

A.3
.
The denominator can be seen as pseudo-data, similar to those derived in
Bui et al. 2017
.
Eq.

14
is exact whenever
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}{(\mathbf{u})}
is an exact stationary point of
Eq.

6
, for example, in the very first update.
However, in sequential learning,
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}{(\mathbf{u})}
is almost never exact because errors can accumulate due to lack of full access to data at subsequent updates.
To handle this, we rewrite
Eq.

15
where only examples in
\mathcal{M}
are used in the denominator:

\frac{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})}{\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}}(\mathbf{u})}\approx\frac{\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})}{\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})},

(18)

where
\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}
is an estimate of
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}{(\mathbf{u})}
(see
Section

3.3
). The denominator is defined in similar ways but using only
\mathcal{M}
.
We will use this as our new prior which can be obtained from the dual parameters by removing contributions of
\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}-\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}},

(19)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}-\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}.

(20)

Here, for notational simplicity, we use
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}},\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}})
to denote the dual parameters of
\hat{q}^{\text{old}}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})
.
A derivation is in
Section

A.3
along with expressions for the mean and covariance.

Replacing
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u})
in
Eq.

13
with the new prior (
\mathcal{Z}
denotes the normalizing constant), we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{q}_{\text{seq}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{new}}\cup\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\bigg[\!q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\|\frac{\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}{(\mathbf{u})}}{\mathcal{Z}\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})}\!\bigg]\!.

(21)

Why do we expect the new objective in
Eq.

21
to be more accurate? One explanation is to see the new objective as an improved version of variational continual-learning (VCL)
(
Nguyen et al. 2018
)
, by rewriting it as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{q}_{\text{seq}}=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{new}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}{(\mathbf{u})}]

\displaystyle\,\,+\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})}[\log\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})].

(22)

The first two terms are equal to the VCL objective where the estimated old posterior is used as the new prior. VCL is exact only when the old posterior is exact, which, as discussed before, is unlikely in practice. To address this issue, in the third term we add a few representative examples of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
using
\mathcal{M}
. The fourth term is subtracting the pseudo-data to avoid double-counting the contributions of the examples in

\mathcal{M}
.

The new objective directly addresses the issues raised by
Bui et al. 2017
regarding hyperparameter learning, who add two KLD terms (see the last two lines of
Eq.

5
). This essentially uses the ratio
q_{\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}}}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}})/p_{\bm{\theta}_{\textrm{old}}}(\mathbf{u}_{\textrm{old}})
, which is proportional to the pseudo-data.
In contrast, in our objective, we use the exact likelihood (the third term in
Eq.

22
) in addition to the pseudo-data (the fourth term). The two terms are added on top of the VCL objective where
\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})
is used.

In the limit of
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
, we have
\mathcal{L}_{\text{seq}}^{q}=\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}
(assuming
\bm{Z}
and
\bm{\theta}
to be the same): In this case, the first terms of both
Eq.

21
and
Eq.

3
are equal; the second terms can also be seen to be equal using
Eq.

14
.
By using a good representative memory of the past data, we expect to mimic the batch-SVGP objective.
Using memory to improve sequential learning is unique to our approach.

3.3
Inference using the Memory-Based Objective

We will optimize the new objective in
Eq.

21
by using the Bayesian learning rule (BLR) of
Khan & Rue 2021
which is a natural-gradient descent algorithm. This method is also used by
Adam et al. 2021
who write the update in a natural-parameter form. We will instead use a form where we update the estimate of the dual pair
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)},\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)})
at each iteration
t
. A detailed derivation is given in
Section

A.4
and
below we give the final update,

\displaystyle\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\rho)\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t-1)}+\rho\Big(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}+\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{(t)}\Big),

(23)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\rho)\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t-1)}+\rho\Big(\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}+\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\Big).

The update adds the contribution of the new data using the dual parameters
(\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{(t)},\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)})
which are defined similarly to
Eqs.

7
and
8
but by using the most recent posterior
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t-1)}(f_{i})
. The mean and covariance of the posterior needed to compute the expectations are obtained as follows,

\mathbf{m}^{(t)}\leftarrow\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)},~~\mathbf{V}^{(t)}\leftarrow\big(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\big)^{-1}.

(24)

3.4
Learning of Hyperparameter and Inducing Inputs

For hyperparameter learning, we will use the new ELBO derived by
Adam et al. 2021
who show faster convergence; also see the recent work by
Li et al. 2023
. As before, we will derive it by assuming that
\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
is available and then we will make approximations to handle the sequential case.

We follow the procedure given in
Adam et al. 2021
.
Let
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}}(\mathbf{u})
denote the solution obtained by maximizing
Eq.

3
. The idea is to take its dual form and fix
(\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{new}},\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{new}})
while treating both
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u})
and
\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}
as functions of
\bm{\theta}
:

\displaystyle q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}}(\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\propto p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}\textstyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}e^{-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{new}}(\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{new}}-\mathbf{u}^{\top}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\bm{\theta}})^{2}},

(25)

where
\text{${\cal D}$}=\text{${\cal D}$}^{\text{new}}\cup\text{${\cal D}$}^{\text{old}}
and we have indicated explicit dependency on
\bm{\theta}
by denoting
\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\bm{\theta}}
and
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}}(\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta})
.
Then, plugging
Eq.

25
in
Eq.

6
gives the following objective
(see App. B in
Adam et al. 2021
, for details)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textrm{seq}}^{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\log\mathcal{Z}(\bm{\theta})+c(\bm{\theta}),

(26)

\displaystyle c(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}}}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i};\bm{\theta})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{E}_{q(\mathbf{u};\bm{\theta})}[\log\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}}(\mathbf{u})],

\log\mathcal{Z}(\bm{\theta})=-\frac{1}{2}\log\left|\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{\bm{\theta}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{\bm{\theta}}\right|\\
-\frac{1}{2}\text{$\bm{b}$}^{\top}\big(\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{\bm{\theta}}\big)^{-1}\text{$\bm{b}$}+\text{const}.

Here, we keep
\text{$\bm{B}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}}
fixed, and treat everything else as a function of
\bm{\theta}
. We define
\text{$\bm{b}$}=(\text{$\bm{B}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}})^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{new}}+\mathbf{m}^{\text{new}}
which contains the part in
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{new}}
that are fixed.

For the sequential setting, we modify the first term in
c(\bm{\theta})
by using a stochastic approximation for the sum over
\mathcal{D}_{\textrm{old}}
. We approximate it by using
\mathcal{M}
and scale it to get an unbiased estimate of the gradient,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}^{\text{new}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i};\bm{\theta})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]

\displaystyle+\frac{n_{\textrm{old}}}{n_{\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\mathbb{E}_{q(f_{i};\bm{\theta})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})].

Here,
n_{\textrm{old}}
and
n_{\mathcal{M}}
are the size of
\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}
and
\mathcal{M}
respectively. This is similar to the approach of
Titsias et al. 2020
.

To update the inducing inputs, we use the pivoted-Cholesky method of
Burt et al. 2020
. A similar method is used by
Maddox et al. 2021
but in combination with gradient based optimization. We find that our framework gives much better performance using the pivoted-Cholesky alone, and we do not need any gradient based optimization of inducing points. We concatenate
[\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{old}},\mathbf{X}_{\textrm{new}}]
and use pivoted-Cholesky to get the new inducing points

\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{new}}
. Having moved the inducing points to
\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{new}}
, we now need to adjust the variational parameters which are still defined over the old inducing points
\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{old}}
.
We adjust this using a projection matrix
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{new}},\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{old}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{old}},\mathbf{Z}_{\textrm{old}}}^{-1}
to get the new dual parameters,

\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}\leftarrow\mathbf{P}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}\leftarrow\mathbf{P}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{P}^{\top}.

(27)

we place a prior over the weights

(29)

Comparing the above linear model to
Eq.

12
, we see that the SVGP posterior is equivalent to the posterior of a linear model with a major difference: the noise is now heteroscedastic with variance
1/\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{new}}
for the
i
th
example. This motivates the following Bayesian generalisation of RLS, which we refer to as the Bayesian leverage score (BLS),

(30)

\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}
explicitly. The score can be computed without any additional cost by rewriting it in terms of predictive variance
\hat{v}_{i}
of
q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})
, that is,

h^{\textrm{BLS}}_{i}=\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{new}}\hat{v}_{i}^{\text{new}}.

(31)

For Gaussian likelihoods, BLS in
Eq.

31

\hat{\beta}_{i}=1/\sigma^{2}
, but unlike RLS, the BLS also applies to non-Gaussian likelihoods.
When selecting the memory, we want representative examples. We achieve this by sampling memory points from the new batch weighted by the BLS score. This biases our memory towards more difficult examples whilst covering the typical set, which we find improves performance. The final algorithm is given in

5
Discussion and Conclusions

The difficulty in building a sequential sparse GP model is due to the lack of access to previous data. Approaches that do not consider the dual parameter perspective fail to see the essence of the inference problem; how to accurately infer the dual parameters in a sequential manner. The problem formulation was presented in
Csató & Opper 2002
, but there an EP inference scheme was used. This paper shows how to update parameters sequentially using variational approximate inference.

Furthermore, we use natural gradient updates which come easily in the dual parameter formulation allowing for a method (
i.e.
, few inference step iterations) that works with general likelihoods. Lack of access to past data also makes it challenging to learn hyperparameters
\bm{\theta}
. The problem worsens in the non-stationary continual learning setting. Here, to counter the problem of forgetting previous data, we introduce the concept of memory, a technique shown to have success in deep learning
(
Pan et al. 2020
)
. We find that a small amount of memory can dramatically improve performance by replicating the offline ELBO solution.

Our memory approach is novel and different from previous work, which attempts to replace missing data with additional regularization terms in the ELBO
(
Bui et al. 2017
;
Kapoor et al. 2021
)
. Our final method manages to control the error that come from inference, learning and representation (memory and inducing points). Furthermore, our method can be applied to a variety of sequential learning tasks.
Given the importance of memory to our method, a good selection technique is paramount. We derive a new Bayesian leverage score, given its connection to Bayesian approximate inference. The score generalizes the prevalent RLS score used for kernel sampling methods
(
Alaoui & Mahoney 2015
)
. We find the importance of the dual parameters again, as they are a crucial component of the BLS. We demonstrate the applicability of our method to a complex combined batch Bayesian optimization and active learning problem, and continual learning problem.

A reference implementation of the methods presented in this paper is available at:

https://github.com/AaltoML/sequential-gp
.

A.1
Equivalence to Csató and Opper

The result from
Csató & Opper 2002
(
cf.
Lemma 1) assumes a Gaussian prior
p(\mathbf{f})
with a mean function
m(\mathbf{x})
and covariance
\kappa(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, with likelihood
p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
where
\mathbf{f}=[f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}]
is a vector of function values
f_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})
.
For this case, they use the following two parameterization,

\begin{split}q_{i}&=\int\nabla_{f_{i}}p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\frac{p(\mathbf{f})}{\int p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f},\\
R_{i,j}&=\int\,\nabla^{2}_{f_{i},f_{j}}p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\frac{p(\mathbf{f})}{\int p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}-q_{i}q_{j}.\end{split}

(32)

For the case when
p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})
, we can show the following,

q_{i}=\alpha_{i}\text{ and }R_{i,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\beta_{i},&\text{ when }i=j\\
0,&\text{ when }i\neq j\end{array}\right.,

where
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
are as defined in
Eq.

2
. We first show the proof for
q_{i}
,

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{f_{i}}p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\frac{1}{p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})}\frac{p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})}{\int p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{j}\,|\,f_{j})p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})p(f_{i}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}f_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{n})}[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})].

(33)

Here, the first line is obtained by simply multiplying and dividing by
p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
, and we get the second line by using the definition of the posterior
p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})
and the fact that
\nabla\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})=[\nabla p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})]/p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
. The third line follows from the assumption that the likelihood factorizes over data examples, and the fourth line obtained by noting that
\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{j}\,|\,f_{j})
is non-zero only when
i=j
.
The fifth line follows by marginalizing out all
f_{j}
other than
f_{i}
, and the final line is just a different way to write the expectation.

For
R_{i,j}
, we proceed in a similar fashion. We will use the following identity to write
\nabla_{f_{i},f_{j}}p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
in terms
\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
:

\frac{1}{p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})}\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})=\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})+\left[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right]\left[\nabla_{f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right].

(34)

This can be proved by rearranging the derivative of
\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
by using the fact that
\nabla\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})=[\nabla p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})]/p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
(which we also used in the derivation of
q_{i}
above). Using this we can simplify
R_{i,j}
,

\displaystyle R_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int\,\nabla^{2}_{f_{i},f_{j}}p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\frac{1}{p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})}\frac{p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})}{\int p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})p(\mathbf{f})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}}\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}-q_{i}q_{j}

\displaystyle=\int\,\left[\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})+\left[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right]\left[\nabla_{f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right]\right]p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}-q_{i}q_{j}

\displaystyle=\int\,\left[\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right]p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}+\int\left[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right]\left[\nabla_{f_{j}}\log p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})\right]p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}-q_{i}q_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\,\left[\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}\log p(y_{k}\,|\,f_{k})\right]p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}+\sum_{l,m}\int\left[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{l}\,|\,f_{l})\right]\left[\nabla_{f_{j}}\log p(y_{m}\,|\,f_{m})\right]p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}-q_{i}q_{j},

(35)

where in the first line we multiply and divide by
p(\text{${\cal D}$}\,|\,\mathbf{f})
and use
Eq.

34
and definition of the posterior to get to the second line. The third line is a rearrangement, and the last line is obtained by using the factorial property of the likelihood.

For the second term we get it to be non-zero when
i=l
and
j=m
, and then integrating over all function values, except
f_{i}
and
f_{j}
, the term reduces to
q_{i}q_{j}
, which can be cancelled out. This gives us the following expression,

\begin{split}R_{i,j}&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int\,\left[\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}f_{j}}\log p(y_{k}\,|\,f_{k})\right]p(\mathbf{f}\,|\,\text{${\cal D}$})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}\\
&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}_{p(f_{i}\,|\,\mathbf{y}_{n})}[\nabla_{f_{i}f_{j}}^{2}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})],&\text{ when }i=j,\\
0,&\text{ when }i\neq j.\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(36)

This is because the derivative inside is only nonzero when
i=j=k
, and we can express the integral only over
f_{i}
. This proves the required result.

A.2
Derivation of the Dual-form of the SVGP Stationary Point

We will derive the dual-form of the stationary point of the following SVGP objective:

\mathcal{L}(q)=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}].

(37)

Derivation simplifies by using natural and expectation parameters of
q_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{m},\mathbf{V})
, denoted by
\bm{\lambda}
and
\bm{\mu}
respectively,

\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}=(\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}^{(1)},\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}^{(2)})=(\text{$\bm{V}$}^{-1}\mathbf{m},\,\,-\frac{1}{2}\text{$\bm{V}$}^{-1}),\qquad\text{$\bm{\mu}$}=(\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(1)},\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(2)})=(\mathbf{m},\,\,\mathbf{m}\mathbf{m}^{\top}+\text{$\bm{V}$}),

Taking the gradient with respect to
\bm{\mu}
of
Eq.

37
at a stationary point
q_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}}^{*}(\mathbf{u})
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}}\mathbb{D}_{\text{KL}}[q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{u})\,\|\,p_{\bm{\theta}}{(\mathbf{u})}],

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-(\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{*}-\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{\text{prior}}),

\displaystyle\implies\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{*}=\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{\text{prior}}+\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})],

(38)

where
\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{\text{prior}}=(0,-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}/2)
is the natural parameter of the prior
p_{\bm{\theta}}(\mathbf{u})
.

We can expand this equation and write two equations corresponding to the two natural parameters
(\mathbf{V}_{*}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{*},\,-\mathbf{V}_{*}^{-1}/2)
. We get the following for the first natural parameter,

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}^{-1}_{*}\mathbf{m}_{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(1)}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\nabla\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]-\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\nabla^{2}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}(\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{*}+\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{*})

(39)

In the second line we used the identity given in
Khan & Rue 2021
which uses chain-rule and Bonnet’s result. This gives us
Eq.

7
. This also matches with Eq. 21 of
Adam et al. 2021
who define
\lambda_{1,i}^{*}=\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{*}+\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{*}
.

Similarly for the second natural parameter,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{V}_{*}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(2)}}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\mathbb{E}_{q^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\nabla^{2}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}

(40)

Here again, in the second line we used the identity given in
Khan & Rue 2021
which uses chain-rule and Price’s result. This gives us
Eq.

8
. This too matches with Eq. 21 of
Adam et al. 2021
.

We will now derive the dual-form similar to
Eq.

1
. Rearranging and substituting
Eq.

40
in to
Eq.

39
, we get,

\displaystyle[\mathbf{V}_{*}^{-1}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}]\mathbf{m}_{*}=\textstyle\sum_{{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{*}\qquad

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}_{*}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{*}}_{=\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{*}^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}(\textstyle\sum_{{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top})\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\qquad

\displaystyle\implies\mathbf{V}_{*}^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_{{i\in\mathcal{D}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}}_{=\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}

(41)

Now substituting these results into
Eq.

4
, we get the results:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{*}(f)}[f_{i}]&=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{*}=\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}},\\
\textrm{Var}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}^{*}(f)}[f_{i}]&=\kappa_{ii}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{V}_{*}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\right]\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}=\kappa_{ii}-\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}-\left(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}+\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\right)^{-1}\right]\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\end{split}

(42)

Thus proving that we can rewrite the posterior process in terms of the sparse dual parameters.

A.3
Derivation of the Pseudo-Data and New Prior

We first derive the distribution form used in
Eq.

14
for the following,

\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}}(\mathbf{u})\propto\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}e^{{-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}(\hat{y}_{i}^{*}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}}}=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}\,|\,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{*},\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{*}),

(43)

where
(\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*},\hat{y}_{i}^{*})
are obtained by training on the data
{\cal D}
. We start by writing the product in a matrix form,

\begin{split}\log\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}}(\mathbf{u})&=-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{\top}(\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{*})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}+\text{const.}\\
\log\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}}e^{{-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}(\hat{y}_{i}^{*}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}}}&=-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{*}+\text{const.}\end{split}

(44)

where
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}
, and
\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{*}
and
\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*}
are vectors of all
\hat{y}_{i}^{*}
and
\hat{\beta}_{i}^{*}
respectively. Then, we simply match the terms in
\mathbf{u}
. First, by matching the quadratic term, we get,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{*})^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{\top}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{x}}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{*}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}

(45)

which gives us the third equation in
Eq.

15
. Next, by matching the linear term in
\mathbf{u}
, we get,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{*}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{x}}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{x}}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})[\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})^{-1}\hat{\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}}^{*}+\mathbf{A}\mathbf{m}^{*}]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{x}}\hat{\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}}^{*}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{x}}\text{{diag}}(\hat{\text{$\bm{\beta}$}}^{*})\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}^{*}_{\mathbf{u}}+\mathbf{m}^{*},

(46)

which recovers the second equation in
Eq.

15
.
Note that we can use different forms as well. For example, an equivalent way to write
Eq.

15
is

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{old}}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}|\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}},\,\,\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\text{old}}),

(47)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}},

(48)

\displaystyle\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\text{old}}

\displaystyle=\left[(\mathbf{V}^{\text{old}})^{-1}-\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\right]^{-1},

which is in terms of the mean and covariance of the posterior.
This expression is similar to the one used in
Bui et al. 2017
. All such forms are equivalent. We use
Eq.

15
because it uses dual parameters which are readily obtained through the natural-gradient method of
Adam et al. 2021
.

We can write
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})
in a similar way,

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i\in\mathcal{M}}e^{{-\text{$\frac{1}{2}$}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}(\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{old}}-\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{u})^{2}}}=\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}\,|\,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathcal{M}},\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\mathcal{M}}).

(49)

The expressions for
\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathcal{M}}
and
\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\mathcal{M}}
are derived by repeating the same derivation but now only involving
i\in\mathcal{M}
.
For example,

\displaystyle(\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\underbrace{\left(\textstyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\right)}_{=\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\mathcal{M}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\hat{y}_{i}^{\text{old}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}(\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}}+\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\text{$\bm{a}$}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\underbrace{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{\text{old}}}_{=\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}}}+\left[\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{\text{old}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\bigg)\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\right]\mathbf{m}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}[\bm{B}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathbf{u}}]^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}}+\mathbf{m}^{*}.

(50)

Next, we derive the mean and covariance of the new prior
\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})/\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})\propto\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{m}_{\text{prior}},\mathbf{V}_{\text{prior}})
. Let us denote the current estimate of the dual pair by
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}},\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}})
as defined in
Eq.

11
. There, the whole
\mathcal{D}_{\text{old}}
is used but, in reality, the dual pairs are learned sequentially and may not represent the exact dual parameters. Still, we use the same notation for
convenience.
The current natural-parameters of the posterior can be then written by rewriting
Eqs.

7
and
8
in terms of the dual pairs,

\begin{split}(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\text{old}}&=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}},\\
(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\text{old}})^{-1}&=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}.\end{split}

(51)

Similarly, we can write the natural parameters of
\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})
in terms (of the current estimate) of
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}},\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}})
,

\begin{split}(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\mathcal{M}}&=(\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{*},\\
(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}&=(\tilde{\text{$\bm{\Sigma}$}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}.\end{split}

(52)

The natural parameters of the new prior are simply obtained by subtracting the natural parameters given above,

\begin{split}(\mathbf{V}^{\text{prior}})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{prior}}&=(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\text{old}})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\text{old}}-(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{m}}^{\mathcal{M}}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{\text{old}},\\
(\mathbf{V}^{\text{prior}})^{-1}&=(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\text{old}})^{-1}-(\hat{\mathbf{V}}^{\mathcal{M}})^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1},\end{split}

(53)

where
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}},\,\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}})
are defined in
Eqs.

19
and
20
.

A.4
Derivation of the Natural-Gradient Descent Algorithm

We will optimize the new objective in
Eq.

21
by using the Bayesian learning rule (BLR) of
Khan & Rue 2021
which is a natural-gradient descent algorithm. We start by denoting the natural and expectation parameters of a posterior
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}(\mathbf{u})
obtained in the
t
’th iteration by
\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}^{(t)}
and
\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(t)}
respectively. We denote the natural parameters of the prior
\hat{q}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}}(\mathbf{u})/\hat{p}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbf{u})\propto\mathrm{N}(\mathbf{u}|\mathbf{m}_{\text{prior}},\mathbf{V}_{\text{prior}})
by
\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{\text{prior}}
; an expression is given in
Eq.

53
.
With these, the BLR update can be written as the following,

\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}^{(t)}\leftarrow(1-\rho)\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}^{(t-1)}+\rho\bigg(\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\left.\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}}\,\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]\right|_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}=\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(t)}}+\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{\text{prior}}\bigg).

(54)

To simplify the implementation, we will write the updates in terms of the estimate of the dual pair
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)},\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)})
at iteration
t
. We make use of the fact that each iterate
\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}^{(t)}
has the same dual form as in
Eq.

52
. This is written below,

\begin{split}(\mathbf{V}^{(t)})^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{(t)}&=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{(t)},\\
(\mathbf{V}^{(t)})^{-1}&=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}.\end{split}

(55)

As shown in
Eq.

53
, the prior
\text{$\bm{\lambda}$}_{\text{prior}}
too has the same form written in terms of the dual parameters
(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}},\,\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}})
. Finally, as shown in
Eqs.

39
and
40
, the natural-gradients too can be written in the same form,

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\left.\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(1)}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]\right|_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}=\text{$\bm{\mu}$}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{(t)}\right)+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\mathbf{m}^{(t)},

(56)

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\left.\nabla_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}^{(2)}}\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})]\right|_{\text{$\bm{\mu}$}=\text{$\bm{\mu}$}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1},

(57)

where
\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{(t)}
and
\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)}
are defined similarly to
Eqs.

7
and
8
, but now by using
q_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t-1)}(f_{i})
,

\displaystyle\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q^{(t-1)}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[\nabla_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})],\qquad\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)}=\mathbb{E}_{q^{(t-1)}_{\mathbf{u}}(f_{i})}[-\nabla^{2}_{f_{i}}\log p(y_{i}\,|\,f_{i})].

(58)

We can use these to simply write the update in terms of the dual pair. Essentially, we use the following equivalent update,

\displaystyle\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\rho)\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t-1)}+\rho\left(\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}+\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\alpha}_{i}^{(t)}\right),

(59)

\displaystyle\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow(1-\rho)\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t-1)}+\rho\left(\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\text{old}\backslash\mathcal{M}}+\textstyle\sum_{i\in\text{${\cal D}$}_{\text{new}}}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}\hat{\beta}_{i}^{(t)}\mathbf{k}_{\mathbf{z}i}^{\top}\right).

This is followed by an update of the mean and the covariance given below,

\begin{split}\mathbf{m}^{(t)}&\leftarrow\text{$\bm{\alpha}$}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)},\qquad\qquad\mathbf{V}^{(t)}\leftarrow\left(\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\bm{B}_{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}+\mathbf{K}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{z}}^{-1}\right)^{-1}.\end{split}

(60)

This is derived by using
Eq.

55
and simplifying similarly to the first equation in
Eq.

41
.

B.3
Magnetic Anomaly Modelling

For the robot experiment for learning magentic field anomalies, we use the data from
Solin et al. 2018
that is available at
https://github.com/AaltoML/magnetic-data
. The exact metric position of the robot is provided time-synced to magnetometer samples from a low-cost/high-noise Invensense MPU-9150 magnetometer sampled at 50 Hz. As discussed in
Solin et al. 2018
, GPs provide a principled approach for modelling smooth anomalies in the magnetic vector field.

We consider the task of online mapping of local anomalies in the ambient Earth magnetic field. These anomalies are induced by the bedrock and magnetic material in the building structure indoors. We follow the experiment setup and data provided in
Solin et al. 2018
, where a small wheeled robot equipped with a 3-axis magnetometer moves around in an indoor space measuring roughly
$6\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$\times$6\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
. We consider a simplified model, where we assign a GP prior to the magnetic field strength
\|\mathbf{H}\|\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,\sigma_{0}^{2}+\kappa^{\mathrm{Mat.}}_{\sigma^{2},\ell}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
(in
\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro T}
) over the space under presence of Gaussian measurement noise with variance
\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}
.

We include two separate experiments with different data paths in the same space. The first experiment (
Fig.

5(a)
) uses four separate paths of the robot that cover slightly different parts of the space. Under the sequential learning framework, we simultaneously learn the four hyperparameters (
\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma^{2},\ell,\sigma_{\mathrm{n}}^{2}
) and the representation in terms of inducing points and memory. The high measurement noise renders the value of single data points small and stresses the importance of the sparse approach. The problem is sequential, as we only receive information as the robot moves through the input space and do not have access to all previous data. Our sequential method is able to form a representation by spreading inducing points and learning the hyperparameters progressively. Showing the method has practical importance in real-world robot estimation tasks, where the robot is not constrained to a predefined area. This has been a weakness in previous approaches that have considered a fixed domain to form an efficient sparse basis function decomposition of the problem in that domain. In
Fig.

5(b)
, the set up of the second experiment is similar, but now we receive data during each robot path instead after one exploration. The visualization shows the mean estimate with marginal variance (uncertainty) controlling the opacity. We recover the same local estimate as in experiment
Fig.

5(a)
.

The data is a set of
9
trajectories out of which we use
5
for the experiments. For Experiment #1,
Fig.

5(a)
, we use trajectory 1, 2, 4, and 5 (with
n=8875,9105,7332,8313
, respectively) and for Experiment #2,
Fig.

5(b)
, we use trajectory 3 (
n=9404
). For both the experiments, we use a sum of two kernels: constant kernel and a Matérn-
\nicefrac{{5}}{{2}}
kernel. For the constant kernel, we set the initial variance as
500
. The number of inducing variables is set to
100
in both the experiments, and we use a Gaussian likelihood initialized with a noise variance of
0.1
. For optimization of the hyperparameters, we use the Adam optimizer with learning rate
10^{-2}
for
20{,}000
iterations. For Experiment #2, we split trajectory 3 into 20 sets, thus each set has around
470
data points.
