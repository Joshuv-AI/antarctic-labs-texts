Abstract
. The entropic doubling σ ent ​ [ X ] \sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[X] of a random variable X X taking values in an abelian group G G is a variant of the notion of the doubling constant σ ⁡ [ A ] \sigma[A] of a finite subset A A of G G , but it enjoys somewhat better properties; for instance, it contracts upon applying a homomorphism. In this paper we develop further the theory of entropic doubling and give various applications, including: (1) A new proof of a result of Pálvölgyi and Zhelezov on the “skew dimension” of subsets of 𝐙 D \mathbf{Z}^{D} with small doubling; (2) A new proof, and an improvement, of a result of the second author on the dimension of subsets of 𝐙 D \mathbf{Z}^{D} with small doubling; (3) A proof that the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture over 𝐅 2 \mathbf{F}_{2} implies the (weak) Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture over 𝐙 \mathbf{Z} .

Introduction and statement of results

Notation.
Throughout the paper we use standard asymptotic notation. The notations
X=O(Y)
,
X\ll Y
, or
Y\gg X
all denote the bound
|X|\leqslant CY
for an absolute constant
C
. Different instances of the notation may imply different constants
C
.

1.1.
Entropy doubling and Ruzsa distance

Let
G=(G,+)
be an abelian group. In this paper, by a “
G
-valued random variable” we mean a random variable
X
taking values in a finite subset of
G
. Given such a variable, the
entropic doubling constant
(first introduced in
[
22
]
)
\sigma_{\operatorname{ent}}[X]
is defined by the formula

\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[X]\coloneqq\exp\bigl(\mathbf{H}(X_{1}+X_{2})-\mathbf{H}(X)\bigr),

where
X_{1},X_{2}
are independent copies of
X
. Here,
\mathbf{H}(X)
denotes the Shannon entropy of
X
, the definition and basic properties of which we review in Appendix
A
.

If
A\subseteq G
is a finite non-empty set, by abuse of notation we write
\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[A]=\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[U_{A}]
, where
U_{A}
is a uniform random variable drawn from
A
. For instance, if
H
is a finite subgroup of
G
, one can check that
\sigma_{\operatorname{ent}}[H]=1
.

The entropic doubling constant is related to other standard measures of additive structure via the inequalities

\frac{|A|^{3}}{\mathrm{E}[A]}\leqslant\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[A]\leqslant\sigma[A].

(1.1)

Here,
\sigma[A]\coloneqq\frac{|A+A|}{|A|}
is the doubling constant of
A
and

\mathrm{E}[A]\coloneqq|\{(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})\in A^{4}:a_{1}+a_{2}=a_{3}+a_{4}\}|

is the additive energy of
A
.

The second inequality in (
1.1
) was noted in
[
22
, Equation 10]
, and we recall the proof in Appendix
B
. The first inequality seems not to have appeared explicitly in the literature, but it follows easily either by a direct argument using the weighted AM–GM inequality, or by quoting the monotonicity of Rényi entropy. For completeness, we give this argument in Appendix
B
. Both inequalities can be far from tight: we give an example in Appendix
B
.

Entropic Ruzsa distance

If
X,Y
are
G
-valued random variables (not necessarily independent, or even defined on the same sample space), then we define the
entropic Ruzsa distance

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
between these variables by the formula

d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)\coloneqq\mathbf{H}(X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(X^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(Y^{\prime}),

(1.2)

where
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
are independent copies of
X,Y
respectively.
This concept, introduced by Ruzsa
[
18
]
and studied in more detail by the third author
[
22
]
, generalizes entropic doubling, since
\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[X]=e^{d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,-X)}
.

It is easy to see that
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)=d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y,X)\geqslant 0
, and also that
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(U_{H},U_{H})=0
for any finite subgroup
H
of
G
. Note that
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
depends only on the distributions

p_{X}(x)\coloneqq\mathbf{P}(X=x);\quad p_{Y}(y)\coloneqq\mathbf{P}(Y=y)

of
X,Y
. We have (see the final paragraph of
[
18
]
,
[
22
, Theorem 1.10]
, or Lemma
1.1
below) the
entropic Ruzsa triangle inequality

d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Z)\leqslant d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)+d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y,Z)

(1.3)

for any three
G
-valued random variables
X,Y,Z
.

Remark
.

It is somewhat traditional to use the letter
K
for the combinatorial doubling constant
\sigma[A]
. We will generally use the letter
k
for distances
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)
. Where these arise from sets (for instance if
X=Y=U_{A}
) one should informally think of
k
being on the order of
\log K
. It should be carefully noted that
k
may take values in
[0,\infty)
and is not constrained to be an integer.

It will be technically convenient to introduce a small modification of the entropic Ruzsa distance. Define the
maximal entropic Ruzsa distance

d_{\operatorname{ent}}^{*}(X,Y)
to be the quantity

d^{*}_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)\coloneqq\sup_{X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{H}(X^{\prime}-Y^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(X^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(Y^{\prime})\right)

(1.4)

where
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
range over all pairs of random variables with marginal distributions
p_{X}
,
p_{Y}
respectively (i.e., all couplings of
X
and
Y
). In particular,
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
are
not
required to be independent.

We have the following observations.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be
G
-valued random variables. Then:

(i)

We have
d^{*}_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Z)\leqslant d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)+d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y,Z)
.

(ii)

We have
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)\leqslant d^{*}_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)\leqslant 3d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
.

For the proof, see Section
2
.

Small Ruzsa distance

We turn now to our first main result, which gives a closer connection than (
1.1
) between small entropy doubling (or more generally small Ruzsa distance) and small doubling.
Here, for a real parameter
p\in(0,1)
, we write
\mathrm{h}(p)\coloneqq p\log\frac{1}{p}+(1-p)\log\frac{1}{1-p}
for the entropy of the Bernouilli random variable with probability
p
.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
C\geqslant 4
be a real parameter. For any
G
-valued random variables
X,Y
there is a non-empty finite subset
S
of
G
such that, if
U_{S}
is a uniform random variable on
S
, then

d^{*}_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(U_{S},Y)\leqslant(C+2)d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)+\mathrm{h}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{C}\biggr)

(1.5)

and

\log\frac{|S-S|}{|S|}\leqslant(2C+4)d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)+2\mathrm{h}\biggl(1-\frac{2}{C}\biggr).

(1.6)

We isolate two special cases of this proposition for future use:

(i)

If we take
C=4
in the above proposition, then (using, in addition, Lemma
1.1
(ii)) we obtain the bounds
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(U_{S},Y)\leqslant 6d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)+\log 2
and
|S-S|\leqslant 4e^{12d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)}|S|
.

(ii)

If
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)=\varepsilon
for some
0<\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{1}{16}
, then on taking
C\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-1/2}
we obtain the bounds
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(U_{S},Y)\ll\varepsilon^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
and
|S-S|\leqslant\bigl(1+O(\varepsilon^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\bigr)|S|
.

We also remark that a qualitative version of this proposition (with unspecified dependence on
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
on the right-hand side) was previously established in
[
22
, Proposition 5.2]
.

In the regime where
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
is small, we can in fact obtain the following more precise result.

Proposition 1.3
.

There is absolute constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that the following is true.
Let
X,Y
be
G
-valued random variables, and suppose that
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
. Then there is some finite subgroup
H
of
G
such that
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,U_{H}),d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y,U_{H})\leqslant 12d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
.

Remark
.

The constant
\varepsilon_{0}
could be specified explicitly if desired, but we have not carried out such a calculation. The constant
12
can be improved, but we will not attempt to optimise it here.

Behaviour under homomorphisms

Given the close relation between notions of entropy doubling and Ruzsa distance and the usual combinatorial notions, one would be forgiven for wondering what the point of introducing the former is.

The answer is that the entropy notions are more flexible and behave better in various ways. Most obviously, they are defined for arbitrary random variables
X
, with no requirement that
X
be uniform on a set. Related to this is the fact that the entropy notions behave well under homomorphisms in a way that the combinatorial notions do not.

The following is our main result in this direction. Here (and below) we use
(X|E)
to denote a random variable
X
conditioned to a positive probability event
E
. We adopt the convention that expressions such as
p_{Y_{1}}(y_{1})p_{Y_{2}}(y_{2})d_{\operatorname{ent}}\bigl((X_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1}),(X_{2}|Y_{2}=y_{2})\bigr)
vanish if one of the events
Y_{1}=y_{1}
,
Y_{2}=y_{2}
occurs with zero probability.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\pi\colon G\to H
be a homomorphism, let
X_{1},X_{2}
be
G
-valued random variables, and set
Y_{1}\coloneqq\pi(X_{1})
and
Y_{2}\coloneqq\pi(X_{2})
.
Then we have

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X_{1},X_{2})\geqslant

\displaystyle\;d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})

\displaystyle+\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in H}p_{Y_{1}}(y_{1})p_{Y_{2}}(y_{2})d_{\operatorname{ent}}\bigl((X_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1}),(X_{2}|Y_{2}=y_{2})\bigr).

In particular, we have

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X_{1},X_{2})\geqslant d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})

and thus

\sigma_{\operatorname{ent}}[\pi(X)]\leqslant\sigma_{\operatorname{ent}}[X]

(1.7)

for any
G
-valued random variable
X
.

Remark
.

The main inequality here is a precise version of the intuition that the doubling constant of a subset of
G
in the presence of a homomorphism
\pi:G\rightarrow H
should somehow be at least the doubling constant of the ‘base’ times some combination of the doubling constants of the ‘fibres’. To make sense of this rigorously we need to pass from sets to general random variables, and replace combinatorial doubling by entropy doubling.

An example of the failure of a similar result in the purely combinatorial setting (in fact of the analogue of (
1.7
)) is outlined in
[
23
, Exercise 2.2.10]
.

Remark
.

Many previous works have noted the advantageous properties of entropy in somewhat related settings.
To give a few examples, in rough chronological order there is the work of Avez
[
1
]
and of Kaĭmanovich and Vershik
[
13
]
on random walks on discrete groups, the work of Hochman
[
12
]
on fractals, and the work of Breuillard–Varjú
[
3
]
on Bernouilli convolutions.

1.2.
Structure of sets with small doubling.

We turn now to applications of the results of the previous subsection to inverse theorems for sets with small doubling.

Skew dimension

The first application is a new proof of a result of Pálvölgyi and Zhelezov
[
17
]
, which they used to give a new and much shorter proof of a celebrated result of Bourgain and Chang
[
2
]
.
For the purposes of this result, an
affine coordinate space
is a subset of
\mathbf{Z}^{D}
(for some
D
) obtained by fixing the values of some possibly empty set of coordinates.
For instance,
\{(2,-1,x_{3}):x_{3}\in\mathbf{Z}\}\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{3}
is an affine coordinate space.
If
A
is a finite subset of some affine coordinate space
V
, its
1
1

1

Pálvölgyi and Zhelezov use the term
query complexity
instead of skew dimension.

skew-dimension

\dim_{*}(A)
is defined inductively, as follows:

•

\dim_{*}(A)=0
if and only if
A
is a singleton (or empty).

•

If
r\geqslant 1
, then
\dim_{*}(A)\leqslant r
if and only if there is a coordinate map
\pi\colon V\rightarrow\mathbf{Z}
such that
\dim_{*}(\pi^{-1}(n)\cap A)\leqslant r-1
for all
n\in\mathbf{Z}
.

Theorem 1.5

(Pálvölgyi–Zhelezov)
.

Suppose
A
is a finite subset of some affine coordinate space with
\sigma[A]\leqslant K
for some
K\geqslant 2
.
Then there is
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
with
|A^{\prime}|\geqslant K^{-O(1)}|A|
and
\dim_{*}A^{\prime}\ll\log K
.

This result is essentially contained in a paper
[
17
]
of Pálvölgyi and Zhelezov: whilst it is not actually stated in that paper, it is mentioned in a talk by Zhelezov
[
25
, minute 27:30]
, and can be established using the methods of
[
17
]
. Lecture notes of the first author may be consulted for a detailed account with some simplifications
[
9
]
, as well as details of the deduction of the result of Bourgain and Chang.

In fact we will establish the following slightly stronger result.

Theorem 1.6
.

There is an absolute constant
C
with the following property. Suppose that
A,B\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{D}
. Then there are
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
with
|A^{\prime}||B^{\prime}|\geqslant e^{-Cd_{\operatorname{ent}}(A,B)}|A||B|
, and such that
\dim_{*}(A^{\prime}),\dim_{*}(B^{\prime})\leqslant Cd_{\operatorname{ent}}(A,B)
.

Setting
B=-A
and using (
1.1
), we recover Theorem
1.5
. The “bilinear” form of Theorem
1.6
will be convenient for induction purposes.

Dimension and PFR over
\mathbf{Z}

Whilst the notion of skew-dimension is useful in the context of the work of Bourgain and Chang, the actual (affine) dimension
\dim A
, defined as the dimension of the span of
A-A
over the reals, is a more intrinsically natural quantity. Note that
\dim_{*}A\leqslant\dim A
for any
A
.
It is conjectured that Theorem
1.5
remains true with
\dim_{*}A^{\prime}
replaced by
\dim A^{\prime}
– this is sometimes (see, for instance,
[
4
]
,
[
17
, Conjecture 1]
) called the weak Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture (PFR) over
\mathbf{Z}
. (The term ‘weak’ comes from the fact that only the dimension is controlled, and no attempt is made to put
A
economically inside a box or homomorphic image of the lattice points in a convex set. Such stronger statements are also speculated to be true, but care must be taken in their formulation, as discussed in
[
15
]
.)

Conjecture 1.7

(Weak PFR over
\mathbf{Z}
)
.

Suppose that
A\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{D}
is a set with
\sigma[A]\leqslant K
. Then there is a subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
,
|A^{\prime}|\geqslant K^{-O(1)}|A|
, with
\dim A^{\prime}\ll\log K
.

Prior to this paper, the best known bound in the direction of this conjecture was a result of the second author
[
16
]
.

Theorem 1.8

(
[
16
, Theorem 1.5]
)
.

Suppose that
A\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{D}
is
a finite set with
\sigma[A]\leqslant K
.
Then there is
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
with
\frac{|A^{\prime}|}{|A|}\gg\exp(-C\log^{2}K)
and
\dim A^{\prime}\ll\log K
.

The proof of this result in
[
16
]
was a little exotic, making use of projections modulo
2
and a kind of “
U^{3}
-energy”. We provide a new, shorter, proof of this result, retaining the first feature but using entropic notions in place of the exotic energy.

We will eventually go further in this paper by using results on sets with additive structure in
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
to improve the bounds, but before doing that we make a detour into the world of structure theorems for sets of small doubling in
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
.

Small doubling in
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
and PFR over
\mathbf{F}_{2}

In the following discussion,
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
denotes the vector space of dimension
D
over
\mathbf{F}_{2}
; the value of
D
is typically somewhat unimportant. Essentially everything we have to say would work equally well over other finite fields
\mathbf{F}
, but this introduces some further technicalities and implied constants would need to depend on
\mathbf{F}
, and we do not discuss this aspect here.

Denote by
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}
any
constant for which the following statement is true: if
A\subseteq\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
, and if the doubling constant
\sigma[A]
is at most
K
, then
A
is covered by
\exp(O(\log^{C_{\operatorname{PFR}}}(2K)))
cosets of some subspace
H\leqslant\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
of size at most
|A|
. The implied constant in the
O()
notation is allowed to depend on
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}
.

A celebrated result of Sanders
[
20
, Corollary A.2]
(together with standard covering lemmas) is that one may take
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}=4
. By an improved version of the argument due to Konyagin (see
[
21
, Theorem 1.4]
), one can in fact take any
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}>3
. Strictly speaking, the statement in
[
21
]
applies to more general abelian groups than
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
, but replaces the subspace
H
by a convex coset progression. However, an inspection of the arguments in the characteristic
2
case shows that the convex coset progression in this case can be taken to be a subspace (basically because the convex coset progressions are constructed via Bohr sets, which are automatically subspaces in the characteristic
2
setting). Alternatively, one can invoke the discrete John theorem (see
[
24
, Theorem 1.6]
) to control the convex coset progression by a generalized arithmetic progression (up to acceptable losses, and increasing
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}
by an epsilon), and then observe that in
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
, all generalized arithmetic progressions are in fact subspaces. We leave the details of these arguments to the interested reader.

We have the following notorious conjecture, known as the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture over
\mathbf{F}_{2}
.

Conjecture 1.9
.

We may take
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}=1
.

There are a large number of equivalent formulations of Conjecture
1.9
; see
[
8
,
11
,
14
,
19
]
. We add a further equivalent form of Conjecture
1.9
, formulated in terms of entropy. It says that, in the case
G=\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
, Proposition
1.3
is valid with no smallness restriction on the entropic distance
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
.

Proposition 1.10
.

Conjecture
1.9
is equivalent to the claim that, for any
\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
-valued random variables
X,Y
, there is a finite subgroup
H\leqslant\mathbf{F}_{2}^{D}
such that
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,U_{H})\ll d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)
.

Small doubling and dimension, again

We now return to the main topic, and offer the following improvement of Theorem
1.8
.

Theorem 1.11
.

Suppose that
A\subseteq\mathbf{Z}^{D}
is a finite set with
\sigma[A]\leqslant K
. Then there is
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
with
\frac{|A^{\prime}|}{|A|}\geqslant\exp(-C\log^{2-\frac{1}{C_{\operatorname{PFR}}}}K)
and
\dim A^{\prime}\ll\log K
.

We remark that the constant
C
is allowed to depend on
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}
(and so by implication on the implied constant in the definition of
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}
).
As it turns out, the implied constant in the
\ll
is independent of
C_{\operatorname{PFR}}
, and in particular can be taken to be
40/\log 2
.

Thus, using the Konyagin/Sanders result we can obtain

\frac{|A^{\prime}|}{|A|}\gg\exp(-C\log^{5/3+o(1)}K),

which is the strongest unconditional result currently known. Perhaps more interestingly, we obtain the following conditional implication between the two forms of the Polynomial Freiman–Ruzsa conjecture discussed above.

Corollary 1.12
.

Conjecture
1.9
implies Conjecture
1.7
.

2.
An improved entropic Ruzsa triangle inequality

In this section we prove Lemma
1.1
.

Proof of Lemma
1.1
.

We begin with part (i). It suffices to show that

\mathbf{H}(X-Z)-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{H}(X)+\mathbf{H}(Z))\leqslant d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)+d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y,Z).

This is equivalent to establishing

\mathbf{H}(X-Z)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(X-Y)+\mathbf{H}(Y-Z)-\mathbf{H}(Y)

whenever
Y
is independent of
(X,Z)
(but
X
and
Z
are not required to be independent of each other).

We apply the submodularity inequality (
A.5
) with
A=X-Y
,
B=Z
,
C=X-Z
. With these choices we have

\mathbf{H}(A,B,C)=\mathbf{H}(X,Y,Z)=\mathbf{H}(X,Z)+\mathbf{H}(Y),

\mathbf{H}(A,C)=\mathbf{H}(X-Y,X-Z)=\mathbf{H}(X-Y,Y-Z)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(X-Y)+\mathbf{H}(Y-Z)

and

\mathbf{H}(B,C)=\mathbf{H}(Z,X-Z)=\mathbf{H}(X,Z).

In the second display we used (
A.3
).
Applying (
A.5
) gives part (i) of Lemma
1.1
.

For part (ii), the first inequality
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)\leqslant d^{*}_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
is trivial. For the second inequality, we apply (i) and (
1.3
) to conclude that

\displaystyle d^{*}_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)+d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y,Y)

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)+d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y,X)+d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y),

giving the claim.
∎

3.
From random variables to sets

The objective of this section is to prove Proposition
1.2
. Let
C,X,Y
be as in that proposition. We may assume without loss of generality that
X,Y
are independent. For brevity we adopt the notation
k\coloneqq d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
. We need to locate a set
S
satisfying (
1.5
) and (
1.6
). The key lemma is the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a finite non-empty subset
S
of
G
such that

\log|S|\geqslant\mathbf{H}(Y)-2\mathrm{h}\left(1-\frac{2}{C}\right)-4k

(3.1)

and such that

d^{*}_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Z,Y)\leqslant Ck+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{H}(Y)-\mathbf{H}(Z)\bigr)

(3.2)

whenever
Z
is an
S
-valued random variable.

Proof.

As
X,Y
are independent and
k=d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)
, we have

\mathbf{H}(X-Y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(Y)+k,

(3.3)

and hence by (
A.14
) it follows that

\mathbf{H}(X-Y)-\mathbf{H}(Y)\leqslant 2k.

Applying the equality case of (
A.18
), we conclude that

\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(x-Y\|X-Y)\leqslant 2k.

(3.4)

Inspired by this, we define
S
by the formula

S\coloneqq\bigl\{x:p_{X}(x)>0,D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(x-Y\|X-Y)\leqslant Ck\bigr\}.

(3.5)

Denote by

the random variable
A\coloneqq 1_{X\in S}
, and write
p\coloneqq\mathbf{P}(A=1)=\mathbf{P}(X\in S)
.
By Markov’s inequality and (

), it follows that

p=\mathbf{P}(X\in S)\geqslant 1-\frac{2}{C}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.

(3.6)

Now we make some observations. First,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}(X,A)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}(X|A)+\mathbf{H}(A)

\displaystyle=p\mathbf{H}(X|A=1)+(1-p)\mathbf{H}(X|A=0)+\mathrm{h}(p).

(3.7)

Second, since
Y
is independent of
X
and
A
, it follows using (
A.13
) that

\mathbf{H}(X-Y|A=i)\geqslant\mathbf{H}(Y),\mathbf{H}(X|A=i)

for

; therefore

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X-Y)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbf{H}(X-Y|A)

\displaystyle=p\mathbf{H}(X-Y|A=1)+(1-p)\mathbf{H}(X-Y|A=0)

\displaystyle\geqslant p\mathbf{H}(Y)+\frac{1-p}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{H}(Y)+\mathbf{H}(X|A=0)\bigr).

(3.8)

Combining (
3.7
), (
3.8
) with (
3.3
) we conclude after a short computation that

k\geqslant\frac{p}{2}\mathbf{H}(Y)-\frac{p}{2}\mathbf{H}(X|A=1)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{h}(p).

(3.9)

By (
A.1
), we have
\mathbf{H}(X|A=1)\leqslant\log|S|
. Substituting into (
3.9
) and rearranging yields

\log|S|\geqslant\mathbf{H}(Y)-\frac{\mathrm{h}(p)}{p}-\frac{2k}{p}.

Using (
3.6
) (and the monotone decreasing nature of
\mathrm{h}(p)
for

), we obtain (
3.1
).

Now we prove (
3.2
). From (
A.18
) (replacing

by

there) we have

\mathbf{H}(Z-Y)-\mathbf{H}(Y)\leqslant\sum_{z}p_{Z}(z)D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(z-Y\|X-Y).

Note here that the Kullback–Leibler divergence is well-defined and finite. Indeed,
Z
takes values

, and hence by the definition of

we have
p_{X}(z)>0
for such

. Thus if
p_{z-Y}(t)>0
then
p_{X-Y}(t)=\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)p_{x-Y}(t)\geqslant p_{X}(z)p_{z-Y}(t)>0
.

By definition of
S
,
D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(z-Y\|X-Y)\leqslant Ck
for

in the range of

, and the claim (
3.2
) follows.
∎

Now we are ready for the proof of Proposition
1.2
itself.

Proof of Proposition
1.2
.

We begin by establishing (
1.5
). Let
S
be as in Lemma
3.1
. Taking

in (
3.2
), we have

d^{*}_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(U_{S},Y)\leqslant Ck+\frac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathbf{H}(Y)-\log|S|\bigr).

The required bound (

).

Now we prove (

). Let

be any pair of

(ii) and (

) we have

\displaystyle d^{*}_{\operatorname{ent}}(Z,Z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Z,Y)+d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Z^{\prime},Y)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2Ck+\mathbf{H}(Y)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(Z)-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(Z^{\prime})

or equivalently

\mathbf{H}(Z-Z^{\prime})\leqslant 2Ck+\mathbf{H}(Y).

(3.10)

Now we observe that it is possible to choose

supported on

so that

has the uniform distribution on

.
To do this, simply take

to have distribution function

p_{(Z,Z^{\prime})}(s_{1},s_{2})\coloneqq\frac{1}{|S-S|\#\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in S:t_{1}-t_{2}=s_{1}-s_{2}\}}

for

In this case

∎

4.
Entropy distance under homomorphisms

In this section we establish Proposition
1.4
. Let notation be as in the statement of that proposition.

Proof of Proposition
1.4
.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-

\displaystyle X_{2}|Y_{1},Y_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in H}p_{Y_{1}}(y_{1})p_{Y_{2}}(y_{2})\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2}|Y_{1}=y_{1},Y_{2}=y_{2}),

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X_{1}|Y_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1}\in H}p_{Y_{1}}(y_{1})\mathbf{H}(X_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1},y_{2}\in H}p_{Y_{1}}(y_{1})p_{Y_{2}}(y_{2})\mathbf{H}(X_{1}|Y_{1}=y_{1}),

(4.2)

and similarly

(4.3)

Subtracting half of (

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(

\displaystyle X_{1}-X_{2}|Y_{1},Y_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(X_{1}|Y_{1})-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(X_{2}|Y_{2})

Now

5.
Very small entropy doubling

In this section we prove Proposition
1.3
, which states that random variables
X,Y
for which
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)
is small are close to uniform on a subgroup. We first handle the case
X=Y
(in which case we will establish Proposition
1.3
with the improved constant of
6
). Assume henceforth that
X
is a
G
-valued random variable with
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,X)=\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
.

We first observe that a weak version of Proposition
1.3
(which in fact suffices for many applications) follows quickly from Proposition
1.2
. As observed in item (ii) after Proposition
1.2
, we see that there is
S
such that

d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(U_{S},Y)\ll\varepsilon^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}

(5.1)

and

|S-S|\leqslant\biggl(1+O\biggl(\varepsilon^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\biggr)\biggr)|S|<\frac{3}{2}|S|,

(5.2)

where the last inequality holds if
\varepsilon_{0}
is small enough.
A well-known classical observation of Freiman
[
6
]
now implies that
H\coloneqq S-S
is a group. We recall the short proof here.

For any
x,y\in S
,
x-S
and
y-S
both lie in
S-S
and so
|(x-S)\cap(y-S)|>\frac{1}{2}|S|
. That is, there are
>\frac{1}{2}|S|
pairs
(u,v)\in S\times S
such that
x-u=y-v
. For each such pair, we have
x-y=u-v
. Now let
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in S
be any other elements. Similarly, there are
>\frac{1}{2}|S|
pairs
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})\in S\times S
such that
x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}=u^{\prime}-v^{\prime}
. There are
>\frac{1}{2}|S|
values of
v
and
>\frac{1}{2}|S|
values of
u^{\prime}
, and all these values lie in
S
; therefore we must have
v=u^{\prime}
for some pair of these elements. It then follows that
(x-y)+(x^{\prime}-y^{\prime})=(u-v)+(u^{\prime}-v^{\prime})=u-v^{\prime}\in S-S
. Since
x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
were arbitrary, it follows that
S-S
is closed under addition. Since it contains
0
and is closed under taking inverses, it must be a group.

From (
A.16
) (noting that
S
is contained in a single coset of
H
) and (
5.2
) we have

d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(U_{H},U_{S})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|H|}{|S|}\ll\varepsilon^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

Therefore from (
5.1
) and (
1.3
) we have

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(U_{H},Y)\ll\varepsilon^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}.

(5.3)

This is weaker than Proposition
1.3
only in the non-linear dependency on
\varepsilon
, which in many applications is not important.

We will deduce the stronger statement of Proposition
1.3
by bootstrapping this bound. To do this, we require two lemmas which are essentially special cases of Proposition
1.3
itself. First, we consider the case in which
X
is highly concentrated near one point.

Lemma 5.1
.

There is
\delta_{0}>0
such that the following is true.
Suppose that
X
is a
G
-valued random variable and
x_{0}\in G
is a value such that
\mathbf{P}(X=x_{0})\geqslant 1-\delta_{0}
. Then
\mathbf{H}(X)\leqslant 2d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,X)
.

Proof.

By replacing
X
by
X-x_{0}
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that
x_{0}=0
.
Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be independent copies of
X
. Then our task is equivalent to showing that

\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2})\geqslant\frac{3}{2}\mathbf{H}(X).

(5.4)

Write
p\coloneqq\mathbf{P}(X\neq 0)
(thus
p\leqslant\delta_{0}
), and let
A
denote the indicator function of the event that
X_{1},X_{2}\neq 0
; then
\mathbf{P}(A=0)=1-p^{2}
and
\mathbf{P}(A=1)=p^{2}
.
As a consequence, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-

\displaystyle X_{2})\geqslant\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2}|A)

\displaystyle=(1-p^{2})\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2}|A=0)+p^{2}\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2}|X_{1},X_{2}\neq 0)

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-p^{2})\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2}|A=0)+p^{2}\mathbf{H}(X|X\neq 0)

(5.5)

where we used (
A.13
) in the last line.

Now note that for any
z
, if
A=0
and
X_{1}-X_{2}=z
then
(X_{1},X_{2})
can take only two values
(z,0)
and
(0,-z)
if
z\neq 0
, and only one value
(0,0)
if
z=0
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X_{1},

\displaystyle X_{2}|A=0)-\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2}|A=0)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2}|X_{1}-X_{2},A=0)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbf{P}(X_{1}-X_{2}\neq 0|A=0)\log 2=\frac{2p(1-p)}{1-p^{2}}\log 2.

Combining with (
5.5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2})\geqslant(1-p^{2})\mathbf{H}(

\displaystyle X_{1},X_{2}|A=0)

\displaystyle+p^{2}\mathbf{H}(X|X\neq 0)-2p(1-p)\log 2.

(5.6)

We also observe that

\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X)=\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2})=\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2},A)=\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2}|A)+\mathbf{H}(A)

\displaystyle=(1-p^{2})\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2}|A=0)+p^{2}\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2}|A=1)+\mathrm{h}(p^{2})

\displaystyle=(1-p^{2})\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2}|A=0)+2p^{2}\mathbf{H}(X|X\neq 0)+\mathrm{h}(p^{2}).

(5.7)

We further note that, writing
I
for the indicator of
X\neq 0
,

\mathbf{H}(X)=\mathbf{H}(X|I)+\mathbf{H}(I)=p\mathbf{H}(X|X\neq 0)+\mathrm{h}(p).

(5.8)

Taking (
5.7
) minus
p
times (
5.8
) gives

\displaystyle(2-p)\mathbf{H}(X)=(1-p^{2}

\displaystyle)\mathbf{H}(X_{1},X_{2}|A=0)+

\displaystyle+p^{2}\mathbf{H}(X|X\neq 0)+\mathrm{h}(p^{2})-p\mathrm{h}(p).

Combining this with (
5.6
), we obtain

\mathbf{H}(X_{1}-X_{2})\geqslant(2-p)\mathbf{H}(X)+p\mathrm{h}(p)-2p(1-p)\log 2-\mathrm{h}(p^{2}).

(5.9)

Recall that our aim is to demonstrate (
5.4
). To get this from (
5.9
), we first note that expansion to leading order gives

2p(1-p)\log 2+\mathrm{h}(p^{2})\leqslant\left(\frac{1}{2}-p\right)\mathrm{h}(p)

(5.10)

provided
\delta_{0}
is small enough: the LHS here is
\sim 2p\log 2
, whilst the right hand side is
\sim\frac{1}{2}p\log\frac{1}{p}
. (A more careful analysis shows that
\delta_{0}=\frac{1}{20}
is sufficient.) We also have

\mathrm{h}(p)=\mathbf{H}(I)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(X).

(5.11)

The desired bound (
5.4
) then follows immediately from (
5.9
), (
5.10
) and (
5.11
).
∎

Remark
.

The constant
2
in the statement of Lemma
5.1
can be replaced by anything larger than
1
, at the expense of making
\delta_{0}
smaller. This may be shown with very minor modifications of the above argument.

We next consider the case of a random variable supported on
H
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
X
is an
H
-valued random variable with
\mathbf{H}(X)\geqslant\log|H|-\frac{1}{8}
. Then

\log|H|-\mathbf{H}(X)\leqslant 2d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,X).

To prove this we will use the following lemma concerning couplings of almost uniform random variables, which is plausibly of independent interest.
Here, for a probability distribution
p
on a group
H
, we write
\|p-u_{H}\|_{1}:=\sum_{x\in H}\bigl\lvert p(x)-\frac{1}{|H|}\bigr\rvert
for the
\ell^{1}
-distance of
p
from the uniform distribution (or, equivalently, twice the total variation distance of
p
from the uniform distribution).

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\colon H\to\mathbf{R}_{\geqslant 0}
are three probability distributions on
H
such that

\|p_{1}-u_{H}\|_{1}+\|p_{2}-u_{H}\|_{1}+\|p_{3}-u_{H}\|_{1}\leqslant 1.

(5.12)

Then there exists a pair of random variables
(X,Y)
on
H

(
not necessarily independent
)
having the marginal distributions
p_{X}=p_{1}
,
p_{Y}=p_{2}
and
p_{X-Y}=p_{3}
.

Proof.

We wish to show that the triple of distributions
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})\in\mathbf{R}^{H}\times\mathbf{R}^{H}\times\mathbf{R}^{H}
lies in the convex hull of the set
\Sigma:=\{(\delta_{x},\delta_{y},\delta_{x-y}):x,y\in H\}\subseteq\mathbf{R}^{H}\times\mathbf{R}^{H}\times\mathbf{R}^{H}
. Here (as usual)
\delta_{t}(u)=1
if
u=t
, and
\delta_{t}(u)=0
otherwise. By the (finite-dimensional) Hahn–Banach theorem, this is equivalent to showing that there is no hyperplane separating
(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
from
\Sigma
, or in other words whenever
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\colon H\to\mathbf{R}
are functions such that

f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(y)+f_{3}(x-y)\geqslant 0

(5.13)

for all
x,y\in H
, one also has

\sum_{x\in H}f_{1}(x)p_{1}(x)+\sum_{y\in H}f_{2}(y)p_{2}(y)+\sum_{z\in H}f_{3}(z)p_{3}(z)\geqslant 0.

(5.14)

Henceforth, assume (
5.13
). Note that (
5.13
) and (
5.14
) are both unaffected if we shift
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
by constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
summing to zero. Thus we may normalize so that

\min f_{1}=\min f_{2}=\min f_{3}.

If this quantity is non-negative then (
5.14
) is immediate, so we may assume that it is negative. By rescaling we may thus normalize so that

\min f_{1}=\min f_{2}=\min f_{3}=-1.

(5.15)

In particular, there exists
x_{0}\in H
such that
f_{1}(x_{0})=-1
. From (
5.13
) and (

), we conclude that for every

one has

f_{2}(y)+f_{3}(x_{0}-y)\geqslant 1\quad\mbox{and}\quad\min(f_{2}(y),f_{3}(x_{0}-y))\geqslant-1.

This implies that

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle(y)p_{2}(y)+f_{3}(x_{0}-y)p_{3}(x_{0}-y)

\displaystyle\geqslant(f_{2}(y)+f_{3}(x_{0}-y))\min(p_{2}(y),p_{3}(x_{0}-y))

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\min(f_{2}(y),f_{3}(x_{0}-y))|p_{2}(y)-p_{3}(x_{0}-y)|

\displaystyle\geqslant\min(p_{2}(y),p_{3}(x_{0}-y))-|p_{2}(y)-p_{3}(x_{0}-y)|

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{2}(y)+p_{3}(x_{0}-y)}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\bigl\lvert p_{2}(y)-p_{3}(x_{0}-y)\bigr\rvert

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{p_{2}(y)+p_{3}(x_{0}-y)}{2}-\frac{3}{2}\biggl\lvert p_{2}(y)-\frac{1}{|H|}\biggr\rvert-\frac{3}{2}\biggl\lvert p_{3}(x_{0}-y)-\frac{1}{|H|}\biggr\rvert.

Summing over

\sum_{y\in H}f_{2}(y)p_{2}(y)+\sum_{z\in H}f_{3}(z)p_{3}(z)\geqslant 1-\frac{3}{2}\|p_{2}-u_{H}\|_{1}-\frac{3}{2}\|p_{3}-u_{H}\|_{1}.

Cyclically permuting the roles of
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
and
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
and averaging, the desired bound (
5.14
) then follows from (
5.12
).
∎

It follows from Lemma
5.2
that
\mathbf{H}(X_{0})\geqslant\log|H|-2.42\varepsilon
, and hence by (
5.24

\mathbf{H}(X)\geqslant\log|H|-6.62\varepsilon.

Combining this with (
5.17
) and (
5.23
) gives
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,U_{H})\leqslant 5.31\varepsilon\leq 6\varepsilon
, which is the statement of Proposition
1.3
(with a better constant) in the symmetric case

.

Finally, we deduce the general case in which
X
and
Y
may be different. Suppose now that
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,Y)=\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{0}:=\varepsilon_{0}/2
with
\varepsilon_{0}
the constant above. By the triangle inequality,
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,X)\leqslant 2\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}
, and so by the symmetric case of Proposition
1.3
established above we have
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,U_{H})\leqslant 12\varepsilon
for some subgroup
H\leqslant G
. Similarly, we have
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y,U_{H^{\prime}})\leqslant 12\varepsilon
for some subgroup
H^{\prime}\leqslant G
.

It remains to argue that

(5.26)

If

, then

is a subgroup of

and therefore of size at least

is uniform on

6.
Skew dimension and a result of Pálvölgyi and Zhelezov

In this section we give the proof of Theorem
1.6
(and thus Theorem
1.5
).

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a small constant to be specified later, and set
C\coloneqq 2/\varepsilon
. We will prove Theorem
1.6
with this particular value of
C
.

We proceed by induction on
|A||B|
and on
D
. Denote by
\pi\colon\mathbf{Z}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbf{Z}
projection onto the first coordinate. We may assume that at least one of the sets
\pi(A),\pi(B)
is not a singleton (otherwise
D
may be reduced to
D-1
).

Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be uniform random variables on
A,B
respectively, and let
Y_{i}=\pi(X_{i})
.
Applying Proposition
1.4
and rearranging, we obtain

\sum_{i,j}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}\geqslant d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})

(6.1)

where

K\coloneqq\exp\bigl(d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X_{1},X_{2})\bigr)

and

K_{i,j}\coloneqq\exp\bigl(d_{\operatorname{ent}}((X_{1}|Y_{1}=i),(X_{2}|Y_{2}=j))\bigr).

We now divide into two cases, according to whether
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon
or not.

Case 1:

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})\leqslant\varepsilon
.
Let
\varepsilon_{0}
be the constant from Proposition
1.3
, and assume that
\varepsilon\leqslant\min\bigl(\varepsilon_{0},\frac{1}{24}\bigr)
.
By Proposition
1.3
and the fact that
H=\{0\}
is the only finite subgroup of
\mathbf{Z}
, we have

d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y_{1},0),d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y_{2},0)\leqslant 12d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2}).

Since
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(Y_{i},0)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}(Y_{i})
, it follows that

\mathbf{H}(Y_{1})+\mathbf{H}(Y_{2})\leqslant 48d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})\leqslant Cd_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})

(6.2)

by the choice of
C,\varepsilon
.
Inserting this into (
6.1
) and rearranging, we obtain

\sum_{i,j}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)\log\left(\frac{K_{i,j}}{K(p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j))^{1/C}}\right)\leqslant 0.

In particular, there exist
i,j
such that

K_{i,j}\leqslant K(p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j))^{1/C}\leqslant K.

Invoking the induction hypothesis (with
A,B
replaced by
A\cap\pi^{-1}(\{i\})
and
B\cap\pi^{-1}(\{j\})
respectively), we see that there are sets
A^{\prime}\subseteq A,B^{\prime}\subseteq B
with

\dim_{*}A^{\prime},\dim_{*}B^{\prime}\leqslant C\log K_{i,j}\leqslant C\log K

and

\displaystyle|A^{\prime}||B^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\geqslant K_{i,j}^{-C}|A\cap\pi^{-1}(\{i\})||B\cap\pi^{-1}(\{j\})|=K_{i,j}^{-C}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)|A||B|

\displaystyle\geqslant K_{i,j}^{-C}\biggl(\frac{K_{i,j}}{K}\biggr)^{C}|A||B|=K^{-C}|A||B|.

This closes the induction in Case 1.

Case 2:

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y_{1},Y_{2})\geqslant\varepsilon
.

In this case we see from (
6.1
) that

\sum_{i,j}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}\geqslant\varepsilon.

The contribution from those
(i,j)
with
\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
is at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Thus if we set

S\coloneqq\biggl\{(i,j):\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}>\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\biggr\}

then

\sum_{(i,j)\in S}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}\geqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(6.3)

Note in particular that, if
(i,j)\in S
,

K_{i,j}<K.

(6.4)

By the induction hypothesis, for each pair
(i,j)\in S
there are sets
A^{\prime}_{i,j}\subseteq A
,
B^{\prime}_{i,j}\subseteq B
with

|A^{\prime}_{i,j}||B^{\prime}_{i,j}|\geqslant K_{i,j}^{-C}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)|A||B|,

(6.5)

and all with skew-dimension at most

C\log K_{i,j}\leqslant C\biggl(\log K-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\biggr)=C\log K-1.

(6.6)

(Here we used the fact that
C=2/\varepsilon
.)

For each
i\in\mathbf{Z}
, set
A^{\prime}_{i}
to be the largest of the sets
A^{\prime}_{i,j}
,
(i,j)\in S
(or
A^{\prime}_{i}=\emptyset
if
(i,j)\notin S
for every
j
), and similarly for each
j\in\mathbf{Z}
set
B^{\prime}_{j}
to be the largest of the sets
B^{\prime}_{i,j}
,
(i,j)\in S
, breaking ties arbitrarily.
Finally, set
A^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in\mathbf{Z}}A^{\prime}_{i}
and
B^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\in\mathbf{Z}}B^{\prime}_{j}
. By the definition of skew-dimension and the bound (
6.6
), we have
\dim_{*}A^{\prime},\dim_{*}B^{\prime}\leqslant C\log K
.

From the elementary inequality
t\geqslant\log t
for
t\geqslant 1
applied to
t=(K/K_{i,j})^{C}
(noting by (
6.4
) that we do indeed have
t\geqslant 1
), we have

K_{i,j}^{-C}\geqslant CK^{-C}\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}

for any
(i,j)\in S
. From this and (
6.5
), (
6.3
) we have

\displaystyle|A^{\prime}||B^{\prime}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathbf{Z}^{2}}|A^{\prime}_{i}||B^{\prime}_{j}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\sum_{(i,j)\in S}|A^{\prime}_{i,j}||B^{\prime}_{i,j}|

\displaystyle\geqslant|A||B|\sum_{(i,j)\in S}K_{i,j}^{-C}p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)

\displaystyle\geqslant|A||B|\sum_{(i,j)\in S}\biggl(CK^{-C}\log\frac{K}{K_{i,j}}\biggr)p_{Y_{1}}(i)p_{Y_{2}}(j)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{C\varepsilon}{2}K^{-C}|A||B|=K^{-C}|A||B|.

This completes the induction, and the theorem is proved.
∎

7.
Dimension and a result of the second author

We turn now to the question of the dimension (as opposed to the weaker skew-dimension) of subsets of
\mathbf{Z}^{D}
with small doubling. Our aim in this section is to give an entropic proof of Theorem

As in

Lemma 7.1

Let

We assume

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(X-2Y)

Lemma 7.2

Let

Appendix A
Basic facts about entropy

In this section we gather together basic facts about entropy, referring the reader to other sources (e.g.,
[
22
, Appendix A]
or
[
7
]
) for the (standard, and mostly easy) proofs.

We begin with the most basic results.

Basic entropy results

If
X
is an
S
-valued random variable for some finite
S
, the Shannon entropy is defined as

\mathbf{H}(X)\coloneqq\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)},

where
x
is understood to range over
S
and
3
3

3

We use the natural logarithm in this paper, but one could easily work with other bases of the logarithm if desired.
we adopt the convention that any term involving a factor of
p_{X}(x)
vanishes when
p_{X}(x)=0
. From Jensen’s inequality we have

\mathbf{H}(X)\leqslant\log|S|.

(A.1)

Also,

\mathbf{H}(X)=\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)\log\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)}\geqslant\min_{x:p_{X}(x)>0}\log\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)},

and therefore

\max_{x}p_{X}(x)\geqslant e^{-\mathbf{H}(X)}.

(A.2)

If
X,Y
are random variables then

\mathbf{H}(X,Y)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(X)+\mathbf{H}(Y),

(A.3)

and equality occurs if
X,Y
are independent. At the other end of the spectrum, if
X
determines
Y
then
\mathbf{H}(X,Y)=\mathbf{H}(X)
. See for instance
[
7
, Lemma 2.3.2]
.

Conditional entropy

We define

\mathbf{H}(X|Y)=\sum_{y}p_{Y}(y)\mathbf{H}(X|Y=y).

Then we have the
chain rule

\mathbf{H}(X,Y)=\mathbf{H}(X|Y)+\mathbf{H}(Y).

If
Y=f(X)
for some function
f
then, since
\mathbf{H}(X,Y)=\mathbf{H}(X)
, it follows that

\mathbf{H}(f(X))\leqslant\mathbf{H}(X).

(A.4)

Submodularity

For any three random variables
A,B,C
we have the submodularity inequality

\mathbf{H}(A,B,C)+\mathbf{H}(C)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(A,C)+\mathbf{H}(B,C)

(A.5)

(which is equivalent to the non-negativity of the conditional mutual information
\mathbf{I}(A:B|C)
); see for instance
[
7
, Lemma 2.5.5]
.

An equivalent and useful way to write the submodularity inequality is

\mathbf{H}(A|B,C)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(A|C).

(A.6)

Note also that, if
B
determines
C
, then
\mathbf{H}(A,B,C)=\mathbf{H}(A,B)
and
\mathbf{H}(B,C)=\mathbf{H}(B)
, and submodularity implies that

\mathbf{H}(A|B)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(A|C).

(A.7)

Kullback–Leibler Divergence.

Suppose that
X,Y
are random variables with distribution functions
\mu_{X},\mu_{Y}
respectively. Then we define

D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(X\|Y)\coloneqq\sum_{t}\mu_{X}(t)\log\left(\frac{\mu_{X}(t)}{\mu_{Y}(t)}\right).

It is conventional to define the summand here to be
0
if
\mu_{X}(t)=0
and
\infty
if
\mu_{Y}(t)=0
but
\mu_{X}(t)\neq 0
; in practice, we will avoid the latter situation.

It is convenient to relate this to the
cross-entropy

\mathbf{H}(X:Y)\coloneqq\sum_{t}\mu_{X}(t)\log\frac{1}{\mu_{Y}(t)}

(A.8)

(where the same conventions are in force). Thus

D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(X\|Y)=\mathbf{H}(X:Y)-\mathbf{H}(X).

(A.9)

In particular, if
X
takes values in a finite set
S
, then
\mathbf{H}(X:U_{S})=\log|S|
and thus

D_{\operatorname{KL}}(X\|U_{S})=\log|S|-\mathbf{H}(X).

(A.10)

Note that
\mathbf{H}(X:Y)
is
not
at all the same thing as
\mathbf{H}(X,Y)
(or
\mathbf{H}(X|Y)
). Indeed, the former depends only on the distribution functions of
X,Y
and not in any way on their dependence, and it is also asymmetric in that in general
\mathbf{H}(X:Y)\neq\mathbf{H}(Y:X)
.
From a standard application of Jensen’s inequality we obtain
Gibbs’ inequality

D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(X\|Y)\geqslant 0

(A.11)

(see e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem 2.3.1]
);
we also have the well known
Pinsker’s inequality

\sum_{t}|p_{X}(t)-p_{Y}(t)|\leqslant\sqrt{2D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(X\|Y)},

(A.12)

see e.g.,
[
7
, Lemma 5.2.8]
.

Now we turn to some simple results about
G
-valued random variables, where
G
is abelian, and we assume all random variables to have finite support. The reader may wish to recall the definitions of
d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}
and
d^{*}_{{\operatorname{ent}}}
, given at (
1.2
) and (
1.4
) respectively.

First, if
X,Y
are independent such variables then

\mathbf{H}(X-Y)\geqslant\mathbf{H}(X-Y|Y)=\mathbf{H}(X).

(A.13)

From this we see that

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,Y)=d_{\operatorname{ent}}(Y,X)\geqslant\frac{|\mathbf{H}(X)-\mathbf{H}(Y)|}{2}\geqslant 0.

(A.14)

Let
X
be a
G
-valued random variable, and let
H
be a finite subgroup of
G
. Denote by
\pi:G\rightarrow G/H
the quotient map.
Let
U_{H}
be a uniform random variable on
H
, independent of
X
. Then we have

\mathbf{H}(X+U_{H})=\mathbf{H}(\pi(X))+\mathbf{H}(U_{H})=\mathbf{H}(\pi(X))+\log|H|.

(A.15)

It follows that

d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,U_{H})=\mathbf{H}(\pi(X))+\frac{1}{2}(\log|H|-\mathbf{H}(X)).

(A.16)

From this and (
A.14
) we have

\mathbf{H}(\pi(X))\leqslant 2d_{{\operatorname{ent}}}(X,U_{H}).

(A.17)

Also, from Lemma
1.1
and
d_{\operatorname{ent}}(U_{H},U_{H})=0
we observe that

d_{\operatorname{ent}}^{*}(X,U_{H})=d_{\operatorname{ent}}(X,U_{H}).

Finally, if
X,Y,Z
are
G
-valued random variables (not necessarily independent), we observe from the Gibbs inequality (
A.11
) the useful bound

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(Z-Y)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{H}(Y)\leqslant\mathbf{H}(Z-Y:X)-\mathbf{H}(Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}p_{Z}(z)\bigl(\mathbf{H}(z-Y:X)-\mathbf{H}(z-Y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z}p_{Z}(z)D_{{\operatorname{KL}}}(z-Y\|X)

(A.18)

where we have used the permutation-invariance of Shannon entropy to observe that
\mathbf{H}(z-Y)=\mathbf{H}(Y)
, as well as the fact that
p_{Z-Y}(t)=\sum_{z}p_{Z}(z)p_{z-Y}(t)
. Note that we in fact have equality when
X=Z-Y
.

Appendix B
Energy, entropy and doubling

In this section we prove the inequalities (
1.1
). Recall the statement, which is that

\frac{|A|^{3}}{\mathrm{E}[A]}\leqslant\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[A]\leqslant\sigma[A].

(B.1)

Proof.

Denote
X\coloneqq U_{A}+U^{\prime}_{A}
to be the sum of two independent uniform random variables on
A
. The right-hand inequality is immediate from the inequality
\mathbf{H}(X)\leqslant\log|A+A|
, which is a special case of Jensen’s inequality. As for the left-hand inequality, observe that

p_{X}(x)\coloneqq\frac{|A\cap(x-A)|}{|A|^{2}}.

and then by the weighted AM–GM inequality,

e^{-\mathbf{H}(X)}=\prod_{x}p_{X}(x)^{p_{X}(x)}\leqslant\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{E}[A]}{|A|^{4}}.

The result follows immediately.∎

The above argument can be reformulated in terms of the
Rényi entropies

\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(X)
, defined for
\alpha\neq 1
by

\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(X)\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\log\left(\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)^{\alpha}\right)

and extended by continuity to
\alpha=1
by setting
\mathbf{H}_{1}(X)\coloneqq\mathbf{H}(X)
. A brief calculation reveals the identities

\displaystyle\exp(\mathbf{H}_{0}(X))

\displaystyle=|A+A|

\displaystyle\exp(\mathbf{H}_{1}(X))

\displaystyle=\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[A]|A|

\displaystyle\exp(\mathbf{H}_{2}(X))

\displaystyle=\frac{|A|^{4}}{\mathrm{E}[A]},

and the claim now follows from the well-known fact that the Rényi entropy
\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}(X)
is non-increasing in
\alpha
.

We conclude with a simple example showing that both inequalities in (
B.1
) can be far from tight. Suppose that
n=2m
is even and
A=H\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}
, with
H
a subgroup of size
m
and
x_{1},\dots,x_{m}
highly dissociated with respect to
H
, for instance with
x_{i}+x_{j}-x_{k}-x_{l}\in H
only if
\{i,j\}=\{k,l\}
. Then we have
|A|^{3}/\mathrm{E}[A]=\frac{1}{16}+o(1)
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Turning to
\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[A]
, we of course have
\mathbf{H}(U_{A})=\log n
. The variable
U_{A}+U^{\prime}_{A}
may be conditioned to subvariables which are, respectively, uniformly distributed on
H
, on the set
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}(x_{i}+H)
, and on the multiset
\bigcup_{i,j=1}^{m}\{x_{i}+x_{j}\}
, with the conditioning probabilities being
\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4}
. One therefore computes that
\mathbf{H}(U_{A}+U^{\prime}_{A})=(\frac{7}{4}+o(1))\log n
and so
\sigma_{{\operatorname{ent}}}[A]=n^{3/4+o(1)}
. Finally,
\sigma[A]=(\frac{3}{4}+o(1))n
.
