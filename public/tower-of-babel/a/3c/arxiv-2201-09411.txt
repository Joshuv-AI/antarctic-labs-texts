Stochastic asymptotical regularization for linear inverse problems
Ye Zhang, Chuchu Chen

Abstract
We introduce Stochastic Asymptotical Regularization (SAR) methods for the uncertainty quantification of the stable approximate solution of ill-posed linear-operator equations, which are deterministic models for numerous inverse problems in science and engineering. We prove the regularizing properties of SAR with regard to mean-square convergence. We also show that SAR is an optimal-order regularization method for linear ill-posed problems provided that the terminating time of SAR is chosen according to the smoothness of the solution. This result is proven for both a priori and a posteriori stopping rules under general range-type source conditions. Furthermore, some converse results of SAR are verified. Two iterative schemes are developed for the numerical realization of SAR, and the convergence analyses of these two numerical schemes are also provided. A toy example and a real-world problem of biosensor tomography are studied to show the accuracy and the advantages of SAR: compared with the conventional deterministic regularization approaches for deterministic inverse problems, SAR can provide the uncertainty quantification of the quantity of interest, which can in turn be used to reveal and explicate the hidden information about real-world problems, usually obscured by the incomplete mathematical modeling and the ascendence of complex-structured noise.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in solving deterministic linear inverse problems of the form

Ax=y,

(1)

where
A
is a compact linear operator acting between two infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. For simplicity, we use
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and
\|\cdot\|
to denote the inner products and norms, respectively, for both
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. Suppose that, instead of the exact data
y
, we are given noisy measurement
y^{\delta}\in\mathcal{Y}
, which also obeys the deterministic noise model with noise level
\delta>0
:

\|y^{\delta}-y\|\leq\delta.

(2)

In addition, we assume that
\mathcal{R}(A)
is an infinite dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{Y}
. Then,
since
A
is compact and
{\rm dim}(\mathcal{R}(A))=\infty
, we have
\mathcal{R}(A)\neq\overline{\mathcal{R}(A)}
, and the problem (
1
) is ill-posed and of type II, as defined by Nashed
[
21
]
. Hence, to obtain the stable approximate solution of problem (
1
)-(
2
), an appropriate regularization method should be designed. The formulation (
1
)-(
2
) is a typical deterministic mathematical model for many linear inverse problems with applications in the natural sciences if it is supposed that unknown physical characteristics
x
cannot be measured directly. From experiments, it is possible to obtain only noisy data
y^{\delta}
connected with
x
with the help of a forward mathematical model
A
. Some important areas in which (
1
) can be applied are inverse source problems in partial differential equations and the Fredholm integral equation of the first kind, which are the basis of fundamental mathematical models in geophysics, imaging, and many other fields.

In classical regularization theory, the deterministic regularization methods are usually designed for deterministic inverse problems (
1
). These deterministic regularization methods can be roughly classified into two categories: variational regularization methods and iterative regularization methods. Tikhonov regularization is probably the most prominent variational regularization method, while the Landweber iteration is certainly the most popular iterative regularization approach. The development of our new regularization approach in this paper was motivated by the latter, as, from the viewpoint of computation, the iterative approach seems more attractive, especially for large-scale problems. In view of this, we begin with the Landweber iteration for (
1
), which is given by

\displaystyle x^{\delta}_{k+1}=x^{\delta}_{k}+\Delta tA^{*}(y^{\delta}-Ax^{\delta}_{k}),\quad x^{\delta}_{0}=x_{0},\quad\Delta t\in(0,2/\|A\|^{2}),

(3)

where
A^{*}
denotes the adjoint operator of
A
. It is well known that the scheme (
3
) is turned into regularization algorithms, i.e.
x^{\delta}_{k^{*}}\to x^{\dagger}
as
\delta\to 0
, by stopping the iteration after an adequate number,
k^{*}=k^{*}(\delta)
, of steps. Here and later on,
x^{\dagger}
denotes the
x_{0}
-minimum norm solution of the operator equation (
1
), which is assumed solvable with exact data.

The continuous analog to (
3
) as
\Delta t
tends to zero is known as
asymptotic regularization
or
Showalter’s method
(see, e.g.,
[
25
,
26
]
). It is written as a first-order evolution equation of the form

\dot{x}^{\delta}(t)=A^{*}(y^{\delta}-Ax^{\delta}(t))

(4)

with some initial condition, where an artificial scalar time
t
is introduced. It has been shown that, by using Runge-Kutta integrators, all the properties of asymptotic regularization (
4
) are carried over to its numerical realization
[
22
,
31
]
. This continuous framework has recently received much attention in the field of computational mathematics. For instance, in the field of inverse problems, the authors in
[
19
]
studied a modified evolution equation, (
4
), for linear inverse problems (
1
) in the presence of white noise. In particular, they connected this formulation, i.e. (
4
), to some classical methods from data assimilation, namely the Kalman-Bucy filter and 3DVAR. Moving in a different direction, the authors in
[
29
,
8
,
28
,
2
]
extended (
4
) to second-order and fractional-order gradient flows. They proved that the developed high-order flows are accelerated optimal regularization methods, i.e. the optimal convergence rates can be obtained with approximately the square root of the iteration number that is needed for the first-order flow (
4
). Besides the community of inverse problems, this idea has also recently been adopted in the field of machine learning for the theoretical understanding of machine learning: the continuous dynamical flows can be used to recover existing machine-learning models and construct new ones (see
[
5
,
6
]
). Building on this idea, in this paper we are interested in the following stochastic differential equation, called the
Stochastic Asymptotical Regularization
(SAR),

dx^{\delta}=A^{*}(y^{\delta}-Ax^{\delta})dt+f(t)dB_{t},\quad x^{\delta}(0)=x_{0},

(5)

where the initial point
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
is non-random, the auxiliary function
f(t)\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
will be defined later, and
B_{t}
is an
{\mathcal{X}}
-valued
Q
-Wiener process, i.e.

B_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\ \sqrt{q_{j}}u_{j}\beta_{j}(t),

(6)

where
\{u_{j}\}
is the orthogonal basis of
N(A)^{\bot}\subset{\mathcal{X}}
,
\{q_{j}\}
denotes the eigenvalues of the covariance operator
Q
under the orthorgomal basis
\{u_{j}\}
, and
\{\beta_{j}\}
is a family of independent
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued Brownian motions. Throughout this paper, we assume that
Q
commutes with
A^{*}A
and
{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})<\infty
. We also note that, compared with conventional regularization methods for deterministic inverse problems (
1
), SAR offers a stochastic solution, which can provide the uncertainty quantification for the solution of the considered inverse problem.

It should be noted that the formulation (
5
) is different from the conventional
Stochastic Gradient Descent
(SGD) method
[
23
]
, which employs an unbiased estimator of the full gradient computed from one single randomly selected data point at each iteration. Recently, SGD and its variants have been established as the workhorses behind many challenging training tasks in deep learning
[
3
]
. The regularizing properties of SGD for linear inverse problems with an
a priori
and an
a posteriori
stopping rule can be found in
[
13
]
and
[
11
]
, respectively. An extended error estimation of SGD, taking into account the discretization levels, the decay of the step-size, and the general source conditions, can be found in
[
18
]
. SGD for non-linear ill-posed problems was investigated in
[
14
]
.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: in Section
2
, we perform the convergence analysis of SAR. Section
3
is devoted to the numerical realization and the convergence analysis of the corresponding numerical scheme of SAR. An abstract mathematical example and a real-world problem of biosensor tomography are presented in Section
4
. Finally, concluding remarks are made in Section
5
.

2
Convergence analysis of SAR

This section presents some basis properties of our new regularization approach based on the stochastic partial differential equation – SAR in (
5
), including the regularization property, convergence rates under general source conditions, some converse results, and the convergence-rate results of the best worst-case mean-square error.

2.1
Preliminary results of SAR

We start with the well-posedness of the dynamical flow (
5
).

Proposition 1
.

For any
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, the stochastic differential equation (
5
) has a unique mild solution
x^{\delta}(t)\in\mathcal{X}
, given by

x^{\delta}(t)=e^{-A^{*}At}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}A^{*}y^{\delta}ds+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}f(s)dB_{s}.

(7)

The random variable
x^{\delta}(t)
is Gaussian on
\mathcal{X}
with mean

\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)=e^{-A^{*}At}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}A^{*}y^{\delta}ds

(8)

and variance operator given by

\textup{Var}(x^{\delta}(t))=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}Qe^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}[f(s)]^{2}ds.

(9)

Proof.

It is sufficient to deal with the stochastic term.
From the Itô isometry, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\Big\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}f(s)dB(s)\Big\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}f(s)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}ds

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\infty}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}ds,

where
HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})
denotes the space of Hilbert-Schmidt operators from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{X}
endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}=\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\|\cdot u_{j}\|^{2}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}
.
Note that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}ds

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})\int_{0}^{t}\|(A^{*}A)^{\frac{1}{2}}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})\int_{0}^{t}\sup_{\lambda>0}\big\{\lambda e^{-2\lambda(t-s)}\big\}ds

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})<\infty.

Therefore, according to
[
4
, Theorem 5.4]
, the proposition holds true.
∎

We now introduce some definitions that will be frequently used for convergence analysis in this paper.

Definition 1
.

A real function
\varphi:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is called an index function if it is continuous and strictly increasing, and satisfies the condition
\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\varphi(\lambda)=0
. Let
\mathcal{I}
denote the set of all index functions.

Definition 2
.

An index function
\varphi
is called
g
-subhomogeneous if there exists a decreasing function
g
such that
\varphi(\gamma\lambda)\leq g(\gamma)\varphi(\lambda)
for all
\gamma>0,\ \lambda>0
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{g}
denote the set of all
g
-subhomogeneous index functions.

In order to study the optimality results of convergence rates for SAR (
5
), we need to consider the following classes of decreasing functions:

\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}:=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{I}:\forall\lambda\in(0,\|A\|^{2}],~\varphi(\lambda)/\varphi(t^{-1})\leq C_{\sigma}e^{\lambda t\sigma}\right\},

(10)

where positive numbers
C_{\sigma}
and
\sigma
are independent on
\lambda
and
t
.

\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varphi\in\mathcal{I}_{g}:~\text{There exists a monotonically decreasing, integrable function~}\zeta\\
\qquad\qquad\text{~such that~}e^{2\lambda t}\zeta\left(\frac{\varphi(\lambda)}{\varphi(t^{-1})}\right)\geq 1\text{~for~}\lambda t>1.\end{array}\right\}.

(11)

In order to investigate the properties of sets
\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
and
\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
, we introduce the following concept, which is generalized from
[
20
, Definition 2]
:

Definition 3
.

A function
\varphi
is said to be
covered
by another function
\psi
if there are
\underline{c},\bar{T}>0
such that, for all
t\geq\bar{T}
,

\underline{c}\,\frac{\psi(t^{-1})}{\varphi(t^{-1})}\leq\min\limits_{t^{-1}\leq\lambda\leq\|A\|^{2}}\frac{\psi(\lambda)}{\varphi(\lambda)}.

(12)

The following assertions are straightforward:

Proposition 2
.

(i)
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
if
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
and
\varphi
is covered by
\psi
.

(ii)
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
if
\psi\in\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
and
\varphi
is covered by
\psi
.

(iii) If the quotient function
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\psi(\lambda)}{\varphi(\lambda)}
is increasing for
0<\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}
and some
\bar{\lambda}>0
,
\varphi
is covered by
\psi
. If, in particular,
\varphi(\lambda)\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
is concave for
0<\lambda\leq\bar{\lambda}
,
\varphi
is covered by
\psi(\lambda)=\lambda
.

Now, we study the elements in the sets
\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
and
\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
, which are related to the range-type source conditions in the regularization theory of inverse problems:

x_{0}-x^{\dagger}=\varphi(A^{*}A)v,\quad\|v\|\leq\rho,\quad\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}.

(13)

In this paper, we are mainly interested in the following two examples of source conditions:

Example 1
.

(i) Hölder-type source conditions
\varphi_{p}
(
p>0
):
\varphi_{p}(\lambda)=\lambda^{p}
.

(ii) Logarithmic source conditions
\varphi_{\mu}
(
\mu>0
):

\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\log^{-\mu}(1/\lambda)\qquad\textrm{~for}\quad 0<\lambda\leq e^{-\mu-1},\\
\mbox{arbitrarily extended as index function for}\;\;\lambda>e^{-\mu-1}.\end{array}\right.

(14)

The relationship between source condition functions
\varphi_{p}
,
\varphi_{\mu}
and the classes
\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
and
\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
is investigated in the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

(i)
\varphi_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
with
C_{\sigma}=p^{p}/(e^{p}\sigma^{p})
, arbitrary positive number
\sigma
,
g(\lambda)=\lambda
and
\zeta(\lambda)=(\ln\lambda/2)^{-p}
.

(ii)
\varphi_{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
with arbitrary positive number
\sigma
.

Proof.

(i) From

\sup_{t>0}e^{-\lambda\sigma t}t^{p}=_{t=p/(\lambda\sigma)}p^{p}/(e^{p}\sigma^{p}\lambda^{p})

we conclude that, for any
\sigma>0
,
\varphi_{p}\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
with
C_{\sigma}=p^{p}/(e^{p}\sigma^{p})
. The relation
\varphi_{p}\in\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
holds, with
g(\lambda)=\lambda
and
\zeta(\lambda)=e(-2\lambda^{1/p})
.

(ii) Note that for arbitrary
\mu>0
the index function
\varphi_{\mu}(\lambda)
is concave for all
0<\lambda\leq e^{-\mu-1}
, and hence, because of (iii) of Proposition
2
, is covered by
\varphi^{1}(\lambda)=\lambda
. Consequently,
\varphi_{\mu}\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}\cap\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
.
∎

2.2
Regularization property of SAR

In this subsection, we perform the convergence analysis of the method (
5
) with regard to regularization theory. Throughout this paper, we assume
x_{0}\in N(A)^{\bot}
. Let
\{\lambda_{j};u_{j},v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be the well-defined singular system for the compact linear operator
A
, i.e. we have
Au_{j}=\lambda_{j}v_{j}
and
A^{*}v_{j}=\lambda_{j}u_{j}
with ordered singular values
\|A\|=\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdot\cdot\cdot\geq\lambda_{j}\geq\lambda_{j+1}\geq\cdot\cdot\cdot\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Since the eigenelement
\{u_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
forms an orthogonal basis in
N(A)^{\bot}\subset\mathcal{X}
, according to the construction of the approximate solution
x^{\delta}(t)
by SAR in (
5
), it has the decomposition
x^{\delta}(t)=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}(t)u_{j}
. Hence, by using

\quad\langle dx^{\delta},u_{j}\rangle=\langle A^{*}(y^{\delta}-Ax^{\delta})dt,u_{j}\rangle+\langle f(t)dB_{t},u_{j}\rangle,~j=1,2,\cdots,

(15)

we obtain the stochastic differential equations for coefficients
\{\xi_{j}(t)\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
:

d\xi_{j}(t)=\left(\lambda_{j}\langle y^{\delta},v_{j}\rangle-\lambda^{2}_{j}\xi_{j}(t)\right)dt+f(t)\sqrt{q_{j}}d\beta_{j}(t),~\xi_{j}(0)=\langle x_{0},u_{j}\rangle,~j=1,2,\cdots.

(16)

Proposition 3
.

The stochastic differential equation (
16
) has a unique solution,

\displaystyle\xi_{j}(t)=e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}t}\langle x_{0},u_{j}\rangle+\frac{1-e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}t}}{\lambda_{j}}\langle y^{\delta},v_{j}\rangle+\int^{t}_{0}\sqrt{q_{j}}e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}(t-s)}f(s)d\beta_{j}(s),

where the stochastic integral
\int^{t}_{0}\sqrt{q_{j}}e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}(t-s)}f(s)d\beta_{j}(s)
is Gaussian with distribution

\mathcal{N}\left(0,\int^{t}_{0}q_{j}e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}(t-s)}[f(s)]^{2}ds\right).

Moreover,
\xi_{j}(t)
is also Gaussian, with mean

\mathbb{E}\xi_{j}(t)=e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}t}\langle x_{0},u_{j}\rangle+\frac{1-e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}t}}{\lambda_{j}}\langle y^{\delta},v_{j}\rangle

(17)

and variance

\textup{Var}\left(\xi_{j}(t)\right)=\int^{t}_{0}q_{j}e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}(t-s)}[f(s)]^{2}ds.

(18)

Consequently, if
f\in\mathcal{I}
,
\xi_{j}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\langle y^{\delta},v_{j}\rangle}{\lambda_{j}},0\right)
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness of (
16
) is a standard result for linear stochastic differential equations; see, e.g.,
[
4
]
. (
17
) is from the mean-zero property of stochastic integrals, and (
18
) is from the Itô isometry.
∎

From Proposition
3
and the decomposition
x^{\delta}(t)=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}(t)u_{j}
we obtain the explicit formula for the mild solution of (
5
):

\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{\delta}(t)=\sum\limits_{j}e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}t}\langle x_{0},u_{j}\rangle u_{j}+\sum\limits_{j}\frac{1-e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}t}}{\lambda_{j}}\langle y^{\delta},v_{j}\rangle u_{j}+\sum\limits_{j}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\lambda^{2}_{j}(t-s)}f(s)\langle dB_{s},u_{j}\rangle u_{j},\\
=:(1-A^{*}Ag(t,A^{*}A))x_{0}+g(t,A^{*}A)A^{*}y^{\delta}+\int^{t}_{0}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}f(s)dB_{s},\end{array}

(19)

where (we identify
\lambda^{2}_{j}
as
\lambda
)

\displaystyle g(t,\lambda)=\frac{1-e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda}.

(20)

Theorem 1
.

Let
x^{\delta}(t)
be the stochastic dynamic solution of (
5
) with
f(t)\in\mathcal{I}
. Then, if the terminating time
t^{*}=t^{*}(\delta,y^{\delta})
is chosen so that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}t^{*}=\infty\textrm{~and~}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\delta\cdot t^{*}=0,

(21)

the
x^{\delta}(t^{*})
converges to
x^{\dagger}
in the sense of mean square, i.e.
\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\to 0
, as
\delta\to 0
.

Proof.

According to the bias-variance decomposition for the mean-squared error, namely,

\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)\|^{2},

(22)

it is sufficient to find estimates for both bias error
\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}
and variance error
\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)\|^{2}
, respectively.

The bias error is deterministic, and thus can be estimated by standard techniques in regularization theory, i.e. we have (let
r(t,\lambda)=1-\lambda g(t,\lambda)=e^{-\lambda t}
)

\begin{array}[]{ll}\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|=\|r(t,A^{*}A)(x_{0}-x^{\dagger})+g(t,A^{*}A)A^{*}(y^{\delta}-y)\|\\
\qquad\leq\|e^{-tA^{*}A}(x_{0}-x^{\dagger})\|+\vartheta t^{1/2}\delta,\end{array}

(23)

where we have used the inequalities

\|g(t,A^{*}A)A^{*}(y^{\delta}-y)\|\leq\sup_{\lambda\in(0,\|A\|^{2})}\sqrt{\lambda}g(t,\lambda)\|y^{\delta}-y\|\leq\delta\sup_{\lambda\in(0,\|A\|^{2})}\frac{1-e^{-\lambda t}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\leq\vartheta t^{1/2}\delta,

(24)

where
\vartheta=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbf{R}_{+}}\sqrt{\lambda}(\lambda-e^{-\lambda})\approx 0.6382

[
26
]
. Hence, with the choice of terminating time in (
21
), we conclude that

\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|\to 0\textrm{~as~}\delta\to 0.

(25)

On the other hand, according to L’Hospital’s rule, we get, for any
\lambda>0
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}[f(s)]^{2}ds}{\int^{t}_{0}e^{\lambda s}ds}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{e^{\lambda t}[f(t)]^{2}}{e^{\lambda t}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}[f(t)]^{2}.

(26)

Hence,
T
exists such that, for all
t\geq T
,

\frac{1}{2}[f(t)]^{2}\int^{t}_{0}e^{\lambda s}ds\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}[f(s)]^{2}ds\leq 2[f(t)]^{2}\int^{t}_{0}e^{\lambda s}ds.

(27)

By using the Itô isometry, we derive, together with the assumption
f(t)\in\mathcal{I}
, the assumption
tr(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})<\infty
and the inequality (
27
) such that, for
t\geq T
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left\|\int^{t}_{0}e^{-A^{*}A(t-s)}f(s)dB_{s}\right\|^{2}=\sum\limits^{\infty}\limits_{j=1}\int^{t}_{0}q_{j}e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}(t-s)}[f(s)]^{2}ds\\
\qquad=\sum\limits^{\infty}\limits_{j=1}q_{j}e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}t}\int^{t}_{0}e^{2\lambda^{2}_{j}s}[f(s)]^{2}ds\leq 2\sum\limits^{\infty}\limits_{j=1}q_{j}e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}t}[f(t)]^{2}\int^{t}_{0}e^{2\lambda^{2}_{j}s}ds\\
\qquad=[f(t)]^{2}\sum\limits^{\infty}\limits_{j=1}\frac{q_{j}}{\lambda^{2}_{j}}\left(1-e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}t}\right)\leq tr(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})[f(t)]^{2}\to 0\end{array}

(28)

as
t\to\infty
. We combine (
25
) and (
28
) to obtain the convergence of the full error of our regularization method (
5
).
∎

2.3
Convergence rates with noisy data under
a priori
and
a posteriori
stopping rules

According to the standard argument in regularization theory
[
24
]
, under the general assumptions of the previous sections, the rate of convergence of mean-square error
\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t(\delta))-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\to 0
as
\delta\to 0
can be arbitrarily slow for solutions
x^{\dagger}
which are not smooth enough. In order to prove convergence rates, some smoothness assumptions imposed on the exact solution must be employed. This subsection focuses on the range-type source conditions (
13
), while in the next subsection we adopt the variational inequalities, which are more natural in the study of converse results. Therefore, we first consider the convergence-rate results under an
a priori
stopping rule.

Theorem 2
.

Let
x^{\delta}(t)
be the stochastic dynamic solution of (
5
) with
f(t)=\mathcal{O}(\varphi(1/t))
. Then, under the source condition (
13
), if the terminating time is chosen as
t^{*}=\Theta^{-1}(\delta)
with
\Theta(t)=t^{-1/2}\varphi(t^{-1})
, we have the convergence rate

\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t^{*})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}([\varphi([\Theta^{-1}(\delta)]^{-1})]^{2})\text{~as~}\delta\to 0.

Consequently, if
\varphi=\varphi_{p}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t^{*})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\frac{4p}{2p+1}})
; if
\varphi=\varphi_{\mu}
, we have
\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t^{*})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\log^{-2\mu}(\delta^{-1}))
.

Proof.

According to the proof of Theorem
1
(see (
23
) and (
28
)), we find, together with the assumption
f(t)=\mathcal{O}(\varphi(1/t))
and the relation
t^{*}(\delta)\to\infty
as
\delta\to 0

Remark 1

Clearly,

Proposition 4

If

Proof.

From the explicit formula of

Proof.

2.4
Convergence rates with exact data and converse results

LetDefine the
spectral tail
be defined by

\omega(\lambda)=\|E_{[0,\lambda]}(x_{0}-x^{\dagger})\|^{2},

(50)

where the mapping
\Theta\mapsto E_{\Theta}
means the spectral measure of
A^{*}A
on Borel set

\|\mathbb{E}x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\int^{\|A\|^{2}}_{0}e^{-\lambda t}d\omega(\lambda),

(51)

where
x(t)
represents the regularized stochastic solution
x(t)
of (
5
) with exact data

. In this subsection, we establish an equivalent relation between the spectral tail

and the convergence rate of

to the
x_{0}
-minimum norm solution

Theorem 4
.

Let
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
with

. Then, the following two statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a constant

with

(52)

(ii)

There exists a constant

with

\omega(\lambda)\leq C_{4}\varphi(\lambda)\quad\text{for all }\lambda>0.

(53)

Moreover, if
f(t)=C_{\varphi}\sqrt{\varphi(1/t)}
(

is a positive number independent of
t
), every one of the above statements is also equivalent to the following one:

(iii)

There exists a constant
C_{5}>0
with

\mathbb{E}\left\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\right\|^{2}\leq C_{5}\varphi(1/t)\quad\text{for all }t>0.

(54)

Proof.

According to the definitions of
x(t)
and
\omega(\lambda)
and the relation (
51
), for all
t>0
,

\omega(1/t)=\int_{0}^{1/t}\mathrm{d}\omega(\lambda)\leq e\int_{0}^{1/t}e^{-\lambda t}d\omega(\lambda)\leq e\left\|\mathbb{E}x(t)-x^{\dagger}\right\|^{2}.

(55)

Let fFirst, let (
52
) hold. Then, (
53
) holds with
C_{4}=eC_{3}
, through theby combining of (
52
) and (
55
).

Conversely, let (
53
) hold. Since
\left\|\mathbb{E}x(t)-x^{\dagger}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{0}-x^{\dagger}\right\|^{2}
(which follows from the representation (
51
)), it is sufficientenough to check the condition (
52
) for all

.

Integrating the right- hand side by parts in (

), we obtain that

\left\|\mathbb{E}x(t)-x^{\dagger}\right\|^{2}=e^{-\|A\|^{2}t}\omega\left(\|A\|^{2}\right)+t\int_{0}^{\|A\|^{2}}\omega(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t}d\lambda

Note that

t\int_{0}^{1/t}\omega(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t}d\lambda\leq t\omega(1/t)\int_{0}^{1/t}e^{-\lambda t}d\lambda=(1-e^{-1})\omega(1/t)\leq C_{4}(1-e^{-1})\varphi(1/t),

(57)

where we used the assumption (
53
) and the monotonic increasing of
\omega
. On the other hand, the assumptions
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{C_{\sigma}}
and (
53
) imply that

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\int_{1/t}^{\|A\|^{2}}\omega(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t}d\lambda\leq C_{4}t\int_{1/t}^{\|A\|^{2}}\varphi(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t}d\lambda

(58)

\displaystyle=C_{4}t\int_{1/t}^{\|A\|^{2}}\varphi(\lambda)e^{-\lambda t\sigma}e^{-\lambda t(1-\sigma)}d\lambda\leq C_{\sigma}C_{4}\varphi(1/t)t\int_{1/t}^{\|A\|^{2}}e^{-\lambda t(1-\sigma)}d\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{\sigma}C_{4}}{(1-\sigma)e^{1-\sigma}}\varphi(1/t).

Inserting (

(59)

From the definition of

, we deduce further that

e^{-\|A\|^{2}t}\leq\frac{C_{\sigma}^{1/\sigma}}{\varphi^{1/\sigma}\left(\|A\|^{2}\right)}\varphi^{1/\sigma}(1/t)=\frac{C_{\sigma}^{1/\sigma}\varphi^{1/\sigma-1}(1/t)}{\varphi^{1/\sigma}\left(\|A\|^{2}\right)}\varphi(1/t)\leq\frac{C_{\sigma}^{1/\sigma}}{\varphi\left(\|A\|^{2}\right)}\varphi(1/t),

since
\varphi
is increasing and

Thus, we obtain,get from (

\left\|\mathbb{E}x(t)-x^{\dagger}\right\|^{2}\leq C_{3}\varphi(1/t)

Finally, the equivalence of inequalities (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+tr(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})[f(t)]^{2}\leq\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+C\varphi(1/t),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\geq\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}[f(t)]^{2}\sum\limits^{\infty}\limits_{j=1}\frac{q_{j}}{\lambda^{2}_{j}}\left(1-e^{-2\lambda^{2}_{j}t}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}tr(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})[f(t)]^{2}\geq\|\mathbb{E}x^{\delta}(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+C^{\prime}\varphi(1/t),

which is obtained from the bias-variance decomposition (

) and the assumption

. Here,

and

are two fixed numbers independent of

.
∎

2.5

Let

with given accuracy level

-minimum norm solution
x^{\dagger}
and the regularized stochastic solution

for some data
\tilde{y}
belonging to the ball

under the optimal choice of the regularization parameter

. The proof technique is similar to that in
[
1
]
, but some proof details were simplified.

We first investigate the convergence-rate results of best worst-case mean-square error in some special situations.

Lemma 2
.

Assume that there exists a constant
C_{6}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|=0\emph{forall}t\geq C_{6}.

Then, we have

\sup_{\tilde{y}\in B_{\delta}(y)}\inf_{t>0}\mathbb{E}\|x(t,\tilde{y})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq 2C_{6}\vartheta^{2}\delta^{2}.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{y}\in B_{\delta}(y)
be fixed. With the use of the inequality (
24
), it follows that, together with the identity

,

\mathbb{E}\left\|x(t;\tilde{y})-x(t)\right\|^{2}=\left\langle\tilde{y}-y,g_{\alpha}^{2}(AA^{*})AA^{*}(\tilde{y}-y)\right\rangle\leq\delta^{2}\max_{\lambda>0}\lambda g^{2}(t,\lambda)\leq\vartheta^{2}\delta^{2}t.

(62)

Since the right-hand side is uniform for all
\tilde{{y}}\in B_{\delta}(y)
, picking

, we get

\sup_{\tilde{y}\in B_{\delta}(y)}\inf_{t>0}\mathbb{E}\|x(t;\tilde{y})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq\inf_{t>0}\left\{2\mathbb{E}\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}+2\vartheta^{2}\delta^{2}t\right\}\leq 2C_{6}\vartheta^{2}\delta^{2},

which is (

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{E}\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|>0
for all
t>0

∎

From Lemma
2
and Lemma
3
, we now get an equivalence relation between the noisy and noise-free convergence rates.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\phi(1/\cdot)\equiv\varphi(\cdot)\in\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
and, denote by

\tilde{\phi}(t)=\sqrt{t^{-1}\phi(t)}\ \text{and }\ \psi(\delta)=\delta^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\delta).

(68)

Then, the following two statements are equivalent:

(a)

There exists a constant
c>0
such that

\sup_{\tilde{y}\in\tilde{B}_{\delta}(y)}\inf_{t>0}\mathbb{E}\|x(t,\tilde{y})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq c\psi(\delta)\quad\text{for all}\ \delta>0.

(69)

(b)

There exists a constant
\tilde{c}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq\tilde{c}\phi(t)\quad\text{for all}\ t>0.

(70)

Proof.

From
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}^{g}_{\zeta}
, we have
\phi(\gamma t)\leq g(1/\gamma)\phi(t)
, which implies that
\tilde{\phi}(\gamma t)\leq\sqrt{\gamma^{-1}g(1/\gamma)}\tilde{\phi}(t)
, and so, by setting
\tilde{g}(\gamma)=\sqrt{\gamma^{-1}g(1/\gamma)},\delta=\tilde{\phi}(t)
and
\tilde{\gamma}=\tilde{g}(\gamma)
, we get

\tilde{g}^{-1}(\tilde{\gamma})\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\delta)\geq\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\tilde{\gamma}\delta).

Thus, we have

\psi(\tilde{\gamma}\delta)=\tilde{\gamma}^{2}\delta^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\tilde{\gamma}\delta)\leq\tilde{\gamma}^{2}\delta^{2}\tilde{g}^{-1}(\tilde{\gamma})\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\delta)=h(\tilde{\gamma})\psi(\delta),

(71)

where
h(\tilde{\gamma})=\tilde{\gamma}^{2}\tilde{g}^{-1}(\tilde{\gamma})
.

In the case where
\mathbb{E}\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|=0
for all
t>C_{6}
for some
C_{6}>0
, the inequality (
70
) is trivially fulfilled for some
\tilde{c}>0
. Moreover, we know from Lemma
2
that then the inequality (
61
) holds, which implies the inequality (
69
) for some constant
c>0
, since we have, according to the definition of the function
\psi
and the decreasing property of
\tilde{\phi}(t)
,
\psi(\delta)\geq a\delta^{2}
for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
for some constants
a>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
.

Thus, we may assume that
\mathbb{E}\|x(t)-x^{\dagger}\|>0
for all
t>0
.

Let (
70
) hold. For arbitrary
\delta>0
, we use the parameter
t_{\delta}
defined in (
63
). Then, the inequality (
70
) implies that

\delta^{2}t_{\delta}\leq\tilde{c}\phi(t_{\delta}).

Consequently,

\tilde{\phi}^{-1}\Big(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\tilde{c}}}\Big)\geq t_{\delta},

and therefore, using the upper bound in (
64
) obtained in Lemma
3
, we find with (
71
) that

\sup_{\tilde{y}\in\tilde{B}_{\delta}(y)}\inf_{t>0}\mathbb{E}\|x(t;\tilde{y})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}\leq C_{7}\delta^{2}t_{\delta}\leq C_{7}\delta^{2}\tilde{\phi}^{-1}\left(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\tilde{c}}}\right)=C_{7}\tilde{c}\psi\left(\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{\tilde{c}}}\right)\leq C_{7}\tilde{c}h(\tilde{c}^{-1/2})\psi(\delta),

which is the estimate (
69
) with
c=C_{7}\tilde{c}h(\tilde{c}^{-1/2})
.

Conversely, if (
69
) holds, we choose an arbitrary
\delta>0
such that
t_{\delta}
is defined by (
63
). Then, we can use the lower bound in (
64
) of Lemma
3
to obtain, from the condition (
69
),

C_{8}\delta^{2}t_{\delta}\leq c\psi(\delta).

Thus, from the definition of
\psi
, we have
\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\leq\tilde{\phi}^{-1}(\delta)
, and consequently

\left(\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\right)^{-1}\phi\left(\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\right)=\left[\tilde{\phi}\left(\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\right)\right]^{2}\geq\delta^{2},

So, finally, we obtain, with the inequality
\phi(\gamma t)\leq g(1/\gamma)\phi(t)
,

\mathbb{E}\|x(t_{\delta})-x^{\dagger}\|^{2}=\delta^{2}t_{\delta}\leq\left(\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\right)^{-1}\phi\left(\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\right)t_{\delta}=\frac{c}{C_{8}}\phi\Big(\frac{C_{8}}{c}t_{\delta}\Big)\leq\frac{c}{C_{8}}g\left(\frac{c}{C_{8}}\right)\phi(t_{\delta}),

and, since this holds for every
\delta
, (
70
) holds with
\hat{c}=\frac{c}{C_{8}}g(\frac{c}{C_{8}})
.

∎

3
Numerical realization of SAR

In order to use SAR in practice, we need to discretize the stochastic flow (
5
) for the introduced artificial time variable. Numerous algorithms have been proposed (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
17
]
) for accurate numerical approximation of stochastic differential equations. Here, we propose two of the simplest numerical approaches: the Euler method,

\displaystyle x^{\delta}_{k+1}=x^{\delta}_{k}+\Delta tA^{*}(y^{\delta}-Ax^{\delta}_{k})+f_{k}\Delta B_{k},\quad x^{\delta}_{0}=x_{0},\quad\Delta t\in(0,2/\|A\|^{2}),

(72)

and the exponential Euler method,

x^{\delta}_{k+1}=e^{-A^{*}A\Delta t}\big[x_{k}^{\delta}+A^{*}y^{\delta}\Delta t+f_{k}\Delta B_{k}\big],

(73)

Where
f_{k}=f(t_{k})
,
\Delta t
is the size of the uniform time step, and

\Delta B_{k}=B(t_{k+1})-B(t_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{q_{j}}u_{j}\big(\beta_{j}(t_{k+1})-\beta_{j}(t_{k})\big)=\sqrt{\Delta t}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{q_{j}}u_{j}\xi_{j}^{k},

with
\{\xi_{j}^{k}\}_{j,k}
being a sequence of independent
{\mathcal{N}}(0,1)
-random variables.

Proposition 5
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
be globally Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
\|f\|_{Lip}
.
The numerical solutions of (
72
) and (
73
) converge to the exact solution (
7
) of (
5
) with order one in the mean-square sense, i.e.
for
k=1,2,\cdots
,

{\mathbb{E}}\|x^{\delta}(t_{k})-x^{\delta}_{k}\|^{2}\leq C\Delta t^{2},

(74)

where
C:=C(t_{k},{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1}),\|A\|,\|A^{*}y^{\delta}\|,f)
.

Proof.

It follows from (
72
) and (
73
) that

x^{\delta}_{k}=(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k}x_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}A^{*}y^{\delta}\Delta t+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}f_{j}\Delta B_{j}

and

x^{\delta}_{k}=e^{-A^{*}At_{k}}x_{0}+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}A^{*}y^{\delta}\Delta t+\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}f_{j}\Delta B_{j}.

We recall that the exact solution (
7
) of (
5
) at time
t_{k}
is

x^{\delta}(t_{k})=e^{-A^{*}At_{k}}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t_{k}}e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}A^{*}y^{\delta}ds+\int_{0}^{t_{k}}e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)dB_{s}.

Subtracting the above two equations and taking the
{\mathbb{E}}\|\cdot\|^{2}
-norm, we get the estimate of the error for the Euler method (
72
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|x^{\delta}(t_{k})-x^{\delta}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|(e^{-A^{*}At_{k}}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k})x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+3{\mathbb{E}}\Big\|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}ds\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+3{\mathbb{E}}\Big\|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}f_{j}\big)dB(s)\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3\|(e^{-A^{*}At_{k}}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k})x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+3k\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}\big\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\quad+3{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}f_{j}\big)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}^{2}ds,

and the estimate of the error for the exponential Euler method (
73
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|x^{\delta}(t_{k})-x^{\delta}_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathbb{E}}\Big\|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}ds\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+2{\mathbb{E}}\Big\|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}f_{j}\big)dB(s)\Big\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2k\Delta t\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}\big\|^{2}ds

\displaystyle\quad+2{\mathbb{E}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\int_{t_{j}}^{t_{j+1}}\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}f_{j}\big)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}^{2}ds.

Note that

\displaystyle\|(e^{-A^{*}At_{k}}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k})\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{X}})}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}e^{-A^{*}At_{k-1-\ell}}(e^{-A^{*}A\Delta t}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t))(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{\ell}\Big\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{X}})}

\displaystyle\leq k\|e^{-A^{*}A\Delta t}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{X}})}

\displaystyle\leq k\sup_{0<\lambda\leq\|A\|^{2}}|e^{-\lambda\Delta t}-(1-\lambda\Delta t)|\leq t_{k}\|A\|^{2}\Delta t,

and, for
0<s-t\leq\Delta t
,

\displaystyle\big\|e^{-A^{*}At}-e^{-A^{*}As}\big\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{X}})}\leq\sup_{\lambda\in(0,\|A\|^{2}]}e^{-\lambda t}\big(1-e^{-\lambda(s-t)}\big)\leq\|A\|^{2}\Delta t.

Hence, for the Euler method (
72
),

\displaystyle\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j+1})}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}\big\|

\displaystyle+\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j+1})}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{*}y^{\delta}\|\|A\|^{2}(1+t_{k})\Delta t,

and

\displaystyle\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}f_{j}\big)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})\|A\|^{2}\Big[\|(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j})f(s)\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\|(I-A^{*}A\Delta t)^{k-1-j}(f(s)-f(t_{j}))\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})\|A\|^{2}\Big[t_{k-1-j}\|f\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|A\|^{4}+\|f\|_{Lip}^{2}\Big](\Delta t)^{2},

which lead to the assertion (
74
). Similarly, for the exponential Euler method, we have (
73
),

\displaystyle\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}\big)A^{*}y^{\delta}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}\big\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{X}};{\mathcal{X}})}\|A^{*}y^{\delta}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{*}y^{\delta}\|\sup_{\lambda\in(0,\|A\|^{2}]}e^{-\lambda(t_{k}-s)}\big(1-e^{-\lambda(s-t_{j})}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{*}y^{\delta}\|\|A\|^{2}\Delta t,

and

\displaystyle\big\|\big(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}f(s)-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}f_{j}\big)Q^{\frac{1}{2}}\big\|_{HS(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{X})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})\|A\|^{2}\Big[\|(e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-s)}-e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})})f(s)\|^{2}+\|e^{-A^{*}A(t_{k}-t_{j})}(f(s)-f(t_{j}))\|^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq{\rm tr}(Q(A^{*}A)^{-1})\|A\|^{2}\Big[\|f\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}}\|A\|^{4}+\|f\|_{Lip}^{2}\Big](\Delta t)^{2},

which yield to the assertion (
74
) for the exponential Euler method.

∎

4
Numerical experiments

This section presents some numerical experiments to illustrate the numerical behavior of SAR. In the first example of abstract integral equations, we demonstrate that, in contrast to the conventional deterministic regularization methods, SAR provides the uncertainty quantification of the regularized solution. In the second example, we further show that the uncertainty quantification of the estimated physical quantity can reveal and explicate the hidden information about the real-world inverse problem.

4.1
A toy example showing the uncertainty capability of SAR

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}=L^{2}[0,1]

A

4.2
Biosensor tomography with real data

In this section, the developed SAR is applied to a biosensor tomography problem, i.e. finding the interaction information from biosensor data. We demonstrate that, in contrast to the conventional deterministic regularization methods, SAR provides the uncertainty quantification of the regularized solution, which can be further used to reveal and explicate the hidden information about the physical quantity of interest. To this end, we first briefly review the mechanism of biosensor tomography.

4.2.1
The mathematical model

For the considered biosensors, the analyte binds to an antibody that is attached to a surface. Then, a physical method (e.g. evanescent wave, surface plasmon resonance) is used to measure the surface concentration of the antibody-analyte complex. To better understand the construction of the mathematical model, we consider the three steps of the binding process during the modeling.

Step 1
: “1-to-1” meta kinetic model. For each interaction we have

[A]+[L]\mathop{\vbox{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{#\cr\raise 1.72218pt\hbox{$\hbox to30.35852pt{$\mathord{\textrm{\char 69\relax}}\mkern-6.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\mathord{\textrm{\char 71\relax}}\mkern-2.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-6.0mu\mathord{\textrm{\char 71\relax}}$\hskip 0.50003pt}$}\crcr$\hbox to30.35852pt{\hskip 0.50003pt$\mathord{\textrm{\char 71\relax}}\mkern-6.0mu\leaders{\hbox{$\mkern-2.0mu\mathord{\textrm{\char 71\relax}}\mkern-2.0mu$}}{\hfill}\mkern-6.0mu\mathord{\textrm{\char 67\relax}}$}$\crcr}}}}}\limits^{\hbox{$k_{a}$}}_{\hbox{$k_{d}$}}[AL],

where
k_{a}
and
k_{d}
denote the association and dissociation rate constants, and
[A]
,
[L]
, and
[AL]
represent the concentrations of the analyte, ligand, and complex, respectively. In our simplified model, the analyte
A
is injected and flushed over the surface in such a way that the concentration
[A]
can be assumed to be constant during the study. The amount of free ligand decreases with time according to
[L](t)=[L](0)-[AL](t)
. We assume that the sensor response
R
is proportional to the complex concentration
[AL](t)
, i.e.
R(t)=C_{b}\cdot[AL](t)
, where
C_{b}
is a constant. Let
R_{max}=C_{b}\cdot[L](0)
. Then, if the mass-transfer kinetics are extremely fast, the rate of complex formation satisfies the following dynamical equation:

\frac{dR(t)}{dt}=k_{a}\cdot[A](t)\cdot\left(R_{max}-R(t)\right)-k_{d}\cdot R(t).

(76)

Setting
C=[A]
(as mentioned above,
C
is a fixed number) and
R(t_{0})=0
, the solution to (
76
) is

R(t)=R_{max}\cdot\frac{k_{a}C}{k_{d}+k_{a}C}\cdot\left(1-e^{(k_{d}+k_{a}C)(t-t_{0})}\right).

(77)

Step 2
: “m-to-n” kinetic model. We now assume that there are
m
analytes and
n
binding sites on the biosensor surface and first-order kinetics. Let
(k_{a,i},k_{d,j})
denote the pair of association and dissociation constants for the interaction between the
i
th analyte and
j
th binding site. Let
R_{i,j}(t)
be the response at time
t
of a complex with association constant
k_{a,i}
and dissociation constant
k_{d,j}
. Then, according to (
77
), we have

R_{i,j}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}l>{\displaystyle}l}&0,\qquad t\leq t_{0}+\Delta t,\\
&R^{max}_{i,j}\frac{k_{a,i}C}{k_{d,j}+k_{a,i}C}\left(1-e^{-(k_{d,j}+k_{a,i}C)(t-t_{0})}\right),\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad t_{0}+\Delta t<t\leq t_{0}+t_{inj}+\Delta t,\\
&R^{max}_{i,j}\frac{k_{a,i}C}{k_{d,j}+k_{a,i}C}\left(1-e^{-(k_{d,j}+k_{a,i}C)t_{inj}}\right)e^{-k_{d,j}(t-t_{0}-t_{inj})},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad t>t_{0}+t_{inj}+\Delta t,\end{array}\right.

(78)

where constant
C
represents the concentration of the analyte,
t_{0}
is the time at which the injection of the analyte begins, and
t_{inj}
is the injection time. The adjustment parameter
\Delta t
is a time delay that accounts for the fact that it usually takes some time for the detector to respond to the injection. In our software,
\Delta t
can be automatically determined; it can be understood as a preconditioning of a real piece of data. Constant
R^{max}_{i,j}
is the total surface-binding capacity, corresponding to association and dissociation constants
k_{a,i}
and
k_{d,j}
, i.e. the detector response when every binding site on the biosensor surface has formed a complex with the analyte.

Step 3
: Continuous kinetic model. We now use the functions
R_{i,j}
to make an approximation of the measured sensorgrams
R_{obs}
. By employing the principle of superposition, the total measured response,
R_{obs}
, of a system can be written as a linear combination of some individual responses, namely
R_{obs}=\sum^{m,n}_{i,j=1}R_{i,j}
. If we let
m,n\to+\infty
in the above equation, we obtain the final mathematical model of biosensor tomography:

R_{obs}(t;C)=\int_{\Omega}K(t,C;k_{a},k_{d})x(k_{a},k_{d})dk_{a}dk_{d},\quad(k_{a},k_{d})\in\Omega,

(79)

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the domain of rate constants that is of interest, and the kernel function
K(\cdot)
is defined as

K(t,C;k_{a},k_{d})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{>{\displaystyle}l>{\displaystyle}l}&0,\qquad t\leq t_{0}+\Delta t,\\
&\frac{k_{a}C}{k_{d}+k_{a}C}\left(1-e^{-(k_{d}+k_{a}C)(t-t_{0})}\right),\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad t_{0}+\Delta t<t\leq t_{0}+t_{inj}+\Delta t,\\
&\frac{k_{a}C}{k_{d}+k_{a}C}\left(1-e^{-(k_{d}+k_{a}C)t_{inj}}\right)e^{-k_{d}(t-t_{0}-t_{inj})},\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad t>t_{0}+t_{inj}+\Delta t,\end{array}\right.

(80)

The function
x(k_{a},k_{d})
, which is the generalization of the total surface-binding capacity
\{R^{max}_{i,j}\}
, is known as the (continuous) rate-constant map. In the
rate-constant-map theory
of chemical reactions, the local and global maximums of a rate-constant map reflect the interaction information about a chemical system consisting of various molecules:

•

The global maximum of a rate-constant map reflects the principal interaction among molecules, and the coordinate of the global maximum represents the value of association and dissociation rate constants of the principle interaction.

•

The local maximums of a rate-constant map reflect the minor interactions among molecules. The number of local maximums shows the number of minor interactions in the considered environment. The corresponding coordinates of the local maximums represent the value of association and dissociation rate constants of the corresponding minor interactions.

In sum, according to the rate-constant-map theory, the biosensor tomography can be solved via the following two steps:

•

Given noisy biosensor data
R_{obs}(t;C)
, find the approximate rate-constant map
x(k_{a},k_{d})
.

•

Determine the interaction information (i.e. the number of interactions and the corresponding association and dissociation rate constants) from the estimated rate-constant map
x(k_{a},k_{d})
.

In the next subsection, we show that SAR can substantially improve the second step of biosensor tomography through the use of the statistical property of SAR.

4.2.2
Simulation study

We tested SAR for biosensor tomography with real experimental data – the parathyroid hormone (PTH). In the experiment, the human PTH1R receptor was immobilized on a LNB-carboxyl biosensor chip using amine coupling according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Using the flow rate 25
\mu L/min
at 20.0
∘
C, we did 35
\mu L
injections of the peptide PTH(1-34) at 9 concentration levels from 1214 nM to 14571 nM; see the solid lines in Figure
3
. The sensorgrams were measured with a QCM biosensor Attana Cell 200 (Attana AB, Stockholm, Sweden) instrument.

A standard adaptive linear finite-element method was adopted to discretize the two-dimensional integral equation (
79
). The initial triangulation was uniformly distributed in the log-scale domain
(\log_{10}(k_{d}),\log_{10}(k_{a}))\in[-4,0]\times[3,7]
with
10\times 10=200
node points. Then, a standard adaptive approach (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
27
]
) was employed to achieve better accuracy with minimum degrees of freedom. More precisely, we first solved integral equation (
79
) to obtain the solution on the current triangulation. The error was then estimated using the solution, and used to mark a set of triangles that were to be refined. The triangles were refined in such a way as to keep two of the most important properties of the triangulations: shape regularity and conformity. Our algorithm stopped at the 6-th iteration with 600 nodes and 1149 triangles, as shown in Figure
3
. The total running time was 13.6 seconds

5
Conclusions

We develop a statistical approach – SAR for deterministic linear inverse problems. In this paper, a complete regularization theory of SAR has been presented and analyzed. In particular, it has been shown that SAR yields an optimal regularization method with regard to mean-square convergence. Compared with the conventional deterministic regularization method, SAR provides the uncertainty quantification of the estimated quantity, which is described by a deterministic forward model. A real data application of SAR for biosensor tomography showed that the uncertainty quantification of SAR can reveal and explicate the interaction information about a chemical system, which is hidden behind the original deterministic forward model. We therefore believe that SAR will be a useful tool for studying biosensor interactions and other real-world inverse problems with deterministic ill-posed forward models.
