Casimir Energy and Modularity in Higher-dimensional Conformal Field Theories


Abstract
An important problem in Quantum Field Theory (QFT) is to understand the structures of observables on spacetime manifolds of nontrivial topology. Such observables arise naturally when studying physical systems at finite temperature and/or finite volume and encode subtle properties of the underlying microscopic theory that are often obscure on the flat spacetime. Locality of the QFT implies that these observables can be constructed from more basic building blocks by cutting-and-gluing along a spatial slice, where a crucial ingredient is the Hilbert space on the spatial manifold. In Conformal Field Theory (CFT), thanks to the operator-state correspondence, we have a non-perturbative understanding of the Hilbert space on a spatial sphere. However it remains a challenge to consider more general spatial manifolds. Here we study CFTs in spacetime dimensions d > 2 d>2 on the spatial manifold T 2 × ℝ d − 3 T^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-3} which is one of the simplest manifolds beyond the spherical topology. We focus on the ground state in this Hilbert space and analyze universal properties of the ground state energy, also commonly known as the Casimir energy, which is a nontrivial function of the complex structure moduli τ \tau of the torus. The Casimir energy is subject to constraints from modular invariance on the torus which we spell out using P ​ S ​ L ​ ( 2 , ℤ ) PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}}) spectral theory. Moreover we derive a simple universal formula for the Casimir energy in the thin torus limit using the effective field theory (EFT) from Kaluza-Klein reduction of the CFT, with exponentially small corrections from worldline instantons. We illustrate our formula with explicit examples from well-known CFTs including the critical O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) model in d = 3 d=3 and holographic CFTs in d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 .

Introduction

Conformal Field Theories (CFTs) provide a unifying and powerful approach to a large class of strongly coupled Quantum Field Theories (QFTs) that arise in both high energy and condensed matter physics. Over the recent years, there has been tremendous development in elucidating the structure of CFTs by the conformal bootstrap method
[
1

2

3

d>2

, which are central objects in QFT. However there is much more to QFT than the flat spacetime. In particular, local QFT is expected to be defined on general spacetime manifolds.

Considerations of QFT on a spacetime manifold of nontrivial topology open the door to a profusion of new observables. For example the correlation functions of local operators (even the partition function itself) may now depend nontrivially on topology and finer geometric data of the manifold. Such dependence
detects global topological features of QFT which
are crucial to distinguish theories that are otherwise identical at the level of local correlation functions

The challenge is to develop methods to extract QFT observables on nontrivial manifolds, understand their general properties, and uncover underlying structures. On the surface this seems an intractable task simply because of the sheer complexity of the geometry of the manifolds. Nonetheless, the locality principle dictates that QFT observables must obey consistency conditions known as cutting-and-gluing axioms
[

Unsurprisingly, such questions are most well-formulated for CFTs.

\displaystyle S_{\tilde{M},Q}=\int_{0}^{\infty}{dT\over T}e^{-{\tilde{M}^{2}}T}\int_{x^{j}(T)=x^{j}(0)}Dx^{j}(\varphi)\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{T}d\varphi\left({1\over 4}g_{ij}\dot{x}^{i}\dot{x}^{j}+2\pi iQa_{j}\dot{x}^{j}\right)\right]\,,

(2.11)

where
x^{j}(\varphi)
parametrizes the worldline of a scalar particle of mass
\tilde{M}\sim{1\over{\beta}}
and KK-charge
Q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
T
is the usual Schwinger parameter. For nonzero
Q
, the particle couples to the background KK-photon
a_{i}
.
The saddle point equation for the worldline path integral over
x^{j}(\varphi)
is simply
\ddot{x}^{i}=0
since the
a_{i}
is flat. The Euclidean solutions compatible with the periodicity condition are

\displaystyle x^{i}(\varphi)=({n\varphi\over T},0,\dots,0)\,,

(2.12)

where the only nonzero entry is in the
S^{1}_{L}
direction and this solution winds
n
times along
S^{1}_{L}
. The one-loop effective action (
2.11
) is dominated by these saddles for large mass
\tilde{M}L\sim{L/{\beta}}\gg 1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tilde{M}L\gg 1}S_{\tilde{M},Q}=e^{-n\tilde{M}L}e^{2\pi inQ\mu}\,,

(2.13)

on top of which there are perturbative contributions in
1/\tilde{M}
coming from the fluctuations.

The
n=0
case of (
2.12
) is the trivial saddle which is insensitive to compact directions of the spacetime manifold. The fluctuations thereof precisely generate the perturbative terms in the effective action that are power law in
\beta
. The case
n\neq 0
of (
2.12
) corresponds to nontrivial worldline instantons and (
2.13
) gives the instanton action which depends on the chemical potential
\mu
if the particle carries a nonzero KK charge
Q
. For convenience we define the dimensionless mass
M
by
\tilde{M}={2\pi M\over{\beta}}
.

The most general contributions to
f_{\rm np}({\beta},\mu)
come from multiple worldline instantons, each labeled by its mass
M
, KK charge
Q
and winding number
n
. Moreover these worldline instantons may interact with one another, producing corrections beyond the dilute instanton gas approximation
[
27
]
.

The non-perturbative contributions to the free energy takes the following form, as a sum over multi-instanton configurations

\displaystyle A^{\frac{d}{2}}f_{\rm np}(\beta,\mu)=-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{M},\vec{Q}}e^{-{2\pi L\over{\beta}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}M_{i}}e^{2\pi i\mu\sum_{i=1}^{j}Q_{i}}g_{j,\vec{M},\vec{Q}}\left({{\beta}\over L}\right)\,.

(2.14)

Here
j
counts the number of worldline instantons each with unit winding number. The vectors
\vec{M},\vec{Q}
each has
j
entries and they specify the masses and KK charges of individual instantons in this configuration.
11
11

11

In this parametrization, a worldline instanton of charge
Q
and winding number
n
is represented as
n
identical worldline instantons of unit winding number and the same charge
Q
.

The fluctuations around each
j
-instanton configuration and the integration over the moduli space of such configurations are captured by the factor
g_{j,\vec{M},\vec{Q}}\left({{\beta}\over L}\right)
which contains power-law terms in
{{\beta}\over L}
.

For free CFTs compactified on
S^{1}_{\beta}
, the worldline instantons
coming from massive KK modes do not interact and
g_{j,\vec{M},\vec{Q}}
can be computed explicitly from (
2.11
). See further discussions in Section
4
for the free scalar CFT.
In general CFT upon circle reduction, there are nontrivial interactions among worldline instantons which are not taken into account by (
2.11
). In Section
5.1
, we will find explicit expressions of
g_{j,\vec{M},\vec{Q}}
for the worldline instantons in the interacting
O(N)
CFT.

Using the relation between the free energy and the Casimir energy (
2.5
) (and also (
2.2
)), we conclude the EFT analysis with the following universal behavior for the Casimir energy density on
T^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-3,1}

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to i\infty}{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=c_{1}\tau_{2}^{d\over 2}+c_{2}\tau_{2}^{1-{d\over 2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\vec{M},\vec{Q}}e^{-{2\pi\tau_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{j}M_{i}}e^{2\pi i\tau_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{j}Q_{i}}g_{j,\vec{M},\vec{Q}}(\tau_{2})\,,

(2.15)

which contains two perturbative terms whose coefficients are related to finite temperature CFT data by (
2.9
) and (
2.10
), and a tower of nonperturbative contributions from the worldline instanton gas.

Modular Properties from

As already explained in the introduction, the toroidal Casimir energy density

is a modular (invariant) function of the complex moduli
\tau
.
Here we want to understand what types of non-holomorphic modular functions are relevant for this CFT observable.

Unlike the holomorphic case where the space of modular functions is highly constrained (to be rational functions of the modular invariant
j(\tau)
), the space of non-holomorphic modular functions is much bigger. This is necessary to accommodate the vast zoo of modular functions of different properties that arise from QFT observables which are mostly non-holomorphic. Well-studied examples of such modular functions include the torus partition functions of
d=2
CFTs and correlation functions in the
d=4

{\mathcal{N}}=4
super-Yang-Mills (SYM) CFT where
\tau
is the complexified Yang-Mills coupling. An important question there is to understand the general properties of the relevant modular functions as dictated by the underlying CFT. Impressive progress is made in the recent years on this question for both the
d=2
CFTs
[
28
,
29
]
and the
d=4
SYM
[
30
]
using the powerful tool of
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
spectral theory (see also previous work
[
31
]
). Here we initiate to tackle the same question in yet another different context, that is the toroidal Casimir energy of general
d>2
CFTs.

3.1
Review of
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
Spectral Theory

Let us start with a quick review of the necessary ingredients for the
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
spectral theory. See for example
[
32
,
33
]
for more details. Here we follow the conventions of mathematics literature to denote the real and imaginary parts of the complex moduli as
\tau=x+iy
(which we haven written as
\tau=\tau_{1}+i\tau_{2}
in the rest of the paper).
The standard
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
fundamental domain is denoted by
{\mathcal{F}}=PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})\backslash{\mathbb{H}}
with the hyperbolic metric

\displaystyle ds^{2}={dx^{2}+dy^{2}\over y^{2}}\,,

(3.1)

and hyperbolic volume

\displaystyle{\rm vol}({\mathcal{F}})={\pi\over 3}\,.

(3.2)

For modular invariant square integrable functions
f(\tau),g(\tau)\in L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})
, there is a natural inner product known as the Petersson inner product defined by the following integral over
{\mathcal{F}}
,

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle\equiv\int_{\mathcal{F}}{d^{2}\tau\over y^{2}}f(\tau)\overline{g(\tau)}\,,

(3.3)

Square integrability here simply means finite
\langle f,f\rangle
.
The hyperbolic Laplacian on
{\mathcal{F}}
defined by

\displaystyle\Delta\equiv-y^{2}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\,,

(3.4)

is self-adjoint with respect to (
3.3
) on
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})
. Consequently, any modular invariant function
f\in L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})
admits a unique orthogonal decomposition into eigenfunctions with respect to
\Delta
,

\displaystyle f(\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\nu_{j}(\tau){\langle f,\nu_{j}\rangle\over\langle\nu_{j},\nu_{j}\rangle}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds\langle f,E_{s}\rangle E_{s}(\tau)\,,

(3.5)

known as the Roelcke-Selberg spectral decomposition. The eigenfunctions here consists of a continuous family of non-holomorphic Eisenstein series
E_{s}(\tau)
with
s={1\over 2}+i{\mathbb{R}}
, which satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta E_{s}=s(1-s)E_{s}\,,

(3.6)

and a
discrete family called the cusp Maass forms
\nu_{i}(\tau)

satisfying

\displaystyle\Delta\nu_{j}=\lambda_{j}\nu_{j}\,,\quad\lambda_{j}={1\over 4}+R_{j}^{2}\,,

(3.7)

where
R_{j}
are a set of sporadic positive real numbers.

Let us review some properties of these modular eigenfunctions below.
The Eisenstein series has the following explicit definition, in the form of a Fourier decomposition,

12

12

Note that the Eisenstein series is not in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})

defines modular functions in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})
.

\displaystyle E_{s}(\tau)=\varphi_{s}(y)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}4\cos(2\pi jx){\sigma_{2s-1}(j)\over j^{s-1/2}\Lambda(s)}\sqrt{y}K_{s-{1\over 2}}(2\pi jy)\,,\quad\varphi_{s}(y)=y^{s}+{\Lambda(1-s)\over\Lambda(s)}y^{1-s}\,,

(3.8)

where
\sigma_{m}(n)=\sum_{d|n}d^{m}
is the divisor function and

\Lambda(s)
is the completed Riemann zeta function defined below,

\displaystyle\Lambda(s)\equiv\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)\zeta(2s)\,.

(3.9)

It satisfies the following reflection relation,

\displaystyle\Lambda(s)=\Lambda\left({1\over 2}-s\right)\,,

(3.10)

and is meromorphic for
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
with simple poles at
s=0,{1\over 2}
and residues

\displaystyle{\rm Res}_{s=0}\Lambda(s)=-{\rm Res}_{s={1\over 2}}\Lambda(s)=-{1\over 2}\,,

(3.11)

\Lambda(s)

for nontrivial zeros
\rho
of the Riemann zeta function (with
{\rm Re}\,\rho={1\over 2}
according to the Riemann hypothesis).

Correspondingly, the Eisenstein series
E_{s}(\tau)
is meromorphic in

, with a simple pole at
s=1
and other sporadic simple poles at
s={\rho\over 2}
. In particular the residue at

\displaystyle{\rm Res}_{s=1}E_{s}(\tau)={3\over\pi}={\rm vol}({\mathcal{F}})^{-1}\,.

(3.12)

For

, the Eisenstein series is equivalently defined by the following Poincaré series

\displaystyle E_{s}(\tau)=\sum_{{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{\infty}\backslash PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})}{\rm Im}({\gamma}\tau)^{s}={1\over 2}\sum_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}},(m,n)=1}{y^{s}\over|m\tau+n|^{2s}}\,,

(3.13)

which is clearly
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
invariant and its meromorphic continuation to

is given by (
3.8
).
It is convenient to define the completed (symmetric) Eisenstein series

\displaystyle E_{s}^{*}(\tau)\equiv\Lambda(s)E_{s}(\tau)\,,

(3.14)

which satisfies

\displaystyle E_{s}^{*}(\tau)=E^{*}_{1-s}(\tau)\,.

(3.15)

For
{\rm Re}\,s>1
, it can also be written as

\displaystyle E^{*}_{s}(\tau)={1\over 2}\pi^{-s}\Gamma(s)\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{y^{s}\over|m\tau+n|^{2s}}\,,

(3.16)

where
\sum^{\prime}
means the

term is dropped.
The overlaps in (
3.5
) are explicitly given by,

\displaystyle\langle F,E_{s}\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{F}}{d^{2}\tau\over y^{2}}F(\tau)E_{1-s}(\tau)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dyy^{-1-s}F_{0}(y)\,,

(3.17)

where
F_{0}\equiv\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dxF(\tau)
is the zero Fourier mode of

and the last equality follows from the unfolding trick (using the Poincaré series definition of
E_{s}
in (
3.13
) and then analytic continuation).

The Maass cusp forms
\nu_{j}(\tau)
are eigenfunctions of
\Delta

\displaystyle\nu_{0}\equiv\sqrt{3\over\pi}\,.

(3.18)

For more general Maass cusp forms, depending on their parity under

\displaystyle\nu_{j}^{+}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{(j,+)}\cos(2\pi kx)\sqrt{y}K_{iR_{j}^{+}}(2\pi ky)\,,

(3.19)

\displaystyle\nu_{j}^{-}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}^{(j,-)}\sin(2\pi kx)\sqrt{y}K_{iR_{j}^{-}}(2\pi ky)\,,

where the order of the Bessel functions are related to the

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{\pm}\equiv{1\over 4}+(R_{j}^{\pm})^{2}\,.

(3.20)

The normalization is commonly fixed by

3.2
Spectral Decomposition of Casimir Energy Density

The spectral decomposition (
3.5
) only applies to modular functions that are square integrable. As we have derived from EFT arguments in Section
2.1
,
the CFT toroidal Casimir energy density
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
has the following cusp behavior (see (
2.15
)),

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to i\infty}{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=c_{1}y^{d\over 2}+c_{2}y^{1-{d\over 2}}+{\mathcal{O}}\left(y^{\alpha}e^{-2\pi Mny}e^{2\pi iknx}\right)\,,

(3.21)

for
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}\geq 0
, thus clearly not square integrable for

.

Nevertheless there is a natural way to regularize this cusp behavior using the hyperbolic Laplacian,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}(\tau)\equiv\left(\Delta+{d(d-2)\over 4}\right){\mathcal{E}}\,,

(3.22)

which is modular invariant and vanishes exponentially at the cusp

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to i\infty}{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}(\tau)={\mathcal{O}}\left(y^{\alpha}e^{-2\pi Mny}e^{2\pi iknx}\right)

(3.23)

and thus now in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})
. Therefore we can apply the decomposition (
3.5
) and write,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}(\tau)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},\nu_{j}\rangle\over\langle\nu_{j},\nu_{j}\rangle}\nu_{j}(\tau)+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},E_{s}\rangle}E_{s}(\tau)\,.

(3.24)

It is straightforward to invert the differential operator in (
3.22
) and obtain,

\displaystyle\left(\Delta+{d(d-2)\over 4}\right)^{-1}{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},\nu_{j}\rangle\over\lambda_{j}+{d(d-2)\over 4}}{\nu_{j}(\tau)\over\langle\nu_{j},\nu_{j}\rangle}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},E_{s}\rangle\over{(d-1)^{2}\over 4}-{(2s-1)^{2}\over 4}}E_{s}(\tau)\,,

(3.25)

which is still in
L^{2}({\mathcal{F}})
since coefficients in the decomposition are now divided by positive numbers that are bounded below by
{(d-1)^{2}\over 4}
.

This inversion is ambiguous up to the kernel of
\Delta+{d(d-2)\over 4}
which can be fixed by adding the Eisenstein series
E_{d\over 2}(\tau)
whose coefficient is fixed by the cusp behavior of
{\mathcal{E}}
in (
3.21
).
We thus find

13

13

In parity-invariant CFT, only the even cusp forms
\nu_{j}^{+}
in (

) appears in the spectral decomposition of the toroidal Casimir energy.

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle c_{1}}E_{d\over 2}(\tau)+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},\nu_{j}\rangle\over\lambda_{j}+{d(d-2)\over 4}}{\nu_{j}(\tau)\over\langle\nu_{j},\nu_{j}\rangle}+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},E_{s}\rangle\over{(d-1)^{2}\over 4}-{(2s-1)^{2}\over 4}}E_{s}(\tau)\,.

(3.26)

This decomposition is unique. Suppose there is another modular function

with the same cusp behavior as in (
3.21

\displaystyle\left(\Delta+{d(d-2)\over 4}\right){\mathcal{E}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}\,.

Then

\displaystyle\left(\Delta+{d(d-2)\over 4}\right)({\mathcal{E}}-{\mathcal{E}}^{\prime})=0\,,

(3.28)

which is not possible by the spectral decomposition (

It is convenient to work with the symmetric Eisenstein series

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=

\displaystyle c_{1}E_{{d\over 2}}(\tau)+{12\over d(d-2)\pi}\int_{\mathcal{F}}{d^{2}\tau\over y^{2}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}(\tau)+{1\over 4\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds{\{{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},E_{s}\}\over{(d-1)^{2}\over 4}-{(2s-1)^{2}\over 4}}E^{*}_{s}(\tau)

(3.30)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},\nu_{j}\rangle\over\lambda_{j}+{d(d-2)\over 4}}\nu_{j}(\tau)\,,

Focusing on the zero Fourier mode of

, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{0}(y)=

\displaystyle c_{1}\varphi_{d\over 2}(y)+{12\over d(d-2)\pi}\int_{\mathcal{F}}{d^{2}\tau\over y^{2}}{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg}(\tau)+{1\over 2\pi i}\int_{{\rm Re\,}s={1\over 2}}ds{\{{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},E_{s}\}\over{(d-1)^{2}\over 4}-{(2s-1)^{2}\over 4}}\Lambda(s)y^{s}\,,

A major advantage of the spectral decomposition is that it isolates the potential unknowns and packages them into the coefficients, which we define as as

\displaystyle h(s)\equiv\{{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},E_{s}\}\,,\quad p_{j}\equiv\langle{\mathcal{E}}_{\rm reg},\nu_{j}\rangle~{\rm for}~j\geq 1\,,

\displaystyle E_{\rm vac}=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\int{d^{d-3}\vec{p}\over(2\pi)^{d-3}}{1\over 2}\omega_{m,n}(\vec{p})\,,

(4.1)

where each individual mode contributes,

\displaystyle\omega_{m,n}({\vec{p}})=\sqrt{\vec{p}^{2}+{(m\vec{k}_{1}+n\vec{k}_{2})^{2}}}\,,

(4.2)

and

\displaystyle\vec{k}_{1}={2\pi\over L_{1}}\left(1,-\frac{\tau_{1}}{\tau_{2}}\right),\quad\vec{k}_{2}={2\pi\over L_{1}}\left(0,\frac{1}{\tau_{2}}\right)\,.

(4.3)

Note that we have explicitly excluded the

The sum-integral (

\displaystyle E_{\rm vac}=-{1\over(L\tau_{2})^{d-2}}{\pi^{d-2\over 2}\over 2}\Gamma\left(1-{d\over 2}\right)\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{m,n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|m\tau+n|^{d-2}\,.

(4.4)

The residual sum over the momentum lattice is regulated by analytic continuation using the real analytic Eisenstein series defined in (
3.16

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)=E^{*}_{2-d\over 2}(\tau)=E^{*}_{d\over 2}(\tau)\,,

(4.5)

where in the last equality we have used the reflection symmetry (

The free scalar Casimir energy density has a simple Fourier decomposition in

(see (

\displaystyle{\cal E}(\tau)={\Lambda(d/2)}\tau_{2}^{d/2}+{{\Lambda(1-d/2)}}\tau_{2}^{1-d/2}+4\tau_{2}^{1/2}\sum_{k,n=1}^{\infty}\left({k\over n}\right)^{d-1\over 2}{\cos(2\pi kn\tau_{1})K_{{d-1\over 2}}(2\pi kn\tau_{2})}\,.

(4.6)

This makes explicit the hierarchy between perturbative and non-perturbative contributions in the limit

2.15
), we find the corresponding coefficients

\displaystyle c_{1}=\Lambda\left({d\over 2}\right)\,,\quad c_{2}=\Lambda\left({d-1\over 2}\right)\,,

(4.7)

where

is defined in (

Indeed, the thermal one-point function of stress tensor of a free real scalar (see for example
[

\displaystyle b_{T}=-{2\zeta(d)\over d{\rm vol}(S^{d-1})}=-{1\over d}\,\Lambda\left({d\over 2}\right)\,,

(4.8)

where we have used

reduction is obviously a free scalar in one lower dimension, we see (

is in agreement with (

The non-perturbative contributions to the toroidal Casimir energy for the free scalar come entirely from the nonzero Fourier modes in (

Finally, in terms of the spectral decomposition discussed in Section

5
Casimir Energy Density in Interacting CFTs

5.1.1
Casimir Energy from Gap Equation

We now study the

Physically,

6
Discussions

In this paper we have studied general properties of
d>2
CFT on
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}=T^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-3,1}
(or
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}=T^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-2}
in the Euclidean signature) which is one of the simplest spacetime manifold of nontrivial topology and a natural generalization of the thermal background
S^{1}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
. We focused on a basic observable on this geometry, namely the Casimir energy (ground state energy) on the spatial manifold
{\mathcal{M}}_{d-1}=T^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-3}
. The dimensionless Casimir energy density
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
is a nontrivial modular invariant function of the complex moduli
\tau
of the torus
T^{2}
and appears to be independent from conventional CFT data.
In the thin torus limit
\tau_{2}\to\infty
, we derived a simple universal formula (
2.15
) for
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
which shows that the toroidal Casimir energy is controlled by two perturbative terms in
\tau_{2}
up to non-perturbative corrections. This was accomplished via an effective field theory (EFT) argument by compactifying the
d
dimensional CFT on the small cycle of the thin torus. The coefficients
c_{1},c_{2}
of the perturbative terms are proportional to familiar finite temperature observables in the CFT and its circle reduction. The remaining non-perturbative terms in (
2.15
) are accounted for by worldline instantons associated with massive particles in the
d-1
dimensional EFT going around the remaining cycle in the base manifold. Combining with
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
spectral theory which is a powerful framework to study modular invariant functions, we translated the EFT constraints into a set of stringent conditions on the spectral overlap in the decomposition of
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
with respect to eigenfunctions of the
PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
invariant Laplacian. This spectral decomposition makes more explicit the class of modular invariant functions that are physically relevant for describing the toroidal Casimir energy
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
in CFT. It also explains intricate relations between EFT data that enters into the universal formula for
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
(
2.15
). For example, although
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
in general receives worldline instanton contributions in the EFT from all KK charges
Q

Q=0
and
Q=\pm 1
sector as a consequence of the modular invariance. We illustrate these universal properties of
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)

in concrete CFT examples including the free scalar CFT, the critical
O(N)
model and holographic CFTs with Einstein gravity duals. Below we discuss a number of open questions and future directions.

Generalizations to Fermionic CFTs

Thus far we have mostly focused on bosonic CFTs (except for comments in the holographic examples). In the presence of fermionic matter, to define the CFT on a general spacetime manifold
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}
requires a spin structure. For
{\mathcal{M}}_{d}=T^{2}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d-2}
, there are four spin structures that correspond to either periodic or anti-periodic boundary conditions for the fermions along each of the two independent cycles on
T^{2}
. The choice of the spin structure
\rho

{\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}(\tau)
depend on
\rho
in addition to the complex moduli
\tau
. Relatedly, for a fixed spin structure
\rho

PSL(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
invariance of the bosonic Casimir energy
{\mathcal{E}}(\tau)
is broken to congruence subgroups preserving
\rho
for the fermionic counterpart
{\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}(\tau)
. Consequently,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\rho}(\tau)
will have different modular properties. There is also a simple generalization of the EFT analysis in Section
2.1
for fermionic CFT with an analogous universal formula as (
2.15
). In subsequent work
[
10
]
, we study the toroidal Casimir energy for fermionic CFTs systematically with concrete examples including the Gross-Neveu model and Chern-Simons-Matter CFTs.

and Line Defects

In contrast to the familiar correspondence between states on
