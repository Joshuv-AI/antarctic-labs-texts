Lower Bounds for the Total Variation Distance Given Means and Variances of Distributions
Tomohiro Nishiyama

Abstract
For arbitrary two probability measures on real d-space with given means and variances (covariance matrices), we provide lower bounds for their total variation distance. In the one-dimensional case, a tight bound is given.



I
Introduction

The
f
-divergence
[
14
]
is an important class of divergence measures, defined by means of convex functions
f
, includes many important divergences such as the total variation (TV) distance and the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence
[
9
]
. Given means and variances of two probability measures, closed-form lower bounds for the
f
-divergence are useful in practice because they can be directly evaluated based on only moments of distributions. These bounds are now beginning to be applied to nonequilibrium physics
[
7
,
15
,
5
]
.

For probability measures on real line, the tight lower bound on the
\chi^{2}
-divergence is known as the
Hammersley–Chapman–Robbins bound
[
1
,
6
]
. In our previous works, we provided tight lower bounds for the KL-divergence and the squared Hellinger distance
[
13
,
10
]
. We generalized these bounds for the asymmetric
\alpha
-divergence
[
2
]
with
\alpha\in[-1,2]

[
11
]
, which include the above three divergences. We also provided a sufficient condition that an arbitrary symmetric
f
-divergence has a closed-form expression for lower bound
[
12
]
.
Although the TV distance is a symmetric
f
-divergence, it does not satisfy this sufficient condition. On the other hand, for the TV distance between single Gaussians or Gaussian mixtures on real
d
-space, the closed-form expressions for lower bounds have recently been derived
[
3
,
4
]
.

In this work, we study closed-form lower bounds for the TV distance between arbitrary probability measures on real
d
-space with given means and variances (covariance matrices). In the one-dimensional case, we provide a tight lower bound.

II
Main Results

II-A

Preliminaries

We provide definitions which are used in this paper.

Definition 1
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability measures defined on a common measurable space
(\mathcal{A},\mathscr{F})
. Let
\mu
be a dominating measure
of
P
and
Q
(i.e.,
P,Q\ll\mu
), and let
p:=\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}\mu}

and
q:=\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}\mu}
be the densities of
P
and
Q
with respect
to
\mu
. The total variation (TV) distance between
P
and
Q
is given by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q):=\frac{1}{2}\int|p-q|\mathrm{d}\mu=\sup_{\mathcal{F}\in\mathscr{F}}|P(\mathcal{F})-Q(\mathcal{F})|.

Definition 2
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. Let
m_{P},m_{Q},\sigma_{P}^{2}
, and
\sigma_{Q}^{2}
denote the means and the variances of
X\sim P
and
Y\sim Q
, i.e.,

\displaystyle m_{P}:=\mathbb{E}[X],\;m_{Q}:=\mathbb{E}[Y],

\displaystyle\sigma_{P}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[(X-m_{P})^{2}],\;\sigma_{Q}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}[(Y-m_{Q})^{2}].

A set of pairs of probability measures
(P,Q)
with given means
(m_{P},m_{Q})
and variances
(\sigma_{P}^{2},\sigma_{Q}^{2})
is defined as
\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]
.
Similarly, a set of pairs of probability measures
(P,Q)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with given means
(\bm{m}_{P},\bm{m}_{Q})
and covariance matrices
(\Sigma_{P},\Sigma_{Q})
is defined as
\mathcal{P}[\bm{m}_{P},\Sigma_{P};\bm{m}_{Q},\Sigma_{Q}]
, where
\Sigma_{P}:=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{X}-\bm{m}_{P})(\bm{X}-\bm{m}_{P})^{T}]
and
\Sigma_{Q}:=\mathbb{E}[(\bm{Y}-\bm{m}_{Q})(\bm{Y}-\bm{m}_{Q})^{T}]
.

II-B

Lower bounds for the TV distance

Our main result is the following tight lower bound between a pair of arbitrary probability measures on real line with given means and variances.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]
.

(a)

If
m_{P}\neq m_{Q}
, then

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\geq\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}},

(1)

where
a:=m_{P}-m_{Q}
.

(b)

The lower bound on the right side of (
1
) is attained by a pair of two or three-element probability distributions as follows:

\displaystyle P=(1-p\;,p,\;0),\quad Q=(1-p,\;0,\;p),\quad\mbox{if}\hskip 4.26773pt\sigma_{P}>0\hskip 4.26773pt\mbox{and}\hskip 4.26773pt\sigma_{Q}>0,

\displaystyle P=(1-p\;,p),\quad Q=(1,\;0),\quad\mbox{if}\hskip 4.26773pt\sigma_{P}>0\hskip 4.26773pt\mbox{and}\hskip 4.26773pt\sigma_{Q}=0,

\displaystyle P=(1\;,0),\quad Q=(1-p,\;p),\quad\mbox{otherwise},

where

\displaystyle p:=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}\in(0,1].

(c)

If
m_{P}=m_{Q}
, then

\displaystyle\inf d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=0.

(2)

The infimum in (
2
) are taken over all
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]
.

Proof.

See Section
III
.
∎

Remark 1
.

For KL-divergence,
\chi^{2}
-divergence and squared Hellinger distance, a pair of probability measures defined on a common two-point set attains lower bounds with given means and variances. However, this does not hold for the TV distance when
\sigma_{P}
and
\sigma_{Q}
are positive.

We next provide a lower bound for probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
P
and
Q
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}[\bm{m}_{P},\Sigma_{P};\bm{m}_{Q},\Sigma_{Q}]
. Then,

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\geq\frac{\bm{a}^{T}\bm{a}}{2(\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{P})+\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma_{Q}))+\bm{a}^{T}\bm{a}},

(3)

where
\mathrm{tr}(A)
denotes the trace of a matrix
A
, and
\bm{a}:=\bm{m}_{P}-\bm{m}_{Q}
.

Proof.

Since the TV distance is invariant under transformation
\bm{x}\rightarrow\bm{x}-\frac{\bm{m}_{P}+\bm{m}_{Q}}{2}
, one can assume
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}[\frac{\bm{a}}{2},\Sigma_{P};-\frac{\bm{a}}{2},\Sigma_{Q}]
without any loss of generality.
By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle(\int|p-q||x_{k}|\mathrm{d}\mu)^{2}=(\int\sqrt{|p-q|}\sqrt{|p-q|}|x_{k}|\mathrm{d}\mu)^{2}\leq\int|p-q|\mathrm{d}\mu\int(p+q)x_{k}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\Bigl(({\Sigma_{P}})_{kk}^{2}+({\Sigma_{Q}})_{kk}^{2}+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}\Bigr).

(4)

By combining this inequality with
\int|p-q||x_{k}|\mathrm{d}\mu\geq|\int(p-q)x_{k}\mathrm{d}\mu|=|a_{k}|
, it follows that

\displaystyle 2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\Bigl(({\Sigma_{P}})_{kk}^{2}+({\Sigma_{Q}})_{kk}^{2}+\frac{a_{k}^{2}}{2}\Bigr)\geq a_{k}^{2}.

(5)

Taking the sum over
1\leq k\leq d
for this inequality yields (
3
).
∎

III
Proof of Theorem
1

III-A

Proofs of Lemmas

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
be a set of pairs of probability measures defined on a common
n
-point set
\{x_{1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n}\}
, where
\{x_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
are arbitrary real numbers. Let
\mathcal{P}_{3}^{*}\subset\mathcal{P}_{3}
be a set of pairs of probability measures
P
and
Q
such that
P=(1-p,p,0)
and
Q=(1-p,0,p)
for
p\in[0,1]
.
Before proving Theorem
1
, we prove the following lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\sigma_{P}
and
\sigma_{Q}
be positive, and let
m_{P}\neq m_{Q}
. Then,

\displaystyle\min_{(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}_{3}^{*}\cap\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]}d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}.

(6)

Proof.

The moment constraints reduce to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&(1-p)x_{1}+px_{2}=m_{P},\\
&(1-p)x_{1}^{2}+px_{2}^{2}=m_{P}^{2}+\sigma_{P}^{2},\\
&(1-p)x_{1}+px_{3}=m_{Q},\\
&(1-p)x_{1}^{2}+px_{3}^{2}=m_{Q}^{2}+\sigma_{Q}^{2},\end{cases}

(7)

where
p:=P(x_{2})=Q(x_{3})
.
Subtracting the square of the first equation in (
7
) from its second equation, we have

\displaystyle x_{1}-x_{2}=\pm\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{1}{p(1-p)}}.

(8)

We first consider the first option in (
8
). Solving simultaneously this relation and the first equation in (
7
), we obtain

\displaystyle x_{1}=m_{P}+\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}},

(9)

\displaystyle x_{2}=m_{P}-\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}}.

(10)

Similarly, from the third and the forth equation in (
7
), we obtain

\displaystyle x_{1}=m_{Q}\mp\sigma_{Q}\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}},

(11)

\displaystyle x_{3}=m_{Q}\pm\sigma_{Q}\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}}.

(12)

By combining (
9
) with the first option in (
11
), we have

\displaystyle-\frac{a}{\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q}}=\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}},\quad a<0.

(13)

Solving this equation for
p
, we have

\displaystyle p=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}\in(0,1).

(14)

It can be verified that
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
, and
x_{3}
are different from each other from (
9
)-(
12
).
Since
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=p
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}.

(15)

Similarly, by combining (
9
) with the second option in (
11
), we have

\displaystyle p=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}-\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}\in(0,1],\quad\frac{a}{\sigma_{P}-\sigma_{Q}}<0.

(16)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}-\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}.

(17)

We next consider the second option in (
8
). By replacing
\sigma_{P}\rightarrow-\sigma_{P}
in (
15
) and (
17
), we obtain the same results for
a>0
and
\frac{a}{\sigma_{P}-\sigma_{Q}}>0
, respectively. One can verify that these solutions satisfy (
7
).
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
m_{P}\neq m_{Q}
. Then, a set
\mathcal{P}_{2}\cap\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]
contains one component
(P,Q)
, and

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\frac{a^{2}}{v},

(18)

where

\displaystyle v:=\sqrt{(\sigma_{Q}^{2}-\sigma_{P}^{2})^{2}+2a^{2}(\sigma_{P}^{2}+\sigma_{Q}^{2})+a^{4}}.

(19)

Proof.

The moment constraints reduce to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&(1-p)x_{1}+px_{2}=m_{P},\\
&(1-p)x_{1}^{2}+px_{2}^{2}=m_{P}^{2}+\sigma_{P}^{2},\\
&(1-q)x_{1}+qx_{2}=m_{Q},\\
&(1-q)x_{1}^{2}+qx_{2}^{2}=m_{Q}^{2}+\sigma_{Q}^{2},\end{cases}

(20)

where
p:=P(x_{2})
and
q:=Q(x_{2})
.
In the similar way to the proof of Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle x_{1}=m_{P}+\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}},

(21)

\displaystyle x_{2}=m_{P}-\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}}.

(22)

It should be noted that an another solution
x_{1}=m_{P}-\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{p}{1-p}}
,
x_{2}=m_{P}+\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{1-p}{p}}
corresponds to switching
(p,q,x_{1})
and
(1-p,1-q,x_{2})
, and it gives the same probability measure.
From these equations, we obtain

\displaystyle x_{1}-x_{2}=\sigma_{P}\sqrt{\frac{1}{p(1-p)}}.

(23)

Subtracting the third equation in (
20
) from its first equation, and subtracting the forth equation in (
20
) from its second equation, we have

\displaystyle(p-q)(x_{2}-x_{1})

\displaystyle=a,

(24)

\displaystyle(p-q)(x_{2}-x_{1})(x_{1}+x_{2})

\displaystyle=a(x_{1}+x_{2})=a(m_{P}+m_{Q})+\sigma_{P}^{2}-\sigma_{Q}^{2}.

(25)

By substituting (
21
) and (
22
) into (
25
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{2p-1}{\sqrt{p(1-p)}}=\frac{\sigma_{P}^{2}-\sigma_{Q}^{2}-a^{2}}{a\sigma_{P}}.

(26)

Solving this equation for
p
gives

\displaystyle p=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{|a|}{a}\frac{(\sigma_{P}^{2}-\sigma_{Q}^{2}-a^{2})}{2v}\in[0,1].

(27)

Substituting (
23
) and (
27
) into (
24
), we obtain

\displaystyle q=p+\frac{a|a|}{v}\in[0,1].

(28)

The TV distance between
P
and
Q
is given by

\displaystyle d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=|p-q|=\frac{a^{2}}{v}.

(29)

Hence, we obtain (
18
).
∎

Lemma 3
.

For
R>0
, let
\mathcal{P}_{n,R}\subset\mathcal{P}_{n}
be a set of pairs of probability measures such that
|x_{i}|\leq R
for all
i=1,2,\cdots,n
.
Let
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}_{n,R}\cap\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]
. If
m_{P}\neq m_{Q}
, the global minimum point
(P^{*},Q^{*},x^{*})=\mathrm{argmin}\;d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)
satisfies any one of the following conditions.

•

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P^{*},Q^{*})=\frac{a^{2}}{(\sigma_{P}+\sigma_{Q})^{2}+a^{2}}
and
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x^{*}_{i}|<R
.

When
\sigma_{P}>0
and
\sigma_{Q}>0
,
(P^{*},Q^{*})\in\mathcal{P}_{3}^{*}
and otherwise,
(P^{*},Q^{*})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

•

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x^{*}_{i}|=R
.

Proof.

Let
\sigma_{P}
or
\sigma_{Q}
is positive since the case when
\sigma_{P}=\sigma_{Q}=0
is trivial.
Consider the following minimization problem.

\displaystyle\mbox{minimize}\quad 2d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)=\sum_{i}|p_{i}-q_{i}|

subject to

\displaystyle g_{k}(p,x):=\sum_{i}p_{i}x_{i}^{k-1}-A_{k}=0,

\displaystyle g_{k+3}(q,x):=\sum_{i}q_{i}x_{i}^{k-1}-B_{k}=0,\quad\mbox{for}\hskip 4.26773ptk=1,2,3,

\displaystyle 0\leq p_{i}\leq 1,\quad 0\leq q_{i}\leq 1,\quad|x_{i}|\leq R,\quad\mbox{for}\hskip 4.26773pt1\leq i\leq n,

where
A:=(1,m_{P},m_{P}^{2}+\sigma_{P}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}
and
B:=(1,m_{Q},m_{Q}^{2}+\sigma_{Q}^{2})^{\mathrm{T}}
. Since the feasible region is compact and the objective function is continuous, there exists a global minimum. In the following, we prove the case when
\max_{1\leq i\leq n}|x^{*}_{i}|<R
.

We first consider the case when
\sigma_{P}>0
and
\sigma_{Q}>0
, then we have
p_{i},q_{i}<1
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. For the global minimum point
(P^{*},Q^{*},x^{*})
, we define sets of subscripts as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&N_{p,q}:=\{i\hskip 4.26773pt|\hskip 4.26773ptp^{*}_{i}\neq q^{*}_{i},\;p^{*}_{i}>0,\;q^{*}_{i}>0\},\\
&N_{p=q}:=\{i\hskip 4.26773pt|\hskip 4.26773ptp^{*}_{i}=q^{*}_{i}>0\},\\
&N_{q=0}:=\{i\hskip 4.26773pt|\hskip 4.26773ptp^{*}_{i}>0,\;q^{*}_{i}=0\},\\
&N_{p=0}:=\{i\hskip 4.26773pt|\hskip 4.26773ptp^{*}_{i}=0,\;q^{*}_{i}>0\},\end{cases}

where
N_{p,q}\bigsqcup N_{p=q}\bigsqcup N_{q=0}\bigsqcup N_{p=0}=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. Since the case when
|N_{p,q}|=|N_{p=q}|=0
must not be minimum, we suppose that
|N_{p,q}|
or
|N_{p=q}|
is positive in the following. For sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, the point
(P^{*},Q^{*},x^{*})
is a global minimum in a region such that
p_{i}\in[p^{*}_{i}-\epsilon,p^{*}_{i}+\epsilon]
for all

, and
q_{j}\in[q^{*}_{j}-\epsilon,q^{*}_{j}+\epsilon]
for all
j\in N_{p,q}\bigsqcup N_{p=0}
.
For constraints
\{g_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq 6}
, we provide the following Lemma. The proof is shown in Appendix
A
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
(P,Q)\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\cap\mathcal{P}[m_{P},\sigma_{P};m_{Q},\sigma_{Q}]
for
m_{P}\neq m_{Q}
,
\sigma_{P}>0
, and
\sigma_{Q}>0
. Then,
\{\nabla_{p,q,x}g_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq 6}
are linearly independent.

From this lemma, the point
(P^{*},Q^{*},x^{*})
must be a stationary point of the following Lagrangian.

\displaystyle L(p,q,x,\lambda,\nu):=\sum_{i\in N_{p,q}}s_{i}(p_{i}-q_{i})+\sum_{i\in N_{q=0}}p_{i}+\sum_{i\in N_{p=0}}q_{i}-\sum_{i\in N_{p,q}\bigsqcup N_{q=0}}p_{i}\phi_{\lambda}(x_{i})

(30)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\in N_{p,q}\bigsqcup N_{p=0}}q_{i}\psi_{\nu}(x_{i})-\sum_{i\in N_{p=q}}p_{i}(\phi_{\lambda}(x_{i})+\psi_{\nu}(x_{i}))+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\lambda_{k}A_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{3}\nu_{k}B_{k},

where

or
-1

At a stationary point, all partial derivatives of the Lagrangian with respect to

\displaystyle\phi(x^{*}_{i})-s_{i}=\psi(x^{*}_{i})+s_{i}=0,

\displaystyle\phi(x^{*}_{i})+\psi(x^{*}_{i})=0.

(34)

\displaystyle\phi(x)+\psi(x)=\alpha_{i}(x-x^{*}_{i})^{2}.

(35)

\displaystyle\phi(x)=\beta_{i}(x-x^{*}_{i})^{2}+1.

(36)

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\gamma_{i}(x-x^{*}_{i})^{2}+1,
