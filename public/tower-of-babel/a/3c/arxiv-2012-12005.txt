The dynamical Schrödinger problem in abstract metric spaces
Léonard Monsaingeon, Luca Tamanini, Dmitry Vorotnikov

Abstract
In this paper we introduce the dynamical Schrödinger problem, defined for a wide class of entropy and Fisher information functionals, as a geometric problem on abstract metric spaces. Under very mild assumptions we prove a generic  \Gamma -convergence result towards the geodesic problem as the noise parameter ε ↓ 0 \varepsilon\downarrow 0 . We also study the dependence of the entropic cost on the parameter ε \varepsilon . Some examples and applications are discussed.



MSC [2020] 49J45, 49Q20, 58B20.

Keywords: gradient flows, metric geometry, optimal transport,
Fisher information, Gamma-convergence

Introduction

Gaspard Monge and Erwin Schrödinger came up with two a priori unrelated problems that are concerned with finding a preeminent way of deforming a prescribed probability distribution into another one. While Monge was interested in optimizing the cost of transportation of goods
[
64
,
65
,
59
]
, Schrödinger’s original thought experiment
[
60
,
61
]
aimed for finding the most likely evolution between two subsequent observations of a cloud of independent particles.
So, even if in both cases we are facing an interpolation and optimization problem, the former is deterministic in nature whereas the latter is strongly related to large deviations theory, and is, at the first glance, purely stochastic. We refer to a recent survey
[
22
]
for various formulations and aspects of the Schrödinger problem, and to
[
66
,
67
]
for a discussion of its role in Euclidean Quantum Mechanics.

Anyway, several analogies and connections exist between the two problems.
They can be appreciated by looking carefully at the interpolation aspects of both problems, namely at their dynamical formulations and the underlying equations governing the respective evolutions.
In the case of a quadratic transportation cost over a Riemannian manifold
M
, the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport problem is solved (at least in a weak sense) by interpolating between the source and the target distributions with a constant-speed, length-minimizing geodesic in the Otto-Wasserstein space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)
.
This gives a curve
(\mu_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}\subset\mathcal{P}_{2}(M)
which formally satisfies (in the sense of the celebrated Otto calculus
[
56
,
64
,
65
]
)

\nabla_{\dot{\mu}_{t}}\dot{\mu}_{t}=0,

(1.1)

where
\nabla_{\dot{\mu}_{t}}
is the covariant derivative along the curve
t\mapsto\mu_{t}
.
The Schrödinger problem with parameter
\varepsilon
(from a physical viewpoint,
\varepsilon
can be seen as a temperature or level of noise) can also be translated into such a geometric language.
By analogy, when looking at the covariant derivative along the optimal evolution
(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\in[0,1]}
, usually called Schrödinger bridge or entropic interpolation, the resulting equation is surprising and can be viewed
[
23
]
as Newton’s second law

\nabla_{\dot{\mu}_{t}^{\varepsilon}}\dot{\mu}_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\nabla I(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon}),

(1.2)

where in the right-hand side
\nabla
denotes the gradient in the Otto-Wasserstein pseudo-Riemannian sense and
I
is the Fisher information

I(\mu)=4\intop\nolimits_{M}|\nabla\sqrt{\rho}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{vol}=\intop\nolimits_{M}|\nabla\log\rho|^{2}\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{vol}

provided
\mu=\rho\cdot\mathrm{vol}
.
A related observation is that the (scaled) heat flow, coinciding with the (scaled) gradient flow of the Boltzmann-Shannon entropy
[
41
,
65
]

H(\mu)=\intop\nolimits_{M}\rho\log\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{vol}

for
\mu=\rho\cdot\mathrm{vol}
, is also a solution to (
1.2
): a simple differentiation in time of
\dot{\mu}_{t}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla H(\mu_{t})
and the fact that
I=|\nabla H|^{2}
in the Otto-Wasserstein sense automatically yield

\nabla_{\dot{\mu}_{t}}\dot{\mu}_{t}=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla^{2}H(\mu_{t})\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\nabla H(\mu_{t})=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\nabla|\nabla H(\mu_{t})|^{2}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\nabla I(\mu_{t}).

This shows that the Schrödinger problem lies between optimal transport and diffusion and is naturally intertwined with both deterministic behaviour and Brownian motion. It shares the same Newton’s law as the gradient flow of the entropy, but unlike the heat flow it has a prescribed final configuration to match:
it is up to the parameter
\varepsilon
to tip the balance in favour of deterministic transport or diffusion.
With this heuristics in mind, we see that as
\varepsilon\to 0
the applied force
\varepsilon^{2}\nabla I(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})
in (
1.2
) vanishes, so that the Schrödinger problem may be interpreted as a noisy (entropic) counterpart of the Monge-Kantorovich optimal transport, corresponding to the unforced geodesic evolution (
1.1
) discussed above.
This informal relationship has a rigorous counterpart, which dates back to the pioneering works on the asymptotic behavior of the Schrödinger problem as
\varepsilon\to 0
of T. Mikami, M. Thieullen
[
51
,
52
]
, and C. Léonard
[
45
,
46
]
.
This was subsequently developed in
[
19
,
8
,
39
]
.
Very recently
[
48
,
31
]
, similar small-noise results were obtained for static Monge-Kantorovich problems regularized with more general entropies.

This first connection can be investigated further and by doing so one can remark that (
1.2
) is exactly the Euler-Lagrange optimality equation for the dynamical Benamou-Brenier formulation of the Schrödinger problem
[
18
,
22
,
46
,
40
]
, which consists in minimizing the Lagrangian kinetic action perturbed by the Fisher information:
In more precise terms,

\inf\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\intop\nolimits_{M}|v_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}{\mathrm{d}}t+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}I(\mu_{t})\,{\mathrm{d}}t\bigg\},

(1.3)

where the infimum runs over all solutions of the continuity equation

\partial\mu_{t}+\mathrm{div}(v_{t}\mu_{t})=0

with prescribed initial and final densities.
Also from this variational standpoint the reader can see that as
\varepsilon\to 0
the Schrödinger problem formally reduces to

\inf\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\intop\nolimits_{M}|v_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu_{t}{\mathrm{d}}t,

(1.4)

namely to the dynamical Benamou-Brenier formulation of the (quadratic) optimal transport problem
[
9
]
. This variational representation depicts in a way clearer than (
1.2
) the double nature of the Schrödinger problem, the competition between the determinism encoded in the kinetic energy and the unpredictability coming from the Fisher information, and the role played by
\varepsilon
in balancing these two opposite behaviours.

The double bond of the Schrödinger problem with optimal transport on the one hand and heat flow on the other hand results in fruitful and wide-ranging applications of both theoretical and applied interest.
Indeed, from the connection with the heat flow the solutions to the Schrödinger problem gain regularity properties which are not available in optimal transport, and thanks to the asymptotic behaviour of the Schrödinger problem as
\varepsilon\to 0
entropic interpolations represent an efficient way to approximate Wasserstein geodesics with second-order accuracy
[
39
,
25
]
.
This approach has already turned out to be successful in conjunction with functional inequalities
[
24
,
35
]
and differential calculus along Wasserstein geodesics
[
39
]
.
But the nice behaviour of Schrödinger bridges is important also for computational purposes.
The impact of Schrödinger problem and Sinkhorn algorithms (deeply related to the static formulation of the former) on the numerical methods used in optimal transportation theory has been impressive, as witnessed by several recent works (see
[
58
]
and references therein as well as
[
26
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
11
,
30
]
).

As a matter of fact neither the particular structure of the Wasserstein space nor the specific choice of the Boltzmann-Shannon functional are required to define the two problems in question (cf. a related discussion in the heuristic paper
[
44
]
): one can of course define length-minimizing geodesics in any metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
, and the Schrödinger problem (or at least its Benamou-Brenier formulation described above) merely involves an entropy functional and a corresponding Fisher information.
Given such a reasonable entropy functional
{\sf E}
on
{\rm X}
that generates a gradient flow in a suitable sense, the corresponding Fisher information is expected to be nothing but the dissipation rate of
{\sf E}
(along solutions of its own gradient flow), just as
I
coincides with the rate of dissipation of the entropy
H
along the heat flow.
This observation is the starting point of the present paper, where we intend to study the abstract Schrödinger bridge problem or, in other words, the entropic approximation of geodesics in metric spaces.

The first main result that we achieve is the
\Gamma
-convergence (Theorems
4.3
and
4.6
)
. Under very mild assumptions on
{\rm X}
and
{\sf E}
, we will prove the solvability of the abstract
\varepsilon
-Schrödinger problem and the
\Gamma
-convergence to the corresponding geodesic problem as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
We will also rigorously justify, in the metric setting, that any trajectory of a gradient flow solves an associated Schrödinger problem (Proposition
4.7
).
Leveraging a quantitative
AC^{2}
estimate based on a straightforward chain-rule in the smooth Riemannian setting, the cornerstone of our analysis will be the systematic construction of an
\varepsilon
-regularized entropic copy
(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
of any arbitrary curve
(\gamma_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
.
These perturbed curves will provide recovery sequences for the
\Gamma
-convergence.
Our construction is completely Eulerian and essentially consists in running the
{\sf E}
-gradient flow for a short time
h_{\varepsilon}(t)
starting at
\gamma_{t}
for all
t
, for well-chosen functions
h_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
.
The challenge here will be to reproduce the (formal, differential) Riemannian chain-rule in metric spaces.
This idea of perturbing curves “in the direction of the gradient flow” appeared first in
[
29
]
in a slightly different context.
Notably and more recently, this approach has also been used independently by A. Baradat and some of the authors
[
8
,
54
]
in order to prove the
\Gamma
-convergence for the classical dynamical Schrödinger problem on the Otto-Wasserstein space and for its counterpart on the non-commutative Fisher-Rao space, respectively.
However, in those papers the computations were
ad hoc
and heavily exploited the underlying structures of the particular spaces as well as the properties of the particular gradient flows (namely, of the classical heat flow and of its restriction to multivariate Gaussians), whereas here we derive everything from the existence of an abstract gradient flow on
{\rm X}
driven by
{\sf E}
.

Remark 1.1
.

In the smooth Riemannian setting, and given
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, elementary calculus shows that the
\lambda
-convexity of
{\sf E}
along geodesics is of course equivalent to a uniform lower bound
\mathrm{Hess}\,{\sf E}(x)\geq\lambda\operatorname{Id}
as quadratic forms in the tangent space, but also more importantly to the
\lambda
-contractivity of the
{\sf E}
-gradient flow.
In the metric setting no second order calculus is available in general, and the very notion of gradient flow as well as its connection with geodesic convexity and contractivity become much more subtle.
The key notion of gradient flow that we shall use throughout is that of
Evolution Variational Inequality
, or
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
flow
[
2
]
.
Under reasonable assumptions it is well known that (a suitable variant of) convexity of
{\sf E}
generally provides existence of an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-flow starting at any
x\in{\rm X}
, see
[
2
]
.
A natural question to ask is whether the converse also holds true, i.e. whether well-posedness of a reasonable gradient flow implies some convexity.
This was proved in
[
15
]
for the specific case of the Euclidean Wasserstein space
{\rm X}=W_{2}(\Omega)
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and at least for the so-called internal energies, and it is shown therein that
0
-contractivity of the gradient flow (or equivalently, of the associated nonlinear diffusion equation) implies
0
-displacement convexity in the sense of McCann
[
50
]
.
In the same spirit, and building up on Otto and Westdickenberg
[
57
]
, Daneri and Savaré proved in a very general metric setting that the generation of an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-flow indeed implies
\lambda
-geodesic convexity
[
27
, Theorem 3.2]
.
A byproduct of our analysis for the
\Gamma
-convergence will give a new independent proof of this latter fact by a completely different approach, essentially by constructing an
\varepsilon
-entropic regularization of geodesics and carefully examining the defect of optimality at order one in
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The second group of main results has to do with the
behaviour of the cost and its Taylor expansion (Proposition
5.5
and Theorem
5.8
).
As a main application of the
\Gamma
-convergence of the Schrödinger problem to the geodesic problem as
\varepsilon\to 0
(and more generally of the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-Schrödinger problem to the
\varepsilon
-one as
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon
) we investigate the behaviour of the optimal value of the dynamical Schrödinger problem, henceforth called
entropic cost
, as a function of the temperature parameter
\varepsilon
, with particular emphasis on the regularity and the small-noise regime.
For the classical dynamical Schrödinger problem (
1.3
), it has recently been proved by the second author with G. Conforti
[
25
]
that the entropic cost is of class
C^{1}((0,\infty))\cap C([0,\infty))
(actually
C^{1}([0,\infty))
under suitable assumptions) and twice a.e. differentiable; once this regularity information is available, the formula for the first derivative is rather easy to guess, as by the envelope theorem it coincides with the partial derivative w.r.t.
\varepsilon
of the functional in (
1.3
) evaluated at any critical point. Denoting by
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)
the value in (
1.3
) with marginal constraints
\mu
and
\nu
and by
(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\in[0,1]}
the associated Schrödinger bridge, this statement reads as

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}I(\mu_{t}^{\varepsilon})\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0

and in
[
25
]
this identity played an important role in the study of both the large- and small-noise behaviour of the Schrödinger problem, obtaining in particular a Taylor expansion around
\varepsilon=0
with
o(\varepsilon^{2})
-accuracy.
Since the central object in the present paper is an abstract and general formulation of (
1.3
), an analogous result is expected to hold. However, from a technical viewpoint the proof is much more subtle and challenging, because unlike (
1.3
) our metric version of the dynamical Schrödinger problem may have multiple solutions.
For this reason the discussion about the regularity of the entropic cost in this paper is less concise than in
[
25
]
.
Nonetheless, we are still able to deduce the same kind of Taylor expansion with the same accuracy.
Given the previous interpretation of the Schrödinger problem as a noisy Monge-Kantorovich problem and the importance of quantitative estimates in approximating optimal transport by means of the Schrödinger problem, it is reasonable to expect that such a Taylor expansion (valid in a general framework for a wide choice of functionals
{\sf E}
) will fit to a countless variety of examples, some of which will be discussed here.

Structure of the paper.

In Section
2
we give a short and formal proof of our fundamental
AC^{2}
estimate in the smooth Riemannian setting, and show how it can be exploited to establish
\Gamma
-convergence and convexity.
Section
3
fixes the metric framework in which we work for the rest of the paper, and extends the previous estimate to this metric setting.
In Section
4
we prove the
\Gamma
-convergence as

, and offer a new proof of the geodesic convexity of the generators of

Section

Finally, we list in Section

Heuristics

Here we remain formal and the computations are carried in a Riemannian setting, where classical calculus and chain-rules are available.
(Significant work will be required later on to adapt the computations in metric spaces.)
All the objects and functions in this section are therefore considered to be smooth, and we deliberately ignore any regularity issue.

(In section

\mbox{s.t. }q\in C([0,1],M)\mbox{ with endpoints }q_{0},q_{1}.

For

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\Phi(s,q_{0})=-\nabla V(\Phi(s,q_{0}))},\\

(ii)

2.1
Convergence of the Schrödinger problem

A first use of (
2.5
) will be crucial in proving the
\Gamma
-convergence of the Schrödinger functional

\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(q):=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\nabla V(q_{t})|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}

towards the kinetic action

\mathcal{A}(q):=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}

as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Theorem 2.1

(formal
\Gamma
-limit)
.

For any
q_{0},q_{1}\in M
it holds

\mathcal{A}=\Gamma-\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}

for the uniform convergence on the space of curves with fixed endpoints
q_{0},q_{1}
.

Proof.

We check separately the
\Gamma-\liminf
and the
\Gamma-\limsup
properties. As for the former, given any curve
q
joining
q_{0},q_{1}
and any
q^{\varepsilon}\to q
uniformly, since the kinetic energy functional
q\mapsto\mathcal{A}(q)
is always lower semicontinuous for the uniform convergence we get first

\mathcal{A}(q)\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathcal{A}(q^{\varepsilon})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(q^{\varepsilon}).

For the
\Gamma-\limsup
, let
H(t)=\min\{t,1-t\}
be the hat function centered at
t=1/2
with height
1/2
and vanishing at the boundaries, set
h(t)=\varepsilon H(t)
, and let
q^{\varepsilon}
be the regularized curve constructed in (
2.6
). In this simple smooth setting it is not difficult to check that
q^{\varepsilon}\to q
uniformly. Moreover, our choice of
h(t)
results in
|h^{\prime}(t)|^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}
with
h^{\prime}(0)=\varepsilon
,
h^{\prime}(1)=-\varepsilon
, and
h^{\prime\prime}(t)=-2\varepsilon\delta_{1/2}(t)
in the distributional sense. Therefore (
2.5
) gives immediately

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(q^{\varepsilon})+2\varepsilon V(q^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})&=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q^{\varepsilon}_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\nabla V(q^{\varepsilon}_{t})|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+2\varepsilon V(q^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda H(t)}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\varepsilon\Big(V(q_{0})+V(q_{1})\Big).\end{split}

The singularity of

at

can be easily and rigorously worked around, simply integrating by parts (
2.2
) separately on each interval
t\in[0,1/2]
and

and keeping track of the boundary terms resulting ultimately in the above

Discarding this latter non-negative term finally gives

\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(q^{\varepsilon})\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda H(t)}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\varepsilon\Big(V(q_{0})+V(q_{1})\Big)\right\}=\mathcal{A}(q)

and concludes the proof.

2.2
Quantifying the convexity

The second consequence of our fundamental estimate (
2.5
) is the quantification of the convexity of the potential
V
in terms of the quantified contractivity (
2.3
).
The point here is that the result can be obtained directly from (
2.2
), which can be established in a purely metric setting without relying on differential calculus (see the next section for details).

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
V
satisfies
2.3
.
Then
V
is
\lambda
-geodesically convex, i.e.

V(q_{\theta})\leq(1-\theta)V(q_{0})+\theta V(q_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\theta(1-\theta)d^{2}(q_{0},q_{1}),\qquad\theta\in(0,1)

for
any
geodesic
(q_{\theta})_{\theta\in[0,1]}
in
M
.

Proof.

Let
(q_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
be an arbitrary geodesic with endpoints
q_{0},q_{1}
.
For fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
let

H_{\theta}(t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\theta}}t&\mbox{if }t\in[0,\theta],\\
-\displaystyle{\frac{1}{1-\theta}(t-1)}&\mbox{if }t\in[\theta,1],\end{cases}

be the hat function centered at
t=\theta
with height
1
and vanishing at
t=0,1
, and for any
\varepsilon>0
let
q^{\varepsilon}
be the regularized curve constructed in (
2.6
) with
h(t)=\varepsilon H_{\theta}(t)
. Note moreover that

h^{\prime}(0)=\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta},\qquad h^{\prime}(1)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\theta},\qquad h^{\prime\prime}(t)=-\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\theta}+\frac{1}{1-\theta}\right)\delta_{\theta}(t)

in the distributional sense. Discarding the non-negative term
|h^{\prime}(t)|^{2}|\nabla V(\tilde{q}_{t})|^{2}
in (
2.5
), the optimality of the geodesic
q
from
q_{0}
to
q_{1}
gives

\begin{split}0&\leq\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q^{\varepsilon}_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}-\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:fundamental}}{\leq}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}h^{\prime\prime}(t)V(q^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left(e^{-2\lambda h(t)}-1\right)\left|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}\right|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\Big(h^{\prime}(0)V(q_{0})-h^{\prime}(1)V(q_{1})\Big)\\
&=-\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\theta}+\frac{1}{1-\theta}\right)V(q^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})+\frac{d^{2}(q_{0},q_{1})}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left(e^{-2\lambda\varepsilon H_{\theta}(t)}-1\right){{\mathrm{d}}t}\\
&\qquad+\varepsilon\left(\frac{1}{\theta}V(q_{0})+\frac{1}{1-\theta}V(q_{1})\right),\end{split}

where the last equality follows from the constant speed property
|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}q_{t}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}|^{2}=d^{2}(q_{0},q_{1})
of the geodesic
(q_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
connecting
q_{0},q_{1}
as well as from the explicit properties of
h(t)=\varepsilon H_{\theta}(t)
listed above.
Multiplying by
\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{\varepsilon}>0
and rearranging gives

V(q^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})\leq(1-\theta)V(q_{0})+\theta V(q_{1})+\theta(1-\theta)\frac{d^{2}(q_{0},q_{1})}{2}\underbrace{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{-2\lambda\varepsilon H_{\theta}(t)}-1}{\varepsilon}{{\mathrm{d}}t}}_{:=I_{\varepsilon}}.

Since
\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}H_{\theta}(t){\mathrm{d}}t=\frac{1}{2}
for all
\theta
we see that
I_{\varepsilon}\to-2\lambda\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}H_{\theta}(t)\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-\lambda
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, and the result immediately follows since
V(q^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})\to V(q_{\theta})
as well in the left-hand side.
∎

3
Estimates in metric spaces

Before trying to adapt the previous computations to the metric context we need to fix once and for all the framework to be used in the sequel.

3.1
Preliminaries and setting

•

By
C([0,1],({\rm X},{\sf d}))
, or simply
C([0,1],{\rm X})
, we denote the space of continuous curves with values in the metric space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
.
The collection of absolutely continuous curves on
[0,1]
is denoted by
AC([0,1],({\rm X},{\sf d}))
, or simply by
AC([0,1],{\rm X})
.
For any curve
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
, its length is well defined as

\ell(\gamma):=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\,{\mathrm{d}}t,

where
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
denotes the metric speed of
\gamma
.
If
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\in L^{2}(0,1)
, then we shall say that
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
.
For these notions of absolutely continuous curves and metric speed in a metric space, see for instance
[
2
, Section 1.1]
.

•

A curve
\gamma:[0,1]\to{\rm X}
is called geodesic provided
{\sf d}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{s})=|t-s|{\sf d}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
for all
t,s\in[0,1]
.

•

The slope
|\partial{\sf E}|
of a functional
{\sf E}:{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
at a point
x\in{\rm X}
is set as
+\infty
if
x\notin D({\sf E})
,
0
if
x
is isolated, and defined as

|\partial{\sf E}|(x):=\limsup_{y\to x}\frac{[{\sf E}(x)-{\sf E}(y)]^{+}}{{\sf d}(x,y)}

if
x\in D({\sf E})
.

•

A curve
(\gamma_{t})_{t>0}\subset{\rm X}
is said to be a gradient flow of
{\sf E}
in the
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
sense (with
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
) provided
(\gamma_{t})\in AC_{loc}((0,\infty),{\rm X})
and

\frac{1}{2}{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{t},y)+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{t},y)+{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})\leq{\sf E}(y),\qquad\forall y\in{\rm X},\,\textrm{a.e. }t>0.

(
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
)

If
\gamma_{t}\to x
as
t\downarrow 0
with
x\in\overline{D({\sf E})}
, then we say that the gradient flow
(\gamma_{t})
starts at
x
.

After this premise, let us fix the framework we shall work within.

Setting 3.1
.

On the space
({\rm X},{\sf d})
and on the functional
{\sf E}:{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
we make the following assumptions:

(A1)

({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a complete and separable metric space;

(A2)

{\sf E}
is lower semicontinuous with dense domain, i.e.
\overline{D({\sf E})}={\rm X}
, and locally bounded from below in the following sense: for any
{\sf d}
-bounded set
B\subset{\rm X}
there exists
c_{B}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
{\sf E}(x)\geq c_{B}
for all
x\in B
;

(A3)

there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
x\in{\rm X}
there exists an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow of
{\sf E}
starting from
x
.
In view of (
3.3
), the corresponding 1-parameter semigroup shall be denoted
\mathsf{S}_{t}
.

Sometimes, and always explicitly indicated, we will also use the following extra hypothesis.

Assumption 3.2
.

There exists a Hausdorff topology
\sigma
on
{\rm X}
such that
{\sf d}
-bounded sets are sequentially
\sigma
-compact.
Moreover, the distance
{\sf d}
and the slope
|\partial{\sf E}|
are
\sigma
-sequentially lower semicontinuous.

Remark 3.3
.

Assumption
3.2
is in particular valid provided
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a locally compact space.
Indeed, in this case the metric topology of
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is an admissible candidate for
\sigma
, since bounded sets are relatively compact (by
[
17
, Proposition 2.5.22]
) and the lower semicontinuity of the slope
|\partial{\sf E}|
w.r.t. the metric topology is a consequence of the forthcoming identity (
3.1
).

\blacksquare

Remark 3.4
.

Assumption
3.2
implies that
{\sf d}
-converging sequences are also
\sigma
-converging.
Indeed, given
(x_{n})\subset{\rm X}
with
{\sf d}(x,x_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for some limit
x\in{\rm X}
, by Assumption
3.2
and by the boundedness of
(x_{n})_{n}
there exist a subsequence
(x_{n_{k}})_{k}
and
y\in{\rm X}
such that

x_{n_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}y\quad\textrm{as}\quad k\to\infty.

Since
{\sf d}
is
\sigma
-sequentially lower semicontinuous (again by Assumption
3.2
) we deduce that

{\sf d}(x,y)\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}{\sf d}(x,x_{n_{k}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\sf d}(x,x_{n})=0,

whence
x=y
.
This classically implies that the whole sequence converges,
x_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}x
.

\blacksquare

We list now some useful properties of
{\rm EVI}
-gradient flows, which hold true in Setting
3.1
and that we shall use extensively in the sequel.
First of all, whenever
x\in X
is the starting point of an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
flow, the slope there (a
local
object, a priori) admits the
global
representation

|\partial{\sf E}|(x)=\sup_{y\neq x}\Big(\frac{{\sf E}(x)-{\sf E}(y)}{{\sf d}(x,y)}+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\sf d}(x,y)\Big)^{+},

(3.1)

see
[
55
, Proposition 3.6]
.
Since we assume that any
x\in X
is the starting point of an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow, this means in particular that
|\partial{\sf E}|:X\to[0,\infty]
is lower semicontinuous, since so is the right-hand side above (as a supremum of lower semicontinuous functions).
This also implies by
[
2
, Theorem 1.2.5]
that
|\partial{\sf E}|
is a
strong upper gradient
for
{\sf E}
in the sense of
[
2
, Definition 1.2.1]
, namely: for every
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
, the map
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})
is Borel and

|{\sf E}(\gamma_{t_{1}})-{\sf E}(\gamma_{t_{0}})|\leq\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t})|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad\forall 0\leq t_{0}\leq t_{1}\leq 1,

(3.2)

the right-hand side being possibly infinite.
In addition, if
(\gamma_{t})
is an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow of
{\sf E}
then the following hold
[
55
, Theorem 3.5]
:

(i)

If
(\gamma_{t})
starts from
x\in\overline{D({\sf E})}
and
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
is a second
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow of
{\sf E}
starting from
y\in\overline{D({\sf E})}
respectively, then

{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{t},\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\leq e^{-2\lambda t}{\sf d}^{2}(x,y),\qquad\forall t\geq 0.

(3.3)

This means that EVI-gradient flows are unique (provided they exist) and thus if there exists an EVI-gradient flow
(\gamma_{t})
starting from
x
, then a 1-parameter semigroup
(\mathsf{S}_{t})_{t>0}
is unambiguously associated to it via
\mathsf{S}_{t}x=\gamma_{t}
.

(ii)

The maps
t\mapsto\gamma_{t}
and
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})
are locally Lipschitz in
(0,\infty)
with values in
{\rm X}
and
\mathbb{R}
, respectively, and satisfy the
Energy Dissipation Equality

-{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})=\frac{1}{2}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma_{t})=|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}=|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma_{t}),\qquad\textrm{for a.e. }t>0.

(3.4)

(iii)

The map

t\mapsto e^{\lambda t}|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t})\quad\textrm{is non-increasing}.

(3.5)

(iv)

If
(\gamma_{t})
starts from
x
and
y\in D(|\partial{\sf E}|)
, then

|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma_{t})\leq\frac{1}{2e^{\lambda t}-1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(y)+\frac{1}{I_{\lambda}(t)^{2}}{\sf d}^{2}(x,y),\qquad\textrm{provided }-\lambda t<\log 2,

(3.6)

where
I_{\lambda}(t):=\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t}e^{\lambda s}\,{\mathrm{d}}s
.

We emphasize that these properties directly follow from the very definition (

EVI
λ

) of gradient flows, and a priori do not require
{\sf E}
to be geodesically
\lambda
-convex.
Although analogous statements can be found in
[
2
]
and
[
1
]
under convexity assumptions on
{\sf E}
, the latter are essentially needed to grant
existence
of EVI-gradient flows.
It is important to stress this fact because in Setting
3.1
we
assume
that for any
x\in{\rm X}
there exists an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow of
{\sf E}
starting there, which from
[
27
]
is known to imply that
{\sf E}
is geodesically convex.
In Section
3
we also provide an alternative proof of this latter fact, whence the necessity for us to avoid all properties of
{\rm EVI}
-gradient flows actually relying on geodesic convexity.

We conclude this preliminary part with a general integrability result about
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flows, which we could not find explicitly written in the literature and will be used later on in the proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Lemma 3.5
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, let
x\in{\rm X}
. Then

t\mapsto{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)
is integrable in
[0,T]
, for all
T>0
,

regardless of whether
{\sf E}(x)
is initially finite or not.

On intervals
[\varepsilon,T]
this computation is easily justified by the fact that
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)
is locally Lipschitz in
(0,\infty)
, hence locally integrable therein.
But this computation is legitimate even if
\varepsilon=0
, as we are going to see.

Proof.

Let
x\in{\rm X}
and
T>0
be as in our statement.
Since
{\sf E}
is bounded from below on
{\sf d}
-bounded sets by
(A2)
, and because
(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)_{t\in[0,T]}
is bounded, there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)\geq c
for all
t\in[0,T]
.
Combining with (

EVI
λ

) this gives

c\leq{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)\leq{\sf E}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}t}{\sf d}^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x,y)-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\sf d}^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x,y)

for any
y\in D({\sf E})
and
t\in(0,T]
.
Integrating from
t=\eta>0
to
t=T
gives

\begin{split}c(T-\eta)\leq\intop\nolimits_{\eta}^{T}{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)\,{\mathrm{d}}t&\leq(T-\eta){\sf E}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\Big({\sf d}^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{T}x,y)-{\sf d}^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{\eta}x,y)\Big)\\
&\qquad-\frac{\lambda}{2}\intop\nolimits_{\eta}^{T}{\sf d}^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.\end{split}

As
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)
is bounded from below on
[0,T]
and the right-hand side has a finite limit as
\eta\downarrow 0
(thanks to the fact that
t\mapsto\mathsf{S}_{t}x
is
{\sf d}
-continuous on
[0,\infty)
by the very definition of
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow), we deduce the desired integrability.
∎

A pseudo-Riemannian computation

In this section the formal Riemannian computations carried out at the beginning of Section
2
, and more precisely (
2.4
), will be reproduced rigorously in the abstract Setting
3.1
.
To this aim, a key role will be played by the following purely metric estimate:

Lemma 3.6
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, let
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
with
{\sf E}(\gamma_{0}),{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})<\infty

For any fixed absolutely continuous function

h(t)>0

are locally integrable therein.
But this computation is also justified when
h(t_{0})=0
by Lemma
3.5
.
More specifically
s\mapsto{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})
is absolutely integrable on
[0,T]
for any
T>0
and a fortiori so is
s\mapsto e^{\lambda s}{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})
.

Now let us estimate the terms in (
3.10
) to get (
3.8
).
First, since
\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}}=\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}\gamma_{t_{1}}
,
\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{0}}=\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}\gamma_{t_{0}}
, and
\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}(\cdot)
is
\lambda
-contractive by (
3.3
), we observe that the second term in the left-hand side of (
3.10
) can be controlled as

{\sf d}^{2}(\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{0}})={\sf d}^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}\gamma_{t_{1}},\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}\gamma_{t_{0}})\leq e^{-2\lambda h(t_{0})}{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{t_{1}},\gamma_{t_{0}}).

(3.11)

On the right-hand side, let us define

I:=\intop\nolimits_{h(t_{0})}^{h(t_{1})}e^{\lambda s}\Big({\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})-{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})\Big)\,{\mathrm{d}}s.

This integral is clearly non-positive by (
3.4
), but we need a finer analysis. To this aim, for fixed
0<s<h(t_{1})
let us write

\begin{split}{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})-{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})&={\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}})-{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})\\
&=\intop\nolimits_{s}^{h(t_{1})}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\tau}{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{\tau}\gamma_{t_{1}})\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau=-\intop\nolimits_{s}^{h(t_{1})}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{\tau}\gamma_{t_{1}})\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau,\end{split}

where the second equality holds due to
\tau\mapsto{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{\tau}\gamma_{t_{1}})
being Lipschitz on
[s,h(t_{1})]
, and the third one stems from (
3.4
) for the gradient flow
\tau\mapsto\mathsf{S}_{\tau}\gamma_{t_{1}}
.
By (
3.5
)

\begin{split}-\intop\nolimits_{s}^{h(t_{1})}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{\tau}\gamma_{t_{1}})\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau&\leq-\intop\nolimits_{s}^{h(t_{1})}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}})e^{2\lambda(h(t_{1})-\tau)}\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau\\
&=-e^{2\lambda h(t_{1})}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}})\intop\nolimits_{s}^{h(t_{1})}e^{-2\lambda\tau}\,{\mathrm{d}}\tau\\
&=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\Big(1-e^{2\lambda(h(t_{1})-s)}\Big)|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}}),\end{split}

so that, as a consequence,

\begin{split}I&\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda}\intop\nolimits_{h(t_{0})}^{h(t_{1})}e^{\lambda s}\Big(1-e^{2\lambda(h(t_{1})-s)}\Big)|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}})\,{\mathrm{d}}s\\
&=\frac{1}{2\lambda}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}})\intop\nolimits_{h(t_{0})}^{h(t_{1})}\Big(e^{\lambda s}-e^{2\lambda h(t_{1})}\cdot e^{-\lambda s}\Big)\,{\mathrm{d}}s\\
&=-\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})e^{\lambda h(t_{1})}\Big(e^{\lambda(h(t_{1})-h(t_{0}))}+e^{\lambda(h(t_{0})-h(t_{1}))}-2\Big).\end{split}

Plugging this estimate together with (
3.11
) into (
3.10
) and dividing by
(t_{1}-t_{0})^{2}>0
entails our claim.
∎

We also need to study the behaviour of the “regularized” curve
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{h(t)}\gamma_{t}
and of the entropy
{\sf E}
along it: this is the content of the following two results.

Lemma 3.8
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, if
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
and
h:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is absolutely continuous with
h(t)>0
for all
t\in(0,1)
, then the curve
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{h(t)}\gamma_{t}
belongs to
AC_{loc}((0,1),{\rm X})\cap C([0,1],{\rm X})
.

Proof.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1/2)
,
t_{0},t_{1}\in[\delta,1-\delta]
with
t_{0}\leq t_{1}
and define

m_{\delta}:=\min_{t\in[\delta,1-\delta]}h(t),\qquad M_{\delta}:=\max_{t\in[\delta,1-\delta]}h(t),

(3.12)

paying attention to the fact that
m_{\delta}>0
by construction.
Write as before
\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}}:=\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}\gamma_{t_{1}}
for the “horizontal” translation of
\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{0}}
(see Figure
2
).
By triangular inequality and the contraction estimate (
3.3
) we get

{\sf d}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{0}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})\leq{\sf d}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{0}},\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}})+{\sf d}(\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})\leq e^{\lambda^{-}M_{\delta}}{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{0}},\gamma_{t_{1}})+{\sf d}(\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}}),

(3.13)

where
\lambda^{-}:=\max\{-\lambda,0\}
. Since
(\gamma_{t})
is absolutely continuous the first term in the right-hand side can be controlled as
{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{0}},\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|{\mathrm{d}}t
.
As regards the second one, by (
3.4
) and up to assuming
h(t_{0})\leq h(t_{1})
(which is not restrictive, as otherwise it is sufficient to swap the boundary values of integration below) it holds

{\sf d}(\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})={\sf d}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{0})}\gamma_{t_{1}},\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq\intop\nolimits_{h(t_{0})}^{h(t_{1})}\Big|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}}\Big|\,{\mathrm{d}}s=\intop\nolimits_{h(t_{0})}^{h(t_{1})}|\partial{\sf E}|(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})\,{\mathrm{d}}s,

(3.14)

where, to avoid possibly ambiguous notations,
|\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}s}\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}}|
denotes the metric speed of the “vertical” curve
s\mapsto\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}}
.
In order to control the slope in the right-most term uniformly both in
s\in[h(t_{0}),h(t_{1})]\subset[m_{\delta},M_{\delta}]
and in
t_{1}\in[\delta,1-\delta]
, for fixed
\delta
, let
\varepsilon
be such that
-\lambda\varepsilon<\log 2
(if
\lambda\geq 0
, choose
\varepsilon=m_{\delta}
) and define
\varepsilon^{\prime}:=\min\{m_{\delta},\varepsilon\}
.
Then by (
3.5
) and the fact that
s\geq h(t_{0})\geq m_{\delta}\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}
we have

|\partial{\sf E}|(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq e^{\lambda(\varepsilon^{\prime}-s)}|\partial{\sf E}|(\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq e^{\lambda^{-}(M_{\delta}-\varepsilon^{\prime})}|\partial{\sf E}|(\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\gamma_{t_{1}}),\qquad\forall s\in[m_{\delta},M_{\delta}]

and by (
3.6
) for any reference point
x\in D(|\partial{\sf E}|)
it holds

|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq\frac{1}{2e^{\lambda\varepsilon^{\prime}}-1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(x)+\frac{1}{I_{\lambda}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}}{\sf d}^{2}(x,\gamma_{t_{1}}).

The squared distance in the right-hand side above is bounded uniformly in
t_{1}\in[\delta,1-\delta]
, since by triangular inequality

{\sf d}(x,\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq{\sf d}(x,\gamma_{0})+{\sf d}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq{\sf d}(x,\gamma_{0})+\ell(\gamma).

Therefore there exists
C_{\delta}>0
such that

|\partial{\sf E}|(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq C_{\delta}\qquad\mbox{for all}\qquad t_{1}\in[\delta,1-\delta]\mbox{ and }s\in[m_{\delta},M_{\delta}].

(3.15)

and plugging this bound into (
3.14
) yields

{\sf d}(\hat{\gamma}_{t_{1}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})\leq C_{\delta}|h(t_{1})-h(t_{0})|\leq C_{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|h^{\prime}(t)|\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

It is now sufficient to combine this inequality with
{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{0}},\gamma_{t_{1}})\leq\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|{\mathrm{d}}t
and (
3.13
) to get

{\sf d}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{0}},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})\leq\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\Big(e^{\lambda^{-}M_{\delta}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|+C_{\delta}|h^{\prime}(t)|\Big)\,{\mathrm{d}}t.

(3.16)

As
e^{\lambda^{-}M_{\delta}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|+C_{\delta}|h^{\prime}(t)|\in L^{1}(\delta,1-\delta)
and
\delta
is arbitrary, the fact that
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\in AC_{loc}((0,1))
is thus proved.

Turning now to the continuity of
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
at the endpoints, let
t_{0}=0
and
t_{1}\in(0,1)
.
Arguing as for (
3.13
) but with a crucial difference in the choice of the third point in the triangular inequality, it holds

\begin{split}{\sf d}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})&\leq{\sf d}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0},\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{0})+{\sf d}(\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{0},\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})\\
&\leq{\sf d}(\mathsf{S}_{h(0)}\gamma_{0},\mathsf{S}_{h(t_{1})}\gamma_{0})+e^{-\lambda h(t_{1})}{\sf d}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{t_{1}}).\end{split}

The second term on the right-hand side vanishes as
t_{1}\downarrow 0
by (absolute) continuity of
\gamma
and so does the first one, since
s\mapsto\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{0}
is continuous in
[0,\infty)
with values in
{\rm X}
and
h(t_{1})\to h(0)
. The continuity at
t=1
is obtained similarly and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.9
.

If
h(t)>0
also in
t=0,1
, then the previous argument can be extended to the whole interval
[0,1]
and therefore
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 3.10
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Lemma
3.8
, the entropy is locally absolutely continuous in
(0,1)
along the regularized curve
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}
, i.e.

t\mapsto{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\quad\in\quad AC_{loc}((0,1)).

If in addition
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
,
{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0}),{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{1})<\infty
and
h
is differentiable at
t=0
and
t=1
with
h^{\prime}(0)>0
and
h^{\prime}(1)<0
, then

t\mapsto{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\quad\in\quad C([0,1]).

Note that
{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0}),{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{1})<\infty
is automatically satisfied if
h(t)>0
also in
t=0,1
.

Proof.

Let us first prove that
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
is locally absolutely continuous.
Since
|\partial{\sf E}|
is a strong upper-gradient, the chain rule (
3.2
) holds and it suffices to show that
|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|\in L^{1}_{loc}(0,1)
, namely

\intop\nolimits_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|\,{\mathrm{d}}t<\infty,\qquad\forall\delta\in(0,1/2),

(3.17)

as this would imply that
{\sf E}\circ\tilde{\gamma}\in AC_{loc}((0,1))
with

\left|{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}({\sf E}\circ\tilde{\gamma})(t)\right|\leq|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\cdot|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|,\qquad\textrm{for a.e. }t\in(0,1).

To this aim, observe from (
3.16
) that
|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|\in L^{1}_{loc}(0,1)
with
|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|\leq e^{\lambda^{-}M_{\delta}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|+C_{\delta}|h^{\prime}(t)|
a.e. on
[\delta,1-\delta]
, with
M_{\delta}
defined in (
3.12
).
Moreover from (
3.15
) we also know that
|\partial{\sf E}|(\mathsf{S}_{s}\gamma_{t})\leq C_{\delta}
uniformly in
t\in[\delta,1-\delta]
and
s\in[m_{\delta},M_{\delta}]
, so that by choosing
s=h(t)
we get in particular
|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\leq C_{\delta}
for all
t\in[\delta,1-\delta]
.
This shows that
t\mapsto|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
belongs to
L^{\infty}_{loc}(0,1)
, whence (
3.17
).

Now assume that
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
,
{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0})<\infty
,
h
is differentiable at
t=0
with
h^{\prime}(0)>0
and let us prove that
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
is continuous at
t=0
.
(The argument is identical for
t=1
.)
On the one hand, as
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
is continuous at
t=0
by Lemma
3.8
and
{\sf E}
is lower semicontinuous, we see that
{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0})\leq\liminf_{t\downarrow 0}{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
.
On the other hand, choosing
t_{0}=0
in (
3.8
), our assumption that
h^{\prime}(0)>0
gives
h(t_{1})>h(0)
for
t_{1}>0
small, hence
t^{-}=0
and
t^{+}=t_{1}
.
Discarding the first two (non-negative) terms on the left-hand side, and multiplying by
(t_{1}-t_{0})=t_{1}
yield

\begin{split}\frac{1-e^{-\lambda(h(t_{1})-h(0))}}{\lambda(t_{1}-0)}\cdot\big({\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})-{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0})\big)&\leq\frac{t_{1}}{2}e^{-\lambda(h(t_{1})+h(0))}\left|\frac{{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{1}},\gamma_{0})}{t_{1}}\right|^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{t_{1}}{2}e^{-\lambda(h(t_{1})+h(0))}\Big(\frac{1}{t_{1}}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\,{\mathrm{d}}t\Big)^{2}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-\lambda(h(t_{1})+h(0))}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}t.\end{split}.

Letting
t_{1}\downarrow 0
, the right-hand side vanishes owing to our assumption that
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
, and clearly the exponential difference quotient in the left-hand side converges to
h^{\prime}(0)
.
Rearranging gives

h^{\prime}(0)\limsup_{t_{1}\downarrow 0}{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t_{1}})\leq h^{\prime}(0){\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0}),

since
h^{\prime}(0)>0
the desired upper semicontinuity follows and the proof is complete.
∎

Gathering the results proven so far, we deduce the following:

Proposition 3.11
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Lemma
3.8
, for a.e.
t\in(0,1)
it holds

\frac{1}{2}\left|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|h^{\prime}(t)|^{2}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})+h^{\prime}(t){\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\lambda h(t)}\left|\dot{\gamma}_{t}\right|^{2}.

(3.18)

Proof.

The argument simply consists in taking the limit
t_{1}\to t_{0}
in (
3.8
), which should clearly lead (at least formally) to (
3.18
) by Taylor-expanding the various exponential difference quotients.
In order to make this rigorous, note that the first and third terms in the left-hand side of (
3.18
) are well defined for a.e.
t\in(0,1)
by Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
3.10
, respectively.
The second term is also unambiguously defined because
h(t)>0
, hence the “vertical”
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow starting from
\gamma_{t}
and defining
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}=\mathsf{S}_{h(t)}\gamma_{t}
falls immediately within the domain
{\rm X}_{1}=D(|\partial{\sf E}|)
.
The right-hand side is well defined for a.e.
t
since
\gamma\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
.

After this premise, let
t\in(0,1)
be any differentiation point for
h
,
t\mapsto\gamma_{t}
,
t\mapsto\tilde{\gamma}_{t}
and
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})
, choose
t_{0}=t
in (
3.8
) and let us take the right limit
t_{1}\downarrow t_{0}
(since we are considering a differentiability point the left and right limits exist and are equal, so there is no need to address the left limit).
From the very definition (
3.7
) of
t^{\pm}
it clearly holds
t^{\pm}\to t_{0}
as
t_{1}\downarrow t
, hence the convergence of the right-hand side of (
3.8
) to the right-hand side of (
3.18
) is clear and so is the convergence of the two difference quotients of
h
.
By Lemma
3.8
the first term in the left-hand side also passes to the limit, as does the third one according to Lemma
3.10
.
The only term left to handle is the Fisher information
|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\tilde{\gamma}^{+})
. From the continuity of
t\mapsto\tilde{\gamma}_{t}
(cf. Lemma
3.8
) we see that
\tilde{\gamma}_{t^{+}}\to\tilde{\gamma}_{t}
in
({\rm X},{\sf d})
, and the lower semicontinuity of the slope (
3.1
) results in

|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\leq\liminf_{t_{1}\downarrow 0}|\partial{\sf E}|(\tilde{\gamma}_{t^{+}}).

Thus rigorously taking the liminf
t_{1}\downarrow t_{0}
in (
3.8
) entails (
3.18
) and achieves the proof.
∎

The interesting consequence for our purpose is then:

Theorem 3.12
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, fix
\varepsilon>0
, and set
h_{\varepsilon}(t):=\varepsilon\min\{t,1-t\}
. Let
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
be such that
{\sf E}(\gamma_{0}),{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})<\infty
and define

\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{h_{\varepsilon}(t)}\gamma_{t},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then
(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
,
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})
belongs to
AC([0,1])
and it holds

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}&\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}-2\varepsilon{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})\\
&\qquad+\varepsilon\Big({\sf E}(\gamma_{0})+{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})\Big).\end{split}

(3.19)

Note here that
h_{\varepsilon}(0)=h_{\varepsilon}(1)=0
, so that the endpoints
\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1}=\gamma_{1}
remain unchanged.

Proof.

The strategy of proof simply consists in integrating (
3.18
) between
0
and
1
while integrating by parts of the term
h_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t){\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})
, separately on
[0,1/2]
and
[1/2,1]
.
Note carefully that our specific choice gives
h_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=\varepsilon
and
h_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=-\varepsilon
on these two time intervals, respectively.
Taking into account
e^{-2\lambda h_{\varepsilon}(t)}\leq e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon}
, where
\lambda^{-}:=\max\{-\lambda,0\}
, this procedure yields

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}&\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}-2\varepsilon{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})\\
&\quad+\varepsilon\Big({\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0})+{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1})\Big).\end{split}

The term
2\varepsilon{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})
simply arises from the two boundary terms at
t=1/2
in the two integrations by parts.
(Alternatively, it can be seen as the result of
-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})h^{\prime\prime}(t)
arising from the integration by parts in the whole interval
[0,1]
, with the singularity
h^{\prime\prime}(t)=-2\varepsilon\delta_{1/2}(t)
).
However, this argument is not fully rigorous because all the terms on the left-hand side of (
3.18
) are only
locally
integrable, hence we may not be allowed to integrate them all the way to
t=0
and
t=1
.

In order to circumvent this slight issue, choose
\delta\in(0,1/2)
and carry out the same argument on
[\delta,1/2]
and
[1/2,1-\delta]
rather than on
[0,1/2]
and
[1/2,1]
: Integration by parts is now justified by Lemma
3.10
and this provides us with

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\dot{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}&\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon}\intop\nolimits_{\delta}^{1-\delta}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\\
&\quad+\varepsilon\Big({\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})-2{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})+{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1-\delta})\Big).\end{split}

(3.20)

It is then sufficient to pass to the limit as
\delta\downarrow 0
.
By monotonicity the left-hand side above converges to the left-hand side in (
3.19
) and for the same reason so does the first term on the right-hand side, while by the current choice of
h
and by Lemma
3.10

t\mapsto{\sf E}(\gamma_{t}^{\varepsilon})
is continuous on the whole interval
[0,1]
, so that

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\varepsilon\Big({\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\delta})+{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1-\delta})\Big)=\varepsilon\Big({\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0})+{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1})\Big)=\varepsilon\Big({\sf E}(\gamma_{0})+{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})\Big)

and (
3.19
) follows.

Finally, since the right-hand side of (
3.19
) is finite we see that
|\dot{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}|\in L^{2}(0,1)
and
|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma^{\varepsilon})\in L^{2}(0,1)
. As a consequence
|\dot{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}|\cdot|\partial{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})|\in L^{1}(0,1)
in the strong upper-chain rule (
3.2
), and
{\sf E}\circ\gamma^{\varepsilon}\in AC([0,1])
as desired.
∎

4
Small-temperature limit and convexity

4.1
\Gamma
-convergence of the Schrödinger problem

Relying on the results of the previous section, we can now turn to Theorem
2.1
and make it rigorous in the metric setting. To this end, let us first introduce two action functionals: the kinetic energy
\mathcal{A}
and the (halved) Fisher information
\mathcal{I}
along a curve, respectively defined as

\mathcal{A}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathcal{I}(\gamma):=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}

for all
(\gamma_{t})\in C([0,1],{\rm X})
, where it is understood that
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=+\infty
whenever
\gamma
is not absolutely continuous. Given two points
x,y\in{\rm X}
and a temperature/slowing-down parameter
\varepsilon>0
, the (metric) Schrödinger problem reads as

\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma)\Big\},

(
\mathrm{Sch}^{\varepsilon}
)

where
(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y
is a short-hand notation meaning that the infimum runs over all
(\gamma_{t})\in C([0,1],{\rm X})
such that
\gamma_{0}=x
and
\gamma_{1}=y
. For sake of brevity we also introduce

\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma):=\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma).

From (

Sch
ε

) it is thus clear that the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}
acts as a perturbation of
\mathcal{A}
and this has a regularizing effect, since minimizers of (

Sch
ε

) live within the regular domain
{\rm X}_{1}=D(|\partial{\sf E}|)
.

Remark 4.1
.

The smoothing effect is well understood for the classic Schrödinger problem in a regular setting, namely when
{\sf E}
is the Boltzmann-Shannon relative entropy and
{\rm X}
is the Wasserstein space over a smooth Riemannian manifold. In this case, under mild assumptions on the end-points, minimizers of (

Sch
ε

) are curves of absolutely continuous measures whose densities are bounded, smooth, Lipschitz, with exponentially fast decaying tails.

In the current metric framework the properties above are meaningless, but still minimizers of (

Sch
ε

) are “regular” from a metric point of view, since as just said they live within
D(|\partial{\sf E}|)
. Moreover, in Proposition
4.2
we are going to see that
{\sf E}
is absolutely continuous along optimal curves.

\blacksquare

Let us first deal with the solvability of (

Sch
ε

).

Proposition 4.2
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
and under Assumption
3.2
, for any fixed
x,y\in{\rm X}
and
\varepsilon>0
the Schrödinger problem (

Sch
ε

) is solvable if and only if
{\sf E}(x),{\sf E}(y)<\infty
and there exists
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
such that
\gamma_{0}=x
and
\gamma_{1}=y
.

As the condition characterizing the solvability of the Schrödinger problem does not depend on
\varepsilon
, it is clear that if (

Sch
ε

) is solvable for some
\varepsilon>0
, then it is actually solvable for all
\varepsilon>0
.

Proof.

Assume that the endpoints have finite entropy and that there exists an absolutely continuous curve
\gamma
connecting
x
to
y
. Up to reparametrization, we can assume that
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
.
Theorem
3.12
thus guarantees that
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
is finite along the regularization
(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
of this curve and therefore the variational problem (

Sch
ε

) is proper.
Let then
(\gamma^{n}_{t})
be any minimizing sequence and observe that the kinetic action
\mathcal{A}
is bounded uniformly in
n
, say
\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{n})\leq C
for all
n
. We now observe that for any pair
0\leq t_{0}<t_{1}\leq 1
it holds

{\sf d}(\gamma^{n}_{t_{0}},\gamma^{n}_{t_{1}})\leq\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}^{n}_{t}|\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\leq|t_{0}-t_{1}|^{1/2}\Big(\intop\nolimits_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}|\dot{\gamma}^{n}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\Big)^{1/2}\leq C|t_{0}-t_{1}|^{1/2}.

(4.1)

Since the endpoints are fixed, this implies that the set of points
\gamma^{n}_{t}
is bounded in
({\rm X},{\sf d})
uniformly in
n,t
, thus it is
\sigma
-relatively sequentially compact by Assumption
3.2
. By the refined Arzelà-Ascoli lemma
[
2
, Proposition 3.3.1]
, there exists a limiting
{\sf d}
-continuous (actually
1/2
-Hölder continuous) curve
\gamma
such that

\gamma^{n}_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\gamma_{t},\qquad\forall t\in[0,1].

We now observe that the kinetic action is lower semicontinuous for this pointwise-in-time convergence w.r.t.
\sigma
, cf.
[
3
, Section 2.2]
(indeed,
{\sf d}
is lower semicontinuous w.r.t.
\sigma
, hence the
2
-energies of the finite partitions of
\gamma
are lower semicontinuous w.r.t.
\sigma
too, whence the lower semicontinuity of the
2
-energy of
\gamma
itself).
Moreover
|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}
is also lower semicontinuous w.r.t.
\sigma
by hypothesis, and this fact together with Fatou’s lemma gives

\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\leq\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\liminf_{n\to\infty}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma^{n}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma^{n}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}.

Therefore
\gamma
is a minimizer of (

Sch
ε

).

Conversely, assume that there exists a minimizer, denoted by
\gamma
(the following argument actually works for any curve along which
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
is finite and without Assumption
3.2
).
Then in particular
t\mapsto|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|
and
t\mapsto|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t})
belong to
L^{2}(0,1)
and by (
3.2
) we see that
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})
is globally absolutely continuous with

\left|{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}({\sf E}\circ\gamma)(t)\right|\leq|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t})\cdot|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|\in L^{1}(0,1).

The fact that
(|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|)\in L^{2}(0,1)\subset L^{1}(0,1)
trivially implies
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
, whereas the fact that
t\mapsto|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t})
belongs to
L^{2}(0,1)
also implies that
|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t})
is finite for a.e.
t\in[0,1]
and a fortiori so is
{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})
, since
D(|\partial{\sf E}|)\subset D({\sf E})
.
Hence let
t^{*}\in(0,1)
be any point satifying
{\sf E}(\gamma_{t^{*}})<\infty
and note that together with (
3.2
) this gives the following global upper bound valid for all
t<t^{*}

{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})\leq{\sf E}(\gamma_{t^{*}})+\intop\nolimits_{t}^{t^{*}}\left|{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}({\sf E}\circ\gamma)(t)\right|{{\mathrm{d}}t}\leq{\sf E}(\gamma_{t^{*}})+\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left|{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}({\sf E}\circ\gamma)(t)\right|{{\mathrm{d}}t}=:\overline{{\sf E}}<\infty.

As a consequence, and taking also into account the facts that
t\mapsto\gamma_{t}
is
{\sf d}
-continuous and
{\sf E}
is lower semicontinuous, we get

{\sf E}(\gamma_{0})={\sf E}\big(\lim_{t\to 0}\gamma_{t}\big)\leq\liminf_{t\to 0}{\sf E}(\gamma_{t})\leq\overline{{\sf E}}

and the proof is thus complete, as the same argument applies
mutatis mutandis
for
t=1
too.
∎

We now fix
x,y\in{\rm X}
and let
C([0,1],{\rm X})\ni\gamma\mapsto\iota_{01}(\gamma)
denote the convex indicator of the endpoint constraints, i.e.

\iota_{01}(\gamma)=\begin{cases}0&\mbox{if }\gamma_{0}=x\mbox{ and }\gamma_{1}=y,\\
+\infty&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

With this said, we can finally state our
\Gamma
-convergence result, where the finite-entropy assumption on the endpoints is motivated by the previous proposition.

Theorem 4.3
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, if
x,y\in{\rm X}
are such that
{\sf E}(x),{\sf E}(y)<\infty
, then

\Gamma-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}+\iota_{01}\Big\}=\mathcal{A}+\iota_{01}

for the uniform convergence on
C([0,1],{\rm X})
.
If Assumption
3.2
holds, then the
\Gamma
-convergence also takes place w.r.t. the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology.

Proof.

The
\Gamma-\liminf
inequality is rather clear, since the kinetic energy
\mathcal{A}
is lower semicontinuous both w.r.t. uniform-in-time
{\sf d}
-convergence and pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-convergence:
for the former topology the fact is well known, for the latter it has been discussed in the proof of Proposition
4.2
.
An analogous claim is also true for the convex indicator
\iota_{01}
.
As a consequence, we have that for any
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
converging to
\gamma
uniformly in time in the metric topology or pointwise in time in the topology
\sigma
(if applicable) it holds

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+\iota_{01}(\gamma)&\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})\Big\}\\
&\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})\Big\}=\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})\Big\},\end{split}

whence the desired
\Gamma-\liminf
inequality.

For the
\Gamma-\limsup
, take any
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
connecting
x
to
y
(if it does not exist, then there is nothing to prove).
Then Theorem
3.12
precisely provides a recovery sequence
\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{h_{\varepsilon}(t)}\gamma_{t}
with
h_{\varepsilon}
defined as therein, both for the uniform-in-time
{\sf d}
-convergence and the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-convergence (the latter is an easy consequence of the former by Remark
3.4
).
To prove this claim, note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
t_{1},...,t_{k}\in[0,1]
such that, for any
t\in[0,1]
,
{\sf d}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t_{i}})<1/n
for at least one
t_{i}
;
in addition, since
\gamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}\to\gamma_{t}
for all
t\in[0,1]
there exists
\varepsilon_{n}
small enough such that
{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t_{i}}^{\varepsilon})<1/n
for all
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{n}
and
i=1,...,k
.
As a consequence, taking (
3.3
) into account,

\begin{split}{\sf d}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\varepsilon})&\leq{\sf d}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t_{i}})+{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{i}},\mathsf{S}_{h_{\varepsilon}(t)}\gamma_{t_{i}})+{\sf d}(\mathsf{S}_{h_{\varepsilon}(t)}\gamma_{t_{i}},\gamma_{t}^{\varepsilon})\\
&\leq{\sf d}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t_{i}})+{\sf d}(\gamma_{t_{i}},\mathsf{S}_{h_{\varepsilon}(t)}\gamma_{t_{i}})+e^{-\lambda h_{\varepsilon}(t)}{\sf d}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t_{i}})\\
&\leq\frac{1}{n}(2+e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon/2})\end{split}

for all
t\in[0,1]
and
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{n}
and by the arbitrariness of
n
we conclude that
\gamma^{\varepsilon}\to\gamma
uniformly.
Furthermore, the
\limsup
inequality can be proved as follows:

\begin{split}\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})&+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})\Big\}=\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+0\Big\}\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:upper_bound_recovery_metric}}{\leq}\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}(\gamma)-2\varepsilon{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})+\varepsilon\big({\sf E}(x)+{\sf E}(y)\big)\Big\}\\
&\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\Big\{e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+\varepsilon\big({\sf E}(x)+{\sf E}(y)\big)\Big\}-2\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})\\
&\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+\iota_{01}(\gamma),\end{split}

where the third inequality comes from the fact that, for any
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
,
(\gamma_{1/2}^{\varepsilon})
is contained in a bounded set and by assumption
{\sf E}
is bounded from below on bounded sets, whence
{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})\geq c
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
The proof is thus complete.
∎

As an easy consequence of this result we obtain the following:

Corollary 4.4
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
and under the further requirements that Assumption
3.2
holds and the Schrödinger problem (

Sch
ε

) relative to
x,y\in{\rm X}
is solvable, let
\varepsilon_{k}\downarrow 0
and
\omega^{k}
be a minimizer of the corresponding Schrödinger problem (

Sch
ε

) with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{k}
.

Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\omega^{k})+\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{k})\Big\}=\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\mathcal{A}(\gamma).

Moreover, there exists
\omega^{0}\in C([0,1],{\rm X})
such that, up to a subsequence,
\omega^{k}\to\omega^{0}
in the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology and

\mathcal{A}(\omega^{0})=\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\mathcal{A}(\gamma).

Proof.

Recall that, under a mild equicoercivity condition,
\Gamma
-convergence precisely guarantees that the limit of the optimal values of the approximating problems is the optimal value of the limit problem and limits of minimizers are minimizers, cf.
[
16
, Theorem 1.21]
.
In view of Theorem
4.3
and
[
16
, Theorem 1.21]
, for the mild equi-coercivity condition to hold it suffices to prove that the set of minimizers
\{\omega^{k}\}
is relatively compact in the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology.
To this aim, the kinetic energies of the curves
\omega^{k}
are uniformly bounded since

\mathcal{A}(\omega^{k})\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{k})+\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{k})\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\overline{\varepsilon}})+\varepsilon_{k}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\overline{\varepsilon}})\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\overline{\varepsilon}})+\overline{\varepsilon}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\overline{\varepsilon}})<+\infty,

where
\omega^{\overline{\varepsilon}}
is the minimizer for the problem with
\varepsilon=\overline{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{k}{\varepsilon_{k}}
. Arguing as in the proof of Proposition
4.2
, we deduce that there exists a continuous curve
(\omega^{0}_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
connecting
x
and
y
such that, up to extracting a suitable subsequence,
\omega^{k}_{t}\to\omega^{0}_{t}
w.r.t.
\sigma
as
k\to\infty
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

Note that in Corollary
4.4
the curve
\omega^{0}
is length-minimizing but not necessarily distance-minimizing, namely it needs not be a geodesic between
x
and
y
, since we only know that

\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\geq\frac{1}{2}{\sf d}^{2}(x,y)

and the inequality might be strict, e.g. if
{\rm X}
is a non-convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
However, if
({\rm X},{\sf d})
is a length metric space, i.e. for all
x,y\in{\rm X}
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists
(\gamma_{t})\in AC([0,1],{\rm X})
such that
\gamma_{0}=x
,
\gamma_{1}=y
and
\ell(\gamma)\leq{\sf d}(x,y)+\varepsilon
, then the inequality above turns out to be an identity and, as a consequence,
\omega^{0}
is a geodesic.
This means that for any two points having finite energy there always exists a geodesic connecting them.

\blacksquare

When the endpoints have infinite entropy, the following variant of Theorem
4.3
may be useful:

Theorem 4.6
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, let
x,y\in{\rm X}
with possibly
{\sf E}(x),{\sf E}(y)=+\infty
and for any fixed
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
, let
(\eta_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be converging to 0 slowly enough so that

\varepsilon_{n}\left({\sf E}(\gamma_{0}^{n})+{\sf E}(\gamma_{1}^{n}\right))\to 0\qquad\mbox{with}\qquad\gamma_{0}^{n}:=\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}}x,\quad\gamma_{1}^{n}:=\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}}y.

Then

\Gamma-\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon_{n}}+\iota^{n}_{01}\Big\}=\mathcal{A}+\iota_{01},

for the uniform convergence on
C([0,1],{\rm X})
.
If Assumption
3.2
holds, then the
\Gamma
-convergence also takes place w.r.t. the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology.
Here
\iota_{01}^{n}
and
\iota_{01}
are the convex indicators of the endpoint constraints for
\gamma_{0}^{n},\gamma_{1}^{n}
and
x,y
, respectively.

Proof.

The proof of the
\Gamma-\liminf
is almost identical to that in Theorem
4.3
, with the only extra observation that

\iota_{01}(\gamma)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\iota_{01}^{n}(\gamma^{n}).

For the
\Gamma-\limsup
, observe that if there does not exist
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
joining
x
and
y
, then there is nothing to prove.
Hence let us suppose that at least one curve
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
connecting
x
and
y
exists, fix it and note that Theorem
3.12
applied to the curve
\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}}\gamma_{t}
still provides a recovery sequence
\gamma^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}+h_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t)}\gamma_{t}
with the same choice
h_{\varepsilon_{n}}(t)=\varepsilon_{n}\min\{t,1-t\}
as before.
Indeed, on the one hand

\begin{split}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Big\{&\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon_{n}}(\gamma^{\varepsilon_{n}})+\iota_{01}^{n}(\gamma^{\varepsilon_{n}})\Big\}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon_{n}})+\varepsilon^{2}_{n}\mathcal{I}(\gamma^{\varepsilon_{n}})+0\Big\}\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:upper_bound_recovery_metric}}}{{\leq}}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Big\{e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon_{n}}\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}}\gamma)-2\varepsilon_{n}{\sf E}(\gamma_{1/2}^{\varepsilon_{n}})+\varepsilon_{n}\big({\sf E}(\gamma_{0}^{n})+{\sf E}(\gamma_{1}^{n})\big)\Big\}\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\Big\{e^{\lambda^{-}\varepsilon_{n}}\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}}\gamma)+\varepsilon_{n}\big({\sf E}(\gamma_{0}^{n})+{\sf E}(\gamma_{1}^{n})\big)\Big\}-2\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}\varepsilon{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{1/2})\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{A}(\mathsf{S}_{\eta_{n}}\gamma)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}e^{-2\lambda\eta_{n}}\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\mathcal{A}(\gamma),\end{split}

where the third inequality follows by the same argument adopted in the proof of the previous theorem and the last one is due to (
3.18
) with
h(t)\equiv\eta_{n}
.
On the other hand,
\gamma^{\varepsilon_{n}}_{t}\to\gamma_{t}
uniformly in
t\in[0,1]
in the
{\sf d}
-topology and, if Assumption
3.2
holds, for all
t\in[0,1]
w.r.t.
\sigma
: the argument described in the previous proof applies also here verbatim.
∎

As conclusion, in the next proposition we show that any
{\rm EVI}
-gradient flow is a solution of the Schrödinger problem with suitable endpoints.
Intuitively this is clear, because up to a rescaling factor
\varepsilon^{2}
both the trajectories of the gradient flow of
{\sf E}
and the solutions to (

Sch
ε

) must formally satisfy the same Newton equation, namely
\ddot{\gamma}_{t}=-\nabla\Phi(\gamma_{t})
where the potential
\Phi
is given by (minus) the Fisher information
-|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}
, cf.
[
34
, Remark 6]
.
This is also in complete analogy with the standard Schrödinger problem, which includes the heat flow as a particular entropic interpolation.

Proposition 4.7
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Then for all
x,y\in{\rm X}
the following lower bound on the optimal value of (

Sch
ε

) holds

\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)\geq\varepsilon\big|{\sf E}(x)-{\sf E}(y)\big|.

(4.2)

If either
y=\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon}x
or
x=\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon}y
, then equality is achieved.
In the former case the curve
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto\hat{\gamma}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon t}x
is a minimizer in the Schrödinger problem and the optimal value is

\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma)=\varepsilon\big({\sf E}(x)-{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon}x)\big).

An analogous statement holds when
x=\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon}y
.

Proof.

By (
3.2
) and Young’s inequality it follows that for any
(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,\varepsilon],{\rm X})
joining
x
and
y
(if it exists; if not, (
4.2
) is trivial) it holds

\big|{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{0})-{\sf E}(\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon})\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\varepsilon}|\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\varepsilon}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\tilde{\gamma}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}.

By setting
\gamma_{t}:=\tilde{\gamma}_{\varepsilon t}
,
t\in[0,1]
, and by the arbitrariness of
\tilde{\gamma}
we thus see that for all
(\gamma_{t})\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
joining
x
and
y
we have

\varepsilon\big|{\sf E}(\gamma_{0})-{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t},

so that

\varepsilon\big|{\sf E}(\gamma_{0})-{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})\big|\leq\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma).

Now assume that
y=\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon}x
: integrating (
3.4
) for the
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow
\hat{\gamma}
(paying attention to the rescaling factor
\varepsilon
) between 0 and 1 we get

\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\hat{\gamma})=\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\hat{\gamma}}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\hat{\gamma}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}=\varepsilon\big({\sf E}(x)-{\sf E}(y)\big)=\varepsilon\big|{\sf E}(x)-{\sf E}(y)\big|,

where the last equality comes from the fact that
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\mathsf{S}_{t}x)
is non-increasing, as a consequence of (
3.4
).
Combining this identity with (
4.2
) yields the conclusion.
∎

4.2
Displacement convexity

In analogy with Section
2.2
, in this short section we establish the geodesic
\lambda
-convexity of
{\sf E}
.
As already explained in the Introduction, here
we do not claim any novelty of the result
(cf.
[
27
, Theorem 3.2]
). Our proof is however independent and new, being a further evidence of the wide range of applications of the Schrödinger problem.
Let us stress once more that all the properties of
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flows stated in Section
3.1
and used so far do not rely on geodesic
\lambda
-convexity, whence the genuine independence of our approach.

Theorem 4.8
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
, the potential
{\sf E}
is
\lambda
-convex along
any
geodesic.

Proof.

Let
(\gamma_{t})
be any constant-speed geodesic. We want to prove that

{\sf E}(\gamma_{\theta})\leq(1-\theta){\sf E}(\gamma_{0})+\theta{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\theta(1-\theta){\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),\qquad\forall\theta\in[0,1].

We will establish this inequality by carefully estimating at order one as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
the defect of optimality, in the geodesic problem from
\gamma_{0}
to
\gamma_{1}
, of a suitably regularized version
(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})
of the geodesic.

If
{\sf E}(\gamma_{0})=+\infty
or
{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})=+\infty
there is nothing to prove, so we can assume without loss of generality that both endpoints have finite entropy. If
\theta=0
or
\theta=1
the inequality is trivial as well.
Fix then an arbitrary parameter
\theta\in(0,1)
and let

H_{\theta}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\frac{1}{\theta}}t&\mbox{if }t\in[0,\theta],\\
-\displaystyle{\frac{1}{1-\theta}(t-1)}&\mbox{if }t\in[\theta,1].\end{array}\right.

be the hat function centered at
t=\theta
with height
1
and vanishing at
t=0,1
.
Setting
h(t):=\varepsilon H_{\theta}(t)
for small
\varepsilon>0
, let
(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})
be the curve constructed as in Lemma
3.6
, i.e.

\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}:=\mathsf{S}_{h(t)}\gamma_{t},\qquad\mbox{for all }t\in[0,1].

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
3.12
, it is easily verified that with the current choice of
h
it is still true that
t\mapsto|\dot{\gamma}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|
and
t\mapsto|\partial{\sf E}|(\gamma_{t}^{\varepsilon})
belong to
AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})
and
t\mapsto{\sf E}(\gamma_{t}^{\varepsilon})
to
AC([0,1])
, so that we can integrate (
3.18
) in time on the whole interval
[0,1]
.
Discarding the non-negative term
|h^{\prime}(t)|^{2}|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})
and using the optimality of the geodesic
\gamma
(namely its optimality between
\gamma_{0}
and
\gamma_{1}
) give

\begin{split}0&\leq\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}^{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}-\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\\
&\overset{\eqref{eq:pseudo_Riemannian_estimate}}{\leq}-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}h^{\prime}(t){\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{1}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left(e^{-2\lambda h(t)}-1\right)|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|^{2}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}\\
&=-\varepsilon\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}H_{\theta}^{\prime}(t){\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}{2}\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\left(e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda H_{\theta}(t)}-1\right)\,{{\mathrm{d}}t},\end{split}

where the last equality follows from the constant speed property of the geodesic
\gamma
, namely
|\dot{\gamma}_{t}|={\sf d}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})
.
Dividing by
\varepsilon>0
and leveraging the explicit piecewise constant values of
H_{\theta}^{\prime}(t)
on each interval
(0,\theta)
and
(\theta,1)
gives

\begin{split}0&\leq-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}H_{\theta}^{\prime}(t){\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}{2}\underbrace{\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}\frac{e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda H_{\theta}(t)}-1}{\varepsilon}\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}}_{:=I_{\varepsilon}}\\
&=-\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\theta}\frac{1}{\theta}{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\intop\nolimits_{\theta}^{1}\frac{1}{1-\theta}{\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{{\mathrm{d}}t}}}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{t})\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}+\frac{{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}{2}I_{\varepsilon}\\
&=\frac{1}{\theta}\Big({\sf E}(\gamma_{0})-{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})\Big)+\frac{1}{1-\theta}\Big({\sf E}(\gamma_{1})-{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})\Big)+\frac{{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}{2}I_{\epsilon}.\end{split}

Now let us multiply by
\theta(1-\theta)>0
and rearrange the terms in order to get

{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})\leq(1-\theta){\sf E}(\gamma_{0})+\theta{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})+\theta(1-\theta)\frac{{\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})}{2}I_{\varepsilon}.

It is easy to check that
\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}H_{\theta}(t){\mathrm{d}}t=\frac{1}{2}
for all
\theta
, so that

\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}I_{\varepsilon}=-2\lambda\intop\nolimits_{0}^{1}H_{\theta}(t)\,{{\mathrm{d}}t}=-\lambda.

On the other hand, by definition of
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
and since
h(\theta)=\varepsilon\to 0
it is clear that
\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\theta}=\mathsf{S}_{h(\theta)}\gamma_{\theta}=\mathsf{S}_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{\theta}\to\gamma_{\theta}
in
{\rm X}
(an
{\rm EVI}_{\lambda}
-gradient flow is continuous up to
t=0
).
By lower semicontinuity of
{\sf E}
this yields

{\sf E}(\gamma_{\theta})\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}{\sf E}(\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{\theta})\leq(1-\theta){\sf E}(\gamma_{0})+\theta{\sf E}(\gamma_{1})-\frac{\lambda}{2}\theta(1-\theta){\sf d}^{2}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}),

whence the conclusion.
∎

5
Derivative of the cost

As a main application of the
\Gamma
-convergence results contained in Theorem
4.3
and Corollary
4.4
(and, in a wider sense, of their strategy of proof), in this section we investigate the dependence of the optimal value of the Schrödinger problem (

Sch
ε

) on the regularization parameter
\varepsilon
, focusing in particular on the regularity as a function of
\varepsilon
and on the behaviour in the small-time regime.
More precisely, and denoting

\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=\inf_{(\gamma_{t})\,:\,x\leadsto y}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma)\Big\},\qquad\forall\varepsilon\geq 0

the optimal entropic cost, we show that
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is (locally) absolutely continuous and admits explicit left and right derivatives in a pointwise sense. The strategy of proof follows an interpolation argument due to De Giorgi.
Moreover, since
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\to\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
by Corollary
4.4
, we aim at measuring the error
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)
and studying the minimizers of the unperturbed problem
\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)
selected by
\Gamma
-convergence.
Since we focus here on the dependence on
\varepsilon
we will assume throughout the whole Section
5
and without further mention the well-posedness of the
\varepsilon
-Schrödinger problem:

Assumption 5.1
.

Fix
x,y\in{\rm X}
and suppose that for some (hence for any, by Proposition
4.2
)
\varepsilon>0
the Schrödinger problem (

Sch
ε

) admits at least one minimizer, in other words the infimum is attained in the definition of
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
.

We accordingly denote the set of
\varepsilon
-minimizers as

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=\Big\{\omega\in AC^{2}([0,1],{\rm X})\,:\,\omega_{0}=x,\,\omega_{1}=y\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega)=\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\Big\}.

Let us start the analysis with a preliminary monotonicity statement for the Fisher information and the entropic cost, which generalizes
[
25
, Lemma 3.3]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
and for any
0\leq\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\infty
there holds

\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\geq\sup_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I},

with possibly
\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}=+\infty
.
Moreover,
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is monotone non-decreasing on
[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
as in the statement and choose
\omega^{i}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{i}}(x,y)
for
i=1,2
, so that by optimality

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}(\omega^{1})+\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{1})&\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{2})+\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{2}),\\
\mathcal{A}(\omega^{2})+\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{2})&\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{1})+\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{1}).\end{split}

Summing these inequalities and dividing by
\varepsilon_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon_{1}^{2}
we obtain
\mathcal{I}(\omega^{1})\geq\mathcal{I}(\omega^{2})
, and since
\omega^{1}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{1}}
and
\omega^{2}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{2}}
are arbitrary the desired conclusion follows.
As regards the last part of the statement, it is sufficient to note that since
\omega^{i}
are minimizers of their respective problems and
\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}
,

\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x,y)=\mathcal{A}(\omega^{1})+\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{1})\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{2})+\varepsilon_{1}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{2})\leq\mathcal{A}(\omega^{2})+\varepsilon_{2}^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{2})=\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y).

∎

Let us then extend Theorem
4.3
and Corollary
4.4
from
\varepsilon=0
to any
\varepsilon\geq 0
.

Proposition 5.3
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
and under the additional Assumption
3.2
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there holds

\Gamma-\lim_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}+\iota_{01}\Big\}=\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}+\iota_{01}

(5.1)

for the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology and

\lim_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x,y)=\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y).

Moreover, for any
\varepsilon_{k}\to\varepsilon
and any minimizer
\omega^{k}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{k}}(x,y)
, there exists a minimizer
\omega\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
such that, up to a subsequence,

\omega_{t}^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\omega_{t},\qquad\forall t\in[0,1]

as
k\to\infty
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to prove (
5.1
), as the other properties follow by a verbatim application of the arguments in the proof of Corollary
4.4
.

Fix
\varepsilon
and take
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon
.
The
\Gamma-\limsup
inequality is trivial:
if
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
is such that the right-hand side of (
5.1
) is finite (otherwise there is nothing to prove), then the constant sequence
\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\equiv\gamma^{\varepsilon}
is an admissible recovery sequence.
For the
\Gamma-\liminf
inequality, note that the kinetic action
\mathcal{A}
and the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}
are lower semicontinuous w.r.t. pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-convergence (see the proof of Proposition
4.2
), and clearly so is the convex indicator.
Hence for any
\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
converging to
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
for the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology it holds

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon})&\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\Big\}\\
&=\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\Big\}\\
&=\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\Big\{\mathcal{A}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\mathcal{I}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\Big\}\\
&=\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\Big\{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+\iota_{01}(\gamma^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\Big\}.\end{split}

∎

As an immediate consequence of this result we deduce the following

Lemma 5.4
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
and under Assumption
3.2
, the function
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is continuous on
[0,\infty)
.

Moreover, if
\varepsilon\mapsto\omega^{\varepsilon}
is a continuous (w.r.t. the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology) selection of minimizers, then
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
are also continuous, on
[0,\infty)
and
(0,\infty)
respectively.

Note that if the minimizers are unique, then
\varepsilon\mapsto\omega^{\varepsilon}
is automatically continuous w.r.t. the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology, simply by Proposition
5.3
, as any sequence of minimizers admits a subsequence converging to a minimizer and the limit is in fact unique.
Also, the continuity of the Fisher information can be strengthened up to
\varepsilon=0
, see later on Theorem
5.8
.

Proof.

The continuity of
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
for
\varepsilon>0
is granted by Proposition
5.3
, while continuity at
\varepsilon=0
has already been proved in Corollary
4.4
.

As regards the kinetic energy
\mathcal{A}
and the Fisher information
\mathcal{I}
, recall that they are both lower semicontinuous in
[0,\infty)
w.r.t. the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology, as already discussed in the proof of Proposition
4.2
. Thus, if
\varepsilon\mapsto\omega^{\varepsilon}
is as in the statement, we are left to prove that
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
and
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
are upper semicontinuous. To this aim, it is sufficient to observe that

\begin{split}\limsup_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})&=\limsup_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\Big\{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x,y)-(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\Big\}\\
&\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x,y)-\liminf_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\\
&\leq\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\varepsilon}),\end{split}

where the last inequality holds by the continuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
and the lower semicontinuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
.
Thus
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{A}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
is upper semicontinuous in
[0,\infty)
.
Interchanging
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{I}
and writing now
\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}(\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{A})
, the same argument shows that
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
is upper semicontinuous in
(0,\infty)
(continuity at
\varepsilon=0
will require a special treatment later).
∎

We have now all the ingredients to discuss the regularity of the cost
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
as a function of the noise parameter
\varepsilon
and explicitly compute its left and right derivatives.

Proposition 5.5
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Setting
3.1
and if Assumption
3.2
holds, the map
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is
AC_{loc}([0,\infty))
, left and right differentiable everywhere in
(0,\infty)
and, for any
\varepsilon>0
, the left and right derivatives are given by

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{-}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=2\varepsilon\max_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I},\qquad\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{+}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=2\varepsilon\min_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}

(5.2)

respectively, and the former (resp. latter) is left (resp. right) continuous.

Note that it is part of our statement that the maximum and the minimum are attained.

Remark 5.6
.

Heuristically, (
5.2
) is nothing but the envelope theorem.
Indeed, if
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
were differentiable, then its derivative would be given by
\partial_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}=2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}
evaluated at any critical point, i.e. at any
\omega^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
.
However, since we do not know in our general metric framework that Schrödinger problem has a unique solution, we are not able to prove pointwise differentiability as in
[
25
]
and we have to face the possibility of a gap between the left and right derivatives.
In any case, for a.e.
\varepsilon>0
this gap is zero, because
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is locally absolutely continuous in
(0,\infty)
and therefore a.e. differentiable.
This means that, up to a negligible set of temperatures, the left and right derivatives match and
\mathcal{I}
is constant on
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
.
All these facts as well as the strategy of proof closely follow a variational interpolation argument which adapts the envelope theorem to the non-smooth setting and, to the best of our knowledge, dates back to De Giorgi.

If for whatever reason the Schrödinger problem (

Sch
ε

) were uniquely solvable (which is in particular true for the classic Schrödinger problem, as proved in
[
40
, Theorem 4.2]
), then the left and right derivatives would be trivially equal and Lemma
5.4
would give that
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is actually
C^{1}((0,\infty))
.

\blacksquare

Proof.

The continuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
follows by Lemma
5.4
, so let us focus on left and right differentiability/continuity and local absolute continuity.

Right differentiability
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
, let
\delta>0
, and choose
\omega^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
,
\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)
. Then write

\begin{split}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}&=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon})}{\delta}\\
&=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon})}{\delta}+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon})}{\delta}\end{split}

(5.3)

and note that the second term on the right-hand side can be rewritten as

\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon})=(2\varepsilon\delta+\delta^{2})\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon}).

The first one is non-positive by optimality of
\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta}
for
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}
, hence we obtain

\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}\leq\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\,(2\varepsilon+\delta)\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})=2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon}).

As this inequality holds for any
\omega^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
, we infer that

\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}\leq 2\varepsilon\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

(5.4)

On the other hand we can also write

\begin{split}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}&=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon})}{\delta}\\
&=\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})}{\delta}+\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon})}{\delta}.\end{split}

(5.5)

Using now the optimality of
\omega^{\varepsilon}
for
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}
, we observe that the second term on the right-hand side is non-negative, whence

\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon})}{\delta}\geq\frac{\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})-\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta})}{\delta}=(2\varepsilon+\delta)\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta}).

For any sequence
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
, Proposition
5.3
guarantees (up to extraction of a subsequence if needed) that
\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta_{n}}\to\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
in the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology for some
\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
.
By lower semicontinuity of
\mathcal{I}
this implies

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta_{n}}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta_{n}}\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}(2\varepsilon+\delta_{n})\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta_{n}})\geq 2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}(\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})\geq 2\varepsilon\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I},

and together with (
5.4
) this yields

\exists\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta_{n}}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta_{n}}=2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}(\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon})=2\varepsilon\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

As the right-hand side does not depend on the particular sequence
\delta_{n}\downarrow 0
we conclude that

\exists\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}=2\varepsilon\min_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I},

in particular
\mathcal{I}
is minimized by any accumulation point
\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
of
\{\omega^{\varepsilon+\delta}\}_{\delta>0}
.

Left differentiability
.
The argument is very similar.
Indeed, if
\delta<0
, then the first term on the right-hand side of (
5.3
) is non-negative and the second one can be handled in the same way.
Hence there holds

\liminf_{\delta\uparrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}\geq 2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon}),

for any
\omega^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
, and therefore

\liminf_{\delta\uparrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}\geq 2\varepsilon\sup_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

Applying the same considerations to (
5.5
) and following the same argument as above we retrieve the
\limsup
inequality, first along
some
subsequence
\delta_{n}\uparrow 0
and then along
any

\delta\uparrow 0
.
Combining with the inequality above gives

\exists\lim_{\delta\uparrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}=2\varepsilon\max_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I},\qquad\forall\varepsilon>0,

whence the pointwise left differentiability of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
.

Left and right continuity
.
In order to prove the right continuity of the right derivative of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
, note that on the one hand by Lemma
5.2
for any
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow\varepsilon
it holds

\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

On the other hand, we can assume up to a subsequence if needed that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

As shown in the proof of right differentiability,
\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}
is attained for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, hence in particular
\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}(\omega^{n})
for some
\omega^{n}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)
, for all
n
.
Up to extracting a further subsequence, by Proposition
5.3
we can assume that
\omega^{n}\to\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}
w.r.t. the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology for some
\overline{\omega}^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
, and moreover by Lemma
5.4

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{n})=\mathcal{I}(\overline{\omega})\geq\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

Putting all these inequalities together provides us with the right continuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}
and, a fortiori, of the right derivative. Left continuity for the left derivative follows along an analogous reasoning.

Local absolute continuity
.
Let
0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\infty
and, for any
0<\delta<1
, define

f_{\delta}(\varepsilon):=\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}{\delta}.

The monotonicity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
from Lemma
5.2
gives
f_{\delta}\geq 0
.
Arguing as in the very beginning of the proof of the right differentiability we see that
f_{\delta}(\varepsilon)\leq(2\varepsilon+1)\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\varepsilon})
for any
\omega^{\varepsilon}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
, and by Lemma
5.2

f_{\delta}(\varepsilon)\leq(2\varepsilon_{2}+1)\sup_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}<\infty,\qquad\forall\varepsilon\in(\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}].

Hence
|f_{\delta}|\leq M
uniformly in
\delta
and
f_{\delta}
converges pointwise to the right derivative of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
as
\delta\downarrow 0
, whence by the dominated convergence theorem

\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{+}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{2}}f_{\delta}(\varepsilon)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon.

The right-hand side can be rewritten as

\begin{split}\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{2}}f_{\delta}(\varepsilon)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon&=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\Big(\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon+\delta}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\Big)\\
&=\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\Big(\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{2}}^{\varepsilon_{2}+\delta}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\delta}\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{1}+\delta}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon\Big)\\
&=\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x,y),\end{split}

where the last equality holds by the Lebesgue differentiation theorem for the continuous function
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
(cf. Lemma
5.4
).
We have thus proved that the cost belongs to
AC_{loc}((0,\infty))
, since

\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{1}}(x,y)=\intop\nolimits_{\varepsilon_{1}}^{\varepsilon_{2}}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{+}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\,{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon,\qquad\forall\,0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}.

For the full
AC_{loc}([0,\infty))
regularity it is then sufficient to let
\varepsilon_{1}\downarrow 0
:
the left-hand side converges to
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon_{2}}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)
by Lemma
5.4
, and by the monotonicity
\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{+}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
the right-hand side also converges by monotone convergence.
∎

As a consequence we get

Corollary 5.7
.

The map
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is locally semiconcave, and more precisely for any
\varepsilon_{0}>0
there holds

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon^{2}}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq 2\max\limits_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{0}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}<+\infty

in the sense of distributions in
(\varepsilon_{0},+\infty)
.
In particular
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is twice differentiable almost everywhere.

Proof.

From Proposition
5.5
we know that, being absolutely continuous, the map
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
is differentiable a.e., in particular the left and right derivatives agree and thus

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x,y):=\max_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}=\min_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}

for a.a.
\varepsilon>0
with

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)

in the sense of distributions.
From Lemma
5.2

\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}
is non-increasing in
\varepsilon
, hence one can legitimately compute in the distributional sense

\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{2}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon^{2}}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\left\{2\varepsilon\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\right\}=2\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)+2\varepsilon\frac{{\mathrm{d}}}{{\mathrm{d}}\varepsilon}\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq 2\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)

and the conclusion follows again by monotonicity for
\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon_{0}
.
The a.e. twice differentiability follows by Alexandrov’s theorem.
∎

Relying on our previous auxiliary results and on Proposition
5.5
, we are finally in position of estimating the error
\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)
with
o(\varepsilon^{2})
precision.
We will also significantly refine Corollary
4.4
by proving that any accumulation point of any sequence of minimizers is not only optimal for the unperturbed problem
\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)
, but also
\mathcal{I}
-minimizing among all competitors in
\Lambda_{0}(x,y)
.

Theorem 5.8
.

With the same assumptions and notations as in Proposition
5.5
, if there exists
\omega^{0}\in\Lambda_{0}(x,y)
such that
\mathcal{I}(\omega^{0})<\infty
, then the map

is right differentiable also at
\varepsilon=0
with

the right derivative is right continuous for any
\varepsilon\geq 0
, and

\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)=\varepsilon^{2}\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}+o(\varepsilon^{2}).

(5.6)

Moreover, for any
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
and any minimizer
\omega^{n}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)
there exists
\omega^{*}\in\Lambda_{0}(x,y)
such that (up to a subsequence)
\omega^{n}\to\omega^{*}
for the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology, and
\omega^{*}
has minimal Fisher information in
\Lambda_{0}(x,y)

\mathcal{I}(\omega^{*})=\min_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

Proof.

The right differentiability of
\varepsilon\mapsto\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)
at
\varepsilon=0
follows by the same argument carried out in Proposition
5.5
. Indeed, given
\omega^{0}
as in the statement, by (
5.3
) with
\varepsilon=0
it holds

\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)}{\delta}\leq\limsup_{\delta\downarrow 0}\delta\mathcal{I}(\omega^{0})=0.

The liminf inequality is straightforward, since
\mathcal{I}\geq 0
and thus by (
5.5
) with
\varepsilon=0

\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathscr{C}_{\delta}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)}{\delta}\geq\liminf_{\delta\downarrow 0}\delta\mathcal{I}(\omega^{\delta})\geq 0

for any
\omega^{\delta}\in\Lambda_{\delta}(x,y)
.
This also shows that the right derivative vanishes at
\varepsilon=0
.

As regards the right continuity of the right derivative, the case
\varepsilon>0
has already been discussed in Proposition
5.5
.
For
\varepsilon=0
the same strategy still works, with the only minor difference that we cannot rely on Lemma
5.4
anymore.
Nonetheless, if
\omega^{n}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)
is as in Proposition
5.5
,
\overline{\omega}\in\Lambda_{0}(x,y)
and
\omega^{n}\to\overline{\omega}
for the pointwise-in-time
\sigma
-topology (the existence of such

is granted by Corollary

) it is still true that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{n})\geq\mathcal{I}(\overline{\omega}),

simply by lower semicontinuity of

.
With this single change in the proof we deduce that
\varepsilon\mapsto\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}
is right continuous and finite also at
\varepsilon=0
, thanks to the present assumptions, and so is the right derivative of the cost due to
\varepsilon\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

The last part of the statement is a slight modification of these lines of thought. Indeed, given any sequence
\varepsilon_{n}\downarrow 0
and
\omega^{n}\in\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)

\omega^{*}\in\Lambda_{0}(x,y)

\omega^{n}\to\omega^{*}

\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\geq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{n})\geq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{I}(\omega^{n})\geq\mathcal{I}(\omega^{*})\geq\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I},

where we used once again Lemma

.

Thus, it only remains to establish (
5.6
).
As

belongs to

\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)=2\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\varepsilon}s\inf_{\Lambda_{s}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\,{\mathrm{d}}s\leq 2\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\varepsilon}s\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\,{\mathrm{d}}s=\varepsilon^{2}\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

By the same monotonicity and the right continuity at

\begin{split}\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)&\geq 2\intop\nolimits_{0}^{\varepsilon}s\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\,{\mathrm{d}}s=\varepsilon^{2}\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\\
&=\varepsilon^{2}\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\inf_{\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}-\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}\Big)\\
&=\varepsilon^{2}\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}+o(\varepsilon^{2})\end{split}.

Combining this lower bound with the previous upper one entails (

).
∎

Remark 5.9
.

It is worth stressing that the upper bound (

) on

is always non-negative, we can rewrite (

|\mathscr{C}_{\varepsilon}(x,y)-\mathscr{C}_{0}(x,y)|\leq\varepsilon^{2}\inf_{\Lambda_{0}(x,y)}\mathcal{I}.

A possible way to further improve (

) would rely on a refined analysis of

, its derivative (which exists a.e. by monotonicity), and possibly absolute continuity.

\blacksquare

Remark 5.10

-minimizing property of the accumulation point

.

6.4
Mean-field Schrödinger problem

In the seminal thought experiment proposed by Schrödinger
[
60
,
61
]
the physical system, whose evolution between two subsequent observations has to be determined, consists of independent Brownian particles.
An important generalization has been recently proposed in
[
7
]
, where particles are allowed to interact through a pair potential
W
.
This leads to the so-called Mean Field Schrödinger Problem (MFSP henceforth), which can be cast as a metric Schrödinger problem by choosing the following setting.

Setting

•

Let
{\rm X}:=\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the 2-Wasserstein space over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, equipped with the
2
-Wasserstein distance
{\sf d}:=W_{2}
.

•

The role played by the Boltzmann-Shannon relative entropy in the “classical” Schrödinger problem is here taken by the functional
{\sf E}:{\rm X}\to\mathbb{R}
defined (up to a shift by a constant) by

{\sf E}(\mu):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{H(\mu\,|\,\mathcal{L}^{d})+\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}W*\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu}&\qquad\text{ if }\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d}\\
+\infty&\qquad\text{ if }\mu\nll\mathcal{L}^{d}\end{array}\right.

where
H(\mu\,|\,\mathcal{L}^{d})
is the Boltzmann-Shannon relative entropy of
\mu
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{L}^{d}
, already introduced in Section
6.2
, and
W
is the pair potential, describing via convolution the interaction between the particles of the system. On such a potential the following assumptions are made: it is of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
, is symmetric, i.e.
W(x)=W(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and satisfies the two-sided bound

\Lambda\mathrm{Id}~\geq\nabla^{2}W\geq\lambda\mathrm{Id}

for some
\Lambda,\lambda>0
(actually
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is enough, but in
[
7
]
the authors are interested in the ergodic behaviour of MFSP). While the upper bound is technical, the lower one is geometric and crucial.

•

As topology
\sigma
we shall use the metric topology of
({\rm X},W_{p})
,
1\leq p<2
.

Indeed, the fact that the MFSP coincides with (

Sch
ε

) with the above choice of
({\rm X},{\sf d})
and
{\sf E}
follows from
[
7
, Theorem 1.2]
and the fact that the slope of
{\sf E}
is explicitly given by

|\partial{\sf E}|^{2}(\mu)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\intop\nolimits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla\log\rho+2\nabla W*\rho|^{2}\,{\mathrm{d}}\mu}&\qquad\text{ if }\mu=\rho\mathcal{L}^{d},\,\nabla\log\rho\in L^{2}_{\mu},\\
+\infty&\qquad\text{ otherwise},\end{array}\right.

cf.
[
7
, Section 1.4.2]
and
[
2
, Theorem 10.4.13]
.

Applicability of our results

In order to see that our abstract metric results hold for this specific example, let us check separately the validity of Setting
3.1
and Assumption
3.2
.

•

Setting
3.1
fully holds. As already said in Section
6.2
,
{\rm X}:=\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a complete and separable metric space. The lower semicontinuity of

is easily seen to hold: the relative entropy has already been discussed, whereas the continuity of the convolution term follows from the fact that if

The fact that

is proper and the density of its domain are also clear.
Moreover, the assumptions on

is bounded from below on

-bounded sets.
As concerns the existence of

-gradient flows starting from any

•

Assumption

is fully satisfied as well. Indeed, by the discussion in Section

we have the validity of Assumption

, so that it only remains to discuss the sequential lower semicontinuity of

w.r.t.
