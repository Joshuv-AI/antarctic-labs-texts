Natural observables and dynamical approximations in multifield cosmological models
C. I. Lazaroiu

Abstract
I give a geometric construction of certain first order natural dynamical observables in multifield cosmological models with arbitrary target space topology and discuss a system of related dynamical approximations and regimes for such models.



keywords
cosmology; differential geometry; dynamical systems.

1
Introduction

Cosmological models with more that one scalar field (“inflaton”) are
natural and preferred in quantum theories of gravity
[
AP
]
and have
acquired increasing importance in recent years, being subject to
renewed study from various perspectives
[
Lilia1
,
Lilia2
,
LiliaRev
,
Paban
,
AL
,
Noether1
,
Noether2
,
Hesse
]
.

Despite their importance in connecting cosmology to supergravity and
string theory, such models are poorly understood when compared to
their one field counterparts – in particular because their dynamics
can be very involved already before considering
perturbations. Moreover, the scalar fields of such models can be
valued in an arbitrary connected Riemannian manifold (called
scalar manifold
) which is generally non-compact. Hence a systematic
study must consider scalar manifolds of arbitrary connected topology.

When the Hubble parameter is positive, the background dynamics of
multifield models can be described mathematically
[
ren
]
using a
geometric dynamical system
[
Palis
,
Katok
]
which is defined on the
tangent bundle to the scalar manifold. The general behavior of this
system can be extremely non-trivial already in the case of models with
two scalar fields
[
genalpha
,
elem
,
modular
,
unif
,
Nis
,
Tim19
]
.

In general, one cannot hope to solve multifield cosmological dynamics
exactly or even to approach it numerically in a satisfactory manner
when the number of scalar fields (i.e. the dimension of the scalar
manifold) is large – and even less so when the topology of the scalar
manifold is involved. Hence the best hope for practical progress is
to develop approximation schemes which might allow one to make
progress – such as the IR and UV expansions
[
ren
,
grad
]
. To do
this in general, one needs a geometric description of the cosmological
observables which control various approximations and regimes of
physical interest while taking into account the topology of the scalar
manifold. In this paper, I give such a description for a collection of
natural cosmological observables and discuss some aspects of the
approximation schemes which they suggest.

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
recalls
the description of general multifield cosmological models and gives a
geometric treatment of their scalar and vector observables. Section

3
gives a careful geometric construction of certain first
order observables (some of which are obtained by “on-shell”
reduction of second order observables) for scalar manifolds of
arbitrary topology, discusses some relations between them and describes
various regions of interest which they define inside the tangent
bundle of the scalar manifold. Section
discusses two
dynamical approximations which are suggested by the first slow roll
conditions. Section
discusses the conservative and
dissipative approximations, which are controlled by the
conservative function
of the model – one of the basic natural
observables introduced in Section
3
. It also gives an
overview of other natural approximations and their relations to the
conservative and dissipative regimes. Section
briefly
describes the limits of large and small Planck mass, relating them to
the conservative and IR approximations. Section

presents our conclusions and a few directions for further research.

Notations and conventions.

All manifolds considered in the paper are smooth and paracompact. For
any manifold
\mathcal{M}
, we let
{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}
denote the slit tangent
bundle of
\mathcal{M}
, defined as the complement of the image of the zero
section in
T\mathcal{M}
. We denote by
\pi:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
the bundle
projection and by
F:=F\mathcal{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi^{\ast}(T\mathcal{M})
the
Finsler
bundle
[
SLK
]
of
\mathcal{M}
, which is a bundle over
T\mathcal{M}
. A section
of
F
defined over an open subset of
T\mathcal{M}
is called a
Finsler
vector field
. We denote by
\dot{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi^{\ast}(\dot{T}\mathcal{M})
the
slit Finsler bundle of
T\mathcal{M}
. A global section of
F
is the same as
a map
f:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
which satisfies
f(u)\in T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M}

for all
u\in T\mathcal{M}
, while a global section of
{\dot{F}}
is a map of
this type which also satisfies
f(u)\neq 0
for all
u
.

2
Multifield cosmological models

In this section we recall the geometric description of multifield
cosmological models with arbitrary target space topology (in the
absence of cosmological perturbations) and give a geometric treatment
of their local observables. We also discuss some natural constructions
afforded by the Finsler bundle of the scalar manifold of such models,
which will be useful in later sections.

2.1
Basics

Throughout this paper, a
multifield cosmological model

is a classical cosmological model with
d
scalar fields (where
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
is a fixed positive integer) which is derived from the
following action on a spacetime with topology
\mathbb{R}^{4}
:

S[g,\varphi]=\int\mathrm{vol}_{g}\mathcal{L}[g,\varphi]~~,

(1)

where:

\mathcal{L}[g,\varphi]=\frac{M^{2}}{2}\mathrm{R}(g)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{g}\varphi^{\ast}(\mathcal{G})-\Phi\circ\varphi~~.

(2)

Here
M
is the reduced Planck mass,
g
is the spacetime metric on

\mathbb{R}^{4}
(taken to be of “mostly plus” signature) while
\mathrm{vol}_{g}
and

\mathrm{R}(g)
are the volume form and Ricci scalar of
g
. The
scalar fields are described by a smooth map
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a (generally non-compact) connected, smooth and
paracompact manifold of dimension
d
which is endowed with a smooth
Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}
, while
\Phi:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth
function which plays the role of potential for the scalar fields. We
require that
\mathcal{G}
is complete to ensure conservation of energy. For
simplicity, we also assume that
\Phi
is strictly positive on

\mathcal{M}
. The ordered system
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
is called the
scalar triple

of the model while the Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
is called the
scalar manifold
.
The model is parameterized by the quadruplet:

\mathfrak{M}=(M_{0},\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)~~,

(3)

where:

M_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}M\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}

is the
rescaled Planck mass
. We denote by:

\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{m\in\mathcal{M}~|~(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(m)=0\}

the critical set of
\Phi
and by:

\mathcal{M}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}\setminus\mathrm{Crit}\Phi

the
noncritical set
of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
. For any
E>0
,
we denote by:

\mathcal{M}(E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{m\in\mathcal{M}~|~\Phi(m)<E\}

(4)

the corresponding open subcritical level set of
\Phi
and set:

\mathcal{M}_{0}(E)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{M}(E)\setminus\mathrm{Crit}\Phi~~.

(5)

The multifield cosmological model parameterized by the quadruplet
\mathfrak{M}
is obtained by assuming
that
g
is an FLRW metric with flat spatial section:

\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{g}=-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+a(t)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{d}x_{i}^{2}

(6)

(where
a
is a smooth and strictly positive function) and that
\varphi
depends only on the
cosmological time

t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x^{0}
. Define the
Hubble parameter

through:

H(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}~~,

where the dot indicates derivation with respect to
t
.

2.2
The cosmological equation

When
H>0
(which we assume throughout this paper), the
variational equations of (
1
) reduce to the
cosmological
equation
:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+\mathcal{H}(\dot{\varphi}(t))\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~

(7)

together with the condition:

H(t)=H_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{3}\mathcal{H}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~,

(8)

where
\mathcal{H}:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the
rescaled Hubble function
of

(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
, which is defined through:

\mathcal{H}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{M_{0}}\left[||u||^{2}+2\Phi(\pi(u))\right]^{1/2}~~\forall u\in T\mathcal{M}~~.

Here
\nabla_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\nabla_{\dot{\varphi}(t)}
and
\pi:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is
the bundle projection. The solutions

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
of (
7
) (where
I
is a
non-degenerate interval) are called
cosmological curves
, while
their images in
\mathcal{M}
are called
cosmological orbits
.

A cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
need not be an immersion.
Accordingly, we define the
singular and regular parameter sets

of
\varphi
through:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{sing}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\dot{\varphi}(t)=0\}\subset I

(9)

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{reg}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}I\setminus I_{\mathrm{sing}}=\{t\in I~|~\dot{\varphi}(t)\neq 0\}\subset I~~.

(10)

It can be shown that
I_{\mathrm{sing}}
is an at most countable set.
The sets of
critical and noncritical times
of
\varphi
are defined through:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{crit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\}

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{noncrit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}I\setminus I_{\mathrm{crit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\varphi(t)\not\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\}~~.

(11)

The cosmological curve
\varphi
is called
noncritical
if

I_{\mathrm{crit}}=\emptyset
, i.e. if its orbit is contained in the non-critical set

\mathcal{M}_{0}
. It is easy to see that a cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}

is constant iff its orbit coincides with a critical point of
\Phi
, which
in turn happens iff there exists some
t\in I_{\mathrm{sing}}
such that
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi
.
Hence for any non-constant cosmological curve we have:

I_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap I_{\mathrm{crit}}=\emptyset~~.

Given a cosmological curve
\varphi
, relation (
8
)
determines
a
up to a multiplicative constant. The cosmological
equation can be reduced to first order
[
SLK
]
by passing to the tangent
bundle of
\mathcal{M}
. This interpretation arises
[
ren
]
by
viewing the second order time derivative
\ddot{\varphi}(t)
appearing
in the expression:

\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}^{i}(t)=\ddot{\varphi}(t)+\Gamma^{i}_{jk}(\varphi(t))\dot{\varphi}^{j}(t)\dot{\varphi}^{k}(t)

as an element of the double tangent bundle
TT\mathcal{M}
and the
opposite of the remaining terms of (
7
) as defining a
vector field
S\in\mathcal{X}(T\mathcal{M})
(called the
cosmological semispray

or
second order vector field
of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
) which is
defined on
T\mathcal{M}
. Then (
7
) is
equivalent with the integral curve equation of
S
. The flow of this
vector field on the total space of
T\mathcal{M}
is called the
cosmological flow
of the model.

2.3
Cosmological observables

For any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, let
\pi^{k}:J^{k}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
denote the bundle of
k
-order jets of curves in
\mathcal{M}
.

Definition 2.1
.

An (off-shell) scalar local cosmological
observable of order
k\geq 0
is a map
f:J^{k}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The observable is called basic if it has order
k=1
; in
this case,
f
is a real-valued function defined on
T\mathcal{M}
.

A simple example of basic cosmological observable is the
norm function
||~||:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
,
which is continuous everywhere but smooth only on the slit tangent
bundle
{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}
. Another example is the rescaled Hubble function

\mathcal{H}:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Given a cosmological observable
f
of order
k
and a
curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
, the evaluation

f_{\varphi}:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
of
f
along
\varphi
is the function
defined though:

f_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}f\circ j^{k}(\varphi):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}~~,

where
j^{k}(\varphi):I\rightarrow J^{k}(\mathcal{M})
is the
k
-th jet
prolongation of
\varphi
.

Remark 2.3
.

Notice that a cosmological observable
f
is completely determined by
its evaluation
f_{\varphi}
on arbitrary curves
\varphi
in

\mathcal{M}
. Accordingly, one can describe such an observable by giving its
evaluation on an arbitrary curve rather than using the jet bundle
description.

The cosmological equation defines a codimension
d
closed submanifold

\mathfrak{S}
(called the
cosmological shell
) of the total space of

J^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, the second order jet bundle of curves of
\mathcal{M}
(which is a
bundle of rank
2d
over
\mathcal{M}
). Notice that
\mathfrak{S}
is the image of a
section
\mathfrak{s}:J^{1}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{2}(\mathcal{M})
of the natural surjection

J^{2}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{1}(\mathcal{M})
with local coordinate expression:

\mathfrak{s}^{i}=-\Gamma^{i}_{jk}\dot{\varphi}^{j}\dot{\varphi}^{k}-\frac{1}{M_{0}}\left[\mathcal{G}_{kl}(\varphi)\dot{\varphi}^{k}\dot{\varphi}^{l}+2\Phi(\varphi)\right]^{1/2}\dot{\varphi}^{i}-\mathcal{G}^{ij}(\varphi)(\partial_{j}\Phi)(\varphi)~~.

In the jet bundle interpretation, the cosmological equation (
7
) reads:

\ddot{\varphi}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}^{i}(\varphi^{1},\ldots,\varphi^{d},\dot{\varphi}^{1},\ldots,\dot{\varphi}^{d})

(12)

and amounts to the defining equations of
\mathfrak{S}
. We say that
\mathfrak{s}
is
the
cosmological section
of
J^{2}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{1}(\mathcal{M})
. Formal differentiation of (
12
) with respect to time defines
sections
\mathfrak{s}^{(k)}:J^{k}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{k+1}(\mathcal{M})
of the projections

J^{k+1}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{k}(\mathcal{M})
for all
k\geq 1
, where

\mathfrak{s}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathfrak{s}
. These determine the higher order prolongations
of (
12
), whose iterative use allows us to express all formal
higher time derivatives of
\varphi
in terms of
\varphi
and

\dot{\varphi}
. In turn, this gives sections

\mathfrak{s}_{k}:J^{1}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{k+1}(\mathcal{M})
of the natural maps

J^{k+1}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{1}(\mathcal{M})
for all
k\geq 1
.

Definition 2.4
.

The dynamical reduction (or on-shell reduction) of a
cosmological observable
f
of order
k\geq 2
is the basic
cosmological observable
f^{\mathrm{red}}:J^{1}(\mathcal{M})=T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined
through:

f^{\mathrm{red}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}f\circ\mathfrak{s}_{k-1}~~.

The following statement is immediate:

Proposition 1
.

For any cosmological curve
\varphi
, we have:

f_{\varphi}=f^{\mathrm{red}}_{\varphi}=f^{\mathrm{red}}\circ\dot{\varphi}~~.

One can also consider local observables of order
k
defined on
non-empty open subsets
U\subset J^{k}(\mathcal{M})
; the considerations above
apply to this situation with very minor modifications. Finally,
one can define
vector local cosmological observables
of order

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
as local sections
{\bf f}
of the
k
-th Finsler
bundle
:

F^{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(\pi^{k})^{\ast}(T\mathcal{M})\rightarrow J^{k}(\mathcal{M})

defined on a non-empty open set
U\subset J^{k}(\mathcal{M})
(notice that

F^{1}=F
is the ordinary Finsler bundle of
\mathcal{M}
). Such observables are
called
basic
if
k=1
, in which case
{\bf f}\in\Gamma(T\mathcal{M},F)
can
be viewed as a map from an open subset
U
of
T\mathcal{M}
to
T\mathcal{M}
which
satisfies
{\bf f}(u)\in T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M}
for all
u\in U
. The evaluation
of a local vector observable
{\bf f}
of order
k
on a curve

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
whose
k
-th prolongation is contained in
U
produces a section

{\bf f}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}[j^{k}(\varphi)]^{\ast}({\bf f})\in\Gamma(I,\varphi^{\ast}(T\mathcal{M}))
, i.e. a map with associates to any
t\in I
a vector
{\bf f}_{\varphi}(t)\in T_{\varphi(t)}\mathcal{M}
. The dynamical
reduction
{\bf f}^{\mathrm{red}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(\mathfrak{s}_{k-1})^{\ast}({\bf f})
is a local section of the Finsler bundle
F\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
which
satisfies:

{\bf f}_{\varphi}=(\dot{\varphi})^{\ast}({\bf f}^{\mathrm{red}})

when
\varphi
is a cosmological curve. We will encounter such
vector observables later on.

The cosmological energy function and rescaled Hubble function.

Definition 2.5
.

The cosmological energy function
is the basic cosmological observable
E:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
defined through:

E(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}||u||^{2}+\Phi(\pi(u))~~\forall u\in T\mathcal{M}~~.

The cosmological
kinetic energy function
E_{\mathrm{kin}}:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and the
cosmological potential energy function
E_{\mathrm{pot}}:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

are the basic cosmological observables defined through:

E_{\mathrm{kin}}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}||u||^{2}~~,~~E_{\mathrm{pot}}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\Phi(\pi(u))~~\forall u\in T\mathcal{M}~~.

(13)

With these definitions, we have:

E=E_{\mathrm{kin}}+E_{\mathrm{pot}}~~.

Notice that
E_{\mathrm{pot}}
coincides with the natural lift of
\Phi
to
T\mathcal{M}
:

E_{\mathrm{pot}}=\pi^{\ast}(\Phi)=\Phi\circ\pi~~.

Also notice the relation:

\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{2E}~~.

Proposition 2
.

The evaluation of the cosmological energy along any
cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
satisfies the cosmological dissipation equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{\varphi}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\sqrt{2E_{\varphi}(t)}}{M_{0}}||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}~~.

(14)

Proof 2.6
.

Follows immediately by using the cosmological equation (
7
).

Notice that the rescaled Hubble function
\mathcal{H}:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a basic cosmological
observable. The dissipation equation is equivalent with:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}

(15)

for any cosmological curve
\varphi
. Integrating (
15
) from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}

along
\varphi
gives:

\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{1})-\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{2})=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}

Since the integral in the right hand side is minimized by the shortest geodesic
which connects the points
\varphi(t_{1})
to
\varphi(t_{2})
in
\mathcal{M}
, we have:

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}\geq\mathrm{d}(\varphi(t_{1}),\varphi(t_{2}))^{2}~~,

where
\mathrm{d}
is the distance function of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
. This gives the
Hubble inequality
:

\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{1})-\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{2})\geq\mathrm{d}(\varphi(t_{1}),\varphi(t_{2}))^{2}~~.

(16)

2.4
Some operations determined by the Finsler bundle of
\mathcal{M}

Recall that
\pi:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
denotes the bundle
projection. The following two fiber bundles defined on
T\mathcal{M}
:

F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi^{\ast}(T\mathcal{M})~~,~~\dot{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi^{\ast}(\dot{T}\mathcal{M})\subset F

are called respectively the
Finsler
and
slit Finsler
bundle
of
T\mathcal{M}
(see
[
SLK
]
). The
\pi
-pullback of
\mathcal{G}
(which we
denote by the same letter) makes
F
into a Euclidean vector bundle, which contains

\dot{F}
as open fiber sub-bundle. The sections of
F
defined over
a non-empty open subset
U\subset T\mathcal{M}
are called
Finsler vector fields
.
A Finsler vector field can be viewed as a map
f:U\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}

which satisfies the condition:

\pi\circ f=\pi|_{U}~~,

i.e. which takes a vector tangent to
\mathcal{M}
at a point into vector
tangent to
\mathcal{M}

at the same point
. The Finsler bundle
allows us to give a global geometric description to various
operations on tangent vectors.

The normalization map.

The
normalization map
of
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})
is the nowhere-vanishing Finsler
vector field
T\in\Gamma({\dot{T}}\mathcal{M},\dot{F})
defined on

\dot{T}\mathcal{M}
through:

T(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{u}{||u||}\in{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}~~\forall u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M}~~.

Remark 2.7
.

Given a curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
and
t\in I_{\mathrm{reg}}
, the unit tangent vector
to
\varphi
at time
t
is given by:

T_{\varphi}(t)=T(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~.

The map
T_{\varphi}:I_{\mathrm{reg}}\rightarrow T\mathcal{M}
identifies with the pull-back of
T
through the
restriction of
\dot{\varphi}
to
I_{\mathrm{reg}}
.

The parallel and normal projection of vector fields.

For any vector field
X
defined on
\mathcal{M}
, let
X^{\parallel},X^{\perp}\in\Gamma({\dot{T}}\mathcal{M},F)
be the Finsler vector fields defined on
{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}
through:

\displaystyle X^{\parallel}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{G}(T(u),X(\pi(u)))T(u)\in T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle X^{\perp}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}X(\pi(u))-X^{\parallel}(u)\in T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M}~~,

for all
u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M}
. For any
u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M}
, we have:

\mathcal{G}(X^{\parallel}(u),X^{\perp}(u))=0~~,~~X(u)=X^{\parallel}(u)+X^{\perp}(u)~~.

The vectors
X^{\parallel}(u)
and
X^{\perp}(u)
are respectively the
orthogonal projections of the vector
X(\pi(u))\in T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M}
on

u
and on the hyperplane
\Pi(u)\subset T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M}
which is
orthogonal to
u
in the Euclidean vector space

(T_{\pi(u)}\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G}_{\pi(u)})
.

3
Natural basic observables

In this section, we give a geometric description of certain basic
cosmological observables which play a special role in the study of
multifield cosmological models. Some of these are defined directly on

T\mathcal{M}
, while others are obtained as the on-shell reduction of
second order observables. Since cosmological observables are
determined by their evaluation on arbitrary curves (see Section

2
), we will use that description instead of the jet
bundle formulation.

3.1
The first IR function of a scalar triple

A first natural basic observable is the
first
IR function
considered in
[
ren
]
, which plays a crucial role in
the infrared dynamics of multifield cosmological models. This is
closely related to the
first slow roll
function
(which is the on-shell reduction of a second order observable).

Definition 3.1
.

The first IR function of the scalar triple
(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)
is
the basic cosmological observable
\kappa:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

defined through:

\kappa(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{E_{\mathrm{kin}}(u)}{E_{\mathrm{pot}}(u)}=\frac{||u||^{2}}{2\Phi(\pi(u))}~~\forall u\in T\mathcal{M}~~.

(17)

Notice that
\kappa
is continuous on

T\mathcal{M}
but smooth only on the slit tangent bundle
\dot{T}\mathcal{M}
. Also
notice that
\kappa(u)
depends only on
\pi(u)\in\mathcal{M}
and
||u||\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, i.e. for all
m\in\mathcal{M}
we have:

The first slow roll “parameter” of a cosmological curve.

Proposition 1

Suppose that
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
is a cosmological curve. Then

\varphi

the on-shell reduction of the (rescaled) first slow roll function along

\varphi

{\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}}_{\varphi}(t)={\hat{\bm{\varepsilon}}}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~\mathrm{and}~~\bm{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}(t)=\bm{\varepsilon}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

Proof 3.5
.

We have:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{G}(\dot{\varphi}(t),\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t))+(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi(t))(\dot{\varphi}(t))}{||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}+2\Phi(\varphi(t))}=-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}}{||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}+2\Phi(\varphi(t))}=-\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)\kappa_{\varphi}(t)}{1+\kappa_{\varphi}(t)}~~,

where in the second equality we used the cosmological equation and the
relation:

\mathcal{G}(\dot{\varphi}(t),(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t)))=(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi(t))(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~.

This immediately implies the conclusion.

A second natural basic observable is the

\end{definition}\par\par\par\noindent Notice that the rescaled acceleration field determines the conservative function:
\begin{equation*}c(u)=\frac{1}{||{\bf q}(u)||}=\frac{1}{||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(u)-n(u)||}~~\forall u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M}_{0}~~.\end{equation*}\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{paragraph}{toc}{The rescaled tangential acceleration function and normal acceleration field of $(\cM,\cG,\Phi)$.}
\par\par\begin{definition}The {rescaled tangential acceleration function} (a.k.a. ``second slow roll function'') ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}:{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is the basic cosmological observable defined
through:
\begin{equation}{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)=\mathcal{G}({\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(u),n(u))=1+\frac{(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\pi(u))(u)}{\mathcal{H}(u)||u||^{2}}=1+\frac{\cos\theta(u)}{c(u)}~~.\end{equation}The {rescaled normal acceleration field} ${\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}\in\Gamma({\dot{T}}\mathcal{M},F\mathcal{M})$ of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ is the Finsler vector
field defined on ${\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}$ through:
\begin{equation}{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}(u)={\bf q}^{\perp}(u)=\frac{(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)^{\perp}(u)}{\mathcal{H}(u)||u||}=\frac{n_{\Phi}^{\perp}(u)}{c(u)}~~.\end{equation}\end{definition}\par\par\noindent We have:
\begin{equation*}\bm{\eta}(u)={\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)n(u)+{\hat{\eta}}^{\perp}(u)~~\forall u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M}~~.\end{equation*}Notice that ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)=1$ and ${\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}(u)=0$ when $\pi(u)\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi$.
\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{paragraph}{toc}{The turning rate field of $(\cM,\cG,\Phi)$.}
\par\par\begin{definition}The {turning rate field} of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ is the Finsler vector field
$\bm{\Omega}\in\Gamma({\dot{T}}\mathcal{M},F\mathcal{M})$ given by:
\begin{equation*}\bm{\Omega}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)^{\perp}(u)}{||u||}~~(u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M})~~.\end{equation*}\end{definition}\par\noindent Relation \eqref{hvetaperp} reads:
\begin{equation}{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}(u)={\bf q}^{\perp}(u)=\frac{\bm{\Omega}(u)}{\mathcal{H}(u)}~~.\end{equation}\par The following result shows that the basic scalar and vector observables introduced above are the
on-shell reductions of the corresponding second order observables.
\par\begin{prop}Suppose that $\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}$ is a cosmological curve. Then
for any $t\in I_{\mathrm{reg}}$ we have:
\begin{equation*}{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}_{\varphi}(t)={\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~,~~{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{\varphi}(t)={\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~,~~{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}_{\varphi}(t)={\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~,~~\bm{\Omega}_{\varphi}(t)=\bm{\Omega}(\dot{\varphi(t)})~~.\end{equation*}\end{prop}\par\begin{proof}The rescaled acceleration of $\varphi$ can be expressed as follows
using the cosmological equation:
\begin{equation*}{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}_{\varphi}(t)=\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))}{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)||\dot{\varphi}(t)||}={\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~.\end{equation*}Thus:
\begin{equation}{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{\varphi}(t)=1+\frac{(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi(t))(\dot{\varphi}(t))}{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}}=1+M_{0}\frac{(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi(t))(\dot{\varphi}(t))}{(2\Phi(\varphi(t)))^{3/2}\kappa_{\varphi}(t)[1+\kappa_{\varphi}(t)]^{1/2}}\end{equation}and:
\begin{equation}{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}^{\perp}_{\varphi}(t)=\frac{(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)^{\perp}(\varphi(t))}{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)||\dot{\varphi}(t)||}=M_{0}\frac{(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)^{\perp}(\varphi(t))}{2\Phi(\varphi(t))[\kappa_{\varphi}(t)(1+\kappa_{\varphi}(t))]^{1/2}}~~,\end{equation}where we used \eqref{kappanorm}. This immediately gives the conclusion.
\end{proof}\par Summarizing, we consider four natural basic observables, namely
$\kappa$, $c$, ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}$ and $\bm{\Omega}$, the last of which is a vector
observable. We also introduce an auxiliary basic observable
$\theta$:
\par\begin{definition}The {characteristic angle function} of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ is the map $\theta:\dot{T}\mathcal{M}_{0}\rightarrow[0,\pi)$ defined through:
\begin{equation*}\cos\theta(u)=\mathcal{G}(T(u),n_{\Phi}(\pi(u)))~~\forall u\in\dot{T}\mathcal{M}~~,\end{equation*}where $n_{\Phi}\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{M}_{0})$ is the normalized gradient field of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$.
\end{definition}\par\noindent The following statement is immediate:
\par\begin{prop}For any curve $\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}$ and any $t\in I_{\mathrm{reg}}\cap I_{\mathrm{noncrit}}$, we have:
\begin{equation*}\theta_{\varphi}(t)=\theta(\dot{\varphi}(t))~~,\end{equation*}where $\theta_{\varphi}$ is the characteristic angle of $\varphi$.
\end{prop}\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Reconstruction of the scalar field metric and potential from $\kappa$ and $\hetap$}
\par\noindent Knowledge of the first and second slow-roll
functions (equivalently, knowledge of the first IR function and of the
second slow-roll function) allows one to reconstruct the positive
homothety class of the pair $(\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ when $M_{0}$ is fixed.
Indeed, relation \eqref{etaomega} gives:
\begin{equation}\Xi(\pi(u))(n(u))=[\kappa(u)(1+\kappa(u))]^{1/2}\left[-1+{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)\right]~~\forall u\in{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}~~,\end{equation}showing that $\kappa$ and ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}$ determine
$\Xi$. Since $\mathcal{M}$ is connected, this implies that $\kappa$ and
${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}$ determine the scalar potential $\Phi$ up to a
multiplicative constant $A>0$. Now relation \eqref{kappanorm}
determines $\mathcal{G}$ up to multiplication by $A$. Thus $\kappa$ and
$\eta^{\parallel}$ determine the positive homothety class of the pair
$(\mathcal{G},\Phi)$. In particular, the rescaled first and second slow-roll
functions encode the same information as this homothety class.

\par\begin{definition}The {no roll shell} of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ is the zero level set of
the second slow roll function ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}$:
\begin{equation*}\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{u\in{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}~|~{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)=0\}~~,\end{equation*}which is a closed codimension one submanifold of ${\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}_{0}$.
\end{definition}\par\noindent By the discussion of the previous subsection, the no roll
shell is a fiber sub-bundle of $T\mathcal{M}_{0}$ whose fiber at each $m\in\mathcal{M}_{0}$
is a connected surface of revolution around the axis determined by the vector
$(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(m)\in T_{m}\mathcal{M}$.
\par\begin{definition}We say that a cosmological curve $\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0}$
satisfies the {no roll condition} at time $t\in I_{\mathrm{reg}}$ if
$\dot{\varphi}(t)\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ i.e. if
${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(\dot{\varphi}(t))=0$.
\end{definition}\par\begin{remark}The no roll condition at time $t\in I_{\mathrm{reg}}$ means that the covariant
acceleration $\nabla_{t}\varphi(t)$ is orthogonal on $\dot{\varphi}(t)$.

\begin{equation*}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||\dot{\varphi}(t)||=0~~,\end{equation*}which means that the proper length parameter $s$ along the cosmological curve
(which satisfies $\dot{s}=||\dot{\varphi}(t)||$) has vanishing second derivative at
time $t$:
\begin{equation*}\ddot{s}=0~~.\end{equation*}\end{remark}\par\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The second slow roll condition}

\begin{equation*}\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{u\in{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}~|~|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)|<\epsilon\}\subset{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}~~.\end{equation*}\end{definition}\par\noindent Notice that $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\subset\mathcal{S}^{2}_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ for all $\epsilon\in(0,1]$.

\par\noindent Since:

\begin{equation*}C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{S}_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\cap C_{\epsilon_{3}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\subset{\dot{T}}\mathcal{M}_{0}~~.\end{equation*}Thus $C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ consists of

\begin{equation}||\Xi(\pi(u))||=\frac{[\kappa(u)(1+\kappa(u))]^{1/2}}{c(u)}~~.\end{equation}Thus \eqref{omegaeta} takes the form:
\begin{equation}{\hat{\Xi}}(\pi(u))(n(u))=c(u)\left[-1+{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)\right]~~,\end{equation}where:
\begin{equation*}{\hat{\Xi}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\Xi}{||\Xi||}\end{equation*}is the normalization of the one-form \eqref{omega}.
\par\par\begin{prop}For any $m\in\mathcal{M}$ which is not a critical point of $\Phi$ and every

\begin{equation*}{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{m}(u)=0~~\mathrm{and}~~||u||=N~~.\end{equation*}In particular, for any $\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{3}\in(0,1]$

\begin{equation*}\kappa_{m}(u)<\epsilon_{1}~~,~~{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{m}(u)=0~~,~~c_{m}(u)<\epsilon_{3}~~.\end{equation*}\end{prop}\par\begin{proof}The first statement follows from Proposition \ref{prop:eta} upon

\begin{equation*}N^{2}<\min\left(2\Phi(m)\epsilon_{1},-\Phi(m)+\sqrt{\Phi(m)^{2}+\epsilon_{3}^{2}M_{0}^{2}||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\pi(u))||^{2}}\right)~~,\end{equation*}where we used the fact that the conservative condition $c_{m}(x)<\epsilon_{3}$ is
equivalent with \eqref{ucons2}.
\end{proof}\par\begin{cor}For $\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}\in(0,1]$, we have:
\begin{equation*}\pi(C_{\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\epsilon_{3}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi))=\mathcal{M}_{0}~~.\end{equation*}\end{cor}\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{The first and second dynamical slow roll approximations}
\par\par The first and second slow roll conditions can be used to define {dynamical approximations} of a cosmological curve by a solution curve
of an approximating equation obtained by neglecting the
corresponding parameters in the cosmological equation.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The first dynamical slow roll approximation}
\par\noindent Relation \eqref{cHkappa} allows us to write the cosmological
equation \eqref{eomsingle} as:
\begin{equation}\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)+\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{2\Phi(\varphi(t))}\left[1+\kappa_{\varphi}(t)\right]^{1/2}\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~.\end{equation}The {first dynamical slow roll approximation} consists
of neglecting $\kappa_{\varphi}$ in this equation, i.e.
approximating a cosmological curve $\varphi$ by the solution
$\varphi_{s}$ of the {slow cosmological equation}:
\begin{equation}\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t)+\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{2\Phi(\varphi_{s}(t))}\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi)(\varphi_{s}(t))=0\end{equation}which satisfies the initial conditions:
\begin{equation*}\varphi_{s}(0)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\dot{\varphi}_{s}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(0)~~.\end{equation*}The approximation is accurate around $t=0$ provided that
$\kappa_{\varphi}(0)\ll 1$, which amounts to the condition:
\begin{equation*}\kappa(\varphi(0))=\kappa(\varphi_{s}(0))=\frac{||\dot{\varphi}(0)||^{2}}{2\Phi(\varphi(0))}\ll 1~~.\end{equation*}A necessary condition for the dynamical first slow
roll approximation $\varphi(t)\approx\varphi_{s}(t)$ to remain accurate
at $t\neq 0$ is that we have:
\begin{equation}\kappa(\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t))\ll 1\Longleftrightarrow||\dot{\varphi}(t)||\ll\sqrt{2\Phi(\varphi(t))}~~,\end{equation}where:
\begin{equation*}\kappa(\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t))=\frac{||\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t)||^{2}}{2\Phi(\varphi_{s}(t))}~~.\end{equation*}Equation \eqref{kappas} requires that the lift of $\varphi$ to $T\mathcal{M}$
be contained within a tubular neighborhood of the zero
section whose radius is much smaller that $\sqrt{2\Phi}$.
This condition constraints the time interval around zero for which the
approximation can remain accurate.
\par The geometric equation \eqref{eomslow} defines the {slow flow} on
the tangent bundle $T\mathcal{M}$. Notice that the stationary points of this
flow coincide with those of the cosmological flow, being given by the
trivial lifts of the critical points of $\Phi$. The cosmological
energy:
\begin{equation*}E_{\varphi_{s}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}E(\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t))=\frac{1}{2}||\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t)||^{2}+\Phi(\varphi_{s}(t))\end{equation*}of $\varphi_{s}$ satisfies:
\begin{equation*}\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{\varphi_{s}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi(\varphi_{s}(t))}}{M_{0}}||\dot{\varphi}_{s}(t)||^{2}\end{equation*}and attains its minima at the stationary points of the slow flow. It
follows that $E:T\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is a Lyapunov function for
the geometric dynamical system defined by \eqref{eomslow} on
$T\mathcal{M}$. When $\mathcal{M}$ is compact, the usual argument shows that the slow
flow is future complete and that the $\omega$-limit point of any slow solution
curve is a critical point of $\Phi$.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The second dynamical slow roll approximation}
\par\noindent Relation \eqref{nablaheta} allows us to write the cosmological equation as:
\begin{equation*}\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)(1-{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{\varphi}(t))\dot{\varphi}(t)-||\dot{\varphi}(t)||{\hat{\eta}}^{\perp}_{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~.\end{equation*}The {second dynamical slow roll approximation} consists of
neglecting ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{\varphi}(t)$ in this equation, i.e. neglecting the
parallel component of the covariant acceleration:
\begin{equation*}[\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)]^{\parallel}=\mathcal{G}(\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t),T_{\varphi}(t))=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||\dot{\varphi}(t)||\end{equation*}in the cosmological equation. This amounts to approximating a cosmological curve $\varphi$ by the
solution $\varphi_{\sigma}$ of the {no second roll equation}:
\begin{equation}[\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)]^{\perp}+\mathcal{H}_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi_{\sigma}(t))=0~~\end{equation}which satisfies the initial conditions:
\begin{equation*}\varphi_{\sigma}(0)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\varphi_{\sigma}(t)=\varphi(t)~~.\end{equation*}Recall that (see \eqref{compacc}):
\begin{equation}[\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)]^{\perp}=\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\log||\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)||\right)\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)~~.\end{equation}When $\dot{\varphi}(t)=0$, we define:
\begin{equation*}[\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)]^{\perp}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)~~(t\in I_{\mathrm{sing}})~~,\end{equation*}which agrees with the limit of \eqref{nablatperp} when $t$ approaches a singular point of $\varphi$.
\par The approximation is accurate at $t=0$ provided that:
\begin{equation*}|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}_{\varphi}(0)|=|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(\dot{\varphi}(0))|=|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(0))|=\Big|1+\frac{\cos\theta(\dot{\varphi}(0))}{c(\dot{\varphi}(0))}\Big|\ll 1~~.\end{equation*}A necessary condition that the approximation remains accurate at $t\neq 0$ is that we have:
\begin{equation*}|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t))|=\Big|1+\frac{\cos\theta(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t))}{c(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t))}\Big|\ll 1~~,\end{equation*}a condition which constrains the time interval around the origin on which the approximation can be applied.
\par Projecting \eqref{eom2sr} on the direction of
$T_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)$ and on the hyperplane inside
$T_{\varphi_{\sigma}(t)}\mathcal{M}$ which is orthogonal to
$T_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)$ gives:
\@@eqnarray where noticed that:
\begin{equation*}[\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)]^{\perp}=||\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)||\nabla_{t}T_{\varphi_{c}}(t)=||\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)||^{2}\chi_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)n_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)~~.\end{equation*}The second condition in \eqref{2sreqs} means that the oscullating
plane of $\varphi_{\sigma}$ contains the vector
$(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi_{\sigma}(t))$ and that we have:
\begin{equation}||\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)||^{2}\chi_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)=||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi_{\sigma}(t))||\sin\theta_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)~~.\end{equation}This relation and the first equation in \eqref{2sreqs} determines the
principal curvature of $\varphi_{\sigma}$ in terms of its speed as:
\begin{equation*}\chi_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)=\frac{||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi_{\sigma}(t))||}{||\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)||^{2}}\left[1-\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)||\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t)||}{||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi_{\sigma}(t))||}\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}~~,\end{equation*}i.e.:
\begin{equation}\chi_{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t)=\frac{||\Xi(\varphi_{\sigma}(t))||}{M_{0}\kappa(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t))}\sqrt{1-c(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t))^{2}}~~.\end{equation}Notice that the first equation in \eqref{2sreqs} amounts to:
\begin{equation*}c(\dot{\varphi}_{\sigma}(t))=-\cos\theta(\dot{\varphi_{\sigma}}(t))~~,\end{equation*}which shows that the flow defined by \eqref{eom2sr} on $T\mathcal{M}$ preserves the no roll shell $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{The conservative and dissipative approximations}
\par In this section we discuss two dynamical approximations controlled by
the conservative parameter $c_{\varphi}$, namely the conservative and
dissipative approximations, which are obtained by taking this
parameter to be very small or very large. We start by explaining the
relation of these approximations to approximations controlled by the
norm of $\bm{\eta}_{\varphi}$.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{Dynamical approximations controlled by the norm of the relative acceleration}
\par\noindent It is natural to consider the approximants of the
cosmological equation which are obtained by requiring that
$||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}_{\varphi}(t)||$ is very small, very large or close to one.
\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{paragraph}{toc}{The gradient flow approximation.}
\par The {gradient flow
approximation} of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{genalpha}{}{}]} consists of replacing the cosmological
equation \eqref{eomsingle} with the {modified gradient flow
equation}:
\begin{equation*}\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~,\end{equation*}whose integral curves are obtained from the gradient flow curves of
$\Phi$ by a certain reparameterization. This approximation (which was
further discussed in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{modular}{}{}]}) is accurate when the {small
relative acceleration condition} $||\bm{\eta}_{\varphi}(t)||\ll 1$ holds.
Notice that this condition implies the small longitudinal relative
acceleration condition $|{\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(u)|\ll 1$ and hence the gradient flow
approximation implies the second slow roll approximation.
\par Combining the gradient flow approximation with the slow motion
approximation (i.e. further neglecting $\kappa_{\varphi}(t)$ in the
rescaled Hubble parameter
$\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)=\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi(t)}}{M_{0}}(1+\kappa_{\varphi}(t))^{1/2}$)
results in the {IR approximation} of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]}. The latter
replaces \eqref{eomsingle} with the equation:
\begin{equation*}\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{2\Phi(\varphi(t))}\dot{\varphi}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(t))=0~~,\end{equation*}which is equivalent with the gradient flow equation of the {classical effective potential} $V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}M_{0}\sqrt{2\Phi}$.
The IR approximation is accurate when $\kappa_{\varphi}(t)\ll 1$ and
$||\bm{\eta}_{\varphi}(t)||\ll 1$ and is a natural generalization to
multifield models of the second order slow roll approximation of
one-field cosmological models.
\par Notice that relation \eqref{veta} gives:
\begin{equation}||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}(u)||=\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{c(u)^{2}}+\frac{2\cos\theta(u)}{c(u)}}\in\left[\big|1-\frac{1}{c(u)}\big|,1+\frac{1}{c(u)}\right]~~.\end{equation}Thus:
\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@The conservative condition $c(u)\ll 1$ is {equivalent} with the {large relative acceleration condition}
$||\bm{\eta}(u)||\gg 1$, namely it forces $||\bm{\eta}(u)||\approx\frac{1}{c(u)}$.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@The dissipative condition $c(u)\gg 1$ is {equivalent} with the {unit relative acceleration condition}
$||\bm{\eta}(u)||\approx 1$.
\end{itemize}On the other hand, the {small relative acceleration condition}
$||\bm{\eta}(u)||\ll 1$ (which is used to define the gradient flow
approximation of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{genalpha}{}{}]}) does not constrain $c(u)$. Thus one
has the following approximations which are controlled by $||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}||$:
\par\begin{itemize}\itemize@item@The gradient flow approximation, which is accurate when
$||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}_{\varphi}(t)||\ll 1$. This approximation was discussed in
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{genalpha}{}{}]} and \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{modular}{}{}]}.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@The conservative approximation, which is accurate when
$||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}_{\varphi}(t)||\gg 1$, i.e. when $c_{\varphi}(t)\ll 1$.
\par\vskip 4.0pt\vskip 4.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 1.0pt\itemize@item@The dissipative approximation, which is accurate when
$||{\hat{\bm{\eta}}}_{\varphi}(t)||\approx 1$, i.e. when $c_{\varphi}(t)\gg 1$.
\end{itemize}\par\noindent Each of these can be combined with the slow motion
approximation, which is accurate when $\kappa_{\varphi}(t)\ll 1$, i.e.
when $\bm{\varepsilon}_{\varphi}(t)\ll 1$. We refer the reader to
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{genalpha}{}{}]} and \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{modular}{}{}]} for further details of the gradient
flow approximation and to \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]} and \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{grad}{}{}]} for further
information on the IR approximation. Below, we discuss
the conservative and dissipative approximations.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The conservative approximation}
\par\noindent The {conservative approximation} consists of considering only
non-critical cosmological curves and neglecting the friction term in
the cosmological equation. This approximation is accurate for a
noncritical cosmological curve $\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0}$ when the
{kinematic conservative condition}:
\begin{equation}c_{\varphi}(t)\ll 1\Longleftrightarrow\dot{\varphi}(t)\in C_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)~~\mathrm{for~some~positive}~\epsilon\ll 1\end{equation}is satisfied. Suppose that $0\in I$. When \eqref{ccond} holds at
$t=0$, the noncritical cosmological curve $\varphi$ is
well-approximated for small $|t|$ by the solution
$\varphi_{c}:I_{c}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}$ of the {conservative equation} of the
scalar triple $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$:
\begin{equation}\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)+(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi_{c}(t))=0\end{equation}which satisfies the initial conditions:
\begin{equation}\varphi_{c}(0)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\dot{\varphi}_{c}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(0)~~.\end{equation}In this case the approximation remains accurate for those cosmological times
$t$ close to zero which satisfy $\dot{\varphi}(t)\in C_{\epsilon}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$. This condition determines a relatively
open subset of $I$ whose connected component which contains $0$ is the
time interval on which the approximation remains accurate.
\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{paragraph}{toc}{Conservation of energy for the conservative approximant.}
\par Notice that \eqref{cons} is the equation of the motion of a particle of
unit mass in the Riemannian manifold $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})$ in the presence of
the potential $\Phi$. This motion is conservative in the sense that
the energy:
\begin{equation}E_{\varphi_{c}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}||\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)||^{2}+\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))\end{equation}is independent of $t$ when $\varphi_{c}$ is a solution of \eqref{cons}. The
initial conditions \eqref{inconscond} determine the energy as:
\begin{equation}E_{\varphi_{c}}=\frac{1}{2}||\dot{\varphi}(0)||^{2}+\Phi(\varphi(0))=E_{\varphi}(0):=E_{0}~~,\end{equation}where:
\begin{equation*}E_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}+\Phi(\varphi(t))\end{equation*}is the cosmological energy of $\varphi$, which is a
strictly-decreasing function of $t$ when $\varphi$ is not constant. We
have (see eq. \eqref{cME} for the definition of the set $\mathcal{M}(E_{0})$):
\begin{equation*}\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))\leq E_{0}~~\mathrm{i.e.}~~\varphi_{c}(t)\subset\mathcal{M}(E_{0})\end{equation*}since $||\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)||^{2}\geq 0$. Equations \eqref{Evarphi0} and \eqref{Econs} give:
\begin{equation}||\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)||=\sqrt{2[E_{0}-\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))]}~~\end{equation}and the rescaled Hubble parameter of $\varphi_{c}$:
\begin{equation*}\mathcal{H}_{\varphi_{c}}(t)=\mathcal{H}(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{2E_{0}}\end{equation*}is independent of $t$. In particular, the e-fold function of
$\varphi_{c}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}\mathcal{N}_{\varphi_{c}}(T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{T}\mathrm{d}t\mathcal{H}_{\varphi_{c}}(t)=\frac{T}{3M_{0}}\sqrt{2E_{0}}\end{equation}and hence is a linear function of $T$. Moreover, we have:
\begin{equation}\kappa_{\varphi_{c}}(t)=\frac{||\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)||^{2}}{2\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}=\frac{E_{0}-\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}{\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}=\frac{E_{0}}{\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}-1~~\end{equation}and the slow roll parameter of $\varphi_{c}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}\bm{\varepsilon}_{\varphi_{c}}(t)=\frac{3\kappa_{\varphi_{c}}(t)}{1+\kappa_{\varphi_{c}}(t)}=3\left[1-\frac{\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}{E_{0}}\right]~~.\end{equation}Thus $\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)$ lies in the inflation region of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$ iff:
\begin{equation*}\kappa_{\varphi_{c}}(t)<\frac{1}{2}\Longleftrightarrow\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))>\frac{2E_{0}}{3}~~,\end{equation*}i.e. iff $\varphi_{c}(t)$ lies in the following sublevel set of $\Phi$:
\begin{equation}\mathcal{M}(2E_{0}/3)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{m\in\mathcal{M}~|~\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))<\frac{2E_{0}}{3}\}\subset\mathcal{M}\end{equation}which we call the {conservative inflation region} of $\mathcal{M}$ at energy $E_{0}$.
\par\par\@@unnumbered@section{paragraph}{toc}{The potential conservative condition.}
\par The conservative approximation $\varphi(t)\approx\varphi_{c}(t)$ implies that the
conservative parameter of $\varphi$ is approximated as
$c_{\varphi}(t)\approx c_{\varphi_{c}}(t)$, where:
\begin{equation}c_{\varphi_{c}}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}c(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))=\frac{\mathcal{H}(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))||\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t)||}{||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi_{c}(t))||}=\frac{2}{M_{0}}\frac{\left[E_{0}(E_{0}-\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t)))\right]^{1/2}}{||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi_{c}(t))||}=c_{E_{0}}(\varphi_{c}(t))~~.\end{equation}Here $c_{E_{0}}:\mathcal{M}_{0}(E_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ is the {$E_{0}$-reduced
conservative function} of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$, which is defined through:
\begin{equation*}c_{E_{0}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{M_{0}}\frac{\left[E_{0}(E_{0}-\Phi)\right]^{1/2}}{||\mathrm{d}\Phi||}=\frac{\left[E_{0}(E_{0}-\Phi)\right]^{1/2}}{||\Xi||\Phi}~~\forall E_{0}>0~~.\end{equation*}The initial conditions \eqref{inconscond} give:
\begin{equation*}c_{\varphi}(0)=c({\dot{\varphi}}(0))=c(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(0))=c_{E_{0}}(\varphi(0))~~.\end{equation*}Consistency with \eqref{ccond} requires that the {potential
conservative condition}:
\begin{equation}c_{\varphi_{c}}(t)\ll 1\Longleftrightarrow c_{E_{0}}(\varphi_{c}(t))\ll 1\end{equation}is satisfied, i.e. that there exists a positive $\epsilon^{\prime}\ll 1$ such that
\begin{equation}\varphi_{c}(t)\in C_{E_{0},\epsilon^{\prime}}(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{m\in\mathcal{M}_{0}~|~c_{E_{0}}(m)<\epsilon^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{0}~~.\end{equation}This condition constrains the cosmological times $t\neq 0$ for which
the conservative approximation can remain accurate.
\par\begin{remark}We have:
\begin{equation*}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}{\mathrm{d}t}=(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))~~\end{equation*}and:
\begin{equation*}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\Phi(\varphi_{c}(t))}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=\mathrm{Hess}(\Phi)(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t),\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))+(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))=\mathrm{Hess}(\Phi)(\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t),\dot{\varphi}_{c}(t))-||\mathrm{d}\Phi||^{2}~~.\end{equation*}\end{remark}\par\par\@@numbered@section{subsection}{toc}{The dissipative approximation}
\par\noindent The {dissipative approximation} consists of considering only
non-critical cosmological curves and neglecting the friction term in
the cosmological equation. This approximation is accurate for a
noncritical cosmological curve $\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{0}$ when the
{kinematic dissipative condition}:
\begin{equation}c_{\varphi}(t)\gg 1\end{equation}is satisfied. Suppose that $0\in I$. When \eqref{ccond} holds at
$t=0$, the noncritical cosmological curve $\varphi$ is
well-approximated for small $|t|$ by the solution
$\varphi_{d}:I_{c}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}$ of the {dissipative equation} of the
scalar triple $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},\Phi)$:
\begin{equation}\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{d}(t)+\mathcal{H}(\varphi_{d}(t))\dot{\varphi}_{d}(t)=0\end{equation}which satisfies the initial conditions:
\begin{equation}\varphi_{d}(0)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\dot{\varphi}_{d}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(0)~~.\end{equation}In this case the approximation remains accurate for those cosmological
times $t$ close to zero which satisfy $c(\dot{\varphi}(t))\gg 1$. This
condition determines a relatively open subset of $I$ whose connected
component which contains $0$ is the time interval on which the
approximation remains accurate.
\par The dissipative equation \eqref{dissip} is a modified
(a.k.a. reparameterized) geodesic equation. Indeed, let $s=s(t)$ be
an increasing parameter along the curve $\varphi$,
chosen such that $s(0)=0$. We have:
\begin{equation*}\dot{\varphi}_{d}=\dot{s}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}~~\mathrm{and}~~\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}_{d}=\ddot{s}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}+\dot{s}^{2}\nabla_{s}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}~~,\end{equation*}where the primes denote derivatives with respect to $s$. Hence \eqref{dissip} is
equivalent with:
\begin{equation*}\nabla_{s}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(s)+\frac{\ddot{s}+\dot{s}\mathcal{H}(\dot{s}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(s))}{\dot{s}^{2}}=0~~\end{equation*}and reduces to the geodesic equation $\nabla_{s}\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(s)=0$
with affine parameter $s$ provided that $s$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}\frac{\ddot{s}}{\dot{s}^{2}}+\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{||\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(s)||^{2}+2\frac{\Phi(\varphi_{d}(s))}{\dot{s}^{2}}}=0~~.\end{equation}Since:
\begin{equation*}\frac{\ddot{s}}{\dot{s}^{2}}=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{1}{\dot{s}}\right)=-\dot{s}t^{\prime\prime}(s)=-\frac{t^{\prime\prime}(s)}{t^{\prime}(s)}~~,\end{equation*}condition \eqref{scond} is equivalent with the following ODE for the inverse function $t=t(s)$:
\begin{equation}t^{\prime\prime}(s)-\frac{1}{M_{0}}[||\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(s)||^{2}+2\Phi(\varphi_{d}(s))t^{\prime}(s)^{2}]^{1/2}t^{\prime}(s)=0~~.\end{equation}It follows that $\varphi_{d}$ is a reparameterized geodesic of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})$, namely $\varphi(s)=\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(t(s))$ is
a normalized geodesic. The initial conditions \eqref{indissipcond} amount to:
\begin{equation*}\varphi(0)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(0)=t^{\prime}(0)\dot{\varphi}(0)~~.\end{equation*}Since $||\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(0)||=1$, the second of these conditions implies:
\begin{equation*}t^{\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{||\dot{\varphi}(0)||}~~,\end{equation*}which together with $t(0)=0$ specifies the initial condition for the
desired solution $t(s)$ of \eqref{tode}. Moreover, the initial
conditions for $\varphi_{d}$ can be written as:
\begin{equation}\varphi_{d}(0)=\varphi(0)~~\mathrm{and}~~\varphi^{\prime}_{d}(0)=T_{\varphi}(0)=\frac{\dot{\varphi}(0)}{||\dot{\varphi}(0)||}~~.\end{equation}When $\varphi$ is a maximal cosmological curve, the curve
$\varphi_{d}(s)$ can be taken to the unique maximal normalized geodesic
of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})$ which satisfies these conditions.
\par For the approximation of $\varphi(t)$ by $\varphi_{d}(t)$ to remain
accurate for $t\neq 0$, it is necessary that the {effective
dissipative condition}:
\begin{equation*}c_{\varphi_{d}}(t)\ll 1\end{equation*}be satisfied. Since $\varphi_{d}(s)$ is a normalized geodesic, we have:
\begin{equation*}||\dot{\varphi}_{d}(t)||=\dot{s}=\frac{1}{t^{\prime}(s)}~~.\end{equation*}Hence:
\begin{equation*}\kappa_{\varphi_{d}}(t)=\frac{1}{2t^{\prime}(s)^{2}\Phi(\varphi_{d}(s))}\end{equation*}and:
\begin{equation*}c_{\varphi_{d}}(s)=\frac{\sqrt{1+2t^{\prime}(s)^{2}\Phi(\varphi_{d}(s))}}{2t^{\prime}(s)^{2}\Phi(\varphi_{d}(s))||\Xi(\varphi_{d}(s))||}=\frac{\sqrt{1+2t^{\prime}(s)^{2}\Phi(\varphi_{d}(s))}}{M_{0}t^{\prime}(s)^{2}||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi_{d}(s))||}~~.\end{equation*}Notice that
$c_{\varphi_{d}}(0)=c(\dot{\varphi}_{d}(0))=c(\dot{\varphi}(0))=c_{\varphi}(0)$
since $\dot{\varphi}_{d}(0)=\dot{\varphi}(0)$.
\par As explained in Subsection \ref{subsec:etageom}, the dissipative
condition $c(\dot{\varphi}(t))\gg 1$ forces ${\hat{\eta}}^{\parallel}(\dot{\varphi}(t))$
to be very close to one. Hence the generalized ``ultra slow roll'' approximation holds
in the strongly dissipative regime, where the dissipative approximation
is accurate.
\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{The limits of large and small rescaled Planck mass}
\par\noindent Recall from \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]} that universal similarities can be used to absorb all
parameters of the model into the rescaled Planck mass $M_{0}$. Thus it
is natural to consider the limits when $M_{0}$ is very large and very
small.
\par When $M_{0}\gg 1$, the friction term can be neglected and hence
cosmological curves are well-approximated by solutions of the
conservative equation \eqref{cons}.
\par When $M_{0}\ll 1$, the scale transformation with parameter
$\epsilon=M_{0}$ brings \eqref{eomsingle} to the form:
\begin{equation}M_{0}^{2}\nabla_{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{M_{0}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+\left[M_{0}^{2}||\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{M_{0}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}||^{2}+2\Phi(\varphi_{M_{0}}(t))\right]^{1/2}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{M_{0}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\mathrm{grad}_{\mathcal{G}}\Phi)(\varphi_{M_{0}}(t))=0~~,\end{equation}where $\varphi_{M_{0}}(t)=\varphi(t/M_{0})$. Hence the limit of very small
$M_{0}$ coincides with the infrared limit of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]} at parameter
$\epsilon=M_{0}$. In this limit, the rescaled equation \eqref{smallM0}
approximates as:
\begin{equation*}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{M_{0}}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\mathrm{grad}V_{1})(\varphi_{M_{0}}(t))\approx 0~~,\end{equation*}where:
\begin{equation*}V_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\sqrt{2\Phi}\end{equation*}coincides with the classical effective potential of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]} for $M_{0}=1$.
Thus $\varphi(t)$ is well-approximated by the curve $\varphi_{0}(t)=\varphi_{1}(M_{0}t)$, where $\varphi_{1}(t)$
is the solution of the gradient flow equation of $V_{1}$:
\begin{equation}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{1}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\mathrm{grad}V_{1})(\varphi_{1}(t))=0~~\end{equation}which satisfies the initial condition:
\begin{equation*}\varphi_{1}(0)=\varphi(0)~~.\end{equation*}Notice that \eqref{gf1} is equivalent with the condition that
$\varphi_{0}$ satisfies the gradient flow equation:
\begin{equation}\frac{\mathrm{d}\varphi_{0}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+(\mathrm{grad}V)(\varphi_{0}(t))=0~~\end{equation}of the classical effective scalar potential:
\begin{equation*}V=M_{0}V_{1}=M_{0}\sqrt{2\Phi}\end{equation*}introduced in \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]}. It also satisfies the initial condition:
\begin{equation*}\varphi_{0}(0)=\varphi(0)~~.\end{equation*}The approximation is most accurate for {infrared optimal curves},
which satisfy:
\begin{equation*}\dot{\varphi}(0)=-M_{0}(\mathrm{grad}V_{1})(\varphi(0))\Longleftrightarrow\dot{\varphi}(0)=-\frac{M_{0}}{\sqrt{2\Phi}}(\mathrm{grad}\Phi)(\varphi(0))~~.\end{equation*}This amounts to the condition that $\dot{\varphi}(0)$ belongs to the
gradient flow shell of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G},V)$:
\begin{equation*}\dot{\varphi}(0)=-(\mathrm{grad}V)(\varphi(0))~~.\end{equation*}The approximation remains accurate for $t\neq 0$ when one can neglect the acceleration
and kinetic energy terms in \eqref{eomsingle}, which requires:
\begin{equation*}\kappa_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{||\dot{\varphi}(t)||^{2}}{2\Phi(\varphi(t))}\ll 1\end{equation*}and:
\begin{equation*}{\tilde{\kappa}}_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{||\nabla_{t}\dot{\varphi}(t)||}{||(\mathrm{d}\Phi)(\varphi(t))||}\ll 1~~.\end{equation*}Notice that ${\tilde{\kappa}}_{\varphi}(t)$ coincides with the second IR parameter of \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{ren}{}{}]}.
Thus:
\par\par\noindent{In leading order, the large rescaled Planck mass limit
$M_{0}\gg 1$ reproduces the conservative approximation, while the small
rescaled Planck mass limit $M_{0}\ll 1$ reproduces the IR
approximation.}
\par\par\@@numbered@section{section}{toc}{Conclusions and further directions}
\par We gave a careful geometric construction of natural basic observables
for multifield cosmological models with arbitrary scalar manifold
topology. Some of these first order observables are obtained by
on-shell reduction of second order observables and their definition on
the tangent bundle of the scalar manifold is not obvious a priori. We
also discussed relations between these observables and the regions of
interest which they determine within the tangent bundle of the scalar
manifold. Finally, we described a system of dynamical approximations
of the cosmological equation which are defined by imposing conditions
on these basic observables -- some of which were not considered
systematically before. This defines a hierarchy of qualitatively
distinct dynamical regimes which deserve detailed study.
\par Our discussion of these approximations only addressed their most basic
features and much remains to be done. In particular, each dynamical
approximation considered in the paper could be studied by expansion
techniques with a view towards extracting asymptotic approximation
schemes for cosmological curves in each
dynamical regime we have identified. This appears to be quite involved
since it requires methods from the asymptotic theory of nonlinear
geometric ODEs and the control of various bounds involving the scalar potential and
its derivatives as well as the distance function of $(\mathcal{M},\mathcal{G})$. In
principle, the dynamical approximations which we identified could
also be used to devise numerical approximation methods which could
shed light on the corresponding dynamical regimes. Finally, it would
be interesting to study these regimes in detail for the class of tame
two field models, similar to the study of the IR regime performed in
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{grad}{}{}]}. We hope to report on these and related questions in the
future.
