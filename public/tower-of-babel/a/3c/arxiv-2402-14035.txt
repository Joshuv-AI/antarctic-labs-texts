Wisdom of Committee: Distilling from Foundation Model to Specialized Application Model
Zichang Liu, Qingyun Liu, Yuening Li, Liang Liu, Anshumali Shrivastava, Shuchao Bi, Lichan Hong, Ed H. Chi, Zhe Zhao

Abstract
Recent advancements in foundation models have yielded impressive performance across a wide range of tasks. Meanwhile, for specific applications, practitioners have been developing specialized application models. To enjoy the benefits of both kinds of models, one natural path is to transfer the knowledge in foundation models into specialized application models, which are generally more efficient for serving. Techniques from knowledge distillation may be applied here, where the application model learns to mimic the foundation model. However, specialized application models and foundation models have substantial gaps in capacity, employing distinct architectures, using different input features from different modalities, and being optimized on different distributions. These differences in model characteristics lead to significant challenges for distillation methods. In this work, we propose creating a teaching committee comprising both foundation model teachers and complementary teachers. Complementary teachers possess model characteristics akin to the student’s, aiming to bridge the gap between the foundation model and specialized application models for a smoother knowledge transfer. Further, to accommodate the dissimilarity among the teachers in the committee, we introduce DiverseDistill , which allows the student to understand the expertise of each teacher and extract task knowledge. Our evaluations demonstrate that adding complementary teachers enhances student performance. Finally, DiverseDistill consistently outperforms baseline distillation methods, regardless of the teacher choices, resulting in significantly improved student performance.

Introduction

The emergence of foundation models, characterized by their immense scale and capacity, has brought remarkable performances
[
36
,
12
,
5
,
8
,
38
]
. These models, trained on massive amounts of data in an unsupervised fashion, can be adapted to various downstream tasks, either by fine-tuning on application data
[
23
,
8
]
or leveraging its few-shot or in-context learning ability
[
5
,
32
,
3
]
.
Concurrently, efforts in machine learning over the past few years have resulted in specialized application models optimized for specific tasks.

For example, large language models, or LLMs, trained for general language understanding, exhibit impressive performance on recommendation tasks even in the few-shot fashion
[
25
]
.
In contrast, recommendation systems based on embedding tables with fully
connected layers stand as the default production recommendation system
[
33
]
. LLMs demonstrated strong generalization ability on unseen task data, while recommendation systems based on embedding tables are constructed with domain-specific knowledge and perform well on its optimization distributions.
Each category of model has its distinct advantages, and to harness the strengths of both, one natural progression involves transferring knowledge from foundation models into specialized application models, a concept aligned with the goal of knowledge distillation. This process entails the specialized application models learning the task knowledge from the foundation models.

However, transferring knowledge from foundation models to specialized application models presents significant challenges for standard knowledge distillation methods due to the great differences in model characteristics. First, a foundation model often involves an enormous amount of parameters, creating a substantial capacity gap between the teacher and the student. Second, differences in architectures can also be observed. For example, large language models are
based on transformer architecture, unlike standard recommendation systems that primarily rely on embedding tables with fully connected layers
[
25
,
15
,
33
]
. Third, foundation models are typically pre-trained on an extensive corpus of data in a self-supervised manner. In contrast, specialized application models are trained for specific application distributions, leading to a significant distribution gap. Lastly, potential differences in input features and modalities further increase the challenge as foundation models and the student could consume different features.
For instance, LLMs can make predictions based on text features such as item descriptions and comments, while standard recommender systems rely on categorical and numerical features such as user history
[
25
]
.

In this paper, we explore adding complementary teachers who are more similar to the specialized application students in the model characteristics discussed above. A teacher committee with both foundation models and complementary models could bridge the vast differences between foundation models and specialized application models, facilitating a more effective knowledge transfer. Prior research in knowledge distillation also provides supportive evidence; for example, teachers with a smaller gap in model capacity lead to more effective student learning
[
34
,
30
,
31
]
. In addition, relying on a single teacher may limit the student’s full potential. This is analogous to forming a Ph.D. committee, which comprises multiple members with diverse expertise, allowing diversity in the teachers to provide unique knowledge and perspective. Foundation models pose a more general understanding, whereas application teachers specialize in downstream tasks.

However, harnessing the benefits of diverse teachers has its challenges.Since the complementary teachers are supposed to be more similar to the student, the teachers (the foundation model teachers and application model teachers) themselves could exhibit considerable differences in capacity, architecture, training distribution, input features, and modalities. For instance, for a rating prediction task where the student employs a standard recommendation architecture, a teaching committee comprising a large language model and a larger recommendation system would teach the student. The recommendation system teacher is more similar to the student regarding architecture, capacity, and data distribution. At the same time, it is evident that these two teachers also possess significant differences. This breaks the common assumption of multi-teacher distillation methods where teachers share similar architecture and training distribution. Under this assumption, standard multi-teacher distillation methods enjoy the performance benefits mainly from a variance reduction perspective, while lack the understanding on teachers’ expertise
[
47
,
9
,
14
]
. Thus, we propose an interactive distillation process, allowing the student to model each teacher’s expertise and ask tailored questions to each teacher. With no constraints on the teachers,
DiverseDistill
is adaptable to various teacher combinations.

Establishing a teacher committee brings additional distillation costs. Fortunately, the distillation cost from adding a complementary teacher is significantly lower, considering foundation models are highly resource-intensive. Further, with
DiverseDistill
, each teacher’s importance for the current data is dynamically weighted based on the student’s modeling of the teacher’s expertise. We imagine such weighting mechanics may enable the student to select only one teacher for every training data when distillation cost becomes the major constraint.

In this work, we argue that forming a teacher committee realizes effective knowledge transfer from foundation models to specialized application models. To accommodate a teacher committee without constraints on teacher choices, we design the distillation process as a question-and-answer process with additional parameters for the student to model each teacher’s expertise on the task. Our contributions are summarized as follows.

•

We hypothesize that forming a teacher committee of both foundation models and complementary models would lead to a smoother knowledge transfer from foundation model to specialized application model.

•

, and how we design and train the distillation module in Section
3.2

2.2
Knowledge Distillation

Knowledge distillation is one of the promising directions to reduce the serving cost of deep neural networks
[
21
]
. Knowledge distillation is often more hardware-friendly than orthogonal directions such as pruning
[
20
]
and quantization
[

One of the main differences arises from the capacity in terms of network depth, width, and number of parameters. Recent works in understanding knowledge distillation discover that a large gap between the teacher and the student capacity may lead to degradation in student performance, and one can often employ a teacher of the smaller network to obtain better transferable representation
[
34
,
30
,
31
]
. With the bloom of pre-trained models, another major distillation challenge comes from the difference in the tasks. Specifically, when using the pre-trained model as the teacher for downstream tasks, the difference between the pre-training and downstream tasks may lead to a less performant student
[
49
,
46
]
.

26
,
44
,
42

43

43

The above foundation model distillation works illustrated the usefulness of the foundation model in obtaining better students; however, they are explored mainly in a setting with high similarity between the teacher and the student. This paper aims to improve the performance of a specialized application model instead of creating a smaller foundation model. We explore a more general setting in which foundation model teachers and the student model share much fewer characteristics, which opens up more possibilities for using the foundation models.

Multi-Teacher Distillation

Multi-teacher distillation extends from standard distillation by leveraging multiple teachers to improve student performance under the philosophy of “two heads are better than one". Previous works focus on various ways to combine teachers’ soft targets, logits, or intermediate representations

These methods often assume similar architectures or training distributions among teachers, varying mainly on capacities. In this work, we explore a more challenging setting where teachers can be different regarding capacities, architectures, training distributions, and input features. Such a setting brings more freedom in teacher choices and allows more diversities in teacher expertise.

3
Distilling from Diverse Teachers

We now introduce
DiverseDistill
, a general distillation framework that learns a distillation module along the student model
\mathcal{S}
to support distillation from a diverse set of
n
teacher networks
\{\mathcal{T}_{1},...,\mathcal{T}_{n}\}
. In Section
3.1
, we describe how the student learns from the teacher committee with the aid of the distillation module. In Section
3.2
and Section
3.3
, we introduce the architecture design and training objective for the distillation module, respectively.

3.1
Learning with the Distillation Module

DiverseDistill
uses a two-way communicative process, in contrast to the standard one-way communication in distillation, where the teacher directly provides features or logits as the soft label to the student. With
DiverseDistill
, the student first asks tailored questions to each teacher, and each teacher then replies to the question with an answer, which will be used as the soft label.

Intuition
Continue with the analogy of a Ph.D. committee, as each teacher has different expertise; formulating the question of interest in the language of the expertise area often leads to more accessible communication and more effective advice. Thus, to gain helpful instructions given teachers’ different expertise,
DiverseDistill
enables each teacher to answer the student’s tailored questions, which are augmented based on the student’s understanding of each teacher.

Distillation Process
The distillation module has a Question Augmenter
\mathcal{F_{Q}}
and an Answer Augmenter
\mathcal{F_{A}}
, as depicted in Figure
1
. The process can be divided into three stages.

1.

Asking questions. Student model’s hidden states
x
will be used as the input to the Question Augmenter
\mathcal{F_{Q}}
. The hidden states are expected to appropriately reflect the student model’s current understanding of the data. For example, one such candidate is the [CLS] token in the transformer-based model, which serves as the representation of the entire image/sentence. Then, the Question Augmenter
\mathcal{F_{Q}}
outputs a set of questions
\mathbf{q}=\{q_{1},...,q_{n}\}
, where one question for each teacher.

2.

Collect answers. Each question will be fed into its corresponding teacher model from the early layer, such that each teacher could run a forward pass on its question respectively. Then, the Answer Augmenter
\mathcal{F_{A}}
takes every teacher’s last layer hidden states and outputs a set of answers
\mathbf{a}=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
.

3.

Learning from the answers. The student is trained in the standard distillation fashion, taking the task loss on the original data and the distillation loss on the answers
\mathbf{a}
. To optimize over the distillation dataset
\mathcal{D}

\min\mathbb{E}_{(d,y_{true})\sim\mathcal{D}}(L_{T}(\mathcal{S}(d),y_{true})+\alpha{}L_{D}(\mathbf{a},h))

where
L_{T}
is the task loss,
L_{D}
is the distillation loss between the answers
\mathbf{a}
and student’s last layer hidden states
h
, and
\alpha{}
is the hyper-parameter for loss weight. Inspired by feature distillation,
DiverseDistill
applies the distillation on the hidden states instead of the logits to handle situations when there are differences in the output space between the teacher and the student. For example, a language model may predict logits over its vocabulary for a recommendation task, while a standard recommendation system predicts logits over the candidate products.

3.2
The Design of the Distillation Module

Question Augmenter

DiverseDistill
introduce Teacher Embedding Dot Product in the Question Augmenter to generate tailored questions based on each teacher’s expertise. Teacher Embedding Dot Product allows the student to model each teacher’s expertise and generate questions conditioned on the learned teacher embedding.

As shown in Figure
2(a)
, the Question Augmenter consists of a Teacher Embedding Dot Product and multiple MLPs. The goal of each MLP is to match the model dimension for each teacher, which can be dramatically different with a diverse set of teachers.
The Teacher Embedding Dot Product takes the student model’s representation of data
x
as input and yields a set of questions with hidden states. The following MLPs outputs questions
\mathbf{q}=\{q_{1},...,q_{n}\}
based on the questions hidden states.

The Teacher Embedding Dot Product has three trainable parameters, the teacher Embedding
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\textit{emb}}}
, the question projection
W_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textit{student}}\times d_{m}\times d_{\textit{emb}}}
, and the teacher importance projection
W_{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\textit{student}}\times d_{\textit{emb}}}
, where
d_{\textit{student}}
refers to the student model dimension,
d_{\textit{emb}}
and
d_{m}
refer to two hidden dimensions of the Question Augmenter. The Teacher Embedding Dot Product computes the dot product of
E
with the projected student hidden states
M
, where
M=xW_{m}
, to model the alignment between the teacher expertise and the student data representation, and computes another dot product to generate question representations that are aligned with each teacher’s expertise.

Teacher Embedding Dot Product also provides a way to dynamically weight each teacher based on the teacher embedding and student data representation. Specifically, we compute the teacher importance
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
w=\sigma(FE^{T})
, where
\sigma
is the Sigmoid function and
F=qW_{f}
.

Answer Augmenter

The Answer Augmenter takes a set of hidden states
\mathbf{p}=\{p_{1},...,p_{n}\}
, one from each teacher as the input, as depicted in Figure
2(b)
. Each hidden state will be passed through a separate MLP to match back with the student model dimension. The Answer Augmenter yields a set of answers
\mathbf{a}=\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
, which will be used as the soft label in distillation.

Distill from the Answers

DiverseDistill
computes the distillation loss
L_{D}
between the student model’s last hidden states
h
with the set of answers
\mathbf{a}
, dynamically weighted by the teacher importance
w
. Formally,

L_{D}(\mathbf{a},h)=\sum_{1}^{n}w_{i}L(a_{i},h)

where
L
denotes a divergence metric such as
L_{2}
or the Kullback-Leibler divergence. We use
L_{2}
in our evaluation.

3.3
Training the Distillation Module

DiverseDistill
learns the Question Augmenter and the Answer Augmenter with the student. We introduce a task regularizer to encourage the distillation module to generate task-oriented questions and answers.

Challenges
The challenges arise as we make no assumptions on the teacher committee.
With a diverse set of teachers, the hidden states on the same input can be dramatically different. When asking questions, as the teacher model needs to run inference pass on the questions, questions must be aligned with each teacher and appropriately query the teacher’s knowledge on the task. When learning from the answers, the information embedded in teachers’ hidden states can be excessive and biased for the student model, especially when considering teachers with capacity, architecture, and distribution gaps.

Figure 3:
Illustration for the task regularizer.
DiverseDistill
takes losses on the teacher’s predictions based on the questions and the student’s predictions based on the answers.

Task Regularizer

We introduce task loss on the predictions based on the questions and answers to align different models’ hidden states and regulate the distillation process to be task-oriented. The intuition is that the question has to be aligned with the teacher model to the extent that the teacher can make the correct prediction. Similarly, the answer should be digestible bites such that the student can make the correct prediction based on the answer.

We visualize the process in Figure
3
with one teacher as an example. The hidden states after the student model’s early layers are fed into the Question Augmenter. The teacher model takes the question as the input for the intermediate layers. The hidden states after the teacher’s intermediate layers are fed into the Answer Augmenter; then, the student takes the answer as the input for the last layer. This process yields two predictions:
\hat{y}_{Q}
is the teacher prediction using tailored questions, and
\hat{y}_{A}
is the student prediction with injected answers. The parameters in the Question Augmenter and the Answer Augmenter are optimized for

\min\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}}(L_{T}(\hat{y}_{Q},y_{true})+L_{T}(\hat{y}_{A},y_{true}))

where
L_{T}
is the task loss function. Note that the gradient from the task regularizer is only applied for the distillation module, as we freeze the student and teacher models and mark them in grey in Figure
3
.

Baselines

We consider state-of-the-art distillation methods from foundation model distillation and multi-teacher distillation to answer the above two evaluation questions.

From foundation model distillation, we organize the baselines in terms of methodologies as some works share the same methodology but were proposed for different foundation models or different applications with slight adjustments to accommodate the architectures.

•

Logit Distillation (LD): The logits of the teacher model are used as the soft target. This is widely used in different foundation model distillation works
[
40
,
45
]
.

•

Feature Distillation (FD): The hidden states of the teacher model are used as the soft target. Projection layers are added to bridge the dimensional differences between the teacher and the student. This is widely used in different foundation model distillation, especially from multi-modality foundation models
[
41
,
43
]
.

•

Student Customized Knowledge Distillation (SCKD)
[
50
]
: SCKD examines the alignment between gradient from distillation loss and that from task loss.

From multi-teacher distillation, we consider the following:

•

MultiTeacher (MT)
[
47
]
: MT simultaneously incorporates multiple teacher networks by averaging the soft labels.

•

Adaptive Multi-Teacher Multi-Level Knowledge Distillation (AMTML)
[
29

: AMTML learns from the intermediate layers of each teacher using a multi-group hint strategy.

•

Stochastic Knowledge Distillation to obtain compact BERT style language model (AutoSKDBert)
[

•

Adaptive Ensemble Knowledge Distillation (AEKD)

4.3
Implementation Detail

In this section, we discuss in details for each application (1) what is used as the input to the Question Augmenter, (2) what is used as the input to the Answer Augmenter, and (3) how do each teacher process the questions.

•

Recommendation tasks. The student model is a standard recommendation system with embedding tables and MLPs. We use the representations retrieved from the embedding table as the input to the Question Augmenter. We use the hidden states before the last layer in teacher models as the input to the Answer Augmenter.

Teacher models include both standard recommendation systems and language models. For recommendation system teachers, we replace the hidden states after the embedding table with the generated questions. For language teachers, we replace the token embedding after the first transformer layer in the decoder with the generated question at the decoding stage.

•

Vision tasks. The student model is a Vision Transformer, and we use the representations of the classification token from the middle layer as the input to the Question Generator.

Teacher models include both CLIP and Vision Transformer. For the Vision Transformer teacher, we use the representation of the classification token from the last layer as the input to the Answer Augmenter. For CLIP teachers, we utilize the representation from the image encoder as the input to the Answer Augmenter. We replace the classification token in the middle transformer layer for the Vision Transformer teacher with the generated question. We replace the classification token in the transformer image module for the CLIP model with the generated question.

Selecting Teacher based on Importance Score

While we showcase significant improvements in distillation quality, one drawback involves the increasing computation cost. One potential approach to mitigate the increased cost due to the growing number of teachers is to select the most suitable teacher for each data based on the importance score.

The importance score is designed to reflect the usefulness of each teacher for every data sample. One can use the importance score for teacher selection to reduce the distillation cost resulting from multiple teachers. Specifically, students only ask questions to the teacher with a score greater than a threshold, which saves the forward computation cost of the teachers who are not selected.
We form a teacher committee with T5 and two MLP recommenders (
i.e.
MLP-L and MLP-M) and visualize the importance score of 11 random data points in Figure
4
. When using 0.6 as the threshold, we observe no loss in the distillation performance while saving around 30% of teachers’ passes.

5
Conclusion

One remarkable aspect of foundation models is their ability to excel across different tasks. And to facilitate intelligence on various tasks under a serving cost, it is crucial to transfer the task knowledge from a giant foundation model to a more efficient task model. In pursuit of this goal, we study distillation from foundation models where differences in architecture, data distribution, modality, and capacity exist between the student and teacher, mirroring the substantial gap between the foundation model and the downstream task model. Our contribution unfolds in two ways. First, we demonstrate that enhancing distillation performance is possible by incorporating complementary teachers and forming a teaching committee. Second, we introduce
DiverseDistill
, which is a general and promising distillation method applicable for distillation from a diverse set of teachers.
