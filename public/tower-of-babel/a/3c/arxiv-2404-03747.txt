Sensitivity, Proximity and FPT Algorithms for Exact Matroid Problems
Friedrich Eisenbrand, Lars Rohwedder, Karol Węgrzycki

Abstract
We consider the problem of finding a basis of a matroid with weight exactly equal to a given target. Here weights can be discrete values from { − Δ , … , Δ } \{-\Delta,\ldots,\Delta\} or more generally m m -dimensional vectors of such discrete values. We resolve the parameterized complexity completely, by presenting an FPT algorithm parameterized by Δ \Delta and m m for arbitrary matroids. Prior to our work, no such algorithms were known even when weights are in { 0 , 1 } \{0,1\} , or arbitrary Δ \Delta and m = 1 m=1 . Our main technical contributions are new proximity and sensitivity bounds for matroid problems, independent of the number of elements. These bounds imply FPT algorithms via matroid intersection.

1.2
Techniques

Our main technical contribution lies in the sensitivity and proximity bounds.
We will briefly review previous techniques, specifically those by
Cook et al.
[
9
]
and by Eisenbrand and Weismantel
[
17
]
.
Suppose for some solution
x
to an integer linear program we want to prove a bound on the distance to the
closest solution
z
, where the right-hand side is slightly perturbed.
One can naturally decompose the change from
z
to
x
into atomic changes that involve the increase or
decrease of a variable by
1
.
On a high level, Cook et al.’s approach can be summarized as defining small bundles of atomic changes that
do not affect feasibility.
In their case they use Hilbert basis elements of a carefully chosen cone and write
x-z
as
a (not necessarily integral) conic combination
of these bundles. If the distance
between
z
and
x
is sufficiently large, one of the bundles is taken at least once and then we can also apply only
this bundle exactly once to
z
, proving that there is a closer solution.
Crucial to this argument is that each bundle of atomic changes can be applied
to
z
independently.
In an inherently different approach, Eisenbrand and Weismantel arrange the atomic changes in a careful sequence
given by the Steinitz Lemma and achieve that if the distance between
x
and
z
is sufficiently large,
by pigeonhole principle there will be a “cycle” in the sequence, which is
an interval of atomic changes that does not affect feasibility.
This cycle is applied to
z
, which then also proves that there is a closer solution to
x
.

In the context of matroids, a natural candidate for an atomic change is the exchange of a pair of elements.
Both of the approaches above are applicable to restrictive classes of matroids, specifically
strongly
base-orderable
matroids, see
[
36
, 42.6c]
.
Essentially, this limited class
allows any subset of the atomic changes to be applied to
z
in isolation.
Implementing this approach to general matroids seems to be elusive.

Our proof is based on a novel structural result on matroids. If two independent sets
A
and
B
are of the same cardinality and are roughly of the same weight, then there are large
unicolor
subsets of them of equal cardinality, that can be mutually exchanged (
Lemma

8
and
Corollary

9
). A set is unicolor if all elements have the same weight. This is a key notion of this paper. Via identifying several such exchanges that nullify the total change of weight, and the theory of matroid intersection
[
16
,
29
]
, we can then derive the existence of another independent set of the matroid of equal cardinality that has the same weight as
A
. Specifically in higher dimension, we furthermore rely on polyhedral combinatorics like Carathéodory’s theorem and bounds on the complexity of facets and vertices.

Preliminaries

A

M=(E,\mathcal{I})

E=\{1,2,\dotsc,n\}

(M1)

(M2)

(M3)

If

.

Condition (M
3

Let

there exists

with

|A^{\prime}|=|B^{\prime}|
and

(I\setminus A^{\prime})\cup B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}
.

Similarly, for each
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
there exists
A^{\prime}\subseteq A

i
) and
ii
)

Proof.

The set
(I\setminus A^{\prime})
is an independent set.
We can identify
|A^{\prime}|

((I\setminus A)\cup B)\setminus(I\setminus A^{\prime})=B
that can be added to this set.
Similarly,
(I\setminus A)\cup B^{\prime}
is an independent set
and can be extended to the cardinality of
I
.
∎

A

is an inclusionwise maximal set of
\mathcal{I}
. All the bases of a matroid have the same cardinality, denoted by
\rank(M)
, the

For a subset

\chi(S)_{i}=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }i\in S,\\

The

The

\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{e\in S}x_{e}\leq\rank(S),&S\subseteq E\\

(2)

The convex hull of the characteristic vectors of bases of

An

. The weight of a subset

. A

. A subset

. We also refer to the condition

FPT Algorithms

In this section we discuss how the proximity bound in Theorem

Let

The goal is to

We first assume that the matroid

[

This means that testing

With the ellipsoid method, we compute a vertex

using

]

Theorem

implies that, if there exists a basis

of the matroid

, then there
exists a such basis with

\|\chi(A)-x^{*}\|_{1}\leq(m\Delta)^{O(m)}.

For
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in\{-\Delta,\dots,\Delta\}^{m}

3.1
Linear matroids

Linear matroids
are matroids that can be defined by a matrix
A
over a
field
\mathbb{F}
, such that the ground set
E
is the set of columns of
A

and
X
is an independent set if these columns are linearly independent. If a
matroid can be defined by a matrix
A

\mathbb{F}
, then we say
that the matroid is
representable
over
\mathbb{F}
. In this paper, when
we consider linear matroids, we always assume that the matrix

that
represents the matroid is also given on the input (along the independence
testing oracle). We assume, that
\mathbb{F}
is either a finite field or the

computational complexity we refer the reader to
[

Camerini et

[
7
]
presented a randomized, pseudopolynomial
time algorithm to find a basis
B

exactly

(\Delta n)^{O(1)}
time. In our setting, this is the one-dimensional case, i.e., the case

combined with our proximity bound leading to one and improving on the running time
of the previous method. This uses the pseudopolynomial time algorithm as a black box and
for matroids where a deterministic variant is known, e.g. for graphic matroids
[
4
]
,
our algorithm is also deterministic.
However, for general matroids given by an independence testing oracle, it is known that
a pseudopolynomial time algorithm cannot exist

]

We use a variant of a standard method of aggregating all

[

]

which can be done more efficiently when the search space is bounded due to proximity.
To this end, suppose there is an algorithm that, for a given matroid
M=(E,\mathcal{I})
with weights
w:E\rightarrow\{-\Delta,\dotsc,\Delta\}
and a target
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
finds a basis
B
of

with

(n\Delta)^{O(1)}
.
We will show that for the same matroid
M

W:E\rightarrow\{-\Delta,\dotsc,\Delta\}^{m}

and a target
{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
one can find a basis
B
with
W(B)={\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
in time

(m\Delta)^{O(m^{2})}n^{O(1)}
.
Let
x^{*}
be a vertex to the matroid base polytope
P_{B}(M)
intersected with
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}:Wx={\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}\}
.
Let
A
be the basis with
W(A)={\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
that is close to

as guaranteed by

We write
\lfloor x^{*}\rceil
for the
vector derived from
x^{*}
by rounding each component to the closest

\Gamma:=\|\chi(A)-\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}\leq 2\|\chi(A)-x^{*}\|_{1}\leq(m\Delta)^{O(m)}.

The first inequality follows from the fact that when

|\chi(A)_{i}-x^{*}_{i}|<1/2
then rounding will decrease the distance
in this dimension, and otherwise, it will increase it by at most

Since we do not know

but we can obtain it through guessing.

Let

We argue that

\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W(B)=\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}

Since the other direction is trivial, assume that

\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W(B)=\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}

multiplied by a higher power of

than constraint

i
. Hence, one has

W(B)_{i}\equiv{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{i}\mod 2\Gamma\Delta+1.

Further,

and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{i}=W(A)_{i}
are both in

\{W\lfloor x^{*}\rceil-\Gamma\Delta,\dotsc,W\lfloor x^{*}\rceil+\Gamma\Delta\}
and therefore the modulo operator is a bijection
and it follows that
W(B)_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{i}
.

It is not enough to run the pseudopolynomial time algorithm with

\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W(B)=\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
, since we also need to enforce
that
\|\chi(B)-\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}=\Gamma
. Towards this, let

be the one-dimensional weight of
element

, where

w_{1}(e)=\left(1-2\lfloor x^{*}_{e}\rceil\right)\text{ and }w_{2}(e)=\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W(e).

Further, let

\alpha:=\left(\Gamma-\|\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}\right)+(2n+1)\cdot\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}

be the target weight. Let
B
be a basis. We argue that

w(B)=\alpha
if and only if

\|\chi(A)-\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}=\Gamma
and
W(B)={\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
.
One has the following connection between

and

\|\chi(B)-\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}
.

\displaystyle w_{1}(B)+\|\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\chi(B)_{e}\cdot(1-2\lfloor x^{*}_{e}\rceil)+\lfloor x^{*}_{e}\rceil

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E}\chi(B)_{e}+\lfloor x^{*}_{e}\rceil-2\chi(B)_{e}\lfloor x^{*}_{e}\rceil

\displaystyle=\|\chi(B)-\lfloor x^{*}_{e}\rceil\|_{1},

where the last inequality follows because

for

. Thus,
\|\chi(A)-\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}=\Gamma

and

implies

. For the other direction
assume that

. Then

w_{1}(B)\equiv w(B)\equiv\beta\equiv\Gamma-\|\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}\mod 2n+1.

Since both sides are in

, it follows that
w_{1}(B)=\Gamma-\|\lfloor x^{*}\rceil\|_{1}

and further
\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}W(B)=w_{2}(B)=\lambda^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
, which means that

.
Thus, it suffices to run the pseudopolynomial time algorithm with

and
\alpha

Sensitivity

We now show Theorem

5.1
One constraint

Recall that

Theorem 6
.

Let

be disjoint with

Proof of Theorem

for

Given a bases

|w(A)-w(B)|\leq\mu

6
Applications

In this section we give specific examples of problems that can be cast
as finding a basis of a matroid subject to
m
constraints, each of
which have one of the following forms.

•

Equality constraint: given
w:E\rightarrow\{-\Delta,\dotsc,\Delta\}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
, require
w(B)=\beta
.

•

Inequality constraint: given
w:E\rightarrow\{-\Delta,\dotsc,\Delta\}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
, require
w(B)\leq\beta
, or alternatively
w(B)\geq\beta
.

•

Congruence constraints: given
p\in\{1,2,\dotsc,\Delta\}
,
w:E\rightarrow\{0,1,\dotsc,p-1\}
, and
\beta\in\{0,1,\dotsc,p-1\}
, require
w(B)\equiv\beta\mod p
.

While we proved our FPT algorithm only for the first type, it is easy to reduce the other two to it.
This follows from standard constructions similar to slack variables.
To this end, consider a matroid
M=(E,\mathcal{I})
.
Suppose for a given weight function
w:E\rightarrow\{-\Delta,\dotsc,\Delta\}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}
, we are searching
for a basis
B
with
w(B)\leq\beta
and possibly other linear constraints (with one of the three types
from above).
We define
M^{\prime}
as the direct sum of
M
and a rank
n
uniform matroid with
2n\Delta
elements. The weight function
w

is extended by setting an arbitrary half of the elements in the uniform matroid to weight zero and the other half to weight
1
. Any other linear constraint is extended with zero coefficients for the new elements, which means that they do not affect it. It follows easily that a basis
B
of the original matroid
M
can be extended to a basis
B^{\prime}
of
M^{\prime}
that satisfies
w(B^{\prime})=\beta
if and only if
w(B)\leq\beta
.

Now suppose we are given
p\in\{1,2,\dotsc,\Delta\}
,
w:E\rightarrow\{0,1,\dotsc,p-1\}
, and
\beta\in\{0,1,\dotsc,p-1\}
and are searching for a basis
B
with
w(B)\equiv\beta\mod p
and possibly other constraints.
Similar to before, we obtain
M^{\prime}
as the direct sum of
M
with a uniform matroid of rank
n
over

2n
elements. The weight function
w
is extended such that
n
many new
elements have weight
-p
and
0
each. Again, any other linear constraint is
extended with zero coefficients for the new elements. Then a basis
B
of
M
is extendible to a basis
B^{\prime}
of
M^{\prime}
that satisfies
w(B^{\prime})=\beta
if and only if
w(B)\equiv\beta\mod p
.

Very recently, matroid problems with labels from an abelian group have gained some attention. Liu and
Xu
[

study the following problem.

Group-Constrained Matroid Base

Input:
Matroid
M=(E,\mathcal{I})

, and

Task:
Find base

Liu and Xu prove that if

and

is

FPT time in

Corollary 15
.

If
(\Gamma,\odot)
is a finite abelian group then Group-Constrained
Matroid Base can be solved in
f(m)\cdot n^{O(1)}
time, for

m=|\Gamma|
.

Proof.

Every finite abelian group is isomorphic to the direct product of cyclic groups. Thus, we may
assume without loss of generality that
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}_{m_{1}}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m_{2}}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{m_{\ell}}
, where
m=m_{1}\cdot\dotsc\cdot m_{\ell}
for prime powers
m_{1},m_{2},\dotsc,m_{\ell}\leq m
.
We can therefore model the problem using
\ell\leq\log_{2}m
congruency constraints with entries bounded by
m
.
∎

Similar to our results, Liu and Xu’s techniques are based on

More precisely, Liu

In this section, we remark that low proximity and sensitivity do not generalize
to matroid intersection, even for
m=1
and
\Delta=1
. Our examples are
based on matchings in a bipartite graph
G=(A\cup B,E)

a bipartite graph does not form an independent set in a single matroid, it can
be represented as a matroid intersection of two partition matroids, where one
partition matroid restricts the degree of each vertex in
A
to be at most one
and the other does the same for
B
.

We start with the example of a high sensitivity for matroid intersection.

Theorem 19
.

For infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist matroids
M=(I,E),M^{\prime}=(I^{\prime},E)

over the same set of elements and a weight function
w:E\rightarrow\{0,1\}
,
such that there exist exactly two
two common bases
B
and
B^{\prime}
of both matroids that satisfy:

1.

w(B)=0
and
w(B^{\prime})=1
,

2.

B\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset
and
|B|=|B^{\prime}|=n/2
.

Proof.

For
n
even, we create an instance of bipartite matching consisting of a cycle of length
n
that has a single edge of weight
1
and all others of weight
0
, see also left cycle in Figure
3
.
There are exactly two perfect matchings, which correspond to the common bases of the two matroids
and trivially satisfy the properties stated in the theorem.
∎

Next, we show an example of a high proximity for matroid intersection.

Theorem 20
.

For infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exist matroids
M=(I,E),M^{\prime}=(I^{\prime},E)

and a weight function
w:E\rightarrow\{0,1\}
with
a vertex solution
x^{*}
to the continuous relaxation
x\in P_{B}(M)\cap P_{B}(M^{\prime}),w^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}x=1

there is a unique common basis
B
of both
M
and
M^{\prime}
with
w(B)=1
and
B
satisfies

\lVert x^{*}-\chi(B)\rVert_{1}=3/4\cdot n
.

Proof.

For
n

4
,
consider an instance of bipartite matching similar to the proof
of
Theorem

19
, but now
on a graph with two disjoint cycles of length
n/2
each, each of which we can think of as the union of two perfect matchings.
The first cycle has a single edge of weight
1
and the second cycle has two edges of weight
1
, which
appear in the same perfect matching.
All other edges have weight zero, see also Figure
3
.
Now suppose we want to find a perfect matching with total weight equal to

The only such perfect matching, i.e., the only common basis of both matroids, takes the perfect matching
with the weight

This is also a vertex solution for the continuous relaxation, but there is a second vertex solution
that takes each edge of the second cycle

The distance between the two solutions is
