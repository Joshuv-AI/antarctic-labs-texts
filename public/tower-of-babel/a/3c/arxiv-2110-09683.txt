Covariant Guiding Laws for Fields
Maaneli Derakhshani, Michael K.-H. Kiessling, A. Shadi Tahvildar-Zadeh

Abstract
. After reviewing what is known about the passage from the classical Hamilton–Jacobi formulation of non-relativistic point-particle dynamics to the non-relativistic quantum dynamics of point particles whose motion is guided by a wave function that satisfies Schrödinger’s or Pauli’s equation, we study the analogous question for the Lorentz-covariant dynamics of fields on spacelike slices of spacetime. We establish a relationship, between the DeDonder–Weyl–Christodoulou formulation of covariant Hamilton–Jacobi equations for the classical field evolution, and the Lorentz-covariant Dirac-type wave equation proposed by Kanatchikov amended by our proposed guiding equation for such fields. We show that Kanatchikov’s equation is well-posed and generally solvable, and we establish the correspondence between plane-wave solutions of Kanatchikov’s equation and solutions of the covariant Hamilton–Jacobi equations of DeDonder–Weyl–Christodoulou. We propose a covariant guiding law for the temporal evolution of fields defined on constant time slices of spacetime, and show that it yields, at each spacetime point, the existence of a finite measure on the space of field values at that point that is equivariant with respect to the flow induced by the solution of Kanatchikov’s equation that is guiding the actual field, so long as it is a plane-wave solution. We show that our guiding law is local in the sense of Einstein’s special relativity, and therefore it cannot be used to analyze Bell-type experiments. W e conclude by suggesting directions to be explored in future research. Dedicated to Demetrios Christodoulou on the occasion of his 70th birthday

It is hard to imagine that any such ad-hoc procedure could have been proposed based on compelling reasoning, without the empirical success of the

That’s precisely what many physicists have been doing for decades.

2.
Least Invasive Quantization of Newtonian Particle Mechanics

Our aim in this section is to explain least invasive quantization of Newtonian mechanics, in which a key role is played by the action principle.
Thus we begin by recalling the main formulations of Newtonian mechanics that involve the action principle, and then we turn to the quantization step.

The basic ideas can be explained by considering a single point particle with Newtonian inertial mass
m
and given potential energy

V=V(t,q)
, with
t\in\mathbb{R}
denoting an instant of time and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
a generic particle position in physical space.
The generalization to the traditional many-body models with
N
point particles interacting pairwise with various Newtonian forces is entirely straightforward.
To go beyond the basic ideas we will also consider a charged, spinning point particle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is acted on by external electromagnetic potentials
(\phi,{A})
.

2.1.
Lagrange, Hamilton, and Hamilton–Jacobi

Our point of departure is the familiar Lagrange function

L(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
for the particle’s motion, given by

(2.1)

L(t,q,v)=\frac{1}{2}m|v|^{2}-V(t,q).

Here,
v\in T_{q}\mathbb{R}^{3}\sim\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes a generic velocity vector in the tangent space at
q
.
An actual particle position at time
t
will be denoted by
q(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the actual particle velocity at time
t
is
\dot{q}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
For any differentiable path
t\mapsto q(t)
that connects
q_{1}:=q(t_{1})
with
q_{2}:=q(t_{2})
the corresponding action is

(2.2)

\mathcal{A}[q(\,\cdot\,)]:=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(t,q(t),\dot{q}(t))dt.

We recall that the classical Newtonian motion is a stationary point of
\mathcal{A}
on the space of differentiable paths from
q_{1}
to
q_{2}
.
If the stationary point is twice-continuously differentiable, it satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation

(2.3)

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L(t,q(t),v)}{\partial v}\Big|_{v=\dot{q}(t)}\right)-\frac{\partial L(t,q,\dot{q}(t))}{\partial q}\Big|_{q={q}(t)}=0,

or, explicitly,

(2.4)

m\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}q(t)=-\frac{\partial V(t,q)}{\partial q}\Big|_{q={q}(t)},

which is the familiar Newtonian equation of motion of a point particle in a conservative (though here time-dependent) force field, as found in introductory
text books on classical mechanics.

The path from the Lagrangian action principle to the Hamilton–Jacobi principle is via Hamilton’s formulation.
Defining the Hamiltonian to be the Legendre transform of
L
as follows,

(2.5)

H(t,q,p):=\max_{v}\{\langle p,v\rangle-L(t,q,v)\}=\frac{1}{2m}|p|^{2}+V(t,q),

where the angular parentheses denote duality pairing between tangent and co-tangent space at
q
.
Here, (after, as usual for Euclidean space, identifying tangent and co-tangent spaces) the generic canonical momentum
p
emerges to be

(2.6)

p:=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v}=mv.

Inverting the Legendre transform
L\mapsto H
yields the map
H\mapsto L
, as

(2.7)

L(t,q,v):=\max_{p}\{\langle p,v\rangle-H(t,q,p)\}=\frac{m}{2}|v|^{2}-V(t,q);

now inserting this definition of
L
into the Lagrangian action principle yields Hamilton’s equations,

(2.8)

\dot{q}(t)=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\Big|_{q={q}(t),p=p(t)},\qquad\dot{p}(t)=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\Big|_{q={q}(t),p=p(t)},

which state that the velocity of the actual phase-space point
(q(t),p(t))
at time
t
is given by evaluating a generic phase-space velocity field

(\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q})
at that actual phase-space point.
Understood in this way the system (
2.8
) is a
guiding law
on phase space, with a given guiding field.

Hamilton’s equations (
2.8
), written explicitly as

(2.9)

\dot{q}(t)=\frac{1}{m}p(t)\qquad,\qquad\dot{p}(t)=f(t,q(t)),

here with
f(t,q)=-\partial_{q}V(t,q)
, are a special conservative-forces case of Newton’s equations of motion, who stated
(
2.9
) for more general forces
f(t,q,v)
.
This first-order system (
2.9
) is manifestly equivalent to the second order formulation obtained from Lagrange’s formalism.

The Hamilton–Jacobi formulation emerges as a consequence of Hamilton’s equations being form-invariant under canonical transformations to a new set
of variables
(Q,P)
, time-dependent functions of
(q,p)
.
This means that there are new variables

(2.10)

Q=Q(q,p,t)\qquad,\qquad P=P(q,p,t),

such that if
K
denotes the Hamiltonian written in the new coordinates, then Hamilton’s equations in the new coordinates read

(2.11)

\dot{Q}(t)=\frac{\partial K}{\partial P}\Big|_{Q={Q}(t),P=P(t)},\qquad\dot{P}(t)=-\frac{\partial K}{\partial Q}\Big|_{Q={Q}(t),P=P(t)}.

Thus,
K
is equivalent to
H
— in the sense that the corresponding Lagrangians
L
and
\tilde{L}
differ at most by a complete time derivative, i.e. along an actual motion we have

(2.12)

L-\tilde{L}=\frac{d}{dt}\widehat{S}(t),

where
\widehat{S}(t)=S(t,q(t),P(t),Q(t))
for some function
S
of
t
,
q
,
Q
, and
P
, evaluated at
q=q(t)
,
Q=Q(t)
, and
P=P(t)
.
This is because we can invert the canonical transformations (
2.10
) to express
p=p(Q,P,t)
.

The Hamilton–Jacobi formulation emerges when one realizes that the new coordinates can (in principle) be chosen such that
K\equiv 0
,
as a result of which
Q(t)
and
P(t)
will be constant:
\dot{Q}(t)=0=\dot{P}(t)
.
We then have (suppressing the dependence of
S
on
Q
and
P
)

(2.13)

L(t)-\tilde{L}(t)=\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}(t,q(t))+\frac{\partial S}{\partial q}\Big|_{q=q(t)}\dot{q}(t),

which can only be satisfied for all actual motions if, generically,

(2.14)

p=\frac{\partial S}{\partial q},\qquad-H=\frac{\partial S}{\partial t}.

The second one of equations (
2.14
) is the
Hamilton–Jacobi (partial differential) equation
,

(2.15)

{{\partial_{t}S}+H(q,\nabla_{q}S,t)=0}\,,

a first-order nonlinear PDE for the
Hamilton–Jacobi phase function

S(t,q)[=S(t,q;Q,P)
; here,
Q=(Q^{1},Q^{2},Q^{3})
and
P=(P^{1},P^{2},P^{3})
represent

6
parameters].
S
is thus a function that depends on time
t
and the generic position of the particle
q
.
In the case of our particular example it’s easy to see that the Hamilton–Jacobi equation has the form of an eikonal equation:

(2.16)

\boxed{\partial_{t}S+\frac{1}{2m}|\nabla_{q}S|^{2}=-V(t,q)}\,.

If more than one particle are present, say
N
, the phase function
S
is a function of time
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and points
q\in\mathcal{C}:={\mathbb{R}}^{3N}
,
the
configuration space
of the
N
point particles in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Every solution
S=S(t,q)
of the Hamilton–Jacobi partial differential equation (
2.16
) gives rise to a

3
-parameter family of solutions of Hamilton’s equations.
This is established through the method of characteristics, which requires solving the system of ODEs corresponding
to the first of the Hamilton’s equations
(
2.9
), with the generic
p
given by the first of equations (
2.14
).
Thus, once a solution
S(t,q)
for given initial data
S(0,q)
has been obtained, the
actual Newtonian particle motion with initial data
q(0)
and
p(0)=\nabla_{q}S(0,q)|_{q(0)}
is obtained by solving

(2.17)

\boxed{\dot{q}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S(t,q)\Big|_{q=q(t)}}

with
q(0)
to be chosen. Note that once
q(0)
is chosen,
p(0)
is fixed by the first of equations (
2.14
).
The second one of Hamilton’s equations (
2.9
) is automatically satisfied by solutions of (
2.17
) by virtue of (
2.14
).

Equation (
2.17
) is a
guiding law
on configuration space: the space gradient of the Hamilton–Jacobi phase function guides the actual motion of the particle by providing its velocity vector at the particle’s actual location.
For more than one particle this is a velocity vector field on configuration space, evaluated at the actual
N
-particle configuration.

We already know that the ordinary differential equations of motion obey an action principle.
Also the Hamilton–Jacobi partial differential equation (
2.16
) is derivable from an action principle, in fact trivially
so at the expense of introducing a new function for the purpose of carrying out variations.
In this vein, let
\varrho\geq 0
be a function of
t
and
q
, compactly supported on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, then set

(2.18)

{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(\varrho,S):=\left(\partial_{t}S+V+\frac{1}{2m}|\nabla_{q}S|^{2}\right)\varrho,

and let
\mathcal{A}_{0}[\varrho,S]:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(\varrho,S)d^{3}\!qdt
be the corresponding action.
The stationary points of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
with respect to variations of
\varrho
on
\varrho
’s support then trivially satisfy (
2.16
) on
\varrho
’s support.

Now, if this was all there is to it, this variational principle for
S
would merely be a “cheap trick” without offering anything new.
Yet if we ask for
\mathcal{A}_{0}
to be
jointly
stationary with respect to compactly supported variations of
both

\varrho
and
S
,
i.e. setting also the variation of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
with respect to
{S}
equal to zero, we obtain the following continuity equation for
\varrho
:

(2.19)

\boxed{\partial_{t}\varrho+\nabla_{q}\cdot\left(\varrho\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S\right)=0}\,.

Thus there is a natural interpretation that can be given to a stationary point
(\varrho,S)
of
\mathcal{A}_{0}
.

Namely, the continuity equation implies that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\varrho(t,q)d^{3}\!q
is preserved in time, hence
normalizing
\varrho\geq 0
such that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\varrho(t,q)d^{3}q=1
we can at each time
t
think of
\varrho
as a probability density on configuration space

\mathcal{C}
for an ensemble of
independently evolving
mechanical systems of the same kind.
Given an initial map
q\mapsto p
through
C^{1}
initial data
S(0,q)
,
by solving the equation (
2.16
) we obtain a time-evolving velocity field on configuration space, given by
\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S(t,q)
, independently of
the ensemble.
Any initial
\varrho(0,q)
defines an ensemble of such
independent
individual systems, obeying the initial assignment
q\mapsto p
, and the
continuity equation for
\varrho(t,q)
captures the change with time of the ensemble density as the velocity field on configuration space
transports the systems in the initial ensemble to other locations in configuration space, in the course of time.

Each individual system in the ensemble of course evolves according to (
2.17
), compatible with the evolution of the probability density.
This is our first instance of what is called
equivariance
.

2.2.
Least invasive quantization

In the Hamilton–Jacobi theory of evolution of a classical ensemble of systems the ensemble density
\varrho
is a
passive
variable, in the
sense that it is transported by the velocity field
\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S
while the evolution of
S
in turn is completely unaffected by the evolution of

\varrho
— perfectly in line with the fact that the classical evolution of the systems in the ensemble is independent of each other.
Thus, once
S(t,q)
has been determined, any initial
\varrho(0,q)
is allowed and will be evolved by the same continuity equation.
In particular, one can let
\varrho(0,q)\to\delta(q-q(0))
and obtain the weak formulation of the guiding law (
2.17
) for the actual motion of
a single system starting at
q(0)
with initial velocity
\dot{q}(0)=\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S(0,q)_{q=q(0)}
.

The spirit of
least invasive quantization
is to change the role of
\varrho
from being passive into
active
, a dynamical variable on par with
S
.
The continuity equation (
2.19
) and the guiding equation (
2.17
) remain the same, but (
2.16
) will be continuously
deformed (with the help of a parameter) into a modified PDE that also involves
\varrho
.
In the realm where Newton’s mechanics gives an accurate description of nature, the
\varrho
-containing term will make an insignificant contribution and
the modified Hamilton–Jacobi PDE will be practically (though not exactly) identical to (
2.16
).

Thus, while
\varrho
still satisfies the mathematical requirements of a probability density, i.e.
\varrho\geq 0
and
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\varrho(t,q)d^{3}\!q=1
,
and therefore can be used for the computation of answers to all the same questions for which it can be used in the classical setting,
it
gains
status beyond the original one by also assuming an active role in the generation of the particle dynamics.

Of course, this idea in itself leaves open infinitely many possible ways to deform the Hamilton–Jacobi dynamics.
Yet there is only one way, modulo equivalences, to arrive at Schrödinger’s equation on configuration space.
In the following we describe two equivalent procedures, each with its own narrative.
We also briefly explain the generalization that leads to the Pauli equation for a spinor wave function that guides the motion of a charged point particle.

2.2.1.
Adding an entropy penalty term to the action functional
\mathcal{A}_{0}[\varrho,S]
.

The first procedure we describe starts from the Hamilton–Jacobi formulation involving the pair
(\varrho,S)
and adds to the action functional the Fisher entropy functional of
\varrho
, multiplied by an appropriately dimensional parameter.
Thus, the least invasive quantization step is the replacement

(2.20)

\mathcal{A}_{0}[\varrho,S]\to{\mathcal{A}}_{\hbar}[\varrho,S]:=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\!\left(\partial_{t}S+V+\frac{1}{2m}|\nabla_{q}S|^{2}\right)\!\varrho d^{3}\!qdt+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!|\nabla_{q}\sqrt{\varrho}|^{2}d^{3}\!qdt.

Stationarity of
\mathcal{A}_{\hbar}
w.r.t. variation of
S
again yields the continuity equation (
2.19
), while stationarity of
\mathcal{A}_{\hbar}
w.r.t. variation of
\varrho
now yields

(2.21)

\boxed{\partial_{t}S+\frac{1}{2m}|\nabla_{q}S|^{2}=-V+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\Delta_{q}\sqrt{\varrho}}{\sqrt{\varrho}}}.

The least invasively quantized version of the classical Hamilton–Jacobi model, in which solutions of the Hamilton–Jacobi PDE (
2.16
)
generate the actual motions through (
2.17
) and transport an ensemble density of systems through (
2.19
), describes a different
kind of dynamics.
Namely, the
\hbar
-deformed Hamilton–Jacobi PDE (
2.21
) has to be solved
jointly
with the continuity equation (
2.19
);
subsequently the actual motions are obtained through solving the guiding equation (
2.17
).

We note that this model obeys equivariance of the evolution of the distribution of configurations.

The just described least invasively quantized Hamilton–Jacobi model is the de Broglie–Bohm model of quantum motions of point particles
(modulo notational variations;
e.g. our
\sqrt{\varrho}
is Bohm’s

Instead, their goal was to vindicate their (independent) proposals that solutions to Schrödinger’s equation

(2.22)

i\hbar\partial_{t}\psi=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\Delta\psi+V\psi

play the role of a

They noted that when they substituted the polar decomposition

(2.23)

\psi:=\sqrt{\varrho}e^{iS/\hbar}

for

one then easily sees that (

2.21

Since (
2.21

superficially appears to be a Hamilton–Jacobi PDE with (what Bohm sanctioned) a “quantum potential”

added to

\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S
is still a velocity field on configuration space that transports the density
\varrho
as per (
2.19
),
it is compelling then to postulate that the velocity field also generates the actual motions through (
2.17
).
De Broglie, in fact, was searching for a deformation of the Hamilton–Jacobi model already in his Ph.D. thesis, retaining (
2.17
) all along,
so for him pieces were falling in place when Schrödinger’s equation appeared.

Remark 2.1
.

There exists an unfortunate but widespread misperception that “Bohm tried to derive quantum mechanics from Newtonian mechanics by adding new
kinds of forces that are very contrived.”
Such and similar statements, which can be found in the Encyclopedia Britannica and other authoritative publications, miss the point that the velocity field
\frac{1}{m}\nabla_{q}S

cannot be eliminated from the de Broglie–Bohm configuration space formulation of quantum mechanics to arrive at a genuine Newtonian theory of motion that
involves only the actual positions and velocities (or momenta), and forces that depend only on these.
The fact that in the de Broglie–Bohm theory
\varrho
plays an active role in the generation of the dynamics, which is impossible in any Newtonian dynamics,
makes this plain: in de Broglie–Bohm theory you can’t evolve
S
without
\varrho
and its second derivatives, and to evolve
\varrho
you need
\nabla_{q}S
.
(This and several other misperceptions of the de Broglie–Bohm theory are addressed in
[
17
]
.)

\square

Least invasive quantization as described above also has the advantage that it is entirely straightforward to see that the mathematical limit
\hbar\to 0
of the
least invasively quantized model will manifestly give us back the Hamilton–Jacobi model of a
\varrho
-ensemble of classical systems, each one of which evolving as per the guiding equation (
2.17
) compatible with equivariance.
Indeed, simply fix the initial data
S(0,q)
and
\varrho(0,q)
and note that the quantum potential term in (
2.21
) vanishes with
\hbar\to 0
.
The guiding equation and the continuity equation remain unaffected by this limit.

Of course, in nature
\hbar
is fixed, and thus the physically more informative criterion for the validity of the classical approximation is not the mathematical
limit
\hbar\to 0
but a comparison of
the two terms
\propto\frac{1}{2m}
in (
2.20
). Since
S
has physical dimension of action, set
S:=\hbar\Phi
. Then
the de Broglie–Bohm particle dynamics described by (
2.17
) and (
2.21
) approximates classical mechanical dynamics described
by (
2.17
) and (
2.16
) whenever
|\nabla_{q}\Phi|^{2}\gg|\nabla_{q}\ln{\varrho}|^{2}
.
Corrections to the classical motions can be computed systematically by looking for solutions of (
2.21
)
in the form of a formal power series in
\hbar
, thus giving rise to WKB or so-called semi-classical approximations, all the time retaining the guiding equation
(
2.17
).

Remark 2.2
.

In the orthodox QM literature (e.g
[
20
]
, sect.6;
[
22
]
, chpt.A, sect.12) one typically finds claims that the
\hbar\to 0
limit of
(
2.21
), producing (
2.16
), would, in concert with (
2.19
), prove that QM converges to classical Newtonian mechanics,
because the “fluid elements on configuration space (with density
\varrho(t,q)
and infinitesimal volume
\delta{V}
)” would, in the Lagrange picture
of fluid mechanics, obey Newton’s equations of motion (with “infinitesimal mass
\varrho(t,q)\delta{V}
”).
However, such claims are misguided when at the same time it is denied, as unfortunately is the case in orthodox accounts of
QM, that an individual
N
-body system would, at time
t
, have an actual configuration
q(t)
unless one measures it.
If the complete quantum state at time
t
of an individual system is
\psi_{t}\equiv(\varrho,S)_{t}
, then this remains the case when
\hbar\to 0
, because there
is nothing in the mathematics of Schrödinger’s equation which would require that for an individual system
\varrho(0,q)\to\delta(q-q(0))
as
\hbar\to 0
.
So, to obtain Newtonian mechanics from QM, in addition to
\hbar\to 0
one has to
postulate
that at time
t
a physical
N
-body system does
have an actual configuration
q(t)
, or one inherits the dilemma of Schrödinger’s cat.
And since one has to postulate this for the purpose of obtaining Newtonian mechanics in
the limit
\hbar\to 0
, then it is prudent to postulate it directly for
\hbar>0
, evolving as per (
2.17
);
that’s precisely what de Broglie–Bohm theory does.
\square

2.2.2.
Switching from the real pair
(\varrho,S)
to a complex
\psi
, then linearizing the PDE for
\psi

There is a variation on the theme of what we call least invasive quantization, presented recently by Mia Hughes
[
12
]
,
which has the enticing charm of showing that the de Broglie–Bohm version of
quantum mechanics could have been discovered by serendipity long before Planck’s discovery of the law of the black body spectrum.

Indeed, having the Hamilton–Jacobi model of an ensemble of Newtonian mechanical systems of the same kind that at time
t=0
have momenta

p=\nabla_{q}S(0,q)
, with
q
distributed by
\varrho(0,q)
, the ensemble distribution evolving as per (
2.19
) for an
S
evolving as per (
2.16
),
and with each member of the ensemble evolving as per (
2.17
), it is conceivable that someone notices that by defining
a complex-valued field defined on the particle configuration space,
\psi:\mathcal{C}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, by
\psi:=\sqrt{\varrho}e^{iS/a}
, where
a
is a parameter
with physical dimension of action (for a narrative that pretends to predate Planck’s law it would be strange to use
\hbar
at this point, of course), the two PDEs
(
2.16
) and (
2.19
) of the Hamilton–Jacobi model can be combined into a single complex-valued PDE, thus

(2.24)

ia\partial_{t}\psi=-\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\Delta\psi+V\psi+\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\frac{\Delta|\psi|}{|\psi|}\psi,

while the Hamilton–Jacobi guiding law expressed in terms of
\psi
becomes

(2.25)

\dot{q}(t)=a\frac{\Im(\psi^{*}\nabla_{q}\psi)}{\psi^{*}\psi}\Big|_{q=q(t)}.

Before coming to the least invasive quantization step, it is noteworthy to register that
while (
2.24
) is locally equivalent to the pair of PDE (
2.16
), (
2.19
), the continuity equation can be extracted
from (
2.24
) without polar decomposition of
\psi
.
Thus multiplying
(
2.24
) with
\psi^{*}
and the complex conjugate equation of (
2.24
) by
\psi
, then subtracting the latter from
the former, the continuity equation for
\varrho=|\psi|^{2}
materializes in the following form, identical to the one in QM:

(2.26)

\partial_{t}|\psi|^{2}+a\nabla_{q}\cdot\Im(\psi^{*}\nabla_{q}\psi)=0.

Having this equivalent complex reformulation of the Hamilton–Jacobi model of a
\varrho
-ensemble in front of us, the
least invasive quantization
step,
modulo identification of
a=\hbar
, consists in purging the nonlinear term in (
2.24
), arriving at Schrödinger’s equation (
2.22
),
except for the parameter
a
in place of
\hbar
.
Mia Hughes argued this step would be plausible because the double slit experiment suggests the linear superposition principle, and this is the obvious way to
implement it.

Remark 2.3
.

Independently of the linear-superposition-principle argument, someone may have considered the approximate Hamilton–Jacobi PDE obtained by purging
the nonlinear term in (
2.24
), simply because linear PDE are easier to discuss.
Conceivably the so-approximated Kepler problem would have been studied, solved, and the Bohr energy spectrum discovered ahead of Bohr’s model.
It is not too far fetched to imagine that someone in the 1890s could also have noticed that the family of differences
\triangle E
between two energy levels resemble the Rydberg formula and (ahead of Planck, hence of Bohr) proposed that the hydrogen frequencies
\nu\propto\triangle E
, thus extracting a value for
a
, discovering
\hbar
.

\square

The above
least invasive quantization
step can also be implemented via the action principle. Expressed in terms of
\psi
and
\psi^{*}
as a real expression,
the classical Lagrangian
{{\mathcal{L}}}_{C}[\psi,\psi^{*}]
reads

(2.27)

{{\mathcal{L}}}_{C}(\psi,\psi^{*})=\frac{ai}{2}\left(\psi^{*}\partial_{t}\psi-\psi\partial_{t}\psi^{*}\right)-\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\nabla_{q}\psi\cdot\nabla_{q}\psi^{*}-V\psi^{*}\psi+\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\big|\nabla_{q}\sqrt{\psi^{*}\psi}\big|^{2},

and the classical action
{\mathcal{A}}_{C}[\psi^{*},\psi]:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{{\mathcal{L}}}_{C}(\psi,\psi^{*})d^{3}\!qdt
.
For the variations,
\psi^{*}
and
\psi
are to be treated as independent;
\psi^{*}
variation yields (
2.24
),
\psi
variation the
complex conjugate of (
2.24
).
The least invasive quantization step, modulo identification of
a
, is the replacement
{\mathcal{A}}_{C}[\psi^{*},\psi]\to{\mathcal{A}}_{Q}[\psi^{*},\psi]
, this time consisting of

dropping
the Fisher entropy of
|\psi|
from
{\mathcal{A}}_{C}[\psi^{*},\psi]
; viz.
{\mathcal{A}}_{Q}[\psi^{*},\psi]:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Q}(\psi,\psi^{*})d^{3}\!qdt
,
with

(2.28)

{{\mathcal{L}}}_{Q}(\psi,\psi^{*})=\frac{ai}{2}\left(\psi^{*}\partial_{t}\psi-\psi\partial_{t}\psi^{*}\right)-\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\nabla_{q}\psi\cdot\nabla_{q}\psi^{*}-V\psi^{*}\psi.

If
\psi
is a stationary point of the action
{\mathcal{A}}_{Q}[\psi^{*},\psi]
then it will satisfy the Schrödinger equation

(2.29)

ia\partial_{t}\psi=\hat{H}\psi,\qquad\hat{H}:=-\frac{a^{2}}{2m}\Delta_{q}+V,

as obtained by purging the nonlinear term in (
2.24
).
Since only the Fisher term has been purged from the classical action
\mathcal{A}_{C}[\psi^{*},\psi]
, while the guiding equation (
2.25
) has been retained,
we have arrived at the de Broglie–Bohm model of quantum motions — modulo identification of
a
with
\hbar
.

We emphasize that in the least invasive quantization narrative, whether the
(\varrho,S)
version or the
\psi
version, there is no room for arguing why the guiding equation (
2.17
), equivalently (
2.25
), should suddenly be purged.
Doing so would give Schrödinger’s equation alone, hence his cat.

Remark 2.4
.

While we have emphasized that the
\psi
version of least invasive quantization has the advantage of an “appearance of plausibility”
(what we mean by “discovering quantum physics by serendipity”), we should also emphasize that
there is a price to be paid, namely it is not at all manifest why (
2.27
) should be the classical limit of (
2.28
).
Superficially, the two formulations, using the real pair
(\varrho,S)
vs. using the complex
\psi
, even seem to contradict each other in their logic:
in the
(\varrho,S)
formulation, the Fisher term is missing from the classical action and is added in the least invasive quantization step; in the

\psi
formulation, the Fisher term is purged in the least invasive quantization step.
Yet the two classical actions are equivalent, and so are the two quantum actions.
The illuminating resolution of this apparent paradox is left for the reader to ponder.
\square

2.2.3.
Generalization: charged particles and spinor wave functions

The classical (test particle) motion in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of a point electron with mass
m
and electric charge
-e
that is acted on
by applied electromagnetic fields obtained from given potentials
\phi
and
A
(assuming for simplicity no non-electromagnetic forces are acting)
is obtained by simply identifying
V(t,q)=-e\phi(t,q)
and replacing
\nabla_{q}S(t,q)\to\nabla_{q}S(t,q)+\frac{e}{c}A(t,q)

in the classical Hamilton–Jacobi formalism that has been recalled above.
Thus the actual Newtonian particle motion with given initial data
q(0)
and
p(0)=\nabla_{q}S(0,q)|_{q(0)}+\frac{e}{c}A(t,q(0))

is obtained by solving

(2.30)

\boxed{\dot{q}(t)=\frac{1}{m}\left(\nabla_{q}S(t,q)\Big|_{q=q(t)}+\frac{e}{c}A(t,q(t))\right)}

with
q(0)
given, where
S
, with
S(0,q)
chosen such that
p(0)=\nabla_{q}S(0,q)|_{q(0)}+\frac{e}{c}A(t,q(0))
, solves the Hamilton–Jacobi PDE
obtained from compactly supported
\varrho
-variations of the action

(2.31)

\mathcal{A}_{0}[\varrho,S]=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\!\left(\partial_{t}S-e\phi+\frac{1}{2m}\big|\nabla_{q}S+\frac{e}{c}A\big|^{2}\right)\!\varrho d^{3}\!qdt;

variations w.r.t.
S
yield the continuity equation
for
\varrho
with velocity field
\frac{1}{m}\left(\nabla_{q}S(t,q)+\frac{e}{c}A(t,q)\right)
.

These so-called minimal coupling steps are of course well-known; they are essentially dictated by demanding gauge-invariance of the dynamical theory.

Also known, though less well-known, is the Hamilton–Jacobi theory for the classical motion of a point electron with charge
-e
, mass
m
, and
magnetic moment
\mu(t)
that obeys the Euler law of evolution
\dot{\mu}(t)=\frac{e}{2mc}\mu(t)\times B\big(t,q(t)\big)
, which implies that
|\mu(t)|
is constant.
For the magnitude of the electron’s magnetic moment a natural choice is what elsewhere we have called the
classical magnetic moment
of the
electron,
e^{3}/4\pi mc^{2}
, i.e. the product of the elementary charge and the so-called
classical electron radius
, divided by
\frac{1}{4\pi}
.
Since the equations now become a bit unwieldy, we only write down the action functional.
Let
\vartheta
and
\varphi
denote the usual angles on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Then
\mu=-(e^{3}/4\pi mc^{2})(\sin\vartheta\cos\varphi,\sin\vartheta\sin\varphi,\cos\vartheta)
.
With
\vartheta
and
\varphi
functions of
t
and
q
, the action reads

(2.32)

\mathcal{A}_{0}[\varrho,S,\vartheta,\varphi]=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\!\left(\partial_{t}S-e\phi+\tfrac{e^{2}}{4\pi c}\cos\vartheta\partial_{t}\varphi+\tfrac{1}{2m}\big|\nabla_{q}S+\tfrac{e}{c}A+\tfrac{e^{2}}{4\pi c}\cos\vartheta\nabla_{q}\varphi\big|^{2}-\mu\cdot B\right)\!\varrho d^{3}\!qdt.

Next, to get from the classical theory of motion of such a point electron with magnetic moment, to the de Broglie–Bohm theory involving Pauli’s
equation by least invasive quantization, takes two ingredients: replace the classical quantum of action
e^{2}/c
by Planck’s
h
in all
expressions that represent the magnetic moment of the electron, call this functional
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\hbar}
,
then add the Fisher entropy term

(2.33)

\mathcal{F}[\varrho,\vartheta,\varphi]=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\sum_{k=1}^{3}\big|\nabla_{q}\sqrt{\varrho_{k}}\big|^{2}d^{3}\!qdt

to
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{\hbar}[\varrho,S,\vartheta,\varphi]
to obtain the quantum action functional
\mathcal{A}_{\hbar}[\varrho,S,\vartheta,\varphi]
; here,

\varrho_{1}=\varrho\sin^{2}\frac{\vartheta}{2}\cos^{2}\frac{\varphi}{2}
,

\varrho_{2}=\varrho\sin^{2}\frac{\vartheta}{2}\sin^{2}\frac{\varphi}{2}
,
and

\varrho_{3}=\varrho\cos^{2}\frac{\vartheta}{2}
.
Now defining

(2.34)

\psi=\sqrt{\varrho}e^{iS/\hbar}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac{\vartheta}{2}e^{i\varphi/2}\\
i\sin\frac{\vartheta}{2}e^{-i\varphi/2}\end{pmatrix},

the least invasively quantized action reveals itself as the action of the Pauli equation, viz.

(2.35)

\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\hbar}[\psi,\psi^{\dagger}]=\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\!\!\left(\frac{\hbar}{2i}\left(\psi^{\dagger}\partial_{t}\psi-(\partial_{t}\psi^{\dagger})\psi\right)+\frac{1}{2m}\left|\vec{\sigma}\cdot\left(-i\hbar\nabla_{q}+\frac{e}{c}A\right)\psi\right|^{2}-e\phi|\psi|^{2}\right)d^{3}\!qdt,

where
\vec{\sigma}
is the vector of the three Pauli matrices.

The reverse steps, going from
\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\hbar}[\psi,\psi^{\dagger}]
to
\mathcal{A}_{\hbar}[\varrho,S,\vartheta,\varphi]
, have been described in
[
24
]
.

3.
Hamilton–Jacobi Theories for Fields

Before we can talk about field-theoretical generalizations of the least invasive quantizaion procedure outlined in the previous section, we need to review the generalization of Hamiltonian dynamics and Hamilton–Jacobi theory to the case of Lorentz-covariant fields. Most of the material in this section are taken from the excellent survey of Kastrup
[
15
]
and the pioneering work of Christodoulou
[
4
]
on the subject.

3.1.
Lagrangian field theory

Consider the action

(3.1)

\mathcal{A}[\phi,\mathcal{D}]:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}L\circ\sigma_{\phi}

for
\mathcal{D}
a bounded open domain in the spacetime
({\mathcal{M}},g)
, which we take to be an
m+1
-dimensional Lorentzian manifold, and
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{N}}
a (classical) field on spacetime that is smooth (at least
C^{1}
) on
\mathcal{D}
, and is taking its values in some smooth manifold
{\mathcal{N}}
of dimension
n
.

Moreover,
L
is a
Lagrangian density
, i.e. an
m
-form-valued section of the
velocity bundle

(3.2)

{\mathcal{V}}:=\bigcup_{(x,q)\in{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{N}}}{\mathcal{L}}(T_{x}{\mathcal{M}},T_{q}{\mathcal{N}})

and
\sigma_{\phi}
is the section of
{\mathcal{V}}
that corresponds to
\phi
(see below.)
Here
{\mathcal{L}}(V,W)
denotes the set of linear maps from vector space
V
to vectorspace
W
. Given local coordinates
(x^{\mu})
on
{\mathcal{M}}
and
(q^{a})
on
{\mathcal{N}}
, let
\{\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}\}
and
\{\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}}\}
denote corresponding bases for the tangent spaces
T_{x}{\mathcal{M}}
and
T_{q}{\mathcal{N}}
, and let
\{dx^{\mu}\}
,
\{dq^{a}\}
be the dual bases to those, for
T^{*}_{x}{\mathcal{M}}
and
T^{*}_{q}{\mathcal{N}}
, resp. A linear transformation
v\in{\mathcal{L}}(T_{x}{\mathcal{M}},T_{q}{\mathcal{N}})
can thus be expanded in these bases as
v=v_{\mu}^{a}(dx^{\mu}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}})
so that
(x^{\mu},q^{a},v_{\mu}^{a})
form a local system of coordinates for
{\mathcal{V}}
. We refer to
v_{\mu}^{a}
as the
canonical velocities
.

{\mathcal{V}}
is clearly a bundle over the field configuration space
\mathcal{C}:={\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{N}}
, and therefore also a bundle over
{\mathcal{M}}
as well as one over
{\mathcal{N}}
. For any bundle
\mathcal{B}
over a base manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
, let
\pi_{\mathcal{B},{\mathcal{M}}}
denote the projection onto the base. Let
\wedge_{p}{\mathcal{M}}
denote the tensor bundle of
p
-forms on the manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
. Consider the pullback bundle
\pi_{{\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{M}}}^{*}\wedge_{m}\!{\mathcal{M}}
. This is a bundle over
{\mathcal{V}}
. A
Lagrangian density

L
is a section of this bundle. Given a map
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{N}}
let
d\phi:T{\mathcal{M}}\to T{\mathcal{N}}
denote its tangent map (also known as its derivative, or differential). It follows that
\sigma_{\phi}:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{V}}
defined by
\sigma_{\phi}(x)=(x,\phi(x),d\phi(x))
is a section of
{\mathcal{V}}
as a bundle over
{\mathcal{M}}
. Hence for any Lagrangian density
L
defined as in the above,
L\circ\sigma_{\phi}
is a section of
\wedge_{m}{\mathcal{M}}
, i.e. an
m
-form, which therefore can be integrated on a domain
\mathcal{D}
in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Moreover, since
{\mathcal{M}}
is here assumed to be a Lorentzian manifold with a metric
g
, it has a distinguished volume form
\epsilon=\epsilon[g]
, the one that is covariantly constant, and thus there exists a function
\ell=\ell(x,q,v)
such that
L\circ\sigma=(\ell\circ\sigma)\epsilon
. We call
\ell
the Lagrangian density function corresponding to
L
.

For the remainder of this paper we are going to assume that
{\mathcal{N}}
is a linear space, i.e. isomorphic to
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and that
{\mathcal{M}}
is the
3+1
-dimensional Minkowski space. This is done mainly to simplify the presentation, and most of what follows is generalizable to curved spacetimes and nonlinear targets. (For instance, when
{\mathcal{N}}
is a nonlinear space, in order to proceed further we would need to fix a symmetric connection on
{\mathcal{N}}
, and then we always need to prove that any result we obtain is independent of the choice of that connection. See
[
4
]
for details.)

As is well known, the Euler-Lagrange equations for critical points of the action (
3.1
) take the form

(3.3)

\partial_{\mu}\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v_{\mu}^{a}}-\partial_{a}\ell=0.

Consider the particular case of the following Lagrangian:

(3.4)

\ell_{SF}(x,q,v)=\frac{1}{2}v_{\mu}^{a}v^{\mu}_{a}-V(q),

so that, when composed with a section
\sigma_{\phi}
we obtain

(3.5)

(\ell_{SF}\circ\sigma_{\phi})(x)=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}\partial^{\mu}\phi_{a}-V(\phi(x)).

This is the so-called Lagrangian of a
covariant (Lorentz-)scalar field
(“scalar” here may be a bit of a misnomer since
\phi
may have multiple components, but it is meant to distinguish this with the case of
\phi
being a vectorfield or other tensorfield defined on
{\mathcal{M}}
, which requires a more careful treatment, see
[
4
]
, Chap. 4.)

The Euler-Lagrange equations for stationary points of the scalar field action
\mathcal{A}_{SF}[\phi,\mathcal{D}]:=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\ell_{SF}\circ\phi\epsilon
are therefore

(3.6)

\square_{x}\phi^{a}-\partial^{a}V(\phi)=0,\qquad\square_{x}:=\partial_{\mu}\partial^{\mu},

which is a coupled system of linear or semi-linear wave equations, depending on whether the dependence of
V
on
\phi
is quadratic or not. One could also obtain
quasilinear
systems of wave equations here, if one considers the more general family of Lagrangians that are of the form
\ell(x,q,v)=f(\xi)-V(q)
with
\xi=v_{\mu}^{a}v^{\mu}_{a}
. For example the so-called membrane equations (a.k.a. “scalar Born-Infeld”) belong to this family, with
f(\xi)=1-\sqrt{1-\xi}
.

3.2.
Covariant Hamiltonian field theory of DeDonder and Weyl

Because Hamilton’s equations (
2.9
) are ordinary differential equations, where the only independent variable is time, at first sight it seems problematic to ask for a covariant Hamiltonian theory, since the specification of a time function on spacetime breaks the Lorentz covariance of the theory.
On the other hand, soon after Hamilton’s original contribution, and beginning with Volterra in 1890, mathematicians had begun to obtain generalizations of Hamiltonian theory to more than one independent variable (see
[
15
]
for the full history of this subject.) One of these was DeDonder, who in 1913 obtained such a generalization, as well as an associated Hamilton–Jacobi theory for variational problems with several independent variables. Another significant contribution was by Caratheodory in 1929. In 1930 DeDonder published a monograph on the subject, and after further discussion and analysis of DeDonder’s theory by Weyl in 1934/35, this theory began to be referred to as the
DeDonder–Weyl
(DW) theory, which is the one we will describe below.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a classical action of the form (
3.1
). Recall that the quantities
v_{\mu}^{a}
are called
canonical velocities
. By analogy with the classical Hamiltonian mechanics, let

(3.7)

p_{a}^{\mu}:=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v_{\mu}^{a}}

be the
canonical momenta
. Just as canonical velocities can be described abstractly as fibers of the velocity bundle
{\mathcal{V}}
, it is possible to define a dual object, called the
momentum bundle

{\mathcal{P}}
, whose fibers are the canonical momenta
p
(More precisely, it is
*p
, the Hodge dual of
p
with respect to the volume form
\epsilon
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, that form the fibers of
{\mathcal{P}}
.) The definition of the momentum bundle is

(3.8)

{\mathcal{P}}=\bigcup_{(x,q)\in{\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{N}}}\wedge_{m-1,1}(T_{x}{\mathcal{M}},T_{q}{\mathcal{N}}),

where
\wedge_{m-1,1}(V,W):={\mathcal{L}}(W,\wedge_{m-1}V)={\mathcal{L}}(\wedge^{m-1}V,W^{*})
for any two vector spaces
V
and
W
. In coordinates, this means that the fibers over
\mathcal{C}
are

(3.9)

*p=p^{\mu}_{a}\epsilon_{\mu,\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{m-1}}dx^{\mu_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge dx^{\mu_{m-1}}\otimes dq^{a}.

One can view the definition (
3.7
) of
p
as a mapping from
{\mathcal{V}}
into
{\mathcal{P}}
that takes
v\mapsto p(v):=\partial\ell/\partial v
. This is called the
Legendre transform
. We will restrict ourselves to those variational problems for which this mapping is invertible, i.e. the Legendre transform is non-singular. (Singular cases can be handled by a further extension of DW theory, see
[
15
]
.)

Let
v=v(p)
denote the inverse of the Legendre transform. The
DeDonder–Weyl Hamiltonian

H_{DW}
is then defined to be

(3.10)

H_{DW}=H_{DW}(x,q,p):=v(p)_{\mu}^{a}p^{\mu}_{a}-\ell(x,q,v(p)).

The
DeDonder–Weyl–Hamilton
(DWH) equations are then as follows:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}q^{a}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H_{DW}}{\partial p_{a}^{\mu}}(x,q(x),p(x))

(3.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}p^{\mu}_{a}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial H_{DW}}{\partial q^{a}}(x,q(x),p(x)),

where
p(x):=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v}(x,q(x),dq(x))
(see
[
15
]
or
[
4
]
for a derivation.)

While the definitions of momentum in the case of one independent variable (
2.6
) and several independent variables (
3.7
) seem identical, as do the definitions of the corresponding Hamiltonians (
2.5
) and (
3.10
), there are some crucial differences between these two Hamiltonian theories, apart from the fact that DWH equations are not ODEs but PDEs:

(i)

Unlike (
2.6
), the definition of canonical momentum (
3.7
) is
not
unique. It turns out that there is quite a bit of freedom in the definition of
p_{a}^{\mu}
. One can in fact define
p_{a}^{\mu}:=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial v_{\mu}^{a}}-h^{\mu\nu}_{ab}v_{\nu}^{b}
where the only restriction on functions
h_{ab}^{\mu\nu}=h_{ab}^{\mu\nu}(x,q,v)
is that they are anti-symmetric in both lower and upper indices. This is connected with the theory of
null Lagrangians
(see
[
4
]
, p. 125). On the one hand this freedom can be used to extend DWH theory to the cases where the Legendre transform is singular. On the other hand, this lack of uniqueness means that there are many different Hamiltonian theories that correspond to the same Lagrangian field theory, and one perhaps needs some other criteria to distinguish between them. Certainly the DeDonder–Weyl theory presented here is the
simplest
such theory, since its
h\equiv 0
, but it may not be the “best” such theory by some other criteria. For example it can be argued that Caratheodory’s theory is superior to DW in some respects (see
[
15
]
for a detailed discussion.)

(ii)

Another main difference between single and multiple independent variable Hamiltonian theories is that in the latter case, the first Hamilton equation (
3.11
) has an
integrability condition
: Suppose there exists a smooth field
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{N}}
such that the section
(x,q,v)
with
q^{a}=\phi^{a}(x)
,
v_{\mu}^{a}=\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}(x)
satisfies (
3.11
). Then the equality of mixed partial derivatives of
\phi
implies that the right hand side of this equation, when evaluated on
q=\phi(x)
, has to satisfy an integrability condition

(3.13)

\partial_{\nu}\frac{\partial H_{DW}}{\partial p_{a}^{\mu}}-\partial_{\mu}\frac{\partial H_{DW}}{\partial p_{a}^{\nu}}=0.

Another way to phrase this integrability condition is to think in terms of the inverse of the Legendre transform
v=v(p)
. Once
p
is determined in some way, this mapping implies that
v
is determined. If this
v_{\mu}^{a}=\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}
for some field
\phi
, then one must have

\partial_{\nu}(v(p)_{\mu}^{a})=\partial_{\mu}(v(p)_{\nu}^{a})
. Expanding this gives

(3.14)

\partial_{\mu}v_{\nu}^{a}+\partial_{b}v_{\nu}^{a}v_{\mu}^{b}-\partial_{\nu}v_{\mu}^{a}-\partial_{b}v_{\mu}^{a}v_{\nu}^{b}=0.

The above can also be written in the language of differential forms: Let
w^{a}:=v_{\mu}^{a}dx^{\mu}
. Then (
3.14
) is equivalent to

(3.15)

dw^{a}+w^{b}\wedge D_{b}w^{a}=0,

(which is the way Christodoulou expresses it in
[
4
]
. This is the second equation in what he calls the
Euler System
). Here
d
denoted exterior differentiation in
{\mathcal{M}}
and
D
is a covariant derivative on
{\mathcal{N}}
(with respect to an arbitrary symmetric connection.)

Clearly, the integrability condition (
3.15
) is a serious restriction. It could make it difficult to solve these equations. In particular, as we shall see in the next section, the existence of these integrability conditions makes the DeDonder–Weyl generalization of Hamilton–Jacobi equations much less useful than the classical HJ theory.

3.3.
DeDonder–Weyl–Christodoulou covariant Hamilton–Jacobi theory

Arguably the most obvious generalization of the first equation in (
2.14
) to the case of several independent variables is to assume the existence of a
vectorfield

\mathbf{S}=S^{\mu}(x,q)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}
defined on the field configuration space
\mathcal{C}
in such a way that

(3.16)

p_{a}^{\mu}=\partial_{a}S^{\mu}.

From (
3.12
) one immediately gets that

(3.17)

\partial_{\mu}S^{\mu}+H(x,q,\partial_{a}S^{\mu})=0.

(We have dropped the “DW” subscript from
H
since that’s the only type of Hamiltonian we will henceforth be talking about.) This is called the DeDonder–Weyl Hamilton–Jacobi (DWHJ) equation. One notes that this is a single equation for a four component object, so there seems to be quite a bit of freedom in choosing
\mathbf{S}
. However, let
v=v(p)
be the inverse Legendre transform, and let
\mathbf{S}(x,q)
be any solution of (
3.17
). By (
3.16
), its gradient (with respect to field variables) determines
p
, and thus
v
through the inverse Legendre transform. As described in the previous section, this immediately gives rise to the integrability condition (
3.15
), which should be viewed as another equation for
\mathbf{S}
.
Solutions of the DWHJ equation (
3.17
) that do not satisfy this integrability condition, do not correspond to actual stationary points of the original Lagrangian, and are therefore of limited use as far as the analysis of solutions relevant to the theory is concerned.

Let us examine this issue in the context of the classical scalar field theory. Recall that
\ell_{SF}=\frac{1}{2}v_{\mu}^{a}v^{\mu}_{a}-V(q)
, and therefore

(3.18)

p_{a}^{\mu}=\frac{\partial\ell_{SF}}{\partial v_{\mu}^{a}}=v^{\mu}_{a},

so that

(3.19)

H_{SF}=\frac{1}{2}p_{a}^{\mu}p^{a}_{\mu}+V(q).

The DWHJ equation for the scalar field is therefore

(3.20)

\partial_{\mu}S^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{a}S^{\mu}\partial^{a}S_{\mu}+V=0.

Moreover, we have

(3.21)

v_{\mu}^{a}=p^{a}_{\mu}=\partial^{a}S_{\mu},

so that
\mathbf{S}
must also satisfy the integrability condition

(3.22)

\partial_{\nu}\left(\partial^{a}S_{\mu}(x,\phi(x))\right)-\partial_{\mu}\left(\partial^{a}S_{\nu}(x,\phi(x))\right)=0,

which, after expansion and using that
\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}=\partial^{a}S_{\mu}(x,\phi)
gives the following nonlinear second-order system for
\mathbf{S}

(3.23)

\partial_{a}\partial_{\nu}S_{\mu}-\partial_{a}\partial_{\mu}S_{\nu}+\partial_{b}S_{\nu}\partial^{b}\partial_{a}S_{\mu}-\partial_{b}S_{\mu}\partial^{b}\partial_{a}S_{\nu}=0,\qquad a=1,\dots,n,\quad 0\leq\mu<\nu\leq 3.

Setting
w^{a}:=\partial^{a}S_{\mu}dx^{\mu}
shows that
w
must satisfy Christodoulou’s second “Euler system” equation (
3.15
).
Therefore a complete set of equations for the DWHJ vectorfield
\mathbf{S}
for the classical scalar field theory is

(3.24)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}S^{\mu}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{a}S^{\mu}\partial^{a}S_{\mu}+V

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(3.25)

\displaystyle dw^{a}+w^{b}\wedge\partial_{b}w^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\qquad w^{a}:=\partial^{a}S_{\mu}dx^{\mu}.

3.4.
Christodoulou’s density function

Suppose now we want to attempt to carry out the same least-invasive quantization procedure that we did in the case of a mechanical system of particles
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Thus we need to begin by considering an action principle for the DWHJ equation (
3.17
).
The obvious candidate for an action for (
3.17
) is

(3.26)

\mathcal{A}:=\iint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\partial_{\mu}S^{\mu}+H(x,q,\nabla_{q}\mathbf{S})\right)\rho\ d^{4}xd^{n}q,

with
\rho=\rho(x,q)
a function defined on the field configuration space
\mathcal{C}={\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{N}}
.
Demanding that
\mathcal{A}
be stationary w.r.t. variations of
\rho
manifestly gives the DWHJ equation (
3.17
), while demanding stationarity of
\mathcal{A}

w.r.t. variations of
S^{\mu}
will give us a system of equations for
\rho
:

(3.27)

\partial_{\mu}\rho+\partial_{a}\left(\rho\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_{\mu}^{a}}\right)=0,\qquad\mu=0,\dots,3.

Once again we note that the above system has an integrability condition. As shown in
[
4
, Prop. 2.11]
, the integrability for the
\rho
system above is the same as the original integrability condition we have seen before, namely (
3.15
).

The above function
\rho
to our knowledge makes its first appearance in mathematical literature in the work of Christodoulou
[
4
, p. 99]
, who called it the
density function
2
2

2

This terminology is justified since it is easy to see from (
3.26
) that
\rho(x,\cdot)
has the physical dimensions of a density (i.e.
1/\mbox{Volume}
) on
{\mathcal{N}}
.
, and proposed the equation it should satisfy, which is the same as (
3.27
), except that he wrote it in differential forms language, and for the Hodge dual of
\rho
(with respect to the volume form of
{\mathcal{N}}
), as

(3.28)

d\rho+\delta(w\cdot\rho)=0

(
\delta
= exterior derivative on
{\mathcal{N}}
). Christodoulou called this “the equation of continuity,” and used it to show that the integral of
\rho
over any domain
\Omega
in
{\mathcal{N}}
is invariant under the Hamiltonian flow (assuming that the integrability conditions are satisfied.)

We remark that, similar to the case of the classical Hamilton–Jacobi theory, here the first DWH equation (
3.11
) also has the interpretation of a
classical guiding law
, this time for a classical field beable
\phi(x)
, namely, once
\mathbf{S}
is known, evaluating (
3.11
) on
q=\phi(x)
results in an equation specifying all derivatives of
\phi
at a point
x
once the value of
\phi
is known at that point:

(3.29)

\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}(x)=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p^{\mu}_{a}}\left(x,\phi(x),\nabla_{q}\mathbf{S}(x,\phi(x))\right).

Since the integrability condition for the above is assumed to be satisfied (recall this is (
3.25
)) the above equation has a unique solution in the neighborhood of any point
(x_{0},q_{0})\in\mathcal{C}
.

For example, in the case where
H
is the classical scalar field Hamiltonian (
3.20
), the above is simply

(3.30)

\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}(x)=\partial^{a}S_{\mu}(x,\phi(x)),

while the equation satisfied by
\rho
, (
3.27
), is simply

(3.31)

\partial_{\mu}\rho+\partial_{a}(\rho\partial^{a}S_{\mu})=0.

As explained by Christodoulou
[
4
, p. 93]
, a solution
\mathbf{S}
of the DWHJ equations (
3.24
–
3.25
) generates a
flow

on field configuration space in the following way: Let
F:{\mathcal{M}}\times\mathcal{C}\to{\mathcal{N}}
be the mapping

F(x,x_{0},q_{0})=\phi(x)

where
\phi
is the unique solution to (
3.30
) with
\phi(x_{0})=q_{0}
, and let
F_{(x,x_{0})}:{\mathcal{N}}\to{\mathcal{N}}
denote the associate (2-parameter) flow on
{\mathcal{N}}
:
F_{(x,x_{0})}(q_{0})=F(x,x_{0},q_{0})
. For fixed
x_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}
and
\Omega_{x_{0}}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
such that
q_{0}\in\Omega_{x_{0}}
, let
\Omega_{x}:=F_{(x,x_{0})}(\Omega_{x_{0}})
.

On the other hand, one can use

the density function
\rho
to define an
x
-dependent
mass function
on
{\mathcal{N}}
: For any point
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
and domain
\Omega\subset{\mathcal{N}}
, one defines the (dimensionless) function

(3.32)

\mu_{x}(\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\rho(x,q)d^{n}q.

Christodoulou showed
[
4
, p. 104]
that the equation of continuity (
3.31
) for
\rho
implies that this mass function is invariant under the DWHJ flow
F
,

in the sense that the function
m(x):=\mu_{x}(\Omega_{x})
is independent of
x
.

Furthermore, integrating (
3.31
) on all of
{\mathcal{N}}
and using the divergence theorem yields that (under appropriate decay conditions on the field)
\mu_{x}({\mathcal{N}})
is a constant, and therefore can be normalized to be equal to one. Thus
x\mapsto\mu_{x}
assigns to a point
x\in{\mathcal{M}}
a
probability measure
on
{\mathcal{N}}
that is invariant with respect to the DWHJ flow.
We wish to see to what extent this can be replicated for a “quantized” version of the DWHJ theory.

The complete set of DeDonder–Weyl–Christodoulou (DWC) equations for a covariant Hamilton–Jacobi theory of the classical scalar field therefore consists of (
3.24
) and (
3.31
),
together
with their common integrability condition (
3.25
).
If we truly wanted to replicate the least-invasive quantization procedure in this context, we would have to first find an action principle for this combined set of equations (including the integrability condition), and then seek to deform
that
action into one for a complex (or hypercomplex)-valued field
\Psi=\Psi(x,q)
defined on the field configuration space that satisfies a linear, Lorentz-covariant equation. At the moment we do not know if the set of equations (
3.24
,
3.25
,
3.31
) is even derivable from a Lagrangian.

In light of this issue, we may choose to proceed differently, first by trying to guess what a linear, Lorentz-covariant equation for
\Psi
could look like, and then asking what would the analog to (
2.23
) be of the polar (or more appropriately, Prüfer) decomposition of
\Psi
in terms of
\rho
and
\mathbf{S}
, to see
if anything resembling the DWCHJ equations emerges in this way. This in a nutshell is the program started by I. V. Kanatchikov in a series of papers beginning with
[
13
]
. We can also ask if the resulting equations for
\rho
allow it to be interpreted probabilistically, and if not, what further assumptions need to be imposed in order to do so. These are the subjects of our next section.

4.
Kanatchikov’s Covariant Generalization of Schrödinger’s Equation

Kanatchikov argued that in order to render the Schrödinger equation (
2.29
) Lorentz-covariant, it is sufficient to
replace the time derivative
\partial_{t}
that appears on its left, by the covariant version of it, namely

\not{\partial}=\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
, with
\{\gamma^{\mu}\}_{\mu=0}^{3}\subset M_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
being a set of Dirac matrices, i.e. generators of the complexified spacetime Clifford algebra
{\mathbb{A}}=\mathop{Cl}_{1,3}({\mathbb{R}})_{\mathbb{C}}
:

(4.1)

i\lambda\not{\partial}\Psi=\hat{H}\Psi,\qquad\hat{H}=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\Delta_{q}+V,

where
\lambda
is a parameter whose relation to
\hbar
is to be determined. (It is interesting to note that Kanatchikov’s wave equation can also be recovered from a covariant canonical quantization procedure developed by von Hippel & Wohlfarth applied to DeDonder-Weyl’s classical Hamiltonian field theory
[
14
]
.)

Since this change makes the operator
\not{\partial}
itself an element of this algebra, it stands to reason that what it acts on, namely
\Psi
, would also belong to this algebra, i.e.
\Psi
would be a section of a vector bundle over field configuration space, with
{\mathbb{A}}
-valued fibers:
\Psi=\Psi(x,q)\in{\mathbb{A}}
.

If on the other hand
\Psi
belongs to a subspace
{\mathbb{B}}
of
{\mathbb{A}}
that is
not
an ideal of
{\mathbb{A}}
, then (
4.1
) also implies that the extra components that may appear on the left-hand side of this equation, those that are not in
{\mathbb{B}}
, would have to be zero, since
\hat{H}
is a scalar operator, and therefore the right-hand side will always be in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

Kanatchikov then takes
{\mathbb{B}}
to be the (5-dimensional) subspace of
{\mathbb{A}}
spanned by the matrices
\{I,\gamma^{0},\dots,\gamma^{3}\}
. Here
I
denotes the
4\times 4
identity matrix. Thus every
\Psi\in{\mathbb{B}}
can be written as

(4.2)

\Psi=\psi I+\psi_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}

for
\psi,\psi_{\mu}\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Note that
{\mathbb{B}}
is not an ideal, since the product of two gamma matrices is not in
{\mathbb{B}}
.

(
4.1
) and (
4.20
) now readily imply that
\psi=\psi(x,q)
and
\psi_{\mu}=\psi_{\mu}(x,q)
must satisfy the following system

(4.3)

\displaystyle i\lambda\partial_{\mu}\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{H}\psi_{\mu}

(4.4)

\displaystyle i\lambda\partial_{\mu}\psi^{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{H}\psi

(4.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\psi_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\psi_{\mu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

(The last equation is the consequence of
{\mathbb{B}}
not being an ideal.) Below we shall see that this system is in fact self-consistent and solvable. In particular, the third equation is the integrability condition of the first equation. (Note that we are only considering potentials
V=V(q)
, so that the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
does not depend explicitly on
x
.)

4.1.
Solvability of the Kanatchikov System

We now turn to the system of equations (
4.3
–
4.5
) satisfied by
(\psi,\psi_{\mu})
, and show that this is solvable so long as the quantum Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
is self-adjoint as an operator on field configuration space and that it does not depend explicitly on the spacetime point
x
. These conditions are for example satisfied by
\hat{H}=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\Delta_{q}+V
for a variety of
x
-independent potentials
V=V(q)
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The Kanatchikov system (
4.3
–
4.5
) is well-posed and uniquely solvable so long as
\hat{H}
is self-adjoint and does not depend explicitly on
x
.

Proof.

We begin by noting that, differentiating (
4.3
) and using (
4.4
) we obtain

(4.6)

i\lambda\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi=\partial^{\mu}(\hat{H}\psi_{\mu})=\hat{H}\partial^{\mu}\psi_{\mu}=\hat{H}\left(\frac{1}{i\lambda}\hat{H}\psi\right)=\frac{1}{i\lambda}\hat{H}^{2}\psi,

so that
\psi
is decoupled from
\psi_{\mu}
and satisfies its own equation

(4.7)

-\lambda^{2}\,\raisebox{3.0pt}{\framebox{}}\,\psi=\hat{H}^{2}\psi

where
\,\raisebox{3.0pt}{\framebox{}}\,:=\partial^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}
is the D’Alembertian (wave) operator on Minkowski space.

Furthermore, (
4.3
) shows that, once
\psi
is known,
\psi_{\mu}
would be determined.
To see this in more detail, let us define a 1-form
\tilde{\psi}(x,q):=\psi_{\mu}(x,q)dx^{\mu}
. We observe that (
4.5
) implies that

(4.8)

(d\tilde{\psi})_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}\psi_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}\psi_{\mu}=0

so that by Poincaré’s lemma there exists a scalar
\chi=\chi(x,q)
with the property that
\tilde{\psi}=d\chi
,
where
d
is the exterior derivative opertor in
x
.
Thus

(4.9)

\psi_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\chi.

Equation (
4.7
) can be solved in a variety of ways, including by separation-of-variables.

S
uppose now that (
4.7
) is solved, so that
\psi
is known. Substituting (
4.9
) into (
4.3
) we then obtain

(4.10)

i\lambda\,\raisebox{3.0pt}{\framebox{}}\,\chi=\hat{H}\psi

which is an inhomogeneous wave equation with a given right-hand-side, and again solvable by various methods. Once
\chi
is known,
\psi_{\mu}
is determined by (
4.9
).

In conclusion,
the Kanatchikov system (
4.3
–
4.5
) is uniquely solvable. Moreover, the above analysis shows that
in Kanatchikov’s Ansatz (
4.2
),
the quantities
\psi_{\mu}
are determined by
\psi
and do not represent additional degrees of freedom for the system.

∎

4.2.
Connection of Kanatchikov’s equation to DeDonder–Weyl Theory

Kanatchikov
[
13
]
also proposed an analog of (
2.23
) for a
\Psi
in
{\mathbb{B}}
, namely

(4.11)

\Psi=R\exp\big({i}\gamma_{\mu}S^{\mu}/{\lambda}\big),\qquad R,S^{\mu}\in{\mathbb{C}}.

This readily implies that

(4.12)

\psi=R\cos\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda}|\mathbf{S}|\Big),\qquad\psi_{\mu}=iR\frac{\sin(\frac{1}{\lambda}|\mathbf{S}|)}{|\mathbf{S}|}S_{\mu},

where

|\mathbf{S}|:=\left(S_{\mu}S^{\mu}\right)^{1/2}.

From here on we are going to make the additional assumptions (not made by Kanatchikov) that
R
and
S_{\mu}
are real, and that
\mathbf{S}
is a timelike vectorfield. These assumptions are made in order to ensure that one does not get exponentially growing terms in (
4.12
) as
\lambda\to 0
.

Kanatchikov then continues his investigation by plugging (
4.12
) into (
4.1
) and proceeds to derive equations for
R
and
S_{\mu}
. He arrives at the surprising conclusion that in the limit
\lambda\to 0
one does
not
recover the DWHJ equation (
3.17
) for
S_{\mu}
(Kanatchikov does not appear to be aware of Christodoulou’s continuity equation (
3.31
) and does not mention any candidate for the
R
equation.) More precisely, he shows that in order for
\mathbf{S}
to satisfy a deformed version of the DWHJ equation, it would have to satisfy a whole set of other constraints, which in essence force
\mathbf{S}
to be equal to
(S,0,0,0)
, thus throwing things back to the classical Hamilton–Jacobi equation of particle mechanics (
2.16
) and not the DeDonder–Weyl generalization of it to fields.

With hindsight, this outcome should not have been surprising: as we have seen, the DWCHJ theory has an integrability condition (
3.25
) that needs to be imposed
in addition
to the equations for
\rho
and
\mathbf{S}
, while the Kanatchikov system
contains
its own integrability condition. Thus it stands to reason that the reduction of this system under the Ansatz (
4.11
) would have to contain more than just the DWHJ equation for
\mathbf{S}
and the continuity equation for
\rho=R^{2}
, but also at least some remnant of the integrability conditions.

Here we take a different approach, recalling that our proposed least
invasive
quantization procedure involves the deformation of the
action
of the classical theory, not directly the equations. We therefore begin by reducing the action corresponding to Kanatchikov’s equation (
4.1
), under the decomposition (
4.11
) he proposed, and compare that to the action of the DWCHJ equations in the same variables, to see how one can arrive at one from the other.

Let
{\mathbb{A}}
denote the complexified spacetime algebra
\mathop{Cl}_{1,3}({\mathbb{R}})_{\mathbb{C}}
. The elements of
{\mathbb{A}}
are called
Clifford numbers
. For
\Psi\in{\mathbb{A}}
we define its
Dirac adjoint

\overline{\Psi}
(also known as
Clifford reversion
) as

(4.13)

\overline{\Psi}:=\gamma^{0}\Psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}.

Since
{\mathbb{A}}
is isomorphic to the matrix algebra
M_{4}({\mathbb{C}})
it is convenient to think of
\Psi
as a
4\times 4
complex matrix. The Dirac gamma matrices
\gamma^{0},\dots,\gamma^{3}
are
4\times 4
matrices satisfying the Clifford relations
\{\gamma^{\mu},\gamma^{\nu}\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}I
where
\eta=(\eta_{\mu\nu})
is the Minkowski metric
\eta=\mbox{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)
. Note that
\overline{\gamma^{\mu}}=\gamma^{\mu}
. A convenient basis for
{\mathbb{A}}
is

(4.14)

\mathcal{B}:=\{I;\quad\gamma^{0},\dots,\gamma^{3};\quad\alpha^{1},\alpha^{2},\alpha^{3};\quad\Sigma^{1},\Sigma^{2},\Sigma^{3};\quad\gamma^{5}\gamma^{0},\dots,\gamma^{5}\gamma^{3};\quad\gamma^{5}\}

where
\alpha^{k}:=\gamma^{0}\gamma^{k}
;
\Sigma^{k}:=i\epsilon_{lj}{}^{k}\gamma^{l}\gamma^{j}
, and
\gamma^{5}=i\gamma^{0}\gamma^{1}\gamma^{2}\gamma^{3}
. The
scalar part

(\Psi)_{sc}
of a Clifford number is the coefficient of
I
in the expansion of
\Psi
in any basis for
{\mathbb{A}}
. Using the matrix isomorphism, we have

(4.15)

(\Psi)_{sc}=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}\,\Psi

where
\mathrm{tr}\,
denotes ordinary matrix trace. We also introduce the non-degenerate bilinear form

(4.16)

\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle=\iint_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\overline{\Psi}\Phi\right)_{sc}\ d^{4}xd^{n}q

for
{\mathbb{B}}
-valued fields
\Phi,\Psi
defined on
\mathcal{C}
.

An action for Kanatchikov’s equation (
4.1
) is

(4.17)

5.
A Guiding Law for Covariant Scalar Fields

5.1.
Equivariance

Let us recall that the guiding law (
2.25
) for Schrödinger’s equation (
2.29
) goes hand-in-hand with the continuity equation (
2.26
) to ensure that the
equivariance property
holds:

A) Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a region in the particle configuration space. Integrating (
2.26
) on
\Omega
and using the divergence theorem one obtains

(5.1)

\partial_{t}\int_{\Omega}\varrho(t,q)d^{3}\!q=-\int_{\partial\Omega}\varrho(t,q)\mathbf{v}_{\psi}(t,q)\cdot\mathbf{n}d\sigma

where

(5.2)

\varrho:=|\psi|^{2},\qquad{\varrho}\mathbf{v}_{\psi}:={\hbar}\Im(\psi^{*}\nabla_{q}\psi).

In particular if
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\psi
decays to zero sufficiently rapidly as
|q|\to\infty
then the right-hand-side of (
5.1
) is zero, so that the total integral of
\varrho
is independent of time. This total integral, when finite, can be normalized to be one, which gives
\varrho=|\psi|^{2}
the interpretation of a probability density function defined on the particle configuration space; recall our discussion in section 2.

B) Now let
\Sigma_{0}\subset\{0\}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a region in the initial time slice of the spacetime. Consider the flow on the particle configuration space generated by the guiding equation (
2.25
):
\Phi_{t}(q)=q(t)
where
q(t)
is the solution to the ODE (
2.25
) with initial data
q(0)=q
. Let
\Sigma_{t}:=\Phi_{t}\Sigma_{0}
and define
\mathcal{D}:=\bigcup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Sigma_{t}
. Note that the continuity equation (
2.26
) implies that the vectorfield
\mathbf{X}:=\varrho\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\varrho\mathbf{v}_{\psi}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}}
is divergence-free on
{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, so that we have

(5.3)

0=\iint_{\mathcal{D}}\mbox{div}\,\mathbf{X}=\int_{\partial\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}d\sigma=\int_{\Sigma_{T}}\varrho(T,q)d^{3}q-\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\varrho(0,q)d^{3}\!q+\int_{\mathcal{K}}\mathbf{X}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{K}}d\sigma,

where
{\mathcal{K}}=\bigcup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Phi_{t}(\partial\Sigma)
is the lateral surface of the cylindrical domain
\mathcal{D}
, obtained by flowing
\partial\Sigma
under the flow
\Phi_{t}
. Since the velocity field of particles is
\mathbf{v}_{\psi}
it follows that the particle current is tangential to the lateral surface
{\mathcal{K}}
so that the last integral in (
5.3
) is zero. We therefore have

(5.4)

\int_{\Phi_{T}(\Sigma)}\varrho(T,q)d^{3}q=\int_{\Sigma}\varrho(0,q)d^{3}\!q.

Conclusion
: if the initial position of the particle is randomly distributed according to the density
\varrho(0,\cdot)
, then the position of the particle at any later time
t
is distributed by the density
\varrho(t,\cdot)
. This is called equivariance
[
6
]
.

More generally, any guiding equation (whether for particles or for fields) needs to be connected with a conserved current, so that there exists an equivariant density on the configuration space. An equivariant density on the configuration space is, along with the
quantum equilibrium hypothesis
, necessary for showing how de Broglie–Bohm guiding laws reproduce the standard QM predictions for subsystems
[
6
]
.

We note here that, in contrast to the classical case (
3.31
), in the quantum case the equation (
4.42
) satisfied by
\rho=R^{2}=\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi
is
not
a continuity equation, due to the presence of extra
u
-dependent terms, so that
\rho
in general does not appear to be the equivariant distribution we expect to have in connection with a Born Rule.

5.2.
Riesz tensor and system of conservation laws for Kanatchikov equations

Consider now the Kanatchikov equation as well as its adjoint

(5.5)

i\lambda\gamma^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\Psi=\hat{H}\Psi,\qquad-i\lambda\partial_{\mu}\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}=\overline{\hat{H}\Psi}.

Multiplying the first equation by
\overline{\Psi}
on the left and
\gamma^{\nu}
on the right, multiplying the second equation by
\Psi\gamma^{\nu}
on the right, and subtracting the two equations, we obtain

(5.6)

i\lambda\partial_{\mu}\left(\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}\right)=\overline{\Psi}\hat{H}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}-\left(\overline{\hat{H}\Psi}\right)\Psi\gamma^{\nu}.

Therefore

(5.7)

i\lambda\partial_{\mu}\left(\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}\right)=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left(\overline{\Psi}\Delta_{q}\Psi-\Delta_{q}\overline{\Psi}\Psi\right)\gamma^{\nu}=-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left[\partial_{a}(\overline{\Psi}\partial^{a}\Psi)-\partial_{a}(\partial^{a}\overline{\Psi}\Psi)\right]\gamma^{\nu}.

Thus
we have a conserved (Clifford-algebra-valued) current

(5.8)

\partial_{\mu}\left(\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}\right)+\partial_{a}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2i}\left(\overline{\Psi}\partial^{a}\Psi-\partial^{a}\overline{\Psi}\Psi\right)\gamma^{\nu}\right)=0.

Let us now define the
Riesz tensor

[
25
]

(5.9)

T^{\mu\nu}:=\left(\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}\right)_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}sc}}=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}\,\left(\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}\right),

and a current defined on field configuration space
\mathcal{C}

(5.10)

K^{a\nu}:=\frac{\lambda}{2i}\left[\left(\overline{\Psi}\partial^{a}\Psi-\partial^{a}\overline{\Psi}\Psi\right)\gamma^{\nu}\right]_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}sc}}.

From (
5.8
) we then have

(5.11)

\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}+\partial_{a}K^{a\nu}=0,\qquad\nu=0,\dots,3.

Our proposed guiding equation will be a consequence of the above conservation laws. Note however that (
5.11
) in its current form is still not suitable for our purpose since what we actually need is a positive density that satisfies a continuity equation. The key observation is that (
5.11
) is not just one but
four
independent conservation laws, and we can use these four to construct a single conserved
timelike and future-directed
current, whose time component will give
rise to an
equivariant density,
at least when
\Psi
is a plane wave.

But before we do that, let us show that (
5.11
) can already be used to obtain a guiding law for the covariant field. First we need to recall an important property of the Riesz tensor:

Proposition 5.1
.

T^{\mu\nu}
is a symmetric rank-two tensor and it satisfies the Dominant Energy Condition, i.e.

(i)

T^{\mu}_{\nu}Y^{\nu}
is
future-directed and
causal (i.e. either timelike or null) whenever
Y^{\nu}
is
future-directed and
causal ;

(ii)

T^{\mu\nu}Y_{\mu}Y_{\nu}\geq 0
whenever the vectorfield
\mathbf{Y}
is

time-like
.

Proof.

To see this, we first compute the Riesz tensor (
5.9
) using Kanatchikov’s decomposition (
4.18
). From the definition of the Dirac adjoint (
4.13
), we have

(5.12)

\overline{\Psi}=\gamma^{0}\Psi^{\dagger}\gamma^{0}=\gamma^{0}\left[\psi^{\star}I+\left(\psi_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\right)^{\dagger}\right]\gamma^{0}\\
=\left[\psi^{*}I+\psi_{\mu}^{\ast}\gamma^{\mu}\right].

So the Riesz tensor becomes

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{tr}\left[\overline{\Psi}\gamma^{\mu}\Psi\gamma^{\nu}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(|\psi|^{2}-\psi_{\alpha}^{\ast}\psi^{\alpha}\right)\eta^{\mu\nu}+\left({\psi^{\ast}}^{\mu}\psi^{\nu}+{\psi^{\mu}}{\psi^{*}}^{\nu}\right).

which establishes that
T
is a symmetric rank-2 tensor.
Now, using the polar decompositions in (
4.20
), the above becomes

(5.13)

T^{\mu\nu}=\rho\left[\cos^{2}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\lambda}\right)-\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\lambda}\right)\right]\eta^{\mu\nu}+2\rho\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\zeta}{\lambda}\right)u^{\mu}u^{\nu}.

Recall that
u^{\mu}
is a unit timelike vectorfield,
u_{\mu}u^{\mu}=1
. It follows that
T^{\mu}_{\nu}u^{\nu}=\rho u^{\mu}
, i.e.,
u^{\mu}
is an eigenvector of
T^{\mu\nu}
with eigenvalue
\rho
. Let

denote the projection onto the orthogonal complement of
u^{\mu}
in the tangent space at a point
x
is spacetime. Then

(5.14)

T^{\mu\nu}=\rho u^{\mu}u^{\nu}+\rho\cos\left(2\frac{\zeta}{\lambda}\right)h^{\mu\nu}.

It follows that the other three eigenvalues of
T^{\mu}_{\nu}
are all equal to
\rho\cos\left(2\frac{\zeta}{\lambda}\right)
, with eigenvectors that are orthogonal to
u^{\mu}
.
The conclusion then follows since cosine in absolute value is less than or equal to one.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

In fact we can see that, as a linear transformation,

is square-integrable on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, i.e.

(5.16)

\|\Psi(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left(\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\right)_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}sc}}d^{n}qd^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}<\infty.

In other words
{\mathcal{H}}=C^{0}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\left(\mathbb{R},L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})\right)}}
.

We are now ready to use the conservation law (
5.11
) to construct

a general guiding equation for fields, and an

equivariant density
in the special case of plane-wave solutions of Kanatchikov’s equation, in analogy with how we did so in the case of particles and Schrödinger’s equation (though of course there we didn’t need to assume plane-wave wave functions).
Let
\mathbf{X}
be any fixed, timelike future-directed, covariantly constant vectorfield on Minkowski spacetime, i.e
\nabla_{\mu}X_{\nu}=0
. In subsection
5.5
we will show how to find such an
\mathbf{X}
that is purely determined by the wave function
\Psi
. Let us define the currents

(5.17)

J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}:=T^{\mu}_{\nu}X^{\nu},\qquad K_{\mathbf{X}}^{a}:=K^{a}_{\nu}X^{\nu}.

Consider the vectorfield
\mathbf{Y}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
on field configuration space as

(5.18)

\mathbf{Y}:=J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+K_{\mathbf{X}}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}}.

Later on we will establish that
this vectorfield is divergence free.

Let
\Omega_{0}\subset{\mathcal{N}}
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary in the target space
{\mathcal{N}}
(where the field takes its values.) Consider the
flow

\Phi_{\tau}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}
on the field configuration space generated by the vector field
\mathbf{Y}
:

(5.19)

\Phi_{\tau}(x_{0},q_{0})=(x(\tau),q(\tau))

where
x(\tau),q(\tau)
solve the following ODE initial value problem

(5.20)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{x}^{\mu}(\tau)&=&J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}(x(\tau),q(\tau))\\
\dot{q}^{a}(\tau)&=&K_{\mathbf{X}}^{a}(x(\tau),q(\tau))\end{array}\right.\qquad;\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}x^{\mu}(0)&=&x_{0}^{\mu}\\
q^{a}(0)&=&q_{0}^{a}\end{array}\right..

Furthermore, since
J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}
is timelike and future directed,
J_{\mathbf{X}}^{0}\geq\|J_{\mathbf{X}}^{i}\|
, the above flow is equivalent to the temporal flow
\Phi_{t}:\Sigma_{0}\times{\mathcal{N}}\to\Sigma_{t}\times{\mathcal{N}}
given by the ODE

(5.21)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{ds^{i}}{dt}&=&\frac{J_{\mathbf{X}}^{i}}{J_{\mathbf{X}}^{0}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}(t),q(t))\\[5.0pt]
\frac{dq^{a}}{dt}&=&\frac{K_{\mathbf{X}}^{a}}{J_{\mathbf{X}}^{0}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}(t),q(t))\end{array}\right.\qquad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}s^{i}(0)&=&s_{0}^{i}\\
q^{a}(0)&=&q_{0}^{a}\end{array}\right..

Let us then define the domain
\mathcal{D}
in field configuration space

(5.22)

\mathcal{D}:=\bigcup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Phi_{t}(\Sigma_{0}\times\Omega_{0}).

Its boundary therefore consists of three hypersurfaces

(5.23)

\partial\mathcal{D}=(\Sigma_{0}\times\Omega_{0})\bigcup\Phi_{T}(\Sigma_{0}\times\Omega_{0})\bigcup\left(\cup_{0\leq t\leq T}\Phi_{t}(\Sigma_{0}\times\partial\Omega_{0})\right)=:\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup{\mathcal{L}}

which we suggestively call the “top”, the “bottom”, and the “lateral” part of the boundary.

Hence

(5.24)

0=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mbox{div}\mathbf{Y}=\int_{\partial\mathcal{D}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\partial\mathcal{D}}d\sigma=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{T}}-\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{B}}+\int_{\mathcal{L}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{L}}d\sigma.

Since by definition the flow
\Phi_{t}
is at every point tangential to the lateral surface
{\mathcal{L}}
, the last integral in the above is zero, and we have

(5.25)

\int_{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{T}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{B}}.

Let
\phi:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{N}}
,
x\mapsto q=\phi(x)
be any covariant field beable defined on
{\mathcal{M}}
. Obviously

(5.26)

\frac{dq}{d\tau}=\frac{\partial\phi^{a}}{\partial x^{\mu}}\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau}.

Thus if we want the flow generated by the field beable to be embedded within the flow of the vector field
\mathbf{Y}
, and hence consistent with (
5.25
), by (
5.20
) we need

(5.27)

\boxed{J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}(x,\phi(x))\partial_{\mu}\phi^{a}(x)-K_{\mathbf{X}}^{a}(x,\phi(x))=0}.

We may view the above as a partial differential equation for
\phi(x)
:
Given a solution
\Psi(x,q)
of the Kanatchikov system, the above is a first-order quasilinear PDE for the unknowns
\phi^{a}(x)
. To see this clearly, let us go into a coordinate frame in which
\mathbf{X}=(1,0,\dots,0)
. Denoting the corresponding coordinates by
x=(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})
, the above then becomes

(5.28)

A(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},\phi)\partial_{t}\phi^{a}+B^{j}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},\phi)\partial_{j}\phi^{a}+C^{a}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},\phi)=0,

where

(5.29)

A(x,q)=T^{0}_{0}(x,q),\qquad B^{j}(x,q)=T^{j}_{0}(x,q),\qquad C^{a}(x,q)=-K^{a}_{0}(x,q).

It is well-known that a PDE of the type (
5.28
) is locally solvable using method of characteristics: one thinks of the solution
q^{a}=\phi^{a}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})
as a surface
F^{a}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q)=q^{a}-\phi^{a}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})=0
in the configuration space
\mathcal{C}={\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{N}}
. Then the equation (
5.28
) expresses the fact that the vectorfield
(A,B^{j},C^{a})
is tangential to this surface. If we therefore parametrize this surface by parameters
(\tau,\xi^{j})
and denote differentiation with respect to
\tau
(at constant
\xi
) by a dot, we obtain the system of ODEs along the characteristics:

(5.30)

\dot{t}=A(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q),\qquad\dot{s}^{j}=B^{j}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q),\qquad\dot{q}^{a}=C^{a}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q).

Given initial values for the field
\phi
on the Cauchy surface
t=0
, i.e.
\phi(0,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})=\phi_{0}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})
one can then pose a family of initial value problems for the above ODE system by setting, for each
\xi
,

(5.31)

t(0,\xi)=0,\qquad s^{j}(0,\xi)=\xi^{j},\qquad q^{a}(0,\xi)=\phi^{a}_{0}(\xi).

Evidently, this has a local solution in a neighborhood of the Cauchy surface, thereby determining the evolution of the field
\phi(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})
.
We thus propose (
5.27
) as the guiding law for the field beable.

It is worth emphasizing that, unlike the usual deBroglie-Bohm guiding law of particle mechanics, (
5.27
) is a PDE, not an ODE. In particular, the said characteristics may intersect after a short time (think of Burgers’ equation), causing a shock formation. Thus going beyond the local solvability stated here is a nontrivial task and presumably depends heavily on the choice of the action for the classical field one starts with.

5.4.
The guiding law obeys Einstein locality

We now address whether or not the
evolution equation (
5.27
) paired with the
Kanatchikov
equations implies
Einstein locality for the field beable
\phi
.
First we recall that
(
5.27
), which we remind ourselves is required for equivariance to hold, is a quasilinear PDE, since
J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}\left(x,\phi(x)\right)
depends on
both
x
and
\phi
but no spacetime derivatives of
\phi
.
As a result, (
5.27
) can be equivalently written as a system of ODEs along characteristics, as in (
5.30
).

Moreover, since
J_{\mathbf{X}}^{\mu}\left(x,\phi(x)\right)
is timelike future-directed, these equations also imply that the domain of dependence of a solution

\left(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},\phi(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})\right)
is just the backwards light cone of the spacetime point
(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}})
, and the domain of influence is the forward light cone.
So (
5.27
) yields a locally causal evolution of
\phi
.

To the extent that our theory is Einstein local, it is expected to be physically and empirically inequivalent to textbook quantum field theory and extant versions of de Broglie-Bohm quantum field theory
[
26
]
, both of which violate Einstein locality
[
26
]
,
[
1
]
. In future work, we will address the question of whether our theory can be generalized to violate Einstein locality while preserving Lorentz-covariance at the level of both the wave equation and the guiding equation, and at the same time preserving equivariance of an associated probability distribution.

5.5.
Construction of a distinguished vector field

We now show how to find a vectorfield
\mathbf{X}_{\Psi}
that is constructed out of the wave function
\Psi
, or more precisely, depends only on the initial values of the wave function
\Psi
on the initial hypersurface
\Sigma_{0}
.

The construction of this vector field follows the same procedure as in
[
19
]
.
Let

(5.32)

\tilde{X}^{\mu}(t):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}T^{\mu\nu}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q)n_{\nu}d^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}d^{n}q.

Note that
T^{\mu\nu}
is quadratic in
\Psi
and we have assumed that
\Psi(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
so that the above integral is finite.

Also note that plane-wave solutions of the form (
4.54
) are not integrable in space, so that
\tilde{X}
is not defined for them.

Let us integrate (
5.11
) on
S_{t}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, for fixed
t>0
, and use the divergence theorem:

(5.33)

0=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{S_{t}}\left(\partial_{\mu}T^{\mu\nu}+\partial_{a}K^{a\nu}\right)d^{3}xd^{n}q=\tilde{X}^{\nu}(t)-\tilde{X}^{\nu}(0)+\lim_{R\to\infty}\int\limits_{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}|=R}\int\limits_{|\mathbf{q}|=R}\left(T^{\mu\nu}N_{\mu}+K^{a\nu}N^{\prime}_{a}\right)d\sigma.

In the surface integral term,
N_{\mu}dx^{\mu}+N^{\prime}_{a}dq^{a}
is the unit normal covector to the lateral boundary. This term is zero provided
\Psi
has sufficient decay at infinity. In that case it would
follow
that
\tilde{X}^{\nu}(t)=\tilde{X}^{\nu}(0)
, i.e. that
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}
is a constant vectorfield defined on
{\mathcal{M}}
.

It thus follows (for example using the usual density argument that smooth compactly supported functions are dense in
{\mathcal{H}}
) that the vectorfield
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}
thus constructed is a future-directed, causal, and constant vectorfield everywhere in
{\mathcal{M}}
. Moreover
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}
clearly depends only on the initial values of the wave function, i.e. on
\left.\Psi\right|_{\Sigma_{0}}
. For
generic
initial values, therefore,
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}
will be
timelike
, so that it has a nonzero length, and thus can be normalized in the following way:

(5.34)

X_{\Psi}^{\nu}:=\frac{\tilde{X}_{\Psi}^{\nu}}{\eta(\tilde{X}_{\Psi},\tilde{X}_{\Psi})}.

Note that
\mathbf{X}_{\Psi}
thus defined is not a unit vector. Let
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}
denote the unit vector in the direction of
\mathbf{X}_{\Psi}
.
We now define the currents
\mathbf{J}_{\Psi}
and
\mathbf{K}_{\Psi}
to be

(5.35)

J_{\Psi}^{\mu}:=T^{\mu}_{\nu}X_{\Psi}^{\nu},\qquad K_{\Psi}^{a}:=K^{a}_{\nu}X_{\Psi}^{\nu}.

It follows that
J_{\Psi}^{\mu}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{\mu}}+K_{\Psi}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{a}}
is a conserved current on the field configuration space
\mathcal{C}={\mathcal{M}}\times{\mathcal{N}}
:

(5.36)

\partial_{\mu}J_{\Psi}^{\mu}+\partial_{a}K_{\Psi}^{a}=0.

Under this assumption, (
5.25
) would then imply:

(5.37)

\int_{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{T}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{B}}=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\int_{\Sigma_{0}}T(\mathbf{X}_{\Psi},\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma_{0}})d^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}d^{n}q.

This relation also holds if we replace the general unit vectorfield
\mathbf{n}_{\Sigma_{0}}
by the fixed unit vectorfield
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}
which defines a foliation of hyperplanes orthogonal to it. With this replacement, we may define
the quantity

(5.38)

\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}(x,q):=T(\hat{\mathbf{X}},\hat{\mathbf{X}})=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\|}T(\mathbf{X}_{\Psi},\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}),

where
\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}
is a leaf of the foliation determined by
\mathbf{X}_{\Psi}
and contains the spacetime point
x
. By Prop.
5.1
it follows that

\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}
is non-negative. Furthermore, it follows from (
5.32
) and (
5.34
) that
we have

(5.39)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}(x,q)d^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}d^{n}q=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\int_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}T_{\mu\nu}\hat{X}_{\Psi}^{\nu}\hat{X}_{\Psi}^{\mu}d^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}d^{n}q\right)=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\|}\eta(\tilde{\mathbf{X}},\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{X}\|}<\infty,

so long as
\mathbf{X}
is strictly timelike. Thus for all
x\in\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}
,
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}(x,\cdot)
is a finite measure on the space
{\mathcal{N}}
of generic field values at
x
.

Equation (
5.37
) now implies that

(5.40)

\int_{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{Y}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\mathcal{T}}=\|\mathbf{X}\|\int_{\Phi_{T}(\Sigma_{0}\times\Omega_{0})}\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}(t,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q)d^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}d^{n}q=\|\mathbf{X}\|\int_{\Omega_{0}}\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}(0,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}},q)d^{3}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{s}}d^{n}q.

There remains the question of how
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}
is related to the
\rho
that appears in (
3.27
) and subsequently throughout section 4. As we saw, the
\rho
that appears there admits an interpretation as a
parameter-dependent
probability density function on
{\mathcal{N}}

when we restrict the Kanatchikov system to the special case of plane-waves
(i.e. (
4.54
), with the parameter being
\sigma=n\cdot x
)
. By contrast,
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}
is
an
x
-dependent finite measure on
{\mathcal{N}}
in the general case, so long as
\Psi
is square integrable on
\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}\times{\mathcal{N}}
(which excludes plane waves.)
In fact there exists a more general relationship between
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}
and
\rho
, namely

Proposition 5.3
.

With the above definitions, we have

\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}\geq\rho,

with the equality achieved (in a limiting sense) whenever the underlying wavefunction
\Psi
is a plane-wave solution of Kanatchikov’s equation.

Proof.

To see this, first
notice that the Riesz tensor (
5.14
) has the form of the energy-momentum
tensor of a relativistic perfect fluid, where the term proportional to
h^{\mu\nu}

is the pressure, the term proportional to
u^{\mu}u^{\nu}
is the energy density, and
u^{\mu}
plays the role of the fluid 4-velocity.
Expectedly, if we pick the frame in which
u^{\mu}=\left(1,0,0,0\right)
, it follows that

(5.41)

T^{00}=\rho.

The general relationship between
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}
and
\rho
is found by using (
5.13
) in (
5.38
) to obtain

(5.42)

\displaystyle\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}=T^{\mu\nu}\hat{X}_{\mu}\hat{X}_{\nu}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}=\rho\left(\left[1-\cos\frac{2\zeta}{\lambda}\right]\eta(\mathbf{u},\hat{X})^{2}+\cos\frac{2\zeta}{\lambda}\right)\geq\rho.}}

The last inequality is due to the reverse Cauchy–Schwarz inequality that holds between timelike vectors in Minkowski space:
\eta(v,w)^{2}\geq\eta(v,v)\eta(w,w)
.

Furthermore, we can see that the inequality in (
5.42
) becomes an equality if
\mathbf{u}\equiv\hat{\mathbf{X}}
, which is a constant unit vector, which implies that
\Psi
was a plane-wave. In other words,

the inequality
in (
5.42
)
is
in some sense
saturated
for plane-waves.

To make that more precise, let
\Psi
be a plane-wave solution of Kanatchikov’s equation, and let
\zeta,\rho,u
be as in (
4.46
), so that
u^{\mu}
is a constant unit timelike vector. Let
\Sigma_{\sigma}:=\{x\in{\mathcal{M}}\ |\ \mathbf{u}\cdot x=\sigma\}
denote the foliation by hyperplanes in
{\mathcal{M}}
that is dual to
\mathbf{u}
. Thus for fixed
q\in{\mathcal{N}}
,
\rho(\cdot,q)
is a constant on each leaf
\Sigma_{\sigma}
of the foliation. Integrating (
4.48
) on
{\mathcal{N}}
and using the divergence theorem, we obtain that

(5.43)

\partial_{\sigma}\int_{\mathcal{N}}g(\sigma,q)dq=0.

As a consequence, for fixed
q
,
\rho(\cdot,q)
is constant everywhere on
{\mathcal{M}}
, and therefore it can be normalized so that

(5.44)

\int_{\mathcal{N}}\rho(x,q)dq=1\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{M}}.

Now, if we cut off the initial data corresponding to this
\Psi
outside a large ball
B_{R}(0)\subset\Sigma_{0}
, so that it becomes square integrable in space, and solve Kanatchikov’s equation with the cut-off data, we obtain a solution
\tilde{\Psi}
that coincides with
\Psi
inside
D_{R}\times{\mathcal{N}}
, the domain of influence of
B_{R}(0)\times{\mathcal{N}}
, but will be square integrable in space, and thus it would have a well-defined vectorfield
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}_{\tilde{\Psi}}
and a corresponding
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}
, such that
\hat{\mathbf{X}}\equiv\mathbf{u}
in
D_{R}\times{\mathcal{N}}
. We would then have that, inside
D_{R}\times{\mathcal{N}}
,

(5.45)

\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}^{\phantom{X}}=T^{\mu\nu}\hat{X}_{\mu}\hat{X}_{\nu}=T^{\mu\nu}u_{\mu}u_{\nu}=\rho,

so that equality is achieved in (
5.42
).
The claims then follow when we take the cut-off away by letting
R\to\infty
.

∎

To complete the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we define the
x
-dependent measure
\varrho_{x}
in the statement of the Theorem to be
\varrho_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}}
where
\Sigma_{\mathbf{X}}
is the leaf of the foliation generated by the vectorfield
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{X}_{\Psi}
that includes the spacetime point
x
.

We can go one step further and ask, when
\Psi
is a plane-wave, what does the proposed guiding equation (
5.27
) reduce to? To answer this question, let us fix a unit timelike direction
u^{\mu}
and let
(f,g)
be a solution of (
4.47
-
4.48
). Let
\zeta,\rho,u
be as in (
4.46
), and let
\Psi
be defined via (
4.20
).
As we argued in the above, by introducing a cut-off we can arrange it that
\hat{\mathbf{X}}_{\Psi}=\mathbf{u}
inside a large region in space.
Let us go into a frame where
\mathbf{u}=(1,0,0,0)
. We have already seen that
T^{0}_{0}=\rho
. It also follows that
T^{j}_{0}=0
for
j=1,2,3
. We also need to calculate
K_{0}^{a}
. We have
\Psi=\sqrt{g}e^{if\mathbf{u}\cdot\gamma/\lambda}
, and plugging this into (
5.10
) we get

(5.46)

K_{\nu}^{a}(x,q)=\sqrt{g(\mathbf{u}\cdot x,q)}\partial^{a}f(\mathbf{u}\cdot x,q)\left((\mathbf{u}\cdot\gamma)\gamma_{\nu}\right)_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}sc}},

so that
K_{0}^{a}=\sqrt{g}\partial^{a}f
. The guiding law (
5.27
) now reads

\sqrt{g}\partial_{t}\phi^{a}-\sqrt{g}\partial^{a}f(t,\phi)=0.
In other words,

(5.47)

\partial_{t}\phi^{a}=\partial^{a}f(t,\phi),

which with hindsight is quite natural and easy to guess from the form of the continuity equation (
4.48
) for plane-wave solutions of Kanatchikov’s equation.

Once
\Psi
is not a single plane wave, but a superposition of them, since the proposed guiding law is nonlinear (note that the tensors
T^{\mu\nu}
and
K^{a}_{\nu}
are quadratic in
\Psi
) the interaction of the different modes will contribute to the guiding of the field
\phi
, as was already the case for the deBroglie-Bohm guiding law (
2.25
). In this
limited
sense our guiding equation is an
analogue
of (
2.25
)
for
covariant fields.

6.
Summary and Outlook

In this article we have shown that
, in the classical limit,
plane wave solutions of Kanatchikov’s relativistic generalization of Schrödinger’s equation

satisfy the same equations as
plane wave solutions
of the DeDonder–Weyl–Christodoulou Hamilton–Jacobi formulation of classical covariant field theory, and that they obey Christodoulou’s integrability condition.
As far as we know, previous investigations did not pay attention to this constraint.
We also formulated a
local
guiding law for covariant fields that evolves the actual fields by a solution of Kanatchikov’s equation, and we proved the equivariance of
the associated
evolving measure,

when that solution is a plane wave.

In future work we hope to extend the class of admissible solutions beyond plane wave category, and to address the issue of the apparent lack of Bell-type nonlocality for our guiding law.

Of particular interest is also to consider fields not defined on spacetime but
taking values in
spacetime. Covariant guiding laws for such fields could be used to develop a de Broglie–Bohm type dynamical theory for
non-point-like
objects, e.g. ring-like particles
[
18
]
, or strings evolving in spacetime
[
26
]
,
[
27
]
. This aspect will be explored elsewhere.
