On the proportions of soluble forms in some families of locally soluble binary quartic forms
Yasuhiro Ishitsuka, Yoshinori Kanamura

Abstract
. An integral binary quartic form is said to be locally soluble (resp. soluble) if the corresponding genus one curve has a rational point over ℚ v \mathbb{Q}_{v} for every place v v of ℚ \mathbb{Q} (resp. over ℚ \mathbb{Q} ). We consider the proportion of soluble integral binary quartic forms in locally soluble forms. Bhargava showed the proportion is positive when one considers all binary quartics, and Bhargava–Ho proved the proportion is zero for a subfamily. In this paper, we estimate the proportions for some other subfamilies. It relies on results for elliptic curves y 2 = x 3 − n 2 ​ x y^{2}=x^{3}-n^{2}x by Heath-Brown, Xiong–Zaharescu and Smith.

1.
Introduction

Let
f(x,y)
be a binary quartic form over
\mathbb{Q}
.
We call the curve
C_{f}\colon z^{2}=f(x,y)

locally soluble
if
C_{f}(\mathbb{Q}_{v})\neq\emptyset
for all places
v
of
\mathbb{Q}
.
We also call the curve
C_{f}

soluble
if
C_{f}(\mathbb{Q})\neq\emptyset
.
In what follows,
we also call
f(x,y)
locally soluble (resp. soluble) when
C_{f}
is locally soluble (resp. soluble).
For an integral binary quartic form
f
,
the naive height means the maximum of absolute values of the coefficients of
f
.
In 2014,
Bhargava showed the following interesting result.

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
[
Bhargava2014
, Theorem 2]
)
.

When locally soluble integral binary quartics
f(x,y)
are ordered by the naive height, the proportion of soluble forms is positive.

In addition to this theorem,
Bhargava conjectured the exact value of the proportion; for details, see
[
Bhargava2014
, Section 1.3]
.
Note that the proportion of locally soluble forms in integral binary quartic forms is already determined by Bhargava–Cremona–Fisher
[
BCF2021
, Theorem 3]
.
Hence, Bhargava also conjectured the exact value of the proportion of soluble forms in integral binary quartics ordered by the naive height.

Recently,
Bhargava–Ho
[
Bhargava-Ho2022
]
showed an analogue theorem on a subfamily of binary quartics.
Before we state their result,
we introduce the
Bhargava–Ho height
.
For an integral binary quartic
f=ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}
, we define the Bhargava–Ho height
H_{\text{BH}}
as

\displaystyle H_{\text{BH}}(f)=\max\{B^{2},\left\lvert ac\right\rvert\}.

Then, Bhargava–Ho’s result is the following.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
Bhargava-Ho2022
, Theorem 1.6]
)
.

When locally soluble integral binary quartic forms
ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}
are ordered by the Bhargava–Ho height,
the proportion of soluble forms is
0\%
.

Comparing Bhargava’s and Bhargava–Ho’s results,
we find that the proportions are totally different in the two cases.
In this paper,
we examine the proportion of soluble forms in some other subfamilies.
Especially, we find some subfamilies whose proportions of soluble forms are totally different.

For an integer
B
and
M
,
we write
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})
for the set of binary quartic forms
f(x,y)=ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}
with
a,c\in\mathbb{Z}
and
ac=M
.
We also define

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}=\{ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})\mid\text{$0\leq\ord_{p}a\leq 1$ for all places $v$ of $\mathbb{Q}$}\}.

In what follows,
we abbreviate squarefree as “sqf.”.

Theorem 1.3

(see
theorem

4.2
)
.

When locally soluble forms
f\in\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}^{0}_{4n^{2}}
are ordered by the Bhargava–Ho height,
the proportion of soluble forms is
100\%
.

By slightly changing the condition on the coefficients,
we obtain the exact opposite result.

Theorem 1.4

(see
theorem

5.5
)
.

When locally soluble forms
f\in\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-n^{2}}
are ordered by the Bhargava–Ho height, the proportion of the soluble forms is
0\%
.

Note that we also obtain similar theorems for four slightly different subfamilies (see
theorem

4.3
and
theorem

5.6
).

Bhargava’s and Bhargava–Ho’s results are deeply related to the average size of Selmer groups in families of elliptic curves.
For example, to prove
theorem

1.1
, we first interpret the locally soluble binary quartics as an element of 2-Selmer group of an elliptic curve. Then
theorem

1.1
is obtained by using the average size of
2
-Selmer groups in all elliptic curves.
Similarly, to prove our results,
theorems

1.3
and
1.4
, we interpret locally soluble binary quartic forms in
\mathcal{F}_{M}^{B}
as
elements of
2
-isogeny or its dual isogeny Selmer groups in the family of elliptic curves
E_{n}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}-n^{2}x
where
n
runs over all squarefree positive integers.
Then the results follow from the average sizes of those Selmer groups.
The average sizes of these Selmer groups in the family have been studied a lot,
for example
[
Aoki
,
Feng1996
,
Feng-Xiong2004
,
Feng-Xue
,
Heath-Brown1993
,
Heath-Brown1994
,
Lozano-Robledo
,
Vrecica
,
Xiong-Zaharescu2009
,
Yu2005
,
Yu2006
]
.
In particular,
our results are deeply relied on the results on
[
Heath-Brown1993
,
Xiong-Zaharescu2009
]
.

Key facts to prove
theorems

1.3
and
1.4
are
the difference in the order between the number of locally soluble quartics and the number of soluble quartics.
Especially, in the case of
theorem

1.4
,
there exist much more locally soluble quartics than soluble quartics.
Hence, we next consider whether there are subsets of locally soluble forms comparable to soluble ones in the sense of order.
To answer this problem,
we introduce the condition which is called
strictly locally soluble
.

Let
n
be a squarefree integer.
A curve
C_{f}\colon z^{2}=f(x,y)
where
f\in\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-n^{2}}
is called strictly locally soluble if the curve “comes from”
2
-Selmer groups of
E_{n}
.
We will explain the meaning of “comes from” explicitly in Section
6
.
In a similar manner to a locally soluble quartic,
we call a binary quartic form
f(x,y)
strictly locally soluble when
C_{f}
is strictly locally soluble.
Then,
we answer the above problem of comparable subsets of locally soluble quartics, which is our last theorem.

Theorem 1.5

(see
theorem

6.1
)
.

The proportion of soluble forms is greater than
42\%
when strictly locally soluble forms
f\in\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\geq 1\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-n^{2}}
are ordered by the Bhargava–Ho height.

To prove this theorem,
we combine Heath-Brown’s result
[
Heath-Brown1993
]
and Browning’s discussion
[
Browning2017
, Proof of Theorem 1.3]
(cf.
[
Bhargava2014
, Proof of Theorems
1
and
2
]
).
Actually, we can show easily that the proportion appeared in
theorem

1.5
is strictly positive.
This is because we can immediately find soluble elements, for example
x^{4}-n^{2}y^{4},-x^{4}+n^{2}y^{4},nx^{4}-ny^{4},-nx^{4}+ny^{4}\in\mathcal{F}^{0}_{-n^{2}}
.
These elements correspond to
2
-torsions of
E_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
.
Our theorem states that there are still many soluble elements even if we remove the soluble elements corresponding to
2
-torsion points in
E_{n}(\mathbb{Q})
.
To prove this theorem,
Smith’s result
[
Smith
]
on the proportion of
E_{n}
with positive ranks plays an important role.

2.
Setting

For an integer
B
and
M
,
we set

\displaystyle W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})

\displaystyle=\{f(x,y)=ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}\mid a,c\in\mathbb{Q},ac=M\},

\displaystyle W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})

\displaystyle=\{f(x,y)=ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}\mid a,c\in\mathbb{Z},ac=M\}.

The discriminants and Bhargava–Ho height of
f\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
is determined by
B,M
since

(2.1)

\displaystyle\Disc(f)

\displaystyle=16M(B^{2}-4M)^{2},

(2.2)

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{BH}}(f)

\displaystyle=\max\{B^{2},|M|\}.

In this paper, we mainly consider the
nondegenerate
quartics, i.e., the quartics with
\Disc(f)\neq 0
.
By the above description, the nondegeneracy of
f\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
only depends on
B
and
M
.

A nondegenerate quartic
f(x,y)\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
defines
a genus one curve
C_{f}\colon z^{2}=f(x,y)
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
We write the subsets of
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
of locally soluble (resp. soluble) binary quartic forms as
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\text{ls}}
(resp.
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\text{sol}}
). In concrete terms,

\displaystyle W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\text{ls}}

\displaystyle=\{f\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})\mid\text{$C_{f}(\mathbb{Q}_{v})\neq\emptyset$ for all place $v$ of $\mathbb{Q}$}\},

\displaystyle W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\text{sol}}

\displaystyle=\{f\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})\mid\text{$C_{f}(\mathbb{Q})\neq\emptyset$}\}.

Similarly we define for
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})^{\text{ls}}
and
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})^{\text{sol}}
.

We define an equivalence relation

\displaystyle ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}\sim a^{\prime}x^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+c^{\prime}y^{4}

on
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
if
a^{\prime}=s^{2}a
and
c^{\prime}=s^{-2}c
for some
s\in\mathbb{Q}^{*}
.
This equivalence preserves solubility and local solubility.
We write
[f]
as an equivalent class of
f\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
.
We set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})=\{ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})\mid\text{$0\leq\ord_{p}a\leq 1$ for all places $v$ in $\mathbb{Q}$}\}.

Then,
we have
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}=\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})\cap W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})
. The set

\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
(resp.
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}
) is a complete system of representatives of the equivalence classes in
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
(resp.
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})
).
We also set

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{B,\text{sol}}_{M}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}\cap W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})^{\text{sol}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{B,\text{ls}}_{M}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}\cap W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Z})^{\text{ls}}.

As we mentioned in the introduction,
we consider the elliptic curves
E_{n}
and over
\mathbb{Q}
defined as

\displaystyle E_{n}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}-n^{2}x

for an integer
n>0
.
We write the point of infinity of
E_{n}
as
\infty
.
The curves
E_{n}
have three types of isogenies:

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle\colon E_{n}\to E_{1,n}\colon y^{2}=x^{3}+4n^{2}x,

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}},-\frac{y(n^{2}+x^{2})}{x^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle\colon E_{n}\to E_{2,n}\colon y^{2}=x(x^{2}-6nx+n^{2}),

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\frac{y^{2}}{(x+n)^{2}},\frac{y(2n^{2}-(x+n)^{2})}{(x+n)^{2}}\right),

\displaystyle\varphi_{3}

\displaystyle\colon E_{n}\to E_{3,n}\colon y^{2}=x(x^{2}+6nx+n^{2}),

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\frac{y^{2}}{(x-n)^{2}},\frac{y(2n^{2}-(x-n)^{2})}{(x-n)^{2}}\right).

For
i=1,2,3
, we write the dual isogenies of
\varphi_{i}
as
\widehat{\varphi_{i}}
.
We write the Selmer group and weak Mordell–Weil group corresponding to the isogeny
\varphi_{i}

as
\mathrm{Sel}_{\varphi_{i}}(E_{n})
and
E_{i,n}(\mathbb{Q})/\varphi_{i}(E_{n}(\mathbb{Q}))
and similar for
\widehat{\varphi_{i}}
.
The relation between these isogenies and the set
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
is summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1

(cf.
[
SilvermanAEC
, Proposition X.4.9]
)
.

For any integer
n\neq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},E_{n}[\varphi_{1}])\cong W^{0}_{4n^{2}}(\mathbb{Q})/{\sim},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[dx^{4}+\frac{4n^{2}}{d}y^{4}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},E_{n}[\varphi_{2}])\cong W^{-6n}_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{Q})/{\sim},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[dx^{4}-6nx^{2}y^{2}+(n^{2}/d)y^{4}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},E_{n}[\varphi_{3}])\cong W^{6n}_{n^{2}}(\mathbb{Q})/{\sim},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[dx^{4}+6nx^{2}y^{2}+(n^{2}/d)y^{4}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},E_{1,n}[\widehat{\varphi_{1}}])\cong W^{0}_{-n^{2}}(\mathbb{Q})/{\sim},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[dx^{4}-\frac{n^{2}}{d}y^{4}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},E_{2,n}[\widehat{\varphi_{2}}])\cong W^{3n}_{2n^{2}}(\mathbb{Q})/{\sim},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[dx^{4}+3nx^{2}y^{2}+2(n^{2}/d)y^{4}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{Q}^{*}/\mathbb{Q}^{*2}\cong H^{1}(\mathbb{Q},E_{3,n}[\widehat{\varphi_{3}}])\cong W^{-3n}_{2n^{2}}(\mathbb{Q})/{\sim},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle\mapsto\left[dx^{4}-3nx^{2}y^{2}+2(n^{2}/d)y^{4}\right].

In each case, the set of equivalence classes of locally soluble quartics (resp. soluble quartics) corresponds to the Selmer groups (resp. weak Mordell–Weil groups).

For the quartics in one of six sets appearing in
lemma

2.1
,
the discriminant and Bhargava–Ho height of the quartics only depends on
n
by (
2.1
) and (
2.2
).
In the followings, we mainly consider those quartics with
n\neq 0
.
In particular, we do not consider degenerate quartics.

3.
integrality lemma

In this section,
we give an integrality lemma analogous to
[
Browning2017
, Lemma 3.2]
. It states that locally soluble quartics in
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
is equivalent to integral ones.
It is presumably well-known to experts; for example,
see
[
SilvermanAEC
, Proposition X.4.9]
for treatments except
p=2
.
We include them for reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.1
.

Fix integers
B,M\in\mathbb{Z}
with
M(B^{2}-4M)\neq 0
.
Any equivalence class of
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\text{ls}}
contains a unique element of
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}
.

Proof.

The uniqueness follows from the fact that
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
contains a unique element in each equivalence class of
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\text{ls}}
.
We have to show that for each equivalence class
[f]
of
W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
, the unique element in
[f]\cap\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
has integral coefficients.

Take a locally soluble quartic
f(x,y)=ax^{4}+Bx^{2}y^{2}+cy^{4}\in W^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})^{\mathrm{ls}}
.
Assume that
f\in\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}(\mathbb{Q})
, or equivalently, that
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0\leq\ord_{p}a\leq 1
for any prime
p
.
Since
a,B\in\mathbb{Z}
, we only have to show that
c\in\mathbb{Z}
. It is enough to prove
\ord_{p}c\geq 0
for any prime
p
.

For a prime
p
dividing
M
, we have
\ord_{p}c\geq 0
since
ac=M
.
Thus, we may assume that
p\nmid M
.
Moreover, since
ac=M\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\ord_{p}a\leq 1
, we only have to consider the case when
(\ord_{p}a,\ord_{p}c)=(1,-1)
.

Since
f
is locally soluble, we may take a nontrivial solution
(Z,X,Y)
of
z^{2}=f(x,y)
in
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
.
By scaling, we may assume
X,Y,Z\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Then, the followings hold:

•

The two values
\ord_{p}(aX^{4}),\ord_{p}(cY^{4})
are odd and distinct because

\displaystyle\text{$\ord_{p}(aX^{4})\equiv 1\bmod{4}$ and $\ord_{p}(cY^{4})\equiv-1\bmod{4}$}.

•

We would like to compare
\ord_{p}(nX^{2}Y^{2})
with
\ord_{p}(aX^{4})
and
\ord_{p}(cY^{4})
. Since
p\nmid M
, we compute

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(aX^{4})+\ord_{p}(cY^{4})

\displaystyle=\ord_{p}(acX^{4}Y^{4})

\displaystyle=\ord_{p}(M)+\ord_{p}(X^{4}Y^{4})

\displaystyle=\ord_{p}(X^{4}Y^{4})

\displaystyle=2\ord_{p}(X^{2}Y^{2}).

Thus, we obtain
(\ord_{p}(aX^{4})+\ord_{p}(cY^{4}))/2=\ord_{p}(X^{2}Y^{2})
.

Combining the above two observations, we have

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(aX^{4})>\ord_{p}(X^{2}Y^{2})>\ord_{p}(cY^{4}),

or

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(aX^{4})<\ord_{p}(X^{2}Y^{2})<\ord_{p}(cY^{4}).

It is enough to consider the former case.
Since
B\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have two inequalities;

\displaystyle\text{$\ord_{p}(aX^{4})>\ord_{p}(cY^{4})$ and $\ord_{p}(BX^{2}Y^{2})>\ord_{p}(cY^{4})$}.

These conclude

\displaystyle\ord_{p}(aX^{4}+BX^{2}Y^{2}+cY^{4})=\ord_{p}(cY^{4}),

and the value is odd.
However, it contradicts to
Z^{2}=aX^{4}+BX^{2}Y^{2}+cY^{4}
since
\ord_{p}(Z^{2})
is even.
∎

Note that we can extend this lemma when
M\neq 0
, but we only consider nondegenerate quartics here.

Applying
lemma

3.1
to six cases in
lemma

2.1
, we see that each element in Selmer groups of the isogenies
\varphi_{i},\widehat{\varphi_{i}}
has a unique representative of binary quartics in
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}
for appropriate
B,M
.
Thus to count integral locally soluble forms in those
\mathcal{F}^{B}_{M}
exactly corresponds to
count elements of corresponding Selmer groups.

4.
Proof of
theorem

Now we prove

.
For

\displaystyle S(X,h)=\{D\in\mathbb{Z}\mid D\equiv h\bmod{8},\text{$1\leq D\leq X$, $D$ : squarefree}\}.

The number of elements is estimated as

\displaystyle\#S(X,h)

Lemma 4.1

(
[

)
.

For

,
one has

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in S(X,h)}\#\mathrm{Sel}_{{\varphi_{i}}}(E_{n})

\displaystyle=\#S(X,h)+o(X)

as
X\to\infty
.
In particular, the contribution of nontrivial elements in
\mathrm{Sel}_{{\varphi_{i}}}(E_{n})
is estimated as
o(X)
.

Proof.

For
h=\pm 1,\pm 3
, see the proof of
[
Xiong-Zaharescu2009
, Theorem 6]
.

In the following, we consider the case
h=\pm 2
and
i=1
.
Suppose
p\neq 2
.
The conditions on
n,d
for the existence of
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-rational points on
C_{1,d}
is given by
[
Feng-Xiong2004
, Lemma 3.1 (1)-(3)]
.
As the case
h=\pm 1,\pm 3
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in S(X,h)}\#\mathrm{Sel}_{\varphi_{1}}(E_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=dd^{\prime}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid d\\
p\neq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{4}\left(\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right)+1\right)\left(\left(\frac{d^{\prime}}{p}\right)+1\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid d^{\prime}\\
p\neq 2\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{d}{p}\right)+1\right)

\displaystyle=\#S(X,h)+o(X).

as
X\to\infty
.
Here,
\left(\frac{\cdot}{\cdot}\right)
is the Legendre symbol.
Moreover,
we obtain

\displaystyle\#S(X,h)\leq\sum_{n\in S(X,h)}\#\mathrm{Sel}_{\varphi_{1}}(E_{n})

by counting the trivial element of
\mathrm{Sel}_{\varphi_{1}}(E_{n})
.
Hence,
we complete the proof.

In a similar manner,
we also obtain the same arguments when we consider the other cases
h=\pm 2
and
i=2,3
.
∎

Now, we will prove
theorem

1.3
.
We can rewrite
theorem

1.3
explicitly as

(4.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\#\{f\in\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{sol}}_{4n^{2}}\mid\text{$n$: sqf.\ and $H_{\text{BH}}(f)=4n^{2}<X$}\}}{\#\{f\in\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{ls}}_{4n^{2}}\mid\text{$n$: sqf.\ and $H_{\text{BH}}(f)=4n^{2}<X$}\}}=1.

The left hand side quantity is equal to

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n<\sqrt{X}/2\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\#\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{sol}}_{4n^{2}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n<\sqrt{X}/2\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\#\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{ls}}_{4n^{2}}},

so that by writing
X
instead of
\sqrt{X}/2
,
we only have to show the following theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\dfrac{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n<X\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\#\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{sol}}_{4n^{2}}}{\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n<X\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}\#\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{ls}}_{4n^{2}}}=1

Proof.

Using
lemma

3.1
, we can freely interpret elements of Selmer group
\mathrm{Sel}_{\varphi_{1}}(E_{n})
as elements in
\mathcal{F}^{0,\text{ls}}_{4n^{2}}
under the bijection in
lemma

2.1
.

In
lemma

4.1
,
the main term
\#S(X,h)
comes from identity elements of Selmer groups
\mathrm{Sel}_{\varphi_{1}}(E_{n})
, and the other elements are negligible.

\displaystyle=\#E_{n}(\mathbb{Q})[2]\times 2^{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}A/\widehat{\varphi}(A_{1})}

,

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n<X\\
\text{$n$: sqf.}\end{subarray}}4\times(1+{\dim_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}A/\widehat{\varphi_{1}}(A_{1})})

hold.

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<n<X\\

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4(6+0.559\times 3)}{\pi^{2}}X+o(X)
