Multiplicative independence in the sequence of k − k- generalized Lucas numbers
Herbert Batte, Mahadi Ddamulira, Juma Kasozi, Florian Luca

Abstract
Let ( L n ( k ) ) n ≥ 2 − k (L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\geq 2-k} be the sequence of k − k- generalized Lucas numbers for some fixed integer k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , whose first k k terms are 0 , … , 0 , 2 , 1 0,\;\ldots\;,\;0,\;2,\;1 and each term afterward is the sum of the preceding k k terms. In this paper, we find all pairs of the k − k- generalized Lucas numbers that are multiplicatively dependent.



Keywords and phrases
:
k-
generalized Lucas numbers; multiplicatively independent integers; linear forms in logarithms.

Introduction

1.1
Background

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer. The sequence of
k
-generalized Fibonacci numbers
(F_{n}^{(k)})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is the sequence given by

F_{n}^{(k)}=F_{n-1}^{(k)}+\cdots+F_{n-k}^{(k)}

with
F_{1}^{(k)}=1
and
F_{i}^{(k)}=0
for
i=2-k,3-k,\ldots,0
. If one keeps the same recurrence relation (every term is the sum of the previous
k
terms) but instead of the initial values being

0,0,\ldots,0,0,1
one starts with
0,0,\ldots,0,2,1
, then one obtains the sequence of
k-
generalized Lucas numbers. When
k=2
, these coincide with the classical sequences of Fibonacci and Lucas numbers.
If
a,~b
are nonzero integers then we say that they are multiplicatively independent if the only solution in integers
x,y
of the equation
a^{x}b^{y}=1
is
x=y=0
and multiplicatively dependent otherwise. Note that any pair of integers such that one of them is
1
is a pair of multiplicatively dependent integers.

In
[
10
]
, Gómez and Luca fixed
k\geq 2
and looked at the instances
n>m\geq 1
for which
F_{n}^{(k)}
and
F_{m}^{(k)}
are multiplicatively dependent. This is certainly so if
m\in\{1,2\}
since

F_{1}^{(k)}=F_{2}^{(k)}=1
. In addition, since
F_{m}^{(k)}=2^{m-2}
for all
m\in[2,k+1]
, it follows that
F_{n}^{(k)}
and
F_{m}^{(k)}
are multiplicatively dependent when
3\leq m<n\leq k+1
. The main result of

[
10
]
is that these are the only instances when
F_{n}^{(k)}
and
F_{m}^{(k)}
are multiplicatively dependent when
k\geq 3
. When
k=2
, there is the additional instance
(m,n)=(3,6)
as
F_{3}^{(2)}=2
and
F_{6}^{(2)}=2^{3}
. The instance
k=2
can be easily treated with Carmichael’s primitive divisor theorem
[
6
]
which asserts that
F_{n}^{(2)}
has a primitive prime factor (a prime factor
p
which does not divide

F_{m}^{(2)}
for any positive integer
m<n
) provided
n>12
. Then an immediate verification of the cases
3\leq m\leq n\leq 12
gives the solution
(m,n)=(3,6)
.

Similar results concerning the multiplicative dependence between terms of other linear recurrence sequences can be seen in
[
9
]
and
[
2
]
. In this paper, we investigate the multiplicative dependence between
L_{m}^{(k)}
and
L_{n}^{(k)}
for
n>m
. We assume again that
n\neq 1,~m\neq 1
as
L_{1}^{(k)}=1
. We prove the following result.

1.2
Main Result

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
(L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
be the sequence of
k
–generalized Lucas numbers. If
n>m\geq 0
are integers with
n\neq 1,~m\neq 1
such that
L_{n}^{(k)}
and
L_{m}^{(k)}
are multiplicatively dependent then
(k,m,n)=(2,0,3),~(3,0,7)
.

Methods

2.1
Preliminaries

The following is a well known identity

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=3\cdot 2^{n-2},\qquad\text{for all}\qquad 2\leq n\leq k.

(2.1)

It can be easily proved by induction. The characteristic polynomial of
(L_{n}^{(k)})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is given by

\Psi_{k}(x)=x^{k}-x^{k-1}-\cdots-x-1.

This polynomial is irreducible in
\mathbb{Q}[x]
. The polynomial
\Psi_{k}(x)
possesses a unique real root
\alpha:=\alpha(k)>1
, see
[
15
]
. All the other roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
are inside the unit circle, see
[
16
]
. The particular root
\alpha
can be found in the interval

\displaystyle 2(1-2^{-k})<\alpha<2,\qquad\text{for}\qquad k\geq 2,

(2.2)

as noted in
[
20
]
. As in the classical case when
k=2
, it was shown in
[
5
]
that

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}\leq L_{n}^{(k)}\leq 2\alpha^{n},\qquad\text{holds for all}\qquad n\geq 1,\quad k\geq 2.

(2.3)

For
k\geq 2
we define

\displaystyle f_{k}(x):=\dfrac{x-1}{2+(k+1)(x-2)}.

If
2(1-2^{-k})<x<2
, then
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}f_{k}(x)<0
and so inequality (
2.2
) implies that

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}=f_{k}(2)<f_{k}(\alpha)<f_{k}(2(1-2^{-k}))\leq\dfrac{3}{4},

(2.4)

holds for all
k\geq 3
. The inequality
1/2=f_{k}(2)<f_{k}(\alpha)<3/4
also holds for
k=2
. In addition, it is easy to verify that
|f_{k}(\alpha_{i})|<1
holds for all
2\leq i\leq k
, where
\alpha_{i}
for
i=2,\ldots,k
are the roots of
\Psi_{k}(x)
inside the unit disk.
Lastly, it was shown in
[
5
]
that

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(k)}=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(2\alpha_{i}-1)f_{k}(\alpha_{i})\alpha_{i}^{n-1}~~\text{and}~~\left|L_{n}^{(k)}-f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}\right|<\dfrac{3}{2},

(2.5)

hold for all
k\geq 2
and
n\geq 2-k
. This means that
L_{n}^{(k)}=f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}+e_{k}(n)
, where
|e_{k}(n)|<1.5
. The first expression in (
2.5
) is known as the Binet-like formula for
L_{n}^{(k)}
. Furthermore, the second inequality expression in (
2.5
) shows that the contribution of the zeros that are inside the unit circle to
L_{n}^{(k)}
is small.

2.2
Linear forms in logarithms

We use Baker-type lower bounds for nonzero linear forms in logarithms of algebraic numbers. There are many such bounds mentioned in the literature but we use one of Matveev from
[
14
]
. Before we can formulate such inequalities we need the notion of height of an algebraic number recalled below.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\gamma
be an algebraic number of degree
d
with minimal primitive polynomial over the integers

a_{0}x^{d}+a_{1}x^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}=a_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x-\gamma^{(i)}),

where the leading coefficient
a_{0}
is positive. Then, the logarithmic height of
\gamma
is given by

h(\gamma):=\dfrac{1}{d}\Big(\log a_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\max\{|\gamma^{(i)}|,1\}\Big).

In particular, if
\gamma
is a rational number represented as
\gamma=p/q
with coprime integers
p
and
q\geq 1
, then
h(\gamma)=\log\max\{|p|,q\}
.
The following properties of the logarithmic height function
h(\cdot)
will be used in the rest of the paper without further reference:

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\pm\gamma_{2})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2})+\log 2;

\displaystyle h(\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}^{\pm 1})

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma_{1})+h(\gamma_{2});

\displaystyle h(\gamma^{s})

\displaystyle=|s|h(\gamma)\quad{\text{\rm valid for}}\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

With these properties, it was easily computed in Section 3 of
[
10
]
that

\displaystyle h\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)<2\log k,\qquad\text{for all}\qquad k\geq 2.

(2.6)

A linear form in logarithms is an expression of the form

\Lambda:=b_{1}\log\gamma_{1}+\cdots+b_{t}\log\gamma_{t},

(2.7)

where
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}
are positive real algebraic numbers and
b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}
are nonzero integers. We assume,
\Lambda\neq 0
. We need lower bounds
for
|\Lambda|
. We write
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{Q}}(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t})
and
D
for the degree of
{\mathbb{K}}
.
We give the general form due to Matveev
[
14
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(Matveev,
[
14
]
)
.

Put
\Gamma:=\gamma_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t}^{b_{t}}-1=e^{\Lambda}-1
. Then

\log|\Gamma|>-1.4\cdot 30^{t+3}\cdot t^{4.5}\cdot D^{2}(1+\log D)(1+\log B)A_{1}\cdots A_{t},

where
B\geq\max\{|b_{1}|,\ldots,|b_{t}|\}
and
A_{i}\geq\max\{Dh(\gamma_{i}),|\log\gamma_{i}|,0.16\}
for
i=1,\ldots,t
.

In our application of Matveev’s result (Theorem
2.1
), we need to ensure that the linear forms in logarithms are indeed nonzero. To ensure this, we shall need the following two results given as Lemma 2.7 and Lemma 2.8 in
[
9
]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 2.7 in
[
9
]
)
.

The only solution in positive integers
k\geq 2
,
x
,
y
of the Diophantine equation

(2^{k+1}-3)^{x}=\left(\frac{2^{k+1}k^{k}-(k+1)^{k+1}}{(k-1)^{2}}\right)^{y}

is
k=2
, for which
x=y
.

Lemma 2.2

(Lemma 2.8 in
[
9
]
)
.

Let
N:=N_{\mathbb{K}/\mathbb{Q}},
where
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
. Then

(i)

For
n,m\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
,
|N(\alpha)|=1
.

(ii)

N(2\alpha-1)=2^{k+1}-3
and
N(f_{k}(\alpha))=(k-1)^{2}/(2^{k+1}k^{k}-(k+1)^{k+1})
.

(iii)

For

N((2\alpha-1)f_{k}(\alpha))<1
.

For the special case when
t=2
in Theorem
2.1
, we instead apply a sharper result from
[
12
]
. Suppose that
A_{1}>1,~A_{2}>1
are real numbers such that

\log A_{i}\geq\max\left\{h(\gamma_{i}),\frac{|\log\gamma_{i}|}{D},\frac{1}{D}\right\}\quad{\text{\rm for}}\quad i=1,2.

(2.8)

Put

b^{\prime}:=\frac{|b_{1}|}{D\log A_{2}}+\frac{|b_{2}|}{D\log A_{1}}.

The following result is Corollary 2 in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Laurent et al, 1995)
.

In case
t=2
, we have

\log|\Lambda|\geq-24.34D^{4}\left(\max\left\{\log b^{\prime}+0.14,\frac{21}{D},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\right)^{2}\log A_{1}\log A_{2}.

However, during the calculations, upper bounds on the variables which are too large are obtained, thus there is need to reduce them. In this paper we use the following result related with continued fractions (see Theorem 8.2.4 in
[
17
]
).

Lemma 2.3

(Legendre)
.

Let
\tau
be an irrational number,
[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
be the continued fraction expansion of
\tau
. Let
p_{i}/q_{i}=[a_{0},a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{i}]
, for all
i\geq 0
, be all the convergents of the continued fraction of
\tau
, and
M
be a positive integer. Let
N
be a non-negative integer such that

q_{N}>M
.
Then putting
a(M):=\max\{a_{i}:i=0,1,2,\ldots,N\}
, the inequality

\bigg|\tau-\frac{r}{s}\bigg|>\dfrac{1}{(a(M)+2)s^{2}},

holds for all pairs
(r,s)
of positive integers with
0<s<M
.

However, since there are no methods based on continued fractions to find a lower bound for linear forms in more than two variables with bounded integer coefficients, we use at some point a method based on the LLL–algorithm. We next explain this method.

Let
k
be a positive integer. A subset
\mathcal{L}
of the
k
–dimensional real vector space
{\mathbb{R}^{k}}
is called a lattice if there exists a basis
\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{Z}b_{i}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}b_{i}\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

We say that
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
form a basis for
\mathcal{L}
, or that they span
\mathcal{L}
. We
call
k
the rank of
\mathcal{L}
. The determinant
\text{det}(\mathcal{L})
, of
\mathcal{L}
is defined by

\displaystyle\text{det}(\mathcal{L})=|\det(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k})|,

with the
b_{i}
’s being written as column vectors. This is a positive real number that does not depend on the choice of the basis (see
[
7
]
, Section 1.2).

Given linearly independent vectors
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}
in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we refer back to the Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization technique. This method allows us to inductively define vectors
b^{*}_{i}
(with
1\leq i\leq k
) and real coefficients
\mu_{i,j}
(for
1\leq j\leq i\leq k
). Specifically,

\displaystyle b^{*}_{i}

\displaystyle=b_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}b^{*}_{j},~~~\mu_{i,j}=\dfrac{\langle b_{i},b^{*}_{j}\rangle}{\langle b^{*}_{j},b^{*}_{j}\rangle},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the ordinary inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Notice that
b^{*}_{i}
is the orthogonal projection of
b_{i}
on the orthogonal complement of the span of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{i-1}
, and that
\mathbb{R}b_{i}
is orthogonal to the span of
b^{*}_{1},\ldots,b^{*}_{i-1}
for
1\leq i\leq k
. It follows that
b^{*}_{1},b^{*}_{2},\ldots,b^{*}_{k}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Definition 2.2
.

The basis
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n}
for the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is called reduced if

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i,j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1\leq j<i\leq n,~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\|b^{*}_{i}+\mu_{i,i-1}b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\|b^{*}_{i-1}\|^{2},\quad\text{for}\quad 1<i\leq n,

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the ordinary Euclidean length. The constant
{3}/{4}
above is arbitrarily chosen, and may be replaced by any fixed real number
y
in the interval
{1}/{4}<y<1
(see
[
13
]
, Section 1).

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a
k-
dimensional lattice with reduced basis
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
and denote by
B
the matrix with columns
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}
.
We define

l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\min_{x\in\mathcal{L}}||x-y||\quad;~~y\not\in\mathcal{L}\\
\min_{0\neq x\in\mathcal{L}}||x||\quad;~~y\in\mathcal{L}\end{array}\right.,

where
||\cdot||
denotes the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. It is well known that, by applying the
LLL–algorithm, it is possible to give in polynomial time a lower bound for
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)
, namely a positive constant
c_{1}
such that
l\left(\mathcal{L},y\right)\geq c_{1}
holds (see
[
19
]
, Section V.4).

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
and
z=B^{-1}y
with
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})^{T}
. Furthermore,

(i)

if
y\not\in\mathcal{L}
, let
i_{0}
be the largest index such that
z_{i_{0}}\neq 0
and put
\lambda:=\{z_{i_{0}}\}
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the distance to the nearest integer.

(ii)

if
y\in\mathcal{L}
, put
\lambda:=1
.

Finally, we set

c_{1}:=\max\limits_{1\leq j\leq k}\left\{\dfrac{||b_{1}||}{||b_{j}^{*}||}\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta:=\lambda\dfrac{||b_{1}||}{c_{1}}.

In our application, we are given real numbers
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}
which are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
and two positive constants
c_{3}
and
c_{4}
such that

\displaystyle|\eta_{0}+x_{1}\eta_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\eta_{k}|\leq c_{3}\exp(-c_{4}H),

(2.9)

where the integers

are bounded as

with

. We write

. The basic idea in such a situation, from

, is to approximate the linear form (

\mathcal{L}
generated by the columns of the matrix

\mathcal{A}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0&0\\
0&1&\ldots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

\lfloor C\eta_{1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{2}\rfloor&\ldots&\lfloor C\eta_{k-1}\rfloor&\lfloor C\eta_{k}\rfloor\end{pmatrix},

where

Proof of the main result

We do this in the following cases.

3.1
The case
k=2

In this subsection we study the Diophantine equation

(L_{n})^{x}=(L_{m})^{y},

(3.1)

where
n>m\geq 0
,
n\neq 1,m\neq 1
and

.

Lemma 3.1
.

The only integer solution
(n,m)
of equation (
3.1
) in the range
n>m\geq 0
,

is
(n,m)=(3,0)
.

Proof.

By Carmichael’s Primitive Divisor Theorem and the known relation

has a primitive prime factor (namely, the primitive prime factor of

). In particular, equation (

. One now checks that in the remaining range

.
∎

3.2
The case

The equation is

(3.2)

where none of

is zero. Since

. If

.
From the above formulas, we get that if

,

, then

. Furthermore,

. All

\displaystyle L_{3}^{(2)}=2^{2},~~~~L_{4}^{(2)}=7,~~~~L_{6}^{(2)}=2\cdot 3^{2},~~~~L_{4}^{(3)}=2\cdot 5,~~~~L_{6}^{(3)}=5\cdot 7,

3.3

\displaystyle\left(L_{n}^{(k)}\right)^{x}=\left(f_{k}(\alpha)\right)^{x}(2\alpha-1)^{x}\alpha^{(n-1)x}\left[1+\dfrac{e_{k}(n)}{f_{k}(\alpha)(2\alpha-1)\alpha^{n-1}}\right]^{x}.

Now, we study the elements
