Hoare-Like Triples and Kleene Algebras with Top and Tests
Lena Verscht, Benjamin Kaminski

Abstract




Towards a Holistic Perspective on Hoare Logic, Incorrectness Logic, and Beyond

1.
Introduction

“Program correctness and incorrectness are

two sides of the same coin.”

—
O’Hearn 2019

We argue that the object of discourse is not a coin and that it has at least three sides or rather
dimensions
:

(1)

correctness
vs.
incorrectness

(2)

totality
vs.
partiality

(3)

reachability
vs.
unreachability

We will explore how one can pigeonhole
total
and
partial correctness
, as well as
incorrectness
into this view and explore further program properties that emerge from exhausting all combinations of the above dimensions.
We will furthermore explore how to express validity of those properties in the language of Kleene algebras with top and tests
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

2.
Canonical Exegeses of Triples

The central notion of Hoare logic
(
Hoare 1969
)
are triples of the form
\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
, where
p
is a program,
b
is a
precondition
on initial states, and
c
is a
postcondition
on final states.
To give meaning to a triple, it must be
exegeted
when a triple is considered
valid
and when it is not.
For Hoare logic, there are two such exegeses: validity for
total
and for
partial
correctness.

Exegesis I: Partial Correctness.

\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
is valid for
partial correctness
iff all executions of
p
that start in
b
can only ever terminate in
c
.
The execution
is
allowed to diverge.
For example, validity for partial correctness of

\displaystyle\left\{\text{wrong password}\right\}\mathrel{\quad p_{\text{login}}\quad}\left\{\text{login failed}\right\}

specifies that when the user enters a wrong password,
p_{\text{login}}
will either diverge or terminate with a failed login.
In particular, successful login is impossible with the wrong password.

Exegesis II: Total Correctness.

\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
is valid for
total correctness
iff all executions of
p
that start in
b
definitely
terminate
and they do so in
c
.
Reusing the example above, validity for total correctness of

\displaystyle\left\{\text{correct password}\right\}\mathrel{\quad p_{\text{login}}\quad}\left\{\text{login successful}\right\}~,

specifies that when the user enters the correct password, the login will definitely be successful.

It is noteworthy that both partial and total correctness are about
coreachability of all initial states
:

\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
is valid if
all
initial states in
b
are (partially) mapped by
p

into
(but not necessarily
onto
) the final states in
c
.

Weakest (Liberal) Preconditions.

Proposed by Dijkstra
(
Dijkstra et al. 1976
)
,
weakest (liberal) preconditions
are a very versatile alternative to Hoare logic and led to numerous extensions
(
Morgan et al. 1996
;
Kaminski et al. 2018
;
Kaminski 2019
;
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
)
.
Given only a program
p
and a postcondition
c
, the
weakest liberal precondition
is the weakest predicate
\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)
, such that all executions of
p
that start in
\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)
either diverge or terminate in
c
.
One can (re)define partial correctness in terms of weakest liberal preconditions:

\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
is valid for partial correctness iff
b\implies\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)
.

For
total
correctness, the weakest precondition is the weakest predicate
\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)
, such that all executions of
p
that start in
\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)
definitely terminate and they do so in
c
.
One can define total correctness in terms of weakest preconditions:

\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
is valid for total correctness iff
b\implies\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)
.

Exegesis III: Incorrectness.

Incorrectness triples were introduced by
De Vries and Koutavas 2011
and later independently reintroduced by
O’Hearn 2019
.

\left[b\right]\mathrel{p}\left[c\right]
is valid for incorrectness iff all executions of
p
that terminate in
c
could have started in
b
.
In other words: iff
p
maps (a subset of)
b

onto

c
.
In that sense, incorrectness is really about
reachability of all final states
.
If
c
is a set of error states, then validity of
\left[b\right]\mathrel{p}\left[c\right]
for incorrectness indeed proves that executing
p
on
b
is not safe, because doing so can reach an error in
c
.
For example, validity for incorrectness of

\displaystyle\left[\text{correct password}\right]\mathrel{\quad p_{\text{login}}\quad}\left[\text{program crash}\right]

specifies that
p_{\text{login}}
can crash on entering a correct password.

Given only a program
p
and a
pre

b
, the
strongest postcondition

(
Dijkstra and Scholten 1990
)
is the strongest predicate
\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
, such that any state in
\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
is reachable from
b
by executing
p
.
We can redefine incorrectness in terms of strongest postconditions
(
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
)
:

\left[b\right]\mathrel{p}\left[c\right]
is valid for incorrectness iff
c\implies\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
.

\mathsf{sp}
has a Galois connection to
\mathsf{wlp}
, namely

(
\dagger
)

\displaystyle b\implies\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)\qquad{\textnormal{iff}}\qquad\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)\implies c~.

Because of this, we can also define partial correctness in terms of strongest postconditions:

\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
is valid for partial correctness iff
\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)\implies c
.
Notice that the difference between incorrectness and partial correctness is merely the direction of the implication between
c
and
\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
.

3.
Completive Exegeses of Triples

Given a triple of precondition
b
, program
p
, and postcondition
c
, we have so far seen three exegesis of such triples, all given in terms of
\mathsf{wlp}
/
\mathsf{wp}
/
\mathsf{sp}
and implications:

total correctness

b\implies\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)

partial correctness

b\implies\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)

\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)\implies c

incorrectness

c\implies\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)

From inspecting this table, we can make out three degrees of freedom one has in exegeting triples:

(1)

The difference between total correctness and partial correctness is the usage of
\mathsf{wp}
vs.
\mathsf{wlp}
.

(2)

The difference between partial correctness and incorrectness is the direction of the implication.

(3)

The difference between total correctness and incorrectness is
\mathsf{wp}
vs.
\mathsf{sp}
.

This immediately gives rise to two questions:
What about
\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right){\implies}b
and
\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right){\implies}b
?
And can we define strongest liberal postconditions?

Strongest Liberal Postconditions.

Indeed, these can be sensibly defined
(
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
)
.
The difference between
\mathsf{wlp}
and
\mathsf{wp}
is that the liberal variant additionally contains all states that diverge, i.e. those
initial states for which there exists no final state
in which the execution of
p
could terminate.
Dually,
\mathsf{slp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
contains also all states that
cannot be reached at all
by executing
p
, i.e. those
final states for which there exists no initial state
in which the execution of
p
could have started.

\mathsf{slp}
also has a Galois connection
(
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
)
, but to
\mathsf{wp}
, namely

(
\ddagger
)

\displaystyle\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)\implies b\qquad{\textnormal{iff}}\qquad c\implies\mathsf{slp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)~.

Exhaustively combining
\mathsf{wp}
,
\mathsf{wlp}
,
\mathsf{sp}
, and
\mathsf{slp}
with all implication directions gives a completed canon of in total 8 different exegeses of triples, four of which reduce to only two via Galois connections, see
Figure

1
.

Exegesis IV: Partial Incorrectness.

The difference between total and partial correctness is the use of
\mathsf{wp}
vs.
\mathsf{wlp}
.
Consequently, when we replace
\mathsf{sp}
by
\mathsf{slp}
in the defining implication
c\implies\mathsf{sp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
of incorrectness, we should arrive at the exegesis of
partial incorrectness
, as suggested (but not further explored) by
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
:

[b]p[c]
is valid for
partial incorrectness
iff
c\implies\mathsf{slp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)
(and by (
\ddagger
) equivalently iff
\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)\implies b
), which means that all states in
c
are either unreachable (from anywhere) or reachable from
b
by executing
p
.
For example, validity for partial incorrectness of

\displaystyle\left[\text{wrong password}\right]\mathrel{\quad p_{\text{login}}\quad}\left[\text{login successful}\right]

specifies that any state where the login was successful is either entirely unreachable, or reachable by entering a wrong password, thus constituting a potential bug.

Exegesis V and VI

There are two remaining triple exegeses, defined by
\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)\implies b
and
\mathsf{slp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(b\right)\implies c
, respectively.
These have also been suggested by
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
, but not further explored.

Contrapositives

Besides some actual
equivalences
via Galois connections, some triples are contrapositives of each other
(
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
)
.
In fact, the entire left-hand-side (in some sense the
correctness side
) is the contrapositive of the right-hand-side (the
incorrectness side
) in
Figure

1
.
For example,

\displaystyle b\implies\mathsf{wlp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)\quad\text{iff}\quad\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(\neg c\right)\implies\neg b

and hence partial correctness is the contrapositive of partial incorrectness and so both describe in some sense the same class of specifications.
We would claim, however, that it might be more intuitive for a working programmer to specify either a partial incorrectness triple or a partial correctness triple, depending on the property they have in mind.

4.
Kleene Algebra with Top and Tests

Kleene algebra with tests
(
KAT
), introduced by
Kozen 1997
, is another alternative for specifying and reasoning about program properties.

KAT
terms are generalized regular expressions over a
two-sorted alphabet
comprising (i) programs (
p,q,\ldots
) and (ii) tests (
b,c,\ldots
).
We interpret these symbols as relations: a program
p
relates (maps) initial states to final states by its execution.
Initial states on which
p
diverges are not related with any final states.
Similarly, unreachable final states are not related with any initial states.
A test
b
maps initial states satisfying
b
to themselves, and those not satisfying
b
to no state.
In this sense, tests act as filters.

Testing for
false
is denoted by
0
and gives the
empty
relation.
In the lattice of relations (ordered by set inclusion),
0
is the least/bottom element.
In
Kleene algebra with top and tests
(
TopKAT
), we now additionally add a
\top
element to
KAT
, which codes for the
universal
relation relating every initial state to every final state
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
.

Composing symbols means composing relations:
For example, the term
bpqc
intuitively means: first test for
b
, then execute
p
(but only on states that satisfy
b
), then execute
q
, then test for
c
.
Executions that fail to satisfy initially
b
or finally
c
are filtered out and not part of the resulting relation.

Partial Correctness in
TopKAT

We can express partial correctness by the
TopKAT
equation
\top bpc=\top bp
.
Prepending the top element on both relations in the equation amounts to comparing their
codomains
, i.e. their
final states
.
Hence,
\top bpc=\top bp
expresses that the set of final states in
c
in which
p
can terminate starting from
b
is exactly those final states in which
p
can terminate from
b
at all.
No statement is made about initial states in
b
on which
p
diverges.

Incorrectness in
TopKAT

It is not possible to express incorrectness in
KAT
, but this
is
possible in
TopKAT

(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, namely by
\top bpc=\top c
.
On the left-hand-side of the equation, we select all final states in
c
that were reachable by executing
p
on
b
.
On the right-hand-side, we select
all
final states in
c
.

Nondeterminism and Correctness in
KAT

So far, we have kept quiet about nondeterminism, but in Kleene algebras it is very natural to model nondeterministic programs.
Considering total correctness of
\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}
, one then has to decide:
(i) must
all
execution paths emerging from any state in
b
terminate in
c
, or (ii) must there merely exist
some
path that does so?
The former is called the
demonic
exegesis of nondeterminism while the latter is called
angelic
.
On that note,
Zhang and Kaminski 2022
always interpret nondeterminism
angelically
for their
non
liberal pre- and postconditions, and
demonically
for their liberal ones.
Only this way, they obtain the Galois connections (
\dagger
) and (
\ddagger
).
However, the standard notion of total correctness is
demonic
and not
angelic
.

Unfortunately, it is known that
demonic
total correctness is inexpressible in
KAT

(
von Wright 2002
)
because it lacks a way of reasoning about nontermination:
Nonterminating executions are just not part of the resulting relation.
For the angelic variant, however,
bpc\top=b\top
indeed expresses that from all intial states in
b
, it is
possible
for
p
to terminate in
c
, thus:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c}\left\{b\right\}\mathrel{p}\left\{c\right\}\textnormal{ is valid for angelic total correctness}\\[5.0pt]
\textnormal{iff}\\[5.0pt]
bpc\top=b\top\end{array}

This is, to the best of our knowledge, a novel result.
Moreover, this result renders
all
triples depicted in
Figure

1
expressible in
TopKAT
in a syntactically very similar manner:

total correctness

bpc\top=b\top

partial correctness

\top bpc=\top bp

incorrectness

\top bpc=\top c

The opposite-hand-sides are expressible via contrapositives.
For example, validity of
\left[b\right]\mathrel{p}\left[c\right]
for partial incorrectness is expressible via
bpc\top=pc\top
.
And more equations fit into this pattern, for example
\top bpc=\top pc
and
bpc\top=bp\top
.
Interestingly, these two express the angelic variants of partial correctness and incorrectness, respectively, and thus lead to further exegeses of triples.

5.
Ongoing and Future Work

There are many open questions left to investigate.
One direction is to investigate deeper the different exegesis of nondeterminism, which would lead to a total combination of 16 possible exegeses, of which we conjecture that still only four of them reduce to two via Galois connections, thus ending up with 14 possible exegeses.
We would then like to investigate the impact of different assumptions about the program on this picture:
For example, what happens if we assume that executions started in
b
always terminate?
What happens if all states in
c
are reachable?
What if the program is deterministic or reversible?

As for a different direction, none of the aforementioned triples expresses the existence of (at least) one path from
b
to
c
.
However, being able to more directly specify a bug like
“it is possible to enter a wrong password and yet obtain successful login”
would be quite desirable.
The following equations would express a property of this form:

\displaystyle bpc\neq 0\quad\text{and equivalently}\quad\mathsf{wp}\llbracket p\rrbracket\left(c\right)\land b\neq\text{false}

In contrast to the existing triples, this property speaks neither about
all
initial states in
b
nor
all
final states in
c
.
