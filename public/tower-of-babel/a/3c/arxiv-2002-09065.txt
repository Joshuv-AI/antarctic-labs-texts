The Dunkl kernel and intertwining operator for dihedral groups
Hendrik De Bie, Pan Lian

Abstract
. Dunkl operators associated with finite reflection groups generate a commutative algebra of differential-difference operators. There exists a unique linear operator called intertwining operator which intertwines between this algebra and the algebra of standard differential operators. There also exists a generalization of the Fourier transform in this context called Dunkl transform. In this paper, we determine an integral expression for the Dunkl kernel, which is the integral kernel of the Dunkl transform, for all dihedral groups. We also determine an integral expression for the intertwining operator in the case of dihedral groups, based on observations valid for all reflection groups. As a special case, we recover the result of [Xu, Intertwining operators associated to dihedral groups. Constr. Approx. 2019]. Crucial in our approach is a systematic use of the link between both integral kernels and the simplex in a suitable high dimensional space.

33C45,44A20; Secondary 33C50, 33C80.

1.
Introduction

Dunkl operators are differential-difference operators that generalize the standard partial derivatives. They are constructed using a finite reflection group and a parameter function on the orbits of this group on its root system. They were initially introduced by Charles Dunkl in
[
18
]
, where he showed that, surprisingly, these operators still commute.
Dunkl operators have found a variety of applications in mathematics and mathematical physics. They motivate the study of double affine Hecke algebras and Cherednik algebras. They are used in the study of probabilistic processes and are crucial for the integration of quantum many body problems of Calogero-Moser-Sutherland type. They have also made a lasting impact in the study of orthogonal polynomials and special functions in one and several variables.
Apart from the seminal book
[
26
]
, several excellent reviews are currently available on this topic. We refer the reader to e.g.
[
22
,
33
]
and to
[
3
]
for a most recent state of the art.

The theory of Dunkl operators is further developed using two key ingredients. The first is the
Dunkl transform
, which is a generalization of the Fourier transform which now maps coordinate multiplication to the action of Dunkl operators and vice versa. It was introduced in
[
23
]
and further studied in
[
9
]
, where the author showed the boundedness and analyticity of the associated integral kernel called Dunkl kernel, and obtained the Plancherel theorem. Various special cases of Paley-Wiener type theorems for the Dunkl transform were obtained in
[
10
]
and multiplier theorems were investigated in e.g.
[
8
,
27
]
. The Dunkl transform was further generalized in
[
5
]
, based on observations made in
[
4
]
.

The second important operator is the
intertwining operator

V
. This operator is a linear and homogeneous isomorphism on the space of polynomials that maps the standard partial derivatives to the Dunkl operators. It is abstractly proven that the intertwining operator can be represented as an integral operator
[
34
]
. This is important if one wants to extend the intertwining property to larger function spaces. Also, for certain parameter values it behaves singularly, which were determined in
[
25
]
.

From an abstract point of view, several satisfactory results are known about both the Dunkl transform and the intertwining operator, as mentioned above. However, it has remained a major problem for thirty years to develop explicit formulas of the Dunkl kernel and the intertwining operator for concrete choices of the finite reflection group. We describe and list the known results.
In the one dimensional case, both the Dunkl kernel and the intertwining operator are explicitly known, see
[
24
,
33
]
. For the reflection groups of type
A_{n}
the action of the intertwining operator on polynomials is determined in
[
19
]
and given as a highly complicated integral for
n=2
in
[
18
]
. An integral expression for the intertwining operator for the group
B_{2}
was obtained in
[
17
]
.
For dihedral groups, acting in the plane, the current state of the art is as follows. For polynomials, explicit formulas giving
V(z^{k}\bar{z}^{\ell})
as complicated sums of monomials are given in
[
21
]
, but no integral formula is provided. Using a Laplace transform technique and knowledge on the Poisson kernel obtained in
[
16
]
, an explicit and concise expression was obtained for the Dunkl transform in the Laplace domain in
[
7
]
.
A series expression for the Dunkl kernel was given in
[
12
]
. Recently, for a restricted class of functions and restrictions on the parameter function, an integral expression was given for the intertwining operator in
[
37
]
using an integral over the simplex.

This lack of explicit formulas has seriously hindered the further development of harmonic analysis for Dunkl operators. The situation is better for the generalized Bessel function, which is a symmetrized version of the Dunkl kernel using the action of the reflection group. For the root systems
A_{n}
, a complicated integral recurrence formula for the generalized Bessel function was obtained in
[
1
]
. An explicit expression is derived in
[
36
]
for the intertwining operator in the symmetrized setting. For the dihedral case, a series expression for the generalized Bessel function was given in
[
15
]
and closed formulas were subsequently obtained for some specific parameters there. An explicit expression in the Laplace domain for the generalized Bessel function was obtained in
[
7
]
, using the same Laplace transform technique as for Dunkl dihedral kernel. Recently, a Laplace type expression for the generalized Bessel function for even dihedral groups with one variable specified is given in
[
14
]
.

The aim of the present paper is to give a complete description in the case of dihedral groups of the Dunkl kernel, the generalized Bessel function and the intertwining operator. For the Dunkl kernel, we obtain an expression in terms of the second class of Humbert functions (see Theorem
4.15
,
4.18
) or, alternatively, as an integral over the simplex (Theorem
4.15
). This is achieved by inverting the Laplace domain expression obtained in
[
7
]
.
For the intertwining operator and its inverse, we first determine an integral expression which is new to our knowledge, linking it to the classical Fourier transform and the Dunkl kernel. This is achieved in Theorems
3.2
and
3.10
, and the formula is valid for arbitrary reflection groups. Using the explicit formula for the Dunkl kernel in the dihedral case, we can subsequently give explicit expressions for the intertwining operator in that case. As expected, our formulas specialise to those given in
[
37
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we briefly introduce the basics of Dunkl theory and the second class of Humbert functions. In Section 3, we give a general integral expression for the intertwining operator and its inverse. Section 4 is devoted to the dihedral case. We give the explicit formulas for the generalized Bessel function, Dunkl kernel and the intertwining operator. A new proof of Xu’s result of
[
37
]
can be found at the end of this section.
We end with conclusions and a list of notations used in Section 4.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basics of Dunkl theory

Let
G
be a finite reflection group with a fixed positive root system
R_{+}
. A multiplicity function
\kappa:R\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
on the root system
R
is a
G
-invariant function, i.e.
\kappa(\alpha)=\kappa(h\cdot\alpha)
for all
h\in G
.
For
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the Dunkl operator
T_{\xi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
associated with the group
G
and the multiplicity function
\kappa(\alpha)
is defined by

T_{\xi}(\kappa)f(x)=\partial_{\xi}f(x)+\sum_{\alpha\in R_{+}}\kappa(\alpha)\langle\alpha,\xi\rangle\frac{f(x)-f(\sigma_{\alpha}x)}{\langle\alpha,x\rangle},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the canonical Euclidean inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\sigma_{\alpha}x:=x-2\langle x,\alpha\rangle\alpha/||\alpha||^{2}
is a reflection.
In the sequel, we write
T_{j}
in place of
T_{e_{j}}(\kappa)
where
e_{j},j=1,\cdots,m
is a vector of the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. The Dunkl Laplacian
\Delta_{\kappa}
is then defined by
\Delta_{\kappa}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}T_{j}^{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
denote the polynomials on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}
the homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
. The Dunkl operators
\{T_{j}\}
generate a commutative algebra of differential-difference operators on
P
. Each
T_{i}
is homogeneous of degree
-1
. For
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
, the Fischer bilinear form

[p,q]_{\kappa}:=(p(T)q)(0)

was introduced in
[
26
]
. Here
p(T)
is the differential-difference operator obtained by replacing
x_{j}
in
p
by
T_{j}
. The Macdonald identity
[
32
]
also has a useful generalization in the Dunkl setting as follows:

[p,q]_{\kappa}=c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{\kappa}/2}p(x)\right)\left(e^{-\Delta_{\kappa}/2}q(x)\right)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\omega_{\kappa}(x)dx

where the weight function is

\omega_{\kappa}(x)=\prod_{\alpha\in R_{+}}|\langle\alpha,x\rangle|^{2\kappa(\alpha)}

and
c_{\kappa}
is the Macdonald-Mehta-Selberg constant, i.e.

c_{\kappa}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\omega_{\kappa}(x)dx.

The Dunkl kernel
E_{\kappa}(x,y)
is the joint eigenfunction of all the
T_{j}
,

(1)

\displaystyle T_{j}E_{\kappa}(x,y)=y_{j}E_{\kappa}(x,y),\qquad j=1,\ldots,m

and satisfies
E_{\kappa}(0,y)=1
. When
\kappa=0
, it reduces to the ordinary exponential function
e^{\langle x,y\rangle}
.
Choosing an orthonomal basis
\{\varphi_{\nu},\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{N}\}
of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to the Fischer inner product, the Dunkl kernel can be expressed as

(2)

\displaystyle E_{\kappa}(x,y)=\sum_{\nu\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{N}}\varphi_{\nu}(x)\varphi_{\nu}(y)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We further introduce the generalized Bessel function defined as the symmetric version of the Dunkl kernel by

\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(x,y)=\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}E_{\kappa}(x,g\cdot y).

The Dunkl transform is defined using the joint eigenfunction
E_{\kappa}(x,y)
and the weight function
\omega_{\kappa}(x)

by

\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}f(y):=c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}E_{\kappa}(-ix,y)f(x)\omega_{\kappa}(x)dx\quad(y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}).

When
\kappa=0
, the Dunkl transform reduces to the ordinary Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
, i.e.

\mathcal{F}f(y):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-i\langle x,y\rangle}f(x)dx.

The definition of Dunkl transform is motivated by the following proposition (Proposition 7.7.2 in
[
26
]
) which will also be used in the following section.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
p
be a polynomial on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
v(y)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}^{2}
for
y\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
, then

c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[e^{-\Delta_{\kappa}/2}p(x)\right]E_{\kappa}(x,y)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\omega_{\kappa}(x)dx=e^{v(y)/2}p(y).

There exists an unique linear and homogenous isomorphism on
\mathcal{P}
which satisfies
V_{\kappa}1=1
and intertwines the ordinary partial differential operators and the Dunkl operators,

(3)

\displaystyle T_{j}V_{\kappa}=V_{\kappa}\partial_{j},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,m.

The operator
V_{\kappa}
is called the Dunkl intertwining operator in the literature. The explicit representation of this operator is only known so far for some special cases, e.g. the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and root system
A_{2},B_{2}
, see
[
20
,
26
]
and
[
3
]
for a recent review. We list the rank one case, which is frequently used in this paper.

Example 2.2
.

For
k>0
, the intertwining operator in the rank one case is

(4)

\displaystyle V_{k}(p)(x)=\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(k)}\int_{-1}^{1}p(xt)(1-t)^{k-1}(1+t)^{k}dt.

Using the intertwining operator, the Dunkl kernel is expressed as

E_{\kappa}(x,y)=V_{\kappa}\left(e^{\langle\cdot,y\rangle}\right)(x).

Throughout this paper, we only consider real multiplicity functions with
\kappa\geq 0
.

2.2.
Humbert function of several variables

There are many ways to define hypergeometric functions of several variables. In this subsection, we introduce the second Humbert function of
m
variables

\Phi_{2}^{(m)}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m};\gamma;x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}):=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{m}\geq 0}\frac{(\beta_{1})_{j_{1}}\cdots(\beta_{m})_{j_{m}}}{(\gamma)_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{m}}}\frac{x_{1}^{j_{1}}}{j_{1}!}\cdots\frac{x_{m}^{j_{m}}}{j_{m}!}

which is one of the confluent Lauricella hypergeometric series, see
[
29
]
(Chapter 2, 2.1.1.2, page 42).

When
\gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}
and each
\beta_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,m
is a positive number, the hypergeometric function
\Phi_{2}^{(m)}
admits the following integral representation

(5)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}^{(m)}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m};\gamma;x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{\beta}^{(\gamma)}\int_{T^{m}}e^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}t_{j}}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}\right)^{\gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}-1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}^{\beta_{j}-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{m}

where

C_{\beta}^{(\gamma)}=\frac{\Gamma{(\gamma)}}{\Gamma(\gamma-\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j})\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Gamma(\beta_{j})}

and
T^{m}
is the open unit simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
given by

\displaystyle T^{m}=\left\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}):t_{j}>0,j=1,\ldots,m,\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_{j}<1\right\}.

See
[
6
,
31
]
for more details on this integral expression.

Moreover, Section 4.24, formula (5) in
[
28
]
shows that the Laplace transform in the variable
t
of
\Phi_{2}^{(m)}
is given by

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(t^{\gamma-1}\Phi_{2}^{(m)}(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{m};\gamma;\lambda_{1}t,\ldots,\lambda_{m}t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{s^{\gamma}}\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{1}}{s}\right)^{-\beta_{1}}\cdots\left(1-\frac{\lambda_{m}}{s}\right)^{-\beta_{m}}

with
{\rm Re}\,\gamma,{\rm Re}\,s>0,{\rm Re}\lambda_{j},j=1,\cdots,m.

3.
New formulas for the intertwining operator and its inverse

In this section, we give an integral expression of
V_{\kappa}
in terms of the Dunkl kernel which will be used to derive the explicit expression for the dihedral groups in subsequent sections.
We first formulate the following lemma using the ordinary Fourier transform. Note that the notation
e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(iy)
used in the following means acting with the operator
e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}
on the complex valued polynomial
p(iy)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
y,z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, let
p(z)
be a polynomial. Then we have

\displaystyle e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-i\langle x,y\rangle}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}p(x)e^{|y|^{2}/2}dx,

(7)

\displaystyle e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}E_{\kappa}(iy,z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-i\langle x,y\rangle}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}E_{\kappa}(-x,z)e^{|y|^{2}/2}dx,

where
\Delta_{(y)}
is the usual Laplace operator
\Delta=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\partial_{j}^{2}
acting on the variable
y
.

Proof.

Let
\kappa=0
in Proposition
2.1
, then one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\biggr)e^{i\langle x,y\rangle}e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy=(2\pi)^{m/2}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}p(x).

Acting with the ordinary Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
on both sides leads to the first identity in the present theorem,

\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\biggr)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{-i\langle x,y\rangle}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}p(x)dx.

For the second part, we first give the following estimation,

(8)

\displaystyle\left|e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}E_{\kappa}(iy,z)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|V_{\kappa}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{i\langle y,\cdot\rangle}\right)(z)\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|V_{\kappa}\left(e^{|\cdot|^{2}/2}e^{i\langle y,\cdot\rangle}\right)(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{|z|^{2}/2}

where the last inequality is by
\left|e^{|x|^{2}/2}e^{i\langle y,x\rangle}\right|\leq e^{|x|^{2}/2}
and the Bochner-type representation of the intertwining operator proved in
[
34
]
. Using the above estimation (
8
) and the dominated convergence theorem, the second equality in the theorem is obtained by replacing
p
with the Dunkl kernel in the first equality.
∎

By the Fischer inner product, we give a general formula for the intertwining operator,
which reveals the relationship between the Dunkl kernel and the intertwining operator. In principle, once the Dunkl kernel is known, our theorem yields the integral expression for the intertwining operator.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
p
be a polynomial and
K(iy,z):=e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}E_{\kappa}(iy,z)
,
then for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the intertwining operator
V_{\kappa}
satisfies

(9)

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)\mathcal{F}\left(p(\cdot)e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}E_{\kappa}(\cdot,-z)\right)(y)\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}p(\cdot)\right)(y)e^{|y|^{2}/2}dy.

Proof.

It is well known that the exponential function
e^{\langle y,z\rangle}
is the reproducing kernel of the polynomials with respect to the classical Fischer inner product, denoted by
[\cdot,\cdot]_{0}
, corresponding to the multiplicity function
\kappa(\alpha)=0
.
More precisely, with the Macdonald identity, we have

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[p(y),e^{\langle y,z\rangle}\right]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(y)\biggr)\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{\langle y,z\rangle}\biggr)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy.

By complexification, we have

(10)

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[p(-iy),e^{i\langle y,z\rangle}\right]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\biggr)\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{i\langle y,z\rangle}\biggr)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy.

Applying
V_{\kappa}
on both sides of (
10
) with respect to
z
, it follows that

(11)

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\biggr)V_{\kappa}\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{i\langle y,\cdot\rangle}\biggr)(z)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\biggr)\biggl(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}E_{\kappa}(iy,z)\biggr)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy.

Now, putting the integral expression (
3.1
) in (
11
), leads to

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|x|^{2}/2}p(x)\right)dy.

The second identity in (
9
) follows from the expression (
3.1
).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

It is known that for any polynomial
p(x)
, the function
\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|x|^{2}/2}p(x)\right)
is in the Schwarz space. Furthermore, by the estimation

|K(iy,z)|=\left|V_{\kappa}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{i\langle y,\cdot\rangle}\right)(z)\right|\leq e^{|z|^{2}/2},

the integral in (
9
) makes sense.

Remark 3.4
.

At the moment, it is not clear to the authors if the above expression (
3.2
) can be rewritten as a Rösler type integral
[
34
]

V_{\kappa}(p)(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}p(x)d\mu_{z}(x).

For a polynomial which is invariant under
G
, i.e.
p(g\cdot z)=p(z),
for all
g\in G
, the reproducing kernel under the ordinary Fischer inner product is given by

\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}e^{\langle x,g\cdot y\rangle}.

Therefore, the intertwining operator acting on invariant polynomials could be obtained as

V_{\kappa}(p)(z)=V_{\kappa}\left(\left[p(\cdot),\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}e^{\langle\cdot,g\cdot y\rangle}\right]_{0}\right)(z).

Following the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we obtain the expression of the intertwining operator on the invariant polynomials by replacing the Dunkl kernel with the generalized Bessel function.

Corollary 1
.

Let
p
be a
G
-invariant polynomial. Then for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the intertwining operator
V_{\kappa}
satisfies

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(\cdot,-z)\right)(y)\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}p(\cdot)\right)(y)e^{|y|^{2}/2}dy.

In the following, we show independently that the integral expression given in (
9
) actually intertwines the usual partial derivatives and the Dunkl operator, i.e. the relations (
3
). We use the notation
[A,B]:=AB-BA
for the commutator of two operators
A,B
. The following lemma from
[
33
]
will help to verify these relations.

Lemma 3.5
.

[
33
]

For
j=1,\ldots,m
, we have

\displaystyle[y_{j},\Delta_{\kappa}/2]=-T_{j};

\displaystyle\left[y_{j},e^{-\Delta_{\kappa}/2}\right]=T_{j}e^{-\Delta_{\kappa}/2}.

Theorem 3.6
.

For any polynomial
p
, the integral operator

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)\mathcal{F}\left(p(\cdot)e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

satisfies the relations

V_{\kappa}\partial_{j}=T_{j}V_{\kappa},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m.

Proof.

By direct computation, we have

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(\partial_{j}p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)\mathcal{F}\left((\partial_{j}p)(\cdot)e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)\mathcal{F}\left(\partial_{j}(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2})+x_{j}p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)i(y_{j}+\partial_{y_{j}})\mathcal{F}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(i(y_{j}-\partial_{y_{j}})K(iy,z)\right)\mathcal{F}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}\left(iy_{j}E_{\kappa}(iy,z)\right)\right)\mathcal{F}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T_{j}V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

where we have used Lemma
3.5
in the fifth equality and the relation (
1
) in the last step.
∎

In the following, we compute some special cases using the integral expression (
9
). The explicit expressions for the intertwining operator associated to some special root systems are reobtained.

Example 3.7
.

When
\kappa_{\alpha}=0
, expression (
9
) reduces to

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}K(iy,z)\mathcal{F}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}e^{i\langle y,z\rangle}e^{|z|^{2}/2}\mathcal{F}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{|z|^{2}/2}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{F}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)\right)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p(z)

This coincides with the result that for
\kappa_{\alpha}=0
the intertwining operator is the identity operator.

Example 3.8
.

(Rank 1 case) For the rank one case with
k>0
, the Dunkl kernel is explicitly known as

E_{k}(iy,z)=\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(k)}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ityz}(1-t)^{k-1}(1+t)^{k}dt.

It follows that

\displaystyle e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}E_{k}(iy,z)=\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(k)}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ityz}e^{t^{2}z^{2}/2}(1-t)^{k-1}(1+t)^{k}dt.

Now, we have

\displaystyle V_{k}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{(2\pi)^{1/2}\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(k)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\right)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}

\displaystyle\times\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ityz}e^{t^{2}z^{2}/2}(1-t)^{k-1}(1+t)^{k}dtdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{(2\pi)^{1/2}\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(k)}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\right)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}e^{ityz}dy\right)

\displaystyle\times e^{t^{2}z^{2}/2}(1-t)^{k-1}(1+t)^{k}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(k+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(k)}\int_{-1}^{1}p(tz)(1-t)^{k-1}(1+t)^{k}dt

where we have used Proposition
2.1
again in the last equality.

Example 3.9
.

(Root system
B_{2}
) When
\gamma=\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}>1/2
, the generalized Bessel function of type
B_{2}
(i.e. the dihedral group
I_{4}
) admits the following Laplace-type integral representation:

\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(iy,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i\langle y,x\rangle}H_{k}(x,z)dz

where
H_{k}(x,z)
is a positive function with explicit expression, see
[
2
]
. It follows that

\displaystyle e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(iy,z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{i\langle y,x\rangle}e^{|x|^{2}/2}H_{k}(x,z)dz.

Now, for the root system
B_{2}
and
p(z)
a
I_{4}
-invariant polynomial, which means that
p(g\cdot z)=p(z)
for
g\in I_{4}
, we have

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\right)\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(iy,z)\right)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{i\langle y,x\rangle}H_{k}(x,z)dz\right)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}p(-iy)\right)\left(e^{-\Delta_{(y)}/2}e^{i\langle y,x\rangle}\right)e^{-|y|^{2}/2}dy\right]H_{k}(x,z)dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}p(x)H_{k}(x,z)dz.

In general, whenever a Laplace-type representation of the generalized Bessel function is obtained, it is possible to obtain the intertwining operator by the method described in this example.

While the precise structure of the Dunkl intertwining operator has been unknown for a long time, the formal inverse of this operator is easy to get, see
[
26
]
. This operator can be expressed as

V_{\kappa}^{-1}(p)(x)={\rm exp}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}T_{j}^{(y)}\right)p(y)\bigg|_{y=0}=\left[e^{\langle x,y\rangle},p(y)\right]_{\kappa}

and satisfies the following relation

V_{\kappa}^{-1}T_{j}=\partial_{j}V_{\kappa}^{-1},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,m.

The integral expression of
V_{\kappa}^{-1}
can be obtained similarly as in Theorem
3.2
. We omit the proof here.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
p(z)
be a polynomial. Denote

L(iy,z):=e^{-\Delta_{\kappa,y}/2}e^{i\langle y,z\rangle}=c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}E_{\kappa}(-ix,y)e^{-\langle x,z\rangle}e^{-|x|^{2}/2}e^{|y|^{2}/2}\omega_{\kappa}(x)dx.

Then the inverse of the intertwining operator
V_{\kappa}^{-1}
satisfies

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}^{-1}(p)(z)=c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}L(iy,z)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\left(p(\cdot)e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}\right)(y)\omega_{\kappa}(y)dy

where
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
is the Dunkl transform.

Theorem 3.11
.

For any polynomial
p(x)
, the integral operator
V_{\kappa}^{-1}
satisfies

V_{\kappa}^{-1}T_{j}=\partial_{j}V_{\kappa}^{-1},\quad j=1,\ldots,m.

Proof.

Since
e^{-|x|^{2}/2}
is
G
-invariant, we have by direct computation,

(12)

\displaystyle T_{j}(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2})=T_{j}(p)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}+p(x)T_{j}(e^{-|x|^{2}/2}).

Now, the intertwining relations are verified as follows

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}^{-1}(T_{j}p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}L(iy,z)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\left((T_{j}p)(\cdot)e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}\right)(y)\omega_{\kappa}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}L(iy,z)\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\left(T_{j}(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2})+x_{j}p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)\omega_{\kappa}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}L(iy,z)i(y_{j}+T_{y_{j}})\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)\omega_{\kappa}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[i(y_{j}-T_{y_{j}})L(iy,z)\right]\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)\omega_{\kappa}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left[e^{-\Delta_{\kappa,y}/2}\left(iy_{j}e^{\langle iy,z\rangle}\right)\right]\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}\left(p(x)e^{-|x|^{2}/2}\right)(y)\omega_{\kappa}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{j}}V_{\kappa}^{-1}(p)(z),

where we have used formula (
12
) in the second line and Lemma
3.5
for the fifth equality.
∎

4.
The case of dihedral groups

The dihedral group
I_{k}
is the group of symmetries of the regular
k-
gon. We use complex coordinates
z=x_{1}+ix_{2}
and identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{C}
. Each reflection
\sigma_{j}
in
I_{k}
is given by
z\rightarrow\overline{z}e^{ij2\pi/k}
,
0\leq j\leq 2k-1.
In this section, we will consider the explicit expressions for the Dunkl kernel and the intertwining operator associated to the dihedral groups. These can be achieved mainly due to the reduction method, i.e. the general
I_{k}
case can be reduced to the explicitly known cases
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}
, see Section 7.6 in
[
26
]
. In turn, the reduction determines the representations of the formulas. As we will see, the Dunkl kernel and the generalized Bessel function are expressed as the compositions of two integrals. One is the Weyl fractional integral ( the intertwining operator associated to the group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
) and the other one corresponds to the reduction.

On the other hand, the dihedral group
I_{k}
is generated by the rotation
z\rightarrow ze^{i2\pi/k}
and the reflection
z\rightarrow\overline{z}
. The action of the rotations and reflection can be seen from the formula, see Theorem
4.8
and Theorem
4.18
. The positivity and bounds of the Dunkl kernel and generalized Bessel function will be seen directly from the formulas as well.

4.1.
The generalized Bessel function

It was proved in
[
15
]
that the generalized Bessel function can be expressed as a symmetric beta integral of an infinite series. Let
z=|z|e^{i\phi_{1}}
,
w=|w|e^{i\phi_{2}}
and
\xi_{u,v}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=v\cos(\phi_{1})\cos(\phi_{2})+u\sin(\phi_{1})\sin(\phi_{2})
, then we have the following expression.

Theorem 4.1
.

[
15
]
For the dihedral group
I_{2k}
,
k\geq 2
and non-negative multiplicity function
\kappa=(\alpha,\beta)
, the generalized Bessel function is given by

(13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\biggl(f_{2k,\alpha+\beta}(|zw|,\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)

\displaystyle+f_{2k,\alpha+\beta}(|zw|,-\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)\biggr)d\nu^{\alpha}(u)d\nu^{\beta}(v)

where
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,t)
is the infinite series

\displaystyle f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,t)=\Gamma(k\lambda+1)\biggl(\frac{2}{b}\biggr)^{k\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{(j+\lambda)}{\lambda}\mathcal{I}_{k(j+\lambda)}(bt)C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\xi)

with
C_{j}^{(\lambda)}(\xi)
the Gegenbauer polynomial and
\mathcal{I}_{k}(b)
the modified Bessel function of the first kind, i.e.

\mathcal{I}_{\nu}(b)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(b/2)^{2n+\nu}}{n!\Gamma(n+\nu+1)},\,b\in\mathbb{R}

as well as the symmetric beta measure

\displaystyle d\nu^{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1/2)}{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(\alpha)}(1-u^{2})^{\alpha-1}du.

The series
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,t)
admits a closed form in the Laplace domain as studied in our previous paper
[
7
]
. By the inverse Laplace transform, it is realized that
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,t)
is in fact a Humbert
\Phi_{2}^{(m)}
function, see
[
7
]
formula (19). Later, this was proved more generally in
[
13
]
using a different method. We summarize the results and present a more compact expression using derivatives in the Laplace domain in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
k\geq 2
,
the Laplace transform of
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,t)
with respect to
t
is given by

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,\cdot)](s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(k\lambda+1)\frac{2^{k\lambda}}{S}\frac{(S+s)^{k}-(s-S)^{k}}{((S+s)^{k}-2b^{k}\xi+(s-S)^{k})^{\lambda+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Gamma(k\lambda)2^{k\lambda}\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{1}{((S+s)^{k}-2b^{k}\xi+(s-S)^{k})^{\lambda}}\right),

where
S=\sqrt{s^{2}-b^{2}}
. Moreover, we have

(15)

\displaystyle f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}^{(k)}\left(\lambda,\ldots,\lambda;k\lambda;b_{0},\ldots,b_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{b_{0}}\Phi_{2}^{(k-1)}\left(\lambda,\ldots,\lambda;k\lambda;b_{1}-b_{0},\ldots,b_{k-1}-b_{0}\right)

where
b_{j}=b\cos\left((q-2j\pi)/k\right)
,
j=0,\ldots,k-1
in which
q=\arccos(\xi).

Remark 4.3
.

The denominator
(S+s)^{k}-2b^{k}\xi+(s-S)^{k}
is a polynomial in
s
and satisfies the factorization

(16)

\displaystyle(S+s)^{k}-2b^{k}\xi+(s-S)^{k}=2^{k}\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}\left(s-b\cos\left(\frac{q-2\pi l}{k}\right)\right).

Formula (
15
) follows from the Laplace transform formula (
6
) and the above factorization (
16
).

Remark 4.4
.

The first identity in (
15
) looks more symmetric than the second one. However, it is not possible to get an integral expression for
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,t)
directly from the integral representation (
5
) of
\Phi_{2}^{(m)}
, due to the the validity of the conditions there. This is not a problem for the second identity.

Combining the Humbert function expression of
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,1)
in (
15
) and the integral expression (
5
) for the function
\Phi_{2}^{(m)}
, we have the following integral expression of the generalized Bessel function. From this integral expression, it is directly seen that the generalized Bessel function
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(x,y)
is positive
and that the complexified generalized Bessel function
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(ix,y)
is bounded by
1
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For
k\geq 2
, the generalized Bessel function associated to the dihedral group
I_{2k}
is given by

(17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma{(k(\alpha+\beta))}}{2\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)^{k}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{T^{k-1}}\left(e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{+}t_{j}}+e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{-}t_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha+\beta-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}d\nu^{\alpha}(u)d\nu^{\beta}(v)

where
a_{j}^{+}=|zw|\cos\left(\frac{q_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})-2j\pi}{k}\right)
,
a_{j}^{-}=|zw|\cos\left(\frac{\pi-q_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})-2j\pi}{k}\right),

j=0,\ldots,k-1
,

q_{u,v}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=\arccos(\xi_{u,v}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}))
and
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
.

Remark 4.6
.

The integral over the simplex in (
17
) is the integral expression of
f_{2k,\alpha+\beta}(|zw|,\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)
. Although many variables
a_{j}^{\pm}
appear, it is a function in the variables
\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})
and
|zw|
by its series expansion, see also a direct verification in
[
13
]
. This further implies that
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)
is a function in the variables
|zw|,\frac{{\rm Re}(z^{k}){\rm Re}({w}^{k})}{|zw|^{k}}
and
\frac{{\rm Im}(z^{k}){\rm Im}({w}^{k})}{|zw|^{k}}
.

It is known that the generalized Bessel function is the reproducing kernel of the invariant polynomials under
[\cdot,\cdot]_{\kappa}
. By the series expansion of
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)
, we obtain the following integral expressions for the reproducing kernels.

Corollary 2
.

Let
p_{n}(z)
be a homogenous polynomial of degree
n
invariant under the action of
I_{2k},k\geq 2
, i.e.
p_{n}(z)=p_{n}(g\cdot z),
for any
g\in I_{2k}
. Then the reproducing kernel for
p_{n}(z)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{(n)}_{\kappa}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma{(k(\alpha+\beta))}}{2\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)^{k}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{T^{k-1}}\left(\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{+}t_{j}\right)^{n}+\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{-}t_{j}\right)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha+\beta-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}d\nu^{\alpha}(u)d\nu^{\beta}(v),

and satisfies
p_{n}(z)=\left[p_{n}(w),\mathcal{J}^{(n)}_{\kappa}(z,w)\right]_{\kappa}
.

Remark 4.7
.

The polynomials invariant under the group
I_{k}
form an algebra. This algebra is generated by
|z|^{2}
and
\frac{z^{k}+\overline{z^{k}}}{2}
(the Chevalley generators). As a linear space, the dimension of the invariant polynomials of degree
n
can be determined by Molien’s generating function, see
[
30
]
.

The integral expression (
17
) looks quite complicated, however, it reflects how the dihedral group
I_{2k}
acts. Due to nature of the reduction method, it will be seen in the following theorem that the expression is a composition of two operators, corresponding to the reflection and rotations in dihedral groups. Alternatively, by this expression, it is seen that
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)
is
I_{2k}
invariant.

Theorem 4.8
.

The generalized Bessel function
\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)
associated to
I_{2k},k\geq 2
is given by

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}J(w,|z|,s_{1}v,s_{2}u)d\nu^{\alpha}(u)d\nu^{\beta}(v)

with
s_{1}=\cos(k\phi_{1}),s_{2}=\sin(k\phi_{1})
and

\displaystyle J(w,|z|,s_{1},s_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}\left(e^{\langle w,z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\rangle}+e^{\langle w,z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{i(2j-1)\pi/k}t_{j}\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha+\beta-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}

where
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
and
c_{\kappa,k}=\frac{\Gamma{(k(\alpha+\beta))}}{2\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)^{k}}
.

Proof.

Note that the series
f_{2k,\lambda}(b,\xi,1)
is a function in variables
b
and
\xi
. Hence, the function
J(w,|z|,s_{1},s_{2})
is a function in the variables
|zw|
and
\xi_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})
. The integrand

\displaystyle J(w,|z|,s_{1}v,s_{2}u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[f_{2k,\lambda}(|zw|,\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)\right.

\displaystyle+\left.f_{2k,\lambda}(|zw|,-\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)\right]

is a function in the variables
|zw|
and
\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})
. The latter one can be obtained by replacing
\xi_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})
in the former one by

\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})=v\cos(k\phi_{1})\cos(k\phi_{2})+u\sin(k\phi_{1})\sin(k\phi_{2}).

This fact and expressions (
13
) and (
17
) lead to the first identity.

In the following, we simplify the function

\displaystyle J(w,|z|,s_{1},s_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(f_{2k,\lambda}(|z||w|,\xi_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)

\displaystyle+f_{2k,\lambda}(|z||w|,-\xi_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}),1)).

It is seen that

\displaystyle q_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arccos(\xi_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\arccos(\cos(k\phi_{1})\cos(k\phi_{2})+\sin(k\phi_{1})\sin(k\phi_{2}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle k(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}).

This yields that

\displaystyle a_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|zw|\cos\left(\frac{q_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})-2j\pi}{k}\right)=|zw|\cos\left((\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})-\frac{2j\pi}{k}\right),

\displaystyle a_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|zw|\cos\left(\frac{\pi-2j\pi-q_{1,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})}{k}\right)=|zw|\cos\left(\frac{\pi-2j\pi}{k}-(\phi_{1}-\phi_{2})\right).

In other words, we have

\displaystyle a_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Re}(w\overline{z}e^{j2i\pi/k})=\langle w,ze^{-i2j\pi/k}\rangle,

\displaystyle a_{j}^{-}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Re}(w\overline{ze^{i(2j-1)\pi/k}})=\left\langle w,ze^{i(2j-1)\pi/k}\right\rangle,

where
\langle z,w\rangle={\rm Re}(z\overline{w})
denote the usual Euclidean inner product for
z,w\in\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Hence for this special case
u=v=1
, the integrand of (
17
) becomes

(19)

\displaystyle e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{+}t_{j}}+e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{-}t_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{\langle w,z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\rangle}+e^{\langle w,z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{i(2j-1)\pi/k}t_{j}\rangle}.

The second formula is obtained.
∎

At the end of this subsection, we express
a_{j}^{\pm}
by Cartesian coordinates instead of the polar coordinates expression (
19
). We denote
(\sqrt[k]{z})_{j},0\leq j\leq k-1
the
k
different
k
-th roots of
z
.

Theorem 4.9
.

For
k\geq 2
, the generalized Bessel function associated to the dihedral group
I_{2k}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\kappa}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma{(k(\alpha+\beta))}}{2\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)^{k}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{T^{k-1}}h_{u,v,t_{0},\ldots,t_{k-1}}(z,w)

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha+\beta-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}d\nu^{\alpha}(u)d\nu^{\beta}(v)

where

\displaystyle h_{u,v,t_{0},\ldots,t_{k-1}}(z,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm exp}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}{\rm Re}\left(\sqrt[k]{{\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+i\sqrt{|zw|^{2k}-({\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}}))^{2}}}\right)_{j}\right)

\displaystyle+{\rm exp}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}{\rm Re}\left(\sqrt[k]{-{\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+i\sqrt{|zw|^{2k}-({\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}}))^{2}}}\right)_{j}\right)

here
\tilde{z}^{k}=v{\rm Re}(z^{k})+ui{\rm Im}(z^{k})
,
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
and

\sqrt{|zw|^{2k}-({\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}}))^{2}}
is the positive root.

Proof.

We only need to express
e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{+}t_{j}}+e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{-}t_{j}}

in the integrand of (
17
) by Cartesian coordinates. It reduces to express
a_{j}^{\pm}
by
z,w
instead of the angles
q_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})
. In fact, it is seen that

a_{j}^{+}=|zw|\cos\left(\frac{q_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})-2j\pi}{k}\right),0\leq j\leq k-1

are the real part of the
k
-roots of
{\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+i\sqrt{|zw|^{2k}-({\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}}))^{2}}
where
\tilde{z}^{k}=v{\rm Re}(z^{k})+iu{\rm Im}(z^{k})
.
Hence, we have

\displaystyle e^{\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a_{j}^{+}t_{j}}={\rm exp}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}t_{j}{\rm Re}\left(\sqrt[k]{{\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+i\sqrt{|zw|^{2k}-({\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}}))^{2}}}\right)_{j}\right).

The expression for
a_{j}^{-}
is obtained similarly.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

The result will not be changed if we choose
\sqrt{|zw|^{2k}-({\rm Re}(\tilde{z}^{k}\overline{w^{k}}))^{2}}
as the negative square root. When

. Hence, it reduces to the formula (

\displaystyle a_{j}^{+}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\left(\sqrt[k]{z^{k}\overline{w^{k}}}\right)_{j}=\langle w,ze^{-i2j\pi/k}\rangle.

However, it is not possible to express
a_{j}
as an inner product of the form
\langle w,g(z)\rangle

4.2.
The Dunkl kernel

In this section, we give two kinds of expressions of the Dunkl kernel based on the Laplace domain result obtained in
[
7
]
.

We still identify
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, and denote
z=|z|e^{i\phi_{1}}
,
w=|w|e^{i\phi_{2}}
.
The Laplace domain result is obtained by introducing an auxiliary variable in the series expansion of the Dunkl kernel and then taking the Laplace transform. More precisely, the series is

\displaystyle E_{\kappa}(z,w,t)=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta}\Gamma(k(\alpha+\beta)+1)}{|zw|^{k(\alpha+\beta)}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{I}_{j+k(\alpha+\beta)}(|zw|t)P_{j}\left(I_{k};e^{i\phi_{1}},e^{i\phi_{2}}\right),

where
P_{j}(I_{k};e^{i\phi_{1}},e^{i\phi_{2}})
is the reproducing kernel of the Dunkl harmonics of degree
j
and
\mathcal{I}_{j}
is the modified Bessel function of first kind, see
[
5
]
for the series expansion of the general Dunkl kernel.
It is proved that the above infinite series admits a closed expression in the Laplace domain for the dihedral groups.

Theorem 4.12
.

[
7
]
For the even dihedral group
I_{2k}
, the Laplace transform of the Dunkl kernel
E_{\kappa}(z,w,t)
with respect to
t
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(E_{\kappa}(z,w,t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(k(\alpha+\beta)+1)\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}f_{I_{2k}}(s,z,w)d\mu^{\alpha}(u)d\mu^{\beta}(v)

where

\displaystyle f_{I_{2k}}(s,z,w)=\frac{2^{k(\alpha+\beta)}}{(s-{\rm Re}(z\overline{w}))}\frac{(s+S)^{k}-2{\rm Re}(z^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+(s-S)^{k}}{((s+S)^{k}-2|zw|^{k}\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})+(s-S)^{k})^{\alpha+\beta+1}}

with
S=\sqrt{s^{2}-|zw|^{2}}
and

d\mu^{\gamma}(\omega)=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma+1/2)}{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma(\gamma)}(1+\omega)(1-\omega^{2})^{\gamma-1}d\omega.

Theorem 4.15

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}(z,w,u,v)

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}^{(k+1)}(\gamma,\ldots,\gamma,1;k\gamma+1;a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1},a_{k})

\displaystyle E_{\kappa}(z,w)

\displaystyle+\frac{2^{1-k}\Gamma(k(\alpha+\beta)+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+1)^{k}}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{T^{k}}|zw|^{k}\xi_{u-1,v-1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})

Proof.

We split

\displaystyle f_{I_{2k}}(s,z,w)

Then the first expression follows from taking the inverse Laplace transform for each term using the

The second formula is obtained by replacing the Humbert function by its integral expression (

Lemma 4.19
.

For
u,v\in[-1,1]
and
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
, we have

(22)

\displaystyle(1+u)(1+v)-\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}(\alpha u(1+v)+\beta v(1+u))\geq 0.

Proof.

When
u\leq 0
and
v\leq 0

22
) holds obviously. When
u\leq 0
and
v\geq 0
,
we have

\displaystyle(1+u)(1+v)-\frac{2\beta}{\alpha+\beta}v(1+u)\geq(1+u)\left(2v-\frac{2\beta}{\alpha+\beta}v\right)\geq 0.

Therefore, the inequality (
22

and
v\leq 0
is similar, we will not repeat the proof.

When
u\geq 0
and
v\geq 0

and

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{(\alpha u(1+v)+\beta v(1+u))}{(1+u)(1+v)}=\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{u}{1+u}+\frac{2\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{v}{1+v}\leq 1

which completes the proof.

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\alpha}(z,u,v)(1-u)d\nu^{\alpha}(u).

In these formulas, we put

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}(z,u,v))=\left[p(w),h_{\gamma}(z,w,u,v)\right]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}h_{\gamma}(z,\cdot,u,v)\right)(y)\mathcal{F}\left(e^{-|\cdot|^{2}/2}p(\cdot)\right)(y)e^{|y|^{2}/2}dy

with

\displaystyle h_{\gamma}(z,w,u,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}^{(k+1)}(\gamma,\ldots,\gamma,1;k\gamma+1;a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1},a_{k})

defined in Theorem
4.15
.

We verify this directly for the group
I_{1}
.

Example 4.24
.

Let
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
and
y=(y_{1},y_{2})
. For the rank one case, the intertwining operator is given by

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\alpha}(x,u,v)(1-u)d\nu^{\alpha}(u)

where

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}(x,u,v))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[p(y),h_{\gamma}(x,y,u,v)\right]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{0}^{1}[p(y),e^{x_{1}y_{1}+(1+ut-t)x_{2}y_{2}}]_{0}t^{\alpha-1}dt.

Computing the Fischer inner product, the intertwining operator is expressed as

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(p)(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}p(x_{1},(ut+1-t)x_{2})t^{\alpha-1}dt(1-u)d\nu^{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}p(x_{1},(ut+1-t)x_{2})t^{\alpha-1}dtd\mu^{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle+\alpha\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}p(x_{1},(ut+1-t)x_{2})t^{\alpha-1}(-2u)dtd\nu^{\alpha}(u).

In the following, we only consider the polynomial
x_{2}^{n}
, because in the present case the intertwining operator has no influence on the variable
x_{1}
. We claim that the following equality holds

(23)

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(ut+1-t)^{n}t^{\alpha-1}dt(-2u)d\nu^{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}n(u-1)(ut+1-t)^{n-1}t^{\alpha}dtd\mu^{\alpha}(u).

The identity (
23
) can be proved by computing both the left and right hand side explicitly.
Indeed, the left hand side is

\displaystyle-2\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\alpha(ut+1-t)^{n}t^{\alpha-1}dt\,u(1-u^{2})^{\alpha-1}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\int_{-1}^{1}2u^{j+1}(1-u^{2})^{\alpha-1}du\int_{0}^{1}t^{j+\alpha-1}(1-t)^{n-j}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\alpha\sum_{j=0}^{n}\binom{n}{j}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{j}{2}+1)\Gamma(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\frac{j}{2}+\alpha+1)}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha)\Gamma(n-j+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(1+(-1)^{j+1})}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{j}{2})\Gamma(j+\alpha)}{\Gamma(j)\Gamma(\frac{j}{2}+\alpha+1)}.

The right hand side is

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}n(u-1)(ut+1-t)^{n-1}t^{\alpha}dt(1+u)(1-u^{2})^{\alpha-1}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}n(ut+1-t)^{n-1}t^{\alpha}dt(1-u^{2})^{\alpha}du

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}n\binom{n-1}{j}\int_{-1}^{1}u^{j}(1-u^{2})^{\alpha}du\int_{0}^{1}t^{j+\alpha}(1-t)^{n-1-j}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{n!(1+(-1)^{j})}{2j!(n-1-j)!}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{j+1}{2})\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\frac{j+1}{2}+\alpha+1)}\frac{\Gamma(j+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n-j)}{\Gamma(n+1+\alpha)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{(1+(-1)^{j})}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{j+1}{2})\Gamma(j+\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(j+1)\Gamma(\frac{j+1}{2}+\alpha+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{(1+(-1)^{j+1})}{2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{j}{2})\Gamma(j+\alpha)}{\Gamma(j)\Gamma(\frac{j}{2}+\alpha+1)}.

Comparing both sides, we obtain the identity (
23
).

Now, with the identity (
23
), the intertwining operator for
x_{2}^{n}
becomes

(24)

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(x_{2}^{n})=\alpha\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(ut+1-t)x_{2})^{n}t^{\alpha-1}dtd\mu^{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle+\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}n(u-1)x_{2}((ut+1-t)x_{2})^{n-1}t^{\alpha}dtd\mu^{\alpha}(u).

On the other hand, by direct verification or using integration by parts, we have

(25)

\displaystyle\alpha\int_{0}^{1}(ut+1-t)^{n}t^{\alpha-1}dt+\int_{0}^{1}(u-1)n(ut+1-t)^{n-1}t^{\alpha}dt=u^{n}.

Hence, combining (
24
) and (
25
), we obtain

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(x_{2}^{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(x_{2}u)^{n}d\mu^{\alpha}(u),

which is the well-known expression.

4.4.
New proof of Xu’s result

In this section, we reobtain the intertwining operator given in
[
37
]
. We start from the odd dihedral group
I_{k}
with multiplicity function
\alpha
. In this case, the Laplace transform of the Dunkl kernel is given by

(26)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{k\alpha}\Gamma(k\alpha+1)}\mathcal{L}(E_{\kappa}(z,w,t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{(s+S)^{k}-2{\rm Re}(z^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+(s-S)^{k}}{(s-{\rm Re}(z\overline{w}))((s+S)^{k}-2|zw|^{k}\xi_{u,1}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})+(s-S)^{k})^{\alpha+1}}d\mu^{\alpha}(u)

where

\xi_{u,v}(k\phi_{1},k\phi_{2})=v\cos(k\phi_{1})\cos(k\phi_{2})+u\sin(k\phi_{1})\sin(k\phi_{2}).

This is obtained by the relations between the Dunkl kernel of
I_{k}
and
I_{2k}
, see also Theorem 12 in
[
7
]
.

Denote
w_{p}=e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}}
, then
w_{p}^{k}=e^{ip\pi}=\cos(p\pi)
, for
p=0,1,\ldots,2k-1
. Putting
w=w_{p}
in formula (
26
), the Dunkl kernel
E_{\kappa}(z,w_{p},t)
in the Laplace domain becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(E_{\kappa}(z,e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}},t))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{k\alpha}\Gamma(k\alpha+1)\frac{(s+S)^{k}-2{\rm Re}(z^{k}\overline{w^{k}})+(s-S)^{k}}{(s-{\rm Re}(z\overline{w}))((s+S)^{k}-2|zw|^{k}\xi_{u,1}(k\phi_{1},p\pi)+(s-S)^{k})^{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Gamma(k\alpha+1)\frac{1}{\left(s-|z|\cos(\phi_{1}-\frac{p\pi}{k})\right)^{\alpha+1}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(s-|z|\cos(\phi_{1}-\frac{p\pi}{k}-\frac{2j\pi}{k})\right)^{\alpha}}

where the first identity is because

\xi_{u,1}(k\phi_{1},p\pi)=\cos(k\phi_{1})\cos(p\pi)+u\sin(k\phi_{1})\sin(p\pi)=(-1)^{p}\cos(k\phi_{1})

which is independent of
u
and
\int_{-1}^{1}d\mu_{\alpha}(u)=1
. The inverse Laplace transform immediately shows that the Dunkl kernel in this special case is

(27)

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\left(e^{\langle\cdot,w_{p}\rangle}\right)(z)=E_{\kappa}(z,w_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{a_{0}}\Phi_{2}^{(k-1)}(\alpha,\ldots,\alpha;k\alpha+1;a_{1}-a_{0},\ldots,a_{k-1}-a_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}e^{\langle e^{ip\pi/k},z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\rangle}t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}

where
c_{\alpha,k}=\frac{\Gamma{(k\alpha+1)}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)^{k}}
,
a_{j}=|z|\cos(\phi_{1}-\frac{p\pi}{k}-\frac{2\pi j}{k})
,
j=0,\ldots,k-1
and
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
. This formula also follows from setting
w=w_{p}
in Theorem
4.18
.

It is known that the intertwining operator preserves homogenous polynomials, i.e.
V_{\kappa}(\mathcal{P}_{n})\subset\mathcal{P}_{n}
where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the space of homogenous polynomials of degree
n
.
Hence, formula (
27
) yields

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}(\langle\cdot,w_{p}\rangle^{n})(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}\left\langle w_{p},z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\right\rangle^{n}t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}.

By a limit discussion, it further leads to the intertwining operator for functions of the form
f(\langle e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}},z\rangle)
as

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\left(f\left(\langle\cdot,e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}}\rangle\right)\right)(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}f\left(\left\langle e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}},z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\right\rangle\right)t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}

where
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
and
c_{\alpha,k}=\frac{\Gamma{(k\alpha+1)}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)^{k}}
, which is the formula given in
[
37
]
, Theorem 1.1.

Based on the above proof, we understand Xu’s formula in the another way, which is the following corollary.

Corollary 4
.

For polynomials
p(z)
, the intertwining operator
V_{\kappa}
for the dihedral group
I_{k}
with
k
odd at the lines
z=|z|e^{i\frac{q\pi}{k}},0\leq q\leq 2k-1
is given by

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\left(p\left(\cdot\right)\right)\left(|z|e^{i\frac{q\pi}{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}p\left(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}|z|e^{i(q-2j)\pi/k}t_{j}\right)t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1},

where
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
and
c_{\alpha,k}=\frac{\Gamma{(k\alpha+1)}}{\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)^{k}}
.

Proof.

The same method used for deriving the formula (
27
) leads to a similar formula for the Dunkl kernel
E_{\kappa}\left(|z|e^{i\frac{q\pi}{k}},w\right)
. With this formula, we have

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\left(p\left(\cdot\right)\right)\left(|z|e^{i\frac{q\pi}{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[p(w),E_{\kappa}\left(|z|e^{i\frac{q\pi}{k}},w\right)\right]_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}\left[p(w),e^{\left\langle w,|z|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{\frac{i(q-2j)\pi}{k}t_{j}}\right\rangle}\right]_{0}t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{\alpha,k}\int_{T^{k-1}}p\left(|z|\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{\frac{i(q-2j)\pi}{k}}t_{j}\right)t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}.

∎

In particular, Corollary
4
offers a way to compute the intertwining action on polynomial
p(z)
when
V_{\kappa}(p(\cdot))(z)
is radial.

At the end of this section, we show that the same method leads to a partial and simple formula for the dihedral group
I_{2k}
with
\kappa=(\alpha,\beta)
without other difficulties. In this case, the Weyl fractional integral vanishes as well.

Example 4.25
.

Consider
w_{p}=e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}}
and
z=|z|e^{i\frac{(q+1/2)\pi}{k}}
,
p,q=0,1,2,\ldots,2k-1
. Then we have

\xi_{u,v}((q+1/2)\pi,p\pi)=v\cos((q+1/2)\pi)\cos(p\pi)+u\sin((q+1/2)\pi)\sin(p\pi)=0.

In this case, the Dunkl kernel at the line
z=|z|e^{i\frac{(q+1/2)\pi}{k}}
and
w=w_{p}
is only given by an integral over the simplex,

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\left(e^{\langle\cdot,w_{p}\rangle}\right)(z)=E_{\kappa}\left(z,w_{p}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(k(\alpha+\beta)+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)^{k}}\int_{T^{k-1}}e^{\langle e^{ip\pi/k},z\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\rangle}t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1}.

The same discussion as in the above shows that for functions
f(\langle e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}},z\rangle)
, the intertwining operator at the line
re^{i\frac{(q+1/2)\pi}{k}}
is given by

\displaystyle V_{\kappa}\left(f\left(\left\langle\cdot,e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}}\right\rangle\right)\right)\left(|z|e^{i\frac{(q+1/2)\pi}{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(k(\alpha+\beta)+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)^{k}}\int_{T^{k-1}}f\left(\left\langle e^{i\frac{p\pi}{k}},|z|e^{i\frac{(q+1/2)\pi}{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}e^{-i2j\pi/k}t_{j}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\times t_{0}^{\alpha}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}^{\alpha-1}dt_{1}\ldots dt_{k-1},

where
t_{0}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}t_{j}
.

Remark 4.26
.

The same method together with the integral expression of the generalized Bessel function leads to an integral expression of the intertwining operator for the
I_{k}
invariant polynomials.

Remark 4.27
.

A similar approach was used to derive the intertwining operator for a special class of functions for symmetric groups, where again the Humbert functions appear, see
[
11
]
.

5.
Conclusions

In this paper, an integral expression of the intertwining operator and it inverse is given for arbitrary reflection groups which is based on the classical Fourier transform and the Dunkl kernel. For the dihedral case, explicit expressions for the generalized Bessel function and Dunkl kernel are obtained by inverting the Laplace domain result of our previous paper
[
7
]
using the second class of Humbert functions. With these explicit formulas, we obtain several integral expressions for the intertwining operators in the symmetric and the non-symmetric settings. The positivity and the bound of the Dunkl kernel can be observed directly from our integral expressions.
