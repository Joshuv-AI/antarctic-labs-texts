Stochastic excitation of waves in magnetic stars
L. Bessila, S. Mathis

Abstract
Context. Stellar oscillations are key to unravelling stars’ properties, such as their mass, radius and age. In turn, it enables to date and characterize their exoplanetary systems. Amplitudes of acoustic (p-) modes in solar-like stars are intrinsically linked to their convective turbulent excitation source, which in turn is influenced by magnetism. In the observations of the Sun and stars, the amplitudes of the modes are modulated following their magnetic activity cycles: the higher the magnetic field, the lower the modes’ amplitudes. When the magnetic field is strong, it can even inhibit acoustic modes, which are not detected in a majority of solar-like stars presenting a strong magnetic activity. Magnetic fields are known to freeze convection when stronger than a critical value: an ”on-off” approach is used in the literature. Aims. In this work, we investigate the impact of magnetic fields on the stochastic excitation of acoustic modes. Methods. First, we generalise the forced wave equation formalism, including the effects of magnetic fields. Second, we assess how convection is affected by magnetic fields using results from Magnetic Mixing-Length Theory. Results. We provide the source terms of the stochastic excitation, including a new magnetic source term and the Reynolds stresses. We scaling laws for the amplitudes of the modes, taking into account both the driving and the damping. Those scalings are based on the inverse Alfvén dimensionless parameter: the damping increases with the magnetic field, and reaches a saturation threshold when the magnetic field is strong. The driving of the modes diminishes when the magnetic field becomes stronger, the turbulent convection being weaker. Conclusions. As expected from the observations, we find that a higher magnetic field diminishes the resulting modes amplitudes. Evaluating the inverse Alfvén number in stellar models provides a means to estimate the expected amplitudes of acoustic modes in magnetic active solar-type stars.



I. Scaling laws for the modes amplitudes

1
Introduction

Over the past decades, studying the oscillation modes of the Sun and stars allowed us to make transformative progresses in understanding stellar physics.
Ulrich 1970
and
Leibacher & Stein 1971
were the first to identify the solar five-minute oscillations as acoustic p modes. Knowing the acoustic oscillation frequencies, space-based helio, and asteroseismology became a powerful method to constrain the global parameters of stars, such as their mass, radius, and age
(
Christensen-Dalsgaard 2015
;
García & Ballot 2019
;
Aerts 2021
)
. Combined with the transit and the radial velocity methods this allows to characterise exoplanets and the dynamics of their host star
(
Huber et al. 2009
;
Gizon et al. 2013
)
.
Moreover, stellar seismology has enabled us to probe the structure and the internal rotation of stars
(
Thompson et al. 2003
;
García et al. 2007
, e.g.)
.

Nevertheless, detecting the acoustic modes is key to knowing more about the stars. Acoustic modes are excited by turbulent convection, which injects power into the oscillations. As all solar-like stars display an external convective zone, we expect to observe acoustic modes in all those stars. However, magnetic activity has proven to hinder the detection of the modes in
Kepler
data
(
Chaplin et al. 2011
;
Mathur et al. 2019
)
. Both authors witnessed the total suppression of
p
-modes in a vast part of solar-like pulsators.
Mathur et al. 2019
effectively detected acoustic modes in 60% of the sample’s stars. Both high magnetic field and high rotation seem to be at the origin of such modes suppression.

Furthermore, amplitudes of acoustic modes are sensitive to the variations of magnetic activity: some amplitude height variations correlated with magnetic activity were first discovered in HD 49933
(
Garcia et al. 2010
)
. In some other solar-like stars, there was evidence that the higher the magnetic activity, the lower the detected modes heights
(
Kiefer et al. 2017
;
Santos et al. 2018
;
Salabert et al. 2018
)
. The interplay between convection and magnetic fields is currently modelled through and ”on-off” approach: when the magnetic field is too strong, it completely suppresses convection.
(
Gough & Tayler 1966
;
MacDonald & Petit 2019
;
Jermyn & Cantiello 2020
, see e.g.)
. Integrating magnetism in theoretical models is thus essential to allow a better understanding of the oscillations and their detectability for the forthcoming space missions such as
PLATO

(
Rauer et al. 2014
;
Goupil et al. 2024
)
.

In all stars, the oscillations’ amplitudes result from a balance between damping and driving. In solar-like stars, stochastic excitation by turbulence inside the convection zone is the dominant driving mechanism. Turbulent motion of stellar fluid and fluctuations of thermodynamical quantities generate power, which in turn is injected into resonant oscillation modes in the stellar cavity.
Lighthill 1952
and
Stein 1967
first demonstrated how a turbulent background could generate acoustic waves.
Goldreich & Keeley 1977
,
Balmforth 1992b
,
Samadi & Goupil 2001
,
Belkacem et al. 2008
,
Philidet et al. 2020
formalised stochastic excitations for p-modes in stars linearising the equations of motion in a turbulent background.
Press 1981
,
Goldreich & Kumar 1990
,
Belkacem et al. 2009
and then
Lecoanet & Quataert 2013
elaborated a model for gravity modes (g-modes) under a similar framework. All these methods differ from each other in the simplifications and approximations they adopt, as well as the way turbulent convection is described. Damping, which is due to several complex non-adiabatic and turbulent processes, has been studied widely
(
Goldreich & Kumar 1991
;
Balmforth 1992a
;
Grigahcène et al. 2005
;
Belkacem et al. 2012
)
. All these formalisms ignore both rotation and magnetism.

All these studies make use of modelisation of turbulent convection, with different approximations
(
Frisch 1995
;
Tennekes & Lumley 1972
;
Lesieur 2008
)
. Homogeneous and isotropic turbulence, with several models for turbulent spectra, are commonly used. Characteristic lengths and velocities in the turbulent flow can be assessed with Mixing Length Theory (hereafter MLT), by considering these values are imposed by the eddies that carry the most heat
(
Böhm-Vitense 1958
;
Gough 1977
)
.

However, convection is strongly affected by both rotation and magnetic fields
(
Barker et al. 2014
;
Hotta 2018
)
. Convection in magnetized and rotating stars has been studied through global numerical simulations
(
Brun et al. 2004
;
Käpylä et al. 2005
;
Brown et al. 2008
;
Charbonneau 2010
;
Augustson et al. 2012
, e.g.)
. From a theoretical point of view,
Stevenson 1979
adapted the Mixing Length Theory to give a prescription for the modification of the characteristic convective velocity and wavenumber in rotating and magnetized convection.
Barker et al. 2014
;
Currie et al. 2020
have found good agreement between
Stevenson 1979
prescriptions and numerical simulations of rotating convection. Furthermore,
Augustson & Mathis 2019
generalised the theory of
Stevenson 1979
by including viscous and heat diffusions to provide with a prescription for convective penetration, which has been successfully compared with numerical simulations by
Korre & Featherstone 2021
.

In addition, magnetic fields and rotation influence waves propagation, and their coupling with excitation sources. The modes frequencies and displacement are affected, both for acoustic modes
(
Belkacem et al. 2009
)
and gravity modes
(
Mathis 2009
;
Augustson et al. 2020
)
. In the geophysics field, the only work that has studied the impact of magnetic fields on the stochastic excitation of oscillation has focused on Magneto-Archimede-Coriolis waves in the Earth core
(
Buffett & Knezek 2018
)
.

Revisiting the existing formalism to take into account rotation and magnetic fields is then necessary. In this first work, we neglect rotation and focus on the impact of magnetic field alone. We generalise the work by
Samadi & Goupil 2001
taking the magnetic field into account. First, in section
2
, we present the general set-up that describes stochastic excitation for both
g
and
p
-modes, in magnetised stars. In particular, we display the additional source terms that inject power into the modes. We assess the impact of these terms using scaling laws in section
3
. We use the outcomes of
Stevenson 1979
to account for the modification of turbulent convection by a magnetic field, and compare it critical magnetic field approach. (section
4
).
Finally, in section
5
, we assess the impact of magnetised convection on the driving and damping of oscillations. We discuss the consequences for asteroseismology, as well as the next theoretical and observational work to come (section
6
).

2
Turbulent stochastic excitation in presence of magnetic field

2.1
The inhomogeneous wave equation

2.1.1
System of equations

Following the method of
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, we derive the inhomogeneous wave equation by taking into account the Lorentz force. We use the usual spherical coordinates
\displaystyle(r,\theta,\varphi)
and the corresponding unit vector basis (
\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{e_{r}}},{\boldsymbol{e_{\theta}}},{\boldsymbol{e_{\varphi}}}
).

Here,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}
is the velocity field associated with both turbulent convective and oscillations motions.

The mass conservation equation writes:

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot{(\rho\boldsymbol{u})}=0,

(1)

where
\displaystyle\rho
is the plasma density.
The equation of momentum, while taking into account the Lorentz force boils down to:

\frac{\partial(\rho\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}:(\rho\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u})=\rho\boldsymbol{g}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}P+\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\boldsymbol{B}})\times{\boldsymbol{B}}}{\mu_{0}},

(2)

where
\displaystyle P
the pressure,
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g}
the gravitationnal field, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}
the magnetic field. As a first step, we neglect the effects of rotation.

One finally needs the magnetohydrodynamics induction equation to account for the coupling between velocity and magnetic field:

\frac{\partial B}{\partial t}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\boldsymbol{u}\times{\boldsymbol{B}})+\eta_{B}\Delta{\boldsymbol{B}},

(3)

where
\displaystyle\eta_{B}
is the Ohmic diffusivity, which we assume here to be uniform to simplify the writing.

2.1.2
Physical hypothesis and decomposition

To study the source term due to the turbulent background, we split each physical quantity
\displaystyle f
into its equilibrium value
\displaystyle f_{0}
, and an Eulerian fluctuation
\displaystyle f_{1}
. Using the Cowling approximation
Cowling 1941
(
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{g_{1}}=0
), and assuming that equilibrium quantities verify the hydrostatic equilibrium equation, we obtain the equations for the perturbations :

\frac{\partial\rho_{1}}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot[{(\rho_{0}+\rho_{1})\boldsymbol{u}}]=0,

(4)

\frac{\partial((\rho_{0}+\rho_{1})\boldsymbol{u})}{\partial t}+\boldsymbol{\nabla}:((\rho_{0}+\rho_{1})\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{u})=\rho_{1}\boldsymbol{g_{0}}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}P_{1}+\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\boldsymbol{B}})\times{\boldsymbol{B}}}{\mu_{0}}.

(5)

The perturbed equation of state, using an order 2 expansion, writes:

P_{1}=c_{s}^{2}\rho_{1}+\alpha_{s}s_{1}+\alpha_{\rho\rho}\rho_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{ss}s_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{\rho s}\rho_{1}s_{1},

(6)

where:

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial s}\right)_{\rho},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rho s}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial\rho\partial s}\right),

\displaystyle\alpha_{ss}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial s^{2}}\right)_{\rho},

\displaystyle\alpha_{\rho\rho}=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}P}{\partial\rho^{2}}\right)_{s},

\displaystyle c_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{1}P_{0}/\rho_{0}.

We introduce
\displaystyle c_{s}
the sound waves velocity and
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}=\left(\frac{\partial\ln P}{\partial\ln\rho}\right)_{s}
is the first adiabatic exponent, with
\displaystyle s
the macroscopic entropy. We assume that the oscillations follow an adiabatic evolution so that the Lagrangian entropy fluctuation is exclusively due to turbulence. We denote
\displaystyle\delta s_{1}
the Lagrangian entropy fluctuations, and
\displaystyle s_{1}
the Eulerian fluctuations. The link between the Eulerian and Lagrangian entropy fluctuations is:

\frac{d\delta s_{t}}{dt}=\frac{\partial s_{1}}{\partial t}+\left(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)(s_{0}+s_{1}).

(7)

Combining the time-derivative of Eq.(
6
) with Eq. (
7
), one finds:

\frac{\partial P_{1}}{\partial t}=\alpha_{s}\frac{d\delta s_{1}}{dt}-\alpha_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)(s_{0}+s_{1})+(c_{s}^{2}+2\alpha_{\rho\rho}\rho_{1}+\alpha_{\rho s}s_{1})\frac{\partial\rho_{1}}{\partial t}+(2\alpha_{s}s_{1}+\alpha_{\rho s}\rho_{1})\frac{\partial s_{1}}{\partial t}.

(8)

To assess the impact of turbulence on excited modes, we consider that the velocity field is composed of the oscillation velocity
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}
and the convective turbulent velocity
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}_{t}
such that:

\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{U}_{t}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}.

(9)

The magnetic field has three components: a large-scale magnetic field
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}
, a small-scale turbulent magnetic field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B_{t}}
, and a fluctuation of the magnetic field induced by the oscillation motion
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}
:

\boldsymbol{B}=\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}+\boldsymbol{B_{t}}+\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}.

(10)

It is important to note the difference between
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B_{t}}
:
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}
is the large-scale fields that vary over long timescale when compared to the oscillation period. It modifies the hydrostatic equilibrium inside the star, and thus the oscillations resonant cavity
(
Duez et al. 2010
, e.g.)
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B_{t}}
is the fluctuating part of the dynamo field and acts on small lengths and short time scales. It acts as a source term for the stochastic excitation of waves, as demonstrated in Geophysics
(
Buffett & Knezek 2018
, e.g.)
. In Eq. (
3
), the last diffusive term characterises a the decrease of the magnetic field because of the Ohmic diffusion. In a Sun-like star, the Ohmic diffusion timescale associated with the acoustic oscillations is
\displaystyle\tau_{\rm ohm,osc}=\ell_{\rm osc}^{2}/\eta_{B}\sim 10^{7}s
, where
\displaystyle\ell_{\rm osc}
is the typical lengthscale for the oscillation computed with GYRE oscillation code
(
Townsend & Teitler 2013
)
. The oscillation period is of the order of
\displaystyle\tau_{\rm osc}\sim 10^{3}s
. We then neglect the Ohmic diffusion acting on the fluctuation of the magnetic field associated with the stellar oscillations, which acts on long timescales compared to the oscillation timescale. In this framework, Eq. (
3
) simplifies into:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bigg(\boldsymbol{B}_{0}+\boldsymbol{B}_{t}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\rm osc}\bigg)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\nabla\times\bigg((\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}+\boldsymbol{U}_{t})\times(\boldsymbol{B}_{0}+\boldsymbol{B}_{t}+\boldsymbol{b}_{\rm osc})\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad+\eta_{B}\Delta^{2}(\boldsymbol{B}_{0}+\boldsymbol{B}_{t}).

(11)

To model the stochastic excitation mechanism of acoustic waves, we need to isolate the terms due to the oscillations from the ones related to the turbulent medium in order to obtain a forced wave equation. As in the previous works
(
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, e.g.)
, this leads us to make several simplifying assumptions. First, we neglect non-linear terms in oscillating quantities to recover the usual linear wave equation for acoustic modes without forcing. As a consequence, the Eulerian fluctuations of entropy and density can be seen as due only to turbulence when it comes to the forcing terms and damping terms. We then substitute
\displaystyle\rho_{1}
(resp.
\displaystyle s_{1}
) by
\displaystyle\rho_{t}
(resp.
\displaystyle s_{t}
) in these terms. Second, we suppose that the turbulent medium evolves freely and is not perturbed by the oscillations. We then assume two separate continuity equations, applied to turbulent and oscillating quantities independently:

\frac{\partial\rho_{t}}{\partial t}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left((\rho_{0}+\rho_{t})\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\right),

(12)

and:

\frac{\partial\rho_{osc}}{\partial t}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\ \boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\right).

(13)

Finally, we use the framework of incompressible turbulence (
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{U}_{t}=0
) to further simplify the modelling of the stochastic excitation.

Differentiating Eq. (
5
) with respect to time and making use of equations (
4
,
3
,
8
) while neglecting the non-linear terms in oscillating quantities yields the forced oscillation equation, also called the inhomogeneous wave equation :

\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\mathcal{L}\right)(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})+\mathcal{D}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc},\boldsymbol{U}_{t})=\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}(\boldsymbol{U}_{t},\boldsymbol{B_{t}})}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}(\boldsymbol{U}_{t},\boldsymbol{B_{t}})}{\partial t}.

(14)

Here,
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
is the linear operator which governs the propagation of stellar oscillations,
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
is the damping term,
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is the excitation source term, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}
contains the source terms that are negligible, or do not contribute to the excitation.

There is a strong physical analogy between this inhomogeneous equation and the mechanical forced mass-spring system (see fig.
1
):
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}
is the homologous of a mechanical damping, while
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is the excitation source which forces the oscillations.

:
Analogy between the forced and damped mass-spring system, and the inhomogeneous wave equation

2.1.3
Source terms

The source term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
accounts for the forcing of waves by turbulent fields. We can break it into several components to identify the various origins of the energy injection into the modes:

\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{R}+\mathcal{S}_{S}+\mathcal{S}_{B},

(15)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}
is the Reynolds-stresses source term:

\mathcal{S}_{R}=-\nabla:\Big(\rho\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\Big),

(16)

and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{S}
is an entropy fluctuations source term :

\frac{\partial\mathcal{S}_{S}}{\partial t}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{U}_{t})\boldsymbol{g}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left\{\alpha_{s}\frac{d\delta s_{t}}{dt}-\alpha_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{U}_{t}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)s_{t}-\alpha_{s}\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{U}_{t}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)s_{0}\\
-\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{U}_{t})\left[c_{s}^{2}+2\alpha_{\rho\rho}\rho_{t}+\alpha_{\rho s}s_{t}\right]+\left(2\alpha_{ss}s_{t}+\alpha_{\rho s}\rho_{t}\right)\frac{\partial s_{t}}{\partial t}\right\}.

(17)

It has been shown in recent works
(
Samadi et al. 2015
)
that this entropy source accounts for less than 10
\displaystyle\%
of the total power injected into the modes. It is then negligible in solar-like stars in a first step.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}
is the source term due to the magnetic field, i.e. the turbulent Maxwell-stresses:

\mathcal{S}_{B}=\nabla:\left(\frac{B_{t,i}B_{t,j}}{\mu_{0}}-\delta_{ij}\frac{B_{t}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}}\right),

(18)

where
\displaystyle\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol, and we use Einstein summation convention for the tensorial form of the stresses.

2.2
Linear wave operator

The linear wave operator
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
is decomposed as :

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{p}+\mathcal{L}_{g}+\mathcal{L}_{b},

(19)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{p}
corresponds to the acoustic character of the waves :

\mathcal{L}_{p}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})=-\boldsymbol{g}_{0}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\right)+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(c_{s}^{2}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\left(\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\right)\right),

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{g}
accounts for the gravity waves behavior :

\mathcal{L}_{g}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\left(\alpha_{s}\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}s_{0}\right),

(21)

and
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{b}
is the Alfvén part of the waves:

\mathcal{L}_{B}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc},\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}})=\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\Bigg[\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}}}\bigg)\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\times\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}}\bigg)-\left(\boldsymbol{\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\times\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}}\bigg)+\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\times\bigg(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}}\bigg)\\
+\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bigg((\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}})\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\bigg)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bigg(\left(\boldsymbol{\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\bigg)}+\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bigg(\left(\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nabla}\right)\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\bigg)}-\eta_{B}\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}\times\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bigg(\Delta\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\bigg)}\Bigg].

(22)

Note that the oscillation velocity
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}
is coupled to the oscillation magnetic field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}
, via the linearised induction equation.

2.2.1
Damping

The damping term can be written as:

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}_{0}+\mathcal{D}_{B}.

(23)

The term
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{0}
is due to the non-magnetic part of the inhomogeneous wave equation, which can be recovered as in previous works such as
Samadi 2012
,
Belkacem et al. 2008
:

\mathcal{D}_{0}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc},\boldsymbol{U}_{t})=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}(\rho_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})-\mathcal{L}_{0}(\rho_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})+2\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\text{: }\rho\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\right)\\
+\nabla\bigg[-(2\alpha_{\rho\rho}\rho_{t}+\alpha_{\rho s}s_{t})\left(\nabla\cdot(\rho_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})\right)\\
-\nabla\cdot(\alpha_{s}s_{t}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})+\alpha_{s}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\cdot\nabla)s_{t}+s_{t}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}\cdot\nabla)\alpha_{s}\bigg],

(24)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathcal{L}_{p}+\mathcal{L}_{g}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{B}
is the magnetic contribution to the damping :

\mathcal{D}_{B}(\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}},\boldsymbol{B_{t}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\boldsymbol{B_{t}}})\times\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}}{\mu_{0}}+\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}})\times\boldsymbol{B_{t}}}{\mu_{0}}\right).

(25)

2.2.2
Negligible source terms

The term
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}}{\partial t}
contains all source terms that are negligible:

\frac{\partial\mathcal{C}}{\partial t}=\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\bar{B}})\times\boldsymbol{B_{t}}}{\mu_{0}}+\frac{(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times{\boldsymbol{B_{t}}})\times\bar{B}}{\mu_{0}}+\mathcal{L}(\rho\boldsymbol{U}_{t})-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t}(\rho\boldsymbol{U}_{t})-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla\text{: }\rho_{t}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\right)+\\
\nabla\left[-(2\alpha_{\rho\rho}+\alpha_{\rho s}s_{t})\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot(\rho\boldsymbol{U}_{t})-(2\alpha_{ss}s_{t}+2\alpha_{\rho s}\rho_{t})\frac{\partial s_{t}}{\partial t}-\alpha_{s}\boldsymbol{U}_{t}\cdot\nabla s_{t}\right].

(26)

It contains linear source terms in turbulent quantities, which do not contribute to the excitation, as well as terms that are negligible due to their order of magnitude
(see e.g.
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, for more details)
.

2.3
Modes amplitudes

We seek the mean square amplitude of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}
, for each mode, of given radial, angular and azimuthal order
\displaystyle(n,\ell,m
respectively). The wave Eulerian displacement is written in complex notations as the product between an instantaneous amplitude
\displaystyle A(t)
, and a Lagrangian eigendisplacement
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r})
, which depends only on the position
(
Samadi & Goupil 2001
;
Belkacem et al. 2009
)
:

\delta\boldsymbol{r}_{osc}(\boldsymbol{r},t)=\frac{1}{2}A(t)\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r})e^{i\omega_{0}t}+c.c.

(27)

We introduce
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
the eigenmode frequency, and
c. c.
denotes the complex conjugate. Deriving Eq. (
27
) with respect to time leads to the wave velocity:

\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}(\boldsymbol{r},t)=A(t)i\omega_{0}\boldsymbol{\xi}(\boldsymbol{r},t)e^{i\omega_{0}t},

(28)

where we consider that
\displaystyle A(t)
is evolving at a longer timescale than the wave oscillation period. Using Eqs. (
14
) and (
28
), one derives the mean squared amplitude of the oscillations:

\left\langle|A|^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{8\eta_{D}\left(\omega_{0}I\right)^{2}}\int d^{3}x_{0}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d^{3}rd\tau e^{-i\omega_{0}\tau}\left\langle(\mathbf{\xi}\cdot\mathcal{S})_{1}(\mathbf{\xi}\cdot\mathcal{S})_{2}\right\rangle.

(29)

Subscripts 1 and 2 make reference to spatio-temporal positions
\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{x}_{0}-\frac{\mathbf{r}}{2},-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)
and
\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{x}_{0}+\frac{\mathbf{r}}{2},\frac{\tau}{2}\right)
respectively.

\displaystyle\langle.\rangle
denotes a statistical average performed on an infinite number of independent realisations. We introduce
\displaystyle\eta_{D}
as damping, and
\displaystyle I
the mode inertia:

I=\int_{0}^{M}\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\star}dm,

(30)

where
\displaystyle dm
is the mass of an elementary fluid parcel.
Furthermore, we assume turbulence is stationary and homogeneous so that the source term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
is invariant by any time translation. Although turbulence in magnetohydrodynamics is known to be anisotropic, this assumption is justified by the fact that most of the excitation is due to small-scale eddies
(
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, see e.g.)
.

There are several contributions to the excitation power:
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}^{2}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}^{2}
correspond to the power injected by the Reynolds and Maxwell stresses, respectively. A cross-term also emerges with the combinations in
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}\mathcal{S}_{R}
.

Figure
2
illustrates the complex interdependencies between magnetic field and modes’amplitudes: not only does the magnetic field add up a source term compared with the non-magnetised case, but it also influences indirectly the existing source terms:

1.

The stellar structure is modified by the magnetic force
(
Duez et al. 2010
, see e.g.)
. The star’s density and thermodynamic quantities profiles, and in turn, the mode frequencies change. This is the so-called indirect effect on stellar oscillations
(
Gough & Thompson 1990
)
.

2.

Waves are affected by the magnetic field so that they become magneto-acoustic-gravity waves. This changes both the frequencies of the modes
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
which are shifted, and the eigenfunctions of the displacement
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}
. This is the direct magnetic effect on stellar oscillations
(
Gough & Thompson 1990
)
.

3.

The characteristics of convection strongly affect the source terms. This happens through a change in the mean parameters from MLT such as the convective wavenumber
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{k}_{0}
and the convective velocity
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
. Magnetic field tends to modify convection, by changing the instability threshold
(
Chandrasekhar 1961
)
. The magnetic field also affects turbulent convection through a change in the kinetic energy spectrum and magnetic energy spectrum.
(
Brun 2004
, e.g.)
. This effect of magnetic field on convection is paramount in the present model, and will be widely discussed in Section
4
.

This complex interplay between magnetic field and excitation has not been studied in the literature. To give a first overview of the impact of magnetic fields on the stochastic excitation of modes, we will as a first step make use of scaling laws for modulation of the modes amplitudes as a function of the strength of the magnetic field. Such scalings prescriptions could be directly compared to observations. We first adopt a Mixing Length Theory approach to model convection.

3
Scaling laws for the power injected into the modes

To give a very first overview of the impact of magnetism on the stochastic excitation of waves, we make use of scaling laws to evaluate the modes amplitudes. Indeed, no physical model up to this date includes the effects of magnetic fields. Scaling relationships have proven to give relevant tendencies and are often used in the litterature, for example in dynamo theories
(
Augustson et al. 2019
, e.g.)
.
When it comes to p-modes, the energy provided by the stochastic excitation is mostly injected very locally at a radius
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
, in the photosphere
(
Samadi & Goupil 2001
, e.g.)
. As gravity modes are evanescent in the convective zone, they are mostly excited at the base of the convective zone. Under this assumption, eq. (
29
) boils down to:

\displaystyle\left\langle|A|^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{8\eta_{D}\left(\omega_{0}I\right)^{2}}\int d^{3}x_{0}\times

(31)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{i})rd^{3}\delta d\tau e^{-i\omega_{0}\tau}\left\langle(\mathbf{\xi}\cdot\mathcal{S})_{1}(\mathbf{\xi}\cdot\mathcal{S})_{2}\right\rangle,

where
\displaystyle\delta
is the usual Dirac function.

We neglect in the present model the influence of the magnetic field on the eigenfunctions
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}
, which means that
\displaystyle\omega_{0}\gg\omega_{A}
, where
\displaystyle\omega_{A}
is the Alfvén pulsation. Under this hypothesis, one can use estimates of the source
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}
and the damping
\displaystyle\eta_{D}
at the location
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{i}
to evaluate the resulting amplitudes of the acoustic modes. Indeed, for a given mode, one has:

\langle\lvert A\rvert\rangle^{2}\propto\frac{\mathcal{S}^{2}}{\eta_{D}}.

(32)

3.1
Scaling of the source terms

First, we estimate the scaling relationship of the source terms. As the entropy source term
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{s}
is negligible, we only consider the Reynolds-stresses
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{R}
, and the Maxwell-stresses
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}
source terms.

We introduce
\displaystyle\ell_{osc}
, which is the typical length of the oscillations. By integrating by part eq. (
29
), all the spatial gradients of the source terms Eqs. (
16
) and (
18
) can be transferred to the oscillations, as detailed in
Samadi & Goupil 2001
;
Belkacem et al. 2009
.
Furthermore, the eddies that contribute the most to the stochastic excitation are the ones whose turnover time is near the wave frequency
\displaystyle\tau_{\rm turnover}\sim\omega_{0}
. For this reason, we consider any time derivative amounts to a multiplication by a factor
\displaystyle\omega_{0}
, and any spatial derivative amounts to a multiplication by
\displaystyle 1/\ell_{\rm osc}
.

We introduce
\displaystyle u_{\lambda}
, the velocity of a given convective eddy, of size
\displaystyle\lambda
. We also introduce
\displaystyle u_{c}
, the MLT convective velocity, which is the velocity of the largest eddy of the turbulent cascade, which has a size
\displaystyle\Lambda
. Similarly,
\displaystyle b_{\lambda}
is the magnetic field associated with a given eddy of size
\displaystyle\lambda
, while
\displaystyle B_{0}
is the magnetic field associated with the largest eddy of size
\displaystyle\Lambda
.

In this framework, the Reynolds-stresses source term scales like:

\mathcal{S}_{R}\sim\frac{-\rho_{0}u_{\lambda}^{2}}{\ell_{osc}};

(33)

and the Maxwell-stresses source term scales like:

\mathcal{S}_{B}\sim\frac{b_{\lambda}^{2}}{2\mu_{0}\ell_{osc}}.

(34)

Furthermore, scaling relationships are often used in turbulence to link
\displaystyle u_{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle u_{c}
. For a given slope
\displaystyle\alpha
for the kinetic energy spectrum, one has
(
Tennekes & Lumley 1972
, see e.g. Chapter 1 of)
:

u_{\lambda}\sim u_{c}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda}\right)^{(\alpha-1)/2}.

(35)

The same can be applied to the magnetic cascade:

b_{\lambda}\sim B_{0}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\Lambda}\right)^{(\alpha-1)/2}.

(36)

In Eqs. (
35
)-(
36
), we assume that the magnetic energy spectrum and the kinetic energy spectrum have the same slope
\displaystyle\alpha
. This is the case for an Iroshnikov-Kraichman spectrum in magnetohydrodynamic turbulence, for which the slope is
\displaystyle\alpha=-3/2
, both for the kinetic and the magnetic energy spectra
(
Iroshnikov 1964
;
Kraichnan 1965
)
. Depending on the turbulent regimes in magnetohydrodynamics, different values can be found for these spectra, as seen in experiments and direct numerical simulations.
(
Sommeria 1986
;
Biskamp & Müller 2000
;
Mininni & Pouquet 2009
, see e.g.)
.
Finally, using equations (
33
)-(
36
) when taking magnetic fields into account, the resulting source
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm mag}=\mathcal{S}_{B}+\mathcal{S}_{R}
scales like:

\mathcal{S}_{\rm mag}\sim-\mathcal{S}_{R}\left(1-\frac{V_{A}^{2}}{2u_{c}^{2}}\right),

(37)

where we have introduced the Alfvén velocity:

V_{A}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{0}\mu_{0}}}.

(38)

As highlighted in Fig.
2
, a magnetic field also has an indirect effect on the stochastic excitation as it influences convection. The convective velocity
\displaystyle u_{c}
must then be evaluated in the framework of magnetised convection, and is then expected to be potentially weaker than in non-magnetised convection.

3.2
Scaling of the damping rate

As shown in Eq. (
29
), knowing the damping coefficient
\displaystyle\eta_{D}
is paramount when it comes to assessing the modes mean amplitudes (see Fig.
1
). This coefficient is proportional to the wave’s loss of energy. We consider in a first step that damping comes from two phenomena: a turbulent viscous dissipation of the wave, and its Ohmic dissipation. The convective zone of solar-like stars is known to be a turbulent medium. The molecular viscosity is then negligible in front of the turbulent eddies viscosity:
\displaystyle\nu\sim\nu_{\rm turb}
. However, more complex formalisms have emerged taking into account the non-adiabatic fluctuations of density, entropy and turbulent pressure inside the star
(
Grigahcène et al. 2005
;
Belkacem et al. 2012
, e.g.)
. To compare hydrodynamical and magnetic effects we restrict ourselves as a first step to the simplest eddy viscosity and Ohmic diffusivity modeling.

The volumic loss of energy due to a turbulent viscous dissipation for the wave is:

\mathcal{D}_{vis}=\rho_{0}\nu_{t}(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc})^{2},

(39)

where
\displaystyle\nu_{t}
is the eddy viscosity. The turbulent viscosity scales like:

\nu_{\rm turb}\sim\frac{\ell_{c}u_{c}}{3},

(40)

where
\displaystyle\ell_{c}
is the convective characteristic length in MLT.

The volumic loss of energy due to Ohmic dissipation for the wave is

\mathcal{D}_{ohm}=\mu_{0}\eta_{B}\boldsymbol{j}_{osc}^{2},

(41)

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}_{osc}
is the current density associated with the wave,
\displaystyle\mu_{0}
the vacuum magnetic permeability and
\displaystyle\eta_{B}
the magnetic diffusivity.

Using the same notations and the same methodology as in Subsection
3.1
, we find:

\mathcal{D}_{vis}\sim\frac{\rho_{0}\nu_{\rm turb}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}^{2}}{\ell_{osc}^{2}}.

(42)

We make use of the induction equation (
3
), while neglecting the diffusive term, to relate the wave’s magnetic field
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{b_{\rm osc}}
and the large-scale equilibrium magnetic field
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}
. We find :

\boldsymbol{b}_{\rm osc}=\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times(\xi\times\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}).

(43)

Furthermore:

\xi\sim\frac{\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}}{\omega_{0}}.

(44)

As
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{j}_{\rm osc}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{b}_{\rm osc}}{\mu_{0}}
from Maxwell-Ampere equation, we derive the scaling relationship for the Ohmic dissipation:

\mathcal{D}_{\rm ohm}\sim\frac{\eta_{B}\boldsymbol{u}_{\rm osc}^{2}\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}^{2}}{\omega_{0}^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}\ell_{\rm osc}^{4}}.

(45)

Moreover, we consider that the magnetic field at the injection scale in the turbulent cascade comes from the large-scale magnetic field, such that:

\boldsymbol{B}_{0}\sim\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}.

(46)

One can express the characteristic oscillation length with the dispersion relation for acoustic waves:

\ell_{osc}\sim\frac{c_{s}}{\omega_{0}},

(47)

where
\displaystyle c_{s}
is the local sound speed.
When taking magnetism into account, the resulting damping contribution is
\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rm mag}=\mathcal{D}_{\rm ohm}+\mathcal{D}_{\rm vis}
. We have:

\mathcal{D}_{mag}\sim\mathcal{D}_{vis}\left(1+\frac{A^{2}\mathcal{M}^{2}}{\mathcal{P}_{m}}\right),

(48)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{m}\equiv\nu/\eta_{B}
is the magnetic Prandtl number, and
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\equiv u_{c}/c_{s}
is the local Mach number. We introduced the dimensionless inverse Alfvén number, which corresponds to the ratio between magnetic energy and convective kinetic energy:

\displaystyle A=\frac{V_{A}^{2}}{u_{c}^{2}}=\frac{B_{0}^{2}}{\rho_{0}\mu_{0}u_{c}^{2}}.

(49)

Again, the convective velocity
\displaystyle u_{c}
depends on the strength of the magnetic field. To assess the amplitudes of the mean modes appropriately, one needs a proper modelling of magnetised convection.

4
Magnetised convection

4.1
Modelling magnetised convection

Magnetic fields modify convection, which in turn impacts the excitation sources and damping. The impact of rotation and magnetic fields on convection is of keen interest for stellar and planetary evolution
(
Maeder 2009
, see e.g.)
. The most realistic approach to modelling stars and planets
convective zone is to solve the underlying equations numerically
(
Brown et al. 2011
;
Brun & Browning 2017
;
Brun et al. 2022
, e.g.)
.

However, as the computational resources available are finite, a local model of convection is often used, particularly for stellar structure modelling and secular evolution. Pioneering studies by
Chandrasekhar 1961
and
Canuto & Mazzitelli 1991
showed that both rotation alone and magnetic fields alone tend to inhibit the convection’strength. However, rotation and magnetic field acting together tend to destabilise convection with respect to the case of a magnetised but no-rotating fluid or a rotating but non-magnetised fluid
(
Horn & Aurnou 2022
)
. The interplay between these two phenomena is complex. In this first work, we focus on the case of the sole magnetic field.
There are two main theoretical approaches to account for the impact of magnetic fields on convection: an instability criterion approach and the Mixing-Length Theory approach.

4.1.1
Instability criterion.

Knowing under which conditions a star is unstable to convection is key.
Schwarzschild 1906
and
Ledoux 1947
derived instability criteria for convection to start, which are used in stellar models. As explained before, convection is stabilised by magnetic fields. At some point, if the external magnetic field is too high, the fluid becomes stable to convection: we consider that convection is frozen in this case
(
Gough & Tayler 1966
)
. The value of the minimum magnetic field that freezes convection is the critical magnetic field
\displaystyle B_{\rm crit}
. This approach is an
”on-off”
approach to magnetoconvection: either the external magnetic field
\displaystyle B
is below
\displaystyle B_{\rm crit}
and convection is active, or
\displaystyle B>B_{\rm crit}
and there is no more convection (see the illustration Fig.
3(a)
). It is often used in stellar physics to tackle strongly magnetised stars
(
Jermyn & Cantiello 2020
, see e.g.)
.

4.1.2
Mixing-Length Theory

Modelling turbulent convection is quite complex due to a large number of space and time scales. Mixing-Length Theory (hereafter MLT)
(
Böhm-Vitense 1958
;
Gough 1977
)
models convection with a single length and velocity scale, which corresponds to the characteristics of the most energetic convective eddy. Currently, MLT is implemented in 1-D stellar evolution codes to provide with the main properties of stellar convection zones.

The critical magnetic field approach described in the previous section does not account for the progressive diminution of the convection’strength when increasing an external magnetic field. When increasing the magnetic field, the turbulent eddies become smaller and smaller, and their characteristic velocity diminishes.
Stevenson 1979
derived a prescription accounting for the modification of Mixing-Length Theory by an external magnetic field, rendering the progressive reduction of the convection’strength (see Fig.
3(b)
).

In the following subsections, we discuss the theory behind those two approaches and their impact on the stochastic excitation of acoustic modes.

4.2
Critical magnetic field

In non-magnetised fluids,
Schwarzschild 1906
derived an instability criterion for convection to begin:

\nabla-\nabla_{\rm ad}>0,

(50)

where the temperature gradient is:

\nabla\equiv\frac{d\ln T}{d\ln P},

(51)

and the adiabatic temperature gradient is:

\left.\nabla_{\mathrm{ad}}\equiv\frac{\partial\ln T}{\partial\ln P}\right|_{s}.

(52)

To take into account the effects of magnetic fields,
Gough & Tayler 1966
extended the previous criterion considering an ideal gas with a vertical magnetic field. They showed that convection exists if:

\nabla-\nabla_{ad}>\delta,

(53)

where:

\delta=\frac{V_{A}^{2}}{V_{A}^{2}+c_{s}^{2}}.

(54)

\displaystyle V_{A}
is the Alfvén velocity introduced previously, and
\displaystyle c_{s}
is the sound speed. Both Schwarzschild (Eq.
50
) and Gough & Tayler (Eq.
53
) criteria make use of the energy principle of
Bernstein 1958
: they study the change of potential energy
\displaystyle\delta W
of any fluid element under a small perturbation. Necessary and sufficient conditions for stability are obtained by minimizing
\displaystyle\delta W
with respect to all the possible perturbations.
One can then assess the critical magnetic field, above which convection is suppressed:

B_{crit}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{0}\rho c_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}\left(\nabla-\nabla_{\mathrm{ad}}\right)}{1-\left(\nabla-\nabla_{\mathrm{ad}}\right)}}.

(55)

Gough & Tayler 1966
’s work has been pursued by
Newcomb 1961
, who showed that a horizontal magnetic field does not influence convection.
Moreno-Insertis & Spruit 1989
,
MacDonald & Mullan 2009
,
MacDonald & Petit 2019
generalised Gough-Tayler criterion to take into account molecular mass variations
\displaystyle\mu
, non-ideal gas behavior, and radiation pressure. For simplicity, we will use Eq. (
53
) derived by
Gough & Tayler 1966
in the following. In this framework, the critical inverse Alfvén number above which convection stops is:

A_{crit}\equiv\frac{B_{crit}^{2}}{u_{c}^{2}\mu_{0}\rho_{0}}=\frac{-c_{s}^{2}(\nabla-\nabla_{ad})}{u_{c}^{2}(1-\nabla+\nabla_{ad})}.

(56)

Once again, as shown in Fig.
3
, the critical magnetic field approach fails to account for the progressive variations in the convective velocity
\displaystyle u_{c}
. To model more precisely the stochastic excitation of modes as seen in Section
3
, one needs a more precise approach to magnetised convection. Here, we consider the Magnetic Mixing-Length Theory, as proposed by
Stevenson 1979
.

4.3
Magnetic Mixing-Length Theory (M-MLT)

Using a linear stability analysis of the convective instability,
Stevenson 1979
derives scaling laws for the way the convective characteristic velocity and the convective characteristic wavenumber are modified by magnetic fields. This study was made under the hypothesis that the dominant convective mode is the one that carries the most energy
(
Malkus & Chandrasekhar 1997
)
.
Stevenson 1979
derives a modulation factor in each case: the convective velocity (resp. convective wavenumber) with magnetism
\displaystyle u_{c}
(resp.
\displaystyle k_{c}
) is expressed with respect to the convective velocity (resp. convective wavenumber) without magnetism
\displaystyle u_{0}
(resp.
\displaystyle k_{0}
):

\displaystyle u_{c}=\tilde{U}(A)u_{0}

(57)

\displaystyle k_{c}=\tilde{K}(A)k_{0}.

Stevenson 1979
derives scalings for the functions
\displaystyle\tilde{U}(A)
and
\displaystyle\tilde{K}(A)
for the asymptotic limits
\displaystyle A\ll 1
(low magnetic field) and
\displaystyle A\gg 1
(high magnetic field). His prescriptions are detailed in Table

As expected, the convective velocity decreases when the magnetic field increases: the magnetic field has a stabilising effect on convection. Moreover, the convective wavenumber rises when the magnetic field is stronger. The size of the dominant eddy in MLT framework then diminishes, as previously illustrated in Fig.

.

, there is a discontinuity at
\displaystyle A=1,28
. In this model, the damping slightly influences the modulation of the modes amplitude, but the overall tendency is dominated by the variations of the source terms
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
. For low values of
\displaystyle A
, and more strongly between
\displaystyle A=10^{-1}
and
\displaystyle A=1/2
the magnetic field tends to inhibit the source of the stochastic excitation. Under
\displaystyle A=10^{-4}
, there is nearly no change because the Maxwell-stresses source term is too low. For higher values of
\displaystyle A
, there is an increase in the excitation. As
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{B}/\mathcal{S}_{R}\sim A/2
so for
\displaystyle A>2
, the Maxwell-stresses become higher than the Reynolds-stresses and the magnetic source term dominates the stochastic excitation.

This tendency is qualitatively in agreement with the observations of
Garcia et al. 2010
: the authors showed how the p-modes amplitudes were modulated along the magnetic cycle of a Sun-like star, HD49933. They used the frequency shifts induced by the magnetic field to assess the magnetic activity of the star. They observed that the stronger the magnetic field, the lower the modes amplitudes (and vice-versa). This is the tendency we predict as well, using these scaling laws.

The Sun’s large-scale magnetic field at the surface is
\displaystyle B_{surf}\sim 4G-8G
, depending on the moment of the magnetic cycle). It corresponds to an inverse Alfvén number between
\displaystyle A=7\cdot 10^{-4}
and
\displaystyle A=1\cdot 10^{-3}
: in this range,
\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{2}(A)
is close to one so the magnetic field slightly inhibits the excitation source, but one should not expect a significant change.

Figure 6
:
Scaling as a function of the inverse Alfvén number
\displaystyle A
. We clearly notice that the higher the magnetic field, the lower the source term, until
\displaystyle A=1.28
. From this value, the magnetic Maxwell-stresses become the dominant excitation mechanism: the resulting injected power increases. However, the
\displaystyle A
parameter in stars is below 1, so we expect to be always in the regime where the excitation is inhibited by the magnetic field.

6
Conclusion and perspectives

This work sheds light on the stochastic excitation of stellar oscillations when a magnetic field is present. It draws a first picture of the power injected into the modes considering both the magnetic source term that emerges, the Maxwell stresses source term, and the modification of convection by the magnetic field. After generalising the forced wave equation with a magnetic field, we provide scaling laws for the excitation source and the damping, making use of
Stevenson 1979
’s prescriptions in the simplified framework of the Magnetic Mixing-Length Theory. Moreover, we used a 1-D Solar model to show that Stevenson’s prescriptions for magnetized convection are in good agreement with the existing theory of a critical magnetic field above which convection is frozen. We then demonstrated that the modes amplitudes tend to decrease with the magnetic field intensity for p-modes. It could explain the observations in
García & Ballot 2019
where some modes amplitudes variations were detected following a magnetic cycle: the lower the magnetic activity, the higher the modes amplitudes and vice-versa. Therefore, it could explain why p-modes are not detected in stars with a high level of magnetic activity as this has been observed for solar-type stars observed by the Kepler mission
(
Mathur et al. 2019
)
. Such scaling laws should be confronted with the observations of the full
Kepler
sample: an approach combining both 1-D stellar evolution modelling to access the inverse Alfvén number parameter, and observational data of p-modes will be relevant. Regarding the results presented in
Mathur et al. 2019
, they are presented as a function of the stellar rotation period and of the magnetic activity index,
\displaystyle S_{ph}
. In a near future, it would be important to establish the relationship between
\displaystyle S_{ph}
and the magnetic field
\displaystyle B
, and in turn the inverse Alfvén number
\displaystyle A
to compare the data with our theoretical prediction. To go further scaling laws, analytical work is ongoing to build on this theoretical formalism and render more precisely the contribution of different sources, using a spectral description of magneto-hydrodynamic turbulence. Such work will help model more precisely the stochastic excitation in the presence of magnetic field.

Our work will be extended and adapted to tackle the stochastic excitation of gravity waves
(
Lecoanet & Quataert 2013
)
, as well as magneto-gravito-inertial waves
(
Mathis & de Brye 2011
;
Rui & Fuller 2023
;
Rui et al. 2024
)
. These waves are excited at the base of the convective zone, as they are evanescent in the convective zone. Understanding their propagation and excitation is paramount, as they are one of the best candidates for the strong angular momentum transport needed in stellar radiative zones to reproduce the observed internal rotation revealed in stars by helio and asteroseismology
(
Schatzman 1993
;
Zahn et al. 1997
;
Rogers et al. 2013
, see e.g.)
. It would in turn yield new insights into the rotational profiles and internal chemical mixing of rotating and magnetic stars.

Finally, the magnetic field generated by the dynamo effect strongly depends on rotation
(
Brun & Browning 2017
, e.g.)
: one must then study the combined impact of rotation and magnetic field on the stochastic excitation of stellar oscillations by turbulent convection. This would allow us to give more accurate predictions of the excitation of waves in both rotating and magnetised stars.
