Zero-divisor graphs and zero-divisor functors
Enrico Sbarra, Maurizio Zanardo

Abstract
. Inspired by a very recent work of A. Đurić, S. Jevđenić and N. Stopar, we introduce a new definition of zero-divisor graphs attached to rings, that includes all of the classical definitions already known in the literature. We provide an interpretation of such graphs as images of a functor, that we call zero-divisor functor and which is associated with a family of special equivalence relations fixed beforehand. We thus recover and generalize many known results for zero-divisor graphs and provide a framework which might be useful for further investigations on this topic.

Primary 05C25; Secondary 18A30, 13P25

Introduction

Throughout this paper a ring will be a unitary commutative ring.
The notion of zero-divisor graph was introduced for the first time by Beck in 1988, see
[
B88
]
, with the purpose of investigating the structure of the underlying ring. Given a ring
A
, Beck’s zero-divisor graph
G(A)
is a graph whose vertex set
V(A)
is the set of all elements of
A
and the set of edges
E(A)
is determined by the following zero-divisor relation on the elements of
A
: two vertices
a,b\in V(A)=A
are adjacent if and only if
a
and
b
satisfy the relation
ab=0
in
A
. The main concern in
[
B88
]
was to study the chromatic and clique numbers of
G(A)
. Shortly after, in
[
AL99
]
, Anderson and Livingston introduced a slight modification of the definition of zero-divisor graph, called
\Gamma(A)
, with the purpose of simplifying
G(A)
and its visualization without losing the relevant pieces of information therein contained; their definition of the graph is considered as classical in the literature. Many properties of
\Gamma(A)
, such as connectedness, see
[
CSSS12
]
, and its invariants, as for instance diameter, girth, and chromatic number, have been later studied in
[
AM04
]
and
[
AL99
]
. These papers also address the problem of finding rings
A
whose zero-divisor graph belongs to special classes of graphs, such as star graphs, complete graphs,
r
-partite complete graphs, planar graphs etc., see also
[
AMY03
]
.

Since
\Gamma(A)
can have very many edges even when
A
is finite of small cardinality, in
[
M02
]
Mulay introduced a new relation, which we denote
\asymp_{A}
, on the elements of
A
, defined as follows: given
a,b\in A
, one lets
a\asymp_{A}b
and, therefore,
a,b
are adjacent as vertices of
\Gamma(A)
, if and only if
\Ann(a)=\Ann(b)
. The resulting graph, denoted by
\Gamma_{E}(A)
, has been later referred to as
compressed zero-divisor graph
, see
[
AL12
]
.
Thus, the purpose of
\Gamma_{E}(A)
is to simplify further the original graph by “compressing” it or, in other words, by diminishing the “noise”, i.e. the redundancy, given by those elements of
A
which essentially behave in the same fashion. Compressed zero-divisor graphs have been subsequently studied by several authors, cf. for instance
[
SW11
]
,
[
CSSS12
]
and
[
AL12
]
.

In a very recent paper
[
DJS21
]
, Đurić, Jevđenić and Stopar propose a new approach to the study of zero-divisor graphs, by looking at functorial properties they might be endowed with. They define a functor, called
\Theta
, from the category of finite rings to that of graphs with loops; the image of a ring
A
is a compressed zero-divisor graph
\Theta(A)
, whose set of vertices is given by
A/\hskip-5.69046pt\sim_{A}
, where
a\sim_{A}b
if and only if
(a)=(b)
is the same ideal of
A
, and where two equivalence classes
[a],[b]\in A/\hskip-5.69046pt\sim_{A}
, i.e. vertices of
\Theta(A)
, are adjacent if and only if
ab=0
in
A
.

The starting point of our work is to develop one of the main ideas in
[
DJS21
]
and the leitmotif of the first part of this paper is to search for equivalence relations that are a good compromise between having functorial properties and producing as little noise as possible on zero-divisor graphs, for instance preserving their compression. From this perspective, one may wonder which are the appropriate coarsest, i.e .minimal, equivalence relations such that the associated zero-divisor functor satisfies a certain property. Another interesting question is to study what happens if we introduce a degree of freedom into the problem by letting each ring to be endowed with its own equivalence relation.

We introduce some new definitions which provide the right framework to analyze and, in some cases, to provide answers to such questions; we do so in the central part of the paper which contains our main results. We have illustrated an application of our approach in the last part of the paper, where we study which rings
(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
have a finite associated zero-divisor graph
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
. Since every artinian ring can be written as finite product of local artinian rings, we choose as a good family of equivalence relations
\{\sim_{A}\}
; in this way the associated functor preserves finite products in both directions and this allows us to provide a partial structure theorem for rings
A
such that

\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\sim_{A})
is finite.

After reviewing a few preliminaries on graphs in Section
1
and the classical definitions of zero-divisor graphs in the literature in Section
2
.1, we look at which are the significant properties that an adjacency relation must have to define a zero-divisor graph. We call such relations zero-divisor relations, see Definition
2.2
and we observe in Proposition
2.4
that a zero-divisor relation
\mathcal{R}_{A}
is just an equivalence relation which is finer than
\asymp_{A}
. In this way, given a ring
A
and a zero-divisor relation
\mathcal{R}_{A}
, we define a zero-divisor graph
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
. Choosing the right
\mathcal{R}_{A}
, by means of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
and of its strong quotients we can recover the definitions of the zero-divisor graphs studied so far. We prove in Theorem
2.6
that the compressed zero-divisor graphs associated with
\mathcal{R}_{A}
, cf. Definition
2.3
, are connected independently of the chosen relation, generalizing
[
AL99
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
SW11
, Proposition 1.4]
, see also
[
M02
]
.

Next, we proceed to make
Z
into a functor from the category of commutative rings
CRing
to that of graphs with loops
Graph
; we do this in Section
3
.1. We consider each object
A\in\textbf{CRing}
associated with a fixed relation
\mathcal{R}_{A}
, i.e. we fix a family
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
of equivalence relations. We define first the key property we need on a family
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
of zero-divisor relations, see Definition
3.1
, and provide some examples. We then define its associated zero-divisor functor
\texttt{\large{Z}}\,:\,\textbf{CRing}\longrightarrow\textbf{Graph}
,
(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})\mapsto\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
, see Definition
3.8
.

We spend the rest of Section
3
in investigating the properties of the newly defined functor
Z
. We first show under which conditions on
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
the associated functor
Z
preserves binary products and equalizers, see Propositions
3.11
and
3.15
, and conclude that the families
\{=_{A}\}
,
\{\approx_{A}\}
and
\{\sim_{A}\}
preserve finite products, cf. Corollary
3.12
, whereas
\{=_{A}\}
is the only one which preserves equalizers, and thus all finite limits. Corollary
3.12
also extends to the case of infinite rings
[
DJS21
, Proposition 3.4]
, see Corollary
3.13
. Section
3
.4 contains one of the main results of this paper, Theorem
3.20
and its consequence Corollary
3.22
, which we call Inversion of Product. There we show under which conditions on
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
the decomposition of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
as a finite direct product of graphs leads to a decomposition of
A
as a finite direct product of rings. This corrects and extends to the case of infinite rings the main result
[
DJS21
, Theorem 3.6]
.

Finally, in the last section, we apply the main results of Section
3
to study the case when
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)=\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
is finite, focusing on the case
\mathcal{R}_{A}=\sim_{A}
, which turns out to be of main interest,
as we observe at the beginning of the section. We prove in Theorem
4.1
that
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is finite exaclty when
A
is the direct product of an artinian PIR with a finite ring. Moreover, we prove that local artinian PIRs are characterized as those rings
A
for which
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is a staircase graph. Finally, thanks to the Inversion of Product Theorem, we characterize artinian PIRs as those rings whose
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is a finite product of staircase graphs, see Proposition
4.6
and Theorem
4.8
, which extend to the case of infinite rings the main results of
[
DJS21
]
, Section 4 and 5.

1.
Preliminaries

All the rings we consider are unitary and commutative, and all rings homomorphisms
\varphi\,:\,A\longrightarrow B
map
1_{A}
to
1_{B}
. We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{A}
the family of ideals of
A
and by
\mathcal{PI}_{A}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{A}
the subfamily of principal ideals.
By
\mathcal{D}(A)
and
\mathcal{N}(A)
we denote the set of zero-divisors and the set of nilpotents of
A
, respectively. We also let
\mathcal{D}^{*}(A):=\mathcal{D}(A)\smallsetminus\{0\}
. By
A^{\times}
we denote the set of invertible elements of
A
and by
\Ann(a)
the ideal
0:a
, with
a\in A
.

We recall next some definition and notation from Graph Theory, and we refer the reader to
[
K11
]
for further details. A
graph

G
is an ordered couple
(V(G),E(G))
, where
V=V(G)
is a set,
the vertex set of
G
and
E=E(G)
is a binary symmetric relation on
V^{2}
, called
the edge set
of
G
.
Given a vertex
v\in V
, we call
N(v)=\{w\in V\,:\,(v,w)\in E\}

the set of adjacents of
v
; we call
degree
of
v
, and denote it by
\deg(v)
, the cardinality of
N(v)
. We say that
v\in V

has a loop
if
v\in N(v)
and, when this is the case we call
(v,v)\in E
a
loop
.

Let
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
be graphs. A
graph (homo)morphism

\varphi\,:\,G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}
is a map
V(G_{1})\to V(G_{2})
such that
\big((x,y)\in E(G_{1})\Rightarrow(\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\in E(G_{2})
, for all
x,y\in V(G_{1})\big)
. Similarly, a
graph comorphism

\varphi:G_{1}\rightarrow G_{2}
is a map
V(G_{1})\to V(G_{2})
such that

\big((\varphi(x),\varphi(y))\in E(G_{2})\Rightarrow(x,y)\in E(G_{1})
, for all
x,y\in V(G_{1})\big)
. When
\varphi
is both a graph morphism and comorphism
\varphi
is called
strong
or
full morphism
. We call a graph morphism
\varphi

(graph) isomorphism
if it bijective and its inverse is also a graph morphism. Observe that
\varphi
is an isomorphism if and only if it is a bijective strong morphism.

Given two graphs
H,G
with
V(H)\subseteq V(G)
, we say that
H
is a
subgraph of
G
if the inclusion map is a graph morphism, and a subgraph
H
of
G
is called
induced
if the inclusion map is a strong morphism. Let now
\mathcal{R}
be an equivalence relation on
V=V(G)
; we call
the quotient graph of
G
by
\mathcal{R}
, and we denote it by
G/\mathcal{R}
, the graph whose vertex set is
V/\mathcal{R}
and, given
[x],[y]\in V(G/\mathcal{R})
, one has
([x],[y])\in E(G/\mathcal{R})
if and only there exist
x^{\prime}\in[x]
and
y^{\prime}\in[y]
with
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in E(G)
, i.e.
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}\,:\,G\longrightarrow G/\mathcal{R}
defined by
v\mapsto[v]
is a graph morphism. When
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}
is a strong morphism, we say that
G/\mathcal{R}
is a
strong quotient
of
G
.

We conclude this section by recalling some common operations defined on graphs.
The
Kronecker product
, or simply
product
, of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, denoted by
G_{1}\times G_{2},
is the graph whose vertex set is
V(G_{1})\times V(G_{2})
and two couple of vertices are adjacent if and only if both the first and second components of the couples are adjacent. The
coproduct

G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
is the graph whose vertex set is the disjoint union of the vertices of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
and two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are adjacent in
G_{1}
or in
G_{2}
, i.e. its edge set is the disjoint union of the edges of both graphs. Finally, given two graph morphisms
\phi,\psi:G_{1}\to G_{2}
, the
equalizer

\Eq(\varphi,\psi)
is the (strong) subgraph induced by the subset of vertex where
\varphi
and
\psi
coincide.

The pair of data given by the class of all graphs and graph morphisms, with composition and identities defined in the natural way, is a category, that will be denoted by
Graph
, with product, coproduct and equalizer defined as above, cf.
[
K11
, Sections 4.1 and 4.2]
.

2.
Zero-divisor graphs

There are various definitions of zero-divisor graphs attached to commutative rings which appear in the literature and we are going to review next some of the most significative. As we did above for graphs, given a ring
A
and an equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}
on
A
, we shall denote by
[a]
the equivalence class of
a
with respect to
\mathcal{R}
. Two elements
a,b\in A
are called
associated
, and we write
a\sim b
, if
(a)=(b)
. We say that
a,b\in A
are
strongly associated
, and we write
a\approx b
, if there exists
u\in A^{\times}
such that
a=ub
. Finally, we call
a,b\in A

equiannihilated
, and we write
a\asymp b
, if
\Ann(a)=\Ann(b)
. It is immediate to see that
\sim,\approx
and
\asymp
are indeed equivalence relations.

2.1.
Definitions in the literature

Essentially, a zero-divisor graph attached to a ring
A
is a graph whose vertex set is
A/\mathcal{R}_{A}
, where
\mathcal{R}_{A}
is an equivalence relation on
A
, together with an adjacency relation.

In
[
B88
]
,
\mathcal{R}_{A}
is the trivial relation, i.e. equality; therefore, Beck’s zero-divisor graph
G(A)
has vertex set
V(G(A))=A
.
Loops are not allowed in
G(A)
; in fact, the adjacency relation
E=E(G(A))
is given by

(a,b)\in E\text{\,\,if and only if\,\,}a\neq b\,\,\text{and}\,\,ab=0.

In
[
AL99
]
, the vertex set is just
\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)
and the resulting graph
\Gamma(A)
is the induced subgraph of
G(A)
by
\mathcal{D}^{*}(A).

In
[
M02
]
the so-called compressed zero-divisor graph
\Gamma_{E}(A)
has vertex set
\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)/\asymp
, and the adjacency relation
E
is defined as

\big([a],[b]\big)\in E\,\,\text{if and only if\,\,}a\neq b\,\,\text{and}\,\,ab=0;

it is a graph with no loop which is a strong quotient of
\Gamma(A)
.

In
[
DJS21
]
, functorial properties of the compressed zero-divisor graph have been studied in the case of a finite ring
A
, by means of a functor
\Theta
; the image
\Theta(A)
results to be a compressed zero-divisor-graph whose vertex set is
A/\!\approx
and the adjacency relations
E
is defined by

\big([a],[b]\big)\in E\,\,\text{if and only if}\,\,ab=0.

At the end of the same paper, the authors propose a modification, apt to treat the general case of infinite rings.

Definition 2.1

(
[
DJS21
]
, Definition 6.1)
.

Let
A
be a ring. The
zero-divisor graph on principal ideals

\Theta(A)
has vertex set
\mathcal{PI}_{A}
and the adjacency relation
E
is defined by

\big((a),(b)\big)\in E\,\,\text{if and only if}\,\,ab=0,

i.e. two principal ideals are adjacent if the product of their principal generators is zero or, equivalently if their product is the zero ideal.

With a slight abuse of notation, we shall use both
(a)
and
[a]
to denote a vertex of
\Theta(A)
, since
(a)\mapsto[a]
is a 1:1 correspondence between
\mathcal{PI}_{A}
and
A/\sim.

Unlike in the other graphs introduced so far, loops are allowed in
\Theta(A)
, and this fact is essential in order to provide categorical properties of the functor
\Theta
for both finite and infinite rings.

2.2.
Zero-divisor relations and a new definition

One of the key observations in order to provide a generalization is that all of the adjacency relations which have been considered so far all share the same “minimal” property, which lead us to the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

An equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}
on
A
is called
zero-divisor relation
if, taken any
a\mathcal{R}a^{\prime}
e
b\mathcal{R}b^{\prime}
, it holds that
\left(ab=0\Rightarrow a^{\prime}b^{\prime}=0\right)
.

Observe that an equivalence relation
\mathcal{R}
is zero-divisor if and only if the adjacency relation
E
on
A/\mathcal{R}
, defined by
([a],[b])\in E\!\iff\!ab=0
, is well defined.
This fact suggests the choice of the name and puts us in a position to provide the announced definition.

Definition 2.3
.

A
zero-divisor graph on
A
is a graph whose vertex set is
A/\mathcal{R}
, for some zero-divisor relation
\mathcal{R}
on
A
, and
[a]
and
[b]
are adjacent if and only if
ab=0
; we denote it by
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R})
.

Given any subset
X\subset A
, we also let
\texttt{\large{Z}}(X,\mathcal{R})
be the subgraph of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R})
induced by
\{[a]\mid a\in X\}
.

When there is no ambiguity, we shall denote
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R})
, resp.
\texttt{\large{Z}}(X,\mathcal{R})
, simply by
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
, resp.
\texttt{\large{Z}}(X)
. A case of special interest is when
X=\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)
, since the essential part of a zero-divisor graph is given by the equivalence classes of the elements of
X
.

By the very definitions, we can capture the graphs
G(A)
,
\Gamma(A)
,
\Gamma_{E}(A)
by means of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,=)
,
\texttt{\large{Z}}(D^{*}(A),=)
,
\texttt{\large{Z}}(D^{*}(A),\asymp)
respectively, removing their loops. Moreover, for any finite ring
A
, we have

\Theta(A)=\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\approx)

Among all zero-divisor relations on
A
, the relation
\asymp
turns out to be the coarsest, and this somewhat suggests the appellative compressed of the graph
\Gamma_{E}(A)
.
Also, maybe surprisingly, the viceversa holds, i.e. if
\mathcal{R}
is finer than the relation
\asymp
, then it is a zero-divisor relation.
We record this fact in the next result.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a an equivalence relation on
A
; then
\mathcal{R}
is zero-divisor if and only if
\mathcal{R}
is finer than
\asymp
, i.e. for all
a
and
b
,
a\mathcal{R}b
implies
a\asymp b
.

Proof.

Let us take
a\mathcal{R}b
, for a given zero-divisor relation
\mathcal{R}
on
A
and, by symmetry, it is sufficient to prove that
\Ann(a)\subseteq\Ann(b)
.
Given any
c\in\Ann(a)
, we have that
ca=0
and, since
a\mathcal{R}b
and
c\mathcal{R}c
, it follows that
cb=0
.

Viceversa, if
a\mathcal{R}a^{\prime}
and
b\mathcal{R}b^{\prime}
for a finer relation than
\asymp
then
\Ann(a)=\Ann(a^{\prime})
and
\Ann(b)=\Ann(b^{\prime})
. So, if
ab=0
, then
b\in\Ann(a)=\Ann(a^{\prime})
, i.e.
a^{\prime}b=0
; therefore
a^{\prime}\in\Ann(b)=\Ann(b^{\prime})
, i.e.
a^{\prime}b^{\prime}=0
.
∎

A consequence of the previous proposition is that, for all zero-divisor relations on
A
, it holds that
[0]=\{0\}
, because
A=\Ann(0)=\Ann(a)
implies
a=1\cdot a=0
. It is also easy to see that
=
,
\approx
and
\sim
are zero-divisor relations, because
a=a^{\prime}
implies
a\approx a^{\prime}
, which in turn yields
(a)=(a^{\prime})
and, thus,
\Ann(a)=\Ann(a^{\prime})
for all
a,a^{\prime}\in A.

Also observe that, in general,
\sim
is not the same as
\asymp
; take for instance the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}
, where we have
2\asymp 1
but
(2)\neq(1)={\mathbb{Z}}.

Remark 2.5
.

Observe that, given zero-divisor relations
\mathcal{R},\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
on
A
such that
\mathcal{R}
is finer than
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
, then
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
is a strong quotient of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R})
.

Many known facts about zero-divisor graphs can be generalized in terms of
Z
, as for instance the following, cf.
[
AL99
]
Theorem 2.3,
[
M02
]
,
[
SW11
]
Proposition 1.4; see also
[
SZ22
]
for more results of this type.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a zero-divisor relation on
A
; then the subgraph
\texttt{\large{Z}}(\mathcal{D}^{*}(A),\mathcal{R})
, when not empty, is connected with diameter
\leqslant 3
.

Proof.

Consider the graph
G
which has the same vertex set as
\texttt{\large{Z}}(\mathcal{D}^{*}(A),\mathcal{R})
and with edge set obtained by
E(\texttt{\large{Z}}(\mathcal{D}^{*}(A),\mathcal{R}))
by removing all the loops.
Observe that, without loss of generality we can prove the statement for
G
, since adding loops does not ruin connectedness nor affects the diameter. Since
G
is a strong quotient of
\Gamma
by Remark
2.5
, the conclusion follows from
[
AL99
]
, Theorem 2.3.
∎

3.
Zero-divisor functors

We denote by
CRing
the category of commutative rings and we recall that the categorical product in
CRing
is the direct product of rings, whereas the categorical coproduct is given by the underlying tensor product of abelian groups, equipped with its canonically induced commutative ring structure.

In this section we generalize to infinite rings the ideas of
[
DJS21
]
, and prove some properties of the map
(A,\mathcal{R})\mapsto\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R})
that make it a functor from the category
CRing
to the category
Graph
, by fixing beforehand a family of equivalence relations indexed by the class of all rings. Some conditions on the family of relations we consider are needed, for
Z
to behave well on ring homomorphisms.

3.1.
Functorial families of equivalence relations

Let now
\{\mathcal{R_{A}}\}
, where
A
varies in
CRing
, denote a collection of equivalence relations.

Definition 3.1
.

We say that
\{\mathcal{R_{A}}\}
is a
functorial family
in
CRing
if, for all morphisms
f\,:\,A\rightarrow B
in
CRing
, it holds that
a\mathcal{R}_{A}a^{\prime}\Rightarrow f(a)\mathcal{R}_{B}f(a^{\prime})
.

A collection of equivalence relations is thus functorial in
CRing
if every ring homomorphism maps equivalence classes to equivalence classes. The appellative functorial in the previous definition is motivated by the following fact.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
be a family of equivalence relations; then
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
is functorial in
CRing
if and only if there exists a functor
F:\textbf{CRing}\rightarrow\textbf{Set}
such that
F(A)=A/\mathcal{R}_{A}
and
F(f)([a])=[f(a)]
for all rings
A
and homomorphisms
f\,:\,A\rightarrow B
.

Proof.

The map
F(f)
is well-defined if and only if
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}
is functorial. The equalities
F(\operatorname{id}_{A})=\operatorname{id}_{F(A)}
and
F(f\circ g)=F(f)\circ F(g)
are a direct consequence of the definition of
F(f)
.
∎

Example 3.3
.

The following are examples of functorial families of relations.

1.

\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}=\{=_{A}\}
, the
trivial
functorial family;

2.

Finite products and finite limits. Coproducts.

Let us fix a zero-divisor functorial family

Clearly, independently of the choice of the family,

Proposition 3.11

Let

Proof.

1. By the very definition,
\phi[(a,b)]=([a],[b])

are adjacent in

if and only if

2. It is immediate.
∎

Notice that, since a composition of strong graph epimorphisms, resp. isomorphisms, is a strong graph epimorphism, resp. isomorphism, the previous proposition extends to arbitrary finite products.

3.4.
An Inversion of Product Theorem

Given a graph
G
and
v\in V(G)
, one can consider the subgraph of
G
induced by
N(v)
, where
N(v)\subseteq V(G)
is the set of adjacents of
v
. We start with the following easy lemma.

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be rings and
G_{1},G_{2}
the subgraphs of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{1})\times\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{2})
induced by
N\big([0],[1]\big)
and
N\big([1],[0]\big)
, respectively; then,
G_{1}\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{1})
and
G_{2}\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{2})
.

Proof.

Let us show for instance that
G_{1}\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{1})
; the other case is similar. Recall that a vertex
\big([a],[b]\big)
belonging
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\times\texttt{\large{Z}}(B)
is adjacent to
\big([0],[1]\big)
if and only if
b=0
. The claim follows directly from the fact that in a product of graphs adjacency is defined component by component.
∎

Definition 3.19
.

We call two elements
a,b\in A

orthogonal
, and we write
a\perp b
, if
ab=0
and
\Ann(a)\cap\Ann(b)=0
.

In relation to the study of graphs, orthogonality has been introduced in
[
ALS03
]
; we provided in the above a modification of the original definition, that will allow us to work with graphs with loops as well.
It is easy to see that two elements
a,b\in A
are orthogonal if and only if
ab=0
and
a+b
is an
A
-regular, cf.
[
ALS03
]
, Lemma 3.3.

We are now ready to prove one of our main results.

Now, let

By our choice of

iii) By symmetry, we may prove the assertion for

3. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem one has

4.
Applications to the finite case

It would be desirable to have a structure theorem for
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)=\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\mathcal{R}_{A})
in case this is finite. Observe that its finitess depends on the chosen relation, as for instance
\texttt{\large{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}},=)
is infinite, whereas
\texttt{\large{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}},\sim)
is finite. Clearly, when
A
is finite then
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is finite, indipendently of the given
\mathcal{R}_{A}
; moreover, we know by Proposition
3.6
and Remark
2.5
then when
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is finite then
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A,\sim)
is finite. Therefore, if we give, and we do, a characterization in the case
\mathcal{R}_{A}=\sim_{A}
, then we will have the largest family of rings for which
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
might be finite.

For the rest of the section let us fix
\{\mathcal{R}_{A}\}=\{\sim_{A}\}
. First of all, notice that, by definition,
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is finite exactly when
A
has finitely many principal ideals, which immediately implies that
A
is artinian.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
A
be a ring. Then, the following are equivalent:

1.

\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is finite;

2.

A
has finitely many ideals;

3.

A\simeq A_{1}\times A_{2}
, where
A_{1}
is an artinian PIR and
A_{2}
is a finite ring.

Proof.

1\Rightarrow 2
: We already observed that
\mathcal{PI}_{A}
is finite and
A
artinian; since every element of
\mathcal{I}_{A}
is a finite sum of principal ideals, the conclusion follows.

2\Rightarrow 3
: This is contained in
[
Z14
]
, Theorem 3.3. Observe that
A
is artinian and can be written as a finite product of artinian local rings, which all have a finite number of ideals. It is thus sufficient to show that each local factor of
A
is either a PIR or a finite ring. By contradiction, say that a factor
(B,\mathfrak{m},k)
of
A
is infinite and not PIR. It is well known that a local ring is finite if and only if
A
is artinian with finite residue field and this is principal if and only if the
k
-vector space
\mathfrak{m}/\mathfrak{m}^{2}
has dimension
>1
. We may thus conclude that
Q
has infinitely many ideals, which is the sought after contradicion.

3\Rightarrow 1
: By Corollary
3.12
we have that
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{1})\times\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{2})
, where
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{2})
is finite. We only need to show that
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{1})
is finite as well. Since an artinian PIR is a finite product of local artinian PIRs, we may assume that
A_{1}
is local. Thus, every ideal of
A_{1}
can be written as
(p^{i})
, for some
p\in A_{1}
, and
p^{n}=0
for some natural number
n\geqslant 1
from which it follows that
\mathcal{PI}_{A}
is finite.
∎

The previous theorem, together with Corollary
3.12
, yields a reduction to the case of local artinian rings, since we can reconstruct
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
from the graphs which are associated with its local factors.

A family of graphs which turns out to be important to this end is staircases graphs, i.e. those whose adjacency matrix is an ascending staircase of 1’s. In other words, a graph with vertex set
V=\{0,\dots,k\}
is called
staircase graph of index
k
, and it is denoted by
SG_{k}
, when

i\text{\, is adjacent to\, }j\text{\,\, if and only if \,\,}i+j\geqslant k.

Observe that in
SG_{k}
it holds that
\{k\}=N(0)\subset N(1)\subset\dots\subset N(k)=\{0,\dots,k\}
and, thus,
1=\deg(0)<\deg(1)<\dots<\deg(k)=k+1
.

These graphs are uniquely determined, up to isomorphisms, by the degree of the vertices. In fact, in
[
DJS21
]
Proposition 4.1 the following was proved: a graph
G=(\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k}\},E)
, with
k>0
, such that
\deg(v_{i})\in\{1,\ldots,k+1\}
for each
i
and
\deg(v_{i})\neq\deg(v_{j})
whenever
i\neq j
, is isomorphic to
SG_{k}
. Moreover, since
SG_{k}\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}}/(p^{k}))
and
{\mathbb{Z}}/(p^{k})
is a local ring, any
A
such that
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq SG_{k}
is local as well, cf.
[
DJS21
]
, Proposition 4.2 and Corollary 5.1.

Before we proceed with the next lemma, we observe the following. Fix
x\in A
; the map
m_{x}:\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\rightarrow\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
defined as
(a)\mapsto(xa)
is a graph morphism. Moreover, for every
z\in A
we have that
(z)\in N((xa))\iff z\in\Ann(xa)\iff zx\in\Ann(a)\iff(zx)\in N((a))
.
Thus, if
a,b\in A
are such that
N((a))\subseteq N((b))
then
N((xa))\subseteq N((xb)).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A
be a ring,
a\in A
and
\varphi\,:\,SG_{k}\longrightarrow\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
a surjective strong morphism, for some integer
k\geqslant 0
; then, for all
0\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant k
, it holds that
N(x\varphi(i))\subseteq N(x\varphi(j))
.

Proof.

It is enough to prove that
N(\varphi(i))\subseteq N(\varphi(j))
. By hypothesis, every vertex in
N(\varphi(i))
is the image of a vertex
l\in\{0,\dots,k\}
; since
\varphi
is a comorphism,
l
is adjacent to
i
and
i\leqslant j
implies that
l\in N(i)\subseteq N(j)
. Thus,
\varphi(l)\in N(\varphi(j))
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A\neq\textbf{0}
be a ring such that
\mathcal{D}(A)=\mathcal{N}(A)
; then, for all
a,b\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)
then
\Ann(a)\cup\Ann(b)\subsetneq\Ann(ab)
.

Proof.

It is well-known that
\Ann(a)\cup\Ann(b)\subseteq\Ann(ab)
; therefore, let us assume by contradiction that there exist
a,b\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)
such that
\Ann(a)\cup\Ann(b)=\Ann(ab)
and let
n,m\geqslant 1
be the nilpotency indexes of
a
and
b
, respectively.
By inducting on
k+l
, one can easily show first that
a^{k}b^{l}\neq 0
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
,
l\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
, and secondly that

\Ann(a^{k}b^{l})=\Ann(a^{k})\cup\Ann(b^{l})
, for all such
k,l
.

Now, if we let
\alpha=a^{n-1}
and
\beta=b^{m-1}
, then
\alpha,\,\beta,\,\alpha\beta\neq 0
and
\alpha^{2}=\beta^{2}=0
; accordingly,
(\alpha+\beta)\in\Ann(\alpha\beta)=\Ann(\alpha)\cup\Ann(\beta)
and, thus,
\alpha\beta=0
, contradiction.
∎

As an easy consequence, one characterizes local artinian rings by means annihilator ideals, cf.
[
DJS21
]
, Theorem 5.3.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
A\neq\textbf{0}
be artinian; then
A
is local if and only if for all
a,b\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)
it holds that
\Ann(a)\cup\Ann(b)\subsetneq\Ann(ab)
.

Proof.

If
A
were not local, the Structure Theorem for artinian rings would yields that
A\simeq A_{1}\times A_{2}
, for some rings
A_{1},A_{2}\neq\textbf{0}
; also, the elements
a=b=(1,0)
would contradict the hypothesis. Viceversa, when
A
is local artinian one has
\mathcal{D}(A)=\mathcal{N}(A)=\mathfrak{m}
, and the conclusion follows directly by the previous lemma.
∎

Recall that, when
A
is local, one has that
(a),(b)\in\mathcal{PI}_{A}
are equal if and only if
a\approx b
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
A
be a local artinian ring and
\varphi\,:\,SG_{k}\longrightarrow\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
be an isomorphism for some
k>0
.

1.

Let
x\in A
; if there exist
0\leqslant i<j\leqslant k
such that
\Ann(x\varphi(i))=\Ann(x\varphi(j))
, then
x\varphi(i)=x\varphi(j)=0;

2.

\varphi(i)=\varphi(1)^{i}
, for all
i\in\{0,\dots,k\}.

Proof.

For all
i,j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
,  if
\varphi(i)=\varphi(j)
then
N(\varphi(i))=N(\varphi(j))
, i.e.
\Ann(\varphi(i))=\Ann(\varphi(j))
, and the viceversa holds since
\varphi
is an isomorphism. The graph
SG_{k}
is staircase and, thus,
i<j
if and only if
N(i)\subset N(j)
; this is equivalent to
N(\varphi(i))\subset N(\varphi(j))
, which in turn means
\Ann(\varphi(i))\subset\Ann(\varphi(j))
. For all
i\in\{0,\dots,k\}
, let
\varphi(i)=(a_{i}).

1. Assume that
\Ann(xa_{i})=\Ann(xa_{j})
; then
(xa_{i})=(xa_{j})
and there exists
u\in A^{*}
such that
a_{i}-ua_{j}\in\Ann(x)
. Moreover, since
i<j
, we have that
\Ann(a_{i})\subset\Ann(a_{j})
and there exists
y\in\Ann(a_{j})\smallsetminus\Ann(a_{i})
; thus,
(a_{i}-ua_{j})y=a_{i}y\neq 0
and it follows that
a_{i}-ua_{j}\not\in\Ann(y)
.

Since
\varphi^{-1}\big((x)\big),\varphi^{-1}\big((y)\big)\in V(SG_{k})
, we have that either
N(\varphi^{-1}\big((x)\big))\subseteq N(\varphi^{-1}\big((y)\big))
,  or
N(\varphi^{-1}\big((x)\big))\supseteq N(\varphi^{-1}\big((y)\big))
and, accordingly, either
\Ann(x)\subseteq\Ann(y)
or
\Ann(y)

\subseteq\Ann(x)
. Since
a_{i}-ua_{j}\in\Ann(x)\smallsetminus\Ann(y)
, we must have
\Ann(y)\subset\Ann(x)
and, by our choice of
y
, it follows that
0=x(a_{j})=x(a_{i})
.

2. The cases
i=0,1
are easy:
(a_{1})\!=\!(a_{1})^{1}
and, since
\Ann(a_{0})\!=0\!=\!\Ann(1)
, it follows that
(a_{0})\!=\!(1)\!=\!(a_{1})^{0}
. Thus, if
k=1
we are done and may assume
k>1
. We are going to prove that
(a_{i+1})=(a_{1})(a_{i})
for all
1\leqslant i<k
, and that implies the conclusion.

First of all, observe that
N(j)\!\neq\!N(0)
for all
j\!\in\!\{1,\dots,k\!-\!1\}
. This implies that
\Ann(a_{j})\neq\Ann(a_{0})=0
and, thus,
a_{j}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
; moreover,
(a_{j})\!\neq\!(a_{k})=0
,  i.e.
a_{j}\in\mathcal{D}^{*}(A)
. By Corollary
4.4
, we have that
\Ann(a_{1}a_{j})\!\supset\!\Ann(a_{j})
and
N((a_{1}a_{j}))\!\supset\!N((a_{j}))
; therefore
\deg((a_{1}a_{j}))\!>\!\deg((a_{j}))\!=\!j+1
for all
j\!\in\!\{1,\dots,k\!-\!1\}
.

Now take
j,l\in\{0,\dots,k\}
with
j\neq l
; by 1, if
\Ann(a_{1}a_{j})=\Ann(a_{1}a_{l})
then
a_{1}a_{j}=a_{1}a_{l}=0
and, since
\varphi
is an isomorphism, the only possible cases are when
j,l\in\{k\!-\!1,k\}
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.2
we get that
N((a_{1}a_{i}))\!\subset\!N((a_{1}a_{i+1}))\!\subset\!N((a_{1}a_{i+2}))\!\subset\dots\subset\!N((a_{1}a_{k-2}))\!\subset\!N((a_{1}a_{k-1}))=N((a_{1}a_{k}))=\mathcal{PI}_{A}
, where the last equality is due to the fact that
a_{k}=0
. Taking the degrees in the above chain of inclusions, we obtain
\deg((a_{1}a_{i}))\,<\,\deg((a_{1}a_{i+1}))\,<\,\deg((a_{1}a_{i+2}))\,<\,\dots\,<\,\deg((a_{1}a_{k-2}))\,<\,\deg((a_{1}a_{k-1}))=\deg((a_{1}a_{k}))=k+1
. We know that
\deg((a_{1}a_{i}))\!>\!i+1
; on the other hand
\deg((a_{1}a_{i}))\leqslant i+2
, otherwise we would have
\deg((a_{1}a_{k-1}))\!>\!i+2+(k\!-\!1-i)=k+1
, which is a contradiction. Therefore,
(a_{1}a_{i})
has degree
i+2
and, therefore,
(a_{1}a_{i})=(a_{i+1})
, as desired.

∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a local artinian ring. Then,
A
is PIR if and only if
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq SG_{k}
, where
k
is the nilpotency index of
\mathfrak{m}
.

Proof.

Since
A
is PIR, it is well-known that every ideal
I\subseteq A
can be written as
I=\mathfrak{m}^{i}
and
\mathfrak{m}^{i}
is adjacent to
\mathfrak{m}^{j}
if and only if
i+j\geqslant k
, where
k
is the nilpotency index of
\mathfrak{m}
; it is easy to conclude that the map
\varphi\,:\,SG_{k}\longrightarrow\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
defined by
\varphi(i)=\mathfrak{m}^{i}
is an isomorphism.

Viceversa, by Lemma
4.5
.2, every principal ideal is a power of a fixed principal ideal
(x)
; since
A
is noetherian, it follows that every ideal is a power of
(x)
and that
A
is PIR.
∎

Example 4.7
.

Let
A={\mathbb{Z}}/(4)[x]/(2x,x^{2}-2)
and
B=\mathbb{F}_{2}[x,y]/(x^{2},xy,y^{2})
. Then,
A
is a local ring which is PIR by Proposition

, since
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq SG_{3}
; since
\texttt{\large{Z}}(\mathbb{F}_{2}[x,y]/(x^{2},xy,y^{2}))
has four distinct vertices with a loop, namely
(\bar{x})
,
(\bar{y})
,
(\bar{x}+\bar{y})
and
(\bar{0})
, in contrast to

which has only two vertices with a loop.

Theorem 4.8
.

An artinian ring
A
is PIR if and only if
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
is isomorphic to a finite product of staircases graphs.

Proof.

If
A
is PIR, then the conclusion follows by a straightforward application of Proposition
4.6
and Corollary
3.12
.

Viceversa, if
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\zzzsyb\char 2\relax}}{\hbox{\zzzsy\char 2\relax}}{\scriptstyle\times}{\scriptscriptstyle\times}}\limits_{i=1}^{n}SG_{k_{i}}
, then
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\zzzsyb\char 2\relax}}{\hbox{\zzzsy\char 2\relax}}{\scriptstyle\times}{\scriptscriptstyle\times}}\limits_{i=1}^{n}\texttt{\large{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}}/(p_{i})^{k_{i}})
. Since an artinian ring is total, Corollary
3.22
implies that
A\simeq\mathop{\mathchoice{\hbox{\zzzsyb\char 2\relax}}{\hbox{\zzzsy\char 2\relax}}{\scriptstyle\times}{\scriptscriptstyle\times}}\limits_{i=1}^{n}A_{i}
where
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{i})\simeq SG_{k_{i}}
.
Each
A_{i}
is necessarily artinian; if
A_{i}
were not local for a certain
i
, we would have
A_{i}\simeq B_{1}\times B_{2}
with
B_{1},B_{2}\not\simeq\textbf{0}
,
\texttt{\large{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}}/(p_{i})^{k_{i}})\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(A_{i})\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(B_{1})\times\texttt{\large{Z}}(B_{2})
by Corollary
3.12
, and another application of Corollary
3.22
would imply that also
{\mathbb{Z}}/(p_{i})^{k_{i}}
can be factorized, contradiction. Finally, each of the
A_{i}
is PIR, as yielded by Proposition
4.6
.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Keeping in mind Theorem
4.1
, in order to complete the picture, one should understand the structure of
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
when
A
is a finite ring, which is not PIR. This does not seem to be an easy task, since if
A
and
B
are rings such that the posets
\mathcal{I}_{A}
and
\mathcal{I}_{B}
are isomorphic, it is not true in general that
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(B)
. For instance consider
A=\mathbb{F}_{2}[x,y]/(x^{2},y^{2})
and
B=\mathbb{F}_{2}[x,y]/(xy,x^{2}-y^{2})
; then
\mathcal{I}_{A}\simeq\mathcal{I}_{B}
as posets but every vertex in
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)
has a loop whereas
(\bar{x})\in\mathcal{PI}_{B}
has none; therefore
\texttt{\large{Z}}(A)\not\simeq\texttt{\large{Z}}(B)
.
