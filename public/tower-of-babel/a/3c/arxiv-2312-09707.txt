A return-diversification approach to portfolio selection
Francesco Cesarone, Rosella Giacometti, Manuel L Martino, Fabio Tardella

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a general bi-objective model for portfolio selection, aiming to maximize both a diversification measure and the portfolio expected return. Within this general framework, we focus on maximizing a diversification measure recently proposed by Choueifaty and Coignard for the case of volatility as a risk measure. We first show that the maximum diversification approach is actually equivalent to the Risk Parity approach using volatility under the assumption of equicorrelated assets. Then, we extend the maximum diversification approach formulated for general risk measures. Finally, we provide explicit formulations of our bi-objective model for different risk measures, such as volatility, Mean Absolute Deviation, Conditional Value-at-Risk, and Expectiles, and we present extensive out-of-sample performance results for the portfolios obtained with our model. The empirical analysis, based on five real-world data sets, shows that the return-diversification approach provides portfolios that tend to outperform the strategies based only on a diversification method or on the classical risk-return approach. Keywords : Risk Diversification, Most Diversified Portfolio, Bi-objective Optimization, Risk Parity, Portfolio Selection, Expectiles.

Introduction

The importance of diversification in portfolio selection has been recently pointed out by several authors
(
Meucci 2009
;
Fabozzi and S. 2010
;
Roncalli 2014b
;
Lhabitant 2017
;
Cesarone et al. 2020
, see, e.g.,)
.
A qualitative definition of diversification is very clear to portfolio managers: a portfolio is well-diversified if it is not heavily exposed to individual shocks
(
Meucci 2009
)
.
In order to formalize a quantitative approach, suitable portfolio diversification measures should be introduced.
One of the first attempts dates back to the Babilonian Talmud
(
Duchin and Levy 2009
, see, e.g.,)
, where the uniform (or naive) capital diversification is recommended.
Some early studies on the use of diversification in finance can be found in the works of
Leavens 1945
and
Bernoulli 1954
.
However, only recently more refined notions of portfolio diversification have been proposed and analyzed.
Among them, the most popular approaches, both from a theoretical and from a practical viewpoint, seem to be the Risk Parity model, introduced by
Maillard et al. 2010

(
Roncalli 2014b
, see also)
,
and the Most Diversified Portfolio, proposed in
Choueifaty and Coignard 2008
and
Choueifaty et al. 2013
for the case of the volatility risk measure.
These two approaches provide different diversified portfolio in general.
However, we show here that, in the case of volatility and under the assumption of equicorrelated assets, the Most Diversified Portfolios and the Risk Parity portfolios actually coincide.
This result establishes a first bridge between these hitherto uncorrelated notions of portfolio diversification.

We recall that other diversification approaches have been proposed.
Among them we find entropy
(
Usta and Kantar 2011
;
Carmichael et al. 2018
;
Pola 2016
;
Bera and Park 2008
;
Deng and Lin 2022
, see, e.g.,)
, extensions of Risk Parity
with general risk measures
(
Ararat et al. 2021
;
Bai et al. 2016
;
Cesarone and Tardella 2017
;
Cesarone and Colucci 2017
;
Chaves et al. 2012
;
Qian 2005
;
Qian 2011
;
Roncalli 2014a
;
Bellini et al. 2021
, see, e.g.,)
, the Herfindahl index
(
Coqueret 2015
;
Dhingra et al. 2023
, see, e.g.,)
, and the Minimum-Torsion Bets
(
Meucci et al. 2015
)
.
For a review of the main contributions on diversification strategies and classes of diversification measures, we refer to
Koumou 2020
.

Typically, in the diversification approach the classical risk-return optimization model is replaced by the quest for a portfolio that maximizes some measure of diversification.
However, the maximization of portfolio diversification could be included as an additional criterion in a Risk-Gain framework as already done by some authors in specific cases
(
Roncalli 2014a
;
Usta and Kantar 2011
;
Bera and Park 2008
;
Deng and Lin 2022
, see, e.g.,)
.

Other studies have been devoted to bi-objective portfolio selection models where risk is minimized and a diversification measure is maximized
(
Bera and Park 2008
;
Coqueret 2015
, see, e.g.,)
.
However, we observe that the goals of risk and diversification are strictly related and hence diversification should be used as a substitute of risk rather than a complement.
Indeed, very few attempts have been made to include gain maximization into
the proposed diversification models.
Here we propose a general bi-objective model for portfolio selection where we aim at maximizing both a diversification measure and the expected return.
We first analyze some general properties of this bi-objective model and then we specialize the analysis to the case of a general diversification measure that extends the one proposed in
Choueifaty et al. 2013
for the case of volatility.
We provide explicit formulations of our model for the cases of volatility, Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), and Expectiles risk measures and we present extensive out-of-sample performance results for the portfolios obtained with our models.
The empirical analysis, based on five real-world data sets, shows that the return-diversification approach provides portfolios that tend to outperform the strategies based only on a diversification method or on the classical risk-return approach.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we recall some definitions and properties of risk measures and we propose a general return-diversification model.
In Section
2.1
, we discuss and extend the maximum diversification ratio approach to the case of a general risk measure.
In Section
2.2
, we recall the Risk Parity approach and we prove its equivalence with the maximum diversification ratio approach in the case of volatility under the assumption of constant correlation.
Section
3
provides explicit linear or quadratic formulations for the problem of maximizing both return and diversification in the case of some specific risk measures typically used for asset allocation.
In Section
4
, we provide an extensive empirical analysis based on five real-world data sets, which highlights promising out-of-sample performance results of return-diversification portfolio strategies.
Some concluding remarks are given in Section
5
together with possible ideas for future research.

2
A Return-Diversification Model

We consider a general probability space
\{\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\}
where all random variables are defined.
We denote by
L^{p}:=L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
the set of all random variables
X
with
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{p}]<+\infty
,
where
p\in[1,+\infty)
.
For our purposes, we are concerned with an investment universe with
n
tradable assets, and we denote by
R_{i}
the random variable representing the return of the asset
i
.
For a nonnegative vector
x
, the random portfolio return is defined as
\displaystyle R_{P}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}R_{i}
where
\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1
and
x_{i}
is the percentage of capital invested in the asset
i
.
Note that
R_{P}(x)
belongs to the convex cone
\mathcal{P}=\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}R_{i}:x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\}
.
To each random variable
R
in
\mathcal{P}
we associate real values
\phi(R),\,\psi(R)
, and
g(R)
, which may be interpreted as diversification, risk, and gain measures respectively.
The diversification measure
\phi(R)
is often expressed as a function of the risk measure
\psi(R)
.
We recall some general properties of risk measures in terms of the mapping
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by
\rho(x)=\psi(R_{P}(x))
, which can also be viewed as a risk measure for the portfolio represented by
x
. The risk measure
\rho
is called:

(i)

positive iff
\qquad\rho(x)>0,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
;

(ii)

convex iff
\qquad\rho(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leq\lambda\rho(x)+(1-\lambda)\rho(y)\qquad\forall x,\,y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\,\,\lambda\in[0,1]
;

(iii)

positively homogeneous (of degree 1) iff
\qquad\rho(\lambda x)=\lambda\rho(x),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},\,\,\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

We recall the following classical definitions.

Definition 1

(
Artzner et al. 1999
)
.

A
coherent risk measure
is a risk measure
\rho
that satisfies Conditions (ii) and (iii), which imply subadditivity, namely
\forall X,Y\in L^{p}

\rho(X+Y)\leq\rho(X)+\rho(Y)
. Furthermore, it satisfies monotonicity, namely,
\forall X,Y\in L^{p},X\leq Y
implies
\rho(X)\geq\rho(Y)
, and translativity, namely
\forall X\in L^{p}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

\rho(X+\alpha)=\rho(X)-\alpha
.

Definition 2

(
Rockafellar et al. 2006
)
.

A
deviation risk measure
is a risk measure
\rho
that satisfies Conditions (ii) and (iii), which imply subadditivity, (i), translational invariance, i.e.,
\forall X\in L^{p}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

\rho(X+\alpha)=\rho(X)
, and normalization, namely
\rho(0)=0
.

On this basis, we propose to address the problem of maximizing some diversification measure
\phi(R_{P}(x))
and, simultaneously, maximizing the portfolio expected return
g(x)=\mu_{P}(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}x_{i}
, where
\mu_{i}
is the expected return of the asset
i
:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\max\limits_{x}&\phi(R_{P}(x))&\\[5.0pt]
\max\limits_{x}&\mu^{T}x&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1&\\[5.0pt]
&x_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\dots,n\\[5.0pt]
\end{array}

(1)

2.1
Maximum Diversification for General Risk Measures

We now recall, extend, and compare two popular diversification approaches (namely the Maximum Diversification ratio and Risk Parity) associated with a risk measure that satisfies Conditions (i), (ii), and (iii).

Definition 3

(Diversification Ratio)
.

For a risk measure
\rho
that satisfies conditions (i), (ii), (iii), we define the
Diversification Ratio (DR)
as follows:

\displaystyle DR(x)=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\rho(R_{i})}{\rho(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}R_{i})}\,.

Note that, since
\rho
is convex, we have

\rho(R_{P}(x))=\rho(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}R_{i})\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\rho(R_{i})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\rho_{i}\,

(2)

where
\rho_{i}=\rho(R_{i})
. Hence, by construction,
DR(x)\geq 1
. Maximizing DR amounts to maximizing the
relative
distance between the portfolio risk in the worst-case scenario, i.e., when
\rho
is additive (right-hand side in (
2
)), and the generic portfolio risk (left-hand side in (
2
)).

Furthermore, we observe that for a risk measure
\rho
satisfying (i), (ii) and (iii), the greatest Diversification Ratio based on at most
m
assets is given by:

DR_{m}(\mathcal{P})=\sup\left\{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\rho(\alpha_{i}X_{i})}{\rho\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}X_{i}\right)}:\,X_{1},\cdots,X_{m}\in\mathcal{P},\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=1,\,\alpha_{i}\geq 0\right\}\,.

(3)

Here,
DR_{m}(\mathcal{P})
represents the maximum
relative
diversification risk benefit we can obtain at a random variable
X\in\mathcal{P}
with respect to a decomposition of
X
as a convex combination of at most
m
random variables
X_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
.
By construction,
DR_{m}(\mathcal{P})\geq 1
and
DR_{m}(\mathcal{P})\leq DR_{m+1}(\mathcal{P})
.
We now show that the maximum value of
DR_{m}(\mathcal{P})
for all
m
is actually attained when
m=n
by using the extreme rays
R_{i}
of the cone
\mathcal{P}
.

Theorem 1
.

If
\rho
satisfies (i)+(ii)+(iii), then

\sup_{m}DR_{m}(\mathcal{P})=DR_{n}(\mathcal{P})=\max\left\{\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\rho(R_{i})}{\rho\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}R_{i}\right)}:\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1,\,\lambda_{i}\geq 0\right\}

(4)

Proof.

Given
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\in\mathcal{P}
, there exist
\beta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, with
i=1,\dots,m
and
j=1,\dots,n
, such that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{ij}=1
and
X_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\beta_{ij}R_{j}
for
i=1,\dots,m
.
Furthermore, given
\alpha_{i},\dots,\alpha_{m}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}=1
and setting
\bar{\lambda}_{j}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\beta_{ij}
, we have

\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\rho(\alpha_{i}X_{i})}{\rho(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}X_{i})}\leq\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\beta_{ij}\rho(R_{j})}{\rho(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\beta_{ij}R_{j})}=\frac{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{j}\rho(R_{j})}{\rho(\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\bar{\lambda}_{j}R_{j})}\leq\max\left\{\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\rho(R_{i})}{\rho(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}R_{i})}:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1,\,\lambda_{i}\geq 0\right\}\,,

where the first inequality follows from assumptions (ii) and (iii) which imply
\rho(\alpha_{i}X_{i})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{ij}\rho(R_{j})
for all
i
.
∎

In financial terms, Theorem (
1
) implies that the greatest diversification benefit that can be obtained by decomposing a portfolio
X
as a convex combination of any number of portfolios
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
, is actually attained by decomposing
X
as a convex combination of the
n
assets
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}
, and hence by maximizing the diversification ratio
DR(x)
for
x\in\Delta
, where
\Delta=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1,\,x\geq 0\}
.
Thus, one can consider the maximum diversification portfolio
x^{MD}
as the one maximizing
DR(x)
over
\Delta
.

2.2
Maximum Diversification and Risk Parity

We now recall the diversification approach based on the general notion of equal risk contribution from each asset which has been originally proposed by
Qian 2005
;
Qian 2011
;
Maillard et al. 2010
for the case of the volatility risk measure.

For a differentiable risk measure
\rho(x)=\psi(R_{P}(x))
satisfying Conditions (i), (ii), (iii), Euler’s theorem guarantees that the total risk can be decomposed into the sum of the total risk contributions of each asset

\rho({x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}TRC_{i}(x)\,

where the total risk contribution of asset
i
is

TRC_{i}(x)=x_{i}\frac{\partial\rho({x})}{\partial x_{i}}

The Risk Parity portfolio is the (unique) portfolio
x^{RP}\in\Delta
that satisfies

TRC_{i}(x)=TRC_{j}(x)\quad\forall i,j\,.

Equivalently, the Risk Parity portfolio can be obtained as a solution of the system

\displaystyle TRC_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda^{RP}\,,\qquad\mbox{with}\,\,\,i=1,\dots,n

\displaystyle x\in

\displaystyle\Delta,

\displaystyle\lambda^{RP}\geq 0

By Euler’s theorem,
\lambda^{RP}=\displaystyle\frac{\rho(x)}{n}
is the equal contribution to the risk from each asset.

In the general case, the Risk Parity portfolio and the maximum diversification portfolio are different. However, we will now show that they coincide for the case of volatility under the assumption of constant correlation.
This hypothesis is supported by some scholars
(
Elton and Gruber 1973
;
Elton et al. 1976
;
Elton et al. 2006
;
Engle and Kelly 2012
, see, e.g.,)
who suggest to estimate the covariance matrix with the constant correlation model, i.e.,
\sigma_{ij}=c\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
and
\sigma_{ii}=\sigma_{i}^{2}
.
In the case of the variance risk measure with constant correlation,
Maillard et al. 2010
show that the Risk Parity portfolio is given by
\displaystyle x_{i}^{RP}=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{-1}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{-1}}
.
The following result shows that this coincides with the maximum diversification portfolio under the same assumptions.

Theorem 2
.

If
\rho(x)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}c_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
, with
c_{ii}=1
and
c_{ij}=k
for all
i\neq j
, then a maximum diversification portfolio is given by
x_{i}^{MD}=\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{i}^{-1}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{-1}}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\sigma_{ij}=k\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}
and
\sigma_{ii}=\sigma_{i}^{2}
. Following
Choueifaty et al. 2013
(see p. 18 - Appendix B, and also Section
3
below for the general case), the maximum diversification portfolio in the case of volatility can be obtained by finding a solution
y^{*}
of the following problem

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y}&y^{\prime}\Sigma y&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\sigma^{\prime}y=1&\\
&y\geq 0\end{array}

(5)

and taking
x_{i}^{MD}=\displaystyle\frac{y_{i}^{*}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}}
with
i=1,\dots,n
.
Note that, under the assumption of the constant correlation model, the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is positive definite.
Hence, Problem (
5
) has a unique solution
y^{*}
.
If
y^{*}>0
, then it must satisfy the following KKT conditions:

\centering\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(\Sigma y)_{i}+\lambda\sigma_{i}&=0\quad\forall i\\
\sigma^{\prime}y=1\end{array}\right.\@add@centering

(6)

for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Consider the points
\displaystyle y^{k}=k\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}\right)
with
k\in\mathbb{R}
and substitute them into the first equation of (
6
).
We obtain

(\Sigma y^{k})_{i}+\lambda\sigma_{i}=(\sigma_{i}(k(1+(n-1)c)))+\lambda\sigma_{i}=0\quad\forall i\,.

Then, solving for
\lambda
we have

\lambda=-(k(1+(n-1)c))\,.

Hence
y^{k}
satisfies the second constraint of Problem (
6
) for
\displaystyle k=\frac{1}{n}
.
Thus,
\displaystyle y^{*}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{\sigma_{n}}\right)
is a solution of Problem (
6
), with
\displaystyle\lambda=-\frac{1+(n-1)c}{n}
.
Thus, by strict convexity,
y^{*}
is also the unique solution of Problem (
5
).
The maximum diversification portfolio is then obtained by normalizing
y^{*}
, namely
\displaystyle x_{i}^{MD}=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{-1}}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{j}^{-1}}
.
∎

3
Linear and Quadratic Formulations for the Return-Diversification Model with Specific Risk Measures

In the case where diversification is measured with the diversification ratio (
3
), Problem (
1
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\max\limits_{x}&DR(x)&\\[5.0pt]
\max\limits_{x}&\mu^{T}x&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle x\in\Delta\end{array}

(7)

The Pareto-optimal solutions of Problem (
7
) can be obtained with the
\varepsilon-
constraint method as follows

\begin{array}[]{lll}\max\limits_{x}&\displaystyle DR(x)=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\rho(R_{i})}{\rho(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}R_{i})}&\\[5.0pt]
\mbox{s.t.}&&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}x_{i}\geq\eta&\\[5.0pt]
&x\in\Delta\end{array}

(8)

where
\eta
is the required level of the portfolio expected return.
Problem (
8
) is a Fractional Programming (FP) problem, and is
a nonconvex problem.
However, by condition (ii), Problem (
8
) belongs to the class of concave-convex FP problems, that can be tackled by means of the Schaible transform
(
Schaible 1974
)
described below.

For a long-only portfolio
x\geq 0
, we set
y=tx
, where
\displaystyle t=\frac{1}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\rho_{i}}
.
Clearly, if
\boldsymbol{\rho}(\cdot)
is a positive function, then
t>0
.
Thus, Problem (
8
) can be reformulated as follows

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min\limits_{x}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{DR(x)}=\frac{\boldsymbol{\rho}(x)}{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\rho_{i}}=t\boldsymbol{\rho}(x)=\boldsymbol{\rho}(tx)&\\[5.0pt]
s.t.&&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}=t&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\frac{y_{i}}{t}\geq\eta&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\rho_{i}=1&\\[5.0pt]
&x_{i}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(9)

By eliminating
x
and
t
from Problem (
9
), we obtain

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y}&\rho(y)&\\[5.0pt]
s.t.&&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\rho_{i}=1&\\[5.0pt]
&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq(\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i})\eta&\\[5.0pt]
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(10)

Note that Problem (
10
) is a simple convex programming problem with linear constraints which can be efficiently solved even for large instances.
Since
\rho
is a homogenous function of degree 1,
we find the portfolio weights that maximize the diversification ratio in Problem (
8
) by simply normalizing the solution
y^{*}
of Problem (
10
).
Thus,
x_{i}^{MD}=\displaystyle\frac{y_{i}^{*}}{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}y_{k}^{*}}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

We are interested in computing the optimal values
h(\eta)

of Problem (
8
) as a function of
\eta
in the interval
[\eta_{min},\eta_{max}]
, where
\eta_{min}
denotes the value of
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}x_{i}
at an optimal solution of the problem obtained by deleting the return constraint in (
8
), and
\eta_{max}=\max\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\}
.
Indeed, the graph of
h(\eta)
on the interval
[\eta_{min},\eta_{max}]
contains the set of all non-dominated portfolios (efficient frontier), which is usually approximated by solving (
8
) for several (equally spaced) values of
\eta
in
[\eta_{min},\eta_{max}]
.

We now describe the explicit formulation of Problem (
10
) in the case of some specific risk measures typically used for asset allocation.

3.1
Volatility

Let
\sigma_{i}
be the volatility of asset
i
and
c_{ij}
be the (linear) correlation
between assets
i
and
j
.
The portfolio volatility is defined as

\sigma(R_{P}(x))=\sqrt{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}x_{i}x_{j}c_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}}

(11)

Note that volatility is a deviation risk measure satisfying Definition
2
.
Hence, by condition (ii)

\sigma(x)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\sigma_{i}\,.

(12)

When the asset returns are perfectly positively correlated, i.e.,
c_{ij}=1
,

\sigma(x)
is additive.

In the case of volatility, Problem (
10
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y}&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}y_{i}y_{j}c_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\sigma_{i}=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(13)

3.2
MAD

The Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) is defined as the expected value of the absolute deviation of the portfolio return
R_{P}(x)
from its mean
\mu_{P}(x)
, namely

MAD(x)=E[\,\lvert R_{P}(x)-\mu_{P}(x)\rvert\,]=E\left[\,\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}x_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}x_{i}\right\rvert\,\right]\,,

(14)

where
R_{i}
is the random return of asset
i
and
\mu_{i}
is its expected value.
Note that MAD is a deviation risk measure satisfying Definition
2
.
Hence, by condition (ii)

MAD(x)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}MAD(R_{i})\,.

(15)

Expression (
15
) holds with equality, i.e., MAD is additive, under the conditions specified in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

(
Ararat et al. 2021
)
.

Let
R_{1},R_{2},\cdots,R_{n}\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.
Then, MAD is additive if and only if for each
i\neq j
the asset returns satisfy the following
conditions:

\left(R_{i}-E[R_{i}]\right)\left(R_{j}-E[R_{j}]\right)\geq 0\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

(16)

We assume that the asset returns are discrete random variables defined on a discrete state space with
T
states of nature having probability
\pi_{t}
, with
t=1,...,T
.
The outcomes of the discrete random returns correspond to the historical scenarios.
More precisely, the portfolio choice is performed using an in-sample window of
T
historical realizations having equal probability
\pi_{t}=1/T
, as is typically done in portfolio optimization
(see, e.g.,
Carleo et al. 2017
;
Cesarone 2020
;
Bellini et al. 2021
, and references therein)
.

In the case of MAD, Problem (
10
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y}&\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i,t}y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\right\rvert&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}MAD_{i}=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(17)

where
r_{i,t}
is the historical realization of the return of asset
i
at time
t
, and
MAD_{i}=MAD(R_{i})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\lvert r_{i,t}-\mu_{i}\right\rvert
.

As in
Konno and Yamazaki 1991
,
we can linearize Problem (
17
) by introducing
T
auxiliary variables

d_{t}
defined as the absolute deviation of the portfolio return from its mean for each
t=1,\ldots,T
, i.e.,
d_{t}=\left\lvert\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i,t}y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\right\rvert
,
and by replacing the absolute value with the following constraints:

d_{t}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{i,t}-\mu_{i})y_{i}
and

d_{t}\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{i,t}-\mu_{i})y_{i}
.
Hence, Problem (
17
) can be rewritten as the following LP problem

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y,d}&\quad\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}&\\
\mbox{s.t.}&&\\
&\displaystyle d_{t}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{i,t}-\mu_{i})y_{i}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\displaystyle d_{t}\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(r_{i,t}-\mu_{i})y_{i}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}MAD_{i}=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(18)

3.3
CVaR

The formal definition of the Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), at a specified confidence level
\varepsilon
, is

CVaR_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int\limits_{0}^{\varepsilon}Q_{R_{P}(x)}(\alpha)d\alpha\,,

where
Q_{R_{P}(x)}(\alpha)
is the quantile function of the portfolio return
R_{P}(x)
.
Note that CVaR is a coherent risk measure satisfying Definition
1
and, in particular, the convexity condition (ii)

CVaR_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}CVaR_{\varepsilon}(R_{i})\,,

(19)

where
CVaR_{\varepsilon}(R_{i})
represents CVaR of asset
i
.
Expression (
19
) holds with equality, i.e., CVaR is additive in the case where the random variables
R_{i}
are comonotonic
(
Tasche 2002
)
.
Similar to the case of MAD, in a discrete world, Problem (
10
) with CVaR as a risk measure becomes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y}&\displaystyle CVaR_{\varepsilon}(y)&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}CVaR_{\varepsilon}(R_{i})=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(20)

where the Conditional Value-at-Risk of asset
i
is given by
CVaR_{\varepsilon}(R_{i})=-\frac{1}{j}\sum_{t=1}^{j}r_{i,(t)}
, with
j=\texttt{round}(\varepsilon T)
, while
r_{i,(1)}\leq\ldots\leq r_{i,(T)}
represent the ordered realizations of the returns of asset
i
.
We recall the LP reformulation of CVaR by
Rockafellar and Uryasev 2000
for a given portfolio
y

\displaystyle CVaR_{\varepsilon}(y):=

\displaystyle\min_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R},d}\;\zeta+\frac{1}{\varepsilon T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}

\displaystyle\mbox{s.t.}\hskip 2.84544ptd_{t}\geq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i,t}y_{i}-\zeta\quad t=1,\ldots,T,

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07182ptd_{t}\geq 0,\;\forall t

Hence, Problem (
20
) can be rewritten as the following LP problem

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y,\zeta,d}&\displaystyle\zeta+\frac{1}{\varepsilon T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}&\\
s.t.&&\\
&d_{t}\geq-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}r_{i,t}y_{i}-\zeta&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&d_{t}\geq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}CVaR_{\varepsilon}(R_{i})=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\\
&\zeta\in\mathbb{R}&\end{array}

(21)

3.4
Expectiles

Expectiles have been defined by
Newey and Powell 1987
as the minimizers of the expected value of an asymmetric piecewise quadratic loss function

e_{\alpha}(X)=\argmin_{\kappa\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}[\alpha(X-\kappa)_{+}^{2}+(1-\alpha)(X-\kappa)_{-}^{2}]\,,

(22)

where
X\in L^{2}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and
(b)_{+}=\max(b,0)
,
(b)_{-}=-\min(b,0)
.
The minimizer in (
22
) is unique and determined by the first-order condition

\alpha\mathbb{E}[(X-e_{\alpha}(X))_{+}]=(1-\alpha)\mathbb{E}[(X-e_{\alpha}(X))_{-}],

(23)

which is indeed an equivalent definition of
e_{\alpha}(X)
valid for each
X\in L^{1}

(
Bellini et al. 2014
, see, e.g.,)
.
We denote by
\zeta_{\alpha}(x)=\zeta_{\alpha}(R_{p}(x))=e_{\alpha}(-R_{p}(x))
the
\alpha
-expectile of the portfolio loss which, as usual, is defined as the opposite of the portfolio return.
For
\alpha\in[1/2,1)
, Expectiles satisfy Definition
1
, and hence condition (ii)

\zeta_{\alpha}(R_{P}(x))\leq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\zeta_{\alpha}(R_{i})\,.

(24)

Expression (
24
) holds with equality, i.e., Expectile is additive, under the conditions specified in the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(
Bellini et al. 2021
)
.

Let
R_{1},R_{2},\cdots,R_{n}\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, and
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.
Then, Expectile is additive if and only if for each
i\neq j
the asset returns satisfy the following
conditions:

\mathbb{P}\left((R_{i}-e_{\alpha}(R_{i}))(R_{j}-e_{\alpha}(R_{j}))\geq 0\right)=1\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

(25)

Again, in a discrete world and in the case of Expectile, Problem (
10
) becomes

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y}&\displaystyle\zeta_{\alpha}(y)&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\zeta_{\alpha}(R_{i})=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(26)

where
\zeta_{\alpha}(R_{i})
represents the
\alpha
-Expectile of asset
i
.

As described in
Bellini et al. 2021
, for a fixed portfolio
y
, its
\alpha
-Expectile
\zeta_{\alpha}(y)=\zeta_{\alpha}
can be obtained by solving the following equation

\alpha\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{i,t}-\zeta_{\alpha}\right)_{+}\,=(1-\alpha)\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{i,t}-\zeta_{\alpha}\right)_{-}\,.

(27)

Therefore, Problem (
26
) can be rewritten as follows

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y,\zeta_{\alpha}}&\displaystyle\zeta_{\alpha}&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\alpha\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{i,t}-\zeta_{\alpha}\right)_{+}\,=(1-\alpha)\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}\left(-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{i,t}-\zeta_{\alpha}\right)_{-}&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\zeta_{\alpha}(R_{i})=1&\\
&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}

(28)

We can linearize Problem (
28
) by replacing the positive and negative parts of
\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{i,t}-\zeta_{\alpha}
with
2T
auxiliary variables
d_{t}^{+},d_{t}^{-}\geq 0
satisfying
\displaystyle d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{i,t}-\zeta_{\alpha}
and
d_{t}^{+}\cdot d_{t}^{-}=0
.
We note that, by using an argument similar to the one of Theorem 5 in
Bellini et al. 2021
, one can show that the complicating complementarity constraints
d_{t}^{+}\cdot d_{t}^{-}=0
are always satisfied and can thus be dropped from the formulation.
Hence, Problem (
10
) for Expectiles can be reformulated as the following LP problem

\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle\min_{y,d^{+},d^{-},\zeta_{\alpha}}&\zeta_{\alpha}&\\
s.t.&&\\
&\displaystyle\alpha\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{+}=(1-\alpha)\sum\limits_{t=1}^{T}d_{t}^{-}&\\
&\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}r_{it}-\zeta_{\alpha}=d_{t}^{+}-d_{t}^{-}&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}(\zeta_{\alpha})_{i}=1&\\
&\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}y_{i}\geq\eta\left(\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\right)&\\
&y_{i}\geq 0&i=1,\ldots,n\\
&d_{t}^{+},d_{t}^{-}\geq 0&t=1,\ldots,T\\
&\zeta_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}&\end{array}

(29)

The out-of-sample performance results of each portfolio strategy are evaluated by considering several performance measures widely used in the literature
(
Cesarone et al. 2015
;
Cesarone et al. 2016
;
Cesarone et al. 2020
;
Bruni et al. 2017
;
Cesarone and Colucci 2017
, see, e.g.,)
, and detailed below.

-

The average
\mathbf{\mu^{out}}
and the standard deviation
\mathbf{\sigma^{out}}
of the out-of-sample portfolio returns
R^{out}
.

-

The
Sharpe
ratio defined as
\mu^{out}/\sigma^{out}

(
Sharpe 1966
;
Sharpe 1994
)
.
Clearly, higher Sharpe ratios are associated with better results.

-

The Maximum DrawDown
(
Chekhlov et al. 2005
,
MDD
,)
is a measure of the maximum potential out-of-sample loss from the observed peak and is expressed as

\mbox{MDD}=\min\limits_{T^{in}+1\leq t\leq T}DD_{t},

where
T^{in}
and
T
are the length of a single in-sample window and the entire time series, respectively.
The DrawDown is computed as

DD_{t}=\frac{W_{t}-\max\limits_{T^{in}+1\leq\tau\leq t}W_{\tau}}{\max\limits_{T^{in}+1\leq\tau\leq t}W_{\tau}},\qquad t\in\{T^{in}+1,\dots T\},

where
W_{t}=W_{t-1}(1+R^{out}_{t})
denotes the portfolio wealth at time
t
, with
W_{0}=1
.
Note that the MDD is always non-positive, hence higher values are preferable.

-

The
Ulcer
index
(
Martin and McCann 1989
)
measures the depth and duration of drawdowns in prices over the out-of-sample period, and is expressed as

\mbox{Ulcer}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum\limits_{t=T^{in}+1}^{T}DD_{t}^{2}}{T-T^{in}}}\,.

Lower Ulcer values indicate better portfolio performance.

-

The Rachev ratio
(
Biglova et al. 2004
,
Rachev10
,)
is a measure of the relative gap between the mean of the best
\alpha\%
values of
R^{out}-r_{f}
and that of the worst
\beta\%
ones, and it is defined as

\mbox{Rachev10}=\frac{CVaR_{\alpha}(r_{f}-R^{out})}{CVaR_{\beta}(R^{out}-r_{f})},

with
r_{f}=0
and where
\alpha=\beta=10\%
. A higher Rachev ratio is clearly preferred.

-

The Turnover
(
DeMiguel et al. 2009
,
Turn
,)
evaluates the amount of trading required to perform in practice the portfolio strategy, and is defined as

\mbox{Turn}=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mid x_{s,k}-x_{s-1,k}\mid,

where
S
indicates the number of rebalances,
x_{s,k}
is the portfolio weight of asset
k
after rebalancing, and
x_{s-1,k}
is the portfolio weight before rebalancing at time
s
.
A lower turnover typically indicates better portfolio performance.
We emphasize that this definition of portfolio turnover is a proxy of the effective one, since it evaluates only the amount of trading generated by the models at each rebalance, without considering the trades due to changes in asset prices between one rebalance and the next.
Hence, by definition, the turnover of the EW portfolio is zero.

-

The Jensen’s Alpha
(
Jensen 1968
,
AlphaJ
,)
is defined as the intercept of the line given by the linear regression of
R^{out}
on
R_{I}^{out}
, namely

\mbox{AlphaJ}=\mathbb{E}[R^{out}]-\beta\mathbb{E}[R_{I}^{out}]\,,

where
\displaystyle\beta=Cov(R^{out},R_{I}^{out})/\sigma^{2}(R_{I}^{out})
and
R_{I}^{out}
is the out-of-sample portfolio return of the Market Index.

-

The Information ratio
(
Treynor and Black 1973
,
InfoR
,)
is defined as the expected value of the difference between the out-of-sample portfolio return and that of the benchmark index divided by the standard deviation of such difference, namely

\mbox{InfoR}=\frac{\mathbb{E}[R^{out}-R_{I}^{out}]}{\sigma[R^{out}-R_{I}^{out}]}.

Clearly, the larger its value, the better the portfolio performance.

-

The Value-at-Risk of the out-of-sample portfolio returns
R^{out}
with a confidence level equal to 5% (
VaR5
).

-

The Omega ratio
(
Harlow and Rao 1989
,
Omega
,)
is the ratio between the average of positive and negative out-of-sample portfolio returns, and is defined as

\mbox{Omega}=\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}[\max\{0,R^{out}-\eta\}]}{\mathbb{E}[\min\{0,R^{out}-\eta\}]}\,,

where
\eta
is fixed to 0.

-

The average number of selected assets (
ave
\#
).

-

The Return on Investment
(
Cesarone et al. 2022a
;
Cesarone et al. 2022b
,
ROI
, see, e.g.,)
, namely the time-by-time return generated by each portfolio strategy over a specified time horizon
\Delta\nu
. ROI is defined as:

ROI_{\tau}=\frac{W_{\tau}-W_{\tau-\Delta\nu}}{W_{\tau-\Delta\nu}}\qquad\tau=T^{in}+\Delta\nu+1,\ldots,T

where
W_{\tau}=W_{\tau-\Delta\nu}\prod_{t=\tau-\Delta\nu+1}^{\tau}(1+R_{t}^{out})
is the portfolio wealth generated by investing at the beginning of the time horizon
the amount of capital
W_{\tau-\Delta\nu}
.
In our experiment, we compute the annual ROI, namely we fix
\Delta\nu
equal to 250 days.

Conclusions

We have proposed a new return-diversification approach to portfolio selection and we have focused on the case of the diversification ratio for general and specific risk measures.
An extensive empirical analysis based on several real-world data sets has shown promising out-of-sample performance results of our approach.

The theoretical and empirical analysis of the models based on our bi-objective approach using different diversification measures is left for future research.
It is also worth investigating further the connections between the Risk Parity and the Maximum Diversification ratio approaches both in the case of volatility and in the case of general risk measures, possibly trying to establish their equivalence under appropriate assumptions.
