On weak stability of shock waves in 2D compressible elastodynamics
Yuri Trakhinin

Abstract
By using an equivalent form of the uniform Lopatinski condition for 1-shocks, we prove that the stability condition found by the energy method in [A. Morando, Y. Trakhinin, P. Trebeschi, Structural stability of shock waves in 2D compressible elastodynamics, Math. Ann. 378 (2020) 1471–1504] for the rectilinear shock waves in two-dimensional flows of compressible isentropic inviscid elastic materials is not only sufficient but also necessary for uniform stability (implying structural nonlinear stability of corresponding curved shock waves). The key point of our spectral analysis is a delicate study of the transition between uniform and weak stability. Moreover, we prove that the rectilinear shock waves are never violently unstable, i.e., they are always either uniformly or weakly stable.

Moreover, for two particular cases for which the Cauchy–Green stress tensor

) always holds).

The main goal of the present paper is to show for general deformations that the stability condition (
25
) found in
[
23
]
by the energy method is not only sufficient but also necessary for uniform stability. It is clear that this can be done only by spectral analysis. However, it seems that the classical tools towards the test of the Lopatinski condition and the uniform Lopatinski condition, as it is described in
[

,

,

,

]
for symmetric hyperbolic systems having the 1-shock property. Roughly speaking, the main idea of
[

]
borrowed from
[

is to carry all the calculations over the unique characteristic incoming in the shock in the region behind of the shock wave.

At the same time, even applying the alternative method from
[

, we are not able to find an explicit form of the roots of the Lopatinski determinant. Instead of this, motivated by
[
1
]
, we analyze the transition between uniform and weak stability (still using the alternative definition of the uniform Lopatinski condition from

by the energy method. Moreover, we show that the rectilinear shock waves are never violently unstable (this was not proved in

). The latter is natural because from the physical point of view the elastic force should play stabilizing role and we know that shock waves in isentropic gas dynamics

are also either uniformly or weakly stable.

The question which is left open in the present paper is that about the deduction of an priori estimate (with a loss of derivatives) for the parameter domain of weak stability. On the one hand, following some constructions of the energy method in
[
3
]
for weakly stable shock waves in gas dynamics, we could write down here an energy a priori estimate with a loss of derivatives for the constant coefficient linearized problem, at least, in the particular case when the Cauchy–Green stress tensor
\rho{F}{F}^{\top}
is diagonal for the unperturbed flow. However, the peculiarity of the energy method in

for weakly stable shocks is such that, roughly speaking, it is unstable against lower-order terms, and so the a priori estimates obtained for the case of constant coefficients cannot be transferred to variable coefficients.

On the other hand, since system (

[

10

, where energy a priori estimates for weakly stable shock waves in isentropic gas dynamics were derived both for constant and variable coefficients. It is worth noting that the structural stability of these shock waves was then proved in
[

) are also always uniformly stable, cf. (
27
).

For the particular deformations for which
\mathcal{F}_{12}=\mathcal{F}_{21}=0
(pure stretching) or
\mathcal{F}_{11}=\mathcal{F}_{22}=0
, the stability condition (
25
) was shown in
[
23
]
by spectral analysis to be not only sufficient but also necessary for uniform stability. Actually, the spectral analysis in
[
23
]
for these two particular cases could be easily generalized to the case
\ell_{0}=0
corresponding to a diagonal Cauchy–Green stress tensor
\mathcal{F}\mathcal{F}^{\top}
. Our goal now is to prove that (
25
) is necessary for uniform stability for general deformations. Moreover, we will show that violent instability never happens, that is maybe evident from the physical point of view.

3
Proof of Theorem (
2.1
)

Standard definitions of the Lopatinski condition and the uniform Lopatinski condition for initial boundary value problems for linear constant coefficient hyperbolic systems were given by Kreiss
[
17
]
. For symmetric hyperbolic systems having the 1-shock property one can use equivalent definitions. They were introduced in
[
27
]
by using ideas of the normal modes analysis in
[

]
for MHD shock waves.
Following
[

]
, we now give them for our problem (

)–(

we give the derivation of the Lopatinski determinant for symmetric hyperbolic systems with the 1-shock property (some points of the analysis are described in Appendix

even in more detail than in

]
).

We first rewrite system (

)–(

where
\mathcal{A}_{0}={\rm diag}\,(1,M^{2}I_{2},I_{4})

\underline{0}^{\top}&-\mathcal{F}_{12}I_{2}&O_{2}&I_{2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{A}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&e_{2}&\underline{0}&\underline{0}\\[7.0pt]

By cross differentiation the perturbation

), (

) form the system

where the matrices
\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha}
(

Following
[

,

]
(see also (

), (

) in Appendix

), we write down the following linear algebraic system for a vector

associated with problem (

\displaystyle(s\mathcal{A}_{0}+\lambda^{+}\mathcal{A}_{1}+{i}\omega\mathcal{A}_{2})X=0\,,

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}\big)\cdot X=0,

(33)

where

,
\eta>0
,
(\xi,{\omega})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(

satisfies

(s\mathfrak{B}_{0}+i\omega\mathfrak{B}_{2}+\mathfrak{B}_{3})\widetilde{U}_{0}=0,

(34)

and
\lambda^{+}=\lambda^{+}(\eta,\xi,\omega)
is a simple root
\lambda

lying strictly in the open right-half complex plane (
\Real\lambda>0
). Note that since our shock waves are 1-shocks, the boundary matrix

has only one negative eigenvalue. Then, in view of Hersh’s lemma
[

Since

]

Definition 3.1

Problem (
29

) satisfies the Lopatinski condition if the Lopatinski determinant

\eta>0

.

Definition 3.2

Problem (
29
), (

\det\mathfrak{L}(\eta,\xi,\omega,\lambda^{+})\neq 0
for all
\eta\geq 0
and
(\xi,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(with
\eta^{2}+\xi^{2}+{\omega}^{2}\neq 0
), where
\lambda^{+}(0,\xi,\omega)=\lim\limits_{\eta\rightarrow+0}\lambda^{+}(\eta,\xi,\omega)
.

We write down the dispersion relation (
35
), which is a polynomial equation for finding the unique
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
:

\Omega^{3}\left(M^{2}\Omega^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}\right)\left(M^{2}\Omega^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}-\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}\right)=0,

(37)

with
\Omega=s+\lambda
,
\sigma_{1}=\mathcal{F}_{11}\lambda+i\omega\mathcal{F}_{21}
and
\sigma_{2}=\mathcal{F}_{12}\lambda+i\omega\mathcal{F}_{22}
. As was shown in
[
23
]
,
\lambda^{+}
is one of the two roots of the equation

M^{2}\Omega^{2}-\sigma_{1}^{2}-\sigma_{2}^{2}-\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2}=0

whose left-hand side is the last multiplier in the left-hand side of (
37
).

Omitting straightforward calculations, we find the vector
\widetilde{U}_{0}
in (
34
), which is determined up to a nonzero factor:

\widetilde{U}_{0}=\bigg(s-\frac{i\ell_{0}}{M^{2}}\omega\,,\;-d_{0}s+\frac{i\ell_{0}}{M^{2}}\omega\,,\;ia_{0}\omega\,,\;-\mathcal{F}_{11}s+i\Big(\frac{a_{0}\mathcal{F}_{21}}{R}+\frac{\ell_{0}\mathcal{F}_{11}}{M^{2}}\Big)\omega\,,\\
ia_{0}\mathcal{F}_{11}\omega\,,\;i\Big(\frac{a_{0}\mathcal{F}_{22}}{R}+\frac{\ell_{0}\mathcal{F}_{12}}{M^{2}}\Big)\omega\,,\;ia_{0}\mathcal{F}_{12}\omega\bigg).

We can then calculate the vector
\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}
appearing in (
33

\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2M^{2}}\big(s\,,\;i\ell_{0}\omega-sM^{2}\,,\;iR(M-M_{1}^{2})\omega\,,\;i\mathcal{F}_{21}\omega-\mathcal{F}_{11}s\,,\;0\,,\;i\mathcal{F}_{22}\omega-\mathcal{F}_{12}s\,,\;0\big).

Replacing the first line of the matrix
s\mathcal{A}_{0}+\lambda^{+}\mathcal{A}_{1}+{i}\omega\mathcal{A}_{2}
with the vector
\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}
considered as a row-vector, we get the matrix
\mathfrak{L}
(see (
32
), (
33
)). Omitting calculations, we obtain

\begin{split}\det\mathfrak{L}=\frac{\beta^{2}\Omega^{2}(\omega^{2}-\lambda^{2})}{2M^{2}}\Big\{(\lambda^{2}-\omega^{2})s&+(M^{2}s-i\ell_{0}\omega)\Omega\lambda+M_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}s\\
&+M_{2}^{2}\omega^{2}\lambda+i\ell_{0}\omega\lambda(s-\lambda)+R(M^{2}-M_{1}^{2})\omega^{2}\Omega\Big\},\end{split}

where

\lambda=\lambda^{+}
, i.e.,
\lambda
should be the solution of equation (
38
) with the property
\Real\lambda>0
for
\eta>0
. Since

\Omega^{2}(\omega^{2}-\lambda^{2})\neq 0
for
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
, the equality
\det\mathfrak{L}=0
is equivalent to

\begin{split}(\lambda^{2}-\omega^{2})s+(M^{2}s-i\ell_{0}\omega)\Omega\lambda+M_{1}^{2}\lambda^{2}s&+M_{2}^{2}\omega^{2}\lambda\\
&+i\ell_{0}\omega\lambda(s-\lambda)+R(M^{2}-M_{1}^{2})\omega^{2}\Omega=0.\end{split}

(39)

The test of the (uniform) Lopatinski condition is thus reduced to the study of solutions
(s,\lambda)
of system (
38
), (
39
) for all real
\omega
.

In view of (
15
) and the fact that

M^{2}\sigma^{2}-\ell_{0}^{2}\beta^{2}=M_{*}^{2}\left(M_{2}^{2}(M^{2}-M_{1}^{2})+M^{2}+(\det\mathcal{F})^{2}\right)>0,

the following three parameters are strictly positive:

K=R(M^{2}-M_{1}^{2})+M_{2}^{2}>0,\quad K_{1}=\frac{(M\sigma-|\ell_{0}|\beta)^{2}}{M_{*}^{4}}>0,\quad K_{2}=1+M_{2}^{2}>0.

For the particular cases
\mathcal{F}_{12}=\mathcal{F}_{21}=0
and
\mathcal{F}_{11}=\mathcal{F}_{22}=0
(for which
\ell_{0}=0
) studied in
[
23
]
by spectral analysis, the domains of uniform and weak stability can be described in terms of the above parameters
K
,
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
. But, as we will see, the same is true for general deformations. We can already check that the stability condition (
25
) is equivalently reformulated as

K<K_{1}+K_{2}.

(40)

It was proved in
[
23
]
by the energy method that (
40
) is sufficient for uniform stability. For simplifying the arguments of our spectral analysis, we will below use this knowledge. Let us assume that

K\geq K_{1}+K_{2}.

(41)

The dispersion relation (
38
) can be rewritten as

M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M_{*}^{2}\lambda^{2}+K_{2}\omega^{2}=2i\ell_{0}\lambda\omega

(42)

whereas equality (
39
) reads

\Omega(M^{2}\lambda s+K\omega^{2})+(M_{*}^{2}\lambda^{2}-K_{2}\omega^{2})s=2i\ell_{0}\lambda^{2}\omega.

(43)

It follows from (
42
) that
M_{*}^{2}\lambda^{2}-K_{2}\omega^{2}=M^{2}\Omega^{2}-2i\ell_{0}\lambda\omega
. Substituting this into (
43
), we obtain

\Omega(M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M^{2}\lambda^{2}+K\omega^{2}-2i\ell_{0}\lambda\omega)=0.

(44)

Since
\Omega\neq 0
for
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
and
\eta>0
, (
44
) is reduced to

M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M^{2}\lambda^{2}+K\omega^{2}=2i\ell_{0}\lambda\omega.

We can thus consider the system

\displaystyle M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M_{*}^{2}\lambda^{2}+K_{2}\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=2i\ell_{0}\lambda\omega,

(45)

\displaystyle M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M^{2}\lambda^{2}+K\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=2i\ell_{0}\lambda\omega

(46)

instead of system (
38
), (
39
). Moreover, we can simplify our arguments below by avoiding a separate consideration of the cases
\ell_{0}\geq 0
and
\ell_{0}<0
. Namely, instead of system (
45
), (
46
) we may consider the system

\displaystyle M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M_{*}^{2}\lambda^{2}+K_{2}\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=2i|\ell_{0}|\lambda\omega,

(47)

\displaystyle M^{2}\Omega^{2}-M^{2}\lambda^{2}+K\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=2i|\ell_{0}|\lambda\omega.

(48)

Indeed, these systems coincide if
\ell_{0}\geq 0
. On the other hand, if
\ell_{0}<0
, then in (
45
), (
46
) we make the change
\widetilde{\omega}=-\omega\in\mathbb{R}
. After dropping tildes we again get system (
47
), (
48
).

The dispersion relation (
47
) has the two roots

\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left(M^{2}s-i|\ell_{0}|\omega+(-1)^{k}\sqrt{M^{2}M_{*}^{2}s^{2}-2i|\ell_{0}|M^{2}s\omega+(K_{2}\beta^{2}-\ell_{0}^{2})\omega^{2}}\right),\quad k=1,2.

In view of (
15
), we have:

\Real\lambda_{1}|_{\omega=0}=\frac{M}{\beta^{2}}(M-M_{*})\eta<0,\quad\Real\lambda_{2}|_{\omega=0}=\frac{M}{\beta^{2}}(M+M_{*})\eta>0\quad\mbox{for}\quad\eta>0.

Hence, by virtue of Hersh’s lemma
[
16
]
,
\Real\lambda_{2}>0
for all
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\lambda^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left(M^{2}s-i|\ell_{0}|\omega\pm\sqrt{M^{2}M_{*}^{2}s^{2}-2i|\ell_{0}|M^{2}s\omega+(K_{2}\beta^{2}-\ell_{0}^{2})\omega^{2}}\right),

(49)

where
\lambda^{-}:=\lambda_{1}
. It follows from (
47
), (
48
) that

\lambda^{2}=\frac{(K_{2}-K)\omega^{2}}{\beta^{2}}.

(50)

By virtue of (
41
), this implies
\Real\lambda=0
. Since
\Real\lambda^{+}>0
for
\eta>0
, we necessarily have that
\eta=0
, i.e., shock waves cannot be violently unstable and in the parameter domain (
41
) they are, at least, weakly stable.

For
\omega=0
, (
49
) and (
50
) yield
s=0
. Hence, the uniform Lopatinski condition holds for the 1D case because the solution
s=0
is prohibited by the requirement
\eta^{2}+\xi^{2}+\omega^{2}\neq 0
(see Definition
3.2
). That is, we may assume that
\omega\neq 0
. Since the left-hand sides in (
47
) and (
48
) are homogeneous functions of
s
,
\lambda
and
\omega
, without loss of generality, from now on we will suppose that
\omega=1
.

For
\eta=0
,

\Imag\lambda^{\pm}=\delta^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\left(M^{2}\xi-|\ell_{0}|\pm\sqrt{M^{2}M_{*}^{2}\xi^{2}-2|\ell_{0}|M^{2}\xi+\ell_{0}^{2}-K_{2}\beta^{2}}\right).

(51)

In the parameter domain of uniform stability, system (
47
), (
48
) has no roots
(s,\lambda)=(i\xi,i\delta^{+})
. Because of the continuous dependence of this system on
s
and
\lambda
, the passage to weak stability may happen only thanks to the merging of
\delta^{+}
and
\delta^{-}
at a point of transition
\xi=\xi_{*}
. It is clear that for
\xi=\xi_{*}
the square root in (
51
) should vanish:

M^{2}M_{*}^{2}\xi^{2}_{*}-2|\ell_{0}|M^{2}\xi_{*}+\ell_{0}^{2}-K_{2}\beta^{2}=0.

(52)

From (
52
) we find

\xi_{*}=\xi_{*}^{\pm}=\frac{M|\ell_{0}|\pm\beta\sigma}{MM_{*}^{2}},

(53)

and we have

\delta^{+}_{|\xi=\xi_{*}}=\delta^{-}_{|\xi=\xi_{*}}=\delta_{*}=\frac{M^{2}\xi_{*}-|\ell_{0}|}{\beta^{2}}\qquad(\mbox{for}\ \xi_{*}=\xi_{*}^{\pm}).

(54)

Since
\delta^{\pm}
is real, the elementary analysis of the quadratic function in (
52
) shows that

\xi\geq\xi_{*}^{+}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\xi\leq\xi_{*}^{-}.

(55)

Substituting (
53
) into (
54
), we obtain

\delta_{*}=\frac{\sqrt{K_{1}}}{\beta}\quad\mbox{for}\ \xi_{*}=\xi_{*}^{+}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\delta_{*}=-\frac{\sqrt{K_{3}}}{\beta}\quad\mbox{for}\ \xi_{*}=\xi_{*}^{-},

(56)

where

K_{3}=\frac{(M\sigma+|\ell_{0}|\beta)^{2}}{M_{*}^{4}}>K_{1}>0.

From (
50
) and (
56
) we find (for
K>K_{2}
, cf. (
41
)) two possible transitions to weak stability:

K=K_{1}+K_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad K=K_{3}+K_{2}.

Here the point
K=K_{1}+K_{2}
(cf. (
40
) and (
41
)) on the
K
–axis corresponds to
\xi_{*}=\xi_{*}^{+}
and lies to the left from the point
K=K_{3}+K_{1}
corresponding to
\xi_{*}=\xi_{*}^{-}
. Recall that at the left from the point
K=K_{1}+K_{2}
on the
K
–axis we have the domain of uniform stability, see (
40
) (this was proved in
[
23
]
by the energy method). Recall also that for the domain of weak stability system (
47
), (
48
) necessarily has a root
(s,\lambda)=(i\xi,i\delta^{+})
. It follows from (
51
) and (
55
) that

\delta^{+}\geq\frac{M^{2}\xi-|\ell_{0}|}{\beta^{2}}\geq\frac{M^{2}\xi_{*}^{+}-|\ell_{0}|}{\beta^{2}}=\frac{\sqrt{K_{1}}}{\beta},

or
(\delta^{+})^{2}\geq K_{1}/\beta^{2}
. In view of (
50
), the last inequality is written as
K\geq K_{1}+K_{2}
, cf. (
41
). Note that, by virtue of (
51
) and (
55
), the point
K=K_{3}+K_{2}
on the
K
–axis just corresponds to the emergence of the root
(s,\lambda^{-})=(i\xi,i\delta^{-})
for
K\geq K_{3}+K_{1}
whereas system (
47
), (
48
) has the “weakly stable” root
(s,\lambda^{+})=(i\xi,i\delta^{+})
for all
K\geq K_{1}+K_{2}
(in particular, for

). That is, inequality (

.

Appendix A
Lopatinski determinant for symmetric hyperbolic systems with the 1-shock property

Let us consider an abstract
n
-dimensional counterpart of problem (
29
), (
31
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}\partial_{t}U+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{j}\partial_{j}U=0

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{for}\ x_{1}>0,

(57)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}_{0}\partial_{t}U+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\mathfrak{B}_{k}\partial_{k}U+\mathfrak{B}_{n+1}U=0

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{on}\ x_{1}=0,

(58)

where
U=U(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{M}(m,m)
(
\alpha=\overline{0,n}
) are symmetric matrices,
\mathcal{A}_{0}>0
and
\mathfrak{B}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{M}(m-1,m)
(
\alpha=\overline{0,n+1}
). Moreover, we assume that the boundary
x_{1}=0
is not characteristic (
\det\mathcal{A}_{1}\neq 0
) and the matrix
\mathcal{A}_{1}
has one negative and
m-1
positive eigenvalues.

We construct an Hadamard-type ill-posedness example

U_{k}=e^{-\sqrt{k}+k(st+i(\omega^{\prime}\cdot x^{\prime}))}\widetilde{U}(kx_{1})\qquad(k\in\mathbb{N})

(59)

for problem (
57
), (
58
), where

s=\eta+{i}\xi,\quad\eta>0,\quad(\xi,{\omega}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad\omega^{\prime}=(\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}),\quad x^{\prime}=(x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

The substitution of (
59
) into (
57
) and (
58
) gives the boundary value problem

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}\widetilde{U}}{{\rm d}x_{1}}=\mathfrak{A}(s,{\omega}^{\prime})\widetilde{U},\quad x_{1}>0,

(60)

\displaystyle\Big(s\mathfrak{B}_{0}+i\sum_{k=2}^{n}\omega_{k}\mathfrak{B}_{k}+\mathfrak{B}_{n+1}\Big)\widetilde{U}_{0}=0

(61)

for
\widetilde{U}(x_{1})
, where

\mathfrak{A}=\mathfrak{A}(s,\omega^{\prime})=-\mathcal{A}_{1}^{-1}\mathcal{A},\quad\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}(s,\omega^{\prime})=s\mathcal{A}_{0}+{i}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\omega_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k},\quad\widetilde{U}_{0}=\widetilde{U}(0).

Applying the Laplace transform to (
60
), we get
\lambda V-\widetilde{U}_{0}=\mathfrak{A}V
or
(\mathcal{A}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1})V=\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}
for

V=V(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda x_{1}}\widetilde{U}(x_{1})dx_{1}.

Then the inverse Laplace transform gives

\widetilde{U}(x_{1})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\limits_{C}e^{\lambda x_{1}}(\mathcal{A}(s,\omega^{\prime})+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1})^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}\,d\lambda,

where
C
is a contour large enough to enclose all the singularities of the integrand
F(\lambda)=e^{\lambda x_{1}}f(\lambda)
, with

f(\lambda)=(\mathcal{A}(s,\omega^{\prime})+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1})^{-1}\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}
. These singularities are the eigenvalues
\lambda
of
\mathfrak{A}
, which satisfy

\det\Big(s\mathcal{A}_{0}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1}+{i}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\omega_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}\Big)=0.

(62)

Then,

\widetilde{U}(x_{1})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\res_{\lambda=\lambda_{j}}F(\lambda),

where
l\leq m
is the number of the eigenvalues
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
without counting multiplicity.
In view of Hersh’s lemma
[
16
]
, for all
\eta>0
and
(\xi,{\omega}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
equation (
62
) has a
unique
solution
\lambda=\lambda^{+}=\lambda^{+}(\eta,\xi,\omega)
lying strictly in the open right-half complex plane (
\Real\lambda>0
). For
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
we have
e^{\lambda x_{1}}\rightarrow+\infty
as
x_{1}\rightarrow+\infty
. We have a bounded
\widetilde{U}
in (
59
) and thus construct an Hadamard-type ill-posedness example if and only if the residue at this simple
\lambda^{+}
is zero. Since
\lambda^{+}
is a simple pole of the integrand
F(\lambda)
, then

\res_{\lambda=\lambda^{+}}F(\lambda)=e^{\lambda^{+}x_{1}}\res_{\lambda=\lambda^{+}}f(\lambda)
. We have
\res_{\lambda=\lambda^{+}}f(\lambda)=0
if and only if
f(\lambda)
is bounded at
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
. This is true if and only if there exists such a bounded vector
Y
that

(\mathcal{A}(s,\omega^{\prime})+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1})Y=\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0},

(63)

where
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
is our unique root of (
62
) with
\Real\lambda>0
.

A left annihilator
X
of the matrix
\mathcal{A}(s,\omega^{\prime})+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1}
should also annihilate the right-hand side in (
63
):
X^{\top}\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}=0
. Since the matrices are symmetric, we finally have

\displaystyle\Big(s\mathcal{A}_{0}+\lambda\mathcal{A}_{1}+{i}\sum_{k=2}^{n}\omega_{k}\mathcal{A}_{k}\Big)X=0,

(64)

\displaystyle\big(\mathcal{A}_{1}\widetilde{U}_{0}\big)\cdot X=0,

(65)

where
\lambda=\lambda^{+}
is the unique root of (
62
) with
\Real\lambda>0
. Since
\lambda^{+}
is a simple eigenvalue, we can choose
m-1
linearly independent equations of system (
64
). Adding them to equation (
65
), we obtain for the vector
X
a linear system

\mathfrak{L}X=0
whose determinant is the
Lopatinski determinant
. Indeed, problem (
57
), (
58
) is ill-posed (we have so-called violent instability) if and only if the equation
\det\mathfrak{L}(s,\omega,\lambda^{+})=0
has a root
s
with
\Real s=\eta>0
for some
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
