Connecting essential triangulations I: via 2-3 and 0-2 moves
Tejas Kalelkar, Saul Schleimer, and Henry Segerman

Abstract
. Suppose that M M is a compact, connected three-manifold with boundary. We show that if the universal cover has infinitely many boundary components then M M has an ideal triangulation which is essential: no edge can be homotoped into the boundary. Under the same hypotheses, we show that the set of essential triangulations of M M is connected via 2-3, 3-2, 0-2, and 2-0 moves. The above results are special cases of our general theory. We introduce L L –essential triangulations: boundary components of the universal cover receive labels and no edge has the same label at both ends. As an application, under mild conditions on a representation, we construct an ideal triangulation for which a solution to Thurston’s gluing equations recovers the given representation. Our results also imply that such triangulations are connected via 2-3, 3-2, 0-2, and 2-0 moves. Together with results of Pandey and Wong, this proves that Dimofte and Garoufalidis’ 1-loop invariant is independent of the choice of essential triangulation.

Paths of triangulations

The study of combinatorial triangulations, and equivalences between them, dates back to at least the 1920’s.

Outline

We give the necessary background in Section
2
.
We prove Theorem
3.1
in Section
3
.
The idea is as follows.
We start with any triangulation.
We then take the first barycentric subdivision.
This breaks apart any
L
–inessential edges at the cost of introducing many material vertices.
We then remove each material vertex by “identifying” it with an ideal vertex.
The tool we use to do this is a
snake

3.11
.
These must be carefully chosen (using Algorithm
3.22
) to avoid creating any new
L
–inessential edges.

The strategy to prove Theorem
6.1
is to repeatedly improve a path of triangulations connecting two given triangulations.
In Section
4
we apply a result of Casali
[
7
]
to construct a path of
L
–essential triangulations using 2-3 moves, bubble moves, and their inverses (Corollary
4.8
).
Bubble moves increase the number of material vertices in the triangulation.
In Sections
5
and
6
we fix this, replacing the use of bubble moves with 0-2 moves.
This is a difficult procedure.
A bubble move introduces a new material vertex, but 2-3 and 0-2 moves do not.
So we must closely shadow the given sequence of triangulations in which the number of vertices changes.
We do this while not actually adding any material vertices or creating any
L
–inessential edges.

The theorems are stated in terms of triangulations.
However, the proofs mostly deal with the dual objects: special spines, following Matveev.
In this paper we use the more descriptive term
foam
.
Foams and triangulations record the same combinatorial information.
However, pictures of foams are often easier to understand than the corresponding pictures for triangulations.

Passing to a double cover, if necessary, we may assume that

Fix now some

.
Let

.
So

Fix a basepoint

Then the full preimage of

has infinitely many components and we are done.
Suppose instead that

has finite index in

.
In this case we replace

with the finite cover with fundamental group

.
So we may assume that

surjects

.
If the homomorphism induced by inclusion is an isomorphism then

(and
by Perelman’s resolution to the Poincaré conjecture), we deduce that

is homeomorphic to a surface-cross-interval

.
If instead the homomorphism has kernel then the disk theorem

realises

as a boundary connect sum; the decomposing disk has boundary in

.
Induction on the (negative of the) Euler characteristic of

is a compression body (possibly with two-spheres in its internal boundary), with exterior boundary equal to

.

So the full preimage of

Corollary 2.22
.

Suppose that

is a compact and connected three-manifold with boundary.
Suppose that

is infinite.

is an

Any ideal tetrahedron in

has all of its edges

2.23.
Hyperbolic geometry

Our generalisation from essential to
L
–essential triangulations has applications in hyperbolic geometry.
Given a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
we can try to solve

.
These give each tetrahedron

\mathcal{T}
the shape of an ideal hyperbolic tetrahedron.
We record this shape as the cross-ratio of its four points in
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
.
Thurston’s gluing equations for
\mathcal{T}
ensure that the hyperbolic tetrahedra fit together properly about each edge.
For details, see [
[
40
]
, Chapter 4;
[

]
, Section 4.2].

Suppose that

By

, associated to

, and a

.

•

of

is

There is a solution

is conjugate to

.

The following lemma controls the behaviour of non-anchorable representations for manifolds with torus boundary.

.

Suppose that

3.3.
Recovering representations

Thurston
[
40
, Chapter 4]
was the first to generate representations from ideal triangulations.
Weeks’ heuristic algorithm, implemented in SnapPy
[
9
]
, finds triangulations that are not only essential, but also geometric.
SnapPy is also very successful at finding solutions for representations coming from (large) hyperbolic Dehn filling.
However, not every representation can be recovered from every essential triangulation.
There may even be entire components of the representation variety that are “missed” by certain essential triangulations.
See
[
36
, Sections 4 and 11.3]
.
In this section, we show that under mild conditions on
\rho
there exists an ideal triangulation of
M
for which a solution to Thurston’s gluing equations recovers
\rho
.

Previous work by the third author
[
36
]
and Goerner-Zickert
[
14
]
used solutions to alternative sets of equations to generate representations.

Definition 3.4
.

Suppose that
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is an anchorable representation.
Let
\Gamma=\rho(\pi_{1}(M))
.
Suppose that for some
c
in
\Delta_{M}
there is some
z\in\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
such that the orbit
\Gamma\cdot z
is infinite.
Then we say that
\rho
is
infinitely anchorable
(at
c
).
∎

Suppose that
M
is a three-manifold with toroidal boundary whose interior admits a finite volume hyperbolic metric.
Then any discrete and faithful representation is infinitely anchorable.

As a simple non-example, suppose that
M
is the lens space
L(p,q)
, minus a ball.
There is a representation
\rho
mapping the generator to an elliptic element of order
p
.
The boundary
\partial M
is a single sphere.
Thus
\Delta_{M}
is a collection of
p
two-spheres.
For each
c
in
\Delta_{M}
, the set
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
is all of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
.
Thus
\rho
is anchorable, but not infinitely anchorable.

Corollary 3.5
.

Suppose that
M
is a compact, connected, oriented three-manifold with non-empty boundary.
Suppose that
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is infinitely anchorable.
Then there is an ideal triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of

satisfying both of the following equivalent properties.

•

There is an anchoring
L
of
\rho
such that
\mathcal{T}
is
L
–essential.

•

There is a solution
Z
to Thurston gluing equations for
\mathcal{T}
so that
\rho_{Z}
is conjugate to
\rho
.

Combined with Remark
2.19
, this corollary gives the triangulation required for, and recovers the first consequence of, Theorem 1.1 in
[

]
.

Proof of Corollary
3.5
.

By hypothesis
\Delta_{M}
is non-empty.
Since

is anchorable, it admits an anchoring

by Lemma

Since

The first conclusion follows from Theorem

The second follows from Lemma

∎

6.

The goal of this section is to prove the following.

Theorem 6.1

Suppose that

Suppose that

Then the set of

–essential ideal triangulations of

Here is the overall plan.
Suppose that

Appendix A
Many infinitely anchorable representations

We now recall a few definitions from the theory of character varieties over
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
, following
[
5
, Section 3]
.
Suppose that
M
is a compact, connected, oriented three-manifold with non-empty boundary.
Suppose that the interior of
M
admits a finite volume complete hyperbolic metric.
We use
\rho_{0}
to denote a discrete and faithful representation from
\pi_{1}(M)
to
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
.

Suppose that
\rho\colon\pi_{1}(M)\to\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
is any representation.
For any
\gamma
in
\pi_{1}(M)
we use
\operatorname{tr}^{2}_{\rho}(\gamma)
to denote the square of the trace of
\rho(\gamma)
.
This gives a function
\operatorname{tr}^{2}_{\rho}
from
\pi_{1}(M)
to
\mathbb{C}
;
we call this the
character
of
\rho
.
We use
X(M)
to denote the resulting
character variety
.
We use the notation
\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\gamma)\colon X(M)\to\mathbb{C}
to denote the regular function that given the character of
\rho
returns the squared trace
\operatorname{tr}^{2}_{\rho}(\gamma)
.

The goal of this appendix is to prove the following.

Theorem A.1
.

Suppose that
M
is a compact, connected, oriented three-manifold with non-empty boundary.
Suppose that the interior of
M
admits a finite volume complete hyperbolic metric.
Then there is a neighbourhood of
\rho_{0}
(in the Zariski topology on
X(M)
) that consists solely of infinitely anchorable representations.

Proof.

Our hypotheses imply that all boundary components of
M
are tori.
Fix one such boundary component
C
.
Let
c\in\Delta_{M}
be an elevation of
C
.
Fix a point
p\in C
.
Fix generators
\mu
and
\lambda
in
\pi_{1}(C,p)
.

Suppose that
\gamma
lies in
\pi_{1}(M,p)
;
let
\Gamma={\langle\mu,\lambda,\gamma\rangle}
be the corresponding subgroup of
\pi_{1}(M)
.
Choosing
\gamma
appropriately, and appealing to Klein’s “combination theorem”
[
22
, III.16]
,
we may assume that all peripheral elements of
\pi_{1}(M,p)
(in
\Gamma
) are conjugate (in
\Gamma
) into
\pi_{1}(C,p)
.
(For other, somewhat more modern, statements of the combination theorem see
[
12
, page 210]
or
[
27
, page 499]
.)

As we proceed we will impose (finitely many) algebraic inequalities of the form
\operatorname{tr}^{2}(w)\neq r
where
w
is an element in
\Gamma
and where
r
lies in
\mathbb{C}
.
We will always check that our inequalities hold at
\rho_{0}
.
Finally, we will prove that any representation
\rho
satisfying our inequalities is infinitely anchorable.

(A.2)

\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\mu)\neq 0,\quad\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\lambda)\neq 0,\quad\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\gamma)\neq 0

An element of
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{C})
has trace equal to zero if and only if it is conjugate to an order two rotation
[
3
, Theorem 4.3.1]
.
Thus Equation
A.2
holds at
\rho_{0}
because
\rho_{0}
is faithful and because
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion free.

From Equation
A.2
and from Lemma
2.26
we deduce that
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
is non-empty.
Therefore
\rho
is anchorable at
c
.
Choosing framings for the finitely many other torus boundary components, and imposing Equation
A.2
for those meridians and longitudes, implies that
\rho
is anchorable.

(A.3)

\operatorname{tr}^{2}([\mu,\gamma])\neq 4,\quad\operatorname{tr}^{2}([\lambda,\gamma])\neq 4

This holds at
\rho_{0}
because the commutators
[\mu,\gamma]
and
[\lambda,\gamma]
are not conjugate in
\Gamma
into
\pi_{1}(C,p)

and because
\rho_{0}
sends non-peripheral elements to loxodromics.

From Equation
A.3
we deduce that
\rho(\mu)
and
\rho(\lambda)
are not the identity.
Thus
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
contains one or two points.

(A.4)

\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\gamma)\neq 4

This holds at
\rho_{0}
because
\gamma
is non-peripheral.
We impose this condition only to simplify our case analysis.

Case
.

\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
is a single point.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)=\{z\}
.
By
[
3
, Theorem 4.3.6]
, since
\rho(\mu)
and
\rho(\lambda)
commute and are not of order two, they are parabolic.
Thus
\rho(\pi_{1}(C,p))
is infinite.

(A.5)

\operatorname{tr}^{2}([\mu,\gamma\mu\gamma^{-1}])\neq 4,\qquad\operatorname{tr}^{2}([\lambda,\gamma\lambda\gamma^{-1}])\neq 4

This holds at
\rho_{0}
because the commutators
[\mu,\gamma\mu\gamma^{-1}]
and
[\lambda,\gamma\lambda\gamma^{-1}]
are not conjugate in
\Gamma
into
\pi_{1}(C,p)
.

By Equation
A.4
we have that
\rho(\gamma)
is either elliptic or loxodromic.
We deduce from Equation
A.5
that
z
is not a fixed point of
\rho(\gamma)
.
Let
A
be the axis of
\rho(\gamma)
.
We deduce that
\rho(\pi_{1}(C,p))\cdot A
is infinite.
Thus the orbit
\rho(\Gamma)\cdot z
is infinite.
Thus
\rho
is infinitely anchorable, as desired.

Case
.

\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
is a pair of points.

By Equation
A.4
we have that
\rho(\gamma)
is not parabolic.
Thus
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(\gamma)
is also a pair of points.
By Equation
A.3
, by Equation
A.2
, and by
[
3
, Theorem 4.3.6]
we have that
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)\neq\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(\gamma)
.

Subcase
.

\rho(\gamma)
is loxodromic.

Suppose that
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
=
\{z,w\}
with, say,
z\notin\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(\gamma)
.
Thus the orbit
\rho({\langle\gamma\rangle})\cdot z
is infinite, and we are done.

Subcase
.

\rho(\mu)
(or
\rho(\lambda)
) is loxodromic.

Set
\delta=\gamma\mu\gamma^{-1}
.
Note that
\rho(\delta)
is loxodromic.
By Equation
A.5
we have that
\delta
does not commute with
\mu
.
Again applying
[
3
, Theorem 4.3.6]
we have that
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)\neq\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(\delta)
.
So we may apply the subcase immediately above with
\delta
in place of
\gamma
.

Subcase
.

\rho(\mu)
,
\rho(\lambda)
, and
\rho(\gamma)
are elliptic.

Let
A
be the common axis of
\rho(\mu)
and
\rho(\lambda)
.
Let

be the axis of
\rho(\gamma)
.

Subsubcase
.

A=B
.

This implies that
\mu
and
\gamma
commute
[
3
, Theorem 4.3.6]
, contradicting Equation
A.3
.

Subsubcase
.

A
and
B
meet at a point of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

Recall that
\Gamma={\langle\mu,\lambda,\gamma\rangle}
.
Thus
\rho(\Gamma)
is conjugate into
\operatorname{SO}(3)
.
By Equation
A.3
, we have that
\rho(\Gamma)
is not cyclic (or infinite cyclic).
By Equation
A.2
, we have that
\rho(\Gamma)
is not dihedral (or infinite dihedral).

(A.6)

\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\mu),\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\lambda),\operatorname{tr}^{2}(\gamma)\neq 4\cos^{2}(\pi/n)\quad\text{for $n=3,4,5$}

This holds at
\rho_{0}
because
\rho_{0}
is faithful and
\pi_{1}(M)
is torsion free.

From Equation
A.6
we deduce that
\rho(\Gamma)
is not a platonic subgroup of
\operatorname{SO}(3)
.
We now work in

equipped with the round metric

.
We arrange matters so that
\rho(\gamma)
acts on

by isometries.
We further arrange matters so that
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)=\{N,S\}
, the north and south poles.

Claim
.

The orbit
\rho(\Gamma)\cdot N
is infinite.

Given this,

is infinitely anchorable, as desired.

Proof of Claim.

Suppose not.
Pick some

in the orbit that minimises

.
Since

is neither cyclic nor dihedral,
\rho(\gamma)
does not fix and does not interchange

and

.
Thus

.

Since the orbit is finite, we deduce that
\rho(\mu)
is finite order.
By Equation

we have that
\rho(\mu)
is not the identity.
By Equation

we have that the order of

is at least six.
Taking

0<d_{S^{2}}(N^{\prime},m(N^{\prime}))<d_{S^{2}}(N,N^{\prime})

This is a contradiction, and we are done.
∎

Subsubcase
.

A
and

meet, but only at a point of
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{3}
.

In this case,
\rho([\mu,\gamma])
is parabolic, contradicting Equation

.

Subsubcase
.

A
and
B
are disjoint, even at

.

In this case

contains an element
\delta
so that
\rho(\delta)
is not elliptic (see
[

, page 62]
or
[

, page 1119]
).
In fact we may assume that

does not fix both endpoints of

.
We deduce that the orbit of
\operatorname{Fix}_{\rho}(c)
under the action of
\rho(\delta)
is infinite.
Thus
\rho
is infinitely anchorable, as desired.
∎
