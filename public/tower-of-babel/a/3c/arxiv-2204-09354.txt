Concatenation of dishonest Feller processes, exit laws, and limit theorems on graphs. Version of August 7, 2026
Adam Bobrowski

Abstract
. We provide a rather explicit formula for the resolvent of a concatenation of N N processes in terms of their exit laws and certain probability measures characterizing the way the processes are concatenated. As an application, we prove an averaging principle saying that by concatenating asymptotically splittable processes one can approximate Markov chains.

Introduction

1.1.
Not honest stochastic processes as building blocks of more complex systems

In modeling natural phenomena one often encounters stochastic processes that are not honest, that is, are undefined after a random time. Such processes include e.g. so-called explosive Markov chains (and particular cases of birth and death processes among them)
[
26
,
32
,
42
,
71

, Feller processes killed upon exiting a certain region
[

]
, processes generated by fractional Laplace operators perturbed by gradients (see
[

*Eq. (20)), and killed Lévy processes (
[

]
*Section 5.5 and
[

]
*Section 4.5).

In biology, they abound for example in the theory of coagulation and fragmentation processes
[
8
,
13

, and form building blocks of models involving semi-permeable membranes, such as direct or facilitated diffusion of ions and molecules across cell/plasma membranes
[

,

To wit, a randomly moving particle that passes through a membrane can be seen as disappearing from one part of the state-space and reappearing in its other part, and it is natural to see the entire process as built from simpler, not honest ones, defined only in parts of the state-space — see below for more on this subject.
Such a structure of the studied process is visible e.g. in the papers
[

]
and
[

]
(see also
[

]
) devoted to reconciliation of two seemingly different models of so-called fast neurotransmitters, to describing activity of kinases, and to intracellular calcium dynamics. It is also apparent, to name a few, (a) in the work of J. E. Tanner
[

, who studied diffusion of particles through a sequence of permeable barriers (see also Powles et al.
[

[

See also the references given by A. Lejay in
[

In fact, it is the abundance of non-honest processes in the natural sciences that is a primary motivation of this work.
As noted e.g. in Hartwell et al.

1.2.
Non-honest Feller processes and boundary conditions

A number of examples of not honest processes can be found in the theory of Feller processes on domains with boundaries — their behavior at these boundaries is customarily described by means of boundary conditions. For a simple concrete case, consider a standard Brownian motion on the right half-axis
\mathbb{R}^{+}\coloneqq[0,\infty)
which after reaching the boundary point
x=0
remains there for an exponential time with parameter, say,
a\geq 0
, and is undefined later, when — figuratively speaking — the Brownian traveller disappears from the state-space. This process, that will be referred to as
elementary exit
(comp.
[
40
]
*p. 3 or
[
25
]
*p. 19) is related to the boundary condition

af^{\prime\prime}(0)+f(0)=0.

(1.1)

The Robin boundary condition

bf^{\prime}(0)=f(0),

(1.2)

where
b>0
describes a similar process, termed
elastic Brownian motion
: initially it behaves precisely as the reflected Brownian motion but the time it spends ‘at the boundary’ where
x=0
is measured with the help of the celebrated Lévy local time; when an exponential time with parameter
b^{-1}
with respect to the Lévy local time elapses, the process is no longer defined
[
54
,
55
,
57
]
.

The role of the
sticky
, or
slowly reflecting
boundary

af^{\prime\prime}(0)-bf^{\prime}(0)+cf(0)=0,

(1.3)

is similar — see
[
58
]
,
[
67
]
*p. 127 or
[
74
]
*p. 421 — the only difference lies in the fact that here the process has the tendency to stick to the boundary for a longer time than in the reflected Brownian motion.

1.3.
Concatenating processes that are not honest; transmission conditions

In describing such processes it is one of the tricks of the trade to make them honest by adjoining an additional point (sometimes called the cemetery or coffin state) to the state-space and agreeing that from the moment when the process is undefined, it actually stays at this new point forever
[
22
,
14
,
56
,
26
]
. There are, however, other, more engaging ways the process may be continued, that is, extended to a possibly honest process. Interestingly, such a continuation can often be obtained by appropriate manipulations on boundary conditions.

For example, in the case of elementary jump from the boundary at
x=0
, we can require that when the exponential time spent at
x=0
elapses the process should start anew at a point
s
of a locally compact space
S
, and evolve according to the rules governing there. To this end, one imposes the transmission condition

f^{\prime\prime}(0)=a[f(s)-f(0)].

(1.4)

A similar modification of the elastic Brownian motion results from changing the boundary condition (
1.2
) to
the transmission condition

bf^{\prime}(0)=f(0)-f(s),

(1.5)

see
[
20
,
23
,
65
]
, consult also
[
25
]
*p. 66 and
[
18
]
*p. 669, where further references are given.

For a more complex example we refer to
[
5
]
(see also
[
6
]
), where a boundary condition of the form

f(x)=\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mu_{x}

commands the process that leaves the state-space
\Omega
at a point
x
of its boundary to start anew inside the state-space at a randomly chosen point with distribution
\mu_{x}
.

Although processes related to transmission conditions discussed above are motivating examples for our paper, the idea that the process which is no longer defined in its original state-space could be continued in an extended state-space, applies, of course, to more general situations than these described above, and to processes that may have nothing to do with transmission or boundary conditions. In fact, such constructions are known under the name of
concatenation of processes
.

In Section II.14 of Sharpe’s monograph
[
76
]
(pp. 77-84) two processes, say,
X^{1}
and
X^{2}
, the first of which is not honest, are concatenated as follows. A particle starting in the state-space of
X^{1}
moves about according to the law of evolution of
X^{1}
, but when the lifetime of this process is over, the particle jumps to the state-space of
X^{2}
and starts to move according to the law of
X^{2}
. A particular example of such concatenation is described in the classical treatise of Ito and McKean
[
55
]
*p. 105 in intuitive probabilistic terms and by means of transmission conditions.

In the recent paper of F. Werner
[
82
]
a more general construction is provided: given a sequence of Markov processes
X^{n},n\geq 1
their concatenation is defined as follows: the new process starts at the state-space of
X^{1}
, behaves like this process for its lifetime, and then starts anew as
X^{2}
; when the lifetime of
X^{2}
is over, the process starts as
X^{3}
, and so on, until it possibly reaches its coffin state.

Figure 1.
Processes before (on the left) and after concatenation (on the right).

Our general concatenation theorem, that is, Theorem
5.4
, is devoted to the case where there is a finite number
N\geq 2
of processes with values in
disjoint compact topological spaces
S_{i},i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
, and some of them are not honest. Those processes that are not honest have a finite, possibly greater than
1
, number of possible ways of exiting from their state-spaces (think, in particular, of a diffusion process on a finite interval which may exit from this interval by either of its end-points, see Section
6
for more examples). The extended process we construct in the state-space

S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}\coloneqq\dot{\bigcup_{i=1,\dots,N}}S_{i}

(the dot above the union stresses the fact that
S_{i}
are disjoint, ‘u’ for ‘union’) intuitively looks as follows (see Figure
1
): when started at an
x\in S_{i}
the process is initially identical to the original process defined in
S_{i}
; when the latter is no longer defined,
the extended process chooses a random point in
S
, the distribution of its new position depending both on the space it came from and the way the process has left it. Starting from this new position, the process forgets its past and, if its new position happens to be in
S_{j}
, behaves according to the rules governing the original process defined in
S_{j}
. Again, after a random time the original process can be undefined in
S_{j}
; then the extended process chooses a random point in
S
and the choice once more depends on
j
and on the way
S_{j}
was exited, but not on the previous history, and so on.

There is a number of differences between our approach and that presented in
[
82
]
; for example, from the technical point of view, F. Werner works with general strong Markov processes whereas we restrict ourselves to Feller processes. The most significant of differences, however, is that in
[
82
]
the order in which the processes are concatenated is deterministic: after behaving like
X^{n}
the concatenated process starts anew as
X^{n+1}
. In our case, the order is stochastic: after exiting from
S_{i}
the process chooses the point from which it starts anew randomly; moreover, its new position depends on the way it exited from
S_{i}
— this mechanism is described by Feller exit boundary for the process governing evolution in
S_{i}
(see Section
4
).

A very similar situation is studied in
[
59
]
*Section IV, where two metric graphs are joined, and a Brownian motion on this larger graph is constructed by means of Brownian motions constructed beforehand on its components: a particle can filter from one subgraph to the other via so-called shadow vertices. It is clear that our approach fits such constructions better than that in
[
82
]
.

1.4.
The main idea

It should be stressed that, in the cases where there are many exits and many processes to concatenate, differential operators with domains described by transmission conditions of the type (
1.4
)–(
1.5
) are difficult to handle, and more often than not, one needs to develop special, frequently involved techniques to obtain appropriate generation theorems for the related stochastic processes. This was the case with generation theorems for diffusion processes on graphs
[
20
,
23
,
49
,
16
,
50
,
51
,
11
,
37
]
and in thin layers
[
18
,
19
,
28
,
27
]
. The core of the problem was the fact that in these situations it is a priori unclear whether the so-called range condition is satisfied — the other two conditions characterizing generators (and pre-generators) of Feller semigroups are usually easy to check.

The main idea of the present paper is that in dealing with the problem of concatenation of processes that are not honest, the limitations of the previous approaches and technical problems which we face when applying them, disappear, if instead of working with generators we use
resolvents
(see our Section
2
for an explanation of these notions) and Laplace transforms of
exit laws
. Namely, we show that in the rather general context described above, whether concatenation involves a change of boundary conditions or the way the involved generators act, the resolvent of concatenation of processes has the same, quite explicit form — see (
5.5
)–(
5.6
). Remarkably, all terms in the formula for the resolvent have a clear probabilistic interpretation, whereas the analytic methods used in the papers cited above seem to be unrelated to any stochastic intuitions. It should be stressed here that this unification and simplification of the previous approaches is quite impossible without the notion of exit law (see Section
5.7
).

Put otherwise: our formula for the resolvent of a concatenated process provides a simple way of checking the range condition for a candidate for a generator; in fact, it yields the form of the solution to the resolvent equation. Moreover, it is an interesting feature of formula (
5.5
)–(
5.6
) that it allows inferring properties of concatenation of processes from the properties of its constituents.

For example, the fact that the resolvent of the process that is obtained as a concatenation of other processes has a quite explicit and manageable form, provides an efficient way to deal with convergence problems involving processes on graphs or similar structures, like those studied in
[
20
,
23
,
25
,
11
,
18
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
.

To explain, we recall that the classical Trotter–Kato–Neveu theorem
[
22
,
25
,
39
,
47
,
72
]
states that a sequence of semigroups converges to a limit semigroup iff their resolvents converge to the resolvent of the limit semigroup — and this is equivalent to convergence in distribution of the corresponding processes (see
[
56
]
*p. 385). Since generation theorems obtained in
[
20
,
23
,
49
,
50
,
51
,
11
,
37
,
16
]
almost never provide explicit formulae for the resolvent, direct use the Trotter–Kato–Neveu theorem was so far impossible. Instead, quite involved manipulations on generators, based for example on the Sova–Kurtz version of the approximation theorem
[
77
,
39
,
22
,
25
,
60
]
or asymptotic analysis (see e.g.
[
10
]
) had to be performed. As exemplified in our Section
9
, formula (
5.5
) allows us to use the original version of the Trotter–Kato–Theorem, to prove a class of known convergence theorems with greater ease and to obtain new theorems of this type (see further down for more details).

1.6.
An averaging principle

As already mentioned, as a sample application of our general concatenation theorem (Theorem
5.4
), in Section
9
, we revisit the question of fast diffusions on graphs. To recall, in
[
23
]
, following the previous work devoted to reconciling two models of evolution of neurotransmitters
[
20
]
, the following theorem was
proved.

Consider a diffusion process on a graph
G
without loops, and assume that each of its vertices is a semi-permeable membrane through which particles diffusing on the graph’s edges can filter. Permeability of the membrane depends on the edge from which the particles filter and on the edge to which they filter; permeability differs also in two possible vertices by which the edges are connected. Now, assume that diffusion on each edge is accelerated and at the same time permeability of the membranes situated at vertices is lowered so that the fluxes through each of them remain constant. In the limit, as diffusion is faster and faster, by averaging property of diffusion, points in each edge become indistinguishable and so each edge collapses to a single point, and the entire graph becomes a collection of disjoint points. Since the fluxes remain constant, however, these points communicate: the limit process is a Markov chain on the state-space composed of points formed from former edges. Intensities of jumps in the limit Markov chain are functions of fluxes of the approximating diffusions and thus functions of permeability coefficients.

There is a couple of other versions, extensions and generalizations of this result — see
[
10
,
11
,
18
,
49
,
50
,
51
]
where also a spectrum of applications to models of applied mathematics is discussed. In particular, Banasiak et al.
[
10
]
(see also
[
20
]
) expound the fact that in biological systems often some interactions occur at a much faster time scale than other, and thus justify the need for theorems of this type.

To explain the idea of the generalization we present in Section
9
, we consider the space of continuous functions on the closed interval
[0,r]
and the operator
Af=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}
with Feller–Wentzel boundary conditions (see (
6.3
)) — processes generated by such operators, when concatenated, describe diffusions on graphs. If
c
and
d
in (
6.3
) are replaced by
\varepsilon c
and
\varepsilon d
, respectively, and
A
is replaced by
\varepsilon^{-1}A
, in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
we obtain a process with the state-space composed of a single point (all points of the interval
[0,r]
are lumped together). This limit process is described by two independent exponential random variables, say,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
. After
T\coloneqq\min(T_{1},T_{2})
the process leaves its state-space and is no longer defined, if
T=T_{1}
the process leaves through the trace of the left end of the interval, if
T=T_{2}
it leaves through its right end — see Section
6.2
and Example
5
for details.

Such a limit property is shared by a large class of Feller processes, except that the number of exponential variables can be different that
2
— we single out such processes in Definition
9.1
. For instance, in the Walsh-type Skew Brownian motion on a star-like graph discussed in Section
6.3
the number of variables coincides with the number of edges, and in a variety of other examples this number is
1
. Our averaging principle of Section
9
(Theorem
9.2
) says that such processes, when concatenated, in the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
converge to Markov chains.

2.
Preliminaries on Feller semigroups and resolvents

To recall (see e.g.
[
14
,
22
,
56
]
): if
S
is a compact, metrizable and separable space, and
C(S)
is the space of continuous functions on
S
, then a Feller semigroup
is a strongly continuous family of positive contraction operators
\left(T(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
in
C(S)
such that
T(0)=I_{C(S)}
(the identity operator) and

T(t)T(s)=T(t+s)\qquad s,t\geq 0.

Because of the Riesz representation theorem, for each
x\in S
and
t\geq 0
there is a Borel measure
\textsf{m}_{x,t}
on
S
such that
\textsf{m}_{x,t}(S)\leq 1
and

T(t)f(x)=\int_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\textsf{m}_{x,t},\qquad f\in\mbox{$C(S)$};

(2.1)

if
\textsf{m}_{x,t}(S)=1
for all
x
and
t
, the process is said to be conservative or honest.

It is well-known that with each Feller semigroup one can associate a Markov process
\left(X(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}
with càdlàg paths
[
14
,
56
]
(i.e., paths that are right-continuous and possess left limits) in such a way that

T(t)f(x)=E_{x}f(X(t))1_{\{t\leq\tau\}},\qquad f\in\mbox{$C(S)$},t\geq 0,x\in S

(2.2)

where
E_{x}
denotes expectation conditional on
X(0)=x
and
\tau
is the lifetime of the process, that is,
\tau=\tau(\omega)
is the random time up to which the path
X(t,\omega)
is defined. (Comparing Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) we see that
\textsf{m}_{x,t}(\Gamma),\Gamma\subset S
should be interpreted as the probability that the related process starting at
x
at time
0
will be in
\Gamma
at a time
t\geq 0
.)

Feller semigroups are conveniently described by their generators. The generator
A
of a Feller semigroup is defined by

Af=\lim_{t\to 0}t^{-1}(T(t)f-f)

on the domain
D(A)
composed of
f
such that the above strong limit exists (that is, the right-hand side converges to
Af
uniformly on
S
). It is well-known that the generator
A
characterizes the semigroup uniquely (in particular, different semigroups have different generators) and that an operator
A
is a generator of a Feller semigroup (shortly: a Feller generator) in
C(S)
iff the following three conditions are met

1.

A
is densely defined,

2.

A
satisfies the positive maximum principle,

3.

A
satisfies the range condition: for any
g\in C(S)
and
\lambda>0
there is an
f\in D(A)
such that
\lambda f-Af=g.

The semigroup generated by
A
will in what follows be denoted
\mbox{$\left(\mathrm{e}^{t{A}}\right)_{t\geq 0}$}.

In defining Feller semigroups it is customary to require also that the related process is honest or conservative (which comes down to the requirement that
1_{S}\in D(A)
and
A1_{S}=0
) but in this paper, for obvious reasons, we will allow the process to be dishonest. It is easy to see that the process is honest iff
T(t)1_{S}=1_{S}
for all
t\geq 0
and this holds iff
\tau
of equation (
2.1
), that is, the lifetime of the process, is a.s. equal to
\infty
.

An alternative description of a Feller semigroup is provided via Feller resolvents. A family
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
of non-negative operators in
C(S)
is said to be a Feller resolvent if the following conditions are met:

1.

the Hilbert equation holds:

(\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}=R_{\mu}-R_{\lambda},\qquad\lambda,\mu>0,

(2.3)

2.

for each
f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
,
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda R_{\lambda}f=f,

3.

\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}\leq 1_{S}
for all
\lambda>0
.

It may be argued (see the already cited references) that for each Feller resolvent there is a Feller generator
A
such that
R_{\lambda}=\left(\lambda-A\right)^{-1}
, and thus also the related Feller semigroup. Moreover, the related process is conservative iff
\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}=1_{S}
for all
\lambda>0
.

A word about terminology: a family
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
of bounded operators is said to be a pseudoresolvent, if it satisfies the Hilbert equation. This family is said to be regular, if additionally condition 2. given above holds.

for

Proposition 3.1
.

Each locally bounded function
(0,\infty)\ni\lambda\mapsto\ell_{\lambda}\in\mbox{$C(S)$}

\ell_{\lambda}^{(n)}=(-1)^{n}n!(R_{\lambda})^{n}\ell_{\lambda},\qquad n\geq 1

(3.2)

Proof.

Fix a

\|\ell_{\mu}\|\leq M

\|\lambda R_{\lambda}\|\leq 1,

|\lambda-\mu|\frac{M}{\lambda}
in this neighborhood. It follows that, as
\mu\to\lambda

\lambda\mapsto\ell_{\lambda}

To prove (

\displaystyle\frac{\ell_{\mu}^{(n)}-\ell_{\lambda}^{(n)}}{\mu-\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{n}n!}{\mu-\lambda}\left[(R_{\mu})^{n}\ell_{\mu}-(R_{\lambda})^{n}\ell_{\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}n!\left[(R_{\mu})^{n}\left(\frac{\ell_{\mu}-\ell_{\lambda}}{\mu-\lambda}\right)+\left(\frac{(R_{\mu})^{n}-(R_{\lambda})^{n}}{\mu-\lambda}\right)\ell_{\lambda}\right].

Since
(R_{\lambda})^{\prime}=-(R_{\lambda})^{2}

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}n!(R_{\lambda})^{n}\ell_{\lambda}^{\prime}+(-1)^{n+1}n!n(R_{\lambda})^{n-1}(R_{\lambda})^{2}\ell_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n+1}(n+1)!(R_{\lambda})^{n+1}\ell_{\lambda},

as desired.

To recall, a real-valued, non-negative function defined on

st derivative has an opposite sign to its
n
th derivative. The Bernstein Theorem says that a function is absolutely monotone iff it is the Laplace transform of a Borel, possibly infinite, measure on
[0,\infty)
(see
[
43
]
*p. 439). Thus, Proposition
3.1
implies that
if
\ell_{\lambda}
is the Laplace transform of an exit law, then
for each
x\in S
,
\lambda\mapsto\ell_{\lambda}(x)
, being absolutely monotone and satisfying
\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\ell_{\lambda}(x)\leq 1
, is the Laplace transform of a Borel sub-probability measure, say
m_{x}
, on
[0,\infty)
:

\ell_{\lambda}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\,m_{x}(\mathrm{d}t),\quad\lambda>0.

(3.3)

We note that, for a Borel subset
\mathcal{B}
of
[0,\infty)
,
m_{x}(\mathcal{B})
should be interpreted as the probability that the process generated by
A
and starting at
x
will leave
S_{A}
at time
t\in\mathcal{B}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

None of the measures
m_{x},x\in S_{A}
has an atom at
t=0
.

Proof.

The resolvent
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
is regular, that is,
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda R_{\lambda}f=f,f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
. This implies that the left-hand side of (
3.1
) converges to
\ell_{\mu}
, as
\lambda\to\infty
. On the other hand, by (
3.3
),
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\ell_{\lambda}(x)=m_{x}(\{0\})
, showing that the value of the right-hand side of (
3.1
) at a point
x\in S
converges to
\ell_{\mu}(x)-m_{x}(\{0\}).
These two limits cannot be reconciled unless
m_{x}(\{0\})=0.

∎

Corollary 3.3
.

As a by-product of the proof of Proposition
3.2
, we obtain
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\ell_{\lambda}=0
(in the norm of
C(S)
).

Our next theorem establishes existence of an exit law for any non-conservative (not honest) Feller generator
A
.
As already recalled, a Feller generator
A
with resolvent
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
is conservative iff
\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}=1_{S}
for each
\lambda>0.

Since
\|\lambda R_{\lambda}\|\leq 1
and
R_{\lambda}\geq 0
, we see that for nonconservative
A
the function
1_{S}-\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
is non-negative and nontrivial (i.e., it does not vanish everywhere), for all
\lambda>0
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose
A
is not conservative. Then, there exists at least one exit law for
A
.

Proof.

By assumption

\ell_{\lambda}\coloneqq 1_{S}-\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}\geq 0,\qquad\lambda>0

is non-zero and
\|\ell_{\lambda}\|\leq 1.
Moreover,

\ell_{\mu}-\ell_{\lambda}=\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}-\mu R_{\mu}1_{S},

and, because of the resolvent equation for
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
,

\displaystyle(\lambda-\mu)R_{\lambda}\ell_{\mu}

\displaystyle=(\lambda-\mu)R_{\lambda}1_{S}+\mu(\mu-\lambda)R_{\lambda}R_{\mu}1_{S}

\displaystyle=(\lambda-\mu)R_{\lambda}1_{S}+\mu(R_{\lambda}-R_{\mu})1_{S}

\displaystyle=\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}-\mu R_{\mu}1_{S}.

This establishes (
3.1
), and in particular implies representation (
3.3
). This representation in turn, when combined with
\|\ell_{\lambda}\|\leq 1
, implies that the limit
\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\ell_{\lambda}(x)
exists and does not exceed
1
for each
x\in S
.
∎

In what follows, we will write
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
to denote the particular Laplace transform of the exit law found in Theorem
3.4
:

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\coloneqq 1_{S}-\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}.

Example 1
.

Let
S=[0,1]
be the unit interval, and let
Af=f^{\prime}
on the domain composed of continuously differentiable
f
such that
f^{\prime}(1)+\alpha f(1)=0,
where
\alpha\geq 0
is a given parameter. In the process related to
A
, a particle starting at an
x\in[0,1)
moves to the right with constant velocity
v=1
; upon reaching
x=1
it stays there for an exponential time with parameter
\alpha
, and after this exponential time elapses, the process is no longer defined (for
\alpha=0
the process stays at
x=1
for ever, and in particular is well-defined for all
t\geq 0
). For this operator, the semigroup may be given explicitly:

\mathrm{e}^{tA}f(x)=\begin{cases}f(x+t),&t\leq 1-x,\\
\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha(t-1+x)}f(1),&t>1-x.\end{cases}

It follows that

R_{\lambda}f(x)=\int_{x}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(x-y)}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pty+\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(x-1)}\frac{f(1)}{\lambda+\alpha}.

(3.4)

Thus, here, (if
\alpha>0
)

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x)=1-\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(x-1)}\frac{\alpha}{\lambda+\alpha},\qquad x\in[0,1].

The first factor in this product is the Laplace transform of the deterministic time
T=1-x
needed for the process starting at an
x\in[0,1]
to reach
x=1
; the other factor is the Laplace transform of the exponential time spent at the boundary
x=1
. Thus, all in all,
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{A}(x)
is the Laplace transform of the lifetime of the process governed by
A
that starts at
x\in[0,1].
∎

The example presented above is a particular case of the more general result. Namely, we have the following observation.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\tau
be the lifetime of the process governed by a Feller resolvent
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0
. Then
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
is the Laplace transform of
\tau
:

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x)=E_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\tau},\qquad x\in S.

Proof.

By (
2.2
),
\mathrm{e}^{tA}1_{S}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{x}(t\leq\tau)
. On the other hand,

\displaystyle E_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\tau}

\displaystyle=\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(\tau\leq t)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917ptt=1-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\mathbb{P}_{x}(t\leq\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917ptt

\displaystyle=1-\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\mathrm{e}^{tA}1_{S}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917ptt=1-\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}(x),

as desired. ∎

Corollary 3.6
.

We have

\max_{x\in S}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x)<1.

Proof.

For no point
x\in S
can we have
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(x)=1
for this would mean that the lifetime of the process starting at
x
is
0
, contradicting Proposition
3.2
. Since
S
is compact and
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
is continuous, we are done. ∎

Example 2
.

Let
A
be an honest Feller generator. Then, for any
\alpha>0,

A_{\alpha}\coloneqq A-\alpha I
where
I
is the identity operator, is a not honest Feller generator. The process related to
A_{\alpha}
is identical to that related to
A
up to an independent exponentially distributed time
T
with parameter
\alpha
; from this point on, the process generated by
A_{\alpha}
is undefined. In other words,
T
is the lifetime of the modified process. Indeed, since
A
is honest,

1_{S}-\lambda\left(\lambda-A_{\alpha}\right)^{-1}1_{S}=1_{S}-\lambda\left(\lambda+\alpha-A\right)^{-1}1_{S}={\textstyle\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\alpha}}1_{S},

and
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\alpha}
is the Laplace transform of the distribution of
T
.

4.
Exit laws and excessive functions; Feller boundary

Let again
A
be a Feller generator in
C(S)
with resolvent
R_{\lambda},\lambda>0.
A nonnegative function
\phi\in C(S)
such that
\phi\leq 1_{S}
and

\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi\leq\phi,\qquad\lambda>0

(4.1)

is termed
excessive
, see
[
14
,
15
,
33
,
35
,
74
]
. We will say that an excessive
\phi
is nontrivial if
\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi\not=\phi
for at least one
\lambda
(and thus for all
\lambda>0
).

Remark 1
.

In most monographs, the requirement that
\phi\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
is omitted in the definition of excessive function; instead other, milder, regularity conditions are imposed. In this paper, the assumption that the Laplace transform of an exit law is a member of
C(S)
seemed more pleasing.

As it turns out, many Laplace transforms of exit laws may be constructed from nontrivial excessive functions. Namely, calculations presented in Theorem
3.4
show that

\ell_{\lambda}\coloneqq\phi-\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi

(4.2)

satisfies (
3.1
), and thus (being bounded by
1_{S}
) is the Laplace transform of an exit law as long as
\phi
is excessive and nontrivial.

Conversely, suppose we are given the Laplace transform of an exit law
\ell_{\lambda},\lambda>0
. Since, for each
x\in S
,
\ell_{\mu}(x)
increases as
\mu\searrow 0
, and
R_{\lambda}
s are integral operators, we may consider

\phi(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\mu\to 0}\ell_{\mu}(x)\leq 1.

(4.3)

Then, letting
\mu\to 0
in (
3.1
) we obtain (by the Monotone Convergence Theorem),

\ell_{\lambda}(x)=\phi(x)-\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi(x),\qquad x\in S,\lambda>0.

(4.4)

It should be stressed, though, that in this representation of
\ell_{\lambda},\lambda>0
, in contrast to (
4.2
),
\phi
need not be continuous. Nevertheless, the right-hand side makes sense because the operator
R_{\lambda}
, being related to a measurable kernel, has a natural extension to the space of bounded measurable functions.

Even if we restrict ourselves to
\phi\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
, there still remains the question of uniqueness of representation of exit laws in terms of excessive functions. Fortunately, there is a straightforward test for such uniqueness: to make sure that
\phi
in (
4.4
) is determined by
\ell_{\lambda},\lambda>0
it suffices to check that there are no non-zero
f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
such that
f=\lambda R_{\lambda}f
, that is, no non-zero
f\in D(A)
such that
Af=0
.

In what follows we restrict ourselves to exit laws of the form (
4.2
) with
\phi\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
and assume that the kernel of
A
is trivial. Moreover, since,
as we shall see in the next section, our work is motivated by Feller processes that have more than one exit law, we will in fact be concerned with the situation described by the following definition (comp.
[
41
]
or
[
26
]
*Section 3.7).

Definition 4.1
.

We will say that the process governed by
A
has a regular Feller exit boundary with
{\mathpzc k}
exit points, if

(i)

the kernel of
A
is trivial (that is, composed of zero function merely),

(ii)

there are nontrivial excessive functions
\phi^{j},j\in\{1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}\}
for
A
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{\mathpzc k}\phi^{j}=1_{S}.

(4.5)

The Laplace transform of the exit law
\ell_{\lambda}^{j}
related to
\phi^{j}
:

\ell_{\lambda}^{j}\coloneqq\phi^{j}-\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi^{j}

will be somewhat informally called the law for exit through the
j
th exit (or:
j
th gate).

We stress again that
\phi^{j}
are, by definition, continuous and that, by assumption (i), representation in terms of excessive functions is unique. It should be noted, however, that our definition says nothing about uniqueness of representation (
4.5
): it is possible, in particular, that one of
\phi^{j}
s is a sum of two smaller nontrivial excessive functions (compare discussion in
[
41
]
or
[
26
]
*Section 3.7). Therefore, the definition should be understood to mean that the process has
at least

{\mathpzc k}
ways of exiting its state-space, and it is in this sense that this definition is applied in what follows. Nevertheless, in the examples of Section
6
it will be clear from the probabilistic description of the processes involved that there are precisely one, two or
{\mathpzc k}
exits there.

5.
Concatenation of
N
processes

5.1.
Set-up

Suppose we are given
N
Feller processes in
N
separate (i.e., disjoint) compact spaces
S_{1},\dots,S_{N}
, generated by operators
A_{1},\dots,A_{N}
, respectively. To avoid trivialities, we assume that at least one of these generators is not conservative. Since
S_{i}
s can be reordered if necessary, we assume without loss of generality that
the first
M\in\{1,\dots,N\}
processes are not conservative, whereas the remaining ones are conservative.
Moreover, we assume that the process governed by
A_{i},i=1,\dots,M
has a regular Feller boundary with
{\mathpzc k}(i)
exit points: there are excessive functions
\phi^{i,j},j=1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i)
such that

\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\phi^{i,j}=1_{S_{i}},\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

(5.1)

For the corresponding exit laws

\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\coloneqq\phi^{i,j}-\lambda R_{\lambda,i}\phi^{i,j}

this means that

\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}=1_{S_{i}}-\lambda R_{\lambda,i}1_{S_{i}}\eqqcolon\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{i},\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

(5.2)

We construct the resolvent of a Feller process on

S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}\coloneqq\dot{\bigcup_{i=1,\dots,N}}S_{i},

(the dot stresses the fact that
S_{i}
are disjoint; ‘u’ for ‘union’), which is governed by the following rules.

(i)

Conditional on starting at an
x\in S_{i}
, the process is identical to that related to
A_{i}
up to the random time when the latter is no longer defined (if
A_{i}
is conservative, this random time is
\infty
and thus both processes are identical at all times).

(ii)

If and when the process related to
A_{i}
is no longer defined, the process we construct starts afresh at a random point
y\in S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}
, the distribution of its position at this moment depending on the gate through which
S_{i}
was exited. More specifically, this position is described by a Borel sub-probability measure
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
on
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}
. In most applications, the latter measure is supported in
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}\setminus S_{i}
, but this assumption is not needed in this section.

(iii)

If
y
belongs to
S_{j}
, the constructed process is identical to that governed by
A_{j}
up to the random time, when the latter is no longer defined, and so on.

5.2.
More notation

To proceed, we need to establish more notation. First of all, to shorten formulae, we introduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{1,\dots,N\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{1,\dots,M\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{IJ}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(i,j);i\in\mathcal{M},j\in\{1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i)\}\}.

Secondly, it will be convenient to think of an
f\in\mbox{$C(S_{i})$}
as a member of
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
also: to this end we extend
f
to the entire
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}
by agreeing that it equals zero outside of
S_{i}
. This allows thinking of sums of
f_{i}\in C(S_{i})
as members of
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
:

f\coloneqq\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}f_{i};

as above, the dot stresses the fact that we add together functions that are defined on separate spaces. Conversely, for an
f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
we will denote its part in
C(S_{i})
by
f_{i}
, that is,
f_{i}\coloneqq f1_{S_{i}}
or, with the identification described above in mind,
f_{i}=f_{|S_{i}}.

Let
R_{\lambda,i},\lambda>0
denote the resolvent of the
i
th process:

R_{\lambda,i}=\left(\lambda-A_{i}\right)^{-1},\qquad\lambda>0,i\in\mathcal{N}.

The formula

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f=\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}R_{\lambda,i}f_{i},\qquad\lambda>0,f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}

(5.3)

describes the resolvent of the process called
disjoint union
of the involved process (see
[
76
]
*p. 83). In this union there are no jumps between spaces
S_{i},i\in\mathcal{N}
: if random evolution is started at
x\in S_{i}
, the rules of
A_{i}
are obeyed throughout the process’s lifetime. When a particle exists
S_{i}
, the process is left undefined; it never jumps to any other
S_{j}
or back to
S_{i}
. We note the following connection between the exit laws of the right-hand side of
(
5.2
) and
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}},\lambda>0
:

\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}{\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{i}}=1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}-\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\eqqcolon\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}.

(5.4)

5.3.
The definition: first attempt

By the
concatenation of processes
governed by
A_{i},i\in\mathcal{N}
we understand the process with the following resolvent:

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}[R_{\lambda}^{i}f_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\left(\int_{S}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}],\qquad\lambda>0,f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$};

(5.5)

for
i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{M}
, the inner sum above is understood to be zero. The Borel measure
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
which, by assumption, satisfies
0\leq\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})\leq 1
, is the distribution of the point from which the process starts anew after exiting from the
i
th region through the
j
th gate. For some of
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
we may have
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})<1
, some of
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
might in fact be zero. Of course, if all of them are zero,
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
coincides with the resolvent
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}},\lambda>0
of the disjoint union process.

We note also that, because of the convention concerning the inner sum, formula (
5.5
) may equivalently be written as

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\left(\int_{S}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j},

(5.6)

where
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}},\lambda>0
is the resolvent of the disjoint union, defined in (
5.3
).

5.4.
Consistency condition

The first and obvious problem with (
5.5
) or its equivalent form (
5.6
) is that these formulae define
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
via yet undefined integrals

\int_{S}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\qquad(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}.

(5.7)

Fortunately, these may be calculated beforehand from consistency condition for (
5.5
), which we will now derive.

To this end, we change summation indexes
i
and
j
in (
5.6
) to
k
and
l
, respectively, then fix
i\in\mathcal{M}
and
j\in\{1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i)\}
, and integrate both sides of equation (
5.6
) with respect to
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}.
Since
\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}
is supported in
S_{k}
, we obtain

\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}+{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mu_{k,l}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}.

(5.8)

We claim that this consistency condition determines integrals (
5.7
) as functions of

\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\quad(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}\quad\text{ and }\quad\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\quad(i,j),(k,l)\in\mathcal{IJ},

and these may be treated as known prior to defining
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f
.

In the proof of our claim, we consider any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
where

{\mathpzc k}={\mathpzc k}(1)+\dots+{\mathpzc k}(M),

as the concatenation (in the linear algebra sense) of vectors

\begin{matrix}&(u_{1,1}&u_{1,2}&\dots&u_{1,{\mathpzc k}(1)})\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathpzc k}(1)},\\
&\vdots&\vdots&\dots&\vdots\phantom{\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathpzc k}(1)},}\\
&(u_{M,1}&u_{M,2}&\dots&u_{M,{\mathpzc k}(M)})\in\mathbb{R}^{{\mathpzc k}(M)},\end{matrix}

and equip
\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
with the maximum norm
\|u\|=\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}|u_{i,j}|
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Fix
\lambda>0
. For any
v=\left(v_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
there is precisely one
u=\left(u_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
such that

u_{i,j}=v_{i,j}+{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}u_{k,l}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\qquad(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}.

(5.9)

Furthermore,
u\geq 0
whenever
v\geq 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the linear map, say,
N_{\lambda}
, that assigns

\left({\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}w_{k,l}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}

to a
w\coloneqq(w_{i,j})_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}
, has norm smaller than
1
. For, then the series
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}N_{\lambda}^{n}
converges and
u\coloneqq(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}N_{\lambda}^{n})v
is the unique solution to (
5.9
). Moreover,
u\geq 0
provided that
v\geq 0
because
N_{\lambda}
is a non-negative operator.

On the other hand, obviously,

\|N_{\lambda}w\|\leq\|w\|\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},

whereas, by (
5.2
) in the first step and (
5.4
) in the last,

\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\int_{S_{k}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\dot{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}^{k}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}.

(5.10)

This means that

\|N_{\lambda}\|\leq\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}.

Now, since
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
s are sub-probability measures, Corollary
3.6
tells us that
0\leq\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}<1
for each
(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}.
This completes the proof, the set
\mathcal{IJ}
being finite.
∎

In what follows we write
M_{\lambda}\coloneqq I-N_{\lambda}
where
I
is the identity operator in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
. Our lemma says that the operator
M_{\lambda}
is invertible with

M_{\lambda}^{-1}\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}N_{\lambda}^{n}.

5.5.
Back to the definition

Lemma
5.1
allows filling the hole in the definition given in Section
5.3
as follows.
For
\lambda>0
and
f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
we define vectors
v=v(f,\lambda)
and
u=u(f,\lambda)
in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
by

v=\left(\int_{S}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\quad\text{
and }\quad u=(u_{i,j}(f,\lambda))_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}=M_{\lambda}^{-1}v,

and then let
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f
be defined by (cf. (
5.6
))

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}.

(5.11)

Since
\ell^{i,j}_{\lambda}
s are continuous functions,
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
maps
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
to
\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$},
and it is clear that it is a linear operator.

In the next section, we will show that the so-defined operators
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
form a Feller resolvent. Here we would like to argue merely that, for the so-defined
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f
, the vector of integrals
\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}
coincides with
u(f,\lambda):

\widetilde{u}\coloneqq\left(\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}=\left(u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}

(5.12)

Indeed, integrating (
5.11
) over
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}
with respect to
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
, we check that

\widetilde{u}=v+N_{\lambda}u.

But
u
is the only solution to the equation
u=v+N_{\lambda}u
, where
v
is given, and so, in particular,
v+N_{\lambda}u
equals
u
. This establishes the claim.

5.6.
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
is a Feller resolvent in
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})

For the proof that
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
is a Feller resolvent, we need two lemmas. The first of these will help us prove that
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
satisfies the Hilbert equation, the second will be instrumental in proving
\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\leq 1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\lambda,\mu>0
and
f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
,

(\lambda-\mu)u(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)=u(f,\mu)-u(f,\lambda).

In other words,

\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}(\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}-\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\qquad(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle u(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)

\displaystyle=M_{\mu}^{-1}\left(\int_{S}R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}

\displaystyle=M_{\mu}^{-1}\left(\int_{S}[R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}]\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}

\displaystyle=M_{\mu}^{-1}\left(\int_{S}[R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)R_{\lambda,i}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}]\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}},

with the last equality by (
5.3
).
Therefore, the resolvent equation and the definition of the Laplace transform of an exit law show that
(\lambda-\mu)u(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)
equals

\displaystyle M_{\mu}^{-1}\left(\int_{S}R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}

\displaystyle-M_{\mu}^{-1}\left(\int_{S}[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)(\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}-\ell_{\mu}^{i,j})]\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,u(f,\mu)-M_{\mu}^{-1}\left(\int_{S}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)\int_{S_{i}}\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}.

Since, as established at the end of Section
5.5
,
\int_{S}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
is the same as
u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)
,
the second term here equals

M_{\mu}^{-1}M_{\mu}u(f,\lambda)=u(f,\lambda)
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\lambda>0
,

\lambda u(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)\leq\left(\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}.

Moreover, the inequality can be replaced by equality provided that
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})=1
for all
(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}.

Proof.

Let

v=v(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)=\left(\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}},

so that, by definition
u(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)=(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}N_{\lambda}^{n})v.
We have, in other words,
u(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)=\lim_{n\to\infty}u^{n}
, where
u^{0}=v
and
u^{n+1}=v+N_{\lambda}u^{n},n\geq 1.
It suffices, therefore, to show that

\lambda u^{n}\leq\left(\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}},

(5.13)

and we will do this by induction argument.

This condition holds for
n=0
, because
\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\leq 1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}.
Moreover, writing

u^{n}=\left(u_{i,j}^{n}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}},\qquad n\geq 0,

and assuming that (
5.13
) holds for an
n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda u^{n+1}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}u_{i,j}^{n}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}+\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},

(5.14)

because, clearly,
u^{n}_{i,j}\leq\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})\leq 1.
As proved in (
5.10
), the double sum here equals
\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
. Hence,

\lambda u^{n+1}_{i,j}\leq\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}[\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}+\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}]\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\int_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}),

as desired.

We are thus left with proving the last assertion. To this end, we note that, as long as all
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
are equal to
1
, the vector
u\coloneqq\left(\lambda^{-1}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}
satisfies

u=v+N_{\lambda}u,

(5.15)

for
v
defined at the beginning of our proof. Indeed, the
(i,j)
th coordinate of the right hand side is equal to the expression in the second line of (
5.14
), divided by
\lambda.
As proved below (
5.14
), this expression further reduces to
\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})=\frac{1}{\lambda}
. But
u(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)
is the unique solution to equation (
5.15
), and so we must have
u(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)=u
. This shows the claim.
∎

We are finally ready to prove our main theorem in this section, and a fundamental result for all that follows.

Theorem 5.4
.

The family
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
defined by (
5.11
) is a Feller resolvent on
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
. It is conservative if all
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
s are probability measures.

Proof.

We start with the Hilbert equation for
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
. Fix
\lambda,\mu>0
and
f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
. By definition (
5.11
),

\displaystyle R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f

\displaystyle=R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}

\displaystyle=R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}.

Therefore, by the resolvent equation and the definition of an exit law,

\displaystyle(\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f

\displaystyle=R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f-R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)(\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}-\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+(\lambda-\mu)\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}.

This differs from
R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f-R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f
by the sum

\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}[u_{i,j}(f,\lambda)-u_{i,j}(f,\mu)+(\lambda-\mu)u_{i,j}(R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\mu)]\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}.

However, all summands here are zero, by Lemma
5.2
, and so we obtain
(\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f-R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f.
Since
\lambda,\mu
and
f
are arbitrary, this shows that
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
is a pseudoresolvent.

Next, by Lemma
5.1
,
u\geq 0
whenever
f\geq 0
. It follows that the operators
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f,\lambda>0
are non-negative.
Moreover, by Lemma
5.3
,

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j}(1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},\lambda)\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\leq R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}

and the right hand side coincides, by (
5.2
) and (
5.4
), with
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}+\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}=1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}.
Hence,

\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}}\leq 1_{S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}},

(5.16)

and if all
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
s are probability measures, the inequality above can be replaced by equality — this is a consequence of the other part of Lemma
5.3
.

We are thus left with showing that
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=f
. Since
R_{\lambda,i},\lambda>0
are regular, we have
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f=f
and thus it suffices to show that

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\left(\int_{S}\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}=0

(5.17)

However, inequality (
5.16
) combined with non-negativity of the operators
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
shows that

\|\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}\|\leq 1,\qquad\lambda>0.

Therefore, the norm of the sum in (
5.17
) does not exceed

\|f\|\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\|\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\|.

This converges to zero, as
\lambda\to\infty
, because, by Corollary
3.3
,
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\|\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\|=0
for all
(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}
.
∎

5.7.
In (
5.6
) Laplace transforms of exit laws need to be used

As we have see above, the fact that
\ell^{i,j}_{\lambda},\lambda>0
is the Laplace transform of an exit law was crucial in proving the resolvent equation for
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
. In fact, the defining equation (
3.1
) was used both in the proof of Theorem
5.4
and in the proof of Lemma
5.2
, preceding the theorem. The following result may be thought of as a converse to Theorem
5.4
; we prove namely that formula (
5.6
) will not work, unless
\ell^{i,j}_{\lambda}
s are Laplace transforms of exit laws.

Suppose thus that, as in Section
5.1
, we have
N
Feller processes, of which
M
first are not conservative, and that
\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\in\mbox{$C(S_{i})$},(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}
are given functions such that
0\leq\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\leq 1_{S_{i}}
and (
5.6
) defines a resolvent in
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
for all measures
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
. We claim that then, for each
(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ},

(\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu,i}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}=\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}-\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j},\qquad\lambda,\mu>0.

(5.18)

To prove this, we fix
(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}
, choose a
k\not=i
, a point
x\in S_{k}
, and take all measures involved to be zero, except for
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
which we define to be the Dirac measure at
x
. Then (
5.6
) takes the form

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(x)]\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}.

Since
\ell^{i,j}_{\lambda}
is supported in
S_{i}
and
i\not=k
, it follows that
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(x)=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)
, and the formula simplifies to

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)]\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}.

(The resulting resolvent is related to the following concatenation of processes: a particle that moves about initially in
S_{i}
and eventually exits this region by the
j
th gate, starts all over again at the
x\in S_{k}
and moves according to the law
known in
S_{k}
, until the process is possibly no longer defined. However, if the initial position is not in
S_{i}
or the process does not exit through the
j
th gate, after its lifetime is over the process is not continued at all.)

A short calculation yields

(\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=(\lambda-\mu)\bigl[R_{\mu}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)]R_{\mu,i}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}+[R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(x)]\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}\bigr].

(5.19)

Furthermore, since
\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}
is supported in
S_{i}
, so is
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}
, and hence the relation

R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)]R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}

proves that
R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(x)=R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)
(because
x\in S_{k}
and
S_{k}
is disjoint with
S_{i}
). Thus, the right-hand side of (
5.19
) differs from
R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f-R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f
by

[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)]\left((\lambda-\mu)R_{\mu,i}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}+\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}-\ell_{\mu}^{i,j}\right),

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}},\lambda>0
being a pseudoresolvent.

Since,
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
is a pseudoresolvent also, to complete the proof it suffices to find an
f\in\mbox{$C(S_{i})$}
such that
R_{\mu}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)
is non-zero for all
\mu>0
. To this end, let
f
be non-negative and such that
f(x)>0
. Since the paths of the process
X_{k}
with generator
A_{k}
are right-continuous on a set of probability
1
, if we restrict ourselves to the paths starting at
x
, we will have
f(X_{k}(t))>0
for sufficiently small
t\in[0,\infty)
, and for other
t
we will have
f(X_{k}(t))\geq 0
. It follows that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}f(X_{k}(t))\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917ptt>0
for all
\lambda>0.
Therefore, by the Fubini Theorem,

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f(x)=E_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}f(X_{k}(t))\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917ptt>0,\qquad\lambda>0,

as desired.

6.
Examples of regular Feller boundary

In this section we discuss three examples of processes with regular Feller exit boundary composed of finite number of gates (identified with excessive functions), as defined in Definition
4.1
. These processes are Brownian motions on
[0,\infty]
,
[0,r]
where
r>0
, and star-like graphs, respectively, with rather general boundary conditions describing the gates. We will be able to provide explicit formulae for exit laws and related excessive functions that feature in these examples: in the first of them, there is one gate, in the second — two gates, and in the third —
{\mathpzc k}\geq 2
gates.
Moreover, this section exemplifies that fact that Theorem
5.4
together with the result of Section
5.7
can be instrumental in finding formulae for exit laws and excessive functions.

6.1.
Example A:
S=[0,\infty]
; one exit law.

Consider the space
C[0,\infty]
of continuous functions on
\mathbb{R}^{+}=[0,\infty)
with limits at infinity. Let
A
be the operator
Af=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}
with domain composed of twice continuously differentiable functions with
f^{\prime\prime}\in C[0,\infty]
, satisfying the boundary condition

af^{\prime\prime}(0)-bf^{\prime}(0)+cf(0)=0,

(6.1)

where
a,b
and
c
are given non-negative constants with
a+b+c>0.

It is well-known that, as long as
a+b>0
,
A
generates a Feller semigroup on
C[0,\infty]
, and that the resolvent
R_{\lambda}=\left(\lambda-A\right)^{-1},\lambda>0
of
A
is given by (see e.g.
[
25
]
*pp. 17-18)

R_{\lambda}f(x)=C\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+D\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{x}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}(x-y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pty,\qquad x\geq 0,

for all
f\in C[0,\infty]
, where
C=C(\lambda,f)
and
D=D(\lambda,f)
are constants defined as follows:

\displaystyle C=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}y}f(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pty,\quad\text{ }\quad D=\frac{(b\sqrt{2\lambda}-2a\lambda-c)C+2af(0)}{2a\lambda+b\sqrt{2\lambda}+c}.

For
f=1_{S}
, the constants are
C=\frac{1}{2\lambda}
and
D=\frac{1}{2\lambda}\frac{2a\lambda+b\sqrt{2\lambda}-c}{2a\lambda+b\sqrt{2\lambda}+c}
. This renders

\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}=1_{S}-\frac{c}{2a\lambda+b\sqrt{2\lambda}+c}e_{\sqrt{2\lambda}},

where
e_{\sqrt{2\lambda}}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x},x\geq 0.

Therefore, as long as
c>0
, we have
\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}<1_{S}
, that is, the resolvent is not conservative. In this case, the Laplace transform of the lifetime of the related process is

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}=1_{S}-\lambda R_{\lambda}1_{S}=\frac{c}{2a\lambda+b\sqrt{2\lambda}+c}e_{\sqrt{2\lambda}}.

(6.2)

Moreover, as it is easy to see, the kernel of
A
is trivial, and thus we have the first example of a regular Feller boundary with (at least) one exit. An intuitive description of the related process tells us furthermore that this process can exit the state-space only through
0
, and therefore, there is precisely one exit here.

We note also that in this example, the excessive function for the exit law is
1_{[0,\infty]}
: this corresponds to the fact that the process considered here with probability
1
eventually hits
0
, regardless of its starting point.

Two sub-cases are of interest here. First of all, if
b=0
, the origin is a so-called
elementary exit
(comp.
[
40
]
*p. 3 or
[
25
]
*p. 19).
The related process is a standard Brownian motion on the right half axis which, however, after reaching the origin is captured there for an exponential time with parameter
\frac{c}{2a};
after this time is over, the process is no longer defined.

Formula (
6.2
) confirms this description:
\frac{c}{2a\lambda+c}
is the Laplace transform of the exponential time with parameter
\frac{c}{2a}
, whereas
e_{\sqrt{2\lambda}}(x)
is the Laplace transform of the time needed for the standard Brownian motion starting at
x>0
to reach the origin — see
[
55
]
* Eq. 5) p. 26,
[
57
]
*Eq. (8.6) p. 96 or the original
[
66
]
*pp. 221-223.

Secondly, in the case of
a=0
, the boundary condition (
6.1
) describes the
elastic Brownian motion
. This is a process in the right half axis which initially behaves as if it were a reflected Brownian motion. However, the time it spends at the origin is measured by means of the local time of P. Lévy; when an exponential time with parameter
\frac{c}{b}
passes with respect to this local time, the process is left undefined. This description is again confirmed by (
6.2
), because
\frac{c}{b\sqrt{2\lambda}+c}
is the Laplace transform of the distribution of exponential time with parameter
\frac{c}{b}
with respect to the Lévy local time — see
[
55
]
*p. 45, Eq. 1, that is, of the time elastic Brownian motion needs to exit the space after reaching the origin for the first time.

If
a=b=0\not=c
, we encounter the
minimal
Brownian motion — here the Laplace transform of the time needed for the process starting at
x>0
to exit the space is
\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}
, that is, the Laplace transform of the time needed to reach the origin for the first time. Strictly speaking, though,
0
is not in the state-space of the minimal Brownian motion, and the related semigroup is defined in the subspace
C_{0}(0,\infty]
of
C[0,\infty]
of
f
from the latter space which satisfy
f(0)=0
.

For
a>0
, the origin is more ‘sticky’ than in the case of
a=0
, and so the time process spends at the boundary is longer; in the case of
c=0
the process is known as
sticky
or
slowly reflecting
Brownian motion — see

[
58
]
,
[
67
]
*p. 127 or
[
74
]
*p. 421. For non-zero
c
,
\frac{c}{2a\lambda+b\sqrt{2\lambda}+c}
is the Laplace transform of the time needed for the related process to exit the space after reaching the boundary for the first time.

6.2.
Example B:
S=[0,r]
; two exits (with exceptions)

Let
S=[0,r]
be a closed interval of length
r>0
, and let
A
be the operator in
C(S)
given by
Af=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}
on the domain composed of
f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
that are twice continuously differentiable and such that

pf^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p)f^{\prime}(0)+cf(0)=0\quad\text{and}\quad qf^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f^{\prime}(r)+df(r)=0,

(6.3)

where
p,q\in[0,1]
and
c,d\geq 0.

To explain, the condition on the left is a new incarnation of (
6.1
): take
p=\frac{a}{a+b}
and redefine
c
as
\frac{c}{a+b}
. The condition on the right is of similar nature.

From this description it is clear that, as long as
c
and
d
are larger than
0
, there are two exits here. We will find the corresponding exit laws and excessive functions with the help of Theorem
5.4
and the result of Section
5.7
. Since these excessive functions turn out to be continuous and add up to
1_{[0,r]}
, the process considered here has a Feller exit boundary.

Before continuing, we note that a standard argument (see e.g.
[
25
]
*p. 17) can be used to show that
A
satisfies the positive maximum principle and is densely defined. The calculations presented below prove also that it satisfies the range condition, and thus is a generator of a Feller semigroup. The same remark applies to
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
introduced below.

6.2.1.
Auxiliary concatenation

To find exit laws for
A
, we think of
S
as of
S_{1}
in Theorem
5.4
and add an auxiliary space
S_{2}
composed of two points:
S_{2}\coloneqq\{o_{1},o_{2}\}
(
o_{1},o_{2}\not\in[0,r]
) and equip
S_{2}
with discrete topology. An auxiliary process in
S_{2}
is trivial, that is, both points are absorbing: the process starting at one of these points stays there for ever. In other words, its generator is
A_{2}=0
. We concatenate these two processes by requiring that (a) when the process related to
A_{1}=A
exists
[0,r]
through the boundary point
0
, it jumps to
o_{1}
to stay there for ever, and (b) when the process exists through
r
, it jumps to
o_{2}
to stay there for every.

The generator
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
of the concatenated process in
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}=[0,r]\cup\{o_{1},o_{2}\}
is defined as follows. Its domain is composed of
f
which, as restricted to
[0,r]
, are twice continuously differentiable, and satisfy the following transmission conditions:

\displaystyle pf^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p)f^{\prime}(0)+cf(0)

\displaystyle=cf(o_{1}),

\displaystyle qf^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f^{\prime}(r)+df(r)

\displaystyle=df(o_{2}).

(6.4)

Also,
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x),x\in[0,r],
whereas
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(o_{1})=A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f(o_{2})=0
.

Next, we solve the resolvent equation for
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
: given
g\in C(S_{1}\dot{\cup}S_{2})
and
\lambda>0
we find an
f\in D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})
such that
\lambda f-A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=g.
The part of this equation in
C(S_{2})
is very simple and we immediately realize that
\lambda f(o_{i})=g(o_{i}),i=1,2.

To solve the other part we search for
f
of the form

f(x)=C\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+D\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+h_{\lambda}(x),\qquad x\in[0,r]

(6.5)

where
h_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\int_{0}^{r}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}|x-y|}g(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pty
,
such that transmission conditions (
6.4
) are satisfied. In fact, for reasons that will become clear in a moment, we will find a unique
f
satisfying slightly more general conditions:

\displaystyle pf^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p)f^{\prime}(0)+cf(0)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{c}f(o_{1}),

\displaystyle qf^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f^{\prime}(r)+df(r)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{d}f(o_{2}),

(6.6)

where
0\leq\widetilde{c}\leq c
and
0\leq\widetilde{d}\leq d.

6.2.2.
A linear system for
C
and
D

We note that (
6.6
) is fulfilled iff
C
and
D
of (
6.5
)
are solutions to the system of linear equations:

\displaystyle a_{1,1}C+a_{1,2}D

\displaystyle=b_{1}+\widetilde{c}f(o_{1}),

\displaystyle a_{2,1}C+a_{2,2}D

\displaystyle=b_{2}+\widetilde{d}f(o_{2}),

where

\displaystyle a_{1,i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2\lambda p+(-1)^{i}\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-p)+c,

(6.7)

\displaystyle a_{2,i}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{e}^{(-1)^{i+1}\sqrt{2\lambda}r}(2\lambda q-(-1)^{i}\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-q)+d),\qquad i=1,2,

and (since
h_{\lambda}^{\prime}(0)=\sqrt{2\lambda}h_{\lambda}(0)
and
h_{\lambda}^{\prime}(r)=-\sqrt{2\lambda}h_{\lambda}(r)
)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2pg(0)-(2\lambda p-\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-p)+c)h_{\lambda}(0),

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq 2qg(r)-(2\lambda q-\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-q)+d)h_{\lambda}(r).

(6.8)

It is clear that
|a_{1,1}|\leq a_{1,2}
(with equality for
p=1
),
|a_{2,2}|<a_{2,1}
and that neither of
a_{1,2}
and
a_{2,1}
is zero. Hence, the main determinant
\wyr
of the system is negative, and the system has a unique solution.

6.2.3.
Two exit laws

It follows that

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\wyr}\begin{vmatrix}b_{1}&a_{1,2}\\
b_{2}&a_{2,2}\end{vmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+\frac{1}{\wyr}\begin{vmatrix}a_{1,1}&b_{1}\\
a_{2,1}&b_{2}\end{vmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{1}{\wyr}\begin{vmatrix}\widetilde{c}f(o_{1})&a_{1,2}\\
\widetilde{d}f(o_{2})&a_{2,2}\end{vmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+\frac{1}{\wyr}\begin{vmatrix}a_{1,1}&\widetilde{c}f(o_{1})\\
a_{2,1}&\widetilde{d}f(o_{2})\end{vmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}.

(6.9)

The first three terms here correspond to the case of
\widetilde{c}=\widetilde{d}=0
, that is, provide the formula for the resolvent of
A
. The remaining two may be written as

\frac{\widetilde{c}}{\wyr}(a_{2,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{2,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x})f(o_{1})+\frac{\widetilde{d}}{\wyr}(a_{1,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{1,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x})f(o_{2}).

Since

f(o_{i})=\frac{g(o_{i})}{\lambda}=R_{\lambda,i}g(o_{i})=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g(o_{i}),\qquad i=1,2,

formula (
6.9
) is a particular case of (
5.6
) with
N=2,M=1,{\mathpzc k}(1)=2,\mathfrak{p}_{1,1}=\frac{\widetilde{c}}{c}\delta_{o_{1}}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{1,2}=\frac{\widetilde{d}}{d}\delta_{o_{2}}
:

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g+{\textstyle\frac{\widetilde{c}}{c}}[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g(o_{1})]\ell_{\lambda}^{1}+{\textstyle\frac{\widetilde{d}}{d}}[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g(o_{2})]\ell_{\lambda}^{2}

where

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{c}{\wyr}(a_{2,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{2,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}),

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{2}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{d}{\wyr}(a_{1,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{1,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}),\qquad x\in[0,r].

(6.10)

Above, we write
\ell_{\lambda}^{1}
and
\ell_{\lambda}^{2}
instead of
\ell_{\lambda}^{1,1}
and
\ell_{\lambda}^{1,2}
for simplicity and, at the same time, to comply with notation of Section
4
.

The argument of Section
5.7
is in force here and we conclude that
\ell_{\lambda}^{j},j=1,2
are exit laws for
A
. We also note that the resolvent of
A
can be expressed in terms of these laws as follows:

\left(\lambda-A\right)^{-1}g=\frac{b_{1}}{c}\ell_{\lambda}^{1}+\frac{b_{2}}{d}\ell_{\lambda}^{2}+h_{\lambda}.

(6.11)

6.2.4.
Two excessive functions

To find the excessive functions corresponding to exit laws (
6.10
), we let

\phi^{j}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\ell_{\lambda}^{j}(x),\qquad j=1,2,

and write

\ell_{\lambda}^{1}=c\frac{\wyr_{1}}{\wyr}\quad\text{ and }\quad\ell_{\lambda}^{2}=d\frac{\wyr_{2}}{\wyr}

(6.12)

where

\wyr_{1}\coloneqq\begin{vmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}&\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}\end{vmatrix}\quad\text{ and }\quad\wyr_{2}\coloneqq\begin{vmatrix}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}\\[4.0pt]
\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}&\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}\end{vmatrix}.

(6.13)

Then formula (
10.1
) in Appendix shows that

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(x)

\displaystyle=c\frac{d(r-x)+1-q}{cdr+(1-q)c+(1-p)d},

\displaystyle\phi^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=d\frac{cx+1-p}{cdr+(1-q)c+(1-p)d},\qquad x\in[0,r].

(6.14)

For
p=q=1
(this is the case of elementary exits from both interval’s endpoints) these quantities are familiar probabilities that a Brownian motion starting at
x\in[0,r]
hits
0
before hitting
r
, and vice versa, respectively. In view of the well-known relationship between excessive functions and exit probabilities (see
[
35
]
*p. 565), in general these are apparently the probabilities of exiting through
0
and
r
, conditional on the process generated by
A
starting at
x
. Notably,

\phi^{1}+\phi^{2}=1_{[0,r]},

and both
\phi^{j},j=1,2
are continuous functions: with probability one the process eventually exits either through the left-end or the right-end of the interval. It is also quite easy to see that the kernel of
A
is trivial.
Thus, as heralded before, the process considered here has Feller exit boundary with (at least) two exits — it is the intuitive description of the related process that tells us that there are precisely two exits here.

6.2.5.
A particular case

For
p=q=1
(two elementary exits) formulae for excessive functions simplify as follows:

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{r-x}{r}\left(\frac{r}{r-x}\frac{\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-x)}{\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}\right)\frac{c}{2\lambda+c},\qquad x\in[0,r),

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x}{r}\left(\frac{r}{x}\frac{\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x}{\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}\right)\frac{d}{2\lambda+d},\qquad x\in(0,r],

and these relations have a clear interpretation. To wit, the first factor in the formula for
\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)
is the probability that a standard Brownian starting at
x\in[0,r]
will reach
0
before reaching
r
. The second is the Laplace transform of the time needed for the standard Brownian motion to reach
0
, conditional on reaching it before reaching
r
— see
[
57
]
*p. 100. The third is the Laplace transform of the exponential time the process generated by
A
spends at
0
before exiting the state-space. The formula for
\ell_{\lambda}^{2}
is interpreted similarly.

6.2.6.
Another particular case

Another case of interest is
p=1
and
q=0
(elementary exit on the left, elastic boundary at the right). Here,

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2\lambda}\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-x)}{d\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r+\sqrt{2\lambda}\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}\frac{c}{2\lambda+c},

\displaystyle\ell_{\lambda}^{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x}{d\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r+\sqrt{2\lambda}\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r},\qquad x\in[0,r].

It is interesting to note how the change in the nature of the boundary at
r
influences the exit at the left. In the new scenario, a particle reaching
r
for the first time is not captured there for an exponential time as in Section
6.2.5
, but is reflected and can thus exit through
0
. Hence, the probability of exiting through
0
, equaling

\phi^{1}(x)=\frac{1+d(r-x)}{1+dr}
is larger than that obtained in Section
6.2.5
.

6.2.7.
The ‘degenerate’ case: one exit law

If either
c=0
or
d=0
(but not both), the related process has one exit law. For instance, we consider the case of elastic boundary at
0
and reflecting boundary at
r
, that is, the case where
p=q=d=0
whereas
c\not=0.
Then,
\ell_{\lambda}^{2}(x)
reduces to zero, reflecting the fact that the process cannot exit
[0,r]
through the interval’s right end. Moreover,

\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)=\frac{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}(x-r)}{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}\frac{c\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}{c\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r+\sqrt{2\lambda}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r},\qquad x\in[0,r].

The first factor here is the Laplace transform of the distribution of the time needed for the Brownian motion starting at
x\in(0,r)
and reflected at
x=r
to reach
x=0
for the first time (see Appendix
10.1
). Also, the second factor is completely monotone (see Appendix
10.2
) and its value for
\lambda=0
is
1
, proving that it is the Laplace transform of a Borel probability measure on
[0,\infty)
. The second factor thus should be interpreted as the Laplace transform of the time needed to exit
[0,r]
after reaching
x=0
for the first time.

Since
\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)=1
, this exit law corresponds to the excessive function
1_{[0,r]}
, that is,
\ell_{\lambda}^{1}
coincides with
\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}.

6.2.8.
A black sheep: representation in terms of excessive functions is not unique

The case of
p=q=1
,
d=0
and
c>0
is somewhat exceptional (together with the analogous case of
p=q=1
,
c=0
and
d>0
). Here, we have again only one exit, but representation in terms of excessive functions is not unique.

First of all, as in the previous section, in this scenario
\ell_{\lambda}^{2}(x)
reduces to zero. Moreover,

\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)=\frac{c\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-x)}{(2\lambda+c)\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r},\qquad x\in[0,r].

Since
\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(x)=1
for all
x\in[0,r]
, we see that, again,
\ell_{\lambda}^{1}=\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}.
However, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
f(x)=\alpha x,x\in[0,r]
belongs to the kernel of
A
. As a result, for any
\alpha\in[0,\frac{1}{r}]
,

\ell_{\lambda}^{1}=\phi^{\alpha}-\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi^{\alpha}

where

0\leq\phi^{\alpha}(x)\coloneqq 1-\alpha x\leq 1,\qquad x\in[0,r].

6.2.9.
A remarkable property of exit laws

Coming back to the general case and
c,d>0
we note the following remarkable properties of exit laws. To begin with, we note
that (derivatives with respect to
x
)

(\ell_{\lambda}^{j})^{\prime\prime}=2\lambda\ell_{\lambda}^{j},\qquad j=1,2.

(6.15)

Next, we calculate:

\displaystyle p(\ell_{\lambda}^{1})^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle-(1-p)(\ell_{\lambda}^{1})^{\prime}(0)+c\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\wyr}((2\lambda p+c)(a_{2,2}-a_{2,1})-(1-p)\sqrt{2\lambda}(a_{2,2}-a_{2,1}))

\displaystyle=\frac{c}{\wyr}(a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{2,1}a_{1,2})=c.

At the same time

\displaystyle p(\ell_{\lambda}^{2})^{\prime\prime}(0)

\displaystyle-(1-p)(\ell_{\lambda}^{2})^{\prime}(0)+c\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(0)=\frac{c}{\wyr}(a_{1,1}a_{1,2}-a_{1,2}a_{1,1})=0.

Introducing
F_{1}:C^{2}[0,r]\to\mathbb{R}
given by
F_{1}f\coloneqq pf^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p)f^{\prime}(0)+cf(0)
we summarize the ‘algebraic’ nature of exits laws at the interval’s left end as follows:

F_{1}\ell_{\lambda}^{j}=\delta_{1,j}c,\qquad j=1,2,

(6.16)

where
\delta_{1,j}
is the Kronecker delta.
There is also a counterpart of these relations at the right end:

\displaystyle F_{2}\ell_{\lambda}^{j}=\delta_{2,j}d,\qquad j=1,2

(6.17)

where
F_{2}f\coloneqq qf^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f^{\prime}(r)+df(r).
It is worth noting that, (
6.15
)–(
6.17
) in fact determine
\ell_{\lambda}^{1}
and
\ell_{\lambda}^{2}
uniquely.

We also remark that quantities
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
of (
6.8
) are equal to
-F_{1}(h_{\lambda})
and
-F_{2}(h_{\lambda})
, respectively. This together with
\lambda h_{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}h_{\lambda}=g
and (
6.15
)–(
6.17
) yields an easy proof of (
6.11
).

We hasten to add that the properties of exit laws discussed above are not characteristic to this particular example merely. For the process considered in Section
6.1
we similarly have
(\mathcal{L}_{\lambda})^{\prime\prime}=2\lambda\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
and
F\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}=c
where
Ff\coloneqq af^{\prime\prime}(0)-bf^{\prime}(0)+c.
Likewise, for
\ell_{\lambda}^{1},\lambda>0
of Section

we have
(\ell_{\lambda}^{1})^{\prime\prime}=2\lambda\ell_{\lambda}^{1}
and
-(\ell_{\lambda}^{1})^{\prime}(0)+c\ell_{\lambda}^{1}(0)=c.

6.3.
Example C: Walsh-type Brownian motion on a star-like graph

Let
r>0
and a natural number
{\mathpzc k}\geq 2
be given. Moreover, let
S
be the union of
{\mathpzc k}
copies of the interval
[0,r]
, with all left ends identified. In other words,
S
is the star-like graph
K_{1,{\mathpzc k}}
. Continuous functions on
S
can be identified with continuous functions
f
on

\widetilde{S}\coloneqq\bigcup_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\{j\}\times[0,r],\qquad\text{where }{\mathpzc K}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}\},

that satisfy
f(j,0)=f(k,0),j,k\in{\mathpzc K}
; their common value at
0
will be denoted
f(0)
. It will also be handy to write
f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
as a sum of
f_{j}\in C[0,r]
such that
f_{j}(0)=f_{k}(0),j,k\in{\mathpzc K}:

f=\dot{\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}}f_{j}.

Let
\beta\in[0,1)
and
\alpha_{j},j\in{\mathpzc K}
be non-negative numbers such that

\beta+\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}=1.

Also, let
q\in[0,1]
and
d>0
be given. We define an operator
A
in
C(S)
as follows. Its domain is composed of
f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
such that

(a)

f\in C^{2}(S);
that is, for each
j\in{\mathpzc K}
,
f_{j}
is twice continuously differentiable on
[0,r]
and

f^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\dot{\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}

(6.18)

belongs to
C(S)
,

(b)

for each
j\in{\mathpzc K}
,

qf_{j}^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f^{\prime}_{j}(r)+df_{j}(r)=0,

(c)

and

\beta f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}f_{j}^{\prime}(0)

(note that
f^{\prime\prime}(0)
is well-defined by (a), even though
f^{\prime}_{j}(0)
depends on
j
).

Moreover, we agree that

Af={\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}f^{\prime\prime}.

Boundary condition (b) is of the same form as the second relation in (
6.4
), and thus says that each ‘outer’ node of the graph is an exit point for the process generated by
A
. Transmission condition (c) describes the law governing the process at the graph’s center. If
\beta
is zero, this condition is known to describe the Skew Brownian Motion
of J. Walsh — see
[
64
]
*p. 45 eq. (57), consult also
[
83
]
*p. 107 and
[
68
]
*pp. 115-117.
In this process, a particle reaching the center of the graph chooses to continue its further motion on
j
th vertex with ‘probability’
\alpha_{j}
(although not quite) — see the already cited
[
64
]
for a more precise description and many equivalent constructions of this process; in particular, transmission condition (c) with
\beta=0
can be obtained in the limit procedure of Friedlin and Wentzell’s averaging principle
[
46
]
— see
[
45
]
*Thm. 5.1, comp.
[
24
]
*Eq. (3.1). In general, when
\beta
is non-zero, the center is more ‘sticky’ and so the process stays at this point ‘longer’, but no probability mass is lost there (if
\beta
were allowed to be
1
, the
graph’s center would be an absorbing point). In its more general form (i.e., with
\beta\not=0
), condition (c) appears also e.g. in
[
59
]
*Eq. (2.5), with yet one more term, describing loss of probability mass.

From this intuitive description it is clear that there are precisely
{\mathpzc k}
exits here. We will argue that
A
is a Feller generator, and calculate the corresponding Laplace transforms of exit laws and the related excessive functions.

6.3.1.
The positive maximum principle for
A

It is rather easy to be persuaded that
A
is densely defined. Turning to the question of the positive maximum principle, we note that if
f\in D(A)
attains its positive maximum at a point
x
different from the nodes of
S
, we obviously have
Af(x)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(x)\leq 0
. The already recalled argument from
[
25
]
*p. 17 shows also that
Af(x)\leq 0
if
x
is one of the ‘outer’ nodes. Finally, if
x
is the graph’s center, we clearly have
f^{\prime}_{j}(0)\leq 0
for all
j\in{\mathpzc K}
. Hence, if
\beta>0
, we have
Af(0)=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}f_{j}^{\prime}(0)\leq 0.
If
\beta=0
, (c) requires that
f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=0
as long as
\alpha_{j}>0
. Since there is at least one
\alpha_{j}>0
, for at least one
j
we have
f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=0
, and it follows that for this
j
,
f^{\prime\prime}_{j}(0)\leq 0
. But
f
belongs to
D(A)
, and so
f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(0)
does not depend on
j
and we have
f^{\prime\prime}(0)=f_{j}^{\prime\prime}(0),j\in{\mathpzc K}
, proving that
Af(0)\leq 0
.
To check that
A
is a Feller generator, it suffices therefore to prove that it satisfies the range condition. This will be a by-product of the analysis presented in the next section.

6.3.2.
The resolvent equation for
A
and
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}

We proceed as in Section
6.2.1
: we concatenate the process generated by

with the trivial process on

S_{2}\coloneqq\{o_{1},\dots,o_{\mathpzc k}\}

(for
o_{j}\not\in S,j\in{\mathpzc K}

\widetilde{d}_{j}=0,j\in{\mathpzc K}
, see below) provide the proof that

satisfies the range condition and is thus a Feller generator.

Given additionally
\widetilde{d}_{j}\in[0,d],j\in{\mathpzc K}

in
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
, where
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}\coloneqq S_{1}\dot{\cup}S_{2}
and

such that

(a)

,

(b)

for each

,

qf_{j}^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f^{\prime}_{j}(r)+df_{j}(r)=\widetilde{d}_{j}f(o_{j}),

(c)

and

\beta f^{\prime\prime}(0)=\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}f_{j}^{\prime}(0).

For such

, we define
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=\frac{1}{2}f_{|S}^{\prime\prime}
(in particular,
A(f_{|S_{2}})=0
).

Our claim is that
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
is a Feller generator in
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
. The related process, if started in
S
, obeys the rules dictated by

through the
j

to stay there for ever.

Arguing as in Section
6.3.1
, we check that
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
(is densely defined and)
satisfies the positive maximum principle (this is where condition
0\leq\widetilde{d}_{j}\leq d
is used). Hence, we are left with showing that

satisfies the range condition.

To this end, given
\lambda>0
and
g\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
we look for
f\in D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})
satisfying
\lambda f-A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=g

of the form:

\displaystyle f(o_{j})

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-1}g(o_{j}),

\displaystyle f_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=C_{j}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+D_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}+h_{\lambda,j}(x),\qquad x\in[0,r],j\in{\mathpzc K},

(6.19)

where

h_{\lambda,j}(x)\coloneqq\frac{2\beta g(0)}{\sqrt{2\lambda}(\beta-1)}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x-\sqrt{\frac{2}{\lambda}}\int_{0}^{x}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}(x-y)g_{j}(y)\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pty,

(6.20)

for
j\in{\mathpzc K},x\in[0,r]
, and the constants
C_{j}
and
D_{j}
are to be found. We also introduce functionals
F,F_{j}
given by

\displaystyle Ff

\displaystyle\coloneqq\beta f^{\prime\prime}(0)-\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}f_{j}^{\prime}(0)

\displaystyle F_{j}f

\displaystyle\coloneqq qf_{j}^{\prime\prime}(r)+(1-q)f_{j}^{\prime}(r)+df_{j}(r),\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K},

on the common domain of
f
such that
f_{|S}\in C^{2}(S)
.
Hence,
f\in D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})
iff
f_{|S}\in C^{2}(S)
,
Ff=0
and
F_{j}f=\widetilde{d}_{j}f(o_{j}),j\in{\mathpzc K}
.

To continue,
\dot{\sum}_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}f_{j}\in C(\widetilde{S})
belongs to

iff

C_{j}+D_{j}\quad\text{ does not depend on }j\in{\mathpzc K}.

(6.21)

Since
\lambda f_{j}-\frac{1}{2}f_{j}^{\prime\prime}=g_{j},j\in{\mathpzc K}

. If (

(6.22)

Next, as in Section

iff

\displaystyle a_{2,1}C_{j}+a_{2,2}D_{j}=\widetilde{d}_{j}f(o_{j})-F_{j}h_{\lambda},\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K}

where

(6.23)

These relations together yield

(a_{2,2}-a_{2,1})D_{j}=\widetilde{d}_{j}f(o_{j})-F_{j}h_{\lambda}-a_{2,1}f(0),\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K}.

(6.24)

Finally, (c) of the definition of
D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})
holds iff

\beta(2\lambda f(0)-2g(0))=\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\sqrt{2\lambda}\alpha_{j}(C_{j}-D_{j}).

(6.25)

To solve the system (

6.24

6.25

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\lambda}\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}(f(0)-2D_{j})=\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-\beta)f(0)-2\sqrt{2\lambda}\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}D_{j},

and that, by (

(a_{2,2}-a_{2,1})\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}D_{j}=\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}(\widetilde{d}_{j}f(o_{j})-F_{j}h_{\lambda})-a_{2,1}(1-\beta)f(0).

Therefore,

f(0)=\frac{2\beta}{m_{\lambda}}g(0)+\frac{2\sqrt{2\lambda}}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})}\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}(\widetilde{d}_{j}f(o_{j})-F_{j}h_{\lambda}),

(6.26)

where

m_{\lambda}\coloneqq 2\beta\lambda+\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-\beta)\frac{a_{2,1}+a_{2,2}}{a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}}.

Once

is known,

s can be calculated from (
6.22

The fact that the so-derived

defines a solution (which is necessarily unique) to the resolvent equation for

6.3.3.
Exit laws for

Fix

The

of (

6.24
)–(

with constants calculated from this system with

\widetilde{d}_{j}\frac{2\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x}{a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}}f(o_{j})+\frac{2\sqrt{2\lambda}(a_{2,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{2,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x})}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})^{2}}\sum_{k\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{k}\widetilde{d}_{k}f(o_{k}).

Hence, the resolvent of

has the form

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g+\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}{\textstyle\frac{\widetilde{d}_{j}}{d}}[R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g(o_{j})]\ell_{\lambda}^{j},

where
\ell_{\lambda}^{j}\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
is defined by its parts
(\ell_{\lambda}^{i})_{k}\in C[0,r],k\in{\mathpzc K}
as follows

(\ell_{\lambda}^{j})_{k}(x)=\frac{2d\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x}{a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}}\delta_{j,k}+\frac{2d\sqrt{2\lambda}(a_{2,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{2,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x})}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})^{2}}\alpha_{j},

As in Section
6.2.9
, we note that

(\ell_{\lambda}^{j})^{\prime\prime}=2\lambda\ell_{\lambda}^{j},\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K},

and

\displaystyle F_{k}\ell_{\lambda}^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{d(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})}{a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}}\delta_{j,k}+\frac{2d\sqrt{2\lambda}(a_{2,2}a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}a_{2,1})}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})^{2}}\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=d\delta_{j,k},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad j,k\in{\mathpzc K}.

A bit longer calculation (see Appendix
10.4

F\ell_{\lambda}^{j}=0,\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K}.

(6.28)

Together with the observation that
Fh_{\lambda}=0
(

in terms of exit laws (comp. (

\left(\lambda-A\right)^{-1}g=h_{\lambda}-d^{-1}\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}(F_{j}h_{\lambda})\ell_{\lambda}^{j}.

(6.29)

6.3.4.
Excessive functions for
A

Using the formula in Appendix
10.3
, it can be seen that

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\frac{a_{2,1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{2\lambda}x}-a_{2,2}\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{2\lambda}x}}{2\sqrt{2\lambda}}

\displaystyle=d(r-x)+1-q,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}{m_{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d(1-\beta)}{rd+1-q}.

Therefore, as long as
\beta<1
, the excessive function corresponding to the exit through the
j
th outer node is
\phi^{j}\coloneqq\dot{\sum}_{k\in{\mathpzc K}}(\phi^{j})_{k}
where

(\phi^{j})_{k}(x)\coloneqq\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}(\ell_{\lambda}^{j})_{k}(x)=\frac{dx}{rd+1-q}\delta_{j,k}+\frac{d(r-x)+1-q}{rd+1-q}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{1-\beta},

(6.30)

for
x\in[0,r]
. It is clear that
(\phi^{j})_{k}(0)
does not depend on
k
, proving that
\phi^{j}
belongs to
\mbox{$C(S)$}.

Since, as we shall see in the next section, in the case of
\beta<1
, the kernel of
A
is trivial, we conclude that

\ell_{\lambda}^{j}=\phi^{j}-\lambda R_{\lambda}\phi^{j},\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K}.

To interpret (
6.30
), we note that the quantity
\frac{dx}{rd+1-q}
coincides with the right-hand side in the second equation in (
6.14
) with
p=1
. Since in the case of
p=1
the left interval’s end in the example of Section
6.2
is absorbing, this quantity is the probability that a particle performing a Brownian motion on
[0,r]
with right-end boundary described by the second equation in (
6.3
) exits through this boundary without touching
0
in the meantime. Since the nature of the boundary at the
i
th node is the same as in the process just described (see (b) in the definition of
D(A)
),
\frac{dx}{rd+1-q}
is the probability that a Walsh-type Brownian motion starting at the point
x
at the
j
th node will exit through this node’s end without ever going through the graph’s center. On the other hand,
\frac{d(r-x)+1-q}{rd+1-q}
is the probability that a particle starting from this

will reach the graph’s center before exiting from the state-space (necessarily through the
i
th node). Finally,
\frac{\alpha_{k}}{1-\beta}
is the probability that a process starting from the graph’s center will exit the graph through its

th node.

It is an interesting feature of this example that even though all the outer nodes, that is, all the exits, have the same ‘local’ nature (being described by a boundary condition with the same coefficients), the related exit laws differ — see the formulae for
\ell_{\lambda}^{j},\lambda>0
and
\phi^{j},j\in{\mathpzc K}.
The reason for that lies in the graph’s center — because of that point the process has a tendency to sojourn in some vertices more often than in other vertices, and thus exits through one node more often than through the other.

6.3.5.
Triviality of the kernel

Finally, we show that, as long as
\beta\in[0,1)
, the kernel of
A
is trivial so that, in particular, the representation of exit laws in terms of excessive functions is unique. This together with the considerations presented above establishes that the process related to

has a regular Feller exit boundary with
{\mathpzc k}\geq 2
exits.

To this end, we note that if
f\in\Ker A
, then
f_{j}(x)=m_{j}x+b,x\in[0,r],j\in{\mathpzc K}
for certain real numbers
b
and
m_{j},j\in{\mathpzc K}
, because
f_{j}(0)
is to be independent of
j
. Next, condition (b) tells us that we should have

(1-q)m_{j}+d(m_{j}r+b)=0.

(6.31)

It follows that
m_{j}
does not depend on
j
either, and thus can be denoted by
m
. Also, by (c), we need to have
0=\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha_{j}m=(1-\beta)m
, implying
m=0
. Since, by (
6.31
), this forces
b=0
and, consequently,
f=0
, we are done.

For
\beta=1
, the kernel of
A
is not trivial. It contains all functions of the form
f=\dot{\sum}_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}f_{j}
where

f_{j}(x)\coloneqq mx-d^{-1}(1-q+dm)r,\qquad x\in[0,1],j\in{\mathpzc K}.

7.
A sample generation theorem

In the foregoing sections we described concatenated stochastic processes by two apparently different means. In Section
5
we used resolvents, whereas in Section
6
each example was commenced by an account in terms of generator and corresponding boundary and transmission conditions. Although is was more or less obvious from the calculations presented in the examples that these are two descriptions of the same stochastic process, we never proved this formally. This is what we aim to do in this section.

To this end, we come back to generation theorems for diffusions on graphs of the type considered in
[
20
,
23
,
49
,
50
,
11
,
51
,
16
]
. To recall, in these papers, it is imagined that graph’s vertices are semi-permeable membranes. Therefore, it is convenient to think of the graph as the union of, say,
N
, disjoint intervals:

S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}=\dot{\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}\{i\}\times[0,r_{i}],

where, as before,
\mathcal{N}\coloneqq\{1,\dots,N\}
and
r_{i},i\in\mathcal{N}
are positive numbers (edge’s lengths).
Each interval’s (i.e., edge’s) end is considered as different even from the ends of adjacent edges, since it is seen as lying on ‘this side’ of the membrane, as opposed to ‘the other side’ of the membrane. Moreover, the membrane’s permeability
depends both on the edge from which a diffusing particle filters and on the edge to which the diffusing particle filters.

A more precise definition involves
N
quadruples of parameters

(p_{i},c_{i},q_{i},d_{i}),i\in\mathcal{N}

such that
p_{i},q_{i}\in[0,1]
and
c_{i},d_{i}>0
, and
2N
sub-probability measures
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},i\in\mathcal{N}
and
j=1,2
on
S
.
The domain of the generator
A
of the process considered is composed of
f=\dot{\sum}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}f_{i}
such that
f_{i}
is twice continuously differentiable on
[0,r_{i}]
, and the following boundary and transmission conditions are satisfied (cf. (
6.4
))

\displaystyle p_{i}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p_{i})f_{i}^{\prime}(0)+c_{i}f_{i}(0)

\displaystyle=c_{i}\int f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,1},

\displaystyle q_{i}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(r_{i})+(1-q_{i})f_{i}^{\prime}(r_{i})+d_{i}f_{i}(r_{i})

\displaystyle=d_{i}\int f\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,2},\qquad i\in\mathcal{N}

(7.1)

(these are particular examples of non-local Feller–Wentzel conditions).
For such
f
,

Af\coloneqq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}f^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}.

In
[
23
]
(and in
[
20
]
, where
N=3
),
p_{i}
s are zeros; the case of
p_{i}\not=0
is considered in
[
51
]
; the measures
\mathfrak{p}_{i,1}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{i,2}
are convex combinations of Dirac measures at the endpoints of edges that are adjacent to
(i,0)
and
(i,r_{i}),
respectively. In
[
11
]
and
[
37
,
16
]
more general operators and transmission conditions are discussed, including those without a clear probabilistic interpretation. Moreover, analysis is carried out also in spaces other than the space of continuous functions. The requirement that
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
s are convex combinations of Dirac measures at the ends of adjacent edges is natural and means that after filtering through the end of the
i
th vertex, a particle starts at one of the ends of the adjacent edges, choosing an edge with probability dictated by the convex combination. In what follows, however, we will work with general
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
s: all we assume is that they are sub-probability measures.

The latter assumption is necessary for the conclusion that the operator
A
defined above satisfies the positive maximum principle. More precisely, it guarantees that if a positive maximum of a function
f\in D(A)
is attained at one of the vertices, the value of
Af
at this vertex is not positive (cf.
[
25
]
*p. 17 or our Section
6.3.1

Meanwhile, the range condition is a direct consequence of our Theorem

, a bit further down, which, in turn, is a corollary to Theorem

.

Before presenting the theorem, we need some notation. To begin with, for
i\in\mathcal{N}
, let
A_{i}
be the operator in

with domain composed of

functions, that is, of twice continuously differentiable functions, satisfying the boundary conditions

\displaystyle p_{i}f^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p_{i})f^{\prime}(0)+c_{i}f(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q_{i}f^{\prime\prime}(r_{i})+(1-q_{i})f^{\prime}(r_{i})+c_{i}f(r_{i})

\displaystyle=0,

(7.2)

and defined by
A_{i}f=\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}.
In Section

we have established that there are two exit laws, say,

) and (

) with

, respectively.

Also, let
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}},\lambda>0
be the resolvent of the disjoint union of the related processes (see (

)). Finally, let
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
defined by

R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j=1,2}u_{i,j}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j},\qquad g\in\mbox{$C(S)$},\lambda>0,

be the resolvent of the process concatenated by means of the measures

featured in (

); this means that

s are unique solutions to the counterpart of the system (

),
and in fact, see (

u_{i,j}=\int R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\qquad i\in\mathcal{N},j=1,2.

(7.3)

Theorem 7.1
.

For any
\lambda>0
and
g\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
there is a unique
f\in D(A)
solving the resolvent equation
\lambda f-Af=g
. This
f
is

f=R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g.

Proof.

Let
C^{2}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
be the subspace of
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
of
f=\dot{\sum}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}f_{i}
such that
f_{i}\in C^{2}[0,r_{i}]
(that is,
f_{i}
is twice continuously differentiable), and let
\Delta:C^{2}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})\to\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
be given by
\Delta f\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\dot{\sum}_{i\in\mathcal{N}}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}.
Also, let
F_{i,j},i\in\mathcal{N},j=1,2
be the following functionals on
C^{2}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
:

\displaystyle F_{i,1}f

\displaystyle=p_{i}f_{i}^{\prime\prime}(0)-(1-p_{i})f_{i}^{\prime}(0)+c_{i}f_{i}(0),

\displaystyle F_{i,2}f

\displaystyle=q_{i}f^{\prime\prime}_{i}(r_{i})+(1-q_{i})f^{\prime}_{i}(r_{i})+d_{i}f(r_{i}),\qquad i\in\mathcal{N}.

Since the kernel of
F_{i,j}
includes all functions that are equal to zero on the
i
th edge and because of the definition of
A_{k}
, we see that if
f=\dot{\sum}_{k\in\mathcal{N}}f_{k}
is such that
f_{k}\in D(A_{k}),k\in\mathcal{N}
, we have
F_{i,j}f=0
. In particular,
F_{i,j}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g=0
for all
g\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}.

Moreover, see (
6.15
)–(
6.17
),

\Delta\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}=\lambda\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j},\qquad i\in\mathcal{N},j=1,2,

(7.4)

and

\displaystyle F_{i,1}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}=\delta_{1,j}c_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad F_{i,2}\ell_{\lambda}^{i,j}=\delta_{2,j}d_{i},\qquad j=1,2.

(7.5)

Therefore,

F_{i,1}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=F_{i,1}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}+c_{i}u_{i,1}=c_{i}u_{i,1},

and, similarly,
F_{i,2}=d_{i}u_{i,2}
. When combined with (
7.3
), this means that

F_{i,1}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}=c_{i}\int R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,1}\quad\text{ and }\quad F_{i,2}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}=d_{i}\int R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,2},

that is, by (

), that

belongs to

. Moreover, since

is a restriction of
\Delta
and so is the generator of the process of disjoint union,

(\lambda-A)R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=(\lambda-\Delta)R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g=g

(7.6)

where we also used (
7.4
).

We have shown that
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g
is a solution to
\lambda f-Af=g
. To complete the proof, we note that, since
A
satisfies the positive maximum
principle, it is dissipative by
[

]
*Lemma 2.1, p. 165: for any
f\in D(A)
,
\|\lambda f-Af\|\geq\|f\|.
Therefore, the solution is unique. ∎

Corollary 7.2
.

The operator
A
coincides with the generator
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
of the process related to
R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
.

Proof.

We have proved that
D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})\subset D(A)
. Moreover, by (
7.6
),
\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g-A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=g=\lambda R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g-AR_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g
, proving that
AR_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g=A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}R_{\lambda}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}g,g\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}
. In other words,
A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
is a restriction of
A
to
D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})
. On the other hand, both operators are generators of Feller semigroups, and thus are dissipative. This is impossible unless
D(A)=D(A^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}})
(see
[
39
]
*Proposition 4.1, p. 21), as desired.
∎

Before closing this section, we note that the argument presented in the proof of Theorem
7.1
applies to
more general scenarios. First of all, for some
i
s, either
c_{i}
or

may be zero, as long as we avoid the
case described in Section

. In such a scenario there is only one exit for the process related to

and one of transmission conditions in (

(these properties allow writing counterparts of (

)–(
7.5
)). Likewise,
some of the intervals can be replaced by half-lines and

and the corresponding generator can be replaced by the generator of Walsh-type Brownian Motion. In the last scenario, we would perhaps face more than
two transmission conditions for each component of

[
59
]
.

Secondly, even though we restrict our applications to stochastic processes on graphs (which are our main motivation), it is worth stressing that the results of Section
5
apply also to processes with exit laws that are not described by boundary and transmission conditions.

Finally, a comment is also in order on the remarkable properties of exit laws (

Thus, it appears that the analysis presented in this paper is another reflection of the fact that classical analysis has its probabilistic counterpart, with the latter providing intuitions that are not easily visible in the former (cf.

8.
Convergence of semigroups

Our last section (Section
9
) is devoted to an applications of the results obtained in the first part of the paper: we prove an averaging principle for fast processes, of the kind considered before in
[
10
,
20
,
23
,
18
]
. These results hinge on the theory of convergence of semigroups which is recalled briefly here.

The classical Trotter–Kato Theorem (see e.g.
[
47
,
72
]
) says that strongly continuous equibounded semigroups
\mbox{$\left(\mathrm{e}^{t{B_{\varepsilon}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}$},\varepsilon\in(0,1]
in a Banach space
E
converge as
\varepsilon\to 0
to a strongly continuous semigroup
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t{B}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
, that is,

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathrm{e}^{tB_{\varepsilon}}f=\mathrm{e}^{tB}f,\qquad t\geq 0,f\in E,

(8.1)

iff

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\lambda-B_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}f=\left(\lambda-B\right)^{-1}f,\qquad f\in E,

for some/all
\lambda>0
; moreover, then the limit (
8.1
) is uniform in
t
in compact subsets of
[0,\infty)
.
In other words, such regular convergence of semigroups is completely characterized (see also
[
22
,
25
,
39
]
for the Sova–Kurtz version
[
60
,
77
]
of this characterization).

However, in the theory of singular perturbations and in the particular example we are studying in this paper the limit semigroup is strongly continuous only on a subspace of
E
: we are facing a limit theorem of the form

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathrm{e}^{tB_{\varepsilon}}f=\mathrm{e}^{tB}Pf,\qquad t>0,f\in E

(8.2)

where
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t{B}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly continuous semigroup on a subspace
E_{0}
of
E
and
P
is a projection on
E_{0}
(in the sense that
P^{2}=P
and
Pf=f,f\in E_{0}
). Needless to say, in this case the classical theory does not work and, in particular, condition

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\lambda-B_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}f=\left(\lambda-B\right)^{-1}Pf,\qquad f\in E,

(8.3)

for all/some
\lambda>0
is necessary but not sufficient for (
8.2
) (see
[
21
]
or
[
25
]
).

Condition (
8.3
) can imply (
8.2
) provided that the semigroups involved possess additional regularity properties (like, for example, uniform holomorphicity — see e.g.
[
25
]
*Chapters 31 and 41 for details). A different set of conditions guaranteeing that (
8.3
) implies (
8.2
) has been given by T. G. Kurtz
[
39
,
60
,
61
,
62
]
. While Kurtz’s singular convergence theorem is usually expressed in terms of the so-called extended limit of generators, for our subsequent analysis the following resolvent-version will be more practical. This result can be easily deduced e.g. from combined Lemma 7.1 and Theorem 42.2 in
[
25
]
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Suppose
B_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon\in(0,1]
are generators of strongly continuous equibounded semigroups. Suppose also that for some
\lambda>0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\lambda-\varepsilon B_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}=\left(\lambda-A_{0}\right)^{-1}

(8.4)

where
A_{0}
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
\left(\mathrm{e}^{t{A_{0}}}\right)_{t\geq 0}
such that

Pf\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{0}}f,\qquad f\in E

exists. Then condition (
8.3
) (for some
\lambda>0
, with the same
P
) implies (
8.2
), and the limit is uniform in
t
in compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
; for
f\in E_{0}
the limit is uniform in
t
in compact subsets of
[0,\infty).

9.
An averaging principle

9.1.
Asymptotically splittable processes

Before we begin our main subject, let us first think what happens when a non-honest process is accelerated. In other words, how multiplying the generator
A
of a non-honest process by
\varepsilon^{-1}
, where
\varepsilon\ll 1
influences the process. It is quite easy to see that a
\phi
is excessive for
\varepsilon^{-1}A
iff it is excessive for
A
. Also, a
\widetilde{\ell}_{\lambda},\lambda>0
is an exit law for
\varepsilon^{-1}A
iff it is of the form
\tilde{\ell}_{\lambda}=\ell_{\varepsilon\lambda}
where
\ell_{\lambda},\lambda>0
is an exit law for
A
. Since, by the representation of (
3.3
),
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ell_{\varepsilon\lambda}(x)=m_{x}([0,\infty))
we conclude that in the limit of accelerated processes the probabilities of exiting through various exits do not change, but the process’s lifetime is
0
. (
\lambda\mapsto m_{x}([0,\infty))
is the Laplace transform of the Dirac measure at
0
multiplied by
m_{x}([0,\infty))
.)

A more interesting limit is obtained if while the process accelerates, the speed with which probability mass escapes from the state-space is tuned so that the fluxes through the boundary’s exit points are more or less constant. These considerations lead to the following definition.

Let
\mathsf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
be a non-zero vector and let
A_{\varepsilon\mathsf{v}},\varepsilon\in[0,1]
be Feller generators in the space
C(S)
of continuous functions on a compact space
S
. We think of all these generators as describing ‘one stochastic process’ with regular Feller boundary and
{\mathpzc k}
exit points, and of
\varepsilon\mathsf{v}
as a vector of parameters that describe the speeds with which probability mass escapes the state-space through exit points. In particular, we assume that
A_{\mathsf{0}}
is a conservative Feller process. Moreover, to each
\varepsilon
there correspond
{\mathpzc k}
excessive functions describing exits, which we denote

\phi_{\varepsilon}^{j},\qquad j\in{\mathpzc K}(=\{1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}\}).

Definition 9.1
.

We will say that
A_{\mathsf{v}}
is a generator of an asymptotically splittable process if the following conditions are satisfied.

(a)

There are constants
\varrho^{j}\in C(S)
such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\phi_{\varepsilon}^{j}=\varrho^{j}1_{S},j\in{\mathpzc K}
(necessarily
\varrho^{j}\geq 0
and
\sum_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}\varrho^{j}=1
).

(b)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\lambda-A_{\varepsilon\mathsf{v}}\right)^{-1}f=\left(\lambda-A_{\mathsf{0}}\right)^{-1}f,f\in C(S)
.

(c)

A_{\mathsf{0}}
is a conservative Feller generator and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{e}^{tA_{\mathsf{0}}}f=Pf,f\in C(S)
where
P
is a projection of
C(S)
onto the subspace

\mbox{$C^{\flat}(S)$}\subset\mbox{$C(S)$}
of functions that are constant on
S.

(d)

There is a
\gamma>0
such that
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\lambda-\varepsilon^{-1}A_{\varepsilon\mathsf{v}}\right)^{-1}f=\frac{1}{\lambda+\gamma}Pf,f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}
.

We note that in view of representation of exit laws in terms of excessive functions, if (d) holds, condition (a) implies convergence of corresponding exit laws. Denoting these laws by
\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{j},\lambda>0,\varepsilon\in(0,1]
, we obtain namely

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{j}=\frac{\rho_{j}\gamma}{\lambda+\gamma}1_{S},\qquad\lambda>0,j\in{\mathpzc K}.

(9.1)

On the other hand, convergence of exit laws, because of formulae like (
6.11
) and (
6.29
), often leads to (d). We also note that one or several of
\rho^{j}
s in condition (a) can be zero — some exits can be lost in approximation. All these points are exemplified below.

9.2.

In this section we collect a number of typical examples of asymptotically splittable processes.

Example 3
.

In Example

of Section

\mathsf{v}

9.3.
An averaging principle

If
A_{\mathsf{v}}
describes an asymptotically splittable process, conditions (b)–(d) of the definition tell us, by the Kurtz convergence theorem, that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathrm{e}^{\varepsilon^{-1}A_{\varepsilon\mathsf{v}}t}f=\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}Pf,\qquad f\in\mbox{$C(S)$}.

Since
P
is a projection on the space of functions that are constant on
S
, this means in particular that as
\varepsilon\to 0
all points of
S
are lumped together to form one single point of the state-space of the limit process. Furthermore, the factor
\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}
says that after an exponential time, say,
T
, spent at this point, the limit process leaves the space and is no longer defined. In other words, the exponentially distributed time
T
with parameter
\gamma
is the lifetime of the limit process. Condition (a) is more specific about this scenario: it says that a particle escaping the single-point state-space does that through the
j
th gate with probability
\varrho^{j}
: there are independent exponential random variables
T_{j},j\in{\mathpzc K}
with
\Pr(T_{j}\geq t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\varrho^{j}\gamma t}
, each representing waiting time at one of the
{\mathpzc k}
gates. At
T\coloneqq\min_{j\in{\mathpzc K}}T_{j}
(which is exponentially distributed with parameter
\gamma
) the limit process leaves the state-space, and if
T=T_{j}
(which happens with probability
\varrho^{j}
) it does that through the
j
th gate — functions
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\varrho^{j}\gamma}{\lambda+\gamma}
of (
9.1
) are exit laws for the resolvent
\lambda\mapsto\frac{1}{\lambda+\gamma}.

It is clear from the description given above that asymptotically splittable processes are akin to Markov chains.
Our main theorem in this section makes this connection more explicit (see Figure
2
). Namely, it says that if we concatenate
N
asymptotically splittable processes then, by accelerating them while keeping the fluxes of probability mass through the boundaries approximately constant, we obtain a Markov chain. If initially we have
N
processes to concatenate, defined in spaces
C(S_{i}),i\in\mathcal{N}
, then
the state-space of the limit Markov chain is composed of
N
points — its
i
th point is obtained by lumping together all the points in
S_{i}
. Moreover, the intensities of jumps in the limit chain depend on the measures
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
that, to recall, describe starting points of the concatenated process after it exits
S_{i}
through the
j
th gate — see (
9.9
) further down.

Figure 2.
An averaging principle. (a) On the left: Subregions of the state-space, marked with various colors, are separated by ‘gates’ (in red), such as semi-permeable membranes. Here we have two star-like graphs (perhaps with Skew Brownian motion on them), an interval, and a ‘general’ state-space, like in Example
4
, symbolized by the green circle. (b) On the right: If all constituents of the concatenation are asymptotically splittable, and all these processes are accelerated, but fluxes through the gates remain constant, in the limit we obtain a finite state Markov chain. Each point in the limit state-space is obtained by lumping together all points in one of the subregions of the original state-space.

Here are the details. Let
A_{\mathsf{v}_{i}},i\in\mathcal{N}
be the generators of asymptotically splittable processes defined in spaces
\mbox{$C(S_{i})$},i\in\mathcal{N}.
Each
A_{\mathsf{v}_{i}}
is characterized by

((a))

the number of gates
{\mathpzc k}(i)\geq 1
with the corresponding

•

excessive functions
\phi_{\varepsilon}^{i,j},j=1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i)
,

•

exit laws
\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{i,j}=\phi_{\varepsilon}^{i,j}-\lambda\left(\lambda-\varepsilon^{-1}A_{\varepsilon\mathsf{v}_{i}}\right)^{-1}\phi_{\varepsilon}^{i,j},j=1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i)
,

•

coefficients
\varrho^{i,j}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\varrho^{i,j}=1
, describing the probabilities of exiting through gates
j=1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i)
,

((b))

asymptotic exponential lifetime parameter
\gamma_{i}>0
,

((c))

projection
P_{i}
of
C(S_{i})
on
C^{\flat}(S_{i})
,

((d))

the generator
A_{\mathsf{0},i}
of the corresponding honest Feller process.

As in Section
5
we concatenate these processes with the help of sub-probability measures

\mathfrak{p}_{i,j},\qquad i,j\in\mathcal{IJ}

on
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}
by requiring that a process that exits
S_{i}
through the gate
j
starts anew at a random point of
S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}}\setminus S_{i}
, its distribution at this moment being
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
. We stress that in contrast to Section
5
,
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}
is assumed to be supported outside of
S_{i}
:

\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{i})=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,{\mathpzc k}(i).

(9.6)

Let
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}},\lambda>0
be the resolvent of disjoint union of accelerated and tuned processes:

R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f\coloneqq\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}\left(\lambda-\varepsilon^{-1}A_{\varepsilon\mathsf{v}_{i}}\right)^{-1}f_{i}.

Also, let (comp. (
5.11
) with
M=N
)

R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}f=R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}f+\dot{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}u_{i,j,\varepsilon}\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{i,j},\qquad f\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$},

(9.7)

where (see Lemma
5.1
)

\left(u_{i,j,\varepsilon}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}=(I-N_{\lambda,\varepsilon})^{-1}\left(\int_{S}R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny du}}g\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}

for
N_{\lambda,\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathpzc k}
(
{\mathpzc k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathpzc k}(i)
) given by

N_{\lambda,\varepsilon}\left(w_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}=\left({\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}w_{k,l}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}\right)_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}.

(9.8)

This formula defines the resolvent of a concatenated process in which evolution in each
S_{i}
is accelerated while the fluxes of probability mass through all the exits are controlled.

Our main theorem is concerned with the limit behavior of the semigroups related to
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0
; we will show that they converge to the semigroup related to a Markov chain in
\mathcal{N}
with intensity matrix
Q=\left(q_{i,k}\right)_{i,k\in\mathcal{N}},
with the following entries:

q_{i,i}\coloneqq-\gamma_{i},\quad q_{i,k}\coloneqq\gamma_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(i)}\varrho^{i,j}\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{k}),\quad k\not=i,\qquad i\in\mathcal{N}.

(9.9)

Here,
\varrho^{i,j}
is the probability that the approximating process started in
S_{i}
will exit through the
j
th gate, and
\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{k})
is the probability that after exiting through this gate it will start anew somewhere in
S_{k}
.

Theorem 9.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}
be the generator of the semigroup related to
R_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}},\lambda>0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathrm{e}^{t\mathcal{A}_{\varepsilon}^{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny co}}}f=\mathrm{e}^{tQ}Pf,\qquad t>0,g\in\mbox{$C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})$}

with the limit uniform for
t
in compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
. Here,

P
given by

is the projection of
C(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})
onto the space

of functions that are constant on each of the spaces
S_{i}
separately. The space

is isometrically isomorphic to

Q:C^{\flat}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})\to C^{\flat}(S_{\textnormal{\hskip 0.56917pt\tiny u}})

Proof.

Clearly, we are dealing with a particular case of the situation described in Kurtz’s Theorem.

Step 1.

Limit of

, as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
By assumption (points (a) and (d) in Definition
9.1
)
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{k,l}=\frac{\varrho^{k,l}\gamma_{k}}{\lambda+\gamma_{k}}1_{S_{k}},
and in particular

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{S_{k}}\ell_{\lambda,\varepsilon}^{k,l}\,\mathrm{d}\hskip 0.56917pt\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}=\frac{\varrho^{k,l}\gamma_{k}}{\lambda+\gamma_{k}}\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{k})\qquad(k,l),(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}.

Hence, the operators
N_{\lambda,\varepsilon}
of (
9.8
) converge as
\varepsilon\to 0
to

defined by

(9.10)

As in Lemma
5.1
,

\|N_{\lambda,0}\|\leq\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\sum_{l=1}^{{\mathpzc k}(k)}\frac{\varrho^{k,l}\gamma_{k}}{\lambda+\gamma_{k}}\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{k})=\max_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{IJ}}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{\lambda+\gamma_{k}}\mathfrak{p}_{i,j}(S_{k})<1.

10.1.
\lambda\mapsto\frac{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-x)}{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}
is the Laplace transform of the distribution of the time needed for the Brownian motion starting at
x\in(0,r)
and reflected at
x=r
to reach
x=0
for the first time

Formula (8.29) p. 100 in
[
57
]
says that the Laplace transform of the distribution of the time needed for the Brownian motion starting at an
x>0
to reach
0
or
a>x
is

\frac{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}(x-\tfrac{a}{2})}{\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}\tfrac{a}{2}}.

On the other hand, point
0
is reached by the Brownian motion starting at
x
and reflected at
r
iff
0
or
2r
is reached by the ordinary (not reflected) Brownian motion starting at
x
. Hence, the Laplace transform we are searching for is obtained by replacing
a
by
2r
in the formula above.

10.2.
k_{\lambda}\coloneqq\frac{c\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}{c\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r+\sqrt{2\lambda}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}
is completely monotone

The proof is based on the following expansion of
\cosh z+az\sinh z
, where
a\not=0
is a constant, into an infinite product (take e.g.
d=0
in formula 13. p. 263 in
[
48
]
) which can be proved using the Hadamard Factorization Theorem
[
80
]
. Let
p_{n}=p_{n}(a),n\geq 0
be all positive solutions to the equation
\tan p=\frac{1}{ap}
(arranged in the increasing order). Then

\cosh z+az\sinh z=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z^{2}}{p_{n}^{2}}\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}.

For
z=\sqrt{2\lambda}r
and
a=\frac{1}{rc}
, this yields

\frac{c}{c\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r+\sqrt{2\lambda}\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{2r^{2}\lambda+p_{n}^{2}},\qquad\lambda>0,

where
p_{n}=p_{n}(\frac{1}{rc}).
Analogously (see
[
48
]
p. 263 formula 1.),

\cosh z=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z^{2}}{q_{n}^{2}}\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C},

where
q_{n}=\tfrac{\pi}{2}+n\pi.
Therefore,

k_{\lambda}=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{q_{n}^{2}}\frac{q_{n}^{2}+2\lambda r^{2}}{p_{n}^{2}+2\lambda r^{2}}\right)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{p_{n}^{2}}{q_{n}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{q_{n}^{2}-p^{2}_{n}}{p_{n}^{2}+2\lambda r^{2}}\right)\right].

Since, by definition,
p_{n}<q_{n}
, it follows that each factor in this expansion is completely monotone.
Recalling that finite products of completely monotone functions are completely monotone, we see that for each
N\in\mathbb{N}
the product of
N
first factors of
k_{\lambda}
is completely monotone.
Therefore, by the extended continuity
theorem for the Laplace transform (see
[
43
]
*p. 433) combined with the Bernstein Theorem, so is
k_{\lambda}
.

10.3.
Formulae for determinants of Section
6.2
and their limit forms

We note the following more explicit formulae for the main determinant
\wyr
and determinants
\wyr_{1}
and
\wyr_{2}
of (
6.13
):

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\wyr

\displaystyle=-[(2\lambda q+d)(2\lambda p+c)+2\lambda(1-p)(1-q)]\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\sqrt{2\lambda}[(1-p)(2\lambda q+d)+(1-q)(2\lambda p+c)]\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r,

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\wyr_{1}

\displaystyle=-(2\lambda q+d)\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-x)-\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-q)\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}(r-x),

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\wyr_{2}

\displaystyle=-(2\lambda p+c)\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}x-\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-p)\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}x,\quad x\in[0,r].

As an immediate result,

\displaystyle-\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\frac{\wyr}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}

\displaystyle=2cdr+2(1-q)c+2(1-p)d,

\displaystyle-\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\frac{\wyr_{1}}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}

\displaystyle=2d(r-x)+2(1-q),

\displaystyle-\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\frac{\wyr_{2}}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}

\displaystyle=2cx+2(1-p).

(10.1)

Moreover, if all occurrences of
\lambda,c
and
d
in these determinants are replaced by
\varepsilon\lambda,\varepsilon c
and
\varepsilon d
, respectively,

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\wyr}{\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{2\lambda}(2\lambda M+c(1-q)+d(1-p)),

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\wyr_{1}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-q),

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\wyr_{2}}{\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=-2\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-p),

(10.2)

where
M
is defined in (
9.2
).

Similarly,

{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}(a_{2,1}+a_{2,2})=(2\lambda+d)\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}r+2\sqrt{2\lambda}(1-q)\sinh\sqrt{2\lambda}r.

It follows that, if
d=0
,

\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\frac{a_{2,1}+a_{2,2}}{4\lambda}=q+(1-q)r.

(10.3)

Moreover, if
\lambda
and
q
are replaced by
\varepsilon\lambda
and
\varepsilon q
, respectively, (but
d
needs not be zero)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0+}\frac{a_{2,1}+a_{2,2}}{\varepsilon}=2\lambda q+d+2\lambda(1-q)r.

(10.4)

10.4.
Proof of (
6.28
)

We have

(\ell_{\lambda}^{j})^{\prime}_{k}(0)=\frac{2d\sqrt{2\lambda}}{a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}}\delta_{j,k}-\frac{4d\lambda(a_{2,1}+a_{2,2})}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})^{2}}\alpha_{j},\quad j,k\in{\mathpzc K}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\mathpzc K}}\alpha(\ell_{\lambda}^{j})^{\prime}_{k}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{2d\sqrt{2\lambda}}{a_{2,1}-a_{2,2}}\alpha_{j}-(1-\beta)\frac{4d\lambda(a_{2,1}+a_{2,2})}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})^{2}}\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{4d\lambda\beta\sqrt{2\lambda}}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})}.

Since, on the other hand,

\beta f^{\prime\prime}(0)=2\lambda\beta f(0)=\frac{4d\lambda\beta\sqrt{2\lambda}}{m_{\lambda}(a_{2,1}-a_{2,2})},

we are done.
