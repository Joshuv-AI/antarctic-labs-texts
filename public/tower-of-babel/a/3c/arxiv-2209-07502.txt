Abstract
. We study a critical problem for an operator of mixed order obtained by the superposition of a Laplacian with a fractional Laplacian. In particular, we investigate the corresponding Sobolev inequality, detecting the optimal constant, which we show that is never achieved. Moreover, we present an existence (and nonexistence) theory for the corresponding subcritical perturbation problem.

Introduction

In this paper we are concerned with
elliptic operators of mixed local and nonlocal type,
in relation to the possible existence of positive solutions for critical problems
and in connection with possible optimizers of suitable Sobolev inequalities.

The investigation of operators of mixed order is a very topical subject of investigation,
arising naturally in several fields, for instance as the superposition of different types of stochastic processes
such as a classical random walk and a Lévy flight, which has also interesting application
in the study of optimal animal foraging strategies, see

of nonlocal difficulties with the lack of invariance under scaling. The contemporary investigation
has specifically focused on several problems in the existence and regularity theory

The stirring motivation
for the problems presented in this paper comes from the study of nonlinear problems with critical exponents,
as the ones suggested by the optimizers of the Sobolev inequality.
Roughly speaking (see Section
2
for a formal definition of the
functional setting) the strategy adopted here is to consider a fractional exponent
s\in(0,1)
,
an open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
not necessarily bounded or connected, and all functions
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which vanish outside
\Omega
,
accounting for a mixed type Sobolev inequality
of the sort

(1.1)

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\,\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leqslant\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.

Here above, the constant
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)
is taken to be the largest
possible one for which
such an inequality holds true and, as usual,
n\geqslant 3
and
2^{*}:=\frac{2n}{n-2}
.

We observe below that indeed (
1.1
) is satisfied by choosing the constant on the left hand side
to be (less than or) equal to the classical Sobolev constant

(1.2)

\mathcal{S}_{n}:=\frac{1}{n(n-2)\pi}\,\bigg(\frac{\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(n/2)}\bigg)^{2/n},

since by the standard Sobolev inequality (in
\Omega
, or even in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
),

\mathcal{S}_{n}\,\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leqslant\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leqslant\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy.

As a result, the largest possible constant in (
1.1
) certainly satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\geqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}
. In principle, one may suspect that in fact a strict inequality occurs
(because, for instance,
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is independent on
\Omega
, as well as on
s
), but this is not the case,
according to the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary open set. Then, we have

(1.3)

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=\mathcal{S}_{n}.

In our setting, Theorem
1.1
plays a pivotal role, since
it allows classical techniques to be efficiently adapted to the setting of operators with mixed order.

As customary, the study of possible optimizers for inequalities of Sobolev type is intimately connected with
the possible existence of nontrivial solutions for critical problems like

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=u^{2^{*}-1}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\gneqq 0&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

\mathcal{L}u=-\Delta u+(-\Delta)^{s}u.

As for the classical case (i.e.

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=u^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda u^{p}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\

Our first result is that there do not exist

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\lambda\leqslant 0

Assume that
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

Then, there do not exist solutions to problem (

To further analyze the existence theory for problem (
1.4

Due to the lack of compactness caused by the critical exponent, an idea borrowed from

\lambda

S(\lambda)<\mathcal{S}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is as in
(

Now, the
strict inequality

S(\lambda)<\mathcal{S}_{n}
is obtained in
[
15

via the following approach: first of all, taking into account that the
minimizers in the Sobolev inequality are given by Aubin-Talenti functions, one considers
the function

u_{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{\phi}{(\varepsilon^{2}+|x|^{2})^{(n-2)/2}}\quad(\varepsilon>0).

Then, using
u_{\varepsilon}
as a

, one gets (at least for
n\geqslant 5

\frac{\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-\lambda\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}=\mathcal{S}_{n}-c\lambda\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2})\quad\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$},

where
c>0
is a suitable constant. From this, choosing
\varepsilon
sufficiently small,
one immediately conclude that
S(\lambda)<\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
A similar approach works for
1<p<2^{*}-1
as well, and it
has also been used in the nonlocal framework
[
32
]
.

Differently from what one can expect, in our mixed setting the situation changes. The main reason is that, in
trying to repeat the above argument, one is led to consider the following
minimization problem

\begin{split}S(\lambda)&:=\inf\left\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[u]^{2}_{s}-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}:\,\text{$u\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)$ and $\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}=1$}\right\}\end{split}

and to prove that

(1.5)

S(\lambda)<\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega),

where

[u]_{s}
denotes the usual Gagliardo seminorm of
u
(here, we agree to identify
u
with its
zero-extension out of
\Omega
) and

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)
is as in (
1.1
).
We explicitly stress that the constant
S(\lambda)

(which is different from the one defined above) actually depends
on
n,s
and on
\Omega
; however, since these quantities are fixed,
to simplify the notation we avoid to keep track of these dependencies in the sequel.

Now, since we know from Theorem
1.1
that
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=\mathcal{S}_{n}
,
in order to prove (
1.5
)
it is natural to consider as a competitor function

the same function

u_{\varepsilon}
defined above; however, the presence
of the nonlocal part
[u_{\varepsilon}]^{2}_{s}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+[u_{\varepsilon}]^{2}_{s}-\lambda\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}}{\|u_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\mathcal{S}_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{2-2s})-c\lambda\varepsilon^{2}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2})\quad\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$}

(see, precisely, identity (
4.28
) in the proof of Lemma
4.10

and the term
O(\varepsilon^{2-2s})

is not negligible
when
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

All that being said, in the linear case
p=1

we obtain that problem (
1.4
) does not admit any solution both in the range of “small” and “large” values of
\lambda
, but it does possess solutions for an “intermediate” regime of
values of
\lambda
; more precisely, denoting by

\lambda_{1,s}
the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
in a bounded open set
\Omega

and by
\lambda_{1}
the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
in
\Omega
(a precise summary of the related spectral
property being recalled on page

have the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open and bounded set and
p=1
.
There exists some
\lambda^{*}\in[\lambda_{1,s},\lambda_{1})

such that problem (
1.4
) possesses at least one
solution if

\lambda^{*}<\lambda<\lambda_{1}.

Moreover, the following facts hold:

(1)

there do not exist solutions to problem (

\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{1}
;

(2)

for every

there do no exist solutions to problem (
1.4

to the closed ball
\mathcal{B}\subseteq L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathcal{B}:=\{u\in L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\|u\|\leqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\,(n-2)/4}\}.

2.
The functional setting

In this section we collect the notation and some
preliminary results which will be used in the rest of the paper.
More precisely, we introduce the adequate function spaces
to study problem (
1.4
), and we investigate
the existence of extremals for some
mixed Sobolev-type inequalities
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
.
If
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, we set

(2.1)

[u]_{s}:=\bigg(\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\bigg)^{1/2},

and we refer to
[u]_{s}
as the
Gagliardo seminorm
of
u
(of order
s
).

Let
\varnothing\neq\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with
n\geqslant 3
) be an
arbitrary
open set,
not necessarily bounded. We define the function space

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
as the completion
of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to the

global norm

\rho(u):=\big(\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u]^{2}_{s}\big)^{1/2},\qquad u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Remark 2.1
.

A couple of observations concerning the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
are in order.

(1)

The norm
\rho(\cdot)
is induced by the scalar product

\langle u,v\rangle_{\rho}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy,

where
\cdot
denotes the usual scalar product in the Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

is a
Hilbert space
.

(2)

Even if the function
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

identically vanishes outside
\Omega
, it is often still convenient to consider
in the definition of
\rho(\cdot)
the
L^{2}
-norm of
\nabla u

on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, rather than restricted to
\Omega
(though of course the result would be the same): this is to stress that the elements in

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

are functions defined
on the entire space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and not only on
\Omega
(and this is consistent with the nonlocal
nature of the operator
\mathcal{L}
). The benefit of having this global functional setting
is that these functions can be
globally approximated on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(with respect to the norm
\rho(\cdot)
)
by smooth functions with compact support in
\Omega
.

In particular, when
\Omega\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we will
see that this
global
definition of

\rho(\cdot)
implies that the functions in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
naturally
satisfy the nonlocal Dirichlet condition
prescribed in problem (
1.4
), that is,

(2.2)

\text{$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$ for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

In order to better understand the
nature
of the space

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, we distinguish two cases.

(i)  If
\Omega
is bounded
. In this case we first recall the following
inequality, which
expresses the
continuous embedding

of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
into
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see, e.g.,
[
19
, Proposition 2.2]
):
there exists a constant
\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{c}(s)>0
such that,
for every
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, one has

(2.3)

[u]^{2}_{s}\leqslant\mathbf{c}(s)\|u\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\mathbf{c}(s)\big(\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big).

This, together with the classical
Poincaré inequality
, implies that

\rho(\cdot)
and the full
H^{1}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

are
actually equivalent
on the space
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
, and hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\overline{C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)}^{\,\,\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}

\displaystyle=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)$ and
$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\}.

(ii)  If
\Omega
is unbounded
. In this case, even if the
embedding inequality

(
2.3
) is still satisfied, the Poincaré inequality

does not hold
; hence, the norm
\rho(\cdot)
is no more equivalent to the full
H^{1}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

is not a subspace of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

On the other had, by the classical
Sobolev inequality
we infer
the existence of a constant
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{n}>0
,

independent of the open set
\Omega
, such that

(2.4)

\mathcal{S}_{n}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\rho(u)^{2}\qquad\text{for every $u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$}.

From this, we deduce that every Cauchy sequence in
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
(with respect
to the norm
\rho(\cdot)
) is also a Cauchy sequence
in the space
L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;
as a consequence, since the functions in
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
identically vanish out of
\Omega
,
we obtain

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)=\{u\in L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$,
$\nabla u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$ and $[u]_{s}<\infty$}\}.

In particular, when
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{u\in L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$\nabla u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})$
and $[u]_{s}<\infty$}\}.

Remark 2.2
.

We stress that the
mixed
Sobolev-type inequality
(
2.4
) holds
for every open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}

(bounded or not); thus, we always have

(2.5)

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2^{*}}(\Omega).

Furthermore, by exploiting
the density of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
we can extend
inequality
(
2.4
) to
every function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, thereby obtaining

\mathcal{S}_{n}\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leqslant\rho(u)^{2}=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}+[u]^{2}_{s}\quad\text{for every $u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

3.
The mixed Sobolev-type inequality

Now we further develop our analysis on the
mixed Sobolev inequality

(
2.4
),
that is,

\mathcal{S}_{n}\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}\leqslant\rho(u)^{2}\qquad\text{for every $u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$},

with the aim of proving Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

To this end, our first goal is to find the
sharp constant
in
(
2.4
), namely,

(3.1)

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega):=\inf\big\{\rho(u)^{2}:\,u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}\big\},

where
\mathcal{M}
is the
unit sphere
in
L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, that is,

\mathcal{M}:=\{u\in L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1\}.

We stress that, since
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
is
dense

in the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
(with respect to the norm
\rho(\cdot)
),
by the continuous embedding (
2.5
) we have

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=\inf\big\{\rho(u)^{2}:\,\text{$u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}$}\big\}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)
is
translation-invariant
with respect to
\Omega
, that is,

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(x_{0}+\Omega)=\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\qquad\text{for every $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}.

Remark 3.1
.

Before starting with the study of the minimization problem (
3.1
),
we review here below some well-known properties
of the
best constant

\mathcal{S}_{n}
in the classical Sobolev
inequality, which is defined as follows:

\mathcal{S}_{n}:=\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}:\,u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}\big\}.

For a proof of these facts we refer to

[
3
,
34
]

(see also
[
15
, Section 1]
).

(1)

The constant
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is
independent of the open set
\Omega
,
and depends only on the dimension
n
,
as stated in (
1.2
).

(2)

Given any open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
(bounded or not), we have

\mathcal{S}_{n}=\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:\,\text{$u\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}$}\big\},

where
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
is the space
defined as the completion of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to the gradient norm

u\mapsto\mathcal{G}(u):=\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.

(3)

If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
bounded open set
, then
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is

never achieved
.

(4)

If
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{n}

is not achieved in
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

but

it is achieved in the bigger space
\mathcal{D}^{1,2}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

by the family of functions

\mathcal{F}=\big\{U_{t,x_{0}}(x)=t^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\mathcal{U}((x-x_{0})/t):\,\text{$t>0,\,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}\big\},

where

\mathcal{U}(z):=c\,(1+|z|^{2})^{\frac{2-n}{2}}

and
c>0
is such that

\|\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
.

We are thus ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Since
\rho(u)\geqslant\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
for every
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)
,
we have

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\geqslant\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}:\,u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}\big\}=\mathcal{S}_{n}.

To prove the reverse inequality, by the
translation-invariance
of
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)

we assume that
x_{0}=0\in\Omega
, and we let
r>0
be such that
B_{r}(0)\subseteq\Omega
.
We now observe that, given any
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\mathcal{M}
,
there exists
k_{0}=k_{0}(u)\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\mathrm{supp}(u)\subseteq B_{kr}(0)\quad\text{for every $k\geqslant k_{0}$};

as a consequence, setting
u_{k}:=k^{\frac{n-2}{2}}u(kx)
(for
k\geqslant k_{0}
), we readily see that

\mathrm{supp}(u_{k})\subseteq B_{r}(0)\subseteq\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1.

Taking into account the definition of
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)
, we find that, for every
k\geqslant k_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)

\displaystyle\leqslant\rho(u_{k})^{2}=\|\nabla u_{k}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u_{k}]^{2}_{s}=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+k^{2s-2}[u]^{2}_{s}.

From this, letting
k\to\infty
(and recalling that
s<1
), we obtain

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\leqslant\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

By the arbitrariness of
u\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}
and the fact that

\mathcal{S}_{n}

is independent of the open set
\Omega

(see Remark
3.1
-(1)), we finally infer that

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\leqslant\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:\,C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}\big\}=\mathcal{S}_{n},

and hence
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=\mathcal{S}_{n}
.
∎

We are now in the position of proving Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Arguing by contradiction, let us suppose that there exists a nonzero
function
u_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

such that

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
and

\rho(u_{0})^{2}=\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u_{0}]^{2}_{s}=\mathcal{S}_{n}.

Taking into account that
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)

(this inclusion being a straightforward of the fact that
\rho(\cdot)\geqslant\mathcal{G}(\cdot)
,
see Remark
3.1
-(2)),
we infer that

\mathcal{S}_{n}\leqslant\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+[u_{0}]^{2}_{s}=\rho(u_{0})^{2}=\mathcal{S}_{n},

from which we derive that
[u_{0}]_{s}=0
.
As a consequence, the function

u_{0}
must be
constant in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, but this is contradiction with the fact that

\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Even if Theorem
1.2
shows that
the constant
\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=\mathcal{S}_{n}
is

never achieved
in the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)

(independently of the open set
\Omega
), in the particular case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}

we can prove that
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is achieved ‘in the limit’:
more precisely, if
\mathcal{F}=\{U_{t,x_{0}}\}
is as in Remark
3.1
-(4), we have

\rho(U_{t,x_{0}})^{2}\to\mathcal{S}_{n}\quad\text{as $t\to\infty$}.

Indeed,

|\mathcal{U}(z)|\leqslant C\,\min\{1,|z|^{2-n}\}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad|\nabla\mathcal{U}(z)|\leqslant C\,\min\{|z|,\,|z|^{1-n}\},

for some
C>0
, therefore (up to renaming
C
line after line)

\displaystyle[\mathcal{U}(z)]_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|\mathcal{U}(x+y)-\mathcal{U}(x)|^{2}}{|y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{1}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\mathcal{U}(x+ty)\cdot y\,dt\right|^{2}\frac{dx\,dy}{|y|^{n+2s}}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}\big(|\mathcal{U}(x+y)|^{2}+|\mathcal{U}(x)|^{2}\big)\frac{dx\,dy}{|y|^{n+2s}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{1}\times(0,1)}\min\{|x+ty|^{2},\,|x+ty|^{2(1-n)}\}\,\frac{dx\,dy\,dt}{|y|^{n+2s-2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+4\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}|\mathcal{U}(z)|^{2}\frac{dz\,dy}{|y|^{n+2s}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\iiint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times B_{1}\times(0,1)}\min\{|z|^{2},\,|z|^{2(1-n)}\}\,\frac{dz\,dy\,dt}{|y|^{n+2s-2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+C\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})}\min\{1,|z|^{2(2-n)}\}\frac{dz\,dy}{|y|^{n+2s}},

which is finite.

Hence,
\mathcal{U}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and consequently
U_{t,x_{0}}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for every
t>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Moreover, recalling that

U_{t,x_{0}}(x)=t^{\frac{2-n}{2}}\mathcal{U}\Big(\frac{x-x_{0}}{t}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad\|U_{t,x_{0}}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1,

by arguing as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
we have

\displaystyle\rho(U_{t,x_{0}})^{2}=\rho(U_{t,0})^{2}=\|\nabla\mathcal{U}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+t^{2s-2}[\mathcal{U}]^{2}_{s}.

From this, since
\mathcal{U}=U_{1,0}
is an optimal function in the classical
Sobolev inequality (and since
s<1
), by letting
t\to\infty
we obtain

\rho(U_{t,x_{0}})^{2}\to\|\nabla\mathcal{U}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}=\mathcal{S}_{n}.

4.
Critical problems driven by
\mathcal{L}

We now develop our study concerning the solvability of
problem (
1.4
).
Throughout this section, we tacitly understand that

(i)

\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
bounded open set
;

(ii)

\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
;

(iii)

1\leqslant p<2^{*}-1
.

Moreover, we inherit all the
definitions and notation of
Sections
2
and
3
.
We also introduce the convex cone

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega):=\{u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega):\,\text{$u\geqslant 0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}\}.

We begin by giving the precise definition of
solution
to (
1.4
).

Definition 4.1
.

We say that a function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega)
is a

solution
to problem (
1.4
) if it
satisfies the following properties:

(1)

|\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>0\}|>0
;

(2)

for every test function
v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
we have

(4.1)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}(u^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda u^{p})v\,dx.\end{split}

We observe that, since
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is bounded,

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)=\{u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\,\text{$u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)$ and
$u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}\}.

As a consequence, the assumption
u\in\mathcal{X}_{+}^{1,2}(\Omega)

contains

both the Dirichlet boundary condition
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
and
the sign condition
u\geqslant 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Moreover, all the integrals in (
4.1
) are finite, due to the embedding
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the fact that
p<2^{*}-1
.

Before proceeding we highlight that, if
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega)

is a given function, the validity of identity (
4.1
)
(which expresses the fact that
u
is a solution to problem
(
1.4
)) is
actually equivalent
to the following condition

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}(u)\equiv 0\quad\text{on $\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$},

where
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}
is the
C^{1}
-functional defined on the Hilbert space

\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
by

(4.2)

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\rho(u)^{2}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2^{*}}\,dx-\frac{\lambda}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{p+1}\,dx.

Thus, we derive that the solutions to problem (
1.4
) are precisely
the
nonnegative critical points
of the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}
.
This characterization will be used, as a crucial tool, in order
to study the existence of solutions to problem (
1.4
).

With Definition
4.1
at hand,
we establish the following boundedness and positivity result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that there exists
a solution
u_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega)
to problem
(
1.4
)
for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
p\in[1,2^{*}-1)
.
Then, the following facts hold:

(1)

u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;

(2)

if, in addition,
\lambda\geqslant 0
, then
u_{0}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

The boundedness of
u_{0}
is a direct consequence of
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]
,
applied here with the choice
f(x,t):=t^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda t^{p}
. Indeed,
u_{0}
is a solution to

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=f(x,u)&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\gneqq 0&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

and the function
f
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

f:\Omega\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function;

(ii)

f(\cdot,t)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
for every
t\geqslant 0
;

(iii)

|f(x,t)|\leqslant(1+|\lambda|)(1+t^{2^{*}-1})
for every
t\geqslant 0
.

In view of these properties of
f
, we are then entitled to invoke
[
11
, Theorem 4.1]

(see also
[
11
, Remark 4.2]
), ensuring that
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. This,
jointly with the fact that

u_{0}\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
,
implies
that
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, as desired.

Assume now that
\lambda\geqslant 0
. Recalling that
u_{0}
is a solution
to (
1.4
) (in the sense of Definition
4.1
),
and since
u_{0}\geqslant 0
a.e. in
\Omega
, for every
v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega)
we have

(4.3)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\nabla u_{0}\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u_{0}(x)-u_{0}(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}(u_{0}^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda u_{0}^{p})v\,dx\geqslant 0.\end{split}

As a consequence, we can apply the Strong Maximum Principle
in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
(this time with the choice
f(x,t)\equiv 0
), ensuring that

\text{either $u_{0}>0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$\quad or\quad$u_{0}\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}.

On the other hand, since
|\{u_{0}>0\}|>0
,
we have that
u_{0}
is not identically vanishing in
\Omega
; thus,

u_{0}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
,
and the proof is complete.
∎

Thanks to Theorem
4.2
, we can now prove
Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Arguing by contradiction, let us suppose that there exists a nonzero
solution
u_{0}
to problem (
1.4
). Owing to Theorem
4.2
,
we have

u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
;
moreover, defining
f(x,t):=|t|^{2^{*}-2}t+\lambda|t|^{p-1}t
(with
t\in\mathbb{R}
),
we see that
u_{0}
solves

(4.4)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=f(x,u)&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

and the nonlinearity
f
satisfies the following properties:

(i)

f\in C^{0,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R})
;

(ii)

for every
x\in\Omega
and every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{n-2}{2}\,tf(x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-2}{2}\big(|t|^{2^{*}}+\lambda|t|^{p+1}\big)=n\bigg(\frac{|t|^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}+\frac{\lambda|t|^{p+1}}{2^{*}}\bigg)

\displaystyle(\text{since $\lambda\leqslant 0$ and $p\leqslant 2^{*}-1$})

\displaystyle\geqslant n\bigg(\frac{|t|^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}+\frac{\lambda|t|^{p+1}}{p+1}\bigg)=n\int_{0}^{t}f(x,\tau)\,d\tau.

As a consequence, since
u_{0}
is a
bounded solution
of
(
4.4
),
f
satisfies (i)-(ii)
and
\Omega
is star-shaped,
we are entitled to apply
[
29
, Theorem 1.3]
, ensuring that

\text{$u_{0}\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$}.

This is clearly a contradiction with the fact that
u_{0}\gneqq 0
in
\Omega

(recall that
u_{0}
is a solution to (
1.4
), see Definition
4.1
),
and the proof is complete.
∎

From now on we assume that

(4.5)

\lambda>0.

Under this assumption
(which is somewhat in agreement with Theorem
1.3
)
and taking into account all the discussion
carried out so far, we can start our study of the
solvability
of
problem (
1.4
). To this end, since the
linear case

p=1

and the
superlinear case

p>1
present some subtle differences,
it is worth treating these cases separately.

4.1.
The linear case
p=1

We begin by studying the solvability of (
1.4
) in the

linear case
p=1
, that is, we begin by considering the problem

(4.6)

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=u^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda u&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\gneqq 0&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$},\end{cases}

In this
linear
context, the existence of solutions
to problem (
4.6
) for a given
\lambda>0

is deeply influenced
by the
first Dirichlet eigenvalues
of
(-\Delta)^{s}
and of
\mathcal{L}
.

We recall that, given the bounded open set
\Omega
, one defines

(i)

the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
(-\Delta)^{s}
in
\Omega
as

(4.7)

\lambda_{1,s}:=\inf\big\{[u]^{2}_{s}:\,\text{$u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$ and $\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1$}\big\};

(ii)

the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
\mathcal{L}
in
\Omega
as

(4.8)

\lambda_{1}:=\inf\big\{\rho(u)^{2}:\,\text{$u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$ and $\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1$}\big\}.

Clearly, both
\lambda_{1,s}
and
\lambda_{1}
depend on the open set
\Omega
;
however, in order to simplify the notation, we avoid to make explicit this dependence
in what follows.

For the sake
of completeness (and for a future reference),
we then present in the next remark a short
overview of the main properties
of
\lambda_{1,s}
and
\lambda_{1}
.

Remark 4.3

(Properties of
\lambda_{1,s}
and
\lambda_{1}
)
.

As regards
\lambda_{1,s}
we first
observe that, since the map

u\mapsto[u]_{s}=:\mathcal{N}(u)
is
a norm on
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)

which is equivalent to the full
H^{s}
-norm
(see, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 6.5]
and recall that
\Omega
is bounded), one has

\lambda_{1,s}=\inf\big\{[u]^{2}_{s}:\,\text{$u\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega)$ and $\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1$}\big\},

where
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega)\subseteq L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the completion of

C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with respect to
\mathcal{N}
.
Moreover, since the embedding
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is compact,
it is not difficult to see that
\lambda_{1,s}

is
actually achieved
in this larger space
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega)
, that is,

\exists\,\,\phi_{0}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{s,2}(\Omega):\,\text{$\|\phi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1$
and $[\phi_{0}]^{2}=\lambda_{1,s}>0$}.

As a matter of fact, the above function
\phi_{0}
can be chosen to be
strictly positive
a.e. in
\Omega
(see, e.g.,
[
31
, Proposition 9]
);
in addition, since
\phi_{0}
is a constrained minimizer of the functional
u\mapsto[u]_{s}^{2}
,
by the Lagrange Multiplier Rule we have that
\phi_{0}
is weak solution to the
eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{s}u=\lambda_{1,s}u&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\not\equiv 0&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}.\end{cases}

As regards
\lambda_{1}
, we have completely analogous
results (with the obvious modifications): first of all, since
\rho(\cdot)

defines
a norm on
C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)

which is equivalent to the full
H^{1}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(as
\Omega
is bounded), we have

\lambda_{1}=\inf\big\{\rho(u)^{2}:\,\text{$u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$ and $\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1$}\big\}.

Moreover, since the embedding
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is compact, it is not difficult to see that
\lambda_{1}(\Omega)

is
actually achieved
in this larger space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, that is,

\exists\,\,\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega):\,\text{$\|\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=1$
and $\rho(\psi_{0})^{2}=\lambda_{1}>0$}.

As a matter of fact, the above function
\psi_{0}
can be chosen to be
strictly positive
a.e. in
\Omega

(see, e.g.,
[
11
, Proposition 5.1]
); in addition,
since
\psi_{0}
is a constrained minizer of the functional

\rho(\cdot)^{2}
, by the Lagrange Multiplier Rule we easily see that

\psi_{0}
is weak solution to the
eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=\lambda_{1}u&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\not\equiv 0&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}.\end{cases}

We are now
approaching the proof of Theorem
1.4
. We will achieve this
through several independent results.
To begin with, we prove a lemma linking the existence of solutions
to (
4.6
) with the existence of constrained minimizers
for a suitable functional.

As a matter of facts, since solutions to
problem (
1.4
)
are precisely the
unconstrained
nonnegative critical points
of the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}
defined in (
4.2
) (for all
p\geqslant 1
),
we now aim at proving that, in this
linear context
, there is a link
between
existence of solutions

to (
4.6
) and
existence of minimizers

for the functional

(4.9)

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u):=\rho(u)^{2}-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\qquad(u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)),

constrained to the manifold
\mathcal{V}(\Omega):=\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

For every fixed
\lambda>0
, we define

(4.10)

\mathcal{S}(\lambda):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u).

We assume that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)>0

and that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)

is achieved
, that is, there exists
some function
w\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)

such that

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(w)=\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
.

Then, there exists a solution to (
4.6
).

Before giving the proof of Lemma
4.4
, we list in the
next remark some properties of the number
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
which will
be used in the sequel.

Remark 4.5

(Properties of
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
)
.

We begin by observing that,
by Hölder’s inequality, for every
u\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)

we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u)

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf_{u\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)}\rho^{2}(u)-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\geqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle(\text{recall that $u\equiv 0$ a.e.\,in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}-\lambda|\Omega|^{4/n}.

As a consequence, we obtain

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\geqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}-\lambda|\Omega|^{4/n}>-\infty\quad\text{for every $\lambda>0$}.

In addition, from the definition of
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
we easily infer that

(i)

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\leqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}
for every
\lambda>0
;

(ii)

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\leqslant\mathcal{S}(\mu)
for every
0<\mu<\lambda
.

Finally, owing to the definition of
\lambda_{1}
in (
4.8
)
(and recalling that

\lambda_{1}
is achieved
in the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
,
see Remark
4.3
), it is easy to see that

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\geqslant 0\,\,\Longleftrightarrow\,\,0<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1}.

We are now ready to provide the proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
be a constrained minimizer for
the functional
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}

(whose existence is guaranteed by the assumptions), that is,

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(w)=\mathcal{S}(\lambda)>0.

Since
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(|w|)\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(w)
,
by possibly replacing
w
with
|w|
we may and do assume that
w\gneqq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
;
moreover,
by the Lagrange Multiplier Rule we have

\mathrm{d}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(w)\equiv\mu\,\mathrm{d}\big(u\mapsto\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)(w)\quad\text{on $\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$},

for some
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. This means that

(4.11)

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\nabla w\cdot\nabla\phi+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(w(x)-w(y))(\phi(x)-\phi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\\
&\qquad\quad=\int_{\Omega}(\mu w^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda w)\phi\,dx\qquad\text{for every $\phi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.\end{split}

Now, if we choose
\phi=w
in this last identity, we get

\displaystyle\mu=\mu\|w\|^{2^{*}}_{L^{2^{*}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\rho(w)^{2}-\lambda\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(w)=\mathcal{S}(\lambda)>0.

As a consequence,
setting
u:=\mathcal{S}(\lambda)^{(n-2)/4}w
, we see that

(i)
u\in\mathcal{X}_{+}^{1,2}(\Omega)\setminus\{0\}
,
since
w\gneqq 0
in
\Omega
and
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)>0
;

(ii)  for every
\phi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, from (
4.11
) we get

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\phi+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(u(x)-u(y))(\phi(x)-\phi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}=\int_{\Omega}(u^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda u)\phi\,dx.

Gathering (i)-(ii), we then conclude that
u
is a solution to (
4.6
), as desired.
∎

On account of Lemma
4.4
, an important information
to study the solvability of problem (
4.6
)
is the
sign
of the
real number

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
.
In this perspective, we prove the following result:

Lemma 4.6
.

For every
0<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1,s}
, we have

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)=\mathcal{S}_{n}>0.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{1,s})
be given. By the definition of
\lambda_{1,s}
in (
4.7
),
for every
u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u)

\displaystyle=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\big([u]_{s}^{2}-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+(\lambda_{1,s}-\lambda)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\geqslant\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\inf\big\{\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u):\,u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}\big\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\inf\big\{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:\,u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{M}\big\}=\mathcal{S}_{n},

from which we deduce that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)=\mathcal{S}_{n}
, as desired.
∎

By combining Remark
4.5
with Lemma
4.6
, we
conclude that

(i)

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)=\mathcal{S}_{n}
for every
0<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1,s}
;

(ii)

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\geqslant 0
for every
0<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1}
;

(iii)

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)<0
for every
\lambda>\lambda_{1}
.

We now turn to prove that
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is

continuous function of
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
; this, together with the above (i)-to-(iii),
ensures that

\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
assumes
all the values

between
0
and the constant
\mathcal{S}_{n}
when
\lambda
ranges in the interval
(0,\lambda_{1}]
.

Lemma 4.7
.

The function
\lambda\mapsto\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
is continuous on
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

By Remark
4.5
,
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)

is
nonincreasing
on the interval
(0,\infty)
.

As a consequence,
for every
\lambda>0
we have

\exists\,\,\,\mathcal{S}(\lambda-):=\lim_{\mu\to\lambda^{-}}\mathcal{S}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}\quad\text{and}\quad\exists\,\,\,\mathcal{S}(\lambda+):=\lim_{\mu\to\lambda^{+}}\mathcal{S}(\mu)\in\mathbb{R}.

To prove the continuity of
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
on
(0,\infty)
, we
then pick any
\lambda_{0}>0
and we show that

\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is
continuous
both from the left and from the right
at
\lambda_{0}
,
that is,

\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0}-)=\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0}+)=\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0}).

As regards the continuity from the left, we proceed as follows: first of all,
given any
\varepsilon>0
,
by definition of
\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})
there exists

u=u_{\varepsilon,\lambda_{0}}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{0}}(u)<\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

From this, using the monotonicity of
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
and exploiting
Hölder’s inequality
(recall that
u\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
and
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
),
for every
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\mathcal{S}(\lambda)-\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u)-\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{0}}(u)-\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})\big)+(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(\lambda_{0}-\lambda)|\Omega|^{4/n}

As a consequence, setting
\delta_{\varepsilon}:=\varepsilon/(2|\Omega|^{4/n})
, we conclude that

0\leqslant\mathcal{S}(\lambda)-\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for every $\lambda_{0}-\delta_{\varepsilon}<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{0}$},

and this proves that
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is continuous from the left at
\lambda_{0}
.

As regards the continuity from the right, we proceed essentially as above:
first of all, given any
\varepsilon>0
and any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, we can find

u=u_{\varepsilon,\lambda}\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u)<\mathcal{S}(\lambda)+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

From this, by the monotonicity of
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
and
Hölder’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{0})-\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda_{0}}(u)-\mathcal{S}(\lambda)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\big(\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u)-\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\big)+(\lambda-\lambda_{0})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\leqslant\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+(\lambda-\lambda_{0})|\Omega|^{4/n}<\varepsilon,

provided that
\lambda_{0}\leqslant\lambda<\lambda_{0}+\delta_{\varepsilon}
(where
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
is as above).
We then conclude that
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)

is also continuous from the right at
\lambda_{0}
,
and the proof is complete.
∎

In the light of the results established so far, we can finally provide the

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

To begin with, we define

\lambda^{*}:=\sup\big\{\lambda>0:\,\mathcal{S}(\mu)=\mathcal{S}_{n}\,\,\text{for all $0<\mu\leqslant\lambda$}\big\}.

On account of Lemma
4.6
, we see that
\lambda_{1,s}\leqslant\lambda^{*}<\infty
;
moreover, since we know from Lemma
4.7
that
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)

is continuous, we have

\mathcal{S}(\lambda^{*})=\mathcal{S}_{n}.

We also notice that,
since
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)\geqslant 0
on
(0,\lambda_{1}]
and
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)<0
on
(\lambda_{1},\infty)
,
again by the continuity of
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
we infer that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{1})=0
;
as a consequence, recalling that
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is nonincreasing on
(0,\infty)
,
we conclude that

\lambda^{*}\in[\lambda_{1,s},\lambda_{1}).

We now turn to prove that the assertion of Theorem
1.4

holds
with this choice of
\lambda^{*}
, that is,
we show that the following assertions hold:

(i)

problem (
4.6
) possesses at least one
solution if

\lambda^{*}<\lambda<\lambda_{1}
;

(ii)

there do not exist solutions to problem (
4.6
)
if
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{1}
;

(iii)

for every fixed
0<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1,s}
,
there do not exist solutions to problem (
4.6
)
belonging to the closed ball

\mathcal{B}=\{u\in L^{2^{*}}(\Omega):\,\|u\|\leqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\,(n-2)/4}\}.

To this end, we treat the following three cases separately.

Case I:
\lambda^{*}<\lambda<\lambda_{1}
. In this case, owing to the definition of
\lambda^{*}
(and recalling that
\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is continuous), we have

0\leqslant\mathcal{S}(\lambda)<\mathcal{S}_{n}
; from this,
by arguing exactly as in the proof of
[
15
, Lem. 1.2]
,
we see that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)

is achieved
, that is,
there exists a nonzero function
w\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(w)=\mathcal{S}(\lambda).

In particular, since
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
(and
w\not\equiv 0
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\bigg[\frac{\rho(w)^{2}}{\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}-\lambda\bigg]\geqslant\|w\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(\lambda_{1}-\lambda)>0.

Now we have proved that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
is strictly positive
and is achieved, we are then entitled to apply Lemma
4.4
,
ensuring that there exists a solution to (
4.6
).

Case II:
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{1}
. In this second case, since

\mathcal{S}(\cdot)
is nonincreasing (and since we know that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda_{1})=0
), we infer that

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\leqslant 0<\mathcal{S}_{n}.

Owing to this fact,
we can argue once again as in
[
15
, Lem. 1.2]
to prove that
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
is achieved;
however, since
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)\leqslant 0
, we
are not entitled
to
apply Lemma
4.6

to derive the existence of a solution to problem (
4.6
).
Rather, we show that
there cannot exist solutions
to
(
4.6
) by
proceeding as in
[
15
, Remark 1.1]
.

Arguing by contradiction, let us assume that there exists (at least)
one solution
u
to problem (
4.6
)
in this case. On account of Remark
4.3
, we know that
exists a nonzero function
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

\psi_{0}>0
a.e. in
\Omega
and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla\psi_{0}\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(\psi_{0}(x)-\psi_{0}(y))(v(x)-v(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}v\,dx\qquad\forall\,\,v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega),

that is,
\psi_{0}
is an
eigefunction
for
\mathcal{L}
associated with
\lambda_{1}
.

In particular, choosing
v=u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}_{+}(\Omega)
in the above identity
and
recalling
that
u
is a solution to problem (
4.6
)
(according to Definition
4.1
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{0}u\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\psi_{0}\cdot\nabla u\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{(\psi_{0}(x)-\psi_{0}(y))(u(x)-u(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(u^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda u)\psi_{0}\,dx

\displaystyle(\text{since $u,\psi_{0}>0$ a.e.\,in $\Omega$, see Theorem \ref{thm:aprioriPropSol}})

\displaystyle>\lambda\int_{\Omega}u\psi_{0}\,dx,

but this is clearly in contradiction with the fact that
\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{1}
.

Case III:
0<\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1,s}
.
Arguing by contradiction, let us suppose that there exists a solution
u
to
problem (
4.6
) such that
u\in\mathcal{B}
.
Then, setting

v:=u/\|u\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)},

we have the following computation, based on the properties of
u
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(u)=\frac{1}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}\big(\rho(u)^{2}-\lambda\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}\bigg\{\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dx\,dy-\lambda\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dx\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}\int_{\Omega}u^{2^{*}}\,dx\qquad(\text{since $u$ solves \eqref{eq:mainPBlinear}, see \eqref{eq:weakSol} with $v=u$})

\displaystyle=\|u\|^{2^{*}-2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}\leqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}\qquad(\text{since $u\in\mathcal{B}$}).

As a consequence, owing to Lemma
4.6
, we obtain

(4.12)

\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(v)=\mathcal{S}_{n},

and this proves that

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)=\mathcal{S}_{n}

is achieved
by
v
.

Now, since
v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, from (
4.12
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\nabla v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(v)-\big([v]^{2}_{s}-\lambda\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\big)

\displaystyle(\text{since $\lambda\leqslant\lambda_{1,s}$ and $v\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{D}^{s,2}_{0}(\Omega)$})

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}(v)=\mathcal{S}_{n},

and this shows that
the best Sobolev constant
\mathcal{S}_{n}
is achieved by
v\in\mathcal{D}_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

On the other hand, since
\Omega
is
bounded
, we know
from Remark
3.1
that
\mathcal{S}_{n}

is never achieved
in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, and so we have a contradiction.

This ends the proof.
∎

4.2.
The superlinear case
1<p<2^{*}-1

Now we continue the study of the solvability of (
1.4
) in the case
1<p<2^{*}-1
,
and we address the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
Compared to the
linear
case previously treated,
the main difference is that we
cannot
link the solvability
of problem (
1.4
) to the existence of constrained

minimizers
for the quadratic form
\mathcal{Q}_{\lambda}

in (
4.9
)
(since the rescaling argument in the proof of Lemma
4.4
cannot be applied when

p>1
); as a consequence, we may try to prove the existence
of solutions to (
1.4
) by showing the existence
of unconstrained and nonnegative
critical points
for the functional
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}
in (
4.2
).

However, this approach presents a drawback:
even if we are able to prove the existence of a nonzero critical point
v
for
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda,p}
,
there is no reason for
u:=|v|
to be a critical point, and so we cannot be sure that
there exist
nonnegative
critical points.
For this reason, and following the approach in

[
15
]
,
we consider a slightly modified functional with respect to (
4.2
), namely,

(4.13)

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u):=\frac{1}{2}\rho(u)^{2}-\frac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{+})^{2^{*}}\,dx-\frac{\lambda}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}(u_{+})^{p+1}\,dx\quad(u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)),

where
u_{+}:=\max\{u,0\}
denotes the positive part of the function
u
.

Now, it is easy to see that any (nonzero) critical point
of
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
is a solution to (
1.4
):
indeed, if
u\not\equiv 0
is a critical point of this functional,
by writing down the associated Euler-Lagrange equation
we see that
u
is a weak solution
of

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}u=(u_{+})^{2^{*}-1}+\lambda(u_{+})^{p-1}&\text{in $\Omega$},\\
u\equiv 0&\text{in $\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega$}.\end{cases}

In particular, since we are assuming
\lambda>0
, we deduce that
\mathcal{L}u\geqslant 0
in
the weak sense in
\Omega
(see, precisely, (
4.3
) in the proof
of Theorem
4.2
).
This, together with the fact that
u\equiv 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
,
allows us to apply the Weak Maximum Principle in
[
8
, Theorem 1.2]
, ensuring that

u\geqslant 0
a.e. in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We then conclude that

u_{+}\equiv u,

and thus
u
is a solution to problem (
1.4
)
(according to Definition
4.1
).

In view of this fact, to prove Theorem
1.5

it then suffices to prove the existence of a nonzero
critical point for

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
.

As in the purely local (see
[
15
]
) or purely nonlocal case (see
[
6
]
),
the main difficulty in the application of the Mountain Pass Theorem consists in proving the validity of a
Palais–Smale condition at level
c\in\mathbb{R}
, usually denoted by
(PS)_{c}
.
In particular, we have to prove that the Mountain Pass
critical level of
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
lies below the threshold of application of the
(PS)_{c}
condition.
In doing this, we will understand better the
strange behaviour in the linear case
and we will notice how
the local part of
\mathcal{L}
affects the problem.

To this end, the first step is to
note that the functional
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
has a nice Mountain Pass geometry,
as described by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

There exist two positive constants
\alpha
,
\beta>0
such that

i)

for any
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
with
\rho(u)=\alpha
, it holds that
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u)\geqslant\beta
;

ii)

there exists a positive function
e\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that

\text{$\rho(e)>\alpha$ and $\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(e)<\beta$}.

Moreover, for every positive function
u\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, it holds that

(4.14)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(tu)=0.

Proof.

The proof is quite standard and exploits the validity of a Sobolev embedding for the space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. See e.g.
[
6
, Proposition 3.1]
.
∎

We now move to show that
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
satisfies a local Palais-Smale condition at a level
c\in\mathbb{R}
related to the best Sobolev constant
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Lemma 4.9
.

The functional
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
satisfies the
(PS)_{c}
for every
c<\dfrac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{n/2}
.

Proof.

Let
\{u_{m}\}
be a
(PS)_{c}
sequence for the functional
\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}
in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
, i.e.

i)

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u_{m})\to c
, as
m\to+\infty
;

ii)

\mathcal{J}^{\prime}_{\lambda,p}(u_{m})\to 0
, as
m\to+\infty
.

By standard arguments, this ensures that
\{u_{m}\}
is bounded in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. In particular, this implies the existence of a function
u_{\infty}\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that, up to subsequences,

(4.15)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\langle

\displaystyle\nabla u_{m},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{m}(x)-u_{m}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle\to\int_{\Omega}\langle\nabla u_{\infty},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx+\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{(u_{\infty}(x)-u_{\infty}(y))(\varphi(x)-\varphi(y))}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy,

for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
Moreover,
u_{m}
converges to
u_{\infty}
(as
m\to+\infty
) weakly in
L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)
, almost everywhere and strongly in
L^{r}(\Omega)
for every
r\in[1,2^{*})
(and hence in norm).
Putting everything together, we get that
u_{\infty}
is a weak solution to (
1.4
) (with
p>1
).

Now, by exploiting
[
13
, Theorem 1]
, we find that

(4.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u_{m})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u_{m}|^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|u_{m}(x)-u_{m}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}((u_{m})_{+})^{2^{*}}\,dx-\dfrac{\lambda}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}((u_{m})_{+})^{p+1}\,dx

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u_{\infty})+\dfrac{1}{2}\rho(u_{m}-u_{\infty})^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}|(u_{m})_{+}-(u_{\infty})_{+}|^{2^{*}}\,dx+o(1).

Arguing as in the proof of Claim 3 of
[
6
, Proposition 3.2]
, we can further prove that

(4.17)

\displaystyle\rho(u_{m}-u_{\infty})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}|(u_{m})_{+}-(u_{\infty})_{+}|^{2^{*}}\,dx+o(1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\Omega}|u_{m}-u_{\infty}|^{2^{*}}\,dx+o(1).

We are now ready to finish the proof of Lemma
4.9
. By (
4.17
), we have that

(4.18)

\dfrac{1}{2}\rho(u_{m}-u_{\infty})^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2^{*}}\int_{\Omega}|(u_{m})_{+}-(u_{\infty})_{+}|^{2^{*}}\,dx=\dfrac{1}{n}\rho(u_{m}-u_{\infty})^{2}+o(1),

and therefore

(4.19)

\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u_{\infty})+\dfrac{1}{n}\rho(u_{m}-u_{\infty})^{2}=\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(u_{m})+o(1)=c_{2}+o(1),

as
m\to+\infty
.
On the other hand, thanks to the boundedness of
\{u_{m}\}
, possibily passing to a subsequence, we have that there exists
L>0
such that

(4.20)

\rho(u_{m}-u_{\infty})^{2}\to L,\quad\textrm{ as }m\to+\infty,

and that there exists
\tilde{L}\geqslant L
such that

(4.21)

\int_{\Omega}|u_{m}-u_{\infty}|^{2^{*}}\,dx\to\tilde{L}.

Recalling that

\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)=\inf\dfrac{\rho(u)^{2}}{\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}=\mathcal{S}_{n},

we find that

(4.22)

L\geqslant\mathcal{S}_{n,s}(\Omega)\tilde{L}^{2/2^{*}}=\mathcal{S}_{n}\tilde{L}^{2/2^{*}},

which implies that either
L=0
or
L\geqslant\mathcal{S}_{n}\tilde{L}^{n/2}
. Arguing as in
[
6
, Proposition 3.2]
we can show that the only possible case is
L=0
, and this shows that the sequence
\{u_{m}\}
strongly converges in
\mathcal{X}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.
∎

The last ingredient needed to establish Theorem
1.5

consists in showing the existence of a path whose energy is below the critical threshold
\mathcal{S}_{n}/n
.
To this aim, we first introduce an auxiliary function reminiscent of the one used in
[
15
]
: let
\phi_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a nonincreasing cut–off function given by

(4.23)

\phi_{0}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1,&{\mbox{ if }}t\in\left[0,\tfrac{1}{4}\right],\\[4.26773pt]
0,&{\mbox{ if }}t\geqslant\frac{1}{2}.\end{array}\right.

Then, we define the function
\phi_{r}(x):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(4.24)

\phi_{r}(x):=\phi_{0}\left(\dfrac{|x|}{r}\right),

for a given
r>0
such that
\overline{B_{r}(0)}\subset\Omega
.
Finally, for all
\varepsilon>0
, let

U_{\varepsilon}(x):=\dfrac{\varepsilon^{(n-2)/2}}{\left(|x|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{(n-2)/2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta_{\varepsilon}(x):=\dfrac{\phi_{r}(x)\,U_{\varepsilon}(x)}{\|\phi_{r}\,U_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2^{*}}(\Omega)}}.

In this framework, we have:

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
and
p\in(1,2^{*}-1)
. Moreover, let

\kappa_{s,n},\,\beta_{p,n}
be as
in (
1.6
).
Then, the following assertions hold.

(i)

If
\kappa_{s,n}>\beta_{p,n}
, then there exists
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(t\eta_{\varepsilon})<\dfrac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{n/2}\quad\text{for all $\lambda>0$}.

(ii)

If, instead,
\kappa_{s,n}\leqslant\beta_{p,n}
, then there exist
\varepsilon>0
and
\lambda_{0}>0
such that

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(t\eta_{\varepsilon})<\dfrac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{n/2}\quad\text{for all $\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{0}$}.

Proof.

We closely follow the argument in
[
6
, Lemma 3.4]
, which in turn is a modification
of the original approach by Brezis and Nirenberg
[
15
]
.

To begin with we observe that, by definition, we have

(4.25)

\begin{split}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(t\eta_{\varepsilon})&=\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\eta_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy\\
&\qquad\qquad-\dfrac{t^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}-\lambda\dfrac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)^{p+1}\,dx;\end{split}

we then turn to estimate the three integrals in the right-hand side of (
4.25
).
As regards the
L^{p+1}
-norm of
\eta_{\varepsilon}
, if
\varepsilon>0
is small enough we have

(4.26)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)^{p+1}\,dx

\displaystyle=C\int_{B_{r}(0)}U_{\varepsilon}(x)^{p+1}\,dx

\displaystyle=C\,\varepsilon^{-(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\dfrac{\tau^{n-1}}{\left(\tfrac{\tau^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+1\right)^{(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}}\,d\tau

\displaystyle=C\,\varepsilon^{n-(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{r/\varepsilon}\dfrac{t^{n-1}}{\left(t^{2}+1\right)^{(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}}\,dt

\displaystyle\geqslant C\,\varepsilon^{n-(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\dfrac{t^{n-1}}{\left(t^{2}+1\right)^{(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}}\,dt

\displaystyle=C\varepsilon^{n-(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}},

where the constant
C>0
is adjusted step by step, but it depends only on
n
.
As regards the
L^{2}
-norm of
\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}
, instead, we know
from
[
15
, Lemma 1.1]
that

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\eta_{\varepsilon}|^{2}\,dx=\mathcal{S}_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2})\qquad\text{as $\varepsilon\to 0^{+}$}.

We are then left to estimate the Gagliardo seminorm of
\eta_{\varepsilon}
.
To begin with, arguing as in
[
32
, Proposition 21]

(and recalling that
B_{r}(0)\subset\Omega
), we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:x\in B_{r/4}(0),y\in B^{c}_{r/4}(0)\,\textrm{ and }|x-y|>r/8\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}:x\in B_{r/4}(0),y\in B^{c}_{r/4}(0)\,\textrm{ and }|x-y|\leqslant r/8\right\}.

With this notation at hand, we get that

(4.27)

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\eta_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{\|\phi_{r}U_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}}\iint_{B_{r/4}(0)\times B_{r/4}(0)}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle+2\iint_{\mathbb{D}}\dfrac{|\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\eta_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle+2\iint_{\mathbb{E}}\dfrac{|\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\eta_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy

\displaystyle+\iint_{B^{c}_{r/4}(0)\times B^{c}_{r/4}(0)}\dfrac{|\eta_{\varepsilon}(x)-\eta_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy.

Now, by repeating the proof of
[
32
, Proposition 21]
, one realizes that the last three integrals
in the above identity
behave like
O(\varepsilon^{n-2})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.
Moreover, we claim that the first integral behaves like
O(\varepsilon^{2-2s})
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
.

Indeed, it is proved in
[
15
,
14
]
that

\|\phi_{r}U_{\varepsilon}\|^{2^{\ast}}_{L^{2^{\ast}}(\Omega)}=C_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{n})\quad\textrm{as }\varepsilon\to 0^{+},

where
C_{n}>0
is a suitable constant only depending on
n
; on the other hand,
by performing the change of variables
x=\varepsilon\xi
and
y=\varepsilon\zeta
, we get

\begin{split}&\iint_{B_{r/4}(0)\times B_{r/4}(0)}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\dfrac{|U_{\varepsilon}(x)-U_{\varepsilon}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{n+2s}}\,dxdy\\
&\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{\varepsilon^{n-2}}{\varepsilon^{(2n-4)+n+2s}}\cdot\dfrac{|U_{1}(\xi)-U_{1}(\zeta)|^{2}}{|\xi-\zeta|^{n+2s}}\cdot\varepsilon^{2n}\,dxdy\\[2.84544pt]
&\qquad=\varepsilon^{2-2s}\cdot[U_{1}]^{2}_{s}=O(\varepsilon^{2-2s}),\end{split}

where we have used Remark
3.2
(notice that
U_{1}=c^{-1}\,\mathcal{U}
).

Gathering all these information, from (
4.25
) we then obtain

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(t\eta_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}(\mathcal{S}_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{n-2})+O(\varepsilon^{2-2s}))-\dfrac{t^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}-C\lambda\dfrac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}\varepsilon^{n-(p+1)\tfrac{n-2}{2}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}\big(\mathcal{S}_{n}+O(\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})\big)-\dfrac{t^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}-C\lambda\dfrac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}\big(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}}\big)-\dfrac{t^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}-C\lambda\dfrac{t^{p+1}}{p+1}\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}=:g(t),

provided that
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small and for a suitable constant
C>0
.

To proceed further, following the proof of
[
6
, Lemma 3.4]
, we turn to study
the maximum/minimum points of
g
. To this end we first observe that, since

\text{$g(t)\to-\infty$ as $t\to\infty$},

there exists some point
t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}\geqslant 0
such that

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}g(t)=g(t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}).

If
t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}=0
, we have
g(t)\leqslant 0
for all
t\geqslant 0
, and the lemma
is trivially established as a consequence
of (
4.28
).
If, instead,
t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}>0
, since
g\in C^{1}((0,\infty))
we get

0=g^{\prime}(t_{\varepsilon,\lambda})=t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})-t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}^{2^{*}-1}-C\lambda t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}^{p}\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}.

In particular, recalling that
t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}>0
, we can rewrite the above identity as

(4.29)

\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}}=t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}^{2^{*}-2}+C\lambda t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}^{p-1}\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}

from which we easily derive that

t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}<(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})^{1/(2^{*}-2)}.

We now distinguish two cases, according to the assumptions.

Case (i):
\kappa_{s,n}>\beta_{p,n}
. In this case we first observe that, since

t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}>0
, from identity
(
4.29
) we easily infer the existence
of some
\mu_{\lambda}>0
such that

t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}\geqslant\mu_{\lambda}>0\quad\text{provided that $\varepsilon$ is small enough}.

This, together with the fact that since the map

t\mapsto\dfrac{t^{2}}{2}(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})-\dfrac{t^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}

is increasing in the closed interval

[0,\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})^{1/(2^{*}-2)}]
, implies

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant 0}g(t)=g(t_{\varepsilon,\lambda})

\displaystyle<\dfrac{(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})^{1+2/(2^{*}-2)}}{2}-\dfrac{(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})^{2^{*}/(2^{*}-2)}}{2^{*}}-C\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}})^{n/2}-C\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\dfrac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{n/2}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}}-C\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}<\dfrac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{n/2},

provided that
\varepsilon>0
is sufficiently small.
We explicitly stress that, in the last estimate, we have exploited in a crucial way
the assumption
\kappa_{s,n}>\beta_{p,n}
.

Case (ii):
\kappa_{s,n}\leqslant\beta_{p,n}
.
In this second case, we
begin by claiming that

(4.30)

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}=0.

Indeed, suppose by contradiction that
\ell:=\limsup_{\lambda\to\infty}t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}>0
:
then, by possibly choosing a sequence
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k}
diverging to
\infty
,
from (
4.29
) we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}}=t_{\varepsilon,\lambda_{k}}^{2^{*}-2}+C\lambda_{k}t_{\varepsilon,\lambda_{k}}^{p-1}\varepsilon^{\beta_{p,n}}\to\infty

but this is clearly absurd. Now we have established
(
4.30
), we can easily complete the proof of the lemma:
indeed, by combining (
4.28
) with (
4.30
), we have

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(t\eta_{\varepsilon})\leqslant g(t_{\varepsilon,\lambda})\leqslant\dfrac{t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}^{2}}{2}\big(\mathcal{S}_{n}+C\varepsilon^{\kappa_{s,n}}\big)-\dfrac{t_{\varepsilon,\lambda}^{2^{*}}}{2^{*}}\to 0\quad\text{as $\lambda\to\infty$},

and this readily implies the existence of
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{0}(p,s,n,\varepsilon)>0
such that

\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\mathcal{J}_{\lambda,p}(t\eta_{\varepsilon})<\frac{1}{n}(\mathcal{S}_{n})^{2/n}\quad\text{for all
$\lambda\geqslant\lambda_{0}$},

provided that
\varepsilon>0
is small enough but
fixed
. This ends the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

The desired result in Theorem
1.5

now follows from the Mountain Pass Theorem, thanks to Lemmata
4.8
,
4.9
and
4.10
.
∎
