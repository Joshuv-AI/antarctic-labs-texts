Gaining Outlier Resistance with Progressive Quantiles: Fast Algorithms and Theoretical Studies
Yiyuan She, Zhifeng Wang, Jiahui Shen

Abstract
Outliers widely occur in big-data applications and may severely affect statistical estimation and inference. In this paper, a framework of outlier-resistant estimation is introduced to robustify an arbitrarily given loss function. It has a close connection to the method of trimming and includes explicit outlyingness parameters for all samples, which in turn facilitates computation, theory, and parameter tuning. To tackle the issues of nonconvexity and nonsmoothness, we develop scalable algorithms with implementation ease and guaranteed fast convergence. In particular, a new technique is proposed to alleviate the requirement on the starting point such that on regular datasets, the number of data resamplings can be substantially reduced. Based on combined statistical and computational treatments, we are able to perform nonasymptotic analysis beyond M-estimation. The obtained resistant estimators, though not necessarily globally or even locally optimal, enjoy minimax rate optimality in both low dimensions and high dimensions. Experiments in regression, classification, and neural networks show excellent performance of the proposed methodology at the occurrence of gross outliers.



1
Introduction

Outliers are bound to occur in real-world big data and may severely affect statistical analysis. Many commonly used methods, including the lasso
(
Tibshirani, 1996
)
, break down in the presence of a single bad observation. We study the problem in a general setup. Given an arbitrary loss function
l
, a response vector
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a design matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, the coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
can be estimated by solving the following optimization problem

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})+\sum_{j=1}^{p}P(\beta_{j})\mbox{ s.t. }{\boldsymbol{\eta}}={\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}},

(1)

where
l
measures the discrepancy between the observed response and the prediction, and
P
represents a regularizer that can also be a constraint. Neither
l
nor
P
is necessarily convex. The loss can be, say, a deviance function corresponding to a generalized linear model (GLM), a robust loss like Huber’s loss,
or the well-known hinge loss or exponential loss for the purpose of classification. The loss function need not be associated with any probability distribution.

The goal of this work is to robustify (
1
) for any given
l
at the occurrence of a small number of
gross outliers
—we stress that the desired
outlier resistance
should not only accommodate samples with mild deviations from the model assumption, but also handle extreme anomalies with severe outlyingness and leverage. Some frequently used robust losses—Huber’s loss and the
\ell_{1}
-norm loss, in particular—are not resistant to gross outliers, and hence one may want a reinforced robustification in the setup of (
1
). The nature of the task eventually leads to an
\ell_{0}
-type regularization proposal.

The research of robust statistics undergoes a long history and leads to fruitful outcomes. A standard means to gain robustness is to modify the loss, or equivalently, reweight the samples. We illustrate the idea with robust regression. Instead of minimizing the quadratic loss
\|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}
, M-estimation
(
Huber and Ronchetti, 2009
;
Hampel et al., 2011
;
Maronna et al., 2006
)
solves
\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\sum_{i}\rho(y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
or
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\psi({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})=\boldsymbol{0}
, where
\rho
is a robust loss and
\psi=\rho^{\prime}
. Let
w(t)=\psi(t)/t
and
{\boldsymbol{r}}={\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}
. The score equation can be written as
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\{w({\boldsymbol{r}})\circ({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{y}})\}=\boldsymbol{0}
, where the multiplicative weights
w(r_{i})
are applied componentwise. The design of a robust loss thus amounts to that of a weight function.
See
Loh, 2017
and
Avella-Medina, 2017
, for example, for some theoretical properties. We call the above scheme the
multiplicative
robustification, to contrast with the
additive
fashion to be introduced in Section
2
. An iterative algorithm called iterative reweighted least squares (IRLS) is popularly used in computation. The most critical and expensive step however lies in the preliminary resistant fit with high-breakdown
(
Rousseeuw and Yohai, 1984
;
Rousseeuw, 1985
)
, also the focus of our paper. Some challenges of resistant estimation in methodology, theory, and computation are summarized as follows.

First, when
l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})
has a more complicated expression than that in terms of
r_{i}=y_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}
only, how to reshape it to guard against gross outliers does not seem straightforward.
According to
Bianco and Yohai, 1996
, simply applying
\rho
on the deviance residuals of a GLM
(
Pregibon, 1982
)
does not ensure consistency and an extra bias correction term must be added into the criterion.
Croux and Haesbroeck, 2003
limited
\rho
to a certain class to guarantee the existence of a finite solution, and included an additional weighting step to get a bounded influence function.
We refer to
Avella-Medina and Ronchetti, 2018
for a robust quasi-likelihood form by use of both a
\psi
function and a weight function. To us, another important line of work on the trimmed likelihood estimator (TLE)
(
Hadi and Luceño, 1997
;
Vandev and Neykov, 1998
;
Kurnaz et al., 2018
)
, as an extension of least trimmed squares (LTS)
(
Rousseeuw, 1985
)
, is most motivating. LTS and TLE give rise to our new regularized form of resistant estimation that is more amenable to both optimization and analysis.

The theoretical challenges faced by today’s robust statisticians are perhaps even more pressing. For example, even under
n>p
(let alone in high-dimensional settings), (a) how trimming inflates the risk, (b) how small the universal minimax lower bound could be, and (c) what makes a theoretically sound model selection criterion, are all surprisingly unknown in the literature. We emphasize the
finite-sample
nature of desired statistical theory (e.g., Theorems
4
–
6
), because in real-life data applications, it is often hard to know how large the sample size should be relative to the number of unknowns to apply asymptotics. Breakdown point provides a useful index of the robustness of a given method, but as pointed out by
Huber and Ronchetti, 2009
, such worst-case studies are not probabilistic and may be too conservative on a particular dataset.
In addition to robust analysis, parameter tuning based on asymptotics or breakdown point suffers the same theoretical difficulties.

The last big challenge lies in computation. Resistant fits are much more costly than IRLS or
\Theta
-IPOD
(
She and Owen, 2011
)
which takes them as initial points. Due to the inherent nonconvexity, subset sampling is heavily used in LTS-like algorithms to generate multiple starting values
(
Rousseeuw and Driessen, 1999
)
. Unfortunately, the number of required samplings grows exponentially in
p
and so these procedures already become inaccurate and/or prohibitive for moderate
p
. Developing more efficient outlier-resistant algorithms and mitigating the burden of subset sampling are at the core of modern-day robust statistics. Moreover, the fact that the computationally obtained solutions are not necessarily globally optimal poses nontrivial challenges in theoretical analysis.

This paper attempts to address some aforementioned
obstacles to gain outlier resistance, with the hope to advance the practice of robust statistics to more sophisticated learning tasks. Our main contributions are threefold.
a) A general resistant learning framework is introduced to robustify an arbitrarily given loss, with an “
\ell_{0}+\ell_{2}
” form of regularization to deal with gross outliers in possibly high dimensions. It has a close connection to the method of trimming but includes explicit outlyingness parameters, which in turn facilitates computation, theory, and parameter tuning. b) Although the associated problem is highly nonconvex and nonsmooth, extremely scalable algorithms with implementation ease can be developed with guaranteed convergence. In particular, the statistical error of the sequence of iterates can converge
geometrically
fast. A progressive optimization technique is proposed to relax the regularity condition and alleviate the requirement on the starting point so that on regular datasets the number of data resamplings can be substantially reduced.
c) Combined statistical and computational treatments make it possible to perform nonasymptotic analysis beyond the M-estimation setting, and the obtained “A-estimators”, though not necessarily globally or even locally optimal, enjoy minimax rate optimality in both low dimensions and high dimensions.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
introduces our regularized resistant learning framework and compares it to trimming. Section
3
deals with the computational issues by progressive iterative quantile thresholding. Section
4
provides nonasymptotic theoretical support for the obtained estimators under proper regularity conditions. Section
5
reveals some universal minimax lower bounds and investigates the issue of parameter tuning. Section
6
performs extensive simulation studies and real world data analysis. The proof details and more simulation results are given in the appendix.

2
L_{0}
-regularization vs. Trimming

We begin with the robust estimation problem (
1
) without regularization. The customizable loss determines the performance metric, so we prefer
not
to alter its form in the process of robustification. This is possible by use of an additive regularized estimation.

Concretely, re-define
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
as
{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, where
\gamma_{i}
characterizes the outlyingness of the
i
th sample and we allow it to be large.
Taking advantage of the sparsity in
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, as outliers are never the norm, we propose an
\ell_{0}
-constrained,
\ell_{2}
-penalized outlier-resistant estimation

\min_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})+\frac{\nu}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}\ \,\mbox{ s.t. }{\boldsymbol{\eta}}={\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\mbox{ }\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\leq q,

(3)

where
q
satisfies
q\leq n/2
and is assumed to be an integer throughout the paper. Here, the number of unknowns,
n+p
, is larger than the number of observations, and the “
\ell_{0}+\ell_{2}
” regularization plays an effective role in dealing with the high-dimensional challenge.

Unlike the popular
\ell_{1}
-norm penalty, the
\ell_{0}
‘norm’ used in the constraint is indifferent to the magnitude of
\gamma_{i}
and does not result in any undesired bias. An alternative idea is to utilize a non-convex sparse penalty—in the regression setting, this amounts to M-estimation, the diverse choices of the penalty lead to different robust losses
(
She and Owen, 2011
)
, and the conclusion extends to regularized
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
-estimation in possibly high dimensions
(
She and Chen, 2017
, Lemma 2)
. But (
3
) has some distinct advantages: the constraint directly controls the maximum number of outliers and
\|\cdot\|_{0}
is arguably the ideal function to enforce sparsity regardless of the severity of aberrant samples. In practice, specifying the value of
q
, rather than a penalty parameter
\lambda
, is easier and more convenient.

In addition to the sparsity-promoting regularization, we include the
\ell_{2}
-shrinkage to enhance numerical stability and compensate for collinearity in the presence of clustered outliers. We will see in Section
3.1
that with
\nu
introduced, the samples with minor deviations can still contribute to the model fitting. Empirically,
\nu
is not a sensitive parameter, and we often set
\nu
to a mild value (say 1e-4). As long as
\nu>0
, the
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
-estimate is finite.
But if we force
\nu=0
, (
3
) has an interesting and important connection to the method of trimming
(
Rousseeuw, 1985
;
Hadi and Luceño, 1997
;
Vandev and Neykov, 1998
)
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume
l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})
takes a sample-additive form
\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{0}(\eta_{i};y_{i})
and

\inf_{\eta}l_{0}(\eta;y)=0\mbox{ for all }y\in\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
.

i) Given any minimizer
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
of the
\ell_{0}
-
constrained

{l}_{0}^{(1)}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})\leq\cdots\leq{l}_{0}^{(n)}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})

l_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\beta}};y_{i})

conversely, given any
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
that minimizes
\sum_{i=1}^{n-q}{l}_{0}^{(i)}({\boldsymbol{\beta}})

\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\bar{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

\ell_{0}
-constrained joint problem.

ii)
The same connection holds between the
\ell_{0}
-
penalized

iii)
Finally, any minimizer

So in a sense, imposing the

Finally, an extension of (

3
Progressive Iterative Quantile-thresholding

This section studies how to solve the computational problems defined in Section
2
, where the main obstacle lies in nonconvexity and nonsmoothness. Thanks to the
sparsity
of the problem, obtaining a globally optimal solution may not be necessary in many real world applications. This section develops two classes of iterative algorithms, the BCD type and the MM type, both of which can accommodate a differentiable loss function that is not necessarily convex. More importantly, we propose a progressive optimization technique to relax the condition required to enjoy the best statistical accuracy.

3.1
Outlier-resistant regression

To illustrate the main ideas and techniques, this part studies outlier-resistant regression with
n>p
:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}:=f(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma})\text{ s.t. }\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q.

(5)

Compared with LTS, the inclusion of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
in (
5
) renders the quadratic loss intact, and so block-wise coordinate descent (
BCD
) can be used for computation. The sub-problem of
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
is trivial, and given
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, the best
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
can be obtained with a quantile thresholding operator
\Theta^{\#}

(
She et al., 2013
)
.
Given any
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define
\Theta^{\#}(\boldsymbol{s};q,\nu)
to be a vector
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

t_{(j)}=s_{(j)}/(1+\nu)\mbox{ if }1\leq j\leq q\mbox{, and }0\mbox{ otherwise,}

where
s_{(1)},\ldots,s_{(n)}
are the order statistics of
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}
satisfying
|s_{(1)}|\geq\cdots\geq|s_{(n)}|
, and
t_{(1)},\ldots,t_{(n)}
are defined similarly.
To ensure
\Theta^{\#}
is a function and avoid ambiguity, we assume throughout the paper that in performing
\Theta^{\#}(\boldsymbol{s};q,\nu)
, either
|s_{(q)}|>|s_{(q+1)}|
or
|s_{(q)}|=|s_{(q+1)}|=0
occurs, referred to as the
\Theta^{\#}
-uniqueness assumption.
The resultant BCD algorithm proceeds as follows

\begin{cases}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}=\Theta^{\#}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)};q,\nu),\\
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}=({\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{X}})^{+}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}),\end{cases}

(6)

or
simply

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}=\Theta^{\#}({\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}+({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}){\boldsymbol{y}};q,\nu),

(7)

starting with
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
or
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
(
6
) and
(
7
) are referred to as iterative quantile-thresholding (IQ) algorithms in the paper. Intuitively, (
7
) repeatedly thresholds a weighted average of
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and the current
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
-estimate. But the quantile thresholding is no ordinary hard-thresholding due to its
iteration-varying
threshold and concurrent
\ell_{2}
shrinkage. (
7
) shares some similarity with the celebrated “C-step” designed on the basis of trimming
(
Rousseeuw and Driessen, 1999
)
. On the other hand, at each iteration C-step only uses a subset of observations to get the coefficient vector, while IQ uses all the samples with adjustment. In a sense, C-step’s subset selection amounts to checking the zero-nonzero pattern of
\gamma_{i}^{(t+1)}
instead of its exact value.

The algorithm also suggests some virtues of the complementary
\ell_{2}
-shrinkage. When
\nu=0
, given the optimal
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
,
\hat{\gamma_{i}}=0
or
y_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
. Letting
d_{i}=1_{\hat{\gamma}_{i}\neq 0}
and
{\boldsymbol{D}}=\mbox{diag}\{d_{i}\}
, from (
6
),
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
satisfies

{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{D}}){\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}={\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{D}}){\boldsymbol{y}}.

Because of the binary nature of
d_{i}
, the
i
th observation is either kept unaltered or removed completely, which might hurt the estimation accuracy in the presence of a number of mildly outlying observations. When
\nu>0
, the above equation still holds for
d_{i}=1_{\hat{\gamma}_{i}\neq 0}/(1+\nu)
, and the fact that
{\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{D}}
is nonsingular even when
q
takes a conservatively large value secures numerical stability and variance reduction. More insights can be gained by transforming
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
to
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\prime}
where
{\boldsymbol{V}}
is the orthogonal complement to the range of
{\boldsymbol{X}}
,
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
. Then, during the occurrence of clustered outliers with a large overall leverage, the
\ell_{2}
regularization has a beneficial effect to cope with the coherent Gram matrix
{\boldsymbol{V}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{V}}={\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}
.

Establishing IQ’s rate of convergence is nontrivial since it is a nonconvex optimization algorithm in high dimensions (rigorously speaking,
n
observations and
p+n
unknowns). We give two results regarding the convergence of its computational error
and
statistical error.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the algorithm defined by (
6
) or (
7
).

a) For any
T\geq 1
,

\begin{split}\mathop{\min}_{1\leq t\leq T}\|{\boldsymbol{X}}&({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}^{2}=\mathop{\min}_{1\leq t\leq T}\|\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)})\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq\ &\frac{1}{T}(\|(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{H})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}^{2}+\nu\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1)}\|_{2}^{2}).\end{split}

b) Let
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{0}=o^{*}
,
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a sub-Gaussian random vector with mean zero and scale bounded by
\sigma
(cf. Definition
1
in Appendix
A.2
). Let
q=\vartheta o^{*}
with
\vartheta>1
. Suppose the design matrix satisfies a restricted isometry property (
RIP
) for some
\varepsilon
:
0<\varepsilon<1

\|\boldsymbol{H}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}\leq(1-\varepsilon)\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2},\ \forall\boldsymbol{\Delta}:\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{0}\leq(1+\vartheta)o^{*}.

(8)

Then with probability at least
1-C(n-p)^{-c}
, for any
t\geq 1

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim&\Big(\frac{1}{1+\kappa}\Big)^{t-1}\|\boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{\kappa}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa}\vartheta o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}},\end{split}

(9)

where

\displaystyle\kappa=\{\varepsilon-1/\sqrt{\vartheta}+(1-1/\sqrt{\vartheta})\nu\}/(1-\epsilon),

(10)

and
C,c>0
are constants.
Furthermore, under a slightly stronger noise assumption that
\epsilon_{i}
are independent sub-Gaussian(0,
\sigma^{2}
), the following convergence result holds for all
t\geq 1
with probability at least
1-C(n-p)^{-c}-C\exp(-cp)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|{\boldsymbol{X}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim&\Big(\frac{1}{1+\kappa}\Big)^{t-1}\|{\boldsymbol{X}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{\kappa}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+p\sigma^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa}\vartheta o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}.\end{split}

(11)

The first result in (a) states a
universal
convergence rate of the order
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
, and the conclusion is free of any conditions. On the other hand, this rate does not take the model parsimony into full account.

The statistical error convergence shown in (b) is surprising and delightful.
Although
\Theta^{\#}
is not a nonexpansive mapping, let alone a contraction, IQ approaches
the statistical target

geometrically
fast with high probability, as long as
\theta
or
\nu
is properly large:

\displaystyle\varepsilon+(1-1/\sqrt{\vartheta})\nu>1/\sqrt{\vartheta}.

(12)

Such a linear convergence result in the discrete nonconvex setup is novel to the best of our knowledge.
A positive
\nu
can improve the linear rate parameter
\kappa
. The error bound remains the same order as long as
{\nu}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
is dominated by a constant multiple of
\sigma^{2}\vartheta o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}
. (The best value of
\nu
should be data-adaptive, but fixing it at a mild value already gives satisfactory performance.)
The statistical error does not vanish as
t\rightarrow\infty
due to the existence of noise, but Section
5
will show that
p\sigma^{2}+o^{*}\sigma^{2}\log(en/o^{*})
is the desired error rate in robust regression. Hence over-optimization seems to be unnecessary.

Of course, to enjoy the fast convergence, a regularity condition on
{\boldsymbol{H}}
must be assumed. Restricted isometry like (
8
) is widely used and known to hold in compressed sensing and many high-dimensional sparse problems
(
Candes and Tao, 2005
;
Hastie et al., 2015
)
.
Intuitively,

{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{I}}-\boldsymbol{H}){\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\geq\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{2}^{2}
,
\forall\boldsymbol{\Delta}:\|\boldsymbol{\Delta}\|_{0}\leq(1+\vartheta)o^{*}
means all principal submatrices of size
(1+\vartheta)o^{*}
of
{\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}
should have eigenvalues greater than or equal to a positive
\epsilon
. The cone to define the restricted eigenvalue shrinks when
o^{*}
is small. It is well known that RIP is much less demanding than the mutual coherence restrictions on
h_{i,j}(i\neq j)

(
van de Geer and Bühlmann, 2009
;
Hastie et al., 2015
)
which are realized by low-leveraged data since
|h_{i,j}|\leq(h_{ii}h_{jj})^{1/2}
.

We will show that for strongly convex losses, pursuing
globally
optimal estimators in our additive regularized framework can ultimately remove all regularity conditions, namely, outlier resistance regardless of leverage and outlyingness. But on regular datasets this may be unnecessary. The global minimization comes at a heavy computational cost: multiple random starts have to be used due to the nonconvexity, and experience shows that even when
p=50
, the subset sampling popularly used in robust statistics already becomes ineffective and expensive
(
Rousseeuw and Hubert, 1997
)
.

So a legitimate question on large data is how to
relax
the condition required for statistical accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. Theorem
2
sheds new light on the issue. For example, with
\nu=0
,
\varepsilon>1/\sqrt{\vartheta}
ensures the geometric statistical convergence, and to make it hold more easily, an obvious means is to increase
\vartheta
. The larger the value of
\vartheta
takes, the bigger
\varepsilon
is, and so the smaller the influence of the initial point according to (
9
) or (
11
). The adverse effect is the larger error term
\vartheta o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}
.
But we can
gradually
tighten the cardinality constraint with a sequence
Q(t)
which deceases from
n
to
q
as
t
increases. The resultant algorithm is termed as
P
rogressive
I
terative
Q
uantile-thresholding (
PIQ
).
PIQ can substantially improve the mediocre performance of IQ. It is easy to extend Theorem
2
from fixed quantiles to varying quantiles, but deriving a theoretically optimal cooling scheme is challenging. Fortunately, this is not a big issue in practice; various schemes seem to do a decent job, such as
Q(t)=n-at^{2}
(quadratic),
Q(t)=2n/(1+\exp(at))
(sigmoidal),
Q(t)=n-a\log t
(logarithmic) and so on.

Our progressive quantile proposal is innovative in that it is not just another intricate sampling scheme and does not increase the number of initial points. Compared with state-of-the-art algorithms, the tables and figures in Section
6.1
clearly show that it can
significantly reduce the need of data resampling (thereby the overall computational time) without sacrificing the statistical accuracy. In addition, we must point out that PIQ works in the
opposite
direction to the class of forward pathwise algorithms and boosting methods
(
Bühlmann and Yu, 2003
;
Needell and Tropp, 2009
;
Zhang, 2013
)
which all grow a model from the null. Both of our analysis and computer experiments support the backward manner in resistant learning.

An alternative (and perhaps simpler) algorithm can be developed following the principle of majorization-minimization (
MM
)
(
Hunter and Lange, 2004
)
. The technique is general and will play a vital role in extending BCD-type PIQ to conquer a general loss.
Recall the concatenated notation
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}=[{\boldsymbol{X}}\ {\boldsymbol{I}}]
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=[{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}\ {\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}]^{T}
.
Instead of directly minimizing the original objective function
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\|\boldsymbol{y}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}/2+\nu\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}/2
, we construct a surrogate function
g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})
by (joint) linearization

g_{\rho}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}):=\|\boldsymbol{y}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}/2+\langle\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}-{\boldsymbol{y}}),\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2},

(13)

where
\rho
is the inverse stepsize parameter to be chosen later.
Then minimizing
g
with respect to
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
yields a sequence of iterates

\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}=\arg\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}g_{\rho}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})\text{ s.t. }\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q.
After some algebraic manipulations,
g
can be rephrased as

\begin{split}g_{\rho}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})=\,&\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}+\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}-{\boldsymbol{y}})/\rho\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&-\frac{1}{2\rho}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}-\boldsymbol{y})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\nu\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}\\
=\,&\frac{\rho}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}+\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}-{\boldsymbol{y}})/\rho\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}+c(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}),\end{split}

(14)

where
c(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
.
Noticing the separability in
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, the resultant update is given by

\begin{cases}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}={\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{y})/\rho,\\
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}=\Theta^{\#}({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}-({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{y})/\rho;q,{\nu}/{\rho}).\end{cases}

(15)

As long as the stepsize is properly small, e.g.,
\rho>\|\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\|_{2}^{2}
, (
15
) is convergent (cf. Theorem
3
).
A similar conclusion to Theorem
2
can be proved, which supports a progressive scheme with
Q(t)
decreasing to
q
to relax the requirement of the initial point.

Different from (
6
), (
15
) updates
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma})
simultaneously and does not involve any matrix inversion or expensive operations. A downside of MM however lies in its smaller step-size, which not only slows down convergence but may (surprisingly) affect statistical accuracy in resistant learning; see Section
4
.

3.2
Harnessing a general loss

This part uses the optimization techniques developed for resistant regression to deal with a general objective with
p
possibly larger than
n
:

\min_{({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}l({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};{\boldsymbol{y}})+P(\varrho{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\lambda)+\frac{\nu}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}\ \mbox{ s.t. }\mbox{ }\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\leq q,

(16)

where
P
is often a sparsity-promoting penalty, but can also take the form of a ridge penalty or an
\ell_{0}
-constraint. Here, we add the algorithmic parameter
\varrho>0
to match the scale of the design and aid stepsize selection.
With linearization and MM, we can extend (
15
) to (
17
), (
18
) below, applicable to a broad family of
l
and
P
.

Theorem 3
.

Assume that
\nabla l(\eta;y)
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous. Given an arbitrary thresholding rule
\Theta
with
\lambda
as the threshold parameter (cf. Appendix
A.3
), consider the following algorithm

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}=\Theta(\varrho{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)};\boldsymbol{y})/\varrho;\lambda)/\varrho,

(17)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}=\Theta^{\#}({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}-\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)};\boldsymbol{y})/\varrho^{2};q,{\nu}/{\varrho^{2}}),

(18)

where
t\geq 0
,
\varrho>0
.
Let
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\boldsymbol{y})+P(\varrho{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\lambda)+\nu\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}/2
, where
P
is defined based on
\Theta
:

P(\theta;\lambda)-P(0;\lambda)=\int_{0}^{|\theta|}(\sup\{s:\Theta(s;\lambda)\leq u\}-u)\,\mathrm{d}u+q(\theta;\lambda)
for some
q(\cdot,\lambda)\geq 0
satisfying
q(\Theta(t;\lambda);\lambda)=0,\forall t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, given any starting point
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}
and any
\varrho>0
,
f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)})\leq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})-(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})^{T}(\varrho^{2}\boldsymbol{I}-L\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{X}})(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})/2
and
the following convergence rate holds

\mathop{\min}_{t\leq T}\ (\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})^{T}(\varrho^{2}\boldsymbol{I}-L\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\bar{\boldsymbol{X}})(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})\leq\frac{2f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)})}{T+1},\forall T\geq 0.

(19)

In particular, if
\varrho^{2}>{L}M_{\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}}(p,2q)
(cf. Section
1
) and
\nu>0
, any limit point
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
of
({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)})
satisfies

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\Theta(\varrho\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y})/\varrho;\lambda)/\varrho
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\Theta^{\#}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y})/\varrho^{2};q,{\nu}/{\varrho}^{2})
provided
\Theta
is continuous at
\varrho\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y})/\varrho
.

In robust regression, (
17
) degenerates to the linear
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
-update in (
15
) with
\rho=\varrho^{2}
, but notice the distinctive scaled form of (
17
) associated with a nonlinear thresholding operator
\Theta
. The Lipschitz condition is satisfied by the squared error loss, logistic deviance, and Huber’s loss, among many others, but is only used to give a universal stepsize. In fact, using a backtracking line search
(
Boyd and Vandenberghe, 2004
)
,
L
need not be known in implementation; see Remark
A.1
. The equations satisfied by
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
actually define a broader class of estimators that will be investigated in Section
4
.

In the remaining, we use BCD to tackle (
16
). The alternating update involves solving two sub-problems:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}l(\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)})+\nu\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}/2\ \,\text{ s.t. }\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q,

(20)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\arg\min}_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)})+P(\varrho\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda).

(21)

The first problem can be addressed using a similar MM trick as in Theorem
3
. But there is a more efficient way to directly locate the optimal support of
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
when
l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}l_{0}(\eta_{i};y_{i})
.
Specifically, with
\tilde{l}(t;a,b)=l_{0}(a+t,b)+\nu t^{2}/2
, the criterion becomes
\sum_{i:\gamma_{i}=0}l_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},y_{i})+\sum_{i:\gamma_{i}\neq 0}\tilde{l}(\gamma_{i};\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},y_{i})
.
Let
l_{0,i}=l_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)};y_{i})
and
\tilde{l}_{\min,i}=\min_{t}\tilde{l}(t;\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},y_{i})
. Then the index set
I
associated with the
q
largest differences
\delta_{i}:=l_{0,i}-\tilde{l}_{\min,i}
(
1\leq i\leq n
) gives the support. When
\nu=0
and
\tilde{l}_{\min,i}=0
, simply sorting the losses
l_{0,i}
achieves the goal.
Next, one merely needs to solve
q
univariate problems
\min_{s}\tilde{l}(s;\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},y_{i})
to get
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)}
.

For (
21
), an iterative MM algorithm similar to (
17
) can be developed, and we will show that it suffices to performing the update just once,

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}=\Theta(\varrho\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}-\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\nabla l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)};\boldsymbol{y})/\varrho;\lambda)/\varrho
. Again, we advocate pursuing
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
-sparsity via the hybrid “
\ell_{0}
+
\ell_{2}
” regularization in place of (
21
),

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}=\mathop{\arg\min}l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)})+\nu_{\beta}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}/2\mbox{ s.t. }\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta}.

By use of linearization, the resultant progressive quantile-thresholding is
given by

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t+1)}=\Theta^{\#}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t+1)};\boldsymbol{y})/\rho;q_{\beta}^{(t)},\nu_{\beta}/\rho),

where
\rho\geq LM_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(2q_{\beta})
(recall the definition of
M_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}
in Section
1
) and the sequence
q_{\beta}^{(t)}
drops to
q_{\beta}
eventually. (One might want to merge the two
\ell_{0}
-constraints into
\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta}+q_{\gamma}
to simplify the computation, but this would result in a larger statistical error due to the enlarged search space; see the analysis in Section
4
.)

At the end of the section, we make a comparison between the MM-type and the BCD-type PIQ algorithms. The first kind has a single-loop structure and maintains low per-iteration complexity. We do observe that MM is faster than BCD in some large-
p
classification problems (say when
p>500
), but in the conventional
n>p
setup and many other scenarios, BCD is more accurate and efficient. We will mainly use the BCD-type PIQ in applications and analysis unless otherwise specified.

4
Statistical Accuracy of A-estimators

A major theoretical challenge brought by the class of estimators from our computational algorithms is their lack of global optimality and stationarity,
which significantly differentiates them from conventional
M
-estimators and
Z
-estimators
(
van der Vaart, 1998
)
. We call such estimators
A
-estimators due to their
alternative
optimality nature.
In fact, even the alternative optimality
may be merely in a local sense. As far as we know, there is a lack of statistical accuracy studies for such algorithm-driven estimators. In this section, we aim for developing new techniques to reveal tight error rates of A-estimators as well as the comprehensive roles of algorithmic parameters in resistant learning, in both low and high dimensions.

Introducing the notion of noise in the non-likelihood setting is an essential component.
We define the
effective noise
associated with a statistical truth
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}=[\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*T},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*T}]^{T}
by

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=-\nabla l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}),

(22)

which is not affected by the regularizer.
In a GLM with cumulant function
b
and canonical link function
g=(b^{\prime})^{-1}
, the deviance based loss is given by
l(\boldsymbol{\eta})=-\langle\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{1},b(\boldsymbol{\eta})\rangle
, and so

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\boldsymbol{y}-g^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})=\boldsymbol{y}-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{y}).

In general, however, the effective noise jointly determined by the loss and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
may not be the raw noise on
{\boldsymbol{y}}
. For example, a robust loss associated with a bounded
\psi
function always gives rise to a bounded
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
, thereby sub-Gaussian regardless of the distribution of
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, making our analysis nonparametric in nature; the same is true for many classification losses.

Unless otherwise specified, we assume that
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is a
sub-Gaussian
random vector with mean zero and scale bounded by
\sigma
(cf. Definition
1
in Appendix
A.2
). This gives a broad family including Gaussian and bounded random variables; in particular, any Lipschitz
l
results in a sub-Gaussian effective noise. However, our analysis is not restricted by sub-Gaussianity; Theorem
A.4
in Appendix
A.11
gives a universal risk bound when
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
has a general Orlicz norm. Also, we allow
\gamma_{i}^{*}
to take arbitrarily large values to capture extreme anomalies; another possible way is to assume
\gamma_{i}
are i.i.d. following a certain distribution, but treating
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}
as an
n
-dimensional unknown parameter vector (with sparsity) is convenient in detecting outliers and in handling models with non-additive noise.

Our main tool to tackle the diverse form of the loss is the
generalized Bregman function
(GBF)
(
She et al., 2021
)
: given any differentiable
l

\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}):=l(\boldsymbol{\alpha})-l(\boldsymbol{\beta})-\langle\nabla l(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\rangle.

(23)

If, further,
l
is strictly convex,
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
becomes the standard Bregman divergence
\mathbf{D}_{\psi}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})

(
Bregman, 1967
)
. When
l(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}/2
,
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})=\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}-\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}/2
, abbreviated as
\mathbf{D}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
. In general,
\Delta_{l}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})
may not be symmetric, and we define its symmetrization by
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}):=(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta})+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\alpha}))/2
.

We work in a general setting with
p
possibly larger than
n
unless otherwise mentioned.
For simplicity, all
\ell_{2}
-shrinkage parameters are set to zero, and we assume
\nabla l
is
L
-Lipschitz continuous, and take
L=1
without loss of generality.

Let’s first consider the
advocated doubly constrained form introduced at the end of Section
3
to exemplify the error bounds. As aforementioned, to make the statistical error study more realistic, rather than restricting to the set of globally optimal solutions, one needs to pay particular attention to the A-estimators defined by

\displaystyle[left={\empheqlbrace}]\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\beta}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta}}\allowbreak l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y})

(24a)

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}:\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q_{\gamma}}l(\boldsymbol{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y}).

(24b)

But the
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
delivered from the fast PIQ algorithm may not possess the global alternative optimality in (
24a
). The good news is that according to Theorem
A.1
in Appendix
A.4
, all these global or local A-estimators satisfy

\begin{cases}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\Theta^{\#}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y})/\rho;q_{\beta})\\
\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\Theta^{\#}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};\boldsymbol{y});q_{\gamma}),\end{cases}

(25)

for any
\rho>M_{{\boldsymbol{X}}}(2q_{\beta})
. Our statistical accuracy analysis is applicable to the whole class (and MM-type non-global estimators as well, with slight modification). For clarity, define
o^{*}=\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{0}
,
s^{*}=\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\|_{0}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
be an A-estimator that satisfies (
25
) for some
\rho>0
,
q_{\gamma}=\vartheta o^{*},q_{\beta}=\vartheta s^{*}
(
\vartheta\geq 1
) and
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}=q_{\gamma}
,
\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}=q_{\beta}
.
Let
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rho}=[{{\boldsymbol{X}}}/{\sqrt{\rho}}\ \ {\boldsymbol{I}}]
. Assume that there exists some
\delta>0
such that

\begin{split}(2\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{l}-\delta{\mathbf{D}}_{2})(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rho}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rho}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime})\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime})\end{split}

(26)

for sparse
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}
satisfying
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq\vartheta s^{*},\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq s^{*}
,
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq\vartheta o^{*},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{0}\leq o^{*}
. Then the following error bound
holds

\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})]\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\Big\{\vartheta o^{*}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{en}{\vartheta o^{*}}+\vartheta s^{*}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{ep}{\vartheta s^{*}}+{\sigma^{2}}\Big\},

(27)

where
C,c
are positive constants.

More generally, if the starting point
({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(0)})
:
\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta},\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(0)}\|_{0}\leq q_{\gamma}
already satisfies
\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{(0)},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})]=O(R)\allowbreak\{\vartheta o^{*}\sigma^{2}\allowbreak\log\frac{en}{\vartheta o^{*}}+\vartheta s^{*}\sigma^{2}\log\frac{ep}{\vartheta s^{*}}+\sigma^{2}\}
with
+\infty\geq R\geq 1
, to obtain the same conclusion (
27
), (
26
) can be relaxed to

\big\{2(1-\frac{1}{R})\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{l}+\frac{C}{R(R\delta\vee 1)}{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}-\delta{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\big\}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rho}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\rho}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime})\geq\frac{(1-1/R)}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime})

(28)

for some constant
C>0
.

The regularity condition is an extension of the classical
\ell_{2}
-form restricted isometry imposed on the augmented design. Indeed, for
l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})=\|{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\|_{2}^{2}/2
, the condition can written as
\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}\geq[{1}/\{{(2-\delta)}\sqrt{\vartheta}\}]\big(\rho\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}\big),\ \forall({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}):\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq(1+\vartheta)s^{*},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq(1+\vartheta)o^{*}
for some
\delta>0
and large enough
\vartheta
.
According to the proof, under
q_{\beta}=p
, the term
\rho\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}
on its right-hand side can be dropped and so the condition degenerates to the ordinary RIP used in Theorem
2
, due to the orthogonal decomposition
\|{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}=\|{\boldsymbol{X}}\{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+({\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{X}})^{+}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\}\|_{2}^{2}+\|({\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}){\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}
. We have argued in Section
3
that in low dimensions, low leverage implies low mutual coherence which in turn implies RIP. But the plain definition of leverage cannot incorporate outlier scarcity, and is noninformative as
p>n
, since
{\boldsymbol{H}}={\boldsymbol{I}}
when
{\boldsymbol{X}}
has full row rank. The Bregman-based restricted isometry on the augmented design gives an extension of the notion of leverage to high dimensions and non-quadratic losses.

Theorem
4
gives useful implications regarding the algorithmic parameters as well. For example, from (
26
), to enjoy the statistical guarantee, the inverse stepsize
\rho
must be properly small. The finding is insightful and novel. Throughout the machine learning literature, slow learning rate is recommended when training a nonconvex learner. But our statistical error analysis strongly cautions against using an extremely small step size when nonconvexity occurs. The BCD-type algorithm design appears advantageous in this aspect. Moreover, a high-quality starting point, usually expensive in computation, certainly brings some statistical benefit seen from the generalized regularity condition (
28
) (consider an extreme case
R=1
). But simply enlarging
\vartheta
gives another promising way to relax (
26
), and supports the progressive tightening proposal in Section
3
.

When
\vartheta,\delta
are treated as constants and
s^{*}\vee o^{*}>0
, (
27
) shows that A-estimators can achieve an error bound of the order
\mathcal{\sigma}^{2}s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})+\sigma^{2}o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})
, which reduces to
p\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{2}o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})
when there is no
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
-sparsity. This error rate involves the number of outliers apart from
the number of relevant predictors. But the influence of outliers remains controlled in the sense that the bound does not grow with the magnitude of
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}
. In the situation of relatively few outliers:
o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})\ll s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})
, the proposed method can attain
the celebrated rate
\sigma^{2}s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})
for (clean) large-
p
variable selection. On the other hand, one should be aware that on ‘big dirty’ data with large
n
and
o^{*}
,
\sigma^{2}o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})
could be the dominant error term.

Remark 4.1
.

One may wonder whether the overall error rate in Theorem
4
can be further improved by pursuing a global minimizer. Theorem
A.2
shows that this is not the case, but the regularity condition gets
relaxed. Concretely, the symmetrized GBF
\bar{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{l}
in
(
26
) will be replaced by
{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{l}
, and the right-hand side of
(
26
) will be just zero. Hence if the loss
l
defined on the systematic component is
\mu
-strongly convex (e.g., regression), the error bound holds with
\delta=\mu
,
free
of any RIP or leverage restriction. This shows the inherent soundness of the proposed resistant learning framework.

Remark 4.2
.

The
\ell_{2}
-recovery result proved in Theorem
4
is fundamental, from which one can also obtain estimation error bounds. For example,
Theorem
A.3
shows that under a slightly stronger regularity condition,

\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\sigma^{2}\{s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})+o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})\}/n

holds with high probability. In the classical
n>p
setup with no penalty imposed on
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, it becomes
\|\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\lesssim\{p\sigma^{2}+o^{*}\sigma^{2}\log(en/o^{*})\}/n
. See Appendix
A.5
for details.

Our theoretical techniques can cope with the penalized form of (
16
) as well. For instance, Theorem
5
studies the outlier-resistant lasso (cf. Section
2
), where

l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma};\boldsymbol{y})=\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}/2,\ P(\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda)=\lambda\varrho\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{1},

with
\varrho>0
. (The theorem proved
in Appendix
A.6
is actually regarding a general loss and its proof applies to all subadditive penalties.)

Theorem 5
.

Let
\lambda=A\sigma\sqrt{\log(ep)}
with
A
a sufficiently large constant. Then if
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
is an A-estimator of resistant lasso for some
\varrho
and
\vartheta
(cf. Theorem
A.1
), the risk bound

\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})]\lesssim\sigma^{2}\vartheta o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}+K^{2}\sigma^{2}s^{*}\log(ep)+\sigma^{2}

(29)

holds under the assumption that there exists a large enough
K
such that

K\sqrt{J}\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}+\varrho\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{J}^{c}}\|_{1}\geq\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\lambda\sqrt{\vartheta})+(1+\varepsilon)\varrho\|\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\mathcal{J}}\|_{1}
for any
\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}
with
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq(1+\vartheta)o^{*}
, where
\varepsilon
is a positive constant,
\mathcal{J}=\{j:\beta_{j}^{*}\neq 0\}
and
J=|\mathcal{J}|
.

When
K
and
\vartheta
are treated as constants, the error rate of the resistant lasso is slightly worse (by a
logarithmic term) than (
27
) for the doubly constrained form.

5
Minimax Lower Bounds and Model Comparison

We first derive some universal lower bounds that are satisfied by all estimators. These nonasymptotic results are stated for GLMs. Let
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}=\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
and
y_{i}|\eta_{i}^{*}
be independent and follow a distribution in the regular exponential family with dispersion
\sigma^{2}
and
l_{0}
be the negative log-likelihood function (cf. Appendix
A.7
for details), and consider a general signal class with
p
possibly larger than
n

\mathcal{B}(s^{*},o^{*})=\{(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}):\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\|_{0}\leq s^{*},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{0}\leq o^{*}\}.

(30)

Theorem 6
.

In the regular exponential dispersion family with
n\geq 2,p\geq 2,1\leq o^{*}\leq n/2,1\leq s^{*}\leq p/2,
define

\displaystyle P(s^{*},o^{*})=s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})+o^{*}\log(en/o^{*}).

(31)

Let
I(\cdot)
be any nondecreasing function with
I(0)=0,I\not\equiv 0
.
(i) Suppose for some
\kappa>0

{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})\sigma^{2}\allowbreak\leq\kappa{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

\forall\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq s^{*}
,
\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq o^{*}
. Then there exist positive constants
\tilde{c},c
, depending on
I(\cdot)
only, such that

\inf_{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}\,\sup_{(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\in\mathcal{B}(s^{*},o^{*})}\mathbb{E}[I({\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*},\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})/\{\tilde{c}\sigma^{2}P(s^{*},o^{*})/\kappa\})]\geq c>0,

(32)

where
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
denotes any estimator of
(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})
. (ii) Suppose
{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1})\sigma^{2}\allowbreak\leq\overline{\kappa}{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{1})
and
\underline{\kappa}{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2})\leq{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\boldsymbol{0},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{2})
,
\forall\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}:\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{i}\|_{0}\leq is^{*},\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i}\|_{0}\leq io^{*},i=1,2
, where
\underline{\kappa}/\overline{\kappa}
is a positive constant. Then there exist positive constants
\tilde{c},c
such that

\inf_{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}\,\sup_{(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\in\mathcal{B}(s^{*},o^{*})}\mathbb{E}[I({\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})/\{\tilde{c}\sigma^{2}P(s^{*},o^{*})\})]\geq c>0.

(33)

Finer analysis and lower bounds are presented in Appendix
A.7
.
We illustrate the conclusion for classification. Because the logistic deviance has a
1/4
-Lipschitz continuous gradient, the regularity condition in (i) is satisfied for any
\kappa\geq M_{\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}}(s^{*},o^{*})/4
(cf. Section
1
and Appendix
A.3
). So when
\kappa\leq cn
,
\{s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})+o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})\}/n
, or
(p+o^{*}\log(en/o^{*}))/n
when
s^{*}=p
, is the desired minimax lower error rate for all classifiers, both with constant positive probability and in expectation (corresponding to
I(t)=1_{t\geq c}
and
I(t)=t
). A similar conclusion for prediction can be drawn from (ii). To the best of our knowledge, such a kind of results with the usage of GBFs for resistant GLMs
is novel in robust statistics.

Together with the upper error bounds (e.g., Theorem
4
and Theorem
A.3
), PIQ enjoys minimax
rate optimality provided that
q_{\gamma}
is not set too large relative to
o^{*}
. It is common in practice to directly specify the cardinality bound based on domain knowledge, say,
\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\leq 0.25n
, for the sake of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
-estimation.
On the other hand, if the goal is to identify all outliers to have the best predictive model, one ought to choose the regularization parameters in a more data-adaptive manner. This is regarded as a vital and challenging task in robust statistics
(
Ronchetti et al., 1997
;
Müller and Welsh, 2005
;
Salibian-Barrera and Van Aelst, 2008
)
. Indeed, outliers may render resampling based cross-validation inappropriate, and although there is an abundance of information criteria, which one is sound in theory and reality lacks a clear conclusion. The joint variable selection in high dimensions and the flexible choice of the loss exacerbate the issue. (The
\ell_{2}
-shrinkage parameter is however much less sensitive and can be fixed at a mild value.)

In the general setup where both
\boldsymbol{\beta}
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are possibly sparse, the upper and lower error bounds suggest a universal function to penalize model complexity

P(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}+\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\log(en/\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0})+\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}+\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}\log(ep/\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}).

(34)

We refer to the associated criterion as the
predictive information criterion
(
PIC
).
In the case that no variable selection is needed (
\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}=p
), one can rewrite (
34
) as

P(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}+\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\log(en/\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0})

(35)

for pure outlier identification.

(
34
) has some involved logarithmic terms and is not a simple
\ell_{0}
-type penalty, but it leads to an ideal model comparison criterion with a solid finite-sample theoretical support.

Theorem 7
.

Assume
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
defined in (
22
) is a sub-Gaussian random vector with mean zero and scale bounded by a constant and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\in\mathcal{M}
and
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
. Define
P(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=P(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=J(\boldsymbol{\beta})\log(ep/J(\boldsymbol{\beta}))+J(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\log(en/J(\boldsymbol{\gamma}))
. Assume that there exist constants
\delta>0
and
A_{0}\geq 0
such that
(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}-\delta\mathbf{D}_{2})(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime})+A_{0}(P(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})+P(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}))\geq 0
,
\forall\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
. Then for a sufficiently large constant
A
, any

(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
that minimizes

l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\boldsymbol{y})+AP(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})

(36)

subject to
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathcal{M}
must satisfy

\mathbb{E}\big[{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})\big]\lesssim s^{*}\log(ep/s^{*})+o^{*}\log(en/o^{*}).

(37)

Theorem
7
achieves the optimal error rate as Theorem
4
and Theorem
5
, but a major difference here is that no parameters (like
q,\lambda
) are involved,
but just some absolute constants. Moreover, Theorem
7
does not require any RIP or large signal-to-noise ratio conditions.

When the noise distribution has a dispersion parameter
\sigma^{2}
, Theorem
7
still applies, but the penalty in (
36
) becomes
A\sigma^{2}P({\boldsymbol{\beta}},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
with an unknown factor. A preliminary robust scale estimate can be possibly used. But an appealing result for robust regression is that the estimation of
\sigma
can be totally bypassed.
For clarity, Theorem
8
assumes
n>p
and
no sparsity in
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, in which situation a scale-free form of PIC, motivated by
She and Tran, 2019
, is given by

(n-p)\log\big(\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}\big)+\alpha_{1}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}+\alpha_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\log(en/\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}),

(38)

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
are absolute constants.
Again, the soundness of (
38
) when the outliers are not awfully many places no limit on their leverage or outlyingness. (More generally, using (
34
) in place of (
35
) gives a more general form of scale-free PIC for joint variable selection and outlier identification; Theorem
8
’
proved in
Appendix
A.9
implies Theorem
8
, and applies to
p>n
.)

Theorem 8
.

Let
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
, where
\boldsymbol{X}
has full column rank,
\epsilon_{i}
are independent sub-Gaussian
(0,\sigma^{2})
and
\,\mathbb{E}\epsilon_{i}^{2}=\sigma_{i}^{2}=c_{i}\sigma^{2}
with
c_{i}
some positive constants and
\sigma^{2}
unknown. Define
l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma};\boldsymbol{y})=\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2}
and
P(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\log(en/\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0})
. Assume
P(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\leq(n-p)/A_{0}
for some constant
A_{0}>0
. Let
\delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})=AP(\boldsymbol{\gamma})/(n-p)
, where
A
is a positive constant satisfying
A<A_{0}
, and so
\delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})<1
. Then, for sufficiently large values of
A_{0}
and
A
, any
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
that minimizes
\log l(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\gamma};\boldsymbol{y})+\delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
subject to
\delta(\boldsymbol{\gamma})<1
must satisfy

{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})\lesssim\sigma^{2}o^{*}\log(en/o^{*})+p\sigma^{2}
with probability at least
1-c(n-p)^{-c^{\prime}}
for some constants
c,c^{\prime}>0
.

Combining Theorem
6
and Theorem
8
supports the modified BIC proposed by
She and Owen, 2011
based on empirical studies.
However,
(
38
) has some subtle but important differences from BIC, and the resemblance is just a coincidence. First, unlike most information criteria, its derivation and justification do not need an infinite sample size. Second, the factor
\log(en/\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0})
—not exactly
\log n
as in BIC that assumes clean data—arises due to the seek for outliers. Third, the general PIC complexity term (
34
) (cf. Theorem
7
or Theorem
8
’) has the overall inflation effect
added
to the degrees of freedom, as opposed to the standard multiplicative relation in AIC, BIC, and many others.

In common with most nonasymptotic studies, our theorems do not show tight constants. However, these numerical constants do not depend on a specific problem and in some easier cases can be determined by Monte Carlo experiments—for example, when
n>p
, we recommend
5.5\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}+\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\log(en/\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0})
in (
38
) for robust regression. Theoretically deriving the optimal constants requires much finer
analysis and we will not pursue in the current paper.

6
Experiments

6.1
Simulations

In this part, we perform simulation studies to compare PIQ with some popular robust and/or sparse estimation methods in
p<n
and
p>n
setups. Unless otherwise mentioned, the raw predictor matrix
\boldsymbol{X}=[\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is generated in the default way:
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small iid}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is a Toeplitz design covariance matrix with
\Sigma_{ij}=\rho^{|i-j|}
. Here, we consider regression and classification models contaminated with highly-leveraged outliers in the following four setups. More experiment results in other settings are in reported in Appendix
A.12
due to limited space. Recall
o^{*}=\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\|_{0},s^{*}=\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\|_{0}
.

Example 1 (
n>p
, regression)
:
n=1000,p=10,\rho=0.5
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=[1,1,0.5,0.5,-1.5,\allowbreak-1.5,-1,\allowbreak-1,1,1]^{T}
. We set
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}=[5,\ldots,5,0,\ldots,0]^{T}
with the first
o^{*}
components nonzero, and modify the first
o^{*}
rows of
\boldsymbol{X}
to
[3,\ldots,3]
. The response vector is generated according to
\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
with
\epsilon_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\small iid}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.

Example 2 (
n>p
, classification)
:
n=1000,p=10,\rho=0.5
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=[3,3,1.5,\allowbreak 1.5,3,3,-3,-3,3,3]^{T}
. To introduce outliers, we change the first
o^{*}
rows of
\boldsymbol{X}
to
[3,\ldots,3]
so that the first
o^{*}
elements in
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
are 45 and set
\gamma_{i}^{*}=-90
,
1\leq i\leq o^{*}
and 0 otherwise. The response vector is generated according to the Bernoulli distribution with mean
1/(1+\exp(-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}-\gamma_{i}^{*}))
for the
i
th observation.

Example 3 (
p>n
, regression)
:
n=200,p=1000
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=[1,0.5,0,0,\allowbreak-0.5,-1,0,\ldots,0]^{T}
so that
s^{*}=4
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}=[5,\ldots,5,0,\ldots,0]^{T}
. High-leveraged outliers are introduced in the same way as in Example 1.

Example 4 (
p>n
, classification)
:
n=200,p=1000
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=[3,1.5,3,\allowbreak 0,\ldots,0]^{T}
. Similar to Example 2, outliers are added, with
\gamma^{*}_{i}=-45
,
1\leq i\leq o^{*}
and 0 otherwise.

The following 11 methods are included for comparison: S-estimator
(
Rousseeuw and Yohai, 1984
)
, LTS
(
Rousseeuw, 1985
)
, Bianco and Yohai’s robust logistic regression (B-Y)
(
Bianco and Yohai, 1996
)
, TLE
(
Hadi and Luceño, 1997
)
, robust quasi-likelihood estimator (QLE)
(
Cantoni and Ronchetti, 2001
)
, quantile lasso (QL)
(
Belloni and Chernozhukov, 2011
)
, robust LARS (RLARS)
(
Khan et al., 2007
)
, sparse LTS (S-LTS)
(
Alfons et al., 2013
)
, sparse maximum tangent-likelihood estimator (S-MTE)
(
Qin et al., 2017
)
, elastic-net LTS for classification (enetLTS)
(
Kurnaz et al., 2018
)
and PENSE
(

)
.

1.5o^{*}
or
1.5s^{*}
nonzeros using the true model.
The other parameters are set to their default values. In calling PIQ, we use the BCD-type, with
\nu
fixed at

.

or

.

already seems satisfactory in PIQ.

) rate, as well as the rate of successful joint detection (

); see

. Concretely, the masking probability is the fraction of undetected true outliers (misses), and the JD is the fraction of simulations with zero miss.

6.3
Robust neural network for Parkinson’s disease detection

Neural networks have attracted a great deal of attention in machine learning. In this experiment, we adapt the PIQ technique to a neural net model and test its performance for Parkinson’s disease (PD) detection. There has been a surge of interest in speech analysis of PD recently, as voice recordings from these patients tend to show typical patterns like aperiodic vibrations.
We use the dataset from
Little et al., 2007
which collects a range of biomedical voice measurements from normal people and patients with PD, where the 22 predictors for 195 recordings include various measures of vocal fundamental frequency and variation in amplitude, as well as some nonlinear metrics constructed from raw voice recordings.
We randomly split the dataset into a training subset (60%) and a test subset (40%).

Previous studies suggest the presence of nonlinear effects and interactions of the features in this challenging classification task (
Little et al., 2007
,
Sakar et al., 2013
). Hence we designed a feedforward neural network that consists of an input layer, two dense layers with 22 and 12 nodes respectively, and one output layer. The dense layers deploy the popular rectified linear units (ReLU)
(
Nair and Hinton, 2010
)
, and the output node uses a softmax activation.
We trained the network for 200 epochs and attained a misclassification error rate of 9.1% on the test data—in contrast, an SVM with a nonlinear RBF kernel gave 17.9%. (Meanwhile, classical robust classification methods like B-Y, QLE, and TLE all behaved poorly, with the misclassification error rates
>25\%
.)

On the other hand, the noisy signals and potential outliers occurring suggest the need of robustification. But the overall network criterion barely resembles that of regression or logistic regression due to its substantial nonlinearity and hierarchy, and how to reweight the samples in this sophisticated model to limit the influence of outliers sounds tricky.
Following the additive robustification scheme, we simply added sample-indicator features into the input layer (cf. Figure
3
), accompanied by a group
\ell_{0}+\ell_{2}
regularization (
q=0.05n
,
\nu=
1e-4) to correct for sample outlyingness in constructing predictive factors.
Our optimization-based PIQ algorithm can be seamlessly incorporated into the process of back-propagation.
Though a simple modification, the robustified neural network gave an impressive misclassification error rate 6.4%, an improvement of about 30% over that of the vanilla neural network.

7
Summary

The work studied how to gain outlier resistance for a given loss that is not necessarily quadratic and may go beyond the likelihood setup.
Motivated by the method of trimming, we proposed an
\ell_{0}
+
\ell_{2}
regularized learning framework. We showed that pursuing its (global) minimizers can cope with extreme outliers regardless of leverage and outlyingness, but on large-scale data, there is always a balance between statistical accuracy and computational efficiency. Hence a more meaningful and deeper question is how to design a scalable algorithm to alleviate the starting point requirement and the regularity conditions to obtain the desired order of statistical accuracy.

In this work, the interplay between high dimensional statistics and nonconvex optimization led to fruitful results for resistant learning on the learning rate, cardinality control, the choice of regularization parameters, and so on. In particular, a novel progressive backward optimization scheme is brought forward which, unlike subset sampling, is cost effective, and can significantly improve the statistical performance of iterative quantile-thresholding.
We hope that the work could raise concern about outliers among (big) data analysts, as well as providing a sensible and universal means to boosting resistance in regression, classification and more sophisticated learning tasks.

\geq\,&\{1-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}),\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}))\}(1+\nu)\mathbf{D}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}).\end{split}

Substituting
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}
for
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
gives

\begin{split}&\{1-\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}),\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}))\}(1+\nu)\mathbf{D}_{2}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})+(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)})\\
\leq\,&-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{f}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})-\langle\nabla f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}),\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\rangle+((1+\nu)\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{f})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)}).\end{split}

(A.6)

Since
\|{\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}\|_{2}=1
,
(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)})\geq 0
. Together with

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}),\allowbreak\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}))\leq 1/\sqrt{\vartheta},

we obtain

\begin{split}&((1-1/\sqrt{\vartheta})(1+\nu)\mathbf{D}_{2}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}+\nu\mathbf{D}_{2})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\\
\leq\,&(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)})+\langle(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{H})^{2}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{y})+\nu\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\rangle\\
=\,&(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)})+\langle-\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}+\nu\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}\rangle\\
=\,&(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)})+\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\rangle+\nu\langle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}\rangle,\\
\end{split}

(A.7)

where
\boldsymbol{X}=[\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}]\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{V}^{T}
is the singular value decomposition of
\boldsymbol{X}
with
\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(n-p)}
and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{T}\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
which is still a sub-Gaussian random vector with mean 0 and scaled bounded by
\sigma
.
From
{\boldsymbol{I}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}={\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{T}
,
\Delta_{l}(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime})=\|{\boldsymbol{U}}_{\perp}^{T}({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}/2
.

The stochastic term
\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\rangle
can be bounded using the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
be a sub-Gaussian random vector with mean zero and scale bounded by
\sigma
. Given
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
0\leq J\leq p
, there exist universal constants
A,C,c>0
such that for any
a>0
, the following event

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{0}\leq J}\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon},\boldsymbol{X}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\rangle-\frac{1}{2a}\|\boldsymbol{X}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\|_{2}^{2}-aA^{2}\sigma^{2}J\log(ep/J)>a\sigma^{2}t

(A.8)

occurs with probability at most
C\exp(-ct)p^{-cA^{2}}
for any

.

The result is a variant of Lemma 4 of
She, 2016

and can be obtained from its proof.
According to Lemma
4
, there exist constants
A,C,c>0
such that
for any
a>0
,

\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}^{T}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\rangle\leq\frac{1}{a}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})+aA^{2}\sigma^{2}(1+\vartheta)o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}

(A.9)

occurs with probability at least
1-C(n-p)^{-c}
. Since
\langle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*},-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}+\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\rangle\leq(b/2)\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}+(1/2b)\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
for any

,
combining (
A.7
) and (

) gives

\begin{split}&\{((1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}})+(2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}-\frac{1}{b})\nu)\mathbf{D}_{2}+(1-\frac{1}{a})\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\\
\leq\,&(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})+aA^{2}\sigma^{2}(1+\vartheta)o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}+\frac{b\nu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

(A.10)

Let

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\big\{(2-\frac{1}{a})\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}+(2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}-\frac{1}{b})\nu\big\}.

(A.11)

By the regularity condition,

\begin{split}&(1+\kappa)(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\\
\leq\,&\{((1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}})+(2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta}}-\frac{1}{b})\nu)\mathbf{D}_{2}+(1-\frac{1}{a})\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l}\}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t+1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\\
\leq\,&(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})+aA^{2}\sigma^{2}(1+\vartheta)o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}+\frac{b\nu}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Finally, by a recursive argument, we have

\begin{split}&(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})\\
\leq\,&\Big(\frac{1}{\kappa+1}\Big)^{t-1}(\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l})(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*})+\frac{a}{\kappa}A^{2}\sigma^{2}(1+\vartheta)o^{*}\log\frac{en}{o^{*}}+\frac{b\nu}{2\kappa}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{split}

Choosing

,
b=1
, which ensures

, we obtain the conclusion on the sequence of
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
-iterates.

In fact, according to (
A.11
), we may choose any

and
b>0
, and so the linear convergence rate holds if
\varepsilon>1/(2\sqrt{\vartheta})-(2-{1}/({\sqrt{\vartheta}})\nu
.

To show the conclusion on the sequence of

-iterates, notice that for any

,

{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(t)}-{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}.

and

\,\mathbb{P}[\|{\boldsymbol{H}}{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{2}^{2}\geq cr({\boldsymbol{X}})\sigma^{2}+t\sigma^{2}]\leq C\exp(-c[\{t^{2}/r({\boldsymbol{X}})\}\wedge t]),

where

denotes the rank of

The last inequality can be shown from the Hanson-Wright inequality
(

under the independent sub-Gaussian assumption since

and

Proof of Theorem

First, a thresholding function
(

(iv)

\Theta
is defined component-wise if either

or
\lambda
is replaced
by a vector. Throughout the paper, assume that

Let

and

if

+\infty

for notational simplicity.

First, we claim that for any
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\tilde{l}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})\leq\varrho^{2}\mathbf{D}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})

\displaystyle\ {\mathbf{\Delta}}_{\tilde{l}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ {\tilde{l}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})-{\tilde{l}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})-\langle\nabla{\tilde{l}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}),\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\tilde{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}+t(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})),\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t-\int_{0}^{1}\langle\nabla\tilde{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}),\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\rangle\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\frac{L}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{LM_{\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}}(p,2q)}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{L\|\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}.

Next, we solve
\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})
. Due to the separability, the problem is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\varrho^{2}\|{\boldsymbol{\beta}}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}+{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}\nabla l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-};{\boldsymbol{y}})/\varrho^{2}\|_{2}^{2}/2+P(\varrho\boldsymbol{\beta};\lambda),\mbox{ and }

\displaystyle\min_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-}+\nabla l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-};{\boldsymbol{y}})/\varrho^{2}\|_{2}^{2}/2+(\nu/\varrho^{2})\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{2}^{2}/2+\iota({\boldsymbol{\gamma}};q).

With a change of variables, it leads to the MM updates given in the theorem, cf.
She, 2012
. Furthermore, it is easy to show (details omitted) that the construction of
g
is equivalent to

g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})=f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})+(\varrho^{2}\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\tilde{l}})(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-}).

Then,
g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})-f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})=\varrho^{2}\mathbf{D}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\tilde{l}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{-})
.
By the optimality of

(A.13)

It follows that

(\varrho^{2}\mathbf{D}_{2}-\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\tilde{l}})(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)},\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})\leq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})-f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)}).

Finally, we prove the limit-point equation. Since

\nu\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}/2+P(\varrho\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)};\lambda)\leq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)})\leq f(\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(0)}),

the sequence of

Let
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
be any limit point of

satisfying

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{0}&=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j_{k}+1)}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(j_{k})}\}\\

(A.15)

and

due to the continuity assumption of

Remark A.1

The conclusion of

A.11
General noise and stochastic breakdown

Given a random variable
X
, its Orlicz
\psi
-norm is defined by

\|X\|_{\psi}=\inf\big\{M>0:\,\mathbb{E}\psi(\frac{|X|}{M})\leq 1\big\},

where
\psi
is a strictly increasing convex function on
[0,+\infty)
with
\psi(0)=0
; see, e.g.,
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
. Similar to the definition of sub-Gaussian random vectors (cf. Definition
1
), we say that a random vector
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
has its
\psi
-norm bounded above by
\sigma
if

\|\langle\boldsymbol{\epsilon},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\rangle\|_{\psi}\leq\sigma\|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\|_{2}

for any vector
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
. The components of
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
are not required to be independent.

Some commonly used
\psi
-norms in statistics are the
L_{p}
norms,
\psi(x)=x^{p}
with
p\geq 1
, which means
|X|
possesses a finite
p
-th moment, and
\psi_{p}
norms,
\psi(x)=\exp(x^{p})-1
, covering sub-Gaussian (
p=2
) and sub-exponential (
p=1
) random variables. By Markov’s inequality, that
X
has a finite
\psi
-norm
\sigma
implies a tail probability bound

\displaystyle\,\mathbb{P}(|X|>t)\leq\frac{2}{\psi(t/\sigma)+1},\quad\forall t>0

which encompasses diverse heavy/light tail decays.

In the following theorem, instead of restricting our attention to a sub-Gaussian type effective noise
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
(cf. (
22
)), which
is
sensible in many applications especially when a Lipschitz continuous loss is in use, we make a more general assumption that
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
has a finite Orlicz
\psi
-norm bounded above by
\sigma
and
\psi(x)\geq cx^{2}
(i.e., the second moment exists), together with the regularity condition
\lim\sup_{x,y\rightarrow\infty}\psi(x)\psi(y)/\psi(cxy)<\infty

(
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
)
for some constant
c>0
that is satisfied by say the
L_{p}
norms and
\psi_{p}
norms. (Note that the
\psi
here is not the same psi function to define an M-estimator.)

Theorem A.4
.

Let
\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
be a solution to
\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q_{\gamma}}l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\boldsymbol{y})

with
0\leq q_{\gamma}\leq n,0\leq q_{\beta}\leq p

\delta>0
such that

({\boldsymbol{\Delta}}_{l}-\delta{\mathbf{D}}_{2})(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\prime})\geq 0

The difficulty lies in the possible

\displaystyle\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\theta}-\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle+\max_{\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\dot{\Gamma}}\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\dot{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rangle

Indeed, by an extension of Massart’s finite class lemma (cf. Lemma 2.2.2 in
van der Vaart and Wellner, 1996
) and Stirling’s formula, we obtain

\|\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Gamma(2q_{\beta},2q_{\gamma})}\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle\|_{\psi}\leq C\psi^{-1}\big(\exp\{c(q_{\gamma}\log\frac{en}{q_{\gamma}}+q_{\beta}\log\frac{ep}{q_{\beta}})\}\big)\sigma

where
C,c
are constants depending on
\psi
only.

Based on (
A.74
) and the regularity condition,

\frac{\delta}{4}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}(\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\leq{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{*})-\frac{\delta}{4}\|\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}(\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{*})\|_{2}^{2}\leq{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}^{*})+\frac{1}{\delta}(\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Gamma(2q_{\beta},2q_{\gamma})}\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle)^{2}.

By assumption,
\{\,\mathbb{E}[(\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Gamma(2q_{\beta},2q_{\gamma})}\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle)^{2}]\}^{1/2}\lesssim\|\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Gamma(2q_{\beta},2q_{\gamma})}\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\boldsymbol{\theta}\rangle\|_{\psi}
. Hence

\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})]\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta}\,\mathbb{E}{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\{[\sigma^{2}\psi^{-1}(\exp\{cq_{\gamma}\log\frac{en}{q_{\gamma}}+cq_{\beta}\log\frac{ep}{q_{\beta}}\})]^{2}\}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark A.4
.

The oracle inequality implies that even if
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
has
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\|_{0}\gg q_{\beta},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{*}\|_{0}\gg q_{\gamma}
, as along as
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
can be well approximated by some
{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
:
\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q_{\gamma}
in the sense that the bias

\,\mathbb{E}{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})
is controlled by
\sigma^{2}\big[\psi^{-1}\big(\exp\{c(q_{\gamma}\log\frac{en}{q_{\gamma}}+q_{\beta}\log\frac{ep}{q_{\beta}})\}\big)\big]^{2}/\delta
up to a multiplicative constant, the prediction risk bound is of the order
\sigma^{2}/\delta^{2}
times

\displaystyle\Big[\psi^{-1}\big(\exp\big\{c(q_{\gamma}\log\frac{en}{q_{\gamma}}+q_{\beta}\log\frac{ep}{q_{\beta}})\big\}\big)\Big]^{2}.

(A.75)

This applicability to approximately sparse signals is quite useful in reality.

Remark A.5
.

Taking a specific
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
in (
A.73
) and assuming the associated regularity condition holds, the error bound depends on
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}
through its support size only, thereby
finite
regardless of its magnitude or outlyingness. This gives a risk-based breakdown point result that accounts for the randomness of the estimators.

Concretely, to define a general stochastic breakdown point, we fix the true systematic component
{\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, but can freely alter the response
{\boldsymbol{y}}
in

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}(o)=\{{\boldsymbol{y}}:\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\leq o,\|{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}\|_{\psi}<+\infty\}

(A.76)

with
o\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}=-\nabla l({\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}};{\boldsymbol{y}})
.
Given any estimator
(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
(that implicitly depends on the data
{\boldsymbol{X}},{\boldsymbol{y}}
), its finite-sample breakdown point
\epsilon^{*}
can be defined by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\min\{o:\sup_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(o)}\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]=+\infty\}.

(A.77)

Then, in the setup of Theorem
A.4
, the estimator given
q_{\beta},q_{\gamma}
has the stochastic breakdown point

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}\geq(q_{\gamma}+1)/n.

(A.78)

For example, for
\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=\arg\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:\|\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{0}\leq q_{\beta},\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q_{\gamma}}l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\boldsymbol{y})

with a quadratic
l({\boldsymbol{\eta}};{\boldsymbol{y}})=\|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{2}^{2}/2
, the contamination model associated with
\mathcal{Y}(o)
is
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\boldsymbol{X}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}

subject to
\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\|_{0}\leq o
, where the nonzero components of
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
can be arbitrarily large, but even in high dimensions the breakdown point of
\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
is no lower than
(q_{\gamma}+1)/n
. The direct link between the
\ell_{0}
-constraint and breakdown point facilitates parameter tuning.

Statistically speaking, obtaining a precise error rate
\big[\psi^{-1}\big(\exp\{c(q_{\gamma}\log\frac{en}{q_{\gamma}}+q_{\beta}\log\frac{ep}{q_{\beta}})\}\big)\big]^{2}
in Theorem
A.4
is much more informative than simply knowing that the risk is finite for the purpose of breakdown studies. However, it is an interesting question to determine what relaxed conditions
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
should satisfy to guarantee (
A.78
) for a general
extended
real-valued function
l:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
. Toward this, we change the
{\mathbf{D}}_{2}
in (
A.77
) to the generalized Bregman
{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}
:
\epsilon^{*}=\min\{o:\sup_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}(o)}\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}})]=+\infty\}/n
,
and make a no-model-ambiguity assumption:
l
is differentiable at
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}\in D=\{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}:l({\boldsymbol{\eta}})<+\infty\}
with the gradient
\nabla l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*})=-\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}
is a finite optimal solution to the
Fenchel conjugate
when
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
:

\displaystyle l^{*}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\rangle-l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}),

(A.79)

and the extended real-valued convex function
l^{*}
is differentiable at
-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
. The assumption simply means that
(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*},-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})
makes a conjugate pair. Note that
l
need not be overall strictly convex, especially when
D
is compact, according to Danskin’s min-max theorem
(
Bertsekas, 1999
)
. Then we have the following conclusion.

Theorem A.5
.

Under
\,\mathbb{E}[{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l^{*}}({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})]<M<+\infty
for all
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}:\|{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\|_{0}\leq q
, the
{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}
-risk based breakdown point for any finite solution
\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
to
\min_{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}:\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_{0}\leq q}l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}};\boldsymbol{y})
satisfies
\epsilon^{*}\geq(q+1)/n
.

In the special case that
l
is strongly convex on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to a certain norm, as long as the effective noise has the second moment, the theorem recovers (
A.78
) since
l^{*}
can be shown to be strongly smooth with respect to the Euclidean norm
(
Rockafellar, 1970
)
(albeit not delivering a concrete error rate as before). Two other notable features apart from the randomness:
l
is a general extended real-valued function (not restricted to be a function of
{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
as in M-estimation), and its conjugate plays an important role in bounding the risk; also, we do not need to assume a positive norm penalty which is a key element to the proof of Proposition 1 in
Alfons et al., 2013
.

Proof.

First, we show that

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}})\leq{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l^{*}}({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}).

(A.80)

By definition,
l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})\leq l(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}})
, from which it follows that
{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}})\leq\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}}\rangle
. Define
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}=\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}}
and

h({\boldsymbol{\delta}})={\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}({\boldsymbol{\delta}}+\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}).

By assumption,
l
is a proper function and applying Fenchel-Young’s inequality gives

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}})\leq\langle{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\rangle|_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}}}\leq\frac{1}{c}{\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}({\boldsymbol{\delta}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}})|_{{\boldsymbol{\delta}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}-\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}}}+\frac{1}{c}h^{*}(c{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})

or
(1-1/c){\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l}(\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}\hat{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}{\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}})\leq h^{*}(c{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})/c

for any
c>0
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle h^{*}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\rangle-l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}})+l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})+\langle\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}),{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}),{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\rangle-l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}})+l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}),\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\rangle-l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}})+l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})-\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}),\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\rangle

\displaystyle=l^{*}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}))+l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*})-\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}),\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\rangle,

where
l(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}),\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*})
are known to be finite.
Using the optimality of
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}
, we have further

\displaystyle h^{*}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})

\displaystyle=l^{*}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}+\nabla l({\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}))-l^{*}(-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})-\langle{\boldsymbol{\zeta}},\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\rangle.

Moreover, from the assumption and definition (
A.79
), it is easy to show that
\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}\in\partial l^{*}(-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})
, and so
\nabla l^{*}(-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}})=\boldsymbol{\eta}^{*}
, from which it follows that

\displaystyle h^{*}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}})={\mathbf{\Delta}}_{l^{*}}({\boldsymbol{\zeta}}-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}},-{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}).

(A.81)

Taking
c=2
gives (
A.80
). The rest of the lines follow Remark
A.5
.
∎
