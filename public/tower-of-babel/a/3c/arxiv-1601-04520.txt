Abstract
. Many natural decision problems can be formulated as constraint satisfaction problems for reducts 𝔸 \mathbb{A} of finitely bounded homogeneous structures. This class of problems is a large generalisation of the class of CSPs over finite domains. Our first result is a general polynomial-time reduction from such infinite-domain CSPs to finite-domain CSPs. We use this reduction to obtain new powerful polynomial-time tractability conditions that can be expressed in terms of the topological polymorphism clone of 𝔸 \mathbb{A} . Moreover, we study the subclass 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of CSPs for structures 𝔸 \mathbb{A} that are reducts of a structure with a unary language. Also this class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} properly extends the class of all finite-domain CSPs. We apply our new tractability conditions to prove the general tractability conjecture of Bodirsky and Pinsker for reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures for the class 𝒞 \mathcal{C} .

1.
Introduction

Many computational problems in various areas of theoretical computer science can be formulated as
constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs)
. Constraint satisfaction problems where the variables take values from a finite domain are reasonably well understood with respect to their computational complexity.
The Feder-Vardi dichotomy conjecture for finite domain CSPs, which states that every finite-domain CSP is either in P or NP-complete, was recently solved independently by Bulatov
[
25
]
and Zhuk
[
42
]
.
The two solutions in fact prove the stronger
tractability conjecture

[
27
]

which provides an effective characterisation of those finite-domain CSPs that are NP-complete, and those that are in P.
These breakthroughs have been obtained using concepts and results from universal algebra.

The universal-algebraic approach can also be applied to classify the complexity of some classes of CSPs over infinite domains.
This approach works particularly well if the constraints can be defined over a homogeneous structure with a finite relational signature.
The class of CSPs that can be formulated this way is a considerable extension of the class of CSPs over finite domains, and captures many computational problems that have been studied in various research areas. For example, almost all CSPs studied in qualitative temporal and spatial reasoning belong to this class
[
9
]
.
The computational complexity of the CSPs where the
constraints can be defined over

({\mathbb{Q}};<)
has been classified
[
13
]
.
Also constraint languages definable over the random graph
[
16
]
,
the random poset
[
36
]
or over homogeneous tree-like structures
[
11
]

have been classified. These results were
obtained by using a generalisation of the universal-algebraic approach from finite-domain CSPs, and structural Ramsey theory.
However, it would be desirable to go further and to
reduce complexity
classification tasks for CSPs over infinite domains
to the rather more advanced classification results that
are known for finite-domain CSPs.

In this paper, we present a first result in this direction.
We study a subclass of
first-order definable structures with atoms

[
34
]

which recently attracted attention in automata theory
as a natural class of infinite structures with finite descriptions; see
[
23
,
22
,
35
]
, and references therein. In the
context of constraint satisfaction problems they were first studied in
[
34
]
, where the authors focussed on structures that are additionally
locally finite
,
a very strong restriction that is not needed for our approach.

We now state our results in more detail.
Let
\mathbb{A}
be a structure with a finite relational signature. The
constraint satisfaction problem
for
\mathbb{A}
, denoted by
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
, is the following computational problem:

Input.
A conjunction
\phi=\phi_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi_{m}
of atomic formulas over the signature of
{\mathbb{A}}
.

Question.
Is
\phi
satisfiable in
{\mathbb{A}}
?

An example of a problem that can be formulated
in this way is the three-colouring problem,
which can be formulated as
\Csp({\mathbb{A}})

where the domain
A
of
{\mathbb{A}}
has size three
and the signature contains a single binary relation symbol
that denotes the inequality relation
\neq
on
A
.
In order to answer whether a given finite graph
G
is 3-colourable, each edge
\{u,v\}
of
G

will be represented by an atomic formula of the form
u\neq v
.
The graph is 3-colourable if and only if the corresponding
conjunction of atomic formulas is satisfiable.

Another example is the problem to decide whether a given set of constraints of the form
x=y
or of the form
x\neq y
has a solution
(over any domain). This can be formulated as

\Csp({\mathbb{A}})
where
the domain of
{\mathbb{A}}

is any countably infinite set

,
and
=
and
\neq
denote the usual equality and inequality relations on
A
. Clearly, this problem
cannot be formulated as
\Csp({\mathbb{B}})
for a
structure
{\mathbb{B}}
over a finite domain (neither can it be formulated with a locally finite structure
\mathbb{B}
as considered in
[
34
]
).

A relational structure
\mathbb{A}
is called a

first-order reduct
of a relational structure

\mathbb{B}
if all relations of
\mathbb{A}
have
a first-order definition over
\mathbb{B}
.
A relational structure
\mathbb{B}
is called

if all relations of
\mathbb{B}
are unary.
We study the computational complexity of
\Csp({\mathbb{A}})
for the class of all finite-signature first-order reducts
\mathbb{A}
of unary structures.
Note that without loss of generality, we can focus on the case where
\mathbb{A}
has a countably infinite domain,
as any reduct of a unary structure has the same
\Csp
as a reduct of a countably infinite structure.
In particular, our class of CSPs properly contains the class of constraint satisfaction problems over finite domains.
The structure
(A;=,\neq)
from above is another example of a first-order reduct of a unary structure.

One of our main results is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let

be a set, and let

be subsets of

Every CSP for a first-order reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
is in P or NP-complete.

In fact, we prove a stronger result and provide an algebraic characterisation of the structures in our class that have a tractable CSP.
This characterisation coincides with the conjectured characterisation of tractable CSPs from
[

]
for
first-order reducts of finitely bounded
homogeneous structures
(confer Conjecture

In contrast to the three classification results for infinite-domain CSPs mentioned above

The class of CSPs studied here

almost any more ambitious classification project for infinite-domain CSPs.
We give some examples.

(1)

We have already mentioned that our structures

structures definable with atoms

]

appeared under different names in various contexts (Fraenkel-Mostowski models, nominal sets, permutation models) and has not yet been studied systematically from the CSP viewpoint.

(2)

An important class of infinite structures

and more generally first-order reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures. For details we have to refer to Section

(3)

Many CSPs that are of special interest in computer science and mathematics are formulated over classical structures such as the integers, the rationals, or the reals.
Some first partial classifications are available also for such CSPs

A natural question here is whether one can

In the first structure, we can define

This implies that every structure with finite unary signature is isomorphic to a first-order reduct of the structures above.

Theorem

Theorem 2
.

Let
A
be a set, and let
\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
be a partition of

Let

be a closed function clone on

such that the unary operations

Then exactly one of the following holds:

•

there are finitely many elements

and a continuous clone homomorphism from

•

there are unary operations

\forall x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\in A,\;e_{1}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})=e_{2}f(x_{2},\dots,x_{m},x_{1})\,.

Our strategy to prove Theorem

is then as follows: we first reduce the task to the situation
where the polymorphism clone of the structure

under consideration satisfies the assumptions of Theorem

(in Section

).
If the first case of this theorem applies, hardness of the CSP follows from general principles

.
If the second case applies, we associate to

a structure over a finite domain which has a polynomial-time tractable CSP, and finally
prove that

Our reduction from infinite-domain CSPs to finite-domains CSPs is very general, and is another main contribution of this paper (Section

All that is needed here is that

is definable in a finitely bounded relational structure.
For structures

known (unconditional) tractability conditions for finite-domain constraint satisfaction (in Section

that has been introduced

of the form

in
\Psi
, where

.
Let

be a qf-formula that defines

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{A}\models R(h(j(1)),\dots,h(j(r)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{B}\models\chi(h(j(1)),\dots,h(j(r)))

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad\chi(z_{v^{-1}j(1)},\dots,z_{v^{-1}j(m)})\in g(v)

(because of

)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\quad T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A})\models U(g(v)).

Next, consider a

\Comp_{u^{-1}k,v^{-1}k}(u,v)

in
\Phi

.
Let

.
Suppose that

.
From
({\ddagger})

\mathbb{B}\models\chi(h(kt(1)),\dots,h(kt(s)))
.
Again by
({\ddagger})
we get that
\chi(z_{v^{-1}kt(1)},\dots,z_{v^{-1}kt(s)})

.
Hence,

\displaystyle T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A})\models\Comp_{u^{-1}k,v^{-1}k}(g(u),g(v)).

(
\Phi
satisfiable implies
\Psi
satisfiable.) Conversely, suppose that
\Phi
is satisfiable
in

. That is, there exists
a map

from
I
to the

m
-types in

that satisfies all conjuncts of
\Phi
.
We show how to obtain an assignment

\Psi
in
\mathbb{A}
.
Define an equivalence relation

on

as follows.
Let

.
Let

.
We define

if, and only if,

contains the formula

.
Note that the choice of

the intersection of

and

contains

.
Let

be a bijection.
By construction, the constraint
\Comp_{u^{-1}k,u^{\prime-1}k}(u,u^{\prime})
is satisfied by

which by definition of the relation means that

contains

iff

contains

.

We prove that

is an equivalence relation. Reflexivity and
symmetry are clear from the definition.
Assume that

and

.
Let

be such that

, and

,
which is possible since

.
Since

contains
the formula

the formula

.
Since

.

Define a structure
\mathbb{C}
on

as follows.
For every
k

and

of

,

(such a

Add the tuple
([y_{1}],\dots,[y_{k}])

if and only if

.
As in the paragraph above, this definition does not depend on the choice of the representatives

or on the choice of

Proving that the definition does not depend on

Suppose now that
y_{1}\sim y_{1}^{\prime}

Let

We prove that

Since

Moreover, the images of

Therefore, the definition of

By iterating this argument for each coordinate, we obtain that

We claim that

\mathbb{B}

Since

(w(p_{1}),\dots,w(p_{k}))=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
.
The quantifier-free type of

It follows that if

\mathbb{D}
into the substructure of

This contradicts the fact that

.

Let

.
For

.
We claim that

is a valid assignment for
\Psi
.
Let

be a constraint from

, where

.
Let

.
Since

contains
\chi(z_{v^{-1}j(1)},\dots,z_{v^{-1}j(r)})
.
It follows that

.
Since

embeds
\mathbb{C}
into

The given reduction can be

,
and if

is the number
of constraints in

, then the number of constraints in

is in

.
Each of the new constraints can be constructed in constant time.

We mention that the reduction is in fact a
first-order reduction

(see
[
1
]
for a definition).
We also note that
Theorem
3

applies to all CSPs that can be
described in SNP (for SNP in connection to CSPs see, e.g.,
[

]
).

4.
New abstract tractability conditions

We first recall basics from universal
algebra that are needed to formulate
the algebraic facts for finite-domain constraint satisfaction that are relevant for the purposes of this paper, collected in Theorem
4
(Subsection
4.1
). We then briefly introduce fundamental concepts
for infinite-domain constraint satisfaction (Subsection
4.2.1
); these concepts will also be needed in the later sections.
Finally, we
state and prove our new tractability conditions (Subsection
4.3
).

4.1.
Finite-Domain CSPs and Universal Algebra

An
algebra
is a structure whose signature contains only function symbols,
whose interpretations are then called the
(fundamental) operations
of the algebra.
Functions that are obtained as compositions of fundamental operations are called the
term operations
.
A substructure of an algebra is referred to as a
subalgebra
.
An
idempotent algebra

{\underline{A}}
is an algebra whose operations are
idempotent
, i.e., they satisfy
f^{\underline{A}}(x,\dots,x)=x
for all
x\in A
.
A
trivial
algebra is an algebra whose operations are projections.
If
{\underline{A}}
is an algebra and
n
is a positive integer,
{\underline{A}}^{n}

is defined as the algebra on
A^{n}
where for each
k
-ary function symbol
f

in the signature of
{\underline{A}}
,
f^{{\underline{A}}^{n}}
is the function

(A^{n})^{k}\to A^{n}
obtained by applying
f^{\underline{A}}
on tuples componentwise.
Given an algebra
{\underline{A}}
, we write
\HSPfin({\underline{A}})
for the class of algebras
that contains an algebra
{\underline{T}}
iff
there is a positive integer
n
, a subalgebra
{\underline{S}}
of
{\underline{A}}^{n}
,
and a surjective homomorphism
{\underline{S}}\to{\underline{T}}
.
The class
\HS({\underline{A}})
is defined similarly, where we only allow
n=1
.

4.1.1.
Clones

A set
\mathscr{C}
of operations over a set
D
is called a
function clone
if for all
k\geq 1

and all
1\leq i\leq k
it contains the
projections

\pi_{i}^{k}\colon(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto x_{i}
,
and if
\mathscr{C}
is closed under composition of operations.
The smallest function clone on
\{0,1\}
is denoted by
\mathscr{P}
.
A typical function clone is the set
\Clo({\underline{A}})
of term operations
of an algebra
{\underline{A}}
, and indeed for every function clone
\mathscr{C}
there exists an algebra
{\underline{A}}
such that
\mathscr{C}=\Clo({\underline{A}})
.
A
clone homomorphism
between two clones

\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
is a map
\xi\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{D}
such that
\xi(\pi_{i}^{k})=\pi_{i}^{k}
and

\xi(f\circ(g_{1},\dots,g_{k}))=\xi(f)\circ(\xi(g_{1}),\dots,\xi(g_{k}))

holds for all
f,g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in\mathscr{C}
.
The
stabilizer
of a clone
\mathscr{C}
by a constant
c
, written
\mathscr{C}_{c}
, is the subclone of
\mathscr{C}

consisting of all the operations
f
such that
f(c,\dots,c)=c
.

4.1.2.
The Finite-Domain Dichotomy

An operation of arity
k\geq 2
is a
weak near-unanimity

[
39
]

if it satisfies the equations

f(y,x,\dots,x)=f(x,y,x,\dots,x)=\dots=f(x,\dots,x,y)

for all
x,y\in A
.
An operation
f
of arity
k\geq 2
is said to be
cyclic
(see
[
4
]
)
if it satisfies
f(\bar{x})=f(\sigma\bar{x})
for every
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
,
where
\sigma
maps
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
to
(x_{2},\dots,x_{k},x_{1})
.
A 6-ary operation
f\colon A^{6}\to A
is
Siggers
(see
[
41
]
)
if it satisfies
f(x,y,x,z,y,z)=f(y,x,z,x,z,y)

for all
x,y,z\in A
.
The following is a combination of several results, old and new.

Theorem 4
.

For finite idempotent algebras
{\underline{A}}
,
1
.-
6
. are equivalent.

(1)

There is no clone homomorphism from

\Clo({\underline{A}})

to
\mathscr{P}
;

(2)

\HSPfin({\underline{A}})
does not contain trivial
2
-element algebras
[
7
]
;

(3)

\HS({\underline{A}})
does not contain trivial
2
-element algebras (Prop. 4.14 from
[
26
]
);

(4)

\Clo({\underline{A}})
contains a weak near-unanimity operation
[
39
]
;

(5)

\Clo({\underline{A}})
contains a cyclic operation
[
4
]
;

(6)

\Clo({\underline{A}})
contains a Siggers operation
[
41
]
.

For not necessarily idempotent finite algebras, this theorem fails in general, but items
4
,
5
,
6
are still equivalent (see
[
5
]
).
Bulatov
[
25
]
and Zhuk
[
42
]
independently proved that these conditions are enough to imply a complexity dichotomy for finite-domain CSPs.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a finite structure with a finite signature, and let
{\underline{A}}
be an algebra such that
\Clo({{\underline{A}}})=\Pol(\mathbb{A})
.
Then CSP
({\mathbb{A}})
is in P if
{\underline{A}}
satisfies item
4
,
5
, or
6
in Theorem
4
,
and
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is NP-complete otherwise.

4.2.
Infinite-Domain CSPs and Topology

The concepts introduced in Section
4.1

are also relevant for infinite-domain CSPs; however,
to study potential tractability conjectures and analogs of Theorem
4
for algebras on infinite domains, we additionally need some model-theoretic and topological definitions that we collected in this subsection.

4.2.1.
Homogeneity

A structure
\mathbb{A}
is said to be
homogeneous
if every isomorphism between finite substructures of
\mathbb{A}
can be extended to an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
.
Examples of homogeneous structures are
(\mathbb{N};=)
and
({\mathbb{Q}};<)
.
Homogeneous structures with finite relational signature and their reducts
are examples of
\omega
-categorical
structures: a structure
\mathbb{A}
is
\omega
-categorical if all countable models of the first-order theory of
\mathbb{A}
are isomorphic. By the theorem of Engeler, Svenonius, and Ryll-Nardzewski (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
),
\omega
-categoricity
of
\mathbb{A}
is equivalent to
oligomorphicity

of
\Aut(\mathbb{A})
: a permutation group on
A
is oligomorphic if for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
the componentwise action on
A^{m}
has finitely many orbits.
It is known that every homogeneous structure in a finite relational signature has
quantifier elimination
, that is,
every first-order formula is equivalent over that structure to a quantifier-free formula. In particular, the first-order reducts and quantifier-free reducts
of a homogeneous structure in a finite relational signature are the same. This allows us to use Theorem
3

also when
\mathbb{A}
is a first-order reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure
\mathbb{B}
.

4.2.2.
The Topology of Pointwise Convergence

A function clone comes naturally equipped with a topology, namely the topology of pointwise convergence where the base set is equipped with the discrete topology. This topology
is characterised by the fact that a sequence
(f_{i})_{i\in\omega}
converges to
f
if, and only if, for every finite subset
S
of
D
there exists
an
i_{0}\in\omega
such that for all
i\geq i_{0}
, we have that
f_{i}
and
f
coincide on
S
.
Given a relational structure
\mathbb{A}
, the set
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
is a function clone
over
A
which is topologically closed in the full clone over
A
(the clone
consisting of all the operations of finite arity over
A
).

Let
f,g
be operations over
D
and let
U
be a set of permutations of
D
.
When the topological closure of

\{\alpha\circ f\circ(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{k})\mid\alpha,\beta_{i}\in U\}
contains
g
,
we say that
f

interpolates
g
modulo
U
.

4.2.3.
Model-Complete Cores

To state an infinite-domain tractability conjecture,
we also need the following concepts.
An
\omega
-categorical structure
\mathbb{A}
is

model-complete
if every self-embedding
of
\mathbb{A}
preserves all first-order formulas,
and it is a
core
if every endomorphism of

\mathbb{A}
is a self-embedding.
It is known that
\mathbb{A}
is a model-complete core if and only if
\Aut(\mathbb{A})
is dense in
\End(\mathbb{A})
; see
[
9
]
. Note that every
\omega
-categorical homogeneous structure is model-complete.

{exa}

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be subsets of a countably infinite set
A
and let
\mathbb{A}
be a reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
such that
\End(\mathbb{A})
is the set of all injections that preserve
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
.
The automorphism group of
\mathbb{A}

contains all the permutations of
A
that preserve the sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
.
Hence, if
e
is an injective function on
A
fixing
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
and
S
is a finite subset of
A
, then there is an automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathbb{A}

such that
\alpha|_{S}=e|_{S}
. Therefore,
e
is
in the topological closure of
\Aut(\mathbb{A})
, and
\Aut(\mathbb{A})
is dense
in
\End(\mathbb{A})
. So
\mathbb{A}
is a model-complete core.

Every
\omega
-categorical structure
is homomorphically equivalent to a model-complete core, which is unique up to isomorphism, and again
\omega
-categorical
[
8
]
.

We can now state the tractability conjecture
for reducts of finitely bounded homogeneous structures from
[
19
]
.

Conjecture 6
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a finite-signature reduct of a finitely bounded homogeneous structure. Then
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is in P if the model-complete core of
\mathbb{A}
does not have an expansion
\mathbb{C}
by finitely many constants so that
\Pol(\mathbb{C})
has a continuous clone homomorphism to
\mathscr{P}
.

If
\mathbb{A}
does
not
satisfy the condition
in this conjecture, then it is known that
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is NP-hard
[
19
]
.

4.2.4.
Siggers operations modulo unary operations

An important question when generalising Theorem
4

to infinite domains is how to replace the last three items involving weak near-unanimity operations, cyclic operations, and Siggers operations.
Given two unary operations
e_{1},e_{2}
,
we say that an operation is
Siggers modulo
e_{1},e_{2}

if for all
x,y,z
in
A
, we have
e_{1}(f(x,y,x,z,y,z))=e_{2}(f(y,x,z,x,z,y))
.

Weak near-unanimity operations modulo
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
and

cyclic operations modulo
e_{1},e_{2}
are defined similarly. A recent breakthrough by Barto and Pinsker
[
6
]
gives the following dichotomy.

Theorem 7

(Theorem 1.4 in
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a clone whose invertible elements form an oligomorphic group that is dense in the unary part of
\mathscr{C}
. Then exactly one of the following is true:

•

there exists a continuous clone homomorphism from
\mathscr{C}_{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}}
to
\mathscr{P}
for some
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
,

•

there exists a Siggers operation in
\mathscr{C}
modulo unary operations of
\mathscr{C}
.

Hence, the tractability conjecture for reducts
\mathbb{A}
of finitely bounded homogeneous structure (Conjecture
6
) has an equivalent formulation using Siggers polymorphisms modulo endomorphisms.

We mention another related recent result which gives an equivalent
formulation of the first item of Theorem
7

if
\mathscr{C}
is additionally the polymorphism clone of a reduct of a homogeneous structures with finite relational signature
[
2
]
.
The clone
\mathscr{C}
satisfies the first item
if and only if
\mathscr{C}
has a uniformly continuous

h1 clone homomorphism
to
{\mathscr{P}}
.
We do not need this fact here,
but we need in Section
5
the notion of h1 clone homomorphisms (introduced in
[
5
]
):
a map
\phi\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{P}
is called an
h1 clone homomorphism
if

\phi(f\circ(\pi^{k}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{k}_{i_{n}}))=\phi(f)\circ(\pi^{k}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{k}_{i_{n}})

holds for every
n
-ary
f\in\mathscr{C}
and all projections
\pi^{k}_{i_{1}},\dots,\pi^{k}_{i_{n}}
.

4.2.5.
Canonical Functions

Canonical functions have been an important tool
for classifying the complexity of classes of infinite-domain
constraint satisfaction problems; for a more detailed
exposition, we refer the reader to
[
18
]
.
Here, we
need canonical functions to formulate our new tractability conditions for reducts of finitely bounded
homogeneous structures.
Let
f\colon A^{k}\to A
, and let
G
be a permutation group on
A
.
We say that
f
is
G
-canonical

if for all
m\in\mathbb{N},\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in G
and
m
-tuples
\bar{a}_{1},\dots,\bar{a}_{k}
, there exists

\beta\in G
such that
\beta f(\alpha_{1}\bar{a}_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\bar{a}_{k})=f(\bar{a}_{1},\dots,\bar{a}_{k})
.
Equivalently, this means that
f
induces an operation
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)
on orbits of
m
-tuples under
G
,
by defining
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)(O_{1},\dots,O_{k})
as the orbit of
f(\bar{a}_{1},\dots,\bar{a}_{k})

where
\bar{a}_{i}
is any
m
-tuple in
O_{i}
.
It is clear from the definition that the functions in
G
are
G
-canonical,
and so are the projections on
A
.
We can also check that the composition of
G
-canonical functions is again
G
-canonical.

If
\mathscr{C}
is a clone whose operations are all canonical with respect to a group
G
then we say that

\mathscr{C}
is canonical with respect to
G
.
If
\mathscr{D}
is a function clone that contains
G
, the set
\mathscr{C}
of operations of
\mathscr{D}
that are
G
-canonical
is again a function clone that contains
G
. We also call the clone
\mathscr{C}
the
canonical subclone of
\mathscr{D}
with respect to
G
.

For every
m\in\mathbb{N}
the set that consists of the operations
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)
for
f\in\mathscr{C}
is a function clone

\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}
on the set of orbits of
m
-tuples. The application
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}

is easily seen to be a continuous clone homomorphism.

4.3.
Abstract Tractability Conditions

It is known that the complexity of
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
for
\omega
-categorical structures
\mathbb{A}
only
depends on the properties of the polymorphism clone of
\mathbb{A}
.
These properties can be of different nature.
Abstract
properties are properties that can be expressed
using only the composition symbol and quantification over the operations in the clone, e.g., “there exists an operation
f
, such that
f\circ(\pi_{2}^{2},\pi_{1}^{2})=f
.”

Topological
properties are properties that can also refer to
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
as a topological object, e.g.,
“there exists a
continuous
clone homomorphism
\Pol(\mathbb{A})\to\mathscr{P}
.”
Finally,
concrete properties
are properties that refer to certain concrete operations in the polymorphism clone.
This distinction reflects the distinction between abstract clones, topological clones, and function clones.

It was shown that for an
\omega
-categorical structure
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
, the complexity of
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
only depends on topological properties of
\Pol(\mathbb{A})

[
17
]
.
However, most of the known conditions that imply that
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is in P are
concrete
conditions.
One notable exception is tractability from
quasi near unanimity polymorphisms
,
that is, polymorphisms that satisfy the identity

\displaystyle f(y,x,\dots,x)

\displaystyle=f(x,y,x,\dots,x)=\cdots

\displaystyle=f(x,\dots,x,y)=f(x,\dots,x).

If
\mathbb{A}
has a quasi near unanimity polymorphism
then
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is in P
[
10
]
.
This tractability condition is an abstract condition (it can be rewritten using only
f
, the projection operations
\pi_{1}^{2},\pi_{2}^{2}
, and the composition symbol).
The tractability conditions that we are able to lift from the finite are all of the abstract type.

We first need to connect
the canonical polymorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
with the polymorphism clone of the associated type structure
T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A})
from Section
3
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a reduct of a homogeneous relational structure
\mathbb{B}
and let
\mathscr{C}
be the polymorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
that are canonical with respect to
\Aut(\mathbb{B})
.
For all
m\geq 1
, we have
\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}\subseteq\Pol(T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A}))
.

Proof 4.1
.

We have to show that
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)\in\Pol(T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A}))
for every
f\in\mathscr{C}
.
Let
k
be the arity of
f
.
Let
\chi(z_{1},\dots,z_{r})
be a qf-definition
of a relation of
\mathbb{A}
.
Let
i\colon[r]\to[m]

and let
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
be types in the relation

\langle\chi(z_{i(1)},\dots,z_{i(r)})\rangle
of

T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A})

Let
\bar{a}^{1},\dots,\bar{a}^{k}
be
m
-tuples whose types are
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
respectively.
Since
\mathbb{B}
is homogeneous the orbits of
m
-tuples under
\Aut(\mathbb{B})

and the qf-types of
\mathbb{B}
are in one-to-one correspondence and
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)
can be seen as an operation on
m

We have that

is the type of

in
\mathbb{B}
.
Since

preserves the relation defined by
\chi(z_{i(1)},\dots,z_{i(r)})

it follows that

satisfies
\chi(z_{i(1)},\dots,z_{i(r)})

which means that
\chi(z_{i(1)},\dots,z_{i(r)})
is contained in the type of this tuple. Therefore,
\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)

preserves the relations of
T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A})
of the first kind.

We now prove that

preserves the relations of the second kind in
T_{\mathbb{B},m}(\mathbb{A})
.
Indeed, let

be pairs of types in
\mathrm{Comp}_{i,j}
.
Let
(\bar{a}^{1},\bar{b}^{1}),\dots,(\bar{a}^{k},\bar{b}^{k})
be pairs of

-tuples such that
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{a}^{l})=p_{l}
and
\mathrm{tp}(\bar{b}^{l})=q_{l}
for all

As noted above, the definition of

implies that the tuples

and

have the same type in

for all

.
Since

is canonical, we have that
(f(a^{1}_{i(1)},\dots,a^{k}_{i(1)}),\dots,f(a^{1}_{i(r)},\dots,a^{k}_{i(r)}))

has the same type as
(f(b^{1}_{j(1)},\dots,b^{k}_{j(1)}),\dots,f(b^{1}_{j(r)},\dots,b^{k}_{j(r)}))

.
This implies that

\mathrm{Comp}_{i,j}\big(\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)(p_{1},\dots,p_{k}),\xi^{\mathrm{typ}}_{m}(f)(q_{1},\dots,q_{k})\big)

holds in

,
which concludes the proof.

Suppose that
\mathbb{B}
is homogeneous in a finite relational language, and that

is a reduct of
\mathbb{B}
.
Suppose moreover that every polymorphism of
\mathbb{A}
is canonical with respect to

.
The lemma above implies that

]
. This proves the following corollary.

.

\mathbb{A}

.

The “if” direction is clear. In the other direction, the assumption that

5.
Extending Clone Homomorphisms

Theorem
14
and Corollary
15
in the previous section are of the form “if the canonical polymorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
satisfy
some nontrivial equations, then
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is in P.” We can reformulate this as follows.
Let
\mathscr{C}\subseteq\Pol(\mathbb{A})
be the clone of polymorphisms of
\mathbb{A}
that are canonical (with respect to the automorphism group of some homogeneous finitely bounded structure
\mathbb{B}
).
Corollary
15
is then equivalent to the statement: if there is no continuous clone homomorphism
\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{P}
, then
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is in P.
On the other hand, we know that the existence of a uniformly continuous h1 homomorphism
\Pol(\mathbb{A})\to\mathscr{P}
implies that
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is NP-complete
[
5
]
.
This naturally raises the question as to when the existence of a continuous clone homomorphism
\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{P}
implies the existence
of a uniformly continuous h1 homomorphism
\Pol(\mathbb{A})\to\mathscr{P}
.

We focus on the case where every operation
f\in\Pol(\mathbb{A})
interpolates modulo
\Aut(\mathbb{B})
an operation that is canonical with respect to
\Aut(\mathbb{B})
.
We call this the
canonisation property
.
In this setting, there is a natural candidate for extending a clone homomorphism
\xi\colon\mathscr{C}\to\mathscr{P}
to
\phi\colon\Pol(\mathbb{A})\to\mathscr{P}
. Indeed,
for every
f\in\Pol(\mathbb{A})
, define
\phi(f)
to be
\xi(g)
, where
g
is any canonical function in
\overline{\Aut(\mathbb{B})f\Aut(\mathbb{B})}
.
We prove that when this definition does not depend on the choice of
g
, then
\phi
is indeed a uniformly continuous h1 homomorphism.
We also give a property of
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
–the
mashup property
– that implies that the extension
\phi
is well-defined.

5.1.
Mashups

We start with the central definition of this section.

{defi}

Let
g,h\colon B^{k}\to B
, let
1\leq\ell\leq k
, and let
r,s\in B
.
An operation
\omega
is an
\ell
-mashup of
g
and
h
over
\{r,s\}
if the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\omega(r,\dots,r,s,r,\dots,r)=g(r,\dots,r,s,r,\dots,r),

\displaystyle\omega(s,\dots,s,r,s,\dots,s)=h(s,\dots,s,r,s,\dots,s),

where the different entry in the arguments above is the
\ell
-th entry. In case
\ell=1
, we simply write
mashup
.

In the following, we encourage the reader to work with the case
k=2
in mind.
In this case, the portion of the Cayley table of
\omega
containing
\{r,s\}
looks like the tables in Figure
1
.

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr w&r&s\\
\hline\cr r&*&g(r,s)\\
s&h(s,r)&*\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|}\hline\cr w&r&s\\
\hline\cr r&*&h(r,s)\\
s&g(s,r)&*\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Figure 1.
The possible Cayley tables of an
\ell
-mashup of
g
and
h
over
\{r,s\}
, for
\ell\in\{1,2\}
. The symbol
*
indicates that the value can be anything.

The motivation for this definition is the following. If
g
and
h
are assumed to be projections, and we know that for all
\ell

there exists an
\ell
-mashup of
g
and
h
, then
g
and
h
must be the same projection. We now show that this remains true
when
g,h
are operations of an algebra
{\underline{B}}
such that
\HS({\underline{B}})
contains a trivial algebra.

Lemma 16
.

Let
{\underline{B}}
be an algebra, and let
g^{\underline{B}},h^{\underline{B}}
be operations of
{\underline{B}}
of arity
k
.
Suppose that for all
\ell\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and all distinct elements
r,s\in B
,
there exists an operation of
{\underline{B}}
that is an
\ell
-mashup of
g^{\underline{B}}
and
h^{\underline{B}}
over
\{r,s\}
.
Then for every trivial algebra
{\underline{T}}
in
\HS({\underline{B}})
, we have
g^{{\underline{T}}}=h^{{\underline{T}}}
.

Proof 5.1
.

Let
{\underline{S}}
be a subalgebra of
{\underline{B}}
and
f
be a surjective homomorphism
{\underline{S}}\to{\underline{T}}
.
Suppose that
g^{{\underline{T}}}
is the
\ell
-th projection.
Let
r,s
be two elements of
{\underline{S}}
which are mapped by
f
to different elements of
{\underline{T}}
,
and let
\omega^{{\underline{B}}}
be an
\ell
-mashup of
g^{\underline{B}}
and
h^{\underline{B}}
over
\{r,s\}
.
By the assumption that
g^{{\underline{T}}}
is the
\ell
-th projection, we have

g^{{\underline{T}}}(f(r),\dots,f(r),f(s),f(r),\dots,f(r))=f(s),

so that

\displaystyle\omega^{{\underline{T}}}(f(r),\dots,f(r),f(s),f(r),\dots,f(r))

\displaystyle=f(\omega^{\underline{B}}(r,\dots,r,s,r,\dots,r))

\displaystyle=f(g^{\underline{B}}(r,\dots,r,s,r,\dots,r))

\displaystyle=g^{{\underline{T}}}(f(r),\dots,f(r),f(s),f(r),\dots,f(r))

\displaystyle=f(s)

which implies that
\omega^{{\underline{T}}}
is the
\ell
-th projection, by the fact that
f(s)\neq f(r)
.
Whence,

, there is no cyclic operation in
\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{typ}}_{2}
. By Proposition
27
, either there
exists a continuous clone homomorphism
\mathscr{D}_{U_{i}}\to\mathscr{P}
for some
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, or there is a clone homomorphism
\mathscr{C}^{\mathrm{typ}}_{1}\to\mathscr{P}
.
In the first case we are done: we obtain by composing with
\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{D}_{U_{i}}
a continuous clone homomorphism
\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{P}
, so
2
does not hold.
Suppose we are in the second case. Proposition
26
implies that
\Aut(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
has the canonisation property.
Note that this group is isomorphic as permutation group to the intransitive action of
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Sym(U_{i})
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}
.
By Corollary
21
,
\Aut(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
has the mashup property.
Corollary
18
implies that
there exist elements
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in A
and a continuous clone homomorphism from
\mathscr{D}_{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}}
to
\mathscr{P}
.
This shows that
2
does not hold in this case either, and concludes the proof of
\ref{itm:no-h1}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:no-homo}
.

6.2.
The General Case

In this section we conclude the proof of the dichotomy theorem for reducts
\mathbb{A}
of unary structures
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.
The previous section treated the special case where
\End(\mathbb{A})
consists exactly of the injective operations preserving
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
.
In the following, we reduce the general case to this situation.

The first step of the strategy for this is to show that we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{A}
is a model-complete core.
Since reducts of unary structures are
\omega
-categorical, and since every
\omega
-categorical structure
has a model-complete core,
it suffices to prove that the model-complete core of a reduct of a unary structure
is again a reduct of a unary structure (Lemma
28
below).
The second step is to show that by adding constants in a suitable way, we obtain
a reduct of a unary structure which satisfies the hypothesis of the previous section (Proposition
29
).

We can assume that the model-complete core
\mathbb{B}
of
\mathbb{A}

is a substructure of
\mathbb{A}
.
Indeed, if
f\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{B}
and
g\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{A}
are homomorphisms,
then
f\circ g
is an endomorphism of
\mathbb{B}
, and is therefore an embedding

\mathbb{B}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{B}
. This implies that
g
is an embedding of
\mathbb{B}
into
\mathbb{A}
.
We can then replace
\mathbb{B}
by the substructure of
\mathbb{A}
induced by
g(\mathbb{B})
.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a reduct of a unary structure, and let
\mathbb{B}
be the model-complete core of
\mathbb{A}
.
Then
\mathbb{B}
is a reduct of a unary structure.

Proof 6.5
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.
Suppose that
\mathbb{B}
is a substructure of
\mathbb{A}
.
Let
h
be a homomorphism from
\mathbb{A}
to
\mathbb{B}
.
We show that
\mathbb{B}
is a reduct of
(B;U_{1}\cap B,\dots,U_{n}\cap B)
.
To this end, we prove that every permutation of
B
preserving the sets
U_{1}\cap B,\dots,U_{n}\cap B
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{B}
.
Let
\beta
be such a permutation. Then
\beta
can be extended by the identity to a permutation
\alpha

of
A
which preserves
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
, and therefore
\alpha
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
.
Thus,
h\circ\beta=h\circ\alpha|_{B}\colon\mathbb{B}\to\mathbb{B}
is an endomorphism of
\mathbb{B}
,
and so an embedding since
\mathbb{B}
is a model-complete core.
This implies that
\beta
is an embedding, i.e., it is an automorphism of
\mathbb{B}
.
Note that
(B;U_{1}\cap B,\dots,U_{n}\cap B)
is
\omega
-categorical.
It is a known corollary of the Ryll-Nardzewski theorem
[
30
]

that if a structure
\mathbb{C}
is
\omega
-categorical
and
\Aut(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\Aut(\mathbb{B})
then
\mathbb{B}
is a reduct of
\mathbb{C}
.
It follows that
\mathbb{B}
is a first-order
reduct of
(B;U_{1}\cap B,\dots,U_{1}\cap B)
.

It can be the case that
\End(\mathbb{A})

contains more operations than the injections preserving
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
even when
\mathbb{A}
is a reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
which is a model-complete core.
An example is
(A;E,{\neq})
where
A=U_{1}\uplus U_{2}
and
E=\{(x,y)\in A^{2}\mid x\in U_{1}\Leftrightarrow y\in U_{2}\}
.
However, for every such reduct there are finitely many constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in A

such that the
(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
satisfies the condition of Theorem
23
.

Proposition 29
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a reduct of a unary structure that is a model-complete core.
There exist elements
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}\in A
and a stabilised partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{m}\}
of
A
such that
(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
is a reduct
of the unary structure
(A;V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
and such that the endomorphisms of
(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})

are precisely the injective functions preserving
V_{1},\dots,V_{m}
.

Proof 6.6
.

Let
\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
be a stabilised partition of
A
where
n
is minimal with the property that
\mathbb{A}
is a reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.
Up to a permutation of the blocks, we can assume that
U_{1},\dots,U_{r}
are the finite blocks of the partition.
For every
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, let
c_{i}\in U_{i}
. We claim that

\Aut(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})=\Aut(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n},c_{1},\dots,c_{n}).

If
r=n
, there is nothing to prove, because of the assumption that the sets
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are either singletons are infinite.
Therefore, if
r=n
, we have
\Aut(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})=\Aut(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.

We prove that
\Aut(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
preserves the binary relation

E:=\{(x,y)\in A^{2}\mid\forall i\in\{r+1,\dots,n\},x\in U_{i}\Leftrightarrow y\in U_{i}\}.

Let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
.
For
i,j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
, define
V_{ij}(\alpha)
to be the set of elements of
U_{i}
that are mapped to
U_{j}
under
\alpha
.

Claim 0:
for every
i\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
and every automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathbb{A}
, there exists a
j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
such that
V_{ji}(\alpha)
is infinite.

Proof 6.7
.

Since
\alpha
is a bijection, every element of
U_{i}
has a preimage under
\alpha
. Since there are only finitely many sets in the partition,
one of the sets
U_{j}
contains infinitely many of those preimages, i.e.,
V_{ji}(\alpha)
is infinite.

Claim 1:
for every
i\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
and every automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathbb{A}
, the set
V_{ii}(\alpha)
is either finite or
U_{i}
.

Proof 6.8
.

Let
\alpha
be an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
, and suppose that
\emptyset\neq V_{ii}(\alpha)\neq U_{i}
.
Since
V_{ii}(\alpha)\neq U_{i}
, there exists a
j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
such that
V_{ij}(\alpha)\neq\emptyset
, which is equivalent to say that
V_{ji}(\alpha^{-1})\neq\emptyset
.
Suppose for contradiction that
V_{ii}(\alpha)
is infinite. Equivalently,
V_{ii}(\alpha^{-1})
is infinite.
We claim that for every finite subset
S
of
A
, there exists an automorphism
\alpha^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{A}
such that
\alpha^{\prime}(S)\cap U_{j}=\emptyset
.
This is clear: let
\beta
be an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
that maps
S\cap U_{i}
to
V_{ii}(\alpha^{-1})
(which is possible since
V_{ii}(\alpha^{-1})
is infinite)
and one element of
S\cap U_{j}
to
V_{ji}(\alpha^{-1})
.
The automorphism
\alpha^{\prime}_{1}:=\alpha^{-1}\circ\beta
maps one point from
S\cap U_{j}
to
U_{i}
, and maps all the elements of
S\cap U_{i}
to
U_{i}
.
Possibly, some elements in
S\cap U_{k}
for
k\not\in\{i,j\}
are mapped by
\alpha^{\prime}_{1}
to
U_{j}
.
We repeat this procedure and obtain automorphisms
\alpha^{\prime}_{2},\dots,\alpha^{\prime}_{m}
with
m\leq|S|
, until
\alpha^{\prime}_{m}(S)\cap U_{j}
is empty.
Using a standard compactness argument, we obtain an operation
e\in\overline{\Aut(\mathbb{A})}
whose image does not intersect
U_{j}
.
This is a contradiction to the minimality of the partition
\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
: the structures
e(\mathbb{A})
and
\mathbb{A}
are isomorphic,
and the relations of
e(\mathbb{A})
are definable in
(A\setminus U_{j};U_{1},\dots,U_{j-1},U_{j+1},\dots,U_{n})
.
Therefore
V_{ii}(\alpha)
is finite.

Claim 2:
for every
i\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
and every automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathbb{A}
, the set
V_{ii}(\alpha)
is either empty or
U_{i}
.

Proof 6.9
.

Suppose that for some
\alpha\in\Aut(\mathbb{A})
, the set
V_{ii}(\alpha)
is not equal to
U_{i}
and is not empty.
We prove that for every
k\geq 1
, there exists an automorphism
\alpha_{k}
of
\mathbb{A}
such that
|V_{ii}(\alpha_{k})|\geq k
and such that
\alpha_{k}
does not preserve
U_{i}
.
Let
k\geq 1
.
By Claim 0, there exists a
j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
such that
V_{ji}(\alpha)
is infinite and by Claim 1, it must be the case that
j\neq i
.
Note that
V_{ij}(\alpha^{-1})
is infinite, and that
V_{ii}(\alpha^{-1})
is not empty.
Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
be pairwise distinct elements in
V_{ij}(\alpha^{-1})
, and let
y\in V_{ii}(\alpha^{-1})
.
Let
z
be an element of
U_{i}
such that
\alpha(z)\not\in U_{i}
, which exists since
V_{ii}(\alpha)\neq U_{i}
.
Let
\beta
be an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
that maps
\alpha^{-1}(y)
to
z
and which leaves
\alpha^{-1}(x_{1}),\dots,\alpha^{-1}(x_{k})
fixed.
Then
\alpha\circ\beta\circ\alpha^{-1}
is an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
such that
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in V_{ii}(\alpha\circ\beta\circ\alpha^{-1})

and such that
(\alpha\circ\beta\circ\alpha^{-1})(y)\not\in U_{i}
.

For each
k\geq 1
, there exists by Claim 0 a
j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
such that
V_{ji}(\alpha_{k})
is infinite.
Since
\alpha_{k}
does not preserve
U_{i}
by assumption,
V_{ii}(\alpha_{k})\neq U_{i}
.
By Claim 1,
V_{ii}(\alpha_{k})
has to be finite, so
j
is distinct from
i
.
By the pigeonhole principle, there is a
j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
distinct from
i
such that
V_{ji}(\alpha_{k})
is infinite
for infinitely many
k
.
Therefore, using another argument one can show that there is an endomorphism of
\mathbb{A}
in

\overline{\langle\Aut(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})\cup\{\alpha_{k}:k\geq 1\}\rangle}

whose image does not intersect
U_{j}
, which is a contradiction to the minimality of the partition
\{U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\}
.
Hence,
V_{ii}(\alpha)
is either empty or
U_{i}
.

Claim 3:
for every
i\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
and every automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathbb{A}
, there is exactly one
j\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
such that
V_{ij}(\alpha)
is nonempty.

Proof 6.10
.

Suppose that
j,j^{\prime}\in\{r+1,\dots,n\}
are distinct and that
V_{ij}(\alpha)
and
V_{ij^{\prime}}(\alpha)
are both nonempty,
say that
\alpha(x)\in U_{j}
and
\alpha(y)\in U_{j^{\prime}}
.
Since
V_{jj}(\alpha^{-1})
is not
U_{j}
, it must be empty by Claim 2. Thus, there exists a
k
distinct from
j
such that
V_{jk}(\alpha^{-1})
is infinite,
which gives the existence of a
z\in U_{j}
distinct from
\alpha(x)
such that
\alpha^{-1}(z)\in U_{k}
.
Let
\beta
be an automorphism of
\mathbb{A}
that maps
x
to
y
, and leaves
\alpha^{-1}(z)
fixed.
Then the map
\alpha\circ\beta\circ\alpha^{-1}
maps
\alpha(x)\in U_{j}
to
\alpha(y)\in U_{j^{\prime}}
, and maps
z\in U_{j}
to itself.
Therefore, we have that
V_{jj}(\alpha\circ\beta\circ\alpha^{-1})
is neither empty nor equal to
U_{j}
, a contradiction to the second claim.

Therefore, the relation
E
is preserved by
\Aut(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
.
This implies that each of
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
is preserved by
\Aut(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
.
We obtain that
(\mathbb{A},c_{1},\dots,c_{n})
is a reduct of
(A;U_{1}\setminus\{c_{1}\},\dots,U_{n}\setminus\{c_{n}\},\{c_{1}\},\dots,\{c_{n}\})

whose endomorphisms are precisely the injective functions that preserve this stabilised partition.

Corollary 30
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a reduct of a unary structure. Then there
exists an expansion
\mathbb{C}
of the model-complete core of
\mathbb{A}

by finitely many constants
such that
\mathscr{D}:=\Pol(\mathbb{C})

satisfies either 1. or 2.:

(1)

there is a continuous clone homomorphism
\mathscr{D}\to\mathscr{P}
;

(2)

\mathscr{D}
contains a cyclic
(equivalently: a Siggers,
or a weak near-unanimity) operation
f

modulo unary operations of
\mathscr{C}
; moreover,
f
is
canonical with respect to
\Aut(\mathbb{C})
.

Proof 6.11
.

Let
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
be a partition of
A
such that
\mathbb{A}
is a reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.
If the model-complete core
\mathbb{B}
of
\mathbb{A}
is finite, then we can expand by a constant for each element of
\mathbb{B}
,
and the statement follows from Theorem
4
.
Otherwise, for some stabilised partition
\{V_{1},\dots,V_{m}\}
of
B
the structure
\mathbb{B}
is a reduct of
(B;V_{1},\dots,V_{m})
, by Lemma
28
.
Then by Proposition
29
, there are finitely many constants
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}
such that
(\mathbb{B},c_{1},\dots,c_{m})
satisfies the
hypothesis of Theorem
23
, and the statement follows directly from Theorem
23
.

We are now ready to give a proof of Theorem
22
, which implies
Theorem
1
from the introduction.

Theorem
22
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a finite-signature reduct of a unary structure.
Then
\Csp(\mathbb{A})
is in P if the model-complete core
\mathbb{B}

of
\mathbb{A}
has a Siggers polymorphism modulo endomorphisms of
\mathbb{B}
,
and is NP-complete otherwise.

Proof 6.12
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a finite-signature reduct of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.
Let
\mathbb{B}
be the model-complete core of
\mathbb{A}
and let
\mathbb{C}
be the expansion of
\mathbb{B}
by finitely many constants given by Corollary
30
.
Since
\mathbb{B}
is a model-complete core, the set of automorphisms of
\mathbb{B}
is dense in the set of endomorphisms.
As in the proof of
\ref{itm:pseudo-cyclic}\Rightarrow\ref{itm:no-h1}
in Theorem
23
, we can use this fact to prove that

\mathbb{C}
has a Siggers polymorphism modulo endomorphisms if, and only if,
\mathbb{B}
has such a polymorphism.

•

If
\mathbb{C}
has such a polymorphism, it has a canonical one, by Corollary
30
.
Let
m\geq 3
be greater than the arity of any relation of
\mathbb{C}
.
Then
T_{\mathbb{C},m}(\mathbb{C})
has a Siggers polymorphism, by Lemma
9
.
The results from
[
25
,
42
]
imply that the CSP of
T_{\mathbb{C},m}(\mathbb{C})
is in P.
It follows from Theorem
3
that
\Csp(\mathbb{C})

is in P, too.

•

If
\mathbb{C}
does not have a Siggers polymorphism modulo endomorphisms, then Corollary
30

gives a continuous clone homomorphism from
\Pol(\mathbb{C})
to
\mathscr{P}
.
By Theorem 1 in
[
17
]
, there exists a polynomial-time reduction
from, say, 3-SAT to
\Csp(\mathbb{C})
. Therefore,
\Csp(\mathbb{C})
is NP-complete.
\lozenge

We mention that using the results from
[
20
]
,
it can be shown that the condition in Theorem
22
is decidable:
given subsets
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
of
\mathbb{A}
(given by the sizes of the sets in the boolean algebra they generate), it is easily seen that one can compute
a finite set of bounds for the age of
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
.
Given first-order formulas that define the relations of
\mathbb{A}
over
(A;U_{1},\dots,U_{n})
, it is also possible to compute the model-complete core
\mathbb{B}
of
\mathbb{A}
.
Our results then imply that
\mathbb{B}
has a Siggers polymorphism modulo endomorphisms if, and only if, it has a canonical one.
Testing the existence of a canonical function is then decidable, using the results from
[
20
]
.

7.
Future Work

We believe that
the statement of Corollary
30
also holds for all CSPs expressible in the logic MMSNP introduced by Feder and Vardi
[
28
]
(MMSNP is a fragment of existential second-order logic).
Since every MMSNP sentence is equivalent to a finite union of CSPs, this would give another proof that MMSNP has a complexity dichotomy if and only if finite-domain CSPs have a dichotomy, a result due to Feder and Vardi
[
28
,
38
]
. The previous proof requires intricate constructions of expander structures
[
38
]
and it would be interesting to by-pass this.

Another exciting open problem is to characterise those MMSNP sentences
that are equivalent to Datalog programs. Such a characterisation is known
for finite-domain CSPs
[
3
]
. However, this result
does not immediately yield the answer to the question for MMSNP since
it is not clear that the reduction of Feder and Vardi
[
28
,
38
]
preserves
Datalog solvability. On the other hand, MMSNP sentences that
are equivalent to first-order sentences have been characterised recently
[
29
]
.
We conjecture that a CSP in MMSNP is equivalent to a Datalog program
if the model-complete core template
\mathbb{A}
of the CSP satisfies the tractability
condition from Theorem
14
, in which case the MMSNP problem can be solved by Datalog,
and that otherwise there is an h1 homomorphism from
\Pol(\mathbb{A})
to
the polymorphism clone of a module, in which case CSP
(\mathbb{A})
cannot be solved
by a Datalog program
[
1
]
.
