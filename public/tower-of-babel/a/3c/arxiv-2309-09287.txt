Modeling Volatility of Disaster-Affected Populations: A Non-Homogeneous Geometric-Skew Brownian Motion Approach
Giacomo Ascione, Michele Bufalo, Giuseppe Orlando

Abstract
This paper delves into the impact of natural disasters on affected populations and underscores the imperative of reducing disaster-related fatalities through proactive strategies. On average, approximately 45,000 individuals succumb annually to natural disasters amid a surge in economic losses. The paper explores catastrophe models for loss projection, emphasizes the necessity of evaluating volatility in disaster risk, and introduces an innovative model that integrates historical data, addresses data skewness, and accommodates temporal dependencies to forecast shifts in mortality. To this end, we introduce a time-varying skew Brownian motion model, for which we provide proof of the solution’s existence and uniqueness. In this model, parameters change over time, and past occurrences are integrated via volatility.

Introduction

This paper deals with total people affected by natural disasters because, as mentioned by
[
24
]
, while preventing infrequent, high-impact events might be difficult, reducing overall disaster-related deaths is achievable through early prediction, resilient infrastructure, emergency preparedness, and response systems. Particularly vulnerable are individuals with low incomes; improving living standards and response mechanisms in these areas will be crucial in preventing natural disaster-related deaths in the future.
The death toll from natural disasters can vary significantly from year to year, with some years having few deaths while others experience major disasters causing numerous fatalities. On average over the past decade, around 45,000 people worldwide die annually due to natural disasters, making up about 0.1% of global deaths. In some years, deaths can be quite low, even as low as 0.01% of total fatalities. However, major events like famines, earthquakes, and tsunamis have caused the death toll to exceed 200,000 in certain years, constituting over 0.4% of deaths
[
24
]
.

Adjusted for inflation, economic losses from natural disasters have increased over recent decades, with the number of significant loss-causing events tripling since the 1980s. Notable examples include the Northridge earthquake (1994), Kobe earthquake (1995), Asian tsunami (2004), Hurricane Katrina (2005), Japan earthquake and tsunami (2011), and Hurricane Harvey (2017)
[
3
]
.
On the contrary, while in the early to mid-20th century annual deaths from disasters often exceeded a million, in recent decades have seen a substantial decline, with most years witnessing fewer than 20,000 deaths and even fewer in the last decade, even during high-impact events. This decline is remarkable, considering population growth
[
24
]
, and it is explained by the fact that economic development functions as implicit insurance against shocks from natural events
[
16
]
.

Catastrophe models employ geographic information systems (GIS) to predict potential losses caused by natural disasters by simulating hypothetical hazard characteristics at specific locations
[
11
]
. For instance, flood hazard maps depict flood-prone areas, inundation depths, and flow velocities. These characteristics help calculate damage to exposed property based on vulnerability assumptions. These models estimate damage across various intensities and probabilities, yielding annual expected damage. While focused on property damage estimation, catastrophe models also project impacts on populations and potential casualties resulting from specific natural disasters
[
15
]
. This is because population and economic growth remain key drivers of these losses
[
3
]
.

With that said, assessing volatility is crucial from both a risk management and mitigation perspective.
Embracing a risk-averse standpoint and incorporating the volatility linked to disaster risk carries significant implications for evaluating core projects and risk management strategies. Considering natural disaster volatility is vital for evaluating both secondary risk management projects and primary investments
[
19
]
. Nations facing high natural hazard exposure and limited coping capacities should carefully weigh disaster risk and volatility in project decisions. Accounting for outcome volatility increases the appeal of risk transfer measures, especially for risk-averse situations in fact, while cost-efficiency through Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) is crucial, it shouldn’t be the sole criterion. CBA aids efficient fund allocation for more resilient development and should be integral to decision-making for a prevention-focused culture, potentially yielding substantial intangible benefits
[
19
]
.

After discussing why we focus on the affected people and their volatility, the next step involves the modeling. Various stochastic models have been developed to understand sudden shifts in mortality rates (often to price catastrophic bonds). These models differ in how they represent mortality jumps’ characteristics. For instance, Cox et al.
[
9
]
combined geometric Brownian motion and compound Poisson processes to model age-adjusted rates, while Chen and Cox
[
6
]
used a normal distribution for jump severity. Chen and Cummins
[
7
]
integrated two types of jumps, and Deng et al.
[
12
]
explored a double-exponential jump process. Liu and Li
[
21
]
focused on the age-related impact of mortality jumps.

These models assume either annual mortality jumps or utilize a Poisson process for jump frequencies. However, due to the rarity and significance of such events, predicting the timing and frequency of future catastrophic events, and hence mortality jumps, remains uncertain
[
6
]
. Historical data, though, can offer insights despite the Poisson process’s limitation stemming from the memoryless nature of the exponential distribution. For the reasons mentioned above, we introduce a time-inhomogeneous skew Brownian motion model in which parameters vary with time, and past occurrences are incorporated through volatility. This design allows the model to account for data skewness. Furthermore, we provide the proof of the existence and uniqueness of the solution.

This article is divided in the following parts. Section
2
describes the data, offers visualizations, and presents the statistical characteristics. Section
3
introduces the time-inhomogeneous geometric skew Brownian motion model in terms of a stochastic differential equation and offers proof of the existence and uniqueness of the solution. Section
4
displays the empirical results, while the final Section
5
provides the conclusion.

3
Stochastic modeling

Throughout the paper, we will make use of the following notation. We denote
{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+}:=[0,+\infty)
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{+}:=(0,+\infty)
and by
C({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};I)
the space of continuous functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+}\to I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};I)
will be the space of Borel-measurable functions
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+}\to I\subset{\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+}):=\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};{\mathbb{R}})
. Since the notation
L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})
is usually adopted to denote Lebesgue-measurable functions that are locally bounded, we underline the requirement of Borel-measurability by writing
f\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})\cap L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})
. The same will hold for all the Lebesgue spaces. By
\mathbf{1}_{I}
we denote the indicator function of
I\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
. We fix a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,{\sf P})
supporting all the involved processes and random variables. By
W:=\{W_{t},\ t\geq 0\}
we denote a standard Brownian motion on it. For any continuous semimartingale
X=\{X_{t},\ t\geq 0\}
we denote by
[X,X]_{t}
the quadratic variation process.

3.1
The time-inhomogeneous skew Brownian motion and related stochastic differential equations

We recall here the definition of time-inhomogeneous skew Brownian motion.

Definition 1
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};[0,1])
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. A time-inhomogeneous skew Brownian motion (starting from
x
, with shape parameter
\alpha
) is a semimartingale
X^{\alpha}
satisfying the following stochastic differential equation (SDE)

X^{\alpha}_{t}=x+W_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}(2\alpha(s)-1)dL_{s}(X^{\alpha})

(1)

where
L(X^{\alpha}):=\{L_{t}(X^{\alpha}),\ t\geq 0\}
is the symmetric local time in
0
of
X^{\alpha}
, i.e.

L_{t}(X^{\alpha}):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}(X^{\alpha}_{s})d[X^{\alpha},X^{\alpha}]_{s}

In
[
25
]
, the author proved pathwise uniqueness of any solution of

{}^{+}X^{\alpha}_{t}=x+W_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}(2\alpha(s)-1)dL^{+}_{s}({}^{+}X^{\alpha})

(2)

where
L^{+}_{s}({}^{+}X^{\alpha})
is the right local time in
0
of
{}^{+}X^{\alpha}
, i.e.

L^{+}_{t}({}^{+}X^{\alpha}):=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbf{1}_{[0,\varepsilon]}({}^{+}X^{\alpha}_{s})d[{}^{+}X^{\alpha},{}^{+}X^{\alpha}]_{s}

under the assumption
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}\left({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};\left(-\infty,\frac{3}{4}\right]\right)
(see also
[
23
, Exercise VI.2.24]
for further details). However, as observed in
[
13
, Remark 2.3]
(see also
[
4
, Therorem 1.1]
), one has, with the same strategy, pathwise uniqueness of the solution of (
1
) for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};[0,1])
. The actual existence and uniqueness result for strong solutions of (
1
) is eventually shown in
[
13
, Theorem 2.13]
. Recall that a further characterization of local time has been given in
[
17
, Theorem 22.1]
, which leads to the a.e. inequality
L_{t}(X^{\alpha})\leq t
.

Remark 3.1
.

In the special case
x=0
and
\alpha(s)\equiv\alpha\in[0,1]
, we get the Itô-McKean skew Brownian motion with shape parameter
\alpha
(see
[
14
]
). In particular, if we consider two independent Brownian motions
W^{(1)},W^{(2)}
, then the process

X^{\alpha}_{t}=2\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)}W^{(1)}_{t}+(2\alpha-1)|W^{(2)}_{t}|

(3)

is a Itô-McKean skew Brownian motion (see
[
8
, Proposition 2.1]
). The process (
3
) is usually referred as the Azzalini construction of the skew Brownian motion, as it can be easily shown to admit density belonging to the class of skew normal distributions introduced in
[
1
]
. It is interesting to observe that if
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
we get the standard Brownian motion, if
\alpha=1
we obtain the reflected Brownian motion and if
\alpha=0
we get a negatively reflected Brownian motion. Hence, in some sense
X^{\alpha}
interpolates between the negatively ad the positively reflected Brownian motion.

Let us also recall that, as proved in
[
13
, Section 5]
,
X^{\alpha}
is a time-inhomogeneous Markov process whose transition density function is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{\alpha}(t,y\mid&s,x):={\mathbb{P}}(X^{\alpha}_{t}\in dy\mid X^{\alpha}_{s}=x)\\
&=\int_{0}^{t-s}\frac{|y|(1+(2\alpha(u+s)-1){\sf sign}(y))}{2\pi(t-s-u)^{\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{u}}\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2(t-s-u)}-\frac{x^{2}}{2u}\right)\,du\\
&+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(t-s)}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(y-x)^{2}}{2(t-s)}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{(y+x)^{2}}{2(t-s)}\right)\right]\mathbf{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}}(xy),\end{split}

(4)

where
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
0\leq s<t
. The joint density of
(X_{t}^{\alpha},L_{t}(X^{\alpha}))
is given in
[
13
, Theorem 2.14]
. If we define

A^{\alpha}_{t}:=x+\int_{0}^{t}(2\alpha(s)-1)dL_{s}(X^{\alpha}),

as a direct consequence of
[
23
, Proposition IV.2.10]
we know that
A^{\alpha}
has locally bounded variation and then the decomposition of
X^{\alpha}
as a semimartingale is given by

X^{\alpha}_{t}=W_{t}+A^{\alpha}_{t},

which in turn implies
[X^{\alpha},X^{\alpha}]_{t}=t
. Once the decomposition of
X^{\alpha}
as a semimartingale is clear, one can easily define the space of stochastic integrands with respect to the time-inhomogeneous skew Brownian motion, according to
[
23
, Definitions IV.2.6, IV.2.8 and IV.2.9]
.

Definition 2
.

Let
Y=\{Y_{t},t\geq 0\}
be a progressively measurable stochastic process. We say that
Y
is locally bounded if there exist an increasing sequence of stopping times
\{T_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
T_{n}\to+\infty
a.s. and a sequence of positive constants
\{C_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t\geq 0
it holds

|Y_{t\wedge T_{n}}|\leq C_{n}\mbox{ a.s.}

We say that a process
Y=\{Y_{t},t\in[0,T]\}\in M^{2}_{T}(\Omega)
if
Y
is progressively measurable, a.s. bounded and

\int_{0}^{T}{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{t}^{2}]dt<\infty.

For any process
Y\in M^{2}_{T}(\Omega)
we can define

\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}dX^{\alpha}_{t}=\int_{0}^{T}Y_{t}dW_{t}+\int_{0}^{T}(2\alpha(t)-1)Y_{t}dL_{t}(X^{\alpha}).

Finally, we say that
Y=\{Y_{t},t\geq 0\}\in M^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
if
Y
is locally bounded and its restriction on
[0,T]
belongs to
M^{2}_{T}(\Omega)
for any
T>0
.

Once this is done, it is not difficult to define stochastic differential equations driven by
X^{\alpha}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
T>0
,
b,\sigma:[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
be measurable functions,
\overline{Y}
be a random variable and
\alpha\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+};[0,1])
. We say that a process
Y\in M^{2}_{T}(\Omega)
is a strong solution of the stochastic differential equation (SDE)

\begin{cases}dY_{t}=b(t,Y_{t})dt+\sigma(t,Y_{t})dX^{\alpha}_{t}&t\in[0,T]\\
Y_{0}=\overline{Y}\end{cases}

(5)

if

(i)

The stochastic process
t\in[0,T]\mapsto b(t,Y_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}
belongs to
L^{1}[0,T]
a.s.

(ii)

The stochastic process
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\sigma(t,Y_{t})\in{\mathbb{R}}
belongs to
M^{2}_{T}(\Omega)

(iii)

It holds

Y_{t}=\overline{Y}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s,Y_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,Y_{s})dX^{\alpha}_{s}.

We say that pathwise uniqueness holds for (
5
) if for any two strong solutions
Y^{1},Y^{2}
we have

{\mathbb{P}}(Y^{1}(t)=Y^{2}(t),\ \forall t\in[0,T])=1.

If
T=\infty
, we ask
Y\in M^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
.

3.2
The time-inhomogeneous geometric-skew Brownian motion (GSBM)

Now let
\mu,\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})
such that
\mu,\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})
and consider a random variable
\overline{G}^{\alpha}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for some
p>2
and such that
{\sf P}(\overline{G}^{\alpha}>0)=1
. We want to focus on the following linear SDE

\begin{cases}dG^{\alpha}_{t}=\mu(t)G^{\alpha}_{t}dt+\sigma(t)G^{\alpha}_{t}dX^{\alpha}_{t}&t\geq 0\\
G^{\alpha}_{0}=\overline{G}^{\alpha}\end{cases}

(6)

Namely, we now prove the following existence and uniqueness theorem.

Theorem 1
.

The pathwise unique strong solution of (
6
) is given by

G_{t}^{\alpha}=\overline{G}^{\alpha}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}\right)

(7)

Proof.

It is not difficult to check, since
\overline{G}^{\alpha}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
for
p>2
, that
G^{\alpha}\in M^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
. Indeed, first observe that
G^{\alpha}
is clearly locally bounded. Furthermore, recalling that
L_{t}(X^{\alpha}_{t})\leq t
and using Young’s inequality, we have for any
T>0
and
t\in[0,T]

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\right)^{p}\right]+\frac{p-2}{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\right)^{p}\right]+\frac{p-2}{p}\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)(2\alpha(s)-1)dL_{s}(X^{\alpha})+\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dW_{s}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\right)^{p}\right]+\frac{p-2}{p}\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}(2\left\|\alpha\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}+1)L_{t}+\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dW_{s}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\right)^{p}\right]+\frac{p-2}{p}\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}(2\left\|\alpha\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}+1)T\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dW_{s}\right)\right].

Next, we recall that
\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dW_{s}
is a centered Gaussian random variable with variance
\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds
, hence we can evaluate its moment generating function obtaining

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}\right)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\overline{G}^{\alpha}\right)^{p}\right]+\frac{p-2}{p}\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\exp\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\left\|\sigma\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}(2\left\|\alpha\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T)}+1)T+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{p}{p-2}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sigma^{2}(s)ds\right),

where the right-hand side is clearly integrable in
[0,T]
. Since
T>0
is arbitrary, we get that
G^{\alpha}\in M^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
.

Let us now show that (
7
) solves (
6
). Upon substituting
G^{\alpha}_{t}
with
\frac{G^{\alpha}_{t}}{\overline{G}^{\alpha}}
, we can assume
\overline{G}^{\alpha}\equiv 1
. Define the process

Y^{\alpha}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}.

This is well-defined since
\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})\subset M^{2}_{t}(\Omega)
for any
t\geq 0
. Furthermore, it is a semimartingale. Indeed, if we define

A^{\alpha,\sigma}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)(2\alpha(s)-1)dL_{s}(X^{\alpha}),

by
[
23
, Proposition IV.2.10]
we know that
A^{\alpha,\sigma}
has locally bounded variation and then
Y^{\alpha}
can be decomposed as

Y^{\alpha}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dW_{s}+A^{\alpha,\sigma}_{t}.

Furthermore, it is clear that

dY^{\alpha}_{t}=\sigma(t)dW_{t}+\sigma(t)(2\alpha(t)-1)dL_{t}(X^{\alpha})=\sigma(t)dX^{\alpha}_{t}

and

d[Y^{\alpha},Y^{\alpha}]_{t}=\sigma^{2}(t)dt.

Next, define

Z^{\alpha}_{t}=Y^{\alpha}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds

which is still a semi-martingale, since we are adding to
Y^{\alpha}
a function of locally bounded variation (precisely, an absolutely continuous function), with
d[Z^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha}]_{t}=\sigma^{2}(t)dt
. By definition, we have
G^{\alpha}_{t}=\exp(Z^{\alpha}_{t})
, hence, by Itô’s formula (see
[
23
, Theorem IV.3.3]
)

\displaystyle dG^{\alpha}_{t}

\displaystyle=G^{\alpha}_{t}dZ^{\alpha}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}G^{\alpha}_{t}d[Z^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha}]_{t}

\displaystyle=G^{\alpha}_{t}\left(\mu(t)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(t)}{2}\right)dt+G^{\alpha}_{t}\sigma(t)dX^{\alpha}_{t}+\frac{G^{\alpha}_{t}\sigma^{2}(t)}{2}dt

\displaystyle=\mu(t)G^{\alpha}_{t}dt+\sigma(t)G^{\alpha}_{t}dX^{\alpha}_{t}.

Now let us show that
G^{\alpha}
is the unique solution. To do this, let
Y
be any other strong solution and fix
T>0
. Since
Y\in M_{\rm loc}^{2}(\Omega)
, it is locally bounded and there exist a sequence
T_{n}\to+\infty
of stopping times and a sequence
C_{n}>0
of constants such that
|Y_{t\wedge T_{n}}|\leq C_{n}
. Fix
T>0
,
\omega\in\Omega
and let
n=n(\omega)
such that
T_{n}(\omega)>T
. It is clear that the function
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\mu(s)Y_{s}(\omega)ds
is continuous. Furthermore for
s\leq t\leq T

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)(2\alpha(\tau)-1)Y_{\tau}(\omega)dL_{\tau}(X^{\alpha})(\omega)-\int_{0}^{s}\sigma(\tau)(2\alpha(\tau)-1)Y_{\tau}(\omega)dL_{\tau}(X^{\alpha})(\omega)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad=\left|\int_{s}^{t}\sigma(\tau)(2\alpha(\tau)-1)Y_{\tau}(\omega)dL_{\tau}(X^{\alpha})(\omega)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\|\sigma\right\|_{L^{\infty}[0,T]}(2\left\|\alpha\right\|_{L^{\infty}[0,T]}+1)C_{n}(L_{t}(X^{\alpha})(\omega)-L_{s}(X^{\alpha})(\omega)).

Since
L_{t}(X^{\alpha})
is the local time of a continuous semimartingale, we can consider a version that is continuous in
t
and then the previous inequality guarantees that
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)(2\alpha(\tau)-1)Y_{\tau}dL_{\tau}(X^{\alpha})
is a.s. continuous. Finally, observe that since
\sigma\in L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}[0,T]
and
Y\in M^{2}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
, the process
t\in[0,T]\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(\tau)Y_{\tau}dW_{\tau}
is a continuous martingale (see the discussion in
[
18
, Section 3.2]
). Hence
Y
is a.s. continuous. Let
T_{0}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\ Y_{t}=0\}
, where, clearly,
T_{0}>0
a.s. Thus, the process
Z_{t}=\log\left(\frac{Y_{t}}{\overline{G}^{\alpha}}\right)
is well-defined for
t<T_{0}
and
Z_{t}\to-\infty
as
t\to T_{0}
a.s. Now assume by contradiction that
{\sf P}(T_{0}<\infty)>0
and consider
\omega\in\{T_{0}<\infty\}
. Then, by Itô’s formula (omitting the dependence on
\omega
for the ease of the reader),

Z_{t\wedge T_{0}}=\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{0}}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t\wedge T_{0}}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}.

and taking the limit as
t\uparrow T_{0}
we have

\lim_{t\to T_{0}}Z_{t}=\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{T_{0}}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}

(8)

which is absurd since the left-hand side is finite. Hence
{\sf P}(T_{0}=\infty)=1
and we have for any
t\geq 0

Z_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)}{2}\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s)dX^{\alpha}_{s}

which in turn implies that
Y_{t}=G^{\alpha}_{t}
for any
t\geq 0
.
∎

3.3
Approximation of
G^{\alpha}_{t}
with piecewise constant parameters

From now on, without loss of generality, we assume that
X^{\alpha}_{0}=0
.

For the forecasting procedure, we will approximate the functional parameters
\mu,\sigma,\alpha
of the process with piecewise constants functions. To do this, let us denote by
\mathcal{A}
the class of functions
f\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})\cap L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})
with the following property: for any
T>0
and any sequence of partitions
\Pi_{n}:t^{n}_{0}=0<t^{n}_{1}<\cdots<t^{n}_{n}=T
with
{\sf diam}(\Pi_{n}):=\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|t^{n}_{i}-t^{n}_{i-1}|\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
, there exist two sequences of càdlàg functions
\underline{f}_{n},\overline{f}_{n}:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
n=1,2,\dots
that are constant on each interval
[t^{n}_{i},t^{n}_{i+1})
,
i=0,\dots,n-1
, and satisfy
\underline{f}_{n}(t)\leq f(t)\leq\overline{f}_{n}(t)
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\underline{f}_{n}(t)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\overline{f}_{n}(t)=f(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
. This condition has been considered for instance in
[
13
]
only in the interval
[0,1]
, where it is called Condition
\mathcal{H}
, and it is clearly satisfied by any continuous function. If
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, then, by a simple adaptation of the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 7.4]
to the case of the interval
[0,T]
(in place of just
[0,1]
) and using explicitly
[
13
, Equation (7.3)]
, one has for any
T>0
and any
\underline{\alpha}_{n},\overline{\alpha}_{n}
as before

\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{\underline{\alpha}_{n}}_{t}-X^{\alpha}_{t}\right|\right]=\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{\overline{\alpha}_{n}}_{t}-X^{\alpha}_{t}\right|\right]=0.

(9)

We can extend the previous result to any piecewise constant approximation of
\alpha
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
. For any sequence of partitions
\Pi_{n}:t^{n}_{0}=0<t^{n}_{1}<\cdots<t^{n}_{n}=T
with
{\sf diam}(\Pi_{n})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
and any sequence of càdlàg functions
\alpha_{n}:[0,T]\to[0,1]
, where
\alpha_{n}(t)
is constant on
[t_{i}^{n},t_{i+1}^{n})
,
i=0,\dots,n-1
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\alpha_{n}(t)=\alpha(t)
for any
t\in[0,T]
, it holds

\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{\alpha_{n}}_{t}-X^{\alpha}_{t}\right|\right]=0.

(10)

Proof.

Consider a sequence of partitions
\Pi_{n}
and a sequence of càdlàg functions
\alpha_{n}:[0,T]\to[0,1]
as in the statement. Since
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
, we can also consider two sequences of càdlàg functions
\underline{\alpha}_{n},\overline{\alpha}_{n}:[0,T]\to[0,1]
that are constant on each interval
[t_{i}^{n},t_{i}^{n+1})
,
\underline{\alpha}_{n}(t)\leq\alpha(t)\leq\overline{\alpha}_{n}(t)
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\underline{\alpha}_{n}(t)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\overline{\alpha}_{n}(t)=\alpha(t)
for any
t\in[0,T]
. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,T]
we define
\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)=\min\{\alpha_{n}(t),\underline{\alpha}_{n}(t)\}
and
\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)=\max\{\alpha_{n}(t),\overline{\alpha}_{n}(t)\}
. By definition,
\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}},\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}
are càdlàg, piecewise constant on
[t_{i}^{n},t_{i}^{n+1})
and
\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)\leq\alpha(t)\leq\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)
,
\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)\leq\alpha_{n}(t)\leq\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)
and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}(t)=\alpha(t)
for any
t\in[0,T]
, hence, by (
9
),

\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}}_{t}-X^{\alpha}_{t}\right|\right]=\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}}_{t}-X^{\alpha}_{t}\right|\right]=0.

By the comparison theorem for solutions of SDEs involving local time (see
[
20
, Theorem 3.4]
), we know that a.e. and for any
t\in[0,T]

X_{t}^{\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}}\leq X_{t}^{\alpha_{n}}\leq X_{t}^{\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}}.

and

X_{t}^{\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}}\leq X_{t}^{\alpha}\leq X_{t}^{\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}}.

In particular, we get, a.e. and for any
t\in[0,T]

|X_{t}^{\alpha_{n}}-X_{t}^{\alpha}|\leq|X_{t}^{\underline{\underline{\alpha_{n}}}}-X_{t}^{\alpha}|+|X_{t}^{\overline{\overline{\alpha_{n}}}}-X_{t}^{\alpha}|.

Taking the supremum over
[0,T]
, the expectation and then the limit as
n\to\infty
we get the desired result.
∎

After the preceding conditions and results have been established, we can provide the following approximation result for
G_{t}^{\alpha}
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume
\alpha\in{\cal A}
,
\mu,\sigma\in\mathcal{M}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})\cap L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}({\mathbb{R}}_{0}^{+})
and fix
T>0
.
Consider a sequence of partitions
\Pi_{n}:t^{n}_{0}=0<t^{n}_{1}<\cdots<t^{n}_{n}=T
with
{\sf diam}(\Pi_{n})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. Let also
\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\alpha_{n}:[0,T]\to[0,1]
be sequences of càdlàg functions such that

(i)

\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}
are constant on each interval
[t_{i}^{n},t^{n}_{i+1})
,
i=0,\dots,n-1
;

(ii)

\alpha_{n}(t)\to\alpha(t)
,
\mu_{n}(t)\to\mu(t)
and
\sigma_{n}(t)\to\sigma(t)
for any
t\in[0,T]
;

(iii)

There exists a constant
M
such that

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(|\mu_{n}(t)|+|\sigma_{n}(t)|)\leq M;

(iv)

There exists a constant
V
such that

\sup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{i=0}^{n}|\sigma_{n}(t_{i+1})-\sigma_{n}(t_{i})|\leq V.

Fix
\overline{G}^{\alpha}
and denote by
G^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}
the solution of (
6
) with parameters
\alpha,\mu,\sigma
. Then

\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}_{t}}{G^{\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|\right]=0.

(11)

Proof.

Let us first observe that

\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}_{t}}{G^{\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|\leq\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}_{t}}{G^{\alpha,\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|+\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}{G^{\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|.

To estimate the first logarithm, notice that

\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}_{t}}{\widetilde{G}^{\alpha,\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|\mu(s)-\mu_{n}(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)-\sigma^{2}_{n}(s)}{2}\right|ds+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dX_{s}^{\alpha}\right|.

For the first integral, we simply get

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\mu(s)-\mu_{n}(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)-\sigma^{2}_{n}(s)}{2}\right|ds\right]\\
={\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\mu(s)-\mu_{n}(s)-\frac{\sigma^{2}(s)-\sigma^{2}_{n}(s)}{2}\right|ds\right]\to 0

as
n\to\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem. To handle the second summand, observe that

\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dX_{s}^{\alpha}=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)(2\alpha(t)-1)dL_{t}(X^{\alpha})+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dW_{s}

and then

\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dX_{s}^{\alpha}\right|\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left|\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right||2\alpha(t)-1|dL_{t}(X^{\alpha})+\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dW_{s}\right|.

Taking the supremum and the expectation we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dX_{s}^{\alpha}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right||2\alpha(t)-1|dL_{t}(X^{\alpha})\right]

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dW_{s}\right|\right].

On the one hand, we have
\left|\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right||2\alpha(t)-1|\leq 2M
, where

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}2MdL_{t}(X^{\alpha})\right]\leq 2M{\mathbb{E}}\left[L_{T}(X^{\alpha})\right]\leq 2MT<\infty.

Hence, by the dominated convergence theorem,

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right||2\alpha(t)-1|dL_{t}(X^{\alpha})\right]=0.

On the other hand, we have, by Doob’s maximal inequality
[
23
, Theorem II.1.7]

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dW_{s}\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left(\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right)dW_{s}\right|^{2}\right]}\\
\leq 2\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\left|\sigma_{n}(s)-\sigma(s)\right|^{2}_{s}\right]}\to 0,

where the limit holds by dominated convergence. This proves that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}_{t}}{\widetilde{G}^{\alpha,\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right]=0.

Now let us work with the second logarithm. Let
Y^{n}_{t}=X^{\alpha}_{t}-X^{\alpha_{n}}_{t}
and
i_{\star}^{n}(t)=\min\{i=0,\dots,n-1:\ t_{i}^{n}\geq t\}
. We have

\displaystyle\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}{G^{\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{i_{\star}^{n}(t)-1}\sigma_{n}(t_{i-1}^{n})(Y^{n}_{t_{i}^{n}}-Y^{n}_{t_{i-1}^{n}})+\sigma_{n}(t_{i^{n}_{\star}(t)-1}^{n})(Y^{n}_{t}-Y^{n}_{t_{i_{\star}^{n}(t)-1}^{n}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{i=1}^{i_{\star}^{n}(t)-1}\left(\sigma_{n}(t_{i-1}^{n})-\sigma_{n}(t_{i}^{n})\right)Y^{n}_{t_{i}^{n}}+\sigma_{n}(t^{n}_{i^{n}_{\star}(t)-1})Y^{n}_{t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{i_{\star}^{n}(t)-1}\left|\sigma_{n}(t_{i-1}^{n})-\sigma_{n}(t_{i}^{n})\right|+|\sigma_{n}(t^{n}_{i^{n}_{\star}(t)-1})|\right)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Y^{n}_{t}|

\displaystyle\leq(V+M)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Y^{n}_{t}|.

Taking the supremum, the expectation and the limit as
n\to+\infty
and using (
10
) we get

\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\log\left(\frac{G^{\alpha,\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}{G^{\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}_{t}}\right)\right|\right]\leq(V+M)\lim_{n\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|Y^{n}_{t}|\right]=0.

This ends the proof.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

Observe that condition
(iv)
is satisfied if, for instance,
\sigma
is a (càdlàg) bounded variation function and
\sigma_{n}(t)=\sigma(t_{i-1}^{n})
for any
t\in[t_{i-1}^{n},t_{i}^{n})
,
i=1,\dots,n
.

Furthermore, as a consequence of the uniform geometric mean convergence (
11
), we know that
\frac{G_{t}^{\alpha_{n},\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}}}{G_{t}^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}}\overset{{\sf P}}{\to}1
and there exists a subsequence such that
\frac{G_{t}^{\alpha_{n_{k}},\mu_{n_{k}},\sigma_{n_{k}}}}{G_{t}^{\alpha,\mu,\sigma}}\overset{a.s.}{\to}1
.

Now, let us evaluate the conditional expectation of
G_{t}^{\alpha}
given
G_{s}^{\alpha}
, where
t\geq s
, in case
\sigma
is constant.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
s\leq t
and assume that
\sigma(s)\equiv\sigma
and assume further that
\overline{G}^{\alpha}
is degenerate. Then we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}[G^{\alpha}_{t}\mid G^{\alpha}_{s}]\\
&=G^{\alpha}_{s}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(\tau)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)d\tau-\log\left(\frac{G_{s}^{\alpha}}{\overline{G}^{\alpha}}\right)\right)\\
&\ \times\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{t-s}\frac{|y|(1+(2\alpha(u+s)-1){\sf sign}(y))}{2\pi(t-s-u)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2(t-s-u)}-\frac{(F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))^{2}}{2u}+\sigma y\right)du\,dy\\
&\ +\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(t-s)}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(y-F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))^{2}}{2(t-s)}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{(y+F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))^{2}}{2(t-s)}\right)\right]e^{\sigma y}\mathbf{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}}(F(G^{\alpha}_{s})y)dy,\end{split}

(12)

where

F(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\log\left(\frac{x}{\overline{G}^{\alpha}}\right)-\int_{0}^{s}\left(\mu(\tau)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)d\tau\right)

(13)

Proof.

We clearly have

G^{\alpha}_{t}=G^{\alpha}_{s}\exp\left(\int_{s}^{t}\left(\mu(\tau)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)d\tau+\sigma(X^{\alpha}_{t}-X^{\alpha}_{s})\right).

Furthermore, observe that

X^{\alpha}_{s}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\log\left(\frac{G_{s}^{\alpha}}{\overline{G}^{\alpha}}\right)-\int_{0}^{s}\left(\mu(\tau)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)d\tau\right)=F(G^{\alpha}_{s}).

Hence we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[G^{\alpha}_{t}\mid G^{\alpha}_{s}]=G^{\alpha}_{s}\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu(\tau)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)d\tau-\log\left(\frac{G_{s}^{\alpha}}{\overline{G}^{\alpha}}\right)\right){\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\sigma X^{\alpha}_{t}}\mid G^{\alpha}_{s}\right].

(14)

To evaluate the remaining conditional expectation, we use (
4
) so that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[e^{\sigma X^{\alpha}_{t}}\mid G^{\alpha}_{s}\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}e^{\sigma y}p_{\alpha}(t,y\mid s,F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))dy

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{0}^{t-s}\frac{|y|(1+(2\alpha(u+s)-1){\sf sign}(y))}{2\pi(t-s-u)^{\frac{3}{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2(t-s-u)}-\frac{(F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))^{2}}{2u}+\sigma y\right)du\,dy

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(t-s)}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(y-F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))^{2}}{2(t-s)}\right)-\exp\left(-\frac{(y+F(G^{\alpha}_{s}))^{2}}{2(t-s)}\right)\right]e^{\sigma y}\mathbf{1}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}}(F(G^{\alpha}_{s})y)dy.

Combining this with (
14
) we get the desired formula.
∎

5
Conclusion

In this paper, a novel model has been introduced for assessing the impact of natural disasters on affected populations, particularly for loss projection. This model integrates historical data, addresses data skewness, and accommodates temporal dependencies to predict shifts in mortality. To accomplish this, a time-varying skew Brownian motion framework has been presented, with the existence and uniqueness of the solution being established. Within this framework, parameters evolve over time, and past occurrences are integrated via volatility. Pseudocode for calibration has been provided, and a test against a deep learning model has demonstrated the advantages of the proposed approach.
