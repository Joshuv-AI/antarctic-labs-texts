Bayesian Algorithms Learn to Stabilize Unknown Continuous-Time Systems
Mohamad Kazem Shirani Faradonbeh, Mohamad Sadegh Shirani Faradonbeh

Abstract
Linear dynamical systems are canonical models for learning-based control of plants with uncertain dynamics. The setting consists of a stochastic differential equation that captures the state evolution of the plant understudy, while the true dynamics matrices are unknown and need to be learned from the observed data of state trajectory. An important issue is to ensure that the system is stabilized and destabilizing control actions due to model uncertainties are precluded as soon as possible. A reliable stabilization procedure for this purpose that can effectively learn from unstable data to stabilize the system in a finite time is not currently available. In this work, we propose a novel Bayesian learning algorithm that stabilizes unknown continuous-time stochastic linear systems. The presented algorithm is flexible and exposes effective stabilization performance after a remarkably short time period of interacting with the system.



1
Introduction

Linear dynamical systems are ubiquitous models in the system theory for effective control of plants
[
1
,
2
]
. In a general setting, the state of the plant understudy follows a multidimensional stochastic differential equation depending on the state vector and control input at the time, together with the exogenous disturbance noise. An interesting problem is that of stabilization under uncertainty to ensure that the system can safely operate for a reasonable time period.

In many situations, a reliable knowledge of the system matrices that determine the differential equations of state evolution is unavailable. Such lack of knowledges can be due to unintended internal interactions between the state quantities of an engineered plant or naturally unknown dynamics of the environment one desires to control, such as biological or financial systems
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
. In such situations, data-driven methods are needed to learn the underlying unknown true dynamical behaviors from the observed data.

Accordingly, design and analysis of learning-based methods for control of unknown dynamical systems is of interest to ensure optimal system performances. For continuous-time stochastic linear systems, study of adaptive policies for near-optimal regulations has a rich literature. That consists of control policies that estimate optimal actions after long-term interactions with the system
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
as well as those who focus on the resulting sub-optimalities of learning-based policies
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
]
. However, algorithms for learning to stabilize with provable performance are limited to infinite time schemes for a class of controllable systems
[
9
,
12
]
. So, finite-time approaches are currently missing and is adopted as the subject of this work, especially for the more general case of stabilizable (rather than controlable) dynamical systems.

Importantly, stabilization procedures require to terminate in a finite time. On one hand, the decision-maker can focus on optimality only when instabilities are fully precluded. On the other hand, during the stabilization period, the system can expose unstable behavior making the magnitude of the state extremely large. Therefore, continuation of stabilization algorithms for long periods of time defeats the purpose. For the case of discrete-times dynamics, the problem of stabilization recently received a lot of attentions. The existing results include performance guarantees for stabilizable
[
15
,
16
]
and controllable systems
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.

In the discrete-time regime, the method of applying random feedback matrices is proposed to ensure that the resulting unknown closed-loop matrix is learnable
[
20
,
15
]
. Intuitively, if (the rates or directions of) explosions for an unstable linear dynamical system are indistinguishable, parameter estimates will be drastically inaccurate
[
21
]
. To avoid these pathological cases for learning stabilizing policies, the algorithm can randomize the inputs, which in turn renders pathological closed-loop matrices unlikely
[
15
]
. Then, once stabilized, low-regret reinforcement learning policies can be applied to the system to learn to minimize the operating costs. The latter trend of research includes many recent works as well, especially for the case of quadratic cost functions
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]
.

In this work, we introduce an algorithm that learns to stabilize unknown continuous-time stochastic linear system in a short time period. The algorithm designs control inputs that effectively excite all modes of the system, and switches the employed feedback policies in sub-intervals of the original time interval. Then, the resulting state vectors are utilized in a Bayesian fashion to form a posterior belief about the unknown dynamics matrices. The performance of the algorithm is studied and effects of different parameters are discussed. We present numerical results showcasing that in a remarkably short time period, the proposed algorithm successfully stabilizes the system with high probability. Introducing a novel framework for fast stabilization, we discuss the implications and provide multiple interesting research avenues for future investigations.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the detailed statement of the problem and discuss different aspects of stabilization under uncertainty. Then, in Section
3
, we propose a Bayesian learning-based stabilization algorithm and outline the detailed steps it relies on. Analysis of the algorithm and interpretations of the effects of different parameters are provided in Section
4
. Finally, we spotlight the vision this work leads to, discuss possible extensions to other regimes such as large-scale and/or non-linear systems, and mention different directions for future studies on data-driven methods and algorithms for learning-to-stabilize.

2
Problem Statement

We study stabilization of continuous-time stochastic systems under uncertainty. In this setting, the dynamics of the plant under consideration consist of the following
unknown
stochastic differential equation:

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=\left(A_{\star}X_{t}+B_{\star}U_{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t+C_{\star}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{W}_{t}.

(1)

That is, the open-loop transition matrix
A_{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
of the system with state
X_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, as well as the input matrix
B_{\star}
that reflects the effect of the control signal
U_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
, both are
unknown
. The differential equation describing the plant in the above model includes a white noise. That is, the system is disturbed by the Brownian motion
\mathbb{W}_{t}
, technically defined as a continuous-time stochastic process with independent Gaussian increments
\mathbb{W}_{t}-\mathbb{W}_{s},s\leq t
of mean zero,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{W}_{t}-\mathbb{W}_{s}\right]=0_{d}
, and covariance matrix
\mathrm{Cov}\left(\mathbb{W}_{t}-\mathbb{W}_{s}\right)=\left(t-s\right)I_{d}
. The
p\times d
matrix
C_{\star}
that rescales the noise influence is assumed
unknown
as well.

Note that if the physical behavior of the system is governed by a differential equation of a higher degree, one can convert it to the state-space model in (
1
). To that end, it suffices to concatenate the physical state together with its derivatives of different orders to obtain the larger state vector
X_{t}

[
1
,
2
,
21
]
. Therefore, studying data-driven control schemes for learning to stabilize the dynamical system in (
1
) captures a general class of plants.

To proceed, we assume that the system is stabilizable.

Assumption 1

There exists an unknown feedback matrix
{L}_{\text{stab}}
such that
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(A_{\star}+B_{\star}{L}_{\text{stab}}\right)<0
.

The above assumption is necessary. Otherwise, stability becomes infeasible even under full certainty about
A_{\star},B_{\star}
. Moreover, thanks to linear dynamics in (
1
), Assumption
1
implies the mean-square stabilizability. Note that due to the Brownian noise
\mathbb{W}_{t}
, fluctuations and large state values in the trajectory of the stochastic system are unavoidable. However, under the stabilizing linear feedback
U_{t}={L}_{\text{stab}}X_{t}
, the state
X_{t}
becomes a multivariate Gaussian random vector with a finite covariance matrix
[
26
]
, and we have
{\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|X_{t}\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{-\log\delta}\right)
, with probability at least
1-\delta

[
21
]
.

It is well-known that stabilizability is intimately related to Riccati equations. For this purpose, let the symmetric matrices
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
R\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
be positive definite. Then, for
{A},M\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}
, define the mapping

\Phi_{{A},{B}}\left(M\right)={A}^{\top}M+M{A}-M{B}R^{-1}{B}^{\top}M+Q.

(2)

So, under Assumption
1
, the algebraic continuous-time Riccati equation
\Phi_{A_{\star},B_{\star}}\left(M\right)=0
has a unique positive semidefinite solution
\boldsymbol{{K}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)
. Conversely, existence of the solution
\boldsymbol{{K}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)
is sufficient for the stabilizability in Assumption
1

[
1
,
2
]
. Furthermore, Riccati equations can be solved relatively fast and explicitly provide the following stabilizing linear feedback matrix
[
1
,
2
]
:

\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)=-R^{-1}B_{\star}^{\top}\boldsymbol{{K}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right).

(3)

Theorem 1

For the linear feedback in (
3
), we have

\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(A_{\star}+B_{\star}\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)\right)<0.

However, when the exact values of the true dynamics matrices
A_{\star},B_{\star}
are not available, the Riccati equation
\Phi_{A_{\star},B_{\star}}\left(M\right)=0
is not solvable and the problem of stabilization is challenging. In the next section, we provide a more general framework for stabilizing the system based on approximations of
A_{\star},B_{\star}
.

2.1
Stabilization under Uncertainty

When the true dynamics matrices are unknown, data-driven methods are required to learn from the state trajectory to stabilize the system. More precisely, learning-based algorithms first design the control signal and observe the resulting state trajectory, and then utilize the observed data to estimate
A_{\star},B_{\star}
. However, learning from trajectories of limited length can never be perfectly accurate and at best can lead to approximate estimates of the dynamics matrices. Especially, since during the stabilization period the system is most likely to be exponentially unstable, this period needs to be as short as possible. In the following result, we generalize Theorem
1
and establish an accuracy in estimating
A_{\star},B_{\star}
that is
sufficient
for stabilization.

Theorem 2

Letting
D_{\star}=A_{\star}+B_{\star}\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)
, assume that
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)\leq-1
. Then, there is a constant
c
such that the followings are sufficient for
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(A_{\star}+B_{\star}\boldsymbol{{L}}\left({A},{B}\right)\right)<0
:

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|{A}-A_{\star}\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-c\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)p^{-1/2},

(4)

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|{B}-B_{\star}\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-c\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)p^{-1/2}{\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|}^{-1}.

(5)

The proof Theorem
2
is provided in the appendix.
Note that according to Theorem
1
, we have
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)<0
. Thus, the condition
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)<-1
expresses that if one closes the loop by the linear feedback policy which is designed for
A_{\star},B_{\star}
, then the stability margin is at least
1
. However,
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)<-1
is not a fundamental requirement, is adopted merely for the ease of presentation, and extensions to the case
-1\leq\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)<0
is mainly the matter of technicality.

The statement of the above result contains the following interpretations. According to Theorem
2
, a coarse-grained approximation of the unknown true matrices suffices for stabilization. The accuracy specified by (
4
) and (
5
) indicates that systems with larger state dimension
p
are harder to stabilize, as well as those with smaller stability margins
-\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(D_{\star}\right)
. The former is caused by the larger number of parameters to learn, while the latter reflects the extent to which the system stability is robust against uncertainty. Furthermore, (
5
) shows that smaller true feedback matrices
\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)
help stabilization because
{\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|}
quantifies the strength of an effective control input for altering a possibly unstable open-loop matrix
A_{\star}
to a stable closed-loop matrix
D_{\star}
, through
B_{\star}
.

Note that (
3
) does
not
imply that a larger matrix
R
leads to a smaller
{\left|\kern-1.29167pt\left|\boldsymbol{{L}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)\right|\kern-1.29167pt\right|}
(i.e., easier stabilization). In fact, according to the Lyapunov equation

\boldsymbol{{K}}\left({A},{B}\right)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{e^{{D}^{\top}t}\left(Q+\boldsymbol{{L}}\left({A},{B}\right)^{\top}R\boldsymbol{{L}}\left({A},{B}\right)\right)e^{{D}t}}\mathrm{d}{t},

(6)

\boldsymbol{{K}}\left(A_{\star},B_{\star}\right)
grows with
R
. So, by (
3
), it is challenging to select the best
Q,R
matrices for relaxing the accuracy in (
5
) for stabilizing the system. However, the numerical studies in the sequel suggest that smaller
R
matrices lead to slightly larger chances for successful stabilization.

3
Bayesian Learning Algorithm

Next, we introduce a stabilization algorithm that leverages Bayesian learning methods to quickly find a stabilizing feedback for the unknown system in (
1
). Broadly speaking, Algorithm
1
commits to interact with the plant in the time interval
0\leq t\leq\boldsymbol{\tau}
, and then provides a stabilizing feedback by using the collected data. It applies randomized control inputs in periods of length
\boldsymbol{\tau}/n
, employs a Bayesian learning method to compute a posterior belief about the unknown truth, and then samples
\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}
from the posterior distribution. Finally, these sampled matrices are used together with the Riccati equation to design a stabilizing feedback. The length
\boldsymbol{\tau}
, the number of periods
n
, and the randomization scales
\sigma_{{L}},\sigma_{V}
, are provided to the algorithm as inputs. In the sequel, we present the detailed procedure of Algorithm
1
.

: Bayesian Learning for Stabilization

\boldsymbol{\tau}
, feedback variance
\sigma_{{L}}^{2}
, randomization variance
\sigma_{V}^{2}
, number of periods
n

\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}

\left\{V_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be the randomization signal in (

)

j=1,\cdots,n

Sample feedback
{L}_{j}
from
\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{{L}}^{2}I_{p}\right)

while
in the interval
\frac{\left(j-1\right)\boldsymbol{\tau}}{n}\leq t<\frac{j\boldsymbol{\tau}}{n}
,
do

Apply input
U_{t}={L}_{j}X_{t}+\sigma_{V}V_{t}

end

while

end

for

Calculate
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, and
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
according to (
8
), (
9
), and (

)

Return

\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}
from the posterior
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

First, fix
\epsilon>0
and let
\left\{V_{t}\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous-time
q
dimensional standard Gaussian signal with independent piece-wise constant values that change after short intervals of length
\epsilon
. Technically, suppose that
\left\{\xi_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence of independent identically distributed
\mathbb{R}^{q}
-valued vectors that share the standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}\left(0_{q},I_{q}\right)
, and define the randomization signal

V_{t}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathds{1}_{\left\{(k-1)\epsilon\leq t<k\epsilon\right\}}\xi_{k}.

(7)

That is, during the time interval
(k-1)\epsilon\leq t<k\epsilon
, we have
V_{t}=\xi_{k}
. Note that if
\epsilon
shrinks,
\epsilon^{1/2}V_{t}
converges in distribution to a continuous-time Gaussian process. However, in Algorithm
1
,
\epsilon
is a fixed value satisfying
\epsilon<\boldsymbol{\tau}q^{-1}n^{-1}
. Indeed, it suffices for
\epsilon
to be small enough to help the control signal
U_{t}
randomizing the input for ensuring that all modes of the dynamical system in (
1
) are properly excited.

Algorithm
1
breaks the interval
0\leq t\leq\boldsymbol{\tau}
to
n

j\in\left\{1,\cdots,n\right\}
, the control input
U_{t}={L}_{j}X_{t}+\sigma_{V}V_{t}
is applied to the plant, where
{L}_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma_{{L}}^{2}I_{q}\right)
is a random feedback matrix for period

. Recall from the definition of normally distributed matrices in the notation that it is equivalent to say that all
p

{L}_{j}
are independently drawn from a zero-mean Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
\sigma_{{L}}^{2}I_{q}
. The rationale of employing random feedbacks is to preclude the resulting true closed-loop matrix
A_{\star}+B_{\star}{L}_{j}
from falling on the pathological manifold of
non-estimable
matrices
[
21
,
15
,
16
]
. Further technical details about necessity and effectiveness of random feedback matrices is beyond the scope of this work and can be found in the aforementioned references.

After designing and applying the above control inputs, Algorithm
1
uses the collected observations
\left\{X_{t},U_{t}\right\}_{0\leq t\leq\boldsymbol{\tau}}
to calculate a posterior distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
on the set of
\left[{A},{B}\right]^{\top}
matrices, and then samples
\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}
from
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, as outlined below. Denote the observation at time
t
by the vector
Y_{t}=\left[X_{t}^{\top},U_{t}^{\top}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p+q}
. Then, define the
\left(p+q\right)\times\left(p+q\right)
precision matrix

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=I_{p+q}+\int\limits_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{Y_{s}Y_{s}^{\top}}\mathrm{d}{s},

(8)

as well as the the
\left(p+q\right)\times p
mean matrix

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{-1}\int\limits_{0}^{\boldsymbol{\tau}}{Y_{s}}\mathrm{d}{X_{s}^{\top}}.

(9)

Now, Algorithm
1
forms the posterior belief

\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{-1}\right),

(10)

about the unknown true dynamics matrices
A_{\star},B_{\star}
. So, as defined in the notation, the posterior distribution of every column
i=1,\cdots,p
of
\left[A_{\star},B_{\star}\right]^{\top}
is a multivariate normal that its mean is the column
i
of the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and its covariance matrix is
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{-1}
. Next, Algorithm
1
samples from
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
, and returns the resulting sample matrix
\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}
.

Finally, expecting that
\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}
is a sufficiently accurate approximation of
A_{\star},B_{\star}
, the feedback matrix
\boldsymbol{{L}}\left({A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)
is expected to stabilize the system. That is, letting
\boldsymbol{{K}}\left({A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)
be the solution of the Riccati equation
\Phi_{{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}\left(M\right)=0
in (
2
) for the dynamics parameters
\left[{A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right]^{\top}
, we define
\boldsymbol{{L}}\left({A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)=-R^{-1}{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{{K}}\left({A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)
, and expect to have
\boldsymbol{\overline{\lambda}}\left(A_{\star}+B_{\star}\boldsymbol{{L}}\left({A}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\right)\right)<0
. It is straightforward to see that the probabilistic belief
\mathcal{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
is the commonly used posterior distribution in Bayesian learning methods, if a Gaussian prior distribution about the true parameter is assumed. Of course, note that we did not assume availability of such prior beliefs about
A_{\star},B_{\star}
.

5
Conclusion and Future Directions

This work proposes the first algorithm for fast stabilization of unknown stochastic continuous-time linear dynamical systems. It is illustrated that the presented algorithm, that utilizes Bayesian methods for learning to stabilize from the observed state trajectories, performs successfully in a relatively short time period. We establish theoretical results quantifying the effects of larger dimensions and smaller stability margins on the difficulty of stabilizing an unknown system. Moreover, numerical analyses are reported showcasing the roles of different parameters on the performance, indicating that moderately larger randomizations lead to faster stabilization.

As the first study on a canonical problem in adaptive and learning-based control, this work introduces multiple interesting problems for future work. First, establishment of theoretical performance guarantees for the proposed algorithm is of interest to understand the fundamental obstacles in learning-based stabilization policies. Besides, study of the performance in relevant problems such as network systems, non-linear dynamics, and partially observed state trajectories, are different directions for further investigations. Finally, characterizations of Bayesian learning algorithms in similar tasks in signal processing and control such as optimal output-tracking and adaptive filter design, constitute important research problems for future work.
