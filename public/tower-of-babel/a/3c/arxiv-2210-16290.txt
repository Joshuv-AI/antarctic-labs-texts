A Data Driven Bayesian Graphical Ridge Estimator
J. Smith a , M. Arashi ab and A. Bekker ac

Abstract
Bayesian methodologies prioritising accurate associations above sparsity in Gaussian graphical model (GGM) estimation remain relatively scarce in scientific literature. It is well accepted that the ℓ 2 \ell_{2} penalty enjoys a smaller computational footprint in GGM estimation, whilst the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} penalty encourages sparsity in the estimand. The Bayesian adaptive graphical lasso prior is used as a departure point in the formulation of a computationally efficient graphical ridge-type prior for events where accurate associations are prioritised over sparse representations. A novel block Gibbs sampler for simulating precision matrices is constructed using a ridge-type penalisation. The Bayesian graphical ridge-type prior is extended to a Bayesian adaptive graphical ridge-type prior. Synthetic experiments indicate that the graphical ridge-type estimators enjoy computational efficiency, in moderate dimensions, and numerical performance, for relatively non-sparse precision matrices, when compared to their lasso counterparts. The adaptive graphical ridge-type estimator is applied to cell signaling data to infer key associations between phosphorylated proteins in human T cell signalling. All computational workloads are carried out using the baygel R package.

Introduction

Graphical models use graph structures to model complex relationships among a multiplicity of variables. In particular, undirected graphs encode the conditional dependence structure as symmetric relationships between pairs of variables. Gaussian graphical models (GGMs) refer to undirectd graphs characterised by non-zero off-diagonal elements of the precision matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
, assuming that the data is generated by a multivariate Gaussian distribution. The GGM approach for covariance selection,
[
6
]

[
14
]
. For example, GGMs are used for architecting gene expressions
[
30
]

[
8
]
; differential networks
[
20
]
and more recently in modeling the connectivity structures between nodes in the brain (
[
4
]
,
[
29
]
).

From a Bayesian viewpoint, statistical inference of GGMs is typically achieved through the use of hierarchical priors for simultaneous precision matrix estimation and structure learning. The
G\mathrm{Wishart}
is a popular prior choice for
\mathbf{\Omega}
. It places positive probability mass at zero for zero off-diagonal elements and and enjoys conjugacy for Gaussian distributed data (
[

,
[

]

[
15
]

[

,
[

[

,
[
23
]
).
[

investigate the use of discrete uniform and truncated Poisson priors for graph structure learning.
[

Although the
G\mh\mathrm{Wishart}
prior has enjoyed success in many applications, its computational demand and restrictive form may prove challenging (

,
[

have been proposed.
[

\mathbf{\Omega}

Alternatively, absolute continuous priors draw direct connections to penalised estimation with the aim of inducing shrinkage to zero, or near zero, of off-diagonal elements. From a classical viewpoint, there is no shortage of literature pertaining to shrinkage estimators under an assortment of penalty functions. The popular graphical lasso (
[

Contributions

Preliminaries

This section aims to introduce the required notation of undirected Gaussian graphical models; a comprehensive review is available in
[
14
]
. Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
define an undirected graphical model where
\mathcal{V}=\{1,2,...,p\}
is the set of nodes and
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}
the set of existing edges. The undirected graph provides a visual depiction of the conditional dependencies between the nodes. Following the notation used by
[
17
]
, let

\mathcal{M}=\{(i,j)\>|\>i,j\in\mathcal{V},\>i<j\},

and
\bar{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{W}\backslash\mathcal{E}
denotes the set of non-existing edges. Next, define a zero mean Gaussian
graphical model with respect to the graph
\mathcal{G}
as

\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{G}}=\big\{\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(0,\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\>|\>\mathbf{\Omega}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}\big\},

where
M^{+}
is the space of positive definite matrices having entries
(i,j)=0
whenever
(i,j)\in\bar{E}
. Let the observations
\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},...,\mathbf{x}_{n})
be an independent and identically distributed sample from
\mathcal{W}_{\mathcal{G}}
.

For the prior distribution of
\mathbf{\Omega}
, a brief review of
[
22
]
’s Bayesian graphical lasso and Bayesian adaptive graphical lasso is provided. Recall that the object of the graphical lasso is to maximize the penalized log-likelihood

\argmax_{\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}\bigg\{\log(\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega})-\mathrm{trace}(\frac{\mathbf{S}}{n}\mathbf{\Omega})-\rho\Arrowvert\mathbf{\Omega}\Arrowvert_{1}\bigg\},

(1)

here,
\rho\geq 0
is the shrinkage parameter and
\mathbf{\Omega}=(\omega_{ij})
is the precision matrix. The Bayesian graphical lasso prior is given by

p\left(\mathbf{\Omega}\>|\lambda\right)=C^{-1}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{DE}(\omega_{ij}\>|\>\lambda)\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\mathrm{EXP}(\omega_{ii}\>|\>\lambda)\bigg\}\>\>\>\>(\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}).

(2)

Here, the prior is given by the product of a double exponential (DE) with form
p(y)=\lambda/2\exp(-\lambda|y|)
for the off diagonal elements and an exponential (EXP) with form
p(y)=\lambda\exp(-\lambda y)1_{y>0}
for the diagonal. The mode of the posterior is the graphical lasso estimate in (
1
) when
\rho=\lambda/n
, hence
\lambda
can be viewed as a shrinkage lever. For computational simplicity a scale mixture of Gaussians is used to represent the double exponential distribution. However, this hierarchical restructuring requires additional simulation of the latent scale parameter in the proposed block Gibbs sampler.

To address the shortcomings of the double exponential, whereby it may over (under) shrink large (small) coefficients, the prior in (
2
) is extended by allowing different shrinkage parameters,
\lambda_{ij}
, for each off-diagonal element
\omega_{ij}
. This formulation, namely the Bayesian adaptive graphical lasso (BAGLASSO) is given by

p(\mathbf{\Omega}\>|\>\{\lambda_{ij}\}_{i\leq j})=C^{-1}_{\{\lambda_{ij}\}_{i\leq j}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{DE}(\omega_{ij}\>|\>\lambda_{ij})\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\mathrm{EXP}(\omega_{ii}\>|\>\frac{\lambda_{ii}}{2})\bigg\}\>\>\>\>(\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}),

p(\{\lambda_{ij}\}_{i<j}\>|\>\{\lambda_{ii}\}_{i=1}^{p})\propto C_{\{\lambda_{ij}\}_{i\leq j}}\prod_{i<j}\mathrm{GA}(r,s).

(3)

The BAGLASSO automatically controls the amount of shrinkage based on the value of
\omega_{ij}
. To see this, consider the distribution of each shrinkage parameter
\lambda_{ij}
conditioned on
\mathbf{\Omega}
in (
3
)

\lambda_{ij}\>|\>\mathbf{\Omega}\sim\mathrm{GA}(1+r,\>|\omega_{ij}|+s),

implying that the conditional expectation of
\lambda_{ij}
is
(1+r)/(|\omega_{ij}|+s)
. To this end, the amount of shrinkage applied to
\lambda_{ij}
is inversely proportional to the value of
\omega_{ij}
, however, the hyperparameters
r
and
s
need to be sufficiently small to enjoy accurate adaptiveness.

The Bayesian graphical lasso, as well as its adaptive variant are not capable of producing
\omega_{ij}=0
for
i\neq j
since it places zero probability on these events. Graphical model determination under these priors can only be achieved via a heuristic procedure such as the thresholding approach recommended by
[
2
]
. In particular,
[
22
]
claims
{\omega_{ij}=0}
if and only if

\frac{\tilde{\rho}_{ij}}{E_{g}(\rho_{ij}\>|\>\mathbf{Y})}>0.5.

(4)

Here,
\tilde{\rho}_{ij}
is the mean estimate of the posterior partial correlation associated with the graphical lasso priors in (
2
) and
g
is chosen as standard conjugate Wishart
\mathrm{W}(3,\mathbf{I}_{p})
with parameter values selected based on evidence provided by
[
11
]
.

A Bayesian graphical ridge approach

3.1
The graphical ridge-type prior

Consider the penalized estimation problem in (
1
) as a departure point for precision matrix estimation. Using a ridge constraint in place of the
\ell_{1}
results in a graphical ridge-type problem where the objective is to maximise the log-likelihood

\argmax_{\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}\bigg\{\log(\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega})-\mathrm{trace}(\frac{\mathbf{S}}{n}\mathbf{\Omega})-\frac{\rho}{2}\Arrowvert\mathbf{\Omega}\Arrowvert_{2}^{2},\bigg\},

(5)

over the space of positive definite matrices
M^{+}
. Here,
\rho>0
is the shrinkage parameter and
\Arrowvert\mathbf{\Omega}\Arrowvert_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i<j}\sum_{i=1}^{p}\omega_{ij}^{2}
. Moreover, (
5
) is a convex objective function and the Bayesian estimator is given by the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation

p\left(\mathbf{\Omega}\>|\mu\>,\sigma\right)=C^{-1}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{N}(\omega_{ij}\>|\>\mu=0\>,\sigma)\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\mathrm{TN}(\omega_{ii}\>|\>\sigma)\bigg\}\>\>\>\>(\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}),

(6)

where,
\mathrm{N}(x\>|\sigma)
represents a Gaussian density function with form
p(y)=(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi})^{-1}\exp(-0.5[y/\sigma]^{2})
and
\mathrm{TN}(y\>|\sigma)
represents a univariate left truncated at zero Gaussian density function with form
p(y)=\sqrt{2}(\sigma\sqrt{\pi})^{-1}\exp(-0.5[y/\sigma]^{2})1_{y>0}
. Moreover,
C
is the normalising constant not involving
\sigma

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{N}(\omega_{ij}\>|\>\mu=0\>,\sigma)\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\mathrm{TN}(\omega_{ii}\>|\>\mu=0\>,\>\sigma)\bigg\}d\mathbf{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{\tilde{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{N}(\tilde{\omega}_{ij}\>|\>\mu=0\>,1)\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\mathrm{TN}(\Omega_{ii}\>|\>\mu=0\>,\>1)\bigg\}d\mathbf{\tilde{\Omega}}.

The last equality relies on the substitution
\mathbf{\tilde{\Omega}}=\mathbf{\Omega}/\sigma
, and it holds that
\{\mathbf{\tilde{\Omega}}:\mathbf{\tilde{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}\}=\{\mathbf{\Omega}:\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}\}
for
\sigma>0
. The log of the posterior density is given by

\displaystyle l_{r}(\mathbf{\Omega})

\displaystyle\propto\log(\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega})-\mathrm{trace}\left(\frac{\mathbf{S}}{n}\mathbf{\Omega}\right)+\frac{1}{n\sigma^{2}}\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\omega_{ij}^{2}\bigg\},

where
l_{r}(\cdot)
denotes a graphical ridge-type log-likelihood. The MAP estimate of
\mathbf{\Omega}
which maximises this log-likelihood is given by

\underset{\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}{\operatorname{argmax}}\bigg\{\log(\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega})-\mathrm{trace}\left(\frac{\mathbf{S}}{n}\mathbf{\Omega}\right)+\frac{1}{n\sigma^{2}}\Arrowvert\mathbf{\Omega}\Arrowvert_{2}^{2}\bigg\},

and the posterior mode of
\mathbf{\Omega}
is given by (
5
) with
\rho=2/n\sigma^{2}
. Figures (
1a
) - (
1c
) display the marginal distributions of one of the
p
diagonal elements, one of the
p(p-1)/2
off-diagonal elements, and one of the
p(p-1)/2
partial correlations, respectively, when
\sigma=1
and
p\in\{3,10,50\}
. The densities are based on the synthetic samples generated by the Monte Carlo sampling mechanism in Section
3.2
. Interestingly, the marginal distribution of the individual diagonal elements is not half Gaussian and tends to have an increasing mean and variance as
p
increases. The partial correlation seems to settle down near zero with decreasing variation. This implies that the Bayesian graphical ridge-type prior also enjoys the desired property of partial correlations favoring near zero values as
p
increases. It is for this reason that the Bayesian graphical lasso is used as a departure point, however, this desired characteristic may not be as pronounced in the Bayesian graphical ridge-type prior.

A data driven block Gibbs sampler

The block Gibbs sampler described in
[

serves as the impetus for the sampler described here. The target distribution associated with the prior in (

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{\Omega}\>|\>\mathbf{Y},\lambda)\propto\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega}^{\frac{n}{2}}\exp\{-\mathrm{trace}(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\Omega})\}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\exp(-\frac{\omega_{ij}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\exp(-\frac{\omega_{ii}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}})\bigg\}1_{\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}.

It is clear that marginally, (

\displaystyle\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega}^{\frac{n}{2}}=(\omega_{22}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}_{11}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{12})^{\frac{n}{2}}\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega_{11}}^{\frac{n}{2}}

\displaystyle\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{ij}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right)\bigg\}\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\omega_{22}}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\left(\omega_{22}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Theta}^{-1}_{11}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{12}\right)^{\frac{n}{2}}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\bigg\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{21}\boldsymbol{D}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{12}+2\boldsymbol{s}_{21}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{12}+\theta_{22}\left(s_{22}+1\right)\bigg\}\right];\>\>\mathrm{for}\>\>k=0.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\omega}_{12}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\omega_{22}-\boldsymbol{\omega}_{21}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}_{11}\boldsymbol{\omega}_{12},

with the Jacobian independent of

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\beta},\gamma\>|\>\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{11},\boldsymbol{\Psi},\boldsymbol{S},\sigma)\propto

3.3
Computational speed investigation

This section aims to compare and evaluate the computational speed and scalability of the block Gibbs sampler in Algorithm
1
. The synthetic samples generated by the Monte Carlo sampling mechanism in Section
3.2
were used where the number of observations
n
were set to 500 and
\mathbf{\Omega}
was initialised at the identity matrix. The computations were evaluated on a 2 GHz Quad-Core Intel Core i5 running macOS Monterey (version 12.4) using the R (version 4.2.0) programming language.

Computational insights for the block Gibbs samplers associated with the Bayesian adaptive graphical lasso and the Bayesian standard graphical ridge-type (
\sigma=1
), referred to as ”BAGL” and ”BSGR” respectively, are displayed in Figure
2
. Figure
2a
compares the number of minutes required to compute 1000 iterations, as
p
varies, between BSGR and BAGL. Here, a single iteration constitutes the block Gibbs sampler to update each entry of

. The BSGR is implemented with C++ code linked to R via the ’Baygel’ R package. The BAGL is implemented using base R from the ’abglasso’ R package. The C++ BSGR algorithm took approximately 2.4 minutes to generate 1000 iterations for
p=100
, whereas the R BAGL algorthim took 5.8 minutes. Figure

highlights the proportional speed improvements between the native R and C++ based samplers. The C++ based algorithms are noticeably faster than the native R implementations, however, the proportional speed improvements display exponential decay as

increases. Finally, the convergence of the BSGR block Gibbs sampler was assessed using the inefficiency factor

1+2\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\eta(k)

\mathbf{\Omega}
was 0.76. This suggests that Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) process mixes well.

3.4
Bayesian adaptive graphical ridge

The Bayesian graphical ridge-type prior in (
6
) requires the selection of
\sigma
. The structure of the prior and the block Gibbs sampler in Section
3.2
allows for extended hierarchical formulations of the prior to allow for adaptive shrinkage on different off-diagonal elements of
\mathbf{\mathbf{\Omega}}
. That is, the Bayesian framework facilitates the usage of a hyperprior on
\tau=\sigma^{2}
and extending the block Gibbs sampler to sample from the posterior distribution thereof. Consider the following Bayesian adaptive graphical ridge-type set of priors

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbf{y_{i}}\>|\mathbf{\Omega}\right)

\displaystyle=N_{p}\left(\mathbf{y}_{i}\>|0,\>\mathbf{\Omega}^{-1}\right)\>\>\>(i=1,\cdots,n)

(9)

\displaystyle p\left(\mathbf{\Omega}\>|\mu\>,\sigma\right)

\displaystyle=C^{-1}_{\{\sigma_{ij}\}_{i<j}}\prod_{i<j}\bigg\{\mathrm{N}(\omega_{ij}\>|\>\mu=0\>,\sigma_{ij})\bigg\}\prod_{i=1}^{p}\bigg\{\mathrm{TN}(\omega_{ii}\>|\>\mu=0,\sigma_{ii})\bigg\}1_{\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{M}^{+}}

\displaystyle p\left(\{\tau_{ij}\}_{i<j}\>|\{\mu_{ii},\sigma_{ii}\}^{p}_{i=1}\right)

\displaystyle\propto C_{\{\tau_{ij}\}_{i<j}}\prod_{i<j}\mathrm{IGA}(a,b),

where
\mathrm{IGA}(a,b)
represents an inverse gamma distribution with a shape parameter
a
and scale parameter
b
and form
p(y)=b^{a}/\Gamma(a)y^{a+1}\mathrm{exp}(-b/y)
. Moreover,
C_{\{\tau_{ij}\}_{i<j}}
is the intractable normalisation constant and
\sigma_{ii}
and
\mu_{ii}
are hyperparameters for the main diagonal elements. Notice that the terms
C_{\{\sigma_{ij}\}_{i<j}}
in (
9
) cancel out when sampling from the posterior of
\tau_{ij}
. That being said, model fitting, using the block Gibbs sampler in Section

from different inverse gamma distributions will suffice. Conditional on
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, the posterior is given by

\tau_{ij}\>|\>\mathbf{\Omega}\sim\mathrm{IGA}(a+\frac{1}{2},b+\frac{\omega_{ij}^{2}}{2}).

6
Discussion

The Bayesian graphical ridge-type estimators are shown to be attractive for estimating Gaussian graphical models when prioritisation is placed on accurate association representation over sparsity and reducing the computational footprint with an increasing feature space. The Bayesian graphical ridge-type estimators also offer insight and inference into the estimation procedure, via the MCMC results, when compared to frequentist counterparts. The Bayesian graphical ridge-type estimators also enjoy low computational costs for low to moderate dimensions due to the efficient block Gibbs sampler, adding to the Bayesian toolbox of precision matrix estimation. Synthetic studies indicate strong empirical evidence, in favour of the Bayesian graphical ridge-type estimators, for the estimation of precision matrices that are relatively non-sparse. The Bayesian adaptive graphical ridge-type estimator demonstrates the ability to successfully infer the associations between key phosphorylated proteins in human T cell signalling that may provide valuable clinical inference, for example in understanding responses to complex drug therapies used to treat cancer. Finally, with regards to graphical structure learning, the Bayesian graphical ridge-type estimators cannot perform graphical structure determination, an obvious requirement for larger dimensions. As a result, a Bayesian elastic net prior is currently being developed to address the latter whilst maintaining the association accuracy of the Bayesian graphical ridge-type estimators, as well as its computational efficiency.
