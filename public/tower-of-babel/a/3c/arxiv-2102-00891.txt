On radius of convergence of q q -deformed real numbers
Ludivine Leclere, Sophie Morier-Genoud, Valentin Ovsienko, Alexander Veselov

Abstract
. We study analytic properties of “ q q -deformed real numbers”, a notion recently introduced by two of us. A q q -deformed positive real number is a power series with integer coefficients in one formal variable q q . We study the radius of convergence of these power series assuming that q q is a complex variable. Our main conjecture, which can be viewed as a q q -analogue of Hurwitz’s Irrational Number Theorem, claims that the q q -deformed golden ratio has the smallest radius of convergence among all real numbers. The conjecture is proved for certain class of rational numbers and confirmed by a number of computer experiments. We also prove the explicit lower bounds for the radius of convergence for the q q -deformed convergents of golden and silver ratios.



1.
Introduction

There is a famous result due to Hurwitz
[
7
]
, which roughly claims that the golden ratio
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is the most irrational number.
More precisely, for any real number
x\in\mathbb{R}
one can define as the measure of irrationality the Markov constant
\mu(x)
, which is the infimum of
c
, for which the inequality
|x-\frac{p}{q}|<\frac{c}{q^{2}}
holds for infinitely many integer
p,q.
Hurwitz’s Irrational Number Theorem claims that for every
x\in\mathbb{R}

\mu(x)\leq\mu(\varphi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}

with equality holding only for
x
which are
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-equivalent to the golden ratio.

In this paper we discuss a possible
q
-analogue of this classical result. Namely, we consider the
q
-deformations (or “
q
-analogues”) of real numbers, which
have been recently introduced in
[
13
,
14
]
and studied further in
[
11
,
15
,
9
]
.
They have several nice properties and connections, including theory of Conway-Coxeter friezes and knot invariants.

For a rational
x=\frac{r}{s}>0

the
q
-deformation is a rational function

(1.1)

\left[\frac{r}{s}\right]_{q}=\frac{\mathcal{R}(q)}{\mathcal{S}(q)},

where
\mathcal{R}(q)
and
\mathcal{S}(q)
are coprime polynomials with positive integer coefficients
both depending on
r
and
s
.
When
x\geq 1
is an irrational, the
q
-deformation of
x
is defined as a power series in
q
:

(1.2)

\left[x\right]_{q}=1+\varkappa_{1}q+\varkappa_{2}q^{2}+\varkappa_{3}q^{3}+\cdots

with coefficients
\varkappa_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
To obtain the series (
1.2
),
one chooses an arbitrary sequence of rationals
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
converging to
x
.
It turns out that the Taylor series of the rational functions
\left[x_{i}\right]_{q}

stabilize, as
i
grows; see
[
14
]
.
Moreover, the stabilized series depends only on
x
(and not on the approximating sequence of rationals).
The series
\left[x\right]_{q}
is defined as the stabilization of
the Taylor series of
\left[x_{i}\right]_{q}
.
It is unknown, in general, how to characterize the class of power series that
represent
q
-deformed real numbers.
One of the goals of this paper is to demonstrate that series arising in this context are
not arbitrary.

We investigate analytic properties of
q
-deformed real numbers.
Considering the parameter of deformation
q
as a
complex variable
,

q\in\mathbb{C}
, we study the radius of convergence,
R(x)
,
of the series (
1.2
).
In particular, we will show that the radius of the golden ratio
\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
is

R_{\star}:=R(\varphi)=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}=0.381966...

We have the following conjecture, which can be considered as a possible
q
-analogue of the Hurwitz claim.

Conjecture 1.1
.

For every real
x>0
the radius of convergence
R(x)
of the series
\left[x\right]_{q}

satisfies the inequality

R(x)\geq R_{\star}

and the equality holding only for
x
which are
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-equivalent to
\varphi
.

Conjecture
1.1
was checked by the extensive computer experiments.
In this paper we get the following general results towards the proof of the conjecture.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every rational number
x>0
the radius of convergence
R(x)
of the series
\left[x\right]_{q}

has the following lower bound

R(x)>3-2\sqrt{2}=0.171572...

Theorem 1.3
.

If all coefficients of the Hirzebruch-Jung
continued fraction expansion of a rational number
x=\llbracket{}c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots,c_{N}\rrbracket{}

are larger than 3,
then the radius of convergence of
\left[x\right]_{q}
is greater than
R_{\star}.

Our main analytical tool is the classical Rouché theorem (see
[
4
,
19
]
). Note that in the rational case the radius of convergence of the Taylor series can also be understood
as the radius of the maximal disc around
0
that contains no zeros of the polynomial
S(q)
in
the denominator of (
1.1
).
We refer also to the recent work
[
17
]
, where our technique was used to prove the conjecture for a very particular
set of numbers (called metallic).

Conjecture
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
give a restriction for the series (
1.2
)
that can appear as a
q
-deformed real number.
However, it is quite clear that the radius of convergence is not the only condition.
It would be interesting to obtain more information about this class of power series.

Note that the inverse
\rho=R^{-1}
of the radius of convergence of power series (
1.2
) can be given by the standard formula (see e.g.
[
4
]
)

\rho(x)=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\varkappa_{n}|^{\frac{1}{n}}

and thus describes the growth of the coefficients
\varkappa_{n}.

Conjecture
1.1
can be reformulated in the form, more similar to Hurwitz, as the inequality

\rho(x)\leq\rho(\varphi)=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}=\varphi^{2}

holding for every real
x
, and thus means that
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}

has the fastest growth of the coefficients among all
q
-deformed real numbers.
In other words, the series
\left[x\right]_{q}
always “converges better” than
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
,
whenever
x
is not equivalent to
\varphi
.
In the particular case where
x=\frac{r}{s}
is rational, Conjecture
1.1
means that
the polynomial
\mathcal{S}(q)
in (
1.1
) has no roots
q
with
|q|<R_{\star}.

We should mention that the analogy with the Diophantine analysis here cannot be extended to the celebrated Markov theorem
[
10
]
, claiming that the set of all possible Markov constants
\mu=\mu(x)>\frac{1}{3}
is discrete (see e.g.
[
1
]
).
The situation might be more similar to the Lyapunov spectrum of Markov and Euclid trees,
which fills the whole segment
[0,\ln\varphi]
, see
[
18
]
.

The paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we briefly recall the notion of
q
-rationals and
q
-irrationals.
Following
[
9
]
, we emphasize the role of the modular group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
We also give explicit formulas in terms of continued fractions.

In Section
3
, we consider the important example of
q
-deformed golden ratio.
Its approximation by the quotients of consecutive Fibonacci numbers leads to interesting “Fibonacci polynomials”.
We prove that the roots of these polynomials belong to the annulus

R_{0}<\left|q\right|<R_{0}^{-1},

where
R_{0}=0.35320...
is the real root of the cubic equation

R^{3}+2R^{2}+2R-1=0.

This provides the lower estimate
R>R_{0}
for the radius of convergence of the corresponding
q
-convergents.

In Section
4
, we consider the
q
-version of the silver ratio
\left[\sqrt{2}\right]_{q}

and the related “Pell polynomials”.
We prove that the roots of these polynomials belong to the annulus

R_{1}<\left|q\right|<R_{1}^{-1}

where
R_{1}=0.43542...
is the smallest positive root of the equation

R^{4}-2R^{3}-2R+1=0.

Finally, in Section
5
we prove our Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

2.
Definition and explicit formulas for
q
-reals

We start with recalling an axiomatic definition of
q
-deformed rational numbers.
For more equivalent definitions see
[
13
,
15
]
.

2.1.
An axiomatic definition

Recall that every rational can be obtained from
0
, by applying a sequence of the operations

x\mapsto{}x+1
and
x\mapsto{}-\frac{1}{x}
.

The following two recurrences (see
[
9
]
) suffice for calculation of
q
-rationals.

Definition 2.1
.

The
q
-deformation sends every rational number
x=\frac{r}{s}
to a rational function in
q

x\longmapsto\left[x\right]_{q}=\frac{\mathcal{R}(q)}{\mathcal{S}(q)},

in such a way that the following two recurrent formulas are satisfied

(2.3)

\left[x+1\right]_{q}=q\left[x\right]_{q}+1,\qquad\qquad\left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{q}=-\frac{1}{q\left[x\right]_{q}}.

Recurrences (
2.3
) determine the
q
-deformation of a rational in a unique way, starting from the
“initial” trivial deformation
\left[0\right]_{q}=0
; see
[
15
]
.

Example 2.2
.

The following examples are easy to obtain applying (
2.3
).

(a) The
q
-deformation of integers corresponds to the standard formulas of Euler and Gauss.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one has

\left[n\right]_{q}=1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{n-1},\qquad\qquad\left[-n\right]_{q}=-q^{-n}-q^{1-n}-q^{2-n}-\cdots-q^{-1}.

Both cases can be written as
\left[x\right]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{x}}{1-x}
, where
x
is integer.

(b)
Already the simplest example of
\frac{1}{2}
shows that the
q
-deformation is not the quotient
of the
q
-deformed integers appearing in numerator and denominator:

\left[\frac{1}{2}\right]_{q}=\frac{q}{1+q},\qquad\qquad\left[-\frac{1}{2}\right]_{q}=-\frac{1}{q\left(1+q\right)}.

The above expression
\left[x\right]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{x}}{1-x}
is no longer true.

(c)
The next examples

\left[\frac{5}{2}\right]_{q}=\frac{1+2q+q^{2}+q^{3}}{1+q},\qquad\qquad\left[\frac{5}{3}\right]_{q}=\frac{1+q+2q^{2}+q^{3}}{1+q+q^{2}}

illustrate the fact
that the numerator and denominator in (
1.1
) depend simultaneously on
r
and
s
.
Indeed, the “quantized
5
” in the numerator depends on the denominator.

Remark 2.3
.

In the case of
q
-rationals, one also has the formula for the negation and inverse:

(2.4)

\left[-x\right]_{q}=-q^{-1}\left[x\right]_{q^{-1}},\qquad\qquad\left[\frac{1}{x}\right]_{q}=\frac{1}{\left[x\right]_{q^{-1}}}.

2.2.
The action and a central extension of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

We briefly mention here a more conceptual way of understanding Definition
2.1
.
This observation will not be used in the sequel, and will be part of a separate work.

Recurrences (
2.3
) can be reformulated as a statement that
“
q
-deformation commutes with
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
-action”.
Indeed, recurrences (
2.3
) define an
action of the modular group
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
on
q
-deformed rationals.
This action is given by fractional-linear transformations and is generated by the matrices

(2.5)

T_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}q&1\\[4.0pt]
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\quad S_{q}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\[4.0pt]
q&0\end{pmatrix}

which are
q
-deformations of the standard generators,
T,S
, of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})

corresponding to
q=1
in (
2.5
).

The relations
S^{2}=\mathrm{Id}
and
(TS)^{3}=\mathrm{Id}
, defining
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
, become

S_{q}^{2}=q\,\mathrm{Id},\qquad\qquad\left(T_{q}S_{q}\right)^{3}=q^{3}\mathrm{Id},

The matrices
T_{q},S_{q}
generate an interesting non-trivial
central extension
of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
,
which is different from the braid group
B_{3}
, yet contains it as a subgroup.
The centre,
\{q^{n}\mathrm{Id}\;|\;n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, of the extended modular group
acts trivially of
q
-deformed rationals,
so that one still has the well-defined action of
\mathrm{PSL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
.

Remark 2.4
.

(a)
Let us stress on the fact that emergence of a central extension of the symmetry group
is a usual situation in geometry; see
[
8
]
.
However, the centre of the extended group usually acts trivially on quantized objects.

(b)
The matrix
S_{q}
arose in the context of quantum groups; see
[
3
]
,
while
T_{q}
can be viewed as a “standard” matrix connected to quantum integers.
However, we did not find in the literature simultaneous appearance of
T_{q}
and
S_{q}
.

2.3.
q
-deformed continued fractions

Every rational number
\frac{r}{s}>0
, where
r,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
are coprime,
has a standard finite continued fraction expansion

\frac{r}{s}\quad=\quad a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{a_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots+\cfrac{1}{a_{2m}}}},

where
a_{i}\geq 1
(except for
a_{1}\geq 0
).
It is usually denoted by
\frac{r}{s}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{2m}]
.
Note that, choosing an even number of coefficients, one
removes the ambiguity
[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n},1]=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}+1]
and makes the expansion unique.

There is also a unique continued fraction expansion
with minus signs, often called the Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction:

\frac{r}{s}\quad=\quad c_{1}-\cfrac{1}{c_{2}-\cfrac{1}{\ddots-\cfrac{1}{c_{N}}}},

where
c_{j}\geq 2
(except for
c_{1}\geq 1
).
The notation used by Hirzebruch is
\frac{r}{s}=\llbracket{}c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\rrbracket{}
.
The coefficients
a_{i}
and
c_{j}
of the above expansions
are connected by the Hirzebruch
formula; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
12
]
and Section
5.2
.

Definition 2.5
.

(a)
The
q
-deformed regular continued fraction is defined by

(2.6)

[a_{1},\ldots,a_{2m}]_{q}:=[a_{1}]_{q}+\cfrac{q^{a_{1}}}{[a_{2}]_{q^{-1}}+\cfrac{q^{-a_{2}}}{[a_{3}]_{q}+\cfrac{q^{a_{3}}}{[a_{4}]_{q^{-1}}+\cfrac{q^{-a_{4}}}{\cfrac{\ddots}{[a_{2m-1}]_{q}+\cfrac{q^{a_{2m-1}}}{[a_{2m}]_{q^{-1}}}}}}}}

where
[a]_{q}
is the Euler
q
-integer.

(b)
The
q
-deformed Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction is

(2.7)

\llbracket{}c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\rrbracket_{q}:=[c_{1}]_{q}-\cfrac{q^{c_{1}-1}}{[c_{2}]_{q}-\cfrac{q^{c_{2}-1}}{\ddots\cfrac{\ddots}{[c_{N-1}]_{q}-\cfrac{q^{c_{N-1}-1}}{[c_{N}]_{q}}}}}

For every rational
\frac{r}{s}
written in two different ways:

\frac{r}{s}=[a_{1},\ldots,a_{2m}]=\llbracket{}c_{1},\ldots,c_{N}\rrbracket{},

the rational functions (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) coincide,
and also coincide with the rational function
\left[\frac{r}{s}\right]_{q}

provided by Definition
2.1
; see
[
13
]
.

2.4.
Stabilization phenomenon,
q
-irrationals

The notion of
q
-deformed rational
was extended to irrational numbers in
[
14
]
.
Let
x\geq 1
be an irrational number, and choose a sequence of rationals
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, converging to
x
.
Consider the corresponding sequence of
q
-rationals

\left[x_{1}\right]_{q},\left[x_{2}\right]_{q},\ldots

It turns out that this sequence of rational functions also converge, but in the sense of formal power series.

Consider the Taylor expansions at
q=0
of the rational functions
\left[x_{n}\right]_{q}
, that,
abusing notation, we also denote by
\left[x_{n}\right]_{q}
:

\left[x_{n}\right]_{q}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\varkappa_{n,k}\,q^{k}.

One has the following stabilization property.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

(i) For every
k\geq 0
, the coefficients
\varkappa_{n,k}
of the Taylor series
of the functions
\left[x_{n}\right]_{q}
stabilize, as
n
grows.

(ii) The limit coefficients,
\varkappa_{k}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\varkappa_{n,k}
,
do not depend on the choice of the sequence of rationals, but only on the irrational number
x
.

The
q
-deformation,
\left[x\right]_{q}
, is the limit power series in
q
,
which has the form (
1.2
) (i.e., the coefficient of degree
0
is equal to
1
).
Furthermore, the recurrence (
2.3
) also holds for the series
\left[x\right]_{q}
,
and allows one to extend the
q
-deformation to the case of
x<1
.
In particular, if
x
is negative, the resulting series in
q
is a
Laurent series
(with integer coefficients):

\left[x\right]_{q}=-q^{-N}+\varkappa_{1-N}\,q^{1-N}+\varkappa_{2-N}\,q^{2-N}+\cdots

where
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
-N\leq{}x<1-N
.

Remark 2.6
.

Let us mention that the stabilization phenomenon fails when a sequence of rationals
(x_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, converges to
another rational, cf.
[
14
,
2
]
.

3.
Convergence radius of
q
-golden ratio and roots of the Fibonacci polynomials

The simplest example of
q
-irrational is the
q
-deformation of the celebrated golden ratio,

\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
.
The series
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
is obtained as stabilized Taylor series of the
q
-deformed quotients of the
consecutive Fibonacci numbers
\left[\frac{F_{n+1}}{F_{n}}\right]_{q}
(see
[
13
,
14
]
).
We call the polynomials in the numerator and the denominator of these rational functions
the
Fibonacci polynomials
.

We prove that the radius of convergence of
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
is
R_{\star}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
,
and that all roots of the Fibonacci polynomials
belong to the annulus bounded by the circles
with the radius
R_{\star}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}
and
R_{\star}^{-1}=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
.
This result is in an accordance with Conjecture
1.1
.

3.1.
The
q
-deformed golden ratio

The
q
-deformation of the golden ratio,

\varphi=\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
,
was considered in
[
14
]
.
The series
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
can be written
as an infinite continued fraction:

(3.8)

\left[\varphi\right]_{q}=1+\cfrac{q^{2}}{q+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{q^{2}}{q+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}}\;=\;1+\cfrac{1}{q^{-1}+\cfrac{1}{q^{2}+\cfrac{1}{q^{-3}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}}

The series starts as follows

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left[\varphi\right]_{q}&=&1+q^{2}-q^{3}+2q^{4}-4q^{5}+8q^{6}-17q^{7}+37q^{8}-82q^{9}+185q^{10}\\[6.0pt]
&&-423q^{11}+978q^{12}-2283q^{13}+5373q^{14}-12735q^{15}+30372q^{16}\\[6.0pt]
&&-72832q^{17}+175502q^{18}-424748q^{19}+1032004q^{20}\cdots\end{array}

The sequence of coefficients in
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
coincides (up to the alternating sign)
with the remarkable sequence A004148 of
[
16
]
called the “generalized Catalan numbers”.

The series
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
is a solution of the functional equation

(3.9)

q\,X^{2}=\left(q^{2}+q-1\right)X+1,

which can be deduced from (
3.8
).

Proposition 3.1
.

The radius of convergence of the series
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
is equal to
R_{\star}
.

Proof.

It follows from (
5.27
), that
the generating function of
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
can therefore be written in radicals:

(3.10)

\mathrm{GF}_{\left[\varphi\right]_{q}}=\frac{q^{2}+q-1+\sqrt{(q^{2}+3q+1)(q^{2}-q+1)}}{2q},

and the series
\left[\varphi\right]_{q}
is the Taylor expansion of
\mathrm{GF}_{\left[\varphi\right]_{q}}
at
q=0
.
The number
R_{\star}
is the modulus of the smallest (i.e., closest to
0
) root of the polynomials under the radical in (
3.10
).
Indeed,

q^{2}+3q+1=\left(q+R_{\star}\right)\left(q+R_{\star}^{-1}\right),

and therefore the Taylor series of (
3.10
) converges for
|q|<R_{\star}
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Observe that formula (
3.8
) has a certain similarity
with the celebrated Rogers-Ramanujan continued fraction

R(q)=1+\cfrac{1}{1+\cfrac{q}{1+\cfrac{q^{2}}{1+\cfrac{q^{3}}{\ddots}}}}

but the deformation (
3.8
) has very different properties.

3.2.
The Fibonacci polynomials

The most natural choice of a sequence of rationals converging to
\varphi
is related to a
quite remarkable and well-known sequence of polynomials.

Let
F_{n}
be the
n^{\hbox{th}}
Fibonacci number,
the sequence of rationals
\frac{F_{n+1}}{F_{n}}
converges to
\varphi
.
Quantizing this sequence, one obtains a sequence of rational functions

(3.11)

\left[\frac{F_{n+1}}{F_{n}}\right]_{q}=:\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n+1}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}.

The polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n+1}(q)
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)

in the numerator and denominator of (
3.11
) are
q
-deformations of the Fibonacci numbers,
considered in
[
13
]
.

Both sequences of polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)
are of degree
n-2
(for
n\geq 2
)
and are mirror of each other:

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)=q^{n-2}\mathcal{F}_{n}(q^{-1}).

The polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
can be calculated recursively.
It will be convenient to separate the sequence of polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
into two subsequences,
with even
n
and odd
n
.
Both of these sequences satisfy the same recurrence,
which is a
q
-analogue of the classical recurrence

F_{n+2}=3\,F_{n}-F_{n-2}

for the Fibonacci numbers.

Proposition 3.3
.

The polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
in the denominator of (
3.11
)
are determined by the recurrence

(3.12)

\mathcal{F}_{n+2}(q)=[3]_{q}\,\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)-q^{2}\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q),

where
[3]_{q}=1+q+q^{2}
, and the initial conditions

\left(\mathcal{F}_{0}(q)=1,\;\mathcal{F}_{2}(q)=1+q\right)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\left(\mathcal{F}_{1}(q)=1,\;\mathcal{F}_{3}(q)=1+q+q^{2}\right).

Proof.

It was shown in
[
13
]
that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{F}_{2\ell+1}&=&q\mathcal{F}_{2\ell}+\mathcal{F}_{2\ell-1},\\[4.0pt]
\mathcal{F}_{2\ell+2}&=&\mathcal{F}_{2\ell+1}+q^{2}\mathcal{F}_{2\ell},\end{array}

Recurrence (
3.12
) follows readily.
∎

The coefficients of the polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)
, with
n\geq 1
, form the triangles

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccc}1\\
1&1\\
1&1&1\\
1&2&1&1\\
1&2&2&2&1\\
1&3&3&3&2&1\\
1&3&4&5&4&3&1\\
\cdots\end{array}\qquad\qquad\begin{array}[]{rcccccccc}&&&&&&1\\

3.3.
Roots of the Fibonacci polynomials

Our next goal is to obtain the bounds for the absolute values of roots of the Fibonacci polynomials.
For this we will use the following classical Rouché theorem (see
[
4
,
19
]
).

Theorem

(Rouché)
.

Let
f
and
g
be two functions of one complex variable
q
, holomorphic inside a disc
D

and continuous on the bound
\partial{}D
.
Suppose that
\left|f(q)\right|>\left|g(q)\right|
on
\partial{}D
, then
f
and
f+g
have the same number of zeros inside
D
.

Let
R_{0}=0.35320...
be the positive root of the equation

(3.13)

R^{3}+2R^{2}+2R-1=0.

Let
D_{0}
be the disc and
C_{0}
the circle with radius
R_{0}
:

D_{0}=\left\{q\in\mathbb{C}\;,|q|\leq R_{0}\right\},\qquad\qquad C_{0}=\left\{q\in\mathbb{C}\;,|q|=R_{0}\right\}.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the roots of the Fibonacci polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)

belong to the annulus

R_{0}<\left|q\right|<R_{0}^{-1}.

Proof.

We need the following useful lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

For every positive integer
n
one has

(3.14)

\left|{\left[n\right]_{q}}\right|\geq\frac{1-R^{n}}{{1+R}},

on a circle
C_{R}=\{q\in\mathbb{C}:|q|=R\}
of any radius
R
.

Proof.

Indeed, the modulus of the polynomial

\left[c\right]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{n}}{1-q}

restricted to
C_{R}
is of the form

\left|\left[n\right]_{q}\right|_{C_{R}}^{2}=\frac{(1-R^{n}e^{in\theta})(1-R^{n}e^{-in\theta})}{(1-Re^{i\theta})(1-Re^{-i\theta})}=\frac{1-2R^{n}\cos(2n\theta)+R^{2n}}{1-2R\cos(\theta)+R^{2}},

where
\theta
is the standard parameter on the circle.
Observe that the numerator of this fraction is greater
than the numerator of the square of the right-hand-side of (
3.14
),
while the denominator is smaller.
The lemma follows.
∎

Recurrence (
3.12
) can be rewritten as follows

(3.15)

\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n+2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}=[3]_{q}-q^{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}.

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that
\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}<\frac{1}{R_{0}}
,
then
\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n+2}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}<\frac{1}{R_{0}}
.

Proof.

Using the recurrence (
3.15
), one has

\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n+2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{R_{0}}}\geq\left|\left[3\right]_{q}\right|_{C_{0}}-R_{0}^{2}\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}.

Lemma
3.6
and the assumption imply that

\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n+2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}>\frac{1-R_{0}^{3}}{{1+R_{0}}}-R_{0}.

Since due to the definition of
R_{0}
we have

\frac{1-R_{0}^{3}}{{1+R_{0}}}-R_{0}=R_{0},

this implies the claim.
∎

Observe that, for
n=2
we have
\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{2}(q)}=\frac{1}{\left[2\right]_{q}}
,
and
\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{3}(q)}=\frac{1}{\left[3\right]_{q}}
.
Using, once again, Lemma
3.6
, we have
\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{0}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{2}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}<\frac{1}{R_{0}}

and
\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{1}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{3}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}<\frac{1}{R_{0}}
.
From Lemma
3.7
we conclude by induction that
\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}<\frac{1}{R_{0}}
for all
n\geq 2.

In particular, we have that

(3.16)

\left|[3]_{q}\right|_{C_{0}}\geq\frac{1-R_{0}^{3}}{{1+R_{0}}}>R_{0}>R_{0}^{2}\left|\frac{\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}.

Let us assume now that
\mathcal{F}_{n-2}(q)
and
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
have no zeros inside
D_{0}
. Since the cyclotomic polynomial
[3]_{q}=q^{2}+q+1
has no roots inside
D_{0}
, then from (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) by
the Rouché theorem,
we conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{n+2}(q)
also has no roots inside
D_{0}
.
The induction completes the proof of the statement that the polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
have no zeros inside
D_{0}
.

The proof of the
same statement for the polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)
is similar, since
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)
also satisfy (
3.12
).
This follows from the recurrent formulas

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2\ell+1}&=&\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2\ell}+q^{2}\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2\ell-1},\\[4.0pt]
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2\ell+2}&=&q\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2\ell+1}+\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{2\ell},\end{array}

proved in
[
13
]
.

Finally, since the polynomials
\mathcal{F}_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}(q)
are mirror of each other,
this implies that these polynomials have no roots outside the disc with the radius
R_{0}^{-1}
.
Theorem
3.1
is proved.
∎

The computer calculations shows that the roots of the Fibonacci polynomials
actually lie in the smaller annulus

R_{\star}<\left|q\right|<R_{\star}^{-1},

but we cannot prove this yet.
This was claimed in the earlier preprint version of this work (and repeated in
[
17
]
), but our proof was incorrect.

4.
\left[\sqrt{2}\right]_{q}
and roots of the Pell polynomials

The second example we consider is
\sqrt{2}
, known also as silver ratio.
The slight modification,
\sqrt{2}+1
, has some tiny advantages, so we will often work with it;
note that
\sqrt{2}+1
is approximated by the quotients of consecutive
Pell numbers

\frac{P_{n+1}}{P_{n}}=\underbrace{\left[2,2,\ldots,2\right]}_{n}
.

We show that the convergence radius of the series
\left[\sqrt{2}\right]_{q}
and
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}
is equal to
R_{\sqrt{2}}:=R(\sqrt{2})
,

(4.17)

R_{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{1+\sqrt{2}-\sqrt{2\sqrt{2}-1}}{2}\quad\approx 0.53101,

and that the roots of the polynomials in the numerators and
denominators of
\left[\frac{P_{n+1}}{P_{n}}\right]_{q}

belong to the annulus bounded by the circles with radius
R_{1}
and
R_{1}^{-1}
, with
R_{1}\approx 0.43542
.

4.1.
The series
\left[\sqrt{2}\right]_{q}
and
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}

These series are related by

\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}=q\left[\sqrt{2}\right]_{q}+1
(cf. (
2.3
)) and obviously have the same convergence radius;
we prefer to perform the calculations for
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}
.

The
q
-deformation
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}
is given by the infinite
2
-periodic continued fraction

(4.18)

\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}=1+q+\cfrac{q^{4}}{q+q^{2}+\cfrac{1}{1+q+\cfrac{q^{4}}{q+q^{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}}

see
[
14
]
.
This is the
q
-deformed classical continued fraction expansion
\sqrt{2}+1=\left[2,2,2,2,\ldots\right]
.

The series
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}
satisfies the following functional equation:

(4.19)

qX^{2}-\left(q^{3}+2q-1\right)X-1=0,

readily obtained from (
4.18
), and can be calculated from it recursively:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}&=&1+q+q^{4}-2q^{6}+q^{7}+4q^{8}-5q^{9}-7q^{10}+18q^{11}+7q^{12}-55q^{13}+18q^{14}\\[4.0pt]
&&+146q^{15}-155q^{16}-322q^{17}+692q^{18}+476q^{19}-2446q^{20}+307q^{21}\\[4.0pt]
&&+7322q^{22}-6276q^{23}-18277q^{24}+33061q^{25}+33376q^{26}-129238q^{27}-10899q^{28}\cdots\end{array}

see Sequence A337589 of
[
16
]
for the coefficients of this series.

Proposition 4.1
.

The radius of convergence of the series
\left[\sqrt{2}\right]_{q}
and
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}
is equal to
R_{\sqrt{2}}
.

Proof.

The generating function of the series can be deduced from (
4.19
):

\mathrm{GF}_{\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}}=\frac{q^{3}+2q-1+\sqrt{(q^{4}+q^{3}+4q^{2}+q+1)(q^{2}-q+1)}}{2q}.

The radius (
4.17
) is equal to the modulus of the root of the polynomial

q^{4}+q^{3}+4q^{2}+q+1
closest to zero.
∎

4.2.
The Pell polynomials

The irrational
\left[\sqrt{2}+1\right]_{q}
can be approximated by the quotient of the consecutive Pell numbers:

\frac{P_{n+1}}{P_{n}}=\underbrace{\left[2,2,\ldots,2\right]}_{n}
.
We define the
Pell polynomials
via

\left[\frac{P_{n+1}}{P_{n}}\right]_{q}=:\frac{\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n+1}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}.

The polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(q)
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
are of degree
2n-3
, and, similarly to the Fibonacci polynomials,
are the mirrors of each other:
q^{2n-3}\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(q^{-1})=\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
are determined by the recurrence

(4.20)

\mathcal{P}_{n+2}={4\choose 2}_{q}\mathcal{P}_{n}-q^{4}\mathcal{P}_{n-2},

where
{4\choose 2}_{q}=1+q+2q^{2}+q^{3}+q^{4}
is the Gaussian
q
-binomial, and the initial conditions

\left(\mathcal{P}_{0}(q)=0,\;\mathcal{P}_{2}(q)=1+q\right)\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\left(\mathcal{P}_{-1}(q)=\mathcal{P}_{1}(q)=1\right).

Proof.

Recurrence (
4.20
) follows from the formulas

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{P}_{2\ell+1}&=&\left(q+q^{2}\right)\mathcal{P}_{2\ell}+\mathcal{P}_{2\ell-1},\\[4.0pt]
\mathcal{P}_{2\ell+2}&=&\left(1+q\right)\mathcal{P}_{2\ell+1}+q^{4}\,\mathcal{P}_{2\ell},\end{array}

proved in
[
13
]
.
∎

The coefficients of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
with
n\geq 1
form a triangular sequence

\begin{array}[]{rccccccccccc}1\\
1&1\\
1&1&2&1\\
1&2&3&3&2&1\\
1&2&5&6&6&5&3&1\\
1&3&7&11&13&13&11&7&3&1\\
1&3&9&16&24&29&29&25&18&10&4&1\\
\cdots\end{array}

(see Sequence A323670 of
[
16
]
).

Example 4.3
.

One has

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left[\frac{5}{2}\right]_{q}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1+2q+q^{2}+q^{3}}{1+q+q^{2}},\\[12.0pt]
\left[\frac{12}{5}\right]_{q}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1+2q+3q^{2}+3q^{3}+2q^{4}+q^{5}}{1+q+2q^{2}+q^{3}},\\[12.0pt]
\left[\frac{29}{12}\right]_{q}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1+3q+5q^{2}+6q^{3}+6q^{4}+5q^{5}+2q^{6}+q^{7}}{1+2q+3q^{2}+3q^{3}+2q^{4}+q^{5}},\\[12.0pt]
\left[\frac{70}{29}\right]_{q}&=&\displaystyle\frac{1+3q+7q^{2}+11q^{3}+13q^{4}+13q^{5}+11q^{6}+7q^{7}+3q^{8}+q^{9}}{1+2q+5q^{2}+6q^{3}+6q^{4}+5q^{5}+3q^{6}+q^{7}},\\

4.3.
Roots of the Pell polynomials

Let
R_{1}=0.43542...
be the smallest positive root of the equation

(4.21)

R^{4}-2R^{3}-2R+1=0.

Let
D_{1}
be the disc and
C_{1}
the circle with radius
R_{1}
:

D_{1}=\left\{q\in\mathbb{C}\;,|q|\leq R_{1}\right\},\qquad\qquad C_{1}=\left\{q\in\mathbb{C}\;,|q|=R_{1}\right\}.

Theorem 4.1
.

The roots of the Pell polynomials
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(q)
belong to the annulus

R_{1}<\left|q\right|<R_{1}^{-1}.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the Fibonacci case.
Recurrence (
4.20
) leads to

(4.22)

\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n+2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}={4\choose 2}_{q}-q^{4}\,\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)},

where
{4\choose 2}_{q}=(q^{2}+1)(q^{2}+q+1).

On the circle
C_{R}
of radius
R
we have
|1+q^{2}|\geq 1-R^{2}

and
|q^{2}+q+1|\geq\frac{1-R^{3}}{1+R}
as before.
Thus on
C_{R}
we have

(4.23)

\left|{4\choose 2}_{q}\right|\geq\frac{(1-R^{3})(1-R^{2})}{1+R}=(1-R^{3})(1-R).

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume that
\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{1}}<\frac{1}{R_{1}}
,
then
\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n+2}(q)}\right|_{C_{1}}<\frac{1}{R_{1}}
.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.
It follows from (
4.22
) and (
4.23
) that

\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n+2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{1}}\geq\left|{4\choose 2}_{q}\right|_{C_{1}}-R_{1}^{4}\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{1}}>(1-R_{1}^{3})(1-R_{1})-R_{1}^{3}=R_{1},

since
(1-R_{1}^{3})(1-R_{1})-R_{1}^{3}-R_{1}=R_{1}^{4}-2R_{1}^{3}-2R_{1}+1=0
by choice of
R_{1}.

∎

From the lemma by induction we can conclude that
\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{0}}<\frac{1}{R_{1}}

and thus

(4.24)

\left|{4\choose 2}_{q}\right|_{C_{1}}>(1-R_{1}^{3})(1-R_{1})=R_{1}^{3}+R_{1}>R_{1}^{4}\left|\frac{\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(q)}{\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{1}}.

Assume that
\mathcal{P}_{n-2}(q)
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
have no zeros inside
C_{1}
. Since
{4\choose 2}_{q}=(q^{2}+1)(q^{2}+q+1)
has no roots inside
D_{1}
, then due to (
4.24
) we can apply Rouché theorem
to conclude that
\mathcal{P}_{n+2}(q)
also has no roots inside
D_{1}
. The induction completes the proof
of this statement for all
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q).

The proof that the polynomials
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(q)
have no
roots inside
D_{1}
is analogous since
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(q)
also satisfy (
4.20
).
Theorem
4.1
then follows from the fact that
\mathcal{P}_{n}(q)
and
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{n}(q)
are mirrors of each other.
∎

Proof of Theorems
1.2

and

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Let
x
be a rational, choose the rational approximations of
x

by the convergents of the Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction:

\frac{r_{n}}{s_{n}}=\llbracket{}c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots,c_{n}\rrbracket{}
, see Section
2.3
,
and consider the
q
-deformation

(5.25)

\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}:=\left[\frac{r_{n}}{s_{n}}\right]_{q}.

Recall that the coefficients of the Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction are at least
2
.

Let
R_{2}=3-2\sqrt{2}=(\sqrt{2}-1)^{2}
and

C_{2}
and
D_{2}
be the circle and the disc with radius
R_{2}
, respectively.
To prove that the radius
R(x)
of convergence of
[x]_{q}
is larger than
R_{2}
, it would be enough to prove that for all
n
the polynomial
\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)
in the denominator of (
5.25
)
has no roots inside
D_{2}
.

We use the same strategy as before based on the recurrence relations and Rouché theorem.
Formula (
2.7
) implies that the polynomial
\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)
satisfy the recurrence

(5.26)

\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)=\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}\,\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)-q^{c_{n}-1}\,\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q),

with the initial values
\mathcal{S}_{0}(q)=0
and
\mathcal{S}_{1}(q)=1
, that we rewrite as follows

(5.27)

\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}=\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}\,-\,q^{c_{n}-1}\,\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}.

By the Rouché theorem,
it suffices to prove that, for every
n
,
the polynomial
\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}=\frac{\;1-q^{c_{n+1}}}{{1-q}}

dominates the second summand of the right-hand-side of (
5.27
),
when restricted on the circle
C_{2}
:

(5.28)

\left|{\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}}\right|_{C_{2}}\;>\;{R_{2}}^{\,c_{n}-1}\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{2}}.

Since for every positive integer
c
the polynomial
\left[c\right]_{q}
has no roots in
D_{2}
, we will then argue by induction that

\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)
also has no roots in
D_{2}
.

To prove (
5.28
), we will need the following inductive step.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{2}}<\frac{1}{{R_{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}
,
then
\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)}\right|_{C_{2}}<\frac{1}{{R_{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}}
.

Proof.

Using the recurrence (
5.27
), one has

\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{2}}\geq\left|\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}\right|_{C_{2}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-1}\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{2}}.

Using Lemma
3.6
and inductive assumption we have

\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{2}}>\frac{1-{R_{2}}^{c_{n+1}}}{{1+{R_{2}}}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{3}{2}}.

We need to show that

\frac{1-{R_{2}}^{c_{n+1}}}{{1+{R_{2}}}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{3}{2}}\geq{R_{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}},

or equivalently

1-{R_{2}}^{c_{n+1}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{1}{2}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{3}{2}}-{R_{2}}^{\frac{3}{2}}-{R_{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0.

Since
c_{i}\geq 2
it is enough to check that for
R=R_{2}

1-R^{2}-2R^{\frac{3}{2}}-2R^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0.

But
1-R^{2}-2R^{\frac{3}{2}}-2R^{\frac{1}{2}}=(1+R)(1-R-R^{\frac{1}{2}})=0
when

R^{\frac{1}{2}}=\sqrt{2}-1=R_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

∎

Lemmas
3.6
and
5.1
imply that the inequality (
5.28
) is guaranteed if

\frac{1-{R_{2}}^{c_{n+1}}}{{1+{R_{2}}}}\geq{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{3}{2}},

which is equivalent to

1-{R_{2}}^{c_{n+1}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{1}{2}}-{R_{2}}^{c_{n}-\frac{3}{2}}\geq 0.

Using once again
c_{i}\geq 2
it is enough to check that

1-R_{2}^{2}-R_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}-R_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq 0,

which is obvious since
1-R_{2}^{2}-R_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}-R_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}=R_{2}^{\frac{3}{2}}+R_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

5.2.
A special class of continued fractions

Now we prove Theorem
1.3
, which claims that if the coefficients of the Hirzebruch-Jung
continued fraction expansion a rational number
x=\llbracket{}c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\ldots,c_{N}\rrbracket{}

satisfies

(5.29)

c_{i}\geq 4,

then the radius of convergence of
\left[x\right]_{q}
is greater than
R_{\star}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Let us reformulate the inequality (
5.29
) in terms of the coefficients of the
regular continued fraction expansion
x=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\ldots]
.
Recall the formula
[
6
]
expressing the coefficients of the Hirzebruch-Jung continued fraction:

x=\llbracket{}a_{1}+1,\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{a_{2}-1},\,a_{3}+2,\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{a_{4}-1},\,a_{5}+2,\ldots,a_{2n-1}+2,\underbrace{2,\ldots,2}_{a_{2n}-1},\ldots\rrbracket

In other words, the coefficients with odd indices,
a_{2m-1}
become
a_{2m-1}+2

(except for
a_{1}
that produces
a_{1}+1
in the Hirzebruch-Jung
continued fraction), and coefficients with even indices,
a_{2m}
produce an
(a_{2m}-1)
-tuple of
2
’s.
Inequality (
5.29
) becomes:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}a_{2m-1}&\geq&2,\\[4.0pt]
a_{2m}&=&1,\end{array}\right.

starting from some
N
.

5.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.3

The proof goes along the same lines as that of Theorem
1.2
.
Let
C_{\star}
be the circle with radius
R_{\star}
.
It suffices to prove that for every
n
the polynomial
\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)
in (
5.25
) has no roots inside
C_{\star}
.

We need to prove that for every
n

the polynomial
\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}=\frac{1-q^{c_{n+1}}}{{1-q}}

dominates the second summand of the right-hand-side of the recursion (
5.27
),
when restricted on the circle
C_{\star}
:

(5.30)

\left|{\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}}\right|_{C_{\star}}\;>\;R_{\star}^{\,c_{n}-1}\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{\star}}.

We argue by induction and will need the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

If
\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{\star}}<\frac{1}{R_{\star}}
, then

\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)}\right|_{C_{\star}}<\frac{1}{R_{\star}}
.

Proof.

Using (
5.27
) and lemma
3.6
we have

\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n+1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{\star}}>\frac{1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}}{1+R_{\star}}\,-\,R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2},

which we claim to be larger than
R_{\star}.

Indeed,

\frac{1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}}{1+R_{\star}}\,-\,R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}-R_{\star}=\frac{1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}-R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}-R_{\star}^{c_{n}-1}-R_{\star}-R_{\star}^{2}}{1+R_{\star}}.

Since
c_{i}\geq 4
for all
i
, we have

\frac{1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}-R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}-R_{\star}^{c_{n}-1}-R_{\star}-R_{\star}^{2}}{1+R_{\star}}\geq\frac{1-R_{\star}^{4}-R_{\star}^{3}-2R_{\star}^{2}-R_{\star}}{1+R_{\star}}=\frac{14-36R_{\star}}{1+R_{\star}},

because
R_{\star}^{2}=3R_{\star}-1
,
and so
R_{\star}^{3}=8R_{\star}-3
, and
R_{\star}^{4}=21R_{\star}-8
.
One can check that
14-36R_{\star}>0
,
which implies the lemma.
∎

From this lemma by induction we have that for all
n

R_{\star}^{c_{n}-1}\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{\star}}<{}R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}.

We claim that

R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}<\frac{1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}}{{1+R_{\star}}}.

Indeed, under our assumptions

1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}-R_{\star}^{c_{n}-1}-R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}>1-R_{\star}^{4}-R_{\star}^{3}-R_{\star}^{2},

which is positive. This means that

\left|{\left[c_{n+1}\right]_{q}}\right|_{C_{\star}}\;\geq\;\frac{1-R_{\star}^{c_{n+1}}}{{1+R_{\star}}}\;>\;R_{\star}^{c_{n}-2}>R_{\star}^{\,c_{n}-1}\left|\frac{\mathcal{S}_{n-1}(q)}{\mathcal{S}_{n}(q)}\right|_{C_{\star}},

and we can apply Rouché theorem to complete the proof.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Note that using the adopted approach
we cannot improve the assumption (
5.29
).
Indeed, assuming
c_{i}\geq 3
in Lemma
5.2
,
leads in the proof to the quantity
3-8R_{\star}
, which is negative.

6.
Miscellaneous experiments

Multiple computer experiments show that the situation in the
q
-deformed case
is very different from classical Markov theory
[
1
]
.

Example 6.1
.

For
\sqrt{3}
, one has:
\sqrt{3}=[1,\overline{1,2}]=\llbracket{}2,\overline{4}\rrbracket{}
.
The generating function of the series
\left[\sqrt{3}\right]_{q}
is

\mathrm{GF}_{\left[\sqrt{3}\right]_{q}}=\frac{q^{3}+q^{2}-q-1+\sqrt{q^{6}+2q^{5}+3q^{4}+3q^{2}+2q+1}}{2q^{2}},

see
[
14
]
.
The absolute value of the minimal root of the polynomial under the radical is

R_{\sqrt{3}}:=R(\sqrt{3})\approx 0.527756\ldots

which is between
R_{\star}
and
R_{\sqrt{2}}
:

R_{\star}<R_{\sqrt{3}}<R_{\sqrt{2}}.

This example demonstrates, that, unlike classical Markov theory
[
1
]
, the series corresponding to
\sqrt{2}

“converges better” than that of
\sqrt{3}
.

Let us also give another interesting example, which is the third “badly approximated” number
in Markov theory, after
\varphi
and the ”silver ratio”
\sqrt{2}
.

Example 6.2
.

The number

\alpha=\frac{9+\sqrt{221}}{10}=[\overline{2,2,1,1}]
, sometimes called “bronze ratio”, is the third most irrational number
[
1
]
.
In this case, the radius of convergence can be calculated explicitly in radicals.
More precisely, the radius of convergence of
\left[\frac{9+\sqrt{221}}{10}\right]_{q}
is

R_{\mathrm{bronze}}:=R(\alpha)=\frac{1+\sqrt{13}-\sqrt{2\left(\sqrt{13}-1\right)}}{4}\quad\approx 0.58069\ldots

Indeed, a direct computation gives

\textstyle\left[\frac{9+\sqrt{221}}{10}\right]_{q}=\frac{q^{6}+2q^{5}+3q^{4}+3q^{3}+q^{2}-1+\sqrt{(q^{4}+3q^{3}+5q^{2}+3q+1)(q^{6}+2q^{5}+3q^{4}+5q^{3}+3q^{2}+2q+1)(q^{2}-q+1)}}{2q(q^{3}+2q^{2}+q+1)}

Note that
221=13\cdot 17
, the factors under the radical are
q
-versions of these numbers.
Quite remarkably, the polynomial under the radical is a palindrome polynomial
(for a general result; see
[
9
]
).
The radius of convergence is equal to the absolute value of the minimal root of the polynomial under the radical,
which can be found explicitly.

We wonder if for other quadratic irrationals the radius of convergence
is an algebraic number of degree
2^{n}
(like in the theory of ruler-and-compass construction)
as it was for
\varphi,\sqrt{2}
, and for the above example,
but we have no explicit formulas in general.
