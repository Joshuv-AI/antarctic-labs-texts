Local Ward identities for collective excitations in fermionic systems with spontaneously broken symmetries
Pietro M. Bonetti

Abstract
We derive Ward identities for fermionic systems exhibiting a gauge symmetry that gets globally broken. In particular, we focus on the relation that connects the gauge field response functions to the transverse susceptibilities of the order parameter. We find that the long-wavelength and zero energy limit of the former are related to the coefficients of a low-energy expansion of the latter. We examine three physical cases: the superconductor, the Néel antiferromagnet and the spiral magnet. In the case of a metallic spiral magnet that completely breaks the SU(2) spin symmetry we explicitly show that the Ward identities are fulfilled within the random phase approximation. We subsequently derive microscopic expressions for the spin stiffnesses and spin wave velocities, which can be plugged into low energy models to study the effect of long-wavelength bosonic fluctuations on top of mean-field solutions.



I
Introduction

Symmetries play a prominent role in several branches of physics. In condensed matter systems, they often allow for making rigorous statements about the nature of a phase transition, or provide insightful information about entire phases. A renowned example is the Goldstone theorem
Goldstone 1961
, which predicts the number of gapless modes when a
global
continuous symmetry is spontaneously broken, irrespective of the microscopic details of the system.
In some systems, a
global
invariance is promoted to a
local
one with the introduction of a
gauge field
.

\color

blackIn this case, constraints for the correlation functions can be derived, corresponding to fundamental conservation laws. These exact relations, going under the name of Ward (or Ward-Takahashi) identities
Ward 1950
;
Takahashi 1957
, play a key role in the development of approximations to tackle the many body problem
Baym and Kadanoff 1961
;
Abrikosov et al. 1965
;
Toyoda 1987
;
Revzen et al. 1989
, as it is desirable for a method to satisfy them. Many works have been produced with this aim, focusing, among others, on the consistency of many-body approximations
Bychkov et al. 1966
;
Hertz and Edwards 1973
;
Bickers et al. 1989
;
Katanin 2004
;
Krien et al. 2017
, or on the gauge invariance of response functions
Giuliani and Vignale 2008
;
Rostami et al. 2017
;
Rostami et al. 2021
. Ward identities have also been analyzed in systems with broken symmetries, such as ferromagnets
Hertz and Edwards 1973
;
Edwards and Hertz 1973
or superconductors
Schrieffer 1999
;
Anderson 1958a
;
Anderson 1958b
;
Nambu 1960
. Some of the most considered Ward identities are those which impose constraints relating single particle properties (as the self-energy) to the two-particle correlators (the vertex functions), providing a benchmark on the consistency of the approximations employed to compute these quantities

In this paper, we derive Ward identities descending from a local symmetry that gets globally broken in a fermionic system.
We consider the gauge field as an external perturbation and probe the linear response of the system to it, thus preserving the massless character of the Goldstone modes.
\color
blackWe derive a functional identity from which a hierarchy of exact constraints for correlation functions can be extracted. We focus on the Ward identity connecting the linear response to the external gauge field and the transverse susceptibilities of the order parameter, containing information on the Goldstone gapless excitations. In particular, we show that the low frequency and momentum expansion of the inverse of the susceptibilities is related to precise limits of the gauge field response kernel.
We discuss three examples: a superconductor breaking the U(1) charge symmetry, a Néel antiferromagnet breaking the SU(2) spin symmetry leaving a residual U(1), and a spiral magnet, completely breaking SU(2).
\color
blackSimilar relations can be found in the literature for special cases (see for example Ref.
Schulz 1995
). Here, we provide a formal derivation within a formalism that allows to extend them to any system and any possible kind of long-range order that breaks a continuous symmetry.
These identities are of fundamental relevance, as from the sole knowledge of the local symmetry group, one can infer the form of the transverse susceptibilities for small energies and long wavelengths. This is reminiscent of and intimately connected to hydrodynamic theories for systems exhibiting spontaneous symmetry breaking, such as superfluid helium
Khalatinkov 1965
or magnets
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
;
Halperin and Saslow 1977
. Moreover, when the gauge field is of ”pure gauge”, that is, it can be removed by means of a local transformation, the Ward identities are useful to derive a low-energy theory for the slow directional fluctuations of the order parameter in the fashion of
nonlinear sigma models

Haldane 1983
.

We focus particularly on the case of doped antiferromagnets, that host low-lying fermionic excitations, forming small Fermi surfaces. We show that from the Ward identities one can compute a dynamical transverse susceptibility
\chi^{\perp}_{\mathrm{dyn}}\equiv\lim_{\omega\to 0}\chi^{\perp}(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0},\omega)
and a spin stiffness
J
and extract the spin wave velocity as
c_{s}=\sqrt{J/\chi^{\perp}_{\mathrm{dyn}}}
. This is in contrast with the hydrodynamic approach of Refs.
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
;
Halperin and Saslow 1977
, which predicts
c_{s}=\sqrt{J/\chi^{\perp}}
, with
\chi^{\perp}\equiv\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}\chi^{\perp}(\mathbf{q},\omega=0)
the uniform transverse susceptibility. As it was noticed in Ref.
Sachdev et al. 1995
, the equality
\chi^{\perp}=\chi^{\perp}_{\mathrm{dyn}}
holds only for insulating antiferromagnets at low temperatures, as the spin systems considered in Refs.
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
;
Halperin and Saslow 1977
. Furthermore, as a consequence of the doping, Néel antiferromagnetism is often destroyed, making way to a coplanar spiral magnet. In this state, the spins are no longer antiferromagnetically ordered but the magnetization rotates in a plane, completely breaking the SU(2) rotational symmetry and giving rise to three rather than two Goldstone modes
Rastelli et al. 1985
;
Chandra et al. 1990
;
Shraiman and Siggia 1992
;
Kampf 1996
. Spiral states have been found to emerge in the two dimensional Hubbard model and in the
t
-
J
model at moderate hole doping away from half filling, and they are possible candidates for the incommensurate magnetic correlations observed in the cuprate superconductors
Shraiman and Siggia 1989
;
Frésard et al. 1991
;
Chubukov and Frenkel 1992
;
Chubukov and Musaelian 1995
;
Kotov and Sushkov 2004
;
Igoshev et al. 2010
;
Yamase et al. 2016
;
Eberlein et al. 2016
;
Mitscherling and Metzner 2018
;
Bonetti et al. 2020
. They have also been proposed in the context of frustrated antiferromagnets
Azaria et al. 1990
;
Azaria et al. 1992
;
Azaria et al. 1993a
;
Azaria et al. 1993b
;
Chubukov et al. 1994a
;
Chubukov et al. 1994b
;
Azaria et al. 1995
. We present a direct evaluation of the Ward identities for a metallic spiral magnet via the random phase approximation, and derive expressions for the spin stiffness and dynamical susceptibility of each gapless mode.
\color
blackThese formulas generalize to finite temperature and doping and to spiral magnetic states the previously derived expressions for spin stiffnesses in the ground state at half filling
Schulz 1995
;
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
.
This formalism represents a convenient starting point to study long-wavelength fluctuation effects on top of mean-field solutions.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
we derive the anomalous Ward identities descending from a gauge symmetry that gets globally broken in a fermionic system. We consider three different systems: the superconductor, the antiferromagnet, and the spiral magnet. In Sec.
III
we present an explicit and detailed evaluation of the Ward identities for a spiral magnet and derive approximate expressions for the spin stiffnesses and dynamical susceptibilities. A conclusion in Sec.
IV
closes the presentation.

II
Ward Identities

In this section we derive and discuss the Ward identities connected with a specific gauge symmetry which gets globally broken due to the onset of long-range order in the fermionic system. We focus on two specific symmetry groups: the (abelian) U(1) charge symmetry and the (nonabelian) SU(2) spin symmetry. All over the paper we employ Einstein’s notation, that is, a sum over repeated indices is implicit.

II.1
U(1) symmetry

\color

blackWe now analyze the Ward identities in a superconductor. According to the Elitzur’s theorem
Elitzur 1975
, the breaking of the U(1) gauge symmetry is not possible, leading to the interpretation of the superconductor as a
topologically ordered
state
Wen 1989
, due to a subtle interplay of the dynamical gauge field and the order parameter
Hansson et al. 2004
. In this work, we treat the gauge field as a
classical
external perturbation and measure the system’s response to it, so that a discussion on the topological nature of the order is not required.

We consider the generating functional of susceptibilities of the superconducting order parameter and gauge kernels, defined as:

\begin{split}&\mathcal{G}\left[A_{\mu},J,J^{*}\right]=\\
&\hskip 14.22636pt-\ln\int\!\mathcal{D}\psi\mathcal{D}\overline{\psi}e^{-\mathcal{S}\left[\psi,\overline{\psi},A_{\mu}\right]+(J^{*},\psi_{\downarrow}\psi_{\uparrow})+(J,\overline{\psi}_{\uparrow}\overline{\psi}_{\downarrow})},\end{split}

(1)

where
\psi=(\psi_{\uparrow},\psi_{\downarrow})
(
\overline{\psi}=(\overline{\psi}_{\uparrow},\overline{\psi}_{\downarrow})
) are Grassmann spinor fields corresponding to the annihilation (creation) of a fermion,
A_{\mu}
is the electromagnetic field,
J
(
J^{*}
) is a source field that couples to the superconducting order parameter
\overline{\psi}_{\uparrow}\overline{\psi}_{\downarrow}
(
\psi_{\downarrow}\psi_{\uparrow}
), and
\mathcal{S}[\psi,\overline{\psi},A_{\mu}]
is the action of the system. The index
\mu=0,1,\dots,d
, with
d
the system dimensionality, runs over temporal (
\mu=0
) and spatial (
\mu=1,\dots,d
) components. In the above equation and from now on, the expression
(A,B)
has to be intended as
\int_{x}A(x)B(x)
, where
x
is a collective variable consisting of a spatial coordinate
\mathbf{x}
(possibly discrete, for lattice systems), and an imaginary time coordinate
\tau
, and
\int_{x}
is a shorthand for
\int d^{d}\mathbf{x}\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau
, with
\beta
the inverse temperature. Even in the case of a lattice system, we define the gauge field over a
continuous
space time, so that expressions involving its gradients are well defined.

We let the global U(1) charge symmetry be broken by an order parameter that, to make the treatment simpler, we assume to be local (
s
-wave)

\langle\psi_{\downarrow}(x)\psi_{\uparrow}(x)\rangle=\langle\overline{\psi}_{\uparrow}(x)\overline{\psi}_{\downarrow}(x)\rangle=\varphi_{0},

(2)

where the average is computed at zero source and gauge fields, and, without loss of generality, we choose
\varphi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. A generalization to systems with nonlocal order parameters, such as
d
-wave superconductors, is straightforward.

The functional (
1
) has been defined such that its second derivative with respect to
J
and
J^{*}
at zero
J
,
J^{*}
and
A_{\mu}
gives (minus) the susceptibility of the order parameter
\chi(x,x^{\prime})
, while (minus) the gauge kernel
K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})
can be extracted differentiating twice with respect to the gauge field. In formulas

\displaystyle\chi(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta J(x)\delta J^{*}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{J=J^{*}=A_{\mu}=0},

(3a)

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{J=J^{*}=A_{\mu}=0}.

(3b)

Let us now consider the constraints that the U(1) gauge invariance imposes on the functional
\mathcal{G}
. Its action on the fermionic fields is

\displaystyle\psi(x)\to e^{i\theta(x)}\psi(x),

(4a)

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(x)\to e^{-i\theta(x)}\overline{\psi}(x),

(4b)

with
\theta(x)
a generic function. Similarly, the external fields transform as

\displaystyle J(x)\to J^{\prime}(x)=e^{2i\theta(x)}J(x),

(5a)

\displaystyle J^{*}(x)\to[J^{\prime}(x)]^{*}=e^{-2i\theta(x)}J^{*}(x),

(5b)

\displaystyle A_{\mu}(x)\to A_{\mu}^{\prime}(x)=A_{\mu}(x)-\partial_{\mu}\theta(x),

(5c)

where
\partial_{\mu}=(i\partial_{\tau},\bm{\nabla})
. In Eqs. (
4
) and (
5
) the spatial coordinate
\mathbf{x}
of the spinors
\psi
and
\overline{\psi}
, as well as the sources
J
and
J^{*}
may be a lattice one, while the gauge field
A_{\mu}
and the parameter
\theta
are always defined over a continuous space. To keep the notation lighter, we always indicate the space-time coordinate as
x
, keeping in mind that its spatial component could have a different meaning depending on the field it refers to.

For
\mathcal{G}
to be invariant under a U(1) gauge transformation, it must not to depend on
\theta(x)
:

\frac{\delta}{\delta\theta(x)}\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu}^{\prime},J^{\prime},(J^{\prime})^{*}]=0.

(6)

Considering an infinitesimal transformation, that is
|\theta(x)|\ll 1
, from Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
), we obtain

\partial_{\mu}\left(\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)}\right)+2i\left[\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{J(x)}J(x)-\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{J^{*}(x)}J^{*}(x)\right]=0.

(7)

We now consider the change of variables

\displaystyle J(x)=J_{1}(x)+iJ_{2}(x),

(8a)

\displaystyle J^{*}(x)=J_{1}(x)-iJ_{2}(x),

(8b)

such that
J_{1}(x)
(
J_{2}(x)
) is a source field coupling to longitudinal (transverse) fluctuations of the order parameter, and the functional
\Gamma
, defined as the Legendre transform of
\mathcal{G}
,

\begin{split}\Gamma[A_{\mu},\phi_{1},\phi_{2}]=\sum_{a=1,2}&\int_{x}\phi_{a}(x)J_{a}(x)+\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu},J_{1},J_{2}],\end{split}

(9)

where
\phi_{a}(x)=\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu},J_{1},J_{2}]}{\delta J_{a}(x)}
. The gauge kernel can be computed from
\Gamma
as well:

K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\Big\rvert_{\vec{\phi}=A_{\mu}=0},

(10)

because, thanks to the Legendre transform properties,
\delta\Gamma/\delta A_{\mu}(x)=\delta\mathcal{G}/\delta A_{\mu}(x)
. Differently, differentiating
\Gamma
twice with respect to the fields
\phi_{a}
returns the inverse correlator

C^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta\phi_{a}(x)\delta\phi_{b}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{\vec{\phi}=A_{\mu}=0},

(11)

which obeys a reciprocity relation
Negele and Orland 1998

\int_{x^{\prime\prime}}C^{ac}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\chi^{cb}(x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime})=\delta_{ab}\delta(x-x^{\prime}),

(12)

with the generalized susceptibility
\chi^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})
, defined as

\chi^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta J_{a}(x)\delta J_{b}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{J_{a}=A_{\mu}=0}.

(13)

Eq. (
7
) can be expressed in terms of
\Gamma
as

\begin{split}&\partial_{\mu}\left(\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)}\right)-2\left[\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\phi_{1}(x)}\phi_{2}(x)-\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\phi_{2}(x)}\phi_{1}(x)\right]=0.\end{split}

(14)

Eq. (
14
) is an identity for the generating functional
\Gamma
stemming from U(1) gauge invariance of the theory. Taking derivatives with respect to the fields, one can derive an infinite set of Ward identities.

We are interested in the relation between the gauge kernel and the transverse inverse susceptibility
C^{22}(x,x^{\prime})
.
For this purpose, we differentiate Eq. (
14
) once with respect to
\phi_{2}(x^{\prime})
and once with respect to
A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})
, and then set the fields to zero. We obtain the set of equations

\displaystyle-\partial_{\mu}\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{2}(x,x^{\prime})=2\varphi_{0}\,C^{22}(x,x^{\prime}),

(15a)

\displaystyle-\partial_{\mu}K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})=2\varphi_{0}\,\mathcal{C}_{\nu}^{2}(x,x^{\prime}),

(15b)

where
\varphi_{0}=\langle\phi(x)\rangle=\langle\phi_{1}(x)\rangle=\langle\psi_{\downarrow}(x)\psi_{\uparrow}(x)\rangle
, and we have defined the quantity

\mathcal{C}_{\mu}^{a}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta\phi_{a}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{\vec{\phi}=A_{\mu}=0}.

(16)

Combining (
15a
) and (
15b
), we obtain

\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})=4\varphi_{0}^{2}\,C^{22}(x,x^{\prime}).

(17)

Fourier transforming Eq. (
17
) and rotating to real frequencies, we have

-q_{\mu}q_{\nu}K_{\mu\nu}(q)=4\varphi_{0}^{2}C^{22}(q),

(18)

with
q=(\mathbf{q},\omega)
a collective variable combining momentum and real frequency.

We now define the superfluid stiffness
J_{\alpha\beta}
and the uniform density-density susceptibility
\chi_{n}

not
as

\displaystyle J_{\alpha\beta}\equiv-\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},\omega=0),

(19a)

\displaystyle\chi_{n}\equiv\lim_{\omega\to 0}K_{00}(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0},\omega),

(19b)

where the minus sign in (
19a
) has been introduced so that
J_{\alpha\beta}
is positive definite. Notice that, even though the limits
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}
and
\omega\to 0
in Eq. (
19b
) have been taken in the opposite order compared to what is conventionally done, they commute in a
s
-wave superconductor because of the absence of gapless fermionic excitations. In the above equation and from now on, we employ the convention that the indices labeled as
\mu
,
\nu
include temporal and spatial components, whereas
\alpha
and
\beta
only the latter. Taking the second derivative with respect to
q
on both sides of (
18
), we obtain

\displaystyle J_{\alpha\beta}=2\varphi_{0}^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}C^{22}(\mathbf{q},\omega=0)\big\rvert_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}},

(20a)

\displaystyle\chi_{n}=-2\varphi_{0}^{2}\partial^{2}_{\omega}C^{22}(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0},\omega)\big\rvert_{\omega\to 0},

(20b)

where
\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}
and
\partial^{2}_{\omega}
are shorthands for
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}
and
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\omega^{2}}
, respectively. Moreover, we have made use of the Goldstone theorem, reading
C^{22}(\mathbf{0},0)=0
. To derive Eq. (
20
) from (
18
) we have exploited the finiteness of the gauge kernel
K_{\mu\nu}(q)
in the
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}
and
\omega\to 0
limits.
Eq. (
20
) states that the superfluid stiffness and the uniform density-density correlation function are not only the zero momentum and frequency limit of the gauge kernel, but also the coefficients of the inverse transverse susceptibility when expanded for small
\mathbf{q}
and
\omega
, respectively. Inverting Eq. (
12
),
C^{22}(q)
can be expressed in terms of
\chi^{ab}(q)
as

C^{22}(q)=\frac{1}{\chi^{22}(q)-\chi^{21}(q)\frac{1}{\chi^{11}(q)}\chi^{12}(q)}.

(21)

In the limit
q\to 0=(\mathbf{0},0)
,
\chi^{22}(q)
diverges for the Goldstone theorem, while the second term in the denominator vanishes like some power of
q
. This implies that, for small
q
,

C^{22}(q)\simeq\frac{1}{\chi^{22}(q)}.

(22)

From this consideration, together with (
20
), we can deduce that the transverse susceptibility can be written as

\chi^{22}(\mathbf{q},\omega)\simeq\frac{4\varphi_{0}^{2}}{-\chi_{n}\omega^{2}+J_{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}},

(23)

for small
\mathbf{q}
and
\omega
.

The above form of the
\chi^{22}(q)
can be also deduced from a low energy theory for the phase fluctuations of the superconducting order parameter. Setting
J
and
J^{*}
to zero in (
1
), and integrating out the Grassmann fields, one obtains an effective action for the gauge fields. The quadratic contribution in
A_{\mu}
is

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}[A_{\mu}]=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{q}K_{\mu\nu}(q)A_{\mu}(-q)A_{\nu}(q),

(24)

where
\int_{q}
is a shorthand for
\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int\frac{d^{d}\mathbf{q}}{(2\pi)^{d}}
. Since we are focusing only on slow and long-wavelength fluctuations of
A_{\mu}
, we replace
K_{\mu\nu}(q)
with
K_{\mu\nu}(0)
. Considering a pure gauge field,
A_{\mu}(x)=-\partial_{\mu}\theta(x)
, where
\theta(x)
is (half) the phase of the superconducting order parameter (
\phi(x)=\varphi_{0}e^{-2i\theta(x)}
), we obtain

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}[\theta]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{x}\left\{-\chi_{n}\left[\partial_{t}\theta(x)\right]^{2}+J_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\theta(x)\partial_{\beta}\theta(x)\right\},

(25)

with
\theta(x)\in[0,2\pi]
a periodic field. The above action is well known to display a Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) transition

;
Kosterlitz and Thouless 1973
for
d=1
(at
T=0
) and
d=2
(at
T>0)
, while for
d=3
(
T\geq 0
) or
d=2
(
T=0
), it describes a gapless phase mode known as Anderson-Bogoliubov phonon
Anderson 1958b
.

From (
25
), we can extract the propagator of the field
\theta(x)

\langle\theta(-q)\theta(q)\rangle=\frac{1}{-\chi_{n}\omega^{2}+J_{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}},

(26)

where we have neglected the fact that
\theta(x)
is defined modulo
2\pi
.
Writing
\phi_{2}(x)=(\phi(x)-\phi^{*}(x))/(2i)=-\varphi_{0}\sin(2\theta(x))\simeq-2\varphi_{0}\theta(x)
,
\chi^{22}(q)
can be expressed as

\begin{split}\chi^{22}(q)=&\langle\phi_{2}(-q)\phi_{2}(q)\rangle\simeq 4\varphi_{0}^{2}\langle\theta(-q)\theta(q)\rangle\\
=&\frac{4\varphi_{0}^{2}}{-\chi_{n}\omega^{2}+J_{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}},\end{split}

(27)

which is in agreement with Eq. (
23
).

II.2
SU(2) symmetry

In this Section, we repeat the same procedure we have applied in the previous one to derive the Ward identities connected to a SU(2) gauge invariant system. We consider the functional

\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu},\vec{J}]=-\ln\int\!\mathcal{D}\psi\mathcal{D}\overline{\psi}e^{-\mathcal{S}\left[\psi,\overline{\psi},A_{\mu}\right]+(\vec{J},\frac{1}{2}\overline{\psi}\vec{\sigma}\psi)},

(28)

where
A_{\mu}(x)=A_{\mu}^{a}(x)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}
is a SU(2) gauge field,
\vec{\sigma}
are the Pauli matrices, and
\vec{J}(x)
is a source field coupled to the fermion spin operator
\frac{1}{2}\overline{\psi}(x)\vec{\sigma}\psi(x)
. Similarly to the previous section, derivatives of
\mathcal{G}
with respect to
A_{\mu}
and
\vec{J}
at zero external fields give minus the gauge kernels and spin susceptibilities, respectively. In formulas,

\displaystyle\chi^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta J_{a}(x)\delta J_{b}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{\vec{J}=A_{\mu}=0},

(29a)

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta A^{a}_{\mu}(x)\delta A^{b}_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{\vec{J}=A_{\mu}=0}.

(29b)

We let the SU(2) symmetry be broken by a (local) order parameter of the form

\left\langle\frac{1}{2}\overline{\psi}(x)\vec{\sigma}\psi(x)\right\rangle=\varphi_{0}\hat{v}(\mathbf{x}),

(30)

with
\hat{v}(\mathbf{x})
a position-dependent unit vector pointing along the local direction of the magnetization
Not
.

A SU(2) gauge transformation on the fermionic fields reads

\displaystyle\psi(x)\to R(x)\psi(x),

(31a)

\displaystyle\overline{\psi}(x)\to\overline{\psi}(x)R^{\dagger}(x),

(31b)

where
R(x)\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
is a matrix acting on the spin indices of
\psi
and
\overline{\psi}
. The external fields
transform as

\displaystyle J_{a}(x)\to J^{\prime}_{a}(x)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{ab}(x)J_{b}(x),

(32a)

\displaystyle A_{\mu}(x)\to A_{\mu}^{\prime}(x)=

\displaystyle R^{\dagger}(x)A_{\mu}(x)R(x)

\displaystyle+iR^{\dagger}(x)\partial_{\mu}R(x),

(32b)

where
\mathcal{R}(x)
is the adjoint representation of
R(x)

\mathcal{R}^{ab}(x)\sigma^{b}=R(x)\sigma^{a}R^{\dagger}(x).

(33)

The SU(2) gauge invariance of
\mathcal{G}
can be expressed as

\frac{\delta}{\delta R(x)}\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu}^{\prime},\vec{J}^{\prime}]=0.

(34)

Writing
R(x)=e^{i\theta_{a}(x)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}}
,
R^{\dagger}(x)=e^{-i\theta_{a}(x)\frac{\sigma^{a}}{2}}
, and considering an infinitesimal transformation
|\theta_{a}(x)|\ll 1
, we obtain the functional identity

\begin{split}\partial_{\mu}\left(\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}^{a}(x)}\right)-\varepsilon^{a\ell m}\bigg[&\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta\phi^{\ell}(x)}\phi^{m}(x)\\
&-\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}^{\ell}(x)}A_{\mu}^{m}(x)\bigg]=0,\end{split}

(35)

where
\varepsilon^{abc}
is the Levi-Civita tensor.
\Gamma[A_{\mu},\vec{\phi}]
is the Legendre transform of
\mathcal{G}
, defined as

\Gamma[A_{\mu},\vec{\phi}]=\int_{x}\vec{\phi}(x)\cdot\vec{J}(x)+\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu},\vec{J}],

(36)

with
\phi_{a}(x)=\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}[A_{\mu},\vec{J}]}{\delta J_{a}(x)}
. The inverse susceptibilities
C^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})
, defined as,

C^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta\phi_{a}(x)\delta\phi_{b}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{\vec{\phi}=A_{\mu}=0},

(37)

II.2.2
Spiral magnetic order

We now turn our attention to the case of spin spiral ordering, described by the magnetization direction

\hat{v}(\mathbf{x})=(\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}),\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}),0),

(50)

where at least one component of
\mathbf{Q}
is neither 0 nor
\pi/a_{0}

to a basis in which

Kampf 1996

\vec{\phi}^{\prime}(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)\vec{\phi}(x),

(52)

\begin{split}\widetilde{C}^{ab}(x,x^{\prime})=&-\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta\phi^{\prime}_{a}(x)\delta\phi^{\prime}_{b}(x^{\prime})}\bigg\rvert_{\vec{\phi}^{\prime}=A_{\mu}=0}\\

40

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}K_{\mu\nu}^{11}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}^{2}\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x})\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}')

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\widetilde{C}^{33}(x,x^{\prime}),

(54a)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}K_{\mu\nu}^{22}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}^{2}\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x})\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}')

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\times\widetilde{C}^{33}(x,x^{\prime}),

(54b)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}K_{\mu\nu}^{33}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\varphi_{0}^{2}\widetilde{C}^{22}(x,x^{\prime}),

(54c)

with

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}^{33}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C^{33}(x,x^{\prime}),

(55a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}^{22}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x})\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}')C^{11}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x})\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}')C^{22}(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x})\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}')C^{12}(x,x^{\prime})

III
Explicit calculation for a spiral magnet

In this section, we present an explicit calculation for a spiral magnet, emerging from a Hubbard model, of the left and right hand sides of (
56
) and (
57
), and show that the Ward identities are fulfilled. We compute the spin susceptibilities and the gauge kernels within the random phase approximation (RPA), which is a conserving approximation in the sense of Baym and Kadanoff
Baym and Kadanoff 1961
, and it is therefore expected to fulfill the Ward identities.

III.1
Mean-field approximation

In a fermionic lattice system, the amplitude
\varphi_{0}
of spin spiral order parameter

\langle\vec{\phi}(x)\rangle=\varphi_{0}(\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}),\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{x}),0),

(68)

can be expressed as

\varphi_{0}=\int_{\mathbf{k}}\langle\overline{\psi}_{\mathbf{k},\uparrow}\psi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q},\downarrow}\rangle,

(69)

where
\int_{\mathbf{k}}
is a shorthand for an integral over the full
d
-dimensional Brillouin zone, that is
\int_{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}\in BZ}}\frac{d^{d}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}
.
From (
69
), we deduce that spiral ordering couples only the electron states
(\mathbf{k},\uparrow)
and
(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q},\downarrow)
, and the mean-field fermion Green’s function can be expressed as a
2\times 2
matrix:

\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}i\nu_{n}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}}&-\Delta\\
-\Delta&i\nu-\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}\end{array}\right)^{-1},

(70)

where
\nu_{n}=(2n+1)\pi T
(
n\in\mathbb{Z}
) is a fermionic Matsubara frequency,
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}-\mu
, with
\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
the single particle dispersion and
\mu
the chemical potential, and
\Delta
is the spiral order parameter. Diagonalizing (
70
), one obtains the quasiparticle dispersions

E^{\pm}_{\mathbf{k}}=g_{\mathbf{k}}\pm\sqrt{h_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}},

(71)

with
g_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}+\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}})/2
, and
h_{\mathbf{k}}=(\xi_{\mathbf{k}}-\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}})/2
.

The Green’s function can be conveniently written as a linear combination of quasiparticle poles

\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})=\sum_{\ell=\pm}\frac{1}{2}u^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{1}{i\nu_{n}-E^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}},

(72)

where the coefficients
u^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}
are given by

u^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}=\sigma^{0}+\ell\frac{h_{\mathbf{k}}}{e_{\mathbf{k}}}\sigma^{3}+\ell\frac{\Delta}{e_{\mathbf{k}}}\sigma^{1},

(73)

with
\sigma^{0}=\mathbb{1}
, and
e_{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{h_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}+\Delta^{2}}
.
We assume the spiral states to emerge from a lattice model with onsite repulsive interactions (Hubbard model), with imaginary time action

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}[\psi,\overline{\psi}]=\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\bigg\{&\sum_{j,j^{\prime},\sigma}\overline{\psi}_{j,\sigma}\left[(\partial_{\tau}-\mu)\delta_{jj^{\prime}}+t_{jj^{\prime}}\right]\psi_{j^{\prime},\sigma}\\
&+U\sum_{j}\overline{\psi}_{j,\uparrow}\overline{\psi}_{j,\downarrow}\psi_{j,\downarrow}\psi_{j,\uparrow}\bigg\},\end{split}

(74)

where
t_{jj^{\prime}}
describes the hopping amplitude between the lattice sites labeled by
j
and
j^{\prime}
and
U
is the Hubbard interaction.
The Hartree-Fock or mean-field (MF) gap equation at temperature
T
reads

\Delta=-U\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\widetilde{G}^{\uparrow\downarrow}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})=U\int_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\Delta}{2e_{\mathbf{k}}}\left[f(E^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})-f(E^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})\right],

(75)

where
\sum_{\nu_{n}}
denotes a sum over the fermionic Matsubara frequencies, and
f(x)=1/(e^{x/T}+1)
is the Fermi function. The bosonic order parameter
\varphi_{0}
is related to
\Delta
as
\Delta=U\varphi_{0}
. Finally, the optimal
\mathbf{Q}
-vector is obtained minimizing the mean-field free energy

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{MF}}(\mathbf{Q})=&-T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\Tr\ln\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{U}+\mu n\\
=&-T\int_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\ell=\pm}\ln\left(1+e^{-E^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}/T}\right)+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{U}+\mu n,\end{split}

(76)

where
n
is the fermion density.

III.2
Random phase approximation

In this Section, we summarize the RPA for a spiral magnet, closely following Ref.
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
. Further details can be also found in Ref.
Kampf 1996
.

The charge and spin susceptibilities are coupled in a spin spiral state. It is therefore convenient to consider the retarded real-time correlators
Bonetti and Metzner 2022a

\chi^{ab}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)=-i\Theta(t)\langle\left[S^{a}_{j}(t),S^{b}_{j^{\prime}}(0)\right]\rangle,

(77)

where
j
and
j^{\prime}
label two lattice sites,
t
is the time,
[\bullet,\bullet]
denotes the commutator,
\Theta(t)
is the Heaviside function, and
a
and
b
run from 0 to 3. The generalized spin operators are defined in terms of the fermionic fields as

S^{a}_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s,s^{\prime}=\uparrow,\downarrow}\overline{\psi}_{j,s}\sigma^{a}_{ss^{\prime}}\psi_{j,s^{\prime}}.

(78)

The
a=1,2,3
components correspond to the usual spin operator, while
a=0
gives half the fermion density
n_{j}=\overline{\psi}_{j,\uparrow}\psi_{j,\uparrow}+\overline{\psi}_{j,\downarrow}\psi_{j,\downarrow}

It is convenient to work in the rotated basis
Kampf 1996
introduced in Eqs. (
51
) and (
52
), the susceptibilities transform as

\widetilde{\chi}_{jj^{\prime}}(t)=\mathcal{M}_{j}\chi_{jj^{\prime}}(t)\mathcal{M}^{T}_{j^{\prime}},

(79)

with

\mathcal{M}_{j}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&0&0&0\\
0&\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{r}_j)&\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{r}_j)&0\\
0&-\sin(\bQ\cdot\bs{r}_j)&\cos(\bQ\cdot\bs{r}_j)&0\\
0&0&0&1\end{array}\right),

(80)

where
\mathbf{r}_{j}
represents the coordinates of the lattice site
j
. As previously mentioned, the
\widetilde{\chi}^{jj^{\prime}}(t)
are translationally invariant, so that their Fourier transform depends only on a single spatial momentum
\mathbf{q}
.

Within RPA, the susceptibilities are given by

\widetilde{\chi}(q)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}(q)\left[\mathbb{1}-\Gamma_{0}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}(q)\right]^{-1},

(81)

where
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}(q)
is a matrix in the
a,b=0,1,2,3
indices,
\mathbb{1}
is the
4\times 4
identity matrix, and
\Gamma_{0}=2U\mathrm{diag}(-1,1,1,1)
. The bare susceptibilities on the real frequency axis are given by

\begin{split}&\widetilde{\chi}^{ab}_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\\
&-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\tr[\sigma^{a}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\,\sigma^{b}\,\widetilde{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}(i\nu_{n}+i\Omega_{m})\big],\end{split}

(82)

where
\Omega_{m}=2m\pi T
(

\widetilde{\chi}^{ab}_{0}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=-\frac{1}{8}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=\pm}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{A}^{ab}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})F_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q},\omega),

(83)

where we have defined

\mathcal{A}^{ab}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}\Tr\left[\sigma^{a}u^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}\sigma^{b}u^{\ell^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}}\right].

Goldstone modes associated with spontaneous symmetry breaking of the SU(2) spin symmetry appear in the susceptibilities. In particular, considering the limit

, all the off-diagonal elements of the type
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(q)

\widetilde{\chi}^{22}(0)=\frac{\widetilde{\chi}^{22}_{0}(0)}{1-2U\widetilde{\chi}^{22}_{0}(0)}.

(87)

\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\pm Q)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)

, one sees that the denominator of (

) is vanishing. The susceptibility
\widetilde{\chi}^{33}(q)
therefore hosts two poles at

, which are due to gapless excitations of the spins outside the plane in which the magnetization lies. We refer to these modes as
out-of-plane
modes. As already stated, in the spiral state three Goldstone modes appear, as
all
three generators of the symmetry group SU(2) are broken. However, the two out-of-plane modes have degenerate dispersions, as it is expected for
coplanar
order.

III.3
Small
\mathbf{q}
and
\omega

We are now in the position to compute the right hand sides of Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
). The small

and

Bonetti and Metzner 2022a

III.3.1
In-plane mode

Using (
81
), the in-plane susceptibility can be conveniently written as

\widetilde{\chi}^{22}(q)=\frac{\overline{\chi}_{0}^{22}(q)}{1-2U\overline{\chi}_{0}^{22}(q)},

(89)

with

\overline{\chi}_{0}^{22}(q)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(q)+\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1,3\}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(q)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}^{ab}(q)\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{b2}(q).

(90)

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}(q)
is given by

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}(q)=\left[\mathbb{1}_{3}-\Gamma_{0,2}\widetilde{\chi}_{0,2}(q)\right]^{-1}\Gamma_{0,2},

(91)

where
\Gamma_{0,2}^{ab}
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0,2}^{ab}(q)
are matrices obtained from
\Gamma_{0}^{ab}
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{ab}(q)
removing the components where
a=2
and/or
b=2
, and
\mathbb{1}_{3}
denotes the
3\times 3
identity matrix.
For later convenience, we notice that for
q=0
, all the off-diagonal elements
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(q)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{a2}(q)
vanish, so that
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}(0)
can be obtained from the full expression

\widetilde{\Gamma}(q)=\left[\mathbb{1}-\Gamma_{0}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}(q)\right]^{-1}\Gamma_{0},

(92)

selecting only the components in which the indices take the values 0,1, or 3.

Setting
\omega=0
, the bare susceptibilities
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{23}(q)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{32}(q)
vanish as they are odd in
\omega
. Moreover, in the limit
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}
,
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(\mathbf{q},0)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{a2}(\mathbf{q},0)
, with
a=0,1
, are linear in
\mathbf{q}
as they are odd under
\mathbf{q}\to-\mathbf{q}
. The in-plane spin stiffness can be therefore written as

\begin{split}J^{{{{\square}}}}_{\alpha\beta}=&-2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\overline{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)\\
=&-2\Delta^{2}\bigg[\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)\\
&+2\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1\}}\partial_{q_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(0)\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{ab}(\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0},0)\,\partial_{q_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{b2}(0)\bigg],\end{split}

(93)

where we have used
\overline{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)=1/(2U)
, descending from the gap equation, and
\partial_{q_{\alpha}}f(0)
is a shorthand for
\partial f(\mathbf{q},0)/\partial q_{\alpha}|_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}
, and similarly for
\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}f(0)
.

In a similar way, if we set
\mathbf{q}
to
\mathbf{0}
and consider the limit of small
\omega
, the terms where
a
and/or
b
are 0 or 1 vanish as
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(q)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{a2}(q)
for a=0,1 are odd in
\mathbf{q}
. On the other hand,
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{23}(q)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{32}(q)
are linear in
\omega
for small
\omega
. With these considerations, the in-plane dynamical susceptibility is given by

\begin{split}\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{{{{\square}}}}=&2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{\omega}\overline{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)\\
=&2\Delta^{2}\Big[\partial^{2}_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)\\
&+2\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{23}(0)\,\widetilde{\Gamma}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega\to 0)\,\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{32}(0)\Big],\end{split}

(94)

where
\partial^{n}_{\omega}f(0)
is a shorthand for
\partial^{n}f(\mathbf{0},\omega)/\partial\omega^{n}|_{\omega\to 0}
, and
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega\to 0)
can be cast in the simple form

\widetilde{\Gamma}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega\to 0)=\frac{2U}{1-2U\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega\to 0)}.

(95)

III.3.2
Out-of-plane modes

Similarly to the in-plane mode, one can write the out-of-plane susceptibility in the form

\widetilde{\chi}^{33}(q)=\frac{\overline{\chi}_{0}^{33}(q)}{1-2U\overline{\chi}_{0}^{33}(q)},

(96)

with

\overline{\chi}_{0}^{33}(q)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(q)+\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1,2\}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{3a}(q)\widetilde{\Gamma}_{3}^{ab}(q)\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{b3}(q),

(97)

where
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{3}(q)
is defined similarly to
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{2}(q)
, removing the components that involve the index 3 instead of the index 2. We also notice that

\widetilde{\Gamma}_{3}^{ab}(\mathbf{q},0)=\widetilde{\Gamma}^{ab}(\mathbf{q},0),

(98)

for
a
,
b=0,1,2
, because all the off-diagonal components
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{3a}(q)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{a3}(q)
vanish for zero frequency. Using
\overline{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\pm Q)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\pm Q)=1/(2U)
, we obtain the out-of-plane spin stiffness

J_{\alpha\beta}^{\perp}=-\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\overline{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\pm Q)=-\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\pm Q),

(99)

where
\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}f(\pm Q)
stands for
\partial^{2}f(\mathbf{q},0)/\partial q_{\alpha}\partial q_{\beta}|_{\mathbf{q}\to\pm\mathbf{Q}}
.

In the limit
\omega\to 0
, all the
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{3a}(q)
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{a3}(q)
, with
a=0,1,2
, are linear in
\omega
, and the dynamical susceptibility is given by

\begin{split}\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}&=\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{\omega}\overline{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\pm Q)\\
&=\Delta^{2}\bigg[\partial^{2}_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}^{33}_{0}(\pm Q)\\
&\phantom{=}+2\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1,2\}}\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{3a}(\pm Q)\widetilde{\Gamma}^{ab}(\pm Q)\partial_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{b3}(\pm Q)\bigg],\end{split}

(100)

with
\partial^{n}_{\omega}f(\pm Q)
a shorthand for
\partial^{n}f(\pm\mathbf{Q},\omega)/\partial\omega^{n}|_{\omega\to 0}
. We remark that for
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{ab}(q)
the limits
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{Q}
and
\omega\to 0
commute if
\mathbf{Q}
is not a high-symmetry wavevector, that is, if
E^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}\neq E^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}
.

III.4
Gauge kernels

To calculate the gauge kernels, that is, the left hand sides of Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
), we couple our system to a SU(2) gauge field via a Peierls substitution in the quadratic part of action (
74
):

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi,\overline{\psi},A_{\mu}]=\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\sum_{jj^{\prime}}&\overline{\psi}_{j}\Big[(\partial_{\tau}-A_{0,j}+\mu)\delta_{jj^{\prime}}\\
&+t_{jj^{\prime}}e^{-\mathbf{r}_{jj^{\prime}}\cdot(\bm{\nabla}-i\mathbf{A}_{j})}\Big]\psi_{j},\end{split}

(101)

where
e^{-\mathbf{r}_{jj^{\prime}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}}
is the translation operator from site
j
to site
j^{\prime}
, with
\mathbf{r}_{jj^{\prime}}=\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{j^{\prime}}
. Notice that under the transformation
\psi_{j}\to R_{j}\psi_{j}
, with
R_{j}\in\mathrm{SU(2)}
, the interacting part of the action (
74
) is left unchanged, while the gauge field transforms according to (
32b
). Since the gauge kernels correspond to correlators of two gauge fields, we expand (
101
) to second order in
A_{\mu}
. After a Fourier transformation one obtains

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{0}[\psi,\overline{\psi},A_{\mu}]=&-\int_{k}\overline{\psi}_{k}\left[i\nu_{n}+\mu-\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\right]\psi_{k}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\int_{k,q}A_{\mu}^{a}(q)\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathbf{k}}\,\overline{\psi}_{k+q}\sigma^{a}\psi_{k}\\
&-\frac{1}{8}\int_{k,q,q^{\prime}}A^{a}_{\alpha}(q-q^{\prime})A^{a}_{\beta}(q^{\prime})\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}\overline{\psi}_{k+q}\psi_{k},\end{split}

(102)

where the first order coupling is given by
\gamma^{\mu}_{\mathbf{k}}=(1,\bm{\nabla}_{\mathbf{k}}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}})
, and the second order one is
\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}=\partial^{2}_{k_{\alpha}k_{\beta}}\epsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
. Analyzing the coupling of the temporal component of the gauge field to the fermions in (
101
) and (
102
), we notice that the temporal components of the gauge kernel are nothing but the susceptibilities in the original (unrotated) spin basis

K_{00}^{ab}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega)=\chi^{ab}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega),

(103)

where
\omega
is a real frequency.

(107a)

In-plane mode

The in-plane spin stiffness is defined as

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K_{0\alpha}^{30}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},0)=\kappa_{\alpha}^{30}(\mathbf{0})\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{0}},

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K_{0\alpha}^{31}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},0)=\kappa_{\alpha}^{31}(\mathbf{0})\frac{\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}+\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}}{2},

(111b)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K_{0\alpha}^{32}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},0)=\kappa_{\alpha}^{32}(\mathbf{0})\frac{\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{Q}}-\delta_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime},-\mathbf{Q}}}{2i},

(111c)

where

\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K^{3a}_{\alpha 0}(\mathbf{q},0)=\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K^{a3}_{0\alpha}(\mathbf{q},0)

104

J_{\alpha\beta}^{{{{{\square}}}}}=J_{\alpha\beta}^{0,{{{{\square}}}}}-\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1\}}\kappa_{\alpha}^{3a}(\mathbf{0})\widetilde{\Gamma}^{ab}(\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0},0)\kappa_{\beta}^{3b}(\mathbf{0}),

(112)

where
\widetilde{\Gamma}(\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0},0)
is the effective interaction in the rotated spin basis, defined in Eq. (

\Gamma

\widetilde{\Gamma}
and, together with the equality

The dynamical susceptibility is defined as

\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{{{{\square}}}}=\lim_{\omega\to 0}K_{00}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega)=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\chi^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega).

(113)

From Eq. (

(114)

Remarking that for

(115)

III.4.2
Out-of-plane modes

To compute the the out-of-plane stiffness, that is,

J_{\alpha\beta}^{\perp}=-\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K_{\alpha\beta}^{22}(\mathbf{q},0),

(116)

\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}^{\prime}\to\mathbf{0}

not

\begin{split}&\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K_{\mathrm{para},\alpha\beta}^{22}(\mathbf{q},0)\\

where we have defined

\begin{split}J^{\perp}_{\alpha\beta}=&-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\gamma^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}\gamma^{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}\,G_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\overline{G}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}}(i\nu_{n})\\

(118)

Finally, we evaluate the dynamical susceptibility of the out-of-plane modes. This is defined as

(119)

Applying transformation (
79

\chi^{22}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega)
in terms of the susceptibilities in the rotated basis as

\begin{split}\chi^{22}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{\zeta=\pm}\Big[&\widetilde{\chi}^{11}(\mathbf{q}+\zeta\mathbf{Q},\omega)\\

where we have used (see Sec.
III.2
)
\widetilde{\chi}^{12}(q)=-\widetilde{\chi}^{21}(q)

\mathbf{q}

\mathbf{0}

It is convenient to express

\begin{split}\widetilde{\chi}^{-+}(\mathbf{Q},\omega)&=\widetilde{\chi}^{-+}_{0}(\mathbf{Q},\omega)\\

where we have defined

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{-a}(q)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{1a}(q)-i\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{2a}(q)\right],

(123a)

\begin{split}\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}&=2\widetilde{\chi}^{-+}_{0}(Q)\\
&\phantom{=}+2\sum_{a,b\in\{0,1,2\}}\widetilde{\chi}^{-a}_{0}(Q)\widetilde{\Gamma}^{ab}(Q)\widetilde{\chi}^{b+}_{0}(Q),\end{split}

(124)

\begin{split}\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}&=2\widetilde{\chi}^{+-}_{0}(-Q)\\

III.5.1
In-plane mode

We start by computing the first term in Eq. (

\begin{split}&-2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)\\

&\hskip 85.35826pt+\frac{f^{\prime}(E^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})+f^{\prime}(E^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})}{4e_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}}\bigg],\end{split}

(126)

where

(Eq. (

\begin{split}J^{0,{{{{\square}}}}}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\Big[&G_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})^{2}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\beta}+\overline{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})^{2}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\beta}\\
&-2F_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})^{2}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\beta}\Big]\\
+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\Big[&G_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\mathbf{k}}+\overline{G}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\gamma^{\alpha\beta}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}\Big].\end{split}

(127)

The second (diamagnetic) term can be integrated by parts, giving

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\Big[&G^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\beta}+\overline{G}^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\beta}\\

(128)

where we have used the properties

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{\alpha}}G_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})=G^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\gamma^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}}+F^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n})\gamma^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}},

(129a)

\begin{split}J_{0,{{{{\square}}}}}^{\alpha\beta}=-\int_{\mathbf{k}}T\sum_{\nu_{n}}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\beta}&F^{2}_{\mathbf{k}}(i\nu_{n}).\end{split}

(130)

Performing the Matsubara sum, we arrive at

\begin{split}J^{0,{{{{\square}}}}}_{\alpha\beta}=-\Delta^{2}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}}^{\alpha}\gamma_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}^{\beta}\bigg[&\frac{f(E^{-}_{\mathbf{k}})-f(E^{+}_{\mathbf{k}})}{4e_{\mathbf{k}}^{3}}\\

which is the same result as in (
126

\displaystyle 2i\Delta\partial_{q_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{20}(0)=-2i\Delta\partial_{q_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{02}(0)=\kappa^{30}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{0}),

(132a)

\displaystyle 2i\Delta\partial_{q_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{21}(0)=-2i\Delta\partial_{q_{\alpha}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{12}(0)=\kappa^{31}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{0}).

Out-of-plane modes

With the help of some lengthy algebra, one can compute the second momentum derivative of the bare susceptibility

\begin{split}-\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(Q)=&\frac{1}{8}\int_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}=\pm}\left(1-\ell\frac{h_{\mathbf{k}}}{e_{\mathbf{k}}}\right)\left(1+\ell\frac{h_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}}{e_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}}\right)\gamma^{\alpha}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}\gamma^{\beta}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}\frac{f(E^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}})-f(E^{\ell^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}})}{E^{\ell}_{\mathbf{k}}-E^{\ell^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}}\\

III.5.3
Remarks on more general models

We remark that in the more general case of an interaction of the type

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{int}}=\int_{k,k^{\prime},q}\!U_{k,k^{\prime}}(q)[\overline{\psi}_{k+q}\vec{\sigma}\psi_{k}]\cdot[\overline{\psi}_{k^{\prime}-q}\vec{\sigma}\psi_{k^{\prime}}],

(138)

producing, in general, a
k
-dependent gap, the identities we have proven above do not hold anymore within the RPA, as additional terms in the derivative of the inverse susceptibilities emerge, containing expressions involving first and second derivatives of the gap with respect to the spatial momentum and/or frequency. In fact, in the case of nonlocal interactions, gauge invariance requires additional couplings to the gauge field in
\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{int}}
, complicating our expressions for the gauge kernels. Similarly, even for action (
74
), approximations beyond the RPA produce in general a
k
-dependent
\Delta
, and vertex corrections in the kernels are required to obtain the same result as the one obtained expanding the susceptibilities.

III.6
Néel limit

In this Section, we analyze the Néel limit, that is,
\mathbf{Q}=(\pi/a_{0},\dots,\pi/a_{0})
. In this case, it is easy to see that, within the RPA, the bare susceptibilities in the rotated basis obey the identities

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{Q},\omega),

(139a)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{20}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{21}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=0,

(139b)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{30}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{31}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=0.

(139c)

Furthermore, we obtain for the mixed gauge kernels (see Appendix
A
)

K^{ab}_{\mathrm{para},\alpha 0}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega)=K^{ab}_{\mathrm{para},0\alpha}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\omega)=0.

(140)

We also notice that
K^{11}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},0)
and
K^{22}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{q}^{\prime},0)
have (different) momentum off-diagonal contributions for which
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}=\mathbf{q}\pm 2\mathbf{Q}
. If
\mathbf{Q}=(\pi/a_{0},\dots,\pi/a_{0})
, these terms become diagonal in momentum, as
2\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K^{11}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},0)=0,

(141a)

\displaystyle K^{22}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},0)=K^{33}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},0).

(141b)

From the above relations, we can see that
J_{\alpha\beta}^{\perp}=J^{{{{\square}}}}_{\alpha\beta}\equiv J_{\alpha\beta}
, and
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}=\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{{{{\square}}}}\equiv\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}
, as expected for the Néel state.

From these considerations, we obtain for the spin stiffness

\begin{split}J_{\alpha\beta}=&-\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K^{22}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},0)=-\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}K^{33}_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},0)\\
&=-2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)=-2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{q_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(Q),\end{split}

(142)

which implies that
J_{\alpha\beta}
is given by Eq. (
131
). If the underlying lattice is
C_{4}
-symmetric, the spin stiffness is isotropic in the Néel state, that is,
J_{\alpha\beta}=J\delta_{\alpha\beta}
.
Similarly, for the dynamical susceptibility, we have

\begin{split}\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}=&\lim_{\omega\to 0}\chi^{22}(\mathbf{0},\omega)=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\chi^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega)\\
=&2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{22}(0)=2\Delta^{2}\partial^{2}_{\omega}\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(Q),\end{split}

(143)

which, combined with (
133
), implies

\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}=\lim_{\omega\to 0}\frac{\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega)}{1-2U\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega)},

(144)

with
\widetilde{\chi}_{0}^{33}(\mathbf{0},\omega\to 0)
given by Eq. (
133
).

We notice that the dynamical susceptibility is obtained from the susceptibility by letting
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}

before

\omega\to 0
. This order of the limits removes the intraband terms (that is, the
\ell=\ell^{\prime}
terms in Eq. (
83
)), which instead would yield a finite contribution to the
uniform transverse susceptibility

\chi^{\perp}\equiv\lim_{\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}}\chi^{22}(\mathbf{q},0)
. In the special case of an insulator at low temperature
T\ll\Delta
, the intraband contributions vanish and one has the identity
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}=\chi^{\perp}
, leading to the hydrodynamic relation for the spin wave velocity
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969

c_{s}=\sqrt{J/\chi^{\perp}}
(in an isotropic antiferromagnet). As noticed in Ref.
Sachdev et al. 1995
, in a doped antiferromagnet this hydrodynamic expression does not hold anymore, and one has to replace the uniform transverse susceptibility with the dynamical susceptibility. Since
J=0
and
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}=0
in the symmetric phase due to SU(2) gauge invariance, the expression
c_{s}=\sqrt{J/\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}}
yields a finite value
c_{s}
at the critical point
\Delta\to 0
, provided that
J
and
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}
scale to zero with the same power of
\Delta
, as it happens within mean-field theory. Note that in the symmetric phase SU(2) gauge invariance does not pose any constraint on
\chi^{\perp}
, which is generally finite.

In the simpler case of perfect nesting, that is, when
\xi_{\mathbf{k}}=-\xi_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}}
, corresponding to the half-filled particle-hole symmetric Hubbard model, and at zero temperature, expressions for
J
and
\chi^{\perp}
have been derived in Refs.
Schulz 1995
;
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
for two spatial dimensions, and it is straightforward to check that our results reduce to these in this limit. Moreover, Eqs. 31-34 in Ref.
Schulz 1995
are similar to our Ward identities but no derivation is provided.

IV
Conclusion

In conclusion, we have derived Ward identities for fermionic systems in which a gauge symmetry is globally broken. In particular, we have shown that the zero-energy and long-wavelength components of the gauge kernels are connected to the transverse susceptibilities of the order parameter by exact relations. We have analyzed several examples, namely a superconductor, a Néel antiferromagnet, and a spiral magnet. In the latter case, we have performed an explicit calculation of the transverse susceptibilities and of the gauge kernels within the random phase approximation and verified that the Ward identities are indeed fulfilled. Furthermore, we have considered the Néel limit
\mathbf{Q}\to(\pi/a_{0},\dots,\pi/a_{0})
and found that our RPA expressions for the spin stiffnesses and susceptibilities reduce to those previously obtained in Refs.
Schulz 1995
;
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
. We have also shown that the hydrodynamic expression for the magnon velocity
c_{s}=\sqrt{J/\chi^{\perp}}
does not hold in presence of gapless fermionic excitations, and it must be replaced by
c_{s}=\sqrt{J/\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}}
. While
\chi^{\perp}
is computed by taking the
\omega\to 0
limit
before
letting
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}
in the transverse susceptibility,
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}
is obtained reversing the order of the limits. The equality
\chi_{\mathrm{dyn}}^{\perp}=\chi^{\perp}
holds only for insulating antiferromagnets at low temperatures
T\ll\Delta
, as it is the case for the spin systems considered in Refs.
Halperin and Hohenberg 1969
;
Halperin and Saslow 1977
.

Our findings find immediate application in the study of large distance properties of interacting fermion systems.
In fact, via a microscopic calculation, one can evaluate the coefficients of the effective actions listed in Sec.
II
, and study phenomena which are purely fluctuations-driven. Examples are the Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless transition in two-dimensional superconductors (see, for example, the microscopic calculation of Ref.
Metzner and Yamase 2019
) or strong magnetic fluctuations leading to the formation of a
pseudogap
in low-dimensional electron systems
Borejsza and Dupuis 2004
;
Scheurer et al. 2018
. Special care, however, must be taken when the fermions form Fermi surfaces, as the low-energy modes of the order parameter can decay in particle-hole pairs, producing a
Landau damping

Bonetti and Metzner 2022a
that can affect the macroscopic and critical properties of the system
Millis 1993
;
Sachdev et al. 1995
. An interesting extension of the present work would be to derive Ward identities for the damping of the Goldstone modes.

Erratum: Local Ward identities for collective excitations in fermionic systems with spontaneously broken symmetries [Phys. Rev. B 106, 155105 (2022)]

We found a mistake in the original paper, namely the statement that the second derivative of the
\Gamma
-functional with respect to the gauge field returns the gauge kernel (Eq. (10) of the original paper).

The Ward identities (WIs) presented in the original paper are nonetheless correct, provided that one does not interpret the object labeled as
K_{\mu\nu}
as the gauge kernel but as the second derivative with respect to the gauge field of the generating functional
\Gamma
computed at zero fields. The physical meaning, the usefulness, and the computation of such object are left for future research.

In the following, we present a revised derivation of the WIs for the
actual
gauge kernel
K_{\mu\nu}
, both for the case of a U(1) and of an SU(2) symmetry.

Appendix A
U(1) symmetry

We consider a fermionic theory, described by the Grassmann fields
\psi
and
\overline{\psi}
and coupled to a U(1) gauge field
A_{\mu}
, and governed by the action
\mathcal{S}[\psi,\overline{\psi},A_{\mu}]
. We define the U(1) gauge kernel as

K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})\equiv\langle j_{\mu}(x)j_{\nu}(x^{\prime})\rangle_{c}+\langle P_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})\rangle\,,

(150)

with
j_{\mu}(x)\equiv\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)}\rvert_{A_{\mu}=0}
the paramagnetic current operator,
P_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})\equiv-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{S}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\rvert_{A_{\mu}=0}
the diamagnetic current operator, and where the (connected) average is taken by means of the path integral defined by
\mathcal{S}
. The gauge kernel can also be defined as the second derivative at zero source fields of the
\mathcal{G}
-generating functional of the original paper (Eq. (1))

K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})\equiv-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}[J,J^{*},A_{\mu}]}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\,\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg|_{J=J^{*}=A_{\mu}=0}\,.

(151)

The U(1) gauge kernel obeys the relation

\partial_{\mu}K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})=0\,,

(152)

even in presence of spontaneous symmetry breaking. This can be readily derived from Eq. (7) of the original paper. The above relation is somewhat obvious as it implies charge conservation, which must be fulfilled even in the superconducting state
Schrieffer 1999
. Fourier transforming Eq. (
152

i\omega K_{0\nu}(\mathbf{q},\omega)-iq_{\alpha}K_{\alpha\nu}(\mathbf{q},\omega)=0\,,

(153)

where
\alpha
is only a spatial index and a sum over it is implied. Here and henceforth we use Einstein’s convention for repeated indices, unless otherwise specified. Note that for the derivative in Eq. (
152

A_{\mu}
needs to be defined on a
continuous

153
) is valid only for those values of
|\mathbf{q}|
that are much smaller then the inverse lattice spacing. Setting
\nu=0
,
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\nu=\beta
(with
\beta
also a spatial index),
\omega=0

\displaystyle K_{00}(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{0},\omega)=0\,,

(154a)

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},\omega=0)\simeq J_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q})-\frac{[J_{\alpha\gamma}(\mathbf{q})q_{\gamma}][J_{\beta\delta}(\mathbf{q})q_{\delta}]}{J_{\gamma\delta}(\mathbf{q})q_{\gamma}q_{\delta}}\,,

(154b)

where
J_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q})
a function approaching the superfluid stiffness for
\mathbf{q}\to\mathbf{0}
in the superconducting state. Eq. (
154b

can take to obey
q_{\alpha}K_{\alpha\beta}(\mathbf{q},0)=0

K
appearing there is the gauge kernel. While

descends from the properties of the Legendre transform, a similar relation does not apply to the second derivative of the
\mathcal{G}
- and
\Gamma
-functionals with respect to the gauge field. The correct form of Eq. (10) is

\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg|_{A_{\mu}=\phi=0}=-K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})+\int_{x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime}}L_{\mu,a}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\chi^{-1}_{ab}(x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime})L_{\nu,b}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime\prime})\,,

(155)

with

L_{\mu,a}(x,x^{\prime})=-\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta J_{a}(x^{\prime})}\bigg|_{A_{\mu}=J=0}.

(156)

A proof of this relation is given in Appendix

.
This proves that the
K

with (minus) the right hand side of Eq. (

Appendix D
Note

In Ref.
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
the expressions for the spin stiffnesses and dynamical susceptibilities derived in the original manuscript have been used. Even though it was not explicitly stated, they have been derived applying a small symmetry breaking field to the system and sending it to zero
after
performing the dynamical or static limit. The expressions in Ref.
Bonetti and Metzner 2022b
are therefore correct within the (renormalization group improved) random phase approximation employed in the paper.

During the review process of this Erratum, Ref.
Goremykin and Katanin 2024
appeared, which presents a similar derivation of the Ward identities above, and, as also discussed here, identifies the presence of an infinitesimal symmetry breaking field as crucial to obtain the correct formulas for the spin stiffnesses.

Appendix E
Derivation of Eq. (
155
)

In this Appendix, we present a derivation of Eq. (
155
). The identity

\frac{\delta\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)}=\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)}\,,

(176)

descends directly from the properties of the Legendre transform that connects
\mathcal{G}
and
\Gamma
. Taking a further derivative with respect to the gauge field in the above equation, we get

\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}=\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}+\int_{x^{\prime\prime}}\frac{\delta^{2}\mathcal{G}}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta J_{a}(x^{\prime\prime})}\,\frac{\delta J_{a}(x^{\prime\prime})}{\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\,.

(177)

Setting the fields to zero, we obtain

\frac{\delta^{2}\Gamma}{\delta A_{\mu}(x)\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg|_{A_{\mu}=\phi=0}=-K_{\mu\nu}(x,x^{\prime})-\int_{x^{\prime\prime}}L_{\mu,a}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\,\frac{\delta J_{a}(x^{\prime\prime})}{\delta A_{\nu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg|_{A_{\mu}=\phi=0}\,,

(178)

with
L_{\mu,a}(x,x^{\prime\prime})
defined as in Eq. (
156
).
At this point,

one needs to express the source field
J_{a}(x)
in terms of the ”classical” field
\phi_{a}(x)
, which
\Gamma
depends on. Since we only need its derivative with respect to the gauge field at zero sources, we are allowed to expand the relation connecting
\phi_{a}(x)
and
J_{a}(x)
to linear order in the fields:

\phi_{a}(x)\equiv-\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta J_{a}(x)}\approx\varphi_{0,a}+\int_{x^{\prime}}\left[\chi_{ab}(x,x^{\prime})J_{b}(x^{\prime})+L_{\mu,a}(x^{\prime},x)A_{\mu}(x^{\prime})\right]+\dots\,,

(179)

with
\varphi_{0,a}\equiv-\frac{\delta\mathcal{G}}{\delta J_{a}(x)}|_{J=A_{\mu}=0}
the condensate fraction.
Solving for
J_{a}(x)
, we obtain

\frac{\delta J_{a}(x)}{\delta A_{\mu}(x^{\prime})}\bigg|_{A_{\mu}=\phi=0}=-\int_{x^{\prime\prime}}\chi^{-1}_{ab}(x,x^{\prime\prime})L_{\mu,b}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\,,

(180)

with the inverse susceptibility
\chi^{-1}_{ab}(x,x^{\prime})
obeying

\int_{x^{\prime\prime}}\chi_{ac}(x,x^{\prime\prime})\chi^{-1}_{cb}(x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime})=\delta(x-x^{\prime})\delta_{ab}\,.

(181)

Inserting Eq. (
180
) into Eq. (
178
), we obtain Eq. (
155
).
