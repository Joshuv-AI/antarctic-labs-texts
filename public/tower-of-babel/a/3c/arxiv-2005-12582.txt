Differentials and distances in probabilistic coherence spaces
Thomas Ehrhard ORCID:0000-0001-5231-5504

Abstract
. In probabilistic coherence spaces, a denotational model of probabilistic functional languages, morphisms are analytic and therefore smooth. We explore two related applications of the corresponding derivatives. First we show how derivatives allow to compute the expectation of execution time in the weak head reduction of probabilistic PCF (pPCF). Next we apply a general notion of “local” differential of morphisms to the proof of a Lipschitz property of these morphisms allowing in turn to relate the observational distance on pPCF terms to a distance the model is naturally equipped with. This suggests that extending probabilistic programming languages with derivatives, in the spirit of the differential lambda-calculus, could be quite meaningful.

3.3.
Glb’s, lub’s and distance

Since we are working with probabilistic coherence spaces, we could
deal directly with families of real numbers and define these
operations more concretely. We prefer not to do so to have a more
canonical presentation which can be generalized to cones such as those
considered in
[
EPT18
,
Ehr20
]
. Given a PCS
X
,
remember that
\|{\_}\|_{X}
denotes the norm
\|{\_}\|_{\overline{\mathsf{P}}{X}}
of
the associated cone, see Section
1.2
.

Given
x,y\in\mathsf{P}{X}
, observe that
x\wedge y\in\mathsf{P}{X}
, where

(x\wedge y)_{a}=\min(x_{a},y_{a})
, and that
x\wedge y
is the glb of
x

and
y
in
\mathsf{P}{X}
(with its standard ordering). It follows that
x

and
y
have also a lub
x\vee y\in\overline{\mathsf{P}}{X}
which is given by

x\vee y=x+y-(x\wedge y)
(and of course

(x\vee y)_{a}=\max(x_{a},y_{a})
).

Let us prove that
x+y-(x\wedge y)
is actually the lub of
x
and

y
. First,
x\leq x+y-(x\wedge y)
simply because
x\wedge y\leq y
.
Next, let
z\in\overline{\mathsf{P}}{X}
be such that
x\leq z
and
y\leq z
. We must
prove that
x+y-(x\wedge y)\leq z
, that is

x+y\leq z+(x\wedge y)=(z+x)\wedge(z+y)
, which is clear since

x+y\leq z+x,z+y
. We have used the fact that
+
distributes over

\wedge
so let us prove this last fairly standard property:

z+(x\wedge y)=(z+x)\wedge(z+y)
. The “
\leq
” inequation is obvious
(monotonicity of
+
) so let us prove the converse, which amounts to

x\wedge y\geq(z+x)\wedge(z+y)-z
(observe that indeed that

z\leq(z+x)\wedge(z+y)
). This in turn boils down to proving that

x\geq(z+x)\wedge(z+y)-z
(and similarly for
y
) which results
from
x+z\geq(z+x)\wedge(z+y)
and we are done.

We define the distance between
x
and
y
by

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,y)=\|{x-(x\wedge y)}\|_{X}+\|{y-(x\wedge y)}\|_{X}\,.

The only non obvious fact to check to prove that this is actually a
distance is the triangular inequality, so let
x,y,z\in\mathsf{P}{X}
. We
have

x-(x\wedge z)\leq x-(x\wedge y\wedge z)=x-(x\wedge y)+(x\wedge y)-(x\wedge y\wedge z)
and hence

\displaystyle\|{x-(x\wedge z)}\|_{X}\leq\|{x-(x\wedge y)}\|_{X}+\|{(x\wedge y)-(x\wedge y\wedge z)}\|_{X}\,.

Now we have
(x\wedge y)\vee(y\wedge z)\leq y
, that is

(x\wedge y)+(y\wedge z)-(x\wedge y\wedge z)\leq y
, that is

(x\wedge y)-(x\wedge y\wedge z)\leq y-(y\wedge z)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\|{x-(x\wedge z)}\|_{X}\leq\|{x-(x\wedge y)}\|_{X}+\|{y-(y\wedge z)}\|_{X}

and symmetrically

\displaystyle\|{z-(x\wedge z)}\|_{X}\leq\|{z-(z\wedge y)}\|_{X}+\|{y-(y\wedge x)}\|_{X}

and summing up we get, as expected

\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,z)\leq\mathsf{d}_{X}(x,y)+\mathsf{d}_{X}(y,z)
.

Remark 15
.

In a cone
P
, glb’s do not necessarily exist; we can nevertheless
define a distance as follows:

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}_{P}(x,y)=\inf\{\|{x-z}\|_{P}+\|{y-z}\|_{P}\mid z\in P\text{ and }z\leq x,y\}

and it is possible to prove that, equipped with this distance,
P

is always Cauchy-complete. Of course if
P=\overline{\mathsf{P}}{X}
this distance
coincides with the distance defined above.

3.4.
A Lipschitz property

Using the differential of Section

, we prove

{\mathbf{Pcoh}}_{\oc}
satisfy a Lipschitz property, with a

First of all, observe that, if

Let

Let

if we assume that

, we have

\displaystyle(1-p){t}^{\prime}(x)\in\mathsf{P}{({X}\multimap{1})}=\mathsf{P}{X^{{\mathord{\perp}}}}\,.

(13)

Let
x\leq y\in\mathsf{P}{X}
be such that
\|{y}\|_{X}\leq p

\displaystyle x+(2-p)(y-x)=y+(1-p)(y-x)\in\mathsf{P}{X}

\displaystyle h:[0,2-p]

\displaystyle\to[0,1]

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\mapsto\widehat{t}(x+\theta(y-x))

Therefore the derivative of

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(\theta)={{t}^{\prime}(x+\theta(y-x))}\cdot{(y-x)}\leq\frac{\|{y-x}\|_{X}}{1-p}

\mathbf{Pcoh}
), a natural and purely syntactic conjecture is that

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}^{\langle p\rangle}_{\mathsf{obs}}(M,M^{\prime})\leq\frac{p}{1-p}\,\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{lin}}(M,M^{\prime})\,.

(15)

This seems easy to prove in the case

\mathbb{P}_{\langle M^{\prime},\pi\rangle}{({\langle M^{\prime},\pi\rangle}\downarrow\underline{0})}=0
: it suffices
to observe that a path which is a successful reduction of

\langle({{C}^{\langle p\rangle}}){M},\varepsilon\rangle
in the “Krivine Machine” of
Section
2.1.2
(considered here as a Markov chain)
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\langle({{C}^{\langle p\rangle}}){M},\varepsilon\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\to^{*}}\langle\operatorname{\underline{\mathsf{if}}}(\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(p),M,\Omega^{\sigma}),\pi_{1}(C,M)\rangle\mathrel{\to^{*}}\langle\operatorname{\underline{\mathsf{if}}}(\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(p),M,\Omega^{\sigma}),\pi_{2}(C,M)\rangle

\displaystyle\mathrel{\to^{*}}\cdots\mathrel{\to^{*}}\langle\operatorname{\underline{\mathsf{if}}}(\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(p),M,\Omega^{\sigma}),\pi_{k}(C,M)\rangle\mathrel{\to^{*}}\langle\underline{0},\varepsilon\rangle

where
(\pi_{i}(C,M))_{i=1}^{k}
is a finite sequence of stacks such that

{\sigma}\vdash{\pi_{i}(M)}
for each
i
. Notice that this sequence of
stacks depends not only on
C
and
M
but also on the considered path
of the Markov chain.

In the general case, Inequation (
15
) seems less
easy to prove because, for a given common initial context
C
, the
sequences of reductions (and of associated stacks) starting with

\langle({{C}^{\langle p\rangle}}){M},\varepsilon\rangle
and

\langle({{C}^{\langle p\rangle}}){M^{\prime}},\varepsilon\rangle
differ. This divergence has
low probability when
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{lin}}(M,M^{\prime})
is small, but it is not
completely clear how to evaluate it. Coinductive methods like
probabilistic bisimulation as in the work of Crubillé and Dal Lago are
certainly relevant here.

Our Theorem
16
shows that another and more
geometric approach, based on a simple denotational model, is also
possible to get Theorem
18
which, though
weaker than Inequation (
15
), allows
nevertheless to control the
p
-tamed distance.

We finish the paper by observing that the equivalence relations
induced on terms by these observational distances coincide with the
ordinary observational distance if
p\not=0
.

Theorem 19
.

Assume that
0<p\leq 1
. If
\mathsf{d}^{\langle p\rangle}_{\mathsf{obs}}(M,M^{\prime})=0
then
M\sim M^{\prime}

(that is,
M
and
M^{\prime}
are observationally equivalent).

Proof 4.2
.

If
{}\vdash{M}:{\sigma}
we set

M_{p}=\operatorname{\underline{\mathsf{if}}}(\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(p),M,\Omega^{\sigma})
. If
\mathsf{d}^{\langle p\rangle}_{\mathsf{obs}}(M,M^{\prime})=0
then

M_{p}\sim M^{\prime}_{p}
by definition of observational equivalence, hence

\llbracket{M_{p}}\rrbracket=\llbracket{M^{\prime}_{p}}\rrbracket
by our Full Abstraction
Theorem
[
EPT18
]
, but
\llbracket{M_{p}}\rrbracket=p\llbracket{M}\rrbracket

and similarly for
M^{\prime}
. Since
p\not=0
we get
\llbracket{M}\rrbracket=\llbracket{M^{\prime}}\rrbracket

and hence
M\sim M^{\prime}
by adequacy
[
EPT18
]
.

So for each
p\in(0,1)
and for each type
\sigma
we can consider

\mathsf{d}^{\langle p\rangle}
as a distance on the observational classes of closed
terms of type
\sigma
. We call it the
p
-tamed observational
distance
. Our Theorem
18
shows that we
can control this distance using the denotational distance. For
instance we have

\mathsf{d}^{\langle p\rangle}_{\mathsf{obs}}(\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(0),\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(\varepsilon))\leq\frac{2p\varepsilon}{1-p}

so that
\mathsf{d}^{\langle p\rangle}_{\mathsf{obs}}(\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(0),\underline{\operatorname{\mathsf{coin}}}(\varepsilon))
tends to
0
when

\varepsilon
tends to
0
.

5.
Conclusion

The two results of this paper are related: both use derivatives
wrt. probabilities to evaluate the number of times arguments are
used. The derivatives used in the second part are more general than
those of the first part simply because the parameters wrt. which
derivatives are taken can be of an arbitrary type whereas in the first
part, they are of ground type, but this is essentially the only
difference.

Indeed in Section
2
we computed partial
derivatives of morphisms

\displaystyle t\in\mathbf{Pcoh}(\oc{X},\mathsf{N})

where
X=\mathsf{N}\mathrel{\&}\cdots\mathrel{\&}\mathsf{N}
(
k
copies). More precisely,
the
t
’s we consider in that section are such that if

t_{\vec{\mu},n}\not=0
then
n=0
and each
\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{fin}}({{\mathbb{N}}})

satisfies
\operatorname{\mathsf{supp}}({\mu_{i}})\subseteq\{0\}
. So actually we can consider
such a
t
as a an element of

\mathbf{Pcoh}(\oc({1\mathrel{\&}\cdots\mathrel{\&}1}),1)
which induces a
function

\displaystyle\widehat{t}:\mathsf{P}{(1\mathrel{\&}\cdots\mathrel{\&}1)}\simeq[0,1]^{k}

\displaystyle\to\mathsf{P}{1}\simeq[0,1]

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}}t_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}^{n_{i}}

where
t_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for each
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let

Y=1\mathrel{\&}\cdots\mathrel{\&}1
. Let
\vec{x}\in\mathsf{P}{\mathsf{N}}^{k}
and

x\in\mathsf{P}{Y}
so that
x
can be seen as a tuple

(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\in[0,1]
and
\|{x}\|_{Y}=\max_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}
. It
follows that
{Y}_{x}
can be described as follows:

\displaystyle|{{Y}_{x}}|

\displaystyle=\{i\mid 1\leq i\leq k\text{ and }x_{i}<1\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}{({Y}_{x})}

\displaystyle=\{u\in(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})^{|{{Y}_{x}}|}\mid\forall i\in|{{Y}_{x}}|\ x_{i}+u_{i}\leq 1\}\,.

and then we have defined the differential of
t
,

\displaystyle{t}^{\prime}(x)\in\mathbf{Pcoh}({Y}_{x},1)

in Section
3.2
. This differential relates as
follows with the partial derivatives used in
Section
2
:

\displaystyle\forall u\in\mathsf{P}{{Y}_{x}}\quad{{t}^{\prime}(x)}\cdot{u}=\sum_{i\in|{{Y}_{x}}|}t^{\prime}_{i}(x)u_{i}

where
t^{\prime}_{i}(x)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the
i
th partial derivative of the
function
\widehat{t}
at
x
. Notice that in
Section
2
we use slightly more general partial
derivatives computed also at indices
i
such that
x_{i}=1
(which are
actually left derivatives since the function can be undefined for

x_{i}>1
) where they can take infinite values, in accordance with the
fact that the expectation of the number of steps can be
\infty
like
in the example
\varphi_{0.5}
, see Fig.
3
. This
is not allowed in Section
3.2
where we insist on
keeping all derivatives finite for upper-bounding them.

We think that these preliminary results provide motivations for
investigating further differential extensions of
\mathsf{pPCF}
and related
languages in the spirit of the differential
lambda-calculus
[
ER03
]
.
