Bringing together two paradigms of non-equilibrium: Fragile versus robust aging in driven glassy systems
Diego Tapias, Charles Marteau, Fabián Aguirre-López, Peter Sollich

Abstract
There are two key paradigms for non-equilibrium dynamics: on the one hand, aging towards an equilibrium state that cannot be reached on reasonable timescales; on the other, external driving that can lead to non-equilibrium steady states. We explore how these two mechanisms interact, by studying the behaviour of trap models, which are paradigmatic descriptions of slow glassy dynamics, when driven by trajectory bias towards high or low activity. To diagnose whether the driven systems continue to age, we establish a framework for mapping the biased dynamics to a Markovian time evolution with time-dependent transition rates. We find that the original aging dynamics reacts in two qualitatively distinct ways to the driving: it can be destroyed by driving of any nonzero strength ( fragile aging), whereby the dynamics either reaches an active steady state or effectively freezes; or it can persist within a finite range of driving strengths around the undriven case ( robust aging). This classification into fragile and robust aging could form the basis for distinguishing different universality classes of aging dynamics.

Summarizing, we have shown that the interplay of aging and driving by trajectory bias leads to significant new insights into aging dynamics. In the trap models we studied, which in the absence of driving exhibit aging below their glass transition temperature, this aging dynamics can respond in two fundamentally different ways to driving. In the BM model for
T>1/2
and in the Bouchaud model, aging is fragile: any drive towards high activity (
s<0
) produces an active steady state, while driving towards low activity leads to frozen dynamics arresting after a finite number of jumps. For
T<1/2
in the BM model, on the other hand, aging is robust, remaining qualitatively unchanged by moderate driving towards either higher or lower activity. We demonstrated these differences using a general analysis framework for Markovian systems driven by trajectory bias, which is complete in the sense that it can be deployed whether or not the underlying undriven system exhibits aging. What is fascinating in particular is that the response to driving detects, without additional physical input via e.g. specifically designed coarse-graining
Cammarota and Marinari 2015
;
Cammarota and Marinari 2018
;
Baity-Jesi et al. 2018
, the qualitative differences between aging dynamics governed by energetic barriers (as in the Bouchaud model and the BM model with
T>1/2
) – which we found to be fragile – and by entropic effects. The resulting robust aging behavior that we found in the low–temperature BM model may have broader implications for the physics of supercooled liquids, bearing in mind a recent proposal that entropic relaxation is dominant in this regime
Baity-Jesi et al. 2021
. To shed further light on the nature of the aging phases one could convert our results into statements about activity fluctuations in the unbiased dynamics.
We do this in
SM
for the Bouchaud model; an analogous calculation for the BM model, yielding additional signatures of robust aging, remains an exciting open challenge.
Generally, the response of aging systems to driving that we have studied here, by bringing together the two key non-equilibrium paradigms, provides new analysis tools for non-equilibrium dynamics and could eventually lead to a classification of aging into distinct universality classes.

(S1)

with

r(\mathcal{C})=\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}}W(\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C^{\prime}})

the escape rate from configuration
\mathcal{C}
. We define the vector
|P(\tau)\rangle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}p(\mathcal{C},\tau)|\mathcal{C}\rangle
(we use bra-ket notation here;
|\mathcal{C}\rangle
can simply be viewed as the Euclidean unit vector in direction
\mathcal{C}
) and introduce the compact notation
\partial_{\tau}|P(\tau)\rangle=\mathbb{W}|P(\tau)\rangle
where the elements of the master operator
\mathbb{W}
are
\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}|\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\rangle=W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C})(1-\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})-\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}r(\mathcal{C})
.

We consider current-like trajectory observables of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\sum_{{\rm{jumps}}\,\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\alpha(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})

(S2)

where
\alpha(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=1
(for
\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
) gives the activity but other choices can be used to capture currents of particles or entropy etc. We are interested in trajectory ensembles that force the average
\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle
to be far
from its average value
\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{0}
. This is most conveniently done not via a hard constraint but with a bias in the probability assigned to each trajectory
\pi
:

P[\pi,s]=\frac{P[\pi,0]\exp\left(-s\mathcal{A}\right)}{Z(s,t)}

(S3)

After discretizing the total trajectory length
t
into small steps
\Delta t
, a trajectory
\pi
is represented as a sequence of configurations
\{\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\}
for
\tau=0,\Delta t,2\Delta t,\ldots,t
. In terms of the original master operator
\mathbb{W}
, the biased probability of a trajectory
can then be written as

\displaystyle P[\pi,s]

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[\prod_{\tau=0}^{t-\Delta t}\langle\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\Delta t}|(\mathbbold{1}+\Delta t\mathbb{W})|\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-s\alpha(\mathcal{C}_{\tau},\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\Delta t})}\right]}{Z(s,t)}\times p(\mathcal{C}_{0},0)

(S4)

with a generic initial distribution
|P(0)\rangle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}p(\mathcal{C},0)|\mathcal{C}\rangle
. Here we use the convention
\alpha(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=0
for
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, so that the reweighting factor in eq. (
S4
) contributes only when the configuration changes, as defined in (
S2
). Because of this, the factors in the product can be seen as matrix elements of a biased master operator defined as (see also
Lecomte et al. 2007
;
Garrahan et al. 2009
)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}|\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-s\alpha(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\mathcal{C})}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C})\mathrm{e}^{-s\alpha(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\mathcal{C})},&\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\\
-r(\mathcal{C}),&\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C^{\prime}}.\end{array}\right.

Summing equation (
S4
) over all possible trajectories gives the normalization factor or partition function

Z(s,t)=\langle e|(\mathbbold{1}+\Delta t\mathbb{W}(s))^{t/\Delta t}|P(0)\rangle\to\langle e|\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{W}(s)t}|P(0)\rangle

(S7)

where
\langle e|=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\langle\mathcal{C}|
is a projection state.

If the original stochastic model has a finite state space and is irreducible, one sees from the spectral decomposition of the biased master operator

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}(s)=\sum_{\alpha}|V_{\alpha}\rangle\lambda_{\alpha}\langle U_{\alpha}|

(S8)

that the dynamical free energy (as introduced in eq. (4) in the manuscript) has a long–time limit
Lebowitz and Spohn 1999
;
Lecomte et al. 2007
;
Jack and Sollich 2010
;
Jack 2020
and is explicitly given by

\psi(s)=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln Z(s,t)=\max_{\alpha}\lambda_{\alpha}.

(S9)

Thus the dynamical free energy is the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{W}(s)
, which dominates the exponential in (
S7
) for large
t
. If

\mathbb{W}(s)
has a spectral gap between
between its largest and second largest eigenvalue, then the biased dynamics approaches a steady state away from the ends (
\tau
close to
0
or
t
) of a long enough trajectory. The extent of the temporal boundary regions is given by the inverse spectral gap.

The systems that we study in the manuscript are interesting because they do not necessarily exhibit a spectral gap. As a matter of fact, in the aging regime the associated relaxation timescales grow without bound (see Refs.
Margiotta et al. 2018
;
Tapias et al. 2020
for the spectral distribution of the relaxation rates in Bouchaud and BM models, respectively). Then even the unbiased master operator
\mathbb{W}
has eigenvalues arbitrarily close to the largest eigenvalue of zero, i.e. no gap in the spectrum. In order to analyse these systems and in particular the time-dependence along their trajectories, one needs to introduce a time–dependent
auxiliary master operator
; this construction is described below.

.2
Construction of time-dependent auxiliary Markov operator

The need for this construction arises from the fact that the biased path probability (
S4
) is not in the usual form for stochastic Markov dynamics. This is because the corresponding biased master operator does not in general conserve probability, i.e.
\langle e|\mathbb{W}(s)\neq 0
. However a multiplicative (“Doob”) transform of
\mathbb{W}(s)
can be used to bring it back into the standard form, even for systems without a spectral gap that can exhibit aging. This was shown by Chetrite and Touchette
Chetrite and Touchette 2013
;
Chetrite and Touchette 2015a
in rather abstract terms for diffusion processes; we make it concrete here for Markov jump processes on discrete state spaces.

One starts by introducing multiplicative factors
q(\mathcal{C},\tau)
into the path probability (
S4
):

\displaystyle P[\pi,s]=\frac{q(\mathcal{C}_{0},0)p(\mathcal{C}_{0},0)}{Z(s,t)}\left[\prod_{\tau=0}^{t-\Delta t}q(\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\Delta t},\tau+\Delta t)\langle\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\Delta t}|(\mathbbold{1}+\Delta t\mathbb{W}(s))|\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\rangle q^{-1}(\mathcal{C}_{\tau},\tau)\right]

(S10)

These factors all cancel except for the one at the final time, which therefore has to be
q(\mathcal{C},t)=1
. The other factors should be chosen so that for every timestep one has a probability-conserving transition matrix, which requires

\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau+\Delta t)\langle\mathcal{C^{\prime}}|(\mathbbold{1}+\Delta t\mathbb{W}(s))|\mathcal{C}\rangle q^{-1}(\mathcal{C},\tau)=1

(S11)

Given the reweighting factors
q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau+\Delta t)
at some time, the factors
q(\mathcal{C},\tau)
at the previous timestep are therefore determined. If one collects the
q
-factors in a time-dependent vector
\langle q(\tau)|=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\langle\mathcal{C}|q(\mathcal{C},\tau)
, the relation (
S11
) reads

\langle q(\tau)|=\langle q(\tau+\Delta t)|(\mathbbold{1}+\Delta t\mathbb{W}(s))

(S12)

or in the continuous time limit
\Delta t\to 0

-\partial_{\tau}\langle q(\tau)|=\langle q(\tau)|\mathbb{W}(s)\,,

(S13)

which is the
backward
master equation for the biased master operator. Together with the final-time boundary condition
\langle q(t)|=\langle e|
one gets the formal solution

\langle q(\tau)|=\langle e|\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{W}(s)(t-\tau)}\,.

(S14)

Once
\langle q(\tau)|
has been found, the auxiliary master operator
\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau)
can be determined. For this, we use that the contribution from each timestep in (
S10
) must equal
\langle\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\Delta t}|(\mathbbold{1}+\Delta t\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau))|\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\rangle
. Dividing both sides of the resulting equality by
\Delta t
, and simplifying the notation by replacing
\mathcal{C}_{\tau+\Delta t}\to\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}\to\mathcal{C}
we get

\displaystyle\frac{q(\mathcal{C},\tau+\Delta t)-q(\mathcal{C},\tau)}{q(\mathcal{C},\tau)\Delta t}\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}+\frac{q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau+\Delta t)}{q(\mathcal{C},\tau)}\langle\mathcal{C^{\prime}}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C}\rangle=\langle\mathcal{C^{\prime}}|\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau)|\mathcal{C}\rangle

(S15)

For
\Delta t\to 0
this simplifies (using (
S13
)) to

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C^{\prime}}|\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau)|\mathcal{C}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)}{q(\mathcal{C},\tau)}\langle\mathcal{C^{\prime}}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C}\rangle-\frac{\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}}{q(\mathcal{C},\tau)}\sum_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}q(\tilde{\mathcal{C}},\tau)\langle\tilde{\mathcal{C}}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C}\rangle

(S16)

Therefore the time-dependent auxiliary master operator
\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau)
is obtained by reweighting all transition rates of the unbiased master operator by an additional multiplicative correction, i.e.

W^{\rm aux}(\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)=W(\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C^{\prime}})\mathrm{e}^{-s\alpha(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})}\frac{q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)}{q(\mathcal{C},\tau)}

(S17)

for all
\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
. Equation (
S17
) corresponds to equation (9) in the manuscript.

Before proceeding we comment briefly on our notation for the “backwards factor”
q
. From equation (
S14
), it is clear that the dependence of
q
on
\tau
is via the time difference
t-\tau
. We generally leave the
t
-dependence implicit and write either
\langle q(\tau)|
or
q(\mathcal{C},\tau)
as used above. For concrete calculations below in the thermodynamic limit, where we will need to perform Laplace transforms with respect to the variable
t-\tau
, we will use the notation
q(E,\tau)=Q(E,t-\tau)
where appropriate.

Having established the auxiliary transition rates in (
S17
), it now remains to find the auxiliary escape rates, i.e. the (negative) diagonal terms of the auxiliary master operator. These follow by combining equations (
S13
) and (
S16
) as

r_{\tau}^{\rm aux}(\mathcal{C})=-\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau)|\mathcal{C}\rangle=-\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C}\rangle-\partial_{\tau}\ln q(\mathcal{C},\tau)

(S18)

With the above results in hand, we proceed to show the relation between the mean auxiliary escape rate and the time-dependent activity (equation (12)). The argument is in fact the same as for standard Markov jump processes, but we include it here for completeness. Let us write the mean contribution to
\mathcal{A}
from jumps between time
\tau=k\Delta t
und
\tau+\Delta t
as

\displaystyle\langle\alpha(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}_{k+1},\mathcal{C}_{k}}P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})\alpha(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})

(S19)

Here the weighting is with the joint probability
P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})
in the biased process of the two configurations at the beginning and end of the time interval,
\mathcal{C}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
. This probability is
P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})=P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\tau)\,\Delta t\,W^{\rm{aux}}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\to\mathcal{C}_{k+1},\tau)
(provided the two configurations are distinct; if they are identical then there is no contribution to (
S19
) anyway). It is for this step that the probability-conserving nature of
\mathbb{W}^{\rm aux}(\tau)
is crucial; had we used
\mathbb{W}(s)
, the sum over the later states in the trajectory would have given an additional nontrivial factor, which in fact is proportional to
q(\mathcal{C}_{k},\tau)
.

Inserting the expression for
P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})
, the mean contribution to our trajectory observable
\mathcal{A}
between timesteps
k
and
k+1
is

\displaystyle\langle\alpha(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}_{k+1},\mathcal{C}_{k}}P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\tau)W^{\rm{aux}}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\to\mathcal{C}_{k+1},\tau)\alpha(\mathcal{C}_{k},\mathcal{C}_{k+1})\,dt

(S20)

For the activity, where
\alpha(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime})=1-\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
, the sum over
\mathcal{C}_{k+1}
just gives the escape rate. Summing also over all timesteps up to
K=t/\Delta t
to get the total activity
\mathcal{A}
one has

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\sum_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}P_{s}(\mathcal{C}_{k},\tau)r^{\rm{aux}}_{\tau}(\mathcal{C}_{k})dt

(S21)

In the continuous time limit this reads

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\langle r^{\rm{aux}}_{\tau}\rangle_{s}\equiv\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{s}

(S22)

where the last equivalence is just the definition of the time-dependent activity
a_{\tau}
. Overall, one sees that the mean time-dependent activity
\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{s}
is just

\langle r^{\rm{aux}}_{\tau}\rangle_{s}
, the average escape rate (calculated from the auxiliary master operator) in the biased process. This demonstrates equation (12) in the manuscript.

For concrete calculations based on the above formalism we need an expression for the probability (again in the driven process) of being in configuration
\mathcal{C}
at time
\tau
,
P_{s}(\mathcal{C},\tau)
. If we sum the trajectory probability (
S10
) over the configurations at all other times we get

\displaystyle P_{s}(\mathcal{C},\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle e|\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{W}(s)(t-\tau)}|\mathcal{C}\rangle\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{W}(s)\tau}|P(0)\rangle}{Z(s,t)}

(S23)

The first factor in the numerator is
q(\mathcal{C},\tau)
; we define the second one as
P_{\rm f}(\mathcal{C},\tau)
. To obtain eq. (13) in the main text we just need to show that this solves the forward master equation for
\mathbb{W}(s)
. But this follows directly because

P_{\rm f}(\mathcal{C},\tau)=\langle\mathcal{C}|P_{\rm f}(\tau)\ \ \mbox{with}\ \ |P_{\rm f}(\tau)\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{W}(s)\tau}|P(0)\rangle

(S24)

and because

{\partial_{\tau}}|P_{\rm f}(\tau)\rangle=\mathbb{W}(s)|P_{\rm f}(\tau)\rangle

(S25)

Concerning the physical interpretation, one sees that
P_{\rm f}(\mathcal{C},\tau)
contains the effect of the bias propagated forwards from the past. Note that even though
P_{\rm f}(\mathcal{C},\tau)
is calculated from
\mathbb{W}(s)
and the initial distribution
P(\mathcal{C},0)
exactly in the same way as for conventional Markov dynamics, it is not a normalized probability distribution because
\mathbb{W}(s)
does not conserve probability.

The factor
q(\mathcal{C},\tau)
can now be interpreted similarly as the effect of the bias acting in the future, propagated backwards to the current time. Equation (
S23
) shows that the actual probability of being in configuration
\mathcal{C}
is simply a product of these past and future factors, suitably normalized by
Z(s,t)
. From equations (
S7
) and (
S23
) one also deduces that the normalization factor is nothing but

\displaystyle Z(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}P_{\rm f}(\mathcal{C},t)

(S26)

Having set out the general construction for driven aging systems, we describe briefly how it simplifies in the conventional scenario where the driven (biased) dynamics reaches a steady state because the biased master operator has a gap in its spectrum below the leading eigenvalue
\psi(s)
. In such a scenario, the exponential of
\mathbb{W}(s)
is dominated by the largest eigenvalue in (
S8
) at times longer than the inverse spectral gap. This largest eigenvalue is
\psi(s)
, so that

\displaystyle Z(s,t)

\displaystyle=\langle e|V_{0}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{\psi(s)t}\langle U_{0}|P(0)\rangle,

(S27)

\displaystyle\langle q(\tau)|

\displaystyle=\langle e|V_{0}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{\psi(s)(t-\tau)}\langle U_{0}|,

(S28)

\displaystyle|P_{\rm f}(\tau)\rangle

\displaystyle=|V_{0}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{\psi(s)\tau}\langle U_{0}|P(0)\rangle

(S29)

with
U_{0}
and
V_{0}
the top left and right eigenvectors of the biased master operator
\mathbb{W}(s)
. Bearing in mind the eigenvector normalization
\langle U_{0}|V_{0}\rangle=1
, one thus retrieves the known
Garrahan et al. 2009
;
Jack and Sollich 2010

\tau
-independent steady state distribution
P_{s}(\mathcal{C},\tau)=\langle U_{0}|\mathcal{C}\rangle\langle\mathcal{C}|V_{0}\rangle
. One also finds that the escape rate shift
-\partial_{\tau}\ln q(\mathcal{C},\tau)=\psi(s)
is just the dynamical free energy,

\displaystyle r^{\rm aux}(\mathcal{C})=r(\mathcal{C})+\psi(s)

(S30)

which is in agreement with previous results
Evans 2004b
;
Evans 2004a
(see also
Jack and Sollich 2010
) and leads to (14) in the main text.

Summarizing, in this section we have extended existing results for the representation of driven
s
–ensemble dynamics in terms of a physical (“auxiliary”) master operator
to the case where the
\tau
-dependence of the latter cannot be ignored, for instance because of the existence of aging effects.
The key identities are (
S17
) and (
S18
) for the elements of the auxiliary master operator, the equations (
S13
) and (
S25
) governing the backward and forward factors, the resulting expression for the
\tau
-dependent configuration probabilities (
S23
) and finally, for the important case of driving by activity bias, the expression (
S22
) for the total and time-dependent activities.

We note finally that the formalism applies without changes also if one adds to the biasing observable a time-integrated contribution of the form

\Delta t\sum_{k}b(\mathcal{C}_{k})=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\,b(\mathcal{C}_{\tau})

(S31)

The biased master operator then merely acquires an additional contribution of
-s\,b(\mathcal{C})
to its diagonal element
\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C}\rangle
, with a corresponding additional term
+s\,b(\mathcal{C})
on the r.h.s. of (
S30
).

.3
Thermodynamic limit and top eigenvector systems

In this subsection we show how to rewrite the master equation (
S1
) in the thermodynamic limit of an infinite number of traps identified by their depth
E
, which in the limit becomes a continuous variable; the result is eq. (3) in the main text. We also derive the thermodynamic limit version of the forward (eq. (
S25
)) and backward (eq. (
S13
)) master equations. We additionally write the discrete and continuous version of the top eigenvalue system for the left and right eigenvectors.

As in the main text we assume that each configuration (or trap)
\mathcal{C}
has an energy (trap depth)
E_{\mathcal{C}}
. The transition rates are taken to be functions of the energies only and we normalize them so that the escape rates in a system of
N
traps with mean-field connectivity are
O(1)
, hence

\displaystyle W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{N}W(E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\to E_{\mathcal{C}})

(S32)

where the difference between the Bouchand and BM models lies only in the function
W(E\to E^{\prime})
.
We introduce the probability for the system to be in a trap with depth
E
at time
\tau
as
P(E,\tau)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}P(\mathcal{C},\tau)\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})
and rewrite equation (
S1
) as an equation for
P(E,t)
. This only requires multiplying both sides by
\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})
and summing over all configurations:

\displaystyle{\partial_{\tau}}P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}}W(E_{\mathcal{C}}\to E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})P(\mathcal{C},\tau)\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}}W(E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\to E_{\mathcal{C}})P(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})

(S33)

The constraint on the sums can safely removed without changing the equality as the terms with
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
cancel. In the transition rate factor of the last term we can replace
E_{\mathcal{C}}
by
E
because of the Dirac delta; the remaining sum over
\mathcal{C}
then gives the density of states
\rho(E)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}W(E\to E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})P(E,\tau)+\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}W(E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\to E)P(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)\rho(E)

(S34)

Finally, we introduce a factor of
1=\int dE^{\prime}\,\delta(E^{\prime}-E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})
on the r.h.s. of equation (
S34
) and perform the sum over
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
. The result is

\displaystyle{\partial_{\tau}}P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-r(E)P(E,\tau)+\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}P(E^{\prime},\tau)W(E^{\prime}\to E)

(S35)

which corresponds to equation (3) in the main text,
with the escape rate

r(E)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}W(E\to E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})=\int dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})

(S36)

Note that the escape rate for finite
N
is
r_{\mathcal{C}}=1/N\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}}W(E_{\mathcal{C}}\to E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})
. Because of the restriction
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}
this differs from
r(E)
by a term of
O(1/N)
, but this becomes irrelevant in the thermodynamic limit
N\to\infty
. Such corrections are ignored by default in all derivations that follow.

The calculation for the continuous limit of the forward master equation (eq. (
S25
)) is essentially the same as for the unbiased case. One only has to consider the biased master operator as defined in (
.1
) instead of the conventional one. The result is, for
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle{\partial_{\tau}}P_{\rm f}(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-r(E)P_{\rm f}(E,\tau)+{\rm{e}}^{-s}\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}P_{\rm f}(E^{\prime},\tau)W(E^{\prime}\to E)

(S37)

For the backward equation (eq. (
S13
)) we show the calculation in more detail because some care is required in the definition of the thermodynamic limit of the backward factor:

\displaystyle q(E,\tau)\rho(E)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})

(S38)

The factor
\rho(E)
on the l.h.s. is needed because in the unbiased case the backward factor should reduce to a unit constant,
q(E,\tau)=q(\mathcal{C},\tau)=1
, corresponding to the conservation of probability. From (
S14
) the same has to hold generally, even in the presence of a trajectory bias, at the final time
\tau=t
.
We now start from equation (
S13
), insert the decomposition
\langle q(\tau)|=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)\langle C|

and multiply by
|\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\rangle
from the right to project onto configuration
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
:

\displaystyle-\sum_{\mathcal{C}}{\partial_{\tau}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\rangle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)\langle\mathcal{C}|\mathbb{W}(s)|\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\rangle

(S39)

\displaystyle-{\partial_{\tau}}q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)\big[W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C}){\rm{e}}^{-s}(1-\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})-r(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})\delta_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\big]

(S40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C}){\rm{e}}^{-s}-q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C})

(S41)

where in the last line we have used the definition (for finite
N
) of the escape rate
r(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})
.
Now express the transition rates in terms of energies (eq. (
S32
)), multiply both sides of the equation by
\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})/N
and sum over all configurations
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
. This yields

\displaystyle-\rho(E){\partial_{\tau}}q(E,\tau)=\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-s}}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)W(E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\to E_{\mathcal{C}})\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}q(\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\tau)W(E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\to E_{\mathcal{C}})\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}})

(S42)

The constraint
\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
only gives
O(1/N)
corrections so can be dropped; performing the sum over
\mathcal{C^{\prime}}
then yields

\displaystyle-\rho(E){\partial_{\tau}}q(E,\tau)={\rm{e}}^{-s}\frac{\rho(E)}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C}}q(\mathcal{C},\tau)W(E\to E_{\mathcal{C}})-\frac{1}{N}q(E,\tau)\rho(E)\sum_{\mathcal{C}}W(E\to E_{\mathcal{C}})

(S43)

Finally we cancel the common factor of
\rho(E)
and again switch to an integration over trap depths on the r.h.s. by inserting
1=\int dE^{\prime}\delta(E^{\prime}-E_{\mathcal{C}})
and performing the remaining sum over
\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle-{\partial_{\tau}}q(E,\tau)

\displaystyle={\rm{e}}^{-s}\int dE^{\prime}q(E^{\prime},\tau)W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})-q(E,\tau)\int dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})

(S44)

As the final integral is just
r(E)
, this is equation (10) in the main text.

In the explicit calculations below we will often work with Laplace transforms. As
q(E,\tau)
depends on the trajectory length
t
and the running time
\tau
only via the time difference
\Delta t=t-\tau
(cf. eq. (
S14
)), it will then be helpful to work with the quantity
Q(E,\Delta t)=q(E,\tau)
, for which equation (
S44
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta t}Q(E,\Delta t)

\displaystyle=-r(E)Q(E,\Delta t)+{\rm{e}}^{-s}\int dE^{\prime}Q(E^{\prime},\Delta t)W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})

(S45)

We finally write down, for later reference, the equations for the top left and top right eigenvectors of the biased master operator, in the discrete case (finite
N
) and in the continuous (
N\to\infty
) limit. These eigenvectors correspond directly to solutions of the backward and forward master equations, respectively,
with the time derivatives replaced by the top eigenvalue, which according to eq. (
S9
) is the dynamical free energy
\psi(s)
.
For the top right eigenvector we use the notation
V_{0}(\mathcal{C})
in the discrete case. The eigenvalue problem then reads

\displaystyle-V_{0}(\mathcal{C})r(\mathcal{C})+{\rm{e}}^{-s}\sum_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}}V_{0}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C})=\psi(s)V_{0}(\mathcal{C})

(S46)

and becomes in the continuous limit (using that
V_{0}(E)=\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})V_{0}(\mathcal{C})
)

\displaystyle-V_{0}(E)r(E)

\displaystyle+{\rm{e}}^{-s}\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}W(E^{\prime}\to E)V_{0}(E^{\prime})=\psi(s)V_{0}(E)

(S47)

Notice that the left hand side is exactly the same as the right hand side of equation (
S37
) with
P_{\rm f}
replaced by
V_{0}
, as it should be.

For the left eigenvector
U_{0}(\mathcal{C})
we have the following condition for a discrete set of states

\displaystyle-U_{0}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})r(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})+\sum_{\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}U_{0}(\mathcal{C})W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C}){\rm{e}}^{-s}=\psi(s)U_{0}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})

(S48)

The continuous limit reads (with
U_{0}(E)\rho(E)=1/N\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\delta(E-E_{\mathcal{C}})U_{0}(\mathcal{C})
)

\displaystyle-U_{0}(E)r(E)

\displaystyle+{\rm{e}^{-s}}\int dE^{\prime}\rho(E^{\prime})W(E\to E^{\prime})U_{0}(E^{\prime})=\psi(s)U_{0}(E)

(S49)

with the l.h.s. again matching eq. (
S44
). Note that because the activity is invariant under time-reversal and because the undriven dynamics obeys detailed balance, i.e.
e^{\beta E}W(E\to E^{\prime})=e^{\beta E^{\prime}}W(E^{\prime}\to E)
, the left and right eigenvector problems can be related to each other and one finds in particular that
V_{0}(E)\propto U_{0}(E)\rho(E)e^{\beta E}
.

.4
Bouchaud trap model

In this section we provide calculational details for the activity–biased dynamics in the Bouchaud trap model. We start for completeness with the calculation of the mean time–averaged activity in the unbiased case, which will act as a baseline for the results in the presence of driving by trajectory bias.

We note that as the Bouchaud trap model is a renewal process, some of our results can be related to those from the classical reference
Godreche and Luck 2001
. We will comment on the specific instances of this connection below. The link
is made via the distribution of interarrival times, i.e. times between jumps, which is given by

\displaystyle\rho(\tau)=\int_{1}^{\infty}d\tilde{\tau}\,\frac{1}{\tilde{\tau}}\exp(-\tau/\tilde{\tau})\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{\tau})

(S50)

in terms of
\tilde{\rho}(\tilde{\tau})=T\tilde{\tau}^{-(T+1)}
, the mean trapping time distribution (which in turn follows from
\rho(E)=\mathrm{e}^{-E}
and
\tilde{\tau}=1/r(E)=\mathrm{e}^{E/T}
). Evaluating the integral in (
S50
) gives

\displaystyle\rho(\tau)=T\tau^{-(T+1)}[T\Gamma(T)-\Gamma(1+T,\tau)]

(S51)

with
\Gamma(\cdot)
and
\Gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
the complete and (upper) incomplete Gamma function, respectively. The latter can be neglected asymptotically and a comparison with equation (1.6) from reference
Godreche and Luck 2001
then reveals that the mapping between the notation there and here is
\theta\mapsto T
,
\tau_{0}\mapsto(T\Gamma(T))^{1/T}
.

.4.1
Zero bias

Our starting point is the master equation (3) with the appropriate transition rates (eq. (1)) and escape rates
r(E)=W(E\to E^{\prime})=\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}
:

\displaystyle{\partial_{\tau}}P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}P(E,\tau)+\rho(E)\int_{0}^{\infty}dE^{\prime}\,{\rm{e}}^{-E^{\prime}/T}P(E^{\prime},\tau)

(S52)

We consider throughout a uniform distribution across traps as initial condition, corresponding to
P(E,0)=\rho(E)={\rm{e}}^{-E}
. Solving the dynamical equation in the Laplace domain
Monthus and Bouchaud 1996
, with
z
conjugate to
\tau
, a straightforward calculation yields

\displaystyle\hat{P}(E,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-E}}{(z+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T})f(z)}

(S53)

with (all integrations over
E
run from 0 to
\infty
in what follows)

f(z)=\int dE\,\frac{z\,{\rm{e}}^{-E}}{z+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}}={}_{2}F_{1}(1,T,1+T,-1/z)

(S54)

which as shown can be expressed in terms of the Gauss hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}
. The mean time–dependent activity, which is equal to the mean escape rate by eq. (
S22
), is

\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{0}=\int dE\,P(E,\tau)\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}

(S55)

and has Laplace transform

\langle\hat{a}(z)\rangle_{0}=\int dE\,\hat{P}(E,z)\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}=\frac{1}{f(z)}\int dE\,\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-E-E/T}}{z+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}}=\frac{1-f(z)}{f(z)}

(S56)

While the above expressions are pleasingly explicit, the inverse Laplace transform cannot be obtained in closed form. We therefore focus on the physically most interesting limit of long times,
z\to 0
. With the variable change
x=\mathrm{e}^{-E}
we obtain

f(z)=\int_{0}^{1}dx\,\frac{z}{z+x^{\beta}}

(S57)

where
\beta=1/T
as usual. For temperatures below the glass transition,
T<1
, the substitution
y=x/z^{T}
transforms this to

f(z)=z^{T}\int_{0}^{z^{-T}}\!\!\frac{dy}{1+y^{\beta}}

(S58)

For
z\to 0
the upper integration boundary diverges and the integral evaluates to
\pi T/\sin(\pi T)
. Combining with the straightforward expansion for
T>1
, one has overall for small
z

f(z)\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}z/(1-\beta)&\mbox{for}&T>1\\
z^{T}\frac{\pi T}{\sin(\pi T)}&\mbox{for}&T<1\end{array}\right.

(S59)

Inserting into (
S56
) and transforming back to the time domain shows that for long times the activity
\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{0}
approaches the constant value
1-\beta
when
T>1
, indicating a conventional equilibrium state. For
T<1
, on the other hand,

\displaystyle\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sin(\pi T)}{\pi T\Gamma(T)}\tau^{T-1}

(S60)

This prediction compares well against results from numerical solutions of the master equation as shown in Fig.
S1
. The mean total activity can be obtained as the integral of the last expression (cf. eq. (
S22
)), with the result

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{0}\approx\frac{\sin(\pi T)}{\pi T^{2}\Gamma(T)}t^{T}

(S61)

which corresponds exactly to equation (3.6) in reference
Godreche and Luck 2001
.

.4.2
Bias towards high activity

Our starting point is the right eigenvalue equation (
S47
) with the appropriate transition and escape rates (see eq. (1))

\displaystyle\psi(s)V_{0}(E)

\displaystyle=-{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}V_{0}(E)+{\rm{e}}^{-s}\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}{\rm{e}}^{-E^{\prime}/T}V_{0}(E^{\prime})

(S62)

which may be rearranged to read

\displaystyle V_{0}(E)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-s}\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}{\rm{e}}^{-E^{\prime}/T}V_{0}(E^{\prime})}{\psi(s)+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}}\propto\frac{\rho(E)}{\psi(s)+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}}

(S63)

Inserting back into eq. (
S62
) gives the condition

\int dE\,\rho(E)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}}{\psi(s)+\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}}=\mathrm{e}^{s}

(S64)

For the case of an exponential density of states that we consider throughout,
\rho(E)=\mathrm{e}^{-E}
for
E>0
, the function on the l.h.s. becomes simply
1-f(\psi(s))
with
f
defined in (
S54
), giving

f(\psi(s))=1-\mathrm{e}^{s}

(S65)

The function
f(z)
increases monotonically with
z
from
f(0)=0
to
f(z\to\infty)=1
; the condition (
S65
) therefore has exactly one positive solution
\psi(s)
for all
s<0
. The other eigenvalues are all negative as one can see from the finite-
N
form of the eigenvalue condition given in (
S73
) below. As the discussion there shows, the second eigenvalue approaches zero for
N\to\infty
so that the spectrum has a gap, of size
\psi(s)
.

To summarize, for bias towards high activity (

) the dynamical free energy of the Bouchaud model is obtained by solving equation (
S64
) with an exponential density of states, or explicitly (
S65
). The resulting free energy is plotted as a function of
s

is nonzero for

so that we always obtain a nonzero time–averaged activity: stationarity in the Bouchaud model is restored by bias to high activity for all temperatures, even those (
T<1
) where the unbiased model is in its glassy phase and exhibits aging.

Differences between the regime below and above the glass transition become visible, however, if we consider the behaviour of the dynamical free energy around
s=0
. For small negative
s
we can expand the exponential in equation (
S64
) as
{\rm{e}}^{s}=1-|s|
, giving the condition

. Using then the small-

behaviour (
S59
) of

one finds
that around the phase boundary
s=0
, the dynamical free energy on the active side (
s<0
) behaves as

\displaystyle\psi(s)

\displaystyle\approx(1-\beta)|s|\,,\qquad T>1

(S66)

\displaystyle\psi(s)

\displaystyle\approx b_{0}|s|^{\beta}\,,\qquad T<1

(S67)

with
b_{0}=\left({\sin(\pi T)}/{\pi T}\right)^{1/T}
.
Equations (
S66
) and (
S67
) tell us that the dynamical phase transition is first order at high temperatures, but continuous in the temperature range where the unbiased system is glassy. Correspondingly, the time–averaged activity
\langle{a}\rangle_{s}=\psi^{\prime}(s)
has a jump at the transition for
T>1
, while it approaches zero continuously for
T<1
(cf. Fig.
S2
(right)).

Let us connect the above result with those of reference
Godreche and Luck 2001
, focussing on the glassy regime
T<1
. One can exploit the Gärtner–Ellis theorem to compute the rate function
I(a)
of the time–averaged activity
\bar{a}=\mathcal{A}/t
in the long–time limit, see e.g.
Jack 2020
, as

\displaystyle I(\bar{a})=\sup_{s}[-s\bar{a}-\psi(s)]

(S68)

Using now equation (
S67
) (together with
\psi(s)=0
for
s>0
, see next subsection), one finds directly that the scaling of the rate function around the minimum
a=0
must be (for
\bar{a}>0
)

\displaystyle I(\bar{a})\sim\bar{a}^{\frac{1}{1-T}}

(S69)

Thus to exponential order for large
t
, the distribution of the time–averaged activity (at zero bias) is

\displaystyle P(\bar{a})\asymp\mathrm{e}^{-tI(\bar{a})}\sim\mathrm{e}^{-ct\bar{a}^{1/(1-T)}}

(S70)

with
\asymp
indicating an equality up to subexponential prefactors and

some

S70
) translates into

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{A})\asymp\mathrm{e}^{-ct^{-T/(1-T)}\mathcal{A}^{1/(1-T)}}=\mathrm{e}^{-c(\mathcal{A}/t^{T})^{1/(1-T)}}

(S71)

This scaling of the activity distribution corresponds exactly to that found for “large” activities in reference
Godreche and Luck 2001

Godreche and Luck 2001
. Interestingly, the scaling regime considered there was that of activities of order of the average (
S61
), i.e.
\mathcal{A}\sim t^{T}
, while in the large deviation rate function

one considers
\mathcal{A}\sim t
. What the comparison above shows, then, is that the result for the probability distribution of
\mathcal{A}
in that regime (for large

) agrees with that of our calculation for small
|s|
, i.e. small
\bar{a}=\mathcal{A}/t
. In other words, the two approaches match for
t^{T}\ll\mathcal{A}\ll t
where the relevant scaling regimes meet. Note that our numerical results for the dynamical free energy for large
|s|
, where the asymptotic expression (
S67
) no longer applies, have no analogue in reference
Godreche and Luck 2001
as they relate to larger activity values with

of order one.

We can gain further insight into the behaviour for bias towards high activity by studying the steady state distribution of trap depths. As explained after (
S29
), the steady state probability for a given configuration is
P_{s}(\mathcal{C})=\langle U_{0}|\mathcal{C}\rangle\langle\mathcal{C}|V_{0}\rangle
, and correspondingly in the thermodynamic limit
P_{s}(E)=U_{0}(E)V_{0}(E)
.
We have already determined the right eigenvector
V_{0}(E)
; the left eigenvector
U_{0}(E)
can be found similarly starting from equation (
S49
) or, as discussed below that equation, one can use directly that
U_{0}(E)\propto V_{0}(E)/[\rho(E)e^{\beta E}]
. Either way one finds for the steady state distribution

P_{s}(E)\propto\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-(1-\beta)E}}{(1+\psi(s)\mathrm{e}^{\beta E})^{2}}

(S72)

and some example plots are shown in Fig.
S3
. While negative bias leads to active phases for all
T
as we saw above, the behaviour of
P_{s}(E)
as
s\to 0
shows the signature of the glass transition: for
T>1
,
P_{s}(E)
smoothly approaches the Boltzmann equilibrium distribution of the unbiased system; in the glass regime
T<1
, on the other hand,
P_{s}(E)
develops a maximum that shifts to arbitrarily large trap depths as
s\to 0
. This makes sense physically: without bias, the system ages into deeper traps over time. Any high activity bias stops this aging, but it does so in increasingly deep regions of the landscape as the bias gets weaker.

.4.3
Bias towards low activity

We next look at the effect of biasing towards lower values than typical activity using a bias parameter
s>0
. That the dynamical free energy in this regime tends to zero for
N\to\infty
can be seen easily starting from the analogue of the condition (
S64
) for a finite number
N
of traps, which reads

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}/T}}{\psi(s)+\mathrm{e}^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}/T}}=\mathrm{e}^{s}

(S73)

Let us assume that the trap depths are arranged in increasing order:
0<E_{1}<\ldots<E_{N}
. It is clear that the left hand side of equation (
S73
) as a function of
\psi
has poles at
\{-\mathrm{e}^{-E_{\mathcal{C}}/T}\}
, and therefore the largest eigenvalue
\psi(s)
must lie between
-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{N}}
and 0
4
4

4

In writing equation (
S73
), we have assumed that
N
is still large. A more precise calculation that does not neglect terms of
O(1/N)
shows that the exact eigenvalue equation for a finite number of configurations is
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E_{i}/T}}{\psi(s)+c\mathrm{e}^{-E_{i}/T}}=\mathrm{e}^{s}
with
c=(N-1+\mathrm{e}^{-s})/N
. As can be seen, this correction does not affect the conclusions for large
N
as
c\to 1
.
. Because
E_{N}
diverges for
N\to\infty
, then
\psi(s)\to 0
in the limit. The implication is that
s>0
always produces an inactive dynamical phase, where the time–averaged activity
\langle{\bar{a}}\rangle_{s}=-\psi^{\prime}(s)
also goes to zero. Note that by extension of the above argument, the second largest eigenvalue of
\mathbb{W}(s)
lies between
-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{N-1}}
and
-\mathrm{e}^{-\beta E_{N}}
. These two bounds also vanish for large
N
and so there is no gap spectral gap in the thermodynamic limit. Thus to understand the dynamics in the inactive biased phase, we need to use the general formalism introduced in an earlier section, with the time-dependent weight factors
q(E,\tau)
.

To look at the time-dependent trap depth distribution
P_{s}(E,\tau)
(equation (
S23
)) for a given
s
and total trajectory length
t
we need three ingredients: the forward probability
P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)
, the backward factor
q(E,\tau)=Q(E,t-\tau)
and the normalizing partition function
Z(s,t)
. If one Laplace transforms these with respect to the relevant time variables
\tau
,
t-\tau
and
t
, respectively, explicit expressions can be found. As before we choose an initial state where the system finds itself in a randomly chosen trap, corresponding to
P_{\rm{f}}(E,0)=\rho(E)=\mathrm{e}^{-E}
. The initial condition for the backward factor is, according to the discussion after equation (
S10
),
q(E,t)=Q(E,0)=1
.

The forward (eq. (
S37
)) and backward (eq. (
S45
)) equations in Laplace space are

\displaystyle z\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(E,z)-P_{\rm{f}}(E,0)

\displaystyle=-{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(E,z)+{\rm{e}}^{-s}\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}\,{\rm{e}}^{-E^{\prime}/T}\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(E^{\prime},z)

(S74)

\displaystyle z\hat{Q}(E,z)-Q(E,0)

\displaystyle=-{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}\hat{Q}(E,z)+{\rm{e}}^{-s}{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}\int dE^{\prime}\,\rho(E^{\prime})\hat{Q}(E^{\prime},z)

(S75)

with corresponding solutions

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(E,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-E}}{(z+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T})\tilde{f}(z)}

(S76)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(E,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z+{\rm{e}}^{-E/T}}\left(1+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-s}f(z)}{\tilde{f}(z)}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}}{z}\right)

(S77)

with the abbreviation

\tilde{f}(z)=1-{\rm{e}}^{-s}(1-f(z)).

(S78)

In the unbiased case
s=0
one has
\tilde{f}(z)=f(z)
and the solution for
\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}
agrees with the one for
\hat{P}
in (
S53
) as it must, while similarly
\hat{Q}(E,z)
simplifies to
1/z
, i.e.
Q(E,\Delta t)=1
as expected.

For the case with bias towards low activity of interest here,
s>0
, the key difference is that
\tilde{f}(z)
now has a finite limit
1-\mathrm{e}^{-s}
for
z\to 0
. Using this in (
S76
) and transforming back to the time domain then gives directly for long times

\displaystyle P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-r(E)\tau}\mathrm{e}^{-E}}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-s}}

(S79)

with
r(E)=\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}
as before. For
\hat{Q}
, if we assume that the relevant traps are those with escape rate
r(E)
of order
z
, the second term in brackets in (
S77
) can be neglected for
z\to 0
because
f(z)\to 0
, giving

\displaystyle q(E,\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\mathrm{e}^{-r(E)(t-\tau)}

(S80)

The partition function is found from equation (
S26
), which in the thermodynamic limit reads

\displaystyle Z(s,t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dE\,P_{\rm{f}}(E,t)

(S81)

The Laplace transform w.r.t. total trajectory length
t
of this is, from (
S76
),

\hat{Z}(s,z)=\frac{f(z)}{z\tilde{f}(z)}

(S82)

Using (
S59
) one then sees that for long times

\displaystyle Z(s,t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{T\Gamma(T)}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-s}}t^{-T}

(S83)

(This result applies also for

, where the singular term from (
S59
) for
T<1
is no longer the leading small
z

The complete distribution according to equation (13) is then

\displaystyle P_{s}(E,\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{t^{T}}{T\Gamma(T)}\mathrm{e}^{-E}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}t}

(S84)

Something remarkable happens here: the forward factor
P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)
and the backward factor
q(E,\tau)
both depend on the time
\tau
along the trajectory, but this dependence cancels from the overall trap depth distribution. The physical interpretation is that after a (large enough,
\tau\gg 1
) transient time the trap depth distribution no longer changes for the remainder of the trajectory. Intriguingly, this distribution is
independent
of the specific value of the bias
s
, as long as it is positive: the
s
dependence of
P_{\rm f}(E,\tau)
and
Z(s,t)
cancels from
P_{s}(E,\tau)
.

Further intuition can be obtained by noting that
P_{s}(E,\tau)
in (
S84
) is peaked at values of

around

because it can be written as a function of
\delta E=E-T\ln t
alone. For values of
E
around the peak one has

, meaning that the system will only make a number of jumps of
O(1)
within the entire duration of the trajectory. The implication is that low activity-trajectories are realized by the system rapidly descending into traps that are so deep that it remains essentially stuck for the rest of the time, the dynamics then being “frozen”.

The character of the low activity-dynamics can also be seen from numerical solutions for the time evolution of
P_{s}(E,\tau)
as shown in Fig.
S4
. Even at
\tau=1
this distribution has a contribution proportional to the limiting form (

), because the bias prefers trajectories that already start in deep traps. As
\tau
grows, the weight of the peak at

of order unity is shifted into the large
\tau

We can substantiate the above picture by looking in more detail at the activity along biased trajectories. Considering first the mean long time–averaged activity, one finds from equations (

) and (5) that

\langle\bar{a}\rangle_{s}=-\frac{1}{t}\partial_{s}\ln Z(s,t)=\frac{1}{t}\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{s}-1}

(S85)

Correspondingly the total activity is

\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{s}=t\langle\bar{a}\rangle_{s}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{s}-1}

(S86)

consistent with our expectation that the system will only make a finite number of jumps along the entire trajectory. As an aside we note that this number diverges as
s\to 0
as it must, to connect with the extensive (
\sim t
) number of jumps that we found in the active phase (
s<0
, see for instance Fig.

).

To get more detailed information we next consider the mean time–dependent activity. This can be evaluated using equation (12). Using the approximations (
S80
) and (
S84
), for which we assumed deep traps (with
e^{-E/T}\sim z
) to be dominant, would actually yield a vanishing result. This tells us that we need a more accurate treatment that includes the contribution from the shallow traps. To achieve this, we first rewrite (12) as

\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{s}=\int dE\,\left[r(E)+\frac{\partial_{\Delta t}Q(E,\Delta t)}{Q(E,\Delta t)}\right]\frac{Q(E,\Delta t)P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)}{Z(s,t)}=\int dE\,\frac{S(E,\Delta t)P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)}{Z(s,t)}

(S87)

where

S(E,\Delta t)=r(E)Q(E,\Delta t)+\partial_{\Delta t}Q(E,\Delta t)

(S88)

Now from (
S77
), the Laplace transform of this function is

\hat{S}(E,z)=\left(1+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-s}f(z)}{\tilde{f}(z)}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}}{z}\right)-Q(E,\Delta t=0)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}\hat{\sigma}(z)

(S89)

with

\hat{\sigma}(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z\tilde{f}(z)}

(S90)

Carrying out the integration over
E
in (
S87
) we then obtain for the mean time-dependent activity

\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{s}=\frac{\sigma(\Delta t)\pi(\tau)}{Z(s,t)}

(S91)

where

\hat{\pi}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int dE\,\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(E,z)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\,\frac{1-f(z)}{\tilde{f}(z)}

(S92)

and
\sigma(\Delta t)
and
\pi(\tau)
are the inverse Laplace transforms of
\hat{\sigma}(z)
and
\hat{\pi}(z)
, respectively. For
\sigma(\Delta t)
, we see by comparing (
S82
) and (
S90
) that in fact
\sigma(\Delta t)=Z(s,\Delta t)
and so from (
S83
)
\sigma(\Delta t)/Z(s,t)=(\Delta t/t)^{-T}
, which is unity if we are not near the end of the trajectory, i.e. as long as
\tau\ll t
so that
\Delta t\approx t
. Moving on to
\pi(\tau)
, for long times
f(z)
is small and we can expand

\hat{\pi}(z)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\frac{1-f(z)}{1-\mathrm{e}^{-s}+\mathrm{e}^{-s}f(z)}\approx\frac{1}{\mathrm{e}^{s}-1}-f(z)\,\frac{\mathrm{e}^{s}}{(\mathrm{e}^{s}-1)^{2}}

(S93)

The singular term from (
S59
) then determines the long-time behaviour (for
1\ll\tau\ll t
), giving for the mean time–dependent activity the final result

\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{s}=\pi(\tau)=\frac{T^{2}\Gamma(T)\mathrm{e}^{s}}{(\mathrm{e}^{s}-1)^{2}}\tau^{-T-1}

(S94)

The power law exponent here is
-(T+1)
as stated in the main text. The prediction (
S94
) is tested against numerics in Fig.
S1
, showing good agreement including the prefactor.

.5
Barrat–Mézard trap model

In this section, we consider activity–biased dynamics in the Barrat–Mézard trap model. Because of the more complicated transition rates (eq. (2)) obtaining analytical results is more difficult than for the Bouchaud case. Here, we focus on the regime that is qualitatively different from the Bouchaud trap model, namely temperatures
T<1/2
(cf. the phase diagrams in Fig. 1 in the main text).

We start as in the previous section with the calculation of the mean time–dependent activity in the unbiased case. Then, we set out the arguments that allow us to obtain the phase boundary of equation (7). Finally, we calculate the mean time–dependent activity in the presence of a bias, at zero temperature, and discuss the validity of the resulting scaling across the whole robust aging phase (as shown e.g. in Fig. 2(b) in the main text).

.5.1
Zero bias

The master equation (3) with the appropriate transition rates (eq. (2)) and corresponding escape rate
r(E)
reads

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-r(E)P(E,\tau)+\rho(E)\int dE^{\prime}\frac{P(E^{\prime},\tau)}{1+{\rm{e}}^{-(E-E^{\prime})/T}}

(S95)

with initial condition
P(E,0)=\rho(E)={\rm{e}}^{-E}
. The escape rate is

r(E)=\int dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})=\int dE^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E^{\prime}}}{1+{\rm{e}}^{-(E^{\prime}-E)/T}}=\int dE^{\prime}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-E^{\prime}}\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}}{\mathrm{e}^{-E/T}+{\rm{e}}^{-E^{\prime}/T}}=f(\mathrm{e}^{-E/T})

(S96)

with
f(z)
as defined in (
S54
). In the long time limit, typical trap depths are large and we can focus on the leading behaviour (
S59
) of
f(z)
for small arguments, giving

\displaystyle r(E)\approx\frac{\pi T}{\sin(\pi T)}{\rm{e}}^{-E}

(S97)

This is proportional to the fraction
\mathrm{e}^{-E}
of traps of depth greater than that of the initial trap, which is consistent with the slow dynamics being governed by entropic barriers. The inverse of
r(E)
yields the corresponding entropic timescale
Sollich 2006
and it is then natural,
following Ref.
Bertin 2003
, to introduce the scaling variable
u^{-1}=\tau{\rm{e}}^{-E}
. The behavior for large and small
u
thus corresponds to deep and shallow traps, respectively.
It turns out that in terms of
u
an asymptotic solution of equation (
S95
) can be found (see details in
Bertin 2003
;
Sollich 2006
). When converted back to the physical variables this solution reads

\displaystyle P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle\sim\tau{\rm{e}}^{-E}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{E}/\tau\gg 1

(S98)

\displaystyle P(E,\tau)

\displaystyle\sim\tau^{1-1/T}\mathrm{e}^{-E}{\rm{e}}^{E/T}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{E}/\tau\ll 1

(S99)

We comment briefly on the regime
1/2<T<1
where activation effects dominate
Bertin 2003
;
Cammarota and Marinari 2015
. One then finds, in analogy with the situation in the Bouchaud model, that the mean time–dependent activity is dominated by the shallow traps, i.e. by the part of the distribution corresponding to equation (
S99
). With this we can compute the scaling of the mean time–dependent activity as

\displaystyle\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\int dE\,P(E,\tau)r(E)\sim\tau^{1-1/T}

(S100)

This was the result used in Fig. 2(a).

For the regime
T<1/2
that we mostly focus on in this section one finds, on the other hand, that the mean time–dependent activity is governed by typical traps with
\mathrm{e}^{E}\sim\tau

Bertin 2003
;
Sollich 2006
. This results in
\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{0}\sim r(E)\sim 1/\tau
, with an exponent that no longer depends on temperature
T
.

.5.2
Dynamical phase boundary for
T<1/2

Here we give the arguments to derive equation (7), which describes the phase boundary of the continuous dynamical phase transition at
T<1/2
(cf. phase diagram in Fig. 1(b)). We tackle the problem by approaching the boundary from the active side, i.e.
s<s^{*}
.

Our starting point is the left eigenvector equation (
S49
), with the transition rates (2). We write it as follows

\displaystyle-r(E)U_{0}(E)+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int dE^{\prime}\frac{\rho(E^{\prime})U_{0}(E^{\prime})}{1+{\rm{e}}^{-(E^{\prime}-E)/T}}=\psi(s)U_{0}(E)

(S101)

with
r(E)\approx\frac{\pi T}{\sin(\pi T)}{\rm{e}}^{-E}
(eq. (
S97
)) and
\rho(E)=\mathrm{e}^{-E}
. With the change of variables
x={\rm{e}}^{-E}
equation (
S101
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{0}^{1}dx^{\prime}\frac{U_{0}(x^{\prime})}{1+(x^{\prime}/x)^{\beta}}=U_{0}(x)(\psi(s)+c_{T}x)

(S102)

with
c_{T}=\frac{\pi T}{\sin(\pi T)}
and
\beta=1/T
. The form of the r.h.s. of (
S102
) suggests a solution with the scaling
U_{0}(x)\propto g(x/\psi(s))
with
g
a master curve to be determined; numerical results supporting this scaling form are shown in Fig.
S5
. Inserting the assumed scaling and changing variables once more to
y=x/\psi(s)
we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{0}^{1/\psi(s)}dy^{\prime}\frac{g(y^{\prime})}{1+(y^{\prime}/y)^{\beta}}=g(y)(1+c_{T}y)

(S103)

and at the phase boundary the upper integration boundary can be taken to infinity. Figure
S5
suggests that the resulting
g(y)
exhibits a crossover between two different power laws at
y=1
. The exponent we find numerically for small
y
is consistent with the value
\beta
. Indeed, evaluating the two sides of (
S103
) to leading order for small
y
gives

\displaystyle y^{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{0}^{\infty}dy^{\prime}\frac{g(y^{\prime})}{y^{\prime}{}^{\beta}}=g(y)

(S104)

and hence
g(y)\sim y^{\beta}
. Of course the remaining integral on the l.h.s. has to be finite for this conclusion to hold: we find below that it is.

For large
y
the exponent is nontrivial and we next establish the equations required to determine it. We rewrite eq. (
S103
) for
y\gg 1
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{0}^{\infty}dy^{\prime}\frac{g(y^{\prime})}{1+(y^{\prime}/y)^{\beta}}\approx g(y)c_{T}y

(S105)

and insert the power law scaling
g(y)\sim y^{\alpha}
, with the exponent
\alpha
to be determined, leading to

\displaystyle c_{T}\mathrm{e}^{s}=\int_{0}^{\infty}dy^{\prime}\frac{y^{\prime\alpha}}{1+y^{\prime\beta}}

(S106)

This first condition relates
\alpha
and
s
, but does not yet determine the position
s^{*}
of the phase boundary.

Figure S5
:
Normalized left (top) eigenvector components
U_{0}(E)
as a function of the reduced variable
y=x/\psi(s)=\mathrm{e}^{-E}/\psi(s)
obtained from the numerical solution of equation (

) for different values of
s
close to the phase boundary

. The resulting dynamical free energies are shown in the legend. Temperature:

. Collapse of the curves into a single (master) curve indicates correctness of the assumption
U_{0}(x)\propto g(x/\psi(s))
. Dashed line:
g(y)\sim y^{\beta}
. Dotted line:
g(y)\sim y^{\beta/2-1}
.

For the second equation we use a physical argument, based on an analogy with the unbiased case
Cammarota and Marinari 2015
. There, the dynamics changes at
T=1/2
from being dominated by activation across effective energy barriers to slowing down by entropic barriers. This change is related to the fact that for
T>1/2
, the system is more likely to move towards shallower traps in each jump, and so eventually to the top of the landscape; while for
T<1/2
it moves on average towards deeper traps. At
T=1/2
, the probabilities of moving to shallower and to deeper traps,

P_{\uparrow}(E)=\frac{1}{r(E)}\int_{0}^{E}dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\downarrow}(E)=\frac{1}{r(E)}\int_{E}^{\infty}dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})

(S107)

are exactly equal (for deep traps,
E\gg 1
) as one can check by explicit calculation using
\rho(E^{\prime})=\mathrm{e}^{-E^{\prime}}
.
Based on the picture that at the dynamical phase boundary for
T<1/2
the driving is just strong enough to stop the system from aging, i.e. to allow it to return to shallow traps, we now posit that the analogous criterion holds there with the appropriate weighting by the left eigenvector
U_{0}(E^{\prime})
, so that

P_{\uparrow}(E)\propto\int_{0}^{E}dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})U_{0}(E^{\prime})\quad\mbox{and}\quad P_{\downarrow}(E)\propto\int_{E}^{\infty}dE^{\prime}\,W(E\to E^{\prime})\rho(E^{\prime})U_{0}(E^{\prime})

(S108)

should again be equal.
In terms of the scaling function
g(y)
this equality reads, by analogy with the l.h.s. of (
S103
),

\displaystyle\int_{y}^{\infty}dy^{\prime}\frac{g(y^{\prime})}{1+(y^{\prime}/y)^{\beta}}=\int_{0}^{y}dy^{\prime}\frac{g(y^{\prime})}{1+(y^{\prime}/y)^{\beta}}

(S109)

or in terms of
w=y^{\prime}/y
,

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}dw\,\frac{g(wy)}{1+w^{\beta}}=\int_{0}^{1}dw\,\frac{g(wy)}{1+w^{\beta}}

(S110)

Finally we take the limit
y\to\infty
because at the dynamical phase transition, where
\psi(s)\to 0
, all traps effectively have large
y=\mathrm{e}^{-E}/\psi(s)
. With the assumed power law scaling
g(wy)\sim(wy)^{\alpha}
for large
y
our condition then becomes

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}dw\,\frac{w^{\alpha}}{1+w^{\beta}}=\int_{0}^{1}dw\,\frac{w^{\alpha}}{1+w^{\beta}}

(S111)

giving as the non–trivial solution for the exponent
\alpha

\displaystyle\alpha=\frac{\beta}{2}-1

(S112)

This prediction for the exponent determining the large-
y
behaviour of
g(y)
is fully consistent with our scaling collapse of the numerical eigenproblem solutions in Fig.
S5
. It also ensures that the integrand in (
S104
) scales as
y^{\prime}{}^{-\beta/2-1}
for large
y^{\prime}
and so the integral converges.
Finally, inserting the exponent value (
S112
) into equation (
S106
) and solving for
s
one finds expression (7) in the manuscript for the location of the dynamical phase boundary.

.5.3
Zero temperature

In the unbiased model, we saw above that the decay of the time-dependent activity at
T<1/2
has a
T
-independent power law; in fact the physics of the slow dynamics here is driven by an entropic mechanism
Bertin 2003
;
Sollich 2006
that exists down to zero temperature. We therefore now focus on this limit and solve for the scaling of the mean time–averaged activity at
T=0
, allowing for any value of the bias (including the undriven case
s=0
). We find that the activity continues to scale as
1/\tau
, consistent with our numerical observation that this scaling applies for any
T<1/2
in the robust aging phase.

At zero temperature, the transition rates (eq. (2)) reduce to Heaviside functions (
\Theta(x)=0
for
x<0
and
\Theta(x)=1
for
x\geq 0
) and the escape rate becomes the (complement of the) cumulative distribution of the density of states, namely
r(E)=\int_{0}^{\infty}dE^{\prime}\Theta(E^{\prime}-E)\rho(E^{\prime})=\int_{E}^{\infty}dE^{\prime}\rho(E^{\prime})
. In what follows, we will consider a general distribution
\rho(E)
because for zero temperature and zero bias it is known that the results are in fact independent of
\rho(E)

Barrat and Mézard 1995b
.

We write for this scenario the forward (eq. (
S37
)) and backward (eq. (
S45
)) equations as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)

\displaystyle=-r(E)P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\rho(E)\int_{0}^{E}dE^{\prime}P_{\rm{f}}(E^{\prime},\tau)

(S113)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta t}Q(E,\Delta t)

\displaystyle=-r(E)Q(E,\Delta t)+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{E}^{\infty}dE^{\prime}\rho(E^{\prime})Q(E^{\prime},\Delta t)

(S114)

with
\Delta t=t-\tau
as before. It will be convenient to transform those equations from trap depths
E
to the corresponding escape rates
r(E)
. We take into account that
P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)
transforms as a probability distribution, i.e.
P_{\rm{f}}(E,\tau)=P_{\rm{f}}(r,\tau)\left|\frac{dr}{dE}\right|=P_{\rm{f}}(r,\tau)\rho(E)
; whereas
Q(E,t-\tau)
transforms as a scalar function. Hence the transformed equations are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}P_{\rm{f}}(r,\tau)

\displaystyle=-rP_{\rm{f}}(r,\tau)+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{r}^{1}dr^{\prime}P_{\rm{f}}(r^{\prime},\tau)

(S115)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta t}Q(r,\Delta t)

\displaystyle=-rQ(r,\Delta t)+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\,Q(r^{\prime},\Delta t)

(S116)

Now we Laplace transform the equations, using the initial conditions
P_{\rm{f}}(r,0)=\frac{P_{\rm{f}}(E,0)}{\rho(E)}=1
and
Q(r,0)=1
, to yield

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(r,z){(z+r)}

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{r}^{1}dr^{\prime}\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(r^{\prime},z)

(S117)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(r,z)(z+r)

\displaystyle=1+\mathrm{e}^{-s}\int_{0}^{r}dr^{\prime}\,\hat{Q}(r^{\prime},z)

(S118)

These equations have the explicit solutions

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(r,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(z+1)^{b}}{(z+r)^{b+1}}

(S119)

\displaystyle\hat{Q}(r,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{(z+r)^{b-1}}{z^{b}}

(S120)

with
b={\rm{e}}^{-s}
. The partition function in Laplace space follows as

\displaystyle\hat{Z}(s,z)=\int_{0}^{1}dr\,\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(r,z)=\frac{(z+1)^{b}}{bz^{b}}-\frac{1}{b}

(S121)

We now focus as usual on the long-time limit
z\ll 1
, where we expect the dynamics to concentrate around deep traps
r\ll 1
. In
\hat{P}_{\rm{f}}(r,z)
the singularity with the largest real part is at
z=-r
. The numerator
(z+1)^{b}
is unity there to leading order so we can approximate
P_{\rm{f}}(r,z)\approx(z+r)^{-b-1}
. For
\hat{Q}(z)
the dominant singularity is at
z=0
so that to leading order
\hat{Q}(r,z)=r^{b-1}z^{-b}
. Using the analogous argument for
\hat{Z}(s,z)
and carrying out the inverse Laplace transforms we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\rm{f}}(r,\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-r\tau}\tau^{b}}{\Gamma(1+b)}

(S122)

\displaystyle q(r,\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(t-\tau)^{b-1}r^{b-1}}{\Gamma(b)}

(S123)

\displaystyle Z(s,t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{t^{b-1}}{\Gamma(1+b)}

(S124)

and hence

\displaystyle P_{s}(r,\tau)\approx\frac{{\rm{e}}^{-r\tau}r^{b-1}\tau^{b}(t-\tau)^{b-1}}{\Gamma(b)t^{b-1}}

(S125)

The auxiliary escape rate can now be calculated from eq. (12) and becomes
r+(b-1)/(t-\tau)
. The correction term is negligibly small as long as we are far from the end of the trajectory,
i.e.
\tau\ll t
.
We therefore have

\displaystyle\langle a_{\tau}\rangle_{s}

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{1}dr\,P_{s}(r,\tau)r

(S126)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{(t-\tau)^{b-1}b}{\tau t^{b-1}}

(S127)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-s}}{\tau}

(S128)

assuming again
\tau\ll t
.
In Figure
S6
we test this prediction against numerical solutions of the forward and backward equations, and see that both the asymptotic scaling and its prefactor are captured correctly.
The mean total activity is from (
S124
)

\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{s}=-\partial_{s}\ln Z(s,t)=\mathrm{e}^{-s}\ln t

(S129)

to leading order for large
t
. This is consistent with (
S128
); it also shows that the system does not freeze, i.e. that the mean number of jumps diverges with
t
. This property characterizes the behavior of the whole robust aging phase that we find for
T<1/2
. We remark that the limit
s\to\infty
is somewhat singular as the prefactor in (
S129
) vanishes; indeed this limit produces strictly zero activity at any finite
t
. Nonetheless the activity diverges for
t\to\infty
at any finite
s>0
, including for large
s
when the (non-commuting) limits are taken in the physically relevant order
\lim_{s\to\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle\mathcal{A}\rangle_{s}
.
The fact that the system continues to age can also be seen from the explicit form of the time-dependent distribution across traps (again for
\tau\ll t
)

P_{s}(r,\tau)\propto P_{\rm{f}}(r,\tau)q(r,t-\tau)\propto\mathrm{e}^{-r\tau}(r\tau)^{b-1}

(S130)

This shows simple aging scaling, i.e. a dependence only on
r(E)\tau
, so that the distribution shifts to deeper and deeper traps as
\tau
increases.

In the main text, in Fig. 2(b), we show that the scaling from eq. (
S128
) is consistent also with numerical results for finite temperatures in the robust aging phase. This supports our conjecture that the scaling
\langle a_{\tau}\rangle\sim\tau^{-1}

is independent of temperature throughout this phase, with both temperature and bias only affecting the prefactor as for the case
T=0
.

.6
First order perturbation theory

In this section, we explain how we obtain the mean time–averaged activity as shown in e.g. Fig. 1(d) (see also Fig.
S2
(right)) , without having to take numerical derivatives of the dynamical free energy
\psi(s)
. We work for simplicity with a finite number
N
of traps
\mathcal{C}
and exploit the fact that
\mathbb{W}(s)
can be symmetrized by a similarity transformation involving the diagonal matrix associated with the formal equilibrium (Boltzmann) distribution for
s=0
, i.e.
{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm{eq}}\propto{\rm{diag}}({\rm{e}}^{\beta E_{\mathcal{C}}})
(see also the comments after (
S49
)). This equilibrium solution always exists for finite
N
, though in the glassy regime the time to reach it diverges with
N
.

Explicitly, the similarity transformation reads

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{W}}(s)={\mathbb{P}}_{\rm{eq}}^{-1/2}\mathbb{W}(s){\mathbb{P}}_{\rm{eq}}^{1/2}

(S131)

or in components

\displaystyle\tilde{W}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}(s)={\rm{e}}^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{C}}/2}W_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}(s){\rm{e}}^{\beta E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}/2}\,.

(S132)

Denoting by
\tilde{V}_{0}
the normalised eigenvector corresponding to the top eigenvalue of
\tilde{\mathbb{W}}(s)
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(s)=\langle\tilde{V}_{0}|\tilde{\mathbb{W}}(s)|\tilde{V}_{0}\rangle

(S133)

One can now deploy standard first–order perturbation theory (as used for instance in Ref.
De Bacco et al. 2016
; the key feature is that the
s
-dependence of
\tilde{V}_{0}
does not contribute) to express the first derivative of the free energy as

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{V}_{0}|\tilde{\mathbb{W}}^{\prime}(s)|\tilde{V}_{0}\rangle

(S134)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\tilde{V}_{0}(\mathcal{C})\tilde{W}^{\prime}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}(s)\tilde{V}_{0}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})

(S135)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}\tilde{V}_{0}(\mathcal{C}){\rm{e}}^{-\beta E_{\mathcal{C}}/2}W^{\prime}_{\mathcal{C}\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}(s){\rm{e}}^{\beta E_{\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}/2}\tilde{V}_{0}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})

(S136)

Now we use that the eigenvectors of the original biased master operator can be written in terms of the symmetrized eigenvector as
\langle U_{0}|=\langle\tilde{V}_{0}|{\mathbb{P}}_{\rm{eq}}^{-1/2}
and
|V_{0}\rangle={\mathbb{P}}_{\rm{eq}}^{1/2}|\tilde{V}_{0}\rangle
(see discussion after (
S49
) and Ref.
Margiotta et al. 2018
for an illustration in the case of the unbiased Bouchad trap model). Additionally, we use the expression of the master operator as given by equation (
.1
). This results in

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime}(s)=\sum_{\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C^{\prime}},\mathcal{C}\neq\mathcal{C^{\prime}}}U_{0}(\mathcal{C})W(\mathcal{C^{\prime}}\to\mathcal{C}){\rm{e}}^{-s}V_{0}(\mathcal{C^{\prime}})

(S137)

and in the thermodynamic limit

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\int dE\,dE^{\prime}\,U_{0}(E)V_{0}(E^{\prime})W(E^{\prime}\to E){\rm{e}}^{-s}\rho(E)

(S138)

One can easily verify that the r.h.s. of equation (
S138
) corresponds to the mean time–averaged activity by using the equation for the top right eigenvector (eq. (
S47
)) to replace the integral over
E^{\prime}
. Then equation (
S138
) transforms to

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\int dE\,U_{0}(E)V_{0}(E)(\psi(s)+r(E))

(S139)

and one can recognize this as equation (14) of the manuscript.

For Barrat–Mézard transition rates, eq. (
S138
) becomes

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime}(s)=\int dE\,dE^{\prime}\,\frac{U_{0}(E)V_{0}(E^{\prime})\rho(E){\rm{e}}^{-s}}{1+{\rm{e}}^{-\beta(E-E^{\prime})}}=\langle\bar{a}\rangle_{s}

(S140)

and this expression was used to generate the data for Fig. 1(d) in the main text.

.7
Numerical details

Here we provide a brief summary of how we generated the main figures of the manuscript. The dynamical free energy in Fig. 1(c) for the active regime is obtained as the top eigenvalue of a biased master operator
\mathbb{W}(s)
of size
N=2^{13}
. An instance of the master operator with elements given by eq. (
.1
) can be generated after randomly sampling
N
energies from to
\rho(E)
and associate to each configuration one of these energies. To use first–order perturbation theory for the determination of the mean time–averaged activity we obtain the top right eigenvector using the Arnoldi iteration (as implemented in the Arpack package
et al. 2018
) and then exploit detailed balance to obtain the top left–eigenvector (see discussion after eq. (
