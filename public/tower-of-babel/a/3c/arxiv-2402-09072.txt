Trace ratio based manifold learning with tensor data
M. Bouallala, F. Dufrenois, K. Jbilou, A. Ratnani

Abstract
In this paper, we propose an extension of trace ratio based Manifold learning methods to deal with multidimensional data sets. Based on recent progress on the tensor-tensor product, we present a generalization of the trace ratio criterion by using the properties of the t-product. This will conduct us to introduce some new concepts such as Laplacian tensor and we will study formally the trace ratio problem by discuting the conditions for the exitence of solutions and optimality. Next, we will present a tensor Newton QR decomposition algorithm for solving the trace ratio problem. Manifold learning methods such as Laplacian eigenmaps, linear discriminant analysis and locally linear embedding will be formulated in a tensor representation and optimized by the proposed algorithm. Lastly, we will evaluate the performance of the different studied dimension reduction methods on several synthetic and real world data sets.



keywords

Dimensionality Reduction, Multilinear Algebra, Trace-Ratio, Tensor Methods, t-product.

Introduction

In the big data era, machine learning methods (ML) are faced with an increasing volume of data which can both mix different modalities and contain several thousands, or millions of features. This unstoppable curse of dimensionality is the « Achilles heel » of most ML methods which involves an increase of the complexity of the model and a loss of generalization capacity. Dimensionality reduction (DR) or more generally manifold learning is a tailored response to minimize this problem and open up the access to modern real world applications such as multiview classification
[
10
]
, object detection
[
14
,
15

[

,

), Linear discriminant analysis (LDA,
[

,

), singular value decomposition (SVD,
[

[
24
]
), ISOMAP
[
3
,
14
]
, Locally Linear Embedding (LLE,
[
14
,
16
,
30

), Laplacian Eigenmaps (LE,
[

,
14
]
),… to name but a few (for a review see
[

A wide majority of these dimensionality reduction methods are formulated under the form of a ratio trace problem whose the optimization amounts to solve a generalized eigenproblem. Although all these methods have been developed in a matricial form (second-order tensor), they are unsuited and loss their efficiency for large multidimensional data sets. Representing data and formulating an optimization problem with tensors of order greater than 2 become a new challenging task for modern ML methods.

Recently, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) have been generalized to deal with multidimensional data sets. Firstly, by using the n-mode product of tensors, numerous optimizations procedures have been proposed to solve PCA and LDA in this context
[
26
,
27
,
31
]
. However, these approaches are not « fully » in a tensor form since the underlying optimization process amounts to find projectives matrices instead of projectives tensors. Recently, based on recent developments on tensor-tensor products
[
23
]
, this question have been solved. For instance, Sparse Regularization Tensor Robust Principal Component Analysis
[
25
]
, and Multilinear Discriminant Analysis (MLDA,
[
19
]
) propose to solve the DR problem by using the properties of the t-product. The particularity of this t-product is to realize the optimization of the trace ratio problem associated to PCA and LDA in a so called « transform domain » where the tensor-tensor product can be defined. The transformation being invertible, a projective tensor, solution of the problem, is next recovered.

Based on these recent developments, we propose here to generalize several Manifold learning methods formulated as a trace ratio criterion. In particular, our study will focus on the generalization of the following approaches: Local Discriminant Embedding (LDE)
[
12
,

]

[

[

[
23
]
. The particularity of these methods is to be based on three key steps: a) building a neighborhood graph,b) computing a weighting vector and c) computing the embedding. Below, let us recall briefly the principle of these methods.

- LE is an unsupervised manifold learning method which relies on the construction of a graph from neighborhood information of the data set and the minimization of given cost function based on this graph. This method ensures that points close to each other on the manifold are mapped close to each other in the low-dimensional space, thus preserving local distances.

- LLE is also an unsupervised dimensionality reduction method which tries as LE to preserve the local structure of data in the embedding space. The principle of LLE is fitting the local structure of manifold in the embedding space. The local structure of the data is obtained by building a k-NN graph.

- LDE is a supervised manifold learning algorithm which makes use of both the neighborhood relationships between data points and the class label information to obtain a lower-dimensional embedding. Unlike LDA and related methods, the discrimination ability of LDE does not strongly depend on the data distribution, such as the Gaussian assumption. Moreover, unlike many manifold learning methods such as Isomap
[
3
]
and locally linear embedding (LLE)
[
16
]
, LDE uses label information to find the embedding and can naturally handle new test data in classification applications.

Our study will based on several contributions:
First, the generalization of theses methods to tensor data will conduct us to define some key concepts and properties associated to the t-product such as trace of tensor, Laplacian tensor, positive definite and semi-definite tensor,…Moreover, we will present a theoretical examination of the trace-ratio tensor problem, discussing both the existence of solutions and optimality conditions. We will develop the Tensor Newton-QR algorithm as a new approach for solving the trace ratio tensor problem. Lastly, we will formulate LE, LLE and LDE as a new trace ratio criterion based on tensor representation.

Multilinear algebra concepts

A tensor is a mathematical object that represents a wide range of data, including scalar values, vectors, matrices, and higher-dimensional arrays. A first-order tensor can be seen as a vector and a second-order tensor as a matrix. Let
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
be a third-order tensor, its
(i,j,k)
-th element is denoted by
\mathscr{A}_{ijk}
. We can extract a fiber of
\mathscr{A}
by fixing two indices, say
j
and
k

\bm{a}_{.jk}
,
\bm{a}_{i.k}
, and
\bm{a}_{ij.}
, respectively. Similarly, we can define a slice of
\mathscr{A}
by fixing one index out of three. For a third-order tensor there are three modes of slices: horizontal (mode 1), lateral (mode 2), and frontal (mode 3) slides, represented by
\mathscr{A}_{i::}
,
\mathscr{A}_{:j:}
, and
\mathscr{A}_{::k}
, respectively.
Further,
\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
denotes the space of reel third-order tensors of size
n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3},\ \mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times 1\times n_{3}}
stands for the space of lateral slices of size
n_{1}\times 1\times n_{3}
, and
\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
denotes the space of tubes with
n_{3}
entries. For more details see the work done by Kolda et al.
[
4
]
.

Consider a tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

\mathscr{A}
can be expressed as follows

\|\mathscr{A}\|_{F}=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\mathscr{A}_{ijk}^{2}},

\langle\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\rangle=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}\mathscr{A}_{i,j,k}\mathscr{B}_{i,j,k}.

2.1
The t-product

The t-product is a tensor-tensor product that has been introduced by Kilmer and her collaborators in
[
23
]
. This product was only restricted to third-order tensors.

To introduce the t-product we need firstly to define some specific block matrices.

•

The block circulant matrix associated with
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

\text{\tt bcirc}(\mathscr{A})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{A}^{(1)}&\mathscr{A}^{(n_{3})}&\ldots&\mathscr{A}^{(2)}\\
\mathscr{A}^{(2)}&\mathscr{A}^{(1)}&\ldots&\mathscr{A}^{(3)}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathscr{A}^{(n_{3})}&\mathscr{A}^{(n_{3}-1)}&\ldots&\mathscr{A}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}n_{3}}.

(1)

•

The operator unfold applied to
\mathscr{A}
gives the matrix made up of its frontal slices,

\text{\tt unfold}(\mathscr{A})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{A}^{(1)}\\
\mathscr{A}^{(2)}\\
\vdots\\
\mathscr{A}^{(n_{3})}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}}.

We also will need the inverse operator
fold
such that
fold
(
unfold

(\mathscr{A}))=\mathscr{A}
.

•

The block diagonal matrix associated with
\mathscr{A}

{\tt bdiag}(\mathscr{A})=\begin{pmatrix}\mathscr{A}^{(1)}&&&\\
&\mathscr{A}^{(2)}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\mathscr{A}^{(n_{3})}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}n_{3}\times n_{2}n_{3}}.

The t-product is given in the following definition.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times q\times n_{3}}
and
\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
be two third-order tensors. The t-product between
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
is defined by

\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}:={\tt fold}\left({\tt bcirc}\left(\mathscr{A}\right){\tt unfold}\left(\mathscr{B}\right)\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}.

According to
[
23
]
, the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) can block-diagonalize the block circulant matrix (
1

{\tt bcirc}\left(\mathscr{A}\right)=\left(F_{n_{3}}^{H}\otimes I_{n_{1}}\right)\text{\tt bdiag}(\widehat{\mathscr{A}})\left(F_{n_{3}}\otimes I_{n_{2}}\right),

where
F_{n_{3}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}
is the discrete Fourier matrix,
F_{n}^{H}
denotes its hermitian transpose,
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}
stands for the Fourier transform of
\mathscr{A}
along each tube,
I_{n_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{1}}
denotes the identity matrix. The tensor
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}

Using MATLAB notations, we have

\widehat{\mathscr{A}}={\tt fft}\left(\mathscr{A},[\,],3\right),

The command for the inverse operation is

\mathscr{A}={\tt ifft}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{A}},[\,],3\right).

Hence, according to

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{(i)}=\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}\widehat{\mathscr{B}}^{(i)},\quad i=1,2,\ldots,n_{3},

(2)

where
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)},\widehat{\mathscr{B}}^{(i)}

\widehat{\mathscr{C}}^{(i)}

\widehat{\mathscr{A}},\widehat{\mathscr{B}}

As mentioned earlier by Kilmer et al
[

Lemma 6

(

Given a real vector

\widehat{v}=F_{n}v

\widehat{v}_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\quad{\tt conj}\left(\widehat{v}_{i}\right)=\widehat{v}_{n-i+2},\quad i=2,3,\ldots,\left[\frac{n+1}{2}\right],

In this context,

\frac{n_{3}+1}{2}

It follows that for a third-order tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}},\quad\text{\tt conj}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}\right)=\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(n_{3}-i+2)},\quad i=2,3,\ldots,\left[\frac{n_{3}+1}{2}\right].

Definition 8

(Identity tensor)
.

The Identity tensor
\mathscr{I}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is the tensor whose first frontal slice is the
n\times n
identity matrix, and whose other frontal slices are all zeros.

Definition 9

(Tensor transpose)
.

The transpose of a real third-order tensor,
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, denoted by
\mathscr{A}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times n_{1}\times n_{3}}
, is the tensor obtained by first transposing each one of the frontal slices of
\mathscr{A}
, and then reversing the order of the transposed frontal slices 2 through
n_{3}
. Moreover, for a square tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
,
\mathscr{A}
is considered f-symmetric if
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}^{\top}
.

Let the third-order tensors
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
be such that the products
\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{B}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}^{T}
are defined. Then, similarly to the matrix transpose, the tensor transpose satisfies
(\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B})^{T}=

\mathscr{B}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}^{T}
.

A tensor
\mathscr{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is said to be f-orthogonal (or f-unitary) if and only if

\mathscr{Q}^{T}\star\mathscr{Q}=\mathscr{Q}\star\mathscr{Q}^{T}=\mathscr{I}_{n}.

Definition 10

(Positive Definite (Semi-Definite) Tensor)
.

Consider the tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
, the tensor
\mathscr{A}
is positive definite (semi-definite) if and only if each frontal slice
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}
is positive definite (semi-definite).

Definition 11

(Laplacian tensor)
.

Consider the tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
. The tensor
\mathscr{A}
is called a Laplacian tensor if each of frontal slices
\widehat{\mathscr{A}}^{(i)}
is a Laplacian matrix.

Note that the Laplacian tensor is f-symmetric semi-definite.

Remark 1
.

A f-symmetric positive definite tensor is invertible.

The proof can be seen easily from the fact that each frontal slice in the Fourier space of a tensor is positive definite.

Definition 12

(Inverse of tensor)
.

We say that the tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}

\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
such that the following conditions hold

\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{I}_{n},

where
\mathscr{B}
is the inverse of the tensor
\mathscr{A}
, denoted by
\mathscr{A}^{-1}
, and
\mathscr{I}_{n}
is the Identity tensor of size

Proposition 14

Consider a tensor
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

Proof.

3
Trace-Ratio Tensor problem

The trace ratio tensor problem is an important concept in machine learning for tasks such as feature extraction and dimensionality reduction, as it aids in the analysis of complicated, multidimensional data and can be expressed as follows

\max_{\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d\times n_{3}}}\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]}{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]},\text{ subject to}\ \ \mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d}.

(4)

In this formulation,
\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times n_{3}}
is required to have f-orthonormal lateral slices, i.e.,
\mathscr{V}(:,i,:)^{T}\star\mathscr{V}(:,i,:)=\bm{e}
, with
\bm{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
has zero components and
\bm{e}(1,1,1)=1
, and
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is an f-symmetric tensor,
\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
is assumed to be f-symmetric and positive definite tensor.
This problem can be replaced by a simpler, yet not equivalent problem

\max_{\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times n_{3}}}\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right],\text{ subject to }\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d}.

(5)

In practice, Problem (
4
) often arises as a simplification of an objective function that is more difficult to maximize, which can be described as follows

\max_{\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times n_{3}}}\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]}{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]},\text{ subject to}\ \ \mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{C}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d},

(6)

where
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{C}
are assumed to be f-symmetric and positive definite for simplicity. The tensor
\mathscr{C}
defines the desired f-orthogonality and in the simplest case, it is just the Identity tensor.

3.1
Existence and uniqueness of a solution

Let us consider the following theorem, which is important in our analysis.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
and
\mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}}
, where
\mathscr{A}
is f-symmetric and
\mathscr{B}
is an f-symmetric positive definite tensor. Consider the optimization problem as follows

\underset{{\mathscr{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times n_{3}}}{\max}\operatorname{Trace}\left({\mathscr{U}}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star{\mathscr{U}}\right),\text{ subject to }{\mathscr{U}}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star{\mathscr{U}}={\mathscr{I}_{d}}.

(7)

The problem (
7
) achieves a maximum, and the solution of (
7
) is the
d
eigenslices associated to the
d
largest eigentube of the following generalized eigentube problem

{\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{\mathscr{U}}=\mathscr{B}}\star\mathscr{U}\star{\Lambda},

(8)

where
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times n_{3}}
is a f-diagonal tensor.

In the case of minimization, the solution of (
7
) is the
d
eigenslices associated with the
d
smallest non-zero eigentube of the generalized eigentube problem (
8
).

Proof.

We have the set of tensors
\mathscr{U}
such that
\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{U}=\mathscr{I}_{d}
is closed, under the assumptions, the function in the right-hand side of (
7
) is a continuous function of its argument, therefore, the maximum of the problem (
7
) is reached. Then the Lagrange multipliers of (
7
) is given by

\phi(\mathscr{U},\mathscr{L})=\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}\right)-\langle\mathscr{L},\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{U}-\mathscr{I}_{d}\rangle,

where
\mathscr{L}
represents the Lagrangian tensor.

To compute the derivative of
\phi
respect to
\mathscr{U}
we need first to compute the derivative of
h_{\mathscr{A}}(\mathscr{U})=\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}\right)
. We have

\langle\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}\rangle=\text{Trace}\left(\mathscr{A}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\right).

Then
h_{\mathscr{A}}
can be write as
h_{\mathscr{A}}(\mathscr{U})=\langle\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U},\mathscr{U}\rangle

Therefore, the directional derivative of
h_{\mathscr{A}}
with respect to
\mathscr{U}
in the direction
\mathscr{H}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle D_{\mathscr{U}}h_{\mathscr{A}}\left(\mathscr{H}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{h_{\mathscr{A}}(\mathscr{U}+t\mathscr{H})-h_{\mathscr{A}}(\mathscr{U})}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{\langle\mathscr{A}\star(\mathscr{U}+t\mathscr{H}),\mathscr{U}+t\mathscr{H}\rangle-\langle\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U},\mathscr{U}\rangle}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{t\langle\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U},\mathscr{H}\rangle+t\langle\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{H},\mathscr{U}\rangle+t^{2}\langle\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{H},\mathscr{H}\rangle}{t}

\displaystyle=\langle 2\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U},\mathscr{H}\rangle.

Therefor the derivative of
h_{\mathscr{A}}
with respect to
\mathscr{U}
is equal to
2\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}
. To find the optimum, we set the derivatives of the Lagrange function equal to zero, so we obtain

\frac{\partial\phi\left(\mathscr{U},\mathscr{L}\right)}{\partial\mathscr{U}}=2\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}-2\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{U}\star\mathscr{L}=0\\
\iff\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}=\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{U}\star\mathscr{L}.

(9)

We know that the optimum verify
{\mathscr{U}}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star{\mathscr{U}}={\mathscr{I}_{d}}
therefor
\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}=\mathscr{L}
and since
\mathscr{A}
is f-symmetric, then
\mathscr{L}
is f-symmetric. Let’s consider the f-diagonalization of
\mathscr{L}
, i.e.,
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{P}\star\Lambda\star\mathscr{P}^{T}
, with
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d\times n_{3}}
is an f-diagonal tensor. Thus, Equation (
9
) becomes

\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}\star\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{U}\star\mathscr{P}\star\Lambda.

(10)

Consequently, to solve the problem (
4
), we must go through the above tensor generalized eigenproblem.

The generalized eigenvalue problem (
10
) admits
d
real eigentube problem, because its eigenvalue are those of
\Lambda
, and
\Lambda
is f-symmetric (f-diagonal tensor), so by referring to
[
21
]
, it admits
n
real eigentubes. Then the generalized eigenproblem admits
d
real eigentubes.

If these eigentubes are labeled decreasingly as has been shown before, and if
\mathscr{V}=\left[\mathscr{V}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{V}_{d}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times n_{3}}
is the set of eigenslices associated with the first
d
eigentube with
\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d}
, then we have

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathscr{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d\times n_{3}}\\
\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{U}=\mathscr{I}_{d}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{U}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{U}\right]=\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]=\frac{1}{n_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{3}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\widehat{\lambda}^{(i)}_{j},

where
\lambda_{i}={\tt ifft}\left(\widehat{\lambda}_{i},[\,],3\right)
of size
{1\times 1\times n_{3}}
is the first
j
-th eigentube of the generalised eigentube problem (
10
).
In the case of minimization,
\mathscr{V}
is the
d
eigenslices associated with the
d

∎

It is helpful to examine the

\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]
in detail. Let
\mathscr{B}=\mathscr{Q}\star\Lambda_{\mathscr{B}}\star\mathscr{Q}^{T}
the f-diagonalization of
\mathscr{B}

\mathscr{Q}
is f-orthogonal and
\Lambda_{\mathscr{B}}

\mathscr{B}

\mathscr{V}

(11)

Lemma 20

Assume that

Using the previous notation

Let
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}

The set of tensors

Necessary conditions for optimality

In this section, we search for the necessary conditions for optimality for the optimization problem (
4

20
and Proposition
14
on
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
.
Therefore, the problem (
4
) admits a maximum, and the corresponding Lagrangian function can write as,

L(\mathscr{V},\Gamma)=\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]}{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]}-\langle\Gamma,\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{V}-\mathscr{I}_{d}\rangle,

(12)

where
\Gamma
represents the Lagrangian tensor.
Based on the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) optimality conditions, as (
4
) has a global maximum
\mathscr{V}_{*}
, there exists a Lagrangian multiplier tensor
\Gamma_{*}
, satisfying the following conditions

\frac{\partial L\left(\mathscr{V}_{*},\Gamma_{*}\right)}{\partial\mathscr{V}}=0\quad\text{ with }\quad\mathscr{V}_{*}^{T}\star\mathscr{V}_{*}=\mathscr{I}_{d}.

We have the derivative of
L
with respect to
\mathscr{V}

\frac{\partial L(\mathscr{V},\Gamma)}{\partial\mathscr{V}}=\frac{2\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}-2\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}}{\left(\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]\right)^{2}}-\mathscr{V}\star\left(\Gamma^{T}+\Gamma\right).

Hence, the optimal solutions
\mathscr{V}_{*}
and
\Gamma_{*}
verifies

\left(\mathscr{A}-\rho_{*}\mathscr{B}\right)\star\mathscr{V}_{*}=\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V_{*}}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V_{*}}\right]}{2}\mathscr{V}_{*}\star\left(\Gamma_{*}^{T}+\Gamma_{*}\right),

(13)

where
\rho_{*}=\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V_{*}}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V_{*}}\right]/\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V_{*}}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V_{*}}\right].

Since the tensor
\left(\Gamma_{*}^{T}+\Gamma_{*}\right)
is f-symmetric, it is f-diagonalizable. Let
\mathscr{Q}
be the tensor which f- diagonalizes

\Gamma_{*}^{T}+\Gamma_{*}=\mathscr{Q}\star\Sigma_{*}\star\mathscr{Q}^{T},\quad\mathscr{Q}^{T}\star\mathscr{Q}=\mathscr{I}_{d}.

Define
\mathscr{U}_{*}=\mathscr{V}_{*}\star\mathscr{Q}
. We have

and we can rewrite (
13

\left(\mathscr{A}-\rho_{*}\mathscr{B}\right)\star\mathscr{U}_{*}=\mathscr{U}_{*}\star\Lambda_{*},\quad\text{ where }\quad\Lambda_{*}=\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V_{*}}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V_{*}}\right]}{2}\Sigma_{*}.

(14)

Equation (

) is the necessary condition of the pair
\rho_{*},\mathscr{U}_{*}
for the problem (

3.3
Newton-QR algorithm

We begin with the understanding that a maximum value, denoted by
\rho_{*}
, is attained for a specific (although not unique) f-orthogonal tensor, represented as
\mathscr{V}_{*}
. Consequently, for any f-orthogonal
\mathscr{V}
, the following inequality holds

\dfrac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]}{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]}\leq\rho_{*}.

(15)

This leads to the expression

\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}\right]-\rho_{*}\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}\right]\leq 0.

Then we have

\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V_{*}}^{T}\star(\mathscr{A}-\rho_{*}\mathscr{B})\star\mathscr{V_{*}}\right]=0.

Therefore, we have the following necessary condition for
\rho_{*},\ \mathscr{V}_{*}
to be optimal

\max_{\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d}}\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\left(\mathscr{A}-\rho_{*}\mathscr{B}\right)\star\mathscr{V}\right]=\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}_{*}^{T}\star\left(\mathscr{A}-\rho_{*}\mathscr{B}\right)\star\mathscr{V}_{*}\right]=0.

(16)

According to Theorem
19
, we can determine the solution of the problem

\underset{{\mathscr{V}}}{\arg\max}\operatorname{Trace}\left[{\mathscr{V}}^{T}\star(\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B})\star{\mathscr{V}}\right]\quad\text{ subject to }{\mathscr{V}}^{T}\star{\mathscr{V}}={\mathscr{I}_{d}}.

(17)

Consider the function

f(\rho)=\max_{\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d}}\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}^{T}\star(\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B})\star\mathscr{V}\right].

The tensor
\mathscr{V}
that reaches the maximum of (
17
) is not unique because changing the lateral slices of
\mathscr{V}
with an f-orthogonal transformation does not affect the trace. To select the optimal
\mathscr{V}
, we use Theorem
19
, the optimal is the set of eigenslices of the tensor
\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}
. We will denote the set of eigenslices that attain the specified maximum as
\mathscr{V}(\rho)
.

Therefore,
f
can be written as

f(\rho)=\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V(\rho)}^{T}\star\left(\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}\right)\star\mathscr{V(\rho)}\right],\text{\ with\ }\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)=\mathscr{I}_{d}.

Also from Theorem
19
,
\mathscr{V}(\rho)
diagonalizes
\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}
and verifies

(\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B})\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)=\mathscr{V}(\rho)\star\mathscr{D}(\rho),

where
\mathscr{D}(\rho)
is a f-diagonal tensor of size
d\times d\times n_{3}
.

From the equality
\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)=\mathscr{I}_{d}
, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\rho}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)+\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}=0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)=-\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\operatorname{Diag}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}\right]=\mathbf{0}.

Because the tensor
\dfrac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)
is anti-symmetry.

Remark 2
.

A tensor
\mathscr{A}
is anti-symmetric if
\mathscr{A}=-\mathscr{A}^{T}
, and
\operatorname{Diag}(\mathscr{A})=\mathbf{0}
if the f-diagonal of each frontal slice
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
is equal to
0
.

Our objective is to calculate the derivative of the function
f(\rho)
. Determining the derivative of
f(\rho)
is essential for deriving this particular expression

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\rho}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star(\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B})\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right]=\frac{d}{d\rho}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right]-\frac{d}{d\rho}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\rho\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\mathscr{A}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)+\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{A}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}-\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\rho\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)

\displaystyle-\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\left[\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)+\rho\mathscr{B}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star[\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}]\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)+\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star[\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}]\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}-\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\star\mathscr{D}(\rho)+\mathscr{D}(\rho)\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}-\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho).

Finally, we can express the final form of the derivative of
f(\rho)
.

\displaystyle f^{{}^{\prime}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Trace}\left[\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}}{d\rho}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\star\mathscr{D}(\rho)+\mathscr{D}(\rho)\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}-\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Trace}\left[\{\mathscr{D}(\rho)\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\frac{d\mathscr{V}(\rho)}{d\rho}\right]-\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right]

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V}(\rho)^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V}(\rho)\right].

We aim to find a solution to the equation where
f(\rho)=0
, and then identify
\mathscr{V}(\rho)
, to do this, we introduce Newton-QR algorithm.
Using the expression of the differential of
f
, the form of Newton’s method is as follows

\displaystyle\rho_{new}

\displaystyle=\rho-\frac{f(\rho)}{f^{{}^{\prime}}(\rho)}

\displaystyle=\rho+\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V(\rho)}^{T}\star\left(\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}\right)\star\mathscr{V(\rho)}\right]}{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V(\rho)}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V(\rho)}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V(\rho)}^{T}\star\left(\mathscr{A}\right)\star\mathscr{V(\rho)}\right]}{\operatorname{Trace}\left[\mathscr{V(\rho)}^{T}\star\mathscr{B}\star\mathscr{V(\rho)}\right]}.

In the tensor Newton-QR algorithm, we use the tensor-QR algorithm (t-QR Algorithm) for tensors to compute the eigenslices associated with the dominant eigentubes of the tensor
\mathscr{A}-\rho\mathscr{B}
. For more details about the t-QR Algorithm see
[
21
]
.

Algorithm 2

Tensor Newton–QR algorithm

Input:

\mathscr{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}},\ \mathscr{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times n_{3}},

d

Graphs and Multidimensional data

In this part, we introduce the concepts of graphs and their relation with high-order data. Additionally, we demonstrate how to compute the affinity tensor

]

\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{i}

4.2
Multilinear Local Discriminant Embedding

This method (MLDE) is a supervised dimensionality reduction algorithm, which requires as inputs a data tensor
\mathscr{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, where
n_{2}
represents the number of data points, and each sample represented by a third-order tensor
\left\{\mathscr{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times 1\times n_{3}}\right\}
, the desired number of dimensions
d<n_{1}
, integers
k_{1}
,
k_{2}
for finding local neighborhoods, and the output is a tensor
\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d\times n_{3}}
, then the reduced data is obtained by
\mathscr{Y}=\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
. This method can be divided into three main steps; construct the neighborhood graphs, compute the affinity weights, and complete the embedding. The key idea behind the third step of the MLDE algorithm is to minimize the distance between the neighboring points of the same class and at the same time maximize the distance between the neighboring points of different classes of each frontal slice in Fourier domain. By considering these two aspects, we get the following optimization problem, for
r=1,\ldots,n_{3}

\underset{\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{1}\times d\times 1}}{\arg\max}\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}^{\prime}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{i}-\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{j}\right\|_{F}^{2}}{\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{i}-\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{j}\right\|_{F}^{2}},\text{ subject to }\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)}=\widehat{\mathscr{I}}^{(r)}_{d},

(18)

where
\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijl}
and
\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijl}^{\prime}
are the elements of the affinity tensors. We compute these affinity tensors using the notions described in Section
4.1
.

To gain more insight into (
18
), we write the square of the norm in the form of a trace

\displaystyle f_{r}(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{2}}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{j}^{(r)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{2}}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\left(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{j}^{(r)}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{j}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}\right)\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Trace}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{2}}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{j}^{(r)}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{X}}_{j}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}^{\prime}\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Trace}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\left(2\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{D}}^{(r)\prime}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)\top}-2\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}^{(r)\prime}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)\top}\right)\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)}\right)

\displaystyle=2\operatorname{Trace}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{D}}^{(r)\prime}-\widehat{\mathscr{W}}^{(r)\prime}\right)\widehat{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)}\right),

where
\widehat{\mathscr{X}}=\texttt{fft}(\mathscr{X},[\,],3)
, with
\mathscr{X}
is the data tensor, and
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
is a f-diagonal tensor with
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}_{iil}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}^{\prime}_{ijl}
.
We set

F({\mathscr{V}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{3}}f_{r}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{V}}^{(r)}\right),

then, by using tensor notation and the definition of trace, we can write
F
as

F(\mathscr{V})=2n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{X}\star\left(\mathscr{D}^{\prime}-\mathscr{W}^{\prime}\right)\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}\star\mathscr{V}\right).

For the denominator of the objective function in (
18
), we use the same analogy as before

H(\mathscr{V})=2n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{X}\star\left(\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}\right)\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}\star\mathscr{V}\right).

Then, the problem (
18
) is equivalent to the following trace ratio tensor problem

\begin{gathered}\underset{\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times d\times n_{3}}}{\arg\max}\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{V}^{T}\star\mathscr{X}\star\left(\mathscr{D}^{\prime}-\mathscr{W}^{\prime}\right)\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}\star\mathscr{V}\right)}{\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{X}\star\left(\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}\right)\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}\star\mathscr{V}\right)},\text{ subject to }\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d}.\end{gathered}

(19)

This optimization problem can be simplified as

\begin{gathered}\underset{\mathscr{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times d\times n_{3}}}{\arg\max}\frac{\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{L^{\prime}}\star\mathscr{V}\right)}{\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{L}\star\mathscr{V}\right)},\text{ subject to }\mathscr{V}^{\top}\star\mathscr{V}=\mathscr{I}_{d},\\
\end{gathered}

(20)

Consider the expressions
\mathscr{L}^{{}^{\prime}}=\mathscr{X}\star(\mathscr{D}^{\prime}-\mathscr{W}^{\prime})\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}
and
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{X}\star(\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W})\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}
. Here, both
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}-\mathscr{W}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}
represent Laplacian tensors.

We have the tensor
\mathscr{L}^{{}^{\prime}}
f-symmetric, because
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}-\mathscr{W}^{\prime}
is f-symmetric then
\mathscr{X}\star(\mathscr{D}^{\prime}-\mathscr{W}^{\prime})\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}
is f-symmetric, and the tensor
\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}
is the Laplacian tensor then is f-symmetric positive semi-definite then the tensor
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{X}\star\left(\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}\right)\star\mathscr{X}^{\top}
is a f-symmetric
positive semi-define tensor. We examine this type of problem in Section

4.3
Multilinear Laplacian Eigenmaps

This method (MLE) is an unsupervised non-linear dimensionality reduction algorithm, requires as inputs, data tensor
\mathscr{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, with each sample represented by a third-order tensor
\left\{\mathscr{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{2}\times n_{3}},i=1,\ldots,n_{1}\right\}
, a dimension
d<n_{2}
and integer
k
for finding local neighborhoods. The output is
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d\times n_{3}}
. The key idea behind this method is to minimize the distance between the neighboring points in low dimensional space for each frontal slice in Fourier domain. With this in mind, we minimize the following function.

g_{r}(\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijl}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)}_{i}-\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\right\|^{2}_{F},\ r=1,2,\ldots,n_{3},

(21)

with
\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijl}
are the elements of the affinity tensor, see sub-Section
4.1
. The objective function (
21
) can be written as

\displaystyle g_{r}(\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\right\|^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\right)\left(\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)\top}-\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)\top}-\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)\top}-\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)\top}+\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{iir}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)\top}-2\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)\top}+\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{jjr}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)\top}\right].

We can simplify it to

g_{r}(\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{iir}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)\top}-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ijr}^{\prime}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{j}^{(r)\top}.

We put

G(\mathscr{Y})=\sum_{r=1}^{n3}g_{r}\left(\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)}\right).

Then we can rewrite the equation using a tensor notation as

\displaystyle G(\mathscr{Y})=n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{Y}^{T}\star(\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W})\star\mathscr{Y}\right)=n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{Y}^{T}\star\mathscr{L}\star\mathscr{Y}\right),

with
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}
and
\mathscr{D}
is an f-diagonal tensor with
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{iir}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{jir}
, and
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d\times n_{3}}
is the tensor of the coordinates for the
n_{1}
points.

Thus, the problem (
21

\underset{\mathscr{Y}}{\arg\min}\ n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{Y}^{T}\star\mathscr{L}\star\mathscr{Y}\right),\quad\text{subject to }\mathscr{Y}^{T}\star\mathscr{D}\star\mathscr{Y}=\mathscr{I}_{d}.

(22)

We have the tensor
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{D}-\mathscr{W}
is f-symmetric, and the tensor
\mathscr{D}

19

eigenslices associated with the

4.4
Locally Multilinear Embedding

This method (LME) is an unsupervised non-linear dimensionality reduction algorithm, requiring as inputs, data tensor
\mathscr{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}
, with each sample represented by a third-order tensor
\left\{\mathscr{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n_{2}\times n_{3}},i=1,\ldots,n_{1}\right\}
, several dimensions
d<n_{2}
and integer
k
for finding local neighborhoods. The output is a tensor
\mathscr{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times d\times n_{3}}
.
The main idea of local Multilinear Embedding is to use the same reconstruction weights in the lower-dimensional integration space as in the higher-dimensional input space. In the following sub-sections, we will explain this.

4.4.1
Multilinear Reconstruction by the Neighbors

In this section, we find the weights for the Multilinear reconstruction of every point by its
k
-NN. The optimization problem for this Multilinear reconstruction in the high-dimensional input space is given by the minimization of the function

\displaystyle\varepsilon({\mathscr{E}^{(r)}})

\displaystyle:=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left\|{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{i}-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{ij}{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{ij}\right\|_{F}^{2},

subject to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{ij}=1,\quad i=1,\ldots,n_{1},\quad r=1,\ldots,n_{3},

(24)

where
\mathscr{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}\times n_{3}}

{\mathscr{E}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k\times n_{3}}
, with

{\mathscr{E}}^{(r)}_{i}=[{\mathscr{E}}^{(r)}_{ik},\ldots,{\mathscr{E}}^{(r)}_{ik}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
includes the weights of Multilinear reconstruction of the
i
-th data point using its
k
neighbors in the
r
-th frontal slice, and
{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
is the
j
-th neighbor of the
i
-th data point in the
r
-th frontal slices. The constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{ij}=1
means that the weights of linear reconstruction sum to one for every point in each frontal slice.

We can write the objective
\varepsilon({\mathscr{E}^{(r)}})
as

\varepsilon({\mathscr{E}^{(r)}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left\|{\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}-{\bm{X}}^{(r)}_{i}{\mathscr{E}}^{(r)}_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2},\quad r=1,\ldots,n_{3},

with
{\bm{X}}^{(r)}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times k}
contain the
k
neighbor of the
i
-th data point in the
r
-th frontal slice.
The constraint
\sum_{j=1}^{k}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{ij}=1
implies that
\mathbf{1}^{\top}{\mathscr{E}}^{(r)}_{i}=1
, where
\mathbf{1}:=

[1,\ldots,1]^{\top}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
therefore,
{\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{i}={\mathscr{X}}^{(r)}_{i}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}
.

We can simplify the term in
\varepsilon(\mathscr{E}^{(r)})
as

\displaystyle\left\|\mathscr{X}^{(r)}_{i}-\bm{X}_{i}^{(r)}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathscr{X}_{i}^{(r)}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}-\bm{X}_{i}^{(r)}\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(\mathscr{X}_{i}^{(r)}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\bm{X}_{i}^{(r)}\right)\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}\right\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{E}^{(r)\top}_{i}\left(\mathscr{X}^{(r)}_{i}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\bm{X}^{(r)}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathscr{X}^{(r)}_{i}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-\bm{X}^{(r)}_{i}\right)\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=\mathscr{E}^{(r)\top}_{i}\bm{G}_{ir}\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)},

where
\bm{G}_{ir}
is a gram matrix defined as

\bm{G}_{ir}:=\left({\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-{\bm{X}}_{i}^{(r)}\right)^{\top}\left({\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}\mathbf{1}^{\top}-{\bm{X}}_{i}^{(r)}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},\ r=1,\ldots,n_{3}.

Finally, we have

\displaystyle\underset{\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}}{\arg\min}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathscr{E}^{(r)\top}_{i}\bm{G}_{ir}\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)},\quad\text{for }i=1,\ldots,n_{1},r=1,\ldots,n_{3},

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}=1.

The Lagrangian of this problem can be formulated as

\mathscr{L}\left(\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i},\lambda_{ir}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathscr{E}^{(r)\top}_{i}\bm{G}_{ir}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\lambda_{ir}\left(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}-1\right),

setting the derivative of Lagrangian to zero gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}\left(\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i},\lambda_{ir}\right)}{\partial\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}}

\displaystyle=2\bm{G}_{ir}\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}-\lambda_{ir}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{0},\ r=1,...,n_{3},

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\mathscr{E}_{i}^{(r)}=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{G}_{ir}}^{-1}\lambda_{ir}\mathbf{1}=\frac{\lambda_{ir}}{2}\bm{G}_{ir}^{-1}\mathbf{1}.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}\left(\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i},\lambda_{ir}\right)}{\partial\lambda_{ir}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}-1=0\Longrightarrow\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}=1,

then we have

\frac{\lambda_{ir}}{2}\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{G}_{ir}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=1\Longrightarrow\lambda_{ir}=\frac{2}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{G}_{ir}^{-1}\mathbf{1}},\ r=1,\ldots,n_{3},

therefore

\mathscr{E}^{(r)}_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{ir}}{2}\bm{G}_{ir}^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\frac{\bm{G}_{ir}^{-1}\mathbf{1}}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bm{G}_{ir}^{-1}\mathbf{1}},r=1,\ldots,n_{3},\ i=1,\ldots,n_{1}.

Moreover, reader must note that the rank of the matrix
\bm{G}_{ir}
, so the rank of matrix

is at most equal to
\min(k,n_{2})

is singular then
\bm{G}_{ir}
should be replaced by
\bm{G}_{ir}+\epsilon\bm{I}
where
\epsilon

\bm{G}_{ir}
is full rank, we will not have any problem with inverting it.

4.4.2
Multilinear Embedding

In the last sub-section, we found the weights for Multilinear reconstruction in the high dimensional input space. In this sub-section, data points are projected in the low dimensional embedding space using the same weights as in the input space. This Multilinear embedding can be formulated as

\displaystyle\underset{\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)}}{\arg\min}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}-\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}^{(r)}_{ij}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)}_{j}\right\|_{F}^{2},\quad r=1,\ldots,n_{3},

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\dfrac{1}{n_{1}}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)\top}={{I}},\quad\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}^{(r)}=\mathbf{0},

(25)

where
{I}
is the Identity matrix, and
\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times d\times n_{3}}
is the
i
-th embedded data point in the Fourier domain, and
\widehat{\mathscr{W}}^{(r)}_{ij}
is given by

\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{ij}^{(r)}:=\begin{cases}\widehat{\mathscr{E}}^{(r)}_{ij}&\text{ if }{\mathscr{X}}_{j}^{(r)}\in\text{k-NN}\left({\mathscr{X}}_{i}^{(r)}\right),\ r=1,\ldots,n_{3},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

The second constraint in equation (
4.4.2
) states that the mean of the projected data points is zero. The first and second constraints together ensure that the projected points have unit covariance.
We have
\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{i}^{(r)}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let

[0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the vector whose

which can be formulated as

\begin{gathered}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{e}_{i}-\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}_{i}^{(r)}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\left\|\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{I}}_{n_{1}}^{(r)}-\widehat{\mathscr{Y}}^{(r)\top}\widehat{\mathscr{W}}^{(r)\top}\right\|_{F}^{2}\\

\displaystyle=n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{Y}^{\top}\star(\mathscr{I}_{n_{1}}-\mathscr{W})^{\top}\star(\mathscr{I}_{n_{1}}-\mathscr{W})\star\mathscr{Y}\right)

\displaystyle=n_{3}\operatorname{Trace}\left(\mathscr{Y}^{\top}\star\mathscr{M}\star\mathscr{Y}\right),

where

5.1
Data sets

In our experiments, we used four multidimensional databases: face recognition AR, FEI, Brain Tumor MRI, and COVID-19 Chestxray. The AR and FEI datasets are specifically designed for face recognition, while the Brain Tumor MRI and COVID-19 datasets provide MRI and X-ray images, respectively, which have applications in medical imaging. In the following, we have a description of datasets.

•

AR database
: The AR database contains 2600 images featuring frontal faces with different expressions, lighting conditions, and occlusions. where each subject has 26 facial images taken in two sessions separated by two weeks, as illustrated in Fig. Furthermore, two formats of data representations are used according to the formulations of algorithms. By 2D formulation, an image is stored as a matrix of size 115 by 115. we resize each image to 32 by 32 pixels and the dimension of the dataset is 2600×32×32×3.

•

FEI face databese
: The FEI database is a Brazilian face database that contains a set of face images taken between June 2005 and March 2006 at the Artificial Intelligence Laboratory of FEI in São Bernardo do Campo, São Paulo, Brazil. There are 14 images for each of 200 individuals, corresponding to 2800 images in total. All images are colorful and taken against a white homogenous background in an upright frontal position with profile rotation of up to about 180 degrees. Scale might vary about 10% and the original size of each image is 640x480 pixels. we resize each image to 32 by 32 pixels and the dimension of the dataset is 2800×32×32×3.

•

Brain Tumor MRI Dataset
The Brain Tumor MRI Dataset contains 1311 images of human brain MRI images, which are classified into 4 classes glioma, meningioma, no tumor, and pituitary. The dimensions of this data are (1311, 32, 32, 3), where 1311 is the number of MRI images, 64 is the height and width of each image in pixels, and 3 represents the RGB color channels. This dataset provides a rich source of medical imaging data for research into the automatic detection and classification of brain tumors and can be used to train and evaluate machine learning algorithms in this domain. The use of MRI in the detection of brain tumors is a well-established medical imaging technique, and this dataset allows for further development and improvement of these techniques. The results of research using this dataset have the potential to improve patient outcomes and save lives.

•

COVID-19
: The COVID-19 is a collection of chest X-ray images of patients with or without COVID-19 and/or pneumonia. The dataset is classified into three classes COVID-19, Pneumonia, and Normal, and has dimensions of (925, 32, 32, 3), with 925 images of 32x32 pixels and 3 color channels. The goal of the dataset is to provide a large and diverse set of data for research and development of machine learning algorithms for the automatic detection of COVID-19 and pneumonia on chest X-rays.

Firstly, we can see from Figure

This difference can be attributed to the computational approach required for each method. Specifically, the n-mode product-based methods necessitate first converting the tensor into a matrix (metricizing) before calculating the eigenvectors. Conversely, t-product-based methods allow for the direct computation of the solution across the entire tensor without the need to metricize the tensor first.

Secondly, also from the figure
5
, we notice that when comparing methods based on the t-product, t-MLE and t-LME perform better in terms of accuracy than t-MDA and t-MLDE. This difference in performance can be attributed to the fact that t-MDA and t-MLDE are linear, whereas t-MLE and t-LME are non-linear. Generally, non-linear methods tend to be more accurate.

Thirdly, when comparing t-MLE with t-LME, it’s observed that t-LME performs better with datasets containing a large number of classes, offering higher accuracy. For example, the AR dataset, which contains 26 classes, shows t-LME achieving an accuracy of 91.53% compared to t-MLE’s 82.08%. Conversely, for datasets with a smaller number of classes, such as the COVID-19 dataset, which contains just 3 classes, t-MLE shows superior results. Specifically, the accuracy of t-MLE on the COVID-19 dataset is 97.19%, whereas t-LME achieves only 76.10% accuracy on the same dataset. Therefore, understanding the number of classes in our dataset allows us to select the method that provides the best accuracy.

Firstly note that the t-LME method requires a significant time for dimensionality reduction, which is expected given its complexity. The process starts with calculating the weights for the multi-linear reconstruction of each data point in the high-dimensional input space. This calculation necessitates the resolution of an optimization problem. Following this, the method projects the data points into a lower-dimensional embedding space, using the previously determined weights. This projection phase also requires the solution of a tarce-ratio tensor problem.

Secondly, it is observed that the t-MLE method demonstrates remarkable speed in processing. For instance, with the COVID-19 dataset, it completes the task in merely 1.86 seconds, and for the MRI dataset, it requires only 5.51 seconds.

In summary, while both t-MLE and t-LME methods are effective for reducing the dimensionality of multidimensional data, However, t-MLE stands out as the better choice, not only for its rapid processing speed but also for its accuracy.

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a generalization of trace ratio methods for multidimensional data. This generalization includes Local Discriminant Embedding, Laplacian Eigenmaps, and Locally Linear Embedding based on the concept of t-product.
To extend these methods to tensors or multilinear data, we present certain definitions and propose theoretical findings. Additionally, we offer a Newton-QR algorithm as a solution to the trace-ratio challenge. Finally, we showcase the numerical results of these methods compared to the state-of-the-art.
