Zeros of generalized hypergeometric polynomials via finite free convolution. Applications to multiple orthogonality
Andrei Martínez-Finkelshtein, Rafael Morales, Daniel Perales

Abstract
. We address the problem of the weak asymptotic behavior of zeros of families of generalized hypergeometric polynomials as their degree tends to infinity. The main tool is the representation of such polynomials as a finite free convolution of simpler elements; this representation is preserved in the asymptotic regime, so we can formally write the limit zero distribution of these polynomials as a free convolution of explicitly computable measures. We derive a simple expression for the S S -transform of the limit distribution, which turns out to be a rational function, and a representation of the Kampé de Fériet polynomials in terms of finite free convolutions. We apply these tools, as well as those from [ 41 ] , to the study of some well-known families of multiple orthogonal polynomials (Jacobi-Piñeiro and multiple Laguerre of the first and second kinds), obtaining results on their zeros, such as interlacing, monotonicity, and asymptotics.

1.
Introduction

The definition of the generalized hypergeometric function
{}_{i}F_{j}
with
i
numerator and
j
denominator parameters is well known. If one of the numerator parameters is equal to a negative integer, say
-n
, with
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then the series terminates and is a polynomial of degree
\leq n
. If all its zeros are real, we usually want to know their properties, such as positivity/negativity, interlacing, and monotonicity with respect to the parameters. In the case of a sequence of such polynomials, enumerated by their degree
n
, we also want to investigate their asymptotic behavior as
n\to\infty
.

For small values of
i
and
j
, the answer to these questions can usually be obtained by exploiting their connection to some classical families of polynomials, in many cases orthogonal, or using their other properties, such as the differential equation or integral representation. But when
i,j\geq 2
, the problem becomes more difficult due to the limited number of tools that allow us to investigate their behavior.

The finite free convolutions (that in this paper come in two flavors, multiplicative
\boxtimes_{n}
and additive
\boxplus_{n}
), are binary operations on polynomials, studied already by Szegő, Schur, Walsh, and others, although under different names. They behave especially well when applied to real-rooted polynomials, preserving zero interlacing and monotonicity. Recently, such convolutions have been rediscovered as expected characteristic polynomials of a multiplication (or addition) of random matrices
[
39
]
, and were also interpreted as finite analogs of the free probability
[
38
]
(thus, named generically as finite free convolution of polynomials).

The connection between these polynomial convolutions and free probability is revealed in the asymptotic regime, when we consider the zero-counting measure (also known in this context as the empirical root distribution) of a sequence of polynomials whose degree tends to infinity. Then the finite free convolution of polynomials turns into a free convolution of limiting distributions of their zeros
[
4
,
5
]
.

In a recent paper
[
41
]
, the authors illustrated the power of finite free convolution of polynomials to prove the real-rootedness, interlacing, or monotonicity of zeros with respect to the parameters. The key tool was the representation of generalized hypergeometric polynomials as finite free convolutions of simpler “building blocks”, much easier to study. We also briefly explained the potential of this approach in the study of asymptotic behavior.

This work is, in a certain sense, a natural continuation of
[
41
]
. Here, the main focus is precisely on the weak asymptotic behavior of zeros of families of generalized hypergeometric polynomials as their degree tends to infinity. Using the representation of a generalized hypergeometric polynomial as a finite free convolution of some simpler elements, we can formally write the limit zero distribution of these polynomials as a free convolution of simpler measures. In order to convert this observation into an effective computational tool, we derive in Section
3.3
an expression for the so-called
S
-transform of the limit zero-counting measures of the original polynomials. Unlike the case of the Cauchy transform of such a measure, which is normally an algebraic function, the
S
-transform turns out to be a rational function, easily expressible in terms of the main parameters of the problem.

In the second part of the article, we apply these tools, as well as those from
[
41
]
, to the study of some well-known families of multiple orthogonal polynomials. While writing the paper, we became aware that the recent contribution
[
59
]
mentions some connections of multiple orthogonal polynomials and finite free convolution using the results from
[
41
]
.

Multiple
, also known as Hermite-Padé,
orthogonal polynomials
(MOPs), are polynomials of one variable that satisfy the orthogonality conditions with respect to several measures. They are a very useful extension of orthogonal polynomials and have recently received renewed interest because tools have become available to investigate their asymptotic behavior, and they do appear in a number of fascinating applications, see
[
43
]
.

In this paper, we consider only the case of absolutely continuous orthogonality measures on the real line. Thus, let
r
be a positive integer and
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{r}
be non-negative integrable functions (“weights”) on
\mathbb{R}
for which all the moments
are finite. Let
\bm{n}=(n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be a multi-index of size
|\bm{n}|=n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{r}
.
There are two types of multiple orthogonal polynomials.
Type I multiple orthogonal polynomials
are given as a vector

(A_{\bm{n},1},A_{\bm{n},2},\ldots,A_{\bm{n},r})
of
r
polynomials, where
A_{\bm{n},j}
has degree
\leq n_{j}-1
, for
which the function

(1)

Q_{\bm{n}}(x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{j=1}^{r}A_{\bm{n},j}(x)w_{j}(x)

is orthogonal to all polynomials of degree
\leq|\bm{n}|-2
:

(2)

\int x^{k}Q_{\bm{n}}(x)\,dx=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\int x^{k}A_{\bm{n},j}(x)w_{j}(x)\,dx=0,\qquad 0\leq k\leq|\bm{n}|-2.

One usually adds the normalization

(3)

\int x^{|\bm{n}|-1}Q_{\bm{n}}(x)\,dx=1.

The
Type II multiple orthogonal polynomial

P_{\bm{n}}
is the monic polynomial of degree
|\bm{n}|
that satisfies
the orthogonality conditions

(4)

\int P_{\bm{n}}(x)x^{k}w_{j}(x)\,dx=0,\qquad 0\leq k\leq n_{j}-1,

for
1\leq j\leq r
.
Both conditions (
2
)–(
3
) and condition (
4
) yield a corresponding linear system of
|\bm{n}|
equations in the
|\bm{n}|
unknowns, either
coefficients of polynomials
(A_{\bm{n},1},\dots,A_{\bm{n},r})
or coefficients of the monic polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
.
The matrices of these linear systems are each other’s transpose and contain moments of the
r
weights
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})
.
A solution of these linear systems may not exist or may not be unique. One needs extra assumptions on the weights

(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})
for a solution to exist and to be unique. If a unique solution exists for a multi-index
\bm{n}
then
the multi-index is said to be
normal
. If all multi-indices are normal, then the system
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})
is said to be a
perfect system
.

The weights
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})
form an
AT-system
on an interval
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}
for a multi-index
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
if for any polynomials
A_{\bm{n},j}
,
j=1,\ldots,r
, in (
1
), satisfying the mentioned degree constraints and not all equal to
0
, the function
Q_{\bm{n}}
has at most
|\bm{n}|-1
zeros on
\Delta
, see, e.g.
[
24
, Chapter 23]
. It is known that if
(w_{1},\ldots,w_{r})
is an AT-system for every multi-index
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, the system is perfect. An example of such systems are the
Nikishin systems
, fact proved in
[
15
,
16
]
. Since this is not central to our discussion, for the definition of such systems we refer the reader to
[
37
,
35
,
34
]
.

For every AT-system and for any
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, the Type I function for
Q_{\bm{n}}
defined by (
1
) and satisfying (
2
), has exactly
|\bm{n}|-1
sign changes in
\Delta
, while the Type II multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
, satisfying (
4
), has
|\bm{n}|
simple zeros on
\Delta
. These zeros exhibit an interlacing property, meaning that there is always a zero of
P_{\bm{n}}
between two consecutive zeros of
P_{\bm{n}+\bm{e}_{k}}
, for each
0\leq k\leq r-1
, where
\bm{e}_{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
is the multi-index that has all entries equal to 0 except the entry of index
k
which is equal to 1.

Various families of special MOPs have been found, extending the classical orthogonal polynomials, but also related to completely new special functions
[
1
]
,
[
24
, Ch. 23]
.

One of the well-known AT-systems is given by the weights

(5)

w_{j}(x)=x^{\alpha_{j}}(1-x)^{\beta},\quad j=1,2,\dots,r,

on the interval
\Delta=[0,1]
.
Here
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r},\beta>-1
and
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for
i\neq j
. The corresponding polynomials are known as the
Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials
, and are studied in Sections
4.1
and
5.1
.

Two different AT-systems correspond to multiple Laguerre weights on
[0,+\infty)
, see, e.g.
[
24
, §23.4]
. First, we can take

(6)

w_{j}(x)=x^{\alpha_{j}}e^{-x},\quad j=1,2,\dots,r,

where again
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}>-1
and
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}
for
i\neq j
. The corresponding polynomials are known as
multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind
, and their behavior is discussed in Sections
4.2
and
5.2
.

Another option is to define the weights

(7)

w_{j}(x)=x^{\alpha}e^{-c_{j}x},\quad j=1,2,\dots,r,

where
\alpha>-1
, with all
c_{i}>0
, and such that
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
for
i\neq j
. The corresponding polynomials are known as
multiple Laguerre polynomials of the second kind
, and their properties are discussed in Sections
4.3
and
5.3
. For Type I, they are a special case of the Kampé de Fériet polynomials, so, in Section
3.1
, we prove the more general fact that they can be represented as a finite free additive convolution of hypergeometric polynomials.

The asymptotic behavior of MOPs is a highly non-trivial subject. Although the study of the Hermite-Padé polynomials goes back to the original works of Hermite, see
[
22
,
23
]
, as well as
[
48
]
, the first important asymptotic result appeared in the work of Kalyagin
[
25
]
. In the 1980s, the ground-breaking works of Aptekarev, Gonchar, Rakhmanov, and Stahl, made clear that the asymptotics of the Type I form (
1
) (but not of the individual entries
A_{\bm{n},i}
) and of the Type II polynomials
P_{\bm{n}}
can be described in terms of a vector equilibrium problem for logarithmic potentials,
[
3
,
17
,
18
]
. However, solving such a problem is usually a formidable task.

Another approach is via the higher-order nearest-neighbor recurrence relations by MOPs, established in the work of Van Assche
[
55
]
(see also
[
24
, Ch. 23]
) that allow to derive an algebraic equation on a weighted distribution of zeros. An important ingredient of this method is the expression (or, at least, the behavior) of the recurrence coefficients. A systematic study of a large number of classical and semi-classical families of orthogonality weights for MOP, and in particular, of their recurrence coefficients, was carried out in a number of contributions by Van Assche and his collaborators in recent years; see, e.g.
[
2
]
. A limitation of this approach is that it allows one to address only the quasi-diagonal case (step line) for the multi-indices
\bm{n}
.

Finally, another recent and formidable tool for asymptotic analysis is the non-linear steepest descent method of Deift and Zhou, applied to the Riemann-Hilbert characterization of MOPs
[
56
]
. This technique renders extremely precise asymptotic information
(see, e.g.
[
7
,
11
,
43
,
42
]
, to mention a few), but at a very high cost of being technically challenging.

In this paper, we address the problem of the asymptotic zero distribution of the three families of MOP mentioned above, when the degree
|\bm{n}|\to\infty
, also allowing a linear dependence of the parameters
\alpha
’s and
\beta
’s on
\bm{n}
. As recent investigations show, these polynomials are hypergeometric, so we can use the free convolution approach at a relatively low cost. We stress that one of the appeals of this technique is its simplicity. Alternatives such as the general methods described above, or using the differential equation or the integral representation
[
60
]
of these MOPs are usually much more involved. It is worth mentioning also that the zero asymptotics of the Type II multiple Laguerre polynomials of the second kind was obtained in terms of free convolution of measure in
[
21
]
using arguments from random matrix theory.

Since a representation of a real-rooted polynomial in terms of free convolution can involve polynomials with complex zeros, the results on the asymptotic regime from
[
4
,
5
]
cannot be directly applied. Taking advantage of the fact the proofs in those references are essentially algebraic, based on the behavior of moments and cumulants, we adapt these arguments to analyze the finite free convolutions in the asymptotic regime for measures compactly supported on the complex plane and not necessarily on the real line; see Section
3.2
and Appendix
A
.

As a by-product of the representation of the hypergeometric polynomials in terms of finite free convolutions, we also derive some zero monotonicity and interlacing properties for the mentioned families of MOP. They appear to be new in the majority of cases; they are especially interesting for the polynomials
A_{\bm{n},i}
in (
1
), where not much is known.

2.2.
Hypergeometric polynomials

For

2.3.
Finite free convolutions

Definition 2.1

(
[
39
]
)
.

Given two polynomials,
p
and
q
, of degree at most
n
, the

n
-th multiplicative finite free convolution
of
p
and
q
, denoted as
p\boxtimes_{n}q
, is a polynomial of degree at most
n
, which can be defined in terms of the coefficients of polynomials written in the form

(15)

p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}x^{n-j}(-1)^{j}e_{j}(p)\quad\text{ and }\quad q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}x^{n-j}(-1)^{j}e_{j}(q).

Namely,

[p\boxtimes_{n}q](x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^{n-k}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(p\boxtimes_{n}q),

with

(16)

e_{k}(p\boxtimes_{n}q)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\binom{n}{k}^{-1}e_{k}(p)e_{k}(q).

In particular, if
p,q
are of degree
n
and monic, then also
p\boxtimes_{n}q
has the same property.

The multiplicative finite free convolution is a bi-linear operator from
\mathbb{P}_{n}\times\mathbb{P}_{n}
to
\mathbb{P}_{n}
: if
p,q,r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(\alpha p+q)\boxtimes_{n}r=\alpha(p\boxtimes_{n}r)+q\boxtimes_{n}r.

From Definition
2.1
it easily follows that multiplicative convolution with the polynomial
(x-\alpha)^{n}
is equivalent to a
dilation
of the roots by
\alpha
:

(17)

\text{Dil}_{\alpha}(p)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\alpha^{n}p\left(\frac{x}{\alpha}\right)=p(x)\boxtimes_{n}(x-\alpha)^{n},\quad\alpha\neq 0.

In consequence,

(18)

(\text{Dil}_{\alpha}p)\boxtimes_{n}q=p\boxtimes_{n}(\text{Dil}_{\alpha}q)=\text{Dil}_{\alpha}\left[p\boxtimes_{n}q\right],\quad\alpha\neq 0,

and

(19)

\text{Dil}_{\alpha}\text{Dil}_{\beta}(p)=\text{Dil}_{\alpha\beta}(p),\quad\alpha,\beta\neq 0.

The following result was proved in
[
41
, Theorem 3.1]
:

Theorem A
.

If
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and

p(x)={\ }_{i_{1}+1}F_{j_{1}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, \bm a_1}{\bm b_1};x\right)},\qquad q(x)={\ }_{i_{2}+1}F_{j_{2}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, \bm a_2}{\bm b_2};x\right)},

where the parameters
\bm{a}_{1},\bm{a}_{2},\bm{b}_{1},\bm{b}_{2}
are tuples (of sizes
i_{1},i_{2},j_{i},j_{2}
, respectively), then their
n
-th free multiplicative convolution is given by

[p\boxtimes_{n}q](x)={\ }_{i_{1}+i_{2}+1}F_{j_{1}+j_{2}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, \bm a_1, \bm a_2}{\bm b_1,\bm b_2};x\right)}.

Definition 2.2

(
[
39
]
)
.

Given two polynomials,
p
and
q
, of degree at most
n
, the
n
-th additive finite free convolution
of
p
and
q
, denoted as
p\boxplus_{n}q
, is a polynomial of degree at most
n
, defined in terms of the coefficients of polynomials written in the form

(20)

p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}x^{n-j}(-1)^{j}e_{j}(p)\quad\text{ and }\quad q(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}x^{n-j}(-1)^{j}e_{j}(q).

Namely,

[p\boxplus_{n}q](x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{k=0}^{n}x^{n-k}(-1)^{k}e_{k}(p\boxplus_{n}q),

with

(21)

e_{k}(p\boxplus_{n}q)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\left(n\right)^{\underline{k}}\sum_{i+j=k}\frac{e_{i}(p)}{\left(n\right)^{\underline{i}}}\frac{e_{j}(q)}{\left(n\right)^{\underline{j}}}

(and thus,
e_{0}(p\boxplus_{n}q)=e_{0}(p)e_{0}(q)
).

The additive finite free convolution is a bi-linear operator from
\mathbb{P}_{n}\times\mathbb{P}_{n}
to
\mathbb{P}_{n}
: if
p,q,r\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
, and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, then

(\alpha p+q)\boxplus_{n}r=\alpha(p\boxplus_{n}r)+q\boxplus_{n}r.

Moreover,

(22)

p(x)\boxplus_{n}(x-\alpha)^{n}=p(x-\alpha),\quad p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}.

From the hypothesis viewed as a differential function we get

p(\mathfrak{d})={\ }_{i_{1}}F_{j_{1}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-\bm b_1-n+1}{-\bm a_1-n+1};(-1)^{i_{1}+j_{1}+l_{1}+1}\mathfrak{d}\right)}{\ }_{i_{2}}F_{j_{2}}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{ -\bm b_2-n+1}{-\bm a_2-n+1};(-1)^{i_{2}+j_{2}+l_{2}+1}\mathfrak{d}\right)}.

2.4.
Real roots, interlacing, and free finite convolution

A very important fact is that in many circumstances the finite free convolution of two polynomials with real roots also has all its roots real. Here, we use the notation introduced at the beginning of Section
2
.

Proposition 2.4

(Szegő
[
52
]
, Walsh
[
58
]
, see also
[
41
]
)
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
. Then

(i)

p,q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\ \Rightarrow\ p\boxplus_{n}q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

(ii)

p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\ q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\ \Rightarrow\ p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
.

(iii)

p,q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\ \Rightarrow\ p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
.

(iv)

p,q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0})\ \Rightarrow\ p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})

(v)

p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}),\ q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\ \Rightarrow\ p\boxtimes_{n}q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0})
.

If we replace above the sets
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
by strict inclusions,
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\mathbb{R}_{<0}
, respectively, the statements of Proposition
2.4
remain valid.

Definition 2.5

(Interlacing)
.

Let

p(x)=e_{0}(p)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\left(x-\lambda_{j}(p)\right)\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{n}(p),

and

q(x)=e_{0}(q)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(x-\lambda_{j}(q)\right)\in\mathbb{P}_{m}(\mathbb{R}),\quad\lambda_{1}(q)\leq\dots\leq\lambda_{m}(q).

We say that
q

interlaces

p
(or, equivalently, that
zeros of
q
interlace zeros of
p
, see, e.g.,
[
12
]
), and denote it
p\preccurlyeq q
, if

(26)

m=n\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{1}(q)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(q)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}(p)\leq\lambda_{n}(q),

or if

(27)

m=n-1\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{1}(p)\leq\lambda_{1}(q)\leq\lambda_{2}(p)\leq\lambda_{2}(q)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n-1}(p)\leq\lambda_{n-1}(q)\leq\lambda_{n}(p).

Furthermore, we use the notation
p\prec q
when all inequalities in (
26
) or (
27
) are strict.

From the real-root preservation and the linearity of the free finite convolution one easily obtains the following interlacing-preservation property:

Proposition 2.6

(Preservation of interlacing)
.

If
p,\widetilde{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})
be of degree exactly
n
such that
p\preccurlyeq\widetilde{p}
, then for a polynomial
q
of degree
n
,

q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R})\quad\Rightarrow\quad p\boxplus_{n}q\preccurlyeq\widetilde{p}\boxplus_{n}q

and

q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})\quad\Rightarrow\quad p\boxtimes_{n}q\preccurlyeq\widetilde{p}\boxtimes_{n}q.

The same statements hold if we replace all
\preccurlyeq
by
\prec
.

For a proof of this result, see
[
41
, Proposition 2.11]
.

Remark 2.7
.

Noticing that
p\preccurlyeq q
if and only if
q(-x)\preccurlyeq p(-x)
and that
p(x)\boxtimes_{n}q(-x)=[p\boxtimes_{n}q](-x)
, we can easily extend Proposition
2.6
to include polynomials with negative real roots. Namely, if
p,\widetilde{p}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}^{*}(\mathbb{R})
and
p\preccurlyeq\widetilde{p}
, then for a polynomial
q
of degree
n
,

q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\widetilde{p}\boxtimes_{n}q\preccurlyeq p\boxtimes_{n}q.

2.5.
Integral transforms and free convolution of measures

We follow the standard notation from the literature on free probability (see, e.g.
[
4
,
39
,
44
,
46
]
) and define several transforms for a Borel measure
\mu
.

The
Cauchy transform
of
\mu
,

(28)

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\int\frac{d\mu(y)}{z-y},

is well defined and analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\supp(\mu)
, even in the case of a signed measure. If
\mu
is compactly supported, then
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
has a Laurent expansion

(29)

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{m_{k}}{z^{k+1}},

convergent in a neighborhood of infinity, where

m_{j}=\int z^{j}\,d\mu(z),\quad j=0,1,2,\dots

are the
moments
of the measure
\mu
. Unless specified otherwise, we assume in what follows that
\mu
is a compactly supported positive probability measure, so that

(30)

m_{0}=1.

The
K
-transform
of the compactly supported probability measure
\mu
is the functional inverse of its Cauchy transform, that is,

\mathcal{K}_{\mu}(w)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathcal{G}^{-1}_{\mu}(w),

analytic in a punctured neighborhood of the origin, while its
R
-transform

\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(w)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathcal{K}_{\mu}(w)-\frac{1}{w}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\kappa_{n+1}w^{n},

is analytic in a neighborhood of the origin
2
2

2

Clearly, the analyticity of
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
at the origin for a compactly supported measure is equivalent to condition (
30
).
. The coefficients
\kappa_{n}
are called the
free cumulants
of
\mu
. Notice that
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\equiv 0
if and only if

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)=1/z

in a neighborhood of infinity; this is the case of
\mu=\delta_{0}
, the Dirac delta or unit mass point at the origin, but not only: any unit Lebesgue measure on a circle or a disk centered at the origin has this property.

Closely related to the Cauchy transform is the
generating function of the moments
(for short, the
M
-transform
) of a compactly-supported probability measure
\mu
,

(31)

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(z)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{1}{z}\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)-1=\int\frac{zy\,d\mu(y)}{1-zy},

for which the expansion

(32)

\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}m_{k}\,z^{k}

converges in a neighborhood of the origin.
We finally define the
S
-transform
of
\mu
as

(33)

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(w)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{w+1}{w}\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{-1}(w),

where
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{-1}
is the functional inverse of
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
(when it exists). Notice that once again
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}\equiv 0
if and only if
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)=1/z
; furthermore, if
\mu
is compactly supported and
m_{1}\neq 0
then
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
is analytic in a neighborhood of origin and nonvanishing at
w=0
, with
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(0)=1/m_{1}.

We can guarantee that
m_{1}\neq 0
if
\mu
is a positive probability measure supported on the positive (or negative) semiaxis.

All these transforms determine each other via the identities

(34)

\left(z\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(z)+z\right)\mathcal{R}_{\mu}\left(z\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(z)+z\right)=\mathcal{M}_{\mu}(z),\qquad\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\left(\frac{z+1}{z\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)}\right)=z\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z),

which follow directly from the definitions above. Combining (
33
) and (
34
) we obtain also a direct relation between the
R
-transform and the
S
-transforms (see for instance
[
46
, Equation (16.8), Definition 18.15 and Remark 18.16]
):

(35)

w\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(w)=\left(w\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(w)\right)^{-1},

where
\left(w\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(w)\right)^{-1}
is the functional inverse of
w\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(w)
(when it exists).

We will later use the well-known fact, see for instance
[
19
, Proposition 3.13]
, that for a measure
\mu
such that
\mu(\{0\})=0
it holds that

(36)

S_{\mu}(z)S_{\mu^{*}}(-z-1)=1,

where
\mu^{*}
is the
reversed measure
of
\mu
(equivalently, the push-forward measure under the mapping
t\mapsto 1/t
), so that its density is
d\mu^{*}(t)=t^{-2}d\mu(t^{-1})
). The identity (
36
) is valid in a punctured neighborhood of origin, in the domain of analyticity of both functions on the left-hand side.

Remark 2.8
.

When
\mu
is not compactly supported, expressions (
28
) and (
31
) make sense and define analytic functions in the open set
\mathbb{C}\setminus\supp(\mu)
and in its image by
z\mapsto 1/z
, respectively. Formula (
33
) is also well-posed, at least where
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
is locally invertible. In this case, we cannot expect the series in (
29
) and (
32
) to converge.

On the other hand, the formulas in (
29
) and (
32
) can be considered as formal power series associated with a “moment” sequence
m_{0},m_{1},\dots,
of complex numbers. In that case, if we denote
the sequence
by
\mu
, we can use these formulas as a
definition
of
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\mu}
, as well as define
\mathcal{R}_{\mu}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
through (
34
). In other words, all the transforms can be defined for any complex sequence but only as formal power series (also called moment series), with no analyticity or convergence assumed a priori.

As we will see in Section
3.2
, the advantage of this approach is that many asymptotic results can be established even for formal moment sequences, without assuming any underlying measure.

Using these definitions, we can introduce two important operations on compactly supported probability measures
\mu
and
\nu
(or, as we have just discussed, on their moment sequences):

(i)

the
free additive convolution

\mu\boxplus\nu
via the identity

(37)

\mathcal{R}_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(w)=\mathcal{R}_{\mu}(w)+\mathcal{R}_{\nu}(w);

(ii)

the
free multiplicative convolution

\mu\boxtimes\nu
via the identity

(38)

\mathcal{S}_{\mu\boxtimes\nu}(w)=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(w)\mathcal{S}_{\nu}(w).

Clearly, identities (
37
)–(
38
) do not define the resulting measures
\mu\boxplus\nu
and
\mu\boxtimes\nu
uniquely, unless they are determined by the analytic expression of their
R-
and
S-
transforms, respectively. This is the case of probability measures compactly supported on
\mathbb{R}
.

3.2.
Finite free convolutions in the asymptotic regime

We have already seen that free finite convolution is useful when studying the zeros of polynomials. An additional crucial advantage is that finite free convolutions tend to free convolution in the asymptotic regime when the degree
n\to\infty
.

The connection between the convolutions of polynomials and free probability (reason for the name of “finite free” convolutions) was first noticed by Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava in
[
39
]
when they used Voiculescu’s
R
- and
S
-transform to improve the bounds on the largest root of a convolution of two real-rooted polynomials. This connection was explored further in
[
38
]
, where Marcus defined a finite analog of the
R
- and
S
-transform that are related to Voiculescu’s transforms in the limit. Using finite free cumulants, Arizmendi and Perales
[
5
]
showed that in the asymptotic regime, a finite free additive convolution becomes a free additive convolution. This was later extended to the multiplicative convolution by Arizmendi, Garza-Vargas and Perales
[
4
]
.

Since the proofs of these facts in
[
5
,
4
]
are essentially algebraic, it turns out that they are applicable in a broader context of moment sequences instead of measures; see Remark
2.8
. More precisely, we need to consider the normalized zero-counting measures for a sequence of polynomials, real-rooted or not, under the only assumptions that their moments converge.

Given a polynomial
p
of degree
n
and roots
\lambda_{j}(p)
,
j=1,\dots,n
(not necessarily all distinct or real), its (normalized)
zero counting measure
(or
empirical root distribution
of
p
) is

(40)

\chi(p)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta_{\lambda_{j}(p)},

where
\delta_{z}
is the Dirac delta (unit mass) placed at the point
z
. The corresponding moments of
\chi(p)
(which we also call the moments of
p
, stretching the terminology a bit) are

m_{k}(p)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}^{k}(p)=\int x^{k}\,d\chi(p),\quad k=0,1,2,\dots

As mentioned above, the connection between finite and standard free probability is revealed in the asymptotic regime, when we let the degree
n\to\infty
. We say that the sequence of polynomials
\mathfrak{p}=\left(p_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that each
p_{n}
is real-rooted and of degree exactly
n

converges in moments
if all finite limits

(41)

m_{k}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{k}(p_{n}),\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots

exist. If the supports of
\chi(p_{n})
are contained in a compact set of the complex plane, then the sequence
\chi(p_{n})
is weakly compact and
m_{0},m_{1},\dots
are the moment of any of its accumulation points. In this case, (
41
) is equivalent to existence of a probability measure
\nu(\mathfrak{p})
, compactly supported on
\mathbb{C}
, with all its moments finite such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{k}(p_{n})=m_{k}(\nu(\mathfrak{p})),\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots

If we know additionally that
\chi(p_{n})
are on
\mathbb{R}
, with their supports contained in the same compact, and if the moment problem for
\nu(\mathfrak{p})
is determined, then (
41
) is equivalent to the weak-* convergence of the sequence
\chi(p_{n})
to
\nu(\mathfrak{p})
.

For real-rooted polynomials, the following proposition is a direct consequence of
[
5
, Corollary 5.5]
and
[
4
, Theorem 1.4]
:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(p_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathfrak{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(q_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
be two sequences of real-rooted polynomials as above, and let
\nu(\mathfrak{p})
and
\nu(\mathfrak{q})
be two compactly supported probability Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathfrak{p}
(respectively,
\mathfrak{q}
) converges in moments to
\nu(\mathfrak{p})
(respectively,
\nu(\mathfrak{q})
). Then

(i)

the sequence
\left(p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges in moments to
\nu(\mathfrak{p})\boxplus\nu(\mathfrak{q})
;

(ii)

if, additionally, for all sufficiently large
n
,
p_{n},q_{n}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
(or if
p_{n},q_{n}\subset\mathbb{P}_{n}(\mathbb{R}_{<0})
) then the sequence
\left(p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
converges in moments to
\nu(\mathfrak{p})\boxtimes\nu(\mathfrak{q})
.

In other words, in the case of real-rooted polynomials, as
n\to\infty
, we can replace the finite free convolution of polynomials by the standard free convolution of measures supported on
\mathbb{R}
. The motivation to consider only real-rooted polynomials is given by their application in free probability. However, the proof of Proposition
3.4
is basically algebraic, based on the notion of convergence in moments, and can be easily extended to more general cases, such as polynomials with uniformly bounded, but not necessarily real, roots.

Namely, we have the following result:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(p_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathfrak{q}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\left(q_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
be two sequences of polynomials, such that
\deg p_{n}=\deg q_{n}=n
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, and all zeros of both
\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathfrak{q}
are uniformly bounded.

If, additionally, all finite limits

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{k}(p_{n})=\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{k}(q_{n})=\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,

exist, then

(i)

all moments of the sequence of polynomials
\left(p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
have a finite limit,

\gamma_{k}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{k}(p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n})\in\mathbb{C}\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,

and the
R
-transform associated to the sequence
\left(\gamma_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{\infty}

satisfies

\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(z)=\mathcal{R}_{\alpha}(z)+\mathcal{R}_{\beta}(z).

(ii)

All moments of the sequence
\left(p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}
have a limit

\theta_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{k}(p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n})\in\mathbb{C}\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,

and the
S
-transform associated to the sequence
\left(\theta_{k}\right)_{k=1}^{\infty}

satisfies

\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(z)=\mathcal{S}_{\alpha}(z)\mathcal{S}_{\beta}(z).

Remark 3.6
.

For the notion of the
S
- and
R
-transforms associated to a sequence, see Remark
2.8
.

This theorem can be established following the arguments leading to Proposition
3.4
. As noted, the proofs of the results in
[
5
,
4
]
rely on the fact that the combinatorial structure behind the finite convolutions of polynomials tends (as
n\to\infty
) to the combinatorial formulas that relate the coefficients of the series in the
M
-,
R
-, and
S
-transforms of the limit sequence. This fact is established regardless of whether this sequence is in fact a moment sequence of a probability measure. When this is the case, the notions of the
R
- and other transforms for a sequence and for the underlying measure match, see, for instance,
[
46
, Lectures 16 and 18]
.

The reader interested in further details can check Appendix
A
.

3.3.
S-transform for a hypergeometric polynomial

In this section, we show that although the Cauchy transform of any limit of zero-counting measures of hypergeometric polynomials are, generally speaking, algebraic functions whose explicit expressions are not available, their
S
-transforms are straightforward rational functions.

Let us first discuss the case of
{}_{2}F_{1}
polynomials.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

p_{n}(x)={\ }_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n , \alpha_{n} }{\beta_n};x\right)},

be a sequence of hypergeometric polynomials such that the finite limits

\lim_{n}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{n}=A,\quad\lim_{n}\frac{\beta_{n}}{n}=B

exist. If additionally

(42)

A\notin[-1,0),\quad B\neq-1,\quad\text{and}\quad A\neq B,

then any weak-* limit
\mu
of the normalized zero-counting measures
\chi(p_{n})
is a positive probability measure compactly supported on
\mathbb{C}
, for which in a neighborhood of the origin,

(43)

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{z+A+1}{z+B+1}.

Proof.

Denote by
\mu_{n}=\chi(p_{n})
the probability zero-counting measure of
p_{n}
.

We use well-known identities expressing
{}_{2}F_{1}
polynomials in standard normalization in terms of Jacobi polynomials,

(44)

\displaystyle\left(\beta\right)^{\overline{n}}{\ }_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, \alpha}{\beta};x\right)}

\displaystyle=n!\,P^{(\beta-1,-n+\alpha-\beta)}_{n}(1-2x)=(-1)^{n}n!\,P^{(-n+\alpha-\beta,\beta-1)}_{n}(2x-1).

Jacobi polynomials also exhibit several transformation formulas in the cases when they can have multiple zeros (at
\pm 1
) or have a degree reduction, such as

P_{n}^{(-k-1,\beta)}(z)=\frac{\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\beta-k)}\frac{(n-k-1)!}{n!}\left(\frac{z-1}{2}\right)^{k+1}P_{n-k-1}^{(k+1,\beta)}(z),\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n},

and several more. For a more detailed discussion, see
[
53
, §4.22]
or
[
28
]
. From these identities it follows that the assumption
A\notin[-1,0)
is sufficient to guarantee that the zeros of
p_{n}
are uniformly bounded, while with
B\neq-1
, its zero-counting measures
\mu_{n}
do not collapse to
\delta_{0}
.

Thus, standard arguments on weak compactness of the sequence
\mu_{n}
show that under these assumptions, there exist
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}
and a unit measure
\mu\neq\delta_{0}
such that

\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{*}\mu,\quad n\in\Lambda.

As a consequence,
\mathcal{G}_{\chi(p_{n})}\to\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
,
n\in\Lambda
, in a neighborhood of infinity.

Polynomials
p_{n}
satisfy the ODE

z(1-z)p_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)+\left(\beta_{n}-(\alpha_{n}-n+1)z\right)p_{n}^{\prime}(z)+n\alpha_{n}p_{n}(z)=0.

Rewriting it in terms of
h_{n}=p_{n}^{\prime}/p_{n}
and noticing that
h_{n}/n\to G=\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
, we get an equation for
G
,

(45)

z(1-z)G^{2}(z)+\left(B-(A-1)z\right)G(z)+A=0.

Observe again that for
B\neq-1
,
G(z)\neq 1/z
.

With the notation
w=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)
, the second identity in (
34
) takes the form

(46)

G\left(\frac{z+1}{zw}\right)=zw.

Thus, making the change of variable
z\mapsto(z+1)/(wz)
in (
45
) and using the identity above, we arrive at

z(w(z+B+1)-(z+A+1))=0,

or

w=\frac{z+A+1}{z+B+1},

which proves (
43
).
∎

Remark 3.8
.

It is interesting to observe that expression (
43
) is meaningful even if we drop the assumptions (
42
). It is worth discussing then the possible consequences of relaxing these restrictions.

As we have seen in the proof, if
B=-1
, then
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)=1/z
, which happens, for instance, if
\mu=\delta_{0}
. If the zeros of
p_{n}
’s are real, it means that they asymptotically collapse to the origin.

The consequence of dropping the assumption
A\neq B
is also clear: if
A=B
, we have
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(z)=1/(z-1)
, which happens, for example, if
\mu=\delta_{1}
.

If
-1<A<0
, the explicit formula (
10
) shows that selecting the sequence
\alpha_{n}
appropriately, we can make the degree of
p_{n}
be of
\mathcal{O}(-An)
. This would mean that non-trivial part of the measures
\mu_{n}
“escapes” to infinity. Any possible weak-* limit could have a bounded support, but it will be of a total mass
|A|
. Notice that it would mean that for this
\mu
,
m_{0}=-A<1
, which is compatible with the algebraic equation (
45
).

Finally, if
A=-1
, the
S
-transform in (
43
) is still rational, but now it vanishes at
z=0
. In this case, one of the branch points of the Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
, solution of (
45
), is at infinity. This shows that if the limit measure
\mu
exists, it is a probability measure with unbounded support.

Now we formulate the general result of this section:

Theorem 3.9
.

For
s,t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
\bm{a}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
,
\bm{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
be such that finite limits

(47)

\lim_{n}\frac{\bm{a}_{n}}{n}=\bm{A}=(A_{1},\dots A_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{s},\quad\lim_{n}\frac{\bm{b}_{n}}{n}=\bm{B}=(B_{1},\dots,B_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{t}

exist.
Assume additionally that

(48)

A_{j}\notin[-1,0),\quad B_{k}\neq-1,\quad A_{j}\neq B_{k},\qquad j\in\{1,\dots,s\},\quad k\in\{1,\dots,t\}.

Then any weak-* limit
\mu
of the normalized zero-counting measures
\chi(p_{n})
of the sequence

(49)

p_{n}(x)={\ }_{s+1}F_{t}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n, \bm a_n}{\bm b_n };n^{t-s}x\right)}

is a positive probability measure compactly supported on
\mathbb{C}
, for which in a neighborhood of the origin,

(50)

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{s}(z+A_{i}+1)}{\prod_{j=1}^{t}(z+B_{j}+1)}.

Proof.

Consider the sequence

q_{n}(x)={\ }_{1}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n }{\beta_n};x\right)},

such that

\lim_{n}\frac{\beta_{n}}{n}=B.

Polynomials
q_{n}
are, in fact, Laguerre polynomials,

q_{n}(x)=\frac{n!}{\left(\beta_{n}\right)^{\overline{n}}}\,L_{n}^{(\beta_{n}-1)}(x).

We are interested in the sequence
\mu_{n}=\chi(\widehat{q}_{n})
of probability zero-counting measures for rescaled polynomials

\widehat{q}_{n}(x)={\ }_{1}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n }{\beta_n};nx\right)}.

It is well known that for each fixed
\beta
, the sequence of monic Laguerre polynomials
\left\{\widetilde{L}_{n}^{\left(\beta\right)}\right\},n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfies the three-term recurrence relation

\widetilde{L}_{n+1}^{\left(\beta\right)}(x)=\left(x-(2n+\beta+1)\right)\widetilde{L}_{n}^{\left(\beta\right)}(x)+n(n+\beta)\widetilde{L}_{n-1}^{\left(\beta\right)}(x).

In particular, the monic polynomials

n^{-n}\widetilde{L}_{n}^{(\beta-1)}(nx)

satisfy

n^{-n-1}L_{n+1}^{\left(\beta-1\right)}(nx)=\left(x-\frac{2n+\beta}{n}\right)n^{-n}L_{n}^{\left(\beta-1\right)}(nx)+\frac{n+\beta-1}{n}\left(n^{-n+1}L_{n-1}^{\left(\beta-1\right)}(nx)\right).

Writing these relations as an eigenvalue problem for a Jacobi matrix and applying Gershgorin’s theorem, it is easy to prove that under the assumption that the finite limit
B
of
\beta_{n}/n
exists, the zeros of
\widehat{q}_{n}
are uniformly bounded. Moreover, the well-known identity

L_{n}^{(-k)}(x)=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}(-x)^{k}L_{n-k}^{(k)}(x),\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n},

see e.g.
[
50
, Section 18.5]
or
[
41
, Section 2.3]
, indicates that
\mu_{n}
’s do not converge to
\delta_{0}
, unless
B\neq-1
. This is indeed the case; see the discussion in
[
40
]
or
[
29
]
.

Polynomials
q_{n}
are solution of Kummer’s differential

zq_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)+(\beta_{n}-z)q_{n}^{\prime}(z)+nq_{n}(z)=0,

see, e.g.
[
27
, Chapter 7]
. Hence,
\widehat{q}_{n}

satisfy

(51)

z\widehat{q}_{n}^{\prime\prime}(z)+(\beta_{n}-nz)\widehat{q}_{n}^{\prime}(z)+n^{2}\widehat{q}_{n}(z)=0.

As before, by weak compactness there exist
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{N}
and a unit measure
\mu
such that

\mu_{n}\xrightarrow{*}\mu,\quad n\in\Lambda.

As a consequence,
\mathcal{G}_{\chi(q_{n})}\to\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
,
n\in\Lambda
, in a neighborhood of infinity. Rewriting (
51
) in terms of
h_{n}=\widehat{q}_{n}^{\prime}/\widehat{q}_{n}
and using that
h_{n}/n\to G=\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
, we get an equation for
G
,

zG^{2}(z)+\left(B-z\right)G(z)+1=0

(notice again that for
B\neq-1
,
G(x)\neq 1/x
). Now we proceed as in the proof of Proposition
3.7
: making the change of variable
z\mapsto(z+1)/(wz)
in this quadratic equation on
G
and using (
46
), we get for
w=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)
the equation

z\left(w(z+B+1)-1\right)=0,

and we conclude that

(52)

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{1}{z+B+1}.

Same arguments, or using identity (

4.
Type I Multiple orthogonal polynomials

Recall that Type I multiple orthogonal polynomials are defined by the orthogonality and normalization conditions (
1
)–(
3
). The potential-theoretic techniques allow to describe the asymptotics of the zeros of function
Q_{\bm{n}}
in (
1
). The recently obtained expressions for the actual type I polynomials allow us to perform this analysis for polynomial coefficients of
Q_{\bm{n}}
.

4.1.
Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials

The AT-system of Jacobi-Piñeiro weights was introduced in (
5
): for
\beta>-1
,
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
,
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
such that, without loss of generality,

(57)

\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{r}>-1\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for }i\not=j,

the Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials of Type I,
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
,
\deg P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\leq n_{j}-1
,
i=1,\dots,r
, are given by the orthogonality conditions on the function

(58)

Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}(x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{j=1}^{r}P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}(x)w_{j}(x),\quad w_{j}(x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}x^{\alpha_{j}}(1-x)^{\beta},

namely,

(59)

\int_{0}^{1}Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}(x)x^{k}\,dx=0,\qquad 0\leq k\leq|\bm{n}|-2,

together with the normalization

\int_{0}^{1}Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}(x)x^{|\bm{n}|-1}\,dx=1.

Recently, it was shown that these are hypergeometric polynomials. In order to write the explicit expression, we borrow notation from
[
9
]
: for
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
, let
\bm{a}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r-1}
stand for the vector obtained from
\bm{a}
by deleting its
i
-th entry; also, in a slight abuse of notation, we also understand that adding a scalar to a multi-index means adding this scalar to each one of its components. Then, see
[
9
]
,

(60)

P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}(x)=c^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}{\ }{}_{r+1}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1,\alpha_i+\beta+|\bm{n}|,\alpha_i+1-\bm\alpha^{(i)}-\bm{n}^{(i)}}{\alpha_i+1,\alpha_i+1-\bm\alpha^{(i)}};x\right)},

where

c^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}(-1)^{\left|\bm{n}\right|-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|)\prod_{k=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{k}+\beta+|\bm{n}|\right)^{\overline{n_{k}}}}{(n_{i}-1)!\,\Gamma(\beta+|\bm{n}|)\Gamma(\alpha_{i}+1)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq r\\
k\neq i\end{subarray}}\left(\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{i}\right)^{\overline{n_{k}}}}

is just a normalizing constant that does not affect the zeros of the polynomial. For
r=2
, this formula was obtained first in
[
8
]
.

Using this expression and Theorem
A
, we get the following representation for polynomials (
59
) for
i=1,\dots,r
:

(61)

P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}=c^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}p_{i,1}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{i,2}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}\cdots\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{i,r},

where

(62)

p_{i,j}(x)=\begin{cases}{\ }{}_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1,\alpha_i-\alpha_j-n_{j}+1}{\alpha_i-\alpha_{j}+1};x\right)},&j\in\{1,\cdots,r\}\setminus\{i\},\\[11.38109pt]
{\ }{}_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1,\alpha_i+\beta+|\bm{n}|}{\alpha_i+1};x\right)},&j=i.\end{cases}

4.1.1.
Real zeros: monotonicity and interlacing

Since the Jacobi-Piñeiro weights with
\bm{\alpha}
satisfying (
57
) form an AT-system, it is known that the corresponding function
Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}
in (
58
) has
|\bm{n}|-1
zeros on
(0,1)
for every multi-index
\bm{n}
, see, e.g.
[
54
, Theorem 2.3]
. Although the potential-theoretic description of the asymptotics of
Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}
is relatively well studied, the behavior of the zeros of the Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
, is much less known.

Let us denote the open intervals
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{r}
, with

(63)

\Delta_{1}=(0,1),\quad\Delta_{k}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}_{<0},&\text{if $2\leq k\leq r$ is even,}\\
\mathbb{R}_{>0},&\text{if $2\leq k\leq r$ is odd.}\end{cases}

A simple calculation shows that for
-1<\alpha<0
,

(64)

\int_{\Delta_{k}}\frac{|t|^{\alpha}}{t-x}dt=(-1)^{k}\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\alpha)}\,|x|^{\alpha},\quad x\in\Delta_{k-1},\quad k\geq 2.

Let

s_{k}(x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}|x|^{\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k-1}},\quad x\in\Delta_{k-1},\quad 2\leq k\leq r.

If together with (
57
) we assume that

(65)

\alpha_{1}-1<\alpha_{r}=\min_{1\leq k\leq r}\alpha_{k},

then by (
64
), each
s_{k}
is equal, up to a constant factor, to its Cauchy transform on
\Delta_{k}
:

s_{k}(x)=\text{const}_{k}\int_{\Delta_{k}}\frac{|t|^{\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k-1}}}{t-x}\,dt,\quad x\in\Delta_{k-1},\quad 2\leq k\leq r,

which means that the weights
w_{j}
in (
58
) form a Nikishin system. By
[
37
]
, under the additional constraint that if the sequence of multi-indices
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
is such that

\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq r}n_{k}\right)-\left(\min_{1\leq k\leq r}n_{k}\right)

is uniformly bounded, then the number of zeros of each
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
,
i=1,\dots,r
, outside
\overline{\Delta}_{r}
is also uniformly bounded, and the zeros accumulate on
\overline{\Delta}_{r}\cup\{\infty\}
as
|\bm{n}|\to\infty
.

If we drop the restriction (
65
), the weights
w_{i}
still form a generalized Nikishin system, with

w_{j+1}(x)=x^{m_{j}}s_{j}(x)w_{j}(x),\quad j=1,\dots,r-1,

for some
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Such a situation is considered in
[
36
]
; the results therein are not immediately applicable due to the assumptions the authors impose on the polynomial factors. However, we conjecture that the zeros of the Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
accumulate on
\overline{\Delta}_{r}\cup\{\infty\}
as
|\bm{n}|\to\infty
under very mild assumptions on the sequence
\bm{n}
.

Using the approach that we have already described in
[
41
]
, we can give some additional sufficient conditions for real-rootedness of Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials, as well as deduce the interlacing and monotonicity of zeros of polynomials with respect to the parameters.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\beta>-1
and
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
satisfy (
57
). Assume that for a multi-index
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and for a specific index
i\in\{1,2,\dots,r\}
,

(66)

\alpha_{1}-1<\alpha_{i}<\min_{1\leq j\leq r}\{\alpha_{j}+n_{j}\}-n_{i}+1.

Then
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\in\mathbb{P}_{n_{i}-1}(\Delta_{r})
.

Additionally, for
0<t\leq 2
,

(i)

P^{(\bm{\alpha}+t;\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\preccurlyeq P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
and
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\preccurlyeq P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta+t)}_{\bm{n},i}
, if
r
is even,

(ii)

P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\preccurlyeq P^{(\bm{\alpha}+t;\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
and
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta+t)}_{\bm{n},i}\preccurlyeq P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
, if
r
is odd.

Observe that if condition (
66
) is satisfied for certain multi-indices
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, it is also satisfied for
\bm{\alpha}+t
, with
t>0
.

Proof.

Let us show first that

p_{i,i}\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})\quad\text{and}\quad p_{i,j}(x)\in\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}_{<0}),\quad j\in\{1,\dots,r\}\backslash\{i\}.

We can establish it using some results from
[
13
]
, many of them re-proved in
[
41
]
.
Indeed, since by assumptions,
\alpha_{i},\beta>-1
,
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, and thus,

\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|>\alpha_{i}+n_{i}-1,

we have by
[
13
, Theorem 1,
(ii)
]
that
p_{i,i}\in\mathbb{P}_{n_{i}-1}((0,1))
.

Similarly, by (
57
) and (
66
), for every
j\in\{1,\dots,r\}\backslash\{i\}
,

\alpha_{j}-1<\alpha_{i}<\alpha_{j}+n_{j}-n_{i}+1,

or equivalently,

-n_{i}+2>\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}-n_{j}+1,\quad\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}+1>0.

It remains to use
[
13
, Theorem 1,
(i)
]
to conclude that
p_{i,j}\in\mathbb{P}_{n_{i}-1}(\mathbb{R}_{<0})
.
By Proposition
2.4
, for

(67)

q_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=p_{i,1}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}\cdots\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{i,i-1}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{i,i+1}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}\cdots\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{{i},r},

we have that

(68)

q_{i}\in\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})&\text{for $r$ odd,}\\
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{R}_{<0})&\text{for $r$ even.}\end{cases}

Since the decomposition (
61
) of
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
can be written as

(69)

P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}=c^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}p_{i,i}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}q_{i},

it remain to Proposition
2.4
again to establish the first assertion of the theorem (about the location of the zeros).

A key observation to prove interlacing is that polynomials
p_{i,j}
in (
62
), with
j\neq i
, are independent of the parameter
\beta>-1
, and invariant with respect to the scalar shift
\bm{\alpha}\mapsto\bm{\alpha}+t
,
t>0
. Thus, the same property is shared by the polynomial
q_{i}
in (
67
).

On the other hand, by
[
41
, Eq. (61)]
, for
0\leq t_{1},t_{2}\leq 2
and
t_{1}+t_{2}\not=0
.

(70)

{\ }{}_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_{i}+1,\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|+t_1}{\alpha_{i}+1};x\right)}\prec{\ }_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_{i}+1,\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|+t_2}{\alpha_{i}+1+t_2};x\right)}.

For
r

) and Proposition
2.6
, we get that

(71)

\begin{split}{\ }{}_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_{i}+1,\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|+t_1}{\alpha_{i}+1};x\right)}&\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}q_{i}\\

while for
r

, we obtain

(72)

\begin{split}{\ }{}_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_{i}+1,\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|+t_2}{\alpha_{i}+1+t_2};x\right)}&\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}q_{i}\\

In particular, setting in (

)-(

),

and

, and noticing that a multiplicative constant does not affect interlacing, we conclude that

P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\preccurlyeq P^{(\bm{\alpha}+t;\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\quad\text{for }r\text{ even}\quad\text{and}\quad P^{(\bm{\alpha}+t;\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\preccurlyeq P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\quad\text{for }r\text{ odd},

while taking

Remark 4.2

The statement about zero interlacing contained in Theorem

Notice that additionally to

\frac{d}{dx}\left(P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}(x)\right)\simeq P^{(\bm{\alpha}+\bm{e}_{i};\beta+1)}_{\bm{n}-\bm{e}_{i},i}(x).

If we try to enforce the assumptions (

) to be valid for every index

, we obtain the following simple consequence of Theorem

:

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\beta>-1
,
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})

). Then

\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}

Proof.

Fix

On the other hand, for any index

4.1.2.
Zero asymptotics of Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials of Type I

We can describe the weak zero asymptotics of a sequence of Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials,

p_{\bm{n},i}=P^{(\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{n}};\beta_{\bm{n}})}_{\bm{n},i},\quad i=1,\dots,r,

where
\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{n}}=\left(\alpha_{1}^{(\bm{n})},\dots,\alpha_{r}^{(\bm{n})}\right)
,
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, under assumption

(75)

\lim_{|\bm{n}|\to\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{(\bm{n})}}{|\bm{n}|}=A_{i}\geq 0,\quad\lim_{|\bm{n}|\to\infty}\frac{\beta_{(\bm{n})}}{|\bm{n}|}=B\geq 0,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{|\bm{n}|\to\infty}\frac{n_{i}}{|\bm{n}|}=\theta_{i}>0,\qquad i=1,\dots,r.

Clearly,
\theta_{1}+\dots+\theta_{r}=1
. Formula (
60
) indicates that we need to consider the parameters

(76)

\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\begin{cases}(A_{i}+B+1)/\theta_{i},&\text{if }j=i,\\
(A_{i}-A_{j}-\theta_{j})/\theta_{i}&\text{if }j\neq i,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{(i)}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\begin{cases}A_{i}/\theta_{i},&\text{if }k=i,\\
(A_{i}-A_{k})/\theta_{i}&\text{if }k\neq i.\end{cases}

Restrictions
\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}\notin[-1,0)
and
\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{(i)}\neq-1
imply that additionally to (
75
) we need to assume that

(77)

A_{j}\neq A_{i}+\theta_{i},\quad A_{j}+\theta_{j}\notin(A_{i},A_{i}+\theta_{i}],\qquad j\in\{1,\dots,r\}\setminus\{i\}.

Notice that the assumption that
\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}\neq\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{(i)}
holds automatically.

By Theorem
3.9
, for any weak-* accumulation point
\mu
of the normalized zero-counting measures
\chi(p_{\bm{n},i})
,

(78)

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\frac{z+\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}+1}{z+\mathfrak{b}_{j}^{(i)}+1},

and by Theorem
3.11
, its Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(u)
in a neighborhood of infinity is an algebraic function
y/u
, where
y=y(u)
satisfies the equation

y\prod_{j=1}^{r}(y+\mathfrak{b}_{j}^{(i)})=u(y-1)\prod_{i=1}^{r}(y+\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}).

Let us discuss the case when
\bm{\alpha}
and
\beta
do not depend on
\bm{n}
, so that

(79)

\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\begin{cases}1/\theta_{i},&\text{if }j=i,\\
-\theta_{j}/\theta_{i}&\text{if }j\neq i,\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{b}_{j}^{(i)}=0,

and constrains (
77
) boil down to

(80)

\theta_{j}\notin(0,\theta_{i}],\qquad j\in\{1,\dots,r\}\setminus\{i\}.

If we assume that there exists an index
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
such that

\theta_{i}<\min\left\{\theta_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,j\in\{1,\dots,r\}\setminus\{i\}\right\},

then for all sufficiently large values of
|\bm{n}|
, the inequality in the right-hand side of (
66
) holds. Thus, imposing additionally that
|\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}|<1
for all
j
’s, we conclude that any weak-* accumulation point
\mu
of the normalized zero-counting measures
\chi(p_{\bm{n},i})
is a compactly supported probability measure on
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, if
r
is odd, and on
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
, otherwise, and such that

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{z+1+1/\theta_{i}}{(z+1)^{r}}\prod_{j\neq i}(z+1-\theta_{j}/\theta_{i}),

while
y=u\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(u)
is a solution of

y^{r+1}=u\left(y+\frac{1}{\theta_{i}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(y-\frac{\theta_{j}}{\theta_{i}}\right).

In the asymptotically diagonal situation, when all
\theta_{j}=1/r
, we have
\mathfrak{a}_{j}^{(i)}=-1
if
j\neq i
, which is one of the degenerate cases discussed in Remark
3.8
. However, the expression for the
S
-transform of the limit measure is still valid:

\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)=\frac{z^{r-1}}{(z+1)^{r}}(z+1+1/r),

as well as for the Cauchy transform
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}(u)=y/u
, where
y=y(u)
solves

(81)

y^{r+1}=u(y-1)^{r}\left(y+r\right).

We conjecture that, in this case, the support of any limit measure
\mu
is unbounded.
If the parameters
\bm{\alpha}
satisfy (
66
), it follows from Theorem
4.1
that all zeros of the corresponding polynomial
P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}
are on
\Delta_{r}
. Furthermore, as follows from the method of proof of

66
) is not satisfied, the zeros still accumulate on
\Delta_{r}\cup\{\infty\}
.

4.2.
Type I Multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind

Let
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r})
be a multi-index satisfying conditions (
57
). Then for
\bm{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, the type I multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind,
L_{\bm{n},i}^{(\bm{\alpha})}
,
\deg L_{\bm{n},i}^{(\bm{\alpha})}\leq n_{j}-1
,
i=1,\dots,r
, are given by the orthogonality conditions on the function

(85)

Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha})}(x)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{j=1}^{r}L_{\bm{n},i}^{(\bm{\alpha})}(x)\,x^{\alpha_{j}}e^{-x},

namely,

(86)

\int_{0}^{+\infty}Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha})}(x)x^{k}\,dx=0,\qquad 0\leq k\leq|\bm{n}|-2,

together with the normalization

\int_{0}^{+\infty}Q_{\bm{n}}^{(\bm{\alpha})}(x)x^{|\bm{n}|-1}\,dx=1.

In
[
9
]
, it was shown that these are hypergeometric polynomials: using the notation introduced before (
60
),

(87)

L_{\bm{n},i}^{(\bm{\alpha})}(x)=c_{\bm{n},i}^{(\bm{\alpha})}{\ }{}_{r}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1,\alpha_i+1-\bm\alpha^{(i)}-\bm{n}^{(i)}}{\alpha_i+1,\alpha_i+1-\bm\alpha^{(i)}};x\right)}

where

c_{\bm{n},i}^{(\bm{\alpha})}=(-1)^{|\bm{n}|-1}\left((n_{i}-1)!\Gamma(\alpha_{i}+1)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq r\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}\left(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i}\right)^{\overline{n_{j}}}\right)^{-1}.

For
r=2
, this formula was previously derived in
[
8
]
).

Using this expression and Theorem
A
, we get the following representation for polynomials (
87
) for
i=1,\dots,r
:

(88)

L^{(\bm{\alpha})}_{\bm{n},i}=c^{(\bm{\alpha})}_{\bm{n},i}p_{i,1}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{i,2}\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}\cdots\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}p_{i,r},

where

(89)

p_{i,j}(x)=\begin{cases}{\ }{}_{2}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1,\alpha_i-\alpha_j-n_{j}+1}{\alpha_i-\alpha_{j}+1};x\right)},&j\in\{1,\cdots,r\}\setminus\{i\},\\[11.38109pt]
{\ }{}_{1}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1}{\alpha_i+1};x\right)},&j=i.\end{cases}

Notice that for
i\neq j
the
p_{i,j}
are exactly the same polynomials appearing in (
62
), thus we have the relation

(90)

L^{(\bm{\alpha})}_{\bm{n},i}\simeq\left(v_{i}(x)\boxtimes_{n_{i}-1}P^{(\bm{\alpha};\beta)}_{\bm{n},i}\right)

with

(91)

v_{i}(x)={\ }_{1}F_{1}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-n_i+1}{\alpha_i+\beta+|\bm n|};x\right)}=\frac{(n_{i}-1)!}{\left(\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|\right)^{\overline{n_{i}-1}}}\,L_{n_{i}-1}^{(\alpha_{i}+\beta+|\bm{n}|-1)}(x),

where
L_{n}^{(\alpha)}
is the Laguerre polynomial. In other words, the Type I multiple Laguerre polynomial of the first kind can be obtained as a finite multiplicative convolution of the Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials with the same parameters and a standard Laguerre polynomial.

5.
Type II Multiple orthogonal polynomials

Recall that Type II multiple orthogonal polynomial
P_{\bm{n}}
are monic polynomial of degree
|\bm{n}|
that satisfy

\int P_{\bm{n}}(x)x^{k}w_{j}(x)\,dx=0,\qquad 0\leq k\leq n_{j}-1,\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

These polynomials are better studied than their Type I counterparts. In particular, it is known that their asymptotic zero distribution can be described via a solution of a vector equilibrium problem. Now we can perform this analysis using our free probability tools.

5.1.

We consider again the AT-system of Jacobi-Piñeiro weights on

as

,

\left(1-\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa\,u}{1-u}}\right)^{1/3}_{+}\bigg|_{u=x}=\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa\,x}{1-x}}-1\right)^{1/3}e^{-\pi i/3}\text{ for }x\in(0,1).

Since

f_{+}(x)=\sqrt[3]{\frac{-\nu x}{2(1-x)}}\,e^{-\pi i/3},\quad x\in(0,1),

we get that on
(0,1)

y_{+}(x)=\sqrt[3]{\frac{-\nu x}{2(1-x)}}\,e^{-\pi i/3}\left(\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa\,x}{1-x}}+1\right)^{1/3}+\left(\sqrt{1+\frac{\kappa\,x}{1-x}}-1\right)^{1/3}e^{-\pi i/3}\right).

Putting all together, we conclude that

is supported on
[0,1]
, with

(119)

\begin{split}\mu^{\prime}(x)&=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}\,\sqrt[3]{\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{2}}\,\,\frac{\left(\sqrt{1+(\kappa-1)x}+\sqrt{1-x}\right)^{1/3}+\left(\sqrt{1+(\kappa-1)x}-\sqrt{1-x}\right)^{1/3}}{x^{2/3}\sqrt{1-x}}.\end{split}

Symbolic integration allows us to check that

is the density of a positive probability measure on
[0,1]
, and that

\mu^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{3}\sqrt[3]{\theta(1-\theta))}}{2\pi\,x^{2/3}}(1+o(1))\text{ as }x\to 0+,\quad\text{and}\quad\mu^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1-\theta(1-\theta)}}{\pi\sqrt{1-x}}(1+o(1))\text{ as }x\to 1-.

5.2.
Type II Multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind

For
\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, the Type II Multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind,
L_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
, corresponding to the weights (
6
), are polynomial of degree
|\bm{n}|-1
, satisfying

\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(x)x^{\alpha_{j}}e^{-x}x^{k}dx=0,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n_{j}-1,\quad j=1,\dots,r,

where parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r}>-1
are such that
\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}\notin\mathbb{Z}
whenever
i\neq j
. These are hypergeometric polynomials (see
[
24
, §23.4.2]
):

(123)

\begin{split}(-1)^{|\bm{n}|}e^{-x}L_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(\alpha_{j}+1\right)^{\overline{n_{j}}}\,{\ }_{r}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\bm\alpha+\bm n +1}{\bm\alpha+1};-x\right)}.\end{split}

Notice that in this identity, neither the left-hand nor the right-hand side is a polynomial. However, the polynomials
L_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
do not vanish at the origin, and we can write their reciprocal as a finite convolution:

Proposition 5.6
.

For the reciprocal
p^{*}
of the Type II Multiple Laguerre polynomials of the first kind
p(x)=L_{\bm{n}}^{\bm{\alpha}}
,

(124)

p^{*}\simeq{\ }_{2}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|,1}{ \cdot};x\right)}\boxtimes_{|\bm{n}|}{\ }{}_{r+1}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|,\ -|\bm n|-\bm\alpha}{-|\bm n|-\bm n-\bm\alpha};x+1\right)}.

Proof.

Since

e^{x}={\ }_{0}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\cdot}{\cdot};x\right)},

we can rewrite (
123
) as

p(x)\simeq{\ }_{0}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\cdot}{\cdot};x\right)}{\ }_{r}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{\bm\alpha+\bm n +1}{\bm\alpha+1};-x\right)}.

Applying the Lemma
2.3
we get that

p^{*}\simeq{\ }_{2}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|,1}{\cdot};x\right)}\boxtimes_{|\bm{n}|}\left({\ }{}_{1}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|}{\cdot};-x\right)}\boxplus_{|\bm{n}|}{\ }{}_{r+1}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|,\ -|\bm n|-\bm\alpha}{-|\bm n|-\bm n-\bm\alpha};x\right)}\right).

On the other hand,

{\ }{}_{1}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|}{\cdot};-x\right)}\simeq(x+1)^{|\bm{n}|},

and from (
22
) we get (
124
).
∎

5.2.2.

5.6
to describe the asymptotic zero distribution of the rescaled polynomials

,

Theorem 5.8
.

In the particular case of

independent on
\bm{n}

(127)

Proof.

If

then

\left(L^{(\bm{\alpha}_{\bm{n}})}_{\bm{n}}(|\bm{n}|\,x)\right)^{*}\simeq p^{*}_{\bm{n}}(x/|\bm{n}|).

By (
124

(128)

p^{*}(x/|\bm{n}|)\simeq{\ }_{2}F_{0}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|,1}{\cdot};x/|\bm{n}|\right)}\boxtimes_{|\bm{n}|}{\ }{}_{r+1}F_{r}{\left(\genfrac{.}{.}{0.0pt}{}{-|\bm n|,\ -|\bm n|-\bm\alpha_{\bm n}}{-|\bm n|-\bm n-\bm\alpha_{\bm n}};x+1\right)}.

By Corollary
3.12

-transform

\widetilde{\mu}

polynomial on the right-hand side satisfies the equation

Taking into account (
53
) and the representation (
128
), we see that the
S
-transform

of the normalized zero-counting measure of reversed scaled polynomials

is

Thus, making the change of variables
\widetilde{w}=w/(z+1)
in (

), we get an equation for
w=\mathcal{S}_{\mu^{*}}(z)
:

(130)

w\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(zw+(z+1)\left(z-A_{i}-\theta_{i}\right)\right)=\left(w+z+1\right)\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(zw+(z+1)\left(z-A_{i}\right)\right).

In particular, the equation for the shifted
S
-transform

w\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(-(z+1)w+z\left(z+1+A_{i}+\theta_{i}\right)\right)=\left(w-z\right)\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(-(z+1)w+z\left(z+1+A_{i}\right)\right).

By (
36
), the
S
-transform
w=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(z)
of the normalized zero-counting measure of the scaled polynomials

By (
33
), the equation for
u=\mathcal{M}_{\mu}^{-1}(z)
is

\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(\left(z+1+A_{i}+\theta_{i}\right)u-1\right)=\left(1-(z+1)u\right)\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(\left(z+1+A_{i}\right)u-1\right).

\mathcal{G}_{\mu}

with

Appendix A
Outline of the proof of Theorem
3.5

Theorem
3.5
is a key result for some of our applications and it was implicitly proved in
[
5
,
4
]
. The connection between the results proved in those papers and the theorem written here uses the established theory of combinatorics in free probability that can be consulted in
[
46
]
. The purpose of this section is to further clarify this connection.

First, recall from
[
5
, Remark 3.5]
that given a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
one can define its degree
n
finite free cumulants
(\kappa_{j}^{(n)}(p))_{j=1}^{n}
as the values uniquely determined by the formulas:

e_{j}(p)=\frac{\left(n\right)^{\underline{j}}}{d^{j}j!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}(k)}n^{|\pi|}\mu(0_{j},\pi)\kappa^{(n)}_{\pi}(p).

where
\mathcal{P}(j)
is family of set partitions of
\{1,\dots,k\}
,
\mu
is the Möbius function on the lattice
\mathcal{P}(k)
(with the reversed refinement order),
|\pi|
is the number of blocks in the partition
\pi
, and
\kappa^{(n)}_{\pi}(p)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{V\in\pi}\kappa^{(n)}_{|V|}(p)
. Finite free cumulants have the remarkable property that in the limit they tend to free cumulants, see
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
, since the proof only relies in the combinatorial structure, the result can be readily updated as follows:

Let
(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of polynomials such that
p_{n}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
for
n\geq 0
. And let
(r_{j})_{j\geq 1}
,
(m_{j})_{j\geq 1}
two sequences of complex numbers that satisfy the formulas:

(154)

m_{k}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(k)}r_{\pi},\qquad\text{for }k=1,2,\dots,

where
\mathcal{NC}(k)
is family of non-crossing set partitions of
\{1,\dots,k\}
, and
r_{\pi}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\prod_{V\in\pi}r_{|V|}
.
Then, we have the equivalence:

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{j}(p_{n})=m_{j},\quad\text{for }j=1,2,\dots\qquad\text{ if and only if }\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{j}^{(n)}(p_{n})=r_{j}\quad\text{for }j=1,2,\dots

Notice that the only difference with
[
5
, Theorem 5.4]
is that we do not require that
(m_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is the sequence of moments of some measure. If this was the case, then
(r_{j})_{j\geq 1}
would be the sequence of free cumulants of the given measure.

Moreover, from
[
5
, Proposition 3.6]
we know that finite free cumulants linearize the finite free additive convolution:

\kappa^{(n)}_{j}(p\boxplus_{n}q)=\kappa^{(n)}_{j}(p)+\kappa^{(n)}_{j}(q)\qquad\text{for }j=1,\dots,n.

Furthermore,
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
asserts that finite free cumulants of the multiplicative convolution satisfy the same relation as the free cumulants of a product in the limit:

\kappa^{(n)}_{j}(p\boxtimes_{n}q)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(j)}\kappa^{(n)}_{\pi}(\mathfrak{p})\kappa^{(n)}_{Kr(\pi)}(\mathfrak{q})+O(1/n)\qquad\text{for }j=1,2,\dots,

where,
Kr(\pi)
denotes the Kreweras complement of a non-crossing partition and
O(1/n)
is a term that tends to 0 when
n\to\infty
. When turning to the limiting behaviour, the previous results imply the following:

Let
\mathfrak{p}=(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,
\mathfrak{q}=(p_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of polynomials such that
p_{n},q_{n}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}
for
n\geq 0
. And assume that
for all
j=1,2,\dots
it holds that

\alpha_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{j}(p_{n})\in\mathbb{C},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{j}(q_{n})\in\mathbb{C}.

Then for all
j=1,2,\dots
one has that

\gamma_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{j}(p_{n}\boxplus_{n}q_{n})\in\mathbb{C}\qquad\text{and,}

\theta_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{j}(p_{n}\boxtimes_{n}q_{n})\in\mathbb{C}.

Furthermore, the sequences
(\gamma_{j})_{j\geq 1}
and
(\theta_{j})_{j\geq 1}
can be computed as follows. For
\mu=\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\theta
we let
(r_{j}(\mu))_{j\geq 1}
be the sequences such that:

(155)

\mu_{k}=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(k)}r_{\pi}(\mu),\qquad\text{for }k=1,2,\dots.

Then

(156)

\displaystyle r_{j}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=r_{j}(\alpha)+r_{j}(\beta)\qquad\text{ for }j=1,2,\dots,\text{ and }

(157)

\displaystyle r_{j}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{NC}(j)}r_{\pi}(\alpha)r_{K(\pi)}(\beta)\qquad\text{for }j=1,2,\dots,r.

It is a well-known fact that the previous relations between sequences are equivalent to the formal power series relations between the R-transform, S-transform and Cauchy transform. More specifically,

3.5
follows from the following facts:

•

155

are the coefficients of the

\mathcal{R}_{\mu}

•

•
