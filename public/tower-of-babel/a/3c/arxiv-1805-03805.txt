A Theory of Elementary Higher Toposes
Nima Rasekh

Abstract
. We define an elementary ∞ \infty -topos that simultaneously generalizes an elementary topos and Grothendieck ∞ \infty -topos. We then prove it satisfies the expected topos theoretic properties, such as descent, local Cartesian closure, locality and classification of univalent morphisms, generalizing results by Lurie [ Lur09 ] and Gepner-Kock [ GK17 ] . We also define ∞ \infty -logical functors and show the resulting ∞ \infty -category is closed under limits and filtered colimits, generalizing the analogous result for elementary toposes and Grothendieck ∞ \infty -toposes. Moreover, we give an alternative characterization of elementary ∞ \infty -toposes and their ∞ \infty -logical functors via their ind-completions. Finally we generalize these results by discussing the case of elementary ( n , 1 ) (n,1) -toposes and give various examples and non-examples.

Introduction

0.1.
Set Theory as a Foundation for Mathematics

Since the work of Cantor in the late 19th century
[
Can74
]
, set theory has established itself as a standard foundation for broad swaths of modern mathematics. As a result it is now fairly standard that in order to define a mathematical object, such as group, variety, manifold, or …, one starts with a set and then adds the desired list of conditions. Having this common foundation allows mathematicians all over the world to effectively communicate ideas and introduce new objects.

The fact that set theory can now be comfortably used to do mathematics follows a century long development using various mathematical foundations. One such approach is axiomatic set theory first developed by Frege
[
Fre93
]
, Zermelo
[
Zer08
]
and Fraenkel
[
Fra22
]
, leading to the now famous
Zermelo-Fraenkel
(ZF) set theory. Another approach arose in the branch of categorical logic and originated with work of Lawvere
[
Law64
]
and Tierney
[
Tie73
]
, leading to a foundation via
elementary toposes
. Another, third approach, comes from
type theory
, which has arose in the work of Russel
[
Rus08
]
, as a way to avoid paradoxes in early versions of axiomatic set theory and is particularly suitable in constructive and computational mathematics
[
Chu40
,
ML75
]
. Moreover, there are ways to translate between these foundations: Every (higher-order) type theory gives us an elementary topos and vice versa
[
LS88
]
. Moreover, we can construct set theories both out of elementary topos theories
[
MLM94
, Subsection VI.10]
and type theories
[
Acz78
]
.

The key aspect of set theory as a foundation for mathematics is not what a set
is
, but rather how it
behaves
in the sense that we want to know which axioms our chosen foundation satisfies. The development above allows mathematicians of all stripes to choose the axioms that fits their needs to develop their mathematical theory, which might or might not include well-known axioms such as the
axiom of choice
, the
continuum hypothesis
, the
law of excluded middle
, and many other axioms, being assured of soundness of the theory without having to necessary grapple with the underlying foundation and which one of the three is used to construct a specific model.

0.2.
Homotopical Mathematics

Homotopical mathematics has its starting point in the work of Poincaré, who recognized that we can distinguish two topological spaces by analyzing homotopy classes of based loops, introducing the first homotopy invariant
[
Poi95
]
. This led to further invariants, such as
higher homotopy groups

[
Čech32
]
and
homology
and
cohomology theories

[
ES45
]
.

The development of these invariants motivated the introduction of up-to-homotopy structures, such
A_{\infty}
-spaces
, which are monoids in spaces where the composition and associativity only hold up to a choice of coherent homotopies, a prominent example being the based loop space
[
Sta63
]
. This example and many other illustrate why set theoretical foundations are not suitable for the study of homotopical mathematics, as we would rather like to use a framework in which the loop space is directly a monoid (in the sense that the structure of a monoid is itself only defined up to coherent homotopies). This vision spurred a journey, which led to the development of various notions of
homotopical
or
weak categories
, such as
quasi-categories

[
BV73
]
,
complete Segal spaces

[
Rez01
]
,
Kan enriched categories

[
Ber07a
]
and others, which are all now considered examples of the theory of
(\infty,1)
-categories
(or simply
\infty
-categories
)
[
Ber10
]
, the standard setting for homotopy coherent mathematics.

Similar to the previous subsection homotopical mathematical requires its own basic building block that can be used to characterize different objects of interest and we could analogously call a
theory of spaces

1

To avoid ambiguity there have also been suggestions to call it
animated set theory

[

]
. Again the key value is to determine how a space behaves rather than what a space is. Unlike the set-theoretical setting this foundation has not been fully established yet, however, we are currently witnessing its development. Ideally we would expect that the three foundations outlines above, namely axiomatic set theory, elementary topos theory and type theory, have a homotopy invariant analogues that also interact with each other in a similar manner.

A first step important step towards realizing this vision is a homotopy invariant analogue of type theory, known as
homotopy type theory
, which started with the work of Awodey, Warren
[
AW09
]
and Voevodsky
[
Voe14
]
, and now covers many fundamental aspects of topology, such as fundamental groups, Blakers-Massey and truncations
[
Uni13
]
.

0.3.
Towards Elementary
\infty
-Toposes

The goal of this paper is to establish another pillar of homotopy invariant foundation, by generalizing elementary toposes to an appropriate notion of
elementary
\infty
-topos
. In order to understand how we can obtain a working definition, it is instructive to review how the definition of elementary toposes arose in the first place.

The story of topos theory starts with the work of Grothendieck and more generally Bourbaki, who needed a proper categorical framework to study algebro-geometric objects, introducing
Grothendieck topologies
and
sheaves
and hence defining a
Grothendieck topos
as a category of sheaves
[
sga72
]
. Lawvere and Tierney recognized that Grothendieck toposes can be an appropriate framework for categorical logic and an axiomatic study of sets if they could avoid the set-theoretical assumptions inherent in the definition of Grothendieck toposes (a technical condition known as
local presentability
). Their solution was to add a certain universal object, the
subobject classifier
, which allowed them to recover the desired results without assuming local presentability
[
Law64
,
Tie73
]
, and defining elementary toposes as
Cartesian closed categories
with subobject classifier and now many important results can now be found in the famous work of Johnstone
[
Joh02a
,
Joh02b
]
.

The
(\infty,1)
-categorical story of topos theory starts again very similarly, namely with the desire by Lurie to study objects in
derived algebraic geometry
, motivating him to define
Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes
as appropriate generalizations of Grothendieck toposes
[
Lur09
]
. Around the same time, Rezk also developed the equivalent theory of
model toposes
, with the intention of better understanding the concept of
descent
, in particular introducing the (now standard) idea that Grothendieck
(\infty,1)
-toposes are locally Cartesian closed presentable
(\infty,1)
-categories that satisfy descent
[
Rez10
]
. The historical analogy would suggest that we can obtain our desired generalization by replacing the presentability condition with appropriately chosen universal objects.

Before we explain how to choose the universal object, we will make a short detour into various characterizations of categories and
(\infty,1)
-categories. The data of a category can be characterized in two equivalent ways: Either as a set of objects along with a hom set for any two objects that has a composition, or as a set of objects and set of morphisms along with functions corresponding to source, target, composition, …
[
Rie16
, Subsection 1.1]
. We can think of the first as a
local
characterization of categories and the second as a
global
characterization.

This philosophy carries over to the
(\infty,1)
-categorical setting. There we have models of
(\infty,1)
-categories that follow the local philosophy, such as relative categories
[
BK12
]
, Kan enriched categories
[
Ber07a
]
or topologically enriched categories
[
Lur09
]
, and models that follow the global philosophy, such as quasi-categories
[
BV73
]
, complete Segal spaces
[
Rez01
]
and Segal categories
[
Ber07b
]
. There are prominent ways of translating between the local and global perspective, such as
(\mathfrak{C},N)
between quasi-categories and Kan enriched categories
[
Lur09
, Theorem 2.2.5.1]
, or
(F,R)
between Segal categories and Kan enriched categories
[
Ber07b
, Theorem 8.6]
,
(K_{\xi},N_{\xi})
between complete Segal spaces and relative categories
[
BK12
, Theorem 6.1]
.

Elementary

Having reviewed local and global approaches to
(\infty,1)

Local:
A

•

A

0.5.
External Universes and Models of Homotopy Type Theory

Having established a theory of elementary
\infty
-toposes one would similarly expect that it corresponds to homotopy type theories, the same way that elementary toposes correspond to higher-order type theories. The main challenge towards realizing this goal is to show that the universes in an elementary
\infty
-topos are as strict as required by type theory, which is complicated by the fact that there are many different universes that need to be strictified.

In the setting of Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes, this issue has been solved by Shulman
[
Shu19
]
by embedding the Grothendieck
\infty
-topos in a category of groupoid valued presheaves and constructing a single external universe, that can then be shown to satisfy the desired strictness results. This approach fundamentally hinges on the fact that Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes are all presentable and so can be modeled by model categories
[
Dug01
]
. Arbitrary elementary
\infty
-toposes will not satisfy such strictness conditions and so we cannot hope to recover such strong results directly.

Hence, as a first step we would like to find a way to characterize elementary
\infty
-toposes in a way that requires only a single universe. One possible motivation could come from
[
ABSS14
]
, where the authors use algebraic set theory
[
JM95
]
to associate to each elementary elementary topos a
category of ideals
using the
directed system of inclusions
. We will leave the
\infty
-categorical approach to algebraic set theory to future work and instead use the ind-completion of an
\infty
-category to obtain the desired characterization with a single universe. In fact, in
6.17
we prove a stronger result by giving a correspondence between various structured
\infty
-categories (such as universes, local Cartesian closure and subobject classifiers) and their ind-completions.

One immediate benefit of this correspondence is that, despite their complicated appearances, the characterizations of elementary
\infty
-toposes and their
\infty
-logical functors via sufficient universes and their preservation are in fact quite sensible, as they do correspond to the existence of a single universe and its preservation (
6.19
). Beyond that, the hope is that this alternative characterization can be employed in future work to prove that elementary
\infty
-toposes model homotopy type theories by strictifying the unique universe, generalizing the result by Shulman
[
Shu19
]
.

0.6.
Where to go from here

While we address some questions regarding elementary
\infty
-topos theory, many questions remain.

(1)

Free Algebras and
\infty
-Operads:
One elegant result in elementary topos theory is the existence of free finitary algebras, and in particular free monoids, using the notion of
W
-types

[
Joh02b
, Theorem D5.3.5]
. The construction fundamentally relies on the existence of natural number objects, which suggests the possibility of similar free constructions in elementary
\infty
-topos theory, however, the
\infty
-categorical nature raises several new challenges that need to be addressed.

Unlike in the
1
-categorical case, in an
\infty
-category
\mathscr{E}
, making an object
E
into a monoid involves constructing an infinite tower of morphisms
\mu_{n}:E^{n}\to E
, which are appropriately compatible. This data is often managed via an appropriate choice of
\infty
-operad and the monoid is then an algebra for such
\infty
-operads
[
Lur17
]
. Given that an
\infty
-operad incorporates the data of operations
\mu_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the current definition relies on the natural numbers, which in an arbitrary elementary
\infty
-topos can differ, and, concretely, involve non-standard natural numbers
[
Ras21c
]
. As a result, before we can even talk about constructing free algebras, the first important step is to give a definition of
\infty
-operads internal to an elementary
\infty
-topos
\mathscr{E}
based on the internal natural numbers of
\mathscr{E}
.

(2)

Stabilizations:
One key development of algebraic topology is stable homotopy theory. It involves defining the
\infty
-category of spectra. Hence we would want a stabilization of an arbitrary elementary
\infty
-topos. There is an established notion of a stabilization of an
\infty
-category, however, in this case we face the same challenges as before, as the stabilization is indexed by the natural numbers
[
Lur17
, Definition 1.4.2.8]
, which again can differ in an arbitrary elementary
\infty
-topos, and can in particular result in the existence of additional spheres. So what we would want is defining a stabilization operation that takes the additional natural numbers and spheres into account.

(3)

Constructing Finite Colimits:
Another elegant result in elementary topos theory is the fact that we can construct finite colimits using Cartesian closure and the subobject classifier and so, unlike the
\infty
-categorical analogue, the definition of elementary toposes does not assume the existence of finite colimits
[
Mik72
,
Mik76
,
Par74
]
. One relevant question is whether a similar result holds in the
\infty
-categorical setting. As a first step, in joint work with Jonas Frey, we show that initial objects and finite coproducts can be constructed in every locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category with subobject classifier, and so in particular in every elementary
\infty
-topos
[
FR21
]
. The remaining step towards recovering all finite colimits is to construct coequalizers using universes.

(4)

Localizations:
Another fascinating result about elementary toposes is our ability to construct all left-exact localizations of an elementary topos via certain endomorphisms of the subobject classifier, the so-called
Lawvere-Tierney topologies

[
MLM94
, Section V]
and we would like to obtain a similar elegant characterization for elementary
\infty
-toposes. We do have some results in the case of Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes
[
Lur09
,
ABFJ22
,
Ste21
]
, however, obtaining an appropriate generalization is greatly complicated by the fact that even in Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes not every left-exact accessible localization can be obtained via Grothendieck topologies, as they can have
\infty
-connected morphisms, and the ones that can be obtained are known as
topological localizations

[
Lur09
, Definition 6.2.1.4]
. This suggests two reasonable next steps, that we should pursue:

•

Proving that we can we can characterize topological localizations of elementary
\infty
-toposes via Lawvere-Tierney topologies on the subobject classifier.

•

Proving that more general left-exact localizations can be classified by certain choice of topology on a specific universe.

(5)

Models of Homotopy Type Theory:
Finally, as explained above, elementary toposes can be understood as models of various higher order type theories
[
Joh02b
, Proposition D.4.3.15]
and it is expected that there are similar connections between intensional type theories and
\infty
-categories, and particularly homotopy type theories with univalent universes and elementary
\infty
-toposes.

Making this connection precise is quite challenging as
\infty
-categories are not strict enough to model type theories directly and all existing results use the fact that we can strictify the
\infty
-category, either to model categories
[
Shu19
]
or at least fibration categories
[
KS19
]
. Hence one first step towards realizing this goal is to give a definition of elementary
\infty
-toposes in a strict model of
\infty
-categories, such as possibly fibration categories.

Definition 2.1

\mathscr{G}
be a locally Cartesian closed presentable
\infty
-category. Then
\mathscr{G}
is an
\infty
-topos if and only if the set of all morphisms in
\mathscr{G}
is local.

We move on to another key property of
\infty
-toposes,
descent
.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. Then
\mathscr{C}
satisfies descent if
(\mathscr{G}_{/-})^{\simeq}:\mathscr{G}^{op}\to\mathscr{S}

We now have the following result
[

, Lemma 6.1.3.7, Theorem 6.1.3.9]
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a Grothendieck
\infty
-topos, then
\mathscr{G}
satisfies descent. Moreover, if
\mathscr{G}
is presentable with universal colimits and satisfies descent, then it is a Grothendieck
\infty
-topos.

Finally, a key property regarding
\infty
-toposes are
universes
. Let
\mathscr{G}
be an
\infty
-topos and
\kappa
be a large enough cardinal. Denote by
(\mathscr{G}_{/-})^{\kappa}
the full subcategory of
\kappa
-compact objects in
\mathscr{C}_{/-}
(
Section

1
(

)). Then, by
2.10
,
((\mathscr{G}_{/-})^{\kappa})^{\simeq}:\mathscr{G}^{op}\to\mathscr{S}
takes colimits to limits and so, by

1
(

\mathscr{U}^{\kappa}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a presentable
\infty
-category and
\kappa
a cardinal. A
universe
or

for
\kappa
-compact objects is an object
\mathscr{U}^{\kappa}
representing the functor
((\mathscr{P}_{/-})^{\kappa})^{\simeq}:\mathscr{P}^{op}\to\mathscr{S}
.

The argument before the definition implies that
\infty
-toposes have universes. However, we do in fact, have the opposite result
[
Lur09
, Theorem 6.1.6.8]
.

Proposition 2.12
.

A presentable
\infty
-category with universal colimits is a Grothendieck

-topos if and only if for every large enough cardinal

there exists a universe
\mathscr{U}^{\kappa}
.

Up until now, we have seen several different ways of characterizing a Grothendieck
\infty
-topos. We can combine them all into the following theorem.

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a presentable
\infty
-category. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathscr{G}
is an
\infty

(2)

\mathscr{G}
is locally Cartesian closed and satisfies descent.

(3)

\mathscr{G}
is locally Cartesian closed and has object classifiers

for large enough
\kappa
.

(4)

\mathscr{G}
is locally Cartesian closed and the set of morphisms in

is local.

We can also define an analogue of Grothendieck toposes for
(n,1)
-categories (
Section

1
(

Theorem 2.14

Let
\mathscr{G}
be a presentable
(n,1)
-category. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathscr{G}
is an
(n,1)
-topos.

(2)

\mathscr{G}\simeq\tau_{n-1}\hat{\mathscr{G}}

(3)

\mathscr{G}

-truncated object classifiers

(4)

\mathscr{G}

.

Similar to

Our goal in the coming sections is to generalize these results from presentable

3.
Descent, Cartesian Closures and Universes

In the last section we reviewed a relation between universes, descent and Cartesian closure in the setting of presentable
\infty
-categories. In this section we want to generalize this relation by introducing a generalization of universes,
complete Segal universes
. This will culminate in
3.27
, which tells us precisely how these notions interact in the presentable and non-presentable setting.

As a first step we need to generalize universes from the presentable setting in order to avoid the reliance on set-theoretical assumptions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. A
universe
is a tuple
(\mathscr{U},i)
, where
\mathscr{U}
is an object in
\mathscr{C}
and
i:\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
is an inclusion of right fibrations.

By the Yoneda lemma (
Section

1
(
11
)) any functor
i:\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
is (homotopically) uniquely determined by a choice of morphism
p_{\mathscr{U}}:\mathscr{U}_{*}\to\mathscr{U}
, which we call the
universal fibration
. Now
Section

1
(
17
) directly implies the following result.

Lemma 3.2
.

(\mathscr{U},i)
is a universe if and only if the corresponding universal fibration
p_{\mathscr{U}}:\mathscr{U}_{*}\to\mathscr{U}
is univalent.

We can use this insight to understand the collection of universes.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. Denote by
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
the (possibly large) poset with objects universes and
\mathscr{U}\leq\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
if
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}}(p_{\mathscr{U}},p_{\mathscr{U}^{\prime}})
is non-empty, which is equivalent to the existence of a diagram of right fibrations
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}^{\prime}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i^{\prime}}

.

We can use this poset to define sufficient universes.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. Then
\mathscr{C}

if the induced map
\displaystyle\coprod_{\mathscr{U}\in\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}}\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}

-categories.

Concretely, this means that for every morphism

in
\mathscr{C}

\mathscr{U}\in\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
and a homotopically unique pullback square

.

If such a pullback square exists then we say

Let

Follows directly from combining the pullback square

in

as the collection of universes form a poset. Next we observe that our definition of universe behaves well with respect to over-categories.

.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete

In certain circumstances we can characterize the existence of sufficient universes in terms that closer resemble a representability condition.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty

\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
is filtered.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{U},\mathscr{U}^{\prime}
be two universes. As
\mathscr{C}
has sufficient universes, there exists a universe

that classifies

, meaning we have pullback diagrams
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{U}_{*}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{U}_{*}\coprod\mathscr{U}_{*}^{\prime}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p_{\mathscr{V}}}

As

are univalent the map

is mono (

\mathscr{U}\leq\mathscr{V}
in

and similarly

, hence we are done.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category with finite coproducts. Then
\mathscr{C}
has sufficient universes if and only if
\displaystyle\underset{\mathscr{U}\in\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}}{\mathrm{colim}}\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
is an equivalence of
\infty
-categories.

Proof.

Assume
\mathscr{C}
has sufficient universes then, by
3.9
,
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
is filtered. As the diagram is filtered, the colimit is still a full sub-category of
\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
(
Section

1
(
9
)). Moreover, the existence of sufficient universes means it is also essentially surjective, proving it is an equivalence.

On the other hand, if
\displaystyle\mathrm{colim}_{\mathscr{U}\in\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}}\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
is an equivalence, then it is in particular essentially surjective and so for every object
f
in
\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
, there exists a universe
\mathscr{U}
that classifies
f
, proving
\mathscr{C}
has sufficient universes.
∎

General universes help us recover certain objects in the category, however, we often also want to those objects to be closed under certain categorical constructions.

Definition 3.11
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be an
\infty
-category.
A universal property

\mathcal{P}
is a collection of functors
P:\mathscr{C}^{op}\to\mathscr{S}
. We say
\mathscr{C}
is
closed under the universal property
\mathcal{P}
or
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
if all functors
P
are representable in
\mathscr{C}
.

Examples of such universal properties includes (finite) (co)limits and Cartesian closure.

Definition 3.12
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category closed under the universal property
\mathcal{P}
and let
\mathscr{U}
be a universe. We say
\mathscr{U}
is closed under
\mathcal{P}
, if for all objects
c
, the full subcategory of
\mathscr{C}_{/c}
classified by
\mathscr{U}
is closed under
\mathcal{P}
. We denote the sub-poset of
\mathcal{P}
-closed universes of
\mathscr{C}
by
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}^{\mathcal{P}}
.

Let us present one example explicitly.

Example 3.13
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category and a
\mathscr{U}
a universe. We say
\mathscr{U}
is Cartesian closed if it closed under the universal property of Cartesian closure.

The existence of universes has valuable implications about the
\infty
-category, however, as proven in
3.10
, they at best can represent the right fibration
\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
rather than the actual Cartesian fibration
\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{C}}
. Hence, in order to obtain a better representability result we need to generalize universes appropriately.

Definition 3.14
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. A
complete Segal universe
is a pair
(\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}:{\mathbbe{\Delta}}^{op}\to\mathscr{C},i)
, where
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
is a simplicial object in
\mathscr{C}
and
i:\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{C}}
is an inclusion of Cartesian fibrations. Here
\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}}
is the representable Cartesian fibration defined in
Section

1
(
13
).

Warning 3.15
.

Notice our definition of complete Segal universe does not coincide with complete Segal objects internal to the category
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
(as defined in
Section

1
(
12
)). Indeed
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
is a poset and so any complete Segal object would be constant.

Despite the possible confusion we can justify our naming convention via the following two results that relate complete Segal universes both to universes and complete Segal objects.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. There is a functor
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}:\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
that takes a complete Segal universe
(\mathscr{U}_{\bullet},i)
to the universe
(\mathscr{U}_{0},i)
, which we call the underlying universe.

Proof.

It suffices to show that
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}
is functorial. If
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}\leq\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}^{\prime}
, then there is an inclusion of Cartesian fibration
\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}^{\prime}}
, which induces an inclusion of right fibrations
\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{0}}\to\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{0}^{\prime}}
, proving that
\mathscr{U}_{0}\leq\mathscr{U}_{0}^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3.17
.

Let
(\mathscr{U}_{\bullet},i)
be a complete Segal universe. Then
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
is a complete Segal object.

Proof.

By definition
\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}}
is a Cartesian fibration and so the result follows from
Section

1
(
13
).
∎

Definition 3.18
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. Denote by
\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
the poset with objects complete Segal universes and
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}\leq\mathscr{U}^{\prime}_{\bullet}
if there is a diagram of Cartesian fibrations
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}^{\prime}_{\bullet}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{C}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces i^{\prime}}

.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category. Then
\mathscr{C}
has
sufficient complete Segal universes
if the map
\displaystyle\coprod_{\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}\in\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}}\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{C}}
is an essentially surjective map of
\infty
-categories.

We now have the result analogous to
3.8
for over-categories.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category, let
(\mathscr{U}_{\bullet},i)
be a complete Segal universe in

and let

\mathscr{C}

(\pi_{2}:\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}\times c\to c,i)

We also have results analogous to

and

for complete Segal universes.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category with finite coproducts and sufficient complete Segal universes. Then
\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
is filtered.

Lemma 3.22
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category with finite coproducts. Then
\mathscr{C}
has sufficient complete Segal universes if and only if
\displaystyle\underset{\mathscr{U}\in\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}}{\mathrm{colim}}\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{C}}

We can also additionally assume that complete Segal universes in

are closed, generalizing

Definition 3.23
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category closed under the universal property
\mathcal{P}
and let
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
be a complete Segal universe. We say
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
is closed under
\mathcal{P}
, if for all objects

\mathscr{C}_{/c}

is closed under

. We denote the sub-poset of

\mathscr{C}
by
\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}^{\mathcal{P}}
.

By

, we immediately have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.24
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category closed under the universal property
\mathcal{P}
. Then a complete Segal universe
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
is closed under
\mathcal{P}
if and only if the underlying universe is closed under
\mathcal{P}
.

Moreover, complete Segal universes can have an additional property that we need later on.

Definition 3.25
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category and let
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
be a complete Segal universe. We say
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
is a
locally small
if
\mathscr{U}_{0}
classifies the map
(d_{1},d_{0}):\mathscr{U}_{1}\to\mathscr{U}_{0}\times\mathscr{U}_{0}
.

The terminology is motivated by locally small categories, which might not have small sets of objects
\mathcal{O}
and morphisms
\mathcal{M}
, but the source target projection
(s,t):\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{O}
is small
[
Rie16
, Definition 1.1.7]
.
Before we can prove the main result, we have the following key proposition.

Proposition 3.26
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category. Then the functor
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}:\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
has an inverse
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}:\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}\to\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
. Moreover, for a given universal property
\mathcal{P}
, it restricts to an inverse
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}:\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}^{\mathcal{P}}
. In particular, there are sufficient universes (closed under
\mathcal{P}
) if and only if there are sufficient complete Segal universes (closed under
\mathcal{P}
).

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{U}
be a universe in
\mathscr{E}
and
p_{\mathscr{U}}:\mathscr{U}_{*}\to\mathscr{U}
the universal fibration. Let
\mathscr{N}(p_{\mathscr{U}})
be the complete Segal object constructed in
Section

1
(
17
), which satisfies
\mathscr{N}(p_{\mathscr{U}})_{0}\simeq\mathscr{U}
. This implies that
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}\circ\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}
is the identity. On the other side, for a given complete Segal universe
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
,
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}(\mathscr{U}_{\bullet})
is a complete Segal universe with an equivalence of Cartesian fibrations over
\mathscr{C}

\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}(\mathscr{U}_{\bullet})}\simeq\mathscr{O}^{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{C}}\simeq\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}},

where
\mathscr{O}^{\mathscr{U}}_{\mathscr{C}}
is the full subcategory of
\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{C}}
consisting of morphisms classified by
\mathscr{U}
. Hence, by the Yoneda lemma for complete Segal objects (
Section

1
(
13
))
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}\simeq\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
, which proves that
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}
is also the identity. This proves that
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}
and
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift}
are inverses.

Now, let
\mathcal{P}
be a universal property. Then, by
3.24
, a complete Segal universe
\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
is
\mathcal{P}
-closed if and only if
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd}(\mathscr{U})
is
\mathcal{P}
-closed. Hence, the bijection
(\mathscr{U}\mathrm{nd},\mathscr{L}\mathrm{ift})
between
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
and
\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}
restricts to a bijection of posets
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathscr{C}}
and
\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}^{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove the main theorem.

Theorem 3.27
.

Let
\mathscr{E}
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category with finite coproducts closed under the (possibly empty) universal property
\mathcal{P}
. Consider the following statements:

(1)

\mathscr{E}
is locally Cartesian closed with sufficient universes closed under
\mathcal{P}
.

(2)

\mathscr{E}
has sufficient Cartesian closed complete Segal universes closed under
\mathcal{P}
.

(3)

Colimits in
\mathscr{E}
are universal and the morphisms in
\mathscr{E}
are local.

(4)

Colimits in
\mathscr{E}
are universal and
\mathscr{E}
satisfies descent.

Then we have the following implications:

•

(1)

5.
The
\infty

\infty
-Toposes

In the last section we defined elementary
\infty

Notice that a functor

Definition 5.1

Let

of

\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P}}_{F}:\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathscr{E}_{1}}\to\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathscr{E}_{2}}

\mathscr{U}_{\bullet}
in

is a complete Segal universe in
\mathscr{C}\mathscr{S}\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathscr{E}_{2}}
.

Note on Terminology
.

Similar to the previous section we will often suppress the notation of the universal property
\mathcal{P}
in order to simplify notation.

Next, we want to observe that the filtered colimit of elementary

For every
\infty

6.

Lemma 6.5

\mathscr{C}
has a subobject classifier if and only if
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{C})
has a subobject classifier that satisfies propositional resizing. Moreover, it is evident that
F:\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{E}^{\prime}
preserves the subobject classifier if and only if
F:(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{E})\to(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{E}^{\prime})
preserves the small subobject classifier, as it is the same object.

As we have confirmed that all properties are preserved appropriately, our proof is finished.
∎

Remark 6.18
.

The equivalence relates elementary
\infty
-toposes with universes and elementary models. One additional question is whether we can give a similar result for elementary
\infty
-toposes with
\mathcal{P}
-closed universes. This would require using a different approach as the one given in the proof of
6.17
. Indeed, the proof uses the fact that the universe
\mathscr{U}
in
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{E})
can be recovered as a filtered colimit of universes
\mathscr{U}_{i}
in
\mathscr{E}
, however, we cannot choose those universes to be closed under
\mathcal{P}
.

Remark 6.19
.

The definition of
\infty
-logical functor given in
5.1
involves an intricate condition because of the existence of sufficient universes. On the other hand, the definition of functors of accessible elementary models is straightforward and very much modeled on the definition of logical functors of elementary toposes. The fact that these two notions coincide (as proven in
6.17
) gives a strong indication that the definition of
\infty
-logical functor is in fact appropriately chosen.

7.
Elementary
(n,1)
-Topos Theory

Up until now our work has focused on generalizing elementary
1
-toposes (
2.2
) to elementary
\infty
-toposes in a way that appropriately generalizes Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes. However, there similarly are Grothendieck
(n,1)
-toposes for all
0\leq n\leq\infty
, which sit in between the two boundary cases and have been studied extensively in
[
Lur09
, Section 6.4]
. In this section we generalize the results from
Section

4
,
Section

5
and
Section

6
, from
\infty
-categories to
(n,1)
-categories (
Section

1
(
8
)), in particular introducing
elementary
(n,1)
-topos theory
.

Note on Terminology
.

It is an unfortunate fact of higher categorical terminology that an
\infty
-category by now refers to an
(\infty,1)
-category, whereas an
n
-category usually refers to an
(n,n)
-category creating possible confusion. In order to avoid such confusion, we will consistently use the notation
(n,1)
-category, when
n<\infty
.

We want to start with an appropriate notion of universe for
(n,1)
-categories. As every object in an
(n,1)
-category is
(n-1)
-truncated, for a given morphism
p:E\to B
, the space
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{C}}(-,B)
is
(n-1)
-truncated and so the induced map (
Section

1
(
11
))
\mathscr{C}_{/B}\to\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
can only be fully faithful if it takes value in the full subcategory
\tau_{n-2}\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
(
Section

1
(
16
)). This suggests the following notion of universe (
3.1
) in the setting of
(n,1)
-categories.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\mathscr{C}
be a finitely complete
(n,1)
-category and
\mathcal{P}
a universal property. A
\mathcal{P}
-closed
(n-2)
-truncated universe
is a pair
(\mathscr{U},i)
, where
\mathscr{U}
is an object in
\mathscr{C}
and
i:\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}\to\tau_{n-2}\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
is a fully faithful functor, such that the full subcategory of
\tau_{n-2}\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{C}}
with objects in the essential image of
i
satisfy
\mathcal{P}
(
3.11
).

Similar to
3.3
we denote the poset of
\mathcal{P}
-closed
(n-2)
-truncated universes by
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}_{\mathscr{C}}^{\mathcal{P},n-2}
and, following
3.4
, a finitely complete
(n,1)
-category has sufficient universes if every
(n-2)
-truncated morphism in
\mathscr{C}
is classified by a universe. We now have the following appropriate result corresponding to
3.27
.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\mathscr{E}
be a finitely complete locally Cartesian closed
(n,1)
-category with finite coproducts closed under the (possibly empty) universal property
\mathcal{P}
. If
\mathscr{E}
has sufficient
(n-2)
-truncated universes closed under
\mathcal{P}
then colimits in
\mathscr{E}
are universal and
(n-2)
-truncated morphisms in
\mathscr{E}
are local. The opposite holds if
\mathscr{E}
is presentable and
\kappa
-compact morphisms are closed under
\mathcal{P}
for
\kappa
large enough.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{E}
has sufficient universes closed under
\mathcal{P}
, then the proof follows using the same argument as in
3.27
. On the other side, assuming
\mathscr{E}
is presentable the other direction follows from
[
Lur09
, Theorem 6.4.1.5]
, as reviewed in
2.14
.
∎

We now have all the necessary ingredients to define elementary
(n,1)
-toposes.

Definition 7.3
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a universal property. and
n>0
. A finitely (co)complete locally Cartesian closed
(n,1)
-category is an
elementary
(n,1)
-topos
if it has a subobject classifier and sufficient
\mathcal{P}
-closed
(n-2)
-truncated universes.

This definition is in fact an appropriate generalization of the previous cases.

Example 7.4
.

If
n=1
, then the subobject classifier itself is precisely a universe for all
(-1)
-truncated morphisms. Hence, the existence of sufficient universes becomes vacuous and elementary
(1,1)
-toposes in the sense of
7.3
directly correspond to elementary toposes, as defined in
2.2
.

Example 7.5
.

If
n=\infty
, then it is a finitely (co)complete locally Cartesian closed
\infty
-category with subobject classifier and sufficient universes for
(n-2)
-truncated morphisms, which corresponds to all morphisms as
n=\infty
and hence recovers the definition of an elementary
\infty
-topos, by
4.2
.

Remark 7.6
.

Unlike in the case of
\infty
-toposes, we cannot recover the Cartesian closure from the universes, as morphisms that are not
(n-2)
-truncated are not classified.

We also have an analogous result to
4.6
.

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
\mathscr{E}
be an elementary
(n,1)
-topos. An
(n-2)
-truncated map
f:x\to y
is univalent if and only if any classifying map
\chi_{f}:y\to\mathscr{U}
is mono.

Notice for
n=1

7.7
recovers the already well-known fact that univalent monos are classified by the subobject classifier, as discussed in
4.7
. There is also a fundamental theorem of elementary
(n,1)
-toposes, with the same proof as in
4.9
.

Corollary 7.8
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a universal property and let
\mathscr{E}
be a
\mathcal{P}
-closed elementary
(n,1)
-topos and
x
an object. Then
\mathscr{E}_{/x}
is also a
\mathcal{P}
-closed elementary
(n,1)
-topos.

We move on to the
(n,1)
-categorical analogue of
Section

5
and study
(n,1)
-logical functors
.

Definition 7.9
.

A finitely (co)continuous locally Cartesian closed functor
F:\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\mathscr{E}_{2}
of elementary
(n,1)
-toposes is an
(n,1)
-logical functor
if
\mathscr{O}_{F}:\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{E}_{1}}\to\mathscr{O}_{\mathscr{E}_{2}}
restricts to a map of posets
\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P},n-2}_{F}:\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P},n-2}_{\mathscr{E}_{1}}\to\mathscr{U}\mathrm{niv}^{\mathcal{P},n-2}_{\mathscr{E}_{2}}
, which preserves the subobject classifier.

Example 7.10
.

In the case
n=1
, this precisely reduces to the case of logical functors of elementary toposes
2.2
, whereas in the case of
n=\infty
it coincides with
\infty
-logical functors, by
5.3
.

Similarly we denote the
\infty
-category with objects
\mathcal{P}
-closed elementary
(n,1)
-toposes and morphisms
(n,1)
-logical functors by
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{og}^{\mathcal{P}}_{(n,1)}
. We now have the analogous result to
5.9
with the same proof.

Corollary 7.11
.

The inclusion functor
\mathscr{L}\mathrm{og}^{\mathcal{P}}_{(n,1)}\to\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{(n,1)}
preserves small limits and filtered colimits.

Finally, we want to adapt the results in
Section

6
. First of all, let
(\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{(n,1)})_{\mathscr{U}},(\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{(n,1)})_{\mathscr{U},\mathrm{LCCC}},(\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{(n,1)})_{\mathscr{U},\mathrm{Prop}}
be defined analogous to
(\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{\infty})_{\mathscr{U}},(\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{\infty})_{\mathscr{U},\mathrm{LCCC}},(\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{\infty})_{\mathscr{U},\mathrm{Prop}}
in
5.8
, respectively.

On the other hand, following
Section

1
(
20
), if
\mathscr{E}
is a finitely complete
(n,1)
-category, where
n<\infty
, then
\mathscr{E}
is idempotent complete. Hence, we do not need to use the notion of accessible
\infty
-category with small objects (
6.1
). Rather we have the following.

Definition 7.12
.

Let
\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}_{(n,1)}
be the full subcategory of
\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}_{\infty}
, consisting of accessible
\infty
-categories such that
\mathscr{A}^{\mathrm{cmp}}
is an
(n,1)
-category and notice the equivalence in
Section

1
(
23
) restricts to an equivalence
\mathrm{Ind}:\widehat{\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}}_{(n,1)}\to\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}_{(n,1)}
.

Let
(\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}_{(n,1)})_{\mathscr{U}},\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}\mathscr{E}\mathrm{lem}_{(n,1)},\ (\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}_{(n,1)})_{\mathscr{U},\mathrm{Prop}},\ (\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}\mathscr{E}\mathrm{lem}_{(n,1)})_{\mathrm{Prop}}
be defined as the full subcategories of
(\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}^{\mathrm{sma}}_{\infty})_{\mathscr{U}}
(
6.7
),
\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}\mathscr{E}\mathrm{lem}_{\infty}
(
6.12
),
(\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}^{\mathrm{sma}}_{\infty})_{\mathscr{U},\mathrm{Prop}}
,
(\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}\mathscr{E}\mathrm{lem}_{\infty})_{\mathrm{Prop}}
(
6.16
), respectively, with objects in
\mathscr{A}\mathrm{cc}_{(n,1)}
. Now, we have the following result.

Corollary 7.13
.

Let
n<\infty
. There is a diagram of
\infty
-categories

,

where the vertical functors are faithful and the horizontal functors are equivalences.

We end this section with a discussion how the various truncation levels compare to each other. It is well established that for all
m\leq n\leq\infty
, every
(m,1)
-category is an
(n,1)
-category. Now that we have established a notion of elementary
(n,1)
-topos, we want to understand whether the various dimensions are related similarly.

Proposition 7.14
.

Let
-1\leq n<m\leq\infty
and
\mathscr{E}
an
(n,1)
-category. Then
\mathscr{E}
is not an elementary
(m,1)
-topos.

Proof.

By
7.1
, a universe for
(m-2)
-truncated objects is necessarily
(m-1)
-truncated, but not
(m-2)
-truncated. Hence, if
n<m
, then every object in
\mathscr{E}
is
(n-1)
-truncated and so also
(m-2)
-truncated and so cannot be a universe, meaning
\mathscr{E}
cannot be an elementary
(m,1)
-topos.
∎

Hence, elementary
(n,1)
-toposes are sensitive to dimension. However, these notions are still related.

Theorem 7.15
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the universal property consisting of finite limits, finite colimits and local Cartesian closure. Then for all
n\geq 0
we have a functor
\tau_{n}:\mathscr{L}\mathrm{og}^{\mathcal{P}}_{\infty}\to\mathscr{L}\mathrm{og}^{\mathcal{P}}_{(n+1,1)}
.

Proof.

We need to prove that for every
\mathcal{P}
-closed elementary
\infty
-topos
\mathscr{E}
,
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-closed elementary
(n+1,1)
-topos and for every
\infty
-logical functor
F:\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\mathscr{E}_{2}
,
\tau_{n}F:\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}_{2}
is an
(n+1,1)
-logical functor. By
4.12
, there is an
n
-truncation functor
\tau_{n}:\mathscr{E}\to\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
, which is left adjoint to the inclusion
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}\hookrightarrow\mathscr{E}
. This implies that
\tau_{n}(\mathscr{E})
is closed under finite limits and colimits. Moreover, by
[
Ras21e
, Proposition 2.11]
,
\tau_{n}
is a locally Cartesian closed localization, which implies that
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
is locally Cartesian closed as well.

Next, the subobject classifier
\Omega
is
0
-truncated and so in particular an object in
\tau_{n}(\mathscr{E})
. Finally, by
Section

1
(
11
), the truncation functor
\tau_{n-1}:\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{E}}\to\mathscr{O}^{(\mathrm{all})}_{\mathscr{E}}
induces a map of universes
\tau_{n-1}:\mathscr{U}\to\mathscr{U}
and applying epi-mono factorization (which exists by
4.12
for
n=-1
) gives us a factorization
\mathscr{U}\to\mathscr{U}_{\leq n-1}\to\mathscr{U}
. By definition,
\mathrm{Map}_{\mathscr{E}}(x,\mathscr{U}_{\leq n-1})\simeq\tau_{n-1}(\mathscr{E}_{/x})
, which proves that
\mathscr{U}_{\leq n-1}
is a universe for
(n-1)
-truncated objects in
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
. As
\mathscr{E}
has sufficient universes and this process constructs a
(n-1)
-truncated universe out of every universe, this proves that
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
also has sufficient
(n-1)
-truncated universes. Hence
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
is an elementary
(n+1,1)
-topos.

Now, let
F:\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\mathscr{E}_{2}
be an
\infty
-logical functor. Then
\tau_{n}F:\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}_{1}\to\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}_{2}
preserves finite limits and colimits and, again by
[
Ras21e
, Proposition 2.11]
, the local Cartesian closure. Finally, as
F(\mathscr{U})
is a universe and
F
respects truncation levels,
F(\mathscr{U}_{\leq n-1})
is again a universe for
(n-1)
-truncated objects, proving
\tau_{n}F
is
(n+1,1)
-logical.
∎

Remark 7.16
.

The result from
7.15
would also hold if
\mathcal{P}
is a universal property that includes finite limits, finite colimits and local Cartesian closure and such that for all
n\geq 0
, if
\mathscr{C}
satisfies
\mathcal{P}
, then
\tau_{n}\mathscr{C}
also satisfies
\mathcal{P}
.

This result has some fascinating implications and possibilities for generalizations. By
4.12
, for a given elementary
\infty
-topos
\mathscr{E}
, with natural number
n:1\to\mathbb{N}
we have an
n
-truncation
\tau_{n}:\mathscr{E}\to\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
.
We would like to consider
\tau_{n}\mathscr{E}
an example of an elementary
(n+1,1)
-topos. However, this is not possible if the natural number is non-standard. This suggest the following interesting conjecture.

Conjecture 7.17
.

Let
\mathscr{E}
be an elementary
\infty
-topos. Let
\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}_{\mathscr{E}}
denote the
\infty
-category of
\mathscr{E}
-enriched
\infty
-categories (as defined by Gepner and Haugseng in
[
GH15
]
) and notice that
\mathscr{E}
is itself
\mathscr{E}
enriched
[
GH15
, Corollary 7.4.10]
. Fix an internal natural number
n
in the natural number object
\mathbb{N}
of
\mathscr{E}
. There is a notion of elementary
(n+1,1)
-topos and applying the
n
-truncation functor
\tau_{n}:\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}_{\mathscr{E}}\to\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}_{\mathscr{E}_{\leq n}}
gives us an example thereof.

Notice
7.15
has an inverse in the context of Grothendieck
\infty
-toposes, meaning every Grothendieck
(n+1,1)
-topos is equivalent to the full subcategory of
n
-truncated objects in a Grothendieck
\infty
-topos, as explained in
2.14
. At this level of generality, this result does not generalize to elementary
(n,1)
-toposes.

Example 7.18
.

The category of finite sets is an elementary topos, but is not equivalent to the category of
0
-truncated objects of an elementary
\infty
-topos. Indeed, by
4.11
, every elementary
\infty
-topos has a natural number object, which is by definition
0
-truncated and so an element in the sub-category of
0
-truncated objects.

What prevents the existence of a lift is the lack of universes, which by definition exist if
n>1
, suggesting the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.19
.

Let
n>1
. For every elementary
(n+1,1)
-topos
\mathscr{E}
, there exists an elementary
\infty
-topos
\hat{\mathscr{E}}
, such that
\tau_{n}\hat{\mathscr{E}}\simeq\mathscr{E}
.

8.
Examples and Non-Examples

In this section we cover various examples and non-examples of elementary
(n,1)
-toposes.

Grothendieck
(n,1)
-Toposes:
Let us start with one key example, for which we have the following result.

Proposition 8.1
.

Let
n\leq\infty
, let
\mathcal{P}
be a universal property and
\mathscr{P}
a presentable
(n,1)
-category that satisfies
\mathcal{P}
. If there exists sufficient cardinals
\kappa
, such that the full subcategory of
\kappa
-compact objects is closed under
\mathcal{P}
, then
\mathscr{P}
is an elementary
(n,1)
-topos with
\mathcal{P}
-closed universes if and only if it is a Grothendieck
(n,1)
-topos.

Proof.

If
\mathscr{P}
is a Grothendieck
(n,1)
-topos, then, by
2.14
, it is locally Cartesian closed and has sufficient universes
\mathscr{U}^{\kappa}
for
\kappa
-small
(n-2)
-truncated objects and a subobject classifier (
2.15
), and hence an elementary
(n,1)
-topos, by
7.2
. On the other side, if
\mathscr{P}
is an elementary
(n,1)
-topos, then it is locally Cartesian closed and has sufficient universes for
(n-2)
-truncated objects and so a Grothendieck
(n,1)
-topos, by
2.14
.
∎

Example 8.2
.

Assuming sufficiently large cardinals,
\mathscr{S}_{\leq n-1}
is an elementary
(n,1)
-topos.

Finite Spaces:
As the category of finite sets is an elementary topos, we might expect that finite spaces are an elementary
\infty
-topos. However, that is not the case.

Example 8.3
.

The category of
\omega
-compact spaces is not an elementary
\infty
-topos. In fact it is not even closed under finite limits and colimits. Indeed, if it has finite limits it has the terminal object
*
. Moreover, if it has finite colimits it has the suspension of
*\coprod*
, which is
S^{1}
, then finite limits imply it has
\Omega S^{1}\cong\mathbb{Z}
, which is certainly not
\omega
-compact.

1
-Inaccessible Spaces:
There is a way to correct the previous example that is due to Lo Monaco
[
LM21
]
. Recall that a cardinal
\kappa_{acc}
is
1
-inaccessible
if it is weakly inaccessible and for every
\kappa<\kappa_{acc}
, there exists a weakly inaccessible cardinal
\kappa<\kappa_{1}<\kappa_{acc}
. We now have the following result as stated in
[
LM21
, Proposition 7.3]
.

Example 8.4
.

Let
\kappa_{acc}
be a
1
-inaccessible cardinal. The
\infty
-category of
\kappa_{acc}
-small spaces
\mathscr{S}^{\kappa_{acc}}
is an elementary
\infty
-topos, using the same argument as in
4.8
. Indeed, for every weakly inaccessible cardinal
\kappa<\kappa_{acc}
, we have a complete Segal universe
\textsc{css}(\mathscr{S}^{\kappa})
(
Section

1
(
5
)).

We can in fact generalize the result by Lo Monaco in the expected manner.

Example 8.5
.

Let
\kappa_{acc}
be a
1
-inaccessible cardinal. The
(n+1,1)
-category of
\kappa_{acc}
-small
n
-truncated spaces
(\mathscr{S}_{\leq n})^{\kappa_{acc}}
is an elementary
(n+1,1)
-topos. Indeed, as
\kappa_{acc}
is weakly inaccessible, it is closed under finite limits, finite colimits and locally Cartesian closed. Moreover,
\{0,1\}
is evidently
\kappa_{acc}
-small and so
(\mathscr{S}_{\leq n})^{\kappa_{acc}}
has a subobject classifier. Finally, for every weakly inaccessible cardinal
\kappa<\kappa_{1}
, let
((\mathscr{S}_{\leq n-1})_{\kappa})^{\simeq}
, be the maximal subgroupoid (
Section

1
(
7
)) of the
\infty
-category of
\kappa
-small
n-2
-truncated spaces. Then by the same argument as the one given in
4.8
we have an equivalence

\mathrm{Map}(X,((\mathscr{S}_{\leq n-1})^{\kappa})^{\simeq})\simeq(((\mathscr{S}_{\leq n-1})_{/X})^{\kappa})^{\simeq},

which proves that
((\mathscr{S}_{\leq n-1})^{\kappa})^{\simeq}
is a universe for
(n-1)
-truncated spaces.

Notice, the inclusion functor
(\mathscr{S}_{\leq n})^{\kappa_{acc}}\to\mathscr{S}_{\leq n}
is the identity functor on all objects, immediately giving us the following result.

Corollary 8.6
.

The inclusion functor
(\mathscr{S}_{\leq n})^{\kappa_{acc}}\to\mathscr{S}_{\leq n}
is
(n+1,1)
-logical.

We can think of this result as a generalization of the fact that the inclusion from finite sets to sets is logical.

\pi
-Finite Spaces:
There is an alternative notion of smallness of spaces, known as
\pi
-finite spaces

[
Lur18
, Definition E.0.7.8]
or
truncated coherent spaces

[
Ane21
]
, which are spaces for which the disjoint union of all homotopy groups is finite. We have the following result similar to
8.3
.

Example 8.7
.

The
\infty
-category of bounded coherent spaces does not form an elementary
\infty
-topos, as it again not closed under finite limits and colimits, using the fact that
\mathbb{Z}
is not bounded coherent.

Remark 8.8
.

The title of
[
Ane21
]
might create the impression that
\pi
-finite spaces are an elementary
\infty

In

Filter Quotient

-Toposes:
One interesting example of elementary toposes that are not Grothendieck are filter quotients
[

]
. They have been generalized to non-trivial examples of elementary
\infty
-toposes in
[

]

(n,1)
-toposes that are not Grothendieck
(n,1)
-toposes, proving that the opposite to
8.1
does not hold.

First we need to review several concepts.

Definition 8.9
.

A filter is a partially ordered set
(\Phi,\leq)
that satisfies the following three conditions:

(1)

\Phi\neq\emptyset
.

(2)

\Phi
is downward directed, meaning that for any two object
x,y\in\Phi
there exists
z\in\Phi
such that
z\leq x
and
z\leq y
.

(3)

\Phi
is upward closed, meaning that if
x\leq y
and
x\in\Phi
, then
y\in\Phi
.

Notice
\Phi^{op}
is a filtered category and if
\Phi
has a maximal object
1
, then
(1)
and
(3)
implies that
1\in\Phi
.

Definition 8.10
.

A finitely complete
\infty
-category with product-closed filter of subobjects is a finitely complete
\infty
-category
\mathscr{C}
along with a filter
\Phi
on the poset
\mathrm{Sub}_{\mathscr{C}}(1)
that is closed under products.

For a given filter, let
\mathscr{C}_{/-}:\Phi^{op}\to\mathscr{C}\mathrm{at}_{\infty}
be the restriction of
\mathscr{C}_{/-}
(
Section

1
(
16
)) with the inclusion
\Phi^{op}\to\mathscr{C}^{op}
. Concretely, it is the functor that takes
U\in\Phi
to
\mathscr{C}_{/\mathscr{U}}
and
U\leq V
to
-\times U:\mathscr{C}_{/V}\to\mathscr{C}_{/U}
. We now have the following definition.

Definition 8.11
.

Let
(\mathscr{C},\Phi)
be a finitely complete
\infty
-category with product-closed filter of subobjects. Define the
filter quotient

\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
as the colimit
\mathrm{colim}_{U\in\Phi^{op}}\mathscr{C}_{/U}
and denote
P_{\Phi}:\mathscr{C}\simeq\mathscr{C}_{/1}\to\mathscr{C}_{\Phi}
as the
quotient functor
.

We now have the following result generalizing
[
Ras21c
]
.

Proposition 8.12
.

Let
(\mathscr{E},\Phi)
be a finitely complete
(n,1)
-category with product-closed filter of subobjects. Then
P_{\Phi}:\mathscr{E}\to\mathscr{E}_{\Phi}

preserves

(1)

finite limits and colimits

(2)

subobject classifiers

(3)

locally Cartesian closure

(4)

(n-2)
-truncated universes

In particular if
\mathscr{E}
is an elementary
(n,1)
-topos, then
\mathscr{E}_{\Phi}
is one as well and
P_{\Phi}
is
(n,1)
-logical

Proof.

The diagram
\Phi^{op}
is filtered (
8.9
) and so
\mathscr{E}_{\Phi}
is a filtered colimit and so the result follows from the same argument given in
7.11
.
∎
