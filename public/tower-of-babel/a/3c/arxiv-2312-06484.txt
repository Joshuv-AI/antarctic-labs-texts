Augmented Island Resampling Particle Filters for Particle Markov Chain Monte Carlo
Kari Heine

Abstract
In modern days, the ability to carry out computations in parallel is key to efficient implementations of computationally intensive algorithms. This paper investigates the applicability of the previously proposed Augmented Island Resampling Particle Filter (AIRPF) — an algorithm designed for parallel implementations — to particle Markov Chain Monte Carlo (PMCMC). We show that AIRPF produces a non-negative unbiased estimator of the marginal likelihood and hence is suitable for PMCMC. We also prove stability properties, similar to those of the α \alpha SMC algorithm, for AIRPF. This implies that the error of AIRPF can be bound uniformly in time by controlling the effective number of filters, which in turn can be done by adaptively constraining the interactions between filters. We demonstrate the superiority of AIRPF over independent Bootstrap Particle Filters, not only numerically, but also theoretically. To this end, we extend the previously proposed collision analysis approach to derive an explicit expression for the variance of the marginal likelihood estimate. This expression admits exact evaluation of the variance in some simple scenarios as we shall also demonstrate.

1
Introduction

Particle Markov chain Monte Carlo (PMCMC)
[
1
]
is a computational methodology with a wide range of applications, see e.g.
[
15
,
14
,
17
]
, for joint Bayesian estimation of a latent signal process together with the model parameters. In a nutshell, PMCMC refers to an MCMC algorithm that deploys a sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithm
[
7
]
within each MCMC iteration to produce a non-negative and unbiased estimate of the marginal likelihood (marginalised over the latent signal process) for a given parameter value. Reminiscent to the pseudo-marginal MCMC of
[
2
]
, the unbiased marginal likelihood estimate is used to compute the correct Metropolis-Hastings acceptance probability which ensures that the resulting Markov chain targets the joint Bayesian posterior distribution for both the latent signal process and the model parameters.

SMC algorithms are notorious for their computational cost. In the context of PMCMC, they may be run thousands of times and thus, the computational efficiency of the deployed SMC algorithm is of utmost importance. To make algorithms computationally efficient by leveraging the power of modern computing systems, they must be suitable for parallel processing. The first SMC algorithms, such as the classical Bootstrap Particle Filter (BPF)
[
9
]
, are inherently sequential, but various suggestions to parallelise particle filters have been made throughout the past decades
[
18
,
12
,
3
,
20
]
. In this paper, we focus specifically on the
Augmented Island Resampling Particle Filter (AIRPF)
of
[
12
]
— an SMC algorithm specifically designed for parallel computing systems — and its application to PMCMC.

1.1
Contributions

AIRPF is known to be consistent and stable
[
12
]
, but in this paper we shall expand the analysis of AIRPF from the PMCMC point of view, first by carrying out the relatively straightforward analysis to show that AIRPF also produces an unbiased estimator of the Feynman-Kac normalisation coefficient, and hence is suitable for PMCMC.

A generic SMC algorithm,
\alpha
SMC, was introduced in
[
20
]
and it admits e.g. BPF, sequential importance sampling (SIS), and the adaptive resampling BPF algorithms as specific instances. The consistency and stability analysis carried out in
[
20
]
for
\alpha
SMC established a connection between the uniform convergence of
\alpha
SMC and the
effective sample size (ESS)

[
16
]
; by controlling the ESS to keep it above a given threshold, the approximation error can be bound uniformly in time. This analysis not only gave an immediate stability result of for adaptive resampling schemes due to the control on the ESS, but it also enabled a rigorous argument in favour of interacting parallel particle filters over independet BPFs
[
20
, see Section 5]
. Our second contribution is to extend the stability result of
[
20
]
to AIRPF, which is not an
\alpha
SMC algorithm. This analysis forms the core of this paper and it enables a simple and rigorous line of arguments to suggest the superiority of AIRPF over multiple independent BPFs.

The third contribution if this paper is a rigorous derivation of the formulae for the marginal likelihood estimate variance for AIRPF and comparison with IBPF and the augmented resampling particle filter (ARPF) of
[
12
]
. This derivation follows the collision analysis of
[
5
]
which was further developed in
[
11
]
and although the resulting formulae can only be evaluated in some academic toy applications, they shed light on the theoretical mechanisms governing the impact of the filter interactions on the error of the PMCMC estimates.

1.2
Formal problem statement

Let
X^{\theta}=(X^{\theta}_{0},X^{\theta}_{1},\ldots)
be a latent discrete time Markovian signal process, and let
Y^{\theta}=(Y^{\theta}_{0},Y^{\theta}_{1},\ldots)
be an observation process.
X^{\theta}
and
Y^{\theta}
are assumed to take values in sufficiently regular measurable spaces
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
and
(\mathbb{Y},\mathcal{Y})
, respectively, with laws specified by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rll}X^{\theta}_{n}\mid X^{\theta}_{n-1}=x&\sim K_{n}^{\theta}(x,\,\cdot\,),\quad X^{\theta}_{0}\sim\pi^{\theta}_{0},&\qquad\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\},\\[5.69054pt]
Y^{\theta}_{n}\mid X^{\theta}_{n}=x&\sim G^{\theta}_{n}(x,\,\cdot,),&\qquad\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\{0,1,\ldots\},\end{array}

where
\pi^{\theta}_{0}
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
, and
K^{\theta}_{n}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
and
G_{n}^{\theta}:\mathbb{X},\mathcal{Y}\to[0,1]
are probability kernels. The kernels and the initial distribution — and consequently
X^{\theta}
and
Y^{\theta}
— are assumed to be parameterised by
\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We fix
Y^{\theta}
to a given realisation
(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)
, and write
g^{\theta}_{n}=G_{n}^{\theta}(\,\cdot\,,y_{n})
where
G_{n}^{\theta}
denotes a conditional probability density w.r.t. some
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathcal{Y}
, typically the Lebesgue measure.

The model (
1.2
) gives rise to the Feynman-Kac measures (see e.g.
[
6
]
)

\displaystyle\pi_{n}^{\theta}=\gamma_{n}^{\theta}/\gamma_{n}^{\theta}(1),\qquad\forall\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

where the unnormalised Feynman-Kac measures
\gamma_{n}^{\theta}
are defined as

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{\theta}(\varphi)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\!\left[\varphi(X^{\theta}_{n})\prod_{q=0}^{n-1}g^{\theta}_{q}(X^{\theta}_{q})\right],\qquad\forall~\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}),~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(3)

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
denotes the set of bounded and measurable real valued functions on
\mathbb{X}
, and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\,\cdot\,]
denotes the expectation over the law of
X^{\theta}
for given
\theta
. The measures

\displaystyle\pi^{\theta}_{n}(\mathrm{d}x)=\mathbb{P}(X^{\theta}_{n}\in\mathrm{d}x\mid Y^{\theta}_{0}=y_{0},\ldots,Y^{\theta}_{n-1}=y_{n-1}),

are known as the prediction filter and they are typically the object of interest in Bayesian inference on hidden Markov models.

The Particle Marginal Metropolis-Hastings (PMMH) algorithm
[
1
]
approximates the joint Bayesian posterior distribution of
\theta
and
X^{\theta}
by deploying SMC to construct sample based approximations of
\pi_{n}^{\theta}
and the marginal likelihood
\gamma_{n}^{\theta}(1)
. In this paper, we shall analyse the approximations of
\pi_{n}^{\theta}
and
\gamma_{n}^{\theta}(1)
obtained with AIRPF and show that not only is AIRPF suitable for PMMH, but in certain situations preferable over independent BPFs (IBPF). From now on — as we shall focus on the SMC part of PMMH — we shall simplify the notation by suppressing the explicit dependency on
\theta
in all notations.

1.3
Organisation

Section
2
describes the AIRPF algorithm and contains the theoretical analysis to establish the lack-of-bias, consistency and stability. Section
3

AIRPF runs

[

]

for simplicity. The analysis does not rely on this assumption.

The

\displaystyle A_{s}=\mathbf{I}_{2^{S_{m}-s}}\otimes\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{2}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{2^{s-1}},\quad s\in\{1,\ldots,S_{m}\},

below, where we also use the notations

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{n,s},\,\cdot\,):=K_{n}^{\otimes M}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{k}_{n,s},\,\cdot\,):=K_{n}(\xi^{k,1}_{n,s},\,\cdot\,)\otimes\cdots\otimes K_{n}(\xi^{k,M}_{n,s},\,\cdot\,),

where

denotes a measure product. We also let

AIRPF produces weighted empirical measures

\displaystyle\pi^{N}_{n,s}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{m}W^{k}_{n,s}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{\xi^{k,i}_{n,s}}}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}W^{k}_{n,s}},\qquad\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},~s\in\{0,\ldots,S_{m}\},

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}^{N}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}W^{k}_{n,0}\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\delta_{\xi^{k,i}_{n,0}}\qquad\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

2.1
Lack-of-bias

Define kernels

2.2
Augmented Feynman-Kac model

Our extension of Theorem 2 of
[
20
]
to AIRPF is based on interpreting the measures
\pi^{N}_{0}\ldots,\pi^{N}_{n}
, obtained with Algorithm
1
, as a subsequence of the normalised measures
\underline{\pi}^{N}_{0},\ldots,\underline{\pi}^{N}_{(S_{m}+1)n}
of
an augmented Feynman-Kac model
, obtained from the original model by regarding the
S_{m}
stages of AIRPF as an artificial Feynman-Kac updates involving weighting with a constant potential function, signifying a non-informative observation, and a mutation with an identity kernel, to signify that the signal cannot mutate over the augmented stages.

Formally, the potential functions
\underline{g}_{\rho}:\mathbb{X}\to[0,+\infty)
and kernels
\underline{K}_{\rho}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]

\rho\in\{0,\ldots,(S_{m}+1)n\}
as

\displaystyle\underline{g}_{\rho}=g_{I_{S_{m}+1}(\rho),R_{S_{m}+1}(\rho)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underline{K}_{\rho}=K_{I_{S_{m}+1}(\rho),R_{S_{m}+1}(\rho)},

(9)

where
I_{q}(p)=\left\lfloor p/q\right\rfloor
and
R_{q}(p)=p-qI_{q}(p)
,
and for all
s\in\{0,\ldots,S_{m}\}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

\displaystyle g_{p,s}:=g_{p}\boldsymbol{1}(s=0)+\boldsymbol{1}(s>0)\quad\text{and}\quad K_{p,s}:=K_{p}\boldsymbol{1}(s=0)+\mathrm{Id}\boldsymbol{1}(s>0).

(10)

Analogously to Section
2.1
, we define
\underline{Q}_{\rho}
and
\underline{Q}_{\rho,n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},~\rho\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
in terms of
\underline{g}_{\rho}
and
\underline{K}_{\rho}
. With
\underline{\pi}_{0}=\pi_{0}
, the Feynman-Kac measures for the augmented model are

\displaystyle\underline{\gamma}_{n}=\underline{\pi}_{0}\underline{Q}_{0,n}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{\pi}_{n}={\underline{\gamma}_{n}}/{\underline{\gamma}_{n}(1)}\qquad\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

and thus
\pi_{p}=\underline{\pi}_{(S_{m}+1)p}
and
\gamma_{p}=\underline{\gamma}_{(S_{m}+1)p}
for all
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

In order to accommodate the augmented Feynman-Kac model interpretation for the particle approximations, the particles in Algorithm

will be indexed as

\displaystyle\underline{\xi}^{(k-1)M+i}_{\rho}=\underline{\xi}^{k,i}_{\rho}=\xi^{k,i}_{I_{S_{m}+1}(\rho),R_{S_{m}+1}(\rho)}\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{W}^{k}_{\rho}=W^{k}_{I_{S_{m}+1}(\rho),R_{S_{m}+1}(\rho)},

for all
\rho\in\{0,1,\ldots,(S_{m}+1)n\}
,

and

. Although the particle weights within filter are constant, it is useful to define individual particle weights as

\displaystyle w^{(k-1)M+i}_{p,s}=w^{k,i}_{p,s}=W^{k}_{p,s}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underline{w}^{(k-1)M+i}_{\rho}=\underline{w}^{k,i}_{\rho}=\underline{W}^{k}_{\rho}

\displaystyle\overline{Q}_{p,n}=\frac{Q_{p,n}}{\pi_{p}Q_{p,n}(1)}\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\underline{Q}}_{p,n}=\frac{\underline{Q}_{p,n}}{\underline{\pi}_{p}\underline{Q}_{p,n}(1)},\qquad\forall~n\in\mathbb{N}_{0},~p\in\{0,\ldots,n\}.

, only one filter will contribute to the IBPF output. For
M=1
, ENF and ESS coincide and IBPF reduces to SIS. With AIRPF, such degeneracy can be avoided by forcing the ENF to remain above some pre-determined threshold.

3.3
Absolute variance of the Marginal likelihood estimate

For any
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
, we define the Cartesian product function
\varphi^{\otimes 2}:(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto\varphi(x)\varphi(x^{\prime})
, and for any kernel
L:(x,\mathrm{d}y)\mapsto L(x,\mathrm{d}y)
we define a product kernel
L^{\otimes 2}(x,x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y\times\mathrm{d}y^{\prime})=L(x,\mathrm{d}y)\otimes L(x^{\prime},\mathrm{d}y^{\prime})
. A collision operator
\mathcal{C}_{i}
, is defined for
i\in\{0,1\}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{0}(\varphi)(x,x^{\prime})=\varphi(x,x^{\prime})
and
\mathcal{C}_{1}(\varphi)(x,x^{\prime})=\varphi(x,x)

[
5
]
. For brevity, we shall write
\mathcal{C}_{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}),(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})}=\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{1}(a_{1}=b_{1}\vee\cdots\vee a_{p}=b_{p})}
for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, define a kernel
\boldsymbol{R}_{n}:\mathbb{X}^{2M}\times\mathcal{X}^{2M}\to[0,+\infty)
as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}_{n}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime})=\overline{\boldsymbol{g}}_{n}^{\otimes 2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime})\sum_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{M}}V_{n}^{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})V^{\boldsymbol{v}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime})\varphi\!\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{v}}\right)

(32)

for all
\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}^{M}
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}^{2M})
, where
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\boldsymbol{u}}=(\xi^{u_{1}},\ldots,\xi^{u_{M}})
and

\displaystyle\overline{\boldsymbol{g}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}g_{n}(\xi^{i})\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{n}^{\boldsymbol{u}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\prod_{k=1}^{M}\frac{g_{n}(\xi^{u_{k}})}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}g_{n}(\xi^{\ell})}

for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{M})\in\mathbb{X}^{M}
and
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{M})\in\{1,\ldots,M\}^{M}
. To give some context,
\boldsymbol{R}_{n}
corresponds to the internal resampling within two filters that are chosen according to their weights. For brevity, we also write
\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}}=\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{m}\cdots\sum_{i_{p}=1}^{m}
for any
p\in\mathbb{N}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
m,M\in\mathbb{N}
with
N=mM
and
S_{m}=\log_{2}(m)
,

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W^{i}_{n,0}\varphi(\xi^{i}_{n,0})\right)^{2}\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}\!\left[\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}\prod_{s_{k}=1}^{S_{m}}A^{i_{k,s_{k}+1}i_{k,s_{k}}}_{s_{k}}A^{j_{k,s_{k}+1}j_{k,s_{k}}}_{s_{k}}\right]\\
\times\pi^{\otimes 2M}_{0}\mathcal{C}_{i_{0,1},j_{0,1}}\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{0},\boldsymbol{j}_{0}}\cdots\boldsymbol{H}_{n-1}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{j}_{n-1}}(\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\otimes 2}),

where
\boldsymbol{i}=(\boldsymbol{i}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{i}_{n-1})
,
\boldsymbol{j}=(\boldsymbol{j}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{j}_{n-1})
and for all
0\leq q<n
, we define
\boldsymbol{i}_{q}=(i_{q,1},\ldots,i_{q,S_{m}+1})
,
\boldsymbol{j}_{q}=(j_{q,1},\ldots,j_{q,S_{m}+1})
,

\boldsymbol{H}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{q},\boldsymbol{j}_{q}}_{q}=\boldsymbol{R}_{q}\mathcal{C}_{(i_{q,1},\ldots,i_{q,S_{m}}),(j_{q,1},\ldots,j_{q,S_{m}})}\boldsymbol{K}_{q+1}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{C}_{i_{q,S_{m}+1},j_{q,S_{m}+1}},

and

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\varphi(\xi^{i}),\qquad\forall~\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{M})\in\mathbb{X}^{M}.

Proof.

The claim follows by writing

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}W^{i}_{n,0}\varphi(\xi^{i}_{n,0})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{n,0},j_{n,0}}\mathbb{E}\left[W^{i_{n,0}}_{n,0}W^{j_{n,0}}_{n,0}\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{n,0}}_{n,0})\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{n,0}}_{n,0})\right],

and repeatedly applying Lemma
3.2
, first to the function
\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\otimes 2}
, then to the function
\boldsymbol{H}_{n-1}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{j}_{n-1}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\otimes 2}
, etc., until finally applying Lemma
3.2
to the function
\boldsymbol{H}_{0}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{0},\boldsymbol{j}_{0}}\cdots\boldsymbol{H}_{n-1}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{n-1},\boldsymbol{j}_{n-1}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}^{\otimes 2}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}^{2M})
,
i_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}+1}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
m,M\in\mathbb{N}
with
N=mM
and
S_{m}=\log_{2}(m)
,

\mathbb{E}\big[W^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n+1,0}W^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n+1,0}{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n+1,0},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n+1,0})\big]\\
=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}\left[\prod_{s=1}^{S_{m}}A^{i_{s+1}i_{s}}_{s}A^{j_{s+1}j_{s}}_{s}\right]\!\mathbb{E}\!\left[W^{i_{1}}_{n,0}W^{j_{1}}_{n,0}\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}\varphi\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{1}}_{n,0},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{1}}_{n,0}\big)\right]\!,

where
\boldsymbol{i}=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{S_{m}}),~\boldsymbol{j}=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{S_{m}})\in\{1,\ldots,m\}^{S_{m}}
.

Proof.

Let
\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{0},\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{1},\ldots
be as defined in Proposition
2.1
. If
i_{S_{m}+1}\neq j_{S_{m}+1}
, then

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1})\,\middle|\,\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{(S_{m}+1)n+S_{m}}\right]\\
=\sum_{{i_{S_{m}},j_{S_{m}}}}\frac{A^{i_{S_{m}+1},i_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}A^{j_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}W^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}W^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}\boldsymbol{K}_{n+1}^{\otimes 2}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}})}{W^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}W^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}},

(33)

and if
i_{S_{m}+1}=j_{S_{m}+1}
, then

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1})\,\middle|\,\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{(S_{m}+1)n+S_{m}}\right]\\
=\sum_{i_{S_{m}}}\frac{A^{i_{S_{m}+1},i_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}W^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}\boldsymbol{K}_{n+1}\mathcal{C}_{1}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}})}{W^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}}.

(34)

In either case,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\!\left[W^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}W^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1})\,\middle|\,\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{(S_{m}+1)n+S_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{S_{m}}\\
j_{S_{m}}\end{subarray}}A^{i_{S_{m}+1},i_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}A^{j_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}W^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}W^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}\boldsymbol{K}_{n+1}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{C}_{i_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}+1}}(\varphi)\big(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}\big).

Thus, by Lemma
3.3

\mathbb{E}\!\left[W^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}W^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}+1}}_{n,S_{m}+1})\,\middle|\,\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{(S_{m}+1)n+1}\right]\\
=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i},\boldsymbol{j}}\left[\prod_{q=1}^{S_{m}}A^{i_{q+1},i_{q}}_{q}A^{j_{q+1},j_{q}}_{q}\right]\!W^{i_{1}}_{n,1}W^{j_{1}}_{n,1}\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{K}_{n+1}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{C}_{i_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}+1}}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{1}}_{n,1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{1}}_{n,1}),

where
\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}=(i_{2},\ldots,i_{S_{m}})
and
\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}=(j_{2},\ldots,j_{S_{m}})
. Finally,

W_{n,1}^{i_{1}}W_{n,1}^{j_{1}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{2:S_{m}},\boldsymbol{j}_{2:S_{m}}}\boldsymbol{K}_{n+1}^{\otimes 2}\mathcal{C}_{i_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}+1}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{1}}_{n,1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{1}}_{n,1})\,\big|\,\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{(S_{m}+1)n}\right]\\
=W^{i_{1}}_{n,0}W^{j_{1}}_{n,0}\boldsymbol{H}_{n}^{\boldsymbol{i}_{n},\boldsymbol{j}_{n}}(\varphi)\!\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{1}}_{n,0},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{1}}_{n,0}\right),

since
W^{i_{1}}_{n,1}W^{j_{1}}_{n,1}=W^{i_{1}}_{n,0}W^{j_{1}}_{n,0}\overline{\boldsymbol{g}}_{n}^{\otimes 2}(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{1}}_{n,0},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{1}}_{n,0})
. The claim then follows.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X}^{2M})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
s\in\{1,\ldots,S_{m}-1\}
and any
i_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}+1}\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,

\sum_{i_{S_{m}},j_{S_{m}}}\mathbb{E}\left[A^{i_{S_{m}+1},i_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}A^{j_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}W^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}W^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}})\,\middle|\,\underline{\mathcal{G}}_{(S_{m}+1)n+s}\right]\\
=\sum_{\boldsymbol{i}_{s:S_{m}},\boldsymbol{j}_{s:S_{m}}}\left[\prod_{q=s}^{S_{m}}A^{i_{q+1}i_{q}}_{q}A^{j_{q+1}j_{q}}_{q}\right]\!W^{i_{s}}_{n,s}W^{j_{s}}_{n,s}\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{s+1:S_{m}},\boldsymbol{j}_{s+1:S_{m}}}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{s}}_{n,s},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{s}}_{n,s}),

where
\boldsymbol{i}_{s+1:S_{m}}=(i_{s+1},\ldots,i_{S_{m}})
and
\boldsymbol{j}_{s+1:S_{m}}=(j_{s+1},\ldots,j_{S_{m}})
.

Proof.

The proof is by backward induction. The induction start at rank
s=S_{m}-1
follows immediately by (
33
) – (
34
) by replacing
\boldsymbol{K}^{\otimes 2}_{n+1}
with the identity kernel. Assume the claim to hold at rank
s
. In this case,

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{S_{m}},j_{S_{m}}}\mathbb{E}\left[A^{i_{S_{m}+1},i_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}A^{j_{S_{m}+1},j_{S_{m}}}_{S_{m}}W^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}W^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}}\varphi(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{S_{m}}}_{n,S_{m}})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{G}_{(S_{m}+1)n+s-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{i}_{s:S_{m}}\\
\boldsymbol{j}_{s:S_{m}}\end{subarray}}\left[\prod_{q=s}^{S_{m}}A^{i_{q+1}i_{q}}_{q}A^{j_{q+1}j_{q}}_{q}\right]W^{i_{s}}_{n,s}W^{j_{s}}_{n,s}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}_{s},\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{s}}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{s}}_{s},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{s}}_{s})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{G}_{(S_{m}+1)n+s-1}\right],

(35)

where we have used the shorthand notations
\boldsymbol{i}^{\prime}_{s}=\boldsymbol{i}_{s+1:S_{m}}
and
\boldsymbol{j}^{\prime}_{s}=\boldsymbol{j}_{s+1:S_{m}}
. Similarly to the proof of Lemma
3.2
,

\displaystyle W^{i_{s}}_{n,s}W^{j_{s}}_{n,s}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{s+1:S_{m}},\boldsymbol{j}_{s+1:S_{m}}}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{s}}_{n,s},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{s}}_{n,s})\,\middle|\,\mathcal{G}_{(S_{m}+1)n+s-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{s-1},j_{s-1}}A^{i_{s}i_{s-1}}_{s-1}A^{j_{s}j_{s-1}}_{s-1}W^{i_{s-1}}_{n,s-1}W^{j_{s-1}}_{n,s-1}\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{i}_{s:S_{m}},\boldsymbol{j}_{s:S_{m}}}(\varphi)(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i_{s-1}}_{n,s-1},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{j_{s-1}}_{n,s-1}),

(36)

and we have the claim by substituting (
36
) into (
35
).
∎

Theorem
3.1
is reminiscent to Lemma 4 of
[
11
]
, which can be directly applied to calculating the variance for IBPF and ARPF, where individual particles at any stage are conditionally independent, given the previous stage. In AIRPF, this is not true because resampling is done across subsets (islands) of
M
particles which makes the particles within the subsets dependent. The conditional independence holds in AIRPF, but only at the filter level, and therefore Theorem
3.1
involves collisions between filters rather than collisions between particles. Due to the internal resampling, particle level interactions too need to be taken into account and this is accomplished by the kernel
\boldsymbol{R}_{n}
, defined in (
32
). This combined particle and filter level collision analysis is the main difference to Lemma 4 of
[
11
]
.

4
Numerical Experiments

4.1
Exact variance

To demonstrate the results of Section
3.3
, consider a simple HMM on
\mathbb{X}=\{0,1\}
:

\displaystyle X_{0}\sim\frac{1}{2}\delta_{0}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{1},\quad X_{n+1}\mid X_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{n}\sim\frac{3}{4}\delta_{x_{n}}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{1-x_{n}},

\displaystyle Y_{n}\mid X_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{n}\sim\frac{3}{4}\delta_{x_{n}}+\frac{1}{4}\delta_{1-x_{n}}.

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This model is not of any practical importance, but allows us to demonstrate the performance of the various estimators in a non-approximate manner. We shall consider the classical BPF and three different parallel particle filter algorithms: (1) IBPF, (2) augmented resampling particle filter (ARPF) of
[
12
]
, and (3) AIRPF. We do not regard ARPF as a practically feasible alternative for AIRPF, but it has been included to demonstrate the impact of the level of interaction to the variance of the marginal likelihood estimator.

As our purpose with this example is only to demonstrate the impact of the different interaction schemes to the variance of the marginal likelihood estimator, we will keep the scenario simple by choosing
m=4
and
M=2
making the total sample size
N=8
.

Figure
1
shows the marginal likelihood estimator variances for IBPF, ARPF and AIRPF relative to that of the classical BPF with
N=8
. All relative variances are above 1, as BPF allows the full interaction between particles while the IBPF, ARPF and AIRPF constrain the interactions and therefore BPF yields the lowest variance. IBPF, without any interaction between the
m
filters, has the highest variance with seemingly exponential growth as predicted in Section
3.1
. The performances of AIRPF and ARPF are intermediate, and suggest sub-exponential growth.

Fig 1:
Variances of the marginal likelihood estimators, relative to BPF.

Note that computational time considerations are intentionally omitted, as our purpose is to demonstrate different orders of complexity between IBPF, ARPF, and AIRPF, which suggests that asymptotically AIRPF will be superior to IBPF for longer data records.

4.2
PMCMC Application

In our second example, we consider simultaneous change point detection and the estimation of the probability of change point occurring in sequential counting data. The data represents the number of on-line news articles that contain a specific keyword. The data contains daily counts of articles containing the keyword and the total number of articles published on the day. The model for the daily rate of articles containing the keyword is a
[0,1]
-valued Markov chain
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
with the initial distribution
X_{0}\sim f_{\alpha,\beta}
, where
f_{\alpha,\beta}
is the law of
\beta
-distribution with parameters
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. For the signal kernel, we set

\displaystyle K(x,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})=(1-p)\delta_{x}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime})+pf_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}),\qquad\forall~x\in[0,1],

We assume
\alpha
and
\beta
to be known, but
p
to be unknown. We impose another
\beta
-prior for
p
with known parameters.

We shall estimate the joint Bayesian posterior law of
p
and the occurrences of the change points PMMH, deploying either IBPF or AIRPF. The experiment was carried out for
m=64
filters with two different sample sizes,
M=200
and
M=500
.

Fig 2:
(a) Marginal posterior distribution of
X_{t}
. (b) The data and the marginal mean of
X_{t}
. (c) Posterior distribution of
p
with IBPF (transparent gray) and AIRPF (black). (d) Marginal probability of change points (black: upwards, gray: downwards). (e) Average ENF for IBPF (dahsed) and AIRPF (solid) across the 523 filter iterations. (f) ACF of
p
for IBPF (dashed) and AIRPF (solid). In (e) and (f), black depicts
M=200
and gray
M=500
.

Figure
2
shows the results for 50000 iterations of PMMH. Except for the approximate posterior for
p
in Figure
2
(c), the results are shown for AIRPF only. The omitted results for IBPF are broadly similar. Figure
2
demonstrates AIRPF to be suitable for PMMH, but we refrain from claiming PMMH in general to be the preferred method for the change point detection, as many other methods exist, such as reversible jump Markov chain Monte Carlo
[
10
]
.

To compare IBPF with AIRPF, the ENF and the estimated autocorrelation function of the resulting Markov chain are shown in Figures
2
(e) – (f). The AIRPF deploys adaptive resampling, i.e. the interaction between different filters was executed only when the ENF went below a predefined threshold value of
0.3\times m
. AIRPF maintains the ENF at the required level while the ENF of IBPF decreases as predicted.

We have included the results for two different sample sizes
M=200
and
M=500
to demonstrate the effect of
M
on the comparative performance. For large
M
, IBPF and AIRPF produce similar results, as expected; for large
M
, running
m
filters adds little to the accuracy of the marginal likelihood estimator as a single filter with large
M
already gives a sufficiently accurate estimate, but for small
M
, multiple filters incur a more notable improvement.
