On cyclic and nontransitive probabilities Thanks: Subject Classification: 60C05, 91A60
Pavle Vuksanovic, A.J. Hildebrand

Abstract
Motivated by classical nontransitivity paradoxes, we call an n n -tuple ( x 1 , … , x n ) ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] n (x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n} cyclic if there exist independent random variables U 1 , … , U n U_{1},\dots,U_{n} with P ⁡ ( U i = U j ) = 0 P(U_{i}=U_{j})=0 for i ≠ j i\not=j such that P ⁡ ( U i + 1 > U i ) = x i P(U_{i+1}>U_{i})=x_{i} for i = 1 , … , n − 1 i=1,\dots,n-1 and P ⁡ ( U 1 > U n ) = x n P(U_{1}>U_{n})=x_{n} . We call the tuple ( x 1 , … , x n ) (x_{1},\dots,x_{n}) nontransitive if it is cyclic and in addition satisfies x i > 1 / 2 x_{i}>1/2 for all i i . Let p n p_{n} (resp. p n ∗ p_{n}^{*} ) denote the probability that a randomly chosen n n -tuple ( x 1 , … , x n ) ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] n (x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n} is cyclic (resp. nontransitive). We determine p 3 p_{3} and p 3 ∗ p_{3}^{*} exactly, while for n ≥ 4 n\geq 4 we give upper and lower bounds for p n p_{n} that show that p n p_{n} converges to 1 1 as n → ∞ n\to\infty . We also determine the distribution of the smallest, middle, and largest elements in a cyclic triple.

Introduction

A classic example of a nontransitive probability paradox
is provided by the
Efron dice
, a set of four dice invented
by Bradley Efron and popularized by Martin Gardner
[
8
]
.
The Efron dice are six-sided dice with face values given as follows:

(1.1)

\begin{split}&A=\{0,0,4,4,4,4\},\quad B=\{1,1,1,5,5,5\},\quad\\
&C=\{2,2,2,2,6,6\},\quad D=\{3,3,3,3,3,3\}.\end{split}

One can easily check that, with probability
2/3
each,
B
beats
A
,

C
beats
B
, and
D
beats
C
while, at the same time,
A
beats
D
with
probability
2/3
. In this sense, the dice
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
form a
nontransitive cycle.

More formally, if we let
A
,
B
,
C
,
D
denote
independent discrete random variables that are uniformly distributed
over the values listed in (
1.1
), then these variables
satisfy

(1.2)

P(B>A)=P(C>B)=P(D>C)=P(A>D)=\frac{2}{3}.

Another classic example of nontransitivity is given by the
following set of three-sided dice which seems to have first appeared
(in a different, but equivalent, context) in a paper by Moon and Moser

[
16
]
:

(1.3)

A=\{1,5,9\},\quad B=\{2,6,7\},\quad C=\{3,4,8\}.

The three-sided dice
A
,
B
,
C
defined by
(
1.3
) form a nontransitive cycle with probabilities

(1.4)

P(B>A)=P(C>B)=P(A>C)=\frac{5}{9}.

There now exists a large body of research motivated by such
nontransitivity phenomena; see
[
2
,
5
,
7
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
13
,
15
]
for some
recent work on this subject.

It is natural to ask what the most “extreme” level of nontransitivity
is that can be achieved with constructions such as the Efron and
Moon-Moser dice. Can one replace the probabilities
2/3
and
5/9
in
(
1.2
) and (
1.4
)
by even larger numbers?

To formalize this question, one can consider, for each
integer
n\geq 3
, the quantity

(1.5)

\pi_{n}=\max\min\left(P(U_{2}>U_{1}),\dots,P(U_{n}>U_{n-1}),P(U_{1}>U_{n})\right),

where the maximum is taken over all sets of independent random variables

U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
.
The Efron and Moon-Moser dice constructions show that

\pi_{4}\geq 2/3
and
\pi_{3}\geq 5/9
.

The quantities
\pi_{n}
were first investigated in the 1960s by
Steinhaus and Trybula
[
18
]
, Trybula

[
21
,
22
]
, Chang
[
6
]
,
and Usiskin
[
23
]

who showed that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\pi_{n}=3/4
and determined the first few
values of
\pi_{n}
. It particular, the values of
\pi_{3}
and
\pi_{4}
are
given by

(1.6)

\pi_{3}=\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2},\quad\pi_{4}=\frac{2}{3}.

Thus the Efron dice construction is best-possible in the sense of
achieving the value
\pi_{n}
for
n=4
; meanwhile, the Moon-Moser dice
construction for
n=3
is not best-possible as
5/9<(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
.
More recently (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
11
,
17
]
)
it was shown that, for any
n\geq 3
,

(1.7)

\pi_{n}=1-\frac{1}{4\cos^{2}(\pi/(n+2))}.

Interestingly, the numbers
\pi_{n}
defined by (
1.5
)
have come up independently in very different contexts such as graph theory

[
4
,
14
,
17
]
and theoretical computer science

[
12
,
20
]
.

In this paper, we focus on another aspect of the nontransitivity
phenomenon that has received less attention in the literature,
namely the question of which
n
-tuples can be realized as
a tuple of
cyclic probabilities

(P(U_{2}>U_{1}),\dots,P(U_{n}>U_{n-1}),P(U_{1}>U_{n}))
, and how common such tuples are among all
n
-tuples in
[0,1]^{n}
.
We introduce the following definitions:

Definition 1.1

(Cyclic and nontransitive tuples)
.

Let
n
be an integer with
n\geq 3
.

(i)

An
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
is called

cyclic
if there exist independent random variables

U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
with
P(U_{i}=U_{j})=0
for
i\not=j

such that

(1.8)

P(U_{i+1}>U_{i})=x_{i}\quad(i=1,\dots,n-1)\text{ and }P(U_{1}>U_{n})=x_{n}.

(ii)

An
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
is called

nontransitive
if it is cyclic and satisfies

x_{i}>1/2
for all
i
.

The dice examples (
1.1
) and (
1.3
)
show that the
4
-tuple
(2/3,2/3,2/3,2/3)
and the triple
(5/9,5/9,5/9)

are nontransitive and, hence, also cyclic. The
n
-tuple

(1/2,1/2,\dots,1/2)
is cyclic for any
n\geq 3
as can be seen by
taking
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
to be independent random variables with a continuous
common distribution.

On the other hand, the tuple
(1,1,\dots,1)
is not cyclic.
Indeed, otherwise there would exist random variables
U_{i}
such that,
with probability
1
, both
U_{1}<U_{2}<\dots<U_{n}
and
U_{n}<U_{1}
hold.
But the first of these relations implies that
U_{1}<U_{n}
holds with
probability
1
, contradicting the second relation. A similar
contradiction arises whenever the components of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
are sufficiently
close to
1
.

The first non-trivial case of
Definition
1.1

is the case
n=3
, i.e., the case of cyclic

triples
. For this case, Trybula
[
21
]
and, independently,
Suck
[
19
]
,
gave necessary and sufficient conditions for a triple
(x,y,z)
to be cyclic
(see Lemma
2.3
below). For
n\geq 4

a complete characterization of cyclic
n
-tuples is not known, though
some partial results are known (see, for example, Trybula

[
22
]
).

In this paper we consider the question of how common cyclic and
nontransitive tuples are among all tuples in the
n
-dimensional unit
cube
[0,1]^{n}
. Let

p_{n}
(resp.
p_{n}^{*}
) be the probability
that an
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
chosen randomly and
uniformly from
[0,1]^{n}
is cyclic (resp. nontransitive). These probabilities
are given by the volumes of the regions
C_{n}
(resp.
\operatorname{C}_{n}^{*}
)
of cyclic (resp. nontransitive)
n
-tuples
inside the unit cube
[0,1]^{n}
; that is, we have

(1.9)

p_{n}=\operatorname{vol}(C_{n}),\quad p_{n}^{*}=\operatorname{vol}(\operatorname{C}_{n}^{*}),

where

(1.10)

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}:\text{ $(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})$ is cyclic}\},

(1.11)

\displaystyle\operatorname{C}_{n}^{*}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}:\text{ $(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})$ is nontransitive}\}.

In our first theorem, we determine the probabilities
p_{3}
and

p_{3}^{*}
exactly.

Theorem 1
.

(i)

A random triple
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
in
[0,1]^{3}
is cyclic with probability

(1.12)

\displaystyle p_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{11\sqrt{5}}{4}-\frac{17}{4}-6\ln(\sqrt{5}-1)\approx 0.627575\dots.

(ii)

A random triple
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
in
[0,1]^{3}
is nontransitive with probability

(1.13)

\displaystyle p_{3}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{11\sqrt{5}}{8}-\frac{43}{16}-3\ln(\sqrt{5}-1)+\frac{3\ln 2}{8}\approx 0.011217\dots

In particular, the theorem shows that a random triple in
[0,1]^{3}
is
more likely than not to be cyclic, while only about 1% of
all such triples are nontransitive.

In our second theorem, we consider the case of general
n\geq 4
.
We derive upper and lower bounds for the probabilities
p_{n}
that
show that
p_{n}
converges to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 2
.

For any integer
n\geq 4
the probability
p_{n}
that a random
n
-tuple

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
is cyclic satisfies

(1.14)

1-3\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n}\leq p_{n}\leq 1-2\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n}.

In particular,
p_{n}
converges exponentially to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

In our final result we consider the distribution of the smallest,
middle, and largest elements of a cyclic triple. Let

(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
be a random vector that is
uniformly distributed over the region

C_{3}
of all cyclic triples, and let
(X_{1}^{*},X_{2}^{*},X_{3}^{*})
be the
order statistics of
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
. Thus,
X_{i}
is the
i
-th
coordinate of a random cyclic triple, while
X_{i}^{*}
is the
i
-th
smallest among the three coordinates of a random cyclic triple, where
“random” is to be interpreted with respect to the usual Lebesgue
measure on
C_{3}
.

In particular,
X_{1}^{*}
is the smallest element of a random cyclic
triple, and the triple
(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})
is nontransitive if and only
if
X_{1}^{*}>1/2
. Thus it is of interest to determine the precise
distribution of the random variable
X_{1}^{*}
. More generally, in the
following theorem we determine the density function
f_{i}(x)
for each of
the three random variables
X_{i}^{*}
,
i=1,2,3
.

.

Let
f_{1}(x)
,
f_{2}(x)
, and
f_{3}(x)
denote, respectively,

2
Auxiliary results

We begin by deriving some elementary properties of cyclic
n
-tuples.
Here, and in the remainder of the paper, we make the convention that
subscripts of
n
-tuples are to be interpreted modulo
n
.
Thus, for example, the definition (
1.8
) of a
cyclic
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
can be written more concisely as

P(U_{i+1}>U_{i})=x_{i}\quad(i=1,\dots,n).

Given a real number
t\in[0,1]
, we write

(2.1)

\overline{t}=1-t.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
be a cyclic
n
-tuple. Then:

(i)

Any cyclic permutation of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is cyclic; that is, for any

i\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, the tuple
(x_{i},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{i+n})

(with subscripts interpreted modulo
n
) is cyclic as well.

(ii)

The “reverse” tuple

(x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{1})
is cyclic.

(iii)

The “complementary” tuple
(\overline{x_{1}},\dots,\overline{x_{n}})
is cyclic.

Proof.

Part (i) of the lemma follows
immediately from the definition (
1.8
)
of cyclic
n
-tuples.

For parts (ii) and (iii) suppose
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
is a
cyclic
n
-tuple with associated
random variables
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
satisfying (
1.8
).
Setting
U_{i}^{*}=-U_{n+1-i}
we have

\displaystyle P(U_{i+1}^{*}>U_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle=P(-U_{n-i}>-U_{n+1-i})

\displaystyle=P(U_{n+1-i}>U_{n-i})=x_{n-i}\quad(i=0,1,\dots,n-1),

which shows that the tuple
(x_{n},x_{n-1},\dots,x_{1})
is cyclic.

Similarly, the fact that
(\overline{x_{1}},\dots,\overline{x_{n}})
is cyclic follows by
letting
U_{i}^{*}=-U_{i}
and observing that

\displaystyle P(U_{i+1}^{*}>U_{i}^{*})

\displaystyle=P(-U_{i+1}>-U_{i})

\displaystyle=P(U_{i}>U_{i+1})=1-x_{i}\quad(i=1,\dots,n).

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

If
(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
is a cyclic triple,
then so is any permutation of
(x,y,z)
.

Proof.

Suppose
(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
is cyclic. By part (i) of Lemma

2.1
the
cyclic
permutations
(y,z,x)
and

(z,x,y)
are also cyclic. By part (ii) the reverse triple
(z,y,x)

is cyclic. Applying part (i) again to the triple
(z,y,x)
,
we obtain that
(y,x,z)
and
(x,z,y)
are cyclic as well. Hence
all permutations of
(x,y,z)
are cyclic.
∎

The next result contains Trybula’s characterization of cyclic triples

[
21
]
. We state this characterization in the slightly
different—though equivalent—version given by Suck
[
19
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(Trybula
[
21
, Theorem 1]
;
Suck
[
19
, Theorems 2 and 3]
)
.

A triple
(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
is cyclic if and only if it
satisfies the following inequalities:

(2.2)

\displaystyle\min\left(x+yz,y+zx,z+xy\right)\leq 1,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\min\left(\overline{x}+\overline{y}\;\overline{z},\overline{y}+\overline{z}\;\overline{x},\overline{z}+\overline{x}\;\overline{y}\right)\leq 1.

The conditions (
2.2
) and (
2.3
)
in this characterization are rather unwieldy to work with directly.
However, by imposing additional constraints on the variables
x
,
y
,

z
, the conditions simplify significantly as the next lemma shows.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
.

(i)

If
x\leq y\leq z
, then the triple

(x,y,z)
is cyclic if and only if
it satisfies the following two inequalities:

(2.4)

\displaystyle x+yz

\displaystyle\leq 1,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\overline{z}+\overline{x}\,\overline{y}

\displaystyle\leq 1.

(ii)

If
x=\min(x,y,z)
and
y,z\geq 1/2
,
then
(x,y,z)
is cyclic
if and only if (
2.4
)
holds.

Proof.

We need to show that the conditions (
2.2
) and
(
2.3
) reduce to
(
2.4
)
and (
2.5
)
under the assumptions of part (i), and to
(
2.4
)
under the assumptions of part (ii).

Suppose first that
(x,y,z)
satisfies the conditions of part (i),
i.e., that
x\leq y\leq z
. Then

x(1-y)\leq z(1-y)
and
x(1-z)\leq y(1-z)
, and therefore

x+yz\leq z+xy
and
x+yz\leq y+zx
. Thus, the minimum
on the left of the inequality
(
2.2
) is equal to
x+yz
,
and the inequality therefore holds if and only if
x+yz\leq 1
,
Similarly, noting that
x\leq y\leq z
is equivalent to

\overline{z}\leq\overline{y}\leq\overline{x}
, we see that
condition (
2.3
) is
equivalent to
\overline{z}+\overline{x}\,\overline{y}\leq 1
.
This proves part (i) of the lemma.

Next, suppose that
y,z\geq 1/2
and
x=\min(x,y,z)
. Then
either
z=\max(x,y,z)
or
y=\max(x,y,z)
.
In the first case we have

x\leq y\leq z
, so part (i) of the lemma applies and
shows that the triple
(x,y,z)
is cyclic if and only if it satisfies
(
2.4
) and
(
2.5
).
But since
y,z\geq 1/2
,
we have
\overline{y},\overline{z}\leq 1/2
and
therefore
\overline{z}+\overline{x}\,\overline{y}\leq(1/2)+1\cdot(1/2)=1
.
Hence condition (
2.5
) holds
trivially, so
(x,y,z)
is cyclic if and only if
(
2.4
) holds.

In the case where we have
x\leq z\leq y
, we note that, by Lemma
2.2
,
(x,y,z)
is cyclic if and only if
(x,z,y)
is cyclic. Applying the above argument to
(x,z,y)
then yields the same conclusion. This completes the proof of part (ii).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1

By (
1.9
) we have
p_{3}=\operatorname{vol}(C_{3})
and

p_{3}^{*}=\operatorname{vol}(\operatorname{C}_{3}^{*})
, so computing these probabilities amounts
to computing the volumes of the regions
C_{3}
and
\operatorname{C}_{3}^{*}
of
cyclic and nontransitive triples.

We begin by using the symmetry properties established in Lemmas

2.1
and
2.2
to reduce
this computation to one involving simpler regions. Let

(3.1)

\displaystyle C_{3}^{(I)}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y,z)\in C_{3}:1/2<x\leq 1\text{ and }x\leq y,z\leq 1\},

By definition,
D_{n}^{*}
is the set of
n
-tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}

satisfying the inequalities (
4.4
)
and in addition
x_{1}=\min(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
. Under the latter condition
the last inequality in (
4.4
),

x_{n}+x_{1}<1
,
is implied by the second-last inequality,
x_{n-1}+x_{n}<1
, and thus can be
omitted. Moreover, the inequalities (
4.4
)
can only hold if
0\leq x_{1}<1/2
. Thus, the set
D_{n}^{*}
is the set of
tuples
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
satisfying

(4.14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &0\leq x_{1}<1/2\\
&x_{1}\leq x_{2}<1-x_{1}\\
&\vdots\\
&x_{1}\leq x_{n}<1-x_{n-1}\end{aligned}\right\}.

Hence we have

(4.15)

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(D_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}\int_{x_{1}}^{1-x_{1}}\int_{x_{1}}^{1-x_{2}}\cdots\int_{x_{1}}^{1-x_{n-1}}dx_{n}\cdots dx_{3}dx_{2}dx_{1}.

To evaluate the latter integral, we apply the change of variables

(4.16)

y_{0}=x_{1},\quad y_{i}=\frac{x_{i+1}-x_{1}}{1-2x_{1}}\quad(i=1,\dots,n-1).

It is easily checked that the transformation
(
4.16
)
has Jacobian determinant
\pm(1-2y_{0})^{n-1}
and maps the
region (
4.14
) to the
set of
n
-tuples
(y_{0},\dots,y_{n-1})
satisfying

(4.17)

0\leq y_{0}<1/2,\quad 0\leq y_{1}<1,\quad 0\leq y_{i}<1-y_{i-1}\quad(i=2,\dots,n-1).

It follows that

(4.18)

\displaystyle\operatorname{vol}(D_{n}^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1/2}(1-2y_{0})^{n-1}\;dy_{0}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1-y_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{1-y_{n-2}}dy_{n-1}\cdots dy_{2}dy_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1-y_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{1-y_{n-2}}dy_{n-1}\cdots dy_{2}dy_{1}=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{vol}(E_{n-1}),

where

\displaystyle E_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{n-1})\in[0,1]^{n-1}:0\leq y_{1}<1,0\leq y_{i}<1-y_{i-1}\quad(i=2,\dots,n-1)\Bigr\}.

Now set, for
i=1,\dots,n-1
,

(4.19)

u_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{i}&\text{if $i$ is odd,}\\
1-y_{i}&\text{if $i$ is even.}\end{cases}

The transformation (
4.19
)
has Jacobian determinant
\pm 1
and thus is volume-preserving.
Moreover, noting that the condition

y_{i}<1-y_{i-1}
is equivalent to
u_{i}<u_{i-1}
when
i
is odd, and to

u_{i-1}<u_{i}
when
i
is even, we see that this transformation
maps the region
E_{n-1}
to the region

E_{n-1}^{*}=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{n-1})\in[0,1]^{n-1}:u_{1}<u_{2}>u_{3}<\dots\}.

The set
E_{n-1}^{*}
is, up to a set of volume
0
,
the set of tuples in the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit cube
whose coordinates form an up-down alternating permutation of length
n-1
.
Since there are
(n-1)!
permutations of the coordinates
and, by symmetry, each such permutation contributes an amount

1/(n-1)!
to the volume of the unit cube, it follows that

(4.20)

\operatorname{vol}(E_{n-1})=\operatorname{vol}(E_{n-1}^{*})=\frac{A_{n-1}}{(n-1)!},

where
A_{n-1}
is the number of up-down alternating permutations of
length
n-1
.
Combining (
4.18
)
with (
4.20
) we obtain

\operatorname{vol}(D_{n}^{*})=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{vol}(E_{n-1})=\frac{1}{2n}\operatorname{vol}(E_{n-1}^{*})=\frac{A_{n-1}}{(2n)(n-1)!}.

This is the desired formula (
4.13
).
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

The number
A_{n}
of alternating permutations satisfies

(4.21)

\frac{A_{n}}{n!}\leq 3\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n+1}\quad(n\geq 1).

Proof.

André
[
1
, p. 23]

showed that, for any integer
m\geq 1
,

(4.22)

\displaystyle\frac{A_{2m}}{(2m)!}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2m+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{3^{2m+1}}+\frac{1}{5^{2m+1}}-\frac{1}{7^{2m+1}}+\dots\right),

(4.23)

\displaystyle\frac{A_{2m-1}}{(2m-1)!}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{2m}\left(1+\frac{1}{3^{2m}}+\frac{1}{5^{2m}}+\frac{1}{7^{2m}}+\dots\right).

The desired bound (
4.21
) follows as the series in
parentheses in (
4.22
) is bounded above by
1
, while
the series in (
4.23
) is at most

\leq 1+\sum_{n=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{2}}=-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}<\frac{3}{2}.\qed

Proof of Theorem
2
, lower bound.

Combining Lemmas
4.3
,
4.4
,
and
4.5

we obtain

\displaystyle p_{n}=\operatorname{vol}(C_{n})

\displaystyle\geq 1-2n\operatorname{vol}(D_{n}^{*})=1-2n\frac{A_{n-1}}{(2n)(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{A_{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\geq 1-3\left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{n},

which is the desired lower bound for
p_{n}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3

Let

(5.1)

C_{3}^{\text{o}}=\left\{(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}:(x,y,z)\text{ is cyclic and
$x\leq y\leq z$}\right\},

i.e.,
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
is the set of triples in
C_{3}
with nondecreasing
coordinates.

The following lemma characterizes the set
C_{3}^{\text{o}}

in terms of inequalities on
x
,
y
, and
z
. Recall (see (
3.10
)
and (
3.11
)) that
\omega=(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
is the
positive root of
\omega^{2}+\omega=1
.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have:

(i)

A triple
(x,y,z)
belongs to the set
C_{3}^{\text{o}}

if and only if it satisfies

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0\leq&x\leq\omega\\
x\leq&y\leq\sqrt{1-x}\\
\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))\leq&z\leq\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right)\end{aligned}\right\}.

(ii)

A triple
(x,y,z)
belongs
to the set
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
if and only if it satisfies

(5.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} 0\leq&y\leq 1\\
0\leq&x\leq\min\left(y,1-y^{2}\right)\\
\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))\leq&z\leq\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right)\end{aligned}\right\}.

Moreover, the intervals for the variables
x
,
y
, and
z
in
(
5.2
)
and (
5.3
) are always non-empty,
i.e., the upper bounds on these variables are always greater or equal to
the corresponding lower bounds.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.4
(i), a triple

(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
with
x\leq y\leq z
is cyclic if and only if it
satisfies the inequalities

(5.4)

x+yz\leq 1\text{ and }(1-z)+(1-y)(1-x)\leq 1.

Hence
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
is the set of triples
(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
satisfying

x\leq y\leq z
and (
5.4
).

Consider now a triple
(x,y,z)\in[0,1]^{3}
satisfying these conditions.
Then
x+x^{2}\leq x+yz\leq 1
, and hence
x\leq\omega
, with

\omega=(\sqrt{5}-1)/2
defined as above. Thus, we must have

(5.5)

0\leq x\leq\omega.

Next, note that
x+y^{2}\leq x+yz\leq 1
and hence
y\leq\sqrt{1-x}
.
Since we also have
x\leq y\leq z
, it follows that

(5.6)

x\leq y\leq\sqrt{1-x}.

Note that since
x\leq\omega
, with
\omega
being the positive root of

\omega^{2}+\omega=1
, we have
x^{2}+x\leq 1
and hence
x\leq\sqrt{1-x}
.
Thus, the interval for
y
in (
5.6
) is non-empty.

Next, we have trivially

(5.7)

0\leq y\leq 1,

while the inequalities
x\leq y\leq z
and
x+y^{2}\leq x+yz\leq 1

imply

(5.8)

0\leq x\leq\min(y,1-y^{2}).

Since
0\leq y\leq 1
,
the interval (
5.8
) is clearly non-empty.

Finally, the inequalities (
5.4
)
imply
z\leq(1-x)/y
and

z\geq(1-y)(1-x)
, which combined with
0\leq z\leq 1
and
x\leq y\leq z

yields

(5.9)

\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))\leq z\leq\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right).

Again the interval for
z
here is non-empty since, by
(
5.6
),
(1-x)/y\geq y
, while we also have

(1-x)/y\geq(1-x)\geq(1-x)(1-y)
,
1\geq y
, and
1\geq(1-x)(1-y)
which follow from
0\leq x,y\leq 1
.

Note that the inequalities
(
5.5
), (
5.6
), and (
5.9
)
are exactly those in the desired characterization
(
5.2
) of
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
,
while (
5.7
),
(
5.8
),
and (
5.9
)
are those in (
5.3
).
Thus, we
have shown that any triple
(x,y,z)\in C_{3}^{\text{o}}
must satisfy
(
5.2
)
and
(
5.3
)
and that
the intervals for
x
,
y
, and
z
in
(
5.2
)
and (
5.3
)
are all non-trivial.

Conversely, assume that
(x,y,z)
satisfies
(
5.2
) or
(
5.3
).
Then
0\leq x\leq y\leq z\leq 1
, and
z
must satisfy (
5.9
).
The upper bound for
z
in (
5.9
) implies

z\leq(1-x)/y
and hence
x+yz\leq 1
,
i.e., the first inequality in (
5.4
).
The lower bound for
z
in (
5.9
) implies

(1-x)(1-y)\leq z
and hence
(1-z)+(1-x)(1-y)\leq 1
, i.e., the second inequality in
(
5.4
).
Thus,
(x,y,z)
must be an element of
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Since, by Lemma
2.2
,
the “cyclic triple” property is invariant with respect to taking
permutations, we have

(5.10)

\operatorname{vol}(C_{3}^{\text{o}})=\frac{1}{3!}\operatorname{vol}(C_{3})=\frac{1}{6}p_{3}.

Moreover, the distribution of the order statistics
(X_{1}^{*},X_{2}^{*},X_{3}^{*})

of a random triple in
C_{3}
is the same as the distribution of
a random vector
(X,Y,Z)
that is distributed uniformly over the region

C_{3}^{\text{o}}
. It follows that the density functions
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
,
and
f_{3}
in Theorem
3
are the marginal densities of the
latter vector and thus can be obtained by integrating over appropriate
slices of the region
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
and dividing by the volume of this region.
That is, we have

(5.11)

f_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{vol}(C_{3}^{\text{o}})}\iint_{A_{1}(x)}1\,dz\,dy=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\iint_{A_{1}(x)}1\,dz\,dy,

where

(5.12)

A_{1}(x)=\{(y,z)\in[0,1]^{2}:(x,y,z)\in C_{3}^{\text{o}}\},

along with analogous formulas for
f_{2}
and
f_{3}
.

Using the first characterization of
C_{3}^{\text{o}}
in
Lemma
5.1
,
we see that

f_{1}(x)
is supported on the interval
[0,\omega]
, and that for each

x
with
0\leq x\leq\omega
we have

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\int_{x}^{\sqrt{1-x}}\int_{\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))}^{\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right)}\,dz\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\int_{x}^{\sqrt{1-x}}\left(\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right)-\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))\right)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(x^{3}-3x^{2}+\frac{1-x}{2-x}-(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right)&\text{if $0\leq x\leq 1-\omega$,}\\[2.15277pt]
\frac{1}{2}\left(x^{2}-3x+1-(1-x)\ln(1-x)\right)&\text{if $1-\omega\leq x\leq 1/2$,}\\[2.15277pt]
\frac{1}{2}\left(x^{2}+x-1+(1-x)\ln(1-x)-2(1-x)\ln x\right)&\text{if $1/2\leq x\leq\omega$.}\end{cases}

This yields the desired formula (
1.15
)
on noting that
\omega=(1-\sqrt{5})/2
and

(5.13)

1-\omega=1-\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}=\frac{3-\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Similarly, using the second characterization of
Lemma
5.1
,
we obtain

\displaystyle f_{2}(y)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\int_{0}^{\min\left(y,1-y^{2}\right)}\int_{\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))}^{\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right)}\,dz\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\int_{0}^{\min\left(y,1-y^{2}\right)}\left(\min\left(1,\frac{1-x}{y}\right)-\max(y,(1-x)(1-y))\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{p_{3}}\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\left(3y^{2}-y^{3}\right)&\text{if $0\leq y\leq 1-\omega$,}\\[2.15277pt]
3y-y^{2}-\frac{1}{2(1-y)}&\text{if $1-\omega\leq y\leq 1/2$,}\\[2.15277pt]
2-y-y^{2}-\frac{1}{2y}&\text{if $1/2\leq y\leq\omega$,}\\[2.15277pt]
\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{y^{3}}{2}-\frac{3y}{2}+1\right)&\text{if $\omega\leq y\leq 1$}\end{cases}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{3}{p_{3}}\left(3x^{2}-x^{3}\right)&\text{if $0\leq x\leq 1-\omega$,}\\[2.15277pt]
\frac{6}{p_{3}}\left(3x-x^{2}-\frac{1}{2(1-x)}\right)&\text{if $1-\omega<x\leq 1/2$,}\\[2.15277pt]
f_{2}(1-x)&\text{if $1/2<x\leq 1$,}\end{cases}

This yields the desired formula (
1.16
)
for
f_{2}(y)
.

Finally, since, by Lemma
2.1
(iii), a triple

(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
is cyclic if and only if
(1-x_{1},1-x_{2},1-x_{3})
is
cyclic, the distribution of
X_{3}^{*}
, the largest element in a cyclic
triple, is the same as that of
1-X_{1}^{*}
. Thus, we have
f_{3}(x)=f_{1}(1-x)
.

This completes the proof of Theorem
3
.

6
Concluding Remarks

In this section we mention some related questions and conjectures
suggested by our results.

Precise asymptotic behavior of
p_{n}
.

Theorem
2
implies that

e^{-(1+o(1))c_{1}n}\leq 1-p_{n}\leq e^{-(1+o(1))c_{2}n}\quad(n\to\infty)

holds with
c_{1}=\ln 4
and
c_{2}=\ln(\pi/2)
. It seems reasonable to
expect that there exists a
single
constant
c
, with
c_{2}\leq c\leq c_{1}
, such that

1-p_{n}=e^{-(1+o(1))cn}\quad(n\to\infty),

i.e., that the limit

c=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\ln(1-p_{n})}{n}

exits. If true, the value of
c
must lie between the constants

c_{2}=\ln(\pi/2)=0.451\dots
and
c_{1}=\ln 4=1.386\dots
.

Prescribing all pairwise probabilities
P(U_{i}>U_{j})
.

By definition, a cyclic
n
-tuple is an
n
-tuple of real numbers in

[0,1]
that can represent the
n
probabilities
P(U_{i+1}>U_{i})
,

i=1,\dots,n
, with a suitable choice of independent random variables

U_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, satisfying
P(U_{i}=U_{j})=0
for
i\not=j
.

As a natural extension of this concept, one can ask which arrays

x_{ij}
,
i,j=1,\dots,n
, can represent probabilities of the form

(6.1)

x_{ij}=P(U_{j}>U_{i})\quad(i,j=1,\dots,n),

under the same assumptions on the random variables
U_{i}
. This problem
arises in the theory of Social Choice, where it is known under the term

utility representations
; see Suck
[
19
]
for background
and references.

Obviously, in order for (
6.1
) to hold,
it is necessary that
x_{ii}=0
for all
i
. Moreover,
the assumption that
P(U_{i}=U_{j})=0
for
i\not=j
implies

x_{ij}=P(U_{j}>U_{i})=1-P(U_{i}>U_{j})=1-x_{ji}
for
i>j
. Thus the array

x_{ij}
is determined by the
\binom{n}{2}
values
x_{ij}
,
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Considering these values as tuples in the

\binom{n}{2}
-dimensional unit cube, one can then ask for the
probability that a
random
tuple in this cube has a representation
of the form (
6.1
).

When
n=3
, the values
x_{ij}
are determined by

x_{12}=P(U_{2}>U_{1})
,
x_{23}=P(U_{3}>U_{2})
, and

x_{13}=P(U_{3}>U_{1})=1-P(U_{1}>U_{3})
. Thus we see that a representation
(
6.1
) is possible if and only if the triple

(x_{12},x_{23},1-x_{13})
is cyclic, so the problem is equivalent to
the problem considered in Theorem
1
.

In the general case, however, the two problems are different. Indeed,
the question of characterizing arrays
x_{ij}
that have
representations of the form (
6.1
) seems to be an
extraordinarily hard problem that remains largely unsolved; we refer to Suck

[
19
]
for further discussion and some partial results.

Representations with dependent random variables
U_{i}
.

In our definition of cyclic tuples we assumed the random
variables
U_{i}
to be independent. This is a natural assumption that is made in most of the mathematical literature on nontransitivity phenomena. In the context of dice rolls the assumption reflects the fact that if
n
dice are rolled, then the values showing on the faces of these dice are indeed independent.

However, there also exist well-known
nontransitivity paradoxes—especially in voting theory and social choice theory—in which the underlying random variables are dependent. Thus, it would be of interest to study the analogs of cyclic and nontransitive tuples if the
independence assumption on
U_{i}
is dropped. We refer to Marengo et al.
[
15
]
for some recent results in this direction, and
to Suck
[
19
]

for an in-depth discussion of the differences between dependent and
independent representations of the form (
6.1
).
