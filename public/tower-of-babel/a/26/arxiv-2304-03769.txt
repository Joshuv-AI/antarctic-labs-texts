Discrete-to-Continuum Limits of Long-Range Electrical Interactions in Nanostructures Thanks: August 9, 2026
Prashant K. Jha, Timothy Breitzman, Kaushik Dayal

Abstract
We consider electrostatic interactions in two classes of nanostructures embedded in a three dimensional space: (1) helical nanotubes, and (2) thin films with uniform bending (i.e., constant mean curvature). Starting from the atomic scale with a discrete distribution of dipoles, we obtain the continuum limit of the electrostatic energy; the continuum energy depends on the geometric parameters that define the nanostructure, such as the pitch and twist of the helical nanotubes and the curvature of the thin film. We find that the limiting energy is local in nature. This can be rationalized by noticing that the decay of the dipole kernel is sufficiently fast when the lattice sums run over one and two dimensions, and is also consistent with prior work on dimension reduction of continuum micromagnetic bodies to the thin film limit. However, an interesting contrast between the discrete-to-continuum approach and the continuum dimension reduction approaches is that the limit energy in the latter depends only on the normal component of the dipole field, whereas in the discrete-to-continuum approach, both tangential and normal components of the dipole field contribute to the limit energy.

Introduction

Electrical and magnetic interactions are long-range; that is, a charge or dipole interacts with all the other charges and dipoles in the system, and the interactions cannot be truncated because the decay with distance is slow
[
Tou56
,
Bro63
,
JM94
,
MD14
]
.
We consider such electrostatic interactions in nanostructures, specifically helical geometries and thin films with uniform bending, in a three-dimensional ambient space.
These geometries are ubiquitous in nanotechnology; while not periodic, their structure has significant symmetry that we exploit in this paper, using the framework of Objective Structures
[
Jam06
]
.
We exploit this symmetry to adapt periodic calculations of the continuum energy to the setting of these nanostructures.
Specifically, starting from a discrete atomic-scale description of the electrostatic energy, we find the limit energy when the discrete lengthscale of the nanostructures goes to zero.

For simplicity and clarity, we assume in this paper that the charge density can be approximated as composed of discrete dipoles.
The electrostatic energy of such a system is the sum of all pairwise dipole-dipole interactions.
Unlike short-range bonded atomic interactions that typically scale as
\displaystyle r^{-6}
with distance
\displaystyle r
, the dipole-dipole interactions decay slowly with distance as
\displaystyle r^{-3}
.
Consequently, we cannot simply truncate after a few neighbors, and naive truncation can lead to qualitatively incorrect results in numerical calculations
[
MD14
,
GD20a
,
GD20b
]
.
While we use the setting of discrete electrical dipoles, the setting of magnetic dipoles has an identical mathematical structure and physical interpretation
[
Bro63
,
JM94
,
MS02
,
SS09
]
, and we borrow key ideas from that literature.
A key physical distinction between the electrical and magnetic situations is the possibility of electrical monopoles that does not exist for magnetic case, but we examine this elsewhere
[
SWB+21
]
and assume here that there are no free charges.
Further, we highlight that the assumption of discrete dipoles is not very restrictive.
Following the approach of
[
JM94
]
, we use a background field in our calculations, and this field enables a straightforward generalization to the more realistic setting of a general charge density field; such an approach was used by
[
Xia05
]
to study charge density fields in periodic crystals.

We turn to the question of dealing with the non-periodic geometry of the nanostructures.
While neither helices nor thin films with curvature are periodic, the framework of Objective Structures (OS) introduced in
[
Jam06
]
provides a powerful approach to deal with such geometries.
In brief, OS provides a group-theoretic description of these nanostructures that enables a parallel to be made with periodic lattices.
This parallel to periodic lattices has enabled the adaptation of various methods developed for lattices to the setting of helices and thin-films, e.g.
[
DJ07
,
HTJ12
,
ADE13a
,
ADE13b
]
.
Our strategy in this work is to use the OS framework to adapt continuum limit calculations from the setting of periodic lattices to the setting of nanostructures.

Our work is focused on obtaining discrete-to-continuum limits of the energy.
This multiscale approach has proven very powerful in enabling the systematic reduction of the very large number of degrees of freedom associated with the discrete problem to a much more tractable continuum problem.
This overall idea has played an important role in developing models, often in conjunction with variational tools such as
\displaystyle\Gamma
-convergence, both for bulk crystals
[
BLBL02
,
BLBL07
,
Sch09
,
ACG08
,
ACG11
,
BBC20
]
as well as for thin films and rods
[
Sch06
,
ALP18
]
.
Further, these ideas have played a role in the development of numerical multiscale atomistic methods such as the quasicontinuum method
[
TOP96
,
MT02
,
TM11
,
KO01
,
LLO12
,
DLO10
]
.
There is also a significant literature on simultaneous dimension reduction and discrete-to-continuum limits, e.g.
[
ABC08
,
ALP18
,
FJ00
,
LPS15
,
LPS17
,
Sch08
]
, but these consider interactions that decay much faster than electrostatic interactions.

All the work in the previous paragraph is restricted to the setting of short-range bonded atomic interactions.
In the context of electrical and magnetic interactions, the calculation of continuum limit energies based on discrete-to-continuum approaches have been examined both formally and rigorously using discrete dipoles on a periodic 3-d lattice
[
Tou56
,
Bro63
,
JM94
,
MS02
,
SS09
,
Sch05
]
.
Further, this has been examined formally for periodic charge distributions, also in 3-d,
[
MD14
]
.
All of these works show that the continuum limit energy consists of a local part and a nonlocal part.
In contrast, in this work, we consider topologically low-dimensional structures embedded in a 3-d space: a 1-d helical nanotube and a 2-d thin film with constant bending curvature.
In the limit that the discrete lengthscale characterizing the nanotube and thin film goes to
\displaystyle 0
, we find that the limit continuum energy is entirely local.
In
[
CDZ09
]
, they obtain the discrete-to-continuum limit of an energy that includes dipole-dipole interactions in 2-d materials using
\displaystyle\Gamma-
convergence, but they use a dipole kernel that goes as
\displaystyle 1/r^{2}
. In contrast, in this work, while the structures are low dimensional, they are embedded in 3-d, and, therefore, the dipole field kernel has a
\displaystyle 1/r^{3}
singularity.

The absence of nonlocality in the limit can be rationalized by observing that the decay of the interactions as
\displaystyle r^{-3}
is sufficiently fast to enable us to obtain a local limit if summed over a (topologically) 1-d or 2-d object.
We highlight a complementary body of work that applies dimension reduction techniques to go from a 3-d continuum to a 2-d or 1-d continuum.
In the context of electrical and magnetic interactions,
[
GJ97
]
and subsequent works
[
Car01
,
KS05
,
KSZ15
]
(for thin films) and
[
GH15
,
CH15
]
(for thin wires)
find, as we do, that the limit energy is not nonlocal.

The techniques employed in this work are broadly based on the rigorous results provided in
[
JM94
]
on the continuum limit of magnetic dipole interactions on a 3-d lattice, with appropriate generalizations and modifications for our setting.
The overall strategy of
[
JM94
]
is as follows.
First, the operator that associates the discrete dipole lattice to the generated electric field is shown to be bounded for smooth test functions; next, the pointwise limit of the action of the operator on smooth test functions is obtained; and, finally, using the boundedness of the operator and the density of the test functions, the limit of the energy density is obtained.
For the helical and thin film nanostructures considered in this work, we adapt this strategy to account for the fact that the lattice sites and dipoles are not related by a translation transformation, but by a more general isometric transformation.

The key results of this work are as follows.
First, the limit energy is rigorously derived and found to be local.
Second, the limiting energy density depends on the macroscopic geometric parameters, such as the pitch, radius and so on for the helical nanotube, and on the stretch and curvature for the thin film.
These parameters can be related to macroscopic measures of deformation, and link the macroscopic deformation to the small-scale structure.
Third, while the limiting energy is local, there are energetic contributions from both the normal and the tangential components of the dipole field.
This is in contrast to the result obtained by dimension reduction from a 3-d continuum:
in these approaches, there are no energetic contributions from the tangential component of the dipole field.
Those approaches have 3-d continuum theory as their starting point, and are valid for situations in which the limiting thin object has all dimensions much larger than the atomic lengthscale.
In contrast, the discrete-to-continuum approach used here is appropriate for nanostructures in which the thin dimensions are comparable to the atomic lengthscale.

3
Results on Continuum Limits of the Electrostatic Energy

We consider two classes of nanostructures: helical nanotubes and thin films, the latter allowing for a constant bending curvature (i.e., nonzero constant mean curvature and zero Gauss curvature), and obtain the corresponding continuum limit electrostatic energy.
In both cases, we start with discrete dipoles, where the discreteness is parametrized by the scale
\displaystyle\lambda>0
, and examine the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
.
We show that the dipole-dipole interaction energy density – per unit cross-sectional area in the case of nanotubes, and per unit thickness in the case of films – converges to a local energy density in the limit.

3.1.3
Limit of the Electrostatic Energy

In this section, we obtain the limit of the energy per unit surface area
\displaystyle e_{\lambda}
as
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
assuming that the background dipole field density
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}
(or equivalently the dipole moment density
\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\lambda}
) converges to some density field
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}
. The idea is to first show that the map
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
in (
15
) is bounded and obtain its limit. With that, the limit of
\displaystyle e_{\lambda}
follows.

Limit of the Discrete Electric Field

Let
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}
be the map with kernel
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}
. For any function
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we have

(T_{1,\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(s)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime},s){\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}.

(19)

We have the following main result on the map
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
.

Proposition 2
.

The maps
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}
and
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
are bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
for all
\displaystyle\lambda>0
and satisfy

\left\lVert T_{\lambda}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2},L^{2})}=\left\lVert T_{1,\lambda}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}(L^{2},L^{2})}.

(20)

Further, for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(s)\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}-h_{0}(\boldsymbol{I}-3\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s)){\mathbfit f}(s)=-h_{0}(\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}(s)-2\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s)){\mathbfit f}(s)

pointwise, where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}(s)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s)
are projection tensors that project onto the normal plane and the tangent line to the helix respectively (see (
8
)). Further,
\displaystyle h_{0}
is a constant given by

h_{0}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\mathbb{Z},\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{4\pi|v|^{3}(\theta^{2}+\delta^{2})^{3/2}}.

(21)

We provide the proof of
Proposition
2
in
subsection 4.1
.

Limit of the Energy

Theorem 3
.

Let
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a sequence of functions for
\displaystyle\lambda>0
with
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
such that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}{\mathbfit f}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}
. Let the system of dipole moments
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{d}_{\lambda}\colon{\cal L}_{\lambda}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be given by (
12
). Then

e_{\lambda}\xrightarrow[\lambda\to 0]{}\dfrac{1}{2}h_{0}\left[||\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}{\mathbfit f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}-2||\boldsymbol{P}_{||}{\mathbfit f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right],

where
\displaystyle h_{0}
is the constant defined in (
21
).

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
is bounded and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}\to{\mathbfit f}
, we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda})=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})+\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}-{\mathbfit f}))=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}),

where the decomposition of the limit into the sum of individual limits is true if the two limits,
\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}-{\mathbfit f}))
, individually exist.
This is indeed true: because
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
is bounded and
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}\to{\mathbfit f}
in
\displaystyle L^{2}
, we have that
\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}-{\mathbfit f}))=0
.
Then, to show that
\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})
exist for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, we proceed as follows.

Let
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}^{k}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a sequence of functions such that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}^{k}\to{\mathbfit f}
. Using
Proposition
2
, we have

\begin{split}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}^{k})+\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f}-{\mathbfit f}^{k}))=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}^{k})\\
&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left(\boldsymbol{H}_{0}{\mathbfit f}^{k}\right)=\boldsymbol{H}_{0}{\mathbfit f},\end{split}

(22)

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{0}=\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s)=-h_{0}(\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}(s)-2\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s))
(see
Proposition
2
).

Using the expression in (
14
) for
\displaystyle e_{\lambda}
, we have

\begin{split}e_{\lambda}&=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left({{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}},{T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\left({{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}-{\mathbfit f}},{T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left({{\mathbfit f}},{T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right]\\
&=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left[\left({{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}-{\mathbfit f}},{T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left({{\mathbfit f}},{T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}+\left({{\mathbfit f}},{T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}-{\mathbfit f})}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right].\end{split}

(23)

The first and third terms are zero in the limit. Taking the limit of the remaining term and using (
22
), we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}e_{\lambda}=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}-\dfrac{1}{2}\left({{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}},{T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}_{\lambda}}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}=\dfrac{1}{2}h_{0}\left[||\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}{\mathbfit f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}-2||\boldsymbol{P}_{||}{\mathbfit f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}\right].

This completes the proof.

Remark 3.3
.

The limiting energy only comprises of a local self-field energy. In the limit, any point on the helix sees a uniform 1-d system of dipole moments along the tangent line. Further, we see that both the normal components and the tangential component of the dipole moment contribute to the energy and electric field.
This is in contrast to
[
CH15
]
, where the thin wire limit of the magnetostatic energy, obtained from dimensional reduction starting from a 3-d continuum, has contributions only from the normal component.
Heuristically, the dimension reduction starting from a 3-d continuum contains minimal information about the detailed atomic arrangements within the nanostructure, and hence does not capture the consequences of the helical geometry.
Because of its starting point in a 3-d continuum model, it is appropriate for thin objects that have all dimensions being much larger than the atomic lengthscale.
On the other hand, the discrete-to-continuum approach is appropriate for nanostructures wherein the thin dimension is comparable to the atomic lengthscale.

Remark 3.5
.

Note that, for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}
,

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\boldsymbol{s})=\boldsymbol{Q}(s_{1})\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\mathbf{0})\boldsymbol{Q}(-s_{1}).

(45)

\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{3}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{3}

However, it is consistent with the result for the helical nanostructure studied in this paper, in that the components of the dipole field aligned with the thin direction contribute to the limit energy.
As we argued there, the dimensional reduction from a 3-d continuum model is appropriate for thin films that have thickness that is much larger than the atomic lengthscale, whereas the discrete-to-continuum approach is appropriate for nanostructures that have thickness that is comparable to the atomic lengthscale.

\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})

\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
is dense in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, it follows that
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}
is also bounded in
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and extends as a bounded linear map from
\displaystyle C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
to
\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. This argument together with (
54
) completes the proof of boundedness of maps
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
and
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}
.
Now, it remains to show (
59
) for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Let
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
and proceed as follows:

\begin{split}||T_{1,\lambda}{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|(T_{1,\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(s)\right|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}s=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime},s){\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right)^{2}\ \mathrm{d}s\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)|\,|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,|{\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|\,\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)|\,|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,\left(\frac{|{\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})|^{2}}{2}+\frac{|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}}{2}\right)\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}s\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\frac{1}{2}2\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)|\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right)\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)\right]\ \mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq C_{3}\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}s}_{=:I},\end{split}

(60)

where, in the third line we have used that
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|\leq|{\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})|^{2}/2+|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}/2
; in the fourth line, we have used symmetry to extract a factor of
\displaystyle 2
; in the last line, we have used the bound (
58
).
Since
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, there exist
\displaystyle R>0
such that the support of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}
is a subset of
\displaystyle(-R,R)
. So, for any
\displaystyle\rho>0
, we have

\begin{split}I_{\rho}&:=\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}s=\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}\left[\int_{-R}^{R}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}s\\
&=\int_{-R}^{R}|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}\left(\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\ \mathrm{d}s\right)\ \mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\leq C_{3}||{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2},\end{split}

(61)

where we have applied Fubini’s theorem to switch the order of integration – this is allowed because
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(t^{\prime},s)|\,|{\mathbfit f}(t^{\prime})|^{2}
is integrable in
\displaystyle(s,t^{\prime})\in(-\rho,\rho)\times(-R,R)
– and then used (
58
).
Thus, we have shown that
\displaystyle I_{\rho}
is bounded, with the bound independent of
\displaystyle\rho
.
This, together with the fact that
\displaystyle I_{\rho}
is monotonically increasing with
\displaystyle\rho
, shows that
\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}I_{\rho}
exists and is equal to
\displaystyle I
, where
\displaystyle I
is defined in (
60
).
The limit
\displaystyle I
is also bounded, i.e.,
\displaystyle I\leq C_{3}||{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}
. Combining this observation with (
60
) completes the proof of (
59
).

4.1.2
Limit of the Map
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}

Let
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Write
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}
as:

T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f})(s)=\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s){\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}=\underbrace{\left[\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right]}_{=:\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(s)}{\mathbfit f}(s)+\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}.

(62)

The second term above is zero in the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
. To see this, we first obtain two useful inequalities. For any
\displaystyle R>0
, we have, using the bound on
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}|
from (
57
),

\begin{split}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq R\lambda}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)|\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\leq\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq R\lambda}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|s-s^{\prime}|^{3}/\lambda^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq R\lambda}\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}=\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}R\lambda\end{split}

(63)

and

\begin{split}&\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)|\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\leq\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+\left\lvert s/\lambda-s^{\prime}/\lambda\right\rvert^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}=\int_{\left\lvert t\right\rvert\geq R}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(64)

where the last equality in the equation above follows from the change of variables
\displaystyle t=(s^{\prime}-s)/\lambda
.
Next, noting that
\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)
, we obtain the following bound on the second term of (
62
):

\begin{split}&\left|\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|\\
&=\left|\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|\\
&=\left|\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda){\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}-\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}{\mathbfit f}(s)\right.\\
&\quad\left.+\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq R\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|\\
&\leq\left|\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda){\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|+\left|\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}{\mathbfit f}(s)\right|\\
&\quad+\left|\dfrac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq R\lambda}\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|\\
&\leq\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}^{\prime}(t)|\frac{R\lambda}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq R\lambda}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)|\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}+2\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}(t)|\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\left\lvert s-s^{\prime}\right\rvert\geq R\lambda}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(s^{\prime}/\lambda,s/\lambda)|\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\\
&\leq\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}^{\prime}(t)|\frac{C_{1}R^{2}}{C_{2}}\lambda+2\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}(t)|\int_{\left\lvert t\right\rvert\geq R}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(65)

where we have used the fact that
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{3})
, and therefore,
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})|\leq\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}(t)|
, and
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s)|\leq R\lambda\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}^{\prime}(t)|
for
\displaystyle s^{\prime}\in\{t:|s-t|\leq R\lambda\}
. We have also used the inequalities (
63
) and (
64
) in the last step.

We note that the final inequality in (
65
) is true for any
\displaystyle R>0
.
Further, the two terms on the right side have a limit as
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
– the second term clearly is independent of
\displaystyle\lambda
– for any
\displaystyle R>0
.
Therefore, the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
of the two terms individually is equal to the limit of the sum, keeping
\displaystyle R
fixed.
Thus, taking the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
in (
65
), we have

\begin{split}&\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\,\left|\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|\\
&\leq\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\left[\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}^{\prime}(t)|\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}R\lambda\right]+\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\left[2\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}(t)|\int_{\left\lvert t\right\rvert\geq R}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t\right]\\
&=2\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}(t)|\int_{\left\lvert t\right\rvert\geq R}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t,\end{split}

(66)

for any
\displaystyle R>0
.
Since the bound above is true for any
\displaystyle R>0
, and the left side is independent of
\displaystyle R
, we can take the limit
\displaystyle R\to\infty
, where the limit exists and is
\displaystyle 0
for the right side, to get

\begin{split}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\,\left|\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)({\mathbfit f}(s^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(s))\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}\right|\leq\lim_{R\to\infty}\,2\sup_{t}|{\mathbfit f}(t)|\int_{\left\lvert t\right\rvert\geq R}\dfrac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+\left\lvert t\right\rvert^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}t=0.\end{split}

(67)

Thus, we have from (
62
) that

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f})(s)=\left[\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(s)\right]{\mathbfit f}(s).

(68)

We next compute the limit of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(s)
. Fix
\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{R}
and suppose
\displaystyle a\in{\cal L}_{\lambda}
such that
\displaystyle s\in U_{\lambda}(a)
. Using the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(s^{\prime},s)
, we have

\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(s)=\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\boldsymbol{K}(s^{\prime},s)\ \mathrm{d}s^{\prime}=\lambda^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in{\cal L}_{1},\\
u\neq a\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{K}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(u)-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(a))\int_{U_{\lambda}(u)}\ \mathrm{d}t=\lambda^{3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\lambda\mathbb{Z},\\
u\neq a\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{K}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(u)-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(a)).

(69)

From (
50
), we have
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(u)-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(a)=\boldsymbol{Q}(a\theta)((\boldsymbol{Q}((u-a)\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+(u-a)\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3})
. Using the identity
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{Q}^{T}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}(\lambda\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})/\lambda^{3}
, we get

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(s)&=\boldsymbol{Q}(a\theta)\left[\sum_{u\in\lambda\mathbb{Z},u\neq a}\boldsymbol{K}((\boldsymbol{Q}((u-a)\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+(u-a)\delta/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{3})\right]\boldsymbol{Q}(-a\theta)\\
&=\boldsymbol{Q}(a\theta)\left[\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z},i\neq 0}\boldsymbol{K}((\boldsymbol{Q}(i\lambda\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3})\right]\boldsymbol{Q}(-a\theta),\end{split}

(70)

where we have changed variables
\displaystyle i=(u-a)/\lambda
. Note that
\displaystyle a\in\lambda\mathbb{Z}
, and, therefore,
\displaystyle(u-a)\in\lambda\mathbb{Z}
for
\displaystyle u\in\lambda\mathbb{Z}
, which implies
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\displaystyle s
is related to
\displaystyle a
by
\displaystyle s\in U_{\lambda}(a)
, we have
\displaystyle a\to s
in the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
. Therefore, we get

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s):=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(s)=\boldsymbol{Q}(s\theta)\left[\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\boldsymbol{K}((\boldsymbol{Q}(i\lambda\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3})\right]\boldsymbol{Q}(-s\theta).

To take the limit inside the summation, we show that the sum is absolutely convergent for all
\displaystyle\lambda>0
as follows:

\begin{split}a_{\lambda}&:=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\left|\boldsymbol{K}\left((\boldsymbol{Q}(i\lambda\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right|\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\dfrac{c}{\left|(\boldsymbol{Q}(i\lambda\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3})\right|^{3}}\\
&=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\dfrac{c}{(4\sin^{2}(i\lambda\theta/2)/\lambda^{2}+i^{2}\delta^{2})^{3/2}}\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\dfrac{c}{|i|^{3}}<\infty,\qquad\forall\lambda>0.\end{split}

(71)

Now, we can write

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s)=\boldsymbol{Q}(s\theta)\left[\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\boldsymbol{K}\left(\dfrac{\boldsymbol{Q}(i\lambda\theta)-\boldsymbol{I}}{i\lambda\theta}(i\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{1})+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right]\boldsymbol{Q}(-s\theta).

Note that for a fixed
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{Z}

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\dfrac{(\boldsymbol{Q}(i\lambda\theta)-\boldsymbol{I})}{i\lambda\theta}i\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}=\lim_{h=i\lambda\theta\to 0}\dfrac{(\boldsymbol{Q}(h)-\boldsymbol{I})}{h}i\theta\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}=i\theta\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3},

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)=\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x\boldsymbol{Q}(x)|_{x=0}
. Now, using the equation above, and the fact that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})
is smooth away from
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}=\mathbf{0}
(which is ensured in the summation), we get

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s)=\boldsymbol{Q}(s\theta)\left[\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\boldsymbol{K}\left(i\theta\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right]\boldsymbol{Q}(-s\theta)=\boldsymbol{Q}(s\theta)\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(0)\boldsymbol{Q}(-s\theta).

Combining this with (
68
), we get

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}T_{\lambda}({\mathbfit f})(s)=\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s){\mathbfit f}(s)=\boldsymbol{Q}(s\theta)\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(0)\boldsymbol{Q}(-s\theta){\mathbfit f}(s).

Next, we simplify
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s)
. Using
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{Q}^{T}=\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{x})
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}(s\theta)\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)=\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(s\theta)
, we can show

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\boldsymbol{K}(i\theta\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(s\theta)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+i\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3})=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\boldsymbol{K}\left(i\;|\boldsymbol{t}(s)|\;\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}(s)\right),

(72)

where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}(s)=\theta\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(s\theta)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}
is the tangent vector, and
\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}(s)=\boldsymbol{t}(s)/|\boldsymbol{t}(s)|
with
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{t}(s)|=\sqrt{\theta^{2}+\delta^{2}}
.
In (
72
), by noting the definition of the dipole field kernel
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}
, it is easy to show that

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(s)=-h_{0}\left[\boldsymbol{I}-3\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}(s)\mathbf{\otimes}\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}(s)\right]=-h_{0}\left[\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}{\mathbfit f}(s)-2\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s)\right],

with
\displaystyle h_{0}
defined as

h_{0}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}}\frac{1}{4\pi|i|^{3}(\theta^{2}+\delta^{2})^{3/2}}

(73)

and projection tensors
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s)=\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}(s)\mathbf{\otimes}\hat{\boldsymbol{t}}(s)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}(s)=\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{P}_{||}(s)
. This finishes the proof of
Proposition
2
.

4.2.1
Boundedness

Let
\displaystyle S_{\lambda}\colon L^{2}({\cal S},\mathbb{R}^{3})\to L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})
be a map such that, for any
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in L^{2}({\cal S},\mathbb{R}^{3})

(S_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(\boldsymbol{s})=\lambda{\mathbfit f}(\lambda\boldsymbol{s}),\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}.

It is easy to see that
\displaystyle S_{\lambda}
is an isometry. The inverse of
\displaystyle S_{\lambda}
is given by

(S^{-1}_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(\boldsymbol{s})=\lambda^{-1}{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}/\lambda),\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}.

Following the similar steps in
subsubsection 4.1.1
, we can show that

||T_{\lambda}||_{{\cal L}(L^{2},L^{2})}=||T_{1,\lambda}||_{{\cal L}(L^{2},L^{2})}.

Thus, to show that
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}
is a bounded map, it is sufficient to show that
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}
is bounded. Towards that goal, we first establish that

|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|^{3}},\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}_{1,\lambda},

(78)

where
\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2}
are constants that may depend on the parameters
\displaystyle R,\theta,\delta
defining the surface
\displaystyle{\cal S}
, but are independent of
\displaystyle\lambda
.

To show (
78
), we recall that
\displaystyle\bar{\theta}
is the fixed angular extent of the film and satisfies the bound
\displaystyle 0<\bar{\theta}<\pi/2
(in fact we restrict it such that
\displaystyle 0<\bar{\theta}<\pi/4
). Let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}
be any two generic points, and let
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}\in{\cal L}_{1,\lambda}
be such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in U_{1}(\boldsymbol{a})
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in U_{1}(\boldsymbol{b})
. We refer to
subsubsection 3.2.1
and
subsubsection 3.2.2
for the notation appearing in this section.

First, consider
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
such that
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\min\{\theta_{l},1\}
. For this case, we have
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{b}
. Noting that
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{I}-3(\boldsymbol{x}/|\boldsymbol{x}|)\mathbf{\otimes}(\boldsymbol{x}/|\boldsymbol{x}|)|=\sqrt{6}
,
\displaystyle\forall\boldsymbol{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
,
we have

\begin{split}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|&\leq\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\pi|\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{a})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{A}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}|^{3}}\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\pi|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|^{3}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|^{3}}{|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}|^{3}}\frac{|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}|^{3}}{|\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{a})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{A}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}|^{3}}\\
&\leq\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\pi|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|^{3}}c_{L}^{3}\frac{1}{c_{A}^{3}},\end{split}

(79)

where we have used the bounds (
74
) and (
75
).

Next, we consider the case when
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|<\min\{\theta_{l},1\}
. This can be further divided in two cases:

•

Case 1
:
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{b}
which implies
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|=0
.

•

Case 2
:
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{b}
. For this case, we have

|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\pi|\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{a})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{b})+\boldsymbol{A}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}|^{3}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\pi c_{A}^{3}|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}|^{3}}.

(80)

Note that when
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\neq\boldsymbol{b}
, we can have either
\displaystyle a_{1}=b_{1},a_{2}=b_{2}\pm 1
; or
\displaystyle a_{1}=b_{1}\pm\theta_{l},a_{2}=b_{2}
; or
\displaystyle a_{1}=b_{1}\pm\theta_{l},a_{2}=b_{2}\pm 1
. For all of these cases, the denominator in (
80
) is bounded from below because
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{a}-\boldsymbol{b}|\geq\min\{\theta_{l},1\}
.
Thus, we have

|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{\sqrt{6}}{4\pi c_{A}^{3}(\min\{\theta_{l},1\})^{3}}.

(81)

In summary (
81
) holds for any
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
such that
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|<\min\{\theta_{l},1\}
.

Combining the bound for the case
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|<\min\{\theta_{l},1\}
with the bound for the case
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\min\{\theta_{l},1\}
, we can write

|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|^{3}},

(82)

where we have renamed the constants for convenience. This completes the proof of (
78
).

Next, we show
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}
is a bounded map on
\displaystyle L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Since
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}
satisfies (
78
), it can be shown that

\frac{{\cal R}}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\leq C_{3},\quad\frac{{\cal R}}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}\leq C_{3},

(83)

for some fixed
\displaystyle C_{3}<\infty
independent of
\displaystyle\lambda
. Following the steps in obtaining inequality (
60
), it is easy to obtain, for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

||T_{1,\lambda}{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}\leq C_{3}\underbrace{\int_{{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}}\left[\frac{{\cal R}}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\,|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}_{=:I}.

(84)

Let
\displaystyle\rho>0
, and let
\displaystyle I_{\rho}
is defined as

I_{\rho}:=\int_{B_{2}(\mathbf{0},\rho)\cap{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}}\left[\frac{{\cal R}}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}_{1,\lambda}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\,|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime})|^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\right]\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s},

(85)

where
\displaystyle B_{2}(\mathbf{0},\rho)=\{(s_{1},s_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\sqrt{s_{1}^{2}+s_{2}^{2}}\leq\rho\}
is the two-dimensional ball of radius
\displaystyle\rho
centered at
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}
. Based on the arguments in the last paragraph of
subsubsection 4.1.1
, we find that
\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}I_{\rho}
exist and it is given by
\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to\infty}I_{\rho}=I
, where
\displaystyle I
is defined in (
84
), and that the limit
\displaystyle I
satisfies the bound
\displaystyle I\leq C_{3}||{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}
. Combining this with (
84
), we have shown that, for
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})
,

||T_{1,\lambda}{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})}\leq C_{3}||{\mathbfit f}||_{L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})}.

Arguing as in the case of the helix, the map
\displaystyle T_{1,\lambda}

is a bounded linear map on
\displaystyle L^{2}({\cal S}_{1,\lambda},\mathbb{R}^{3})
.

4.2.2
Limit of the Map
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}

Let
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}\in C^{\infty}_{0}({\cal S},\mathbb{R}^{3})
. We write
\displaystyle T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f}
as follows

\begin{split}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(\boldsymbol{s})&=\frac{{\cal R}\lambda}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s}){\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&=\underbrace{\left[\frac{{\cal R}\lambda}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\right]}_{=:\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})+\frac{{\cal R}\lambda}{\theta_{l}}\int_{{\cal S}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})({\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}))\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}.\end{split}

(86)

We next show that the second term in (
86
) is zero in the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
.
Fix
\displaystyle\bar{R}>0
,
then we have

\begin{split}I&:=\left|\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})({\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}))\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\right|\\
&\leq\left|\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})({\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}))\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\right|\\
&\quad+\left|\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\leq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})({\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}))\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\right|\\
&\leq\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\,|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})|\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&\quad+\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\leq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\,|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})|\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&\leq\left(2\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\underbrace{\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}_{=:I_{1}}\\
&\quad+\left(\bar{R}\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|\nabla{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\underbrace{\lambda^{2}\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\leq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}_{=:I_{2}},\end{split}

(87)

where, in the last step, we have used that
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})|\leq 2\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}}|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{t})|
, and, for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\in\{\boldsymbol{t}:|\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{s}|\leq\bar{R}\lambda\}
, we have
\displaystyle|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})|\leq\bar{R}\lambda\sup_{\boldsymbol{t}}|\nabla{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{t})|
.
To bound
\displaystyle I_{1}
and
\displaystyle I_{2}
, first observe that, due to
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})=\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}/\lambda,\boldsymbol{s}/\lambda)/\lambda^{3}
and the bound on
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}
in (
82
), there holds

|\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})|=\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}|\boldsymbol{K}_{1,\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}/\lambda,\boldsymbol{s}/\lambda)|\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s}|^{3}/\lambda^{3}},

(88)

and as a direct consequence, we have

\begin{split}I_{1}&\leq\lambda\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s}|^{3}/\lambda^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\geq\bar{R}\lambda}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s}|^{3}/\lambda^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&=\int_{|\boldsymbol{t}|\geq\bar{R}}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{t}|^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t},\end{split}

(89)

where, in the second step, the domain of integration was enlarged; and, in the final step, the change of variables
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s})/\lambda
(so that
\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}=\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}
) was introduced. Next, using the definition of
\displaystyle I_{2}
in (
87
) and (
88
), we get

\begin{split}I_{2}&\leq\lambda^{2}\frac{1}{\lambda^{3}}\int_{{\cal S}}\chi_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\leq\bar{R}\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s}|^{3}/\lambda^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{|\boldsymbol{s}-\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}|\leq\bar{R}\lambda}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s}|^{3}/\lambda^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\\
&\leq\lambda\int_{|\boldsymbol{t}|\leq\bar{R}}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{t}|^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}\\
&\leq\left[\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\pi\bar{R}^{2}\right]\lambda,\end{split}

(90)

where, in the second step, the domain of integration was enlarged; in the third step, the change of variables
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}=(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{s})/\lambda
was introduced; and, finally, in the last step,
\displaystyle C_{1}/(C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{t}|^{3})\leq C_{1}/C_{2}
was used. Combining the results of (
89
) and (
90
) with (
87
), we have shown that

\begin{split}I&\leq\left(2\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\,I_{1}+\left(\bar{R}\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|\nabla{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\,I_{2}\\
&\leq\left(2\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\,\int_{|\boldsymbol{t}|\geq\bar{R}}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{t}|^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}+\left(\bar{R}\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|\nabla{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\,\left[\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\pi\bar{R}^{2}\right]\lambda,\end{split}

(91)

for any
\displaystyle\bar{R}>0
. Arguing as in the case of helix – see the discussion associated with (
67
) – we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}I\leq\left(2\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\,\int_{|\boldsymbol{t}|\geq\bar{R}}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{t}|^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t},

for any
\displaystyle\bar{R}>0
. We can take the limit
\displaystyle\bar{R}\to\infty
of both sides above since the inequality is valid for any
\displaystyle\bar{R}>0
, and the left side is independent of
\displaystyle\bar{R}
and the limit of
\displaystyle\bar{R}\to\infty
of the right side is well-defined and equal to
\displaystyle 0
. We have therefore shown

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}I=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\left|\lambda\int_{{\cal S}}\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{s})({\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})-{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}))\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}\right|\leq\lim_{\bar{R}\to\infty}\left[\left(2\sup_{\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}}|{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime})|\right)\,\int_{|\boldsymbol{t}|\geq\bar{R}}\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}+|\boldsymbol{t}|^{3}}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{t}\right]=0.

Thus, from (
86
), we have

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}(T_{\lambda}{\mathbfit f})(\boldsymbol{s})=\left[\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})\right]{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s}).

(92)

Limit of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}

Consider a typical
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}
such that
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in U_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{a})
where
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a}\in{\cal L}_{\lambda}
.
Recall that
\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\lambda}
is the lattice for
\displaystyle\lambda>0
and
\displaystyle U_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{a})=[a_{1},a_{1}+\theta_{l}\lambda)\times[a_{2},a_{2}+\lambda)
is the lattice cell. In the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}
, we substitute
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}_{\lambda}
, to get

\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})=\frac{{\cal R}\lambda}{\theta_{l}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in{\cal L}_{\lambda},\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{K}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{u})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{a}))\int_{U_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{u})}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}^{\prime}={\cal R}\lambda^{3}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in{\cal L}_{\lambda},\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{K}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{u})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{a})).

(93)

Substituting the definition of transformation
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
in (
24
), we can show for
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{u}\in{\cal L}_{\lambda}
that

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{u})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{a})=\boldsymbol{Q}(a_{1}\theta)\left[{\cal R}(\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+(u_{2}-a_{2})\delta\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right].

Using the identities
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{Q}(t)\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{Q}(t)\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{Q}^{T}(t)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}(\lambda\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x})/\lambda^{3}
, from (
93
), we have

\boldsymbol{H}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})={\cal R}\boldsymbol{Q}(a_{1})\underbrace{\left[\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in{\cal L}_{\lambda},\boldsymbol{u}\neq\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}(\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I})/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+(u_{2}-a_{2})\delta/\lambda\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right]}_{=:\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})}\boldsymbol{Q}(-a_{1}).

(94)

We analyze
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}
as follows. First, we expand the sum
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\in{\cal L}_{\lambda}

\begin{split}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})&=\sum_{u_{2}\in\lambda\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta}),\\
(u_{1},u_{2})\neq\boldsymbol{a}\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta\frac{u_{2}-a_{2}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right]\\
&=\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta}),\\
(u_{1},t_{2}^{\prime})\neq(a_{1},0)\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right],\end{split}

(95)

where we introduced the new variable
\displaystyle t_{2}^{\prime}=(u_{2}-a_{2})/\lambda
. Since
\displaystyle u_{2},a_{2}\in\lambda\mathbb{Z}
, we have
\displaystyle t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Using a Taylor expansion and the mean value theorem, we have the identity

\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I}=\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(\xi)(u_{1}-a_{1}),

(96)

where
\displaystyle\xi=\xi(u_{1}-a_{1})\in(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta})
depends on
\displaystyle u_{1}-a_{1}
.
Formula (
96
) suggests that we decompose (
95
) as:

\begin{split}\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{s})=&\underbrace{\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta}),\\
(u_{1},t_{2}^{\prime})\neq(a_{1},0)\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\frac{u_{1}-a_{1}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right]}_{=:\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{s})}\\
&+\underbrace{\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta}),\\
(u_{1},t_{2}^{\prime})\neq(a_{1},0)\end{subarray}}\left\{\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)-\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\frac{u_{1}-a_{1}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right\}\right]}_{=:\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{s})}.\end{split}

(97)

Step 1:
We show
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}^{(2)}
goes to zero in the limit
\displaystyle\lambda\to 0
. Let

\boldsymbol{x}_{1}={\cal R}\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{x}_{2}={\cal R}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\frac{u_{1}-a_{1}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\quad\boldsymbol{z}=\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}.

(98)

Consider a function
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
defined as

\boldsymbol{y}(r)=\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+r(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}).

(99)

Note that, since
\displaystyle(u_{1},t_{2}^{\prime})\neq(a_{1},0)
and
\displaystyle t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

|\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z}|\geq\min\{\delta,\min_{r\in[0,1],u_{1}\in(\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{a_{1}\})\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta})}|\boldsymbol{y}(r)|\}.

(100)

We show that
\displaystyle\min_{r\in[0,1],u_{1}\in(\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{a_{1}\})\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta})}|\boldsymbol{y}(r)|>0
and the lower bound is independent of
\displaystyle\lambda
. For convenience, let
\displaystyle t=u_{1}-a_{1}
. Since
\displaystyle u_{1},a_{1}\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta})
, and
\displaystyle u_{1}\neq a_{1}
, we have
\displaystyle t\in(\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{0\})\cap(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})
. The hypothesis of
Proposition
5
restricts
\displaystyle\bar{\theta}
such that

0<\bar{\theta}<\pi/4\quad\Rightarrow 0<\cos(2\bar{\theta})<1.

(101)

With
\displaystyle t=u_{1}-a_{1}
, writing out the action of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}(t)
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)
on
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
, we get

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{x}_{1}&={\cal R}\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}(t)-\boldsymbol{I}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=\frac{{\cal R}}{\lambda}[(\cos(t)-1)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\sin(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{2}],\\
\boldsymbol{x}_{2}&={\cal R}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\frac{t}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}=\frac{{\cal R}t}{\lambda}[-\sin(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\cos(t)\boldsymbol{e}_{2}].\end{split}

(102)

Through elementary calculations, we can show

|\boldsymbol{y}(r)|^{2}=|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+r(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})|^{2}=\frac{{\cal R}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\left[2(1-r)^{2}(1-\cos(t))+r^{2}t^{2}+2r(1-r)t\sin(t)\right].

(103)

Using a Taylor expansion and noting that
\displaystyle t\in(\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{0\})\cap(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})
, there exists
\displaystyle\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})
with
\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\xi_{1}(t),\xi_{2}=\xi_{2}(t)
such that

1-\cos(t)=\cos(\xi_{1})t^{2}/2,\quad\sin(t)=t\cos(\xi_{2}).

(104)

Thus

\begin{split}|\boldsymbol{y}(r)|^{2}&=\frac{{\cal R}^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\left[2(1-r)^{2}\cos(\xi_{1})t^{2}/2+r^{2}t^{2}+2r(1-r)t\cos(\xi_{2})t\right]\\
&=\frac{{\cal R}^{2}t^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\left[(1-r)^{2}\cos(\xi_{1})+r^{2}+2r(1-r)\cos(\xi_{2})\right]\\

&\geq\frac{{\cal R}^{2}t^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\min_{r\in[0,1]}\left[(1-r)^{2}\cos(2\bar{\theta})+r^{2}+2r(1-r)\cos(2\bar{\theta})\right]\\

where we used the fact that

in the fourth line, and
\displaystyle\cos(2\bar{\theta})
is the minimum with respect to

of the function in the square bracket in the fifth line. Further, since

(106)

for any
\displaystyle t\in(\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{0\})\cap(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})
and
\displaystyle r\in[0,1]
. The lower bound on
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{y}(r)|
is independent of
\displaystyle\lambda
and
\displaystyle r
. Finally, combining (

0<C_{yz}:=\min\{\delta,{\cal R}\theta_{l}\sqrt{\cos(2\bar{\theta})}\}\leq|\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z}|.

(107)

Proceeding further, we have, from the fundamental theorem of calculus,

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{z})-\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}+\boldsymbol{z})&=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\ \mathrm{d}}{\ \mathrm{d}r}\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z})\ \mathrm{d}r=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z})\frac{\ \mathrm{d}}{\ \mathrm{d}r}\boldsymbol{y}(r)\ \mathrm{d}r\\
&=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z})(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1})\ \mathrm{d}r.\end{split}

(108)

Note that because of (

),
\displaystyle\nabla\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z})
exists and is bounded. From the definition of
\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}^{(2)}
in (

), a change of variable
\displaystyle t=u_{1}-a_{1}
, the definition of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\boldsymbol{z}

\begin{split}|\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{s})|&\leq\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u_{1}\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\cap(-\bar{\theta},\bar{\theta}),\\
(u_{1},t_{2}^{\prime})\neq(a_{1},0)\end{subarray}}\left|\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\frac{\boldsymbol{Q}(u_{1}-a_{1})-\boldsymbol{I}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)-\boldsymbol{K}\left({\cal R}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\prime}(0)\frac{u_{1}-a_{1}}{\lambda}\boldsymbol{e}_{1}+\delta t_{2}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{e}_{3}\right)\right|\right]\\
&\leq\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{t\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}\cap(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})}\left|\boldsymbol{K}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\boldsymbol{z}\right)-\boldsymbol{K}\left(\boldsymbol{x}_{2}+\boldsymbol{z}\right)\right|\right]\\
&\leq\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{t\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}\cap(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\nabla\boldsymbol{K}(\boldsymbol{y}(r)+\boldsymbol{z})\right|\;|\boldsymbol{x}_{2}-\boldsymbol{x}_{1}|\ \mathrm{d}r\right]\\

(109)

where we used the bound on the gradient of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}
with constant
\displaystyle C>0
fixed.

Next, we get an upper bound on
\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{2}|
in terms of

\begin{split}|\bar{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\lambda}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{s})|&\leq\sum_{t_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}}\left[\sum_{t\in\lambda\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}-\{0\}\cap(-2\bar{\theta},2\bar{\theta})}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{C}{(|\boldsymbol{y}(r)|^{2}+|\boldsymbol{z}|^{2})^{2}}\frac{{\cal R}}{\lambda}\frac{|t|^{2}}{2}\ \mathrm{d}r\right]\\

5
Summary of Results

We have shown rigorously that certain low-dimensional nanostructures do not have long-range dipole-dipole interaction in the continuum limit.
The energy density in the limit is entirely because of the Maxwell self-field.
In 1-d and 2-d lattices (in a 3-d ambient space), the dipole field kernel decay is sufficiently fast that long-range interactions do not contribute to the limit energy.

While our calculations show that the energy is local in the continuum limit for 1-d and 2-d discrete systems, in agreement with dimension reduction approaches that reduce a 3-d continuum to a 1-d or 2-d continuum (e.g.,
[
GJ97
,
CH15
]
and others), we note an interesting difference.
As shown in
[
GJ97
]
and other work following it, the component of the dipole moment along the normal direction to the film is the only contributor to the continuum electrostatic energy in the thin film limit.
Similarly,
[
CH15
]
show that the component of the dipole moment in the plane normal to the wire is the only contributor to the continuum electrostatic energy in the thin wire limit.

This is different from the limit energy in the discrete-to-continuum limit obtained in this work: for the case of a helical nanotube, the limiting energy density is given by (see
Theorem 3
)

h_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left[|\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}{\mathbfit f}|^{2}-2|\boldsymbol{P}_{||}{\mathbfit f}|^{2}\right]\ \mathrm{d}s,

where
\displaystyle h_{0}
is a constant, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{||}{\mathbfit f}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{\perp}{\mathbfit f}
are, respectively, the projections of the dipole moment field
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}
along the axis of the helix and in the plane normal to the axis of the helix.
Therefore, unlike the thin wire limit using dimension reduction, the discrete-to-continuum energy has contributions from both the normal and tangential components of the dipole moment field.
For the case of a thin film with curvature, the limiting energy density is given by (see
Theorem 6
)

-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\cal S}}{\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})\cdot\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\boldsymbol{s}){\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{s},

where

\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\boldsymbol{s})={\cal R}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\theta_{l}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z},\\
\boldsymbol{u}\neq\mathbf{0}\end{subarray}}\boldsymbol{K}\left(u_{1}\boldsymbol{t}_{1}(\boldsymbol{s})+u_{2}\boldsymbol{t}_{2}(\boldsymbol{s})\right).

Here,
\displaystyle{\cal S}
is the parametric domain of the film,
\displaystyle{\cal R}
is the inverse of the curvature,
\displaystyle\theta_{l}
is the angular width of the unit cell, and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{i}(\boldsymbol{s})
,
\displaystyle i=1,2
, are the tangent vectors at coordinate
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}
.
For simplicity, we fix
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}\in{\cal S}
and assume
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{1}=\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
and
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{t}_{2}=\boldsymbol{e}_{2}
; then the lattice sum above is over a 2-d lattice in
\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{e}_{2})
plane.
By substituting the form of
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{K}
and computing
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{0}(\boldsymbol{s}){\mathbfit f}(\boldsymbol{s})
, we can show that both the normal and the tangential components of
\displaystyle{\mathbfit f}
are present in the final expression for the energy above.

We can understand these differences physically, by first noticing that the dimension reduction starting from the 3-d continuum contains minimal information about the detailed geometry of the underlying lattice within the nanostructure; these approaches have 3-d continuum theory as their starting point, and are valid for situations in which the limiting thin object has all dimensions much larger than the atomic lengthscale.
In contrast, the discrete-to-continuum approach presented here is appropriate for nanostructures in which the thin dimensions are comparable to the atomic lengthscale.
For this reason, the thin-film model obtained in this work may capture better the electromechanics of lipid bilayers, as these are composed of only 1-2 unit cells in the thickness direction
[
LS13
,
ADLS13
,
TMLS22
,
Ste18
]
.
