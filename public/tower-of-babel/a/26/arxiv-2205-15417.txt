Channel Model Mismatch Analysis for XL-MIMO Systems from a Localization Perspective
Hui Chen 1, Ahmed Elzanaty 2, Reza Ghazalian 3, Musa Furkan Keskin 1, Riku Jäntti 3, Henk Wymeersch 1

Abstract
Radio localization is applied in high-frequency (e.g., mmWave and THz) systems to support communication and to provide location-based services without extra infrastructure. For solving localization problems, a simplified, stationary, narrowband far-field channel model is widely used due to its compact formulation. However, with increased array size in extra-large MIMO systems and increased bandwidth at upper mmWave bands, the effect of channel spatial non-stationarity (SNS), spherical wave model (SWM), and beam squint effect (BSE) cannot be ignored. In this case, localization performance will be affected when an inaccurate channel model deviating from the true model is adopted. In this work, we employ the MCRB (misspecified Cramér-Rao lower bound) to lower bound the localization error using a simplified mismatched model while the observed data is governed by a more complex true model. The simulation results show that among all the model impairments, the SNS has the least contribution, the SWM dominates when the distance is small compared to the array size, and the BSE has a more significant effect when the distance is much larger than the array size.



I
Introduction

Radio localization is playing an important role in the fifth/sixth generation (5G/6G) communication systems to support various emerging applications, e.g., autonomous driving
[
1
]
, digital twins
[
2
]
, and augmented reality
[
3

. In general, localization starts with channel estimation and channel geometric parameters extraction by assuming a sparse channel consisting of a limited number of paths. From the angle and delay estimation with respect to known anchors, e.g., base stations, the user equipment position can be estimated. Benefiting from the large bandwidth and array size of mmWave and THz systems, high angular and delay resolution, and hence accurate localization performance is expected
[
4
]
.

Research on 5G/6G radio localization has drawn significant attention recently and the works range from 2D
[
5
]
to 3D
[
6
]
scenarios, from non-line-of-sight-assisted
[
7
]
to reconfigurable intelligent surface-supported localization
[
8
]
. Most of the works consider a stationary, narrowband far-field model due to its simplicity in algorithm design and performance analysis. This simplified model works well in conventional communication systems with limited bandwidth and antennas. Nevertheless, to combat high path loss with signals at high carrier frequencies, extra-large MIMO and large reconfigurable intelligent surfaces will be deployed, resulting in spatial non-stationarity and spherical wave model, which are considered as the near-field features
[
9
,
10
,
8
]
. In addition, a much wider bandwidth causes beam squint effect that makes the simplified model insufficient
[
11
,
12
]
. Therefore, there is a need to understand to what extent the conventional model holds.

Some recent works investigate error bounds and develop localization algorithms in near-field scenarios considering both SNS and SWM
[
13
,
14
]
, or only SWM
[
15
]
. In particular, tracking with filter evaluations
[
13
]
, compressive sensing-based algorithm
[
15
]
and constrained RIS profile optimization
[
14
]
are discussed for NF localization. However, the complexity of the near-field models precludes the development of scalable algorithms for extra-large MIMO systems. Some approximations exist, such as second-order approximation of the far-field model
[
16
]
, or adopting array-of-subarray structures considering near-field across the subarrays while retaining the far-field model for each subarray
[
12
]
, but the qualities of these approximations on localization are not evaluated. Compared with the near-field model, the beam squint effect is considered less frequently in localization and sensing works than communication
[
11
]
. As a result, the model mismatch by adopting a simplified model is an important factor affecting the localization performance, especially for large bandwidth extra-large MIMO systems
[
9
,
10
]
, and the level of performance loss caused by such approximations needs to be studied.

In this work, instead of discussing an accurate propagation channel model, we aim to answer the question:
when is the conventional simplified model sufficient?

To answer this question, we define a model mismatch boundary with the help of misspecified Cramér-Rao bound
[
17
]
. The main contributions of this paper can be summarized as follows:

•

We formulate a “true model” considering SNS, SWM, BSE and explain how these three types of impairments (w.r.t. the conventional model) can be removed one by one to obtain the conventional model, which is treated as a “mismatched model”.

•

We resort to MCRB analysis to formulate the lower bound (LB) of localization using a mismatched model in processing the data obtained from the true model, and define a model-mismatch error as the absolute difference between the LB and the CRB of the TM, normalized by the CRB of the TM.

•

We provide extensive numerical results for the derived LB and evaluate the contributions of different types of mismatches for various scenarios to provide
guidelines on when the conventional simplified model does not considerably affect the localization performance.

II
System and Signal Model

In this section, we start with the signal model and describe the considered mismatched model and true model.
Consider an uplink system with a base station equipped with an
N
-element uniform linear array estimating the location of a single-antenna user equipment. The center of base station is at the origin of the global coordinate system, and each antenna is located at
{\bf b}_{n}=[0,{(2n-N-1)\lambda_{c}}/{4}]^{\top}
,
1\leq n\leq N
, where
\lambda_{c}
is the wavelength of the carrier frequency
f_{c}
. We analyze a simple scenario by assuming the system is synchronized and only LOS path exists. Then, the position of UE
{\bf p}
can be expressed as
{\bf p}={\tau}{c}\,[\cos(\vartheta),\sin(\vartheta)]^{\top}
, where
\vartheta
is the angle-of-arrival,
\tau
is the time-of-arrival and
c
is the speed of light.

II-A

Signal Model

Considering
x_{g,k}
(
|x_{g,k}|^{2}={P}
, where
P
is the average transmission power) as the transmitted orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing symbol at
g
-th transmission (
1\leq g\leq\mathcal{G}
) and
k
-th subcarrier (
1\leq k\leq K
), the observation at the base station can be formulated as

{\bf y}_{g,k}={{\bf W}_{\text{}g}^{\top}{\bf h}_{k}x_{g,k}}+{\bf W}_{\text{}g}^{\top}{\bf n}_{g,k},

(1)

where
{\bf W}_{\text{}g}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}
is the unitary (i.e.,
{\bf W}_{\text{}g}^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf W}_{\text{}g}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}_{M}
, where
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}_{M}
is an
M\times M
identity matrix) combining matrix at the BS for the
g
-th transmission with
M\leq N
representing the number of radio-frequency chains,
{\bf h}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
is the channel vector at the
k
th subcarrier, which is assumed to be constant during
\mathcal{G}
transmissions, and
{\bf n}_{g,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
denotes the noise component following a complex normal distribution
{\bf n}_{g,k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}_{N})
, with
\sigma_{n}^{2}=N_{0}W
, where
N_{0}
is the noise power spectral density (PSD) and
W=K\Delta_{f}
is the total bandwidth. In this work, we will focus on the analysis of digital arrays to elimiate the effect of the combining matrix, as
{\bf W}_{g}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}_{N}
can be removed from (
1
).

II-B

Mismatched Channel Model

We consider a widely used channel model as the mismatched model. The channel vector for the
k
th subcarrier
{\bf h}_{k}^{\text{MM}}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
can be formulated using a complex channel gain
\alpha
, a steering vector
{\bf a}_{\text{}}(\vartheta)\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
, and a delay component
D_{k}(\tau)
as

\displaystyle{\bf h}_{k}^{\text{MM}}=

\displaystyle\alpha{\bf a}_{\text{}}(\vartheta)D_{k}(\tau),

(2)

\displaystyle\alpha=

\displaystyle\rho e^{-j\xi}=\frac{\lambda_{c}}{4\pi\|{\bf p}\|}e^{-j\xi},

(3)

\displaystyle{\bf a}_{\text{}}(\vartheta)=

\displaystyle[e^{-j\pi\frac{N-1}{2}\sin(\vartheta)},\ldots,1,\ldots,e^{-j\pi\frac{1-N}{2}\sin(\vartheta)}]^{\top},

(4)

\displaystyle D_{k}(\tau)=

\displaystyle e^{-j2\pi(f_{c}+k\Delta_{f})\tau}=D_{k}({\bf p})=e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{k}}\|{\bf p}\|},

(5)

where
f_{k}=c/\lambda_{k}=f_{c}+k\Delta_{f}
is the frequency of the
k
th subcarrier,
\alpha
is an unknown complex channel gain during the coherence time determined by the environment and antenna radiation pattern. Note that in this model, the channel gain
\alpha
is the same for all the antennas and subcarriers, and the steering vector is only determined by the angle-of-arrival. The delay term
D_{k}
is usually simplified as
e^{-j2\pi k\Delta_{f}\tau}
since the constant component
e^{-j2\pi f_{c}\tau}
can be incorporated into the channel gain.

II-C

True Channel Model

We compare the mismatched model with a true model, which is the standard NF model used in array signal processing
[
18
]
including the beam squint effect. The channel vector for the true model can then be formulated as

\displaystyle{\bf h}_{k}^{\text{TM}}=

\displaystyle\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}({\bf p})\odot{\bf d}_{k}({\bf p})D_{k}({\bf p}),

(6)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k,n}({\bf p})=

\displaystyle\alpha\,c_{k,n}({\bf p}),\ \ c_{k,n}({\bf p})=\frac{\lambda_{k}\|{\bf p}\|}{\lambda_{c}\|{\bf p}-{\bf b}_{n}\|},

(7)

\displaystyle d_{k,n}({\bf p})=

\displaystyle e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda_{k}}(\|{\bf p}-{\bf b}_{n}\|-\|{\bf p}\|)}.

(8)

Here,
\alpha
is defined in (
3
),
\alpha_{k,n}
is the
n
th element of the vector
\hbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}_{k}
indicating the channel nonstationarity, and
d_{k,n}
is the
n
th entry of the delay vector
{\bf d}_{k}
.
In contrast to the FF model formulated in (
2
)–(
5
), we can see that three types of model impairments are introduced in (
6
)–(
8
) as follows.

1.

Spatial non-stationarity
: Non-stationarities represented by
\alpha_{k,n}
in (
7
) occur because different regions of the array see different propagation paths when the array is large
[
9
]
, i.e., the distance between the source and the various antennas may be quite different in the near-field. Also, when the bandwidth of the system is large, different antennas and subcarriers will have different channel amplitudes, as shown in (
7
).

2.

Spherical wave model
: In the NF, the phase delay between different antennas can no longer be formulated using a steering vector. Only when the distance between the transceivers is much larger than the array aperture, the term
(\|{\bf p}-{\bf b}_{n}\|-\|{\bf p}\|)
can be approximated into
\sin(\vartheta){(2n-N-1)}\lambda_{c}/4

[
16
]
. By further replacing
\lambda_{k}
with
\lambda_{c}
(ignore BSE), (
8
) is identical to (
4
).

3.

Beam squint effect
: The steering vector in (
4
) is the same for all the subcarriers (frequency-independent). When the beam squint effect is considered
[
12
,
4
]
, the FF steering vector
{\bf a}(\vartheta)
need to be modified to be frequency-dependent as
\tilde{\bf a}_{k}(\vartheta)
, with each element

\tilde{\bf a}_{k,n}(\vartheta)=e^{j\pi\frac{2n-N-1}{2}\sin(\vartheta)\frac{\lambda_{c}}{\lambda_{k}}}
. This mismatch can be seen from
\lambda_{k}
(instead of
\lambda_{c}
) in (
8
).

Note that the NF model is parameterized by the UE position
{\bf p}
, while the FF model is parameterized by the signal AOA
\vartheta
and delay
\tau
. When clock offset is introduced in the delay (which is a practical assumption as perfect synchronization is quite challenging to achieve and maintain), the NF model can still be sufficient for positioning in an unsynchronized system with a single base station by exploiting the wavefront curvature.

Typically, the near-field is considered in the area between Fresnel distance
D_{\text{F}}
and Fraunhofer distance
D_{\text{N}}

[
19
]
as

D_{\text{N}}\triangleq 0.62\sqrt{\frac{R^{3}}{\lambda_{c}}}<r<D_{\text{F}}\triangleq\frac{2R^{2}}{\lambda_{c}},

(9)

where
r
is the distance between the transceivers, and
R
is the largest dimension of the array.
However, the Fraunhofer distance is just a simple rule of thumb calculation for the boundary between the far-field and near-field, which does not consider the BSE and other system parameters (e.g., transmission power or AOA). In the rest of this work, we will show that this distance is insufficient to suggest when the MM can be used in practice without performance degradation.

In this section, we described a MM (FF channel model) and a TM (NF model with BSE). To facilitate the analysis in the following, we further define several true models, and all the considered models are summarized as follows:

MM: the model described in (
2
)–(
5
) is considered as the MM.

2.

TM: the model involves all the model impairments described in (
6
)-(

) is considered as the TM.

III
Estimators and Lower Bounds

In this section, we briefly describe the maximum likelihood estimation and the mismatched maximum likelihood estimation for localization, and derive the Cramér-Rao bound and lower bounds (based on misspecified Cramér-Rao bound) for the two estimators, respectively.

III-A

Maximum Likelihood Estimator

For an observed signal vector from the TM
{\bf y}\sim f_{\text{TM}}({\bf y}|\alpha,{\bf p})
(where
{\bf y}\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathcal{G}K\times 1}
is a concatenation of all the received symbols from different subcarriers and transmissions),
the maximum likelihood estimation of the UE position and channel gain is

\displaystyle[\hat{\bf p}_{\text{MLE}},\hat{\alpha}_{\text{MLE}}]

\displaystyle={\hbox{arg}}\max_{{\bf p},\alpha}\ln f_{\text{TM}}({\bf y}|\alpha,{\bf p}),

(10)

where
\ln f_{\text{TM}}({\bf y}|\alpha,{\bf p})
is the log-likelihood of the TM.
We can then use a plug-in estimate to remove the nuisance parameter
\alpha

[
21
]
. Then, the position estimation can be obtained as

\displaystyle\hat{\bf p}_{\text{MLE}}

\displaystyle={\hbox{arg}}\min_{\bf p}\left\|{\bf y}-\frac{\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf y}}{\|\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})\|^{2}}\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})\right\|^{2},

(11)

where
\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})=\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}(\alpha,{\bf p})/\alpha
, and
\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}(\alpha,{\bf p})=[\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{1,1}^{\top},\ldots,\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{G,K}^{\top}]^{\top}
is the concatenation of all the noise-free observations of the TM with
\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{g,k}={\bf W}_{\text{}g}^{\top}{\bf h}^{\text{TM}}_{k}x_{g,k}
based on (
1
) and (
6
). The problem in (
11
) can be solved by gradient descent with backtracking line search
[
22
]
. An initial point for the algorithm can be obtained through a 2D coarse grid search.

Considering MM, the mismatched maximum likelihood estimation can be formulated as

\displaystyle\hat{\bf p}_{\text{MMLE}}

\displaystyle={\hbox{arg}}\min_{\bf p}\left\|{\bf y}-\frac{\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})^{\mathsf{H}}{\bf y}}{\|\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})\|^{2}}\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})\right\|^{2},

(12)

with
\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}({\bf p})=\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}(\alpha,{\bf p})/\alpha
, and
\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}(\alpha,{\bf p})=[\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{1,1}^{\top},\ldots,\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{G,K}^{\top}]^{\top}
is the concatenation of all the noise-free observations of the MM with
\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}_{g,k}={\bf W}_{\text{}g}^{\top}{\bf h}^{\text{MM}}_{k}x_{g,k}
based on (
1
) and (
2
).

III-B

CRB

The Cramér-Rao bound is sufficient to lower bound the maximum likelihood estimation. We define a channel parameter vector as
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{c}}}=[\vartheta,\tau,\rho,\xi]^{\top}
and a state vector
{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{s}}}=[p_{x},p_{y},\rho,\xi]^{\top}
. In this work, these two vectors have a one-to-one mapping and either of them can sufficiently describe the channel model. Regardless of which vector is used, the FIM of the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
can be expressed as
[
4
]

{\mathcal{I}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{2}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\sum^{{\cal G}}_{g=1}\sum^{K}_{k=1}\mathrm{Re}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial\mu_{g,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}\left(\frac{\partial\mu_{g,k}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)\right\},

(13)

where
\mu_{g,k}
could be
\bar{\mu}_{g,k}
or
\tilde{\mu}_{g,k}
to derive the CRB for the TM or MM,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
could be
\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{c}}
or
\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{s}}
and the corresponding FIM
\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{c}})
or
\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{s}})
can be obtained. The derivative of TM can be found in the Appendix
A
.
Usually, the FF model starts with the channel parameters since the channel depends on angle and delay as described in (
11
), whereas the NF model calculates the FIM of
\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{s}}
directly as
\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{s}})
.
However, the scenario in this work is a special case (synchronized and LOS channel only) and the two FIMs can be transformed from each other through a Jacobian matrix
{\bf J}_{\text{c}}
or
{\bf J}_{\text{s}}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{s}})

\displaystyle={\bf J}_{\text{s}}\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{c}}){\bf J}_{\text{s}}^{\top},\ \ {\bf J}_{\text{s}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bf J}_{\text{s}}&\mathbf{0}_{2}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2}&{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}{I}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{c}})

\displaystyle={\bf J}_{\text{c}}\mathcal{I}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{\text{s}}){\bf J}_{\text{c}}^{\top},\ \ {\bf J}_{\text{c}}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\bf J}_{\text{c}}&\mathbf{0}_{2}\\
\mathbf{0}_{2}&{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}{I}_{2}\end{bmatrix},

(15)

where
\mathbf{0}_{2}
is a
2\times 2
zero matrix.

\tilde{\bf J}_{\mathrm{s}}=[{\partial\vartheta}/{\partial{\bf p}},{\partial\tau}/{\partial{\bf p}}]
is the Jacobian matrix from angle/delay to position using a denominator-layout notation with
{\partial\vartheta}/{\partial{\bf p}}={1}/{(c\tau)}[-\sin(\vartheta),\cos(\vartheta)]^{\top}
and
{\partial\tau}/{\partial{\bf p}}={{\bf p}}/{(c\tau)}
.
Similarly,
\tilde{\bf J}_{\mathrm{c}}=[({\partial{\bf p}}/{\partial\vartheta})^{\top},({\partial{\bf p}^{\top}}/{\partial\tau})^{\top}]
is the Jacobian matrix from position to angle/delay to position as
{\partial{\bf p}}/{\partial\vartheta}={c\tau}[-\sin(\vartheta),\cos(\vartheta)]^{\top}

and
{\partial{\bf p}}/{\partial\tau}=c[\cos(\vartheta),\sin(\vartheta)]^{\top}
.

Based on the above discussions, we can define the position error bound (PEB), angle error bound (AEB), and delay error bound (DEB) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{PEB}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{{\hbox{tr}}(\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{s}}})^{-1})},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{AEB}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{([\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{c}}})^{-1}]_{1,1})},

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{DEB}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{([\mathcal{I}({\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{c}}})^{-1}]_{2,2})},

(18)

where
{\hbox{tr}}(\cdot)
is the trace operation, and
[\cdot]_{i,j}
is getting the element in the
i
th row,
j
th column of a matrix. The bounds from (
16
)–(
18
) can assist us to evaluate the position, angle, and delay estimation performance that will be affected by the model mismatch.

III-C

MCRB

The CRB described in Section
III-B
can be implemented for performance analysis when the models used for the estimator and the data generation are the same. When a mismatched model is implemented, we need to rely on MCRB to analyze the performance.
The lower bound of a mismatched estimator can be obtained as
[
17
]

\displaystyle\text{LB}=\text{LB}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{{\bf A}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}^{-1}{\bf B}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}{\bf A}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}^{-1}}_{\text{MCRB}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})}+\underbrace{(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})^{\top}}_{\text{Bias}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0})},

(19)

where
\bar{\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}}
is the parameter vector for the true model and
\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}_{0}
is the pseudo-true parameter vector by minimizing the KL divergence between
f_{\text{TM}}({\bf y}|\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
f_{\text{MM}}({\bf y}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
,
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
are two possible generalizations of the FIMs as
[
21
]

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}

\displaystyle={\hbox{arg}}\min_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\|\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|^{2},

(20)

\displaystyle[{\bf A}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{2}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\text{Re}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}\partial\theta_{j}}\hbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\frac{\partial\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}\right]\right|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}},

(21)

\displaystyle[{\bf B}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}]_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{\sigma_{n}^{4}}\text{Re}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\hbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right]\text{Re}\left[\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{j}}\hbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right]

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{2}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\text{Re}\left[\frac{\partial\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{j}}\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}{\partial\theta_{i}}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}\right]\right|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}}.

(22)

Here,
\hbox{\boldmath$\epsilon$}(\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$})\triangleq\bar{\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-\hbox{\boldmath$\mu$}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and the detailed derivation can be found in
[
21
]
.
The obtained LB satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\text{TM}}\{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MMLE}}-\bar{\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MMLE}}-\bar{\hbox{$\boldsymbol{\theta}$}})^{\top}\}\succeq\text{LB}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}),

(23)

which can be used to evaluate the performance of a mismatched estimator.

III-D

Model Mismatch Boundary

Based on the LB derived from previous sections, we can define a model-mismatch error as the log-normalized difference between the LB and the CRB of the true model as

\text{MME}=10\log_{10}\left(\frac{|\text{CRB}_{\text{TM}}-\text{{LB}}|}{\text{CRB}_{\text{TM}}}\right).

(24)

Here,
MME
,
\text{CRB}_{\text{TM}}
and
LB
are general terms could be used to indicate angle, delay or position estimation performance.
We further define a model mismatch boundary between the TM and the MM as the
-3\,\mathrm{dB}
contour of the MME.
Although there is no closed-form solution for this boundary, it is helpful to identify in which area we can use a simplified mismatched model.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we derived the LB of a mismatched estimator using MCRB and analyzed the effect of different types of model impairments, namely, SNS, SWM, and BSE. A model-mismatch error model mismatch error is further defined as the absolute difference between the LB and the CRB of the TM, normalized by the CRB of the TM, which is determined by system parameters. From the analysis, we see that the SNS has the least contribution among all the model impairments, the SWM dominates when the distance is small compared to the array size, and BSE has a more significant effect when the distance is much larger than the array size.
The analysis in this work can provide suggestions on the tradeoff between the complexity of channel model and performance loss (by using an approximated, simple model).
In future work, we would like to analyze the effect of model mismatch on the 3D position and 3D orientation of an unsynchronized system.
