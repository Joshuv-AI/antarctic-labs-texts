Orthogonal Polynomials Associated with Complementary Chain Sequences This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Orthogonal Polynomials, Special Functions and Applications. The full collection is available at http://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/OPSFA2015.html
Kiran Kumar BEHERA †, A. SRI RANGA ‡ and A. SWAMINATHAN †

Abstract
Using the minimal parameter sequence of a given chain sequence, we introduce the concept of complementary chain sequences, which we view as perturbations of chain sequences. Using the relation between these complementary chain sequences and the corresponding Verblunsky coefficients, the para-orthogonal polynomials and the associated Szegő polynomials are analyzed. Two illustrations, one involving Gaussian hypergeometric functions and the other involving Carathéodory functions are also provided. A connection between these two illustrations by means of complementary chain sequences is also observed.

Preliminaries on Szegő polynomials

The Szegő polynomials
\{\Phi_{n}\}
, also referred to as orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle (OPUC), enjoy the orthogonality property

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}(\bar{z})^{j}\Phi_{n}(z)d\mu(z)=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}(z)^{-j}\Phi_{n}(z)d\mu(z)=0\qquad\mbox{for}\quad j=0,1,\dots,n-1,\quad n\geq 1.

Here
\mu(z)=\mu(e^{i\theta})
is a nontrivial measure defined on the unit circle
\partial{\mathbb{D}}=\{z=e^{i\theta}\colon 0\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\}
. Denoting the orthonormal Szegő polynomials by
\phi_{n}(z)=\chi_{n}\Phi_{n}(z)
, we also have the equivalent definition

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\phi_{n}(z)\overline{\phi_{m}(z)}d\mu(z)=\delta_{m,n}.

Further, defining the moments
\mu_{n}=\int_{\partial{\mathbb{D}}}e^{-in\theta}d\mu(\theta)
,
n=0,\pm 1,\dots
, where
\mu_{-n}=\bar{\mu}_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}(\bar{z})^{n}\Phi_{n}(z)d\mu(z)=\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\Delta_{n-1}}\neq 0,\qquad n=0,1,\dots.

Here
\Delta_{n}=\det\{\mu_{i-j}\}_{i,j=0}^{n}
are the associated Toeplitz matrices with
\Delta_{-1}=1
.

The monic Szegő polynomials satisfy the first order recurrence relations

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z)=z\Phi_{n-1}(z)-\bar{\alpha}_{n-1}\Phi_{n-1}^{*}(z),\qquad\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=-\alpha_{n-1}z\Phi_{n-1}(z)+\Phi_{n-1}^{*}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

where
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)=z^{n}\overline{\Phi_{n}(1/\bar{z})}
. The complex numbers
\alpha_{n-1}=-\overline{\Phi_{n}(0)}
are called the Verblunsky coefficients
[
22
]
. The Verblunsky coefficients completely characterize the Szegő polynomials in the sense that any sequence
\{\alpha_{n-1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
lying within the unit circle gives rise to a unique probability measure
\mu(z)
which leads to a unique sequence of Szegő polynomials. The above result, called the Verblunsky theorem in
[
22
]
, is the analogue of Favard’s theorem on the real line. Conversely, algorithms exist in the literature that extracts these coefficients from any given Szegő system of orthogonal polynomials. Notable among them are the Schur algorithm, the Levinson algorithm and their modified versions given in
[
7
,
14
,
26
]
.

The Szegő polynomials also satisfy the three term recurrence relation

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}(z)=\left(\frac{\Phi_{n+1}(0)}{\Phi_{n}(0)}+z\right)\Phi_{n}(z)-\frac{(1-|\Phi_{n}(0)|^{2})\Phi_{n+1}(0)}{\Phi_{n}(0)}z\Phi_{n-1}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

(1.1)

with
\Phi_{0}(z)=1
and
\Phi_{1}(z)=z+\Phi_{1}(0)
. Note that if
\Phi_{n}(0)=0
,
n\geq 1
, then the three term recurrence relation ceases to exist. In such a case,
\Phi_{n}(z)=z^{n}
, which is given as the free case in
[
22
, p. 85]
. Denoting

\displaystyle\eta_{n+1}=\frac{\Phi_{n+1}(0)}{\Phi_{n}(0)}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\rho_{n+1}=\frac{\big(1-|\Phi_{n}(0)|^{2}\big)\Phi_{n+1}(0)}{\Phi_{n}(0)},\qquad n\geq 1,

the following expressions are easily obtained from (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}(0)=\frac{\Phi_{n}(0)}{1-|\Phi_{n}(0)|^{2}}\frac{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}z\Phi_{n}(z)d\mu(z)}{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}z\Phi_{n-1}(z)d\mu(z)},\qquad 1-|\Phi_{n}(0)|^{2}=\frac{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}z^{-n}\Phi_{n}(z)d\mu(z)}{\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}z^{-(n-1)}\Phi_{n-1}(z)d\mu(z)}

and

\displaystyle\chi_{n}^{-2}=\frac{\rho_{2}\rho_{3}\cdots\rho_{n+1}}{\eta_{2}\eta_{3}\cdots\eta_{n+1}}\mu_{0}=\mu_{0}\big(1-|\Phi_{1}(0)|^{2}\big)\big(1-|\Phi_{2}(0)|^{2}\big)\cdots\big(1-|\Phi_{n}(0)|^{2}\big).

(1.2)

For early developments on the subject, we refer to the monographs
[
8
,
10
,
25
]
. For a compendium of modern research in the area as well as historical notes, we refer to
[
22
,
23
]
.

In order to develop a quadrature formula on the unit circle, Jones et al.
[
15
]
introduced the para-orthogonal polynomials which vanish only on the unit circle and, for
z,\omega_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\omega_{n}|=1
, have the representation

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{n}(z,\omega_{n})=\Phi_{n}(z)+\omega_{n}\Phi^{*}_{n}(z),\qquad n\geq 1.

The para-orthogonal polynomials satisfy the properties

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{X}_{n},z^{m}\rangle=0,\qquad m=1,2,\dots,n-1,\qquad\langle\mathcal{X}_{n},1\rangle\neq 0,\qquad\langle\mathcal{X}_{n},z^{n}\rangle\neq 0,

which are termed as deficiency in the orthogonality of these para-orthogonal polynomials. In recent years, these para-orthogonal polynomials have been linked to kernel polynomials
K_{n}(z,\omega)
, see
[
6
,
11
,
27
]
. The kernel polynomials
K_{n}(z,\omega)
satisfy the Christoffel–Darboux formula

\displaystyle K_{n}(z,\omega)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\phi_{k}(z)\overline{\phi_{k}(\omega)}=\frac{\phi^{*}_{n+1}(z)\overline{\phi^{*}_{n+1}(\omega)}-\phi_{n+1}(z)\overline{\phi_{n+1}(\omega)}}{1-z\bar{\omega}}.

Denoting
\tau_{n}(\omega)=\Phi_{n}(\omega)/\Phi^{*}_{n}(\omega)
, for
n\geq 1
, the monic kernel polynomials related to the Szegő polynomials are given by

\displaystyle P_{n}(\omega;z)=\frac{z\Phi_{n}(z)-\omega\tau_{n}(\omega)\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)}{z-\omega},\qquad n\geq 1,

(1.3)

and are shown in
[
6
]
to satisfy a three term recurrence relation of the form

\displaystyle P_{n+1}(\omega;z)=[z+b_{n+1}(\omega)]P_{n}(\omega;z)-a_{n+1}(\omega)zP_{n-1}(\omega;z),\qquad n\geq 1,

where

\displaystyle b_{n}(\omega)=\frac{\tau_{n}(\omega)}{\tau_{n-1}(\omega)},\qquad a_{n+1}=[1+\tau_{n}(\omega)\alpha_{n-1}]\big[1-\overline{\omega\tau_{n}(\omega)\alpha_{n}}\big]\omega,\qquad n\geq 1.

The polynomials
P_{n}(\omega;z)
are
\overline{\tau_{n}(w)}
-invariant sequences of polynomials which can be easily verified from (
1.3
). Note that a sequence of polynomials
\{\mathcal{Y}_{n}\}
is called
\tau_{n}
-invariant if
[
15
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}^{*}_{n}(z)=\tau_{n}\mathcal{Y}_{n}(z),\qquad n\geq 1.

An important concept that is used in the sequel is the theory of chain sequences. We give a brief introduction to chain sequences and then illustrate the role played by them in the theory of OPUC.

A sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
which satisfies

\displaystyle d_{n}=(1-g_{n-1})g_{n},\qquad n\geq 1,

is called a positive chain sequence
[
5
]
(see also
[
12
, Section 7.2]
). Here
\{g_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, called the parameter sequence is such that
0\leq g_{0}<1
,
0<g_{n}<1
for
n\geq 1
. This is a stronger condition than the one used in
[
26
]
, in which
d_{n}
is also allowed to be zero. The parameter sequence
\{g_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is called a minimal parameter sequence and denoted by
\{m_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
if
m_{0}=0
. Every chain sequence has a minimal parameter sequence
[
5
, pp. 91–92]
. Further, for a fixed chain sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, let
\mathcal{G}
be the set of all parameter sequences
\{g_{k}\}
of
\{d_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
. Let the sequence
\{M_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be defined by

\displaystyle M_{n}=\inf\{g_{n},\,\mbox{for each }n,\,\{g_{k}\}\in\mathcal{G}\},\qquad n\geq 0,

where
\inf
is infimum of the set. Then,
\{M_{n}\}
is called the maximal parameter sequence of
\{d_{n}\}
.

The role of chain sequences in the study of orthogonal polynomials on the real line is well known. Similarly, a positive chain sequence
\{d_{n}\}
appears
in the three term recurrence relation for the polynomials
R_{n}(z)
, that turn out to be scaled versions of the (kernel) polynomials
P_{n}(1,z)
, namely,

\displaystyle R_{n+1}(z)=[(1+ic_{n+1})z+(1-ic_{n+1})]R_{n}(z)-4d_{n+1}zR_{n-1}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

(1.4)

with
R_{0}(z)=1
and
R_{1}(z)=(1+ic_{1})z+(1-ic_{1})
. It is indeed shown in
[
6
]
, that

\displaystyle R_{n}(z)=\frac{\prod\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}[1-\tau_{j}\alpha_{j}]}{\prod\limits_{j=0}^{n-1}[1-\operatorname{Re}(\tau_{j}\alpha_{j})]}P_{n}(1;z),

where

\displaystyle\tau_{j}=\tau_{j}(1)=\prod_{k=1}^{j}\frac{1-ic_{k}}{1+ic_{k}},\qquad j\geq 1,

on condition that

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{-\operatorname{Im}(\tau_{n-1}\alpha_{n-1})}{1-\operatorname{Re}(\tau_{n-1}\alpha_{n-1})}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad d_{n+1}=(1-g_{n})g_{n+1},\qquad n\geq 1,

is a chain sequence with parameter sequence

\displaystyle g_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{|1-\tau_{n-1}\alpha_{n-1}|^{2}}{[1-\operatorname{Re}(\tau_{n-1}\alpha_{n-1})]},\qquad n\geq 1.

It is also not difficult to verify that in this case
R_{n}(z)
has
r_{n,n}=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}(1+ic_{k})
as the leading coefficient and
r_{n,0}=\bar{r}_{n,n}=\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}(1-ic_{k})
as the constant term.

It is known that
\{R_{n}(z)\}
can be used to obtain a sequence of OPUC
[
2
,
3
,
6
]
, with respect to the measure
\mu(z)
and having the shifted sequence
\{\alpha_{n-1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
as the Verblunsky coefficients. A further interesting fact is that the above parameter sequence

\{g_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is such that
g_{1}=(1-\epsilon)M_{1}
, (
0\leq\epsilon<1
), where
\{M_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is the maximal parameter sequence of
\{d_{n+1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and that
\epsilon
, is the size of the pure point at
z=1
in the probability measure
\mu(z)
associated with the
Verblunsky coefficients
\{\alpha_{n-1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. This means, if the measure does not have a pure point at
z=1
then
\{g_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is the maximal parameter sequence of
\{d_{n+1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.

Consider now the Uvarov transformation of the measure
\mu(z)
,
[
6
, p. 11]
,

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)d\mu^{(t)}(z)=\frac{(1-t)}{1-\epsilon}\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)d\mu(z)+\frac{t-\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}f(1),

so that
\mu^{(t)}(z)
has a jump
t
,
0\leq t<1
, at
z=1
. These measures
\mu^{(t)}(z)
are associated with the positive chain sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
obtained from
\{d_{n+1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
by including the additional term
d_{1}=(1-t)M_{1}
. We also denote the generalized sequence of Verblunsky coefficients associated with
\mu^{(t)}(z)
by
\big\{\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}\big\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.

Note 1.1
.

Since the measure
\mu^{(t)}(z)
has a parameter ‘
t
’, the notations for the polynomials
R_{n}(z)
and the sequences
\{c_{n}\}
,
\{d_{n}\}
should have involved a
{}^{\prime}t^{\prime}
. However, it has been proved
[
6
, p. 7]
, that the kernel polynomials
P_{n}(1;z)
and hence
R_{n}(z)
as well as the sequences
\{c_{n}\}
and
\{d_{n}\}
are independent of ‘
t
’ and so their notations are devoid of ‘
t
’. But the minimal parameters depend on
d_{1}
and this has been reflected in the notation
m_{n}^{(t)}
.

As shown in
[
6
, Theorem 1.1]
,
\mu^{(t)}(z)
can also be given by

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)d\mu^{(t)}(z)=(1-t)\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}f(z)d\mu^{(0)}(z)+tf(1).

(1.5)

As is obvious from the notation,
\mu^{(0)}(z)
are the measures arising when
t=0
. This is the case when
d_{1}=M_{1}
, so that both the minimal and maximal parameter sequences coincide. This equality can also be interpreted as the measure having zero jump.

Further, the Verblunsky coefficients
\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}
have the representation

\displaystyle\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}=\overline{\tau}_{n}\left[\frac{1-2m_{n}^{(t)}-ic_{n}}{1+ic_{n}}\right],\qquad n\geq 1.

(1.6)

where
\big\{m_{n}^{(t)}\big\}
is the minimal parameter sequence of the positive chain sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. The Szegő polynomials corresponding to (
1.6
) are
[
3
, Theorem 5.2]

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(z)=\frac{R_{n}(z)-2\big(1-m_{n}^{(t)}\big)R_{n-1}(z)}{\prod\limits_{k=1}^{n}(1+ic_{k})},\qquad n\geq 1.

(1.7)

It can be verified from (
1.4
) that if
c_{k}=0
,
k\geq 0
,
\alpha_{n-1}
,
n\geq 1
, are all real. The
R_{n}(z)
are then the singular predictor polynomials of the second kind given in
[
7
]
. Indeed, if
c_{n}=0
,
n\geq 1
, it can be easily shown from (
1.7
) that

\displaystyle(z-1)R_{n}(z)=z\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(z)-\big(\Phi^{(t)}_{n}\big)^{*}(z).

We would like to mention here that the Szegő polynomials, Verblunsky coefficients and the related measure have also been obtained for the para-orthogonal polynomials that are an extension of singular predictor polynomials of first kind. See
[
2
]
for the details of these extensions and also for a survey of recent developments in the theory connecting chain sequences and OPUC.

The purpose of the present manuscript is to introduce a particular perturbation in the chain sequence
\{d_{n}\}
, called the complementary chain sequence,
and study its effect on the Verblunsky coefficients of the corresponding Szegő polynomials. The motivation for this follows from the fact that (
1.6
) guarantees an explicit relation between the Verblunsky coefficients and the minimal parameter sequence
\big\{m_{n}^{(t)}\big\}
of
\{d_{n}\}
.

This manuscript is organized as follows. In Section
2
the concept of complementary chain sequences using the minimal parameter sequences is introduced. Using this concept, perturbations of Verblunsky coefficients are studied. As an illustration of this concept, in Section
3
, the Szegő polynomials which characterizes the positive Perron–Carathéodory (PPC) fractions from a particular chain sequence are constructed. An interplay by these PPC fractions in finding a relation between this chain sequence, its complementary chain sequence and their respective Carathéodry functions is obtained in this section. In Section
4
, another illustration of characterizing the
Szegő polynomials using Gaussian hypergeometric functions is provided. For particular values, using complementary chain sequences, the corresponding Verblunsky coefficients of these Szegő polynomials are also shown to be perturbed Verblunsky coefficients obtained earlier.

2
Complementary chain sequences

As is obvious from the definition of chain sequences, the minimal and maximal parameter sequences are uniquely defined for any given chain sequence. Also, the chain sequence for which the minimal and maximal parameter sequences coincide, that is,
M_{0}=0
, has its own importance as illustrated in the previous section. Such a chain sequence is said to determine its parameters uniquely and is referred to as a single parameter positive chain sequence (SPPCS)
[
2
]
. By Wall’s criteria for maximal parameter sequence
[
26
, p. 82]
, this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{m_{1}}{1-m_{1}}\cdot\frac{m_{2}}{1-m_{2}}\cdot\frac{m_{3}}{1-m_{3}}\cdots\frac{m_{n}}{1-m_{n}}=\infty.

(2.1)

Thus, introducing a perturbation in the minimal parameters
m_{n}
will lead to a uniquely defined change in the chain sequence.

Definition 2.1
.

Suppose
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a chain sequence with
\{m_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
as its minimal parameter sequence. Let
\{k_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be another sequence given by
k_{0}=0
and
k_{n}=1-m_{n}
for
n\geq 1
. Then the chain sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
having

\{k_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
as its minimal parameter sequence is called the complementary chain sequence of
\{d_{n}\}
.

Such chain sequences enjoy interesting relations like
[
26
, equation (75.3)]

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{1+z}}{1+\frac{d_{1}z}{1+\frac{d_{2}z}{1+\frac{d_{3}z}{1+\ddots}}}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{1+z}}{1+\frac{a_{1}z}{1+\frac{a_{2}z}{1+\frac{a_{3}z}{1+\ddots}}}}=1.

They also satisfy

\displaystyle d_{1}-a_{1}=1-2k_{1}=2m_{1}-1

and

\displaystyle d_{n}-a_{n}=\triangle{m_{n-1}}=-\nabla{k_{n}},\qquad n\geq 2.

where
\triangle
and
\nabla
are the forward and backward difference operators respectively. Further of particular interest is the ratio of these two chain sequences given by

\displaystyle\frac{d_{1}}{a_{1}}=\frac{m_{1}}{1-m_{1}},\qquad\frac{d_{n}}{a_{n}}=\frac{k_{n-1}}{1-k_{n-1}}\frac{m_{n}}{1-m_{n}},\qquad n\geq 2.

This implies

\displaystyle\frac{m_{n}}{1-m_{n}}=\frac{d_{n}}{a_{n}}\frac{m_{n-1}}{1-m_{n-1}}=\cdots=\frac{d_{n}d_{n-1}\cdots d_{1}}{a_{n}a_{n-1}\cdots a_{1}},\qquad n\geq 1.

(2.2)

Substituting (
2.2
) in (
2.1
), we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}

be two complementary chain sequences of each other. Then
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
will be a SPPCS if and only if

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{d_{1}d_{2}\cdots d_{j}}{a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{j}}=\infty.

Remark 2.3
.

The above lemma is useful while considering a chain sequence and its complementary chain sequence without using the information on the corresponding minimal parameters.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be two complementary chain sequences of each other. If
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is not a SPPCS, then
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a SPPCS.

Proof.

If
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is not a SPPCS then its minimal parameter sequence
\{m_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{m_{j}}{1-m_{j}}<\infty.

Hence,
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}/(1-m_{j})=0
, and we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{k_{j}}{1-k_{j}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1-m_{j}}{m_{j}}=\infty.

Thus, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a chain sequence and
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be its complementary chain sequence with minimal parameter sequences
\{m_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
\{k_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

respectively.

–

If
0<m_{n}<1/2
,
n\geq 1
, then
a_{n}
is a SPPCS.

–

If
1/2<m_{n}<1
,
n\geq 1
, then
d_{n}
is a SPPCS.

Proof.

Observe that if
0<m_{n}<1/2
,
k_{n}/(1-k_{n})>1
for all
n\geq 1
. Similarly,
1/2<m_{n}<1
implies
m_{n}/(1-m_{n})>1
for all
n\geq 1
. The results now follow from (
2.1
).
∎

It is known that
[
26
, p. 79]
if
d_{n}\geq 1/4
,
n\geq 1
, every parameter sequence
\{g_{n}\}
, in particular the minimal parameter sequence
\{m_{n}\}
of
\{d_{n}\}
is non-decreasing. For the special case when
d_{n}=1/4
,
n\geq 1
,
m_{n}\rightarrow 1/2
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This implies
0<m_{n}<1/2
,
n\geq 1
.
By Lemma
2.5
,
\{a_{n}\}
is a SPPCS. In other words, the chain sequence complementary to the constant chain sequence
\{1/4\}
determines its parameters
g_{n}
uniquely, which are further given by

\displaystyle g_{0}=0,\qquad g_{n}=\frac{n+2}{2(n+1)},\qquad n\geq 1.

Moreover, if
d_{n}\geq 1/4
, there exist some
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
a_{n}<1/4\leq d_{n}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle d_{n}=(1-m_{n-1})m_{n}\geq m_{n-1}(1-m_{n})=a_{n},\qquad n\geq 2,

with the sign of the difference of
d_{1}
and
a_{1}
depending on whether
m_{1}\in(0,1/2)
or
(1/2,1)
. If
a_{n}\in(1/4,1)
for
n\geq 1
,
k_{n}
has to be
non-decreasing. This is a contradiction as
k_{n}=1-m_{n}
for
n\geq 1
.

The effect of complementary chain sequences in studying perturbation of Verblunsky coefficients given by (
1.6
) has interesting consequences. In this context, we give the following result.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\{c_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\{d_{n+1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be, respectively, the real sequence and positive chain sequence as given in (
1.4
). Let
\big\{m_{n}^{(t)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the minimal parameter sequence of the augmented positive chain sequence
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, where
d_{1}=(1-t)M_{1}
and
\{M_{n+1}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
is the maximal parameter sequence of
\{d_{n+1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Let
\big\{k_{n}^{(t)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
be the minimal parameter sequence of the positive chain sequence
\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
obtained as complementary to
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Set
\tau_{n}=\frac{1-ic_{n}}{1+ic_{n}}\tau_{n-1}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}=\overline{\tau}_{n}\left[\frac{1-2m_{n}^{(t)}-ic_{n}}{1+ic_{n}}\right]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\beta_{n-1}^{(t)}=\overline{\tau}_{n}\left[\frac{1-2k_{n}^{(t)}-ic_{n}}{1+ic_{n}}\right],

for
n\geq 1
, with
\tau_{0}=1
. Let
\mu^{(t)}(z)
and
\nu^{(t)}(z)
be, respectively, the probability measures having

1.

For

2.

\beta_{n-1}^{(t)}=-\overline{\tau}_{n}\overline{\tau}_{n-1}\overline{\alpha}_{n-1}^{(t)}

3.

For

,

\beta_{n-1}^{(t)}=-\frac{1-ic}{1+ic}\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}
,
n\geq 1
.

4.

If
c_{n}=0
,
n\geq 1
then the Verblunsky coefficients, which are real, are such that
\beta_{n-1}^{(t)}=-\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}
,
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

First we observe that
\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}
are the generalized Verblunsky coefficients of the measure
\mu^{(t)}(z)
as given by (
1.5
). Consequently, for
0<t<1
the probability measure
\mu^{(t)}(z)
has a pure point of size
t
at
z=1
. Since
d_{1}=(1-t)M_{n}
, choosing
M_{0}=t>0
, the sequence
\{t,M_{1},M_{2},M_{3},\ldots\}
is the maximal parameter sequence of
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Since
t>0
,
\{d_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is a non SPPCS and hence, by Lemma
2.4
the sequence

is a SPPCS so that

is also its maximal parameter sequence. Thus, by results established in
[

, the measure
\nu^{(t)}(z)
does not have a pure point at

Now to prove the second part, we first have

\displaystyle\beta_{n-1}^{(t)}=\overline{\tau}_{n}\left[\frac{1-2k_{n}^{(t)}-ic_{n}}{1+ic_{n}}\right]=\overline{\tau}_{n}\left[\frac{-1+2m_{n}^{(t)}-ic_{n}}{1+ic_{n}}\right].

By conjugation of the expression for
\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}
, we have

\displaystyle-\overline{\alpha}_{n-1}^{(t)}=\tau_{n}\left[\frac{-1+2m_{n}^{(t)}-ic_{n}}{1-ic_{n}}\right],

which leads to the second part of the theorem.

Clearly with
c_{n}=(-1)^{n}c
,
n\geq 1
we have
\overline{\tau}_{2n}=1
and
\overline{\tau}_{2n+1}=\frac{1-ic}{1+ic}
. Thus, the third part of
the theorem is established.

The last part follows by taking
\overline{\tau}_{n}\overline{\tau}_{n-1}=1
,
n\geq 1
. This is only possible if

,
n\geq 1
.
∎

3
An illustration involving Carathéodory functions

In a series of papers
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, Jones et al. during their investigation of the connection between Szegő polynomials and continued fractions introduced the following

\displaystyle\delta_{0}-\frac{2\delta_{0}}{1}\begin{array}[]{cc}\\
$+$\end{array}\frac{1}{\bar{\delta}_{1}z}\begin{array}[]{cc}\\
$+$\end{array}\frac{\big(1-|\delta_{1}|^{2}\big)z}{\delta_{1}}\begin{array}[]{cc}\\
$+$\end{array}\frac{1}{\bar{\delta}_{2}z}\begin{array}[]{cc}\\
$+$\end{array}\frac{\big(1-|\delta_{2}|^{2}\big)z}{\delta_{2}z}\begin{array}[]{cc}\\
$+$\end{array}\cdots.

These are called Hermitian Perron–Carathéodory fractions or HPC-fractions and are also used to solve the trigonometric moment problem. They are completely determined by
\delta_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\delta_{0}\neq 0
and
|\delta_{n}|\neq 1
for
n\geq 1
. Under the stronger conditions
\delta_{0}>0
and
|\delta_{n}|<1
, for
n\geq 1
, (
3
) is called a positive PC fraction (PPC-fractions). Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}(z)
and
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(z)
be respectively the
numerator and denominator of the
n^{th}
approximant of a PPC-fraction where
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(z)
is a polynomial of degree
n
and
\mathcal{P}_{n}(z)
of degree at most
n
. Then
[
15
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2]

\Phi_{n}(z)
are precisely the odd ordered denominators
\mathcal{Q}_{2n+1}(z)
and
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)
the even ordered denominators
\mathcal{Q}_{2n}(z)
. The
\delta_{n}^{\prime}s
are then given by
\delta_{n}=\Phi_{n}(0)
and are called the Schur parameters or the reflection coefficients. This gives the following equivalent set of recurrence relations for the Szegő polynomials:

\displaystyle\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)=\bar{\delta}_{n}z\Phi_{n-1}{z}+\Phi_{n-1}^{*}(z),

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}(z)=\delta_{n}\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)+\big(1-|\delta_{n}|^{2}\big)z\Phi_{n-1}(z),\qquad n\geq 1.

Further, if (
3
) is a positive PC-fraction, there exists a pair of formal power series

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mu_{0}+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{k}z^{k},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=-\mu_{0}-2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{-k}z^{-k},

where
\mu_{k}
are the moments as defined earlier and such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}-\Lambda_{0}\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{2n}}{\mathcal{Q}_{2n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\big(z^{n+1}\big),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\infty}-\Lambda_{\infty}\left(\frac{\mathcal{P}_{2n+1}}{\mathcal{Q}_{2n+1}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{z^{n+1}}\right).

Here,
\Lambda_{0}(\mathcal{R}(z))
and
\Lambda_{\infty}(\mathcal{R}(z))
are the Laurent series expansion of the rational function
\mathcal{R}(z)
about 0 and
\infty
respectively. For details regarding correspondence of continued fractions to power series, see
[
16
,
17
]
.

For
|\zeta|<1
, the polynomials

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}(z)=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{z+\zeta}{z-\zeta}(\Phi_{n}(z)-\Phi_{n}(\zeta))d\mu(\zeta),\qquad n\geq 1,

are known in literature as the associated Szegő polynomials or polynomials of the second kind
[
10
]
. They arise as the odd ordered numerators of
(
3
). The function
-\Psi^{*}_{n}(z)
is called the polynomial associated with
\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)
and are the even ordered numerators in (
3
). It is also known that for
|z|<1
, there exists a function
\mathcal{C}(z)=\int_{\partial\mathbb{D}}\frac{\zeta+z}{\zeta-z}d\mu(\zeta)
with
\operatorname{Re}\mathcal{C}(z)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(z)-\frac{\Psi^{*}_{n}(z)}{\Phi^{*}_{n}(z)}=\mathcal{O}\big(z^{n+1}\big).

\mathcal{C}(z)
is called the Carathéodory function associated with the PPC-fraction (
3
) or with the Szegő polynomials
\Phi_{n}(z)
obtained from this PPC-fraction. The ratio
\Psi_{n}(z)/\Phi_{n}(z)
also converges to a function
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(z)
called the Carathéodory reciprocal of
\mathcal{C}(z)

[
14
]
and is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(z)=-\overline{\hat{\mathcal{C}}(1/\bar{z})}.

The convergence is uniform on compact subsets of
|z|<1
and
|z|>1
respectively. Also,
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is the Taylor series expansion of
\mathcal{C}(z)
about 0 and
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
is that of
\hat{\mathcal{C}}(z)
about
\infty
.

Consider the sequence
\{\delta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
, which satisfies
\delta_{0}>0
,
|\delta_{n}|<1
and

\displaystyle\delta_{n+1}-\delta_{n}=\delta_{n}\delta_{n+1},\qquad n\geq 1.

(3.11)

Our aim in this section is to use a chain sequence to construct the Szegő polynomials
\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(z)
, having
\delta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and satisfying (
3.11
) as the Verblunsky coefficients. We will also use the complementary chain sequence to get another sequence of
Szegő polynomials
\tilde{\Phi}_{n}^{(t)}(z)
which has
-\delta_{n}
as the Verblunsky coefficients. The associated Carathéodory function in each case is also given and it is shown that there exists a relation between them.

We start with the sequence
\big\{m_{n}^{(t)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
, where
m_{0}^{(t)}=0
and
m_{n}^{(t)}=(1-\delta_{n})/2
,
n\geq 1
. These minimal parameters are obtained by first substituting
c_{k}=0
,
k\geq 1
in the Verblunsky coefficients (
1.6
) and then equating them to
\delta_{n}
. The corresponding chain sequence is

\displaystyle d_{1}=\frac{1-\delta_{1}}{2}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad d_{n}=\frac{1}{4}(1+\delta_{n-1})(1-\delta_{n})=\frac{1}{4}(1-2\delta_{n-1}\delta_{n}),\qquad n\geq 2.

The following are two algebraic relations of
\delta_{n}
which will be needed later and can be proved by simple induction using (
3.11
).

\displaystyle\delta_{1}\delta_{2}+\delta_{2}\delta_{3}+\delta_{3}\delta_{4}+\cdots+\delta_{n}\delta_{n+1}=\delta_{n+1}-\delta_{1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

and

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=\frac{\delta_{n+1}}{1+\delta_{n+1}}=\cdots=\frac{\delta_{n+k}}{1+k\delta_{n+k}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

(3.12)

Proposition 3.1
.

The monic polynomial

\displaystyle R_{n}(z)=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}[1+2k(n-k)\delta_{1}\delta_{n}]z^{k}

(3.13)

satisfies the recurrence relation

\displaystyle R_{n+1}(z)=(z+1)R_{n}(z)-(1-2\delta_{n}\delta_{n+1})zR_{n-1}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

with the initial conditions,
R_{0}(z)=1
and
R_{1}(z)=z+1
.

Proof.

First, note that
R_{1}(z)
given by (
3.13
) satisfies the initial condition. Suppose
R_{n}(z)
has this form and satisfies the recurrence relation for
n=1,2,\dots,j
. We shall now show

\displaystyle R_{j+1}(z)+(1-2\delta_{j}\delta_{j+1})zR_{j-1}(z)=(z+1)R_{j}(z).

(3.14)

Using (
3.12
), the coefficient of
z^{k}
in the left-hand side of (
3.14
) is

\displaystyle 1+2k(j-k+1)\delta_{1}\delta_{j+1}+(1-2\delta_{j}\delta_{j+1})[1+2(k-1)(j-k)\delta_{1}\delta_{j-1}]

\displaystyle\qquad{}=1+2\frac{k(j-k+1)}{j}(\delta_{j+1}-\delta_{1})+1-2(\delta_{j+1}-\delta_{j})+2\frac{(k-1)(j-k)}{j-2}(\delta_{j-1}-\delta_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad{}-\frac{2\cdot 2(k-1)(j-k)}{j-2}(\delta_{j-1}-\delta_{1})(\delta_{j+1}-\delta_{j}).

(3.15)

It is easy to verify that the coefficients of
\delta_{j+1}
and
\delta_{j-1}
vanish in (
3.15
). The coefficient of
\delta_{1}
is

\displaystyle-\frac{2k(j-k+1)}{j}-\frac{2(k-1)(j-k)}{j-2}-\frac{2\cdot 2(k-1)(j-k)}{j(j-2)}+\frac{2\cdot 2(k-1)(j-k)}{(j-1)(j-2)}

\displaystyle\qquad{}=-\frac{2k(j-k)}{j-1}-\frac{2(k-1)(j-k+1)}{j-1}.

(3.16)

Similarly, the coefficient of
\delta_{j}
is

\displaystyle 2+\frac{2\cdot 2(k-1)(j-k)}{j-1}=\frac{2k(j-k)}{j-1}+\frac{2(k-1)(j-k+1)}{j-1}.

(3.17)

Using (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) in (
3.15
), the coefficient of
z^{k}
in the left-hand side of (
3.14
) is given by

\displaystyle[1+2(k-1)(j-k+1)\delta_{1}\delta_{j}]+[1+2k(j-k)\delta_{1}\delta_{j}],

which is nothing but the coefficient of
z^{k}
in the right-hand side of (
3.14
). Hence, by induction the proof is complete.
∎

We now obtain the Szegő polynomials
\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(z)
from the para-orthogonal polynomials

given by (

). Using (

) and (

, in

is

. Hence, the Szegő polynomials are given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(z)=z^{n}-\delta_{n}\big[(1-2(n-1)\delta_{1})z^{n-1}+\cdots+(1-2\delta_{1})z+1\big],\qquad n\geq 1,

(3.18)

with
\alpha_{n-1}^{(t)}=-\delta_{n}
.

We now give the Carathéodory function associated with the parameters
\delta_{n}
’s given by (
3.11
). Consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(z)=1-\frac{2(1-\sigma)z}{1+(1-2\sigma)z}=\frac{1-z}{1+(1-2\sigma)z},\qquad|z|<1,

where
0<\sigma<1
. That
\mathcal{C}(z)
corresponds to a PPC-fraction with the parameter
\gamma_{n}
, where

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\frac{1}{n+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}},\qquad n\geq 1.

(3.19)

can be shown by applying the algorithm
[
14
]
which is similar to the Schur algorithm. With the initial values
\mathcal{C}_{0}(z)=(1-z)/(1+(1-2\sigma)z)
,
\gamma_{0}=\mathcal{C}_{0}(0)=1
, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}(z)=\frac{\gamma_{0}-\mathcal{C}_{0}(z)}{\gamma_{0}+\mathcal{C}_{0}(z)},\qquad\gamma_{1}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{1}(0).

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}(z)=\frac{z}{1+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}-\left(1-\frac{1-2\sigma}{1-\sigma}\right)z},\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\gamma_{1}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}.

Assume for

the following

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}(z)=\frac{z}{k+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}-\left(k-\frac{1-2\sigma}{1-\sigma}\right)z},\qquad\gamma_{k}=\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{k}(0).

This is true for

. Now define

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k+1}(z)=\frac{\gamma_{k}z-\mathcal{C}_{k}(z)}{\gamma_{k}\mathcal{C}_{k}(z)-z},\qquad n\geq 1.

(3.20)

It can be shown that

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\frac{1-\sigma}{k-(k-1)\sigma}=\frac{1}{k+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}},

which is also true for

. Simplifying (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k+1}=\frac{z}{\left(k+1+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}\right)-\left(k+1-\frac{1-2\sigma}{1-\sigma}\right)z},

from which

. Hence by induction, (

\delta_{n}=-\gamma_{n}
satisfies (
3.11
) and so
\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(0)=\frac{1}{n+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}
.

From the power series expansion of
\mathcal{C}(z)
, we also obtain the moments as

\displaystyle\mu_{0}=1,\qquad\mu_{k}=(-1)^{k}(1-\alpha)(1-2\alpha)^{k-1},\qquad k\geq 1.

Using the fact that the Verblunsky coefficients are all real, from (
1.2
), we have

\displaystyle\chi_{n}^{-2}=\prod_{k=1}^{n}\big(1-\delta_{k}^{2}\big).

Further

\displaystyle\delta_{n}=\frac{1}{n+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}}=\frac{1-\sigma}{n(1-\sigma)+\sigma},\qquad n\geq 1,

and we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&1-\delta_{n}^{2}=\frac{[n(1-\sigma)+\sigma-1+\sigma][n(1-\sigma)+\sigma+1-\sigma]}{[n(1-\sigma)+\sigma]^{2}}\\
&\hphantom{1-\delta_{n}^{2}}{}=\frac{[(n-1)-(n-2)\sigma][(n+1)-n\sigma]}{[n-(n-1)\sigma]^{2}},\end{split}

which yields the fact that

\displaystyle\chi_{n}^{-2}=\frac{\sigma[(n+1)-n\sigma]}{[n-(n-1)\sigma]}.

Rewriting the right-hand expression as

\displaystyle\chi_{n}^{-2}=\big\|\Phi_{n}^{(t)}(z)\big\|^{2}=\sigma(1+\delta_{n}),

which tends to

as

Consider now the parameter sequence
\big\{k_{n}^{(t)}\big\}_{n=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{n}(z)=1+z+z^{2}+\cdots+z^{n}=\frac{1-z^{n+1}}{1-z},\qquad n\geq 1.

Then (

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{n}^{(t)}(z)=z^{n}+\delta_{n}z^{n-1}+\cdots+\delta_{n}z+\delta_{n},\qquad n\geq 1,

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}_{n}^{(t)}(0)=\delta_{n}=-\frac{1}{n+\frac{\sigma}{1-\sigma}},\qquad n\geq 1.

4
An illustration using Gaussian hypergeometric functions

The Gaussian hypergeometric function, with the complex parameters
a
,
b
and
c
is defined by the power series

\displaystyle F(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{n}(b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}(1)_{n}}z^{n},\qquad|z|<1,

where
c\neq 0,-1,-2,\dots
and
(a)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol. With specialized values of the parameters
a
,
b
and
c
, many elementary functions can be
represented by the Gaussian hypergeometric functions or their ratios. If
\operatorname{Re}(c-a-b)>0
, the series converges for
|z|=1
to the value given by

\displaystyle F(a,b;c;1)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}k!}=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}.

In case the series is terminating, we have the Chu–Vandermonde identity
[
1
]

\displaystyle F(-n,b;c;1)=\frac{(c-b)_{n}}{(c)_{n}}.

(4.1)

Two hypergeometric functions
F(a_{1},b_{1};c_{1};z)
and
F(a_{2},b_{2};c_{2},z)
are said to be contiguous if the difference between the corresponding parameters
is at most unity. A linear combination of two contiguous hypergeometric functions is again a hypergeometric function. Such relations are called contiguous relations and have been used to explore many hidden properties of the hypergeometric functions, for example by Gauss who found continued fraction expansions for ratios of hypergeometric functions
[
19
]
and hence for the special functions that these ratios represent. In some special cases, the contiguous
relations can also be related to the recurrence relations for orthogonal polynomials. Consider one such relation
[
1
]

\displaystyle(c-a)F(a-1,b;c;z)=(c-2a-(b-a)z)F(a,b;c;z)+a(1-z)F(a+1,b;c;z),

which as shown in
[
24
]
, can be transformed to the three term recurrence relation

\displaystyle\varrho_{n+1}(z)=\left(z+\frac{c-b+n}{b+n}\right)\varrho_{n}(z)-\frac{n(c+n-1)}{(b+n-1)(b+n)}\varrho_{n-1}(z),\qquad n\geq 1,

(4.2)

satisfied by the monic polynomial

\displaystyle\varrho_{n}(z)=\frac{(c)_{n}}{(b)_{n}}F(-n,b;c;1-z).

(4.3)

It was also shown that for the specific values
b=\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
c=2\lambda-1
, the polynomials (
4.3
) are Szegő polynomials. We note that with
b=\lambda+1
,
\varrho_{n}(z)
given by (
4.3
) are called the circular Jacobi polynomials
[
12
, Example 8.2.5]
. For other specialized values of
b
and
c
in (
4.2
),
