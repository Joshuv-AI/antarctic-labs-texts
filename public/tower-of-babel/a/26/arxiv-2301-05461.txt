Hypergraph Horn Functions
Kristóf Bérczi, Endre Boros, Kazuhisa Makino

Abstract
Horn functions form a subclass of Boolean functions possessing interesting structural and computational properties. These functions play a fundamental role in algebra, artificial intelligence, combinatorics, computer science, database theory, and logic. In the present paper, we introduce the subclass of hypergraph Horn functions that generalizes matroids and equivalence relations. We provide multiple characterizations of hypergraph Horn functions in terms of implicate-duality and the closure operator, which are respectively regarded as generalizations of matroid duality and Mac Lane – Steinitz exchange property of matroid closure. We also study algorithmic issues on hypergraph Horn functions, and show that the recognition problem (i.e., deciding if a given definite Horn CNF represents a hypergraph Horn function) and key realization (i.e., deciding if a given hypergraph is realized as a key set by a hypergraph Horn function) can be done in polynomial time, while implicate sets can be generated with polynomial delay. Keywords: Algorithms, Duality, Horn functions, Hypergraphs

Introduction

In this paper we introduce and study a special subclass of Horn functions that is rich in mathematical structure and has advantageous algorithmic properties.

Horn logic is a segment of propositional logic that came to prominence with the advent of computers, relational databases, logic programming, see e.g.,

Horn expressions and functions appear in many equivalent forms in mathematics and computers science, such as directed hypergraphs, implications systems,
closure operators, functional dependencies in relational databases, formal concept analysis, etc. We refer the reader to
[
1

for a more comprehensive overview of these equivalent areas and related literature.

In this paper we use conjunctive normal form (CNF) representations for Boolean functions, and view Horn clauses equivalently as simple implications. For instance if
x_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
are propositional variables (that is logical variables that can only take false/true values), then
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\to x_{3}
is such a simple implication that can also be represented as a clause (an elementary disjunction) of the form
(\overline{x}_{1}\vee\overline{x}_{2}\vee x_{3})
, where
\overline{x}_{i}
denotes the logical negation of
x_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Horn functions can be represented by a collection (conjunction) of such implication rules. If for this example we also have
\{x_{1},x_{3}\}\to x_{2}
and
\{x_{2},x_{3}\}\to x_{1}
as rules of the given Horn system, then we call
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}\to x_{3}
circular. The subject of our study is the family of Horn functions that can be represented by a collection of circular implication rules. Since such a circular rule can be represented by the set of the propositional variables appearing in it (since any one is implied by the others), the Horn functions we are interested in can be represented by a hypergraph over the set of variables. For this reason we call this subfamily
hypergraph Horn
.

The family of hypergraph Horn functions turns out to be highly structured and still very general. They generalize equivalence relations, and more generally matroids. We provide various characterizations for this class, allowing to derive efficient algorithms for several related algorithmic problems.

We introduce a new type of “duality”, called implicate-duality or
i
-duality for short. We show that every Boolean function has a unique
i
-dual, which is always hypergraph Horn. Furthermore,
i
-duality is an involution over the set of hypergraph Horn functions. It turns out that
i
-duality generalizes matroid duality. More precisely, if a hypergraph Horn function corresponds to a matroid, then its
i
-dual corresponds to the dual matroid.
We provide a characterization for self
i
-dual functions, which is surprisingly analogous to the characterization of self dual monotone Boolean functions (see e.g.,
[
3
]
).
We also characterize hypergraph Horn functions in terms of the standard closure operator. Our characterization can be regarded as an extension of the Mac Lane – Steinitz exchange property of matroid closure (see the companion paper
[
2
]
).

While satisfiability (SAT) is easy for Horn CNFs, one typical difficulty in this area arises from the fact that Horn functions have many different Horn CNF representations. For instance, an arbitrary Horn function may have several circular rules and several rules that are not circular. It may be representable by a collections of circular rules, but it may also have representations that involve non-circular rules. We provide an efficient algorithm that recognizes if a given Horn CNF defines a hypergraph Horn function or not. If yes, the algorithm outputs a circular representation the size of which is polynomially bounded by the size of the input CNF. We also provide an algorithm that generates all circular rules incrementally efficiently for a Horn function represented by a Horn CNF. Note that there might be an exponential number of such rules.

Keys are another important notion of Horn theory. A subset of the variables is a key of a given Horn function if the truth of these variables imply the truth of all other variables. The set of minimal keys plays important roles in applications, in particular in databases. We provide an efficient algorithm that decides if a given hypergraph is the set of minimal keys of a hypergraph Horn function or not, and if yes, it outputs a circular representation of such a function the size of which is polynomially bounded by the size of the given hypergraph.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. While we use standard terminology and notation, for clarity we introduce precise definitions in the next section. In Section

4

5

we provide the efficient algorithms mentioned above, while in the last section we close with listing a few open problems.

Hypergraphs.

For a finite set

\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}

\mathcal{H}

. We denote by
\mathcal{H}^{d}
the
family of minimal transversals
of
\mathcal{H}
. For simplicity, we omit the parentheses from the notation when applying these operations repeatedly, for example, we write
\mathcal{H}^{dc}=(\mathcal{H}^{d})^{c}

\mathcal{H}^{cd}=(\mathcal{H}^{c})^{d}
. For a Sperner hypergraph

of

Typically we reserve the notation

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-bases-circuits}\mathcal{B}^{cd}=\mathcal{C}~~~\text{ and equivalently }~~~\mathcal{C}^{dc}=\mathcal{B}.

Independent sets are exactly the subsets of hyperedges of

For an arbitrary hypergraph

\mathcal{H}^{\cap}~=~\left\{\left.\bigcap_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F~\right|~\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\right\}.

We note that the intersection of an empty family is defined as
V
, and thus we have
V\in\mathcal{H}^{\cap}
for all hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
. Analogously, we denote by
\mathcal{H}^{\cup}
the
union closure
of
\mathcal{H}
defined as

\mathcal{H}^{\cup}~=~\left\{\left.\bigcup_{F\in\mathcal{F}}F~\right|~\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{H}\right\}.

We note that the union of an empty family is defined as the empty set, and thus we have
\emptyset\in\mathcal{H}^{\cup}
for all hypergraphs
\mathcal{H}
.

Horn functions.

We denote by
V
the set of
n
Boolean variables
x
and call these together with their negations
\overline{x}
as
literals
. Members of
V
are called
positive literals
while their negations are
negative literals
. A disjunction of a subset of the literals is called a
clause
if it contains no complementary pair of literals

\overline{x}

conjunctive normal form
(or in short a

[
7
]
) that every Boolean function

can be represented by a CNF (typically not in a unique way). We use Greek letters to denote CNFs, and Latin letters to denote (Boolean) functions. For a CNF
\Psi=\bigwedge_{k}C_{k}
, let
|\Psi|
denote the number of clauses in
\Psi
, and let
\|\Psi\|
denote the length of
\Psi
, i.e.,
\|\Psi\|=\sum_{k}|C_{k}|
.

Truth assignments (i.e., Boolean vectors)
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{V}
can be viewed equivalently as characteristic vectors of subsets. For a subset
Z\subseteq V
we denote by
\chi_{Z}\in\{0,1\}^{V}
its characteristic vector, i.e.,
(\chi_{Z})_{i}=1
if and only if
i\in Z
. Since we use primarily a combinatorial notation in this paper, we use for a function
f
and Boolean expression
\Phi
the notation
f(Z)
and
\Phi(Z)
instead of
f(\chi_{Z})
and
\Phi(\chi_{Z})
, to denote the evaluation of
f
and
\Phi
at the binary vector
\chi_{Z}
, respectively. We say that a set
Z\subseteq V
is a
true set
of
f
if
f(Z)=1
, and a
false set
otherwise. We denote by
\mathcal{T}(f)
and
\mathcal{F}(f)
the
families of true sets
and
false sets
of
f
, respectively. Every Boolean expression defines/represents a unique Boolean function. If
A
and
B
denote Boolean functions or Boolean expressions, we write
A=B
if
A(X)=B(X)
for all
X\subseteq V
, and
A\leq B
if
A(X)\leq B(X)
for all
X\subseteq V
, where
B
is called a
majorant
of

in the latter case. We also write

.

A clause is called

if it contains exactly one positive literal.
It is easy to see that definite Horn clauses represent simple implications. Namely, for a proper subset
B\subsetneq V
and
v\in V\setminus B
the implication
B\to v
is equivalent to the definite Horn clause
C=v\vee\left(\bigvee_{u\in B}\overline{u}\right)
: the true sets of both expressions are exactly the sets
T\subseteq V
such that either
T\not\supseteq B
or
T\supseteq B\cup\{v\}
.
A CNF is called
definite Horn
if it consists of definite Horn clauses, and a Boolean function is called
definite Horn
if it can be represented by a definite Horn CNF.
The following characterization of definite Horn functions is well-known.

Lemma 1

(see e.g.,

[
17
,
7
]
)
.

A Boolean function
f
is definite Horn if and only if the family
\mathcal{T}(f)
of its true sets is closed under intersection and contains
V
.

\Box

Lemma
1
implies that for any set
Z\subseteq V
there exists a unique minimal true set containing
Z
, the so-called
closure
of
Z
denoted by
\mathbb{T}_{h}(Z)
. In fact such a closure can be computed efficiently from any definite Horn CNF representation
\Phi
of
h
by the so-called
forward chaining procedure
(see e.g.,
[
7
]
): Let
A\subseteq V
denote the set of all variables
v\in V\setminus Z
for which there exists a clause
B\to v
in
\Phi
with
B\subseteq Z
and
v\in V\setminus Z
, and define
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(Z)\coloneqq Z\cup A
. For
i\geq 2
we define
\mathbb{T}^{i}_{\Phi}(Z)=\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(\mathbb{T}^{i-1}_{\Phi}(Z))
. Since
Z\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(Z)\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{2}_{\Phi}(Z)\subseteq\dots\subseteq V
,
\mathbb{T}^{i+1}_{\Phi}(Z)=\mathbb{T}^{i}_{\Phi}(Z)
holds for some integer
i\leq n
. Let
i^{*}
be the smallest such index

. Then we have
\mathbb{T}^{t}_{\Phi}(Z)=\mathbb{T}^{i^{*}}_{\Phi}(Z)
for all

, and
\mathbb{T}^{i^{*}}_{\Phi}(Z)
is the minimal true set of
\Phi
that contains
Z
. Thus we can define
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi}(Z)=\mathbb{T}^{i^{*}}_{\Phi}(Z)
, and we say that
Z
can be closed by
\Phi

steps
. While we may have
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi}(Z)\neq\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Psi}(Z)

\Phi

\Psi

[
7
]
) that the resulting set
\mathbb{T}_{\Phi}(Z)
does not depend on the particular choice of the representation

\mathbb{T}_{h}(Z)=\mathbb{T}_{\Phi}(Z)
for this uniquely defined closure of

closed

For a definite Horn function

A clause

Given a definite Horn CNF

A subset

\mathcal{K}(h)=\{K\subseteq V\mid\mathbb{T}_{h}(K)=V\ \text{and}\ \mathbb{T}_{h}(K^{\prime})\neq V\ \text{for all $K^{\prime}\subsetneq K$}\},

of

Note that

of

Both families,

It is easy to verify that maximal nontrivial true sets form the family of maximal independent sets of the family of minimal keys, and minimal keys are exactly the minimal sets that are not contained in a maximal nontrivial true set.

Lemma 3

(see e.g.,
[

]
)

For a definite Horn function

\mathcal{M}(h)=\mathcal{K}(h)^{dc}

\mathcal{K}(h)=\mathcal{M}(h)^{cd}

\Box

For a Sperner hypergraph

let us denote by

Given a hypergraph

(a)

\mathcal{K}^{-1}(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\mathcal{H}^{dc})
.

(b)

If

(c)

Lemma

is a true set of

Matroids.

Matroids were introduced by Whitney
[
23
]
and independently by Nakasawa

To simplify our notation, we respectively write
H+v

and

and

. Given a definite Horn function

we associate the definite Horn CNF

defined as

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-circulant}\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}~=~\bigwedge_{H\in\mathcal{H}}\left(\bigwedge_{v\in H}\left((H-v)\to v\right)\right)

and call

associated to the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
. Note that circular CNFs generalize equivalence relations. Namely, if all hyperedges of

are of size two, then
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}
represents an equivalence relation, and all equivalence relations can be represented in this way. For example, if
\mathcal{H}=\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{4,5\}\}
, then

represents two equivalence classes:
\{1,2,3\}
and
\{4,5\}
. We remark that even equivalence relations can be represented by different hypergraphs. For example,
\mathcal{H}=\{\{1,2\},\{2,3\},\{4,5\}\}

.

We say that a definite Horn function

is

if it has a circular CNF representation, that is, if there exists a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
such that

. The next example shows that
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}

.

Consider the set
V=\{1,2,\dots,8\}
, the subsets
H_{1}=\{1,2,5\}

H_{2}=\{4,5,6\}

H_{4}=\{2,7,8\}

H_{5}=\{1,2,3,4\}

For every Boolean function

Given two families
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{V}
, we call
\mathcal{G}
a
generator
of
\mathcal{F}
if its union closure is
\mathcal{F}
, that is,
\mathcal{G}^{\cup}=\mathcal{F}
. For every Boolean function
f:2^{V}\to\{0,1\}
, there exists a unique minimal generator of
\mathcal{I}(f)
. Indeed, let
\mathcal{G}(f)
consist of those implicate sets that are not the union of others. These sets are clearly contained in any subfamily whose union closure is
\mathcal{I}(f)
, and every other set in
\mathcal{I}(f)
can be obtained as the union of such sets. We call
\mathcal{G}(f)
the
standard generator
of
\mathcal{I}(f)
. Note that
\mathcal{G}(f)
may not form a Sperner hypergraph.

Example 7
.

Let us note that the set
\mathcal{I}(h)
can be much larger than the input representation. Furthermore, it can happen that
\mathcal{I}(h)
cannot be obtained by taking unions of the input, even for hypergraph Horn functions.

For a set
V=\{i,i^{\prime}\mid i=1,\dots,n\}
of
2n
elements, consider the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=\{\{i,i^{\prime}\}\mid i=1,\dots,n\}\cup\{\{1,2,\dots,n\}\}
of
n+1
hyperedges and the corresponding hypergraph Horn function
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}
. It is not difficult to see that

\mathcal{I}(h)=(\mathcal{H}\cup\left\{Z\subseteq V\mid Z\cap\{i,i^{\prime}\}\neq\emptyset~\forall~i=1,\dots,n\right\})^{\cup}.

This family of implicate sets contains exponentially many hyperedges that are not in the union closure of the input.

As pointed out in Lemma
6
, implicate sets of a Boolean function
f
are closed under taking unions. This implies that every subset
S\subseteq V
has a unique maximal implicate set as its subset. We denote by
\mathbb{I}_{f}(S)
this unique maximal implicate subset of
S
and call it the
core
of
S
(or the
f
-
core
of
S
, if the function is not clear from the context). Recall that
\mathcal{T}(f)
denotes the family of true sets of
f
.

Lemma 8
.

For a definite Horn function
f
, we have the following equivalences:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-truesets=FC}T\in\mathcal{T}(f)\,\,\iff\,\,\mathbb{T}_{f}(T)=T,

(1a)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-implicate-set-of-haa}I\in\mathcal{I}(f)\,\,\iff\,\,\mathbb{I}_{f}(I)=I.

(1b)

Proof.

The lemma follows by the definitions, as for a subset
S\subseteq V
the closure
\mathbb{T}_{f}(S)
is the unique smallest true set containing
S
, while the core
\mathbb{I}_{f}(S)
is the unique maximal implicate set contained in
S
.
∎

The next technical lemma, together with its corollary, describes a certain duality relation between true sets and implicate sets specifically for hypergraph Horn functions, which will be useful in our proofs later.

Lemma 9
.

For a hypergraph Horn function
h
and subsets
I,T\subseteq V
, we have the following equivalences:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-true-set-of-h}T\in\mathcal{T}(h)\,\,\iff\,\,\nexists\,J\in\mathcal{I}(h)\text{ with }~|J\setminus T|=1,

(2a)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-implicate-set-of-h}I\in\mathcal{I}(h)\,\,\iff\,\,\nexists\,S\in\mathcal{T}(h)\text{ with }~|I\setminus S|=1.

(2b)

Proof.

For (
2a
), note that the right hand side is equivalent with
\mathbb{T}^{1}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{I}(h)}}(T)=T
, which is indeed equivalent with
T\in\mathcal{T}(h)
since
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{I}(h)}
is hypergraph Horn.

For (
2b
), note that the right hand side is equivalent to the claim that for every
u\in I
and for every
S\in\mathcal{T}(h)
the inclusion
I-u\subseteq S
implies
u\in S
. This is equivalent to
\mathbb{T}_{h}(I-u)\ni u
for all
u\in I
, which is the definition of
I
being an implicate set of
h
.
∎

As a direct consequence, we get the following characterizations of true and implicate sets for hypergraph Horn functions.

Corollary 10
.

For a hypergraph Horn function
h
, we have the following equalities:

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline 2-true-set-of-h}\mathcal{T}(h)~=~\{T\subseteq V\mid\nexists~I\in\mathcal{I}(h)\text{ with }~|I\setminus T|=1\},

(3a)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline 2-implicate-set-of-h}\mathcal{I}(h)~=~\{I\subseteq V\mid\nexists~T\in\mathcal{T}(h)\text{ with }~|I\setminus T|=1\}.

(3b)

\Box

Let us remark that (
2b
) and (
3b
) hold for arbitrary Boolean functions
h
, not only for hypergraph Horn ones. Moreover, later we show that both (
2a
) and (
3a
) provide necessary and sufficient conditions for
h
being hypergraph Horn.

Observe that the conjunction of two hypergraph Horn functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
is also hypergraph Horn. Indeed, if
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
are hypergraph such that
h_{i}=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}_{i}}
for
i=1,2
, then
h_{1}\wedge h_{2}=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\mathcal{H}_{2}}
. Therefore any Boolean function
f
admits a unique minimal hypergraph Horn majorant which we denote by
f^{\circ}=\Phi_{\mathcal{I}(f)}
. Note that
f^{\circ}=1
may hold for a Boolean function
f
. Note also that
f
is a hypergraph Horn function if and only if
f=f^{\circ}
.

The natural “duality” between the families of true sets and implicate sets pointed out above motivates the following definition. To a given Boolean function
f
we associate another Boolean function, called its
implicate-dual
and denoted by
f^{i}
, defined uniquely by the equality

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-f->f^{i}}\mathcal{T}(f^{i})~=~\mathcal{I}(f)^{c}.

(4)

Since
\mathcal{I}(f)=\mathcal{I}(f^{\circ})
for every Boolean function
f
, we have
f^{i}=(f^{\circ})^{i}=f^{\circ i}
. Furthermore, since
\emptyset\in\mathcal{I}(f)
we have
V\in\mathcal{T}(f^{i})
and by Lemma
6
the family
\mathcal{I}(f)^{c}
is closed under intersections. Consequently, by Lemma
1
, the function
f^{i}=f^{\circ i}
is definite Horn for every Boolean function
f
.

4
Characterizations of hypergraph Horn functions

In this section we provide multiple characterizations of hypergraph Horn functions. Recall that for a Boolean function
f
,
\mathcal{G}(f)
denotes the standard generator of
\mathcal{I}(f)
. Our main theorem below summarizes these equivalences.

Theorem 11
.

For a definite Horn function
f:2^{V}\to\{0,1\}
, the following claims are equivalent.

(i)

f
is hypergraph Horn.

(ii)

f^{\circ}=f
.

(iii)

f^{ii}=f
.

(iv)

For every false set
F
of
f
, there exists an implicate set
I\in\mathcal{I}(f)
such that
|I\setminus F|=1
.

(v)

For every false set
F
of
f
, there exists an implicate set
I\in\mathcal{G}(f)
such that
|I\setminus F|=1
.

(vi)

For every false set
F
of
f
, there exists a variable
u\in\mathbb{T}_{f}(F)\setminus F
such that
v\in\mathbb{T}_{f}(F-v+u)
holds for every
v\in F
with
f(F-v)=1
.

Let us note that property
(iii)
is a generalization of
matroid duality
. Property
(iv)
above is the same as (
2a
) and (
3a
) and characterizes hypergraph Horn functions. This property will be used in Section
5.2
for an efficient algorithm to recognize hypergraph Horn functions from definite Horn CNFs. Property
(vi)
is a generalization of the so-called
Mac Lane – Steinitz exchange property
for closure operator of matroids
[
22
]
.

In what follows, we show several simpler claims and we use those put together at the end of this section to prove Theorem
11
. Our first result shows the equivalence of
(ii)
and
(iv)
in Theorem
11
.

Lemma 12
.

For a Boolean function
f:2^{V}\to\{0,1\}
, we have
f^{\circ}=f
if and only if for every false set
F
of
f
there exists an implicate set
I\in\mathcal{I}(f)
such that
|I\setminus F|=1
.

Proof.

Since
f=f^{\circ}
is equivalent to the condition that
f
is hypergraph Horn, the only-if part follows from (
2a
). For the if part,
assume that
f\neq f^{\circ}
. Since
f\leq f^{\circ}
holds for all Boolean functions, there exists a subset
F\subseteq V
for which
f(F)=0
and
f^{\circ}(F)=1
. By applying (
2a
) to
f^{\circ}
, no implicate set
I\in\mathcal{I}(f)
satisfies
|I\setminus F|=1
, which completes the proof of the if part.
∎

Next we provide a characterization of hypergraph Horn functions that proves the equivalence of
(i)
and
(vi)
of Theorem
11
.

Lemma 13
.

A definite Horn function
h
is hypergraph Horn if and only if for every false set
F
of
h
, there exists a variable
u\in V\setminus F
such that

(i)

F\overset{h}{\to}u
, and

(ii)

F-v+u\overset{h}{\to}v
for all variables
v\in F
with
h(F-v)=1
.

Proof.

Consider a hypergraph Horn function
h
, i.e.,
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{I}(h)}
. Let us consider an arbitrary false set
F\subseteq V
of
h
. Then (
2a
) implies that there exists an implicate set
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)
with
I\setminus F=\{u\}
for some
u\in V
. We claim that
u
satisfies
(i)
and
(ii)
in Lemma
13
. Indeed,
I\setminus F=\{u\}
implies
F\overset{h}{\to}u
. Moreover, for any
v\in F
with
h(F-v)=1
, we have
I\setminus(F-v)=\{v,u\}
, as otherwise
I\setminus(F-v)=\{u\}
holds, implying that
F-v
is a false set of
h
, a contradiction. Therefore, we have
(F-v+u)\overset{h}{\to}v
, which proves
(ii)
.

For the reverse implication, assume that
h<h^{\circ}
(i.e.,
h\not=h^{\circ}
), and choose a minimal subset
F\subseteq V
such that
h(F)=0
and
h^{\circ}(F)=1
. By the minimality of
F
and by
h\leq h^{\circ}
, for every variable
v\in F
we have either
h(F-v)=1
or
h^{\circ}(F-v)=0
. Let us define
Q=\{v\in F\mid h(F-v)=1\}
and
R=F\setminus Q=\{v\in F\mid h^{\circ}(F-v)=0\}
. Since
h^{\circ}(F)=1
and
h^{\circ}(F-v)=0
for a variable
v\in R
, there exists an implicate set
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)=\mathcal{I}(h^{\circ})
such that
v\in I\subseteq F
. This implies that
(F-v)\overset{h}{\to}v
for all
v\in R
. If we assume that some
u\in V\setminus F
satisfies
(i)
and
(ii)
, then
F+u
is an implicate set of
h
. This however contradicts
h^{\circ}(F)=1
, which completes the proof of the if part of the theorem.
∎

We say that a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}
is a
minimal representation
of a hypergraph Horn function
h
if
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}=h
but
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\neq h
for every
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{H}
. The next lemma, that is of independent combinatorial interest on its own, shows that hypergraph Horn functions can be represented by their standard generators; this in turn implies the equivalence of
(iv)
and
(v)
.

Lemma 14
.

If
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{I}(h)
is a minimal representation of the hypergraph Horn function
h
and
H\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{G}(h)
, then
H
can be replaced by a member
H^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{G}(h)
such that for the resulting hypergraph
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}\setminus\{H\}\cup\{H^{\prime}\}
we have
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}=h
.

Proof.

Take an arbitrary hyperedge
H\in\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{G}(h)
, and let
\mathcal{J}=\{J_{1},\dots,J_{k}\}
be an inclusionwise minimal family of sets from
\mathcal{G}(h)
whose union is
H
. Let
H_{j}=\bigcup_{\ell\neq j}J_{\ell}
. By the minimality of
\mathcal{J}
,
H_{j}
is a proper subset of
H
for every
j
.

We claim that
H
can be replaced by
H_{j}
for some
j
; the repeated application of such a step immediately proves the theorem. Suppose to the contrary that the claim does not hold. Then for each
j
, by Lemma
9
and the minimality of
\mathcal{H}
, there exists an element
x_{j}\in H\setminus H_{j}
and a false set
F_{j}\in\mathcal{F}(h)
such that
H\setminus F_{j}=\{x_{j}\}
and
H
is the unique hyperedge of
\mathcal{H}
with that property. Let
F=F_{1}\cap F_{2}
. As
|J_{1}\setminus F|=1
,
F
is a false set by Lemma
9
. Since
H
is the only set in
\mathcal{H}
such that
|H\setminus F_{j}|=1
for both
j=1
and
2
, there exists no hyperedge
K\in\mathcal{H}
with
|K\setminus F|=1
. This contradicts our assumption that
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}=h
.
∎

The equivalence of
(iv)
and
(v)
then follows:
(iv)
is clearly a strengthening of
(v)
, while the other direction holds as
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{G}(h)}
by Lemma
14
. It is worth formulating the latter observation as a corollary.

Corollary 15
.

Any hypergraph Horn function
h
admits a minimal representation
\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{G}(h)
.
\Box

We show next that implicate-duality associates a hypergraph Horn function to every Boolean function.

Lemma 16

The function

.

Proof.

By the definition of the

is always definite Horn. Thus, it suffices to show that every false set

of

and

Let

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-F->u}F\overset{f^{i}}{\to}u~~~\mbox{ for all}~u\in U,

\bar{F}\notin\mathcal{I}(f)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q-aege1}\bar{F}-u\overset{f}{\nrightarrow}u.

We claim that this

To prove the claim, let us assume that there exists a variable

that

∎

Next we show that implicate-duality is an involution when restricted to the family of hypergraph Horn functions. More precisely, we prove the following statement.

Lemma 17
.

We have
f^{ii}=f^{\circ}
for every Boolean function

.

Proof.

Since a Boolean function is uniquely determined by its family of true sets, the statement is equivalent to the equality

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-Ifi=Tfcirc}\mathcal{T}(f^{\circ})~=~\mathcal{I}(f^{i})^{c}.

(6)

by the definition of the implicate-dual of

. Note that

and Lemma

\nexists J\in\mathcal{I}(f^{\circ})\text{ s.t. }|J\setminus T|=1\,\,\iff\,\,\nexists S\in\mathcal{T}(f^{i})\text{ s.t. }|\bar{T}\setminus S|=1.

5
Computational problems for hypergraph Horn functions

In this section, we consider computational problems for hypergraph Horn functions. We first show that the core
\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}(S)
can be computed in polynomial time from a definite Horn CNF
\Psi
and an arbitrary set
S\subseteq V
. Note that operators
\mathbb{T}
and
\mathbb{I}
play a dual role in hypergraph Horn functions. While
\mathbb{T}_{\Psi}(S)
is possible to compute in linear time,
\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}(S)
seems to be difficult to compute in linear time. We then show that the recognition problem (i.e., the problem of deciding if a given definite Horn CNF represents a hypergraph Horn function) and the key realization problem (i.e., the problem of deciding if a given hypergraph is realized as the set of minimal keys of a hypergraph Horn function
h
) can be solved in polynomial time, and implicate sets can be generated with polynomial delay. We also discuss problems related to implicate-duality.

5.1
Core computation

Let us start with the core computation, i.e., finding the largest implicate set contained in a given set. For a definite Horn CNF
\Psi
, we define

\mathbb{I}^{1}_{\Psi}(S)~=~\{v\in S\mid v\in\mathbb{T}_{\Psi}(S-v)\}.

Algorithm 1

Core Computation

1:

Input
: A definite Horn CNF
\Psi
and a set
S\subseteq V
.

2:

Output
: Core
\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}(S)
of
S
.

3:

Initialize
:
\mathbb{I}^{0}_{\Psi}(S)\coloneqq S

4:

for

k=1,2,\dots,|S|

do

5:

Compute
\mathbb{I}^{k}_{\Psi}(S)=\mathbb{I}^{1}_{\Psi}(\mathbb{I}^{k-1}_{\Psi}(S))

6:

end

for

7:

return

\mathbb{I}^{|S|}_{\Psi}(S)

The following theorem shows that Algorithm
Core Computation
computes the core in polynomial time.

Theorem 21
.

For a definite Horn CNF
\Psi
and a subset
S\subseteq V
, core
\mathbb{I}_{f}(S)
of
S
can be obtained in
O(|S|^{2}{\cdot}\|\Psi\|)
time.

Proof.

By definition, we have
\mathbb{I}^{1}_{\Psi}(S)\subseteq S
, and note that equality holds if and only if
S
is an implicate set. We also note that
\mathbb{I}^{k}_{\Psi}(S)=\mathbb{I}^{k-1}_{\Psi}(S)
implies
\mathbb{I}^{\ell}_{\Psi}(S)=\mathbb{I}^{k-1}_{\Psi}(S)
for all
\ell\geq k-1
. Since
\emptyset
is an implicate set for any Boolean function, Algorithm
1
always outputs an implicate set of
\Psi
contained in
S
. Moreover, for any implicate set
I\subseteq S
of
\Psi
, all variables
v\in I
satisfy
v\in\mathbb{T}_{f}(S-v)
, since
I-v\subseteq S-v
. Thus, the set generated by the algorithm contains all implicate sets
I
contained in
S
. This implies that the algorithm computes the core of
S
correctly. Since for a subset
X\subseteq V
and variable
v\in X
we can test the condition
v\in\mathbb{T}_{\Psi}(X-v)
in
O(\|\Psi\|)
time, the theorem follows.

5.3
Key realization

In the key realization problem we are given a hypergraph
\mathcal{K}\subseteq 2^{V}
, and the goal is to decide if
\mathcal{K}
can be realized as the set of minimal keys of a hypergraph Horn function
h
, i.e.,
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(h)
. Note that the
key set
(i.e., family of all minimal keys) of a definite Horn function is Sperner and any Sperner hypergraph is realized as the key set of a definite Horn function. Thus we may assume that
\mathcal{K}
is Sperner.

Let us first review basic properties of minimal keys and maximal nontrivial true sets of definite Horn functions. For a Sperner hypergraph
\mathcal{K}\subseteq 2^{V}
, let
\mathcal{K}^{+}
denote the family of supersets of the hyperedges of
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{K}^{+}=\{W\subseteq V\mid W\supseteq K\mbox{ for some }K\in\mathcal{K}\},

and we denote by
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{K}^{dc}
the associated family of maximal independent sets of
\mathcal{K}
. Recall that if
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(h)
for a definite Horn function
h
, then we have
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}(h)
by Lemma
3
.

Lemma 23
.

A Sperner hypergraph
\mathcal{K}
is realized as a key set of a definite Horn function
h
if and only if

(i)

\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(h)
, and

(ii)

\mathcal{K}^{+}\setminus\{V\}\subseteq\mathcal{F}(h)
.

Proof.

The statement follows from the definitions of
\mathcal{K}(h)
and
\mathcal{M}(h)
and their relation
\mathcal{M}(h)=\mathcal{K}(h)^{dc}
by Lemma
3
.
∎

We restate this lemma for the key realization problem in more constructive terms. We call a subset
I\subseteq V
a
potential implicate set
for a hypergraph
\mathcal{K}
if
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{\{I\}})
. By Lemma
23
(i)
, any implicate set of a definite Horn function
h
with
\mathcal{K}(h)=\mathcal{K}
must be a potential implicate set, since
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{K}(h)^{dc}=\mathcal{M}(h)\subseteq\mathcal{T}(h)
. Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})\subseteq 2^{V}
denote the
family of all potential implicate sets
for a Sperner hypergraph
\mathcal{K}
. As
\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{I})\cup\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{J})\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{I\cup J})
holds for any pair
I,J\subseteq V
of subsets,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})
is closed under taking union.

Lemma 24
.

A Sperner hypergraph
\mathcal{K}
is realized as a key set of a hypergraph Horn function
h
if and only if

(i)

\mathcal{I}(h)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})
, and

(ii)

for any
F\in\mathcal{K}^{+}\setminus\{V\}
there exists
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)
with
|I\setminus F|=1
.

Proof.

The statement follows from (
2a
) and Lemma
23
.
∎

Clearly, having more implicate sets makes it easier to satisfy Lemma
24
(ii), which implies the following lemma.

Lemma 25
.

A Sperner hypergraph
\mathcal{K}
is realized as the key set of a definite Horn function if and only if the hypergraph Horn function
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})}
realizes
\mathcal{K}
as its key set. Furthermore,
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})}
is the unique maximal circular minorant of the hypergraph Horn functions that have
\mathcal{K}
as their key sets, i.e., any hypergraph Horn function
f
such that
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}(f)
satisfies
f\geq\Phi_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})}
.

Proof.

Let
h
be a hypergraph Horn function represented by
\Phi_{\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})}
. Then
h
satisfies
(i)
of Lemma
23
by the definition of potential implicate sets. Furthermore, we have
h\leq f
for any hypergraph Horn function
f
satisfying Lemma
23
(i)
. This together with Lemma
23
(ii)
completes the proof.
∎

In order to compute a hypergraph Horn function that realizes
\mathcal{K}
as its key set, let us first consider potential implicate sets. The following characterization shows that it can be checked in polynomial time whether a given a subset is in
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{K})
or not.

Lemma 26
.

A subset
I\subseteq V
is a potential implicate set for a hypergraph
\mathcal{K}
if and only if

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline-pimp}\text{for every}\ K\in\mathcal{K}\ \text{and}\ u\in K\cap I\ \text{there exists}\ K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}\text{ such that }K^{\prime}\subseteq(K\cup I)-u.

(7)

We first assume that
I
is not a potential implicate set for
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.,

for some

. Then there exists a variable

such that

. By the definition of

, there exists a set

such that

and

5.4
Implicate set generation

Finally, we consider the problem of generating implicate sets of a given definite Horn CNF. Note that a definite Horn function might have exponentially many implicate sets in the size of its CNF representation (see e.g., Example
7
). It is customary to measure the complexity of such generation problems in both the size of the input and output, see e.g.,
[
13
,
21
]
. A generation algorithm is called
polynomial delay
if the time intervals between the start and the first output, between consecutive outputs, and between the last output and the halt are all bounded by a polynomial in the input size.

In order to have such an algorithm for generating implicate sets, we apply the so-called flashlight search discussed e.g., in
[
4
]
, where flashlight search explores the space of all possible solutions by checking if the current partial solution can be extended to a complete solution. More precisely, our algorithm solves the following problem as a subroutine.

Implicate Set Extension(
\Psi,X,Y
)

Input:
A definite Horn CNF
\Psi
and disjoint subsets
X
and
Y
in
V
.

Question:
Does
\Psi
has an implicate set
I
such that
X\subseteq I
and
I\cap Y=\emptyset
?

Lemma 30
.

Let
X
and
Y
be disjoint subsets in
V
, and let
\Psi
be a definite Horn CNF. Then
\Psi
has an implicate set
I
such that
X\subseteq Y
and
I\cap Y=\emptyset
if and only if
\mathbb{I}_{\Psi}(V\setminus Y)\supseteq X
. Hence
Implicate Set Extension
(\Psi,X,Y)
can be solved in
O(|V|^{2}{\cdot}\|\Psi\|)
time.

Proof.

The claim follows by the fact that
\mathbb{I}_{h}(V\setminus Y)
is the unique maximal implicate set of
h
that is disjoint from
Y
. The complexity of Problem
Implicate Set Extension
(\Psi,X,Y)
follows by Theorem
21
.
∎

Therefore we get the following theorem.

Theorem 31
.

For a given definite Horn CNF
\Psi
, we can generate implicate sets of
\Psi
in polynomial delay.

Proof.

The claim follows from Lemma
30
and the flashlight search, see e.g.,
[
4
]
.
∎

Note that
\mathcal{I}(\Psi)
is closed under union and
\mathbb{I}_{h}(V\setminus Y)
is the unique maximal implicate set of
h
that is disjoint from
Y
. Hence, by utilizing such properties, implicate sets can be generated faster than the standard flashlight search, although we do not discuss it in this paper.

5.5
Checking implicate-duality

Deciding the computational complexity of checking implicate-duality remains an intriguing open question. In this problem, we are given two definite Horn CNFs
\Psi
and
\Gamma
, and the goal is to decide if
\Psi=\Gamma^{i}
. The problem is open even when
\Psi=\Gamma
, that is, one would like to check if a definite Horn CNF
\Psi
represents a self
i
-dual Horn function or not. We call a definite Horn function
h

self
i
-dual
if
h^{i}=h
. We know by Lemma
16
that
h
must be hypergraph Horn in this case.

As initial steps toward understanding these problems, we show that
\Psi\geq\Gamma^{i}
can be checked in polynomial time and that the problem of deciding
\Psi=\Psi^{i}
belongs to co-NP.

Theorem 32
.

Let
\Psi
and
\Gamma
be two definite Horn CNFs. Then
\Psi\geq\Gamma^{i}
can be checked in
O(|V|^{2}{\cdot}|\Psi|{\cdot}\|\Gamma\|)
time.

Proof.

Note that
\Psi\not\geq\Gamma^{i}
holds if and only if there exists a false set
F
of
\Psi
such that
V\setminus F
is an implicate set of
\Gamma
. This is equivalent to the existence of a clause
A\to v
in
\Psi
such that the output of
Implicate Set Extension
(
\Gamma,\{v\},A
) is “Yes”. Therefore, by Lemma
30
,
\Psi\geq\Gamma^{i}
can be checked in
O(|V|^{2}{\cdot}|\Psi|{\cdot}\|\Gamma\|)
time.
∎

We show next that self implicate duality of
h
can be characterized as a property of its complete implicate set
\mathcal{I}(h)
. This characterization then allows us to show that the problem of deciding
h=h^{i}
for a function
h
represented by definite Horn CNF belongs to co-NP.

Theorem 33
.

A definite Horn function
h
is self
i
-dual if the hypergraph
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{I}(h)
satisfies the following properties.

(a)

|H\cap H^{\prime}|\neq 1
for all
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
.

(b)

\mathcal{H}
is a maximal subfamily of
2^{V}
with respect to property
(a)
.

Let us remark that since
H=H^{\prime}
is possible in
(a)
,
|H|\geq 2
follows for all
H\in\mathcal{I}(h)
for a self
i
-dual Horn function
h
.

Before proving this theorem, we need a technical lemma that maybe of interest on its own.

Lemma 34
.

Given a hypergraph
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
, we have
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\Phi^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}
if and only if
\mathcal{H}
satisfies condition
(a)
of Theorem
33
.

Proof.

Let us introduce
h=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}
. By definition
\mathcal{T}(h^{i})=\mathcal{I}(h)^{c}
. Thus we have
\mathcal{T}(h^{i})\subseteq\mathcal{T}(h)
if and only if
\mathcal{I}(h)^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(h)
. By Lemma
9
we have that a subset
S\subseteq V
is a true set of
h
if and only if there exists no implicate set
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)
such that
|I\setminus S|=1
. Thus, we have
\mathcal{I}(h)^{c}\subseteq\mathcal{T}(h)
if and only if for all
S\in\mathcal{I}(h)^{c}
and
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)
we have
|I\setminus S|\neq 1
, or equivalently, for all
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(h)
we have
|I\cap I^{\prime}|\neq 1
.

We claim that this latter condition holds if and only if
\mathcal{H}
satisfies property
(a)
of Theorem
33
. Since
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{I}(h)
, the necessity of condition
(a)
is implied. For the sufficiency, assume for a contradiction that there exist implicate sets
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(h)
such that
|I\cap I^{\prime}|=1
, i.e.,
|I\setminus(V\setminus I^{\prime})|=1
. This relation then implies that
\mathbb{T}_{h}(V\setminus I^{\prime})\neq(V\setminus I^{\prime})
. Since
\mathbb{T}_{h}=\mathbb{T}_{\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}}
, there exists a hyperedge
H\in\mathcal{H}
such that
|H\setminus(V\setminus I^{\prime})|=1
, or equivalently,
|H\cap I^{\prime}|=1
. We can write this equivalently, as
|I^{\prime}\setminus(V\setminus H)|=1
from which
\mathbb{T}_{h}(V\setminus H)\neq(V\setminus H)
follows, implying the existence of an
H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
|H^{\prime}\setminus(V\setminus H)|=1
. Thus these
H
and
H^{\prime}
would violate condition
(a)
, completing the sufficiency.
∎

Proof of Theorem
33
.

Since
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}\geq\Phi^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}
must hold for self-
i
-dual functions, property
(a)
of Theorem
33
is necessary by Lemma
34
. Assume first that
\mathcal{H}
satisfies that
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}>\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}
. Then we have a subset
S\subseteq V
such that
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(S)=0
and
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}(S)=1
. The equality
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(S)=0
implies
(V\setminus S)\notin\mathcal{H}
by the definition of
i
-duality. The equality
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}(S)=1
implies that for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
we have
|H\setminus S|\neq 1
, or equivalently that
|H\cap(V\setminus S)|\neq 1
.
This implies that
\mathcal{H}\cup\{V\setminus S\}
satisfies
(b)
, that is,
\mathcal{H}
is not maximal with respect to
(a)
.

For the converse direction, assume that
\mathcal{H}
satisfies
(a)
, but not maximal with respect to
(a)
, i.e., there exists a subset
S\subseteq V
such that
S\notin\mathcal{H}
and
|S\cap H|\neq 1
for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
.
The latter condition implies
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}(V\setminus S)=1
by Lemma
9
.
On the other hand, since
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{I}(h)
,
we have
\mathcal{T}(\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}^{i})=\mathcal{H}^{c}
.
Thus
S\notin\mathcal{H}
implies that
\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}^{i}(V\setminus S)=0
,
which concludes that

\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}>\Phi^{i}_{\mathcal{H}}
.
∎

The characterization given above is analogous to the characterization of monotone duality, see
[
3
]
for further details.

Corollary 35
.

The decision problem
h=h^{i}
for functions
h
represented by definite Horn CNFs belongs to co-NP.

Proof.

To prove
h\neq h^{i}
it is enough by Theorem
33
to present either two implicate sets
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(h)
such that
|I\cap I^{\prime}|=1
or a subset
S\subseteq V
such that
S\notin\mathcal{I}(h)
and
|S\cap I|\neq 1
for all
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)
.

In the first case we can test memberships
I,I^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}(h)
in polynomial time using any Horn CNF representation of
h
.

In the second case we can also test
S\notin\mathcal{I}(h)
in polynomial time using the Horn CNF representing
h
. Furthermore, we can test for all
v\in S
if
v\in I=\mathbb{I}_{h}((V\setminus S)+v)
in polynomial time by Theorem
21
. If the answer is “No” for all
v\in S
, then we must have
|S\cap I|\neq 1
for all
I\in\mathcal{I}(h)
.
∎

6
Conclusions and open problems

In the present paper, we introduced the notion of hypergraph Horn functions, a subclass of Boolean functions with rich structural properties that generalizes matroids and equivalence relations. We gave several equivalent characterizations of hypergraph Horn functions, introduced a new duality that generalizes matroid duality, and studied algorithmic problems including the recognition problem, key realization, and implicate set generation.

We conclude our paper with listing a few open problems. Assume that
\Psi
and
\Gamma
are definite Horn CNFs, and all questions are about polynomial time computability.

Question 1
.

Does
\Psi\leq\Gamma^{i}
hold?

Question 2
.

Does
\Psi=\Psi^{i}
hold?

Question 3
.

Is there a Sperner hypergraph
\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{V}
such that
\Psi=\Phi_{\mathcal{H}}
?
