Improved Algorithm for Reachability in d d -VASS
Yuxi Fu, Qizhe Yang, Yangluo Zheng

Abstract
An 𝖥 d \mathsf{F}_{d} upper bound for the reachability problem in vector addition systems with states (VASS) in fixed dimension is given, where 𝖥 d \mathsf{F}_{d} is the d d -th level of the Grzegorczyk hierarchy of complexity classes. The new algorithm combines the idea of the linear path scheme characterization of the reachability in the 2 2 -dimension VASSes with the general decomposition algorithm by Mayr, Kosaraju and Lambert. The result improves the 𝖥 d + 4 \mathsf{F}_{d+4} upper bound due to Leroux and Schmitz (LICS 2019).

Introduction

Petri nets, or equivalently vector addition system with states (VASS), are a well studied model of concurrency. A VASS consists of a finite state control where each state transition has as its effect an integer-valued vector, and its configurations are pairs of a state and a vector with
natural number
components. A transition may lead from one configuration to another by adding its effect component-wise, conditioned on that the components of the resulting vector remain non-negative. The
reachability problem
, which asks whether from one configuration there is a path reaching another configuration, lies in the center of the algorithmic theory of Petri nets and has found a wide range of applications due to its generic nature. Since the problem was shown to be decidable by Mayr
[
17
]
in 1981, its computational complexity had been a long-standing open problem in the field. In 2015, Leroux and Schmitz
[
14
]
presented the first complexity upper bound, stating that the reachability problem is cubic-Ackermannian. This was later improved to an Ackermannian upper bound in 2019, again by Leroux and Schmitz
[
15
]
. Regarding the hardness, in 2021 seminal works by Czerwiński and Orlikowski
[
6
]
, and independently by Leroux
[
13
]
, provided matching Ackermannian lower bounds, settling the exact complexity of the problem.

Concerning the parameterization by dimension, i.e. the reachability problem in
d
-dimensional VASSes where
d
is fixed, there is still a gap in the known complexity bounds. Currently, we only have exact complexity bounds for dimension one and two
[
7
,
1
]
. For dimension
d\geq 3
, the result of Leroux and Schmitz
[
15
]
shows that the problem is in
\mathsf{F}_{d+4}
, the
(d{+}4)
-th level of the Grzegorczyk hierarchy of complexity classes. Note that a recent work by Yang and Fu
[
22
]
points out that the problem for dimension
3
is in
\mathsf{F}_{3}=\mathsf{TOWER}
. On the other hand, the best known lower bound by Czerwiński, Jecker, Lasota, Leroux and Orlikowski
[
5
]
states that reachability in
(2d{+}3)
-dimensional VASSes is
\mathsf{F}_{d}
-hard. Motivated by this gap, our paper focuses on the computational complexity of reachability problem in the fixed-dimensional VASSes.

Our contribution.

In this paper we show that the reachability problem in the
d
-dimensional VASS is in
\mathsf{F}_{d}
for
d\geq 3
, improving the previous
\mathsf{F}_{d+4}
upper bound by Leroux and Schmitz
[
15
]
, and generalizing the tower upper bound for the reachability problem in
3
-VASS
[
22
]
.
The new upper bound is obtained with the help of two novel technical lemmas.

1.

Our main technical tool (Theorem
5
) is a generalization of the linear path scheme characterization for the reachability relation in the
2
-dimensional VASSes
[
1
]
.
By borrowing the key idea from the work of Yang and Fu
[
22
]
, we show that as long as the “geometric dimension” of a VASS (that is, the dimension of the vector space spanned by the effects of cyclic paths) is bounded by
2
, its reachability relation can be characterized by short linear path schemes.
We then apply the lemma to simplify the KLMST algorithm so that (i) a VASS is replaced by a short linear path scheme whenever its geometric dimension is no more than
2
and (ii) the linear path schemes will not be decomposed further.
It is then routine
[
15
]
, using the tools from
[
21
]
, to show that the reachability problem in the
d
-dimensional VASS is in
\mathsf{F}_{d+1}
for all
d\geq 3
.

2.

Our second lemma (Lemma
28
) allows us to improve further the bound from
\mathsf{F}_{d+1}
to
\mathsf{F}_{d}
.
This is done by a careful analysis of the properties of the fast-growing functions
[
21
]
.

Due to space limitation the proofs of the two lemmas are placed in the appendices.

Organization.

Section
2
fixes notation, defines the VASS model and its reachability problem.
Section
3
generalizes the linear path scheme characterization
[
1
]
to VASSes whose geometric dimension are bounded by
2
. Section
4
recalls the characterization system of linear inequalities for linear path schemes.
Section
5
makes use of the results of Section
3
to give an improved version of the classic KLMST decomposition algorithm.
Section
6
analyzes the complexity of our modified algorithm, proving the main result. Section
7
concludes. Proofs omitted from the main text can be found in the appendices.

Preliminaries

We use
\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q}
to denote respectively the set of non-negative integers, integers, and rational numbers.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a number, we write
[n]
for the range
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
Let
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
be
d
-dimensional vectors where
\mathbb{X}
can be any one of
\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{Q}
.
We write
\bm{v}(i)
for the
i
-th component of
\bm{v}
where
i\in[d]
, so
\bm{v}=(\bm{v}(1),\ldots,\bm{v}(d))
.
The
maximum norm
of
\bm{v}
is defined to be
\left\|{\bm{v}}\right\|:=\max_{i\in[d]}|\bm{v}(i)|
.
We extend component-wise the order
\leq
for vectors in
\mathbb{X}^{d}
, so
\bm{u}\leq\bm{v}
if and only if
\bm{u}(i)\leq\bm{v}(i)
for all
i\in[d]
.
Addition and subtraction of vectors are also component-wise, so
(\bm{u}+\bm{v})(i)=\bm{u}(i)+\bm{v}(i)
for all
i\in[d]
.
Define
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v}):=\{i\in[d]:\bm{v}(i)\neq 0\}
to be the set of indices of non-zero components of
\bm{v}
. This notation is extended to sets of vectors naturally, so
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(S)=\bigcup_{\bm{v}\in S}\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})
for any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{X}^{d}
.

For technical reasons we introduce the symbol
\omega
that stands for the infinite element. Let
\mathbb{N}_{\omega}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{\omega\}
.
We stipulate that
n<\omega
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and
x+\omega=\omega+x=\omega
for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Define the partial order
\sqsubseteq
over
\mathbb{N}_{\omega}
so that
x\sqsubseteq y
if and only if
x=y
or
y=\omega
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}
.
The relation
\sqsubseteq
is also extended component-wise to vectors in
\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}
.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet and
s,t\in\Sigma^{*}
be two strings over
\Sigma
. We write
st
for the concatenation of
s
and
t
, and
s^{n}
for the concatenation of
n
copies of
s
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
s=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{\ell}
where
a_{1},\ldots,a_{\ell}\in\Sigma
, we write
|s|:=\ell
for the length of
s
, and
s[i\ldots j]:=a_{i}a_{i+1}\ldots a_{j}
for the substring of
s
between indices
i
and
j
where
1\leq i\leq j\leq|s|
.

2.1
Vector Addition Systems with States

Let
d\geq 0
be an integer. A
d
-dimensional vector addition system with states (
d
-VASS)
is a pair
G=(Q,T)
where
Q
is a finite set of
states
and
T\subseteq Q\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\times Q
is a finite set of
transitions
.
Clearly a VASS can also be viewed as a directed graph with edges labelled by integer vectors.
Given a word
\pi=(p_{1},\bm{a}_{1},q_{1})(p_{2},\bm{a}_{2},q_{2})\ldots(p_{n},\bm{a}_{n},q_{n})\in T^{*}
over transitions, we say that
\pi
is a
path from
p_{1}
to
q_{n}
if
q_{i}=p_{i+1}
for all
i=1,\ldots,n-1
. It is a
cycle
if we further have
p_{1}=q_{n}
.
The
effect
of
\pi
is defined to be
\Delta(\pi):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{a}_{i}
, and the
action word
of
\pi
is the word
\llbracket{\pi}\rrbracket:=\bm{a}_{1}\bm{a}_{2}\ldots\bm{a}_{n}
over
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
The
Parikh image
of
\pi
is the function
\phi\in\mathbb{N}^{T}
mapping each transition to its number of occurrences in
\pi
. Given a function
\phi\in\mathbb{N}^{T}
we also define
\Delta(\phi):=\sum_{t=(p,\bm{a},q)\in T}\phi(t)\cdot\bm{a}
. Note that
\Delta(\phi)=\Delta(\pi)
if
\phi
is the Parikh image of
\pi
.
Let
L\subseteq T^{*}
be a language (i.e. subset of words), we define its effect as
\Delta(L):=\{\Delta(\pi):\pi\in L\}
.

The norm of a transition
t=(p,\bm{a},q)
is defined by
\left\|{t}\right\|:=\left\|{\bm{a}}\right\|
. The norm of a path
\pi=t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n}
is
\left\|{\pi}\right\|:=\max_{i\in[n]}\left\|{t_{i}}\right\|
.
For a
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)
we write
\left\|{T}\right\|:=\max\{\left\|{t}\right\|:t\in T\}
. The
size
of
G
is defined by

|G|:=|Q|+|T|+d\cdot|T|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|.

(1)

Semantics of VASSes.

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS.
A
configuration
of
G
is a pair of a state
p\in Q
and a vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, written as
p(\bm{v})
. Let
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, we define the
\mathbb{D}
-semantics for
G
as follows.
For each transition
t=(p,\bm{a},q)\in T
, the one-step transition relation
\xrightarrow{t}_{\mathbb{D}}
relates all pairs of configurations of the form
(p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v}))
where
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{D}
and
\bm{v}=\bm{u}+\bm{a}
.
Then for a word
\pi=t_{1}t_{2}\ldots t_{n}\in T^{*}
, the relation
\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{D}}
is the composition
\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{D}}{}:={}\xrightarrow{t_{1}}_{\mathbb{D}}\circ\cdots\circ\xrightarrow{t_{n}}_{\mathbb{D}}
.
So
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{D}}q(\bm{v})
if and only if there are configurations
p_{0}(\bm{u}_{0}),\ldots,p_{n}(\bm{u}_{n})\in Q\times\mathbb{D}
such that

p(\bm{u})=p_{0}(\bm{u}_{0})\xrightarrow{t_{1}}_{\mathbb{D}}p_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})\xrightarrow{t_{2}}_{\mathbb{D}}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{n}}_{\mathbb{D}}p_{n}(\bm{u}_{n})=q(\bm{v}).

(2)

Also, when
\pi=\epsilon
is the empty word, the relation
\xrightarrow{\epsilon}_{\mathbb{D}}
is the identity relation over
Q\times\mathbb{D}
.
Note that
\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{D}}
is non-empty only if
\pi
is a path.
When
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{D}}q(\bm{v})
we also say that
\pi
induces (or is) a
\mathbb{D}
-run
from
p(\bm{u})
to
q(\bm{v})
. We emphasize that all configurations on this run lie in
\mathbb{D}
, and that they are uniquely determined by
p(\bm{u})
and
\pi
.
For a language
L\subseteq T^{*}
we define
\xrightarrow{L}_{\mathbb{D}}
as
\bigcup_{\pi\in L}\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{D}}
.
Finally, the
\mathbb{D}
-reachability relation
of
G
is defined to be
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{D}}{}:={}\xrightarrow{T^{*}}_{\mathbb{D}}
.

For the above definitions, we shall often omit the subscript
\mathbb{D}
when
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

We mention that in Section
5
we need to generalize the VASS semantics to configurations in
Q\times\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}
, allowing
\omega
components in vectors. The definitions of
\xrightarrow{t}_{\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}}
,
\xrightarrow{\pi}_{\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}}
, and
\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}}
are similar to the above.

Reachability problem.

The reachability problem in vector addition systems with states is formulated as follows:

Reachability in
d
-Dimensional Vector Addition System with States

Input:

A
d
-dimensional VASS
G=(Q,T)
, two configurations
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

Question:

Does
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{N}^{d}}q(\bm{v})
hold in
G
?

Note that we study the reachability problem for fixed-dimensional VASSes, where the dimension
d
is treated as a constant to allow more fine-grained analysis. So we shall use the big-
O
notation to hide constants that may depend on
d
. The general problem where the dimension can be part of the input was already shown to be Ackermann-complete
[
6
,
13
]
.

Cycle spaces and geometric dimensions.

One of the key insights of
[
15
]
is a new termination argument for the KLMST decomposition algorithm based on the dimensions of vector spaces spanned by cycles in VASSes, which yielded the primitive recursive upper bound of VASS reachability problem in fixed dimensions.
The vector spaces spanned by cycles also play an important role in our work.

Definition 1
.

Let
G
be a
d
-VASS. The
cycle space
of
G
is the vector space
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
spanned by the effects of all cycles in
G
, that is:

\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G):=\operatorname{span}\nolimits\{\Delta(\beta):\beta\text{ is a cycle in }G\}.

(3)

The dimension of the cycle space of
G
is called the
geometric dimension
of
G
. We say
G
is
geometrically
k
-dimensional
if
\dim(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G))\leq k
.

3
Flattability of Geometrically 2-dimensional VASSes

A VASS is
flat
if each of its states lies on at most one cycle. Flat VASSes form an important subclass of VASSes due to its connection to Presburger arithmetic, and we refer the readers to
[
12
]
for a survey.
In dimension
2
, it was proved in
[
1
]
that
2
-VASSes enjoy a stronger form of flat representation, known as the
linear path schemes
.
A linear path scheme is a regular expression of the form
\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{*}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{*}\alpha_{k}
where
\alpha_{0},\ldots\alpha_{k}
are paths and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
are cycles of the VASS, such that they form a path when joined together. The results of
[
1
]
show that the reachability relation of every
2
-VASS can be captured by short linear path schemes.

Linear path schemes are extremely useful as they can be fully characterized by linear inequality systems so that standard tools for linear or integer programming can be applied.
In this section, we generalize the results in
[
1
]
and show that the reachability relation of any
d
-VASS whose geometric dimension is bounded by
2
can also be captured by short linear path schemes.

Our proof follows closely to the lines of
[
1
]
. Given a geometrically
2
-dimensional VASS
G
, we first cover
\mathbb{N}^{d}
by the following two regions: one for the region far away from every axis:

\mathbb{O}:=\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\bm{u}(i)\geq D\text{ for all }i\in[d]\}

(4)

where
D
is some properly chosen threshold; the other one for the region close to some axis:

\mathbb{L}:=\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\bm{u}(i)\leq D^{\prime}\text{ for some }i\in[d]\}

(5)

where
D^{\prime}
is chosen slightly above
D
to create an overlap with
\mathbb{O}
.
Let
\pi
be a run that we are going to capture by linear path schemes. We can extract its maximal prefix that lies completely in either
\mathbb{O}
or
\mathbb{L}
, depending on where
\pi
starts. This prefix must end at a configuration that lies in
\mathbb{L}\cap\mathbb{O}
, if we haven’t touched the end of
\pi
. From this configuration we then extract a maximal cycle that also ends in
\mathbb{L}\cap\mathbb{O}
. Continuing this fashion, we can break
\pi
into segments of runs that lie completely in
\mathbb{O}
or
\mathbb{L}
, interleaved by cycles that start and end in
\mathbb{O}
(actually in
\mathbb{L}\cap\mathbb{O}
). Note that the number of such cycles cannot exceed the number of states of
G
since they are maximal.
Now we only need to capture the following three types of runs by short linear path schemes:

1.

Runs that are cycles starting and ending in
\mathbb{O}
.

This will be handled in Section
A.4
in the appendix. Briefly speaking, since the geometric dimension of
G
is
2
, the effect of such a cycle must belong to a plane in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. We will find a clever way to project this plane to a coordinate plane, and then project the
d
-VASS
G
onto this plane to get a
2
-VASS.
This is made possible by a novel technique called the “sign-reflecting projection” developed in Section
A.3
. Intuitively speaking, for any vector in a plane we are able to determine whether it belongs to a certain orthant by observing only
2
entries of this vector. The
d
-VASS can then be projected onto these
2
coordinates. (See Example
2
for a more concrete demonstration.)
Now we apply the results of
[
1
]
to obtain a linear path scheme that captures the projected cyclic run. The projection guarantees that we can safely project it back to get a linear path scheme for the run in
G
.

2.

Runs that lie completely in
\mathbb{O}
.

This is just an easy corollary of the first type, and will also be handled in Section
A.4
. Just note that any run can be broken into a series of simple paths interleaved by cycles.

3.

Runs that lie completely in
\mathbb{L}
.

This will be handled in Section
A.5
, by a long and tedious case analysis. In principle, we are going to argue that any such run essentially corresponds to a run in some
(d-1)
-VASS, so that we can use induction.

.
It consists of a single state

(1,0,-1)
and

.
In order to apply the results of
[

, one would like to derive a

that reflects runs in

. A simple idea is to discard one coordinate of

. Two possibilities of this idea are shown in Figure

, which discards the third coordinate, and

, which discards the second coordinate. However, not all of them are satisfactory. For example, the legal run

3.1
Linear Path Schemes

A
linear path scheme (LPS)
is a pair
(G,\Lambda)
where
G
is a VASS and
\Lambda
is a regular expression of the form

\Lambda=\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{*}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{*}\alpha_{k}

such that
\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
are paths in
G
and
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}
are cycles in
G
, and
\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}\alpha_{k}
is a path in
G
.
We say an LPS
(G,\Lambda)
is
compatible to
a VASS
G^{\prime}
if
G^{\prime}
contains all states and transitions in
\Lambda
.
Very often we shall omit the VASS
G
and say that
\Lambda
on its own is an LPS, with
G
understood as any VASS to which
\Lambda
is compatible.
We write
|\Lambda|=|\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}\alpha_{k}|
for the length of
\Lambda
,
\left\|{\Lambda}\right\|=\left\|{\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}\alpha_{k}}\right\|
for its norm, and
|\Lambda|_{*}=k
for the number of cycles in
\Lambda
.

We also treat
\Lambda
as the language defined by it, and thus for two configurations
p(\bm{u})
and
q(\bm{v})
we write
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{D}}q(\bm{v})
if and only if there exists
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{e_{1}}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{e_{k}}\alpha_{k}}_{\mathbb{D}}q(\bm{v}).

(6)

Positive linear path schemes.

A
positive LPS
is a regular expression of the form

\Lambda^{+}=\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{+}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{+}\alpha_{k}

which is similar to a linear path scheme except that we require each cycle to be used at least once. We write
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda^{+}}_{\mathbb{D}}q(\bm{v})
if and only if there are positive integers
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that

p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{e_{1}}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{e_{k}}\alpha_{k}}_{\mathbb{D}}q(\bm{v}).

(7)

A path
\pi
is said to be
admitted by
\Lambda^{+}
if
\pi=\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{e_{1}}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{e_{k}}\alpha_{k}
for some
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.
We prefer positive LPSes as they can be easily characterized by linear inequality systems (see Section
4
for details). In fact, positive LPSes can be easily obtained from LPSes:

Lemma 3
.

For every linear path scheme
\Lambda
there exists a finite set
S
of positive linear path schemes compatible to the same VASSes with
\Lambda
, such that
\xrightarrow{\Lambda}{}={}\bigcup_{\Lambda^{+}\in S}\xrightarrow{\Lambda^{+}}
and
|\Lambda^{+}|\leq|\Lambda|
for every
\Lambda^{+}\in S
.

Proof.

Suppose
\Lambda=\alpha_{0}\beta_{1}^{*}\alpha_{1}\ldots\beta_{k}^{*}\alpha_{k}
. For each cycle component
\beta_{i}^{*}

5
The Modified KLMST Decomposition Algorithm

In this section we apply our results of Section
3
to improve the notoriously hard KLMST decomposition algorithm for VASS reachability. Our narration will base on the work of Leroux and Schmitz
[
15
]
. For readers familiar with
[
15
]
, the major modifications are listed below:

•

The decomposition structure is now a sequence of generalized VASS reachability instances linked by (positive) linear path schemes rather than by single transitions.

•

We introduce a new “cleaning” step that replaces all VASS instances which are geometrically
2
-dimensional by polynomial-length linear path schemes compatible to them.

•

We do not guarantee the exact preservation of action languages at each decomposition step. Instead, we only preserve a subset of action languages. This is a compromise since linear path schemes capture only the reachability relation but not every possible run. Nonetheless, it is enough for the reachability problem.

In this section we focus on the effectiveness and correctness of the modified KLMST decomposition algorithm. Its complexity will be analyzed in Section
6
.

5.1
Linear KLM Sequences

The underlying decomposition structure in the KLMST algorithm was known as KLM sequences, named after Mayr
[
17
]
, Kosaraju
[
10
]
, and Lambert
[
11
]
.

Definition 9
.

A
KLM tuple
of dimension
d
is a tuple
\left\langle{p(\bm{x})Gq(\bm{y})}\right\rangle
where
G=(Q,T)
is a
d
-VASS and
p(\bm{x}),q(\bm{y})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}
are two (generalized) configurations of

.
A
KLM sequence
of dimension
d
is a sequence of KLM tuples interleaved by transitions of the form

\xi=\left\langle{p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})G_{0}q_{0}(\bm{y}_{0})}\right\rangle t_{1}\left\langle{p_{1}(\bm{x}_{1})G_{1}q_{1}(\bm{y}_{1})}\right\rangle t_{2}\ldots t_{k}\left\langle{p_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})G_{k}q_{k}(\bm{y}_{k})}\right\rangle,

(11)

where each tuple
\left\langle{p_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})G_{i}q_{i}(\bm{y}_{i})}\right\rangle
is a KLM tuple of dimension
d
and each

is a transition of the form
(q_{i-1},\bm{a}_{i},p_{i})
from state
q_{i-1}
to
p_{i}
with effect
\bm{a}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

In this paper we generalize the definition of KLM sequences to allow (positive) linear path schemes to connect KLM tuples.

Definition 10
.

A
linear KLM sequence
of dimension
d
is a sequence

\xi=\left\langle{p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})G_{0}q_{0}(\bm{y}_{0})}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}\left\langle{p_{1}(\bm{x}_{1})G_{1}q_{1}(\bm{y}_{1})}\right\rangle\Lambda_{2}\ldots\Lambda_{k}\left\langle{p_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})G_{k}q_{k}(\bm{y}_{k})}\right\rangle,

(12)

where each tuple
\left\langle{p_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})G_{i}q_{i}(\bm{y}_{i})}\right\rangle
is a KLM tuple of dimension
d
and each
\Lambda_{i}
is a positive linear path scheme from state
q_{i-1}
to
p_{i}
.

One immediately sees that KLM sequences are just special cases of linear KLM sequences. Let
\xi
be a linear KLM sequence given as (
12
), we write
\xi_{i}:=\left\langle{p_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})G_{i}q_{i}(\bm{y}_{i})}\right\rangle
for the

Action languages.

Let
\xi
be a linear KLM sequence given as (

is

for each

We are more interested in the action languages because in some decomposition steps we have to modify the set of transitions, and only the action word of admitted runs can be preserved. Notice that action languages preserve not only the effects of admitted runs, but also their lengths.

We define in this section the characteristic systems of linear KLM sequences, which are systems of linear inequalities that serve as an under-specification of admitted runs. Let

We also need the homogeneous version of

\bm{0}=\sum_{t=(r,\bm{a},s)\in T}\phi(t)\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{s}-\mathbf{1}_{r}).

.

Let

5.3
Cleaning of Linear KLM Sequences

In this section we define three conditions that require a linear KLM sequence to be

. Together with the satisfiability condition, they make up the so-called “clean” condition of linear KLM sequences. Note that the purity condition is new compared to

-dimensional VASSes occur in a linear KLM sequence to be replaced by linear path schemes.

Strongly Connected Sequences

A linear KLM sequence

is

if all the VASSes

are strongly connected (as they are understood as directed graphs). One can easily obtain strongly connected sequences by expanding the strongly connected components of each VASS:

Lemma 16

(
[
L
]
em. 4.2)
.

LS19]

For any linear KLM sequence
\xi
which is not strongly connected, one can compute in time
{\textsf{exp}({|\xi|})}
a finite set
\Xi
of strongly connected linear KLM sequences such that

and that
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})\leq_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
and
|\xi^{\prime}|\leq(2d+1)|\xi|
for every
\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi
.

Pure Sequences

A KLM tuple
\left\langle{p(\bm{x})Gq(\bm{y})}\right\rangle
is called

and

contains no transition and only a single state

for this tuple. Note that the action language of a trivial tuple contains exactly the empty word.

A linear KLM sequence
\xi=\left\langle{p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})G_{0}q_{0}(\bm{y}_{0})}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}\cdots\Lambda_{k}\left\langle{p_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})G_{k}q_{k}(\bm{y}_{k})}\right\rangle

implies that the tuple

is trivial. By Corollary

\bm{0}
strongly connected VASS is geometrically

Lemma 17

Let
\xi
be a strongly connected linear KLM sequence. Whether

.

(

\xi
, one can compute in time
{\textsf{exp}({|\xi|^{O(|\xi|)}})}
a finite set
\Xi
of saturated pure linear KLM sequences such that
L_{\xi}=\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi}L_{\xi^{\prime}}
, and such that
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})=\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
and
|\xi^{\prime}|\leq|\xi|^{O(|\xi|)}
for every
\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi
.

Proof.

By Lemma
15
, if a variable
\bm{m}_{i}(j)
or
\bm{n}_{i}(j)
is bounded in
\mathcal{E}({\xi})
, we can replace the corresponding
\omega
component in

by all possible values bounded by
(10|\xi|)^{12|\xi|}\leq|\xi|^{O(|\xi|)}
.
∎

The Cleaning Lemma

A linear KLM sequence
\xi
is called
clean
if it is satisfiable, strongly connected, pure and saturated. The lemmas
16
through
18
show how to make a linear KLM sequence clean.

Lemma 19
.

From any linear KLM sequence
\xi
, one can compute in time
{\textsf{exp}({g(|\xi|)})}
a finite set
{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}
of clean linear KLM sequences such that
\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}}L_{\xi^{\prime}}\subseteq L_{\xi}
and
\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}}L_{\xi^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
whenever
L_{\xi}\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, for every
\xi^{\prime}\in{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}
we have
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})\leq_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
and
|\xi^{\prime}|\leq g(|\xi|)
where
g(x)=x^{x^{O(1)}}
.

5.4
Decomposition of Linear KLM Sequences

In this section we recall three conditions that require a linear KLM sequence to be
unbounded
,
rigid
, and
pumpable
. If any one of them is violated, a decomposition into a set of linear KLM sequences with strictly smaller ranks can be performed. Essentially there is nothing new in this section compared to
[
15
]
. The decomposition operations in
[
15
]
can be directly applied here, since they operate on a single KLM tuple and produce KLM sequences that are just special cases of linear KLM sequences. The proofs in
[
15
]
can also be adapted easily, and we will omit the details here. Especially, the next lemma shows that the arguments of strict decrease in ranks are still valid even though we discard the lower three components of ranks.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\xi^{\prime}
be a pure linear KLM sequence. For any linear KLM sequence
\xi^{\prime}
with
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits_{\text{full}}(\xi^{\prime})<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits_{\text{full}}(\xi)
, we have
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
.

Unbounded Sequences

Let
\xi=\left\langle{p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})G_{0}q_{0}(\bm{y}_{0})}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}\cdots\Lambda_{k}\left\langle{p_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})G_{k}q_{k}(\bm{y}_{k})}\right\rangle
be a linear KLM sequence.
We say
\xi
is
unbounded
if for all
i=0,\ldots,k
and every transition
t\in T_{i}
where
T_{i}
is the set of transitions of
G_{i}
, the set of values
\phi_{i}^{\bm{h}}(t)
where
\bm{h}\models\mathcal{E}({\xi})
is unbounded. Bounded transitions can be expanded exhaustively according to the bounds given by Lemma
15
.

Lemma 21

(
[
15
, Lem. 4.6]
)
.

Whether a linear KLM sequence
\xi
is unbounded is decidable in
\mathsf{NP}
. Moreover, if
\xi
is pure and bounded, one can compute in time
{\textsf{exp}({|\xi|^{O(|\xi|)}})}
a finite set
\Xi
of linear KLM sequences such that
L_{\xi}=\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi}L_{\xi^{\prime}}
and such that
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
and
|\xi^{\prime}|<|\xi|^{O(|\xi|)}
for every
\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi
.

Rigid Sequences

A coordinate
j\in[d]
is said to be
fixed
by a VASS
G=(Q,T)
if there exists a function
f_{j}:Q\to\mathbb{N}
such that
f_{j}(q)=f_{j}(p)+\bm{a}(j)
for every transition
(p,\bm{a},q)\in T
. We also say that
f_{j}

fixes

G
at coordinate
j
in this case.

A KLM tuple
\left\langle{p(\bm{x})Gq(\bm{y})}\right\rangle
is said to be
rigid
if for every coordinate
j
fixed by
G=(Q,T)
, there exists a function
g_{j}:Q\to\mathbb{N}
that fixes
G
at coordinate
j
and such that
g_{j}(p)\sqsubseteq\bm{x}(j)
and
g_{j}(q)\sqsubseteq\bm{y}(j)
.

A linear KLM sequence
\xi=\left\langle{p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})G_{0}q_{0}(\bm{y}_{0})}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}\cdots\Lambda_{k}\left\langle{p_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})G_{k}q_{k}(\bm{y}_{k})}\right\rangle

is said to be
rigid
if every tuple
\left\langle{p_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})G_{i}q_{i}(\bm{y}_{i})}\right\rangle
in
\xi
is rigid.

Lemma 22

(
[
15
, Lem. 4.9]
)
.

From any pure linear KLM sequence
\xi
one can decide in polynomial time whether
\xi
is not rigid. Moreover, in that case one can compute in polynomial time a linear KLM sequence
\xi^{\prime}
such that
L_{\xi}=L_{\xi^{\prime}}
,
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
, and
|\xi^{\prime}|\leq|\xi|
.

Pumpable Sequences

Given a KLM tuple
\left\langle{p(\bm{x})Gq(\bm{y})}\right\rangle
, recall the
forward and backward acceleration vectors

\textsc{Facc}_{G,p}(\bm{x}),\textsc{Bacc}_{G,q}(\bm{y})\in\mathbb{N}_{\omega}^{d}
defined by

\displaystyle\textsc{Facc}_{G,p}(\bm{x})(j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\omega&\text{if }p(\bm{x})\xrightarrow{*}p(\bm{x}^{\prime})\text{ for some }\bm{x}^{\prime}\text{ with }\bm{x}^{\prime}\geq\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}(j)>\bm{x}(j)\\
\bm{x}(j)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(19)

\displaystyle\textsc{Bacc}_{G,q}(\bm{y})(j)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\omega&\text{if }q(\bm{y}^{\prime})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{y})\text{ for some }\bm{y}^{\prime}\text{ with }\bm{y}^{\prime}\geq\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}(j)>\bm{y}(j)\\
\bm{y}(j)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(20)

A tuple
\left\langle{p(\bm{x})Gq(\bm{y})}\right\rangle
is said to be
pumpable
if
\textsc{Facc}_{G,p}(\bm{x})(j)=\textsc{Bacc}_{G,q}(\bm{y})(j)=\omega
for every coordinate

not
fixed by
G
.

A linear KLM sequence given by
\xi=\left\langle{p_{0}(\bm{x}_{0})G_{0}q_{0}(\bm{y}_{0})}\right\rangle\Lambda_{1}\cdots\Lambda_{k}\left\langle{p_{k}(\bm{x}_{k})G_{k}q_{k}(\bm{y}_{k})}\right\rangle

is said to be
pumpable
if every tuple
\left\langle{p_{i}(\bm{x}_{i})G_{i}q_{i}(\bm{y}_{i})}\right\rangle
in
\xi
is pumpable.

Lemma 23

(
[
15
, Lem. 4.15]
)
.

Whether a linear KLM sequence
\xi
is pumpable is decidable in
\mathsf{EXPSPACE}
. Moreover, if
\xi
is pure and unpumpable, one can compute in time
{\textsf{exp}({|\xi|^{O(1)}})}
a finite set
\Xi
of linear KLM sequences such that
L_{\xi}=\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi}L_{\xi^{\prime}}
and such that
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
and
|\xi^{\prime}|<|\xi|^{O(1)}
for every
\xi^{\prime}\in\Xi
.

Note that the
O(1)
term here hides a constant depending on

, which essentially arises from a result on the coverability problem by Rackoff
[
19
]
. The
O(1)
term also captures the difference between the sizes of linear KLM sequences defined here and that in
[
15
]
.

The Decomposition Lemma

A linear KLM sequence is
normal
if it is clean, unbounded, rigid, and pumpable. The lemmas
21
through
23
show that when a clean linear KLM sequence is not normal, we are able to decompose it into a finite set of linear KLM sequences of strictly smaller ranks.

Lemma 24
.

From any clean linear KLM sequences
\xi
that is not normal, one can compute in time
{\textsf{exp}({h(\xi)})}
a finite set
{\textrm{dec}({\xi})}
of clean linear KLM sequence such that
\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in{\textrm{dec}({\xi})}}L_{\xi^{\prime}}\subseteq L_{\xi}
and
\bigcup_{\xi^{\prime}\in{\textrm{dec}({\xi})}}L_{\xi^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
whenever
L_{\xi}\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, for every
\xi^{\prime}\in{\textrm{dec}({\xi})}
we have
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)
and
|\xi^{\prime}|\leq h(\xi)
where
h(x)=x^{x^{x^{O(1)}}}
.

5.5
Normal Sequences

The following lemma shows that a normal linear KLM sequence is guaranteed to have non-empty action language, thus one can terminate the decomposition process once a normal sequence is produced.

Lemma 25
.

Let
\xi
be a normal linear KLM sequence, then there is a word
\sigma\in L_{\xi}
whose length is bounded by
|\sigma|\leq\ell(|\xi|)
where
\ell(x)\leq x^{O(x)}
.

5.6
Putting All Together: The Modified KLMST Algorithm

Here we describe the modified KLMST decomposition algorithm for VASS reachability problem. Suppose we are given a
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)
and two configurations
p(\bm{m}),q(\bm{n})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
. To decide whether
p(\bm{m})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{n})
holds in
G
, it is enough to decide whether
L_{\xi}
is non-empty where
\xi=\left\langle{p(\bm{m})Gq(\bm{n})}\right\rangle
. To start with, we use Lemma
19
to clean the sequence
\xi
, and then choose
\xi^{0}\in{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}
non-deterministically. If
\xi^{0}
is normal then we are done by Lemma
25
. Otherwise, we decompose
\xi^{0}
using Lemma
24
and choose
\xi^{1}\in{\textrm{dec}({\xi^{0}})}
non-deterministically. The procedure continues to produce a series of linear KLM sequences
\xi^{0},\xi^{1},\xi^{2},\ldots
where
\xi^{i+1}\in{\textrm{dec}({\xi^{i}})}
, until either we finally obtain a normal sequence
\xi^{n}
, or at some point we have to abort because the decomposition of a linear KLM sequence is the empty set. The procedure terminates because
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{0})>_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{1})>_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{2})>_{\text{lex}}\cdots
form a decreasing chain of the well-order
(\mathbb{N}^{d-2},<_{\text{lex}})
, which must be finite. If
L_{\xi}=\emptyset
then we cannot get a normal sequence since the action languages
L_{\xi}\supseteq L_{\xi^{0}}\supseteq L_{\xi^{1}}\supseteq\cdots
are all empty. On the other hand, if
L_{\xi}\neq\emptyset
then there are non-deterministic choices that always choose the linear KLM sequences with non-empty action languages, which finally lead to a normal sequence. This shows the correctness of the algorithm.

6
Complexity Upper Bound

The termination of the modified KLMST decomposition algorithm is guaranteed by a ranking function that decreases along a well-ordering. In order to analyze the length of this decreasing chain, we recall the so-called “length function theorems” by Schmitz
[
20
]
in Section
6.1
. After that, we can locate the complexity upper bound of the algorithm in the fast-growing complexity hierarchy
[
21
]
which we recall in Section
6.2
. Readers familiar with
[
15
]
may realize that the complexity upper bound for
d
-VASS can be improved to
\mathsf{F}_{d+1}
, i.e. the
(d{+}1)
-th level in the fast-growing hierarchy, with our ranking function. In fact, by a careful analysis on a property of fast-growing functions, we further improve this bound to
\mathsf{F}_{d}
.

In this section we assume some familiarity with ordinal numbers (see, e.g.
[
9
]
). We write
\omega
here for the first infinite ordinal, not to be confused with the infinite element in previous sections.

6.1
Length of Sequences of Decreasing Ranks

Let
\xi
be a linear KLM sequence of dimension
d
with
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)=(r_{d},\ldots,r_{3})
, we define the
ordinal rank

\alpha_{\xi}
of
\xi
as the ordinal number given by

\alpha_{\xi}:=\omega^{d-3}\cdot r_{d}+\omega^{d-4}\cdot r_{d-1}+\cdots+\omega^{0}\cdot r_{3}.

(21)

Note that
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)<_{\text{lex}}\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi^{\prime})
if and only if
\alpha_{\xi}<\alpha_{\xi^{\prime}}
. With this reformulation, we now focus on the decreasing chain of ordinal ranks.

Let
\alpha<\omega^{\omega}
be an ordinal given in Cantor Normal Form as
\alpha=\omega^{n}\cdot c_{n}+\cdots+\omega^{0}\cdot c_{0}
where
n,c_{0},\ldots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the
size
of
\alpha
as
N\alpha:=\max\{n,\max_{0\leq i\leq n}c_{i}\}
. For the linear KLM sequence
\xi
with
\operatorname{rank}\nolimits(\xi)=(r_{d},\ldots,r_{3})
, we have
N\alpha_{\xi}=\max\{d-3,\max_{3\leq i\leq d}r_{i}\}\leq|\xi|
.

Given a number
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and a function
h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
that is monotone inflationary (that is,
x\leq h(x)
and
h(x)\leq h(y)
whenever
x\leq y
), we say a sequence of ordinals
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\ldots
is
(n_{0},h)
-controlled
if
N\alpha_{i}\leq h^{i}(n_{0})
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
, where
h^{i}(n_{0})
is the
i
th iteration of
h
on
n_{0}
.

Let
\xi^{0},\xi^{1},\ldots
be the linear KLM sequences produced in the modified KLMST algorithm, by Lemma
24
we know that the sequence of ordinal ranks

\alpha_{\xi^{0}}>\alpha_{\xi^{1}}>\cdots

(22)

is
(|\xi^{0}|,h)
-controlled where
h
is defined in Lemma
24
. Recall that
\xi^{0}\in{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}
where
\xi=\left\langle{p(\bm{m})Gq(\bm{n})}\right\rangle
corresponds to the input reachability instance. Then
|\xi^{0}|\leq g(|\xi|)
where
g
is defined in Lemma
19
, and (
22
) is indeed
(g(n),h)
-controlled where
n:=|\left\langle{p(\bm{m})Gq(\bm{n})}\right\rangle|
.

Length function theorem.

The length of the controlled sequence of ordinals (
22
) can be bounded in terms of the hierarchies of fast-growing functions of Hardy and Cichoń
[
4
]
. First recall that given a limit ordinal
\lambda\leq\omega^{\omega}
, the standard
fundamental sequence
of
\lambda
is a sequence
(\lambda(x))_{x<\omega}
defined inductively by

\omega^{\omega}(x):=\omega^{x+1},\qquad(\beta+\omega^{k+1})(x):=\beta+\omega^{k}\cdot(x+1)

(23)

where
\beta+\omega^{k+1}
is in Cantor Normal Form. Now given a function
h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
that is monotone inflationary, we define the
Hardy hierarchy

(h^{\alpha})_{\alpha\leq\omega^{\omega}}
and the
Cichoń hierarchy

(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\leq\omega^{\omega}}
as two families of functions
h^{\alpha},h_{\alpha}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
indexed by ordinals
\alpha\leq\omega^{\omega}
given inductively by

\displaystyle h^{0}(x)

\displaystyle:=x,\qquad h^{\alpha+1}(x):={}

\displaystyle h^{\alpha}(h(x)),\qquad h^{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle:=h^{\lambda(x)}(x),

(24)

\displaystyle h_{0}(x)

\displaystyle:=0,\qquad h_{\alpha+1}(x):={}

\displaystyle 1+h_{\alpha}(h(x)),\qquad h_{\lambda}(x)

\displaystyle:=h_{\lambda(x)}(x).

(25)

Observe that Cichoń hierarchy counts the number of iterations of
h
in Hardy hierarchy, that is,
h^{h_{\alpha}(x)}(x)=h^{\alpha}(x)
.
Also note that as
h
is monotone inflationary, by induction on
\alpha
we have
h_{\alpha}(x)\leq h^{\alpha}(x)
.
Now we give the length function theorem as follows.

Theorem 26

(Length function theorem,
[
20
, Thm. 3.3]
)
.

Let
n_{0}\geq d-2
, then the maximal length of
(n_{0},h)
-controlled decreasing sequences of ordinals in
\omega^{d-2}
is
h_{\omega^{d-2}}(n_{0})
.

Small witness property.

By Theorem
26
we can bound the length of (
22
), which then yields a bound on the minimal length of runs witnessing reachability.

Lemma 27

(Small witnesses)
.

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS where
d\geq 3
, let
p(\bm{m}),q(\bm{n})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
be two configurations, and let
n:=|\left\langle{p(\bm{m})Gq(\bm{n})}\right\rangle|
. If
p(\bm{m})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{n})
holds in
G
, then there is a path
\sigma
such that
p(\bm{m})\xrightarrow{\sigma}q(\bm{n})
and

|\sigma|\leq\ell(h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n)))
,
where
g,h,\ell
are defined in lemmas
19
,
24
, and
25
.

Proof.

Suppose
p(\bm{m})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{n})
, then there is a sequence of linear KLM sequence
\xi^{0},\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{L}
produced in the modified KLMST algorithm, such that
\xi^{L}
is normal. We have discussed that the sequence of their ordinal ranks
\alpha_{\xi^{0}}>\alpha_{\xi^{1}}>\cdots>\alpha_{\xi^{L}}
is
(g(n),h)
-controlled, so by Theorem
26
we have
L\leq h_{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n))
. From Lemma
24
and the fact that
h^{h_{\alpha}(x)}=h^{\alpha}(x)
, the size of
\xi^{L}
is bounded by

|\xi^{L}|\leq h^{L}(|\xi^{0}|)\leq h^{h_{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n))}(g(n))=h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n)).

(26)

Now Lemma
25
bounds the length of the minimal witnesses by
\ell(h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n)))
.
∎

6.2
Fast-Growing Complexity Hierarchy

We recall the fast-growing hierarchy formally introduced by Schmitz
[
21
]
that captures the complexity class high above elementary. Define
H(x):=x+1
, we shall use the Hardy hierarchy
(H^{\alpha})_{\alpha<\omega^{\omega}}
, where for example
H^{\omega^{2}}(x)=2^{x+1}(x+1)
and
H^{\omega^{3}}(x)
grows faster than the tower function. First we define the family

\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}:=\bigcup_{\beta<\omega^{\alpha}}\mathsf{FDTIME}(H^{\beta}(n))

which contains functions computable in deterministic time
O(H^{\beta}(n))
. Observe that, for example,
\mathscr{F}_{3}
contains exactly the Kalmar elementary functions. Now we define

\mathsf{F}_{\alpha}:=\bigcup_{p\in\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}}\mathsf{DTIME}(H^{\omega^{\alpha}}(p(n)))

(27)

which is the class of decision problems solvable in deterministic time
O(H^{\omega^{\alpha}}(p(n)))
. Note that non-deterministic time Turing machines can be made deterministic with an exponential overhead in
\mathscr{F}_{3}
, thus for
\alpha\geq 3
, we have equivalently that

\mathsf{F}_{\alpha}=\bigcup_{p\in\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}}\mathsf{NDTIME}(H^{\omega^{\alpha}}(p(n)))
.
Observe that
\mathsf{F}_{\alpha}
is closed under reductions in
\mathscr{F}_{\alpha}
.

6.2.1
Relativized Fast-Growing Functions

In order to express the complexity of the modified KLMST algorithm in terms of the hierarchy
(\mathsf{F}_{\alpha})_{\alpha<\omega}
, one needs to locate the function
h^{\omega^{d-2}}
in the Hardy hierarchy
(H^{\alpha})_{\alpha<\omega^{\omega}}
where
h\in\mathscr{F}_{3}
is the elementary function from Lemma
24
. Previously we can upper bound
h^{\omega^{d-2}}
by
H^{\omega^{d+1}}
with the help of
[
21
, Lem. 4.2]
. Here we show a slightly better result, from which we can bound
h^{\omega^{d-2}}(x)
by
H^{\omega^{d}}(O(x))
.

Lemma 28

(cf.
[
L
]
em. A.5)
.

Schmitz16]

Let
h:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
be a monotone inflationary function, let
a,b,c\geq 1
and
x_{0}\geq 0
be natural numbers. If for all
x\geq x_{0}
,
h(x)\leq H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c}(x)
, then
h^{\omega^{a}}(x)\leq H^{\omega^{b+a}}((c+1)x)
for all
x\geq\max\{2c,x_{0}\}
.

6.3
Upper Bounds for VASS Reachability

Now we analyze the time complexity of the modified KLMST algorithm. Given as input the
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)
and two configurations
p(\bm{m}),q(\bm{n})
, let
\xi:=\left\langle{p(\bm{m})Gq(\bm{n})}\right\rangle
and
n:=|\xi|
. The initial sequence
\xi^{0}\in{\textrm{clean}({\xi})}
can be computed in (non-deterministic) time elementary in
n
by Lemma
19
. Then the algorithm produces
\xi^{0},\xi^{1},\ldots,\xi^{L}
with
L\leq h_{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n))
, where
g,h
are defined in lemmas
19
,
24
. Note that in each step, the sequence
\xi^{i+1}\in{\textrm{dec}({\xi^{i}})}
can be computed in time elementary in
|\xi^{i}|
by Lemma
24
, and the sizes
|\xi^{i}|
are all bounded by
h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n))
as we have discussed above in the proof of Lemma
27
. To sum up, the entire algorithm finishes in non-deterministic time elementary in
h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n))
.

Lemma 29
.

On input a
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)
where
d\geq 3
and
p(\bm{m}),q(\bm{n})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
, the modified KLMST algorithm finishes in non-deterministic time

e(h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n)))
where
n=|\langle{p(\bm{m})Gq(\bm{n})}\rangle|
, g, h are defined in lemmas
19
,
24
, and
e\in\mathscr{F}_{3}
is some fixed function.

Since
h
is an elementary function, there is a number
c\in\mathbb{N}
such that
h
is eventually dominated by
H^{\omega^{2}\cdot c}
. By Lemma
28
we can upper bound
h^{\omega^{d-2}}(x)
by
H^{\omega^{d}}((c+1)x)
. Observe that the inner part
g(n)
is elementary in the binary encoding size of the input
G,p(\bm{m}),q(\bm{n})
, thus can be captured by a function
p\in\mathscr{F}_{3}
. Finally,
[
21
, Lem. 4.6]
allows us to move the outermost function
e
to the innermost position. Hence we have the following upper bound.

Theorem 30
.

Reachability in
d
-dimensional VASS is in
\mathsf{F}_{d}
for all
d\geq 3
.

Also, by Lemma
27
there is a simple combinatorial algorithm for
d
-VASS reachability. We fist compute the bound
B:=\ell(h^{\omega^{d-2}}(g(n)))
, which can be done in time elementary in
B
by
[
21
, Thm. 5.1]
. Then we can decide reachability by just enumerate all possible paths in
G
with length bounded by
B
.

7
Conclusion

We have shown that the reachability problem in
d
-dimensional vector addition system with states is in
\mathsf{F}_{d}
, improving the previous
\mathsf{F}_{d+4}
upper bound by Leroux and Schmitz
[
15
]
. By capturing reachability in geometrically
2
-dimensional VASSes with linear path schemes, we are able to reduce significantly the number of decomposition steps in the KLMST decomposition algorithm. Combined with a careful analysis on fast-growing functions, we finally obtained the
\mathsf{F}_{d}
upper bound. It should be noticed though, that our algorithm avoids computing the “full decomposition”
[
14
]
of KLM sequences, thus cannot improve the complexity of problems that essentially rely on the full decomposition, e.g., the VASS downward language inclusion problem
[
8
,
23
,
15
]
.

It has been shown that the reachability problem in
(2d{+}3)
-VASS is
\mathsf{F}_{d}
-hard
[
5
]
.
In the case of
3
-VASS, it is known that the reachability problem is
\mathsf{PSPACE}
-hard. The gap between the lower bound and the upper bound
\mathsf{F}_{3}=\mathsf{TOWER}

[
22
]
is huge.

A.3
Sign Reflecting Projection

In this section we develop a technical tool called the “sign reflecting projection” which will be used to project a geometrically
2
-dimensional
d
-VASS to a
2
-VASS so that cyclic runs with effect belonging to a certain orthant can be safely projected back. We first introduce the notion of projection of vectors.

Projection.

Let
I\subseteq[d]
be a subset of indices. Given a vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
where
\mathbb{X}
can be one of
\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{N},\mathbb{Z}
, we define the
projection of
\bm{u}
onto indices in
I
as a function
\bm{u}|_{I}\in\mathbb{X}^{I}
given by

(\bm{u}|_{I})(i)=\bm{u}(i)\text{ for all }i\in I.

(31)

We tacitly identify the function
\bm{u}|_{I}\in\mathbb{X}^{I}
as a vector in
\mathbb{X}^{|I|}
.
Let
i\in[d]
, we abbreviate
\bm{u}^{-i}
for the vector
\bm{u}|_{[d]\setminus\{i\}}
. The definition of projection is naturally extended to set of vectors
V
:

V|_{I}:=\bigl\{\bm{v}|_{I}:\bm{v}\in V\bigr\}.

(32)

It should be clear that the projection of a vector space onto indices in
I
is again a vector space in
\mathbb{Q}^{|I|}
, and the projection of an orthant
Z_{t}
onto
I
is an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{|I|}
defined by
t|_{I}
.

Sign reflecting projection.

Definition 37
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a vector space and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. A set of indices
I\subseteq[d]
is called a
sign-reflecting projection for
P
with respect to
Z
if for any
\bm{v}\in P
,
\bm{v}|_{I}\in Z|_{I}
implies
\bm{v}\in Z
.

Sign-reflecting projection helps us project the vectors in a vector space to some of its components so that the pre-image of a certain orthant still belongs to one orthant. Moreover, we can show that such a projection is one-to-one when restricted to that orthant.

Lemma 38
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a vector space and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Let
I\subseteq[d]
be a sign-reflecting projection for
P
w.r.t.
Z
. Then every vector
\bm{v}\in P\cap Z
is uniquely determined by
\bm{v}|_{I}
. In other words, for any
\bm{v},\bm{v}^{\prime}\in P\cap Z
,
\bm{v}|_{I}=\bm{v}^{\prime}|_{I}
implies
\bm{v}=\bm{v}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Suppose otherwise, there are vectors
\bm{v}\neq\bm{v}^{\prime}\in P\cap Z
with
\bm{v}|_{I}=\bm{v}^{\prime}|_{I}
. Then there must be an index
j\in[d]
with
\bm{v}(j)\neq\bm{v}^{\prime}(j)
. Note that
\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime}\in P
and
\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{v}\in P
cannot belong to the same orthant, as the signs of their
j
th component differ. On the other hand,
(\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime})|_{I}=(\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{v})|_{I}=\bm{0}\in Z|_{I}
. Since
I
is a sign-reflecting projection, we must have
\bm{v}-\bm{v}^{\prime}\in Z
and
\bm{v}^{\prime}-\bm{v}\in Z
, a contradiction.
∎

We mainly care about finding sign-reflecting projections for a plane
P
, that is, a
2
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. The main technical theorem is stated as follows.

Theorem 39
.

For any dimension
d\geq 2
, let
P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a plane (i.e. a
2
-dimensional subspace), and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
such that
P\cap Z
contains two linearly independent vectors. Then there is a sign-reflecting projection
I
for
P
w.r.t.
Z
such that
|I|=2
.

The proof of
39
makes use of the Farkas-Minkowski-Weyl’s Theorem. We first introduce some additional definitions about
cones
.

A
polyhedral cone
is a set of the form
\{\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}:A\bm{v}\leq 0\}
where
A
is a rational matrix. Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a set of vectors, the
cone generated by
V
is the following set:

\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(V):=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\{\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}x_{k}:\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in V\}.

(33)

A
finitely generated cone
is a cone generated by a finite set. The Farkas-Minkowski-Weyl’s Theorem states that:

Theorem 40

(Farkas-Minkowski-Weyl)
.

A set
C\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
is a polyhedral cone if and only if it is a finitely generated cone.

Let
P
be a plane and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
, one easily verifies that
P\cap Z
is a polyhedral cone, where the inequality
A\bm{v}\leq 0
asserts that (i)
\bm{v}
is orthogonal to all normal vectors of
P
, and (ii)
\bm{v}
belongs to the orthant
Z
.
Theorem
40
then shows that the intersection of a plane and an orthant is a finitely generated cone. We need a stronger fact that it is generated by at most 2 vectors.

Lemma 41
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a plane and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. Then there exists
V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
such that
P\cap Z=\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(V)
and
|V|\leq 2
.

Proof.

Let
P
be a plane and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
. The existence of a finite set
V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
such that
P\cap Z=\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(V)
is guaranteed by Theorem
40
. Let
V
have minimal size. Towards a contradiction we assume that
V
contains 3 distinct non-zero vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v},\bm{w}
. These vectors must be linearly dependent since
V\subseteq P
and
P
is 2-dimensional. Suppose
\bm{w}=\lambda_{1}\bm{u}+\lambda_{2}\bm{v}
. We can make sure that
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
are of the same sign since otherwise we just rename
\bm{u},\bm{v},\bm{w}
properly. If
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0
then
\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(V)=\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(V\setminus\{\bm{w}\})
, contradicting to the minimality of
|V|
. Thus
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\leq 0
. But then
\bm{u},\bm{v}
and
\bm{w}
cannot belong to the same orthant, contradicting to
V\subseteq Z
. Therefore, we must have
|V|\leq 2
.
∎

We can now prove Theorem
39
.

Proof of Theorem
39
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{d}
be a plane, and
Z
be an orthant in
\mathbb{Q}^{d}
such that
P\cap Z
contains two linearly independent vectors. Without loss of generality we assume that
Z=\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}
is the first orthant. By Lemma
41
there are non-zero vectors
\bm{u},\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{d}
such that
P\cap Z=\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(\{\bm{u},\bm{v}\})
. We emphasize that
\bm{u},\bm{v}\geq\bm{0}
.

Clearly
\bm{u},\bm{v}
are linearly independent since the cone generated by them contains two linearly independent vectors. We claim that
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{u})
and
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})
are incomparable under
\subseteq
.
Otherwise, suppose
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})\subseteq\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{u})
, then
\lambda\bm{u}-\bm{v}\in P\cap Z
for sufficiently large
\lambda>0
. As
P\cap Z=\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(\{\bm{u},\bm{v}\})
, we have
\lambda\bm{u}-\bm{v}=\alpha\bm{u}+\beta\bm{v}
for some
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
. Then
(\beta+1)\bm{v}=(\lambda-\alpha)\bm{u}
, contradicting to the linear independence of
\bm{u}
and
\bm{v}
.
Symmetrically, we cannot have
\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{u})\subseteq\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})
.
Now take
i\in\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{u})\setminus\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})
and
j\in\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{v})\setminus\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{u})
.
We show that
I=\{i,j\}
is a sign-reflecting projection for
P
w.r.t.
Z
.
Indeed, let
\bm{w}\in P=\operatorname{span}\nolimits(\{\bm{u},\bm{v}\})
be such that
\bm{w}(i),\bm{w}(j)\geq 0
. Suppose
\bm{w}=\alpha\bm{u}+\beta\bm{v}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Q}
. Then
\bm{w}(i)=\alpha\bm{u}(i)\geq 0
implies
\alpha\geq 0
, and
\bm{w}(j)=\beta\bm{v}(j)\geq 0
implies
\beta\geq 0
. Thus
\bm{w}\in\operatorname{cone}\nolimits(\{\bm{u},\bm{v}\})\subseteq Z
.
∎

Finally, we remark that the requirement in Theorem

contains two linearly independent vectors is not essential:

Lemma 42
.

Let

be a plane, and
\bm{v}\in P

such that
\bm{v}\in Z
and

contains two linearly independent vectors.

Proof.

Let
\bm{v}\in P
be non-zero. Pick
\bm{u}\in P
such that

. Choose

large enough such that

for all

. Observe that
\bm{v}
and

∎

we have
\Delta(\rho)|_{I}=\bm{v}|_{I}-\bm{u}|_{I}\in Z|_{I}
. We then have
\Delta(\rho)\in Z
since
I
is sign-reflecting. By Lemma
38
, any vector in
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)\cap Z
is uniquely determined by its components in
I
. As
\Delta(\rho)|_{I}=(\bm{v}-\bm{u})|_{I}
we must have
\Delta(\rho)=\bm{v}-\bm{u}
as claimed.

Finally, we bound the length of
\Lambda
. Just note that
|\Lambda|=|\sigma|\leq|G|^{O(1)}
.
∎

Runs that stay far away from the axes can be decomposed into a short path interleaved by at most
|Q|
cycles, where each cycle can be handled by Lemma
43
. This leads to the second result we will prove in this section:

Lemma 44
.

For every
d
-VASS
G=(Q,T)
that is geometrically
2
-dimensional, there exists a natural number
D\leq|G|^{O(1)}
such that for every pair of configurations
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times[D,\infty)^{d}
with
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{[D,\infty)^{d}}q(\bm{v})
, there exists an LPS
\Lambda
compatible to
G
such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
and
|\Lambda|\leq|G|^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

We put
D
to be the same constant as in Lemma
43
. Denote
\mathbb{O}:=[D,\infty)^{d}
. Let
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{O}
be such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{O}}q(\bm{v})
.

Let
\pi
be a run witnessing
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{O}}q(\bm{v})
. By extracting maximal cycles from
\pi
, we can write
\pi
in the following form:

\pi:p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\alpha_{0}}_{\mathbb{O}}q_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})\xrightarrow{\beta_{1}}_{\mathbb{O}}q_{1}(\bm{v}_{1})\xrightarrow{\alpha_{1}}_{\mathbb{O}}q_{2}(\bm{u}_{2})\cdots q_{k}(\bm{u}_{k})\xrightarrow{\beta_{k}}_{\mathbb{O}}q_{k}(\bm{v}_{k})\xrightarrow{\alpha_{k}}_{\mathbb{O}}q(\bm{v})

(38)

where

•

q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\in Q
are pairwise distinct, so
k\leq|Q|
;

•

\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{k},\bm{v}_{1},\ldots,\bm{v}_{k}\in\mathbb{O}
;

•

\alpha_{0}\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{k}
is a simple path, so
|\alpha_{0}|+\cdots+|\alpha_{k}|\leq|Q|
.

By Lemma
43
, for each cycle
\beta_{i}
there exists an LPS
\Lambda_{i}
compatible to
G
such that
q_{i}(\bm{u}_{i})\xrightarrow{\Lambda_{i}}q_{i}(\bm{v}_{i})
and
|\Lambda_{i}|\leq|G|^{O(1)}
. Let

\Lambda=\alpha_{0}\Lambda_{1}\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\Lambda_{k}\alpha_{k},

(39)

it is easy to see that
\Lambda
is compatible to
G
, and
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
holds.
Finally, we bound the length of
\Lambda
:

|\Lambda|=\sum_{i=0}^{k}|\alpha_{i}|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\Lambda_{i}|\leq|Q|+|Q|\cdot|G|^{O(1)}\leq|G|^{O(1)}.

(40)

∎

We remark that Lemma
44
captures only the runs that stay in the region
[D,\infty)^{d}
, while the run derived from the LPS
\Lambda
given by it may go out of
[D,\infty)^{d}
but stays in
\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

A.5

We have captured runs that stay far away from axes by small LPSes in Section
A.4
. In this section we study the complementary region. Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
be any natural number, we define the region

\mathbb{L}[D]:=\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\bm{u}(i)\leq D\text{ for some }i\in[d]\}.

(41)

The following is to be proved:

Lemma 45
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
and let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS that is geometrically
2
-dimensional. For every pair of configurations
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{L}[D]

\Lambda

p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})

Let’s first subdivide the region

Lemma 46
.

Let
D\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[d]
, and let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS that is geometrically
2

p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}}[D]}q(\bm{v})
there exists an LPS
\Lambda
compatible to
G
such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
, and
|\Lambda|\leq(|G|+D)^{O(1)}
.

Note that Lemma
45

A.5.1
The Base Case: Reachability with One Bounded Component

A run that stays in
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
has its
i
th component bounded by
D
. In this case we can simply encode this component in the states and reduce the dimension by one. Recall that we are proving Theorem
5
by induction on the dimension
d
, so here we assume that Theorem
5
already holds for
(d-1)
-VASSes as the induction hypothesis.

Lemma 47
.

Let
0\leq B\leq C
and
i\in[d]
, let
\mathbb{L}_{i}[B,C]
denote the region
\{\bm{u}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:B\leq\bm{u}(i)\leq C\}
. Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS that is geometrically
2
-dimensional. For every pair of configurations

\Lambda
compatible to
G
such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
, and
|\Lambda|\leq(|G|+(C-B))^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

Let
G^{-i}=(Q^{-i},T^{-i})
be the
(d-1)
-VASS obtained from

where

\displaystyle Q^{-i}

\displaystyle=\{(q,z):q\in Q,z\in[B,C]\},

(43)

\displaystyle T^{-i}

\displaystyle=\{((p,z),\bm{a}^{-i},(q,z^{\prime})):(p,\bm{a},q)\in T,z^{\prime}-z=\bm{a}(i)\}.

(44)

For each transition

let
g(t)
be the unique transition
(p,\bm{a},q)\in T
where
\bm{a}(i)=z^{\prime}-z
. The mapping
g
is extended to linear path schemes compatible to

as a word morphism.

Note that
G^{-i}
is still geometrically
2
-dimensional as

is. Moreover,
|Q^{-i}|=|Q|\cdot(C-B+1)
, and
\left\|{T^{-i}}\right\|\leq\left\|{T}\right\|
, so
|G^{-i}|\leq(|G|+C-B)^{O(1)}
. Let

be configurations in

such that

holds in

. Then
(p,\bm{u}(i))(\bm{u}^{-i})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{N}^{d-1}}(q,\bm{v}(i))(\bm{v}^{-i})
holds in

. Recall we have assumed that Theorem
5
holds for dimension

. In particular, it holds for

. Thus there exists an LPS
\Lambda
compatible to

such that
(p,\bm{u}(i))(\bm{u}^{-i})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}_{\mathbb{N}^{d-1}}(q,\bm{v}(i))(\bm{v}^{-i})
. Note that

is an LPS compatible to

. Since the

th coordinate is encoded in the state of

also holds in

.

As for the size of

|g(\Lambda)|=|\Lambda|\leq|G^{-i}|^{O(1)}\leq(|G|+C-B)^{O(1)}
.
∎

The base case of Lemma
46
where

by taking

and

. The general form of Lemma

will be used later.

A.5.2
The Inductive Proof of Lemma
46

We now consider
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}}[D]
-reachability for
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[d]
with
|\mathcal{I}|>1
, assuming that Lemma
46
holds for all subsets
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}\subsetneq\mathcal{I}
. First we identify a special case where
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
is orthogonal to one of the axes, which makes

essentially a
(d-1)
-VASS.

Lemma 48
.

Let
G=(Q,T)
be a
d
-VASS that is geometrically
2
-dimensional. Suppose there exists
i\in[d]
such that
i\notin\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G))
. Then for every pair of configurations
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{v})
there exists an LPS
\Lambda
compatible to
G
such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
, and
|\Lambda|\leq|G|^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

Let
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
be two configurations of
G
with
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}q(\bm{v})
. We show that there exists an LPS
\Lambda
compatible to
G
such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
and
|\Lambda|\leq|G|^{O(1)}
.

First we claim that for any configurations
r(\bm{w})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
reachable from
p(\bm{u})
, we have
|\bm{w}(i)-\bm{u}(i)|\leq|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
. Otherwise, assume there is a run
\pi:p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}r(\bm{w})
such that
|\bm{w}(i)-\bm{u}(i)|>|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
. Assume w.l.o.g. it is the case that
\bm{w}(i)-\bm{u}(i)>|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
. Write
\pi
in the following form as a sequence of sub-runs that increase the
i
th component like a stair:

\pi:p(\bm{u})=q_{0}(\bm{u}_{0})\xrightarrow{*}q_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})\xrightarrow{*}\cdots\xrightarrow{*}q_{k}(\bm{u}_{k})\xrightarrow{*}r(\bm{w})

(45)

such that
\bm{u}_{k}(i)\geq\bm{w}(i)>\bm{u}(i)+|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
and for all
j\in[k]
,
\bm{u}_{j}(i)>\bm{u}_{j-1}(i)
and every configuration on the sub-run from
q_{j-1}(\bm{u}_{j-1})
to
q_{j}(\bm{u}_{j})
has its
i
-th component no greater than
\bm{u}_{j-1}(i)
. Clearly
\bm{u}_{j}(i)-\bm{u}_{j-1}(i)\leq\left\|{T}\right\|
, so
k\geq(\bm{u}_{k}(i)-\bm{u}(i))/\left\|{T}\right\|>|Q|
. By the Pigeonhole Principle there exists
j_{1}<j_{2}
such that
q_{j_{1}}=q_{j_{2}}
. The sub-run from
q_{j_{1}}(\bm{u}_{j_{1}})
to
q_{j_{2}}(\bm{u}_{j_{2}})
is a cycle which has positive effect in its
i
-th component, contradicting to the assumption that
i\notin\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G))
.

We have shown that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{L}_{i}[B,C]}q(\bm{v})
where
B=\bm{u}(i)-|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
and
C=\bm{u}(i)+|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
. Note that
C-B=2\cdot|Q|\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|\leq|G|^{O(1)}
. The result follows directly from Lemma
47
.
∎

Now we prove Lemma
46
.

Proof of Lemma
46
.

We prove by induction on
|\mathcal{I}|
. The base case where
|\mathcal{I}|=1
follows from Lemma
47
by taking
B=0
and
C=D
. Now assume that
\mathcal{I}=\{i\}\uplus\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
where
i\in\mathcal{I}
is arbitrary and
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}=\mathcal{I}\setminus\{i\}
is non-empty. Fix
p(\bm{u}),q(\bm{v})\in Q\times\mathbb{N}^{d}
with
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}}[D]}q(\bm{v})
, we will exhibit an LPS
\Lambda
compatible to
G
such that
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\Lambda}q(\bm{v})
.

Fix a run
\pi
witnessing
p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{*}_{\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}}[D]}q(\bm{v})
such that no configuration occurs more than once on
\pi
.
We assume that
\pi
must visit both
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
and
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D]
, otherwise we can directly apply the induction hypothesis.
Observe that
\pi
can be written in the following form as an alternating sequence of sub-runs lie in either
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
or
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D]
:

\pi:p(\bm{u})\xrightarrow{\sigma}_{\mathbb{R}_{0}}q_{0}(\bm{u}_{0})\xrightarrow{\pi_{1}}_{\mathbb{R}_{1}}q_{1}(\bm{u}_{1})\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}}_{\mathbb{R}_{2}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\pi_{n}}_{\mathbb{R}_{n}}q_{n}(\bm{u}_{n})\xrightarrow{\rho}_{\mathbb{R}_{n+1}}q(\bm{u})

(46)

where

•

\mathbb{R}_{h}\in\{\mathbb{L}_{i}[D],\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D]\}
for
h=0,\ldots,n+1
, and
\mathbb{R}_{h}\neq\mathbb{R}_{h-1}
for
h\in[n+1]
,

•

\bm{u}_{h}\in\mathbb{J}_{i\ell}
for some (not necessarily unique)
\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
for each
h=0,\ldots,n
, where

\mathbb{J}_{i\ell}:=\{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:\bm{w}(i),\bm{w}(\ell)\leq D+\left\|{T}\right\|\}.

(47)

(Note that we increase the bound by
\left\|{T}\right\|
so that any one-step transition from
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
to
\mathbb{L}_{\ell}[D]
lies completely in
\mathbb{J}_{i\ell}
.)

Note that the prefix
\sigma
and the suffix
\rho
can be captured by LPSes of length
(|G|+D)^{O(1)}
by induction hypothesis. In the following we show that the infix
\overline{\pi}:=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\ldots\pi_{n}
can also be captured by an LPS
\overline{\Lambda}
of length
(|G|+D)^{O(1)}
. Then the entire run
\pi
is captured by a concatenation of these three LPSes.

Let
\pi_{h}
be any sub-run of
\overline{\pi}
such that
\mathbb{R}_{h}=\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
where
h\in[n]
. Expand
\pi_{h}
as

\pi_{h}:q_{h-1}(\bm{u}_{h-1})=:s_{0}(\bm{v}_{0})\xrightarrow{t_{1}}_{\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]}s_{1}(\bm{v}_{1})\xrightarrow{t_{2}}_{\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]}\cdots\xrightarrow{t_{m}}_{\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]}s_{m}(\bm{v}_{m}):=q_{h}(\bm{u}_{h}),

(48)

where
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in T
are transitions.
Denote
D^{\prime}:=(D+\left\|{T}\right\|)+|Q|\cdot D\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
,
we classify
\pi_{h}
into the following types:

Type 1:

There exists
\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
such that
\bm{v}_{k}(\ell)\leq D^{\prime}
for all
k=0,\ldots,m
.

In this case,
\pi_{h}
is actually an
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D^{\prime}]
-run.

Type 2(
\ell
), where
\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
:

There exists
k\in[m]
such that
\bm{v}_{k}(\ell)>D^{\prime}
, and
\bm{u}_{h-1}=\bm{v}_{0}\in\mathbb{J}_{i\ell}
.

In this case
\bm{v}_{k}(\ell)-\bm{v}_{0}(\ell)>D^{\prime}-(D+\left\|{T}\right\|)=|Q|\cdot D\cdot\left\|{T}\right\|
.
Let
k
be minimal such that
\bm{v}_{k}(\ell)>D^{\prime}
.
For each
\kappa<k
we define the index
\operatorname{next}\nolimits(\kappa)
as the minimal index
\kappa^{\prime}
with
\kappa<\kappa^{\prime}\leq k
and
\bm{v}_{\kappa^{\prime}}(\ell)>\bm{v}_{\kappa}(\ell)
.
Let
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\ldots,\kappa_{M}
be the sequence of indices such that
\kappa_{1}=0
and
\kappa_{j+1}=\operatorname{next}\nolimits(\kappa_{j})
for all
j=1,\ldots,M-1
, and
\kappa_{M}=k
. We must have
M>|Q|\cdot D
.
Since
\bm{v}_{\kappa_{j}}(i)\leq D
for all
\kappa_{j}
(recall
\pi_{h}
stays in
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
), by the Pigeonhole Principle there exist

such that

s_{\kappa_{a}}=s_{\kappa_{b}},\quad\bm{v}_{\kappa_{a}}(i)=\bm{v}_{\kappa_{b}}(i),\quad\bm{v}_{\kappa_{a}}(\ell)<\bm{v}_{\kappa_{b}}(\ell).

(49)

Therefore, the existence of a Type 2(
\ell
) sub-run
\pi_{h}
implies that
\operatorname{Cyc}\nolimits(G)
contains a vector
\bm{r}
such that
i\notin\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{r})
and
\ell\in\mathop{\operatorname{supp}}\nolimits(\bm{r})
.

Recall that the run
\pi_{h}
must start from a place in
\mathbb{J}_{i\ell}
for some

. So
\pi_{h}
is either of Type 1 or of Type 2(
\ell
) (or both, but we let Type 1 take precedence).

If all the
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
-sub-runs

are of Type 1, then
\overline{\pi}
is indeed an
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D^{\prime}]

.
Next we assume there exist Type 2(
\ell

\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D^{\prime}]
instead of
\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D]

\overline{\pi}
in the following form to get rid of all Type 1 sub-runs in

\displaystyle\overline{\pi}:q_{0}(\bm{u}_{0})=q_{0}^{\prime}(\bm{u}_{0}^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\pi_{1}^{\prime}}_{\mathbb{R}_{1}^{\prime}}q_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{u}_{1}^{\prime})\xrightarrow{\pi_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathbb{R}_{2}^{\prime}}\cdots\xrightarrow{\pi_{N}^{\prime}}_{\mathbb{R}_{N}^{\prime}}q_{N}^{\prime}(\bm{u}_{N}^{\prime})=q_{n}(\bm{u}_{n})

(50)

where

•

\mathbb{R}_{h}^{\prime}\in\{\mathbb{L}_{i}[D],\mathbb{L}_{\mathcal{I}^{\prime}}[D^{\prime}]\}
for

, and
\mathbb{R}_{h}^{\prime}\neq\mathbb{R}_{h-1}^{\prime}
for

,

•

\bm{u}_{h}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{J}_{i\ell}
for some (not necessarily unique)

for each

,

•

for each

is of Type 2(
\ell
) for some

.

Let

\mathcal{H}_{\ell}=\{h\in[N]:\mathbb{R}_{h}^{\prime}=\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]\text{ and $\pi_{h}^{\prime}$ is of Type 2($\ell$)}\}

(51)

be the set of indices of those
\mathbb{L}_{i}[D]
-sub-runs that are of Type 2(
\ell
). There remain two cases:

Case 1.

For all
\ell\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
, we have

.

In this case, the total number of indices

and

and thus

. This situation is already solved by Lemma

.

Let

Lemma 49

\bm{y}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{b}\biggr\},

(68)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{0}^{+}:=\biggl\{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\times\mathbb{N}^{m}:\begin{bmatrix}A&I_{m}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{x}\\

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

We need to use the fact that if

is a monotone inflationary function, then so are the functions
h^{\alpha}
in the Hardy hierarchy, which can be proved by induction on

. Let
G:=H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c}
, first we show the following inequality holds for all

(89)

{claimproof}

We proceed by induction on
a
. For
a=1
:

\displaystyle(c+1)G^{\omega}(x)

\displaystyle=(c+1)G^{x+1}(x)

\displaystyle=(c+1)H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c(x+1)}(x)

\displaystyle\leq H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c}(H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c(x+1)}(x))

\displaystyle\text{since }H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c}(x)\geq H^{\omega\cdot c}(x)\geq 2^{c}\cdot x

\displaystyle=H^{\omega^{b}\cdot c(x+2)}(x)

\displaystyle\leq H^{\omega^{b}\cdot((c+1)x+1)}(x)

\displaystyle\text{by }x\geq 2c

\displaystyle\leq H^{\omega^{b}\cdot((c+1)x+1)}((c+1)x)

by monotony

\displaystyle=H^{\omega^{b+1}}((c+1)x).

Next let’s consider the case
a+1
, assuming (
89
) holds for
a
. We first prove the following inequality holds for all

by induction on

:

(90)

The base case

holds trivially. For the induction step, note that

\displaystyle H^{\omega^{b+a}\cdot(j+1)}((c+1)y)

\displaystyle=H^{\omega^{b+a}}(H^{\omega^{b+a}\cdot j}((c+1)y))

\displaystyle\geq H^{\omega^{b+a}}((c+1)G^{\omega^{a}\cdot j}(y))

by induction hypothesis of (
90
)

\displaystyle\geq(c+1)G^{\omega^{a}}(G^{\omega^{a}\cdot j}(y))

by induction hypothesis of (
89

\displaystyle=(c+1)G^{\omega^{a}\cdot(j+1)}(y).

\displaystyle H^{\omega^{b+a+1}}((c+1)x)
