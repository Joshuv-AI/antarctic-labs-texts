Optimal stopping with nonlinear expectation: geometric and algorithmic solutions Thanks: This work was supported by the Lloyd’s Register Foundation-Alan Turing Institute programme on Data-Centric Engineering under the LRF grant G0095.
Tomasz Kosmala, John Moriarty

Abstract
We use the geometry of functions associated with martingales under nonlinear expectations to solve risk-sensitive Markovian optimal stopping problems. Generalising the linear case due to Dynkin and Yushkievich (1969), the value function is the majorant or pointwise infimum of those functions which dominate the gain function. An emphasis is placed on the geometry of the majorant and pathwise arguments, rather than exploiting convexity, positive homogeneity or related analytical properties. An algorithm is provided to construct the value function at the computational cost of a two-dimensional search. Key words: Optimal stopping, nonlinear expectation, risk measures, geometric method, Markov property. MSC2010 Classification: 60G40, 91B08, 91B06, 60J25. JEL Classification: C61, D81.



Convexity and associated properties have been used as main tools in the global solution of both stochastic and deterministic optimisation problems. This paper concerns maximising a Markovian performance criterion when stopping a Brownian motion, where the relationship between concavity and excessivity has been used since the seminal work of Dynkin
[
15
]
. However this concavity arises from the linearity of the expectation operator, and there is increasing recent interest in the use of nonlinear, including nonconcave, expectations. In this paper we use the geometry of the
majorant
(Figure
1
), that is the pointwise infimum of certain functions associated with martingales under the nonlinear expectation, to obtain constructive formulae for the value function and optimal stopping policy in the Markovian context. The formulae are thus analogues to those of Dynkin and Yushkievich
[
16
]
for the linear case.

Interest in such nonlinear criteria has arisen in numerous domains. A few examples include the theory of preferences, see for example
[
17
, Ch. 2]
and
[
33
]
, optimisation under ambiguity
[
6
,
7
,
10
]
, and even reinforcement learning
[
19
,
25
]
. The celebrated
prospect theory
of Kahneman and Tversky
[
21
]
describes how the same agent can switch between risk averse (concave) and risk seeking (convex) criteria, depending on whether losses or gains are considered. This helps to motivate methods based on other properties.

In the absence of convexity or related global analytical properties it is natural to seek local solutions. Accordingly we use the geometry of this majorant, which is generated using a set
\mathcal{H}
of functions associated with martingales under nonlinear expectations, to solve the problem locally. A pathwise argument then confirms this local solution to be optimal.

Our main result is that, under sufficient regularity, the value of the nonlinear optimal stopping problem is the pointwise infimum over those functions in
\mathcal{H}
which dominate the gain function determining the reward or payoff. It is optimal to stop the first time this value function coincides with the gain function. Thus the value function and optimal stopping region are constructed from the geometry of the gain function and majorant. In the linear case, this result was obtained for the Wiener process in
[
16
, Ch. III.7]
, and extended to one-dimensional regular diffusion processes (with exponential discounting of the reward) in
[
13
]
by weakening the notion of concavity. For
\kappa
-ambiguity, which is a positively homogeneous expectation, a result of this type was obtained in
[
10
]
using ideas from
[
4
]
.

A common solution technique in optimal stopping problems is to generate a heuristic solution and then apply a verification argument (see for example
[
7
, Th. 3.4]
). Although the assumptions on the nonlinear expectation are strong (see for example
[
24
]
), in Section
1.1
we confirm that the solution is correct for the worst-case risk mapping, which is less regular. If used as a heuristic then, as illustrated by Figure
2
, the construction differs from the well-known principle of smooth fit or smooth pasting (see for example
[
28
]
). As a corollary, no smooth transformation of Figure
2
could reduce the solution to the classical one of
[
16
, Ch. III.7]
.

To the best of our knowledge the method of proof is different to existing approaches using linearity, generalised concavity, positive homogeneity or related analytical properties, and is novel even in the linear case. This may be of independent interest, particularly as few explicit solutions are known for optimal stopping of Brownian motion in dimension greater than one
[
11
]
, although the topic is outside the scope of this paper.

A simple algorithmic implementation of the construction returns the value function and optimal stopping region, at the cost of a two-dimensional nonconvex optimisation. The algorithm is presented under regularity assumptions which may be compared to those made in
[
12
]
, where the authors use analytic methods to provide an algorithmic solution in the linear case.

1.4
Related work

Several studies have been made of
robust
optimal stopping, where multiple possible linear expectations are considered. The stopping policy is chosen assuming the worst case, making these robust criteria concave. Related work in the Markovian setting includes
[
1
]
, and
[
7
]
in higher dimension, where solutions are characterised using the Bellman equation.
In the non-Markovian framework (also in higher dimension), martingale methods are applied to characterise solutions in terms of Snell envelopes, see for example
[
2
,
3
,
20
,
27
]
. Such problems are also closely related to stochastic games of control and stopping, see for example
[
22
]
.

To the best of our knowledge, algorithmic solutions have thus far only been provided in the linear case. In addition to
[
12
]
there is a series of studies
[
9
,
18
,
30
]
which embed the problem within an infinite dimensional linear program over a space of measures and examine its dual, yielding a semi-infinite linear program and a nonlinear optimisation problem which facilitate the construction of the value function. Our algorithm has some similarity to the latter approach, in that it begins by seeking local solutions.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section

states assumptions and provides preliminary results, Section
2.2
states and proves the main result, Section
2.3
establishes properties of the stopping set and the regularity of the value function, Section
2.4
develops the solution algorithm, and Section
3

\beta,\gamma\geq 0

\displaystyle\rho_{s}^{x}(M_{t})

\displaystyle=\rho_{s}^{x}\left(h(X_{t\wedge\tau_{y,z}})\right)=\rho_{s}^{x}\left(\rho^{X_{t\wedge\tau_{y,z}}}(g(X_{\tau_{y,z}}))\right)=\rho_{s}^{x}\left(\rho_{t\wedge\tau_{y,z}}^{x}(g(X_{\tau_{y,z}}\circ\theta_{t\wedge\tau_{y,z}}))\right)

\displaystyle=\rho_{s\wedge\tau_{y,z}}^{x}(g(X_{\tau_{y,z}}\circ\theta_{t\wedge\tau_{y,z}}))=\rho_{s\wedge\tau_{y,z}}^{x}(g(X_{\tau_{y,z}}\circ\theta_{s\wedge\tau_{y,z}}))\qquad(*)

\displaystyle=\rho^{X_{s\wedge\tau_{y,z}}}(g(X_{\tau_{y,z}}))=h(X_{s\wedge\tau_{y,z}})=M_{s},

where in step

\displaystyle\rho_{\tau_{1}}^{x}(\rho_{\tau_{2}}^{x}(Z))

\displaystyle=\rho_{\tau_{1}}^{x}(\rho_{\tau_{2}}^{x}(Z)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}\}}+\rho_{\tau_{2}}^{x}(Z)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau_{2}<\tau_{1}\}})

The second claim follows by taking

-a.s. finiteness of

2.1
Assumptions and preliminary results

We will appeal to the continuity of the nonconcave majorant

(9)

\displaystyle w(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf\{h(x):h\in\mathcal{H},\;h\geq g\text{ on }[0,1]\},

(see Figure
1
). In line with the classical case (cf. (
7
)),
\mathcal{H}
will be continuous, so the nonconcave majorant

is upper semicontinuous (since for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
with
h\geq g
and
h(x)<w(x)+\varepsilon
, and
w\leq h
). To ensure the lower semicontinuity of

we assume that
\mathcal{H}
is uniformly equicontinuous. The assumptions made in this paper are the following:

Assumption 2.4
.

The dynamic risk mapping
\varrho
is strongly Markov, time consistent and continuous,
\mathcal{H}
is uniformly equicontinuous, and
g
is continuous and nonnegative.

(Note that by translation invariance, the assumption that
g
is nonnegative is made without loss of generality. It is also interesting to note that the worst-case risk mapping of Section
1.1
is outside the scope of Assumption
2.4
, since
\mathcal{H}
is not continuous in that case.)

We will use the following version of the optional sampling theorem (a related result in the case of positively homogeneous nonlinear expectation appears in
[
26
, Th. 4.10]
):

Proposition 2.5
.

Under Assumption
2.4
,
if
M
is a càdlàg
\varrho
-martingale then for all
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
and
x\in[0,1]
we have
\rho^{x}(M_{\tau})=\rho^{x}(M_{0})
.

Proof.

Supposing first that time is discrete, we show that
\rho_{n}^{x}(M_{\tau\wedge(n+1)})=M_{\tau\wedge n}
. Indeed, since
M_{\tau\wedge n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{n}
-measurable and
\{\tau>n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
we have

\rho_{n}^{x}(M_{\tau\wedge(n+1)})-M_{\tau\wedge n}=\rho_{n}^{x}(M_{\tau\wedge(n+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge n})\\
=\rho_{n}^{x}((M_{\tau\wedge(n+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge n})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau>n\}})=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau>n\}}\rho_{n}^{x}(M_{n+1}-M_{n})=0.

It follows by time consistency that

\rho^{x}(M_{\tau\wedge(n+1)})=\rho^{x}(M_{\tau\wedge n})=\cdots=\rho^{x}(M_{0}).

Taking

it follows by the continuity of
\rho^{x}
that
\rho^{x}(M_{\tau})=\rho^{x}(M_{0})
.

In continuous time there exists a sequence
(\tau_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of stopping times attaining countably many values and decreasing almost surely to
\tau

[
31
, Th. 77.1, p. 188]
. Taking the limit in
\rho^{x}(M_{\tau_{n}})=\rho^{x}(M_{0})
and using the fact that

is càdlàg and
\rho^{x}
is continuous, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Lemma 2.6

(Properties of

.

Writing

For part

h_{w}(w)=g(w)<\bar{g}+1
and so the above procedure may be applied (with the same value for
\delta
) to extend the function
h_{w}
to
h^{\prime}_{w}:=h^{w^{\prime},x^{+}}_{\gamma^{\prime},g(x^{+})}
where
w^{\prime}=\max\{w-\delta,0\}\leq w_{\tilde{n}+1}
.
By the definition of
V
and of
h_{w}
, and recalling Lemma
2.11
, suboptimality gives
h_{w}(x^{-})\leq V(x^{-})=g(x^{-})
. On the other hand, the
\varrho
-martingale property and monotonicity give
h_{w}(x^{-})\geq h^{\circ}(x^{-})=g(x^{-})
, so
h_{w}(x^{-})=g(x^{-})
. The suprema taken during the extension process (cf. (
15
)) therefore give
h^{\prime}_{w}(w_{k})\leq h^{\circ}(w_{k})
for
k=1,2,\ldots,\tilde{n}+1
. The
\varrho
-martingale property then gives
g(w)=h^{\prime}_{w}(w)\leq\rho^{w}(h^{\circ}(X_{\tau_{w_{\tilde{n}},w_{\tilde{n}+1}}}))=h^{\circ}(w)
, which is the required contradiction. An analogous argument establishes that
h^{\circ}\geq g
on
[x^{+},1]
.

Let

(16)

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\sup\{g(u)-h^{\circ}(u):u\in[x^{-},x^{+}]\},

and suppose for a contradiction that
\varepsilon>0
. By translation invariance
h^{\circ}+\varepsilon
lies in
\mathcal{H}
(modulo the above truncation procedure), and it dominates
g
on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
and thus on
[0,1]
. By continuity the supremum in (
16
) is attained on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
and by the construction of
h^{\circ}
it must be attained at some
u\in(x^{-},x^{+})
.
Then we have

g(u)\leq w(u)\leq h^{\circ}(u)+\varepsilon=g(u),

thus
g(u)=w(u)
, contradicting (
12
)–(
13
). We conclude that
h^{\circ}\geq g
on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
and thus on
[0,1]
.
∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathscr{T}

with
0\leq\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}
,
\mathbb{P}^{x}-
a.s.. Then there exists a (random) stopping time
\tau_{3}\in\mathscr{T}
such that
\mathbb{P}^{x}-
a.s. we have

\displaystyle\rho^{x}_{\tau_{1}}(g(X_{\tau_{2}}))

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\rho^{X_{\tau_{1}}}(g(X_{\tau_{3}})),&\{\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}\},\\
g(X_{\tau_{1}}),&\{\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}\}.\end{cases}

Proof.

We divide the proof into two steps.

Step (i).
Fix
\omega\in\Omega
and consider the map
G^{\omega}\colon\Omega\to\Omega
given by
\bar{\omega}\mapsto G^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})
, which constructs a sample path
G^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})
by (a) following
\omega
until the time
\tau_{1}(\omega)
, then (b) following the increments of
\bar{\omega}
. That is,

\displaystyle G^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})(t)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\omega(t),&t\leq\tau_{1}(\omega),\\
\omega(\tau_{1}(\omega))+\bar{\omega}(t-\tau_{1}(\omega))-\bar{\omega}(0),&t>\tau_{1}(\omega).\end{cases}

For any stopping time
\tau\in\mathscr{T}
consider the random time
\tau^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})
obtained by applying the function
\tau
to this sample path:

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}\mapsto\tau^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})

\displaystyle:=\tau(G^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})).

Then since
G^{\omega}(\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\omega))=\omega
, we have
\tau^{\omega}(\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\omega))=\tau(\omega)
and so
\tau_{i}^{\omega}\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\omega)=\tau_{i}(\omega)
for
i=1,2
. Thus

\tau_{2}(\omega)=\tau_{1}(\omega)+(\tau_{2}^{\omega}-\tau_{1}^{\omega})\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\omega),

and recalling that
\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}

\mathbb{P}^{x}
-almost surely we have

(17)

\displaystyle g(X_{\tau_{2}(\omega)})=g(X_{\tau_{2}^{\omega}-\tau_{1}^{\omega}})\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\omega).

Define an auxiliary bounded random variable
Z^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}
by

\displaystyle Z^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}

\displaystyle\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R},

(18)

\displaystyle Z^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\bar{\omega})

\displaystyle=g\left(X_{\tau_{2}^{\omega}-\tau_{1}^{\omega}}(\bar{\omega})\right).

Now suppose that
\omega
and
\bar{\omega}
are independent random draws from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}^{x})
.
Then by the translation invariance and strong Markov properties for Brownian motion, the law of
\tilde{\omega}:=G^{\omega}(\bar{\omega})
is again that of a random draw from
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}^{x})
.
Putting the above together we have
\mathbb{P}^{x}
-a.s. that

(19)

\displaystyle\rho^{x}_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(g(X_{\tau_{2}(\omega)}))=\rho^{x}_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(Z^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(\omega))=\rho^{x}_{\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})}(Z^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})}(\tilde{\omega})),

where the first equality follows from (
17
)–(
18
) and the second from Definition
1.3
, the fact that the law of
\tilde{\omega}
is Brownian motion, and the identity
\tau_{1}(\omega)=\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})
.

Step (ii).

Next we show that
\tau_{3}^{\omega}:=\tau_{2}^{\omega}-\tau_{1}^{\omega}
lies in
\mathscr{T}
. Recalling (
5
), for each fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, the operator
\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}
is understood to be the time shift
\theta_{s}
by the fixed amount
s=\tau_{1}(\omega)
; similarly for
\theta_{-\tau_{1}(\omega)}
. From the construction in Section
1.2
, the filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is homogeneous with respect to time. Defining

\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}:=\mathcal{F}_{t}\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}=\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{1}(\omega)+t},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R},

the filtrations
\mathbb{F}^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}=(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau_{1}(\omega)})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
and
\mathbb{F}
can be identified, and thus
\mathbb{F}^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}
-stopping times and
\mathbb{F}
-stopping times can also be identified. Since
\tau_{2}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time,
for each
\bar{\omega}\in\Omega
we have

\displaystyle\{(\tau_{2}^{\omega}-\tau_{1}^{\omega})(\bar{\omega})\leq t\}

\displaystyle=\{(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})(\tilde{\omega})\leq t\}

\displaystyle=\{\tau_{2}(\tilde{\omega})\leq\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})+t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})+t}=\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{1}(\omega)+t}=\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\tau_{1}(\omega)},

so that
\tau_{3}^{\omega}
is an
\mathbb{F}^{\tau_{1}(\omega)}
-stopping time. By the above identification

\hat{\tau}_{3}^{\omega}:=\tau_{3}^{\omega}\circ\theta_{-\tau_{1}(\omega)}
is a (random)
\mathbb{F}
-stopping time. By the strong Markov property for Brownian motion we have
\tau_{2}^{\omega}<\infty
a.s., giving
0\leq\tau_{3}^{\omega}<\infty
a.s., so that
\tau_{3}^{\omega}\in\mathscr{T}
.

Combining
(
18
) and (
19
) and then applying the strong Markov property of
\varrho
gives
\mathbb{P}^{x}
-a.s.

\displaystyle\rho^{x}_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}(g(X_{\tau_{2}(\omega)}))

\displaystyle=\rho^{x}_{\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})}(g(X_{\tau_{3}^{\omega}})\circ\theta_{\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})}(\tilde{\omega}))

\displaystyle=\rho^{X_{\tau_{1}(\tilde{\omega})}}\left(g\left(X_{\tau_{3}^{\omega}}\right)\right)=\rho^{X_{\tau_{1}(\omega)}}\left(g\left(X_{\tau_{3}^{\omega}}\right)\right),

completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.14
.

V(x)=\check{V}(x):=\sup_{\tau\in\mathscr{T},\tau\leq\tau^{*}}\rho^{x}(g(X_{\tau})).

Proof.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
. Setting
\tau_{1}=\tau\wedge\tau^{*}
and
\tau_{2}=\tau
in Lemma
2.13
gives that for some stopping time
\tau_{3}
,

\displaystyle\rho^{x}(g(X_{\tau}))

\displaystyle=\rho^{x}(\rho^{x}_{\tau\wedge\tau^{*}}(g(X_{\tau})))=\rho^{x}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau<\tau^{*}\}}g(X_{\tau})+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau\geq\tau^{*}\}}\rho^{X_{\tau^{*}}}(g(X_{\tau_{3}})))

\displaystyle\leq\rho^{x}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau<\tau^{*}\}}\rho^{X_{\tau}}(g(X_{0}))+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\tau\geq\tau^{*}\}}\rho^{X_{\tau^{*}}}(g(X_{0})))

\displaystyle=\rho^{x}(\rho^{X_{\tau\wedge\tau^{*}}}(g(X_{0})))=\rho^{x}(g(X_{\tau\wedge\tau^{*}})),

where the first equality follows from time consistency and the inequality follows from the fact that
g(X_{\tau^{*}})=V(X_{\tau^{*}})
and optimality. This gives
V\leq\check{V}
and hence
V=\check{V}
.
∎

Thus to evaluate
V(x)
it suffices to study
\check{V}
on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
.
For this, the above analysis may be repeated with the interval
[0,1]
replaced by
[x^{-},x^{+}]
. Denote the resulting analogues of all objects with a check mark so that, for example,

\check{w}(x)=\inf\left\{\check{h}(x):\check{h}\in\check{\mathcal{H}},\check{h}\geq g\text{ on }[x^{-},x^{+}]\right\}.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

From Lemma
2.12
we have
g\leq h^{\circ}\in\mathcal{H}
, so

w(x)\leq h^{\circ}(x)=\rho^{x}(g(X_{\tau^{*}}))\leq\check{V}(x).

Conversely, if
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
and
\tau\leq\tau^{*}
then

h^{\circ}(x)=\rho^{x}(h^{\circ}(X_{\tau}))\geq\rho^{x}(g(X_{\tau}))
, giving
h^{\circ}(x)=\check{V}(x)=V(x)
by Proposition
2.14
. To complete the proof note that for any
\tilde{h}=h^{y,z}_{\beta,\gamma}\in\mathcal{H}
with
x\in[y,z]
and
\tilde{h}\geq g
we have

\displaystyle\tilde{h}(x)

\displaystyle=\rho^{x}(\tilde{h}(X_{\tau^{*}\wedge\tau_{y,z}}))\geq\rho^{x}(g(X_{\tau^{*}}))=h^{\circ}(x),

(cf. the last inequlity in (
11
)) so that
w(x)\geq h^{\circ}(x)
and hence

w(x)=h^{\circ}(x)=V(x)
.

∎

2.3
Stopping set and regularity

Since the proof of Theorem
2.9
concludes with
h^{\circ}(x)=V(x)
, we have

Corollary 2.15
.

The stopping policy

\tau^{*}=\tau_{x^{-},x^{+}}=\inf\{t\geq 0:g(X_{t})=V(X_{t})\}

is optimal in (
1
).

In this section we characterise the
stopping set
, the set of points
S=\{x\in[0,1]:V(x)=g(x)\}
at which stopping is optimal, or equivalently the
continuation set

C=[0,1]\setminus S
. We establish the continuity of the value function
V
(Proposition
2.16
) and use the characterisation of
S
(Lemma
2.17
) to show in Corollary
2.18
that differentiability of
g
and
\mathcal{H}
are sufficient for the differentiability of
V
.

Proposition 2.16
.

V
is continuous.

Proof.

The upper semicontinuity of
V
was noted at the beginning of Section
2.1
. If
V(x)=g(x)
then the lower semicontinuity of
V
follows from the continuity of
g
and the fact that
V\geq g
. If
V(x)>g(x)
then since
g(x^{\pm})=V(x^{\pm})
(Lemma
2.11
) we have
x^{-}<x<x^{+}
. Since
V=h^{\circ}
on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
it follows that
V
is continuous on
(x^{-},x^{+})
.
∎

Lemma 2.17
.

Suppose that
g
and
\mathcal{H}
are differentiable. Then
x
lies in the stopping set if and only if at least one of the following holds:

(i)

There exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
with:

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}h(0)<\infty\\
h(x)=g(x)\\
h^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{ if }x>0\\
h\geq g\text{ on }[0,1],\end{cases}

(ii)

There exists
h\in\mathcal{H}
with:

(21)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}h(1)<\infty\\
h(x)=g(x)\\
h^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)\quad\text{ if }x<1\\
h\geq g\text{ on }[0,1].\end{cases}

Proof.

Condition
(i)
is sufficient to imply
x\in S
, as is condition
(ii)
, so we assume that
x\in S
and seek to establish the necessity of either condition
(i)
or
(ii)
.

We first construct
h_{*}\in\mathcal{H}
with
h_{*}\geq g
and
h_{*}(x)=g(x)
. Let
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\downarrow 0
with
\varepsilon_{0}<\frac{1}{2}
. Since
x\in S
implies that
w(x)=g(x)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
h_{n}\in\mathcal{H}
with
h_{n}\geq g
and
h_{n}(x)<g(x)+\varepsilon_{n}
. By the construction of
\mathcal{H}
(cf. Definition
2.1
) and monotonicity, either
h_{n}(0)\leq g(x)+\varepsilon_{n}
infinitely often or
h_{n}(1)\leq g(x)+\varepsilon_{n}
infinitely often. Without loss of generality, by the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem (and choosing an appropriate subsequence in
\mathbb{N}
if necessary) we may suppose that the sequence
(h_{n}(0))_{n}
converges, so that
\beta:=\lim_{n}h_{n}(0)\leq g(x)
. If
x=1
then, by ‘continuity in
(\beta,\gamma)
’, the functions
h^{0,1}_{h_{n}(0),h_{n}(1)}
converge pointwise to
h_{*}:=h^{0,1}_{\beta,g(1)}\in\mathcal{H}
with
h_{*}\geq g
and
h_{*}(1)=g(1)
.

Suppose therefore that
x<1
and, with the convention
\inf\emptyset=\infty
, set

z_{n}:=\inf\{z\in(x,1]:h_{n}(z)>\bar{g}+1\}\wedge 1.

By again choosing a suitable subsequence we may assume that
(z_{n})_{n}
converges to some
z^{\prime}\leq 1
, and
since
h_{n}(x)<\bar{g}+\frac{1}{2}
, and
\mathcal{H}
is uniformly equicontinuous, we then have
z^{\prime}>x
. Appealing again to uniform equicontinuity, there exists
z\in(x,z^{\prime})
such that
h_{n}(z)>\bar{g}+\frac{1}{2}
for all
n
.
Then by ‘continuity in
(\beta,\gamma)
’, the functions
h^{0,z}_{h_{n}(0),h_{n}(z)}
converge along some subsequence of
\mathbb{N}
to
h_{*}:=h^{0,z}_{\beta,\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in[\bar{g}+\frac{1}{2},\bar{g}+1]
with
h_{*}(x)=g(x)
. If
z<1
then
h_{*}
may be extended to an element of
\mathcal{H}
(cf. the proof of Lemma
2.12
). Assume that this extension has been performed.
Then
h_{*}
lies in
\mathcal{H}
and monotonicity gives
h_{*}\geq g
on
[0,1]
. If
x\in(0,1)
, this tangency implies that
h^{\prime}_{*}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)
, so that condition
(i)
of the lemma is satisfied. An analogous proof shows that if
\sup_{n}h_{n}(1)\leq g(x)
then condition
(ii)
of the lemma is satisfied.
∎

Corollary 2.18

(Smooth fit principle)
.

If
g
and
\mathcal{H}
are differentiable then
V
is differentiable on
(0,1)
. In particular
V^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)
for
x\in S\cap(0,1)
.

Proof.

For each
x\in S\cap(0,1)
, taking
h
as in Lemma
2.17
with
h(x)=g(x)
,
h^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)
and
h\geq g
we have
h(x)=g(x)=V(x)
, so Theorem
2.9
gives
g(x)\leq V(x)=w(x)\leq h(x)
and

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{g(x+\varepsilon)-g(x)}{\varepsilon}\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{V(x+\varepsilon)-V(x)}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{h(x+\varepsilon)-h(x)}{\varepsilon}=h^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x),

so that
V^{\prime}(x)=g^{\prime}(x)
. The differentiability of
V
on
C
follows directly from that of
h^{\circ}
in Lemma
2.12
.
∎

2.4
Solution algorithm

The following algorithm aims to construct the value function globally by returning the set of continuation regions - that is, the connected components of the continuation region
C
. It exploits the fact, due to the smooth fit principle (Corollary
2.18
) and the proof of Theorem
2.9
, that for each
x\in C
the value function is tangent to the gain function at both
x^{-}
and
x^{+}
. Then since
V=h^{x^{-},x^{+}}_{g(x^{-}),g(x^{+})}
on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
, this double tangency can be used to search for
x^{-}
and
x^{+}
. The computational cost is thus on the order of a search over
[0,1]^{2}
. In order to terminate in finite time with the correct result, we make the following additional assumptions:

Assumption 2.19
.

(i)

C
has finitely many connected components,

(ii)

\delta>0
is strictly smaller than the minimum length of the connected components of
C
,

(iii)

the gain function
g
and
\mathcal{H}
are differentiable,

(iv)

(strong monotonicity) if
X,Y\in b\mathcal{F}
with
X\leq Y
a.s. and
\mathbb{P}^{x}(X<Y)>0
then
\rho^{x}(X)<\rho^{x}(Y)
.

Recall from the proof of Theorem
2.9
that if
x\in C
then
there exist
0\leq x^{-}<x<x^{+}\leq 1
such that
V=h^{x^{-},x^{+}}_{g(x^{-}),g(x^{+})}
on
[x^{-},x^{+}]
. Denoting by
h^{\prime+}
,
h^{\prime-}
the right and left derivatives of
h
respectively, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 2.20
.

Setting

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle:=\big\{(y,z):0\leq y<x<z\leq 1,\;h=h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}\text{ satisfies }

\displaystyle\qquad y(h^{\prime+}(y)-g^{\prime}(y))=(1-z)(h^{\prime-}(z)-g^{\prime}(z))=0\big\},

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\sup\{h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x):(y,z)\in F(x)\},

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\arg\max\{h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x):(y,z)\in F(x)\},

(with the convention that
\sup\emptyset=-\infty
) we have:

(i)

x\in S
iff
G(x)\leq g(x)
,

(ii)

x\in C
iff
G(x)>g(x)
and then

\displaystyle x^{-}=\sup\{y:(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)\},\quad x^{+}=\inf\{z:(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)\},

\displaystyle V=h^{x^{-},x^{+}}_{g(x^{-}),g(x^{+})}\text{ on }[x^{-},x^{+}].

Proof.

For part
(i)
note that if
x\in C
then
G(x)=V(x)>g(x)
, giving the implication
G(x)\leq g(x)\implies x\in S
. Conversely, if
x\in S
then by optimality we have
G(x)\leq g(x)=V(x)
.

For part
(ii)
, by the proof of Theorem
2.9
and the smooth fit principle we have
(x^{-},x^{+})\in\tilde{F}(x)
, and it is straightforward to show that
x^{-}=\sup\{y:(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)\}\}
and
x^{+}=\inf\{z:(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)\}\}
, as follows (we show only the latter). Suppose that
(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)
. Then if
z<x^{+}
we have either
y>x^{-}
or
y\leq x^{-}
.

If
x^{-}<y<x<z<x^{+}
, by construction we have
\min\{V(y)-g(y),V(z)-g(z)\}>0
and monotonicity and the
\varrho
-martingale property give

h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x)<h^{y,z}_{V(y),V(z)}(x)=V(x)=h^{x^{-},x^{+}}_{g(x^{-}),g(x^{+})}(x),

contradicting the definition of
\tilde{F}(x)
.

Supposing alternatively that
y\leq x^{-}<x<z<x^{+}
, we again have
V(z)>g(z)=h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(z)
.
Now if
h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x^{-})\leq V(x^{-})
then monotonicity and part
(iii)
of Assumption
2.19
give
h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x)<V(x)
, again contradicting the definition of
\tilde{F}(x)
. Thus
h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x^{-})>V(x^{-})=g(x^{-})
, which contradicts the definition of
V
. We conclude that
z\geq x^{+}
.
∎

Lemma
2.20
is the basis for Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Under Assumptions
2.4
and
2.19
this algorithm solves the optimal stopping problem by returning the set of connected components of the continuation region
C
.

Evaluate

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle:=\big\{(y,z):0\leq y<z\leq 1,\;h=h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}\text{ satisfies }

\displaystyle\qquad y(h^{\prime+}(y)-g^{\prime}(y))=(1-z)(h^{\prime-}(z)-g^{\prime}(z))=0\big\},

Set
x=0
,
B=\emptyset

while

x\leq 1

do

\displaystyle:=\{(y,z)\in F:y<x<z\},

\displaystyle=\sup\{h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x):(y,z)\in F(x)\},

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\arg\max\{h^{y,z}_{g(y),g(z)}(x):(y,z)\in F(x)\},

\displaystyle x^{-}

\displaystyle=\sup\{y:(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)\},

\displaystyle x^{+}

\displaystyle=\inf\{z:(y,z)\in\tilde{F}(x)\},

if

G(x)>g(x)

then

Append the element
(x^{-},x^{+})
to
B

Set
x=x^{+}

end

if

Increase

by
\delta

end

while

return

B

Remark 2.21
.

Since the assumptions made in this section are strong, it is worth remarking on the output of Algorithm
1
when Assumptions
2.4
and
2.19
do not hold. The algorithm identifies the points
x^{+},x^{-}
defined in (
12
)–(
13
) using the principle of smooth fit. It may be that for certain initial points
x_{0}
, the principle of smooth fit applies in a sufficiently large neighbourhood of
x_{0}
. An example is provided in Section
3.2
below using the worst-case risk mapping. In this case it is not difficult to see that Algorithm
1
correctly returns the value
V(x_{0})
and the optimal stopping time
\tau^{*}
for initial points
x_{0}\in(\frac{1}{8},\frac{5}{8})
. However for initial points
x_{0}\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
, the lack of smooth fit at
x=\frac{3}{4}
prevents the component
(\frac{1}{8},\frac{5}{8})
of

from being identified.

Nevertheless the characterisation (
12
)–(
13
) of the points
x^{+},x^{-}
uses a weaker property than smooth fit, namely
continuous fit
between
g
and
V
. Since formula (
2
) correctly constructs
V
under the worst-case risk mapping, it seems possible to modify Algorithm
1
to use continuous fit instead to identify the points
x^{+},x^{-}
. While outside the scope of the present paper, such a modification would enable the algorithm to solve the example of Section
3.2
for all
x\in C
.

, Ch. III.7]
and Section
3.2
revisits the worst-case risk mapping of Section
1.1
, while Section
3.3
addresses the entropic risk mapping.

3.1
Linear case

When
\varrho
is the linear expectation
\mathbb{E}
:

\displaystyle\rho^{x}(Z)={\mathbb{E}}^{x}[Z],\qquad\rho_{t}^{x}(Z)={\mathbb{E}}^{x}[Z|\mathcal{F}_{t}],\qquad t\geq 0,

then
\mathcal{H}
consists of line segments:

(22)

h^{y,z}_{\beta,\gamma}(x)=\beta\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{y}<\tau_{z})+\gamma\mathbb{P}^{x}(\tau_{z}<\tau_{y})=\beta\frac{z-x}{z-y}+\gamma\frac{x-y}{z-y},\qquad x\in[y,z],

(see for example
[
32
, Th. 30]
). Thus
\mathcal{H}
is differentiable, and uniformly equicontinuous by Lemma
2.7
. Also,
\varrho
is continuous by the conditional bounded convergence theorem for
\mathbb{E}
and time consistent by its conditional tower property. Part
(i)
of the strong Markov property (Definition
1.3
) is trivially satisfied as Brownian motion is a strong Markov process under
\mathbb{E}
, and part
(ii)
is satisfied as
\rho^{x}(Z)={\mathbb{E}}^{x}[Z]=:\tilde{\rho}(\mathscr{L}(Z,\mathbb{P}^{x}))
. (Note that, by allowing
\beta\to\infty
, it can be seen that the unbounded set
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
of (
7
) is not uniformly equicontinuous; however
\mathcal{H}
is uniformly equicontinuous as it is bounded.)

Thus Assumption
2.4
holds, and Theorem
2.9
gives that if
g
is continuous then
V=w
. It is straightforward to check that the nonconcave majorant
w
of (
9
) is equal to the pointwise infimum over those
h\in\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
(cf. (
7
)) which dominate
g
, and we recover the classical geometric method for optimal stopping problems
[
16
, Ch. III.7]
.

3.2
Worst-case risk mapping

Although the worst-case risk mapping does not satisfy Assumption
2.4
, it was observed in Section
1.1
that formula (
2
) generates the correct solution and, as such, is a useful heuristic. This heuristic is novel to the best of our knowledge: for example, it differs from the use of the simpler set
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
(cf. (
7
)). Indeed it is easy to see that if
\mathcal{H}
is replaced by
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
in (
9
), instead of
w
we obtain the constant function
w^{\dagger}(x)\equiv\bar{g}
, and for all
x\notin\arg\sup_{y\in[0,1]}g(y)
we have

V(x)=w(x)=\min\{\sup_{y\in[0,x]}g(y),\sup_{y\in[x,1]}g(y)\}\neq w^{\dagger}(x)=\bar{g}.

Formula (
2
) also differs from the heuristic use of the principle of smooth fit (cf. Corollary
2.18
). This can be seen in Figure
2
, where the gain function is
g(x)=1+\sin(4\pi x)
and, as observed in Remark
2.21
, the use of smooth fit fails for initial points
x_{0}\in(\frac{3}{4},1)
.

3.3
Entropic case

The entropic risk mapping is given by (for each
Z\in b\mathcal{F}
):

\displaystyle\rho^{x}(Z)

\displaystyle=-\ln\mathbb{E}^{x}[e^{-Z}],

\displaystyle\rho_{t}^{x}(Z)

\displaystyle=-\ln\mathbb{E}^{x}[e^{-Z}|\mathcal{F}_{t}],\qquad t>0.

From (
22
), for
x\in[y,z]
we have
h_{\beta,\gamma}^{y,z}(x)=-\ln\left(e^{-\beta}\frac{z-x}{z-y}+e^{-\gamma}\frac{x-y}{z-y}\right)
, so
\mathcal{H}
is again differentiable, and uniformly equicontinuous by Lemma
2.7
. The continuity, strong Markov property and time consistency of
\varrho
follow from the corresponding properties for
\mathbb{E}
as in Section
3.1
, so Assumption
2.4
holds and Theorem
2.9
gives that if
g
is continuous then
V=w
.

In this case it is a priori equivalent to rescale the problem and instead maximise the linear expectation
\mathbb{E}^{x}[e^{-g(X_{\tau})}]
, for which the classical construction using
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
suffices. Interestingly, though, the direct construction of the present paper is qualitatively different, in the sense that the set
\mathcal{H}
must again be used rather than
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
. Indeed, fix
x\in(0,1)
and let
g
be any gain function with
g(0)=1
and
g(x)>2-\ln(1-x)
. We have
h_{0,\gamma}^{0,1}(x)\uparrow-\ln(1-x)
as
\gamma\uparrow\infty
. Then if
\mathcal{H}^{\dagger}
is used to construct
w^{\dagger}
(rather than using
\mathcal{H}
to construct
w
), by part
(iii)
of Lemma
2.6
(translation invariance) we would have
w^{\dagger}(0)\geq 2>1=w(0)=V(0)=g(0)
, so
w^{\dagger}
cannot be the value function.
