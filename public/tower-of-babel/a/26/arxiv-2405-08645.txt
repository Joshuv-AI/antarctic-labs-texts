Certifying Robustness of Graph Convolutional Networks for Node Perturbation with Polyhedra Abstract Interpretation
Boqi Chen, Kristóf Marussy, Oszkár Semeráth, Gunter Mussbacher, Dániel Varró

Abstract
Graph convolutional neural networks (GCNs) are powerful tools for learning graph-based knowledge representations from training data. However, they are vulnerable to small perturbations in the input graph, which makes them susceptible to input faults or adversarial attacks. This poses a significant problem for GCNs intended to be used in critical applications, which need to provide certifiably robust services even in the presence of adversarial perturbations. We propose an improved GCN robustness certification technique for node classification in the presence of node feature perturbations. We introduce a novel polyhedra-based abstract interpretation approach to tackle specific challenges of graph data and provide tight upper and lower bounds for the robustness of the GCN. Experiments show that our approach simultaneously improves the tightness of robustness bounds as well as the runtime performance of certification. Moreover, our method can be used during training to further improve the robustness of GCNs.



keywords
graph neural networks, robustness certification, graph representation learning, node classification

1
Introduction

Context.
Recent advances in graph neural networks, particularly graph convolutional neural networks (GCNs) have significantly expanded their deployment in knowledge-intensive applications
Yu et al. (2020)
;
Sun et al. (2020a)
;
Dettmers et al. (2018)
where structured semantic data is represented as knowledge graphs. GCNs leverage existing data (e.g., samples of nodes and edges) to infer further insights from the underlying knowledge base.

A popular graph inference task is
node classification
which aims to classify nodes within graph-based representations. For example, a GCN can be trained to identify predatory conferences within a publication database based on the attributes (e.g., dates and page length) and the connections of nodes (e.g., authorship, papers, and citations).

However, GCNs are susceptible to
adversarial examples

Sun et al. (2022)
;
Günnemann (2022)
. These are carefully selected small perturbations that can change a GCN’s output for a specific node. In the context of publication graph analysis, an author could manipulate the classification of their own paper by carefully choosing a publication date, leading the underlying GCN model to fail.

Consequently, ensuring
the robustness of a GCN
is important
Najafi and Yu (2021)
, especially in critical applications
Sun et al. (2020a)
. For that purpose, a GCN needs to be ensured to tolerate some (intentionally or accidentally) mislabelled attributes both in training sets or runtime inputs. For example, a GCN for detecting predatory conferences should be robust, so a few changed publication dates should not change its output.

Problem statement.

Certifying the robustness of a GCN

Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
aims to ensure that no adversarial examples exist (within a designated input range) for a GCN. Without such guarantees, small perturbations could mislead the GCN. In some cases, certification may even refute the GCN robustness by providing an appropriate counterexample.

Unfortunately, computing the exact robustness of even the simplest neural network poses a significant challenge
Sälzer and Lange (2021)
. As such, existing work largely focuses on estimating the lower or upper limits of GCN robustness
Liu et al. (2020)
;
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
. Yet, these methods can be imprecise or have poor runtime performance, limiting their practical use.

Abstract interpretation
Cousot and Cousot (1977)
is a mathematical framework originally developed for program analysis. It has recently been adapted to the certification of regular neural networks with non-relational continuous inputs
Singh et al. (2019)
;
Urban et al. (2020)
, leading to enhanced precision and runtime performance in the certification tasks. This technique has also been applied to certify the robustness of GCNs
Liu et al. (2020)
. However, their certification approach solely relies only on the interval abstract domain, which has been shown to be less precise compared to other, more expressive abstract domains
Singh et al. (2018)
. Moreover, it fails to capture the relations between node features across different nodes, a critical aspect of the message-passing mechanism in GCNs.

Objectives.

The main objective of this paper is to improve robustness certification for graph convolutional neural networks.
For this purpose, we either provide a certificate that formally guarantees the robustness of the GCN or derive a counterexample that demonstrates the GCN’s vulnerability within the desired perturbation limit.

Contributions.

We present a novel framework based on polyhedra abstract interpretation, a more expressive abstract domain, and tailored to certify the robustness of GCN classifiers specified for node classification tasks on a graph. The framework can leverage GPU accelerations to efficiently provide a precise robustness certificate or a potential adversarial example. Moreover, the certification operations are reversible and differentiable, facilitating robust training to improve the robustness of the GCNs. In particular:

•

We propose a novel certification approach by defining a polyhedra abstract interpretation for graphs to provide an
over-approximation
for the output of a GCN within a given feature perturbation limit (space).

•

With the certification approach, we propose a
pair of
novel certifiers that can provide
both lower and upper bound
for GCN robustness by estimating the impact of a node feature on the output. Besides node-level robustness, we also demonstrate how our approach can derive the
collective robustness
of the entire graph.

•

We propose the
uncertainty region as a new metric
to evaluate the tightness of a pair of GCN certifiers in a
holistic
way using a perturbation space range.

•

We carry out
experimental evaluation
on three popular node classification datasets to compare our approach with existing certification approaches on certification tightness, runtime performance, and robust training.

Added value.

Though abstract interpretation is actively used for certifying other neural networks, the unique attributes of graph data in GCNs pose distinct challenges. These challenges arise from the discrete input space and iterative aggregation of data across neighboring nodes, preventing the direct adaptation of existing techniques to GCNs.

Compared to current GCN certification approaches, our approach distinctly improves both the tightness and runtime efficiency of the certification within a given node feature perturbation limit.
Our polyhedra abstract interpretation approach determines both upper and lower robustness bounds for GCN and uses reversible and differentiable matrix operations to improve scalability. This leads to faster certification for given GCN classifiers and enables GCN certification for larger graphs. Furthermore, it can also be used for robust training to improve the robustness of GCNs.

Background

2.1
Graph neural networks

Node classification
assigns a label (from a set
\mathcal{C}{}
of predefined labels) to each node in a graph.
Let
\mathbf{G}{}=(A,X)
be an attributed graph with
n{}
nodes defined by an adjacency matrix
A\in\mathbb{\mathbb{B}{}}^{(n{},n){}}
(
\mathbb{\mathbb{B}{}}=\{0,1\}
) and a feature matrix with
m
features (attributes) for each node
X\in\mathbb{\mathbb{R}{}}^{(n,m)}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that nodes are ordered from 1 to n and use the index to represent a node. Given the correct labels for the first
k
nodes (
c_{i}\in\mathcal{C}{},i\leq k
) as ground truth,

node classification
aims to assign appropriate labels to the remaining nodes
k+1,\dots,n
in the graph
(
c_{j}\in\mathcal{C}{},k<j\leq n
).

Graph neural networks (GNN)

support learning on graphs, which have strong input data dependency caused by edges. This paper focuses on the semi-supervised node classification problem using graph convolutional neural networks (GCNs)
Kipf and Welling (2016)
.

\chadded
Following prior work
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
;
Liu et al. (2020)
;
Schuchardt et al. (2021)
, this paper focuses on homogeneous graphs, where there is only a single type of nodes and a single type of edges.

Given a graph
\mathbf{G}=(A,X)
,
GCNs operate on a
normalized adjacency matrix

\tilde{A}=D^{-\frac{1}{2}}(A+I_{n})D^{-\frac{1}{2}}
where
I_{n}
is an identity matrix of size
n
and
D=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(\sum_{j}{(A+I_{n})_{:,j}}\bigr)
is the diagonal degree matrix for each node on the graph. A
GCN layer
consists of three phases. (1) The graph convolution
\mathrm{GC}_{A}(H):=\tilde{A}H
to aggregate features of neighboring nodes. (2) The linear layer
\mathrm{Lin}_{W_{l},b_{l}}(H):=HW_{l}+b_{l}
where
W_{l}
and
b_{l}
are the layer-specific trainable weight matrix and bias vector, respectively. (3) Finally
\mathrm{ReLU}(H):=max(0,H)
is the
non-linear activation function
. Given
\tilde{A}
, a set of labels
\mathcal{C}
, and the input feature matrix
H_{l}
, a GCN with
z
layers can be defined recursively as
H_{l+1}=\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathrm{Lin}_{W_{l},b_{l}}(\mathrm{GC}_{A}(H_{l}))),0\leq l<z
such that
H_{0}=X
is the input matrix and
H_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n,|\mathcal{C}|)}
is the output score matrix. We refer to the output of a GCN classifier
\mathit{cl}
as
H_{z}=\mathit{cl}(A,X)
. The final label for each node is obtained by selecting the label with the highest score per node:
argmax(H_{z})
.

While our experiments focus on binary node features
(X{}\in\mathbb{B}{}^{(n{},m{})})
with ReLU activations, the approach naturally extends to continuous node features and other non-linear activations through linear relaxation
Singh et al. (2019)
.

2.2
Robustness of GNNs

Perturbation model:

We investigate the robustness of a classifier by making assumptions about potential perturbations in the graph. Such perturbations may be caused by either uncertain measurements or adversarial changes of an attacker.
Following previous work
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
;
Liu et al. (2020)
, we define node feature perturbations as flips of some (binary) node features limited by two types of change constraints.
Local limit

{p_{l}}
constrains the total number of feature changes for a node
i
, and
global limit

{p_{g}}
states the maximum number of feature changes for all nodes. Then, the
perturbation space
can be defined as all feature matrices
X{}^{\prime}
within the two limits from the original feature matrix
X
:
\mathcal{P}^{{p_{l}},{p_{g}}}_{X{}}=\{X{}^{\prime}\mid(\forall_{i}\sum_{j}(|X{}^{\prime}-X{}|)_{i,j}\leq{p_{l}})\land\sum_{i,j}(|X{}^{\prime}-X{}|)_{i,j}\leq{p_{g}}\}
. When the context is clear, we omit
{p_{l}},{p_{g}}
in the notation for simplicity and use
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
.

Attack model:

Given a perturbation budget, this paper is centered around an attack model where the main objective of the perturbation is to modify the classification outcomes of selected target nodes. Let
R_{\mathit{cl}}(\mathbf{G},X{}^{\prime},i)
be an indicator function on whether the prediction of a node is changed for some feature matrix
X{}^{\prime}
compared to the original feature matrix
X{}
. It gives
1
if the prediction remains consistent for the perturbed feature matrix
X{}^{\prime}
and
0
otherwise.
\chadded
Formally, let
c
be the original label prediction for the feature matrix
X
,
R_{\mathit{cl}}(\mathbf{G},X{}^{\prime},i)
is defined as:

\begin{aligned} R_{\mathit{cl}}(\mathbf{G},X{}^{\prime},i):=&\;argmax(\mathit{cl}(A{},X{})_{i}{})=argmax(\mathit{cl}(A{},X{}^{\prime})_{i}{})\\
\;\equiv&\forall c^{\prime}\neq c,\bigl[\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}\bigr]>0\text{,}\\
\end{aligned}

(1)

where
\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}:=\mathit{cl}{}(A{},X{}^{\prime})_{i{},c{}}-\mathit{cl}{}(A{},X{}^{\prime})_{i{},{c{}^{\prime}}}
and
\mathit{cl}{}(\cdot,\cdot)_{i{},c{}}
denotes the output score of label
c{}
at node
i{}
. The goal of perturbation is to identify an adversarial matrix
X{}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
where the prediction of the node is changed:
R_{\mathit{cl}}(\mathbf{G},X{}^{\prime},i)=0
.

This attack model can be further categorized into two distinct attack types. The
single-node
attack model assumes the attacker can individually perturb the graph for each target node, i.e.,
X{}^{\prime}
may vary for each node. In this scenario, the perturbation space is uniquely defined for each target node, ensuring that perturbations to one target node do not affect another target node. However, in many practical applications, perturbations for individual nodes may not be independent, given that all nodes are frequently considered collectively as a graph
Schuchardt et al. (2021)
. The
collective
attack model revises this perspective by dropping the assumption on perturbation independence. It operates under the assumption that the graph can only be perturbed
once
for all target nodes, thus the result
X{}^{\prime}
stays the same for all nodes.

Single-node robustness:

In single-node robustness, one assumes each node has a perturbation space independent of other nodes. The output of a classifier
\mathit{cl}
for node
i
is
robust
against a perturbation space
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
if no perturbed feature matrix
X{}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
can change classification of
i{}
by
\mathit{cl}{}
. We call such node a
robust node
for
\mathit{cl}
.

Node-level robustness

\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})\in\mathbb{B}{}
can be defined as:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{}):=&\;\forall X{}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{X{}}\colon R_{\mathit{cl}}(\mathbf{G},X{}^{\prime},i)>0\;\equiv\bigl[min_{X{}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{X{}},c{}^{\prime}\neq c{}}\;\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}\bigr]>0\text{,}\\
\end{aligned}

(2)

For a robust node, the output scores for all other labels are always lower than the score of the original GCN label for any feature changes inside the perturbation space.
The (graph-level)
robustness

\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}\in[0,1]
of a classifier
\mathit{cl}
on a graph
\mathbf{G}{}
with
n
nodes against a perturbation space
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
is defined as the proportion of robust nodes:

\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}=\frac{\sum_{i{}=1}^{n}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i)}}{n{}}

(3)

A perturbed matrix
X{}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
is a
counterexample
(or adversarial example) for the robustness of a GCN classifier
\mathit{cl}{}
on node
i
if the classification of node
i{}
in
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}=(A,X{}^{\prime})
differs from the classification of the same node in
\mathbf{G}=(A,X{})
:
argmax(\mathit{cl}(A{},X{})_{i}{})\neq argmax(\mathit{cl}{}(A{},X{}^{\prime})_{i}{})
.

Collective robustness:

In collective robustness, a single shared perturbation space is used for all nodes on the graph. Consequently, only
graph-level
robustness is defined for this attack model. This graph-level robustness is categorized by the minimum ratio of nodes whose label stays unchanged by any feature matrix within the perturbation space:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}_{\text{collect}}:=min_{X{}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{X{}^{\prime}}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{R_{\mathit{cl}}(\mathbf{G},X{}^{\prime},i)}}{n}\\
\end{aligned}

(4)

Throughout this paper, we dominantly focus on the
single-node
robustness certification, unless otherwise specified. Nevertheless, our method on single-node robustness certification can be used to determine the
collective robustness
through a subsequent post-processing approach, as shown in
Section 3.6
.

2.3
Robustness certification

Robustness certification aims to provide formal proof that a GCN is not sensitive to small perturbations of the input graph.
Given a GCN classifier
\mathit{cl}
with label set
\mathcal{C}{}
for graph
\mathbf{G}{}=(A{},X{})
with
n{}
nodes, and a perturbation space
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
,
GCN robustness certification
provides a
certifier

\mathscr{V}{}:(A{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}},\mathit{cl}{})\to\mathbb{R}{}^{n}{}
. The output of the certifier is a
judgment
for each node that estimates the minimum difference (in
Equation 1
) between the original label and other labels for all inputs within the perturbation space.
We define the
node-level certified
robustness as

\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})=\mathscr{V}(A{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}},\mathit{cl}{})_{i}{}>0
. Similarly, the
graph-level certified robustness
of a
\mathit{cl}
for
\mathbf{G}{}
using
\mathscr{V}{}
is the ratio of certified nodes defined as:

\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}=\frac{\sum_{i{}=1}^{n}{\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i)}}{n{}}

(5)

However, it is intractable to enumerate all possible perturbations
X{}^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
to analytically calculate
exact
robustness
\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})
for each node
i

Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
.
Hence, the adoption of some approximation methods is necessary. Techniques such as randomized smoothing are designed to estimate this value, providing a probabilistic approximation on a
smoothed
version of the GCN
Wang et al. (2021a)
. Nevertheless, in practice, it is usually desirable to estimate the robustness of the
original
GCN with some
absolute
attributes.
Consequently, this paper aims to create a certifier
\mathscr{V}{}
that provides an exact (tight) lower and upper bound of single-node robustness. Since collective certification is defined similarly for collective robustness
Schuchardt et al. (2021)
, we omit the details for conciseness. We define the concept of sound and complete certifiers similarly to others
Liu et al. (2021)
.

Definition 1

(Sound and complete certifiers)
.

A certifier
\mathscr{V}
is
sound
if whenever it flags a node
i
as a robust node, the node will indeed
be robust against the perturbation space
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
:
\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})\implies\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})
. A certifier
\mathscr{V}
is
complete
if all robust nodes are flagged for a given perturbation space
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
:
\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})\implies\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}({\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}},i{})
.

A sound certifier provides a lower bound for graph-level robustness, i.e.,
\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}\leq\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}
.
A complete certifier gives an upper bound for robustness, i.e.,
\mathrm{R}^{\mathscr{V}{}}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}\geq\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}
.
A
sound and complete certifier
judges each node correctly, i.e., the lower and upper bounds are the same. A certification approach may provide a pair of certifiers: a sound certifier for a lower bound
\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}_{\leq}{}
and a complete certifier for an upper bound
\mathrm{R}_{\mathit{cl}{}}(\mathbf{G}{},\mathcal{P}_{X{}}){}_{\geq}{}
.

Given a GCN node classifier
\mathit{cl}
that assigns a label to a graph node, an input graph
\mathbf{G}{}
and perturbation limit
({p_{l}},{p_{g}})

\chadded

3.1
AbsInt for GCN certification

The core technique behind our approach is abstract interpretation (AbsInt)
Cousot and Cousot (1977)
, which is a mathematical framework initially designed to reason about the behavior of computer programs. Recently, it has also been adapted to neural network certification of non-relational continuous inputs
Urban et al. (2020)
;
Singh et al. (2019)
. Here, we first define a
general abstract interpretation framework for certifying GCN node classifiers
.

Abstract interpretation relies on an
abstract domain

\mathbb{A}

Cousot and Cousot (1977)
, which can be any numerical or relational domain that provides a sound abstraction of elements in the
concrete domain

\mathbb{C}
. Moreover, AbsInt defines sound abstractions of operations in the concrete domain.

For
AbsInt of GCN certification
, the concrete domain captures the perturbations space
\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
and the set of possible latent features
H_{l}
produced by the layers in the GCN over the perturbations space. Then AbsInt aims to produce an approximation of the concrete domain (a set of feature matrices) with an abstract domain by sound abstraction of GCN operations (e.g., graph convolution, ReLU).

3.1.1
Concrete domain and operations

Formally, the concrete domain of a GCN on a graph with
n
nodes can be defined as sets of binary and real-valued matrices
\mathbb{C}{}=\{\wp(\mathbb{B}^{(n,m_{0})})\}\cup\{\wp(\mathbb{R}^{(n,m_{l})})\mid 0<l\leq z\}
where

z
is the number of layers in the GCN,
m_{l}
is the number of node features at layer
l
and

\wp(\mathcal{S})
represents the power set of set
\mathcal{S}
.
For any operation
\mathrm{T}
defined over such matrices, the corresponding
set valued
operation
\overline{\mathrm{T}}\colon\mathbb{C}{}\to\mathbb{C}{}
computes all possible resulting matrices from the input set
\mathcal{S}
:
\overline{\mathrm{T}}(\mathcal{S})=\{\mathrm{T}(H)\mid H\in\mathcal{S}\}
.
The set of concrete operations
\mathcal{T}{}
for a GCN includes
\overline{\mathrm{GC}_{A}}
for
graph convolution

\mathrm{GC}_{A}
,
\overline{\mathrm{ReLU}}
for the
non-linear

\mathrm{ReLU}
, and
\overline{\mathrm{Lin}_{W_{l},b_{l}}}
for the
fully connected
operation
\mathrm{Lin}_{W_{l},b_{l}}
.

3.1.2
Abstract interpretation (domain and operations)

The
abstract interpretation
over a GCN is a tuple
(\mathbb{A}{},\alpha{},\gamma{},\mathcal{T}{}^{\#})
with
abstraction function

\alpha\colon\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{A}
,
concretization function

\gamma\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{C}
,
and a set of
abstract operations

\mathcal{T}{}^{\#}
.
Functions
\alpha
and
\gamma
transform elements between the concrete domain and the abstract domain. Since
\gamma
and

\alpha
mutually determine each other
Cousot and Cousot (1977)
, we define
input abstraction
and then use
\gamma
in the rest of the paper.
For each concrete operation
\overline{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathcal{T}
, we use
\mathrm{T}^{\#}
to denote the corresponding abstract operation that
over-approximates

\overline{\mathrm{T}}
(i.e.,
\overline{\mathrm{T}}(\gamma(\mathbf{a}))\subseteq\gamma(\mathrm{T}^{\#}(\mathbf{a}))
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{A}
).
For any GCN
\mathit{cl}{}
, we use
\overline{\mathit{cl}{}}
to represent the GCN with concrete operations from
\mathcal{T}{}
, and we use
\mathit{cl}{}^{\#}
to represent the abstract GCN with the abstract operations from
\mathcal{T}{}^{\#}
.

Theorem 1

(Over-approximation of AbsInt)
.

Any abstract interpretation
(\mathbb{A}{},\gamma{},\mathcal{T}{}^{\#})
defined for GCN as above over-approximates the behavior of the GCN classifier, i.e.,
\overline{\mathit{cl}}(A,\mathcal{P}_{X})=\{\mathit{cl}(A,X^{\prime})\mid X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{X}\}\subseteq\gamma(\mathit{cl}^{\#}(\mathbf{a}))
for all abstract elements
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{A}
with
\mathcal{P}_{X}\subseteq\gamma(\mathbf{a})
.

\chadded

The proof for
Theorem 1
is presented in Appendix
A.2

One instantiation of the general AbsInt framework for GCN certification is interval AbsInt
Liu et al. (2020)
, which
(1) bounds each latent feature with an interval, and (2) uses interval arithmetic as abstract operations.
However, interval AbsInt can be imprecise as it does not capture the relations between node features.
A novel contribution of our paper is to define polyhedra-based AbsInt for GCN certification (in
Section 3.2
-
3.3
), which incorporates such relations.

3.2
Efficient polyhedra AbsInt

3.2.1
Challenges

The
polyhedra abstract domain

Cousot and Halbwachs (1978)
bounds variables by linear inequalities to offer a precise domain to capture relations between node features. Specifically, we tackle the following challenges when designing the approach:

Scalability:
The
number of inequalities may grow exponentially with each layer of the GCN
. We mitigate this issue similarly to other neural networks
Singh et al. (2019)
by keeping only one upper-bound inequality and one lower-bound inequality to improve certification efficiency.

Tightness:

While existing work uses linear bounds to approximate non-linear layers (like
\mathrm{ReLU}
) without increasing the number of variables, we
provide an analytical solution for the tightest approximation
.

Discrete data:
While our approach is inspired by abstract interpretation-based certification methods for neural networks on non-relational and continuous data,
a challenging and novel aspect of our work is to handle an interdependent and discrete input space
. Due to the relational nature of graphs, the GCN’s output of one node depends on other nodes. However, naively incorporating all neighboring nodes in the certification will significantly increase the runtime due to unnecessary computations.

Efficiency:
Most abstract interpretation methods are performed on CPUs, which makes them hard to scale up for GCNs of large real-world graphs. To improve the scalability, we combine the polyhedra and interval AbsInt approach to balance speed and precision. At the same time,
our abstract GCN operators in the polyhedra domain are defined with reversible and differentiable matrix operations
, enabling efficient GPU-accelerated implementation when used on large-scale graphs. Since our abstract operators are reversible, we design a backward process such that a neighbor’s feature is only added to the computation when needed. Moreover, our abstract operators are defined for each node independently while taking the neighbors into consideration. This enables efficient subgraph batching based on neighbors during the certification process, which further improves the certification speed. Finally, the same abstraction can also be used to provide counterexample candidates.

3.2.2
Overview

Building on the elements outlined for an abstract interpretation approach (see
Section 3.1
), our method begins with an
abstract domain
, which involves explaining how polyhedra can be used to overapproximate a set of features. Subsequently, we describe the
input abstraction
process, which transforms a set of input features into their overapproximation within the abstract domain. Similarly, the
concretization
process describes how the abstract element can be mapped into a set of elements in the concrete domain. The next stage includes a series of
abstract operations
designed to overapproximate the neural network’s layers. Specifically, for GCNs, these operations include the fully connected layer (
\mathrm{Lin}{}
), graph convolution (
\mathrm{GC}_{A}{}
), and the ReLU activation function (
ReLU
).

3.2.3
Polyhedra AbsInt

Let us assign a symbolic variable
\texttt{x}_{i{},j{}}
to an input feature
X_{i,j}
of node
i

\mathcal{X}
to be the set of all symbolic variables from the graph and
\mathcal{V}_{i}

For a concrete domain of feature vectors

Since the neighbors of a node contribute to a prediction by the
\mathrm{GC}_{A}{}
operator, the variables

used for feature bounds of node
i{}
at layer

can include both features of node
i{}
and its

Abstract domain:
We define the abstract domain for graph features as a set of tuples

Q^{i{}}_{\geq},d^{i{}}_{\geq})

and

\mathcal{V}_{i}

and

are vectors of constants in coefficients
\mathbf{q}_{i}

H_{i,j}
of each node

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{\texttt{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}{}_{i}}\!\bigl((Q^{i{}}_{\leq})_{j,k}\cdot\texttt{x}_{k}\bigr)+(d^{i{}}_{\leq})_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\texttt{h}_{i,j}\text{,}

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{\texttt{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{V}{}_{i}}\!\bigl((Q^{i{}}_{\geq})_{j,k}\cdot\texttt{x}_{k}\bigr)+(d^{i{}}_{\geq})_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\texttt{h}_{i,j}\text{,}

An abstract element

uniformly refers to a set of linear inequalities and the tuples of matrices that define them.

Input abstraction:

In the input layer, each latent feature variable
\texttt{h}_{i,j}
corresponds to a node feature variable
\texttt{x}_{i,j}
.
To create an initial abstraction for node features
X

\texttt{x}_{i,j}
is used as its own lower and upper bound, thus the retained inequalities are
1\cdot\texttt{x}_{i,j}+0\leq\texttt{x}_{i,j}
and
1\cdot\texttt{x}_{i,j}+0\geq\texttt{x}_{i,j}

This corresponds to the abstract element
\mathbf{a}_{0}=\bigl((I^{m{}_{0}},\mathbf{0}^{m{}_{0}},I^{m{}_{0}},\mathbf{0}^{m{}_{0}}),\{\texttt{x}_{i{},j{}}|j<m_{0}\}\bigr)_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{A}

\chadded
m_{0}
is the number of input features,

is an
m_{0}\times m_{0}

\mathbf{0}^{m{}_{0}}
is a vector of zeros.

Concretization:

To compute concretization
\gamma(\mathbf{a})

\mathcal{P}_{X{}}
of node features
\mathcal{X}
and the linear inequalities retained in
\mathbf{a}
. Formally, we have
\gamma(\mathbf{a})=\{H\in\mathbb{R}^{(n,m)}\mid H\vDash\mathbf{a}\cup\mathcal{P}_{X}^{{p_{l}},{p_{g}}}\}
to obtain a set of matrices that simultaneously satisfy (
\vDash
) inequalities in
\mathbf{a}
as well as the local and global limits
{p_{l}},{p_{g}}
.

Abstract operations:

We define three abstract operations to capture the relations between features from layer
l{}
and the input features.
\mathrm{Lin}_{W{},b}^{\#_{P}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{A}{}^{\#_{P}}
are linear operations, hence such relations are captured exactly using the polyhedra domain.
\mathrm{ReLU}{}^{\#_{P}}
is a non-linear function hence approximations are required.
For illustration, we separate the abstraction and transformation for the coefficients (
\mathbf{q}_{i}
) and set of variables (
\mathcal{V}_{i}
) and only the node-level abstract operations are presented in detail in

\mathbf{a}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{i},\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}

3.3
Abstract operations

Fully connected:

\mathrm{Lin}_{W{},b}^{\#_{P}}
keeps all linear relations of input node features and output node features.
Intuitively, the lower bound of its output can be calculated by multiplying the positive weights by the input lower bound and the negative weights by the input upper bound. The upper bound can be calculated similarly. For an input element of node
i{}
with coefficients
\mathbf{q}_{i}=(Q^{i{}}_{\leq},d^{i{}}_{\leq},Q^{i{}}_{\geq},d^{i{}}_{\geq})
and variables
\mathcal{V}{}_{i}{}
, the
\mathrm{Lin}_{W{},b}^{\#_{P}}
operation can be formally defined as:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lin}_{W{},b}^{\#_{P}}(Q^{i{}}_{\leq},d^{i{}}_{\leq},Q^{i{}}_{\geq},d^{i{}}_{\geq})=(Q^{\prime i{}}_{\leq},d^{\prime i{}}_{\leq},Q^{\prime i{}}_{\geq},d^{\prime i{}}_{\geq})\text{, where}

\displaystyle Q^{\prime i{}}_{\leq}=max(W{}^{T},0)\cdot Q^{i{}}_{\leq}+min(W{}^{T},0)\cdot Q^{i{}}_{\geq},

\displaystyle Q^{\prime i{}}_{\geq}=max(W{}^{T},0)\cdot Q^{i{}}_{\geq}+min(W{}^{T},0)\cdot Q^{i{}}_{\leq},

\displaystyle d^{\prime i{}}_{\leq}=max(W{}^{T},0)\cdot d^{i{}}_{\leq}+min(W{}^{T},0)\cdot d^{i{}}_{\geq}+b,

\displaystyle d^{\prime i{}}_{\geq}=max(W{}^{T},0)\cdot d^{i{}}_{\geq}+min(W{}^{T},0)\cdot d^{i{}}_{\leq}+b,

\chadded

W^{T}
represents the transpose of the weight matrix
W
.
max(W{}^{T},0)
and
min(W{}^{T},0)
keep positive and negative values in matrix
W{}^{T}
, respectively. Notice that this operation does not introduce any new variables. Thus, the set of node feature variables stays unchanged
\mathcal{V}{}^{\prime}_{i}{}=\mathcal{V}{}_{i}{}
.

Graph convolution:

The graph convolution
\mathrm{GC}_{A}{}^{\#_{P}}
operation collects variables from node
i{}
and its neighbors and combines them into a matrix. Since all entries in the normalized adjacency matrix
\tilde{A}
are non-negative, we can obtain the upper bound and lower bound by calculating a normalized sum using bounds from neighbors. Since the summation step may introduce new variables from neighbors, we can incorporate the new variables using matrix concatenation. Let
\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{1}
be the immediate neighbors of node
i{}
(including itself);
we define
\mathrm{GC}_{A}{}^{\#_{P}}
for node
i{}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{GC}_{A}{}^{\#_{P}}(Q^{i{}}_{\leq},d^{i{}}_{\leq},Q^{i{}}_{\geq},d^{i{}}_{\geq})=(Q^{\prime i{}}_{\leq},d^{\prime i{}}_{\leq},Q^{\prime i{}}_{\geq},d^{\prime i{}}_{\geq})\text{, where}

\displaystyle Q^{\prime i{}}_{\leq}=[\tilde{A}_{i{},k}\cdot Q^{k}_{\leq}]_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{1}}\hskip 9.24994ptQ^{\prime i{}}_{\geq}=[\tilde{A}_{i{},k}\cdot Q^{k}_{\geq}]_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{1}}

\displaystyle d^{\prime i{}}_{\leq}=\textstyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{1}}(\tilde{A}_{i{},k}\cdot d^{k}_{\leq})\hskip 9.24994ptd^{\prime i{}}_{\geq}=\textstyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{1}}{(\tilde{A}_{i{},k}\cdot d^{k}_{\geq})}

where
[H_{s}]_{s\in\mathcal{S}}
concatenates all matrices
H_{s}
defined by
s\in\mathcal{S}
horizontally.
In this operation, the set of variables for the new element is the union of all variables from the neighbors:
\mathcal{V}{}^{\prime}_{i}{}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{1}}\mathcal{V}{}_{k}
. Note that there may be overlapping between the set of variables. In that case, one sums the corresponding columns of the coefficient matrix, and the number of new variables will be reduced. We ignore such cases for conciseness.

As the

one
upper bound and lower bound, respectively. Such relaxation of the

To get the numerical bounds efficiently for each variable, we use the interval bounds

and

of the variable calculated by

Liu et al. (2020)

The polyhedron bounding of

(1) If

\mathrm{ReLU}{}

3.4
Robustness certification for GCNs

Certifying node robustness requires to compute the difference
\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
between the scores
\mathit{cl}(A,X)_{i,c}
and
\mathit{cl}(A,X)_{i,c^{\prime}}
of the original label
c
and any other labels
c^{\prime}
for the node
i
being certified within the perturbation space (see
Equation 1
). To (1) approximate such difference, we use polyhedra AbsInt for the abstract domain to (2) derive the lower bound for
\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}

3.4.1
Robustness certification

Step 1:

To get bounds for
\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
in the abstract domain, we apply the
fully connected
operation
\mathrm{Lin}_{\Delta^{c,c^{\prime}},0}^{\#_{P}}
to the output of the classifier
\mathit{cl}
. Here
\Delta^{c,c^{\prime}}
is a
1\times|\mathcal{C}|
matrix where column
c
is set to 1, column
c^{\prime}
is set to
-1
, and all other entries are set to
0
. This corresponds to subtracting the score
\mathit{cl}(A,X)_{i,c^{\prime}}
from the score
\mathit{cl}(A,X)_{i,c}
for each node of the graph.

Step 2:

Certifying node level robustness requires to find the minimum value of
\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
allowed by the abstraction.
Formally, given an abstract element with coefficients
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{i,c^{\prime}}=\mathrm{Lin}_{\Delta^{c,c^{\prime}},0}^{\#_{P}}(\mathbf{q}_{i})
(for the original label
c
and some other label
c^{\prime}\neq c
) and the set of variables
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{i}
, we
define a
minimization problem
:

\min_{\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{i}}\delta_{i,c,c^{\prime}}\textit{ subject to }\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{i,c^{\prime}}\cup\mathcal{P}_{X}^{{p_{l}},{p_{g}}}

(6)

Since inequalities in
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{i,c^{\prime}}
are linear, the solution
\delta^{*}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
of the minimization problem can be calculated by greedily flipping the feature variables with maximum negative changes as follows.

i)

For a
l
-layered GCN, let
\mathcal{V}{}^{\prime}_{i}{}
denote the variables representing features from
l{}
-hop neighbors
\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{l{}}
for node
i{}
:
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{i}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{l{}}}\{\texttt{x}_{k,j}|j<m_{0}\}
.
Let
Q^{*i}_{\leq}
be the reshaped matrix of

Q^{\prime i}_{\leq}
from
\mathbf{q}^{\prime}_{i,c^{\prime}}
such that
(Q^{*i}_{\leq})_{k,j}
corresponds to the coefficient of variable
\texttt{x}_{k,j}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{i}
in the lower bound for each feature
j
of node
k
.

ii)

Using the input feature matrix
X
, the lower bound of score difference between label
c
and
c^{\prime}
can be calculated as
\delta^{X}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}=\sum_{\texttt{x}_{k,j}\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{i}}\bigl((Q^{*i}_{\leq})_{k,j}\cdot X_{k,j}\bigr)+d^{i}_{\leq}
.

iii)

Starting from
\delta^{X}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
, the minimum
\delta^{*}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
can be found by flipping the values of feature variable
\texttt{x}_{k,j}
that reduce
\delta^{X}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}
the most within a local and global perturbation limit.

iv)

Let perturbation matrix

be:
P_{k,j}=1
if

and
P_{k,j}=-1
if
X_{k,j}=1
.
Then
\theta_{k,j}=(Q^{*i}_{\leq})_{k,j}\cdot P_{k,j}
represents the change of

if the value of variable
\texttt{x}_{k,j}

v)

Given a local perturbation limit
{p_{l}}

{p_{l}}

k\in\mathcal{N}^{i{}}_{l{}}
.
Formally, these negative changes can be calculated as:
\hat{\theta}=\bigl[min\bigl(\mathrm{MinK}_{{p_{l}}}([\theta_{k,j}]_{\texttt{x}_{k,j}\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}),0\bigr)\bigr]_{k\in\mathcal{N}^{i}_{l}}
where
\mathrm{MinK_{k}}
gets the minimum

vi)

Given a global perturbation limit
{p_{g}}
, we also identify the
{p_{g}}

\hat{\delta}

{p_{g}}
changes is the amount to deduct from

to derive the solution:
\delta^{*}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}=\sum{\mathrm{MinK}_{{p_{g}}}(\hat{\theta})+\delta^{X}_{i,c,c^{\prime}}}.

Step 3:
If the lower bound of the minimum difference between the original label and other labels is larger than zero, then we can certify that node.
This
lower bound
of the

between the original label

and any other label

) and the upper bound is
1
(no perturbation). Similarly, we obtain
u_{2,1}\in[1,2]
.

\chadded

The remaining calculations are carried out using linear arithmetic. Since the lower bounds of
u_{2,0}
and
u_{2,1}
are both positive, the
\mathrm{ReLU}{}
function acts as the identity function, yielding
u_{3,0}\in[0.5,1]
and
u_{3,1}\in[1,2]
. The output bounds are then given by

\displaystyle o_{0}=0\times u_{3,0}+1\times u_{3,1}=0\times[0.5,1]+1\times[1,2]=[1,2],

\displaystyle o_{1}=1\times u_{3,0}+1\times u_{3,1}=1\times[0.5,1]+1\times[1,2]=[1.5,3],

\displaystyle o_{1}-o_{0}=[1.5,3]-[1,2]=[-0.5,2].

Since the lower bound of the difference between two labels is
-0.5<0
, the robustness of node
u
cannot be certified.

Polyhedra AbsInt.

Now, we perform the same certification using the polyhedra AbsInt proposed in this paper.
From
Section 3.3
, one can express each latent feature as linear inequalities of input terms. For simplicity, we ignore the bias term in the abstract element and use equal sign when the upper and lower bounds are equal.

The calculation for the initial two layers are similar to the interval AbsInt.

\chadded
Since the first
\mathrm{GC}_{A}
layer is linear, the bounds can be calculated precisely,
u_{1,j}=0.5(u_{0,j}+v_{0,j}),j=[0,3]

u_{2,0}=0.5(u_{0,2}+v_{0,2})

u_{2,1}=0.5(u_{0,0}+v_{0,0}+u_{0,3}+v_{0,3})

\chadded

Different from the interval approach, polyhedra AbsInt continue calculates the symbolic bounds using the interval bounds.
From the interval AbsInt described previously,
u_{2,0}\in[0.5,1]
and
u_{2,1}\in[1,2]
, since both lower bounds are positive,
\mathrm{ReLU}
becomes an identity function:
u_{3,0}=u_{2,0}
and
u_{3,1}=u_{2,1}

\displaystyle o_{0}=0\times u_{3,0}+1\times u_{3,0}=0.5(u_{0,0}+v_{0,0}+u_{0,3}+v_{0,3})

\displaystyle o_{1}=1\times u_{3,0}+1\times u_{3,0}=0.5(u_{0,0}+v_{0,0}+u_{0,2}+v_{0,2}+u_{0,3}+v_{0,3})

\displaystyle o_{1}-o_{0}=0.5(u_{0,2}+v_{0,2})

In the original graph, both

\chadded
Notably, since the

Implementation:

The abstract operations are
reversible
. One can start from the last layer and continuously back-substitute the latent feature variables until the input feature variables are obtained. This approach can further reduce memory and computation needs for certification by reducing unnecessary neighboring node computations, making the certification more efficient. Our implementation uses differentiable matrix operations to exploit the benefits of GPUs. Further details for implementation and experiments can be found in our reproducibility package.

3.7
Robust training

Since the certification process is differentiable, it can also be used for robust training. We define the loss function used in the robust training for each node. The final loss function for a batch can be the average of all loss values of the nodes in the batch. We also omit training details, such as backpropagation and gradient update.

Given a graph
\mathbf{G}=(A,X)
and perturbation space
\mathcal{P}_{X}
, let classifier
\mathit{cl}
be a GCN, and
\mathit{cl}^{\#_{P}}
be the polyhedra abstract classifier corresponding to
\mathit{cl}
. For the input abstraction
\mathbf{a}_{0}
of the perturbation space, let
\mathbf{a}_{l}=\mathit{cl}^{\#_{P}}(\mathbf{a}_{0})
be the output abstract element. For any node
i
, we compute
\delta^{*}_{i,c_{i}}=[\delta^{*}_{i,c_{i},c^{\prime}}]_{c^{\prime}\neq c_{i}}
for some label
c_{i}
as the lower bound between the output score differences of
c_{i}
and other labels.

During training, we can use the ground truth label if the node is labeled. Let
c_{gt}
be the ground truth label of node
i
:
c_{i}=c_{gt}
. When a node
i
is not labeled, we can either ignore it during the training or use the label predicted by the GCN
c_{i}=argmax(\mathit{cl}(A,X)_{i}).

Ideally,
\delta^{*}_{i,c_{i}}
should be a matrix of positive values, meaning
\mathit{cl}
predicts the ground truth label for all possible changes in the perturbation region. To achieve this, the binary cross entropy loss function can be used during training:
\mathrm{bce}(\delta^{*}_{i,c_{i}})=\mathbf{1}^{(1,|\mathcal{C}|-1)}\cdot\log\sigma(\delta^{*}_{i,c_{i}})
, where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function and
\mathcal{C}
is the set of all labels.

In some cases, controlling the values in
\delta^{*}_{i,c}
is necessary so that it does not grow indefinitely. With this in mind, one can use the robust hinge loss
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
:
\mathrm{hinge}_{t}(\delta^{*}_{i,c})=max(-\delta^{*}_{i,c}+t,0)
where
t
is some predefined threshold. The hinge loss only penalizes the GCN if
\delta^{*}_{i,c}
is smaller than
t
. Consequently, this loss is suitable for semi-supervised learning with unlabeled nodes. One can give a larger threshold for labeled nodes with ground truth labels and a smaller threshold for unlabeled nodes with predicted labels.

3.8
Discussion

This section presents our GCN certification approach based on polyhedra AbsInt in detail. Despite its increased computational demands, the polyhedra-based approach offers many advantages over the interval-based approach.

Abstraction tightness:
The polyhedra abstract domain provides a notably tighter approximation to the perturbation space when compared to the interval domain. The interval domain approximates possible outputs of the GCN with a multi-dimensional box, as it solely tracks the minimum and maximum values. In contrast, the polyhedra domain approximates the values through linear inequalities of input features, resulting in a more precise approximation. Indeed, as demonstrated in
Section 3.4.3
, the interval-based method fails to certify the GCN under a simple example, whereas the polyhedra-based approach can readily certify the example.

Counterexamples:
In addition to the standard lower-bound robustness offered by the interval-based method, the polyhedra-based approach can also create potential counterexamples, identifying upper bounds of robustness. In the polyhedra domain, the coefficient for each input feature used to approximate the output of a GCN can be treated as the impact of altering that particular input feature on the output. Thus, the set comprising the influential features could form a counterexample. Such counterexamples serve as valuable input for understanding the failure patterns of a GCN.

Applicability:
Our method can serve as input for other techniques aimed at deriving other types of robustness certification or enhancing robustness. While the interval-based method may also be used for collective certification, its inherent imprecision can significantly impact the resulting performance when compared to the tight approximation of the polyhedra domain. Moreover, our method can further improve the robustness of GCNs by integrating lower bounds into the loss function during robust training, facilitated by the differentiable operations used in the method.

4.6
Threats to validity

\chadded

Internal validity.

Internal validity concerns the experimental setup. Specifically, we observed potential imprecision in the Dual approach caused by sparse matrix multiplications. Although the fixed version improves the tightness of the upper bound, we report results for both versions to ensure completeness. In addition, the runtime of each approach may fluctuate across runs. To mitigate this variability, we executed all approaches in the same environment and report the median over ten runs. For robust training, GCN training involves several random factors, such as dataset shuffling. To address this threat, we report average metric values over five random seeds.

External validity.

\chadded

The main external validity threat of this work lies in the datasets used for evaluation. Although widely adopted in prior studies
Liu et al. (2020)
;
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
;
Lee et al. (2019)
, these datasets primarily contain homogeneous graphs of small to medium size. This choice reflects the design limitations of GCNs, which operate on homogeneous graphs. In contrast, many real-world large-scale graphs, such as knowledge graphs (e.g., Chameleon, Squirrel, and Texas
Pei et al. (2020)
;
Rozemberczki et al. (2021)
), are heterogeneous, containing multiple node and edge types. Extending the evaluation to such settings would require adapting the approach to alternative GNN architectures. We therefore leave this direction to future work.

\chadded

Construct validity.

This threat mostly concerns metric selection in this study. For all experiments, we adopt widely used metrics, such as the certified lower bound for robustness tightness and accuracy for node classification. In addition, we introduce a new metric, the uncertainty region, which provides a holistic assessment of a certifier’s performance across a range of perturbation limits.

5.1
Graph adversarial examples

The robustness of neural networks against adversarial examples has been studied extensively
Szegedy et al. (2013)
. Graph adversarial examples also receive significant interest, and multiple surveys exist on this topic

;
Chen et al. (2020)
;
Günnemann (2022)
. Two main types of approaches exist for this task: node-level and edge-level.

Node-level
adversarial examples focus on either modifying node attributes or adding nodes to the target graph. The task type determines the approaches used to select nodes or node features to change. Many adversarial attacks by changing node features use GNN gradients to increase task-specific loss
Zügner et al. (2018)
;
Takahashi (2019)
;
Ma et al. (2020)
. Methods for fake node injection typically use reinforcement learning
Sun et al. (2020b)
and optimization

;

. Generative models such as generative adversarial networks (GAN) have also been applied to both node feature perturbations
Bose et al. (2019)
and node injection
Wang et al. (2018c)
.

Edge-level
changes such as adding, removing, or rewiring edges in the graph can also create adversarial examples for GNNs. Reinforcement learning-based approaches are used to learn to select the most influential edge to modify
Ma et al. (2019)
;
Dai et al. (2018)
. Some meta-learning
Zügner and Günnemann (2019a)
and optimization-based methods
Wang and Gong (2019)
;
Xu et al. (2019)
;
Zou et al. (2021)
are also used for adversarial construction with edge addition or removal.

At the same time, different defense methods against adversarial examples have been proposed. Many of the methods improve the training of GNNs. For example, the Dual
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
baseline used in this paper can improve the robustness of GNNs by showing the lower bound of robustness during training. Other methods improve the robustness by focusing on specific graph structures during training
Miller et al. (2024)
, or even modifying the GNN architecture
Chen et al. (2021)
. Tao et. al.
Tao et al. (2023)
proposed a method to fix and immunize certain parts of the graph from any modification to improve the robustness of GNNs.

Counterexamples derived from our approach can be seen as node-level adversarial examples for GCNs. But our approach also provides certified lower bounds of the GCN being robust under
any
node-level graph adversarial examples. Furthermore, our method can be used to improve the robustness during training, similar to the Dual method.

5.2
Neural networks certification

Many approaches have been proposed to certify properties of neural networks. These approaches can be categorized by the types of abstraction they use during the verification. Two surveys provide comprehensive overviews

;

Optimization-based
methods abstract the neural network into a set of optimization clauses where the property is formulated as the objective function. Then an external optimization solver can be utilized to check the property can be satisfied. NSVerify

Singh et al. (2019)
uses the Deeppoly abstract domain to verify convolutional neural networks with ReLU or Sigmoid activation functions. ReluVal
Wang et al. (2018b)
and Neurify

Hybrid methods
combine different approaches to design a hybrid verifier. Alpha-beta crowning

;

;

combines bound propagation and branch and bound techniques for fast and precise certification on neural networks.

6
Conclusions

In this study, we tackle the challenge of GCN certification for node classification in the presence of node feature perturbation. According to the perturbation model, each node can be altered within a specific local limit while the entire graph is subject to changes within a global limit. We introduce generic concepts for sound and complete certifiers for node classification. We propose a polyhedra AbsInt method for certifying GCN robustness within a defined perturbation region, ensuring
no
changes to the original graph can influence the GCN’s prediction. Our experimental evaluations, conducted on three widely used node classification datasets and compared with two other certification methods, indicate that our method provides tighter certification bounds and significantly improves the certification runtime. This improvement in the tightness is further reflected in the collective certification. Moreover, our approach demonstrates promising results in improving GCN robustness through robust training.

We believe our approach introduces a novel direction in GNN certification. While our polyhedra abstract interpretation presents strong advantages for GCN certification in node classification, it is important to note that real-world scenarios might encounter
\chadded
larger-scale heterogeneous graphs, other types of graph perturbations, diverse GNN architectures, and different graph tasks. One direction for future work involves investigating using AbsInt in these contexts. Notably, despite the better tightness of our approach, it does not outperform baselines in robust training. Thus, subsequent research could target to optimize robustness training methods to further improve the robustness of GCNs. Finally, an added merit of our method is its ability to express the GCN results as linear expressions. These expressions can potentially be used to enhance the interpretability of GCNs.

A.6
Implementation details

The polyhedra abstract operations can be implemented using matrix operations since the transformation of each node feature is independent with a straightforward modification: the input abstraction does not give an identity matrix. Instead, it yields a matrix of shape
m_{0}\times(n\times m_{0})
with the entry corresponding to feature
j
of node
i
being 1. This matrix may be memory intensive if
n
is large. Nevertheless, we can mitigate this by (1) using a sparse matrix and (2) using batched computation such that
n
is not the entire graph but the subgraph of
l
-hop neighbors from the nodes in the batch.
With matrix operations, the certification can be run efficiently on GPUs. Furthermore, the certification process is differentiable such that the certifier can be combined with computation frameworks such as PyTorch or TensorFlow for robust training.

A.7
Training configuration

We implement both
Poly
and
Interval
approaches in Pytorch. We randomly split the nodes into training and testing sets for five random nodes and trained with the same random nodes.
During training, we use a learning rate of 0.005 and weight decay of 0.00001 with an Adam optimizer. We use a batch size of 8 and accumulate a gradient for every five batches, making a 40-node batch per gradient update. We use node sampling during training. We set the training step to 1500 for experiments with labeled nodes and 15000 for experiments with both labeled and unlabeled nodes for all datasets. Finally, there are some nodes in Pubmed causing the Dual approach to crash; we exclude these nodes during training for both approaches. For the hinge loss, we use a margin of
log\frac{90}{10}
for labelled nodes and
log\frac{60}{40}
for unlabelled nodes, same as previous work
Zügner and Günnemann (2019b)
.
