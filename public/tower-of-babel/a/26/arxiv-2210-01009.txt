Scaling limit of a long-range random walk in time-correlated random environment
Guanglin Rang, Jian Song, Meng Wang

Abstract
. This paper concerns a long-range random walk in random environment in dimension 1 + 1 1+1 , where the environmental disorder is independent in space but has long-range correlations in time. We prove that two types of rescaled partition functions converge weakly to the Stratonovich solution and the Itô-Skorohod solution respectively of a fractional stochastic heat equation with multiplicative Gaussian noise which is white in space and colored in time.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
60F05; 60H15; 82C05

1.
Introduction

The model of directed polymer in random environment was first introduced by Huse and Henley
[
24
]
in the study of the Ising model and became a canonical model for disordered systems (see e.g. the lecture notes by Comets
[
11
]
). In recent years, it has attracted much attention in particular due to its intimate connections to the stochastic heat equation (parabolic Anderson model), the stochastic Burgers equation, and the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equation and its universality class (see
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
for a review).

For a simple symmetric random walk in i.i.d. random environment on
\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
, Alberts et al.
[
1
]
proved that the rescaled partition function converges weakly to the
Itô-Skorohod
solution of the stochastic heat equation (SHE) with multiplicative space-time Gaussian white noise. This result was extended by Caravenna et al.
[
8
]
to a long-range random walk in i.i.d. random environment and to other disordered models (disordered pinning model and random field Ising model). Later on, Rang
[
37
]
proved that, for a simple random walk in random environment which is white in time but correlated in space, the rescaled partition function converges weakly to the
Itô-Skorohod
solution of SHE with Gaussian noise white in time and colored in space; this result was extended to long-range random walks by Chen and Gao
[
9
]
; if the random environment is given by the occupation field of a Poisson system of independent random walks on
{\mathbb{Z}}
which is now correlated in both time and space, Shen et al.
[
41
]
showed that the scaling limit is the
Stratonovich
solution of SHE with space-time colored Gaussian noise whose covariance coincides with the heat kernel. It is suggested by the results and methodologies in the above-mentioned papers that the
temporal independence
of the random environment plays a critical role when identifying the scaling limits for partition functions, which will be further discussed in Section
1.3
.

In this paper, we aim to study the scaling limit of the partition function for a long-range random walk in random environment on
\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
where the disorder is independent in space but has long-range correlations in time.

We remark that the model of directed polymer in space-time correlated random environment whose covariance has a power-law decay was first considered in physics literature by Medina et al.
[
32
]
where the Burgers equation with colored noise was studied and then applied to analyse directed polymer and interface growth. We also would like to mention that Rovira and Tindel
[
39
]
introduced Brownian polymer in a centered Gaussian field that is white in time and correlated in space on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
and studied the asymptotic behavior of the partition function; in the two subsequent papers, Bezerra et al.
[
6
]
obtained the superdiffusivity and Lacoin
[
30
]
investigated the effect of strong spatial correlation. Finally, we remind the reader that the model of long-range directed polymer has been studied by Comets
[
10
]
, and more recently by Wei
[
44
]
.

1.1.
Notations and known results

For the reader’s convenience, we collect the mathematical notations that will be used throughout this article.

Notations 1.1
.

Let
\mathbb{N}
denote the set of natural numbers without 0, i.e.,
\mathbb{N}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{1,2,\dots\}
; for
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\llbracket N\rrbracket\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{1,2,\dots,N\}
; for
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
[a]
means the greatest integer that is not greater than
a
;
\|\cdot\|
is used for the Euclidean norm; let
{\bm{k}}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(k_{1},\dots,k_{d}),{\bm{l}}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(l_{1},\dots,l_{d}),{\bm{x}}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}),{\bm{y}}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
etc stand for vectors in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
depending on the context; we use
C
to denote a generic positive constant that may change from line to line; we say
f(x)\lesssim g(x)
if
f(x)\leq Cg(x)
for all
x
; we write
f(x)\sim g(x)
(as
x\to\infty
), if
\lim\limits_{x\to\infty}f(x)/g(x)=1
. We use
\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}
to denote the convergence in distribution (also called weak convergence) for random variables/vectors. For a random variable
X
,
\|X\|_{L^{p}}=\left({\mathbb{E}}[|X|^{p}]\right)^{1/p}
for
p\geq 1
.

Let
S=\{S_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
be a random walk in
{\mathbb{Z}}
and
{\omega}=\{{\omega}(i,k),(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{Z}}\}
be a family of random variables independent of
S
serving as the random environment (disorder). We shall use
{\mathbb{P}}_{S}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{S}
(resp.
{\mathbb{P}}_{{\omega}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\omega}
) to denote the probability and expectation in the probability space of
S
(resp.
{\omega}
), respectively. The probability and expectation in the product probability space of
(S,{\omega})
is denoted by
{\mathbb{P}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}
, respectively.

Given
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
S^{(N+1,k)}=\big\{S^{(N+1,k)}_{i},i\in\llbracket N+1\rrbracket\big\}
be a
backward
random walk in
\mathbb{Z}
with
S^{(N+1,k)}_{N+1}=k
, and the
partition function
is defined by

(1.1)

\displaystyle Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\beta,k)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\sum_{S}{\rm e}^{{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}\left(i,S^{(N+1,k)}_{i}\right)}{\mathbb{P}}(S)={\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[{\rm e}^{{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}\left(i,S^{(N+1,k)}_{i}\right)}\right],

where
{\beta}=1/T>0
is the inverse temperature. We stress that the random walk is backward on the time interval
[1,N+1]
in the sense that
S^{(N+1,k)}_{N+1}=k
while there is no restriction on
S^{(N+1,k)}_{1}
. Hence,
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\beta,k)
given by (
1.1
) is indeed a point-to-line partition function, and it corresponds to directly a discrete version of the solution to stochastic heat equation (see Proposition
2.10
), which facilitates our calculations. Throughout the rest of the article, we shall omit the superscript
(N+1,k)
for the backward random walk to simplify the notation.

When
S
is a simple symmetric random walk with i.i.d. increments and
{\omega}(i,k)
are i.i.d. random variables with

\lambda(\beta)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\log{\mathbb{E}}[{\rm e}^{\beta{\omega}(i,k)}]<\infty

for some
\beta
sufficiently small, Alberts et al.
[
1
]
introduced the so-called
intermediate disorder regime
. More precisely, if
\beta
is scaled in the way

\beta\to\hat{\beta}_{N}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\beta N^{-\frac{1}{4}},

one has the following weak convergence

{\rm e}^{-N\lambda(\hat{\beta}_{N})}Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/2}x)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}u(1,x)\text{ as }N\to\infty,

where
u(t,x)
is the Itô-Skorohod solution of the equation

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u(t,x)+\sqrt{2}\beta u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x),~t>0,x\in\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=1.\end{cases}

Here,
\dot{W}
is space-time Gaussian white noise and Itô-Skorohod solution means that the product in
u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x)
is a Wick product, or equivalently the associated stochastic integral is an Itô-Skorohod integral.

Under the same setting except that the random walk
S
may be long-range (i.e., the increments of
S
now lie in the domain of attraction of a
\rho
-stable law with
\rho\in(1,2]
), the result of
[
1
]
was extended in Caravenna et al.
[
8
]
where a unified framework based on the Lindeberg principle for polynomial chaos expansion was developed to study the scaling limits for pinning model, directed polymer model, and Ising model. Still using the setting of
[
1
]
but assuming that the disorder
\{{\omega}(i,k),(i,k)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{Z}\}
is correlated in space (but still independent in time), Rang
[
37
]
and Chen-Gao
[
9
]
obtained the weak convergence of the rescaled partition function to the Itô-Skorohod solution of (
1.2
) with the Gaussian noise
\dot{W}(t,x)
being colored in space (still white in time).

1.2.
Model description

Motivated by the above-mentioned works, we aim to study the weak convergence of the rescaled partition function for a long-range random walk
\{S_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
in random environment on
\mathbb{N}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
, where the disorder
\{{\omega}(i,k),i\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is correlated in time and independent in space.

In our model, the directed polymer (random walk) is given by
S_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}Y_{i}
, where
\{Y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are independent and identically distributed random variables with mean zero which have a 1-lattice distribution belonging to the domain of attraction of a
\rho
-stable distribution with density function denoted by
\mathfrak{g}_{\rho}(\cdot)
. We assume
1<\rho\leq 2
and

(1.3)

\begin{cases}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{i}=k)\lesssim|k|^{-1-\rho}\text{ for }k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\backslash\{0\},&\text{ if }\rho\in(1,2),\\
{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{i}]=0\text{ and }{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{i}^{2}]=1,&\text{ if }\rho=2.\end{cases}

Let
\psi(u)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}[{\rm e}^{{\imath}uY_{i}}]
be
the characteristic function of
Y_{i}
. Then the 1-lattice distribution of
Y_{i}
implies that
\psi(u)
is periodic with period
2\pi
, and furthermore,
|\psi(u)|<1
for all
u\in[-\pi,\pi]\backslash\{0\}
(see, e.g.,
[
25
, Theorem 1.4.2]
); this property of
\psi(u)
usually makes analysis easier. The assumption of
1
-lattice distribution on
Y_{i}
also yields that the random walk
S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
has period 1 and hence is
aperiodic
. We remind the reader that a simple symmetric random walk has period 2.

Denote by
P_{n}(k)={\mathbb{P}}(S_{n}=k)
for
n\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
the probability of
S
being at
k
at time
n
. Then by the local limit theorem for
\rho
-stable distribution (see
[
40
, Theorem 6.1]
), the convergence

(1.4)

n^{1/\rho}P_{n}(k)-\mathfrak{g}_{\rho}\big(k/n^{1/\rho}\big)\to 0,\text{ as }n\to\infty,

holds uniformly in
k
. Noting that
\mathfrak{g}_{\rho}
is a bounded function, (
1.4
) yields

(1.5)

\displaystyle P_{n}(k)\lesssim n^{-1/\rho},\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

Also under the condition of (
1.3
), we have a local deviation estimation (see, e.g.,
[
5
, Theorem 2.6]
):

(1.6)

\displaystyle P_{n}(k)\lesssim n|k|^{-1-\rho},\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}.

This together with (
1.5
), we get

(1.7)

\displaystyle P_{n}(k)\lesssim\left(n|k|^{-1-\rho}\right)\wedge n^{-1/\rho}=n^{-1/\rho}\left(|n^{-1/\rho}k|^{-1-\rho}\wedge 1\right),\text{ for }n\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
a\wedge b:=\min\{a,b\}
for
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.

It is well known that

\frac{S_{n}}{n^{1/\rho}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\xi,~~~~\mbox{as}~~n\rightarrow\infty,

where
\xi
has the symmetric
\rho
-stable distribution with characteristic function
\exp\{-c_{\rho}|\eta|^{\rho}\}
for some
c_{\rho}>0
. Letting
\mathfrak{g}_{\rho}(t,x)
be the density function of the corresponding
\rho
-stable process, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{\imath\eta x}\mathfrak{g}_{\rho}(t,x){\mathrm{d}}x={\rm e}^{-c_{\rho}t|\eta|^{\rho}}.

Throughout the rest of the paper we will omit the subscript
\rho
and use
\mathfrak{g}(t,x)
exclusively to denote the density function of the
\rho
-stable process
X
. Note that
\mathfrak{g}
has the following scaling property

(1.8)

\mathfrak{g}(t,x)=t^{-\frac{1}{\rho}}\mathfrak{g}(1,t^{-\frac{1}{\rho}}x),

and the upper bound in parallel with (
1.7
)

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}(t,x)\lesssim(t|x|^{-1-\rho})\wedge t^{-1/\rho}=t^{-1/\rho}\Big(|t^{-1/\rho}x|^{-1-\rho}\wedge 1\Big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~x\in\mathbb{R}.

We assume that the disorder in the environment is given by a family of Gaussian random variables
\{{\omega}(n,k),n\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
with mean zero and covariance

(1.10)

{\mathbb{E}}[{\omega}(n,k){\omega}(n^{\prime},k^{\prime})]=\gamma(n-n^{\prime})\delta_{kk^{\prime}},

where
\delta_{kk^{\prime}}
is the Kronecker delta
function, i.e.,
\delta_{kk^{\prime}}=1
if
k=k^{\prime}
and
\delta_{kk^{\prime}}=0
otherwise, and
\gamma(n)
has a power law decay:

(1.11)

\gamma(n)\lesssim|n|^{2H-2}\wedge 1,\text{ for }n\in{\mathbb{Z}},

where
H\in(1/2,1]
denotes a fixed constant throughout this paper. We further assume that for all
t\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}
,

(1.12)

\lim_{N\to\infty}N^{2-2H}\gamma([Nt])=|t|^{2H-2}.

1.3.
Main result, strategy and discussions

Consider the stochastic fractional heat equation on
\mathbb{R}

(1.13)

\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial t}=-c_{\rho}(-\Delta)^{\frac{\rho}{2}}u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x),\\
u(0,x)=1,\end{cases}

where
\rho\in(1,2]
and
\dot{W}(t,x)
is Gaussian noise with covariance function given by

(1.14)

{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)\bigg]=K(t-s,x-y)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}|t-s|^{2H-2}\delta(x-y),

with
H\in(1/2,1]
and
\delta(\cdot)
being the Dirac delta function. In particular,
\dot{W}
is a spatial white noise independent of time if
H=1
.

We consider two types of solutions of (
1.13
)—the
Stratonovich solution
if the product
u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x)
is an ordinary product (i.e., the associated stochastic integral is a Stratonovich integral) and the
Itô-Skorohod solution
if
u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x)
is a Wick product (i.e., the associated stochastic integral is a Skorohod integral). See Section
2.1
for details.

Now we are ready to present our main results. For the first type of partition function
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}
which is given in (
1.1
), we have the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the random walk
S
and the disorder
{\omega}
be given as in Section
1.2
. Let
H
and
\rho
be parameters satisfying

(1.15)

H\in(1/2,1]~\text{ and }~\theta\overset{\rm{def}}{=}H-\frac{1}{2\rho}>\frac{1}{2}.

Consider the rescaled partition function
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})
given in (
1.1
) under the scaling

\beta\to\hat{\beta}_{N}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\beta N^{-\theta}.

Let
u(t,x)
be the Stratonovich solution of (
1.13
). Then we have

Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}u(1,x_{0}),\text{ as }N\to\infty.

In comparison with (
1.1
), another type of point-to-line partition function is given by

(1.16)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\beta,k)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[{\rm e}^{{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}(i,S_{i})-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\gamma(i-j)\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{j}\}}}\right],

where
S=S^{(N+1,k)}=\big\{S_{i},i\in\llbracket N+1\rrbracket\big\}
is a backward random walk with
S_{N+1}=k
. The extra term in the exponential is half of the variance of
{\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}(i,S_{i})
conditional on the random walk
S
. Indeed, we have

(1.17)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\omega}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}(i,S_{i})\right)^{2}\right]=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{\omega}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\omega}(i,k)\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{i}=k\}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\omega}(j,l)\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{j}=l\}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\sum_{k,l\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\gamma(i-j)\delta_{kl}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{i}=k\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{j}=l\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\gamma(i-j)\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{j}\}},

which can be viewed as a weighted intersection local time of
S
.

For the scaling limit of
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}
given in (
1.16
), we have the following result in parallel with Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let the random walk
S
and the disorder
{\omega}
be given as in Section
1.2
. Let
H
and
\rho
be parameters satisfying

(1.18)

H\in(1/2,1],~\rho\in(1,2],\text{ and }~\theta=H-\frac{1}{2\rho}>0.

Consider the rescaled partition function
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})
given in (
1.16
) under the scaling

\beta\to\hat{\beta}_{N}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\beta N^{-\theta}.

Let
\tilde{u}(t,x)
be the Itô-Skorohod solution of (
1.13
). Then we have

\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\tilde{u}(1,x_{0}),\text{ as }N\to\infty.

Remark 1.2
.

In Theorem
1.2
, the condition
\rho>1
in (
1.18
) arises to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of the Itô-Skorohod solution of (
1.13
) (see Remark
2.13
in Section
2.3
). In Theorem
1.1
, the condition (
1.15
) also implies
\rho>\frac{1}{2H-1}\geq 1
. This is why we restrict us on the case
\rho\in(1,2]
in this article.

Remark 1.3
.

For (
1.13
), it requires less restrictive conditions to have an Itô-Skorohod solution than to have a Stratonovich solution (see Remark
2.17
and see also
[
42
]
for a more general class of SPDEs). This explains why condition (
1.18
) for the Itô-Skorohod case is weaker than condition (
1.15
) for the Stratonovich case.

The proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
will be presented in Section
3.2
. For the reader’s convenience, here we explain briefly the strategy for Theorem
1.1
(the proof of Theorem
1.2
is similar but easier). By Taylor’s expansion, the rescaled partition function can be written as

\displaystyle Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{(N)},

where

(1.19)

\displaystyle{{\mathbb{S}}}_{m}^{(N)}=\hat{\beta}^{m}_{N}\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\omega}(n_{1},k_{1})\cdots{\omega}(n_{m},k_{m})P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};k_{1},\dots,k_{m}),

with
P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}
(see eq. (
3.4
)) being the product of the transition densities of the random walk
S
. Meanwhile, the Stratonovich solution of the continuum equation (
1.13
) has the following series representation (see eq. (
2.27
)):

u(1,x_{0})=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\beta^{m}\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m}(\cdot;1,x_{0})),

where
\mathbb{I}_{m}(\cdot)
is a multiple Stratonovich integral (see Section
2.1.2
) and
\mathfrak{g}_{m}
(see eq. (
2.28
)) is the product of the transition densities of the stable process
X
. We remind the reader that the continuum multiple Stratonovich integral
\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m}(\cdot;1,x_{0}))
resembles the discrete sum
\mathbb{S}_{m}^{(N)}
.

Under condition (
1.15
), one can obtain the
L^{1}
-convergence of the series of
u(t,x)
(see Proposition
2.12
) and the uniform (in
N
)
L^{1}
-convergence of the series of
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})
(see (
3.40
)). Thus, in order to prove the weak convergence
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}u(1,x_{0})
, in light of Lemma
B.1
, it suffices to obtain joint weak convergences for
\mathbb{S}_{m}^{(N)}
’s, that is, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
l_{1},\dots,l_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
,

\left(\frac{1}{l_{1}!}\mathbb{S}_{l_{1}}^{(N)},\dots,\frac{1}{l_{k}!}\mathbb{S}_{l_{k}}^{(N)}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\Big(\beta^{l_{1}}\mathbb{I}_{l_{1}}(\mathfrak{g}_{l_{1}}(\cdot;1,x_{0})),\dots,\beta^{l_{k}}\mathbb{I}_{l_{k}}(\mathfrak{g}_{l_{k}}(\cdot;1,x_{0}))\Big),\text{ as }N\to\infty,

which is proved in Proposition
3.4
.

Multiple Wiener integrals
\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)
and multiple Stratonovich integrals
\mathbb{I}_{m}(f)
are linked via the celebrated Hu-Meyer’s formula (
2.19
). Usually it is more convenient to deal with multiple Wiener integrals
\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)
whose second moment is easier to calculate. For this reason, in Section
3.1
we first consider
U
-statistics given in (
3
) which are multi-linear Wick polynomials of
{\omega}(n_{i},k_{i})
’s, and prove that they converge weakly to multiple Wiener integrals (see Proposition
3.3
). This together with Hu-Meyer’s formula (
2.19
) then yields Proposition
3.4
.

Note that the exponential term in the partition function
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}
in (
1.16
) is actually a
Wick exponential
(see eq. (
2.15
)) of
\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}(i,S_{i})
conditional on the random walk
S
. Thus, to prove Theorem
1.2
, one only needs Proposition
3.3
on the weak convergence of multi-linear Wick polynomials.

As can be seen, our approach is very much inspired by
[
1
]
where the random variables of disorder are i.i.d. However, in our setting the disorder is correlated in time, and it turns out that this difference is critical in the sense that it prevents us from using the trick of “modified partition function” (see in
[
1
, eq. (4)]
, which was also employed in
[
37
]
), as this method relies on the temporal independence of the disorder. For the same reason, the Mayer expansion (see
[
8
, equations (1.5) and (1.7)]
), which was employed in
[
8
]
to linearize the exponential while introducing an error term that comes from the Itô correction, cannot be applied here either, since it works well only if the disorder is independent in both time and space.

To conclude the introduction, we make some remarks on our main results Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

(i)
In the polymer model, the temporal independence of the disorder plays a critical role. Recently, a directed polymer in time-space correlated random environment was studied in
[
41
]
and it was shown that the rescaled partition function converges weakly to a Stratonovich solution. Assuming that the disorder is correlated in time and white in space in our setting, the rescaled partition functions
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}
and
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}
converge weakly to
Stratonovich
and
Itô-Skorohod
solutions, respectively. In contrast, if the disorder possesses temporal independence, after a proper scaling, the limit of the rescaled partition function is an
Itô-Skorohod
solution but not a
Stratonovich
solution, as has been shown in
[
1
,
8
,
37
,
9
]
. Among others, a technical explanation for the critical role of the temporal independence of the disorder is the following: when the temporal independence of disorder appears, by using the “modified partition function” trick, the term corresponding to
\mathbb{S}_{m}^{(N)}
given by (
1.19
) becomes a multi-linear polynomial of
{\omega}(n_{i},k_{i})
for
i=1,\dots,m
with
n_{1}<n_{2}<\cdots<n_{m}
, which converges weakly to a
multiple Wiener integral
due to the independence (see
[
34
,
8
]
).

(ii)
In contrast to the case that the disorder is independent in time where one can employ the “modified partition function” trick (or Mayer’s expansion for the case that the disorder is independent both in time and space) to make time indices in the summation distinct from each other, we expand the partition function directly via Taylor’s expansion, and as a consequence the time indices
n_{1},\dots,n_{m}
in the summation (
1.19
) can be repeated. It turns out that this in fact gives negligible contribution for the Stratonovich case, due to the condition
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
in (
1.15
) which is more restrictive than the condition
\theta>0
in (
1.18
) for the Skorohod case (see also
[
1
,
8
,
37
]
). For instance, when
m=2
, the expectation of the sum of the diagonal terms in (
1.19
) is
\beta\gamma(0)N^{1-2\theta}
which converges to zero as
N
tends to infinity if we assume
\theta>1/2
.

(iii)
The study of the scaling limit of partition functions was initiated in
[
1
]
in order to understand the polymer behavior in the so-called
intermediate disorder regime
which sits between weak and strong disorder regimes. Meanwhile, as pointed out in
[
8
]
, the fact that the rescaled partition functions converge
weakly to a non-trivial limit indicates that the directed polymer model is
disorder relevant
, since it implies that the presence of disorder, no matter how small it is, changes the qualitative features of the underlying homogeneous model.

(iv)
As pointed out in
[
32
]
, it is of interest to study directed polymer in random environment which has long-range correlations in time and space. In light of the work of
[
41
]
, we expect that our results and methodology can be extended to the model in dimension
1+d
with space-time long-range correlated disorder. Note that the corresponding continuum SPDEs have been investigated in
[
23
,
42
]
.
For instance, consider the limiting Gaussian noise with covariance

{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)\bigg]=|t-s|^{-\alpha_{0}}|x-y|^{-\alpha},

where
\alpha_{0}\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,d)
. Then (
1.2
) has a Stratonovich solution if
\alpha<\rho(1-\alpha_{0})
(see Remark 3.1 in
[
42
]
) and an Itô-Skorohod solution if
\alpha<\rho
(see Remark 5.1 and Theorem 5.3 in
[
42
]
). In particular, this allows to consider the case
d>1,\rho\in(0,1]
(recall that the spatial independence forces us to consider only the case
d=1,\rho\in(1,2]
, see Remarks
1.2
and
2.13
).

(v)
In our model, we assume the Gaussianity of the disorder
{\omega}
for technical reasons. The loss of Gaussian property in
{\omega}
shall cause a lot more complexity in computations (see e.g. (
A.7
) and (
A.8
) for a comparison of the computations of moments for Gaussian and non-Gaussian random variables). The functional analytic approach developed in
[
41
]
might be a choice to circumvent this difficulty. Nevertheless, we conjecture that our result still holds for sub-Gaussian disorder.

(vi)
Assume the disorder
\{{\omega}(i,k),i\in\mathbb{N},k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is a family of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables. In this situation, the exponent in the correction term of the rescaled partition function
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)
becomes, recalling (
1.17
) and replacing
\gamma(i-j)
by
\delta_{ij}
therein,

\frac{1}{2}{\hat{\beta}_{N}^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\delta_{ij}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{i}=S_{j}\}}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\beta}_{N}^{2}N=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}N^{\frac{1}{\rho}},

which is now independent of the random walk
S
. Thus, the rescaled partition function for the Itô-Skorohod case can be written as

\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)={\rm e}^{-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}N^{\frac{1}{\rho}}}Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k),

which converges weakly to the Itô-Skorohod solution of (
1.13
) with space-time white noise.
This is consistent with
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
, noting that
N\log{\mathbb{E}}[{\rm e}^{\hat{\beta}_{N}{\omega}(i,k)}]=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\beta}_{N}^{2}N
if we assume the disorder is Gaussian.

(vii)
If the disorder
{\omega}
and the limiting noise
\dot{W}
live in the same probability space such that
\dot{W}
is the scaling limit of
{\omega}
, we can get strong convergence (a.s. convergence) in Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.2
. In this sense, the rescaled partition functions
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}
and
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}
can be viewed as approximations for the Stratonovich solution and the Itô-Skorohod solution of equation (
1.13
), respectively. See e.g. Foondun at el.
[
15
]
and Joseph et al.
[
28
]
for related results.

(viii)
As a byproduct, we obtain a Jensen type inequality for the integrals induced by fractional Brownian motion (see Lemmas
2.2
and
C.1
) which is new in the literature to our best knowledge. We provide a new approach to prove the exponential integrability of the weighted self-intersection local time of the
\rho
-stable process in Proposition
2.7
which plays a critical role in obtaining the Feynman-Kac formula for the solution of (
1.13
); this approach also simplifies the proof in
[
23
]
which works exclusively for the weighted self-intersection local time of Brownian motion.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

are proved in Section

2.1.1.
Banach spaces associated with
\dot{W}

On probability space
({\Omega},{\mathcal{F}},P)
satisfying usual conditions, let
\dot{W}=\{\dot{W}(t,x):t\in[0,1],x\in\mathbb{R}\}
be real-valued centered Gaussian noise with covariance

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)]=

\displaystyle K(t-s,x-y)=|t-s|^{2H-2}\delta(x-y),

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. The Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
associated with
\dot{W}
is the completion of smooth functions with compact support under the inner product

(2.2)

\displaystyle\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,x)K(s-t,x-y)g(t,y){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s-t|^{2H-2}f(s,x)g(t,x){\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}t.

We assume
H\in(1/2,1].
For the case
H=1
, the noise
\dot{W}
is indeed a spatial white noise which does not depend on time.

We remark that the Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}
contains distributions (see
[
36
]
). For our purpose, it suffices to just consider classical measurable functions. We introduce the following Banach space
\mathcal{B}
which is a subset of the set
\mathscr{B}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R})
of measurable functions on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.1
.

We define

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\Bigg\{

\displaystyle f\in\mathscr{B}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}):

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s-t|^{2H-2}|f(s,x)||f(t,x)|{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}t\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}<\infty\Bigg\}.

Clearly
\mathcal{B}
is a dense subset of
\mathscr{H}
, and if
f\in\mathscr{H}
is a nonnegative function,
f
also belongs to
\mathcal{B}
.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}
, we denote by
\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)
the conditional expectation of
f
with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{B}_{N}^{m}=\sigma({\mathfrak{R}}_{N}^{m})
generated by the set
{\mathfrak{R}}_{N}^{m}
of rectangles

(2.4)

{\mathfrak{R}}_{N}^{m}:=\left\{\left[\frac{{{\bm{i}}}}{N},\frac{{{\bm{i}}}+\overrightarrow{1}_{m}}{N}\right)\times\left[\frac{{{\bm{k}}}}{N^{1/\rho}},\frac{{{\bm{k}}}+{\overrightarrow{1}}_{m}}{N^{1/\rho}}\right):{{\bm{i}}}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket^{m},{{\bm{k}}}\in{{\mathbb{Z}}}^{m}\right\},

where
{\overrightarrow{1}}_{m}
is the
m
-dimensional vector of all ones. That is,
\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)
is defined by the average values of
f
on the blocks
B\in{\mathfrak{R}}_{N}^{m}
:

(2.5)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}({{\bm{t}}},{{\bm{x}}})=\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}f({{\bm{s}}},{{\bm{y}}}){\mathrm{d}}{{\bm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}}{{\bm{y}}}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{B}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}}),

where
\mathbf{1}_{B}
is an indicator function and
|B|
is the Lebesgue measure of

B
.

The following Jensen type of inequality will be used to prove the weak convergence of U-statistics in Section
3.1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
m,N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}
and let
{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}
be given by (
2.5
). Then, we have

(2.6)

\|{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}}\leq C^{m}\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}},

where
C
is a constant depending only on
H
.

Proof
We prove (
2.6
) for
m=1
and the general case can be proved in a similar way. Note that by Fubini’s theorem,
\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)=\mathcal{A}^{\text{t}}_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f))=\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(\mathcal{A}^{\text{t}}_{N}(f))
, where
\mathcal{A}^{\text{t}}_{N}
(resp.
\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}
) means taking average in time (resp. space) only. Therefore, we have

\|\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)\|_{\mathcal{B}}=\|\mathcal{A}_{N}^{\text{t}}(\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f))\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq C\|\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f)\|_{\mathcal{B}},

for some
C
depending on
H
only, where the inequality follows from Lemma
C.1
.

Now, it suffices to prove
\|\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f)\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}
. Using the identity, for
H\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
,

|s-t|^{2H-2}=c_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}|t-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathrm{d}}\tau

where
c_{H}
is a finite number only depending on
H
,
we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f)\|_{\mathcal{B}}^{2}

\displaystyle=c_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}x\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{d}}s\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{d}}t\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}\tau|s-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}|t-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\left|\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f)\right|(s,x)\left|\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f)\right|(t,x)

\displaystyle=c_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}x\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}\tau\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{d}}s|s-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\left|\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}(f)\right|(s,x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}x\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}\tau\left(\mathcal{A}^{\text{x}}_{N}\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{d}}s|s-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}|f|(s,\cdot)\right)(\tau,x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq c_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}x\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{d}}\tau\left(\int_{0}^{1}{\mathrm{d}}s|s-\tau|^{H-\frac{3}{2}}|f|(s,x)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}^{2},

where the last inequality follows from the classical Jensen’s inequality.
∎

2.1.2.
Chaos expansion, Wick product, multiple integrals, etc

Recall that
\mathscr{H}
is the Hilbert space associated with Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
with covariance (
2.1
). Let
\{W(f),f\in\mathscr{H}\}
be an isonormal Gaussian process with covariance

(2.7)

{\mathbb{E}}[W(f)W(g)]=\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}.

In particular, if
f=\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]\times[0,x]}
, we denote

W(t,x)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}W(\mathbf{1}_{[0,t]\times[0,x]}).

Then the fractional noise
\dot{W}(t,x)
can be viewed as the partial derivative
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t\partial x}W(t,x)
in the sense of distribution.
For
f\in\mathscr{H}
, we also use the integral form to denote the Wiener integral
W(f)
:

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s,y)W({\mathrm{d}}s,{\mathrm{d}}y)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}W(f).

For
m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, let

(2.8)

H_{m}(x)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(-1)^{m}{\rm e}^{x^{2}/2}\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{m}}{{\mathrm{d}}x^{m}}{\rm e}^{-x^{2}/2},\quad x\in{\mathbb{R}}

be the
m
th
Hermite polynomial
. For
g\in\mathscr{H}
, the
multiple Wiener integral
of
g^{\otimes m}\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
can be defined via (see e.g.
[
20
,
35
]
)

(2.9)

\mathbf{I}_{m}(g^{\otimes m})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\|g\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{m}\,H_{m}\left(W(g)\|g\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{-1}\right).

In particular, we have
W(g)=\mathbf{I}_{1}(g)
and
\mathbf{I}_{m}(g^{\otimes m})=:\!W(g)^{m}\!:
, where
:\!W(g)^{m}\!:
is a physical Wick product (see Section
A
).

For
f\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
, let
\hat{f}
be its symmetrization, i.e.,

\hat{f}(t_{1},x_{1},\dots,t_{m},x_{m})=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}f(t_{\sigma(1)},x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,t_{\sigma(m)},x_{\sigma(m)}),

where
\mathcal{P}_{m}
is the set of all permutations of
\llbracket m\rrbracket=\{1,2,\dots,m\}.
Let
{\mathscr{H}}^{\hat{\otimes}m}
be the symmetrization of
{\mathscr{H}}^{\otimes m}
. Then for
f\in{\mathscr{H}}^{\hat{\otimes}m}
, one can define the
m
th multiple Wiener integral
\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)
via (
2.9
) by a limiting argument. Moreover, for
f\in{\mathscr{H}}^{\hat{\otimes}m}
and
g\in{\mathscr{H}}^{\hat{\otimes}n}
, we have

(2.10)

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\mathbf{I}_{n}(g)]=m!\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}\delta_{mn},

where we recall that
\delta_{mn}
is the Kronecker delta function. For
f\in{\mathscr{H}}^{\otimes m}
which is not necessarily symmetric, we simply let
\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\mathbf{I}_{m}(\hat{f}).
We also take the following notation for multiple Wiener integrals:

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})^{m}}f({{\bm{t}}},{{\bm{x}}})W({\mathrm{d}}{t_{1}},{\mathrm{d}}{x_{1}})\diamond\cdots\diamond W({\mathrm{d}}{t_{m}},{\mathrm{d}}{x_{m}})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\mathbf{I}_{m}(f),\text{ for }f\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}.

For
f\in\mathscr{H}^{\hat{\otimes}m}
and
g\in\mathscr{H}^{\hat{\otimes}n}
, their contraction of
r
indices for
1\leq r\leq m\land n
is defined by

\displaystyle(f\otimes_{r}g)(t_{1},x_{1},\dots,t_{m+n-2r},x_{m+n-2r})

\displaystyle\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\int_{[0,1]^{2r}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2r}}f(t_{1},x_{1},\dots,t_{n-r},x_{n-r},u_{1},y_{1},\dots,u_{r},y_{r})\prod_{i=1}^{r}K(u_{i}-v_{i},y_{i}-z_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\times g(t_{n-r+1},x_{n-r+1},\dots,t_{n+m-2r},x_{n+m-2r},v_{1},z_{1},\dots,v_{r},z_{r}){\mathrm{d}}{{\bm{y}}}{\mathrm{d}}{{\bm{z}}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{u}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{v}},

and we have the following recursive formulas:

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\mathbf{I}_{n}(g)=\sum_{r=0}^{m\land n}r!\binom{n}{r}\binom{m}{r}\mathbf{I}_{m+n-2r}(f\otimes_{r}g).

In particular, when
n=1
we have,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\mathbf{I}_{1}(g)=\mathbf{I}_{m+1}(f\otimes g)+m\mathbf{I}_{m-1}(f\otimes_{1}g).

Square integrable random variables have a unique chaos expansion as stated below.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the
\sigma
-field generated by
W
. Then for any
F\in L^{2}({\Omega},\mathcal{G},P)
, it admits a unique chaos expansion

F=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{I}_{m}(f_{m})~\text{ with }~f_{m}\in\mathscr{H}^{\hat{\otimes}m},

where the series converges in
L^{2}
. Moreover,

{\mathbb{E}}[F^{2}]=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}{m!}\|f_{m}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}.

For
f\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
and
g\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes n}
, the
probabilistic Wick product
(or
Wick product
) of
\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)
and
\mathbf{I}_{n}(g)
is defined by

(2.14)

\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\diamond\mathbf{I}_{n}(g)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\mathbf{I}_{m+n}(f\otimes g).

Unlike the
physical
Wick product, the Wick product defined by (
2.14
) has the same properties as the ordinary product. As an example, by (
2.14
) and (
2.13
), we can show that for
f\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
and
g\in\mathscr{H}
,

\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\diamond\mathbf{I}_{1}(g)=\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)\mathbf{I}_{1}(g)-m\mathbf{I}_{m-1}(f\otimes_{1}g).

For two square integrable random variables
F=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{I}_{m}(f_{m})
and
G=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{I}_{n}(g_{n})
, their Wick product is given by

F\diamond G=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{I}_{m+n}(f_{m}\otimes g_{n}),

as long as the right-hand side is well defined. For instance,
let
\varepsilon(u)
be the
exponential vector
of
u
for
u\in\mathscr{H}
:

\varepsilon(u)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\exp\left(W(u)-\frac{\|u\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}}{2}\right).

Then
\varepsilon(u)\diamond\varepsilon(v)=\varepsilon(u+v)
for
u,v\in\mathscr{H}
. We also recall that for a centered Gaussian random variable
F
, its
Wick exponential
is given by

(2.15)

\exp^{\diamond}(F)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\exp\left(F-\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{E}}[F^{2}]\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}F^{\diamond m}.

As mentioned in Section
A
, the physical and probabilistic Wick products of a Gaussian vector coincide. In particular, for
f_{i}\in\mathscr{H},i=1,\dots,n
, we have

(2.16)

:\!\mathbf{I}_{1}(f_{1})\cdots\mathbf{I}_{1}(f_{n})\!:=\mathbf{I}_{1}(f_{1})\diamond\cdots\diamond\mathbf{I}_{1}(f_{n}).

In contrast to the
multiple Wiener integral
given in (
2.9
), the
multiple Stratonovich integral
of
g^{\otimes m}
for
g\in\mathscr{H}
is defined by (
[
19
,
21
]
):

(2.17)

\mathbb{I}_{m}(g^{\otimes m})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}W(g)^{m}.

By a limiting argument, one can define
\mathbb{I}_{m}(g)
for
g
in some appropriate space. We also take the following notation for
\mathbb{I}_{m}(f)
(in comparison with
\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)
in (
2.11
)):

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{m}(f)=

\displaystyle\int_{([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})^{m}}f({{\bm{t}}},{\bm{x}})W({\mathrm{d}}{t_{1}},{\mathrm{d}}{x_{1}})\cdots W({\mathrm{d}}{t_{m}},{\mathrm{d}}{x_{m}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{([0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}})^{m}}f({{\bm{t}}},{{\bm{x}}})\prod_{i=1}^{m}W({\mathrm{d}}{t_{i}},{\mathrm{d}}{x_{i}}).

For
f\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
, define the
k
th order trace
\mathrm{Tr}^{k}f
of
f
by

(2.18)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}^{k}f(t_{1},x_{1},\dots,t_{m-2k},x_{m-2k})

\displaystyle\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\int_{([0,1]\times\mathbb{R})^{2k}}f(s_{1},y_{1},\dots,s_{2k},y_{2k},t_{1},x_{1},\dots,t_{m-2k},x_{m-2k})

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\times K(s_{1}-s_{2},y_{1}-y_{2})\cdots K(s_{2k-1}-s_{2k},y_{2k-1}-y_{2k}){\mathrm{d}}{{\bm{s}}}{\mathrm{d}}{{\bm{y}}}.

The following Hu-Meyer’s formula (see
[
19
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
27
]
) connects
multiple Stratonovich integrals

\mathbb{I}_{m}(f)
with
multiple Wiener integrals

\mathbf{I}_{m}(f)
,

(2.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}_{m}(f)=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{m}{2}]}

\displaystyle\frac{m!}{k!(m-2k)!2^{k}}\,\mathbf{I}_{m-2k}(\mathrm{Tr}^{k}\hat{f}\,),~~f\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m},

as long as the right-hand side is well-defined, i.e.,
\mathrm{Tr}^{k}\hat{f}\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes(m-2k)}
for
k=0,1,\dots[m/2]
. In this case, we have

(2.20)

\|f\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{m}}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbb{I}_{m}(f)|^{2}]=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{m}{2}]}\frac{1}{(m-2k)!}\left(\frac{m!}{k!2^{k}}\right)^{2}\|\mathrm{Tr}^{k}\hat{f}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes(m-2k)}}.

To end this section, we introduce the stochastic Fubini theorem which will be used in the proof of Proposition
2.10
. Note that stochastic Fubini theorem has been proved in different contexts (see e.g.
[
13
,
29
]
), and here we provide a version working for multiple Wiener and Stratonovich integrals.

Let
(\mathbf{X},\mathcal{X},\mu)
be a measurable space with
\mu(\mathbf{X})<\infty
. For a measurable function
f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we take the notation

\mu(f)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\int_{\mathbf{X}}f(x)\mu({\mathrm{d}}x).

The following result holds for general Gaussian spaces associated with a
separable
Hilbert space.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, let
h:\mathbf{X}\to\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
be a measurable mapping such that

\mu({\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbf{I}_{m}(h)|^{2}])=\mu(\|h\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}})<\infty.

Then the following stochastic Fubini theorem holds,

(2.21)

\mu(\mathbf{I}_{m}(h))=\mathbf{I}_{m}(\mu(h)).

Similarly, if we assume

\mu\left({\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbb{I}_{m}(h)|^{2}]\right)=\mu(\|h\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{m}})=\sum_{k=0}^{[\frac{m}{2}]}\frac{1}{(m-2k)!}\left(\frac{m!}{k!2^{k}}\right)^{2}\mu\left(\|\mathrm{Tr}^{k}\hat{h}\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes(m-2k)}}\right)<\infty,

we also have

(2.22)

\mu(\mathbb{I}_{m}(h))=\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mu(h)).

Proof
Let
\{e_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathscr{H}
. Suppose

h(x)=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}(x)e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{m}}.

Then the condition
\mu(\|h\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}})<\infty
implies
\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\mu(\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}^{2})<\infty
. This further implies

(2.23)

\mu(h)=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\mu(\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}})e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{m}},

where the series on the right-hand side converges in the Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
due to Jensen’s inequality and the assumption
\mu(\mathbf{X})<\infty
.

Consider
g\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
and suppose

g=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}e_{i_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes e_{i_{m}}

with
\|g\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\beta^{2}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}<\infty
. We have

(2.24)

\displaystyle\mu(\langle h,g\rangle_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}})

\displaystyle=\mu\left(\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\beta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\mu(\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}})\beta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}=\langle\mu(h),g\rangle_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}},

where the second equality follows from classical Fubini’s theorem, noting that

\mu\left(\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}|\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\beta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}|\right)\leq\mu\left(\left(\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\alpha_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)\left(\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}\beta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty,

and the last equality follows from (
2.23
).

By (
2.24
) we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\mu(\mathbf{I}_{m}(h))\mathbf{I}_{m}(g)]={\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{I}_{m}(\mu(h))\mathbf{I}_{m}(g)]
for any
g\in\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}
, and thus the desired equality (
2.21
) holds. Finally, equation (
2.22
) follows from (
2.21
) and(
2.19
). ∎

2.2.
Mild Stratonovich solution

In this subsection, we consider the fractional SHE (
1.13
) with initial value
u(0,x)=u_{0}(x),x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We call
\{u(t,x)\}_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}
a
mild Stratonovich solution
of (
1.13
) if
u(t,x)
is an adapted process (with respect to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
where
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(W(s,x),0\leq s\leq t,x\in\mathbb{R})
) such that
{\mathbb{E}}[|u(t,x)|^{2}]<\infty
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
and satisfies the following integral equation

(2.25)

u(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{g}(t,x-y)u_{0}(y){\mathrm{d}}y+{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{g}(t-r,x-y)u(r,y)W({\mathrm{d}}r,{\mathrm{d}}y),

where
\mathfrak{g}(t,x)
is the density function of the
\rho
-stable process
\{X_{t},t\geq 0\}
with
X_{0}=0
and the integral on the right-hand side is understood in the sense of Stratonovich which will be specified below.

Assuming
u_{0}(x)\equiv 1
, we have

(2.26)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=1+{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{g}({t-r},x-y)u(r,y)W({\mathrm{d}}r,{\mathrm{d}}y).

Iterate the equation and formally we have a series expansion for a mild Stratonovich solution:

(2.27)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}{\beta}^{m}\int_{[0,t]_{<}^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathfrak{g}(t_{i+1}-t_{i},x_{i+1}-x_{i})W({\mathrm{d}}t_{i},{\mathrm{d}}x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}{\beta}^{m}\int_{[0,t]^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathfrak{g}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}};t,x)\prod_{i=1}^{m}W({\mathrm{d}}t_{i},{\mathrm{d}}x_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta^{m}\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)),

where

(2.28)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}};t,x)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathfrak{g}(t_{i+1}-t_{i},x_{i+1}-x_{i})\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]^{m}_{<}}(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})

with

[0,t]_{<}^{m}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\{0<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}<t\}\text{ and }t_{m+1}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}t,x_{m+1}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}x,

and on the right-hand side are
multiple Stratonovich integrals
. The series in (
2.27
) converges in
L^{1}(\Omega)
under the condition (
1.15
) (see Proposition
2.12
). We also refer to
[
23
,
42
]
for an equivalent description.

Knowing that
u(t,x)
solves a stochastic heat equation, we now aim to derive a Feynman-Kac type representation for
u(t,x)
. Given a path of the
\rho
-stable process
X
, the following Wiener integral

(2.29)

\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\varepsilon}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\ \int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-r}^{x}-y)W({\mathrm{d}}r,{\mathrm{d}}y),

is well defined, where
X_{s}^{x}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}X_{s}+x
and
p_{\varepsilon}(x)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2\varepsilon}}
is the heat kernel.

As for the discrete model, we use
{\mathbb{E}}_{X}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{W}
to denote the expectations in the probability space of
X
and
W
, respectively, and we abuse the notation
{\mathbb{E}}={\mathbb{E}}_{W}\times{\mathbb{E}}_{X}
in this section.

Proposition 2.5
.

Assume the condition (
1.15
). Then, for each
\varepsilon>0
,
p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)
belongs to
\mathscr{H}
a.s. and the family of random variables
\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\varepsilon}
defined by (
2.29
) converges in
L^{2}
to a
limit denoted by

(2.30)

\mathcal{I}_{t,x}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta(X_{t-r}^{x}-y)W({\mathrm{d}}r,{\mathrm{d}}y),

where
\delta(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)
is an
\mathscr{H}
-valued random variable given by the
L^{2}
-limit of
p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)
. Moreover,
\delta(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)\in L^{2}(\Omega_{X},\mathcal{G},P;\mathscr{H})
, where
(\Omega_{X},\mathcal{G},P)
is the probability space of
X
.

Conditional on
X
,
\mathcal{I}_{t,x}
is a Gaussian random variable with mean
0

and variance

(2.31)

\mathrm{Var}[\mathcal{I}_{t,x}|X]=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|r-s|^{2H-2}\delta(X_{r}-X_{s}){\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}s\,.

Proof
For
\varepsilon,\sigma>0
, by (
2.2
) we have for
t\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\left\langle p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)\mathbf{I}_{[0,t]},p_{{\sigma}}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)\mathbf{I}_{[0,t]}\right\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}

(2.32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s-r|^{2H-2}p_{\varepsilon}(X_{s}^{x}-y)p_{\sigma}(X_{r}^{x}-y){\mathrm{d}}y{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|s-r|^{2H-2}p_{\varepsilon+{\sigma}}(X_{s}-X_{r}){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r.

By (
1.8
), we have

{\mathbb{E}}[p_{\varepsilon+{\sigma}}(X_{s}-X_{r})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{\varepsilon+\sigma}(y)\mathfrak{g}(|r-s|,y){\mathrm{d}}y\lesssim|r-s|^{-1/\rho}.

Thus, the condition (
1.15
) yields

{\mathbb{E}}\left\langle p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot),p_{{\sigma}}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}\lesssim\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|s-r|^{2H-2-\frac{1}{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r<\infty,

hence
p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)
belongs to
\mathscr{H}
for all
\varepsilon>0
almost surely and

(2.33)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\sigma}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left\langle p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot),p_{\sigma}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|s-r|^{2H-2}p_{\varepsilon+{\sigma}}(X_{s}-X_{r}){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r.

As
(\varepsilon,\sigma)\to 0
, by the dominated convergence theorem we have

\displaystyle\lim_{(\varepsilon,\sigma)\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\sigma}]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|s-r|^{2H-2}\delta(X_{s}-X_{r}){\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|s-r|^{2H-2-\frac{1}{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r<\infty

for some proper constant
C
. As a consequence, we have

\lim_{(\varepsilon,\sigma)\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}\Big[\Big(\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{{\varepsilon}}-\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\sigma}\Big)^{2}\Big]=0
.

This implies that, as
\varepsilon
converges to zero,
p_{\varepsilon}(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{2}(\Omega_{X},\mathcal{G},P;\mathscr{H})
, and the limit is denoted by
\delta(X_{t-\cdot}^{x}-\cdot)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{I}_{t,x}^{\varepsilon}
converges to
\mathcal{I}_{t,x}
given in (
2.30
) in
L^{2}
. Finally, (
2.31
) can be proven by a similar argument.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

By the computation above, it is clear that we have

{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathcal{I}_{t,x}|^{2}]=C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{t}|s-r|^{2H-2-\frac{1}{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}}s{\mathrm{d}}r,

which is finite iff (
1.15
) holds. It turns out that (
1.15
) is also sufficient (and necessary, of course) for
{\mathbb{E}}[{\rm e}^{\mathcal{I}_{t,x}}]<\infty
by (
2.31
) and Proposition
2.7
below.

Proposition 2.7
.

Under the condition (
1.15
), we have

(2.34)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\beta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|r-s|^{2H-2}\delta(X_{r}-X_{s}){\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}s\right)\right]<\infty\text{ for all }\beta>0.

Proof
The proof essentially follows from that of
[
42
, Theorem 3.3]
with some minor modifications. Taylor expansion and Fubini’s theorem yield

(2.35)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\beta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|r-s|^{2H-2}\delta(X_{r}-X_{s}){\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}s\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{m}\int_{[0,1]^{2m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|s_{2i}-s_{2i-1}|^{2H-2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm e}^{{\imath}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}(X_{s_{2i}}-X_{s_{2i-1}})}\right]{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{\xi}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\left(\frac{\beta}{2\pi}\right)^{m}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}}\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{2m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|s_{\sigma(2i)}-s_{\sigma(2i-1)}|^{2H-2}

\displaystyle\times{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm e}^{{\imath}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}(X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i)}}-X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i-1)}})}\right]{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{\xi}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}},

where we recall that
[0,1]_{<}^{2m}=\{0<s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots<s_{2m}<1\}
and
\mathcal{P}_{2m}
is the set of all permutations on
\llbracket 2m\rrbracket
. Here we use the fact
\delta(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{{\imath}\xi x}d\xi
. We remark that the integral appearing in (
2.34
) is defined as follows

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|r-s|^{2H-2}\delta(X_{r}-X_{s}){\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}s:=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|r-s|^{2H-2}p_{\varepsilon}(X_{r}-X_{s}){\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}s,

where the limit is taken in
L^{2}
, and the computations in (
2.35
) can be made rigorous by a limiting argument (see, e.g., the proof of
[
42
, Theorem 4.1]
).

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{2m}
be arbitrarily chosen and fixed. Consider
(s_{1},\dots,s_{2m})\in[0,1]^{2m}_{<}
, and for each pair
(s_{\sigma(2i-1)},s_{\sigma(2i)})
, we let
t^{*}_{2i}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}s_{\sigma(2i-1)}\vee s_{\sigma(2i)}
and
t^{*}_{2i-1}
be the unique
s_{j}
which is the closest point to
t^{*}_{2i}
from the left. Then clearly, we have

\prod_{i=1}^{m}|s_{\sigma(2i)}-s_{\sigma(2i-1)}|^{2H-2}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}|t^{*}_{2i}-t^{*}_{2i-1}|^{2H-2}.

Noting that

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}(X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i)}}-X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i-1)}})=\sum_{j=2}^{2m}\eta_{j}(X_{s_{j}}-X_{s_{j-1}})

where each
\eta_{j}
is a linear combination of
\xi_{i}
’s for
i=1,\dots,m.
Then we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm e}^{{\imath}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}(X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i)}}-X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i-1)}})}\right]=\prod_{j=2}^{2m}{\rm e}^{-c_{\rho}|\eta_{j}|^{\rho}(s_{j}-s_{j-1})}\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\rm e}^{-c_{\rho}|\tilde{\eta}_{i}|^{\rho}(t_{2i}^{*}-t_{2i-1}^{*})},

where the inequality holds because we only keep the factors resulting from the characteristic function of
X_{t_{2i}^{*}}-X_{t_{2i-1}^{*}}
for
i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
and drop all the others. Here, for each
i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket
, we define
\tilde{\eta}_{i}=\eta_{j}
where
j
is the unique index such that
s_{j}=t^{*}_{2i}=s_{\sigma(2i)}\vee s_{\sigma(2i-1)}
.

Thus, we have

(2.36)

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{2m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|s_{\sigma(2i)}-s_{\sigma(2i-1)}|^{2H-2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm e}^{{\imath}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}(X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i)}}-X_{s_{{\sigma}(2i-1)}})}\right]{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{\xi}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{2m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|t^{*}_{2i}-t^{*}_{2i-1}|^{2H-2}\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\rm e}^{-c_{\rho}|\tilde{\eta}_{i}|^{\rho}(t^{*}_{2i}-t^{*}_{2i-1})}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{\xi}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=C^{m}\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{2m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|t^{*}_{2i}-t^{*}_{2i-1}|^{2H-2-\frac{1}{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C^{m}}{\Gamma\left(m(2H-\frac{1}{\rho})+1\right)}.

where the equality follows from the fact that the Jacobian determinant
|[\partial\xi_{i}/\partial{\tilde{\eta}_{j}}]_{m\times m}|
is one and that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{-a|\xi|^{\rho}t}{\mathrm{d}}\xi=Ca^{-1/\rho}
for
a>0
, and the last step follows from Lemma
C.3
.

Therefore, by (
2.35
) and (
2.36
), and noting that
|\mathcal{P}_{2m}|=(2m)!
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\beta\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}|r-s|^{2H-2}\delta(X_{r}-X_{s}){\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}s\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}C^{m}\beta^{m}\frac{(2m)!}{m!\Gamma\left(m(2H-\frac{1}{\rho})+1\right)}<\infty,

where the last step follows from Stirling’s formula and
2H-\frac{1}{\rho}>1
.
∎

2.3.
Mild Itô-Skorohod solution

Mild Itô-Skorohod solution is defined similarly as for mild Stratonovich solution in Section
2.2
, and the only difference is that the stochastic integral in (
2.26
) is now understood in the Skorohod sense. More precisely, still assuming
\tilde{u}_{0}(x)\equiv 1
, the Itô-Skorohod solution
\tilde{u}(t,x)
of (
1.13
) satisfies

(2.41)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t,x)=1+{\beta}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathfrak{g}({t-r},x-y)\tilde{u}(r,y)\diamond W({\mathrm{d}}r,{\mathrm{d}}y),

and its chaos expansion is given by

(2.42)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(t,x)=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}{\beta}^{m}\int_{[0,t]^{m}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\mathfrak{g}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}};t,x)W({\mathrm{d}}t_{1},{\mathrm{d}}x_{1})\diamond\cdots\diamond W({\mathrm{d}}t_{m},{\mathrm{d}}x_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta^{m}\mathbf{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)),

where
\mathfrak{g}_{m}
is given in (
2.28
) and the stochastic integrals above are in the Skorohod sense. A sufficient and necessary condition for the existence and uniqueness of the Itô-Skorohod solution is (see, e.g.,
[
22
,
42
]
),

(2.43)

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta^{m}m!\left\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\right\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}<\infty,

where
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}
is the symmetrization of
\mathfrak{g}_{m}
:

(2.44)

\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}};t,x)=\frac{1}{m!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\mathfrak{g}_{m}({\bm{t}}_{\sigma},{\bm{x}}_{\sigma};t,x),

with
{\bm{t}}_{\sigma}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(t_{\sigma(1)},\dots,t_{\sigma(m)})
and
{\bm{x}}_{\sigma}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(m)})
.

By
[
42
, Theorem 5.3]
, there exists a unique mild Itô-Skorohod solution for (
1.13
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

(2.45)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}}\xi<\infty\Longleftrightarrow\rho>d.

Remark 2.13
.

Note that the requirement
\rho\leq 2
together with (
2.45
) forces us to consider the case
d=1
only. By (
2.45
), we also need to assume
\rho>1
in Theorem
1.2
.

We shall provide an alternative proof for (
2.43
) under the condition
H\in(1/2,1],\rho\in(1,2]
, which will be easier to be adapted to estimate the moments of the terms arising from the partition function
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}
(see Remark
2.16
below).

Proposition 2.14
.

Assume
H\in(1/2,1]
and
\theta=H-\frac{1}{2\rho}>0
. Then there exists a constant
C
such that for all
(t,x)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}
,

(2.46)

\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\|_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}\leq C^{m}(m!)^{H-1}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\theta/H)^{m}}{\Gamma(m\theta/H+1)}\right)^{H},

where
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}
is given in (
2.44
). Moreover, (
2.43
) holds under the condition (
1.18
).

Proof
Recalling (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|r_{i}-s_{i}|^{2H-2}\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{r}},{\bm{x}};t,x)\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{s}},{\bm{x}};t,x){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{r}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{H}^{m}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{m}}|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{s}},{\bm{x}};t,x)|^{\frac{1}{H}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}\right)^{2H}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{H}^{m}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{s}},{\bm{x}};t,x)|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2H}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}\right)^{2H}

where the first inequality follows from Lemma
C.2
and the second one follows from the Minkowski inequality. Recalling the definition (
2.44
) of
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}
and the definition (
2.28
) of
\mathfrak{g}_{m}
, we have

\displaystyle|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{s}},{\bm{x}};t,x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}\left|\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\mathfrak{g}_{m}({\bm{s}}_{\sigma},{\bm{x}}_{\sigma};t,x)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}\left|\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathfrak{g}(s_{\sigma(i+1)}-s_{\sigma(i)},x_{\sigma(i+1)}-x_{\sigma(i)})\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]^{m}_{<}}(s_{\sigma(1)},\dots,s_{\sigma(m)})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left|\mathfrak{g}(s_{\sigma(i+1)}-s_{\sigma(i)},x_{\sigma(i+1)}-x_{\sigma(i)})\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]^{m}_{<}}({\bm{s}}_{\sigma}),

where we use the convention
s_{\sigma(m+1)}=s,x_{\sigma(m+1)}=x
, and hence

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{H}^{m}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{m}}(m!)^{-\frac{1}{H}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left|\mathfrak{g}(s_{\sigma(i+1)}-s_{\sigma(i)},x_{\sigma(i+1)}-x_{\sigma(i)})\right|^{2}\mathbf{1}_{[0,1]^{m}_{<}}({\bm{s}}_{\sigma}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2H}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}\right)^{2H}

\displaystyle=C_{H}^{m}\left(\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{m}}(m!)^{1-\frac{1}{H}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|\mathfrak{g}(s_{i+1}-s_{i},x_{i+1}-x_{i})|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2H}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}\right)^{2H},

Noting that by (
1.9
), we have that for all
t>0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathfrak{g}(t,x)|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x\leq Ct^{-\frac{1}{\rho}},

where
C
is a positive constant depending only on
\rho
. Combining the last two estimates together with Lemma
C.3
, we get
the desired inequality (
2.46
).

Finally, by (
2.46
) and Stirling’s formula we get that (
2.43
) holds if
\rho>1
.
∎

Remark 2.15
.

The scaling limit of long-range random walk in i.i.d. random environment was considered in
[
8
]
. In this situation, the condition
\rho>1
is also a necessary condition for (
2.43
). Indeed, now we have

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{s}},{\bm{x}};t,x)|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m!}\int_{[0,1]^{m}_{<}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|\mathfrak{g}(s_{i+1}-s_{i},x_{i+1}-x_{i})|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}.

By the scaling property (
1.8
), we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathfrak{g}(s,x)|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x=As^{-\frac{1}{\rho}},

where
A=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathfrak{g}(1,x)|^{2}{\mathrm{d}}x
is finite by (
1.9
). Thus, we get

\displaystyle\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}=\frac{A^{m}}{m!}\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}(s_{i+1}-s_{i})^{-\frac{1}{\rho}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}},

which is finite only if
\rho>1
. One can also check that (
2.43
) holds by Stirling’s formula if
\rho>1
.

Remark 2.16
.

From the proof of Proposition
2.14
, we can see that the estimation (
2.46
) still holds if we replace
\mathfrak{g}(t,x)
by its upper bound
C(t\|x\|^{-\rho-1})\wedge t^{-1/\rho}
(see (
1.9
)). This fact together with the upper bound for
P_{n}(k)
given in (
1.7
) will be used to estimate the moments of
{\mathbf{S}}_{m}^{(N)}
for the Skorohod case in Section
3.3
.

Remark 2.17
.

Assume
H\in(\frac{1}{2},1]
and
\rho\in(0,2]
. For (
1.13
), by the analysis in this section (or by Theorem 4.6 and Theorem 5.3 in
[
42
]
), we know that the condition for the existence of a Stratonovich solution is
\theta=H-\frac{1}{2\rho}>\frac{1}{2}
and the condition for the Itô-Skorohod case is
\rho>1.
Note that for the Stratonovich case,
\theta>\frac{1}{2}
yields
\rho>\frac{1}{2H-1}\geq 1
and moreover
\theta>\frac{1}{2},\rho\leq 2
implies
H\geq\frac{3}{4}
. Thus, it requires more restrictive condition for the existence of a Stratonovich solution. This is because the
L^{2}
-norm of
\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m})
is strictly bigger than
\mathbf{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m})
due to the extra trace terms appearing in
\|\mathfrak{g}_{m}\|^{2}_{\mathbf{S}_{m}}
(see (
2.20
)).

3.
On weak convergences

In this section, we aim to prove the main results Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
. Recall that
\theta=H-\frac{1}{2\rho}
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, under the scaling
\beta\rightarrow\hat{\beta}_{N}=\beta N^{-\theta}
, the rescaled partition function
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}
given by (
1.1
) is

(3.1)

\displaystyle Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})={\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\exp\left(\hat{\beta}_{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\omega}\left(n,S^{(N+1,k)}_{n}\right)\right)\right],

where
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is fixed such that
S^{(N+1,k)}_{N+1}=N^{1/\rho}x_{0}=k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. To simply the notation, we use
S
to denote the backward random walk
S^{(N+1,k)}
. Taylor expansion yields

(3.2)

\displaystyle Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{(N)},

where
{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{(N)}
is the
m
th moment of the Hamiltonian (with a factor
\hat{\beta}_{N}
) given by

(3.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{(N)}=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\left(\hat{\beta}_{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\omega}(n,S_{n})\right)^{m}\right]=\hat{\beta}_{N}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket^{m}}{\omega}(n_{1},S_{n_{1}})\cdots{\omega}(n_{m},S_{n_{m}})\right].

For every
{\bm{n}}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{m})\in\llbracket N\rrbracket^{m}
, the components can be arranged in an increasing order and the resulted sequence is denoted by
{\bm{n}}^{*}=(n_{1}^{*},\cdots,n_{m}^{*})
with
n^{*}_{1}\leq n^{*}_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n^{*}_{m}
. For each
{\bm{n}}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket^{m}
, there is a permutation
\sigma
of
\llbracket N\rrbracket
such that
n_{i}^{*}=n_{\sigma_{i}}
for
i=1,\dots,m
. Denote

(3.4)

\displaystyle P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}=P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};k_{1},\dots,k_{m})

\displaystyle=P_{n^{*}_{2}-n^{*}_{1}}(k_{\sigma_{2}}-k_{\sigma_{1}})\cdots P_{n_{m}^{*}-n_{m-1}^{*}}(k_{\sigma_{m}}-k_{\sigma_{m-1}})P_{(N+1)-n_{m}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0}-k_{\sigma_{m}}),

where we use the convention
P_{0}(0)=1
and
P_{0}(k)=0
for
k\neq 0
. We remark that
P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}
is
symmetric
in its
m
arguments. The
m
th moment in (
3.3
) can be written as

(3.5)

\displaystyle{{\mathbb{S}}}_{m}^{(N)}=\hat{\beta}^{m}_{N}\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\omega}(n_{1},k_{1})\cdots{\omega}(n_{m},k_{m})P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};k_{1},\dots,k_{m}),

recalling that
S
is a backward random walk with
S_{N+1}=N^{1/\rho}x_{0}
.

A similar calculation can be done for the partition function
\tilde{\mathbb{Z}}_{\omega}^{(N)}
given in (
1.16
). Note that the exponential is actually a Wick exponential of
\beta\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\omega}(i,S_{i})
conditional on the random walk
S
. Hence, applying (
2.15
), we get the following resemblance of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) respectively,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbf{S}}_{m}^{(N)},

where

(3.7)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{S}}_{m}^{(N)}=

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\left(\hat{\beta}_{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\omega}(n,S_{n})\right)^{\diamond m}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{N}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}_{S}\left[\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket^{m}}:\!{\omega}(n_{1},S_{n_{1}})\cdots{\omega}(n_{m},S_{n_{m}})\!:\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta^{m}N^{-m(\theta+\frac{1}{\rho})}\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}:\!{\omega}(n_{1},k_{1})\cdots{\omega}(n_{m},k_{m})\!:

\displaystyle\times\left(N^{m/\rho}P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};k_{1},\dots,k_{m})\right).

Now, we introduce the following general U-statistics
\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)
which will be proven to converge weakly to a multiple Wiener integral. For
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}
where we recall that
\mathcal{B}
is given in (
2.3
), we denote

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}^{(N)}_{m}(f)

\displaystyle\overset{\rm{def}}{=}N^{-m(\theta+\frac{1}{\rho})}\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{m}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k_{1},\dots,k_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\bigg[:\!{\omega}(n_{1},k_{1})\cdots{\omega}(n_{m},k_{m})\!:

(3.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(\frac{n_{1}}{N},\frac{k_{1}}{N^{1/\rho}},\cdots,\frac{n_{m}}{N},\frac{k_{m}}{N^{1/\rho}}\right)\bigg]

\displaystyle\overset{\rm{def}}{=}N^{-m(\theta+\frac{1}{\rho})}\sum_{{\bm{n}}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket^{m}}\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}:\!{\omega}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!:\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}}),

where
:\!{\omega}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!:\overset{\rm{def}}{=}:\!{\omega}_{1}\cdots{\omega}_{m}\!:
is the physical Wick product of
{\omega}_{i}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}{\omega}(n_{i},k_{i})
,
{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}
is given in (
2.5
), and

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{t}}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{m})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}{\bm{n}}/N=(n_{1}/N,\cdots,n_{m}/N),

\displaystyle{\displaystyle\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}{\bm{k}}/N^{\frac{1}{\rho}}=(k_{1}/N^{\frac{1}{\rho}},\cdots,k_{m}/N^{\frac{1}{\rho}}).

In order to write
\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}
defined in (
3.7
) in the form of
\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)
given in (
3
), it suffices to extend the domain of the following function of
({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}})

N^{m/\rho}P_{N{\bm{t}}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};N^{1/\rho}x_{1},\dots,N^{1/\rho}x_{m}):=N^{m/\rho}P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};k_{1},\dots,k_{m})

to the whole
[0,1]_{<}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
in a natural way, i.e., we define

(3.10)

\tilde{P}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}})\overset{\rm{def}}{=}N^{m/\rho}P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}(N^{1/\rho}x_{0};k_{1},\dots,k_{m}),\text{ if }({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}})\in\Big[\frac{{{\bm{n}}^{*}}}{N},\frac{{{\bm{n}}^{*}}+\overrightarrow{1}_{m}}{N}\Big)\times\Big[\frac{{{\bm{k}}}}{N^{1/\rho}},\frac{{{\bm{k}}}+\overrightarrow{1}_{m}}{N^{1/\rho}}\Big),

In this way, we can write
\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}=\beta^{m}\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(\tilde{P}_{m})
.

The rest of this Section is organized as follows. In Section
3.1
, we prove the joint weak convergence for
U
-statistics
\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)
defined by (
3
). The joint weak convergence of
m
th moment
{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{(N)}
(resp.
\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}
) appearing in the rescaled partition function
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})
(resp.
(\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})
) is established in Section
3.2
. The
L^{p}
-bounds of
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)
and
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)
are obtained in Section
3.3
.

3.1.
Weak convergence of U-statistics

We first introduce the following lemma which provides a uniform (in
N
) bound for the second moment of
\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)
with
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}
, which will be used to prove Propositions
3.2
and
3.3
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume the disorder
{\omega}
is given as in Section
1.2
. Then, there exists a constant
C
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}
and
m,N\in\mathbb{N}
,

(3.11)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]\leq C^{m}m!\|\hat{f}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}},

where
\hat{f}
is the symmetrization of
f
.

Proof
For
m=1
, we have

(3.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]\\
=&N^{-2(\theta+1/\rho)}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\mathbb{E}}[{\omega}(n,k){\omega}(n^{\prime},k^{\prime})]{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(\frac{n}{N},\frac{k}{N^{1/\rho}}\right){\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(\frac{n^{\prime}}{N},\frac{k^{\prime}}{N^{1/\rho}}\right)\\
=&N^{-2(\theta+1/\rho)}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\gamma(n-n^{\prime}){\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(\frac{n}{N},\frac{k}{N^{1/\rho}}\right){\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(\frac{n^{\prime}}{N},\frac{k}{N^{1/\rho}}\right).\end{split}

For
t\in\mathbb{R}
, let

(3.13)

\gamma_{N}(t)\overset{\rm{def}}{=}N^{2-2H}\gamma\left(\left[|t|N\right]\right).

Then, by (
1.11
) and (
1.12
), we have for all
t\in\mathbb{R}

(3.14)

\gamma_{N}(t)\lesssim|t|^{2H-2}\text{ and }\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\gamma_{N}(t)=|t|^{2H-2}.

Then, using the notations
t=n/N,t^{\prime}=n^{\prime}/N,x=k/N^{1/\rho}
, we have

(3.15)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=N^{-2-1/\rho}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\in\llbracket N\rrbracket}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}N^{2-2H}\gamma\left(n-n^{\prime}\right){\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(t,x\right){\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\left(t^{\prime},x\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|t-t^{\prime}|^{2H-2}\left|{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}(t,x){\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}(t^{\prime},{x})\right|{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}t{\mathrm{d}}t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\|{\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}}\lesssim\|{f}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}},

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
2.2
.

Recall that
\theta=H-\frac{1}{2\rho}
given in (
1.15
). For general
m
,

(3.16)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]=N^{-2m(\theta+1/\rho)}\sum_{{\bm{n}},{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}\sum_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[:\!{\omega}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!::\!{\omega}^{\prime}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!:\right]\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}})\mathcal{A}_{N}(f)({\bm{t}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}),

where
:\!{\omega}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!:=:\!\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\omega}(n_{i},k_{i})\!:
and similarly for
:\!{\omega}^{\prime}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!:
.

Recalling that we have assumed that
{\omega}
is Gaussian, then by (
A.8
) we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[:\!{\omega}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!::\!{\omega}^{\prime}_{\llbracket m\rrbracket}\!:\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[:\!\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\omega}(n_{i},k_{i})\!::\!\prod_{i=1}^{m}{\omega}(n_{i}^{\prime},k_{i}^{\prime})\!:\right]=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\gamma(n_{i}-n_{\sigma(i)}^{\prime})\delta_{k_{i}k^{\prime}_{\sigma(i)}},

where the summation
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{m}}
is taken over the set
\mathcal{P}_{m}
of all the permutations of
\llbracket m\rrbracket
. Note that by the symmetry of the summation
\sum_{{\bm{n}},{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}\sum_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}
, we have
\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)=\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(\hat{f})
. Thus, by the symmetry of
\mathcal{A}_{N}(\hat{f})
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(\hat{f})\right)^{2}\right]=m!\,N^{-2m(\theta+1/\rho)}\sum_{{\bm{n}},{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}\sum_{{\bm{k}}}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}\gamma(n_{i}-n_{i}^{\prime})\right)\mathcal{A}_{N}(\hat{f})({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}})\mathcal{A}_{N}(\hat{f})({\bm{t}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}}).

Then, similarly to the case
m=1
, by Lemma
2.2
we can get
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]\leq C^{m}\|\hat{f}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{B}^{\otimes m}}.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove the weak convergence for
\mathbf{I}_{m}^{(N)}(f)
defined in (
3
). We first prove the weak convergence for
m=1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function in
\mathcal{B}
. Then,
\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f)
converges weakly to the Wiener integral
\mathbf{I}_{1}(f)
as
N\to\infty
. Moreover, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
, we have the joint convergence in distribution:

(3.17)

\left(\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f_{k})\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\left(\mathbf{I}_{1}(f_{1}),\dots,\mathbf{I}_{1}(f_{k})\right),\text{ as }N\to\infty.

Proof
First we consider an indicator function
f={\mathbf{1}}_{[s,t]\times[y,z]}
with
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
and
y\leq z
. In this case,

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f)=N^{-\theta-1/\rho}\sum_{n\in[Ns,Nt]\cap\mathbb{N}}~\sum_{k\in[N^{1/\rho}y,N^{1/\rho}z]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}{\omega}(n,k).

Clearly it has mean zero and the covariance is

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=N^{-2-1/\rho}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}\in[Ns,Nt]\cap\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k\in[N^{1/\rho}y,N^{1/\rho}z]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}}\gamma_{N}(r-r^{\prime}),

where
\gamma_{N}
is given by (
3.13
) and
r=n/N,r^{\prime}=n^{\prime}/N
. By (
3.14
) and the dominated convergence theorem, we get

(3.18)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left(\mathbf{I}_{1}^{(N)}(f)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{y}^{z}|r-r^{\prime}|^{2H-2}{\mathrm{d}}x{\mathrm{d}}r{\mathrm{d}}r^{\prime}=\|f\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}}

where we recall that the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}
is given in (
2.2
). Thus, we have

Similarly, one can show that (

The main result in this section is presented below.

Proposition 3.3

and

. For such

\displaystyle:\!\Big({\mathbf{I}}_{1}(h)\Big)^{m}\!:=\mathbf{I}_{m}(h^{\otimes m})=\mathbf{I}_{m}(f).

\textstyle{\mathbf{I}_{m}(f^{(n)})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{n\to\infty}
\scriptstyle{\mathrm{in\ }L^{2}}
\textstyle{\mathbf{I}_{m}(f))}

3.2.

In this section, we prove Theorem

Recall that the so-called

Proposition 3.4

Assume condition (

Proof

sufficiently large, it suffices to find a uniform (in N) upper bound for

(3.34)

A\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\int_{[0,1]^{2(m-2j)}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-2j}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-2j}|t_{i}-t_{i}^{\prime}|^{2H-2}\mathrm{Tr}^{j}\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}};1,x_{0})\mathrm{Tr}^{j}\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}({\bm{t}}^{\prime},{\bm{x}};1,x_{0}){\mathrm{d}}{\bm{t}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{t}}^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

and

(3.35)

B\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\int_{[0,1]^{2(m-2j)}\times\mathbb{R}^{m-2j}}\prod_{i=1}^{m-2j}|t_{i}-t_{i}^{\prime}|^{2H-2}\mathcal{Q}_{N}\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}_{N}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{t}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{t}}^{\prime}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{x}}

where
\mathcal{Q}_{N}
is given in (
3.33
) and
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}^{\prime}_{N}=\mathcal{Q}_{N}({\bm{n}}^{\prime},{\bm{k}}).
This is true, noting that Remark
2.11
yields

A=\frac{1}{(m-2j)!}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathbf{I}_{m-2j}(\mathrm{Tr}^{j}\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m})\right|^{2}\right]\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m})\right|^{2}\right]<\infty,

and similarly, recalling that
\mathbb{S}_{m}^{(N)}
is given in (
3.5
), we have
B\lesssim{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathbb{S}_{m}^{(N)}|^{2}]
which has a uniform upper bound by (
3.39
).

In the above, we have assumed
m
is an odd integer. If
m
is even, the analysis is the same except for the case
j=\frac{m}{2}
.
In this case, we have

\displaystyle Y_{N}=N^{-m\theta}\sum_{n_{i}:i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket}\sum_{k_{i}:i\in\llbracket m\rrbracket}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m/2}{\mathbb{E}}[{\omega}_{2\ell-1}{\omega}_{2\ell}](P_{{\bm{n}}^{*}}/m!)-\mathrm{Tr}^{m/2}\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m},

which is deterministic and converges to 0 by the local limit theorem and a similar argument proving
\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{N}^{2}]=0
for
j=0
.

Finally, one can prove the weak convergence for linear combinations of
\left(\mathbb{S}_{l_{1}}^{(N)},\dots,\mathbb{S}_{l_{k}}^{(N)}\right)
in a similar way, and hence (
3.22
) holds due to Theorem
B.1
. ∎

By the proof of Proposition
3.4
, in particular the part proving (
3.27
) for
j=0
under the condition
H\in(1/2,1]
and
\rho\in(1,2]
, we can get a parallel result for the Itô-Skorohod case which is stated below. Recall that
\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}
is given in (
3.7
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume
H\in(1/2,1],\rho\in(1,2]
. For each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbf{S}}_{m}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\beta^{m}\mathbf{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m}),\text{ as }N\to\infty,

where
\mathfrak{g}_{m}=\mathfrak{g}_{m}({\bm{t}},{\bm{x}};1,x_{0})
is given in (
2.28
) and
\mathbf{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m})
is an
m
th multiple Wiener integral. Moreover, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
l_{1},\dots,l_{k}\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the joint convergence in distribution:

\left(\frac{1}{l_{1}!}\mathbf{S}_{l_{1}}^{(N)},\dots,\frac{1}{l_{k}!}\mathbf{S}_{l_{k}}^{(N)}\right)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\Big(\beta^{l_{1}}\mathbf{I}_{l_{1}}(\mathfrak{g}_{l_{1}}),\dots,\beta^{l_{k}}\mathbf{I}_{l_{k}}(\mathfrak{g}_{l_{k}})\Big),\text{ as }N\to\infty.

Now we are ready to prove our main results.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.
By Proposition
3.4
, we have for all
M\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle Z_{\omega}^{(N,M)}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbb{S}}_{m}^{(N)}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{Z}^{(M)}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\sum_{m=0}^{M}\beta^{m}\mathbb{I}_{m}(\mathfrak{g}_{m}(\cdot;1,x_{0})),\text{ as }N\to\infty.

Recalling Proposition
2.12
which yields the
L^{1}
-convergence of
\mathcal{Z}^{(M)}
to
\mathcal{Z}
as
M\to\infty
, we only need to show that
Z_{\omega}^{(N,M)}
converges to
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}=Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},N^{1/\rho}x_{0})
given in (
3.2
) in probability uniformly in
N
as

by Lemma

. This follows from (
3.40
) obtained in Section
3.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

except that Proposition
3.4
and (

and (

), respectively.

3.3.
L^{p}
-bounds of rescaled partition functions

In this section, we study the
L^{p}
-bounds of
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)
and
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)
.
We first deal with the Stratonovich case to find
L^{1}
-bound of
Z_{\omega}^{(N)}(\hat{\beta}_{N},k)
, and then as a consequence we obtain the
L^{2}
-bound for the Itô-Skorohod case.

Recalling that
{\omega}
is Gaussian, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(N^{-\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\omega}(n,S_{n})\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(N^{-\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\omega}(n,k)\mathbf{1}_{[S_{n}=k]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}N^{-2\theta}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\gamma(n-n^{\prime})\delta_{kk^{\prime}}\mathbf{1}_{[S_{n}=k]}1_{[S_{n^{\prime}}=k^{\prime}]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}N^{-2\theta}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\gamma(n-n^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{[S_{n}=S_{n^{\prime}}]}\right)\right].

In this section, we shall prove a discretised version of Proposition
2.7
, i.e., to show that the above exponential moment is uniformly bounded. As a consequence, we get a discretised version of Proposition
2.12
(see eq. (
3.40
)).

Noting that
S_{n}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and using the identity

\mathbf{1}_{[S_{n}=S_{n^{\prime}}]}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}{\rm e}^{{\imath}(S_{n}-S_{n^{\prime}})\lambda}{\mathrm{d}}\lambda,

we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}N^{-2\theta}\sum_{n,n^{\prime}=1}^{N}\gamma(n-n^{\prime})\mathbf{1}_{[S_{n}=S_{n^{\prime}}]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}(4\pi)^{-m}N^{-2m\theta}\sum_{n_{1},\dots,n_{m}=1}^{N}\sum_{n_{1}^{\prime},\dots,n^{\prime}_{m}=1}^{N}\int_{[-\pi,\pi]^{m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\gamma(n_{i}-n_{i}^{\prime}){\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm e}^{{\imath}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(S_{n_{i}}-S_{n_{i}^{\prime}})\lambda_{i}}\right]{\mathrm{d}}\bm{\lambda}.

Using changes of variables
n_{i}=Nt_{i},n_{i}^{\prime}=Nt_{i}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{i}=u_{i}/N^{1/\rho}
for
i=1,\dots,m
, we have for each term on the right-hand side of the above equation,

(3.36)

\displaystyle A_{m}\overset{\rm{def}}{=}\frac{1}{m!}(4\pi)^{-m}N^{-2m}\sum_{{\bm{n}},{\bm{n}}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left[N^{2-2H}\gamma(N(t_{i}-t_{i}^{\prime}))\right]

\displaystyle\times\int_{[-\pi N^{1/\rho},\pi N^{1/\rho}]^{m}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\rm e}^{{\imath}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(S_{n_{i}}-S_{n_{i}^{\prime}})u_{i}/N^{1/\rho}}\right]{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{u}}.

Recall that
S_{n}=S_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}
and that
\psi(u)={\mathbb{E}}[{\rm e}^{{\imath}uY_{j}}]
is the characteristic function of
Y_{j}
.
The 1-lattice distribution of
Y_{j}
implies that, for any
\varepsilon>0
, one can find a positive constant
c
such that (see
[
38
, eq. (5.14)]
)

(3.37)

|\psi(u/N^{1/\rho})|\leq{\rm e}^{-c|u|^{\rho-\varepsilon}/N},\text{ for }1\leq|u|\leq\pi N^{1/\rho}.

Thus, for
n\in\llbracket N\rrbracket
, we have

(3.38)

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi N^{1/\rho}}^{\pi N^{1/\rho}}\left|\psi(u/N^{1/\rho})\right|^{n}{\mathrm{d}}u

\displaystyle\leq 2+\int_{\{1\leq|u|\leq\pi N^{1/\rho}\}}\left|\psi(u/N^{1/\rho})\right|^{n}{\mathrm{d}}u

\displaystyle\leq 2+\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm e}^{-c|u|^{\rho-\varepsilon}n/N}{\mathrm{d}}u

\displaystyle\leq C(n/N)^{-\frac{1}{\rho-\varepsilon}}.

Under the condition (
1.15
), we may choose
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
2H-\frac{1}{\rho-\varepsilon}>1
. To estimate
A_{m}
given in (
3.36
), we combine the estimate (
3.38
) with the argument used in the proof of Proposition
2.7
which leads to (
2.36
), and we can get for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle A_{m}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(2m)!}{m!}C^{m}\int_{[0,1]_{<}^{2m}}\prod_{i=1}^{m}|t^{*}_{2i}-t^{*}_{2i-1}|^{2H-2-\frac{1}{\rho-\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}}{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{(2m)!C^{m}}{m!\Gamma\left(m(2H-\frac{1}{\rho-\varepsilon})+1\right)}

Therefore, we have uniformly in
N
,

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}A_{m}\leq\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2m)!C^{m}}{m!\Gamma\left(m(2H-\frac{1}{\rho-\varepsilon})+1\right)}<\infty,

where the finiteness follows from Stirling’s formula and
2H-\frac{1}{\rho-\varepsilon}>1
. This implies

(3.39)

\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\exp\left(N^{-\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\omega}(n,S_{n})\right)\right]<\infty.

Finally, the above analysis also yields

(3.40)

\lim_{M\to\infty}\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{m!}\left|N^{-\theta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\omega}(n,S_{n})\right|^{m}\right]=0,

which shall be used to prove the weak convergence of rescaled partition functions.

Now we consider the Itô-Skorohod case under the condition (
1.18
). Recall the partition function
\tilde{Z}_{\omega}^{(N)}
in (
3.6
). Noting that
\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}
and
\mathbf{S}_{n}^{(N)}
given in (
3.7
) are orthogonal in
L^{2}(\Omega)
if
m\neq n
, we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbf{S}}_{m}^{(N)}\right|^{2}\right]=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}\right|^{2}\right].

Applying (
A.8
), one can calculate
\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\mathbf{S}_{m}^{(N)}\right|^{2}\right]
to get the same upper bound uniformly in
N
(up to a multiplicative constant
C^{m}
) as
m!\|\hat{\mathfrak{g}}_{m}(\cdot;t,x)\|^{2}_{\mathscr{H}^{\otimes m}}
(see (
2.46
) in Proposition
2.14
and see also Remark
2.16
).
Thus, assuming (
1.18
) we have

(3.41)

\lim_{M\to\infty}\sup_{N\in\mathbb{N}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left|\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}{\mathbf{S}}_{m}^{(N)}\right|^{2}\right]=0.
