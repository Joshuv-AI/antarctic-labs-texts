Kernelizing Problems on Planar Graphs in Sublinear Space and Polynomial Time
Arindam Biswas, Johannes Meintrup

Abstract
In this paper, we devise a scheme for kernelizing, in sublinear space and polynomial time, various problems on planar graphs. The scheme exploits planarity to ensure that the resulting algorithms run in polynomial time and use O ⁡ ( ( n + k ) ​ log ⁡ n ) \ohop\left((\sqrt{n}+k)\log{n}\right) bits of space, where n n is the number of vertices in the input instance and k k is the intended solution size. As examples, we apply the scheme to Dominating Set and Vertex Cover . For Dominating Set , we also show that a well-known kernelization algorithm due to Alber et al. (JACM 2004) can be carried out in polynomial time and space O ⁡ ( k ​ log ⁡ n ) \ohop\left(k\log{n}\right) . Along the way, we devise restricted-memory procedures for computing region decompositions and approximating the aforementioned problems, which might be of independent interest.

Introduction

With the rise of big data and massive distributed systems, space requirements of algorithms are becoming a principal concern. In such scenarios, the size of the problem instance at hand may be too large to store in volatile memory.
In this paper, we tackle the issue on two fronts using the kernelization approach: first, the kernelization itself is sublinear-space, for appropriate ranges of the parameter, and second, the kernel produced has size independent of the instance size.
Intuitively, a kernelization algorithm shrinks a given
large problem instance to an equivalent (but smaller) instance using a set of
reduction rules such that it can subsequently be solved using a
standard algorithm. We consider a setting were we measure our resources using
two parameters: the size
n
of the input, and an additional problem-dependent
parameter
k
. Our kernelization algorithms shrink a given problem instance of
size
n
to an instance of linear size in
k
, using polynomial time and sublinear space.
Roughly speaking, we try to minimize the dependence of the resource costs on
n
, but with the tradeoff
of increasing the dependence on
k
. Such parameterized algorithms
tend to be of interest in the case were one has a value of
k
that is much smaller
than
n
: the problems we consider are
NP
-hard to solve in the general case, but for smallish parameter values, they can be solved in subexponential time.

We devise kernelization and approximation algorithms for
Dominating Set
and
Vertex Cover
on planar graphs under space restrictions. In particular, we try to maintain the polynomial (or FPT) running time of standard (i.e. without a space restriction) algorithms for the
problems we consider, while trying to bring down the space requirements as much
as possible. We consider two well-known techniques specific to planar graphs, and show how they can be realized in less-than-linear space. The first is a polynomial-time approximation scheme (PTAS) for planar graphs due to Baker
[
Bak1994JACM
]

(commonly referred to as
Baker’s technique
), and the second is a polynomial-time kernelization strategy for
Dominating Set

[
AFN2004JACM
]
(later generalized to a scheme by Guo et al.
[
GN2007ICALP
]
) on planar graphs that produces kernels of linear size relative to the parameter. We consider our technique to be of general interest, as it is an interesting use of so-called region decompositions (outlined in later sections), which comprise small "regions" in a planar graph that satisfy certain conditions.
These regions have mostly been used as an analysis
tool in the design of kernelization algorithms for planar graphs. Indeed, Guo et al.
[
GN2007ICALP
]
devised a scheme for designing such algorithms
that works by first applying problem-specific reduction rules, and then using region decompositions to show that the reduction rules produce (linear) kernels. To our knowledge, the only work that also directly uses region decompositions directly in the kernelization algorithm (and not just in the analysis), is the
Connected Dominating Set
kernelization algorithm of Lokshtanov et al.
[
LMS2011TCS
]
. Our work can thus be seen as a novel
application of region decompositions. We believe our technique can be used to devise further kernelization algorithms for planar graphs that use sublinear space.

Related Work.
The research on space-efficient parameterized problems
was arguably kickstarted by Cai et al.
[
CCDF1997AnnPureApplLogic
]
, who introduced
parameterized complexity classes now commonly referred to as
para
-
L
and

XL
. For example, the well-studied
Vertex Cover
problem (parameterized by solution size) is contained in

para
-
L
. In the same vein, Flum and Grohe
[
FG2003InfComput
]
showed that parameterized model-checking problems
expressible in first-order logic are in
para
-
L
. Later, Elberfeld et al.
[
EJT2010FOCS
]
showed that
Courcelle’s Theorem

[
Cou1990InfComput
]
, which concerns solving monadic second order problems on graphs with constant (or bound) treewidth, can be realized in logarithmic space.

As regards kernelization results, the work of Alber et al.
[
AFN2004JACM
]
prompted various follow-up results, such as computing a linear kernel in linear time
[
Hag2011IPEC
,
vHK+2012IPEC
]
, and linear kernels for
Dominating Set
-variants on planar graphs
[
FK2015MFCS
,
GST2017DAM
,
LWF+2013TCS
]
. For other graph classes, e.g. bounded-diameter graphs, results regarding
Dominating Set
kernels exist as well
[
BSBB2014EJOR
,
FLST2013STACS
,
GRS2012TALG
]
.

The Model

We work with the standard RAM model and count space in bits, i.e. the space bounds in our results are in bit units. We also impose a space constraint: an algorithm can only use
\lohop\left(n\right)
bits of work memory on inputs of size
n
. The algorithm has read-only access to its input; this ensures that the input may not be used as some kind of read-write memory, for example, using non-destructive rearrangement (see Chan et al.
[
CMR2018TALG
]
). The amount of read-write memory available to the algorithm is
\lohop\left(n\right)
bits in size, and we assume that output is written to a stream, i.e. items cannot be read back from the output. This ensures that the output can also not be used to store information to be used by the algorithm while it runs.

All in all, ignoring polynomial-time computational overheads, our model is equivalent to a Turing machine with a read-only input tape, a write-only output tape and a read-write tape of length
\lohop\left(n\right)
.

Sublinearity.
The space costs our algorithms on
n
-vertex planar graphs have a
k\log{n}
component, which becomes linear for
k=\Omega(n/\log{n})
. However, for such large parameter values, it is probably more profitable to use exponential-time algorithms. For parameter values
k=\ohop\left(n^{\epsilon}\right)
for some
\epsilon<1
, our algorithms become non-trivial: they use space
\ohop\left((\sqrt{n}+n^{\epsilon})\log{n}\right)
and additionally only require FPT time.

Difficulties.
It is pertinent to note that the restriction on available work memory makes even primitive operations difficult to carry out in polynomial time. For example, the simple polynomial-time operation of computing
maximal independent sets
in graphs becomes difficult to perform in sublinear space: no general space-constrained algorithm for this task currently exists. On the other hand, the hard problem of computing
dominating sets in tournaments
can be solved in space
\ohop\left(\log^{2}{n}\right)
, but the problem cannot be solved in polynomial time, unless the exponential time hypothesis (ETH)
[
IP1999CCC
]
is false. The catch is to solve the problems simultaneously in polynomial time and sublinear space.

Preliminaries

We use the following standard notation and concepts. The set
\left\{0,1,\dotsc\right\}
of natural numbers is denoted by
\mathbb{N}
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
[n]
denotes the set
\left\{1,2,\dotsc,n\right\}
. For a graph
G
, we denote by
\Vop\left(G\right)
the vertex set, and by
\Eop\left(G\right)
the edge set.

2.1
Region Decompositions

The problems we consider satisfy a certain
distance property
: there are constants
c_{V}
and
c_{E}
such that for any input graph
G
and a solution set
S
, the following conditions are satisfied.

•

For every
v\in\Vop\left(G\right)
, there is a vertex
w\in S
such that
\operatorname{d}(u,w)\leq c_{V}
.

•

For every edge
uv\in\Eop\left(G\right)
, there is a vertex
w\in S
such that
\min\left\{\operatorname{d}(u,w),\operatorname{d}(v,w)\right\}\leq c_{E}
.

Remark 1
.

The distance property is satisfied by a number for problems. For
Dominating Set
, the constants are
c_{V}=c_{E}=1
, and for
Vertex Cover
, the constants are
c_{V}=1
and
c_{E}=0
.

Now suppose we have an embedding of
G
in the plane. With respect to the constants
c_{V}
and
c_{E}
, we consider
region decompositions
.

Definition 2

(Guo & Niedermeier
[
GN2007ICALP
]
, Definition 2)
.

A
region

R(u,v)
between two distinct vertices
u,v\in S
for any
S\subseteq V
and constants
c_{V},c_{E}
is a closed subset of the plane with the
following properties:

•

The
boundary
of
R(u,v)
is formed by two (not necessarily
disjoint or simple) length-at-most-
(c_{V}+c_{E}+1)
paths between
u
and
v
.

•

All vertices which lie on the boundary or strictly inside the region

R(u,v)
have distance at most
c_{V}
to at least one of the vertices u and v
and all

•

Except
u
and
v
, none of the vertices which lie inside the region

R(u,v)
are from
S
.

The vertices
u
and
v
are called the anchor vertices of
R(u,v)
. A vertex
is
inside of
R(u,v)
if it is a boundary vertex of
R(u,v)
or lies strictly inside
R(u,v)
.
We use
V(R(u,v))
to denote the set of
vertices that lie inside a region
R(u,v)
.

Definition 3

(Guo & Niedermeier
[
GN2007ICALP
]
, Definition 3)
.

An
S
-region decomposition of an embedded planar graph
G=(V,E)
for any
S\subseteq V
and constants
c_{V},c_{E}
is a set
\mathcal{R}
of regions such that there is no vertex that lies strictly inside more than one region of
\mathcal{R}
(the boundaries of regions may overlap). For an
S
-region decomposition
\mathcal{R}
, let
V(\mathcal{R}):=\bigcup_{R\in\mathcal{R}}V(R)
. An
S
-region decomposition
\mathcal{R}
is called maximal if there is no region
R\notin\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{R^{\prime}}:=\mathcal{R}\cup R
is an
S
-region decomposition with
V(\mathcal{R})\subsetneq V(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})
.

It is known that the number of regions in a maximal region decomposition is upper-bounded by
c_{V}(3\left\lvert S\right\rvert-6)
.

Proposition 4

(Guo and Niedermeier
[
GN2007ICALP
]
, Lemma 1)
.

For the appropriate problem-dependent constant
c_{V}
, any maximal
S
-region decomposition of
G
contains at most
c_{V}(3\left\lvert S\right\rvert-6)
regions.

Maximal region decompositions can be constructed in linear time
[
GN2007ICALP
]
. In Section
, we devise an algorithm that constructs region decompositions in polynomial time and space
\ohop\left((c_{V}+c_{E})\left\lvert S\right\rvert\log n\right)
.

Approximating
Dominating Set
and
Vertex Cover
on planar graphs in sublinear space

In this section, we develop
(1+\epsilon)
-approximation schemes for
Dominating Set
and
Vertex Cover
that run in time
n^{\ohop\left(1/\epsilon\right)}
and space
\ohop\left(((1/\epsilon)\log{n}+\sqrt{n})\log{n}\right)
on
n
-vertex planar graphs. Later on, we use the schemes to compute
2
-approximate solutions for these problems, to be used by the region decomposition algorithm of Section
.

The following well-known result shows how
Dominating Set
and

can be approximated when there is no restriction on space.

Proposition 5

(Baker
[
Bak1994JACM
]
, A1)
.

For any
0<\epsilon<1
, one can
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate
Dominating Set
and

on
n
-vertex planar graphs in time
f(1/\epsilon)\,n^{\ohop\left(1\right)}
, where
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{N}
is a computable, increasing function.

The main result of this section provides a restricted-memory equivalent of the above proposition.

Theorem 6
.

For any
0<\epsilon<1
, one can
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate
Dominating Set
and
Vertex Cover
on
n
-vertex planar graphs in space
\ohop\left(((1/\epsilon)\log{n}+\sqrt{n})\log{n}\right)
and time
n^{\ohop\left(1/\epsilon\right)}

In what follows, we describe our approach for
Dominating Set
, which can be readily adapted to
Vertex Cover
. One only needs to make slight changes to the procedures in Lemmas
10
and
. We defer the details to the full version of this paper, but the reader could briefly consult
[
Bak1994JACM

To begin with, we show how to decompose an input planar graph
G
into subgraphs that together cover all of
G
. For this, we need the following components.

Proposition 7

(Chakraborty and Tewari
[
CT2015report
]
, Theorem 1)
.

One can compute a BFS traversal for any
n
-vertex planar graph in polynomial time and space
\ohop\left(\sqrt{n}\log{n}\right)
.

Remark 8
.

Theorem 1 in
[
CT2015report
]
shows how to compute distances between vertices in planar graphs, but such a procedure can be used in a straightforward manner (with polynomial time and logarithmic space overheads) to produce BFS traversals as well.

Proposition 9

(Rei2008JACM)
.

One can determine whether two vertices in a graph belong to the same connected component in polynomial time and logarithmic space.

The procedure in the lemma below performs a BFS traversal to organize
G
—trivially connecting all components using a single vertex—into levels
L_{1},\dotsc,L_{h}
and then produces subgraphs
G_{i}
(
i\in[l+2]
) which roughly correspond to
k
(
k\in\mathbb{N}
, freely selectable) consecutive levels of the BFS traversal.

Lemma 10
.

Let
1\leq j\leq d<h
be arbitrary and set
l=\left\lfloor(h-j)/d\right\rfloor
. One can compute a sequence
G_{1},\dotsc,G_{l+2}
of subgraphs of
G
such that

•

G_{1}=G[L_{1}\cup\dotsb L_{j}]
,

•

for
1<i<l+2
,
G_{i}=G[L_{j+(i-2)d}\cup\dotsb\cup L_{j+(i-1)d}]
, and

•

G_{l+2}=G[L_{j+ld}\cup\dotsb\cup L_{h}]
.

The procedure runs in polynomial time and uses space
\ohop\left(\sqrt{n}\log{n}\right)
.

Remark 11
.

The graphs
G_{1},\dotsc,G_{l+2}
all have diameter at most
d
, together cover all of
G
, and any two graphs have at most a single level of the BFS traversal in common.

Proof.

Let
{v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}}
be the vertex set of
G
and
\left\{e_{1},\dotsc,e_{m}\right\}
be the edge set of
G
. Consider the following procedure.

Adding a dummy vertex.
This step produces a connected graph
G^{\prime}
equivalent, for our purposes, to
G
. Determine the connected components of
G
using the procedure in Proposition
9
: for any two vertices, it determines connectivity in logarithmic space. Then add a dummy vertex
v_{n+1}
which has an edge to the first vertex (in the ordering
v_{1},\dotsc,v_{n}
) in each connected component, making the resulting graph
G^{\prime}
connected. The edge additions are implicit, meaning they are written to the output stream on-the-fly.

Now output
G^{\prime}
, and denote this output stream by
S_{G^{\prime}}
. With random access to
G
, it is not hard to see that this transformation runs in time
n^{\ohop\left(1\right)}
and space
\ohop\left(\sqrt{n}\log{n}\right)
.

Decomposing
G^{\prime}
.

Using the procedure in Proposition
7
, perform a BFS traversal of
G^{\prime}
(using
S_{G^{\prime}}
) starting at
v_{n+1}
. Suppose the depth of the traversal is
h
. For
i\in[h]
, set
L_{i}=\left\{v\in\Vop\left(G^{\prime}\right)\mid\operatorname{dist}(v_{n+1},v)=i\right\}
. Observe that
L_{1},\dotsc,L_{h}
are precisely the levels of the BFS tree and
\Vop\left(G\right)=L_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup L_{h}
.

Now set
l=\left\lfloor(h-j)/id\right\rfloor
, and write

•

G_{1}=G[L_{1}\cup\dotsb L_{j}]
,

•

for
1<i<l+2
,
G_{i}=G[L_{j+(i-2)d}\cup\dotsb\cup L_{j+(i-1)d}]
, and

•

G_{l+2}=G[L_{j+ld}\cup\dotsb\cup L_{h}]

to the output stream.

To prove the claim, one only needs to show that the resource bounds hold. Observe that the two steps involve polynomial-time computations. The first step uses space
\ohop\left(\sqrt{n}\log{n}\right)
and the second, because it consists in simply scanning the BFS traversal of
G^{\prime}
, uses logarithmic space. The output of the first step is used as input for the second, so the overall resource bounds are polynomial time and
\ohop\left(\sqrt{n}\log{n}\right)+\ohop\left(\log{n}\right)=\ohop\left(\sqrt{n}\log{n}\right)
space.
∎

The next result provides a restricted-memory procedure that solves
Dominating Set
on graphs of bounded treewidth. Let
H
be a graph with
n

. Consider a tree decomposition

for

For each

the subgraph of
