We develop some preliminary definitions and results from incidence geometry

2.1  Incidence Geometry

An incidence geometry is a triple
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
where
\mathcal{P}
is called the
set of points,
\mathcal{L}
is the set of lines disjoint from
\mathcal{P}
and

\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{I}
is the incidence relation. We say that a point

\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
is incident with a
line
L\in\mathcal{L}
if
(\alpha,L)\in\mathcal{I}
. This pair
(\alpha,L)
is called a
flag. We say two points
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{P}
are collinear if there exists
a line
L\in\mathcal{L}
such that
(\alpha,L)\in\mathcal{I}
and
(\beta,L)\in\mathcal{I}
.
Finally, the dual of an incidence geometry
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
is the
incidence geometry
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{L}^{\prime},\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
where
the set of points
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}
, the set of lines
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}

and the incident pair
(L,\alpha)\in\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
precisely when

(\alpha,L)\in\mathcal{I}
.

We may also view an incidence structure as a graph. More precisely, given an
incidence structure
\Gamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
, we construct a graph called the

incidence graph
of
\Gamma
in the following way: we take the vertex
set to be
\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}
and we join an edge between
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
and

L\in\mathcal{L}
if
(\alpha,L)\in\mathcal{I}
. Note that the disjointness of
\mathcal{P}

and
\mathcal{L}
ensures that the incidence graph does not contain loops. Finally, an
automorphism of
\Gamma
is a bijective map
\theta:\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}
that sends points to points and lines to lines as well as
preserving the incidence relation.
The group of automorphisms of
\Gamma
is denoted by
\textsf{Aut}(\Gamma)
.
While we have defined generalised polygons via this incidence graph, we will
often think of them as incidence geometries. For more
information on incidence structures and generalised polygons, we refer to

[
10
,
14
,
19
]

From here on, a generalised quadrangle is assumed to be finite,

In our study of groups of Lie type, we will come across subgroups that are

Observe that

\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
.

Suppose that an almost simple group with socle
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
acts primitively on
the point-set and the line-set of a generalised quadrangle
\Gamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
with order
(s,t)
where
s,t\geqslant 2
.
Then
|\mathcal{P}|=(s+1)(st+1)
. Note that
s+1\geqslant 3
and

st+1\geqslant 3
. Therefore, their product cannot be a prime. The argument
in
[
13
, Section 3]
shows that the socle of this almost simple group
acts primitively on both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
. Therefore, it suffices to consider
the Suzuki group
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
acting primitively on both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
. Let

G=\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
. For a point
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}

and a line
L\in\mathcal{L}
, their respective stabilisers
G_{\alpha}
and
G_{L}

are maximal subgroups of
G
. By
Theorem

3.1
, a maximal subgroup
of
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
is isomorphic to one of the following:

1.

(Parabolic)
E_{q}.E_{q}.C_{q-1}
;

2.

(Dihedral)
D_{q-1}
;

3.

(Frobenius)
C_{q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1}:C_{4}
; or

4.

(Subfield)
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)
, where
q_{0}=2^{n_{0}}>2
and
q=q_{0}^{r_{0}}
for
some prime
r_{0}
.

Moreover, there is only one conjugacy class of each type of maximal subgroup,
see also
[
4
, Table 8.16]
.

Note that if
G_{\alpha}\cong E_{q}.E_{q}.C_{q-1}
, then by
[
17
]
, the
action of
G
is
2
-transitive. Thus, a pair of collinear points can
be mapped to a pair of non-collinear points, which is a contradiction.
Therefore, we only need to investigate the cases:
D_{q-1}
,
C_{q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1}:C_{4}
and
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)
for the point and line-stabiliser and show
that there are no generalised quadrangles in those scenarios. To this end,
suppose
G_{\alpha}
is isomorphic to
D_{q-1}
,
C_{q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1}:C_{4}
or

\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)
and
G_{L}
is isomorphic to
D_{q-1}
,
C_{q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1}:C_{4}

or
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)
, where
q_{i}=2^{n_{i}}>2
and
q_{i}^{r_{i}}=q
for primes

r_{i}
and
i=1,2
.
Observe that both
G_{\alpha}
and
G_{L}
contain an involution,
say
g\in G_{\alpha}
and
h\in G_{L}
. Since all the involutions in
G
are
conjugate (
Lemma

3.2
), there exists a
k\in G
such that
g=h^{k}
. Thus,
g\in G_{L}^{k}
. Since
G_{L}^{k}=G_{L^{\prime}}
where

L^{\prime}=L^{k}\in\mathcal{L}
, we can take
L
to be
L^{\prime}
and assume that
we have an involution
g\in G_{\alpha}\cap G_{L}
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{g}
and
\mathcal{L}_{g}
be
the set of points and the set of lines fixed by
g
, respectively.
By
Lemma

3.4
, we find that the number of fixed points and lines are

|\mathcal{P}_{g}|=2^{a}\ \textrm{and}\ |\mathcal{L}_{g}|=2^{b},

where
a\in A\vcentcolon=\{4m,4m+1,2n_{0}(r_{0}-1)\}
and
b\in B\vcentcolon=\{4m,4m+1,2n_{1}(r_{1}-1)\}
.
By
Lemma

2.1
, the
fixed substructure,
(\mathcal{P}_{g},\mathcal{L}_{g},\mathcal{I}_{g})
is a generalised quadrangle of
order
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
for some positive integers
s^{\prime}
and

t^{\prime}
. Therefore,

|\mathcal{P}_{g}|=(s^{\prime}+1)(s^{\prime}t^{\prime}+1)=2^{a}\ \textrm{and}\ |\mathcal{L}_{g}|=(t^{\prime}+1)(s^{\prime}t^{\prime}+1)=2^{b}.

By
Lemma

2.2
, we have that
b=a/2+1
or dually,
a=b/2+1
. Let us
suppose that
b=a/2+1
. Thus,
a
is even and so,
a=4m
or
2n_{0}(r_{0}-1)
.
If
a=4m
, then
b-1=a/2=2m
.
Hence,
b=2m+1
, which is odd. However, the
only odd element in
B
is
4m+1
and so, we have a contradiction. Next,
if
a=2n_{0}(r_{0}-1)
, then
b=a/2+1=n_{0}(r_{0}-1)+1
.
However, since
r_{0}
is an odd
prime, it follows that
b
is odd. Therefore,
b=4m+1
. Consequently, we
find
4m+1=b=n_{0}r_{0}-n_{0}+1<n_{0}r_{0}=2m+1
, which is a contradiction. The
case where
a=b/2+1
is analogous. Therefore, the Suzuki
group
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
cannot act primitively on both the set of points and the set of
lines of a generalised quadrangle. Consequently, an almost simple group with
socle isomorphic to
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{B}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
cannot act primitively on both the set of
points and the set of lines of a generalised quadrangle.
∎

4  Ree Groups

We provide some background information on the family of small Ree
groups. Throughout this section, we denote a small Ree group by
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
,
where
q=3^{2m+1}
for some positive integer
m
.

4.1  Structure of Ree Groups

We have the following theorem regarding the maximal subgroups of
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)

(see
[
4
, Table 8.43]
).

Theorem 4.1

(Maximal Subgroups of a Small Ree Group)
.

Let
G=\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
and
H
be a maximal subgroup of
G
. Then
H
is
isomorphic to one of the following:

1.

E_{q}.E_{q}.E_{q}.C_{q-1}
;

2.

C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
;

3.

(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
;

4.

C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}:C_{6}
; and

5.

\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)
, where
q_{0}=3^{n_{0}}>3
and
q=q_{0}^{r_{0}}
for some
prime
r_{0}
.

Moreover, there is only one conjugacy class of each type of maximal subgroup.

Analogous to the Suzuki groups, elements of order
3
play a crucial role in
studying the action of a small Ree group on a generalised quadrangle. First,
we recall a definition from group theory.
For a group
G
and
g\in G
, we say that
g
is
real
in
G
if

g
is conjugate to its inverse in
G
,
i.e.
, there exists an element

h\in G
such that
g^{-1}=g^{h}
.
We may omit the “in
G
” part and simply refer to
g
as a real element.

The following lemma is useful for analysing the maximal subgroup isomorphic to

(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
H=KR\cong(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
where
K\cong E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}
and
R=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle\cong C_{3}
. Then
y
centralises an involution
in
H
.

Note that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle
acts by conjugation on the
set of involutions in
H
. We count the number of involutions in
H
.
Since
q+1=3^{2m+1}+1=3(3^{2})^{m}+1\equiv 3+1\pmod{8}\equiv 4\pmod{8}
, it
follows that
(q+1)/4
is odd and thus,

D_{(q+1)/4}
has
(q+1)/4
involutions.
Since an involution in
K
is the product of an element of
E_{4}
and an
involution in
D_{(q+1)/4}
, or is just an involution in
E_{4}
, the number of
involutions in
K
is
4(q+1)/4+3=q+4
.
Now, all the involutions in
H
are in
K
because
K\trianglelefteq H
and

\gcd(|K|,|R|)=1
. Therefore,
H
has
q+4
involutions. Focusing on the
action of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle
on the set of involutions, suppose that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle
has no
fixed points,
i.e.
,
y
does not centralise any involution. Then the
orbits of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle
must have length divisible by
3
.
This implies that
q+4
is
divisible by
3
, which is a contradiction as
q=3^{2m+1}
. Therefore,
y

has a fixed point,
i.e.
,
y
centralises an involution.
∎

Lemma 4.3

(Conjugacy Classes of Elements of Order Three)
.

Let
G=\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
and
H
be a maximal subgroup of
G
. Then the following
statements hold:

1.

In
G
, there is one conjugacy class of real elements of
order
3
and two conjugacy classes of non-real elements of order
3
.

2.

If
H\cong C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
,

H\cong(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
, or
H\cong C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}:C_{6}
,
then there are two
H
-conjugacy classes of non-real elements of order

3
.

First, we find that the centre of a
Sylow
3
-subgroup of
G
contains one conjugacy class of elements with order

3
with representative labelled
X

[
20
, Chapter III, Paragraph 4]
.
Also, in
[
20
, Chapter III, Paragraph 7]
, we find that there are two
conjugacy classes of elements of order
3
with representatives labelled
T

and
T^{-1}
. This yields (i).

Now we focus on (ii). From
[
20
, Chapter III, Paragraphs 1 and 3]
, we
deduce that an order
3
element in
G
is not real precisely when it
centralises an involution. Therefore, it suffices to show the following two
points:

1.

An element of order
3
in
H
centralises an involution.

2.

There are at most two conjugacy classes of elements with order
3
in

H
.

Indeed, once we have established that there are at most two conjugacy classes,
it follows that we have exactly two conjugacy classes since the elements are
not real.

Let us suppose
H\cong C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
. Note that all the
order
3
elements of
H
are in
\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
. Therefore, the involution in

C_{2}
is centralised by the order
3
elements of
H
. Also, there are exactly
two conjugacy classes of elements with order
3
in
\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})

(see
[
16
, Chapter 3, (6.3) (iii)]
) and hence, two conjugacy classes in

H
.

Next, we consider
H=KR\cong(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
where
K\cong E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}
and
R=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle\cong C_{3}
. Observe that

\gcd(|E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}|,|C_{3}|)=\gcd(2(q+1),3)=1
. Hence, by
Corollary

2.5
, any element of order
3
must
be conjugate in
H
to
y
or
y^{-1}
. So there are at most two conjugacy
classes of elements with order
3
. Furthermore,
y
centralises an involution
in
H
by
Lemma

4.2
.

Finally, let us suppose that
H=KR\cong C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}:C_{6}
where

K\cong C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}
and
R=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle y}}\right\rangle\cong C_{6}
. We find that

\gcd(|C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}|,|C_{6}|)=\gcd(q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1,6)=1
since

q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1\equiv 1\pm 1+1\pmod{2}\equiv 1\pmod{2}
and
q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. There are two
conjugacy classes of elements with order
3
in
R
, namely,
(y^{2})^{R}
and

(y^{4})^{R}
. Using

Corollary

2.5
again, we find that the number of conjugacy classes of
elements with order
3
in
H
is at most
2
. Moreover, we note that
y^{2}

centralises
y^{3}
, which is an involution.
∎

Lemma 4.4

(Centralisers of Elements of Order Three)
.

Let
G=\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
,
x\in G
be a real element
of order
3
and
y\in G
be a non-real element of order
3
. Then

|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)|=q^{3}\ \textrm{and}\ |\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)|=2q^{2}.

Consider maximal subgroups
H_{1}
,
H_{2}
and
H_{3}
of
G
where

H_{1}\cong C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
,
H_{2}\cong(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}

and
H_{3}\cong C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}:C_{6}
. Suppose
h_{i}\in H_{i}
are elements of
order
3
for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Then

|\textbf{C}_{H_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{1}}}\right)|=2q,\ |\textbf{C}_{H_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{2}}}\right)|=6\ \textrm{and}\ |\textbf{C}_{H_{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{3}}}\right)|=6.

From
[
20
, Chapter III, Paragraphs 2 and 3]
, we obtain

|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)|=2q^{2}
and
|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)|=q^{3}
,
respectively.

From
[
16
, Chapter 3, (6.4) (i)]
,
we find that
|\textbf{C}_{\textsf{PGL}_{2}({q})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)|=q
when
x
is an element of order
3
. Since
\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
has index
2
in

\textsf{PGL}_{2}({q})
and
C\vcentcolon=\textbf{C}_{\textsf{PGL}_{2}({q})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)
has odd order, it
follows that
C\leqslant\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
. Now, back to
H_{1}
. Since the
C_{2}
in

H_{1}
also centralises
h_{1}
, we obtain
|\textbf{C}_{H_{1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{1}}}\right)|=2q
.

Next, observe that
|\textbf{C}_{H_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{2}}}\right)|=|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{2}}}\right)\cap H_{2}|\leqslant 6
since

\gcd(|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{2}}}\right)|,|H_{2}|)=\gcd(2q^{2},4\cdot 2(q+1)/4\cdot 6)=\gcd(2q^{2},12(q+1))=6
. Furthermore,
h_{2}
centralises an involution in
H_{2}

(
Lemma

4.2
). Therefore,
|\textbf{C}_{H_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{2}}}\right)|=6
.

Finally, we consider
H_{3}
. By the argument as above, we see that

\gcd(|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{3}}}\right)|,|H_{3}|)=\gcd(2q^{2},6(q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1))=6
,
whence,

|\textbf{C}_{H_{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{3}}}\right)|=|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{3}}}\right)\cap H_{3}|\leqslant 6
.
Since
h_{3}
lies in some subgroup isomorphic to
C_{6}
in
H_{3}

(
Corollary

2.5
), it follows that
h_{3}
is
centralised by
6
elements. Therefore,
|\textbf{C}_{H_{3}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h_{3}}}\right)|=6
.
∎

Lemma 4.5

(Number of Fixed Points of Order Three Elements)
.

Let
G=\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
act primitively on a set
\Omega
and take a point
\alpha\in\Omega
. Suppose we have a non-real element
g\in G_{\alpha}
of order

3
. Then

|\Omega_{g}|=\begin{cases}q&\textrm{if $G_{\alpha}\cong C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})$},\\
q^{2}/3&\textrm{if $G_{\alpha}\cong(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}$ or
$G_{\alpha}\cong C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}:C_{6}$}\\
(q/q_{0})^{2}&\textrm{if $G_{\alpha}\cong\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)$ where $q_{0}=3^{n_{0}}>3$
and $q=q_{0}^{r_{0}}$ for some prime $r_{0}$}.\end{cases}

Furthermore,
\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
acts transitively on
\Omega_{g}
, the set of
points fixed by
g
.

By
Lemma

4.3
,
G_{\alpha}
has precisely two conjugacy classes
of order
3
non-real elements. Since
G
also has two conjugacy classes of
these elements, the conjugacy classes do not split,
i.e.
,
g^{G}\cap G_{\alpha}=g^{G_{\alpha}}
. Thus, applying
Lemma

2.7
, we find that

\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
acts transitively on
\Omega_{g}
and
|\Omega_{g}|=|\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right):\textbf{C}_{G}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\cap G_{\alpha}|
.
Using
Lemma

4.4
, we obtain the desired formulae for

|\Omega_{g}|
. ∎

4.2  Ree Groups Acting on Generalised Quadrangles

We now prove
Theorem

1.1
in the case where the socle is isomorphic to

\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
.

Suppose that an almost simple group with socle
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
acts primitively on
the point-set and the line-set of a generalised quadrangle
\Gamma=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
with order
(s,t)
where
s,t\geqslant 2
.
Then
|\mathcal{P}|=(s+1)(st+1)
. Note that
s+1\geqslant 3
and

st+1\geqslant 3
. Therefore, their product cannot be a prime. The argument
in
[
13
, Section 4]
shows that the socle of this almost simple group
acts primitively on both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
. Therefore, it suffices to consider
the Ree group
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
acting primitively on both
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
. Let

G=\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
. For a point
\alpha\in\mathcal{P}
and a line

, their respective stabilisers
G_{\alpha}
and
G_{L}
are maximal
subgroups of
G
. We use the list of maximal subgroups from
Theorem

4.1
.

If
G_{\alpha}\cong E_{q}.E_{q}.E_{q}.C_{q-1}
, then the action of
G
is

2
-transitive
[
20
, Theorem (v)]
. Thus, a pair of collinear points can
be mapped to a pair of non-collinear points, which is a contradiction.
Therefore, we only need to investigate the cases:
C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
,

(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
,
C_{q\pm\sqrt{3q}+1}:C_{6}
and
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)
for
the point and line-stabiliser and show
that there are no generalised quadrangles in these scenarios. To this end,
suppose
G_{\alpha}
is isomorphic to
C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
,
(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
,
C_{q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1}:C_{4}
or

\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)
and

is isomorphic to
C_{2}\times\textsf{PSL}_{2}({q})
,

(E_{4}\times D_{(q+1)/4}):C_{3}
,
C_{q\pm\sqrt{2q}+1}:C_{4}
or
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)
,
where
q_{i}=3^{n_{i}}>3
and
q_{i}^{r_{i}}=q
for primes

and

.
By
Lemma

4.3
,
both
G_{\alpha}
and
G_{L}
contain a non-real element of order

3
, say
g\in G_{\alpha}
and
h\in G_{L}
. Moreover, there are
precisely two classes of non-real elements of order
3
in
G
, and so

g
is conjugate to

h
or
h^{-1}
. Without loss of generality, suppose
g
is conjugate to
h
,
and so, we can write

for some

.
Since
G_{L}^{k}=G_{L^{\prime}}
where

, we can take
L
to be
L^{\prime}
and assume that
we have an order
3
non-real element

. Let

\mathcal{P}_{g}
and
\mathcal{L}_{g}
be the set of points and the set of lines fixed by

,
respectively. By
Lemma

4.5
, we find that
the number of fixed points and lines are

|\mathcal{P}_{g}|=3^{a}\ \textrm{and}\ |\mathcal{L}_{g}|=3^{b},

where

, the

(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
for some positive integers
s^{\prime}
and

. Therefore,

However, there are no solutions for

and
t^{\prime}
by

2.2
and so, we have a contradiction. Therefore, the small
Ree group
\prescript{2}{}{\textrm{G}_{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
cannot act primitively on both the set of points and
the set of
