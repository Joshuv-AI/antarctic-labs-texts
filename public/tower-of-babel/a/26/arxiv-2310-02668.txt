Gauss curvature flow with shrinking obstacle
Ki-Ahm Lee, Taehun Lee

Abstract
. We consider a flow by powers of Gauss curvature under the obstruction that the flow cannot penetrate a prescribed region, so called an obstacle. For all dimensions and positive powers, we prove the optimal curvature bounds of solutions and all time existence with its long time behavior. We also prove the C 1 C^{1} regularity of free boundaries under a uniform thickness assumption.

53E10 (35R35, 35K96)

1.
Introduction

We study an evolution of hypersurfaces by powers of Gauss curvature under the restriction that the hypersurface cannot enter a prescribed region. The prescribed region is called an obstacle, and we assume that it shrinks slowly with time.

Recall that for a given number
\alpha>0
, a one-parameter family of immersions
X:M^{n}\times[0,T)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
defining complete convex hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{t}=X(M^{n},t)
is a solution of the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow
if
X
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(p,t)=K^{\alpha}(p,t)\vec{n}(p,t).

Here
K(p,t)
and
\vec{n}(p,t)
are the Gauss curvature and the inward unit normal vector of
\Sigma_{t}
at
X(p,t)
, respectively.

The classical Gauss curvature flow (
\alpha=1
) was first introduced by Firey
[
14
]
to model the process of wearing stone on a beach.
Later, Chow
[
9
]
generalizes this flow to the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow (
\alpha>0
).
Since then, many authors have studied this flow, and most of them focused on the analysis of singularities which was completely carried out when
\alpha\geq\tfrac{1}{n+2}
, see
[
2
,
3
,
6
,
5
]

and the references therein. See also
[
4
]
for the case
0<\alpha<\tfrac{1}{n+2}
and
n=1
.
Note that the evolution of any closed initial hypersurface under the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow develops a singularity.

We consider an
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow with an obstacle in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, which will block the development of collapsing of the hypersurface to a point.
Precisely, given a strictly convex, closed hypersurface
\Sigma_{0}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, we consider a time dependent obstacle
\Phi=\Phi_{t}
inside of
\Sigma_{0}
, which is a one-parameter family of strictly convex, closed hypersurfaces. We then evolve the hypersurface
\Sigma_{0}
by the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow,
\alpha>0
, on the condition that the evolving hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
cannot penetrate the obstacle
\Phi_{t}
for all time. Here the obstacle shrinks slowly with time, and for example one can consider obstacles of the form

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}=\tfrac{1+e^{-t}}{2}\Phi_{0}.

We remark that if the obstacle shrinks quickly so that it disappears in a finite time, then it cannot prevent the development of the collapse.
The precise definition of obstacles will be given in Definition
2.1
below.

Note that in general the evolutions of such hypersurfaces cannot be defined in the classical sense since the speed
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}X
has jump discontinuities when the hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
touches the obstacle
\Phi_{t}
for the first time. Thus we need to consider a generalized concept of solutions, that is, viscosity solutions.

A one-parameter family of immersions
X:M^{n}\times[0,T)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
with
\Sigma_{t}=X(M^{n},t)
is said to be a viscosity solution to the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow with a shrinking obstacle
if it satisfies

(1.1)

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X(p,t)&=K^{\alpha}(p,t)\vec{n}(p,t)\quad\text{for all}\quad X(p,t)\notin\Phi_{t},\\
\mathrm{conv}(\Sigma_{t})&\supseteq\mathrm{conv}(\Phi_{t})\quad\text{for all}\quad 0\leq t<T,\\
\left\langle{\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X},{\vec{n}}\right\rangle&\leq K^{\alpha}\quad\text{in}\quad M^{n}\times[0,T),\end{split}

in the viscosity sense (see Definition
2.3
), where
\mathrm{conv(\Sigma)}
denotes the convex hull of
\Sigma
.

As we discussed above, the solutions to (
1.1
) are at most Lipschitz continuous in the time variable, which induces that solutions to (
1.1
) have at most
C^{1,1}
regularity. We will show that solutions to (
1.1
) have indeed
C^{1,1}
regularity, and therefore this is the optimal regularity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be a closed strictly convex smooth hypersurface that encloses a shrinking obstacle
\Phi
(Definition
2.1
). Then (
1.1
) starting from
\Sigma_{0}
has a unique viscosity solution
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
that exists for all time, and the solution
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
has the optimal regularity which is
C^{1,1}(\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty))
. In particular, principal curvatures
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
of
\Sigma_{t}
are globally bounded away from zero and infinity. Moreover, there exists a finite time
T^{*}=T^{*}(n,\alpha,\Sigma_{0},\Phi)
such that
\Sigma_{t}=\Phi_{t}
for
t\geq T^{*}
.

It is worth noting that our result for the optimal regularity relies on several curvature bounds. We first prove that the several curvatures are bounded by some constant that depends on
T
. By showing that the final shape of the solution becomes exactly the same shape as the obstacle after a finite time
T^{*}
, we can remove the time dependence in the curvature estimates.

To prove curvature estimates, we employ the method of penalization, which allows penetration of the obstacle up to order of
\delta>0
and then recovers the original obstacle problems by pushing the hypersurfaces out of the obstacle as
\delta\rightarrow 0
.
In the other words, we obtain smooth approximate solutions,

, of (

∗

δ

1.1
) as the penalty increases, i.e.
\delta\rightarrow 0

A crucial ingredient in this paper is the smallest curvature estimate (Lemma

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}^{-1}e^{-\chi\beta_{\delta}(u-\varphi)},

where

is the penalty term defined in Section

Let us review some related results for stationary obstacles,
\Phi_{t}\equiv\Phi_{0}
.
In
[
20
]
, we studied the obstacle problem in dimension

with

. In that paper, we established the optimal curvature bounds for principal curvatures,
0\leq\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\leq C
. We point out that for stationary obstacles the lowest principal curvature may become zero.
For the mean curvature flow, Rupflin and Schnürer
[
27

studied an obstacle problem by considering graphical mean curvature flows in one dimension higher. They proved local

regularity for solutions in the graphical setting, which implies the existence of solutions for all time in the original dimension. See also
[

Once the optimal regularity of solutions to obstacle problems is established, the main interest will be a question of regularity of its free boundaries. In obstacle problems, a
free boundary
of a solution is the interface between a
coincidence set
and
non-coincidence set
of the solution and its obstacle.

To find a local regularity of free boundaries, we use the following local graph representation of (
1.1
): for functions
w:Q_{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and
\phi:Q_{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\Sigma_{t}
and
\Phi_{t}
are locally represented by the graphs of
w
and
\phi
, respectively, we consider

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}w

\displaystyle=\frac{\det(D^{2}w)^{\alpha}}{(1+|Dw|^{2})^{\frac{(n+2)\alpha-1}{2}}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\Omega(w),

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle\leq\phi\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad Q_{1}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}w

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\det(D^{2}w)^{\alpha}}{(1+|Dw|^{2})^{\frac{(n+2)\alpha-1}{2}}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad Q_{1},

where
Q_{r}=B_{r}\times(-r^{2},0)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}
is the parabolic ball of radius
r
and
\Omega(w)=\{(x,t)\in Q_{1}:w(x,t)<\phi(x,t)\}
is the non-coincidence set. Note that the coincidence set
\Lambda(w)
and the free boundary
\Gamma(w)
is then given by
\Lambda(w)=Q_{1}\setminus\Omega(w)
and
\Gamma(w)=\partial\Omega(w)
.

The regularity of free boundaries has been studied by many authors since the seminal work of Caffarelli
[
7
]
which proves local
C^{1}
regularity of free boundaries in obstacle problems for a class of elliptic equations and the Stefan problem. In the first named author’s thesis
[
19
]
and
[
23
]
, the authors analyzed the regularity of free boundaries in the obstacle problem for a fully nonlinear operator. See also
[
21
,
22
]
for double obstacle problems.

In the recent works
[
12
,
13
]
, Figalli and Shahgholian proved
C^{1}
regularity of free boundaries for general fully nonlinear elliptic/parabolic equations of the form
F(D^{2}w)=1
or
F(D^{2}w)-\partial_{t}w=1
. Soon after Indrei and Minne
[
17
]
extended the result to the equations
F(D^{2}w,x)=f(x)
and
F(D^{2}w,x,t)-\partial_{t}w=f(x,t)
, where
F
and
f
are Lipschitz continuous in both the space and time variable. We note that in these results
F
is assumed to be convex in the hessian variable.

However, our equation (
1.2
) does not fall into the known cases. This is because our
F
has
Dw
dependence which is not Lipschitz continuous in the time variable although it is Lipschitz in the space variable after establishing the optimal
C^{1,1}
regularity. We stress that most geometric PDEs have similar gradient dependence on the operator, which requires some extra work.

As indicated above, the theory for the free boundary regularity has been developed for convex operators. Hence we assume
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{n}
to make the operator
-\det(\cdot)^{\alpha}
convex.
We will prove
C^{1}
regularity of the free boundary
\Gamma(w)
under the so called
uniform thickness assumption
on the coincidence set
\Lambda(w)
.

To describe the thickness assumption, we first define the minimal diameter (also known as the minimal width)
\mathrm{MD(E)}
of a set
E
by the minimum distance between two parallel hyperplanes that contain the set
E
. Then the thickness of
\Lambda(w)
at
x_{0}
in each time slice
t\in[t_{0}-r^{2},t_{0}+r^{2}]
is measured as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{r}(w,x_{0},t_{0})=\inf_{|t-t_{0}|\leq r^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{MD}(\Lambda(w)^{t}\cap B_{r}(x_{0}))}{r},

where
\Lambda(w)^{t}=\{x\in B_{1}:(x,t)\in\Lambda(w)\}
is the time slice of
\Lambda(w)
with respect to
t
.
We say that
\Lambda(w)
satisfies a uniform thickness condition if there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
0<r<\tfrac{1}{4}
and
(x,t)\in\Gamma(w)\cap Q_{r}
, it holds
\mathfrak{d}_{r}(w,x,t)>\varepsilon
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
w
be a solution to (
1.2
) with
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{n}
. If
\Lambda(w)
satisfies a uniform thickness condition, then the free boundary
\Gamma(w)
is locally a
C^{1}
graph in space-time.

The thickness condition is necessary for Theorem
1.2
since the free boundary
\Gamma(w)
may have arbitrary lower dimensional shapes such as a point when solutions to (
1.2
) touch the obstacle for the first time.

We remark that there is another free boundary problem arising in the Gauss curvature flow, the Gauss curvature flow with flat sides. Hamilton
[
16
]
observed that if an initial hypersurface has flat sides, then its evolution by the Gauss curvature flow also has flat sides for some time, in contrast with the mean curvature flow whose flat sides disappear instantly. In the problem, free boundaries are the interface between flat sides and non-flat sides. Comparing with our obstacle problem that has growing contact sets, this free boundary problem has shrinking flat sides. In dimension
n=2
, regularity for the free boundary has been established by Daskalopoulos and Hamilton
[
10
]
for short time and by Daskalopoulos and the first named author
[
11
]
for all time. See also
[
8
]
for waiting time effects when an initial data is smooth enough. Note that the same phenomena were observed for the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow when
\frac{1}{2}<\alpha\leq 1
and
n=2
, see
[
18
]
.

and

7

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout the paper we use the Einstein summation convention in which repeated upper and lower indices are summed.

For a smooth hypersurface
\Sigma^{n}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
, we denote the induced metric by
g=\{g_{ij}\}
, the second fundamental form by
h=\{h_{ij}\}
, and the Weingarten map by
W=\{h_{i}^{j}\}
. The principal curvatures are the eigenvalues of
W
denoted by
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. We will use the mean curvature
H=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}
and the Gauss curvature
K=\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{n}
.

If
\Sigma^{n}
is compact without boundary and strictly convex, then the Gauss map
\nu:\Sigma^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{n}
given by the outward unit normal vector to
\Sigma^{n}
is a diffeomorphism. In the case we describe the hypersurface
\Sigma^{n}
as its support function
u:\mathbb{S}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
u(z)=\left\langle{\nu^{-1}(z)},{z}\right\rangle
. In other words, the hypersurface
\Sigma^{n}
is the image of the embedding
\nu^{-1}(z)=\overline{\nabla}u(z)+u(z)z
, where
\overline{\nabla}
is the Levi-Civita connection on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
induced by the standard metric
\overline{g}=\{\overline{g}_{ij}\}
. It can be checked that the second fundamental form
h
is expressed as

(2.1)

\displaystyle h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u+u\overline{g}_{ij},

and that the eigenvalues of
h_{ij}
with respect to
\overline{g}
are the reciprocal of the principal curvatures, i.e.,

(2.2)

\displaystyle h_{ij}\overline{g}^{jk}=g_{ij}b^{jk}

where
b=\{b^{ij}\}
is the inverse of the second fundamental form
h
.

Initial data.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be a strictly convex, closed initial hypersurface embedded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
and
u_{0}:\mathbb{S}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be the support function of
\Sigma_{0}
. Both
\Sigma_{0}
and
u_{0}
are used as initial data. Geometric quantities associated to the initial data will be used with subscript
0
, for example
H_{0}
,
K_{0}
, and so on.

Obstacle.

Let
\{\Omega(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
be a family of bounded open sets that are strictly convex. We always assume that
\Omega(t)
shrinks in
t
, that is,
\Omega(t_{1})\supset\Omega(t_{2})
for
t_{1}<t_{2}
, and that the limit
\Omega_{\infty}:=\cap_{t\geq 0}\Omega(t)
has an interior point.
For convenience, we assume that the limit obstacle
\Omega_{\infty}
contains the origin.
We also assume that the initial obstacle
\Omega(0)
is strictly enclosed by the initial data, i.e.,
\overline{\Omega(0)}\subset\operatorname{conv}(\Sigma_{0})
, where
\operatorname{conv}(\Sigma_{0})
denotes the convex hull of
\Sigma_{0}
.
We denote the boundary of
\Omega(t)
by
\Phi_{t}
and the support function of
\Phi_{t}
by
\varphi(\cdot,t):\mathbb{S}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
. Denote furthermore the principal curvatures of
\Phi_{t}
by
\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{n}
(not necessarily in increasing order).
Other geometric quantities associated to the obstacle will be used with subscript
\Phi
, for example
H_{\Phi}
,
K_{\Phi}
, and so on.

Definition 2.1

(shrinking obstacles)
.

An obstacle
\Phi
is said to be
shrinking
if its support function
\varphi
is in
C^{3,1}(\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty))
and satisfies (i)
\partial_{t}\varphi<0
, (ii) the speed
-\partial_{t}\varphi
is non-increasing, (iii) the final shape
\operatorname{conv}(\Phi_{\infty})
has an interior point, and (iv) the principal curvatures
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
are non-decreasing and bounded.

A natural example of the shrinking obstacle is any strictly convex hypersurface that homothetically shrinks with decreasing speed, i.e., if
A:[0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
with
A(0)=1
and
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}A(t)>0
is decreasing and convex, then
A(t)\varphi_{0}(z)
is a shrinking obstacle. More generally, given an initial shape
\Omega_{0}
and a final shape
\Omega_{\infty}
with their support function
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{\infty}
satisfying
\varphi_{0}>\varphi_{\infty}>0
, the obstacle defined by

\displaystyle\varphi(\cdot,t)=e^{-t}\varphi_{0}+(1-e^{-t})\varphi_{\infty}

is clearly shrinking.

Note that for
a>0
, a rescaled obstacle
\varphi^{a}(\cdot,t)=\varphi(\cdot,at)
is also shrinking and has the same initial and final shape with
\varphi
. Moreover, given an initial data
\Sigma_{0}
, the following holds if we choose
a
small enough:

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\varphi^{a}(\cdot,0)=-a\partial_{t}\varphi_{0}<\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{0}^{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad-\partial_{t}\varphi^{a}(\cdot,0)<\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{\Phi_{0}}^{\alpha}.

Thus we additionally assume for simplicity that the shrinking obstacle satisfies

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\varphi_{0}<\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{0}^{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad-\partial_{t}\varphi_{0}<\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{\Phi_{0}}^{\alpha}.

Remark 2.2

The condition (ii) and (iv) in Definition

(2.7)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})K^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\alpha K^{2\alpha}H+{\mathcal{L}}\beta_{\delta}+\alpha K^{\alpha}H\beta_{\delta}

(2.8)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})h_{ij}

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{C}_{ij}+(n\alpha-1)K^{\alpha}\overline{g}_{ij}-\alpha K^{\alpha}Hh_{ij}-\overline{\nabla}^{2}_{ij}\beta_{\delta}-\beta_{\delta}\overline{g}_{ij}

where
\mathcal{C}_{ij}=\alpha^{2}K^{\alpha}b^{kl}b^{pq}\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{pq}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}+\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kp}b^{lq}\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{pq}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}
.

Proof.

By (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}u=\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u=\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}(h_{ij}-u\overline{g}_{ij})=n\alpha K^{\alpha}-\alpha K^{\alpha}Hu.

Then the equation (
2.6
) follows from
\partial_{t}u=-K^{\alpha}-\beta_{\delta}
.

To prove (
2.7
), we recall (
2.4
) so that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}K=-Kb^{ij}\partial_{t}h_{ij}=Kb^{ij}(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}(-\partial_{t}u)+(-\partial_{t}u)\overline{g}_{ij}).

Using
-\partial_{t}u=K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}
and (
2.2
) again, we see that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}K^{\alpha}=\alpha K^{\alpha-1}\partial_{t}K={\mathcal{L}}(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})+(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})\alpha K^{\alpha}H

which gives (
2.7
).

For the last assertion, note first that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h_{ij}=-\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})-(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})\overline{g}_{ij}.

On the other hand, since
\overline{\nabla}_{j}K^{\alpha}=-\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kl}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}
and
\overline{\nabla}_{i}b^{kl}=-b^{kp}b^{lq}\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{pq}
, we have

(2.10)

\begin{split}\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}K^{\alpha}&=\alpha^{2}K^{\alpha}b^{kl}b^{pq}\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{pq}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}+\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kp}b^{lq}\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{pq}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}-\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kl}\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}\\
&=\mathcal{C}-\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kl}\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}.\end{split}

To proceed further we need the following result that we will prove in the next lemma:

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{k}\overline{\nabla}_{l}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}-h_{kj}\overline{g}_{il}+\overline{g}_{kl}h_{ij}-h_{kl}\overline{g}_{ij}+\overline{g}_{kj}h_{il}.

Multiplying with
\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kl}
we get

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}h_{ij}=\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{kl}\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}+\alpha K^{\alpha}(-\overline{g}_{ij}+Hh_{ij}-n\overline{g}_{ij}+\overline{g}_{ij}),

and substituting this into (
2.10
) we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}K^{\alpha}=\mathcal{C}_{ij}-{\mathcal{L}}h_{ij}+\alpha K^{\alpha}(Hh_{ij}-n\overline{g}_{ij}).

This together with (
2.9
) gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

The second fundamental form
h_{ij}
satisfies

(i)

\overline{\nabla}h
is totally symmetric, i.e.,
\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{kj}=\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{ki}
,

(ii)

\overline{\nabla}_{k}\overline{\nabla}_{l}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}-h_{kj}\overline{g}_{il}+\overline{g}_{kl}h_{ij}-h_{kl}\overline{g}_{ij}+\overline{g}_{kj}h_{il}
.

Proof.

Since
\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{ji}
, it suffices to show that
\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{kj}
. Recall that
h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u+u\overline{g}_{ij}
and
\overline{\nabla}\overline{g}=0
. Then we compute

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{ij}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}_{k}(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u+u\overline{g}_{ij})=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{k}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u-\overline{R}_{kipj}\overline{\nabla}^{p}u+(\overline{\nabla}_{k}u)\overline{g}_{ij},

where
\overline{R}_{kipj}
denotes the Riemannian curvature tensor on the sphere.
Since

(2.11)

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{ijkl}=\overline{g}_{ik}\overline{g}_{jl}-\overline{g}_{il}\overline{g}_{jk},

we find

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}(h_{kj}-u\overline{g}_{kj})-(\overline{g}_{kp}\overline{g}_{ij}-\overline{g}_{kj}\overline{g}_{ip})\overline{\nabla}^{p}u+(\overline{\nabla}_{k}u)\overline{g}_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{kj},

and the first assertion follows.

To prove the second assertion, note that we have shown
\overline{\nabla}_{l}h_{ij}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{lj}
. Again by (
2.11
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{k}\overline{\nabla}_{l}h_{ij}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}_{k}\overline{\nabla}_{i}h_{lj}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{k}h_{lj}-\overline{R}_{kipl}\overline{g}^{pr}h_{rj}-\overline{R}_{kipj}\overline{g}^{pr}h_{rl}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}-(\overline{g}_{kp}\overline{g}_{il}-\overline{g}_{kl}\overline{g}_{ip})\overline{g}^{pr}h_{rj}-(\overline{g}_{kp}\overline{g}_{ij}-\overline{g}_{kj}\overline{g}_{ip})\overline{g}^{pr}h_{rl}

\displaystyle=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}h_{kl}-h_{kj}\overline{g}_{il}+\overline{g}_{kl}h_{ij}-h_{kl}\overline{g}_{ij}+\overline{g}_{kj}h_{il}

and the conclusion follows.
∎

3.
Uniform boundedness of the penalty term

To extract a solution from approximate solutions of (

∗

δ

), we need several estimates that will be presented in this and the following three sections. In this section we establish the uniform boundedness of the penalty term
\beta_{\delta}(u-\varphi)
. Once we obtain the estimate, any possible limit of subsequence of the approximate solutions can not pass through the obstacle
\Phi
. Indeed,
since
\beta_{\delta}(x)\rightarrow-\infty
as
\delta\rightarrow 0
for each
x<0
, we conclude from the uniform boundedness of
\beta_{\delta}(u-\varphi)
that

\displaystyle\liminf_{\delta\rightarrow 0}(u-\varphi)\geq 0.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (

∗

δ

) in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)
. Then

\displaystyle-C_{0}\leq\beta_{\delta}(u(z,t)-\varphi(z,t))\leq 0\quad\text{for}\quad(z,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T),

where
C_{0}=\left\lVert K_{\Phi}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty))}^{\alpha}
is independent of
\delta
.

Proof.

The inequality
\beta_{\delta}\leq 0
follows from the definition of
\beta_{\delta}
. To obtain the lower bound, assume that there exists a point
(z_{0},t_{0})
in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)
such that
u=\varphi
at
(z_{0},t_{0})
for the first time. Since the initial hypersurface
\Sigma_{0}
strictly encloses the initial obstacle
\Phi_{0}
, we see
t_{0}>0
, and
u-\varphi
attains an interior minimum at
(z_{0},t_{0})
over
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,t_{0}]
. At the minimum point, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}(u-\varphi)=0,\quad\overline{\nabla}^{2}(u-\varphi)\geq 0,\quad(u-\varphi)_{t}\leq 0,\quad u=\varphi,

and it follows

\displaystyle K=\frac{\det\overline{g}_{ij}}{\det(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u+u\overline{g}_{ij})}\leq\frac{\det\overline{g}_{ij}}{\det(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}\varphi+\varphi\overline{g}_{ij})}=K_{\Phi}.

Then

\displaystyle-\varphi_{t}\leq-u_{t}=K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}(u-\varphi)\leq K_{\Phi}^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}(0),

and hence it is contradict to our choice of
\beta_{\delta}(0)=-\left\lVert K_{\Phi}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty))}^{\alpha}
in the definition of the penalty term since
\varphi_{t}<0
.
Therefore, we conclude
u>\varphi
for all
(z,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)
. Hence, the monotonicity of the function
\beta_{\delta}:{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
implies

\beta_{\delta}(u-\varphi)\geq\beta_{\delta}(0)=-\left\lVert K_{\Phi}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty))}^{\alpha}=-C_{0}

for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In the proof, we have shown
u>\varphi
for all
(z,t)\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)
, which means that the evolving hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
under (

∗

δ

) cannot penetrate or even touch the obstacle. This is because
|\beta_{\delta}(0)|
is chosen sufficiently large. If one consider that the condition
|\beta_{\delta}(0)|
has another uniform constant, for example
|\beta_{\delta}(0)|=1
, then one can prove
u>\varphi-C(\delta)
for some constant
C(\delta)
with
C(0+)=0
, in which case the hypersurface might penetrate the obstacle but the depth of penetration is controlled.

4.
Speed estimates I: Uniform positive lower bounds

In this section we prove uniform positive lower bounds for the speed of the solution to (

∗

δ

). As a direct corollary, we also obtain uniform positive lower bounds on the Gauss curvature of
\Sigma_{t}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be an initial hypersurface and
\Phi
be a shrinking obstacle. If
u:\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth solution to (

∗

δ

), then

(4.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(u-\varphi)\leq 0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T).

Proof.

Since
\Sigma_{0}
is strictly convex and
\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(u_{0}-\varphi(\cdot,0))\geq\delta>0
by our choice of
\delta
, we have at
t=0
,
\partial_{t}u=-K^{\alpha}-\beta_{\delta}=-K_{0}^{\alpha}<0
. On the other hand, from the definition of the shrinking obstacle
\Phi
, we have

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}\varphi(\cdot,0)<\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{0}^{\alpha}\leq K_{0}^{\alpha}

which implies (
4.1
) at
t=0
.

Recall that
K=\det\overline{g}_{ij}/\det(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}u+u\overline{g}_{ij})
. If we differentiate
-\partial_{t}u=K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}
with respect to
t
, we obtain

(4.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(-\partial_{t}u)=-\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}\partial_{t}u+\partial_{t}u\overline{g}_{ij})+\beta_{\delta}^{\prime}(u-\varphi)_{t}.

For
Z(x,t):=\partial_{t}(u(x,t)-\varphi(x,t))
, (
4.2
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-Z_{t}-\partial^{2}_{t}\varphi=-\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}(Z+\partial_{t}\varphi)+(Z+\partial_{t}\varphi)\overline{g}_{ij})+\beta_{\delta}^{\prime}Z.

Thus we have

\displaystyle Z_{t}={\mathcal{L}}Z+(\alpha K^{\alpha}H-\beta_{\delta}^{\prime})Z+\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}\partial_{t}\varphi+\partial_{t}\varphi\overline{g}_{ij})-\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi.

To estimate the terms involving the obstacle
\varphi
, recall that the shrinking obstacle satisfies
\partial_{t}^{2}\varphi\geq 0
and
\partial_{t}\mu_{i}(\cdot,t)\geq 0
, where
\mu_{1}(\cdot,t),\cdots,\mu_{n}(\cdot,t)
is the principal curvatures of
\Phi_{t}
. Since the second fundamental form of the obstacle is given by

\displaystyle h_{ij}^{\varphi}=\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}\varphi+\varphi\overline{g}_{ij},

it follows from the strict convexity of the solution and the property
\partial_{t}\mu_{i}\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}(\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}\partial_{t}\varphi+\partial_{t}\varphi\overline{g}_{ij})=\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}\partial_{t}(h_{ij}^{\varphi})=\alpha K^{\alpha}b^{ij}\overline{g}_{jk}\partial_{t}(\overline{g}^{kl}h_{lj}^{\varphi})\leq 0.

Note that in the last inequality we have used that the eigenvalues of
\overline{g}^{kl}h_{lj}^{\varphi}=(b^{\varphi})^{kl}g^{\varphi}_{lj}
are
1/\mu_{1},\cdots,1/\mu_{n}
which are decrease in time. Hence,

(4.3)

\displaystyle Z_{t}\leq{\mathcal{L}}Z+(\alpha K^{\alpha}H-\beta_{\delta}^{\prime})Z.

Assuming the contrary, we take a time
t_{0}
such that
\sup_{\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,t_{0}]}Z>0
. Set
m=\sup_{\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,t_{0}]}(\alpha K^{\alpha}H-\beta_{\delta}^{\prime})<\infty
and let
\tilde{Z}=Ze^{-mt-t}
. By (
4.3
),
\tilde{Z}
satisfies

(4.4)

\displaystyle\tilde{Z}_{t}=Z_{t}e^{-mt-t}-(m+1)Ze^{-mt-t}\leq{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{Z}-\tilde{Z}.

For a small number
\varepsilon>0
, we take a point
(x_{1},t_{1})
satisfying
\tilde{Z}(x_{1},t_{1})=\varepsilon
for the first time. Clearly,
t_{0}>0
.
Then we have that
\tilde{Z}\leq\varepsilon
on
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,t_{1}]
and
\tilde{Z}(z_{1},t_{1})=\varepsilon
,
and that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{Z}\geq 0,\quad\overline{\nabla}_{i}\tilde{Z}=0,\quad\overline{\nabla}_{i}\overline{\nabla}_{j}\tilde{Z}\leq 0\quad\text{at }(z_{1},t_{1}),

which is contradict to (
4.4
). Thus
\sup_{\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,t]}Z\leq 0
for all
t\in(0,T)
.
∎

Using the lemma above, we obtain the uniform positive lower bound for the Gauss curvature.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be an initial hypersurface and
\Phi
be a shrinking obstacle. If
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{0\leq t<T}
is the solution to (

∗

δ

), then the Gauss curvature of
\Sigma_{t}
has a uniform positive lower bound, i.e.,

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)}K\geq c_{T}>0,

where
c_{T}=c(\alpha,T,\Phi)
is a constant independent of
\delta
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
and the non-positivity of
\beta_{\delta}
, we have

\displaystyle K^{\alpha}=-\partial_{t}u-\beta_{\delta}\geq-\partial_{t}u\geq-\partial_{t}\varphi\geq\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T]}(-\partial_{t}\varphi)>0.

Now the conclusion follows by choosing

5.
Speed estimates II: Uniform upper bounds

In the previous section we proved the positive lower bound on the speed

and the Gauss curvature
K
. In this section we will obtain the opposite bounds on the speed and the Gauss curvature.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be an initial hypersurface and
\Phi
be a shrinking obstacle. If
u:\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a smooth solution of the penalized problem (

∗

δ

), then

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}u\leq C\quad\text{in }\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T),

where
C=C(n,\alpha,\Sigma_{0},\Phi)
is a constant independent of
\delta
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be the support function of the obstacle and
\rho_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\varphi_{\infty}>0
. We consider an auxiliary function on
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T)

\displaystyle w=\frac{K^{\alpha}(z,t)+\beta_{\delta}(u(z,t)-\varphi(z,t))}{u(z,t)-\rho_{0}}.

Notice that the denominator remains positive since

by Lemma
3.1
and the definition of
\rho_{0}
.

Our first task is to derive the evolution equation for the quantity
w

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{j}w

\displaystyle=\frac{\overline{\nabla}_{j}(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})}{u-\rho_{0}}-\frac{K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}}{(u-\rho_{0})^{2}}\overline{\nabla}_{j}(u-\rho_{0}),

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}w

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathcal{L}}(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})}{u-\rho_{0}}-\frac{2\left\langle{\overline{\nabla}(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})},{\overline{\nabla}u}\right\rangle}{(u-\rho_{0})^{2}}-\frac{K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}}{(u-\rho_{0})^{2}}{\mathcal{L}}u+\frac{2(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})}{(u-\rho_{0})^{3}}\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathcal{L}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathcal{L}}(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})}{u-\rho_{0}}-\frac{K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}}{(u-\rho_{0})^{2}}{\mathcal{L}}u+\frac{2}{u-\rho_{0}}\left\langle{\overline{\nabla}w},{\overline{\nabla}u}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}},

we obtain

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})w&=\frac{(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})}{u-\rho_{0}}-\frac{K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}}{(u-\rho_{0})^{2}}(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})u-\frac{2}{u-\rho_{0}}\left\langle{\overline{\nabla}w},{\overline{\nabla}u}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}.\end{split}

It follows from (
2.7
) in
Lemma

2.5
that

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})=\alpha K^{\alpha}H(K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta})+(u-\varphi)_{t}\beta_{\delta}^{\prime}.

Plugging this and the evolution equation (

) in
Lemma

2.5
into (

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})w

\displaystyle=\frac{(u-\varphi)_{t}\beta_{\delta}^{\prime}}{u-\rho_{0}}-\frac{K^{\alpha}+\beta_{\delta}}{(u-\rho_{0})^{2}}(\alpha K^{\alpha}H\rho_{0}-(n\alpha+1)K^{\alpha}-\beta_{\delta})

Therefore, we arrive at

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})w

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{u-\rho_{0}}\left\langle{\overline{\nabla}w},{\overline{\nabla}u}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(-w-\frac{\varphi_{t}}{u-\rho_{0}}\right)\beta_{\delta}^{\prime}-\frac{w}{u-\rho_{0}}(\alpha K^{\alpha}H\rho_{0}-(n\alpha+1)K^{\alpha}-\beta_{\delta}).

Next, we apply the maximum principle argument to
w
. Fix a time

is a compact set and

attains positive maximum value at some point

(5.2)

\displaystyle w\leq\frac{\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}(K_{0})^{\alpha}}{\rho_{0}},

where
K_{0}

\Sigma_{0}
.
Now we assume that
t_{0}>0

w
has an interior maximum in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T^{\prime}]
and thus
w
satisfies at the interior maximum point

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}w=0,\quad\overline{\nabla}^{2}w\leq 0,\quad\partial_{t}w\geq 0.

This gives that

\displaystyle 0\leq\left(-w-\frac{\varphi_{t}}{u-\rho_{0}}\right)\beta_{\delta}^{\prime}-\frac{w}{u-\rho_{0}}(\alpha K^{\alpha}H\rho_{0}-(n\alpha+1)K^{\alpha}-\beta_{\delta})

at

\displaystyle w\leq\frac{-\partial_{t}\varphi}{\rho_{0}}\leq\frac{-\partial_{t}\varphi_{0}}{\rho_{0}}<\frac{\min_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{0}^{\alpha}}{\rho_{0}}

since
\varphi
is convex in time variable.
Otherwise, it follows from

and

that

\displaystyle 0\leq\frac{w}{u-\rho_{0}}(-\alpha K^{\alpha}H\rho_{0}+(n\alpha+1)K^{\alpha})

which implies by the arithmetic-geometric mean and

,

(5.4)

\displaystyle nK^{\frac{1}{n}}\leq H\leq\frac{n\alpha+1}{\alpha\rho_{0}}

at the point

. Hence, combining (

\displaystyle\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,T^{\prime}]}w\leq\frac{1}{\rho_{0}}\max\left\{(\max_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}K_{0})^{\alpha},\left(\frac{n\alpha+1}{n\alpha\rho_{0}}\right)^{n\alpha}\right\}=:C_{1}.

Since

is an arbitrary number in

and the constant

.

Finally, by Lemma

.
∎

6.
Uniform bounds on principal curvatures

Here we establish uniform (independent of

Lemma 6.1

(Euler type formula)

Let
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
be a smooth, strictly convex hypersurface, and let

\displaystyle\frac{h_{ii}(z)}{\overline{g}_{ii}(z)}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{\min}(z)},

where
\overline{g}_{ij}

\mathbb{S}^{n}

\Sigma

Proof.

Fix a point

\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}
are the principal curvatures of
\Sigma
at

\overline{\nabla}_{i}X=\sum_{j}a_{ij}E_{j}
with

, and denote by

\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,t_{1})
by the comparison principle. Since
u_{p}(z_{1},t_{1})=\varphi(z_{1},t_{1})
and
u_{p}\geq u\geq\varphi
, we conclude that
u(z_{1},t_{1})=\varphi(z_{1},t_{1})
.
Hence, setting
T_{*}=(\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}+\rho)^{n\alpha+1}/(n\alpha+1)
yields
u\equiv\varphi
in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[T_{*},\infty)
since
(z_{1},t_{1})
was an arbitrary point in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times(T_{*},\infty)
.

It remains to prove that the principal curvatures
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}
are globally bounded from above and below in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
. Taking
T=T_{*}
in (
7.1
), we get
C_{T_{*}}^{-1}\leq\lambda_{i}\leq C_{T_{*}}
on the time interval
[0,T_{*})
. On another time interval
[T_{*},\infty)
, since
u
is identically equal to
\varphi
and the principal curvatures
\mu_{1},\cdots,\mu_{n}
of the obstacle is globally bounded from above and below in
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
, we can obtain the desired bounds. This completes the proof.
∎

8.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

To prove that the free boundary is locally
C^{1}
graph, we need several ingredients: non-degeneracy (Lemma
8.1
), classification of blowups (
8.4
), continuity of speed (
8.5
), and directional monotonicity (Lemma
8.2
and (
8.7
)).

Let
w
be a solution to the local graph representation of the
\alpha
-Gauss curvature flow with a shrinking obstacle, (
1.2
). Set
v=\phi-w
. Then
v:Q_{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

(8.1)

\displaystyle F(D^{2}v,Dv,x,t)-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}v

\displaystyle=f(Dv,x,t)\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad\Omega(v)=\{v>0\},

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\geq 0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad Q_{1},

\displaystyle|D^{2}v|+|\partial_{t}v|

\displaystyle\leq C\quad

\displaystyle\text{in}\quad Q_{1},

where

\displaystyle F(M,p,x,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\det D^{2}\phi(x,t)^{\alpha}-\det(D^{2}\phi(x,t)-M)^{\alpha}}{(1+|D\phi(x,t)-p|^{2})^{\frac{(n+2)\alpha-1}{2}}},

\displaystyle f(p,x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi(x,t)+\frac{\det D^{2}\phi(x,t)^{\alpha}}{(1+|D\phi(x,t)-p|^{2})^{\frac{(n+2)\alpha-1}{2}}}.

Observe that
F(0,Dv,x,t)=0
,
f\geq c>0
for some constant
c
, and
F(\cdot,Dv)
is convex if
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{n}
. Moreover, the operator
F
is uniformly elliptic since the eigenvalues of
D^{2}w
are bounded above and below by positive constants; there exists a constant
\theta>0
such that
\theta^{-1}|\xi|^{2}\leq F^{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\leq\theta|\xi|^{2}
, where

\displaystyle F^{ij}:=\frac{\partial F}{\partial M_{ij}}(D^{2}v,Dv,x,t)=\frac{\alpha\det(D^{2}w)^{\alpha}[(D^{2}w)^{-1}]^{ij}}{(1+|D\phi-Dv|^{2})^{\frac{(n+2)\alpha-1}{2}}}.

Let us fix some notations. For a point
X=(x,t)
, let
Q_{r}(X)=B_{r}(x)\times(t-r^{2},t)
. We denote by
\partial_{p}Q_{r}(X)
the parabolic boundary of
Q_{r}(X)
.

In Theorem
1.1
, we proved the optimal curvature bounds for the solution, which implies the optimal
C^{1,1}
regularity of the solution. In particular, the solution has at most quadratic growth especially near the free boundary. The following lemma says the quadratic growth is actually achieved near the free boundary.

Lemma 8.1

(non-degeneracy)
.

Let
v
be a solution of (
8.1
) with
\Omega=\Omega(v)
. Then for any
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in\overline{\Omega}\cap Q_{1/2}
, we have the inequality

(8.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{\partial_{p}Q_{r}(X_{0})}v\geq v(X_{0})+\frac{cr^{2}}{2n\theta+1}\quad\text{for all}\quad 0<r<1/4.

Proof.

By an approximation argument, it suffices to prove (
8.2
) for
X_{0}\in\Omega\cap Q_{1/2}
. For a point
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in\Omega\cap Q_{1/2}
, we define a function
\overline{v}:Q_{1}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
by

\displaystyle\overline{v}(x,t)=v(x,t)-\frac{c(|x-x_{0}|^{2}-(t-t_{0}))}{2n\theta+1}.

We claim that
\overline{v}
satisfies
F(D^{2}\overline{v},Dv,x,t)-\partial_{t}\overline{v}\geq 0
in
\Omega\cap Q_{1}
. If the claim holds, then it follows from
\overline{v}(X_{0})=v(X_{0})\geq 0
and
\overline{v}=-\frac{c(|x-x_{0}|^{2}-(t-t_{0}))}{2n\theta+1}<0
on
\partial\Omega
that

\displaystyle 0\leq\sup_{\Omega\cap Q_{r}(X_{0})}\overline{v}\leq\sup_{\partial_{p}Q_{r}(X_{0})}\overline{v}\leq\sup_{\partial_{p}Q_{r}(X_{0})}v-\frac{cr^{2}}{2n\theta+1},

by the maximum principle, which proves (
8.2
).

We now prove the claim. Note that
D^{2}\overline{v}=D^{2}v-\frac{2c}{2n\theta+1}I
, where
I
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix. Then by the uniformly ellipticity, we have

\displaystyle F(D^{2}\overline{v},Dv,x,t)-\partial_{t}\overline{v}

\displaystyle=F(D^{2}v-\frac{2c}{2n\theta+1}I,Dv,x,t)-\partial_{t}v-\frac{c}{2n\theta+1}

\displaystyle\geq F(D^{2}v,Dv,x,t)-\frac{2n\theta c}{2n\theta+1}-\partial_{t}v-\frac{c}{2n\theta+1}

\displaystyle=f(Dv,x,t)-c\geq 0\quad\text{in }\Omega.

Therefore, the claim holds.
∎

Take a free boundary point
X_{0}=(x_{0},t_{0})\in\partial\Omega\cap Q_{1/8}
and consider the rescaled function
v_{r}
(
r>0
) of
v
around
X_{0}
defined by

\displaystyle v_{r}(y,s)=\frac{v(x_{0}+ry,t_{0}+r^{2}s)-v(x_{0},t_{0})}{r^{2}},\quad(y,s)\in Q_{\tfrac{1}{2r}}.

By Theorem
1.1
and the scaling properties
D^{2}_{y}v_{r}(y,s)=D^{2}_{x}v(x_{0}+ry,t_{0}+r^{2}s)
and
\partial_{s}v_{r}(y,s)=\partial_{t}v(x_{0}+ry,t_{0}+r^{2}s)
, the rescaled functions
\{v_{r}\}_{r>0}
have uniform
C^{1,1}
estimates. Then we can extract a converging subsequence
v_{r_{j}}\rightarrow v_{0}
in
C_{\text{loc}}^{1,\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}})
for any
0<\gamma<1
, where
v_{0}\in C^{1,1}_{\text{loc}}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}})
.

By the standard argument for blowups (see Lemma 18 in
[
17
]
or Proposition 3.17 in
[
26
]
), together with the non-degeneracy (Lemma
8.1
),
v_{0}
satisfies

(8.3)

\displaystyle F(D^{2}v_{0}(y,s),0,x_{0},t_{0})-\partial_{s}v_{0}(y,s)=f(0,x_{0},t_{0})\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega(v_{0}).

Indeed, it follows from the equation

\displaystyle F(D^{2}v_{r_{j}},r_{j}Dv_{r_{j}},x_{0}+r_{j}y,t_{0}+r_{j}^{2}s)-\partial_{s}v_{r_{j}}

\displaystyle=f(r_{j}Dv_{r_{j}},x_{0}+r_{j}y,t_{0}+r_{j}^{2}s)

in
\Omega(v_{r_{j}})
by taking
j\rightarrow\infty
, where we used again the scaling properties
D_{y}v_{r}=D_{x}v/r
,
D^{2}_{y}v_{r}=D^{2}v
, and
D_{s}v_{r}=D_{t}v
.

Recall that
F
is a convex operator in
D^{2}v
variable since
\alpha\leq 1/n
, that
F(0,0,x_{0},t_{0})=0
, and that
F(\cdot,0,x_{0},t_{0})
is uniformly elliptic. By the work of Figalli and Shahgholian
[
13
, Proposition 3.2]
on the classification of global solutions to (
8.3
), it follows from the uniform thickness condition in Theorem
1.2
that, after a rotation,

(8.4)

\displaystyle v_{0}(y,s)=\tfrac{\gamma}{2}[(x_{1})_{+}]^{2},

where
\gamma\in(1/\theta,\theta)
such that
F(\gamma e_{1}\otimes e_{1},0,x_{0},t_{0})=f(0,x_{0},t_{0})
. In particular,
v_{0}
is time-independent.

We now claim the continuity of the speed,

(8.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{X\rightarrow\partial\Omega}\partial_{t}v(X)=0,

under the uniform thickness assumption.
We prove the claim by contradiction.
If there exists a sequence
X_{j}\in\Omega(v)
such that
X_{j}\rightarrow\partial\Omega(v)
and
|\partial_{t}v(X_{j})|\geq\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, we define
d_{j}=\text{dist}(X_{j},\partial\Omega(v))
and consider

\displaystyle v_{d_{j}}(y,s)=\frac{v(x_{j}+d_{j}y,t_{j}+d_{j}^{2}s)-v(x_{j},t_{j})}{d_{j}^{2}}.

Then it can be verified that
v_{d_{j}}
converges to a global solution
\tilde{v}_{0}
of (
8.3
) with
|\partial_{s}\tilde{v}(0)|>0
, which contradicts the fact that the global solution
\tilde{v}_{0}
is time-independent.

Our next lemma is the key ingredient of the directional monotonicity for solutions to

(8.6)

\displaystyle F(D^{2}v,rDv,x_{0}+ry,t_{0}+r^{2}s)-\partial_{s}v

\displaystyle=f(rDv,x_{0}+ry,t_{0}+r^{2}s).

Note that the rescaled function
v_{r}
solves (
8.6
) in
\Omega(v_{r})
.
For simplicity, we assume for a moment
(x_{0},t_{0})=(0,0)
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Assume that
v
satisfies (
8.6
) in
\Omega(v)
. For a number
\kappa\in(0,1)
, let
e=(e_{x},e_{t})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
be any space-time direction satisfying
e\cdot(e_{1},0)\geq\kappa>0
.
If
C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v-v\geq-\varepsilon_{0}
in
Q_{1}
for some constants
C_{\kappa}
and
\varepsilon_{0}
, then
C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v-v\geq 0
in
Q_{1/2}
provided that
\varepsilon_{0}<\frac{c}{32(2n\theta+1)}
and
0<r\leq\frac{c}{2C}
, where
C
is a constant depending only on
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{1}},\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{C^{1}},\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{1}}
and
\kappa
.

Proof.

By convexity of
F(\cdot,rDv,ry,r^{2}s)
and
F(0,rDv,ry,r^{2}s)=0
, we have

\displaystyle F(D^{2}v,rDv,ry,r^{2}s)-F^{ij}(D^{2}v,rDv,ry,r^{2}s)\partial_{ij}v\leq F(0,rDv,ry,r^{2}s)=0.

Thus we obtain
F^{ij}\partial_{ij}v-\partial_{s}v\geq F-\partial_{s}v=f\geq c>0
. On the other hand, by differentiating (
8.6
) with respect to a direction
e\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
, we get

\displaystyle Lv_{e}:=(F^{ij}\partial_{ij}-\partial_{s}+r(F_{p_{i}}-f_{p_{i}})\partial_{i})v_{e}=r(f_{x}-F_{x})\cdot e_{x}+r^{2}(f_{t}-F_{t})e_{t},

where
\partial_{i}=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y_{i}}
and
v_{e}=e_{x}\cdot D_{y}v+e_{t}\partial_{s}v
.
Since
f_{x}
,
F_{x}
,
f_{t}
, and
F_{t}
are bounded, the right hand side is as small as we want, provided
r>0
is sufficiently small.

Assume by contradiction that there is
(y_{0},s_{0})\in Q_{\frac{1}{2}}\cap\Omega(v)
such that
C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v(y_{0},s_{0})-v(y_{0},s_{0})<0
. If we consider

\displaystyle\overline{v}(x,t)=C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v-v+\frac{c(|y-y_{0}|^{2}-(s-s_{0}))}{2(2n\theta+1)},

where
c
is the lower bound of
f
and
\theta
is the ellipticity constant, then

\displaystyle L\overline{v}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\kappa}(r(f_{x}-F_{x})\cdot e_{x}+r^{2}(f_{t}-F_{t})e_{t})

\displaystyle\quad-f-r((F_{p_{i}}-f_{p_{i}})\partial_{i}v+\frac{c}{2}+r(F_{p_{i}}-f_{p_{i}})(y-y_{0})_{i}

\displaystyle\leq rC(\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{C^{1}},\left\lVert F\right\rVert_{C^{1}},\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C^{1}},\kappa)-\frac{c}{2}.

Hence, for sufficiently small
r
, we have
L\overline{v}\leq 0
in
Q_{1/4}(y_{0},s_{0})\cap\Omega(v)
. By the minimum principle, the minimum is achieved on the boundary of
Q_{1/4}(y_{0},s_{0})\cap\Omega(v)
. However, since
\overline{v}>0
on
\Omega(v)
and
\overline{v}(y_{0},s_{0})<0
, the function
\overline{v}
attains its minimum on
\partial_{p}Q_{1/4}(y_{0},s_{0})
. Therefore, we conclude that

\displaystyle 0>\min_{\partial_{p}(Q_{1/4}(y_{0},s_{0})\cap\Omega(v))}\overline{v}=-\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{c}{32(2n\theta+1)},

which is a contradiction.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
1.2
, we take any number
\kappa\in(0,1)
and consider any direction
e=(e_{x},e_{t})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\subset R^{n+1}
such that
e\cdot(e_{1},0)=e_{x}\cdot e_{1}\geq\kappa>0
. Recall that the limit
v_{0}
of
v_{r_{j}}
has the form
\frac{\gamma}{2}[(x_{1})_{+}]^{2}
. Then for the constant
C_{\kappa}=2/\kappa
, we have
C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v_{0}-v_{0}\geq 0
in
Q_{1}
. Using the
C^{1}
convergence
v_{r_{j}}
in the space variable and the continuity of the speed (
8.5
), we induce
C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v_{r_{j}}-v_{r_{j}}\geq-\varepsilon_{0}
in
Q_{1}
for sufficiently large
j\geq j_{0}
, where
\varepsilon_{0}
is the constant given in Lemma
8.2
. Then by Lemma
8.2
, we obtain the improved inequality

(8.7)

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}\partial_{e}v_{r_{j}}-v_{r_{j}}\geq 0\quad\text{in}\quad Q_{1/2}

for
j\geq j_{0}
. Since
v_{r_{j}}\geq 0
, this implies
\partial_{e}v_{r_{j}}\geq 0
in
Q_{1/2}
. Scaling back to
v
, we conclude that there exists
r=r(\kappa)>0
such that
\partial_{e}v\geq 0
in
Q_{r}(X_{0})
for all directions
e=(e_{x},e_{t})\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
satisfying
e\cdot(e_{1},0)\geq\kappa>0
. This together with a simple compactness argument implies that the free boundary
\Gamma(v)
is
\kappa
-Lipschitz graph for any
\kappa\in(0,1)
. Then the
C^{1}
graphness of the free boundary
\Gamma(v)
follows by the standard argument, see
[
26
]
for instance.
