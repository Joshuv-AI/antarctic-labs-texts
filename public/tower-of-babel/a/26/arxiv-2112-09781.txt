Abstract
Jordan algebras arise naturally in (quantum) information geometry, and we want to understand their role and their structure within that framework. Inspired by Kirillov’s discussion of the symplectic structure on coadjoint orbits, we provide a similar construction in the case of real Jordan algebras. Given a real, finite-dimensional, formally real Jordan algebra 𝒥 \mathcal{J} , we exploit the generalized distribution determined by the Jordan product on the dual 𝒥 ⋆ \mathcal{J}^{\star} to induce a pseudo-Riemannian metric tensor on the leaves of the distribution. In particular, these leaves are the orbits of a Lie group, which is the structure group of 𝒥 \mathcal{J} , in clear analogy with what happens for coadjoint orbits. However, this time in contrast with the Lie-algebraic case, we prove that not all points in 𝒥 ∗ \mathcal{J}^{*} lie on a leaf of the canonical Jordan distribution. When the leaves are contained in the cone of positive linear functionals on 𝒥 \mathcal{J} , the pseudo-Riemannian structure becomes Riemannian and, for appropriate choices of 𝒥 \mathcal{J} , it coincides with the Fisher–Rao metric on non-normalized probability distributions on a finite sample space, or with the Bures–Helstrom metric for non-normalized, faithful quantum states of a finite-level quantum system, thus showing a direct link between the mathematics of Jordan algebras and both classical and quantum information geometry.

Introduction

Since their introduction in
[
46
]
in the context of the foundations of quantum mechanics, Jordan algebras proved to be extremely versatile in both mathematics and physics.
For instance, we mention the link between Jordan algebras, symmetric and harmonic analysis
[
18
,
19
,
30
,
52
,
78
]
, the connection between Jordan algebras and quantum theories
[
7
,
27
,
29
]
, and the role Jordan algebras play in the reconstruction of quantum theories
[
62
,
79
]
.
We also refer to
[
43
]
for an extensive discussion of different fields of application of Jordan algebras in both mathematics and physics.

Motivated by classical and quantum information geometry, we want to present here another point of view from which to explore Jordan algebras and their mathematics.

Information geometry is a multidisciplinary field of research in which different aspects of mathematics, physics, statistics, and information theory coexist and mutually influence each other.
From a mathematical point of view, information geometry explores the mathematical structures living on suitable manifolds of classical probability distributions or quantum states, known as parametric models, and their relation with information-theoretic and statistical tasks
[
3
,
4
]
.
In particular, we have the fundamental concept of an information metric (classical or quantum), a Riemannian metric tensor on a manifold
M
that parameterizes classical probability distributions or quantum states.
The first example of such an information metric is the so-called Fisher–Rao metric tensor, whose introduction in the classical context traces back to the work of Fisher
[
31
]
, Mahalanobis
[
59
]
, and Rao
[
66
]
.
The appearance of the Fisher–Rao metric tensor in different applied contexts like population genetics and statistical inference is partially explained by Cencov’s theorem
[
16
]
which states that, for finite outcome spaces, the Fisher–Rao metric tensor is the only metric tensor which is invariant with respect to the class of Markov kernels which are the most general type of maps respecting the convex geometry of the space of probability distributions.
Cencov’s theorem has recently been extended to continuous outcome spaces
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
33
]
.

In the quantum case, the situation is not so simple. The first type of quantum information metric appeared in the context of parameter estimation and quantum metrology already at the end of the sixties in the work of Helstrom
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
.
This Riemannian metric tensor is known as the Bures–Helstrom because it was proved by Uhlmann
[
76
,
77
]
that it is the “infinitesimal” version of the distance function among states of a von Neumann algebra introduced by Bures
[
15
]
to generalize a result by Kakutani
[
47
]
.
Later, it has been proved by Petz
[
64
]
– building on previous works by Cencov and Morozowa
[
61
]
– that uniqueness is lost in the finite-dimensional quantum case, that is, there is no analogue of Cencov’s theorem mentioned above.
This led to an intensive study of the so-called monotone quantum metric tensors generalizing the Fisher–Rao metric tensor to the quantum case
[
21
,
28
,
34
,
35
,
56
,
60
]
and of their information-theoretic and statistical applications
[
58
,
63
,
73
,
74
,
80
]
.
However, the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor remains the “best one” when it comes to quantum metrology because it leads to the tightest version of the quantum Cramer–Rao bound
[
32
,
63
]
.

A common framework to deal with classical and quantum information geometry simultaneously is provided by
W^{*}
-algebras
[
22
,
23
,
24
,
44
,
45
,
54
]
.
In this context, it has been recently argued that the Fisher–Rao metric tensor and the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor are related through the Jordan algebra associated with a
W^{*}
-algebra
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
These types of Jordan algebras allow for a unification of the classical and quantum states in terms of normal states (i.e., the normalized and normal positive linear functionals) on the algebra.
When the Jordan algebra is the associative Jordan algebra of self-adjoint elements in the commutative
W^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
of complex-valued,
\mu
-essentially bounded (equivalence classes of) functions on the measurable space
(\mathcal{X},\Sigma)
, the normal states are probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
which are absolutely continuous with respect to
\mu
, and we recover the classical case.
The quantum case is recovered considering the Jordan algebra of self-adjoint elements in the
W^{*}
-algebra
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
of bounded linear operators on the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, so that the normal states are density operators on
\mathcal{H}
(i.e., trace-class, positive semidefinite operators with unit trace) which identify quantum states.
In this context, the Jordan product induces a Riemannian metric tensor on suitable parametric models of normal states, which becomes the Fisher–Rao metric tensor when the algebra is
\mathcal{L}^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mu)
and the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor when the algebra is
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

In the finite-dimensional case, it has been observed that the metric tensor on normal states may be obtained as a kind of inverse of a contravariant tensor on the dual space of the Jordan algebra determined by the Jordan product itself
[
23
]
.

Let us recall that setting so that we can subsequently explain how to abstract and generalize it in order to develop new insight into classical and quantum information geometry and at the same time into the structure of Jordan algebras from that of Lie algebras operating on them.
Thus, we let
\mathscr{A}
be a finite-dimensional, unital
C^{*}
-algebra, and let
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
be the self-adjoint part of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{V}
be the self-adjoint part of the Banach dual
\mathscr{A}^{*}
of
\mathscr{A}
.
Of course, there is some duality here, as
\mathbf{a}\in\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
can be identified with a real-valued, linear function
l_{\mathbf{a}}
on
\mathscr{V}
, via

l_{\mathbf{a}}(\xi):=\xi(\mathbf{a}).

Since
\mathscr{V}
is a finite-dimensional Banach space,
\mathbf{a}\mapsto l_{\mathbf{a}}
yields an isomorphism between
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
and
\mathscr{V}^{*}=\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}^{**}
. In other words, the differentials of the linear functions on
\mathscr{V}
associated with elements in
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
generate the cotangent space
T_{\xi}^{*}\mathscr{V}
at each
\xi
.

The associative product of
\mathscr{A}
leads to a commutative product
\{,\}
and to a non-commutative product
[[\,,\,]]
on
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
, making
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
into a
Banach–Lie–Jordan algebra

[
1
]
; they are

\displaystyle\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}:=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{ab}+\mathbf{ba}),\qquad[[\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]]:=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{-1}}(\mathbf{ab}-\mathbf{ba}).

We can then exploit these products to introduce a symmetric and an antisymmetric tensor on the dual of
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
.
Both these tensors play a role in the context of the geometry of finite-level quantum systems and their open quantum dynamical evolutions
[
17
,
20
]
.

Specifically, we define

\left(\mathcal{R}(\mathrm{d}l_{\mathbf{a}},\mathrm{d}l_{\mathbf{b}})\right)(\xi):=l_{\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}}(\xi)=\xi(\{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\}),

and, of course, extend it by linearity to the entire cotangent space, obtaining a symmetric contravariant tensor associated with the Jordan product.

In the same way that the Jordan structure induces the symmetric tensor
\mathcal{R}
, the Lie structure also induces an antisymmetric tensor

\left(\Lambda(\mathrm{d}f_{1},\mathrm{d}f_{2})\right)(\xi):=\xi\left([[\mathrm{d}f_{1}(\xi),\mathrm{d}f_{2}(\xi)]]\right).

Since the Lie algebra of the group
\mathscr{U}
of unitary elements in
\mathscr{A}
may be identified with the space
\mathscr{A}_{\rm sa}
of self-adjoint elements in
\mathscr{A}
, the tensor
\Lambda
may be interpreted as the Kostant–Kirillov–Souriau Poisson tensor associated with the coadjoint action of the unitary group
\mathscr{U}
.
Importantly, for our purposes, also
\mathcal{R}
can be studied with the help of the action of this Lie algebra.

Returning to the covariant symmetric tensor
\mathcal{R}
, it happens that, on certain orbits of the Banach–Lie group of invertible elements of
\mathscr{A}
(of which
\mathscr{U}
is a subgroup), it makes sense to consider the covariant tensor
\mathrm{G}:=\mathcal{R}^{-1}
, which becomes a Riemannian metric tensor when the above-mentioned orbits lie in the space of positive linear functionals on
\mathscr{A}
. The result is a Riemannian metric tensor, which is precisely that used in classical and quantum information geometry.

The observation in
[
23
]
that we have just explained only hinted at the possibility of obtaining Riemannian metric tensors with a coadjoint orbit-like procedure, and the main purpose of this work is to systematically explore this possibility in the more general context of finite-dimensional, formally real Jordan algebras.

In fact, the action of the Lie algebra of
\mathscr{U}
is reminiscent of what happens in the case of Lie groups and Lie algebras, where the Lie algebra induces a contravariant tensor on its dual that can be inverted on the so-called coadjoint orbits in order to equip them with a symplectic structure known as the Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau (KKS) symplectic form
[
48
,
49
,
53
,
71
]
.

Therefore, in this paper, we develop a more abstract perspective, moving from the Jordan algebras arising from
C^{*}
-algebras to general Jordan algebras. In particular, this will clarify how much of the above construction actually depends on the ambient
C^{*}
-algebras.
In fact, we shall find that, at least when we deal with finite-dimensional formally real Jordan algebras, the ambient
C^{*}
-algebras play no role.
On one hand, this will provide a deeper explanation of the Fisher–Rao and Bures–Helstrom tensors and their generalizations. On the other hand, from
this more general perspective, we obtain Riemannian metric tensors on suitable models of positive linear functionals on Jordan algebras that do not necessarily arise in the context of classical and quantum information geometry as the spin factors that are recently being employed in a new formulation of color perception theory
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
65
,
67
]
.

As anticipated above, our guiding question is to what extent we can develop a coadjoint orbit-like construction for Jordan algebras that is analogous to that of Lie algebras, at least in finite dimensions.
Specifically, we start from Kirillov’s fundamental observation that a coadjoint orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}
of the Lie group
\mathrm{G}
carries a natural homogeneous symplectic structure
[
48
,
49
]
.
Here,
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}
denote the Lie algebra of
\mathrm{G}
and its dual.
Kirillov’s observation relates algebraic structures to differential geometry and mathematical physics in a deep and very productive way, led to spectacular results in representation theory, classical and quantum mechanics, and it is closely related to geometric quantization
[
50
]
.

As far as we know, no analogue of the coadjoint orbit construction for Lie algebras has been investigated in the case of Jordan algebras, and the main purpose of this work is precisely to fill this gap.
To understand the picture from a more abstract perspective, we need to go somewhat beyond the setting sketched above. We shall start by considering a general, finite-dimensional algebra
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., a finite-dimensional (real or complex) vector space with a bilinear product
\bullet\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
.
At this moment, in contrast to the setting above, no further conditions like associativity or identities of Jacobi/Jordan type are assumed.
We denote with
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
the dual space of
\mathcal{A}
, and the corresponding pairing is denoted by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Due to the finite-dimensionality, we have the identification
\mathcal{A}^{\star\star}\cong\mathcal{A}
, and
for the tangent and cotangent spaces of
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, we then have
T_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}\cong\mathcal{A}^{\star}
and
T^{\ast}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}\cong\mathcal{A}^{\star\star}\cong\mathcal{A}
. We may then represent the product
\bullet

via

\langle\xi,a\bullet b\rangle\qquad\text{for }\xi\in\mathcal{A}^{\star},\ a,b\in\mathcal{A}.

(1.1)

Furthermore, this induces a multiplication on
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})
via

f\bullet g(\xi):=\langle\xi,d_{\xi}f\bullet d_{\xi}g\rangle,

(1.2)

and for
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})
, we may define its
\mathcal{A}
-dual vector field of
f
as

\big(\nabla^{\mathcal{A}}(f)\big)_{\xi}(g):=\{f,g\}_{\mathcal{A}}(\xi).

Note that, in general, this is not a gradient because
\bullet
need not be symmetric, but we use the symbol
\nabla
because it satisfies many of the formal identities of gradients.

The multiplication in equation (
1.2
) is of course compatible with the product
\bullet
in the sense that, identifying
a\in\mathcal{A}
with the linear functional
f_{a}(\xi):=\langle\xi,a\rangle
, the inclusion
\mathcal{A}\hookrightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})
is an algebra monomomorphism

f_{a}\bullet f_{b}=f_{a\bullet b},

justifying the symbol
\bullet
to denote the multiplication on both
\mathcal{A}
and
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})\supset\mathcal{A}
. The automorphisms of
\mathcal{A}
, i.e., the linear isomorphisms
g\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
with
g(a\bullet b)=(ga)\bullet(gb)
, form a Lie group.
The Lie
algebra of that group consists of the derivations, i.e., the linear maps
d\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
with
d(a\bullet b)=(da)\bullet b+a\bullet(db)
.
Moreover, we have the structure Lie group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
whose Lie algebra is generated by left multiplications, i.e., by the maps
l_{a}\colon(b\mapsto a\bullet b)\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
.

Therefore, even though we do not assume
\mathcal{A}
to be a Lie algebra, there is a Lie algebra that is naturally associated to
\mathcal{A}
, and we may hope to use its theory to gain insight about
\mathcal{A}
itself.
This works to some extent, but a problem arises from the fact that the
\mathcal{A}
-dual distribution

\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
defined by

\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}:=\big\{\big(\nabla^{\mathcal{A}}(f)\big)_{\xi}\mid f\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})\big\}\subset T_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}.

is in general not integrable.
We recall that, in the Lie algebra case,
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
is integrable, and its leaves are precisely the coadjoint orbits which carry a symplectic structure induced by equation (
1.2
).
Because
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
is not integrable for general algebras, we cannot expect the same level of generality as in Lie algebras.

However, the results become stronger if we also assume some additional structures on
\mathcal{A}
.
Associativity already gives us some leverage, but the more specific case that we are interested in here is when
\mathcal{A}
is a Jordan algebra.
Our strategy then is to combine this Jordan structure, and the identities resulting from it, with the Lie algebra structure that we just have identified.

Thus, we consider a finite-dimensional real Jordan algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{J}
.
In this case, we can also define an extended structure Lie algebra
\hat{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{J})
that maps surjectively onto
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{J})

[
13
,
52
,
55
]
.
This algebra is the direct sum
\mathcal{D}{\rm er}_{0}(\mathcal{J})\oplus\mathcal{J}
of the inner derivations (those generated by left multiplications
l_{x}
with algebra elements
x
) and
\mathcal{J}
itself.
The Lie bracket on
\mathcal{D}{\rm er}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
is the commutator, while, for
x,y\in\mathcal{J}
, it is
[x,y]:=[l_{x},l_{y}]\in\mathcal{D}{\rm er}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
, and, for
d\in\mathcal{D}{\rm er}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
and
x\in\mathcal{J}
, it is
[d,x]=-[x,d]:=d(x)\in\mathcal{J}
.
Therefore, putting
{\mathfrak{k}}=\mathcal{D}{\rm er}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
,
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{J}
, we have
[{\mathfrak{k}},{\mathfrak{k}}]\subset{\mathfrak{k}}
,
[{\mathfrak{k}},{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}]\subset{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
,
[{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}},{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}]={\mathfrak{k}}
, i.e., we have a transvective symmetric pair (see Definition
2.5
) which provides us with further structure to work with.

The generalized distribution
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{J}}
on
\mathcal{J}^{\star}
is still not integrable in general, but the bilinear form
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
induced by equation (
1.1
) on
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{J}}_{\xi}
is symmetric, and therefore it defines a pseudo-Riemannian metric on the
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular part of each
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit
\mathcal{O}^{\rm reg}_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}}\subset\mathcal{J}^{\star}
(see Definition
2.3
).

On
\mathcal{J}
there is the symmetric, bilinear form
\tau(x,y):=\operatorname{tr}l_{\{x,y\}}
, which is also associative with respect to the Jordan product, and we can use it to decompose
\mathcal{J}

[
52
, p. 59]
.
Specifically, in the case of
positive Jordan algebras
,
\tau
is positive definite so that there is a canonical identification
{\mathcal{J}\cong\mathcal{J}^{\star}}
, and we can also derive additional properties from the above-mentioned decomposition.
Each
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}
has a
spectral decomposition
associating with
\xi
its
spectral coefficients
(\lambda_{i})_{i=1}^{r}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r}
, where
r
denotes the rank of
\mathcal{J}
. The pair
(n_{+},n_{-})
counting the number of positive and negative spectral coefficients is called the
spectral signature of
\xi
. We then show the following:

Theorem A

(cf. Theorem
4.7
)
.

If
\mathcal{J}
is a positive simple real Jordan algebra, then the orbits of the structure group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
consist of all elements with the same spectral signature.

We also characterize the
regular
points (i.e., those where the
generalized distribution
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{J}}
is integrable) in such a
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit in Theorem
4.8
and describe the pseudo-Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
at each regular point
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}
in Proposition
4.9
.
Specifically, let
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
denote the
cone of squares of
\mathcal{J}
, i.e., the interior of the set
\big\{x^{2}\mid x\in\mathcal{J}\big\}
. Then
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
is the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit of the identity
{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{J}}
.
The characterizations in Theorem
4.8
, Proposition
3.3
, and Proposition
4.9
lead to the following remarkable description:

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be a positive simple real Jordan
algebra. Then all points of a
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{J}^{\star}
are
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular iff
\mathcal{O}\subset\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
or
\mathcal{O}\subset-\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
. The form
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{O}
is positive definite in the first and negative definite in the second case, thus defining a Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{O}
which is invariant with respect to the action of the automorphism group of
\mathcal{J}
.

For all regular
\xi\notin\pm\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
the form
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
is indefinite, so the definiteness of
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
gives a new characterization of
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
.
We provide descriptions of the orbits
\mathcal{O}
and the metric
\mathcal{G}
for the standard examples of positive simple real algebras. Moreover, the above results easily generalize to the case of non-simple positive Jordan algebras, as these algebras are direct sums of positive simple Jordan algebras.

This work is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we set the notation and recall some standard results on generalized distributions and group actions on manifolds that are needed.
In Section
3
, we discuss the structure group and the generalized distributions on an arbitrary finite-dimensional algebra
\mathcal{A}
.
In Section
4
, we focus on Jordan algebras, prove the main results, and discuss relevant examples.
Let us stress that some results recalled in Section
4
are well known to researchers working with Jordan algebras, but we decided to recall them nonetheless in order to make the work as self-contained
as possible for readers, perhaps coming from information geometry, who are not familiar with Jordan algebras.
Finally, in Section
5
, we discuss our results in relation with some important established results on the mathematics and geometry of Jordan algebras.

Preliminary material

2.1
Notational conventions

For a finite-dimensional (real or complex) vector space
V
, we denote by
{\mathrm{Gl}}(V)
the Lie group of linear automorphisms, whose Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{gl}}(V)
consists of all linear endomorphisms of
V
.

For finite-dimensional vector spaces
V
,
W
and
U
, we define the contraction map

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{V}\colon\ V^{\star}\otimes V\otimes U\longrightarrow U,\qquad\langle\alpha,v\otimes u\rangle_{V}:=\alpha(v)u,

(2.1)

where
V^{\star}
denotes the dual space of
V
, and we shall usually omit the subscript if this causes no ambiguity. The notation (
2.1
) can also be used to denote maps

V^{\star}\otimes\Lambda^{k}V^{\star}\otimes U\longrightarrow\Lambda^{k-1}V^{\star}\otimes U,\qquad V^{\star}\otimes S^{k}V^{\star}\otimes U\longrightarrow S^{k-1}V^{\star}\otimes U,

denoting by
S^{k}V^{\star}
the symmetric
k
-forms, since
\Lambda^{k}V^{\star}\hookrightarrow V\otimes\Lambda^{k-1}V^{\star}
and
S^{k}V^{\star}\hookrightarrow V\otimes S^{k-1}V^{\star}
are canonically included.

Finally, the dual of a linear map
\phi\colon V\to W
is denoted as

\phi^{\star}\colon\ W^{\star}\longrightarrow V^{\star},\qquad\langle\theta,\phi(v)\rangle_{W}=\langle\phi^{\star}\theta,v\rangle_{V}.

2.2
Generalized distributions

Let
M
be a finite-dimensional, real smooth manifold.
A
generalized distribution on
M
is a family
\mathcal{D}=(\mathcal{D}_{p})_{p\in M}
of subspaces
\mathcal{D}_{p}\subset T_{p}M
.
We let
\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
be the set of (smooth) vector fields
X
on
M
with
X_{p}\in\mathcal{D}_{p}
for all
p
, and we call
\mathcal{D}

smooth
if for each
v\in\mathcal{D}_{p}
there is a vector field
X\in\Gamma(\mathcal{D})
with
X_{p}=v
.

Given a smooth generalized distribution
\mathcal{D}
, we define the
Frobenius tensor
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
at
p\in M
as

{\mathcal{F}}_{p}\colon\ \Lambda^{2}\mathcal{D}_{p}\longrightarrow T_{p}M/\mathcal{D}_{p},\qquad(X_{p},Y_{p})\longmapsto[X,Y]_{p}\mod\mathcal{D}_{p}

for

. It is straightforward to verify that

depends on

and

only, i.e.,

is a well-defined tensor field. We also define the generalized distribution

{}[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}]_{p}:=\mathcal{D}_{p}+\{[X,Y]_{p}\mid X,Y\in\Gamma(\mathcal{D})\},

so that the image of
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}
is

.

Definition 2.1
.

We call a smooth generalized distribution
\mathcal{D}

involutive at

, if
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}=0
or, equivalently, if
[\mathcal{D},\mathcal{D}]_{p}=\mathcal{D}_{p}
, and we call it

, if this holds for every

.

Furthermore, an (immersed) submanifold

with

for all

is called an

\mathcal{D}
. If there is an integral leaf of
\mathcal{D}
containing

\mathcal{D}

integrable at
p\in M
and call

an

\mathcal{D}
; if this is the case for each

\mathcal{D}

integrable
.

Clearly, if
\mathcal{D}
is integrable (at
p
), then it is also involutive (at
p
); according to Frobenius’ theorem, the converse of this statement holds if
\mathcal{D}
has constant rank. However, if the rank of
\mathcal{D}
is non-constant, then the converse may fail to hold

72
]
.

2.3
\boldsymbol{G}
-manifolds

Let
G
be a finite-dimensional, real Lie group with identity element

, Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}\cong T_{e}G
, and let
M
be a
G
-manifold, i.e., a finite-dimensional, real smooth manifold with a smooth left action
\pi\colon G\times M\to M
,
(g,p)\mapsto g\cdot p
. For
p\in M
we define the
stabilizer of

to be the subgroup

H_{p}:=\{g\in G\mid g\cdot p=p\}\subset G\qquad\text{with Lie algebra}\quad{\mathfrak{h}}_{p}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}.

Evidently,
H_{g\cdot p}=gHg^{-1}
, and
{\mathfrak{h}}_{g\cdot p}=\operatorname{Ad}_{g}({\mathfrak{h}}_{p})
, so that the stabilizer on each
G
-orbit is unique up to conjugation.

We define the
orbit distribution on
M
by

\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{p}:=\{(X_{\ast})_{p}\mid X\in{\mathfrak{g}}\},

where
X_{\ast}
denotes the action field on
M
. Evidently,
\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{g}}
is integrable, as the
G
-orbits are integral leaves of
\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Moreover,

X\in{\mathfrak{h}}_{p}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad(X_{\ast})_{p}=0.

Also recall that the map
X\mapsto X_{\ast}
is a anti-homomorphism of Lie algebras, i.e.,

{}[X,Y]_{\ast}=-[X_{\ast},Y_{\ast}].

(2.2)

For any linear subspace
{\mathfrak{m}}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}
, we define the smooth generalized distribution
\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}
by

\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}:=\{(X_{\ast})_{p}\mid X\in{\mathfrak{m}}\}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{g}}_{p}=T_{p}(G\cdot p),

(2.3)

and evidently,

(X_{\ast})_{p}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad X\in{\mathfrak{m}}+{\mathfrak{h}}_{p}.

(2.4)

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\mathfrak{m}}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}
be a linear subspace. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}
is involutive at
p\in\mathcal{O}
,

(2)

[{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}]\subset{\mathfrak{m}}+{\mathfrak{h}}_{p}
,

(3)

\mathcal{D}^{[{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}]}_{p}\subset\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}
.

Proof.

For
X,Y\in{\mathfrak{m}}
,
{\mathcal{F}}_{p}(X_{p},Y_{p})=0
iff
[X_{\ast},Y_{\ast}]_{p}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}
, which, by equation (
2.2
), is the case iff
([X,Y]_{\ast})_{p}\in\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}
, and, by equation (
2.4
), this is the case iff
[X,Y]\in{\mathfrak{m}}+{\mathfrak{h}}_{p}
, showing the equivalence of the first two conditions.

The second condition is equivalent to saying that for each
X\in[{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}]
there is a
Y\in{\mathfrak{m}}
such that
X-Y\in{\mathfrak{h}}_{p}
or, equivalently, that for each
X\in[{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}]
there is a
Y\in{\mathfrak{m}}
such that
(X_{\ast})_{p}=(Y_{\ast})_{p}
, and this is evidently equivalent to the third condition.
∎

Definition 2.3
.

Let
M
be a
G
-manifold and
{\mathfrak{m}}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}
a linear subspace. We call
p\in M
an
{\mathfrak{m}}
-regular point
if
\mathcal{D}^{\mathfrak{m}}_{p}=T_{p}\mathcal{O}
, where
\mathcal{O}\subset M
is the

-orbit of

. The subset of

-regular points in

As the rank of

is open (but possibly empty).
As we shall see in later sections,

Corollary 2.4
.

Suppose that
{\mathfrak{m}}\subset{\mathfrak{g}}
is a linear subspace such that

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{m}}+[{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}].

(2.5)

Then for each

the following are equivalent:

(1)

p
is involutive,

(2)

p
is integrable,

(3)

is an

-regular point.

In this case, the maximal integral leaf through

Proof.

By Lemma

,

Definition 2.5
.

A

is a pair

of Lie algebras with a decomposition

satisfying

{}[{\mathfrak{k}},{\mathfrak{k}}]\subset{\mathfrak{k}},\qquad[{\mathfrak{k}},{\mathfrak{m}}]\subset{\mathfrak{m}},\qquad[{\mathfrak{m}},{\mathfrak{m}}]\subset{\mathfrak{k}}.

(2.6)

We call this pair

3
Structure groups and canonical distributions

on duals of algebras

In this section, we shall consider a finite-dimensional algebra
\mathcal{A}
, by which we simply mean a finite-dimensional (real or complex) vector space with a bilinear product
\bullet\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
, i.e., a constant
(2,1)
-tensor.
We do not assume any further conditions on this multiplication such as associativity, Jacobi or Jordan identities, but we shall later discuss the general definitions in each of these cases.

The dual of
\bullet
is a map
\mathcal{A}^{\star}\to\mathcal{A}^{\star}\otimes\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, and as
T_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}\cong\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, we may regard this as a linear bivector field on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
:

\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{A}^{\star},T\mathcal{A}^{\star}\otimes T\mathcal{A}^{\star}),\qquad\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}(a,b):=\langle\xi,a\bullet b\rangle

(3.1)

for all
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
.
Therefore, there is an induced multiplication on the space
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})
of real-valued, smooth functions on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
which by abuse of notation we also denote by
\bullet
, given by

f\bullet g(\xi):=\big(\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{A}}\big)_{\xi}(d_{\xi}f,d_{\xi}g)=\langle\xi,d_{\xi}f\bullet d_{\xi}g\rangle

(3.2)

with the canonical identification
d_{\xi}f,d_{\xi}g\in T^{\ast}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}\cong\mathcal{A}^{\star\star}\cong\mathcal{A}
. In particular, regarding
\mathcal{A}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathcal{A}^{\star})
as the set of linear functions
f_{a}(\xi):=\langle\xi,a\rangle
, equation (
3.2
) implies

f_{a}\bullet f_{b}=f_{a\bullet b},

justifying the ambiguous use of the symbol
\bullet
. Contraction in the first entry yields a linear map

\sharp_{\xi}\colon\ T^{\star}_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}\longrightarrow T_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star},\qquad\theta\longmapsto\theta\lrcorner\mathcal{R}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi},

(3.3)

whose image we call the
\mathcal{A}
-dual distribution

\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
by

\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}:=\sharp_{\xi}(T_{\xi}^{\star}\mathcal{A}^{\star})\subset T_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}.

(3.4)

Definition 3.1
.

For a finite-dimensional algebra
(\mathcal{A},\bullet)
we define the following:

(1)

For
a\in\mathcal{A}
we let
l_{a}\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
be the map
(b\mapsto a\bullet b)\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
.

(2)

The
structure Lie algebra of
\mathcal{A}

is the Lie subalgebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
generated by
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\{l_{a}\mid a\in\mathcal{A}\}
.

(3)

The
structure Lie group of
\mathcal{A}

is the connected Lie subgroup
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})\subset{\mathrm{Gl}}(\mathcal{A})
with Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})
.

(4)

A
derivation of
\mathcal{A}

is a linear map
d\in{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
with
d(a\bullet b)=(da)\bullet b+a\bullet(db)
.

(5)

An
automorphism of
\mathcal{A}

is a linear isomorphism
g\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
with
g(a\bullet b)=(ga)\bullet(gb)
.

It is straightforward to verify that the automorphisms and derivations form a regular Lie subgroup and a Lie subalgebra
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})\subset{\mathrm{Gl}}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{D}{\rm er}(\mathcal{A})\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
, respectively, called the
automorphism group
and
derivation algebra of
\mathcal{A}
, respectively. In fact,
\mathcal{D}{\rm er}(\mathcal{A})
is the Lie algebra of
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})
. Moreover,
g\in\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})
and
d\in\mathcal{D}{\rm er}(\mathcal{A})
iff for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
we have

gl_{a}g^{-1}=l_{ga},\qquad[d,l_{a}]=l_{da}.

(3.5)

That is, the adjoint action of
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})
and
\mathcal{D}{\rm er}(\mathcal{A})
on
{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
preserves the subspace
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
and hence the structure Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})
and structure Lie group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
.

{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})

For

, regarded as a linear

. It follows that

\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}=\mathcal{D}^{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}}_{\xi}

with
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}
from equation (

from Definition

, regarding
\mathcal{A}^{\star}

\mathcal{A}

\nabla^{\mathcal{A}}(f):=\sharp df\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{A}^{\star}).

Remark 3.2
.

If the multiplication
\bullet
is symmetric (e.g., if
\mathcal{A}
is a Jordan algebra), the dual vector field
\nabla^{\mathcal{A}}(f)
is usually referred to as the

, while in the case of an antisymmetric multiplication

(e.g., if
\mathcal{A}
is a Lie algebra), it is called the

\mathcal{A}
-dual vector field
subsumes both cases.

We wish to caution the reader that in case of a skew-symmetric multiplication
\bullet
, the notation
\nabla^{\mathcal{A}}(f)
for the Hamiltonian vector does not match the standard convention. We use it nevertheless to unify our notation.

If
g\in\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})
is an automorphism, then by equation (

) the action of
g^{\ast}
on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
preserves the distribution
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
and hence permutes integral leaves of equal dimensions, preserving
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
-regular points.

As it turns out, if the product
\bullet
is symmetric or skew-symmetric, then there is a canonical bilinear pairing on
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\bullet)
be a finite-dimensional real algebra such that
\bullet
is symmetric
(
skew-symmetric, respectively
)
.
Then on
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}=\mathcal{D}^{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}}_{\xi}\subset T_{\xi}\mathcal{A}^{\star}
there is a canonical non-degenerate symmetric
(
skew-symmetric, respectively
)
bilinear form, given by

\mathcal{G}_{\xi}(l_{a}^{\ast}(\xi),l_{b}^{\ast}(\xi)):=\langle\xi,a\bullet b\rangle.

(3.8)

Furthermore,
\mathcal{G}
is preserved by the action of the automorphism group
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})
.

Proof.

Since
\langle\xi,a\bullet b\rangle=\langle l_{a}^{\ast}(\xi),b\rangle=\pm\langle l_{b}^{\ast}(\xi),a\rangle
, where the sign
\pm
depends on the symmetry or skew-symmetry of
\bullet
, it follows that
\mathcal{G}
is indeed well defined and non-degenerate.

Finally, if
g\in\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{A})
is an automorphism, then equation (
3.5
) implies that

g^{\ast}l_{a}^{\ast}\big(g^{-1}\big)^{\ast}=l_{g^{-1}a}^{\ast}

and from here, the invariance of
\mathcal{G}
under the action of the automorphism group follows.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algebra. A
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
-orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{A}^{\star}
is called
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
-regular
if
\mathcal{O}^{\rm reg}_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}}=\mathcal{O}
.

We shall now give classes of examples of these notions.

1. Lie algebras.

Let
(\mathcal{A},\bullet)=({\mathfrak{g}},[\cdot,\cdot])
be a Lie algebra. Then the induced section
\Lambda:=\mathcal{R}^{\mathfrak{g}}\in\Gamma\big({\mathfrak{g}}^{\star},\Lambda^{2}T{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}\big)
from equation (
3.1
) is a skew-symmetric bi-vector field, and the Jacobi identity implies that the Schouten–Nijenhuis bracket
[\Lambda,\Lambda]\in\Gamma({\mathfrak{g}}^{\star},\Lambda^{3}T{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star})
vanishes
[
57
,
75
]
, so that
\Lambda
defines a linear Poisson structure
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}
, also known as the
Kirillov–Kostant–Souriau structure

[
51
]
.

Comparing our notions with those established for Poisson manifolds, we observe that for a function
f\in C^{\infty}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\star})
, the
{\mathfrak{g}}
-gradient vector field
\nabla^{\mathcal{A}}(f)
corresponds to the
Hamiltonian vector field
X_{f}
for Poisson manifolds, so that the dual distribution
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\xi}
from equation (
3.4
) is the Hamiltonian distribution of the Poisson manifold.
It is integrable, as the Hamiltonian vector fields satisfy the identity

[X_{f},X_{g}]=-X_{\{f,g\}}.

The Jacobi identity implies that
l_{a}=ad_{a}
satisfies
[l_{a},l_{b}]=l_{[a,b]}
, so that
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is closed under the commutator bracket and therefore,
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})={\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}\cong{\mathfrak{g}}/\mathfrak{z}({\mathfrak{g}})
.
That is, the action of the structure group is induced by the coadjoint action of
G
on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}
, and the skew-symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{A}}
from equation (
3.8
) coincides with the symplectic form on each coadjoint orbit in
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}
.
By the Jacobi identity, this action consists of automorphisms of the Lie algebra structure, whence this symplectic form is preserved under the coadjoint action.

Therefore, the integral leaves of
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
are the coadjoint orbits of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\star}
, equipped with their canonical symplectic form, and hence, each orbit is regular in the sense of our definition.

2. Associative algebras.
The associativity of the product
\bullet
is equivalent to saying that
l_{a}l_{b}=l_{a\bullet b}
, so that
\{l_{a}\mid a\in\mathcal{A}\}\subset{\mathfrak{gl}}(\mathcal{A})
is a subalgebra, which means that the structure algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})
equals
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
with the Lie bracket being the commutator.

Thus, if we regard
\mathcal{A}
as a Lie algebra with the Lie bracket
[a,b]:=a\bullet b-b\bullet a
, then the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
-orbits are the coadjoint orbits on
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
, regarded as the dual of a Lie algebra and thus described in the preceding paragraph.

Note that by Proposition
3.3
the bilinear form
\mathcal{G}
on these orbits only exists if
\bullet
is symmetric or antisymmetric.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a
commutative
and associative algebra, then
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
and
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})
are
abelian
Lie groups, respectively.
In this case, the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
-orbits of
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
are diffeomorphic to the direct product of a torus and Euclidean space.

In the two preceding cases,
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is closed under Lie brackets, so that it coincides with the structure algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{A})
.
This implies that, by the very definition,
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is integrable having the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{A})
-orbits in
\mathcal{A}^{\star}
as leaves.
In particular, all orbits are
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{A}}
-regular.

In contrast, for a

\mathcal{J}

is a Lie algebra, so that not all
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbits on the dual

Since the Jordan product is symmetric,

from (

) defines a pseudo-Riemannian metric on the regular part

of each orbit.

We shall describe these structures on the

-orbits on
\mathcal{J}^{\star}
and the pseudo-Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}
in more details, and we will see how, for some specific type of positive Jordan algebras, and suitable orbits,

is intimately connected with either the Fisher–Rao metric tensor or with the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor used in classical and quantum information geometry, respectively.

4.1
Jordan frames and the Peirce decomposition

Let
\mathcal{J}
be a real finite-dimensional Jordan algebra.
We define the symmetric bilinear form
\tau
on
\mathcal{J}
by

\tau(x,y):=\operatorname{tr}l_{\{x,y\}}.

(4.3)

Observe that for
x,y\in\mathcal{J}

\tau(gx,gy)=\tau(x,y),\quad g\in\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{J}),\qquad\tau(dx,y)+\tau(x,dy)=0,\quad d\in\mathcal{D}{\rm er}(\mathcal{J}).

Namely, if
g\in\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{J})
, then
\tau(gx,gy)=\operatorname{tr}l_{g\{x,y\}}=\operatorname{tr}gl_{\{x,y\}}g^{-1}=\tau(x,y)
, and the second identity follows as
\mathcal{D}{\rm er}(\mathcal{J})
is the Lie algebra of
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}(\mathcal{J})

A symmetric bilinear form
\beta

\beta(\{x,y\},z)=\beta(x,\{y,z\}),

i.e., if all

are self-adjoint w.r.t.
\beta

Proposition 4.4

(
[
52
, p. 59]
)
.

The bilinear form
\tau
from equation (
4.3
) is associative.

An element

is called an

if
c^{2}:=\{c,c\}=c
. Such an idempotent is called

with idempotents
c_{1},c_{2}\neq 0
. For an idempotent

,

is diagonalizable with eigenvalues in
\big\{0,\frac{1}{2},1\big\}

[
30

Therefore, it follows that

\tau(c,c)=\operatorname{tr}l_{\{c,c\}}=\operatorname{tr}l_{c}\geq 1,

as the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues, and

1

.

If
\mathcal{J}
has an identity element
\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{J}}

is a set

Note that

\tau(c_{i},c_{j})=\tau(\{c_{i},c_{i}\},c_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:l-adjoint})}}{{=}}\tau(c_{i},\{c_{i},c_{j}\})=0\qquad\text{for $i\neq j$},

so that

-orthogonal system. From this, one can show that the maps

commute pairwise
[
30

Let

. Then, as all

are diagonalizable, there is a

-orthogonal decomposition of

into the common eigenspace of

\mathcal{J}_{\rho}:=\{x\in\mathcal{J}\mid l_{c}(x)=\rho(c)x,c\in{\mathfrak{c}}\}

. Since

has only eigenvalues

and

where

with respect to the Jordan frame

) it follows that

\exp({\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{J}))\cdot\Sigma^{\leq m}\subset\Sigma^{\leq m},

and as the connected group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
is generated by
\exp({\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{J}))
, the asserted
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-invariance of
\Sigma^{\leq m}
follows.

Since
\Sigma^{m}=\Sigma^{\leq m}\backslash\Sigma^{\leq m-1}
is the difference of two
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-invariant sets, it follows that
\Sigma^{m}
is
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-invariant as well.

Next, we assert that
\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}\subset\Sigma^{m}
is relatively closed. For if
(x_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}\in\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}
converges to
x_{0}\in\Sigma^{m}
, then, fixing a Jordan frame
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
, we find
h_{k}\in\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
such that

y_{k}:=h_{k}\cdot x_{k}=\sum_{a}\lambda_{a,k}c_{a},\qquad\lambda_{1,k}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{m,k}.

Since
y_{k}\in\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}
as well, it follows that the signs of
0\neq\lambda_{a,k}
are equal for all
k
. As
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
is compact, we may pass to a subsequence to assume that
h_{k}\to h_{0}
, whence
y_{k}\to h_{0}x_{0}
, i.e.,

h_{0}x_{0}=\sum_{a}\lambda_{a,0}c_{a},\qquad\lambda_{a,0}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{a,k}.

Since
x_{0}
and hence
h_{0}x_{0}\in\Sigma^{m}
, it follows that
\lambda_{a,0}\neq 0
for all
a
, whence
\lambda_{a,0}
has the same sign as all
\lambda_{a,k}
, so that
h_{0}x_{0}\in\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}
, i.e.,
x_{0}\in\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}
.

Thus, equation (
4.9
) is the disjoint decomposition of
\Sigma^{m}
into finitely many relatively closed subsets, and since
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
and hence all orbits are connected, it follows that each
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit must be contained in some
\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}
.

On the other hand, as elements with equal spectral coefficients lie in the same
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit, equation (
4.14
) immediately implies that
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
acts transitively on
\Sigma_{n_{+},n_{-}}
, which completes the proof.
∎

For a positive Jordan algebra
\mathcal{J}
we identify
\mathcal{J}
and
\mathcal{J}^{\star}
by the isomorphism

\displaystyle\flat\colon\ \mathcal{J}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J}^{\star},\qquad x\longmapsto x^{\flat}:=\tau(x,\cdot),

\displaystyle\#\colon\ \mathcal{J}^{\star}\longrightarrow\mathcal{J},\qquad\#:=\flat^{-1}.

By the spectral theorem (cf. point (4) of Proposition
4.5
), for each
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}
there is a Jordan frame
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
on
\mathcal{J}
such that

\xi^{\#}=\lambda_{i}c_{i},\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad\xi=\lambda_{i}c_{i}^{\flat},

and we define the spectral coefficients
(\lambda_{i})_{i}
and the spectral signature
(n_{+},n_{-})
of
\xi
to be the spectral coefficients and signature of
\xi^{\#}
. We let

\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},n_{-}}\subset\mathcal{J}^{\star}

be the set of elements of spectral signature
(n_{+},n_{-})
. Furthermore, we define the
dual of
\tau

to be the scalar product on
\mathcal{J}^{\star}
given by

\tau^{\flat}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2}):=\tau\big(\eta_{1}^{\#},\eta_{2}^{\#}\big)\qquad\mathrm{or}\qquad\tau^{\flat}\big(x_{1}^{\flat},x_{2}^{\flat}\big):=\tau(x_{1},x_{2}).

For
x,y\in\mathcal{J}
and
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}

(l_{x}^{\ast}\xi)(y)=\xi(l_{x}y)=\tau\big(\xi^{\#},l_{x}y\big)=\tau\big(l_{x}\xi^{\#},y\big)=\big(l_{x}\xi^{\#}\big)^{\flat}(y)
, so that

l_{x}^{\ast}\xi=\big(l_{x}\xi^{\#}\big)^{\flat}.

(4.17)

Therefore, by the definition of the dual action,
it follows that

\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi=\big(\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi^{\#}\big)^{\flat},\qquad\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi=\big(\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi^{\#}\big)^{\flat},

(4.18)

so that, by Theorem
4.7
, we obtain that the orbits of the action of
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
on

.

The
open cone of squares in
\mathcal{J}

is

\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}:=\mathrm{Int}\big\{x^{2}\mid x\in\mathcal{J}\big\}.

Looking at the spectral decomposition in equation (

iff all its spectral coefficients are positive iff

is positive definite, and the latter description shows that

is indeed a convex cone; in fact, it easily follows from this characterization that

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{O}_{r,0}={\mathfrak{g}}(\mathcal{J})\cdot{\bf 1}_{\mathcal{J}},\qquad\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}=\dot{\bigcup}_{n_{+}\geq 0}\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},0}.

(4.19)

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
be a positive, simple Jordan algebra with structure group
G(\mathcal{J})\subset{\mathrm{Gl}}(\mathcal{J})
.
Then
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}
is
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular iff the spectral coefficients
(\lambda_{i})
of
\xi
satisfy:

\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{b}\neq 0\qquad\text{whenever $\lambda_{a},\lambda_{b}\neq 0$}.

(4.20)

In particular, the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-orbit
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},n_{-}}
is
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular iff
n_{+}=0
or
n_{-}=0
, i.e., iff it is contained in
\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
or
-\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

Proof.

Let
x:=\xi^{\#}\in\mathcal{J}
.
By equation (
4.18
),
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi\subset\mathcal{J}\cdot\xi
iff
\mathcal{A}{\rm ut}_{0}(\mathcal{J})\cdot x\subset\mathcal{J}\cdot x=l_{x}(\mathcal{J})
, and, recalling point (8) in Proposition
4.5
, by equation (
4.8
) this condition is satisfied iff equation (
4.20
) holds.
Recalling Proposition
4.3
, the first statement follows.

If
n_{+},n_{-}>0
, then evidently,
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},n_{-}}
contains elements two of whose spectral coefficients satisfy
\lambda_{a}=-\lambda_{b}\neq 0
, so that
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},n_{-}}
is not
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular.

On the other hand, on
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},0}
(
\mathcal{O}_{0,n_{-}}
, respectively)
\lambda_{a},\lambda_{b}>0
(
<0
, respectively) so that equation (
4.20
) holds; whence
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},0}
and
\mathcal{O}_{0,n_{-}}
are the only
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular orbits, and by equation (

) these are the orbits contained in
\bar{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
or
-\bar{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
, respectively.
∎

Let us now describe the pseudo-Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{O}^{\rm reg}_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}}
.
Take
\xi\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}
with spectral decomposition

\xi=\lambda_{a}c_{a}^{\flat}\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x:=\xi^{\#}=\lambda_{a}c_{a}\in\mathcal{J}

(4.21)

for some Jordan frame
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
, and assume it satisfies equation (
4.20
).
Then, it holds

T_{\xi}\mathcal{O}=\mathfrak{g}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi=(\mathfrak{g}(\mathcal{J})\cdot x)^{\flat}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:orbit-xlambda})}}{{=}}\bigoplus_{a,i}\mathcal{J}_{ai}^{\flat},

and we have the following Proposition.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\xi=\lambda_{a}c_{a}^{\flat}\in\mathcal{J}^{\star}
be as above.
Then, it holds

\mathcal{G}_{\xi}=\sum_{a,i}\frac{2}{\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{i}}\tau^{\flat}|_{\mathcal{J}_{ai}^{\flat}},

(4.22)

which is equivalent to

\mathcal{G}_{\xi}\big(x_{ai}^{\flat},y_{bj}^{\flat}\big)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2}{\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{i}}\tau^{\flat}\big(x_{ai}^{\flat},y_{ai}^{\flat}\big)&\text{if $(a,i)=(b,j)$,}\\[2.84526pt]
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Proof.

For
x_{ij}\in\mathcal{J}_{ij}
, we have

l_{x_{ij}}^{\ast}(\xi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:l*dual})}}{{=}}\big(l_{x_{ij}}\xi^{\#}\big)^{\flat}=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j})x_{ij}^{\flat}.

Therefore, evaluating both sides of equation (
3.8
) gives us

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\xi}(l_{x_{ai}}^{\ast}(\xi),l_{y_{bj}}^{\ast}(\xi))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{i})(\lambda_{b}+\lambda_{j})\mathcal{G}_{\xi}\big(x_{ai}^{\flat},y_{bj}^{\flat}\big)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\xi}(l_{x_{ai}}^{\ast}(\xi),l_{y_{bj}}^{\ast}(\xi))

\displaystyle=\xi(\{x_{ai},y_{bj}\})=\tau\big(\xi^{\#},\{x_{ai},y_{bj}\}\big)

\displaystyle=\tau(\{\xi^{\#},x_{ai}\},y_{bj})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq:define-xi})}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{i})\tau(x_{ai},y_{bj}).

Since both equations must be equal, (
4.22
) follows as the Peirce decomposition
{\mathcal{J}=\bigoplus_{ij}\mathcal{J}_{ij}}
is
\tau
-orthogonal.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

(1)

Comparing the description of the regular points in
\mathcal{O}
in Theorem
4.8
and equation (
4.22
), it follows that
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
has a pole of order
1
on
\mathcal{O}\backslash\mathcal{O}^{\rm reg}_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}}
.

(2)

As
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
is positive or negative definite on
\mathcal{J}_{aa}^{\flat}
, depending on the sign of
\lambda_{a}\neq 0
, it follows that
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
is indefinite at any regular point of spectral signature
(n_{+},n_{-})
with
n_{+},n_{-}>0
.

That is,
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{O}
is definite (and hence defines a Riemannian metric) iff
\mathcal{O}=\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})\cdot\xi
is a regular orbit, iff
\mathcal{O}\subset\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
is contained in the closure of the cone of squares (
\mathcal{G}>0
) or
\mathcal{O}\subset-\overline{\Omega}_{\mathcal{J}}
(
\mathcal{G}<0
).

(3)

It is also evident from the description of regular points in Theorem
4.8
that for a non-regular orbit
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},n_{-}}
with
n_{+},n_{-}>0
the regular part
\left(\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},n_{-}}\right)^{\rm reg}_{{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}}
is not path connected.

(4)

Note that the Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},0}
(and, similarly,
-\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{O}_{0,n_{-}}

Namely, for

\alpha(t):=t^{2}(c_{1}+\dots+c_{n_{+}})^{\flat}\in\mathcal{O}_{n_{+},0}

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha(t)}(\dot{\alpha},\dot{\alpha})=\sum_{a=1}^{n_{+}}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\tau(2tc_{a},2tc_{a})=4\sum_{a=1}^{n_{+}}\tau(c_{a},c_{a}).

4.3
Examples

We shall now describe the metric
\mathcal{G}
for the standard examples of positive Jordan algebras.

4.4
The Fisher–Rao metric for finite sample spaces

We regard
\mathcal{J}:={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
as a positive Jordan algebra whose algebraic operations are defined in a component-wise way.
Then, it is not difficult to see that
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
can be identified with the first orthant
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\cong\mathcal{J}^{\star}
.
The metric
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
at
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\in\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
is given by

\mathcal{G}_{\xi}(u,v)=\sum_{i}\dfrac{1}{\xi_{i}}u_{i}v_{i},\qquad u=(u_{i})_{i=1}^{n},v=(v_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

When interpreting
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
as the set of positive finite measures on
\mathcal{X}_{n}=\{1,\dots,n\}
, it is clear that
\mathcal{G}
is such its pullback to the submanifold of strictly positive probability distributions on
\mathcal{X}_{n}
(i.e., open interior of the unit simplex inside
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}
) coincides with the
Fisher–Rao metric tensor
which naturally occurs in classical information geometry
[
2
,
6
]
.
As partially noted in
[
22
,
23
]
, this instance shows that we may look at the non-normalized Fisher–Rao metric tensor on
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}
as the analogue of the homogeneous symplectic form on co-adjoint orbits in the case of Lie algebras.

4.5
The Jordan algebras
\boldsymbol{M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})}
,
\boldsymbol{{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{H}}}

Let
{\mathbb{K}}
denote either the real, complex or quaternionic numbers, and we define the Jordan algebra of self-adjoint matrices

M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}}):=\{A\in{\mathbb{K}}^{n\times n}\mid A=A^{\ast}\},\qquad\{A,B\}:=\dfrac{1}{2}(AB+BA).

For convenience, we replace
\tau
from equation (
4.3
) by the associative inner product

\hat{\tau}(A,B):=\operatorname{Tr}(AB),

so that
\tau
and
\hat{\tau}
only differ by the multiplicative constant
\frac{1}{n}\dim_{\mathbb{R}}M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})
.

Let
E_{ij}\in{\mathbb{K}}^{n\times n}
denote the matrix with a
1
in the
(i,j)
-entry.
Then
\{E_{11},\dots,E_{nn}\}
is a Jordan frame of
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})
, and the remaining Pierce spaces with respect to this frame are given as

(M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}}))_{ij}=\{zE_{ij}+\bar{z}E_{ji}\mid z\in{\mathbb{K}}\},\qquad i<j.

For
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}
and
{\mathbb{H}}
, the automorphism group of
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})
is
{\rm SO}(n)
,
{\rm U}(n)
and
{\rm Sp}(n)
, respectively, acting on
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})
by conjugation. Thus, in particular, each
A\in M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})
is diagonalizable by an element in the automorphism group, so that the spectral coefficients are the eigenvalues of
A
. Thus, by Proposition
4.9
, for
\xi=\lambda_{a}E_{aa}^{\flat}\in(M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}}))^{\star}
the metric
\mathcal{G}_{\xi}
reads

\mathcal{G}_{\xi}\big(zE_{ai}^{\flat},wE_{bj}^{\flat}\big)=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2}{\lambda_{a}+\lambda_{i}}(z\bar{w}+w\bar{z})&\text{if $(a,i)=(b,j)$,}\\[2.84526pt]
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Moreover, because of Proposition
3.3
, it follows that
\mathcal{G}
is preserved by the automorphism group of
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{K}})
.

As already mentioned in the introduction, and in accordance with the results put forward in
[
22
,
23
]
, an interesting link between Jordan algebras and quantum information geometry appears when
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
.
In this case, we may identify
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{C}})
with the Jordan algebra of self-adjoint observables of a finite-level quantum system with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\cong{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
Then, if we focus on the
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular orbit
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
of faithful, non-normalized quantum states, which can be identified with the dual of the orbit of invertible positive matrices in
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{C}})
, the metric tensor
\mathcal{G}
is such that its pullback to the submanifold of faithful quantum states, determined by the condition
\operatorname{Tr}(A)=1
, coincides with the so-called Bures–Helstrom metric tensor
[
9
,
25
,
26
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
,
68
,
69
,
70
,
77
]
.
Analogously, if we focus on the
{\mathfrak{m}}_{\mathcal{J}}
-regular orbit through non-normalized pure states, which are identified with rank-one matrices in
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{C}})
, the metric tensor
\mathcal{G}
is such that its pullback to the submanifold of pure states, determined by the condition
\operatorname{Tr}(A)=1
, is a multiple of the Fubini–Study metric tensor, essentially because of its unitary invariance.
Accordingly, and in analogy with the Fisher–Rao metric tensor seen before, we may think of the non-normalized version of the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor and of the Fubini–Study metric tensor as the analogue of the Kostant–Kirillov–Souriau symplectic form in the case of the Jordan algebra
M_{n}^{\rm sa}({\mathbb{C}})
.

4.6
The spin-factor Jordan algebra
\boldsymbol{{\mathcal{J}\mathrm{Spin}}(n)}

Denoting the standard inner product of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, we let

{\mathcal{J}\mathrm{Spin}}(n):={\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}={\mathbb{R}}{\bf 1}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\qquad\{x,y\}:=\langle x,y\rangle{\bf 1},\qquad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

and where
{\bf 1}
is the identity element of
{\mathcal{J}\mathrm{Spin}}(n)
. An associative inner product is given by

\hat{\tau}|_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\qquad\hat{\tau}({\bf 1},{\bf 1}):=1,\qquad\hat{\tau}({\bf 1},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})=0.

The automorphism group is
{\rm SO}(n)
, acting on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and fixing
{\bf 1}
. Every Jordan frame is given by

\left\{\dfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}+e_{0}),\dfrac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-e_{0})\right\}

for a fixed unit vector
e_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and the Peirce space complementary to the Jordan frame is

{\mathcal{J}\mathrm{Spin}}(n)_{12}:=e_{0}^{\perp}.

The two spectral coefficients of an element
X=t{\bf 1}+x
are

\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(t+\|x\|),\qquad\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(t-\|x\|),

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the norm on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Therefore,
\xi\in{\mathcal{J}\mathrm{Spin}}(n)^{\star}
is regular iff
0\neq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=\hat{\tau}^{\flat}\big(\xi,{\bf 1}^{\flat}\big)
. If

\xi=t_{0}{\bf 1}^{\flat}+s_{0}e_{0}^{\flat}\in{\mathcal{J}\mathrm{Spin}}(n)^{\star},\qquad t_{0}\neq 0

is regular, where
e_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is a unit vector, then the tangent vectors
X_{1},X_{2}\in T_{\xi}\mathcal{O}
are of the form

X_{i}=t_{i}{\bf 1}^{\flat}+s_{i}e_{0}^{\flat}+x_{i}^{\flat},

where
x_{i}\in e_{0}^{\perp}
, and where
t_{0}=\pm s_{0}\Rightarrow t_{i}=\pm s_{i}
. The spectral coefficients of
\xi
are
\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(t_{0}+s_{0})
and
\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(t_{0}-s_{0})
, and

X_{i}=(t_{i}+s_{i})\frac{1}{2}({\bf 1}+e_{0})^{\flat}+(t_{i}-s_{i})\frac{1}{2}({\bf 1}-e_{0})^{\flat}+x_{i}^{\flat}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\xi}(X_{1},X_{2})=\dfrac{2}{t_{0}+s_{0}}(t_{1}+s_{1})(t_{2}+s_{2})+\dfrac{2}{t_{0}-s_{0}}(t_{1}-s_{1})(t_{2}-s_{2})+\dfrac{2}{t_{0}}\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle.

This metric is positive definite if
t_{0}\geq|s_{0}|
and negative definite if
t_{0}\leq-|s_{0}|
, as predicted by Proposition
4.9
.

4.7
Non-simple, semisimple positive Jordan algebras

By point (1) of Proposition
4.5

\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{J}_{k}
into positive

\mathcal{J}

Then, applying Theorems

4.8

\mathcal{O}^{1}_{n^{1}_{+},n^{1}_{-}}\times\dots\times\mathcal{O}^{k}_{n^{k}_{+},n^{k}_{-}}\subset\mathcal{J}^{\star}=\mathcal{J}_{1}^{\star}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{J}^{\star}_{k},

5
Discussion

As mentioned in Section
1
, the mathematics of Jordan algebras has been intensively studied, and we want to discuss here where our investigation place itself in this context.

First of all, it is clear that our construction is highly influenced and inspired by Kirillov’s theory, and the relation between Jordan algebras and Kirillov’s theory has been already investigated in the literature
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
.
However, the approaches and constructions already available are different from ours, both conceptually and in terms of mathematical structures.
For instance, in the works mentioned above, the emphasis is on the study of nilpotent coadjoint orbits of convex types, while we do not study the coadjoint orbits of the structure group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
, rather we try to develop an analogue of coadjoint orbits for a Jordan algebra.
In order to do so, it turns out we need to rely on the structure group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
and on (some of) its homogeneous spaces.
A clear departure point from ordinary Kirillov theory is then the fact that the orbits we find do not possess a natural symplectic structure, but rather a pseudo-Riemannian one.
The shift from an antisymmetric tensor to a symmetric one is clearly related to the fact that we develop Kirillov’s theory for Jordan algebras, which have a symmetric product.
Moreover, as commented in Sections
1
and
4.3
, a direct output of our investigation is the fact that some relevant Riemannian structures that naturally arise in the context of Classical and quantum information geometry (namely, the Fisher–Rao metric tensor and the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor) can be thought of as the Jordan-algebraic analogue of the natural homogeneous symplectic form on the co-adjoint orbits of a Lie group.

Other important avenues of research around the mathematics of Jordan algebras concern the relation between symmetric spaces/cones/domains and Jordan algebras, as well as the differential geometric structures related with the algebraic structure of Jordan algebras
[
13
,
14
,
18
,
30
,
52
,
78
]
.

In all these investigations, the geometrical objects are subsets of a given Jordan algebra
\mathcal{J}
, while we consider positive linear functionals on
\mathcal{J}
.
This change of perspective is dictated by the role Jordan algebras play in information geometry, where the relevant objects live in the dual space of
\mathcal{J}
(e.g., classical probability distributions, quantum states).
It is true that it is possible to identify
\mathcal{J}
with its dual in finite dimensions, and we exploit this technicality in our proofs, but the focus of our approach is conceptually different from the previous ones, and this difference is particularly relevant with respect to possible generalizations to infinite dimensions (a task that we plan to address in the future).

Again in relation with the above-mentioned references, the symmetric cones in real Hilbert spaces that turn out to be in one-to-one correspondence with formally real (Euclidean) Jordan algebras, in the sense that every such symmetric cone can be realized as the open cone
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
of invertible positive elements of a suitable Jordan algebra
\mathcal{J}
, are given from the onset.
From our point of view, the open cone
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
is not an
a priori
datum of the problem, but it appears because it is diffeomorphic to an integral manifold of the Jordan distribution
\mathcal{H}^{\mathcal{J}}
.
Therefore,
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
is a byproduct of our investigation of the integrability properties of the Jordan distribution canonically associated with the Jordan algebra product.
Moreover, the symmetric cones are also naturally endowed with a Riemannian structure
g
, which is invariant under the action of the symmetry group
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
of the cone itself, while the Riemannian structure we obtain is invariant only under the subgroup of
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
composed by automorphisms of
\mathcal{J}
.
This instance follows from the fact that we obtain the Riemannian structure by implementing a symmetric analogue of Kirillov theory, and not by symmetry considerations.
In particular, in the case of the real associative Jordan algebra
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-invariant Riemannian metric tensor
g
on
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
can be written as
[
18
, Theorem 2.3.19]

g=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{d}p^{j}\otimes\mathrm{d}p^{j}}{(p^{j})^{2}},

where
\{p^{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,n}
is a Cartesian coordinate system adapted to
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
in the sense that elements in
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}}
have strictly positive values of the coordinates.
This Riemannian metric tensor is different from the Fisher–Rao metric tensor appearing in classical information geometry
[
6
]
.
However, as shown in Section
4.3
(in accordance with
[
23
]
), the Riemannian structure emerging from our construction is precisely the Fisher–Rao metric tensor.
Moreover, the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
invariance of
g
implies it is invariant with respect to dilations also when
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{B}_{\rm sa}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\cong M_{n}^{\rm sa}(\mathbb{C})
, so that
g
can not be the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor appearing in quantum information geometry
[
25
]
, while, again referring to Section
4.3
, the Riemannian metric tensor we obtain reduces to the Bures–Helstrom metric tensor when
\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{B}_{\rm sa}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\cong M_{n}^{\rm sa}(\mathbb{C})
.

Another relevant point to stress is that our construction relies entirely on the algebraic Jordan product.
This detail contributes to further differentiate our work from previous ones, where part of the geometrical structures associated with Jordan algebras necessarily involve the so-called
Jordan triple product
, a trilinear map that exists in every Jordan algebra (and also in more general objects known as
Jordan triple systems
).
For instance, the
\mathrm{G}(\mathcal{J})
-invariant Riemannian metric tensor discussed above is defined in terms of the Jordan triple product of
\mathcal{J}

[
18
, Theorem 2.3.19]
, while our Riemannian metric tensor only requires the Jordan product.
Also, the Jordan triple product induces a natural Lie triple system that leads to a curvature-like tensor
[
13
]
, and again, this differential geometric object makes use of the Jordan product only indirectly, highlighting the difference with our approach.
At this point, it is worth noting that we plan to address the role of the Jordan triple product and its associated Lie triple system in information geometry because we believe they are connected with the so-called Amari–Cencov tensor in the classical case, and with a non-symmetric generalization of that tensor in the quantum case.

Therefore, while and perhaps because our approach is partly extrinsically motivated by classical and quantum information geometry, and partly intrinsically by developing an orbit method-like theory, our constructions and results are different from previous ones and, taken together, they yield a fuller picture of the structure of Jordan algebras.
