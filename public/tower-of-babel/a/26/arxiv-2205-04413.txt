Equations of tensor eigenschemes
Valentina Beorchia, Francesco Galuppi and Lorenzo Venturello

Abstract
. We study schemes of tensor eigenvectors from an algebraic and geometric viewpoint. We characterize determinantal defining equations of such eigenschemes via linear equations in their coefficients, both in the general and in the symmetric case. We give a geometric necessary condition for a 0-dimensional scheme to be an eigenscheme.

Primary 15A18, 14M12; Secondary 13P25, 15A69

1.
Introduction

Tensor spectral theory is an active area of research. Its applications range from hypergraphs theory to quantum physics, from dynamical systems to polynomial optimization, from signal processing to medical imaging. A reference for these and many more applications is
[
13
]
. In this article we study
eigenvectors of tensors
. There are several notions of tensor eigenvectors as introduced independently in
[
12
]
and
[
15
]
, all extending the case of matrices. In the present paper we shall focus on the definition
[
16
, Section 2.2.1]
. In this context it is not
restrictive to consider partially symmetric tensors of order
d
, that is on elements of
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
.

If we choose a basis for
\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
, then we can identify a partially symmetric tensor
T
with a tuple
(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})
of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d-1
. With such a choice, an
eigenvector
of
T
is a nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
such that

(1)

(g_{0}(v),\ldots,g_{n}(v))=\lambda v

for some constant
\lambda
. We observe that such vectors are called
E-eigenvectors
in
[
16
, Section 2.2.1]
. Since
property (
1
) is preserved under scalar multiplication, it is natural to regard eigenvectors as points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
; hence the name
eigenpoints
instead of eigenvectors.

Motivated by
[
14
, Question 16]
, we are interested in the geometry of eigenpoints of tensors in the framework of complex projective geometry. One of the reasons is that being an eigenpoint of a tensor is an algebraic condition, described by the vanishing of minors of a suitable matrix - see
Definition

2.1
. Hence, eigenpoints of a tensor form a scheme.

There are several challenging questions about eigenschemes, their dimension, their degree, their configurations and their defining equations.
In this paper we extend some of the results obtained in
[
3
]
in the case
n=2
to arbitrary dimension. For the convenience of the reader, we summarize here our main contributions:

•

In
2.6
we explicitly compute the minimal graded free resolution of the defining ideal of a general eigenscheme.

•

In
3.1
and
3.4
we characterize determinantal equations of eigenschemes of tensors and symmetric tensors, thereby giving a positive solution to
[
3
, Conjecture 3.13]
. These theorems allow us to design an algorithm which checks if a tuple of homogeneous polynomials is the tuple of determinantal equations of an eigenscheme. The second algorithm checks whether, given a set of points
Z\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
, there exists a tensor
T
, possibly symmetric, whose eigenscheme contains
Z
. If the answer is positive, it explicitly reconstructs
T
.

•

We prove that the variety parametrizing eigenschemes of symmetric tensors is rational and we give a birational parametrization. This is the content of
4.3
.

•

Finally, in
5.4
we give a geometric necessary condition for a
0
-dimensional scheme to be the eigenscheme of a tensor. This gives a partial answer to
[
14
, Question 16]
.

2.
Definitions and first results

In this section we define eigenschemes and discuss their algebraic invariants.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
T=(g_{0},\ldots,g_{n})
be a partially symmetric tensor. The
eigenscheme
of
T
is the closed subscheme
E(T)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by the
2\times 2
minors of

\left(\begin{matrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&\dots&x_{n}\\
g_{0}&g_{1}&\dots&g_{n}\end{matrix}\right).

These minors are called
determinantal generators
of the ideal
I_{E(T)}
. When
T
is symmetric, that is
T
is identified with a homogeneous polynomial
f
, we have
g_{i}=\partial_{i}f
and we denote its eigenscheme by
E(f)
.

Remark 2.2
.

The property of being an eigenvector
is not invariant under linear transformations. For this reason, when we speak of “the eigenpoints of a tensor
T
” we are abusing terminology. In order to avoid ambiguities, we choose once and for all a basis consisting of real vectors
that are orthonormal with respect to the Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. With this choice we identify the space
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
of symmetric tensors with the space
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d}
of degree
d
homogeneous polynomials. In the same way we identify
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
with
(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\oplus(n+1)}
. In order to remember this important choice, we always think of partially symmetric tensors as tuples of polynomials. Once the basis is chosen, we can safely compute eigenvectors as in (
1
).

It is possible to define the eigenscheme of a tensor which is not partially symmetric, as in
[
4
, Definition 1.1]
or in
[
2
, Section 1]
. However, with the choice of a basis described in Remark
2.2
, it is apparent from the definition that for every tensor
T
there exists a partially symmetric tensor
T^{\prime}
such that
E(T^{\prime})=E(T)
. For this reason, it is not restrictive to consider partially symmetric tensors.

Definition 2.3
.

We define the integer
w(n,d)
as

w(n,d)=\frac{(d-1)^{n+1}-1}{d-2},

for every
n\geq 1
and
d\geq 3
. When
d=2
, we set
w(n,2)=n+1
.

The number
w(n,d)
is the number of eigenpoints of a general tensor in
(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\oplus(n+1)}
, as proven in
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 1
,
d\geq 2
and let
T\in(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\oplus(n+1)}
be a general tensor. Then
E(T)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a
0
-dimensional scheme of degree
w(n,d)
.

Since eigenschemes have an algebraic definition, it is natural to study algebraic properties of their ideals. For instance, we are interested in their minimal free resolution. In general this can be quite complicated, because eigenschemes can have positive dimension and they can even have components of different dimensions. However, the general tensor has a
0
-dimensional eigenscheme, and the same is true for the general symmetric tensor - see
[
1
, Section 5.2]
. For this reason we focus on the minimal graded free resolution of the ideal of a
0
-dimensional eigenscheme. We accomplish that in Theorem
2.6
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
, let
g_{0},\dots,g_{n}\in R_{d-1}
and consider the matrix

(2)

M=\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&\dots&x_{n}\\
g_{0}&g_{1}&\dots&g_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

We regard
M
as a map
R^{n+1}\to R(-1)\oplus R(-d+1)
of free graded
R
-modules. In order to study the ideal of minors of
M
, we recall the definition of the Eagon-Northcott complex.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\alpha:F\to G
a map of finitely generated
R
-modules, with

f=\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits(F)\mbox{ and }g=\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits(G).

The
Eagon-Northcott complex

EN(\alpha)_{\bullet}
is the complex defined by

\begin{cases}EN(\alpha)_{1}=\bigwedge^{g}G&\\
EN(\alpha)_{i}=(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{i-1}(G))^{*}\otimes\bigwedge^{i+g-1}F&\mbox{ for every }2\leq i\leq f-g+1,\end{cases}

with differentials as given in
[
7
, Section A2H]
.

In our setting
F=R^{n+1}
,
G=R(1)\oplus R(d-1)
and
\alpha
is the map defined by left multiplication with
M
. The shifts are such that the map
\alpha:F\to G
with
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto M(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})^{T}
is homogeneous and they induce a shift in the modules
EN(\alpha)_{i}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
n
and
d
be positive integers such that
d\geq 2
and let
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
. Let
I
be the ideal generated by the
2\times 2
minors of a matrix
M
as in (
2
) and let
V(I)
be the zero locus of
I
.
If
\dim V(I)=0
, then
I
is saturated and the minimal graded free resolution

F_{n}\to\ldots\to F_{1}\to I\to 0

of
I
as an
R
-module satisfies

F_{i}\cong\bigoplus_{j=1}^{i}R(-j(d-2)-i-1)^{\binom{n+1}{i+1}}.

Proof.

By hypothesis we have

\mathop{\rm codim}\nolimits V(I)=n=n+1-2+1
, so
V(I)
is a determinantal subscheme of the expected codimension given by the Porteous’ Formula - see
[
10
, Corollary 11]
.
By the Hochster-Eagon Theorem - see
[
9
]
or
[
6
, Theorem 18.18]
-
R/I
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring, so
I
is saturated. By
[
7
, A2.10.C]
, the Eagon-Nothcott complex associated with
\alpha
is exact and it gives a minimal free resolution of
I
.

Specifically, in our setting we have

\displaystyle(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{i-1}(R

\displaystyle(1)\oplus R(d-1)))^{*}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{i-1-j}(R(1)))^{*}\otimes(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{j}(R(d-1)))^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{i-1-j}(R)(i-1-j))^{*}(-1)\otimes(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{j}(R)(j(d-1)))^{*}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{i-1-j}(R))^{*}(-i+1+j)\otimes(\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits_{j}(R))^{*}(-j(d-1))

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}R(-j(d-2)-i+1).

The image of the map

\bigwedge^{2}F\cong R^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\to\bigwedge^{2}G\cong R(d)

is the ideal
I
of
2\times 2
minors of a matrix representing
\alpha
as in
2.5
. In our application, this is the defining ideal of the eigenscheme of a partially symmetric tensor in
\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
. As we consider this ideal in a standard graded polynomial ring
R
, we shift all the modules in
EN(\alpha)
by a factor of
-d
, i.e., we consider the complex
EN(\alpha)_{\bullet}\otimes R(-d)
. Its
i
-th piece is equal to

\displaystyle EN(\alpha)_{i}\otimes R(-d)

\displaystyle\cong\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}R(-j(d-2)-i+1-d)^{\binom{n+1}{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{i-1}R(-(j+1)(d-2)-i-1)^{\binom{n+1}{i+1}}.\qed

Remark 2.7
.

Notice that the defining ideal of an eigenscheme of a tensor, even of a symmetric tensor, may fail to be saturated, as shown in
[
3
, Remark 3.2]
. In this case the eigenscheme has dimension at least 1.

Remark 2.8
.

The statement of Theorem
2.6
holds for any
0
-dimensional determinantal scheme given by the
2\times 2
minors of a
2\times(n+1)
matrix whose first row consists of linear entries and the second row of degree
d-1
homogeneous forms.

As a direct consequence we can see in the following Corollary that any matrix whose maximal minors define a
0
-dimensional eigenscheme involves
n+1
linearly independent linear entries in the first row. Indeed,
by
[
11
, Lemma 2.9]
, any such a matrix is of size
2\times(n+1)
, with homogeneous rows of degrees
1
and
d-1
. The next result is a generalization of the analogous result for
n=2
given in
[
2
, Proposition 5.2]
.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
d\geq 2
. Let
l_{0},\dots,l_{n}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{1}
and
h_{0},\dots,h_{n}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-1}
. Let

N=\begin{pmatrix}l_{0}&l_{1}&\dots&l_{n}\\
h_{0}&h_{1}&\dots&h_{n}\end{pmatrix}

and let
W\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be the scheme defined by the
2\times 2
minors of
N
. If
\dim W=0
, then
W
is the eigenscheme of a tensor
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
if and only if the linear forms
l_{0},l_{1},\dots,l_{n}
are linearly independent.

Proof.

If
l_{0},l_{1},\dots,l_{n}
are linearly independent, then there exists
B\in\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits(n+1,\mathbb{C})
such that

N^{\prime}=N\cdot B=\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&x_{1}&\dots&x_{n}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}&\dots&b_{n}\end{pmatrix},

where each
b_{j}\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-1}
is a linear combination of the polynomials
h_{k}
. The zero set of the
2\times 2
minors of
N^{\prime}
is
W
, and by definition it is the eigenscheme of the partially symmetric tensor
(b_{0},b_{1},\dots,b_{n})
.
Conversely, assume that there exists a partially symmetric tensor
T=(g_{0},g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
such that
E(T)=W
. As the
2\times 2
minors of
N
define a
0
-dimensional eigenscheme, the Eagon-Northcott
complex associated with
N
is exact and. By Remark
2.8
, its first terms are given by

R(-d-1)^{\binom{n+1}{3}}\oplus R(-2d+1)^{\binom{n+1}{3}}\xrightarrow{d_{2}^{N}}R(-d)^{\binom{n+1}{2}}\to R

By the definition of the first syzygy map
d_{2}^{N}:F_{2}^{N}\to F_{1}^{N}
,
it is possible to see that
d_{2}^{N}
is defined by a matrix which is linear in the entries of
N
. More precisely, by setting

m_{ij}=l_{i}h_{j}-l_{j}h_{i},

the generating syzygies are given by

l_{i}m_{jk}-l_{j}m_{ik}+l_{k}m_{ij}=0\mbox{ and }h_{i}m_{jk}-h_{j}m_{ik}+h_{k}m_{ij}=0

for every
0\leq i<j<k\leq n
. In particular, the first
\binom{n+1}{3}
columns of
d_{2}^{N}
contain only the forms
l_{i}
and
0
. Since
W
is an eigenscheme by assumption, its ideal is generated also by the
2\times 2
minors of a matrix
M
as in (
2
), so by Theorem
2.6
the Eagon-Northcott complex associated with
M
gives also a minimal free resolution of
I_{W}
.
By
[
6
, Theorem 20.2]
, the minimal free resolution of a projective module is unique up to isomorphism of complexes. This means that there is a matrix
H\in M(\binom{n+1}{3},R_{d-2})
with degree
d-2
forms as entries, and there are three invertible matrices
A\in\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits(\binom{n+1}{2},\mathbb{C})
and
B,C\in\mathop{\rm GL}\nolimits(\binom{n+1}{3},\mathbb{C})
such that if we call

G=\begin{pmatrix}B&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&H\\
\hline\cr 0&\hskip-5.0pt\vline\hskip-5.0pt&C\end{pmatrix},

then we have
d_{2}^{M}=A\cdot d_{2}^{N}\cdot G
. In particular, the linear entries
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
of
d_{2}^{M}
are linear combinations of

l_{0},\dots,l_{n}
. So these forms span the whole of
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{1}
, thus they are linearly independent.
∎

3.
Characterization of determinantal equations

In this section we give a characterization of tuples of homogeneous polynomials which are determinantal generators of the homogeneous ideal of an eigenscheme, as introduced in Definition
2.1
.

When
n=2
, such a characterization has been found in
[
3
, Section 3]
for both general and symmetric tensors. The main ingredients in our proof are two prominent chain complexes in commutative algebra, namely the Koszul complex and the de Rham complex. We begin with the general case.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
. A tuple
(f_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n)\in R_{d}^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
of homogeneous polynomials is the tuple of determinantal equations of the eigenscheme of a tensor if and only if

(3)

x_{i}f_{jk}-x_{j}f_{ik}+x_{k}f_{ij}=0

for every
0\leq i<j<k\leq n
.

Proof.

By
[
6
, Section 17.2]
, the Koszul complex of
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
starts with

0\to R\xrightarrow{\alpha}R^{\oplus n+1}\xrightarrow{\beta}\bigwedge^{2}R^{\oplus n+1}\xrightarrow{\gamma}\bigwedge^{3}R^{\oplus n+1},

where

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle(h)=x_{0}h+\ldots+x_{n}h,

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle(g_{0},\ldots,g_{n})=(x_{i}g_{j}-x_{j}g_{i}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle((f_{ij})_{i<j})=(x_{i}f_{jk}-x_{j}f_{ik}+x_{k}f_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j<k\leq n).

The tuple
(f_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n)
is the tuple of determinantal equations of the eigenscheme of a tensor if and only if
(f_{ij})_{i<j}\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\beta)
. Since
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
is regular sequence, the sequence is exact. In particular,
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\beta)=\ker(\gamma)
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

We observe that exactness of the Koszul complex in the second term implies that
two partially symmetric tensors
T=(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})
and
T^{\prime}=(g_{0}^{\prime},\dots,g_{n}^{\prime})

determine the same determinantal equations for their eigenschemes if and only if there exist
c\in\mathbb{C}^{*}
and
h\in R_{d-2}
such that

g^{\prime}_{k}=cg_{k}+x_{k}h

for every
k\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Indeed,
since
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\alpha)=\ker(\beta)
,
we have
T^{\prime}-T=(x_{0}h,\dots,x_{n}h)
, for some
h\in R_{d-2}
.

As a consequence of the previous remark, we observe that the eigenschemes are an example of general determinantal schemes, which admit a positive dimensional family of defining matrices and they are not obtained by left and right multiplication by a scalar invertible matrix. This is not the case for general determinantal schemes of dimension
\geq 1
, which are defined by the maximal minors of a matrix, as shown in
[
8
, Main Theorem]
.

Now we are interested in a more refined version of Proposition
3.1
. Namely, we would like a similar characterization for determinantal equations of symmetric tensors. In particular, we will prove
[
3
, Conjecture 3.13]
. Our argument relies on the exactness of the affine de Rham complex, which we briefly recall.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
. The
de Rham complex
of
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
is the complex of
R
-modules defined as

0\to\Omega^{0}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}\to\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}\to\cdots\to\Omega^{n+1}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}\to 0,

where
\Omega^{k}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}
is the module of algebraic differential
k
-forms over
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
. By letting
dx_{0},\dots,dx_{n}
be an
R
-basis for
\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}
we can write a
k
-form
\omega=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n+1]}{k}}f_{I}dx_{I}\in\Omega^{k}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}
, with
f_{I}\in R
and with
dx_{I}=dx_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge dx_{i_{k}}
for
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
. The differentials of the de Rham complex are given by exterior derivatives, i.e.

d\omega=\sum_{I\in\binom{[n+1]}{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\partial_{j}f_{I}dx_{j}\wedge dx_{I}.

We are interested in the differential

\displaystyle d:\Omega^{1}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\to\Omega^{2}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}f_{i}dx_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\partial_{j}f_{i}dx_{j}\wedge dx_{i}=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n}(\partial_{i}f_{j}-\partial_{j}f_{i})dx_{i}\wedge dx_{j}.

As the de Rham complex of
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
is exact everywhere but in degree 0 (this follows, from instance, from de Rham’s theorem relating the cohomology of de Rham complex with the singular cohomology of
\mathbb{A}^{n+1}
), we conclude that whenever a tuple of
n+1
polynomials
(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
satisfy the relations
\partial_{i}f_{j}-\partial_{j}f_{i}
for every
0\leq i<j\leq n
, then there exists an element of
\Omega^{0}_{\mathbb{A}^{n+1}}\cong R
such that
\nabla g=(f_{0},\dots,f_{n})
. If moreover the polynomials
f_{0},\dots,f_{n}
are homogeneous of degree
d-1
, then by Euler formula
g
is homogeneous of degree
d
.

We are now in position to characterize determinantal generators of symmetric tensors. The next result proves
[
3
, Conjecture 3.13]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
n\geq 2
and let
R=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
. A tuple
(f_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n)\in R_{d}^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
is the tuple of determinantal equations of the eigenscheme of a polynomial in
R_{d}
if and only if

(4)

x_{i}f_{jk}-x_{j}f_{ik}+x_{k}f_{ij}=0

and

(5)

\partial_{i}f_{jk}-\partial_{j}f_{ik}+\partial_{k}f_{ij}=0

for every
0\leq i<j<k\leq n
.

Note that the equations labelled with (
4
) are precisely the equations in (
3
).

Proof.

It is immediate to check that the determinantal equations of the autoscheme of a polynomial satisfy (
4
) and (
5
). Now we assume that the two equations hold and we have to prove the existence of a polynomial
f\in R_{d}
such that
x_{i}\partial_{j}f-x_{j}\partial_{i}f=f_{ij}
. Assume that (
4
) holds for every
(i,j,k)
. By
3.1
there exist polynomials
g_{0},\dots,g_{n}\in R_{d-1}
such that
f_{ij}=x_{i}g_{j}-x_{j}g_{i}
for every
0\leq i<j\leq n
. If (
5
) holds for a fixed triple
(i,j,k)
, then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}f_{jk}-\partial_{j}f_{ik}+\partial_{k}f_{ij}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}(x_{j}g_{k}-x_{k}g_{j})-\partial_{j}(x_{i}g_{k}-x_{k}g_{i})+\partial_{k}(x_{i}g_{j}-x_{j}g_{i})

(6)

\displaystyle=x_{i}(-\partial_{j}g_{k}+\partial_{k}g_{j})-x_{j}(-\partial_{i}g_{k}+\partial_{k}g_{i})+x_{k}(-\partial_{i}g_{j}+\partial_{j}g_{i}).

If
d=2
, then
\deg(-\partial_{j}g_{k}+\partial_{k}g_{j})=\deg(-\partial_{i}g_{k}+\partial_{k}g_{i})=\deg(-\partial_{i}g_{j}+\partial_{j}g_{i})=0
. The last equation above implies then that
-\partial_{j}g_{k}+\partial_{k}g_{j}=-\partial_{i}g_{k}+\partial_{k}g_{i}=-\partial_{i}g_{j}+\partial_{j}g_{i}=0
. By repeating the same step with other triples
(i,j,k)
we obtain that
\partial_{j}g_{i}-\partial_{i}g_{j}=0
for every
0\leq i<j\leq n
. By what we discussed above, this implies that there exists
f\in R_{d}
, such that
\nabla f=(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})
. Substituting the identities
\partial_{i}f=g_{i}
in
f_{ij}=x_{i}g_{j}-x_{j}g_{i}
we obtain that
f_{ij}=x_{i}\partial_{j}f-x_{j}\partial_{i}f
which proves the claim.

Assume the claim holds for every degree smaller than
d
. Then (
6
) shows that the tuple
(\partial_{i}g_{j}-\partial_{j}g_{i}:0\leq i<j\leq n)\in R_{d-2}
satisfy (
4
). By Schwartz theorem, it also satisfies (
5
). By induction, there exists a polynomial
h\in R_{d-2}
such that

(\partial_{i}g_{j}-\partial_{j}g_{i})-(\partial_{i}x_{j}h-\partial_{j}x_{i}h)=\partial_{i}(g_{j}-x_{j}h)-\partial_{j}(g_{i}-x_{i}h)=0\\

for every
0\leq i<j\leq n
. This implies the existence on a polynomial
f\in R_{d}
such that
g_{i}-x_{i}h=\partial_{i}f
. Since
\partial_{i}x_{j}f-\partial_{j}x_{i}f=x_{j}(g_{i}-x_{i}h)-x_{i}(g_{j}-x_{j}h)=x_{j}g_{i}-x_{i}g_{j}=f_{ij}
we conclude.
∎

3.1
and

give an effective algorithm to test whether

Algorithm 1

Test if homogeneous forms are defining equations of an eigenscheme

Another computational problem one is naturally concerned with is the following: given
m

\mathbb{P}^{n}

) on the points. This yields a system of

Input:

p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
,

.

Step 1a:
For

where

. In words, each

is a polynomial of degree

with distinct unknowns
\lambda_{i,\alpha}

Step 2a:
Consider the system of

are contained in the eigenscheme of a partially symmetric tensor in

. A witness tensor is given by

, after evaluating the coefficients of each

in a solution of the system.

Step 1b:
Let

f=\sum_{\alpha}\lambda_{i,\alpha}x^{\alpha},

where

ranges among all degree

.

m\binom{n+1}{2}\binom{n+d}{d}

x_{i}\partial_{j}f(p_{k})-x_{j}\partial_{i}f(p_{k})=0

are contained in the eigenscheme of a symmetric tensor in

Test if points are contained in an eigenscheme of a tensor of fixed degree

4.
The variety of eigenpoints

Now we take a more geometric approach and we turn our attention to the variety parametrizing configurations of eigenpoints of tensors. Let
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{w(n,d)}
be the symmetric power of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
with itself
w(n,d)
times, where
w(n,d)
is the integer in
Definition

2.3
. In other words,
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{w(n,d)}
parametrizes sets of
w(n,d)
points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Since
w(n,d)
is precisely the degree of the eigenscheme of a generic tensor, following
[
2
, Section 5]
we define the
variety of eigenconfigurations

\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d}\subseteq(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{w(n,d)}
to be the Zariski closure of the set of all eigenconfigurations of
tensors in

(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
.

The dimension of
\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{2,d}
has been determined in
[
2
, Theorem 5.5]
. Thanks to Remark
3.2
, we can give an upper bound for such a dimension in general.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 3
we have

\dim\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d}\leq\dfrac{n(n+d)}{d-1}\binom{n+d-2}{n}-1.

Proof.

Consider the dominant rational map

\eta_{n,d}:\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-1})\times\dots\times\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-1})\dasharrow\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d},

with
\eta_{n,d}([g_{0}],\dots,[g_{n}])=E(T)
and
T=(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})
. By Remark
3.2
, the general fiber of

\eta_{n,d}
contains a copy of
\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{d-2})
. Since the (affine) dimension of
\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d}
is equal to the dimension of the source of the map
\eta_{n,d}
minus that of the generic fiber, we have projectively that

\dim\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d}\leq(n+1)\binom{n+d-1}{n}-\binom{n+d-2}{n}-1,

and the claim follows.
∎

By
[
2
, Theorem 5.5]
we have
\dim\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{2,d}=d^{2}+2d-1
, so the bound given by
4.1
is sharp for
n=2
. We expect equality to hold for every
n\geq 2
, but at the moment we lack a characterization of all possible defining matrices for a given
0
-dimensional eigenscheme.

Now we turn to the symmetric case. The dimension of the corresponding variety has already been computed in

[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
. Here we prove that
\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d,\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits}
is rational and give a birational parametrization. Our argument is similar to the one presented in
[
3
, Remark 3.7]
.

Definition 4.2
.

We set

\begin{matrix}\phi_{n,d}:&\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d})&\dashrightarrow&(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{w(n,d)}\\
&f&\mapsto&E(f),\end{matrix}

and we define
\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d,\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits}
to be the closure of the image of
\phi_{n,d}
.

Using
[
4
, Theorem 1.2]
, we obtain that
\phi_{n,d}
is defined on all forms
f
such that
E(f)
is reduced and
0
-dimensional.

Let
\alpha
be the projectivization of the linear map

\begin{matrix}\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}&\to&(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d})^{\oplus\binom{n+1}{2}}\\
f&\mapsto&(x_{i}\partial_{j}f-x_{j}\partial_{i}f)_{i<j},\end{matrix}

and define

T_{n,d}=\overline{\alpha(\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d}))}\subseteq\mathbb{P}((\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d})^{\oplus\binom{n+1}{2}})

the closure of the image of
\alpha
. Then
T_{n,d}
is a linear subspace of
\mathbb{P}((\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d})^{\oplus\binom{n+1}{2}})
and there is a commutative diagram

\textstyle{\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]_{d})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\phi_{n,d}}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d,\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits}}
\textstyle{T_{n,d}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi_{n,d}}

where
\psi_{n,d}(f_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n)
is the set of points defined by the ideal
(f_{ij}~:~i<j)
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
n
and
d
be positive integers such that
d\geq 3
. The map
\psi_{n,d}:T_{n,d}\dashrightarrow\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d,\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits}
is birational. In particular,
\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{n,d,\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits}
is an irreducible, rational variety.

Proof.

We distinguish two cases. If
d
is odd, then
\alpha
is an isomorphism by
[
3
, Lemma 2.8]
. Since
\phi_{n,d}
is birational by
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
,
\psi_{n,d}
is birational as well. Assume now that
d
is even. The fibers of
\alpha
are lines by
[
3
, Lemma 2.8]
and the general fiber of
\phi_{n,d}
is a line by
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
. This implies that
\psi_{n,d}
is generically injective.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

Another description of the eigenvariety was found in
[
5
, Section 5]
. The authors show that
\mathop{\rm Eig}\nolimits_{3,3,\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits}
is birational to a linear section of the Grassmanian

, but their argument can be easily adapted to different values of
n
and

.

5.
Generalized Laguerre maps

The goal of this section is to give a necessary condition for a
0
-dimensional scheme to be the eigenscheme of a tensor. We accomplish this task in Proposition
5.4
. Our argument relies on
[
3
, Section 5]
and consists in studying the fibers of the Laguerre map associated with a partially symmetric tensor.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 3
, and
let
T=(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a partially symmetric tensor. For every
0\leq i<j\leq n
, let
f_{ij}=x_{i}g_{j}-x_{j}g_{i}
be the
2\times 2
minors of the matrix
(
2
).
The
Laguerre map
associated with
T
is the map

\lambda_{T}:\mathbb{P}^{n}\dasharrow\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})

given by
\lambda_{T}(P)=(f_{ij}(P):0\leq i<j\leq n)
. It is defined on the open subset
\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus E(T)
.

Remark 5.2
.

Let us take a closer look at the image of
\lambda_{T}
. It is easy to see that
\lambda_{T}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\subsetneq\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{C}^{n+1})
. Indeed, the coordinates of
\lambda_{T}(P)
are the Plücker coordinates of the line in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
joining
P
and
(g_{0}(P):\ldots:g_{n}(P))
. Therefore
\lambda_{T}(\mathbb{P}^{n})\subseteq\mathbb{G}(1,n)
.

By definition, the ideal of
E(T)
is generated by the polynomials
f_{ij}
, hence the closure of the graph of
\lambda_{T}
is a model of the blow-up
{\mathop{\rm Bl}\nolimits}_{E(T)}\mathbb{P}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{G}(1,n)
. To determine its equations, observe that a point of
\mathbb{G}(1,n)
with Plücker coordinates corresponding to the
2\times 2
minors of a rank 2 matrix

\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&\dots&a_{n}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}&\dots&b_{n}\\
\end{matrix}\right)

lies in the image of
\lambda_{T}
if and only if there exists a point
P=(P_{0}:P_{1}:\ldots:P_{n})\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that

\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits\left(\begin{matrix}a_{0}&a_{1}&\dots&a_{n}\\
b_{0}&b_{1}&\dots&b_{n}\\
P_{0}&P_{1}&\dots&P_{n}\\
g_{0}(P)&g_{1}(P)&\dots&g_{n}(P)\end{matrix}\right)=2.

Therefore the equations of
{\mathop{\rm Bl}\nolimits}_{E(T)}\mathbb{P}^{n}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{G}(1,n)
are given by the

3\times 3
minors. Since the first two rows are linearly independent, by Kronecker’s theorem on the rank of a matrix it is enough to consider the
3\times 3
minors obtained by deleting the third row
and the

3\times 3
minors obtained deleting the fourth row. In particular, there are
\binom{n+1}{3}
equations of bidegree
(1,1)
and
\binom{n+1}{3}
equations of bidegree
(d-1,1)
.

We now want to study the fibers of
\lambda_{T}
. Let

p_{1}:{\mathop{\rm Bl}\nolimits}_{E(T)}\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}~\mbox{ and }~p_{2}:{\mathop{\rm Bl}\nolimits}_{E(T)}\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{G}(1,n)

be the projections. We observe that
\lambda_{T}^{-1}(\omega)=p_{1}(p_{2}^{-1}(\omega))
for every
\omega\in\mathbb{G}(1,n)
. Hence it suffices to look at fibers of
p_{2}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
T=(g_{0},\dots,g_{n})\in\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
be a partially symmetric tensor. Then every fiber of
\lambda_{T}:\mathbb{P}^{n}\dasharrow\mathbb{G}(1,n)
is contained in a line in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
\omega\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\lambda_{T})\subseteq\mathbb{G}(1,n)
. Then there exist
Q_{0},\ldots,Q_{n}\in\mathbb{C}
such that the homogeneous coordinates of
\omega
are the
2\times 2
minors of the matrix (
2
) evaluated at
Q=(Q_{0}:\ldots:Q_{n})
. In other words, the Plücker coordinates of
\omega
are
(\bar{p}_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n)
, where
\bar{p}_{ij}=Q_{i}g_{j}(Q)-Q_{j}g_{i}(Q)\in\mathbb{C}
. The fiber
p_{2}^{-1}(\omega)
is contained in the subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{G}(1,n)
whose equations in
P_{0},\dots,P_{n}
are the
3\times 3
minors of the matrix

\left(\begin{matrix}Q_{0}&Q_{1}&\dots&Q_{n}\\
g_{0}(Q)&g_{1}(Q)&\dots&g_{n}(Q)\\
P_{0}&P_{1}&\dots&P_{n}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

For all
0\leq i<j<k\leq n
, we get

(7)

\bar{p}_{ij}P_{k}-\bar{p}_{ik}P_{j}+\bar{p}_{jk}P_{i}=0.

Once we project on the first factor, we see that the fiber
\lambda_{T}^{-1}(\omega)=p_{1}(p_{2}^{-1}(\omega))\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is contained in the linear subspace defined by the
\binom{n+1}{3}
linear equations (
7
) in the variables
P_{0},\dots,P_{n}
. It is not difficult to see that the coefficient matrix
A_{\omega}
of this linear system gives the linear map

\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\to\bigwedge^{3}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
defined by

A_{\omega}(v)=\omega\wedge v.

For instance, for
n=3
we get

A_{\omega}=\left(\begin{matrix}\bar{p}_{12}&-\bar{p}_{02}&\bar{p}_{01}&0\\
\bar{p}_{13}&-\bar{p}_{03}&0&\bar{p}_{01}\\
\bar{p}_{23}&0&-\bar{p}_{03}&\bar{p}_{02}\\
0&\bar{p}_{23}&-\bar{p}_{13}&\bar{p}_{12}\\
\end{matrix}\right).

By recalling that a 2-vector
\omega
is decomposable if and only if
\dim\ker A_{\omega}=2
, we have that
\mathop{\rm rank}\nolimits A_{\omega}=n-1
. It follows that
\lambda_{T}^{-1}(\omega)
is contained in an intersection of
n-1
hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, which is a line.
∎

Equations (
7
) determine the projectivization of the tautological bundle of
\mathbb{G}(1,n)
inside
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{G}(1,n)
.

Proposition
5.3
allows us to prove the main result of this section, thereby generalizing
[
3
, Theorem 5.5]
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
d\geq 2
and let
T\in(\mathbb{C}^{n+1})^{\otimes d}
be a tensor such that
\dim E(T)=0
. Then for every
k\in\{2,\dots,d-1\}
, no degree
kd
subscheme of
E(T)
is contained in an irreducible curve of degree
k
. Moreover, no degree
d+1
subscheme of
E(T)
is contained in a line.

Proof.

As usual, we can assume that
T\in\mathop{\rm Sym}\nolimits^{d-1}\mathbb{C}^{n+1}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n+1}
is partially symmetric. In
[
3
, Proposition 2.6(1)]
we can see how
E(T)
cannot contain
d+1
collinear points, and the argument holds verbatim for a
0
-dimensional scheme of degree
d+1
. Assume that there exists a degree
kd
subscheme of
E(T)
contained in an irreducible curve
C\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
of degree
k
. If we call
\mathcal{L}=\langle f_{ij}~:~0\leq i<j\leq n\rangle
the linear system on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
associated with the Laguerre map
\lambda_{T}
, then we can interpret
\lambda_{T}:\mathbb{P}^{n}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{L}^{*})
as the map assigning to a point
P\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus E(T)
the hyperplane

\mathcal{L}_{P}=\{D\in\mathcal{L}~:~P\in D\}\subseteq\mathcal{L}.

If
P\in C
, then every
D\in\mathcal{L}_{P}
contains
E(T)\cup\{P\}
, so it intersects
C
with multiplicity at least
kd+1>\deg(C)\cdot\deg(D)
. Since
C
is irreducible, we deduce that
C\subseteq D
by Bézout’s Theorem. Hence

\lambda_{T}(P)=\mathcal{L}_{P}=\{D\in\mathcal{L}~:~C\subseteq D\}

is constant for every
P\in C\setminus E(T)
. It follows that
C
is contracted by
\lambda_{T}
, and this implies that
k=1
by Proposition
5.3
.
∎

The bound on collinear points given by Proposition
5.4
is sharp. Indeed, it is easy to find order
d
tensors whose eigenscheme contains
d
points on a line, see for instance
[
5
, Example 4.2]
.
