Fluctuation Results for Multi-species Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model in the Replica Symmetric Regime
Partha S. Dey ⋆, Qiang Wu †

Abstract
. We study the Replica Symmetric region of general multi-species Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (MSK) Model and answer some of the questions raised in Ann. Probab. 43 (2015), no. 6, 3494–3513, where the author proved the Parisi formula under positive-definite assumption on the disorder covariance matrix Δ 2 \Delta^{2} . First, we prove exponential overlap concentration at high temperature for both indefinite and positive-definite Δ 2 \Delta^{2} MSK model. We also prove a central limit theorem for the free energy using overlap concentration. Furthermore, in the zero external field case, we use a quadratic coupling argument to prove overlap concentration up to β c \beta_{c} , which is expected to be the critical inverse temperature. The argument holds for both positive-definite and emphindefinite Δ 2 \Delta^{2} , and β c \beta_{c} has the same expression in two different cases. Second, we develop a species-wise cavity approach to study the overlap fluctuation, and the asymptotic variance-covariance matrix of overlap is obtained as the solution to a matrix-valued linear system. The asymptotic variance also suggests the de Almeida–Thouless (AT) line condition from the Replica Symmetry (RS) side. Our species-wise cavity approach does not require the positive-definiteness of Δ 2 \Delta^{2} . However, it seems that the AT line conditions in positive-definite and indefinite cases are different. Finally, in the case of positive-definite Δ 2 \Delta^{2} , we prove that above the AT line, the MSK model is in Replica Symmetry Breaking phase under some natural assumption. This generalizes the results of J. Stat. Phys. 174 (2019), no. 2, 333–350, from 2-species to general species.

1.
Introduction and Main Results

Multi-species Sherrington-Kirkpatrick (MSK) model, as a generalization of the classical SK model
[
34
,
35
,
27
]
, was introduced by Barra
et. al.
in
[
9
]
. In the MSK model, the spins are divided into finitely many species, and the density of each species is asymptotically fixed with increasing system volume. The interaction parameter between different spins depends only on the species structure. Thus, one can consider the MSK model as an inhomogeneous generalization of the classical SK model. The detailed definition is given in Section
1.1
.

One important question in spin glasses is to understand the behavior of limiting free energy. In the classical SK model, Parisi
[
31
,
30
]
proposed his celebrated variational formula, known as the Parisi formula, which gives the limiting free energy at all temperatures. Talagrand
[
33
]
proved this in the setting of mixed
p
-spin models for even
p
. Later Panchenko
[
28
]
, using a different approach, proved the Parisi formula of mixed
p
-spin models for all
p
.

The authors in
[
9
]
proposed a Parisi type formula for the limiting free energy in the MSK model. They also proved the upper bound by adapting Guerra’s interpolation method under
positive-definite
condition on the interaction matrix
\Delta^{2}
. Later, Panchenko
[
29
]
completed the proof by proving a multi-species version of the Ghirlanda-Guerra identities to match the lower bound. The
positive-definite
condition on
\Delta^{2}
was used only in the proof of the upper bound. In
[
29
]
, Panchenko also proposed several open questions, such as understanding behaviors of the model when the interaction matrix
\Delta^{2}
is
indefinite
, deriving an analog of the AT line condition in MSK model, among others. The authors in
[
12
]
derived the AT line condition of two-species
positive-definite
case and proved that above the AT line, the 2-species SK model is RSB. The essential tool of their argument is a perturbation of the Parisi formula, which is only known for
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
. The idea fails in the general species case because of algebraic difficulties.

For the interesting question about what happens when
\Delta^{2}
is
indefinite
, to the best of our knowledge, only some partial results on a few particular models have been known very recently. In
[
11
,
10
]
, there are some conjectured min-max type formulas by physicists for the bipartite SK model, which is a special case of
indefinite
MSK model. Another particular case of
indefinite
MSK model known as deep Boltzmann machine (DBM) was investigated in
[
2
,
4
]
in the replica symmetry regime, and a complete solution was obtained in
[
3
]
under assumptions on the Nishimori line. Besides that, a min-max formula for the replica symmetric solution in the DBM model is proved in
[
16
]
. Recently, Mourrat
[
22
,
21
,
25
,
26
,
23
,
24
]
has reinterpreted the Parisi formula as the solution to an infinite-dimensional Hamilton-Jacobi equation in the Wasserstein space of probability measures on the positive half-line. Particularly in
[
25
]
, he studied the bipartite SK model and questioned the possibility to represent the limiting free energy as a variational formula. In the setting of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation, non-convex
\Delta^{2}
breaks down the application of the Hopf-Lax formula while solving the PDE. For the spherical case, Auffinger and Chen
[
7
]
studied the bipartite spherical SK (BSSK) model and proved a variational formula for the limiting free energy at sufficiently high temperature. Later, Baik and Lee
[
8
]
computed the limiting free energy and its fluctuations at all non-critical temperatures in the BSSK model using tools from random matrix theory. However, computing limiting free energy for general
indefinite
model is still unknown. Specifically for Ising spins, we do not even know the limiting free energy at any temperature, other than the lower bound by Panchenko
[
29
]
.

In this paper, we do a high-temperature analysis of the Ising MSK model, and our main contributions are:

(1)

For external field
h\geqslant 0
, by extending Latala’s argument, we prove an RS regime in the MSK model, where the overlap has exponential concentration. Note that our approach is unified for both
positive-definite
and
indefinite
MSK model, but the proved RS regimes have a different form; see Theorem
1.7
. For
h=0
, by using a quadratic coupling argument, we prove the concentration of overlap up to
\beta_{c}=\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, where
\Delta^{2}
is the interaction matrix and
\Lambda
is the diagonal matrix containing the species size ratios. This result is true for
indefinite
and
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
,
i.e.,
we prove the whole RS regime for the general MSK model, which is given as
\beta<\beta_{c}
, see Theorem
1.8
. The above overlap concentrations also enable us to prove the limit and central limit theorem of free energy in the corresponding RS regime; see Theorem
1.9
.

(2)

By developing a different species-wise cavity method, we derive a linear system of Gibbs average of a quadratic form of overlap vectors. The system is solved using linear algebraic methods, enabling us to compute the variance-covariance structure of overlap vectors. The computation also suggests the AT-line condition in the MSK model from the replica symmetry side. Note that our species-wise cavity method does not require
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
, but the AT line condition for
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
seems to be more complicated; see the discussions below Theorem
1.13
.

(3)

In the case of
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
, we prove the AT line condition from the RSB side under some natural assumption. The key is still the perturbation idea of the Parisi formula. This generalizes the result for
2
-species SK model in
[
12
]
. For the
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
case, we use our species-wise cavity approach to give a conjectured form of the AT line condition. To get a rigorous proof of the AT line in this case is challenging. Because, first, the Parisi formula for
indefinite
MSK model is still in mystery, so the classical perturbation technique can not be used. Second, proving the uniqueness of stationary point is hard. We prove a uniqueness result in 2-species case using an elementary approach, see Proposition
1.6
.

The definition of the MSK model is given in Section
1.1
, then we review the main results of the
2
-species case in
[
12
]
, and conclude Section
1
by the statement of our results. Readers can always check notations back in Section
1.4
. A road map is given in Section
1.5
.

1.1.
MSK model

Fix
N\geqslant 1
. For a spin configuration on the
N
-dimensional hyper-cube,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N})\in\Sigma_{N}:=\{-1,+1\}^{N}
, we consider the Hamiltonian given by

(1)

\displaystyle H_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}+h\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sigma_{i}

where
g_{ij}
, the disorder interaction parameters are independent centered Gaussian random variables,
\beta>0
is the inverse temperature and
h\geqslant 0
is the external field.

In the classical SK model, the variance structure of disorder
g_{ij}
is homogeneous, usually taken as
g_{ij}\sim\N(0,1)
. While in the MSK model, the variance of
g_{ij}
depends on the structure of species among
N
spins.

Assume that, there are
m\geqslant 2
species. When
m=1
the MSK model reduces to the classical SK model. We partition the set of spins into
m
disjoint sets, namely,

I=\bigcup_{s=1}^{m}I_{s}=\{1,2,\ldots,N\},\qquad I_{s}\cap I_{t}=\emptyset\text{ for }s\neq t.

For
i\in I_{s},j\in I_{t}
, we assume

\E g_{ij}^{2}=\Delta_{st}^{2},

i.e.,
the inhomogeneity of MSK model comes from the interaction among different species. While proving MSK Parisi formula in
[
29
,
9
]
, the assumption that
\Delta^{2}=(\!(\Delta_{st}^{2})\!)_{s,t=1}^{m}
is symmetric and
positive-definite
was used. But in this paper, we do not require the positive-definiteness condition for most of the results. Besides that, we assume that the ratio of spins in each species is fixed asymptotically,
i.e.,
for
s=1,2,\ldots,m
and

\lambda_{s,N}:={|I_{s}|}/{N},

we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lambda_{s,N}=\lambda_{s}\in(0,1).

Since
\lambda_{s,N}
and
\lambda_{s}
are asymptotically the same, for the rest of the article we will use
\lambda_{s}
instead of
\lambda_{s,N}
for convenience. We denote
\Lambda:=\diag(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
.

The overlap vector between two replicas
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}\in\Sigma_{N}
is given by

\mathbf{R}_{12}=(R_{12}^{(1)},R_{12}^{(2)},\ldots,R_{12}^{(m)})^{\intercal},

where

R_{12}^{(s)}:=\frac{1}{|I_{s}|}\sum_{i\in I_{s}}\sigma_{i}^{1}\sigma_{i}^{2}.

is the overlap restricted to species
s\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. In some cases we write it as
R^{(s)}
for short if there are only 2 replicas involved. All vectors will be considered as a column vector in the rest of the article.

A central question in spin glasses is to understand the free energy

(2)

\displaystyle F_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\log Z_{N},\quad\text{where}\quad Z_{N}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}}\exp(H_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))

is the partition function, and the Gibbs measure is given by

(3)

\displaystyle G_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})={\exp(H_{N}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}))}/{Z_{N}},\qquad\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}.

Later we will also use
\langle\cdot\rangle
to denote the Gibbs average just for convenience; check Section
1.4
. When
\Delta^{2}
is
positive-definite
, Parisi formula gives a variational representation of the limiting free energy for all
\beta>0,h\in\mathds{R}
, which was set up in
[
9
,
29
]
for MSK model. This paper is mainly about the phase transitions and fluctuation of free energy in the RS regime. Before stating the main results, let us recall some related results in MSK model in
[
12
,
29
,
9
]
. We will only use the Parisi formula in the proof of Theorem
1.15
.

1.2.
Related results in MSK model

Consider a sequence of real numbers

(4)

\displaystyle 0=\zeta_{0}<\zeta_{1}<\cdots<\zeta_{k}<\zeta_{k+1}=1,

and for each
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, the sequences

(5)

\displaystyle 0=q_{0}^{s}\leqslant q_{1}^{s}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant q_{k+1}^{s}\leqslant q_{k+2}^{s}=1.

For
0\leqslant l\leqslant k+2
, define
\mathbf{q}_{\ell}=(q_{\ell}^{s})_{s=1}^{m}
,

(6)

\displaystyle Q_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{2}{\mathbf{q}_{\ell}}^{\intercal}\Lambda\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q}_{\ell},\quad Q_{\ell}^{s}:=(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q}_{\ell})_{s}\quad\text{for}\ 1\leqslant s\leqslant m.

Given these sequences, consider i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables
(\eta_{\ell})_{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant k+2}
. Define

X_{k+2}^{s}:=\log\cosh\bigl(h+\beta\sum_{0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k+1}\eta_{\ell+1}(Q_{\ell+1}^{s}-Q_{\ell}^{s})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\bigr),

then recursively define for
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant k+1
,

X_{\ell}^{s}:=\frac{1}{\zeta_{\ell}}\log\E_{\ell+1}\exp(\zeta_{\ell}X_{\ell+1}^{s}).

where
\E_{\ell+1}
denotes expectation w.r.t.
\eta_{\ell+1}
. The following theorem gives the Parisi formula in MSK model.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
29
]
*Theorem 1)
.

For the MSK model with positive-definite
\Delta^{2}
, the limiting free energy is given by

(7)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}=\inf_{\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{q}}\mathscr{P}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{q}),

where

\mathscr{P}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{q})=\log 2+\sum_{s=1}^{m}\lambda_{s}X_{0}^{s}-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k+1}\zeta_{\ell}(Q_{\ell+1}-Q_{\ell}).

is the Parisi functional and the
\inf
in (
7
) is taken over all the sequences in (
4
) and (
5
).

In the above variational formula (
7
), let
k=0
and
q_{1}^{s}=q_{s}\in[0,1]
, then the Parisi functional on the RS regime simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{\RS}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle:=\log 2+\sum_{s=1}^{m}\lambda_{s}\E\log\cosh(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}+h)+\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{q})^{\intercal}\Lambda\Delta^{2}\Lambda(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{q})

where
\mathbf{1}
is the vector of all
1
’s and
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m})^{\intercal}
. Taking derivative w.r.t.
q_{t}
for
t=1,\ldots,m

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\RS}}{\partial q_{t}}=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\lambda_{t}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\Delta_{s,t}^{2}\lambda_{s}\left[q_{s}-\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}+h)\right].

and setting it 0, we get the set of critical points (when
\Delta^{2}
is invertible) to be

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)=\left\{\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{m}\ \bigl|\ \E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}+h)=q_{s}\ \text{for}\ s=1,2,\ldots m\right\}.

For the MSK model with positive-definite
\Delta^{2}
, we define the Replica Symmetric solution as

(10)

\displaystyle\RS(\beta,h):=\inf_{\mathbf{q}\in[0,1]^{m}}\mathscr{P}_{\RS}(\mathbf{q}).

Note that
\mathscr{P}_{\RS}
is well-defined for
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
. However, one expects the replica symmetric solution to be achieved at a saddle point of
\mathscr{P}_{\RS}
instead of a minimizer, see
[
11
]
*Theorem 4. The
\mathscr{P}_{\RS}
functional in the
indefinite
case seems to be not convex. We only use the
\RS(\beta,h)
expression for proving the replica symmetry breaking in Theorem
1.15
for
positive-definite
case. Actually, in the RS region for
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
, the infimum should be achieved at the critical point in
\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)
instead of some boundary point. This fact can easily be partially checked by comparing
\RS(\beta,h)
with the replica symmetric solution we obtained from the overlap concentration results in Theorem
1.9
. However, to rigorously prove this for the whole region can be a nasty calculus problem. The other way is to prove the convexity of the
\mathscr{P}_{\RS}
functional, which is beyond the scope of the current paper. Therefore, we will assume that the infimum in (
10
) is not achieved at the boundary for Theorem
1.15
. The following results were proved in
[
12
]
for
m=2
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
12
]
*Theorem 1.1–1.2)
.

Restricted to
m=2
species model, under the assumption

(11)

\displaystyle\Delta_{12}^{2}=1,\Delta_{11}^{2}\Delta_{22}^{2}>1\ \text{and}\ \lambda_{1}\Delta_{11}^{2}\geqslant\lambda_{2}\Delta_{22}^{2},

(i)

If either
h>0
or

(12)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}<\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}\Delta_{11}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\Delta_{22}^{2}+\sqrt{(\lambda_{1}\Delta_{11}^{2}-\lambda_{2}\Delta_{22}^{2})^{2}+4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}}}

then
\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)=\{\mathbf{q}_{\star}\}
is a singleton.

(ii)

Assume
h>0
, let
\mathbf{q}\in\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)
, and
\gamma_{s}:=\E\sech^{4}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}+h)
for
s=1,2
. If

(13)

\displaystyle\beta^{2}>\frac{2}{\lambda_{1}\gamma_{1}\Delta_{11}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\gamma_{2}\Delta_{22}^{2}+\sqrt{(\lambda_{1}\gamma_{1}\Delta_{11}^{2}-\lambda_{2}\gamma_{2}\Delta_{22}^{2})^{2}+4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}}}

then

\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}<\RS(\beta,h).

Comparing with the classical SK model, since the RHS of (
12
) is reduced to the critical
\beta
for
h=0
in SK model when
m=1
and the similar form of RHS of (
12
) and (
13
), one can reasonably guess that (
12
) gives an RS regime of MSK model, and (
13
) is the AT line condition. The authors in
[
12
]
proved that (
13
) indeed gives RSB phase for
m=2
using the idea in
[
35
]
*Chapter 13 by a 1-RSB perturbation of the Parisi formula, and the uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
is essential in the proof of Theorem
1.2
part (ii), without that, it is hard to give an accurate description of the AT line.

In this paper, we prove the uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
in the general species case under an analogous condition of (

). To fully generalize Theorem

in the
m>2
case (
i.e.,
incorporating the case

), one needs to analyze the uniqueness of the solution to a nonlinear system. Unfortunately, we are not able to prove that. However, assuming

is

,

is unique when

) is not achieved at the boundary, we can prove the AT line condition for general

. The idea is still based on the perturbation technique of the Parisi formula. On the other hand, our variance analysis in Section

1.3.
Statement of the main results

First, by adapting Lalata’s argument, we prove that the MSK model is in the RS regime when
\beta<\beta_{0}
. As noted in Section
1
, our argument holds for
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
and proves the asymptotics of the free energy in the MSK model with
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result dealing with general
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
in the Ising MSK model.

For the rest of the article, we will assume the following:

Assumption 1.3
.

Assume that
\Delta^{2}
is a symmetric and invertible
m\times m
nonzero matrix with non-negative entries. However, it need not be positive-definite.

Before stating the RS phase diagram results, we need to generalize that the set
\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)
is singleton from
2
-species
[
12
]
to general
m
-species.
We define

(14)

\displaystyle\beta_{c}:=\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda)^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

where
\rho(A)
is the

or the largest absolute value of the eigenvalues of

. In general, one has
\rho(A)\leqslant\|A\|
. But it is easy to check that for symmetric
A
,
\rho(A)=\|A\|
.

Theorem 1.4

(Uniqueness of solution)
.

Assume that
\beta<\beta_{c}
. Then
\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)
is a singleton set,
i.e.,
the system

(15)

\displaystyle q_{s}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}+h),\qquad s=1,2,\ldots,m

has a unique solution, where
\eta\sim\N(0,1)
.

Remark 1.5
.

If we remove the invertiblity of
\Delta^{2}
in Assumption

, then one has to replace the equations (
15
) by setting (
8
) to 0 and uniqueness holds for the vector
\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q}
.

The proof is similar to the SK model, which is because the RHS map is a contraction for small

. For large

, the contraction is not true anymore. In the SK model with

, Latala–Guerra lemma
[

]
*Proposition 1.3.8 tells us

is still unique. The proof is based on the monotone property of a nonlinear function and intermediate value theorem. In the MSK model, the analog of Latala–Guerra is not obvious since we are dealing with a system of nonlinear equations (

). The authors in
[
12
]
give proof for the

case using an elementary approach, but the idea is hard to generalize for

. Their proof holds only for
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
case. For a particular

MSK model, the deep Boltzmann machine, the Latala-Guerra lemma

]
has been extended to arbitrary depth with Gaussian random field

. For

\Delta^{2}
, the following Proposition

gives the uniqueness of

.

Proposition 1.6
.

For

, assume

. If

, then the system (

) has a unique solution for all

.

This proposition will be used to discuss AT line for some bipartite SK models in Example

. The proof of Proposition

will be given in Section

.

Now we define the symmetric

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}:=|\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}|.

obtained by taking absolute values of all the eigenvalues in the spectral decomposition of the symmetric matrix
\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
. We also denote by

(17)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12}:=\mathbf{R}_{12}-\mathbf{q}.

\mathbf{q}
is the unique solution to (

Theorem 1.7

(Overlap concentration for general

)
.

Assume that
\beta<\beta_{0}:={\beta_{c}}/{\sqrt{4\alpha}}
, where
\alpha=\alpha(\Delta^{2}):=1+\mathds{1}\{\Delta^{2}\text{ is indefinite}\}
. For
2\eta<\beta_{c}^{2}-4\alpha\beta^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\nu(\exp(\eta N\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})))\leqslant\det(I-(2\eta+4\alpha\beta^{2})\mathcal{V})^{-1/2}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{x}):=\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathcal{V}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x}.

Theorem
1.7
says that the overlap vector
\mathbf{R}_{12}
concentrates around
\mathbf{q}
when

for all

, and its proof is given in Section

.

Theorem 1.8

(Overlap Concentration for

)

If

(18)

\displaystyle\nu\left(\exp\bigl(\frac{1}{8}(\beta_{c}^{2}-\beta^{2})N\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{R}_{12})\bigr)\right)\leqslant K

The proof of Theorem

With the control of overlap, we also prove the following.

Theorem 1.9

(LLN and CLT for free energy)

If

\displaystyle\left|F_{N}-\RS(\beta,h)\right|\leqslant\frac{K}{N},

2.
Concentration of overlap in MSK

Originally Latala’s argument
[
20
]
in the SK model is used to prove the concentration of overlap in part of the RS regime. The idea is based on Guerra’s interpolation of two different spin glass models, one of which is the fully decoupled model,
i.e.,
the associated Gibbs measure is a product measure, while the other model is the standard SK model of our interest. With the concentration of overlap in the decoupled model, one can obtain the concentration of overlap in the SK model by controlling the derivative of the interpolated model. For the details of this classical story, see Section 1.3 and 1.4 in
[
34
]
. This section employs a similar idea in the MSK model to prove the concentration of overlap vectors. With this concentration result, we also prove a part of the RS phase diagram.

Following Guerra’s interpolation, given two independent centered Gaussian vectors
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}),\boldsymbol{v}=(v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})
indexed by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\Sigma_{N}
, consider the interpolation given by
u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(t)=\sqrt{t}\cdot u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}+\sqrt{1-t}\cdot v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
, for
t\in[0,1]
. Suppose
F
is a twice differentiable function allowing us to use Gaussian integration by parts. Taking
\phi(t):=\E F(\boldsymbol{u}(t))
and using Gaussian integration by parts we get the following.

Proposition 2.1
.

For
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\Sigma_{N}
,

\phi^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}}U(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})\E\left(\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}x_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}(\boldsymbol{u}(t))\right)

where
U(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}):=\frac{1}{2}(\E u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\E v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}v_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})
.

Given
(\eta_{i})_{i=1,2,\ldots,N}
i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables and independent of
(g_{ij})_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}
, we take

u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i<j}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},\quad v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\beta\sum_{s=1}^{m}\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}\cdot\sum_{i\in I_{s}}\eta_{i}\sigma_{i},\quad w_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\exp\bigl(h\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\bigr)

and

to be the free energy, where

. In this case, the interpolated Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{N,t}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}):=u_{t}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=\sqrt{t}u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}+\sqrt{1-t}v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}.

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\E u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=\frac{N\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\sum_{s,t}\lambda_{s}R^{s}\Delta_{s,t}^{2}\lambda_{t}R^{t}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}\Delta_{s,s}^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\E v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}v_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}

\displaystyle=N\beta^{2}\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}R^{s}Q^{s}=N\beta^{2}\sum_{s,t}\lambda_{s}R^{s}\Delta_{s,t}^{2}\lambda_{t}q_{t}.

In particular,

\displaystyle U(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\E u_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}u_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}-\E v_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}v_{\boldsymbol{\tau}})=\frac{N\beta^{2}}{4}\left(\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\mathbf{R}})-\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{q})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}\Delta_{s,s}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\text{ and }U(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{N\beta^{2}}{4}\left(\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{q})-\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{q})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s}\lambda_{s}\Delta_{s,s}^{2}\right).

By a simple computation, we have

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}=\frac{1}{N}\cdot w_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}e^{x_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}/Z_{N},\quad\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial x_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\partial x_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}=\frac{1}{N}\cdot(\mathds{1}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}=\boldsymbol{\tau}}-w_{\tau}e^{x_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}}/Z_{N})\cdot w_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}e^{x_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}/Z_{N}

and we use this to rewrite the formula of

(31)

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\E(\langle U(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\sigma})\rangle_{t}-\langle U(\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau})\rangle_{t})=\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\left(\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{q})-\nu_{t}(\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12}))\right).

In the following section, we will prove that

is concentrated around
\mathbf{q}
at some high temperature regime,

the term

along the interpolation path. In order to prove the Theorem

,

with respect to

Lemma 2.2

(

]
*Page 33)

If

, then

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant l,l^{\prime}\leqslant n}\nu_{t}(U(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l^{\prime}})f)+n(n+1)\nu_{t}(U(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n+2})f)

\displaystyle\nu_{t}^{\prime}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant l<l^{\prime}\leqslant n}2\nu_{t}(U(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l^{\prime}})f)+n(n+1)\nu_{t}(U(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n+1},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n+2})f)-2n\sum_{l\leqslant n}\nu_{t}(U(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{l},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{n+1})f).

Next, we prove a useful lemma comparing the quadratic forms of overlap under

Lemma 2.3
.

Consider

, for

\displaystyle\left|\nu_{t}(\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}})\exp(\rho N\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})))\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu_{t}(\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})\exp(\rho N\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})))

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Let

Without loss of generality, we assume

\displaystyle\left|\nu_{t}(\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{13})\exp(\rho N\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})))\right|

\displaystyle\nu_{t}(\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{13})\cdot\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})^{k})

\displaystyle\leqslant\nu_{t}(\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{13})^{k+1})^{1/(k+1)}\cdot\nu_{t}(\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})^{k+1})^{k/(k+1)}=\nu_{t}(\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})^{k+1})

First, take a Gaussian vector

\displaystyle\nu_{0}(\exp(\kappa N\mathcal{P}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12}))=\E\nu_{0}(\exp(\sqrt{N}\boldsymbol{u}^{\intercal}\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12}))

First, let us prove the RS solution (

Next, we prove the second part of Theorem

Our goal is to prove a Central Limit Theorem for

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

be the interpolated Gibbs measure. For any bounded twice differentiable function

and

(36)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\E g(X_{t})=\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\E\left(g^{\prime\prime}(X_{t})\bigl(\langle\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})\rangle_{t}-\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{q})-N^{-1}\tr(\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\bigr)-\sqrt{N}g^{\prime}(X_{t})\langle\mathcal{Q}(\overline{\mathbf{R}}_{12})\rangle_{t}\right).

and

.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

For

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\E g(X_{t})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\E g^{\prime}(X_{t})\biggl(\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}G_{t}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\biggl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{tN}}\sum_{i<j}g_{ij}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-t}}\sum_{r=1}^{m}\sqrt{Q^{r}}\sum_{i\in I_{r}}\eta_{i}\sigma_{i}\biggr)-\frac{N\beta^{2}}{4}\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{q})\biggr).

are both Gaussian random variables. Applying Gaussian integration by parts, we get that the RHS is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{2}}{2N}\E\Bigg[g^{\prime\prime}(X_{s})\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\tau}}G_{t}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})G_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\left(\frac{1}{2}(N\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{R}_{12})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}\lambda_{r}\Delta_{r,r}^{2})-N\sum_{r,s}\lambda_{r}R_{12}^{r}\Delta_{r,s}^{2}\lambda_{s}q_{s}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sqrt{N}}\E\Bigg[g^{\prime}(X_{s})\Bigg(\sum_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}G_{t}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})\Big(\frac{1}{2}(N\mathcal{Q}(\mathbf{1})-\sum_{r=1}^{m}\lambda_{r}\Delta_{r,r}^{2})-N\sum_{r,s}\lambda_{r}\Delta_{r,s}^{2}\lambda_{s}q_{s}

For the second part, taking

5.
MSK cavity solution

For the classical SK model, the cavity method is an induction on the number of spins. In this section, we generalize the cavity method to the MSK model and derive a linear system for the variance-covariance matrices of the overlap vectors. More specifically, to compare the system with
N
spins and
(N-1)
spins, we need to choose a spin to be decoupled from others.

Since the classical SK model is the one-species case of the MSK model, the spin can be chosen uniformly. For the multi-species case, decoupling a spin now depends on the structure of the species. However, we can choose a species
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
first with probability
{\lambda_{s}}
, then select a spin uniformly inside that species to be decoupled, then compare this decoupled system and the original one. For convenience, we denote the released spin as
\varepsilon:=\sigma_{s_{\star}}
, where
s_{\star}
is the index of last spin in species
s
for a configuration
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. Once the species is determined, we take the convention to decouple the last spin in the chosen species because of symmetry inside a particular species. Note that this is equivalent to choosing a species, u.a.r. from all spins.

Let
\delta
represent the species to be chosen, which is a random variable taking value in
\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
with probability
\mathbb{P}(\delta=s)=\lambda_{s}
. Given
\delta=\tilde{s}
, we decouple the last spin
\sigma_{\tilde{s}_{\star}}
in that species, then the interpolated Hamiltonian between the original system and the decoupled system is

(49)

\displaystyle H_{t}^{\tilde{s}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})=H_{\tilde{s}_{\star}^{-}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})+\sigma_{\tilde{s}_{\star}}\biggl(\sqrt{t}\cdot\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i\neq\tilde{s}_{\star}}g_{i\tilde{s}_{\star}}\sigma_{i}+\sqrt{1-t}\cdot\beta\eta\sqrt{Q^{\tilde{s}}}+h\biggr)

where
H_{\tilde{s}_{\star}^{-}}(\boldsymbol{\rho})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i<j}g_{ij}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}+\sum_{i\neq\tilde{s}_{\star}}\rho_{i}
, and
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is a (
N-1
)-dimensional vector of spins in
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
without
\sigma_{\tilde{s}_{\star}}
.

Recall that the overlap vector between two replicas
\sigma^{l},\sigma^{l^{\prime}}
in MSK model is defined as

(50)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{ll^{\prime}}=(R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(1)},R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(2)},\cdots,R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(m)})^{\intercal}

where each coordinate in the above vector represents the marginal overlap in the corresponding species. Similarly,

(51)

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}_{ll^{\prime}}^{(\tilde{s}-)}=(R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(1)},R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(2)},\cdots,R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(\tilde{s}-)},\cdots R_{ll^{\prime}}^{(m)})^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=\beta^{-2}(3b_{s}(0)-4b_{s}(1)+b_{s}(2))=1-4q_{s}+3\hat{q}_{s},

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle=\beta^{-2}(-3b_{s}(0)+2b_{s}(1))=2q_{s}+q_{s}^{2}-3\hat{q}_{s}.

Then,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{2}V_{2i}\hat{B}(i)

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{2}V_{1i}\hat{B}(i)=\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda,\quad\sum_{i=0}^{2}V_{0i}\hat{B}(i)=\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda,

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}(2)

\displaystyle=\Gamma\Lambda^{-1},\quad\hat{\Theta}(1)=\Gamma^{\prime}\Lambda^{-1},\quad\hat{\Theta}(0)=\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\Lambda^{-1}.

Combining with (
74
) we have the result.
\blacksquare

Before going to the proof of Theorem
1.13
, we will prove the following lemma, which essentially solves the continuous Lyapunov equation. Recall that a square matrix
A
is
stable
if all the eigenvalues of
A
have a strictly negative real part.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
A
be a
m\times m
stable matrix, and
C
be a symmetric matrix. Suppose that the symmetric matrix
X
satisfies the equation,

\sym(AX)=-C.

Then we have

X=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{t}{2}A}Ce^{\frac{t}{2}A^{\intercal}}dt.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

First we consider the case when
A
is stable and similar to a diagonal matrix,
i.e.,

A=SDS^{-1}
for a diagonal matrix
D
with negative diagonal entries and an invertible matrix
S
. We can write
\sym(AX)=S\sym(DS^{-1}XS^{-\intercal})S^{\intercal}
and solve for
\sym(DY)=-S^{-1}CS^{-\intercal}
where
Y=S^{-1}XS^{-\intercal}
. Furthermore,

\int_{0}^{\infty}Se^{\frac{t}{2}D}S^{-1}CS^{-\intercal}e^{\frac{t}{2}D}S^{\intercal}dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{t}{2}A}Ce^{\frac{t}{2}A^{\intercal}}dt.

Thus, w.l.o.g.
A
can be taken as a diagonal matrix.

We write
\vect(A)
as the
m^{2}\times 1
vector formed by stacking the columns of
A
,
i.e.,

\vect(A)=\sum_{s=1}^{m}e_{s}\otimes Ae_{s}=\sum_{s=1}^{m}A^{\intercal}e_{s}\otimes e_{s}
, where
e_{s}
is the canonical basis vector with 1 at
s
-th entry and 0 elsewhere,
\otimes
is the Kronecker product of matrices. We define a linear map
\phi
from
m\times m
matrices to
m^{2}\times m^{2}
matrices:

\phi(A):=\frac{1}{2}(A\otimes I+I\otimes A).

One can easily check that,
\vect(AXB^{\intercal})=(B\otimes A)\vect(X)
for any symmetric matrix
X
, and thus we have

\vect(\sym(AX))=\phi(A)\vect(X).

From
\sym(AX)=-C
, we get

(75)

\displaystyle\phi(A)\vect(X)=-\vect(C).

Since
A
is negative definite diagonal matrix,
\phi(A)
is also a negative definite diagonal matrix and hence invertible with inverse given by

(\phi(A))^{-1}=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{t\phi(A)}dt=-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\otimes e^{\frac{t}{2}A}dt.

Thus, we have

\vect(X)=-(\phi(A))^{-1}\vect(C)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{\frac{t}{2}A}\otimes e^{\frac{t}{2}A})\vect(C)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\vect(e^{\frac{t}{2}A}Ce^{\frac{t}{2}A})dt

and

X=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{t}{2}A}Ce^{\frac{t}{2}A}dt.

In the general case, by Jordan decomposition,
A
is similar to an upper triangular matrix with diagonal entries having negative real parts. The exact proof goes through.

\blacksquare

6.2.
Proof of Theorem
1.13

First we note that
0\leqslant\gamma_{s}\leqslant 1,-1<\gamma_{s}^{\prime}\leqslant\gamma_{s}\leqslant 1
and thus
\rho(\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\leqslant\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda),\rho(\Gamma^{\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\leqslant\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda)
.
If
\beta^{2}<\beta_{c}^{2}=\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda)^{-1}
, it is easy to see that the matrices
\beta^{2}\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda-I,\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda-I
are stable. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\hat{U}(2)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\right)\Gamma\Lambda^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Lambda\Delta^{2}\Gamma)\right)dt+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\exp\left(-t(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\right)\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}dt+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})^{-1}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\Gamma(I-\beta^{2}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\Gamma)^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}.

In the second equality, we used the fact that
ABA^{T}=B^{1/2}(B^{-1/2}AB^{1/2})(B^{-1/2}AB^{1/2})^{T}B^{1/2}
with
A=\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\right)
and
B:=\Gamma\Lambda^{-1}
, a diagonal matrix with positive entries.

Similarly, we get

\displaystyle\hat{U}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\cdot\Gamma^{\prime}(I-\beta^{2}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\Gamma^{\prime})^{-1}\Lambda^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}

and

(76)

\displaystyle\hat{U}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\right)\sym\bigl(\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\cdot\hat{U}(1)\bigr)\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Lambda\Delta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime})\right)dt

(77)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\cdot\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime}\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\right)\Gamma^{\prime\prime}\Lambda^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{t}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Lambda\Delta^{2}\Gamma^{\prime})\right)dt+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}.

Finally, we use the fact that
N\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{R}}
converges to
0
entrywise as
N\to\infty
.
\blacksquare

7.
Uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
and Replica Symmetry Breaking

In this section, for
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
, we prove the AT line condition of the MSK model when
h>0
, beyond which the MSK model is in replica symmetry breaking phase. From classical literature of the SK model, we know that the uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
is essential to characterize the AT line condition. In the SK model, the uniqueness was proved using contraction argument for
\beta
small, and the Latala-Guerra Lemma
[
34
]
*Proposition 1.3.8 gives the uniqueness result for
h>0
. In the MSK model, we prove the uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
for small
\beta
by extending the contraction argument, but extending Latala-Guerra Lemma becomes more challenging, which is about analysis of a complicated nonlinear system (
15
). In
[
12
]
, they use an elementary approach to prove this for
positive-definite

2
-species model, but the idea seems difficult to be generalized. We first prove the uniqueness result for small
\beta
in Theorem
1.4
, then we prove Proposition
1.6
which gives the uniqueness result for
indefinite
2-species models when
h>0
.

7.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

We need to prove that the following system

(78)

\displaystyle q_{s}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}}+h),\qquad s=1,2,\ldots,m

has a unique solution where
\mathbf{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{m})^{\intercal}
.
We rewrite the equations in terms of
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m})^{\intercal}:=\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{q}
. Let
f(y):=\tanh^{2}(y)
. Then the system of equations (
78
) is equivalent to

(79)

\displaystyle x_{s}={\psi}_{s},\qquad s=1,2,\ldots,m

where

\psi_{s}:={\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{s}}\E f(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x})_{s}}+h).

We define

{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\ldots,\psi_{m})
.
It’s easy to compute the Jacobian matrix of the map
\Psi
,

J({\Psi})=\left(\!\!\!\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{s}}{\partial x_{t}}\right)\!\!\!\right)_{s,t=1}^{m}

where

\frac{\partial\psi_{s}}{\partial x_{t}}=\frac{\beta{\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{s}}\Delta_{s,t}^{2}{\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{t}}}{2\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x})_{s}}}\E\eta f^{\prime}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x})_{s}}+h)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}{\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{s}}\Delta_{st}^{2}{\lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}_{t}}\E f^{\prime\prime}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x})_{s}}+h)

and the last equality follows by Gaussian integration by parts. Now, note that

f^{\prime}(y)=2\cdot\frac{\tanh(y)}{\cosh^{2}(y)},\ f^{\prime\prime}(y)=2\cdot\frac{1-2\sinh^{2}(y)}{\cosh^{4}(y)}\in[-2,2].

In particular, we have

\displaystyle J({\Psi})=\beta^{2}A\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}

where
A=\diag(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m})
and
a_{s}:=\frac{1}{2}\E f^{\prime\prime}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{x})_{s}}+h)\in[-1,1]

for
s=1,2,\ldots,m
. Thus

\|J(\Psi)\|\leqslant\beta^{2}\|\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\|=\beta^{2}\rho(\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})=\beta^{2}\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda).

In particular,
\beta^{2}<\frac{1}{\rho(\Delta^{2}\Lambda)}
implies that
\|J({\Psi})\|<1
,
i.e.,

{\Psi}
is a contraction and the system (
78
) has a unique solution.
\blacksquare

Next, we present an elementary approach to prove Proposition
1.6
, which concerns the uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
for
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
with
m=2
.

7.2.
Proof of Proposition
1.6

We use an elementary approach to prove Proposition
1.6
. Recall
\mathbf{Q}=A\mathbf{q}
, where
A=\Delta^{2}\Lambda
. For
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
, we have
\det(A)<0
, then

A^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-a&b\\
c&-d\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{where}\quad a,b,c,d>0.

Then we can rewrite the equation,

\begin{cases}q_{1}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{Q^{1}}+h),\\
q_{2}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{Q^{2}}+h).\end{cases}

as

(80)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-aQ^{1}+bQ^{2}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{Q^{1}}+h),\\
cQ^{1}-dQ^{2}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{Q^{2}}+h).\end{cases}

Let
g_{1}(x):=\frac{1}{b}(\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{x}+h)/x+a)
and
g_{2}(x):=\frac{1}{c}(\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{x}+h)/x+d)
, by the classical Latala-Guerra lemma
[
34
]
*Appendix A.14,
g_{1}(x),g_{2}(x)
are both strictly decreasing on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The equation (
80
) can be rewritten further:

(81)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}Q^{1}=Q^{2}g_{2}(Q^{2}),\\
Q^{2}=Q^{1}g_{1}(Q^{1}).\end{cases}

From this, we get

g_{1}(Q^{1})g_{2}(Q^{2})=1,

then
Q^{2}=g_{2}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{g_{1}(Q^{1})}\right)
is a decreasing function of
Q^{1}
. On the other hand, taking cross product of equation (
81
), we have

(82)

\displaystyle(Q^{2})^{2}g_{2}(Q^{2})=(Q^{1})^{2}g_{1}(Q^{1}).

Let
h_{i}(x)=x^{2}g_{i}(x),i=1,2.
Next we show
h_{1},h_{2}
are both increasing functions on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Since

\displaystyle h_{1}(x)=x^{2}g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{x\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{x}+h)+ax^{2}}{b}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sqrt{x}}{b}\tanh^{2}(y)\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta^{2}}}e^{-\frac{(y-h)^{2}}{2\beta^{2}x}}dy+\frac{ax^{2}}{b},

where we expand the expectation part as a Gaussian integral. For this integral form, it’s easy to check
h_{1}(x)
is an strictly increasing function of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. Similarly, we can prove
h_{2}
is also strictly increasing. From (
82
), we have

h_{1}(Q^{1})=h_{2}(Q^{2}).

Thus
Q_{2}=h_{2}^{-1}\left(h_{1}(Q^{1})\right)
is strictly increasing as a function of
Q^{1}
. Recall
Q_{2}=g_{2}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{g_{1}(Q^{1})}\right)
is a decreasing function of
Q^{1}
. Therefore, we conclude the uniqueness of
Q^{1}
, similarly for
Q^{2}
. The proof is complete.
\blacksquare

In the following part, we will prove Theorem
1.15
. As we mentioned before, we assume
\Delta^{2}
is
positive-definite
because the Parisi formula is only known in that case. We further assume the uniqueness of
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
when
h>0
, in order for accurate and correct characterization of AT line condition.

Consider
\mathbf{p}\in[0,1]^{m}
with
p^{s}\geqslant q_{\star}^{s}
for all
s=1,2,\ldots,m
, define

(83)

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{p})=\frac{\partial\mathscr{P}_{\text{1RSB}}(\mathbf{q}_{\star},\mathbf{p},\zeta)}{\partial\zeta}\bigg|_{\zeta=1},

where
\mathscr{P}_{\text{1RSB}}
is Parisi functional of 1-step replica symmetry breaking. The explicit expression and related computations can be found in the appendix of
[
12
]
. The following Lemma gives some useful properties of
V(\mathbf{p})
.

Lemma 7.1

(
[
12
]
*Lemma 3.1)
.

Fix any
\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{q}_{\star}\in\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)
, The following identities hold:

(1)

V(\mathbf{q}_{\star})=0
.

(2)

\nabla V(\mathbf{q}_{\star})=0
.

(3)

HV(\mathbf{q}_{\star})=\beta^{2}\Lambda(\beta^{2}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\Gamma\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{2})\Lambda
, where
\Lambda,\Gamma
are diagonal matrices defined in Theorem
1.15
.

The following Corollary characterizes the RSB condition using
HV(\mathbf{q}_{\star})
in the above lemma.

Corollary 7.2

(
[
12
]
*Corollary 3.2)
.

Assume the Parisi formula (
7
), and that
\text{RS}(\beta,h)=\mathscr{P}_{\RS}(\mathbf{q}_{\star})
for some
\mathbf{q}_{\star}\in\mathcal{C}(\beta,h)
. If there exists a
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
with all nonnegative entries such that
\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}HV(\mathbf{q}_{\star})\mathbf{x}>0
, then

\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}<\RS(\beta,h).

Now we use the characterization of RSB in Corollary
7.2
to prove Theorem
1.15
.

7.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.15

Based on the definition, the matrix
\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}\Lambda^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
has all entries positive, by Perron-Frobenius theorem, we know that there exists an eigenvector
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
associated to the largest eigenvalue
\rho(\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)>0
such that
u_{s}>0
for
s=1,2,\ldots,m
. Without loss of generality, we assume
\mathbf{u}
is a unit vector,
i.e.,

\mathbf{u}^{\intercal}\mathbf{u}=1
. Take
\mathbf{x}=\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{u}
which has positive entries. Then

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\left(\Lambda(\beta^{2}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\Gamma\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{2})\Lambda\right)\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\intercal}\left[\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Lambda(\beta^{2}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\Gamma\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{2})\Lambda\Lambda^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Gamma^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right]\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}^{\intercal}\left(\beta^{2}[(\Lambda\Gamma)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}(\Lambda\Gamma)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}]^{2}\mathbf{u}-[(\Lambda\Gamma)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Delta^{2}(\Lambda\Gamma)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}]\mathbf{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho(\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)\left({\beta^{2}\rho(\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)-1}\right).

where the last step uses the fact that
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector associated with
\rho(\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)
and
\mathbf{u}^{\intercal}\mathbf{u}=1
. If
\beta^{2}>\frac{1}{\rho(\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)}
, we have

\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}\left(\Lambda(\beta^{2}\Delta^{2}\Lambda\Gamma\Delta^{2}-\Delta^{2})\Lambda\right)\mathbf{x}>0,

i.e.,

\mathbf{x}^{\intercal}HV(\mathbf{q}_{\star})\mathbf{x}>0
. By Corollary
7.2
, we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}<\RS(\beta,h),

and this completes the proof.
\blacksquare

8.
Discussions and Further Questions

In this section, we discuss some open questions spread out in the previous sections.

•

In Theorem
1.9
, the RS solution in
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
case, obtained using Guerra’s interpolation has the same expression as evaluating the Parisi functional
\mathscr{P}_{\text{RS}}
at
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
, while
\mathbf{q}_{\star}
is not a minimizer of
\mathscr{P}_{\text{RS}}
any more as in
positive-definite
case. This fact suggests that a modified Parisi formula as conjectured in
[
11
]
should be true at least in the RS regime. Our argument verifies this in part of the RS regime. The question is whether one can prove it in the whole RS regime while adapting Guerra’s interpolation?

•

For
positive-definite

\Delta^{2}
, when
h>0
, analyze the uniqueness of solution to the nonlinear system:

\displaystyle q_{s}=\E\tanh^{2}(\beta\eta\sqrt{(\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{q})_{s}+h},\quad\text{for}\quad s=1,2,\ldots,m.

As we mentioned, the uniqueness condition is essential to characterize AT line in Theorem
1.15
. In
[
12
]
, it was proved in the 2-species case, where one needs to identify the signs of matrix entries, but this is nearly impossible for general
m>2
. Similarly for
indefinite
case with
m>2
.

•

In Theorem
1.15
, we proved the RSB condition when
h>0
. In the case
h=0
, to prove RSB when
\beta>\beta_{c}
is still challenging. In that case,
\mathcal{C}(\beta,0)=\{0,\mathbf{q}_{\diamond}\}
,
\RS(\beta,0):=\mathscr{P}_{\RS}(\mathbf{q}_{\diamond})
achieves the minimum at
\mathbf{q}_{\diamond}
, to prove
\lim_{N\to\infty}F_{N}(\beta)<\RS(\beta,0)
is still not clear and depends on a good understanding of
\mathbf{q}_{\diamond}
.

•

Can one prove or disprove the Conjecture
1.17
? In this case, one also needs to understand the solution to (
15
) for
indefinite

\Delta^{2}
.

•

In Theorem
1.13
, we proved the asymptotic variance of overlap, then the next question will be a central limit theorem. In the classical SK model, Talagrand
[
34
]
proved it using the moment method, but adapting the idea for the MSK model seems hopeless because now we have overlap vectors and many computations involve matrices. One has to find a different method to prove it.

•

Note that, the function

\mathbf{X}(t):=\int_{0}^{-\ln(1-t)}e^{-\frac{s}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda)}\Gamma e^{-\frac{s}{2}(I-\beta^{2}\Lambda\Delta^{2}\Gamma)}ds,\ t\in[0,1]

is the unique solution of

\displaystyle(1-t)\mathbf{X}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\beta^{2}\cdot\sym(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}(0)\Delta^{2}\Lambda\mathbf{X}^{\prime}(t))\text{ with }\mathbf{X}(0)=0,\mathbf{X}^{\prime}(0)=\Gamma.

when
I-\beta^{2}\Gamma\Delta^{2}\Lambda
has all eigenvalues strictly positive. Is it possible to connect this equation with Guerra’s interpolation?

•

Finally, continuous Lyapunov equation arises in studying the variance-covariance matrices for OU-type SDE of the form
dX_{t}=AX_{t}dt+CdB_{t}
, where
A
is a stable matrix and
\lim_{t\to\infty}\var(X_{t})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{tA}CC^{\intercal}e^{tA^{\intercal}}dt
. It will be interesting to connect the appearance of continuous Lyapunov equation in Replica Symmetric MSK model with an appropriate SDE.
