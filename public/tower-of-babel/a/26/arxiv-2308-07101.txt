Small sunflowers and the structure of slice rank decompositions
Thomas Karam

Abstract
Let d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 be an integer. We show that whenever an order- d d tensor admits d + 1 d+1 decompositions according to Tao’s slice rank, if the linear subspaces spanned by their one-variable functions constitute a sunflower for each choice of special coordinate, then the tensor admits a decomposition where these linear subspaces are contained in the centers of these respective sunflowers. As an application, we deduce that for every nonnegative integer k k and every finite field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} there exists an integer C ⁡ ( d , k , | 𝔽 | ) C(d,k,|\mathbb{F}|) such that every order- d d tensor with slice rank k k over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} admits at most C ⁡ ( d , k , | 𝔽 | ) C(d,k,|\mathbb{F}|) decompositions with length k k , up to a class of transformations that can be easily described.

Introduction

Throughout we will use the following notations. If
k

will denote a field. All our statements will be uniform with respect to the choice of the field
\mathbb{F}

\mathbb{F}
is finite. Even in that latter case, the dependence will only be on the size of the field
\mathbb{F}

We will prove various statements involving order-

[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{d}]\to\mathbb{F}

n_{1},\dots,n_{d}

n_{1},\dots,n_{d}

Let
k
be a positive integer, and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field. If
M:[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\to\mathbb{F}
is a matrix with rank

as a sum of
k

1

A_{1}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}},A_{2}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{2}}
such that if

M(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(x)g_{i}(y)

is a decomposition of

, then
\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\rangle=A_{1}
and
\langle g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\rangle=A_{2}
. If
\mathbb{F}
is a finite field, then it is well-known that the number of decompositions of
M
, where two decompositions are viewed as the same if their
2k

(f_{1},\dots,f_{k},g_{1},\dots,g_{k})
are the same, is equal to the number

(|\mathbb{F}|^{k}-1)(|\mathbb{F}|^{k}-|\mathbb{F}|)\dots(|\mathbb{F}|^{k}-|\mathbb{F}|^{k-1})=|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-|\mathbb{F}|^{-i})

(1)

\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\rangle
. This number is between
\omega|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}}
and
|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}}
, where

\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-2^{-i})>0.

We note that even from a purely qualitative perspective, the analogous boundedness statement becomes false in general for decompositions of length even one greater than the rank of
M
. For instance, if
M(x,y)=f(x)g(y)
, then for any functions
f_{1},f_{2}:[n]\to\mathbb{F}
satisfying

, we have

M(x,y)=f_{2}(x)g(y)-f_{1}(x)g(y),

so as long as the function

is not fixed, the function

can be completely arbitrary.

Our main aim in the present paper is to obtain a comparable statement for the notion of slice rank on higher-order tensors. We have found the task of formulating an appropriate corresponding statement to already be challenging, which is why we shall state it precisely only in Section
3
, after going through several constructions guiding us towards its formulation and ruling out stronger ones in Section
2
. Informally speaking, we will prove that up to a natural class of transformations, the number of minimal-length slice rank decompositions of a tensor over a finite field is bounded above in a way that depends only on the order of the tensor, on its slice rank, and on the size of the field. As we will also discuss, the bound that we obtain cannot be too far from the optimal bound. Let us recall the definition of the slice rank and even before this, the definition of the tensor rank.

.

Let

be an integer, and let

We say that an expression such as (

, say that the

For every

Let

Before going further, let us recall the history of the slice rank and some of the ways in which it has been studied.

The slice rank was originally introduced by Tao
[
20
]
in 2016 as a reformulation of a central idea from the breakthrough of Croot, Lev and Pach
[
6
]
, which led to the solution to the cap-set problem by Ellenberg and Gijswijt
[
7
]
. Since then, the slice rank has been used successfully several times as a tool to solve combinatorial problems. For instance, it was also shown by Naslund and Sawin
[
17
]
that subsets of the cube
\{0,1\}^{n}
containing no
3
-sunflower are exponentially sparse, and further properties on the slice rank from Sawin and Tao
[
19
]
were later used by Sauermann
[
18
]
to obtain properties guaranteeing the existence of solutions with pairwise distinct variables to systems of equations in subsets of vector spaces over finite prime fields which are not exponentially sparse. In another direction, a variant of the slice rank, the partition rank, was defined by Naslund in
[
16
]
, originally to obtain polynomial upper bounds on problems on
k
-right corners, and was more recently used again by Naslund
[
15
]
, this time to obtain exponential lower bounds on the chromatic number of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with multiple forbidden distances.

Although all these works have primarily used the slice rank and the partition rank as tools, interest in studying them and their basic properties for their own sake has recently been building up. In the post of Sawin and Tao
[
19
]
, a characterisation of the slice rank in terms of coverings in the case of tensors supported inside an antichain had already been discussed, and this characterisation later played an important role in the work of Sauermann
[
18
]
that we previously mentioned. Later, it was proved by Gowers
[
8
]
that the slice rank of a direct sum of two tensors is the sum of their slice ranks.

As another example in this direction, the facts that a high-rank matrix must contain a high-rank submatrix of not too large size and that a matrix with high rank after every modification of the diagonal must have a high rank submatrix for which the sets of rows and columns are disjoint were extended by the author in
[
11
]
to a class of notions of rank containing the slice and partition ranks. Still on the topic of subtensors, it was shown by Briët and Castro-Silva
[
4
]
, again for a wide class of notions of rank, that a random subtensor of a high-rank subtensor must have high rank for some natural way of choosing the restriction at random, together with some analogous results on random restrictions of polynomials.

These three results found applications. The first, conditionally on better bounds, was discussed in
[
4
]
as a way to provide an alternate proof of some of the main results of that paper. The second was used by Gowers and the author
[
9
]
as a key stepping stone to extend to distributions inside
\{0,1\}^{n}
the result of Green and Tao
[
10
]
on the equidistribution of high-rank polynomials over finite prime fields. The third was applied (primarily in the case of polynomials, although the case of tensors was involved as well) by Briët, Buhrman, Castro-Silva, and Neumann
[
5
]
to prove limitations on the decoding of corrupted classical and quantum error-correcting codes with
\mathrm{NC}^{0}[\oplus]
circuits.

The slice and partition ranks have also been studied using more algebraic methods. For instance, a “universal” role that the partition rank plays among notions of rank has been explored by Bik, Draisma and Eggermont
[
3
]
. Another universality result was proved by Kazhdan and Ziegler
[
12
]
on the strength of polynomials (an analogue of the partition rank of tensors), and later led to an extension by Bik, Danelon, Draisma, and Eggermont
[
2
]
to the more abstract setting of arbitrary polynomial functors.

Let us finally mention that in the case of the tensor rank, much effort has gone into understanding the notion of identifiability of a tensor, which is much stronger than the mere boundedness of its set of minimal-length decompositions that we focus on (primarily in the case of the slice rank) in the present paper: if
k
is a nonnegative integer, then a tensor
T
with tensor rank
k
is said to be identifiable if there is only one way of writing it as a sum

T_{1}+\dots+T_{k},

up to permutations of the
k
tensors
T_{1},\dots,T_{k}
. This area of research, perhaps started by Kruskal
[
13
]
in 1977, is still very active. A recent paper
[
1
]
of Ballico, Bernardi and Santarsiero contains, among other interesting things, a wealth of references on identifiability.

2
Constructions of different minimal-length slice rank decompositions

We next go over five ways in which several minimal-length slice rank decompositions can arise for the same tensor.

Construction 1
.

Since it is possible to obtain new decompositions of matrices using a change of basis, it is possible to obtain new decompositions of
T
from an existing decomposition by rewriting the decompositions of any of the individual matrices
M_{1},\dots,M_{d}
while leaving the
d
-tuple
(M_{1},\dots,M_{d})
unchanged.

However, this
d
-tuple is not unique in general, and there are other ways of obtaining new decompositions, which brings us to the remaining four constructions.

Construction 2
.

To see that the matrices
M_{1},\dots,M_{d}
are not individually determined, consider any tensor
T
for which the slice rank and tensor rank are equal to some common positive integer
k
. Let

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{1,1}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,1}(x_{d})

be a tensor rank decomposition of
T
with length
k
. Then for every partition
\{I_{1},\dots,I_{d}\}
of
[k]
into
d
sets
I_{1},\dots,I_{d}
that are possibly empty, the decomposition

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i\in I_{j}}a_{j,i}(x_{i})(\prod_{j’\neq j}a_{j’,i}(x_{j’}))

is a slice-rank decomposition of
T
with length
k
.

These tensors exist, since for instance the “identity” tensor
I_{d,k}:[k]^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
defined by

I_{d,k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}1_{x_{1}=i}\dots 1_{x_{d}=i}

was shown by Tao (
[
20
]
, Lemma 1) to have slice rank
k
. Since the tensor rank of this tensor is at most
k
by definition, and since the slice rank is always at most the tensor rank, it follows that both ranks are equal for this tensor.

For instance, in the case
d=3
, we obtain a slice rank decomposition

I_{3,k}(x,y,z)=\sum_{i\in I_{1}}1_{x=i}1_{y=z=i}+\sum_{i\in I_{2}}1_{y=i}1_{x=z=i}+\sum_{i\in I_{3}}1_{z=i}1_{x=y=i}

for every tripartition
\{I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}\}
of
[k]
(again, with
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}
allowed to be empty).

As we can see from Construction
2
not even the sizes of the three parts of the decomposition are determined in general. One comment that can nonetheless be made about Construction
2
is that although the matrices
M_{1},\dots,M_{d}
are not always the same, the set of all summands is always the same set

\{1_{x_{1}=i}\dots 1_{x_{d}=i}:i\in[k]\}.

However, there is another class of examples where this usually does not hold, even up to changes of bases in the matrices
M_{1},\dots,M_{d}
, and which even encompasses typical tensors.

Construction 3
.

A tensor
T:[k]^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
can in particular be written in
d
different ways as a sum of its
(d-1)
-variable slices of each type, i.e. be decomposed as

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}1_{x_{j}=i}T(x_{1},\dots,x_{j-1},i,x_{j+1},\dots,x_{d}).

If
T
has slice rank
k
, then these
d
decompositions are each minimal-length decompositions. If the field
\mathbb{F}
is finite, then the number of tensors
[k]^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
with slice rank at most
k-1
is at most

k^{d}|\mathbb{F}|^{(k-1)(k^{d-1}+k)}=k^{d}|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{d}-k^{d-1}+k^{2}-k}.

Provided that
d\geq 3
, we can crudely bound this number above by

(k^{d}/|\mathbb{F}|^{k})|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{d}}.

The ratio is less than
1
for
k
large enough, and tends to
0
as
k
tends to infinity, which shows that random tensors have slice rank
k
. Unlike in the
I_{d,k}
example, the sets of
k
summands involved in each of the
d
decompositions are in particular usually very different.

Construction 4
.

Provided that a tensor
T
with slice rank
k
does have a decomposition with length
k
that does not have all its terms of the same of the three types, there is a systematic way of changing some of the components of the decomposition and obtain another decomposition with length
k
. In the
d=3
case, for instance, if

T(x,y,z)=a(x)b(y,z)+c(y)d(x,z),

then for any function
e:[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F}
, the functions
b^{\prime}=b+c\otimes e
and
d^{\prime}=d-a\otimes e
satisfy

T(x,y,z)=a(x)b^{\prime}(y,z)+c(y)d^{\prime}(x,z).

Because the function
e
is arbitrary, and in particular there are unboundedly many such functions as
n
tends to infinity, it is never true, for any integers
d\geq 3
,
k\geq 2
and any field
\mathbb{F}
, that every order-
d
tensor with slice rank
k
has a number of minimal-length decompositions which is bounded above by some function of
d,k,\mathbb{F}
. A boundedness statement of this kind may only hold up to this type of transformation.

If
T
is an order-
3
tensor with a more general slice rank decomposition

T(x,y,z)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}(x)b_{i}(y,z)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{j}(x)d_{j}(y,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t}e_{k}(z)f_{k}(x,y)

(4)

is a decomposition of
T
, then whenever
(i,j)\in[r]\times[s]
and

is a function, replacing

and

by respectively

b_{i}+c_{j}\otimes e\text{ and }d_{j}-a_{i}\otimes e

leads to a new decomposition of

.

Returning to the case of general

, if

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=a_{1}(x_{1})b_{1}(\overline{x_{1}})+\dots+a_{d}(x_{d})b_{d}(\overline{x_{d}})

is a slice rank decomposition of
T
, then for any function

, replacing

and

by respectively

b_{1}+a_{2}\otimes c\text{ and }b_{2}-a_{1}\otimes c

leads to a new decomposition of

.

Combining both axes of generality, if

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}a_{j,i}(x_{j})b_{j,i}(\overline{x_{j}})

is a slice rank decomposition of

,

is a function, and

,

are two indices, then replacing

by respectively

b_{1,i_{1}}+a_{2,i_{2}}\otimes c\text{ and }b_{2,i_{2}}-a_{1,i_{1}}\otimes c

leads to a new decomposition of
T
. The roles of either of the first two coordinates may of course be exchanged with those of the other coordinates.

\displaystyle d_{j}

3
Statements of main results

The previous section was devoted to constructions illustrating how a variety of different slice rank decompositions may arise for the same tensor. In the present section we describe our main results, which will be in the converse direction and show that to some extent, the set of minimal-length slice rank decompositions of the same tensor cannot be too rich. We will do so separately for the
(d-1)
-variable functions and for the one-variable functions of minimal-length decompositions. We will go through the following three steps, corresponding respectively to the proofs of the three main results of this paper, Proposition
3.2
, Theorem
3.3
, and Theorem
3.5
.

1.

We will begin by showing that if two decompositions have the same one-variable functions, then any differences between the two decompositions arise from Construction
4
and Construction
5
.

2.

After this, we will show a statement which in particular implies that no more than
d
decompositions can have their sets of one-variable functions in the
j
th coordinate be jointly linearly independent for every
j\in[d]
simultaneously.

3.

This result will allow us to deduce that, in the finite field case, there are only a bounded number of possibilities for the one-variable functions, with a bound depending only on the order of the tensor, on its slice rank, and on the size of the finite field.

In summary, we can partition the decompositions according to their one-variable functions in a bounded number of sets, and inside each of these sets we can then completely describe the possibilities for the
(d-1)
-variable functions once we know what these are for one of the decompositions from the set.

It may seem slightly surprising that we begin with and use fewer steps to obtain a satisfactory result with the
(d-1)
-variable functions than we do with the one-variable functions. That this is the case is due to the fact that whenever we compare two decompositions in the part of our argument studying the former, we always assume that the one-variable functions are the same, whereas while studying the latter we do not make any assumption on the
(d-1)
-variable functions.

Let us now go through these steps more formally. Although the following definition is a bit long, all that it describes is differences between the original and the new
(d-1)
-variable functions before and after successively applying Construction
4
and Construction

as much as can be done. If

is a subset of

, then for simplicity of notation we will denote a sequence

by

.

Let

be an integer, and let

be nonnegative integers. We say that a decomposition

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}a_{i}(x_{j})b_{i}(\overline{x_{j}})

(instead taken to be elements of

if

) for every subset

with

, every

For every

For every

Given this definition, the first component of our results can be easily formulated. It can be checked that any decomposition that is in zero form adds up to the zero tensor, and our first result is a converse of that. We assume as usual that the functions

of decompositions are linearly independent for each

Proposition 3.2

Let

be an integer, and let

be nonnegative integers. If a decomposition

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}a_{j,i}(x_{j})b_{j,i}(\overline{x_{j}})

The next theorem corresponds to the second step of the argument that we described at the beginning of this section. The key structure that we will use is that of sunflowers in a linear algebra sense: if

What we will show is that if we can find

\theta\in\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa})}
. If
\kappa<k
, then let
M_{\kappa+1}
be a maximal subset of
\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa})}
such that the linear subspaces
A_{j}^{\theta}
with
\theta\in M_{\kappa+1}
have pairwise intersection
W_{j}
for every
j\in[d]
. Theorem
3.3
then shows that
M_{\kappa+1}
has size at most
d
(as otherwise we would have
\sr T\leq\kappa)
, and we can hence write

\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa})}=\bigcup_{1\leq j_{\kappa+1}\leq d}\bigcup_{w_{\kappa+1}}\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa}),(j_{\kappa+1},w_{\kappa+1})},

(8)

where the union over
w_{\kappa+1}
is over all vectors of

(+_{\theta\in M_{\kappa+1}}A_{j_{\kappa+1}}^{\theta})\setminus W_{j_{\kappa+1}}.

We then take the sets
\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa}),(j_{\kappa+1},w_{\kappa+1})}
to be the immediate descendants of the set
\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa})}
in the tree.

If instead
\kappa=k
then
\dim W_{1}+\dots+\dim W_{d}
and
\dim A_{1}^{\theta}+\dots+\dim A_{d}^{\theta}
are both equal to
k
, so are equal to one another. Hence, once we reach depth
k
the linear subspaces
A_{1}^{\theta},\dots,A_{d}^{\theta}
are all completely determined by
W_{1},\dots,W_{d}
. By our initial assumption that for any distinct
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in[h]
we have
A_{j}^{\theta}\neq A_{j}^{\theta^{\prime}}
for some
j\in[d]
, the sets
\Theta_{(j_{1},w_{1}),(j_{2},w_{2}),\dots,(j_{\kappa},w_{\kappa})}
each have size at most
1
.

We have obtained a tree with depth
k
, and each node of the tree has at most
d|\mathbb{F}|^{dk}
immediate descendants, so the number of leaves, and hence of
d
-tuples of linear subspaces
(A_{1}^{\theta},\dots,A_{d}^{\theta})
is at most
(d|\mathbb{F}|^{dk})^{k}=d^{k}|\mathbb{F}|^{dk^{2}}
. Furthermore, for each choice of a
d
-tuple of subspaces
(A_{1}^{\theta},\dots,A_{d}^{\theta})
with respective dimensions
r_{1},\dots,r_{d}
, by the number (
1
) of decompositions of matrices there are at most

|\mathbb{F}|^{dr_{1}^{2}}\dots|\mathbb{F}|^{dr_{d}^{2}}\leq|\mathbb{F}|^{dk^{2}}

choices for

((a_{1,1},\dots,a_{1,r_{1}}),\dots,(a_{d,1},\dots,a_{d,r_{d}})).

This finishes the proof of the first item of Theorem
3.5
. ∎

5
Simpler analogue for tensor rank decompositions

In this section we prove a simpler analogue of Theorem
3.5
in the case of the tensor rank, which is much more similar to the statement for matrices that we discussed in the introduction.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer, let
k
be a nonnegative integer, and let
\mathbb{F}
be a field. If
T:[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{d}]\to\mathbb{F}
is an order-
d
tensor with tensor rank
k
, then there exist linear subspaces
A_{1}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}},\dots,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{d}}
such that if

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{1,i}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i}(x_{d})

(9)

is a tensor rank decomposition of
T
with length
k
, then we have

A_{1}=\langle a_{1,1},\dots,a_{1,k}\rangle,\dots,A_{d}=\langle a_{d,1},\dots,a_{d,k}\rangle.

In particular, if the field
\mathbb{F}
is finite, then the number of possible tensor rank decompositions of
T
with length
k
is at most
|\mathbb{F}|^{(d-1)k^{2}}
.

Proof.

Assume that

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{1,i}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i}(x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{1,i}^{\prime}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i}^{\prime}(x_{d})

are two decompositions of
T
with length
k
. To prove the first conclusion, it suffices to show that for each
j\in[d]
the linear subspaces
\langle a_{j,1},\dots,a_{j,k}\rangle
and
\langle a_{j,1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{j,k}^{\prime}\rangle
are the same. Assume for contradiction that they are not. Then without loss of generality the linear subspace
\langle a_{1,1},\dots,a_{1,k}\rangle
is not contained in the linear subspace
\langle a_{1,1}^{\prime},\dots,a_{1,k}^{\prime}\rangle
, so there exists
u:[n_{1}]\to\mathbb{F}
such that
(u.a_{1,1}^{\prime},\dots,u.a_{1,k}^{\prime})=0
but
(u.a_{1,1},\dots,u.a_{1,k})\neq 0
. Applying
u
to both decompositions of
T
we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(u.a_{1,i})a_{2,i}(x_{2})\dots a_{d,i}(x_{d})=0,

so the products
a_{2,i}\otimes\dots\otimes a_{d,i}
with
i\in[k]
are linearly dependent. Assuming without loss of generality that the product
a_{2,k}\otimes\dots\otimes a_{d,k}
is a linear combination of the products
a_{2,i}\otimes\dots\otimes a_{d,i}
with
i\in[k-1]
, we can hence write

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\alpha_{1,i}(x_{1})a_{2,i}(x_{2})\dots a_{d,i}(x_{d})

for some new functions
\alpha_{1,1},\dots,\alpha_{1,k-1}:[n_{1}]\to\mathbb{F}
. This is a tensor rank decomposition of
T
with length
k-1
, which contradicts that
T
has tensor rank
k
.

If the field
\mathbb{F}
is finite, then for each of the
k(d-1)
functions
a_{j,i}
with
j\in\{2,\dots,d\}
and
i\in[k]
involved in a given decomposition of
T
, there are at most
|A_{j}|\leq|\mathbb{F}|^{k}
possibilities. Once these functions are fixed, the linear independence of the products

a_{2,i}\otimes\dots\otimes a_{d,i}

with
i\in[k]
, shows that the functions
a_{1,1},\dots,a_{1,k}
are determined. The bound follows. ∎

Although the upper bound that we obtain specialises in the case
d=2
to the upper bound obtained at the start of the introduction, the number of tensor rank decompositions can be much less than that for general
d
. Indeed if an order-
d
tensor
T
with tensor rank
k
is identifiable in the sense that we mentioned in the introduction, then it can be written in at most
k!
ways as a sum of
k
tensors with tensor rank
1
(those being the same, up to permutation); as in turn there are
(|\mathbb{F}|-1)^{d-1}
ways of rewriting a tensor with tensor rank
1
as a product of
d
functions, such as tensor has at most
k!(|\mathbb{F}|-1)^{d-1}
choices for the number of possible tensor rank decompositions in our sense. Nonetheless, the following example shows that the behaviour from the
d=2
case for
k
large can carry over to tensors of higher order, and that the exponent in the bound from Proposition
5.1
cannot be improved by a factor greater than
d
in general. We recall the absolute constant
\omega=\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}(1-2^{-i})>0
defined at the start of the introduction.

Example 5.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer, and let
k
be a nonnegative integer. Let
T
be the order-
d
tensor defined by

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=M(x_{1},x_{2})a_{3}(x_{3})\dots a_{d}(x_{d})

(10)

for some matrix
M:[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\to\mathbb{F}
with rank
k
and some non-zero functions
a_{3}:[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F},\dots,a_{d}:[n_{d}]\to\mathbb{F}
. Then
T
has tensor rank equal to
k
, and the number of tensor rank decompositions of length
k
of
T
is exactly

(|\mathbb{F}|-1)^{(d-2)k}|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-|\mathbb{F}|^{-i}),

which is between
\omega|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}}
and
|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}+(d-2)k}
.

Proof.

In one direction, writing a rank decomposition of the matrix
M
with length
k
and plugging it in (
10
) shows
\tr T\leq k
. In the other direction, if

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}a_{1,i}(x_{1})a_{2,i}(x_{2})a_{3,i}(x_{3})\dots a_{d,i}(x_{d})

(11)

is a tensor rank decomposition of
T
for some integer
k^{\prime}
, then letting
a_{3}^{*}:[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F},\dots,a_{d}^{*}:[n_{d}]\to\mathbb{F}
such that
a_{j}^{*}.a_{j}=1
for each
j\in\{3,\dots,d\}
and applying
a_{3}^{*},\dots,a_{d}^{*}
to both decompositions (
10
) and (
11
) leads to

M(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}C_{i}a_{1,i}(x_{1})a_{2,i}(x_{2})

for some coefficients
C_{i}\in\mathbb{F}
, so
k^{\prime}\geq\rk M
and hence
\tr T\geq k
.

The decomposition (
10
) shows that the linear subspaces
A_{3},\dots,A_{d}
of Proposition
5.1
each have dimension
1
, and there are hence at most
(|\mathbb{F}|-1)^{(d-2)k}
choices for

((a_{3,1},\dots,a_{3,k}),\dots,(a_{d,1},\dots,a_{d,k}))

(12)

since if one of these functions were zero, then this would contradict
\tr T=k
. Once this choice is made, there are

|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{2}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(1-|\mathbb{F}|^{-i})

choices for
(a_{2,1},\dots,a_{2,k})
, and then
(a_{1,1},\dots,a_{1,k})
is completely determined. Conversely, for any given choice of (
12
) such that
a_{j,i}\in A_{j}\setminus\{0\}
for every
j\in\{3,\dots,d\}
and
i\in[k]
, the decomposition (
9
) becomes

\sum_{i=1}^{k}D_{i}a_{1,i}(x_{1})a_{2,i}(x_{2})a_{3}(x_{3})\dots a_{d}(x_{d})

for some
D_{1},\dots,D_{k}\in\mathbb{F}\setminus\{0\}
, so whenever
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}:[n_{1}]\to\mathbb{F}
,
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}:[n_{2}]\to\mathbb{F}
provide a decomposition

M(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}(x_{1})g_{i}(x_{2})

of
M
with length
k
, taking
a_{1,i}=f_{i}
and
a_{2,i}=g_{i}/D_{i}
for every
i\in[k]
provides a decomposition of
T
with length
k
. ∎

6
Proof of the theorem on sunflowers for order-
3
tensors

The main goal of this section is to prove Proposition
3.2
and Theorem
3.3
in the case of order-
3
tensors, and discuss the main ideas behind the proofs as well as how they were discovered.

The statement of the next lemma is similar to Lemma 9 from
[
8
]
. The proof that we give is slightly different from the proof in that paper, but they are based on the same idea.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
F_{A}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}},F_{C}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{2}}
both be families of linearly independent vectors, and let
E
be a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}^{n_{3}}
. For each
(a,c)\in F_{A}\times F_{C}
, let
p_{a,c}
be an element of
\mathbb{F}^{n_{3}}
. Assume that the linear combination

\sum_{a\in F_{A},c\in F_{C}}a\otimes c\otimes p_{a,c}

belongs to
\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{n_{2}}\otimes E
. Then
p_{a,c}\in E
for every
(a,c)\in F_{A}\times F_{C}
.

Proof.

We define dual families
F_{A}^{*}
,
F_{C}^{*}
to the families
F_{A}
,
F_{C}
respectively. The assumption states that we can write

\sum_{a\in A,c\in C}a\otimes c\otimes p_{a,c}=\sum_{i\in[n_{1}]}\sum_{j\in[n_{2}]}\tau_{i,1}\otimes\tau_{j,2}\otimes e_{i,j}

where the vectors
\tau_{i,1},\tau_{j,2}
are respectively the canonical basis vectors of
\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}}
and
\mathbb{F}^{n_{2}}
respectively, and
e_{i,j}\in E
for every
(i,j)\in[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]
. For any given pair
(a,c)\in F_{A}\times F_{C}
, applying
a^{*}\otimes c^{*}
provides

p_{a,c}=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}a^{*}(i)c^{*}(j)e_{i,j}.

This establishes the lemma. ∎

We first prove Proposition
3.2
in the
d=3
case. It can be restated as follows, in a way that highlights the successive functions that will appear in the proof. As much of the proof is similar to that of Lemma 10 from
[
8
]
and to the discussion between that lemma and Corollary 11 there, we have decided to re-use the same notations as those of that paper.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
r,s,t
be nonnegative integers. Assume that we have the decomposition

\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}(x)b_{i}(y,z)+\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{j}(y)d_{j}(x,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t}e_{k}(z)f_{k}(x,y)

(13)

of the order-
3

[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F}
and that the families

(a_{i}:i\in[r]),(c_{j}:j\in[s]),(e_{k}:k\in[t])

are each linearly independent. Then we may write

\displaystyle b_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}c_{j}\otimes p_{i,j}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}q_{i,k}\otimes e_{k}

(14)

\displaystyle d_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\otimes g_{i,j}+\sum_{k=1}^{t}h_{j,k}\otimes e_{k}

(15)

\displaystyle f_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}a_{i}\otimes u_{i,k}+\sum_{j=1}^{s}v_{j,k}\otimes c_{j}

(16)

for each
i\in[r],j\in[s],k\in[t]
respectively, where
h_{j,k},v_{j,k}:[n_{1}]\to\mathbb{F}
,
q_{i,k},u_{i,k}:[n_{2}]\to\mathbb{F}
,
p_{i,j},g_{i,j}:[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F}
are some functions satisfying

\displaystyle p_{i,j}+g_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{t}\lambda_{i,j,k}e_{k}

(17)

\displaystyle q_{i,k}+u_{i,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s}\mu_{i,j,k}c_{j}

(18)

\displaystyle h_{j,k}+v_{j,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\nu_{i,j,k}a_{i}

(19)

for each
(i,j)\in[r]\times[s]
, for each
(i,k)\in[r]\times[t]
, and for each
(j,k)\in[s]\times[t]
respectively, with
\lambda_{i,j,k},\mu_{i,j,k},\nu_{i,j,k}\in\mathbb{F}
such that

\lambda_{i,j,k}+\mu_{i,j,k}+\nu_{i,j,k}=0

(20)

for each
(i,j,k)\in[r]\times[s]\times[t]
.

Proof.

Applying dual functions
a_{i}^{*},c_{j}^{*},e_{k}^{*}
we get the expressions (
14
), (
15
), (
16
). Substituting in (
13
) and applying Lemma
6.1
shows that

\displaystyle p_{i,j}+g_{i,j}

\displaystyle\in\langle e_{k}:k\in[t]\rangle

\displaystyle q_{i,k}+u_{i,k}

\displaystyle\in\langle c_{j}:j\in[s]\rangle

\displaystyle h_{j,k}+v_{j,k}

\displaystyle\in\langle a_{i}:i\in[r]\rangle

respectively for each
(i,j)\in[r]\times[s]
, for each
(i,k)\in[r]\times[t]
, and for each
(j,k)\in[s]\times[t]
, so can write these functions as in (
17
), (
18
), (
19
). Substituting in (

) we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\sum_{k=1}^{t}(\lambda_{i,j,k}+\mu_{i,j,k}+\nu_{i,j,k})a_{i}\otimes c_{j}\otimes e_{k}=0.

Because the products
a_{i}\otimes c_{j}\otimes e_{k}
with
(i,j,k)\in[r]\times[s]\times[t]
are linearly independent, we conclude (
20
). ∎

Now turning to the one-variable functions, we can begin by asking for the weakest possible property asserting some lack of independence between the one-variable functions involved in several decompositions. As the strongest form of independence is to have, for every fixed
j\in[d]
, the linear subspaces
A_{j}^{\theta}
spanned by the functions
a_{j,i}^{\theta}
of the decompositions (indexed by
\theta
) all be in direct sum, we aim to show that this can only occur for a bounded number of decompositions.

As formulated this last goal is not yet completely reasonable, as shown by the fact that the zero tensor can be decomposed as

a(x)(bc)(y,z)-b(y)(ac)(x,z)

for all
a:[n_{1}]\to\mathbb{F}
,
b:[n_{2}]\to\mathbb{F}
,
c:[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F}
(or even more simply, by repeating the length-
0
decomposition of the zero tensor arbitrarily many times). What we will rather show is that the zero tensor is the only order-
3
tensor for which there exist four decompositions where for each of the three choices of special coordinate the one-variable functions may all be in direct sum.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
T:[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F}
be an order-3 tensor. Assume that for every
\theta\in[4]
we have a decomposition

T(x,y,z)=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\theta}}a_{i}^{\theta}(x)b_{i}^{\theta}(y,z)+\sum_{j=1}^{s_{\theta}}c_{j}^{\theta}(y)d_{j}^{\theta}(x,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{\theta}}e_{k}^{\theta}(z)f_{k}^{\theta}(x,y)

(21)

for some nonnegative integers
r_{\theta},s_{\theta},t_{\theta}
. Assume that the families

(a_{i}^{\theta}:\theta\in[4],i\in[r_{\theta}])\text{, }(c_{j}^{\theta}:\theta\in[4],j\in[s_{\theta}])\text{, }(e_{k}^{\theta}:\theta\in[4],k\in[t_{\theta}])

(22)

are each linearly independent. Then
T=0
.

Let us briefly outline the proof before writing it formally. Writing an equality between two decompositions of
T
(indexed by, say,
1
and
2
) and applying the dual functions then allows us to write any given two-variable function of the first decomposition (such as, say,
b_{1}^{1}
) as a sum of products of two one-variable functions, one of which, of one of the types
c_{j}^{1}
,
c_{j}^{2}
,
e_{k}^{1}
,
e_{k}^{2}
, is specified and may come from either of the two decompositions, and the other of which is unspecified. We can write similar expressions of
b_{1}^{1}
where the third and fourth decompositions supply the one-variable functions that the second decomposition previously supplied, which provides two other decompositions of
b_{1}^{1}
, after which it is only moderately difficult to show that
b_{1}^{1}
has a decomposition of the kind that we have just described, where all specified one-variable functions are of the types
c_{j}^{1}
and
e_{k}^{1}
. In other words we show that for every
\theta\in[4]
the two-variable functions
b_{i}^{\theta},d_{j}^{\theta},f_{k}^{\theta}
have decompositions as sums of products of two functions where at least one function of each product is of the type
a_{i}^{\theta},c_{j}^{\theta},e_{k}^{\theta}
for that same value of
\theta
. This is the statement of Lemma
6.4
, and is a property which is strong enough to allow us to conclude that
T=0
once we apply it to two different values of
\theta
.

For every decomposition
\theta
we respectively write
A^{\theta},C^{\theta},E^{\theta}
for the linear subspaces

\langle a_{i}^{\theta}:i\in[r_{\theta}]\rangle\text{, }\langle c_{j}^{\theta}:j\in[s_{\theta}]\rangle\text{, }\langle e_{k}^{\theta}:k\in[t_{\theta}]\rangle.

We begin with a lemma which informally says that under the assumptions of Proposition
6.3
, the two-variable functions of every decomposition
\theta
can themselves be further decomposed as sums of products of two one-variable functions, where in every product one of the functions is a one-variable function from that same decomposition
\theta
of the original tensor. So far for we have used notations such as
a_{i}^{\theta}
,
b_{i}^{\theta}
for functions arising from a decomposition
\theta
. As will now write equalities between decompositions, and use them to obtain expressions for the functions
b_{i}^{\theta}
, our calculations will involve terms that may depend either only on one decomposition or simultaneously on both of the decompositions between which we wrote the original equality. In the first case these terms will be denoted for instance by
p_{i,j}^{\theta}
to indicate that they only depend on the decomposition
\theta
, and the second case they will instead be written for instance as
p_{i,j}^{\theta\theta^{\prime}}
to indicate that they depend on both decompositions
\theta
,
\theta^{\prime}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
T:[n_{1}]\times[n_{2}]\times[n_{3}]\to\mathbb{F}
be an order-
3
tensor. Under the assumptions of Proposition
6.3
, for each
\theta\in[4]
we can write decompositions of the type

\displaystyle b_{i}^{\theta}(y,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{\theta}}c_{j}^{\theta}(y)p_{i,j}^{\theta}(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{\theta}}q_{i,k}^{\theta}(y)e_{k}^{\theta}(z)

(23)

\displaystyle d_{j}^{\theta}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\theta}}a_{i}^{\theta}(x)g_{i,j}^{\theta}(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{\theta}}h_{j,k}^{\theta}(x)e_{k}^{\theta}(z)

(24)

\displaystyle f_{k}^{\theta}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\theta}}a_{i}^{\theta}(x)u_{i,k}^{\theta}(y)+\sum_{j=1}^{s_{\theta}}v_{j,k}^{\theta}(x)c_{j}^{\theta}(y).

(25)

Proof.

We choose dual families
(a_{i}^{\theta*})
,
(c_{j}^{\theta*})
,
(e_{k}^{\theta*})
to the respective (linearly independent) families (
22
). We begin with the equality

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r_{1}}a_{i}^{1}(x)b_{i}^{1}(y,z)+\sum_{j=1}^{s_{1}}c_{j}^{1}(y)d_{j}^{1}(x,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{1}}e_{k}^{1}(z)f_{j}^{1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r_{2}}a_{i}^{2}(x)b_{i}^{2}(y,z)+\sum_{j=1}^{s_{2}}c_{j}^{2}(y)d_{j}^{2}(x,z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{2}}e_{k}^{2}(z)f_{j}^{2}(x,y).

Applying the function
a_{i}^{1*}
we get a decomposition of the type

b_{i}^{1}(y,z)=\sum_{j=1}^{s_{1}}c_{j}^{1}(y)p_{i,j}^{1}(z)-\sum_{j=1}^{s_{2}}c_{j}^{2}(y)p_{i,j}^{12}(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{1}}q_{i,k}^{1}(y)e_{k}^{1}(z)-\sum_{k=1}^{t_{2}}q_{i,k}^{12}(y)e_{k}^{2}(z).

(26)

Here
2
could instead have been taken to be an arbitrary element of
\{2,3,4\}
, so if we write the previous identity for the pair
(1,3)
rather than for the pair
(1,2)
, and then subtract it from the identity for the pair
(1,2)
then we get

\sum_{j=1}^{s_{3}}c_{j}^{3}(y)p_{i,j}^{13}(z)-\sum_{j=1}^{s_{2}}c_{j}^{2}(y)p_{i,j}^{12}(z)+\sum_{k=1}^{t_{3}}q_{i,k}^{13}(y)e_{k}^{3}(z)-\sum_{k=1}^{t_{2}}q_{i,k}^{12}(y)e_{k}^{2}(z)=0.

Applying the dual functions
c_{j}^{2*}
then shows that

p_{i,j}^{12}\in E^{2}\oplus E^{3}

for each
j\in[s_{2}]
. This argument also shows (by replacing
3
by
4
) that

In this section we show Proposition

for general

and Proposition

respectively. Whenever

for the set of

In order to avoid excessively heavy notation we will for instance for a linear subspace

However, this should not cause any confusion to the reader, because the dimension (in

We begin by proving Proposition

[

]
. Throughout the process, we collect terms together whenever they are of the same type, which may involve cancellations, but as these are all of the kind allowed in Definition
3.1

Proof of Proposition

.

For every

Whenever a term does not have any function of the type
a_{j,i}^{0}
as a factor, its product of specified one-variable functions is of one of the types

\otimes_{1\leq t\leq m}a_{j_{t},i_{t}}^{1}\text{, }\otimes_{1\leq t\leq m}a_{j_{t},i_{t}}^{2},

and it must come from the left-hand side and from the right-hand side respectively in these cases.

(iv)

In the situation of terms described in the previous item, the remaining unspecified
(d-m)
-variable functions depend only on the decompositions
1
and
2
respectively.

In particular, after
d-2
steps we have an equality between two decompositions of
T-S_{d-2}
, that obeys the properties above for
m=d-1
. This equality is of the type

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\in\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}[r_{j^{\prime}}^{1}]}\left(\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}a_{j^{\prime},i_{j^{\prime}}}^{1}(x_{j^{\prime}})\right)p_{j,i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}}^{1}(x_{j})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{\beta_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\in\{0,1,2\}^{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\setminus\{1,2\}^{[d]\setminus\{j\}}}\sum_{i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\in\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}[r_{j^{\prime}}^{\beta_{j^{\prime}}}]}\left(\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}a_{j^{\prime},i_{j^{\prime}}}^{\beta_{j^{\prime}}}(x_{j^{\prime}})\right)p_{j,i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}},\beta_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}}^{12}(x_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\in\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}[r_{j^{\prime}}^{2}]}\left(\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}a_{j^{\prime},i_{j^{\prime}}}^{2}(x_{j^{\prime}})\right)p_{j,i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}}^{2}(x_{j})

where we have written
i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}
for
(i_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\in[d]\setminus\{j\}}
and
\beta_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}
for
(\beta_{j^{\prime}})_{j^{\prime}\in[d]\setminus\{j\}}
.

Each term is now a product of
d
one-variable functions, where
d-1
of them are specified functions of the type
a_{j,i}^{\beta}
and the last one is an unspecified function. Applying one last time the products of the
d-1
one-variable specified functions shows that the unspecified function is a linear combination of functions of the type
a_{j,i}^{\beta}
(for the relevant value of
j
). After yet again gathering terms, we obtain some tensor
S_{d-1}\in R_{\mathrm{target}}
and an equality between two decompositions of
T-S_{d-1}
of the type

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}^{1}],\dots,i_{d}\in[r_{d}^{1}]}\lambda_{(1,i_{1},1),\dots,(d,i_{d},1)}a_{1,i_{1}}^{1}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i_{d}}^{1}(x_{d})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\in\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}[r_{j^{\prime}}^{1}]}\lambda_{(1,i_{1},1),\dots,(j-1,i_{j-1},1),(j,i_{j},2),(j+1,i_{j+1},1),\dots,(d,i_{d},1)}\left(\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}a_{j^{\prime},i_{j^{\prime}}}^{1}(x_{j^{\prime}})\right)a_{j,i_{j}}^{2}(x_{j})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in\{0,1,2\}^{d}\setminus\{1,2\}^{d}}\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}^{\beta_{1}}],\dots,i_{d}\in[r_{d}^{\beta_{d}}]}\lambda_{(1,i_{1},\beta_{1}),\dots,(d,i_{d},\beta_{d})}a_{1,i_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i_{d}}^{\beta_{d}}(x_{d})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i_{[d]\setminus\{j\}}\in\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}[r_{j^{\prime}}^{2}]}\lambda_{(1,i_{1},2),\dots,(j-1,i_{j-1},2),(j,i_{j},1),(j+1,i_{j+1},2),\dots,(d,i_{d},2)}\left(\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}a_{j^{\prime},i_{j^{\prime}}}^{2}(x_{j^{\prime}})\right)a_{j,i_{j}}^{1}(x_{j})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{1}\in[r_{1}^{2}],\dots,i_{d}\in[r_{d}^{2}]}\lambda_{(1,i_{1},2),\dots,(d,i_{d},2)}a_{1,i_{1}}^{2}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i_{d}}^{2}(x_{d})

(48)

for some
\lambda_{(1,i_{1},\beta_{1}),\dots,(d,i_{d},\beta_{d})}\in\mathbb{F}
. In this equality, all terms on both sides are multiples of products of the type

a_{1,i_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes a_{d,i_{d}}^{\beta_{d}}.

(49)

If a term has
\beta_{j}\neq 0
for every
j\in[d]
, then it has either at least
d-1
or at most one index equal to
1
(and the remaining ones are equal to
2
), and in these two cases it necessarily must come from the left-hand and right-hand side respectively, so the sets of values of
(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})
from the terms appearing in both sides are disjoint. Because the products (
49
) are linearly independent, it follows that either side of (
48
) is equal to
0
. These sides are equal to
T-S_{d-1}
, so we have shown
T=S_{d-1}
. Therefore,
T
belongs to
R_{\mathrm{target}}
. In particular it has the desired decomposition and has slice rank at most

r_{1}^{0}+\dots+r_{d}^{0}.\qed

8
Open questions

Let us finish by discussing a few questions that are left open by our current results and proofs. As we had explained in Section
5
, in the tensor rank case the exponent in the upper bound that we show on the number of minimal-length tensor rank decompositions cannot be improved by more than a factor of
d
, but this nonetheless does not give a complete answer to our first question.

Question 8.1
.

What are the optimal bounds in Proposition
5.1
, and for which tensors are they attained ?

Returning to the slice rank, Example
3.6
shows that Theorem
3.5
is false for infinite fields, that in the finite field case the bounds cannot be uniform with respect to
\mathbb{F}
, and furthermore that even in the finite field case they grow square-exponentially in the slice rank of the tensor. This leads us instead to the following formulation, which we believe provides a statement of a similar kind and which is uniform over all fields.

Conjecture 8.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
,
k\geq 1
be integers. Then there exists a positive integer
C(d,k)
such that the following holds. Let
T
be an order-
d
tensor over some arbitrary field
\mathbb{F}
and with slice rank equal to
k
. Then there exist linear subspaces

A_{1}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{1}},\dots,A_{d}\subset\mathbb{F}^{n_{d}}

each with dimension at most
C(d,k)
such that if
r_{1},\dots,r_{d}
are nonnegative integers satisfying

r_{1}+\dots+r_{d}=k

and
a_{j,i}:[n_{j}]\to\mathbb{F}
with
j\in[d]
and
i\in[r_{j}]
are functions satisfying

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{j}}a_{j,i}(x_{j})b_{j,i}(\overline{x_{j}})

for some
(d-1)
-variable functions
b_{j,i}:\prod_{j^{\prime}\neq j}[n_{j^{\prime}}]\to\mathbb{F}
, then

\langle a_{1,1},\dots,a_{1,r_{1}}\rangle\subset A_{1},\dots,\langle a_{d,1},\dots,a_{d,r_{d}}\rangle\subset A_{d}.

We would furthermore not be surprised if for any fixed
d\geq 3
we could take
C(k,d)
to be linear in
k
, as we have not managed to build a counterexample disproving this.

Question 8.3
.

Is Conjecture
8.2
true with furthermore
C(k,d)=O(k)
for every
d\geq 3
?

One further direction in which we believe that our results can be taken further is that of formulating and proving suitable generalisations of Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.5
for other notions of rank. As described in the introduction, one such notion that is of interest is the partition rank.

Question 8.4
.

Can we prove an analogue of Theorem
3.5
for the partition rank ?

As was the case for the slice rank, formulating the property already appears to be challenging, and it is useful to keep in mind that the five ways in which various slice rank decompositions can arise all extend to the partition rank. Let us finish by describing these extensions. For
d\geq 2
an integer, we write
\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])
for the set of bipartitions
\{J,J^{c}\}
of
[d]
with
J,J^{c}\neq\emptyset
. We recall that if
d\geq 2
is an integer,
J
is a subset of
[d]
, then for every
x\in[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{d}]
we write
x(J)
for the restriction of
x
to its coordinates in
J
. Let us also recall the definition of the partition rank.

Definition 8.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
be an integer, and let
T:[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{d}]\to\mathbb{F}
be an order-
d
tensor. We say that the
partition rank
of
T
, denoted by
\pr T
, is the smallest nonnegative integer
k
such that there exist nonnegative integers
r_{\{J,J^{c}\}}
for each bipartition
\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])
, such that

\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}r_{\{J,J^{c}\}}=k

and satisfying one of the following two equivalent properties.

1.

There exist matrices
M_{\{J,J^{c}\}}:(\prod_{j\in J}[n_{j}])\times(\prod_{j\in J^{c}}[n_{j}])\to\mathbb{F}
with rank at most
r_{\{J,J^{c}\}}
for each
\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])
such that

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}M_{\{J,J^{c}\}}(x(J),x(J^{c}))

(50)

is satisfied for all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{d}]
.

2.

For each
\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])
and each
i\in[r_{\{J,J^{c}\}}]
there exist some functions
a_{J,i}:\prod_{j\in J}[n_{j}]\to\mathbb{F}
and
b_{J^{c},i}:\prod_{j\in J^{c}}[n_{j}]\to\mathbb{F}
such that

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\{J,J^{c}\}}}a_{J,i}(x(J))b_{J^{c},i}(x(J^{c}))

(51)

is satisfied for all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[n_{1}]\times\dots\times[n_{d}]
.

We say that an expression such as (
51
) is a
partition rank decomposition
of
T
, say that the
length
of the decomposition is the integer

\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}r_{\{J,J^{c}\}},

and say that the decomposition has
minimal length
if its length is equal to the partition rank of
T
.

Constructions
1
to
5
respectively adapt to the following. Let
d\geq 3
,
k\geq 1
be integers, and let
T
be an order-
d
tensor with partition rank
k
.

Construction 6
.

If
M_{\{J,J^{c}\}}:(\prod_{j\in J}[n_{j}])\times(\prod_{j\in J^{c}}[n_{j}])\to\mathbb{F}
with
\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])
are matrices satisfying (
50
) and

\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}\rk M_{\{J,J^{c}\}}=\pr T,

then rewriting any minimal-length matrix decomposition of any of the matrices
M_{\{J,J^{c}\}}
leads to a new minimal-length partition rank decomposition of
T
.

Construction 7
.

If the tensor rank of
T
is equal to the partition rank
k
of
T
, and

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{1,i}(x_{1})\dots a_{d,i}(x_{d})

is a length-
k
tensor rank decomposition of
T
then for any map
\iota:[k]\to\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])
the decomposition

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}\sum_{i\in\iota^{-1}(\{J,J^{c}\})}(\prod_{j\in J}a_{j,i})(x(J))(\prod_{j\in J^{c}}a_{j,i})(x(J^{c}))

is a minimal-length partition rank decomposition of
T
. This is merely the most extreme form that this construction can take, and tensors
T
satisfying much weaker assumptions than

will lend themselves to it. For instance if
T
has partition rank
k
and can merely be written as

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i,J_{i,1}}(x(J_{i,1}))a_{i,J_{i,2}}(x(J_{i,2}))a_{i,J_{i,3}}(x(J_{i,3}))

where for every

the set
\{J_{i,1},J_{i,2},J_{i,3}\}
is a tripartition of
[d]
with
J_{i,1},J_{i,2},J_{i,3}
each non-empty and
a_{i,J_{i,s}}:\prod_{j\in J_{i,s}}[n_{j}]\to\mathbb{F}
is a function for each

then we already obtain different minimal-length decompositions of
T
depending on how we view each summand as a product of two terms.

Construction 8
.

A tensor
[k]^{d}\to\mathbb{F}
usually has partition rank equal to
k
. Indeed, for any
d\geq 3
, if the field
\mathbb{F}
is finite then there are at most

k^{2^{d}}|\mathbb{F}|^{(k-1)(k^{d-1}+k)}\leq(k^{2^{d}}/|\mathbb{F}|^{k})|\mathbb{F}|^{k^{d}}

tensors with partition rank at most
k-1
. So writing
T
as the sum of its
(d-1)
-variable slices as in Construction
3

T
. We can also write
T
as a sum of its
d^{\prime}
-variable slices for

, but the resulting partition rank decomposition then has length

, which is not minimal, and we hence have less of a generalisation in this direction.

Construction 9
.

If the decomposition

T(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=\sum_{\{J,J^{c}\}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}([d])}\sum_{i=1}^{r_{\{J,J^{c}\}}}a_{J,i}(x(J))a_{J^{c},i}(x(J^{c}))

is a minimal-length decomposition of
T
,
\{J_{1},J_{2},J\}
is a tripartition of
[d]
with
J_{1},J_{2}
each with size at least
1
,
i_{1}\in[r_{\{J_{1},J_{1}^{c}\}}]
and
i_{2}\in[r_{\{J_{2},J_{2}^{c}\}}]
are indices, and
c:\prod_{j\in J}[n_{j}]\to\mathbb{F}
is a function if

is non-empty and an element of

otherwise, then replacing
a_{J_{1}^{c},i_{1}}
and

a_{J_{1}^{c},i_{1}}+a_{I_{2},i_{2}}\otimes c\text{ and }a_{J_{2}^{c},i_{2}}-a_{J_{1},i_{1}}\otimes c

leads to a new minimal-length partition rank decomposition of

.

Construction 10
.

More generally, if
s\in[2,d]
is an integer,
\{J_{1},\dots,J_{s},J\}
is a partition of
[d]
with
J_{1},\dots,J_{s}
each with size at least
1
,
i_{1}\in[r_{\{J_{1},J_{1}^{c}\}}],\dots,i_{s}\in[r_{\{J_{s},J_{s}^{c}\}}]

.

Construction
9
shows that whenever

, it is never true (for any integers

,

, and any field

Moreover, in the case of the partition rank there may be new constructions in addition to the analogues above.
