Almost everywhere balanced sequences of complexity 2 ​ n + 1 2n+1
Julien Cassaigne, Sébastien Labbé, Julien Leroy

Abstract
. We study ternary sequences associated with a multidimensional continued fraction algorithm introduced by the first author. The algorithm is defined by two matrices and we show that it is measurably isomorphic to the shift on the set { 1 , 2 } ℕ \{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}} of directive sequences. For a given set 𝒞 \mathcal{C} of two substitutions, we show that there exists a 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -adic sequence for every vector of letter frequencies or, equivalently, for every directive sequence. We show that their factor complexity is at most 2 ​ n + 1 2n+1 and is 2 ​ n + 1 2n+1 if and only if the letter frequencies are rationally independent if and only if the 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -adic representation is primitive. It turns out that in this case, the sequences are dendric. We also prove that μ \mu -almost every 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -adic sequence is balanced, where μ \mu is any shift-invariant ergodic Borel probability measure on { 1 , 2 } ℕ \{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}} giving a positive measure to the cylinder [ 12121212 ] [12121212] . We also prove that the second Lyapunov exponent of the matrix cocycle associated with the measure μ \mu is negative.

Introduction

A theorem of Dirichlet says that every positive irrational number
\alpha

has infinitely many rational approximations
\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}

such that
|\alpha-\frac{p}{q}|<\frac{1}{q^{2}}
.
Such approximations can be computed from the continued
fraction expansion of
\alpha

\alpha=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\dots]=a_{0}+\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle a_{1}+\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle a_{2}+\frac{\displaystyle 1}{\displaystyle\dots}}}

where
a_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
.
Indeed, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the truncation
\frac{p_{n}}{q_{n}}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}]

provides a sequence
(p_{n}/q_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of rational approximations of
\alpha

called convergents satisfying Dirichlet’s theorem.
Equivalently, the convergents
p_{n}/q_{n}
can be computed from a product of the matrices

A_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

and

A_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)

involving the above sequence of partial quotients:

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{2n+1}&p_{2n}\\
q_{2n+1}&q_{2n}\end{array}\right)=A_{1}^{a_{0}}A_{2}^{a_{1}}A_{1}^{a_{2}}\cdots A_{2}^{a_{2n+1}}.

The convergence of
p_{n}/q_{n}
to
\alpha
then implies that

(1)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\\
1\end{array}\right)\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}=\bigcap_{k\geq 0}A_{i_{0}}A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{k}}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}

where the sequence
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}

is
1^{a_{0}}2^{a_{1}}1^{a_{2}}\cdots 1^{a_{2k}}2^{a_{2k+1}}\cdots
.
Equation (
1
) holds even
if
1
and
2
do not both occur infinitely many times
in
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, in which case
\alpha
is rational.
If
\Delta=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{2}\mid x+y=1\}
denotes
the projection of the positive cone,
Equation (
1
) defines a continuous and onto map

\pi:\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Delta
.
This map is almost one-to-one and its (almost everywhere) inverse is obtained by iterating the normalized Euclid
algorithm
f_{E}
which successively applies either
\mathbf{x}\mapsto A_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{x}/\|A_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

or
\mathbf{x}\mapsto A_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{x}/\|A_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\|_{1}
,
according to whether
\mathbf{x}\in A_{1}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
or
\mathbf{x}\in A_{2}\mathbb{R}^{2}_{\geq 0}
.
Thus the shift map on
\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
defines a symbolic representation of the dynamical system
(\Delta,f_{E})
.

Sturmian words give a combinatorial flavor to
Equation (
1
).
With the matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are respectively associated the substitutions

s_{1}:1\mapsto 1,2\mapsto 12

and

s_{2}:1\mapsto 21,2\mapsto 2.

With the directive sequence
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is then associated the
\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
-adic word
\mathbf{w}\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
:

(2)

\mathbf{w}=\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{i_{0}}s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{n}}(1^{\omega})

which is a Sturmian word
[
Arn02
]
if both letters
1
and
2
appear infinitely often in the directive sequence.
Since
A_{j}
is the incidence matrix of the substitution
s_{i}
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, Equation (
1
) ensures that
the vector of frequencies of letters in
\mathbf{w}
exists and is equal to
\pi((i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=\frac{1}{1+\alpha}(\alpha,1)
.
Recall that the incidence matrix of a substitution
\sigma:A^{*}\to A^{*}
is the matrix
M_{\sigma}=(|\sigma(a)|_{b})_{b,a\in A}
, where
|u|_{v}
stands for the number of occurrences of a word
v
in a word
u
.
It is easily seen that for any word
w\in A^{*}
,
M_{\sigma}(|w|_{a})_{a\in A}=(|\sigma(w)|_{a})_{a\in A}
.

Sturmian words form a deeply studied class of binary words with lots of equivalent definitions
[
Lot02
]
.
They are for instance the aperiodic words with minimal factor complexity
\#\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=n+1

[
CH73
]
, where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}(n)
denotes the language of words of length
n
of
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=\{u\in A^{n}\mid u\text{ occurs in }\mathbf{w}\}
.
Sturmian words are also the aperiodic 1-balanced binary words
[
MH40
]
, where an infinite word
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
is
K
-balanced if any two finite words of the same length occurring in
\mathbf{w}
have, up to
K
, the same number of occurrences of each letter.
The balance property allows to prove that for any Sturmian word
\mathbf{w}
, the frequencies of
1
and
2
exist and are irrational.
More than that, any Sturmian word
\mathbf{w}
has uniform word frequencies, that is, for all finite word
u
occurring in
\mathbf{w}
, the ratio
\frac{|w_{k}w_{k+1}\cdots w_{k+n}|_{u}}{n+1}
has a limit
f_{u}
when
n
goes to infinity, uniformly in
k
.

Results

We consider an extension of Equation (
1
) to a set of two
3\times 3
matrices.
Using a generalization of Euclid’s algorithm, which is thus named a Multidimensional Continued Fraction Algorithm (MCFA), we show that these matrices allow to represent any direction in the positive cone
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
.
Doing so, we generalize Sturmian words on a three-letter alphabet by extending Equation (
2
) to two well-chosen substitutions.
We obtain words
\mathbf{w}
of complexity
2n+1
that are balanced for
almost every given vector of letter frequencies.
This article extends our previous work
[
CLL17
]
presented
during the conference WORDS 2017.

The two matrices are

C_{1}={\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{2}={\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{array}\right)}

and we show that for each sequence
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the set

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}C_{i_{0}}C_{i_{1}}\cdots C_{i_{n}}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}

is one-dimensional.
This property, sometimes called
weak convergence
, is not satisfied by all choices of
3\times 3
matrices.
For instance, Nogueira proved that the Poincaré MCFA is not convergent
[
Nog95
]
.
In our case, convergence allows to define a continuous map

\pi:\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Delta=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\mid\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=1\}
by

(3)

\pi((i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}=\bigcap_{n\geq 0}C_{i_{0}}C_{i_{1}}\cdots C_{i_{n}}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}.

This map is not injective, as for example
\pi(1222\dots)=(0,1,0)=\pi(2111\dots)
,
but it is onto.
We also show that
\pi
is injective exactly on the set
\mathcal{P}
of primitive sequences, i.e., sequences
(C_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
m
and all large enough
n>m
,
C_{i_{m}}\cdots C_{i_{n}}
has only positive entries.
Furthermore, the image
\pi(\mathcal{P})
is the set
\mathcal{I}
of normalized vectors with rationally independent entries.
The inverse of
\pi:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{I}
is given by the MCFA introduced by the first author
[
Cas
]
that consists in iterating the map
f_{C}
on
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{I}
that applies either
\mathbf{x}\mapsto C_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{x}/\|C_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

or
\mathbf{x}\mapsto C_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{x}/\|C_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{x}\|_{1}

according to whether

\mathbf{x}\in C_{1}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
or
\mathbf{x}\in C_{2}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
.
Thus we obtain a similar symbolic representation as for the classical Euclid algorithm.

Theorem A
.

The symbolic dynamical system
(\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}},S)
is a symbolic representation of
(\Delta,f_{C})
.
More precisely,

•

for any shift-invariant Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mu(\mathcal{P})=1
, the map
\pi:(\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}},S,\mu)\to(\Delta,f_{C},\pi_{*}\mu)
is a measure-preserving isomorphism;

•

for any
f_{C}
-invariant Borel probability measure
\nu
on
\Delta
such that
\nu(\mathcal{I})=1
, the map
\pi:(\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}},S,\pi^{-1}_{*}\nu)\to(\Delta,f_{C},\nu)
is a measure-preserving isomorphism.

This result in particular applies to any positive Bernoulli measure
\beta
on
\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and to the
f_{C}
-invariant probability measure
\xi
defined by the density function
6/(\pi^{2}(1-x_{1})(1-x_{3}))

[
AL17
]
.
Observe that any Bernoulli measure on
\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is ergodic and that the measure
\xi
is also ergodic
[
FS21
]
.
Thus the pointwise ergodic theorem may be applied to obtain properties for Bernoulli-almost every directive sequence
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
or for Lebesgue-almost every vector
\mathbf{x}
.
Theorem
C
below is an example of such a result.

We pursue the analogy with Euclid’s algorithm by giving a combinatorial flavor to the symbolic representations
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
We consider the substitutions

c_{1}:\begin{cases}1\mapsto 1\\
2\mapsto 13\\
3\mapsto 2\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad c_{2}:\begin{cases}1\mapsto 2\\
2\mapsto 13\\
3\mapsto 3\end{cases}

whose incidence matrices are respectively
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
and we show that the
class of
\mathcal{C}
-adic words with
\mathcal{C}=\{c_{1},c_{2}\}

provides a nice generalization of Sturmian words over
a three-letter alphabet.
We indeed have the following interpretations of the previous discussion:

•

by weak convergence, the frequencies of letters exist in every
\mathcal{C}
-adic word;

•

by surjectivity of
\pi
, every
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta
is the vector of letter frequencies of a
\mathcal{C}
-adic word;

•

the bijection
\pi:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{I}
induces a bijection between primitive
\mathcal{C}
-adic words and vectors of letter frequencies with rationally independent entries.

We give another equivalence of primitive
\mathcal{C}
-adic words in terms of their factor complexity, generalizing the Sturmian case.
We also show that the primitive
\mathcal{C}
-adic words are exactly the
\mathcal{C}
-adic words that are dendric, a property recently introduced under the name of “tree sets”
[
BDFD+15
]
(see Section
10
for the definition).

Theorem B
.

Let
\mathbf{w}
be a
\mathcal{C}
-adic word with directive sequence
(i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

\mathbf{w}
has factor complexity
p(n)=2n+1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)

the frequencies of letters in
\mathbf{w}
are rationally independent;

(3)

(C_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is primitive;

(4)

\mathbf{w}
is a uniformly recurrent dendric word.

The last property of Sturmian words that we consider is their balancedness.
Not all primitive
\mathcal{C}
-adic words are balanced
[
And21
]
, but we prove that almost all of them are (for many measures).
Our proof is based on the method proposed by Avila and
Delecroix
[
AD19
]
for Brun and Fully Subtractive MCFA.
It consists in applying the pointwise ergodic theorem to show that some fixed contracting matrix appears sufficiently often in almost every sequence
(C_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
The same method allows to show that the second Lyapunov exponent
is negative. The definition of Lyapunov exponents can be found
in Section
9
.

An application of multidimensional continued fraction algorithms is
to provide simultaneous Diophantine approximation of a vector of real numbers

[
Sch80
]
.
The quality of the approximations can be evaluated in terms of the first two Lyapunov exponents of the MCFA
[
Bal92
,
Lag93
]
.
In particular, if the second Lyapunov exponent is negative, this implies that
the algorithm is strongly convergent
[
Har02
,
HK00
,
HK02
]
.

.

Let
\mu
be a shift-invariant ergodic
Borel probability measure on
\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
If

\mu([12121212])>0,

then for
\mu
-almost every directive sequence

It turns out that the map

(see Section

Also note that Selmer algorithm is conjugate on the absorbing simplex to

Comparison with other generalizations of Sturmian words over larger alphabets

There exist many other generalizations of Sturmian words over larger alphabets, each focusing on particular properties satisfied by Sturmian words.

Words of complexity
2n+1
were for instance considered by Arnoux and Rauzy
[
AR91
]

with the condition that, like Sturmian words, there is exactly one left and one right special factor of each length;
these words are now called Arnoux-Rauzy words.
It is known that the frequencies of any Arnoux-Rauzy word are well defined and belong to the Rauzy gasket
[
AS13
]
, a fractal set of Lebesgue measure zero.
Thus the above condition on the number of special factors is very restrictive for the possible letter frequencies.

Words of complexity
p(n)\leq 2n+1
include Arnoux-Rauzy words,
codings of interval exchange transformations and more
[
Ler14
]
.
For any given letter frequencies one can construct words of factor
complexity
2n+1
by the coding of a 3-interval exchange transformation. It is however known that these words are almost always unbalanced
[
Zor97
]
.

In recent years, multidimensional continued fraction algorithms were used to obtain ternary balanced words with low factor complexity for any given vector of letter frequencies.
Indeed the Brun algorithm leads to balanced words
[
DHS13
]
and it was shown that the Arnoux-Rauzy-Poincaré algorithm leads to words of factor complexity
p(n)\leq\frac{5}{2}n+1

[
BL15
]
.

Thus the words that we consider in this paper provide the first class of words which simultaneously generalize the three Sturmian properties of having factor complexity
(\#A-1)n+1
, having any vector of rationally independent letter frequencies and being almost always balanced.
The problem of finding an analogue of
f_{C}
in dimension
d\geq 4
, generating balanced
\mathcal{S}
-adic sequences with complexity
(d-1)n+1
for almost every vector of letter frequencies, is still open.

2.
A bidimensional continued fraction algorithm

On
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
, the bidimensional continued fraction algorithm
introduced by the first author
[
Cas
]
is

F_{C}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\begin{cases}(x_{1}-x_{3},x_{3},x_{2}),&\mbox{if }x_{1}\geq x_{3};\\
(x_{2},x_{1},x_{3}-x_{1}),&\mbox{if }x_{1}<x_{3}.\end{cases}

More information on multidimensional continued fraction algorithms
can be found in
[
Bre81
,
Sch00
]
.

Alternatively, the map
F_{C}
can be defined by associating nonnegative matrices
to each part of a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
into
\Lambda_{1}\cup\Lambda_{2}
where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\mid x_{1}\geq x_{3}\},

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\mid x_{1}<x_{3}\}.

The matrices are given by the rule

\mathsf{M}(\mathbf{x})=C_{i}

if and only if

\mathbf{x}\in\Lambda_{i}
where

C_{1}={\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad C_{2}={\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&1\end{array}\right)}.

The map
F_{C}
on

2.1.
Background on substitutions and
\mathcal{S}
-adic words

Let
A
be an alphabet, i.e., a finite set.
By
substitution
over
A
we mean an endomorphism
\sigma
of the free monoid
A^{*}
which is non-erasing, i.e.
\sigma(a)\neq\varepsilon
for all
a
, where
\varepsilon
is the empty word.
If
\mathcal{S}
is a set of substitutions over
A^{*}
,
a word
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
is said to be
\mathcal{S}
-adic
if there is a sequence

\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{N}}
and a sequence
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
such that the limit
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{n-1}(a_{n})
exists and is equal to
\mathbf{w}
.
The 2-tuple
(\bm{\sigma},\mathbf{a})
is called an
\mathcal{S}
-adic representation
of
\mathbf{w}
and the sequence
\bm{\sigma}
a
directive sequence
of
\mathbf{w}
.

A sequence of substitutions
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathbb{N}}
is said to be
everywhere growing
if
\min_{a\in A}|\sigma_{[0,n)}(a)|
goes to infinity as
n
goes to infinity.

With an substitution
\sigma:A^{*}\to A^{*}
, we associate its
incidence matrix

M_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}^{A\times A}
defined by
(M_{\sigma})_{a,b}=|\sigma(b)|_{a}
.
Thus, for any word
w\in A^{*}
, we have
\overrightarrow{\sigma(w)}=M_{\sigma}\overrightarrow{w}
, where
\overrightarrow{w}\in\mathbb{N}^{A}
is defined by
\overrightarrow{w}_{a}=|w|_{a}
.

For every directive sequence

More precisely,

(1)

(2)

(3)

Proof.

For all

We also let

denote the longest prefix of

which is a prefix of

tends to infinity as

3.
Semi-norm of matrices and convergence

Equation (
4
) shows that the iteration of the map
F_{C}
on
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
defines a sequence of matrices
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\mathbf{x}\in\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}.

In this section, we give sufficient conditions for a sequence of

d
-dimensional nonnegative matrices
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to be
weakly
convergent
, i.e., to be such that the cone

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}

is one-dimensional.

The following result states that the notion of weak convergence is related to the existence of (uniform) frequencies in
\mathcal{S}
-adic words.
Let
\mathbf{w}=(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
be an infinite word and let
u\in A^{*}
be a word occurring in
\mathbf{w}
.
The
frequency
of
u
in
\mathbf{w}
is the limit, whenever it exists,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|w_{[0,n)}|_{u}}{n}
, where
w_{[m,n)}=w_{m}w_{m+1}\cdots w_{n-1}
and
|v|_{u}
stands for the number of occurrences of
u
in the word
v
.
The word
\mathbf{w}
has
uniform word frequencies
if for every
u\in A^{*}
, the ratio
\frac{|w_{[k,k+n)}|_{u}}{n}
converges when
n
goes to infinity, uniformly in
k
.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
BD14
]

Let
A
be an alphabet of size
d
.
Let
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
be a word that admits an everywhere growing directive sequence
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and let
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the associated sequence of incidence matrices.
If for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the cone

(5)

\bigcap_{n\geq k}M_{[k,n)}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}

is one-dimensional, then
\mathbf{w}
has uniform word frequencies.
In particular, if
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
is such that
\|\mathbf{f}\|_{1}=1
and

(6)

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{f},

then
\mathbf{f}
is the vector of letter frequencies of
\mathbf{w}
.

3.1.
A semimetric on the projective space

Recall that the
Hilbert metric
is defined as

d_{H}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\log\frac{v_{i}w_{j}}{v_{j}w_{i}}

where
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
.
Here, we define another closely related function as

(7)

d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|v_{i}w_{j}-v_{j}w_{i}|

where
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
and

\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
.
It is not a distance as it does not satisfy the triangle inequality, but it is
a semimetric, that is, it satisfies the first three axioms of a distance as
shown below.

Lemma 3.2
.

d_{M}
is a semimetric, i.e.,

(1)

d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})\geq 0
,

(2)

d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=0
if and only if
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w}
,

(3)

d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v})
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
.

(i)
We have
d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})\geq 0
by definition.

(ii)
If
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w}
, then there exists
k>0
such that

\mathbf{w}=k\mathbf{v}
.
Then

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|v_{i}w_{j}-v_{j}w_{i}|=\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|v_{i}(kv_{j})-v_{j}(kv_{i})|=k\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|v_{i}v_{j}-v_{j}v_{i}|=0.

Thus
d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=0
.
Conversely, if
d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=0
, then

\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|v_{i}w_{j}-v_{j}w_{i}|={\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}}\cdot d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})=0.

Therefore, for every
i,j
such that

1\leq i,j\leq d
, we have
v_{i}w_{j}=v_{j}w_{i}
.
Choose
j
such that
w_{j}\neq 0
and set
k=\frac{v_{j}}{w_{j}}
.
Thus, for all
i
, we have
v_{i}=kw_{i}
.
As
\mathbf{v}\neq 0
, we have
k>0
and we conclude that
\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w}
.

(iii) We have

\displaystyle d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|v_{i}w_{j}-v_{j}w_{i}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|_{2}\cdot\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}|w_{i}v_{j}-w_{j}v_{i}|=d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v}).

∎

Using the semimetric
d_{M}
, we define the
diameter
of a cone
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
as

(8)

\mathrm{diam}(\Lambda)=\sup_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{w}).

The fact that the diameter is defined from a semimetric is enough for our
needs since the following lemma proves that a cone of diameter zero is reduced to a single line.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
be a cone.
If
\mathrm{diam}(\Lambda)=0
, then
there exists
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying
\Lambda=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{u}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
.
By definition, we have
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{u}\subseteq\Lambda
.
Now let
\mathbf{v}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
.
Since
\mathrm{diam}(\Lambda)=0
, we have
d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{u},\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{v})=0
.
From Lemma
3.2
(ii), there exists
k\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

\mathbf{v}=k\mathbf{u}
. Therefore
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\mathbf{u}
.
We have proved

\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{u}
.
∎

Our aim is now to study the diameter of
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}
for a given sequence of matrices
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
To that aim, we provide an upper bound for the diameter of a cone defined by the image of the nonnegative orthant under the application of a nonnegative matrix.
It is defined in terms of the entries of the matrix and in terms of a matrix semi-norm that we define below.

If
V
is a non-trivial vector subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\|\cdot\|
is a
semi-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is a norm on
V
,
then the matrix semi-norm
\|\cdot|_{V}\|
is defined for any
d\times d

matrix
M
as

(9)

\left\|M\middle|_{V}\right\|:=\sup_{v\in V\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|Mv\|}{\|v\|}.

For any vector
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
,
\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
stands for the vector space of codimension 1 orthogonal to
\mathbf{f}
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
be some cone.
As done in
[
AD19
]
,
if
\|\cdot\|
is a norm on
\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
for all
\mathbf{f}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
,
we define a matrix semi-norm
on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
as

(10)

\left\|M\right\|^{\Lambda}=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\left\|M\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|.

Thus, we have

\left\|M\right\|^{\Lambda}=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{v\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\|Mv\right\|}{\left\|v\right\|}.

The diameter of the image of the nonnegative orthant under a positive matrix can be bounded
by the semi-norm of its transpose matrix.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d\times d}
be a positive matrix.
Then

(11)

\mathrm{diam}(A\,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0})\leq\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a_{ij}}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|_{2}^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}.

Proof.

From the definition of
d_{M}
and of the diameter of a cone, and using the fact that for all
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
,
|\vphantom{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\!wv|\leq\|w\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle=\sup_{v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}d_{M}(\mathbb{R}_{>0}Av,\mathbb{R}_{>0}Aw)

\displaystyle=\sup_{v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}\|Aw\|_{2}}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|(\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Aw)-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)(\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Aw)\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}\|Aw\|_{2}}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|(\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)(\vphantom{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\!w\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Ae_{j})-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)(\vphantom{w}^{\mathsf{T}}\!w\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Ae_{i})\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}\|Aw\|_{2}}\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left|\vphantom{w}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!w\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\left((\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}\|Aw\|_{2}}\cdot\left\|w\right\|_{2}\cdot\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\left((\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\|w\right\|_{2}}{\|Aw\|_{2}}\cdot\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\left((\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\right)\right\|_{2}.

Now observe that

\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\dfrac{\left\|w\right\|_{2}}{\|Aw\|_{2}}\leq\dfrac{1}{\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a_{ij}}.

Furthermore, for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
, we have
\mathbf{f}=Av\in A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
and, for all
1\leq i,j\leq d
,

(\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},

and, as a consequence,

\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\left((\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|_{2}^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\left\|(\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\right\|_{2}.

We finally get

\displaystyle\mathrm{diam}(A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a_{ij}}\cdot\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|_{2}^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\cdot\max_{1\leq i,j\leq d}\left\|\left((\vphantom{e_{i}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{i}Av)e_{j}-(\vphantom{e_{j}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!e_{j}Av)e_{i}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a_{ij}}\cdot\sup_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{\|Av\|_{2}}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|_{2}^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\cdot\left\|Av\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a_{ij}}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|_{2}^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}.

∎

The next lemma shows that the matrix semi-norm defined in Equation (
10
) behaves well with respect
to product of matrices.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{\geq 0}
such that
AB\neq 0
and
let
\|\cdot\|
be any semi-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is a norm on every

\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
with
\mathbf{f}\in(A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\cup B\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0})\setminus\{0\}
.
We have

\left\|\vphantom{(AB)}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)\right\|^{AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\leq\left\|\vphantom{B}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!B\right\|^{B\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}.

Proof.

We have

\left\|\vphantom{(AB)}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)\right\|^{AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{(AB)}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)z\|}{\|z\|}.

If for all
\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
and
z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
,
we have
\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az=0
, then

\left\|\vphantom{(AB)}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)\right\|^{AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}=0

and the result follows.
Otherwise, we have

\left\|\vphantom{(AB)}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)\right\|^{AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\neq 0}\frac{\|\vphantom{(AB)}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)z\|}{\|z\|}.

Observe that for all
\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
and all
z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
, we have
\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\in\mathbf{g}^{\perp}
for some
\mathbf{g}\in B\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
.
Since
\|\cdot\|
is a norm on every

\mathbf{g}^{\perp}
with
\mathbf{g}\in B\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
, we have
\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\right\|\neq 0
for all
\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\neq 0
.
Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\vphantom{(AB)}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(AB)\right\|^{AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\neq 0}\frac{\|\vphantom{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\!B\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}{\|\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}\cdot\frac{\|\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}{\|z\|}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\neq 0}\frac{\|\vphantom{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\!B\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}{\|\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}\cdot\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in AB\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\},\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\neq 0}\frac{\|\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}{\|z\|}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathbf{g}\in B\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z^{\prime}\in\mathbf{g}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{B}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Bz^{\prime}\|}{\|z^{\prime}\|}\cdot\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\},z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az\|}{\|z\|}

\displaystyle=\left\|\vphantom{B}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!B\right\|^{B\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{A}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!A\right\|^{A\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}

where we substituted
z^{\prime}=\vphantom{A}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Az
,
\mathbf{f}=A\mathbf{g}
and
\mathbf{g}=Bv
for some
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}

since there exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
such that
\mathbf{f}=ABv
.
∎

3.2.
Primitive sequences and convergence

Recall that a square matrix
M
is
primitive
if there is some positive integer
k
such that
M^{k}
has only positive entries.
As an analogue, if
\mathbf{m}=(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of nonnegative square matrices of the same size
d
, we say that
\mathbf{m}
is
primitive
if for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
s>r
such that
M_{[r,s)}
has only positive entries.

If
\mathbf{m}
is the sequence of incidence matrices associated with a sequence of endomorphisms
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
A^{*}
, then primitivity of
\mathbf{m}
means that for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
s>r
such that for all letters
a,b\in A
,
a
occurs in
\sigma_{[r,s)}(b)
.

The next result provides sufficient conditions for having weak convergence of a
primitive sequence of matrices without any recurrence hypothesis like in
Proposition 3.5.5 of
[
AA20
]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{m}=(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a primitive sequence
of nonnegative integer matrices.
If there exists
K>0
such that

\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\right\|^{M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\leq K

for some norm and for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
then there exists a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
satisfying

(12)

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{u}.

Proof.

Since
\mathbf{m}
is primitive, there exists an increasing sequence

(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
n_{0}=0
and
M_{[n_{k},n_{k+1})}
has only positive entries for
every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Furthermore, the sequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
can be chosen among the indices
n
for which

\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\right\|^{M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\leq K
.
If
A^{(k)}=(a^{(k)}_{ij})=M_{[0,n_{k})}
, then for
k\geq 1
, one has

\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a^{(k)}_{ij}\geq d^{k-1}.

Moreover, as the dimension is finite, the chosen norm is equivalent to the

2
-norm, which implies that there exists a constant
K^{\prime}
such that

\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\right\|_{2}^{M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\leq K^{\prime}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\right\|^{M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}.

Therefore, as
M_{[0,n_{k-1})}\,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\subset M_{[0,n)}\,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\subset M_{[0,n_{k})}\,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
whenever
n_{k-1}\leq n\leq n_{k}
, we get, using Lemma
3.4
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{diam}(M_{[0,n)}\,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathrm{diam}(M_{[0,n_{k})}\,\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0})

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{\min_{1\leq i,j\leq d}a^{(k)}_{ij}}\cdot\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{k})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{k})}\right\|_{2}^{M_{[0,n_{k})}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{1}{d^{k-1}}\cdot K^{\prime}\cdot K=0.

We conclude from Lemma
3.3
that the cone
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
is one-dimensional.
∎

3.3.
A piecewise linear semi-norm

Proposition
3.6
holds for any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In this section, we consider the following function
\|\cdot\|_{D}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and show that it is a semi-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a norm on well-chosen subspaces.
It is defined as

(13)

\|v\|_{D}=\max(v)-\min(v),

where
\max(v)=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}v_{i}
and
\min(v)=\min_{1\leq i\leq d}v_{i}
.
It is invariant under the addition of constant vectors, that is,

(14)

\|v+a\vphantom{(1,\dots,1)}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(1,\dots,1)\|_{D}=\|v\|_{D}

for every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that the function
\|\cdot\|_{D}
is not a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
\|v\|_{D}=0
for some nonzero vector

v
. But
\|\cdot\|_{D}
is a norm on some well-chosen subspaces.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

(1)

\|\cdot\|_{D}
is a semi-norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(2)

\|\cdot\|_{D}
is a norm on
\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
,

(3)

\|\cdot\|_{D}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
are equivalent norms on
\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
.
More precisely,

2\|v\|_{\infty}\geq\|v\|_{D}\geq\|v\|_{\infty}

for every
v\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

(i) We show that it is a semi-norm.
It is
absolutely homogeneous
.
Let
a\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We have
\|av\|_{D}=\max(av)-\min(av)=a\max(v)-a\min(v)=a\|v\|_{D}
,
and
\|-v\|_{D}=\|v\|_{D}
.
It is
subadditive
.
Let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We have
\|u+v\|_{D}=\max(u+v)-\min(u+v)\leq\max(u)+\max(v)-\min(u)-\min(v)=\|u\|_{D}+\|v\|_{D}
.
It is
non-negative
.
For every
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\max(v)\geq\min(v)
so that
\|v\|_{D}\geq 0
.

(ii) Now we show that it is a norm on
\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
.
It is
definite
.
Let
v\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
and suppose that
\|v\|_{D}=0
.
We have
\max(v)=\min(v)
so that
v=a(1,\dots,1)
for some
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
By definition of
v
, we have that
0=\vphantom{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\!v\mathbf{f}=a\|\mathbf{f}\|_{1}
which holds only if
a=0
since
\|\mathbf{f}\|_{1}\neq 0
.
Therefore
v=0
.

(iii) We always have

\|v\|_{D}=\max(v)-\min(v)\leq|\max(v)|+|\min(v)|\leq 2\|v\|_{\infty}.

If
\min(v)>0
, then
\vphantom{v}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!v\mathbf{f}>0
which contradicts the fact that
v
is orthogonal
to
\mathbf{f}
.
Similarly,
\max(v)<0
implies
\vphantom{v}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!v\mathbf{f}<0
and contradicts the fact that
v
is orthogonal
to
\mathbf{f}
.
Therefore
v\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
implies that
\min(v)\leq 0\leq\max(v)
.
We conclude that

\|v\|_{D}=\max(v)-\min(v)=|\max(v)|+|\min(v)|\geq\|v\|_{\infty}.

∎

If follows from Lemma
3.7
that

\|\cdot|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\|_{D}
is a matrix semi-norm as soon as

\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
.
Note that it follows from Equation (
14
) that it satisfies

(15)

\left\|\left(M+\vphantom{(1,\dots,1)}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(1,\dots,1)u\right)|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{D}=\left\|M|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{D}

for every matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and row vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Finally, if
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and

\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
, then
it follows from Lemma
3.7
(iii) that

(16)

\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|M\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{D}\leq\left\|M\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 2\cdot\left\|M\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{D}.

3.4.
The supremum is attained on the boundaries

We now state a general result which states that the supremum of

\left\|\vphantom{M}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M\right\|_{D}^{M\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}

is attained on the boundaries of a finite number of
subcones forming a partition of
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}
.
It is used in this article for proving the balancedness of almost all
\mathcal{C}
-adic sequences.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{>0}
be a positive and invertible matrix.
Consider the set
\mathcal{H}
of hyperplanes orthogonal to some vector in

(17)

S=\left(M\mathcal{E}\cup(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E})\cup M(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E})\right)\setminus\{0\}

where
\mathcal{E}=\{e_{i}:1\leq i\leq d\}
and
\mathcal{D}
be the finite union of one-dimensional intersections of several hyperplanes of
\mathcal{H}
:

(18)

\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}h_{1},\dots,h_{d-1}\in\mathcal{H}\\
\mathrm{dim}\bigcap_{1\leq i<d}h_{i}=1\end{subarray}}\bigcap_{1\leq i<d}h_{i}.

Then the maximal value of the semi-norm is attained at some vector
z
in

\left(\mathcal{D}\setminus\pm(\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M)^{-1}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}\right)\setminus\{0\}
, i.e.,

(19)

\left\|\vphantom{M}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M\right\|_{D}^{M\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}=\max_{z\in\left(\mathcal{D}\setminus\pm(\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M)^{-1}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}\right)\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}}.

Proof.

We have

\left\|\vphantom{M}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M\right\|_{D}^{M\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}}=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in M\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\left\|\vphantom{M}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{D}=\sup_{\mathbf{f}\in M\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}}=\sup_{z\in Z\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}},

where

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid z\perp\mathbf{f}\text{ for some }\mathbf{f}\in M\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}\}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\vphantom{z}^{\mathsf{T}}\!zMu=0\text{ for some }u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}\}

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\text{ entries of }\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\text{ are not all positive or all negative}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\pm(\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M)^{-1}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}

The vectors of
Z
correspond to
2^{d}-2
of the
2^{d}
cones delimited by
the hyperplanes orthogonal to the vectors of
M\mathcal{E}
.

The norm
\|z\|_{D}
is a piecewise linear form which is linear on
each of the cones delimited by the hyperplanes orthogonal to the nonzero vectors
of
\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}
.
There are
d!
such cones.
Similarly, the norm
\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}
is a piecewise linear form which is linear on
every of the cones delimited by the hyperplanes orthogonal to the nonzero vectors
of
M(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E})
.

We consider any of the subcones
\Lambda
delimited by hyperplanes orthogonal to
some vectors in
S
defined in Equation (
17
) that
are inside of
Z
.
Remark that by construction both
\|z\|_{D}
and
\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}

are linear on
\Lambda
.
The intersection of
\Lambda
with the euclidean sphere
of radius 1 is compact.
Therefore, the maximum
m
of the function

\frac{\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}}
restricted to

\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
is attained at some point
z_{0}
with
\|z_{0}\|_{2}=1
:

\frac{\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz_{0}\|_{D}}{\|z_{0}\|_{D}}=m.

Let
L
be a linear form of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
L(z)=\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}-m\|z\|_{D}
for
z\in\Lambda
. If
L=0
, then

\frac{\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}}=m

is constant on
\Lambda
, so its maximum is attained on an edge of
\Lambda
.
Otherwise,
the equation
L(z)=0
defines a hyperplane
H
containing
the origin.
By definition of the maximum we have

L(z)\leq 0
for any
z\in\Lambda
.
Therefore
\Lambda
is contained in one of the halfspaces delimited by
H
.
The set
H\cap\Lambda
is either an edge, or contains an edge of
\Lambda
.
Therefore, the maximum of
\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}-m\|z\|_{D}
must the attained on an edge of
\Lambda
, that is,
at some point in
\mathcal{D}\setminus\pm(\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M)^{-1}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

A sequence

(B(M))_{ij}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }M_{ij}>0;\\

M
has only positive entries if and only if
B(M)
contains only 1’s.

Assume first that there is some integer
N\geq 0
such that for all

M_{N+2i}=M_{N+2i+1}

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{m}=(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of matrices.
If
\mathbf{m}
contains infinitely many occurrences of both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, then
there exists an increasing sequence of integers

(n_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

n_{0}=0
and

(20)

M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1)}}\in\{C_{1}C_{2}^{k}C_{1},C_{2}C_{1}^{k}C_{2}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

By induction, suppose that there exist
M\in\mathbb{N}
and an increasing sequence of integers

(n_{m})_{0\leq m\leq M}
such that
n_{0}=0
and satisfying Equation (

) for every

such that

.
Then, the next value

n_{M+1}=\min\{k>n_{M}\mid M_{k}=M_{n_{M}}\}+1.

The existence of
n_{M+1}
is obvious since both
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
occur infinitely often in
\mathbf{m}
.
∎

4.2.
The semi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{D}
in the monoid generated by
C_{1}
,
C_{2}

The next lemma presents a nice property of the matrices of the form

C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1}
or
C_{2}C_{1}^{n}C_{2}
for
n\geq 0
in terms of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{D}
.
Note that when
d=3
,
\|(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})\|_{D}=\max\{|v_{1}-v_{2}|,|v_{1}-v_{3}|,|v_{2}-v_{3}|\}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\left\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1})}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1})\right\|_{D}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}=1\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|\vphantom{(C_{2}C_{1}^{n}C_{2})}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{2}C_{1}^{n}C_{2})\right\|_{D}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}=1.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
be a nonzero nonnegative vector
and let
z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}
.
We only prove it for
C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1}
, the other one being symmetric.
We separate the odd and even cases.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Using Equation (
14
), we have

\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k}C_{1})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k}C_{1})z\|_{D}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&k&0\\
1&k+1&0\\
1&k&1\end{array}\right)z\right\|_{D}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)z\right\|_{D}

and

\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k+1}C_{1})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k+1}C_{1})z\|_{D}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&k&1\\
1&k+1&1\\
1&k+1&0\end{array}\right)z\right\|_{D}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right)z\right\|_{D}

Since
z
is orthogonal to
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
, we have
\min(z)\leq 0\leq\max(z)
.
Thus, for

z_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right)z\quad\text{and}\quad z_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}0&-1&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&-1\end{array}\right)z,

we have

\displaystyle\min(z)\leq\min(z_{1})\leq

\displaystyle 0\leq\max(z_{1})\leq\max(z)

\displaystyle-\max(z)\leq\min(z_{2})\leq

\displaystyle 0\leq\max(z_{2})\leq-\min(z),

which implies that
\|z_{1}\|_{D},\|z_{2}\|_{D}\leq\|z\|_{D}
.

This shows that for all
z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1})z\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}}\leq 1.

Furthermore,
\mathbf{f}
being nonzero nonnegative, there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
with
a\leq 0\leq b
such that
z=(0,a,b)\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
.
For this vector
z
, we have

\displaystyle\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k}C_{1})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k}C_{1})z\|_{D}

\displaystyle=\|(0,a,b)\|_{D}=\|z\|_{D};

\displaystyle\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k+1}C_{1})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{2k+1}C_{1})z\|_{D}

\displaystyle=\|(-a,0,-b)\|_{D}=\|z\|_{D},

showing that
\sup_{z\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2}^{n}C_{1})z\|_{D}}{\|z\|_{D}}=1
.
∎

Observe that Lemma
4.3
does not hold in general.
Indeed some matrices
M
obtained as the product of matrices
C_{1}
and

C_{2}
are such that
\|\vphantom{M}^{\mathsf{T}}\!Mz\|_{D}>\|z\|_{D}
. For example,
it is the case for
M=C_{1}C_{2}
. For
z=(8,-5,13)
we
compute

\|\vphantom{(C_{1}C_{2})}^{\mathsf{T}}\!(C_{1}C_{2})z\|_{D}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&0&1\\
1&1&0\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right)\cdot\left(\begin{array}[]{c}8\\
-5\\
13\end{array}\right)\right\|_{D}=\left\|\left(\begin{array}[]{c}21\\
3\\
-5\end{array}\right)\right\|_{D}=26

which is larger than
\|z\|_{D}=18
.

4.3.
Convergence in the monoid generated by
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

The next result shows that any sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is weakly convergent.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbf{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
be a vector belonging to the cone
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the vector

\mathbf{u}^{(k)}=M_{[0,k)}^{-1}\mathbf{u}.

Proposition 4.4
.

For any sequence
\mathbf{m}=(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying

(21)

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{u}.

Proof.

We split the proof into two cases, depending on whether
\mathbf{m}
is primitive or not.

Assume first that
\mathbf{m}=(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a primitive sequence.
From Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
4.2

there exists an increasing sequence of integers

(n_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

n_{0}=0
and

(22)

M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1)}}\in\{C_{1}C_{2}^{k}C_{1},C_{2}C_{1}^{k}C_{2}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}

for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
We compute using Equation (
16
), Lemma
3.5

and Lemma
4.3
that

\displaystyle\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{\ell})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{\ell})}\right\|_{\infty}^{M_{[0,n_{\ell})}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{\ell})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{\ell})}\right\|_{D}^{M_{[0,n_{\ell})}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle=2\left\|\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\vphantom{M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\right\|_{D}^{\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\left\|\vphantom{M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\right\|_{D}^{M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\prod_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\left\|\vphantom{M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\right\|_{D}^{\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}}=2.

Therefore, from Proposition
3.6
,
there exists a vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\setminus\{0\}
satisfying

(23)

\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\bigcap_{m\geq 0}M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{u}

and the conclusion follows.

Assume now that
\mathbf{m}
is not primitive.
From Proposition
4.1
, there is an integer
N\geq 0
such that
M_{N+2i}=M_{N+2i+1}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let us show that
\bigcap_{n\geq N}M_{[N,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
is one-dimensional.

Let
\mathbf{u}\in\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let us write
\mathbf{u}^{(k)}=(u_{1}^{(k)},u_{2}^{(k)},u_{3}^{(k)})
and let us show that
u_{2}^{(N)}=0
.
Indeed, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the vector
\mathbf{u}^{(N+2k+2)}
is equal to one of the following two vectors:

\displaystyle C_{1}^{-2}\mathbf{u}^{(N+2k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{1}^{(N+2k)}-u_{2}^{(N+2k)}-u_{3}^{(N+2k)},u_{2}^{(N+2k)},u_{3}^{(N+2k)});

\displaystyle C_{2}^{-2}\mathbf{u}^{(N+2k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(u_{1}^{(N+2k)},u_{2}^{(N+2k)},u_{3}^{(N+2k)}-u_{1}^{(N+2k)}-u_{2}^{(N+2k)}).

In both cases, the middle entry is unchanged.
Thus, by induction, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
u^{(N+2k)}_{2}=u_{2}^{(N)}
.
Also the sum of the two other entries decreases by at least
u_{2}^{(N)}
.
Therefore, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

0\leq u^{(N+2k)}_{1}+u^{(N+2k)}_{3}\leq u_{1}^{(N)}+u_{3}^{(N)}-ku_{2}^{(N)},

which implies that
u_{2}^{(N)}=0
.

To end the proof, it suffices to observe that if
u_{2}^{(N)}=0
, the action of
C_{1}^{-2}
and
C_{2}^{-2}
on the vectors
\mathbf{u}^{(N+k)}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, corresponds to the well-known additive Euclidean algorithm applied to the first and third components.
This shows that
\bigcap_{n\geq N}M_{[N,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
is one-dimensional and thus that so is
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
.
∎

5.
Rational dependencies of the limit cone

Proposition
4.4
states that for any sequence of matrices
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, the cone
\bigcap_{n\geq 0}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
converges to a half-line
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{f}
with
\|\mathbf{f}\|_{1}=1
.
In this section, we give more insight on the properties of
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in terms of the rational dependencies of the entries of the vector
\mathbf{f}
.
We define the dimension of a vector
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the dimension of the
\mathbb{Q}
-vector space spanned by its entries, denoted

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})
.
If
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})<3
, then there exists a rational dependency between its
entries.
If
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=3
, we say that
\mathbf{f}
is totally irrational.
In this section, we show that
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is primitive if and only if
the vector
\mathbf{f}
is totally irrational.
More precisely, we prove the following result.
Recall that
\Delta
denotes the simplex

\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\mid\|\mathbf{x}\|_{1}=1\}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\mathbf{f}\in\Delta
such that

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{f}
.

(1)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=1

if and only if

(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{*}\{C_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},C_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
.

(2)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=2

if and only if

(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\left(\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{*}\{C_{1}^{2},C_{2}^{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}\right)\setminus\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{*}\{C_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},C_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
.

(3)

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=3

if and only
if
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is primitive.

Note that the three conditions are mutually exclusive since
we proved in Proposition
4.1

that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is primitive
if and only if

(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\notin\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{*}\{C_{1}^{2},C_{2}^{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The proofs of the first two cases of Theorem
5.1

are done separately in
Lemma
5.2
and
Lemma
5.3
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}

and let
\mathbf{f}\in\Delta
such that

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{f}
.
We have

(1)

M_{n}=C_{1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if

\mathbf{f}=(1,0,0)
,

(2)

M_{n}=C_{2}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if

\mathbf{f}=(0,0,1)
,

(3)

(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{*}\{C_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},C_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}

if and only if
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=1
.

Proof.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\mathbf{f}^{(k)}=(f^{(k)}_{1},f^{(k)}_{2},f^{(k)}_{3})=M_{[0,k)}^{-1}\mathbf{f}
.

(i)
If
\mathbf{f}=(1,0,0)
, then
M_{0}=C_{1}
, since
(1,0,0)\notin C_{2}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{3}
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{f}^{(1)}=\mathbf{f}
.
Therefore, by induction,
M_{n}=C_{1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Conversely,

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{1}^{n}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}(1,0,0)
.

(ii) The proof is done similarly to the proof of (i).

(iii)
Suppose that

(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{*}\{C_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},C_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
.
Then, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(M_{n})_{n\geq k}\in\{C_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},C_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
.
From (i) and (ii),

\mathbf{f}^{(k)}\in\{(1,0,0),(0,0,1)\}
.
Thus
\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
so that
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=1
.
Conversely, if

\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=1
then

\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{Q}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
.
We may suppose
b\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
for some
b\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.
If
\min(\mathbf{f}^{(k)})\neq 0
, then
\|\mathbf{f}^{(k+1)}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{f}^{(k)}\|_{1}-1/b
.
Thus there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}

such that
\min(\mathbf{f}^{(k)})=0
.
Then, if
\mathbf{f}^{(k^{\prime})}
is not of the form
(0,0,a)
,
(0,a,0)
or
(a,0,0)
for some
a
, then
\|\mathbf{f}^{(k^{\prime}+2)}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{f}^{(k^{\prime})}\|_{1}-1/b
.
Thus there exists
k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}

such that
\mathbf{f}^{(k^{\prime})}\in\{(a,0,0),(0,a,0),(0,0,a)\}
for some
a>0
.
If
\mathbf{f}^{(k^{\prime})}=(0,a,0)
, then
\mathbf{f}^{(k^{\prime}+1)}\in\{(a,0,0),(0,0,a)\}
.
Like for the cases (i) and (ii), we deduce that
(M_{n})_{n\geq k^{\prime}+1}
is in
\{C_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},C_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
, which ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{C_{1},C_{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}

and let
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})\in\Delta
such that

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M_{[0,n)}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{f}
.
We have

(1)

if
M_{2n}=M_{2n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}

then
f_{2}=0
and
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})\leq 2
,

(2)

if
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=2
and
f_{2}=0
,
then
M_{2n}=M_{2n+1}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\mathbf{f}^{(k)}=(f^{(k)}_{1},f^{(k)}_{2},f^{(k)}_{3})=M_{[0,k)}^{-1}\mathbf{f}
.
The proof of the first item uses the same arguments as in the proof of Proposition
4.4
.

(i)
Note that,
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbf{f}^{(2k+2)}
is equal to one of the following two vectors:

(24)

\displaystyle C_{1}^{-2}\mathbf{f}^{(2k)}

\displaystyle=(f^{(2k)}_{1}-f^{(2k)}_{2}-f^{(2k)}_{3},f^{(2k)}_{2},f^{(2k)}_{3})\text{ or }

(25)

\displaystyle C_{2}^{-2}\mathbf{f}^{(2k)}

\displaystyle=(f^{(2k)}_{1},f^{(2k)}_{2},f^{(2k)}_{3}-f^{(2k)}_{1}-f^{(2k)}_{2}).

In both cases, the middle entry is unchanged.
Thus, by induction, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
f^{(2k)}_{2}=f_{2}
.
Also the sum of the two other entries decreases by at least
f_{2}
.
Therefore, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

0\leq f^{(2k)}_{1}+f^{(2k)}_{3}\leq f_{1}+f_{3}-kf_{2}

which implies that
f_{2}=0
.

(ii)
We do the proof by induction.
Suppose that
f_{2}^{(2k)}=0
.
We have that
\mathbf{f}^{(2k)}\in M_{2k}M_{2k+1}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}
.
If
M_{2k}M_{2k+1}=C_{1}C_{2}
and
\mathbf{f}^{(2k+2)}=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq 0
,
then
\mathbf{f}^{(2k)}=(\alpha+\beta,\beta+\gamma,\alpha)

so that
\beta=\gamma=0
and
f_{1}^{(2k)}=\alpha=f_{3}^{(2k)}
.
Thus we have
\mathbf{f}^{(2k+1)}=(0,\alpha,0)
, so that
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f}^{(2k+1)})=1
, which is a contradiction.
We similarly reach a contradiction when supposing
M_{2k}M_{2k+1}=C_{2}C_{1}
.
We thus obtain that

M_{2k}M_{2k+1}=C_{1}C_{1}
or
M_{2k}M_{2k+1}=C_{2}C_{2}
.
As in both cases we get, using (
24
) or (
25
),
f^{(2k+2)}_{2}=0
, this ends the proof.
∎

We now give the description of primitive sequences.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Statement (i)
follows from
Lemma
5.2
.
Statement (ii) follows from Statements (i) and (iii) and from Proposition
4.1
.
Let us thus show Statement (iii).

For every
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
\mathbf{f}^{(k)}=(f^{(k)}_{1},f^{(k)}_{2},f^{(k)}_{3})=M_{[0,k)}^{-1}\mathbf{f}
.
Let us assume that
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=3
.
If
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not primitive, then by Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
5.3
(i), there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f}^{(N)})\leq 2
.
As
\mathbf{f}=M_{[0,N)}\mathbf{f}^{(N)}
, we obtain
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})\leq 2
, which contradicts our hypothesis.

Let us now assume that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is primitive.
Using Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
5.2
(iii), we cannot have
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=1
.
So we assume
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})=2
.
Observe first that, if
\mathbf{f}^{(N)}
has a zero entry
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, then either
f_{2}^{(N)}=0
or
f_{2}^{(N+1)}=0
.
Then, since
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f}^{(N)})=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f}^{(N+1)})=\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})
, this would imply by Proposition
4.1
and Lemma
5.3
(ii) that
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not primitive, which is a contradiction.
From now on we assume that all entries of
\mathbf{f}^{(n)}

are positive for all
n
, and we show that we again reach a contradiction.

Since
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{f})<3
, there exists some integer vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
.
The sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be factored over

\{C_{1}C_{2}^{k}C_{1},C_{2}C_{1}^{k}C_{2}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}
by Lemma
4.2
.
Let us consider the sequence
(n_{m})
such that
n_{0}=0
and
M_{n_{m}}\ldots M_{n_{m+1}-1}

is in this set for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are unimodular, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}
is an integer vector and so
(\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}\|_{D})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is a nonnegative integer sequence.
In what follows, we reach a contradiction by showing that
(\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}\|_{D})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing and decreases infinitely often.

The proof that
(\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}\|_{D})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing is already done in the first part of the proof of Lemma
4.3
, observing that for all
m
,
\mathbf{f}^{(n_{m})}
has positive entries and
\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}\in(\mathbf{f}^{(n_{m})})^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}
.
Furthermore, if
\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}=(a,b,c)
, then
\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m})}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m})}\mathbf{v}\|_{D}=\max(|b-a|,|c-b|,|c-a|)
and, using Equation (
14
) and considering separately the even and odd cases (like in the proof of Lemma
4.3
), we get

\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n_{m+1})}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n_{m+1})}\mathbf{v}\|_{D}=\begin{cases}\max\{|b|,|c|,|c-b|\},&\text{if }M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\in\{C_{1}C_{2}^{k}C_{1}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\};\\
\max\{|a|,|b|,|a-b|\},&\text{if }M_{[n_{m},n_{m+1})}\in\{C_{2}C_{1}^{k}C_{2}\mid k\in\mathbb{N}\}.\end{cases}

Since

6.
Symbolic representation of
(\Delta,f_{C})

In this section, we prove Theorem

.
Let us first define the measure-preserving dynamical systems we are dealing with.

6.1.
Background on dynamical systems

Let
(X,\mathcal{B}_{X},\mu)
,
(Y,\mathcal{B}_{Y},\nu)
be two measured spaces.
A map
f:X\to Y
is
measure-preserving
if it is measurable and satisfies
\mu(f^{-1}(B))=\nu(B)
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
.
If furthermore,
f
is a bijection and
f^{-1}
is measurable, then
f^{-1}
is also measure-preserving.
In that case we say that
f
is an
invertible measure-preserving
map.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B}_{X},\mu)
be a measured space and
(Y,\mathcal{B}_{Y})
be a measurable space.
If
\pi:X\to Y
is measurable, then the
pushforward measure
on
Y
is the measure
\pi_{*}\mu
defined by
\pi_{*}\mu(B)=\mu(\pi^{-1}(B))
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
.
The map
\pi:(X,\mathcal{B}_{X},\mu)\to(Y,\mathcal{B}_{Y},\pi_{*}\mu)
is then measure-preserving.

A
measure-preserving dynamical system
is a tuple
(X,T,\mathcal{B},\mu)
, where
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a probability space and
T:X\to X
is measure-preserving.
We also say that the measure
\mu
is
T
-invariant
.
It is said to be
ergodic
if for every set

B\in\mathcal{B}
,
T^{-1}B=B
implies that
\mu(B)\in\{0,1\}
.

Two measure-preserving dynamical systems
(X,T,\mathcal{B}_{X},\mu)
,
(Y,S,\mathcal{B}_{Y},\nu)
are said to be
isomorphic
if there exist sets
\tilde{X}\in\mathcal{B}_{X},\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{B}_{Y}
of measure 1 such that
T(\tilde{X})\subset\tilde{X}
,
S(\tilde{Y})\subset\tilde{Y}
and an invertible measure-preserving map
\pi:\tilde{X}\to\tilde{Y}
such that
\pi\circ T(x)=S\circ\pi(x)
for all
x\in\tilde{X}
.
Such a map
\pi
is called an
isomorphism
.

In this paper, the measure-preserving dynamical system are always on a topological space
X
and we always consider the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on it, so we simply denote them by
(X,T,\mu)
.

6.2.
Dynamical systems associated with
f_{C}

Equipping
\Delta
with its natural Borel
\sigma
-algebra,
f_{C}:\Delta\to\Delta
is measurable.
Furthermore, the measure
\xi
defined
for any measurable set

by the density function

\xi(A)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\int_{A}\frac{1}{(1-x_{1})(1-x_{3})}dx_{1}dx_{3}

is
a

-invariant

[

]

a measure-preserving dynamical system. The reader may confirm that it is a probability measure by computing the following integral:

\displaystyle\xi(\Delta)

\displaystyle=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1-x_{1}}\frac{1}{(1-x_{1})(1-x_{3})}dx_{3}dx_{1}=1.

The measure

so it is the unique

-invariant probability measure which is equivalent to the Lebesgue measure on the simplex

The set

The shift map

For every

.
It is

For any

It is classical to show that any Bernoulli measure is ergodic.

We now show that

\displaystyle\pi((i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}})=\mathbf{f},\quad

\displaystyle\text{where }\bigcap_{n\geq 0}C_{i_{0}}C_{i_{1}}\cdots C_{i_{n}}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mathbf{f}

The map

We finally let

Proof of Theorem

The map

8.
Balance property

A word
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
is said to be
C
-balanced
or
balanced
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all words
u,v\in A^{*}
of the same length and occurring in
\mathbf{w}
, and for every letter
i\in A
,
||u|_{i}-|v|_{i}|\leq C
.
In this article, the notion of balance is thus more inclusive than what is
normally used in the context of Sturmian sequences
[
MH40
]
where
balanced sequences refer here to
1
-balanced sequences.
Assuming that an
\mathcal{S}
-adic word has uniform word frequencies, a sufficient condition for finite balance can be expressed using the incidence matrices of the directive sequence.

Theorem 8.1
.

[
BD14
, Theorem 5.8]

Let
(\sigma_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the directive sequence of an
\mathcal{S}
-adic representation of a word
\mathbf{w}
. For each
n
, let
M_{n}
be the incidence matrix of
\sigma_{n}
. Assume that
\mathbf{w}
has uniform letter frequencies and let
\mathbf{f}
be the letter frequencies vector. If

(27)

\sum_{n\geq 0}\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|\cdot\|M_{n}\|<\infty

for some norm
\|\cdot\|
, then the word
\mathbf{w}
is balanced.

Note that if the substitutions
\sigma_{n}
belong to a finite set, then the
norms
\|M_{n}\|
are uniformly bounded and can be removed from the sum.

Therefore we want to show that

\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|=\sup_{\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{f}^{\perp}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\mathbf{v}\|}{\|\mathbf{v}\|}.

converges to 0 as
n
goes to infinity fast enough so that the sum at
Equation (
27
) converges.
We achieve this in the current section using the semi-norm
\|\cdot\|_{D}
defined earlier.

The strategy that we use is inspired by Lemma 6 from Avila and
Delecroix
[
AD19
]
which provides sufficient conditions
so that the second Lyapunov exponent is negative and so that the associated words are balanced
[
BD14

Proof.

\mu

which implies that

\left\|\vphantom{C_{1}^{n}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!C_{1}^{n}\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\infty}=1

for all

.

Assume now that
\mu([1])
and
\mu([2])
are positive.
By ergodicity of
\mu
,
\mu
-almost every sequence
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contains infinitely many occurrences of
C_{1}
and of
C_{2}
.

4.2
, there is an increasing sequence

and

\mathbf{g}={M_{[0,n_{m})}^{-1}\mathbf{f}}

3.5
,

\displaystyle\left\|\vphantom{M_{[0,n)}}{}^{\mathsf{T}}\!M_{[0,n)}\middle|_{\mathbf{f}^{\perp}}\right\|_{\infty}

10.
Factor complexity

If
\mathbf{w}
is an infinite word over some alphabet
A
, we let

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}
denote the set of its factors, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}=\{u\in A^{*}\mid\exists i\in\mathbb{N}:\mathbf{w}_{i}\cdots\mathbf{w}_{i+|u|-1}=u\}
.
The
factor complexity
of
\mathbf{w}
is the function

p_{\mathbf{w}}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N},n\mapsto\#\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=\#(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}\cap A^{n}).

An infinite word
\mathbf{w}
is said to be
uniformly recurrent
if for all
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}
,
u
occurs infinitely many times in
\mathbf{w}
and the gap between two successive occurrences is bounded.
It is classical to prove that every primitive
\mathcal{S}
-adic word is uniformly recurrent.

In this section, we study the factor complexity of
\mathcal{C}
-adic words.
In particular, we prove the following result, which ends the proof of Theorem
B
.
It is worth noticing the analogy with Theorem
5.1
.

Theorem 10.1
.

Let
\mathbf{w}
be a
\mathcal{C}
-adic word with directive sequence
(c_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(1)

there exists
k\geq 1
such that
p_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=k
for all large enough
n

if and only if

(c_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\{c_{1},c_{2}\}^{*}\{c_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},c_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
.

(2)

there exists
k\geq 1
such that
p_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=n+k
for all large enough
n

if and only if

(c_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\left(\{c_{1},c_{2}\}^{*}\{c_{1}^{2},c_{2}^{2}\}^{\mathbb{N}}\right)\setminus\{c_{1},c_{2}\}^{*}\{c_{1}^{\mathbb{N}},c_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}\}
.

(3)

p_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=2n+1
for all
n

if and only
if
(c_{i_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is primitive.
In particular, this is also equivalent to the fact that
\mathbf{w}
is a uniformly recurrent dendric word (see Section
10.1
for the definition).

The proof essentially consists in studying the bispecial factors of
\mathcal{C}
-adic words.

10.1.
Bispecial factors and extension sets

For every infinite word
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
and every factor
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}
, we set

\displaystyle E^{-}(u,\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\{a\in A\mid au\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}\};

\displaystyle E^{+}(u,\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\{b\in A\mid ub\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}\};

\displaystyle E(u,\mathbf{w})

\displaystyle=\{(a,b)\in A\times A\mid aub\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}\}.

The set
E(u,\mathbf{w})
is called the
extension set
of
u
in
\mathbf{w}
.
We represent it by an array of the form

E(u,\mathbf{w})\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&\cdots&j&\cdots\\[-1.0pt]
\hline\cr\vdots&&&\\[-1.0pt]
i&&\times\\[-1.0pt]
\vdots&&&\end{array},

where a symbol
\times
in position
(i,j)
means that
(i,j)
belongs to
E(u,\mathbf{w})
.

The elements of
E^{-}(u,\mathbf{w})
,
E^{+}(u,\mathbf{w})
and
E(u,\mathbf{w})
are respectively called the
left extensions
, the
right extensions
and the
biextensions
of
u
in
\mathbf{w}
.
When the context is clear, we will omit the information on
\mathbf{w}
and simply write
E^{-}(u)
,
E^{+}(u)
and
E(u)
.
The word
u
is said to be
left special
if
\#E^{-}(u)>1
,
right special
if
\#E^{+}(u)>1
and
bispecial
if it is both left special and right special.

The factor complexity of an infinite word is completely governed by the biextensions of its bispecial factors
[
CN10
]
.
In particular, we have the following result.

Proposition 10.2
.

[
CN10
, Proposition 4.5.3]

Let
\mathbf{w}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
be an infinite word.
If for every bispecial factor
u
, one has

(29)

\#E(u)-\#E^{-}(u)-\#E^{+}(u)+1=0,

then
p_{\mathbf{w}}(n)=(p_{\mathbf{w}}(1)-1)n+1
for every
n
.

Equation (
29
) is in particular satisfied when there exists
(a,b)\in E(u)
such that
E(u)\subset(\{a\}\times A)\cup(A\times\{b\})
.
Such a bispecial factor is said to be
ordinary
.
On our tabular representation, this means that the biextensions form a cross as follows:

E(u)\quad=\quad\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&&\cdots&b&\cdots&\\[-1.0pt]
\hline\cr&&&\times&&\\
\vdots&&&\vdots&&\\
a&\times&\cdots&\times&\cdots\times\\[-1.0pt]
\vdots&&&\vdots&&\\
&&&\times&&\end{array}.

Another representation of the extension set of a word
u\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathbf{w}}
is given by the
extension graph
of
u
.
It is the undirected bipartite graph whose set of vertices is the disjoint union of
E^{-}(u)
and
E^{+}(u)
and whose set of edges is
E(u)
.
A bispecial factor
u
is said to be
dendric
whenever its extension graph is a tree.
Dendric bispecial factors thus also satisfy Equation (
29
).
Infinite words for which all bispecial factors are dendric are also called
dendric
and were recently introduced under the name of tree sets
[
BDFD+15
]
.
This family of words contains Arnoux-Rauzy words, codings of regular interval exchange and more
[
GLL21
]
.

11.
Conjugacy with a semi-sorted version of Selmer algorithm

The Selmer algorithm
[
Sel61
,
Sch00
]
(also called the
GMA algorithm

[
Bal92
]
) is an algorithm which subtracts the smallest entry to the largest.
As observed in
[
Pan08
]
,
Selmer algorithm is also conjugate on the absorbing simplex to
Mönkemeyer’s algorithm
[
Mön54
]
which makes it an algorithm which have
been rediscovered many times.
We prove in this section that
F_{C}
also belongs to this family.

As recalled in Section
9
, the numerical computation of Lyapunov exponents
[
Lab15
]
indicates that exponents for the Selmer algorithm and
F_{C}
have
statistically equal values (the difference is at most
10^{-5}
).
We confirm this observation by showing
a relation between
F_{C}
and the Selmer algorithm.
The map
F_{C}
is not conjugate to the Selmer algorithm, however,
we show that
F_{C}
is conjugate to a semi-sorted version of the Selmer
algorithm which keeps the largest entry at index
1
.
We also show that the application of this semi-sorted Selmer algorithm on its absorbing subset defines
\mathcal{S}
-adic subshifts that actually are images of
\mathcal{C}
-adic subshifts by a permutation of the alphabet.

On
\Theta=\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}\mid\max(x_{2},x_{3})\leq x_{1}\}
, the semi-sorted version of Selmer algorithm is defined by

F_{S}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})=\begin{cases}(x_{2},x_{1}-x_{3},x_{3})&\mbox{if }x_{1}\leq x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}\geq x_{3},\\
(x_{3},x_{2},x_{1}-x_{2})&\mbox{if }x_{1}\leq x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}<x_{3},\\
(x_{1}-x_{3},x_{3},x_{2})&\mbox{if }x_{1}>x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}\geq x_{3},\\
(x_{1}-x_{2},x_{3},x_{2})&\mbox{if }x_{1}>x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}<x_{3}.\end{cases}

Like with
F_{C}
, we consider the partition

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\Theta\mid x_{1}\leq x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}\geq x_{3}\},

\displaystyle\Theta_{2}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\Theta\mid x_{1}\leq x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}<x_{3}\},

\displaystyle\Theta_{3}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\Theta\mid x_{1}>x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}\geq x_{3}\},

\displaystyle\Theta_{4}

\displaystyle=\{(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\Theta\mid x_{1}>x_{2}+x_{3}\text{ and }x_{2}<x_{3}\}

and the matrices

S_{1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&1&1\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\end{array}\right),\quad S_{2}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}0&1&1\\
0&1&0\\
1&0&0\end{array}\right)\quad S_{3}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right),\quad S_{4}=\left(\begin{array}[]{rrr}1&0&1\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{array}\right).

The map
F_{S}
is then defined by
F_{S}(\mathbf{x})=S_{i}^{-1}\mathbf{x}
whenever
\mathbf{x}\in\Theta_{i}
.
The Selmer algorithm being weakly convergent
[
Sch00
]
, there is a continuous map
\pi:\{1,2,3,4\}^{\mathbb{N}}\to\Theta
defined by

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}S_{i_{0}}S_{i_{1}}\cdots S_{i_{n}}\mathbb{R}^{3}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\pi((i_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}).

Note that if
\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{x})=3

n
,
F_{S}^{n}(\mathbf{x})
belongs to
\Gamma=\Theta_{1}\cup\Theta_{2}
.
Therefore, if
\mu
is a shift-invariant ergodic measure on
\{1,2,3,4\}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\pi_{*}\mu(\{\mathbf{x}\in\Theta\mid\mathrm{dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbf{x})=3\})=1

\mu([3])=\mu([4])=0
.
To compute the Lyapunov exponents associated with such a measure, we may thus restrict the Selmer algorithm to the absorbing set
\Gamma
.
The next result shows that
F_{C}
and

(restricted to
