On the Error-Propagation of Inexact Hotelling’s Deflation for Principal Component Analysis
Fangshuo Liao, Junhyung Lyle Kim, Cruz Barnum, Anastasios Kyrillidis

Abstract
Principal Component Analysis (PCA) aims to find subspaces spanned by the so-called principal components that best represent the variance in the dataset. The deflation method is a popular meta-algorithm that sequentially finds individual principal components, starting from the most important ones and working towards the less important ones. However, as deflation proceeds, numerical errors from the imprecise estimation of principal components propagate due to its sequential nature. This paper mathematically characterizes the error propagation of the inexact Hotelling’s deflation method. We consider two scenarios: OPEN i ) i) when the sub-routine for finding the leading eigenvector is abstract and can represent various algorithms; and OPEN i ​ i ) ii) when power iteration is used as the sub-routine. In the latter case, the additional directional information from power iteration allows us to obtain a tighter error bound than the sub-routine agnostic case. For both scenarios, we explicitly characterize how the errors progress and affect subsequent principal component estimations.

Introduction

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)
(
Pearson 1901
;
Hotelling 1933
)
is a fundamental tool for data analysis with applications that range from statistics to machine learning and can be used for dimensionality reduction, classification, and clustering, to name a few
(
Majumdar 2009
;
Wang et al. 2013
;
d’Aspremont et al. 2007
;
Jiang et al. 2011
;
Zou et al. 2006
)
.
Since its proposition
Pearson 1901
, many algorithms for PCA have been devised, even recently
(
Allen-Zhu & Li 2017
)
, to efficiently find approximations of the first principal component, which is a vector that captures the most variance in the data. Mathematically, single-component PCA can be described as:

\vskip-5.69046pt{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*}=\texttt{PCA}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{v}\|_{2}=1\\
\end{subarray}}{\arg\max}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{v},\vskip-2.84544pt

(1)

where
\bm{\Sigma}=\tfrac{1}{n}\cdot\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the empirical covariance matrix given a set of
n
centered
d
-dimensional data-points
{\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}
.

. As the number of power iteration steps increases (

It is sometimes helpful to know not only the first but also the top-
K
principal components, especially in the high-dimensional regime.
This is becoming more relevant in the modern machine learning era, where the number of features reaches as large as billions
(
Fedus et al. 2022
;
Touvron et al. 2023
)
.
Mathematically, the multi-component PCA (
K\geq 1
) can be formulated as the following constrained optimization problem:

\vskip-5.69046pt{\mathbf{U}}^{*}=\underset{{\mathbf{V}}\in\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{:,:K}:\;{\mathbf{Q}}\in\text{SO}(d)\}}{\arg\max}\left\langle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V},\mathbf{V}\right\rangle,\vskip-2.84544pt

(2)

where
\text{SO}(d)
denotes the group of rotations about a fixed point in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space.
Since
{\mathbf{U}}^{*}
contains the first
K
columns of an orthogonal matrix, each principal component
{\mathbf{U}}_{:,k}
must be a unit vector, and the pairwise principal components, say
{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{:,k_{1}},{\mathbf{U}}^{*}_{:,k_{2}}
for
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
, are orthogonal.
A popular method for solving (
2
) is the
deflation method

(
Hotelling 1933
)

via sequentially solving
(
1
), which is the focus of our work.

Deflation proceeds as follows.
After approximating the top component
\mathbf{u}_{1}^{*}
, the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
is further processed to exist on the subspace that is orthogonal to the subspace which is spanned by the first component. This process is repeated by applying single-component PCA in (
1
) on the deflated matrix, which leads to an approximation of the second component
\mathbf{u}_{2}^{*}
, and so on, as below:

\begin{gathered}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma};\quad{\mathbf{v}}_{k}=\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},t\right);\\
\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-{\mathbf{v}}_{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}^{\top},\end{gathered}

(3)

where
\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},\cdot\right)
returns a normalized approximation of the top eigenvector of the deflated matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
.
One estimates the subsequent principal components by running the same single-component PCA algorithm repetitively.

In this work, we focus on the more realistic scenario where the sub-routine,
\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},\cdot\right)
, incurs numerical errors due to limited computation budget and finite precision: even solving (
1
) for a single component, our estimate is only an approximation to the true principal component.
The overall procedure with inexact
\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},\cdot\right)
sub-routine is described in Algorithm
1
.
Line 4 performs the sub-routine algorithm for
t
iterations, denoted by
\texttt{PCA}(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},t)
, which approximately computes the top eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
.
In line 5,
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
is deflated using
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
; hence, the numerical error from approximately solving
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
in line 4 affects the quality of the deflated matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}
in the next iteration.

To motivate why studying the accumulation of errors is critical, we apply spectral clustering
(
Pothen et al. 1990
)
on the MNIST dataset and measure its performance using the mutual information metric

Spectral clustering involves computing the top-
K
eigenvectors from a similarity matrix and uses the corresponding entries in the
K
eigenvectors as features for the following clustering procedure.
In our experiments, eigenvectors are computed using the deflation in Algorithm
1
with different precision levels of the

We can observe from Figure

This shows that the errors incurred from inexactly solving each top eigenvector in the deflation process indeed have a non-negligible impact on the downstream machine learning tasks.

This interdependent accumulation of errors coming from approximately solving the sub-routine –which results in the less accurate deflation matrix, and further affects the quality of the approximation of the top eigenvector in the next iteration– is precisely what we characterize in this work.
To the best of our knowledge,
this is the first work that analyzes the inexact setting, although this procedure is vastly used in practice.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

In Theorem
3.1
, we characterize how the errors from approximately solving the sub-routine (
PCA
) propagate into the overall error of the deflation procedure. Informally, the error between the approximate principal component and the actual one needs to be exponentially small to control the accuracy for the top-
K
eigenvectors for an increasing
K
, where the base of the exponential growth depends on the eigengap of the target matrix.

•

In Theorem
4.1
, we consider the case where
PCA
is fixed to the power iteration
(
Müntz et al. 1913
)
. By leveraging the directional information of the numerical error vector produced by each power iteration, we improve the error bound in Theorem
3.1
(which is agnostic to the

•

Along with the proof, we also present empirical results to justify the core ideas in our theoretical analysis.

Related work.

Most works focus on the convergence proof of numerically solving the top eigenvector of matrices
(
Lanczos 1950
;
Jain et al. 2016
;
Xu et al. 2018b
)
.
However, these results only concern the case of the first eigenvector, and their result does not readily apply to numerical algorithms, such as deflation, that solve for multiple top eigenvectors.

Iterative methods for multi-component PCA include
Schur complement deflation

(
Zhang 2006
)
,
orthogonalized deflation

(
Sriperumbudur et al. 2007
)
, and
projection methods

(
Saad 1988
)
.
Among them, Hotelling’s deflation, our focus, was reported to have the highest accuracy in practice
(
Danisman et al. 2014
)
.
For the comparison of these methods, we refer the readers to
Mackey 2008
, which extends some of the above deflation methods to the sparse PCA setting
(
Zou et al. 2006
;
Johnstone & Lu 2009
)
, which is out of scope of our work. Importantly, all the works mentioned above assume the
PCA
step in (
3
) is solved exactly, i.e.,
t=\infty
. Works that analyze the resulting error when solving multiple top eigenvectors include
Allen-Zhu & Li 2017
;
Gemp et al. 2021
. However, the theoretical analysis in
Allen-Zhu & Li 2017
and
Gemp et al. 2021
only applies to specific algorithms such as EigenGame and Oja++.
At the same time, our work focuses on deflation, a widely used algorithmic framework for solving multiple eigenvectors.

Subspace methods such as orthogonal iterations
(
Golub & Van Loan 2013
)
are also widely used for discovering multiple eigenvectors. However, the deflation serves a different purpose than subspace methods. In particular, the sequential discovery of the eigenvectors in the deflation methods applies to scenarios where users have a dynamic necessity of the eigenvectors and require flexibility, especially in modern ML settings
(
Fawzi et al. 2022
;
Coulaud et al. 2013
)
. For instance, compared with orthogonal iteration, which outputs all
k
eigenvectors at the same time, the deflation method will be more suitable for scenarios where the top principal component is known but more eigenvectors need to be computed depending on the need, and scenarios where eigenvectors are output in a consecutive way to facilitate downstream tasks.

In terms of the analysis technique, our work utilizes Weyl’s inequality
(
Weyl 1912
)
and Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
Theorem
(
Davis & Kahan 1970
)
to characterize the differences between eigenvalues of two matrices. In our fine-grained analysis in Section
4
, we also utilize the Neumann expansion introduced by
Eldridge et al. 2017
and characterize the subspace that the top eigenvector of a perturbed matrix lies in with a similar technique to
Chen et al. 2020
. However, the problem they study is entirely orthogonal to ours.

Problem Setup and Notations

Notation.
For a vector
{\mathbf{a}}
, we use
\left\|{\mathbf{a}}\right\|_{2}
to denote its
\ell_{2}
-norm. For a matrix
{\mathbf{A}}
,
\left\|{\mathbf{A}}\right\|_{2}
to denote its spectral norm and
\left\|{\mathbf{A}}\right\|_{F}
its Frobenius norm.
\lambda_{j}\left({\mathbf{A}}\right)
denotes its
j
-th eigenvalue.

Problem setup.

Given a symmetric matrix
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with eigenvectors
{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{*},\dots{\mathbf{u}}_{d}^{*}
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{*},\dots,\lambda_{d}^{*}
, sorted in descending order (c.f., Assumption
2.1
), we want to estimate the top
K\leq d
eigenvectors using the deflation method.
Formally, let
PCA
be a sub-routine that returns an approximation of the top eigenvector of a given matrix.
The deflation method repeatedly computes approximation
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
of the principal eigenvector of the current deflation matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
.
It forms a new deflation matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}
by subtracting the component of
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
from
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
.
This process is made formal in Algorithm
1
and is mathematically defined in (
3
).
If the sub-routine
PCA
is solved precisely, then the output satisfies
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}={\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
.
In this ideal scenario, the sequence of deflation matrices has the following form:

\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k+1}=\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*\top}\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*\top};\quad\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}.

(4)

We term the scenario in (
4
) as
ideal deflation
and the matrices
\left\{\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\}_{k=1}^{d}
as
“ground-truth” deflation matrices
.
The ideal deflation has the nice property that
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
is the top eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
.
Therefore, solving for the top-
K
eigenvectors can be exactly reduced to repeatedly solving for the top-
1
eigenvector of the “ground-truth” deflation matrices.

Yet, solving for the top eigenvector exactly is challenging, which makes the ideal deflation almost impossible in practice.
Thus, we are interested in a more general case, where
PCA
computes the top-eigenvector
inexactly
, and results in numerical error
\bm{\delta}_{k}
, as defined below:

\begin{gathered}\bm{\delta}_{k}:=\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},t\right)-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}={\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k};~~\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}>0.\end{gathered}

(5)

Here,
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
is the top eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
; recall that
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
in (
3
) is constructed recursively using
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
.
When
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
is solved inexactly, we cannot guarantee that
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}=\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
.
Consequently, it is almost always the case that the top eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
does not equal to
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
, namely
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}>0
.
However, since
PCA
only knows
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
, its output
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
converges to
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
instead of
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
when
t
is large.
This difference between the top eigenvector of the matrices
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
further complicates the analysis of the deflation process in (
3
), since
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
returned by
\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},t\right)
approximates
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
instead of
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
, which further builds up the difference between
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
and thus the subsequent
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}^{*}\right\|_{F}
.

Our work aims to characterize this complicated error propagation through the deflation steps.
To build up our analysis, we make the following assumption on the matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The matrix
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a real symmetric matrix with eigenvalues and eigenvectors
\{\lambda_{i}^{*},{\mathbf{u}}_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{d}
, satisfying
1=\lambda_{1}^{*}>\lambda_{2}^{*}>\dots>\lambda_{d}^{*}>0
.

Assumption
2.1
states the real symmetric matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
has strictly decaying eigenvalues.
Observing such phenomenon is quite common in practice; see
Papailiopoulos et al. 2014
,
Papyan 2020
, and
Goujaud et al. 2022
for instance.
Further,
\lambda_{1}^{*}=1
is not strictly required but assumed without loss of generality.

Assumption
2.1
guarantees that
\bm{\Sigma}
is positive definite.
By construction, all
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
’s are guaranteed to be positive semi-definite.
To enforce a similar condition on
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
’s, we need to characterize the difference between the eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
: for
\lambda_{k}
being the top eigenvalue of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
, we need to control
\big|\lambda_{k}^{*}-\lambda_{k}\big|
.
This is possible by Weyl’s inequality:

Lemma 2.2

(Weyl’s Inequality
(
Weyl 1912
)
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}},{\mathbf{M}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be real symmetric matrices. Let
\sigma_{j},\sigma_{j}^{*}
be the
j
-th eigenvalue of
{\mathbf{M}}
and
{\mathbf{M}}^{*}
, respectively. Then:

\left|\sigma_{j}^{*}-\sigma_{j}\right|\leq\left\|{\mathbf{M}}^{*}-{\mathbf{M}}\right\|_{2}.

A direct consequence of Weyl’s inequality to our scenario is that
\big|\lambda_{k}^{*}-\lambda_{k}\big|\leq\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
.

Another tool that we will utilize in the following sections of our paper is the Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
Theorem:

Lemma 2.3

(
\sin\Theta
Theorem
(
Davis & Kahan 1970
)
)
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and let
{\mathbf{M}}={\mathbf{M}}^{*}+{\mathbf{H}}
. Let
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{a}}_{1}
be the top eigenvectors of
{\mathbf{M}}^{*}
and
{\mathbf{M}}
, respectively. Then we have:

\sin\angle\left\{{\mathbf{a}}_{1}^{*},{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right\}\leq\frac{\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{2}}{\min_{j\neq k}\left|\sigma_{k}^{*}-\sigma_{j}\right|}.

3
Sub-routine Agnostic Error Propagation

In this section, we aim to provide a characterization of the error propagation in the deflation methods in (
3
) that is agnostic to the detail of the sub-routine
PCA
.
In particular, the only information known about
PCA
is the magnitude of the error of
PCA
, namely
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}=\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\texttt{PCA}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k},t\right)-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\|_{2}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider the scenario of looking for the top-
K
eigenvectors of
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and
suppose
\bm{\Sigma}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{*}-\lambda_{j+1}^{*}
, for
j\in[d-1]
, and
\mathcal{T}_{d}=\lambda_{d}^{*}
.
Also, let
\mathcal{T}_{K,\min}=\min_{k\in[K]}\mathcal{T}_{k}
, and let
\bm{\delta}_{k}
and
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
be defined in (
5
).
If
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}
’s are small enough such that

\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{*}\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k-1}\left(3+\frac{2\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{20}\mathcal{T}_{K,\min}.

(6)

then the output of Algorithm
1
satisfies for all
k\in[K]
:

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}+\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 5\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}\frac{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{*}}{\lambda_{k}^{*}}\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(3+\frac{2\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}\right).

(7)

Remark 1.
Theorem
3.1
characterizes how the errors from approximately solving the sub-routine (
PCA
) propagate into the overall error of the deflation procedure.
The condition in (
6
) is to make sure that the difference between the “ground-truth” deflated matrices in (
4
) and the empirical ones in (
3
) is controlled and is sufficiently small, i.e.,
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k}\right\|_{F}\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\mathcal{T}_{k,\min}
for all
k\in[K]
. As a simplification, this condition can be guaranteed as long as

\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{\mathcal{T}_{K,\min}}{20K}\prod_{j=k+1}^{K-1}\left(3+\tfrac{2\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}\right)^{-1}.\vskip-5.69046pt

Remark 2.
Notice that the error upper bound in (
3.1
) takes the form of a summation over components that depend on the error of the sub-routine
\left\{\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}\right\}_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}
. In particular, not only do the number of summands grow as
k
grows, but each summand also has a multiplicative factor of
\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(3+\tfrac{2\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}\right)
which grows as
k
becomes larger. When the eigen spectrum of
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}
decays slowly, this multiplicative factor can grow near factorially. For instance, in the case of a power-law decay spectrum where
\lambda_{j}=\tfrac{1}{j}
, the multiplicative factor becomes
\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(3+2j\right)
, since
\mathcal{T}_{j}=\tfrac{1}{j(j+1)}
. In this case, the errors
\left\{\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}\right\}_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}
need to be nearly factorially small as we attempt to solve for more eigenvectors. To be more specific, based on Theorem
3.1
, to guarantee that
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
, it is
necessary
to have:

\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k^{\prime}}\right\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{k}^{*}}{5\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{*}}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(3+\frac{2\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}\right)^{-1}.

The corollary below states a
sufficient
condition to guarantee that
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\in[K]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Consider the scenario of solving the top-
K
eigenvectors of
\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and
suppose
\bm{\Sigma}
satisfies Assumption
2.1
.
Let
\mathcal{T}_{j}=\lambda_{j}^{*}-\lambda_{j+1}^{*}
for
j\in[d-1]
, and
\mathcal{T}_{d}=\lambda_{d}^{*}
.
Also let
\mathcal{T}_{K,\min}=\min_{k\in[K]}\mathcal{T}_{k}
.
Finally, let
\bm{\delta}_{i}
and
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
be defined as in (
5
).
If for all
k\in[K]
it holds that:

\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\tfrac{\min\left\{\varepsilon\lambda_{K}^{*},\mathcal{T}_{K,\min}\right\}}{20K}\prod_{j=k+1}^{K}\left(3+\frac{2\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}\right)^{-1},

(8)

then the output of Algorithm
1
satisfies
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\in[K]
.

Remark 3.

Corollary
3.2
characterizes how accurately the sub-routine (
PCA
) has to be solved to achieve
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
,
\forall k\in[K]
, i.e., the desired accuracy of the overall deflation procedure.

Below, we provide lower bounds on the number of sub-routine iterations to satisfy (
8
), assuming a linearly-converging sub-routine with convergence rate
\alpha_{k}
exists. In Section
4
, we improve this rate by specifically setting the sub-routine to be the
power iteration

(
Müntz et al. 1913
)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

Under the same assumptions as Corollary
3.2
, we further assume a sub-routine exists for computing the top eigenvalue with linear convergence rate
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
.
If
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq c_{0}\cdot\alpha_{k}^{t}
for some constant
c_{0}>0
for all
k\in[K]
, where
t
is the number of steps in the sub-routine satisfying:

t\geq\Omega\left(\frac{\log\frac{c_{0}K}{\min\left\{\varepsilon\lambda_{k}^{*},\mathcal{T}_{K,\min}\right\}}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{K}\log\left(\frac{\lambda_{j}^{*}}{\mathcal{T}_{j}}+1\right)}{\log\alpha_{k}^{-1}}\right),

(9)

then the output of Algorithm
1
satisfies
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\in[K]
.

Remark 4.

Corollary
3.3
is constructed by further assuming that
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq c_{0}\cdot\alpha_{k}^{t}
based on Corollary
3.2
and derive the lower bound on
t
. The linear convergence property of solving the top eigenvector is shared among multiple existing algorithms
(
Xu et al. 2018a
;
Golub & Van Loan 1996
)
. In particular, when
PCA
is chosen to be the power iteration, we can show that in our setting
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq c_{0}\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\right)}{\lambda_{k}}\right)^{t}
. In the case of a power-law decay spectrum, when we treat
c_{0}
as constants, the bound in (
9
) can be simplified to

t\geq\Omega\left(\log\frac{K}{\varepsilon}+\sum_{j=k+1}^{K}\log\left(j+1\right)\right).\vskip-5.69046pt

Applying a slightly loose bound over the summation, we can see that based on the analysis of Theorem
3.1
, one needs
t\geq\Omega\left(\log\frac{K}{\varepsilon}+K\log K\right)
to achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy. Section
4
compares this rate with the lower bound we obtain when
PCA
is fixed to power iteration.

3.1
Proof Overview

The proof involves bounding
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
and
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k}\right\|_{F}
.
The former quantifies the difference between the true
k
-th eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}
and the leading eigenvector of the
k
-th deflation step
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
.
The latter quantifies the difference between the “ground-truth” deflation matrices defined in (
4
) and the ones in practice. Recall that
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
is the top eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
(c.f., Lemma
C.1
). Let
\texttt{nev}_{1}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\right)
denote a normalized top eigenvector of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
. Since both
\texttt{nev}_{1}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\right)
and
-\texttt{nev}_{1}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\right)
are unit-norm eigenvectors of
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
, we will choose
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
to be the one such that
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\geq 0
:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{u}}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\underset{s\in\{\pm 1\}}{\arg\min}\left\|s\cdot\texttt{nev}_{1}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\right)-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\cdot\texttt{nev}_{1}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{k}\right).

(10)

The difference between
{\mathbf{v}}_{k}
, the
k
-th approximate eigenvector returned by Algorithm
1
, and
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
, the
k
-th ground-truth eigenvector, consists of two components:
i)

\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
, the difference between the top eigenvectors of the empirical deflated matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
and the “ground-truth” deflated matrix
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
; and
ii)

\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\|_{2}
, the numerical error from not solving the sub-routine
\texttt{PCA}(\cdot)
exactly.
We can decompose the difference
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
in the following sense:

\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\underbrace{\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}\right\|_{2}}_{:=\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}}+\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

(11)

where the last equality follows from the definition in (
5
). The norm of
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}
depends largely on the sub-routine
PCA
and can be controlled as long as
t
, the number of sub-routine iterations, is large enough. Therefore, our analysis will mainly focus on upper-bounding
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
.
Intuitively,
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
depends on the difference between
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}
. As a building block of our proof, we will first provide a characterization of
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose that
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{6}
for all
k\in[K-1]
, then we have that for all
k\in[K-1]
,

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}^{*}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq 3\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}+5\lambda_{k}^{*}\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}

(12)

\displaystyle+2\lambda_{k}^{*}\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}.

Lemma
3.4
upper-bounds
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}^{*}\right\|_{F}
in terms of
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F},\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
, and
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}
. To establish an recursive characterization of
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}
, we need to obtain an upper bound for
\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
. Indeed, this can be easily obtained by applying the Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
theorem
(
Davis & Kahan 1970
;
Eldridge et al. 2017
)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{k}:=\min_{j\neq k}\left|\lambda_{k}^{*}-\lambda_{j}^{*}\right|
. If
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{T}_{k}
, then we shall have that

\left\|{\mathbf{u}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{2}{\mathcal{T}_{k}}\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}.

(13)

Plugging (
13
) into (
12
) gives a recurrence that depends purely on
\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}
and the spectrum of
\bm{\Sigma}
:

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k+1}^{*}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\left(3+\frac{2}{\mathcal{T}_{k}}\right)\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+5\lambda_{k}^{*}\left\|\bm{\delta}_{k}\right\|_{2}.

Unrolling this recurrence gives a closed-form upper bound for
\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k}-\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{F}
. Combining this upper bound with (
13
) and plugging the result into (
11
) gives us an upper bound for
\left\|{\mathbf{v}}_{k}-{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}
.

\left|\bm{\delta}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k+j-1}\right|\leq c_{0}\left(\tfrac{\lambda_{j}\left(\Sigma\right)_{k}}{\lambda_{k}^{*}}\right)^{t}

What Does Power Iteration Tell Us?

Recall our previous observation that the error vector returned by power iteration has different magnitudes along different eigenvector directions of the matrix of interest.
In this section, we perform the same analysis, assuming that the matrix passed into the power iteration sub-routine is a perturbation of some “ground-truth” matrix. Let

{\mathbf{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Let
\sigma_{i}^{*},{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{*}
be the
i
-th eigenvalue and eigenvector of
{\mathbf{M}}^{*}

be the
i
-th eigenvalue and eigenvector of
{\mathbf{M}}
. Then, for all

with

, we have:

{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}^{*}=\frac{{\mathbf{a}}_{i}^{\top}{\mathbf{H}}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}^{*}}{\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{j}^{*}}.

Unfortunately, we cannot directly characterize
{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{\top}{\mathbf{u}}_{k}^{*}
using Lemma

by plugging in

and

Indeed, going from Lemma

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose that

5
Conclusion and Future Work

Numerical algorithms for solving the top eigenvector of a given matrix often produce non-negligible errors, which will propagate and accumulate through multiple deflation steps. In this paper, we mathematically analyze this error propagation for a scenario agnostic to the sub-routine for solving the top eigenvectors and the case where this sub-routine is fixed to power iteration. In particular, for the sub-routine agnostic case, our analysis gives an exponential growth of the errors as one aims to solve more eigenvectors, where the base of the exponential growth depends on the eigengap. This upper bound on the errors is improved to exponential growth with a constant base by utilizing the directional information of the error vector produced by power iteration. Our result implies a lower bound on the number of power iteration steps required to solve for multiple leading eigenvectors using deflation up to a certain accuracy.

This paper focuses on the case where
\bm{\Sigma}
is positive definite and has distinct eigenvalues. However, the analysis can also be extended to accommodate repeated and zero eigenvalues. When eigenvalues are repeated, the corresponding eigenvector and the method for measuring the error of the approximate eigenvector need to be redefined. Instead of considering a single eigenvector for each eigenvalue, we can generalize the approach by considering the subspace spanned by the eigenvectors corresponding to each repeated eigenvalue. The core ideas of our analysis can be applied to account for the error propagation between subspaces, but one must account for additional error propagation within subspaces. To extend the analysis to positive semi-definite matrices,
\bm{\Sigma}
is of rank
r
and we are interested in finding top-
K
eigenvectors with
K\leq r
. In this case, our Theorem
3.1
still holds, as it does not depend on eigenvalues beyond the
K
th. Theorem
4.1
may require slight modification, but we hypothesize that the case where
\bm{\Sigma}
is low rank will relax the condition on
t
in (
16
) to
k\leq r
instead of
k\leq d
.

While this paper focuses on the sub-routine agnostic scenario and the power iteration, future work can consider extending the analysis to the case where other algorithms, such as Lanczos method
(
Golub & Van Loan 1996
)
and power iteration with momentum
(
Xu et al. 2018a
)
, are used to approximate the top eigenvectors in the deflation process.

Impact Statement

This paper aims to deepen the theoretical understanding of principal component analysis, a long-standing method in machine learning. The theoretical results presented herein may change the computation power used for principal component analysis. While the direct impact of this work may be limited to computational efficiency gains, the broader implications extend beyond this, potentially catalyzing advancements in various domains where principal component analysis finds application. However, no specific societal consequences can be directly attributed to the work itself.

Lemma C.1
.

Let

be defined in (
4

\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{*\top};\quad\bm{\Sigma}_{k}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{*}=\begin{cases}\lambda_{\ell}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{*}&\text{if }{\ell}\geq k\\

\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{k}=\sum_{j=k}^{d}\lambda_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{*}{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{*\top}

\bm{0}&\text{if }{\ell}<k\end{cases}

∎

\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|=\frac{\left|\hat{{\mathbf{x}}}_{t}\top{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|}{\left\|{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}}\leq\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{t}\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|

Since
\left\{{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{d}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we must have that
\left\|{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right)^{2}
. Thus

\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right)^{2}=1-\sum_{j=2}^{d}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right)^{2}\geq 1-\sum_{j=2}^{d}\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{2t}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right)^{2}\geq 1-\left(\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{2t}

where the last inequality follows from
\sigma_{j}\leq\sigma_{2}
for
j\geq 2
and
\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right)^{2}=\left\|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}^{2}=1
. Therefore, we can conclude that

\min_{s\in\{\pm 1\}}\left\|s\cdot{\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(1-\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right)^{2}\right)\leq 2\left(\frac{\sigma_{2}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{2t}

Taking a square root for both sides gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Consider a sequence of quantities
\left\{Q_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

Q_{k}=a_{k}+b_{k}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}Q_{k^{\prime}}

with some
a_{k},b_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Setting
Q_{1}=a_{1}
, then we shall have that

Q_{k}=a_{k}+b_{k}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}a_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=k^{\prime}+1}^{k-1}\left(1+b_{\ell}\right)

Proof.

We shall prove by induction start from
Q_{1}=a_{1}
. Suppose that
Q_{1},\dots Q_{k}
satisfies the characterization. To start, we can see that

\displaystyle\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}Q_{k^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{b_{k}}\left(Q_{k}-a_{k}\right)=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}a_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=k^{\prime}+1}^{k-1}\left(1+b_{\ell}\right)

Then, by the inductive hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}Q_{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=Q_{k}+\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}Q_{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=a_{k}+\left(b_{k}+1\right)\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}a_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=k^{\prime}+1}^{k-1}\left(1+b_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=a_{k}+\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}a_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(1+b_{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}a_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(1+b_{\ell}\right)

This implies that

Q_{k+1}=a_{k+1}+b_{k+1}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}Q_{k^{\prime}}=a_{k+1}+b_{k+1}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}a_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{\ell=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}\left(1+b_{\ell}\right)

which finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.4
.

Consider a sequence of quantities
\left\{Q_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
satisfying

Q_{k+1}=a_{k}Q_{k}+b_{k}

with some
a_{k},b_{k}\geq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Set
b_{0}=Q_{1}
. Then we have that

Q_{k}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}b_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k-1}a_{j}

Proof.

We shall prove by induction. For the base case, let
k=1
. In this case, we have that

Q_{1}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{0}b_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{0}a_{j}=b_{0}=Q_{1}

For the inductive case, assume that the property holds for
k
. Then we have that

Q_{k+1}=a_{k}Q_{k}+b_{k}=a_{k}\cdot\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}b_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k-1}a_{j}+b_{k}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}b_{k^{\prime}}\prod_{j=k^{\prime}+1}^{k}a_{j}

This proves the inductive step and finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.5
.

Let
\left\{p_{k}\right\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a positive increasing sequence, and
g,t>0
. Let the series
S_{k}
be defined as

S_{k}=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\frac{p_{k^{\prime}}^{t}}{g^{k^{\prime}}}

As long as
t>\frac{\log g}{\log p_{k^{\prime}}-\log p_{k^{\prime}+1}}
for all
k^{\prime}\in[k-1]
, we shall have that

S_{k}\leq\left(1-\max_{k^{\prime}\in[k-1]}g\left(\frac{p_{k^{\prime}}}{p_{k^{\prime}+1}}\right)^{t}\right)^{-1}\frac{p_{k-1}^{t}}{g^{k-1}}

Proof.

Set
\gamma=\max_{k^{\prime}\in[k-1]}g\left(\frac{p_{k^{\prime}}}{p_{k^{\prime}+1}}\right)^{t}
. Since
t>\frac{\log g}{\log p_{k^{\prime}+1}-\log p_{k^{\prime}}}
for all
k^{\prime}\in[k-1]
, we must have that
\gamma<1
. Moreover, by definition of
\gamma
, it must holds that for all
k^{\prime}\in[k-1]

\frac{p_{k^{\prime}}^{t}}{g^{k^{\prime}}}\leq\gamma\cdot\frac{p_{k^{\prime}+1}^{t}}{g^{k^{\prime}+1}}

Thus, we have that for all
k^{\prime}\in[k-1]

\frac{p_{k^{\prime}}^{t}}{g^{k^{\prime}}}\leq\gamma^{k-k^{\prime}-1}\cdot\frac{p_{k-1}^{t}}{g^{k-1}}

Therefore
S_{k}
can be upper-bounded as

S_{k}\leq\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\gamma^{k-k^{\prime}-1}\cdot\frac{p_{k-1}^{t}}{g^{k-1}}=\frac{p_{k-1}^{t}}{g^{k-1}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k-2}\gamma^{k^{\prime}}\leq\frac{p_{k-1}^{t}}{g^{k-1}(1-\gamma)}

∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
be the result of running power iteration starting from
{\mathbf{x}}_{0}
for
t
iterations, as defined in (
14
). Let
\sigma_{j},{\mathbf{a}}_{j}
be the
j
-th eigenvalue and eigenvector of
{\mathbf{M}}
in (
14
). Assume that there exists
c_{0}>0
such that
\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right|\geq c_{0}^{-1}
. Then for all
j=2,\dots,r
, we have:

\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|=\left|\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|\leq c_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{t}.

Proof.

We notice that for
j\geq 2
, it holds that
\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}
.
Since
{\mathbf{M}}
is symmetric, we have that

{\mathbf{x}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\alpha{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\alpha\sigma_{j}^{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\frac{\sigma_{j}^{t}}{\left\|{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}

This brings the focus to
\left\|{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}
. Let
r
be the rank of
{\mathbf{M}}
. Let
\{{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
be an extended orthogonal basis of
\{{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r}
. Then we can write

{\mathbf{x}}_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}_{j}

Let
\sigma_{j}=0
for
j\geq r+1
. Then we have that
{\mathbf{M}}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\sigma_{j}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}
. Therefore

{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\cdot{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\cdot\sigma_{j}^{t}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\cdot\sigma_{j}^{*t}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}

where the last equality follows from
\sigma_{j}^{t}=0
when
t>0
. Therefore, according to the Pythagorean Theorem,

\left\|{\mathbf{M}}^{k}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sigma_{j}^{2t}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq\sigma_{1}^{t}\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|

Recall that
\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|\geq c_{0}^{-1}>0
. Thus, we have

\left|\left({\mathbf{x}}_{t}-{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right)^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|=\frac{\sigma_{j}^{t}}{\left\|{\mathbf{M}}^{t}{\mathbf{x}}_{0}\right\|_{2}}\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}\right|\leq\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{t}\cdot\left|\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{j}}{{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}}\right|\leq\left|{\mathbf{x}}_{0}^{\top}{\mathbf{a}}_{1}\right|^{-1}\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{t}\leq c_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{t}

∎

Appendix D
Experiment Details of Figure
1

We conduct spectral clustering
(
Belkin & Niyogi 2003
)
on a subset of the MNIST dataset
(
Deng 2012
)
, namely
\mathcal{S}=\left\{{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
. In particular, our experiment starts with building a weighted graph
\mathcal{G}=\left(V,E\right)
where each node in the graph
\mathcal{G}
represents a sample image, and each edge weight is chosen according to

e_{ij}=\begin{cases}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\mathbf{x}}_{i}-{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)&\text{ if }{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\in\texttt{rNN}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{j}\right)\text{ or }{\mathbf{x}}_{j}\in\texttt{rNN}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right)\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Here
\texttt{rNN}\left({\mathbf{x}}_{i}\right)
represents the set of
r
Nearest Neighbors of
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}
. To be more specific, two nodes in the graph are considered adjacent if the two corresponding samples are top-
r
nearest neighbors, and the weight of the edge between the two nodes is computed using the RBF kernel with unit variance. Next, we compute the degree-normalized graph Laplacian as our similarity matrix

{\mathbf{L}}={\mathbf{I}}-{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{D}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

where
{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the diagonal degree matrix, and
{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the adjacency matrix. We extract the top-
k
eigenvectors of the similarity matrix
{\mathbf{L}}
with the deflation algorithm in Algorithm
1
with
t
power iteration steps in each call to the
PCA
sub-routine, which results in the approximate eigenvectors
{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k}
. We form a matrix
{\mathbf{U}}=[{\mathbf{u}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{u}}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
, and assign the
i
th sample with a new feature vector
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
as the
i
th row of
{\mathbf{U}}
. Lastly, we perform
k^{\prime}
-means clustering with the newly extracted features
\left\{{\mathbf{v}}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
, forming clusters
C=\left\{C_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k^{\prime}}
, where
C_{j}\subseteq[n]
.
The clustering result is evaluated using the mutual information metric
(
Hubert & Arabie 1985
)
.

\text{MI}\left(C,C^{*}\right)=\sum_{j,j^{\prime}=1}^{k^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}\cap C^{*}_{j^{\prime}}\right)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}\cap C_{j^{\prime}}^{*}\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j^{\prime}}^{*}\right)}

(62)

where
\mathbb{P}\left(C_{j}\right)=\frac{|C_{j}|}{n}

is the ground-truth clustering with

In our experiment, we use a subset of MNIST with
n=1000
randomly drawn samples. When constructing the graph, we use
r=10

k^{\prime}=10
clusters. In Figure
