1.
Introduction

The generators for many interacting particle systems can be written with creation or annihilation operators, or raising and lowering operators. This holds true not only for bosons, fermions, or spin chains in quantum mechanics, but also for classical systems that evolve according to some continuous-time Markov process; this helps uncover useful hidden symmetries
[
50
]
. Sometimes the generators of two different Markov semigroup (
P_{t}=\exp(tL)
,
t\geq 0
) have the
same
formal expression with raising and lowering operators; the difference comes from different representations of the raising and lowering operators and their commutation relations. These observations lead to the algebraic approach to duality
[
15
,
23
,
28
,
55
]
.

A concrete example is the following model for energy transport, see Giardinà, Kurchan, Redig and Vafayi
[
23
]
and references therein. It is closely related to a model by Kipnis, Marchioro and Presutti
[
31
]
. The simplest version of the
Brownian momentum process
on a finite 1D lattice
\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\}
is a continuous-time Markov process with state space
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and formal generator

L=\sum_{i,j}\Bigl(p_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}-p_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}\Bigr)^{2}.

The sum is over nearest-neighbor pairs
\{i,j\}
, say with periodic boundary conditions. (More general versions allow for open systems interacting with reservoirs at the boundaries and for vector-valued momenta.) The interpretation is that at each site
i
, sits a particle with momentum
p_{i}
; two neighboring sites may exchange momentum but the total kinetic energy
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}p_{i}^{2}
is conserved. The
symmetric inclusion process
instead describes particles hopping on the 1D lattice. The state space is
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{\ell}
and the formal generator is

\bigl(\tilde{L}f\bigr)(\boldsymbol{n})=\sum_{i,j}n_{i}\bigl(n_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\bigr)\Bigl(f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{i}-1,\ldots,n_{j}+1,\ldots,n_{\ell})-f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})\Bigr).

Both
L
and
\tilde{L}
can be written as

\sum_{i,j}\Bigl(k_{i}^{+}k_{j}^{-}+k_{j}^{+}k_{i}^{-}-2k_{i}^{0}k_{j}^{0}+\frac{1}{8}\Bigr)

(1)

with raising, lowering and neutral operators
k_{i}^{\pm}
,
k_{j}^{0}
that satisfy the
su(1,1)
commutation relations,

[k_{i}^{-},k_{i}^{+}]=2k_{i}^{0},\quad[k_{i}^{0},k_{i}^{\pm}]=\pm k_{i}^{\pm}.

Operators associated with different lattice sites commute.
For the Brownian energy process,

k_{i}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}p_{i}^{2},\quad k_{i}^{-}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p_{i}^{2}},\quad k_{i}^{0}f=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}p_{i}f+p_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}f\Bigr)

For the symmetric inclusion process,

\displaystyle k_{i}^{+}f(\boldsymbol{n})

\displaystyle=n_{i}f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{i-1},n_{i}-1,n_{i+1},\ldots,n_{\ell}),

\displaystyle k_{i}^{-}f(\boldsymbol{n})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}+n_{i}\Bigr)f(n_{1},\ldots,n_{i-1},n_{i}+1,n_{i+1},\ldots,n_{\ell}),

\displaystyle k_{i}^{0}f(\boldsymbol{n})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(n_{i}+\frac{1}{4}\Bigr)f(\boldsymbol{n}).

Notice that the lowering operator
k_{i}^{-}
annihilates the indicator
\mathbbm{1}_{\{\boldsymbol{n}=0\}}
that there is no particle at all.
The common algebraic formula (
1
) helps explain dualities between the two processes
[
23
]
. In turn, duality helps investigate transport phenomena
[
22
,
31
]
.

How can we extend the algebraic formalism to systems on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
? Infinite-dimensional Lie algebras and current algebras provide the proper algebraic framework; concretely, we look for representations of the
su(1,1)
current algebra. Those keywords may suggest a daunting background from quantum field theory, algebra or geometry
[
20
,
29
]
, but the definition we shall adopt (Definition
2.1
), loosely following Araki
[
5

and Accardi and Boukas
[

is a simple variant of the familiar canonical commutation relations for bosons. In a previous article
[
19

, we have already introduced a family of operators that form a representation of the

current algebra, and we have showed how to deduce intertwining relations for consistent Markov processes. Here we continue the analysis and place a stronger emphasis on the algebraic structure.

We give three representations of the
su(1,1)
current algebra. The first representation is similar to the representation in Fock space of the canonical commutation relations from quantum mechanics (Theorem
3.2
). The other two representations are closer to Gaussian models for the Euclidean free fields, in which Hermite polynomials play a distinguished role
[

]
. The Hilbert spaces are
L^{2}
-spaces with respect to some probability measure, the vacuum is the constant function
1
, and iterated application of raising operators to the vacuum gives rise to orthogonal polynomials. Gaussian fields are replaced with Gamma random measures and negative binomial point processes (also called Pascal point processes). Hermite polynomials are replaced with Laguerre and Meixner polynomials (Theorem
3.7
and
3.9
).

In addition, we prove an infinite-dimensional version of the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula (Theorem
3.4
) and we make explicit the action of a familiy of unitaries, analogous to the Weyl operators
\exp(\mathrm{i}(c(f)+c^{\dagger}(f))
from quantum field theory, on exponential vectors (Theorem
3.3
). The theorem should yield a representation of the current group for the universal cover of
SU(1,1)
, but we refrain from making this explicit.

To the best of our knowledge, the three representations are new, however the ingredients that we use are not and there are closely related results in the literature. The first representation is similar to Boukas’s representation of Feinsilver’s finite-difference algebra
[
12

and Śniady’s representation of commutation relations for quadratic white noise
[

. All these algebras are related: Accardi and Skeide
[

linked the representations of the finite-difference algebra and the renormalized square of white noise, which in turn is related to to representations of the current algebra of

[

]
; finally, as is well known, the groups

and

The extended Fock space used in our first representation also appears in chaos decompositions in non-Gaussian white noise analysis, see Kondratiev, da Silva, Streit, and Us
[

, Berezansky and Mierzejewski
[

. Gamma random measures and negative binomial (Pascal) point processes, together with raising, lowering, and neutral operators and infinite-dimensional Meixner and Laguerre polynomials have been intensely analyzed, see Lytvynov
[

and references therein. Lytvynov also made the connection with the algebra of the square of the white noise
[

[

A slightly different series of articles goes back to Gelfand, Graev and Vershik
[
21
]
.
Araki
[
5
]
proposed a general scheme for finding factorizable representations of current groups in Fock spaces—but did not implement it for

or

. Vershik, Gelfand and Graev however did investigate those groups
[

]
. For a full account and further references we refer the reader to Graev and Vershik
[

. We only mention this: A key role is played by a kind of generalized infinite-dimensional Lebesgue measure, defined as a specific
\sigma

[
59

. These representations are different from ours, but in Appendices
A
and
B
we explain how our representations relate to Araki’s scheme and to the infinite-dimensional Lebesgue measure.

All of the above-mentioned references have a strongly analytic flavor to them. This makes them hard to access for readers who may not wish to learn about distribution theory, nuclear spaces, rigged Hilbert spaces, or white noise analysis. Therefore we have strived to make the setup as simple as possible. We keep topological assumptions to a minimum and we allow for non-smooth test functions and reference measures with atoms.

Another distinctive feature of our presentation is the emphasis on probabilistic interpretations of operators. It turns out that the lowering and neutral operators in our second and third representations generate continuous-time Markov processes that are of considerable intrinsic interest: measure-valued branching processes (Dawson-Watanabe superprocesses) and spatial birth-death processes. This observation, to the best of our knowledge, is new, not only for the current algebra but also for the Lie algebra where the associated processes are diffusions (Laguerre and Bessel) and linear birth-death processes.

2.
Preliminaries

Here we gather some relevant background, most importantly, we define the representations of
su(1,1)
current algebras that we investigate in this article (Definition
2.1
). In addition, we provide some background that aids the understanding of our main results:
basics on the group
SU(1,1)
; representations of the canonical commutation relations with Hermite and Charlier polynomials; how Gamma and and negative binomial (Pascal) laws naturally appear; and why we may expect relations with continuous-time Markov processes.

2.1.
The group
SU(1,1)

The group
SU(1,1)
consists of the complex
2\times 2
matrices

A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&\overline{a}\end{pmatrix}

(2)

with
|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1
. Every such matrix can be written as

A(\xi,\theta)=\exp\bigl(\xi k^{+}-\overline{\xi}k^{-})\exp(2\mathrm{i}\theta k^{0})

(3)

where
\xi\in\mathbb{C}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, and

k^{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathrm{i}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad k^{-}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
\mathrm{i}&0\end{pmatrix},\quad k^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&0\\
0&-1/2\end{pmatrix}.

The matrices satisfy the commutation relations

[k^{-},k^{+}]=2k^{0},\quad[k^{0},k^{\pm}]=\pm k^{\pm}.

(4)

The
su(1,1)
algebra consists of the complex linear combinations of the matrices
k^{0},k^{+},k^{-}
. The exponential in (
3
) is easily computed, one has

A(\xi,\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cosh|\xi|&\mathrm{i}\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\sinh|\xi|\\
-\mathrm{i}\frac{\overline{\xi}}{|\xi|}\sinh|\xi|&\cosh|\xi|\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}&0\\
0&\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\end{pmatrix}.

(5)

Bargmann
[
7
]
investigated representations of
SU(1,1)
in the context of the Lorentz and Poincaré groups from special relativity. The
su(1,1)
algebra also appears in quantum optics for pairs of photons: If
a
and
a^{\dagger}
are bosonic annihilation and creation operators, then
k^{-}=\frac{1}{2}a^{2}
,
k^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger})^{2}
and
k^{0}=\frac{1}{2}a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{4}
satisfy the
su(1,1)
algebra
[
14
,
16
]
. The operator
\exp(\frac{1}{2}(\xi a^{2}-\overline{\xi}(a^{\dagger})^{2}))
is the
squeeze operator

[
51
, Chapter 2.7]
.

2.2.
Current algebra

As is customary with bosonic creation and annihilation operators, we work with operators indexed by functions. Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
be a measurable space and
\alpha
be a
\sigma
-finite reference measure on
\mathbb{X}
; for example,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra and the Lebesgue measure.
We introduce an algebra of test functions

\mathcal{C}=\bigl\{\varphi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}:\,\text{ bounded, measurable, }\alpha(\{x:\varphi(x)\neq 0\})<\infty\bigr\}.

(6)

The following definition is modeled after a similar definition in Accardi and Boukas
[
2
]
for the algebra of the renormalized square of white noise.
Araki
[
5
]
defines the
su(1,1)
current algebra over
\mathbb{X}
as the set of
su(1,1)
-valued, bounded measurable functions, equipped with the pointwise Lie algebra operations. Our function-indexed operators
k^{\pm}(\varphi),k^{0}(\theta)
represent the matrix-valued maps
x\mapsto\varphi(x)k^{+}
,
x\mapsto\overline{\varphi(x)}k^{-}
,
x\mapsto\theta(x)k^{0}
with
k^{\pm},k^{0}
the
2\times 2
-matrices from Section
2.1
.

Definition 2.1
.

A family
k^{\pm}(\varphi),k^{0}(\varphi)
,
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
of operators in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a
Fock representation of the
su(1,1)
current algebra
with
vacuum

\Psi
if:

(i)

The operators are defined on a common domain
\mathcal{D}
that is dense in
\mathcal{H}
and they map
\mathcal{D}
to itself, i.e.,
k^{\#}(\varphi)\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{D}
.

(ii)

k^{+}(\varphi)
and
k^{0}(\varphi)
are linear in
\varphi
while
k^{-}(\varphi)
is antilinear in
\varphi
.

(iii)

For all
\varphi,\theta\in\mathcal{C}
,

\begin{gathered}[k^{-}(\varphi),k^{+}(\theta)]=2k^{0}(\overline{\varphi}\theta)\\
[k^{0}(\varphi),k^{+}(\theta)]=k^{+}(\varphi\theta),\quad[k^{0}(\varphi),k^{-}(\theta)]=-k^{-}(\overline{\varphi}\theta)\end{gathered}

(7)

and
[k^{\#}(\varphi),k^{\#}(\theta)]=0
for
\#\in\{0,+,-\}
.

(iv)

For all
f,g\in\mathcal{D}
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
,

\displaystyle\langle f,k^{0}(\varphi)g\rangle=\langle k^{0}(\overline{\varphi})f,g\rangle,\quad\langle f,k^{+}(\varphi)g\rangle=\langle k^{-}(\varphi)f,g\rangle.

(8)

(v)

\Psi\in\mathcal{D}
,
||\Psi||=1
and
k^{-}(\varphi)\Psi=0
for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
.

(vi)

\Psi
is cyclic: the linear combinations of
\Psi
and
k^{+}(\varphi_{1})\cdots k^{+}(\varphi_{n})\Psi
where
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
, are dense in
\mathfrak{F}
.

All our representations are
factorizable
—when test functions have disjoint support, the associated operators commute. In addition, our three representations from Section
3
satisfy

k^{0}(\varphi)\Psi=\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\alpha\Bigr)\Psi

for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
. Thus, the measure
\frac{1}{2}\alpha
plays a role analogous to the Bargmann index for the discrete series, minimal weight representations of
SU(1,1)
(see Section
4
).

We remark that the three representations are unitarily equivalent. More precisely, the isomorphism from
[
33
, Equation (4.9)]
associated with the chaos decomposition of functionals of a Gamma random measure switches between the representation in extended Fock spaces and the representation outlined in Section
3.3
. Similarly, the isomorphism from
[
38
, Corollary 5.3]
, which relates to the chaos decomposition of functionals associated with a Pascal point process, switches between the representation in extended Fock spaces and the representation introduced in Section
3.4
.

2.3.
Canonical commutation relations

The raising and lowering operators
k^{+}(f)
,
k^{-}(f)
are similar to bosonic creation and annihilation operators
c^{\dagger}(f)
,
c(g)
with the canonical commutation relations

[c(f),c^{\dagger}(g)]=\langle f,g\rangle
. The best known representation, in quantum many-body theory, is in the Fock space of sequences of symmetric functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. For better comparison with our results, we recall two additional representations. To keep things concrete in this section
\mathbb{X}
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra and with the Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. The scalar product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{X},\lambda)
is
\langle f,g\rangle=\int\overline{f}\,g\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
.

2.3.1.
Gaussian white noise, Hermite polynomials

The setting of the first additional representation is similar to that of the Euclidean quantum free field, we refer to Glimm and Jaffe
[
24
]
for background and proofs. By the Bochner-Minlos theorem, there is a unique probability measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}
, the space of real-valued tempered distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with

\int\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\varphi(f)}\mu(\mathrm{d}\varphi)=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\int f(x)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\Bigr).

for all real-valued Schwartz functions
f
. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and real-valued Schwartz functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
, the vector
(\varphi(f_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(f_{n}))
is a Gaussian random vector with covariance matrix
C=(\int f_{i}g_{j}\mathrm{d}\lambda)_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}
.
In particular

•

If
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
have disjoint support then
\varphi(f_{1})
,…,
\varphi(f_{n})
are independent.

•

Each variable
\varphi(f)
has a normal distribution with mean zero and variance
\int f^{2}\mathrm{d}\lambda
.

The Hilbert space is
L^{2}(\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mu)
, the space of complex-valued square-integrable functions. Let
\mathcal{D}
be the set of functions
F:\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
of the form

F(\varphi)=P_{n}\bigl(\varphi(f_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(f_{n})\bigr)

with
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
Schwartz functions, and
P_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
a multivariate polynomial. The annihilation operators are

c(f)F(\varphi)=\int\overline{f(x)}\frac{\delta}{\delta\varphi(x)}F(\varphi)\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle f,f_{j}\rangle\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial x_{j}}\bigl(\varphi(f_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(f_{n})\bigr)

and the creation operators are

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}(g)F(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\varphi(g)F(\varphi)-\int g(x)\frac{\delta}{\delta\varphi(x)}F(\varphi)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\varphi(g)F(\varphi)-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle f_{j},g\rangle\frac{\partial P_{n}}{\partial x_{j}}\bigl(\varphi(f_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(f_{n})\bigr).

The vacuum is the constant function
\mathbbm{1}
. Iterated application of creation operators to the vacuum gives rise to Hermite polynomials
[
24
]
.

2.3.2.
Poisson point process. Charlier polynomials

The second additional representation comes from Poisson white noise analysis (Kondratiev, da Silva, Streit, Us
[
33
]
and references therein). It is similar to the previous representation except that the Gaussian measure
\mu
is replaced by the law of a Poisson point process, supported on a simpler space of point configurations—there is no need for tempered distributions.

Let
\mathbf{N}
be the set of finite or countable sums of Dirac measures, equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the counting variables
\eta\mapsto\eta(B)
,
B\in\mathcal{X}
. The law
\pi_{\lambda}
of the
Poisson point process with intensity measure
\lambda
is the uniquely defined probability measure on
\mathbf{N}
under which

•

Each counting variable
\eta\mapsto\eta(B)
,
B\in\mathcal{X}
, has a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda(B)

\pi_{\lambda}\bigl(\{\eta:\eta(B)=n\}\bigr)=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(B)}\frac{\lambda(B)^{n}}{n!}.

•

For all disjoint
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
, the associated counting variables are independent.

Mathematical physicists should think of
\pi_{\lambda}
as the grand-canonical Gibbs measure for a classical ideal gas in which the expected number of particles in a region
B
is
\lambda(B)
. When
\lambda
is Lebesgue measure, the ideal gas is homogeneous with density
1
.

We work in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbf{N},\pi_{\lambda})
.
Abbreviate
\eta(f)=\int f\mathrm{d}\eta
and let
\mathcal{D}
be the space of polynomials in the variables
\eta(f)=\int f\mathrm{d}\eta
. The annihilation and creation operators are
[
33
]

\displaystyle c(f)F(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\overline{f(x)}\bigl(F(\eta+\delta_{x})-F(\eta)\bigr)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x),

\displaystyle c^{\dagger}(f)F(\eta)

\displaystyle=\int f(x)F(\eta-\delta_{x})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)-F(\eta)\int f\mathrm{d}\lambda.

The vacuum
\Psi
is again the constant function
1
, and iterated application of creation operators for indicators
f
gives rise to
Charlier polynomials
in the occupation numbers
\eta(B)
,
B\subset\mathbb{X}
. For example, for a finite-volume set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\bigl(c^{\dagger}(\mathbbm{1}_{B})\bigr)^{n}\mathbf{1}(\eta)=\mathscr{C}_{n}\bigl(\eta(B);\lambda(B)\bigr)

where
\mathscr{C}_{n}(x;\beta)=x^{n}+\text{lower order terms}
is a univariate Charlier polynomial, orthogonal with respect to the measure
\nu(\{x\})=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta}\beta^{x}/x!
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}

[
32
]
.

2.4.
From Gauss and Poisson to Gamma and Pascal laws

Our second and third representations of the
su(1,1)
current algebra rely on Gamma and Pascal laws. Here we briefly motivate how these distributions arise, building on results from quantum stochastic calculus.
Let
a^{\dagger}
and
a
be the creation and annihilation operators for the harmonic oscillator in some Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with vacuum
\psi_{0}
, for example,
a=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
and
a^{\dagger}=x-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\exp(-x^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}x)
, see the next subsection.
Write
\langle f(A)\rangle
for the vacuum expectation values
\langle\psi_{0},f(A)\psi_{0}\rangle
of polynomial functions
f
of an operator
A
.
The following is known:

(i)

The vacuum expectations of
a+a^{\dagger}
are Gaussian:

\langle f(a+a^{\dagger})\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(z)\exp(-z^{2}/2)\mathrm{d}z

for every polynomial
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
(just think of the harmonic oscillator).

(ii)

a^{\dagger}+a+ca^{\dagger}a
, where
c\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
, is associated with a Poisson law of parameter
\lambda=1/c^{2}
:

\langle f(a+a^{\dagger}+ca^{\dagger}a)\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{n}}{n!}f\bigl(c^{-1}(n-\lambda)\bigr).

See Hudson and Parthasarathy
[
26
]
and Meyer
[
41
, Chapter IV.2(5)]
. A related result is given by Ito and Kubo
[
27
, Proposition 5.2]
.

(iii)

\frac{1}{2}(a+a^{\dagger})^{2}
goes with a Gamma distribution:

\langle f\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}(a+a^{\dagger})^{2}\Bigr)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{-1/2}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathrm{d}x.

This follows from (i)—think
x=z^{2}/2
.

(iv)

a^{2}+(a^{\dagger})^{2}+\beta a^{\dagger}a
with
\beta>2
goes with negative binomial (Pascal) distribution with parameters
\alpha=1/2
and
p\in(0,1)
the unique solution of
\sqrt{p}+{\sqrt{p}}^{-1}=\beta
: there exist non-zero reals
c_{1},c_{2}
such that

\langle f\bigl(a^{2}+(a^{\dagger})^{2}+\beta a^{\dagger}a\bigr)\rangle=(1-p)^{\alpha}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{n!}\,p^{n}f\bigl(c_{1}n+c_{2}\bigr)

where
(\alpha)_{0}=1
,
(\alpha)_{n}=\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)
. See Accardi, Franz and Skeide
[
3
]
and the survey by Accardi and Boukas
[
1
, Section 17]
. A similar result holds for
\beta<-2
.

(v)

a^{2}+(a^{\dagger})^{2}+\beta a^{\dagger}a
with
\beta\in(-2,2)
is associated with a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
, the orthogonality measure for the Meixner-Pollaczek polynomials
[
3
]
.

Remembering that
k^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(a^{\dagger})^{2}
,
k^{-}=\frac{1}{2}a^{2}
,
k^{0}=\frac{1}{2}a^{\dagger}a+\frac{1}{4}
satisfy the commutation relations of the
su(1,1)
algebra, we see that the operators

X=k^{+}+k^{-}+2k^{0},\qquad Y=k^{+}+k^{-}+({\sqrt{p}}^{-1}+\sqrt{p})k^{0}

(9)

with
p\in(0,1)
,
are naturally associated with Gamma and negative binomial (Pascal) laws.
Our three representations give priority to different operators: roughly, we diagonalize
k^{0}
,
X
or
Y
.

Combinations of the type (
9
) are also motivated by the theory of orthogonal polynomials and Jacobi matrices, see Berezansky
[
9
]
, Lytvynov
[
39
]
and references therein.
Consider for example the Laguerre polynomials
p_{n}(x)=\mathscr{L}_{n}^{(\alpha-1)}(x)
, orthogonal with respect to the measure
x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-x)\mathrm{d}x
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and with leading coefficient
1
. They satisfy the three-term recurrence relation
[
32
, Eq. (9.12.4)]

xp_{n}(x)=p_{n+1}(x)+(2n+\alpha)p_{n}(x)+n(n+\alpha-1)p_{n-1}(x).

Every polynomial
f(x)
is a linear combination
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}g(n)p_{n}(x)
, with at most finitely many non-zero
g(n)
’s. When multiplied with
x
, it is

xf(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}p_{n}(x)\Bigl(ng(n-1)+(2n+\alpha)g(n)+(\alpha+n)g(n+1)\Bigr).

Thus multiplication by
x
corresponds to a linear transformation of the sequence of coefficients, given by an infinite band-diagonal matrix, the Jacobi matrix. Let us define

J^{+}g(n)=ng(n-1)
,
J^{0}g(n)=(n+\alpha/2)g(n)
,
J^{-}g(n)=(\alpha+n)g(n+1)
, then multiplication by
x
corresponds to the action of
J^{+}+2J^{0}+J^{-}
in the space of sequences.

For infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials, the linear operators
J^{\pm}
and
J^{0}
are replaced by families of operators
J^{\pm}(\varphi)
and
J^{0}(\varphi)
, indexed by test functions
\varphi
. The focus in this context is on the commutative family of operators
J^{+}(\varphi)+\lambda J^{0}(\varphi)+J^{-}(\varphi)
(
Jacobi fields
) and not so much on the full set of commutation relations (see, however,
[
40
]
).

2.5.
Continuous-time Markov processes

Finally we sketch how continuous-time Markov processes naturally enter the scene, using the Gaussian model for the harmonic oscillator
[
41
, Chapter III.2(2)]
. Let
\mu=\mathcal{N}(0,1)
be the standard normal distribution on
\mathbb{R}
. The norm in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mu)
is

||f||^{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x.

The densely defined operators
a=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
,
a^{\dagger}=x-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}
satisfy
[a,a^{\dagger}]=\mathrm{id}
. Now, the point we wish to highlight is this: Both the annihilation operator
a
and
L=-\widehat{n}
, with
\widehat{n}
the number operator

\widehat{n}=a^{\dagger}a=-\Bigl(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}x^{2}}-x\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\Bigr)

are generators of continuous-time Markov processes. The operator
L=-\widehat{n}
generates an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process for which the Gaussian measure
\mu
is reversible.
The annihilation operator generates deterministic motion to the right,
X_{t}=X_{0}+t
. The creation operator corresponds to time-evolution of densities. Indeed
\exp(ta^{\dagger})
maps
f_{0}
to
f_{t}(x)=f_{0}(x-t)\exp(tx)
. Thus, if
X_{0}
has probability density function
\rho_{0}(x)=f_{0}(x)\exp(-x^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}
, then
X_{t}=X_{0}+t
has probability density function
\rho_{t}(x)=\rho_{0}(x-t)=f_{t}(x)\exp(-x^{2}/2)/\sqrt{2\pi}
.

We will encounter a similar structure for the second and third representations of the current algebra (and of the Lie algebra, see Section
4
): The lowering and the neutral operator are related to continuous-time Markov processes, concretely spatial birth-death processes and measure-valued branching processes.

3.
Main results

3.1.
Representation in extended Fock space

Let
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
be a Borel space, for example, a complete metric separable space with its Borel
\sigma
-algebra. Let
\alpha
be a
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbb{X}
.

3.1.1.
Extended Fock space

Define measures
\lambda_{n}
on
\mathbb{X}^{n}
, equipped with the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{X}^{n}
, as follows. Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the set of permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Every permutation is a product of cycles with disjoint supports; the number of cycles is denoted
|\sigma|
. Given
f_{n}:\mathbb{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
a new function
f_{n}^{\sigma}:\mathbb{X}^{|\sigma|}\to\mathbb{C}
is obtained by identifying variables that belong to the same cycle. For example, for
n=3
and
\sigma=(1\ 3)(2)
, we get
f_{3}^{\sigma}(x,y)=f_{3}(x,y,x)
. The measure
\lambda_{n}
on
\mathbb{X}^{n}
is the uniquely defined measure for which

\int f_{n}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\int f_{n}^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\alpha^{|\sigma|}

for all measurable
f_{n}:\mathbb{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For example, we have
\lambda_{1}=\alpha
and

\int f_{2}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{2}=\int f_{2}(x,y)\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)\alpha(\mathrm{d}y)+\int f(x,x)\alpha(\mathrm{d}x).

Let
L^{2}(\lambda_{n})=L^{2}(\mathbb{X}^{n},\mathcal{X}^{n},\lambda_{n})
be the space of complex-valued, square-integrable functions
f_{n}
and
L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\lambda_{n})
be the subspace of symmetric functions, i.e., for every permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
,

f_{n}(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})=f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

for
\lambda_{n}
-almost all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. The
interacting
or
extended Fock space

\mathfrak{F}

[
33
]
consists of the sequences
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
where
f_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
,
f_{n}\in L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\lambda_{n})
, and

|f_{0}|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int|f_{n}|^{2}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n}<\infty.

The extended Fock space is equipped with the scalar product

\langle(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\rangle=\overline{f_{0}}g_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int\overline{f_{n}}g_{n}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n}.

The
vacuum
is the sequence
\Psi=(1,0,0,\ldots)
. The symmetrized tensor product of functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}
is

\bigl(f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}\bigr)(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f_{1}(x_{\sigma(1)})\cdots f_{n}(x_{\sigma(n)}).

We shall often identify a function
F_{n}:\mathbb{X}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
with the sequence
(0,\ldots,F_{n},0,\ldots)
in
\mathfrak{F}
. With this identification in mind, the following lemma is useful. Remember the algebra
\mathcal{C}
of bounded functions supported on sets of finite measure.

Lemma 3.1
.

The space
\mathcal{D}
of finite linear combinations of the vacuum
\Psi
and symmetrized tensor products
f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}
of functions
f_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
is dense in
\mathfrak{F}
.

The lemma is proven in Section
5.1
.

3.1.2.
Exponential vectors

For
u\in\mathcal{C}
with
\sup|u|<1
, we define a vector
\mathcal{E}_{u}\in\mathfrak{F}
by

(\mathcal{E}_{u})_{0}=1,\quad(\mathcal{E}_{u})_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\prod_{i=1}^{n}u(x_{i}).

In terms of the raising operators introduced below,
\mathcal{E}_{u}=\exp(k^{+}(u))\Psi
, whence the name
exponential vector

[
41
, Chapter IV.1]
.
(Mathematical physicists may be more familiar with the notion of
coherent states
; for bosons, these are just the normalized exponential states, but for
SU(1,1)
the relation is more subtle, see the remark after Theorem
3.4
below.)
We note

\langle\mathcal{E}_{u},\mathcal{E}_{v}\rangle=\exp\Bigl(-\int\log\bigl(1-\overline{u(x)}v(x)\bigr)\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr)

(10)

where
-\log(1-z)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}z^{j}/j
for
|z|<1
. Eq. (
10
) already appeared in Boukas
[
12
,
13
]
, however Boukas’s Hilbert space is more abstract than our interacting Fock space.

The linear combinations of exponential vectors are dense in
\mathfrak{F}
, moreover if
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
are linearly independent in
L^{2}(\mathbb{X},\alpha)
, then the associated exponential vectors are linearly independent in
\mathfrak{F}
. These properties are proven in the same way as their counterparts in the usual bosonic Fock space, we refer to Meyer
[
41
, Chapter IV.1.3]
.

3.1.3.
Representation of the
su(1,1)
current algebra

Define
raising, lowering and neutral operators

k^{+}(\varphi),k^{-}(\varphi),k^{0}(\varphi):\mathcal{D}\to\mathfrak{F}
, indexed by
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
, as follows: for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\bigl(k^{+}(\varphi)f\bigr)_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x_{i})f_{n-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}),

\displaystyle\bigl(k^{-}(\varphi)f\bigr)_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=(n+1)\int\overline{\varphi(y)}f_{n+1}(y,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\alpha(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\varphi(x_{i})}f_{n+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{i}),

\displaystyle\bigl(k^{0}(\varphi)f\bigr)_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\alpha\Bigr)f_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

For
n=0
we use the same expressions, with
\sum_{i=1}^{0}(\cdots)=0
. In particular,
(k^{+}(\varphi)f)_{0}=0
,
(k^{-}(\varphi)f)_{0}=\int\overline{\varphi}f_{1}\mathrm{d}\alpha
, and
(k^{0}(\varphi)f)_{0}=(\frac{1}{2}\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\alpha)f_{0}
.

Similar operators already appear in Śniady
[
52
]
and Lytvynov
[
39
]
. Śniady studies them in the context of the renormalized algebra of the square of white noise, Lytvynov investigates infinite-dimensional orthogonal polynomials and relates Jacobi fields to the square of white noise in
[
40
]
. Neither of them explicitly comments on the relation with the
su(1,1)
current algebra.

Theorem 3.2
.

The operators
k^{\pm}(\varphi),k^{0}(\varphi):\mathcal{D}\to\mathfrak{F}
form a Fock representation of the
su(1,1)
current algebra in
\mathfrak{F}
with vacuum
\Psi=(1,0,0,\ldots)
.

The operators
k^{\#}(\varphi)
may be written as

k^{+}(\varphi)=a^{\dagger}(\varphi),\quad k^{-}(\varphi)=a(\varphi)+b(\varphi),\quad k^{0}(\varphi)=\widehat{n}(\varphi)+\frac{1}{2}\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\alpha

where

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}(\varphi)f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

\displaystyle=(n+1)\varphi\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n},

\displaystyle a(\varphi)f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle\varphi,f_{i}\rangle f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\widehat{f_{i}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n},

\displaystyle b(\varphi)f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bigl(\overline{\varphi}f_{i}f_{j}\bigr)\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\widehat{f}_{i}\cdots\widehat{f}_{j}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n},

\displaystyle\widehat{n}(\varphi)f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}(\varphi f_{i})\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}.

Here, as usual,
\widehat{f}_{i}
means omission of
f_{i}
. The scalar product is
\langle\varphi,f\rangle=\int\overline{\varphi}f\mathrm{d}\alpha
.
In
a^{\dagger}(\varphi)
,
a(\varphi)
we recognize the bosonic creation and annihilation operators;
\widehat{n}(\varphi)
is a smeared bosonic number operator (think
\int\varphi(x)a_{x}^{\dagger}a_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x
).
(Factors of
\sqrt{n+1}
or
1/\sqrt{n}
often included in explicit formulas
[
46
, Chapter X.7]
are missing because the scalar product in our (extended) Fock space has the factor
1/n!
.) The operators satisfy

[a(f),a^{\dagger}(g)]=\langle f,g\rangle,\quad[\widehat{n}(f),a^{\dagger}(g)]=a^{\dagger}(fg),\quad[\widehat{n}(f),a(g)]=-a(f\overline{g}).

(11)

3.2.
Unitary operators, Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula

For
\xi,\theta\in\mathcal{C}
with real-valued
\theta
, define unitary operators, analogous to (
3
), by

U(\xi,\theta)=\exp\bigl(k^{+}(\xi)-k^{-}(\xi)\bigr)\exp\bigl(2\mathrm{i}k^{0}(\theta)\bigr).

(12)

The second exponential on the right is a multiplication operator, with multiplier
\exp(\mathrm{i}\int\theta\mathrm{d}\alpha+2\mathrm{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\theta(x_{j}))
. The first exponential is by definition
\exp(\mathrm{i}A)
with
A
the self-adjoint closure of
\frac{1}{i}(k^{+}(\xi)-k^{-}(\xi))
. The latter operator is essentially self-adjoint on
\mathcal{D}
; the proof with Nelson’s analytic vector theorem is similar to
[
19
, Lemma 4.1]
and Reed and Simon
[
46
, Theorem X.41(a)]
and therefore omitted.

The unitaries
U(\xi,\theta)
act on exponential vectors in a fairly explicit way.
For
z:\mathbb{X}\to\{\zeta\in\mathbb{C}:\,|\zeta|<1\}
, let

z_{\xi}(x)=\frac{z(x)+\frac{\xi(x)}{|\xi(x)|}\tanh|\xi(x)|}{1+z(x)\frac{\overline{\xi(x)}}{|\xi(x)|}\tanh|\xi(x)|}

(13)

and

C_{\xi}(z)=\exp\Biggl(-\int\log\Bigl(\cosh|\xi(x)|+z(x)\frac{\overline{\xi(x)}}{|\xi(x)|}\sinh|\xi(x)|\Bigr)\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)\Biggr).

(14)

As explained by us in
[
19
, Section 5]
, the equations are related to the action of
SU(1,1)
on the open unit disk by Möbius transforms, and to representations of
SU(1,1)
given by Bargmann
[
7
, Section 9]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\xi:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}
,
\theta:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,
z:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{C}
be bounded functions in
\mathcal{C}
with
\sup|z|<1
. Then

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(k^{+}(\xi)-k^{-}(\xi)\bigr)\mathcal{E}_{z}

\displaystyle=C_{\xi}(z)\mathcal{E}_{z_{\xi}},

\displaystyle\exp\bigl(2\mathrm{i}k^{0}(\theta)\bigr)\mathcal{E}_{z}

\displaystyle=\exp\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\int\theta\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\Bigr)\mathcal{E}_{\exp(2\mathrm{i}\theta)z}.

The proof of the first formula is similar to Proposition 5.1 in
[
19
]
and therefore omitted. The second formula is straightforward, we leave the details to the reader.

The
2\times 2
matrices in
SU(1,1)
satisfy the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula

\exp\Bigl(\xi k^{+}-\overline{\xi}k^{-}\Bigr)=\exp\Bigl(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}(\tanh|\xi|)k^{+}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{1}{\cosh|\xi|}\Bigr)^{2k^{0}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}(\tanh|\xi|)k^{-}\Bigr).

(15)

The unitaries
U(\xi,0)
satisfy a similar equation.

Theorem 3.4

(Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula)
.

For all
\xi\in\mathcal{C}
and all
f\in\mathcal{D}
, we have

\exp\Bigl(k^{+}(\xi)-k^{-}(\xi)\Bigr)f=\exp\bigl(k^{+}(v)\bigr)\exp\bigl(k^{0}(w)\bigr)\exp\bigl(-k^{-}(v)\bigr)f,

(16)

where

v:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\xi\neq 0\}}\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\tanh|\xi|
and
w:=-2\log\cosh|\xi|
.

On the right side
\exp(k^{0}(w))
is a multiplication operator and
\exp(\pm k^{\pm}(v))
are defined by exponential series that converge on the relevant domains (Lemma
5.2
). The theorem is proven in Section
5.2
.

Remark
.

The analogue of the
Perelomov coherent state

[
14
,
44
]
is

\mathscr{C}_{\xi}=\exp\bigl(k^{+}(\xi)-k^{-}(\xi)\bigr)\Psi
. Theorem
3.4
yields
\mathscr{C}_{\xi}=\lambda_{\xi}\exp(k^{+}(v))\Psi
with
\lambda_{\xi}=\exp(-\int\log(\cosh|\xi|)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha)
and
v
as given in the theorem. Thus, the coherent state
\mathscr{C}_{\xi}
differs from the exponential state
\mathcal{E}_{v}
by a normalization and an additional reparametrization from
\xi
to
v
.

Araki
[
5
]
emphasizes the role of expectation functionals
g\mapsto E(g)
for current groups. In light of this, we mention as a corollary an explicit expression for the vacuum expectations.

Corollary 3.5

(Vacuum expectations)
.

For all
\xi\in\mathcal{C}
and all real-valued
\theta\in\mathcal{C}
,

\langle\Psi,U(\xi,\theta)\Psi\rangle=\exp\Bigl(\mathrm{i}\int\theta\,\mathrm{d}\alpha-\int\bigl(\log\cosh|\xi|\bigr)\,\mathrm{d}\alpha\Bigr).

3.3.
Gamma random measure and Laguerre polynomials

Next comes a representation in a probability space of random measures somewhat analogous to the Gaussian model for the canonical commutation relations from Section
2.3.1
as the raising and lowering operators involve differential operators.

Let
\mathbf{M}
be the space of
s
-finite measures on
\mathbb{X}
(a measure is
s
-finite if it is a countable sum of finite measures
[
36
]
). The space
\mathbf{M}
is equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the variables
B\mapsto\mu(B)
with
B\in\mathcal{X}
. Given the
\sigma
-finite reference measure
\alpha
, let
\rho_{\alpha}
be the uniquely defined probability measure on
\mathbf{M}
such that:

•

For all disjoint
B_{1},\ldots,B_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
, the variables
\mu\mapsto\mu(B_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, are independent.

•

For every
B\in\mathcal{X}
, the variable
\mu\mapsto\mu(B)
has a Gamma distribution with shape parameter
\alpha(B)
and scale parameter
1
: if
\alpha(B)<\infty
,

\rho_{\alpha}\bigl(\{\mu:\mu(B)\leq y\}\bigr)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha(B))}\int_{0}^{y}t^{\alpha(B)-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t}\mathrm{d}t\quad(y\geq 0).

When
\alpha(B)=0
or
\infty
, we ask that
\mu(B)=0
or
\infty
,
\rho_{\alpha}
-almost surely.

The probability measure
\rho_{\alpha}
is the law of a
Gamma random measure
. It is the law of a compound Poisson process
[
36
, Chapter 15]
: Let
\Pi=\sum_{i}\delta_{(X_{i},Z_{i})}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{X}\times(0,\infty)
with intensity measure
\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)z^{-1}\exp(-z)\mathrm{d}z
, then

\xi=\sum_{i}Z_{i}\delta_{X_{i}}
has law
\rho_{\alpha}

[
36
, Example 15.6]
.

We work in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
. Let
\mathbb{D}
be the space of polynomials in the variables
\mu(\varphi)=\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\mu
, where
\varphi
is in the algebra
\mathcal{C}
of test functions from (
6
). Thus
\mathbb{D}
consists of the functions
F
of the form

F(\mu)=P_{n}\bigl(\mu(f_{1}),\ldots,\mu(f_{n})\bigr)

(17)

with
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
, and
P_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
a multivariate polynomial with complex coefficients. Notice that if
f\in\mathcal{C}
, then

\mu(|f|)<\infty
for
\rho_{\alpha}
-almost all
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
, thus
F(\mu)
is well-defined except possibly on
\rho_{\alpha}
null sets on which we may define it to be zero.

Lemma 3.6
.

The space
\mathbb{D}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
.

The lemma is a classical infinite-dimensional variant of a result due to Riesz
[
47
]
: For probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
, uniqueness in the moment problem implies density of polynomials. For the reader’s convenience we give a self-contained proof in Section
5.3
.

For a polynomial
F
as in (
17
),
\mu\in\mathbf{M}
and
x\in\mathbb{X}
, set

\displaystyle\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu(x)}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F(\mu+t\delta_{x})\Big|_{t=0}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(x)\partial_{j}P_{n}\bigl(\mu(f_{1}),\ldots,\mu(f_{n})\bigr),

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta\mu(x)^{2}}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}F(\mu+t\delta_{x})\Big|_{t=0}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}f_{i}(x)f_{j}(x)\partial_{i}\partial_{j}P_{n}\bigl(\mu(f_{1}),\ldots,\mu(f_{n})\bigr)

where
\partial_{j}P_{n}
is the partial derivative of
P_{n}
with respect to the
j
-th variable. Finally, we define lowering, raising and neutral operators
K^{\#}(\varphi):\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
, where
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
, by

\displaystyle K^{-}(\varphi)F(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int\overline{\varphi(x)}\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta\mu(x)^{2}}(\mu)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)+\int\overline{\varphi(x)}\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu(x)}(\mu)\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle K^{+}(\varphi)F(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int\varphi(x)\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta\mu(x)^{2}}(\mu)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)+\int\varphi(x)\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu(x)}(\mu)(\alpha-2\mu)(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+(\mu(\varphi)-\alpha(\varphi))F(\mu)

\displaystyle K^{0}(\varphi)F(\mu)

\displaystyle=-\int\varphi(x)\frac{\delta^{2}F}{\delta\mu(x)^{2}}(\mu)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)-\int\varphi(x)\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu(x)}(\mu)(\alpha-\mu)(\mathrm{d}x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\varphi)F(\mu).

Remark
.

For every real-valued test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
,

K^{+}(\varphi)+K^{-}(\varphi)+2K^{0}(\varphi)=\mu(\varphi).

(18)

The right side is the multiplication operator with
\mu(\varphi)
. The above relation is in agreement with the univariate example (iii) from Section
2.4
. It should remind the reader of the definition of Segal’s field operator
\Phi_{\mathrm{S}}(f)
as a sum of bosonic creation and annihilation operators
[
46
, Chapter X.7]
.

For
\beta>0
, let
\mathscr{L}_{n}^{(\beta-1)}(x)
,

n
with leading coefficient

that are orthogonal with respect to the measure

\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Theorem 3.7
.

The operators
K^{\pm}(\varphi),K^{0}(\varphi):\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{D}
, with

, form a Fock representation in

of the

current algebra with vacuum

, the constant function
1
. Moreover,

K^{+}(\mathbbm{1}_{B_{1}})^{n_{1}}\cdots K^{+}(\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\ell}})^{n_{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}(\mu)=\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathscr{L}_{n_{i}}^{(\alpha(B_{i})-1)}\bigl(\mu(B_{i})\bigr)

(19)

for all

We remark that for non-negative

The lowering operator

The raising operator

Moreover, the process with formal generator

or
[

If

.

3.4.
Pascal point process and Meixner polynomials

We conclude with a representation in a probability space of point configurations analogous to the Poisson-Charlier representation for the canonical commutation relations from Section
2.3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{N}=\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X})
be the space of finite or countable sums of Dirac measures (including the zero measure) on the Borel space
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
. The space
\mathbf{N}
is equipped with the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{N}
generated by the counting variables
B\mapsto\eta(B)
,
B\in\mathcal{X}
. For
a>0
the rising factorial (Pochhammer symbol) is

(a)_{0}=1,\quad(a)_{n}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1).

For
p\in(0,1)
and
\alpha
a
\sigma
-finite measure
\alpha
on
\mathbb{X}
, let
\rho_{p,\alpha}
be the distribution of a Pascal point process, i.e.,
\rho_{p,\alpha}
is the uniquely defined measure under which:

•

The counting variables
\eta(B_{1}),\ldots,\eta(B_{k})
are independent, for all
k\geq 2
and all pairwise disjoint
B_{1},\ldots,B_{k}\in\mathcal{E}
.

•

For each
B\in\mathcal{E}
with
0<\alpha(B)<\infty
,

\rho_{p,\alpha}\bigl(\{\eta:\,\eta(B)=n\}\bigr)=(1-p)^{\alpha(B)}\frac{p^{n}}{n!}\bigl(\alpha(B)\bigr)_{n}.

If
\alpha(B)=0
or
\infty
then
\eta(B)=0
or
\infty
respectively,
\rho_{p,\alpha}
almost surely.

We work in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbf{N},\rho_{p,\alpha})
. Functions are complex-valued and the scalar product is
\langle f,g\rangle=\int\overline{f}g\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{p,\alpha}
. Let
\mathbb{D}
be the set of polynomials in the variables
\eta(\varphi)=\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\eta
, i.e.,
\mathbb{D}
is the set of functions of the form

F(\eta)=P_{n}\bigl(\eta(\varphi_{1}),\ldots,\eta(\varphi_{n})\bigr)

with
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
, and
P_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
a multivariate polynomial.

Lemma 3.8
.

The space
\mathbb{D}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbf{N},\rho_{p,\alpha})
.

The proof of the lemma is similar to the proof of Lemma
3.6
and it is omitted.
We introduce difference operators

\mathrm{D}_{x}^{+}F(\eta)=F(\eta+\delta_{x})-F(\eta),\quad\mathrm{D}_{x}^{-}F(\eta)=F(\eta-\delta_{x})-F(\eta)

and the constant
c=\sqrt{p}^{-1}-\sqrt{p}
. The backward difference
\mathrm{D}_{x}F(\eta)
is only defined when
x
belongs to the configuration
\eta
, i.e.,
\eta(\{x\})\geq 1
. For
F\in\mathbb{D}
, let

\displaystyle K^{-}(\varphi)F(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\Bigl(\int\overline{\varphi(x)}\,\mathrm{D}_{x}^{+}F(\eta)\,(\alpha+\eta)(\mathrm{d}x)+\int\overline{\varphi(x)}\,\mathrm{D}_{x}^{-}F(\eta)\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr),

(20)

\displaystyle K^{+}(\varphi)F(\eta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\Bigl(p\int\varphi(x)F(\eta+\delta_{x})(\eta+\alpha)(\mathrm{d}x)+\frac{1}{p}\int\varphi(x)F(\eta-\delta_{x})\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{c}\bigl(2\eta(\varphi)+\alpha(\varphi)\bigr)F(\eta)

(21)

\displaystyle K^{0}(\varphi)F(\eta)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}\Bigl(\sqrt{p}\int\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{D}_{x}^{+}F(\eta)\,(\alpha+\eta)(\mathrm{d}x)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\int\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{D}_{x}^{-}F(\eta)\,\eta(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\varphi)F(\eta).

(22)

Remark
.

Let us denote the operator in the first line of (
22
) by
\widehat{N}(\varphi)
so that
K^{0}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\alpha(\varphi)+\widehat{N}(\varphi)
. An explicit computation yields, for real-valued
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
,

K^{+}(\varphi)+K^{-}(\varphi)+(\sqrt{p}+{\sqrt{p}}^{-1})\widehat{N}(\varphi)=c\,\eta(\varphi)-\sqrt{p}\,\alpha(\varphi).

The equation is also similar to relations between quadratic white noise, Pascal laws and Meixner polynomials, see
[
1
, Section 17]
,
[
3
, Section 4]
and case (iv) in Section
2.4
.

Given
a>0
and
p\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{M}_{n}(x;a,p)
be the Meixner polynomials of degree
n
with leading coefficients
1
, orthogonal with respect to the negative binomial law
\nu(\{x\})=(1-p)^{a}(a)_{x}p^{x}/x!
on
\mathbb{N}_{0}

[
32
]
.

Theorem 3.9
.

The operators
K^{+}(\varphi)
,
K^{0}(\varphi)
,
K^{-}(\varphi)
,

, are a Fock representation in
L^{2}(\mathbf{N},\mathcal{N},\rho_{p,\alpha})
of the current algebra of
su(1,1)
with vacuum
\mathbbm{1}
, the constant function
1
. Moreover, with
c={\sqrt{p}}^{-1}-\sqrt{p}
,

\displaystyle K^{+}(\mathbbm{1}_{B_{1}})^{n_{1}}\cdots K^{+}(\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\ell}})^{n_{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}(\eta)=\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}c^{n_{j}}\mathcal{M}_{n_{j}}\bigl(\eta(B_{j});\alpha(B_{j}),p\bigr)

(23)

for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, disjoint
B_{1},\ldots,B_{\ell}\in\mathcal{X}
with

,

and
\rho_{p,\alpha}
-almost all

.

The theorem is proven in Section
5.4
.

To conclude, we remark that for non-negative
\varphi
, the operators are amenable to a probabilistic interpretation.
The processes that appear are variants of the linear birth-death processes on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
called discrete Bessel and discrete Laguerre processes by Miclo and Patie
[

]

[

The lowering operator

The neutral operator

4.
The univariate case: three representations of
su(1,1)

Our three representations of the
su(1,1)
current algebra generalize much simpler univariate counterparts that we recall here.
The benefit is twofold. First, we connect to standard representation theory for
su(1,1)
and univariate orthogonal polynomials. Second, the probabilistic interpretation brings in well known processes: linear birth-death processes on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
and diffusions (squared Bessel and Laguerre) on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

4.1.
Representation in weighted
\ell^{2}
space

There are several classes of unitary irreducible representations of
SU(1,1)
. The representations studied here all belong to what is called the “discrete class, minimal
m
,
D_{k}^{+}
”
[
7
, §5.i]
or “principal discrete series
D_{j}^{+}
”
[
37
, Section C]
. Within that class, the representation is uniquely characterized by a half-integer
m_{0}\in\{\frac{1}{2},1,\frac{3}{2},\ldots\}
, sometimes called
Bargmann index
. The neutral operator
k^{0}
has simple eigenvalues
m_{0},m_{0}+1,\ldots
and the Casimir operator
(k^{0})^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(k^{+}k^{-}+k^{-}k^{+})
, analogous to total spin, is
m_{0}(1-m_{0})
times the identity operator.

The following is an example of such a representation. It is essentially taken from Giardinà, Kurchan, Redig and Vafayi
[
23
]
; it differs from the usual representations in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0})
by a similarity transformation. Remember the rising factorial
(\alpha)_{0}=1
,
(\alpha)_{n}=\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)
.
Fix
\alpha>0
and define weights
w_{\alpha}(n)=(\alpha)_{n}/n!
. We work in the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},w_{\alpha})
and define operators
k^{\pm}
,
k^{0}
with common dense domain
d
, the space of sequences
(f(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that have at most finitely many non-zero entries.
The operators are given by

k^{+}f(n)=nf(n-1),\quad k^{-}f(n)=(\alpha+n)f(n+1),\quad k^{0}f(n)=\Bigl(n+\frac{\alpha}{2}\Bigr)f(n).

Equivalently, with
e_{n}(j)=\delta_{n,j}
,

k^{+}e_{n}=(n+1)e_{n+1},\quad k^{-}e_{n}=(\alpha+n-1)e_{n-1},\quad k^{0}e_{n}=\Bigl(n+\frac{\alpha}{2}\Bigr)e_{n}.

(24)

and
k^{-}e_{0}=0
. One easily checks that
k^{\pm}
and
k^{0}
map
d
into itself and on
d
they satisfy the commutation relations (
4
). Moreover,
k^{0}
is symmetric on
d
and
\langle f,k^{+}g\rangle=\langle k^{-}f,g\rangle
for all
f,g\in d
.

When
\alpha
is integer, we get a representation of the group
SU(1,1)
with Bargmann index
m_{0}=\alpha/2
.
The representation maps the
2\times 2
matrix
A(\xi,\theta)\in SU(1,1)
from Eq. (
5
) to the unitary operator
U(\xi,\theta)=\exp(\xi k^{+}-\overline{\xi}k^{-})\exp(2\mathrm{i}\theta k^{0})
. When
\alpha
is not integer, we get instead a representation of the universal covering group of
SU(1,1)

[
48
]
. (Notice that
A(\xi,\theta+2\pi)=A(\xi,\theta)
no matter what, but
U(\xi,\theta+2\pi)=U(\xi,\theta)
only if
\alpha
is integer.)

4.2.
Representation with Laguerre polynomials

Let
\beta>-1
. The
Laguerre polynomials
in hypergeometric form are given by
[
32
, Chapter 9.12]

L_{n}^{(\beta)}(x)=\frac{(\beta+1)_{n}}{n!}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(-n)_{j}}{(\beta+1)_{j}}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}=\frac{(-x)^{n}}{n!}+O(x^{n-1})

they satisfy the orthogonality relation,

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}L_{n}^{(\beta)}(x)L_{m}^{(\beta)}(x)x^{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathrm{d}x=\delta_{m,n}\frac{(\beta+1)_{n}}{n!}.

(25)

For
\alpha>0
, let
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},w_{\alpha})
be the Hilbert space with weights
w_{\alpha}(n)=(\alpha)_{n}/n!
from Section
4.3
. Let
\mu_{\alpha}
be the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with density
\Gamma(\alpha)^{-1}x^{\alpha-1}\exp(-x)
and
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mu_{\alpha})
be the space of Borel-measurable square-integrable functions (complex-valued). By the orthogonality relation (
25
), the linear operator

U:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},w_{\alpha})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mu_{\alpha}),\quad f\mapsto Uf=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f(n)L_{n}^{(\alpha-1)}

is an isometry that maps
e_{n}
to
L_{n}^{(\alpha-1)}
and in particular
e_{0}
to the constant function
\mathbbm{1}
. The linear hull of
e_{0},\ldots,e_{n}
is mapped to the space of polynomials of degree at most
n
.
Upon setting
K^{\#}=U^{-1}k^{\#}U
we obtain a representation of the
su(1,1)
algebra that satisfies

K^{-}\mathbbm{1}=0
and

K^{+}L_{n}=(n+1)L_{n+1},\ K^{-}L_{n}=(\alpha+n-1)L_{n-1},\quad K^{0}L_{n}=\Bigl(n+\frac{\alpha}{2}\Bigr)K^{0}L_{n},

(26)

compare (
24
). For better readability we have dropped the superscripts on the Laguerre polynomials. The following proposition identifies
K^{\#}
as differential operators; it is similar to Proposition 4.13 in Groenevelt
[
25
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

We have

\displaystyle K^{+}

\displaystyle=-x\partial_{x}^{2}+(2x-\alpha)\partial_{x}+(\alpha-x),

\displaystyle K^{-}

\displaystyle=-x\partial_{x}^{2}-\alpha\partial_{x},

\displaystyle K^{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}-\bigl(x\partial_{x}^{2}+(\alpha-x)\partial_{x}\bigr).

Notice that both
L=(\frac{\alpha}{2}-K^{0})
and
-K^{-}
are infinitesimal generators of diffusions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The operator
L
is the generator of the
Laguerre semigroup

[
6
, Section 2.7.3]
which is the Markov semigroup of the
Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process
(CIR) in mathematical finance to model interest rate movements
[
17
]
. It has the Gamma distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
as a reversible measure. The lowering operator corresponds to a squared Bessel process.

Proof.

The generating function of our Laguerre polynomials is
[
32
, Eq. (9.12.10)]

G_{t}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}L_{n}(x)=(1-t)^{-\alpha}\exp\Bigl(\frac{xt}{t-1}\Bigr).

In view of Eq. (
26
), we have

\displaystyle\bigl(K^{0}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}nL_{n}(x)=t\partial_{t}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\Bigl(\alpha\frac{t}{t-1}+\frac{xt}{(t-1)^{2}}\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\Bigl(x\frac{t^{2}}{(t-1)^{2}}+(\alpha-x)\frac{t}{t-1}\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-(x\partial_{x}^{2}+(\alpha-x)\partial_{x})G_{t}(x).

This holds true for all sufficiently small
t
, the differential expression for
K^{0}
follows. For the raising operators:

\displaystyle K^{+}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^{n}(n+1)L_{n+1}(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}nt^{n-1}L_{n}(x)=\partial_{t}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-\frac{\alpha}{t-1}-\frac{x}{(t-1)^{2}}\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-x\frac{t^{2}}{(t-1)^{2}}+(2x-\alpha)\frac{t}{t-1}+\alpha-x\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-x\partial_{x}^{2}+(2x-\alpha)\partial_{x}+(\alpha-x)\Bigr)G_{t}(x).

Finally

\displaystyle K^{-}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}t^{n}(\alpha+n-1)L_{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\alpha tG_{t}(x)+t^{2}\partial_{t}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\alpha t-\alpha\frac{t^{2}}{t-1}-x\frac{t^{2}}{(t-1)^{2}}\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-\alpha\frac{t}{t-1}-x\frac{t^{2}}{(t-1)^{2}}\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(x\partial_{x}^{2}+\alpha\partial_{x}\bigr)G_{t}(x).\qed

4.3.
Representation with Meixner polynomials

For
p\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>0
, the Meixner polynomials are given by
[
32
, Chapter 9.10]

M_{n}(x;\alpha,p)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{(-n)_{j}(-x)_{j}}{(\alpha)_{j}}\frac{1}{j!}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{j}=\frac{x^{n}}{(\alpha)_{n}}\Bigl(1-\frac{1}{p}\Bigr)^{n}+O(x^{n-1})

They satisfy the orthogonality relation

(1-p)^{\alpha}\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{x}}{x!}p^{x}M_{n}(x;\alpha,p)M_{m}(x;\alpha,p)=\Bigl(\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{n!}p^{n}\Bigr)^{-1}\delta_{m,n}.

(27)

Consider the probability weights

\rho_{p,\alpha}(x)=(1-p)^{-\alpha}\frac{p^{x}}{x!}(\alpha)_{x}\quad(x\in\mathbb{N}_{0})

and the Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},\rho_{p,\alpha})
. By the orthogonality relation (
27
), the operator

U:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},w_{\alpha})\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},\rho_{p,\alpha}),\quad f\mapsto Uf=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f(n)\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{n!}\sqrt{p}^{n}M_{n}(\cdot;\alpha,p).

is an isometry. Let
\mathcal{M}_{n}(x;\alpha,p)
be the Meixner polynomials with leading coefficient
1
, then

Ue_{n}=\frac{1}{n!}\bigl(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{-1}\bigr)^{n}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\cdot;\alpha,p).

Let us define
K^{\#}=U^{-1}k^{\#}U
, then we obtain operators with common domain the set of polynomials, that satisfy

K^{+}\mathbbm{1}=0,\quad(K^{+})^{n}\mathbbm{1}=(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p}^{-1})^{n}\mathcal{M}_{n}(\cdot;\alpha,p)

(28)

in analogy with
k^{-}e_{0}=0
and
(k^{+})^{n}e_{0}=n!e_{n}
. The following proposition identifies the operators
K^{\#}
. We define forward and backward difference operators

\partial^{+}f(x)=f(x+1)-f(x),\quad\partial^{-}f(x)=f(x-1)-f(x)

with
f(-1)=0
. The proposition is similar to Proposition 4.7 in Groenevelt
[
25
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For every polynomial
f:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle K^{+}f(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{p}}{1-p}\Bigl(p(\alpha+x)f(x+1)+\frac{1}{p}xf(x-1)-(\alpha+2x)f(x)\Bigr),

\displaystyle K^{-}f(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{p}}{1-p}\Bigl((\alpha+x)\partial^{+}f(x)+x\partial^{-}f(x)\Bigr),

\displaystyle K^{0}f(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{2}f(x)-\frac{1}{1-p}\bigl(p(\alpha+x)\partial^{+}f(x)+x\partial^{-}f(x)\bigr).

We remark that up to additive and multiplicative constants, the lowering and the neutral operators are the generators of linear birth-death chains on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. As mentioned after Theorem
3.9
, those processes were already studied in connection with orthogonal polynomials by Karlin and McGregor
[
30
]
; Miclo and Patie
[
42
]
provided intertwining relations between these linear birth-death chains and squared Bessel and Laguerre diffusions. Our representation clarifies the algebraic similarity of the processes: they admit the same formal expression with the
su(1,1)
algebra.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

The generating function of the Mexiner polynomials is
[
32
]

G_{t}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{n!}\,t^{n}M_{n}(x)=\Bigl(1-\frac{t}{p}\Bigr)^{x}(1-t)^{-\alpha-x}.

We note

\displaystyle\partial_{+}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=G_{t}(x+1)-G_{t}(x)=\Bigl(\frac{1-t/p}{1-t}-1\Bigr)G_{t}(x)=\frac{1-1/p}{1-t}\,tG_{t}(x).

and

\partial_{-}G_{t}(x)=\Bigl(\frac{1-t}{1-t/p}-1\Bigr)G_{t}(x)=\frac{1/p-1}{1-t/p}\,tG_{t}(x).

Therefore, in view of
(K^{0}-\frac{\alpha}{2})M_{n}=nM_{n}
, we get

\displaystyle\bigl(K^{0}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{n!}t^{n}nM_{n}(x)=t\partial_{t}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\Bigl(-\frac{x}{p}\bigl(1-\frac{t}{p}\bigr)^{-1}+(\alpha+x)(1-t)^{-1}\Bigr)tG_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{x}{1-p}\partial_{-}G_{t}(x)-\frac{p(\alpha+x)}{1-p}\partial_{+}G_{t}(x).

Turning to the lowering operators, we note first,
K^{-}M_{0}=0
and, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle K^{-}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{n!}t^{n}M_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\frac{t}{\sqrt{p}}\bigr)^{n}K^{-}Ue_{n}=\bigl(\frac{t}{\sqrt{p}}\bigr)^{n}Uk^{-}e_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\frac{t}{\sqrt{p}}\bigr)^{n}(\alpha+n-1)Ue_{n}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\frac{t}{\sqrt{p}}\bigr)^{n}(\alpha+n-1)\frac{(\alpha)_{n-1}}{(n-1)!}{\sqrt{p}}^{n-1}M_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\frac{(\alpha)_{n}}{(n-1)!}\,t^{n}M_{n-1}.

We change the summation index in
G_{t}
from
n
to
\ell=n-1
, note
(\alpha)_{n}=(\alpha)_{\ell+1}=(\alpha+\ell)(\alpha)_{\ell}
and obtain

\displaystyle K^{-}G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{\ell}}{\ell!}(\alpha+\ell)t^{\ell+1}M_{\ell}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\bigl(\alpha t+t^{2}\partial_{t}\bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\Bigl(\alpha t-\frac{xt^{2}}{p-t}+\frac{(\alpha+x)t^{2}}{1-t}\Bigr)G_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\Bigl(-\frac{x}{1-t/p}+\frac{\alpha+x}{1-t}\Bigr)tG_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{p}}{1-p}\bigl(x\partial_{-}G_{t}(x)+(\alpha+x)\partial_{+}G_{t}(x)\bigr)

The above relations hold true for all sufficiently small
t
, the expressions for
K^{0}
and
K^{-}
follow. The expression for the raising operator is proven by similar explicit computations that we omit. Alternatively, the reader may check the adjointness
\langle f,K^{+}g\rangle=\langle K^{-}f,g\rangle
on a dense subspace of polynomials in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}_{0},\rho_{p,\alpha})
.
∎

5.
Proofs

In this section we prove our main results, namely the three representations of the
su(1,1)
current algebra, the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula, and the explicit action of the unitaries
\exp(k^{+}(\xi)-k^{-}(\xi))\exp(2\mathrm{i}k^{0}(\theta))
on exponential vectors. In addition, we prove an integration by parts formula for the Gamma random measure (Proposition
5.6
) that is of interest in its own right.

5.1.
Representation in extended Fock space. Proof of Theorem
3.2

We start with a lemma that helps prove the adjointness relations for the lowering and raising operators in extended Fock space.

Lemma 5.1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and non-negative or integrable
f:\mathbb{X}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{C}
,

\int f\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n+1}=\int\Bigl(\int f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)\alpha(\mathrm{d}y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},x_{i})\Bigr)\lambda_{n}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}).

(29)

Proof.

We have to evaluate
\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}\int f^{\sigma}\mathrm{d}\alpha^{|\sigma|}
. The permutations with
\sigma(n+1)=n+1
are in one-to-one correspondence with permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, they contribute the first term on the right in (
29
). Any permutation with
\sigma(n+1)=i\leq n
admits a cycle decomposition with a single cycle containing
n+1
and
i
and remaining cycles containing neither
n+1
nor
i
. Deleting
n+1
from the cycle containing
n+1
(e.g.,
n+1\to i\to 3\to n+1
becomes
i\to 3\to i
), we obtain the cycle decomposition of a permutation
\tau\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
that has the same number of cycles. These permutations contribute the second term on the right in (
29
).
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
V_{n}
be the space of linear combinations of symmetrized tensor products of indicators of sets
B_{j}\in\mathcal{X}
with
\alpha(B_{j})<\infty
. It is enough to show that, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the space
V_{n}
is dense in
L^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}(\lambda_{n})
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Cartesian products
B_{1}\times\cdots\times B_{n}
of measurable sets with finite mass
\alpha(B_{i})<\infty
form a generating
\pi
-system of the product
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{X}^{n}
. Therefore, by the functional monotone class theorem
[
11
, Theorem 2.12.9]
, the linear combinations of the constant function and indicators of Cartesian products are dense, with respect to the supremum norm, in the space of bounded measurable functions from
\mathbb{X}^{n}
to
\mathbb{C}
. It follows that the linear hull of
V_{n}
and the constant function
1
is dense, with respect to the supremum norm, in the space of bounded, measurable symmetric functions.

If
\alpha
has finite total mass, then
\lambda_{n}(\mathbb{X}^{n})<\infty
as well and we have
\mathbbm{1}\in V_{n}\subset L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\lambda_{n})
. Furthermore, uniform convergence implies
L^{2}(\lambda_{n})
-convergence. Thus, every bounded function in
L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\lambda_{n})
can be approximated by a function in
V_{n}
. As the bounded functions are dense in
L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\lambda_{n})
, we find that
V_{n}
is dense.

If
\alpha(\mathbb{X})=\infty
, then the previous argument still applies to
L^{2}_{\mathrm{s}}(\lambda_{n}\mathbbm{1}_{C^{n}})
, for every set
C\in\mathcal{X}
with
\alpha(C)<\infty
(hence
\lambda_{n}(C^{n})<\infty
). As
\alpha
is
\sigma
-finite, every function
f\in L_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}(\lambda_{n})
is the limit in
L^{2}
-norm of a sequence
(f\mathbbm{1}_{C_{j}^{n}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\alpha(C_{j})<\infty
and the proof of the lemma is easily concluded.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

By the expressions given above, the operators
k^{\#}(\varphi)
map symmetrized tensor products to combinations of symmetrized tensor products, hence
\mathcal{D}
is invariant. The common domain
\mathcal{D}
is dense by Lemma
3.1
. Clearly
k^{+}(\varphi)
and
k^{-}(\varphi)
are linear in
\varphi
while
k^{-}(\varphi)
is antilinear in
\varphi
, moreover the vacuum is a unit vector annihilated by all lowering operators. The vacuum is cyclic because repeated application of raising operators gives symmetrized tensor products, whose linear hull is precisely the dense space
\mathcal{D}
.

For the commutation relations (
7
), we note

\displaystyle b(\varphi)a^{\dagger}(\psi)f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\overline{\varphi}\psi f_{j})\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{1}\cdots\widehat{f}_{j}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

\displaystyle+2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(\overline{\varphi}f_{i}f_{j})\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\psi\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{1}\cdots\widehat{f}_{i}\cdots\widehat{f}_{j}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}(\psi)b(\varphi)f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}=

\displaystyle 2\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\psi\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\bigl(\overline{\varphi}f_{i}f_{j}\bigr)\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\widehat{f}_{i}\cdots\widehat{f}_{j}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

hence

\bigl[b(\psi),a^{\dagger}(\varphi)\bigr]f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}=2\widehat{n}(\overline{\psi}\varphi)\,f_{1}\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}\cdots\otimes_{\mathrm{s}}f_{n}

and

\bigl[k^{-}(\psi),k^{+}(\varphi)\bigr]=\bigl[a(\psi),a^{\dagger}(\psi)\bigr]+\bigl[b(\psi),a^{\dagger}(\psi)\bigr]=\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle+2\widehat{n}(\overline{\psi}\varphi)=2k^{0}(\overline{\psi}\varphi).

The other commutation relations, as well as Eq. (
11
), are proven by similar explicit computations that we omit; see also Śniady
[
52
, Theorem 4]
.

The adjointness relation for the neutral operator is straightforward:
k^{0}(\varphi)
is a multiplication operator that multiplies with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\alpha
, its adjoint multiplies with the complex conjugate. For the raising and lowering operators, let
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
and
(F_{n})
,
(G_{n})
in
\mathcal{D}
. We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\int\overline{G_{n+1}}\,(k^{+}(\varphi)F_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n+1}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\int\overline{G_{n+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\varphi(x_{i})F_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,\widehat{x}_{i},\ldots,x_{n+1})\Bigr)\lambda_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{n!}\int\overline{G_{n+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n+1})}\varphi(x_{n+1})F_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\lambda_{n+1}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}).

We apply Lemma
5.1
to
f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)=\overline{G_{n+1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)}\varphi(y)F_{n}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. The inner integral on the right of (
29
) becomes

\int\varphi(y)\overline{G_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{x},y)}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\alpha(\mathrm{d}y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(x_{i})\overline{G_{n+1}(\boldsymbol{x},x_{i})}F_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})

in which we recognize
\overline{k^{-}(\overline{\varphi})G_{n+1}}F_{n}
. Thus, we get

\frac{1}{(n+1)!}\int\overline{G_{n+1}}\,(k^{+}(\varphi)F_{n})\,\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n+1}=\frac{1}{n!}\int\overline{k^{-}(\overline{\varphi})G_{n+1}}F_{n}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda_{n}.

Summation over
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
yields the required adjointness relation. See also Śniady
[
52
, Theorem 3]
or Lytvynov
[
39
, Section 2]
.
∎

5.2.

First we check that the product of exponentials

in Theorem
3.4
is well defined on
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}
and
v,w\in\mathcal{C}
. Then:

(a)

k^{-}(v)^{n}f
vanishes for all except finitely many
n
, hence
\exp(-k^{-}(v))f\in\mathcal{D}
.

(b)

\exp(k^{0}(w))f
is in
\mathcal{D}
.

(c)

If
\sup|v|<1
, then
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left(k^{+}(v)\right)^{n}f
converges absolutely in norm.

The condition
\sup|v|<\infty
is satisfied for
|v|=\tanh|\varphi|
as
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
is bounded.

Proof.

It is enough to treat functions
f=k^{+}(\varphi_{1})\cdots k^{+}(\varphi_{m})\Psi
with
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\in\mathcal{C}
,
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Item (a) of the lemma follows from
\left(k^{-}(\varphi)\right)^{n}f=0
for
n>m
. For item (b), notice that

\exp\bigl(k^{0}(w)\bigr)k^{+}(\varphi_{1})\cdots k^{+}(\varphi_{m})\Psi=\exp\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}\int w\>\mathrm{d}\alpha\Bigr)k^{+}(\varphi_{1}e^{w})\cdots k^{+}(\varphi_{m}e^{w})\Psi\in\mathcal{D}.

(30)

For item (c), let
C>0
,
0<c<1
and
B\in\mathcal{X}
be such that
\alpha(B)<\infty
and
{\left|\varphi_{j}\right|}\leq C\mathbbm{1}_{B}
for
j\in{\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}}
and
{\left|v\right|}<c\mathbbm{1}_{B}
. Then

\bigl(k^{+}(v)^{n}f\bigr)_{\ell}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell})\\
=\delta_{n+m,\ell}\,\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+m}}v(x_{1})\cdots v(x_{n})\varphi_{\sigma(n+1)}(x_{n+1})\cdots\varphi_{\sigma(n+m)}(x_{n+m}).

Thus,

\left\lVert k^{+}(v)^{n}f\right\rVert^{2}\leq\frac{1}{(n+m)!}\Bigl((n+m)!c^{n}C^{m}\Bigr)^{2}\lambda_{n+m}(B)

and

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\left\lVert(k^{+}(v))^{n}f\right\rVert\leq C^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c^{n}}{n!}\Bigl((n+m)!(\alpha(B))_{(n+m)}\Bigr)^{1/2}<\infty.\qed

For the proof of Theorem
3.4
we adapt Truax
[
56
]
, with extra care for unbounded operators. For
t\geq 0
, set

\displaystyle v_{t}:=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\varphi\neq 0\}}\frac{\varphi}{{\left|\varphi\right|}}\tanh{t{\left|\varphi\right|}},\quad w_{t}:=-2\log\cosh{t{\left|\varphi\right|}}

and define

\displaystyle S_{t}f:=\exp\bigl(k^{+}(v_{t})\bigr)\exp\bigl(k^{0}(w_{t})\bigr)\exp\bigl(-k^{-}(v_{t})\bigr)f,\qquad f\in\mathcal{D}.

The operator

is well-defined on
\mathcal{D}
by Lemma

.
We show that

is norm-differentiable and solves the same differential equation as
\exp(t(k^{+}(\varphi)-k^{-}(\varphi))f
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The map
t\mapsto S_{t}f
is norm-differentiable on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, for every
f\in\mathcal{D}
, with derivative

\partial_{t}S_{t}f=\Big(\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v_{t})}k^{+}(\partial_{t}v_{t})\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w_{t})}\mathrm{e}^{-k^{-}(v_{t})}+\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v_{t})}k^{0}(\partial_{t}w_{t})\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w_{t})}\mathrm{e}^{-k^{-}(v_{t})}\\
-\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v_{t})}\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w_{t})}k^{-}(\partial_{t}v_{t})\mathrm{e}^{-k^{-}(v_{t})}\Big)f.

(31)

Note that
\partial_{t}v_{t}
,
\partial_{t}w_{t}\in\mathcal{C}
. The proof of the lemma is based on straightforward but somewhat tedious bounds and it is omitted.

The following commutation relations help us express the right side of (
31
) with
k^{+}(\varphi)
and
k^{-}(\varphi)
only.
We remark that
k^{+}(\theta)
,
k^{0}(\theta)
and
k^{-}(\theta)
are closable because they have densely defined adjoints. We use the same letters for the closed operators.

Lemma 5.4
.

For all
v,w,\theta\in\mathcal{C}
with
\sup|v|<\infty
, and all
f\in\mathcal{D}
, the following holds true:

(a)

[k^{0}(\theta),\exp(k^{+}(v))]f=\exp(k^{+}(v))k^{+}(\theta v)f
.

(b)

\exp(k^{+}(v))f
is in the domain of the closure of
k^{-}(\theta)
and

\bigl[k^{-}(\theta),\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v)}\bigr]f=\bigl(-k^{+}(\overline{\theta}v^{2})+2k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v)\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v)}f.

(c)

\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w)}k^{-}(\theta)f=k^{-}(\theta\mathrm{e}^{-\overline{w}})\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w)}f
.

Proof.

An induction over
n
, based on
[A,B^{n+1}]=[A,B]B^{n}+B[A,B^{n}]
and the commutation relations for
k^{0}(\theta)
and
k^{+}(v)
, yields

[k^{0}(\theta),k^{+}(v)^{n}]=nk^{+}(\theta v)k^{+}(v)^{n-1}

from which one deduces

[k^{0}(\theta),\exp(k^{+}(v))]=\exp(k^{+}(v))k^{+}(\theta v).

(32)

Next we check by induction over
n
that

[k^{-}(\theta),k^{+}(v)^{n}]=n(n-1)k^{+}(\overline{\theta}v^{2})k^{+}(v)^{n-2}+2nk^{+}(v)^{n-1}k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v).

(33)

For
n=1
the previous relation reads
[k^{-}(\theta),k^{+}(v)]=2k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v)
, which holds true. For the induction step from
n
to
n+1
, we write

\displaystyle[k^{-}(\theta),k^{+}(v)^{n+1}]

\displaystyle=\bigl[k^{-}(\theta),k^{+}(v)\bigr]k^{+}(v)^{n}+k^{+}(v)\bigl[k^{0}(\theta),k^{+}(v)^{n}\bigr]

\displaystyle=2\Bigl[k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v),k^{+}(v)^{n}\Bigr]+2k^{+}(v)^{n}k_{0}(\overline{\theta}v)+k^{+}(v)\Bigl[k^{0}(\theta),k^{+}(v)^{n}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=2nk^{+}(\overline{\theta}v^{2})k^{+}(v)^{n-1}+2k^{+}(v)^{n}k_{0}(\overline{\theta}v)+k^{+}(v)\Bigl[k^{0}(\theta),k^{+}(v)^{n}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=n(n+1)k^{+}((\overline{\theta}v^{2})k^{+}(v)^{n-1}+2(n+1)k^{+}(v)^{n}k^{0}(v\overline{\theta}).

The inductive proof of (
33

f\in\mathcal{D}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
. Eq. (
33

k^{-}(\theta)\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{n!}k^{+}(v)^{n}f\\
=\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{n!}k^{+}(v)^{n}\Bigr)k^{-}(\theta)f+\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^{N-2}\frac{1}{n!}k^{+}(v)^{n}\Bigr)k^{+}(\overline{\theta}v^{2})f+2\Bigl(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\frac{1}{n!}k^{+}(v)^{n}\Bigr)k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v)f.

The right side converges in norm as

by Lemma
5.2
, hence so does the left side. It follows that
\exp(k^{+}(v))f
is in the domain of the closure of
k^{-}(\theta)
and we obtain

\bigl[k^{-}(\theta),\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v)}\bigr]f=\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v)}\bigl(k^{+}(\overline{\theta}v^{2})+2k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v)\bigr)f.

In view of (
32
), we may also write

\bigl[k^{-}(\theta),\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v)}\bigr]f=\bigl(-k^{+}(\overline{\theta}v^{2})+2k^{0}(\overline{\theta}v)\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v)}f.

For (c) it is enough to consider functions
f=k^{+}(\varphi_{1})\cdots k^{+}(\varphi_{m})\Psi
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{m}\in\mathcal{C}
. We write down the proof for

, the general case is similar. We have

\displaystyle k^{-}(\theta)f

\displaystyle=k^{-}(\theta)k^{+}(\varphi_{1})k^{+}(\varphi_{2})\Psi

\displaystyle=\langle\theta,\varphi_{1}\rangle k^{+}(\varphi_{2})\Psi+\langle\theta,\varphi_{2}\rangle k^{+}(\varphi_{1})\Psi+2k^{+}(\varphi_{1}\overline{\theta}\varphi_{2})\Psi.

By (
30

\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w)}k^{-}(\theta)f=\mathrm{e}^{\int w\mathrm{d}\alpha/2}\Bigl(\langle\theta,\varphi_{1}\rangle k^{+}(\varphi_{2}\mathrm{e}^{w})\Psi+\langle\theta,\varphi_{2}\rangle k^{+}(\varphi_{1}\mathrm{e}^{w})\Psi+2k^{+}(\varphi_{1}\overline{\theta}\varphi_{2}\mathrm{e}^{w})\Psi\Bigr).

Similarly,

k^{-}(\psi)\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w)}f\\
=\mathrm{e}^{\int w\mathrm{d}\alpha/2}\Bigl(\langle\psi,\mathrm{e}^{w}\varphi_{1}\rangle k^{+}(\varphi_{2}\mathrm{e}^{w})\Psi+\langle\psi,\mathrm{e}^{w}\varphi_{2}\rangle k^{+}(\varphi_{1}\mathrm{e}^{w})\Psi+2k^{+}(\varphi_{1}\mathrm{e}^{w}\overline{\psi}\varphi_{2}\mathrm{e}^{w})\Psi\Bigr).

The expressions are equal for

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{D}
. Then,
S_{t}f
is in the domain of the closure of
k^{+}(\varphi)-k^{-}(\varphi)
, denoted by
k^{+}(\varphi)-k^{-}(\varphi)
as well. Moreover,

\partial_{t}S_{t}f=\left(k^{+}(\varphi)-k^{-}(\varphi)\right)S_{t}f.

Proof.

In Lemma
5.4

\exp(k^{+}(v))f
is in the domain of the closure of
k^{-}(\theta)
by proving that
k^{-}(\theta)\sum_{n=0}^{N}n!^{-1}k^{+}(v)^{n}f
converges in norm as

\exp(k^{+}(v))f
also belongs to the domain of the closure of
k^{+}(\varphi)-k^{-}(\varphi)
.
As
\exp(k^{0}(w_{t}))
and
\exp(-k^{-}(v_{t}))
map
\mathcal{D}
to
\mathcal{D}
by Lemma
5.2
, it follows that
S_{t}f
is in th domain of the closure, for all

. By Lemma
5.3
, the derivative

is a sum of three terms. The first is

. The second term is

\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v_{t})}k^{0}(\partial_{t}w_{t})\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w_{t})}\mathrm{e}^{-k^{-}(v_{t})}f=\bigl(k^{0}(\partial_{t}w_{t})-k^{+}(v_{t}\partial_{t}w_{t})\bigr)S_{t}f,

The third term is

\displaystyle-\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v_{t})}\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w_{t})}k^{-}(\partial_{t}v_{t})\mathrm{e}^{-k^{-}(v_{t})}f

\displaystyle\qquad=-\mathrm{e}^{k^{+}(v_{t})}k^{-}(\mathrm{e}^{-w_{t}}\partial_{t}v_{t})\mathrm{e}^{k^{0}(w_{t})}\mathrm{e}^{-k^{-}(v_{t})}f

\displaystyle\qquad=\Bigl(-k^{+}(\mathrm{e}^{-w_{t}}\overline{\partial_{t}v_{t}}v_{t}^{2})+2k^{0}(\mathrm{e}^{-w_{t}}\overline{\partial v_{t}}vt)-k^{-}(\mathrm{e}^{-w_{t}}\partial_{t}v_{t})\Bigr)S_{t}f.

\displaystyle=\varphi,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w_{t}+2v_{t}\overline{(\partial_{t}v_{t})}e^{-w_{t}}

Now we have all the ingredients to prove the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

As noted after Eq. (

), the closure

of

is self-adjoint. By Stone’s theorem, for every

.
Hence

5.3.
Gamma random measure. Proof of Theorem
3.7

First we prove that the space of polynomials
\mathbb{D}
is dense in
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
. We actually prove a slightly stronger statement: the space of polynomials in the masses
\mu(B)
, with
\alpha(B)<\infty
, is dense.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Let
\mathscr{U}
be the set of finite linear combinations of indicators
\mathbbm{1}_{B}
, with non-negative coefficients, of sets with finite mass
\alpha(B)<\infty
. For
u\in\mathscr{U}
, let
\mathscr{H}_{0}
be the set of exponential maps
F_{u}:\mathbf{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\mu\mapsto\exp(-\mu(u))
. Further let
\mathscr{H}
be the closure of
\mathscr{H}_{0}
, with respect to the supremum norm, of the linear combinations of exponentials. The set
\mathscr{H}_{0}
is closed with respect to pointwise multiplication and the vector space
\mathscr{H}
contains the constant function
1
and is closed in
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{M})
. Therefore, by the functional monotone class theorem
[
11
, Theorem 2.12.9]
,
\mathscr{H}
contains all bounded functions that are measurable with respect to
\sigma(\mathscr{H}_{0})
. In view of
\mu(B)=-\log F_{\mathbbm{1}_{B}}(\eta)
, the set
\mathscr{H}_{0}
generates the full
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbf{M}
. Thus,
\mathscr{H}
contains all bounded measurable functions.

As a consequence, the linear combinations of exponentials
\mu\mapsto\exp(-\mu(u))
are dense in
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mathbf{M})
with respect to uniform convergence, hence a fortiori in
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
with respect to
L^{2}
-norm. To conclude, we note that the exponential in turn can be approximated in
L^{2}
-norm by polynomials: Simply consider a truncated exponential series and bound the remainder, using that there exists
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{u}>0
such that
\int\exp(\varepsilon\mu(u))\rho_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}\mu)<\infty
.

We obtain that polynomials in the variables
\mu(u)
are dense in
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
. Every polynomial in
\mu(u)
, with
u
a linear combination of indicators, is also a a polynomial in masses
\mu(B_{i})
,
\alpha(B_{i})<\infty
. The lemma and the stronger claim stated before this proof follow.
∎

Next we prove an identity for Gamma random measures that will help us prove the adjointness relations (
8
). A straightforward integration by part shows that for every polynomial
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
, given
a>0
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}xf^{\prime}(x)x^{a-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{0}^{\infty}(x-a)f(x)x^{a-1}\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathrm{d}x.

The generalization for Gamma random measures is as follows.
We write
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\alpha}}[F(\mu)]=\int F(\mu)\rho_{\alpha}(\mathrm{d}\mu)
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\xi
be a Gamma random measure with law
\rho_{\alpha}
. For all
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}
and all polynomials
F\in\mathbb{D}
,

\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\alpha}}\Bigl[\int\varphi(x)\frac{\delta F}{\delta\mu(x)}(\mu)\,\mu(\mathrm{d}x)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\alpha}}\Bigl[\bigl(\mu(\varphi)-\alpha(\varphi)\bigr)F(\mu)\Bigr].

Proof.

We consider first exponential functions
F(\mu)=\exp(\mu(f))
and exploit that the Gamma random measure is compound Poisson. Let
\Pi=\sum_{i}\delta_{(X_{i},Z_{i})}
be a Poisson point process on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
with intensity measure
\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)z^{-1}\exp(-z)\mathrm{d}z
and
\xi=\sum_{i}Z_{i}\delta_{X_{i}}
. By the Mecke equation for
\Pi

[
36
]
,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{i}G(X_{i},Z_{i};\Pi)\Bigr]=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[G(x,z;\Pi+\delta_{(x,z)})\Bigr]z^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr\}\alpha(\mathrm{d}x)

for all measurable
G:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbf{N}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{R}_{+})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Let
\varphi
and
f
be non-negative test functions in
\mathcal{C}
. We assume that
||f||_{\infty}
is sufficiently small so that
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\alpha}}[\exp(2\mu(f))]<\infty
. The Mecke equation applied to

G\Bigl(x,z;\sum_{j}\delta_{(x_{j},z_{j})}\Bigr)=z\varphi(x)f(x)\exp\Bigl(\sum_{j}z_{j}f(x_{j})\Bigr)

yields

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi(\varphi f)\mathrm{e}^{\xi(f))}\Bigr]=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Bigl\{\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[z\varphi(x)f(x)\mathrm{e}^{zf(x)}\mathrm{e}^{\xi(f)}\Bigr]z^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-z}\mathrm{d}z\Bigr\}\alpha(\mathrm{d}x).

(34)

An integration by parts gives

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)\mathrm{e}^{zf(x)}\mathrm{e}^{-z}\mathrm{d}z=-1+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{zf(x)}\mathrm{e}^{-z}\mathrm{d}z.

We insert this identity on the right side in Eq. (
34
) (after changing the order of integration). This gives rise to two terms. The first, coming from
-1
, stays as is. For the second term, we apply again the Mecke equation. At the end, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi(\varphi f)\mathrm{e}^{\xi(f))}\Bigr]=-\alpha(\varphi)\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\mathrm{e}^{\xi(f)}\Bigr]+\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi(\varphi)\mathrm{e}^{\xi(f)}\Bigr].

The same identity holds true for
f
replaced by
sf
with
s\in(-1,1)
. We expand in powers of
s
, equate coefficients on the left and right side, and obtain

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\xi(\varphi f)n\xi(f)^{n-1}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\bigl(\xi(\varphi)-\alpha(\varphi)\bigr)\xi(f)^{n}\Bigr].

The compound Poisson process
\xi
has law
\rho_{\alpha}
and every polynomial is a linear combination of monomials
\mu\mapsto\mu(f)^{n}
. It follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\alpha}}\Bigl[\mu(\varphi f)P^{\prime}\bigl(\mu(f)\bigr)\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\alpha}}\Bigl[\bigl(\mu(\varphi)-\alpha(\varphi)\bigr)P\bigl(\mu(f)\bigr)\Bigr]

which is precisely the statement that we were looking for.
∎

The statement extends, by linearity, to all polynomials

Appendix A
Representation in standard Fock space. Araki’s scheme

Araki’s factorizable representations of a current group
G
are of the form
[
5
]

\mathcal{U}_{g}\mathscr{E}_{f}=\exp(\mathrm{i}c_{g})\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}||\varphi_{g}||^{2}+\langle\varphi_{g},f\rangle\Bigr)\mathscr{E}_{Q_{g}(f-\varphi_{g})}.

The unitaries
\mathcal{U}_{g}
operate in a Fock space
\mathfrak{H}
for which the single-particle Hilbert space has the structure of a fiber integral
\mathfrak{h}_{1}=\int^{\oplus}\mathfrak{h}\mathrm{d}\alpha
. The exponential states
\mathscr{E}_{f}
are dense. The unitaries
Q_{g}
form a representation of
G
in the one-particle Hilbert space
\mathfrak{h}_{1}
, they are diagonal with respect to the fiber decomposition. The phase
c_{g}\in\mathbb{R}
and the map
g\mapsto\varphi_{g}\in\mathfrak{h}_{1}
have to satisfy some consistency conditions (cocycle conditions). The difficulty in Araki’s approach is to find cocycles.

Here we sketch how our representation in extended Fock space on
\mathbb{X}
may be lifted to a representation in a Fock space on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{N}
and how it fits Araki’s scheme.

A.1.
Isometric embedding into a standard Fock space. Chinese restaurant process

We start with a known isomorphism of the extended Fock space with a subspace of a standard Fock space
[
33
,
38
]
.
Let
\mathfrak{H}
be a standard Fock space for the one-particle Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{N},\alpha\otimes\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\delta_{n})
. We include a factorial in the norm, i.e., the norm of an element
\boldsymbol{F}=(F_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
of
\mathfrak{H}
is

||\boldsymbol{F}||^{2}=|F_{0}|^{2}\\
+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m!}\int\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}}\bigl|F_{m}\bigl((x_{1},n_{1}),(x_{2},n_{2}),\ldots(x_{m},n_{m})\bigr)\bigr|^{2}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\cdots\frac{1}{n_{m}}\,\alpha^{m}(\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}).

In the picturesque language of the
Chinese restaurant process

[
45

represents a seating plan in a restaurant with

(More prosaically, think of

The total number of customers (particles) is
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{m}
and we may list the locations of the customers by repeating the entry

n_{i}

This induces a mapping from the extended Fock space
\mathfrak{F}
to the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}

The map

\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\boldsymbol{F}
is norm-preserving. It maps exponential states to exponential states: The image of
\mathcal{E}_{z}\in\mathfrak{F}

F_{0}=0
and

F_{m}\bigl((x_{1},n_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},n_{m})\bigr)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}z(x_{i})^{n_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}f(x_{i},n_{i})

is an exponential state in
\mathfrak{H}
.

Lifting the representation

The operators

K^{0}(\varphi)F_{m}\bigl((x_{1},n_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},n_{m})\bigr)\\

The lowering operator is

K^{-}(\varphi)F_{m}\bigl((x_{1},n_{1}),\ldots,(x_{m},n_{m})\bigr)\\

Matching the lifted representation to Araki’s scheme

\mathfrak{h}=\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N},\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n}\delta_{n})

Appendix B
Generalized Lebesgue measure and
SL(2,\mathbb{R})

Here we explain how our representations with the Gamma random measure relate to representations of the special linear group with the multiplicative measure. Let us first briefly describe the construction, following Tsilevich, Vershik and Yor
[
57
]
. We assume that the measure
\alpha
on
\mathbb{X}
is finite so that the Gamma random measure with law
\rho_{\alpha}
has almost surely finite total mass,
\mu(\mathbb{X})<\infty
. The measure
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
is by definition the
\sigma
-finite measure on
\mathbf{M}
that is absolutely continuous with respect to
\rho_{\alpha}
, with Radon-Nikodym derivative

\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{\rho}_{\alpha}}(\mu)=\exp(\mu(\mathbb{X})).

The definition is motivated by a simple observation in the univariate case: The Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Gamma distribution
\mathrm{e}^{-x}\mathrm{d}x
with parameter
1
, with Radon-Nikodym derivative
\exp(x)
.

The
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
current group over
\mathbb{X}
consists of the bounded measurable maps taking values in
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, the real
2\times 2
matrices with determinant
1
. The lower triangular subgroup consists of the elements
g:\mathbb{X}\to SL(2,\mathbb{R})
of the form

g(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a(x)^{-1}&0\\
b(x)&a(x)\end{pmatrix}

with bounded measurable maps
a,b:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. The map
a
is bounded away from zero.
For
\mu\in\mathbf{M}
, the dilated measure
M_{a^{2}}\mu
is given by
M_{a^{2}}\mu(B)=\int_{B}a^{2}(x)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)
. For
F\in L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
and
g
as above, define

\mathcal{U}_{g}F(\mu)=\exp\Bigl(\int|\log a|\mathrm{d}\alpha+\mathrm{i}\int ab\mathrm{d}\mu\Bigr)F(M_{a^{2}}\mu).

The map
g\mapsto\mathcal{U}_{g}
is a unitary representation of the triangular subgroup of the
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
current group (
[
57
, Section 5]
and references therein). The action of the full group is more complicated; in the univariate case it requires, in addition to the multiplication and dilation operators, an integral operator

[
57
]
.

To establish the connection with our representation from Section
3.3
, we first notice that the spaces are unitarily equivalent. The map
F\mapsto\tilde{F}
with
\tilde{F}(\mu)=\exp(\mu(\mathbb{X})/2)F(\mu)
is a unitary isomorphism from
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
onto
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
. In
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
, the unitary
\mathcal{U}_{g}
becomes

U_{g}F(\mu)=\exp\Bigl(\int|\log a|\mathrm{d}\alpha+\mathrm{i}\int ab\,\mathrm{d}\mu+\frac{1}{2}\int(1-a^{2})\mathrm{d}\mu\Bigr)F(M_{a^{2}}\mu)

For
a(x)\equiv 1
, this is simply multiplication with
\exp(\mathrm{i}\mu(b))
hence, in view of (
18
), this is the operator
\exp(\mathrm{i}(K^{+}(b)+K^{-}(b)+2K^{0}(b)))
. For
b(x)\equiv 0
and
a(x)=\exp(t\varphi(x))
with real-valued bounded
\varphi
,

U_{g}
maps
F
to

F_{t}(\mu)=\exp\Bigl(t\int\varphi\mathrm{d}\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\int(1-\mathrm{e}^{2t\varphi})\mathrm{d}\mu\Bigr)F(M_{\exp(2t\varphi)}\mu).

An explicit computation for polynomial
F
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{t}(\mu)

\displaystyle=2\int\varphi(x)\frac{\delta F_{t}}{\delta\mu(x)}(\mu)\mu(\mathrm{d}x)+\bigl(\alpha(\varphi)-\mu(\varphi)\bigr)F_{t}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\bigl(K^{-}(\varphi)-K^{+}(\varphi)\bigr)F_{t}(\mu)

and hence, the unitary
U_{g}
for
g
the diagonal matrix with diagonal entries
\exp(-t\varphi(x))
,
\exp(t\varphi(x))
, corresponds to
\exp(t(K^{-}(\varphi)-K^{+}(\varphi))
.

To conclude, let
k^{\pm},k^{0}
be the
2\times 2
matrices from Section
2.1
. The correspondence

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}(k^{+}+k^{-}+2k^{0})

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle k^{-}-k^{+}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}(k^{+}+k^{-}-2k^{0})

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}

extends to an isomorphism from the real Lie algebra
su(1,1)

onto
sl(2,\mathbb{R})
.

Thus, the representations with the multiplicative measure
\mathscr{L}_{\alpha}
agree with our representations with the Gamma random measure. We pass from one to the other by (1) mapping
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\mathscr{L}_{\alpha})
to
L^{2}(\mathbf{M},\rho_{\alpha})
, and (2) exploiting the isomorphism between the real Lie algebra
su(1,1)
and
sl(2,\mathbb{R})
.
