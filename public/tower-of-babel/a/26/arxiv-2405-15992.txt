Data Complexity Estimates for Operator Learning
Nikola B. Kovachki, Samuel Lanthaler, Hrushikesh Mhaskar

Abstract
Operator learning has emerged as a new paradigm for the data-driven approximation of nonlinear operators. Despite its empirical success, the theoretical underpinnings governing the conditions for efficient operator learning remain incomplete. The present work develops theory to study the data complexity of operator learning, complementing existing research on the parametric complexity. We investigate the fundamental question: How many input/output samples are needed in operator learning to achieve a desired accuracy ϵ \epsilon ? This question is addressed from the point of view of n n -widths, and this work makes two key contributions. The first contribution is to derive lower bounds on n n -widths for general classes of Lipschitz and Fréchet differentiable operators. These bounds rigorously demonstrate a “curse of data-complexity”, revealing that learning on such general classes requires a sample size exponential in the inverse of the desired accuracy ϵ \epsilon . The second contribution of this work is to show that “parametric efficiency” implies “data efficiency”; using the Fourier neural operator (FNO) as a case study, we show rigorously that on a narrower class of operators, efficiently approximated by FNO in terms of the number of tunable parameters, efficient operator learning is attainable in data complexity as well. Specifically, we show that if only an algebraically increasing number of tunable parameters is needed to reach a desired approximation accuracy, then an algebraically bounded number of data samples is also sufficient to achieve the same accuracy.

Introduction

In recent years, operator learning has emerged as a new paradigm for the data-driven approximation of operators arising in engineering and the physical sciences. Popular operator learning frameworks build on deep neural networks, suitably generalized to infinite dimensions. They approximate nonlinear operators, mapping input functions to output functions. Such operators abound in scientific computing and are often associated with the solution operator of an underlying partial differential equation (PDE), e.g. mapping initial conditions, boundary data, or coefficient fields to the solution of the PDE. In these applications, operator learning frameworks have shown great potential as surrogate models, achieving significant gains in computational efficiency over traditional numerical methods. Since they are data-driven, these methods can also be used to approximate operators for which a mathematical description in terms of a PDE is incomplete or not available.

While there is an increasing body of empirical evidence demonstrating the practical efficacy of operator learning frameworks, our theoretical understanding of these methods is still far from complete. There are several papers studying operator learning from an approximation theoretic perspective, aiming to bound the total number of parameters (model size) required to approximate a given operator, or general classes of operators, to a desired approximation accuracy. For a recent review, we refer to
[
22
]
. We highlight work on holomorphic operators, which represent a general class of operators for which
efficient
quantitative error and complexity estimates can be derived. Approximation theoretic results for holomorphic operators build on the influential work by Cohen, DeVore and Schwab
[
9
,
10
]
, including further developments in
[
38
,
36
,
39
,
18
,
32
,
25
,
39
]
. Under suitable conditions, these results allow one to prove that ReLU deep operator networks (DeepONet) approximating holomorphic operators can achieve convergence rates that decay algebraically in the number of tunable parameters of the architecture. Algebraic convergence rates have also been obtained for operator Barron spaces, which are discussed in the recent paper
[
20
]
, and a related context of operator reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS)
[
35
,
26
]

The previously mentioned results are restricted to either holomorphic operators or operators belonging to a Barron/RKHS space. Another general and natural class of non-linear operators are general Lipschitz continuous operators. Upper error and complexity bounds for the approximation of such operators have been considered e.g. in
[

,

\gtrsim\exp(c\epsilon^{-\gamma})

[

,
28
,
8

However, even with these non-standard architectures, the curse re-appears when accounting for the number of bits required to represent the real parameters to sufficient precision on computational hardware
[
24

The complementary question of the data-complexity of operator learning has received considerably less attention in the literature. The sample complexity of operator learning for holomorphic operators has been studied in

For general classes of Lipschitz continuous operators, we mention the early work by

Another paper of note is

The general curse of parametric complexity identified in
[
27
,
24

-width estimates derived in the specific setting of
[

suggest that operator learning on general classes of operators might suffer from an analogous “curse of sample complexity”, potentially requiring an exponential number of samples to achieve a desired approximation accuracy. A general theory which would imply such a curse of data-complexity has so far been outstanding. Furthermore, the empirically observed efficiency of operator learning for concrete problems raises the question how such a theoretical exponential lower bound on the sample complexity of operator learning can be reconciled with practical observations.

The results of the present paper shed new light on these fundamental questions. We develop lower and upper bounds on the sample complexity of operator learning. Our main contributions are outlined as follows.

Lower bounds.

We prove that operator learning on general classes of operators, such as Lipschitz operators or continuously differentiable operators possessing
k
bounded Fréchet derivatives, suffers from a curse of sample complexity; in the following prototypical settings we prove that approximation of such operators requires an exponential number of samples in a minimax sense that is independent of the reconstruction algorithm. We demonstrate such bounds for:

1.

uniform approximation of operators over common compact sets of input functions, defined by a smoothness constraint,

2.

approximation in a Bochner
L^{p}
-norm with respect to input functions drawn from a Gaussian measure with algebraically decaying covariance spectrum.

Upper bounds.

Taking the Fourier neural operator (FNO) as a case study, we prove that on a narrower class of operators, efficiently approximated by FNO in terms of the required model size, the data complexity of operator learning is similarly benign; specifically, we introduce suitable spaces of operators
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}

[

7
,
1

, and show that a number of samples that grows at most algebraically in the inverse of the desired approximation error
\epsilon^{-1}
suffices for approximation of operators in
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
. These results hold in the root mean square sense with respect to a probability measure
\mu
of input functions.

Lower data complexity bounds

In the present section, we provide an overview of our results on lower data complexity bounds. To promote readability, we will only outline the proofs of these results, and refer the reader to the appendices for detailed derivations. Before stating our main results, we recall two notions of
n
-widths, which provide a measure of the complexity of a space of functions/operators, and on which our theoretical lower bounds are based.

2.1
Two notions of
n
-widths

We recall two different notions of
n
-widths which will be the primary objects of study throughout the first part of present work. Let
(X,\|\cdot\|_{X})
be a normed vector space and let
K\subset X

be a subset. Define the
encoder

\mathcal{E}_{n}:X\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be any mapping such that
\mathcal{E}_{n}|_{K}
is continuous in the topology of
X
. Let the
decoder

\mathcal{D}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to X
be any mapping. Following
[
12
]
, we define the
continuous, nonlinear
n
-width
of
K
in
X
by

d_{n}(K)_{X}\coloneqq\inf_{\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sup_{f\in K}\|f-(\mathcal{D}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{n})(f)\|_{X}.

Suppose now that
X
is a real-valued, Banach function space, in the sense of
[
6
, Definition 1.3]
, over a topological measure space
(\Omega,\mu)
. Suppose that there exists a unique, linear injection
\iota:K\to C(\Omega)
such that
f=\iota f

\mu
-almost everywhere. Let
Z_{n}=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}\subset\Omega
be any set of points. We may then define, for any
f\in K
, the functional
\delta_{Z_{n}}:K\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\delta_{Z_{n}}(f)=\big((\iota f)(z_{1}),\dots(\iota f)(z_{n})\big)
. We define the
sampling, nonlinear
n
-width
of
K
in
X
by

s_{n}(K)_{X}\coloneqq\inf_{Z_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sup_{f\in K}\|f-(\mathcal{D}_{n}\circ\delta_{Z_{n}})(f)\|_{X}.

Note that, in general,
\delta_{Z_{n}}
will
not
be continuous in the topology of
X
. A special case when continuity holds is
X=C(\Omega)
. Another such case is when
Y\hookrightarrow X
is another Banach space which compactly embeds into
X
and for which
\delta_{Z_{n}}:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous. Then for any bounded set
K\subset Y
, Lemma
B.2
in Appendix
B
shows that the map
\delta_{Z_{n}}|_{K}
is continuous in
X
. In both of these cases, we obtain

s_{n}(K)_{X}\geq d_{n}(K)_{X}.

A particularly important case which we will often consider is
X=L^{p}_{\mu}(\Omega)
the Lebesgue space and
Y=W^{k,q}_{\mu}(\Omega)
the Sobolev space with
K=\mathcal{U}(Y)
the unit ball of
Y
. In this setting, whenever
Y
embeds continuously in
C(\Omega)
and compactly into
X
, the above lower bound holds.

2.2
Bounds for function spaces

In this section, we consider Banach function spaces defined over the measure space
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
equipped with the Lebesgue measure or
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with a Gaussian measure. The finite-dimensional results of this section prepare our later extension to the infinite-dimensional context of operator learning considered in section
2.3
.
Here, we establish lower bounds for the continuous, non-linear
n
-width
d_{n}
and the sampling
n
-width
s_{n}
of the
unit ball of
C^{k}(\Omega)
or
W^{k,q}(\Omega)
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
and of the unit ball of
C^{k}(\Omega)
in

.
For

, with

, such results have first been derived in

2.2.1
Lower
n
-width bounds for
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}

The following theorem summarizes our sampling width estimate for the unit balls in the function spaces
W^{k,q}(\Omega)
and
C^{k}(\Omega)
, with approximation error measured in the
L^{p}
-norm. We note that the sampling width is defined via point-evaluation; to apply this notion to spaces
W^{k,q}(\Omega)
, we impose that
k>d/q
if
q>1
or
k\geq d
if
q=1
, which ensures point-evaluation is well-defined by the Sobolev embedding
W^{k,q}(\Omega){\hookrightarrow}C(\Omega)

[
2
]
. Note that here and throughout the rest of this work, we denote by
\mathcal{U}(E)
, the unit ball of any normed space

; see Appendix
A
for further details on notation.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
. Let
K=\mathcal{U}\big(W^{k,q}(\Omega)\big)
for any
1\leq q\leq\infty
with
k>d/q
if
q>1
or
k\geq d
if
q=1
, or let
K=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}(\Omega)\big)
with

and

. Let

then there exists a constant
Q=Q(k,q)>0
such that, for any

,

s_{n}(K)_{L^{p}}\geq Q\left(1+\frac{d}{kp}\right)^{-k}d^{-k/q}n^{-k/d}.

The following theorem summarizes the corresponding non-linear
n
-width estimates. These hold only under the restriction

, but are true for a more general class of continuous encoders than the encoding by sample evaluation considered in the previous Theorem
2.1
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
K=\mathcal{U}\big(W^{k,q}(\Omega)\big)
for any
1\leq q\leq\infty
or
K=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}(\Omega)\big)
with
q=\infty
. Let
p\geq q
then there exists a constant
Q=Q(k,q)>0
such that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

d_{n}(K)_{L^{p}}\geq Q\left(1+\frac{d}{kp}\right)^{-k}d^{-k/q}n^{-k/d}.

Remark 2.3

In Theorems
2.1
and

, the constant is independent of the dimension

.
Furthermore, by considering this special case in each proof, we verify that the same lower bounds holds for

Remark 2.4
.

It has been claimed, for example in

]
, that

has been asymptotically determined for all

2.2.2
Overview of the proofs of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2

The proofs of Theorems
2.1
and
2.2

[0,1]^{d}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}Q_{j}

Our main technical improvement over

is the introduction of a free parameter

. This parameter controls the extent of the set

, with

as

. The choice of

can then be optimized to control the ratio of

, resulting in the following

which holds both in Theorems

and
2.2
. We note that a fixed choice, such as
\gamma=1/2
as in
[

]
, would give the same asymptotics in

, but would lead to a constant

with exponential dependency on

. We refer to Appendix
C.1
for the details of the required argument.

Remark 2.5
.

The argument used to prove Theorem

does not achieve the same lower bound for the case

Tracing through the proof given in the appendix, we note that (

) leads to

Then (

and therefore

and the expression on the right-hand side appears to overcome the curse of dimensionality, decaying at least as fast as

even in the limit

This inadequacy stems from using the Bernstein width to lower bound

as proposed in

2.2.4
Overview of the proof of Theorems
2.6
and
2.7

Detailed proofs of Theorems
2.6
and
2.7
are given in Appendix
C.2
. The main difference with the case of

is that the partition has to be modified so that the union of the support of all functions becomes

instead of

. We achieve this by defining an invertible transport map between the standard Gaussian measure on

and the uniform measure on
[0,1]^{d}
. In particular, we define
\xi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
by

\xi(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\rho_{1}(y)\>\mathsf{d}y,

to be the cumulative distribution function of

. We use

, applied to each variable

, to define a measure-theoretic isomorphism between

and

and are again based on a partition of the domain. The main difference in the present case are some additional technical complications arising due to the fact that derivatives now have to be applied to a composition of the bump functions

2.3
Bounds for spaces of operators

We will now consider the case when the goal is to approximate operators
\mathcal{G}
belonging to some class of operators
\mathsf{U}
and inputs are taken from an infinite dimensional, separable Banach space
\mathcal{X}
.

Reconstruction error norms.

We will restrict our attention to the following two approximation theoretic settings.

In the first setting, we fix a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
and the aim is to approximate an operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
, uniformly over
\mathcal{K}
. Thus, we study approximation in spaces of non-linear functionals
C(\mathcal{K})=C(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
, and the approximation error of an encoder/decoder pair
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
is measured by

\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})\|_{C(\mathcal{K})}=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}|\mathcal{G}(u)-\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})(u)|.

In the second setting, we fix a Gaussian measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and we are interested in approximating operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with respect to the norm
L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
. The approximation error of an encoder/decoder pair
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{D})
is measured by

\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\Big[|\mathcal{G}(u)-\mathcal{D}\circ\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{G})(u)|^{p}\Big]^{1/p}.

A class of
C^{k}
operators.

To define the relevant sampling and nonlinear
n
-widths, we need to specify a class of operators
\mathsf{U}\subset C(\mathcal{K})
or
\mathsf{U}\subset L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})
, respectively. In the following discussion, we consider the relevant class to consist of
k
-times Fréchet differentiable operators. We summarize the main points here; additional discussion can be found in Appendix
A
. Given
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
, we denote by

C^{k}(\mathcal{K}):=\{\mathcal{G}|_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}\,|\,\mathcal{G}\in C^{k}(\mathcal{X})\},

the set of “trace-operators” which is obtained by restricting to
\mathcal{K}
the set of
k
-times Fréchet differentiable operators in
C^{k}(\mathcal{X})
. Here
C^{k}(\mathcal{X})
is defined as the set of operators that possess globally, uniformly bounded derivatives. We will establish lower bounds on the sampling
n
-with and the nonlinear
n
-width for the unit ball
\mathsf{U}=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\big)
. According to our convention,
\mathcal{U}(C^{k}(\mathcal{K}))
consists of all nonlinear operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
possessing an extension
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C^{k}(\mathcal{X})}=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq k}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\|d^{\ell}\mathcal{G}(u)\|\leq 1.

Assumptions on
\mathcal{K}

In order to quantify the effect of working in the
infinite dimensional setting, we must ensure that the set
\mathcal{K}
is large enough. Note that if
\mathcal{K}\cong[0,1]^{d}
then
C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\cong C^{k}([0,1]^{d})

and the results of subsection
2.2.1
apply. Therefore
\mathcal{K}
cannot be isomorphic to any finite dimensional space and must be truly infinite dimensional for any new interesting effect to be observed. Following
[
27
]
, we make this notion precise by requiring that
\mathcal{K}
contain hypercubes of any dimension with algebraically decaying side-length. These hypercubes can be naturally related to smoothness classes when
\mathcal{X}
is a Banach function space.

Bounded Bi-orthogonal System.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we say that a sequence of elements
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
and a sequence of dual elements
\phi^{\ast}_{1},\dots,\phi^{\ast}_{n}\in\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
form a
bi-orthogonal
M
-bounded system
, if for all
j,k\in[n]
, we have
\|\phi_{j}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq 1
,
\phi_{k}^{*}(\phi_{j})=\delta_{kj}
, and

\|\phi_{j}^{*}\|_{\mathcal{X}^{*}}\leq M
.

Hypercubes.

We say that a set
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
contains
\alpha
-hypercubes
of arbitrary dimension with decay rate
\alpha>0
, if there exist constants
M,c>0
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a bi-orthogonal
M
-bounded system with elements
\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{n}\in\Omega
such that

\bigg\{cn^{-\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}\phi_{j}:y_{1},\dots,y_{n}\in[0,1]\bigg\}\subseteq\mathcal{K}.

The next proposition shows that prototypical compact sets on function spaces which are defined by a smoothness constraint, contain
\alpha
-hypercubes in the above sense. We focus here on
L^{p}
-based Sobolev functions
W^{s,p}
and continuously differentiable input functions in
C^{s}
. Extension of this observation to other spaces, such as Besov spaces, is also possible.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact Lipschitz domain and fix
s\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
,
p\in[1,\infty)
. If
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{U}\big(W^{s,p}(D)\big)
viewed as a subset of
\mathcal{X}=L^{p}(D)
then
\mathcal{K}
contains an
\alpha
-hypercube with decay rate
\alpha=s/d+1
. If
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{s}(D)\big)
viewed as a subset of
\mathcal{X}=C(D)
, then
\mathcal{K}
contains an
\alpha
-hypercube with decay rate
\alpha=s/d
.

The details of the proof of Proposition
2.8
are given in Appendix
D.0.1
.

Remark 2.9
.

The decay rate for
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{U}\big(W^{s,p}(D)\big)
could potentially be improved to
\alpha=s/d
, based on a wavelet characterization of Sobolev spaces. We do not pursue this refinement here, contending ourselves with the rate
\alpha=s/d+1
which is derived based on an elementary argument involving Fourier series.

2.3.1
Sample Complexity of Operator Learning on Compact Sets

We first consider the following setting.
Given a class of non-linear,
k
-times Fréchet differentiable, functionals
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\mathcal{K}
compact, we aim to approximate any such
\mathcal{G}
from data pairs
\{u_{j},\mathcal{G}(u_{j})\}
uniformly over
\mathcal{K}
, i.e. with respect to the uniform norm

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K})}:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}|\mathcal{G}(u)|.

(2.1)

More precisely, we aim to approximate
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\big)
, belonging to the unit ball of
C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
, uniformly over the compact set
\mathcal{K}
.
In this setting we can prove the following result on the sample-complexity in a minimax sense.

Theorem 2.10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
contains
\alpha
-hypercubes of arbitrary dimension and let
\mathsf{U}=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}(\mathcal{K})\big)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists a constant
R=R(k,\alpha,\mathcal{K})>0
such that

\displaystyle s_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq d_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq R\big(\log(n)\big)^{-(\alpha+1)k}.

(2.2)

Theorem
2.10
shows that operator learning of
C^{k}
-operators, with respect to the supremum norm on input functions, generally requires a number of samples
n\gtrsim\exp(c\epsilon^{-\lambda})
scaling exponentially in the desired approximation accuracy
\epsilon
.

Proof.

(Proof of Theorem
2.10
)

The proof relies on the following lemma, which we prove in detail in Appendix
D.0.2
:

Lemma 2.11
.

Suppose
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
contains
\alpha
-hypercubes of arbitrary dimension with decay rate
\alpha>0
. Then for any
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a linear embedding
\iota_{d}:C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d}){\hookrightarrow}C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
such that, for all
f\in C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})
,

\|\iota_{d}f\|_{C^{k}(\mathcal{K})}\leq Rd^{(\alpha+1)k}\|f\|_{C^{k}([0,1]^{d})},

for some constant
R=R(k,\mathcal{K})>0
, and

\|\iota_{d}f\|_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq\|f\|_{C([0,1]^{d})}.

Furthermore, there exists a continuous, linear mapping
h_{d}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathcal{K}
such that,
for all
f\in C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})
and
y\in[0,1]^{d}
,

f(y)=\big(\iota_{d}f\big)\big(h_{d}(y)\big).

Lemma
2.11
allows us to “embed” a finite-dimensional approximation problem in the infinite-dimensional approximation problem of Theorem
2.10
. This allows us to use the lower bounds on the continuous non-linear
n
-widths of Theorem
2.2
combined with an optimally chosen embedded dimension
d
to prove Theorem
2.10
.

Recall that
\mathsf{U}=\mathcal{U}(C^{k}(\mathcal{K}))
. Assuming Lemma
2.11
, we will now derive (
2.2
). The inequality
s_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq d_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}
follows directly by continuity of the delta functionals.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}:C(\mathcal{K})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary mapping such that
\mathcal{E}_{n}|_{\mathsf{U}}
is continuous and
\mathcal{D}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to C(\mathcal{K})
be an arbitrary mapping. Let
\iota_{d}:C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})\to C(\mathcal{K})
and
h_{d}:[0,1]^{d}\to\mathcal{K}
be the mappings from Lemma
2.11
. For any
f\in C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})
, define
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}:C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n}(f)=\mathcal{E}_{n}(\iota_{d}f)
. Furthermore, for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, define

\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to C([0,1]^{d})
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}(w)=\mathcal{D}_{n}(w)(h_{d}(\cdot))
.
From these definitions, it immediately follows that

(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n})(f)(y)=(\mathcal{D}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{n})(\iota_{d}f)(h_{d}(y))

for any
f\in C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})
and
y\in[0,1]^{d}
. Thus, by Lemma
2.11
, we find

\displaystyle|f(y)-(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n})(f)(y)|=|(\iota_{d}f)(h_{d}(y))-(\mathcal{D}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{n})(\iota_{d}f)(h_{d}(y))|.

Denote
\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}_{0}([0,1]^{d})\big)
then

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}}\sup_{y\in[0,1]^{d}}|f(y)-(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n})(f)(y)|\leq\sup_{\mathcal{F}\in\iota_{d}(\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0})}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}|\mathcal{F}(y)-(\mathcal{D}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{n})(\mathcal{F})(y)|

follows by the inclusion
h_{d}([0,1]^{d})\subseteq\mathcal{K}
. Note that, for any
\mathcal{F}\in\iota_{d}(\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0})
, there exists
f\in\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}
such that
\mathcal{F}=\iota_{d}f
. Then by Lemma
2.11
, we have

\|\mathcal{F}\|_{C^{k}(\mathcal{K})}\leq Rd^{(\alpha+1)k}\|f\|_{C^{k}([0,1]^{d})}\leq Rd^{(\alpha+1)k}

for some constant
R=R(k,\mathcal{K})>0
. It follows that
\iota_{d}\big(\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}\big)\subseteq\rho\mathsf{U}
with
\rho=Rd^{(\alpha+1)k}
. Therefore

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}}\sup_{y\in[0,1]^{d}}|f(y)-(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n})(f)(y)|\leq\sup_{\mathcal{F}\in\rho\mathsf{U}}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}|\mathcal{F}(y)-(\mathcal{D}_{n}\circ\mathcal{E}_{n})(\mathcal{F})(y)|.

Taking the infimum over all
\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we find

\inf_{\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}}\sup_{y\in[0,1]^{d}}|f(y)-(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n})(f)(y)|\leq\rho d_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}.

Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n},\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
are specific instances of encoder, decoder pairs build from
\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we find

d_{n}\big(\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}\big)_{C([0,1]^{d})}\leq\inf_{\mathcal{E}_{n},\mathcal{D}_{n}}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}}\sup_{y\in[0,1]^{d}}|f(y)-(\widetilde{\mathcal{D}}_{n}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}_{n})(f)(y)|

and therefore

d_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq\rho^{-1}d_{n}\big(\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}\big)_{C([0,1]^{d})}.

Note that Theorem
2.2
holds for
\mathcal{B}^{k,d}_{0}
with the same proof, therefore

d_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq Rd^{-(\alpha+1)k}n^{-k/d}

where we have re-defined
R
to absorb the relevant constant. The above inequality holds for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
we can therefore optimize by choosing
d=k(\alpha+1)^{-1}\log(n)
to obtain,

d_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{C(\mathcal{K})}\geq R\big(\log(n)\big)^{-(\alpha+1)k},

where we have, again, similarly re-defined
R
. This is the claimed lower bound on the operator learning
n
-width.

∎

2.3.2
Sample Complexity of Operator Learning in Expectation

Theorem
2.10
exhibits an exponential lower bound on the sample complexity of operator learning in the setting where the goal is to approximate operators uniformly over a compact set
\mathcal{K}
. One may wonder whether more benign bounds could be achieved if the approximation error was measured in a mean squared distance, or a more general
L^{p}_{\mu}
distance with respect to a measure
\mu
, instead of the uniform
C(\mathcal{K})
-norm (
2.1
).

The next theorem shows that the fundamental curse of data-complexity persists even with respect to a weaker
L^{p}_{\mu}
norm. To this end, we next consider a Gaussian measure
\mu
on a Banach function space
\mathcal{X}
, with at most algebraically decreasing eigenvalues,
\lambda_{j}\gtrsim j^{-\alpha}
, of the covariance operator.
We measure the approximation error with respect to the
L^{p}_{\mu}
-norm,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{p}_{\mu}}:=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}[|\mathcal{G}(u)|^{p}]^{1/p}.

(2.3)

The following theorem shows that the sampling
n
-width scales only logarithmically in the number of samples
n
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Assume the setting described above and let
\mathsf{U}=\mathcal{U}\big(C^{k}(\mathcal{X})\big)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for any
1\leq p<\infty
, there exists a constant
R=R(k,p,\alpha,\mathcal{K})>0
such that

\displaystyle s_{n}(\mathsf{U})_{L^{p}_{\mu}(\mathcal{X})}\geq R\big(\log(n)\big)^{-(\alpha+3)k}.

(2.4)

Remark 2.13
.

We note that the above theorem provides a lower bound only on the sampling width
s_{n}(\mathsf{U})
. We have not managed to derive a similar lower bound on the continuous non-linear
n
-widths
d_{n}(\mathsf{U})
in this setting. Our tightest bound exhibits a scaling of the form
n^{-1/p}\log(n)^{-\lambda}
(cp. Theorem
D.2
in Appendix
D.0.3
).

Theorem
2.12
shows that operator learning of
C^{k}
-operators, in the
L^{p}_{\mu}
-norm with respect to random input functions drawn from a Gaussian measure
\mu
, generally requires a number of samples
n\gtrsim\exp(c\epsilon^{-\lambda})
scaling exponentially in the desired approximation accuracy
\epsilon
.

The proof of Theorem
2.12
is similar to the proof of Theorem
2.10
, relying on the finite-dimensional lower bound implied by
2.6
instead of
2.2
. We refer to Appendix
D.0.3
for additional details.

3
Upper data complexity bounds

In the previous section, we derived lower bounds on the sampling widths and non-linear
n
-widths of spaces of operators characterized merely by their
C^{k}
-regularity. In particular, we show that, in this generality, operator learning suffers from a curse of data-complexity, generally requiring a number of data pairs which is exponential in the inverse of the desired accuracy
\epsilon
.

The present section will discuss a setting where more benign bounds on the data-complexity are possible, in the context of operator learning. The purpose of this section is to show that
parameter-efficiency
implies
data-efficiency
. More precisely, we aim to derive “efficient” upper bounds on the amount of data required to approximate a given operator, when we restrict attention to only those operators that allow for approximation by a class of neural operators with moderate model size. Here, we posit that efficient data-complexity bounds should require a number of data pairs,
n\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/\lambda}
, which scales at most algebraically with the desired accuracy
\epsilon
, for some fixed
\lambda>0
. The parameter
\lambda
is the convergence rate in terms of available data-pairs, since the error then decays as
\epsilon\lesssim n^{-\lambda}
. Similarly, we posit that an operator allows for efficient approximation by a class of neural operators
\Psi
, if the required model size to achieve given accuracy
\epsilon
scales at most algebraically with respect to
\epsilon
, i.e.
\mathrm{size}(\Psi)\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/\gamma}
. Here,
\gamma
corresponds to the theoretically achievable approximation rate in terms of model size. We aim to quantify the relation between the model convergence rate
\gamma
and the data convergence rate
\lambda
.

Setting.

Rather than trying to propose a completely general theory, we will develop these ideas for the special case of Fourier neural operators (FNO), approximating a relevant class of operators,

\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D),

mapping square-integrable input functions in a set
\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)
to square-integrable output functions. For simplicity and due to certain restrictions of the FNO architecture, the underlying domain
D=[0,1]^{d}
is taken to be the unit cube in
d
spatial dimensions where typically
d\in\{1,2,3\}
. Even though we restrict attention to this particular class of neural operators, we point out that the ideas that will be developed are much more general, and can readily be adapted to other frameworks, such as DeepONets
[
31
]
, PCA-Net
[
8
]
, and variants of these frameworks.

As will be explained in further detail in the subsequent sections, we will consider the following setting. We assume we are given:

1.

A compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)
of input functions on
D=[0,1]^{d}
,

2.

A probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(L^{2}(D))
, supported on
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.

\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K},

3.

An operator
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
belonging to a class of operators to be specified below.

The question to be addressed is how many data pairs
\{u_{j},\mathcal{G}(u_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
are needed to approximate
\mathcal{G}
to a prescribed accuracy
\epsilon
?

In the following, we will first review the FNO architecture, then define relevant spaces
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
consisting of operators
\mathcal{G}
that are “efficiently approximated” by FNO, and finally proceed to estimate the sampling widths and nonlinear
n
-widths of these spaces.

3.0.1
Fourier neural operator (FNO)

Fourier neural operator is an operator learning framework proposed in
[
29
]
. We first recall the notion of Fourier neural operators.

Architecture

Let
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}})
and
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{W}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}})
be two Banach function spaces, consisting of functions
u:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}
and
w:D\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}
, respectively. A Fourier neural operator (FNO) defines a nonlinear operator

\Psi:\mathcal{Z}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}})\to\mathcal{W}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}}),

mapping between these spaces.
By definition of the FNO architecture, such
\Psi
takes the form

\displaystyle\Psi(u;\theta)=Q\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ P(u).

(3.1)

where
P:u(x)\mapsto Pu(x)
is a linear, lifting layer,
Q:v(x)\mapsto Qv(x)
is a linear, projection layer, and the
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}:\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}})\to\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}})
are the hidden layers, mapping between hidden states
v\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)\in\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}})
. The hidden states are vector-valued functions with
{d_{c}}
components,
v:D\to\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}}
, belonging to a Banach function space
\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}})
. Here, the “channel width”
{d_{c}}
is a hyperparameter of the architecture. Each hidden layer
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}
is of the form

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v)(x):=\sigma\big(Wv(x)+Kv(x)+b(x)\big)

where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}\times{d_{c}}}
is a matrix multiplying
v(x)
pointwise.
K
is a non-local operator of the form

v(x)\mapsto(Kv)(x):=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big(\widehat{P}_{k}\mathcal{F}v(k)\big)(x),

with
\mathcal{F}
(and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
) the Fourier transform (and its inverse). The matrix
\widehat{P}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{{d_{c}}\times{d_{c}}}
is a tunable Fourier multiplier indexed by
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. It is assumed that
\widehat{P}_{k}\equiv 0
for
|k|_{\ell^{\infty}}\geq\kappa
, i.e. for wavenumbers
k
above a specified Fourier cut-off parameter
\kappa
. This Fourier cut-off
\kappa
is a second hyperparameter of the FNO architecture. We collect the values for different
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
|k|_{\ell^{\infty}}<\kappa
, in a tensor
\widehat{P}=\{\widehat{P}_{k}\}_{|k|_{\ell^{\infty}}<\kappa}\in\mathbb{C}^{(2\kappa-1)^{d}\times{d_{c}}\times{d_{c}}}
, which acts on the Fourier coefficients
\widehat{v}(k)=\mathcal{F}(v)(k)
, by

(\widehat{P}\widehat{v})(k)_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{c}}\widehat{P}_{k,ij}\widehat{v}(k),\quad(k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\;|k|_{\ell^{\infty}}<\kappa).

Finally, the bias functions are assumed to be of the form

b(x)=\sum_{|k|_{\ell^{\infty}}<\kappa}\widehat{b}_{k}e^{ikx},\quad\widehat{b}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{c}}.

The resulting FNO architecture depends on the channel width
{d_{c}}
, Fourier cut-off parameter
\kappa
and depth
L
.

Remark 3.1
.

Following the theoretical work in

, we assume the lifting and projection layers to be linear. We note that in practical applications,
P
is often replaced by a shallow neural network, resulting in a mapping
u(x)\mapsto P(x,u(x))
. Furthermore, the biases
b(x)
are often chosen to be constant in practice; extension of our results to this alternative setting is straightforward, but will not be considered here for simplicity of the exposition and to make our discussion consistent with the analysis of
[
21

.

We collect all tunable parameters in a vector
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
. Any parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
can be decomposed layer-wise, as

\theta=(\theta_{L+1},\theta_{L},\dots,\theta_{1},\theta_{0}),

where

\theta_{\ell}={\left\{W^{(\ell)}_{ij},\widehat{P}_{k,ij}^{(\ell)},\widehat{b}_{k}^{(\ell)}\,\middle|\,i,j=1,\dots,{d_{c}},\,|k|<\kappa,\,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right\}},

collects the parameters of the
\ell

1\leq\ell\leq L
. We denote by
\theta_{0}={\left\{P_{ij}\,\middle|\,i,j=1,\dots,{d_{c}}\right\}}
the parameters of the projection
P
and by
\theta_{L+1}={\left\{Q_{ij}\,\middle|\,i,j=1,\dots,{d_{c}}\right\}}
the parameters of lifting
Q
. Assuming that
d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}\leq d_{c}
, the dimension of
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle d_{\theta}\leq{d_{c}}d_{\mathrm{in}}+L({d_{c}}^{2}+(2\kappa)^{d}{d_{c}}^{2}+2\kappa{d_{c}})+{d_{c}}d_{\mathrm{out}}\leq 5(2\kappa)^{d}L{d_{c}}^{2}.

(3.2)

Consistent with practical implementations, we will assume throughout that the hidden channel dimension of the FNO is at least as large as both the input and output dimensions
d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}
. We include a list of hyperparameters in Table
1
to aid clarify notation.

3.0.2
FNO approximation spaces

In this section, the goal is to introduce certain spaces of operators
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
that are efficiently approximated by FNO, at rate
\gamma>0
. Roughly speaking, the space
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
is the most natural alternative to the space of
C^{k}
-differentiable operators in the context of operator learning with FNO. In contrast to the approximation of operators
\mathcal{G}\in C^{k}
, which is not tractable in general, the approximation of operators
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
is tractable by definition. Thus,
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
can be interpreted as a space of operators that allow, in principle, for efficient approximation by FNO. Our discussion is inspired by
[
17
]
, where similar spaces are defined for finite-dimensional neural networks.

Definition of
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
.

We first define a set of operators
\Sigma_{m}
as the set of all FNOs with Fourier cut-off
\kappa
, hidden channel dimension
{d_{c}}
, depth
L
and uniform weight bound
\|\theta\|_{\infty}\leq B
, satisfying the bounds:

\displaystyle\kappa^{d},{d_{c}},L\leq m,\quad B\leq\exp(m).

(3.3)

Given the sets
\Sigma_{m}
defined by (
3.3
), we next define
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
as the set of all continuous operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
, which can be approximated by FNOs at approximation rate
\gamma>0
. More precisely, by definition we let
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
if, and only if,
\mathcal{G}\in C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
and if there exists a constant
C_{\mathcal{G}}\geq 0
, such that

\displaystyle\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}}\|\mathcal{G}-\Psi\|_{C\left(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\right)}\leq C_{\mathcal{G}}m^{-\gamma},\quad\forall\,m\in\mathbb{N},

(3.4)

where we recall that

\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\|\Psi(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

We will define the following quantity on
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
:

|\mathcal{G}|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}:=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{m^{\gamma}\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}}\|\mathcal{G}-\Psi\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\right\}.

This quantity can be loosely thought of as a pseudo-seminorm. Technically, the homogeneity condition only holds approximately, so it is not a true pseud-seminorm (see
[
17
]
for extended discussion in the finite-dimensional case). On the space
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
we introduce the following quantity, which we loosely think of as the corresponding “pseudo-norm”:

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}:=\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}+|\mathcal{G}|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}.

The class
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
is arguably the relevant class of operators
\mathcal{G}
, for which meaningful approximation theory for the FNO architecture can be developed.
We will denote by
\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
the unit ball in
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}):={\left\{\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}\,\middle|\,\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}\leq 1\right\}}.

(3.5)

Our ultimate aim is to derive upper bounds on the data-complexity of operator learning with FNO over this set.

Remark 3.2
.

Our understanding of the class
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
introduced above
is still very limited. In particular, we are far from a useful characterization of
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
or even subsets thereof. Nevertheless, in specific settings, it is known that solution operators of certain PDEs, such as the solution operator of the Navier-Stokes equations, and the solution operator of the Darcy flow problem, belong to some
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
(cp.
[
21
, Thm. 26 and Thm. 28]
). These results can likely be generalized to many other PDE solution operators. At present, it remains unclear to which extent a general theory, including a (partial) characterization of the condition
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
, can be achieved even in the context of PDE solution operators.

Given the last Remark
3.2
, we will not attempt to further characterize
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
. Our goal in the following is instead to take
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
(or the unit ball
\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
) as our
definition
of the relevant set of operators of interest, and investigate only the data-complexity of operator learning in this class. Note that the definition of
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
is completely independent of any data; a priori, assuming that
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
only guarantees the existence of good approximations, but does not guarantee that good approximations can be found from data pairs
\{u_{j},\mathcal{G}(u_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
.

Approximation theoretic setting.

As already mentioned above, we will be interested in the approximation of continuous operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
, belonging to
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
. Throughout this discussion, we will fix a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}\big(L^{2}(D)\big)
, with support
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
. Our notion of “efficiency”, to be made precise below, will be related to approximation of the underlying operator
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
with respect to the Bochner
L^{2}_{\mu}
-norm:

\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}:=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\|\Psi(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

Measuring the error in the
L^{2}_{\mu}
-norm is motivated by the practical application of FNOs, which are usually optimized by minimizing a mean-squared error (MSE) over an input distribution. In the following discussion, we fix the compact set
\mathcal{K}
and the probability measure
\mu
with
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
. An important ingredient of our analysis will be the interplay between the spaces
C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
and
L^{2}_{\mu}\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
.

3.0.3
Data-complexity bound for FNO

In the first part of this work, we have shown that any deterministic, query-based algorithm needs an exponential number of queries to approximate arbitrary
k
-times continuously differentiable operators in the unit ball in
C^{k}(\mathcal{K};\mathcal{Y})
even for
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
. The goal of this section is to derive upper data-complexity bounds when the unit ball in
C^{k}
is replaced by the set of efficiently approximated operators
\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
of the last section.

Main result.

We first state our main result, and then outline the general strategy to derive this result. After this outline, we will proceed to provide the details of our derivation. Throughout the following discussion, we continue to denote by
\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)
the compact set of inputs, and by
\mu
be the probability measure on
L^{2}(D)
with
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
.
Our main result is the following bound on the data-complexity of operator learning on
\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
.

Theorem 3.3
.

There exists a constant
C=C(d,\mathcal{K},\gamma)>0
with the following property:
For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist evaluation points
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in L^{2}(D)
defining an encoder
C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)\to[L^{2}(D)]^{n}
by sampling, and an associated decoder
\mathcal{D}_{n}:[L^{2}(D)]^{n}\to\Sigma_{m}\subset C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\lambda)}},

where
\lambda:=8\gamma^{-1}
depends only on
\gamma
.

Remark 3.4
.

To prove Theorem
3.3
, the decoder
\mathcal{D}_{n}
will be identified explicitly; it is obtained via empirical risk minimization. We will refer to this map as the ERM decoder. In our proof, we will furthermore show that suitable evaluation points
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
can be obtained as i.i.d. samples from
\mu
.

Remark 3.5
.

It may be of further interest whether it is possible to obtain similar approximation rates when measuring the error as a uniform error over
\mathcal{K}
, rather than in the
L^{2}_{\mu}
-norm with respect to a measure
\mu
. In particular, whether one can achieve an upper bound of the form,

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\lesssim n^{-1/2(1+\lambda)}?

Unfortunately, there is little hope to achieve such error rates in the sup-norm; indeed, this uniform approximation problem has been shown to suffer from a curse of dimensionality in a finite-dimensional setting
[
17
, Thm. 1.1]
, requiring
n\gtrsim\epsilon^{-d}
samples to achieve accuracy
\epsilon
in
d
dimensions (see also
[
7
,
1
]
). In the present infinite-dimensional case, which roughly corresponds to the scaling limit
d\to\infty
, we can therefore not expect to achieve any algebraic rates.

If we restrict attention to the subspace
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}_{0}\subset\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
consisting of operators
\mathcal{G}
with constant-valued output functions, then we can identify
\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}_{0})
canonically with a set of functionals
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}
. In this case, the encoding
\mathcal{G}\mapsto(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n}))
takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we immediately obtain the following theorem as a simple consequence of Theorem
3.3
.

Corollary 3.6
.

There exists a constant
C=C(d,\mathcal{K},\gamma)>0
, such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist evaluation points
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in L^{2}(D)
, and ERM decoder
\mathcal{D}_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\Sigma_{m}\subset C(\mathcal{K};\mathbb{R})
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n}))\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\lambda)}},

where
\lambda=8\gamma^{-1}
depends only on
\gamma
. In particular, the sampling
n
-width
s_{n}
and continuous non-linear
n
-width
d_{n}
of
K=\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}_{0})
are upper bounded by

d_{n}(K)\leq s_{n}(K)\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\lambda)}}.

It is interesting to note that, even in the limit
\gamma\to\infty
, we only reach the asymptotic Monte-Carlo rate
\sim n^{-1/2}
. Thus, there is an absolute bound on the achievable data-complexity rate, even when the convergence rate
\gamma
(in terms of FNO model size) is arbitrarily high. This observation of a limited rate, even when
\gamma\to\infty
, is consistent with the rigorous theory-to-practice gap proved in
[
17
]
for ReLU neural networks, which indicates that this is a fundamental limitation and not a deficit of our analysis. The extension of this theory-to-practice gap to the operator learning setting is the subject of upcoming work
[
16
]
.

3.0.4
Outline of the proof of Theorem
3.3

We next outline the elements of the proof of Theorem
3.3
. Our proof is based on an error estimate for minimizers of the empirical risk over a relevant subset
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{m}
in the FNO model classes.
First, we will briefly review the definition of the empirical risk minimizer (ERM). Next, we will provide a general estimate that bounds the error of the ERM over
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}
in terms of the metric entropy of
\Sigma_{m}
. Finally, we will estimate the metric entropy of
\Sigma_{m}
, resulting in the claimed bound of Theorem
3.3
. We now proceed to explain this argument in further detail, with certain technical proofs delegated to the appendices.

Definition of the subset
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{m}
.

We define a subset
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{m}
by

\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}:={\left\{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}\,\middle|\,\|\Psi\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq 2\right\}}.

We note that for any
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
, if
\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}
satisfies

\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq m^{-\gamma}|\mathcal{G}|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}\leq m^{-\gamma},

then, since
\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}\leq 1
and
m\geq 1
,
\gamma>0
, we have

\|\Psi\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}+\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq 2,

and hence such
\Psi
necessarily belongs to
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}
. In particular,
\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
is well approximated by the relevant subset
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{m}
, at the same rate
\gamma>0
, i.e.

\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}}\|\mathcal{G}-\Psi\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq|\mathcal{G}|_{\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}}m^{-\gamma},\quad\forall\,\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}).

Empirical risk minimizer (ERM).

To derive the claimed upper bound on the data-complexity, we consider the minimization of the empirical risk functional,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\Psi(u_{j})-\mathcal{G}(u_{j})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)},

(3.6)

for certain samples
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathcal{K}
and a given
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
. Given the discussion of the previous paragraph, we restrict our search over the subset
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{m}
, consisting of FNOs with bound
\|\Psi\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq 2
, i.e. we consider empirical risk minimizers,

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}\in\argmin_{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G}).

(3.7)

We will denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G}):=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\|\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\right],

(3.8)

the “population risk” over the probability measure
\mu
. We note that minimizers of the empirical risk functional (
3.6
) exist in view of the following remark.

Remark 3.7
.

The sets
\Sigma_{m}\subset C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
, i.e. the model class of FNOs satisfying the bounds (
3.3
), are compact. In fact, we will show in Lemma
E.6
that, for any fixed FNO architecture
\Psi(\theta)=\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
depending on parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
, the parameter-to-operator mapping
[-B,B]^{d_{\theta}}\to C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
,
\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
is Lipschitz continuous. Hence, the image of the compact set
[-B,B]^{d_{\theta}}
under this continuous mapping is itself compact in
C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
. The constraint (
3.3
) in our definition of
\Sigma_{m}
allows only finitely many different architectures for a given
m
; say
\Psi_{j}({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
, with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta_{j}}}
and
j=1,\dots,J
. Thus,

\Sigma_{m}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}\Psi_{j}\left([-B,B]^{d_{\theta_{j}}}\right)\subset C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)\subset L^{2}_{\mu}\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)

is compact in both
C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
and
L^{2}_{\mu}\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
, being a finite union of compact sets.

Error estimate for ERM.

The intuition behind our estimates for the empirical risk minimizer is that for sufficiently large
n
, and for independent and identically distributed samples
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\sim\mu
, the empirical risk (
3.6
) should be a good approximation of the population risk (
3.8
), and in turn, the minimizer of (
3.6
) should be an almost minimizer of (
3.8
).
The first step in our derivation is to make this intuition precise and show that it implies the upper data-complexity bound of the next proposition.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\gamma>0
, and fix
\epsilon\in(0,1]
. Assume that
m\geq(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}
is an integer. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that

n\geq\epsilon^{-1}\log\left(2\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}\right).

Then there exist fixed evaluation points
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in L^{2}(D)
, such that for any given
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
, the empirical risk minimizer
\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}
in (
3.7
),
satisfies

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}-\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}\leq 145\epsilon.

The detailed proof of Proposition
3.8
is given in Section
E.0.1
.

Metric entropy estimate for
\Sigma_{m}
.

Assuming Proposition
3.8
, the main missing ingredient in our proof of Theorem
3.3
is to estimate the metric entropy of
\Sigma_{m}
. This is the subject of the next proposition and its corollary.

Proposition 3.9

(FNO covering number estimate)
.

Let
\bm{\mathsf{FNO}}_{\kappa,{d_{c}},L,B}
denote the set of FNOs on a
d
-dimensional domain
\Omega=[0,1]^{d}
, with Fourier cut-off
\kappa
, hidden channel dimension
{d_{c}}
, depth
L
and uniform weight bound
\|\theta\|_{\infty}\leq B
. Assume that
{d_{c}}\geq d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}
and
B\geq 1
. Then there exists a constant
C=C(d)>0
, depending on the dimension of the underlying domain, but independent of the hyper-parameters
\kappa,{d_{c}},L,B
, such that the metric entropy of
\bm{\mathsf{FNO}}=\bm{\mathsf{FNO}}_{\kappa,{d_{c}},L,B}
is upper bounded by,

\log\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathsf{FNO}},\epsilon)_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq C\kappa^{d}L^{2}{d_{c}}^{2}\log\left(\frac{BL{d_{c}}\kappa}{\epsilon}\right).

We provide the details of the proof of Proposition
3.9
in Appendix
E.0.2
. The following corollary follows from the upper bounds (
3.3
).

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)
be compact. Let
\Sigma_{m}\subset C\big(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)\big)
denote the set of FNOs. Then there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{K},d)>0
, such that, for all
\epsilon>0
,

\log\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq Cm^{7}\log(m\epsilon^{-1}).

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Given the results of Proposition
3.8
and Corollary
3.10
, we can now prove Theorem
3.3
.

Proof.

(Proof of Theorem
3.3
)
Let
\epsilon\in(0,1]
be given, and let us choose an integer
m\geq(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}
; in fact, we make the specific choice,

m:=\left\lceil(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}\right\rceil,

so that
m\sim(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}
.
Proposition
3.8
shows that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
with

\displaystyle n\geq\epsilon^{-1}\log\left(2\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}\right),

(3.9)

there exist samples
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in L^{2}(D)
, such that the corresponding ERM decoder,

\mathcal{D}_{n}:\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\mapsto\Psi_{\mathcal{G}},

defined by minimization of the empirical risk over
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}
, satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}\leq 145\epsilon.

(3.10)

As a consequence of Corollary
3.10
, and the fact that
\epsilon^{-1}\sim m^{\gamma}
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,\mathcal{K},\gamma)>0
, such that

\log\left(2\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}\right)\leq Cm^{7}\log(m).

In particular, it follows that

\epsilon^{-1}\log\left(2\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}\right)\lesssim m^{\gamma+7}\log(m)\sim\epsilon^{-1-7\gamma^{-1}}\log(\epsilon^{-1})\lesssim\epsilon^{-1-8\gamma^{-1}},

with an implied constant
C=C(d,\mathcal{K},\gamma)
depending only on
d
,
\mathcal{K}
and
\gamma
. Let us summarize the resulting bound:

\displaystyle\epsilon^{-1}\log(2\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2})\leq C\epsilon^{-(1+8\gamma^{-1})},

(3.11)

with constant
C=C(d,\mathcal{K},\gamma)
.
In particular, it follows from (
3.11
) that for the specific choice

n:=\left\lceil C\epsilon^{-(1+8\gamma^{-1})}\right\rceil\geq\epsilon^{-1}\log(2\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}),

the bound (
3.9
) is satisfied, and hence, by (
3.10
),
n
samples suffice to achieve approximation accuracy,

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}\leq 145\epsilon.

The implied constant in the asymptotic relation only depends on
d,\mathcal{K},\gamma
. In particular, upon estimating
\epsilon
in terms of
n\sim\epsilon^{-(1+8\gamma^{-1})}
, we conclude that there exists a constant
C=C(d,\mathcal{K},\gamma)>0
, such that

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{1+8\gamma^{-1}}},

or equivalently, upon introducing
\lambda:=8\gamma^{-1}
and taking the square-root of both sides,

\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})}\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{D}_{n}\big(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{n})\big)\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}\leq Cn^{-\frac{1}{2(1+\lambda)}}.

This is the claimed upper bound.
∎

4
Conclusion

Operator learning is rapidly emerging as a new paradigm to complement traditional numerical solvers in scientific computing.
While empirical evidence demonstrates the practical efficacy of operator learning frameworks, a complete theoretical explanation of this empirical observation is still outstanding. Several papers have studied operator learning from an approximation theoretic perspective, with the goal of bounding the total number of parameters (model size). The complementary question of the data-complexity of operator learning has received considerably less attention in the literature.

In this paper, we have studied the sample complexity of operator learning in three main settings: (1) uniform approximation of Fréchet differentiable operators, (2) approximation of Fréchet differentiable operators in the
L^{p}_{\mu}
-norm and, (3) the sample complexity of operator learning in spaces of operators which theoretically allow for efficient approximation by FNO at moderate model size.

Our results in the settings (1) and (2) show rigorously that operator learning on general classes of Fréchet differentiable operators suffers from a “curse of sample complexity”, requiring an exponential number of samples to achieve a desired approximation accuracy. In particular, this result implies that existing bounds on the generalization error which suffer from such an exponential curse of dimensionality, as derived in
[
30
]
, are essentially optimal. Our complexity estimates generalize well-known bounds to the infinite-dimensional context and complement the notion of the “curse of parametric complexity” from
[
27
,
23
]
to establish a similar curse for the sample complexity. These exponential lower bounds on the complexity of operator learning strongly suggest that a theory of operator learning should be developed on spaces of operators that are considerably smaller than the set of bounded Fréchet (or Lipschitz) operators. As a consequence of these lower bounds, we argue that the empirically observed efficiency of operator learning for concrete problems cannot be explained by general arguments based on notions of operator smoothness such as Lipschitz continuity or Fréchet differentiability.

In the second part of this paper, we study the sample complexity of operator learning on sets of operators which theoretically allow for efficient approximation, requiring only a modest number of tunable parameters, growing at most algebraically in the inverse of the desired approximation accuracy
\epsilon
. To illustrate the main ideas, we take the Fourier neural operator (FNO) as a case study, and propose a rigorous definition of the relevant approximation spaces
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
. For these spaces, we prove that a number of samples which grows at most algebraically in the inverse of the desired approximation error
\epsilon
is sufficient for operator learning. These results hold in the root mean square sense with respect to a probability measure
\mu
of input functions supported on a compact set.

While we restrict attention to a specific setting to derive efficient upper sample complexity bounds in the second part of this work, we expect the basic ideas outlined in present work to apply to many other operator learning frameworks. We hope these results will motivate future investigations of the relevant approximation spaces
\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}
and provide a new point of view on the practical successes of operator learning.

Appendix A
Spaces and sets of differentiable functionals

C^{k}(\Omega)
-spaces in finite-dimension.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
be a set. We denote by
C^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})=C(\Omega;\mathbb{R})=C(\Omega)
the set of continuous, bounded functions on
\Omega
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
. We equip
C(\Omega)
with the norm

\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}|f(x)|,\qquad f\in C(\Omega)

which makes it a Banach space. For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we denote by
C^{k}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})=C^{k}(\Omega)
the set of bounded,
k
-times continuously differentiable functions on
\Omega
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
.
We equip
C^{k}(\Omega)
with the norm

\|f\|_{C^{k}}=\max_{0\leq|\nu|_{1}\leq k}\|D^{\nu}f\|_{\infty},\qquad f\in C^{k}(\Omega)

which makes it a Banach space. We denote by
\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
any multi-index and by
|\cdot|_{p}
the
p
-th order Euclidean norm.
D^{\nu}
denotes the partial derivative operator with respect to the multi-index
\nu
.
We write
C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})=C^{\infty}(\Omega)
for the set of infinitely differentiable functions on
\Omega
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
and similarly
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})=C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
for the subset of
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
containing all compactly supported functions. Furthermore, we denote by
C_{0}(\Omega)\subset C(\Omega)
, the subset of functions which decay to zero at
\partial\Omega
.

Fréchet differentiable functionals.

When
\mathcal{X}
is an infinite-dimensional Banach space, we say that a non-linear functional
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is Fréchet differentiable, if for any
u\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a bounded linear functional
d\mathcal{G}(u):\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that

d\mathcal{G}(u)[v]=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{G}(u+\delta v)-\mathcal{G}(u)}{\delta},\quad\forall\,v\in\mathcal{X}.

Furthermore, we will say that
\mathcal{G}
is continuously Fréchet differentiable, if
\mathcal{G}
is Fréchet differentiable, and the differential
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
,
u\mapsto d\mathcal{G}(u)
is continuous as a mapping from
\mathcal{X}
into the space of bounded linear functionals
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
, equipped with the operator norm,

\|d\mathcal{G}(u)\|:=\sup_{v\neq 0}\frac{|d\mathcal{G}(u)[v]|}{\|v\|}.

Higher-order differentiability is defined analogously. For example, we say that
\mathcal{G}
is twice Fréchet differentiable, if
\mathcal{G}
is Fréchet differentiable, and
d\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
is differentiable; i.e. if there exists a bounded linear operator
d^{2}\mathcal{G}(u):\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
, such that

d^{2}\mathcal{G}(u)[v]=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{d\mathcal{G}(u+\delta v)-d\mathcal{G}(u)}{\delta},\quad\forall\,v\in\mathcal{X}.

Upon identifying linear operators
\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
in the canonical way with bounded bi-linear maps in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
, i.e. using the isomorphism,

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R}))\simeq\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R}),

we can interpret
d^{2}\mathcal{G}(u):\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
as a bilinear operator. We say that
\mathcal{G}
is twice continuously differentiable if
u\mapsto d^{2}\mathcal{G}(u)
is continuous. Proceeding iteratively, the
k
-th Fréchet derivative
\mathcal{G}
is defined analogously. In particular, the
k
-th Fréchet differential is given by a bounded
k
-linear operator,

d^{k}\mathcal{G}(u):\underbrace{\mathcal{X}\times\dots\times\mathcal{X}}_{\text{$k$ times}}\to\mathbb{R},

with norm

\|d^{k}\mathcal{G}(u)\|=\sup_{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\neq 0}\frac{|d^{k}\mathcal{G}(u)[v_{1},\dots,v_{k}]|}{\|v_{1}\|\dots\|v_{k}\|}.

Consistent with the finite-dimensional case, we introduce the spaces

consisting of

, with

, i.e. such that the quantity

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C^{k}(\mathcal{X})}:=\max_{0\leq\ell\leq k}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\|d^{\ell}\mathcal{G}(u)\|<\infty.

The unit ball
\mathcal{U}(C^{k}(\mathcal{X}))
is defined as the set of
\mathcal{G}\in C^{k}(\mathcal{X})
such that
\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C^{k}(\mathcal{X})}\leq 1
.

C^{k}(\mathcal{K})
trace-functionals for
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
compact.

If
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
is a compact set, then we will denote by

C^{k}(\mathcal{K}):=\{\mathcal{G}|_{\mathcal{K}}\,|\,\mathcal{G}\in C^{k}(\mathcal{X})\},

the restriction to

of functionals belonging to

. Similarly, we denote by

\mathcal{U}(C^{k}(\mathcal{K})):=\{\mathcal{G}|_{\mathcal{K}}\,|\,\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(C^{k}(\mathcal{X}))\},

the corresponding “unit ball”. We emphasize that derivatives are usually not well-defined for functionals
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
is compact, and hence, we effectively work with a set of trace-functionals, i.e. nonlinear functionals possessing an extension to all of

with globally bounded

to define a set of Fréchet differentiable operators of interest. When studying the approximation of functionals

on a compact set of inputs

Appendix B
Continuity of linear maps over pre-compact sets

We will show that over pre-compact sets, all norms are essentially equivalent which will allows us to lower bound the sampling width by the non-linear width for many useful situations. Consider two normed vectors spaces
(X,{\|\cdot\|_{X}})
and
(Y,\|\cdot\|_{Y})
and suppose that
Y
compactly embeds in
X
. Then we have the following results.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
K\subset Y
be a bounded set. Then
K
is pre-compact in
X
and there exists a constant
C=C(K)>0
such that

\|f\|_{Y}\leq C\|f\|_{X},\qquad\forall\>f\in K.

Proof.

Pre-compactness of
K
follows immediately by the compact embedding
{Y\hookrightarrow X}
. Since
K
is bounded, there exists some constant
C_{1}>0
such that

K\subseteq\widetilde{K}\coloneqq\{f\in Y:\|f\|_{Y}\leq C_{1}\}.

Consider
\partial\widetilde{K}=\{f\in Y:\|f\|_{Y}=C_{1}\}
. Since this is a closed and bounded set in
Y
, it is compact in
X
. Therefore
\|\cdot\|_{X}
is uniformly continuous on
\partial\widetilde{K}
. Since
0\notin\partial\widetilde{K}
, there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that
C_{2}\leq\|f\|_{X}
for all
f\in\partial\widetilde{K}
. It follows that

\sup_{f\in\partial\widetilde{K}}\frac{\|f\|_{Y}}{\|f\|_{X}}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}

and, in particular,

\|f\|_{Y}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\|f\|_{X},\qquad\forall\>f\in\partial\widetilde{K}.

Now let
f\in\widetilde{K}
and assume that
f\neq 0
, noting that the desired inequality for the case
f=0
follows trivially since
\|0\|_{Y}=\|0\|_{X}=0
. Define
g\coloneqq C_{1}f/\|f\|_{Y}
then clearly
g\in\partial\widetilde{K}

hence

\|g\|_{Y}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\|g\|_{X}.

Plugging in the definition of
g
implies

\|f\|_{Y}\leq\frac{C_{1}}{C_{2}}\|f\|_{X}.

Since
f
was arbitrary and
K\subseteq\widetilde{K}
, the result follows.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Let
K\subset Y
be a bounded set and let
E:Y\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a continuous, linear map. Then there exists a constant
C=C(E,K)>0
such that

|E(f)-E(g)|\leq C\|f-g\|_{X},\qquad\>\forall f,g\in K.

In particular,
E|_{K}
is continuous in
X
.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in K
. Since
E
is continuous and linear, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

|E(f)-E(g)|\leq C_{1}\|f-g\|_{Y}.

Define
\widetilde{K}\coloneqq\{f-g\in Y:f,g\in K\}
. The triangle inequality implies that
\widetilde{K}
is bounded since
K
is bounded. Therefore Lemma
B.1
implies that there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\|f-g\|_{Y}\leq C_{2}\|f-g\|_{X}.

Hence,

|E(f)-E(g)|\leq C_{1}C_{2}\|f-g\|_{X}

as desired.
∎

Proof.

(Proof of Proposition

Let
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
be given.
And let

\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}

\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{\mathcal{G}};\mathcal{G})=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\|\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}\right]=\|\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}-\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{2}_{\mu}}^{2}.

\mathcal{L}
.

Following a well-known strategy from statistical learning theory, our first step in estimating the population risk
\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{\mathcal{G}};\mathcal{G})
is to split this risk into an approximation error contribution and an estimation error contribution. Upon bounding the approximation error based on the assumption that
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})

E.2

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{\mathcal{G}};\mathcal{G})\leq m^{-2\gamma}+2\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}),\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}}[\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})].

(E.1)

To bound the estimation error contribution, we will follow some ideas of

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \sup_{u\in K}\|\Psi(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}&=\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\\
&\leq\|\Psi\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}+\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\\
&\leq 3.\end{aligned}

(E.2)

For iid samples
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\sim\mu
, we now define iid random variables

Y_{j}:=\|\Psi(u_{j})-\mathcal{G}(u_{j})\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}.

Note that the random variables satisfy
0\leq Y_{j}\leq 9
, and in particular, the
Y_{j}
are non-negative. Therefore, by Bernstein’s inequality
[
41
, Prop. 2.14, in particular (2.23)]
, we obtain that for any
\beta>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left[\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})\geq\beta\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Prob}\left[\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}\geq\beta\right]

(E.3)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{2}n}{\frac{2}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}^{2}]}\right),

(E.4)

where, by (
E.2
), we can estimate

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}[Y_{j}^{2}]\leq 9\mathbb{E}\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|^{2}=9\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G}).

Replacing
\beta
by the product
\alpha\,(\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+\rho)
for
\alpha,\rho>0
(we note that the quantity
\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})
is independent of the samples and
not random
), this implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+\rho}\geq\alpha\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}n(\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+\rho)^{2}}{18\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}\right).

Using the trivial inequality
(\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+\rho)^{2}\geq 2\rho\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})
, this in turn implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+\rho}\geq\alpha\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}n\rho}{9}\right),

(E.5)

for any fixed pair
(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
. For future reference, we note this bound in the following lemma:

Lemma E.2
.

Let
(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
, and
\mathcal{L}(\Psi,\mathcal{G})=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\|\Psi(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
the population risk, and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi,\mathcal{G})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\Psi(u_{j})-\mathcal{G}(u_{j})\|^{2}_{L^{2}}
the empirical risk. If the samples
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\sim\mu
are chosen iid, then for any
\rho,\alpha>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+\rho}\geq\alpha\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}n\rho}{9}\right).

(E.6)

A similar bound can be derived for the supremum over all pairs
(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
, via simple a union bound argument. This is summarized in the next lemma, with proof detailed in Appendix
E.3
. The bound below also makes a specific choice for
\rho\propto\alpha^{-1}\epsilon
:

Lemma E.3
.

Let
\epsilon>0
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Denote
\mathcal{H}=\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left[\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+72\alpha^{-1}\epsilon}\geq\alpha\right]\leq N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}\exp\left(-2\alpha n\epsilon\right).

(E.7)

Here
N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}
can be thought of as bounding the covering number of
\mathcal{H}
, and for
m\geq(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}
, we can estimate

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}\leq\mathcal{N}\left(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon\right)^{2},

(E.8)

where
\mathcal{N}\left(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon\right)
denotes the
\epsilon
-covering number of
\Sigma_{m}
with respect to the
C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))
-norm.

The last lemma implies the following corollary, also proved in Appendix
E.3
:

Corollary E.4
.

Fix
\delta\in(0,1]
. Let
\epsilon>0
be given, and let
m\geq\left(2/\epsilon\right)^{1/\gamma}
be integer. If

n\geq\epsilon^{-1}\log(\delta^{-1}\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}),

then with probability at least
1-\delta
in the random samples
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\sim\mu
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})\leq 2\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+144\epsilon,

(E.9)

uniformly for all
(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
.

Given Corollary
E.4
, the claim of Proposition
3.8
now follows by setting
\Psi=\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}
in (
E.9
), and noting that
\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}
minimizes the first term on the right over all possible choices
\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}
, and hence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{\mathcal{G}};\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\leq 2\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi_{\mathcal{G}};\mathcal{G})+144\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}}\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+144\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma_{m}}\|\Psi-\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}^{2}+144\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2m^{-2\gamma}+144\epsilon.

By assumption, we have
m^{-2\gamma}\leq(\epsilon/2)^{2}\leq\epsilon/2
, and hence,

\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{\mathcal{G}};\mathcal{G})\leq 145\epsilon.

This concludes our proof of Proposition
3.8
.

E.0.2
Proof of Proposition
3.9

Our proof of Proposition
3.9
relies on several lemmas. We will provide a proof of Proposition
3.9
and Corollary
3.10
here, and refer the reader to Appendix
E.4
for the detailed proofs of the lemmas.

Proof.

(Poof of Proposition
3.9
)

Given a FNO architecture
\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
with fixed hyper-parameters
{d_{c}}
,
\kappa
and
L
, our goal is to bound the covering number of the set
\bm{\mathsf{FNO}}\subset C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))
of operators that can be represented by this architecture. To this end, we note that this set is the image under the mapping
[-B,B]^{d_{\theta}}\to C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))
,
\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
, from parameters
\theta
to corresponding operator
\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
. This simple observation is relevant in view of the following elementary lemma (cp. Appendix
E.4.1
for a proof):

Lemma E.5
.

If
F:[-B,B]^{d}\to Y
is a Lipschitz continuous mapping into a Banach space
Y
, then

\mathcal{N}(F([-B,B]^{d});\epsilon)\leq\mathcal{N}([-B,B]^{d};\epsilon/\mathrm{Lip}(F)).

In parameter space, estimates for the covering number of a cube
[-B,B]^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
are well-known. In particular, the covering number of the hypercube
[-B,B]^{d}
with respect to the
\ell^{\infty}
-norm,
|{\,\cdot\,}|_{\infty}
, is bounded by,

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}([-B,B]^{d},\epsilon)_{|{\,\cdot\,}|_{\infty}}\leq\left(\frac{2B}{\epsilon}\right)^{d}.

(E.10)

This follows by covering the cube
[-B,B]^{d}
by sub-cubes with side-length
\epsilon
in each direction. Since
2B/\epsilon
intervals of length
\epsilon
cover
[-B,B]
, we require at most
(2B/\epsilon)^{d}
sub-cubes to cover
[-B,B]^{d}
.

In order to apply Lemma
E.5
and (
E.10
) to obtain an estimate of the covering number of the space of FNOs represented by each
\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
introduced above, it remains to bound the Lipschitz constant of the mapping
\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,},\theta)
. This is the subject of the following lemma:

Lemma E.6
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset L^{2}(D)
be a compact set. Let

M:=\max_{u\in\mathcal{K}}\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}<\infty.

The Lipschitz constant of the mapping
[-B,B]^{d_{\theta}}\to C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))
,
\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
is bounded by

\mathrm{Lip}\left(\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)\right)\leq(L+2)(2{d_{c}}B)^{L+2}\left(M+(2\kappa)^{d/2}\right).

For a proof of this lemma, we refer to Appendix
E.4.2
(cp. Proposition
E.15
).
Combining Lemma
E.5
with (
E.10
) and Lemma
E.6
, it now follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathsf{FNO}},\epsilon)_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{N}\left([-B,B]^{d_{\theta}},\frac{\epsilon}{\mathrm{Lip}(\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta))}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2B\mathrm{Lip}(\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta))}{\epsilon}\right)^{d_{\theta}}.

We also recall that, by (
3.2
),

d_{\theta}\leq 5(2\kappa)^{d}L{d_{c}}^{2}.

Taking logarithms in the above bound on
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathsf{FNO}},\epsilon)
, it now follows that there exists a constant
C=C(d,M)>0
, depending only on the dimension of the function domain
\Omega
, and the norm bound
M
over the compact set
\mathcal{K}
, such that

\log\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathsf{FNO}},\epsilon)_{C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D))}\leq C\kappa^{d}L^{2}{d_{c}}^{2}\log\left(\frac{{d_{c}}\kappa LB}{\epsilon}\right).

This completes our proof of Proposition
3.9
.
∎

3.10
from Proposition

.

)

, let

and any operator

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\big(\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})\big)_{+}}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})+s}+\frac{36\epsilon}{s}.

We can now bound the first term on the right by the maximum over all possible choices
k\in[N]
and
\ell\in[M]
, and then take the supremum over all
(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}:=\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\times\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
on the left, to find

\displaystyle\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+s}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in[N],\ell\in[M]}\frac{\big(\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})\big)_{+}}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})+s}+\frac{36\epsilon}{s}.

Let us now make the choice
s=72\epsilon/\alpha
, so that
the above bound implies the following inclusion of sets:

\left\{\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+s}\geq\alpha\right\}\subset\left\{\max_{k\in[N],\ell\in[M]}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+s}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\}.

And hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\Bigg[\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+s}\geq\alpha\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Prob}\left[\max_{k\in[N],\ell\in[M]}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})+s}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq NM\max_{k\in[N],\ell\in[M]}\mathrm{Prob}\left[\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{k};\mathcal{G}_{\ell})+s}\geq\frac{\alpha}{2}\right].

Where we use a union bound to pass to the third line. The last probability can be estimated by Bernstein’s inequality (
E.3
), yielding

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\Bigg[\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+s}\geq\alpha\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq NM\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha^{2}ns}{36}\right)

\displaystyle=NM\exp\left(-2\alpha n\epsilon\right).

We also recall that
N=\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m}^{\prime},\epsilon)
and
M=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}),2\epsilon)
. Since
\Sigma_{m}^{\prime}\subset\Sigma_{m}
implies
\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m}^{\prime},\epsilon)\leq\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)
, the above estimate proves the claimed inequality of Lemma
E.3
with constant

N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}=\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}),2\epsilon),

for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. If we assume, in addition, that
m\geq(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}
, then Lemma
E.7
implies that

\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma}),2\epsilon)\leq\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon).

And hence, we have

\mathrm{Prob}\Bigg[\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})}{\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})+s}\geq\alpha\Bigg]\leq N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}\exp\left(-2\alpha n\epsilon\right),

with
N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}\leq\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}
,
whenever
m\geq(2/\epsilon)^{1/\gamma}
, and where we recall that we chose
s=72\epsilon/\alpha
.

∎

We next prove Corollary
E.4
.

Proof.

(Proof of Corollary
E.4
)
Given
m\in\mathbb{N}
, define
\epsilon=2m^{-\gamma}
, so that by Lemma
E.3
, we have

N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}\leq\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}.

We now note that

\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}n}{9^{2}}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}n}{9^{2}}+\log\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}\right).

The right hand side is
\leq\delta
, if

\frac{\epsilon^{2}n}{9^{2}}\geq\log\left(\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}/\delta\right),

or equivalently,

n\geq 81\epsilon^{-2}\log\left(\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)^{2}/\delta\right).

Thus, by Lemma
E.3
, we find that for such choice of
n
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}\left[\sup_{(\Psi,\mathcal{G})\in\mathcal{H}}\Big[\mathcal{L}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(\Psi;\mathcal{G})\Big]\geq 37\epsilon\right]

\displaystyle\leq N_{\mathcal{H},\epsilon}\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^{2}n}{9^{2}}\right)\leq\delta.

∎

E.4
Entropy estimates

Proposition
3.8
relates the amount of data that is needed to approximate operators
\mathcal{G}\in\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{\bm{A}}^{\gamma})
to the complexity of the family
\{\Sigma_{m}\}
, as measured by the growth of the metric entropy
\log\mathcal{N}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)
.

Remark E.10
.

If
\mathcal{H}(\Sigma_{m},\epsilon)\lesssim\epsilon^{-\alpha}
grows at most algebraically, and
n\sim\epsilon^{-1-\alpha}
, then Proposition
3.8
leads to a data-complexity bound,

\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\|\Psi_{\mathcal{G}}(u)-\mathcal{G}(u)\|^{2}\right]\lesssim n^{-1/(2+\alpha)},

where the exponent
1/(2+\alpha)
lies in the interval
(0,1/2]
. In particular, this shows that
algebraic data-complexity rates
are obtained in this case. This should be contrasted with the logarithmic lower bound (
2.4
), requiring an exponential amount of data
n
to achieve a similar accuracy.

E.4.1
Proof of Lemma
E.5

Proof.

(Proof of Lemma
E.5
)
Fix
\epsilon>0
. Let
\delta=\epsilon/\mathrm{Lip}(F)
, and let
B_{\delta}(u_{1}),\dots,B_{\delta}(u_{N})
be a
\delta
-covering of
\mathcal{K}
. We claim that,

F(\mathcal{K})\subset\bigcup_{k=1}^{N}B_{\epsilon}(F(u_{k})).

To see why, let
v\in F(\mathcal{K})
be given. We aim to show that
v\in B_{\epsilon}(F(u_{k}))
for some
k\in\{1,\dots,N\}
. Since
v\in F(\mathcal{K})
, there exists
u\in\mathcal{K}
, such that
v=F(u)
. By the choice of
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}
, there exists an index
k
, such that
\|u-u_{k}\|<\delta
, and hence

\|v-F(u_{k})\|=\|F(u)-F(u_{k})\|\leq\mathrm{Lip}(F)\|u-u_{k}\|<\epsilon,

by definition of
\delta=\epsilon/\mathrm{Lip}(F)
. It follows that
v\in B_{\epsilon}(F(u_{k}))
. Choosing
N
minimal, it thus follows that
\mathcal{N}(F(\mathcal{K}),\epsilon)\leq N=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{K},\delta)=\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{K},\epsilon/\mathrm{Lip}(F))
, as claimed.
∎

E.4.2
Bounding the Lipschitz constant of
\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)

Our next goal in this section is to estimate the Lipschitz constant of the mapping

\displaystyle[-B,B]^{d_{\theta}}\mapsto C(\mathcal{K};L^{2}(D)),\quad\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta),

(E.15)

for a fixed FNO architecture
\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
with parameters
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
, and with a fixed set of hyperparameters. We recall that the channel width is denoted
{d_{c}}
, the Fourier cut-off by
\kappa
, the depth
L
and we have also introduced a parameter bound
\|\theta\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq B
.

To reach our goal of estimating the Lipschitz constant of (
E.15
), we start by deriving basic estimates for the linear parts of the hidden layers. This is the subject of the following lemma:

Lemma E.11
.

We have the following estimates:

1.

If
W:v(x)\mapsto Wv(x)
is a linear layer, defined by a matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}\times{d_{c}}}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(W)_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq{d_{c}}\|W\|_{\infty},

(E.16)

where
\|W\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i,j}|W_{ij}|
.

2.

If
K:v(x)\mapsto\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\widehat{P}\mathcal{F}v)
is a (linear) spectral convolution layer, defined by the Fourier multiplier
\widehat{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{\kappa\times{d_{c}}\times{d_{c}}}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(K)_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq{d_{c}}\|\widehat{P}\|_{\infty},

(E.17)

where
\|\widehat{P}\|_{\infty}:=\max_{k,i,j}|\widehat{P}_{k,i,j}|
.

3.

If
\sigma(x)
is a Lipschitz continuous activation function, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)_{\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}}.

(E.18)

4.

If
b(x)=\sum_{|k|<\kappa}\widehat{b}_{k}e^{ikx}
is a bias function, then

\displaystyle\|b\|_{L^{2}}\leq{d_{c}}^{1/2}(2\kappa)^{d/2}\|\widehat{b}\|_{\infty},

(E.19)

where
\|\widehat{b}\|_{\infty}=\max_{k}|\widehat{b}_{k}|
.

Proof.

The claimed estimates are elementary. To see (
E.16
), we note that

\|Wv\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}W_{ij}v_{j}\right|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|W_{ij}|^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|v_{j}|^{2}\leq d^{2}\|W\|_{\infty}^{2}\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

Similarly, (
E.17
) follows from,

\displaystyle\|Kv\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\widehat{P}\widehat{v}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{|k|<\kappa}\|\widehat{P}_{k}\widehat{v}(k)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|k|<\kappa}{d_{c}}^{2}\|\widehat{P}_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\widehat{v}(k)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{d_{c}}^{2}\|\widehat{P}\|_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{|k|<\kappa}\|\widehat{v}(k)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle={d_{c}}^{2}\|\widehat{P}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|v\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

Estimate (
E.18
) is immediate, while (
E.19
) follows from,

\displaystyle\|b\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|k|<\kappa}\|\widehat{b}(k)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\sum_{|k|<\kappa}{d_{c}}\|\widehat{b}(k)\|_{\infty}^{2}\leq(2\kappa)^{d}{d_{c}}\|\widehat{b}\|_{\infty}^{2}.

∎

The next lemma, immediate from Lemma
E.11
, summarizes the relevant Lipschitz estimate for a hidden layer:

Lemma E.12
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell})=\sigma\left(Wv+Kv+b\right)
be a hidden FNO layer, with concatenated parameters
\theta_{\ell}=(W,\widehat{P},\widehat{b})
, satisfying the size bound

\|W\|_{\infty},\;\|\widehat{P}\|_{\infty},\;\|\widehat{b}\|_{\infty}\leq B,

and with Lipschitz continuous activation function
\sigma
, s.t.
\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)_{\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}}\leq 1
. Then
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
is a Lipschitz operator with,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(v\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell}))\leq 2{d_{c}}B.

(E.20)

The previous corollary estimates the Lipschitz constant of the mapping
v\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell})
for fixed
\theta
satisfying the bound
\|\theta_{\ell}\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq B
. The next Lemma provides a similar estimate for the mapping
\theta_{\ell}\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell})
for fixed
v
.

Lemma E.13
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell})=\sigma\left(Wv+Kv+b\right)
denote a hidden FNO layer, with
1
-Lipschitz continuous activation
\sigma
, and depending on the concatenated parameters
\theta_{\ell}=(W,\widehat{P},\widehat{b})
. Then, for any
v\in L^{2}(D)
, we have the bound,

\mathrm{Lip}(\theta_{\ell}\mapsto\mathcal{L}(v;\theta_{\ell}))\leq 3{d_{c}}(2\kappa)^{d/2}\max(1,\|v\|_{L^{2}}).

Proof.

Let
v\in L^{2}(D)
be arbitrary, consider two parameter vectors
\theta_{\ell},\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}
, and let us denote

\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell})=\sigma(Wv+Kv+b),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell}^{\prime})=\sigma(W^{\prime}v+K^{\prime}v+b^{\prime}).

We note that, by the estimates in
E.11
:

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell})-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v;\theta_{\ell}^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)(\|W-W^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\|K-K^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\|v\|_{L^{2}}+\|b-b^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}})

\displaystyle\leq{d_{c}}\|W-W^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|v\|_{L^{2}}+{d_{c}}\|\widehat{P}-\widehat{P}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\|v\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+{d_{c}}^{1/2}(2\kappa)^{d/2}\|\widehat{b}-\widehat{b}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq{d_{c}}(2\kappa)^{d/2}(3\max(1,\|v\|_{L^{2}}))\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

∎

In addition to the Lipschitz constant of all layers, we also need a bound on the output of hidden states of the FNO. This is provided in the next lemma:

Lemma E.14
.

\Psi
FNO of depth
L
, with
1
-Lipschitz activation
\sigma
, such that
\sigma(0)=0
. Assume that
\|\theta\|_{\ell^{\infty}}\leq B
for some constant
B\geq 1
. Then

\|\Psi(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{L+2}\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}+(2\kappa)^{d/2}\right),\quad\forall\,u\in L^{2}(D).

Proof.

Recall that the FNO
\Psi:L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
is of the form

\Psi=Q\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ P.

For
\ell=1,\dots,L+1
, we introduce the notation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ P,

and define
\mathcal{L}_{\leq 0}(u):=Pu
. We will estimate
\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell}(u)\|
recursively, making use of our bound on
\mathrm{Lip}(v\mapsto\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v))
in Lemma
E.12
. To this end, fix
u\in L^{2}(D)
, and denote
M_{\ell}:=\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}
. Then, writing
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(v):=\sigma(Wv+Kv+b)
, we obtain

\displaystyle M_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell-1}(u)\right)\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell-1}(u)\right)-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(0)\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(0)\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L}_{\ell})\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell-1}(u)-0\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\|\sigma(b({\,\cdot\,}))\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L}_{\ell})\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell-1}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\mathrm{Lip}(\sigma)\|b({\,\cdot\,})\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq(2{d_{c}}B)\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell-1}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}+{d_{c}}^{1/2}(2\kappa)^{d/2}B,

where we made use of (
E.20
) and (
E.19
) to pass to the last line. Thus, we have the following recursion:

M_{\ell}\leq C_{0}M_{\ell-1}+C_{1},

with
C_{0}:=2{d_{c}}B
and
C_{1}:={d_{c}}^{1/2}(2\kappa)^{d/2}B
. This recursion implies that

\displaystyle M_{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}M_{\ell-1}+C_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}^{2}M_{\ell-2}+C_{0}C_{1}+C_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\dots

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}^{\ell}M_{0}+C_{1}\sum_{k=0}^{\ell-1}C_{0}^{k}

\displaystyle=C_{0}^{\ell}M_{0}+C_{1}\frac{C_{0}^{\ell}-1}{C_{0}-1}.

For
C_{0}=2{d_{c}}B\geq 2
, we have

\frac{C_{0}^{\ell}-1}{C_{0}-1}\leq C_{0}^{\ell}.

Furthermore, by definition of
M_{0}
and from (
E.16
), we find

M_{0}=\|Pu\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq{d_{c}}\|P\|_{\infty}\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq{d_{c}}B\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}.

Thus, we finally obtain the upper bound,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}&=M_{\ell}\\
&\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{\ell}\left({d_{c}}B\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}+{d_{c}}^{1/2}(2\kappa)^{d/2}B\right)\\
&\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{\ell+1}\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}+(2\kappa)^{d/2}\right).\end{aligned}

(E.21)

This, in turn, entails

\displaystyle\|\Psi(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}

\displaystyle=\|Q\mathcal{L}_{\leq L}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq{d_{c}}\|Q\|_{\infty}M_{L}

\displaystyle\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{L+2}\left(\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}+(2\kappa)^{d/2}\right).

This is the claimed upper bound.
∎

Based on the previous lemmas, we can now state a local Lipschitz estimate for FNOs.

Proposition E.15
.

Let
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
with
\|\theta\|_{\infty},\|\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq B
. Assume that
B\geq 1
and
d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}}\leq d_{c}
. Assume that the activation
\sigma
is Lipschitz and
\sigma(0)=0
. Then, we have

\|\Psi(u;\theta)-\Psi(u,\theta^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\lambda(u)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where

\lambda(u):=(L+2)(2{d_{c}}B)^{L+2}(\|u\|+(2\kappa)^{d/2}).

Lemma
E.6
is an immediate consequence of Proposition
E.15
.

Proof.

(Proof of Proposition
E.15
)
We first consider two parameter vectors
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
, differing in only their
\ell
-th component for
\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}
, and with

\|\theta\|_{\infty},\|\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq B.

Notice that, by definition,

\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)=Q\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ P,

where the
\ell
-th hidden layer
\mathcal{L}_{\ell}=\mathcal{L}_{\ell}({\,\cdot\,};\theta_{\ell})
depends only on
\theta_{\ell}
. To simplify notation, let us now introduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{>\ell}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\mathcal{L}_{\ell+1},

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{L}_{\ell-1}\circ\dots\circ\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ P.

We then have,

\displaystyle\|\Psi(u;\theta)-\Psi(u,\theta^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q\|\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L}_{>\ell})\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}(u);\theta_{\ell})-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}(u);\theta_{\ell}^{\prime})\|.

We note that
\|Q\|\leq{d_{c}}\|Q\|_{\infty}\leq{d_{c}}B
, by Lemma
E.11
. By Lemma
E.12
, we can bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L}_{>\ell})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L}\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L}_{k})\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{L-\ell}.

Furthermore, Lemma
E.13
implies that, with
\omega:=\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}(u)
,

\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\omega;\theta_{\ell})-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\omega;\theta_{\ell}^{\prime})\|\leq 3{d_{c}}(2\kappa)^{d/2}\max(1,\|\omega\|_{L^{2}}))\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

Using the following upper bound, from (
E.21
) in the proof of Lemma
E.14
,

\|\omega\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\|\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}=\|\mathcal{L}_{\leq\ell-1}(u)\|_{L^{2}(D)}\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{\ell+1}(\|u\|+(2\kappa)^{d/2}),

we thus find

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\omega;\theta_{\ell})-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\omega;\theta_{\ell}^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\leq 3{d_{c}}(2\kappa)^{d/2}(2{d_{c}}B)^{\ell+1}(\|u\|+(2\kappa)^{d/2})\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

And finally,

\displaystyle\|\Psi(u;\theta)-\Psi(u,\theta^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|Q\|\mathrm{Lip}(\mathcal{L}_{>\ell})\|\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}(u);\theta_{\ell})-\mathcal{L}_{\ell}(\mathcal{L}_{<\ell}(u);\theta_{\ell}^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle\leq{d_{c}}B(2{d_{c}}B)^{L-\ell}(2{d_{c}}B)^{\ell+1}(\|u\|+(2\kappa)^{d/2})\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq(2{d_{c}}B)^{L+2}(\|u\|+(2\kappa)^{d/2})\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}.

It follows that for general
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}
with
\|\theta\|_{\infty},\|\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq B
, but allowed to differ in
\theta_{\ell}\neq\theta_{\ell}^{\prime}
for
\ell=0,\dots,L+1
, we have

\|\Psi(u;\theta)-\Psi(u,\theta^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}}\leq\lambda(u)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{\infty},

where

\lambda(u):=(L+2)(2{d_{c}}B)^{L+2}(\|u\|+(2\kappa)^{d/2}).

This provides the claimed upper bound on the local Lipschitz constant of the mapping
\mathbb{R}^{d_{\theta}}\to C(L^{2}(D);L^{2}(D))
,
\theta\mapsto\Psi({\,\cdot\,};\theta)
.
∎
