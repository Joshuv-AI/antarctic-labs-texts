Learning Low-dimensional Latent Dynamics from High-dimensional Observations: Non-asymptotics and Lower Bounds
Yuyang Zhang, Shahriar Talebi, Na Li

Abstract
In this paper, we focus on learning a linear time-invariant (LTI) model with low-dimensional latent variables but high-dimensional observations. We provide an algorithm that recovers the high-dimensional features, i.e. column space of the observer, embeds the data into low dimensions and learns the low-dimensional model parameters. Our algorithm enjoys a sample complexity guarantee of order 𝒪 ~ ​ ( n / ϵ 2 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n/\epsilon^{2}) , where n n is the observation dimension. We further establish a fundamental lower bound indicating this complexity bound is optimal up to logarithmic factors and dimension-independent constants. We show that this inevitable linear factor of n n is due to the learning error of the observer’s column space in the presence of high-dimensional noises. Extending our results, we consider a meta-learning problem inspired by various real-world applications, where the observer column space can be collectively learned from datasets of multiple LTI systems. An end-to-end algorithm is then proposed, facilitating learning LTI systems from a meta-dataset which breaks the sample complexity lower bound in certain scenarios.

Contributions.

In this paper, we study learning LTI systems with
high-dimensional noisy observations
. We adopt a two-stage procedure to first extract high-dimensional features, i.e. column space of the observer. We subsequently perform standard SYSID on the resulting low-dim data and recover the original model parameters. We establish sample complexity for this bifold procedure that scales as

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}([n+\text{poly}\left(r,m\right)]/\epsilon^{2})
which further reduces to only
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\text{poly}\left(r,m\right)/\epsilon^{2})
in the absence of observation noises. This then naturally leads to the following question:

Can this linear dependence on observer dimension
n
be possibly improved by some carefully designed algorithm?

We show that the linear dependence on
n
is unavoidable in the presence of observation noises — as shown in our lower bound result
Theorem

4.1
, requiring at least
\mathcal{O}(n/\epsilon^{2})
samples from our learning problem.
Unfortunately, observation noises always exist for real-world applications, as the sensors always involve uncertainties. The lower bound also indicates that the sample complexity of the proposed algorithm is optimal up to some logarithmic factors and dimension-independent constants.

Additionally, our pragmatic solution–namely, the separation of learning the high-dimensional features (i.e. the observer column space) and learning the rest of the model parameters–
can be extended to meta-learning setups by collecting metadata from a set of dynamical systems that share the same observer model.
By adopting a “leave-one-out” strategy for statistical consistency, we show that as long as the metadata is
collectively
large in order of
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n/\epsilon^{2})
, we can successfully obtain an
\epsilon
-well approximation of all the systems parameters involved in the meta-data (
Theorem

5.1
). We finally note that such metadata with the same observer is common in real-world applications
(
Hajnal et al., 2023
;
Xia et al., 2021
;
Marks & Goard, 2021
;
Gallego et al., 2020
)
. One example is in neuroscience where neuron activities are measured over a long period by the same set of electrodes or imaging devices. Although the subject may perform different tasks or demonstrate behavior corresponding to different latent dynamics, the observer model (i.e. the electrodes or the imaging device) remains the same, resulting in the metadata considered here.

1.1
Other Related Works

Other Linear Models with Low-Dimensional Dynamics.

Recently, there are lines of research on autoregressive models with low-dimensional representations
(
Qin, 2022
;
Dong et al., 2022
;
Poloni & Sbrana, 2019
)
. Indeed, we may also be able to reconstruct low-dimensional dynamics from the model with the learned representations. However, sample complexity results are not currently available for such models. Another related line of research is on dynamic factor models
(
Hallin et al., 2023
;
Anderson et al., 2022
;
Breitung & Eickmeier, 2006
)
.
Anderson et al. (2022)
only provides asymptotic convergence guarantees. Finite time convergence is indeed developed in
Hallin et al. (2023)
. However, their results can not imply dependence on the system dimensions and are only about the convergence rates. Moreover, certain assumptions might not be easily verified in our setting.

General SYSID Algorithms.

The most relevant sample complexity results are provided in recent SYSID literature for learning partially observed LTI systems
(
Zheng & Li, 2021
;
Lee, 2022
;
Djehiche & Mazhar, 2022
;
Sarkar et al., 2021
;
Oymak & Ozay, 2019
)
. The last four papers focus on systems with stable latent dynamics, while the first paper extends to unstable latent dynamics. Unfortunately, all algorithms have suboptimal sample complexities.
There also exists a line of work providing sample complexity for learning fully observed systems
(
Sarkar & Rakhlin, 2019
;
Shirani Faradonbeh et al., 2018
)
, whose analysis techniques is applicable to unstable dynamics.
This paper focuses on stable latent dynamics, and we leave for future directions to extend the results to unstable latent dynamics.

Existing SYSID Lower Bounds.

There exists literature providing lower bounds for learning partially observed LTI systems. However, their results are not directly comparable to ours in our setting. The most relevant papers are
Sun et al. (2023)
;
Fattahi (2021)
. The former provides a lower bound depending on
r/n
, which is tailored for systems with
r\gg n
. The latter provides a lower bound proportional to
1/\epsilon^{4}
. The lower bound follows a slower rate than ours, though being logarithmic in the system dimensions.
Sun et al. (2022)
provides lower bounds defined for noise-free settings.
Mao et al. (2021)
provides lower bounds for systems with
C=I
.
Bakshi et al. (2023)
provides lower bounds for learning almost uncontrollable and unobservable systems. Other related works include
Djehiche & Mazhar (2021)
;
Jedra & Proutiere (2023)
;
Simchowitz et al. (2018)
, which develop lower bounds for fully observed systems.

General Subspace Learning Algorithms.

To learn the observer column space, several existing literature may be related.
Vaswani et al. (2018)
;
Balzano et al. (2018)
;
Candès et al. (2011)
;
Blanchard et al. (2007)
focus on analysis for PCA subspace learning. However, they assume that the dataset is i.i.d. sampled, which is not the case for dynamic systems. Dynamic factor model techniques are also related
(
Hallin et al., 2023
)
. As discussed previously, the results can not imply the dependence on the system dimensions. Other ideas include
Deng et al. (2020)
, whose sample complexity remains an open question.

Lower Bounds

Now that
Algorithm

1

Is this linear dependence on the observer dimension

In this section, we provide the following theorem to show that this linear dependence is in fact necessary.

While we present this lower bound for learning LTI systems with a single input-output data trajectory, it can be extended to multiple trajectories. The more general version is deferred to the appendix. (

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
n\geq m\geq r
with
n\geq 2
, and choose any positive scalars
\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Consider the class of minimal systems
\mathcal{M}=(r,n,m,A,B,C,\Sigma_{w},\Sigma_{\eta})
with different

B

C

and

denote the associated single trajectory dataset.
Here the input
u_{t}
satisfies: 1).
u_{t}
is independently sampled; 2).
\mathbb{E}(u_{t})=0
.
Consider any estimator
\widehat{f}
mapping
\mathcal{D}
to

We defer the proof of
Theorem

to
Appendix

. Therein, we construct a set of system instances with different observation matrices such that their column spaces are “relatively close” but “quantifiably distinct”. In fact, we take advantage of the fact that in the high dimensional space

, there are too many distinct subspaces that are close to each other. And therefore, any estimator needs correspondingly many samples, i.e.
\mathcal{O}(n/\epsilon^{2})

This difficulty is indeed due to the high dimensional observation noises.

In the following section, we introduce a more general problem pertinent to high-dimensional “meta-datasets.” While this lower bound still has implications even for this general problem, we show how to confine its implied difficulty on the total length of the meta-dataset and not on each personalized portions.

Conclusions and Future Directions

In conclusion, our focus has been on learning a linear time-invariant (LTI) model characterized by low-dimensional latent variables and high-dimensional observations. The introduced

Extending the scope of our results, we address a meta-learning setting where datasets from multiple analogous systems are available. This leads to the

\begin{split}\|(y{}^{\intercal}w_{t})(x{}^{\intercal}w_{t})-y{}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w,t}x\|_{\psi_{1}}\lesssim\left\|(y{}^{\intercal}w_{t})(x{}^{\intercal}w_{t})\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq\left\|y{}^{\intercal}w_{t}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\left\|x{}^{\intercal}w_{t}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\lesssim\psi_{w}.\end{split}

(65)

Here the first and second inequalities are from Lemma 2.6.8 and Lemma 2.7.7 in
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
, where
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{1}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}
denote the sub-exponential and sub-Gaussian norms, respectively.
Directly applying Bernstein’s inequality on
\{(y{}^{\intercal}w_{t})(x{}^{\intercal}w_{t})-y{}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w,t}x\}_{t=1}^{T}
(as sub-exponential random variables) gives the following for some positive constant
c_{1}
with probability at least
1-\delta^{\prime}

\begin{split}\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big[(y{}^{\intercal}w_{t})(x{}^{\intercal}w_{t})-y{}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w,t}x\Big]\right|\leq c_{1}\psi_{w}\sqrt{T\log(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}})},\end{split}

(66)

which is equivalent to

\begin{split}5^{2a}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big[(y{}^{\intercal}w_{t})(x{}^{\intercal}w_{t})-y{}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w,t}x\Big]\right|>c_{1}\psi_{w}\sqrt{T\log(\frac{2}{\delta^{\prime}})}\right)\leq 5^{2a}\delta^{\prime}.\end{split}

(67)

Letting
\delta=5^{2a}\delta^{\prime}
gives

\begin{split}5^{2a}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Big[(y{}^{\intercal}w_{t})(x{}^{\intercal}w_{t})-y{}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{w,t}x\Big]\right|>c_{1}\psi_{w}\sqrt{2aT\log(\frac{10}{\delta})}\right)\leq\delta.\end{split}

(68)

Substituting back into Equation
64
gives

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}w_{t}{}^{\intercal}-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\Sigma_{w,t}\right\|>4c_{1}\psi_{w}\sqrt{2aT\log(\frac{10}{\delta})}\right)\leq\delta.\end{split}

(69)

This completes the proof.

∎

Lemma A.6
.

Consider the same setting as
Lemma

A.2
. For any
\delta
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{t=0}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}{}^{\intercal}\right\|\lesssim\frac{\psi_{w}\psi_{A}^{2}r}{1-\rho_{A}^{2}}T\log\frac{1}{\delta}.\end{split}

(70)

Proof.

Directly applying Proposition 8.4 in

)
gives the following with probability at least

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{t=0}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}{}^{\intercal}\right\|\lesssim{}&\psi_{w}\text{tr}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}(A)\right)\log\frac{1}{\delta}\leq\psi_{w}r\left\|\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\Gamma_{t}(A)\right\|\log\frac{1}{\delta}.\end{split}

From Assumption

, we know that

(72)

Substituting back gives

\begin{split}\left\|\sum_{t=0}^{T}x_{t}x_{t}{}^{\intercal}\right\|\lesssim\frac{\psi_{w}\psi_{A}^{2}r}{1-\rho_{A}^{2}}T\log\frac{1}{\delta}.\end{split}

(73)

A.1.2
Other Lemmas

Lemma A.7
.

Given perturbation matrices
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
and p.d square matrix
M
of the same dimension. Suppose they satisfy the following for some positive constant
a\in[0,1)
and
b\geq 0

\begin{split}\left\|M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{1}\right\|\leq a<\sqrt{\phi_{M}},\quad\left\|\Delta_{2}\right\|\leq b.\end{split}

(74)

Denote the SVD of the matrices as follows

\begin{split}M=\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}&U_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{1}&\\
&\Lambda_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}U_{1}{}^{\intercal}\\
U_{2}{}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix},\quad\tilde{M}\coloneqq M+\left(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}\right)+\Delta_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{U}_{1}&\tilde{U}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\Lambda}_{1}&\\
&\tilde{\Lambda}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{U}_{1}{}^{\intercal}\\
\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix},\end{split}

(75)

and define the following constants

\begin{split}\psi_{M}=\sigma_{1}(M),\quad\phi_{M}=\sigma_{\min}(M),\quad 0<\delta_{M}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\Lambda_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\Lambda_{2}).\end{split}

(76)

Then we have

\begin{split}\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|\leq\frac{a^{2}+b}{\delta_{M}-4a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-3a^{2}-b}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\left(1+\frac{2-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}{1-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\delta_{M}-2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-a^{2}}\right)\end{split}

(77)

In the case where
\sqrt{\phi_{M}}\geq Ca
,
\delta_{M}\geq C\left(a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+b\right)
for large enough
C
, the above result is further simplified to

\begin{split}\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|\lesssim\frac{b}{\delta_{M}}+\frac{a\sigma_{1}(\Lambda_{2})/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}{\delta_{M}}+\frac{a^{2}\sqrt{\psi_{M}/\phi_{M}}}{\delta_{M}}.\end{split}

(78)

Remark A.8
.

This result is adapted from previous subspace relative perturbation results (Theorem 3.2 in
(
Li, 1998
)
). It provides tighter bound for matrices with smaller condition number, i.e. when
\phi_{M}
is close to
\psi_{M}
, as compared to the standard Davis-Kahan
\sin\Theta
theorem. To be more specific, Davis-Kahan gives the bound of order
\frac{a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}}{\delta_{M}}
. Here our result features the bound
\frac{a\sigma_{1}(\Lambda_{2})/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}{\delta_{M}}
.

Proof.

Define

\begin{split}\widehat{M}\coloneqq M+\left(\Delta_{1}+\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}\right)+\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}M^{-1}\Delta_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{U}_{1}&\widehat{U}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\Lambda}_{1}&\\
&\widehat{\Lambda}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{U}_{1}{}^{\intercal}\\
\widehat{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(79)

From its definition, we immediately have

\begin{split}\left\|\widehat{M}-M\right\|\leq{}&2\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|+\left\|\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}M^{-1}\Delta_{1}\right\|\\
\overset{(i)}{\leq}{}&2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+\left\|M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{1}\right\|^{2}\\
\leq{}&2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}.\end{split}

(80)

Here the first term in
(i)
is because

\begin{split}a\geq\left\|M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{1}\right\|\geq\sigma_{\min}\left(M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\psi_{M}}}\left\|\Delta_{1}\right\|.\end{split}

(81)

And the second term is because
\left\|A{}^{\intercal}A\right\|=\left\|A\right\|^{2}
. Then from Theorem 1 in
(
Stewart, 1990
)
, we know that

\begin{split}\sigma_{1}\left(\widehat{\Lambda}_{2}\right)\leq\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+\left\|\widehat{M}-M\right\|\leq\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}.\end{split}

(82)

which in turn gives

\begin{split}\widehat{\delta}\coloneqq\sigma_{\min}(\Lambda_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{2})\geq\delta_{M}-2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-a^{2}.\end{split}

(83)

For the rest of the proof, we upper bound
\left\|\widehat{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|
,
\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\widehat{U}_{1}\right\|
and then derives the desired result.
To upper bound
\left\|\widehat{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|
, we let
D=M^{-1}\Delta_{1}
with

\begin{split}{}&\left\|D\right\|\leq\left\|M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\left\|M^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Delta_{1}\right\|\leq\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}<1,\\
{}&\left\|\left(I+D\right)^{-1}\right\|=\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}\left(I+D\right)}\overset{(i)}{\leq}\frac{1}{1-\sigma_{1}(D)}\leq\frac{1}{1-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}.\end{split}

(84)

Here
(i)
is because
|\sigma_{\min}(I+D)-\sigma_{\min}(I)|\leq\sigma_{1}(D)
from Thereom 1 in
(
Stewart, 1990
)
. And the definition of
D
gives

\begin{split}\widehat{M}=\left(I+\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}M^{-1}\right)M\left(I+M^{-1}\Delta_{1}\right)=\left(I+D{}^{\intercal}\right)M\left(I+D\right).\end{split}

(85)

Now from Equation
83
we apply Theorem 3.2 in
(
Li, 1998
)
which gives

\begin{split}\left\|\widehat{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|\leq{}&\left\|I-(I+D{}^{\intercal})\right\|+\left(\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}\right)\frac{\left\|(I+D){}^{\intercal}-(I+D)^{-1}\right\|}{\widehat{\delta}}\\
\leq{}&\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}+\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\widehat{\delta}}\left(\left\|(I+D){}^{\intercal}-I\right\|+\left\|I-\left(I+D\right)^{-1}\right\|\right)\\
\leq{}&\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}+\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\widehat{\delta}}\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}+\left\|\left(I+D\right)^{-1}\right\|\left\|I+D-I\right\|\right)\\
\leq{}&\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}+\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\widehat{\delta}}\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}+\frac{1}{1-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\right)\\
={}&\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\left(1+\frac{2-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}{1-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\widehat{\delta}}\right).\end{split}

(86)

We now upper bound
\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\widehat{U}_{1}\right\|
. We start from upper bounding
\left\|\widehat{M}-\tilde{M}\right\|

\begin{split}\left\|\widehat{M}-\tilde{M}\right\|=\left\|\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}M^{-1}\Delta_{1}-\Delta_{2}\right\|\leq\left\|\Delta_{1}{}^{\intercal}M^{-1}\Delta_{1}\right\|+\left\|\Delta_{2}\right\|\leq a^{2}+b.\end{split}

(87)

Moreover, we know that

\begin{split}{}&\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{2})\\
={}&\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{2})+\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{2})-\sigma_{1}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{2})\\
\geq{}&\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{2})-a^{2}-b\\
={}&\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{1})-\sigma_{\min}(\Lambda_{1})+\sigma_{\min}(\Lambda_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\Lambda_{2})+\sigma_{1}(\Lambda_{2})-\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{2})-a^{2}-b\\
\geq{}&-2\left\|\widehat{M}-M\right\|-a^{2}-b+\delta_{M}\\
\geq{}&-4a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-2a^{2}-a^{2}-b+\delta_{M}.\end{split}

(88)

Here the first and second inequalities are due to
Stewart (1990, Theorem 1)
. Directly applying the Davis-Kahan
\sin\theta
Theorem in
(
Davis & Kahan, 1970
)
gives

\begin{split}\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\widehat{U}_{1}\right\|\leq\frac{\left\|\widehat{M}-\tilde{M}\right\|}{\sigma_{\min}(\widehat{\Lambda}_{1})-\sigma_{1}(\tilde{\Lambda}_{2})}\leq\frac{a^{2}+b}{-4a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-3a^{2}-b+\delta_{M}}.\end{split}

(89)

Finally, we conclude that

\begin{split}\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|={}&\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\left(\widehat{U}_{1}\widehat{U}_{1}{}^{\intercal}+\widehat{U}_{2}\widehat{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\right)U_{1}\right\|\leq\left\|\tilde{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}\widehat{U}_{1}\right\|+\left\|\widehat{U}_{2}{}^{\intercal}U_{1}\right\|\\
\leq{}&\frac{a^{2}+b}{\delta_{M}-4a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-3a^{2}-b}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\left(1+\frac{2-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}{1-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\widehat{\delta}}\right)\\
\leq{}&\frac{a^{2}+b}{\delta_{M}-4a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-3a^{2}-b}+\frac{a}{\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\left(1+\frac{2-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}{1-a/\sqrt{\phi_{M}}}\frac{\sigma_{1}\left(\Lambda_{2}\right)+2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}+a^{2}}{\delta_{M}-2a\sqrt{\psi_{M}}-a^{2}}\right).\end{split}

(90)

∎

.

For any positive integer

, let

(94)

Appendix B

hdsysid
— Proof of
Theorem

B.2

An essential part for the proof is the Birge’s Inequality stated below.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\{\mathbb{P}_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
be probability distributions on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
. Let
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}\in\mathcal{F}
be
pairwise disjoint events
. If
\delta=\min_{i\in[N]}\mathbb{P}_{i}(F_{i})\geq\frac{1}{2}
(the success rate), then

\begin{split}(2\delta-1)\log\left(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}(N-1)\right)\leq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}||\mathbb{P}_{1}).\end{split}

(95)

Proof.

From the Birge’s inequality
(
Boucheron et al., 2013
)
, we directly have

\begin{split}\delta\log\left(\delta/\frac{1-\delta}{N-1}\right)+(1-\delta)\log\left((1-\delta)/(1-\frac{1-\delta}{N-1})\right)\leq\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i\in[N]}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}||\mathbb{P}_{1}),\end{split}

(96)

On the other hand, the following holds for any
N\geq 3

\begin{split}{}&\delta\log\left(\delta/\frac{1-\delta}{N-1}\right)+(1-\delta)\log\left((1-\delta)/(1-\frac{1-\delta}{N-1})\right)\\
\overset{(i)}{\geq}{}&\delta\log\left(\delta/\frac{1-\delta}{N-1}\right)+(1-\delta)\log\left((1-\delta)/((N-1)\delta)\right)\\
={}&(2\delta-1)\log\left(\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}(N-1)\right)\end{split}

(97)

Here
(i)
holds because
1-\frac{1-\delta}{N-1}\leq 1\leq 2\delta\leq(N-1)\delta
.
For
N=2
, the above inequality naturally holds.
Combining the two cases finishes the proof.
∎

Now we prove
Theorem

4.1
by proving the following more general version that holds for multiple trajectories.

Theorem B.2
.

Suppose
n\geq m\geq r
with
n\geq 2
,
K\geq 1
, and choose positive scalars
\delta\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Consider the class of minimal systems
\mathcal{M}=(r,n,m,A,B,C,\Sigma_{w},\Sigma_{\eta})
with different
A
,
B
,
C
matrices. All other parameters are fixed and known. Moreover,
r<n
and
\Sigma_{\eta}
is positive definite. For every
k\in[K]
, let
\mathcal{D}_{k}=\{y_{k,t}\}_{t=0}^{T_{k}}\cup\{u_{k,t}\}_{t=0}^{T_{k}-1}\cup\text{\lx@text@lbrace all known parameters\lx@text@rbrace}
denote the associated single trajectory dataset.
Here the inputs
u_{k,t}
satisfy: 1).
u_{k,t}
is sampled independently;2).
\mathbb{E}(u_{k,t})=0
. Consider any estimator
\widehat{f}
mapping the datasets
\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\cup_{k\in[K]}\mathcal{D}_{k}
to

(98)

there exists a system
\mathcal{M}_{0}=(r,n,m,A_{0},B_{0},C_{0},\Sigma_{w},\Sigma_{\eta})
with dataset
\mathcal{D}
such that

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}{}&\left\{\left\|\widehat{C}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\widehat{B}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)-C_{0}B_{0}\right\|\geq\epsilon\right\}\geq\delta.\end{split}

(99)

Here
\mathbb{P}
denotes the distribution of
\mathcal{D}
generated by system
\mathcal{M}_{0}
. Related constants are defined as follows

\begin{split}\phi_{\eta}&\coloneqq\sigma_{\min}(\Sigma_{\eta}),\quad\psi_{w}\coloneqq\sigma_{1}(\Sigma_{w}),\\
\quad\psi_{u}&\coloneqq\max_{k,t}\sigma_{\max}\left(\mathbb{E}(u_{k,t}u_{k,t}{}^{\intercal})\right).\end{split}

Proof.

Step 1: We first construct a set of candidate minimal systems with slightly different observer column spaces.

Fix a constant
\epsilon<0.1
and a
\frac{1}{2}
-packing of the intersection of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted by
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}
. Now let
\mathcal{P}=\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\backslash\{(-1,0,\dots,0){}^{\intercal}\}

|\mathcal{P}|\geq 2^{n}-1
and we let
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{P}|}
.
Now consider the following set of matrices
\{A_{i},B_{i},C_{i}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{P}|}
where

\begin{split}{}&A_{i}=\frac{1}{2}I\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\quad B_{i}=\sum_{l\in[r]}E_{l,l}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times m},\quad C_{i}=4\epsilon p_{i}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}+\sum_{l\in[r]}E_{l,l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},\end{split}

(100)

where
E_{l,l}
is a matrix (of appropriate dimensions) with
1
in the
(l,l)
entry and
0
elsewhere,
e_{l}
is a vector (of appropriate dimensions) with
1
in the
l
entry and
0
elsewhere.
It is clear that all systems are minimal, since
B_{i}
are full row rank and
C_{i}
are full column rank. Moreover, in all systems, only matrices
C_{i}
are different for each
i
, and
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
remain the same. We will see that this “high-dimensional perturbation” only in each observer matrix

Now consider any estimator
\widehat{f}
mapping the dataset
\mathcal{D}
to
\left(\widehat{A}(\mathcal{D}),\widehat{B}(\mathcal{D}),\widehat{C}(\mathcal{D})\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}\times\mathbb{R}^{r\times m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}

\begin{split}F_{i}={}&\left\{\mathcal{D}:\left\|\widehat{C}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\widehat{B}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)-C_{i}B_{i}\right\|<\epsilon\right\}\end{split}

(101)

\begin{split}\left\|C_{i}B_{i}-C_{j}B_{j}\right\|=\left\|(C_{i}-C_{j})B_{j}\right\|=4\epsilon\left\|(p_{i}-p_{j})e_{1}{}^{\intercal}\right\|=4\epsilon\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|_{2}\geq 2\epsilon.\end{split}

(102)

\begin{split}{}&\left\|\widehat{C}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\widehat{B}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)-C_{i}B_{i}\right\|+\left\|\widehat{C}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)\widehat{B}\left(\mathcal{D}\right)-C_{j}B_{j}\right\|\geq\left\|C_{i}B_{i}-C_{j}B_{j}\right\|\geq 2\epsilon.\end{split}

(103)

Step 2: We now bound the KL-divergence of dataset distribution coming from any pair of systems we constructed.

For any
k\in[K]
, let
u^{k}
,
x^{k}
,
y^{k}
denote the corresponding trajectories of the inputs, latent variables and observations. Moreover, let
u=\cup_{k\in[K]}u^{k}
,
y=\cup_{k\in[K]}y^{k}
and
x=\cup_{k\in[K]}x^{k}
denote the collection of all trajectories. For any
i\in[|\mathcal{P}|]
, let
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}
denote the joint distribution of
\{u,x,y\}
generated by system
\mathcal{M}_{i}=(r,n,m,A_{i},B_{i},C_{i},\Sigma_{w},\Sigma_{\eta})
. Let
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}
,
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UX}
be the marginal distribution of
\{y,u\}
and
\{x,u\}

\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U}
,
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|UY}
and
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{Y|UX}
to be the conditional distributions.
For the rest of the proof, we apply Theorem
B.1
on the events
\{F_{i}\}_{i\in[|\mathcal{P}|]}
and distributions
\{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}\}_{i\in[|\mathcal{P}|]}
.

We aim to upper bound the KL-divergence between distributions
\{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{P}|}
. To do this, we start from showing that
\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UY})\leq\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})
.

\begin{split}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})={}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}(u,x,y)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}(u,x,y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY}(u,x,y)}\text{d}u\text{d}x\text{d}y\\
={}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UY}(u,y)\mathbb{P}_{j}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)}\text{d}u\text{d}x\text{d}y\\
={}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UY}(u,y)}\text{d}u\text{d}x\text{d}y\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt+\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)}\text{d}u\text{d}x\text{d}y\\
={}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\left(\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}(x)\text{d}x\right)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UY}(u,y)}\text{d}u\text{d}y\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt+\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\text{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u,Y=y}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{X|U=u,Y=y}\right)\text{d}u\text{d}y\\
\overset{(i)}{\geq}{}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}(u,y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UY}(u,y)}\text{d}u\text{d}y\\
={}&\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UY}).\end{split}

(104)

Here
(i)
holds because KL-divergence (the second term) is non-negative. Therefore, we only need to upper bound
\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})
. Notice that

\begin{split}{}&\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}(u,x,y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY}(u,x,y)}=\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{U}(u)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{X|U=u}(x)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{Y|U=u,X=x}(y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{U}(u)\mathbb{P}_{j}^{X|U=u}(x)\mathbb{P}_{j}^{Y|U=u,X=x}(y)}\\
={}&\log\frac{\prod_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\mathbb{P}_{i}(u_{k,t})}{\prod_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\mathbb{P}_{j}(u_{k,t})}+\log\frac{\prod_{k=1,t=1}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{i}(x_{k,t}|x_{k,0:t-1},u_{k,0:t-1})}{\prod_{k=1,t=1}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{j}(x_{k,t}|x_{k,0:t-1},u_{k,0:t-1})}\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt+\log\frac{\prod_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{i}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,0:t},u_{k,0:t})}{\prod_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{P}_{j}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,0:t},u_{k,0:t})}\\
={}&\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}(u_{k,t})}{\mathbb{P}_{j}(u_{k,t})}+\sum_{k=1,t=1}^{K,T_{k}}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}(x_{k,t}|x_{k,t-1},u_{k,t-1})}{\mathbb{P}_{j}(x_{k,t}|x_{k,t-1},u_{k,t-1})}\\
{}&\hskip 20.00003pt+\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}{\mathbb{P}_{j}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}\\
={}&\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}{\mathbb{P}_{j}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}.\\
\end{split}

(105)

Here the last line is because the distribution of
u
and
x
doesn’t depend on the
i
or

. This is holds for

Therefore,

\begin{split}{}&\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})\\
={}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}(u,x,y)\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}(u,x,y)}{\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY}(u,x,y)}\text{d}x\text{d}y\\
={}&\int\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}(u,x,y)\left(\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}{\mathbb{P}_{j}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}\right)\text{d}u\text{d}x\text{d}y\\
={}&\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(\log\frac{\mathbb{P}_{i}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}{\mathbb{P}_{j}(y_{k,t}|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})}\right)\\
\overset{(i)}{=}{}&\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(\text{KL}\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{Y}(\cdot|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{Y}(\cdot|x_{k,t},u_{k,t})\right)\right)\\
\overset{(ii)}{=}{}&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(x_{k,t}{}^{\intercal}(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}(C_{i}-C_{j})x_{k,t}\right)\end{split}

(106)

Here in
(i)
follows from the definition of KL-divergence and by taking the expectation of
x_{t}
(or

in the second term). And
(ii)
follows from the fact that

(this is because

) and from the KL-divergence between two gaussians. We further simplify the expression as follows

\begin{split}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})={}&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(x_{k,t}{}^{\intercal}(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}\Sigma_{\eta}^{-1}(C_{i}-C_{j})x_{k,t}\right)\\
\leq{}&\frac{1}{2\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(x_{k,t}{}^{\intercal}(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}(C_{i}-C_{j})x_{k,t}\right)\\
={}&\frac{1}{2\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\text{tr}\left((C_{i}-C_{j})\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(x_{k,t}x_{k,t}{}^{\intercal}\right)(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}\right)\\
\end{split}

(107)

Now we consider term

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{i}(x_{k,t}x_{k,t}{}^{\intercal})={}&\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}A_{i}^{\tau}\left(B_{i}u_{k,t-1-\tau}+w_{k,t-1-\tau}\right)\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left(B_{i}u_{k,t-1-\tau}+w_{k,t-1-\tau}\right){}^{\intercal}(A_{i}^{\tau}){}^{\intercal}\right]\\
={}&\sum_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}=0}^{t-1}A_{i}^{\tau_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\left(B_{i}u_{k,t-1-\tau_{1}}+w_{k,t-1-\tau_{1}}\right)\left(B_{i}u_{k,t-1-\tau_{2}}+w_{k,t-1-\tau_{2}}\right){}^{\intercal}\right](A_{i}^{\tau_{2}}){}^{\intercal}\\
\overset{(i)}{=}{}&\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}A_{i}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[\left(B_{i}u_{k,t-1-\tau}+w_{k,t-1-\tau}\right)\left(B_{i}u_{k,t-1-\tau}+w_{k,t-1-\tau}\right){}^{\intercal}\right](A_{i}^{\tau}){}^{\intercal}\\
={}&\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}A_{i}^{\tau}\left(B_{i}\mathbb{E}(u_{k,t}u_{k,t}{}^{\intercal})B_{i}{}^{\intercal}+\Sigma_{w}\right)(A_{i}^{\tau}){}^{\intercal}\\
\preceq{}&\left(\psi_{w}+\left\|B\right\|^{2}\psi_{u}\right)\Gamma_{t-1}(A_{i})\\
={}&\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\Gamma_{t-1}(A_{i}).\end{split}

(108)

Here
(i)
is because

are zero-mean and independent of

for

.
Substituting back gives

\begin{split}{}&\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})\\
\leq{}&\frac{1}{2\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}}\text{tr}\left((C_{i}-C_{j})\mathbb{E}_{i}\left(x_{k,t}x_{k,t}{}^{\intercal}\right)(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}\right)\\
\leq{}&\frac{\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}}{2\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=1}^{K,T_{k}}\text{tr}\left((C_{i}-C_{j})\Gamma_{t-1}(A_{i})(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}\right).\end{split}

(109)

Here the last line is because
x_{0}=0
. Since

, we conclude that

\begin{split}\Gamma_{t}(A_{i})=\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}A_{i}^{t}(A_{i}^{t}){}^{\intercal}\preceq\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2t}I\preceq\frac{4}{3}I.\end{split}

(110)

Substituting it back gives

\begin{split}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})\leq{}&\frac{2(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u})}{3\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\text{tr}\left((C_{i}-C_{j})(C_{i}-C_{j}){}^{\intercal}\right)\\
\end{split}

(111)

From the definitions of

and

\begin{split}C_{i}-C_{j}={}&4\epsilon(p_{i}-p_{j})e_{1}{}^{\intercal}.\end{split}

(112)

Then

\begin{split}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})\leq{}&\frac{32\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\epsilon^{2}}{3\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\text{tr}\left(\left(p_{i}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}-p_{j}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}\right)\left(p_{i}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}-p_{j}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}\right){}^{\intercal}\right)\\
={}&\frac{32\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\epsilon^{2}}{3\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\left\|p_{i}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}-p_{j}e_{1}{}^{\intercal}\right\|_{\text{F}}^{2}\\
={}&\frac{32\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\epsilon^{2}}{3\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|_{2}^{2}\\
\end{split}

(113)

Since

and

lie in the unit ball, we know that
\left\|p_{i}-p_{j}\right\|\leq 2
. Therefore,

\begin{split}\text{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{UXY}||\mathbb{P}_{j}^{UXY})\leq{}&\frac{128\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\epsilon^{2}}{3\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1,t=0}^{K,T_{k}-1}1=\frac{128\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\epsilon^{2}}{3\phi_{\eta}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}T_{k}\\
\leq{}&\frac{50\left(\psi_{w}+\psi_{u}\right)\epsilon^{2}}{\phi_{\eta}}\mathcal{T}.\end{split}

(114)

Here the last line is due to the definition of

Step 3: Now we invoke

B.1
and show that learning at least one of those minimal realizations is hard.

Now applying Theorem
B.1
on distributions

(115)

For the LHS, since

.

Proof.

Lemma B.4
.

Fix any invertible matrix
S
. Suppose
\left\|\widehat{C}\widehat{B}-CB\right\|\geq\epsilon
for
\epsilon\leq 3\left\|\widehat{B}\right\|\left\|\widehat{C}\right\|
. Then either

\begin{split}\left\|\widehat{C}-CS\right\|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{3\left\|\widehat{B}\right\|}\quad\text{ or }\quad\left\|\widehat{B}-S^{-1}B\right\|\geq\frac{\epsilon}{3\left\|\widehat{C}\right\|}.\end{split}

(125)

Remark B.5
.

In other words, for any similarity transformation, we can not learn either
\tilde{B}
or
\tilde{C}
well.

Proof.

We show this claim by contradiction. For any invertible matrix
S
, suppose

\begin{split}\left\|\widehat{C}-CS\right\|<\frac{\epsilon}{3\left\|\widehat{B}\right\|}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|\widehat{B}-S^{-1}B\right\|<\frac{\epsilon}{3\left\|\widehat{C}\right\|}.\end{split}

(126)

Then for any
\epsilon\leq\left\|B\right\|\left\|C\right\|
, we know that

\begin{split}{}&\left\|\widehat{C}\widehat{B}-CB\right\|\\
\leq{}&\left\|\widehat{C}\widehat{B}-CS\widehat{B}\right\|+\left\|CS\widehat{B}-CSS^{-1}B\right\|\\
\leq{}&\left\|\widehat{C}-CS\right\|\left\|\widehat{B}\right\|+\left\|CS\right\|\left\|\widehat{B}-S^{-1}B\right\|\\
\leq{}&\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\left(\left\|CS-\widehat{C}\right\|+\left\|\widehat{C}\right\|\right)\frac{\epsilon}{3\left\|\widehat{C}\right\|}\\
\leq{}&\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\frac{\epsilon}{3\left\|\widehat{B}\right\|\left\|\widehat{C}\right\|}\\
\leq{}&\epsilon,\end{split}

(127)

which causes a contradiction.
∎
