A Lightcone Embedding of the Twin Building of a Hyperbolic Kac–Moody Group
Lisa CARBONE †, Alex J. FEINGOLD ‡ and Walter FREYN §

Abstract
Let A A be a symmetrizable hyperbolic generalized Cartan matrix with Kac–Moody algebra 𝔤 = 𝔤 ⁡ ( A ) \mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A) and (adjoint) Kac–Moody group G = G ( A ) = ⟨ exp ( ad ( t e i ) ) , exp ( ad ( t f i ) ) | t ∈ ℂ ⟩ G=G(A)=\langle\exp({\rm ad}(te_{i})),\allowbreak\exp({\rm ad}(tf_{i}))\,|\,t\in{\mathbb{C}}\rangle where e i e_{i} and f i f_{i} are the simple root vectors. Let ( B + , B − , N ) \big(B^{+},B^{-},N\big) be the twin B ​ N BN -pair naturally associated to G G and let ( ℬ + , ℬ − ) \big(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-}\big) be the corresponding twin building with Weyl group W W and natural G G -action, which respects the usual W W -valued distance and codistance functions. This work connects the twin building ( ℬ + , ℬ − ) \big(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-}\big) of G G and the Kac–Moody algebra 𝔤 = 𝔤 ⁡ ( A ) \mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A) in a new geometrical way. The Cartan–Chevalley involution, ω \omega , of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} has fixed point real subalgebra, 𝔨 \mathfrak{k} , the ‘compact’ (unitary) real form of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} , and 𝔨 \mathfrak{k} contains the compact Cartan 𝔱 = 𝔨 ∩ 𝔥 \mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{k}\cap\mathfrak{h} . We show that a real bilinear form ( ⋅ , ⋅ ) (\cdot,\cdot) is Lorentzian with signatures ( 1 , ∞ ) (1,\infty) on 𝔨 \mathfrak{k} , and ( 1 , n − 1 ) (1,n-1) on 𝔱 \mathfrak{t} . We define { k ∈ 𝔨 | ( k , k ) ≤ 0 } \{k\in\mathfrak{k}\,|\,(k,k)\leq 0\} to be the lightcone of 𝔨 \mathfrak{k} , and similarly for 𝔱 \mathfrak{t} . Let K K be the compact (unitary) real form of G G , that is, the fixed point subgroup of the lifting of ω \omega to G G . We construct a K K -equivariant embedding of the twin building of G G into the lightcone of the compact real form 𝔨 \mathfrak{k} of 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} . Our embedding gives a geometric model of part of the twin building, where each half consists of infinitely many copies of a W W -tessellated hyperbolic space glued together along hyperplanes of the faces. Locally, at each such face, we find an SU ⁡ ( 2 ) {\rm SU}(2) -orbit of chambers stabilized by U ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rm U}(1) which is thus parametrized by a Riemann sphere SU ⁡ ( 2 ) / U ⁡ ( 1 ) ≅ S 2 {\rm SU}(2)/{\rm U}(1)\cong S^{2} . For n = 2 n=2 the twin building is a twin tree. In this case, we construct our embedding explicitly and we describe the action of the real root groups on the fundamental twin apartment. We also construct a spherical twin building at infinity, and construct an embedding of it into the set of rays on the boundary of the lightcone.

Introduction

Buildings were introduced by J. Tits in the 1950’s to provide a ‘geometric’ interpretation of simple algebraic groups and finite groups of Lie type. Following ideas originally developed by F. Klein in his Erlangen program, Tits aimed at understanding a large class of groups, including simple algebraic groups and finite groups of Lie type, as the automorphism groups of carefully constructed geometric objects he called ‘buildings’. It turns out that the buildings of Tits correspond to groups which admit an additional structure, called a
BN
-pair
or equivalently a
Tits system
. This structure also gives rise to a Bruhat decomposition for the corresponding group. Tits’s approach was the geometric counterpart to the ‘algebraic’ construction of these groups by C. Chevalley using automorphisms of Lie algebras
[
9
]
.

In general, the building
\mathcal{B}
of a group
G
with a
BN
-pair is an abstract simplicial complex constructed from group theoretical data. The simplices in
\mathcal{B}
are in bijection with the union of all cosets
G/P
, where
P
runs through a set of representatives of the conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups. These simplices satisfy incidence relations which can be phrased in terms of inclusions of these cosets.

For Kac–Moody groups, the closest infinite-dimensional analogue of simple algebraic groups, the structure of buildings and
BN
-pairs is richer. As Kac–Moody groups have two conjugacy classes of Borel subgroups, they admit the definition of two ‘opposite’
BN
-pairs which together form a ‘twin
BN
-pair’. Consequently the geometry associated to a Kac–Moody group
G
naturally consists of two related components. This object is called a ‘twin building’
\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}^{+}\cup\mathcal{B}^{-}
associated to a twin
BN
-pair,
(
B^{+},B^{-},N
)
, where the subgroups
B^{+}
and
B^{-}
are the standard Borel subgroups constructed from the positive and negative roots of the Kac–Moody algebra respectively. Thus, by construction, the building is related to the combinatorial structure of its Kac–Moody group.

Kac–Moody algebras and groups fall naturally into three types,
finite type
,
affine type
and
indefinite type
. While Kac–Moody groups of finite type (simple Lie groups) and of affine type are well-understood, there are far fewer results known for the indefinite type. The most important subclass of indefinite type is the hyperbolic type, studied since the 1980s by various authors
[
17
,
18
,
36
,
40
,
45
]
but certainly of interest to physicists
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
34
,
59
]
.

While the algebraic properties of the hyperbolic Kac–Moody groups and algebras attracted some attention, there have been only a few mathematical results concerning their geometry, for example, the study of homogeneous or symmetric spaces associated to them
[
26
]
.
These classes of objects are very well understood for finite-dimensional Lie groups and for affine Kac–Moody groups. It is hoped that the understanding of this geometry, called Kac–Moody geometry, will shed new light on algebraic and structural properties of these groups. As a first step towards the goal of understanding the geometry of hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras and groups, we show in this work that for
hyperbolic Kac–Moody groups
G
over
{\mathbb{C}}
, the associated Tits building is not only an abstract simplicial complex admitting an action of
G
, but that it admits a natural embedding inside the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
of the Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. As a consequence, the structure of the Tits building and of the compact real form of the Kac–Moody algebra are closely related.

Our results generalize work of Quillen and Mitchell in the finite-dimensional case, and Kramer and Freyn in the affine case. Mitchell, in a paper based on ideas of Quillen, used embeddings of spherical buildings associated to simple (real or complex) Lie groups into the tangent space of
the associated noncompact real symmetric space
[
47
]
. In particular, the topological building of a real noncompact Lie group
G
with maximal compact subgroup
K
is canonically identified with a space homeomorphic to the unit sphere in the tangent space of the noncompact real symmetric space
G/K
. For example, the topological building of type
A_{1}
, isomorphic to
S^{1}
, can be embedded into the unit circle in the tangent space of
\mathcal{H}^{2}={\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})/{\rm SO}(2)
which is
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Kramer gave a topological construction of the complex twin building of type
A_{n}^{(1)}
and an equivariant embedding of this building into the associated affine Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}

[
43
]
.
Freyn gave a 2-parameter family
\varphi_{\ell,r}
of equivariant embeddings of affine ‘twin cities’
\mathcal{B}\!=\!\mathcal{B}^{+}\!\cup\!\mathcal{B}^{-}
into the ‘
s
-representations’ of affine Kac–Moody symmetric spaces
[
19
,
20
,
22
,
23
,
25
]
. A ‘twin city’ is the natural completion of a twin building, so affine twin cities correspond to completions of affine Kac–Moody groups as twin buildings correspond to minimal Kac–Moody groups. Twin cities carry a natural topology that is derived from the topology on the corresponding Kac–Moody group.

Denoting by
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
the Cartan decomposition, and restricting Freyn’s result to non-completed affine Kac–Moody algebras, yields an identification of the twin building with the intersection
\mathfrak{p}_{\ell,r}
of the sphere of squared length
\ell\in\mathbb{R}
with horospheres parametrized by
r_{d}=\pm r\not=0
, where
r_{d}
is the real coefficient of the derivation
d
. The positive and negative components of the twin building,
\mathcal{B}^{+}
and
\mathcal{B}^{-}
, are immersed into the two sheets of
\mathfrak{p}_{\ell,r}
described by
r_{d}<0
respectively
r_{d}>0
.

For affine Kac–Moody groups of the compact type, which are symmetric spaces called of
‘type II’
following Helgason’s classification (see
[
24
,
32
]
), this result includes embeddings of complex buildings into the compact forms of affine Kac–Moody algebras; this case is the affine counterpart to the embeddings constructed in this paper for symmetrizable hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras. We refer the reader to
[
19
,
20
,
21
,
22
]
for additional details.

For
\mathfrak{g}
a hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra we are motivated in part by the appearance of
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{k}
and
G/K
in coset models of certain supergravity theories, where coset spaces of the split real forms occur as parameter spaces for the scalar fields of the theory
[
11
,
34
,
59
]
.

The ideas in this paper may be extended to the wider class of Lorentzian Kac–Moody algebras, but it remains to be seen how much the results are affected by the differences in the geometry of the lightcone and the Tits cone. Some work in this direction was started by A. Tichai
[
55
]
.

1.1
Summary of results

The following paragraph summarizes the main results of this paper.
For
G
of hyperbolic type, we construct a
K
-equivariant embedding of the twin building of
G
into the lightcone of the compact
real form
\mathfrak{k}
. In
\mathfrak{t}
,
W
acts on rays in the interior of the lightcone (and on certain rays on the nullcone), tessellating a copy of
hyperbolic space
H^{n-1}
in each half of the lightcone (and some limit points in its boundary), giving a twin apartment. The
W
-images of the fundamental domain form the chambers of that apartment, so each face of any chamber is in a hyperplane fixed by a
W
-conjugate of a simple reflection
w_{i}
. The associated conjugate of the compact subgroup
{\rm SU}(2)_{i}
fixes that hyperplane pointwise, but rotates the rest of
\mathfrak{t}
into a
K
-conjugate of
\mathfrak{t}
, another apartment sharing that fixed chamber wall.
This gives our geometric model of part of the twin building where each half is infinitely many copies of a tessellated hyperbolic space glued together along hyperplanes of the faces. Locally, at each such face, a family of chambers meet, the orbit of an
{\rm SU}(2)
with a
{\rm U}(1)
stabilizer, so the family is indexed by a Riemann sphere,
{\rm SU}(2)/{\rm U}(1)\cong S^{2}
.

In rank
2
the Weyl group is the infinite dihedral group,
D_{\infty}
, each apartment is a line, and each building
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
is a tree. We model each apartment as a copy of the real line tessellated into unit intervals (chambers)

C(n)=\big[n-\frac{1}{2},n+\frac{1}{2}\big]
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, so the vertices are
\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{2}
.
At each vertex a family of intervals (chambers) is attached, each in a line (apartment) which is tessellated, and in each of those lines chambers are attached, on ad infinitum. We describe the action of the real root groups on the fundamental twin apartment. The family of chambers attached at any vertex is the projective space
P_{1}({\mathbb{F}})
, where
{\mathbb{F}}
is the field over which the group is defined, so for
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
, we have
P_{1}({\mathbb{C}})=\hat{\mathbb{C}}
is the Riemann sphere. Thus, in rank
2
each building
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
is a
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
-tree.
In that case, we also find a spherical twin building
at infinity
, and construct an embedding of it into the set of rays on the boundary of the lightcone.

In rank
3
the Weyl group is a hyperbolic triangle group, each apartment is a copy of the Poincaré disk,
\mathcal{P}
, tessellated into hyperbolic triangles by
W
. The boundary of each triangle is a segment in a hyperbolic geodesic. Along each geodesic segment we
have a
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
-family of attached triangles, each in a copy of
\mathcal{P}
, which is tessellated and has attached disks along each
geodesic, on ad infinitum. The geometrical embedding we found is only
K
-equivariant, so from a fundamental apartment we get only
to those apartments all of whose chambers are in some
K
-conjugate,
k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1}
. It means that in such apartments, pairs of triangles
attached to a geodesic edge are ‘balanced’ opposite each other, corresponding to antipodal points in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
. So under the action of
K
, the fundamental
\mathcal{P}
can be rigidly rotated along any geodesic into another one, having only that geodesic line in common.
But the full Kac–Moody group
G
can leave a half-apartment fixed and rotate the other half up, creating a ‘hinge’. The complete apartment system would then be ‘hinged’ copies of
\mathcal{P}
made up of pieces glued along geodesics.

2
Kac–Moody algebras and Kac–Moody groups

2.1
Kac–Moody algebras

A Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{{\mathbb{F}}}(A)
over a field
{\mathbb{F}}
may be constructed by generators and relations using a collection of data which includes a matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
called a
generalized Cartan matrix
satisfying the following conditions for all
i,j\in I=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
:

a_{ij}\in{\mathbb{Z}},\qquad a_{ii}=2,\qquad a_{ij}\leq 0\ \text{if}\ i\neq j,\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_{ij}=0\iff a_{ji}=0.

A generalized Cartan matrix
A
is
indecomposable
if there is no partition of the set
I=I_{1}\cup I_{2}
into non-empty subsets
so that
a_{ij}=0
for
i\in I_{1}
and
j\in I_{2}
. The matrix
A
is called
symmetrizable
if there is an invertible diagonal matrix

D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{\ell})
such that
DA=(d_{i}a_{ij})
is symmetric. One distinguishes various types of generalized Cartan matrices:

–

Finite type:

A
is positive-definite. In this case
A
is the Cartan matrix of a finite-dimensional semisimple Lie algebra and
\det(A)>0
.

–

Affine type:

A
is positive-semidefinite, but not positive-definite, and all minors are positive definite. In this case
\det(A)=0
.

–

Hyperbolic type:

A
is neither of finite nor affine type, but every proper, indecomposable submatrix is either of finite or of affine type. In this case
\det(A)<0
.

–

Strictly hyperbolic type:

A
is hyperbolic type, but every proper, indecomposable submatrix is of finite type.

The terminology “hyperbolic” goes back to the original (independent) papers of Kac and Moody, and comes from the
geometry of the root systems for the corresponding Kac–Moody Lie algebras. The Weyl group orbits of roots lie on hyperbolas in the
rank 2 hyperbolic case and on hyperboloids in higher rank cases. We have included in Section 7 two figures illustrating the rank 2 root
systems which show this hyperbolic geometry clearly. The definitions of roots, root systems and Weyl groups are below.

A complex Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}(A)
has at least two
real forms
, that is, real Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}(A)={\mathbb{C}}\otimes\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
. The
split real form
of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}(A)
is
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
(see
[
3
]
).
From this point on, all generalized Cartan matrices in this paper are assumed to be indecomposable, symmetrizable and of hyperbolic type.

Given field
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{C}}
or
{\mathbb{F}}=\mathbb{R}
:

–

a hyperbolic generalized Cartan matrix
A=(a_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
, and

–

a vector space
\mathfrak{h}
over
{\mathbb{F}}
(which will play the role of a Cartan subalgebra) with
dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathfrak{h})=\ell
, and basis
\{h_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
,

then there is a set of
simple roots

\Pi=\{\alpha_{j}\,|\,j\in I\}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{*}
such that the pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\colon\mathfrak{h}^{*}\times\mathfrak{h}\to{\mathbb{F}}
given by
\langle\alpha,h\rangle=\alpha(h)
satisfies
\alpha_{i}(h_{j})=a_{ij}
for all
i,j\in I
, and
the hyperbolic Kac–Moody Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{F}}(A)
is generated by the elements
\{e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
, subject to the relations
[
27
,
35
]
:

–

[h_{i},h_{j}]=0
,

–

[h,e_{i}]=\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle e_{i}
,
h\in\mathfrak{h}
and
[h,f_{i}]=-\langle\alpha_{i},h\rangle f_{i}
,
h\in\mathfrak{h}
,

–

[e_{i},f_{j}]=\delta_{ij}h_{i}
,

–

({\rm ad}_{e_{i}})^{1-a_{ij}}(e_{j})=0
,
i\neq j
and
({\rm ad}_{f_{i}})^{1-a_{ij}}(f_{j})=0
,
i\neq j
,

where
{\rm ad}_{x}(y)=[x,y]
. For each
i\in I
, let
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}^{i}
be the Lie subalgebra (isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbb{F}})
) with basis
\{e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}\}
, so that
\mathfrak{g}
is generated by these subalgebras. The abelian Lie subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}
with basis
\{h_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
is called the
standard Cartan subalgebra
of
\mathfrak{g}
.

The algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A)
is
infinite-dimensional
since
A
is not positive definite, and it
admits an invariant symmetric bilinear form
(\hskip 2.0pt,\hskip 2.0pt)
which is unique up to a global scaling factor

[
35
, Section II]
, and which extends the form on
\mathfrak{h}
given by
2(h_{i},h_{j})/(h_{j},h_{j})=\alpha_{i}(h_{j})=a_{ij}
. The nondegeneracy
of the pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
between
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{h}
determines a corresponding form on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. This means that

(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=(h_{i},h_{j})
and

a_{ji}(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})/2=(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{i})=(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})=a_{ij}(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j})/2,

so that we may take the diagonal matrix
D=\operatorname{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{\ell})
with
d_{i}=2/(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i})
and the symmetric matrix

DA=(d_{i}a_{ij})=(2a_{ij}/(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i}))
. The standard way to choose
the global scaling factor is so that the longest square length of any simple root is
2
.

The adjoint action of
\mathfrak{h}
on
\mathfrak{g}
is diagonalizable, and the simultaneous nonzero eigenspaces for that action,

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\,|\,[h,x]=\alpha(h)x,\,h\in\mathfrak{h}\}

for
\alpha\neq 0
are called
root spaces
.

The root system of
\mathfrak{g}
is the set
{\Phi}=\{\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}\,|\,\alpha\neq 0,\,\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
, and the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of
{\Phi}
, called the
root lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
, is denoted by
Q
. From the relations defining
\mathfrak{g}
we see that for each
i\in I
,
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}}={\mathbb{C}}e_{i}
and

\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_{i}}={\mathbb{C}}f_{i}
, so that
\pm\alpha_{i}\in{\Phi}
. In fact, we have

Q=\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathbb{Z}\alpha_{\ell}
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module.
Every
\alpha\in{\Phi}
can be written uniquely as
\alpha=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\ell}k_{i}\alpha_{i}
where either all
k_{i}\geq 0
, in which case
\alpha
is called
positive
, or all
k_{i}\leq 0
, in which case
\alpha
is called
negative
. The set of all positive roots is denoted
{\Phi}^{+}
, and
the set of all negative roots is denoted by
{\Phi}^{-}
. Any root is either positive or negative.

For each simple root
\alpha_{i}
,
i\in I=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
, we define the
simple root reflection

\displaystyle w_{i}(\alpha_{j}):=\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{j}(h_{i})\alpha_{i}.

(2.1)

The set
S=\{w_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq\ell\}
generates a group
W=W(A)
of orthogonal transformations of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, called the
Weyl group
of
A
. The non-degenerate pairing between
\mathfrak{h}
and
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
gives a corresponding action of
W
as orthogonal transformations on
\mathfrak{h}
. A root
\alpha\in{\Phi}
is called a
real root
if there exists
w\in W
such that
w\alpha
is a simple root. A root
\alpha
which is not real is called
imaginary
. We denote by
{\Phi}^{\rm re}
the set of all real roots and
{\Phi}^{\rm im}
the set of all imaginary roots.

The multibracket,
[e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}]={\rm ad}_{e_{i_{1}}}{\rm ad}_{e_{i_{2}}}\cdots{\rm ad}_{e_{i_{n-1}}}e_{i_{n}}
is in the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
for
\alpha=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i_{j}}\in{\Phi}^{+}
, while a similar multibracket with each
e_{i_{j}}
replaced
by
f_{i_{j}}
is in
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
. Therefore,
\mathfrak{g}
has a root space decomposition

[
35
, Theorem 1.2]

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in{\Phi}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{+}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-},

where

\mathfrak{g}^{+}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in{\Phi}^{{}^{+}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha},\qquad\mathfrak{g}^{-}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in{\Phi}^{{}^{-}}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

The standard positive Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}\equiv\mathfrak{b}^{+}
is defined by
\mathfrak{b}^{+}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{+}
and the standard negative Borel subalgebra by
\mathfrak{b}^{-}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{g}^{-}
.

2.2
Kac–Moody groups

There are various ways to define abstract Kac–Moody groups
(see for example
[
37
,
46
,
52
,
58
]
).
The main point of the abstract approach is to give a flexible definition of Kac–Moody groups, allowing the construction of groups whose adjoint action is not faithful. There are indeed important examples of that kind, the smallest one being the finite type Kac–Moody group
{\rm SL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, where the two matrices
\pm Id
both act as the identity operator in the adjoint representation. Hence the adjoint representation of this Kac–Moody group is actually the group
{\rm PSL}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
, and that is what we get by using the definition of the adjoint Kac–Moody group given in equation (
2.2
) for the Cartan matrix
A=[2]
of type
A_{1}
.

By the definition of the abstract Kac–Moody group, there is a surjective group homomorphism:
Ad:G\longrightarrow G^{\rm ad}
from an abstract Kac–Moody group onto the adjoint Kac–Moody group whose kernel is exactly the center of G (see
[
52
, Proposition 9.6.2]
). As we will see in Section
2.3
, for the subgroups
B^{\pm}
and
N
defined there, we have

B^{\pm}\cap N
is abelian, and the center of
G
is the kernel of the action of
G
on the twin building (see
[
6
, Lemma 1.7]
). Hence, without loss of generality, to understand the action on twin buildings we can work with the adjoint Kac–Moody group.
Our references for this section are
[
37
,
44
,
46
,
48
]
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
,
L
be a complex vector space and let

\phi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to{\rm End}(L)
be any integrable representation, so that
all
\phi(e_{i})
and
\phi(f_{i})
are locally nilpotent on
L
and the linear operators

Definition 2.1

Twin
\boldsymbol{BN}
-pair and twin Tits building of a minimal Kac–Moody group

Our references for this section are

Definition 2.2

(BN-pair)
.

A group
G
is said to have a
BN
-pair if
G
has subgroups
B
and
N
such that

T1:

G=\langle B,N\rangle
,
T=B\cap N\lhd N
,
W=N/T
is generated by a set
S
.

T2:

For
s\in S
and
w\in W
we have
sBw\subseteq BswB\cup BwB\,
.

T3:

For
s\in S
we have
sBs^{-1}\not\subseteq B\,
.

The group
W
is called the
Weyl group
of the
BN
-pair, and
(G,B,N,S)
is called a
Tits system
. Furthermore,
(W,S)
is a
Coxeter system
and there is a length function
\ell\colon W\to\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 2.3

(twin-BN-pair)
.

A group
G
is said to have a twin
BN
-pair with Weyl group
W
if
G
has subgroups
B^{+}
,
B^{-}
and
N
such that

TW1:

\big(G,B^{\pm},N,S\big)
is a Tits system.

TW2:

If
\ell(sw)<\ell(w)
for
s\in S
and
w\in W
, then
B^{\pm}sB^{\pm}wB^{\mp}=B^{\pm}swB^{\mp}
.

TW3:

B^{+}s\cap B^{-}=\varnothing
.

In this case,
(G,B^{+},B^{-},N,S)
is called a
twin Tits system
.

For a hyperbolic adjoint Kac–Moody group
G=G^{\rm ad}_{\mathbb{C}}(A)
we have standard Borel subgroups,

B^{\pm}=(B^{\rm ad})^{\pm}
, the standard maximal torus,
T=T^{\rm ad}_{\mathbb{C}}
, and its normalizer in
G
,

N=N_{\mathbb{C}}^{\rm ad}
. Thus the group
T=N\cap B^{\pm}
is a normal subgroup of
N
.
The group
W=N_{G}(T)/T
generated by
S=\big\{\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}\,|\,1\leq i\leq\ell\big\}
, is isomorphic to our
earlier definition in Section
2.1

of the Weyl group
W
generated by simple reflections as a group of orthogonal transformations of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
given by formula (
2.1
). Thus, we have a twin
BN
-pair or twin Tits system, for the hyperbolic adjoint Kac–Moody group
G
.

We have the (positive and negative) standard Borel subgroups, corresponding to the standard Borel subalgebras,
B^{\pm}=T(G)U^{\pm}
where
U^{+}
is generated by all positive real root groups and
U^{-}
is generated by all negative real root groups. The
BN
-pairs
\big(B^{+},N\big)
and
\big(B^{-},N\big)
have Birkhoff and Bruhat decompositions:

G=\coprod_{w\in W}B^{\pm}wB^{\mp}=\coprod_{w\in W}B^{\pm}wB^{\pm}.

In these double cosets,
w\in W=N/T
is a coset
nT
for
n\in N
, but for any two representatives of the same coset,
w=nT=n^{\prime}T
, we have

B^{\pm}nB^{\pm}=B^{\pm}nTB^{\pm}=B^{\pm}n^{\prime}TB^{\pm}=B^{\pm}n^{\prime}B^{\pm},

so we can label a double coset by
w\in W
.

We can use the Bruhat decomposition to define a
W
-valued distance function on
G/B^{\pm}
,

\delta^{\pm}\colon\ G/B^{\pm}\times G/B^{\pm}\to W

by
\delta^{\pm}\big(g_{1}B^{\pm},g_{2}B^{\pm}\big)=w
when
g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\in B^{\pm}wB^{\pm}
. Similarly, we can use the
Birkhoff decomposition to define a
W
-valued codistance function

\delta^{*}\colon\ G/B^{\pm}\times G/B^{\mp}\to W

by
\delta^{*}\big(g_{1}B^{\pm},g_{2}B^{\mp}\big)=w
when
g_{1}^{-1}g_{2}\in B^{\pm}wB^{\mp}
.

A proper subgroup
P^{\pm}
of
G
is called
parabolic
when it contains a conjugate of a Borel subgroup
B^{\pm}
, and it is called
positive
or
negative
, depending on the sign. For each subset
J\subset I
define the subgroup

W_{J}=\langle w_{j}\,|\,j\in J\rangle
of
W
and the corresponding subgroups of
G
,

P_{J}^{\pm}\quad=\quad\coprod_{w\in W_{J}}B^{\pm}wB^{\pm}.

Note that
P_{I}^{\pm}=G
is not parabolic since it is not proper,

P_{\varnothing}^{\pm}=B^{\pm}
, and we write
P_{i}^{\pm}=P_{\{i\}}^{\pm}
. For
J\subsetneq I
we
call
P_{J}^{\pm}
a
standard parabolic subgroup
, and these form a complete set of representatives of the conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups, so there are
2\cdot(2^{\ell}-1)
conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups. A parabolic subgroup
P^{\pm}
is called
maximal
if there is no parabolic subgroup
P^{\prime\pm}
such that
P^{\pm}\subsetneq P^{\prime\pm}
. For each
i\in I
,

P_{[i]}^{\pm}=P_{I\backslash\{i\}}^{\pm}
is a maximal standard parabolic subgroup, so there are
2\ell
conjugacy classes of maximal parabolic subgroups,
\ell
positive and
\ell
negative.

Definition 2.4

(Tits building)
.

A
Tits building
of type
(W,S)
consists of a simplicial complex
\mathcal{B}
together with a collection
\mathcal{A}
of subcomplexes,
each of which is called an apartment, such that

1)

each apartment is a Coxeter complex for the Coxeter system
(W,S)
,

2)

each pair of
chambers
, i.e., simplices of maximal dimension in
\mathcal{B}
, is contained in a common apartment,

3)

for two apartments
A
and
A^{\prime}
there is an isomorphism
\varphi\colon A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
, fixing the intersection
A\cap A^{\prime}
.

A Coxeter complex for
(W,S)
is a simplicial complex on which there is a simply transitive action of a Coxeter group
W
on
the simplices of maximal dimension
\ell-1
(chambers). The simplices of dimension
\ell-2
are called panels, and are
each labeled by a generator,
s\in S
. We say that two chambers
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are
s
-adjacent when
sC_{1}=C_{2}
, which
means their intersection is an
s
-panel. Each element
w\in W
is a product of generators from
S
, so we define the
length

|w|
to be the minimal number of generators in an expression for
w
.
Suppose
C_{i}
for
0\leq i\leq d
is a sequence of chambers such that
C_{i-1}
and
C_{i}
are

s_{i}
-adjacent for
1\leq i\leq d
, so that
C_{i}=r_{i}C_{0}
for
r_{i}=s_{1}\cdots s_{i}
. Then we define the
W
-valued distance function
\delta(C_{0},C_{d})=r_{d}=s_{1}\cdots s_{d}
, so for any
w_{1},w_{2}\in W
and any chamber
C
, we have
\delta(w_{1}C,w_{2}C)=w_{1}^{-1}w_{2}
. Since any two chambers in the building
\mathcal{B}
are in a common apartment, we have defined the
W
-valued distance function
\delta\colon\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}\to W
where
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{B})
is the set of all chambers of
\mathcal{B}
.
One may choose a pair
(A,C)
consisting of an apartment
A
and a chamber
C
in

, which we call

, so that the chambers of

are uniquely labeled by the elements of
W
.

From
[
1
, Proposition 4.84]
, we have the following properties of

. For any chambers
C,C^{\prime},D\in\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{B})
we have

1.

\delta(C,D)=1
iff
C=D
.

2.

\delta(D,C)=\delta(C,D)^{-1}
.

3.

If
\delta(C^{\prime},C)=s\in S
and
\delta(C,D)=w\in W
, then
\delta(C^{\prime},D)=sw
or
\delta(C^{\prime},D)=w
. If, in addition,

|sw|=|w|+1
then
\delta(C^{\prime},D)=sw
.

4.

If
\delta(C,D)=w
then for any
s\in S
, there exists a chamber
C^{\prime}
such that
\delta(C^{\prime},C)=s
and
\delta(C^{\prime},D)=sw
.
If
|sw|=|w|-1
then there exists a unique such

.

Definition 2.8

(twin apartment)
.

A twin apartment in a twin building

is an ordered pair
\big(A^{+},A^{-}\big)
where
A^{\pm}\in\mathcal{A}^{\pm}

From now on let
\mathcal{B}
denote the twin building associated to a Kac–Moody group
G
.
The simplices of
\mathcal{B}
are in bijection with parabolic subgroups in such a way that simplices in
\mathcal{B}^{+}
correspond to positive parabolic subgroups and simplices in
\mathcal{B}^{-}
correspond to negative parabolic subgroups. The vertices (
0

\mathcal{B}
are in bijection with maximal parabolic subgroups in
G
. Chambers are in bijection with positive and negative Borel subgroups.
We will denote these simplices by their corresponding parabolic subgroups.
Since parabolic subgroups are self-normalizing, the simplices in the building can be equivalently indexed by the coset spaces
G/P_{J}^{\pm}
, where
\big\{P_{J}^{\pm}\,|\,J\subsetneq I\big\}
is a complete set of representatives of the conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups.

The incidence relation on the set of vertices is given by intersections of parabolic subgroups as follows. For
0\leq r\leq\ell-1
the
r+1
vertices
P_{[i_{1}]}^{\pm},\dots,P_{[i_{r+1}]}^{\pm}
span an
r
-simplex if and only if the intersection

P_{[i_{1}]}^{\pm}\cap\dots\cap P_{[i_{r+1}]}^{\pm}=P_{[i_{1},\dots,i_{r+1}]}^{\pm}
is a parabolic subgroup, so
\mathcal{B}
is a simplicial complex of dimension
\dim(\mathcal{B})=\ell-1
.
For non-finite type Kac–Moody groups such as the hyperbolic type we consider,
B^{+}
and
B^{-}
are not conjugate in
G
, so the intersection of a positive parabolic subgroup with a negative parabolic subgroup never contains a Borel subgroup.
Hence, from the twin
BN
-pair
\big(B^{+},B^{-},N\big)
we get two buildings
\big(\mathcal{B}^{+},\delta^{+}\big)
and
(\mathcal{B}^{-},\delta^{-})
, each equipped with a
W
-valued distance function,
\delta^{\pm}
,
each of type
(W,S)
, and a codistance function
\delta^{*}
, yielding a twin building
\mathcal{B}=\big(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-},\delta^{*}\big)
. In
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
the
(\ell-1)
-simplex
P_{[1,\dots,{\ell}]}^{\pm}=P_{[I]}^{\pm}=P_{\varnothing}^{\pm}=B^{\pm}
is called the
fundamental chamber
of
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
. Each fundamental chamber has boundary consisting of the simplices
\Delta_{J}^{\pm}=P_{J}^{\pm}
, and has closure

\Delta^{\pm}=\bigcup_{J\subsetneq I}P_{J}^{\pm}.

We also choose a
fundamental apartment

A^{\pm}_{\rm fund}
in
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
to be the one whose chambers are

\big(NB^{\pm}\big)/B^{\pm}=\big\{wB^{\pm}\,|\,w\in W\big\}.

Note that
\delta^{\pm}\big(w_{1}B^{\pm},w_{2}B^{\pm}\big)=w_{1}^{-1}w_{2}
and
\delta^{*}\big(w_{1}B^{\pm},w_{2}B^{\mp}\big)=w_{1}^{-1}w_{2}
, so that
\big(A^{+}_{\rm fund},A^{-}_{\rm fund}\big)
is a twin apartment.

Using the property that the simplices in each building are in bijection with the union of the coset spaces

\bigcup_{J\subsetneq I}G/P_{J}^{\pm}
, we describe the buildings
\mathcal{B}^{+}
and
\mathcal{B}^{-}
associated to a twin
BN
-pair,
\big(B^{+},B^{-},N\big)
for a Kac–Moody group
G
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\pm}:=\big(G/B^{\pm}\times\Delta^{\pm}\big)/{\sim}.

(2.3)

The equivalence relation
\sim
is defined by
\big(fB^{\pm},\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)\sim\big(gB^{\pm},\Delta_{J^{\prime}}^{\pm}\big)
in
\big(G/B^{\pm},\Delta^{\pm}\big)
if and only if
\Delta_{J}^{\pm}=\Delta_{J^{\prime}}^{\pm}
(so
J=J^{\prime}
) and
f^{-1}gP_{J}^{\pm}\subset P_{J}^{\pm}
.

Hence on the chambers
\Delta_{\varnothing}^{\pm}
, the equivalence relation
\sim
is trivial, while on simplices in the boundary it is nontrivial.

Let
\phi\colon G\rightarrow G
be an involution centralizing the Weyl group and such that
\phi\big(B^{\pm}\big)=B^{\mp}
. Then
\phi
induces a twin building involution as follows (for details see
[
15
,
33
]
)

\displaystyle\phi\big(gB^{\pm},\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)=\big(\phi(g)B^{\mp},\Delta_{J}^{\mp}\big).

(2.4)

One can give a
geometric realization
of a building as follows. Let
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{\ell}\}
denote the standard orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
. Each
r
-simplex is identified with a copy of the standard simplex

\Delta^{r}:=\left\{x=\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}a_{i}e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+1}\,\big|\,0\leq a_{i}\leq 1,\,\sum_{i=1}^{r+1}a_{i}=1\right\}

which inherits the topology from
\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
. Appropriate identifications must be made among the copies of these standard simplices in order to reflect the incidence structure among the simplices in the building. For details see
[
1
]
.

For the buildings associated with the hyperbolic Kac–Moody groups we wish to study, the geometric realization of apartments in the buildings
\mathcal{B}^{+}
and
\mathcal{B}^{-}
can be chosen to be isometric to hyperbolic spaces tessellated by the action of the hyperbolic Weyl group

. In the case when the Cartan matrix is strictly hyperbolic, that tessellation is by compact simplices, but otherwise these simplices have ideal vertices stabilized by affine type subgroups of
W
.

2.4
Compact real forms of Kac–Moody algebras and groups

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(A)
be a complex Kac–Moody algebra and let
\mathfrak{h}
be the standard Cartan subalgebra. The
Cartan involution

{\omega}_{0}\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}

is the automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
determined by
{\omega}_{0}(e_{i})=-f_{i}
,
\omega_{0}(f_{i})=-e_{i}
and
{\omega}_{0}(h_{i})=-h_{i}
. Composing
{\omega}_{0}
with complex conjugation, we obtain a conjugate linear involution
\omega
, called the
Cartan–Chevalley involution
. Then
\mathfrak{k}=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\,|\,\omega(x)=x\}
is a Lie algebra over
\mathbb{R}
called the
compact real form
of
\mathfrak{g}

[
44
, p. 243]
.

Note that
\omega_{0}
and
\omega
both centralize the Weyl group, so they each induce twin building involutions via formula (
2.4
).

We may give generators for the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
as follows.
For each
j=1,\dots,\ell
, let
\mathfrak{g}_{j}=\mathfrak{sl}_{2}^{j}
be the Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
isomorphic to
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
with basis
\{e_{j},f_{j},h_{j}\}
, so that

\mathfrak{g}
is generated by the subalgebras
\mathfrak{g}_{j}
and
\omega(\mathfrak{g}_{j})=\mathfrak{g}_{j}
. Then, using the notation
{\bf i}=\sqrt{-1}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
the real Lie algebra of fixed points of
\omega
on
\mathfrak{g}_{j}
,
\mathfrak{k}_{j}=\mathfrak{su}_{2}^{j}
has basis

x_{j}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{j}-f_{j}),\qquad y_{j}=\frac{{\bf i}}{2}(e_{j}+f_{j}),\qquad z_{j}=\frac{{\bf i}}{2}(h_{j})

with brackets
[x_{j},y_{j}]=z_{j}
,
[y_{j},z_{j}]=x_{j}
,
[z_{j},x_{j}]=y_{j}
, and the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
is generated by all of the subalgebras

\mathfrak{k}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,\ell
(see
[
4
, Proposition 1]
). A Cartan subalgebra in the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
is an abelian subalgebra whose complexification is a Cartan subalgebra in
\mathfrak{g}
. The standard Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{h}\cap\mathfrak{k}
in
\mathfrak{k}
has real basis
\{z_{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\ell\}
.

Let
G=G_{\mathbb{C}}(A)
be the complex adjoint Kac–Moody group associated to
\mathfrak{g}
. The involution
\omega
of
\mathfrak{g}
lifts to a unique involution of
G
, also denoted by
\omega
, exchanging positive and negative real root groups since
\omega(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
. We have the following more general lemma about the action of any Lie algebra automorphism, which we will apply to
\omega
as well as to
\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}\in\widetilde{W}^{\rm ad}
.

Lemma 2.9
.

For any
\alpha\in{\Phi}^{\rm re}
,
e_{\alpha}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and any
\phi\in{\rm Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
, we have

\phi\circ\exp({\rm ad}_{e_{\alpha}})\circ\phi^{-1}=\exp({\rm ad}_{\phi(e_{\alpha})})
so that, in particular,

\omega U^{\rm ad}_{\alpha}\omega^{-1}=U^{\rm ad}_{-\alpha}
and
\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}U^{\rm ad}_{\alpha_{i}}\big(\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}\big)^{-1}=U^{\rm ad}_{-\alpha_{i}}
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
.

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, since
\phi
is a Lie algebra automorphism, we have

\phi(\exp({\rm ad}_{e_{\alpha}})x)=\phi\left(\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{k!}({\rm ad}_{e_{\alpha}})^{k}(x)\right)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{k!}({\rm ad}_{\phi(e_{\alpha})})^{k}(\phi(x))=\exp({\rm ad}_{\phi(e_{\alpha})})(\phi(x)).

The formula with
\phi=\omega
gives
\omega U^{\rm ad}_{\alpha}\omega^{-1}=U^{\rm ad}_{-\alpha}
since
\omega(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
, and with
\phi=\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}
gives
\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}U^{\rm ad}_{\alpha_{i}}\big(\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}\big)^{-1}=U^{\rm ad}_{-\alpha_{i}}
since
\widetilde{w}^{\rm ad}_{i}(e_{i})=-f_{i}
.
∎

We set

. Then

is called the

or

of

. We will use the latter by analogy with the finite-dimensional case, even though

for each

[
6
,
37
,
56
]
.

For each

and

we write a generator of

as

\exp({\rm ad}_{vh_{j}})=\exp({\rm ad}_{ah_{j}})\exp({\rm ad}_{b{\bf i}h_{j}})=\exp({\rm ad}_{ah_{j}})\exp({\rm ad}_{b2z_{j}}).

This gives the decomposition
T_{\mathbb{C}}^{\rm ad}=T_{\mathbb{R}}^{\rm ad}\,T
where

(2.5)

is the split real torus and

is the compact real torus.

It is clear from the two expressions for

that
\widetilde{W}^{\rm ad}\leq K
, but it is not so obvious that these operators can be expressed as
a single exponential

. This is a special case of the formula

for any
integrable representation

, where the inner

\mathbb{R}
, proven in Theorem
A.1

and first found in certain important cases by
[

]
.
See also
[

,

]
.

,. See also

3
Tits cone and lightcone of hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras

of compact type

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{C}}(A)
be a hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra and let
\mathfrak{h}
be its standard Cartan subalgebra.
The split real form
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}(A)
of
\mathfrak{g}
contains the
\mathbb{R}
-subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}=\mathfrak{h}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
which is just the
\mathbb{R}
-span of
\{h_{i}\,|\,i\in I\}
. We call
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
the standard Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
.

The Weyl group
W
has been defined above as the group of orthogonal transformations of
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
generated by the simple reflections. The isomorphism between
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
and
\mathfrak{h}
gives the action of
W
on
\mathfrak{h}
, where the formula for simple reflections is just
w_{i}(h_{j})=h_{j}-\alpha_{j}(h_{i})h_{i}
. This same formula restricted to
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
gives the action of
W
and it also determines the action of
W
on
\mathfrak{t}
by
w_{i}(z_{j})=z_{j}-\alpha_{j}(h_{i})z_{i}
. These operators are orthogonal with respect to the restriction of the bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
to
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
and to
\mathfrak{t}
, and therefore
W
preserves each surface of constant
square length,
(\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}})_{r}=\{x\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,(x,x)=r\}
and
\mathfrak{t}_{r}=\{x\in\mathfrak{t}\,|\,(x,x)=r\}
.

Since we are assuming that the Cartan matrix
A
is hyperbolic, the form
(\cdot,\cdot)
is Lorentzian on
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
and on
\mathfrak{t}
. The surface where
r=0
is called the
nullcone
, each surface where
r<0
is called
timelike
, and each surface where
r>0
is
called
spacelike
. The set of all timelike points has two connected components, one called
forward
and denoted
TL^{+}
and the other called
backward
and denoted
TL^{-}
. We have
TL^{-}=-TL^{+}
.

Each of these components is preserved by the linear action of
W
, which acts consistently on rays,
{\rm Ray}_{x}=\{rx\,|\,0<r\in\mathbb{R}\}
since

w(rx)=rw(x)
. A fundamental domain for the action of
W
on each of the timelike components is defined by

\mathcal{C}^{\pm}=\{x\in TL^{\pm}\,|\,\alpha_{i}(x)\geq 0,\,1\leq i\leq\ell\}.

The union

X^{\pm}=\bigcup_{w\in W}w\big(\mathcal{C}^{\pm}\big)

is called the
positive
(
respectively negative
)
Tits cone
and
X=X^{+}\cup X^{-}
is called the
Tits cone
.

Clearly, we have
X^{-}=-X^{+}
. For
\mathfrak{g}
hyperbolic,
X^{\pm}\supseteq TL^{\pm}
, since it is possible that
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
, and therefore
X^{\pm}
, contains rays on the nullcone. This happens for the rank
3
hyperbolic Cartan matrices

\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&0\\
-2&2&-1\\
0&-1&2\end{bmatrix}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\begin{bmatrix}2&-2&-2\\
-2&2&-2\\
-2&-2&2\end{bmatrix}

corresponding to the hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra
\mathcal{F}

[
18
]
whose Weyl group is the hyperbolic triangle group

T(2,3,\infty)
, and the “ideal” hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra
\mathcal{I}
whose Weyl group is the hyperbolic triangle group

T(\infty,\infty,\infty)
, respectively.
Another such an example is
E_{10}
because it contains the affine Kac–Moody algebra
E_{9}=E_{8}^{(1)}
.
For
A
strictly hyperbolic, that is, whose principal minors are of finite type, we have
X^{\pm}=TL^{\pm}
.

We have the following description of the closure of the Tits cone (see
[
35
, equation (5.10.2)]
)

\overline{X}=\overline{X^{+}}\cup\overline{X^{-}}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,(h,h)\leq 0\}.

We introduce the notations

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,(h,h)\leq 0\},\qquad\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}}^{0}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,(h,h)<0\}\qquad\hbox{and}

\displaystyle\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\,|\,(h,h)=0\}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
,
\mathfrak{k}
its compact real form and
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
its split real form. Let
\mathfrak{h}
,
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mathfrak{t}
be the standard Cartan subalgebras of
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
,
\mathfrak{k}
, respectively. Then
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{\ell-1,1}
has signature
(\ell-1,1)
and

\mathfrak{t}={{\bf i}}\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}=\{{{\bf i}}x\,|\,x\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\}
, where
{{\bf i}}^{2}=-1
. The signature of
\mathfrak{t}\cong\mathbb{R}^{1,\ell-1}
is
(1,\ell-1)
.

Proof.

The definition of a hyperbolic Cartan matrix gives that the bilinear form on the split real form of the Cartan subalgebra (the real span of the generators
h_{i}
) is Lorentzian, so the statements in the proposition are clear.
∎

We use a sign convention on
\mathfrak{k}
adopted from the theory of finite-dimensional Riemannian symmetric spaces
[
32
]
and set

\displaystyle(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}=-(\cdot,\cdot)|_{\mathfrak{k}}.

(3.1)

Note that in the affine case
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}
, this sign convention naturally occurs in the loop group realizations
[
19
,
51
]
. With respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}
, the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
has Lorentzian signature
(\ell-1,1)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

The invariant symmetric bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}
on
\mathfrak{k}
is Lorentzian with signature
(\infty,1)
.

Proof.

As a vector space, a complex hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
has a basis consisting of the Cartan subalgebra
generators,
h_{i},i=1,\dots,\ell
, and certain multibrackets
[e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}]
and
[f_{i_{1}},f_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{n}}]
.
The action of
\omega
on such multibrackets is simply
\omega([e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}])=(-1)^{n}[f_{i_{1}},f_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{n}}]

and
\omega(h_{i})=-h_{i}
so an
\mathbb{R}
-basis for the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
consists of the compact Cartan basis elements

z_{i}=\frac{{\bf i}}{2}h_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,\ell
, and the elements obtained from basis multibrackets above

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}([e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}]+[f_{i_{1}},f_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{n}}])\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf i}}{2}([e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}]-[f_{i_{1}},f_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{n}}])\qquad\text{for $n$ even},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}([e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}]-[f_{i_{1}},f_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{n}}])\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf i}}{2}([e_{i_{1}},e_{i_{2}},\dots,e_{i_{n}}]+[f_{i_{1}},f_{i_{2}},\dots,f_{i_{n}}])\qquad\text{for $n$ odd}.

In particular, for
n=1
these include the elements
x_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(e_{i}-f_{i})
and

y_{i}=\frac{{\bf i}}{2}(e_{i}+f_{i})
.

Following our sign convention, the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
spanned by the elements
z_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,\ell
, has signature
(\ell-1,1)
. Using our sign convention and the characterization of the
{\rm ad}
-invariant scalar product in
[
35
, equation (2.2.1)]
, applied to the basis vectors
y_{i}
and
z_{i}
, we find that the bilinear form on each subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}_{i}=\mathfrak{su}_{2}^{i}
is positive definite.

Furthermore the
{\rm ad}
-invariant bilinear form has the following properties on root spaces (see
[
35
, Sections 2.1 and 2.2]
). The root spaces
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
, which are interchanged by
\omega
, have dual bases

\big\{e_{\alpha}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\big\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big\{f_{\alpha}^{j}=e_{-\alpha}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\big\},\qquad\text{respectively}

such that

\big(f_{\alpha}^{j},e_{\beta}^{m}\big)=\delta_{j,m}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}.

As a consequence, for positive roots
\alpha
, the basis elements of
\mathfrak{k}
,
x_{\alpha}^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}-f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)
and

y_{\alpha}^{j}=\frac{{{\bf i}}}{2}\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}+f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)
satisfy

\big(x_{\alpha}^{j},x_{\beta}^{m}\big)=-\frac{1}{2}\delta_{j,m}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}=\big(y_{\alpha}^{j},y_{\beta}^{m}\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big(x_{\alpha}^{j},y_{\beta}^{m}\big)=0.

Hence it follows from (
3.1
) that

\big(x_{\alpha}^{j},x_{\beta}^{m}\big)_{\mathfrak{k}}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{j,m}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}=\big(y_{\alpha}^{j},y_{\beta}^{m}\big)_{\mathfrak{k}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big(x_{\alpha}^{j},y_{\beta}^{m}\big)_{\mathfrak{k}}=0.

The Cartan subalgebra has signature
(\ell-1,1)
and for each positive root
\alpha
the subspaces

\big\langle x_{\alpha}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\big\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big\langle y_{\alpha}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\big\rangle

in
\mathfrak{k}
each have positive signature and are orthogonal to each other. For different
\alpha
these spaces are orthogonal to each other and to the Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
. Thus
\mathfrak{k}
has Lorentzian signature
(\infty,1)
.
∎

.

Note that the bilinear form

on

We recall that the

-invariant bilinear form on

extends

-bilinearly to the complexification

\displaystyle({{\bf i}}x,{{\bf i}}x)=-(x,x)=(x,x)_{\mathfrak{k}}

We introduce notations for certain subsets in

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{k}}=\{x\in\mathfrak{k}\,|\,(x,x)_{\mathfrak{k}}\leq 0\},\qquad

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{k}}\cap\mathfrak{t}=\varphi(\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}}),

The ones inside

.
Furthermore, we have

which correspond to those subsets defined earlier in

.

.

4.1
The adjoint action of
\boldsymbol{K}
on
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{k}}

Recall that
K
denotes the compact form of the complex adjoint Kac–Moody group
G
. We set

\mathfrak{H}=\bigcup_{k\in K}k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1}=\big\{kxk^{-1}\in\mathfrak{k}\,|\,k\in K,x\in\mathfrak{t}\big\}.

By Proposition
2.10
all Cartan subalgebras of
\mathfrak{k}
are
K
-conjugate, thus the definition of
\mathfrak{H}
is independent of the choice of
\mathfrak{t}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{t}
be any Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{k}
and let
z={\bf i}h\in\mathfrak{t}
for
h\in\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfying
\alpha(h)\neq 0
for all
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
.
Then the subspace
[\mathfrak{k},z]
of
\mathfrak{k}
has basis

\bigcup_{\alpha\in\Phi^{+}}\big\{x_{\alpha}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\big\}\cup\big\{y_{\alpha}^{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})\big\}.

Proof.

For
\alpha\in\Phi^{+}
and
1\leq j\leq\dim(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})
we have

\displaystyle\big[x_{\alpha}^{j},z\big]=-\big[{\bf i}h,\textrm{\scriptsize$\frac{1}{2}$\normalsize}(e_{\alpha}^{j}-f_{\alpha}^{j})\big]=-\textrm{\scriptsize$\frac{{\bf i}}{2}$\normalsize}\big[h,\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}-f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)\big]=-\textrm{\scriptsize$\frac{{\bf i}}{2}$\normalsize}\alpha(h)\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}+f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)=-\alpha(h)y_{\alpha}^{j},

\displaystyle\big[y_{\alpha}^{j},z\big]=-\big[{\bf i}h,\textrm{\scriptsize$\frac{{\bf i}}{2}$\normalsize}\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}+f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)\big]=\textrm{\scriptsize$\frac{1}{2}$\normalsize}\big[h,\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}+f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)\big]=\textrm{\scriptsize$\frac{1}{2}$\normalsize}\alpha(h)\big(e_{\alpha}^{j}-f_{\alpha}^{j}\big)=\alpha(h)x_{\alpha}^{j}

and
[\mathfrak{t},z]=0
, so no basis vectors of
\mathfrak{t}
are in
[\mathfrak{k},z]
.
∎

By analogy with the finite-dimensional and affine cases, the following proposition shows that in the hyperbolic case, the
K
-orbits,
K\cdot z=\big\{kzk^{-1}\in\mathfrak{k}\,|\,k\in K\big\}
for each
z\in\mathfrak{t}
, intersect each Cartan subalgebra orthogonally. For
z\in\mathfrak{t}
let
T_{z}(K\cdot z)
be the
tangent space
of the submanifold
K\cdot z
at the point
z
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathfrak{t}
be any Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{k}
.
The orbits
K\cdot z
for
z\in\mathfrak{t}
are orthogonal to
\mathfrak{t}
with respect to the
{\rm ad}
-invariant bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}
, that is,
(T_{z}(K\cdot z),\mathfrak{t})_{\mathfrak{k}}=0
.

Proof.

For
z\in\mathfrak{t}
we have

T_{z}(K\cdot z)=[\mathfrak{k},z].

By definition, for
w\in T_{z}(K\cdot z)
, there is some
y\in\mathfrak{k}
such that
w=[y,z]
. Since the form
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}
is
{\rm ad}
-invariant, for
z^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{t}
we obtain

\displaystyle(w,z^{\prime})_{\mathfrak{k}}=([y,z],z^{\prime})_{\mathfrak{k}}=(y,[z,z^{\prime}])_{\mathfrak{k}}=(y,0)_{\mathfrak{k}}=0.

∎

Definition 4.3

(surface notation)
.

Let
X\subset V
be a subset of a real vector space
V
equipped with a bilinear form
(\cdot,\cdot)
. For any real number
r
we define a ‘sphere’ of radius
r
in
X
by

X_{r}:=\{x\in X\,|\,(x,x)=r\}.

If
(\cdot,\cdot)
is Lorentzian and
r<0
then
X_{r}
is a two-sheeted hyperboloid of constant curvature
\kappa=-\frac{1}{r^{2}}
whose connected components we call
X_{r}^{+}
and
X_{r}^{-}
.
In particular we will use
\mathfrak{t}_{r}\subset\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}}^{0}\subset\mathfrak{t}
,
\mathfrak{k}_{r}\subset\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{k}}^{0}\subset\mathfrak{k}
and
\mathfrak{H}_{r}\subset\mathfrak{H}
and note that
\mathfrak{k}_{0}=\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{k}}
and
\mathfrak{t}_{0}=\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}}
. For
\ell>2
we find
\mathfrak{t}_{-1}^{+}\cong\mathfrak{t}_{-1}^{-}\cong\mathbb{H}^{\ell-1}
,
(\ell-1)
-dimensional hyperbolic space.

Definition 4.4

(lightlike closure)
.

Let
V
be a possibly infinite-dimensional real vector space equipped with a Lorentzian form
(\cdot,\cdot)
and let

\partial\mathcal{L}_{V}=\{x\in V\,|\,(x,x)=0\}
denote its nullcone. The
boundary at infinity

B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{V})
of the nullcone consists of all rays
{\rm Ray}_{x}
for
0\neq x\in\partial\mathcal{L}_{V}
. If
\dim(V)=2
it consists of four points. If
\dim(V)>2
it consists of two components, corresponding to the future timelike boundary
B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{V})^{+}
and the past timelike boundary
B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{V})^{-}
, each of which can be identified with a sphere of dimension
\ell-2
We define the
lightlike closure
of
V
by

\widehat{V}=V\cup B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{V}).

For each
r<0
, define
B^{\infty}(V_{r}^{\pm})
, the boundary at infinity of
V_{r}^{\pm}
, to be equivalence classes of geodesic rays, where two rays are equivalent if the distance between them is finite at all points. For details, see
[
16
]
. Note that for each
r<0
,

B^{\infty}(V_{r}^{\pm})
can be identified with
B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{V})^{\pm}
. Using this observation we define the lightlike closure of

V_{r}^{\pm}
to be

\widehat{V}_{r}^{\pm}=V_{r}^{\pm}\cup B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{V})^{\pm}.

(4.1)

Since the bilinear form
(\cdot,\,\cdot)_{\mathfrak{k}}
is
{\rm ad}
-invariant, the adjoint action of
K
on
\mathfrak{k}
preserves the surfaces
\mathfrak{H}_{r}\subset\mathfrak{H}
defined in Definition
4.3
.
For
k\in K
we have
k\cdot\mathfrak{t}_{r}=(k\cdot\mathfrak{t})_{r}
. There is an induced action of
K
on
B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}})
and for each
r<0
on

B^{\infty}(\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}_{r}})^{\pm}
, as well as on
{\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}}
and on
{\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}}_{r}^{\pm}
.

Hence, for each
r<0
, the adjoint action of
K
on
\mathfrak{k}
induces a well–defined action on the surface:

K\colon\ \mathfrak{H}_{r}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{H}_{r},\qquad k\cdot x={\rm Ad}_{k}(x)=kxk^{-1}

as well as on the lightlike closure
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}
.

Since all Cartan subalgebras are conjugate, we can define the following surjective map

\psi\colon\ K\times\mathfrak{t}_{r}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{H}_{r},\qquad\psi(k,x)=kxk^{-1}.

Note that the choice of the standard Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}
in the definition of
\psi
does not restrict the generality.

Let
T=\exp(\mathfrak{t})=T\big(G^{\rm ad}\big)\cap K
be the torus associated to
\mathfrak{t}
and let

N=N_{K}(T)=N_{G^{\rm ad}}\big(T\big(G^{\rm ad}\big)\big)\cap K

be the normalizer of
T
in
K
. For
k\in K
,
t\in T
and

we have:

{\rm Ad}_{kt}(u)=ktut^{-1}k^{-1}=kuk^{-1}={\rm Ad}_{k}(u).

Thus the adjoint action on
\mathfrak{t}
is
T
-invariant and factors to the quotient space
K/T
yielding a surjective map

\psi\colon\ K/T\times\mathfrak{t}_{r}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{H}_{r},\qquad\psi(kT,u)=kuk^{-1}.

For any Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}=k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1}
of
\mathfrak{k}

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}}
coincides with the closure of the Tits cone of
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}

. Hence

, for any fixed

, which includes the boundary

\partial\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}}
. We distinguish two cases:

1.

When every proper Cartan submatrix of

is of finite type (i.e.,

is
(\ell-1)
-spherical or

) then we have

and there is a bijection between each surface

for

and the set of rays in

. In this case

2.

When

contain rays that accumulate at rays on the lightcone

accumulate at points on the boundary of the

(\ell-1)
-dimensional hyperbolic space

Since

Restriction of the second coordinate of the domain of

4.2
The local structure of the adjoint action

In this section we describe the geometry ‘close’ to a fixed Cartan subalgebra in the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
. We show that for
i\in\{1,\dots,{\ell}\}
, the adjoint action of the fundamental
{\rm SU}(2)_{i}
-subgroup of the compact real form
K
, is a rotation of the standard Cartan subalgebra around the hyperplane

L_{\mathfrak{t},i}:=\{z\in\mathfrak{t}\,|\,\alpha_{i}(z)=0\}

fixed by the generator
w_{i}
of
W
.

The standard Cartan subalgebra is given by
\mathfrak{t}=\mathbb{R}z_{1}+\dots+\mathbb{R}z_{\ell}
.
For each
1\leq i\leq\ell
,
{\rm SU}(2)_{i}+\mathfrak{t}={\rm SU}(2)_{i}\oplus L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
since
\alpha_{i}(z_{i})={\bf i}
.
For any
s,t,u\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})\in{\rm SU}(2)_{i}\leq K
and for
z\in\mathfrak{t}
we have

[sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i},z]=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)(tx_{i}-sy_{i})=0
when
\alpha_{i}(z)=0
which means
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})(z)=z
when

\alpha_{i}(z)=0
. While
({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})z=({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z
for any
z\in\mathfrak{t}
, we see that

\displaystyle({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})^{2}z=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})(tx_{i}-sy_{i})=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\big(usx_{i}+uty_{i}-\big(s^{2}+t^{2}\big)z_{i}\big)

differs from
({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{2}z
, and higher powers have increasingly complicated expressions involving
u
, so that
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})z
certainly depends on
u
. Nevertheless, the factor
\alpha_{i}(z)
tells us that
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
is the fixed point set in
\mathfrak{t}
of
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})
, so the Cartan subalgebra
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})\mathfrak{t}
is
spanned by
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
and the vector
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}+uz_{i}})z_{i}
. It appears that this is a three-parameter family of Cartan subalgebras, each containing
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
, but, in fact, we can see that the entire family is obtained from the two-parameter family with
u=0
.

While these operators are defined on the entire Kac–Moody algebra,
\mathfrak{g}
, we are really only interested in the orbit of
z_{i}
inside
\mathfrak{su}(2)_{i}
under the action of
{\rm SU}(2)_{i}
, so we can do this calculation with
2\times 2
matrices. An arbitrary matrix in
{\rm SU}(2)
is

A=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
-\bar{\beta}&\bar{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}
where
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
\det(A)=1
. With
z=\frac{{\bf i}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}
we have

AzA^{-1}=\frac{{\bf i}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}(\alpha\bar{\alpha}-\beta\bar{\beta})&-2\alpha\beta\\
-2\bar{\alpha}\bar{\beta}&-(\alpha\bar{\alpha}-\beta\bar{\beta})\end{bmatrix}.

So the stabilizer of
z
consists of those
A
such that
\alpha\beta=0
and
\alpha\bar{\alpha}-\beta\bar{\beta}=1
, which implies that
\beta=0
and
the stabilizer of
z
is the diagonal
{\rm U}(1)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}\alpha&0\\
0&\bar{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}\,|\,\alpha\bar{\alpha}=1\right\}
. The orbit
{\rm SU}(2)\cdot z
is in
bijection with
{\rm SU}(2)/{\rm U}(1)
which is well-known to be the
2
-sphere. We will show below that the two-parameter family

\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z_{i}
gives a
2
-sphere, so it is enough to find all the Cartan subalgebras containing
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
.

For each
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
is either another Cartan subalgebra such that
\mathfrak{t}\cap\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)=L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
or else

\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
. With
v=\big(\frac{s}{2}+\frac{t{\bf i}}{2}\big)\in{\mathbb{C}}
we have
sx_{i}+ty_{i}=ve_{i}-\overline{v}f_{i}
, so

\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})=\exp({\rm ad}_{ve_{i}-\overline{v}f_{i}})\in{\rm SU}(2)_{i}

gives another parameterization of
\{\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)\,|\,s,t\in\mathbb{R}\}=\{\exp({\rm ad}_{ve_{i}-\overline{v}f_{i}})\mathfrak{t}\,|\,v\in{\mathbb{C}}\}
. For fixed
i
, distinct choices of
(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}
can give the same Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
, so we would like to know exactly how to
parameterize the distinct Cartan subalgebras in this set. The first part of the following theorem shows that for each
1\leq i\leq\ell
the family of distinct Cartan subalgebras obtained this way can be parameterized by a 2-sphere with antipodes identified, the real projective space
P_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. A more careful interpretation of this calculation will later give us information related to the set of chambers in the building which share a common panel.

Theorem 4.5
.

For any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
such that
0<r^{2}=s^{2}+t^{2}

and for any
z\in\mathfrak{t}
, we have

1)

\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z=z-{{\bf i}}\alpha_{i}(z)(\cos(r)-1)z_{i}-{{\bf i}}\alpha_{i}(z)\frac{\sin(r)}{r}(tx_{i}-sy_{i})
,

2)

\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})x_{i}=x_{i}-\frac{t\sin(r)}{r}z_{i}-\frac{t}{r^{2}}(\cos(r)-1)(tx_{i}-sy_{i})
,

3)

\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})y_{i}=y_{i}+\frac{s\sin(r)}{r}z_{i}-\frac{s}{r^{2}}(\cos(r)-1)(tx_{i}-sy_{i})
.

Proof.

We prove only the first relation since the others are analogous. We have

\displaystyle({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{1}z=[sx_{i}+ty_{i},z]=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)(tx_{i}-sy_{i}),

\displaystyle({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{2}z=[sx_{i}+ty_{i},-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)(tx_{i}-sy_{i})]={\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\big(s^{2}+t^{2}\big)z_{i},

\displaystyle({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{3}z=\big[sx_{i}+ty_{i},{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\big(s^{2}+t^{2}\big)z_{i}\big]={\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\big(s^{2}+t^{2}\big)(tx_{i}-sy_{i}),

\displaystyle({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{4}z=\big[sx_{i}+ty_{i},{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\big(s^{2}+t^{2}\big)(tx_{i}-sy_{i})\big]=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\big(s^{2}+t^{2}\big)^{2}z_{i}.

In the third step we have used that
\alpha_{i}(z_{i})={\bf i}
.

Using
r^{2}=s^{2}+t^{2}\neq 0
, it is clear that for
n\geq 1
we have

({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{2n}z=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)(-1)^{n}\big(r^{2}\big)^{n}z_{i}

and for
n\geq 0
we have

({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})^{2n+1}z=-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)(-1)^{n}\big(r^{2}\big)^{n}(tx_{i}-sy_{i})

so we get

\displaystyle\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z

\displaystyle=z-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}r^{2n}}{(2n)!}z_{i}-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}r^{2n}}{(2n+1)!}(tx_{i}-sy_{i})

\displaystyle=z-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)(\cos(r)-1)z_{i}-{\bf i}\alpha_{i}(z)\frac{\sin(r)}{r}(tx_{i}-sy_{i}).

∎

Corollary 4.6
.

For each
1\leq i\leq\ell
, the family of distinct Cartan subalgebras in
\{\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)\,|\,s,t\in\mathbb{R}\}
, including
\mathfrak{t}
, is parametrized by the unit hemisphere

\big\{(\sin(r)\sin(\psi),-\sin(r)\cos(\psi),\cos(r))\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,|\,0\leq r<\pi,\,0\leq\psi<\pi\big\},

where
r=\sqrt{s^{2}+t^{2}}\geq 0
and
\psi
is defined when
r>0
by

\sin(\psi)=\frac{t}{r}
and
\cos(\psi)=\frac{s}{r}
. Also,
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{r-\pi}{r}(s,t)
corresponds to the antipodal
point determined by
(\pi-r,\psi+\pi)
and for any
z\in\mathfrak{t}
, we have

\exp({\rm ad}_{s^{\prime}x_{i}+t^{\prime}y_{i}})z=\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})w_{i}(z).

Proof.

The Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
is spanned by
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
(for any choice of
(s,t)
) and the vector

\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z_{i}=\sin(r)\sin(\psi)x_{i}-\sin(r)\cos(\psi)y_{i}+\cos(r)z_{i}\in\mathfrak{su}_{2}^{i}

from Theorem
4.5
(1) and the fact that
\alpha_{i}(z_{i})={\bf i}
.
With respect to basis
\{x_{i},y_{i},z_{i}\}
of
\mathfrak{su}_{2}^{i}
, the coordinates of this vector are

(\sin(r)\sin(\psi),-\sin(r)\cos(\psi),\cos(r))

so the vector is on a unit sphere. Each point on one hemisphere is uniquely determined by the choices

0\leq r<\pi
and
0\leq\psi<\pi
, and no two vectors of the above form are co-linear, so all of the corresponding subspaces
\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
are distinct. The point on the sphere determined by
(r,\psi)
corresponding to
(s,t)
has antipodal point determined by
(\pi-r,\psi+\pi)
corresponding to
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\frac{r-\pi}{r}(s,t)
. The corresponding subspaces are the same since

\exp({\rm ad}_{s^{\prime}x_{i}+t^{\prime}y_{i}})z_{i}=-\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z_{i}
.

We have
(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})
determined by
sin(\psi+\pi)=\frac{t^{\prime}}{\pi-r}
and
\cos(\psi+\pi)=\frac{s^{\prime}}{\pi-r}
so
\frac{-t}{r}=-\sin(\psi)=\frac{t^{\prime}}{\pi-r}
and
\frac{-s}{r}=-\cos(\psi)=\frac{s^{\prime}}{\pi-r}
, which gives
t^{\prime}=\frac{r-\pi}{r}t
and
s^{\prime}=\frac{r-\pi}{r}s
. For
\alpha_{i}(z)=0
, that is, for
z\in L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
, we have
w_{i}(z)=z
and so
\exp({\rm ad}_{s^{\prime}x_{i}+t^{\prime}y_{i}})z=z=\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})w_{i}(z)
. For
z=z_{i}
we have
w_{i}(z_{i})=-z_{i}
, and we have shown above that
\exp({\rm ad}_{s^{\prime}x_{i}+t^{\prime}y_{i}})z_{i}=-\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})z_{i}
. Since these operators are linear, that proves the claimed formula for any
z\in\mathfrak{t}
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

We may find it useful later to associate
w\in\hat{\mathbb{C}}
with a point
(x,y,z)
on the unit sphere, and to record the relationship between antipodal points on the sphere and their associated complex numbers. The standard formula for a stereographic projection from the north pole
(0,0,1)
to a point in the complex plane
z=0
is

(x,y,z)\to\frac{x}{1-z}+{\bf i}\frac{y}{1-z}=w

so for the antipodal point

(-x,-y,-z)\to\frac{-x}{1+z}+{\bf i}\frac{-y}{1+z}=w^{a}.

Since
x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=1
, we then have
w^{a}=-{\bar{w}}^{-1}
.

We wish to realize the twin apartments for a hyperbolic Kac–Moody group as a geometric object inside the lightcone of the compact real form of the Kac–Moody Lie algebra. It is not hard to see the Coxeter complex structure
in each side of the Tits cone of a standard compact real Cartan subalgebra. The tessellation of the forward and backward Tits cones by the action of the hyperbolic Weyl group provides a pair of opposite fundamental apartments,
(\Sigma^{+},\Sigma^{-})
, with tessellations
\Sigma^{\pm}=\bigcup\limits_{w\in W}w\big(\mathcal{C}^{\pm}\big)
where
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
is a fundamental chamber in
\Sigma^{\pm}
. A panel is a nonempty intersection of maximal dimension of two
w_{i}
-adjacent chambers, that is,
\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\pm}=w\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\pm}=ww_{i}\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
, so that
ww_{i}w^{-1}\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\pm}=\mathcal{C}_{2}^{\pm}
. It means that the panel is contained in the wall of all fixed points of the Weyl group reflection
ww_{i}w^{-1}
which is a
W
-conjugate of the simple reflection
w_{i}
. We may simplify the discussion by considering just the panels of a fundamental chamber,
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
, contained in the wall of fixed points of the simple reflection
w_{i}
. The hyperplane
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
intersects both sides of the Tits cone and determines a wall containing a panel of
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
in each side of the twin apartment. The operators
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})\in{\rm SU}_{2}^{i}
for any
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
fix
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
pointwise, but take
z_{i}
to a vector in
\mathfrak{su}_{2}^{i}
not in
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
, giving a family of compact Cartan
subalgebras,
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})\mathfrak{t}=\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
, each of which contains a Tits cone, and all of which share the common subspace
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
. Consider the distinct chambers in this family of apartments which share a common panel with the fundamental chamber in the fundamental apartment (in either side of the twin fundamental apartment). For now, let us only think about
\Sigma^{+}
, and its images under this family of operators. The fundamental chamber,
\mathcal{C}^{+}
, has a unique panel fixed by
w_{i}
, and that panel is contained in
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
. So the family of distinct chambers
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})\mathcal{C}^{+}
obtained as
s
and
t
vary, all contain that
w_{i}
-fixed panel. The same can be said of the chamber
w_{i}\mathcal{C}^{+}
in
\Sigma^{+}
, and we may wish to consider its orbit under this family of operators. But we know that with
(s,t)=(\pi,0)
, which corresponds to
(r,\psi)=(\pi,0)
, that operator restricted to
\mathfrak{t}

equals
w_{i}
, so we should be able to understand that orbit starting from either chamber.

The point of the first part of Corollary
4.6
is that for
(s,t)
corresponding to
(r,\psi)
giving distinct points on the hemisphere
0\leq r<\pi
,
0\leq\psi<\pi
, the real compact Cartan subalgebras
\mathfrak{t}^{i}(s,t)
are all distinct, so the chambers
\exp({\rm ad}_{sx_{i}+ty_{i}})\mathcal{C}^{+}
must all be distinct. But the second part of the corollary says that for

corresponding to the antipodal point determined by

,
and for any

in the apartment

Antipodal points give distinct chambers in the same apartment, and the set of all chambers sharing the common panel is parametrized by a real 2-sphere, exactly corresponding to the abstract building picture, where the answer is the Riemann sphere. \big(\mathfrak{t}^{\sin}_{r}\big)^{\pm}
and
\big(\mathfrak{t}^{\rm reg}_{r}\big)^{\pm}

We denote by

the set of connected components of

.
In each sheet one connected component,
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}=\mathcal{C}^{\pm}\cap\big(\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{\rm reg}\big)^{\pm}
, corresponds to the fundamental domain
\mathcal{C}^{\pm}
. For each sign
\pm

. We use the Weyl group action to index the elements of
{\rm Comp}_{r}^{\pm}
as follows: Let

denote the identity element. In each sheet
\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{\pm}
we index the
fundamental chamber
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}
with
1

\mathcal{C}_{r}^{-}=-\mathcal{C}_{r}^{+}
.
Then we index the connected component

by

w
yielding

Let
\overline{w\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}}
denote the closure of the component
w\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}
and let
\mathcal{U}^{\pm}
denote the union

\mathcal{U}^{\pm}=\bigcup_{w\in W}\overline{w\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}}

so

covers
\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{\pm}
. It may happen that the closure

includes points at infinity, that is, ideal points not on the surface

, but which correspond to rays in the null cone as discussed in Definition
4.4
. In that case, some vertices (
0
-simplices) will be in

.

For any subset
J\subsetneq S
recall that
W_{J}=\langle s\,|\,s\in J\rangle
, and define the intersection

{\rm Simp}_{J}^{\pm}=\bigcap_{w\in W_{J}}w\overline{\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}}\subset\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}_{r}^{\pm}.

We identify
{\rm Simp}_{J}^{\pm}
with a simplex of dimension
\ell-1-|J|
.

For example, when

,
{\rm Simp}_{J}^{\pm}=\overline{\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}}
is an
\ell-1
simplex, and when

,

is a

-simplex.

Theorem 5.1

(simplicial structure on
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}
)
.

For each
r<0
, with the notations above, we have a Coxeter complex on
\big(k\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}k^{-1}\big)_{r}^{\pm}
for each
k\in K
and their union over all

\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}=\big\{{\rm Simp}_{J}^{\pm}\,|\,J\subsetneq S\big\}
is a Coxeter complex
[
1
]
for
(W,S)
. That is,
\mathcal{S}^{\pm}
admits a
W
-action which is simply transitively on simplices of maximal dimension. Thus for any two simplices of maximal dimension, there is a chain of maximal-dimensional simplices such that two consecutive ones share a common face of codimension 1. Since our compact real Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{k}
has rank
\ell
, all of its
Cartan subalgebras,
k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1}
, for
k\in K
, have dimension
\ell
and each surface
\big(k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1}\big)_{r}
of radius

has dimension
\ell-1
. We have now defined a Coxeter complex on
\big(k\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}k^{-1}\big)_{r}^{\pm}
for each

.

The union of all these Coxeter complexes forms a simplicial complex in
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
as follows. We must only check that the Weyl group tessellations on different Cartan subalgebras fit together in a well-defined way. Cartan subalgebras intersect exactly in hyperplanes fixed by Weyl group elements (as in Section
4.2
), hence they intersect in singular elements. Thus each simplex of
maximal dimension lies in exactly one Cartan subalgebra. Thus the simplicial complexes in different Cartan subalgebras fit together, leading to a simplicial complex in
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
.

Each simplex of dimension
\ell-2
lies in the intersection of a fixed point hyperplane
L_{k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1},i}
with the surface
\big(k\mathfrak{t}k^{-1}\big)_{r}
of radius

as calculated in Section

, while simplices of dimension

lie in the intersection of
n-1
such hyperplanes with the surface. The stabilizer in
K
of
\mathfrak{t}
is the Weyl group
W
since

is a quotient of the normalizer of
T
. Hence simplicies of lower dimension are fixed by nontrivial subgroups of
W
.
∎

Before we can state and prove the main embedding theorem in the next section, we must define the distance and codistance functions on
\mathfrak{H}_{r}
. For any two chambers
k_{1}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{1}^{-1}
and
k_{2}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{2}^{-1}
in

, define the

\delta^{\pm}\big(k_{1}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{1}^{-1},k_{2}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{2}^{-1}\big)=w\in W\qquad\hbox{when}\qquad k_{1}^{-1}k_{2}\in B^{\pm}wB^{\pm}

and the codistance function

\delta^{*}\big(k_{1}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{1}^{-1},k_{2}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\mp}k_{2}^{-1}\big)=w\in W\qquad\hbox{when}\qquad k_{1}^{-1}k_{2}\in B^{\pm}wB^{\mp}.

Note that

is a Tits building with an apartment system determined by

, and

to be a fundamental chamber in

and then

is a fundamental apartment in
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
.

6
The main embedding theorem

The main result of this paper, given in this section, is an embedding of the twin building
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-},\delta^{*})
of the Kac–Moody
group
G
with twin
BN
-pair
(B^{+},B^{-},N)
into the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
. This is a bijective simplicial map from
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
onto the simplicial complex defined in
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
for each
r<0
defined in Section
5
, such that the

W
-valued distance and codistance functions are respected, and the map is
K
-equivariant, but not
G
-equivariant.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
A
be a symmetrizable hyperbolic generalized Cartan matrix,
\mathfrak{g}
its complex Kac–Moody algebra and
G
its complex Kac–Moody group with twin
BN
-pair
(B^{+},B^{-},N)
,
W
-valued distance functions,
\delta^{\pm}
,
W
-valued codistance function,
\delta^{*}
, and let
r<0
. Let
K
be the compact real form of
G
and let
\mathfrak{k}
be its Lie algebra. Let
\mathcal{B}=(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-},\delta^{*})
be the geometric realization
(
Section
2.3
)
of the twin building of
G
over
{\mathbb{C}}
, with Tits buildings,
(\mathcal{B}^{\pm},\delta^{\pm})
and codistance function
\delta^{*}
in
\mathcal{B}
. We use the same notations,
\delta^{\pm}
and
\delta^{*}
, for distance functions between chambers in
\mathfrak{H}_{r}^{\pm}
and codistance function in
\mathfrak{H}_{r}
.

1.

There is a
K
-equivariant simplicial map

\Psi_{r}\colon\mathcal{B}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}\subset\widehat{\mathfrak{k}}
, that is, the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{\mathcal{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{K}
\scriptstyle{\Psi_{r}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Psi_{r}}
\textstyle{\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{{\rm Ad}_{K}}
\textstyle{\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}}

where
{\rm Ad}_{K}
denotes the adjoint action and
K\subset G
acts on
\mathcal{B}
by left multiplication.

2.

When
A
is strictly hyperbolic the
K
-equivariant restrictions
\Psi_{r}^{\pm}\colon\mathcal{B}^{\pm}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{H}_{r}^{\pm}
are bijective, otherwise,
\Psi_{r}^{\pm}\colon\mathcal{B}^{\pm}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
are injective. For any chambers
C
,
D
in
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
, we have
\delta^{\pm}(C,D)=\delta^{\pm}\big(\Psi_{r}^{\pm}(C),\Psi_{r}^{\pm}(D)\big)
, so
\Psi_{r}^{\pm}
respects the distance functions
in
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
and
\mathfrak{H}_{r}^{\pm}
.

3.

There is a
W
-equivariant embedding of the fundamental apartment of
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
into

\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}^{\pm}_{r}\subset\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}_{r}
.

4.

The map
\Psi_{r}
is a twin building isomorphism. In particular, for any chamber
C
in

\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
and any chamber
D
in
\mathcal{B}^{\mp}
, we have
\delta^{*}(C,D)=\delta^{*}\big(\Psi_{r}^{\pm}(C),\Psi_{r}^{\mp}(D)\big)
, so
\Psi_{r}
respects the codistance functions in
\mathcal{B}
and
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}
.

Remark 6.2
.

The image
{\rm Im\,}(\Psi_{r})
is contained in the interior of the lightcone if
A
is strictly hyperbolic. Examples of this type are the rank
2
hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras (see Section
7
). Otherwise it contains points on the boundary of the lightcone. Examples of this type are the algebras
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{I}
and
E_{10}
.

Proof.

To construct the embedding of the twin building we use the description given in equation (
4.4
)

\mathcal{B}^{\pm}=\big(K/T\times\Delta^{\pm}\big)/{\sim}.

Our construction is in three steps. We first define the embedding of the fundamental chamber. Then we make use of the
K
-action on the building and the adjoint action of
K
on the Lie algebra. In a third step we establish the properties claimed in the theorem.

Part 1:
We choose the fundamental chambers in the buildings
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
to be
c^{\pm}=\big(1T,\Delta_{\varnothing}^{\pm}\big)\in\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
. The
\ell
panels of the fundamental chamber correspond to the simplices
\big(1T,\Delta_{i}^{\pm}\big)
for
1\leq i\leq\ell
, its vertices correspond to the simplices
(1T,\Delta_{[i]}^{\pm})
. Recall that
[i]=I\backslash\{i\}
. Two pairs
\big(fT,\Delta_{i}^{\pm}\big)
and
\big(gT,\Delta_{i}^{\pm}\big)
describe the same simplex if any only if
fK_{i}=gK_{i}
where
K_{i}=K\cap P_{i}=K\cap(B\sqcup Bw_{i}B)
; similarly for any subset
J\subsetneq I
two
J
-cells
\big(fT,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
and
\big(fT,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
are equivalent if and only
fK_{J}=gK_{J}
for
K_{J}=K\cap P_{J}
.

For fixed
r<0
we have chosen fundamental chambers
\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r}^{-}=-\mathcal{C}_{r}^{+}.

(6.1)

If
A
is strictly hyperbolic the fundamental domain is contained in the interior of
\mathfrak{t}_{r}
; hence its closure is contained in
\mathfrak{t}_{r}
. On the other hand if
A
is not strictly hyperbolic then it is unbounded and its closure is contained in
\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}_{r}
but not in
\mathfrak{t}_{r}
.
We identify the boundary hyperplanes
L_{\mathfrak{t},i}
of the fundamental chamber with the generators
w_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq\ell
, of the Weyl group
W
. We also identify intersections of hyperplanes
L_{\mathfrak{t},i_{1}}\cap\dots\cap L_{\mathfrak{t},i_{n}}
for distinct indices
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\}\subset I
with the subset of generators
\{w_{i_{1}},\dots,w_{i_{n}}\}
. Vertices correspond to the intersection of
(\ell-1)
hyperplanes and hence to subsets
[i]=I\backslash\{i\}
. For a subset
J\subsetneq I
we define the boundary components

\mathcal{C}_{J}^{\pm}=\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}\cap\bigcap_{j\in J}L_{\mathfrak{t},j}.

We define
\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta_{[i]}^{\pm}\big)=\mathcal{C}_{[i]}^{\pm}
and extend this map to
\Delta^{\pm}
by mapping a point
x
in the geometric realization of the simplex
\big(1T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
with normalized barycentric coordinates
x=[\lambda_{1}:\dots:\lambda_{\ell}]
to the point with the same normalized hyperbolic barycentric coordinates.

Recall the definition of normalized hyperbolic barycentric coordinates: Let
\Delta
be a simplex in
n
-dimensional hyperbolic space with vertices
(v_{0},\dots,v_{n})
. Some vertices may be on the boundary of hyperbolic space. We denote by
V=V(\Delta)
the volume of
\Delta
. For any point
p\in\Delta
we can define
n+1
simplices
\Delta_{[i]}
for
0\leq i\leq n
, spanned by the
(n+1)
-tuple of vertices
(v_{0},\dots,p,\dots,v_{n})
, where the vertex
v_{i}
has been replaced by
p
. Let
V_{i}=V(\Delta_{[i]})
denote the volume of
\Delta_{[i]}
. Then the normalized hyperbolic barycentric coordinates of
p
are given by

\left[\frac{V_{0}}{V}:\dots:\frac{V_{n}}{V}\right].

This yields a simplicial map
\Psi_{r}\colon\big(1T,\Delta^{\pm}\big)\longrightarrow\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}
.

Part 2:
Let
x^{\pm}\in\Delta^{\pm}
. We extend the map defined in Part
1
to a map

\Psi_{r}\colon\ \mathcal{B}\hookrightarrow\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}

by defining

\Psi_{r}\big(kT,x^{\pm}\big)={\rm Ad}_{k}\big[\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x^{\pm}\big)\big].

We have to check that
\Psi_{r}
is well-defined. Assume we have two equivalent elements
\big(k_{1}T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)\allowbreak\sim\big(k_{2}T,x_{2}^{\pm}\big)
, hence
x_{1}^{\pm}=x_{2}^{\pm}
and assuming
x_{1}^{\pm}\in\Delta^{\pm}_{J}
for some subset
J\subsetneq I
, we have
k_{1}K_{J}=k_{2}K_{J}
. Then there is some
l\in K_{J}
such that
k_{1}=k_{2}l
and we have

\Psi_{r}\big(k_{1}T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)={\rm Ad}_{k_{1}}\big[\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)\big]={\rm Ad}_{k_{2}l}\big[\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)\big].

Since
x_{1}^{\pm}\in\Delta_{J}^{\pm}
we have

\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)\in\bigcap_{j\in J}L_{\mathfrak{t},j}.

Hence
{\rm Ad}_{l}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)=\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)
. Thus, from
{\rm Ad}_{k_{2}l}={\rm Ad}_{k_{2}}{\rm Ad}_{l}
we get

{\rm Ad}_{k_{2}l}\big[\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{1}^{\pm}\big)\big]={\rm Ad}_{k_{2}}\big[\Psi_{r}\big(1T,x_{2}^{\pm}\big)\big]=\Psi_{r}\big(k_{2}T,x_{2}^{\pm}\big).

Note that
\Psi_{r}
maps the simplex
(kT,\Delta_{J}^{\pm})
for
J\subsetneq I
spanned by vertices
\big(kT,\Delta^{\pm}_{[i]}\big)
,

i\in J
, onto the simplex spanned by
\Psi_{r}\big(kT,\Delta^{\pm}_{[i]}\big)
,
i\in J
.
If two simplices
\big(k_{1}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)
and
\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)
share a common face
\big(lT,\Delta^{\pm}_{L}\big)
,
for
J\subsetneq L
in
\mathcal{B}
then by definition we have
k_{1}T\subset{\rm Ad}_{l}K_{L}
and
k_{2}T\subset{\rm Ad}_{l}K_{L}
. But then

\Psi_{r}\big(k_{1}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{L}\big)=\Psi_{r}\big(lT,\Delta^{\pm}_{L}\big)=\Psi_{r}\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{L}\big)
is the commonly shared
face of the simplices
\Psi_{r}\big(k_{1}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)
and
\Psi_{r}\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)
. Hence
\Psi_{r}
preserves the simplicial structure of
\mathcal{B}
and is thus a simplicial complex map.

Part 3:
We continue to check that
\Psi_{r}
satisfies the properties stated in the theorem.

Proof of
(1)
:
Recall the left
K
-action on the building
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
:

K\colon\ \mathcal{B}^{\pm}\longrightarrow\mathcal{B}^{\pm},\qquad k\cdot\big(fT,\Delta^{\pm}\big)\mapsto\big(kfT,\Delta^{\pm}\big).

We need to verify that for
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
and
J\subsetneq I

\Psi_{r}\big(k_{1}\cdot\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)\big)={\rm Ad}_{k_{1}}\Psi_{r}\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big).

We have

\displaystyle\Psi_{r}(k_{1}\cdot\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J})\big)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{r}\big(k_{1}k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)={\rm Ad}_{k_{1}k_{2}}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)

\displaystyle={\rm Ad}_{k_{1}}\big({\rm Ad}_{k_{2}}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)\big)={\rm Ad}_{k_{1}}\big(\Psi_{r}\big(k_{2}T,\Delta^{\pm}_{J}\big)\big).

Proof of
(2)
:

Assume two simplices
\big(k_{1}T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
and
\big(k_{2}T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
satisfy

\Psi_{r}\big(k_{1}T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)=\Psi_{r}\big(k_{2}T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big).

Then we have

{\rm Ad}_{k_{1}}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)={\rm Ad}_{k_{2}}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big),

which implies
{\rm Ad}_{k_{1}^{-1}k_{2}}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)=\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
.

As
\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)=\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{J}
its stabilizer in
K
is
K_{J}
. Hence
k_{1}^{-1}k_{2}\in K_{J}
.
Thus
\big(k_{1}T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)\sim\big(k_{2}T,\Delta_{J}^{\pm}\big)
which proves injectivity.

Suppose we have an arbitrary element
x^{\pm}\in\mathfrak{H}_{r}^{\pm}
. Then there is some group element
k\in K
such that

{\rm Ad}_{k}x^{\pm}\in\mathfrak{t}^{\pm}_{r}
so it is in the closure of some chamber,
w\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}
, uniquely labeled by an element
w\in W
.
The action of the corresponding element

matches the action of
w
on
\mathfrak{t}
. So we have

{\rm Ad}_{\widetilde{w}^{-1}}{\rm Ad}_{k}x^{\pm}\in\mathcal{C}^{\pm}_{r}
. Therefore

x^{\pm}\in{\rm Ad}_{k^{-1}}{\rm Ad}_{\widetilde{w}}\Psi_{r}\big(1T,\Delta^{\pm}\big)=\Psi_{r}\big(k^{-1}\widetilde{w}T,\Delta^{\pm}\big)
.
This shows that in the case when

is strictly hyperbolic,
\Psi_{r}\colon\mathcal{B}^{\pm}\to\mathfrak{H}_{r}^{\pm}
is surjective. Otherwise there can be elements

which are in
{\rm Im}(\Psi_{r})
, but some may not. It would be interesting to understand precisely which points in

are in
{\rm Im}(\Psi_{r})
.

Let
C=\big(k_{1}T,\Delta^{\pm}\big)
and

be chambers in
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}

when

.

\displaystyle\delta^{\pm}\big(\Psi_{r}^{\pm}(C),\Psi_{r}^{\pm}(D)\big)=\delta^{\pm}\big(k_{1}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{1}^{-1},k_{2}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{2}^{-1}\big)=w,

The proof of (3) is clear.

\displaystyle\Psi_{r}^{\mp}(D)={\rm Ad}_{k_{2}}\big[\Psi_{r}^{\mp}\big(1T,\Delta^{\mp}\big)\big]=k_{2}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\mp}k_{2}^{-1}\qquad\hbox{so}

\displaystyle\delta^{*}\big(\Psi_{r}^{\pm}(C),\Psi_{r}^{\mp}(D)\big)=\delta^{*}\big(k_{1}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\pm}k_{1}^{-1},k_{2}\mathcal{C}_{r}^{\mp}k_{2}^{-1}\big)=w,

.

Remark 6.3
.

Based on our understanding of the examples
\mathcal{F}
and
E_{10}
, we believe that the intersection of the nullcone with
{\rm Im}(\Psi_{r})
can be characterized as those rays on the nullcone which contain roots of
\mathfrak{g}
. Each such ray of null vectors corresponds to a copy of an affine Kac–Moody subalgebra inside
\mathfrak{g}
. Each such ray should be conjugate under the action of the Weyl group
W
to one ray in the closure of the fundamental chamber, so the number of
W
orbits is the number of ideal points in that fundamental domain. Classifying those rays then becomes a significant problem involving the arithmetic properties of
W
, essentially understanding its cusps as a modular group. Such an analysis was carried out for
E_{10}
in
[
42
, Lemma 5.2]
.

Thus for each
r<0
we have established an embedding
\Psi_{r}
of the twin building
\mathcal{B}=\big(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-},\delta^{*}\big)
into
\mathfrak{H}_{r}^{\pm}
which is inside the lightcone

\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{k}}=\{x\in\mathfrak{k}\,|\,(x,x)\leq 0\}

of the compact real form
\mathfrak{k}
of a strictly hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and into its lightlike closure,
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
, otherwise.

Remark 6.4
.

Recall that
\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
is contractible since each apartment is contractible (see
[

]
). Since
\Psi_{r}
for

is a simplicial complex isomorphism onto its image, the image

This observation yields the corollary

Corollary 6.5
.

The spaces
\widehat{\mathfrak{H}}_{r}^{\pm}
are contractible.

We conclude this section with a discussion of Cartan involutions and the embedding of the twin building.
Recall from Section
2.4
that the Cartan involution is the automorphism
{\omega}_{0}\colon

\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
determined by
{\omega}_{0}(e_{i})=-f_{i}
,
\omega_{0}(f_{i})=-e_{i}
and
{\omega}_{0}(h_{i})=-h_{i}
, and
\omega

\omega_{0}\big(\mathfrak{t}^{\pm}\big)=\mathfrak{t}^{\mp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\omega\big(\mathfrak{t}^{\pm}\big)=\mathfrak{t}^{\mp}\,

as well as

\omega_{0}\big(\mathfrak{t}^{\pm}_{r}\big)=\mathfrak{t}_{r}^{\mp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\omega\big(\mathfrak{t}^{\pm}_{r}\big)=\mathfrak{t}^{\mp}_{r}\,

for each

\omega_{0}\big(\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}^{\pm}_{r}\big)=\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}_{r}^{\mp}\qquad\text{and}\qquad-\omega\big(\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}^{\pm}_{r}\big)=\widehat{\mathfrak{t}}^{\mp}_{r}.

The following diagram is commutative:

By definition

7
Special results for the twin building in rank 2

In this section assume that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A)
is a rank
2
hyperbolic Kac–Moody Lie algebra, so that

A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-a\\
-b&2\end{bmatrix},\qquad ab>4.

Rank
2
hyperbolic Kac–Moody algebras were studied intensively by Lepowsky and Moody
[
45
]
, by
Feingold
[
17
]
for the “Fibonacci hyperbolic” (
a=b=3
),
\mathcal{F}{\rm ib}
, by Kang and Melville
[
40
]
,
by Carbone, Kownacki, Murray and Srinivasan
[
8
]
and by Andersen, Carbone and Penta
[
2
]
. Twin trees and their relationship to Kac–Moody groups of rank 2 are studied in
[
57
]
.
In these rank 2 cases the hyperboloids
\mathfrak{t}_{r}
for
r\neq 0
are hyperbolas, and there is no topological distinction between “one-sheeted” for
r>0
and “two-sheeted” for
r<0
, as there is in higher rank.
In this section we will provide a detailed construction of the twin building,
\mathcal{B}=\big(\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{B}^{-},\delta^{*}\big)
, whose simplicial structure is a pair of trees, each equipped with a
W
-valued distance function,
W
-valued codistance function, and give the apartment system and the action of the complex group
G_{\mathbb{C}}
on each tree. We will also give an explicit description of the apartments in the
K
-orbit of the fundamental twin apartment, which will
provide more details of the embedding of the twin building into the compact real form in this rank 2 case.
In the next section we show how this allows the embedding of a spherical building at infinity.

The eigenvalues of
A
are
\lambda_{\pm}=2\pm\sqrt{ab}
so
\lambda_{+}>0
and
\lambda_{-}<0
. This means that
the signature of the bilinear form determined by
A
on the split real Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
is
(1,1)
. By equation (
3.1
), the bilinear form on the compact real Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}=i\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}
also has signature
(1,1)
.

The Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}
is the infinite dihedral group

W=\big\langle w_{1},w_{2}\,|\,w_{1}^{2}=1=w_{2}^{2}\big\rangle\cong{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\ast{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}\cong{\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes\{\pm 1\},

which acts on
\mathfrak{h}\subset\mathfrak{g}
as well as on
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}\subset\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbb{R}}
and on
\mathfrak{t}\subset\mathfrak{k}
.
Denote the index
2
maximal infinite cyclic subgroup of
W
by

W^{\rm even}=\big\{(w_{2}w_{1})^{m}\,|\,m\in\mathbb{Z}\big\}\cong\mathbb{Z}

so that for
i=1,2
,

W^{\rm odd}=\big\{(w_{2}w_{1})^{m}w_{i}\,|\,m\in\mathbb{Z}\big\}

is the other coset, and we have the relations

w_{i}(w_{2}w_{1})^{m}w_{i}^{-1}=(w_{2}w_{1})^{-m}\qquad\hbox{for}\quad m\in\mathbb{Z},\quad i=1,2.

With
t=w_{2}w_{1}
and
r=w_{i}
this gives the presentation of
W
as the infinite dihedral group

W=\big\langle t,r\,|\,r^{2}=1,\,rtr^{-1}=t^{-1}\big\rangle
. It can be useful to index the elements of
W
by the integers so that

W^{\rm even}
and
W^{\rm odd}
correspond to even and odd integers, respectively (after making a definite choice for
i
, say
i=2
):

w(n)=\begin{cases}(w_{2}w_{1})^{m}&\hbox{if }n=2m,\\
(w_{2}w_{1})^{m}w_{2}&\hbox{if }n=2m+1.\end{cases}

Then we have
w_{1}w(n)=w(-1-n)
and
w_{2}w(n)=w(1-n)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, and

w(n)^{-1}=\begin{cases}(w_{2}w_{1})^{-m}&\hbox{if }n=2m,\\
w_{2}(w_{2}w_{1})^{-m}&\hbox{if }n=2m+1\end{cases}=\begin{cases}w(-n)&\hbox{if }n=2m,\\
w(n)&\hbox{if }n=2m+1.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to check that for any
n,k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

w(n)w(k)=\begin{cases}w(n+k)&\hbox{if }n=2m,\\
w(n-k)&\hbox{if }n=2m+1.\end{cases}

We will also find it useful to similarly label certain elements of

\widetilde{W}
by the integers in the same way:

\widetilde{w}(n)=\begin{cases}(\widetilde{w}_{2}\widetilde{w}_{1})^{m}&\hbox{if }n=2m,\\
(\widetilde{w}_{2}\widetilde{w}_{1})^{m}\widetilde{w}_{2}&\hbox{if }n=2m+1.\end{cases}

We define the non-standard partition of the real roots of
\mathfrak{g}
,
{\Phi}^{\rm re}={\Phi}_{1}\cup{\Phi}_{2}
where

{\Phi}_{1}=W^{\rm even}\alpha_{1}\cup W^{\rm odd}\alpha_{2}=W^{\rm even}\{\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2}\}

and

{\Phi}_{2}=W^{\rm even}\alpha_{2}\cup W^{\rm odd}\alpha_{1}=W^{\rm even}\{-\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}.

\alpha_{j},\alpha_{k}\in{\Phi}_{i}
and for any root vectors
e_{\alpha_{j}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{j}}
and
e_{\alpha_{k}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{k}}
. A general element in

can be
written as
u=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\exp({\rm ad}_{e_{\alpha_{j}}})

we have

\displaystyle u(h)=h-\sum_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}(h)e_{\alpha_{j}}\in\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{r}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{j}}.

∎

Lemma 8.10
.

We have
B_{i,K}=TW^{\rm even}=B_{K}
is independent of
i=1,2
.

Proof.

By Theorem
8.8
we have
B_{i}=TW^{\rm even}U_{i}
. It is clear that
TW^{\rm even}\subset K
. For
i=1,2
, set
(U_{i})_{K}=U_{i}\cap K=\{u\in U_{i}\,|\,\omega(u)=u\}
where
\omega
denotes the Cartan involution. Since
\omega(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})=\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha}
for any
\alpha\in{\Phi}
, we get
\omega\circ U_{\alpha}\circ\omega^{-1}=U_{-\alpha}
from Lemma
2.9
.
Suppose
1\neq u=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\exp({\rm ad}_{e_{\alpha_{j}}})\in(U_{i})_{K}
for
\alpha_{j}\in{\Phi}_{i}
,
1\leq j\leq r
, where
e_{\alpha_{j}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{j}}

u=\omega(u)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{r}\exp({\rm ad}_{e_{-\alpha_{j}}})
for some basis vector

Let
h\in\mathfrak{h}
be an element such that
\alpha_{j}(h)\neq 0

u(h)=h-\sum\limits_{j=1}^{r}\alpha_{j}(h)e_{\alpha_{j}}

gives the non-trivial dependence relation

among the independent basis vectors from the root spaces

\oplus_{j=1}^{r}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{j}}
and
\oplus_{j=1}^{r}\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_{j}}
. This contradiction proves that

(U_{i})_{K}=\{1\}
.
∎

Corollary 8.11
.

The maps
{\rm Ch}^{\pm}\colon K/B_{K}\to{\rm Cham}\big(\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{\pm}\big)

{\rm Ch}^{\pm}(kB_{K})=k\big(e_{i,\infty}^{\pm}\big)

For each choice of

∎

Lemma 8.12

For

the twin halo of

The action of the even Weyl group

The results in this section suggest that on the Lie algebra level one could study a
non-standard Cartan decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in P_{1}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\bigoplus_{\alpha\in P_{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

based on a non-standard partition
\Phi=P_{1}\cup P_{2}
such that
\Phi_{i}\subset P_{i}
for
i=1,2
, and such that

\mathfrak{n}_{1}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in P_{1}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}\qquad\hbox{and}\qquad\mathfrak{n}_{2}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in P_{2}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}

are subalgebras. Then
\mathfrak{b}_{i}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, would be non-standard Borel subalgebras corresponding to the subgroups

B_{i}
. That would be accomplished if each subset
P_{i}
were closed under addition.
Since
\Phi
is a subset of the dual of the split real Cartan subalgebra,
\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}^{*}
, one might consider using a timelike line in the interior of the lightcone
\mathcal{L}_{\mathfrak{h}_{\mathbb{R}}}^{0}
to partition all of
\Phi
. If such a line does not contain any roots, every root is on one side or the other, and
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are on opposite sides for any choice of the line. But if the line contains
roots, one would have to decide which ones go in which part of the partition, and it must be done in such a way that each
P_{i}
is closed under addition. The solution would be to divide up the line into two rays from the origin, and divide up the roots on the line according to which ray they are in.

There are two obvious partitions determined by the two lightcone lines themselves. For the line determined by
x^{+}_{2}
the partition would be
P_{1}=\Phi_{1}\cup\big(\Phi^{\rm im}\big)^{+}
and
P_{2}=\Phi_{2}\cup\big(\Phi^{\rm im}\big)^{-}
, while for the line determined by
x^{+}_{1}
the partition would be
P_{1}=\Phi_{1}\cup\big(\Phi^{\rm im}\big)^{-}
and
P_{2}=\Phi_{2}\cup\big(\Phi^{\rm im}\big)^{+}
. We have found only two distinct non-standard Borels at infinity,
B_{i}
,
i=1,2
which may correspond to these two partitions, but we have not yet seen a family of non-standard Borels corresponding to
other choices of partitions.

Having found a non-standard Cartan decomposition as above, one gets a corresponding non-standard decomposition of the universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{n}_{1})\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{h})\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{n}_{2})
and can construct induced Verma modules of two types,

{\rm Verma}^{i}(\lambda)=\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{n}_{i})v^{i}_{\lambda}

are vectors such that

for any

,
\mathfrak{n}_{1}\cdot v^{2}_{\lambda}=0
and
\mathfrak{n}_{2}\cdot v^{1}_{\lambda}=0
.
The quotient of such a Verma module by its maximal proper submodule would be an irreducible module,
{\rm Irred}^{i}(\lambda)
generated by

. These would be integrable
\mathfrak{g}
-modules for

in the weight lattice of

(

,

are the fundamental weights of
\mathfrak{g}
such that

and

have opposite signs. Examples of such integrable modules, in addition to the adjoint representation, were mentioned by

Such modules have been found to occur in the decomposition of the rank 3 hyperbolic algebra,

9
Conclusion and further directions

Our lightcone embedding of the twin building of a hyperbolic Kac–Moody group is motivated by the conjectural existence of hyperbolic Kac–Moody symmetric spaces. There have been some efforts recently to develop the geometry of hyperbolic Kac–Moody symmetric spaces, building on work of the third author on the construction of affine Kac–Moody symmetric spaces
[
19
,
20
,
22
,
23
,
25
,
26
]
.

Recalling the well-known finite-dimensional theory (see for example
[
16
,
32
]
), the boundary of a symmetric space
M
of non–compact type
IV
corresponding to a complex simple Lie group
G
, can be identified with the building over
\mathbb{C}
associated to
G
. Via the isotropy representation at a point
p\in M
, the building can be embedded into the unit sphere of the tangent space
T_{p}M
. In this way, points in the building get identified with directions in the tangent space of the symmetric space. Via the duality between the compact type and non-compact types, we can identify spaces of type
IV
with spaces of type
II
and thus also obtain an embedding of the building into the tangent space of a compact symmetric space.

In the absence of hyperbolic Kac–Moody symmetric spaces, important properties of the local geometry are captured via the embedding of the building into the Lie algebra. Our embedding of the building into the Lie algebra gives local pictures of the tangent spaces of conjectural hyperbolic Kac–Moody symmetric spaces of types
II
and
IV
. We note however, that since our twin building embeds into the lightcone of the compact form of the Lie algebra, it captures only the timelike directions in the tangent space.

Via Proposition
4.2
, we have also obtained a hyperbolic analog of the notion of a
polar representation
by the group
K
on the
K
-conjugacy class of a Cartan subalgebra. Recall that a group representation
G\colon V\longrightarrow V
on a vector space
V
is called
polar
if there exists a subspace
\Sigma\subset V
, called a
section
, such that each orbit
G\cdot v
for
v\in V
intersects
\Sigma
orthogonally. Finite-dimensional polar representations are orbit equivalent to isotropy representations of finite-dimensonal Riemannian symmetric spaces
[
10
]
. Similar observations were made in the affine case
[
28
,
31
]
. Our Proposition
4.2
shows that the action of
K
on
\mathfrak{H}
is a polar action with section
\mathfrak{t}
.

Further questions about polar representations for hyperbolic Kac–Moody groups remain open and the full differential geometry need to develop hyperbolic Kac–Moody symmetric spaces remains elusive. We hope to take this up elsewhere.

Appendix A
A new formula for the Weyl group generators

on any integrable module

It is remarkable that the proof of the following theorem, starting from a simple calculation in the two-dimensional representation of

\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})_{i}
, generalizes to a result in any integrable module for any Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Special cases of this formula appeared
in some physics papers, for example, in
[
12
]
, where Damour and Hillmann found it in a representation of

K(E_{10})
.

It is known
[
35
]
that for any integrable representation
\phi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to{\rm End}(V)
, the group
{\widetilde{W}}
generated by the operators

{\widetilde{w}}_{i}^{\phi}=\exp(\phi(e_{i}))\exp(\phi(-f_{i})\exp(\phi(e_{i})),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n

is a subgroup of the Kac–Moody group
G
that acts on
V
as follows. If
V_{\mu}
is the
\mu
-weight space of
V
, then
{\widetilde{w}}_{i}^{\phi}(V_{\mu})=V_{w_{i}(\mu)}
, where
w_{i}
is the reflection with respect to the simple root
\alpha_{i}
in the dual space,
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, of the standard Cartan subalgebra,
\mathfrak{h}
. The Weyl group,
W
, is the Coxeter group generated by those simple reflections, and is also defined on
\mathfrak{h}
by the formula
w_{i}(h)=h-\alpha_{i}(h)h_{i}
. There is a surjection from
{\widetilde{W}}
onto
W
whose kernel is given by Remark 3.8 in
[
35
]
. In the case when
\phi
is the adjoint representation, the restriction of each
{\widetilde{w}}_{i}^{\phi}
to
\mathfrak{h}
equals
w_{i}
.

For any integrable representation
\phi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to{\rm End}(V)
, define the “compact” operators from
G^{\theta}
,

s_{i}^{\phi}=\exp(\phi(\pi x_{i}))=\exp(\phi(\pi(e_{i}-f_{i})/2)),\qquad 1\leq i\leq n.

We prove that
{\widetilde{w}}_{i}^{\phi}=s_{i}^{\phi}
. This provides a new description of
{\widetilde{W}}
as generated by operators from the real compact form. This generalizes a result discovered from a physics point of view by Damour and Hillmann in a representation of
K(E_{10})
.

Theorem A.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be any Kac–Moody algebra of rank
n
with the usual Chevalley generators
e_{i}
,
f_{i}
,
h_{i}
and let
\phi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to{\rm End}(V)
be any integrable representation. Then for
1\leq i\leq n
, we have

\exp(\phi(e_{i}))\exp(\phi(-f_{i})\exp(\phi(e_{i}))=\exp(\phi(\pi(e_{i}-f_{i})/2)).

Proof.

Using the notations as above, for
1\leq i\leq n
, let
\mathfrak{g}_{i}=\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})_{i}
be the Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
with basis
\{e_{i},f_{i},h_{i}\}
. Then
V
has a direct sum decomposition into irreducible
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
-modules

V=\bigoplus_{j\in J}V_{j}(m),

where
\dim(V_{j}(m))=m+1
and the index set
J
includes information about the
\mathfrak{g}_{i}
highest weight vector in
V_{j}(m)
that locates it in
V
.
It is clear that
{\widetilde{w}}_{i}^{\phi}=s_{i}^{\phi}
on
V
if and only if their restrictions to each
V_{j}(m)
are equal. They are certainly equal for all trivial one-dimensional modules,
V_{j}(0)
. On any two-dimensional module,
V_{j}(1)
, the computation comes down to the simple fact that

\exp\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)\exp\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}\right)\exp\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{bmatrix}

matches

\exp\left(\begin{bmatrix}0&t\\
-t&0\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(t)&\sin(t)\\
-\sin(t)&\cos(t)\end{bmatrix},

when
t=\pi/2
. The well-known tensor product decomposition of irreducible
\mathfrak{sl}(2,{\mathbb{C}})
-modules says that for any integer
m\geq 1
, we have

V(m)\otimes V(1)=V(m+1)\oplus V(m-1).

So if the operators are equal on modules
V(m)
and
V(1)
then they are equal on the tensor product, so they are equal on the component

V(m+1)
. This proves by induction that they are equal on any
V_{j}(m)
that occurs in
V
.
∎
