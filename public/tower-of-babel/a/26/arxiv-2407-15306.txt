Distribution of the zeros of polynomials near the unit circle
Mithun Kumar Das

Abstract
. We estimate the number of zeros of a polynomial in ℂ ⁡ [ z ] \mathbb{C}[z] within any small circular disc centered on the unit circle, which improves and comprehensively extends a result established by Borwein, Erdélyi, and Littmann [ 4 ] in 2008. Furthermore, by combining this result with Euclidean geometry, we derive an upper bound on the number of zeros of such a polynomial within a region resembling a gear wheel. Additionally, we obtain a sharp upper bound on the annular discrepancy of such zeros near the unit circle. Our approach builds upon a modified version of the method described in [ 4 ] , combined with the refined version of the best-known upper bound for angular discrepancy of zeros of polynomials.

1.
Introduction

Let
P_{n}
be a polynomial in
\mathbb{C}[z]
of degree
n
. Our primary focus in this study is to understand the distribution of the zeros of
P_{n}
on the complex plane, emphasizing their distribution within and around the unit disk.
One of the most extensively studied classes of polynomials is the family of unimodular polynomials denoted as
\mathcal{K}_{n}
. These polynomials are defined as follows:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{n}:=\Big\{P_{n}:P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}z^{k},\>a_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\,|a_{k}|=1\Big\}.

If we further restrict the coefficients to only
-1
and
1
, such polynomials are called Littlewood polynomials, named in honor of Littlewood, who extensively studied various analytical properties (the mean value, the number of zeros, etc.) related to polynomials and power series with constrained coefficients lying on the complex unit circle
|z|=1
(see
[
10
,
11
]
).
The study of complex zeros of Littlewood polynomials and polynomials with coefficients
\{-1,\,0,\,1\}
is an old subject, dating back to the pioneering work of Bloch and Pólya
[
2
]
. Their research established that, on average, such polynomials of degree
n
have at most
\operatorname{O}(\sqrt{n})
real roots.
Modern investigations into the distribution of zeros often originate from the seminal result of Erdős and Turán
[
7
]
, which addresses the angular equidistribution of zeros and is applicable to polynomials within the
\mathbb{C}[z]
. In a later section, we will delve into a more detailed discussion of this matter.
In their work
[
3
]
, Borwein and Erdélyi explore a broader perspective by considering a generalization of the class
\mathcal{K}_{n}
as follows:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\Big\{P_{n}:P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}z^{k},\>a_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\,|a_{0}|=|a_{n}|=1,\,|a_{k}|\leq 1\Big\}.

They demonstrate that any polygon with vertices on the unit circle
|z|=1
contains at most
\operatorname{O}(\sqrt{n})
zeros of such polynomials. Such a result has been established in
[
14
,
15
]
for random polynomials.

In 2008, Borwein, Erdélyi, Littmann
[
4
]
studied the distribution of zeros for any polynomial in
\mathcal{G}_{n}
that lies within small circles centered on the unit circle. Specifically, they established the following result:

Theorem 1

(Theorem 1,
[
4
]
)
.

Let
P_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
be a polynomial of degree
n
.
Then any disk of radius
33\pi\log{n}/\sqrt{n},(\leq 1)
with the center on the unit circle
|z|=1
contains at least
8\sqrt{n}\log{n}
zeros of
P_{n}
.

For random polynomials, Pritsker and Sola
[
14
]
, as well as Pritsker and Yeager
[
15
]
, have derived the expectation of the above result asymptotically. In their work
[
14
]
, they considered the polynomial
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}C_{k}z^{k}
with coefficients that are iid complex random variables with absolutely continuous distribution and
\mathbb{E}(|C_{0}|^{t})<\infty
for any
t>0
. Then, they showed that the expected number of zeros inside any disc of radius
r<2
with center on the unit circle
|z|=1
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(r/2)n+\operatorname{O}(\sqrt{n\log{n}})\quad\mbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

2.
New results

After the pioneering work of Borwein, Erdélyi and Littmann
[
4
]
on the polynomial class
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, no further extensions of general polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[z]
have been explored, except for the results mentioned above about to random polynomials. In this study, we establish a lower bound for the number of zeros of polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[z]
situated within a circular disk, with its center located on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. Furthermore, we introduce a parametrization to the radius of this circular disk. Defining some notations regarding the
p
-norm of polynomials on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
is necessary to present our results. Let

(2.1)

\displaystyle P_{n}(z)=c_{n}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\big(z-\alpha_{j}\big)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}z^{j}

be a polynomial of degree
n
, with
c_{0}\neq 0
and roots
\alpha_{j}=\rho_{j}\,e(\theta_{j})
,
j=1,2,\ldots,n
, where
e(\theta):=e^{2\pi i\theta}
and throughout the paper we use the same notation. For
0<p<\infty
, a
p
-norm of
P_{n}
is defined on unit circle by

\|P_{n}\|_{p}=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}|P_{n}\big(e^{i\theta}\big)\big|^{p}\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}|P_{n}\big(e(\theta)\big)\big|^{p}\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Thus, for
p=2
, we get
\|P_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=|c_{n}|^{2}+\ldots+|c_{0}|^{2}
. The
\sup
-norm of
P_{n}
on
\mathbb{T}
is defined by
\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\mathbb{T}}|P_{n}\big(e^{i\theta}\big)\big|
. Furthermore, let
E
be the subset of
[0,1]
which is given by
E=\{\theta\in[0,1]:|P_{n}(e(\theta))|<1\}
and for
0<p\leq\infty
, defined the logarithmic
p
-norm by

(2.2)

\displaystyle B_{p}(P_{n})=\begin{cases}\log{\left(\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)}&\mbox{ if }p=\infty,\\
(1-|E|)\log{\left(\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{p}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)}+\frac{1}{ep}&\mbox{ if }0<p<\infty.\end{cases}

Now we state our new results as follows:

Theorem 2
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
P_{n}(0)\neq 0
and
\theta
be a fixed real number in
[\frac{1}{2},\,1]

\gamma_{n}=7{\frac{(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}}{\sqrt{n}}}\leq 1

, contains at least
\sqrt{n}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}
zeros of
P_{n}
.

Furthermore, if
p\in(0,\,\infty)
and
P_{n}
satisfy the conditions
|c_{n}c_{0}|\geq 1
and

, then any disk of radius
\gamma_{n}=9{\frac{(2B_{p}(P_{n}))^{\theta}}{\sqrt{n}}}\leq 1
with center on the unit circle
|z|=1
, contains at least
\sqrt{n}(2B_{p}(P_{n}))^{\theta}
zeros of
P_{n}
.

The subsequent result presents an analog of Theorem
2
for the class of polynomials
P_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
P_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
a polynomial and

be any fixed real number. Then any open disk of radius
\gamma_{n}=9{\frac{(\log{n})^{\theta}}{\sqrt{n}}}\leq 1
with centre on

contains at least
\sqrt{n}(\log{n})^{\theta}
zeros of

.

In Theorem
3
, if we set

, we can draw a meaningful comparison with Theorem

. Notably, when the condition

holds, we are permitted to work with polynomials of degree as small as

Furthermore, the disk’s radius described in Theorem

is approximately
11.5

.

Further, if we decide to maintain the same radius, denoted by
\gamma_{n}=33\pi{\frac{\log{n}}{\sqrt{n}}}
, then the disk of radius

contains at least
31\sqrt{n}\log{n}
zeros of

.

Now, we look at an application of Theorem

in order to estimate the number of zeros inside a certain gear wheel type region. In mathematics, a gear wheel (also known as a gear or cogwheel) is typically defined in the context of geometry and trigonometry. Gears are used to transmit motion and force from one rotating object to another, and they have a wide range of applications in mechanical engineering, physics, and other fields. A geometric construction for

Create a sizeable circular disk, denoted as
C

T

d

T

P_{n}
on the gear wheel.

Theorem 5
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
P_{n}(0)\neq 0
. Set
\gamma_{n}=7{\frac{(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}}{\sqrt{n}}}\leq\frac{1}{2}
with
\theta\in[\frac{1}{2},\,1]
and

\frac{\pi}{1+\delta}(\arcsin(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\sqrt{4-\gamma_{n}}))^{-1}

Further, if

and

P_{n}
.

If

2.1.
Height or mean of polynomials

We consider here three notions of size, or
height
, of a polynomial
P_{n}(z)
of degree
n
. The simple one is given by

\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}=\max_{|z|=1}\,|P_{n}(z)|.

The Mahler measure (geometric mean)
M(P_{n})
of
P_{n}
is defined as the following equivalent forms by using Jensen’s formula,

M(P_{n})=\exp{\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log\!\left(\big|P_{n}\big(e^{i\theta}\big)\big|\right)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)}=\exp{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\log\!\left(\big|P_{n}\big(e(\theta)\big)\big|\right)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)}=|c_{n}|\prod_{j=1}^{n}\max\{1,|\alpha_{j}|\}.

The logarithmic Mahler measure, denoted by
m(P_{n})
, is the natural logarithm of
M(P_{n})
. Another one is defined as follows:

M^{+}(P_{n})=\exp{\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log^{+}\!\left(\big|P_{n}\big(e^{i\theta}\big)\big|\right)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)}=\exp{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}\log^{+}\!\left(\big|P_{n}\big(e(\theta)\big)\big|\right)\,\text{\rm d}\theta\right)},

where
\log^{+}{x}=\max\{0,\,\log{x}\}
and then set
m^{+}(P_{n})=\log{M^{+}(P_{n})}
. From the definition, one observed that

(2.3)

M(P_{n})\leq M^{+}(P_{n})\leq\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}.

For a monic polynomial
P_{n}
, the Mahler measure
M(P_{n})\geq 1
implies that the logarithmic Mahler measure
m(P_{n})\geq 0
. A classical theorem of Kronecker implies that
M(P_{n})=1
if and only if
P_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is a product of cyclotomic polynomials and the monomial
x
. An old question asks by Lehmer
[
9
]
if there exist polynomials with Mahler measure between
1
and
1+\epsilon
for arbitrary
\epsilon>0
, and he notes that the polynomial

L(x)=x^{10}+x^{9}-x^{7}-x^{6}-x^{5}-x^{4}-x^{3}+x+1

has
M(L)=1.1762808\ldots
. Several extensions have been done. But following
[
1
,
6
,
20
]
we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle m(P_{n})>\frac{1}{4}\Big(\frac{\log\,\log{n}}{\log{n}}\Big)^{3}

for other general polynomials.

2.2.
Discrepancy of zeros of polynomials

This section will discuss a modified analysis of the angular distribution of the zeros of the polynomial
P_{n}(z)
given in (
2.1
). This refinement serves as a fundamental tool for deriving the new results mentioned earlier. A standard way to define the normalized zero-counting measure for such a polynomial is

\tau_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\delta_{\alpha_{k}}.

A sector
S(\alpha,\,\beta)
on
\mathbb{C}
is defined as

(2.5)

S(\alpha,\,\beta)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<\alpha\leq\arg{z}<\beta\leq 2\pi\}.

Then, the discrepancy between the normalized zero counting measure in
S(\alpha,\,\beta)
and the Lebesgue measure of the arc on the unit circle in this sector is known as the angular discrepancy. It is denoted by
\mathcal{D}(P_{n})
and given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(P_{n})=\sup_{[\alpha,\beta]\subset\mathbb{T}}\left|\tau_{n}{(S(\alpha,\,\beta))}-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|.

In a pioneering work in 1950 concerning the angular discrepancy of zeros, Erdős, and Turán
[
7
]
proved that

(2.6)

\mathcal{D}(P_{n})\leq C\sqrt{\frac{B_{\infty}(P_{n})}{n}}

with
C=16
. In 1954, Ganelius
[
8
]
established (
2.6
) with the constant
C=\sqrt{2\pi/k}=2.5619\ldots
, where
k=1/1^{2}-1/3^{2}+1/5^{2}-\ldots=0.9159\ldots
denotes the Catalan’s constant. In 1992, Mignotte
[
13
]
refined Ganelius’s result by establishing the stronger inequality

(2.7)

\mathcal{D}(P_{n})\leq C\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\,m^{+}\left(\frac{P_{n}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)}.

with the same constant
C=\sqrt{2\pi/k}=2.5619\ldots
. In 2019, Soundararajan
[
19
]
improved this result by establishing (
2.7
) with the constant
C=\frac{8}{\pi}=2.5464\ldots
. Only recently, Carneiro et al.
[
5
]
, where the author of this article is one of the co-authors,
improved the upper bound in (
2.7
) by establishing the following result.

Theorem 7

(Carneiro et al.
[
5
]
, (2021)8)
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
P_{n}(0)\neq 0
. Then we have

\mathcal{D}(P_{n})\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\,m^{+}\left(\frac{P_{n}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)}.

However, more recently, in a preprint, Shu and Wang
[
18
]
obtained a sharp discrepancy upper bound obtained in terms of the weaker norm. Precisely, they proved

Theorem 8

(Shu-Wang(2021)
[
18
]
)
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
P_{n}(0)\neq 0
. Then

(2.8)

\mathcal{D}(P_{n})\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}B_{\infty}(P_{n})}.

Note that in Theorem
7
and Theorem
8
, the computation of the asymptotic formulas for the associated norms is more complicated than the
p
-norms,
0<p<\infty
, and thus we need to use a trivial upper bound only. In the following proposition, we refine the Theorem
7
in terms of
p
-norm.

Proposition 9
.

Let
p\in(0,\infty)
and
P_{n}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
P_{n}(0)\neq 0
and
\|P_{n}\|_{p}\geq 1
. Then

(2.9)

\mathcal{D}(P_{n})\leq\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{B_{p}(P_{n})}{n}}.

Although, the leading constant in the upper bound in Proposition
9
may be larger in compare to (
2.8
), but we have
\|P_{n}\|_{p}\leq\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}
.

Conditionally, we obtain the following explicit result with the distribution of
P_{n}
on the unit circle.

Corollary 10
.

Let
P_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
such that
\|P_{n}\|_{p}\geq 1
. Then

\left|\tau_{n}(S(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(1-|E|)\log{(n+1)}+{e}^{-1}}{n}}.

Further, if
\theta
is drawn randomly from
(0,1)
, and
P_{n}(e(\theta))/\sqrt{n+1}
converges in distribution (and in moments) to a standard complex Gaussian. Then, for sufficiently large
n
, we have

\left|\tau_{n}(S(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|<\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\sqrt{\Big(1-\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{erf}^{2}{\Big(\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}\Big)}\Big)\log{(n+1)}+e^{-1}},

where
\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}dt
.

Now, we look at a particular class of polynomials and find the effective upper bound of the discrepancy.

Example 11

(Rudin–Shapiro polynomials)
.

The Rudin–Shapiro polynomials are defined recursively, as follows:
P_{0}(z)=1=Q_{0}(z)
and for
k=0,\,1,\,2,\,\ldots
,

P_{k+1}(z)=P_{k}(z)+z^{2^{k}}Q_{k}(z)\quad\mbox{and}\quad Q_{k+1}(z)=P_{k}(z)-z^{2^{k}}Q_{k}(z).

We see that
P_{k}
and
Q_{k}
are two Littlewood polynomial of degree
n-1
, where
n:=2^{k}
. By the Parseval formula,
\|P_{n}\|_{2}=2^{k/2}
. By using this we obtain a discrepancy upper bound in Proposition
9

\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(1-|E|)k\log{2}+e^{-1}}{n-1}}.

Recently, Rodgers
[
17
]
proved Saffari’s conjecture and this gives that
|E|\sim(2)^{-(k+1)}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, for sufficiently large
k
, the discrepancy upper bound in Proposition
9
is

(2.10)

\left|\tau_{n}(S(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sqrt{\Big(2-\frac{1}{n-1}\Big)\frac{k\log{2}+e^{-1}}{n-1}}.

2.3.
Annular discrepancy of zeros of a polynomial

Our next result directly applies angular discrepancy, drawing inspiration from recent works
[
14
,
16
,
15
]
. We establish an upper bound for the discrepancy between the counting measure of zeros of the polynomial
P_{n}
within an annular sector and the uniform measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. To provide context, let’s define the annulus
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}
and the annular sector
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\beta)
on the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
, where
0<\rho<1
:

(2.11)

\mathcal{A}_{\rho}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\rho<|z|<1/\rho\}\,\mbox{ and }\,\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\rho<|z|<1/\rho,\,0<\alpha\leq\arg{z}<\beta\leq 2\pi\}.

Then, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 12
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
|c_{n}c_{0}|\geq 1
and
\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\leq e^{\epsilon n}
for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
. Then for sufficiently large
n
(depending on
\epsilon
and
\rho
), there exists a suitably small
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
such that

\left|\tau_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|\leq\left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\epsilon^{\prime}\right)\sqrt{\frac{B_{p}(P_{n})}{n}}.

Further, for
P_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
and sufficiently large
n
(depending on
\epsilon
and
\rho
), there exists a suitably small
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}>0
such that

\left|\tau_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log{(n+1)}}{n}}(C+\epsilon^{\prime\prime}),

where
C
is given by

C=\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}(1-|E|+\frac{1}{e\log{(n+1)}}},\,\sqrt{2}\right\}\leq 1.974\ldots.

Note that if
|E|<1-\pi/4
then
C=\sqrt{2}
. The inequality
\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\leq e^{\epsilon n}
with suitably small
\epsilon
is essentially the condition for angular equidistribution zeros of
P_{n}
(see Section 1 in
[
19
]
).

3.
Proof of results

In order to prove the main results in Section
2
, i.e., Theorem
2
,
3
, and
5
, we need to establish Proposition
9
, Theorem
12
and Proposition

Proof of Proposition
9
.

Let us set
E^{c}=[0,1]\setminus E
. Then, by the hypothesis
\|P_{n}\|_{p}\geq 1
implies that
|E^{c}|\neq\emptyset
.
Thus, we write

m^{+}(P_{n})=\int_{E^{c}}\log{|P_{n}(e(\theta))|}d\theta=\frac{|E^{c}|}{p}\left(\frac{1}{|E^{c}|}\int_{E^{c}}\log{|P_{n}(e(\theta))|^{p}}d\theta\right).

By using the concavity of the logarithmic function along with the Jensen’s inequality on the integral above, we obtain

m^{+}(P_{n})\leq\frac{|E^{c}|}{p}\log\left(\frac{1}{|E^{c}|}\int_{E^{c}}{|P_{n}(e(\theta))|^{p}}d\theta\right)=\frac{|E^{c}|}{p}\log{\frac{1}{|E^{c}|}}+(1-|E|)\log{\|P_{n}\|_{p}}.

But
\max_{|E^{c}|\in(0,1]}|E^{c}|\log{\frac{1}{|E^{c}|}}=\frac{1}{e}
, and hence, we have

(3.1)

m^{+}(P_{n})\leq(1-|E|)\log{\|P_{n}\|_{p}}+\frac{1}{ep}.

The above argument is essentially a part of the proof
[
14
, Proposition 2.2]
. We conclude the result by combining (
3.1
) and Theorem
7
.
∎

Proof of Corollary
10
.

For
P_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{n}
, we have
\log{|c_{0}c_{n}|}=0
and
\|P_{n}\|_{2}=\sqrt{n+1}
and hence we obtain the first part of the corollary form Proposition
9
.

Since
P_{n}(e(\theta))/\sqrt{n+1}
converges to a standard complex Gaussian, for sufficiently large
n
we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{meas}\left\{\theta\in[0,\,1]\,:\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n+1}}P_{n}(e(\theta))\in R\right\}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{R}e^{-x^{2}-y^{2}}dx\,dy,

where
R
is any rectangle in
\mathbb{C}
. Let
R
be a square of sides
\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}
and centered at the origin, then by simple Euclidean geometry, we have

|E|>\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{R}e^{-x^{2}-y^{2}}dx\,dy=\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{erf}^{2}{\Big(\sqrt{\frac{2}{n+1}}\Big)}.

Hence, the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
12
.

We recall the annulus
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}
annular sector
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta)
from (
2.11

Proposition 13
.

Let
P_{n}(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}z^{k}
be a polynomial of degree
n
such that
|c_{n}c_{0}|\geq 1
and for
p\in(0,\,\infty)
, recall
B_{p}(P_{n})
from Proposition
9
. For any
\rho\in(0,\,1)
and
0\leq\alpha<\beta<2\pi
, we have

(3.2)

\tau_{n}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{\rho})\leq\frac{2}{n(1-\rho)}m\left(\frac{P_{n}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{n(1-\rho)}B_{p}(P_{n})

and

(3.3)

\left|\tau_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\min\left\{\frac{8}{\pi}B_{p}(P_{n}),\,B_{\infty}(P_{n})\right\}}+\frac{2}{n(1-\rho)}B_{p}(P_{n})

Assume the mild condition
\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\leq e^{\epsilon n}
for suitably small
\epsilon>0
. Then we must find a suitably small
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
such that
\sqrt{B_{p}(P_{n})/n}\leq\frac{1-\rho}{2}\epsilon^{\prime}
. Specifically, we can choose
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{2}{1-\rho}\sqrt{(1-|E|)\epsilon+\frac{1}{epn}}
, and this would be sufficiently small whenever
n\geq n_{\epsilon,\rho}
. Thus, for
p\in(0,\,\infty)
, (
3.3
) of Proposition
13
gives

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}\min\left\{\frac{8}{\pi}B_{p}(P_{n}),\,B_{\infty}(P_{n})\right\}}+\epsilon^{\prime}\sqrt{\frac{B_{p}(P_{n})}{n}}\leq\left(\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\epsilon^{\prime}\right)\sqrt{\frac{B_{p}(P_{n})}{n}}.

For
p=2
we have
\|P_{n}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{n+1}
for
P_{n}\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
. This gives
B_{2}(P_{n})=\frac{1}{2}((1-|E|)\log{(n+1)}+e^{-1})
. Also, we get the upper bound
B_{\infty}(P_{n})\leq\log{(n+1)}
. Thus, (
3.3
) of Proposition
13
gives

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\log{(n+1)}}{n}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\min\left\{\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}(1-|E|+(e\log{(n+1)})^{-1})},\,\sqrt{2}\right\}+\bigg(1-|E|+\frac{1}{e\log{(n+1)}}\bigg)\epsilon^{\prime}\right).

Now, we choose
\epsilon^{\prime\prime}=\bigg(1-|E|+\frac{1}{e\log{(n+1)}}\bigg)\epsilon^{\prime}
in above to complete the proof.
∎

Let
D_{\rho}

\displaystyle\tau_{n}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{\rho})=\tau_{n}(\mathbb{C}\setminus D_{\frac{1}{\rho}})+\tau_{n}(D_{\rho}).

[

in above equality we obtain the first inequality of (

(3.4)

\tau_{n}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{\rho})\leq\frac{2}{n(1-\rho)}m\left(\frac{P_{n}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right).

To prove the second inequality of (

), at first, we recall
E
from Proposition

and we have

) and using the condition

).

To prove the inequality (
3.3
), we are following the same strategy as in the proof of Proposition 2.1 in

]
. That is, we write

By using triangle inequality and the inequality

Proof of Theorem
2
.

For the polynomial
P_{n}
, following (
2.8
) we get

(3.6)

\displaystyle\Big|n\tau_{n}(S(\alpha,\,\beta))-\frac{\beta-\alpha}{2\pi}n\Big|\leq\sqrt{2nB_{\infty}(P_{n})},

where
B_{\infty}(P_{n})=\log{\left(\frac{\|P_{n}\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)}
.
For
0<\alpha<1
, Proposition
13
gives

(3.7)

\displaystyle n\tau_{n}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{1-\alpha})\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}m\left(\frac{P_{n}}{\sqrt{|c_{0}c_{n}|}}\right)\leq\frac{2}{\alpha}B_{\infty}(P_{n}).

Now, set
\delta_{n}=\frac{\pi a}{\sqrt{n}}\left(2B_{\infty}(P_{n})\right)^{\theta}
, where
\theta\in\big[\frac{1}{2},\,1\big]
,
a>1
is a positive real number and
z_{0}=e^{i\varphi}
for some
\varphi\in[0,\,2\pi)
. From (
3.6
) we have

\displaystyle n\tau_{n}(\mathbb{C}\setminus S(\varphi-\delta_{n},\,\varphi+\delta_{n}))\leq n-\frac{\delta_{n}n}{\pi}+\sqrt{2nB_{\infty}(P_{n})}\leq n-(a-1)\sqrt{n}\left(2B_{\infty}(P_{n})\right)^{\theta}.

The sector
S(\varphi-\delta_{n},\,\varphi+\delta_{n})
contains atleast
(a-1)\sqrt{n}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}
zeros of
P_{n}
. Thus, the number of zeros in
\mathcal{A}_{1-\alpha}\cap S(\varphi-\delta_{n},\,\varphi+\delta_{n})
is atleast

\displaystyle(a-1)\sqrt{n}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}-\frac{2}{\alpha}B_{\infty}(P_{n}).

Taking
\alpha=\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{1-\theta}
on the above we get

n\tau_{n}(\mathcal{A}_{1-\alpha}\cap S(\varphi-\delta_{n},\,\varphi+\delta_{n}))\geq(a-b-1)\sqrt{n}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}.

Without lose of generality, if we take
z_{0}=1
, we find a circle of radius

r=\max\{\operatorname{dist}\big(1,(1-\alpha)e^{i\delta_{n}}\big),\,\operatorname{dist}\big(1,(1-\alpha)^{-1}e^{i\delta_{n}}\big)\},

which contains the intersection
\mathcal{A}_{1-\alpha}\cap S(-\delta_{n},\,\delta_{n})
and simple calculation gives

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\max\Big\{\Big(\frac{4\sin^{2}(\delta_{n}/2)}{1-\alpha}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}},\Big(\alpha^{2}+4(1-\alpha)\sin^{2}(\delta_{n}/2)\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{4\sin^{2}(\delta_{n}/2)}{1-\alpha}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\frac{\delta_{n}^{2}}{1-\alpha}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The last inequality uses the inequality
\sin{x}\leq x
for any non-negative
x
with
0\leq x\leq 1/2
.
Since
\delta_{n}\leq 1
and
1-\theta\leq\theta
, the definition of
\delta_{n}
gives

\alpha\leq\frac{b}{\sqrt{n}}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}\leq\frac{b}{\pi a}

and hence we get

(3.8)

\displaystyle r\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}\pi a\sqrt{\frac{\pi a}{\pi a-b}+\frac{b^{2}}{(\pi a-b)^{2}}}.

Note that if we choose
a=2.195
and
b=0.195
then we have

and the inequality (
3.8
) reduce to

r<\frac{7}{\sqrt{n}}(2B_{\infty}(P_{n}))^{\theta}=\gamma_{n}.

.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

At first, we want to compute the length of an arc of the unit circle that lies inside the circle of radius
\gamma_{n}
, whose center is at some point of the unit circle. Let
O_{1}
be such point and
O
be the center of the unit circle. Also, denote the intersecting points of two circles by
A
and
B
. Let
\theta
denote the angle
\angle{AOB}
and
\Gamma
be the arc length of
AB
of the unit circle. Since
O_{1}
is the middle point of the arc
AB
on the unit circle, we have
\angle{O_{1}OA}={\theta}/{2}
. Thus, the arc length of
O_{1}A=\theta/2
. Now, the area of the triangle,
\triangle O_{1}OA=\frac{1}{2}\sin{(\theta/2)}
, since the side
O_{1}A=\gamma_{n}
. On the other hand, by the Heron’s formula, we get

\displaystyle\triangle O_{1}OA=[s(s-1)^{2}(s-\gamma_{n})]^{\frac{1}{2}}

where
s
is the semi-perimeter of the triangle, i.e.,
s=1+\gamma_{n}/2
. As a result, we get

\displaystyle\triangle O_{1}OA=\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{n}^{2}}{4}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\sin{(\theta/2)}.

Since the
\Gamma=1\cdot\theta=\theta
, we get

\displaystyle\Gamma=2\arcsin\left(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\sqrt{4-\gamma_{n}^{2}}\right).

Now, in order to make the unit disk a gear wheel, we have to draw circles consecutively of radius
\gamma_{n}
with the center at the boundary of the unit disk such that the gap between any two consecutive small circles is
\delta\Gamma
, for some
\delta\in[0,1]
. Then, we have to remove the region of the disk that is inside of each of the small circles.
Thus, the total number of the teeth of the gear wheel equals the total number of gaps between consecutive circles drawn with the center at the boundary of the unit disk. Let
G
be the such number.
Then

\displaystyle G=\frac{2\pi}{\Gamma(1+\delta)}=\frac{\pi}{1+\delta}\left(\arcsin\left(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\sqrt{4-\gamma_{n}^{2}}\right)\right)^{-1}.

By Theorem
2
, the number of zeros of
P_{n}(z)
inside the gear wheel is at most

(3.11)

\displaystyle n-G\sqrt{n}(2B(P_{n}))^{\theta}=n-\frac{\pi}{1+\delta}\sqrt{n}(2B(P_{n}))^{\theta}\left(\arcsin\left(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\sqrt{4-\gamma_{n}^{2}}\right)\right)^{-1},

where
\gamma_{n}=\frac{9}{\sqrt{n}}(2B(P_{n}))^{\theta}\leq\frac{1}{2}
and width of each tooth is

\displaystyle 2\delta\arcsin\left(\frac{\gamma_{n}}{2}\sqrt{4-\gamma_{n}^{2}}\right).

A sharp version of Shafer-Fink’s inequality to bounding
\arcsin
-function by Malešević in
[
12
, Theorem 1.3]
gives

\displaystyle\arcsin{x}\leq\frac{\pi x}{2+(\pi-2)\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},\quad 0\leq x\leq 1.

Using this inequality in (
3.11
), we get the number of zeros of
P_{n}(z)
inside the gear wheel at most

\displaystyle n-\frac{\pi}{1+\delta}\frac{\sqrt{n}(2B(P_{n}))^{\theta}}{\gamma_{n}}\left(1-\frac{\pi-2}{2\pi}\gamma_{n}^{2}\right)\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{\gamma_{n}^{2}}{4}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=n-\frac{\pi n}{7(1+\delta)}\left(1-\frac{\pi-2}{2\pi}\gamma_{n}^{2}\right)\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{\gamma_{n}^{2}}{4}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

where the last passage uses the definition of
\gamma_{n}
. Since
\gamma_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2}
we get that

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\pi-2}{2\pi}\gamma_{n}^{2}\right)\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{\gamma_{n}^{2}}{4}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}>0.97.

Thus, the result follows.
∎

In our results, we establish bounds for quantities in terms of
