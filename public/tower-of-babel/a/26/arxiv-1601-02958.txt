Measurable equidecompositions for group actions with an expansion property
Łukasz Grabowski, András Máthé, Oleg Pikhurko

Abstract
Given an action of a group Γ \Gamma on a measure space Ω \Omega , we provide a sufficient criterion under which two sets A , B ⊆ Ω A,B\subseteq\Omega are measurably equidecomposable , i.e., A A can be partitioned into finitely many measurable pieces which can be rearranged using some elements of Γ \Gamma to form a partition of B B . In particular, we prove that every bounded measurable subset of ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} , n ⩾ 3 n\geqslant 3 , with non-empty interior is measurably equidecomposable to a ball via isometries. The analogous result also holds for some other spaces, such as the sphere or the hyperbolic space of dimension n ⩾ 2 n\geqslant 2 . Keywords. Banach-Tarski Paradox, finitely additive mean, local spectral gap, measurable equidecomposition

1
Introduction

In this paper, we present a general sufficient criterion for equidecomposing a given pair of sets using measurable pieces.
In order to state quickly some concrete results obtainable by our method, we discuss first the Euclidean space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
, which is probably the most important special case.

1.1
Euclidean space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}

Let us call two subsets
A
and
B
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

(set-theoretically) equidecomposable
if it is possible to find a partition of
A
into finitely many pieces and rearrange these pieces using orientation-preserving isometries to form a partition of
B
. The most famous result about equidecomposable sets is known as the
Banach-Tarski paradox
: in

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the unit ball and two disjoint copies of the unit ball are equidecomposable. It is a special case of the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(Banach and Tarski
[
3
]
)
.

When
n\geqslant 3
, any two bounded subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
with non-empty interiors are equidecomposable.

An earlier result of Banach
[
2
]
gives that, when
n\leqslant 2
, equidecomposable subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
which are measurable have the same Lebesgue measure.
In view of this, Tarski
[
45
]
formulated the following problem, known as
Tarski’s circle squaring
:
is a disk in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
equidecomposable to a square of the same area?
Some 65 years later, Laczkovich
[
21
]
showed that Tarski’s circle squaring is possible.

There are various results which imply the impossibility of equidecompositions when additional regularity of the pieces is required. Examples include Dehn’s theorem
[
9
]
solving Hilbert’s third problem and the result of Dubins, Hirsch and Karush
[
12
]
which shows that circle squaring is not possible with Jordan domains.

On the other hand, until recently there have been very few general positive results on the existence of
measurable equidecompositions
(where each piece has to be Lebesgue measurable), although a related problem of measurable equidecompositions with countably many pieces was studied already by Banach and Tarski
[
3
, Théorème 42]
.
(See
[
50
, Section 11.3]
for a survey of “countable equidecompositions”.)

Some progress has been recently made for sets with “small boundary”. Namely, if the boundaries of
A,B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 1
, have upper Minkowski dimension strictly less than
n
while the sets have the same positive Lebesgue measure, then
A
and
B
are equidecomposable with pieces that are both Lebesgue and Baire measurable (Grabowski, Máthé and Pikhurko
[
15
]
), Jordan measurable (Máthé, Noel and Pikhurko
[
33
]
), or Borel if the sets
A
and
B
are also Borel (Marks and Unger
[
32
]
).

In this paper we give a general criterion for measurable equidecomposability, which in particular applies to
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
for
n\geqslant 3
. An important feature of the present work when compared with
[
15
,
32
,
33
]
is
that for a large natural class of sets
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 3
, we are able to completely characterise sets
B
which are measurably equidecomposable to
A
. Furthermore, we do not need to assume anything about the boundaries of the sets. Given
n
, let
{{\mathcal{C}}}
consist of all bounded sets
A
in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
such that some (equivalently, every bounded) non-empty open set can be covered by finitely many sets obtained by applying orientation-preserving isometries to
A
. The
following theorem is a direct consequence of our more general Corollary
1.12
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
and let
A\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
belong to
{{\mathcal{C}}}
and be Lebesgue measurable.
Then a set
B\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is measurably equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
B
belongs to
{{\mathcal{C}}}
, is Lebesgue measurable, and has the same Lebesgue measure as
A
.

In particular, for
n\geqslant 3
, every two bounded measurable subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
with non-empty interior and of the same Lebesgue measure are measurably equidecomposable.

Remarks 1.3
.

1.

The assumption that
n\geqslant 3
is needed in Theorem
1.2
. For example, in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
(resp.
{\mathbbm{R}}^{1}
), Laczkovich in
[
24
, Theorem 3]
(resp.
[
22
, Theorem 3.3]
) constructed continuum many Jordan domains with boundaries differentiable everywhere (resp. bounded sets that are countable unions of intervals) that all have measure 1 but no two of these sets are set-theoretically equidecomposable.

2.

Theorem
1.2
can be combined with the results of Dougherty and Foreman
[
10
]
(or recent generalisations by Marks and Unger
[
31
]
) to show that if the sets
A
and
B
in the theorem also have the property of Baire, then the obtained pieces can be additionally required to have the property of Baire, see Corollary
1.10
3
.

3.

Various questions were posed early in the 20th century regarding the axiomatic theory of the Lebesgue measure, see e.g.
[
50
, Chapter 11]
.
A remaining key open problem is whether, for
n\geqslant 3
, every
mean
(that is, a finitely additive and isometry invariant function)

\kappa:{{\mathcal{A}}}\to[0,\infty)

where
{{\mathcal{A}}}
is the family of all bounded Borel subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
,
is a constant multiple of the Lebesgue measure. In a breakthrough, Margulis
[
30
]
showed that the answer is in the affirmative if we take for
{{\mathcal{A}}}
the (larger) family of all bounded measurable sets. This resolved the famous Banach-Ruziewicz Problem whose origins can be traced to the 1904 monograph of Lebesgue
[
25
]
(see the discussion in
[
50
, Page 267]
). The special case
\Omega={\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
of our Theorem
1.14

reduces the family
{{\mathcal{A}}}
in the result of Margulis to those sets that additionally have the property of Baire. In brief, the connection comes from the observation that an equidecomposition between
A
and
B
with all pieces in
{{\mathcal{A}}}
gives a certificate that
\kappa(A)=\kappa(B)
.

4.

An upper bound on the number of pieces needed in Theorem
1.2
can be derived from our proof. For example,
our estimates indicate that one can measurably equidecompose a ball into a cube in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
using at most
20^{10^{10}}
pieces, see Remark
7.5
.
On the other hand, we do not know any non-trivial lower bounds for the number of necessary pieces.

1.2
A general criterion for measurable equidecompositions

In this section, we present our general criterion for measurable equidecomposability. We start by noting the following assumption that applies to the whole paper.

Assumption 1.4
.

Assume that
\Gamma
is a group,
(\Omega,\tau)
is a Polish topological space,
{\mathcal{B}}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra of
(\Omega,\tau)
, and
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
is a (left) action by Borel automorphisms.

Furthermore, whenever we mention a measure
\mu
on
\Omega
, we additionally assume that the following holds.

Assumption 1.5
.

Assume that
\mu
is a

\sigma
-finite measure on
(\Omega,{\mathcal{B}})
which is non-zero on all non-empty open sets and that the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
preserves the measure
\mu
.

We need a few definitions first. The result of the action of
\gamma\,{\in}\,\Gamma
on
x\,{\in}\,\Omega
is denoted by
\gamma.x:=a(\gamma,x)
. Similarly, when
U\,{\subseteq}\,\Omega
we put
\gamma.U:=\{\gamma.u\colon u\in U\}
, and when
T\subseteq\Gamma
we put
T.U:=\cup_{\gamma\in T}\gamma.U
.
We say that a set
A\subseteq\Omega

covers
another set
B\subseteq\Omega
if there is a finite set
T\subseteq\Gamma
such that
B\subseteq T.A
.

Let us also define various families of subsets of
\Omega
that we will use. The completion of
{\mathcal{B}}
with respect to
\mu
is denoted by
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu}
. Elements of the
\sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu}
are called
(Lebesgue) measurable
.
Let
{{\mathcal{T}}}

denote the
\sigma
-algebra consisting of sets with the property of Baire.
Let
{{\mathcal{C}}}
consist of all subsets of
\Omega
that have compact closure and cover a non-empty open set. While
{{\mathcal{C}}}
is closed under finite unions, it is not an algebra. Clearly, the
\sigma
-algebras
{{\mathcal{B}}}
and
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}
are invariant (under the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
). If, for example,
\Gamma
acts by homeomorphisms, then
{{\mathcal{T}}}
and
{{\mathcal{C}}}
are invariant too.

Given an
a
-invariant set family
{{\mathcal{A}}}\subseteq 2^{\Omega}
, two subsets
A,B\subseteq\Omega
are called
{{\mathcal{A}}}
-equidecomposable (with respect to the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
)
if for some
m\,{\in}\,{\mathbbm{N}}
there exist group elements
\gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{m}\in\Gamma
and a partition
A=A_{1}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup A_{m}
with each piece belonging to
{{\mathcal{A}}}
such that
\gamma_{1}.A_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{m}.A_{m}
partition
B
.
If
{{\mathcal{A}}}
is equal to
{{\mathcal{B}}}
,
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}
,
{{\mathcal{T}}}
and
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}}
, then an
{{\mathcal{A}}}
-equidecomposition is called respectively

Borel
,
measurable
,
Baire
and
Baire-Lebesgue
. If
{{\mathcal{A}}}=2^{\Omega}
, then we usually omit any reference to
{{\mathcal{A}}}
;
however, if we need to emphasize that no restriction is imposed on the pieces, then we will use the term

set-theoretic equidecomposition
.

Given
\mu
, we say that
A

essentially covers

B
if
A
covers
B\setminus N
for some null set
N
.
If
A,B\subseteq\Omega
are measurable sets and there exist null sets
N
and
N^{\prime}
such that
A\setminus N
and
B\setminus N^{\prime}
are Borel equidecomposable, then we say that
A
and
B
are
essentially Borel equidecomposable
. It is easy to show (see Proposition
3.4
1
) that the existence of an essential Borel equidecomposition together with a set-theoretic equidecomposition is equivalent to the existence of a measurable equidecomposition.

We say that
C\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\mu}
is a
domain of expansion
if
0<\mu(C)<\infty
and for every real
\eta>0
there is a finite set
R\subseteq\Gamma
such that
for all measurable
sets
Y\subseteq C
we have

\mu((R.Y)\cap C)\geqslant\min\left((1-\eta)\,\mu(C),\,\frac{\mu(Y)}{\eta}\right).

Informally speaking, this states
that all measurable subsets of
C
“uniformly expand” inside
C
under a suitable finite subset
R
of
\Gamma
, unless their
R
-images cover most of
C
.
This property is crucial in the following general criterion, based on a result of Lyons and Nazarov
[
28
]
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
apply. Let
A\subseteq\Omega
be a domain of expansion.
Then the following holds.

1.

A subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is essentially Borel equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
A
and
B
essentially cover each other,
B
is measurable and
\mu(A)=\mu(B)
.

2.

A subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is measurably equidecomposable to
A
if and only if they are set-theoretically equidecomposable,
B
is measurable and
\mu(A)=\mu(B)
.

1.3
Paradoxical actions

Equidecompositions have also been considered for spaces other than the Euclidean space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
, often
with the aim of establishing “paradoxes” and concluding that certain kinds of measures do not exist. We refer the reader to
the excellent monograph on the subject by Tomkowicz and Wagon
[
50
]
.
Having a rich family of set-theoretic equidecompositions will be very useful
when applying Theorem
1.6
2
. For the purposes of this paper,
we make the following (non-standard) definition.

Definition 1.7
.

Under Assumption
1.4
, the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
called
paradoxical
if all the following properties hold.

1.

The topological space
\Omega
is locally compact.

2.

For every
\gamma\in\Gamma
and every compact
C\subseteq\Omega
,
the closure of
\gamma.C
is compact.

3.

Any two subsets of
\Omega
with compact closure and non-empty interior are equidecomposable.

Of course, the crucial part of this definition is the last property.
The other (mild technical) properties will be needed in some of our arguments.
For example, Property
2
implies that the family
{{\mathcal{C}}}
(which consists of sets with compact closure that cover a non-empty open set) is invariant.
As a small detour, let us note the following general proposition, whose
second part relies on a powerful result of Marks and Unger
[
31
]
.

Proposition 1.8
.

Let
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
be paradoxical
and let
A\in{{\mathcal{C}}}
.
Then the following holds.

1.

A subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
B\in{{\mathcal{C}}}
.

2.

Suppose additionally that
A\in{{\mathcal{T}}}
and that the family of all meager subsets of
\Omega
is
a
-invariant. Then a subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is
{{\mathcal{T}}}
-equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
B\in{{\mathcal{C}}}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}}
.

Note that if a Borel bijection preserves meager sets, then it is also preserves sets with the property of Baire, so the family
{{\mathcal{T}}}
in Proposition
1.8
2
is invariant.

Let us now look at some concrete examples of actions known to be paradoxical.
Let
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
denote the unit sphere in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with respect to the Euclidean metric. Let
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
denote the
n
-dimensional hyperbolic space with the hyperbolic distance; see Section
6.3
for all formal definitions.
Let
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
,
\operatorname{SO}(n)
and
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})
denote the group of orientation-preserving isometries of respectively
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}

and
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
.
For each of these groups, we consider its natural action on the corresponding space.
Also, let
G_{2}
be the subgroup of affine bijections of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
generated by
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{Z}})
(the linear maps given by
2\times 2
matrices with determinant 1 and all entries in
{\mathbbm{Z}}
) and all translations (that is, maps of the form
x\mapsto x+u
for some vector
u\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
).
This group naturally acts on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
.

Hausdorff
[
16
]
showed that a “third” of
{\mathbbm{S}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
, is equidecomposable to a “half” of
{\mathbbm{S}}^{n}
, which was enough to his intended application, namely, the non-existence of
a mean defined on all subsets of the sphere. The paradoxicality of
\operatorname{SO}(n+1)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
, as stated
in Definition
1.7
3
, was established by
Banach and Tarski
[
3
]
.
The paradoxicality of
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{n})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
for
n\geqslant 3
is the content of
Theorem
1.1
(of Banach and Tarski
[
3
]
) while, as we mentioned already, this fails for
n\leqslant 2
by the results of Banach
[
2
]
. One can ask
what happens if we allow a richer
group of transformations of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
. The paradoxicality of
G_{2}\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
was established in the influential paper of von Neumann
[
37
]
that introduced the concept of a non-amenable group.
In fact, some (explicit) smaller subgroups of
G_{2}
suffice here, see Mycielski
[
35
, Corollary 5]
(compare also with Wagon
[
52
, Theorem 2]
). Laczkovich
[
23
]

showed that the natural action
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
is paradoxical. (See also Tomkowicz
[
48
]
for a strengthening of this result.)
As noted by Mycielski
[
34
, Page 143]
, the paradoxicality of

\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{H}}_{n}
,
n\geqslant 3
, can be established by observing that the subgroup of isometries that fix a point of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
acts on its every non-trivial orbit
in the same way as
\operatorname{SO}(n)
acts on
{\mathbbm{S}}^{n-1}
. Mycielski
[
34
]
showed that the action
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{2})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{H}}_{2}
is also paradoxical. (Some small gaps in Mycielski’s proof were
fixed by Mycielski and Tomkowicz
[
36
]
; the proof, with some further modifications, can also be found in
[
50
, Theorem 4.17]
.)
We also refer the reader to Tomkowicz
[
49
]
for a general short proof
of the paradoxicality of many of the above actions.

Let us collect these five (probably, best known) examples of paradoxical actions together. For later reference, we also state the standard invariant measure
\mu
on
\Omega
in each case.

Example 1.9

(Some known paradoxical actions
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
)
.

1.

\Gamma=\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with the Lebesgue measure,
n\geqslant 3
,

2.

\Gamma=\operatorname{SO}(n)
and
\Omega={\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
with the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure,
n\geqslant 3
,

3.

\Gamma=G_{2}
and
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with the Lebesgue measure,

4.

\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})
and
\Omega={\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
with the Lebesgue measure,

5.

\Gamma=\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})
and
\Omega={\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
with the measure defined by (
6.2
),
n\geqslant 2
.

1.4
Expanding actions

Let us call an action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
satisfying Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5

expanding (with respect to the measure
\mu
)

if every set in
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{C}}}

is a domain of expansion.

This notion is of interest because of the following corollary that can be derived with some work from Theorem
1.6
and Proposition
1.8
.

Corollary 1.10
.

In addition to Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
, assume that the action is paradoxical and expanding. Let
A\in{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{C}}}
. Then the following statements hold.

1.

A subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is essentially Borel equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
B\in{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}
,
\mu(B)=\mu(A)
and
there is a null set
N
with
B\bigtriangleup N\in{{\mathcal{C}}}
.

2.

A subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is measurably equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
B\in{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{C}}}
and
\mu(B)=\mu(A)
.

3.

Suppose additionally that
A
has the property of Baire and
that the action preserves the family of meager sets. Then a subset
B\subseteq\Omega
is Baire-Lebesgue equidecomposable to
A
if and only if
B\in{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{C}}}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}}
and
\mu(B)=\mu(A)
.

On the other hand, many natural actions can be shown to be expanding:

Theorem 1.11
.

Each of the actions in Example
1.9
is expanding.

Each these actions is paradoxical and acts by homeomorphisms (in particular, preserving meager sets), so it satisfies all conclusions of Proposition
1.8
and Corollary
1.10
.
It is remarkable that one can obtain exact characterisations for so many types of equidecompositions under rather general assumptions (that in particular include all actions in Example
1.9
). We did not see such a characterisation anywhere in the previous literature, surprisingly not even for set-theoretic equidecompositions in any case of Example
1.9
. In fact, Tomkowicz and Wagon
[
50
, Page 176]
write:
“It is not completely clear which subsets
E
of
{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
are
\operatorname{SO}_{3}({\mathbbm{R}})
-equidecomposable with all of
{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
.”
One answer to this question (exactly those sets that cover the whole of
{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
) is given by Proposition
1.8
1
.

As an illustration, here is one (easy to state) direct consequence of Corollary
1.10
2
and Theorem
1.11
.

Corollary 1.12
.

For each action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
of Example
1.9
, every two measurable sets
A,B\in{{\mathcal{C}}}
of the same measure are equidecomposable with measurable pieces.

1.5
Connections to (local) spectral gap and finitely additive means

It is not hard to show (see Proposition
5.3
)
that if
\mu
is a finite measure and the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
has spectral gap, then the action is expanding.
Thus the case of
\operatorname{SO}(n)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{n-1}
,
n\geqslant 3
, of Theorem
1.11
can be derived from the known spectral gap results established by Drinfel’d
[
11
]
, Margulis
[
29
]
, and Sullivan
[
44
]
.

The case of the infinite measure space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
in Theorem
1.11
is not directly covered by the above approach. However, we were able to derive it from the spectral gap of
\operatorname{SO}(3)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
, using lengthy but rather elementary arguments. Later, we became aware of the general powerful results by Boutonnet, Ioana and Salehi Golsefidy
[
6
]
that can be used here.
Let us discuss this connection in general.

Let
X\subseteq\Omega
be a measurable set of finite positive measure. For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
, we define

\|f\|_{2,X}:=(\int_{X}|f(x)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x))^{1/2}
.
We say that an action
\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
satisfying Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
has
local spectral gap with respect to
X
if there exist a finite set
Q\subseteq\Gamma
and a constant
c>0
such that for any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
with
\int_{X}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0
we have

\|f\|_{2,X}\leqslant c\sum_{\gamma\in Q}\|\gamma.f-f\|_{2,X},

(1.1)

where
\gamma.f:\Omega\to{\mathbbm{R}}
is defined by
(\gamma.f)(x):=f(\gamma^{-1}.x)
,
x\in\Omega
.

This notion is of interest to us because, as we will show in Lemma
5.2
,
the action has local spectral gap with respect to a set
X\subseteq\Omega
if and only if
X
is a domain of expansion.
Modulo Lemma
5.2
, Boutonnet et al
[
6
, Theorem A]
presented a sufficient condition for the action to be expanding (stated here as Theorem
5.1
). While it seems that Theorem
5.1
can be used to derive the full Theorem
1.11
, we use it here for
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and the hyperbolic space only,
presenting more direct proofs of the other cases of Theorem
1.11
.

We decided to include also our initial proof of Theorem
1.11
for
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{n})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
. As we have already mentioned, it is rather elementary, apart from using the spectral gap property of
\operatorname{SO}(3)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
.

Lemma 2.1

Under Assumptions

be a countable subset and let

be Borel. Then there are Borel sets

In brief, we have to pick in a Borel way, for every edge of

For completeness, we include a more direct proof.

Let

For example, each auxiliary set

\{x\in\Omega\colon\gamma.x=\beta.x\}=\pi_{1}(\,\{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}\colon y=\gamma.x\}\cap\{(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}\colon y=\beta.x\}\,)

\eta:=\varepsilon/t^{2}
and

\eta

We claim that

is an

Otherwise, we have

Thus,

2.

the odd-indexed vertices
x_{1},x_{3},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\in B
are pairwise distinct;

3.

for every
i\in[\ell]
, we have that
(x_{i-1},x_{i})\in E\setminus M
if
i
is odd and
(x_{i},x_{i-1})\in M
if
i
is even.

Since
M
is a matching and
x_{0}\in A
is unmatched, Item
2
implies that the even-indexed vertices
x_{0},x_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\in A
are also pairwise distinct.
This definition works also when
A\cap B\not=\emptyset
, since the parity of the position in
P
determines the part a vertex is assigned to. If we are to work with the unordered graph
\tilde{G}
as defined in (
2.1
), then the corresponding definition is that

\tilde{P}:=((x_{0},-1),(x_{1},1),\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,(x_{\ell},(-1)^{\ell+1}))

is a path in
\tilde{G}
that starts with an unmatched vertex and whose edges alternate between
\tilde{E}\setminus\tilde{M}
and
\tilde{M}
. The
length
of
P
is
\ell
, the number of edges.

An
augmenting path (starting at
A
)
is an alternating path
P=(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{\ell})
of odd length
\ell\geqslant 1
such that
x_{\ell}\in B\setminus\pi_{2}(M)
.
The
augmentation
of
M
along
P

is the matching
M^{\prime}:=M\bigtriangleup E(P)
which is obtained by taking the symmetric difference between
M
and

E(P):=\{(x_{0},x_{1}),(x_{2},x_{1}),(x_{2},x_{3}),\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,(x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell})\}.

In order words, we modify
M
by including
(x_{i-1},x_{i})
for all odd
i\in[\ell]
and removing
(x_{i},x_{i-1})
for all even
i\in[\ell]
. Note that the augmented matching
M^{\prime}
covers two new vertices:
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})=\pi_{1}(M)\sqcup\{x_{0}\}
and
\pi_{2}(M^{\prime})=\pi_{2}(M)\sqcup\{x_{\ell}\}
.

Suppose that
E\subseteq E_{R}
for some fixed countable set
R\subseteq\Gamma
, where
E_{R}
is defined by (
2.3
). Then a
Borel augmenting family
is
a tuple
(U,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{\ell})
, where
U
is a Borel subset of
A
and

\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{\ell}
are elements of
R
(with repetitions allowed) so that (i) for every
x\in U
the sequence

P_{x,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{\ell}}:=(x,\,\beta_{1}.x,\,\beta_{2}\beta_{1}.x,\,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\,\beta_{\ell}\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\beta_{2}\beta_{1}.x)

(3.1)

is an augmenting path for
M
; (ii) for distinct
x,y\in U
the corresponding augmenting paths
\tilde{P}_{x,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{\ell}}
and
\tilde{P}_{y,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{\ell}}
in
\tilde{G}
are vertex-disjoint. Informally speaking, a Borel augmenting family is a Borel collection of vertex-disjoint augmenting paths in
\tilde{G}
. The
length
of such a family is
\ell
, the number of edges in each path.
The
augmentation

M^{\prime}
of
M
along a Borel augmenting family
(U,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{\ell})
is obtained from
M
by augmenting along the paths in (
3.1
) for all
x\in U
. (Since the paths are vertex-disjoint in
\tilde{G}
, all these augmentations can be done in parallel.) Note that
M^{\prime}
is a matching,
\pi_{1}(M^{\prime})=\pi_{1}(M)\sqcup U
and
\pi_{2}(M^{\prime})=\pi_{2}(M^{\prime})\sqcup(\beta_{\ell}\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\beta_{1}.U)
. Also,
M^{\prime}
is Borel if
M
is.

By swapping the roles of
A
and
B
, we can define in the obvious way an
alternating path starting at
B
. Note that there is no need to define an augmenting path starting at
B
: by reversing the order of vertices we can consider augmenting paths starting at
A
only.

3.2
Proof of the result of Lyons and Nazarov on measurable matchings

In this section we provide all details of the result of Lyons and Nazarov
[
28
, Remark 2.6]
(stated as Theorem
3.3
here) which gives a sufficient condition for the existence of a Borel a.e.-perfect matching. We need the following result first, which does not use measure and, in fact, was proved in
[
14
]
for arbitrary (not necessarily bipartite) Borel graphs.

Lemma 3.1

(Elek and Lippner
[
14
]
)
.

Under Assumption
1.4
, let
G=(A,B,E)
be a Borel locally finite bipartite graph with
E\subseteq E_{R}

for some countable
R\subseteq\Gamma
. (In particular,
A
and
B
are Borel subsets of
\Omega
.)
Then there are Borel matchings

M_{0}=\emptyset
,
M_{1},M_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq E

such that

1.

for every
i\geqslant 1
, the matching
M_{i}
admits no augmenting path of length at most
2i-1
;

2.

for every
i\geqslant 0
, there is a countable sequence of Borel matchings
K_{0},K_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq E
such that (a)
K_{0}=M_{i}
; (b) for every
j\geqslant 1
,
K_{j}
is the augmentation of
K_{j-1}
along some Borel augmenting family of length at most
2i+1
; (c) every
(x,y)\in E
eventually belongs to either all or none of the matchings
K_{j}
; (d)
M_{i+1}=\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}\cap_{t=j}^{\infty}K_{t}
.

Proof.

The first item is
[
14
, Proposition 1.1]
. The second item follows directly from the proof of
[
14
, Proposition 1.1]
.
In brief, the proof, when adopted to our setting, proceeds as follows.

Suppose that
i\geqslant 0
and we have already constructed matchings
M_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,M_{i}
. By a result of Kechris et al
[
19
]
, there is a Borel vertex colouring
\phi:A\to{\mathbbm{N}}
such that
\phi(x)\not=\phi(y)
for every two distinct vertices
x,y\in A
with
(x,-1),(y,-1)\in\tilde{A}
being at distance at most
4i+2
in
\tilde{G}
. Fix some sequence
(\mathbold{x}_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
where each
\mathbold{x}_{j}
belongs to
X:=\cup_{t=1}^{i+1}R^{2t-1}\times{\mathbbm{N}}
so that each element of
X
appears as
\mathbold{x}_{j}
for infinitely many choices of
j
.

Define
K_{0}:=M_{i}
. Suppose that
j\geqslant 1
and we have already defined
K_{j-1}
. Let
\mathbold{x}_{j}=((\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{2t-1}),c)
with
t\in[i+1]
. Let
U
consist of those
x\in A
such that
P_{x}:=P_{x,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{2t-1}}
, as defined in (
3.1
), is an augmenting path for
K_{j-1}
and
\phi(x)=c
. The set
U
is Borel. Also, for distinct
x,y\in U
, the paths
\tilde{P}_{x}
and
\tilde{P}_{y}
are vertex disjoint for otherwise

(x,-1)
and
(y,-1)
are at distance at most
4i+2
in
\tilde{G}
and satisfy
\phi(x)=\phi(y)=c
, contradicting the choice of the colouring
\phi
.
Thus
(U,\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{2t-1})
is a Borel augmenting family for
K_{j-1}
. Define
K_{j}
to be the augmentation of
K_{j-1}
along
(U,\beta_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\beta_{2t-1})
. Increase
j
and repeat.

Each augmentation as above that flips any given edge
(x,y)\in E
strictly decreases the set of currently unmatched vertices in
\tilde{G}
at distance at most
2i
from the edge. This set is finite, e.g. by König’s Infinity Lemma. Thus
(x,y)
can be flipped only finitely many times, that is, it eventually belongs to all or none of the matchings
K_{j}
. It follows that
M_{i+1}:=\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}\cap_{t=j}^{\infty}K_{t}
is a Borel matching that does not admit any augmenting path of length at most
2i+1
as every potential path was considered for augmentation for infinitely many values of
j
.

Remark 3.2
.

A result of Hopcroft and Karp
[
17
]
states that if a matching
K
admits no augmenting path of length at most
2i-1
and we flip an augmenting path of length
2i+1
(that is, shortest possible), then no new augmenting paths of length at most
2i+1
appear. Thus, when we construct
M_{i+1}
from
M_{i}
in the proof of Lemma
3.1
, it is enough in fact to take for
X
just some enumeration of
R^{2i+1}\times{\mathbbm{N}}
.

Theorem 3.3

(Lyons and Nazarov
[
28
]
)
.

In addition to the assumptions of Lemma
3.1
, let Assumption
1.5
apply and let the graph
G=(A,B,E)
be a bipartite expander, that is,
\mu(A)=\mu(B)\in(0,\infty)
and (
2.5
) holds for some
c>0
.
Then
G
has a Borel a.e.-perfect matching
M\subseteq E
.

Proof.
Let
M_{0},M_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
be the sequence of Borel matchings returned by Lemma
3.1
. Let
M
be defined by

M:=\cup_{j=0}^{\infty}\cap_{i=j}^{\infty}M_{i},

(3.2)

that is,
M
consists of edges that are eventually included in every
M_{i}
. Clearly,
M
is a Borel matching. Thus it remains to show that the set of vertices not matched by
M
has measure 0. As we will see, this will be a consequence of Claims
3.3.1
and
3.3.6
via a Borel-Cantelli-type argument.

For
i\in{\mathbbm{N}}
, let
X^{i}:=A\setminus\pi_{1}(M_{i})
and
Y^{i}:=B\setminus\pi_{2}(M_{i})
be the subsets of
A
and
B
of vertices not matched by
M_{i}
.
By Lemma
2.4
, the sets
\pi_{1}(M)
and
\pi_{2}(M)
(and thus the sets
X^{i}
and
Y^{i}
) have the same measure.

Note that the set
\pi_{1}(M_{i}\bigtriangleup M_{i+1})
consists of those
x\in A
on which
M_{i}
and
M_{i+1}
differ as partial functions (in particular, it includes those
x\in A
for which exactly one of these functions is defined).

Claim 3.3.1
.

For every
i\in{\mathbbm{N}}
, it holds that
\mu(\pi_{1}(M_{i}\bigtriangleup M_{i+1}))\leqslant(i+1)\,\mu(X^{i}).

Proof of Claim.
In brief, when we construct
M_{i+1}
from
M_{i}
, each individual augmentation that matches an extra point of
A
changes the current matching at at most
i+1
elements of
A
so the claim follows from the invariance of the measure
\mu
.

Let us give a full proof. Given
i
, let
K_{0}=M_{i},K_{1},K_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq E
be the
Borel matchings returned by the second part of Lemma
3.1
.

Take
j\geqslant 1
and let
(U_{j},\gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{2\ell+1})
with
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant i
be the Borel augmenting family
for
K_{j-1}
that augments it into
K_{j}
. Since each augmentation increases the set of matched vertices and thus
\pi_{1}(K_{j-1})\supseteq\pi_{1}(M_{i})
, we have that
U_{j}\subseteq A\setminus\pi_{1}(K_{j-1})
is a subset of

A\setminus\pi_{1}(M_{i})=X^{i}
. For
t\in\{0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\ell\}
,
let
\psi_{t}
map
x\in U_{j}
to
\gamma_{2t}\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\gamma_{1}.x
.
Then
\psi_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\psi_{\ell}
are Borel injections
whose images
partition
\pi_{1}(K_{j}\bigtriangleup K_{j-1})
. By Lemma
2.4
, we have

\mu(\pi_{1}(K_{j-1}\bigtriangleup K_{j}))=\sum_{t=0}^{\ell}\mu(\psi_{t}(U_{j}))=(\ell+1)\mu(U_{j})\leqslant(i+1)\,\mu(U_{j}).

Again, note that augmentations can only increase the set of matched vertices. Thus, for every
h>j
, we have that
U_{h}\subseteq A\setminus\pi_{1}(K_{h-1})
is disjoint from

U_{j}\subseteq\pi_{1}(K_{j})\subseteq\pi_{1}(K_{h-1})
. Also,
M_{i}\bigtriangleup M_{i+1}\subseteq\cup_{j=1}^{\infty}(K_{j}\bigtriangleup K_{j-i})
. Finally, since every
U_{j}
is a subset of
X^{i}
, we conclude that

\mu(\pi_{1}(M_{i}\bigtriangleup M_{i+1}))\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu(\pi_{1}(K_{j-1}\bigtriangleup K_{j}))\leqslant(i+1)\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu(U_{j})\leqslant(i+1)\,\mu(X^{i}).

The claim is proved.

Fix
i\geqslant 1
.
For integer
j\geqslant 0
, let
X_{j}^{i}
consist of the end-points of alternating paths for
M_{i}
(that start at
A
) whose length is at most
j
and has the same parity as
j
. Since
i
is fixed (until (
3.3
)), we abbreviate
X_{j}:=X_{j}^{i}
.
Since, for each
x\in X^{i}
, the length-0 path
(x)
is alternating, we have in particular that
X_{0}=X^{i}
.
As the graph is bipartite, it holds that
X_{0}\subseteq X_{2}\subseteq\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq A
and
X_{1}\subseteq X_{3}\subseteq\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq B
. Define
X_{0}^{\prime}:=X_{0}
,
X_{1}^{\prime}:=X_{1}
and, for
j\geqslant 2
,
X_{j}^{\prime}:=X_{j}\setminus X_{j-2}
. By definition, the sets

X_{0}^{\prime},X_{2}^{\prime},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
(resp.
X_{1}^{\prime},X_{3}^{\prime},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
) are pairwise disjoint.

Claim 3.3.2
.

For every
j\in[i]
, the matching
M_{i}
gives a bijection between

X_{2j}^{\prime}
and
X_{2j-1}^{\prime}
.

Proof of Claim.

Let
x
be any element of
X_{2j}^{\prime}\subseteq A
. By definition, there is an alternating path
(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{2j})
with
x_{0}\in X_{0}
and
x_{2j}=x
. This path has even length, so
(x,y)\in M_{i}
, where
y:=x_{2j-1}
. The truncated alternating path
(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{2j-1})
shows that
y\in X_{2j-1}
. Suppose, on the contrary to the claim, that
y\not\in X_{2j-1}^{\prime}
. Then
y\in X_{2j-3}
. Let this be witnessed by some alternating path
Q
of length at most
2j-3
. The odd-length path
Q
ends with an edge in
{E}\setminus{M_{i}}
. Either
Q
already contains
x
or we can extend
Q
by adding
x
, in each case obtaining a contradiction to
x\not\in X_{2j-2}
.

Conversely, take any
y
in
X_{2j-1}^{\prime}\subseteq B
. Fix an alternating path
P=(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{2j-1})
with
x_{0}\in X_{0}
and
x_{2j-1}=y
. The last edge of this path of odd length is in
{E}\setminus{M_{i}}
and, since
M_{i}
admits no augmenting path of length
2j-1\leqslant 2i-1
, the vertex
y
has to be matched. Let
(x,y)\in M_{i}
. The vertex
x\in\pi_{1}(M_{i})
cannot belong to
X_{0}=A\setminus\pi_{1}(M_{i})
. Also,
x
cannot belong to
X_{2t}^{\prime}
for some

: otherwise an even-length alternating path

witnessing this has to end with the pair
(x,y)\in M_{i}
(as

is the unique
M_{i}
-match of
x
) and a truncation of

shows that
y\in X_{2t-1}\subseteq X_{2j-3}
, a contradiction.
In particular,
x
cannot occur in
P
as an even-indexed vertex. Thus we can add
x
to
P
, obtaining an alternating path which shows that
x\in X_{2j}
. We have already argued that
x\not\in X_{2j-2}
.
Thus we conclude that
x\in X_{2j}^{\prime}
, as desired.

Recall that
X_{j}
is a subset of
A
(resp.
B
) if
j
is even (resp. odd).

Claim 3.3.3
.

For every
j\in[i]
, we have
X_{2j+1}=N(X_{2j})
.

Proof of Claim.

The inclusion
X_{2j+1}\subseteq N(X_{2j})
is clear. For the other direction, take arbitrary
x\in X_{2j}
and
y\in N(\{x\})\subseteq B
. Pick an alternating path
P
of length at most
2j
starting at
X_{0}
and ending in
x
. Suppose that
y
does not belong to
P
, as otherwise a truncation of
P
shows that
y
is in
X_{2j-1}\subseteq X_{2j+1}
, giving the required. In particular, we have that
(x,y)\not\in M_{i}
as otherwise
y
precedes
x
on
P
. Thus we can extend
P
by adding
y
. This shows that
y\in X_{2j+1}
, as desired.

Claims
3.3.2
and
3.3.3
show by induction on
j\in[i]
that the set
X_{j}^{\prime}
is Borel. (In fact, this is true for every
j
since
X_{j}^{\prime}
is defined by a local rule; however the stated range of

will suffice for our purposes.)

Claim 3.3.4
.

For every
j\in[i]
we have that

\mu(X_{2j})\geqslant\mu(X_{2j-1})
while
\mu(X_{2j-1})

is strictly larger than
\frac{1}{2}\,\mu(B)
or at least
(1+{c})\,\mu(X_{2j-2})
.

Proof of Claim.

Claim
3.3.2
implies by induction on

that the matching
M_{i}
gives a bijection between
X_{2j}\setminus X_{0}=X_{2}^{\prime}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup X_{2j}^{\prime}
and
X_{2j-1}=X_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup X_{2j-1}^{\prime}
.
Thus we have by Lemma
2.4
that
\mu(X_{2j}\setminus X_{0})=\mu(X_{2j-1})
,
giving the first inequality.

The stated lower bound on
\mu(X_{2j-1})
is a direct consequence of Claim
3.3.3
and the bipartite expansion property of

assumed by the theorem.

For
j\in{\mathbbm{N}}
, define
Y_{j}
to consist of the end-points of alternating paths which start in

whose length is at most
j
and has the same parity as

. (This becomes the same definition as that of

when we swap the roles of the sets

and

.)
By symmetry, Claim
3.3.4
also holds when we swap

and
B
, and replace each

by
Y_{t}
.

Claim 3.3.5
.

The sets
X_{i-1}
and
Y_{i}
are disjoint.

Proof of Claim.

Assuming the contrary, pick alternating paths
P=(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{\ell})
and
Q=(y_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,y_{k})
starting at
A
and
B
respectively
(thus
x_{0}\in X_{0}
and
y_{0}\in Y_{0}
) such that
\ell+k
is odd,
x_{\ell}=y_{k}
,
\ell\leqslant i-1
,
k\leqslant i
, and the value of
\ell+k
is smallest possible. Note that
k\geqslant 1
as otherwise the path
P
of length
\ell\leqslant 2i-1
is
M_{i}
-augmenting, contradicting the choice of
M_{i}
. Similarly,
\ell\geqslant 1
.

The minimality of
\ell+k
implies that the paths
\tilde{P}
and
\tilde{Q}
in
\tilde{G}
intersect only in their end-points.
Also, if
\ell
is odd (resp. even), then exactly one of the edges
(x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}),(y_{k-1},y_{k})\in E

(resp.
(x_{\ell},x_{\ell-1}),(y_{k},y_{k-1})\in E
) belongs to
M_{i}
; namely
(y_{k-1},y_{k})\in M_{i}
(resp.
(x_{\ell},x_{\ell-1})\in M_{i}
). Therefore the concatenation of
P
with the reversal of
Q
at the common end-point, that is,

(x_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{\ell},y_{k-1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,y_{0})
,
is an augmenting path of length at most

, contradicting the choice of
M_{i}
.

Note that
\mu(X^{i})=\mu(Y^{i})
, since
\mu(A)=\mu(B)<\infty
by our assumption and

\mu(\pi_{1}(M_{i}))=\mu(\pi_{2}(M_{i}))
by Lemma
2.4
.

Claim 3.3.6
.

It holds that

\mu(X^{i})=\mu(Y^{i})\leqslant(1+c)^{\frac{-i+1}{2}}\mu(A).

Proof of Claim.

Suppose first that
i
is odd. By Claim
3.3.5
, the sets
X_{i-1}
and
Y_{i}
are disjoint. As both are subsets of
A
, at least one of these two sets, suppose
X_{i-1}
, has measure at most
\mu(A)/2
.
By Claim
3.3.4
, the measures of the sets
X_{0}\subseteq X_{2}\subseteq\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq X_{i-1}
increase by factor at least
1+c
each time. Thus,
by

Recall that the local spectral gap property was defined in Section
1.5
.
Note that we do not assume in the definition that
\Gamma
is countable. The authors of
[
6
]
consider only countable
\Gamma
, in which case our definition coincides with the one in
[
6
]
. However, the definition of local spectral gap, as stated in Section
1.5
, makes sense also when
\Gamma
is not countable.

For a finite multiset
Q\subseteq\Gamma
, let
T_{Q}\colon L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
be the averaging operator defined by

(T_{Q}f)(x):=\frac{1}{|Q|}\sum_{\gamma\in Q}f(\gamma^{-1}.x),\hskip 20.00003ptf\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu),\ x\in\Omega.

(5.1)

We say that the operator
T_{Q}
has
spectral gap
if there exists a
constant
c>0
such that for any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
with
\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0
we have
\|T_{Q}f\|_{2}\leqslant(1-c)\,\|f\|_{2}
. Also, we say that the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega

has spectral gap
if there is a finite multiset (equivalently, a finite set)
Q
such that the operator
T_{Q}
has spectral gap. If
\mu(\Omega)<\infty
, then the latter property is easily seen to be equivalent to the local spectral gap of the action
a
with respect to the whole space
\Omega
.

Boutonnet et al
[
6
, Theorem A]
proved the following sufficient condition for local spectral gap.

Theorem 5.1

(Boutonnet et al
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected Lie group with
a fixed left Haar measure
m_{\Gamma}
. Suppose that
the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of
\Gamma
is simple. Let
\mathrm{Ad}:\Gamma\to GL(\mathfrak{g})
denote its adjoint
representation.
Let
\Lambda
be a dense countable subgroup of
\Gamma
and let
\mathfrak{B}
be a
basis of
\mathfrak{g}
such that for every

\mathrm{Ad}(g)
in the basis

has all entries algebraic. Then the left translation action
\Lambda\curvearrowright(\Gamma,m_{\Gamma})
has local spectral gap with respect to every

with compact closure and non-empty interior.

the Introduction.

we show only that
1
implies
1
, leaving to the reader to check that the proof of the implication
1

\implies

2
from
[
6
, Theorem 7.6]
does not use ergodicity.

In order to prove
2

\implies

1
, we need some preliminaries.
Let us say that the action

\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
has a
highly local spectral gap
with respect to
a measurable set
B\subseteq\Omega
with
0<\mu(B)<\infty
if there are
a finite set
S\subseteq\Gamma
and a real
\kappa
such that

\|f\|_{2,B}\leqslant\kappa\sum_{g\in S}\|g.f-f\|_{2,B\,\cap\,g.B}

(5.3)

for any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
with
\int_{B}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
(equivalently, for every
f\in L^{2}(B,\mu)
with
\int_{B}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
).

One motivation behind this definition (besides that it is useful for our proof) is that each side of (
5.3
) depends only on the restriction of
f
to
B
(but not on any values of
f
outside
B
as is the case in (
1.1
)).

Clearly, the highly local spectral gap for
B
implies the local spectral gap for

B
(with the same choice of
S
and
\kappa
). Let us show that the converse implication
also holds.

Claim 5.2.1
.

If the action has local spectral gap with respect to a measurable set
B\subseteq\Omega
with
0<\mu(B)<\infty
, then it has the highly local spectral gap property with respect to
B
.

Proof of Claim.
Let a finite set
S
and a real
\kappa
satisfy the local
spectral gap property with respect to
B\subseteq\Omega
. By enlarging
S
, we can assume that
e\in S
.
Define
\kappa^{\prime}:=\kappa\,|S|
and

S^{\prime}:=\{gh^{-1}\colon g,h\in S\},

where we view
S^{\prime}
as a set, not as a multiset. (Alternatively, if one views
S^{\prime}
as a multiset so e.g.
|S^{\prime}|=|S|^{2}
, then
\kappa^{\prime}=\kappa
suffices in the argument below.)

Let us show that
S^{\prime}
and
\kappa^{\prime}
satisfy
the highly local spectral gap condition. Take any
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)

with

\int_{B}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
.

We define
f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
as follows. Fix some total order on
S
with the identity
e
being the smallest element. For
x\in\Omega
, if there is
\gamma\in S
such that
\gamma.x\in B
then let
\gamma_{x}
be the smallest such
\gamma
and define
f^{\prime}(x):=f(\gamma_{x}.x)
; otherwise (i.e., if
S.\{x\}\cap B=\emptyset
), we let
f^{\prime}(x):=0
(while
\gamma_{x}
is undefined). Since
e
comes before any other element of
S
, we have that
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x)

for all
x\in B
. For
g,h\in S
, define

B_{g,h}:=\{x\in B\colon\gamma_{g^{-1}.x}=h\}.

It follows from the definition that, for every
g\in S
, we have
B=\sqcup_{h\in S}B_{g,h}
, that is,
the sets
B_{g,h}
,
h\in S
, are disjoint and partition
B
. Also, trivially,
B_{g,h}\subseteq B\cap gh^{-1}.B
.

As
f
and
f^{\prime}

coincide on
B
, we have

\int_{B}f^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{B}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
. Also, the square of the
L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
-norm of

f^{\prime}
is
finite, as it is at most
|S|
times the square of
\|f\|_{2,B}
. By the properties stated above and the inequality

(\sum_{h\in S}x_{h})^{1/2}\leqslant\sum_{h\in S}x_{h}^{1/2}
valid for any non-negative reals
x_{h}
, we have that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{2,B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|f^{\prime}\|_{2,B}\ \leqslant\ \kappa\sum_{g\in S}\|g.f^{\prime}-f^{\prime}\|_{2,B}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\sum_{g\in S}\left(\sum_{h\in S}\int_{B_{g,h}}(f(hg^{-1}.x)-f(x))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\kappa\sum_{g\in S}\left(\sum_{h\in S}\int_{B\,\cap\,gh^{-1}.B}(f(hg^{-1}.x)-f(x))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\kappa\sum_{g\in S}\sum_{h\in S}\left(\int_{B\,\cap\,gh^{-1}.B}(f(hg^{-1}.x)-f(x))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\kappa\,|S|\sum_{\gamma\in S^{\prime}}\left(\int_{B\,\cap\,\gamma.B}(f(\gamma^{-1}.x)-f(x))^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa^{\prime}\sum_{\gamma\in S^{\prime}}\|\gamma.f-f\|_{2,B\,\cap\,\gamma.B},

that is, the real
\kappa^{\prime}
and the set
S^{\prime}
establish the highly local spectral gap for
B
.
This proves Claim
5.2.1
.

Now, we can give a direct proof that
2
implies
1
. Since the properties in question are invariant under scaling
the measure by a constant factor, assume that
\mu(X)=1
. By Claim
5.2.1
, we can find a real
\kappa
and a symmetric finite set
S\subseteq\Gamma
with
S\ni e
that satisfy the highly local spectral
gap condition for
X
. Now, given
\eta>0
, let
\ell\in{\mathbbm{N}}
satisfy
(1+\eta/(2\kappa^{2}|S|^{2}))^{\ell}\geqslant 1/\eta
and consider the set

Q:=S^{\ell}=\{\gamma_{1}\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\gamma_{\ell}\colon\gamma_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\gamma_{\ell}\in S\}

of all possible
\ell
-wise products of
elements of
S
. We will show that this set
Q
is
\eta
-expanding for
X
. We need an auxiliary claim first.

Claim 5.2.2
.

For every measurable
Y\subseteq X
, we have

\mu(S.Y\cap(X\setminus Y))\geqslant\frac{1-\mu(Y)}{2\kappa^{2}|S|^{2}}\,\mu(Y).

(5.4)

Proof of Claim.

Let
y:=\mu(Y)
. Define
f:\Omega\to{\mathbbm{R}}
by

f(x):=(1-y){\mathbbm{1}}_{Y}-y{\mathbbm{1}}_{X\setminus Y}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-y,&x\in Y,\\
-y,&x\in X\setminus Y,\\
0,&x\in\Omega\setminus X.\end{array}\right.

(5.5)

Then
\|f\|_{2,Y}^{2}=(1-y)^{2}y+y^{2}(1-y)=y(1-y)
and
\int_{X}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0
.

Let
\gamma\in S
. For
x\in X\cap\gamma.X
, we have that
\gamma^{-1}.x\in X
and thus
|(\gamma.f)(x)-f(x)|=|f(\gamma^{-1}.x)-f(x)|
assumes value 0 or
1
; in fact, it is 1 if and only if
x\in Y
and
\gamma^{-1}.x\in X\setminus Y
or vice versa, that is, precisely if

x\in(Y\cap\gamma.(X\setminus Y))\cup(\gamma.Y\cap(X\setminus Y)).

Since
S=S^{-1}
, the latter set is a subset of
\gamma.Z\cup Z
, where
Z:=S.Y\cap(X\setminus Y)
.
Since the action is measure-preserving, we conclude that
\|\gamma.f-f\|_{2,X\,\cap\,\gamma.X}^{2}\leqslant\mu(\gamma.Z\cup Z)\leqslant 2\mu(Z)
.

The above (in)equalities and the choice of
S,\kappa
give that

(y(1-y))^{1/2}=\|f\|_{2,X}\leqslant\kappa\sum_{\gamma\in S}\|\gamma.f-f\|_{2,X\,\cap\,\gamma.X}\leqslant\kappa|S|\,\big(2\mu(Z)\big)^{1/2},

which implies the claim.

Now we are ready to show that
Q
is
\eta
-expanding for
X
. Take an arbitrary measurable subset
Y\subseteq X
. Let
Y_{0}:=Y
and, inductively for
i\in[\ell]
, let
Y_{i}:=S.Y_{i-1}\cap X
. Clearly,
Y_{\ell}\subseteq Q.Y\cap X
. If for some
i\leqslant\ell
, we have

\mu(Y_{i})\geqslant 1-\eta
, then (since
e\in S
) we have that each of
Y_{i}\subseteq\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\subseteq Y_{\ell}\subseteq Q.Y\cap X
has
measure at least
1-\eta
, as required. Otherwise, we obtain from Claim
5.2.2
by induction on
i=0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\ell
that
\mu(Y_{i})\geqslant(1+\eta/(2\kappa^{2}|S|^{2}))^{i}\mu(Y)
. Taking
i=\ell
, we get the the required lower bound
\mu(Q.Y\cap X)\geqslant\mu(Y_{\ell})\geqslant\mu(Y)/\eta
. Since
\eta>0
was arbitrary, we conclude that
X
is a domain of expansion. We have shown that
2
implies
1
.

Now, let us show that
1
implies
2
. Let
X
be a domain of expansion. For each positive integer
n
, fix some finite set
S_{n}\subseteq\Gamma
which is
(1/n)
-expanding for
X
. Let
\Lambda

be the subgroup of
\Gamma
generated by
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}S_{n}
.

First, let us show that the countable group
\Lambda
satisfies
1
. Suppose on the contrary that some sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbbm{N}}}
violates this property.
Let
A_{n}^{\prime}:=A_{n}\cap X
. By passing to a subsequence, we can assume that there is an integer
m\geqslant 2
such that
\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})<\mu(X)(1-2/m)
for every
n
.
Let
S:=S_{m}
, that is,
S
is a
(1/m)
-expanding set for
X
. Thus, for every
n\in{\mathbbm{N}}
,

\mu(S.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\geqslant\min\left(\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\mu(X),\ m\,\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})\right).

By passing to a subsequence again, we can assume that

1.

for all
n
we have
\mu(S.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\mu(X)
, or

2.

for all
n
we have
\mu(S.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\geqslant m\,\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})
.

If
1
holds then clearly
\mu((S.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\setminus A_{n}^{\prime})\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\mu(X)-(1-\frac{2}{m})\mu(X)=\mu(X)/m
and thus, for some
\gamma\in S
and infinitely many
n
, we have
\mu((\gamma.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\setminus A_{n}^{\prime})\geqslant\mu(X)/(m|S|)
, and so also

\mu\big((\gamma.A_{n}\setminus A_{n})\cap X\big)=\mu((\gamma.A_{n}\cap X)\setminus(A_{n}\cap X))\geqslant\frac{\mu(X)}{m|S|}

for infinitely many
n
. This is in contradiction with the assumption (
5.2
) of Property
1
.

Suppose now that
2
holds. Since
m\geqslant 2
, we have
\mu((S.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\setminus A_{n}^{\prime})\geqslant\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})
for all
n
. Therefore for some
\gamma\in S
and infinitely many
n
we have
\mu((\gamma.A_{n}^{\prime}\cap X)\setminus A_{n}^{\prime})\geqslant\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})/|S|
, and hence

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mu((\gamma.A_{n}\bigtriangleup A_{n})\cap X)}{\mu(A_{n}\cap X)}\geqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mu((\gamma.A_{n}\cap X)\setminus(A_{n}\cap X))}{\mu(A_{n}^{\prime})}\geqslant\frac{1}{|S|},

which again is a contradiction to (
5.2
).

Thus
1
holds for
X
with respect to the action
\Lambda\curvearrowright\Omega
. By
[
6
, Theorem 7.6]
, the action of the countable group
\Lambda
on
\Omega
has local spectral gap with respect to
X
, that is,
2
holds for the group
\Lambda
. Of course, when we enlarge the group to
\Gamma
, then

2
still holds.

The following proposition will be needed later, for estimating the number of pieces in some equidecompositions given by our proofs.

Proposition 5.3
.

In addition to Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
, assume that
\mu
is a finite measure. Let
S\subseteq\Gamma
be a finite symmetric multiset, and let
c\in(0,1)
be such that for every
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
with
\int f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0
we have
\|T_{S}f\|_{2}\leqslant(1-c)\|f\|_{2}
. Define
c^{\prime}:=c(2-c)
. Then the following statements hold.

1.

For every measurable
Y\subseteq\Omega
it holds that

\mu(S.Y)\geqslant\frac{\mu(Y)\mu(\Omega)}{(1-c)^{2}\mu(\Omega)+2c\mu(Y)-c^{2}\mu(Y)}\geqslant\mu(Y)\left(1+c^{\prime}\,\frac{\mu(\Omega\setminus Y)}{\mu(\Omega)}\right).

2.

Let
\eta>0
and
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
(1+c^{\prime}\eta)^{\ell}>1/\eta
. Then
S^{\ell}\subseteq\Gamma

is an
\eta
-expanding set for
\Omega
.

Proof.

By scaling the measure, we can assume that
\mu(\Omega)=1
. Take any measurable
Y\subseteq\Omega
with
y:=\mu(Y)>0
. Analogously to (
5.5
),
define
f:=(1-y){\mathbbm{1}}_{Y}-y{\mathbbm{1}}_{\Omega\setminus Y}
. We have
\|f\|_{2}^{2}=y(1-y)
and
\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=0
.

Let
Z:=S.Y
and
z:=\mu(Z)
. Clearly, for every
x\in\Omega\setminus Z
we have that
(T_{S}f)(x)=-y
. By
the invariance of the measure,
it also holds that
\int_{\Omega}T_{S}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=|S|^{-1}\sum_{\gamma\in S}\int_{\Omega}\gamma.f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
. Under these constraints on
T_{S}f
, its
L^{2}
-norm is
minimised when the function is constant on
Z
, that is, assumes the value
(1-z)y/z
there.
Thus

\|T_{S}f\|_{2}^{2}\geqslant\left(\frac{(1-z)y}{z}\right)^{2}z+y^{2}(1-z)=\frac{y^{2}(1-z)}{z}.

By the spectral gap property, the left-hand side is at most
(1-c)^{2}\|f\|^{2}_{2}=(1-c)^{2}y(1-y)
. Solving the obtained linear inequality in
z
, we obtain the first inequality of Part
1
. The second inequality is obtained by observing that by
c,y\in[0,1]
,

\frac{1}{(1-c)^{2}+2cy-c^{2}y}-1=\frac{c(2-c)}{(1-c)^{2}+2cy-c^{2}y}\,(1-y)\geqslant c^{\prime}(1-y).

We prove Part
2
similarly as we did after Claim
5.2.2
. Define
Y_{0}:=Y
. Inductively for
i\in{\mathbbm{N}}
, let
Y_{i+1}:=S.Y_{i}
. If for some
i\leqslant\ell
, we have
\mu(Y_{i})\geqslant 1-\eta
, then
\mu(Y_{\ell})\geqslant\mu(Y_{\ell-1})\geqslant\dots\geqslant\mu(Y_{i})
(since the set
S
is non-empty and
\mu
is invariant). Otherwise, the measure of each new set
Y_{i}
,
i\leqslant\ell
, increases by factor at least
1+c^{\prime}\eta
by Part
1
and thus
\mu(S^{\ell}.Y)\geqslant\mu(Y)/\eta
by the choice of
\ell
.

Proof of Theorem

for
\operatorname{SO}(n)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{n-1}
,

Here,
\Omega
is the sphere
{\mathbbm{S}}^{n-1}
with the uniform probability measure
\mu
. It was shown independently by Margulis
[

]
and Sullivan
[

]
for

for

6.2
Proof of Theorem
1.11
for
G_{2}\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}

Recall that
G_{2}
is the subgroup of affine bijections of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
generated
by the special linear group
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{Z}})
and all translations. In order to prove that the action
a:G_{2}\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
is expanding, we consider another action
b
defined as follows.

Identify the torus
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}/{\mathbbm{Z}}^{2}
with
X:=[0,1)^{2}
and let
\nu
denote the uniform probability measure on Borel subsets of
X
.
The group
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{Z}})
acts naturally on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
, commuting with the reduction of vectors modulo
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{2}
. Thus we obtain the standard measure-preserving action
b:\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{Z}})\curvearrowright(X,\nu)
.
A classical result of Rosenblatt
[
41
]
states that

has spectral gap. By Proposition
5.3
, the set
X
is a domain of expansion for the action

.

By Remark
2.6
,
it is enough to show that
X
is a domain of expansion for
the paradoxical action
a
.
Let
\pi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to X
be the natural projection that reduces each coordinate modulo
1
. For
\gamma\in\textrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
, let
\gamma^{\prime}
denote the corresponding element of
G_{2}
under the natural inclusion of
\textrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{Z}})
into
G_{2}
. For example, the restriction of the composition
\pi\circ a(\gamma^{\prime},\cdot)
to

is equal to
b(\gamma,\cdot)
, the
b
-action of
\gamma
.

Take any
\eta>0
. Since
X
is a domain of expansion for the action
b
, there exists an
\eta
-expanding finite set
S\subseteq\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{Z}})
for
X
under the action
b
. We construct
an
\eta
-expanding set
T
for

under the action

as follows.
For every
\gamma\in S
and

.
Since

, the constructed set

indeed, for every integer square intersecting

, the set

with the integer translation moving this square back to

. Thus,

and, if

\mu(T.U\cap X)\geqslant\mu(S.U)\geqslant\min\big((1-\eta)\,\mu(X),\,{\mu(U)}/{\eta}\big),

where the last inequality follows from the facts that

is
\eta
-expanding for

and the measures

. Thus

is an

-expanding set for

. As

is a domain of expansion for the action

, as desired.

6.3
Proof of Theorem
1.11
for the hyperbolic space
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}

For an introduction to hyperbolic spaces see
e.g. Bridson and Haefliger
[
7
, Section 2]
or Ratcliffe
[
40
]
. One representation
of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
(that we will use here) is to take the bilinear form

\langle u,v\rangle_{n,1}:=-u_{n+1}v_{n+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}v_{i},\hskip 10.00002ptu,v\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1},

(6.1)

identify
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
with upper sheet of the hyperboloid

{{\mathcal{H}}}:=\{u\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}\colon\langle u,u\rangle_{n,1}=-1\}

(namely, the sheet where
u_{n+1}>0
), and
define the metric
d
by
\cosh d(u,v)=-\langle u,v\rangle_{n,1}
for
u,v\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
.
The group of isometries of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}

can be identified with
O(n,1)_{0}
, the group of
(n+1)\times(n+1)
-matrices which leave
the bilinear form in (
6.1
) invariant
and do not swap the two sheets of
{{\mathcal{H}}}
; see
[
7
, Theorem 2.24]
or
[
40
, Theorem 3.2.3]
.
The group
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})
of orientation-preserving isometries of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
corresponds to the
subgroup
\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}
of index 2 in
O(n,1)_{0}
, which
consists of
matrices with determinant 1. The space
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
is equipped with an isometry-invariant measure
\mu
whose push-forward
under the projection on the first
n
coordinates of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}
has density

\rho(x_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{n}):=(1+(x_{1}^{2}+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+x_{n}^{2}))^{-1/2}

(6.2)

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
for
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
.

First, we show that Theorem
5.1
applies to
the group
\Gamma=\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})=\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}
, obtaining a countable subgroup
\Lambda
. Then we show that the expansion of
\Lambda\curvearrowright(\Gamma,m_{\Gamma})
, where
m_{\Gamma}
is a left Haar measure on
\Gamma
,
can be transferred to the action
\Lambda\curvearrowright({\mathbbm{H}}^{n},\mu)
.

The Lie algebra of
\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}
(or
\operatorname{SO}(n,1)
) is
\mathfrak{so}(n,1)
which consists of
(n+1)\times(n+1)
-matrices

M
such that

M^{T}I_{n,1}+I_{n,1}M=0,

(6.3)

where
I_{n,1}
is the diagonal matrix having
n
entries equal to 1 and the last entry equal to
-1
.
It is well-know that
\mathfrak{so}(n,1)
is simple; in fact, a complete characterisation of simple real Lie algebras is
available,
see e.g. Knapp
[
20
, Theorem 6.105]
.

The linear system of equations (
6.3
) has integer coefficients, so we can choose a basis
\mathfrak{B}

for
\mathfrak{so}(n,1)
that consists of matrices with all entries rational.

The adjoint
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}
maps
M\in\mathfrak{so}(n,1)
to the matrix product
\gamma M\gamma^{-1}
. If a matrix
\gamma
has algebraic entries, then so does its inverse; it follows that the matrix of the map
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
when expressed in the basis
\mathfrak{B}
has all entries algebraic.
Thus the only non-trivial remaining assumption of Theorem
5.1
is the existence of a countable dense set
X\subseteq\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}

such that each
\gamma\in X
as a matrix has algebraic entries (as then
we can take
\Lambda
to be the subgroup generated by

).
We can identify
\operatorname{SO}(n,1)
with the variety in
V\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}
defined by the system of polynomials with
integer coefficients, stating that the determinant is
1
and the bilinear form in (
6.1
) is preserved.
Since
\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}
is a connectivity component of
\operatorname{SO}(n,1)
, the existence of

follows from the following general lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,f_{m}\in{\mathbbm{Z}}[x_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{r}]
be polynomials
with integer coefficients. Let

V:=\{x\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{r}\colon\forall i\in[m]\ f_{i}(x)=0\}

be the real variety defined by these polynomials. Then the set of vectors in
V
with all entries algebraic is dense in

V
(in the standard topology on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{r}
generated by open Euclidean balls).

Proof.

Although this lemma has surely been proved before, we could not find a suitable statement (of the real case) anywhere in print. So we present our proof.

One of the consequences of the Tarski-Seidenberg Theorem
[
47
,
42
]
is that the ordered field of reals
{\mathbbm{R}}
and the ordered field
{\mathbbm{A}}
of real algebraic numbers satisfy the same set of first-order sentences, where the language includes the constants
0
and
1
, the multiplication and addition functions, the equality relation, and the binary order relation. For an
r
-vector
x
, we can express the statement that
f_{i}(x)=0
for each
i\in[m]

in first-order logic.
Thus, for every Euclidean ball
B\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
whose centre and radius are rational,

B\cap V=\emptyset
if and only if
B\cap V\cap{\mathbbm{A}}^{n}=\emptyset
.

Now, suppose on the contrary to the claim that we have some
x\in V
and rational
r>0
such that the radius-
r
ball
B\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{r}
around
x
has no algebraic points from
V
. Pick a rational vector
x^{\prime}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
within distance
r/3
from
x
and let
B^{\prime}
be the ball around
x^{\prime}
of radius
r/2
. Then
B^{\prime}\subseteq B
, so
B^{\prime}\cap V
has no algebraic points. However,
B^{\prime}\cap V
is non-empty as it contains
x
, a contradiction.

Thus, by Theorem
5.1
the action
b:\Lambda\curvearrowright(\Gamma,m_{\Gamma})
of the countable subgroup
\Lambda
of
\Gamma
generated by
X
has local spectral gap with respect to every measurable subset of
\Gamma
with compact closure and non-empty interior.
We will show that the restriction
a^{\prime}
of the measure-preserving action

to
\Lambda
is expanding, thus finishing the proof.

Take any Borel

which has non-empty
interior and compact closure. Let us show that

is a domain of expansion.

One can derive from the formula for the hyperbolic distance that
the topology on
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
is the induced topology from
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}
. Thus
B

is a bounded subset of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}
.
Since the density
\rho
is uniformly bounded (namely, by 1),
we have that
\mu(B)<\infty
. Also,
\mu(B)>0
as

everywhere.

Let
x_{0}:=(0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,0,1)\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
and define

by

, that is, under the assumed identification

{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}
,

the application of the matrix
\gamma
to the vector
x_{0}
.
Define

(6.4)

As it is easy so see,
a:\Gamma\times{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}\to{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
is continuous. Thus

is also continuous and

is a Borel set. Let
U\not=\emptyset
be the interior of

. Fix

and
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma

with

. Again, by the continuity of

there is an open set in

around

has a non-empty

Let us argue that the closure
\overline{B^{\prime}}
of

is compact. Take an arbitrary infinite sequence
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\in B^{\prime}
. Viewing
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
as a subset of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}

so that the
n+1

x_{0},x_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{n}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}
are linearly independent.
Each isometry
\gamma_{i}\in\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{H}}^{n})
can be represented by a linear
{\mathbbm{H}}^{n}
-preserving transformation
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathbbm{R}}^{n+1}
given by
some matrix
M_{i}\in\operatorname{SO}(n,1)_{0}

by

are in the compact set

,

Let

It remains to show is that

is measure-preserving. This is
exactly the statement of, for example,

(which follows with some work from the uniqueness of the Haar measure).

By Remark

6.4
Proof of Theorem
1.11
for
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}

Here, we follow the same strategy as in Section
6.3
. One new caveat is that, unlike for the hyperbolic space, the stabiliser

of a point is not a compact subgroup. So we cannot define

by the direct analogue of (

) as its closure would not be compact. We get around this by, essentially, taking a compact subset of positive measure in each coset of

[

, Lemma 4 of Section 11.6]
that we could just cite here, so we prove it (as well as a few other claims) via direct explicit calculations.

Proof of Theorem
1.11
for
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

Recall that
m_{\Gamma}
is a left Haar measure on
\Gamma=\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})
.
We view each element
\gamma\in\Gamma

as a
2\times 2
-matrix
(\gamma_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{2}
. It is easy to to find a countable dense subgroup
\Lambda
of
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})
: just take the
subgroup of matrices with all entries rational.
The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbbm{R}})
of
\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})
consists of
2\times 2
matrices with trace zero and is well known to be simple. For the
basis
\mathfrak{B}
, one can take, for example,

X:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
0&0\end{array}\right],\hskip 10.00002ptY:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
1&0\end{array}\right],\hskip 10.00002ptZ:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right].

For
\gamma\in\operatorname{SL}(2,{\mathbbm{R}})
, the adjoint
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
maps a
2\times 2
-matrix
M=(M_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{2}\in\mathfrak{sl}_{2}({\mathbbm{R}})
to
\gamma M\gamma^{-1}
. Clearly, we have
M=M_{1,2}X+M_{2,1}Y+M_{1,1}Z
. If we write the linear map
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
in
the basis
\mathfrak{B}
, then each entry of the corresponding
3\times 3
-matrix is, in fact, a quadratic polynomial with integer coefficients in the entries of the matrix
\gamma
.
Therefore, if
\gamma\in\Lambda
then
\gamma
(and thus the matrix of
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
) has all entries rational.

So the conclusion of Theorem
5.1
applies to the action
b:\Lambda\curvearrowright(\Gamma,m_{\Gamma})
. In order to derive that the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
is expanding, where
\Omega={\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
, we need some preparation.
Define

H:=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\colon\gamma e_{1}=e_{1}\}=\{M(u)\colon u\in{\mathbbm{R}}\},

(6.5)

where

e_{1}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
0\end{array}\right]
and
M(u):=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&u\\
0&1\end{array}\right]
.

The map
M
gives an isomorphism between the topological groups
({\mathbbm{R}},+)

and
H
. Let
\rho
be
the Haar measure on
H
with
\rho(I)=1
, where
I:=\{M(u)\colon 0\leqslant u\leqslant 1\}
, that is,
\rho
is the push-forward of the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbbm{R}}
by
M
.

Clearly, the map
\char 16\relax:\Gamma\to\Omega
, where
\char 16\relax(\gamma):=\gamma e_{1}
for
\gamma\in\Gamma
, is continuous and the pre-images under
\char 16\relax
of the points in
\Omega
are exactly
the left cosets of
H
. Consider the map
\sigma:\Omega\to\Gamma
defined by

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right]\mapsto\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}x&\frac{-y}{{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\\
y&\frac{x}{{x^{2}+y^{2}}}\end{array}\right],\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for }\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right]\in\Omega.

The map
\sigma
is continuous on
\Omega={\mathbbm{R}}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and
\sigma
is the right inverse of
\char 16\relax
:
\char 16\relax\,\circ\,\sigma=\operatorname{Id}_{\Omega}
.
By e.g.
[
40
, Theorem 5.1.5]
, the function
\phi:\Omega^{\prime}\to\Gamma
, defined by
\phi(x,h)=\sigma(x)h
is a homeomorphism, where we let
\Omega^{\prime}:=\Omega\times H
.
The space
\Omega^{\prime}
comes with the measure
\mu^{\prime}:=\mu\times\rho
, the product of
the Lebesgue measure
\mu
on
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
and the Haar measure
\rho
on
H
.

Let us show that the push-forward of
\mu^{\prime}
by
\phi

is a constant multiple of the Haar measure
m_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
. By the uniqueness of
the Haar measure, it is enough to argue that, for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
, viewed as the map
\Gamma\to\Gamma
where
\beta\mapsto\gamma.\beta
, the composition

\gamma^{\prime}:=\phi^{-1}\circ\gamma\circ\phi
from
\Omega\times H
to itself preserves the measure
\mu\times\rho
. In this concrete case, it is easy to show this by writing explicit formulas. Namely, the inverse of

is

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}x&c\\
y&d\end{array}\right]\mapsto\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right],M(u)\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{where }u:=\frac{c+y/({x^{2}+y^{2}})}{x}=\frac{d-x/({x^{2}+y^{2}})}{y}.

It routinely follows that
\gamma^{\prime}((x,y),M(u))=(\gamma.(x,y),M(u+F))
, where

does not depend on

We see that the bijection
\gamma^{\prime}
is the measure preserving map
a(\gamma,\cdot)
in the first coordinate. Also, if the first coordinate is fixed, then the second coordinate of
\gamma^{\prime}
corresponds to some translation of
{\mathbbm{R}}

under the parametrisation

from (

is measure-preserving, as claimed.

Thus, by scaling the measure

, we can assume that

is a measure-preserving homeomorphism between

and
(\Gamma,m_{\Gamma})
.
For the rest of the proof, it will be more convenient to replace the action

with its conjugate by

. Namely, let the measure-preserving action

be defined by
b^{\prime}(\gamma,(x,h)):=\phi^{-1}(b(\gamma,\phi(x,h)))
for

and
\gamma\in\Lambda
.
By the calculations in the previous paragraph, for every

so that

;
moreover, each
\gamma^{x}
under the isomorphism

corresponds to some translation of

and thus preserves the Haar measure

.

Now, we are ready to show that the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega
is expanding.
Take any Borel
B\subseteq\Omega
with non-empty interior and

B^{\prime}:=B\times I
.
Clearly,
B^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
has compact closure and non-empty interior and
\mu^{\prime}(B^{\prime})=\mu(B)
.
Since
\phi
is a measure-preserving homeomorphism, Theorem
5.1
(when applied to

) gives
that the conjugated action

has local spectral gap with respect to every measurable subset of
\Omega^{\prime}
with compact closure and non-empty interior.
Thus there are a real
\kappa
and a finite set

such that

\|f^{\prime}\|_{2,B^{\prime}}\leqslant\kappa\sum_{s\in S}\|s.f^{\prime}-f^{\prime}\|_{2,B^{\prime}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for all $f^{\prime}\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})$ with $\int_{B^{\prime}}f^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}=0$.}

(6.6)

Let us show that the same choice of
\kappa
and
S
also witnesses the local spectral gap of the action

with respect to
B
. Take any

with

\int_{B}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0
. The idea is to apply the inequality in (
6.6
)
to the function
(x,h)\mapsto f(x)
except, in order to have an
L^{2}
-function, we set it to 0 at the points which do not matter when we consider local spectral gap for
B^{\prime}
under

(namely, those points that do not appear in (

)). So, let

for
(x,h)\in B^{\prime}\cup S^{-1}.B^{\prime}
and let

be zero otherwise. The obtained function

is in

because is it obtained by patching together compositions of

with finitely many measure-preserving maps

,
\gamma\in S

if

otherwise. Since

Since

\displaystyle\|s.f^{\prime}-f^{\prime}\|_{2,B^{\prime}}

It follows by (

\|f\|_{2,B}=\|f^{\prime}\|_{2,B^{\prime}}\leqslant\kappa\sum_{s\in S}\|s.f^{\prime}-f^{\prime}\|_{2,B^{\prime}}=\kappa\sum_{s\in S}\|s.f-f\|_{2,B}.

Thus the action

is expanding by Lemma

(and Remark

6.5.2

The rest of this section is devoted to domains of expansion in

S_{\delta}

occupies at least
(1-\delta)
-fraction of
Y_{z}
or expands by factor at least
1/\delta
in the measure
\mu_{z}
.
It follows that, if
U
does not expand in measure under
S_{\delta}
, then this means
that the set
U
is “close” to a
ring set
(a union of some spheres
Y_{z}
).

In order to deal with such sets, we add a finite set
T
of isometries of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
with the property that there is
c>0
such that if
U
is any Borel ring set then
\mu^{2}((T.U)_{z})\geqslant c\mu(U)
for every
z\in[1,\rho]
. We will call such a set
T
a
diffuser
. Informally speaking, a diffuser spreads any ring set across all radii of interest fairly uniformly. If we apply a spherical expanding set
S_{\beta}
with
\beta\ll\delta
to any such “uniformly spread” set
T.U
, then we ensure that, for each
z\in[1,\rho]
, its
z
-leaf
(T.U)_{z}
expands or occupies most of
Y_{z}
. It follows from the above properties that
S_{\beta}.T
has a good expansion property when applied to any (almost) ring set. Thus, our proof strategy is as follows: if
U
expands under
S_{\delta}
then we are done; otherwise
S_{\delta}.U
is close to a ring set and
consequently expands under
S_{\beta}T
.
The exact value of
\rho
will be chosen to simplify finding a diffuser set; in fact, we will choose
\rho
so that the diffuser can be taken to be a one-element set (see Figure
1
and Lemma
6.8
).

Let us give all formal general definitions (that are motivated by the above discussion). Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
apply everywhere in this section.

Definition 6.3
.

A
foliation
of a Borel set
Y\subseteq\Omega
is a pair
((Z,{{\mathcal{B}}}_{Z},\nu),(Y_{z},\mu_{z})_{z\in Z})
, where

1.

(Z,{{\mathcal{B}}}_{Z},\nu)
is a standard measure space with
0<\nu(Z)<\infty
,

2.

the sets
Y_{z}
,
z\in Z
, are pairwise disjoint subsets of
Y
,

3.

for every Borel
X\subseteq Z
, the set
Y_{X}:=\cup_{z\in X}Y_{z}
is a Borel subset of
Y
,

4.

each
\mu_{z}
is a finite measure on
(\Omega,{{\mathcal{B}}})
supported on
Y_{z}
such that for every Borel set
U\subseteq Y
the function
z\mapsto\mu_{z}(U)
is Borel and integrable,
and it holds that

\mu(U)=\int_{Z}\mu_{z}(U)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z).

(6.7)

Note that if
\nu
happens to be the push-forward of
\mu
under the map
Y\to Z
that sends each
Y_{z}
to
z
(when necessarily
\mu_{z}
is a probability measure for
\nu
-a.e.
z\in Z
), then (
6.7
) states that the map
z\mapsto\mu_{z}
gives a disintegration of the measure
\mu
, see e.g.
[
51
, Section 5.1.2]
.

Under Definition
6.3
, the
support
of a Borel set
U\subseteq\Omega
is defined as

\operatorname{Supp}(U):=\{z\in Z\colon\mu_{z}(U)>0\}.

Of course,
\operatorname{Supp}(U)=\operatorname{Supp}(U\cap Y)
. Also, for
z\in Z
and
X\subseteq Z
we denote
U_{z}:=U\cap Y_{z}
and and
U_{X}:=\bigcup_{x\in X}U_{x}\subseteq Y
. Note that
U_{z}=U\cap Y_{z}
is Borel by
Item
3
of the above definition. Also, since each
\mu_{z}
is supported on
Y_{z}
, we have
\mu_{z}(U)=\mu_{z}(U_{z})
.

Definition 6.4
.

Let
Y
be a subset of
\Omega
with a foliation
((Z,{{\mathcal{B}}}_{Z},\nu),(Y_{z},\mu_{z})_{z\in Z})
. For
\varepsilon>0
, we say that a finite set
S\subseteq\Gamma
is
leaf-wise
\varepsilon
-expanding
if, for every Borel
U\subseteq Y
, it holds that

\mu_{z}(S.U)\geqslant\min\left((1-\varepsilon)\,\mu_{z}(Y),\ \frac{\mu_{z}(U)}{\varepsilon}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for every $z\in Z$}.

We say that
Y
is a
domain of leaf-wise expansion
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a leaf-wise

\varepsilon
-expanding finite set
S_{\varepsilon}\subseteq\Gamma
.

For
c>0
, we say a finite set
T\subseteq\Gamma
is a
c
-diffuser
for
Y
if for every Borel
R\subseteq Z
and every
z\in Z
we have

\mu_{z}(T.Y_{R})\geqslant c{\,}\mu(Y_{R})\,/\,\nu(Z).

(6.8)

A finite set
T\subseteq\Gamma
is called a
diffuser
if it is a
c
-diffuser for some
c>0
.

The following lemma states, informally speaking, that a diffuser spreads well not only ring sets
Y_{R}

but also those sets that are “close” to them.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
(Y_{z},\mu_{z})_{z\in Z}
be a foliation of
Y\subseteq\Omega
.
Let
T
be a diffuser for
Y
and let
c>0
satisfy (
6.8
). Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, let
\delta:=\varepsilon c/(\varepsilon+|T|)
.
Then, for any Borel set
V\subseteq Y
of positive measure satisfying
\mu_{z}(V)\geqslant(1-\delta)\,\mu_{z}(Y)
for each
z\in\operatorname{Supp}(V)
, we have

\nu\big(\left\{z\in Z\colon\mu_{z}(T.V)>\delta{\,}\mu(V)/\nu(Z)\right\}\big)\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\,\nu(Z).

(6.9)

Proof.

Note that if we divide
\nu
by some
\alpha>0
and multiply each
\mu_{z}
by the same constant
\alpha
,
then all statements of Definitions
6.3
and
6.4
remain valid. (This will be used later in Remark
7.5
and this is why we divide by
\nu(Z)
in (
6.8
).) Thus, we can assume without loss of generality that
\nu(Z)=1
.

Let
R:=\operatorname{Supp}(V)
. Note that the set
R
is Borel by Item
4
of Definition
6.3
. The measure of
V_{Z\setminus R}
is 0 by (
6.7
), so by removing this set from
V
we may assume that
V_{z}=\emptyset
for
z\in Z\setminus R
. Let
W:=Y_{R}\setminus V
.
Note that
Y_{R}=V\sqcup W
and
\mu(Y_{R})\neq 0
. Define

R^{\prime}:=\left\{z\in Z\colon\mu_{z}(T.W)\geqslant\left(c-\delta\right)\mu(Y_{R})\right\}.

Since
T
is a
c
-diffuser, we obtain that, for all

z\in Z\setminus R^{\prime}
,

\mu_{z}(T.V)=\mu_{z}(T.(Y_{R}\setminus W))\geqslant c{\,}\mu(Y_{R})-\mu_{z}(T.W)>\delta\,\mu(Y_{R})\geqslant\delta\,\mu(V).

Thus, in order to finish the proof, it is enough to show that
\nu(R^{\prime})\leqslant\varepsilon
.
By (
6.7
), we have that

\mu(T.W)\geqslant\mu(T.W\cap Y_{R^{\prime}})=\int_{R^{\prime}}\mu_{z}(T.W)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\geqslant(c-\delta)\,\mu(Y_{R})\,\nu(R^{\prime}).

Since
\mu_{z}(W)=\mu_{z}(Y\setminus V)\leqslant\delta{\,}\mu_{z}(Y)
for each
z\in R
by the assumption on
V
and since
W\subseteq Y_{R}
, we have, again by (
6.7
), that

\mu(W)\leqslant\delta{\,}\mu(Y_{R}).

Hence

(c-\delta)\,\mu(Y_{R})\,\nu(R^{\prime})\leqslant\mu(T.W)\leqslant|T|\,\mu(W)\leqslant|T|\,\delta\,\mu(Y_{R}),

giving the required bound
\nu(R^{\prime})\leqslant\varepsilon
by the choice of
\delta
.

We are ready to state our criterion for being a domain of expansion. Recall that
S_{\eta}
for
\eta>0
is a finite leaf-wise
\eta
-expanding subset of
\Gamma
. Let us additionally assume that
S_{\eta}\ni e
.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
((Z,{{\mathcal{B}}}_{Z},\nu),(Y_{z},\mu_{z})_{z\in Z})
be a foliation of Borel
Y\subseteq\Omega
. Suppose that there is
M\geqslant 1
such that for all
z\in Z
we have
M\geqslant\mu_{z}(Y)\geqslant\frac{1}{M}
.
If
Y
is a domain of leaf-wise expansion which admits a diffuser
T
, then
Y
is a domain of expansion.

More precisely, given
\eta\in(0,1/2)
and
c>0
such that
T
is a
c
-diffuser, let
\varepsilon:=\eta/(2M^{2})
,
\delta:=\varepsilon c/(\varepsilon+|T|)
, and
\beta:=\delta\varepsilon/(2M)
. Then
R:=S_{\beta}\,T\,S_{\delta}\cup S_{\delta}
is an
\eta
-expanding set for
Y
.

Proof.

By scaling the measures
\nu
and
\mu
by the same constant, we can assume for convenience that
\nu(Z)=1
.
By taking
R:=Z
in (
6.8
) and integrating it over all
z\in Z
, we see that
c\leqslant 1
. Thus

\delta\leqslant c\varepsilon\leqslant\varepsilon\leqslant 1/4
and
\beta\leqslant 1/32
.

Take an arbitrary Borel set
U\subseteq Y
. We need to lower bound
\mu(R.U\cap Y)
. Define

X:=\{z\in Z\colon\mu_{z}(S_{\delta}.U)\geqslant(1-\delta)\,\mu_{z}(Y)\}.

Case 1.
Suppose that
\mu(U_{Z\setminus X})>\mu(U)/2
.

Since
S_{\delta}
is leaf-wise
\delta
-expanding, we have for every
z\in Z\setminus X
that
\mu_{z}(S_{\delta}.U)\geqslant\mu_{z}(U)/\delta
. Thus, by (
6.7
),
we obtain the required lower bound as follows:

\mu(R.U\cap Y)\geqslant\mu(S_{\delta}.U\cap Y)\geqslant\int_{Z\setminus X}\frac{\mu_{z}(U)}{\delta}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)=\frac{1}{\delta}\,\mu(U_{Z\setminus X})\geqslant\frac{1}{\delta}\,\frac{\mu(U)}{2}\geqslant\frac{\mu(U)}{\eta}.

The last inequality follows since, by
c\leqslant 1
, we have
\delta\leqslant c\varepsilon=c\eta/(2M^{2})\leqslant\eta/2
.

Case 2.
Suppose that Case 1 does not hold, that is,
\mu(U_{X})\geqslant\mu(U)/2
.

Define
V:=(S_{\delta}.U)_{X}\subseteq Y
.
Since
e\in S_{\delta}
, we have
\mu(V)\geqslant\mu(U_{X})\geqslant\mu(U)/2
. Let

W:=\left\{z\in Z\colon\mu_{z}(T.V)>\delta\,\mu(U)/2\right\}.

By Lemma
6.5
(that is, by our choice of
\delta
), we have

\nu\left(W\right)>1-\varepsilon.

(6.10)

We consider two subcases. First, suppose that
\mu(U)>\varepsilon
. For
z\in W
, we have
\mu_{z}(T.V)>\delta\varepsilon/2=\beta M\geqslant\beta\mu_{z}(Y)

and thus
S_{\beta}
cannot increase the
\mu_{z}
-measure of
(T.V)_{z}
by factor
1/\beta
or larger. Since

S_{\beta}
is leaf-wise
\beta
-expanding, it follows that
\mu_{z}\left(S_{\beta}T.V\right)\geqslant(1-\beta)\,\mu_{z}(Y)
for every
z\in W
.
Thus, by (
6.7
) and (
6.10
),

\displaystyle\mu(R.U\cap Y)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\mu\left(S_{\beta}T.V\cap Y\right)\ \geqslant\ \int_{W}\left(1-\beta\right)\mu_{z}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{Z}\left(1-\beta\right)\mu_{z}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)\,-\,\varepsilon M

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\left(1-\beta\right)\mu(Y)-\varepsilon M^{2}\mu(Y)\ \geqslant\ (1-\eta)\,\mu(Y).

as desired. (Note that
\beta+\varepsilon M^{2}=\varepsilon(\delta/(2M)+M^{2})=\eta(\delta/(4M^{3})+1/2)\leqslant\eta
.)

Finally, suppose that
\mu(U)\leqslant\varepsilon
. Let

B:=\left\{z\in W\colon\mu_{z}\left(S_{\beta}T.V\right)<(1-\beta)\,\mu_{z}(Y)\right\}.

For each
z\in B
, since
S_{\beta}
is
\beta
-expanding for
Y_{z}
and
B\subseteq W
, we have

\mu_{z}\left(S_{\beta}T.V\right)=\mu_{z}(S_{\beta}.(T.V)_{z})\geqslant\frac{\mu_{z}(T.V)}{\beta}\geqslant\frac{\delta\,\mu(U)}{2\beta}.

Thus, using (
6.7
) again, we obtain

\displaystyle\mu(R.U\cap Y)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\mu\left(S_{\beta}T.V\cap Y\right)\ \geqslant\ \int_{W\setminus B}(1-\beta)\,\mu_{z}(Y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z)+\int_{B}\frac{\delta\,\mu(U)}{2\beta}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(z),

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1-\beta}{M}\,\nu(W\setminus B)+\frac{\delta\,\mu(U)}{2\beta}\,\nu(B).

We would like to argue that this is at least
\mu(U)/\eta
. Since both parts are linear in
\mu(U)
it is enough to check only the extreme values of
\mu(U)
. Suppose
that
\mu(U)=\varepsilon
(as the inequality for
\mu(U)=0
trivially holds by
\beta<1
). The coefficient at
\nu(B)
, namely
\delta\varepsilon/(2\beta)=M\geqslant 1
is clearly larger than the coefficient at
\mu(W\setminus B)
. Thus (for
\mu(U)=\varepsilon
) the lower bound on
\mu(R.U\cap Y)
is at least

\frac{1-\beta}{M}\,\nu(W)\geqslant\frac{(1-\beta)(1-\varepsilon)}{M}\geqslant\frac{31}{32}\cdot\frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{M}\geqslant\frac{1}{2M^{2}}=\frac{\mu(U)}{\eta},

as required.

We proceed to apply Proposition
6.6
. Here, we have that
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with the
L^{2}
-norm
\|\cdot\|
and
\Gamma=\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. Define

Y:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\colon 1\leqslant\|y\|\leqslant\rho\},

(6.11)

where
\rho:=1+\sqrt{2}/2
. For
d=1,2,3
, let
\mu^{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
scaled so that
\mu^{d}([0,1]^{d}\times\{0\}^{3-d})=1
. Let
\mu:=\mu^{3}
be the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
.
The foliation of
Y
is given by the concentric spheres, where
(Z,\nu)
is the interval
[1,\rho]
with the Lebesgue measure. Here,
\mu_{z}
is the restriction of
\mu^{2}
to
Y_{z}:=\{y\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}\colon\|y\|=z\}
, the sphere of radius
z
.
By our scaling,
\mu_{z}(Y_{z})=4\pi z^{2}
for
z\in Z
.
The map
Y\to(Z,\nu)\times({\mathbbm{S}}^{2},\mu^{2})
, which sends
x
to
(\|x\|,x/\|x\|)
, is a measure-preserving Borel isomorphism. Since a product of two standard finite measure spaces gives a foliation by Tonelli’s theorem,
we conclude that we indeed have a foliation of
Y
.

Two-dimensional analogue of the set
Y

The following lemma is an exercise in elementary geometry.

Lemma 6.7
.

There is a solid 3-dimensional cube
K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with the following properties.

1.

The side length of
K
is
1
.

2.

There are two opposing faces
A
and
B
of
K
such that
A\cap Y_{1}
consists of the four corners of
A
while
B
is tangent to
Y_{\rho}
.

3.

For every
z\in Z
and every point
x\in K\cap Y_{z}
the angle between the plane tangent to
Y_{z}
at
x
and the plane extending the face
A
is at most
{\pi}/{4}
.

Sketch of Proof.

The cube
K
is pictured in Figure
1
. We construct it as follows. We start by inscribing into
Y_{1}
a 2-dimensional square
A
of side length
1

whose third coordinate is a positive constant. Then we extend
A
to a cube of side length
1
in such a way that
A
is its lower face (lower with respect to the third coordinate in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
). The first property of the lemma is clear by construction.
Let
h
be the height of a triangle whose vertices are the centre of
Y_{1}
together with two opposite vertices of
A
. One can easily compute that
h={\sqrt{2}}/{2}
. Thus
h+1=\rho
and the second property follows.

Let us show the third property. By elementary considerations the angle in question is maximized at points
x
which are corners of the face
A
(when
z=1
). At such
x
the angle in question is equal to the angle between the diagonal of
A
and the tangent plane to
Y_{1}
, and that angle is easily computed to be equal to
{\pi}/{4}
.

The following lemma, which gives
f\in\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{3})
such that
\{f\}
is a diffuser, is a routine calculus exercise.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
f\colon K\to K
be an isometry which maps the face
A
to one of the side faces of
K
. Let
R\subseteq[1,\rho]
be a Borel subset. Then for all
z\in[1,\rho]
we have

\mu_{z}(f(K_{R}))\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\,\mu(K_{R})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\,\frac{\mu(K_{R})}{\nu(Z)}.

Proof.

Let
g\colon K\to A
be the orthogonal projection onto
A
. Let
{\mathcal{L}}
be the set of straight line segments contained in the face
A
that connect
f(B)
to
f(A)
and are orthogonal to both
f(B)
and
f(A)
.

Let us fix
z\in[1,\rho]
and let
(g\circ f)_{z}
be the restriction of
g\circ f
to
f^{-1}(K_{z})
. Note that
(g\circ f)_{z}
is a diffeomorphism from
f^{-1}(K_{z})
to
A
. For
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}
let
\widehat{\ell}
be the preimage of
\ell
under
(g\circ f)_{z}
.

Let
U\subseteq\widehat{\ell}
be a Borel subset. Note that
f
maps
\widehat{\ell}
isometrically into the sphere
K_{z}
. It follows that
we have

\mu^{1}((g\circ f)_{z}(U))\geqslant\cos({\pi}/{4})\,\mu^{1}(U)=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\mu^{1}(U).

Since the map
x\mapsto\|x\|
does not increase distances and maps
K_{R}\cap\widehat{\ell}
onto
R
, we have
\mu^{1}(K_{R}\cap\widehat{\ell})\geqslant\nu(R)
. Consequently we have

\mu^{1}((g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R}\cap\widehat{\ell}))\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\nu(R).

(6.12)

Let us fix
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}
. We clearly have
A=(A\cap f(A))\times\ell
. Furthermore, the elements of
{\mathcal{L}}
are exactly the sets
\{x\}\times\ell
in this decomposition. Therefore, by Tonelli’s theorem, and since the side length of
K
is equal to
1
,

{\mu^{2}((g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R}))}\geqslant\min_{\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}}\mu^{1}((g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R})\cap\ell).

Note that for
\ell\in{\mathcal{L}}
we have
(g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R})\cap\ell=(g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R}\cap\widehat{\ell})
, and so by (
6.12
),

{\mu^{2}((g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R}))}\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\nu(R).

Note that
(g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R})=g(f(K_{R})_{z})
. Hence, since
g
is an orthogonal projection, we have
\mu_{z}(f(K_{R}))=\mu^{2}(f(K_{R})_{z})\geqslant\mu^{2}((g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R}))
. On the other hand, by (
6.7
) we have
\mu(K_{R})\leqslant\nu(R)\sup\{\mu_{z}(K)\colon{z\in R}\}
. Note that for
z\in R
the set
K_{z}
projects onto the face
A
. Hence, by the third item of Lemma
6.7
, we have that

\sup\,\{\mu_{z}(K)\colon{z\in R}\}\leqslant\frac{\mu^{2}(A)}{\cos(\frac{\pi}{4})}=\sqrt{2}.

Putting these inequalities together, we obtain that

\mu_{z}(f(K_{R}))\geqslant\mu^{2}((g\circ f)_{z}(K_{R}))\geqslant\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\,\nu(R)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\,\mu(K_{R}),

which is exactly our claim.

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
\rho:=1+\sqrt{2}/2
and let
X\subseteq[1,\rho]
be a Borel set of positive measure. Then the set
Y:=\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}:\|y\|\in X\}
is a domain of expansion for the action
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{3})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
.

Proof.

Recall that the set
Y
comes with the spherical foliation by
Z=[1,\rho]
.
By the spectral gap property of
\operatorname{SO}(3)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{3}
and Proposition
5.3
,
the set
Y
is a domain of leaf-wise expansion. By Lemma
6.8
,
Y
admits a (single-element) diffuser. Now, the corollary follows from Proposition
6.6
.

We are ready to give another proof for the Euclidean case of Theorem
1.11
.

Second proof of Theorem
1.11
for
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{n})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 3
.

We use induction on
n
. If
n=3
, then
Y:=\{y\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}\colon 1\leqslant\|y\|\leqslant\rho\}
is a domain of expansion by Corollary

. Lemma
2.5
gives that every measurable subset
X\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
that belongs to
{{\mathcal{C}}}
is a domain of expansion (since
X
and

cover each other), as desired.

Suppose that
n\geqslant 4
. By Lemma
2.5
again, we only need to show that
Y:=[0,1]^{n}
is a domain of expansion.
Note that
Y
becomes foliated when we take
Z:=[0,1]
and
Y_{z}:=[0,1]^{n-1}\times\{z\}
for

(with the uniform probability measures).

Take any
\eta>0
. By the inductive assumption, there is a finite
\eta
-expanding set
S\subseteq\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{n-1})
for
[0,1]^{n-1}
. Consider
S^{\prime}:=\{\gamma^{\prime}\colon\gamma\in S\}\subseteq\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{n})
, where we define
\gamma^{\prime}.x:=(\gamma.(x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}),x_{n})
for
x\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
(that is,
\gamma^{\prime}
acts at
\gamma
on the first
n-1
coordinates and is trivial on the last coordinate).
The set
S^{\prime}
is leaf-wise-
\eta
-expanding. Thus

Y
is a domain of leaf-wise expansion.

Let
\gamma
be the isometry of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which is the identity on the first
n-2
coordinates and which is the rotation by
\pi/2
around the centre of
[0,1]^{2}
on the last two coordinates. It is easy to see that for any Borel set
R\subseteq Z
and any

\mu_{z}(\gamma.Y_{R})=\mu(Y_{R})
. In particular, the singleton set

is a diffuser (with the constant

). Now, Proposition

shows that

is a domain of expansion, finishing the proof.

Also, it is easy now to derive Theorem
1.13
from the Introduction, namely the existence of a domain of expansion

,

, which is closed and nowhere dense. (Note that such a set

Proof of Theorem
1.13
.

Let
X
be a closed nowhere dense subset of
[0,1+\sqrt{2}/2]
of positive measure, for example, a “fat” Cantor set.

If
n=3
, let
Y:=\{y\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}:\|y\|\in X\}
; otherwise let
Y:=[0,1]^{n-1}\times X
. The only non-trivial property that we have to check is that
Y
is a domain of expansion. If
n=3
, this is the result of Corollary
6.9
.

Suppose that
n\geqslant 4
. Here, we take the product-space foliation of
Y=[0,1]^{n-1}\times X
by the last factor
Z:=X
. Since the action
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{n-1})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{n-1}
is expanding,
Y
is a domain of leaf-wise expansion.
Similarly to the previous proof, one can show that
Y
admits a diffuser consisting of a single isometry (which is trivial on the first
n-2
coordinates and is a rotation by angle
\pi/2
in the last two coordinates). Thus
Y
is a domain of expansion by Proposition
6.6
.

7
Sample estimates of the number of pieces

In this section we give sample estimates of the number of pieces in some of our constructed equidecompositions. We will need the following result of Lubotzky, Phillips and Sarnak
[
26
,
27
]
.

Theorem 7.1
.

For every integer
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
with
k-1\geqslant 3
being a prime, there is a symmetric
k
-set
Q\subseteq\operatorname{SO}(3)
such that, for the action
\operatorname{SO}(3)\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
, the averaging operator
T_{Q}(f)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\delta\in Q}\delta.f
on
L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{2},\mu)

has the second largest eigenvalue (in absolute value) at most
2\sqrt{k-1}/k
and thus
satisfies

\|T_{Q}f\|_{2}\leqslant(1-c)\|f\|_{2},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for every $f\in L^{2}({\mathbbm{S}}^{2},\mu)$ with $\int_{{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}}f\,\mathrm{d}\mu=0$},

(7.1)

where
c:=1-2\sqrt{k-1}/2k
.

Proof.
Let us outline just the construction of such a set. Take integer quaternions of the form
q=a_{0}+a_{1}\mathbf{i}+a_{2}\mathbf{j}+a_{3}\mathbf{k}
with
\sum_{i=0}^{3}a_{i}^{2}=k-1
,
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
even and
a_{0}>0
odd, and then take the standard map that sends a unit quaternion
q/\sqrt{k-1}
to an element of
\operatorname{SO}(3)
, see e.g.
[
27
, Equation (2.4)]
. There are exactly
k
such quaternions (
[
27
, Equation (2.1)]
) which come in conjugate pairs, giving a symmetric
k
-set of rotations. The stated spectral gap property is exactly the content of
[
27
, Theorem 2.1]
.

Note that the
Alon-Boppana bound
[
38
]
implies that no larger value of
c
can work in (
7.1
) for a symmetric
k
-set
Q
.

First, we do one example where calculations are easy.

Lemma 7.2
.

Consider the action
\operatorname{SO}(3)\curvearrowright({\mathbbm{S}}^{2},\mu)
.
Let
A,B\subseteq{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
be measurable subsets of the same measure such that each contains a closed hemisphere. Then
A
and
B
are essentially Borel equidecomposable with at most
54
pieces.

Proof.

Before equidecomposing, we can realign the sets so that they contain the same closed hemisphere
H\subseteq{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
.
Fix an involution
\gamma\in\operatorname{SO}(3)
with
H\cup\gamma.H={\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
. Let
T:=\{e,\gamma\}
. Thus
T=T^{-1}
and
T.A=T.B={\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
. Let
Q\subseteq\operatorname{SO}(3)
be the set returned by Theorem
7.1
for
k:=18
.
With
c:=1-2\sqrt{k-1}/k
as in Theorem
7.1
, define

f(x):=\frac{x}{(1-c)^{2}+2cx-c^{2}x}=\frac{81x}{64x+17},\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for $x\in[0,1]$}.

Let us show that there is
\varepsilon>0
such that

f(x)\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)\min(2x,2/3),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{for every $x\in(0,1]$}.

(7.2)

First, it is easy to see that for all
x\in[0,1]
we have
f(x)\geqslant\min(2x,2/3)
(which reduces to a linear in
x
inequality on each of the intervals
[0,1/3]
and
[1/3,1]
) with equality if and only if
x=0
.
Also,
f^{\prime}(0)=81/17>2
. Thus there is
\delta>0
such that
f(x)\geqslant(1+\delta)\,2x
for all
x\in[0,\delta)
. By the continuity of the involved functions, there is
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta]
such that (
7.2
) holds in the remaining compact interval
[\delta,1]
, thus proving (
7.2
).

Fix
\varepsilon>0
satisfying (
7.2
) and let
R:=TQ\cup(TQ)^{-1}
.

Let us show that the graph

G:=(A,B,E_{R}\cap(A\times B))
is a bipartite
(\varepsilon/2)
-expander.
Take any measurable
U
inside a part of
G
. By
R=R^{-1}
and the symmetry between
A
and
B
, assume that
U\subseteq A
. By Proposition
5.3
1
, we have
\mu(Q.U)\geqslant f(u)
, where
u:=\mu(U)
. (Recall that
\mu({\mathbbm{S}}^{2})=1
.) If
\mu(Q.U)\geqslant(2+\varepsilon)u
, then by
T.B={\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
we can choose
\beta\in T
with

\mu(N(U))\geqslant\mu(TQ.U\cap B)\geqslant\mu(\beta Q.U\cap B)=\mu(Q.U\cap\beta.B)\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\,\mu(Q.U),

(7.3)

which is at least
(1+\varepsilon/2)u
,
as desired. (Recall that
\beta^{-1}=\beta
for every
\beta\in T
.)
Otherwise, by (
7.2
), we have that
\mu({\mathbbm{S}}^{2}\setminus Q.U)\leqslant 1-f(u)<1/3
. Let
b:=\mu(B)
. If
b\geqslant 2/3
, then
\mu(B\setminus TQ.U)\leqslant\mu({\mathbbm{S}}^{2}\setminus Q.U)<b/2
. Otherwise we have by the argument in (
7.3
) that

\mu(N(U))\geqslant\mu(Q.U)/2>b/2
.
We conclude that
G
is indeed a bipartite
(\varepsilon/2)
-expander.

By Theorem
3.3
,
G
admits an a.e.-perfect Borel matching. By Lemma
2.2
, this matching corresponds to an essential Borel equidecompositions between
A
and
B
using elements from
R
only.
Since
Q
is symmetric and
T\ni e
, we have that
|TQ\cap(TQ)^{-1}|\geqslant|Q|
and thus
|R|\leqslant 2\cdot|T|\cdot|Q|-|Q|=54
, as required.

Next, we give a rough estimate of the number of pieces needed for measurable equidecomposition between a cube and a Euclidean ball in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
. Our purpose is just to demonstrate how an explicit bound can be derived from our results.

First, we need to understand how large the constructed
\eta
-expanding sets are.
For a domain of expansion
Y\subseteq\Omega
and
\eta\in(0,1)
, let
s(\eta;Y)
be the smallest
s\in{\mathbbm{N}}
such that there is a real
\delta\in(0,\eta)
and a
\delta
-expanding set
S\subseteq\Gamma
for
Y
with
|S|\leqslant s
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\eta\in(0,1)
. Let
k\equiv 2\pmod{4}
be such that
k-1
is a prime. Define
c:=1-2\sqrt{k-1}/k
and

c^{\prime}:=c(2-c)=\frac{(k-2)^{2}}{k^{2}}.

If
\ell\in{\mathbbm{N}}
satisfies
(1+c^{\prime}\eta)^{\ell}>1/\eta
, then
s(\eta;{{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}})\leqslant 1+\sum_{i=0}^{\ell-1}k(k-1)^{i}
.

Proof.
Let
Q\subseteq\operatorname{SO}(3)
be the symmetric
k
-set returned by Theorem
7.1
that satisfies (
7.1
).
By Proposition
5.3
2
,
the set
Q^{\ell}
is
\eta
-expanding for the whole space
{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
. The size of
Q^{\ell}
can be upper bounded by the number of words over
Q
of length at most
\ell
that do not contain an element next to its inverse, giving the stated bound.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
A
and
B
be, respectively, a cube and a ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of the same volume. Let
Y
be the annulus, as defined in (
6.11
). Then
A
and
B
are measurably equidecomposable with
at most
1400\cdot s(1/1109;Y)+2000
pieces.

Proof.

First we rescale
A
and
B
by the same factor so that each fits inside the annulus. Namely, we scale the ball to have radius
r:=(\rho-1)/2
. Then the cube has side length
\lambda:=(\frac{4\pi}{3})^{1/3}r
and elementary calculations show that it fits into
Y
(e.g. touching the unit sphere
Y_{1}
in the centre of its face). We can cover
Y
by at most
m:=\lceil 2\rho/\lambda\rceil^{3}
scaled cubes, arranged as a grid. Also, if we take a maximal packing of balls of radius
r/2
with centres in
Y
, then balls with the same centres but of the twice larger radius
r
cover
Y
. Since the smaller balls are disjoint and lie entirely between the spheres of radii
1-r/2
and
\rho+r/2
, we have at most
n:=\lfloor((\rho+\frac{r}{2})^{3}-(1-\frac{r}{2})^{3})/(\frac{r}{2})^{3}\rfloor
balls. Numerical calculations show that

m=216
and
n=1109
.
Let
N,M\subseteq\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{3})
be the sets of translations of these sizes that achieve
M.A\supseteq Y
and
N.B\supseteq Y
.
By translating the ball and the cube, we can assume that each of
M
and
N
contains the identity.
Let
S\subseteq\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{3})
be an
\eta
-expanding set for
Y
with
\eta<1/1109
of size
s(1/1109;Y)
. Define
R:=S^{-1}M\cup N^{-1}S
.

Let us show that the graph
G:=(A,B,E_{R}\cap(A\times B))
generated by
R
with parts
A
and
B
is a bipartite
c
-expander with
c:=1/(1109\,\eta)-1>0
. Indeed, take any measurable set
U
inside one part, say
U\subseteq B
. Note that the neighbourhood of
U
in
A
is
R^{-1}.U\cap A\supseteq M^{-1}S.U\cap A
. If
\mu(S.U\cap Y)\geqslant\mu(U)/\eta
then, by
M.A\supseteq Y
,
there is
\gamma\in M
with

\mu(\gamma^{-1}S.U\cap A)=\mu(S.U\cap\gamma.A)\geqslant\frac{\mu(S.U\cap Y)}{m}\geqslant\frac{\mu(U)}{\eta m}\geqslant(1+c)\mu(U),

as desired. Otherwise, by the choice of
S
, we have
\mu(Y\setminus S.U)\leqslant\eta\mu(Y)<\mu(A)/2
. (Note that
\mu(Y)/\mu(A)=(\rho^{3}-1)/r^{3}<1109/2
.) Since
M\ni e
and
A\subseteq Y
, we have
\mu(M^{-1}S.U\cap A)\geqslant\mu(S.U\cap A)>\mu(A)/2
. Thus
G
is indeed a bipartite
c
-expander.

By Theorem
3.3
, the set
A
and
B
are essentially Borel equidecomposable using elements from
R
only.
In order to get a measurable exact decomposition between
A
and
B
, it is enough by Proposition
3.4
1
to add some isometries to
R
showing that
A
and
B
are set-theoretically equidecomposable.
By the standard proof of Banach-Schröder-Bernstein Lemma (Lemma
4.1
), it is enough that the extra isometries show, in the notation of Section
4
, that
[A]\preccurlyeq[B]
and
[B]\preccurlyeq[A]
. The original proof of the Banach-Tarski Paradox shows that the ball doubling
[B]\sim 2[B]
can be shown with 5 isometries and thus
2^{k}[B]\preccurlyeq[B]
can be shown with
5^{k}
isometries.
Rather roughly, 8 copies each of the sets
A
and
B
are enough to cover the other:
indeed, “one eighth” of each set, namely
[0,\lambda/2]^{3}
and
\{v\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}\colon v\geqslant 0,\,\|v\|_{2}\leqslant r\}
respectively, can be covered by one translate of the other set.
Thus
[A]\preccurlyeq 8[B]\preccurlyeq[B]
can be shown with
8\cdot 5^{3}
isometries. Also,
8[B]\preccurlyeq[B]\preccurlyeq 8[A]
needs at most
5^{3}\cdot 8
isometries. The relation
8[B]\preccurlyeq 8[A]
means that these isometries can be used to construct a bipartite (multi-)graph
H
with parts
A
and
B
such that every vertex of
A
(resp.
B
) has degree at most
8
(resp. exactly
8
). By Rado’s theorem
[
39
]
,
H
has a matching covering every vertex of
B
. Thus we can show
[B]\preccurlyeq[A]
with at most 1000 isometries. In total, we need to add at most
2000
further elements to
R
to satisfy the lemma.

Remark 7.5
.

In order to estimate the number of pieces in Lemma
7.4
, we need to estimate the function
s(\eta;Y)
for the annulus
Y
.
By Lemma
6.8
, there is a single-element
c
-diffuser
T:=\{f\}
with
c:=1/(2\sqrt{2})
. As we are free to scale each
\mu_{z}
by
\alpha
and
\nu
by
1/\alpha
, we can assume that
\mu_{1}(Y)=1/\rho
and
\mu_{\rho}(Y)=\rho
. Thus, when we apply Proposition
6.6
, we can take
M:=\rho
. For any
\eta\in(0,1/2)
, Proposition
6.6
tells us to set
\varepsilon:=\eta/(2\rho^{2})
,
\delta:=\varepsilon c/(\varepsilon+|T|)
and
\beta:=\delta\varepsilon/(2\rho)
. In particular, if we take

\eta=1/1109
, then our numerical calculations indicate that
\delta=5.46...\cdot 10^{-5}
and
\beta=2.47...\cdot 10^{-9}
, and that
k:=6
and
\ell:=4.3\cdot 10^{5}
(resp.
\ell:=1.8\cdot 10^{10}
) satisfy Lemma
7.3
when estimating
s(\delta;Y)
(resp.
s(\beta;Y)
).
Thus, by taking minimum expanding sets
S_{\delta},S_{\beta}\subseteq\operatorname{SO}(3)
in Proposition
6.6
, we get that at most

1400\cdot|S_{\delta}|\cdot(|S_{\beta}|+1)+2000<5^{1.81\cdot 10^{10}}<20^{10^{10}}

pieces should be enough to measurably equidecompose the ball and the cube.
Of course, many improvements of this bound
are possible, even with
very simple additional ideas.
However, we do not see a way to obtain any reasonable bounds here, so we do not pursue this direction any further.

8
Proof of Theorem
1.14

Theorem
1.14
follows from the following result where we have collected all the properties that are used in our proof: it is trivial to check that each action listed in Example
1.9
(which is expanding by Theorem
1.11
)
satisfies all assumptions of Lemma
8.1
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Under Assumptions
1.4
and
1.5
, let the action
a:\Gamma\curvearrowright\Omega

be paradoxical and expanding. Suppose that the family of meager subsets of
\Omega
is
a
-invariant, the measure
\mu
is atomless and there is a non-empty open set
W\subseteq\Omega
with
\mu(W)<\infty
. Let

{{\mathcal{A}}}:=\{X\subseteq\Omega\colon X\in{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}}\mbox{ and $\overline{X}$ is compact}\}.

Then every mean
\kappa:{{\mathcal{A}}}\to[0,\infty)
is a constant multiple of the measure
\mu
.

Proof.

If
|\Omega|=1
, then there is nothing to do. Otherwise, by e.g. the paradoxicality of the action,
\Omega
has infinitely many points. Fix pairwise disjoint non-empty open sets
U_{1},U_{2},U_{3}\subseteq\Omega
with compact closures.

Claim 8.1.1
.

Let
i\in[3]
and
A,B\in{{\mathcal{A}}}
be subsets of
\Omega\setminus U_{i}
.

1.

If
\mu(A)=\mu(B)
, then
\kappa(A)=\kappa(B)
.

2.

If
\mu(A)\geqslant r\mu(B)>0
for some
r\in{\mathbbm{R}}
, then
\kappa(A)\geqslant r\kappa(B)
.

Proof of Claim.

Let us show the first part. The sets
A^{\prime}:=A\cup U_{i}
and
B^{\prime}:=B\cup U_{i}
are in
{{\mathcal{A}}}
(since
U_{i}\in{{\mathcal{A}}}
while
{{\mathcal{A}}}
is closed under unions).

Since the open set
W
with
\mu(W)<\infty
covers every other set in
{{\mathcal{C}}}
by Lemma
2.3
and
\mu
is invariant, we have for every
Y\in{{\mathcal{C}}}
that
\mu(Y)<\infty
(and that
\mu(Y)>0
since
Y
covers a non-empty open set). In particular,
0<\mu(A^{\prime})=\mu(B^{\prime})<\infty
. Since
the action
a
is expanding, the sets
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are domains of expansion.
By Proposition
1.8
2
(resp. Theorem
1.6
2
), the sets
A
and
B
are Baire (resp. Lebesgue) equidecomposable. Thus, by Proposition
3.4
2
, these sets are Baire-Lebesgue equidecomposable, say with pieces
A^{\prime}=A_{1}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup A_{m}
and
B^{\prime}=B_{1}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup B_{m}
. Each piece belongs to
{{\mathcal{A}}}
: it is in
{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}}
by definition and has compact closure as a subset of
A^{\prime}
or
B^{\prime}
.
By the finite
additivity and
a
-invariance of
\kappa
, we have

\kappa(A^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\kappa(A_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\kappa(B_{j})=\kappa(B^{\prime}).

Thus
\kappa(A)=\kappa(A^{\prime})-\kappa(U_{i})=\kappa(B^{\prime})-\kappa(U_{i})=\kappa(B)
, proving the first part of the claim.

Let us show the second part.

First, assume that
r=p/q
with
p,q\in{\mathbbm{N}}
.
We use a consequence of Sierpiński’s theorem
[
43
]
(for a modern proof see e.g.
[
13
, Proposition A.1]
) that if
(\Omega^{\prime},{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
is an arbitrary non-atomic
measure space with
\mu^{\prime}(\Omega^{\prime})<\infty
, then
for every
\rho\in[0,\mu^{\prime}(\Omega^{\prime})]
there is
Y\in{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\prime}
with
\mu^{\prime}(Y)=\rho
. By applying this result to the
\sigma
-algebra

{{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}}\supseteq{{\mathcal{A}}}
of all Baire-Lebesgue subsets of
\Omega
, we can find
({{\mathcal{B}}}_{\mu}\cap{{\mathcal{T}}})
-measurable partitions

A=A_{0}\sqcup A_{1}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup A_{p}
and
B=B_{1}\sqcup\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\sqcup B_{q}
such that each of the
p+q
sets
A_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,A_{p},B_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,B_{q}

has measure exactly
\mu(B)/q
. Again, each piece is in
{{\mathcal{A}}}
. We conclude by the first part of the claim that all these sets, except
A_{0}
, have the same
\kappa
-value which has to be
\kappa(B)/q
.
Thus

\kappa(A)\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{p}\kappa(A_{i})\geqslant p\,\kappa(B)/q
, as required.

Now, take an arbitrary positive
r\in{\mathbbm{R}}
. Suppose on the contrary that
\kappa(A)<r\,\kappa(B)
. Then, trivially,
\kappa(B)>0
and there are
p,q\in{\mathbbm{N}}
with
\kappa(A)<\frac{p}{q}\,\kappa(B)
and
p/q<r
. However, this contradicts the conclusion of the previous paragraph.

Observe that, for every
\mu
-null set
A\in{{\mathcal{A}}}
, we have that
\kappa(A)=0
.
Indeed, since
A=(A\setminus U_{1})\sqcup(A\cap U_{1})
, it is
enough by the additivity of
\kappa
to prove this when
A\subseteq\Omega\setminus U_{i}
for some
i\in[3]
. By taking
B=\emptyset
in the first part of Claim
8.1.1
we get that
\kappa(A)=\kappa(\emptyset)=0
, as desired.

This and the second part of Claim
8.1.1
give for every
i\in[3]
that there is
c_{i}\in{\mathbbm{R}}
such that, for every
{{\mathcal{A}}}
-subset

A
of
\Omega\setminus U_{i}
, we have
\kappa(A)=c_{i}\,\mu(A)
. Furthermore, by
\kappa(U_{3})=c_{1}\,\mu(U_{3})=c_{2}\,\mu(U_{3})

and
0<\mu(U_{3})<\infty
, we conclude that
c_{1}=c_{2}
. This constant
c:=c_{1}=c_{2}
works for every
A\in{{\mathcal{A}}}
:

\kappa(A)=\kappa(A\cap U_{1})+\kappa(A\setminus U_{1})=c_{2}\,\mu(A\cap U_{1})+c_{1}\,\mu(A\setminus U_{1})=c\,\mu(A),

finishing the proof of the lemma.

9
Concluding Remarks

Note that there are paradoxical actions which are not expanding. For example, take two isometries in
SO(3)
such that they generate a rank-2 free group
F
and the
action
b:F\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}
has spectral gap, let
c:{\mathbbm{Z}}\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{S}}^{1}
where the generator of
{\mathbbm{Z}}
is a rotation by some angle
\theta\not\in\pi{\mathbbm{Q}}
, and consider the action
a:(F\times{\mathbbm{Z}})\curvearrowright\Omega
, where
\Omega:={\mathbbm{S}}^{2}\times{\mathbbm{S}}^{1}
and
a((\gamma,n),(x,y)):=(b(\gamma,x),c(n,y))
.
Since
b
is paradoxical (e.g. by
[
50
, Theorem 5.5]
), we can double the whole space
\Omega
also under the action
a
(by using only elements of the form
(\gamma,0)\in F\times{\mathbbm{Z}}
). Since every orbit of
b
or
c
is dense (in the former case by, for example, the proof of
[
26
, Theorem 2.7]
), this also holds for the new action
a
. Thus, with respect to the action
a
, every set with non-empty interior covers
\Omega
and is equidecomposable to
\Omega
by the proof of Proposition
1.8
1
. It follows that the action
a
is paradoxical. On the other hand, the products
{\mathbbm{S}}^{2}\times Y
, where we take for
Y

almost
b
-invariant sets of measure
1/2
, are almost
a
-invariant sets. This shows that the action
a

is not expanding.

An interesting open question that remains is whether any two bounded Borel subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
,

n\geqslant 3
, that have non-empty interior and the same Lebesgue measure are equidecomposable
with Borel pieces. Our Theorem
1.2
implies that the answer is in the affirmative,
provided we can first remove a Borel null set from each set.

Also, it would be interesting to find some alternative characterization of bounded measurable subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 3
, that are domains of expansion. By Lemma
2.5
, essentially covering a non-empty open set is a sufficient condition. However, it is not necessary by Theorem
1.13
.

Of course, the above questions can also be asked for other group actions. In particular, they are open for all actions listed in Example
1.9
, as far as the authors know.

Another interesting question is to get reasonable upper bounds on the sizes of expanding sets (namely to estimate the function
s(\eta;X)
from Section
7
) when, for example,
X=[0,1]^{3}
is the unit cube under the action
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathbbm{R}}^{3})\curvearrowright{\mathbbm{R}}^{3}
.
