Unified approach to Floquet lattices, topological insulators, and their nonlinear dynamics
Mark Ablowitz, Justin T. Cole, S. D. Nixon

Abstract
A unified method to analyze the dynamics and topological structure associated with a class of Floquet topological insulators is presented. The method is applied to a system that describes the propagation of electromagnetic waves through the bulk of a two-dimensional lattice that is helically-driven in the direction of propagation. Tight-binding approximations are employed to derive reduced dynamical systems. Further asymptotic approximations, valid in the high-frequency driving regime, yield a time-averaged system which governs the leading order behavior of the wave. From this follows an analytic calculation of the Berry connection, curvature and Chern number by analyzing the local behavior of the eigenfunctions near the critical points of the spectrum. Examples include honeycomb, Lieb and kagome lattices. In the nonlinear regime novel equations governing slowly varying wave envelopes are derived. For the honeycomb lattice, numerical simulations show that for relatively small nonlinear effects a striking spiral patterns occurs; as nonlinearity increases localized structures emerge and for somewhat higher nonlinearity the waves collapse.



1
Introduction

The theoretical study of topological insulators began nearly 40 years ago with the
Quantum Hall Effect
in electronics
[
1
,
2
]
. Topological insulators are materials that behave as an insulator in its interior, but conduct edge waves along their boundaries. An effective model predicting the same phenomena for periodic systems without any magnetic flux termed the
Quantum Anomalous Hall Effect
), was introduced by Haldane in
[
3
]
.
The underlying structure was later reinterpreted to fit a photonic setting where light waves in an optical lattice with suitable permittivity took the place of electrons in magnetic field
[
4
]
.

The quantization of the Hall conductance can be expressed in terms of invariant integrals
[
5
]
and gives a unique classification for the geometric structure of the problem
[
6
]
. At the same time, by interpreting the eigenspace of a Hermetian operator as a line bundle over its parameter space
[
7
]
, it follows that these topological
invariants are related to the Berry phase
[
8
]
. In an optical
setting, this means the Floquet-Bloch spectral bands for a periodic waveguide array, form a natural line bundle over the Brillouin zone. The relevant characterization is the Chern class (and the related Chern number)
[
9
]
.

The broken time-reversal symmetry that characterizes Chern insulators and protected edge states has since been realized experimentally using numerous techniques. In
[
10
]
, the first observation of an electromagnetic protected edge state, capable of propagating around defects, was observed in a magneto-optical setting. In this set up, the symmetry breaking was achieved by the application of an external magnetic field to a square lattice of ferrite rods.

An experiment in the photonic regime that does not rely on magnetic fields was reported in
[
11
]
.
Here a honeycomb lattice is constructed with waveguides that are helically varying in the direction of beam propagation, which acts as the symmetry breaking mechanism and supports topologically protected edge states.
Under an appropriate change of variables, this has the same effect as a magnetic field in the governing Schrödinger equation.
A tight-binding system in linear and nonlinear regimes was derived in
[
12
]
.
The field of
Topological Photonics
has since seen many important developments with novel experimental designs that realize spectral bands with underlying nontrivial topological invariants (see review articles
[
13
,
14
]
).

Topological invariants can only change discretely; as a result, induced behavior will be robust against perturbations/defects.
The phase transition points of the band structure, where the Chern number change, occur at degeneracies of the linear spectrum.
In a 2D optical lattice, the simplest point degeneracies occur at conically shaped Dirac points in the dispersion bands. Thus, the modulation of honeycomb, Lieb, or kagome lattices, all of which exhibit Dirac/Dirac-like points in their band structure, can lead to nontrivial topologies. The edges states for honeycomb
[
12
,
15
]
and Lieb
[
16
]
lattices with longitudinal driving have been investigated analytically using tight-binding approximations in the lattice nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS). Here, nonlinear edge solitons are also found to be unidirectional solutions.
A tight binding model associated with magnetized ferrite rods was recently derived using Wannier functions in
[
17
]
.

This paper presents a unified approach that starts with the lattice NLS equations and derives averaged, constant coefficient, models suitable for the analytical study of underlying Floquet-Bloch eigenfunctions,
computation of the Chern number and modeling nonlinear dynamics of the original equation. We use a tight-binding approximation that forgoes the typical Peierls transformation in favor of a more analytically tractable discrete system and further reduce the model by averaging the effect of modulations in the direction of propagation in the bulk (not gap) regime. We apply this method to a wide range of examples including honeycomb, Lieb and kagome lattices in an effort to both illustrate it’s ease of use and highlight the commonalities among topological spectra. For honeycomb lattices, it is noteworthy that this averaging technique connects with the well known Haldane model
[
3
]
in Brillouin/Fourier space.

In the nonlinear regime, we derive envelope equations for the evolution of wave packets near the degeneracy of the linear spectrum. There are two common spectral phenomena we consider. The first is the typical Dirac point which appears in the spectrum of the honeycomb and kagome lattices. The second is a Dirac point with a flat band intersecting it as occurs in the Lieb and 1/5 depleted lattices and results in a novel third-order system. In both cases, we investigate the relative effects of driving and nonlinear refraction.

The nonlinear evolution in the case of a honeycomb lattice is particularly interesting. The equations are a Dirac system with an additional Dirac mass term
which was not present in prior conical diffraction studies
[
18
,
19
]
. With this extra term numerical computations show that with fixed mass at relatively small nonlinearity there are striking spiral patterns that gradually disperse; as nonlinearity increases a localized stationary soliton-like structure appears and at yet higher nonlinearity collapse occurs. This similar to what occurs with two dimensional NLS equations cf.
[
20
]
.

1.1
Governing Wave Equation

The governing equation for intense paraxial light propagating in an optical lattice is the nonlinear Schrödinger equation, which we write in normalized form as

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial z}+\nabla^{2}\Psi-V(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}},z)\Psi+\sigma\left|\Psi\right|^{2}\Psi=0

(1)

where
\Psi
is the envelope of the electric field,
z
is the propagation distance,
\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}
is the transverse plane, and the potential
V(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}},z)
is the index of refraction for a doubly periodic waveguide array in the transverse direction subject to longitudinal driving in the
z
direction

V(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}},z)={V}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}-\mathrm{\mathbf{h}}(z)).

(2)

Optical lattices of this type have been constructed experimentally
[
11
,
21
]
and used in the construction of topological insulators.
The broken time-symmetry created by rotating the lattice induces an effective magnetic field and yields nontrivial topology in the spectrum and the construction of topologically protected edge modes.

The base lattice is doubly periodic in the transverse plane with periods
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}
and consists of
L
sublattices.
A unit cell of the lattice can be pictured as a parallelogram with sides
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}
and
L
lattice sites in its interior, one from each sublattice; see Figures
1
,
4(a)
,
6
and
7
.
The full lattice comes from tiling the plane with copies of the unit cell, i.e.,

V(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})=V_{0}^{2}~-\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\widetilde{V}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}-\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}_{nm}^{\ell})

(3)

where
\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}_{nm}^{\ell}=n~\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}+m~\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}+\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{\ell}
, the vectors
\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{\ell}
define the locations of the sublattice sites within the unit cell and
(n,m)
is a numbering of the unit cell tiling. For simplicity, we assume that the individual lattice sites have a uniform Gaussian shape,

\widetilde{V}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})=V_{0}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}^{2}/w}

(4)

and the lattice sites occur at the minimums of
{V}
. We take driving function of the form

\mathrm{\mathbf{h}}(z)=\frac{\kappa}{\omega}\Big(\cos(\omega z),~\sin(\omega z)\Big).

(5)

It is now convenient to move to a frame of reference that follows the lattice,
\widetilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}-\mathrm{\mathbf{h}}(z)
, which transforms equation (
1
) into

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial z}-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(z)\cdot\widetilde{\nabla}\Psi+\widetilde{\nabla}^{2}\Psi-{V}\big(\widetilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}}\big)\Psi+\sigma\left|\Psi\right|^{2}\Psi=0.

(6)

In contrast to some previous discrete models for Floquet lattices, cf.
[
11
,
15
,
16
]

we do not employ the common Peierls transform. As a useful consequence, the coefficients of the final averaged system can be expressed in a closed form. For future work, this allows for the study of lattices where the waveguides are not rotating in unison since the
\mathrm{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}
term can be treated as the local rotation for the purposes of tight-binding approximations. Results for this non-Peierls approximation agree with those found in models that do take the Peierls transform on lattices that have a moving frame of reference. In Section
3
, we consider a the fast oscillation,
\omega\gg 1
, regime. Alternate methods instead consider an adiabatic Floquet problem
[
22
]
.

2
Tight-binding approximation

The tight-binding approximation assumes a strong lattice regime where the contrast between the waveguides and the background is large, i.e.,
V_{0}\gg 1

[
19
,
12
,
15
]
.
With the lattice sites well-separated, we replace equation (
1
) by a suitable discrete model.
Local behavior near each lattice site is approximated by an exponentially localized orbital function that approximates the discrete eigenmode for a single site lattice times a modulating function that adjusts the local interaction magnitude.
Due to the rapid decay of these orbitals, only the interactions between nearest neighbor lattice sites are considered in the final approximation.

In the neighborhood around
\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}_{nm}^{\ell}
, we define
\phi_{nm}^{\ell}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})=\phi_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}-\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}_{nm}^{\ell})
where
\phi_{0}(\bm{r})
is the orbital for a lattice site centered at the origin satisfying the equation

\left[\mu+\nabla^{2}+\widetilde{V}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})\right]\phi_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})=0.

(7)

where the orbital function has been normalized to have
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
norm one. The orbital expansion for the wave field,
\psi
, is now given by

\psi(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}},z)\approx\sum_{(n,m)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}a_{nm}^{\ell}(z)\phi_{nm}^{\ell}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\left(\mu+V_{0}^{2}\right)z}

(8)

where each orbital function
\phi_{nm}^{\ell}(\mathrm{\mathbf{r}})
is modulated by a related coefficient
a_{nm}^{\ell}(z)
.
A system of difference equations is obtained by substituting anatz (
8
) into the modified Schrödinger equation (
1
) and taking the inner product of the result with
\phi_{nm}^{\ell}
for each of lattice sites.

By combining the cluster of lattice sites within each unit cell,
\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_{nm}(z)=(a_{nm}^{1}(z),\ldots,a_{nm}^{\ell}(z))^{T}
, we arrive at a nearest neighbor system of difference equations. Note: No interaction occurs more than one unit cell away in any direction.

\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_{nm}}{\mathrm{d}z}+\sum_{i=-1}^{1}\sum_{j=-1}^{1}\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j}(z)\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_{n+i,m+j}(z)+\sigma\rm{N}(\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_{nm})\,\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}_{nm}=0

(9)

where the
\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j}
are a set of connectivity matrices,
\rm{N}(\mathrm{\mathbf{a}})=\rm{diag}\left(|a^{1}|^{2},\ldots,|a^{L}|^{2}\right)
is the onsite nonlinear response and
\widetilde{\sigma}
is a constant.
The connectivity matrices are given by the definition

\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j}(z)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell k}}\delta_{ij}(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell k})\big[q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell k})\big]_{\ell k}

(10)

where the inner sum is taken over all the vectors
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}
that point from a lattice site on the sublattice
\ell
to a nearest neighbor on any other sublattice
k
, the delta function
\delta_{ij}(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})
is
1

\spadesuit
if the vector
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}
with starting point
\mathrm{\mathbf{r}}_{00}^{\ell}
has its end point in the lattice cell
(i,j)
and
0
otherwise
\spadesuit
,
and
\big[q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})\big]_{\ell k}
is the matrix with the function
q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})
in row
\ell
column
k
and
0
everywhere else. For example, the matrix
\mathcal{L}_{1}^{0}(z)
will have one element, of the form
q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})
, for each lattice site that has a nearest neighbor in the lattice cell one over in the direction
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}
, whereas
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{0}(z)
will have one element for each nearest within the same lattice cell. The connectivity matrices follow the symmetry properties,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j}(z)

\displaystyle=\Big[\mathcal{L}_{-i}^{~j}(z)\Big]^{H}

(11a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}^{j}(z)

\displaystyle=\Big[\mathcal{L}_{~i}^{-j}(z)\Big]^{H}

(11b)

where
H
is the conjugate transpose.
See Appendix
A
for further details.

The topological properties of the lattice, such as the Chern Number, come from the associated complex vector bundle constructed by taking the reciprocal unit cell, or Brillouin zone, as a base space with attached vector space at each point defined by the eigenspace for a particular band, see Section
4
. To carry this out, we take a discrete Fourier transform of the system (
9
), i.e, we assume plane waves of the form

\widetilde{\mathrm{\mathbf{a}}}_{nm}(z)=\bm{\alpha}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}},z)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(n\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}+m\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2})},

(12)

where
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}=k_{x}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}}+k_{y}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}}
and move the problem to the wavenumber domain. The end result of the tight-binding approximations is a system of nonlinear differential equations, parameterized by
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
, of the form

\mathrm{i}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}}{\partial z}+M\left(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\right)\bm{\alpha}+\widetilde{\sigma}\rm{N}(\bm{\alpha})\bm{\alpha}=0.

(13)

where the matrix
M(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
is
L\times L
, periodic in
z
, doubly periodic in
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
, Hermitian, due to the symmetry of the pairwise interactions, and has zeros along the diagonal, since any onsite interactions can be removed by a phase transformation. The individual elements of
M(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
can be written as

M_{ij}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{j}-\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{i})}\sum_{\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}}q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}}

(14)

where

q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})=q_{0}-\mathrm{i}s_{0}\mathrm{\mathbf{h}}^{\prime}(z)\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{w}},

(15)

is a
z
-dependent coefficient function, with
q_{0}
and
s_{0}
constants, derived in the Appendix
A
and the sum is taken over the set of vectors,
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}
, that point from a site on sublattice
i
to a site on sublattice
j
(see
1
,
4(a)
,
6
or
7
).

3
Averaged System

Since equation (
13
) is a system of differential equations with periodic coefficients, it usually does not have an explicit solution. So, we consider the case where the frequency of the driving
\mathrm{\mathbf{A}}(z)
occur on a faster scale than the background.
This leads to an averaged system, independent of
z
.
Employing a multiple scales approximation where the lattice matrix is a function of the fast scale:
\zeta=\displaystyle\frac{z}{\varepsilon}
,
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
,

M\left(z\right)=M\left(\displaystyle\zeta\right),

(16)

and the eigenvectors are expanded as
\bm{\alpha}=\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\varepsilon\bm{\alpha}_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{\alpha}_{2}\ldots\,
. In terms of the driven potential in Section
1.1
, we define
\omega=\varepsilon^{-1}
. Note: The dependence on
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
is not explicitly represented for this analysis, since the steps proceed independently.

Matrix lattice equation (
13
) becomes

\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}}{\partial\zeta}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}}{\partial z}+M\left(\zeta\right){\bm{\alpha}}=0.

(17)

At
\mathrm{O}(\varepsilon^{-1})
,

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{0}}{\partial\zeta}=0

(18)

and as a result
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
will be strictly a function of
z
, i.e.,
\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\zeta,z)=\bm{\alpha}_{0}(z)
. At
\mathrm{O}(1)
,

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}{\partial\zeta}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{0}}{\partial z}+M(\zeta)\bm{\alpha}_{0}=0.

(19)

To avoid secular growth in
\bm{\alpha}_{1}
, we require that the average effect of the forcing from
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
is at higher order.
Define
\left<f\right>=\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}f(\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta
where
T
is the period of
M(\zeta)
. We note that the terms:

\mathrm{i}\partial_{z}\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\left<M\right>\bm{\alpha}_{0}

grow without bound in
\zeta
.
Now, the secularity condition can be written as

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{0}}{\partial z}+\left<M\right>\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\varepsilon\mathrm{\mathbf{F}}_{0}.

(20)

Substituting
\mathrm{i}\partial_{z}\bm{\alpha}_{0}=-\left<M\right>\bm{\alpha}_{0}
into equation (
19
) allows us to solve for first correction as

\bm{\alpha}_{1}=\mathrm{i}M_{1}(\zeta)\bm{\alpha}_{0}(z)

(21)

where
M_{1}(\zeta)=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\zeta}\left(M\left(\widetilde{\zeta}\right)-\left<M\right>\right)\mathrm{d}\tilde{\zeta}
.

At
\mathrm{O}(\varepsilon)
,

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}{\partial\zeta}+\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{1}}{\partial z}+M(\zeta)\bm{\alpha}_{1}+\mathrm{\mathbf{F}}_{0}=0

(22)

where the terms left over from the previous order must now be balanced as well.
We can use (
21
) to replace
\bm{\alpha}_{1}
and once again substitute
\mathrm{i}\partial_{z}\bm{\alpha}_{0}=-\left\langle M\right\rangle\bm{\alpha}_{0}
to simplify the correction terms we will be deriving for the
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
equation.
These substitutions transform equation (
22
) into

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{2}}{\partial\zeta}

\displaystyle+\mathrm{i}\big(M(\zeta)M_{1}(\zeta)-M_{1}(\zeta)\left<M\right>\big)\bm{\alpha}_{0}

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{0}}{\partial z}+\left<M\right>\bm{\alpha}_{0}\right)=0

(23)

The secularity condition now gives us a final (for our purposes) higher order equation for
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
that will be used to approximate the spectral properties of the lattice,

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}_{0}}{\partial z}+\left\langle M\right\rangle\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\varepsilon\left\langle N\right\rangle\bm{\alpha}_{0}=0

(24)

where
\left\langle N\right\rangle=\mathrm{i}\left[\left<MM_{1}\right>-\left<M_{1}\right>\left<M\right>\right]
.
The average equation (
24
) has constant coefficients and hence allows for significant analytic insights; e.g. the computation of the Chern number.

For
M(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
of the form derived in the tight-binding analysis from Section
2
and determined by the nearest neighbor interactions in the lattice, the matrix
\left\langle N\right\rangle
will be determined by all the next-nearest neighbors. This means, the averaged equation at first order is

\left\langle M\right\rangle_{ij}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{j}-\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{i})}\sum_{\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}}q_{0}e^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}}

(25)

where
\left\langle q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\right\rangle=q_{0}
and
the elements of the first correct matrix,
\left\langle N\right\rangle
, take the form

\varepsilon\left\langle N\right\rangle_{ij}=e^{-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{j}-\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{i})}\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2}}Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}+\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})}

(26)

where the vectors
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
are such that
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}
points from an element of sublattice
i
to any nearest neighbor and
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
that starts at the tip of
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}
and ends at an element of the sublattice
j
. So, the averaged system (
24
) depends on next-nearest neighbors, even though the original Floquet system (
13
) only depends on nearest neighbor interactions.
The coefficients for the next-nearest neighbor terms are defined by the function

\displaystyle Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})=\mathrm{i}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\left\langle q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1})\int_{0}^{z}q(\tilde{z},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})-\left\langle q(\tilde{z},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\left\langle\int_{0}^{z}q(\tilde{z},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1})-\left\langle q(\tilde{z},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}\right\rangle\left\langle q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\right\rangle\Bigg)

which can be found explicitly for the driving function (
5
) as

Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})=\Big(\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}s_{0}^{2}\kappa^{2}(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}\wedge\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})+q_{0}s_{0}\kappa(w_{1x}-w_{2x})\Big).

(27)

Here
\wedge
is the wedge product
(w_{x},w_{y})\wedge(v_{x},v_{y})=w_{x}v_{y}-v_{x}w_{y}
.

4
Chern Number

We are now situated to analytically calculate the Chern number from the related eigenvalue problem
\bm{\alpha}_{0}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda z}\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\lambda\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\left(\left\langle M\right\rangle+\varepsilon\left\langle N\right\rangle\right)\bm{\alpha}_{0}=0.

(28)

The number of eigenvalues/eigenvectors depends on the number of sublattices
L
; e.g, for a honeycomb lattice
M
is a
2\times 2
matrix.
For a particular band,
\lambda^{\ell}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
, the eigenvector,
\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{\ell}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
, parameterized by the wavenumber,
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
, forms a natural vector bundle over the torus of the Brillouin Zone
[
7
]
.
The curvature
\Omega(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
of the band (defined below) determines a classification within the de Rham cohomology of the 2D torus
[
23
]
. This is called the Chern class and the Chern number gives an integer indexing of the possible classes.

In concrete terms, for a particular eigenfunction the Berry curvature is given by

\Omega(\bm{k})=\Big(\nabla_{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}\times\bm{\mathcal{A}}\Big)\cdot\widehat{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}

(29)

where

\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{*}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}\bm{\alpha}_{0},

(30)

*
represents complex conjugate and
\nabla_{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}=\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}}\partial_{k_{x}}+\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}}\partial_{k_{y}}
.
\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
is the Berry connection and
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
is chosen such that
\lVert\bm{\alpha}_{0}\rVert=1
. The Chern class of the vector bundle is in fact independent of the choice of connection. For any connection on the vector bundle, the associated curvature is determined from the differential of the connection. While the curvatures vary depending on the choice of connection, they all belong to the same Chern class. It is traditional, however, in the field of
Topological Photonics
to express the curvature in terms of the adiabatic theory developed by Berry
[
8
]
.

The Chern number is computed from the curvature as

C=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathcal{B}}\Omega(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})~\mathrm{d}k_{x}\mathrm{d}k_{y}

(31)

where
\mathcal{B}
is the Brillouin zone (reciprocal unit cell). When the Berry connection is a continuously differentiable function of the wavenumber
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
over the entire Brillouin zone, Stokes’ theorem may be directly applied to find a zero Chern number,

C=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}=0,

(32)

since the connection,
\bm{\mathcal{A}}
, has period matching that of
\mathcal{B}
.
This means nonzero Chern numbers are associated with discontinuities in the connection which in turn are associated with discontinuities which appear in the phase of of the eigenfunction
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
.

Global information about the curvature is found from local behavior of the Berry connection by isolating and removing discontinuities of the spectrum from the region of integration,
\mathcal{B}
.
This can be done without effecting the Chern number since the curvature is bounded and vanishing small regions in the domain of integration correspond to vanishing small contributions to the integral.
The Berry curvature is gauge invariant, however, this not true of the connection where a transformation in the eigenfunction
\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\chi(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})}\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
results in a corresponding transform in the connection,
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}+i\nabla_{\bm{k}}\chi(\bm{k})
.
As a consequence, the discontinuities in the connection may be arbitrarily positioned within the Brillouin zone. To remove this ambiguity the eigenfunction can be chosen to have a constant phase in one component.
The location of discontinuities will now be uniquely defined and may be dealt with analytically.

Alternatively, the Chern number may be obtained numerically using the method presented in
[
24
]
. This method similarly relies on a fixed choice of gauge, however it does not require knowledge of where the discontinuities of the Berry phase lie. Thus, for higher order systems where a closed form for the eigenvectors can’t be easily obtained, a numerical calculation for the Chern number will suffice. Nonetheless, we note that in principal the calculation of the Chern number can be carried out analytically as demonstrated in Section
4.2
.

4.1
Honeycomb Lattice

The honeycomb lattice, named for its hexagonal tiling, is well known for its connections to graphene
[
25
]
and for being the simplest lattice model giving rise to Dirac points
[
26
]
.
It can be thought of as two interlaced triangular sublattices. (See Figure
1
).
For the honeycomb lattice, there are two Dirac points in the Brillouin zone.
Thus, there are two possible locations for discontinuities to naturally occur in the eigenvectors. Although this doesn’t effect the Chern number, the expression for the eigenvectors does have consequences for the Berry connection and the local behavior being used to arrive at the Chern number analytically.

In terms of the framework of Section
1.1
, the Honeycomb lattice is defined by
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}=\left(\frac{3}{2},~\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}=\left(\frac{3}{2},-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{1}=(1,0)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{2}=(2,0)
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}=\left(-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2}=\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{3}=\left(1,0\right)
, where the distance between nearest neighbors is taken to be one. In the wavenumber domain of equation (
13
), the system for our honeycomb lattice is

\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}z}+\begin{bmatrix}0&\gamma^{*}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\\
\gamma(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\end{bmatrix}\bm{\alpha}=0

(33)

where

\gamma(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w_{3}}})+\rho\Big(q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w_{1}}})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}+q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w_{2}}})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}\Big)

(34)

and
q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})
is the coefficient function (
15
) defined earlier. The parameter
\rho
represents a stretching/contracting of the lattice in the
y
-direction, in which case the strength of the connection along the vectors
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2}
differs from the strength of the connection along the vector
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{3}
. This amounts to a breaking of the
120^{\circ}
rotational symmetry of the lattice.

Substituting the matrix from (
33
) into the general averaged system (
24
), we have the averaged matrix

\left\langle M\right\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\langle\gamma\rangle^{*}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\\
\langle\gamma\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\end{bmatrix}

where

\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=q_{0}\left(1+\rho(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}+\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}})\right)

(35)

and the constant
q_{0}=\left\langle q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle)
for all
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}
is the same as defined in Section
2
and can be factored out.
The order
\varepsilon
correction term is given by

\left\langle N\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=\begin{bmatrix}\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\\
0&-\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\end{bmatrix}

(36)

where

\displaystyle\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=\rho Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{3})

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}}+\rho^{2}Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2},-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}-\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1})}

(37)

\displaystyle+\rho Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{3},-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}}+c.c.

and “c.c.” stands for the complex conjugate, so
\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
is a real valued function.
Combining equations (
35
) and (
36
) we arrive at the eigenvalue problem of the averaged system from equation (
28
)

\lambda\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle^{*}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\\
\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&-\varepsilon\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\end{bmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0}=0.

(38)

with eigenvalues

\lambda(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=\pm\sqrt{\lvert\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle\rvert^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\lvert\nu\rvert^{2}}

(39)

This averaged equation for the honeycomb lattice is equivalent to the well-known Haldane model
[
3
]
in Fourier space; this is due to the fact that
the first correction in the averaging introduces the next-nearest neighbor terms that are used to break time reversal symmetry in Haldane’s model. In terms of the Pauli matrices, equation (
38
) can be written as

\displaystyle\lambda I\bm{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma_{x}q_{0}\Big(1+\rho\cos(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1})+\rho\cos(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2})\Big)

(40)

\displaystyle+\sigma_{y}q_{0}\Big(1+\rho\sin(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1})+\rho\sin(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\omega}\sigma_{z}s_{0}^{2}\kappa^{2}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\Big(\rho\sin(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1})+\rho^{2}\sin(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}-\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}))+\rho\sin(-\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\omega}\sigma_{z}q_{0}s_{0}\kappa\Big(\rho\cos(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1})-2\rho^{2}\cos(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}-\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}))+\rho\cos(-\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2})\Big)=0.

This corresponds to the Haldane model with zero mass, i.e. inversion symmetry.

Below we give the analytic step for calculating the Chern number within the framework of Section
2
and this paper in general. The eigenvectors of equation (
38
) are

\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\frac{1}{D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})}\begin{bmatrix}\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle^{*}\\
-\left(\lambda+\varepsilon\nu\right)\end{bmatrix}

(41)

where
D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
is a normalizing term such that
\bm{\alpha}_{0}^{*}\cdot\bm{\alpha}_{0}=1
; it is given by

\displaystyle D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\left(\lambda+\varepsilon\nu\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\lambda^{2}+2\varepsilon\lambda\nu+\varepsilon^{2}\nu

\displaystyle=2\lambda\left(\lambda+\varepsilon\nu\right)

(42)

Note: As a result of the longitudinal driving (
\varepsilon\not=0
), the honeycomb spectrum in equation (
38
) is no longer conical at the Dirac point. Furthermore, at the Dirac point, where
\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})=0
, there is a gap width of
2\lambda(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})=2\varepsilon\nu
.

At any point where
\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})=0
, and therefore
\lambda=\pm\varepsilon\left|\nu\right|
, the vanishing of the normalizing function leads to a discontinuity in the eigenvector.
So, at a critical point
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}
,

D(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})^{2}=2(\varepsilon\nu)^{2}\left(1+\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda\nu)\right)

(43)

and thus
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
has an integrable singularity if
\mathrm{sgn}(\nu)=-\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda)
.

As an example, we take
\rho=1
. The critical points now occur at
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}=\left(0,\,\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}=\left(0,-\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)
. We look for the local behavior of the eigenvectors near one of these points, as well as the Berry connection and curvature which are derived from them. For illustrative proposes, we take the expansion near

\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}+\frac{1}{q_{0}}\delta\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}

(44)

where
\left\lVert\delta\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\right\rVert\ll 1
and the expansion around
\bm{K}_{D}^{\prime}
follows similarly.
Approximations for the key terms are

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\approx-\frac{3}{2}(\mathrm{i}\delta k_{x}+\delta k_{y})

(45a)

\displaystyle\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\approx\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})\equiv\nu_{0}

(45b)

\displaystyle\lambda(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\approx\pm\sqrt{\frac{9}{4}(\delta k_{x}^{2}+\delta k_{y}^{2})+\varepsilon^{2}\nu_{0}^{2}}

(45c)

where
\nu_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Substituting these approximations into
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
gives

\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\approx-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\lambda}}\begin{bmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\frac{3}{2}(\delta k_{y}-\mathrm{i}\delta k_{x})}{\sqrt{\lambda+\varepsilon\nu_{0}}}\\
\sqrt{\lambda+\varepsilon\nu_{0}}\end{bmatrix}.

(46)

The local approximation of the Berry connection is

\bm{\mathcal{A}}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\approx\displaystyle\frac{9\mathrm{i}}{8\lambda(\lambda+\varepsilon\nu_{0})}(-\delta k_{y}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}}+\delta k_{x}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}}).

(47)

This shows that the discontinuity from the eigenvector carries through to the connection in the form of a singularity that behaves like
\frac{1}{\lVert\delta\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\rVert}
when
\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda\nu_{0})=-1
. By a similar set of calculation, we find that a potential singularity exists at
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}
as well.
The approximation of the Berry curvature is

\Omega(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\approx-\varepsilon\displaystyle\frac{9\nu_{0}\mathrm{i}}{8\lambda^{3}}{\bm{\hat{\textnormal{k}}}}.

(48)

Note that
|\lambda|>0
and so the curvature is bounded. Due to the radial symmetry of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
around the critical points, it’s convenient to define the disk regions

\mathcal{D}(\bm{\delta})=\left\{\bm{k}\middle|\left\lVert\bm{K}_{D}-\bm{k}\right\rVert\leq\delta\right\},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\bm{\delta})=\left\{\bm{k}\middle|\left\lVert\bm{K}^{\prime}_{D}-\bm{k}\right\rVert\leq\delta\right\}

(49)

with the punctured Brillouin zone

\iint_{\mathcal{B}}=\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\iint_{\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{D}(\bm{\delta})-\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\bm{\delta})}.

(50)

In this way, we may carve out a small region around any potential singularities without effecting the the calculation of the curvature or the Chern number. Once all singularities have been excised from the Brillouin zone, by removing infinitesimal regions around
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}
, we can apply Stokes theorem to represent global information about the curvature in terms of the local behavior of connection near the critical points

\iint_{\mathcal{B}}\Omega(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\cdot{\bm{\hat{\textnormal{k}}}}\,\mathrm{d}k_{x}\mathrm{d}k_{y}=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}-C_{\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}}-C_{\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}^{\prime}}

(51)

where

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}(\delta)}\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}

(52a)

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\delta)}\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\,\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}.

(52b)

An illustration of the contours taken can be found in figure
3
. Here we take
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
on the upper band. At
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}
the first component of the eigenvector approaches a magnitude of 1 and exhibits a
2\pi
change in phase around this point. This is the discontinuity that must be integrated around. By contrast, the first component of the eigenvector is zero at
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}
and same
2\pi
phase change no longer corresponds to a discontinuity. On the lower band this is reversed, which is why we have chosen to remove both points from the domain. From the periodicity of the eigenvectors in
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
, the boundary integral around
\partial\mathcal{B}
is zero.

Define
\nu_{0}=\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})=-\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D})
. The Chern number is found to be

\displaystyle C=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\left(-C_{\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}}-C_{\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{\bm{\delta}\rightarrow 0}\Bigg[-\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda)\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\nu_{0}}{\sqrt{9\delta^{2}+4(\varepsilon\nu_{0})^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda)\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\nu_{0}}{\sqrt{9\delta^{2}+4(\varepsilon\nu_{0})^{2}}}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda\nu_{0}).

For
\rho=1
, the Chern number is
-1
for the upper band,
\lambda>0
, and
+1
for the lower band,
\lambda<0
. This is consistent with results from
[
16
,
27
]
.
This topology persists for
\rho>1/2
. When
\rho=1/2
the two critical points merge at the corner of the Brillouin zone, i.e.,
\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}=\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}^{\prime}_{D}=\left(0,\pm\frac{4\pi}{\sqrt{3}}\right)
, as seen in Figure (
2(b)
). At this point
\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})=0
and so the gap in the spectrum caused by the driving of the lattice closes and a Dirac point forms. For
\rho<1/2
, a gap opens that is caused by the stretching of the lattice instead of the driving of the lattice. In the analysis, this corresponds to
|\gamma|>0
for all
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
in the Brillouin zone, so that no discontinuities exist in the phase of the eigenvectors and the Chern Number on both bands is zero.

4.2
Lieb Lattice

Here we consider the Lieb lattice
[
28
,
29
]
as an example. The Lieb lattice is a 1/4-depleted square lattice, i.e., one in four lattice sites have been removed, consisting of three sublattice sites in each unit cell. The Lieb system features a single spectral degeneracy within the Brillouin zone where upper and lower bands intersect with a middle flat-band at a Dirac point (see Figure
4
(b)). While the three distinct sub-lattices result in a 3rd order system in
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
-space, the lone Dirac point streamlines the calculations of the Chern number. As we will show, the Dirac point lines up with a singularity in the Berry connection that occurs on both the upper and lower bands.

In terms of the framework laid out in Section
1.1
, the Lieb lattice is defined by
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}=(2,0)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}=(0,2)
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{1}=(0,1)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{2}=(1,1)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{d}}_{3}=(1,0)
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1}=(1,0)
,
\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2}=(0,1)
. The system of differential equations, in the form (
13
), derived for this lattice is

\mathrm{i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\bm{\alpha}}{\mathrm{d}z}+\begin{bmatrix}0&\gamma_{1}^{*}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\\
\gamma_{1}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0&\gamma_{2}^{*}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\\
0&\gamma_{2}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\end{bmatrix}\bm{\alpha}=0

(53)

where the
\gamma
functions are given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2k_{x}}+q(z,-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1})

(54a)

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=q(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2k_{y}}+q(z,-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})

(54b)

and
q
as defined in equation (
15
).

Looking at Figure
4
, we see that there is no interaction between the first and third sublattice, which correspond to lattice sites positioned between the lattice sites of the square lattice created by the second sublattice. This lack of interaction is reflected in the zeros found in the upper right and lower left corners of the matrix from equation (
53
). A detailed derivation of the specific
\gamma
functions given here as well as a more generalized version of this system appears in
[
16
]
.

Substituting the matrix from the Lieb system (
53
) into the general averaged equation (
24
) gives the averaged matrix

\left\langle M\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle^{*}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\\
\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0&\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle^{*}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\\
0&\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0\end{bmatrix}.

(55)

For the averaged
\gamma_{i}
functions, we again use the fact that
\left\langle q(\zeta,\mathrm{\mathbf{w}})\right\rangle=q_{0}
. This gives

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=q_{0}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2k_{x}}+1\right)

(56a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle=q_{0}\left(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2k_{y}}+1\right)

(56b)

At order
\varepsilon
, the correction term is

\left\langle N\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\,\nu^{*}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\\
0&0&0\\
\,\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\,\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})&0&0\end{bmatrix}

(57)

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})=

\displaystyle~Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})+Q(-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot(\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}-\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1})}

(58)

\displaystyle+Q(\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{2}}+Q(-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{1},-\mathrm{\mathbf{w}}_{2})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\mathrm{\mathbf{v}}_{1}}

and
Q
is defined by equation (
27
). The nonzero terms in (
57
) correspond to next-nearest neighbor interactions between the first and third lattice sites.
The eigenvalue problem for approximating the spectrum of the Lieb lattice is now found to be

\lambda\bm{\alpha}_{0}+\begin{bmatrix}0&\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle^{*}&-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nu^{*}\\
\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle&0&\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle^{*}\\
\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nu&\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle&0\end{bmatrix}\bm{\alpha}_{0}=0.

(59)

This systems has the eigenvalues

\lambda=0,\pm\sqrt{\left|\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\left|\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left|\nu\right|^{2}}.

(60)

Note: The constant term in the characteristic polynomial,
\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\nu^{*}-\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle^{*}\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle^{*}\nu
, is
zero and we find that
\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\nu^{*}
is strictly real.
The eigenvectors can be expressed in terms of
\lambda(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
as

\bm{\alpha}_{0}=\displaystyle\frac{1}{D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle^{*}+\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nu^{*}\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\\
-\lambda^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left|\nu\right|^{2}\\
\lambda\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle-\mathrm{i}\varepsilon\nu\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle^{*}\end{bmatrix}

(61)

where
D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
is a normalizing function

\displaystyle D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})^{2}=

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}(2\left|\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+2\left|\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\right|^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\left|\nu\right|^{2})

\displaystyle+\varepsilon^{2}\left|\nu\right|^{2}(\left|\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\left|\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\left|\nu\right|^{2})

(62)

which makes
\lVert\bm{\alpha}_{0}\rVert=1
.
In this case, we have chosen to fix the second component of
\bm{\alpha}_{0}
as strictly real.
When not on the flat band, i.e.,
\lambda=0
,
D
further reduces to
D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})^{2}=2\lambda^{2}(\left|\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\left|\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle\right|^{2})
.
Notice that
D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})
vanishes when
\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle=\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle=0
, which corresponds to the Dirac point of the unperturbed spectrum, i.e.,
\varepsilon=0
.
This has the effect of creating a discontinuity from the twist in the phase around the zeros of the first and third components of the normalized eigenvector (
61
) (see Figure
5
).
In terms of the magnitude, the zeros of
D
are removable discontinuities and the important discontinuity appears only in the phase of the eigenvector.
As such, there is a
2\pi
change in the eigenfunction or so called Berry phase for a loop around the discontinuity.

The discontinuity in the spectrum occurs at
\bm{K}_{D}=\left(\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
, which means our calculation of the Chern number will be determined by the local behavior near this point. For
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}+\delta\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}/q_{0}
we have the approximations

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma_{1}\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\approx-2\mathrm{i}\,\delta k_{x}

(63a)

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma_{2}\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\approx-2\mathrm{i}\,\delta k_{y}

(63b)

\displaystyle\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\approx\nu(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})\equiv\nu_{0}

(63c)

where
\nu_{0}
is a real nonzero constant. In this region the set of eigenvalues are approximated by
\lambda(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\approx\left\{0,\pm\sqrt{4\delta k_{x}^{2}+4\delta k_{y}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\nu_{0}^{2}}\right\}
. In terms of their graphs, these spectral surfaces correspond to a flat plane and a hyperboloid of two sheets. On the upper and lower bands, the related eigenvectors are given by

\bm{\alpha}_{0}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\approx\displaystyle\frac{2}{D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})}\begin{bmatrix}-\mathrm{i}\delta k_{x}\lambda+\delta k_{y}\varepsilon\nu_{0}\\
-2\left(\delta k_{x}^{2}+\delta k_{y}^{2}\right)\\
~~\mathrm{i}\delta k_{y}\lambda+\delta k_{x}\varepsilon\nu_{0}\end{bmatrix}

(64)

where
D(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})^{2}\approx 8\left(\delta k_{x}^{2}+\delta k_{y}^{2}\right)\lambda^{2}
.

From here, the approximation of the Berry connection is found to be

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}}+\mathcal{A}_{2}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}}\approx\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\nu_{0}\mathrm{i}}{\lambda\left(\delta k_{x}^{2}+\delta k_{y}^{2}\right)}\left(\delta k_{y}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{i}}}-\delta k_{x}\hat{\mathrm{\mathbf{j}}}\right)

(65)

and the discontinuity in the eigenvectors is seen to produce a singularity in the Berry connection.
Note: On the flat band where
\lambda=0
the connection is zero.
The approximation for the Berry curvature is then

\Omega=(\partial_{k_{x}}\mathcal{A}_{2}-\partial_{k_{y}}\mathcal{A}_{1}){\bm{\hat{\textnormal{k}}}}\approx\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{i}4\varepsilon\nu_{0}}{\lambda^{3}}{\bm{\hat{\textnormal{k}}}}.

(66)

Unlike the eigenvectors and the Berry connection, the Berry curvature for the upper and lower bands are both smooth and bounded since
|\lambda|>0
.
As a result, analogous to what was done in the honeycomb lattice calculation, we can remove an infinitesimal region around
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}
from the Brillouin zone without effecting the total curvature or Chern number.
Once the singular point has been removed in this manner, Stokes’ theorem can be employed to calculate the Chern number using the local behavior of the connection instead of the global behavior of the curvature. The radial symmetry of the differential
\bm{\mathcal{A}}\cdot\mathrm{d}\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
implies that removing a circular disk
\mathcal{D}(\delta)=\left\{\bm{k}\middle|\lVert\bm{k}-\bm{K}_{D}\rVert\leq\delta\right\}
will facilitate the direct calculation of all relevant integrals.

The particular steps of this reasoning are outlined below

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{B}}\Omega(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\,\,\mathrm{d}k_{x}\mathrm{d}k_{y}

(67a)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{D}(\delta)}\Big(\nabla_{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}\times\bm{\mathcal{A}}\Big)\cdot{\bm{\hat{\textnormal{k}}}}\,\mathrm{d}k_{x}\mathrm{d}k_{y}

(67b)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\left(\oint_{\partial\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\cdot\mathrm{d}\bm{k}-\displaystyle\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}(\delta)}\mathcal{A}(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\cdot\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\right)

(67c)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\left(-\displaystyle\oint_{\partial\mathcal{D}(\delta)}\mathcal{A}(\bm{k})\cdot\mathrm{d}\bm{k}\right)

(67d)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\left(-\mathrm{sgn}(\lambda)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon\nu_{0}}{\sqrt{4\delta^{2}+(\varepsilon\nu_{0})^{2}}}\mathrm{d}\,\theta\right)

5
Nonlinear Envelope Equations

From the linear analysis, we have found that even though the Chern Number is a global property of the spectrum, it can be successfully calculated from the local behavior near critical points in the spectrum under suitable restrictions on the eigenvectors. We now consider the nonlinear dynamics of wave packets in the gap near the critical points of the linear spectrum. For this, we assume weak nonlinearity.

\mathrm{i}\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}}{\partial z}+M\left(z,\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\right)\bm{\alpha}+\varepsilon\widetilde{\sigma}\mathbb{N}(\bm{\alpha})\bm{\alpha}=0.

(76)

where the nonlinear term once again is defined as
\mathbb{N}(\bm{\alpha})=\mathrm{diag}(|\alpha^{1}|^{2},\ldots,|\alpha^{\ell}|^{2})
.

Following the steps from Section
3
the averaged nonlinear system when the nonlinear term first appears is found to be

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial\bm{\alpha}}{\partial z}+\left(\left<M\right>(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})+\varepsilon\left\langle N\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{k}})\right)\bm{\alpha}+\varepsilon\widetilde{\sigma}\mathbb{N}(\bm{\alpha})\bm{\alpha}=0.

(77)

Note that we are assuming a balance between the nonlinearity and the higher order term due to the averaging.

Near a critical point of the linear spectrum,
\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}=\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}+\varepsilon\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}
, we can expand the linear terms as

\left\langle M\right\rangle+\varepsilon\left\langle N\right\rangle\approx\left\langle M\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})+\varepsilon\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}}\left\langle M\right\rangle+\varepsilon\left\langle N\right\rangle(\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D})+\ldots

(78)

where the gradient of the matrix
\left\langle M\right\rangle
is applied element-wise.

To find an equation for the slowly-varying envelope of a wave packet, we expand the solution in form

\bm{\alpha}=\left(\bm{\alpha}_{0}\left(Z,\varepsilon\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\right)+\varepsilon\bm{\alpha}_{1}\left(Z,\varepsilon\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{\alpha}_{2}\left(Z,\varepsilon\mathrm{\mathbf{k}}\right)+\ldots\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\lambda_{0}z}.

Equation (

\varepsilon=0

\mathrm{\mathbf{K}}_{D}

We assume linearly independent solutions (two in the case of Dirac , three in the case of the Lieb and kagome lattices).

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}\bm{\alpha}_{1}+\left\langle M\right\rangle

6
Conclusions

Tight-binding approximations are constructed for a longitudinally driven two-dimensional lattices with uniform lattice sites.
The general lattice is doubly periodic in the transverse directions and consists of an arbitrary number of sublattice sites within each unit cell.
An orbital expansion results in a system of ODE’s with order equal to the number of sublattice sites. For fast oscillations in the longitudinal direction, an averaged equation is derived with constant coefficients in terms of
z
and parameterized by the transverse wavenumber,
\bm{k}
.

These averaged models have the effect of introducing next-nearest neighbor interactions into the system. For the honeycomb lattice the averaging process leads to the well known Haldane Model in Fourier space.
Analytic calculation of the Berry connection and curvature means that global information about the topology of the spectral bands can be obtained. Only a local approximation of the eigenvectors is required for the connection and curvature since the Chern number is determined by the local behavior near discontinuities. Detailed analysis is also carried out for the Lieb lattice.

Nonlinear wave equations are derived that govern the envelopes of the underlying eigenvectors. In the case of a honeycomb lattice the resulting nonlinear Dirac system contains an extra ”mass” term, in comparison to previous models
[
18
,
19
]
. Numerical computation shows spiral diffraction patterns for relatively small nonlinear effects. As the effect of the nonlinearity is increased the wave envelopes tend towards a soliton type structure and at larger nonlinear coefficent the envelopes undergo collapse. For Dirac points of the spectrum with a flatband all crossing the point, as appears in the Lieb and
1/5
-depleted lattices, a novel nonlinear system is derived for the envelope dynamics. In this system simulations show energy oscillates between the eigenfunctions.
