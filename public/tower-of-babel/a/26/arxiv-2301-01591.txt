Extremal polynomials on the n n -grid
Arno B.J. Kuijlaars

Abstract
The n n -grid E n E_{n} consists of n n equally spaced points in [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] including the endpoints ± 1 \pm 1 . The extremal polynomial p n ∗ p_{n}^{*} is the polynomial that maximizes the uniform norm ‖ p ‖ [ − 1 , 1 ] \|p\|_{[-1,1]} among polynomials p p of degree ≤ α ​ n \leq\alpha n that are bounded by one in absolute value on E n E_{n} . For every α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) , we determine the limit of 1 n ​ log ⁡ ‖ p n ∗ ‖ [ − 1 , 1 ] \frac{1}{n}\log\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]} as n → ∞ n\to\infty . The interest in this limit comes from a connection with an impossibility theorem on stable approximation on the n n -grid.



1
Statement of result

The
n
-grid from the title refers to
n
equally spaced points in
[-1,1]

E_{n}=\{\xi_{k,n}=\tfrac{2k-n-1}{n-1}\mid k=1,\ldots,n\},

(1.1)

ranging from
-1
to
1
. Let
0<\alpha<1
.
This paper is about the determination of the limit of the expression

\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{\deg p\leq\alpha n}\frac{\|p\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p\|_{E_{n}}}

(1.2)

as
n\to\infty
, where the norms are uniform norms over the indicated
sets, and the supremum is over univariate polynomials
p

of degrees at most
\alpha n
that do not vanish identically.
The result was already announced in the paper
[
13
]
from 2011.
Renewed interest in it is due to
[
10
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\alpha\in(0,1)
the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\sup_{\deg p\leq\alpha n}\frac{\|p\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p\|_{E_{n}}}=C(\alpha)

(1.3)

exists and is equal to

C(\alpha)=\frac{(1+\alpha)\log(1+\alpha)+(1-\alpha)\log(1-\alpha)}{2}.

(1.4)

The limit
C(\alpha)
is positive and strictly increasing with
\alpha
.
There is a nice Taylor expansion

C(\alpha)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{2k}}{2k(2k-1)}.

The odd Taylor coefficients vanish since the power series defines an odd function.
The even Taylor coefficients are positive and therefore
C(\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}
.
It is also worth noticing that
C(\alpha)\to\log 2
as
\alpha\to 1-
.
For
\alpha=1
however, we have that the limit in (
1.3
)
is
+\infty
, since for each
n
, there is a non-zero polynomial of degree
n

that vanishes on the
n
-grid.

Discussion

The limit (
1.3
) shows that a polynomial of degree
\leq\alpha n

that is bounded on
E_{n}
can be exponentially large somewhere in the interval
[-1,1]
.
Namely, if
|p(\xi_{k,n})|\leq 1
for each
k=1,\ldots,n
, then
|p(x)|

at some
x\in[-1,1]
can be as large as
e^{n(C(\alpha)+o(1))}
as

n\to\infty
,
and the constant
C(\alpha)
is sharp.
The result is related to earlier work of
Coppersmith and Rivlin
[
6
]
who showed that there
exist universal constants
C_{2}>C_{1}>1
such that
for
n
large enough, and for every
d\leq n-1
,

C_{1}^{d^{2}/n}\leq\sup_{\deg p\leq d}\frac{\|p\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p\|_{E_{n}}}\leq C_{2}^{d^{2}/n}.

(1.5)

The inequalities (
1.5
) show that polynomials of degree
d\leq c\sqrt{n}
that are bounded by one on
E_{n}
are uniformly bounded on
[-1,1]

with a constant that only depends on
c
.
However, if
d
grows proportionally with
n
then (
1.5
)
shows that polynomials
that are bounded by one on
E_{n}
may be exponentially large on
[-1,1]
,
and this behavior is made more precise in
the limit (
1.3
).

The comparisons of the two uniform norms
\|\cdot\|_{E_{n}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{[-1,1]}
arises naturally when studying approximation or
interpolation methods for analytic functions based on function values
on the
n
-grid. There is a trade-off between convergence
and stability properties that was made precise in the
impossibility theorem of
[
13
, Theorem 3.1]
. For example, exponential
convergence as
n\to\infty
comes together with exponential instability.
The proof of the impossibility theorem in
[
13
]
relies on
the Coppersmith-Rivlin inequalities (
1.5
).
For recent work in this direction we refer to
[
1
,
10
]
.

It was observed in
[
13
, section 4]
that the phenomenon that
polynomials of degree
d\approx\alpha n

can be much larger on
[-1,1]
than on
E_{n}
may be understood
in terms of potential theory. Here one thinks of a polynomial in terms
of its zeros. A polynomial may be small at certain gridpoints in
E_{n}

by simply having a zero very close to these gridpoints.
Since there are more gridpoints than zeros this cannot
happen for every gridpoint. The extremal polynomial

p_{n}^{*}
for (
1.2
) will place a certain fraction of its zeros
extremely close to gridpoints lying in a subset
S
of
[-1,1]
(with
S
depending on
\alpha
).
Then
p_{n}^{*}
is small at the gridpoints in
S
but not necessarily in between,
and in fact it has high oscillations in
S
. Following
[
3
,
8
]
we call

S
the saturated region.
The non-saturated region
[-1,1]\setminus S
has
fewer zeros than gridpoints. The extremal polynomial
p_{n}^{*}
is
not only small at the gridpoints in
[-1,1]\setminus S
but
it is of comparable size over the full non-saturated region,
see
[
15
]
for very precise estimates.

This phenomenon was first described by Rakhmanov
[
14
]
for orthogonal
polynomials on the
n
-grid, or more generally, for polynomials that
minimize a discrete
L_{p}
norm on
E_{n}
. These polynomials
have their zeros in
[-1,1]
and they are separated by the gridpoints,
in the sense that in between any two distinct zeros there is at least
one gridpoint. In the limit
n\to\infty
the zeros of the extremal polynomials
of degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
considered in
[
14
]

have a limiting distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
(depending only on
\alpha
)
that is characterized by a constrained equilibrium problem from potential
theory. The measure
\mu_{\alpha}
has a density with respect to
Lebesgue measure on
[-1,1]
with
\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}\leq\frac{1}{2}

where
\frac{1}{2}
is the limiting density of the gridpoints as
n\to\infty
.
The saturated region
S
is where the
equality
\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}=\frac{1}{2}
. holds.
We give details in section
2.2
below.
The extremal polynomials
p_{n}^{*}
for (
1.2
)
have the same limiting zero distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
as
n\to\infty
,
as we will show in this paper.
Also in other aspects they behave similarly to the orthogonal polynomials
on the
n
-grid, and this will be the clue to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Outline of the proof

The extremal polynomial for (
1.2
) is a polynomial
p_{n}^{*}

of degree
\leq\alpha n
such that

\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=\sup_{\deg p\leq\alpha n}\frac{\|p\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p\|_{E_{n}}}.

(1.6)

The proof of (
1.3
) then naturally comes in two steps.
In the first step we prove the lower bound

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}\geq C(\alpha)

(1.7)

and in the second step the corresponding upper bound

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}\leq C(\alpha).

(1.8)

The lower bound comes from considering the
L_{\infty}
-extremal
polynomials
P_{n}^{*}
on
E_{n}
, where
P_{n}^{*}
is the monic polynomial
of degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
that minimizes the uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{E_{n}}
.
Using the results from
[
8
,
14
]
and some additional calculations
we prove in section
2
that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=C(\alpha),

(1.9)

and this implies the lower bound (
1.7
).

The upper bound (
1.8
) is proved in section
3
.
It comes from a study of the zeros of the extremal
polynomials
p_{n}^{*}
satisfying (
1.6
). We show in Lemma
3.1

that the zeros are real and simple and at least
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1

zeros are in
[-1,1]
where they are separated by the gridpoints. Note that
one zero could be in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1]
.
We then use potential theoretic arguments to show that the limiting
distribution of the zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
is equal to the constrained
equilibrium measure
\mu_{\alpha}
. Along the way we prove that

\mu_{\alpha}
is the maximizer of a functional
J
that we define in
(
3.6
) with
J(\mu_{\alpha})=C(\alpha)
, which leads to
the upper bound (
1.8
).

We finally note that discrete orthogonal polynomials and the constrained
equilibrium problem also play a role in the analysis of iterative methods
from numerical linear algebra
[
4
,
11
,
12
]
, and the asymptotic
analysis of integrable systems
[
7
]
, random matrices and
random tiling models
[
3
,
5
]
.

2
Proof of the lower bound

2.1
Extremal polynomials on the
n
-grid

As explained above, we are going to consider the monic polynomial

P_{n}^{*}
of degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
such that

\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}=\min_{\scriptsize\begin{array}[]{c}\deg P=\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor\\
P\text{ is monic}\end{array}}\|P\|_{E_{n}}.

(2.1)

We are going to show that the limit
(
1.9
) holds.

Rakhmanov
[
14
]
considered polynomials
P
of degree
n
that are monic (leading coefficient
equal to
1
) and that minimize either the uniform norm
\|P\|_{E_{N}}
,
or a discrete
p
-norm on
E_{N}
among all such polynomials. The interest is in their asymptotic
behavior as both
n,N\to\infty
with
n/N\to c<1
. The equispaced
n
-grid (
1.1
) is
actually only a special case of far more general discrete sets that are considered in
[
14
]
.
Compared to
[
14
]
we change
N\mapsto n
,
n\mapsto\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
,
c\mapsto\alpha
.

2.2
Limiting behavior of zeros

The zeros of
P_{n}^{*}
are real and simple. They belong to the interval
(-1,1)
,
where they are separated by the nodes in
E_{n}
, see
[
8
,
14
]
.
To
P_{n}^{*}
we associate
the normalized zero counting measure

\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}\delta_{x_{k,n}},

where
x_{k,n}
for
k=1,\ldots,\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
,
denote the zeros of
P_{n}^{*}
. Note that we normalize with the factor
1/n

while the degree of
P_{n}^{*}
is
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
. Thus

\nu_{n}
is not a probability measure but rather has
a total mass
\frac{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}{n}

Rakhmanov
[
14
, Theorem 2]
, see also
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
,
proved that the weak
∗
limit

\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\to}}\mu_{\alpha}

(2.2)

exists, where
\mu_{\alpha}
is the measure on
[-1,1]
with
density
[
14
, Theorem 1]

\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{2},&\text{ for }x\in[-1,-r]\cup[r,1],\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{r^{2}-x^{2}}}\right),&\text{ for }x\in[-r,r],\end{cases}

(2.3)

where

r=r(\alpha)=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}.

(2.4)

The density on
[-r,r]

can alternatively be written as

\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arccos\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\right),\quad\text{ for }x\in[-r,r].

(2.5)

The measure
\mu_{\alpha}
belongs to the class

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}:=\{\mu\mid\smallint d\mu=\alpha,\,0\leq\mu\leq\sigma\}

(2.6)

where

d\sigma=\frac{1}{2}\chi_{[-1,1]}(x)dx

(2.7)

denotes the
Lebesgue measure restricted to
[-1,1]
with normalization such that

\int d\sigma=1
. The upper constraint

\mu_{\alpha}\leq\sigma
comes from the fact that the zeros of
P_{n}^{*}
are separated by the nodes
\xi_{k,n}
in the equispaced grid
E_{n}
.

Rakhmanov also characterized
\mu_{\alpha}
in terms of notions from
logarithmic potential theory
[
16
]
. Let

I(\mu)=\iint\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}d\mu(x)d\mu(y)

(2.8)

and

U^{\mu}(x)=\int\log\frac{1}{|x-y|}d\mu(y)

(2.9)

denote the logarithmic energy and the logarithmic potential of a
measure
\mu
, respectively. Then

I(\mu_{\alpha})=\min_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}}I(\mu)

(2.10)

and
\mu_{\alpha}
is the unique minimizer within the class (
2.6
).
Furthermore, there is a constant
\ell_{\alpha}
such that

U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)\begin{cases}=\ell_{\alpha},&\text{ for }x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu_{\alpha}),\\
\leq\ell_{\alpha},&\text{ for }x\in[-1,1],\end{cases}

(2.11)

and
\mu_{\alpha}
is the only measure
\mu
in

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
such that
U^{\mu}(x)=\ell
is constant
on
\supp(\sigma-\mu)
and
U^{\mu}(x)\leq\ell
on
[-1,1]

for a certain constant
\ell
.

Because of the upper constraint
\mu\leq\sigma
, the
measure
\mu_{\alpha}
is called a constrained equilibrium
measure, see
[
3
,
8
]
. The saturated region
S
is
where
\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}=\frac{d\sigma}{dx}=\frac{1}{2}

and in view of (
2.3
) we have
S=[-1,-r]\cup[r,1]
.
The constraint is not active in the region where
\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}}{dx}<\frac{1}{2}
.
This is the non-saturated region and its closure is
\supp(\sigma-\mu_{\alpha})=[-r,r]
.

2.3
Two lemmas

The connection between potential theory and the asymptotics theory of
polynomials is well-known. If
P
is a monic polynomial and

\nu=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x:P(x)=0}\delta_{x}

is its normalized zero counting measure (each zero is included in the sum according to
its multiplicity), then

\frac{1}{n}\log|P(x)|=-U^{\nu}(x).

If
(P_{n})_{n}
is a sequence of monic polynomials, and
(\nu_{n})_{n}

is the corresponding sequence of normalized zero counting measures then
the convergence of
(\nu_{n})_{n}
contains information on the
n
th root asymptotic behavior of the polynomials.
We need two such results.

Lemma 2.1
.

(a)

Let
(P_{n})_{n}
be a sequence of monic polynomials,

\deg P_{n}\leq\alpha n
, having real and simple zeros, such that
the zeros of
P_{n}
are separated by the points of
E_{n}
for every
n
.
Suppose that the sequence of normalized zero counting measures
(\nu_{n})_{n}

where
\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x:P_{n}(x)=0}\delta_{x}
,
has a weak
∗
limit
\mu
as
n\to\infty
. Then
\mu\leq\sigma
, and

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}\|_{E_{n}}\geq-\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu}(x).

(2.12)

(b)

If
(P_{n}^{*})_{n}
is the sequence of extremal polynomials
satisfying (
2.1
) then
\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\to}}\mu_{\alpha}
as

n\to\infty
, and equality holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}=-\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x).

(2.13)

Proof.

Part (a) is Lemma 4.2 of
[
14
]
, where it is stated
under the assumption that the zeros are in
[-1,1]
.
See Lemma 5.5 in
[
8
]
for the statement without
this extra assumption. Part (b) is in
[
14
, Theorem 2]
or
[
8
, Theorem 3.3]
.
∎

Part (b) of Lemma
2.1
will be used in the proof of the lower bound,
while part (a) will be used in the proof of the upper bound, see the proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the logarithmic potential
U^{\mu}
of a positive measure
\mu

is a lower semi-continuous function
[
16
]

and therefore its minimum over a compact (as in (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), as well as in (
2.14
) below) exists.

In the situation of Lemma
2.1
, however, the logarithmic potential
U^{\mu}

is actually continuous.
This follows from
\mu\leq\sigma
and the fact that
U^{\sigma}

is continuous. Indeed,
U^{\sigma-\mu}
is lower semi-continuous,
and therefore
U^{\mu}=U^{\sigma}-U^{\sigma-\mu}
is upper semi-continuous
as well, hence continuous.

The second lemma is probably well-known, but I was not able to find an
appropriate reference for it.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
(P_{n})_{n}
is a sequence of monic polynomials,
\deg P_{n}\leq\alpha n
,
such that the zeros of all
P_{n}
are in a compact set.
Suppose that the sequence of normalized zero counting measures
(\nu_{n})_{n}

has a weak
∗
limit
\mu
as
n\to\infty
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}=-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x).

(2.14)

Proof.

By the principle of descent
[
16
]
we have

U^{\mu}(x^{*})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}U^{\nu_{n}}(x_{n})

whenever
x_{n}\to x^{*}
.
Since
|P_{n}(x)|=e^{-nU^{\nu_{n}}(x)}
, this means that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log|P_{n}(x_{n})|\leq-U^{\mu}(x^{*})\leq-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x),

whenever
(x_{n})_{n}
is a convergent sequence with a limit
x^{*}\in[-1,1]
.
Taking
x_{n}\in[-1,1]
with
|P_{n}(x_{n})|=\|P_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}

and passing to convergent subsequences if necessary, we then find

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x).

(2.15)

It remains to show that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}\geq-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x).

(2.16)

Suppose that (
2.16
) does not hold. Then there exist
\varepsilon>0

and
x_{0}\in[-1,1]
such that

\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq-U^{\mu}(x_{0})-\varepsilon

for an infinite number of
n
, say for
n\in\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{N}
.
This means that

U^{\nu_{n}}(x)\geq U^{\mu}(x_{0})+\varepsilon,\qquad x\in[-1,1],\ n\in\mathcal{N}.

(2.17)

By the lower envelope theorem
[
16
]
and the assumption that
\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\to}}\mu
as
n\to\infty
, we have

U^{\mu}(x)=\liminf_{n\to\infty\atop n\in\mathcal{N}_{1}}U^{\nu_{n}}(x)\quad\text{q.e.}

(2.18)

where q.e. means quasi everywhere, i.e., the exceptional set is
a polar set (a small set for potential theory).
In (
2.17
) we then take
n\to\infty
along the subsequence
\mathcal{N}

and we find using (
2.18
)

U^{\mu}(x)\geq U^{\mu}(x_{0})+\varepsilon\quad\text{for q.e. }x\in[-1,1].

This means that

\{x\in[-1,1]\mid U^{\mu}(x)<U^{\mu}(x_{0})+\varepsilon\}

(2.19)

is a polar set, which is not the case, as we now show.

If
U^{\mu}
is a continuous function then (
2.19
)
contains an open non-empty interval and thus it is not a polar set, see e.g.
[
9
, Example 5.2.7]
. If
U^{\mu}
is not continuous, then we come to the same
conclusion, if we use certain more advanced
results from potential theory, in particular around thinness and the fine
topology, which we will not explain here, but see
[
2
,
9
,
16
]
and other works
on potential theory. The set

\{x\in\mathbb{C}\mid U^{\mu}(x)<U^{\mu}(x_{0})+\varepsilon\}

is an open neighborhood of
x_{0}
in the fine topology,
and the interval
[-1,1]
is not thin at
x_{0}\in[-1,1]
, see
[
9
, Corollary 6.7.8]
.
Therefore (
2.19
) is not a polar set.

We thus arrive at a contradiction and we conclude that (
2.16
) does hold.
Together with (
2.15
) we get (
2.1
) and the lemma follows.
∎

2.4
Conclusion of the proof of the lower bound

We apply the two lemmas to the
extremal polynomials
P_{n}^{*}
satisfying (
2.1
).
By Lemma
2.1
(b) we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}=-\min_{x\in[-r,r]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x),

(2.20)

since
\supp(\sigma-\mu_{\alpha})=[-r,r]
by (
2.3
).
From Lemma
2.3
and (
2.2
) we get

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}=-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x).

(2.21)

Combining (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) we obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|P_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=\min_{x\in[-r,r]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x).

(2.22)

The limit (
1.9
) and thereby the lower bound (
1.7
)
follows from (
2.22
) and the following proposition.

Proposition 2.4
.

We have

C(\alpha)=\min_{x\in[-r,r]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)

(2.23)

with
C(\alpha)
as in (
1.4
) above.

Proof.

The derivative of
U^{\mu_{\alpha}}
is a principal value integral
that can be calculated explicitly. The result is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)

\displaystyle=-\fint\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}(y)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-x^{2}\right)-\log\left(\alpha+\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}\right),\qquad\text{for }r<x<1.

(2.24)

We give the details of the calculations for (
2.24
) later, after
finishing the main line of the argument.

The derivative (
2.24
) is negative for
r<x<1
.
Therefore (and by symmetry) the minimum of
U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)

over
[-1,-r]\cup[r,1]
is at
x=\pm 1
. Also
U^{\mu_{\alpha}}
is constant
on
[-r,r]
by (
2.11
). Hence

\min_{x\in[-r,r]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)=U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(r)-U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(1).

In view of (
2.24
) and the fundamental theorem of calculus, we arrive at
(
2.23
) provided that

C(\alpha)=\int_{r}^{1}\left(\log\left(\alpha+\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-x^{2}\right)\right)dx,

(2.25)

with
r=r(\alpha)=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}
.
Thus the proof of the proposition is complete up to the verification of the two
identities (
2.24
) and (
2.25
) to which we turn next.

Proof of the identity (
2.24
).

By (
2.3
) and (
2.5
) the principal value integral in (
2.24
) (with
x\in(r,1)
) splits into two parts

\displaystyle\fint\frac{d\mu_{\alpha}(y)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\fint_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{x-y}dy-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-r}^{r}\frac{1}{x-y}\arccos\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\right)dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+x)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-x)-I_{\alpha}(x)

(2.26)

where

I_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-r}^{r}\frac{1}{x-y}\arccos\left(\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}\right)dy

is a usual integral (not a principal value integral)
that converges for every
x>r
.
We integrate by parts

I_{\alpha}(x)=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{-r}^{r}\log(x-y)\frac{y}{1-y^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}-y^{2}}}dy,\quad x>r,

and then compute the derivative

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dx}I_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{-r}^{r}\frac{1}{x-y}\frac{y}{1-y^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^{2}-y^{2}}}dy

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha x}{1-x^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{x-1}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{x+1}

by first turning the integral into an integral on a contour
around the interval
[-r,r]
in the complex plane, and then
evaluating it by the residue theorem for the exterior domain.
The result can be integrated again to give

I_{\alpha}(x)=-\log\left(\alpha+\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}\right)+\log(1+x),\qquad x>r,

(2.27)

where we note that the constant of integration vanishes since
I_{\alpha}(x)\to 0

as
x\to+\infty
.
Using this in (
2.26
) we obtain (
2.24
).

Proof of the identity (
2.25
).

Observe that (
2.25
) holds for
\alpha=0

since then both sides are equal to
0
.
Thus it is enough to show that the
\alpha
-derivatives of the two
sides agree.

For the left-hand side of (
2.25
) we have by (
1.4
)

\frac{d}{d\alpha}C(\alpha)=\frac{\log(1+\alpha)-\log(1-\alpha)}{2}.

(2.28)

For the right-hand side we first compute the
\alpha
-derivative
of the integrand. Using
r=r(\alpha)=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}

we find by direct calculation

\frac{d}{d\alpha}\left(\log\left(\alpha+\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-x^{2}\right)\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}}.

The integrand of (
2.25
) vanishes
at
x=r
, and thus we obtain the following derivative of
the right hand side of (
2.25
)

\displaystyle\int_{r}^{1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}}dx

\displaystyle=\left.\log\left(x+\sqrt{x^{2}-r^{2}}\right)\right|_{x=r}^{x=1}

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\sqrt{1-r^{2}}\right)-\log r

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\alpha\right)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\alpha^{2})

which after simplification agrees with (
2.28
).
∎

3
Proof of the upper bound

To prove the upper bound (
1.8
) we start by
showing that the extremal polynomial
p_{n}^{*}
has only real
zeros that are separated by the
n
-grid
E_{n}
.

3.1
Zeros of the extremal polynomial

We fix
0<\alpha<1
. For each
n
, we take a polynomial
p_{n}^{*}

of degree
\leq\alpha n
as in (
1.6
) that we normalize
such that

\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}=1=p_{n}^{*}(x_{n}^{*})

(3.1)

for some
x_{n}^{*}\in[-1,1]
. It is clear that
x_{n}^{*}\not\in E_{n}

since otherwise
\|p_{n}\|_{E_{n}}=1
, and the polynomial
would not maximize the ratio.

Lemma 3.1
.

(a)

The polynomial
p_{n}^{*}
minimizes

\|p\|_{E_{n}}
among all polynomials
p

of degree
\leq\alpha n
with
p(x_{n}^{*})=1
.

(b)

The polynomial
q_{n}^{*}
defined by

q_{n}^{*}(x)=x^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}p_{n}^{*}\left(x_{n}^{*}+\tfrac{1}{x}\right)

(3.2)

is a monic polynomial of degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor

that minimizes the weighted uniform norm

\max_{x\in\Sigma_{n}}\left|x^{-\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}q(x)\right|,

(3.3)

among all monic polynomials of degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
,
where
\Sigma_{n}
is the transformed grid,

\quad\Sigma_{n}=\{(x-x_{n}^{*})^{-1}\mid x\in E_{n}\}.

(3.4)

(c)

p_{n}^{*}
has only simple real zeros and
\deg p_{n}^{*}\geq\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1
.

(d)

At least
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1
zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
are in
(-1,1)
.

(e)

The zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
are separated by the
points in the
n
-grid
E_{n}
,

Proof.

(a) Suppose
p
is a polynomial of degree
\alpha n
with
p(x_{n}^{*})=1
and
\|p\|_{E_{n}}<\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}
.
Since
x_{n}^{*}\in[-1,1]
and
p(x_{n}^{*})=1
, we then have
\|p\|_{[-1,1]}\geq 1=\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}
, and therefore

\frac{\|p\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p\|_{E_{n}}}>\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}

which contradicts the extremal property (
1.6
) of
p_{n}^{*}
.

(b) It is easy to see that (
3.2
) is indeed a polynomial of
degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
and it is monic because
p_{n}^{*}(x_{n}^{*})=1
.

Likewise, we associate to any polynomial
p
of degree
\leq\alpha n

with
p(x_{n}^{*})=1
the monic polynomial

q(x)=x^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}p(x_{n}^{*}+\frac{1}{x})
of degree
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
.
Then

p(x)=(x-x_{n}^{*})^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}q\left(\tfrac{1}{x-x_{n}^{*}}\right)

and, with
\Sigma_{n}
as in (
3.3
)

\displaystyle\|p\|_{E_{n}}

\displaystyle=\max_{x\in E_{n}}\left|(x-x_{n}^{*})^{\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}q\left(\tfrac{1}{x-x_{n}^{*}}\right)\right|=\max_{x\in\Sigma_{n}}\left|w(x)q(x)\right|

with

w(x)=|x|^{-\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor}.

Because of part (a) we see that
q_{n}^{*}
minimizes

\|wq\|_{\Sigma_{n}}
among monic polynomials of degree

\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
.

(c)
q_{n}^{*}
has only real zeros. Indeed if
z_{0}

were a non-real zero of
q_{n}^{*}
then

q(x)=\frac{x-\Re z_{0}}{x-z_{0}}q_{n}^{*}(x)

would be a monic polynomial of the same degree satisfying

|q(x)|<|q_{n}^{*}(x)|
for every real
x
that is not a zero of
q_{n}^{*}
.
This would lead to a contradiction with part (b).

Also the zeros of
q_{n}^{*}
are simple, since if
x_{0}
is a higher
order real zero then for small enough
\varepsilon>0
the
monic polynomial

q(x)=\frac{(x-x_{0}-\varepsilon)(x-x_{0}+\varepsilon)}{(x-x_{0})^{2}}q_{n}^{*}(x)

would have a smaller weighted norm
\|wq\|_{\Sigma_{n}}
than
q_{n}^{*}
.
Because of (
3.2
) it then follows that
p_{n}^{*}
has only
simple real zeros as well, since any zero
x_{0}\neq 0
of
q_{n}^{*}
corresponds
to the zero

x_{n}^{*}+\frac{1}{x_{0}}

(3.5)

of
p_{n}^{*}
.

Since
q_{n}^{*}
has
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
simple real zeros, at
least
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1
of them are different from
0
,
and thus
p_{n}^{*}
has at least that number of simple real
zeros.
In particular
p_{n}^{*}
has degree
\geq\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1
.

(d)
The zeros of
q_{n}^{*}
are separated by the points of
\Sigma_{n}
.
Indeed if
x_{1}<x_{2}
are two zeros of
q_{n}^{*}
and the
interval
[x_{1},x_{2}]
would not contain any points of
\Sigma_{n}

then

x\mapsto\frac{(x-x_{1}+\varepsilon)(x-x_{2}-\varepsilon)}{(x-x_{1})(x-x_{2})}q_{n}^{*}(x)

would be a monic polynomial of the same degree with a strictly
smaller weighted norm
\|wq_{n}\|_{\Sigma_{n}}<\|wq_{n}^{*}\|_{\Sigma_{n}}
, provided
\varepsilon>0

is small enough, which would contradict part (b).

From (
1.1
) and (
3.4
) it is easily checked that
(-1-x_{n}^{*})^{-1}
and
(1-x_{n}^{*})^{-1}

are consecutive points in
\Sigma_{n}
. Thus at most one zero of
q_{n}^{*}

is in the closed interval between those points. This zero (if it exists) corresponds
by (
3.5
) to a zero of
p_{n}^{*}
in
(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)
,
or to
\infty
in case
0
is a zero of
q_{n}^{*}
. The other zeros of
q_{n}^{*}

correspond to zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
in
(-1,1)
, and thus
p_{n}^{*}

has at least
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1
zeros in
(-1,1)
.

(e)
The zeros of
q_{n}^{*}
belong to the open interval
(\min\Sigma_{n},\max\Sigma_{n})
.
Indeed, if
x_{0}
is a zero of
q_{n}^{*}
with
x_{0}\geq\max\Sigma_{n}
,
then
q(x)=\frac{x-x_{0}+\varepsilon}{x-x_{0}}q_{n}^{*}(x_{0})
has a smaller weighted
norm
\|wq\|_{\Sigma_{n}}
than
q_{n}^{*}
, if
\varepsilon>0
is small enough,
and we get a similar contradiction if
x_{0}\leq\min\Sigma_{n}
.

We already proved that the zeros of
q_{n}^{*}
are separated by the points in
\Sigma_{n}
,
and so we conclude that the zeros of
q_{n}^{*}
are also separated by the points in
\Sigma_{n}

when the real line is considered as part of the Riemann sphere. The separation
is then preserved under the inversion that is included in the mapping (
3.5
)
to the zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
. This implies that the zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
are
separated by the gridpoints of
E_{n}
as claimed in part (e).
∎

3.2
The functional
J

Recall from (
2.10
) that
\mu_{\alpha}
minimizes
I(\mu)
among measures

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
.
We consider another functional

J(\mu)=\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu}(x)-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x)

(3.6)

on measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
.
Note that by Proposition
2.4
we have

J(\mu_{\alpha})=C(\alpha)>0,

(3.7)

since
\supp(\sigma-\mu_{\alpha})=[-r,r]
.

Proposition 3.2
.

We have

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}\leq\sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}}J(\mu).

Proof.

We start by taking a subsequence
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathbb{N}
such that

\lim_{\mathcal{N}\ni n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}.

(3.8)

The zeros of
p_{n}^{*}
may not be uniformly bounded. However, by Lemma
3.1
(c), there is at most one zero outside
[-2,2]
. If there is such
a zero, say
x_{0}
, then we set

\widehat{p}_{n}(x)=\kappa_{n}^{-1}\frac{p_{n}^{*}(x)}{x-x_{0}},

(3.9)

where
\kappa_{n}
is the leading coefficient of
p_{n}^{*}
. Otherwise we set

\widehat{p}_{n}(x)=\kappa_{n}^{-1}p_{n}^{*}(x).

(3.10)

Then
\widehat{p}_{n}
is a monic polynomial of degree

\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor
or
\lfloor\alpha n\rfloor-1
.
In case (
3.10
) we clearly have

\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=\frac{\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{E_{n}}},

(3.11)

while in case (
3.9
) we can claim that

\frac{1}{3}\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}\leq\frac{\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{E_{n}}}\leq 3\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}.

(3.12)

To obtain (
3.12
) we note that since
|x_{0}|>2

we have
|x_{0}|-1\leq|x-x_{0}|\leq|x_{0}|+1
for
x\in[-1,1]
,
so that

|\kappa_{n}^{-1}|\frac{|p_{n}^{*}(x)|}{|x_{0}|+1}\leq|\widehat{p}_{n}(x)|\leq|\kappa_{n}^{-1}|\frac{|p_{n}^{*}(x)|}{|x_{0}|-1},\qquad\text{for }x\in[-1,1].

Taking the supremum over
x\in[-1,1]
and over
x\in E_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{|\kappa_{n}^{-1}|}{|x_{0}|+1}\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\frac{|\kappa_{n}^{-1}|}{|x_{0}|-1}\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]},

\displaystyle\frac{|\kappa_{n}^{-1}|}{|x_{0}|+1}\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}\leq\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{E_{n}}\leq\frac{|\kappa_{n}^{-1}|}{|x_{0}|-1}\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}.

Taking ratios of these inequalities leads to (
3.12
) since
\frac{|x_{0}|+1}{|x_{0}|-1}<3
for
|x_{0}|>2
.

From (
3.8
) (
3.11
), (
3.12
) it then follows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=\lim_{\mathcal{N}\ni n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{E_{n}}}.

(3.13)

Next, by taking a further subsequence if necessary, we may also
assume that the sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n}
of normalized zero counting measures, i.e.,

\nu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{x:\widehat{p}_{n}(x)=0}\delta_{x}

converges in the weak
∗
sense as
n\to\infty
with
n\in\mathcal{N}
.
Here we use Helly’s selection theorem, and Lemma
3.1
(c).
By Lemma
3.1
(d) the weak
∗
limit, say
\mu
, belongs to

\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
.

Now we apply Lemmas
2.1
and
2.3
to the polynomials
\widehat{p}_{n}
.
From part (a) of Lemma
2.1
we get

\liminf_{\mathcal{N}\ni n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{E_{n}}\geq-\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu}(x)

and from Lemma
2.3

\lim_{\mathcal{N}\ni n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\|\widehat{p}_{n}\|_{[-1,1]}=-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x).

These limits and (
3.13
) then imply that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}\leq J(\mu)

and the proposition since
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
.
∎

3.3
Conclusion of the proof of the upper bound

In view of Proposition
3.2
it remains to show
that
\sup\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}}J(\mu)=C(\alpha)

in order to obtain (
1.8
). This is the final result of the paper.

Proposition 3.3
.

For every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}

with
\mu\neq\mu_{\alpha}
we have

J(\mu)<J(\mu_{\alpha})=C(\alpha).

(3.14)

Proof.

We already noted in (
3.7
) that
J(\mu_{\alpha})=C(\alpha)>0
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
with
\mu\neq\mu_{\alpha}
.
Take
x_{0}\in[-1,1]
with

U^{\mu}(x_{0})=\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu}(x).

(3.15)

If
x_{0}\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)
, then the minimimu of
U^{\mu}
over

\supp(\sigma-\mu)
is also attained at
x_{0}
, and it would follow from (
3.6
) that
J(\mu)=0
.
Then the strict inequality (
3.14
) holds.
Hence we may assume that
x_{0}\in[-1,1]\setminus\supp(\sigma-\mu)
.

Since
\mu\leq\sigma
and
\mu_{\alpha}\leq\sigma
we see that
both
U^{\mu}
and
U^{\mu_{\alpha}}
are continuous functions on
\mathbb{C}
,
see also Remark
2.2
, and they are
both harmonic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,1]
.
Also
\mu\geq\mu_{\alpha}
on
[-1,1]\setminus\supp(\sigma-\mu)
,
and therefore

U^{\mu-\mu_{\alpha}}
is superharmonic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\supp(\sigma-\mu)
.
It has a finite limit at infinity since
\mu
and
\mu_{\alpha}
have the same total mass,
and therefore
U^{\mu-\mu_{\alpha}}
, when viewed as a function on the Riemann sphere,
extends to a function that is harmonic at infinity, cf.
[
2
, Corollary 5.2.3]
.
The minimum principle for superharmonic functions
[
9
,
16
]

then tells us that the minimum of
U^{\mu-\mu_{\alpha}}
is taken
on
\supp(\sigma-\mu)
only. In particular, since
x_{0}\not\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)

U^{\mu-\mu_{\alpha}}(x_{0})>\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu-\mu_{\alpha}}(x).

(3.16)

Combining (
3.16
) with the obvious inequality (since
\supp(\sigma-\mu)\subset[-1,1]
)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu-\mu_{\alpha}}(x)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu}(x)-\max_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x),

we obtain

\displaystyle U^{\mu}(x_{0})-U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x_{0})>\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu}(x)-\max_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x),

which leads to

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu)}U^{\mu}(x)-U^{\mu}(x_{0})

\displaystyle<\max_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)-U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)-\min_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x).

(3.17)

The left-hand side of (
3.17
) is equal to
J(\mu)
because
of (
3.6
) and (
3.15
).
For the right-hand side, we note that by the special property (
2.11
)
of
U^{\mu_{\alpha}}
we have

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x)=\ell_{\alpha}=\min_{x\in\supp(\sigma-\mu_{\alpha})}U^{\mu_{\alpha}}(x),

and therefore the right-hand side of (
3.17
) is equal to
J(\mu_{\alpha})
. Thus
J(\mu)<J(\mu_{\alpha})
and the proposition is proved.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

According to Proposition
3.3
the constrained equilibrium measure

\mu_{\alpha}
is the unique maximizer of
J(\mu)
among measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
.
We may conclude from this that the sequence of normalized zero counting
measures of the extremal polynomials
p_{n}^{*}
tends to the constrained
equilibrium measure
\mu_{\alpha}
as
n\to\infty
.
This follows from the proof of Proposition
3.2
, combined with the proven
fact that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\frac{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{[-1,1]}}{\|p_{n}^{*}\|_{E_{n}}}=C(\alpha),

as this gives that the weak
∗
limit of any convergent subsequence is a measure

\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\alpha,\sigma}
with
J(\mu)=C(\alpha)
.
Because of (
3.14
) this limit has to be
\mu_{\alpha}
, and thus
by a compactness argument the full sequence tends to
\mu_{\alpha}
indeed.
