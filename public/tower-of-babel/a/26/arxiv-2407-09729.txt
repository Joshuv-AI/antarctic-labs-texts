The Casimir-Lifshitz formula for rectangular dielectric waveguide
E. Arias, G. O. Heymans, N. F. Svaiter

Abstract
We analyze the Casimir-Lifshitz effect associated with the electromagnetic field in the presence of a rectangular waveguide consisting of two distinct dielectric materials in a ( 3 + 1 ) (3+1) -dimensional spacetime. We employ the surface mode technique to derive a generalized Lifshitz formula for this specific geometry. Our formulation accounts for the unique dielectric properties of the materials composing the waveguide, leading to a precise calculation of the Casimir-Lifshitz energy. In the asymptotic limit, our results recover the classical expressions for perfect reflecting boundaries. This work extends the applicability of the Lifshitz formula to more complex systems and provides valuable insights into the influence of dielectric materials on the electromagnetic Casimir effect.

Introduction

The Casimir effect is a remarkable consequence of the
quantization of the electromagnetic field and the distortion of vacuum modes by the presence of classical boundaries. This effect demonstrates the influence of vacuum modes of quantum fields in macroscopic phenomena. Since the seminal work of Casimir
Casimir 1948
, this effect has attracted great research interest due to its wide connections with different areas of theoretical explorations and practical applications

;
Bordag et al. 2009
;
Klimchitskaya et al. 2009
. From the theoretical perspective, one can mention the importance of zero-point energy in cosmology with
standard model extension perspectives
Weinberg 1989
. Also, from the point of view of technological applications, the Casimir effect could bring us limits for classical boundaries interference
over quantum effects in nanotechnology devices
Woods et al. 2016
;
Gong et al. 2021
.
The experimental verification of Casimir prediction have been established along the years by numerous efforts
Sparnaay 1958
;
Mohideen and Roy 1998
;
Babb et al. 2000
;
Chan et al. 2001
. These works confirm the existence of forces, induced by vacuum fluctuations, between macroscopic objects. In this way, we mention, for example, the works of
Lamoreaux where a micromechanical torsional oscillator have been employed to measure the Casimir pressure with high precision
Lamoreaux 1997
.
An interesting discussion of MEMS (MicrosElectroMechanical System) using vacuum force was presented by Serry et al. in Ref.
Serry et al. 1995
. These authors consider an anharmonic Casimir oscillator constructed with a spring attached to one of the plates of the Casimir slab configuration.

The Casimir effect in real materials is an important topic of modern research. In this way, the work of Lifshitz is a cornerstone that boosted the analysis of Casimir effect in real material media
Lifshitz 1956
. The basic idea is to model vacuum quantum fluctuations by a stochastic fluctuating electromagnetic field and explore the fluctuation-dissipation theorem to infer the frequency dependent Casimir energy. The Lifshitz general result
give us a formula for the Casimir energy density and pressure in a plane geometry fulfill with different dielectric media.
Other approaches have enriched the discussion and reinforce the result obtained by Lifshitz. For instance, by using Green’s function approach, Dzyaloshinskii et al. obtain the same result for the Casimir effect in dielectrics
Dzyaloshinskii et al. 1960
.
In the framework of spectral theory, it is possible to obtain the correction to Casimir energy due to finite conductivity effects, by using approximate functional equations. Recently it was obtained
the Casimir
energy for a massless scalar field in the presence of a rectangular box with non-ideal boundary conditions using an approximate functional equation of the Epstein zeta-function
Arias et al. 2023
.
This derivation was possible using an analytic regularization procedure. For a discussion of the relation between an analytic regularization procedure and the cut-off method see
Svaiter and Svaiter 1992a
;
Svaiter and Svaiter 1992b
;
Svaiter and Svaiter 1993
.
We also mention
the approach proposed by Van Kampen et al.,
where the physical effect of dielectric is carried on by the so call surface stationary modes of the electromagnetic field

.
The discrepancy between the dissipationless plasma model of dielectric materials and the dissipative Drude model
was also discussed in the literature, e.g. Ref.
Klimchitskaya and Mostepanenko 2023
.
Although finite-sized cavity with perfect conductors have been discussed before, see Ref.
Ambjorn and Wolfram 1983
, the inclusion of the dielectric lead to highly non-trivial problems. The case of a dielectric cylinder was discussed in Ref.
Cavero-Pelaez and Milton 2005
. However, the rectangular waveguide was not discussed, we believe, because the conditions to make the boundary conditions consistent alongside to the corner is problematic. Our approach to such problems will be discussed in this manuscript.

In the present work we
study the Casimir effect in a rectangular waveguide that is filled with different dielectric materials using the Van Kampen method.
The key point is the sensibility

Balian and Duplantier 1978

Milton et al. 1999

Lukosz 1971

II
Casimir energy in a perfect conducting waveguide

In this section, we present the Casimir energy of a rectangular waveguide with perfect conductivity walls by using an analytic regularization procedure. For convenience, we consider a
(d+1)
dimensional spacetime.
We regularize the spectrum of the Hamiltonian in the spatial domain
\mathbb{R}^{d-2}\times[0,a]\times[0,b]

Svaiter and Svaiter 1992a
;
Valuyan et al. 2008
.

The quantization of the electromagnetic field can be performed using the vector potential. In this case,
the scalar and longitudinal modes are presented in the theory. However, one can remove such modes in a Lorentz-covariant way using the Gupta-Blueler approach
Gupta 1950
;
Bleuler 1950
. For our proposal, the manifest Lorentz covariance can be dropped out, of choosing the Coulomb gauge. In this situation, the
wave equation of the electromagnetic potential
can be written as

\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}-\Delta\right)\mathbf{A}(t,\mathbf{x})=0,

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}
is the vector potential, and
\Delta
is the Laplace operator in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From that one can proceed with the usual process of quantization, i.e., define the canonical momentum and impose the commutation relations
Källén 2013
.

To proceed we will first restrict the system to a box with sides
(L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{d-2},a,b)
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Now we have a pure point spectrum and one can go and define the occupation number and a Fock representation. States of such a system are characterized by occupation numbers and, in particular, one can define a vacuum state.

Due to the spectral theorem, the expected value of the Hamiltonian in the vacuum state, that will be denoted by
E_{d}(L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{d-2},a,b)
, is given by the Riemann-Stieljes integral of the spectral measure of the Hamiltonian operator in such a domain. Assuming that
L_{i}\gg a,b
for all
i=1,\dots,d-2
one can write such a quantity as

E_{d}(L_{1},...,L_{d-2},a,b)=\frac{A_{d-2}}{(2\pi)^{d-2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d-2}q\sum_{m,n=1}^{\infty}\hbar\,\omega_{mn}(q)

(2)

where we have used the following definition of the hyperarea

A_{d-2}=\prod_{i=1}^{d-2}L_{i},

(3)

with the frequencies given by

\omega_{mn}(q)=c\sqrt{q^{2}+\left(\frac{m\pi}{a}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}},

(4)

and the continuous momenta

q^{2}=q_{1}^{2}+...+q_{d-2}^{2}.

(5)

Of course, as usual in this type of calculation, one need to implement some regularization procedure. Here we will apply an analytic regularization procedure in which we will insert a parameter,
s\in\mathbb{C}
, and our Casimir energy will be given by an analytic extension. With some straightforward manipulations and inserting the parameter in which we will perform the analytic continuation, one can recast the Eq. (
2
), in a more enlightening form

\displaystyle\epsilon_{d}(a,b;s)=\frac{1}{A_{d-2}}E_{d}(L_{1},...,L_{d-2},a,b;s)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hbar}{2(2\pi)^{d-2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d-2}q\left[\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{m,n=-\infty}^{\infty}\omega_{mn}^{-s}(q)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{0n}^{-s}(q)-2\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\omega_{m0}^{-s}(q)\right],

(6)

where the prime over the summation sign means that the term with
m=n=0
is removed from the double series. In order to keep with the calculations, we can perform a change of variables in the continuum momenta to a spherical system of coordinates, with radii variable given by
q
and angular element
\mathrm{d}\Omega_{\mathrm{d}-2}
. The angular integration leads to the factor

\int\mathrm{d}\Omega_{d-2}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d-2}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-2}{2}\right)},

(7)

the integration over
q
can also be performed, leading to

\displaystyle\epsilon_{d}(a,b;s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar\,c\,\pi^{-}\frac{d}{2}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{s}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\right)}{2^{d-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)}\left[Z_{2}\left(\frac{1}{a},\frac{1}{b};s-d+2\right)-\left(\frac{1}{a^{d-2-s}}+\frac{1}{b^{d-2-s}}\right)\zeta(s-d+2)\right],

(8)

where we have used the definition of the Epstein zeta-function

Z_{2}(x,y;s)=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{m,n=-\infty}^{\infty}\left[(xm)^{2}+(yn)^{2}\right]^{-s/2},

(9)

and the definition of the Riemann zeta-function

\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}.

(10)

Using the reflection formula, we can write the Epstein zeta-function as

\displaystyle Z_{2}\left(\frac{1}{a},\frac{1}{b};s-d+2\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{ab}{\pi{{}^{s+\frac{d-1}{2}}}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2-d+s}{2}\right)}

(11)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle Z_{2}(a,b;d-s),

to the Riemann zeta-function, we use the reflection formula alongside with the Legendre duplication formula for the
\Gamma
-function to obtain

\Gamma\left(1+\frac{s}{2}-\frac{d}{2}\right)\zeta(s-d+2)=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{d-1-s}{2}\right)}{\pi^{-s+\frac{d}{2}+1}}\zeta(d-1-s).

(12)

One can write general expression for
\epsilon_{d}(a,b;s)
employing analytic extension procedure. The Casimir energy for the waveguide is obtained for
s=-1
.
Which is finite for
d\geq 3
. In our case of interest,
d=3
, one can write

\epsilon_{3}(a,b;-1)=\frac{\hbar\,c}{16\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{a^{2}}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}\right)\zeta(3)-\frac{\hbar\,c}{8\pi}ab\,Z_{2}\left(a,b;4\right).

(13)

The sign difference between the two contributions is what ensures the well known behavior of change of sign of the Casimir force in a box geometry.

In Section
III
we explore the stationary modes in a dielectric rectangular waveguide. Later, such modes are being used alongside with the analytic continuation procedure and plasma frequencies definition to obtain the Casimir force of such a system.

III
Stationary electromagnetic modes in dielectric waveguide

In order to find the zero-point energy of the electromagnetic field in a waveguide fulfill with dielectric material, we find the surface stationary modes in this specific geometry.
In this manner, let us begin the discussion by considering the Maxwell equations for dielectric (non-magnetic) materials

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf D}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot{\bf B}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bf E}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial{\bf B}}{\partial t},

\displaystyle\nabla\times{\bf B}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial{\bf D}}{\partial t},

(14)

where the electric displacement vector is given by
{\bf D}({\bf r},t)=\epsilon(\omega){\bf E}({\bf r},t)
. Since we are assuming an isotropic homogeneous media, the dielectric constant is not dependent on the spatial coordinates, but it depends on the wave frequency. We are interested in finding stationary solutions of the field equations of the form

\displaystyle{\bf E}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle={\bf E}_{0}({\bf r})e^{-i\omega t},

\displaystyle{\bf B}({\bf r},t)

\displaystyle={\bf B}_{0}({\bf r})e^{-i\omega t}.

(15)

Our purpose is to find the natural frequencies
\omega
that satisfy the Maxwell equations with the appropriate boundary conditions. Let us consider a geometry given by a waveguide along the
z
-direction that have
a
length in the
x
-direction and
b
length in the
y
-direction, that is, the waveguide is defined by the set of ordered pairs
\{(x,y);\,x\in[0,a],\,y\in[0,b]\}
. The non-perfect conducting materials have dielectric constants
\epsilon_{1}
inside and
\epsilon_{2}
outside the waveguide, see Figure (
1
).

Geometry of the electromagnetic waveguide with rectangular cross-section and filled with different dielectric materials from inside and outside.

By the translation symmetry along the
z
-axis we expect that the electric and magnetic fields only depend on coordinates
x
and
y
, while in the
z
direction we expect a free plane wave term. Hence, we look for a solution of the form

\displaystyle{\bf E}_{0}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\left(e_{x}(x,y)\hat{i}+e_{y}(x,y)\hat{j}+e_{z}(x,y)\hat{k}\right)e^{ikz},

\displaystyle{\bf B}_{0}({\bf r})

\displaystyle=\left(b_{x}(x,y)\hat{i}+b_{y}(x,y)\hat{j}+b_{z}(x,y)\hat{k}\right)e^{ikz}.

(16)

By using the Gauss law in the electric displacement field, one find that

\frac{\partial e_{x}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial e_{y}}{\partial y}+ik\,e_{z}=0.

(17)

From the Faraday law we have that the spatial dependence of the magnetic field is
{\bf B}_{0}=-i(c/\omega)\nabla\times{\bf E}_{0}
, and therefore we can write

\displaystyle{\bf B}_{0}=-i\left(\frac{c}{\omega}\right)\bigg\{

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial e_{z}}{\partial y}-ike_{y}\right)\hat{i}+\left(ike_{x}-\frac{\partial e_{z}}{\partial x}\right)\hat{j}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{\partial e_{y}}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial e_{x}}{\partial y}\right)\hat{k}\bigg\}e^{ikz},

(18)

from this equation it follows immediately that the Gauss law for the magnetic field is satisfied, i.e.
\nabla\cdot{\bf B}_{0}=0
. Also, after some manipulations,
we have straightforward that
\nabla\times{\bf B}_{0}=-i(c/\omega)\nabla\times(\nabla\times{\bf E}_{0})=i(c/\omega)\nabla^{2}{\bf E}_{0}
. Replacing this into the Ampere law, we obtain the wave equation

\nabla^{2}{\bf E}_{0}+\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon(\omega){\bf E}_{0}=0.

(19)

By using Eq. (
16
) into Eq. (
19
), we have that the components of the electric field must satisfy

\frac{\partial^{2}e_{i}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}e_{i}}{\partial y^{2}}-K^{2}\,e_{i}=0,

(20)

where the index is
i=\{x,y,z\}
and we have defined the wave number on the transverse section of the waveguide

K^{2}=k^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon(\omega).

(21)

From the analysis of the boundary conditions, discussed in Appendix
A

The origin of this problematic is the impossibility of defining a normal and tangential component in the corners of the rectangular waveguide. For example, the
y
-component of the electric field is normal to the horizontal surfaces
horizontal surfaces
H_{1}=\{(x,y)\in[0,a]\times[0,0]\}
and
H_{2}=\{(x,y)\in[0,a]\times[b,b]\}
,
so that it must be satisfied that
\epsilon(\omega)\,e_{y}
should be continuous at that surfaces, however the same
y
-direction is tangential when referring to the vertical surfaces
V_{1}=\{(x,y)\in[0,0]\times[0,b]\}
and
V_{2}=\{(x,y)\in[a,a]\times[0,b]\}
,
where only
e_{y}
must be continuous. For this case one can conclude that, in order to satisfy both conditions at the four corners of the waveguide,
(x,y)\in\{(0,0),(a,0),(0,b),(a,b)\}
, it must be that the transverse component should vanish, i.e.,
e_{x}=e_{y}=0
.
We can extend this observation and look for stationary solutions where we have always
e_{y}(x,y)\equiv 0
, for all the points of space and this conditions will define our
X
-mode solution. Similarly, we can search for and independently solutions where

, and this conditions will define our

-mode.

and

:

\epsilon(\omega)e_{x}
,
\partial_{x}e_{x}
and
\partial_{y}e_{x}
must be continuous.
For the horizontal surfaces,
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
:
the components
e_{x}
,
\partial_{x}e_{x}
and
\partial_{y}e_{x}
must be continuous.

Here we note some apparent contradiction. From the continuity of the normal component of the electric field we have that
\epsilon(\omega)e_{x}
must be continuous at the verticals

and

; while from the continuity of the tangential component of the electric field only
e_{x}
must be continuous at horizontals
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. But we know that it is impossible to both

be continuous on the interface between dielectrics with different properties. In order to fulfill both requirement independently, we have two possibilities, that defines the mode

For the mode

,

e_{x}(x,0)=e_{x}(x,b)=0
, for all values

. The wave equation given by Eq. (
20
), can be solve by separation of variables method.
Hence, we look for solutions of the form

By considering explicitly the boundary conditions for

-modes and avoiding nonphysical exponentially growing solutions, we find oscillatory solutions in the

-direction and exponentially decaying functions in the

-direction. This means that the

-modes solutions are given by
e^{(n)}_{x}(x,y)=f_{n}(x)g_{n}(y)

where

. We have a surface decaying behavior from the waveguide surfaces at the

De^{-\Lambda_{2}x},&a<x\\
\end{cases}

(25)

where we have defined the wave numbers

\Lambda_{1,2}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+k^{2}-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon_{1,2}(\omega)}.

(26)

These wave numbers indeed depend on integer

. However, we have omitted an explicit dependence on these parameters in order to emphasize the dependence of

-mode solutions. Now, the components

,

.
As before, we look for solutions that are exponentially decaying from the waveguide surface.

-direction and exponentially decaying solutions in the

-direction. For the

-modes we have solutions of the form
e_{x}^{(n)}(x,y)=f_{n}(x)g_{n}(x)
, where in this case

(28)

where

and in the

g_{n}(y)=\begin{cases}{\cal A}e^{\Upsilon_{2}y},&\,y<0\\

where we have defined the wave numbers

.

{\cal F}_{Y2}(n,k,\omega)=e^{b\Upsilon_{1}}\left(\frac{\Upsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}+\Upsilon_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{\Upsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}-\Upsilon_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2}-e^{-b\Upsilon_{1}}=0,

IV
Casimir energy in dielectric waveguide

In the previous section, we have focus on the exponentially decaying stationary solutions of the electromagnetic field in the waveguide geometry. These are referred as surface modes, and it can be proved that, in the small distances, only these modes contribute to vacuum energy
Langbein 1973
;
Barton 1979
. The zero-point vacuum energy is given by the sum of all the possible frequency that ensure the existence of modes
X
and
Y
. Then in the case of dielectric waveguide consider here we zero-point energy is

E_{ZP}=\sum_{N}\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{N}^{X}+\sum_{N}\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega_{N}^{Y},

(34)

where
\omega_{N}^{X}
and
\omega_{N}^{Y}
are the allowed frequencies for
X
-mode and
Y
-mode, respectively. Since these modes depend on the continuum variable
k
, associated with the plane waves in the
z
-direction and on the discrete index
n
, that ensure the appropriate boundary conditions the sum refers to

\sum_{N}\rightarrow\left(\frac{L_{z}}{2\pi}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r},

where
L_{z}
is the dimension of the large non-compactified dimension of the waveguide and the sum
\sum_{r}
refers to roots or zeros of the boundary condition equations given by Eq.(
27
) and Eqs. (
31
-
33
). For example, in order to consider an
X
-mode frequency it must be ensured that this frequency is a root of the functions that defines the boundary conditions, i.e.,
{\cal F}_{X}(n,k,\omega_{r}^{X})=0
, where we must consider the function indicated in Eq. (
27
) or Eq. (
31
). It is clear that the frequencies that satisfy this conditions depends on
n
and
k
, such that
\omega_{r}^{X}=\omega_{r}^{X}(n,k)
and the zero-point energy can be written as

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{4\pi}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r}\bigg(\omega_{r}^{X}(n,k)+\omega_{r}^{Y}(n,k)\bigg).

(35)

To proceed, we use the argument theorem of complex variables. This theorem indicates that for any meromorphic complex function, it is satisfied that

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C}\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}dz={\cal N}-{\cal P},

(36)

where
C
is any closed contour,
{\cal N}
is the quantity of roots or zeros of the function inside
C
and
{\cal P}
is the number of poles inside
C
. This theorem could be extended in such a way that

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C}z\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}dz=\sum_{r}z_{r}-\sum_{p}z_{p},

(37)

where the first sum is restricted to the roots of the function inside the contour,
f(z_{r})=0
, while the second sum is for the poles of the function,
f(z_{p})\rightarrow\infty
.
Since
\omega_{r}^{X}
are the roots of the function
{\cal F}_{X}(n,k,\omega)
, for a given
n
and
k
, one can write the sum as

\sum_{r}\omega_{r}^{X}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{C}\omega\frac{{\cal F^{\prime}}_{X}(\omega)}{{\cal F}_{X}(\omega)}d\omega+\sum_{p}\omega_{p}^{X},

where
\omega_{p}^{X}
are poles of
{\cal F}_{X}(\omega)

and we consider the contour
C
as a semicircle (to be considered of large radius) that contains the imaginary axis and is closed counterclockwise from the right. The last sum in the above equation is the sum of all the poles of the function, and we assume that this does not depend on the boundaries in such a way that would not contribute to the Casimir energy. Hence, we obtain that

\displaystyle E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{4\pi}\right)\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\oint_{C}\omega\frac{{\cal F^{\prime}}_{X}(n,k,\omega)}{{\cal F}_{X}(n,k,\omega)}d\omega+\oint_{C}\omega\frac{{\cal F^{\prime}}_{Y}(n,k,\omega)}{{\cal F}_{Y}(n,k,\omega)}d\omega\bigg),

(38)

here the notation is such that the derivative
{\cal F^{\prime}}_{X}(n,k,\omega)=\partial{\cal F}_{X}(n,k,\omega)/\partial\omega
. In the limit of infinite radius for the contour
C
the only non-zero contribution to the complex integral above comes from the imaginary axis
\omega=i\xi
where
\xi\in(+\infty,-\infty)
. By a change of variables, we define
F_{X}(\xi)={\cal F}_{X}(i\xi)
.
Performing an integration by parts and some manipulations, we find

\displaystyle E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{8\pi^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\bigg(\ln F_{X}(n,k,\xi)+\ln F_{Y}(n,k,\xi)\bigg).

(39)

Now, we have to consider that there are two contributions for each
X
-mode and
Y
-mode. Hence, by using explicitly the boundary equations for all the surface modes Eq. (
27
) and Eqs. (
31
-
33
) we obtain the zero-point energy in the dielectric waveguide as

\displaystyle E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{8\pi^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi

\displaystyle\bigg\{\ln\left(e^{a{\lambda}_{1}}\left(\frac{{\lambda}_{1}\epsilon_{2}+{\lambda}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{{\lambda}_{1}\epsilon_{2}-{\lambda}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2}-e^{-a{\lambda}_{1}}\right)+\ln\left(e^{b{\upsilon}_{1}}\left(\frac{{\upsilon}_{1}+{\upsilon}_{2}}{{\upsilon}_{1}-{\upsilon}_{2}}\right)^{2}-e^{-b{\upsilon}_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle+\ln\left(e^{b{\upsilon}_{1}}\left(\frac{{\upsilon}_{1}\epsilon_{2}+{\upsilon}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{{\upsilon}_{1}\epsilon_{2}-{\upsilon}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2}-e^{-b{\upsilon}_{1}}\right)+\ln\left(e^{a{\lambda}_{1}}\left(\frac{{\lambda}_{1}+{\lambda}_{2}}{{\lambda}_{1}-{\lambda}_{2}}\right)^{2}-e^{-a{\lambda}_{1}}\right)\bigg\},

(40)

where we have denoted the wave numbers for imaginary frequencies by

\displaystyle{\lambda}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+k^{2}+\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon_{1,2}(i\xi)},

(41)

and

\displaystyle{\upsilon}_{1,2}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^{2}+k^{2}+\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon_{1,2}(i\xi)},

(42)

these variables depend on the integer
n
, the wave number in the
z
-direction,
k
, and on the imaginary frequency
\xi
. It is worth to note that in general the dielectric constant depend on the wave frequency.

The main result of this work is Eq. (
40
), that gives the zero-point energy associated to an electromagnetic field in a waveguide of rectangular cross-section surrounded by two dielectric materials. In our case the finite dimensions of the waveguide are
a,b
while the third dimension is
L_{z}
and
L_{z}\gg a,b
.
The original Lifshitz result, in the case of two parallel plates, can be recovered from our result
if we keep one direction of the waveguide fixed and allow the other ones goes to infinity.
More specific, in this limit we can consider that
a
is finite while
L_{z},b\gg a
.
In order to take this limit, we use the following relation

\lim_{b\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)=\left(\frac{b}{2\pi}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tilde{k}\,f(\tilde{k})

and define the variable

K_{i}=\sqrt{\tilde{k}^{2}+k^{2}+\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon_{i}(i\xi)}.

In this manner, we can write

\displaystyle E^{plates}_{ZP}=

\displaystyle\,{\cal N}\!\!\int\!dkd\tilde{k}\,d\xi\bigg\{\ln\left(\left(\frac{{K}_{1}+{K}_{2}}{{K}_{1}-{K}_{2}}\right)^{2}e^{2a{K}_{1}}-1\right)

\displaystyle+\ln\left(\left(\frac{{K}_{1}\epsilon_{2}+{K}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{{K}_{1}\epsilon_{2}-{K}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2}e^{2a{K}_{1}}-1\right)\bigg\}.

(43)

where

{\cal N}=\hbar L_{z}b/16\pi^{3}.

This expression is obtained by considering the limits of the contributions of the first and fourth terms inside the integral of the general result Eq. (
40
). This is because the dependence of second and third term inside the integral Eq. (
40
) only depends on length
b
through the exponential factor that give us an infinite (constant) term in the limit
b\rightarrow\infty
.
In the above integral, we have that all the limits of integration are from
-\infty
to
+\infty
. By considering
k
and
\tilde{k}
as coordinates of a two-dimensional space, defining
\kappa=\sqrt{k^{2}+\tilde{k}^{2}}
and performing the angular integration we obtain

\displaystyle\!\!E^{plates}_{ZP}=

\displaystyle{\tilde{\cal N}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!d\kappa\,\kappa\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!d\xi\bigg\{\ln\left(\left(\frac{{K}_{1}+{K}_{2}}{{K}_{1}-{K}_{2}}\right)^{2}e^{2a{K}_{1}}-1\right)

\displaystyle+\ln\left(\left(\frac{{K}_{1}\epsilon_{2}+{K}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{{K}_{1}\epsilon_{2}-{K}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2}e^{2a{K}_{1}}-1\right)\bigg\},

(44)

where

{\tilde{\cal N}}=\hbar L_{z}b/8\pi^{2}
.
The above equation is exactly the Lifshitz formula, for the case of two parallel plates (separated by a finite distance
a
) with a media of dielectric constant
\epsilon_{1}
between two media of dielectric constant
\epsilon_{2}
in a slab configuration.

Let us discuss the general result given by Eq. (
40
)
for the case where the surfaces are perfect conductors.
In order to do this, first one can rewrite the expression for the zero-point energy in a more compact way

\displaystyle E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{8\pi^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\bigg\{\ln\left({\cal X}_{1}e^{a{\lambda}_{1}}-e^{-a{\lambda}_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!+\ln\left({\cal X}_{2}e^{b{\upsilon}_{1}}-e^{-b{\upsilon}_{1}}\right)+\ln\left({\cal Y}_{1}e^{b{\upsilon}_{1}}-e^{-b{\upsilon}_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!+\ln\left({\cal Y}_{2}e^{a{\lambda}_{1}}-e^{-a{\lambda}_{1}}\right)\bigg\},

(45)

where the reflectivity indexes are defined as follows

\displaystyle{\cal X}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{{\lambda}_{1}\epsilon_{2}+{\lambda}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{{\lambda}_{1}\epsilon_{2}-{\lambda}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt{\cal X}_{2}=\left(\frac{{\upsilon}_{1}+{\upsilon}_{2}}{{\upsilon}_{1}-{\upsilon}_{2}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle{\cal Y}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{{\upsilon}_{1}\epsilon_{2}+{\upsilon}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}{{\upsilon}_{1}\epsilon_{2}-{\upsilon}_{2}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt{\cal Y}_{2}=\left(\frac{{\lambda}_{1}+{\lambda}_{2}}{{\lambda}_{1}-{\lambda}_{2}}\right)^{2}.

(46)

In the ideal case the media inside the waveguide is a perfect vacuum with
\epsilon_{1}=1
whereas the boundaries are perfect reflecting surfaces with
\epsilon_{2}\rightarrow\infty
.
From this follows that the wave numbers inside the waveguide are given by
\lambda_{1}\rightarrow\lambda^{(0)}
and
\upsilon_{1}\rightarrow\upsilon^{(0)}
with

\displaystyle{\lambda}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+k^{2}+\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}},

\displaystyle{\upsilon}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^{2}+k^{2}+\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}}.

(47)

For the other side, outside the waveguide one has a perfect conductor with
\epsilon_{2}\rightarrow\infty
and consequently
\lambda_{2},\upsilon_{2}\rightarrow\infty
.
It can be shown that in this limit all the reflectivity indexes, Eq. (
46
), tend to unity and hence in the zero-point energy for the ideal conducting waveguide is just given by

\displaystyle E^{Ideal}_{ZP}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{4\pi^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!\!dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\bigg\{a{\lambda}^{(0)}+b{\upsilon}^{(0)}

\displaystyle+\ln\left(1-e^{-2a{\lambda}^{(0)}}\right)+\ln\left(1-e^{-2b{\upsilon}^{(0)}}\right)\bigg\}.

(48)

The above expression is symmetric under the permutation of the cavity length, so we can write

E^{ideal}_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar cL_{z}}{4\pi^{2}}\right)\bigg({\cal I}(a,b)+{\cal I}(b,a)\bigg),

(49)

with the integral

\displaystyle{\cal I}(a,b)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int d^{2}\rho

\displaystyle\bigg\{\,a\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+\rho^{2}}

\displaystyle+\ln\left(1-e^{-2a\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\right)\bigg\},

(50)

where we have defined the bidimensional vector
\vec{\rho}=(k,\xi/c)
with
\rho^{2}=k^{2}+\xi^{2}/c^{2}
.
The ideal result of the zero-point energy given by Eq. (
48
) need to be regularized.
Here we use the dimensional regularization
Bollini and Giambiagi 1972
;
Ashmore 1972
;
Edery 2006
. In this manner, let us define the
s
-dimensional integrals

\displaystyle{\cal J}_{s}(a,b)

\displaystyle=a\,\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int d^{s}\rho\,\,\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+\rho^{2}},

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{s}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int d^{s}\rho\,\,\ln\left(1-e^{-2a\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\right),

(51)

from these expressions, we see that the zero-point energy Eq. (
48
) can be recovered when
s=2
, since it is clear that

{\cal I}(a,b)={\cal J}_{2}(a,b)+{\cal K}_{2}(a,b)
.
The terms in Eq. (
51
) only depends on the modulus of the
s
-dimensional vector
\vec{\rho}
, one can perform the general solid angle integration with Eq. (
7
).
For other side, the integration over the modulus
\rho
can be realized by using the Beta function representations

B(x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)}{\Gamma(x+y)}.

Finally, by using the reflection formula of the Riemann zeta function, analogously to the Eq. (
12
),
one can prove that

{\cal J}_{s}(a,b)=-\frac{a}{2b^{s+1}}\pi^{s/2-1}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)\zeta(s+2).

(52)

The second regularized integral
{\cal K}_{s}
, in Eq. (
51
), can be rewritten by using the Taylor expansion of the logarithm function as follows

\ln(1-x)=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x^{k}/k
valid for
|x|<1
. Hence, we can write

{\cal K}_{s}(a,b)=\!-\frac{2\pi^{s/2}}{\Gamma(s/2)}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m}\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\!d\rho\,\rho^{s-1}e^{-2ma\sqrt{\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+\rho^{2}}}.

In order to perform the integration above, we use
the integral representation of the modified Bessel function
Gradshteyn and Ryzhik 1943

K_{\nu}(xz)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\Gamma(\nu+\frac{1}{2})}\left(\frac{x}{2z}\right)^{\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,t^{2\nu}\frac{e^{-x\sqrt{t^{2}+z^{2}}}}{\sqrt{t^{2}+z^{2}}},

hence, one obtain that

{\cal K}_{s}(a,b)=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{3s-1}{2}}}{b^{s}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m}\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(K_{\frac{s-1}{2}}(\lambda n)\left(\frac{2n}{\lambda}\right)^{\frac{s-1}{2}}\right),

where we have defined
\lambda=2m\pi a/b
. Now, by using the following recursion formula of modified Bessel function

\left(\frac{1}{z}\frac{d}{dz}\right)^{m}K_{0}(z)=(-1)^{m}z^{-m}K_{m}(z),

and also considering the series expansion

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{0}(\lambda n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(C+\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi}\right)\right)+\frac{\pi}{2\lambda}

\displaystyle+\pi\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda^{2}+(2\pi p)^{2}}}-\frac{1}{2\pi p}\right),

(53)

one can obtain that

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{s}(a,b)=

\displaystyle 2\pi^{\frac{s-1}{2}}\bigg\{\frac{1}{4a^{s}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)\zeta(s+1)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)\zeta(s+2)\frac{a}{b^{s+1}}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{8\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)ab\,Z_{2}(a,b;s+2)\bigg\},

(54)

where we have used the definition of the Epstein zeta function, Eq. (
9
). Now, putting all together, one can recast Eq. (
49
) as

E^{Ideal}_{C}=\frac{\hbar cL_{z}}{8\pi^{2}}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\zeta(3)\left(\frac{1}{a^{2}}+\frac{1}{b^{2}}\right)-abZ_{2}(a,b,4)\right).

(55)

By using the variable
r=a/b
one can express this energy as

E_{C}^{Ideal}=\left(\frac{\hbar cL_{z}}{a^{2}}\right){\cal E}(r),

with the adimensional function

{\cal E}(r)=\frac{1}{16\pi}\zeta(3)(1+r^{2})-\frac{r^{3}}{8\pi^{2}}Z_{2}(r,1,4).

(56)

With the Casimir energy at hands, one can find the Casimir force

F_{C}^{Ideal}=-\frac{\partial E_{C}^{Ideal}}{\partial a}.

(57)

We can write it in terms of adimensional variable
r
as

F_{C}^{Ideal}=\left(\frac{\hbar cL_{z}}{a^{3}}\right)f(r),

(58)

where

f(r)=\frac{\zeta(3)}{8\pi}-\frac{\zeta(4)}{\pi^{2}r}+\frac{r^{3}}{8\pi^{2}}Z_{2}(r,1,4)-\frac{2r^{3}}{\pi^{2}}z(r),

(59)

and we have defined the function
z(r)
given by

z(r)=\sum_{m,p=1}^{\infty}\frac{(mr)^{2}}{\left[(mr)^{2}+p^{2}\right]^{3}}.

(60)

In Fig. (

r_{c}
of the ratio between the waveguide lengths
r=a/b
. Near that point, the Casimir force is null and changes its behavior from attractive for
r<r_{c}
, to repulsive for
r>r_{c}
. The dependence of the attractive-repulsive nature of the Casimir force with the length ratio of the cavity shape is a known in the literature that we are recovering in the limit of perfect conductivity
Lukosz 1971
;
Caruso et al. 1999
.

We conclude this section, presenting the corrections to the zero-point energy due to a finite conductivity of the waveguide material. First, we define some useful variables. In this way, instead of working with the variables
\lambda_{1,2}
and
\upsilon_{1,2}
, see Eq. (
41
) and Eq. (
42
), we define the variables
p
and
\tilde{p}
given by

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^{2}+k^{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{1}\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}(p^{2}-1),

\displaystyle\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^{2}+k^{2}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{1}\frac{\xi^{2}}{c^{2}}(\tilde{p}^{2}-1),

(61)

for the other side, we define the variables
s
and
\tilde{s}
as

\displaystyle p^{2}-1+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}}=s^{2},

\displaystyle\tilde{p}^{2}-1+\frac{\epsilon_{2}}{\epsilon_{1}}=\tilde{s}^{2},

(62)

with these variables one can show that
\lambda_{1}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}\xi p/c
,
\lambda_{2}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}\xi s/c
,
\upsilon_{1}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}\xi\tilde{p}/c
, and
\upsilon_{2}=\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}}\xi\tilde{s}/c
. Hence, the reflectivity index, Eq. (
46
), can be rewritten as

\displaystyle{\cal X}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{p\epsilon_{2}+s\epsilon_{1}}{p\epsilon_{2}-s\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt{\cal X}_{2}=\left(\frac{\tilde{p}+\tilde{s}}{\tilde{p}-\tilde{s}}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle{\cal Y}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\tilde{p}\epsilon_{2}+\tilde{s}\epsilon_{1}}{\tilde{p}\epsilon_{2}-\tilde{s}\epsilon_{1}}\right)^{2},\hskip 14.22636pt{\cal Y}_{2}=\left(\frac{{p}+{s}}{{p}-{s}}\right)^{2}.

(63)

We showed in the previous section that this reflectivity indexes tend to unity in the case of vacuum waveguide with perfect conductor in the outside
{\cal X}_{1,2},{\cal Y}_{1,2}\rightarrow 1
.
For the case of imperfect conducting surface we expect a behavior of the kind that give us some finite corrections to the ideal perfect conducting case, such as

{\cal X}_{1,2}=1+\Delta{\cal X}_{1,2},\hskip 19.91684pt{\cal Y}_{1,2}=1+\Delta{\cal Y}_{1,2}.

(64)

Considering small corrections, we can expand for the imperfect conductor case the zero-point energy as

E_{ZP}=E^{Ideal}_{ZP}+\Delta E_{ZP},

(65)

where the ideal case is given by Eq. (
48
) or equivalently by Eq. (
55
). The correction due to finite conductivity of the dielectric is given by the term
\Delta E_{ZP}
. Explicitly, in this case we assume vacuum inside the waveguide with dielectric constant
\epsilon_{1}=1
, while for the outside we consider that the dielectric constant follows the plasma model and is given by
\epsilon_{2}=\epsilon(\omega)
, where

\epsilon(\omega)=1-\frac{\omega_{p}^{2}}{\omega^{2}},

(66)

and the plasma frequency
\omega_{p}
is a specific characteristic of the material. Considering this, we have that in terms of the imaginary frequency
\epsilon_{2}=1+\omega_{p}^{2}/\xi^{2}
.

Since inside the waveguide we have vacuum, then the wave number there is
\lambda_{1}\rightarrow\lambda^{(0)}
, see
Eq, (
47
), and one can define
p_{0}
such that

\lambda^{(0)}=\xi p_{0}/c
and

s=\sqrt{p_{0}^{2}+\omega_{p}^{2}/\xi^{2}}
,
by expanding
s
up to first order in
\xi/\omega_{p}
, we obtain

s\approx\frac{\omega_{p}}{\xi}+\frac{p_{0}^{2}\,\xi}{2\omega_{p}},

(67)

by using the above expansion together with Eq. (
66
) and that
\epsilon_{1}\rightarrow 1
with
p\rightarrow p_{0}
one find that the corrections to the reflectivity indexes

\Delta{\cal X}_{1}\approx\frac{4\xi}{p_{0}\,\omega_{p}},\hskip 17.07182pt\Delta{\cal Y}_{2}\approx\frac{4p_{0}\,\xi}{\omega_{p}}.

(68)

In a very similar way, we have that inside the waveguide

\upsilon_{1}\rightarrow\upsilon^{(0)}
, and with this we define the variable
\tilde{p}_{0}
such that

\upsilon^{(0)}=\xi\tilde{p}_{0}/c
and

\tilde{s}=\sqrt{\tilde{p}_{0}^{2}+\omega_{p}^{2}/\xi^{2}}
. By realizing the expansion, one obtain the first corrections to the reflectivity indexes

\Delta{\cal Y}_{1}\approx\frac{4\xi}{\tilde{p}_{0}\,\omega_{p}},\hskip 17.07182pt\Delta{\cal X}_{2}\approx\frac{4\tilde{p}_{0}\,\xi}{\omega_{p}}.

(69)

By considering this finite conductivity corrections, one can write the zero-point energy correction as given by

\Delta E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}}{8\pi^{2}}\right)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\xi\bigg\{(\Delta{\cal X}_{1}+\Delta{\cal Y}_{2})\left(1-e^{-2a\lambda^{(0)}}\right)^{-1}+(\Delta{\cal X}_{2}+\Delta{\cal Y}_{1})\left(1-e^{-2b\lambda^{0}}\right)^{-1}\bigg\}.

(70)

By exploring the symmetry of this energy correction under the permutation of the cavity length
a
and
b
, one can express this as a function of only the scale
a
and on the ratio
r=a/b
. Also, considering that the plasma wavelength of the material is
\lambda_{p}=2\pi c/\omega_{p}
, we can write

\Delta E_{ZP}=\hbar L_{z}c\lambda_{p}\left(\frac{{\cal U}(r)}{a^{3}}+\frac{{\cal U}(r^{-1})}{b^{3}}\right),

(71)

where we have defined the adimensional function

{\cal U}(r)=\frac{1}{2\pi^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\chi\,\chi\,\left(\sqrt{(n\pi r)^{2}+\chi^{2}}+\frac{\chi^{2}}{2\sqrt{(n\pi r)^{2}+\chi^{2}}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\sqrt{(n\pi r)^{2}+\chi^{2}}}}\right),

(72)

as expected, this integral need to be regularized. We use dimensional regularization in the same line as realized before for the case of ideal perfect conducting waveguide. We find that

{\cal U}(r)=-\frac{3\zeta(3)}{32\pi^{2}}+\frac{\zeta(4)}{16\pi^{3}r}+\frac{r^{3}}{2\pi^{3}}z(r),

(73)

one can express

\Delta E_{ZP}=\left(\frac{\hbar L_{z}c\lambda_{p}}{a^{3}}\right)\Delta{\cal E}(r),

(74)

where
\Delta{\cal E}(r)={\cal U}(r)+r^{3}{\cal U}(r^{-1})
.
Which allow us to write the Casimir-Lifshitz energy for the dielectric wave guide in the plasma model as

E_{C}=\left(\frac{\hbar cL_{z}}{a^{2}}\right)\left({\cal E}(r)+\frac{\lambda_{p}}{a}\Delta{\cal E}(r)\right).

(75)

V
Conclusions

In this paper we present an analysis of the Casimir-Lifshitz effect associated with the electromagnetic field confined within a rectangular waveguide consisting of two distinct dielectric materials.
We employ the surface mode technique to derive a generalized Lifshitz formula for this specific geometry. Our formulation accounts for the unique dielectric properties of the materials composing the waveguide, leading to a precise calculation of the Casimir-Lifshitz energy.
The behavior of the force on the walls of the rectangular cavity open the possibility to construct a Casimir-Lifshitz anharmonic oscillator.
A natural continuation of this work is to generalized our calculations considering a dissipative model for the dielectric dependence on the wave frequency
Barton 2001
.
