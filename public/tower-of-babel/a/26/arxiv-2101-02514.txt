Number of bounded distance equivalence classes in hulls of repetitive Delone sets
Dirk Frettlöh, Alexey Garber, Lorenzo Sadun

Abstract
. Two Delone sets are bounded distance equivalent to each other if there is a bijection between them such that the distance of corresponding points is uniformly bounded. Bounded distance equivalence is an equivalence relation. We show that the hull of a repetitive Delone set with finite local complexity has either one equivalence class or uncountably many.



1.
Introduction

Delone sets are central objects of study in the theory of aperiodic order
and give rise to dynamical systems and topological objects with interesting
properties
[
3
]
.
A
Delone set

\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a set that is both
uniformly
discrete
(that is, there is
r>0
such that each open ball of radius
r
contains
at most one point of
\Lambda
) and
relatively dense
(that is,
there is
R>0
such that each closed ball of radius
R
contains at least one point
of
\Lambda
). A
point lattice
is the
\mathbb{Z}
-span
\langle v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\rangle_{\mathbb{Z}}
of
d
linear independent vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Each point
lattice is a Delone set.
A Delone set
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
d
-periodic
if the set

P_{\Lambda}=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid t+\Lambda=\Lambda\}

of its period vectors is a point lattice. Of course for every point lattice

\Lambda
we have
\Lambda=P_{\Lambda}
, hence each point lattice
is
d
-periodic. A Delone
\Lambda
set is
nonperiodic
if

P_{\Lambda}=\{0\}
.

Two Delone sets
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are called

bounded distance equivalent
(
\Lambda\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm bd}}{{\sim}}\Lambda^{\prime}
) or
bde
, if there
is a bijection
\varphi\colon\Lambda\to\Lambda^{\prime}
such that
|x-\varphi(x)|

is uniformly bounded. It is easy to see that
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm bd}}{{\sim}}
is an equivalence relation
for Delone sets.

In the 1990s several authors studied the question of whether a given Delone
set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is bounded distant equivalent to a point lattice

[
6
,
7
,
16
]
.
In
[
7
]
it was shown that any two point lattices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the
same density are bde. It is a simple consequence that any two
d
-periodic Delone sets
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the same density are bde. This leads to considering aperiodic
Delone sets, for instance the vertices of a Penrose tiling. There are two well
studied classes of aperiodic Delone sets: cut-and-project sets and Delone
sets arising from substitution tilings. For details and a precise definition
of an aperiodic Delone set, see
[
3
]
.
Recently, the question of whether an aperiodic Delone set is bde
to a point lattice has gained some interest. For the bd-equivalence of
cut-and-project sets to a point lattice see
[
8
,
10
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
The bd-equivalence of Delone sets from substitution tilings to a point lattice
was studied in
[
1
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
23
,
24
]
. Since the bd-equivalence of
Delone sets to a point lattice is well understood, the focus now turns
to the question of when two Delone sets are bde.

This is where hulls of nonperiodic Delone sets come into play.
In the theory of aperiodic order the
hull

\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
of a
given Delone set
\Lambda
is a central object of interest.
The hull of
\Lambda
is the closure of the orbit
of
\Lambda

under translations in the topology defined by the ‘‘big box’’ metric (aka the Gromov-Hausdorff topology).

1

1

Some authors work
with a different topology, called the “local rubber” or “Chabauty-Fell” topology. When the
Delone set has finite local complexity, defined below, then the two
topologies agree.

Specifically, the distance between two
(r,R)
-Delone sets
\Lambda
and

\Lambda^{\prime}
is the infimum over all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that there
exist

, each of size
\varepsilon/2
or less, such that

\Lambda-x
and
\Lambda^{\prime}-x^{\prime}
agree exactly on
B_{1/\varepsilon}
. If no
such

d(\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime})=1
.

The hull may be studied as a topological object
[
2
,
17
,
21
]
,
or as a dynamical system
(\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda},\mathbb{R}^{d})

[
3
]
, where
\mathbb{R}^{d}

stands for the action of translations by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

This raises the question “How many bde classes
does
\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
contain?”. A partial answer was given in
[
25
]
:
If
\Lambda
comes from a substitution tiling meeting some technical conditions, then

\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
consists
of uncountably many bde classes. Here we provide a generalization that covers all
Delone sets that are repetitive and have finite local complexity (see definitions below).
This category includes the vast majority of the substitution tilings and cut-and-project sets
that have been studied to date.

Two geometric properties of the Delone set
\Lambda
are closely associated with
dynamical properties of
(\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
A
patch
in a Delone set
\Lambda
is a set
\Lambda\cap K

for some compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A Delone set
\Lambda
has

finite local complexity
(FLC)
if for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
there are only finitely many different
patches
(\Lambda-x)\cap K

(x\in\mathbb{R}^{d})
, up to translation.
A Delone set
\Lambda
is called
repetitive
if for each compact set

K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
K\cap\Lambda
is non-empty, the set

\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid(\Lambda-x)\cap K=\Lambda\cap K\}

is a Delone set. The uniform density radius
R
of this Delone set is the

repetitivity radius
of the patch
\Lambda\cap K
.
The following fact is the essence of the work of several
authors, see
[
3
,
21
]
for details.

Fact 1
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a Delone set. Then
\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
is compact if
and only if
\Lambda
has FLC.
If
\Lambda
has FLC, then the dynamical system
(\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda},\mathbb{R}^{d})

is minimal (that is, each orbit is dense) if and only if
\Lambda
is repetitive.

Now we can state our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a repetitive Delone set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
having FLC and such that the
density of
\Lambda
exists.
Let
\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
be the hull of
\Lambda
. Then

\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
either consists of a single bde class or
\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}

contains uncountably many bde classes.

In fact we show that the number of bde classes is either 1 or
2^{\aleph_{0}}
,
where
\aleph_{0}
is the cardinality of
\mathbb{Z}
. It is clear that the number of bde classes
can’t be larger than
2^{\aleph_{0}}

[
12
,
22
]
, insofar as there are only
2^{\aleph_{0}}
elements of

\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
, up to translation.

During the completion of this paper, Smilansky and Solomon released a
preprint
[
22
]
in which a stronger version of this result is proven using
the language of dynamical systems. Their theorem, which was developed completely independently
from ours, does not require FLC and
considers the orbit closure of
\Lambda
in the local rubber topology. They replace
our assumption of repetitivity with one of minimality. They also do not require
that the density of
\Lambda
exists. The cost of that generality is
complexity, in that their proof is longer and considerably more technical than ours.

An interesting consequence of both results is the following:
In
[
10
]
it was shown that a certain one-dimensional collection
of Delone sets, namely, the set of cut-and-project sets using
half of the window of the famous Fibonacci tiling
[
3
]
, has
at least two different bde classes. Theorem

1.1
then yields the following result.

Corollary 1.2
.

The hull
\mathbb{X}_{HF}
of the ’Half-Fibonacci’ cut-and-project tiling
contains uncountably many bde classes.

2.
Auxiliary results

Let us fix some more notation. In the sequel, let
\#M
denote the cardinality of
a (typically finite) set
M
. We denote the Euclidean norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

by
\|x\|

is denoted by
B_{r}(x)

The
d

is
denoted by
\mu(A)
and all sets in the paper are compact and measurable unless noted otherwise. Let
A^{+\varepsilon}
denote the
\varepsilon

of the boundary of

A^{+\varepsilon}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid d_{2}(x,\partial A)\leq\varepsilon\},

\partial A
is the topological boundary of

and

is the standard Euclidean distance in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mu(A^{+1})

is the Lebesgue measure of the set of all points whose distance
to the boundary of

Any
d
-periodic Delone set has a well defined density, in the sense
of “average number of points per unit volume”. The same holds for cut-and-project sets and for Delone sets from primitive substitutions.
In general, the definition of the density of a Delone set can be
tricky. It is easy to construct Delone sets having no well defined density
(for instance
-\mathbb{N}\cup 2\mathbb{N}
in
\mathbb{R}
). There are even repetitive
Delone sets without density, see
[
19

, or
[
9
]

for a simpler example.

In order to define the density of an arbitrary Delone sets we need
van Hove sequences. A
van Hove sequence
is a sequence

(A_{i})_{i}
of compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that for all
\varepsilon>0

(1)

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mu(A_{i}^{+\varepsilon})}{\mu(A_{i})}=0.

A Delone
\Lambda
set has density
\dens(\Lambda)
if for
all van Hove sequences
(A_{i})_{i}
the limits

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{\#(A_{i}\cap\Lambda)}{\mu(A_{i})}

exist and are identical. In that case
\dens(\Lambda)
is the value of these limits.
An important tool in this context is the following result. In the context of
this paper it translates as follows.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a Delone set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with density
\dens(\Lambda)
.

\Lambda
is bde to some lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if and only if there is

such that, for all bounded measurable sets

\big|\#(\Lambda\cap E)-\dens(\Lambda)\mu(E)\big|\leq c\mu(E^{+1})

The proof of this result relies on the infinite version of
the Hall Marriage Theorem
[
20
]
. The same arguments can be used
not only to compare a Delone set
\Lambda
with
\alpha\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
but to compare two arbitrary Delone sets
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}

as well.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\Lambda,\Lambda^{\prime}
be two Delone sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Suppose there is a van Hove sequence
(A_{i})_{i}
such that

(2)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\#(\Lambda\cap A_{i})-\#(\Lambda^{\prime}\cap A_{i})|}{\mu(A^{+1}_{i})}=\infty,

then
\Lambda\not\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm bd}}{{\sim}}\Lambda^{\prime}
.

In

, the result above was formulated using sets

. For more details

]

]

Example 2.3
.

As a test case for what follows we consider the set

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Note the difference between a patch
E\cap\Lambda
, which is a finite collection of points,
and its support
E
. Also note that we have two different ways to move
patches around: either by translating both its support and
\Lambda
to get

(E\cap\Lambda)-x=(E-x)\cap(\Lambda-x)

(E-x)\cap\Lambda

Both ways yield patches occurring in some

The strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.1
is as follows. If
\Lambda
is not bde to a

\Lambda

\Lambda

original region; we will refer to the latter sets as “normal” anyway. We will recursively define larger and larger
regions
P_{i}
around the origin and find elements of
\mathbb{X}_{\Lambda}
for which the regions
P_{i}
are either deviant
or normal. In fact, for each infinite word in

where the form of each

Any two such elements of

2.2

elements of

We construct deviant and normal patches
D_{i}^{\prime}

Let

\Lambda

Next we construct Delone sets in

The following result is standard, but for completeness we provide a sketch of the proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Lambda
be a Delone set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with density
\varrho

\#((E+x_{1})\cap\Lambda)\leq\varrho\mu(E)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\#((E+x_{2})\cap\Lambda)\geq\varrho\mu(E).

If we average
\#((E+x)\cap\Lambda)

\varrho\mu(E)

However,

\displaystyle|\#\big((E+x)\cap\Lambda\big)-\#\big(E\cap\Lambda\big)|

\displaystyle\leq\#\Big(\Big(\big((E+x)\setminus E\big)\cup\big(E\setminus(E+x)\big)\Big)\cap\Lambda\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\#\Big(\big((E+x)\setminus E\big)\cap\Lambda\Big)+\#\Big(\big(E\setminus(E+x)\big)\cap\Lambda\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(x\leq\ell)}}{{\leq}}\#\big((E+x)^{+\ell}\cap\Lambda\big)+\#\big(E^{+\ell}\cap\Lambda\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize(Lem. \ref{lem:r-R-estim})}}}{{\leq}}\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}\Big(\mu\big((E+x)^{+\ell}\big)+\mu\big(((E+x)^{+\ell})^{+r}\big)\Big)+\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}\Big(\mu\big(E^{+\ell}\big)+\mu\big((E^{+\ell})^{+r}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize(Lem. \ref{lem:r+l})}}}{{\leq}}2\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}\big(\mu(E^{+\ell})+\mu(E^{+(\ell+r)})\big)\leq 2\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}\big(\mu(E^{+\ell})+\mu(E^{+(\ell+\ell)})\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize(Lem. \ref{lem:thickboundary}})}}{{\leq}}2\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}(\ell^{d}+(\ell+\ell)^{d})\mu(E^{+1})\leq 2\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}(1+2^{d})\ell^{d}\mu(E^{+1}).

With
q=2\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}(1+2^{d})
and
\eta^{\prime}
the constant from Lemma

3.4
the claim follows.
∎

The next lemma is the key to proving Theorem

and particularly to the construction of “deviant” patches of
\Lambda

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a repetitive Delone set of FLC with

\dens(\Lambda)=\varrho
such that
\Lambda
is not bde to any
lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
c>0
and
\ell>0
be given. Then there exists
a bounded measurable set
E
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\|x\|\leq\ell
holds:

|\#\big((E-x)\cap\Lambda\big)-\varrho\mu(E)|>c\mu(E^{+1})

Proof.

Let
q
be as in Lemma
3.6
. By Theorem
2.1
there
is
E
such that

(3)

|\#(E\cap\Lambda)-\varrho\mu(E)|>(c+q\ell^{d})\mu(E^{+1}).

By Lemma
3.6
we have that
|\#(E\cap\Lambda)-\#((E-x)\cap\Lambda)|\leq q\ell^{d}\mu(E^{+1})
. Replacing

by

less than
q\ell^{d}\mu(E^{+1})
. This yields the claim.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\Lambda
be a Delone set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\dens(\Lambda)=\varrho
.
Let
(E_{i})_{i}
be a sequence of bounded measurable subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that
violate the condition in Theorem
2.1
. That is, let the sequence
(c_{i})_{i}
be such
that
\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}c_{i}=\infty
, and for each
i

(4)

|\#(E_{i}\cap\Lambda)-\varrho\mu(E_{i})|>c_{i}\mu(E^{+1}).

Then
(E_{i})_{i}
is a van Hove sequence.

Proof.

Inequality (
4
) is equivalent to

(5)

\frac{1}{c_{i}}\Big|\frac{\#(E_{i}\cap\Lambda)}{\mu(E_{i})}-\varrho\Big|>\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}.

Using Lemma
3.4
we get

\frac{\eta}{R^{d}}-\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+R})}{\mu(E_{i})}\leq\frac{\#(E_{i}\cap\Lambda)}{\mu(E_{i})}\leq\frac{\eta^{\prime}}{r^{d}}+\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+r})}{\mu(E_{i})}.

From Lemma
3.5
we get upper bounds for
\mu(E_{i}^{+r})
and
\mu(E_{i}^{+R})
in terms of
\mu(E_{i}^{+1})
(there is the trivial bound
\mu(E_{i}^{+1})
if
r\leq 1
or
R\leq 1
). Combining these estimates we get the following inequality for some positive constants
\alpha
and
\beta

\Big|\frac{\#(E_{i}\cap\Lambda)}{\mu(E_{i})}-\varrho\Big|\leq\alpha+\beta\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}.

Once we plug this in inequality (
5
), we get

\frac{1}{c_{i}}\left(\alpha+\beta\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}\right)>\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}.

Considering the limits of both sides yields
\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mu(E^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}=0
. In order to show
\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mu(E^{+\varepsilon})}{\mu(E_{i})}=0
for all
\varepsilon>0
, we consider two cases. If
\varepsilon\leq 1
, then

\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+\varepsilon})}{\mu(E_{i})}\leq\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}=0.

If
\varepsilon>1
, then we use the estimate from Lemma
3.5
to get

\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\frac{\mu(E_{i}^{+\varepsilon})}{\mu(E_{i})}\leq\lim\limits_{i\to\infty}\frac{\varepsilon^{d}\mu(E_{i}^{+1})}{\mu(E_{i})}=0.

∎

Frequently a van Hove sequence
(E_{i})_{i}
is defined by requiring another
property in addition to (
1
): namely, that the
E_{i}

exhaust the entire space. For instance, the sequence
(B_{i}(x_{i}))_{i}
,
with
x_{i}=(i,0,\cdots,0)^{T}
, fulfills (
1
), but the (closure of the) union of the balls is only
the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. The next result ensures that we do not need this
further requirement in our context.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
(E_{i})_{i}
be a van Hove sequence. Then for each
R>0
there are

i\geq 0,t_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
E_{i}
contains a ball
B_{R}(t_{i})
.

Proof.

Assume the contrary; that is, suppose there is
R>0
such that for
all
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\geq 0
holds:
B_{R}(t_{i})\not\subseteq E_{i}
. This implies
that for all
t,i
we have that the distance between
t
and
\partial E_{i}

is not greater than
R
. Consequently, for all
i
we have
E_{i}\setminus(E_{i})^{+R}=\varnothing
. Since
E_{i}\neq\varnothing
this implies
that for all
i>0
holds
\mu(E_{i})<\mu((E_{i})^{+R})
, contradicting
the van Hove property (
1
).
∎

Remark
.

In order to maintain a strict separation between our results and those of Smilansky and Solomon
[
22
]
,
we stated and proved Theorem
1.1
in the form in which we originally developed it, including the assumption
that an overall density exists. However, it is extremely easy to drop that assumption. If there is no overall density,
then there is an upper density
\varrho_{+}
and a lower density
\varrho_{-}
. Instead of looking for regions
that are rich or poor relative to “the” overall density, we can look instead for regions that are rich or poor relative to
an arbitrary intermediate density
\varrho\in(\varrho_{-},\varrho_{+})
. For instance, we can look for Euclidean balls of large radius
whose densities are greater than
(\varrho_{+}+\varrho)/2
or are less than
(\varrho_{-}+\varrho)/2
. The rest of the proof that there
exist uncountably many bde classes proceeds exactly as before.
