The class of grim reapers in ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
Antonio Bueno, Rafael López

Abstract
. We study translators of the mean curvature flow in the product space ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} . In ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} there are three types of translations: vertical translations due to the factor ℝ \mathbb{R} and parabolic and hyperbolic translations from ℍ 2 \mathbb{H}^{2} . A grim reaper in ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} is a translator invariant by a one-parameter group of translations. The variety of translators and translations in ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} makes that the family of grim reapers particularly rich. In this paper we give a full classification of the grim reapers of ℍ 2 × ℝ \mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R} with a description of their geometric properties. In some cases, we obtain explicit parametrizations of the surfaces.

1.
Introduction

In the last decades, the theory of mean curvature flow (MCF for short) in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an active and fruitful field of research, see e.g.
[
12
,
13
,
15
,
22
]
for an outline of the development of this theory. Specifically, let
\Sigma
be an orientable smooth surface and let
\psi:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an isometric immersion. Consider a variation of
\Sigma
given by a one-parameter smooth family of immersions
\psi_{t}:\Sigma\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
t\in[0,T)
,
T>0
, where
\psi_{0}=\psi
. We say that
\{\psi_{t}:t\in[0,T)\}
evolves by mean curvature flow if

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle{\frac{\partial\psi_{t}}{\partial t}}&=H(\psi_{t})N(\psi_{t}),\\
\psi_{0}&=\psi,\end{array}\right.

where
H(\psi_{t})
and
N(\psi_{t})
are the mean curvature and the unit normal of
\psi_{t}
, respectively. Of special interest are those surfaces that are self-similar solutions to the MCF in the sense that the surface moves under a combination of dilations and isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. An example of self-similar solutions that has received a great interest is when
\Sigma
evolves purely by translations of the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Specifically, fix a unit vector
\textbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. A surface
\Sigma
is said to be a translator if the MCF evolves by

\psi_{t}(p)=\psi(p)+t\textbf{v}.

Then,
H(\psi_{t})
and
N(\psi_{t})
coincide with the mean curvature
H
and the unit normal vector of
\Sigma
respectively. The evolution equation writes then as

(1)

H=\langle N,\textbf{v}\rangle.

Translators also are important because they arise in the singularity theory of MCF after a proper rescaling around type-II singularities
[
16
]
. Without aiming to collect all the bibliography, we refer the reader to
[
11
,
25
]
and references therein. Examples of translators are those one which are invariant by isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The simplest case is then the translator is invariant in a spatial direction of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In such a case and besides the plane, the translator is called a grim reaper. A grim reaper is a cylindrical surface erected over a planar curve which is a solution of the curve shortening flow, the analogy of the MCF in dimension
2
.

A space where it is of interest the study of the MCF is the hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{3}
. Motivated by a work of Huisken in arbitrary Riemannian manifolds
[
15
]
, Cabezas-Rivas and Miquel considered the MCF in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
assuming that the volume is preserved along the flow
[
8
,
9
]
. If the initial surface is convex, an interesting question is how the convexity is preserved or not along the flow and what is the limit. See also
[
2
,
3
,
10
,
17
,
14
,
27
]
. In contrast to the Euclidean case, the study of the analogs of translators of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
has been unexplored until the last year, when several authors, including the present ones, approached this problem. Following the motivation from the Euclidean space, for the definition of translator, it is necessary to consider the two types of translations of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, obtaining two types of translators
[
6
,
19
]
. Each type of translations of
\mathbb{H}^{3}
has associated a Killing vector field
X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{H}^{3})
which is placed in (
1
) instead of the vector
v
. Then a translator with respect to
X
is characterized by the equation
H=\langle N,X\rangle
. In
\mathbb{H}^{3}
, conformal vector fields can be also used in this equation. For some of these conformal vector fields, the corresponding surfaces satisfying this equation are the analogs of self-shrinkers and self-expanders of
\mathbb{R}^{3}

[
7
,
23
]
. In a more general setting, it was in
[
1
]
where self-similar solutions to the MCF were investigated under the presence of a conformal vector field. Some results in
\mathbb{H}^{3}
were achieved in
[
1
]
by viewing this space as a warped product.

The product space
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is other space where the MCF is interesting. The importance of this space is because it is one of the eight model geometries of Thurston
[
26
]
. The space
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is defined as the Riemannian product of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
with the Euclidean real line
\mathbb{R}
, endowed with the usual product metric. The product structure defines a natural projection
\pi:\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}
whose fibers are vertical geodesic.

Following with the motivation from Euclidean and hyperbolic spaces, the definition of translator of the MCF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
consists in replacing the vector
{\bf v}
of the right hand-side of Eq. (
1
) by a Killing vector field whose isometries are translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. In order to make the definitions, we will consider the upper half-plane model of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, that is, the half-plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}=\{(x,y)\colon y>0\}
endowed with the metric
\langle,\rangle=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\langle,\rangle_{e}
, where
\langle,\rangle_{e}
stands for the Euclidean metric in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. The ideal boundary
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}
is the one-point compactification of the boundary line
y=0
, and consequently, we have the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, namely,
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}\times\mathbb{R}
.

The product structure makes readily available three Killing vector fields which generate translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

The vector field
\partial_{z}
. The corresponding isometries are translations along the fibers. They are called vertical translations. From the Euclidean viewpoint, they are Euclidean translations
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x,y,z+t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

The vector field
\partial_{x}
. Then
\partial_{x}
induces parabolic translations in
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. In our model for
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, they are translations in the direction of the
x
-axis,
(x,y,z)\mapsto(x+t,y,z)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

(3)

The vector field
x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}
. The isometries of this Killing vector field are hyperbolic isometries. From the Euclidean viewpoint, they are dilations in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
,
(x,y,z)\mapsto(e^{t}x,e^{t}y,z)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Coming back to Eq. (
1
), it is natural to assume that the role of
v
can be played by each one of the above vector fields. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be an orientable immersed surface in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. Let
H
and
N
be the mean curvature and the unit normal of
\Sigma
. Then
\Sigma
is called

(1)

a v-translator if
H
satisfies

(2)

H=\langle N,\partial_{z}\rangle,

(2)

a p-translator if
H
satisfies

(3)

H=\langle N,\partial_{x}\rangle,

(3)

a h-translator if
H
satisfies

(4)

H=\langle N,x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}\rangle,

The theory of v-translators in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
was initiated by the first author in
[
4
,
5
]
obtaining the classification of rotational v-translators about a vertical geodesic. The asymptotic behavior at infinity and several uniqueness and non-existence results were also obtained. Independently, Lira and Martín
[
20
]
investigated v-translators in the general setting of Riemannian products
M^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, in particular, when
M^{2}=\mathbb{H}^{2}
. In that paper, they studied rotational v-translators and also v-translators invariant by hyperbolic and parabolic translations, but a specific geometric description of these surfaces was missing. More recently, and the same time of the present paper, Lima and Pipoli have studied v-translators in arbitrary dimension
\mathbb{H}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}

[
18
]
. They have extended the notion of v-translators to the so-called translators of the
r
-mean curvature flow, where their Killing vector field agree with our Killing vector field
\partial_{z}
. In particular, they also named parabolic and hyperbolic grim reapers to those v-translators invariant by parabolic and hyperbolic translations.

Besides the theory of v-translators, in Def.
1.1
we have introduced two new notions of translators that have not been previously considered in the literature of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. Although the vector field
\partial_{z}
is a relevant Killing vector field, the Killing vector fields
\partial_{x}
and
x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}
coming from the factor
\mathbb{H}^{2}
are equally important from the MCF viewpoint. So p-translators and h-translators are self-similar solutions of the MCF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
that open new perspectives in this field.

As a first contribution to this unexplored theory of p-translators and h-translators, we give a natural definition of grim reapers in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
that extends that of Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and is also consistent to the one considered in
[
18
,
20
]
. Recall that a grim reaper in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a translator invariant by translations along a spatial direction. The translator is associated with a Killing vector field of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which in this case, it is identified with
v
. In our situation, a grim reaper in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is defined as a translator invariant by a one-parameter group of translations of the ambient space. Consequently, we have to distinguish the one-parameter group of translations that will define these grim reapers, and also take into account the vector field that defines the translator equation. The next definition clarifies this distinction.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\delta\in\{\mathrm{v,p,h}\}
. A vertical (resp. parabolic, hyperbolic)
\delta
-grim reaper is a
\delta
-translator invariant by the one-parameter group of vertical (resp. parabolic, hyperbolic) translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Since we have three different types of translations and three different types of Killing vector fields, we have a total of nine different types of grim reapers. In this paper and for some cases, we are able to integrate the ODE fulfilled by the coordinate functions of the corresponding generating curve, obtaining explicit examples of translators in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Another vector fields of interest that can be considered in the translator equation are the conformal vector fields. This gives a new family of translators which has not been previously considered in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. So, we replace the Killing vector fields of Def.
1.1
by particular conformal vector fields. Indeed, motivated by the recent works
[
7
,
23
]
, we can also define self-similar solutions to the MCF in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
that extend the notions of self-shrinkers and self-expanders. For this, we consider the conformal vectors fields
\partial_{y}
and
-\partial_{y}
.

Definition 1.3
.

A
c_{+}
-grim reaper is a surface in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
such that its mean curvature
H
satisfies

(5)

H=\langle N,\partial_{y}\rangle.

Similarly, a
c_{-}
-grim reaper is a surface that satisfies

(6)

H=-\langle N,\partial_{y}\rangle.

These six new examples of grim reapers together with the nine grim reapers of Def.
1.2
add a total of fifteen types of grim reapers in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. We will adopt the convention that when use
translators
we mean all the three types of translators;
grim reapers
assigns all types of grim reapers;
vertical grim reapers
are the vertical
\delta
-grim reapers, and so on.

In this paper we exhibit a full classification of every type of grim reaper. A case that can be considered as trivial is when the right hand-side of the translator equation
H=\langle N,X\rangle
is zero, since the surface is minimal (
H=0
). This occurs for vertical v-grim reapers, parabolic p-grim reapers and hyperbolic h-grim reapers (Thm.
3.1
). In Table

As for the rows, they indicate the one-parameter group of translations under which the grim reaper is invariant. A direct consequence is the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

All grim reapers of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
are embedded surfaces.

2.
Preliminaries

The purpose of this section is to investigate the translator equation
H=\langle N,X\rangle
for each of the grim reapers in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, as defined in Definitions
1.2
and
1.3
. For that matter, we begin by recalling the three types of one-parameter group of translations. Then, we introduce suitable parametrizations for each grim reaper and compute their mean curvatures
H
and their unit normals
N
. With all these computations we will stand in position to study individually each of Eqns. (
2
)–(
6
).

Starting with the translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, each one belongs to any of the following one-parameter group of isometries:

(1)

Vertical translations. These are generated by the Killing vector field
\partial_{z}
. Each vertical translation is an Euclidean vertical traslation given by

\mathcal{V}_{t}:\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R},\qquad\mathcal{V}_{t}(x,y,z)=(x,y,z+t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

The flow of a point
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
under these translations is the vertical straight-line through
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
. This curve is a geodesic of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
and its curvature is
0
.
The one-parameter group is
\mathcal{V}=\{\mathcal{V}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

(2)

Parabolic translations. They fix a point of
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}
. Assuming that this point is
\infty
, they are Euclidean translations

\mathcal{P}_{t}:\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{t}(x,y,z)=(x+t,y,z),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

These are generated by the Killing vector field
\partial_{x}
. The flow of a point
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
under these translations is the horizontal straight-line through
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
and parallel to the
x
-direction, whose projection on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is a horocycle. The one-parameter group is
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{P}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

(3)

Hyperbolic translations. They fix two points
\mathbb{H}^{2}_{\infty}
. Assuming that both points are
(0,0)
and
\infty
, these translations are the Euclidean homotheties

\mathcal{H}_{t}:\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{t}(x,y,z)=(e^{t}x,e^{t}y,z),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

These are generated by the Killing vector field
x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}
. The flow of a point
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
is the horizontal straight-line through
(x_{0},y_{0},z_{0})
and
(x_{0},y_{0},0)
, whose projection on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
is an equidistant line from the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
. The one-parameter group is
\mathcal{H}=\{\mathcal{H}_{t}:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
.

Using the three types of translations of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
, we have the corresponding types of grim reapers given in Def.
1.2
.

Our next goal is to introduce suitable parametrizations
\Psi=\Psi(s,t)
of grim reapers in order to compute their mean curvatures
H
and unit normals
N
. This is necessary for computing the translator equations (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
). For that matter, we begin by considering surfaces invariant under the aforementioned translations of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
without assuming hypothesis on its mean curvature.

(1)

Vertical surfaces. The surface is a ruled surface with vertical rulings. Thus

(7)

\Psi(s,t)=(x(s),y(s),t),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

The generating curve is
\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s))
contained in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\{0\}
.

(2)

Parabolic surfaces. The surface is a ruled surface with rulings parallel to the
x
-axis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
. Thus

(8)

\Psi(s,t)=(t,y(s),z(s)),\quad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

The generating curve is
\alpha(s)=(y(s),z(s))
contained in the
yz
-plane.

(3)

Hyperbolic sufaces. The surface is a ruled surface whose rulings are horizontal half-lines starting from the
z
-axis. Hence, there exists
r=r(s)
such that
x(s)=\cos r(s)
,
y(s)=\sin r(s)
, where
r(s)\in(0,\pi)
because of the condition
y>0
. A parametrization for
\Sigma
is

(9)

\Psi(s,t)=(e^{t}\cos r(s),e^{t}\sin r(s),z(s)),\qquad s\in I\subset\mathbb{R},\ t\in\mathbb{R}.

We calculate the mean curvature
H
and the unit normal
N
of each of the above three types of surfaces. We will employ local coordinates
(x,y,z)
for
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
. A global orthonormal frame of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is
\{E_{1},E_{2},E_{3}\}
where

E_{1}=y\partial_{x},\quad E_{2}=y\partial_{y},\quad E_{3}=\partial_{z}.

The Levi-Civita connection
\overline{\nabla}
of
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is given by

\overline{\nabla}_{E_{1}}E_{1}=E_{2},\quad\overline{\nabla}_{E_{1}}E_{2}=-E_{1}

and
\overline{\nabla}_{E_{i}}E_{j}=0
in the rest of the cases. The mean curvature
H
of a surface
\Sigma
parametrized by
\Psi=\Psi(s,t)
is given by the formula

H=\frac{b_{11}g_{22}-2b_{12}g_{12}+b_{22}g_{11}}{2(g_{11}g_{22}-g_{12}^{2})},

where

g_{11}=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{s}\rangle,\quad g_{12}=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{t}\rangle\quad g_{22}=\langle\Psi_{t},\Psi_{t}\rangle,

and

b_{11}=\langle N,\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{s}\rangle,\quad b_{12}=\langle N,\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{t}\rangle,\quad b_{22}=\langle N,\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{t}\rangle.

Now we find
H
and
N
of the above surfaces.

(1)

Vertical surfaces. Consider a surface
\Sigma
parametrized by (
7
). Then
\Sigma
is a vertical cylinder erected over the curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s))
. A direct argument show that the mean curvature of
\Sigma
is
H=\kappa_{g}/2
, where
\kappa_{g}
is the geodesic curvature of the curve
\alpha
. This is because the vertical rulings are geodesics of
\Sigma
. However, we compute
H
by completeness. We parametrize
\alpha
by the hyperbolic arc-length

(10)

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}(s)&=y(s)\cos\theta(s)\\
y^{\prime}(s)&=y(s)\sin\theta(s),\end{split}\right.

for some smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
. Then

\Psi_{s}=\cos\theta E_{1}+\sin\theta E_{2},\quad\Psi_{t}=E_{3},

and

(11)

N=\sin\theta E_{1}-\cos\theta E_{2}.

Then
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}
. Moreover,

\begin{split}\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{s}&=-\sin\theta(\theta^{\prime}+\cos\theta)E_{1}+\cos\theta(\theta^{\prime}+\cos\theta)E_{2},\\
\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{t}&=\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{t}=0.\end{split}

Hence,
b_{11}=-\theta^{\prime}-\cos\theta
and
b_{12}=b_{22}=0
. Thus

(12)

H=-\frac{\theta^{\prime}+\cos\theta}{2}.

(2)

Parabolic surfaces. The surface is parametrized by (
8
). Consider the curve
\alpha(s)=(y(s),z(s))
parametrized by

(13)

\left\{\begin{split}y^{\prime}(s)&=y(s)\cos\theta(s)\\
z^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\theta(s),\end{split}\right.

for some smooth function
\theta=\theta(s)
. Then

\Psi_{s}=\cos\theta E_{2}+\sin\theta E_{3},\quad\Psi_{t}=\frac{1}{y}E_{1}

and

(14)

N=\sin\theta E_{2}-\cos\theta E_{3}.

Thus
g_{11}=1
,
g_{12}=0
and
g_{22}=1/y^{2}
. Moreover,

\begin{split}\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{s}&=\theta^{\prime}(-\sin\theta E_{2}+\cos\theta E_{3}),\\
\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{t}&=-\cos\theta E_{1},\\
\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{t}&=\frac{1}{y^{2}}E_{2}.\end{split}

Hence,
b_{11}=-\theta^{\prime}
,
b_{12}=0
and
b_{22}=\sin\theta/y^{2}
. Thus

(15)

H=-\frac{\theta^{\prime}-\sin\theta}{2}.

(3)

Hyperbolic surfaces. Suppose that the surface is parametrized by (
9
). Consider the curve
\alpha(s)=(\cos r(s),\sin r(s),z(s))
parametrized by arc-length, that is,

(16)

\left\{\begin{split}r^{\prime}(s)&=\sin r(s)\cos\rho(s)\\
z^{\prime}(s)&=\sin\rho(s),\end{split}\right.

for some function
\rho=\rho(s)
. Then

\begin{split}\Psi_{s}&=-\sin r\cos\rho E_{1}+\cos r\cos\rho E_{2}+\sin\rho E_{3},\\
\Psi_{t}&=\cot rE_{1},\end{split}

and

(17)

N=-\sin r\sin\rho E_{1}+\cos r\sin\rho E_{2}-\cos\rho E_{3}.

Thus
g_{11}=1
,
g_{12}=0
and
g_{22}=1/(\sin r)^{2}
. Moreover,

\begin{split}\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{s}=&(\rho^{\prime}\sin r\sin\rho+\sin r\cos r(\cos\rho)^{2}-r^{\prime}\cos r\cos\rho)E_{1}\\
&((\sin r\cos\rho)^{2}-r^{\prime}\sin r\cos\rho-\rho^{\prime}\cos r\sin\rho)E_{2}+\rho^{\prime}\cos\rho E_{3}\\
\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{s}}\Psi_{t}=&(\sin r\cos\rho-r^{\prime}(\csc r)^{2})E_{1}-\cos r\cos\rho E_{2},\\
\overline{\nabla}_{\Psi_{t}}\Psi_{t}=&-\cot rE_{1}+(\cot r)^{2}E_{2}.\end{split}

Hence,

\begin{split}b_{11}&=\rho^{\prime},\\
b_{12}&=\sin\rho\left(r^{\prime}\csc r-\cos\rho\right),\\
b_{22}&=\cot r\csc r\sin\rho.\end{split}

Thus

(18)

H=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho^{\prime}-\cos r\sin\rho\right).

3.
Minimal surfaces and rotational translators

An immediate consequence of the computations of the previous section is the following partial classification which states that three of the fifteen types of grim reapers are trivial because they are minimal surfaces.

Theorem 3.1
.

Any vertical v-grim reaper, parabolic p-grim reaper and hyperbolic h-grim reaper is a minimal surface.

Proof.

It is immediate because in all cases, the normal vector of the surface is orthogonal to the Killing vector field.

Although this paper is focused on grim reapers, it is natural, once two new notions of translators have been defined in

Slices

\Sigma

\Psi(s,t)=\left(-\frac{2\left(y(s)^{2}-1\right)\sin t}{2\left(y(s)^{2}+1-\left(y(s)^{2}-1\right)\cos t\right)},\frac{2y(s)}{y(s)^{2}+1-\left(y(s)^{2}-1\right)\cos t},z(s)\right).

4.2.
Hyperbolic v-grim reapers

We now study hyperbolic v-grim reapers. In contrast to the parabolic v-grim reapers, we cannot give explicit parametrizations of the surfaces. To describe the geometric properties of these surfaces, we will study the phase plane associated to the generating curves. Part of this section coincides with
[
18
]
in their study of the
1
-mean curvature flow (see also
[
20

).

A first example of a hyperbolic v-grim reaper is the vertical plane of equation
x=0
. Indeed, this surface is invariant by the group
\mathcal{H}
and because its generating curve is the geodesic
x=z=0

is horizontal, then

2

For the general classification of the hyperbolic v-grim reapers, we know from Sect.
2
that the generating curve of a hyperbolic v-grim reaper is

\rho^{\prime}=2\cos\rho+\cos r\sin\rho.

We study the properties of this ODE system by projecting the solutions into the autonomous second order system

(24)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}r^{\prime}=\sin r\cos\rho\\
\rho^{\prime}=2\cos\rho+\sin\rho\cos r,\end{array}\right.\qquad r\in(0,\pi),\ \rho\in(-\pi,\pi).

The phase plane of (

\Theta=\{(r,\rho)\colon r\in(0,\pi),\ \rho\in(-\pi,\pi)\},

and

The orbits are the solutions

24

In this section we classify h-grim reapers. Recall that the case when the surface is invariant by hyperbolic translations, then the surface is minimal (Theorem
3.1
). In the following result, we approach the case when the h-grim reaper is vertical.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a vertical h-grim reaper parametrized by (
7
), where the generating curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),y(s))
satisfies (
10

\theta
satisfies

(26)

\theta^{\prime}=\cos\theta-\frac{2x\sin\theta}{y}.

Proof.

The fact that
\theta
is a solution to the aforementioned ODE is immediate from (
11
), (
12

\langle N,x\partial x+y\partial_{y}\rangle=x\sin\theta/y
. By (
4
), the function
\theta
satisfies (
26

16
), the curve
\alpha
satisfies

\left\{\begin{split}x^{\prime}&=y\cos\theta,\\
y^{\prime}&=y\sin\theta,\\
\theta^{\prime}&=\cos\theta-\frac{2x\sin\theta}{y}.\end{split}\right.

From this system we see that the generating curve
\alpha
is symmetric with respect to the line
x=z=0
.

At this point, we cannot project a solution
(x,y,\theta)
to an autonomous
2D
-system because of the dependence of both
x,y
in the expression of
\theta^{\prime}
. If we express
\alpha(s)
as a graph
(x,y(x))
, the mean curvature and the product
\langle N,x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}\rangle
are

H=-\frac{1+y^{\prime 2}+yy^{\prime\prime}}{2(1+y^{\prime 2})^{3/2}},\qquad\langle N,x\partial_{x}+y\partial_{y}\rangle=\frac{y^{\prime}}{y\sqrt{1+y^{\prime 2}}},

hence
y(x)
is a solution of

(27)

y^{\prime\prime}=\frac{(y-2xy^{\prime})(1+y^{\prime 2})}{y^{2}}.

By the even condition on
\alpha
, it suffices to consider
x\geq 0
. Let us assume initial conditions
y(0)=y_{0}>0
,
y^{\prime}(0)=0
. Then,
y^{\prime\prime}(0)=1/y_{0}>0
and
y(x)
attains at
x=0
a minimum. Moreover, this is its unique critical point since
y^{\prime}(x_{0})=0
implies
y^{\prime\prime}(x_{0})=1/y(x_{0})>0
and hence would be another minimum, making impossible the existence of a maximum or an inflection point. In particular, the right-hand side of (
27
) is bounded and hence
y(x)
is defined for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now, for
x>0
increasing the function
y(x)
also increases. Thus
y^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x>0
since
y^{\prime}(x)
never vanishes again. We claim that
y^{\prime\prime}(x)
vanishes at some point and hence
y(x)
changes its curvature. Arguing by contradiction, assume that
y^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
for every
x>0
. By (
27
) this would imply

y(x)>2xy^{\prime}(x),\qquad\forall x>0.

Now fix some
x_{0}>0
and an integration from
x_{0}
to
x>x_{0}
yields

\log x-\log x_{0}>2\log\frac{y(x)}{y(x_{0})}.

At this point, letting
x_{0}\rightarrow 0
, we conclude that the left-hand side of this equation is eventually negative, a contradiction since its right-hand side is always positive. Consequently,
y(x)
is strictly convex around
x=0
and then changes its curvature at some
x_{0}>0
. See Fig.
7
