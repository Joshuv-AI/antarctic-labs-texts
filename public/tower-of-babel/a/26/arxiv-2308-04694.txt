The steady Euler-Poisson system and accelerating flows with transonic C 1 C^{1} -transitions
Myoungjean Bae, Ben Duan, Chunjing Xie

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove the existence of two-dimensional solutions to the steady Euler-Poisson system with continuous transonic transitions across sonic interfaces of codimension 1. First, we establish the well-posedness of a boundary value problem for a linear second order system that consists of an elliptic-hyperbolic mixed type equation with a degeneracy occurring on an interface of codimension 1, and an elliptic equation weakly coupled together. Then we apply the Schauder fixed point theorem to prove the existence of two-dimensional solutions to the potential flow model of the steady Euler-Poisson system with continuous transonic transitions across sonic interfaces. With the aid of Helmholtz decomposition, established in [ 6 ] , we extend the existence result to the full Euler-Poisson system for the case of nonzero vorticity. Most importantly, the solutions constructed in this paper are classical solutions to Euler-Poisson system, thus their sonic interfaces are not weak discontinuities in the sense that all the flow variables are C 1 C^{1} across the interfaces.

1.1.
Introduction

The Euler-Poisson system

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\rho_{t}+{\rm div}_{{\rm{x}}}(\rho\mathbf{u})=0,\\
&(\rho\mathbf{u})_{t}+{\rm div}_{{\rm{x}}}(\rho\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{u})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}p=\rho\nabla_{{\rm{x}}}\Phi,\\
&(\rho\mathscr{E})_{t}+{\rm div}_{{\rm{x}}}(\rho\mathscr{E}\mathbf{u}+p\mathbf{u})=\rho\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\Phi,\\
&\Delta_{{\rm{x}}}\Phi=\rho-\bar{\rho}_{I},\end{aligned}\right.

describes the motion of plasma or electrons in semiconductor devices
where
\rho
,
{\bf u}
,
p
and
\mathscr{E}
represent the density, velocity, pressure and the energy density of electrons, respectively. In particular, this system describes an electron fluid dynamics in a background of ions with a constant density and a constant charge (
[
20
]
and
[
30
]
). The Poisson equation
\text{\eqref{UnsteadyEP}}_{4}
describes how the two fluids of electrons and ions interact through the electric field
\nabla\Phi
. In this paper, we consider ideal polytropic gas, where
p
and
\mathscr{E}
are given by

(1.2)

p=S\rho^{\gamma}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{E}=\frac{|\mathbf{u}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{p}{(\gamma-1)\rho},

respectively, for a function
S>0
and a constant
\gamma>1
.
The function
\ln S
represents
the physical entropy
, and the constant
\gamma>1
is called the
adiabatic exponent
. For the Mach number
M
, defined by

M:=\frac{|{\bf u}|}{\sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}},

if
M<1
, then the flow corresponding to
(\rho,{\bf u},p,\Phi)
is said to be subsonic. On the other hand, if
M>1
, then the flow is said to be supersonic. Finally, if
M=1
, then the flow is said to be sonic.

The goal of this work is to prove the existence of a steady classical solution to the system (
1.1
) that yields a flow accelerating from a subsonic state to a supersonic state in a two-dimensional flat nozzle of a finite length. The main feature of the solution constructed in this paper is that the sonic interface on which the subsonic-supersonic transition occurs has codimension 1. Most importantly, the sonic interface is not treated as a free boundary as opposed to the case of transonic shocks (cf.
[
8
,
10
,
14
,
28
,
40
,
41
,
42
]
and references therein).

The system (
1.1
) consists of two parts, a compressible Euler system with source terms and a Poisson equation, weakly coupled in a nonlinear way. One of longstanding open problems related to the compressible Euler system is to prove the existence of a solution that consists of two types of transonic flows, an accelerating transonic flow with a sonic interface and a transonic shock in a convergent-divergent nozzle, called a
de Laval nozzle
. For the last several decades, there have been extensive studies on transonic shocks in a nozzle, see
[
8
,
15
,
26
,
27
,
41
,
42
]
. On the other hands, there are very few known results on continuous transonic flows with sonic interfaces as it involves an analysis of degenerate PDEs. In the pioneering works
[
31
,
32
,
33
,
34
]
by Morawetz, it is shown that two-dimensional smooth transonic potential flows around an airfoil are unstable in general. The smooth transonic solutions for transonic small disturbance equations was analyzed in
[
23
]
. Recently, the transonic solutions for the Euler system with sonic interfaces are studied, see
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
39
]
, etc. Another examples of weak solutions to the unsteady Euler system with sonic interfaces, which appear as circular arcs, are given in
[

,

]
. In
[

, it is shown that the sonic interfaces studied in

]
are weak discontinuities, in the sense that the velocity potentials across the sonic arcs are
C^{1,1}
but not
C^{2}
. This means that the flow velocity fields are continuous but their derivatives are discontinuous across the sonic arcs. For more studies on regular shock reflection for other models, one may refer to
[
21
,
22
,
43
]
and references therein.

In this paper, we prove the existence of two dimensional solutions to the steady Euler-Poisson system with
continuous transonic transitions
across
sonic interfaces
of codimension 1. More importantly, we show that the solutions are classical solutions, thus their sonic interfaces are not weak discontinuities. Differently from the Euler system, the steady Euler-Poisson system has one-dimensional continuous transonic solutions, and this can be found by a phase plane analysis
[

,

[

As we seek for a two-dimensional steady solution to (
1.1
), let us set the velocity vector field
{\bf u}
as
{\bf u}({\rm{x}}):=u_{1}({\rm{x}}){\bf e}_{1}+u_{2}({\rm{x}}){\bf e}_{2}
for
{{\rm{x}}}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Here,
{\bf e}_{j}(j=1,2)
represents the unit vector in the positive
x_{j}
-direction. Next, we define the vorticity function
\omega
by

\omega({\rm{x}}):=\partial_{x_{1}}u_{2}-\partial_{x_{2}}u_{1}.

If the solution satisfies
\rho>0
and
u_{1}>0
, then
(\rho,{\bf u},S,\Phi)
solves the following nonlinear system:

(1.3)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\nabla\cdot(\rho\mathbf{u})=0\\
&\omega=\frac{1}{u_{1}}\left(\frac{\rho^{\gamma-1}\partial_{x_{2}}S}{(\gamma-1)}-\partial_{x_{2}}(\mathscr{B}-\Phi)\right)\\
&{\bf m}\cdot\nabla S=0\\
&{\bf m}\cdot\nabla(\mathscr{B}-\Phi)=0\\
&\Delta\Phi=\rho-\bar{\rho}_{I}\end{aligned}\right.

where
{\bf m}
and
\mathscr{B}
are given by

{\bf m}:=\rho{\bf u},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathscr{B}:=\frac{|{\bf u}|^{2}}{2}+\frac{\gamma S\rho^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma-1}.

For one-dimensional solutions to (
1.3
), we solve the ODE system

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\bar{\rho}\bar{u}_{1}=J\\
&\bar{S}=S_{0}\\
&\frac{\bar{u}_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{\gamma\bar{S}\bar{\rho}^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma-1}-\bar{\Phi}=\kappa_{0}\\
&\bar{\Phi}^{\prime\prime}=\bar{\rho}-\bar{\rho}_{I}\end{aligned}\right.

for some constants
J>0
,
S_{0}>0
and
\kappa_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
.
By setting as
\bar{E}:=\bar{\Phi}^{\prime}
, one can rewrite the above system as

\bar{\rho}=\frac{J}{\bar{u}_{1}},\quad\bar{S}=S_{0},\quad\begin{cases}\bar{u}_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{\bar{E}\bar{u}_{1}^{\gamma}}{\bar{u}_{1}^{\gamma+1}-\gamma S_{0}J^{\gamma-1}},\\
\bar{E}^{\prime}=\frac{J}{\bar{u}_{1}}-\bar{\rho}_{I}.\end{cases}

Hereafter, the constants
\gamma>1
,
J>0
and
S_{0}>0
are fixed.
Let us define two constants
\bar{u}_{I}
and
u_{s}
by

\bar{u}_{I}:=\frac{J}{\bar{\rho}_{I}},\quad\text{and}\quad u_{s}:=\left(\gamma S_{0}J^{\gamma-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma+1}}.

For later use, we shall define a parameter
\zeta_{0}
by

(1.4)

\zeta_{0}:=\frac{\bar{u}_{I}}{u_{s}}.

And, assume that

(1.5)

\zeta_{0}>1.

Under this assumption, suppose that
(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})
is a
C^{1}
-solution to the system

(1.6)

\begin{cases}\bar{u}_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{\bar{E}\bar{u}_{1}^{\gamma}}{\bar{u}_{1}^{\gamma+1}-u_{s}^{\gamma+1}},\\
\bar{E}^{\prime}=\frac{J}{\bar{u}_{1}}-\bar{\rho}_{I}.\end{cases}

In addition, suppose that

(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})(l_{\rm s})=(u_{s},0)

for some constant
l_{\rm s}>0
. Next, we define a function
H:(0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

(1.7)

H(u)=\int_{u_{s}}^{u}\frac{J}{\bar{u}_{I}t^{\gamma+1}}(t^{\gamma+1}-u_{s}^{\gamma+1})\left(\bar{u}_{I}-t\right)dt.

Also, define a two-variable function
\mathfrak{H}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

\mathfrak{H}(u,E):=\frac{1}{2}E^{2}-H(u).

Straightforward computations show that the solution
(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})
satisfies

(1.8)

\mathfrak{H}(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})\equiv 0

as long as it exists.

Define

\mathcal{T}:=\{(u,E):\mathfrak{H}(u,E)=0\}.

The set
\mathcal{T}
is called the
critical trajectory
of the system (
1.6
)(see Figure
1
).
We define a subset
\mathcal{T}_{\rm{acc}}
by

(1.9)

\mathcal{T}_{\rm{acc}}=\{(u,E)\in\mathcal{T}:(u-u_{s})E\geq 0\}.

Lemma 1.1
.

Given constants
\gamma>1
,
J>0
and
S_{0}>0
, assume that the condition (
1.5
) holds.
Then, the set
\mathcal{T}_{\rm{acc}}
represents the trajectory of an accelerating smooth transonic solution to (
1.6
) in the following sense: for any fixed
(u_{0},E_{0})\in\mathcal{T}_{\rm{acc}}
with
u_{0}<u_{s}
, there exists a finite constant
l_{\rm max}>0
depending only on
(\gamma,J,S_{0},\bar{u}_{I},u_{0})
so that the initial value problem

(1.10)

\begin{split}&\begin{cases}\bar{u}_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{\bar{E}\bar{u}_{1}^{\gamma}}{\bar{u}_{1}^{\gamma+1}-u_{s}^{\gamma+1}}\\
\bar{E}^{\prime}=\frac{J}{\bar{u}_{1}}-\bar{\rho}_{I}\end{cases}\text{for $x_{1}>0$},\\
&(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})(0)=(u_{0},E_{0})\end{split}

has a unique smooth solution for
x_{1}\in[0,l_{\rm max})
with satisfying the following properties:

(i)

\displaystyle{\bar{u}_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})>0\,\,\text{on $[0,l_{\rm max})$}}
.

(ii)

\displaystyle{\lim_{x_{1}\to l_{\rm max}-}\bar{u}_{1}^{\prime}(x_{1})=0}
.

(iii)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}_{\rm acc}\cap\{(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})(x_{1}):0\leq x_{1}\leq l_{\rm max}\}=\mathcal{T}_{\rm acc}\cap\{(u,E):u\geq u_{0}\}}
.

(iv)

There exists a unique constant
l_{\rm s}\in(0,l_{\rm max})
depending only on
(\gamma,J,S_{0},\bar{u}_{I},u_{0})
so that
\bar{u}_{1}
satisfies

(1.11)

\bar{u}_{1}(x_{1})\begin{cases}<u_{s}\quad&\mbox{for $x_{1}<l_{\rm s}$ {\emph{(subsonic)}}},\\
=u_{s}\quad&\mbox{at $x_{1}=l_{\rm s}$ {\emph{(sonic)}}},\\
>u_{s}\quad&\mbox{for $x_{1}>l_{\rm s}$ {\emph{(supersonic)}}}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Define a function
F(t)
by

(1.12)

F(t):=\frac{t^{\gamma}\sqrt{2H(t)}}{|t^{\gamma+1}-u_{s}^{\gamma+1}|}\quad\text{for $t\neq u_{s}$}

where
H(u)
is given by (
1.7
).

Clearly,
F(t)
is smooth for
t\in(0,\infty)\setminus\{u_{s}\}
as long as
H(t)\geq 0
.

Note that
H(u_{s})=H^{\prime}(u_{s})=0
. It follows from the assumption (
1.5
) that one has

H^{\prime\prime}(u_{s})=(\gamma+1)J\left(\frac{1}{u_{s}}-\frac{1}{\bar{u}_{I}}\right)>0.

By applying L’Hôpital’s rule, it is obtained

\lim_{t\to u_{s}}\frac{2H(t)}{(t^{\gamma+1}-u_{s}^{\gamma+1})^{2}}=\lim_{t\to u_{s}}\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(t)}{(\gamma+1)^{2}t^{2\gamma}}=\frac{J}{(\gamma+1)u_{s}^{2\gamma}}\left(\frac{1}{u_{s}}-\frac{1}{\bar{u}_{I}}\right)>0

so one can extend the definition of
F(t)
up to
t=u_{s}
as

F(u_{s}):=\sqrt{\frac{J}{\gamma+1}\left(\frac{1}{u_{s}}-\frac{1}{\bar{u}_{I}}\right)}.

Therefore,
F(t)
is continuous for all
t>0
as long as
H(t)\geq 0
holds.

By Taylor’s theorem, there exist two smooth functions
P(t)
and
Q(t)
satisfying

\begin{split}&H(t)=\frac{1}{2}H^{\prime\prime}(u_{s})(t-u_{s})^{2}+(t-u_{s})^{3}P(t),\\
&t^{\gamma+1}-u_{s}^{\gamma+1}=(\gamma+1)u_{s}^{\gamma}(t-u_{s})+(t-u_{s})^{2}Q(t),\end{split}

so that the function
F(t)
is written as

F(t)=\frac{t^{\gamma}\sqrt{H^{\prime\prime}(u_{s})+2(t-u_{s})P(t)}}{(\gamma+1)u_{s}^{\gamma}+(t-u_{s})Q(t)}.

This shows that
F(t)
is smooth and positive for all
t>0
as long as the inequality
H(t)\geq 0
holds.

By the definition (
1.9
), one has

\mathcal{T}_{\rm acc}=\{E={\rm sgn}(u-u_{s})\sqrt{H(u)}:H(u)\geq 0\}.

If
(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{E})(x_{1})
is a
C^{1}
-solution to (
1.10
), then it also solves the initial value problem:

(1.13)

\begin{cases}\bar{u}_{1}^{\prime}=F(\bar{u}_{1})\\
\bar{u}_{1}(0)=u_{0}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{E}={\rm sgn}(\bar{u}_{1}-u_{s})\sqrt{2H(\bar{u}_{1})}.

One can directly check from (
1.7
) that there exists a constant
u_{\rm max}\in(\bar{u}_{I},\infty)
satisfying that

H(t)\begin{cases}>0\quad&\mbox{for $0<t<u_{\rm max}$},\\
=0\quad&\mbox{at $t=u_{\rm max}$},\\
<0\quad&\mbox{for $t>u_{\rm max}$}.\end{cases}

Hence it follows from the unique existence theorem of ODEs and the method of continuation that there exists a finite constant
l_{\rm max}>0
so that the initial value problem (
1.13
) has a unique smooth solution for
0\leq x_{1}\leq l_{\rm max}
with
(\bar{u},\bar{E})(l_{\rm max})=(u_{\rm max},0)
. Note that the inequality

0<u_{0}<u_{s}<\bar{u}_{I}<u_{\rm max}

holds. Since
F(t)>0
for
0<t\leq u_{\rm max}
, the velocity function
\bar{u}_{1}
strictly increases with respect to
x_{1}\in[0,l_{\rm max})
. Therefore, there exists a unique constant
l_{\rm s}\in(0,l_{\rm max})
that satisfies (
1.11
).

The main difficulty of this work is to establish the well-posedness of the boundary value problem (
2.21
) for

(\psi,\Psi)
. According to the statement (g) of Lemma
2.8
, the system

\begin{cases}\mathfrak{L}_{1}^{P}(\psi,\Psi)=f_{1}^{P}\\
\mathfrak{L}_{2}(\psi,\Psi)=f_{2}^{(\tilde{T},P)}\end{cases}

consists of a second order mixed-type equation with a degeneracy and a second order elliptic equation, which are weakly coupled together by lower order derivative terms. Note that the type of the equation
\mathfrak{L}_{1}^{P}(\psi,\Psi)=f_{1}^{P}
changes from being elliptic to being hyperbolic across an interface of codimension 1. To our best knowledge, there is no general theory that guarantees the well-posedness of a boundary value problem of this type.
Our strategy to solve the boundary value problem (
2.21
) is as follows:

(1)

First, we shall apply the method developed in
[
23
, Chapter 1]
to handle the degeneracy of the operator
\mathfrak{L}_{1}^{P}
occurring on
x_{1}=g_{\rm s}^{P}(x_{2})
. Namely, we set up a new boundary value problem with a singular perturbation by introducing an auxiliary differential operator
\mathfrak{L}_{1}^{P}(v,w)+\varepsilon\partial_{111}v
for a constant
\varepsilon>0
. The crucial step is to establish a priori
H^{1}
estimate of weak solutions to an auxiliary boundary value problem that includes

\mathfrak{L}_{1}^{P}(v,w)+\varepsilon\partial_{111}v=f_{1}.

\begin{split}\mathscr{D}_{1}&=D^{4}\vartheta,\\
\mathscr{D}_{2}&=-6\partial_{x_{2}}^{3}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L})(D\mathscr{L})^{2}D^{2}\mathscr{L},\\
\mathscr{D}_{3}&=-3\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L})(D^{2}\mathscr{L})^{2},\\
\mathscr{D}_{4}&=-4\partial_{x_{2}}^{2}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L})D\mathscr{L}D^{3}\mathscr{L},\\
\mathscr{D}_{5}&=-\partial_{x_{2}}^{4}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L})(D\mathscr{L})^{4}.\end{split}

By using (
6.25
) and applying the Sobolev inequality, we can directly estimate the terms
\mathscr{D}_{j}
for
j=1,2,3,4
as follows:

\begin{split}&\|\mathscr{D}_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\|{\bf m}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega_{L})},\\
&\|\mathscr{D}_{j}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\|\vartheta\|_{C^{3}(\overline{\Omega_{L}\cap\{x_{1}<\frac{l_{\rm s}}{3}\}})}\|\mathscr{L}\|^{3}_{H^{3}(\Omega_{L})}\quad\text{for $j=2,3,4$.}\end{split}

And, applying the generalized Sobolev inequality gives

\|\mathscr{D}_{5}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}\leq C\|\mathscr{L}\|_{H^{3}(\Omega_{L})}^{3}\|\partial_{x_{2}}^{4}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}.

For each
t\in(0,L)
, the function
\mathscr{L}(t,\cdot):[-1,1]\rightarrow[-1,1]
is
C^{1}
-diffeomorphic. By (
6.24
) and (
6.25
), the function
\partial_{x_{2}}^{4}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L}(t,\cdot))
belongs to
L^{2}((-1,1))
due to the trace theorem. Then we apply the Fubini’s theorem and use (
6.24
)–(
6.26
) to get

\begin{split}\|\partial_{x_{2}}^{4}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{L})}^{2}&=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{\Omega_{L}\cap\{x_{1}=t\}}|\partial_{x_{2}}^{4}\vartheta(0,\mathscr{L}(x_{1},x_{2}))|^{2}\,dx_{2}dt\\
&=\int_{0}^{L}\int_{\Omega_{L}\cap\{x_{1}=t\}}\frac{|\partial_{x_{2}}^{4}\vartheta(0,y)|^{2}}{\partial_{x_{2}}\mathscr{L}(t,\mathscr{L}^{-1}(t,y))}\,dy\,dt\\
&\leq C\end{split}

where
\mathscr{L}^{-1}
represents the inverse of
\mathscr{L}(t,\cdot)
as a mapping of
x_{2}\in[-1,1]
. Then, the estimate (
6.20
) easily follows.

∎

Appendix A
A further discussion on the nozzle length
L

Returning to the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we give examples of
(\gamma,J)
for which the nozzle length
L
can be large while the condition (
1.20
) holds.

By (
3.30
) and (
3.31
), one can express the nozzle length
L
as

(A.1)

L:=\sqrt{\frac{h_{0}^{3}}{2}}J^{\frac{\gamma-2}{\gamma+1}}\sqrt{\lambda(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{L})}.

Define

\mathfrak{p}:=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}h_{0}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\mathcal{H}(\kappa)((\gamma-1)\kappa^{\gamma+1}+\eta(\kappa^{\gamma+1}-1)+2)\kappa^{-\eta}h_{0}^{-\eta},

and

\hat{\alpha}:=\frac{\alpha}{J^{\frac{2-\gamma+2\eta}{\gamma+1}}\mathfrak{p}}.

for
\alpha
given by (
3.20
).

Example 1.
Back to Step 4 in the proof of Lemma
3.3
,
where the parameter
\eta
in (
3.12
) is fixed as
\eta=\frac{3}{4}\gamma
, we take a closer look on how the parameter
\bar{J}
is fixed.

By (
3.33
) and (
3.34
), we can roughly express
\hat{\alpha}
as

\hat{\alpha}=1-\mu_{1}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}-\mu_{2}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)\lambda(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{L})J^{-\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}\left(J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}+\mu_{3}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)\right)^{2}.

Next, we express
\lambda(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{L})
as

(A.2)

\lambda(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{L})=\varepsilon_{0}J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}.

Then we have

\hat{\alpha}=1-\mu_{1}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}-\mu_{2}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)\varepsilon_{0}\left(J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}+\mu_{3}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)\right)^{2}.

Assume that

|\kappa-1|\leq q_{0}

for some constant
q_{0}>0
. Then one can fix a constant
\bar{\mu}>0
depending only on
(\gamma,S_{0},\zeta_{0},q_{0})
to satisfy

\max_{{j=1,2,3,}\atop{|\kappa-1|\leq q_{0}}}|\mu_{j}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)|\leq\bar{\mu}

so we have

\hat{\alpha}\geq 1-\bar{\mu}J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}-\bar{\mu}\varepsilon_{0}\left(J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}+\bar{\mu}\right)^{2}.

Now we fix a constant
\bar{J}>0
sufficiently small depending only on
(\gamma,S_{0},\zeta_{0},q_{0})
so that, whenever the background momentum density
J
satisfies
0<J\leq\bar{J}
, it holds that

1-\bar{\mu}J^{\frac{\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}\geq\frac{2}{3}.

Then, we fix a constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
sufficiently small depending only on
(\gamma,S_{0},\zeta_{0},q_{0})
so that if
J\in(0,\bar{J}]
, then we finally obtain that

(A.3)

\hat{\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{3},

and this yields the result comparable to (
3.38
).

Substituting (
A.2
) into (
A.1
) yields

L=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}h_{0}^{3}}{2}}J^{\frac{5\gamma-8}{4(\gamma+1)}}.

This expression is valid for any
\gamma>1
as long as the inequality
0<J\leq\bar{J}
holds. Suppose that
1<\gamma<\frac{8}{5}
. Note that the constant
\varepsilon_{0}h_{0}^{3}
is fixed independent of
J\in(0,\bar{J}]
. Therefore, the value of
L
becomes large if
J
is fixed sufficiently small.

Example 2.
Back to Step 4 in the proof of Lemma
3.3
,
where the parameter
\eta
in (
3.12
) is fixed as
\eta=\frac{\gamma}{4}
, repeat the argument given in Example 1 to get

\hat{\alpha}=1-\mu_{1}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)J^{\frac{-\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}-\mu_{2}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)\varepsilon_{0}\left(J^{\frac{-\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}}+\mu_{3}(\kappa,h_{0},\gamma)\right)^{2}.

Then one can easily see that there exist a constant
\underline{J}>1
sufficiently large, and a small constant
\varepsilon_{0}>0
depending only on
(\gamma,S_{0},\zeta_{0},q_{0})
so that, for any
J\in[\underline{J},\infty)
, if
\lambda(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{L})
is expressed as

\lambda(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{L})=\varepsilon_{0}J^{\frac{-\gamma}{2(\gamma+1)}},

then the estimate (
A.3
) holds true, thus the result comparable to (
3.39

L
is expressed as

L=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon_{0}h_{0}^{3}}{2}}J^{\frac{3\gamma-8}{4(\gamma+1)}}.

So we conclude that the value of
