Estimating the size of a closed population by modeling latent and observed heterogeneity
Antonio Forcina, Department of Economics

Abstract
The paper describes a new class of capture-recapture models for closed populations when individual covariates are available. The novelty consists in combining a latent class model for the distribution of the capture history, where the class weights and the conditional distributions given the latent may depend on covariates, with a model for the marginal distribution of the available covariates as in Liu et al., 2017 . In addition, any general form of serial dependence is allowed when modeling capture histories conditionally on the latent and covariates. A Fisher-scoring algorithm for maximum likelihood estimation is proposed, and the Implicit Function Theorem is used to show that the mapping between the marginal distribution of the observed covariates and the probabilities of being never captured is one-to-one. Asymptotic results are outlined, and a procedure for constructing likelihood based confidence intervals for the population size is presented. Two examples based on real data are used to illustrate the proposed approach Keywords Capture-recapture data; Implicit function Theorem; Latent class models; Likelihood based confidence intervals; Logistic models; Log-linear models; Recursive dependence.



1
Introduction

The literature on capture-recapture methods for estimating the size of populations not directly observable is wide; a recent overview is provided in
Böhning et al., 2018
, a coauthored book with contributions on the most important branches of the subject.
The present paper deals with situations where units belonging to a closed population
may be detected by a set of lists (or capture occasions) and the full capture history for each unit is available together with a set of individual covariates which may affect the probability of being detected by the different lists.

There is substantial agreement that likelihood based confidence intervals for the population total are preferable to intervals based on the normal approximation, when the sample size is not sufficiently large
(
Evans et al., 1994
, see, among others,)
. The likelihood based approach for confidence interval has been dealt in several papers,
starting with
Cormack, 1992

whose method has been extended by
Stanghellini and van der Heijden, 2004
to the case of unobserved heterogeneity, modeled by latent classes, to construct intervals for the population size within a specified sub-population indexed by a discrete covariate.
The approach proposed by
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2006
also assumes latent class models and is designed to provide confidence intervals for the population size within a specified collection of sub-populations indexed, again, by a discrete covariate.
More recently,
Liu et al., 2017
have proposed an approach which takes also into accounts the marginal distribution of the covariates; because a non-parametric model is used for the latter component, connections with
Owen, 1988
’s Empirical Likelihood are exploited.

This paper extends
Liu et al., 2017
’s work, which relies on models for the distribution of the capture history that only account for observed heterogeneity and assume independence between capture occasions, by considering unobserved heterogeneity in the capture probabilities through a latent class model with parameters that may depend on the covariates; in addition, flexible models accounting for the capture history conditional on the latent variable and observable covariates are presented. Among other features, our models allow pairs of lists to be dependent conditionally on the latent class as in a log-linear model, or to exhibit serial dependence, leading to models for which the conditional distribution of the capture configuration given the latent class is not log-linear in the parameters.
In addition, we provide an alternative to the Empirical Likelihood approach in
Liu et al., 2017
to compute a profile likelihood for the parameters of interest. Our approach highlights the relation between the marginal distribution of the covariates and the conditional probability of being never captured which we show to be one-to-one.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In the next section we describe the proposed class of models. Maximum likelihood estimation is described in Section
3
and asymptotic properties of the estimator are outlined in Section
4
. Finally, an illustration consisting of two applications based on data about human and animal populations is provided in Section
5
.
Software for fitting the models described in the paper, both in
R
and
Matlab
, is available from the authors.

2
The proposed class of models

2.1
Preliminaries

Let
N
denote the overall population size,
n
be the number of subjects observed at least once, and suppose that, for each observed subject, a vector
x
of covariates is available. Let
s
denote the number of distinct covariate configurations and
n_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,s
, be the number of subjects captured within the stratum having vector of covariates
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
, so that
\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}=n
. Though, in practice, covariates will always be discrete, if they can assume a large number of distinct configurations, we might have
n_{i}=1,\forall i
, meaning that each stratum contains just one subject; in general
s\leq n
.

Following
Liu et al., 2017
, let
\tau_{i}
denote the marginal weight attached to individuals with
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
in the population
and
\phi_{i}
be the probability that an individual with covariates
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
is never captured. The probability of being never captured for a subject whose covariate configuration is unknown may be computed as
\phi
=
\sum_{i}\phi_{i}\tau_{i}
; because
\sum_{i}\tau_{i}=1
, this may be seen as a weighted average of the
\phi_{i}
s.

Assuming that population units may be captured by
J
different lists, the capture history may be coded into a binary vector
h
=
(h_{1},\dots,h_{J})^{\prime}
, where
h_{j}=1
if a subject is captured by the
j
th list and 0 otherwise. We arrange the capture configurations in lexicographic order starting with
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
, where
0
denotes a column vector of 0s of suitable dimension. Let
k
=
2^{J}
denote the number of possible configurations.

Suppose that subjects can belong to
C
disjoint latent classes and let
\pi_{ic,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}}
denote the probability that subjects within the
i
th stratum belong to latent class
c
and experience capture configuration
h
conditionally on
x
=
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
; let
p_{i,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}}
denote the probability of capture configuration
h
conditionally on the covariates and

\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{i}
the vector whose entries are the frequencies for each capture configuration with the one corresponding to
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
excluded.
When
n_{i}
= 1, namely only one subject with
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
has been captured,
\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{i}
is a vector of 0s except for the entry corresponding to the observed capture configuration which equals 1.
Let also
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$\pi$}_{i}
denote the corresponding vectors of probabilities for the manifest and latent distributions respectively, with the latent running the slowest and capture configurations in lexicographic order with the
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
configuration omitted.
Thus,
p_{i,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$0$}}}
=
\phi_{i}
=
1-\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i}
, where, in general, by
1
we denote a column vector of ones of suitable dimension.

2.2
A latent class model for capture probabilities

The set of models for capture data proposed here is a natural extension of the one described in
Forcina, 2017
;
relative to the class of models in
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2006
, neither is a subset of the other, but the one proposed here is easier to estimate.
For a related class of models see also
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2001
.

Let
\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}
denote a
C
-dimensional column
vector whose entries are the marginal probabilities of the latent for the
i
th stratum and
\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{ic}
be a vector of multinomial probabilities that determine the conditional distribution of the responses conditionally on the latent equal to
c
, with the configuration
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
removed; let also
\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}_{i}
be the matrix with columns
\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{i1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{iC}
, so that
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i}
=
\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}
corresponds to the manifest distribution of captures for the
i
th stratum.
Below we use the convention that a vector of multinomial probabilities including the response configuration
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}
will be tilted.

As in
Forcina, 2017
, we assume that the vector of latent weights follows a multinomial logistic regression model to account for the possible dependence of these weights on covariates; the model may be written as

\log\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}-\mbox{\boldmath$1$}\log[\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\prime}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$})],

where
\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i}
are suitable design matrices and
\zeta
is the corresponding vector of regression parameters.

Let
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{ic}
denote the vector whose elements are the
q_{ic,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}}
, having arranged the capture histories
h
in lexicographic order; we assume that
\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{ic}
=
\exp[\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{ic}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic})]
,
where
\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{ic}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic})
is a vector of smooth functions of
\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic}
, a working vector of parameters which, as we shall see, may depend on covariates and be subjected to linear restrictions. Log-linear models correspond to the special case

\mbox{\boldmath$m$}_{ic}(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic})=\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic}-\log[\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\prime}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$G$}\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic})],

where
G
is the design matrix of the log-linear model coding main effects (capture-occasions) and, possibly, a limited number of bivariate interactions.

To model recursive dependencies in a capture-recapture contest, let
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}
denote the vector containing the first
j-1
elements of
h
, that is, the capture history up to occasion
j-1
, with
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{1}
=
\emptyset
; let also
\eta_{icj,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}_{j}}
denote the logit of the probability of capturing the
i
th subject at the
j
th occasion, conditionally on the latent class and the previous capture history, and
q_{ic,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}}
denote the conditional probability of capture configuration
h
within latent class
c
.
The saturated model may be written as the product of the density of
J
binary variables

\log q_{ic,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\log q_{icj,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left\{h_{j}\eta_{icj,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}_{j}}-\log[1+\exp(\eta_{icj,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}_{j}})]\right\},

where
h_{j}
is the
j
th element of
h
; this formulation generalizes the logit model by
Huggins, 1989
to latent heterogeneity and the models by
Farcomeni, 2011
and
Alunni Fegatelli and Tardella, 2016
to the case where observed and latent heterogeneity is present.
For fixed
i
and
c
, the saturated model involves
k-1
parameters; parsimonious models may be defined in two stages. First, like in
Alunni Fegatelli and Tardella, 2016
, partition the set of all possible partial capture histories into
V
disjoint sub-sets
(\mbox{${\cal H}$}_{1},\dots,\mbox{${\cal H}$}_{V})
, where, usually,
\mbox{${\cal H}$}_{1}
corresponds to absence of capture history or no previous capture and assume that
\eta_{icj,\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$h$}}_{j}}
=
\delta_{ic,v}
for all
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}\in\mbox{${\cal H}$}_{v}
.
This may be translated into a matrix formulation as follows: first, arrange the
k
possible capture histories
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}^{\prime}
in lexicographic order into the rows of the matrix
H
and
let
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{v}
,
v=1,\dots,V
, be a matrix of the same size as
H
and such that the
j
th element in its
r
th row is equal to 1 if
\mbox{\boldmath$h$}_{j}
, the vector of partial history in the
r
th row of
H
, belongs to
\mbox{${\cal H}$}_{v}
and 0 otherwise.
Because the equivalence classes are disjoint, if element
(r,j)
of
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{u}
is equal to 1, the corresponding elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{v}
,
\forall v\neq u
, must be 0.
The non-zero elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{v}
might be replaced by quantitative summaries
of the previous capture history as in
Alunni Fegatelli and Tardella, 2016
.
Direct calculations show that we may write

\log\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$q$}}_{ic}=\mbox{\boldmath$A$}\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic}-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}\log[1+\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic})],

where the matrices
A
and
B
may be constructed as follows: let ’
*
’ denote the element-wise product between matrices; then the
v
th column of
A
is
(\mbox{\boldmath$H$}*\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{v})\mbox{\boldmath$1$}
while the corresponding column of
B
is
\mbox{\boldmath$H$}_{v}\mbox{\boldmath$1$}
.
Once a parsimonious model has been defined, linearity within the components of a recursive model, linear dependence on covariates and restrictions across latent classes may be defined as

\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic}=\mbox{\boldmath$M$}_{ic}\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$},\quad i=1,\ldots,s,\>c=1,\ldots,C.

Example 1
.

A model slightly more complex than
M_{b}
in
Otis et al., 1978
which, for fixed
i
and
c
, requires four parameters, may be defined by crossing the following two conditions, each of which may be true independently of the other: (i) captured more than once before and (ii) captured in the previous occasion. Thus we may have four types of capture histories: (1) at most once before, not in the previous occasion; (2) first time in the previous occasion; (3) more than once before, not in the previous occasion; (4) captured in the previous occasion and in at least one occasion before. An additive effect of these two features might be assumed.

3
Maximum likelihood estimation

3.1
The likelihood function

By adapting the formulation in
Liu et al., 2017
to the case of subjects possibly grouped into strata, the log-likelihood may be split into three components:

1.

the logarithm of the binomial likelihood, corresponding to the probability that
n
out of
N
subjects whose stratum is unknown are captured at least once, that is,

\log\Gamma(N+1)-\log\Gamma(N-n+1)+(N-n)\log(\phi)+n\log(1-\phi);

2.

the sum of the logarithm of the probability of the observed capture configurations conditionally on belonging to the
i
the stratum,
i=1,\dots,s
, and on being captured at least once, equal to

\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{i}^{\prime}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\phi_{i}}\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i}\right);

(1)

3.

the sum of the logarithm of the probability of observing
\mbox{\boldmath$x$}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$}_{i}
conditionally on being captured at least once irrespective of the covariate value, times
n_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,s
, that is,

\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}\log\left(\tau_{i}\frac{1-\phi_{i}}{1-\phi}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}\log\tau_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}n_{i}\log(1-\phi_{i})-n\log(1-\phi).

After simplifying terms appearing twice with opposite signs, the log-likelihood may be written as

L(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})=\log\frac{\Gamma(N+1)}{\Gamma(N-n+1)}+(N-n)\log(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{i}^{\prime}\log(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i})+n_{i}\log\tau_{i}\right],

(2)

where
\psi
=
(N,\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime})^{\prime}
,
\beta
=
(\mbox{\boldmath$\zeta$}^{\prime},\mbox{\boldmath$\lambda$}^{\prime})^{\prime}
and
\phi
is the
s
dimensional vector with elements
\phi_{i}
.
To phrase the problem into an Empirical Likelihood context,
Liu et al., 2017
treat
\phi
(the weighted average of the
\phi_{i}
) as an independent parameter, while we make explicit the dependence of
\phi
on
\phi
which is determined by
\beta
.
Though the two approaches are equivalent, ours may be more easily implemented into a latent class context; in addition, as we show in Section
3.4
, for fixed
N
and
\beta
, the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) of
\tau
is an invertible function of
\phi
. This result is useful for studying the profile likelihood of
N
and
\beta
by treating
\tau
as a vector of nuisance parameters. We also derive an element-wise relationship between the
\tau_{i}
and the
\phi_{i}
parameters which has an interesting interpretation and allows comparisons with the similar relationship which holds with the conditional MLE.

Rather than maximizing the likelihood simultaneously with respect to the full set of parameters, we suggest updating the three components (
N,\>\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\>\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}
) separately and to iterate until convergence:

1.

for fixed
N
and
\tau
, maximize
L(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})
with respect to
\beta
by a Fisher-scoring algorithm;

2.

for fixed
N
and
\beta
, maximize
L(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})
with respect to
\tau
under the constraint
\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$1$}=1
;

3.

for fixed
\beta
and
\tau
maximize
L(\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$},\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})
with respect to
N
.

These steps are illustrated in detail in the following.

3.2
Updating
\beta

For fixed
N
and
\tau
,
\beta
can be updated by a Fisher-scoring algorithm similar to the one described in
Forcina, 2017
.
Let
\Phi
denote the matrix of derivatives of
\phi
with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime}
; the score vector with respect to
\beta
may be written as

\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\beta$}}}=\frac{N-n}{\phi}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{\prime}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$y$}_{i},

where
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}
is the derivative of
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i}
with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime}
and may be partitioned as

\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}=(\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}(\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}_{i})\mbox{\boldmath$X$}_{i},\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i1},\dots,\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{iC})

and, for
c=1,\dots,C
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\mbox{\it Log-linear }&\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ic}=\xi_{ic}\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}(\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{ic})\mbox{\boldmath$M$}_{ic},\\
\mbox{\it Recursive logistic }&\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{ic}=\xi_{ic}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$q$}_{ic})\left[\mbox{\boldmath$A$}-\mbox{\boldmath$B$}{\rm diag}(1+\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic}))^{-1}\exp(\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}_{ic})\right]\mbox{\boldmath$M$}_{ic},\end{array}

where, given a vector of multinomial probabilities
v
,
\mbox{\boldmath$\Omega$}(\mbox{\boldmath$v$})
=
{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$v$})-\mbox{\boldmath$v$}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime}
.
Lemma
2
in the Appendix implies that the derivative of
\Phi
and
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}
with respect to the elements of
\beta
may be neglected, thus the expected information matrix is easy to compute and takes the form

\tilde{\mbox{\boldmath$F$}}=N\left[\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}/\phi+\sum_{i=1}^{s}\tau_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{\prime}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}\right].

3.3
Estimation of
\tau

An alternative to the Empirical Likelihood approach for estimating
\tau
as in
Liu et al., 2017
may be obtained by maximizing the log-likelihood as a function of
\tau
by Lagrange multipliers to account for the constraint
\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}
= 1. Let
n
denote the vector with entries
n_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,s
, and
\gamma
be the Lagrange multiplier; ignoring constant terms with respect to
\tau
, we have

L(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})=(N-n)\log(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})+\mbox{\boldmath$n$}^{\prime}\log(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})-\gamma(\mbox{\boldmath$1$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}-1).

By differentiating with respect to
\tau
and a few simple algebraic manipulations, see the Appendix for details, we obtain the following updating equation where
u
is the step counter

\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{(u+1)}=\frac{1}{N}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$n$}+\frac{N-n}{\phi^{(u)}}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{(u)}\right].

(3)

In practice, this equation will be iterated until
\sum_{i}\mid\tau_{i}^{(u-1)}-\tau_{i}^{(u)}\mid

is sufficiently close to 0; formally, at convergence,
\tau_{i}^{(u-1)}
=
\tau_{i}^{(u)}
=
\tau_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,s
. With this in mind, by simple algebraic manipulations we obtain

\tau_{i}=\frac{n_{i}\phi}{N\phi-(N-n)\phi_{i}},\quad i=1,\dots s;

(4)

though the right hand side still depends on
\tau
through
\phi
, at convergence this is a constant with respect to
i
and, under the assumption that the elements of
\phi
are strictly positive, Lemma
1
in the Appendix imply that
N\phi-(N-n)\phi_{i}>0
, thus the points
(\phi_{i},\tau_{i})
lie on the branch of an hyperbola which is strictly increasing and concave.

Once written in matrix notation, (
4
) may be interpreted as an implicit equation in
\phi
and
\tau
; to get a more convenient expression, multiply both sides by
N\phi\mbox{\boldmath$I$}-(N-n){\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$})
,
rearrange terms and, for the moment, consider the case of just one subject captured in each stratum, that is
n
=
1
; then we have

N\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\phi-\mbox{\boldmath$1$}\phi-(N-n){\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}=\mbox{\boldmath$0$}.

(5)

An equivalent expression may be obtained from equation (11) in the Supplementary Material in
Liu et al., 2017
. In order to derive an expression for
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}
=
\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}/\partial\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{\prime}
, differentiate (
5
) with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{\prime}
and rearrange terms (see the Appendix for details); we obtain

\displaystyle[\mbox{\boldmath$I$}+\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{\prime}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0})^{-1}]{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0})\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}=[\mbox{\boldmath$I$}-\mbox{\boldmath$a$}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{\prime}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{1})^{-1}]{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{1}),

(6)

\displaystyle\mbox{ where }\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0}=N\phi\left(\mbox{\boldmath$1$}-\frac{N-n}{N\phi}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}\right),\>\mbox{\boldmath$a$}=N(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}-\mbox{\boldmath$1$}/N),\>\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{1}=(N-n)\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}.

(7)

Let
{\cal T}
denote the collection of pairs
(\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$},\>\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})
for which
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}
exists; the implicit function theorem implies that
\tau
is an invertible function of
\phi
within
{\cal T}
. Lemma
1
in the Appendix shows that the elements of both
a
and
\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0}
are strictly positive as long as the elements of
\tau
and
\phi
are also strictly positive. Thus, the following result holds.

Proposition 1
.

Within
{\cal T}
,
\tau
is an invertible function of
\phi
with derivative matrix
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}
.

Proof.
The left hand side of (
6
) is well defined because the elements of
\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0}
are strictly positive; it is easily checked that matrices of the form
\mbox{\boldmath$I$}+\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime}
are singular if and only if
\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}=-1
which, in our context, is impossible because the elements of both
a
and
\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0}
are strictly positive.
Using the fact that
(\mbox{\boldmath$I$}+\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime})^{-1}
=
\mbox{\boldmath$I$}-\mbox{\boldmath$u$}\mbox{\boldmath$v$}^{\prime}/(1+\mbox{\boldmath$u$}^{\prime}\mbox{\boldmath$v$})

and some additional algebraic computations (see the Appendix for details), we have that

\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}={\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0})^{-1}\left[\mbox{\boldmath$I$}-\mbox{\boldmath$a$}({\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}+{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{1})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$})^{\prime}/g\right]{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{1}),

with
g=1+\mbox{\boldmath$a$}^{\prime}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$d$}_{0})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}
.

When
\mbox{\boldmath$n$}\neq\mbox{\boldmath$1$}
, as in
Liu et al., 2017
(paragraph before their equation (6)), to account for ties, define
\omega_{i}
=
\tau_{i}/n_{i}
; then, an implicit equation similar to (
5
) with
\tau

,

, is easily derived: start from (
5
), replace
1
with

and divide the
i

.

Remark 1
.

Proposition
1
indicates that
\tau
is uniquely determined by
\phi

\beta
; though no explicit equation exists, once an estimate of
\beta
is available, an estimate of
\tau

).
In addition, equation (
4
) implies that, element-wise, the relation between
\phi
and
\tau
is strictly increasing and has a simple functional form. It may be interesting to note that, because the conditional MLE of the population size in the

N\tau_{i}^{(c)}

\tau_{i}^{(c)}
, the relative size of the

\tau_{i}^{(c)}=\frac{1}{1-\phi_{i}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\frac{1}{1-\phi_{i}}\right)^{-1},

which is again the increasing branch of an hyperbola.
Values of
\tau_{i}

Updating

This may be performed by a one-dimensional Fisher-scoring step based on the derivatives of the log-gamma function
\log[\Gamma(v)]
to compute the score and
{}_{o}F_{N}
, the observed information for

These quantities may be expressed as

\displaystyle s_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{lg}(1,N+1)-D_{lg}(1,N-n+1)+\log(\phi),

{}_{o}F_{N}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D_{lg}(2,N-n+1)-D_{lg}(2,N+1),

4
Asymptotic results

Proposition
1
implies that one can replace the original likelihood with its profile with respect to
N
and
\beta
by replacing
\tau
with
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}(N,\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})
, its MLE for a given value of the arguments.
Recalling that the parameter vector
\psi
=
(N,\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime})^{\prime}
does not contain the nuisance parameters
\tau
, to derive the asymptotic distribution of
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}
we can use the results in
Liu et al., 2017
, Supplementary Material, with minor adaptations which are described in the Appendix.
It follows that, as
N_{0}
, the true value of the population size, goes to infinity, the MLE is consistent and

\sqrt{N_{0}}(\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}-\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$})\overset{d}{\to}N(\mbox{\boldmath$0$},\mbox{\boldmath$F$}^{-1}),

where
F
is the asymptotic information of the profile likelihood, as explained below.

4.1
The information matrix

The information matrix used in the Fisher scoring algorithm, in addition to being
N
times larger than
F
, assumes that
\tau
is known; to take into account uncertainty about
\tau
, we need to compute the information matrix of the profile likelihood (see for instance
Pace and Salvan, 1997
, Sec. 4.6). The following argument may be used to compute an explicit expression for the expected information matrix: first, use the results in
Liu et al., 2017
who derive an approximation for
F_{NN}
and show that
\mbox{\boldmath$F$}_{N\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\beta$}}}
converges to a vector of 0s when
N_{0}
, the true population size, tends to infinity.
In addition, because Lemma
2
in the Appendix implies that the expected value of all components of the observed information involving derivatives of
\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$},\>\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,s
, with respect to the elements of
\beta
are 0, when differentiating
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\beta$}}}[\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})]
with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime}
, we can restrict attention to terms containing
\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i},\>\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$},\>\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}
.
Direct calculations show that

\displaystyle F_{NN}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\phi}{\phi},

\displaystyle\mbox{\boldmath$F$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\beta$}}\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\beta$}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{s}\tau_{i}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}^{\prime}{\rm diag}(\mbox{\boldmath$p$}_{i})^{-1}\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{i}+\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}^{\prime}\left[\left(\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}^{\prime}/\phi-\mbox{\boldmath$I$}\right)\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}+\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}^{\prime}/\phi\right]\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}.

Remark 2
.

Though the latter expression may appear hard to compute when
s
is very large, the results in Proposition
1
provide an explicit expression for
\mbox{\boldmath$D$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\phi$}}}
which, in addition, has a structure that makes matrix multiplications simpler.
Though an explicit expression for the observed information matrix of the profile likelihood is very hard to compute, an accurate numerical approximation may be obtained by computing the numerical derivative of
\mbox{\boldmath$s$}_{\mbox{\boldmath{\scriptsize$\beta$}}}[\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$},\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}(\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$})]
with respect to
\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}^{\prime}
.

4.2
Confidence intervals for
N

The previous results could be used to construct a confidence interval for
N
; however, this would lead to an interval which is symmetric around
\hat{N}
, the MLE, while it is likely that uncertainty is larger on the right hand side.
Confidence intervals for
N
based on the conditional likelihood were proposed by
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2006
among others; an approach for constructing confidence intervals based on
Owen, 1988
’s notion of Empirical Likelihood is proposed by
Liu et al., 2017
.

Let
\hat{N}
and
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}
denote the unconditional MLEs of the corresponding parameters; let also
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{N}
denote the corresponding estimate when the population total is kept fixed at
N
; define

D_{N}=2\left[\tilde{L}(\hat{N},\>\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}})-\tilde{L}(N,\>\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}_{N})\right];

then the following results holds.

Proposition 2
.

The asymptotic distribution of
D_{N}
converges to
\chi_{1}^{2}
as
N_{0}
grows to infinity.

Proof.
The proof is similar to that of Theorem 1 in
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2006
which uses, essentially, two steps: (i) show that
D_{N}
may be approximated by a constrained quadratic function of the parameter vector
\psi
and (ii) exploiting the asymptotic normal distribution of the ML estimates.

5
Applications

5.1
Bacterial meningitis

The data were produced by the research unit on Infectious Diseases of Lazio, a region which includes the metropolitan area of Rome. Cases were recorded by four different sources:

•

HSS
: hospital surveillance of bacterial meningitis;

•

NDS
: the mandatory infectious diseases notifications;

•

LIS
: the laboratory information system;

•

HIS
:
hospital information system.

Records appearing in the four lists from 2001 to 2005 were combined into a single archive. For a detailed description of the context, see
Giorgi Rossi et al., 2009
. For each of the
n
= 944 patients appearing in at least one list, several covariates were recorded. As in
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2018
, we restrict our attention to the following covariates that were considered to be the most relevant:

•

Age
: binary variable equal to 1 for up to 1 year old and 0 otherwise, because the incidence of meningitis is much higher among very younger children;

•

Aez
: binary variable for the recorded type of bacteria, which is equal to 1 for Pneumococcus, Meningococcus or Tuberculosis and 0 otherwise;

•

Year
: year of first appearance in a list; this is included because the way of functioning of certain lists has evolved during the study period.

Here we fit the log-linear analog of the best marginal model according to
Bartolucci and Forcina, 2018
, who however used a conditional likelihood approach.
There are two latent classes whose weights are assumed to depend on Age and Aez, the conditional logits depend linearly on Year and, in addition, there are log-linear interactions between lists (1,2) and (2,4) not depending on the latent.
To compare the estimated capture probabilities by strata of the marginal model relative to the log-linear one, the Kulback-Leibler divergence times the population size (as estimated by the log-linear model) was used. Considering that the maximum divergence is about 1.7 and that the correlation coefficient between the probability of being never capture by strata in the two approaches is about 0.9998, we may conclude that the two models produce almost the same estimates. However, some differences emerge in the estimate of the population size which is 1,359 according to the log-linear model against 1,386 in the marginal model. The 95% confidence interval ranges from 1127 to 1828, against 1142 to 1875 in the marginal model. These differences are more likely to be due to the fact that the new approach takes into account the marginal distribution of the covariates.

5.2
Deer mouse data

The data, included in the
R
package
VGAM
, are about a small rodent native to North America, called deer mouse.
Overall, 38 individuals were trapped at least once during 6 consecutive nights. Available covariates are sex, age (binary), and weight in grams.
According to
Otis et al., 1978
, page 32, who analyzed the data, model
M_{b}
, assuming that already captured animals are more likely to be captured again, fits well, giving
\hat{N}
= 41.
Based on the assumption of normality, they estimated a confidence interval between 35 to 47, where clearly the lower limit has to be corrected to 38.

Model search was limited by the fact that
n
, the number of subjects captured at least once, is small relative to the number of occasions (lists). Thus in all models we assume the existence of two latent classes and that the capture probability conditionally on the latent, covariates,
and capture history is constant over time. Several models with latent weights or conditional distributions depending on covariates were considered; among the covariates, only sex seems to have a substantial effect on the latent distribution. This model has just four parameters which determine the probability of being in latent class 2 for males, how this probability changes for females and the capture probability for subjects in latent class 1 and 2. Capture probability for latent class 2, which includes mostly males, is about 0.83 against 0.36 for latent class 1. The likelihood ratio for testing the dependence of latent weights on sex is 7.8 with 1 d.o.f., thus highly significant.

The latent class version of the
M_{b}
model has two additional parameters which are highly significant; however, two parameter estimates are near the boundary and the corresponding standard errors are very large indicating that the asymptotic approximations may not be reliable. A constrained version of the same model where the difference, on the logit scale, between the probability of capture in the first occasion and that of recapture is constant across latent classes, has more reasonable estimates, except that, for females, the probability of being in latent class two is very close to 0. Both latent types seem to be trap lovers: the estimates of the capture probabilities in the first occasion are 0.26 and 0.74 respectively and they jump to 0.45 and 0.86 when already captured in a previous occasion. Taking this as our final model, the point estimate of
N
equals 42 and the 95% confidence interval goes from 38 to 60; the fact that the upper limit is so much larger than the one in
Otis et al., 1978
cannot be attributed to the refinements in the model and are plausibly due to the fact that the uncertainty in the size of
N
is much larger on the upper side, a feature which cannot be captured by the normal approximation.

6
Concluding remarks

A natural question is what do we gain by modeling the marginal distribution of the covariates. The main role of the
\tau
parameter is to allow us defining the average probability that a subject with unspecified covariate values is never captured. More importantly, it allows us to deal with the overall population size
N
directly, rather than to estimate the population size within each strata, say
N_{i}
, and then define
N=\sum_{i=1}^{s}N_{i}
.
Moreover, the simulation results in
Liu et al., 2017
indicate that the present approach, relative to the one where we condition to the observed covariates and to being captured at least once, leads to more efficient estimates of the population size and to shorter width of the corresponding confidence intervals. A limited simulation that we performed indicates that the same holds within our extended context.

As far as we know, the idea of modeling the marginal distribution of the covariates appeared in
Liu et al., 2017
for the first time; the previous literature dealing with a collection of populations, see for instance
Stanghellini and van der Heijden, 2004
and
Huggins, 1989
, use as parameters the size of individual populations. Though
Sanathanan, 1972
, Section 5, shows that the unconditional ML estimate are consistent and asymptotically normal if the relative size of each stratum does not tend to 0 as
\sum_{i=1}^{s}N_{i}
tends to infinity, unless
N
is sufficiently large, the estimates within strata may be problematic as explained below.

Unless there is a certain amount of under-count in each stratum, that is
\hat{N}_{i}>n_{i}
, estimates of
\beta
may be close to the boundary.
When the marginal weights of the strata are not taken into account, each
N_{i}
may be estimated separately and Lemma 1 in
Sanathanan, 1972
implies that, for the condition
\hat{N}_{i}>n_{i}
to be satisfied, we must have
\hat{\phi}_{i}>1/(n_{i}+1)
. It can be easily seen that, unless
n_{i}
is sufficiently large,
\hat{N}_{i}=n_{i}
because the score with respect to
N_{i}
is negative at
N_{i}=n_{i}
. For instance, when
n_{i}=1
, from the properties of the
D_{lg}
function, it can be shown that the score at
N_{i}=n_{i}
equals
1+\log(\phi_{i})
which is negative unless
\phi_{i}>0.3679
; when
n_{i}=2
, the score equals
1.5+\log(\phi_{i})
which, again, is negative unless
\phi_{i}>0.2231
. In practice, unless the under-count is sufficiently large relative to
n_{i}
, it cannot be detected during model estimation.
