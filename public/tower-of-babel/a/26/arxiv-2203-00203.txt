Hirota Varieties and Rational Nodal Curves
Claudia Fevola, Yelena Mandelshtam

Abstract
The Hirota variety parameterizes solutions to the KP equation arising from a degenerate Riemann theta function. In this work, we study in detail the Hirota variety arising from a rational nodal curve. Of particular interest is the irreducible subvariety defined as the image of a parameterization map, we call this the main component. Proving that this is an irreducible component of the Hirota variety corresponds to solving a weak Schottky problem for rational nodal curves. We solve this problem up to genus nine using computational tools.



The
degenerate Riemann theta function
associated to an irreducible rational nodal curve
X
of genus
g
is a finite sum of exponentials

\theta_{\mathcal{C}}({\bf z})\,\,=\,\,a_{1}\exp[\mathbf{c}_{1}^{T}{\bf z}]\,\,+\,\,a_{2}\exp[\mathbf{c}_{2}^{T}{\bf z}]\,\,+\,\,\dots\,\,+\,\,a_{2^{g}}\exp[\mathbf{c}_{2^{g}}^{T}{\bf z}]

(1)

supported on the configuration of points
\mathcal{C}=\{0,1\}^{g}
in the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{g}
. Each point
\mathbf{c}_{i}=(\mathbf{c}_{i1},\mathbf{c}_{i2},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{ig})\in{\cal C}
determines a linear form
\mathbf{c}_{i}^{T}{\bf z}=\sum_{j=i}^{g}c_{ij}z_{j}
, where
{\bf z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{g})
is a vector of complex unknowns, while the coefficients
{\bf a}\penalty\ =\penalty\ (a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{2^{g}})
play the role of coordinates on the algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}
.
The classical Riemann theta function associated to a smooth algebraic curve of genus
g
is a complex analytic function expressed by an infinite sum of exponentials supported on the whole integer lattice, namely

\theta\,\,=\,\,\theta({\bf z}\,|\,B)\,\,=\,\,\sum_{{\bf c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{g}}\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{c}^{T}B\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{c}^{T}{\bf z}\right],

(2)

where
B
is the
Riemann matrix
associated to the curve. This is a symmetric
g\times g
matrix that we normalize to have negative definite real part.
The analogue of this matrix for a rational nodal curve is given by the
tropical Riemann matrix
. To compute it, one considers the dual graph
\Gamma

[
10
]
corresponding to
X
and associates a matrix to it by looking at the generators of the first homology group of the graph
\Gamma
. The dual graph of the curve
X
consists of one unique vertex with precisely
g
cycles attached to it, one for each node, and the tropical Riemann matrix is the identity matrix of size
g
.

The
Hirota variety

\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
associated to the curve
X
, first introduced in
[
5
]
for curves with at worst nodal singularities, lives in the parameter space given by the product of the algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}
with coordinates
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{2^{g}})
, and the weighted projective space
\mathbb{W}\mathbb{P}^{3g-1}
whose coordinates are the vectors
{\bf u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{g})
,
{\bf v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{g})
, and
{\bf w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{g})
with weights
1
,
2
, and
3
for
u_{i},v_{i}
, and
w_{i}
, respectively. It parameterizes the points
{({\bf a},({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}))\in(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}\times\mathbb{W}\mathbb{P}^{3g-1}}
such that the
\tau
-function
\tau(x,y,t)=\theta_{\mathcal{C}}({\bf u}x+{\bf v}y+{\bf w}t)
satisfies the
Hirota’s bilinear equation

\tau\tau_{xxxx}\,\,-\,\,4\tau_{xxx}\tau_{x}\,\,+\,\,3\tau_{xx}^{2}\,\,+\,\,4\tau_{x}\tau_{t}\,\,-\,\,4\tau\tau_{xt}\,\,+\,\,3\tau\tau_{yy}\,\,-\,\,3\tau_{y}^{2}\,\,=\,\,0.

(3)

This provides a sufficient condition for the function

p(x,y,t)\,=\,2\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\log\tau(x,y,t)

(4)

to be a solution for the
Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation
. This is a nonlinear partial differential equation describing the motion of water waves given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(4p_{t}-6pp_{x}-p_{xxx})\,=\,3p_{yy},

(5)

where the unknown function
p=p(x,y,t)
depends on two space variables
x
and
y
, and on the time variable
t
. Furthermore, points in the Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
correspond to a special class of solutions to the KP equation, namely
soliton
solutions,
[
1
,
19
]
.

These are solutions of type (
4
) with the
\tau
-function having a particular form. To define it, one fixes integers
k<n
and a vector of parameters
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\dots,\kappa_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\tau(x,y,t)\,\,=\,\,\sum_{I\in\binom{[n]}{k}}p_{I}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in I\\
i<j\end{subarray}}(\kappa_{i}-\kappa_{j})\cdot\exp\biggl[x\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_{i}+y\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_{i}^{2}+t\cdot\sum_{i\in I}\kappa_{i}^{3}\biggr],

(6)

where
I
runs over all possible
k
-tuples of elements in the set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, and the
p_{I}
are Plücker coordinates of a point in the Grassmannian Gr
(k,n)
,
[
19
,
23
]
. We call (
6
) a
(k,n)
-soliton.

Finding solutions to the KP equation is related to the
Schottky problem
, in that a theta function satisfies the KP equation when the corresponding abelian variety is the Jacobian of a curve
[
20
,
24
]
. More precisely, it concerns the Torelli map
J:\mathcal{M}_{g}\to\mathcal{A}_{g}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{g}
is the moduli space of curves of genus
g
, and
\mathcal{A}_{g}
is the moduli space of abelian varieties of dimension
g
. The Schottky problem is to find the defining equations for the locus of Jacobians, defined as the closure of
J(\mathcal{M}_{g})
in
\mathcal{A}_{g}
.
The
weak Schottky problem
is to find an ideal whose zero locus contains the locus of Jacobians as an irreducible component. In genus 3, the Schottky problem is trivial, in the sense that every polarized abelian variety is the Jacobian of a curve. For genus
g\geq 4
, one has a proper inclusion
J(\mathcal{M}_{g})\subset\mathcal{A}_{g}
. For genus larger than
5
, the Schottky problem has proven to be difficult, with only partial results for genus 5 and weak solutions in higher genera. Many approaches to the Schottky problem have been developed (e.g.,
[
7
,
17
]
), and an explicit solution to the weak Schottky problem was provided in
[
13
]
and investigated numerically for genus 5 in
[
4
]
. The proof of Theorem
3
in our paper relies on a solution to the weak Schottky problem for nodal curves, which we provide for
g\leq 9
.

This article is organized as follows. In Section
1
, we introduce the Hirota variety of a rational nodal curve and discuss its main component. We prove for genus
g\leq 9
that this is an irreducible component of dimension
3g
and we explain how this relates to the Schottky problem. Section

2
studies the equations of the main component of the variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
and how they relate to the combinatorics of the cube.

1
The Main Component of the Hirota Variety

Let
X
be a rational nodal curve of genus
g
. The degenerate theta function (
1
) associated to
X
is supported on the vertices of the
g
-dimensional cube
\mathcal{C}
whose vertices are all binary
g
-dimensional vectors. The Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
lives in the space
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}\times\mathbb{W}\mathbb{P}^{3g-1}
with coordinate ring
\mathbb{C}[{\bf a}^{\pm 1},{\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}]
, where
\deg(u_{i})=1,\deg(v_{i})=2,
and
\deg(w_{i})=3
for
i=1,2,\dots,g
.

We want to investigate the subvariety denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{I}
of the Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
, where the superscript
I
stands for ‘invertible’. We define
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{I}
to be the Zariski closure of the set

\bigl\{\,(\mathbf{a,(u,v,w)})\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}\,\,:\,\,\bf{u}\neq\bf{0}\,\bigr\}.

(7)

This subvariety also contains a meaningful irreducible subvariety, which we call the
main component
.
To rigorously define it, consider the map from the affine space
\mathbb{C}^{3g}
with coordinates

(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{g},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{2g})
into the ambient space of the Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
given by

\displaystyle\phi\;:\;\mathbb{C}^{3g}\quad

\displaystyle\dashrightarrow\,(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}\times\mathbb{W}\mathbb{P}^{3g-1}

(8)

\displaystyle(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{g},\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{2g})

\displaystyle\longmapsto\,(a_{\mathbf{c}_{1}},a_{\mathbf{c}_{2}},\dots,a_{\mathbf{c}_{2^{g}}},{\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}),

where the coordinates
{\bf a}=(a_{\mathbf{c}_{1}},a_{\mathbf{c}_{2}},\dots,a_{\mathbf{c}_{2^{g}}})
are indexed by the points in
\mathcal{C}=\{0,1\}^{g}
. The image of
\phi
is defined as follows

\begin{matrix}u_{i}=\kappa_{2i-1}-\kappa_{2i},\quad v_{i}=\kappa_{2i-1}^{2}-\kappa_{2i}^{2},\quad w_{i}=\kappa_{2i-1}^{3}-\kappa_{2i}^{3}\qquad\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,g,\vskip 12.0pt plus 4.0pt minus 4.0pt\\
a_{\mathbf{c}}=\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in I\\
i<j\end{subarray}}(\kappa_{i}-\kappa_{j})\displaystyle\prod_{\{i\,:\,c_{i}=1\}}\lambda_{i},\,\quad\text{ where }I=\{2i:c_{i}=0\}\cup\{2i-1:c_{i}=1\}\quad\text{for }\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}.\end{matrix}

(9)

We call the closure of the image of
\phi
the
main component of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
and denote it
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
.

In the first part of this section, we will explain the geometric intuition that leads us to the definition of the main component
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
by way of the
Abel map
for curves and the
theta divisor
, see
[
6
]
. In the second part, we will focus on the study of the main component and its connection with the weak and classical Schottky problems.

1.1
The Theta Divisor of a Rational Nodal Curve

Let
X
be a curve as above and denote by
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{g}
its nodes. The normalization
{\nu:\tilde{X}\to X}
that separates the
g
nodes of
X
is given by a projective line. We can consider
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{2g}
to be points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and set
\nu^{-1}(n_{i}):=\{\kappa_{2i-1},\kappa_{2i}\}
. Hence, each rational curve with only nodal singularities corresponds to a copy of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
2g
points on it. This fact motivates that the moduli space of rational nodal curves has dimension
2g-3
, where one subtracts
3
to account for the dimension of the automorphism group of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
A basis of holomorphic differentials for such curves is given by

\omega_{i}\,\,=\,\,\frac{1}{y}\left(\frac{1}{1-\kappa_{2i}y}-\frac{1}{1-\kappa_{2i-1}y}\right)dy\qquad\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,g,

when fixing
y=1/x
as local coordinate. These define a map
\alpha^{\prime}:(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{g-1}\dashrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g}
such that

(y_{1},\dots,y_{g-1})\longmapsto\left(\,\sum_{i=1}^{g-1}\int_{0}^{y_{i}}\omega_{j}\right)_{j=1,2,\dots,g}\quad\text{where}\quad\int_{0}^{y_{i}}\omega_{j}\,=\,\log\left(\frac{1-\kappa_{2j-1}y_{i}}{1-\kappa_{2j}y_{i}}\right).

The (generalized) Jacobian of the curve
X
is an algebraic torus
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{g}
. Exponentiation allows to map in the Jacobian through the map
\mathbb{C}^{g}\to(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{g}
given by (
(z_{1},\dots,z_{g})\mapsto(\exp[z_{1}],\dots,\exp[z_{g}])
). The composition gives the Abel map
\alpha:(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{g-1}\dashrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{g}
defined by

(y_{1},\dots,y_{g-1})\longmapsto\left(\,\prod_{i=1}^{g-1}\frac{1-\kappa_{1}y_{i}}{1-\kappa_{2}y_{i}},\,\,\prod_{i=1}^{g-1}\frac{1-\kappa_{3}y_{i}}{1-\kappa_{4}y_{i}},\dots,\prod_{i=1}^{g-1}\frac{1-\kappa_{2g-1}y_{i}}{1-\kappa_{2g}y_{i}}\,\right).

(10)

The theta divisor of
X
is the image of the Abel map
\alpha
up to translation.
We will motivate later that we expect each point in the main component of the Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
to correspond (non-injectively) to the choice of a curve in this moduli space and a theta divisor. Following this reasoning, the projection of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
into the space
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}
has dimension
3g-3
, accounting for the choice of a rational nodal curve and its theta divisor. For each point in this projection, the fiber is a threefold (analogous to the Dubrovin threefold studied in
[
3
]
) given by

\{\,(\mathbf{u,v,w})\in\mathbb{W}\mathbb{P}^{3g-1}\,\,:\,\,\tau(x,y,t)=\theta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{u}x+{\bf v}y+{\bf w}t)\text{ solves }\eqref{eq:hirotabilinearform}\,\}.

Thus, the expected dimension of the main component is
2g-3+g+3=3g
.
This discussion also provides a way to parameterize the main component of the Hirota variety. The idea is that the choice of the curve
X
yields
2g
parameters
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\dots,\kappa_{2g}
, and the family of theta divisors corresponding to shifts of the unknown vector
{\bf z}\in\mathbb{C}^{g}
yields
g
parameters
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{g}
.
In particular, we are able to compute, using
Macaulay2
, the theta divisor as a shift (through a change of variables) of the image of the map (
10
) up to genus
6
. This is done following the method described in
[
21
, Corollary 4.8]
. The equation of the theta divisor returned by the code coincides precisely with (
19
) with coefficients
{\bf a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{2g})
parameterized in a way which can be shown, through a change of coordinates and some calculations, to be geometrically equivalent to (
9
), up to the
\lambda
parameters. More precisely, this justifies that the parameterization in (
8
) is not unique. The torus action on the theta divisor provides new suitable parameterizations. The approach for computing the theta divisor was inspired by
[
2
]
. A construction for the theta divisor of a singular curve is also discussed in
[
22
, Chapter IIIb]
.

To understand how the
\lambda
parameters arise, we consider the Riemann theta function (
1
) evaluated at a point
{\bf z}\in\mathbb{C}^{g}
shifted by a vector
{\bf h}=(h_{1},h_{2},\dots,h_{g})\in\mathbb{C}^{g}
, namely

\theta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{z+h})=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{C}}a_{\mathbf{c}}\exp[\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{h}]\exp[\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{z}]

Both functions
\theta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{z})
and
\theta_{\mathcal{C}}(\mathbf{z+h})
provide solutions to the KP equation (with nonzero
u_{i}
), hence they correspond to points in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
. More explicitly, denote
\lambda_{i}:=\exp[h_{i}]
, we see that for a point
(\mathbf{a,(u,v,w)})\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
, the point
(\mathbf{\tilde{a},(u,v,w)})\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
, where
\mathbf{\tilde{a}}=(a_{\mathbf{c}}\exp[\mathbf{c}^{T}\mathbf{h}])=(a_{\mathbf{c}}\prod_{\{i\,:\,c_{i}=1\}}\lambda_{i})
. Thus, we conclude that the choice of the theta divisor is exactly represented by the parameterizing variables
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{g}
.

1.2
Geometry of the Main Component

The following result justifies the definition of the main component
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
and provides a connection with soliton solutions to the KP equation.

Theorem 2
.

Consider the map
\phi
given in (
8
). This is a birational map onto its image, which is an irreducible subvariety of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
and has dimension
3g
.

Proof.

Let
I
be as in (
9
). Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{3g}
be the closed set where at least two of the
\kappa_{i}
coincide. The expression of
\tau(x,y,t)=\theta_{\mathcal{C}}({\bf u}x+{\bf v}y+{\bf w}t)
where
({\bf a},({\bf u},{\bf v},{\bf w}))
is attained as the image of a point in
\mathbb{C}^{3g}\setminus K
through the map
\phi
, described in (
8
), is a point in the Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
since
it can be expressed as a
(g,2g)
-soliton
[
18
]
for the matrix

A\,=\,\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&1&0&0&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&\lambda_{2}&1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&0&0&\lambda_{3}&1&\dots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&0&0&0&\dots&\lambda_{g}&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

Indeed, if we denote
E_{i}:=\exp(\kappa_{i}x+\kappa_{i}^{2}y+\kappa_{i}^{3}t)
, plugging the parameterization in
\theta_{\mathcal{C}}({\bf u}x+{\bf v}y+{\bf w}t)
we obtain:

\tilde{\tau}_{\mathcal{C}}(x,y,t)\,=\,\frac{1}{E_{2}E_{4}\cdots E_{2g}}\biggl(\displaystyle\sum_{I}\displaystyle\prod_{i<j\in I}(\kappa_{i}-\kappa_{j})\displaystyle\prod_{\{i\,:\,c_{i}=1\}}\lambda_{i}\prod_{i\in I}E_{i}\biggr),

(14)

where the sets
I
defining the sum are the same ones defined in (
9
). The extraneous exponential factor
(E_{2}E_{4}\cdots E_{2g})^{-1}
disappears after
we pass from
\tilde{\tau}(x,y,t)
to
\partial_{x}^{2}\,{\rm log}(\tilde{\tau}(x,y,t))
. Both
versions of the
(g,2g)
-soliton satisfy the Hirota’s bilinear form and they represent the same solution to the KP equation. Hence, it follows that the image of
\mathbb{C}^{3g}
through the map
\phi
is contained in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Furthermore, the map
\phi
is invertible outside the closed set where the
u_{i}
’s vanish: given a point
(\mathbf{a,(u,v,w)})
in the image, one can write

\kappa_{2i-1}\,=\,\frac{u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}}{2u_{i}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\kappa_{2i}\,=\,\frac{v_{i}-u_{i}^{2}}{2u_{i}},

(15)

and the
\lambda_{i}
’s can be obtained sequentially, starting from
\lambda_{1}
, by plugging in the values for the
\kappa
variables into the
a_{i}
’s. Hence, we can conclude that the map
\phi
is birational. This implies that the closure of the image is irreducible and has dimension
3g
.
∎

Notice that the method above gives a way to parameterize solutions arising from a genus
g
rational nodal curve as
(g,2g)
-solitons. This is also consistent with
[
5
, Example 29]
.

In what follows, we show that
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
is an irreducible component of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
whose points correspond to genus
g
rational nodal curves. This is equivalent to solving a version of the
weak Schottky problem
. In fact,
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
parameterizes some solutions to the KP equation arising from irreducible rational nodal curves of genus
g
, and hence corresponds to a variety containing the locus of Jacobians of such curves as an irreducible component.

Theorem 3
.

For genus
g\leq 9
, the subvariety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
is an irreducible component of the Hirota variety.

Proof.

The proof is mainly computational. A direct computation performed in
Macaulay2
shows that the Jacobian matrix of the Hirota variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
evaluated at the image of a general point in
\mathbb{C}^{3g}
through
\phi
has rank
r-(3g)
, where
r
is the dimension of the space
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2^{g}}\times\mathbb{W}\mathbb{P}^{3g-1}
. Therefore, the map
\phi
is dominant into the main component
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}^{M}
.
∎

The code used for the proof above can be found at (
12
).
The rapid increase in the number of variables
{\bf a}=(a_{\mathbf{c}_{1}},a_{\mathbf{c}_{2}},\dots,a_{\mathbf{c}_{2^{g}}})
makes the computation difficult for higher genera. One possible way to compute the ideal defining the variety
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}
is to use the condition provided by the Hirota’s bilinear form (
3

Lemma 11
.

The closure of the image of the map

Recall that the

We will now use the linear algebra fact that for a symmetric

\left(\,\sum_{i\in I}{e}_{i}\right)R\,\biggl(\,\sum_{j\in J}{e}_{j}\biggr)^{T}=\sum_{i\in I,j\in J}R_{ij}

Therefore, we have

a_{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{k}}a_{\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{\ell}}\,\,=\,\,\exp\biggl[\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in D_{1}\cup E}R_{ij}\biggr]\exp\biggl[\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in D_{2}\cup E}R_{ij}\biggr]\,\,=\,\,\exp\biggl[\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in D\cup E}R_{ij}\biggr]\exp\biggl[\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j\in E}R_{ij}\biggr],

which one can easily see is a multiple of
\exp[\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\{i,j\in D\}}R_{ij}]=a_{{\mathbf{c}}_{k}}a_{{\mathbf{c}}_{\ell}}
which only depends on the sets
D
and
E
, as desired.
∎

(25)

]
