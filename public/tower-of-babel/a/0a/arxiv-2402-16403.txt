Entropy production for diffusion processes across a semipermeable interface
Paul C. Bressloff

Abstract
The emerging field of stochastic thermodynamics extends classical ideas of entropy, heat and work to non-equilibrium systems. One notable finding is that the second law of thermodynamics typically only holds after taking appropriate averages with respect to an ensemble of stochastic trajectories. The resulting average rate of entropy production then quantifies the degree of departure from thermodynamic equilibrium. In this paper we investigate how the presence of a semipermeable interface increases the average entropy production of a single diffusing particle. Starting from the Gibbs-Shannon entropy for the particle probability density, we show that a semipermeable interface or membrane 𝒮 {\mathcal{S}} increases the average rate of entropy production by an amount that is equal to the product of the flux through the interface and the logarithm of the ratio of the probability density on either side of the interface, integrated along 𝒮 {\mathcal{S}} . The entropy production rate thus vanishes at thermodynamic equilibrium, but can be nonzero during the relaxation to equilibrium, or if there exists a nonzero stationary equilibrium state (NESS). We illustrate the latter using the example of diffusion with stochastic resetting on a circle, and show that the average rate of interfacial entropy production is a nonmonotonic function of the resetting rate and the permeability. Finally, we give a probabilistic interpretation of the interfacial entropy production rate using so-called snapping out Brownian motion. This also allows us to construct a stochastic version of entropy production.

Introduction

In recent years there has been a rapid growth of interest in stochastic thermodynamics, which uses tools from the theory of stochastic processes to extend classical ideas of entropy, heat and work to non-equilibrium systems

;

;

One characteristic feature of such systems is that the second law of thermodynamics typically only holds after taking appropriate averages with respect to an ensemble of stochastic trajectories or over long time intervals. The resulting average rate of entropy production then
quantifies the degree of departure from thermodynamic equilibrium. In addition, probabilistic methods such as Itô stochastic calculus, path integrals and Radon-Nikodym derivatives have been used to derive a variety of important fluctuation relations from the stochastic entropy evaluated along individual trajectories
Seifert05
;
Chetrite08
;
Sevick08
;
Jarzynski11
. These fluctuation relations have subsequently been generalized using martingale theory

;
Pigliotti17
;

;
Neri19
;
Gal21
;
Roldan24
.

In this paper we consider the following problem: how does the presence of a semipermeable interface contribute to the average entropy production rate of a single diffusing particle? Diffusion through semipermeable membranes has a wide range of applications, including molecular transport through biological membranes
Philips12
;
Nik21
;
Bressloff22
, diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI)

;
Coy94
;
Grebenkov10
, drug delivery
Pontrelli07
;
Todo13
, reverse osmosis

, and animal dispersal in heterogeneous landscapes
Beyer16
;
Assis19
;
Kenkre21
. At the macroscopic level, the classical boundary condition for a semipermeable membrane takes the particle flux across the membrane to be continuous and to be proportional to the difference in concentrations on either side of the barrier. The constant of proportionality
\kappa_{0}
is known as the permeability. The semipermeable boundary conditions are a particular version of the thermodynamically-derived Kedem-Katchalsky (KK) equations
Kedem58
;
Kedem62
;
Kargol96
;
Aho16
. At the single-particle level, the resulting diffusion equation can be reinterpreted as the Fokker-Planck (FP) equation for the particle probability density, which is supplemented by the interfacial boundary conditions. In particular, suppose that a semipermeable interface
{\mathcal{S}}
partitions
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
into two complementary domains
\Omega_{\pm}
with
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}=\Omega_{+}\cup\Omega_{-}\cup{\mathcal{S}}
. If
{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{y},t)
denotes the continuous flux across a point
\mathbf{y}\in{\mathcal{S}}
from
\Omega_{-}
to
\Omega_{+}
, then
{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{y},t)=\kappa_{0}\ [p_{(}\mathbf{y}^{-},t)-p(\mathbf{y}^{+},t)]/2
, where
p(\mathbf{y}^{\pm},t)
are the solutions on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\pm}=\overline{\Omega_{\pm}}\cap{\mathcal{S}}
, where
\overline{\Omega}
denotes closure of a set
\Omega

Starting from the Gibbs-Shannon entropy for the particle probability density
p(\mathbf{x},t)
,
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we show in Sect. II that a semipermeable interface
{\mathcal{S}}
increases the average rate of entropy production at a given time
t
by an amount
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)=\int_{{\mathcal{S}}}{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{y},t)\ln[p(\mathbf{y}^{-},t)/p(\mathbf{y}^{+},t)]d\mathbf{y}
. In other words,
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)
is equal to the product of the flux through the interface and the logarithm of the ratio of the probability density on either side of the interface, integrated along
{\mathcal{S}}
. It immediately follows that the entropy production rate vanishes at thermodynamic equilibrium, but can be nonzero during the relaxation to equilibrium. We illustrate the theory by calculating the average rate of entropy production for one-dimensional (1D) diffusion. In the 1D case,
{\mathcal{S}}
reduces to a single point
x=0
, say, so that
\Omega_{-}=(-\infty,0^{-}]
and
\Omega_{+}=[0^{+},\infty)
.

In Sect. III we turn to a well-studied mechanism for maintaining a diffusing particle out of thermodynamic equilibrium, namely, stochastic resetting. Resetting was originally introduced within the context of a Brownian particle whose position
\mathbf{X}(t)
instantaneously resets to a fixed position
\mathbf{x}_{0}
at a sequence of times generated from a Poisson process of constant rate
r

Evans11a
;
Evans11b
;
Evans14
. There have subsequently been a wide range of generalizations at the single particle level, see the review
Evans20
and references therein. One of the signature features of diffusion processes with resetting is that there typically exists a nonequilibrium stationary state (NESS) that supports nonzero time-independent fluxes. A number of recent studies have considered the stochastic thermodynamics of diffusive systems with resetting
Fuchs16
;
Pal17
;
Busiello20
;
Gupta20
;
Pal21
;
Boyer21
;
Boyer22
. One issue that emerges from these studies is that sharp resetting to a single point is a unidirectional process that has
no time-reversed equivalent. This means that the average rate of entropy production calculated using the Gibbs-Shannon entropy cannot be related to the degree of time-reversal symmetry breaking. This connection can be established by considering resetting to a random position
Mori23
or Brownian motion in an intermittent harmonic confining potential
Alston22
. An additional subtle feature arises when considering the effects of resetting in the presence of a semipermeable interface
Bressloff22b
;
Grebenkov22
;
Bressloff23a
. In particular, it is natural to assume that the interface screens out resetting, in the sense that a resetting event cannot cross the interface. This means that a particle on one side of the interface
\partial{\mathcal{M}}
cannot reset to a point on the other side. Hence, it is not possible to have a non-zero stationary flux across the interface, since there is no countervailing reset current in the opposite direction. We bypass the screening effect in Sect. III by considering the example of single-particle diffusion on a ring with both stochastic resetting and a semipermeable interface. We derive an explicit expression for the resulting NESS and use this to calculate the various contributions to the average rate of entropy production in the stationary state, including those associated with resetting as well as those arising from the semipermeable interface.

Finally, in Sect. IV we present a probabilistic interpretation of
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}
based on so-called snapping out BM
Lejay16
;
Lejay18
;
Bressloff22a
;
Bressloff23
. The latter generates individual stochastic trajectories of the dynamics by sewing together successive rounds of partially reflected BMs that are restricted to either the left or right of the barrier. Each round is killed (absorbed) at the barrier when its boundary local time exceeds an exponential random variable parameterized by the permeability
\kappa_{0}
. (The local time is a Brownian functional that specifies the contact time between a particle and a given boundary
Levy40
;
Ito63
;
Dynkin65
;
McKean75
;
Grebenkov06
.) A new round is then immediately started in either direction with equal probability. It is the random switching after each killing event that is the source of the entropy production. We also use snapping out BM to construct a stochastic version of entropy production. Averaging the latter with respect to the distribution of sample paths recovers the results based on the Gibbs-Shannon entropy.

II.1
Diffusion in
{\mathbb{R}}

Consider an overdamped Brownian particle diffusing in a 1D domain with a semipermeable barrier or interface at
x=0
. Suppose that the particle is also subject to a force
F(x,t)
. Let
p(x,t)
denote the probability density of the particle at position
x
at time
t
. The corresponding FP equation takes the form

\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial J(x,t)}{\partial x},\quad x\neq 0,\quad t>0,

(2.1a)

with the probability flux

J(x,t)=-D\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial x}+\frac{1}{\gamma}F(x,t)p(x,t),

(2.1b)

and the following pair of boundary conditions at the interface:

J(0^{\pm},t)={\mathcal{J}}(t):=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(0^{-},t)-p(0^{+},t)],

(2.1c)

where
\kappa_{0}
is a constant permeability. (The arbitrary factor of
1/2
on the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.1
c) is motivated by the corresponding probabilistic interpretation of snapping out BM, see Sect. IV.) In addition,
D
is the diffusivity,
\gamma
is the friction coefficient, and the two quantities are related according to the Einstein relation
D\gamma=k_{B}T
. (In the following we set the Boltzmann constant
k_{B}=1
.)
For simplicity, we take the diffusive medium to be spatially homogeneous. However, the domains
(-\infty,0^{-}]
and
[0^{+},\infty)
could have different diffusivities, for example. That is,
D=D_{-}
for
x<0
and
D=D_{+}
for
x>0
with
D_{-}\neq D_{+}
; the drag coefficients would also differ due to the Einstein relation. Finally, we assume that the force
F(x,t)
is continuous at the interface.

In order to evaluate various thermodynamic quantities such as the average heat, work and entropy, we have to integrate with respect to
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Since the probability density has a discontinuity at
x=0
, we partition each integral into the two domains
(-\infty,0^{-}]
and
[0^{+},\infty)
, and introduce the notation

\fint dx=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}dx.

(2.2)

Continuity of the flux at
x=0
means that in most cases there is no contribution to the integral from the discontinuity. One notable exception occurs when we evaluate the average rate of entropy production.

In the 1D case we can always write the force as a gradient of a potential,
F(x,t)=-\partial_{x}V(x,t)
. This means that the average internal energy is

{\mathcal{E}}(t)=\fint dx\,p(x,t)V(x,t),

(2.3)

and

\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathcal{E}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\fint dx\,\left[\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}V(x,t)+\frac{\partial V(x,t)}{\partial t}p(x,t)\right].

(2.4)

Using Eqs. (
2.1
a,b) and integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}dx\,\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}V(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{0}dx\,\frac{\partial V(x,t)}{\partial x}J(x,t)-J(0^{-},t)V(0,t)

(2.5)

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}V(x,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\partial V(x,t)}{\partial t}J(x,t)+J(0^{+},t)V(0,t).

(2.6)

Imposing flux continuity at the interface leads to a nonequilibrium version of the first law of thermodynamics:

\frac{d{\mathcal{E}}}{dt}=\frac{d{\mathcal{W}}}{dt}-\frac{dQ}{dt},

(2.7)

where
{\mathcal{W}}(t)
is the average work done on the particle and
Q(t)
is the average heat dissipated into the environment with

\displaystyle\frac{dQ}{dt}

\displaystyle=-\fint dx\,\frac{\partial V(x,t)}{\partial x}J(x,t),

(2.8)

and

\displaystyle\frac{d{\mathcal{W}}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\fint dx\,\frac{\partial V(x,t)}{\partial t}p(x,t).

(2.9)

If the potential is time-independent then we have the further simplification that there exists a unique equilibrium stationary state given by the Boltzmann-Gibbs distribution,

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}p(x,t)=\frac{1}{Z}{\rm e}^{-V(x)/T},\quad\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}J(x,t)=0.

(2.10)

Since there are no fluxes at equilibrium, the semipermeable membrane becomes invisible, that is, it has no effect on the stationary state.

and (b) various

II.2
Average entropy production

The average system entropy at time
t
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm sys}(t):

\displaystyle=-\fint dx\,p(x,t)\ln p(x,t),

(2.11)

which takes the form of a Gibbs-Shannon entropy.
In order to calculate the average rate of entropy production, we differentiate both sides of Eq. (
2.11
) with respect to time
t
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm sys}(t)}{dt}=-\fint dx\,\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial t}[1+\ln p(x,t)].

(2.12)

Using the FP equation and performing an integration by parts, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)=\fint dx\,\frac{\partial J(x,t)}{\partial x}[1+\ln p(x,t)]

\displaystyle=-\fint dx\,\frac{J(x,t)}{p(x,t)}\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial x}

(2.13)

\displaystyle\quad+J(0^{-},t))[1+\ln p(0^{-},t)]-J(0^{+},t))[1+\ln p(0^{+},t)].

Using the definitions of the probability fluxes, the integrand can be rewritten as

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{p(x,t)}\frac{\partial p(x,t)}{\partial x}J(x,t)=\frac{J(x,t)^{2}}{Dp(x,t)}-\frac{F(x,t)J(x,t)}{T}.

(2.14)

In addition, imposing the permeability conditions shows that

\displaystyle J(0^{-},t))[1+\ln p(0^{-},t)]-J(0^{+},t))[1+\ln p(0^{+},t)]

\displaystyle\qquad={\mathcal{J}}(t)\ln[p(0^{-},t)/p(0^{+},t)].

(2.15)

We thus obtain a generalization of the classical entropy production rate given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm tot}(t)

\displaystyle:={\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)+{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm env}(t)

\displaystyle=\fint dx\,\frac{J(x,t)^{2}}{Dp(x,t)}+{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t),

(2.16)

where

{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(0^{-},t)-p(0^{+},t)]\ln\left[\frac{p(0^{-},t)}{p(0^{+},t)}\right]

(2.17)

is the contribution to the entropy production from the semipermeable membrane, and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm env}(t):=\frac{1}{T}\frac{dQ}{dt}=\fint dx\,\frac{F(x,t)J(x,t)}{T}

(2.18)

is the average environmental entropy production rate due to heat dissipation, see Eq. (
2.8
). Since
[p(0^{-},t)-p(0^{+},t)]
and
\ln[p(0^{-},t)/\ln p(0^{+},t)]
have the same sign, it follows that
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)\geq 0
, and hence the average total entropy production rate satisfies the second law of thermodynamics in the sense that

{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)+{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm env}(t)\geq 0,\quad t\geq 0.

(2.19)

The existence of the contribution
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}
is one of the main results of our paper. We will give a physical interpretation of this result in Sect. IV.

II.3
Interfacial entropy production for pure diffusion

In the particular case of a time-independent force
F(x)=-V^{\prime}(x)
, the rate of entropy production vanishes in the limit
t\rightarrow\infty
since there are no fluxes at equilibrium. However,
{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm tot}(t)>0
at finite times
t
. This result holds even for pure diffusion, where
an explicit solution of Eqs. (
2.1
) can be obtained. The simplest way to proceed is to Laplace transform Eqs. (
2.1
), under the initial condition
p(x,0)=\delta(x-x_{0})
. For the sake of illustration we take
x_{0}>0
. It follows that

\widetilde{p}(x,s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-st}p(x,t)dt=G(x,x_{0};s),

(2.20)

where
G(x,_{0};s)
is the Green’s function of the modified Helmholtz equation

\frac{d^{2}G}{dx^{2}}-sG(x,x_{0};s)=-\delta(x-x_{0}),

(2.21a)

supplemented by the interfacial conditions

\displaystyle-D\frac{dG(x,x_{0};s)}{dx}|_{x=0^{-}}=-D\frac{dG(x,x_{0};s)}{dx}|_{x=0^{+}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[G(0^{-},x_{0};s)-G(0^{+},x_{0};s)],

(2.21b)

and with
\lim_{x\rightarrow\pm\infty}G(x,x_{0};s)=0
.

Eq. (
2.21
a) has the general solution

G(x,x_{0};s)=\frac{{\rm e}^{\sqrt{s/D}|x-x_{0}|}}{2\sqrt{sD}}+A(s){\rm e}^{\sqrt{s/D}x}+B(s){\rm e}^{-\sqrt{s/D}x},

(2.22)

with the pair of coefficients
A(s)
and
B(s)
determined by the supplementary conditions.
Finally, inverting the resulting solution in Laplace space gives

\displaystyle p(x,t)

(2.23a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{4Dt}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{(x+x_{0})^{2}}{4Dt}\right)\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2D}\exp\left(\frac{\kappa_{0}}{D}(x+x_{0}+\kappa_{0}t)\right)\mbox{erfc}\left(\frac{(x+x_{0}+2\kappa_{0}t}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right),

for
x>0
and

\displaystyle p(x,t)

(2.23b)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2D}\exp\left(\frac{\kappa_{0}}{D}(x_{0}-x+\kappa_{0}t)\right)\mbox{erfc}\left(\frac{x_{0}-x+2\kappa_{0}t}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)

for
x<0
.
The complementary error function is

\mbox{erfc}(x):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{x}^{\infty}{\rm e}^{-y^{2}}dy

(2.24)

In the limit
\kappa_{0}\rightarrow 0
, we see that
p(x,t)\rightarrow 0
for
x<0
and
p(x,t)\rightarrow p_{+}(x,t)
for
x>0
, with

\displaystyle p_{+}(x,t)

(2.25)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi Dt}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{(x-x_{0})^{2}}{4Dt}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{(x+x_{0})^{2}}{4Dt}\right)\right].

This is consistent with the fact that the interface becomes completely impermeable in the limit
\kappa_{0}\rightarrow 0
and the particle started to the right of the interface. We thus recover the solution of the diffusion equation on the half-line with a totally reflecting boundary at
x=0
. In order to determine what happens in the limit
\kappa_{0}\rightarrow\infty
, we use
the asymptotic expansion

\mbox{erfc}(x)\sim\frac{{\rm e}^{-x^{2}}}{\sqrt{\pi}x}\left[1-\frac{1}{2x^{2}}+\ldots\right].

(2.26)

We find that the interface becomes completely transparent and
p(x,t)
is given by the classical solution of free diffusion in
{\mathbb{R}}
. In Fig.
2
we show sample plots of the interfacial entropy production rate
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)
, see Eq. (
2.17
), as a function of time
t
for various initial positions
x_{0}
and permeabilities
\kappa_{0}
. As expected, the effects of the interface at
x=0
are greater when
x_{0}
is closer to the origin. In addition, increasing the permeability
\kappa_{0}
increases the initial rise of
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)
but the effects of the interface decay more quickly.

II.4
Diffusion across a closed semipermeable membrane in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

The expression (
2.17
) for the average rate of entropy production through a semipermeable interface generalizes to higher spatial dimensions. Suppose that
{\mathcal{M}}
denotes a closed bounded domain
{\mathcal{M}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with a smooth concave boundary
\partial{\mathcal{M}}
separating the two open domains
{\mathcal{M}}
and its complement
{\mathcal{M}}^{c}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\backslash{\mathcal{M}}
, see Fig.
3
. The boundary acts as a semipermeable interface with
\partial{\mathcal{M}}_{+}
(
\partial{\mathcal{M}}_{-}
) denoting the side approached from outside (inside)
{\mathcal{M}}
. Let
p(\mathbf{x},t)
denote the probability density function of an overdamped Brownian particle subject to a force field
{\bf F}(\mathbf{x})
. The multi-dimensional analog of the FP Eq. is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=-{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t),\ \mathbf{x}\in{\mathcal{M}}\cup{\mathcal{M}}^{c},

(2.27a)

\displaystyle{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=-D{\bm{\nabla}}p(\mathbf{x},t)+\frac{1}{\gamma}{\bf F}(\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x},t),

(2.27b)

\displaystyle{\bf J}(\mathbf{y}^{\pm},t)\cdot{\bf n}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{y},t),\quad\mathbf{y}\in\partial{\mathcal{M}},

(2.27c)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{y},t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(\mathbf{y}^{-},t)-p(\mathbf{y}^{+},t)],\quad\mathbf{y}\in\partial{\mathcal{M}},

(2.27d)

where
\mathbf{n}
is the unit normal directed out of
{\mathcal{M}}
.

The average system entropy at time
t
is defined by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm sys}(t):

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{x}\,p(\mathbf{x},t)\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{{\mathcal{M}}^{c}}d\mathbf{x}\,p(\mathbf{x},t)\ln p(\mathbf{x},t).

(2.28)

Differentiating both sides of Eq. (
II.4
) with respect to time
t
gives

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)

\displaystyle:=\frac{d{\mathcal{S}}^{\rm sys}(t)}{dt}=-\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{x}\,\,\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}[1+\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)]

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{{\mathcal{M}}^{c}}d\mathbf{x}\,\frac{\partial p(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}[1+\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)].

(2.29)

Using the FP Eq. (
2.27
) and performing an integration by parts according to the divergence theorem, we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)=\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{x}\,[1+\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)]{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\int_{{\mathcal{M}}^{c}}d\mathbf{x}\,[1+\ln p(\mathbf{x},t)]{\bm{\nabla}}\cdot{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{x}\,{\bm{\nabla}}p(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\frac{{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)}{p(\mathbf{x},t)}-\int_{{\mathcal{M}}^{c}}d\mathbf{x}\,{\bm{\nabla}}p(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\frac{{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)}{p(\mathbf{x},t)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\partial{\mathcal{M}}_{-}}d\mathbf{y}\,[1+\ln p(\mathbf{y},t)]{\bf J}(\mathbf{y},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{\partial{\mathcal{M}}_{+}}d\mathbf{y}\,[1+\ln p(\mathbf{y},t)]{\bf J}(\mathbf{y},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}.

(2.30)

We now decompose the various contributions to
{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}
along analogous lines to the 1D case. This leads to the higher dimensional version of Eq. (
2.16
):

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm tot}(t):={\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)+{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm env}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{x}\,\frac{{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)^{2}}{Dp(\mathbf{x},t)}+\int_{{\mathcal{M}}^{c}}d\mathbf{x}\,\frac{{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)^{2}}{Dp(\mathbf{x},t)}+{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t),

(2.31)

where

{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)=\int_{\partial{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{y}\,{\mathcal{J}}(\mathbf{y},t)\ln\left[\frac{p(\mathbf{y}^{-},t)}{p(\mathbf{y}^{+},t)}\right]

(2.32)

is the contribution to the entropy production from the semipermeable membrane, and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm env}(t)=\frac{1}{T}\frac{dQ}{dt}

(2.33)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathcal{M}}}d\mathbf{x}\,\frac{{\bf F}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)}{T}+\int_{{\mathcal{M}}^{c}}d\mathbf{x}\,\frac{{\bf F}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot{\bf J}(\mathbf{x},t)}{T}.

II.5
Imperfect contacts and the chemical potential

A classical generalization of the permeable boundary condition (
2.1
c), or its higher-dimensional generalization (
2.27
c), is to include a directional asymmetry in the permeability, which can be interpreted as a step discontinuity in a chemical potential
Kedem58
;
Kedem62
;
Kargol96
;
Farago20
:

J(0^{\pm},t)={\mathcal{J}}(t):=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(0^{-},t)-\sigma p(0^{+},t)]

(2.34)

for
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
. This tends to enhance the concentration to the right of the interface. (If
\sigma>1
then we would have an interface with permeability
\kappa_{0}\sigma
and bias
1/\sigma
to the left.) The derivation of the average entropy production rate for a 1D diffusive process proceeds along similar lines as the symmetric case, leading to Eq. (
2.16
). However, the interfacial contribution (
2.17
) now takes the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(0^{-},t)-\sigma p(0^{+},t)]\ln\left[\frac{p(0^{-},t)}{p(0^{+},t)}\right],

(2.35)

which is not necessarily positive. In order to obtain the correct second law of thermodynamics, we decompose
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)

\displaystyle={\mathcal{I}}_{\sigma}(t)+\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(0^{-},t)-\sigma p(0^{+},t)]\ln\sigma,

(2.36)

with

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{\sigma}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{0}}{2}[p(0^{-},t)-\sigma p(0^{+},t)]\ln\left[\frac{p(0^{-},t)}{\sigma p(0^{+},t)}\right]\geq 0.

(2.37)

Suppose that there is a discontinuity in the chemical potential across the interface, with
\mu=\mu_{-}
for
x<0
and
\mu=\mu_{+}
for
x>0
. We then make the identification
\sigma={\rm e}^{(\mu_{+}-\mu_{-})/T}
with
\mu_{+}<\mu_{-}
for
\sigma<1
, such that

{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)={\mathcal{I}}_{\sigma}(t)+{\mathcal{J}}(t)\frac{\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}}{T},

(2.38)

The second term on the right-hand side represents the rate of reduction in the free energy due to the probability flux
{\mathcal{J}}(t)
from a region with a high chemical potential
\mu_{-}
to a region with a low chemical potential
\mu_{+}
. This change in free energy contributes to the heat dissipated into the environment. Hence, redefining the environmental entropy according to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm env}(t)

\displaystyle=\fint dx\,\frac{F(x,t)J(x,t)}{T}+{\mathcal{J}}(t)\frac{\mu_{-}-\mu_{+}}{T},

(2.39)

we obtain the modified second law of thermodynamics

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm tot}(t)=\fint dx\,\frac{J(x,t)^{2}}{Dp(x,t)}+{\mathcal{I}}_{\sigma}(t)\geq 0.

(2.40)

III
Stochastic resetting on circle with a semipermeable interface

We now turn to an example that supports an NESS in the large time limit by combining diffusion across a semipermeable interface with stochastic resetting. In previous work we considered scenarios similar to the one shown in Fig. 4(a)
Bressloff22b
;
Bressloff23a
. We assumed that the semipermeable interface acts as a screen for resetting in the sense that a particle located in
{\mathcal{M}}
cannot reset to a point
\mathbf{x}_{0}\in{\mathcal{M}}^{c}
and vice versa. This means that although the NESS is characterized by non-zero stationary fluxes in both domains, the net flux across the interface is zero. The last result can be understood as follows. Suppose, for concreteness, that the stationary interfacial flux
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}
is positive so that there is a net flow of probability from
{\mathcal{M}}
to
{\mathcal{M}}^{c}
. The screening effect of the interface means that there is no reset flux in the opposite direction, which is impossible for a stationary state with
p^{*}(\mathbf{x})>0
unless
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}=0
. The vanishing of the interfacial flux can also be confirmed by explicitly calculating the NESS
Bressloff22b
;
Bressloff23a
.

Therefore, in contrast to our previous work, we consider a Brownian particle diffusing on a circle of circumference
L
. The circle is topologically equivalent to a finite interval
[0,L]
with a semipermeable interface at
x=\{0^{+},L^{-}\}
. The particle is taken to reset at a rate
r
to a random position
y\in(0,L)
generated from the density
\sigma_{0}(y)

\sigma_{0}(y)
does not have to be a periodic function. In Fig.

x_{0}

\sigma_{0}(y)=\delta(y-x_{0})

X(t)\in[0^{+},x_{0})
and resets in the clockwise direction when
X(t)\in(x_{0},L^{-}]
. For a random reset location,
the probability density
p(x,t)
,
x\in[0,L]
, evolves according to
the equation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=D\frac{\partial^{2}p(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}-rp(x,t)+r\sigma_{0}(x),

(3.1a)

together with the interfacial conditions

\displaystyle-D\frac{\partial p(0^{+},t)}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=-D\frac{\partial p(L^{-},t)}{\partial x}={\mathcal{J}}(t),

(3.1b)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{J}}(t)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{0}[p(L^{-},t)-p(0^{+},t)].

(3.1c)

(A factor of
1/2
has been absorbed into
\kappa_{0}
.)

III.1
Average entropy production

We begin by calculating the average rate of entropy production, and show that there are contributions from the interface and the resetting protocol, We then calculate these contributions in the NESS. Substituting Eqs. (
3.1
a) into the formula (
2.12
) for the average rate of entropy production gives

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\frac{\partial J(x,t)}{\partial x}[1+\ln p(x,t)]+{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t),

(3.2)

where
J(x,t)=-D\partial p(x,t)/\partial x
and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=r\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\ [p(x,t)-\sigma_{0}(x)]\ln p(x,t).

(3.3)

The integral on the right-hand side of Eq. (
3.2
) can be analyzed along identical lines to the case of no resetting. Since there are no forces, we find that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\frac{J(x,t)^{2}}{Dp(x,t)}+{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)+r{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t),

(3.4)

with

{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)=\kappa_{0}[p(L^{-},t)-p(0^{+},t)]\ln\left[\frac{p(L^{-},t)}{p(0^{+},t)}\right].

(3.5)

Hence, there are contributions from both the semipermeable interface and resetting.

In the special case
\sigma_{0}(x)=\delta(x-x_{0})
(resetting to a fixed location
x_{0}
), we have

{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t)=r\int_{0}^{L}dx\,p(x,t)\ln p(x,t)-r\ln p(x_{0},t).

(3.6)

Following along analogous lines to Ref.
Seifert12
we define
{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm reset}(t)=-{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t)
and rewrite Eq. (
3.4
) as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)+{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm reset}(t)=\int_{0}^{L}dx\,\frac{J(x,t)^{2}}{Dp(x,t)}+{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)\geq 0.

(3.7)

As highlighted in the introduction, resetting to a single point is a unidirectional process that has
no time-reversed equivalent. This means that the average rate of entropy production calculated using the Gibbs-Shannon entropy cannot be related to the degree of time-reversal symmetry breaking.
On the other hand, as shown in Ref.
Mori23
, such a connection can be made in the case of a regular resetting density
\sigma_{0}(x)>0
. The contribution
{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t)
is now decomposed as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}_{r}(t)

\displaystyle=K[p(\cdot,t)|\sigma_{0}]+K[\sigma_{0}|p(\cdot,t)]

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{L}dx\,[\sigma_{0}(x)-p(x,t)]\ln\sigma_{0}(x),

(3.8)

where
K[p|q]
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence of any two measures
p,q
on
{\mathbb{R}}
:

K[p|q]=\int dx\,p(x)\ln[p(x)/q(x)].

(3.9)

Using Jensen’s inequality for convex functions it is straightforward to prove that
K[p|q]\geq 0
. Hence, we can rewrite the entropy production equation as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm sys}(t)+{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm res}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}\frac{J(x,t)^{2}}{Dp(x,t)}+{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}(t)+r(K[p(\cdot,t)|\sigma_{0}]+K[\sigma_{0}|p(\cdot,t)])

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.10)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm res}(t)

\displaystyle=r\int_{0}^{L}dx\,[\sigma_{0}(x)-p(x,t)]\ln\sigma_{0}(x).

III.2
Non-equilibrium stationary state (NESS)

In contrast to the system without resetting, there exists a nonequilibrium stationary state (NESS) for which there are non-zero stationary fluxes
J^{*}(x)
and
{\mathcal{J}}^{*}
, both of which contribute to the average rate of entropy production.
Setting all time derivatives to zero in Eqs. (
3.1
) gives

\displaystyle D\frac{d^{2}p^{*}(x)}{dx^{2}}-rp^{*}(x)=-r\sigma_{0}(x),

(3.11a)

\displaystyle-D\frac{dp^{*}(0)}{dx}=-D\frac{dp^{*}(L)}{dx}={\mathcal{J}}^{*}=\kappa_{0}[p^{*}(L)-p^{*}(0)].

(3.11b)

For the moment, suppose that
\sigma_{0}(x)=\delta(x-x_{0})
. The general solution of Eq. (
3.11
a) then takes the form

\displaystyle p^{*}=p_{-}^{*}(x,x_{0})

\displaystyle=A_{1}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{\eta x}+B_{1}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{-\eta x},\quad 0<x<x_{0}

(3.12a)

\displaystyle p^{*}=p_{+}^{*}(x,x_{0})

\displaystyle=A_{2}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{\eta x}+B_{2}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{-\eta x},\quad x_{0}<x<L,

(3.12b)

with
\eta=\sqrt{r/D}
. There are four unknown coefficients and four supplementary conditions. The first pair arises from the conditions at
x_{0}
:

\displaystyle p_{-}^{*}(x_{0},x_{0})=p_{+}^{*}(x_{0},x_{0}),

(3.13a)

\displaystyle\left.D\frac{dp_{+}^{*}(x,x_{0})}{dx}\right|_{x=x_{0}}-\left.D\frac{dp_{-}^{*}(x,x_{0})}{dx}\right|_{x=x_{0}}=-r/D,

(3.13b)

that is

\displaystyle A_{2}{\rm e}^{\eta x_{0}}+B_{2}{\rm e}^{-\eta x_{0}}=A_{1}{\rm e}^{\eta x_{0}}+B_{1}{\rm e}^{-\eta x_{0}},

(3.14a)

\displaystyle A_{2}{\rm e}^{\eta x_{0}}-B_{2}{\rm e}^{-\eta x_{0}}=A_{1}{\rm e}^{\eta x_{0}}-B_{1}{\rm e}^{-\eta x_{0}}-\frac{r}{\eta D}.

(3.14b)

The second pair follow from the interfacial conditions (
3.11
b):

\displaystyle A_{2}{\rm e}^{\eta L}-B_{2}{\rm e}^{-\eta L}=A_{1}-B_{1},

(3.15a)

\displaystyle A_{2}{\rm e}^{\eta L}+B_{2}{\rm e}^{-\eta L}=\left(1-\frac{D\eta}{\kappa_{0}}\right)A_{1}+\left(1+\frac{D\eta}{\kappa_{0}}\right)B_{1}.

(3.15b)

After some algebra we find that

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a_{-}+b_{-})\Gamma_{-}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{\eta L}-(a_{-}-b_{-})\Gamma_{+}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{-\eta L}}{b_{-}a_{+}+b_{+}a_{-}},

(3.16a)

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(a_{+}-b_{+})\Gamma_{-}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{\eta L}-(a_{+}+b_{+})\Gamma_{+}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{-\eta L}}{b_{-}a_{+}+b_{+}a_{-}},

(3.16b)

and

A_{2}(x_{0})=A_{1}(x_{0})-\Gamma_{-}(x_{0}),\quad B_{2}(x_{0})=B_{1}(x_{0})+\Gamma_{+}(x_{0}),

(3.16c)

where

\displaystyle a_{\pm}={\rm e}^{\pm\eta L}-1,\quad b_{\pm}=a_{\pm}\pm\frac{D\eta}{\kappa_{0}},\quad\Gamma_{\pm}(x_{0})=\frac{r}{2\eta D}{\rm e}^{\pm\eta x_{0}}.

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})

\displaystyle=\Lambda_{0}(x_{0}){\rm e}^{-|y-x_{0}|\xi_{0}},

(3.19a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0}(x_{0})

(3.19b)

\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})\rightarrow\delta(x-x_{0})

\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})\rightarrow 1/L

\displaystyle p^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}dy\,p_{+}(x,y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})+\int_{x}^{L}dy\,p_{-}(x,y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})

\displaystyle={\mathcal{A}}(x,x_{0}){\rm e}^{\eta x}+{\mathcal{B}}(x,x_{0}){\rm e}^{-\eta x},

(3.20)

where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}(x,x_{0})

(3.21a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}dy\,A_{1}(y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})-\int_{0}^{x}dy\,\Gamma_{-}(y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})

(3.21b)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{L}dy\,B_{1}(y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})+\int_{0}^{x}dy\,\Gamma_{+}(y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0}).

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}dy\,\Gamma_{\pm}(y)\sigma_{0}(y,x_{0})

(3.22)

\displaystyle\quad=f_{\pm}(x):=\frac{\sqrt{r/D}\Lambda_{0}{\rm e}^{-x_{0}\xi_{0}}}{2}\frac{\left({\rm e}^{x[\pm\eta+\xi_{0}]}-1\right)}{\pm\eta+\xi_{0}}

for

(3.23)

\displaystyle\quad=f_{\pm}(x_{0})+\frac{\sqrt{r/D}\Lambda_{0}{\rm e}^{x_{0}\xi_{0}}}{2}\frac{\left({\rm e}^{x[\pm\eta-\xi_{0}]}-{\rm e}^{x_{0}[\pm\eta-\xi_{0}]}\right)}{\pm\eta-\xi_{0}}

for

IV
Probabilistic interpretation of the entropy contribution
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}

In Sect. II we analyzed Brownian motion across a semipermeable interface using a Fokker-Planck description of the distribution of sample paths. In order to understand the origins of the interfacial entropy production term
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}
, see Eq. (
2.17
) and its higher-dimensional analog (
2.32
), we turn to a probabilistic description of individual trajectories based on so-called snapping out Brownian motion
Lejay16
;
Lejay18
;
Bressloff22a
;
Bressloff23
. Here we only cover the essential elements necessary for interpreting
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}
in 1D. Extensions of the probabilistic framework to higher-dimensional interfaces can be found in Ref.
Bressloff23
.

IV.1
Snapping out Brownian motion

The dynamics of snapping out BM is formulated in terms of a sequence of killed reflected Brownian motions
in either
\Omega_{-}=(-\infty,0^{-}]
or
\Omega_{+}=[0^{+},\infty)

Lejay16
;
Lejay18
;
Bressloff22a
;
Bressloff23
. Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{n}
denote the time of the
n^{\rm th}
killing (with
{\mathcal{T}}_{0}=0
). Immediately after the killing event, the position of the particle is taken to be

\displaystyle X({\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{+})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left[-Y_{n}\epsilon+(1-Y_{n})\epsilon\right],

(4.1)

where
Y_{n}
is an independent Bernoulli random variable with
\mathbb{P}[Y_{n}=1]=\mathbb{P}[Y_{n}=0]=1/2
. Suppose that
X(t)\in\Omega_{+}
for
t\in({\mathcal{T}}_{n},{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1})
, that is,
X({{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{+}})=0^{+}
, and introduce the boundary local time
Levy40
;
Ito63
;
Dynkin65
;
McKean75
;
Grebenkov06

\displaystyle L_{n}^{+}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{D}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}I\{0\leq X({\mathcal{T}}_{n}+s)\leq\epsilon\}ds,\quad

(4.2)

where
I
is the indicator function. The boundary local time
L_{n}^{+}(t)
is a Brownian functional that tracks the amount of the time the particle is in contact with the right-hand side of the interface over the time interval
[{\mathcal{T}}_{n},t]
. It can be proven that the local time exists, and is a continuous, positive increasing function of time. The SDE for
X(t)
,
t\in({\mathcal{T}}_{n},{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1})
, is given by the so-called Skorokhod equation for reflected BM in the half-line
\Omega_{+}
:

\displaystyle dX(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma}F(X(t))dt+\sqrt{2D}dW(t)+dL_{n}(t)

(4.3)

for
t\in({\mathcal{T}}_{n},{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1})
, where
W(t)
is a Wiener process with
W(0)=0
.
Formally speaking,

dL^{+}_{n}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\delta(X(t)-\epsilon)dt,

(4.4)

so that each time the particle hits the interface it is given a positive impulsive kick back into the domain.
The time of the next killing is then determined by the condition

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1}={\mathcal{T}}_{n}+\inf\left\{t>0,\ L_{n}^{+}(t)\geq\widehat{\ell}\right\},

(4.5)

where
\widehat{\ell}
is an independent randomly generated local time threshold with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\widehat{\ell}>\ell]={\rm e}^{-\kappa_{0}\ell},\ \ell\geq 0.

(4.6)

On the other hand, if
X({{\mathcal{T}}_{n}^{+}})=0^{-}
then the next round of reflected BM takes place in the domain
\Omega_{-}
. The corresponding SDE is

\displaystyle dX(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma}F(X(t))dt+\sqrt{2D}dW(t)-dL_{n}^{-}(t)

(4.7)

with
t\in({\mathcal{T}}_{n},{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1})
,
X(t)\in\Omega_{-}
,

\displaystyle L_{n}^{-}(t)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{D}{\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}I\{-\epsilon\leq X({\mathcal{T}}_{n}+s)\leq 0\}ds

(4.8)

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{n+1}={\mathcal{T}}_{n}+\inf\left\{t>{\mathcal{T}}_{n}:\quad L_{n}^{-}(t)\geq\widehat{\ell}\right\}.

(4.9)

In summary, the dynamics of snapping out BM consists of sewing together successive rounds of reflected BM, each of which evolves according to the SDE (
4.3
) or (
4.7
), see Fig.
10
. Each round is killed when the local time at the right-hand or left-hand side of the interface exceeds an exponentially distributed random threshold. (The threshold is independently generated each round.) Following each round of killing, an unbiased coin is thrown to determine which side of the interface the next round occurs. It is this randomization that accounts for the term
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}
appearing in Eq. (
2.16
). That is,
{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm int}
is given by the product of the flux
{\mathcal{J}}(t)
across the interface, which specifies the effective rate of randomization, and the corresponding entropy difference
\ln p(0^{-},t)-\ln p(0^{+},t)
. Of course, this assumes that snapping out BM generates sample paths whose distribution is given by the solution of the corresponding FP Eq. (
2.1
). We sketch the basic proof based on renewal theory. For an alternative proof in 1D see Ref.
Lejay16
and for the generalization to higher spatial dimensions see Ref.
Bressloff23
.
Let
p(z,t)

V
Discussion

In this paper we showed how the presence of a semipermeable interface
{\mathcal{S}}
increases the average rate of entropy production of a single diffusing particle by an amount that is equal to the product of the flux through the interface and the logarithm of the ratio of the probability density on either side of the interface, integrated along
{\mathcal{S}}
. We illustrated the theory using the example of diffusion with stochastic resetting on a circle, and showed that the average rate of interfacial entropy production in the NESS is a nonmonotonic function of the resetting rate and the permeability. Finally, we presented a probabilistic interpretation of the interfacial entropy production rate that is based on snapping out BM. The latter represents individual stochastic trajectories as sequences of partially reflected BMs that are restricted to one side of the interface or the other. When a given round of partially reflected BM is terminated, a Bernoulli random variable is used to determine which side of the interface the next round takes place. We identified this switching process as the source of interfacial entropy production. Moreover, we showed how a biased switching process is equivalent to a directionally biased interface arising from a jump discontinuity in the chemical potential. The latter contributes to the dissipation of heat into the environment.
Snapping out BM also allowed us to construct a stochastic version of entropy production defined along individual trajectories. Averaging with respect to the distribution of trajectories recovered the expression for the average rate of entropy production obtained from the Gibbs-Shannon entropy.

One direction for future work would be to relate the stochastic entropy for diffusion through a semipermeable interface to the ratio of forward and backward path probabilities
Seifert12
;
Peliti21
. More specifically, a fundamental result of stochastic thermodynamics is that for many continuous stochastic processes, the total stochastic or instantaneous entropy production can be expressed as

S^{\rm tot}(t)=\ln\left[\frac{{\mathcal{P}}[X(\tau),0\leq\tau\leq t]}{{\mathcal{P}}[X(t-\tau),0\leq\tau\leq t]}\right].

(5.1)

Such a relationship provides a basis for deriving a variety of fluctuation relations
Seifert12
;
Peliti21
.
The corresponding average production rate in steady state is then

{\mathcal{R}}^{\rm tot}=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{t}\left\langle\ln\left[\frac{{\mathcal{P}}[X(\tau),0\leq\tau\leq t]}{{\mathcal{P}}[X(t-\tau),0\leq\tau\leq t]}\right]\right\rangle.

(5.2)

One way to establish a result of the form (
5.1
) is to use path integrals. Within a path integral framework, one could treat diffusion as a random walk on a lattice in which a semipermeable barrier is represented in terms of local defects
Powles92
;
Kenkre08
;
Novikov11
;
Kay22
.

Finally, note that in this paper we considered a mesoscopic model of diffusion through a semipermeable interface, which involved phenomenological parameters such as the permeability
\kappa_{0}
and the directional bias
\sigma
. These also appeared as parameters in snapping out BM, with
\kappa_{0}
determining the rate at which each round of partially reflected BM is killed and
\sigma
specifying the bias of the switching Bernoulli process. Another direction of future work would be to develop a microscopic model of a semipermeable interface that identifies the biophysical mechanisms underlying
\kappa_{0}
,
\sigma
, and interfacial entropy production. Along these lines, we have recently proposed a more general model of snapping out BM, in which each round of partially reflected BM is killed according to a more general threshold distribution than the exponential (
4.6
)
Bressloff22a
;
Bressloff23
. The corresponding effective permeability becomes a time-dependent function that can have heavy tails.
