Using Elementary Techniques to Characterize the Relationship Between Wythoff’s Game and the Golden Ratio
Vincent Wang, Nikhil Sampath, Eric Yule, Ethan Wang

Abstract




1
Introduction

Wythoff’s game is a modification of the well-known game of “nim.” Wythoff’s game, which does not resemble the Fibonacci sequence, has direct relation to the Golden ratio. We will explore the sequence behind this surprising relationship, and consider the implications of our elementary methods.

Wythoff’s game is also known as the game of Ps and Qs. The game has two players taking turns removing chips of two types: P and Q (hence the game’s name). Each turn has a player take any number of available Ps (and only Ps), any number of available Qs (and only Qs), or any same number of available Ps and Qs (for example a turn can have 2 Ps and 2 Qs being taken). Players alternate their turns until the player to remove the last chip wins
[
1
]
.

Let us begin with notations and conventions. The first player is the player who took the first turn. The second player is the other player. Denote the state of
x
Ps and
y
Qs by
\left<x,y\right>
. If we start with
\left<1,2\right>
, no matter what turn the first player took, the second player can take a turn to win. We call a state where the second player can force a win a losing state, so that
\left<1,2\right>
is losing. Analogously define a state where the first player can force a win a winning state.

We have some early observations. We observe that “most” states are winning states, which motivates us to find the “fewer” losing states. With some work, we find that
\left<3,5\right>
is the next losing state (because of symmetry of the game with respect to P and Q, take
x<y
). Our final observation is that if
\left<x,y\right>
has the property that exactly one of

or
y
was a component of a previously found losing state
\left<z,w\right>
, we can sometimes reduce
\left<x,y\right>
to
\left<z,w\right>
, making
\left<x,y\right>
a winning state. Wythoff, the mathematician for which the game is named after, characterizes this precisely in his fourth observation

Lemma 7:

Proof of Lemma 7:
Consider the set

[q(n)]

The crux idea of this proof is that

\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, so each positive integer is either a

or a

. We believe that strong theorems can be proven using set constructions exploiting the properties of the elements of that set. Here we used the property that there cannot be two consecutive

s and

Lemma 8:
\Delta p(n)=2
if and only if

is a

for all

Proof of Lemma 8:
We present a non-typical method of proof, which we verify first as follows. Let

\neg a\rightarrow\neg b

as any statement is equivalent to its contrapositive. From this we find

In our proof,

is “

”,

is “

and
\neg a\rightarrow\neg c

Suppose

Corollary 10:
q(p(n))=p(n)+q(n)-1

Proof of Corollary 10:
Follows from

Consider

Corollary 12:
If

We know

Follows from our forms for

4
Linking
p
,
q
, and
\phi

Recall that we had the really motivating conjecture of
p(n)=\left\lfloor\phi n\right\rfloor
and
q(n)=\left\lfloor\phi^{2}n\right\rfloor
. With our knowledge of the behavior of
p
and
q
, we are finally ready to attack this conjecture. Unfortunately, we were unable to show equality. However, we present promising partial work.

Lemma 14:

Define by
E(n)=p(n)-\left\lfloor n\phi\right\rfloor
. Then
E(n)\in\{-1,0,1\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Proof of Lemma 14:

We proceed by strong induction on
n
.

Base case(s):

By direct computation, we have
E(1)=E(2)=E(3)=E(4)=E(5)=E(6)=0\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

Inductive Step:

Assume that the result holds for
n=1,2,3,\dots,k
for some
k\geq 6
. We wish to show that the result holds for
n=k+1
. That is,
E(k+1)\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

There is a unique
j\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
for which
p(j)\leq k<p(j+1)
. Our subgoal is to show that
j\leq k-1
, so we can apply the inductive hypothesis to
p(j)
and
p(j+1)
. Denote by
j^{\prime}
the maximum positive integer for which
j^{\prime}\leq k-1\text{ }(1)
and
j^{\prime}<\frac{k-E(j)+1}{\phi}\text{ }(2)
. We can write
(2)
equivalently as:

j^{\prime}\phi<k-E(j^{\prime})+1\Longleftrightarrow\left\lfloor j^{\prime}\phi\right\rfloor\leq k-E(j^{\prime})\Longleftrightarrow\left\lfloor j\phi\right\rfloor+E(j^{\prime})\leq k\Longleftrightarrow p(j^{\prime})\leq k.

Now
(1)
is weaker than
(2)
(as for
k\geq 6
,
\frac{k-E(j^{\prime})+1}{\phi}\leq\frac{k+2}{\phi}\leq k-1
), so we in fact have
j^{\prime}=j
. We can now safely apply the induction hypothesis to
j
and
j+1
.

We know
j<\frac{k-E(j)+1}{\phi}\leq\frac{k+2}{\phi}
, so
j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k+2}{\phi}\right\rfloor
. From
k<p(j+1)
, we have

\displaystyle k<\left\lfloor(j+1)\phi\right\rfloor+E(j+1)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow k-E(j+1)<\left\lfloor(j+1)\phi\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow k-E(j+1)+1\leq\left\lfloor(j+1)\phi\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow k-E(j+1)+1<(j+1)\phi

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\frac{k-E(j+1)+1}{\phi}-1<j

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\frac{k-1+1}{\phi}-1<j

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor\leq j.

This means that
\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor\leq j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k+2}{\phi}\right\rfloor
.

We now use the properties that for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
\left\lfloor a+b\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor a\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor b\right\rfloor+1
and
\left\lfloor a+b\right\rfloor\geq\left\lfloor a\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor b\right\rfloor
. Let
X=j-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor\geq 0
. Then

X\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k+2}{\phi}\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\frac{2}{\phi}\right\rfloor+1-\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor=2.

Somewhat similarly, define
Y=\left\lfloor(k+1)\phi\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor
for some
Y\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Again using the above floor property, we find

Y\leq\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\phi\right\rfloor+1-\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor=2.

Using the other floor property, we find

Y\geq\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor+\left\lfloor\phi\right\rfloor-\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor=1.

In all, we know
X\in\{0,1,2\}
and
Y\in\{1,2\}
.

Note that the number of
l\leq k
for which
l
is in
p(1),p(2),p(3),\dots,p(n)
is precisely
j
. It follows by Corollary 3, the recursive definition of
p(n)
using itself, that
p(k+1)=k+1+j
, and that:

\displaystyle E(k+1)

\displaystyle=p(k+1)-\left\lfloor(k+1)\phi\right\rfloor

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{=}}k+1+\left\lfloor k/\phi\right\rfloor+X-\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor-Y

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 2}}{{=}}k+1+\left\lfloor k(\phi-1)\right\rfloor+X-\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor-Y

\displaystyle=k+1+\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor-k+X-\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor-Y

\displaystyle=1+X-Y.

Note that step
1
is from the definitions of
X
and
Y
and step
2
is from a well known property of
\phi
. It remains to show that
1+X-Y\in\{-1,0,1\}
.

The only case when
E(k+1)\not\in\{-1,0,1\}
is when
(X,Y)=(2,1)
. It remains for us to show that this case is impossible. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that
E(j)\leq 0
. Then from
j<\frac{k-E(j)+1}{\phi}
, we have
j<\frac{k+1}{\phi}
, so
j\leq\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor+1
and hence
X\leq 1
, a contradiction. This means
E(j)=-1
and
j=\frac{k+2}{\phi}
, from which we find
\left\lfloor\frac{k+2}{\phi}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor+2
as
X=2
. We rewrite this equation as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{k+2}{\phi}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\phi}\right\rfloor+2

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left\lfloor(k+2)(\phi-1)\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor k(\phi-1)\right\rfloor+2

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left\lfloor(k+2)\phi\right\rfloor-k-2=\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor-k+2

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\left\lfloor(k+2)\phi\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor+4\text{ }(3).

Using the fact that
Y=1
, or that
\left\lfloor(k+1)\phi\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor k\phi\right\rfloor+1
with
(3)
, we get
\left\lfloor(k+2)\phi\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor(k+1)\phi\right\rfloor+3
, which is impossible.

We have therefore shown
E(k+1)\in\{-1,0,1\}
and hence completed the strong induction.

The main takeaway from this proof is that we used local information relative to the functions
f(n)=\left\lfloor n\phi\right\rfloor
and
g(n)=\left\lfloor n/\phi\right\rfloor
by applying it to what we already know about
p(n)
. If we can extract stronger local information of
f
and
g
, we can possibly sharpen our result to
p(n)=\left\lfloor n\phi\right\rfloor
. Regardless of potential improvement, our result is already very strong. If we are given any
n
, we can guarantee
p(n)
to be within
1
away from
\left\lfloor n\phi\right\rfloor
without having to perform any recursion.

5
Future Work

Our method of set construction has weak application to the primes. Let
P
denote the sequence of all primes in increasing order. That is,
P
contains
2,3,5,7,\dots
in that order. Define by
Q
the gaps of composites that
P
left. That is,
Q
contains
4,6,8,9,10,12,14,15,16,18,20,21,22,\dots
in that order. We claim that
Q(P(n)-n-1)=P(n)-1
for
n\geq 3
. Consider the set
[P(n)]
. There are
n

P
s in this set, so there are
P(n)-n-1

Q
s in this set (as we do not count
1
in either
P
or
Q
). For
n\geq 3
,
P(n)-1
must be a
Q
as the only consecutive primes are
2
and
3
. Hence
Q(P(n)-n-1)=P(n)-1
, as desired. We believe that it is possible to create constructions that better exploit the properties of the primes and create more useful equations. Here we only used the fact that most primes are nonconsecutive.

6
Conclusion

We used elementary techniques to prove non-elementary results. Our initial confusing definition of
p
and
q
gave the unexpected conjecture of
p(n)=\left\lfloor\phi n\right\rfloor
and
q(n)=\left\lfloor\phi^{2}n\right\rfloor
. We were close to proving this relationship by showing that
p(n)
is at most
1
away from
\left\lfloor\phi n\right\rfloor
. Our elementary technique of clever set constructions allowed us to convert qualitative data about
p
and
q
into quantitative data.
