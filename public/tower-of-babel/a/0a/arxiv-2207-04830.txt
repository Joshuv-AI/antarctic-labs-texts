Abstract
. A cornerstone in convex analysis is the crucial relationship between functions and their convex conjugate via the Fenchel-Young inequality. In this dual variable setting, the maximal monotonicity of the contact set { ( x , y ) | f ⁡ ( x ) + f ∗ ​ ( y ) = ⟨ x , y ⟩ } \big\{(x,y)\ \big|\ f(x)+f^{*}(y)=\langle x,y\rangle\big\} is due to the involution f ∗ ⁣ ∗ = f f^{**}=f holding for convex lower-semicontinuous functions defined on any Hilbert space. We investigate the validity of the cyclic version of involution and maximal monotonicity for multiple (more than two) convex functions. As a result, we show that when the underlying space is the real line, cyclical involutivity and maximal monotonicity induced by multi-conjugate convex functions continue to hold as for the dual variable case. On the other hand, when the underlying space is multidimensional, we show that the corresponding properties do not hold in general unless a further regularity assumption is imposed. We provide detailed examples that illustrate the significant differences between dual- and multi-conjugate convex functions, as well as between uni- and multi-dimensional underlying spaces.

Introduction and our contribution

Over the last few decades, the theory and applications of duality in convex analysis and monotone operator theory have advanced significantly (
[
8
,
34

), the study of dual variables
x,y\in{\cal H}
, a function
f
, and its convex conjugate

(1.1)

f^{*}(y):=\sup_{x\in{\cal H}}\,\langle x,y\rangle-f(x)

which are linked by the Fenchel-Young inequality (see
[
8

(1.2)

f(x)+f^{*}(y)\geq\langle x,y\rangle,\quad x,y\in{\cal H}.

We refer to
[
9
,
10
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
11

for recent treatment of duality in optimization and convex analysis. Studies showed that if

are convex conjugate to each other, i.e., if

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in{\cal H}^{2}\ |\ f(x)+g(y)=\langle x,y\rangle\}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in{\cal H}^{2}\ |\ y\in{\partial}f(x)\}

\displaystyle=\{(x,y)\in{\cal H}^{2}\ |\ x\in{\partial}g(y)\}

(Rockafellar
[
33

[
8

),
equivalent to

(1.3)

S(\Gamma)={\cal H}

that is,

is not a proper subset of
{\cal H}
. Note that if
\Gamma\subseteq{\cal H}\times{\cal H}
is

These results established by Minty, Rockafellar, Fenchel, Moreau, and others have laid the groundwork for modern theory of nonlinear monotone operators
[

35

36

Recently, S. Bartz, H.H. Bauschke, H.M. Phan, and X. Wang

1.1.
Connection with theory of multi-marginal optimal transport

Let
(X_{1},\mu_{1}),\ldots,(X_{N},\mu_{N})
be
Borel probability spaces, and
X:=X_{1}\times\cdots\times X_{N}
. Denote by
\Pi(X)
the set of all Borel probability measures
\pi
on
X
whose marginals are the
\mu_{i}
’s
[
37
,
39
]
. Given a cost function
c:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
, the optimal transport problem refers to the following optimization problem:

(1.8)

P_{c}:=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(X)}\int_{X}c(x)d\pi(x).

To distinguish it from the two-marginal case, the problem is commonly referred to as multi-marginal optimal transport problem when
N\geq 3
. In this problem, the optimal transport cost
P_{c}
, as well as the geometry and structure of optimal transport plans – the solutions to (
1.8
) – are sought. It is well known that the problem (
1.8
) is attained, i.e., the OT plans exist, under suitable assumptions on the cost and marginals.

Because (
1.8
) is an infinite-dimensional linear programming problem, it has a dual problem whose formulation turns out to have the following form:

(1.9)

D_{c}:=\sup_{\begin{array}[]{c}f_{i}\in L_{1}({\mu_{i}}),\\
\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}f_{i}(x_{i})\leq c({\bf x})\end{array}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq N}\int_{X_{i}}f_{i}(x_{i})d\mu_{i}(x_{i}).

Kellerer’s
[
19
]
generalization
of the Kantorovich duality states that, under mild assumptions on the marginals
\mu_{1},...,\mu_{N}
and cost function
c
, it holds

(1.10)

P_{c}=D_{c}.

This has the important implication that every optimal transport
\pi
solving (
1.8
) is concentrated on the contact set

(1.11)

\Gamma=\bigg\{{\bf x}\in X\ \bigg|\ \sum_{1\leq i\leq N}f_{i}(x_{i})=c({\bf x})\bigg\}

where
(f_{1},\dots,f_{N})
is a solution to the dual problem (
1.9
), which is also attained under suitable assumptions. This provides a crucial information for investigating the geometry of optimal transport plans.

The interaction between the optimal transport and its dual problems is what makes the theory surprisingly powerful for many applications in fields such as analysis, geometry, PDEs, probability, statistics, economics, data sciences, and many researchers have helped to advance the field
[
16
,
18
,
25
,
30
,
31
,
37
,
39
]
. Regarding the geometry of optimal transport for
N=2
, arguably one of the most well-known and widely applied result is the Brenier’s theorem
[
12
]
: given marginals
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in{\cal P}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and the cost function
c(x_{1},x_{2})=|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2}
, there exists a convex function
\varphi
such that for any solution
\pi
to (
1.8
), it holds

(1.12)

y\in{\partial}\varphi(x)\ \ \pi-a.e.\,(x,y),\text{ and moreover, }y=\nabla\varphi(x)\text{ if $\mu_{1}$ has density.}

Because the geometry of the subdifferential
{\partial}\varphi
is well understood by studies in convex analysis, Brenier’s theorem could yield important further results. Likewise, a better understanding of multi-conjugate convex analysis should also have a significant impact on the theory of multi-marginal optimal transport, their geometry, and applications. This is one of the motivations of this paper.

Recent advances in the theory of multi-marginal optimal transport and its geometrical structures have been rapid and fruitful, yielding numerous new research directions and open problems
[

,
7
,

,

,

,

,

,

1.3
: Proof of Theorem
1.4

We will need the following lemma whose proof is given in
[
8
, Proposition 14.19]
for the case
N=1
. Extension for arbitrary
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
appears to be useful.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
g,f_{1},f_{2},...,f_{N}\in{\cal A}({\cal H})
, we have the following identity

(2.1)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f_{i}\\
i=1,...,N\end{subarray}}\bigg(g(x+x_{1}+...+x_{N})-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})\bigg)=\bigg(g^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{*}\bigg)^{*}(x)\ \ \text{for all }x\in{\cal H},

where
g^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{*}:{\cal H}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is defined as (note
-\infty
can be assumed)

\bigg(g^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{*}\bigg)(y):=\begin{cases}\big(g^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{*}\big)(y)\ \text{ if }\,y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g^{*},\\
+\infty\ \text{ if }\,y\notin\mathop{\rm dom}g^{*}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Recall
S({\bf x}):=x_{1}+\dots+x_{N}
. We can proceed as follows:

\displaystyle\bigg(g^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{*}\bigg)^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g^{*}}\langle x,y\rangle-\bigg(g^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}^{*}\bigg)(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g^{*}}\langle x,y\rangle-g^{*}(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sup_{x_{i}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f_{i}}\langle y,x_{i}\rangle-f_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g^{*}}\langle x,y\rangle-g^{*}(y)+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f_{i}\\
i=1,...,N\end{subarray}}\langle y,S({\bf x})\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f_{i}\\
i=1,...,N\end{subarray}}\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g^{*}}\langle y,x+S({\bf x})\rangle-g^{*}(y)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{i}\in\mathop{\rm dom}f_{i}\\
i=1,...,N\end{subarray}}g(x+S({\bf x}))-\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i}).

This proves the lemma.
∎

Our proof of Theorem
1.4
will closely follow the proof presented in
[
5
]
, utilizing Lemma
2.1
along with the following fact given in
[
38
]
.

[
38
, Corollary 2.3]
.
Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
be proper and lower-semicontinuous. If
f^{*}
is essentially smooth, then
f
is convex.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Recalling
f_{1}=(\oplus_{i=2}^{N}f_{i})^{c}
, we proceed

\displaystyle(f_{1}+q)(x_{1})=\sup_{x_{2},...,x_{N}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}c({\bf x})+q(x_{1})-\sum_{i=2}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{2},...,x_{N}}x_{2}\cdot(x_{1}+x_{3}+...+x_{N})-f_{2}(x_{2})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j<k\leq N\\
j\neq 2,k\neq 2\end{subarray}}x_{j}\cdot x_{k}+q(x_{1})-\sum_{i=3}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{3},...,x_{N}}f_{2}^{*}(x_{1}+x_{3}+...+x_{N})+q(x_{1}+x_{3}+...+x_{N})-\sum_{i=3}^{N}(f_{i}+q)(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\bigg((f_{2}^{*}+q)^{*}-\sum_{i=3}^{N}(f_{i}+q)^{*}\bigg)^{*}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\bigg(e_{f_{2}}-\sum_{i=3}^{N}e_{f^{*}_{i}}\bigg)^{*}(x_{1})=\bigg(q-\sum_{i=2}^{N}e_{f^{*}_{i}}\bigg)^{*}(x_{1})

where the last two equalities are due to Lemma
2.1
and Moreau’s decomposition (
(v)
) for
N=2
. Now since
f_{1}+q
is essentially smooth,
[
38
, Corollary 2.3]
implies
q-\sum_{i=2}^{N}e_{f^{*}_{k}}
is convex. And
e_{f^{*}_{k}}
, which is a Moreau envelope of a function in
{\cal A}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}),
is continuous in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Consequently,

e_{f_{1}^{*}}=(f_{1}+q)^{*}=(q-\sum_{i=2}^{N}e_{f^{*}_{i}})^{**}=q-\sum_{i=2}^{N}e_{f^{*}_{i}},

that is,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{f^{*}_{i}}=q
, which is the Moreau decomposition (
(v)
).
∎

3.
One-dimensional domain: Proof of Theorems
1.5
and
1.7

We begin with a lemma about strong convexity and how it is inherited.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f,g,h
be proper functions on a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, satisfying

f(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g}h(x+y)-g(y).

If
h
is lower-semicontinuous and
\lambda
-strongly convex, then so is
f
.

Proof.

Let
k=h-\lambda q
, which belongs to
{\cal A}({\cal H})
, thus
k=k^{**}
. We compute

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g}h(x+y)-g(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g}\sup_{z\in\mathop{\rm dom}k^{*}}\lambda q(x+y)+\langle x+y,z\rangle-k^{*}(z)-g(y)

\displaystyle=\lambda q(x)+\sup_{z\in\mathop{\rm dom}k^{*}}\big\{\langle x,z\rangle-k^{*}(z)+\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g}\{\langle y,\lambda x+z\rangle-(g-\lambda q)(y)\}\big\}

\displaystyle=\lambda q(x)+\sup_{z\in\mathop{\rm dom}k^{*}}\langle x,z\rangle-k^{*}(z)+(g-\lambda q)^{*}(\lambda x+z)

\displaystyle=:\lambda q(x)+\xi(x).

Observe
\xi
is convex lower-semicontinuous as a supremum of such functions.
∎

Let
\Delta
be the diagonal subspace of
{\cal H}^{N}
. The concept of a “directional convex envelope” is clearly relevant to the study of
c
-conjugate convex functions. We introduce the following definition as we are not aware of it appearing elsewhere.

Definition 3.2

(
\Delta
-convex envelope)
.

Let
{\bf x}=(x_{1},...,x_{N})\in{\cal H}^{N}
,
S({\bf x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}
. Let
f:{\cal H}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, satisfying
f({\bf x})\geq\langle S({\bf x}),y\rangle+b
for some
y\in{\cal H}
,
b\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
g
is called the
\Delta
- convex envelope of
f
if
g
is the largest convex lower-semicontinuous function on
{\cal H}
satisfying
f({\bf x})\geq g(S({\bf x}))
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f
satisfy the condition in Definition
3.2
. Then
g
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
f
if and only if
g^{*}(y)=\sup_{\bf x}\langle S({\bf x}),y\rangle-f({\bf x})
.

Proof.

Let
h
be any element in
{\cal A}({\cal H})
. The following equivalence

\displaystyle f({\bf x})

\displaystyle\geq h(S({\bf x}))\ \text{ for every }{\bf x}\in{\cal H}^{N}

\displaystyle\iff f({\bf x})

\displaystyle\geq\langle S({\bf x}),y\rangle-h^{*}(y)\ \text{ for every }{\bf x}\in{\cal H}^{N},y\in{\cal H}

\displaystyle\iff h^{*}(y)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{\bf x}\langle S({\bf x}),y\rangle-f({\bf x})\ \text{ for every }y\in{\cal H}

yields the lemma, since maximality of
g
corresponds to minimality of
g^{*}
.
∎

Definition 3.4
.

For proper functions
f,g,h:{\cal H}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{+\infty\}
, we say that
f
and
g
are
h
-conjugate if the following holds:

(3.1)

f(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathop{\rm dom}g}h(x+y)-g(y),\quad g(y)=\sup_{x\in\mathop{\rm dom}f}h(x+y)-f(x).

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that
f,g,h\in{\cal A}({\mathbb{R}})
and that
f,g
are
h
-conjugate. Assume further that
f
and
h
are continuous, and that
h
is
\lambda
-strongly convex for some
\lambda>0
. Then
h
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
f\oplus g
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
,
f
and
g
are
\lambda
- stongly convex. In particular,
\bigcup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}}{\partial}f(x)=\bigcup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}}{\partial}g(y)={\mathbb{R}}
.
Firstly, we claim that the following set
\cal I
is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}
:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\cal I

\displaystyle=\{s\in{\mathbb{R}}\ |\ s=x+y\text{ such that }{\partial}f(x)\cap{\partial}g(y)\neq\emptyset,\text{ and}

\displaystyle\text{either $f$ is differentiable at $x$ or $g$ is differentiable at $y\}$}.

Let us prove the claim later. Let
H
denote the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
f\oplus g
. Then
H\geq h
since
f(x)+g(y)\geq h(x+y)
by (
3.1
). The proposition asserts
H=h
. To prove this, we claim that it is sufficient to prove the following tightness assertion: For any
x_{0},y_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\partial}f(x_{0})\cap{\partial}g(y_{0})\neq\emptyset
and either
f
is differentiable at
x_{0}
or
g
is differentiable at
y_{0}
, we have

(3.3)

\displaystyle f(x_{0})+g(y_{0})=h(x_{0}+y_{0}).

The sufficiency is because (
3.3
) implies
H=h
on
\cal I
, and thus for any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, by the first claim, there exists a sequence
s_{n}
in
\cal I
such that
\lim s_{n}=s
, and

H(s)\leq\liminf H(s_{n})=\liminf h(s_{n})=h(s)

as desired, thanks to the continuity of
h
.

Now to verify (
3.3
), by translation, we may assume without loss of generality that
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
. Moreover we may assume that
0\in{\partial}f(0)\cap{\partial}g(0)
. To see why this can be assumed, let
a\in{\partial}f(0)\cap{\partial}g(0)
. Consider
\tilde{f}(x)=f(x)-\langle a,x\rangle-f(0)
,
\tilde{g}(y)=g(y)-\langle a,y\rangle-g(0)
, and
\tilde{h}(z)=h(z)-\langle a,z\rangle-f(0)-g(0)
. Then
f
and
g
are
h
-conjugate if and only if
\tilde{f}
and
\tilde{g}
are
\tilde{h}
-conjugate. And since
\min\tilde{f}=\tilde{f}(0)=0
and
\min\tilde{g}=\tilde{g}(0)=0
, (
3.3
) holds if and only if
\tilde{h}(0)=0
. Our discussion so far indicates it is sufficient to prove the claim (
3.3
) under the assumption
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
,
f,g
are
h
-conjugate,
f(0)=\min f=g(0)=\min g=0
, and either
f
or
g
is differentiable at
0
; and the goal is to prove
h(0)=0
.

Now to derive a contradiction, suppose
h(0)<0
, so that
m=\min h<0
. Assume
f
is differentiable at
0
(the proof will be the same in the case
g
is differentiable at
0
, by switching the role of
f
and
g
). Let
K=\{x\ |\ h(x)\leq m/2\}
. Since
h
is strongly convex, there exists
\delta>0
such that

(3.4)

x\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus K\text{ and }z\in{\partial}h(x)\ \text{ implies }\ |z|\geq\delta.

By (
3.1
), given
\epsilon>0
, there exists
y_{\epsilon}
such that
-\epsilon<h(y_{\epsilon})-g(y_{\epsilon})\leq 0=f(0)
. Now
f(x)\geq h(x+y_{\epsilon})-g(y_{\epsilon})
for all
x
and
\nabla f(0)=0
imply that for all sufficiently small
\epsilon
, we must have
y_{\epsilon}\in K
by (
3.4
). However, we then have

-\epsilon<h(y_{\epsilon})-g(y_{\epsilon})\leq h(y_{\epsilon})\leq m/2,

a contradiction for small
\epsilon
, proving the tightness assertion.

It remains to prove
\cal I
is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}
. For any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, recall that there exists
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
s\in{\partial}f(x)\cap{\partial}g(y)
. By translation and subtracting affine functions as before, we may assume
0\in{\partial}f(0)\cap{\partial}g(0)
. Then notice the desired denseness will follow if we can show that for any
r>0
, there exists
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
|x|<r
,
|y|<r
,
{\partial}f(x)\cap{\partial}g(y)\neq\emptyset
, and either
f
is differentiable at
x
or
g
is differentiable at
y
.

Now to prove the claim, assume that neither
f
nor
g
is differentiable at
0
, since otherwise there is nothing to prove.
{\partial}f(0)
is a compact interval, say
[a,b]
, since
f
is continuous. And
W:={\partial}g((-r,r))
is an open interval since
g
is strongly convex, thus
g^{*}
is differentiable and
{\partial}g^{*}=({\partial}g)^{-1}
is continuous.

Suppose
b\in W
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
x\in[0,\epsilon)
such that
f
is differentiable at
x
and
\nabla f(x)\in[b,b+\epsilon)
since
f
is differentiable a.e.. Thus
\nabla f(x)\in W
for small
\epsilon
, and this implies
\nabla f(x)\in{\partial}g(y)
for some
y\in(-r,r)
, proving the claim. Likewise, the claim holds in the case
a\in W
. Finally, if
\{a,b\}\cap W=\emptyset
, then
W\subseteq(a,b)
, yielding that
g
is Lipschitz and differentiable a.e. in
(-r,r)
. Hence there exists
y\in(-r,r)
such that
\nabla g(y)\in{\partial}f(0)
. This proves the desired denseness of
\cal I
in
{\mathbb{R}}
, hence the proposition.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

The above proof shows that Proposition
3.5
will continue to hold for functions
f,g,h
defined on an arbitrary Hilbert space
{\cal H}
if the corresponding set
\cal I
is dense in
{\cal H}
given the mutual conjugacy (
3.1
).

Now we combine Theorems
1.5
and
1.7
into the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
{\cal H}={\mathbb{R}}
, and let
c:{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be given by (
1.4
). Suppose
f_{i}\in{\cal A}({\mathbb{R}})
,
i=1,...,N
, satisfy

(3.5)

f_{i}(x_{i})=\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq i}f_{j}\Big)^{c}(x_{i})\ \text{ for every }\ i=2,...,N.

Then
(f_{1},\ldots,f_{N})
is a
c
-conjugate tuple, that is,
f_{1}
also satisfies

(3.6)

f_{1}(x_{1})=\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq 1}f_{j}\Big)^{c}(x_{1}).

In this case, the contact set
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\{f_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}}
satisfies (
1.3
), and thus, the assertions
(i)
–
(vii)
in Theorem
1.2
hold true if
{\cal H}={\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

If
N=2
, (
3.6
) is equivalent to the fact
f^{**}=f
by Fenchel and Moreau, and (
1.3
) is also shown by Rockafellar and Minty. We will proceed by an induction on
N\geq 3
, thus when
{\cal H}={\mathbb{R}}
, firstly we will extend these results (
3.6
), (
1.3
) for
N=3
. However, since the proof presented below will be valid for an arbitrary Hilbert space
{\cal H}
, in the sequel, we will denote
{\cal H}
(rather than
{\mathbb{R}}
) by the underlying space, though we will eventually assume
{\cal H}={\mathbb{R}}
.

Step 1:
N=3
case.
To begin, let us write
(f,g,h)=(f_{1},f_{2},f_{3})
. We have

f(x)+g(y)+h(z)\geq\langle x,y\rangle+\langle y,z\rangle+\langle z,x\rangle,

hence

(3.7)

\varphi(x,y):=f(x)+g(y)-\langle x,y\rangle\geq h^{*}(x+y).

We will show
h^{*}
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
\varphi
. Notice (
3.7
) is equivalent to

(3.8)

F(x)+G(y)\geq H(x+y)

where
F=f+q
,
G=g+q
,
H=h^{*}+q
, and we recall
q(s)=\frac{1}{2}|s|^{2}
. Observe that the conjugacy assumption (
3.5
), which reads

\displaystyle f(x)=\sup_{y}\{h^{*}(x+y)+\langle x,y\rangle-g(y)\},\ g(y)=\sup_{x}\{h^{*}(x+y)+\langle x,y\rangle-f(x)\}

implies that
F
and
G
are also
H
-conjugate (see (
3.1
)).
We claim that
H
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
F\oplus G
. Observe that this implies (
3.6
) for
N=3
, because if
H
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
F\oplus G
, then
h^{*}
must be the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
\varphi
due to the equivalence between (
3.7
) and (
3.8
). But this precisely means that
h=(f\oplus g)^{c}
by Lemma
3.3
, as desired.

Then as argued in the proof of Proposition
3.5
, it is sufficient to prove the claim under the assumption that
F
and
G
are
H
-conjugate,
F(0)=0=\min F
,
G(0)=0=\min G
, and the goal is to show
H(0)=0=\min H
.

To this end, for each
R>0
, define

\displaystyle G_{R}(y)

\displaystyle=G(y)\ \text{ if }\ |y|\leq R,\quad G_{R}(y)=+\infty\ \text{ if }\ |y|>R,

\displaystyle h^{*}_{R}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{|y|\leq R}\langle x,y\rangle-h(y),\quad H_{R}=h^{*}_{R}+q,

\displaystyle F_{R}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y}\{H_{R}(x+y)-G_{R}(y)\},

\displaystyle F_{R}^{H}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x}\{H_{R}(x+y)-F_{R}(x)\},

\displaystyle K_{R}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x}\{H_{R}(x+y)-F(x)\}.

Assume
R
is large enough so that
\min G_{R}=\min G
and
h_{R}^{*}
is proper. We have

(3.9)

\displaystyle h_{R}^{*}\text{ has Lipschitz constant at most $R$,}

(3.10)

\displaystyle F_{R}\text{ is locally Lipschitz and monotone increasing to }F\text{ as }R\to\infty,

(3.11)

\displaystyle F_{R}^{H}\text{ converges pointwise to }G.

(
3.10
) is because
H_{R}
and
-G_{R}
monotonically increase in
R
, and
F_{R}
is locally Lipschitz because the supremum defining
F_{R}
is taken over
|y|\leq R
only. And (
3.11
) is because
G_{R}\geq F_{R}^{H}\geq K_{R}
,
G_{R}
decreases to
G
, and
K_{R}
increases to
G
in
R
.
Now since
F_{R}
and
F_{R}^{H}
are
H_{R}
-conjugate, by Proposition
3.5
, we have

(3.12)

H_{R}(x+y)
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
F_{R}(x)+F_{R}^{H}(y)
.

Recall that our goal is to show
\min H=\min F+\min G
. It is clear that
\min H\leq\min F+\min G
. To show the reverse, since
H_{R}\leq H
and
\min H_{R}=\min F_{R}+\min F_{R}^{H}
by (
3.12
), and also
G_{R}\geq F_{R}^{H}\geq K_{R}
, it is enough to show

(3.13)

\min F_{R}\nearrow\min F\ \text{ and }\ \min K_{R}\nearrow\min G\ \text{ as }\,R\to\infty.

The fact that
\min F=F(0)=0
and that
F
is of the form
F=f+q
implies
F\geq q
, thus
F^{*}\leq q
. Since
F_{R}
is increasing to
F
,
F_{R}^{*}
is decreasing in
R
and is bounded below by
F^{*}
. Let
F_{\infty}
denote the limit of
F_{R}^{*}
. We claim
F_{\infty}=F^{*}
. To see this, first notice
F_{\infty}
is convex as a limit of convex functions. Secondly we have
F_{\infty}
is continuous, because
F^{*}\leq F_{\infty}\leq F_{R}^{*}
and
F_{R}
is
1
-strongly convex by Lemma
3.1
, thus
q-F_{R}^{*}
is continuous and convex. This implies
F_{\infty}
is bounded on every bounded subset of
{\cal H}
, thus is locally Lipschitz, hence continuous on
{\cal H}
. This yields
F_{\infty}=(F_{\infty})^{**}=F^{*}
as claimed. This implies in particular,

\min F=-F^{*}(0)=-F_{\infty}(0)=-\lim_{R\to\infty}F_{R}^{*}(0)=\lim_{R\to\infty}\min F_{R}.

Similarly,
\min K_{R}\nearrow\min G
. This proves
\min H=H(0)=0
, and thus (
3.6
).

Now to prove (
1.3
), fix any
s\in{\cal H}
. By (
1.3
) holding for
N=2
, there exists
z\in{\cal H}
such that
h(z)+h^{*}(s-z)=\langle z,s-z\rangle
. This yields
z\in{\partial}h^{*}(s-z)
, implying
s\in{\partial}H(s-z)
. Since
F,G,H
are strongly convex, there exist unique
x,y,u\in{\cal H}
such that
s\in{\partial}F(x)\cap{\partial}G(y)\cap{\partial}H(u)
. The fact that
H
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
F\oplus G
now yields
F(x)+G(y)=H(x+y)
and
s\in{\partial}H(x+y)
. Then the uniqueness of
u
implies
s-z=x+y
, that is,
s=x+y+z
. Finally,

\displaystyle F(x)+G(y)=H(x+y)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)+g(y)=h^{*}(x+y)+\langle x,y\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)+g(y)=h^{*}(s-z)+\langle x,y\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)+g(y)=\langle z,s-z\rangle-h(z)+\langle x,y\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle f(x)+g(y)+h(z)=\langle x,y\rangle+\langle y,z\rangle+\langle z,x\rangle.

This proves the maximality (
1.3
), hence the theorem for
N=3
.

Step 2: induction on
N
.
From now on, we extend the proof for
N\geq 4
. We proceed by an induction on
N
. Suppose the theorem holds for
N-1
. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{\bf x}

\displaystyle=(x_{3},...,x_{N}),\quad S(\tilde{\bf x})=\sum_{i=3}^{N}x_{i},\quad c(\tilde{\bf x})=\sum_{3\leq i<j\leq N}\langle x_{i},x_{j}\rangle,

\displaystyle\psi(\tilde{\bf x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=3}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})-c(\tilde{\bf x}),\quad\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})=f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})-\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle,

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=(f_{1}\oplus f_{2})^{c},\text{ i.e., }g(y)=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}}\big\{\langle y,x_{1}+x_{2}\rangle-\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})\big\}.

From the inequality

\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}(x_{i})\geq c({\bf x})
, we have

(3.14)

\displaystyle\psi(\tilde{\bf x})

\displaystyle\geq g\big(S(\tilde{\bf x})\big)=\sup_{x_{1},x_{2}}\big\{\langle S(\tilde{\bf x}),x_{1}+x_{2}\rangle-\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})\big\}.

Now comes the crux of the observation: (
3.14
), the induction hypothesis (on the cyclical involutivity), and the conjugacy (
3.5
) (i.e., each of the
f_{3},...,f_{N}
is the smallest convex function satisfying (
3.14
) given others) combine to imply

(f_{3},...,f_{N},g^{*})\text{ are }c(\tilde{\bf x},y)-\text{conjugate, where }c(\tilde{\bf x},y)=c(\tilde{\bf x})+\langle S(\tilde{\bf x}),y\rangle.

In other words,
g\big(S(\tilde{\bf x})\big)
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
\psi(\tilde{\bf x})
, by Lemma
3.3
.

This in turn implies
f_{2}=(f_{1}\oplus g)^{c}
due to the following calculation:

\displaystyle f_{2}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq 2}f_{j}\Big)^{c}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1},\tilde{\bf x}}\big\{\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle+\langle x_{1}+x_{2},S(\tilde{\bf x})\rangle-f_{1}(x_{1})-\psi(\tilde{\bf x})\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1}}\big\{\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle-f_{1}(x_{1})+\sup_{\tilde{\bf x}}\{\langle x_{1}+x_{2},S(\tilde{\bf x})\rangle-\psi(\tilde{\bf x})\}\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1},y}\big\{\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle-f_{1}(x_{1})+\langle x_{1}+x_{2},y\rangle-g(y)\big\}

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1},y}\big\{\langle x_{2},x_{1}+y\rangle-(f_{1}(x_{1})+g(y)-\langle x_{1},y\rangle)\big\}

\displaystyle=(f_{1}\oplus g)^{c}(x_{2})

where the fourth equality is because
g
is the
\Delta
-convex envelope of
\psi
. The induction hypothesis (or the theorem we established for
N=3
) now implies

\displaystyle f_{1}=(f_{2}\oplus g)^{c}=\Big(\bigoplus_{j\neq 1}f_{j}\Big)^{c}(x_{1})

where the second equality is by an analogous calculation given above. This completes the proof of the cyclical involutivity (
3.6
).

Finally, we establish the maximality (
1.3
). Recall (
3.14
), that is

\psi(\tilde{\bf x})\geq g(S(\tilde{\bf x}))\geq\langle y,S(\tilde{\bf x})\rangle-g^{*}(y)\ \text{ for every }x_{3},...,x_{N},y.

Fix any
s\in{\cal H}
. By the conjugacy of
(f_{3},...,f_{N},g^{*})
and the induction hypothesis on the maximality, there exists
\tilde{\bf x}^{s}=(\tilde{x}^{s}_{3},...,\tilde{x}^{s}_{N})
such that

(3.15)

\psi(\tilde{\bf x}^{s})=g(S(\tilde{\bf x}^{s}))=\langle s-S(\tilde{\bf x}^{s}),S(\tilde{\bf x}^{s})\rangle-g^{*}(s-S(\tilde{\bf x}^{s})).

Similarly, the conjugacy of
(f_{1},f_{2},g)
yields

\varphi(x_{1},x_{2})\geq g^{*}(x_{1}+x_{2})\geq\langle z,x_{1}+x_{2}\rangle-g(z)\ \text{ for every }x_{1},x_{2},z,

and for the same
s
, there exists
x^{s}_{1},x^{s}_{2}\in{\cal H}
such that

(3.16)

\varphi(x^{s}_{1},x^{s}_{2})=g^{*}(x^{s}_{1}+x^{s}_{2})=\langle s-x^{s}_{1}-x^{s}_{2},x^{s}_{1}+x^{s}_{2}\rangle-g(s-x^{s}_{1}-x^{s}_{2}).

However, the pair
u,v\in{\cal H}
that satisfies
u+v=s
and
g(u)+g^{*}(v)=\langle u,v\rangle
is unique. Hence (
3.15
), (
3.16
) implies
S(\tilde{\bf x}^{s})=s-x^{s}_{1}-x^{s}_{2}
, or
s=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x^{s}_{i}
. From this identity, by adding the identities (
3.15
), (
3.16
), we obtain

\displaystyle\varphi(x^{s}_{1},x^{s}_{2})+\psi(\tilde{\bf x}^{s})=\langle S(\tilde{\bf x}^{s}),x^{s}_{1}+x^{s}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle(x^{s}_{1},...,x^{s}_{N})\in\Gamma=\bigg\{{\bf x}\ \bigg|\ \sum_{j=1}^{N}f_{j}(x_{j})=c({\bf x})\bigg\}.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

, yielding
S(\Gamma)={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. If
\lambda=\infty
, then
(au,bv,0)\in\Gamma
for any
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
, again yielding
S(\Gamma)={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Thus we henceforth assume
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
. We have

h^{*}(z^{*})=\begin{cases}0\,\text{ if }\,z^{*}=(z^{*}_{1},z^{*}_{2}),\,-\lambda\leq z^{*}_{1},z^{*}_{2}\leq\lambda,\\
\infty\,\text{ else.}\end{cases}

Then the pair of
(x,y)\in I_{1}\times I_{2}
satisfying (
4.5
) is equal to
I_{1}\times I_{2}
, since
x\cdot y=0
. Thus the sum set, i.e., the set of
x+y
, becomes

(4.9)

\{x+y\ |\ h^{*}(x+y)=-x\cdot y,\ x\in I_{1},y\in I_{2}\}=:D

where
D=I_{1}+I_{2}
is the convex hull of its four vertices
\pm\lambda(u+v),\pm\lambda(v-u)
. Notice the difference between (
4.6
) in Step 4 of Example
4.1
, where the sum set is equal to the boundary of
D
, and not all of
D
.

Now for
(x,y)
with
x+y\in{\rm int}D
, clearly
\Gamma_{x,y}=\{z\ |\ (x,y,z)\in\Gamma\}=\{0\}
. For each
(x,y)
such that
x+y\in{\partial}D
, verifying
\Gamma_{x,y}
is again straightforward from the definition of
h
. For example, it is easy to see that
\Gamma_{\lambda u,\lambda v}=\{z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\ |\ z_{1}\geq 0,z_{2}\geq 0\}
,
\Gamma_{-\lambda u,\lambda v}=\{z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\ |\ z_{1}\leq 0,z_{2}\geq 0\}
, and
\Gamma_{t\lambda u,\lambda v}=\{z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\ |\ z_{1}=0,z_{2}\geq 0\}
for any
t\in(-1,1)
, and so on. Gathering
x+y+\Gamma_{x,y}
for every
x+y\in D
, we verify
S(\Gamma)={\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We thus conclude that the triple
(f,g,h)
does not provide a counterexample to Question
1
when
u
and
v
are perpendicular.

Example 4.4

(Infinite support of
f,g
with
u\cdot v\neq 0
yields
h\equiv+\infty
.)
.

Let
u,v
be non-perpendicular unit vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, i.e., assume
u\cdot v\in(0,1]
. Define

\displaystyle f(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\,\text{ on }\,I_{1}=\{x=au\ |\ a\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\\
\infty\,\text{ else,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle g(y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\,\text{ on }\,I_{2}=\{y=bv\ |\ b\in{\mathbb{R}}\},\\
\infty\,\text{ else.}\end{cases}

In this case, we claim that
h:=(f\oplus g)^{c}
is not proper but
h\equiv+\infty
. Because of this, the triple
(f,g,h)
does not provide a counterexample to Question
1
.

The claim is straightforward from the following calculation:

\displaystyle(f\oplus g)^{c}(z)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}x\cdot y+y\cdot z+z\cdot x-f(x)-g(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in{\mathbb{R}},b\in{\mathbb{R}}}(au+bv)\cdot z+ab(u\cdot v)

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{a\in{\mathbb{R}}}a(u+v)\cdot z+a^{2}(u\cdot v)

\displaystyle=\infty

for any
z
, since
u\cdot v>0
.

5.
Dual perspective of cyclical conjugation and maximality

In this section, we illustrate cyclical involutivity and maximal monotonicity in the
N=3
case in the context of duality.
For
f,g,h\in{\cal A}({\cal H})
, we recall the following inequality

(5.1)

f(x)+g(y)+h(z)\geq\langle x,y\rangle+\langle y,z\rangle+\langle z,x\rangle,\ x,y,z\in{\cal H}

is equivalent to

(5.2)

F(x)+G(y)\geq H(x+y)

where
F=f+q
,
G=g+q
,
H=h^{*}+q
, which in turn yields the equivalence

(5.3)

\displaystyle f=(g\oplus h)^{c}\,\text{ and }\,g=(h\oplus f)^{c}

\displaystyle\iff F(x)=\sup_{y\in{\cal H}}H(x+y)-G(y)\,\text{ and }\,G(y)=\sup_{x\in{\cal H}}H(x+y)-F(x)

\displaystyle\iff F=(H^{*}-G^{*})^{*}\,\text{ and }\,G=(H^{*}-F^{*})^{*}\ \text{ by Lemma \ref{switch}}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\iff U=(W-V)^{**}\,\text{ and }\,V=(W-U)^{**}

where
U=F^{*}
,
V=G^{*}
,
W=H^{*}
. This naturally leads us to consider the following class of convex functions (see
[
8
, Propositions 12.30, 14.2]
)

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{1}({\cal H}):=

\displaystyle\,\{F\in{\cal A}({\cal H})\ |\ F\text{ is $1$-strongly convex}\},

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H}):=

\displaystyle\,\{U\in{\cal A}({\cal H})\ |\ U=F^{*}\text{ for some }F\in{\cal A}_{1}({\cal H})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\{U\in{\cal A}({\cal H})\ |\ U=f\square q\text{ for some }f\in{\cal A}({\cal H})\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\{U\in{\cal A}({\cal H})\ |\ q-U\text{ is continuous and convex}\}.

We see that, given (
5.3
), the question of
3
-cyclical involutivity, that is, whether
h
is equal to
(f\oplus g)^{c}
or not, can be recast as the following dual problem:

Is
W:=h\square q
the smallest (pointwise in
{\cal H}
) function in
{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H})
satisfying (
5.4
)?

Note that
W:=U+V
is obviously the smallest convex function satisfying (
5.4
), but
U+V
may not belong to the class
{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H})
in general. This makes the question of finding the smallest
W
in
{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H})
nontrivial. Indeed, we show below how the cyclical involution can fail only under the assumption (
5.3
).

Now we turn to the maximality question (
1.3
) in the context of duality. Assume
f,g,h\in{\cal A}({\cal H})
satisfies (
5.1
). The equivalence between (
(v)
) and (
(vii)
) in Theorem
1.2
is then recast, via Moreau decomposition, as follows:

(5.5)

\Gamma=\Gamma_{\{f,g,h\}}\text{ satisfies \eqref{maximality} if and only if }W=U+V.

Now assume
f,g\in{\cal A}({\cal H})
, and set
h:=(f\oplus g)^{c}
. Observe that (
5.5
) then yields the following equivalence:

(5.6)

\Gamma=\Gamma_{\{f,g,h\}}\text{ satisfies \eqref{maximality} if and only if }U+V\in{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H}).

This is because if
W:=U+V
is in
{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H})
, then
W^{*}-q
is convex, thus the corresponding triple
f:=U^{*}-q
,
g:=V^{*}-q
and
h:=(W^{*}-q)^{*}
satisfies (
1.3
) and
c
-conjugate, by the equivalence between (
(v)
) and (
(vii)
) in Theorem
1.2
.

Let us explore the above discussion through the examples from the previous section. In Example
4.1
, the triple
(f,g,h)
is
c
-conjugate, which yields
W=h\square q
is indeed the smallest element in
{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H})
satisfying (
5.4
). However,
W(0)=-\min W^{*}>0=U(0)+V(0)
, causing the equation
W=U+V
to fail. We deduce
\Gamma
does not satisfy (
1.3
), without having to precisely compute
S(\Gamma)
.

On the other hand, in Example
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle U(x)=(f+q)^{*}(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}|x_{1}|^{2}\,\text{ for }\,x_{1}\in[-\lambda,\lambda],\\
\lambda x_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\,\text{ for }\,x_{1}\geq\lambda,\\
-\lambda x_{1}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}\,\text{ for }\,x_{1}\leq-\lambda,\end{cases}

and
V(x_{1},x_{2})=U(x_{2},x_{1})
. It is easy to see that
U+V
belongs to
{\cal A}_{1}^{*}({\cal H})
, and hence by (
5.6
), we conclude that the triple
(f,g,h)
does satisfy (
1.3
).

Finally, we conclude this paper by detailing Proposition
1.9
.

Example 5.1
.

We will construct
f,g,h\in{\cal A}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
such that
f=(g\oplus h)^{c}
,
g=(h\oplus f)^{c}
, but
h\neq(f\oplus g)^{c}
. We denote
x=(x_{1},x_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
,
y=(y_{1},y_{2})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Let
a\lor b:=\max\{a,b\}
. Recall
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
denote the standard basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. Define

\displaystyle H_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=|x_{1}|\lor|x_{2}|+q(x),

\displaystyle G_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}H_{1}(y)\ \text{ if }\ y=e_{1}\text{ or }\,y=-e_{1},\\
+\infty\ \text{ else,}\end{cases}

\displaystyle F_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}H_{1}(x+y)-G_{0}(y),

\displaystyle G_{1}(y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}H_{1}(x+y)-F_{1}(x).

Then
F_{1},G_{1}
are
H_{1}
-conjugate. And by definition of
F_{1}
, we have

F_{1}(0)=\min F_{1}=0,\text{ and }\ \frac{{\partial}F_{1}}{{\partial}x_{2}}(0)=0,

that is,
F_{1}
is smooth at its minimum (origin) in the
x_{2}
-direction. Next, notice
G_{1}=G_{0}
at
y=\pm e_{1}
, and by symmetry of the construction, we have
G_{1}(0)=\min G_{1}
. We claim
G_{1}(0)>0
. This can be easily seen from the inequality

F_{1}(x)\geq H_{1}(x)-G_{1}(0)\ \text{ for all }\ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

and the fact that
F_{1}(0)=H_{1}(0)=0
, and at the origin
x=0
,
F_{1}
is smooth in
x_{2}
-direction but
H_{1}
is singular. This proves the claim. Let
m:=G_{1}(0)>0
, and redefine

H=H_{1}-m,\quad F=F_{1},\quad G=G_{1}-m,

so that we have

(5.7)

\displaystyle F(0)=\min F=G(0)=\min G=0,\ H(0)=\min H<0,\text{ and }

(5.8)

\displaystyle F,G\text{ are $H$-conjugate.}

Now we define
f,g,h\in{\cal A}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
by

f=F-q
,
g=G-q
, and
h=(H-q)^{*}
. Then as discussed before, (
5.8
) implies that
f=(g\oplus h)^{c}
and
g=(h\oplus f)^{c}
. We need to show
h\neq(f\oplus g)^{c}
. To show this, rather than finding precise formulas for
h
and
(f\oplus g)^{c}
, we derive a contradiction. Recall that the equality
h=(f\oplus g)^{c}
implies
h^{*}+q
is the largest
1
-strongly convex lower-semicontinuous function among all
H\in{\cal A}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
satisfying (
5.2
). We claim that this is false with
h=(H-q)^{*}
.

Set
\tilde{H}:=H\lor 0
. Notice that (
5.7
) then readily implies
F,G
are
\tilde{H}
-conjugate as well. But
\tilde{H}
is not
1
-strongly convex yet. Note that for any
K\in{\cal A}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
satisfying
H\leq K\leq\tilde{H}
, we have that
F,G
are
K
-conjugate. Hence the claim will follow if we can find a
1
-strongly convex function
K
satisfying:

(5.9)

H\leq K\leq\tilde{H},\text{ and }\,H(0)<K(0).

But the definitions
H(x)=|x_{1}|\lor|x_{2}|+q(x)-m
and
\tilde{H}=H\lor 0
readily imply that for all sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, the following function

K_{\epsilon}:=H\lor(q-m+\epsilon)

is a
1
-strongly convex function satisfying (
5.9
). This yields
h\neq(f\oplus g)^{c}
.
