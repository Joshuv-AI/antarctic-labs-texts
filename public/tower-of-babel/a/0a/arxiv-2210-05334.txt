Orthomodular and generalized orthomodular posets
Ivan Chajda, Miroslav Kolařík, Helmut Länger

Abstract
We prove that the 18-element non-lattice orthomodular poset depicted in the paper is the smallest one and unique up to isomorphism. Since not every Boolean poset is orthomodular, we consider the class of the so-called generalized orthomodular posets introduced by the first and third author in a previous paper. We show that this class contains all Boolean posets and we study its subclass consisting of horizontal sums of Boolean posets. For this purpose we introduce the concept of a compatibility relation and the so-called commutator of two elements. We show the relationship between these concepts and we introduce the notion of a ternary discriminator for these posets. Numerous examples illuminating these concepts and results are included in the paper.

Introduction

It is well-known that the set of closed subspaces of a Hilbert space forms a complete orthomodular lattice with respect to set-inclusion. Because these subspaces correspond to self-adjoint bounded operators which correspond to observables in quantum measurements, this orthomodular lattice is often considered as an algebraic counterpart of the logic of quantum mechanics, see e.g.
[
2
]
or
[
7
]
. Recall that an
ortholattice
is a bounded lattice
(L,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
with an antitone involution
′
which is a complementation, and an
orthomodular lattice
is an ortholattice
(L,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
satisfying the so-called
orthomodular law
, i.e.

(OM)

if
x\leq y
then
y=x\vee(y\wedge x^{\prime})

which is equivalent to its dual

if
x\leq y
then
x=y\wedge(x\vee y^{\prime})
.

However, it was recognized later that if the elements
x
and
y
are not
orthogonal
, i.e. if not
x\leq y^{\prime}
then the join
x\vee y
need not exist in accordance with quantum theory. Hence, so-called orthomodular posets were introduced (see e.g.
[
6
]
) as follows:

An
orthomodular poset
is a bounded poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
with an antitone involution
′
which is a complementation satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

if
x\perp y
then
x\vee y
is defined,

(ii)

if
x\leq y
then
y=x\vee(y\wedge x^{\prime})
. (OM)

Here and in the following,
x\perp y
means
x\leq y^{\prime}
. Observe that the expression in (ii) is well-defined because
x\perp y^{\prime}
yields that
y^{\prime}\vee x
exists and, by De Morgan’s laws, also
y\wedge x^{\prime}=(y^{\prime}\vee x)^{\prime}
is defined and, due to
y\wedge x^{\prime}\perp x
also
x\vee(y\wedge x^{\prime})
is defined. Of course, (ii) is equivalent to its dual

x\leq y\text{ implies }x=y\wedge(x\vee y^{\prime}).

It is evident that if the lattice
\mathbf{L}=(L,\vee,\wedge,{}^{\prime},0,1)
is Boolean, i.e. a distributive complemented lattice then it is orthomodular. Unfortunately, a similar result does not hold for distributive posets. This is the reason why we introduced the concept of a generalized orthomodular poset (see e.g.
[
5
]
) which, as we will show, can be also a Boolean poset. Hence, we essentially extend the class of orthomodular posets in such a way that they share more natural properties with orthomodular lattices than orthomodular posets do. This is one of our goals in this paper. The second problem connected with orthomodular posets was to find such a poset of minimal size that is not a lattice. As far as we know, this problem was not solved previously. We will present a complete solution.

Basic concepts

In the following we need several concepts and notations which are presented in this section.

Let
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq)
be a poset,
A,B\subseteq P
and
a,b\in P
. We define
A\leq B
if and only if
x\leq y
for all
x\in A
and all
y\in B
. Instead of
A\leq\{b\}
,
\{a\}\leq B
and
\{a\}\leq\{b\}
we simply write
A\leq b
,
a\leq B
and
a\leq b
, respectively. The sets

\displaystyle L(A)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid x\leq A\},

\displaystyle U(A)

\displaystyle:=\{x\in P\mid A\leq x\}

are called the
lower cone
and
upper cone
of
A
, respectively. Instead of
L(A\cup B)
,
L(A\cup\{b\})
,
L(\{a,b\})
and
L\big(U(A)\big)
we write
L(A,B)
,
L(A,b)
,
L(a,b)
and
LU(A)
, respectively. Analogously, we proceed in similar cases. Recall that
\mathbf{P}
is called
distributive
(see e.g.
[
9
]
) if it satisfies the identity

L\big(U(x,y),z\big)\approx LU\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big)

or, equivalently, one of the following identities:

\displaystyle UL\big(U(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx U\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle U\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx UL\big(U(x,z),U(y,z)\big),

\displaystyle LU\big(L(x,y),z\big)

\displaystyle\approx L\big(U(x,z),U(y,z)\big).

Here and in the following
L\big(U(x,y),z\big)\approx LU\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big)
means that

L\big(U(x,y),z\big)=LU\big(L(x,z),L(y,z)\big)\text{ holds for all }x,y,z\in P.

It can be easily seen that if
\mathbf{P}
is a lattice then it is a distributive poset if and only if it satisfies the distributive law

(x\vee y)\wedge z\approx(x\wedge z)\vee(y\wedge z).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(P,\leq,0,1)
be a bounded distributive poset and
a,b,c,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in P
. Then
(i)
and
(ii)
hold:

(i)

If
b
and
c
are complements of
a
then
b=c
,

(ii)

if
a\leq b
,
U(a,a^{\prime})=\{1\}
and
L(b,b^{\prime})=\{0\}
then
b^{\prime}\leq a^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(i)

We have

\displaystyle L(b)

\displaystyle=L(1,b)=L\big(U(c,a),b\big)=LU\big(L(c,b),L(a,b)\big)=LU\big(L(c,b),0\big)=

\displaystyle=LU\big(L(b,c),0)=LU\big(L(b,c),L(a,c)\big)=L\big(U(b,a),c\big)=L(1,c)=L(c)

and hence
b=c
.

(ii)

We have

\{0\}\subseteq L(a,b^{\prime})\subseteq L(b,b^{\prime})=\{0\}

and hence
L(a,b^{\prime})=\{0\}
which implies

\displaystyle b^{\prime}

\displaystyle\in L(b^{\prime})=L(1,b^{\prime})=L\big(U(a,a^{\prime}),b^{\prime}\big)=LU\big(L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=LU\big(0,L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=LUL(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})=L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\subseteq L(a^{\prime}),

i.e.
b^{\prime}\leq a^{\prime}
.

∎

In order to avoid the mentioned discrepancy that a distributive complemented poset need not be orthomodular, we define a concept that is a bit more general and which was introduced in
[
5
]
(cf. also the paper
[
3
]
):

Definition 2.2
.

A generalized orthomodular poset is a bounded poset
\mathbf{P}=(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
with an antitone involution that is a complementation that satisfies the condition

(GOM)

x\leq y
implies
U(y)=U\big(x,L(y,x^{\prime})\big)
.

It is worth noticing that (GOM) is equivalent to its dual

x\leq y\text{ implies }L(x)=L\big(y,U(x,y^{\prime})\big),

and if the poset is
orthogonal
, i.e. if for all
x,y\in P
with
x\leq y^{\prime}
there exists
x\vee y
, then (GOM) is equivalent to (OM) and hence
\mathbf{P}
is an orthomodular poset.

Recall that a
Boolean poset
is a distributive complemented poset.

By Lemma
2.1
the complementation in a Boolean poset is unique and antitone. This fact will be used in proofs of our results in Section 4. It is easy to prove the following assertion.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{B}=(B,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a Boolean poset. Then
\mathbf{B}
is a generalized orthomodular poset.

Proof.

Since
x
and
x^{\prime\prime}
are complements of
x^{\prime}
, we obtain
x^{\prime\prime}\approx x
by Lemma
2.1
(i). According to Lemma
2.1
(ii),
′
is antitone. Finally, if
x\leq y
then

U(y)=ULU(y)=UL\big(U(y),1\big)=UL\big(U(x,y),U(x,x^{\prime})\big)=U\big(x,L(y,x^{\prime})\big)

using distributivity of
\mathbf{B}
.
∎

.

This poset is not an orthomodular poset since

The smallest non-lattice orthomodular poset

As mentioned in the introduction, as far as we know, the smallest non-lattice orthomodular poset is not known up to now. Sometimes the following 20-element non-lattice orthomodular poset was considered (Fig. 2). It is the poset of all subsets
A
of the set

4
Horizontal sums

The aim of this section is to describe a construction of generalized orthomodular posets by means of so-called horizontal sums. For the reader’s convenience, let us recall this concept.

Let
\mathbf{P}_{i}=(P_{i},\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1),i\in I,
be a non-empty family of bounded posets with an antitone involution. By the
horizontal sum
of the
\mathbf{P}_{i}
we mean a poset
\mathbf{P}
that is the union of disjoint copies of the posets
\mathbf{P}_{i}
where the bottom and top elements of the
\mathbf{P}_{i}
, respectively, are identified.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{P}_{i}=(P_{i},\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1),i\in I,
be a non-empty family of generalized orthomodular posets. Then the horizontal sum
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
of the
\mathbf{P}_{i},i\in I,
is a generalized orthomodular poset.

Proof.

If
a,b\in P
and
a\leq b
then there exists some
i\in I
with
a,b\in P_{i}
, thus (GOM) surely holds. If there does not exist some
i\in I
with
a,b\in P_{i}
then
a\parallel b
and hence (GOM) is satisfied trivially for these elements
a
and
b
.
∎

Let us note that if
|I|>1
and each
\mathbf{P}_{i}
is non-trivial (i.e. has more than two elements) then the horizontal sum of the
\mathbf{P}_{i}
is a non-distributive generalized orthomodular poset. Namely, if
j,k\in I
,
j\neq k
,
a\in P_{j}\setminus\{0,1\}
and
b\in P_{k}\setminus\{0,1\}
then

L\big(U(a,a^{\prime}),b\big)=L(1,b)=L(b)\neq L(0)=LU(0)=LU(0,0)=LU\big(L(a,b),L(a^{\prime},b)\big).

Corollary 4.2
.

The horizontal sum of a family of Boolean posets is a generalized orthomodular poset.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.3
, every Boolean poset is a generalized orthomodular poset. The rest follows from Proposition
4.1
.
∎

Example 4.3
.

The orthomodular poset
\mathbf{P}
depicted in Fig. 3 is not a horizontal sum of Boolean posets.

It is a question if every generalized orthomodular poset is the horizontal sum of Boolean posets. In the next example we show that this is not the case.

Example 4.4
.

Consider the poset
\mathbf{P}
depicted in Fig. 6:

\mathbf{P}
is a non-lattice Boolean poset that is not orthomodular since

In what follows we will study generalized orthomodular posets that are horizontal sums of Boolean ones. For this purpose we introduce the compatibility relation analogously as it was done in orthomodular lattices, see e.g.
[
8
]
.

In orthomodular lattices
(L,\vee,\wedge)
the compatibility relation
{\rm C}
is defined as follows:

a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b\text{ if }a=(a\wedge b)\vee(a\wedge b^{\prime})

(
a,b\in L
). Of course, in a Boolean algebra every two elements are compatible. For our reasons, define the relation
{\rm C}
in a generalized orthomodular poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
as follows:

a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b\text{ if }U(a)=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)

(
a,b\in P
). Then
a,b
are called
compatible
and
{\rm C}
is called the
compatibility relation
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a generalized orthomodular poset and
a,b\in P
. Then the following hold:

(i)

a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
if and only if
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b^{\prime}
,

(ii)

a\leq b
implies
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
,

(iii)

if
\{a,b\}\cap\{0,1\}\neq\emptyset
then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
.

Proof.

(i)

This is clear.

(ii)

a\leq b
implies
U(a)=UL(a)=U\big(L(a),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)
.

(iii)

If
a=0
or
b=1
then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
follows from (ii), if
a=1
then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
follows from

\displaystyle U(a)

\displaystyle=U(1)=\{1\}=U(b,b^{\prime})=U\big(L(b),L(b^{\prime})\big)=U\big(L(1,b),L(1,b^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)

and if
b=0
then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
follows from (i) and (ii).

∎

The following result is almost evident.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(B,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a Boolean poset and
a,b\in B
. Then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
.

Proof.

We have
U(a)=UL(a)=UL(a,1)=UL\big(a,U(b,b^{\prime})\big)=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)
.
∎

However, we can prove a more interesting and important result.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be the horizontal sum of the Boolean posets
(B_{i},\leq,{}^{\prime},0,

1),i\in I
, and
a,b\in P
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
,

(ii)

there exists some
i\in I
with
a,b\in B_{i}
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii):

If there would exist no
i\in I
with
a,b\in B_{i}
then there would exist
j,k\in I
with
j\neq k
,
a\in B_{j}\setminus\{0,1\}
and
b\in B_{k}\setminus\{0,1\}
which would imply

U(a)\neq U(0)=U(0,0)=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big),

a contradiction.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i):

If
\{a,b\}\cap\{0,1\}\neq\emptyset
then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
according to Lemma
4.5
. Now assume
a,b\in B_{i}\setminus\{0,1\}
. Because of Lemma
4.6
we have

U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)\cap B_{i}=U\big(L(a,b)\cap B_{i},L(a,b^{\prime})\cap B_{i}\big)\cap B_{i}=U(a)\cap B_{i}=U(a)

which implies
L(a,b)\cup L(a,b^{\prime})\neq\{0\}
and hence

U(a)=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)\cap B_{i}=U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big),

i.e.
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
.
∎

For orthomodular lattices
(L,\vee,\wedge)
the commutator
c(x,y)
was introduced as follows:

c(x,y):=(x\wedge y)\vee(x\wedge y^{\prime})\vee(x^{\prime}\wedge y)\vee(x^{\prime}\wedge y^{\prime})

for all
x,y\in L
(cf. e.g.
[
8
]
). In generalized orthomodular poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
we define analogously

c(x,y):=\Min U\big(L(x,y),L(x,y^{\prime}),L(x^{\prime},y),L(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)

for all
x,y\in P
. Here and in the following
\Min A
for a subset
A
of a poset means the set of all minimal elements of
A
, and
\Min U(A)
means
\Min\big(U(A)\big)
. Let us note that it may happen that
\Min A=\emptyset
if
A
has no minimal elements.

In the following we often identify singletons with their unique element.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a generalized orthomodular poset and
a,b\in P
. Then the following hold:

(i)

c(a,b)=c(b,a)
,

(ii)

c(a,b)=c(a,b^{\prime})=c(a^{\prime},b)=c(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
,

(iii)

c(0,b)=c(1,b)=1
,

(iv)

if
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
and
a^{\prime}\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
then
c(a,b)=1
.

Proof.

(i)

and (ii) are clear.

(iii)

According to (ii) we have

c(0,b)=c(1,b)=\Min U\big(L(1,b),L(1,b^{\prime}),L(0,b),L(0,b^{\prime})\big)=\Min U(b,b^{\prime},0)=1.

(iv)

If
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
and
a^{\prime}\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
then

\displaystyle c(a,b)

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=\Min\Big(U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime})\big)\cap U\big(L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)\Big)=\Min\big(U(a)\cap U(a^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=\Min U(a,a^{\prime})=1.

∎

Corollary 4.9
.

Let
(B,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a Boolean poset and
a,b\in B
. Then
c(a,b)=1
.

Proof.

We have
a,a^{\prime},b\in B
and hence
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
and
a^{\prime}\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
according to Lemma
4.6
which implies
c(a,b)=1
by Lemma
4.8
.
∎

Now we prove a result similar to Theorem
4.7
for the commutator instead of compatibility.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be the horizontal sum of the Boolean posets
(B_{i},\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1),

i\in I
, and
a,b\in P
. Then

c(a,b)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\text{if there exists some }i\in I\text{ with }a,b\in B_{i}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Hence
c(a,b)=1
if and only if there exists some
i\in I
with
a,b\in B_{i}
.

Proof.

First assume there exists some
i\in I
with
a,b\in B_{i}
. If
\{a,b\}\cap\{0,1\}\neq\emptyset
then
c(a,b)=1
according to Lemma
4.8
. Now assume
a,b\in B_{i}\setminus\{0,1\}
. Because of Corollary
4.9
we have

\displaystyle U\big(L(a,b)\cup L(a,b^{\prime})\cup L(a^{\prime},b)\cup L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)\cap B_{i}=

\displaystyle=U\big(L(a,b)\cap B_{i},L(a,b^{\prime})\cap B_{i},L(a^{\prime},b)\cap B_{i},L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\cap B_{i}\big)\cap B_{i}=1

which implies
L(a,b)\cup L(a,b^{\prime})\cup L(a^{\prime},b)\cup L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\neq 0
and hence

\displaystyle c(a,b)

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=

\displaystyle=\Min\Big(U\big(L(a,b)\cup L(a,b^{\prime})\cup L(a^{\prime},b)\cup L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)\cap B_{i}\Big)=1.

Conversely, assume there exists no
i\in I
with
a,b\in B_{i}
. Then there exist
j,k\in I
with
j\neq k
,
a\in B_{j}\setminus\{0,1\}
and
b\in B_{k}\setminus\{0,1\}
and hence

c(a,b)=\Min U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=\Min U(0)=0.

∎

It is worth noticing that the assumptions of Theorem
4.10
are essential. Namely if the generalized orthomodular poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
is neither Boolean nor a horizontal sum of such posets then for
x,y\in P
it may happen that
c(x,y)
differs from both
0
and
1
, see the following example.

Example 4.11
.

(i)

Consider the orthomodular poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
depicted in Fig. 3. Then we compute

\displaystyle c(a,b)

\displaystyle=\Min U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)=\Min U(0,0,0,0,g,h\})=

\displaystyle=\Min U(g,h)=\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\}

which differs from both
0
and
1
.

(ii)

However, the condition from Theorem
4.10
does not characterize the class of generalized orthomodular posets that are horizontal sums of Boolean posets. For example, consider the ortholattice
\mathbf{O}_{6}=(O_{6},\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
visualized in Fig. 7:

One can easily check that
c(x,y)=1
for all
x,y\in O_{6}

(
if we define the commutator in ortholattices in the same way as it was done for orthomodular lattices
)
. Of course, this lattice is not a horizontal sum of Boolean posets, but it is also not a generalized orthomodular poset.

On the other hand, for arbitrary generalized orthomodular posets we can prove the following result.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a generalized orthomodular poset. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

c(x,y)\in\{0,1\}
for all
x,y\in P
,

(ii)

If
x,y\in P
then either
L(x,y)=L(x,y^{\prime})=L(x^{\prime},y)=L(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=0
or

U\big(L(x,y),L(x,y^{\prime}),L(x^{\prime},y),L(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big)=1
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in P
. Then the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle c(a,b)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\Min U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\in U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big),

\displaystyle L(a,b)\cup L(a,b^{\prime})\cup L(a^{\prime},b)\cup L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle L(a,b)=L(a,b^{\prime})=L(a^{\prime},b)=L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0.

Moreover the following are equivalent:

\displaystyle c(a,b)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\Min U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle U\big(L(a,b),L(a,b^{\prime}),L(a^{\prime},b),L(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\big)

\displaystyle=1.

∎

The mutual relationship between the compatibility relation and the commutator is expressed in the following result.

Corollary 4.13
.

If
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
is a horizontal sum of Boolean posets and
a,b\in P
then
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
if and only if
c(a,b)=1
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorems
4.7
and
4.10
.
∎

For the next theorem we extend the notion of the commutator from elements to subsets. For a generalized orthomodular poset
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
and subsets
A
and
B
of
P
we define

c(A,B):=\bigcup_{a\in A,b\in B}c(a,b).

Corollary 4.14
.

The class of generalized orthomodular posets that are horizontal sums of Boolean posets satisfies the identity
c(c(x,y),z)\approx 1
.

Proof.

We have
c(x,y)\in\{0,1\}
according to Theorem
4.10
and
c(0,z)\approx c(1,z)\approx 1
according to Lemma
4.8
.
∎

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a generalized orthomodular poset and
A\subseteq P
. Then we put
A^{\prime}:=\{x^{\prime}\mid x\in A\}
and define

t(x,y,z):=\Min U\bigg(L\Big(\big(c(x,y)\big)^{\prime},x\Big),L\big(c(x,y),z\big)\bigg)

for all
x,y,z\in P
.

The next theorem shows that
t
behaves on horizontal sums of Boolean posets similarly as the ternary discriminator.

Theorem 4.15
.

Let
(P,\leq,{}^{\prime},0,1)
be a generalized orthomodular poset that is a horizontal sum of Boolean posets and
a,b,c\in P
. Then

t(a,b,c)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c&\text{if }a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b\\
a&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

If
a\mathrel{{\rm C}}b
then according to Theorem
4.10
we have
c(a,b)=1
and hence

\displaystyle t(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=\Min U\bigg(L\Big(\big(c(a,b)\big)^{\prime},a\Big),L\big(c(a,b),c\big)\bigg)=\Min U\big(L(0,a),L(1,c)\big)=

\displaystyle=\Min U(0,c)=c.

Otherwise, according to Theorem
4.10
we have
c(a,b)=0
and hence

\displaystyle t(a,b,c)

\displaystyle=\Min U\bigg(L\Big(\big(c(a,b)\big)^{\prime},a\Big),L\big(c(a,b),c\big)\bigg)=\Min U\big(L(1,a),L(0,c)\big)=

\displaystyle=\Min U(a,0)=a.

∎
