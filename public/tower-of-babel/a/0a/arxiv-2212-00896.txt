Nonlinear controllability and function representation by neural stochastic differential equations
Tanya Veeravalli, Maxim Raginsky

Abstract
There has been a great deal of recent interest in learning and approximation of functions that can be expressed as expectations of a given nonlinearity with respect to its random internal parameters. Examples of such representations include “infinitely wide” neural nets, where the underlying nonlinearity is given by the activation function of an individual neuron. In this paper, we bring this perspective to function representation by neural stochastic differential equations (SDEs). A neural SDE is an Itô diffusion process whose drift and diffusion matrix are elements of some parametric families. We show that the ability of a neural SDE to realize nonlinear functions of its initial condition can be related to the problem of optimally steering a certain deterministic dynamical system between two given points in finite time. This auxiliary system is obtained by formally replacing the Brownian motion in the SDE by a deterministic control input. We derive upper and lower bounds on the minimum control effort needed to accomplish this steering; these bounds may be of independent interest in the context of motion planning and deterministic optimal control.



1
Introduction

There has been a great deal of recent interest in learning and approximation of functions that admit continuous representations of the form

\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x;\omega)\mu(\dif\omega),

(1.1)

where
x
takes values in a domain
\mathsf{X}
,
\varphi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{X}\times\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a structured nonlinearity parametrized by the elements of a parameter space
\Omega
, and
\mu
is a probability measure on
\Omega
. For example, the seminal paper of
Barron 1993
on neural net approximation uses Fourier-analytic techniques to express a broad class of continuous functions on a compact set
\mathsf{X}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
in the form (
1.1
) with
\varphi(x;\omega)=\alpha\sigma(\langle\theta,x\rangle+\beta)
, where
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a sigmoidal activation function,
\omega=(\alpha,\theta,\beta)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, and the probability measure
\mu
is obtained from the Fourier transform of
f
.

The attractiveness of such a continuous viewpoint, apart from its considerable abstraction power which will be discussed below, is that it affords efficient finite approximation by sampling. The underlying idea, now commonly referred to as
Maurey’s empirical method
, is as follows: Let
\mathsf{X}
,
\Omega
, and
\varphi
be given. Fix a probability measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathsf{X})
and let
\mathcal{F}
denote the class of all
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathsf{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that can be expressed as in (
1.1
) for some
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
, such that

\displaystyle V_{\pi}(F)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathsf{X}}{\rm var}_{\omega\sim\mu}[\varphi(x;\omega)]\pi(\dif x)<\infty.

Then a simple probabilistic selection argument shows that, for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
and any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exist
N
points
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N}\in\Omega
, such that the function
\hat{F}_{N}(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\varphi(\cdot;\omega_{i})
satisfies

\displaystyle\|F-\hat{F}_{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq\frac{V_{\pi}(F)}{N}

(1.2)

— for
N
randomly sampled points
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mu
, the expectation
{\mathbf{E}}\|F-\hat{F}_{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}=N^{-1}V_{\pi}(F)
, so there exists at least one realization
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{N}
, such that (
1.2
) holds. For instance, in the work of
Barron 1993
and in various extensions
(
Yukich et al. 1995
;
Gurvits and Koiran 1997
;
Ji et al. 2020
)
the finite approximation
\hat{F}_{N}
is a neural net with one hidden layer consisting of
N
neurons.

This idea naturally extends to multilayer neural nets
(
Barron and Klusowski 2018
;
Araújo et al. 2019
;
E et al. 2022
)
, where the elements of the finite-dimensional parameter space
\Omega
are the weights of all the neurons in the net. One can also consider continuum limits of neural nets, such as neural ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
(
Hirsch 1989
;
Chen et al. 2018
;
Li et al. 2019
)
and stochastic differential equations (SDEs)
(
Wong 1991
;
Tzen and Raginsky 2019a
;
Tzen and Raginsky 2019b
;
Li et al. 2020
)
. where the parameter space

\Omega
becomes
infinite-dimensional
. For example, as observed by
E 2017
;
Haber and Ruthotto 2017
and others, one can view the
L
-layer ResNet

\displaystyle x_{\ell+1}=x_{\ell}+\frac{1}{L}f(x_{\ell},w_{\ell}),\qquad\ell=0,\ldots,L-1

(1.3)

with
d
-dimensional input
x_{0}=x
,
k
-dimensional weights
w_{\ell}
, and scalar output
F(x)=\langle\alpha,x_{L}\rangle
as an Euler discretization of a controlled ODE
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t),w(t))
with initial condition
x(0)=x
and output
F(x)=\langle\alpha,x(1)\rangle
. Similar analysis can be given for continuum limits of stochastic neural nets
(
Sonoda and Murata 2019
;
Peluchetti and Favaro 2020
)
. In these settings, the continual representation (
1.1
) becomes a
path integral
, where
\Omega=C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{k})
is the space of continuous paths
\omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{k}
and
\mu
is a probability measure on
\Omega
that emerges in the limit as
L\to\infty
under appropriate assumptions on the distributions of the random vectors
(w_{\ell})^{L-1}_{\ell=0}
for each

.

In this paper, we consider a different class of stochastic models that give rise to path-integral function representations. Instead of (
1.3
), we start with the continuum limit of the
L
-layer net

\displaystyle x_{\ell+1}=x_{\ell}+\frac{1}{L}f(x_{\ell})+\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}g(x_{\ell})w_{\ell},\qquad\ell=0,\dots,L-1

(1.4)

with
d
-dimensional input
x_{0}=x
and scalar output
F(x)={\mathbf{E}}[\langle\alpha,x_{L}\rangle]
, where
w_{0},w_{1},\ldots
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian random vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The vector
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the maps
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
are fixed. In contrast to (
1.3
), the
w_{\ell}
’s in (
1.4
) are not the model weights, but rather serve as a source of stochasticity for generating
random
functions of the initial condition
x
. The recursion (
1.4
) is readily recognized as an Euler discretization of the Itô SDE
\dif X_{t}=f(X_{t})\dif t+g(X_{t})\dif W_{t}
with drift
f
and diffusion matrix
g
. We are specifically interested in the case when
f
and
g
are elements of some parametric class of functions, thus following the formulation of neural SDEs by
Tzen and Raginsky 2019a
;
Tzen and Raginsky 2019b
. Stated informally, our results show that the functions
F
that can be represented by models of this kind are of the (approximate) form

\displaystyle F(x)\approx C_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle\alpha,y\rangle\exp\big(-C_{2}I(x,y)\big)\dif y,

(1.5)

where
c_{1},c_{2}
are some constants and
I(x,y)
is a certain
minimum action
functional pertaining to the deterministic optimal control problem of steering the state of the dynamical system
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t)
from
x
to
y
in finite time by choosing a suitable control
u(\cdot)
. This connection between the existence of densities of diffusion processes and nonlinear controllability goes back to the pioneering work of
Elliott 1969
;
Sheu 1991
gave sharp quantitative estimates involving
I(x,y)
. Conceptually, the representation of the transition densities of a diffusion process via the minimum action
I(x,y)
is related to Feynman’s path integral approach to quantum mechanics and its classical formulation via optimal control
(
Fleming 1983
;
Guerra and Morato 1983
)
. On the other hand, the representation of functions realizable by neural SDEs in the form (
1.5
) allows us to both characterize their structure and quantify the rates of approximation of such functions by finite sums via Maurey’s method. Moreover, some of our results (in particular, upper and lower bounds on the minimum action
I(x,y)
) may be of independent interest in the context of optimal control.

, respectively; the spectral norm of a

. The

. The Jacobian of a differentiable map

2
The set-up and some background

We consider the case when the real-valued function
F(x)
of a vector-valued
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is given by a linear functional of the state of an Itô diffusion process, i.e.,
F(x)={\mathbf{E}}[Z_{T}|X_{0}=x]
where

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}

\displaystyle=f(X_{t})\dif t+g(X_{t})\dif W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x;\,0\leq t\leq T

(2.1a)

\displaystyle Z_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha,X_{t}\rangle.

(2.1b)

Here,
T<\infty
is a fixed time horizon,
(W_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion,
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is the drift,
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is the diffusion matrix, and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a fixed vector. To obtain a representation of
F
in the form of (
1.1
), we take
\Omega=C([0,T];{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, the space of continuous paths
\omega\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
;
\mu
the Wiener measure, under which
(W_{t}(\omega))_{t\in[0,T]}
with
W_{t}(\omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\omega(t)
is the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion; and
\varphi(x;\omega)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\langle\alpha,X_{1}(x,\omega)\rangle
, where
X_{t}(x,\omega)
is the solution of (
2.1a
) at time
t\geq 0
with the initial condition
X_{0}=x
. With these definitions, we have

\displaystyle F(x)={\mathbf{E}}[Z_{1}|X_{0}=x]=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(x;\omega)\mu(\dif\omega).

(2.2)

This representation of
F
as a path integral with respect to the Wiener measure in (
2.2
), while succinct, is not readily amenable to analysis. However, we can reduce the analysis to finite-dimensional integration if the diffusion process (
2.1a
) is regular enough to admit transition densities. One such set of regularity assumptions, while by no means the most general, is sufficient for our purposes:

Assumption 2.1
.

The drift
f(x)
and the diffusion matrix
g(x)
satisfy the following:

1.

Both
f(\cdot)
and
a(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g(\cdot)g(\cdot)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}
are Lipschitz-continuous.

2.

The diffusion is uniformly elliptic, i.e., there exist constants
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{0}>0
, such that
\lambda_{0}\leq\lambda_{\min}(a(x))\leq\lambda_{\max}(a(x))\leq\lambda_{1}
for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Under these assumptions, there exists a family of functions
p_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
, such that each
p_{t}(x,\cdot)
is a probability density, and for any bounded measurable function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[h(X_{t})|X_{0}=x]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}h(y)p_{t}(x,y)\dif y,\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

In fact, more can be said about the regularity of
p_{t}(x,y)
as a function of
t
,
x
, and
y
, stemming from the fact that it is the fundamental solution of the parabolic PDE

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho(t,x)=\langle f(x),\nabla_{x}\rho(t,x)\rangle+\frac{1}{2}{\rm tr}[a(x)\nabla^{2}_{x}\rho(t,x)],\qquad t\geq 0,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

i.e.,
p_{t}(x,y)=\rho(t,x)
with the initial condition
\rho(0,x)=\delta(x-y)
. At any rate, we can now express
F(x)
in terms of
p_{T}(x,y)
as

\displaystyle F(x)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle\alpha,y\rangle p_{T}(x,y)\dif y

(2.3)

and investigate the questions of expressiveness and rates of approximation by analyzing the structure of
p_{t}(x,y)
. For instance, classical results in the theory of parabolic PDEs give Gaussian estimates

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}}{t^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}|y-x|^{2}}{t}\right)-\frac{C_{2}}{t^{(d-\kappa)/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}|y-x|^{2}}{t}\right)\leq p_{t}(x,y)\leq\frac{C_{3}}{t^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}|y-x|^{2}}{t}\right),

where
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},\kappa
are some positive constants. (See
Stroock 2008
for these results and relevant background.) However, these bounds are not suitable for our purposes because the dependence of these constants on various parameters (such as the dimension
d
) may not be optimal and because we are specifically interested in the capability of neural SDEs to represent nonlinear functions of
x
. For this reason, we appeal to more fine-grained estimates due to Sheu:

Theorem 2.1

(
Sheu 1991
)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the transition densities
p_{T}(x,y)
of (
2.1a
) can be bounded below and above as

\displaystyle\frac{k_{1}}{\sqrt{(2\pi T)^{d}\det a(y)}}\exp\left(-c_{1}I_{T}(x,y)\right)\leq p_{T}(x,y)\leq\frac{k_{2}}{(\sqrt{(2\pi T)^{d}\det a(y)}}\exp\left(-c_{2}I_{T}(x,y)\right)

(2.4)

where
k_{1},k_{2},c_{1},c_{2}
are polynomial in
d
,
\lambda_{1}/\lambda_{0}
,
T
, and the Lipschitz constants of
f
and
a
, and
I_{T}(x,y)
is the minimum action functional for the following deterministic control problem:

\displaystyle I_{T}(x,y)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\min_{u(\cdot)}\frac{1}{2}\int^{T}_{0}|u(t)|^{2}\dif t

subject to

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t)

(2.5)

\displaystyle x(0)=x,x(T)=y

Sheu’s estimates sharpen and improve earlier results, which are primarily asymptotic and apply to small
T>0
, small Euclidean (or Riemannian) distance between
x
and
y
, and small noise. For instance,
Kifer 1976
obtains the following Taylor-like expansion under the replacement
g\to\varepsilon g
for a small
\varepsilon>0
under further smoothness conditions on
f,g
, and under the above-mentioned restrictions on
T
,
x
, and
y
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{T}(x,y)&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi\varepsilon^{2})^{d/2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon^{2}}I_{T}(x,y)\right)\\
&\quad\cdot\left(K_{0}(T,x,y)+K_{1}(T,x,y)\varepsilon^{2}+\ldots+K_{m}(T,x,y)\varepsilon^{2m}+o(\varepsilon^{2m})\right),\quad m=0,1,\ldots\end{split}

(2.6)

where the coefficients
K_{i}(T,x,y)
are given by an explicit recursion. The main point here is that, to a reasonable approximation,
p_{T}(x,y)
is inversely proportional to the exponential of the minimum action
I_{T}(x,y)
(with a multiplicative constant).

2.1
The role of finite-time nonlinear controllability

Another lens through which we can view the above observation is that the bounds of Sheu are inversely proportional to the exponential of the minimal “control energy” needed to transfer the state of the
deterministic
control-affine system

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t)

(2.7)

from
x(0)=x
to
x(T)=y
. (This system is obtained from (
2.1a
) by formally replacing the Brownian motion
W
with a deterministic input
u
.) Such a relationship between the form of the transition density of a diffusion process and deterministic optimal control is, in fact, exact for linear SDEs with
f(x)=Ax
and
g(x)=G
for some matrices
A,G\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

(
Brockett 1976
)
. In this case, the transition densities are Gaussian,

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi t)^{d}\det W(t)}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle y-e^{tA}x,W(t)^{-1}(y-e^{tA}x)\rangle\right),

where the covariance matrix

\displaystyle W(t)=\int^{t}_{0}e^{sA}GG^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}(e^{sA})^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}\dif s

is the time-
t
controllability Gramian of the linear time-invariant system
\dot{x}(t)=Ax(t)+Gu(t)

(
Brockett 1970
)
. Moreover, the quantity appearing in the exponent is (minus) the minimum action
I_{T}(x,y)
defined in (
2.5
) for this system. Assumption
2.1
will be satisfied if
GG^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}
is positive definite, in which case the controllability Gramian is nonsingular for all
t
. On the qualitative side, a classic result of
Elliott 1969
says that the complete controllability of the deterministic system (
2.7
) is sufficient for the existence of smooth transition densities of the Itô diffusion (
2.1a
); moreover,
Clark 1973
showed that it is also necessary. Remarkably, the result of Sheu (Theorem
2.1
) provides a sharp
quantitative statement
of this equivalence for uniformly elliptic diffusions, phrased in terms of the minimum action functional
I_{T}(x,y)
. (Note that the system (
2.7
) is completely controllable since
g(x)
has full rank for each
x
by virtue of Assumption
2.1
, cf. for example
Sontag 1998
).

The importance of this result for our purposes is twofold: First, since Eq. (
2.3
) expresses the function
F(x)
as an expectation of the linear form
\langle\alpha,\cdot\rangle
w.r.t. the transition density
p_{T}(x,\cdot)
, we conclude that the capability of neural SDEs to represent nonlinear functions hinges on the extent to which the minimum action
I_{T}(x,y)
differs from a quadratic form like
\frac{1}{2}\langle y-L(T)x,\Sigma(T)^{-1}(y-L(T)x)\rangle
for some
L(T)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
and a symmetric positive-definite
\Sigma(T)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
. Second, tight upper and lower bounds on
I_{T}(x,y)
translate into estimates of the constant
V_{\pi}(F)
that controls the dependence of the rate of approximation of
F
by finite sums, in particular in terms of the dimension
d
and other model parameters. We turn to the derivation of such bounds next.

3
Upper and lower bounds on the minimum action functional

In this section, we obtain upper and lower bounds on the minimum action
I_{T}(x,y)
, which we need in order to estimate the transition density
p_{T}(x,y)
. Finite-time optimal transfer of a controlled dynamical system between a prescribed pair of initial and final states is a fundamental primitive in various settings, such as motion planning in robotics
(
Li and Canny 1993
)
or the theory of optimal synthesis
(
Piccoli and Sussmann 2000
)
, so these bounds may be of independent interest.

3.1
Upper bound via feedback linearization

To obtain an upper bound on
I_{T}(x,y)
, it suffices to consider any suboptimal control that transfers (
1.1
) from
x
to
y
in time
T
. A particularly simple way to do this is by
feedback linearization

(
Sontag 1998
, Section 5.3)
: We use a state feedback control of the form
u=k(x,v)
so that the closed-loop system
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))k(x(t),v(t))
becomes, possibly after a smooth and invertible change of coordinates, a completely controllable linear system with input
v(\cdot)
. In our case, since
g(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is invertible by Assumption
2.1
, we can take
k(x,v)=g(x)^{-1}(v-f(x))
, resulting in
\dot{x}(t)=v(t)
. This system is evidently completely controllable and, in particular, can be steered from
x(0)=x
to
x(T)=y
by means of the constant control
v(t)=\frac{y-x}{T}
. Using this together with uniform ellipticity, we obtain the following simple estimate:

Proposition 3.1
.

\displaystyle I_{T}(x,y)\leq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{0}}\int^{T}_{0}\Big|\frac{y-x}{T}-f\Big(x+\frac{t}{T}(y-x)\Big)\Big|^{2}\dif t.

3.2
Lower bound via nonlinear variation of parameters

To obtain a lower bound on
I_{T}(x,y)
, we fix an arbitrary control
u\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and consider two dynamical systems, one with control and one without:

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\dot{x}(t)

\displaystyle=f(x(t)).

For
t\geq s
, denote by
\varphi^{u}_{s,t}(x)
the state of the first system at time
t
starting from
x(s)=x
, and define
\varphi_{s,t}(x)
similarly for the second system. Since the system
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))
is time-invariant, we have
\varphi_{s,t}(\cdot)=\varphi_{0,t-s}(\cdot)
, so we can write
\varphi_{t-s}(\cdot)
instead. The solutions of the two systems at time
T
are related by the nonlinear variation-of-parameters formula
(
Hairer et al. 1993
, p. 96)

\displaystyle\varphi^{u}_{0,T}(x)-\varphi_{T}(x)=\int^{T}_{0}(\varphi_{T-t})_{*}(\varphi^{u}_{0,t}(x))g(\varphi^{u}_{0,t}(x))u(t)\dif t,

(3.1)

where
(\varphi_{T-t})_{*}
denotes the Jacobian of the flow map
\varphi_{T-t}
. We then have the following:

Proposition 3.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
,

\displaystyle I_{T}(x,y)\geq\frac{1}{2\lambda_{1}S_{T}(f)}|y-\varphi_{T}(x)|^{2},

(3.2)

where

\displaystyle S_{T}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int^{T}_{0}\sup_{\bar{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|(\varphi_{T-t})_{*}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\dif t.

(3.3)

Proof.

Choose any control
u(\cdot)
that transfers
x
to
y
in time
T
subject to
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t)
. Since we then have
\varphi^{u}_{0,T}(x)=y
, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in (
3.1
) gives

\displaystyle|y-\varphi_{T}(x)|^{2}\leq\left(\int^{T}_{0}\|(\varphi_{T-t})_{*}(\varphi_{0,t}(x))\|^{2}\dif t\right)\left(\int^{T}_{0}|g(\varphi^{u}_{0,t}(x))u(t)|^{2}\dif t\right).

Moreover, because
a(\cdot)=g(\cdot)g(\cdot)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}\preceq\lambda_{1}I_{d}
, we can further upper-bound this by

\displaystyle|y-\varphi_{T}(x)|^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}\cdot\int^{T}_{0}\sup_{\bar{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|(\varphi_{T-t})_{*}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\dif t\cdot\int^{T}_{0}|u(t)|^{2}\dif t.

Rearranging and using the definition of
I_{T}(x,y)
gives (
3.2
).
∎

Note that the quantity
S_{T}(f)
defined in (
3.3
) depends only on the drift
f
and pertains to stability properties of the the autonomous dynamics
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))
. In order to instantiate the lower bound (
3.2
), we need estimates on
S_{T}(f)
. To that end, we will make use of the fact that the Jacobian
(\varphi_{t})_{*}(\bar{x})
of the flow map
\varphi_{t}
is the solution
\Lambda(t)
at time
t
of the variational equation

\displaystyle\dot{\Lambda}(t)=f_{*}(\varphi_{t}(\bar{x}))\Lambda(t),\qquad t\geq 0

(3.4)

with the initial condition
\Lambda(0)=I_{d}

(
Sontag 1998
, Section 2.8)
.

Assumption 3.1
.

The drift
f
is such that

\displaystyle M(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\lambda_{\max}\Bigg(\frac{f_{*}(x)+f_{*}(x)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}}{2}\Bigg)<\infty.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that the quantity
M(f)
can be positive, negative, or zero.

Proposition 3.3
.

Under Assumption
3.1
,

\displaystyle S_{T}(f)\leq\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2|M(f)|}{e^{2\max\{M(f),0\}T}},&M(f)\neq 0\\
T,&M(f)=0\end{cases}.

(3.5)

Proof.

In order to upper-bound
S_{T}(f)
, we need uniform control on the operator norms
\|(\varphi_{t})_{*}(\bar{x})\|
for all
\bar{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. To that end, we make use of the
measure
(or the
logarithmic norm
) of a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

(
Desoer and Vidyasagar 1975
, Section II.8)
: Let a norm
\|\cdot\|
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be given, and let
\|A\|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sup\{\|Av\|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\|v\|=1\}
denote the corresponding induced norm of
A
. The measure of
A
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|
is then defined as the right directional derivative of
\|\cdot\|
at
I_{d}
in the direction of
A
:

\displaystyle\mu[A]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim_{\theta\searrow 0}\frac{\|I_{d}+\theta A\|-1}{\theta}.

The following result is key (see, e.g.,
(
Desoer and Vidyasagar 1975
, p. 34)
):

Lemma 3.1

(Coppel’s inequality)
.

Let
A\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
be a continuous matrix-valued function. Then any solution of the time-inhomogeneous matrix ODE
\dot{\Lambda}(t)=A(t)\Lambda(t)
for
0\leq t\leq T
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\Lambda(t)\|\leq\|\Lambda(0)\|\exp\Bigg(\int^{t}_{0}\mu[A(s)]\dif s\Bigg)

where
\mu[A]
is the measure of
A
w.r.t. the induced norm
\|\cdot\|
.

Since
\mu[A]
can be positive, negative, or zero, Lemma
3.1
is a strict improvement over the usual estimates based on Grönwall’s inequality. We will apply it to the case when
\|\cdot\|
is the spectral norm, i.e., the matrix norm induced by the Euclidean norm
|\cdot|
, so that

\mu[A]=\lambda_{\max}\Big(\frac{A+A^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}}{2}\Big)

(
Desoer and Vidyasagar 1975
, p. 33)
. Now fix some
\bar{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Since
(\varphi_{t})_{*}(\bar{x})
is the solution of the variational equation (
3.1
) with initial condition
\Lambda(0)=I_{d}
, Lemma
3.1
gives

\displaystyle\|(\varphi_{t})_{*}(\bar{x})\|

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\int^{t}_{0}\mu[f_{*}(\varphi_{s}(\bar{x})]\dif s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Big(t\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mu[f_{*}(\varphi_{s}(\bar{x})]\Big)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(t\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{f_{*}(\varphi_{s}(\bar{x}))+f_{*}(\varphi_{s}(\bar{x}))^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{M(f)t},

where we have invoked Assumption
3.1
at the end. Now we estimate
S_{T}(f)
. When
M(f)\neq 0
,

\displaystyle S_{T}(f)\leq\int^{T}_{0}\sup_{\bar{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|(\varphi_{T-t})_{*}(\bar{x})\|^{2}\dif t\leq\int^{T}_{0}e^{2M(f)(T-t)}\dif t=\frac{e^{2M(f)T}-1}{2M(f)}.

When
M(f)=0
(e.g., when the Jacobians
f_{*}(\bar{x})
are skew-symmetric),
\|(\varphi_{t})_{*}(\bar{x})\|\leq 1
for all
t\geq 0
and
\bar{x}
, so
S_{T}(f)\leq T
.
∎

We should point out that the lower bound of Proposition
3.2
did not need any structural properties of the candidate control
u(\cdot)
, other than that it transfers
x
to
y
in time
T
. It should be possible to obtain tighter bounds by exploiting the fact that any
optimal
control arises from a Hamiltonian system by Pontryagin’s maximum principle
(
Bonnard and Chyba 2003
)
. We leave this for future work.

4
Function representation by neural SDEs

We are now in a position to look at function representation by neural SDEs. As alluded to earlier, with “non-Gaussian” expressions or estimates for the transition density
p_{T}(x,y)
: (a) we can pinpoint the source of nonlinear dependence of the function
F
on its input
x
and (b) we can obtain explicit bounds on the rate of approximation of
F
by finite sums via Maurey’s empirical method.

We begin with the first of these. The key point here, which is primarily qualitative, is that the dominant contribution of the minimum action
I_{T}(x,y)
to
p_{T}(x,y)
suggests that we should formally think of the function
F
realized by the neural SDE model

\displaystyle\begin{split}\dif X_{t}&=f(X_{t})\dif t+g(X_{t})\dif W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x\\
F(x)&={\mathbf{E}}[\langle\alpha,X_{T}\rangle|X_{0}=x].\end{split}

(4.1)

as approximated by expressions like

\displaystyle\hat{F}(x)=C_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\langle\alpha,y\rangle\exp\left(-C_{2}I_{T}(x,y)\right)\dif y,

where the constants
C_{1},C_{2}
depend on various model parameters; this approximation can be sharpened in appropriate regimes by applying asymptotic expansions for
p_{T}(x,y)
, such as Eq. (
2.6
) due to
Kifer 1976
. To a rough first approximation, we can think of
F(x)
in terms of sampling from a Gaussian density with mean
\varphi_{T}(x)
and the inverse of the covariance matrix given by the controllability Gramian of the linearization of the deterministic system
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))+g(x(t))u(t)
along the trajectory generated by zero control
u(\cdot)\equiv 0
(this controllability Gramian is referred to as the “Malliavin covariance matrix” in
Bismut 1984
due to the deep links between these ideas and Malliavin calculus). Here, as we recall,
\varphi_{T}(x)
is the flow map of the autonomous ODE
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))
, i.e., just the deterministic contribution of the drift. Moreover, we can iterate the method of Section
3.2
to further express
I_{T}(x,y)
in terms of
\frac{1}{2}|y-\varphi_{T}(x)|^{2}
plus higher-order terms (see, e.g.,
Lesiak and Krener 1978
). We can also appeal to recent results on the expressive power of neural ODEs
(
Li et al. 2019
;
Tabuada and Gharesifard 2021
)
in order to isolate the contribution of the drift to the expressiveness of neural SDEs. We plan to explore these directions in future work.

Moving on to the rates of approximation, we instantiate Maurey’s empirical method by Monte Carlo simulation, i.e., generating
N
independent copies
X^{x,1}_{T},\ldots,X^{x,N}_{T}
of
X_{T}
in (
4.1
) and then building the finite approximation
\hat{F}_{N}(x)=N^{-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\langle\alpha,X^{x,i}_{T}\rangle
. Since each
X^{x,i}_{T}
is an independent sample from
p_{T}(x,\cdot)
, for any probability distribution
\pi
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
\varphi_{T}(\cdot)\in L^{2}(\pi,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\|F-\hat{F}_{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathbf{E}}\Bigg[\Bigg(\langle\alpha,X_{T}\rangle-\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\langle\alpha,X^{x,i}_{T}\rangle\Bigg)^{2}\Bigg]\pi(\dif x)=\frac{V_{\pi}(F)}{N},

where

\displaystyle V_{\pi}(F)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\rm var}[\langle\alpha,X_{T}\rangle|X_{0}=x]\pi(\dif x).

We can then estimate
V_{\pi}(F)
as follows:

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
f
and
g
satisfy Assumption
2.1
. Then

\displaystyle V_{\pi}(F)\leq k_{2}|\alpha|^{2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}}{c_{2}\lambda_{0}}\frac{S_{T}(f)}{T}\right)^{d/2}\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}d}{c_{2}}S_{T}(f)+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|\varphi_{T}(x)|^{2}\pi(\dif x)\right).

(4.2)

Proof.

Using the upper bound in Theorem
2.1
and the lower bound in Proposition
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle{\rm var}[\langle\alpha,X_{T}\rangle|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha|^{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|y|^{2}p_{T}(x,y)\dif y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k_{2}|\alpha|^{2}}{\sqrt{(2\pi\lambda_{0}T)^{d}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|y|^{2}\exp\Big(-c_{2}I_{T}(x,y)\Big)\dif y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k_{2}|\alpha|^{2}}{\sqrt{(2\pi\lambda_{0}T)^{d}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|y|^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{c_{2}}{2\lambda_{1}S_{T}(f)}|y-\varphi_{T}(x)|^{2}\Big)\dif y

Evaluating the Gaussian integral and taking expectations w.r.t.
\pi
, we get (
4.2
).
∎

If Assumption
3.1
is also in force, then one can substitute the bounds of Proposition
3.3
for
S_{T}(f)
. Then
V_{\pi}(F)
may or may not scale exponentially with dimension
d
and the time horizon
T
depending on the stability properties of the autonomous ODE
\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))
. Just as in the case of feedforward neural nets
(
Barron 1993
;
Ji et al. 2020
)
, this exponential scaling is to be generally expected, apart from various special cases, such as stable linear and nonlinear systems.

4.1
Example: stochastic recurrent neural nets

We illustrate the above results in the particular case when

\displaystyle f(x)=-\frac{x}{\tau}+A\boldsymbol{\sigma}(x),\qquad g(x)=cI_{d}

where
\tau,c
are given positive constants,
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is a given matrix, and

\boldsymbol{\sigma}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\big(\sigma(x_{1}),\sigma(x_{2}),\ldots,\sigma_{d}(x)\big)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}

is a diagonal map constructed from a sigmoidal scalar nonlinearity
\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
0\leq\sigma^{\prime}(r)\leq\gamma
for all
r\in{\mathbb{R}}
. This corresponds to a stochastic recurrent neural net model

\displaystyle\dif X_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(-\frac{X_{t}}{\tau}+A\boldsymbol{\sigma}(X_{t})\right)\dif t+c\dif W_{t},\qquad X_{0}=x,\,0\leq t\leq T

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{E}}[\langle\alpha,X_{T}\rangle|X_{0}=x].

It is easy to see that Assumption
2.1
is satisfied, and
\lambda_{0}=\lambda_{1}=c^{2}
.
To verify Assumption
3.1
, we first write down the Jacobian of
f
:

\displaystyle f_{*}(x)=-\frac{1}{\tau}I_{d}+A\,{\rm diag}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}(x)),\qquad\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\prime}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\big(\sigma^{\prime}(x_{1}),\ldots,\sigma^{\prime}(x_{d})\big)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}.

Following
Hirsch 1989
, we can use the Geršgorin disc theorem
(
Bhatia 1997
, p. 244)
to estimate the eigenvalues of
\frac{1}{2}[f_{*}(x)+f_{*}(x)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}]
:

\displaystyle M(f)=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\lambda_{\max}\left(\frac{f_{*}(x)+f_{*}(x)^{\hbox{{\tiny T}}}}{2}\right)\leq-\frac{1}{\tau}+\gamma\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\Bigg(A_{ii}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\,j\neq i}(|A_{ij}|+|A_{ji}|)\Bigg).

For example, if
A_{ii}\leq\kappa
,
|A_{ij}|\leq\beta
for all
i,j
and if every neuron is connected to at most
m\leq d
other neurons, then we can take
M(f)=-\frac{1}{\tau}+\gamma(\kappa+m\beta)
, so different scalings of
S_{T}(f)
with
T
can be achieved by varying the relative magnitudes of the net parameters.
