Divergence conforming finite element methods for flow-transport coupling with osmotic effects Thanks: Updated: August 7, 2026.
Arbaz Khan, David Mora, Ricardo Ruiz-Baier, Jesus Vellojin

Abstract
We propose a model for the coupling of flow and transport equations with porous membrane-type conditions on part of the boundary. The governing equations consist of the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations coupled with an advection-diffusion equation, and we employ a Lagrange multiplier to enforce the coupling between penetration velocity and transport on the membrane, while mixed boundary conditions are considered in the remainder of the boundary. We show existence and uniqueness of the continuous problem using a fixed-point argument. Next, an H(div)-conforming finite element formulation is proposed, and we address its a priori error analysis. The method uses an upwind approach that provides stability in the convection-dominated regime. We showcase a set of numerical examples validating the theory and illustrating the use of the new methods in the simulation of reverse osmosis processes.



keywords

Navier–Stokes equations coupled with transport; Lagrange multipliers; Reverse osmosis; Divergence-conforming finite element methods.

1
Introduction

1.1
Scope

The coupling of Navier–Stokes equations and advection-diffusion equations is central in many applications in industry and engineering. One of such instances is the simulation of filtration processes that occur in water purification where high velocity flow of water with relatively high concentration of salt goes through a unit under reverse osmosis. Even without considering the mechanisms of ion transport and molecule interactions through the membrane, the sole fluid dynamics process already poses interesting open questions. In that context, we can consider a simplified model where water and salt transport are coupled through advection and through a boundary condition at the membrane stating that the filtration velocity is proportional to a linear function of the salt concentration. This model has been recently studied numerically in
[
10
]
, where the coupling mechanisms on the membrane are incorporated through a Nitsche approach. Here we rewrite that model using a membrane Lagrange multiplier and provide a complete well-posedness analysis as well as the analysis of a pressure-robust discretisation.

In analysing the coupled system and separating for a moment the salt transport from the incompressible flow equations, we end up with a generalisation of the Navier–Stokes equations with slip boundary conditions which have been studied extensively starting from the conforming finite element methods proposed in
[
41
,
42
]
. The off-diagonal bilinear form is different from the usual one in that it also includes a pairing between the normal trace of velocity and the Lagrange multiplier taking into account the membrane coupling. Another difficulty in the model considered herein is that a non-homogeneous boundary condition is needed at the inlet. Often the analysis is simply restricted to the case of homogeneous essential boundary conditions, but here the non-homogeneity is important as it permits that the coupling occurs (otherwise the membrane coupling vanishes, and the only solution is the trivial one).
We note that for smooth domains it is expected that discretisations are prone to see the so-called Babuška paradox – a variational crime associated with the approximation of the boundary, and where a sub-optimal convergence is expected (see more details in, e.g.,
[
26
,
40
]
). Similar works focusing on slip boundary conditions imposed with Lagrange multipliers or with penalty can be found in
[
32
,
30
,
43
]
. However in our case we focus the analysis to the case of polygonal boundaries, which are typically encountered in the driving application of water desalination.

We also stress that the coupling with salt transport adds complexity to the model. The unique solvability of the coupled problem is analysed by casting it as a fixed-point equation and using Banach’s fixed-point theorem. The fixed-point operator consists of the solution operator associated with the Navier–Stokes equations with membrane (or mixed slip-type) boundary conditions, composed with the solution operator associated with an advection-diffusion equation. The unique solvability of the first sub-problem is established using a Stokes linearisation combined with the fixed-point theory in the case of non-homogeneous boundary conditions. The unique solvability of the outer fixed-point problem results as a consequence of an assumption of smallness of data, which in our context reduces to impose a condition on the inlet velocity and on the constitutive equation for the membrane interaction term.

We mention that related works where non-homogeneous mixed boundary conditions are of importance include, for instance, the solvability analysis of Navier–Stokes equations with free boundary
[
31
]
, Boussinesq-type of equations with leaking boundary conditions and Tresca slip
[
28
]
, the fixed-point analysis for Navier–Stokes equations with mixed boundary conditions
[
35
]
, the work
[
24
]
addressing the analysis on viscous flows around obstacles with non-homogeneous boundary data, the solvability of Boussinesq equations with mixed (and non-homogeneous) boundary data
[
2
,
11
]
, and the regularity of split between normal and tangential parts of the velocity as boundary conditions for the Navier–Stokes equations in
[
14
]
.
However it is important to mention that, up to our knowledge, the set of equations we face here has not been analysed in the existing literature.

The divergence-conforming discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method (introduced in
[
12
]
) represents a very useful numerical approach for solving partial differential equations, particularly in the context of fluid dynamics and electromagnetism. In contrast with the conforming formulation for incompressible flows and standard DG methods, the divergence-conforming variant ensures that the discrete velocity is divergence-free, a critical feature in view of conservative discretisations. Additionally, velocity error estimates could be determined in a manner that is resilient to variations in pressure. Moreover, locally, conservation guarantees a divergence-form representation for the coupled systems at the discrete level. Studies on this regard can be found in, for instance,
[
3
,
37
,
9
,
38
,
25
]
. In
[
37
]
the authors propose an div-free conforming scheme for a double diffusive flow on porous media, where the divergence-conforming Brezzi-Douglas-Marini (BDM) elements of order
k\geq 1
are used for velocity approximation, discontinuous elements of order
k-1
are used for the pressure, and continuous elements of order
k
for the temperature. To enforce
\mathrm{H}^{1}
-continuity of velocities, they resort to an interior penalty DG technique. We also have the work of
[
9
]
, in which the authors study a divergence-conforming finite element method for the doubly-diffusive problem. It considers temperature-dependent viscosity and potential cross-diffusion terms while maintaining the coercivity of the diffusion operator. The numerical scheme is based on H(div)-conforming BDM elements for velocity of order
k
, discontinuous elements for pressure of order
k-1
, and Lagrangian finite elements for temperature and solute concentration of order
k
. This formulation ensures divergence-free velocity approximations.

2
Model problem

Let us consider that the boundary of
\Omega
is decomposed as
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}
. The sub-boundary
\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}
corresponds to the inflow,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}
is the outflow boundary,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}}
is a no-slip no penetration boundary, and
\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}
is the porous membrane boundary, see Figure
2.1
for the particular case when
\Omega=[0,L]\times[0,d]
.

The fluid inside the channel is assumed to be Newtonian, incompressible, and composed only by water and salt. The density is taken to be
\rho_{0}
, whereas the viscosity is given as
\mu_{0}
. The solute diffusivity through the solvent is given by
D_{0}
. It is also assumed that the effect of pressure drop inside the channel due to viscous effects on the permeate flux (solution-diffusion equation) is negligible.

Cross-flow membrane filtration model.

The resulting model is a coupling between Navier–Stokes and convection–diffusion equations:

\displaystyle-\mu_{0}\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\rho_{0}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

(1)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle-D_{0}\Delta\theta+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\theta

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Here,

,

, and

represent the fluid velocity, pressure and concentration profile, respectively. Along with (

) we have the following set of boundary conditions:

(2a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}},\;\theta=\theta_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}},

(2b)

\displaystyle-D_{0}\nabla\theta\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}},

(2c)

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})\boldsymbol{n}-p\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}},

(2d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}},

(2e)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle=0

In (

), a parabolic inflow is considered with a fixed salt concentration representing a fully developed sea water flow trough the channel. In (

denotes the flow velocity at the membrane as a function of the concentration and it can be represented using the Darcy–Starling law. As usual in membrane filtration processes, there are several orders of magnitude difference between the inlet and permeate flow velocities. More precisely, relating (

) we have the following inequality:

(3)

0\leq g(\theta)\ll|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}|.

Furthermore, and motivated by mass conservation properties of the flow, it is well-known that the inflow velocity (

where we are also assuming that the fluid density is a positive constant.

2.1
Weak formulation and preliminary properties

For the forthcoming analysis, instead of (
3
) we can simply assume that there exist positive constants
0<g_{1}\leq g_{2}
such that

(5)

g_{1}\leq g(s)\leq g_{2}\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

and note that, in practical applications,

Note that the Cauchy pseudostress associated with the fluid is defined as

\boldsymbol{\sigma}:=\mu_{0}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-p\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}
is the identity tensor in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}

On the permeable sub-boundary
\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}

(6)

\lambda:=-(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{n},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\;:\;\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}},\,\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}},\,\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}}\},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)\;:\;\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{t}=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}},\,\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}}\},\qquad Q:=L^{2}(\Omega),\qquad W:=\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}),

\displaystyle Z:=\{\tau\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\;:\;\tau=\theta_{\mathrm{in}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}\},\qquad Z_{0}:=\{\tau\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)\;:\;\tau=0\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}\},

where the boundary specifications in the spaces

On the other hand, using the incompressibility condition we can rewrite (

Furthermore, we introduce the following bilinear and trilinear forms

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\mu_{0}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\boldsymbol{u}:\nabla\boldsymbol{v},\qquad\widetilde{a}(\boldsymbol{w};\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\rho_{0}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{v},(q,\xi)):=-\int_{\Omega}q\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\langle\xi,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}},

\displaystyle=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega):\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{v}=0\text{ in }\Omega,\quad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=g(\zeta)\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}},\quad\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}}\right\}.

For the advection term we use the well-known identity

\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla\theta)\tau+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla\tau)\theta=-\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{w})\theta\tau+\int_{\partial\Omega}(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})\theta\tau,

to readily obtain

(15)

\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{w};\tau,\tau)=\frac{1}{2}[(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\tau^{2})_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}}-(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\tau^{2})_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}]\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g},\;\forall\tau\in Z_{0},

and thanks to the inlet boundary condition, the property (
3
), and a simple conservation argument following (
4
), it follows that
\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}}}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\geq\int_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
. Hence,

(16)

\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{w};\tau,\tau)\geq 0,\qquad\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g},\tau\in Z_{0}.

3
Well-posedness of the continuous problem

For the analysis of existence and uniqueness of solution we will use a fixed-point argument separating the solution between two uncoupled problems. First consider the Navier–Stokes equations, which consist in finding, for a given
\zeta=\zeta_{0}+\Theta
(with
\zeta_{0}\in Z_{0}
), the tuple
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},p,\lambda)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\times Q\times W
such that

(17a)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+\widetilde{a}(\boldsymbol{u};\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},(p,\lambda))

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0},

(17b)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u},(q,\xi))

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,g(\zeta)\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W.

Note also that if
(\boldsymbol{w},p,\lambda)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\times Q\times W
is a solution to (
17a
)-(
17b
) then
\boldsymbol{w}
is in the space
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g}
.

Secondly, consider the uncoupled advection–diffusion equation in weak form: For a given advecting velocity
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{w}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g}
(with
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}
), find
\theta_{0}\in Z_{0}
such that

(18)

c(\theta_{0},\tau)+\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{w};\theta_{0},\tau)=-c(\Theta,\tau)-\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{w};\Theta,\tau)\quad\forall\tau\in Z_{0}.

3.1
Well-posedness of the Navier–Stokes equations with membrane boundary conditions

In order to address the unique solvability of (
17a
)-(
17b
), we use a linear Stokes problem with membrane boundary conditions, the Banach fixed-point theorem, and the Babuška–Brezzi theory for saddle-point problems
[
18
]
. For this we follow the analysis from
[
13
]
.
Let us consider the problem of finding, for a given
\zeta\in Z
and a given
\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}
, the tuple
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p,\lambda)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}\times Q\times W
such that

(19a)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},(p,\lambda))

\displaystyle=-a(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{v})-\widetilde{a}(\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{U};\boldsymbol{w}+\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0},

(19b)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},(q,\xi))

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,g(\zeta)\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W.

In order to show that this linear Stokes system is well-posed we follow arguments similar to
[
41
, Lemma 3.1]
,
[
15
,
30
]
, and
[
17
, Section 2.4.3]
. We start with the following result (the proof is carried out in a standard way, but we present it for sake of completeness).

Lemma 1
.

The following inf-sup condition holds

\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{v},(q,\xi))}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}}\gtrsim\|q\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W.

Proof.

Thanks to the Riesz representation theorem, for a given
\xi\in W
there exists
\tilde{\xi}\in\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}})
such that
\|\tilde{\xi}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}=\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}
.
For a given pair
(q,\xi)\in Q\times W
, let us consider the following auxiliary
Stokes
problem with mixed boundary conditions

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}+\nabla\zeta=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=q\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(20)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}-\zeta\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}})\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{out}},

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}\cup\Gamma_{\mathrm{wall}},

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\xi}\boldsymbol{n}\quad\text{on }\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}.

Thanks to
[
19
]
, we can assert that there exists a unique velocity solution to (
20
), for which there holds

(21a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}=q,\quad(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}=\tilde{\xi},\quad(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot\boldsymbol{t})|_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}=0,

(21b)

\displaystyle\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}\lesssim\|q\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}},

and so
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
.
In this way we can write

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{b(\boldsymbol{v},(q,\xi))}{\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}}\geq\frac{b(\hat{\boldsymbol{v}},(q,\xi))}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}}=\frac{\|q\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\|\xi\|^{2}_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}}{\|\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}\|_{1,\Omega}}\gtrsim\|q\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}},

where we have used (
21a
) and (
21b
).

In the context of the fixed point analysis of (
17a
)-(
17b
), for a given
\zeta=\zeta_{0}+\Theta\in Z
we define the linear functional
G_{\zeta}\in(Q\times W)^{\prime}
as follows

\langle G_{\zeta},(q,\xi)\rangle:=\langle\xi,g(\zeta)\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W,

(which, thanks to (
5
) satisfies
\|G_{\zeta}\|_{(Q\times W)^{\prime}}=g_{2}
).
Similarly, for a given
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}
we define the linear functional
F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}^{\prime}
:

\boldsymbol{v}\mapsto\langle F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{v}\rangle:=-\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{w}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{v})-a(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{v}),

where
\boldsymbol{U}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}^{1}(\Omega)
is the divergence-free lifting defined in (
10
).
Then, there holds (see
[
13
, Lemma 16]
)

(22)

\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1,\Omega}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}.

Lemma 2
.

For known liftings
\boldsymbol{U},\Theta
and given
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}
and
\zeta_{0}\in Z_{0}
, there exists a unique
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p,\lambda)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}\times Q\times W
such that

(23a)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},(p,\lambda))

\displaystyle=F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0},

(23b)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},(q,\xi))

\displaystyle=G_{\zeta}(q,\xi)\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W.

Moreover, the following estimates hold

(24a)

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}}\left[\|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}^{\prime}}+\frac{\underline{\alpha}+\|a\|}{\beta}g_{2}\right],

(24b)

\displaystyle\|(p,\lambda)\|_{Q\times W}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\left[\left(1+\frac{\|a\|}{\underline{\alpha}}\right)\|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}^{\prime}}+\frac{\|a\|(\underline{\alpha}+\|a\|)}{\underline{\alpha}\beta}g_{2}\right].

Proof.

Note that the linear functionals are bounded. Indeed, we have

|G_{\zeta}(\xi)|\leq\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\|g(\zeta_{0}+\Theta)\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\leq g_{2}\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\qquad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W,

which, owing to (
5
), implies that

(25)

\|G_{\zeta}\|_{(Q\times W)^{\prime}}=g_{2}.

Also, after using triangle inequality together with the boundedness properties in (
12a
) and (
12d
), we obtain

\displaystyle|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{v})|

\displaystyle=|-\tilde{a}(\boldsymbol{w}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{w}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{v})-a(\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{v})|,

\displaystyle\leq C_{i}\rho_{0}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1}+\mu_{0}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega},

\displaystyle\leq\left[C_{i}\rho_{0}\left(\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\right)+\mu_{0}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1,\Omega}\right]\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega},

and due to (
22
), there holds

\|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}^{\prime}}=C_{i}\rho_{0}\left(\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2}\right)+\mu_{0}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}},

where
C_{i}>0
is the continuity constant of the Sobolev embedding used in (
12d
).

Using Lemma
1
with inf-sup constant
\beta
depending on the trace inequality constant, embedding theorems and elliptic regularity, the coercivity of the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
(
13a
) with constant
\underline{\alpha}=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2}\min\{\frac{1}{C_{P}^{2}},1\}
(where
C_{P}
denotes the Poincaré constant), the continuity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
(
12a
) with constant
\|a\|\leq\max\{1,\mu_{0}\}
, and the continuity of the bilinear form
b(\cdot,(\cdot,\cdot))
with constant
\|b\|\leq 1
in (
12b
), the Babuška–Brezzi theory (see, e.g.,
[
15
, Theorem 2.34]
) guarantees that there exists a unique tuple
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p,\lambda)
solution of (
23a
)–(
23b
), which also satisfies the continuous dependence on data.

Let us remark that the unique Stokes velocity from Lemma
2
will be in the space
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g}
. We now derive a fixed-point strategy for (
17a
)–(
17b
). Let us define the map

\mathcal{J}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\to\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\times Q\times W,\quad\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w})=(\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}),\mathcal{J}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w}),\mathcal{J}_{3}(\boldsymbol{w}))=:(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},p,\lambda),

where
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0},p,\lambda)
is the unique solution to (
23a
)–(
23b
) and
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},p,\lambda)
includes the non-homogeneous boundary condition. We focus our attention on the first component of the mapping, i.e.
\mathcal{J}_{1}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}
. The following results collect the required properties for the application of the Banach fixed-point theorem on
\mathcal{J}_{1}
, and hence the existence and uniqueness of a solution to (
17a
)–(
17b
).

Lemma 3
.

Consider the following closed ball of
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}

\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}}=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g}:\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq R_{0}\}.

Assume that the data (in particular,
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}
) are sufficiently small so that

(26)

0<4R_{0}<1-\sqrt{1-4(\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2}+g_{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}})}.

Then
\mathcal{J}_{1}(\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}})\subseteq\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}}
.

Proof.

Let us fix
R_{0}>0
and consider
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}}
. We have, thanks to the definition of
\mathcal{J}_{1}
, triangle inequality, and (
24a
), that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w})\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}}\left[C_{i}\rho_{0}\left(\|\boldsymbol{w}_{0}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2}\right)+\mu_{0}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}\right]+\frac{\|a\|(\underline{\alpha}+\|a\|)}{\underline{\alpha}\beta}g_{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2}+g_{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim R_{0}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2}+g_{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}},

where the hidden constant depends on
\underline{\alpha},\|a\|,\beta,C_{i}
, and
\rho_{0}
.
After elementary algebraic manipulations we can assert that the right-hand side above is smaller or equal than
\frac{R_{0}}{2}
if (
26
) holds.

Lemma 4
.

There exists a positive constant
L_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}
, depending only on data (in particular, on the inlet velocity
\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}
), such that

\|\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\|_{1,\Omega}\leq L_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}}.

Proof.

Given
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}}
, let us consider the two well-posed Stokes problems (
19a
)-(
19b
) for each fixed velocity and giving the unique solutions

(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}+\boldsymbol{U},p_{1},\lambda_{1})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}),\quad(\boldsymbol{u}_{02}+\boldsymbol{U},p_{2},\lambda_{2})=\mathcal{J}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}),

respectively. Subtracting the corresponding first and second equations in these problems and noticing that
G
does not depend on
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}
, we arrive at

(27a)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02},\boldsymbol{v})+b(\boldsymbol{v},(p_{1}-p_{2},\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}))

\displaystyle=F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{v})-F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{v})\quad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0},

(27b)

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02},(q,\xi))

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W.

Regarding the right-hand side of (
27a
), note now that

\displaystyle|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{v})-F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{v})|

\displaystyle=\biggl|\int_{\Omega}[(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}+\boldsymbol{U})\cdot\nabla(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}+\boldsymbol{U})-(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}+\boldsymbol{U})\cdot\nabla(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}+\boldsymbol{U})]\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}|(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}-\boldsymbol{w}_{1})\cdot\nabla(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}+\boldsymbol{U})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}|+\int_{\Omega}|(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}+\boldsymbol{U})\cdot\nabla(\boldsymbol{w}_{2}-\boldsymbol{w}_{1})\cdot\boldsymbol{v}|,

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}.

On the other hand, in (
27
) we can use as test functions
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02}
,
q=p_{1}-p_{2}
,
\xi=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}
and subtract the two equations to obtain

a(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02},\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02})=F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02})-F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02}).

Finally, we use the definition of
\mathcal{J}
, the two previous results, and the coercivity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
to get

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\|^{2}_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{u}_{01}+\boldsymbol{U}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02}-\boldsymbol{U}\|^{2}_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}^{2}}a(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02},\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}^{2}}|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02})-F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{2},\boldsymbol{U}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02})|

\displaystyle\lesssim(\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{01}-\boldsymbol{u}_{02}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle=(\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}})\|\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\|_{1,\Omega}.

Then the desired result follows by dividing through
\|\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})-\mathcal{J}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})\|_{1,\Omega}
, recalling that
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\mathbf{M}_{R_{0}}
, and choosing

(28)

L_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}=\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}^{2}}(2R_{0}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}).

Theorem 5
.

Given
\zeta_{0}\in Z_{0}
, assume that the data is sufficiently small as in (
26
), and in light of (
28
), further assume that
R_{0}
is taken such as

(29)

L_{\mathcal{J}_{1}}=2R_{0}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}<\underline{\alpha}^{2}.

Then there exists a unique solution
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},p,\lambda)\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\times Q\times W
to (
17a
)–(

).

Proof.

The result is a direct consequence of the well-definedness of
\mathcal{J}
together with Lemmas

and

, and the fact that (

is a contraction mapping.

Note that the proof of Theorem

is also valid if in Lemmas
3
-

we take any
\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}
with

3.2
Well-posedness of the advection–diffusion equation

The unique solvability of problem (

\displaystyle\|\theta_{0}\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}}\left[\|F_{\boldsymbol{w}_{0},\boldsymbol{U}}\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{0}^{\prime}}+\frac{\underline{\alpha}+\|a\|}{\beta}g_{2}\right](1+\|\theta_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}})+\|\theta_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}},

3.3
Fixed-point analysis for the coupled flow–transport problem

With the development above, we are now able to properly define the following solution operators

\tilde{S}:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\to Z,\quad\boldsymbol{w}\mapsto\tilde{S}(\boldsymbol{w}):=\theta_{0}+\Theta,

where
\theta_{0}
is the unique solution of (
18
) (confirmed in Section
3.2
), and

S:Z\to\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}},\quad\zeta\mapsto S(\zeta)=(S_{I}(\zeta),S_{I\!I}(\zeta),S_{I\!I\!I}(\zeta)):=(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},p,\lambda),

where
(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U},p,\lambda)
is the unique solution of (
17a
)-(
17b
) (established in Section
3.1
).
The nonlinear problem in weak form (
11
) is therefore equivalent to the following fixed-point equation

(31)

\text{find $\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}$ such that}\ \boldsymbol{u}=T(\boldsymbol{u}),

where
T:\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\to\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}
is defined as
\boldsymbol{u}\mapsto T(\boldsymbol{u})=({S}_{I}\circ\tilde{S})(\boldsymbol{u}_{0}+\boldsymbol{U})
.

We proceed then to define the closed ball in
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}

\mathbf{M}_{R_{1}}=\{\boldsymbol{w}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g}:\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}\leq R_{1}\},

and assume that
R_{1}<1
, which (owing to Lemma
3
and (
3.2
)) amounts to consider the assumption on the model data

(32)

\displaystyle\max\{R_{0}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2}+g_{2}+2\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\underline{\alpha}}\left[C_{i}\rho_{0}(R_{0}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}^{2})+\mu_{0}\|\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}+\frac{\underline{\alpha}+\|a\|}{\beta}g_{2}\right](1+\|\theta_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}})+\|\theta_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}}\}<1.

Then we have that
T(\mathbf{M}_{R_{1}})\subseteq\mathbf{M}_{R_{1}}
(
T
maps the ball above into itself).

We can also assert that
T
is Lipschitz continuous. By definition, it suffices to verify the Lipschitz continuity of both
S
(actually, we only require the component
S_{I}
) and
\tilde{S}
.

Lemma 6
.

Assume that (
32
) holds. Then there exists
L_{S}>0
such that

\|S_{I}(\zeta_{1})-S_{I}(\zeta_{2})\|_{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}}\leq L_{S}\|\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}\|_{Z}\qquad\text{ for all $\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in Z$}.

Proof.

For given
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}\in Z
, let
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},p_{1},\lambda_{1}),(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},p_{2},\lambda_{2})\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}\times Q\times W
be the unique solutions to the decoupled Navier–Stokes equations (
17a
)-(
17b
), with
S_{I}(\zeta_{1})=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}
,
S_{I}(\zeta_{2})=\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
, respectively. Precisely from (
17b
), and using the linearity of
g
, we obtain

(33)

b(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2},(q,\xi))=\langle\xi,g(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2})\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall(q,\xi)\in Q\times W.

Then, for a given
(q,\xi)\in Q\times W
and with
\xi\neq 0
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\|_{1,\Omega}(\|q\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}})

\displaystyle\lesssim b(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{2},(q,\xi))

\displaystyle=\langle\xi,g(\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2})\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\|\zeta_{1}-\zeta_{2}\|_{1,\Omega},

where we have used the inf-sup condition from Lemma
1
, the relation (
33
), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Then the result follows after dividing by
\|\xi\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}
on both sides of the inequality. The Lipschitz constant

Lemma 7

Assume that (

Proof.

Consider
\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\boldsymbol{w}_{2}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{X}}^{g}

of (

\boldsymbol{w}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{2}
, respectively. Since

(see (

Then, adding and subtracting

\displaystyle=-\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{2};\theta_{1},\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})-\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2};\theta_{1}-\theta_{2},\theta_{1}-\theta_{2})

In summary, from Lemmas

Numerical experiments

We perform a series of computational tests using the finite element library FEniCS
[
1
]
together with the special module FeniCS
ii

[
29
]
for the treatment of bulk-surface coupling mechanisms.
We perform an experimental error analysis through manufactured solutions. We monitor the errors of each individual unknown, the local convergence rate, and the number of necessary Newton–Raphson iterations to achieve convergence up to a prescribed tolerance of
10^{-7}
on the residuals.
By
e(\cdot)
we denote the error associated with the quantity
\cdot
in its natural norm, and will denote by
h_{i}
the mesh size corresponding to a refinement level
i
. The experimental convergence order is computed as

r(\cdot)=\frac{\log(e_{i}(\cdot))-\log(e_{i+1}(\cdot))}{\log(h_{i})-\log(h_{i+1})}.

To compute
\|\lambda-\lambda_{h}\|_{s,}
(with
s\in\{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}
because we will use Lagrange multipliers in these two spaces) we use the characterisation of
\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}})

in terms of the spectral decomposition of the Laplacian operator (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
).
For this, let
R:\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}})\rightarrow\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}})
be the bounded linear operator defined by

(Ru,v)_{1,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}=(u,v)_{0,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall u,v\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}).

This operator has eigenfunctions
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
forming a basis, associated with a non-increasing sequence of positive eigenvalues
\eta_{i}
. Then for any

u=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}r_{i}
there holds

\|u\|_{s,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}c_{i}^{2}\eta_{i}^{s}\,,

and so
\mathrm{H}^{s}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}})
is the closure of the span of
\{r_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}

in this norm.
During the experiments, different values for the stabilisation parameters will be considered in order to capture the convergence of the method.

In the conforming case, the discrete inf-sup condition to be satisfied is given by

\sup_{\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{b_{h}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},(q_{h},\xi_{h}))}{\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{1,\Omega}}\gtrsim\|q_{h}\|_{0,\Omega}+\|\xi_{h}\|_{-1/2,\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\quad\forall(q_{h},\xi_{h})\in Q_{h}\times W_{h}.

However, in
[
41
]
it is shown that despite choosing stable inf-sup elements such as Taylor–Hood, Mini-element,
\mathbb{P}_{2}-\mathbb{P}_{0}
, etc, together with a typical choice for the Lagrange multiplier space as above, this condition may not be satisfied. To circumvent this difficulty, one can either enrich the velocity space with bubbles having compact support along
\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}
(see
[
41
]
for details), or add suitable residual stabilisation in the discrete problem (see, for example
[
42
,
40
]
). We adopt the latter option.
We define generic finite element spaces
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{h}\subset\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}
,
Q_{h}\subset Q
,
W_{h}\subset W
and
Z_{h}\subset Z
for the velocity, pressure, Lagrange multiplier, and concentration, respectively.
Following
[
40
]
, we first define the following mesh-dependent bilinear form
d_{h}:W_{h}\times W_{h}\to\mathbb{R}
:

(46)

d_{h}(\lambda_{h},\xi_{h}):=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{m}}}h_{e}\int_{e}\lambda_{h}\,\xi_{h}\,\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\forall\lambda_{h},\xi_{h}\in W_{h}.

The resulting stabilised formulation consists in finding
(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h},\theta_{h})\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{h}\times Q_{h}\times W_{h}\times Z_{h}
such that

(47)

\displaystyle a(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+\widetilde{a}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h};\boldsymbol{u}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})+b(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},(p_{h},\lambda_{h}))+s_{1}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle b(\boldsymbol{u}_{h},(q_{h},\xi_{h}))+s_{2}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\lambda_{h}),(q_{h},\xi_{h}))

\displaystyle=\langle\xi_{h},g(\theta_{h})\rangle_{\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}},

\displaystyle c(\theta_{h},\tau_{h})+\widetilde{c}(\boldsymbol{u}_{h};\theta_{h},\tau_{h})

\displaystyle=0,

for all
(\boldsymbol{v}_{h},q_{h},\xi_{h},\tau_{h})\in\boldsymbol{\mathrm{H}}_{h}\times Q_{h}\times W_{h}\times Z_{h}
, where the stabilising bilinear forms are

\displaystyle s_{1}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\xi_{h}),\boldsymbol{v}_{h})

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{0}d_{h}\bigl(\xi_{h}+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\delta\mu_{0}(\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\bigr),

\displaystyle s_{2}((\boldsymbol{u}_{h},p_{h},\xi_{h}),(q_{h},\chi_{h}))

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{0}d_{h}\bigl(\xi_{h}+(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\chi_{h}-\delta(q_{h}\mathbb{I}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}\bigr).

Note that for the conforming method,
the discrete quantities
\xi_{h},\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\,\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n},\,q_{h}\mathbb{I}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}
all belong to
W_{h}
.
Note also that, for a Navier–Stokes model with slip boundary condition,
[
42
]
proved that choosing
\delta=0
and
\alpha_{0}
lower than a threshold yields a stable method. As in
[
23
]
, for
\delta=1
we have symmetry, however a smallness condition on
\alpha_{0}
is needed for sake of stability. For
\delta=-1
we have the anti-symmetric variation of the method
[
16
,
4
]
, whose main advantage is the unconditional stability with respect to
\alpha_{0}
.
