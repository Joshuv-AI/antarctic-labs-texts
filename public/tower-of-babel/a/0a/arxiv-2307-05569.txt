The Redei–Berge symmetric function of a directed graph
Darij Grinberg and Richard P. Stanley

Abstract
Abstract. Let D = ( V , A ) D=\left(V,A\right) be a digraph with n n vertices, where each arc a ∈ A a\in A is a pair ( u , v ) \left(u,v\right) of two vertices. We study the Redei–Berge symmetric function U D U_{D} , defined as the quasisymmetric function ∑ L Des ( w , D ) , n ∈ QSym . \sum\limits L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right),\ n}\in\operatorname*{QSym}. Here, the sum ranges over all lists w = ( w 1 , w 2 , … , w n ) w=\left(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\right) that contain each vertex of D D exactly once, and the corresponding addend is L Des ( w , D ) , n := ∑ i 1 ≤ i 2 ≤ ⋯ ≤ i n ; i p < i p + 1 for each p satisfying ( w p , w p + 1 ) ∈ A x i 1 x i 2 ⋯ x i n L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right),\ n}:=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n};\\ i_{p}<i_{p+1}\text{ for each }p\text{ satisfying }\left(w_{p},w_{p+1}\right)\in A\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}} (an instance of Gessel’s fundamental quasisymmetric functions). While U D U_{D} is a specialization of Chow’s path-cycle symmetric function, which has been studied before, we prove some new formulas that express U D U_{D} in terms of the power-sum symmetric functions. We show that U D U_{D} is always p p -integral, and furthermore is p p -positive whenever D D has no 2 2 -cycles. When D D is a tournament, U D U_{D} can be written as a polynomial in p 1 , 2 ​ p 3 , 2 ​ p 5 , 2 ​ p 7 , … p_{1},2p_{3},2p_{5},2p_{7},\ldots with nonnegative integer coefficients. By specializing these results, we obtain the famous theorems of Redei and Berge on the number of Hamiltonian paths in digraphs and tournaments, as well as a modulo- 4 4 refinement of Redei’s theorem. Keywords: directed graph, symmetric function, tournament, Hamiltonian path, power sum symmetric function. Mathematics Subject Classification 2020: 05A05, 05A19, 05E05, 05C30.

1.1
Digraphs,
V
-listings and
D

We let
\mathbb{N}:=\left\{0,1,2,\ldots\right\}
and
\mathbb{P}:=\left\{1,2,3,\ldots\right\}
. We set
\left[n\right]:=\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}

\left[n\right]

empty if
n\leq 0

The words “list” and “tuple” will be used interchangeably, and will always mean
finite ordered tuples.

We shall next introduce some basic notations regarding digraphs (i.e.,
directed graphs):

Definition 1.1
.

A
digraph
means a pair
\left(V,A\right)
, where
V
is a finite
set and where
A
is a subset of
V\times V
. The elements of
V
are called
the
vertices
of this digraph, and the elements of
A
are called the

arcs
of this digraph. For any further notations, we refer to standard
literature (the definitions in
[
Grinbe17
, §1.1-§1.2]
should suffice)
and common sense. (Our digraphs are allowed to have loops, but this has no
effect on what follows.)

Definition 1.2
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Then, the digraph
\left(V,\ \left(V\times V\right)\setminus A\right)
will be denoted by

\overline{D}
and called the
complement
of the digraph
D
. Its arcs
will be called the
non-arcs
of
D
(since they are precisely the pairs

\left(u,v\right)\in V\times V
that are not arcs of
D
).

Example 1.3
.

If
D
is the digraph

\left(\left\{1,2,3\right\},\ \left\{\left(1,2\right),\ \left(2,2\right),\ \left(3,3\right)\right\}\right),

then its complement
\overline{D}
is the digraph

\left(\left\{1,2,3\right\},\ \left\{\left(1,1\right),\ \left(1,3\right),\ \left(2,1\right),\ \left(2,3\right),\ \left(3,1\right),\ \left(3,2\right)\right\}\right).

Definition 1.4
.

Let
V
be a finite set. A
V
-listing
will mean a list of elements of

V
that contains each element of
V
exactly once.

For example,
\left(2,1,3\right)
is a
\left\{1,2,3\right\}
-listing.

Of course, if
V
is a finite set, then there are exactly
\left|V\right|!
many
V
-listings. They are in a canonical bijection with the
bijective maps from
\left[\left|V\right|\right]
to
V
, and in
a non-canonical bijection with the permutations of
V
.

Convention 1.1
.

If
w
is any list (i.e., tuple), and if
i
is a positive
integer, then
w_{i}
shall denote the
i
-th entry of
w
. (Thus,
w=\left(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{k}\right)
, where
k
is the length of
w
.)

Definition 1.5
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Let
w=\left(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\right)
be a
V
-listing. Then:

(a)

A
D
-descent
of
w
means an
i\in\left[n-1\right]
satisfying
\left(w_{i},w_{i+1}\right)\in A
.

(b)

We let
\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right)
denote
the set of all
D
-descents of
w
.

Example 1.6
.

Let
D
be the digraph
D
from Example
1.3
, and let

w
be the
V
-listing
\left(3,1,2\right)
. Then,
2
is a
D
-descent of

w
(since
\left(w_{2},w_{3}\right)=\left(1,2\right)\in A
), but
1

is not a
D
-descent of
w
(since
\left(w_{1},w_{2}\right)=\left(3,1\right)\notin A
). Hence,
\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right)=\left\{2\right\}
.

Example 1.7
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
V=\left[n\right]
. Let
D
be the digraph
whose vertices are the elements of
V
and whose arcs are all the pairs

\left(i,j\right)\in\left[n\right]^{2}
satisfying
i>j
. Let
w
be a

V
-listing. Then, the
D
-descents of
w
are exactly the descents of
w
in
the usual sense (i.e., the numbers
i\in\left[n-1\right]
satisfying

w_{i}>w_{i+1}
).

We note that
D
-descents for general digraphs
D
have already implicitly
appeared in the work of Foata and Zeilberger
[
FoaZei96
]
, which considers
the number
\operatorname*{maj}\nolimits_{D}^{\prime}w:=\sum\limits_{i\in\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right)}i
for each
V
-listing
w
. We would
not be surprised if what follows can shed some new light on the results of

[
FoaZei96
]
, but so far we have not found any deeper connections.

1.2
Quasisymmetric functions

Next, we introduce some notations from the theory of quasisymmetric functions
(see, e.g.,
[
Stanle01
, §7.19]
or
[
GriRei20
, Chapter 5]
):

Definition 1.8
.

(a)

A
composition
means a finite list of positive
integers. If
\alpha=\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right)

is a composition, then the number
k
is called the
length
of
\alpha
,
whereas the number
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}
is called the

size
of
\alpha
. If
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then a
composition of
n

shall mean a composition having size
n
.

(b)

A
partition
(or
integer partition
) means a
composition that is weakly decreasing.

For example,
\left(2,5,3\right)
is a composition of
10
that has length

3
and is not a partition (since
2<5
).

Definition 1.9
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For any subset
I
of
\left[n-1\right]
, we let

\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)
be the list

\left(i_{1}-i_{0},\ i_{2}-i_{1},\ i_{3}-i_{2},\ \ldots,\ i_{k}-i_{k-1}\right),

where
i_{0},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
are the elements of
\left\{0\right\}\cup I\cup\left\{n\right\}
listed in strictly increasing order. This list

\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)
is a composition of
n
.

Example 1.10
.

If
n=6
and
I=\left\{2,3,5\right\}
, then
\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)=\left(2,1,2,1\right)
.

Note that
\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)
is denoted by

\operatorname*{co}\left(I\right)
in
[
Stanle01
, §7.19]
, but we prefer
to make the dependence on
n
explicit here. In the notation of

[
GriRei20
, Definition 5.1.10]
, the composition
\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)
is the preimage of
I
under the bijection

D:\operatorname*{Comp}\nolimits_{n}\rightarrow 2^{\left[n-1\right]}
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the map

\displaystyle\left\{\text{subsets of }\left[n-1\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left\{\text{compositions of }n\right\},

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\mapsto\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)

is a bijection.

Definition 1.11
.

Consider the ring
\mathbb{Z}\left[\left[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots\right]\right]
of formal power series in countably many indeterminates

x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots
. Two subrings of this ring
\mathbb{Z}\left[\left[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots\right]\right]
are:

•

the ring
\Lambda
of
symmetric functions
(defined, e.g., in

[
Stanle01
, §7.1]
or in
[
GriRei20
, §2.1]
);

•

the ring
\operatorname*{QSym}
of
quasisymmetric functions

(defined, e.g., in
[
Stanle01
, §7.19]
or in
[
GriRei20
, §5.1]
).

We will not actually use any properties of these rings
\Lambda
and

\operatorname*{QSym}
anywhere except in Sections
8
,

6
and
7
(and even there, only
\Lambda
will be
used); thus, a reader unfamiliar with symmetric functions can read

\mathbb{Z}\left[\left[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots\right]\right]
instead
of
\Lambda
or
\operatorname*{QSym}
everywhere else.

Definition 1.12
.

Let
\alpha
be a composition. Then,
L_{\alpha}
will denote the

fundamental quasisymmetric function
corresponding to
\alpha
. This is
a formal power series in
\operatorname*{QSym}
, and is defined as follows:
Let
I
be the unique subset of
\left[n-1\right]
satisfying

\alpha=\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)
. Then, we set

L_{\alpha}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n};\\
i_{p}<i_{p+1}\text{ for each }p\in I\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}\in\operatorname*{QSym}

(where the summation indices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}
range over

\mathbb{P}
).

See
[
Stanle01
, §7.19]
or
[
GriRei20
, §5]
for more about these
fundamental quasisymmetric functions
L_{\alpha}
(originally introduced by
Ira Gessel)
1
1

1

Note that the definition of
L_{\alpha}
given in

[
GriRei20
, Definition 5.2.4]
differs from ours. However, it is equivalent
to ours, since
[
GriRei20
, Proposition 5.2.9]
shows that the
L_{\alpha}

defined in
[
GriRei20
, Definition 5.2.4]
satisfy the same formula that we
used to define our
L_{\alpha}
.
. We will actually find it easier to index
them not by the compositions
\alpha
but rather by the corresponding subsets

I
of
\left[n-1\right]
. Thus, we define:

Definition 1.13
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
I
be a subset of
\left[n-1\right]
. Then,
we will use the notation
L_{I,n}
for
L_{\operatorname*{comp}\left(I,n\right)}
. Explicitly, we have

L_{I,n}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n};\\
i_{p}<i_{p+1}\text{ for each }p\in I\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}\in\operatorname*{QSym}

(1)

(where the summation indices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}
range over

\mathbb{P}
).

Example 1.14
.

If
n=3
and
I=\left\{2\right\}
, then

\displaystyle L_{I,n}

\displaystyle=L_{\left\{2\right\},3}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq i_{3};\\
i_{p}<i_{p+1}\text{ for each }p\in\left\{2\right\}\end{subarray}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}<i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}

\displaystyle=x_{1}x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{1}x_{3}+\cdots+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{4}+\cdots+\cdots+x_{2}x_{2}x_{3}+\cdots.

1.3
The Redei–Berge symmetric function

We are now ready to define the main protagonist of this paper:

Definition 1.15
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph
with
n
vertices. We define the
Redei–Berge symmetric function

U_{D}
to be the quasisymmetric function

\sum\limits_{w\text{ is a }V\text{-listing}}L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right),\ n}\in\operatorname*{QSym}.

Example 1.16
.

Let
D
be the digraph
D
from Example

1.3
. Then,

\displaystyle U_{D}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{w\text{ is a }V\text{-listing}}L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,D\right),\ 3}

\displaystyle=L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(\left(1,2,3\right),D\right),\ 3}+L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(\left(1,3,2\right),D\right),\ 3}+L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(\left(2,1,3\right),D\right),\ 3}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(\left(2,3,1\right),D\right),\ 3}+L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(\left(3,1,2\right),D\right),\ 3}+L_{\operatorname*{Des}\left(\left(3,2,1\right),D\right),\ 3}

\displaystyle=L_{\left\{1\right\},\ 3}+L_{\varnothing,\ 3}+L_{\varnothing,\ 3}+L_{\varnothing,\ 3}+L_{\left\{2\right\},\ 3}+L_{\varnothing,\ 3}

\displaystyle=4\cdot\underbrace{L_{\varnothing,\ 3}}_{=\sum\limits_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}}+\underbrace{L_{\left\{1\right\},\ 3}}_{=\sum\limits_{i_{1}<i_{2}\leq i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}}+\underbrace{L_{\left\{2\right\},\ 3}}_{=\sum\limits_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}<i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}}

\displaystyle=4\cdot\sum\limits_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}+\sum\limits_{i_{1}<i_{2}\leq i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}+\sum\limits_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}<i_{3}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}x_{i_{3}}.

From this expression, we can easily see that
U_{D}
is actually a symmetric
function, and can be written (e.g.) as
p_{1}^{3}+2p_{1}p_{2}+p_{3}
, where

p_{k}:=x_{1}^{k}+x_{2}^{k}+x_{3}^{k}+\cdots
is the
k
-th power-sum
symmetric function for each
k\geq 1
.

The name “Redei–Berge symmetric function” for the power series
U_{D}
was chosen because (as we will soon see) it is
actually a symmetric function and is related to two classical results of Redei
and Berge on the number of Hamiltonian paths in digraphs. In
[
EC2sup22
, Problem
120]
, it is called
U_{X}
, where
X
is what we call
A
(that is,
the set of arcs of
D
); but we shall here put the entire digraph
D
into the subscript.

The Redei–Berge symmetric function
U_{D}
equals the quasisymmetric function

\Xi_{\overline{D}}\left(x,0\right)
from Chow’s
[
Chow96
]
.
2
2

2

Indeed, this equality follows immediately from
[
Chow96
, Proposition
7]
, since the quasisymmetric function we call
L_{I,n}
appears under
the name of
Q_{I,n}
in
[
Chow96
]
, and since our
\operatorname*{Des}\left(w,\overline{D}\right)
is what is called
S\left(w\right)
in

[
Chow96
]
.
It also is denoted by
\Pi_{\overline{D}}
in
[
Wisema07
]
.
3
3

3

Indeed, comparing the definition of
\Pi_{D}
in
[
Wisema07
, Definition
2.2]
with the definition of
\Xi_{D}
in
[
Chow96
, §2]
shows
that
\Pi_{D}=\Xi_{D}\left(x,0\right)
. Thus,
\Pi_{\overline{D}}=\Xi_{\overline{D}}\left(x,0\right)=U_{D}
(as we already know).
Several
properties of
U_{D}
have been shown in
[
Chow96
]
and in
[
Wisema07
]
,
and some of them will be reproved here for the sake of self-containedness and

1.30
,
1.38

and

Question 1.17
.

Can these results be extended to the more general functions
\Xi_{D}\left(x,y\right)
from

1.4
Arcs and cyclic arcs

The main results of this paper are explicit (albeit not, in general,
subtraction-free) expansions of
U_{D}
in terms of the power-sum symmetric
functions. To state these, we need some more notations. We shall soon define
cycles of digraphs and cycles of permutations, and we will then connect the
two notions. First, some auxiliary notations:

Definition 1.18
.

Let
V
be a set. Let
v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)\in V^{k}
be a nonempty tuple of elements of
V
.

(a)

We define a subset
\operatorname*{Arcs}v
of
V\times V

by

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Arcs}v:=\

\displaystyle\left\{\left(v_{i},v_{i+1}\right)\ \mid\ i\in\left[k-1\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left\{\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right),\ \left(v_{2},v_{3}\right),\ \ldots,\ \left(v_{k-1},v_{k}\right)\right\}

(2)

\displaystyle\subseteq\

\displaystyle V\times V.

This subset
\operatorname*{Arcs}v
is called the
arc set
of the tuple

v
. Its elements
\left(v_{i},v_{i+1}\right)
are called the
arcs

of
v
.

(b)

We define a subset
\operatorname*{CArcs}v
of
V\times V

by

\displaystyle\operatorname*{CArcs}v:=\

\displaystyle\left\{\left(v_{i},v_{i+1}\right)\ \mid\ i\in\left[k\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\left\{\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right),\ \left(v_{2},v_{3}\right),\ \ldots,\ \left(v_{k-1},v_{k}\right),\ \left(v_{k},v_{1}\right)\right\}

(3)

\displaystyle\subseteq\

\displaystyle V\times V,

where we set
v_{k+1}:=v_{1}
. This subset
\operatorname*{CArcs}v
is called
the
cyclic arc set
of the tuple
v
. Its elements
\left(v_{i},v_{i+1}\right)
are called the
cyclic arcs
of
v
.

(c)

The
reversal
of
v
is defined to be the tuple

\left(v_{k},v_{k-1},\ldots,v_{1}\right)\in V^{k}
.

Example 1.19
.

Let
V=\mathbb{N}
and
v=\left(1,4,2,6\right)\in V^{4}
. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Arcs}v

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(1,4\right),\ \left(4,2\right),\ \left(2,6\right)\right\}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle\operatorname*{CArcs}v

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(1,4\right),\ \left(4,2\right),\ \left(2,6\right),\ \left(6,1\right)\right\}.

Note that if we cyclically rotate a nonempty tuple
v\in V^{k}
, then the set

\operatorname*{CArcs}v
remains unchanged: i.e., for any
\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)\in V^{k}
, we have

\operatorname*{CArcs}\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)=\operatorname*{CArcs}\left(v_{2},v_{3},\ldots,v_{k},v_{1}\right).

1.5
Permutations and their cycles

Now, let us discuss permutations and their cycles. We start with some basic notations:

Definition 1.20
.

Let
V
be a finite set. Then,
\mathfrak{S}_{V}
shall denote the symmetric
group of
V
(that is, the group of all permutations of
V
).

Note that the order of this group is
\left|\mathfrak{S}_{V}\right|=\left|V\right|!
.

Definition 1.21
.

Let
V
be a set.

(a)

Two tuples
v\in V^{k}
and
w\in V^{\ell}
of elements of

V
are said to be
rotation-equivalent
if
w
can be obtained from
v

by cyclic rotation, i.e., if
\ell=k
and
w=\left(v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{k},v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i-1}\right)
for some
i\in\left[k\right]
.

(b)

The relation “rotation-equivalent” is an equivalence relation on the set
of all nonempty tuples of elements of
V
. Its equivalence classes are called
the
rotation-equivalence classes
. In other words, the
rotation-equivalence classes are the orbits of the operation

\left(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{k}\right)\mapsto\left(a_{2},a_{3},\ldots,a_{k},a_{1}\right)

on the set of all nonempty tuples of elements of
V
.

(c)

The rotation-equivalence class that contains a given
nonempty tuple
v\in V^{k}
will be denoted by
v_{\sim}
.

For instance, the tuple
\left(1,2,3,4\right)
is rotation-equivalent to

\left(3,4,1,2\right)
, but not to
\left(4,3,2,1\right)
. Thus,

\left(1,2,3,4\right)_{\sim}=\left(3,4,1,2\right)_{\sim}\neq\left(4,3,2,1\right)_{\sim}.

Also,

\left(1,3,6\right)_{\sim}=\left\{\left(1,3,6\right),\ \left(3,6,1\right),\ \left(6,1,3\right)\right\}.

Definition 1.22
.

Let
V
be a set. Let
\gamma
be a
rotation-equivalence class (of nonempty tuples of elements of
V
). Then:

(a)

All tuples
v\in\gamma
have the same length (i.e., number
of entries). This length is denoted by
\ell\left(\gamma\right)
, and is
called the
length
of
\gamma
. Thus, if
\gamma=v_{\sim}
for some
tuple
v\in V^{k}
, then
\ell\left(\gamma\right)=k
.

(b)

All tuples
v\in\gamma
have the same cyclic arc set

\operatorname*{CArcs}v
(since
\operatorname*{CArcs}v
remains unchanged if
we cyclically rotate
v
). This cyclic arc set is denoted by

\operatorname*{CArcs}\gamma
, and is called the
cyclic arc set
of

\gamma
. Thus, the cyclic arc set of a rotation-equivalence class

\gamma=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)_{\sim}
is

\operatorname*{CArcs}\gamma=\left\{\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right),\ \left(v_{2},v_{3}\right),\ \ldots,\ \left(v_{k-1},v_{k}\right),\ \left(v_{k},v_{1}\right)\right\}.

(c)

All tuples
v\in\gamma
have the same entries (up to
order). These entries are called the
entries
of
v
. Thus, the entries
of a rotation-equivalence class
\gamma=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)_{\sim}
are
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}
.

(d)

The reversals of all tuples
v\in\gamma
are the elements
of a single rotation-equivalence class
\operatorname*{rev}\gamma
. This
latter class will be called the
reversal
of
\gamma
. Thus, the
reversal of a rotation-equivalence class
\gamma=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)_{\sim}
is the rotation-equivalence class
\left(v_{k},v_{k-1},\ldots,v_{1}\right)_{\sim}
.

(e)

We say that
\gamma
is
nontrivial
if
\ell\left(\gamma\right)>1
.

For instance, the rotation-equivalence class
\left(3,1,4\right)_{\sim}

has length
3
, cyclic arc set
\left\{\left(3,1\right),\ \left(1,4\right),\ \left(4,3\right)\right\}
, and entries
3,1,4
. Its
reversal is
\left(4,1,3\right)_{\sim}
, and it is nontrivial (since

\ell\left(\left(3,1,4\right)_{\sim}\right)=3>1
).

Definition 1.23
.

Let
V
be a finite set. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}

be any permutation.

(a)

The
cycles
of
\sigma
are the rotation-equivalence
classes of the tuples of the form

\left(\sigma^{0}\left(i\right),\ \sigma^{1}\left(i\right),\ \ldots,\ \sigma^{k-1}\left(i\right)\right),

where
i
is some element of
V
, and where
k
is the smallest positive
integer satisfying
\sigma^{k}\left(i\right)=i
.

For example, the permutation
w_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\left[7\right]}
that
sends each
i\in\left[7\right]
to
8-i
has cycles
\left(1,7\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(2,6\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(3,5\right)_{\sim}
and

\left(4\right)_{\sim}
. (Note that we do allow a cycle to have length
1
.)

(b)

The
cycle type
of
\sigma
means the partition
whose entries are the lengths of the cycles of
\sigma
. We denote this cycle
type by
\operatorname*{type}\sigma
. It is a partition of the number

\left|V\right|
.

(c)

We let
\operatorname*{Cycs}\sigma
denote the set of all
cycles of
\sigma
.

Example 1.24
.

Let
w_{0}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\left[7\right]}
be the permutation that sends
each
i\in\left[7\right]
to
8-i
. We have already seen that
w_{0}
has
cycles
\left(1,7\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(2,6\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(3,5\right)_{\sim}
and
\left(4\right)_{\sim}
. Their respective lengths
are
2,2,2,1
. Thus, the cycle type of
w_{0}
is
\operatorname*{type}w_{0}=\left(2,2,2,1\right)
. We have
\operatorname*{Cycs}\sigma=\left\{\left(1,7\right)_{\sim},\ \left(2,6\right)_{\sim},\ \left(3,5\right)_{\sim},\ \left(4\right)_{\sim}\right\}
. The first three of
the four cycles
\left(1,7\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(2,6\right)_{\sim}
,

\left(3,5\right)_{\sim}
and
\left(4\right)_{\sim}
are nontrivial.

1.6
D
-paths and
D
-cycles

Next, we define paths and cycles in a digraph:

Definition 1.25
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph.

(a)

A
D
-path
(or
path of
D
) shall mean a
nonempty tuple
v
of distinct elements of
V
such that
\operatorname*{Arcs}v\subseteq A
.

(b)

A
D
-cycle
(or
cycle of
D
) shall mean a
rotation-equivalence class
\gamma
of nonempty tuples of distinct elements of

V
such that
\operatorname*{CArcs}\gamma\subseteq A
.

We note that our notion of “cycle of
D
” differs slightly from the common one used in graph theory
4
4

4

Namely,
cycles in graph theory have their first vertex repeated at the end, whereas
our cycles don’t. However, this difference is purely notational: A cycle

\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\right)_{\sim}
in our sense corresponds to
the cycle
\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k},v_{1}\right)
in the
graph-theorists’ terminology.
.

Example 1.26
.

Let
D
be the digraph
D
from Example
1.3
. Then:

(a)

The pair
\left(1,2\right)
as well as the three

1
-tuples
\left(1\right)
,
\left(2\right)
and
\left(3\right)

are
D
-paths. The triple
\left(1,2,2\right)
is not a
D
-path (even
though it satisfies the “
\operatorname*{Arcs}v\subseteq A
” condition), since its entries
1,2,2
are not distinct.
The triple
\left(1,2,3\right)
is not a
D
-path, since
\left(2,3\right)
is not an arc of
D
.

The triple
\left(2,3,1\right)
is a
\overline{D}
-path (and there are
several others).

(b)

The only
D
-cycles are the rotation-equivalence classes

\left(2\right)_{\sim}
and
\left(3\right)_{\sim}
. The
\overline{D}
-cycles are
\left(1\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(1,3\right)_{\sim}
,

\left(2,3\right)_{\sim}
and
\left(2,1,3\right)_{\sim}
.

1.7
The sets
\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D\right)
and
\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right)

Now, we can connect digraphs with permutations by comparing their cycles:

Definition 1.27
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Then, we
define
5
5

5

As we warned in Definition
1.23

(a)
, we
are being cavalier about the distinction between rotation-equivalence classes
and their representatives. Thus, when we say that a certain cycle
\gamma
of

\sigma
is a
D
-cycle, we really mean that some tuple in the
rotation-equivalence class
\gamma
(and therefore every tuple in
\gamma
) is
a
D
-cycle.

\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D\right):=\left\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\ \mid\ \text{each nontrivial cycle of }\sigma\text{ is a }D\text{-cycle}\right\}

and

\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right):=\left\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\ \mid\ \text{each cycle of }\sigma\text{ is a
}D\text{-cycle or a }\overline{D}\text{-cycle}\right\}.

Note that we could just as well replace “each
cycle” by “each nontrivial
cycle” in the definition of
\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right)
, since a cycle of length
1
is always a
D
-cycle or
a
\overline{D}
-cycle (depending on whether its only cyclic arc belongs to

A
or not). However, we could not replace “nontrivial
cycle” by “cycle” in the
definition of
\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D\right)
.

Example 1.28
.

Let
D
be the digraph
D
from Example
1.3
. Let

V=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
be its set of vertices. Then:

(a)

We have
\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D\right)=\left\{\operatorname*{id}\nolimits_{V}\right\}
, since the only
D
-cycles have
length
1
.

(b)

We have

\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right)=\left\{\operatorname*{id}\nolimits_{V},\ \operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3},\ \operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{2,3},\ \operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3,2}\right\},

where
\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}}
denotes the
permutation that cyclically permutes the elements
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}

while leaving all other elements of
V
unchanged.

1.8
Formulas for
U_{D}

1.8.1
The power-sum symmetric functions

We now introduce some of the best-known (and easiest to define) symmetric functions:

Definition 1.29
.

(a)

For each positive integer
n
, we define the

power-sum symmetric function

p_{n}:=x_{1}^{n}+x_{2}^{n}+x_{3}^{n}+\cdots\in\Lambda.

(b)

If
\lambda=\left(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{k}\right)
is a partition with
k
positive entries, then we set

p_{\lambda}:=p_{\lambda_{1}}p_{\lambda_{2}}\cdots p_{\lambda_{k}}\in\Lambda.

For instance,
p_{\left(2,2,1\right)}=p_{2}p_{2}p_{1}=\left(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+\cdots\right)^{2}\left(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+\cdots\right)
.

1.8.2
The first main theorem: general digraphs

We now state our first main theorem (which will be proved in Section

2
):

Theorem 1.30
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Set

\varphi\left(\sigma\right):=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\in\operatorname*{Cycs}\sigma;\\
\gamma\text{ is a }D\text{-cycle}\end{subarray}}\left(\ell\left(\gamma\right)-1\right)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for each }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}.

Then,

U_{D}=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right)}\left(-1\right)^{\varphi\left(\sigma\right)}p_{\operatorname*{type}\sigma}.

Example 1.31
.

Let
V=\left\{1,2,3,4,5,6\right\}
and
D=\left(V,\ V\times V\right)
.
Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be the permutation whose cycles are
\left(1,3\right)_{\sim}
,
\left(2,4,5\right)_{\sim}
and
\left(6\right)_{\sim}
. Then, every cycle of
\sigma
is a
D
-cycle, and the number

\varphi\left(\sigma\right)
(as defined in Theorem
1.30
) is

\displaystyle\left(\ell\left(\left(1,3\right)_{\sim}\right)-1\right)+\left(\ell\left(\left(2,4,5\right)_{\sim}\right)-1\right)+\left(\ell\left(\left(6\right)_{\sim}\right)-1\right)

\displaystyle=\left(2-1\right)+\left(3-1\right)+\left(1-1\right)=3.

Example 1.32
.

Let
D
be the digraph
D
from Example
1.3
. Recall
that
\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right)=\left\{\operatorname*{id}\nolimits_{V},\ \operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3},\ \operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{2,3},\ \operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3,2}\right\}
. Thus, Theorem
1.30
yields

\displaystyle U_{D}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\varphi\left(\operatorname*{id}\nolimits_{V}\right)}}_{=\left(-1\right)^{0}=1}\underbrace{p_{\operatorname*{type}\left(\operatorname*{id}\nolimits_{V}\right)}}_{=p_{\left(1,1,1\right)}=p_{1}^{3}}+\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\varphi\left(\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3}\right)}}_{=\left(-1\right)^{0}=1}\underbrace{p_{\operatorname*{type}\left(\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3}\right)}}_{=p_{\left(2,1\right)}=p_{2}p_{1}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\varphi\left(\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{2,3}\right)}}_{=\left(-1\right)^{0}=1}\underbrace{p_{\operatorname*{type}\left(\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{2,3}\right)}}_{=p_{\left(2,1\right)}=p_{2}p_{1}}+\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\varphi\left(\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3,2}\right)}}_{=\left(-1\right)^{0}=1}\underbrace{p_{\operatorname*{type}\left(\operatorname*{cyc}\nolimits_{1,3,2}\right)}}_{=p_{\left(3\right)}=p_{3}}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{3}+p_{2}p_{1}+p_{2}p_{1}+p_{3}=p_{1}^{3}+2p_{1}p_{2}+p_{3}.

This agrees with the result found in Example
1.16
.

Example 1.33
.

Let
D
be the digraph
\left(V,A\right)
, where
V=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
and

A=\left\{\left(1,3\right),\ \left(2,1\right),\ \left(3,1\right),\ \left(3,2\right)\right\}.

Then, a straightforward computation using Theorem
1.30
shows that

U_{D}=p_{1}^{3}-p_{1}p_{2}+p_{3}
. (This example is due to Ira Gessel.)

The following two corollaries can be easily obtained from Theorem

1.30
(see Section
4
for their proofs):

Corollary 1.34
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Then,
U_{D}

is a
p
-integral symmetric function (i.e., a symmetric function that can be
written as a polynomial in
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\ldots
). That is, we have

U_{D}\in\mathbb{Z}\left[p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\ldots\right]
.

Corollary 1.35
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph.
Assume that every
D
-cycle has odd length. Then,

U_{D}=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\left(D,\overline{D}\right)}p_{\operatorname*{type}\sigma}\in\mathbb{N}\left[p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},\ldots\right].

1.8.3
The second main theorem: tournaments

After we will have proved Theorem
1.30
, we will use it to derive a
simpler formula, which however is specific to tournaments. First, we recall
the definition of a tournament:

Definition 1.36
.

A
tournament
means a digraph
D=\left(V,A\right)
that satisfies
the following two axioms:

•

Looplessness
: We have
\left(u,u\right)\notin A
for any

.

•

Tournament axiom
: For any two distinct vertices
u
and
v
of

D
,
exactly
one of the two pairs
\left(u,v\right)
and
\left(v,u\right)
is an arc of
D
.

Proof of Theorem

In the following, we will outline the proof of Theorem

. We hope

2.1
Basic conventions

The following two conventions are popular in enumerative combinatorics, and we
too will use them on occasion:

Convention 2.1
.

The symbol # shall mean “number”. For instance,
\left(\text{\# of subsets of
}\left\{1,2,3\right\}\right)=8
.

Convention 2.2
.

We shall use the
Iverson bracket notation
: For any
logical statement
\mathcal{A}
, we let
\left[\mathcal{A}\right]
denote
the truth value of
\mathcal{A}
. This is the number
\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\mathcal{A}\text{ is true};\\
0,&\text{if }\mathcal{A}\text{ is false}.\end{cases}

Our proof of Theorem
1.30
will rely on many lemmas. The first is a
well-known cancellation lemma (see, e.g.,
[
Grinbe21
, Proposition 7.8.10]
):

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
B
be a finite set. Then,
\sum\limits_{F\subseteq B}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}=\left[B=\varnothing\right]
.

2.2
Path covers and linear sets

We begin with some more notations:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
V
be a finite set.

(a)

A
path
of
V
means a nonempty tuple of distinct
elements of
V
.

(b)

An element
v
is said to
belong
to a given tuple

t
if
v
is an entry of
t
.

(c)

A
path cover
of
V
means a set of paths of
V

such that each
v\in V
belongs to exactly one of these paths.

For example,
\left\{\left(1,4,3\right),\left(2,8\right),\left(5\right),\left(7,6\right)\right\}
is a path cover of
\left[8\right]
. We stress once again the words “exactly
one” in the definition of a path cover. Thus, the paths
constituting a path cover are disjoint (i.e., have no entries in common). For
instance,
\left\{\left(1,2\right),\left(2,3\right)\right\}
is

not
a path cover of
\left[3\right]
.

In Definition
1.18

(a)
, we have introduced the
arc set of a path of
V
(and, more generally, of any nonempty tuple of
elements of
V
). We now extend this to path covers in the obvious way:

Definition 2.3
.

Let
V
be a finite set.

(a)

If
C
is a path cover of
V
, then the
arc set
of

C
is defined to be the subset

\bigcup\limits_{v\in C}\operatorname*{Arcs}v\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{of }V\times V.

This arc set will be denoted by
\operatorname*{Arcs}C
.

(b)

A subset
F
of
V\times V
will be called
linear

if it is the arc set of some path cover of
V
.

For example, the path cover
\left\{\left(1,4,3\right),\left(2,8\right),\left(5\right),\left(7,6\right)\right\}
of
\left[8\right]
has arc set

\displaystyle\operatorname*{Arcs}\left\{\left(1,4,3\right),\left(2,8\right),\left(5\right),\left(7,6\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\operatorname*{Arcs}\left(1,4,3\right)\cup\operatorname*{Arcs}\left(2,8\right)\cup\operatorname*{Arcs}\left(5\right)\cup\operatorname*{Arcs}\left(7,6\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(1,4\right),\ \left(4,3\right)\right\}\cup\left\{\left(2,8\right)\right\}\cup\varnothing\cup\left\{\left(7,6\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(1,4\right),\ \left(4,3\right),\ \left(2,8\right),\ \left(7,6\right)\right\}.

Thus, the latter set is linear (as a subset of
\left[8\right]\times\left[8\right]
).

Note that the notion of “path of
V
” depends on
V
alone, not on any digraph structure on
V
. Thus, if
V
is
the vertex set of a digraph
D=\left(V,A\right)
, then a path of

Next, let us address a technical issue. We defined the notion of a

), as the

Proposition 2.9

. Then,

.

.

\Longrightarrow:

. Thus,

is the arc set of some path cover

of

. If we add a trivial path

\left(v\right)

, but its arc set does not change (and thus

. In

.

\Longleftarrow:

. Thus,

. Consider this

This is straightforward.

\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in J}V_{j}
, and its arc set is

.

Corollary 2.27

Let

and

Proposition 2.28
.

Let

Proposition 2.29
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Let

be any map. Let

be any set. Then:

(a)

The set

Next, we return to studying permutations.

When a set

is a union of two disjoint subsets

and
B
, and we are
given a permutation
\sigma_{A}
of
A
and a permutation
\sigma_{B}
of

,
then we can combine these two permutations to obtain a permutation

of

\sigma_{A}\left(a\right)
, and sends each

to
\sigma_{B}\left(b\right)
. That is, this permutation
\sigma_{A}\oplus\sigma_{B}
is “acting as
\sigma_{A}
” on the
subset
A
and “acting as
\sigma_{B}
” on
the subset
B
.

The same construction can be performed when
V
is a union of more than two
disjoint subsets (and we are given a permutation of each of these subsets). We
will encounter this situation when a map
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
subdivides
the set
V
into its level sets
f^{-1}\left(1\right),\ f^{-1}\left(2\right),\ f^{-1}\left(3\right),\ \ldots
, and we are given a
permutation
\sigma_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{f^{-1}\left(j\right)}
of each
level set
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
(to be more precise, we only need

\sigma_{j}
to be given when
j\in f\left(V\right)
, since the level set

f^{-1}\left(j\right)
is empty otherwise). The permutation of
V

obtained by combining these permutations
\sigma_{j}
will then be denoted by

\bigoplus\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\sigma_{j}
. Here is its explicit definition:

Definition 2.30
.

Let
V
be any set. Let
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
be any map.

For each
j\in f\left(V\right)
, let
\sigma_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{f^{-1}\left(j\right)}
be a permutation of the level set

.

Then,
\bigoplus\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\sigma_{j}
shall denote the
permutation of
V
that sends each
v\in V
to
\sigma_{f\left(v\right)}\left(v\right)
. This is the permutation that acts as
\sigma_{j}
on
each level set
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
.

Proposition 2.31
.

Let
V
be any set. Let
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
be any map. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be any permutation. Then:

(a)

We have
f\circ\sigma=f
if and only if
\sigma
can be
written in the form
\sigma=\bigoplus\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\sigma_{j}
,
where
\sigma_{j}\in\mathfrak{S}_{f^{-1}\left(j\right)}
for each
j\in f\left(V\right)
.

(b)

In this case, the permutations
\sigma_{j}
for all
j\in f\left(V\right)
are uniquely determined by
\sigma
(namely,
\sigma_{j}

is the restriction of
\sigma
to the subset
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
for
each
j\in f\left(V\right)
).

Now, we recall the set
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}
defined in Definition

2.13
for any finite set
V
and any permutation
\sigma
of
V
.

Proposition 2.32
.

Let
V
be a finite set. Let
f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
be any map. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be a permutation
satisfying
f\circ\sigma=f
. Write
\sigma
in the form
\sigma=\bigoplus\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\sigma_{j}

Next, we connect the above construction with the level subdigraphs of a digraph:

Proposition 2.33
.

Let

be a digraph. Let

Lemma 2.34

Let

\sigma
with

Now, we come to a crucial lemma, which generalizes Lemma

2.18
to the case of a digraph

“shattered” by a map

:

Lemma 2.35
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Let

f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
be any map. For each

, we define a

D_{j}
as in Definition
2.23

(c)
. Then,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V};\\
f\circ\sigma=f\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A\\
\text{is linear}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}=\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(\text{\# of hamps of }\overline{D_{j}}\right).

Proof 2.36
.

We shall use the notations from Definition
2.20
, Definition

2.23
and Definition
2.30
. We
recall that every
j\in\mathbb{P}
satisfies

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be a permutation satisfying
f\circ\sigma=f
.
Then, Proposition
2.33
yields
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A\subseteq A_{f}
. Hence,
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A=\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A_{f}
(because
\underbrace{\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}}_{=\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}}\cap A=\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap\underbrace{\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A}_{\subseteq A_{f}}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A_{f}
and
conversely
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap\underbrace{A_{f}}_{\subseteq A}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A\\
\text{is linear}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A_{f}\\
\text{is linear}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq A_{f}\text{ is linear};\\
F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}

(10)

(since a subset of
\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A_{f}
is the same thing as a

F
of
A_{f}
that satisfies

).

Forget that we fixed
\sigma
. We thus have proved
(
10
) for every
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}

satisfying
f\circ\sigma=f

Summing up the equality (
10
) over all

\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}

obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V};\\

\text{is linear}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V};\\
f\circ\sigma=f\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq A_{f}\text{ is linear};\\

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{F\subseteq A_{f}\text{ is linear}}\ \ \underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V};\\
F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma};\\
f\circ\sigma=f\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}}_{=\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}\cdot\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{ satisfying }F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{ and
}f\circ\sigma=f\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{F\subseteq A_{f}\text{ is linear}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}\cdot\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{
satisfying }F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{ and }f\circ\sigma=f\right).

(11)

Now, a linear subset

of

2.29

(a)

furthermore, if

2.29

(b)

Hence, we can substitute
\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}
for

in the sum on the right hand side of (
11
). We
thus obtain

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{F\subseteq A_{f}\text{ is linear}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}\cdot\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{
satisfying }F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{ and }f\circ\sigma=f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(F_{j}\right)_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\text{ is
a family}\\
\text{of linear subsets }F_{j}\subseteq A_{j}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}\right|}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{ satisfying }\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{ and }f\circ\sigma=f\right).

(12)

Furthermore, a permutation

for each

2.31

(a)
). Moreover, if

(b)

\displaystyle\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{ satisfying }\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\begin{array}[c]{c}\text{since the condition \textquotedblleft}\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}\subseteq\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\mathbf{A}_{\sigma_{j}}\text{\textquotedblright}\\

(16)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{here, we have renamed
the index }\sigma_{j}\text{ as }\sigma\right).

Thus, (
12
) becomes

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{F\subseteq A_{f}\text{ is linear}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}\cdot\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{
satisfying }F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{ and }f\circ\sigma=f\right)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(F_{j}\right)_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\text{ is
a family}\\
\text{of linear subsets }F_{j}\subseteq A_{j}\end{subarray}}\ \ \underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{\left|\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}\right|}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\left(-1\right)^{\sum\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left|F_{j}\right|}\\
=\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F_{j}\right|}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \cdot\underbrace{\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}\text{ satisfying }\bigsqcup\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}F_{j}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\text{ and }f\circ\sigma=f\right)}_{\begin{subarray}{c}=\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{f^{-1}\left(j\right)}\text{ satisfying }F_{j}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\right)\\
\text{(by
(\ref{pf.lem.hamps-by-lin-f.short.6}))}\end{subarray}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(F_{j}\right)_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\text{ is

\text{of linear subsets }F_{j}\subseteq A_{j}\end{subarray}}\underbrace{\left(\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F_{j}\right|}\right)\cdot\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(\text{\# of

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\left(F_{j}\right)_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\text{ is
a family}\\
\text{of linear subsets }F_{j}\subseteq A_{j}\end{subarray}}\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(\left(-1\right)^{\left|F_{j}\right|}\cdot\left(\text{\# of }\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{f^{-1}\left(j\right)}\text{ satisfying }F_{j}\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\right)\right)

2.7
\left(f,D\right)
-friendly
V
-listings

The following restatement of Lemma
2.35
will be useful for us:

Lemma 2.37
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Let

f:V\rightarrow\mathbb{P}
be any map. A
V
-listing
v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\right)
will be called
\left(f,D\right)
-friendly
if it has the properties that
f\left(v_{1}\right)\leq f\left(v_{2}\right)\leq\cdots\leq f\left(v_{n}\right)
and that

f\left(v_{p}\right)<f\left(v_{p+1}\right)\text{ for each }p\in\left[n-1\right]\text{ satisfying }\left(v_{p},v_{p+1}\right)\in A.

(17)

Then,

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V};\\
f\circ\sigma=f\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}F\subseteq\mathbf{A}_{\sigma}\cap A\\
\text{is linear}\end{subarray}}\left(-1\right)^{\left|F\right|}=\left(\text{\# of }\left(f,D\right)\text{-friendly }V\text{-listings}\right).

Proof 2.38
.

For each
j\in f\left(V\right)
, we define a digraph
D_{j}
as in
Definition
2.23

(c)
. This digraph
D_{j}
is the
restriction of the digraph
D
to the subset
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
. In
particular, its vertex set is
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
. In other words, its
vertices are precisely those vertices of
D
that have level
j
(with respect
to
f
).

Clearly, a
V
-listing
v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\right)
satisfies

f\left(v_{1}\right)\leq f\left(v_{2}\right)\leq\cdots\leq f\left(v_{n}\right)
if and only if it lists the vertices of
D
in the order of
increasing level, i.e., if it first lists the vertices of the smallest level,
then the vertices of the second-smallest level, and so on.

In other words, a
V
-listing
v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\right)

satisfies
f\left(v_{1}\right)\leq f\left(v_{2}\right)\leq\cdots\leq f\left(v_{n}\right)
if and only if it can be constructed by choosing an

f^{-1}\left(j\right)
-listing
v^{\left(j\right)}
for each
j\in f\left(V\right)
and concatenating all these
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
-listings
v^{\left(j\right)}
in the order of increasing
j
. Moreover,
if it can be constructed in this way, then its construction is unique (i.e.,
each
v^{\left(j\right)}
is determined uniquely by
v
). Finally, for a

V
-listing
v
that is written as a concatenation of such
f^{-1}\left(j\right)
-listings
v^{\left(j\right)}
, the condition
(
17
) is equivalent to the condition that each

v^{\left(j\right)}
is a hamp of
\overline{D_{j}}
(indeed, this is
easiest to see by rewriting (
17
) in the
contrapositive form “if
p\in\left[n-1\right]
satisfies

f\left(v_{p}\right)=f\left(v_{p+1}\right)
, then
\left(v_{p},v_{p+1}\right)\notin A
”). Thus, a
V
-listing

v=\left(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\right)
satisfies both
f\left(v_{1}\right)\leq f\left(v_{2}\right)\leq\cdots\leq f\left(v_{n}\right)
and (
17
) at the same time if
and only if it can be constructed by choosing a hamp of
\overline{D_{j}}
for
each
j\in f\left(V\right)
and concatenating all these hamps in the order
of increasing
j
. In other words, a
V
-listing
v
is
\left(f,D\right)
-friendly if and only if it can be constructed in this way. Since this
construction is unique, we thus have

\left(\text{\# of }\left(f,D\right)\text{-friendly }V\text{-listings}\right)=\prod\limits_{j\in f\left(V\right)}\left(\text{\# of hamps of
}\overline{D_{j}}\right).

Thus, Lemma
2.37
follows from Lemma

2.35
.

The following lemma is well-known, e.g., from the theory of Pólya enumeration:

Lemma 2.39
.

Let
V
be a finite set. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be a permutation of

Proof 8.4
.

. This

\mathbb{Z}
-algebra homomorphism.

For each positive integer
n

\displaystyle\zeta\left(\omega\left(p_{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\zeta\left(\left(-1\right)^{n-1}p_{n}\right)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{by

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\left(-1\right)^{n-1}}_{\equiv 1\operatorname{mod}2}\zeta\left(p_{n}\right)\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left(\text{since }\zeta\text{ is }\mathbb{Z}\text{-linear}\right)

\zeta

9
A multiparameter deformation

Let us now briefly discuss a multiparameter deformation of the Redei-Berge
symmetric functions
U_{D}
, which replaces the digraph
D
by an arbitrary matrix.

We fix a commutative ring
\mathbf{k}
, which we shall now be using instead of

\mathbb{Z}
as a base ring for our power series.

We fix an
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and a set
V
with
n
elements.

For any
a\in V\times V
, we fix an element
t_{a}\in\mathbf{k}
. (Thus, the
family
\left(t_{\left(i,j\right)}\right)_{i,j\in V}
of these
elements can be viewed as a
V\times V
-matrix.)

For any
a\in V\times V
, we set
s_{a}:=t_{a}+1\in\mathbf{k}
.

The following definition is inspired by a comment from Mike Zabrocki:

Definition 9.1
.

We define the
deformed Redei–Berge symmetric function

\widetilde{U}_{t}
to be the formal power series

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}w=\left(w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\right)\\
\text{is a }V\text{-listing}\end{subarray}}\ \ \sum\limits_{i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{n}}\left(\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\left[n-1\right];\\
i_{k}=i_{k+1}\end{subarray}}s_{\left(w_{k},w_{k+1}\right)}\right)x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{n}}

\displaystyle\in\mathbf{k}\left[\left[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots\right]\right].

For example, if
n=2
and
V=\left\{1,2\right\}
, then

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i_{1}<i_{2}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}+\sum\limits_{i_{1}=i_{2}}t_{\left(1,2\right)}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}+\sum\limits_{i_{1}<i_{2}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}+\sum\limits_{i_{1}=i_{2}}t_{\left(2,1\right)}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum\limits_{i}t_{\left(1,2\right)}x_{i}^{2}+\sum\limits_{i<j}x_{i}x_{j}+\sum\limits_{i}t_{\left(2,1\right)}x_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}+\left(s_{\left(1,2\right)}+s_{\left(2,1\right)}-1\right)p_{2}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}+\left(t_{\left(1,2\right)}+t_{\left(2,1\right)}+1\right)p_{2}

For a more complicated example, if
n=2
and
V=\left\{1,2,3\right\}
,
then a longer computation shows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{3}+\left(s_{\left(1,2\right)}+s_{\left(2,1\right)}+s_{\left(1,3\right)}+s_{\left(3,1\right)}+s_{\left(2,3\right)}+s_{\left(3,2\right)}-3\right)p_{2}p_{1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\left(s_{\left(1,2\right)}s_{\left(2,3\right)}+s_{\left(2,3\right)}s_{\left(3,1\right)}+s_{\left(3,1\right)}s_{\left(1,2\right)}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.+\ s_{\left(1,3\right)}s_{\left(3,2\right)}+s_{\left(3,2\right)}s_{\left(2,1\right)}+s_{\left(2,1\right)}t_{\left(1,3\right)}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.-\ s_{\left(1,2\right)}-s_{\left(2,1\right)}-s_{\left(1,3\right)}-s_{\left(3,1\right)}-s_{\left(2,3\right)}-s_{\left(3,2\right)}+2\right)p_{3}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{3}+\left(t_{\left(1,2\right)}+t_{\left(2,1\right)}+t_{\left(1,3\right)}+t_{\left(3,1\right)}+t_{\left(2,3\right)}+t_{\left(3,2\right)}+3\right)p_{2}p_{1}

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ +\left(t_{\left(1,2\right)}t_{\left(2,3\right)}+t_{\left(2,3\right)}t_{\left(3,1\right)}+t_{\left(3,1\right)}t_{\left(1,2\right)}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.+\ t_{\left(1,3\right)}t_{\left(3,2\right)}+t_{\left(3,2\right)}t_{\left(2,1\right)}+t_{\left(2,1\right)}t_{\left(1,3\right)}\right.

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left.+\ t_{\left(1,2\right)}+t_{\left(2,1\right)}+t_{\left(1,3\right)}+t_{\left(3,1\right)}+t_{\left(2,3\right)}+t_{\left(3,2\right)}+2\right)p_{3}.

Why are we calling

a deformation of

Example 9.2
.

Let
D=\left(V,A\right)
be a digraph. Set
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{Z}
, and
let

Then,
\widetilde{U}_{t}=U_{D}
, as can be seen by comparing the definitions.

All the above results leading up to Theorem
1.30

this deformation, culminating in the following deformation of Theorem

1.30

Theorem 9.3
.

We have

\widetilde{U}_{t}=\sum\limits_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}}\left(\prod\limits_{\gamma\text{
is a cycle of }\sigma}\left(\prod\limits_{a\in\operatorname*{CArcs}\gamma}s_{a}-\prod\limits_{a\in\operatorname*{CArcs}\gamma}t_{a}\right)\right)p_{\operatorname*{type}\sigma}.

Alternatively, Theorem
9.3
can be deduced from Theorem

1.30
via the “multilinearity
trick”: View each

as an indeterminate, and argue that
both sides in Theorem

are polynomials in degree
\leq 1

in these indeterminates (over the base ring

is
specialized to either

1.30
. (Thus, Theorem
9.3

Theorem
9.3
shows that the

are

for generalizing any of Theorem
