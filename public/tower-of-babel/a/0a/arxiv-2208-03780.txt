Fueling limits in a cylindrical viscosity-limited reactor
T. Rubin, E. J. Kolmes, I. E. Ochs, M. E. Mlodik, N. J. Fisch

Abstract
Recently, a method to achieve a “natural hot-ion mode” was suggested, by utilizing ion viscous heating in a rotating plasma with a fixed boundary. We explore the steady-state solution to the Braginskii equations and find the parameter regime in which a significant temperature difference between ions and electrons can be sustained in a driven steady state. The threshold for this effect occurs at ρ i ≳ 0.1 ​ R \rho_{i}\gtrsim 0.1R . An analytic, leading order low flow solution is obtained, and a numerical, moderate Mach number M ≲ 2 M\lesssim 2 is investigated. The limitation is found to be at moderate Mach numbers.

. Plasma mass filters

;

;

;

;
Gueroult et al. 2015
;
Gueroult et al. 2019
;
Fetterman and Fisch 2011
;
Bonnevier 1966
;
Bonnevier 1971
are another rotating plasma application in which density gradients are of particular importance, and are similar to rotating mirrors in many respects.

Sufficiently long mirrors may be analyzed using classical transport theory. Radial cross field ion currents
Kolmes et al. 2019
;
Rax et al. 2019
in such devices appear to be an attractive fueling method, as they can induce rotation in the plasma due to their interaction with the magnetic field. The hydrodynamic variables - densities, momenta and pressures - in such configurations can be asymptotically solved for
Rubin et al. 2021
or numerically integrated using a variety of tools, such as the MITNS: Multiple-Ion Transport Numerical Solver code
Kolmes et al. 2021a
.

Nuclear fusion in magnetic devices is realized by confinement of hot ions for sufficient time
Lawson 1957
. In these devices, the plasma confinement is often limited by the total plasma pressure, which sums the electron and ion pressures. As such, a hot-ion mode is preferable, as it can produce more fusion power for the same magnetic field strength
Clarke 1980
, in addition to a decrease in energy radiation losses through electrons.

Plasma heating can be accomplished using a variety of methods. One proposed method is to use viscous heating due to sheared rotation.
Kolmes et al. 2021b
showed how heat dissipation channels in an axisymmetric cylindrical plasma could preferentially heat the ion population. Such a configuration may be realized by a radial flow of fuel ions into the hot center of the cylinder, and the removal of ash ions by a fast process other than classical transport - such as
\alpha
-channeling
Fisch and Rax 1992
;
Fisch and Herrmann 1994
. The heating is the result of the viscous dissipation of the ion fluid, i.e., the rate of work done by the viscous stress times strain-rate. The electron fluid viscous stress, and the resultant heating rate, is smaller by a factor of
(m_{e}/m_{i})^{3/2}
.

Kolmes et al. 2021b
predicted that the ion temperature could be higher than the electron temperature, and suggested that the temperature difference could be large in cases with sufficiently large radial influxes of fuel ions and high radial electric fields. However, that paper left open the question of what kinds of radial influxes and fields could be self-consistently supported, and of precisely what are the density, velocity, and pressure profiles in this case.
The present paper addresses that question.
One of the key results that follows from this calculation is that there are nontrivial limitations on these hot-ion-mode solutions.

In this paper, we explore a particular solution to the proposed concept. We consider a one-dimensional Braginskii fluid model
Braginskii 1965
to explore the nonlinear effects due to the density and temperature dependence of the viscosity and heat conductivities for a uniform volumetric charge extraction, i.e. a radial ion current. We compare analytic solutions using constant coefficients to the full numerical non-linear solution.

Because the Braginskii fluid model is the most usual model, it serves well our purposes here. We do note that several authors published corrections to the transport coefficients
Epperlein and Haines 1986
;
Simakov 2022
;
Ji and Held 2013
;
Sadler et al. 2021
;
Davies et al. 2021
. In addition, a magnetic mirror machine would have a distribution function that contains voids where particles are not confined. In the magnetic mirror case, it is not clear that Braginskii, or other closures that expand the distribution function in a polynomial basis, produce the precise transport coefficients. However, the effects discussed in this work do not rely on the specific transport coefficients.

The limitation on the viscous heating arises because the radial ion current into the center of the cylinder leads to a large rotation of the plasma, and the centrifugal force caused by this rotation empties out the density at the core. The quadratic dependence of the viscosity on the density means that large angular velocity gradients are required to produce the viscous shear needed to balance the torque produced by the radial current and the magnetic field - leading to progressively larger rotation. This effect limits the amount of charge extraction possible in this configuration. The viscous heating itself, which increases the plasma temperature, further reduces the viscosity coefficient. Of course, diverging angular velocities are not physical, and are the result of an attempt to balance a finite torque when the viscosity coefficient approaches 0. In a physical system, the torque would be limited.

As a result, the proposed “natural hot-ion mode” is limited. Beyond a certain radial ion flux, the nonlinearities in the viscosity coefficient would cut off the shear stress in the plasma. This limitation in shear limits the viscous heating; the limit depends on the magnetization of the plasma. We calculate a magnetization threshold above which the viscous heating is small compared to the rate of temperature equilibration between species.

II
we present the nondimensionalized two-fluid equations. In Sec.
III
we present the low-flux approximate solution, assuming constant coefficients. In Sec.
IV
we discuss the deviation of the full nonlinear (variable coefficients) solution from the linear approximation.

II
Model

Consider an axisymmetric, infinitely long plasma cylinder, in equilibrium, such that
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial}{\partial z}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}=0
, with a constant axial magnetic field. The plasma species to be considered, for simplicity, are fuel ions and electrons. This setting might be realizable in a steady-state rotating mirror machine, which is fueled radially rather than axially, and in which fusion ash is removed quickly radially using
\alpha
-channeling, before it can interact with the fuel ions or the electrons. It is assumed the
\alpha
-channeling does not affect electrons.

In steady state, fuel ions fuse, the ash is removed, and more fuel is supplied continuously from the outer edge, while the electrons have no average radial velocity. The ion sink term produces in steady state an inward-flowing ion current. The
\mathbf{r}\times(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B})
torque due to this current induces rotation in the plasma. The plasma rotation, in addition to the other radial forces acting on it, determines the steady-state density profile.

The radial expulsion of the fusion ash produces the opposite
\mathbf{r}\times(\mathbf{j}\times\mathbf{B})
torque on the ash ions. However, the fusion ash is kept at such a low density, using
\alpha
-channeling, that its collisional interactions with other plasma constituents can be ordered out of the momentum and energy equations. A density ratio of
n_{a}/n_{i}\sim\rho_{*}^{2}
is sufficient, with the small parameter
\rho_{*}
being the normalized ion Larmor radius, also defined later.

In this work, we consider only classical transport effects in order to determine the temperatures. In many real-life plasmas, other effects contribute to the energy balance - examples include radiative cooling, and RF heating. These, and energy exchange with fusion ash may even be the dominant mechanisms, over and above classical transport effects. However, one purpose of this paper is to determine the merit of the proposed natural ion mode, which cannot be separated from the other effects considered here

\left.-\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}\tilde{\eta}_{3i}}{\epsilon\tilde{\eta}_{1i}\tilde{r}^{\prime 2}\tilde{n}_{i}}\left[\frac{2}{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}+\frac{d\ln(\tilde{n}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}\right]\right].~

(51)

Notice that each term in (
51
) is proportional to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{\eta}_{1i}}\propto\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{T}_{i}}}{\tilde{n}_{i}^{2}}.

(52)

If
\tilde{n}_{i}
were to continuously decrease from
1
to near
0
when moving from
\tilde{r}=1
inwards or if the ion temperature should diverge,
\tilde{\omega}_{i}
would diverge. This is due to the decrease in viscosity while the magnetic field torque remains the same.

The leading order solution for
\tilde{\omega}_{i}
, taking
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{s}_{i}\tilde{r}^{2}
, which is the solution to the continuity equation with a uniform steady source term
\tilde{s}_{i}=\mathrm{const.}
, and
\tilde{B}=\mathrm{const.}
, in addition to
\tilde{\eta}_{1i}=\mathrm{const.}
is,

\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{(0,0)}=\frac{Z_{i}\tilde{B}\tilde{s}_{i}}{8\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon\tilde{\eta}_{1i}}e^{\frac{\tilde{s}_{i}\tilde{m}_{i}}{4\rho_{*}\epsilon\tilde{\eta}_{1i}}(\tilde{r}^{2}-1)}\cdot\int_{1}^{r}e^{-\frac{\tilde{s}_{i}\tilde{m}_{i}}{4\rho_{*}\epsilon\tilde{\eta}_{1i}}(\tilde{r}^{\prime 2}-1)}\tilde{r}^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\tilde{r}^{\prime}~\\
=\frac{\tilde{\Omega}_{i}}{4\rho_{*}}\left(e^{F_{i}(\tilde{r}^{2}-1)}-1\right),

(53)

It is useful to define the variables
F_{i}=\frac{\tilde{s}_{i}\tilde{m}_{i}}{4\rho_{*}\epsilon\tilde{\eta}_{1i}}
, which is the mass flux over viscosity, and
\tilde{\Omega}_{i}\doteq\frac{Z_{i}\tilde{B}}{\tilde{m}_{i}}
which is the dimensionless gyro-frequency. The solution is exponentially dependent on the strength of the source term
\tilde{s}_{i}
.

The azimuthal velocity in this case is

\displaystyle\tilde{v}_{\theta i}^{(0,0)}=\frac{\tilde{\Omega}_{i}}{4\rho_{*}}\tilde{r}\left(e^{F_{i}(\tilde{r}^{2}-1)}-1\right).

(54)

The angular velocity becomes
\mathcal{O}(1)
if
F_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*})
. An azimuthal Mach 1 (
\tilde{v}_{\theta i}=1
) is obtained when
F_{i}\approx-6\sqrt{3}\rho_{*}/\tilde{\Omega}_{i}
, corresponding to a source term
\tilde{s}_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon)
.
The next corrections are of
\mathcal{O}(\tilde{m}_{e}^{3/2})
due to the electron viscosity and
\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2})
due to the
\tilde{\eta}_{3i}
rotation term.

III.2
Temperatures

The ion temperature equation, when substituting
\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}=\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(2,1)}
, and remembering
\tilde{\omega}_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)

-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}\tilde{\kappa}_{i}\frac{d\tilde{T}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)+\rho_{*}\frac{5}{2}\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\tilde{T}_{i}\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(2,1)}\\
=3\frac{1}{\rho_{*}^{2}}\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}(\tilde{T}_{e}-\tilde{T}_{i})+\tilde{\eta}_{1i}\left(\tilde{r}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}\\
+\rho_{*}\frac{d\tilde{p}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(2,1)}}{\tilde{r}\tilde{n}_{i}}+\frac{3}{2}\rho_{*}\tilde{s}_{i}^{(2,1)}\tilde{T}_{i}\\
+\rho_{*}^{4}\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{0i}}{3}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(2,1)}}{\tilde{n}_{i}}\right)^{2}+\tilde{\eta}_{1i}\left(\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon\tilde{r}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\left(\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(2,1)}}{\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{n}_{i}}\right)\right)^{2},~

(55)

and the electron equation,

-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}\frac{d\tilde{T}_{e}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)=-3\frac{1}{\rho_{*}^{2}}\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}(\tilde{T}_{e}-\tilde{T}_{i})\\
+\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\frac{\tilde{m}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}}{\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{n}_{i}}\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{(2,1)^{2}}+\tilde{\eta}_{1e}\left(\tilde{r}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{e}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}

(56)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}=\left[4.66+\frac{9}{4}\left[1-\frac{I_{1}\left(\frac{\tilde{r}}{\rho_{*}}\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{n}_{e}\tilde{\nu}_{ei}}{\tilde{\eta}_{1e}}}\right)}{I_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{*}}\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{n}_{e}\tilde{\nu}_{ei}}{\tilde{\eta}_{1e}}}\Big|_{\tilde{r}=1}\right)}\right]\right]\frac{\tilde{p}_{e}\tilde{\nu}_{ei}}{\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{\Omega}_{e}^{2}}~,

(57)

being the effective heat transfer coefficient for the electrons, due to the contribution of the Ettingshausen,
\tilde{\kappa}^{\omega}
term.
The plasma heat transfer coefficient, for
Z_{i}=1
,
\tilde{m}_{i}=1
, is
\tilde{\kappa}_{tot}=\tilde{\kappa}_{i}+\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}\approx 1.16\tilde{\kappa}_{i}
, outside of the boundary layer.

This is a linear ODE for
\tilde{T}_{i}
, with a single term - the collisional equilibration between ions and electrons
\sim\rho_{*}^{-2}
. Thus, in the low-flow case,
F_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*})
,
\Gamma_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon)
, the temperature difference between the ion and electron fluids is of
\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2}\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{2}/\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{e}})\sim\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2}/\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{e}})
.

To leading order, with constant coefficients, the sum of the two equations is,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}\tilde{\kappa}_{tot}\frac{d\tilde{T}_{i}^{(0,0)}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)=\tilde{\eta}_{1i}\left(\tilde{r}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)^{2},

(58)

yielding

\tilde{T}_{i}^{(0,0)}=-\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{1i}}{\tilde{\kappa}_{tot}}\frac{\tilde{\Omega}_{i}^{2}e^{-2F_{i}}}{32\rho_{*}^{2}F_{i}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}(Ei(2F_{i}\tilde{r}^{2})-Ei(2F_{i}))-\ln(\tilde{r})\\
+\frac{1}{4}(e^{2F_{i}\tilde{r}^{2}}(2F_{i}\tilde{r}^{2}-3)-e^{2F_{i}}(2F_{i}-3))\Bigg)+\tilde{T}_{i}(1)

(59)

with
\tilde{\eta}_{1i}/\tilde{\kappa}_{i}=0.15\tilde{m}_{i}
and
\tilde{\eta}_{1i}/\tilde{\kappa}_{tot}\approx 0.13\tilde{m}_{i}
. These ratios are related to the Prandtl number as
\tilde{\eta}/\tilde{\kappa}=\frac{2}{3}\tilde{m}\mathrm{Pr}
. The function
Ei(x)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{e^{t}}{t}dt
is the exponential integral. For small values of
F_{i}
, such as
F_{i}\approx-\rho_{*}
, the boundary term
\tilde{T}_{i}(1)=1
is the dominant term in the leading-order temperature solution.

The temperature difference between electrons and ions
\Delta T_{ei}=\tilde{T}_{e}-\tilde{T}_{i}
, is determined, to leading order, by

-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\left(\tilde{r}\frac{d\Delta T_{ei}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)=-\frac{3\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}}{\rho_{*}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\kappa}_{i}}\right)\Delta T_{ei}\\
+\frac{2}{3}\left(\tilde{m}_{e}\mathrm{Pr}_{e}-\tilde{m}_{i}\mathrm{Pr}_{i}\right)\left(\tilde{r}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\right)^{2}.~

(60)

The leading order solution to this equation is

\Delta T_{ei}=\left.-\frac{2\rho_{*}^{2}}{9\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}}\frac{\tilde{m}_{i}\mathrm{Pr}_{i}-\tilde{m}_{e}\mathrm{Pr}_{e}}{\frac{1}{\tilde{\kappa}_{i}}+\frac{1}{\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}}}\right(\tilde{r}^{2}\left[\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\right]^{2}\\
\left.-\left[\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}\right]^{2}\Bigg|_{\tilde{r}=1}\frac{I_{0}\left(\frac{\tilde{r}}{\rho_{*}}\sqrt{3\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}^{-1}+\tilde{\kappa}_{i}^{-1}\right)}\right)}{I_{0}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{*}}\sqrt{3\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}^{-1}+\tilde{\kappa}_{i}^{-1}\right)\Big|_{\tilde{r}=1}}\right)}\right)~

(61)

with
I_{0}
being the modified Bessel function of the first kind.

Since
\tilde{m}_{e}\mathrm{Pr}_{e}<\tilde{m}_{i}\mathrm{Pr}_{i}
, the solution is negative, indeed yielding a hot-ion mode, but it also contains a pre-factor of
\rho_{*}^{2}/\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{e}}
. In order to achieve a temperature difference of
\mathcal{O}(1)
, we identify a course of action, that does not suffer from the fueling limit discussed later. In any case, one has to push
\tilde{\omega}
to be as large as possible. However, the
\rho_{*}^{2}/\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{e}}
pre-factor has to be dealt with as well: Use low magnetization, such that
\rho_{*}\gtrsim\sqrt[4]{\tilde{m}_{e}}\sim 0.1
. This would bring the pre-factor to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
. In this case,
\tilde{s}_{i}^{(2,1)}
can remain small such that the angular velocity is still linear in
\tilde{s}_{i}
. The source term magnitude must then be larger than the square root of thecombined value of the reminder of (
61
), which is of
\mathcal{O}(0.001)
, when substituting
\tilde{\omega}_{i}
as a function of
\tilde{s}_{i}
. In case of Braginskii coefficients for
Z_{i}=\tilde{m}_{i}=1
, this source term is
\tilde{s}_{i}\approx-20\rho_{*}^{2}\epsilon
. It is perhaps easier to increase
\rho_{*}
rather than
\tilde{s}_{i}
, as the maximal temperature difference increases as
\rho_{*}^{3}\left(\tilde{s}_{i}^{(2,1)}\right)^{2}
at large
\rho_{*}
, see Figure
3
. In the figure, the temperature difference is
\sim 0.01
because the source term magnitude kept relatively small for numerical reasons. This Larmor radius is becoming somewhat large for a small parameter, required for the fluid approximation. Some magnetic traps operate with this Larmor radius / machine size scale.

III.3
Density

The density profile is determined from the radial force balance. Summing electrons and ions, taking
\tilde{n}_{e}=Z_{i}\tilde{n}_{i}
, and neglecting electron viscosity, the radial force balance of the plasma as a whole is

\frac{d\tilde{p}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}+\frac{d\tilde{p}_{e}}{d\tilde{r}}=\frac{3}{2}Z_{i}\tilde{n}_{i}\frac{d\tilde{T}_{e}}{d\tilde{r}}+\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{r}\left(\tilde{m}_{i}\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{2}+Z_{i}\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{\omega}_{e}^{2}\right)\\
+\frac{\tilde{m}_{i}\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{2}}{\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{n}_{i}}\left(\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}+\frac{d\ln(\tilde{n}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}-\frac{d\ln(\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}^{2})}{d\tilde{r}}\right)\\
+\frac{\rho_{*}}{\epsilon}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\left(\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{0i}\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}}{3\tilde{r}\tilde{n}_{i}}\left[\frac{d\ln(\tilde{\Gamma}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}-\frac{d\ln(\tilde{n}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}\right]\right)\\
+\frac{\rho_{*}}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\frac{d}{d\tilde{r}}\tilde{r}^{3}\left(\tilde{\eta}_{3i}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{i}}{d\tilde{r}}+\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{\eta}_{3e}\frac{d\tilde{\omega}_{e}}{d\tilde{r}}\right.\\
\left.+\epsilon\frac{\tilde{\eta}_{1i}\tilde{\Gamma}_{i}}{\tilde{r}^{2}\tilde{n}_{i}}\left[\frac{d\ln(\tilde{\Gamma}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}-\frac{d\ln(\tilde{n}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}-\frac{2}{\tilde{r}}\right]\right).~

(62)

This is a second-order nonlinear ODE in
\tilde{n}_{i}
, and solving the leading-order terms in it would both remove the non-linearity, and reduce it to a first-order differential equation.
To leading order,

\displaystyle\frac{d\ln(\tilde{n}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}+\frac{1-\frac{1}{2}Z_{i}}{1+Z_{i}}\frac{d\ln(\tilde{T}_{i})}{d\tilde{r}}=\frac{\tilde{r}\left(\tilde{m}_{i}\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{2}+Z_{i}\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{\omega}_{e}^{2}\right)}{\tilde{T}_{i}(1+Z_{i})},

(63)

or for
Z_{i}=1
,

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{i}^{(0,0)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt[4]{\tilde{T}_{i}}}e^{\frac{\tilde{m}_{i}\tilde{\Omega}_{i}^{2}}{32\rho_{*}^{2}}\int_{1}^{\tilde{r}}\frac{\tilde{r}^{\prime}\left(e^{F_{i}(\tilde{r}^{\prime 2}-1)}-1\right)^{2}}{\tilde{T}_{i}}d\tilde{r}^{\prime}}.

(64)

This density profile is hollow - matter is pushed to outer radii from the center.

IV
Variable coefficients solution

Braginskii’s fluid model is inherently nonlinear, with collision frequencies that depend on the densities and temperatures. The diffusion coefficients (viscosities and heat transfer coefficients), present additional nonlinearity. From the linear, constant coefficients solution, it is evident that the viscosity and heat transfer coefficients drop as the magnitude of the particle flux increases. The shorthand
F_{i}
would become larger at smaller radii, and the angular velocity increase beyond its constant coefficients solution values.

There are two ways in which a particle flux would fail to produce a physical solution:

1.

The solution breaks the ordering
\rho_{i}\ll R
.

2.

The solution produces a negative pressure, or is unable to satisfy both boundary conditions.

For the first case, even a constant coefficient solution with
F_{i}\sim\mathcal{O}({\rho_{*}})
would produce rotations that are
\tilde{\omega}\sim\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{-1})
, and temperature that is
\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{-2})
. The Larmor radius
\rho_{i}\propto\sqrt{T_{i}}
, and increasing the temperature above its reference value by a factor of
\rho_{*}^{-2}
puts us firmly in the kinetic regime, where the fluid model is inapplicable. Using a variable-coefficients solution would bring that threshold to smaller values of particle flux.

The second case is a feature of non-linear ODEs, where there is no guarantee for the existence of a boundary-value-problem solution in all cases. We can attempt solving initial value problems, and using a shooting method to pinpoint the correct values and derivatives at one boundary to hit the correct values at the other, or transform the ODEs into PDEs, and attempt to relax the solution to a steady state at finite times.

Even at Mach numbers in which a solution for the nonlinear case exists, the solution might develop an angular velocity boundary layer at
\tilde{r}=0
which might be nonphysical. This boundary layer enforces the axisymmetry condition
\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}(0)=0
, which appears in (
30
). The boundary layer width, shrinks rapidly around
M=2
, while the temperature rises, as can be seen in Figure
2
. When the boundary layer width becomes smaller than an ion Larmor radius, the solution becomes nonphysical. See appendix.
Steady state solutions for
M\gtrsim 2
don’t exist in the full nonlinear case, as the viscosity drops quite significantly, and it could not balance the torque produced by the magnetic field.

V
Conclusion

A solution to the flow and temperature profiles in a highly magnetized rotating cylindrical two-fluid (ion-electron) plasma, driven by constant ion charge extraction following Braginskii’s fluid was investigated.
First, a leading-order solution in the low-flow limit was presented.
Second, the physical validity of the asymptotic solution was considered, and the existence of a hot-ion mode was evaluated. It was shown that the collisional temperature coupling between the fluids is stronger than the difference in viscous heating between the fluids by a factor of
\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{e}}/\rho_{*}^{2}
, and this limits the temperature difference to be of
\mathcal{O}(\rho_{*}^{2}/\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{e}})
.

The ratio of ion to electron heating is the same as the ratio of viscosity coefficients
\tilde{m}_{e}^{3/2}
. Picking a
\rho_{*}\sim\sqrt[4]{\tilde{m}_{e}}\sim 0.1
would bring the temperature difference to
\mathcal{O}(0.1-1)
, depending on the exact sink term magnitude. This would be a large
\rho_{*}
, but not impossibly so - some magnetic traps or FRCs
Steinhauer 2011
operate in this regime.
Finally, the departure of the non-linear solution from the linear approximation at moderate Mach numbers (
M\gtrsim 0.5
) was demonstrated and is explained by the hollowing-out of the viscosity and heat conductivity profiles.

We have shown the inherent difficulties in achieving a significant hot-ion mode, even in the absence of electron heating, due to the limitation on ion heating. We have shown that low magnetization devices present an easier avenue for a hot-ion mode. Paradoxically, this is a point in favor of devices that contain fewer ion Larmor radii - which can be accomplished using a smaller magnetic field or a smaller device size - in view of the large advantage a hot-ion mode might present for a fusion plasma.

There are other possible ion sink profiles,
\tilde{s}_{i}(\tilde{r})
, and the details of such solutions may differ from the solution presented here. The main effect discussed here, however, the hollowing out of the density and the viscosity profiles, is weakly dependant on the specific
\tilde{s}_{i}(\tilde{r})
profile, and requires only the functional dependence
\tilde{\eta}_{1}\propto\tilde{n}^{2}
.

Appendix A
Boundary layers

The above solution includes several boundary layers, in the electron angular velocity, effective heat transfer coefficient, and the temperature difference between electrons and ions.
The arguments of the boundary layers are expressed in terms of the electron viscosity and heat transfer coefficients.
Using Braginskii’s classical transport coefficients, the argument of the electron angular velocity boundary layer term is

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{r}}{\rho_{*}}\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{n}_{e}\tilde{\nu}_{ei}}{\tilde{\eta}_{1e}}}=\frac{\tilde{r}}{\rho_{*}}\frac{\tilde{B}}{\sqrt{0.51\tilde{T}_{e}\tilde{m}_{e}}}=1.4\frac{r\Omega_{e}}{v_{the}}=1.4\frac{r}{\rho_{e}}.

(66)

Here,
\rho_{e}
is the electron Larmor radius. This boundary layer is thinner than a Larmor orbit. As such, the classical transport model breaks down at these length scales, hence, we must abandon this boundary layer solution and the boundary condition that produced it, and resort to a full slip condition for the electron fluid at the outer edge of the cylinder.
This same boundary layer appears in the effective electron heat conduction, and must be discarded there as well.

As such, in the limit of Braginskii’s classical transport, equations (
27
), (
50
), and (
57
) should simply read

\displaystyle(\tilde{\nabla}\cdot\tilde{\pi}_{e})_{\theta}(1)=0,

(67)

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{e}=\tilde{\omega}_{i}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\rho_{*}}{\tilde{r}\tilde{B}}\frac{d\tilde{T}_{e}}{d\tilde{r}},

(68)

\displaystyle\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}=6.91\frac{\tilde{p}_{e}\tilde{\nu}_{ei}}{\tilde{m}_{e}\tilde{\Omega}_{e}^{2}}.

(69)

However, some non-classical transport effects
Rognlien and Ryutov 1999
, such as turbulent anomalous transport, or the effects of perturbations to the magnetic field
Finn et al. 1992
, might enhance the electron viscosity above Braginskii’s values, while still maintaining the validity of the fluid approximation.
In these cases, if the effective electron viscosity might be large enough such that the boundary layer thickness might encompass several Larmor orbits, the boundary layer solution in the electron angular velocity might be a true physical effect.

The argument of the temperature-difference boundary layer, using Braginskii’s classical transport coefficients, is

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{r}}{\rho_{*}}\sqrt{3\tilde{n}_{i}\tilde{\nu}_{ie}\left(\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}^{-1}+\tilde{\kappa}_{i}^{-1}\right)}=\frac{\tilde{r}}{\rho_{*}}\frac{0.71}{\sqrt{\tilde{m}_{i}}}.

(70)

This boundary layer does encompass many electron gyro-orbits, and is plausible because of it.
Physically, it must be enforced by some effect on the boundary. We are not concerned with modeling the plasma-surface interactions that might lead to this electron temperature boundary layer, and conclude that the fluid approximation is valid for a boundary layer, with, possibly a different boundary value for the electrons other than the ions.

Note that the magnitude of
\Delta T_{ei}
, for the constant coefficient solution, is controlled by the scaling of the flux (
\tilde{s}_{i}
or equivalently
F_{i}
), which directly affects
\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{\prime}
, and is not dependent on the exact form of the viscosity or heat transfer coefficients. Trying to maximize
\tilde{T}_{i}-\tilde{T}_{e}
in equation (
61
), by changing
\tilde{\kappa}_{e_{eff}}
only, or in conjunction with
\tilde{\eta}_{1e}
, using an anomalous electron transport, for example, would yield limited results.

In a fusion reactor, when
\alpha
-channeling is missing or is insufficiently effective at removing the fusion ash, the ash would slow down on the electrons and introduce significant electron heating. Indeed this mechanism typically causes a hot electron mode. Alternatively, Bremsstrahlung radiation introduces a heat sink in the electrons, which would help maintain a hot-ion mode, when dealing with mildly relativistic plasmas such as in
p-B^{11}
reactors.
