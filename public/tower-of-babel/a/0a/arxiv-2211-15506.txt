Fast non-Hermitian Toeplitz eigenvalue computations, joining matrix-less algorithms and FDE approximation matrices
M. Bogoya, S.M. Grudsky, S. Serra–Capizzano

Abstract
The present work is devoted to the eigenvalue asymptotic expansion of the Toeplitz matrix T n ​ ( a ) T_{n}(a) whose generating function a a is complex valued and has a power singularity at one point. As a consequence, T n ​ ( a ) T_{n}(a) is non-Hermitian and we know that the eigenvalue computation is a non-trivial task in the non-Hermitian setting for large sizes. We follow the work of Bogoya, Böttcher, Grudsky, and Maximenko and deduce a complete asymptotic expansion for the eigenvalues. After that, we apply matrix-less algorithms, in the spirit of the work by Ekström, Furci, Garoni, Serra-Capizzano et al, for computing those eigenvalues. Since the inner and extreme eigenvalues have different asymptotic behaviors, we worked on them independently, and combined the results to produce a high precision global numerical and matrix-less algorithm. The numerical results are very precise and the computational cost of the proposed algorithms is independent of the size of the considered matrices for each eigenvalue, which implies a linear cost when all the spectrum is computed. From the viewpoint of real world applications, we emphasize that the matrix class under consideration includes the matrices stemming from the numerical approximation of fractional diffusion equations. In the final conclusion section a concise discussion on the matter and few open problems are presented. MSC Classes : Primary 15B05, 65F15, 47B35. Secondary 15A18, 47A38.

Introduction

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the complex unit circle. For a complex-valued function
a\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
let
a_{k}
be its
k
th Fourier coefficient, that is

a_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}a(\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}\theta})\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{-\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}k\theta}\dif\theta,\quad\ k\in\mathbb{Z},\ \hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}^{2}=-1.

The
n\times n
Toeplitz matrix with
generating function

a
is given by
T_{n}(a)\equiv(a_{j-k})_{j,k=0}^{n-1}
, and is characterized by having the fixed constant
a_{k}
along the
k
th diagonal, parallel to the main one. In formulae, we have

T_{n}(a)=\begin{bmatrix}a_{0}&a_{-1}&a_{-2}&\cdots&\cdots&a_{-(n-1)}\\
a_{1}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
a_{2}&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a_{-2}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&a_{-1}\\
a_{n-1}&\cdots&\cdots&a_{2}&a_{1}&a_{0}\end{bmatrix}.

We will follow the notation of
[
1
]
. Let
H^{\infty}
be the Hardy space of (boundary values of) bounded analytic functions in the complex unit disk
\mathbb{D}
,
\mathbb{T}=\partial\mathbb{D}
. For a continuous generating function
a
, we denote by

\wind_{\lambda}(a)\equiv\frac{1}{2\pi\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\mathcal{R}(a)}\frac{\dif\zeta}{\zeta-\lambda},

the winding number of
a
in connection with a point
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathcal{R}(a)
where
\mathcal{R}(a)
is the range of
a
. Intuitively,
\wind_{\lambda}(a)
is the number of times that the range of
a
travels around the point
\lambda
in counterclockwise sense. We also denote by
\mathcal{D}(a)
the set of all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for which
\wind_{\lambda}(a)\neq 0
. In plane words
\mathcal{D}(a)
is the collection of all the points trapped by
\mathcal{R}(a)
. In the first part of the current work, we will study the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(a)
for functions
a(t)=\frac{1}{t}(1-t)^{\alpha}f(t)
satisfying the following properties:

1.

The constant
\alpha
belongs to the interval
(0,1)
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})\cap H^{\infty}
.

2.

f(z)\neq 0
for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
, and
f
has an analytic extension to an open neighborhood
W
of
\mathbb{T}\setminus\{1\}
not containing the point
1
.

3.

The range of
a
is a Jordan curve in
\mathbb{C}
which is the boundary of a connected region in the complex plane.

Strictly speaking, the complex-valued function
(1-z)^{\alpha}
is multi-valued, hence we take the argument specified by
\arg(1-z)^{\alpha}\in(-\alpha\pi,\alpha\pi]
when
\arg(1-z)\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Property (ii) is necessary because, in that case,
b
belongs to the Hardy space
H^{\infty}
and the nice relation
T_{n}^{-1}(b)=T_{n}\big(\frac{1}{b}\big)
holds, which is used for expressing the determinant
D_{n}(a)
of
T_{n}(a)
as a Fourier integral. Additionally, the condition
\alpha\in(0,1)
implies that
a
has a power singularity at
t=1
, i.e. no continuous derivatives there.

At this point it is worth noticing that the considered set of functions includes the generating functions of a class of Hessenberg Toeplitz matrices stemming from the numerical approximation of certain fractional diffusion equations (FDEs); see
[
5
,
6
,
13
]
. In the latter context, the parameter
\alpha
belongs to the open interval
(0,2)
. We remind that the quoted FDEs with noninteger parameter
\alpha\in(0,2)
have gained a tremendous attention in real world applications, because they model anomalous diffusion processes arising in Biology, Physics, etc. (see again
[
13
]
and references therein).

Property (ii) also guaranties that the only point of
\mathbb{T}
where
a
is not analytic is
t=1
, and finally, property (iii) amounts in avoiding that
\mathcal{R}(a)
shows the presence of loops. The resulting Toeplitz matrices
T_{n}(a)
are lower Hessenberg, meaning that they can be obtained by adding one upper diagonal to a lower triangular matrix.

We emphasize that this knowledge is important when analyzing fast methods for the solution of large linear systems arising from the numerical approximation of the considered FDEs, via preconditioned Krylov methods, multigrid algorithms, and multi-iterative solvers (see e.g.
[
12
]
and references therein).

In
[
1
]
an asymptotic expansion was deduced for the eigenvalues bounded away from zero, which the authors named
inner
, while in
[
4
]
the
extreme
(closest to zero) eigenvalues were studied. With a different technique, the authors were able to obtain an asymptotic expression for the eigenvalues located near to the singularity point. The result is reminiscent of the works by S. Parter
[
18
,
19
]
and H. Widom
[
24
]
, for the real-valued case; see also
[
9
,
20
,
21
,
23
]
for a more complete picture on the subject, including newer matrix theoretic tools.

The case
\alpha\in(1,2)
or more generally,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, can be managed analogously for the inner eigenvalues, while the extreme eigenvalues remain a challenge that will be the subject of a future investigation. For this reason we restrict ourselves to the case
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Regarding computational costs, the best option relies in employing extrapolation techniques based on the asymptotic expansion, because the latter leads to a direct eigenvalue approximation which is independent of the matrix entries or its size. Indeed the computation can be done in two different ways: the first one is to use the analytic expression of the coefficient functions, if available; the second one is to employ a numerical and matrix-less algorithm to approximate them. After the respective precomputing phases, in both cases, the calculation will need only a fraction of a second in a standard computer. The standard option is to use any commercial eigensolver which are limited by the matrix size and its conditioning. To put it in numbers, the eigenvalue calculation of a
4000
size moderately conditioned matrix with 50 precision digits, can be done in a week by a standard computer. If the matrix size is
10000
, only a supercomputer can handle the same task, but if the matrix size is
10^{10}
(a size appearing in several applications) no computer can do it.

Taking inspiration from the literature, in the present work we split our study into the mentioned cases. The article is organized as follows: in Section
2
we deduce a complete asymptotic expansion for the inner eigenvalues of
T_{n}(a)
which served as the theoretical ground for the subsequent matrix-less numerical algorithm. In Section
3
we use a previous theoretical result to conjecture an expansion which was the base for the respective matrix-less numerical algorithm. In Section
4
we join the two processes in order to design a global algorithm approximating all the eigenvalues with high accuracy: in this section we also report and discuss our numerical experiments. Finally, in Section
5
we draw some future works.

Theorem 2.2

n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}
and multiplying, we reach

t_{\lambda}\sim\omega_{n}^{j}\bigg\{1+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\zeta^{(1)}_{l}(t_{\lambda})\frac{\log^{\ell}(n)}{n^{\ell}}+\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\zeta^{(2)}_{r,m,l}(t_{\lambda})\frac{\log^{\ell}(n)}{n^{\alpha r+m}}\bigg\},

(2.5)

where the functions
\zeta_{l}^{(1)}
and
\zeta_{r,m.l}^{(2)}
belong to
C^{\infty}(W\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}})
and can be determined explicitly. Since the expanded terms in (
2.5
) have order
\frac{\log(n)}{n}
, our first iteration is
t_{\lambda}^{(1)}=\omega_{n}^{j}+O(\frac{\log(n)}{n})
. The second iteration is

\displaystyle t_{\lambda}^{(2)}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{j}n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}\bigg\{1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geqslant 0\\
m\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\xi_{r,m}(t_{\lambda}^{(1)})}{n^{\alpha r+m}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{j}n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}\bigg\{1+\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor}\frac{\xi_{r,1}(\omega_{n}^{j})}{n^{\alpha r+1}}+O\Big(\frac{\log(n)}{n^{2}}\Big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{j}\bigg\{1+\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor}\frac{\xi_{r,1}(\omega_{n}^{j})}{n^{\alpha r+1}}+O\Big(\frac{\log(n)}{n^{2}}\Big)\bigg\}.

To reveal the shape in the full expansion we need a third iteration,

\displaystyle t_{\lambda}^{(3)}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{j}n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}\bigg\{1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\geqslant 0\\
m\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\frac{\xi_{r,m}(t_{\lambda}^{(2)})}{n^{\alpha r+m}}\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{j}n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}\bigg\{1+\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{2}{\alpha}\rfloor}\frac{\xi_{r,1}(\omega_{n}^{j})}{n^{\alpha r+1}}+\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=0}^{1}\zeta_{l,r}^{(3)}(\omega_{n}^{j})\frac{\log^{\ell}(n)}{n^{\alpha r+2}}+O\Big(\frac{\log(n)}{n^{3}}\Big)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\omega_{n}^{j}\bigg\{1+\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{2}{\alpha}\rfloor}\frac{\zeta_{r}^{(4)}(\omega_{n}^{j})}{n^{\alpha r+1}}+\sum_{r=0}^{\lfloor\frac{1}{\alpha}\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=0}^{1}\zeta_{j,r}^{(5)}(\omega_{n}^{j})\frac{\log^{\ell}(n)}{n^{\alpha r+2}}+O\Big(\frac{\log(n)}{n^{3}}\Big)\bigg\},

where the functions
\zeta_{l,r}^{(3)}
,
\zeta_{r}^{(4)}
, and
\zeta_{j,r}^{(5)}
belong to
C^{\infty}(W\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}})
.
By continuing this process we finish the theorem.
∎

2.1
Algorithm

As in previous works, one key observation is that
\omega_{n}^{j}
can be set unchanged under several combinations of
n
and
j
, for example

\omega_{n}^{j}=\omega_{cn}^{cj},

for any constant
c
, thus we take
n_{\ell}\equiv\ell n_{1}
and
j_{\ell}\equiv\ell j_{1}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. The index
j_{\ell}
depends on
j_{1}
, and similarly, the matrix sizes
n_{\ell}
depend on
n_{1}
, but for notation simplicity, we suppressed those dependencies. Therefore we have

\sigma_{j_{1}}\equiv\omega_{n_{1}}^{j_{1}}=\omega_{n_{2}}^{j_{2}}=\cdots=\omega_{n_{k-1}}^{j_{k-1}},

see Figure
2
.
The sequence
\{\omega_{n}^{j}\}_{j=0}^{n-1}
corresponds to the
n
roots of the unity in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and is a regular partition of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
with step angle of
\frac{2\pi}{n}
. We assume that
n_{1}
and
k
are fixed natural numbers and
n\gg n_{1}
.

Our algorithm is composed by three phases: precomputing, extrapolation, and interpolation. We now describe them in detail.

Figure 2
:
For
n_{1}=12
the regular circular grids
\{\omega_{n_{\ell}}^{j}\}
for
j=1,\ldots,n_{\ell}
and
\ell=1,2,3
. In the precomputing phase we will need to calculate the eigenvalues of
T_{n_{\ell}}(a)
for
\ell=1,\ldots,k-1
corresponding to the blue and red dots combined, but in the interpolation phase, we will use only the eigenvalues corresponding to the red dots, that is
\lambda_{j_{\ell}}(T_{n_{\ell}}(a))
for
j_{1}=1,\ldots,n_{1}
and
\ell=1,\ldots,k-1
.

Precomputing.

In this phase we need to calculate the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(a)
for
n=n_{1},\ldots,n_{k-1}
. The numerical computations for the “exact” eigenvalues are conducted with the
BigFloat
data type in
Julia v.1.7.2
using 1024 bit precision which approximately corresponds to 300 decimal digits of accuracy. We use an AMD Epyc with 32 cores/64 threads and 1024 GB RAM. The remaining computations are carried using
Mathematica v13
.

Extrapolation.
Here we need to simplify the expansion in Theorem
2.1
, for that purpose, let
\xi_{1}(n),\xi_{2}(n),\ldots
, be the asymptotic ordering of the set

\Big\{\frac{\log^{\ell}(n)}{n^{\alpha r+m}}\colon r\geqslant 0,\ m\geqslant 1,\ \ell=0,\ldots,m-1\Big\}.

This ordering depends on
\alpha
, for example for
\alpha=\frac{3}{4}
we obtain

\displaystyle\xi_{1}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n},

\displaystyle\xi_{2}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\alpha+1}},

\displaystyle\xi_{3}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(n)}{n^{2}},

\displaystyle\xi_{4}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}

\displaystyle\xi_{5}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2\alpha+1}},

\displaystyle\xi_{6}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log(n)}{n^{\alpha+2}},

\displaystyle\xi_{7}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{\alpha+2}},

\displaystyle\xi_{8}(n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\log^{2}(n)}{n^{3}}.

For each positive entire number
k
, rewrite the expansion as follows

t_{j,n}=\omega_{n}^{j}n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}\Big\{1+\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}c_{s}(\omega_{n}^{j})\xi_{s}(n)+E_{k,j,n}\Big\},

(2.6)

where
E_{k,j,n}=O(\xi_{k}(n))
is a remainder or error term satisfying
|E_{k,j,n}|\leqslant\kappa\,\xi_{k}(n)
for some constant
\kappa
depending only on the generating function
a
. For each fixed
j_{1}=1,\ldots,n_{1}
let
\sigma_{j_{1}}\equiv\omega_{n_{1}}^{j_{1}}=\cdots=\omega_{n_{k-1}}^{j_{k-1}}
(see the radially aligned red dots in Figure
2
), and apply
k-1
times the expansion (
2.6
), obtaining

\displaystyle t_{j_{1},n_{1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}}n_{1}^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n_{1}}}\Big\{1+c_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\xi_{1}(n_{1})+\cdots+c_{k-1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\xi_{k-1}(n_{1})+E_{k,j_{1},n_{1}}\Big\},

\displaystyle t_{j_{2},n_{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}}n_{2}^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n_{2}}}\Big\{1+c_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\xi_{1}(n_{2})+\cdots+c_{k-1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\xi_{k-1}(n_{2})+E_{k,j_{2},n_{2}}\Big\},

\displaystyle t_{j_{k-1},n_{k-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{j_{1}}n_{k-1}^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n_{k-1}}}\Big\{1+c_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\xi_{1}(n_{k-1})+\cdots+c_{k-1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\xi_{k-1}(n_{k-1})+E_{k,j_{k-1},n_{k-1}}\Big\}.

Let
\hat{c}_{s}(\sigma_{j_{1}})
be the approximation of
c_{s}(\sigma_{j_{1}})
obtained by removing all the remainder terms
E_{k,j_{\ell},n_{\ell}}
and solving the resulting linear system:

\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}(n_{1})&\xi_{2}(n_{1})&\cdots&\xi_{k-1}(n_{1})\\[3.44444pt]
\xi_{1}(n_{2})&\xi_{2}(n_{2})&\cdots&\xi_{k-1}(n_{2})\\[3.44444pt]
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\[3.44444pt]
\xi_{1}(n_{k-1})&\xi_{2}(n_{k-1})&\cdots&\xi_{k-1}(n_{k-1})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{c}_{1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\\[3.44444pt]
\hat{c}_{2}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\\[3.44444pt]
\vdots\\[3.44444pt]
\hat{c}_{k-1}(\sigma_{j_{1}})\end{bmatrix}=\sigma_{j_{1}}^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}n_{1}^{-(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n_{1}}}t_{j_{1},n_{1}}\\
n_{2}^{-(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n_{2}}}t_{j_{2},n_{2}}\\
\vdots\\
n_{k-1}^{-(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n_{k-1}}}t_{j_{k-1},n_{k-1}}\end{bmatrix}\!\!-\!\!\begin{bmatrix}1\\[3.44444pt]
1\\[3.44444pt]
\vdots\\[3.44444pt]
1\end{bmatrix}.

Thanks to properties (i)–(vi), the function
a
is bijective, and hence the value of each
t_{j_{\ell},n_{\ell}}
can be calculated as
a^{-1}(\lambda_{j_{\ell}}(T_{n_{\ell}}(a)))
. In general, the latter computation can be done numerically with any standard root finder (i.e.
FindRoot
in
Mathematica
or
fzero
in
Matlab
). As mentioned in previous works, a variant of this extrapolation strategy was first suggested by Albrecht Böttcher in
[
2
, §7]
and is analogous to the Richardson extrapolation employed in the context of Romberg integration
[
22
, §3.4]
.

Interpolation.
The objective of this phase is to estimate
\hat{c}_{s}(\omega_{n}^{j})
, for any index
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, any
n
, and any level
s=1,\ldots,k-1
. If
\omega_{n}^{j}
coincides with one of the points in the grid
\{\omega_{n_{1}}^{1},\ldots,\omega_{n_{1}}^{n_{1}}\}
, then we have the approximations
\hat{c}_{s}(\omega_{n}^{j})
from the extrapolation phase for free. In any other case, we will do it by interpolating the data

(\omega_{n_{1}}^{1},\hat{c}_{s}(\omega_{n_{1}}^{1})),(\omega_{n_{1}}^{2},\hat{c}_{s}(\omega_{n_{1}}^{2})),\ldots,(\omega_{n_{1}}^{n_{1}},\hat{c}_{s}(\omega_{n_{1}}^{n_{1}})),

and then evaluating the resulting polynomial at
\omega_{n}^{j}
. This interpolation can be done in many ways, but to avoid spurious oscillations explained by the Runge phenomenon
[
11
, p. 78]
, and following the strategy of the previous works, we decided to do it considering only the
k-s+8
nodes in the grid
\{\omega_{n_{1}}^{1},\ldots,\omega_{n_{1}}^{n_{1}}\}
which are closest to
\omega_{n}^{j}
. Those nodes can be determined uniquely unless
\omega_{n}^{j}
is the mid point of two consecutive nodes in the grid, in which case we can take any of the two possible choices.

Finally, our eigenvalue approximation with
k
terms, is given by

\lambda_{j}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}(T_{n}(a))\equiv a\Big(\omega_{n}^{j}n^{(\alpha+1)\frac{1}{n}}\Big\{1+\sum_{s=1}^{k-1}\hat{c}_{s}(\omega_{n}^{j})\xi_{s}(n)\Big\}\Big).

(2.7)

Remark 2.4
.

The approximation (
2.7
) can be tuned in a number of ways. To guarantee acceptable overall accuracy, the precomputing phase must be done with a significant amount of precision digits, let us say 60. In the precomputing and extrapolation phases, we can manage more levels
k
than in the interpolation phase, for example 2 or 3 additional levels will improve the final result. Finally, in the interpolation phase we can increase the number of interpolated points.

3
Extreme eigenvalues

In this section we develop a numerical algorithm to approximate the extreme eigenvalues of
T_{n}(a)
. In the paper
[
4
]
the authors considered the generating function

a(t)=\frac{1}{t}(1-t)^{\alpha}f(t),

satisfying the following properties:

1.

The function
f
belongs to
H^{\infty}
with
f(0)\neq 0
and for some
\varepsilon>0
,
f
has an analytic continuation to the region

K_{\varepsilon}\equiv B(1,\varepsilon)\setminus\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon 1<x<1+\varepsilon\},

and is continuous in
\hat{K}_{\varepsilon}\equiv\overline{B(1,\varepsilon)}\setminus\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon 1<x<1+\varepsilon\}
. Additionally,
f_{\varphi}(x)\equiv f(1+x\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}\varphi})
belongs to the algebra
C^{2}[0,\varepsilon]
for each
\varphi\in(-\pi,\pi]
. Here
B(z_{0},r)
is the open disk centered at
z_{0}
with radius
r
.

2.

Let
0<\alpha<1
be a constant and take the argument
-\alpha\pi<\arg(1-z)^{\alpha}\leqslant\alpha\pi
when
-\pi<\arg(1-z)\leqslant\pi
.

3.

The range
\mathcal{R}(a)
is a Jordan curve in
\mathbb{C}
with
\wind_{\lambda}(a)=-1
for each
\lambda\in\mathcal{D}(a)
.

For the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))
located in
W_{0}
, it was proved that

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))=\frac{\Lambda_{j}^{\alpha}}{(n+1)^{\alpha}}\{1+\Delta(\Lambda_{j},n)\},

(3.1)

where
\Delta(\Lambda_{j},n)=O(n^{-\alpha})
, as
n\to\infty
, represents the remainder or error term, which satisfies the inequality
|\Delta(\Lambda_{j},n)|\leqslant\kappa n^{-\alpha}
for some constant
\kappa
depending only on
a
. The numbers
\Lambda_{j}
are certain roots of the function

F(\Lambda)\equiv\frac{2\pi\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}}{\alpha}\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda}-\int_{C}\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda u}\beta(u)\dif u,

where
C
is the radial segment in
\mathbb{C}
with argument
\pm\frac{3}{4}\pi
(the sign depending on the sign of the imaginary part of
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))
) and
\beta(u)\equiv(u^{\alpha}\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}\alpha\pi}-1)^{-1}-(u^{\alpha}\hskip 0.6pt\mathrm{e}^{-\hskip 0.2pt\mathrm{i}\alpha\pi}-1)^{-1}
.

In our paper, instead of the exact calculation of the zeros
\Lambda_{j}
, we use (
3.1
) as an inspiration to formulate the following conjecture.

Conjecture 3.1
.

Let
\varepsilon
be a small positive number, and
a
be a generating function satisfying the previous properties
(i)
–
(iii)
. There exists an entire number
k\geqslant 1
such that the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))
with
j=1,\ldots,\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor
or
j=n-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+1,\ldots,n
, satisfy the expansion

\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{q_{\ell}(j)}{(n+1)^{\alpha+\ell}}+E_{k,j,n},

where

•

E_{k,j,n}
is the remainder term which satisfies the inequality
|E_{k,j,n}|\leqslant cn^{-\alpha-k}
for some constant
c
depending only on
\alpha
;

•

the coefficients
q_{\ell}

(\ell\geqslant 1)
are continuous functions from
\mathbb{T}
to
\mathbb{C}
depending only on
a
.

3.1
Algorithm

For notation simplicity, take
h=\frac{1}{n+1}
. Since Conjecture
3.1
does not include a factor of the type
\omega_{j}^{n}
or
\pi jh
, we cannot handle the grid trick previously used, hence this algorithm is essentially different from the one in Subsection
2.1
and from the algorithms employed in previous works.

Precomputing.

This phase is similar to the precomputing phase in Subsection
2.1
, but we can work with a different number of levels
k
. However, in order to avoid a heavy notation, we will use the same letter anyway. In this phase we calculate the eigenvalues of
T_{n}(a)
for
n=n_{1},\ldots,n_{k-1}
.

Extrapolation.
For some fixed index
j_{0}\leqslant n_{1}
and each
j=1,\ldots,j_{0}
or
j=n-j_{0}+1,\ldots,n
, apply
k
times the expansion in Conjecture
3.1
, reaching

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(T_{n_{1}}(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{1}(j)h_{1}^{\alpha}+q_{2}(j)h_{1}^{\alpha+1}+\cdots+q_{k}(j)h_{1}^{\alpha+k-1}+E_{k,j,n_{1}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(T_{n_{2}}(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{1}(j)h_{2}^{\alpha}+q_{2}(j)h_{2}^{\alpha+1}+\cdots+q_{k}(j)h_{2}^{\alpha+k-1}+E_{k,j,n_{2}},

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(T_{n_{k}}(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q_{1}(j)h_{k}^{\alpha}+q_{2}(j)h_{k}^{\alpha+1}+\cdots+q_{k}(j)h_{k}^{\alpha+k-1}+E_{k,j,n_{k}},

where
E_{k,j,n}=O(h^{\alpha+k})
is a remainder term satisfying the inequality
|E_{k,j,n}|\leqslant c\,h^{\alpha+k}
for some constant
c
depending only on
a
. As in the inner eigenvalue case, let
\hat{q}_{\ell}(j)
be the approximation of
q_{\ell}(j)
obtained by removing all the remainder terms
E_{k,j,n_{\ell}}
and solving the resulting linear system:

\begin{bmatrix}h_{1}^{\alpha}&h_{1}^{\alpha+1}&\cdots&h_{1}^{\alpha+k-1}\\
h_{2}^{\alpha}&h_{2}^{\alpha+1}&\cdots&h_{2}^{\alpha+k-1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
h_{k}^{\alpha}&h_{k}^{\alpha+1}&\cdots&h_{k}^{\alpha+k-1}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\hat{q}_{1}(j)\\
\hat{q}_{2}(j)\\
\vdots\\
\hat{q}_{k}(j)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{j}(T_{n_{1}}(a))\\
\lambda_{j}(T_{n_{2}}(a))\\
\vdots\\
\lambda_{j}(T_{n_{k}}(a))\end{bmatrix}.

(3.2)

Finally, our extreme eigenvalue approximation with
k
terms is given by

\lambda_{j}^{\textsc{ext}(k)}(T_{n}(a))\equiv\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\frac{\hat{q}_{\ell}(j)}{(n+1)^{\alpha+\ell}}.

(3.3)

Numerical experiments

In this section we test the accuracy of the proposed algorithms related to the formula (
2.7
) and to the formula (
3.3
), for approximating the inner and extreme eigenvalues of
T_{n}(a)
, respectively. Our numerical experiments are performed by using
Mathematica v.12 (64 bit)
on a platform with 16GB RAM, using an Intel processor QuadCore IntelCore i7 2.6 GHz. For a generating function
a
and a level
k
, consider the individual absolute errors

\textrm{AE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}\equiv|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))-\lambda_{j}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}(T_{n}(a))|,\quad\textrm{AE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{ext}(k)}\equiv|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))-\lambda_{j}^{\textsc{ext}(k)}(T_{n}(a))|.

To improve the readability of the obtained errors, we decide to define the maximum error

\textrm{AE}_{n}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}\equiv\max\{\textrm{AE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}\colon j=\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor,\ldots,n-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor\},

and the individual relative errors

\textrm{RE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{ext}(k)}\equiv\frac{\textrm{AE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{ext}(k)}}{|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))|},\qquad\textrm{RE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}\equiv\frac{\textrm{AE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{inn}(k)}}{|\lambda_{j}(T_{n}(a))|}.

Given an
\varepsilon>0
, our global individual relative error is

\textrm{RE}_{j,n}^{\varepsilon}\equiv\begin{cases}\textrm{RE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{ext}(k_{2})}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad j\in[1,\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor]\cup[n-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+1,n];\\
\textrm{RE}_{j,n}^{\textsc{inn}(k_{1})}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad j\in[\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor+1,n-\lfloor\varepsilon n\rfloor],\end{cases}

where
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are the largest available. Note that these constants are limited by the precomputing phase, and that
\varepsilon
must be selected within the range
0<\varepsilon<\frac{n_{1}}{n}
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, consider the generating function

a(t)=\frac{1}{t}(1-t)^{\alpha}.

A detailed description of this function and all the related neighborhoods appears in Figure

. Using the binomial theorem we obtain

a(t)=\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}(-1)^{j+1}\binom{\alpha}{j+1}t^{j},

then the Fourier coefficients
a_{j}
are given by
a_{j}=(-1)^{j+1}\binom{\alpha}{j+1}
for
j\geqslant-1
and
a_{j}=0
in any other case. It is also worth noticing that since the matrices

Future work

The case
m<\alpha<m+1
for

is very interesting for the applications. We will consider those cases in a future project.

It would be of interest to use such results also for the fast solution of the resulting large linear systems, so covering the preconditioned case and the setting of FDEs defined on
d
-dimensional domains with
d\geqslant 2
.
