Groups of Binary Operations and Binary G G -Spaces
Pavel S. Gevorgyan

Abstract
. The group of continuous binary operations on a topological space is studied; its relationship with the group of homeomorphisms is established. The category of binary G G -spaces and bi-equivariant maps is constructed, which is a natural extension of the category of G G -spaces and equivariant maps. Results related to the foundations of the theory of binary G G -spaces are obtained.

The Group of Continuous Binary Operations

Let
X
be any topological space.

A continuous map
f:X^{2}\to X
is called a
continuous binary operation
,
or a
binary map
, on
X
.
We denote the set of all continuous binary operations on
X
by
C_{2}(X)
.
On
C_{2}(X)
we define an operation “
\ast
” by

(1)

(f*\varphi)(t,x)=f(t,\varphi(t,x))

for all
t,x\in X
.

Proposition 1
.

The set
C_{2}(X)
under the operation “
\ast
” is a topological semigroup with identity element

e(t,x)=x
, i.e., a topological monoid.

Proof.

Let
f
,
\varphi
, and
h
be continuous binary operations on a topological space
X
.
Let us check the semigroup axiom:

[f*(\varphi*h)](t,x)=f(t,(\varphi*h)(t,x))=f(t,\varphi(t,h(t,x)))\\
=(f*\varphi)(t,h(t,x))=[(f*\varphi)*h](t,x).

The binary operation
e:X^{2}\to X
defined by
e(t,x)=x
is the identity element of the
semigroup, because
f*e=e*f=f
. Indeed,

(f*e)(t,x)=f(t,e(t,x))=f(t,x),

(e*f)(t,x)=e(t,f(t,x))=f(t,x).

∎

Definition 1
.

A continuous binary operation
f\in C_{2}(X)
is said to be
invertible
if there exists
a continuous binary operation
\varphi\in C_{2}(X)
such that

(2)

f*\varphi=\varphi*f=e.

In this case,
f
and
\varphi
are said to be
mutually inverse binary operations
.

We denote the set of all invertible elements of
C_{2}(X)
by
H_{2}(X)
. The set
H_{2}(X)
is a group.

Theorem 3
.

If a continuous binary operation
f:X^{2}\to X
is invertible, then, for any
t\in X
,
the continuous map
f_{t}:X\to X
defined by

(3)

f_{t}(x)=f(t,x)

for
x\in X
is a homeomorphism.

Proof.

Suppose that a binary operation
f:X^{2}\to X
is invertible, i.e., there exists a binary operation

\varphi\in C_{2}(X)
satisfying relations (
2
).
Take any element
t\in X
. Let us prove that the continuous map
f_{t}:X\to X
is a homeomorphism.

The map
f_{t}
is a monomorphism. Indeed, suppose that
f_{t}(x)=f_{t}(x^{\prime})
, i.e.,
f(t,x)=f(t,x^{\prime})
.
Then

x=e(t,x)=(\varphi*f)(t,x)=\varphi(t,f(t,x))=\\
=\varphi(t,f(t,x^{\prime}))=(\varphi*f)(t,x^{\prime})=e(t,x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}.

The continuous map
\varphi_{t}:X\to X
defined by

\varphi_{t}(x)=\varphi(t,x)

is inverse to
f_{t}:X\to X
. Indeed,

(f_{t}\circ\varphi_{t}(x)=f_{t}(\varphi_{t}(x))=f_{t}(\varphi(t,x))=f(t,\varphi(t,x))=\\
=(f*\varphi)(t,x)=e(t,x)=x,

i.e.,
f_{t}\circ\varphi_{t}=1_{X}
. A similar argument proves that

\varphi_{t}\circ f_{t}=1_{X}
.
∎

It follows from Theorem
3
that if an invertible binary operation
f:X^{2}\to X

is represented in the form of a family
of homeomorphisms
\{f_{t}\}
,
t\in X
, then the inverse binary operation has the form

\{f^{-1}_{t}\}
.

The converse of Theorem
3
is true for locally compact and locally connected spaces.

Theorem 4
.

Suppose that a space
X
is locally compact and locally connected and
f:X^{2}\to X

is a continuous binary operation. If the map
f_{t}:X\to X
is a homeomorphism
for each
t\in X
, then the binary operation
f
is invertible, and the inverse binary
operation
f^{-1}
is defined by
f^{-1}(t,x)=f_{t}^{-1}(x)
.

Proof.

Consider the binary operation
\varphi
given by
\varphi(t,x)=f_{t}^{-1}(x)
. Let us prove that
this binary operation is continuous and inverse to
f:X^{2}\to X
:
\varphi=f^{-1}
.

First, we prove the continuity of the map
\varphi:X^{2}\to X
. Let
(t_{0},x_{0})\in X^{2}
be
any point, and let
\varphi(t_{0},x_{0})=f_{t_{0}}^{-1}(x_{0})=y_{0}
. Consider any open neighborhood

W\subset X
of
y_{0}
such that the closure
\overline{W}
is compact. There exists a
compact connected neighborhood
K
of
x_{0}
for which

(4)

f_{t_{0}}^{-1}(K)\subset W.

We denote the interior of
K
by
K^{\circ}
. We have

(5)

f_{t_{0}}(y_{0})=x_{0}\in K^{\circ}.

Inclusion (
4
) implies

(6)

f_{t_{0}}(W^{C}\cap\overline{W})\subset K^{C},

where
W^{C}
and
K^{C}
are the complements of
W
and
K
, respectively.

Since
f:X^{2}\to X
is a continuous binary operation,
\{y_{0}\}

and
W^{C}\cap\overline{W}
are compact supsets of
X
, and
K^{\circ}
and
K^{C}
are open subsets,
it follows from (
5
) and (
6
) that the point
t_{0}
has an open neighborhood
U

such that, for any
t\in U
, we have

(7)

f_{t}(y_{0})\in K^{\circ}

and

(8)

f_{t}(W^{C}\cap\overline{W})\subset K^{C}.

Inclusion (
8
) implies that

K\subset f_{t}(W\cup\overline{W}^{C})

for any
t\in U
. Therefore,

f_{t}^{-1}(K)\subset W\cup\overline{W}^{C}.

Since
f_{t}^{-1}(K)
is connected and
W
and
\overline{W}^{C}
are disjoint open sets,
it follows from the last inclusion that
f_{t}^{-1}(K)
is contained in one of the sets

W
and
\overline{W}^{C}
. However, by virtue of (
7
), we obviously have

f_{t}^{-1}(K)\subset W
. Hence

(9)

f_{t}^{-1}(K^{\circ})\subset W

for all
t\in U
.

Thus, given any open neighborhood
W
of
y_{0}=f_{t_{0}}^{-1}(x_{0})
, we have found open
neighborhoods
U
of
t_{0}
and
K^{\circ}
of
x_{0}
for which (
9
) holds.
This proves the continuity of the binary operation
\varphi(t,x)=f_{t}^{-1}(x)
.

It is easy to show that the continuous binary operation
\varphi(t,x)=f_{t}^{-1}(x)

is inverse to
f:X^{2}\to X
. Indeed,

(f*\varphi)(t,x)=f(t,\varphi(t,x))=f(t,f_{t}^{-1}(x))=f_{t}(f_{t}^{-1}(x))=x,

i.e.
f*\varphi=e
. The relation
\varphi*f=e
is proved in a similar way.
∎

Theorems
3
and
4
imply the following criterion for the invertibility
of continuous binary operations on locally compact and locally connected spaces.

Theorem 5
.

A continuous binary operation
f:X^{2}\to X

on a locally compact locally connected space
X
is
invertible if and only if
the continuous map
f_{t}:X\to X
defined by (
3
) is a homeomorphism for any
t\in X
.

Proposition 2
.

The group
H(X)
of all self-homeomorphisms of a topological space
X
is isomorphic
(algebraically and topologically) to a subgroup of the group
H_{2}(X)
of invertible binary
operations.

Proof.

To each
f\in H(X)
we assign the continuous map
\tilde{f}:X^{2}\to X
defined by

\tilde{f}(t,x)=f(x)
,
t,x\in X
. Obviously,
\widetilde{f^{-1}}=\tilde{f}^{-1}
. Thus,

\tilde{f}
is a continuous invertible binary operation, i.e.,
\tilde{f}\in H_{2}(X)
.
The correspondence
f\to\tilde{f}
is the required isomorphism between the group
H(X)

and a subgroup of
H_{2}(X)
.
∎

Theorem 6
.

For any locally compact locally connected space
X
, the group
H_{2}(X)
is
isomorphic (algebraically and topologically) to
C(X,H(X))
.

Proof.

Consider the map
p:C(X,H(X))\to H_{2}(X)
defined by

(10)

p(f)(t,x)=f(t)(x)

for
f\in C(X,H(X))
and
t,x\in X
. The map
f(t):X\to X
is a homeomorphism
for each
t\in X
. Therefore, by virtue of Theorem
5
, the binary operation

p(f):X\times X\to X
is invertible, that is, indeed belongs to the group
H_{2}(X)
.

Let us prove that
p
is a monomorphism. Take
f,g\in C(X,H(X))
,
f\neq g
. There exists
a point
t_{0}\in X
such that
f(t_{0})\neq g(t_{0})
. Since
f(t_{0}),g(t_{0})\in H(X)
,
it follows that
f(t_{0})(x_{0})\neq g(t_{0})(x_{0})
for some
x_{0}\in X
. Thus,

p(f)(t_{0},x_{0})\neq p(g)(t_{0},x_{0})
, and hence
p(f)\neq p(g)
.

The map
p
is also an epimorphism. Indeed, let
\varphi\in H_{2}(X)
be any continuous binary
operation. By virtue of Theorem
5
, the map
\varphi_{t}:X\to X
defined by

\varphi_{t}(x)=\varphi(t,x)
,
t,x\in X
, is a homeomorphism. It is easy to see
that the element
f\in C(X,H(X))
determined by the condition
f(t)=\varphi_{t}
is the preimage
of the binary operation
\varphi
. Indeed, we have
p(f)(t,x)=f(t)(x)=\varphi_{t}(x)=\varphi(t,x)
.

Thus, the map
p^{-1}:H_{2}(X)\to C(X,H(X))
defined by

p^{-1}(\varphi)(t)(x)=\varphi(t,x)

for
\varphi\in H_{2}(X)
and
t,x\in X
is inverse to
p:C(X,H(X))\to H_{2}(X)
.

The map
p
is a homomorphism, that is,
p(f\circ g)=p(f)*p(g)
. Indeed,
for any
t,x\in X
, we have

p(f\circ g)(t,x)=(f\circ g)(t)(x)=(f(t)\circ g(t))(x)=f(t)(g(t)(x))\\
=f(t)(p(g)(t,x))=p(f)(t,p(g)(t,x))=(p(f)*p(g))(t,x).

Let us prove the continuity of
p
. Take any element
W(K\times K^{\prime},U)
of the subbase
of the compact-open topology on
H_{2}(X)
, where
U\subset X
open and
K,K^{\prime}\subset X
are compact
subsets of
X
. Let us show that the preimage of
W(K\times K^{\prime},U)
is the set
W(K,W(K^{\prime},U))
,
which is an element of the subbase of the compact-open topology on
C(X,H(X))
.
Indeed, for any
\varphi\in W(K\times K^{\prime},U)
and
f=p^{-1}(\varphi)\in C(X,H(X))
, we have

\varphi\in W(K\times K^{\prime},U)\iff\varphi(t,x)\in U\iff p(f)(t,x)\in U\\
\iff f(t)(x)\in U\iff f\in W(K,W(K^{\prime},U)),

where
t\in K
and
x\in K^{\prime}
are arbitrary elements.

The continuity of the inverse map
p^{-1}:H_{2}(X)\to C(X,H(X))
is proved in precisely the same way.
∎

Corollary 1
.

If
X
is a locally compact locally connected space, then
H_{2}(X)
is a topological group.

Proof.

By Theorem
2
,
H(X)
is a topological group. Thus,
C(X,H(X))
is
a topological group as well (by Theorem
1
). According to Theorem
6
,

H_{2}(X)
is a topological group.
∎

Corollary 2
.

If
|X|=n<\infty
, then
|H_{2}(X)|=(n!)^{n}
.

Proof.

For a finite set
X
, we have
H(X)=S_{n}(X)
, where
S_{n}(X)
is the symmetric group of
permutations of
X
. Since
|S_{n}(X)|=n!
, the corollary follows directly from Theorem
6
.
∎

3.
Binary Actions of Groups

Definition 2
.

Let
G
be a topological group, and let
X
be a space.
A continuous map
\alpha:G\times X^{2}\to X
is called a
binary action

of
G
on
X
if the following conditions hold:

(11)

\alpha(gh,t,x)=\alpha(g,t,\alpha(h,t,x)),

(12)

\alpha(e,t,x)=x,

where
e
is the identity element of
G
,
g,h\in G
, and
t,x\in X
.

We refer to a space
X
together with a fixed binary action of a group
G
, that is, to a triple

(G,X,\alpha)
, as a
binary
G
-space
.

Note that in the notation

(13)

g(t,x)=\alpha(g,t,x)

relations (
11
) and (
12
) take the form

gh(t,x)=g(t,h(t,x)),\quad e(t,x)=x.

Example 2
.

The continuous map
\alpha:G\times G^{2}\to G
defined by

(14)

\alpha(g,h_{1},h_{2})=h_{1}gh_{1}^{-1}h_{2},\quad\text{or}\quad g(h_{1},h_{2})=h_{1}gh_{1}^{-1}h_{2},

is a binary action of the topological group
G
on itself.

Indeed, conditions (
11
) and (
12
) in Definition
2
are satisfied: we have

\alpha(gh,h_{1},h_{2})=h_{1}ghh_{1}^{-1}h_{2}=h_{1}gh_{1}^{-1}h_{1}hh_{1}^{-1}h_{2}=\\
=\alpha(g,h_{1},h_{1}hh_{1}^{-1}h_{2})=\alpha(g,h_{1},\alpha(h,h_{1},h_{2}))

and

\alpha(e,h_{1},h_{2})=h_{1}eh_{1}^{-1}h_{2}=h_{2}

for all
g,h,h_{1},h_{2}\in G
.

Example 3
.

Let
GL(n,\mathbf{R})
be the general linear group of degree
n
. Consider the continuous map

\alpha:GL(n,\mathbf{R})\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\times\mathbf{R}^{n}\to\mathbf{R}^{n}
defined by

(15)

\alpha(A,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(E-A)\mathbf{x}+A\mathbf{y},

or, equivalently,

(16)

A(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(E-A)\mathbf{x}+A\mathbf{y},

where
A\in GL(n,\mathbf{R})
,
E
is the identity matrix,
and
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Note that, for any
A,B\in GL(n,\mathbf{R})
, we have

A(\mathbf{x},B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))=(E-A)\mathbf{x}+A(B(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))=(E-A)\mathbf{x}+A((E-B)\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y})\\
=(E-A)\mathbf{x}+A(E-B)\mathbf{x}+AB\mathbf{y}=(E-A+A-AB)\mathbf{x}+AB\mathbf{y}\\
=(E-AB)\mathbf{x}+AB\mathbf{y}=AB(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

and

E(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(E-E)\mathbf{x}+E\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}.

Therefore, relation (
16
) defines a binary action of the general linear group

GL(n,\mathbf{R})
on
the
n
-dimensional vector space
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Let
\alpha
be a binary action of a topological group
G
on a space
X
.
For each
g\in G
, we define a continuous map
\alpha_{g}:X^{2}\to X
as

(17)

\alpha_{g}(t,x)=\alpha(g,t,x).

Considitions (
11
) and (
12
) imply that

\alpha_{gh}=\alpha_{g}*\alpha_{h}
and
\alpha_{e}
is the identity element in the monoid of
binary operations on
X
.
Thus, the following proposition is valid.

Proposition 3
.

The map
g\to\alpha_{g}
is a continuous homomorphism from
G
to the group
H_{2}(X)
of all invertible
continuous binary operations.

Thus, the elements of the group
G
can be treated as continuous binary operations on the
space
X
which act by the rule
g(t,x)=\alpha_{g}(t,x)=\alpha(g,t,x)
.

Take any
t\in X
. Consider the continuous map
\alpha_{t}:G\times X\to X

defined by

(18)

\alpha_{t}(g,x)=\alpha(g,t,x).

Proposition 4
.

The map
\alpha_{t}
is an action of the group
G
on the space
X
.

Proof.

The map
\alpha_{t}
satisfies the conditions in the definition of an action
of a group on a topological space. Indeed, we have

(1)
\alpha_{t}(gh,x)=\alpha(gh,t,x)=\alpha(g,t,\alpha(h,t,x))=\alpha(g,t,\alpha_{t}(h,x))=\alpha_{t}(g,\alpha_{t}(h,x))
and

(2)
\alpha_{t}(e,x)=\alpha(e,t,x)=x

for all
g,h\in G
and
x\in X
.
∎

Thus,
a binary action
\alpha
of a group
G
on a space
X
induces the family

\{\alpha_{t}\}
,
t\in X
, of
“ordinary” actions of
G
on
X
.

In the case of the binary action (
14
) (see Example
2
), all induced
actions of
G
on itself are equivalent. To be more precise,
the homeomorphism
f:G\to G
given by
f(g)=h^{-1}gh
, where
h
is any fixed element of
G
,
is an equivalence with respect to the actions
\alpha_{e}
and
\alpha_{h}
. Indeed,

f(\alpha_{e}(g,\tilde{g}))=f(e^{-1}ge\tilde{g})=f(g\tilde{g})=h^{-1}g\tilde{g}=\\
=h^{-1}ghh^{-1}\tilde{g}h=h^{-1}ghf(\tilde{g})=\alpha_{h}(g,f(\tilde{g})).

The binary action of the general linear group
GL(n,\mathbf{R})
on

\mathbf{R}^{n}
defined by (
15
) (see Example
3
)
induces the family
\{\alpha_{\textbf{a}};\textbf{a}\in\mathbf{R}^{n}\}
of ordinary actions
of the group
GL(n,\mathbf{R})
on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
It is easy to show that
all these actions are equivalent. To be more precise,
the map
f(\textbf{x})=\textbf{x}-\textbf{a}
is an equivariant self-homeomorphism
of the space
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with respect to the actions
\alpha_{\textbf{a}}

and
\alpha_{\textbf{0}}
. Moreover, the action
\alpha_{\textbf{0}}
is
multiplication of a given matrix by a given element of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
:

\alpha_{\textbf{0}}(A,\textbf{y})=\alpha(A,\textbf{0},\textbf{y})=\textbf{0}+A\textbf{y}=A\textbf{y}
.

Given
A\subset X
and
g\in G
, we set
gA=\{g(a,a^{\prime});a,a^{\prime}\in A\}
. Similarly,
given
K\subset G
, we set

KA=\{g(a,a^{\prime});g\in K,a,a^{\prime}\in A\}
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
A
be a subset of a binary
G
-space
X
, and let
K
be a subset of a
compact topological group
G
. Then the following assertions hold:

(i) if
A
is open, then so is
KA
;

(ii) if
A
is compact and
K
is closed, then
KA
is compact.

Proof.

(i) Note that
KA=\bigcup\limits_{g\in K}\bigcup\limits_{a\in A}g_{a}(A)
, where
g_{a}:X\to X

is the map defined by
g_{a}(x)=g(a,x)
,
x\in X
. By virtue of Theorem
3
,

g_{a}:X\to X
is a homeomorphism; therefore, the set
g_{a}(A)
is open. Hence
KA
is open as well.

(ii) If

Thus, the set
K\times A^{2}
is compact, and so is its continuous image
KA=\alpha(K\times A^{2})
.
∎

Corollary 3
.

Suppose that
G

.

4.
The Category of Binary
G
-Spaces

Let
(G,X,\alpha)
and
(G,Y,\beta)
be two binary
G
-spaces.

Definition 3
.

We say that a continuous map
f:X\to Y
is
bi-equivariant
if

f(\alpha(g,t,x))=\beta(g,f(t),f(x)),

or, equivalently,

f(g(t,x))=g(f(t),f(x)),

for all
g\in G
and
t,x\in X
.

We refer to a bi-equivariant map
f:X\to Y
which is simultaneously a homeomorphism as
a
bi-equivalence
of binary
G
-spaces.
Note that the inverse map
f^{-1}:Y\to X
is bi-equivariant as well. Indeed, we have

f^{-1}(g(y,y^{\prime}))=f^{-1}(g(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))=f^{-1}(f(g(x,x^{\prime})))=\\
=g(x,x^{\prime})=g(f^{-1}(y),f^{-1}(y^{\prime})),

where
x,x^{\prime}\in X
and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
.

The following assertion is valid.

Theorem 8
.

The binary
G
-spaces and bi-equivariant maps form a category.

We denote the category of binary
G
-spaces and bi-equivariant maps by
G
-
Top^{2}
.

Proposition 5
.

Any bi-equivariant map
f:X\to Y
is equivariant with respect to the induced actions
\alpha_{t}

and
\beta_{f(t)}
for any
t\in X
.

Proof.

Indeed,

f(\alpha_{t}(g,x))=f(\alpha(g,t,x))=\beta(g,f(t),f(x))=\beta_{f(t)}(g,f(x)).

∎

Now, let
X
be any
G
-space. On
X
we define a binary
G
-action by

(19)

g(x,x^{\prime})=g\cdot x^{\prime}

for all
g\in G
and
x,x^{\prime}\in X
.

Note that if
X
and
Y
are
G
-spaces, then any equivariant map
f:X\to Y

is bi-equivariant with respect to the action (
19
). Indeed,

f(g(x,x^{\prime})=f(g\cdot x^{\prime})=g\cdot f(x^{\prime})=g(f(x),f(x^{\prime})).

Thus, the category
G
-
Top
is a subcategory of the category
G
-
Top^{2}
.
We have the following chain of natural extensions of categories:

Top\subset G\text{-}Top\subset G\text{-}Top^{2}.

Let
(G,X,\alpha)
be a binary
G
-space. Consider the
G
-space

(G,X\times X,\tilde{\alpha})
on which the group
G
acts as

\tilde{\alpha}(g,x,x^{\prime})=(x,\alpha(g,x,x^{\prime})).

Using the notations
\tilde{\alpha}(g,x,x^{\prime})=g\cdot(x,x^{\prime})
and
\alpha(g,x,x^{\prime})=g(x,x^{\prime})
, we
rewrite this formula as

g\cdot(x,x^{\prime})=(x,g(x,x^{\prime})).

Note that if
X
and
Y
are binary
G
-spaces, then any bi-equivariant map
f:X\to Y

generates the equivariant map
\tilde{f}:X\times X\to Y\times Y
defined by

\tilde{f}(x,x^{\prime})=(f(x),f(x^{\prime})),

where
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. Indeed,

\tilde{f}(g\cdot(x,x^{\prime}))=\tilde{f}(x,g(x,x^{\prime}))=(f(x),f(g(x,x^{\prime})))\\
=(f(x),g(f(x),f(x^{\prime})))=g\cdot(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))=g\cdot\tilde{f}(x,x^{\prime}).

This correspondence is a covariant functor from the category
G
-
TOP^{2}

to the category
G
-
TOP
.

5.
Invariant Sets

Definition 4
.

We say that a subset
A\subset X
is
invariant
with respect to the binary action of a
group
G
if
GA=A
.

It is easy to see that the intersection
A\cap B
of invariant sets
A,B\subset X

is invariant.
However, the union
A\cup B
of two invariant sets is not generally invariant.
Indeed, any one-point set
\{x\}
,
x\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, is invariant with respect to
the binary action (
15
) of the general linear group
GL(n,\mathbf{R})
on
the
n
-dimensional vector space
\mathbf{R}^{n}
(see Example
3
), since

A(x,x)=(E-A)x+Ax=x
for all
A\in GL(n,\mathbf{R})
. However, the union

\{x\}\cup\{y\}
of two one-point sets is not invariant, because

GL(n,\mathbf{R})\{x,y\}=\mathbf{R}^{n}
.

Question 1
.

What are binary
G
-spaces in which the union
A\cup B
of any invariant subsets
A,B\subset X

is invariant?

Definition 5
.

The orbit of an element
x\in X
of a binary
G
-space
X
is defined as the
minimal invariant set
[x]\subset X
containing
x
.

Obviously,
x\in G(x,x)\subset[x]
for all
x\in X
. Therefore, if
G(x,x)
is
invariant, then
G(x,x)=[x]
. The following problem naturally arises.

Question 2
.

In what binary
G
-spaces is the set
G(x,x)
invariant?

The solution of this problem in a special case is given by Theorem
9
below.

Definition 6
.

A binary
G
-space
X
is said to be
distributive
if

(20)

g(h(x,x^{\prime}),h(x,x^{\prime\prime}))=h(x,g(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}))

for all
x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in X
and
g,h\in G
.

Example 4
.

A group
G
with binary action (
14
) (see Example
2
) is a distributive binary

G
-space. Indeed, for any
g,h,k,k_{1},k_{2}\in G
, we have

g(h(k,k_{1}),h(k,k_{2}))=g(khk^{-1}k_{1},khk^{-1}k_{2})=\\
=khk^{-1}k_{1}gk_{1}^{-1}kh^{-1}k^{-1}khk^{-1}k_{2}=khk^{-1}k_{1}gk_{1}^{-1}k_{2}=\\
=h(k,k_{1}gk_{1}^{-1}k_{2})=h(k,g(k_{1},k_{2})).

Theorem 9
.

If
X
is a distributive binary
G
-space, then the set
G(x,x)
is invariant for any

.

Proof.

We must show that
g(h(x,x),k(x,x))\in G(x,x)

for any
h(x,x)
,

and any

. By virtue of (

g(h(x,x),k(x,x))=g(h(x,x),h(x,h^{-1}k(x,x)))=\\
=h(x,g(x,h^{-1}k(x,x)))=h(x,gh^{-1}k(x,x))=\\
=hgh^{-1}k(x,x)\in G(x,x),

because
hgh^{-1}k\in G
.
∎
