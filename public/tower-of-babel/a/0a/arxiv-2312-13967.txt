Asynchronous Authentication
Marwa Mouallem, Ittay Eyal

Abstract
A myriad of authentication mechanisms embody a continuous evolution from verbal passwords in ancient times to contemporary multi-factor authentication. Nevertheless, digital asset heists and numerous identity theft cases illustrate the urgent need to revisit the fundamentals of user authentication. We abstract away credential details and formalize the general, common case of asynchronous authentication , with unbounded message propagation time. Our model, which might be of independent interest, allows for eventual message delivery, while bounding execution time to maintain cryptographic guarantees. Given credentials’ fault probabilities (e.g., loss or leak), we seek mechanisms with the highest success probability. We show that every mechanism is dominated by some Boolean mechanism —defined by a monotonic Boolean function on presented credentials. We present an algorithm for finding approximately optimal mechanisms. Previous work analyzed Boolean mechanisms specifically, but used brute force, which quickly becomes prohibitively complex. We leverage the problem structure to reduce complexity by orders of magnitude. The algorithm is readily applicable to practical settings. For example, we revisit the common approach in cryptocurrency wallets that use a handful of high-quality credentials. We show that adding low-quality credentials improves security by orders of magnitude.

Previous work (§

These encompass numerous

.
But Zimmermann et al.
[

However, these works leave open the question of how to model and compare mechanisms’ security.
Eyal

analyzes a specific type of mechanisms, defined by monotonic Boolean functions on presented credentials for a small number of credentials.
Maram et al.
[
16
]
study the authentication problem but assume a bounded-delay communication channel between the mechanism and the user.

In this work, we study general authentication mechanisms in the common asynchronous setting.
First, we define the
asynchronous authentication problem
(§
III
),
where an authentication mechanism interacts with a
user
and an
attacker
(representing all adversarial entities
[
15
,
16
]
) and tries to identify the user.
The mechanism utilizes credentials to identify the user.
Each credential can be known to the user, the attacker, both, or neither.
The states of all credentials define a
scenario
.
A mechanism is
successful
in a scenario if it correctly identifies the user, for all attacker behaviors and message delivery times, except with negligible probability.
The
profile
of a mechanism is the set of all scenarios in which it is successful.

We seek to reason about a mechanism’s success when message delivery time is unbounded, yet cryptographic assumptions bound running time according to a security parameter.
Therefore, we use
mechanism families
—sets of mechanisms that work similarly for different security parameters.
Roughly, we require success when the security parameter is large enough.
This approach may be of independent interest for analyzing distributed protocols that rely on cryptography in asynchronous networks.

To design a mechanism, one must estimate the probabilities of credential faults, e.g., loss or leak.
An optimal mechanism has the maximum probability of success given those estimates.
To find optimal mechanisms we turn our attention to
Boolean mechanisms
: mechanisms that decide according to a Boolean function of the credentials presented to them.
For any authentication mechanism, there exists a Boolean mechanism that dominates it—one that succeeds in the same scenarios or more (§
IV-A
).
The proof uses a series of reductions.
First, interactive message exchanges do not enhance mechanism security—every interactive mechanism is dominated by a non-interactive mechanism.
Second, decisions can be made solely on credential availability proofs.
Third, randomness does not improve the mechanism’s security.
The proofs are constructive and rely on a mechanism’s ability to simulate an execution of another. Finally, for any deterministic mechanism, there exists a dominating mechanism defined by a monotonic Boolean function of the credentials’ availability—a
monotonic Boolean mechanism
.

Let us thus focus on such mechanisms.
Any monotonic Boolean mechanism defined by a non-trivial function is not dominated (maximal), and any maximal mechanism is equivalent (same profile) to a monotonic Boolean mechanism (§
IV-B
).
This result shows that studying such mechanisms suffices in the asynchronous setting and reveals a somewhat surprising fact:
There is no probability-agnostic hierarchy of Boolean mechanisms in the asynchronous setting unlike under synchrony
[
16
]
.
For example, with two credentials
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, consider the mechanism where both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are required to authenticate and the mechanism where only
c_{1}
is required.
One can easily verify that neither mechanism dominates the other.
In fact, this is true in general, for any two non-trivial
n
-credential Boolean mechanisms.

Since there is no hierarchy of Boolean mechanisms, in general, any such mechanism could be optimal.
But evaluating all possible mechanisms, as done by Eyal
[
15
]
, quickly becomes prohibitively complex, as their number grows super-exponentially with the number of credentials
[
17
]
.

Instead, we present a scenario-based algorithm to find approximately optimal mechanisms (§
V
). Given
\delta>0
, the algorithm finds a
\delta
-optimal mechanism
, i.e., a mechanism whose success probability is at most
\delta
lower than that of an optimal mechanism.
The algorithm works greedily by building a mechanism that succeeds in scenarios with the highest probability.
Once no more scenarios can be added, it removes existing scenarios to add new ones.
An analysis of different probability distributions shows that it is sufficient to focus on a small number of high-probability scenarios.
Rather than searching all mechanisms, the algorithm stops when the scenarios that might be added have additional probability lower than
\delta
.
This is calculated by bounding the number of scenarios in which a mechanism can succeed.

Our algorithm allows designing approximately optimal mechanisms with many credentials. This is directly useful for mechanism design.
For example, when designing a cryptocurrency wallet, users tend to use a handful of high-quality credentials
[
18
,
19
]
, like hardware keys and long memorized mnemonics.
However, we show that adding a few easy-to-lose credentials (e.g., passwords that the user remembers but does not back up) reduces wallet failure probability by orders of magnitude.
Similarly, incorporating easy-to-leak credentials (e.g., the name of the user’s pet) with high-quality credentials achieves a similar effect. (Though note this is not the typical use of such credentials.)

In summary (§
VI
), our contributions are:
(1) formalization of the asynchronous authentication problem;
(2) proof that all maximal mechanisms can be defined by a monotonic Boolean function;
(3) proof that any two non-trivial monotonic Boolean mechanisms are either equivalent or neither is better,
(4) an approximation algorithm of optimal mechanisms given the credentials fault probabilities, and
(5) concrete lessons for the design of cryptocurrency wallets and online service authentication.

Eyal
[
15

analyzes mechanisms that are Boolean functions of credential availability.

Although our analysis uses methods similar to those used in distributed systems’ theory like asynchronous communication and simulations, the authentication problem is distinct from classical problems like consensus or broadcast

Success is defined by the decision of a single party and not multiple ones, and credentials take a central role.

III
Model

We formalize the asynchronous authentication problem (§
III-A
), specifying an execution, its participants, and their behavior.
Then we define mechanism success and the relation between different mechanisms (§
III-B
).

III-A

Asynchronous Authentication

The system comprises three entities, an
authentication mechanism

M
and two
players
: a
user

U
and an
attacker

A
.
All three are finite deterministic automata that can draw randomness from a
random tape

v
, an infinite stream of random bits.
We specify the automata as computer programs
[
42
]
.

An execution is orchestrated by a
scheduler
,
whose pseudocode is given in Appendix
A
.
Each of the players interacts with the mechanism by sending and receiving messages.
The scheduler, parametrized by
\gamma_{\textit{ID}}\in\{0,1\}
, assigns the user identifier
\gamma_{\textit{ID}}
and the attacker identifier
{(1-\gamma_{\textit{ID}})}
.
E.g., if
\gamma_{\textit{ID}}=0
, then the user is player
0
and the attacker is player
1
.
The identifiers serve a similar purpose to that of cookies that identify a website visitor during a single session.
They allow the mechanism to identify and distinguish between the players during an execution without revealing which is the user.

To facilitate authentication, the players take advantage of private information to convince the mechanism that they are the user.
This information is a set of credentials that might be data (e.g., passwords), biometric properties (e.g., fingerprints), and physical objects (e.g., phones or smart cards).
Each credential has two parts: a public part, known to the mechanism, and a secret part.
For example, when using a password to authenticate to a website, the user remembers the password (the secret part) and the website stores its hash (the public part);
or when using public key authentication, a blockchain has the public key (the public part) and the user has the private key (the secret part).

(Credential)
.

A

c
is a tuple
{c=(c^{P},c^{S})}
where
c^{P}
is the
public part
of the credential and
c^{S}
is the
secret part
of the credential.

The system uses a set of
n

credential
s,
\{c_{1},...,c_{n}\}
.
A
credential
can be
available
to a player. An
availability vector
represents the availability of all credentials to a player.
Each entry
i
in the vector indicates the availability of credential
i
.
Denote by
\sigma_{U}
,
\sigma_{A}
the availability vector of the user and the attacker respectively.
A scenario
\sigma
is a tuple of availability vectors
(\sigma_{U},\sigma_{A})
.
Denote by
C_{U}^{\sigma}
,
C_{A}^{\sigma}
the set of
credential
s available to the user and attacker in scenario

respectively.

The system uses a credential generation function
\textit{gen}^{\lambda}({n})
, parametrized by a security parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
The function generates a set of
n
credentials
\{(c_{i}^{P},c_{i}^{S})\}_{i=1}^{n}

Let

be a polynomial function in the security parameter

that maps it to the maximum number of steps that an attacker is allowed to perform.
A credential generation function is

if the attacker’s probability of guessing information about the credentials correctly in

steps is negligibly better than a random guess.
That is, if the security parameter is large enough, the attacker cannot guess the secrets better than a random guess in a polynomial number of steps
[
43
]
.

An

exchanges messages with the players to decide which is the user.

Definition 2

(Authentication mechanism)
.

An

M^{\lambda}
is a finite deterministic automaton, parametrized by the security parameter
\lambda
and specified by two functions,
\textit{gen}_{M^{\lambda}}({\cdot})
and
\textit{step}_{M^{\lambda}}({\cdot})

•

\textit{gen}_{M^{\lambda}}({n})
, where

•

\textit{step}_{M^{\lambda}}({\textit{msg},i})
, where

), sent from player

(\textit{msg}s_{0},\textit{msg}s_{1})
of sets of messages (maybe empty) to send to the players by their identifiers.
It can access and update the mechanism’s state and the set of credentials public part
\{c_{i}^{P}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

The mechanism uses a variable
\textit{decide}_{M^{\lambda}}
that marks which identifier it decides belongs to the user.
The variable’s initial value is

.
When

A

(Strategy)
.

A

S^{\lambda}
of a player is a function, parametrized by the security parameter

We now describe the execution of the system.
Full details including pseudocode are in Appendix

The execution consists of two parts:

(
Setup

(
Execute

The setup generates a set of

\textit{gen}_{M^{\lambda}}({n})

Then it assigns an identifier to each player based on
\gamma_{\textit{ID}}\in\{0,1\}

Whenever a random coin is used by

or the players, the result is the next bit of

Once the setup is complete, the main loop begins and both players

can send messages to the mechanism, each based on her strategy

and set of credentials

Time progresses in discrete steps.
The communication network is reliable but asynchronous.
Messages arrive eventually, but there is no bound on the time after they were sent and no constraints on the arrival order.

This is implemented as follows.
The scheduler maintains three sets of pending messages, one for each entity.
The scheduler is parametrized by an ordering function
\gamma_{\textit{ord}}
and a random tape
v_{\gamma}
.
In each step, it uses
\gamma_{\textit{ord}}
to choose a subset of messages (maybe empty) from each of the pending message sets, and returns them as an ordered list.
The scheduler’s random coins used in
\gamma_{\textit{ord}}
are taken from
v_{\gamma}
.
The scheduler removes the chosen messages from the pending messages sets.

Each player receives the messages chosen by the ordering function
\gamma_{\textit{ord}}
and sends messages to the mechanism according to her strategy. These messages are added to the mechanism’s pending messages set.
Similarly, the mechanism receives the messages chosen by
\gamma_{\textit{ord}}
.
After every message

it receives from player with identifier

, the mechanism runs its function
\textit{step}_{M^{\lambda}}({\textit{msg},i})
and sends messages to the players.

In each step, the scheduler checks if
\textit{decide}_{M^{\lambda}}\neq\bot

Once it does, the execution ends and the player with the matching index (either
0
or
1
) wins.
The tuple
(\gamma_{\textit{ID}},\gamma_{\textit{ord}},v_{\gamma})
thus defines the
scheduler
’s behavior.
And an execution
E^{\lambda}
is thus defined by its parameter tuple
(M^{\lambda},\sigma,S_{U}^{\lambda},S_{A}^{\lambda},\gamma,v)
; by slight abuse of notation we write
{E^{\lambda}=(M^{\lambda},\sigma,S_{U}^{\lambda},S_{A}^{\lambda},\gamma,v)}
.

The number of operations that the mechanism and the players can perform in a single execution step is polynomial in the security parameter
\lambda
.
The number of steps in an execution is thus the minimum between the number of steps the mechanism takes to decide and
T(\lambda)
.
This ensures the guarantees of the credentials are maintained.
We assume that the attacker cannot affect the delivery time of user messages, in particular, she cannot perform a denial of service attack (details in Appendix
A-A
).

We define the
winner
of an execution as the player with the identifier that the mechanism decides on in that execution.
If the mechanism does not reach a decision within the execution’s runtime, we say that the execution
times out
.

III-B

Mechanism Success

In an asynchronous environment we cannot guarantee that the mechanism correctly identifies the user in any scenario within any bounded time due to the simple fact that the scheduler can delay messages indefinitely.
Thus, if we bound execution time we cannot require termination and must allow the mechanism to timeout even in scenarios where it supposedly identifies the user.
This allows for a trivial solution where the mechanism always times out.

However, to maintain cryptographic security, execution time must be bounded, in our case by the polynomial
T(\lambda)
.
If we allow the execution to run longer we can no longer bound the attacker’s probability of guessing credentials beyond those given to her by the scheduler.

Previous work
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
sidesteps this issue with protocols that use an abstract signature scheme that cannot be broken indefinitely.
However, in our case this abstraction is not possible as we make claims on general mechanisms using standard communication channels, so for any bounded message length we cannot rule out forgery.

We therefore define success as follows.
If the mechanism times out in a given execution, we would like to extend the execution and allow it to succeed in the extended execution.
To extend the execution without violating cryptographic security, we must increase the security parameter.
Intuitively, we are interested in mechanisms that behave similarly for all security parameters, e.g., verify a signature and send a message independent of the signature details.
But a different security parameter implies changes to the mechanism and the behavior of the players and the scheduler, which might extend the decision time further, beyond the new bound.
Therefore, to keep the extension meaningful, the scheduler must also behave in a similar way for different security parameters, that is, it is oblivious to the security parameter and message content.
The requirement is that given such a scheduler, for all sufficiently large security parameters, the mechanism succeeds.

Rather than mechanisms, success is therefore defined for mechanism families
that behaves similarly for different security parameters.
A
mechanism family

{M}
is a function that maps a security parameter
\lambda
to a mechanism
M^{\lambda}
.
Similarly, a
strategy family

S
is a function that maps a security parameter
\lambda
to a strategy
S^{\lambda}
.
A mechanism family is
successful
in a scenario
\sigma
if, for a large enough security parameter, the user wins against all attacker strategies and schedulers.
Formally,

Definition 4

(Mechanism success)
.

A mechanism family
{M}
is
successful
in a scenario
\sigma
, if there exists a user strategy family
S_{U}

such that for all attacker strategy families
S_{A}
, schedulers
\gamma
, and random tapes
v
, there exists a security parameter
\lambda_{0}
such that:

1.

for all security parameters
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
, the user wins the execution
E^{\lambda}=(M^{\lambda},\sigma,S_{U}^{\lambda},S_{A}^{\lambda},\gamma,v)
. And,

2.

for all security parameters
\lambda
, either the user wins or the execution times out.

Such a user strategy family
S_{U}
is a
winning user strategy family
in
\sigma
with
M
.
Otherwise, the mechanism family

The set of scenarios in which the mechanism succeeds is its

defined for a synchronous network).

A

The

A mechanism family

IV
Maximal Mechanisms

Having defined partial ordering on mechanisms, we proceed to identify
maximal mechanisms
, i.e., mechanisms that are not strictly dominated.
We show that for any mechanism family there exists a dominating mechanism family that is a Boolean function of the credentials’ availability (§
IV-A
).
Then we show that mechanism families defined by monotonic Boolean functions are maximal, and all maximal mechanisms are equivalent to a Boolean mechanism (§
IV-B
).

IV-A

Domination by Boolean Mechanisms

We show that any mechanism family, is dominated by a mechanism family that is a Boolean function of the credentials’ availability.
We take 4 similar steps.
First, we show that for all mechanism families there exists a dominating
one-shot
mechanism family in which mechanisms decide based on up to a single message from each player (§
IV-A2
).
Second, we show that decisions can be made solely on credential availability proofs (§
IV-A3
).
Then we show that randomness does not help (§
IV-A4
).
Finally, we show that for any deterministic mechanism family, there exists a dominating mechanism defined by a monotonic Boolean function of the credentials’ availability (§
IV-A5
).

IV-A
1
Step template

Each step in our proof has the following parts:
Given a mechanism family
M_{1}
we show it is dominated by a mechanism family
M_{2}
with a certain property.
We prove constructively by defining a mechanism family
M_{2}
and show the required property holds.
The domination proof takes advantage of a mechanism’s ability to simulate the execution of another mechanism.
That is, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
the mechanism
M_{2}^{\lambda}
takes a mechanism
M_{1}^{\lambda}
, two strategies
S_{0}
and
S_{1}
, the sets of credentials each strategy uses
C_{0}
and
C_{1}
, an ordering function
\gamma_{\textit{ord}}
, a scheduler random tape
v_{\gamma}
, and a random tape
v
,
and runs
\textit{Execute}(M_{1}^{\lambda},S_{0},C_{0},S_{1},C_{1},\gamma_{\textit{ord}},v_{\gamma},v)
(Appendix
A
).
The simulation result is the identifier that
\textit{decide}_{M_{1}^{\lambda}}
gets during the execution of

\bot

Note 1

At the end of this section we show that there always exists a dominating polynomial

Note 2

In the following proofs, we assume the user sends the secret part of her credentials (as with passwords).
Although this is not always the case (e.g., with cryptographic signatures),
this is only a theoretical construction and does not suggest that the user should generally send her secrets in plaintext.
In practice, she must only be able to prove she can access them.

2

We define a

(OS) mechanism as one that, in every execution, reaches a decision based only on the first message it receives (if any) from each player.
If a player sends multiple messages, the mechanism ignores all but the first it receives.

Definition 8

(One-shot mechanism)

A mechanism

V
Probabilistic Analysis

Using the properties we found of maximal mechanisms, we present an efficient method for finding mechanisms with approximately maximal success probability.

V-A

Preliminaries

As in previous work
[
15
]
, we derive the probability of scenarios from the fault probability of credentials.
Each credential
c_{i}
can be in one of the four states,
(1)
safe
: Only available to the user, (2)
loss
: Not available to either player, (3)
leak
: Available to both players, or (4)
theft
: Available only to the attacker. Its
c_{i}^{S}
is available to each player accordingly by the scheduler.

Each credential has a probability of being in each of the four states.
For example, a complex password that the user memorized has a low probability of being leaked (guessed) but a high probability of being lost (forgotten).
While a simple password has a high probability of being leaked but a low probability of being lost.
The states of the different credentials are determined by a probability space specified by independent probabilities of each of the credentials.
The probabilities of a credential
c_{i}
being in each of the four states are denoted by
P_{i}^{\textit{safe}}
,
P_{i}^{\textit{loss}}
,
P_{i}^{\textit{leak}}
, and
P_{i}^{\textit{theft}}
, respectively,
P_{i}^{\textit{safe}}+P_{i}^{\textit{loss}}+P_{i}^{\textit{leak}}+P_{i}^{\textit{theft}}=1
.
We assume the mechanism designer estimates the fault probabilities of the credential.
But note that this is always the case, even if done implicitly.

The probability of a scenario given the probability vector tuple of each credential is the product of the probabilities of each credential being in the state it is in the scenario.
Let
M_{f}
be the Boolean mechanism of the function
f
.
The success probability of
M_{f}
is the sum of the probabilities of all scenarios in which
M_{f}
is successful, denoted by
P^{\textit{suc}}(M_{f})
,
and the failure probability is its complement
P^{\textit{fail}}(M_{f})=1-P^{\textit{suc}}(M_{f})
.
A mechanism is
better
than another if its success probability is higher.

Given the number of credentials

Using exhaustive search over the space of all possible mechanisms (equivalently, all monotonic Boolean functions) is infeasible for more than just a few credentials

Scenario-Based Search Algorithm

Our algorithm finds a mechanism with success probability
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
close to that of an optimal mechanism.
It takes advantage of three key observations:
(1) The number of scenarios that might be added to a profile of a partial Boolean mechanism is bounded (Observation

).
(2) Non-viable scenarios can be ignored (Claim

).
And (3) the probability of different scenarios drops exponentially fast (Figures

and
1(a)
).

Instead of searching all possible mechanisms to find the optimal one, we build the truth table (and thus mechanism) incrementally:
Given the number of credentials

n
variables of the function (total of

By Lemma

such that

is equivalent to the mechanism defined by

.
The monotonic partial truth table corresponding to

Changing a value of a row in the truth table from

By Theorem (Theorem

) every mechanism is dominated by a Boolean mechanism.
Because every maximal mechanism is a monotonic Boolean mechanism (Theorem

We extend the monotonic partial Boolean function that defines the mechanism until it is full (representing a Boolean function).

Note that if the value of the all zeroes row is set to

.
As for all

.
Similarly, if the value of the all ones row is set to

.

It is easy to confirm that the profile of a Boolean mechanism of a constant function is empty.
Let

and

{\textit{F}(\sigma_{U})\wedge\neg\textit{F}(\sigma_{A})=0}

{\textit{T}(\sigma_{U})\wedge\neg\textit{T}(\sigma_{A})=0}
.

So we consider only non-constant monotonic Boolean functions, and set the value of the all zeroes row to

and the value of the all ones row to

Given the credentials’ probability vectors, we calculate the probability of each viable scenario.
Then we progress greedily, from scenarios with the highest to the lowest probabilities.
For each scenario, there are two options: either include it in the profile or not.
Given a scenario
\sigma
and a partially filled truth table,
we check whether the scenario contradicts the truth table.
We do so by checking if the value of the user’s availability vector
\sigma_{U}
is set to

or not chosen yet and the value of the attacker’s availability vector
\sigma_{A}
is set to
0
or not chosen yet.
If so, the scenario does not contradict the truth table, and we can include it in the profile.

To include a scenario

\sigma_{U}

\sigma_{A}

Then, to have the truth table represent a monotonic Boolean function, we update it by setting all the entries

And setting all entries

.

After each update, the partial monotonic truth table remains a (possibly partial) monotonic truth table, and the above update does not contradict previous ones.

Proof.

Given a monotonic partial truth table, it holds that for all

f(y)=1
then
f(x)=1
, and if
f(x)=0
then
f(y)=0
.
According to the algorithm, in every step where we add a viable scenario
\sigma

\sigma_{U}
is not set to
0
and
\sigma_{A}
is not set to
1

If so, we set
\sigma_{U}
to
1
and
\sigma_{A}
to
0

\sigma

\sigma_{U}\nleq\sigma_{A}

\sigma_{U}

∎

In each step, first the algorithm checks if it tried all viable scenarios or if the truth table is full.
The latter means we reached a Boolean function that cannot be further extended.
In both cases, it returns the success probability of the mechanism defined by the function, and the truth table.
Otherwise, the algorithm checks if it is beneficial to continue extending the current table.
It does so by computing the success probability of the current partial truth table.
Then, calculating an upper bound on the additional success probability for the current branch by considering the next
k
scenarios in the sorted list and summing their probabilities where
k
is the maximum number of scenarios that may be added to the current truth table without contradicting it (Observation
3
).
If the potential best solution is not better by at least
\delta
than the global best, we prune this branch and do not consider any of the mechanisms it results in.
This is a key step in ensuring the algorithm’s efficiency.

If, at any step, all the next scenarios have a probability of
0
, we can complete the truth table arbitrarily as long as the function stays monotonic.
That is because additional scenarios would contribute
0
to the success probability.

For the recursive exploration, given a scenario, we have two options: (1) Include the scenario in the truth table and recursively call the algorithm (only if it does not contradict the current truth table).
Or (2) Exclude this scenario and recursively call the algorithm with the next scenario.

After exploring both options, we update the global best solution probability if a better solution is found.
Finally, the algorithm returns the best solution probability and the corresponding truth table.
The result is thus a mechanism that is
\delta
close to the optimal mechanism.

V-D

Case Studies

The algorithm allows designers choose approximately optimal mechanisms given their credentials. The ability to study mechanisms with larger number of credentials compared to previous work allows taking advantage of more elaborate schemes.
We demonstrate this with two use cases.

Our algorithm takes advantage of the fact that the probability of different scenarios drops exponentially (Figure

).
Instead of searching all possible mechanisms, it builds them incrementally, exploring only a small fraction of the space of all possible mechanisms.
This improves the efficiency by orders of magnitude compared to previous work.

VI
Conclusion

We formalize the common asynchronous authentication problem, overcoming the apparent tension between asynchrony and cryptographic security.
We show every mechanism is dominated by a non-trivial monotonic Boolean mechanism, which are not strictly dominated themselves.
So in every pair of distinct such mechanisms, neither strictly dominates the other.
We present an efficient algorithm for finding approximately optimal mechanisms, given the credential fault probabilities.
This highlights the need for user studies to quantify credential fault probabilities.

One case study shows that a user designing her cryptocurrency wallet can reduce the failure probability by orders of magnitude by incorporating with her carefully-guarded credentials a few additional ones that are perhaps easy to lose.
More generally, both individuals and companies can use these results directly to rigorously design authentication mechanisms addressing the demands of current and forthcoming challenges.
