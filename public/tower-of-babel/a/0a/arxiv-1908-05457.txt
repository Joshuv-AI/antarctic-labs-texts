On physical optics approximation of viscous stratified shear flows
Natanael Karjanto

Abstract
We study a mathematical model of a perturbed stratified shear mean flow in the presence of eddy coefficients of turbulent viscosity. We adopt the standard Boussinesq approximation in the natural convection of the buoyancy-driven flow and neglect the influence of the eddy coefficients of turbulent diffusivity. Comprising both the vertical and horizontal viscosity effects, a model for the vertical velocity perturbation corresponds to a fourth-order Taylor-Goldstein (TG) differential equation. Considering only the latter, we obtained a modified TG equation with the same order as the classical, inviscid one. Under an assumption of the slowly varying Brunt-Väisälä frequency and background horizontal velocity, we discuss the corresponding geometrical and physical optics approximations of the modified TG equation using the WKB method. We further investigate the behavior of these asymptotic solutions near singular values of a turning point and critical level.

Introduction

We consider dynamics of internal gravity waves in the atmosphere for continuously stratified parallel flows in the presence of horizontal eddy coefficients of turbulent viscosity. Under the Boussinesq approximation, we derive a vertical velocity perturbation equation from the two-dimensional Euler equations as the governing model. By including both the horizontal and vertical eddy viscosity, an equation for the vertical velocity perturbation is given by a fourth-order ordinary differential equation (ODE). On the other hand, in the absence of vertical eddy viscosity, it is modeled by a second-order ODE, an extended version of the classical Taylor-Goldstein (TG) equation for the inviscid case
Taylor 1931
;
Goldstein 1931
;
Haurwitz 1931
. The latter governs the dynamics of Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) instability, which in general refers to the instability for continuous distribution of velocity and density
Kelvin 1871
;
Helmholtz 1868
.

The study of flow with viscosity has a long history, starting from the Orr-Sommerfeld equation, an eigenvalue equation that models two-dimensional linear stability problem for nearly parallel viscous flows in a straight channel or a boundary layer
Orr 1907a
;
Orr 1907b
;
Sommerfeld 1909
. An extended version of the TG equation to include the effects of constant viscosity and diffusivity is modeled by Koppel using a sixth-order ODE
Koppel 1964
. Hazel solved numerically the higher-order equation for the vertical velocity of internal gravity waves with kinematic viscosity and thermal conductivity
Hazel 1967
. A couple of years later, the infinitesimal stability of inviscid, parallel, stratified shear flows described by the TG equation was studied numerically
Hazel 1972
. For a mathematical perspective of instability in viscous fluids, see
Renardy and Renardy 2003
.

The TG equation and its variants have been used as mathematical models in various physical settings where stratified fluids or density stratification and shear flow occur. The interest lies in hydrodynamic stability since it sheds light on the problem of transition from the laminar to turbulent flow. Some examples where the TG equation aids in understanding this stability include surface gravity waves in the atmosphere
Moninger and Gossard 1976
;
Janssen and Komen 1985a
;
Janssen and Komen 1985b
, internal gravity waves in a stably stratified boundary layer
Anne and Driedonks 1985
;
Chimonas and Nappo 1989
, and internal waves in the ocean
Sun et al. 1998
;
Watson 1994
;
Thorpe 1999
, cf.
Hall 1993
;
Lawrence et al. 1991
;
Farrell and Ioannou 1993
;
Sutherland 2005
;
Zhou and Morton 2007
;
Tedford et al. 2009
;
Camassa and Viotti 2013
;
Pütz 2018
.

There exists, however, theoretical studies of the TG equation. For instance, Baldwin and Roberts studied the critical layer in a stratified shear flow where they introduced small viscosity and heat conductivity to remove the singularity
Baldwin and Roberts 1970
. Banerjee presented a geometry of the TG equation and some generalized results related to the stability
Banerjee 2005
. Using a variational approach, Hirota and Morrison developed an efficient method for finding marginally stable (neutral mode) solutions of the TG equation
Hirota and Morrison 2015
;
Hirota and Morrison 2016
.

The interest in studying the TG model has been growing for the past two decades. Pratt and collaborators proposed an extended TG equation for modeling the stratified shear flow of arbitrary cross-section
Pratt et al. 2000
;
Deng et al. 2003
. While these authors also formulated the corresponding semicircle theorem, Subbiah and Ganesh sharpened it by deriving a semi-ellipse theorem for unstable modes. In addition to estimating their growth rate, they also obtained the upper and lower bounds for the neutral modes phase velocity
Subbiah and Ganesh 2008
;
Ganesh and Subbiah 2009
.

After the classical works on the TG equation with viscosity in the 1970s
Hazel 1972
;
Baldwin and Roberts 1970
, it was not until the 1990s that the work starts to blossom again. Hall discussed the nonlinear instability of a boundary layer on a heated flat plate positioned in an incoming flow
Hall 1992
. Dando examined the nonlinear evolution of inviscid Görtler vortices in three-dimensional boundary layers
Dando 1996
. Ng and Reid considered the stability problem in plane Poiseuille flow with the combined effects of stratification and viscosity
Ng and Reid 1997
. Mureithi et al. investigated the effect of buoyancy on the upper-branch linear stability characteristics of an accelerating boundary layer flow in Tollmien-Schlichting waves
Mureithi et al. 1997
. See also
Watson and Otto 2001
;
Hogg et al. 2001
;
Hogg et al. 2002
;
Thorpe 2012
;
Lawrence et al. 2013
;
Xiao et al. 2015
.

Fast forward to the past decade, Smyth et al. investigated properties of shear instabilities with small-scale turbulence represented by vertical eddy viscosity and diffusivity coefficients
Smyth et al. 2011
. Liu et al. examined an extended TG equation by including the effects of small-scale turbulence on the stability of stratified shear flows
Liu et al. 2012
. Thorpe et al. investigated the marginal stability of a stably stratified shear flow with ambient turbulence represented by vertical small eddy viscosity and diffusivity
Thorpe et al. 2013
. In some circumstances, they found that viscosity amplifies instability. Li et al. further examined the effects of vertically varying eddy viscosity and diffusivity on the KH instability of a stratified shear flow
Li et al. 2015
. Additionally, Khani and Waite measured horizontal and vertical eddy viscosity in stratified turbulence using high-resolution simulations
Khani and Waite 2013
. Recently, Lian et al. performed numerical computations for the stability analysis of stratified and parallel shear flows with the effects of small-scale turbulence described by eddy viscosity and diffusivity
Lian et al. 2020
.

Our focus in this article is a modified TG equation with the presence of horizontal eddy viscosity but the absence of vertical eddy viscosity. Under the assumption of slowly varying background horizontal velocity and Brunt-Väisälä frequency, we propose an asymptotic solution for this modified TG equation using the WKB method. The technique is named after and developed by physicists Gregor Wentzel, Hendrik Anthony Kramers, and Léon Brillouin in 1926. They refined the ideas of George Green and Joseph Liouville
Gill 1982
;
Steele 1976
. Although mathematician Harold Jeffreys had developed a general method for approximating solutions of second-order linear ODEs in 1923, his contribution is often neglected since the trios WKB were not really aware of this earlier work. Indeed, a better name for this method should be coined as the JWKB or WKBJ approximation
Bender and Orszag 2010
;
Baines 1995
. There are in fact at least 12 people who contributed to the development of WKB theory, including Liouville, Green, Horn, Rayleigh, Gans, Jeffrey, Wentzel, Kramers, Brillouin, Langer, Olver, and Meyer
Hinch 1991
.

The WKB method has some limitations. The so-called physical optics WKB approximation turns singular and vanishes at a turning point and critical level, respectively. In the vicinity of the critical level, i.e., a level where the mean fluid velocity is equal to the horizontal phase velocity, an internal gravity wave flow is unstable
Booker and Bretherton 1967
;
Geller et al. 1975
;
Fritts and Geller 1976
. The behavior of both 2D and 3D internal gravity wave packets approaching a critical level has been examined both analytically and numerically
Winters and D’Asaro 1989
;
Winters and Riley 1992
;
Winters and D’Asaro 1994
. Shear instability is responsible for maintaining deep-cycle turbulence due to the extraction of energy from the mean flow near the critical level through the interaction of mean shear and subgrid-scale stress
Sun et al. 1998
. Note that although this reference refers to the latter as Reynolds stress, mean shear generally interacts with non-resolving fluxes, which are not necessarily Reynolds stress.

We organize this article as follows. The first part of Section
2
presents a derivation of mathematical modeling for vertical velocity perturbation with eddy coefficients of turbulent viscosity. Subsection
2.2
focuses on a modified TG equation in the presence of horizontal coefficient eddy viscosity only. Section
3
derives both the geometrical and physical optics approximation using the WKB method. Section
4
presents the solution near a turning point and critical level for different values of wind shears. Section
5
concludes from our discussion.

2.1
An extended Taylor-Goldstein equation

Consider the Navier-Stokes momentum equation for an incompressible flow of the Newtonian fluid

where

The Navier-Stokes equation (

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+U\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)\theta+w\frac{d\Theta}{dz}

\displaystyle=K_{H}\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(K_{T}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z}+k_{T}\frac{d\Theta}{dz}\right),

Modified Taylor-Goldstein equation with horizontal eddy viscosity

Let

be the background velocity,

be the horizontal eddy viscosity, where both are real-valued functions of the vertical coordinate

. Let

and
U(z)-c-ikA_{H}=u_{2}(z)=\rho_{2}e^{i\phi_{2}}
, where both the amplitudes
\rho_{1,2}(z)
and the phases
\phi_{1,2}(z)
are real-valued functions. The square of the Brunt-Väisälä frequency is defined as
N^{2}=g\frac{d}{dz}\ln\Theta(z)
, with
\Theta
as the basic state of potential temperature. Consider a modified TG equation for the vertical velocity profile

in the absence of

but only the presence of
A_{H}
. It reads

\hat{w}^{\prime\prime}+Q_{1}(z)\hat{w}^{\prime}+Q_{0}(z)\hat{w}=0,

(9)

where both
Q_{0}
,
Q_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
and are given as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u_{2}}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{u_{1}}-U^{\prime\prime}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle Q_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=-ik\frac{A_{H}}{u_{2}}.

(11)

Applying the following Liouville transformation by introducing a new variable
\tilde{w}
and an integration factor

\hat{w}(z)=\text{exp}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\int^{z}Q_{1}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\right)\tilde{w}(z),

(12)

we can simplify the modified TG equation (

\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}+Q_{2}(z)\tilde{w}=0,

where

Q_{2}(z)=Q_{0}-\frac{Q_{1}^{2}}{4}-\frac{Q_{1}^{\prime}}{2}.

Rescaling the vertical coordinate

, where

where the complex-valued potential term

\displaystyle u_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}k^{2}(\dot{A_{H}})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}ik\dot{U}\dot{A_{H}},

\displaystyle u_{4}

\displaystyle=\ddot{U}-\frac{1}{2}ik\ddot{A_{H}}.

Note that the differentiation with respect to the scaled vertical variable

3
WKB method and matrix exponential

WKB theory provides relatively simple approximate solutions for the Fourier component of the perturbed vertical velocity
\tilde{w}

WKB theory is a particular case of multiple-scale analysis and an early application of this method comes from quantum mechanics when the conditions of the particle motion are nearly classical. 3.1
WKB approximation

Consider the modified TG equation (
15
) and write its potential term

by separating the real and imaginary parts, given as follows:

Q_{\varepsilon}(Z)=A(Z)+iB(Z)-\varepsilon^{2}\left[C(Z)+iD(Z)\right]

where

\displaystyle A(Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}\left[(U-c_{r})^{2}-c_{i}^{2}-kc_{i}A_{H}\right]}{\left[(U-c_{r})^{2}-c_{i}^{2}-kc_{i}A_{H}\right]^{2}+(U-c_{r})^{2}(2c_{i}+kA_{H})^{2}}-k^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}(U-c_{r})(2c_{i}+kA_{H})}{\left[(U-c_{r})^{2}-c_{i}^{2}-kc_{i}A_{H}\right]^{2}+(U-c_{r})^{2}(2c_{i}+kA_{H})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}k^{2}\left(\dot{A_{H}}\right)^{2}\left[(U-c_{r})^{2}-c_{i}^{2}\right]-kc_{i}(U-c_{r})\dot{U}\dot{A_{H}}+\ddot{U}(U-c_{r})\left[(U-c_{r})^{2}+c_{i}^{2}+2kc_{i}A_{H}\right]

\varepsilon=\max_{z\ \geq\ 0}\left|\frac{M^{\prime}(z)}{M^{2}(z)}\right|\approx\max_{z\ \geq\ 0}\left|\frac{M_{0}^{\prime}(z)}{M_{0}^{2}(z)}\right|=\max_{z\ \geq\ 0}\sqrt{\frac{\left(m_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{2}+\left(n_{0}^{\prime}\right)^{2}}{A^{2}+B^{2}}}.

As an example, for
U(z)=1+2\tanh(z)
,
N^{2}(z)=1+4\tanh^{2}(z)
,
c=5+0.1i
,
k=0.1
, and
A_{H}(z)=2e^{-z^{2}}
, any value of
\varepsilon
satisfying
0<\varepsilon\leq 0.3
would be sufficient.

The first-order of
\varepsilon
yields the following system of equations:

\displaystyle 2(m_{0}m_{1}-n_{0}n_{1})+m_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 2(m_{0}n_{1}+m_{1}n_{0})+n_{0}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=0.

Solving these equations and using the fact that
|M_{0}|^{2}=m_{0}^{2}+n_{0}^{2}=\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}
, we obtain
m_{1}
and
n_{1}
and their integrals:

\displaystyle m_{1}(Z)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\frac{\frac{d}{dZ}(m_{0}^{2}+n_{0}^{2})}{m_{0}^{2}+n_{0}^{2}},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{Z}m_{1}(\zeta)\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=\ln\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(Z)}\right|},

\displaystyle n_{1}(Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{d}{dZ}(m_{0}/n_{0})}{2\left[1+(m_{0}/n_{0})^{2}\right]},

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{Z}n_{1}(\zeta)\,d\zeta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{m_{0}(Z)}{n_{0}(Z)}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{m_{0}(0)}{n_{0}(0)}\right).

The physical optics approximation of the WKB method reads

\tilde{w}(z)=\tilde{w}(z=0)\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(z=0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\,\text{exp}\left[\frac{1}{2}i(\alpha-\alpha_{0})+\int_{0}^{z}M_{0}(\zeta)\,d\zeta\right],

(18)

where we have defined

\tan\alpha(z)=\frac{m_{0}}{n_{0}}=\pm\sqrt{1+2\left(\frac{A}{B}\right)^{2}-2\frac{A}{B}\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{A}{B}\right)^{2}}}.

In the literature, what is called as the “WKB solution” usually refers to the physical optics approximation. The geometric approximation is rarely used when we discuss the WKB method, as it shortfalls to provide the correction for the spatially varying coefficients.

The system of equations for the second-order
\varepsilon
is given by

\displaystyle m_{1}^{2}-n_{1}^{2}+2(m_{0}m_{2}-n_{0}n_{2})+m_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=C,

\displaystyle 2\left(m_{0}n_{2}+m_{1}n_{1}+m_{2}n_{0}\right)+n_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=D.

These solve as

\displaystyle m_{2}(Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[C-m_{1}^{\prime}-(m_{1}^{2}-n_{1}^{2})\right]m_{0}+\left[D-n_{1}^{\prime}-2m_{1}n_{1}\right]n_{0}}{2\left(m_{0}^{2}+n_{0}^{2}\right)},

\displaystyle n_{2}(Z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left[D-n_{1}^{\prime}-2m_{1}n_{1}\right]m_{0}-\left[C-m_{1}^{\prime}-(m_{1}^{2}-n_{1}^{2})\right]n_{0}}{2\left(m_{0}^{2}+n_{0}^{2}\right)}.

Although in principal we can proceed further to obtain an expression for the WKB approximation at higher-order of
\varepsilon
, integrating
M_{2}(Z)
is already cumbersome at this stage due to its dependence on
C(Z)
and
D(Z)
. Hence, the WKB approximation (
18
) suffices for our analysis. The left panel of Figure

displays a physical optics approximation of the WKB solution (
18
) for a Gaussian profile of horizontal eddy viscosity
A_{H}
at the onset of instability. The horizontal background velocity and the square of the Brunt-Väisälä frequency are taken as
U(z)=1+2\tanh(z)
and
N^{2}(z)=1+4\tanh^{2}(z)
, respectively, which are shown in the right panel of Figure
1
. Both quantities are symmetric with respect to the
z
-axis.

3.2
Matrix exponential and fundamental matrix physical optics approximations

Consider the following well-known theorem on matrix exponential and fundamental matrix solutions from ODE theory
Braun 1993
;
Boyce and DiPrima 2012
;
Edwards et al. 2018
;
Strang 2014
. Let
\boldsymbol{G}
be an
n\times n
matrix and let
\boldsymbol{\Phi}(z)
be a fundamental matrix for the homogeneous linear system
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{x}
, then the unique solution of the initial (in this case, boundary) value problem (IVP or BVP)

\frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{dz}=\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{x},\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{x}(0)=\boldsymbol{x}_{0},

(19)

is given by

\boldsymbol{x}(z)=e^{\boldsymbol{G}z}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(z)\left[\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0)\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}.

Consider again the modified TG equation (
13
) or (
15
). Defining
\tilde{v}^{\prime}=\tilde{w}
, we can write it as a system of ODEs in the form of (
19
):

\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{G}\boldsymbol{x}\qquad\qquad\text{or}\qquad\qquad\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{x}

where the prime and dot signs represent the derivative with respect to
z
and
Z
, respectively and

\boldsymbol{x}=\begin{bmatrix}[c]\tilde{w}\\
\tilde{v}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{G}=\begin{bmatrix}[c]0&1\\
-Q_{2}&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad\qquad\text{and}\qquad\qquad\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{bmatrix}[c]0&1\\
-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}Q_{\varepsilon}&0\end{bmatrix}.

The eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{G}_{\varepsilon}
are complex-valued functions that depends on the variable
Z
:

\lambda(Z)=\pm\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(C-\frac{A}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(D-\frac{B}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}}+C-\frac{A}{\varepsilon^{2}}}\pm i\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\left(C-\frac{A}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}+\left(D-\frac{B}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}}-\left(C-\frac{A}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)}.

Figure
2
depicts the real and imaginary parts of eigenvalues
\lambda
as well as their trajectories in the complex plane for two different values of
\varepsilon
.

Figure 2:
(Top panels) The real and imaginary parts of the eigenvalues
\lambda
for the identical set parameters as in Figure
1
. (Bottom panels) The corresponding eigenvalue trajectories in the complex plane. (Left panels) Plots of the eigenvalues with negative real and imaginary values with
\varepsilon=0.1
. (Right panels) Similar to the left panels but with Re
(\lambda)>0
, Im
(\lambda)>0
, and
\varepsilon=0.15
.

We need to seek the corresponding eigenvectors from these eigenvalues to obtain exact solutions using the fundamental matrix and matrix exponential, which turns out to be nontrivial. Instead, we will use the corresponding eigenfunctions from the WKB solution that we have obtained in the previous subsection to construct the physical optics approximation for the matrix exponential. Pursuing this course is particularly useful once we follow the path in solving the BVP numerically. One notable novel numerical integration technique combines the standard Runge-Kutta approach and the WKB method, known as the Runge-Kutta-Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin (RKWKB) method
Handley et al. 2016
;
Agocs et al. 2020
;
Bamber and Handley 2020
.

For the geometrical optics approximation of the WKB method, the complex-valued frequency can be approximated as

\displaystyle\langle m_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}m_{0}(\zeta)\,d\zeta,\qquad\qquad\qquad\langle n_{0}\rangle=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}n_{0}(\zeta)\,d\zeta

\displaystyle\langle M_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{z}\int_{0}^{z}M_{0}(\zeta)\,d\zeta=\langle m_{0}+in_{0}\rangle=\langle m_{0}\rangle+i\langle n_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle\langle\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\langle m_{0}\rangle}{\langle n_{0}\rangle}\right).

The physical optics approximation provides a correction to the phase and amplitude of the solution. Using the fact that there are two linearly independent eigenfunction solutions, the matrix exponential approximation can be expressed as follows:

e^{\boldsymbol{G}z}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}(z)\left[\boldsymbol{\Phi}(0)\right]^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}[r]\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\cosh\phi(z)&\frac{1}{q(0)}\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\sinh\phi(z)\\
q(z)\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\sinh\phi(z)&\frac{q(z)}{q(0)}\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\cosh\phi(z)\end{bmatrix}

where the Wronskian
W(z)=\text{det}\,\boldsymbol{\Phi}(z)=-2q(z)/\sqrt{M_{0}(z)}
and

\displaystyle\phi(z)

\displaystyle=\langle M_{0}\rangle z+\frac{1}{2}i\varepsilon\langle\alpha\rangle z

\displaystyle p(z)

\displaystyle=\left[-(m_{0}m_{0}^{\prime}+n_{0}n_{0}^{\prime})+i(m_{0}n_{0}^{\prime}-m_{0}^{\prime}n_{0})\right]

\displaystyle q(z)

\displaystyle=M_{0}(z)+\frac{\varepsilon p(z)}{2|M_{0}(z)|^{2}}.

We observe that
\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\cosh\phi(z)
and
\sqrt{\left|\frac{M_{0}(0)}{M_{0}(z)}\right|}\sinh\phi(z)
play a role as two linearly independent eigenfunctions of the WKB solution. This is guaranteed from the fact that if the Wronskian starts from
W\neq 0
at
z=0
, then
W(z)\neq 0
for all
z>0

Strang 2014
.

4
Behavior near turning point and critical level

There are at least two possibilities where the WKB approximation becomes invalid, i.e., at a turning point or at a critical level. In this section, we will discuss the behavior of the perturbed vertical velocity profile near these points.

4.1
Behavior near a turning point

A
turning point
is the point
z=z_{0}
where
M_{0}
vanishes, i.e., when it satisfies
N^{2}(z_{0})=k^{2}\left[U(z_{0})-c\right]\left[U(z_{0})-c-ikA_{H}(z_{0})\right]
. At this incidence, the WKB solution (
18
) becomes singular. Let
f(z)=N^{2}-k^{2}u_{1}u_{2}
, then its Taylor expansion about the turning point
z_{0}
gives

f(z)=f(z_{0})+f^{\prime}(z_{0})(z-z_{0})+\frac{1}{2}f^{\prime\prime}(z_{0})(z-z_{0})^{2}+\dots.

Multiplying the TG equation (
13
) with
u_{1}u_{2}
, taking the first-order of Taylor-expansion of
f
, and dividing again with
u_{1}u_{2}
after evaluating them at the turning point
z_{0}
, we obtain

\frac{d^{2}\tilde{w}}{dz^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime}(z_{0})}{u_{1}(z_{0})u_{2}(z_{0})}(z-z_{0})\tilde{w}=0.

By defining a new variable
\zeta
:

\zeta=\left(\frac{f^{\prime}(z_{0})}{u_{1}(z_{0})u_{2}(z_{0})}\right)^{1/3}(z_{0}-z),

we arrive at the Airy ODE

\frac{d^{2}\tilde{w}}{d\zeta^{2}}-\zeta\tilde{w}=0.

(20)

The Airy equation (
20
) has two linearly independent solutions, called the Airy function of the first and second kinds, denoted by Ai
(\zeta)
and Bi
(\zeta)
, respectively. Since Bi
(\zeta)
blows up for
\zeta>0
, this will not correspond to the physical setting of the stratified shear flows. We are more interested in Ai
(\zeta)
because it decays for
\zeta>0
or for
z<z_{0}
. The function Ai
(\zeta)
is defined by the following improper Riemann integral:

\text{Ai}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\cos\left(\frac{t^{3}}{3}+\zeta t\right)\,dt.

(21)

The convergence of this function can be shown by Dirichlet’s test using the integration by parts
Hall 2013
.

4.2
Behavior near a critical level

A
critical level
(or
steering level
) is a height
z=z_{c}
, if it exists, at which the background state of the velocity flow
U(z)
is equal to the real part of the horizontal phase speed
c
, i.e.,
U(z_{c})=c_{r}
. When this height is reached and
c\in\mathbb{R}
(
c_{i}=0
), the denominator of
Q_{2}
or
Q_{\varepsilon}
vanishes. Hence, the modified TG equation (
13
) or (
15
) becomes singular, and consequently, the WKB solution (
18
) vanishes too. Assume that
c_{i}
is small, in particular,
|U^{\prime\prime}(z)|c_{i}/[U^{\prime}(z)]^{2}\ll 1
. For stable flow, the function
\tilde{w}(z)
oscillate rapidly in
z
near the critical level and the oscillations become infinitely rapid as the wave approaches it
Haynes 2051
. Following an analysis presented in
Booker and Bretherton 1967
;
Nappo 2013
, we examine the behavior of the vertical velocity profile near the critical level.

Denoting
\zeta=z-z_{c}
, we proceed with Taylor-expanding the background wind speed
U
as well as
U+ikA_{H}
up to second-order terms

\displaystyle U(z)-c\approx a_{1}\zeta+\frac{1}{2}a_{2}\zeta^{2},

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\;a_{1}=U^{\prime}(z_{c})\;\text{and}\;a_{2}=U^{\prime\prime}(z_{c})

(22)

\displaystyle U(z)-ikA_{H}(z)-c\approx b_{1}\zeta+\frac{1}{2}b_{2}\zeta^{2},

\displaystyle\quad\text{where}\;b_{1}=a_{1}-i\alpha_{1}\;\text{and}\;b_{2}=a_{2}-i\alpha_{2}.

(23)

Here,
\alpha_{1}=kA_{H}^{\prime}(z_{c})
and
\alpha_{2}=kA_{H}^{\prime\prime}(z_{c})
. Upon substitution to the modified TG equation (
13
), we obtain another TG equation with a regular singular point at
\zeta=0
:

\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{Q_{22}}{\zeta^{2}}-\frac{Q_{21}}{\zeta}+Q_{20}\right)\tilde{w}=0

(24)

where

\displaystyle Q_{22}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{a_{1}b_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-1\right)\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-2\right)

(25)

\displaystyle Q_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{N^{2}}{a_{1}b_{1}}\left(\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}}+\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}}\right)+\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}}\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-1\right)\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-2\right)+\frac{a_{2}}{b_{1}}+2\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}}\right]

(26)

\displaystyle Q_{20}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left[N^{2}\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}^{2}}\cdot\frac{b_{2}}{b_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{b_{2}^{2}}{b_{1}^{2}}\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-1\right)\left(\frac{a_{1}}{b_{1}}-2\right)+\frac{a_{2}b_{2}}{b_{1}^{2}}+2\frac{b_{2}^{2}}{b_{1}^{2}}\right]-k^{2}.

(27)

We seek a series solution near the critical level by employing the method of Frobenius by expanding
\tilde{w}
as follows
Braun 1993
;
Boyce and DiPrima 2012
;
Edwards et al. 2018
;
Frobenius 1873
:

\tilde{w}(z)=\tilde{w}(z_{c}+\zeta)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}\,\zeta^{n+\lambda},\qquad C_{n}\in\mathbb{C}.

(28)

Substituting (
28
) to (
24
) and ordering the terms according to the powers of
\zeta
yields the indicial equation

\lambda(\lambda-1)+Q_{22}=0
, with indicial roots or characteristic exponents at the singularity
\lambda
at the regular singular point
\zeta=0
are given by

\lambda=\frac{1}{2}\pm\rho_{c}e^{i\phi_{c}},

where

\displaystyle\rho_{c}

\displaystyle=\sqrt[4]{\text{Re}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}-Q_{22}\right)+\text{Im}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}-Q_{22}\right)}

\displaystyle\phi_{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\left[\frac{\text{Im}\left(\frac{1}{4}-Q_{22}\right)}{\text{Re}\left(\frac{1}{4}-Q_{22}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle\text{Re}\left(\frac{1}{4}-Q_{22}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{|b_{1}|^{4}}\left(\alpha_{1}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{|b_{1}|^{2}}\left(\frac{3}{2}a_{1}^{2}-N^{2}\right)-\frac{3}{4}

\displaystyle\text{Im}\left(\frac{1}{4}-Q_{22}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{|b_{1}|^{2}}\left(\frac{3}{2}a_{1}-\frac{N^{2}}{a_{1}}\right)-\alpha_{1}\frac{a_{1}^{3}}{|b_{1}|^{4}}.

We can express the solution of the modified TG equation (
24
) as the following linear combination:

\tilde{w}(z)=\tilde{w}_{1}(z)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}|z-z_{c}|^{n}+\tilde{w}_{2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}|z-z_{c}|^{n},

(29)

where
\tilde{w}_{1}
and
\tilde{w}_{2}
are two linearly independent Frobenius solutions, given as follows:

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=|z-z_{c}|^{\frac{1}{2}+\rho_{c}\cos\phi_{c}}\cos\left(\rho_{c}\sin\phi_{c}\ln|z-z_{c}|\right)

\displaystyle\tilde{w}_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=|z-z_{c}|^{\frac{1}{2}+\rho_{c}\cos\phi_{c}}\sin\left(\rho_{c}\sin\phi_{c}\ln|z-z_{c}|\right).

The real-valued coefficients are given by
A_{n}=\text{Re}\left\{C_{n}\right\}
and
B_{n}=\text{Im}\left\{C_{n}\right\}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and the complex-valued coefficients
C_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
satisfy the following recursive relationship:

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=C_{0}\frac{Q_{21}}{2\lambda},\qquad C_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\;\text{is arbitrary},

(30)

\displaystyle C_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{n-1}Q_{21}-C_{n-2}Q_{20}}{n(2\lambda+n-1)},\qquad n\geq 2.

The behavior of Frobenius solutions is rather nontrivial. Although both trigonometric terms are bounded, since they oscillate infinitely many times between their maximum and minimum values, they do not approach any single value as
z\rightarrow z_{c}
, cf.
Haynes 2051
. Additionally, we also need to ensure the positive power for the small term
|z-z_{c}|
as the limit of
|z-z_{c}|
for negative power as
z\rightarrow z_{c}
does not exist.

In what follows, we will verify that the corresponding power series (
28
) without the term
\zeta^{\lambda}
converges for all
|\zeta|<R
, where
R\geq 0
is the radius of convergence of the term
(Q_{22}-Q_{21}\zeta+Q_{20}\zeta^{2})
. In this particular case,
R=\infty
. Let
I(n+\lambda)=n(2\lambda+n-1)
be a second-degree polynomial function in
n
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
I(n+\lambda)\neq 0
for
n=N_{0}
,
N_{0}+1
,
N_{0}+2
,
\cdots
. The coefficients of the power series (
28
) in the absence of
\zeta^{\lambda}
satisfy the recursive relationship (
30
).

Let us express the modified TG equation (
24
) as
\tilde{w}^{\prime\prime}+\zeta^{-2}\widetilde{Q}(\zeta)\tilde{w}=0
, where

\widetilde{Q}(\zeta)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tilde{q}_{k}\zeta^{k}

with

\tilde{q}_{0}=Q_{22},\qquad\tilde{q}_{1}=-Q_{21},\qquad\tilde{q}_{2}=Q_{20},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{q}_{k}=0\quad\text{for}\quad k=3,4,5,\dots.

Note that this particular representation extends easily to the case where
\widetilde{Q}(\zeta)
is any power series convergent for
|\zeta|<R
, where
R>0
is the corresponding radius of convergence. For any
\zeta\in(-R,R)
, choose
\Lambda_{1}>0
and
\Lambda_{2}>0
such that for
k\in\mathbb{N}
:

|\zeta|<\Lambda_{1}<\Lambda_{2},\qquad\left|\tilde{q}_{1}\Lambda_{1}\right|<\Lambda_{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\tilde{q}_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{2}\right|<\Lambda_{2}.

Furthermore, since
\tilde{q}_{k}=0
for
k\geq 3
, we have

\left|\tilde{q}_{k}\right|<\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{-k},\qquad\text{for all}\;k\in\mathbb{N}.

We now consider a modified power series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\widetilde{C}_{n}\,\zeta^{n}

(31)

with

\widetilde{C}_{0}=\left|C_{0}\right|,\qquad\widetilde{C}_{1}=\left|C_{1}\right|,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{C}_{n}=\frac{1}{I(n+\lambda)}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{C}_{j}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{j-n}\qquad\text{for}\quad n\geq 2.

One could verify immediately that

\left|C_{n}\right|\,\left|\zeta^{n}\right|\leq\widetilde{C}_{n}\,\left|\zeta^{n}\right|,\qquad\text{for}\;n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

As a consequence, the convergence of the series (
28
) without the factor
\zeta^{\lambda}
can be confirmed by showing the convergence of the series (
31
) by means of the Ratio Test. Moreover, for
n>N_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{C}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I(n+1+\lambda)}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\widetilde{C}_{j}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{j-n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{I(n+1+\lambda)}\left[\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\widetilde{C}_{j}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{j-n}\right)\Lambda_{1}^{-1}+\widetilde{C}_{n}\Lambda_{2}\Lambda_{1}^{-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{I(n+\lambda)+\Lambda_{2}}{I(n+1+\lambda)}\,\widetilde{C}_{n}\Lambda_{1}^{-1}.

Since
I
is a second-degree polynomial in
n
, we can straightforwardly verify that

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{I(n+\lambda)+\Lambda_{2}}{I(n+1+\lambda)}\right|=1.

Since
|\zeta|<\Lambda_{1}
, we obtain

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{\widetilde{C}_{n+1}\ \zeta^{n+1}}{\widetilde{C}_{n}\ \zeta^{n}}\right|=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\widetilde{C}_{n+1}}{\widetilde{C}_{n}}\ |\zeta|=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{I(n+\lambda)+\Lambda_{2}}{I(n+1+\lambda)}\right|\ \frac{|\zeta|}{\Lambda_{1}}=1.

Hence, the Frobenius series solution (
29
) converges for
|\zeta|<R
. A similar proof for a more general second-order linear homogeneous ODEs and an exposition of different possible solutions obtained using the Frobenius method can be found in advanced texts on differential equations, e.g.,
Agarwal and O’Regan 2009
;
Birkhoff and Rota 1989
;
Coddington 1989
;
Howell 2019
;
Teschl 2012
. Note that the Frobenius solution presented in
Booker and Bretherton 1967
;
Nappo 2013
only includes two terms of the lowest-order and discards the higher-order terms in the series. This suggests that those higher-order terms might not be essential and can be neglected from the physical point of view.

Finally, by employing the continuity equation (
6
), applying the Liouville transformation (
12
), and restoring the normal mode, one may obtain the corresponding horizontal and vertical velocity fields.

5
Conclusion

We have considered a modified TG equation describing a model for complex-valued vertical velocity perturbation in stratified shear flows with horizontal eddy coefficient of turbulent viscosity. Under an assumption of slowly varying background horizontal velocity and Brunt-Väisälä frequency, we implemented an asymptotic approach using the WKB method to obtain the geometrical and physical optics solutions for the modified TG equation.

Furthermore, we also investigate the behavior of the WKB solution near a turning point and seek a series solution near a critical level by employing Frobenius’ method. The former can be described by the Airy function while the latter exhibits an oscillating behavior but vanishes as the height approaches a critical level.

For the future work, it would be illuminating to discuss and analyze rigorously the relationship between the WKB method and a transformation at complex geometry. Although there exists an abundant literature on complex WKB method, e.g.,
Voros 1983
;
Maslov 1994
;
Fedotov and Klopp 2004
, most of them deals with the complex variable. In our case, however, the variable remains real-valued even though the potential-like function is complex-valued. Furthermore, we are interested in investigating the effect of vertical eddy coefficients of turbulent viscosity where its contribution is more essential in the study of atmospheric boundary layer in comparison to the horizontal one. Additionally, we would like to investigate the case when both horizontal and vertical eddy coefficients of turbulent diffusivity are present in the model equation.
