viscosity solution to complex Hessian equations on compact Hermitian manifolds
Jingrui Cheng, Yulun Xu

Abstract
. We prove the existence of viscosity solutions to complex Hessian equations on a compact Hermitian manifold that satisfy a determinant domination condition. This viscosity solution is shown to be unique when the right hand is strictly monotone increasing in terms of the solution. When the right hand side does not depend on the solution, we reduces it to the strict monotonicity of the solvability constant.



1.
introduction

The goal of this note is to study the existence and uniqueness of viscosity solutions to complex Hessian equations on a closed Hermitian manifold. There has been numerous works on the existence of viscosity solutions to complex Hessian equations, as well as pluripotential solutions, either on domains or on manifolds. We refer the readers to
[
6
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
32
]
and references therein for the quickly expanding literatures on this topic. The techniques developed by the pioneering work of Guo-Phong-Tong
[
25
]
allows us to develop stability estimates that make it possible to prove existence of weak solutions for more general complex Hessian equations. On the other hand, the regularization technique developed by our previous work
[
2
]
allows us to get a quite general uniqueness result.

Let
(M,\omega_{0})
be a closed Hermitian manifold. In local coordinates, we can write
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{-1}g_{i\bar{j}}dz_{i}\wedge d\bar{z}_{j}
.
Let
\chi
be a real
(1,1)
form on
M
and in local coordinates we can write it as:
\chi=\sqrt{-1}\chi_{i\bar{j}}dz_{i}\wedge d\bar{z}_{j}
. For any
C^{2}
function
\varphi:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we obtain a new real
(1,1)
form:
\chi+dd^{c}\varphi
. We can define an operator
A:TX\rightarrow TX
by
A^{i}_{j}=g^{i\bar{k}}\big(\chi_{j\bar{k}}+\varphi_{j\bar{k}}\big)
in local coordinates. Let
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]
be the (unordered) eigenvalues of
A
. Equivalently,
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]
is the set of roots for:

\det\big(\lambda g_{i\bar{j}}-(\chi_{i\bar{j}}+\varphi_{z_{i}\bar{z}_{j}})\big)=0.

Then we consider equations for
\varphi
that may be written in the form:

(1.1)

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=h,\,\,\,h=e^{G(x)}\text{ or $e^{G(x,\varphi)}$}.

In the above,

(1.2)

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=f(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]),

where
f(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
is a smooth symmetric function.

G(x)
or
G(x,\varphi)
are some right hand side one prescribes. The reason we write the right hand side in the form
e^{G}
is to emphasize that it is strictly positive. Such equations have been
studied extensively in the literature, going back to the work of Caffarelli-Nirenberg-Spruck
[
1
]
on the Dirichlet problem in the real case, when
\omega_{0}
is the Euclidean metric
and
M
is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We assume that the function
f
in (
1.2
) is defined in a closed convex symmetric cone
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
\Gamma\subset\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}>0\}
and containing the first octant
\Gamma_{n}
. Therefore, we need to assume that

(1.3)

\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma.

In addition, we are going to assume that:

Assumption 1.1
.

(1)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}>0,\,\,1\leq i\leq n
,
f
is concave and
>0
on
\Gamma
and
f=0
on
\partial\Gamma
.

(2)

\lambda[\chi](x)\in Int(\Gamma)
for any
x\in M
.

(3)

(determinant domination condition)
f
is a positive homogeneous function with degree 1, and there exist constants
c_{0}>0
, such that
f(\lambda)\geq c_{0}(\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i})^{\frac{1}{n}}
for
\lambda\in\Gamma_{n}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{i}>0,\,\,1\leq i\leq n\}
.

In the above, the assumption that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\geq 0
implies the ellipticity of the equation (
1.1
), (
1.3
). The determinant domination condition is motivated by the pioneering work of Guo-Phong-Tong
[
25
]
, which developed a unified PDE approach to
L^{\infty}
estimate which satisfies:

(1.4)

\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\leq C_{0}f,\,\,\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\geq c_{0},\text{ for some $c_{0},\,C_{0}>0$}.

[
25
]
also observed that determinant domination condition implies (
1.4
). The assumption that
f
is positive homogeneity one implies
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}=f
. The proof of the other property is contained in Lemma
2.4
, originally due to Guo-Phong-Tong
[
25
]
, which we reproduce for the convenience of the readers.

There are many examples which satisfy the Assumption
1.1
above. The most well-known example is probably
f(\lambda)=\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\lambda),\,1\leq k\leq n
, defined on
\Gamma_{k}:=\{\lambda:\sigma_{i}(\lambda)\geq 0,\,1\leq i\leq k\}
, where
\sigma_{k}(\lambda)
is the
k
-th symmetric polynomial of
\lambda
. These
\sigma_{k}
equations have been extensively studied. See
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
and references therein.
However, there are other examples satisfying Assumption
1.1
which are less studied, and we just name a few here:

(1)

(
\sigma_{k}
-equation for
(n-1)-
plurisubharmonic function)
f(\lambda)=\sigma_{k}^{\frac{1}{k}}(\tilde{\lambda})
, where
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\lambda_{j}
.

(2)

(
p
-fold sum operator)
f(\lambda)=\big(\Pi_{|J|=p}\lambda_{J}\big)^{\frac{1}{N}}
, where
\lambda_{J}=\lambda_{j_{1}}+\lambda_{j_{2}}+\cdots+\lambda_{j_{p}}
and
N={n\choose p}
.

The first example with
k=n
was studied by Tosatti-Weinkove
[
30
]
and the second example was considered by Harvey-Lawson
[
17
]
related to
p
-geometry/
p
-potential theory. Moreover, we have the following general result due to Leonid Gurvits
[
15
]
(see also
[
16
]
for a proof) that produces a large number of examples of
f
satisfying the determinant domination condition.

Proposition 1.2
.

Let
p(x)
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
N
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Denote
e=(1,\cdots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume that:

(1)

All coefficients of
p
are
\geq 0
,

(2)

p(e)>0
,

(3)

\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{1}}(e)=\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{2}}(e)\cdots=\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{n}}(e)=k
for some
k>0
.

Then
p(x)^{\frac{1}{N}}\geq c(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})^{\frac{1}{n}}
for some
c>0
on
\{x_{1}>0,\cdots,x_{n}>0\}
.

However, the Inverse
\sigma_{k}
-equation does not satisfy the above determinant domination condition.

For a general Hessian equation without the determinant domination condition, the apriori estimates will usually require the existence of a subsolution. Székelyhidi
[
27
]
derived apriori estimates up to
C^{2,\alpha}
assuming the existence of a
\mathcal{C}
-subsolution. In order to prove existence of solutions, the recent work of Guo-Song
[
14
]
shows that one needs a more delicate notion of subsolution. It is no trivial issue to determine whether such subsolutions exist.
Assuming determinant domination condition alleviates this issue since we will always have 0 as a subsolution.

Since there has been many works on the solvability in the smooth category, it is a natural question to find weak solutions. Most of the previous works have centered around
\sigma_{k}
-equation using pluripotential theory. The work by Lu
[
22
]
studied the existence and uniqueness of viscosity solutions to
\sigma_{k}
-equations on bounded domains in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
as well as homogeneous Hermitian manifolds. We generalize this result and prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that Assumption
1.1
holds:

(1)

Let
G\in C(M)
, then there exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\varphi\in C(M)
that solves the following equation in the viscosity sense:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G+c},\,\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma.

(2)

Let
G(x,u)\in C(M\times\mathbb{R})
. Assume that
G
is monotone increasing in
u
, and
f(\lambda[\chi])(x)<e^{G(x,C_{0})}
for some
C_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and any
x\in M
. Then there exists
\varphi\in C(M)
that solves the following equation in the viscosity sense:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)},\,\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma.

Remark 1.3
.

The present result only applies to the case with strictly positive right hand side, and we hope to deal with the degenerate case in subsequent works.

When the right hand side is increasing with respect to
\varphi
, the uniqueness of the solution to Kähler-Einstein equation whose right hand side is in
L^{p}
is proved in
[
23
]
. For the uniqueness of solution to general Hessian equations, we prove:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G\in C(M\times\mathbb{R})
. Assume that
G(x,u_{1})<G(x,u_{2})
for any
x\in M
and
u_{1}<u_{2}
. Then there exists at most one viscosity solution to:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)},\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma.

When the right hand side does not depend on
\varphi
, the uniqueness is more subtle. For the complex Monge-Ampére equation, the uniqueness is proved in
[
19
]
if the right hand side of the equation is in
L^{p}
and has a positive lower bound. For the general Hessian equation, as the first step, we prove:

Proposition 1.4
.

Assume that Assumption
1.1
holds.
Let
G\in C(M)
, then there exists a unique
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that the following equation is solvable in the viscosity sense:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G+c},\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma.

We are not able to prove the uniqueness of the solution
\varphi
and the main obstacle seems to be a lack of understanding of the constant
c
that makes the equation solvable in the viscosity sense. Indeed, for
G\in C(M)
, we may denote
c(G)
to be the above said (unique) constant. It is not very hard to see that
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
implies
c(G_{1})\leq c(G_{2})
. The question that is of interest to us is whether this monotonicity is strict. More precisely:

Question 1.5
.

Assume that
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
,
G_{1}\neq G_{2}
. Do we have
c(G_{1})<c(G_{2})
?

We show that an affirmative answer to Question
1.5
will lead to the uniqueness of viscosity solutions. More precisely:

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
G\in C(M)
. Assume that for any
G^{\prime}\leq G,\,G^{\prime}\neq G
one has
c(G^{\prime})>c(G)
, then there is at most one solution to the following equation in the viscosity sense:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G+c},\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma,\,\,\sup_{M}\varphi=0.

As to Question
1.5
, we observe that the answer is yes if
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
is smooth:

Proposition 1.7
.

If
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
is smooth, then the answer to Question
1.5
is affirmative.

Next we explain our strategy of proof of the above results. For the existence proof, we first approximate the right hand side
G
with a sequence of smooth right hand side
G_{j}
. The apriori estimates developed in Székelyhidi
[
27
]
allows us to solve:

F[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j}]=e^{G_{j}(x)+c_{j}}\text{ or $e^{G_{j}(x,\varphi)}$}.

In order to get a viscosity solution, all we need is to prove that
\varphi_{j}
converges uniformly (at least up to a subsequence). Using that
\varphi_{j}
are all subharmonic and that
\varphi_{j}
are uniformly bounded, we see that
\varphi_{j}
is precompact in
L^{1}
. In order to improve the convergence to uniform convergence, we need the following stability estimate (roughly stated): there exists
a>0
, such that for any
v\in C^{2}(M)
,
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}v]\in\Gamma
,

(1.5)

\sup_{M}(v-\varphi)\leq C||(v-\varphi)_{+}||_{L^{1}}^{a}.

The proof of (
1.5
) is really a variant of the
L^{\infty}
estimate by Guo-Phong-Tong
[
25
]
.

For the uniqueness proof, we need to consider the super/inf convolution, adapted to manifolds. We use the super/inf convolution considered in Cheng-Xu
[
2
]
, and show that it gives a semi-convex/concave approximation of the viscosity solutions.

Finally we explain the organization of this paper.

In Section 2, we explain some basic notations , definitions and some preliminary results that we need later on.

In Section 3, we prove the existence of solution with smooth right hand side.

In Section 4, we prove the stability result, that allows us to improve the
L^{1}
convergence to
L^{\infty}
convergence, thereby proving the existence of a viscosity solution.

In Section 5, we address the uniqueness issues.

2.
Notations and preliminaries

In the following, we denote
d^{c}=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2}(\bar{\partial}-\partial)
, so that one has
\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}=dd^{c}
. The advantage of working with
d
and
d^{c}
is that they are real operators.

The central idea of viscosity solution is to use a
C^{2}
test function to touch the solution from above and below, which we define more precisely in the following:

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\varphi
be a function defined on
M
and
x_{0}\in M
. Let
\psi
be another function defined on an open subset of
M
containing
x_{0}
.

(1)

We say that
\psi
touches
\varphi
from above at
x_{0}
, if there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
such that
\psi(x_{0})=\varphi(x_{0})
and
\psi\geq\varphi
on
U
,

(2)

We say that
\psi
touches
\varphi
from below at
x_{0}
, if there exists an open neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
such that
\psi(x_{0})=\varphi(x_{0})
and
\psi\leq\varphi
on
U
.

The notion of viscosity solution we work with is consistent with Definition 1.5 in Crandall, Ishii and Lions
[
3
]
:

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\varphi\in C(M)
and
\Gamma
be a closed convex symmetric cone in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that contains the first octant. Let
\tilde{\chi}
be a real
(1,1)
form on
M
. We say that
\lambda[\tilde{\chi}+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma
in the viscosity sense, if for any
x_{0}\in M
, and any
C^{2}
function
P
defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
that touches
\varphi
from above at
x_{0}
, one has:

\lambda[\tilde{\chi}+dd^{c}P](x_{0})\in\Gamma.

We call such a function to be
\Gamma
-subharmonic with respect to
\tilde{\chi}
.

Let
f(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
and
\Gamma
be as described in Section 1, we will put
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=f(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi])
. We may interchangably use both notations in the following.

Definition 2.3
.

(1)

Let
\varphi
be an upper semicontinuous function, We say that
\varphi
is a viscosity subsolution to:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)},\,\,\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma,

if for any
x_{0}\in M
and any
C^{2}
function
P
defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
that touches
\varphi
from above at
x_{0}
, one has:

F(\chi+dd^{c}P)(x_{0})\geq e^{G(x_{0},P(x_{0}))},\,\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}P](x_{0})\in\Gamma.

(2)

Let
\varphi
be a lower semicontinuous function, we say that
\varphi
is a viscosity supersolution to

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)},\,\,\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma,

if for any
x_{0}\in M
and any
C^{2}
function
P
defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
that touches
\varphi
from above at
x_{0}
, one has either

\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}P](x_{0})\in\Gamma\text{ and }F(\chi+dd^{c}P)(x_{0})\leq e^{G(x_{0},P(x_{0}))}.

or

\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}P](x_{0})\notin\Gamma.

(3)

We say that a continuous function
\varphi
is a viscosity solution to

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)},\,\,\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi]\in\Gamma,

if
\varphi
is both a viscosity subsolution and a viscosity supersolution.

We will also need the following theorem of Gauduchon
[
13
]
:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\omega
be a Hermitian metric on
M
. Then there exists a unique function
v\in C^{\infty}(M)
such that
\inf_{M}v=0
,
dd^{c}\big(e^{(n-1)v}\omega^{n-1}\big)=0
.

In general, let
\chi
be a real
(1,1)
form on
M
with
\lambda[\chi]\in\Gamma
. We choose coordinates on an open subset of
M
. Define:
F^{i\bar{j}}=\big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{a\bar{b}}}(\chi)\big)^{-1}_{ji}.
That is,
F^{i\bar{j}}\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{q\bar{j}}}(\chi)=\delta_{iq}
. We define:

(2.1)

\Omega=\sqrt{-1}F^{i\bar{j}}dz_{i}\wedge d\bar{z}_{j}.

Then one can verify that the above defined
\Omega
is actually independent of the choice of coordinates. Moreover, from ellipticity, one sees that
\Omega>0
, hence
\Omega
defines a Hermitian metric. Another thing we note that the function
\det g_{i\bar{j}}\det(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\chi))
is also independent of the choice of coordinates. Moreover, for any
u\in C^{2}(M)
, the following formula holds:

(2.2)

\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\chi)u_{i\bar{j}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}=\det g_{i\bar{j}}\det(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\chi))\frac{\Omega^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}u.

We want to mention the following Lemma which is the Lemma 4 in
[
25
]
:

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a concave and homogeneous function of degree one, which satisfies
\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}>0
for any
\lambda
in an admissible cone
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Assume that there is a
\gamma>0
such that

(2.3)

f(\mu)\geq n\gamma^{\frac{1}{n}}(\Pi_{j}\mu_{j})^{\frac{1}{n}},\text{ for all }\mu\in\Gamma_{n}\triangleq\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{1}>0,...,\lambda_{n}>0\}.

Then there exists a constant
\gamma>0
such that
f
satisfies the structural condition:

(2.4)

\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}(\lambda)\geq\gamma,\text{ for all }\lambda\in\Gamma.

Proof.

By the concavity of
f
on
\Gamma
, for any
\lambda,\mu\in\Gamma
we have

(2.5)

f(\mu)\leq f(\lambda)+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-\lambda_{j}+\mu_{j})\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}},

where we have used the homogeneity of degree one assumption on
f
, which implies that
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}=f(\lambda)
. Taking the infimum of the right hand side of
(\ref{e 2.4})
over all
\mu\in\Gamma_{n}
with
\Pi_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}=1
, we see that:

\inf_{\Pi_{j}\mu_{j}=1,\,\mu\in\Gamma_{n}}\big(\sum_{j}\mu_{j}\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\big)\geq\inf_{\Pi_{j}\mu_{j}=1,\,\mu\in\Gamma_{n}}f(\mu)\geq n\gamma^{\frac{1}{n}}.

The last inequality follows from (
2.3
). On the other hand, from the equality case of the arithmetic-geometric inequality, we see that:

\inf_{\Pi_{j}\mu_{j}=1,\,\mu\in\Gamma_{n}}\big(\sum_{j}\mu_{j}\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\big)=n\big(\Pi_{j}\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

Therefore we get:

\Pi_{j}\frac{\partial f(\lambda)}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\geq\gamma.

∎

3.
existence of solution with smooth right hand side

In this section, our goal is to establish:

Theorem 3.1
.

(1)

Assume that
G(x,u)
is smooth, and
G_{u}(x,u)>0
. Then there exists a unique solution to

F\big(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi\big)=e^{G(x,\varphi)}.

(2)

Assume that
G(x)
is smooth, then there is a unique
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
that solves:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x)+c},\,\,\sup_{M}\varphi=0.

The above result probably exists somewhere in the literature, but we were unable to locate the exact reference.
The uniqueness part (of both
\varphi
and
c
) is an easy consequence of maximum principle.

For the existence part, we are going to use a continuity path.
The continuity path when
G_{u}>0
will be:

(3.1)

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{(1-t)(\varphi+B_{0}(x))+tG(x,\varphi)},\,\,t\in[0,1].

If we denote
G(t,x,u)=(1-t)(u+B_{0}(x))+tG(x,u)
, then we see that
G_{u}(t,x,u)
remains positive. In the above,
B_{0}(x)
is chosen so as to make sure
\varphi=0
solves the equation with
t=0
, namely:

F(\chi)=e^{B_{0}(x)}.

When the right hand side does not depend on
\varphi
, we are going to use the following continuity path:

(3.2)

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{(1-t)B_{0}(x)+tG(x)+c_{t}},\,\,t\in[0,1].

Note that in the above, there is a unique constant
c_{t}
for which the solution could exist, for each
t\in[0,1]
. Clearly
c_{0}=0
and
\varphi=0
when
t=0
.
It only remains to establish the openness and closedness.

3.1.
Openness of the coutinuity path

First we consider the openness of (
3.1
). We are going to set up the nonlinear mapping as follows:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}:&\mathbb{R}\times C^{k,\alpha}(M)\rightarrow C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)\\
&(t,\varphi)\mapsto\log\big(F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)\big)-((1-t)(\varphi+B_{0}(x))+tG(x,\varphi)).\end{split}

In the above,
k
is sufficiently large.
By implicit function theorem, all we need is to verify that
D_{\varphi}\mathcal{F}(t_{0},\varphi_{t_{0}})
is an invertible map from
C^{k,\alpha}(M)
to
C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)
, where
t_{0}\in[0,1]
and
\varphi_{t_{0}}
is the solution to (
3.1
) corresponding to
t_{0}
. We can compute that:

\begin{split}D_{\varphi}\mathcal{F}(t_{0},\varphi_{t_{0}}):&C^{k,\alpha}(M)\rightarrow C^{k-2,\alpha}(M),\\
&\psi\mapsto\frac{1}{F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{t_{0}})}\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{t_{0}})\partial_{i\bar{j}}\psi-((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi.\end{split}

Let us denote
L=D_{\varphi}\mathcal{F}(t_{0},\varphi_{0})
and we have:

Lemma 3.1
.

The operator
L
is invertible from
C^{k,\alpha}(M)
to
C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)
.

Proof.

Injectivity is quite easy to see. Indeed, one can look at the point where
\psi
achieves positive maximum or negative minimum. One can take a coordinate chart near that point, so that locally
Lu=0
could be written as:

a_{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}\psi+c\psi=0,\,\,c<0.

We see by (strong) maximum principle that
u
must be a constant and clearly this constant must be zero.

To prove surjectivity, we only need to show that
L
is surjective from
H^{2}
to
L^{2}
. With the right hand side in
C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)
, one can use the standard elliptic regularity theory and work locally to improve the pre-image to be in
C^{k,\alpha}(M)
.
In order to show the surjectivity of
L
from
H^{2}
to
L^{2}
, it will not work well to consider
L
, but we will need to consider
\tilde{L}=e^{(n-1)v_{0}}L
, where
v_{0}
is the Gauduchon factor of the following Hermitian metric:

(3.3)

\tilde{\Omega}=F^{-\frac{1}{n-1}}(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{t_{0}})(\det g\det(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}})(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{t_{0}}))^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Omega.

That is,
dd^{c}\big(e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\big)=0
. In the above,
\Omega
is defined in (
2.1
), evaluated at
\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{t_{0}}
. The existence of
v_{0}
was first proved by Gauduchon
[
13
]
(A modern proof was given by Fu-Wang-Wu in
[
12
]
).
We will be able to show that
\tilde{L}
is surjective (so
L
is surjective) if we can show that:

(1)

Im(\tilde{L})
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}
. For this we just need to show
||\psi||_{H^{2}}\leq C||\tilde{L}\psi||_{L^{2}}
.

(2)

Ker(\tilde{L}^{*})=0
, where
\tilde{L}^{*}:L^{2}\rightarrow H^{-2}
is the adjoint map of
\tilde{L}
and
H^{-2}
is the dual space of
H^{2}
.

To prove (1), first we observe that
L
can be re-written as:

L\psi=\frac{1}{F}\frac{\det g\det(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}})\frac{\Omega^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}\psi}{\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}}-((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi.

Therefore

(3.4)

(\tilde{L}\psi)\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}=e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge dd^{c}\psi-e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}.

Now we multiply both sides by
\psi
and integrate, we see that:

\begin{split}&\int_{M}\psi\tilde{L}\psi\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}=\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge(\psi dd^{c}\psi)-\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi^{2}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}\\
&=\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge(dd^{c}(\frac{\psi^{2}}{2})-d\psi\wedge d^{c}\psi)-\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi^{2}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}\\
&=-\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge d\psi\wedge d^{c}\psi-\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi^{2}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}.\end{split}

At this point, Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we see that:

(3.5)

||\psi||_{L^{2}}+||\nabla\psi||_{L^{2}}\leq C||\tilde{L}\psi||_{L^{2}}.

To get the estimate for the second derivative, we go back to (
3.4
) and re-write it as:

\begin{split}&d\big(e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge d^{c}\psi\big)=(\tilde{L}\psi)\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}+d\big(e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\big)\wedge d^{c}\psi\\
&+e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})\psi\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}.\end{split}

Note that the right hand side is in
L^{2}
now. Writing this equation in local coordinates would be of the form:

\partial_{i}(a_{ij}\partial_{j}u)=k,\,\,k\in L^{2}.

The standard estimate (see, for example, Evans
[
9
]
, Section 6.3, Theorem 1) would give:
||u||_{H^{2}(B_{\frac{1}{2}})}\leq C(||u||_{L^{2}(B_{1})}+||k||_{L^{2}(B_{1})})
. This estimate combined with (
3.5
) gives what we need.

Next we show that
\tilde{L}^{*}
is injective. we can compute that:

(\tilde{L}^{*}v)=\frac{dd^{c}\big(ve^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\big)}{\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}}-e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})v,\,\,v\in L^{2}.

If
\tilde{L}^{*}v=0
, then this equation written locally takes the form:

\partial_{ij}(a_{ij}v)-hv=0,\,\,h\in C^{\infty}(B_{1}),a_{ij}\in C^{\infty},\,\,v\in L^{2}.

Using elliptic theory, we can improve the regularity of
v
to
C^{\infty}
, but then
v
satisfies:

e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge dd^{c}v+2dv\wedge d^{c}(e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1})-e^{(n-1)v_{0}}((1-t)+tG_{u})v\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}=0.

In local coordinates, the above equation reads:

a_{ij}\partial_{ij}v+b_{i}\partial_{i}v+cv=0,\,\,c<0.

If one looks at this equation in a neighborhood of the point where
v
achieves positive maximum or negative minimum, we see from strong maximum principle that
v=0
.
∎

Now we consider the openness of (
3.2
). We set up the nonlinear mapping in a similar way:

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}&:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times C_{0}^{k,\alpha}(M)\rightarrow C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)\\
&(t,c,\varphi)\mapsto\log\big(F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)\big)-(1-t)B_{0}(x)-tG(x)-c.\end{split}

In the above:

C_{0}^{k,\alpha}(M)=\{h\in C^{k,\alpha}(M):\int_{M}h\omega_{0}^{n}=0\}.

Assume that (
3.7
) is solvable with
t=t_{0}
, that is, there exist
(c_{t_{0}},\varphi_{t_{0}})
such that
\mathcal{F}(t_{0},c_{t_{0}},\varphi_{t_{0}})=0
. By implicit function theorem, we just need to verify the linearized map
D_{(c,\varphi)}\mathcal{F}
defines a bijective map from
\mathbb{R}\times C_{0}^{k,\alpha}(M)
to
C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)
. One can compute that:

\begin{split}D_{(c,\varphi)}\mathcal{F}&:\mathbb{R}\times C_{0}^{k,\alpha}(M)\rightarrow C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)\\
&(\lambda,u)\mapsto\frac{1}{F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{t_{0}})}\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\varphi_{t_{0}})\partial_{i\bar{j}}u-\lambda.\end{split}

Denote this linear operator to be
\mathcal{L}
. We then have:

Lemma 3.2
.

\mathcal{L}
is bijective from
\mathbb{R}\times C_{0}^{k,\alpha}(M)
to
C^{k-2,\alpha}(M)
.

Proof.

First, similar as before,
\mathcal{L}
may be written as:

\mathcal{L}(\lambda,u)=\frac{1}{F}\frac{\det g\det(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}})\frac{\Omega^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}u}{\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}}-\lambda=\frac{\frac{\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}u}{\frac{1}{n!}\omega_{0}^{n}}-\lambda.

Here
\tilde{\Omega}
is defined by (
3.3
). Assume that
\mathcal{L}(\lambda,u)=0
, we need to show that
\lambda=0,\,u=0
.
Let
v_{0}
be the Gauduchon factor of
\tilde{\Omega}
, so that
dd^{c}(e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1})=0
. Multiplying by
e^{(n-1)v_{0}}
and integrating gives:

0=\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}u-\int_{M}\lambda e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}=-\lambda\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}.

This proves
\lambda=0
, so that one has
\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\wedge dd^{c}u=0
. Then one can use the strong maximum principle (similar to the argument of part (1)) to show that
u
is a constant. But we are requiring its integral to be equal to zero, so
u=0
.

Next we show that
\mathcal{L}
is surjective. Just as before, it will be sufficient to show that
\mathcal{L}
is surjective from
\mathbb{R}\times H_{0}^{2}
to
L^{2}
. Here
H_{0}^{2}
denotes the element
u\in H^{2}
such that
\int u\omega_{0}^{n}=0
. Also it will be necessary to consider
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)=e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
. We will be able to show
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
is surjective (hence
\mathcal{L}
), if we can show:

(1)

Im(\tilde{\mathcal{L}})
is a closed subspace of
L^{2}
. For this we just need to show that
||u||_{H^{2}}+|\lambda|\leq C||\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(u,\lambda)||_{L^{2}}
.

(2)

Ker(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*})=0
, where
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}:L^{2}\rightarrow H^{-2}\times\mathbb{R}
is the adjoint map of
\mathcal{L}
.

To see (1), first note that one has:

(3.6)

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}=e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}u-\lambda e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}.

Integrating both sides, we get
|\lambda|\leq C||\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)||_{L^{1}}\leq C^{\prime}||\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)||_{L^{2}}
.
Then we multiply (
3.6
) by
u
, and use that
udd^{c}u=dd^{c}(\frac{u^{2}}{2})-du\wedge d^{c}u
, we integrate and find that:

\int_{M}u\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}=-\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}(n-1)!\wedge du\wedge d^{c}u-\int_{M}\lambda ue^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}.

This way we get:

||\nabla u||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon||u||_{L^{2}}^{2}+C_{\varepsilon}(|\lambda|^{2}+||\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)||_{L^{2}}^{2}),\,\,\,\forall\varepsilon>0.

On the other hand, since
\int_{M}u\omega_{0}^{n}=0
, we may use Poincaré inequality to see that
||\nabla u||_{L^{2}}\geq c_{0}||u||_{L^{2}}
. This way we obtain that

||u||_{L^{2}}+||\nabla u||_{L^{2}}\leq C||\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\lambda,u)||_{L^{2}}.

Then we get the second derivative estimates by writing (
3.6
) under local coordinates, and argue in the same way as part (1).

Next, we can find the adjoint map is:

\begin{split}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}&:L^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\times H^{-2}\\
&g\mapsto(-\int_{M}ge^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!},dd^{c}\big(ge^{(n-1)v_{0}}\frac{\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\big)).\end{split}

If
dd^{c}\big(ge^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\big)=0
with
g\in L^{2}
, then one can improve the regularity of
g
and see that
g\in C^{\infty}
. But then

0=\int_{M}gdd^{c}(ge^{(n-1)v_{0}}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1})=-\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v_{0}}dg\wedge d^{c}g\wedge\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}.

This implies
g
is a constant. On the other hand, from
\int_{M}ge^{(n-1)v_{0}}\omega_{0}^{n}=0
, we see that
g=0
. This proves
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{*}
is injective.
∎

3.2.
Closedness of the continuity path

The required apriori estimates we need can be found in Székelyhidi
[
27
]
, who proved that:

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider the Hessian equation
f\big(\lambda[\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}u]\big)=h(x)
, where
f
and
h
satisfy the following assumptions:

(1)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}>0
and
f
is convex.

(2)

\sup_{\partial\Gamma}f<\inf_{M}h
,

(3)

For any
\sigma<\sup_{\Gamma}f
and
\lambda\in\Gamma
we have
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}f(t\lambda)>\sigma
.

Suppose
u
is a (smooth) solution with
\sup_{M}u=0
and
\underline{u}
is a
\mathcal{C}
-subsolution, then we have an estimate
||u||_{2,\alpha}\leq C
, where
C
depends on the given data
M,\,g,\,\chi,\,h
and the subsolution
\underline{u}
.

We are going to apply this result to (
3.2
). Note that by evaluating at the minimum and maximum of
\varphi
, we see that
c_{t}
is actually uniformly bounded in
t
. So we would be able to apply Theorem
3.2
if we can show the existence of a
\mathcal{C}
-subsolution.

Indeed, a
\mathcal{C}
-subsolution simply means a (smooth) function
\underline{u}
, such that for each
x\in M
, the following set is bounded:

(3.7)

\{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma:f(\lambda^{\prime})=h(x),\,\,\text{ and }\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\bar{\partial}\underline{u})\in\Gamma_{n}\}.

In our setting, the situation is simple and we are going to see that
\underline{u}=0
will be a
\mathcal{C}
-subsolution. More precisely:

Lemma 3.3
.

For any
C_{0}>0
and any
x\in M
, we define the following set:

\{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma:f(\lambda^{\prime})\leq C_{0},\text{ and }\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda(\chi)(x)\in\Gamma_{n}\}.

This set is bounded and one can estimate the diameter of this set in terms of
f
,
C_{0}
,
\chi
and the background metric
g
.

Proof.

Denote
\Gamma_{\infty}=\{(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n-1}):\text{$(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\in\Gamma$ for some $\lambda_{n}$}\}.
For any
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n-1})\in\Gamma_{\infty}
, we consider the limit

\lim_{\lambda_{n}\rightarrow+\infty}f(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}).

Trudinger
[
31
]
proved that either this limit is infinite for all
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\infty}
or this limit is finite for all
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\infty}
. Moreover, this convergence is uniform on any compact subset of
\Gamma_{\infty}
. The proof essentially follows from the concavity of
f
. In our setting, we will have the above limit is infinite for all
\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\infty}
. Indeed, since
f
satisfies the determinant domination condition, we see that, for
\lambda_{1}>0,\cdots,\lambda_{n-1}>0
, we have:
f(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})\geq c\big(\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}\rightarrow+\infty
as
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
.

From this, we see that, for each
1\leq i\leq n
, we have:

\lim_{t\rightarrow+\infty}f\big(\lambda[\chi]+t\mathbf{e}_{i}\big)=+\infty,

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the standard basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, since
\lambda[\chi]
is strictly contained in
Int(\Gamma)
as
x
varies over
M
, the above convergence is uniform on
M
. Using the concavity of
f
again, we see that:

\lim_{\mu\in\Gamma_{n},\,\mu\rightarrow\infty}f\big(\lambda[\chi]+\mu\big)=+\infty,

and this convergence is uniform on
M
.
∎

Therefore, we may use Theorem
3.2
to conclude that
||\varphi||_{2,\alpha}
is uniformly bounded in (
3.2
). From the standard elliptic theory, we see that
\varphi
is uniformly bounded in any higher order norm.

Now let us consider the continuity path (
3.1
). First we show that
\varphi
is bounded from above, uniform for
t\in[0,1]
. Indeed, we fix any
t\in[0,1]
, and assume that
\varphi
achieves maximum at
x_{0}
, then at this point, we have

(3.8)

F(\chi)(x_{0})\geq F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)(x_{0})=e^{(1-t)(\varphi+B_{0}(x_{0}))+tG(x_{0},\varphi)}.

Our assumption on
G
was that there exists
C_{0}>0
large enough, such that for all
\varphi\geq C_{0}
, one has

F(\chi)(x)<e^{G(x,\varphi)},\,\,\varphi\geq C_{0}.

Therefore, there exists
C_{0}^{\prime}
, such that for any
t\in[0,1],\,\varphi\geq C_{0}^{\prime}
, one has:

(3.9)

F(\chi)(x)<e^{(1-t)(\varphi+B_{0}(x))+tG(x,\varphi)}.

(
3.8
) and (
3.9
) combined shows that
\varphi(x_{0})\leq C_{0}^{\prime}
, hence
\sup_{M}\varphi\leq C_{0}^{\prime},\,\,t\in[0,1]
.

Once we have a bound of
\sup_{M}\varphi
, we see that the right hand side of (
3.1
) is uniformly bounded. Using Lemma
3.3
, we see that the following set is uniformly bounded in
x\in M
and
t\in[0,1]
:

\{\lambda^{\prime}\in\Gamma:\,\,f(\lambda^{\prime})=e^{(1-t)(\varphi+B_{0}(x))+tG(x,\varphi)}\text{ and $\lambda^{\prime}-\lambda[\chi]\in\Gamma_{n}$}\}

Therefore, Theorem
3.2
carries over in this case (indeed, the monotone increasing dependence on
\varphi
actually helps with the estimates) and gives
||\varphi||_{2,\alpha}
is uniformly bounded. From standard elliptic estimates, we see that
\varphi
is uniformly bounded in any higher order norm.

4.
stability estimate and existence of viscosity solutions

The key stability result we need is the following:

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
v
be a bounded smooth
\Gamma
-subharmonic function, and let
\varphi
be a smooth solution to (
1.1
), (
1.3
) with
h=e^{G_{0}(x)}
and
\sup_{M}\varphi=0
. Then for any
p_{0}>1
, and any
a<\frac{p_{0}-1}{np_{0}+p_{0}-1}
we have

||(v-\varphi)_{+}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq C||(v-\varphi)_{+}||_{L^{1}}^{a}.

Here
C
depends only on
||v||_{L^{\infty}}
,
||e^{nG_{0}}||_{L^{p_{0}}}
, the background metric and the choice of
a
.

Remark 4.2
.

The above estimate uses the
L^{p},\,p>n
norm of the right hand side
e^{G_{0}}
. This is more than enough for us at the moment, but it may be needed in the future.

Let us postpone the proof for the moment, and we first use this proposition to prove existence.

First we look at the case when
G
depends only on
x
.
We can take a sequence of smooth
G_{j}(x)
that converges to
G(x)
uniformly. By Theorem
3.1
, there exists
\varphi_{j}\in C^{\infty}(M)
which is strictly
\Gamma
-subharmonic, that solves:

(4.1)

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j})=e^{c_{j}+G_{j}(x)},\,\,\sup_{M}\varphi_{j}=0.

Our first goal will be to show that
\varphi_{j}
is uniformly bounded. For this we first need to estimate the constants
c_{j}
. Evaluating at the maximum and minimum point of
\varphi_{j}(x)
respectively, we find that:

|c_{j}|\leq\max|G_{j}|+\max|\log F(\chi)|.

Since
G_{j}
approximates
G
uniformly, we see that
\max|G_{j}|
, hence
c_{j}
is uniformly bounded. Next, the uniform
C^{0}
bound of
\varphi_{j}
follows from Székelyhidi’s work
[
27
]
.

Now we look at the situation when
G(x,u)
is monotone increasing in
u
. We can approximate
G(x,u)
uniformly by a sequence of
G_{j}(x,u)
, such that:

(1)

Each
G_{j}(x,u)
is
C^{\infty}
smooth in
x
and
u
,

(2)

\frac{\partial G_{j}}{\partial u}(x,u)>0
,

(3)

There exists
C_{0}>0
, such that for all
j
,
x\in M
and
u>C_{0}
, one has
F(\chi)(x)<e^{G_{j}(x,u)}
.

We can use Theorem
3.1
to conclude that one can solve:

(4.2)

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j})=e^{G_{j}(x,\varphi_{j})}.

Evaluating at the maximum point of
\varphi_{j}
, from point (3) above we see that

\sup_{M}\varphi_{j}\leq C_{0}.

Once we have the uniform upper bound of
\varphi_{j}
, we see that the right hand side
e^{G_{j}(x,\varphi_{j})}
is uniformly bounded from above, from which we can deduce a uniform lower bound for
\varphi_{j}
following Székelyhidi
[
27
]
or Guo-Phong-Tong
[
25
]
.

Next we show that there is uniform
L^{2}
bound for
\nabla\varphi
:

Lemma 4.3
.

There exists a constant
C
, depending only on the
C^{0}
bound of
G
, and also the Hermitian metric
\omega_{0}
, such that

\int_{M}d\varphi_{j}\wedge d^{c}\varphi_{j}\wedge\omega_{0}^{n-1}\leq C.

Proof.

We use the fact that each
\varphi_{j}
is strictly
\Gamma
-subharmonic, and that
\Gamma\subset\Gamma_{1}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\geq 0\}
.
This would imply that:

\Delta_{\omega_{0}}\varphi_{j}\geq-n,\,\,\,\text{ where $\Delta_{\omega_{0}}\varphi_{j}=g^{p\bar{q}}\partial_{p\bar{q}}\varphi_{j}$}.

This is equivalent to:

dd^{c}\varphi_{j}\wedge\omega_{0}^{n-1}\geq-\omega_{0}^{n}.

Let
C_{1}>0
be large enough such that
\varphi_{j}+C_{1}\geq 0
. Then you multiply this to both sides above:

-\int_{M}d\varphi_{j}\wedge d^{c}\varphi_{j}\wedge\omega_{0}^{n-1}+\int_{M}(\varphi_{j}+C_{1})d^{c}\varphi_{j}\wedge d\omega_{0}^{n-1}\geq-\int_{M}(\varphi_{j}+C_{1})\omega_{0}^{n}.

For the middle term, one has

\begin{split}&\int_{M}(\varphi_{j}+C_{1})d^{c}\varphi_{j}\wedge d\omega_{0}^{n-1}=\int_{M}d^{c}\big(\frac{(\varphi_{j}+C_{1})^{2}}{2}\big)\wedge d\omega_{0}^{n-1}=-\int_{M}d\big(\frac{(\varphi_{j}+C_{1})^{2}}{2}\big)\wedge d^{c}\omega_{0}^{n-1}\\
&=\int_{M}\frac{(\varphi_{j}+C_{1})^{2}}{2}dd^{c}\omega_{0}^{n-1}.\end{split}

Therefore, we see that the integral
\int_{M}d\varphi_{j}\wedge d^{c}\varphi_{j}\wedge\omega_{0}^{n-1}
is uniformly bounded.
∎

Hence we are in a position to apply the Rellich compact embedding theorem to conclude that there is a subsequence
\varphi_{j_{k}}
that converges in
L^{1}
.

Before we proceed with the proof, first we explain how to use this to obtain existence:

Corollary 4.4
.

Theorem
1.1
holds.

Proof.

First we prove part (1). We have shown that one can find smooth solutions to

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j})=e^{G_{j}+c_{j}},\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j}]\in\Gamma,\,\,\sup_{M}\varphi_{j}=0.

Now we can use Proposition
4.1
, and take
v=\varphi_{j_{l}}
,
\varphi=\varphi_{j_{k}}
to get:

||(\varphi_{j_{l}}-\varphi_{j_{k}})_{+}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq C||(\varphi_{j_{l}}-\varphi_{j_{k}})_{+}||_{L^{1}}^{a}.

Switching the choice of
v
and
\varphi
gives:

||\varphi_{j_{k}}-\varphi_{j_{l}}||_{L^{\infty}}\leq C||\varphi_{j_{k}}-\varphi_{j_{l}}||_{L^{1}}^{a}.

That is, the subsequence
\varphi_{j_{k}}
actually converges uniformly. Therefore, it is easy to see that their uniform limit will solve the limit equation in the viscosity sense.

Now we look at part (2). We have shown that one can find smooth solutions to

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j})=e^{G_{j}(x,\varphi_{j})},\,\,\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{j}]\in\Gamma.

Moreover, we have seen that
\varphi_{j}
has uniform
C^{0}
bound. Denote
\tilde{c}_{j}=\sup_{M}\varphi_{j}
and define
\tilde{\varphi}_{j}=\varphi_{j}-\tilde{c}_{j}
, so that

\tilde{\varphi}_{j}
solves:

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
A
,
B
be two Hermitian matrices such that
\lambda(g^{i\bar{k}}A_{j\bar{k}}),\,\lambda(g^{i\bar{k}}B_{j\bar{k}})\in\Gamma
. Define
F(h)=f\big(\lambda(g^{i\bar{k}}h_{j\bar{k}})\big)
. Then we have:

\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{j\bar{k}}}(A)B_{j\bar{k}}\geq 0.

Proof.

We first prove this statement, assuming that
g_{i\bar{j}}=\delta_{ij}
and that
A
is diagonal. With this assumption, we then have:

\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{j\bar{k}}}(A)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}(\lambda(A))\delta_{jk}.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\lambda_{n}
, so that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{1}}\geq\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{2}}\geq\cdots\geq\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{n}}
. Therefore, from above we see that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{j\bar{k}}}(A)B_{j\bar{k}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}(\lambda(A))B_{j\bar{j}}\geq\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}(\lambda(A))\mu_{j}.

In the above,
\mu_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
B
, also listed in the increasing order. The inequality above used the Horn-Shur lemma, which says that the vector
(B_{1\bar{1}},\cdots,B_{n\bar{n}})
is contained in the convex hull of
(\mu_{\sigma(1)},\cdots,\mu_{\sigma(n)})
, where
\sigma
is a permutation of the indices. Moreover, since
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}
is in the decreasing order,
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}\mu_{j}
will be minimized if
\mu_{j}
is in the increasing order, hence the inequality above. Now it only remains to show
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}(\lambda(A))\mu_{j}\geq 0
. Indeed, we just need to note that
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{j}}(\lambda(A))\mu_{j}=\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}f(\lambda(A)+t\mu)
. Note that
t\mapsto f(\lambda(A)+t\mu)
is concave, and is bounded from below on
[0,\infty)
, hence
\frac{d}{dt}|_{t=0}f(\lambda(A)+t\mu)\geq 0
.

Next we explain how to reduce the general case to the special case considered above.

First we observe that one can reduce to the case when
g_{i\bar{j}}=\delta_{ij}
. Indeed, we may assume that there is an invertible
n\times n
matrix, such that
g=P\overline{P^{T}}
(that is,
g_{i\bar{j}}=P_{ir}\overline{P_{jr}}
).
On the other hand,

(g^{i\bar{k}}A_{j\bar{k}})^{i}_{j}=\big((g^{-1})_{ki}A_{j\bar{k}}\big)^{i}_{j}=(A\cdot g^{-1})_{ij}=(A\cdot\overline{(P^{T})^{-1}}\cdot P^{-1})_{ij}

Therefore, if we define
\tilde{A}=P^{-1}A\overline{(P^{T})^{-1}}
, then one has
\lambda((g^{i\bar{k}}A_{j\bar{k}})_{j}^{i})=\lambda(\tilde{A})
. Likewise, we define
\tilde{B}=P^{-1}B\overline{(P^{T})^{-1}}
, then one gets
\lambda(\tilde{A}),\,\lambda(\tilde{B})\in\Gamma
. Also we define
\tilde{F}(h)=f\big(\lambda(h)\big)
, where
h
is a Hermitian matrix and
\lambda(h)
means the usual eigenvalue of
h
, then we have
F(h)=\tilde{F}\big(P^{-1}h\overline{(P^{T})^{-1}}\big)
. From this we may calculate:

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{j\bar{k}}}(A)B_{j\bar{k}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial h_{p\bar{q}}}(\tilde{A})\frac{\partial(P^{-1}h\overline{(P^{T})^{-1}})_{p\bar{q}}}{\partial h_{j\bar{k}}}P_{ja}\tilde{B}_{a\bar{b}}\overline{(P^{T})_{ak}}\\
&=\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial h_{p\bar{q}}}(\tilde{A})(P^{-1})_{pj}\overline{(P^{-1})_{qk}}P_{j\bar{a}}\tilde{B}_{a\bar{b}}\overline{(P^{T})_{ak}}=\frac{\partial\tilde{F}}{\partial h_{a\bar{b}}}(\tilde{A})\tilde{B}_{a\bar{b}}.\end{split}

Therefore, we see that, as long as we can verify the lemma with
\tilde{A},\,\tilde{B},\,\tilde{F}
(which is equivalent to taking
g=I
), the general statement would follow.

Next, we explain why we can assume that
A
is diagonal. Indeed, from the definition of
F
, one has
F(h)=F(Uh\overline{U^{T}})
, for any unitary matrix
U
. Therefore one has

\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(h)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{p\bar{q}}}(Uh\overline{U^{T}})U_{pi}\overline{U_{qj}}.

Now we choose
h=A
, and choose
U
to be the unitary matrix such that
UA\overline{U^{T}}
is diagonal. Then one gets:

\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(A)B_{i\bar{j}}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{p\bar{q}}}(UA\overline{U^{T}})U_{pi}\overline{U_{qj}}B_{i\bar{j}}.

All we need to do is to consider
\tilde{B}=UB\overline{U^{T}}
in place of
B
, and they have the same eigenvalues.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
A,\,B
be two positive definite Hermitian matrices. Then we have:

\frac{1}{n}tr\big(\bar{A}^{T}B\big)\geq\big(\det A\cdot\det B\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

Proof.

Since
B
is positive definite and Hermitian, we can find an invertible matrix
P
such that
B=P\bar{P}^{T}
. Then we have:

\frac{1}{n}tr\big(\bar{A}^{T}B\big)=\frac{1}{n}tr\big(\bar{A}^{T}P\bar{P}^{T}\big)=\frac{1}{n}tr\big(\bar{P}^{T}\bar{A}^{T}P\big)\geq\big(\det(\bar{P}^{T}\bar{A}^{T}P)\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}=\big(\det A\cdot\det B\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}.

The inequality above follows from the Arithmetic-Geometric inequalities applied to the eigenvalues of
\bar{P}^{T}\bar{A}^{T}P
.

\begin{split}&A_{\delta,s,\kappa}=||((1-\delta)v-\varphi-s)e^{nG_{0}}||_{L^{\kappa}(\Omega_{\delta,s})}\\
&\leq||((1-\delta)v-\varphi-s)||_{L^{\frac{n+1}{n}q}(\Omega_{\delta,s})}||e^{nG_{0}}||_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega_{\delta,s})}\\
&\leq C(q)A_{\delta,s,\kappa}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}||e^{nG_{0}}||_{L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega_{\delta,s})}\leq C(q)A_{\delta,s,\kappa}^{\frac{1}{n+1}}||e^{nG_{0}}||^{\lambda}_{L^{1}(\Omega_{\delta,s})}||e^{nG_{0}}||^{1-\lambda}_{L^{p_{0}}}.\end{split}

In the second line above, the

will be chosen sufficiently large and
q^{\prime}
is such that
\frac{1}{\kappa}=\frac{n}{n+1}\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}
. By choose
\kappa>1
sufficiently close to 1 and
q
sufficiently large, we may make

arbitrarily close to 1. This just follows from Hölder’s inequality.

In the first inequality of the third line, we used (
4.13
). In the second inequality of the third line,

\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=\lambda+\frac{1-\lambda}{p_{0}}
. By making

sufficiently close to 1, we may make
\lambda
as close to 1 as we want.

Hence we see from (

) that, for any

A_{\delta,s,\kappa}\leq C_{\varepsilon}||e^{nG_{0}}||_{L^{1}(\Omega_{\delta,s})}^{\frac{n+1}{n}-\varepsilon}.

Here

above depends on
||e^{nG_{0}}||_{L^{p_{0}}}
as well.

On the other hand, since

on

Hence the result follows.
∎

We need to use the following Lemma of De Giorgi which was first used in the setting of complex Monge-Ampère equations in
[
18
]
:

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\phi:[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a decreasing function, such that there exists
\mu>0
,
B_{0}>0
,
s_{0}\geq 0
, such that for any
r>0,\,s\geq s_{0}
, one has:

r\phi(s+r)\leq B_{0}\phi(s)^{1+\mu}.

Then
\phi(s)\equiv 0
for
s\geq s_{0}+\frac{2B_{0}\phi(s_{0})^{\mu}}{1-2^{-\mu}}.

Proof.

We can choose a sequence
\{s_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
by induction:

s_{k+1}-s_{k}=2B_{0}\phi(s_{k})^{\mu}.

Then we choose
s=s_{k},\,r=s_{k+1}-s_{k}
, we see that

\phi(s_{k+1})\leq\frac{B_{0}\phi(s_{k})^{1+\mu}}{s_{k+1}-s_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{2}\phi(s_{k}).

That is,
\phi(s_{k})\leq 2^{-k}\phi(s_{0})
, hence:

s_{k+1}-s_{k}\leq 2B_{0}\phi(s_{0})^{\mu}2^{-k\mu}.

Therefore,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(s_{k+1}-s_{k})\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2B_{0}\phi(s_{0})^{\mu}2^{-k\mu}=\frac{2B_{0}\phi(s_{0})^{\mu}}{1-2^{-\mu}}.

It implies that
s_{k}
is increasing and bounded from above. Hence we see that
s_{k}\rightarrow s_{\infty}
. Moreover, for all
s\geq s_{\infty}
, we see that:

\phi(s)\leq\phi(s_{\infty})\leq\phi(s_{k})\leq 2^{-k}\phi(s_{0}).

Letting
k\rightarrow\infty
we see that
\phi(s)\equiv 0
for
s\geq s_{\infty}
. Moreover, one can find that

s_{\infty}\leq s_{0}+\frac{2B_{0}\phi(s_{0})^{\mu}}{1-2^{-\mu}}
.
∎

5.
uniqueness issues

5.1.
sup/inf convolution of the viscosity solution

The basic strategy to prove uniqueness of viscosity solutions will be to perform sup/inf convolution of the solution, so that we get a sub/super solution that is punctually second order differentiable a.e. Then we get the pointwise differential inequality wherever the solution is punctually second order differentiable.

First we explain what we mean by punctually second order differentiable which is different from the usual definition of second order differentiable:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set, and
x_{0}\in U
. Let
\varphi
be a function defined on
U
. We say that
\varphi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
, if there exists a quadratic polynomial
P_{\varphi,x_{0}}(x)
, such that

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}|\varphi(x)-P_{\varphi,x_{0}}(x)|=0.

It would be useful to observe that:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open set and
x_{0}\in U
. Let
\varphi
be a function defined on
U
. Then
\varphi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
if and only if there is a
C^{2}
function
\psi
defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
such that

(5.1)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}|\varphi(x)-\psi(x)|=0.

Moreover, let
\psi_{1},\,\psi_{2}
be any two
C^{2}
functions defined near
x_{0}
that satisfies (
5.1
), then the second order Taylor polynomial of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are equal.

Proof.

If
\varphi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
, then we can take the
C^{2}
function
\psi=P_{\varphi,x_{0}}
which is defined on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Conversely, assume that there is a
C^{2}
function
\psi
defined near
x_{0}
such that
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}|\varphi(x)-\psi(x)|=0
. Let
P
be the Taylor polynomial of
\psi
up to second order, then
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}|\psi(x)-P(x)|=0
. Therefore,
P
will be the quadratic polynomial satisfying the condition in the definition of punctually second order differentiability.

Let
\psi_{1},\,\psi_{2}
be any two
C^{2}
functions satisfying (
5.1
), then

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{0})}|\psi_{1}(x)-\psi_{2}(x)|=0
. This implies the Taylor polynomial of
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
must agree up to second order.
∎

Using this lemma, we observe that the notion of punctually second order differentiability is invariant under diffeomorphism. Indeed, we have:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
U,\,V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be open sets. Let
\Phi:V\rightarrow U
be a smooth map. Let
x_{0}\in U,\,y_{0}\in V
, and
x_{0}=\Phi(y_{0})
. Let
\varphi
be a function defined on
U
which is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
, then
\varphi\circ\Phi
is punctually second order differentiable at
y_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\psi(x)
be a
C^{2}
function defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
with
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\varphi(x)-\psi(x)|=0
. Then
\psi\circ\Phi
will be a
C^{2}
function defined in a neighborhood of
y_{0}
. We wish to show that:

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{y\in B_{r}(y_{0})}|\varphi\circ\Phi(y)-\psi\circ\Phi(y)|=0.

Note that there exists
C_{1}>0
such that
\Phi(B_{r}(y_{0}))\subset B_{C_{1}r}(x_{0})
for all
r>0
small enough. Hence
\sup_{y\in B_{r}(y_{0})}|\varphi\circ\Phi(y)-\psi\circ\Phi(y)|\leq\sup_{x\in B_{C_{1}r}(x_{0})}|\varphi(x)-\psi(x)|
. So the result follows.
∎

If
\varphi
is defined on a manifold, then we can define punctually second order differentiability as follows:

Definition 5.4
.

Let
M
be a manifold and
x_{0}\in M
. Let
\varphi
be a function defined on
M
. We say that
\varphi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
if there exists a coordinate chart around
x_{0}
such that
\varphi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
under this coordinate chart in the sense of Definition
5.1
.

Because of Lemma
5.3
, we see that the notion of punctually second order differentiability is actually independent of the choice of coordinate chart. In other words,
\varphi
would be punctually second order differentiable under any coordinate chart that contains
x_{0}
. Moreover, the Hessian of
\varphi
is well-defined at
x_{0}
, and one defines that to be the Hessian of any
C^{2}
function
\psi
at
x_{0}
, where
\psi
satisfies (
5.1
).
This definition is independent of the choice of
\psi
due to Lemma
5.2
.

One thing we observe is that, if a viscosity subsolution/supersolution is twice differentiable at a point, then at that point, the differential inequality holds in the classical sense.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
G:M\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous
function.

(1)

Let
\varphi\in C(M)
be a viscosity subsolution to
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)}
. Assume that
\varphi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
. Then the following holds in the classical sense:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)(x_{0})\geq e^{G(x_{0},\varphi(x_{0}))},

(2)

Let
\psi\in C(M)
be a viscosity supersolution to
F(\chi+dd^{c}\psi)=e^{G(x,\psi)}
. Assume that
\psi
is punctually second order differentiable at
x_{0}
. Then one has
either

\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\psi](x_{0})\notin Int(\Gamma)

or

\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\psi](x_{0})\in Int(\Gamma)\text{ and }F(\chi+dd^{c}\psi)(x_{0})\leq e^{G(x_{0},\psi(x_{0}))}.

Proof.

First we prove (1). Let
\tilde{\varphi}
be a
C^{2}
function defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
such that
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\varphi(x)-\tilde{\varphi}(x)|=0
.
Then we see that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\tilde{\varphi}(x)+\varepsilon|x-x_{0}|^{2}
would touch
\varphi
from above at
x_{0}
in the sense of Definition
2.1
. By writing
|x-x_{0}|^{2}
, we have chosen some holomorphic coordinate chart near
x_{0}
. Also we may assume that
\delta_{ij}\leq Cg_{i\bar{j}}
on this chart, for some
c>0
.
Since
\varphi
is a viscosity subsolution, we see that:

f\big(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{0})+C\varepsilon\omega_{0}]\big)\geq f\big(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}(P_{\varphi,x_{0}}+\varepsilon|x-x_{0}|^{2})]\big)\geq e^{G(x_{0},\varphi(x_{0}))}.

Let
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we see that
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{0})+C\varepsilon\omega_{0}]\rightarrow\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\varphi}](x_{0})
. Since
f
is a coutinuous function, we see that

f\big(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\varphi}]\big)(x_{0})\geq e^{G(x_{0},\varphi(x_{0}))}.

The proof of (2) is similar. Indeed, let
\tilde{\psi}
be a
C^{2}
function defined in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
such that
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}r^{-2}\sup_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|\psi(x)-\tilde{\psi}(x)|=0
, we know that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
\tilde{\psi}-\varepsilon|x-x_{0}|^{2}
would touch
\psi
from below.

If it happens that
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\psi}](x_{0})\notin Int(\Gamma)
, then we are in the first possibility and we are done.

If
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\psi}](x_{0})\in Int(\Gamma)
, then
\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\tilde{\psi}(x_{0})-C\varepsilon\omega_{0}]\in Int(\Gamma)
for small enough
\varepsilon
. Then the argument is the same as (1), by sending
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and using the continuity of
f
.
∎

In general, a viscosity solution is only continuous, and there is no guarantee that it is punctually second order differentiable anywhere. So our first step would be to find suitable regularization, that makes the original solution a subsolution/supersolution, and that it is punctually second order differentiable a.e.

Henceforth we assume that
\varphi\in C(M)
is a viscosity solution to
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)}
. We define the super convolution of
\varphi
as follows:

(5.2)

\varphi^{\varepsilon}(z)=\sup_{\xi\in T_{z}M}\big(\varphi(\exp_{z}(\xi))+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\xi|_{z}^{2}\big),

where
\exp_{z}(\xi)
is the exponential map at
z
, defined using the metric
\omega_{0}
, and
|\xi|_{z}
denotes the length of the tangent vector
\xi
, again using the metric
\omega_{0}
. Similarly we define the inf convolution of
\varphi
:

(5.3)

\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)=\inf_{\xi\in T_{z}M}\big(\varphi(\exp_{z}(\xi))-\varepsilon+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\xi|_{z}^{2}\big).

A key step is to show that
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
defined above produce viscosity subsolution and supersolution, up to a small error. Also from the definition of
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
, they will be semi-convex and semi-concave, hence punctually second order differentiable a.e. Next we make this precise.
We first verify that
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
defined above are subsolution/supersolution.

Denote
\rho_{\varphi}(r),\,\,0<r<1
to be the modulus of continuity of
\varphi
. That is,
\rho_{\varphi}(r)=\max\{|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|:\,\,d_{g}(x,y)\leq r,\,x,y\in M\}
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\varphi
be a viscosity solution to
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)}
with
G(x,\varphi)
continuous. Then there exist continuous functions

\rho(0+)=0
, and
\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}),\,\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}):(0,1)^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

(1)

\frac{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}
is
\Gamma

\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}
in the viscosity sense, and
F(\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})\geq e^{G(x,\frac{\varphi^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1})

(2)

\frac{\varphi_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}

Here the functions

are determined by
\rho_{\varphi}
,

, the form
\chi
and the background metric.

For the proof of Proposition

or
\varphi_{\varepsilon}

\varphi

∎

In the above, we used the following lemma about the Taylor expansion of the exponential map. This lemma can be found in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 5.13
.

The exponential map on a Hermitian manifold has the Taylor expansion in the following form under local coordinates:

\exp_{z}(\xi)_{m}=g_{m}(z,\zeta)+\sum_{j,k,l}c_{jklm}(\frac{1}{2}\bar{z}_{k}+\frac{1}{6}\bar{\zeta}_{k})\zeta_{j}\zeta_{l}+O\big(|\zeta|^{2}(|z|+|\zeta|)^{2}),

where

g_{m}(z,\zeta)=z_{m}+\zeta_{m}-\sum_{j,l}a_{jlm}z_{j}\zeta_{l}+\sum_{j,k,l,p}a_{jlp}a_{kpm}z_{j}z_{k}\zeta_{l}-\sum_{j,k,l}b_{jklm}(z_{j}z_{k}\zeta_{l}+z_{k}\zeta_{j}\zeta_{l}+\frac{1}{3}\zeta_{j}\zeta_{k}\zeta_{l}),

and
\xi
and
\zeta
are related through:

\zeta_{m}=\xi_{m}+\sum_{j,l}a_{jlm}z_{j}\xi_{l}+\sum_{j,k,l}b_{jklm}z_{j}z_{k}\xi_{l}.

In the above,
(\exp_{z}\xi)_{m}
denotes the
m
-th component of the exponential map under local coordinates.

Finally we observe that
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
and
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable a.e. Indeed, one has:

Lemma 5.14
.

Let
\varphi\in C(M)
, and we define
\varphi^{\varepsilon},\,\varphi_{\varepsilon}
according to (
5.2
) and (
5.3
). Let
x_{0}\in M
and we choose local coordinates in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
. Then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
, and
C_{\varepsilon}>0
, such that:
z\mapsto\varphi^{\varepsilon}(z)+C_{\varepsilon}|z|^{2}
is convex on
U
under the coordinates, and
z\mapsto\varphi_{\varepsilon}(z)-C_{\varepsilon}|z|^{2}
is concave on
U
under the coordinates. In particular,
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
,
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable a.e.

Proof.

We just prove that
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
is semi-convex. The proof that
\varphi_{\varepsilon}
is semi-concave follows similar lines. We can choose
U_{0}
small enough, such that for any
z,\,w\in U_{0}
, there is a unique
\xi\in T_{z}M
such that
\exp_{z}(\xi)=w
. Moreover, we can assume that
\xi
depends smoothly on
z
and
w
, and that
|\xi|^{2}_{z}
is also smooth in
z
and
w
. Therefore, one has, for some neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
(possibly smaller than
U_{0}
):

(5.22)

\begin{split}&\varphi^{\varepsilon}(z)=\sup_{\xi\in T_{z}M}\big(\varphi(\exp_{z}(\xi)+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\xi|^{2}_{z})\big)=\sup_{\xi\in T_{z}M,\,|\xi|<r_{0}}\big(\varphi(\exp_{z}(\xi))+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\xi|^{2}\big)\\
&=\sup_{w\in U_{0}}\big(\varphi(w)+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\xi(z,w)|^{2}_{z}\big),\,\,z\in U.\end{split}

The second equality used Lemma
5.8
on the estimate of
\xi
that achieves the sup. In the last inequality, we noted that for some neighborhood
U
of
x_{0}
, the image of
U_{0}\ni w\mapsto\xi(z,w)
will cover
\{\xi\in T_{z}M:|\xi|_{z}<r_{0}\}
for any
z\in U
.
Note that in (
5.22
), the function
z\mapsto\varphi(w)+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\xi(z,w)|^{2}_{z}
is smooth, and has uniform
C^{2}
bound (in
z
, uniform with respect to
w
). Therefore, taking sup will imply that
\varphi^{\varepsilon}
is semi-convex.
∎

5.2.
when the right hand side has strict monotonicity

In this subsection, we assume that
G(x,\varphi)
is continuous and strictly monotone increasing in
\varphi
. We wish to show that:

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G(x,\varphi)
be continuous, and strictly monotone increasing in
\varphi
. Then there exists at most one viscosity solution to
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G(x,\varphi)}
.

If not, then there exist two viscosity solutions
\varphi_{1},\,\varphi_{2}
, and
\varphi_{1}\neq\varphi_{2}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that:

(5.23)

\kappa_{0}:=\max_{M}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})>0.

Now we consider the super-convolution, applied to
\varphi_{1}
, and the inf-convolution, applied to
\varphi_{2}
. We define:

\kappa_{\varepsilon,a_{1},a_{2}}=\max_{M}\big(\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}\big).

Then it is easy to see that, as
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}\rightarrow 0+
,
\kappa_{\varepsilon,a_{1},a_{2}}\rightarrow\kappa_{0}
. Assume that the above max is achieved at
x_{*}
. Then we have:

Proposition 5.15
.

Assume that
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
are chosen so that
\frac{c_{*}+\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}}<\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}
. Assume also that both
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{*}
. Then one has:

(1)

\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}](x_{*})\in\Gamma
,

(2)

F(\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})(x_{*})\leq F(\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}})(x_{*})
.

In the above,
c_{*}>0
is the constant that allows one to write
\chi=\tilde{\chi}+c_{*}\omega_{0}
with
\lambda(\tilde{\chi})\in\Gamma
.

This proposition allows us to exclude the non-uniqueness of viscosity solutions, as long as
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
,
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are both punctually second order differentiable at
x_{*}
.

Proof.

(of Theorem
5.1
, assuming
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{*}
)
We choose
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
so that
\frac{c_{*}+\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}}<\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}
.
Combining Proposition
5.6
and Proposition
5.15
, we see that at
x_{*}
:

(5.24)

\begin{split}&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\big)(x_{*})\leq f(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}])(x_{*}),\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\big)(x_{*})\geq e^{G(x_{*},\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}(x_{*})}{1+a_{1}})}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}),\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}])\big)(x_{*})\leq e^{G(x_{*},\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}(x_{*})}{1-a_{2}})}+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}).\end{split}

Combining the three inequalities, we see that:

(5.25)

e^{G(x_{*},\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}(x_{*})}{1+a_{1}})}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1})\leq e^{G(x_{*},\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}(x_{*})}{1-a_{2}})}+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}).

On the other hand,
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}(x_{*})}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}(x_{*})}{1-a_{2}}=\kappa_{\varepsilon,a_{1},a_{2}}\rightarrow\kappa_{0}>0
as
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}\rightarrow 0
. This is clearly inconsistent with (
5.25
) when
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
are all small enough.
∎

Now we prove Proposition
5.15
.

Proof.

(Of Proposition
5.15
) Since both
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are differentiable at
x_{*}
, and that
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
achieves maximum at
x_{*}
, we see that:

dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}\geq 0.

Moreover,

\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}=\tilde{\chi}(\frac{1}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{1}{1+a_{1}})+(\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{c_{*}+\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}})\omega_{0}.

Therefore,

(5.26)

\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}\geq\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}+c_{1}\tilde{\chi}+\eta.

In the above,
c_{1}=\frac{1}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{1}{1+a_{1}}>0
,
\lambda(\tilde{\chi})\in\Gamma
,
\eta\geq 0
. Also by Proposition
5.6
, part (1), we also have
\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\in\Gamma
. Therefore, we see that
\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}]\in\Gamma
.

To prove the second part, we wish to use concavity. Indeed, one has:

\begin{split}&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\big)(x_{*})\leq f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}]\big)(x_{*})\\
&+\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}})\big(\frac{\chi_{i\bar{j}}+\rho(\varepsilon)g_{i\bar{j}}}{1+a_{1}}+\frac{(\varphi_{1})_{i\bar{j}}^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}\\
&-\frac{\chi_{i\bar{j}}-\rho(\varepsilon)g_{i\bar{j}}}{1-a_{2}}-dd^{c}\frac{((\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon})_{i\bar{j}}}{1-a_{2}}\big).\end{split}

So we just need to show that:

\begin{split}&\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}})\big(\frac{\chi_{i\bar{j}}+\rho(\varepsilon)g_{i\bar{j}}}{1+a_{1}}+\frac{(\varphi_{1})_{i\bar{j}}^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}\\
&-\frac{\chi_{i\bar{j}}-\rho(\varepsilon)g_{i\bar{j}}}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{((\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon})_{i\bar{j}}}{1-a_{2}}\big)\leq 0.\end{split}

By Lemma
4.7
, we just need to show that
\lambda\big(\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}-dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}\big)\in\Gamma
. However, one can already see this from (
5.26
).
∎

Next we look at the general case, without assuming
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
,
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
punctually second order differentiable at
x_{*}
. For this, we need a perturbation argument from
[
10
]
and
[
22
]
.

First we choose normal coordinate near
x_{*}
, such that
x_{*}
is given by
z=0
. We wish to show that:

Lemma 5.16
.

There exists a neighborhood
U_{0}
of
x_{*}
, and
there exists a sequence
p_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
p_{k}\rightarrow 0
, and a sequence
\delta_{k}>0,\,\delta_{k}\rightarrow 0
, such that one can find a sequence of points
x_{k}\in U_{0}
with the following properties hold:

(1)

x_{k}\rightarrow x_{*}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
,

(2)

\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-<p_{k},z>-\delta_{k}|z|^{2}
has local maximum at
x_{k}
,

(3)

Both
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{k}
.

Another thing we note is that:

Lemma 5.17
.

Define
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}(z)=\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-<p_{k},z>-\delta_{k}|z|^{2}
on
U_{0}
. Then for large enough
k
(
\varepsilon,\,a_{1}
is fixed now)
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
solves the following inequalities on
U_{0}
in the viscosity sense:

\begin{split}&\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}]\in\Gamma.\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}]\big)\geq e^{G(x,\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k})}-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon).\end{split}

We first explain how this implies the uniqueness of viscosity solutions in the general case.

Proof.

(Of Theorem
5.1
, in the general case)
We just need to suitably choose
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
, and choose
k
large enough, then we evaluate at
x_{k}
. Now we know that
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
achieves maximum at
x_{k}
. Moreover, both
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{k}
. Therefore, if we follow the argument of Proposition
5.15
, we see that if we choose
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
so that
\frac{c_{*}+2\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}}<\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}
, we would be able to conclude that:

f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}]\big)(x_{k})\leq f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}]\big)(x_{k}).

Therefore, we see that:

(5.27)

e^{G(x_{k},\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}(x_{k}))}-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon)\leq e^{G(x_{k},\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}(x_{k}))}+\rho_{2}(a_{2},\varepsilon).

Now one passes to limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Note that
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}(x_{k})\rightarrow\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}(x_{*})
, we see that:

e^{G(x_{*},\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}(x_{*}))}-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon)\leq e^{G(x_{*},\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}(x_{*}))}+\rho_{2}(a_{2},\varepsilon).

On the other hand,
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}(x_{*})-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}(x_{*})
is strictly positive and bounded away from zero as
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}\rightarrow 0
. From the strict monotonicity of
G
, we see a contradiction.
∎

Now it only remains to establish the technicalities Lemma
5.16
and
5.17
. We start with Lemma
5.17
.

Proof.

(Of Lemma
5.17
)
Let
x_{0}\in U_{0}
and let
P
be a
C^{2}
function on
U_{0}
that touches
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
from above at
x_{0}
. This would imply that
P+<p_{k},z>+\delta_{k}|z|^{2}
touches
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}
at
x_{0}
. Therefore, we may use Proposition
5.6
, part (1) to conclude that:

\begin{split}&\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}(P+<p_{k},z>+\delta_{k}|z|^{2})]\in\Gamma,\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}(P+<p_{k},z>+\delta_{k}|z|^{2}]\big)\\
&\geq e^{G(x_{0},(P+<p_{k},z>+\delta_{k}|z|^{2})(x_{0}))}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}).\end{split}

On the other hand, it is easy to see that when
k
is large enough (so that
\delta_{k}
is small enough), one has:

\begin{split}&\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}(P+<p_{k},z>+\delta_{k}|z|^{2})\leq\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}P,\\
&e^{G(x_{0},(P+<p_{k},z>+\delta_{k}|z|^{2})(x_{0}))}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1})\geq e^{G(x_{0},P(x_{0}))}-2\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}).\end{split}

Combining, we get:

\begin{split}&\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}P]\in\Gamma,\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}P]\big)\geq e^{G(x_{0},P(x_{0}))}-2\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}).\end{split}

∎

Now we prove Lemma
5.16
.

Proof.

(Of Lemma
5.16
) Without loss of generality, let us assume that
U_{0}=B_{1}(0)
,
x_{*}=0
under the local coordinates. Then this lemma really follows from a lemma of Crandall, Ishii and Lions (
[
4
]
lemma A.3), which states that:

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be semi-convex and
\hat{x}
be a strict local maximum of
\varphi
. For
p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, put
\varphi_{p}(x)=\varphi(x)+<p,x>
. Then for any
r>0,\,\delta>0
, the following set
K
has positive measure:

K=\{x\in B_{r}(\hat{x}):\text{there exists $p\in B_{\delta}(0)$ such that $\varphi_{p}(x)$ has a local maximum at $x$}\}.

We are going to apply this lemma with
\varphi=\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{1}{k}|z|^{2}
. This function will be semi-convex due to Lemma
5.14
. Moreover, we know that
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{1}{k}|z|^{2}
has strict maximum at
x_{*}
(given by
z=0
). Then the above lemma applies.

We will also choose
\delta=\frac{1}{k},\,r=\frac{1}{k}
. Then we can conclude the set of
x\in B_{\frac{1}{k}}(x_{*})
such that
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-\frac{1}{k}|z|^{2}+<p,z>
has local maximum at
x
for some
|p|\leq\frac{1}{k}
has positive measure. Since
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are both punctually second order differentiable a.e., we can find
x_{k}\in B_{\frac{1}{k}}(x_{*})
belonging to the above said set, such that both
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{k}
. If we denote the corresponding
p
to be
p_{k}
, we see that we are done.
∎

5.3.
when the right hand side does not depend on
\varphi

In this subsection, we will assume that the right hand side
G
depends only on
x
. In this case, one can only hope to solve
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G+c}
, for some constant
c
.
Even though we can show that there is a unique
c\in\mathbb{R}
that makes this equation solvable in the viscosity sense, we still don’t have a good enough understanding of this constant, which is the main hurdle to a proof of uniqueness of viscosity solutions in this case.
Let us start with observing the monotonicity of the constant, in terms of the right hand side. More specifically, we have:

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
G_{1},\,G_{2}\in C(M)
with
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
. Assume that there exist
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R},\,\varphi_{i}\in C(M),\,i=1,\,2
, that solve
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{i})=e^{G_{i}+c_{i}}
in the viscosity sense. Then one has
c_{1}\leq c_{2}
.

Proof.

Assume that this is false, namely
c_{1}>c_{2}
. This would imply that
e^{G_{1}+c_{1}}>e^{G_{2}+c_{2}}+\delta_{0}
on
M
, for some
\delta_{0}>0
. Heuristically this would lead to a contradiction if one evaluates at
x_{0}
, where
\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}
achieves maximum. At this point, one would have
\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{1}\leq\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2}
, so that
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{1})\leq F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2})
at
x_{0}
.

To proceed rigorously, one needs to perform the super/inf convolutions considered in Subsection 4.1. One consideres
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
, where
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are defined according to (
5.2
) and (
5.3
).

Assume that the maximum is achieved at
x_{*}
. If both
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are both twice differentiable at
x_{*}
, then one has:

\begin{split}&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\big)(x_{*})\geq e^{G_{1}(x_{*})+c_{1}}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}),\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}]\big)(x_{*})\leq e^{G_{2}(x_{*})+c_{2}}+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}).\end{split}

Since
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
has maximum at
x_{*}
, we see that, if
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
are chosen so that
\frac{c_{*}+\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}}<\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}
, we can follow the argument of Proposition
5.15
to see that:

f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\big)(x_{*})\leq f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}]\big)(x_{*}).

Then one gets:
e^{G_{1}(x_{*})+c_{1}}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1})\leq e^{G_{2}(x_{*})+c_{2}}+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2})
. This is inconsistent with
e^{G_{1}+c_{1}}\geq e^{G_{2}+c_{2}}+\delta_{0}
, if one chooses
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
all small enough, and
\frac{c_{*}+\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}}<\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}
.

In the general case, we can take a coordinate chart in a neighborhood of
x_{*}
, and consider
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}-<p_{k},z>-\delta_{k}|z|^{2}
with
p_{k},\,\delta_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Using the same argument as in the proof of Theorem
5.1
in the general case, we can find a sequence
x_{k}\rightarrow x_{*}
such that both
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}
and
(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{k}
, and the above function has local minimum at
x_{k}
. We still get a contradiction after evaluating at
x_{k}
and passing to the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

A direct consequence of the above proposition is that there is a unique constant
c
that allows for a viscosity solutions:

Corollary 5.19
.

Let
G\in C(M)
, there is at most one constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G+c}
is solvable in the viscosity sense.

Because of this result, we may simply denote this constant to be
c(G)
. Proposition
5.18
then implies that
c(G_{1})\leq c(G_{2})
whenever
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
.

The question that is of crucial importance to us is the following:

Question 5.20
.

Assume that
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
, and
G_{1}\neq G_{2}
. Do we actually have
c(G_{1})<c(G_{2})
?

In order to justify its importance, we are going to show the uniqueness of viscosity solutions, assuming we have an affirmative answer to Question
5.20
. More precisely:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
G\in C(M)
. Assume that for any
G^{\prime}\in C(M),\,G^{\prime}\leq G
and
G^{\prime}\neq G
, one has
c(G^{\prime})>c(G)
. Then there is at most one viscosity solutions to
F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)=e^{G+c}
.

Before presenting the proof in full rigor, let us first explain heuristically how the above said strict monotonicity helps.

Let
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
be two viscosity solutions to the equation. Denote
E=\{x\in M:\varphi_{2}(x)-\varphi_{1}(x)=\min_{M}(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})\}
. Clearly
E
is a compact subset of
M
and we will be done if we can show
E=M
. Assume otherwise, we can take
\delta>0
small enough, such that
E_{\delta}\neq M
, where
E_{\delta}
denotes the
\delta
-neighborhood of
E
.

Now we can define
\tilde{G}\in C(M)
as follows:
e^{\tilde{G}}=e^{G}
on
E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
,
e^{\tilde{G}}=\frac{1}{2}e^{G}
outside
E_{\delta}
, and
\tilde{G}\leq G
on
M
. Let
\eta
be a viscosity solution to:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\eta)=e^{\tilde{G}+\tilde{c}},\,\,\sup_{M}\eta=0.

From the previous section, we know such
\eta
exists.
From the assumption, we know that
\tilde{c}>c
. Let
0<r<1
, we consider the minimum of
\varphi_{2}-((1-r)\varphi_{1}+r\eta)
and assume that it is achieved at
x_{\delta,r}
. Evaluating at
x_{\delta,r}
, we would have:

f\big(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2}]\big)\geq f\big(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}(1-r)\varphi_{1}+r\eta]\big)\geq(1-r)f(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{1}])+rf(\lambda[\chi+dd^{c}\eta]).

This would mean that:

(5.28)

e^{G+c}(x_{\delta,r})\geq(1-r)e^{G+c}(x_{\delta,r})+re^{\tilde{G}+\tilde{c}}(x_{\delta,r}).

On the other hand, for fixed
\delta
, the minimum of
\varphi_{2}-((1-r)\varphi_{1}+r\eta)
can only be achieved in
E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
, as long as
r
is small enough, but then one would have
e^{\tilde{G}}(x_{\delta,r})=e^{G}(x_{\delta,r})
, and this is inconsistent with (
5.28
).

Next we are going to make the above argument rigorous.

As before, in place of
\varphi_{1}
, we wish to consider
\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}
,
\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}},\,\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
, with parameters
\varepsilon,\,a_{i},\,i=1,\,2
small enough, then similar arguments as before shows the following:

Lemma 5.21
.

There exist continuous functions
\rho(\varepsilon),\,\rho_{i}(\varepsilon,a_{i}),\,i=1,2
with
\rho(0+)=0,\,\rho_{i}(0+,0+)=0,\,i=1,2
, such that:

(1)

\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}
solves the following in the viscosity sense:

\begin{split}&\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\in\Gamma,\\
&F(\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})\geq e^{G+c}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}).\end{split}

(2)

\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
solves
F(\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}})\leq e^{G+c}+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2})
in the viscosity sense.

(3)

\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}
solves
\lambda[\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\in\Gamma
and
F(\frac{\chi+\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})\geq e^{\tilde{G}+\tilde{c}}-\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{1})
in the viscosity sense.

Next we consider the maximum point of
((1-r)\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}+r\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
. Assume that the maximum is achieved at
x_{*}
, then we have:

Lemma 5.22
.

For any fixed
\delta>0
,
x_{*}\in E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
as long as
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2},\,r
are all small enough.

Proof.

We observe that for
x\notin E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
, there exists
\delta^{\prime}>0
, such that

\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\leq\max_{M}(\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2})-\delta^{\prime},

On the other hand, we know that as
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2},\,r\rightarrow 0
, we have that
((1-r)\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}+r\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
will converge to
\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}
uniformly, so if the parameters are all small enough and
x\notin E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
, one would have:

\big((1-r)\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}+r\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}\big)-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}\leq\max_{M}\big(\big((1-r)\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}+r\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}\big)-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}\big)-\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{2}.

This proves
x_{*}\in E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
.
∎

In order to fix the issue that the functions
(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon},\,\eta^{\varepsilon},\,(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are not punctually second order differentiable at
x_{*}
, we need to consider slight perturbations of the original function. As before, we take a coordinate chart near
x_{*}
, and we have the following analogue of Lemma
5.16
:

Lemma 5.23
.

There exists a neighborhood
U_{0}
of
x_{*}
, and there exists a sequence
p_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
p_{k}\rightarrow 0
, and a sequence
\delta_{k}>0,\,\delta_{k}\rightarrow 0
, such that one can find a sequence of
x_{k}\in U_{0}
, such that if we define
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}:=\frac{(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}-<p_{k},z>-\delta_{k}|z|^{2}
, we have:

(1)

x_{k}\rightarrow x_{*}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
,

(2)

((1-r)\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}+r\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}})-\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}
has local maximum at
x_{k}
,

(3)

(\varphi_{1})^{\varepsilon},\,\eta^{\varepsilon},\,(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}
are all punctually second order differentiable at
x_{k}
.

With this preparation, we are ready to present the rigorous proof of Theorem
5.2
:

Proof.

(Of Theorem
5.2
)
From Lemma
5.21
and the definition of
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
, we see that
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
solves the following equation in the viscosity sense:

(5.29)

\begin{split}&\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}]\in\Gamma,\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}]\big)\geq e^{G+c}-\rho_{1}(\varepsilon,a_{1}).\end{split}

From Lemma
5.21
, part (3), we also have:

(5.30)

\begin{split}&\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\in\Gamma,\\
&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\frac{\eta^{\varepsilon}}{1+a_{1}}]\big)\geq e^{\tilde{G}+\tilde{c}}-\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{1}).\end{split}

Since
x_{k}\rightarrow x_{*}
and
x_{*}\in E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
, we see that
x_{k}\in E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
for large enough
k
. Moreover, since
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
and
\eta^{\varepsilon}
are punctually second order differentiable at
x_{k}
, we can evaluate (
5.29
), (
5.30
) at
x_{k}
and see that:

(5.31)

\begin{split}&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}((1-r)\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}+r\eta^{\varepsilon})]\big)(x_{k})\\
&\geq(1-r)f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k})])(x_{k})+rf\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}\eta^{\varepsilon}]\big)(x_{k})-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon)\\
&\geq(1-r)e^{G+c}(x_{k})+re^{\tilde{G}+\tilde{c}}(x_{k})-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon)=(1-r)e^{G+c}(x_{k})+re^{G+\tilde{c}}(x_{k})-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon).\end{split}

In the first inequality above, we used the concavity of
f
. In the second inequality above, we used that
\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}
and
\eta^{\varepsilon}
are subsolutions. Also since
x_{k}\in E_{\frac{\delta}{2}}
, we have
\tilde{G}(x_{k})=G(x_{k})
.
On the other hand, if one chooses the parameters such that:

\frac{c_{*}+2\rho(\varepsilon)}{1+a_{1}}\leq\frac{c_{*}-\rho(\varepsilon)}{1-a_{2}},

we then have (similar to Proposition
5.15
):

\begin{split}&f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi+2\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1+a_{1}}+dd^{c}((1-r)\psi_{\varepsilon,a_{1},k}+r\eta^{\varepsilon})]\big)(x_{k})\leq f\big(\lambda[\frac{\chi-\rho(\varepsilon)\omega_{0}}{1-a_{2}}+dd^{c}\frac{(\varphi_{2})_{\varepsilon}}{1-a_{2}}]\big)(x_{k})\\
&\leq e^{G+c}(x_{k})+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}).\end{split}

Therefore, we finally obtain that:

(5.32)

(1-r)e^{G+c}(x_{k})+re^{G+\tilde{c}}(x_{k})-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon)\leq e^{G+c}(x_{k})+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}).

First we let
k\rightarrow\infty
and use that
x_{k}\rightarrow x_{*}
, we see that

(1-r)e^{G+c}(x_{*})+re^{G+\tilde{c}}(x_{*})-2\rho_{1}(a_{1},\varepsilon)\leq e^{G+c}(x_{*})+\rho_{2}(\varepsilon,a_{2}).

Note that if
r>0
and fixed,
e^{G+c}(x_{*})
is strictly less than
(1-r)e^{G+c}(x_{*})+re^{G+\tilde{c}}(x_{*})
. Thereby we get a contradiction with (
5.32
) after
\varepsilon,\,a_{1},\,a_{2}
are chosen sufficiently small.
∎

Now let us go back to Question
5.20
. The only positive examples we know are the classical ones: if
\omega_{0}
is a Kähler metric and if
\chi
is closed, so the equation could be written as:

\big(\frac{(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi)^{k}\wedge\omega_{0}^{n-k}}{\omega_{0}^{n}}\big)^{\frac{1}{k}}=e^{G+c},\,\,\,1\leq k\leq n.

Then we can see that:

e^{kc}=\frac{\int_{M}\chi^{k}\wedge\omega_{0}^{n-k}}{\int_{M}e^{kG}\omega_{0}^{n}}.

Therefore, we see that Question
5.20
indeed holds in this case. Another observation we make is that, if either one of them is smooth, then Question
5.20
also has an affirmative answer:

Lemma 5.24
.

If either
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
is smooth, then the answer to Question
5.20
is yes.

Proof.

First we assume that both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are smooth. Assume otherwise, that is,
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are both smooth,
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
,
G_{1}\neq G_{2}
, but still
c(G_{1})=c(G_{2})
. Let us denote this constant to be
c
. Then we have:

F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{1})=e^{G_{1}+c},\,\,F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2})=e^{G_{2}+c}.

Subtracting and using the concavity of
F
, we get:

e^{G_{2}+c}-e^{G_{1}+c}=F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2})-F(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{1})\geq\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}}(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2})(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})_{i\bar{j}}.

Therefore, if we define

\tilde{\Omega}=\big(\det g\det(\frac{\partial F}{\partial h_{i\bar{j}}})(\chi+dd^{c}\varphi_{2})\big)^{\frac{1}{n-1}}\Omega,

where
\Omega
is defined in (
2.1
),
then the above equation can be written as:

\frac{\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})\leq(e^{G_{2}+c}-e^{G_{1}+c})\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}.

Now let
v
be the Gauduchon factor corresponding to
\tilde{\Omega}
, that is
dd^{c}\big(e^{(n-1)v}\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}\big)=0
, then we have:

0=\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v}\frac{\tilde{\Omega}^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}\wedge dd^{c}(\varphi_{2}-\varphi_{1})=\int_{M}e^{(n-1)v+c}(e^{G_{2}}-e^{G_{1}})\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!}\leq 0.

Here we use the fact that
c(G_{1})=c(G_{2})
and
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
. This implies
G_{1}=G_{2}
, a contradiction.

Next we consider when only one of
G_{1}
or
G_{2}
is smooth. Assume, say,
G_{1}
is smooth,
G_{1}\geq G_{2}
, and
G_{1}>G_{2}
on some open set
U
. By adding a bump function supported on
U
, it is easy to find a smooth function
G_{2}^{\prime}
such that
G_{1}\geq G_{2}^{\prime}\geq G_{2}
, and
G_{1}\neq G_{2}^{\prime}
. Then from the strict monotonicity in the smooth case, we can conclude that:

c(G_{1})<c(G_{2}^{\prime})\leq c(G_{2}).

The other possibility that
G_{2}
is smooth can be dealt with similarly.
∎
