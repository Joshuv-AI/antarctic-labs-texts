When action is not least for systems with action-dependent Lagrangians
Joseph Ryan

Abstract
The dynamics of some non-conservative and dissipative systems can be derived by calculating the first variation of an action-dependent action, according to the variational principle of Herglotz. This is directly analogous to the variational principle of Hamilton commonly used to derive the dynamics of conservative systems. In a similar fashion, just as the second variation of a conservative system’s action can be used to infer whether that system’s possible trajectories are dynamically stable, so too can the second variation of the action-dependent action be used to infer whether the possible trajectories of non-conservative and dissipative systems are dynamically stable. In this paper I show, generalizing earlier analyses of the second variation of the action for conservative systems, how to calculate the second variation of the action-dependent action and how to apply it to two physically important systems: a time-independent harmonic oscillator and a time-dependent harmonic oscillator.

The principle of stationary action is a powerful unifying principle for physics. The dynamics of any conservative system, whose configuration at a given time
t

\vec{x}(t)=\left(x_{1}(t),x_{2}(t),...,x_{n}(t)\right)^{\rm T}

S=\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}L\left(\vec{x}(t),\vec{v}(t)\right)dt,

(1)

(2)

where

;

\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}=0.

(3)

Herglotz variational principle

II.1
First variation

To obtain the equation(s) of motion, it is necessary to calculate the functional derivative, not of an integral equation, but of a differential equation

\frac{dS}{dt}=L\left(t,x_{i},\dot{x}_{i},S\right),

(5)

with the initial condition on the action being
S(t_{i}=0)=:S_{t_{i}}
.
Guenther, R. B., Guenther, C. M., and Gottsch,
J. A. 1996
The reader should note here that the action
S
defined in Eq. (
5
) depends on the time
t
, unlike the action
S
defined in Eq.
1
, which does not (assuming
t_{f}
and
t_{i}
are not allowed to vary). In the rest of this paper,
t_{i}=0
. We want
S
to be stationary with respect to variations of the form shown in Eq. (
2
), and by “stationary” I mean that

\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\{\epsilon\rightarrow 0,\hskip 2.84526ptt=t_{f}\}}=0,

(6)

which is similar to, but not identical with, the condition that conservative systems must satisfy. In contrast to the standard conservative action, the first variation of the action vanishes only at the final time
t_{f}
.
Guenther, R. B., Guenther, C. M., and Gottsch,
J. A. 1996
Taking the functional derivative of Eq. (
5
), we find

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left[\frac{dS}{dt}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{i}}y_{i}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\dot{y}_{i}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial S}\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0},

where the partial derivatives of
L
are evaluated in the limit
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
(that is, on the trajectory that solves the equations of motion). In the first line of Eq. (
7
), the operators
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{d}{d\epsilon}
commute because
\epsilon
is independent of time. If we define

f:=\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0},

(8)

and

B:=\frac{\partial L}{\partial S},

(9)

then Eq. (
7
) becomes

\frac{df}{dt}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{i}}y_{i}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\dot{y}_{i}+Bf.

(10)

This equation has the form
\frac{df}{dt}=\left(...\right)+Bf
, suggesting that the solution should be something like

f=Ae^{\int B\left(\tau\right)d\tau}+{\rm const},

(11)

where
A
is an undetermined function of time. The derivative of

is

\frac{df}{dt}=\frac{dA}{dt}e^{\int B\left(\tau\right)d\tau}+Bf,

(12)

and by comparing this equation to Eq. (
10

11
) solves Eq. (
10
) if

\frac{dA}{dt}:=\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{i}}y_{i}+\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\dot{y}_{i}\right)e^{-\int B(\tau)d\tau}

(13)

More formally, the solution of Eq. (
10

f(t_{f})=Ae^{\int_{0}^{t_{f}}B(\tau)d\tau}+f_{i},

(14)

where
f_{i}:=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}S_{t_{i}}\big|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}
.

Because
S_{t_{i}}
is independent of
x
, it follows that
\frac{d^{n}}{d\epsilon^{n}}S_{t_{i}}\big|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}=0
, where

is an integer, so

.
Vermeeren, Bravetti, and Seri 2019
At time
t_{f}
, we demand
\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\big|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}=0
, so

vanishes and Eq.

(15)

Integrating the second term in parentheses in Eq. (
15

y_{i}(0)=y_{i}(t_{f})=0
to discard the boundary terms, we find

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int^{t_{f}}_{0}\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_{i}}y_{i}e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}B(\tau_{1})d\tau_{1}}d\tau_{2}

(16)

\displaystyle-\int^{t_{f}}_{0}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}y_{i}e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}B(\tau_{1})d\tau_{1}}-B\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}y_{i}e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}B(\tau_{1})d\tau_{1}}\right]d\tau_{2},

which requires

For conservative systems, the first variation of the action is typically used to derive the equation(s) of motion, and the second variation of the action can be used to infer whether the system is dynamically stable, as I mentioned above.
Gutzwiller 1990
;
Gray and Taylor 2007
;
Papastavridis 1983
To see how this works, consider a trajectory
\vec{x}_{0}(t)
that satisfies a given system’s equation(s) of motion. As shown in Ref.
37
, the second variation of the action for a conservative system is minimized, for all variations
\{\epsilon\vec{y}(t)\}
away from the stationary path, as long as
t_{f}<t_{\rm KF1}
, where
t_{f}
is the final time and “KF1" stands for “first kinetic focus". As defined in Refs.
37
and
42
, a kinetic focus (also known as a “conjugate point”
Gray and Poisson 2011
) is a point along
\vec{x}_{0}(t)
at which a nearby trajectory coalesces with it in the limit that the difference of the initial velocities of both trajectories vanishes. More precisely, a kinetic focus is a point in time on
\vec{x}_{0}(t)
that satisfies

{\rm det}\left[\frac{\partial\vec{x}_{0}(t)}{\partial\vec{v}_{0}}\right]=0,

(18)

where
\vec{v}_{0}
is the initial velocity of the particle following
\vec{x}_{0}(t)
(note that, for one-dimensional trajectories, Eq. (
18
) reduces to
\frac{\partial x_{0}(t)}{\partial v_{0}}=0
). From these equations, we can see that kinetic foci play an important role in characterizing the dynamical stability of a given system. In a dynamically unstable system, two nearby trajectories with similar initial velocities will move farther apart as time goes on. The possible trajectories spread, or “fan out”
Gutzwiller 1990
;
Gray and Taylor 2007
in a dynamically unstable system, whereas the possible trajectories of a system that is dynamically stable will converge, in the sense described above.
Gutzwiller 1990
;
Gray and Taylor 2007
When
\vec{x}_{0}(t)
reaches its first kinetic focus, the second variation of the system’s action will be minimized for all but one of the variations away from
\vec{x}_{0}(t)
. This special variation (call it
\epsilon\vec{y}_{*}(t)
) will be another true trajectory of the system, because it satisfies the system’s equation(s) of motion.
Gray and Taylor 2007
;
Gray and Poisson 2011
Further, when
t_{f}>t_{\rm KF1}
, the second variation of the system’s action will be maximized with respect to
\epsilon\vec{y}_{*}(t)
, and minimized with respect to all other variations (until the system reaches another kinetic focus; for more details see Refs.
37
;
42
). In general, then, the action of a conservative system is minimized if
t_{f}<t_{\rm KF1}
, and is a saddle point if
t_{f}>t_{\rm KF1}
.
Gray and Taylor 2007
;
Papastavridis 1983

To calculate the second functional derivative of the Herglotz action, we take the functional derivative of Eq. (

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\frac{dS}{d\epsilon}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}

(23)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left[\frac{df}{dt}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{d\tilde{A}}{d\epsilon}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}+\left[\frac{dB}{d\epsilon}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}f+B\left[\frac{df}{d\epsilon}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0},

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left[\frac{df}{dt}\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}

(32)

\displaystyle+2\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial S}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{j}y_{i}+\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial S^{2}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}\bigg)e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}Bd\tau_{1}}d\tau_{2}.

Eq. (
32
) is the general form of the second variational derivative of the Herglotz action. Note that, if the Lagrangian does not depend on the action, then Eq. (
32
) reduces to

f^{\prime}=\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}\dot{x}_{j}}\dot{y}_{i}\dot{y}_{j}+2\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{i}\dot{y}_{j}+\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}\right)d\tau_{1},

(33)

which is the second variation of the action for a conservative system.

In Sec.
III
, I show, following Ref.
37
, that the second variation of the Herglotz action vanishes for a particular variation when the system’s trajectory terminates at a kinetic focus. I then show that the Herglotz action is a saddle point when the system’s trajectory terminates after a kinetic focus.

III
The Herglotz action and kinetic foci

In this section I examine the necessary and sufficient conditions for the second variation of the Herglotz action to vanish, for the first time, at the first kinetic focus on a given trajectory. The sufficient condition in this case is just the same as the sufficient condition for conservative systems proved in Ref.
37
. In that paper the authors showed that, given a stationary path (call it
x_{0}
), there is a point in time (
t_{\rm KF}
) at which the second variation of the action along
x_{0}
vanishes, this being the point at which a nearby stationary path (call it
x_{1}
) coalesces with
x_{0}
in the limit that their initial velocities are made equal. The argument in Ref.
37
makes no reference to the specific form of the action, only requiring that
\delta^{2}S_{0}>0
for
t<t_{\rm KF}
. As long as
\delta^{2}S_{0}>0
for
t<t_{\rm KF}
, where in this case

is the Herglotz action, then by the same logic the sufficient condition for
\delta^{2}S
to vanish is that the given stationary path ends at
t_{\rm KF}
.

The necessary condition will require a little more work, but this can be done along the same lines as Ref.
37

x_{0}
be

, and let the action along

S_{0}=S_{1}+\delta S_{1}+\delta^{2}S_{1}+\delta^{3}S_{1}+...

(34)

S_{1}=S_{0}+\delta S_{0}+\delta^{2}S_{0}+\delta^{3}S_{0}+...

(35)

In Eqs. (

35

\delta^{(n)}S_{m}=\left(-1\right)^{nm}\frac{\epsilon^{n}}{n!}\left[\frac{d^{n}}{d\epsilon^{n}}S\right]\bigg|_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}

(36)

with

. The factor of

x_{0,i}

If we subtract Eq. (

To summarize the results of this section, the second variation of the Herglotz action of a non-conservative system vanishes for the first time when the trajectory of the system terminates at that trajectory’s first kinetic focus. The Herglotz action is a saddle point (

37

for a conservative system, only with somewhat more complicated equations. One convenient method for calculating the sign of
\delta^{2}S

43
and
52

(Eq.

. I will apply this method to Eq. (

IV
Eigenvalue analysis of second variation of Herglotz action

First, because we’re interested in the sign of
f^{\prime}
, we needn’t worry about the
e^{\int Bd\tau_{2}}
term outside the integral. Because the exponential is positive-definite along the system’s entire trajectory, it will affect the amplitude of
f^{\prime}
but not the location of its roots, so it won’t have any effect on when the sign of
f^{\prime}
changes (assuming, as I do throughout this paper, that
B(t)
is a real-valued function). We can therefore define a new quantity

\delta^{2}\mathcal{A}:=f^{\prime}e^{-\int Bd\tau_{3}}

(57)

and determine how the sign of this quantity changes with
t
. We want to determine the sign of

\displaystyle\delta^{2}\mathcal{A}=\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}\dot{x}_{j}}\dot{y}_{i}\dot{y}_{j}

\displaystyle+2\left[\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{i}+\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial S\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{i}\right]\dot{y}_{j}+\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}

(58)

\displaystyle+2\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x_{i}\partial S}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{j}y_{i}+\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial S^{2}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{i}}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{x}_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}\bigg)e^{-\int_{0}^{\tau_{2}}Bd\tau_{1}}d\tau_{2}.

Following Refs.
43
and
52
, I will define

R_{ij}:=r_{ij}+BQ_{ji}+\frac{B^{2}}{4}P_{ij}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{dB}{dt}P_{ij}-\frac{B}{2}\frac{dP_{ij}}{dt},

(59)

and

\Lambda_{ij}:=-\frac{d}{dt}\left(P_{ij}\frac{d}{dt}+Q_{ji}\right)+\left(Q_{ij}\frac{d}{dt}+R_{ij}\right),

(60)

where
P_{ij}
,
Q_{ij}
and
r_{ij}
are defined in Eqs. (
40
-
42
). One can then show (see Appendix
A
), that Eq. (
58
) takes the form

\delta^{2}\mathcal{A}=\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{i}\Lambda_{ij}\eta_{j}\hskip 1.42262ptd\tau_{2}

(61)

where the
\{\eta_{i}\}
functions are defined to be

\eta_{i}:=y_{i}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}Bd\tau_{1}}.

(62)

By defining the inner product

\left(\eta_{i},\eta_{j}\right):=\int\eta_{i}\eta_{j}\hskip 1.42262ptd\tau,

(63)

and then expanding the
\{\eta_{i}\}
functions in an orthonormal basis
\{b_{in}\}

\eta_{i}=\sum_{n}\beta_{n}b_{in},

(64)

with

\int b_{n}b_{m}d\tau=\delta_{nm},

(65)

then Eq. (
58
) takes the form

\delta^{2}\mathcal{A}=\sum_{n,m}A_{nm}\beta_{n}\beta_{m},

(66)

where

A_{nm}=\left(b_{n},\Lambda b_{m}\right)=\int b_{in}\Lambda_{ij}b_{jm}d\tau.

(67)

If the basis diagonalizes
A_{nm}
, then

\Lambda_{ij}b_{jn}=\lambda_{n}b_{jn}.

(68)

We have now reduced the problem of determining the sign of
\delta^{2}\mathcal{A}
to a linear eigenvalue problem. If all the eigenvalues are positive at time
t
, then the action is minimized up to that time. If one of the eigenvalues (
\lambda_{n}
) vanishes at time
t
, then the action along the trajectory of the
n^{\rm th}
variation vanishes, and
t
is a kinetic focus. If one of the eigenvalues (
\lambda_{n}
) is negative at time
t
, then the action is maximized along the trajectory of the
n^{\rm th}
variation.
Papastavridis 1983
Determining the sign of the Herglotz action is then just a matter of working out the eigenvalue spectrum
\{\lambda_{n}\}
from Eq. (
68
). To illustrate the use of this technique, I will make the simplifying assumption that the Herglotz Lagrangian takes the form shown in Eq. (
55
). Then Eq. (
68
) becomes

\left(-P\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}+R\right)b_{n}=\lambda_{n}b_{n},

(69)

where

P=\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\dot{x}^{2}}=m,

(70)

and

R=\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}\left(g^{2}-2\frac{dg}{dt}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}L}{\partial\dot{x}^{2}}=-\frac{\partial^{2}U}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{m}{4}\left(g^{2}-2\frac{dg}{dt}\right).

(71)

Eq. (
69
) is, as described in Ref.
43
, directly analogous to the eigenvalue equation for a particle in a box with an effective mass
m=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2P}
moving in an effective potential

\mathcal{U}=\begin{cases}R&\text{if}\ 0<t<t_{f},\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\infty&\text{if}\ t=0\hskip 2.84526pt{\rm or}\hskip 2.84526ptt=t_{f},\\
\end{cases}

(72)

with
t
playing the role that
x
ordinarily plays in time-independent perturbation theory. Inserting Eqs. (
70
) and (
71
) into Eq. (
69

\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{U}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{g^{2}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{dg}{dt}\right)b_{n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{m}b_{n},

(73)

where
\tilde{U}:=\frac{U}{m}
. In general,
\tilde{U}
is a function of the solution

of the equation of motion given by Eq. (
17
). If this solution is known, then one can write
\tilde{U}
explicitly as a function of time, and then attempt to solve Eq. (
73
). In the special case that
\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{U}}{\partial x^{2}}=F(t)+C
, where
F(t)
is a function only of time and
C
is a constant, one can solve the eigenvalue spectrum of the non-conservative system without solving the equation of motion beforehand. Eq. (
73

Hussein et al. 1980
;
Schumacher, Benjamin and Westmoreland, Michael
D. 2010
;
Shankar, Ramamurti 1994

IV.1
Harmonic oscillator with time-independent damping

For an oscillator with time-independent damping, let
g(t)=-a
and
\tilde{U}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{2}x^{2}
, where
a
and
\omega_{0}
are real-valued constants. In this case Eq. (
73
) reduces to

\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}-\omega_{d}^{2}\right)b_{n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{m}b_{n},

(74)

where I have defined
\omega_{d}^{2}:=\omega_{0}^{2}-\frac{a^{2}}{4}
. From Refs.
37
and
43
, we know that the action eigenvalue spectrum of an undamped harmonic oscillator is

\frac{\lambda_{n}^{c}}{m}=\left(\frac{n\omega}{2}\right)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2},

(75)

where
\omega:=\frac{2\pi}{t}
, so the eigenvalue spectrum of the damped oscillator must be

\frac{\lambda_{n}}{m}=\left(\frac{n\omega}{2}\right)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}+\frac{a^{2}}{4}.

(76)

This leads to a familiar result. As is well known, the frequency of a harmonic oscillator subject to linear damping, with an equation of motion given by

\ddot{x}+a\dot{x}+\omega_{0}^{2}x=0,

(77)

is
\omega_{\rm d}=\omega_{0}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{a}{2\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}}
(see Ref.
2
, for example). If
n=1
, then the second variation of the action vanishes when

0=\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}+\frac{a^{2}}{4},

(78)

or

\omega=2\omega_{0}\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{a}{2\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}}=2\omega_{d}.

(79)

From Eq. (
18
), one can show that the first kinetic focus of a damped harmonic oscillator occurs when

t_{\rm KF,1}=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=\frac{\pi}{\omega_{d}}=\frac{\pi}{\omega_{0}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{a}{2\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}}}=\frac{T_{0}}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\left(\frac{a}{2\omega_{0}}\right)^{2}}},

(80)

which is just the half-period of the damped oscillator (

IV.2
Harmonic oscillator with time-dependent damping

Consider an equation of motion of the form

\ddot{x}+\gamma(t)\dot{x}+\omega^{2}(t)x=0,

(81)

where
\gamma(t)
is the dissipation function and the frequency depends on time. This equation describes a time-dependent harmonic oscillator (TDHO), a system that has been used to model the motion of a non-relativistic charged particle in a time-dependent magnetic field
Lewis 1967
as well as the normal mode dynamics of a scalar field in an expanding universe
Robles-Pérez 2017
;
Finelli, Vacca, and Venturi 1998
(see also Ref.
59
;
31
;
36
).

Eq. (
81
) can be derived from the Herglotz Lagrangian of an oscillator with time-dependent frequency and unit mass

L=\frac{1}{2}\dot{x}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}(t)x^{2}+g(t)S,

(82)

where, in this case,
g(t)=-\gamma(t)
. Eq. (
73
) tells us that the action eigenvalue equation for the TDHO is

\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}-\omega^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{4}\gamma^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\gamma}(t)\right)b_{n}=\lambda_{n}b_{n}.

(83)

To simplify the problem, I will assume that the damping function and the frequency vary slowly over the time interval
0\leq t\leq t_{f}
. I will also assume that these functions can be Taylor expanded around
t=0
, so that

\omega(t)=\omega(0)+t\frac{d\omega}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}+O(t^{2}),

(84)

and

\gamma(t)=\gamma(0)+t\frac{d\gamma}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}+O(t^{2}).

(85)

Define
\omega_{0}:=\omega(0)
,
\omega_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{d\omega}{dt}\big|_{t=0}
,
\gamma_{0}:=\gamma(0)
, and
\gamma_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{d\gamma}{dt}\big|_{t=0}
. Then Eq. (
83
) takes the form

\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}-\omega_{0}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{2}-2\left(\omega_{0}\omega_{0}^{\prime}-\frac{\gamma_{0}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{4}\right)t-\left(\omega^{\prime 2}_{0}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime 2}_{0}}{4}\right)t^{2}\right)b_{n}=\lambda_{n}b_{n}.

(86)

Here we have an effective Schrödinger equation for a particle of unit mass moving in an effective potential

\mathcal{U}=\begin{cases}-\omega_{0}^{2}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{2}}{4}+\frac{\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{2}-2\left(\omega_{0}\omega_{0}^{\prime}-\frac{\gamma_{0}\gamma_{0}^{\prime}}{4}\right)t-\left(\omega^{\prime 2}_{0}-\frac{\gamma^{\prime 2}_{0}}{4}\right)t^{2}&\text{if}\ 0<t<t_{f},\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\infty&\text{if}\ t=0\hskip 2.84526pt{\rm or}\hskip 2.84526ptt=t_{f}.\\
\end{cases}

(87)

We can see that, depending on the initial values of the frequency and the damping function (as well as the initial values of their first derivatives), the depth of the effective potential can increase with time. Therefore, even if the system is critically damped at first (so that
\omega_{0}=2\gamma_{0}
), it may eventually develop a kinetic focus. This happens because, for a sufficiently deep effective potential, one or more of the action eigenvalues can be
\leq 0
(the analogous situation in quantum mechanics would be to a particle in a potential well that is deep enough to admit energy eigenvalues that are
\leq 0
).

To investigate this possibility, I will estimate the lowest eigenvalue of the action spectrum, as a function of the final time

(88)

\alpha

Schumacher, Benjamin and Westmoreland, Michael
D. 2010

Shankar, Ramamurti 1994
The accuracy of the upper bound

D. 2010
;
Shankar, Ramamurti 1994

V
Conclusion

The sign of the second variation of the action, for a conservative system, can be used as an indicator of that system’s dynamical stability. As we have seen (and as shown in Refs.
37
;
39
;
42
, for example), the second variation changes sign when a conservative system passes through a kinetic focus, and it is the existence of these kinetic foci that indicate dynamical stability.

In this paper, I have generalized the analyses of Refs.
37
and
43
by showing that the second variation of the Herglotz action for a non-conservative system also changes sign when that system passes through a kinetic focus. This means that the second variation of the Herglotz action can be used to infer the dynamical stability properties of the class of non-conservative systems that can be described by the Herglotz action. Further, the eigenvalue analysis of the action for conservative systems described in Refs.
43
and
52
can also be applied to non-conservative systems. By doing this, I was able to locate the first kinetic focus of a TDHO, and I showed that the dynamical stability of this system, under a particular set of assumptions, is qualitatively different from the dynamical stability of a time-independent harmonic oscillator. It would be interesting to extend these results to more physically motivated applications of the TDHO, like those mentioned in Sec.
IV.2
.

Another way to extend these results would be to derive the semiclassical propagator, in the path integral formalism, for a quantum system that has dissipation and whose classical action is the Herglotz action. As I mentioned in the Introduction, the number of kinetic foci along a stationary (classical) path of a conservative quantum system determines the phase of the semiclassical propagator for that system. It would therefore be interesting to use the formalism developed here to investigate how dissipation affects the phase of the semiclassical propagator for a system whose dynamics can be derived from the Herglotz action. I leave this for future work.
