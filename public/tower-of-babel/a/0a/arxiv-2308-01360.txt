Introduction

Second-order cone programming is an important convex optimization problem
[
9
,
14
,
19
,
17
]
.
A second-order
cone constraint has the form
\|Ax+b\|\leq c^{T}x+d
,
where
\|\cdot\|
is the Euclidean norm.
This second-order cone constraint is equivalent to the inequality
f(x)\geq 0
, where
f
is
what we call a second-order cone function.
The solution set of the constraint is convex, and the function

f
is concave
[
9
,
15
]
.

In the following definition, we use
\mathbb{R}
to denote the set of real numbers and

\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
to denote the set of
m\times n
matrices with real entries. Of course,
m
and
n
are positive integers.

Definition 1
.

A
second-order cone function (SOCF)
is a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that can be written as

f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\|Ax+b\|

(1.1)

with parameters
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
d\in\mathbb{R}
,
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

In second-order cone programming
a linear function of
x
is minimized subject to one or more second-order cone constraints, along with the constraint

Fx=g
, where
F\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
and
g\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
The solution set of
Fx=g
is an affine subspace, and we will show that
the restriction of an SOCF to an affine subspace
is another SOCF. Thus, from a mathematical point of view the constraint
Fx=g

is not necessary, although in applications it can be convenient.
In this paper we do not consider the constraint

Fx=g
but instead focus on understanding the family of SOCFs.

There are interior-point methods for solving second-order cone programming problems.
These methods usually use SOCFs to
impose the second-order cone constraints
[
12
,
14
,
15
,
5
]
.
Solvers for second-order cone programming problems include CVXOPT and MATLAB
[
11
,
2
]
.
The study of second-order cone programming and its applications has continued to generate interest for over three decades
[
18
,
6
,
19
,
10
,
7
,
13
,
4
]
.

The current research was started to get a deeper
understanding of SOCFs to
improve interior-point algorithms for finding the
weighted analytic center of a system of second-order cone constraints

[
1
,
3
,
5
]
.
The current work can lead to improved algorithms.

In this paper, we give a thorough description of the family of SOCFs. In
the form of Equation (
1.1
),
the parameters
A
and
b
are not uniquely determined,
since
\|Ax+b\|=\|Q(Ax+b)\|=\|(QA)x+(Qb)\|
for any orthogonal
m\times m

matrix
Q
.
We show that every SOCF can be written in the
form

f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x_{*})},

with the parameters
\delta\geq 0
,
x_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the positive semidefinite

M=A^{T}A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
replace the parameters
A
and
b
.
We show that these new parameters are unique if and only if
M
is positive definite.

It is known that every SOCF
f
is concave
[
9
,
15
]
.
We show that
f
is strictly concave
if and only if
\text{rank}(A)=n
and
b\not\in\text{col}(A)
,
where
\text{col}(A)
denotes the column space of
A
.
In terms of the new parameters, the SOCF is strictly concave
if and only if
M
is positive definite and
\delta>0
.

In the case where
M
is positive definite, we show that
f
is bounded above if and only if
c^{T}M^{-1}c\leq 1
.
We show that the convex set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\geq 0\}
is bounded if and only
if
M
is positive definite and
c^{T}M^{-1}c<1
.

Our results have computational implications for convex optimization problems involving second-order constraints such as the problem of minimizing weighted barrier functions presented in
[
3
,
1
]
. This is related to the problem of finding a weighted analytic center for second-order cone constraints given in
[
5
]
. There are also computational implications for the problem of computing the region of weighted analytic centers of a system of several second-order cone constraints. This is under investigation as part of our current research which is an extension of the work given in
[
5
]
.

In the problems presented in
[
3
,
1
,
5
]
,
the boundedness of the feasible region guarantees the existence of a minimizer,
and the strict convexity of the barrier function guarantees the uniqueness of the minimizer. Also, the strict convexity of the barrier function affects how quickly we can find the minimizer using these algorithms. The determination of the strict concavity of
f
is related to the strict convexity of the barrier function. The boundedness of the feasible region of the SOC constraints system is also related to the boundedness of
f
. If a single
f
is bounded, then the feasible region of the SOC constraints system is bounded.

Convex optimization algorithms perform well and more efficiently when the problem is known to be bounded and the objective function is strictly convex. If a second-order cone function is strictly concave, its gradient and Hessian matrix are defined, and the Hessian is invertible.
The corresponding barrier function is similarly well-behaved,
and Newton’s method and Newton-based methods work well for the problem.
However, many optimization problems are not bounded or have objective functions that are not strictly convex. Our results would allow one to recognize convex optimization problems involving second-order cone constraints (as in
[
3
,
1
,
5
]
) that can be solved efficiently, or to assist in reformulating those that are hard to solve.

Theorem 5

Every second-order cone function

Theorem 7
.

Every SOCF of the form
f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\|Ax+b\|
is identically equal to

f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x_{*})},

(2.1)

where
M=A^{T}A
is positive semidefinite,
x_{*}=-A^{+}b
, and
\delta=\|Ax_{*}+b\|
.

Proof.

It is well-known that the least squares solution to

Ax=-b
is
x_{*}=-A^{+}b
, and that
Ax_{*}=-AA^{+}b
is the
orthogonal projection of
-b
onto
\text{col}(A)
.
That is,
Ax_{*}
is the point in
\text{col}(A)
that is closest to
-b
.
Thus, the distance squared from
Ax
to
-b
is the distance squared from
Ax
to
Ax_{*}
plus the distance squared from
Ax_{*}
to
-b
.
That is,

\displaystyle\|Ax+b\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|Ax-Ax_{*}\|^{2}+\|Ax_{*}+b\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\|A(x-x_{*})\|^{2}+\|Ax_{*}+b\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-x_{*})^{T}A^{T}A(x-x_{*})+\|Ax_{*}+b\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x_{*})+\delta^{2}.

The last equality uses the definitions of
M
and
\delta
. The result follows.

t\in[0,\infty)
,
we consider the function

f(vt)
and its asymptotic behavior as
t\to\infty
.
If
v^{T}Mv=0
, then
f(vt)=c^{T}v\,t+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+x_{*}^{T}Mx_{*}}
.
If
v^{T}Mv\neq 0
, then

\displaystyle f(vt)

\displaystyle=c^{T}v\,t+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+(vt-x_{*})^{T}M(vt-x_{*})}

\displaystyle=c^{T}v\,t+d-\sqrt{v^{T}Mv\,t^{2}-2v^{T}Mx_{*}t+x_{*}^{T}Mx_{*}+\delta^{2}}

\displaystyle=c^{T}v\,t+d-\sqrt{v^{T}Mv}\,t\,\sqrt{1+\frac{-2v^{T}Mx_{*}\,t+x_{*}^{T}Mx_{*}+\delta^{2}}{v^{T}Mv\,t^{2}}}

\displaystyle=\left(c^{T}v-\sqrt{v^{T}Mv}\right)t+d+\frac{v^{T}Mx_{*}}{\sqrt{v^{T}Mv}}+O(1/t)\text{ as }t\to\infty.

(2.2)

The third equation uses the fact that
t\geq 0
, and the
fourth equation uses the Taylor series
\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}=1+\varepsilon/2+O(\varepsilon^{2})

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
The fourth equation describes the slant asymptote of the graph of
f(vt)
, and is crucial for the remainder of the proof.

For all
v\neq 0
, Equation (
2.2
) implies that

\frac{f(vt)-f(-vt)}{2}=\begin{cases}c^{T}v\,t&\mbox{ if }v^{T}Mv=0\\
c^{T}v\,t+\frac{v^{T}Mx_{*}}{v^{T}Mv}+O(1/t)&\mbox{ if }v^{T}Mv\neq 0.\end{cases}

(2.3)

A similar expression where
c
is replaced by
\tilde{c}
holds.
If
v^{T}Mv=0
, then
\tilde{c}^{T}v=c^{T}v
.
If
v^{T}Mv\neq 0
, then
the slope of the slant asymptote is the same for both sets of parameters, so again
\tilde{c}^{T}v=c^{T}v
. This holds for all
v
, so
\tilde{c}=c
.

For all
v\neq 0
, Equation (
2.2
) implies that

\frac{f(vt)+f(-vt)}{2}=\begin{cases}d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+x_{*}^{T}Mx_{*}}&\mbox{ if }v^{T}Mv=0\\
d-\sqrt{v^{T}Mv}\,t+O(1/t)&\mbox{ if }v^{T}Mv\neq 0,\end{cases}

(2.4)

along with a similar expression where
d
is replaced by
\tilde{d}
, etc.
If
\tilde{M}\neq M
, then there is some vector

v
such that

v^{T}\tilde{M}v\neq v^{T}Mv
.
This leads to a contradiction since the slope
of the slant asymptote
in Equation (
2.4
) would be different. Thus,
\tilde{M}=M
.

Assume
M=0
.
Then
f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\delta=c^{T}x+\tilde{d}-\tilde{\delta}
, since
\tilde{c}=c
, and
\tilde{M}=M=0
.
Thus,
\tilde{d}-\tilde{\delta}=d-\delta
.

Assume
M\neq 0
. Then there exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies
Mv\neq 0
.
Using Equation (
2.4
) with
v^{T}\tilde{M}v=v^{T}Mv\neq 0
,
we find that
\tilde{d}=d
.
At this point we conclude, from the equality of the two expressions for
f
, that

\delta^{2}+(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x_{*})=\tilde{\delta}^{2}+(x-\tilde{x}_{*})^{T}M(x-\tilde{x}_{*})^{T}
for all

.
Expanding the quadratic term and canceling like terms, we find that

for all

.
Thus

.

Now we show that the parameterization of

is unique if and only if

is positive definite.
If

is not positive definite there exist

such that

M\tilde{x}_{*}=Mx_{*}
.
If
M
is positive definite then
M\neq 0
and
M
is invertible, so
\tilde{x}_{*}=x_{*}
and all of the parameters are unique.
∎

Theorem 12

Theorem 14

The SOCF

,

f
,

2.

if
c^{T}M^{-1}c=1
and
\delta=0
,
then every point in the ray

\{x_{*}+tM^{-1}c\mid t\geq 0\}
is a critical

f
, on which
f
attains its maximum value of

3.

if
c^{T}M^{-1}c>1

if

\delta>0

Proof.

To simplify the proof, we will analyze the SOCF

\tilde{f}(x):=c^{T}x-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+x^{T}Mx}

Note that

\tilde{f}(x-x_{*})=f(x)-d+c^{T}x_{*}

so we can easily relate the critical points, and the upper bounds, of

\tilde{f}

Case I:

\delta=0
.
In this case
\tilde{f}(x)=c^{T}x-\sqrt{x^{T}Mx}

Let
v
be any nonzero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
v^{T}Mv>0

t\mapsto\tilde{f}(vt)=(c^{T}v)t-\sqrt{v^{T}Mv}\,|t|

0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\tilde{f}
at which
\tilde{f}
is not differentiable, and
f
has a critical point at
x_{*}
.
To determine if
\tilde{f}
has a global maximum at 0, define
g_{v}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by
g_{v}(t)=\tilde{f}(vt)=(c^{T}v)t-t\sqrt{v^{T}Mv}
.
Note that
g_{v}
is a linear function giving the value of
\tilde{f}

along a ray starting at
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the direction vector
v
.
The function
\tilde{f}
is bounded above if and only if the slope of
g_{v}
is non-positive
for all directions
v
.

Let
\mathcal{E}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x^{T}Mx=1\}
.
Note that
\mathcal{E}
is an ellipsoid centered at
0
, since
M
is positive definite.
Furthermore,
g_{v}(0)=0
, so
\tilde{f}
is bounded above if and only if

\tilde{f}

\mathcal{E}

We compute this maximum value using the method

\tilde{f}
restricted to
\mathcal{E}
occur at

\nabla(c^{T}x)=\lambda\nabla\sqrt{x^{T}Mx}

This is equivalent to

\mathcal{E}
.
Thus, the extrema of
\tilde{f}
are at

\lambda

x^{T}Mx=1

\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}c^{T}M^{-1}MMc=1

.
There are two antipodal points on

\mathcal{E}

\tilde{f}
restricted to
\mathcal{E}
.
We see that
\tilde{f}(x_{\pm})=c^{T}x_{\pm}-1=\pm\sqrt{c^{T}M^{-1}c}-1

\tilde{f}
restricted to
\mathcal{E}
is
\sqrt{c^{T}M^{-1}c}-1
,

x_{+}
.
Thus, the maximum slope of
g_{v}
occurs when

of
M^{-1}c
, and that maximum slope has the same sign as

is bounded above if and only if
c^{T}M^{-1}c\leq 1
.

If
c^{T}M^{-1}c<1

0
is the unique critical point of
\tilde{f}
,
and
\tilde{f}(0)=0

\tilde{f}
.

is the unique critical point of
f
,
and

f
.
This proves part 1 in the theorem.
If

g_{v}
has slope

, and

\tilde{f}
achieves its maximum value of 0

M^{-1}c
.
Every point in this ray,

Translating this result to the original

If

is positive for some

\tilde{f}
has an isolated critical point at
0
, but
\tilde{f}
is not bounded above. This proves part 3 of the theorem.

Case II:

\delta>0
.
The gradient of
\tilde{f}
at

is smooth, and

\tilde{f}

\nabla\tilde{f}(x)=0
.
Since

\tilde{f}
is strictly concave

5
and
\tilde{f}
has at most one critical point.
If
\tilde{f}
has a critical point

\tilde{f}
is bounded above.
Denote the critical point of
\tilde{f}

Mx_{cp}=c\sqrt{\delta^{2}+{x_{cp}}^{T}Mx_{cp}}

.
Let

\alpha=\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\alpha^{2}(M^{-1}c)^{T}M(M^{-1}c)}=\sqrt{\delta^{2}+\alpha^{2}c^{T}M^{-1}c}

If

\alpha_{s}=\delta/\sqrt{1-c^{T}M^{-1}c}
,
and if

.
Thus if

\tilde{f}
has the critical point
\alpha_{s}M^{-1}c
,
and the critical point of

is

,
and a calculation of

We have already seen that

, and therefore

,
has no critical points when

.
The results about boundedness and upper bounds need the following asymptotic analysis.

is positive definite, and

Theorem 16
.

The SOCF
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
written in the form (
2.1
),

f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x_{*})},

with
M
positive semidefinite
is bounded above if and only if
c\in\text{col}(M)
and
c^{T}M^{+}c\leq 1
.

Proof.

Since
M
is symmetric, the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra states that the null space of
M

is the orthogonal complement of the
column space of
M
.
We can write any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
x=x_{n}+x_{r}
for unique
x_{n}\in N(M)
and
x_{r}\in\text{col}(M)
.
Similarly, we split
c=c_{n}+c_{r}
.
For a fixed
x_{*}
, we write
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
x=x_{*}+x_{n}+x_{r}
.
Thus
M(x-x*)=M(x_{n}+x_{r})=Mx_{r}
,
and
the general second-order cone function is

f(x_{*}+x_{n}+x_{r})=c_{n}^{T}x_{n}+c_{r}^{T}x_{r}+(c^{T}x_{*}+d)-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+{x_{r}}^{T}Mx_{r}}.

Assume
c\not\in\text{col}(M)
. Then
c_{n}\neq 0
and
f
is not bounded since

f(x_{*}+x_{n})=c_{n}^{T}x_{n}+(c^{T}x_{*}+d-\delta)

is an unbounded linear function.

Assume
c\in\text{col}(M)
, so
c_{n}=0
. Define
g:\text{col}(M)\to\mathbb{R}
by

g(x_{r}):=f(x_{*}+x_{n}+x_{r})=c_{r}^{T}x_{r}+(c^{T}x_{*}+d)-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+{x_{r}}^{T}Mx_{r}}.

Note that
M
, restricted to
\text{col}(M)
is a nonsingular map,
so we can apply Theorem
14
to
g
as follows. The pseudo-inverse of
M
satisfies

M^{+}(x_{n}+x_{r})=M^{+}x_{r},\quad MM^{+}(x_{n}+x_{r})=M^{+}M(x_{n}+x_{r})=x_{r}.

Thus, the restriction of
M^{+}
to
\text{col}(M)
is the inverse of the restriction of
M
to
\text{col}(M)
.
Theorem
14
says that
g
is bounded above if and only if

c_{r}^{T}M^{+}c_{r}\leq 1
.
Note that
c^{T}M^{+}c=c_{r}^{T}M^{+}c_{r}
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We have shown that
f
is not bounded above if
c\not\in\text{col}(M)
.
We have also shown that if
c\in\text{col}(M)
then
f
is bounded above
if and only if
c^{T}M^{+}c\leq 1
.
These two statements can be combined into one:

f
is bounded above if and only if

c\in\text{col}(M)
and
c^{T}M^{+}c\leq 1
.
∎

and
M^{+}=M^{-1}
.
Thus Theorem
16
, in the case where
M
is positive definite, implies the that
f
is bounded above if and only if
c^{T}M^{-1}c\leq 1
, which is the first part of Theorem
14
.

One of that main uses of SOCFs is to define convex sets for optimization problems.
Optimization over a bounded set is very different from optimization over an unbounded set,

Theorem 19
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by

f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x^{*})}
, where
M
is positive semidefinite.
Let
\mathcal{R}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\geq 0\}
, and assume

\mathcal{R}\neq\emptyset
.
The feasible region

\mathcal{R}

is closed and convex. Furthermore,
\mathcal{R}
is bounded if and only if
M
is positive
definite and
c^{T}M^{-1}c<1
.

Proof.

The set
\mathcal{R}
is convex since
f
is concave, and it is closed since
f
is continuous. We now prove the last statement of the theorem by determining whether
\mathcal{R}
is bounded or unbounded for any SOCF.

First, assume
M
is not positive definite and
c\not\in\text{col}(M)
. Theorem
16
says that
f
is not bounded above and hence
\mathcal{R}
is not bounded.

Second, assume
M
is not positive definite and
c\in\text{col}(M)
. The proof of Theorem
16
shows that

f(x+x_{n})=f(x)

3
Conclusion

The second-order cone function has important application in optimization problems. Our work gives
necessary and sufficient conditions for strict concavity of a second-order cone function. We show that every SOCF can be written in the
form

f(x)=c^{T}x+d-\sqrt{\delta^{2}+(x-x_{*})^{T}M(x-x_{*})}
,
which has unique parameters in many cases.
This alternative parameterization gives a deep understanding of the family
of SOCFs.
This alternative description leads to new results about SOCFs.
We characterize the critical points and global maxima of
f
, depending
on the parameters.
We give necessary and sufficient conditions for
f
to be bounded above,
and for the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)\geq 0\}
to be bounded.
Our results can lead to improved algorithms for optimization problems involving second-order cone constraints.
