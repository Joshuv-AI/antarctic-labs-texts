Existence of minimisers of variational problems posed in spaces of mixed smoothness

Abstract
The present work constitutes a first step towards establishing a systematic framework for treating variational problems that depend on a given input function through a mixture of its derivatives of different orders in different directions. 2
Spaces of mixed smoothness

We begin by introducing the function spaces in which our variational problems are set. We collect basic facts about Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
which, while available in the literature, might not be as classical and well-known as the corresponding theory of the usual Sobolev spaces
\mathrm{W}^{k,p}
. Let us note that, what we call Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness, is often referred to as ‘anisotropic Sobolev spaces’ in the literature that we cite. We have opted for the name ‘mixed smoothness’ as the term ‘anisotropic variational problems’ is already widespread and used to describe problems where the integrand and the input functions exhibit different growth properties in derivatives in different directions. That is, ‘anisotropic variational problems’ usually refer to problems posed in the space
\mathrm{W}^{k,\mathbf{p}}
with vector parameter
\mathbf{p}
, rather than
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
with vector parameter
\mathbf{a}
, which we are interested in here. Nevertheless, our setting is certainly anisotropic and we shall use this term occasionally, particularly when talking about scaling.

2.1
Preliminaries

Any partial differentiation operator
\partial^{\alpha}:=\partial_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\partial_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\ldots\partial_{N}^{\alpha_{N}}
acting on functions mapping a subdomain of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
may be identified with the multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}_{+}
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{N}_{+}
denotes the set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with non-negative integer coordinates.

Specifying a set of derivatives
A\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N}_{+}
and their desired integrability defines a Sobolev-like space — for example the classical
\mathrm{W}^{k,p}
Sobolev spaces correspond to
A:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}_{+}\colon|\alpha|\leqslant k\}
with
\mathrm{L}^{p}
integrability on all the derivatives. In this work our principal assumption is that the functions in our spaces admit maximal pure derivatives in each direction, but the orders of these maximal derivatives remain arbitrary.

Here and in all that follows,
\Omega
is a bounded open Lipschitz subset of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
|\partial\Omega|=0
, where
|\partial\Omega|
denotes the
N
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of the the boundary of
\Omega
. We denote by
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
the space of smooth and compactly supported functions
\varphi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We often omit the target space when it is clear from the context and simply write
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

We fix a vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, where the respective entries
a_{i}
denote the desired maximal order of differentiability with respect to the
x_{i}
coordinate. We fix an exponent
p\in(1,\infty)
, let
\mathbf{a}^{-1}:=(a_{1}^{-1},\ldots,a_{N}^{-1})
, and introduce the following

Definition 2.1
.

For a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
the Sobolev space
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined as the completion of
C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap\{\varphi\colon\sum_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\|\partial^{\alpha}\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\infty\}
with respect to the norm

\|u\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}}:=\sum_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\ldotp

We often omit the target space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and write simply
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
or even
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
if the domain
\Omega
is clear from the context.
We also denote by
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
the completion of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the same norm.

The set
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{N}\colon\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1\}
has the important property that if
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{N}
are two multi-indices with
\beta\leqslant\alpha
(coordinate-wise) and
\alpha
is in our set then so is
\beta
, which makes the collection a smoothness in the language of Pełczyński-Senator (see
[
69
]
). Moreover, all the maximal elements of the smoothness lie on a common hyperplane
\{\alpha\colon\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1\}
, thus allowing for convenient scaling, which we will discuss later. This hyperplane is called a pattern of homogeneity by Kazaniecki, Stolyarov, and Wojciechowski in
[
45
]
. It is worth mentioning that their paper is the first one to introduce a simple version of anisotropic quasiconvexity (to be discussed later) thus inspiring the present work. Let us observe that, in our case, the hyperplane of homogeneity intersects all coordinate axes at integer points (i.e. we have maximal pure derivatives in all directions), which is important for the structure of the relevant Sobolev spaces. Our exposition of the theory of these spaces is based on the book
[
16
]
by Besov, Il’in, and Nikolskii.
Finally, let us remark that the functions considered are, in general, vector-valued and we impose the same differentiability on all components of the functions considered. In general, it would be interesting to allow for different smoothnesses in different components. This is, however, outside of the scope of the present paper.

Proposition 2.2

(see
[
16
]
)
.

For
p\in[1,\infty)
the space
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
coincides with the space of functions
u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
with distributional derivatives
\partial^{\alpha}u\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1
.
The spaces
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
are both separable Banach spaces. For
p\in(1,\infty)
the two spaces are also reflexive.

This is shown in
[
16
]
, bar the reflexivity part, which may be immediately deduced by considering
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
as a closed subspace of
\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
through the embedding
u\mapsto(\partial^{\alpha}u)_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}
.

2.2
Embeddings of Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness

An important structural property of spaces of mixed smoothness is the existence of continuous and compact embeddings, similar to the classical Sobolev embeddings. To begin with, we note that, as in the classical case of, say
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
, there are certain regularity assumptions that one must impose on the domain
\Omega
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
b\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a vector with non-zero coordinates. Fix
h\in(0,\infty)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
. The set

V(b,h,\varepsilon):=\bigcup_{0<v<h}\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon\frac{x_{i}}{b_{i}}>0,v<(\frac{x_{i}}{b_{i}})^{a_{i}}<(1+\varepsilon)v\text{ for all }i\in\{1,2\ldots,N\}\right\}

is called an
\mathbf{a}
-horn of radius
h
and opening
\varepsilon
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be open and let
K\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose that for
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,K\}
there exist open sets
\Omega_{k}
and
\mathbf{a}
-horns
V_{k}
(with coefficients
b_{k},h_{k},\varepsilon_{k}
depending on
k
) such that

\Omega=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{k}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{K}(\Omega_{k}+V_{k})\ldotp

Then we say that
\Omega
satisfies the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition.

Theorem 2.5

(see Theorem 9.5 in
[
16
]
)
.

Suppose that an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition and let
p\in(1,\infty)
. Then there exists a real number
h_{0}\in(0,\infty)
depending on
\Omega
and a constant
C
such that, for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
and all
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
, one has, for all multi-indices
\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}_{+}
with
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1
, that

\|\partial^{\beta}u\|_{p}\leqslant C\left(h^{1-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{i}^{a_{i}}u\|_{p}+h^{-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\|u\|_{p}\right)\ldotp

Thus, on domains satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition (see
[
15
]
where the condition was first studied) the intermediate derivatives are controlled by the maximal pure derivatives and the function itself, so that the relevant Sobolev space is well behaved.

In the isotropic case (i.e.,
a_{i}=a_{j}
for all
i,j
) the
\mathbf{a}
-horn is in fact a cone and horn conditions are equivalent to the, more familiar, cone conditions. In the genuinely anisotropic scenario the
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition is more surprising. For instance, (see
[
16
]
) the two-dimensional disc only satisfies the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition if
\frac{1}{2}a_{1}\leqslant a_{2}\leqslant 2a_{1}
. Fortunately, there are no issues when working with rectangular domains, which we note in the following:

Lemma 2.6

(see
[
16
]
)
.

Any set of the form
(l_{1},r_{1})\times\ldots\times(l_{N},r_{N})\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
for some
l_{i},r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfies the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition.

Before we proceed, let us note several important consequences of the embeddings:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open set with a Lipschitz boundary. Then the embedding of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
into
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
is compact.

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{a}
one has the inclusion
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)\subset\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)
into the standard Sobolev space. Since the inclusion
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}(\Omega)\subset\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
is compact the proof is finished.
∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Suppose that a bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with a Lipschitz boundary satisfies the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition and let
p\in(1,\infty)
.
Then for any
\beta
with
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle<1
the mapping
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
given by
u\mapsto\partial^{\beta}u
is completely continuous, i.e., if
u_{j}\rightharpoonup u
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
then
\partial^{\beta}u_{j}\to\partial^{\beta}u
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Considering
u_{j}-u
instead of
u_{j}
we may assume that
u=0
. Fix a
\beta
with
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle<1
and note that Theorem
2.5
shows that there exists an
h_{0}>0
such that, for all
h\in(0,h_{0})
, one has

\|\partial^{\beta}u_{j}\|_{p}\leqslant C\left(h^{1-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\left(\sum_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{p}\right)+h^{-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\|u_{j}\|_{p}\right)\ldotp

Proposition
2.7
implies that
u_{j}
converges strongly to
0
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
. Hence, there exists a sequence
h_{j}\in(0,h_{0})
with
h_{j}\to 0
and
Ch_{j}^{-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\|u_{j}\|_{p}\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Finally, because
u_{j}
is bounded in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
, we know that
\left(\sum_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1}\|\partial^{\alpha}u\|_{p}\right)
is bounded, and since
Ch_{j}^{1-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\to 0
we conclude that
\|\partial^{\beta}u_{j}\|_{p}\to 0
, which ends the proof.
∎

\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
.

In closing this subsection let us note that the study of embeddings for spaces of mixed smoothness has been started by Besov and Il’in in
[
15
]
. Together with Nikolskii, these authors have expanded, compiled, and clarified the theory in
[
16
]
. It is, however, not the only relevant source. Similar questions have been studied, among others, by Boman (see
[
18
]
) who also treats the case
p=\infty
, as well as Demidenko and Upsenskii (see
[
33
]
) who worked on quasielliptic operators, a class important in regularity of solutions to mixed smoothness variational problems, which we will return to in a forthcoming paper. Finally, in the particular case of rectangular sets (like in Lemma
2.6
) a bounded extension may be constructed using the Hestenes’ method (as observed by Burenkov and Fain in
[
22
]
) which then, together with embeddings on the full space, yields existence of embeddings on rectangular domains as well.

2.3
Canonical Projection

For
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
we write
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u
for the
\mathbf{a}
-gradient of
u
given by
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u:=(\partial^{\alpha}u)_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1}
. For future use let us denote the cardinality of the set
\{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}\colon\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1\}
by
m
, so that for
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
the
\mathbf{a}
-gradient is a map defined on
\Omega
with values in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
In what follows we will often need to carry out certain operations, for example truncations, on
\mathbf{a}
-gradients of various functions. These are easy to do on mappings
\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, but they need not preserve the
\mathbf{a}
-gradient structure, and to remedy that we turn to Canonical Sobolev Projections following Pełczyński’s work in
[
68
]
. The aim is to obtain an analogue of the Helmholtz decomposition for the mixed smoothness setting. We will then use it for regularizing generating sequences of certain Young measures, similarly to what has been done by Fonseca and Müller in
[
40
]
in the case of
\mathcal{A}
-free vector fields and
\mathcal{A}
-quasiconvexity.

There is a canonical embedding

\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})

given by
u\mapsto(\partial^{\alpha}u)_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1},

but it is not surjective. With
p=2
one may define the canonical projection of the target space onto the image of this embedding, i.e.,

\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}\colon\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\im\left(\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})\right).

(2.2)

It has been shown in
[
68
]
(see Corollary 5.1 therein) that the projection from (
2.2
) is of strong type
(p,p)
for
1<p<\infty
, thus one can extend it by continuity from

\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})

to

\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Lemma 2.10
.

Fix any
p\in(1,\infty)
and denote by
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}
the extension of the canonical projection discussed above. Then:

i) the map
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}
is a bounded linear operator on
\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
;

ii) for any
V\in\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}
we have
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V)=\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V
;

iii) if the family
\{V_{j}\}\subset\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}
is
p
-equiintegrable then so is
\{\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V_{j}\}
.

Proof.

The first assertion is the content of Corollary 5.1 in
[
68
]
.
Point ii) is, by definition, true in
\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For a general exponent
p
let us fix
V\in\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a family
V_{j}\subset\bigoplus_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
\|V-V_{j}\|_{p}\to 0
. By continuity of
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}
(and thus of
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}\circ\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}
) one has

\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V_{j}\to\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V\,\text{ and }\,\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V_{j})\to\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V)\,\text{ in }\,\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N};\mathbb{R}^{n}),

and since
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V_{j}=\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}(\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}V_{j})
for each
j
the claim is proven.

For the last part consider the standard truncation
\tau_{k}
given by

\tau_{k}(X):=\begin{cases}X\quad\text{if }|X|\leqslant k,\\
k\frac{X}{|X|}\quad\text{if }|X|>k\ldotp\end{cases}

(2.3)

Then fix any sequence
V_{j}
satisfying the assumptions of point iii). Since
\{\tau_{k}(V_{j})\}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
and in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
we know, by continuity of
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}
as a map from
\mathrm{L}^{q}
to
\mathrm{L}^{q}
, that
\{\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}\tau_{k}(V_{j})\}
is bounded in any
\mathrm{L}^{q}
with
p\leqslant q<\infty
, so that this family is equiintegrable in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
. Then again,
p
-equiintegrability of
\{V_{j}\}
itself yields

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{j}\left|\left|V_{j}-\tau_{k}(V_{j})\right|\right|_{p}=0,

so again continuity of
\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}:\mathrm{L}^{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}
gives

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{j}\left|\left|\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}(V_{j}-\tau_{k}(V_{j}))\right|\right|_{p}=0,

hence
\{\mathrm{P}_{\mathbf{a}}(V_{j})\}
is
p
-equiintegrable as claimed.

∎

2.4
Anisotropic scaling

In what follows we will often need to use a specific anisotropic scaling. For a real number
R>0
and a vector
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
we let

R\odot v:=(R^{1/{a_{1}}}v_{1},R^{1/a_{2}}v_{2},\ldots,R^{1/a_{N}}v_{N}).

Here, and in all that follows,
Q\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
will, unless otherwise specified, denote
[-1,1]^{N}
. We let
Q_{R}(x_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be the open box centred at
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and scaled according to the rule just described, so that

Q_{R}(x_{0}):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\colon|x^{i}-x^{i}_{0}|^{a_{i}}<R\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant N\}.

Equivalently, we could write
Q_{R}(x_{0})=x_{0}+R\odot Q
. We call
R
the anisotropic radius of the box
Q_{R}
and
x_{0}
its centre.
From now on we will always understand a ‘box’ to mean a set of the form above, i.e., an anisotropically scaled and translated unit cube.

Observe that, unless the scaling is in fact isotropic (that is,
a_{i}=a_{j}
for all
i,j
), our family of boxes is not of bounded eccentricity, i.e., there does not exist a constant
c>0
such that each box
Q_{R}(x_{0})
in our collection is contained in some (Euclidean) ball
B
with
|Q_{R}(x_{0})|\geqslant c|B|
. Therefore, it is not obvious if standard results such as the Vitali covering lemma, or the Lebesgue differentiation theorem, hold for balls replaced by anisotropically scaled boxes. Note that even sharper statements of the Vitali covering lemma, such as the one in
[
76
]
by Saks (which only requires the eccentricity to be bounded along fixed sequences converging to a given point), or the one in
[
59
]
by Mejlbro and Topsøe (where the condition on the eccentricity is in integral form), are not directly applicable.

Nevertheless, these results may be proven in a straightforward way — it suffices to follow the proof of Vitali’s covering lemma given in
[
11
]
, and the Lebesgue differentiation theorem (which is what we are after) follows immediately. In fact, the result could also be deduced as a special case of the more general work by Calderón and Torchinsky (see
[
25
]
), thus we omit the proof of the following:

Theorem 2.11

(Anisotropic Lebesgue differentiation theorem)
.

Let
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. For Lebesgue almost every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
one has

\limsup_{R\to 0}\frac{1}{|Q_{R}(x_{0})|}\int_{Q_{R}(x_{0})}|f(x)-f(x_{0})|\,\mathrm{d}x=0\ldotp

2.5
Polynomial approximation

For a function
f\in\mathrm{L}^{1}(Q_{R}(x_{0}))
we denote by
(f)_{Q_{R}(x_{0})}
its average over
Q_{R}(x_{0})
, i.e.,

(f)_{Q_{R}(x_{0})}:=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q_{R}(x_{0})}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

We often write simply
Q_{R}
if the center is not important or clear from the context.

Lemma 2.12
.

There exists a constant
C
such that, for any
r>0
and any
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, there exists a cut-off function
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q_{r};[0,1])
which is identically equal to
1
on
Q_{(1-\sigma)r}
and satisfies

\|\partial^{\beta}\eta\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant Cr^{-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\sigma^{-|\beta|},

for all multi-indices
\beta
with
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1
.

Proof.

It is enough to consider the case
r=1
, as the general result will then follow by scaling. With
r=1
observe that the distance between the faces of
Q
and
Q_{1-\sigma}
along the
x_{i}
axis is equal to
1-(1-\sigma)^{1/a_{i}}
. For sufficiently small
C
the function
C\sigma+(1-\sigma)^{1/a_{i}}
is decreasing in
\sigma
, so there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for all
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, we have

1-(1-\sigma)^{1/a_{i}}\geqslant C\sigma,

for all
i
. Now it is enough to construct one dimensional cut-off functions
\eta^{i}(x_{i})
that realise the desired cut-off along the particular axes and satisfy
\|\partial^{k}\eta^{i}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\leqslant 2(C\sigma)^{-k}
. We then conclude by setting
\eta(x):=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\eta^{i}(x_{i})
.
∎

We recall the following version of the Poincaré inequality in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
proven by Dupont and Scott in
[
35
]
, where, instead of requiring zero boundary values, we allow for correction in terms of the kernel of the operator
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}
.

Proposition 2.13

(see Theorem 4.2 in
[
35
]
)
.

There exists a constant
C
such that for any function
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(Q)
with
p\in[1,\infty)
there exists a polynomial
P_{u}\in C^{\infty}(Q)
with
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}P_{u}\equiv 0
satisfying

\|u-P_{u}\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(Q)}\leqslant C\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)}.

Observe that, in the above,
Q
is fixed to be the unit cube, and we do not assert anything about approximations on other domains. However, we will only ever use this result on anisotropic boxes, and it is easy to see how to adjust the constant to scaling, as shown in the following:

Corollary 2.14
.

There exists a constant
C
such that for any function
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(Q_{r})
with
p\in[1,\infty)
there exists a polynomial
P_{u}\in C^{\infty}(Q_{r})
with
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}P_{u}\equiv\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u\right)_{Q_{r}}
such that for any
\beta
with
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1
we have

r^{-1+\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\|\partial^{\beta}(u-P_{u})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q_{r})}\leqslant C\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(u-P_{u})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q_{r})}.

The constant
C
does not depend on the function
u
nor the radius
r
.

Proof.

First of all, note that using our anisotropic rescaling we may reduce to the case
r=1
. This also determines the scaling, i.e., the
r^{-1+\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}
factor. Secondly, it is clearly enough to prove the result for
u\in C^{\infty}(Q)
, as the general case then follows from density of smooth functions in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(Q)
. Observe that by considering

\widetilde{u}(x):=u(x)-\sum_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1}x^{\alpha}\left(\partial^{\alpha}u\right)_{Q},

we reduce our task to finding a polynomial
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{u}}
with
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{u}}\equiv 0
and such that

\|\partial^{\beta}(\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{u}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)}\leqslant C\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\widetilde{u}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)}

for all
\beta
with
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle<1
, as the case
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1
is trivial. The existence of such a
\widetilde{P}_{\widetilde{u}}
is the content of Proposition
2.13
, which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.15

(see Theorem 10.16 in
[
30
]
)
.

For any bounded open Lipschitz domain
\Omega
, any
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and any
\varepsilon>0
there exist a function
u_{\varepsilon}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{u}(\Omega)
and a finite family of disjoint boxes
\{Q_{\varepsilon,i}\}_{i}
such that
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{\varepsilon}
is constant on each
Q_{\varepsilon,i}\subset\Omega
,
\left|\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q_{\varepsilon,i}\right|<\varepsilon
, and
\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)}<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and a parameter
\tau\in(0,1)
to be determined later. Decompose
\Omega
, up to a set of measure zero, into a countable family of disjoint, open boxes
\{Q_{\tau,i}\}
of radii equal, or smaller than,
\tau
. Select a finite subfamily of
I
boxes covering
\Omega
up to a set of measure less than
\varepsilon/2
and relabel the elements so that
\left|\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}Q_{\tau,i}\right|<\varepsilon/2
. From now on we focus our attention only on the boxes
Q_{\tau,i}
with
1\leqslant i\leqslant I
.
Let
P_{\tau,i}
denote the polynomial approximating
u
on
Q_{\tau,i}
given by Corollary
2.14
. Let
\sigma\in(0,1/2)
be a parameter to be determined later. For every
Q_{\tau,i}
take a cut-off function
\eta_{\tau,i}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q_{\tau,i})
identically equal to
1
on
(1-\sigma)\odot Q_{\tau,i}
, as in Lemma
2.12
. Define

v(x):=u(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{I}\eta_{\tau,i}(x)\left(P_{\tau,i}(x)-u(x)\right),

so that
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v
is constant on each
(1-\sigma)\odot Q_{\tau,i}
and
v\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{u}(\Omega)
. We may now calculate

\displaystyle\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)}^{p}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\int_{\Omega}|\partial^{\beta}(u-v)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\int_{Q_{\tau,i}}\left|\partial^{\beta}\left(\eta_{\tau,i}(x)\left(u(x)-P_{\tau,i}(x)\right)\right)\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sum_{\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\int_{Q_{\tau,i}}\sum_{0\leqslant\gamma\leqslant\beta}\left|\partial^{\gamma}\eta_{\tau,i}(x)\right|^{p}\left|\partial^{\beta-\gamma}\left(u(x)-P_{\tau,i}(x)\right)\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using the bounds on the derivatives of
\eta
we may write

\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leqslant C\sum_{\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\sum_{0\leqslant\gamma\leqslant\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sigma^{-p|\beta|}\tau^{-p\langle\gamma,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\int_{Q_{\tau,i}}\left|\partial^{\beta-\gamma}\left(u(x)-P_{\tau,i}(x)\right)\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x.

2.14
on each

now yields

\|u-v\|_{\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)}^{p}\leqslant C\sum_{\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}\sum_{0\leqslant\gamma\leqslant\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{I}\sigma^{-p|\beta|}\tau^{-p\langle\gamma,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\tau^{p-p\langle\beta-\gamma,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(u-P_{\tau,i})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q_{\tau,i})}^{p}.

3
Young measures

The main technical tool that we will use in studying lower semicontinuity of integral functionals defined on Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness is the theory of Young measures (see
[
85
]
,
[
86
]
,
[
87
]
, and
[
88
]
). These measures describe the behaviour of weakly converging sequences more accurately than just their weak limits and facilitate limit passages in nonlinear quantities.

In this section we introduce, and study, the oscillation Young measures generated by weakly convergent sequences of
\mathbf{a}
-gradients.
In doing so we follow the strategies of Kristensen in
[
54
]
(who studies classical gradients) and Fonseca and Müller in
[
40
]
(who work with
\mathcal{A}
-free vector fields). In what follows we focus on the adjustments required by the mixed smoothness setting whilst skipping the technical parts that translate without major adaptations. We refer the reader to the author’s thesis
[
72
]
, where a full step-by-step exposition may be found.

3.1
Oscillation Young measures

We denote by
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
the set of all Radon measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and by
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. We say that a function
F\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is a normal integrand if
F
is Borel measurable and, for every fixed
x\in\Omega
, the function
W\mapsto F(x,W)
is lower semicontinuous. Similarly, we say that a function
F\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is Carathéodory if both
F
and
-F
are normal integrands. Finally, a map
\nu\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
is said to be weak*-measurable if
x\mapsto\langle\nu_{x},\varphi\rangle
is (Lebesgue) measurable for any continuous and compactly supported function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
.

We begin with the following version of the Fundamental Theorem of Young Measures which may be found, for example, in Pedregal’s book, (see
[
67
]
, followed by a simple result on translations.

Theorem 3.1

(see
[
67
]
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a measurable set of finite measure and
V_{j}\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be a bounded sequence of
\mathrm{L}^{p}
functions for some
p\in[1,\infty]
. Then there exists a subsequence
V_{j_{k}}
and a weak
∗
-measurable map
\nu\colon\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
such that the following hold:

i) every
\nu_{x}
is a probability measure;

ii) if
F\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a normal integrand bounded from below, then

\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(x,V_{j_{k}}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

where

\overline{F}(x):=\langle\nu_{x},F(x,\cdot)\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}(y);

iii) if
F\colon\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is Carathéodory and bounded from below, then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(x,V_{j_{k}}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty

if and only if
\{F(\cdot,V_{j_{k}}(\cdot))\}
is equiintegrable (in the usual
\mathrm{L}^{1}
sense). In this case

F(\cdot,V_{j_{k}}(\cdot))\rightharpoonup\overline{F}\text{ in }\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega)\cdot

The family
\{\nu_{x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
is called the (oscillation) Young measure generated by
V_{j_{k}}
. If there exists some
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that
\nu_{x}=\nu_{x_{0}}
for almost every
x\in\Omega
then we say that
\nu
is a homogeneous Young measure and often identify the family
\{\nu_{x}\}
with the single measure
\nu_{x_{0}}
if there is no risk of confusion.

Proposition 3.2

(see
[
67
]
)
.

If
\{V_{j}\}
generates an oscillation Young measure
\nu
and if
W_{j}\rightarrow W
in measure, then
\{V_{j}+W_{j}\}
generates the translated Young measure

\widetilde{\nu}_{x}:=\delta_{W(x)}\ast\nu_{x},

where

\langle\delta_{U}\ast\mu,\varphi\rangle=\langle\mu,\varphi(\cdot+U)\rangle

for
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
\varphi\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
. In particular, if
W_{j}\rightarrow 0
in measure, then
\{V_{j}+W_{j}\}
still generates
\nu
. Similarly, if
\|V_{j}-W_{j}\|_{p}\rightarrow 0
for some
p\in[1,\infty]
then both
V_{j}
and
W_{j}
generate the same Young measure.

3.2
Decomposition and localisation

First, following the approach from Kristensen’s lecture notes (see
[
54
]
), we show how to decompose a given weakly convergent sequence of
\mathbf{a}
-gradients into a
p
-equiintegrable oscillation part supported away from the boundary, and a concentration part that converges to
0
in measure.

In this section we denote by
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u
the full gradient of
u
given by
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u:=(\partial^{\alpha}u)_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1}
and by
\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u
its lower gradient
\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u:=(\partial^{\alpha}u)_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle<1}
, so that
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u=\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u\oplus\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u
. We also set
d:=\left|\left\{\alpha\colon\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1\right\}\right|
so that for
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
and
\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{(d-m)\times n}
.

Definition 3.3
.

For
p\in(1,\infty)
we say that a family of functions
\{V_{k}\}\subset\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
is
p
-equiintegrable if the family
\{|V_{k}|^{p}\}\subset\mathrm{L}^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
is equiintegrable in the usual sense.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open Lipschitz set satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Then, for any sequence
u_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence
v_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that the sequence
(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j})
converges to
0
in measure. In particular, if
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}
(or, equivalently,
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}
) generates some oscillation Young measure
\nu
then
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}
(respectively
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j}
) also generates
\nu
. Furthermore, if
\{\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}\}
is
p
-equiintegrable then so is
\{\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j}\}
.

Proof.

Take an increasing family of smooth, open sub-domains
\Omega_{k}\Subset\Omega_{k+1}\Subset\Omega
(here
\Subset
denotes compact inclusion) with
\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{k}=\Omega
. Fix a family of cut-off functions
\varphi_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;[0,1])
with
\varphi_{k}\equiv 1
on
\Omega_{k}
and denote
M_{k}:=||\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}\varphi_{k}||_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\infty
.

For any
k,j
we may write
u_{j}=\varphi_{k}u_{j}+(1-\varphi_{k})u_{j}
, and the goal is to show that the second term is small. We have

\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}((1-\varphi_{k})u_{j})|\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}}|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}((1-\varphi_{k})u_{j})|\,\mathrm{d}x,

because on
\Omega_{k}
the integrand is identically equal to
0
. Differentiating the product we distinguish between the case where all the derivatives fall on
u_{j}
and the one where we also differentiate
(1-\varphi_{k})
, which yields

\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}((1-\varphi_{k})u_{j})|\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}}(1-\varphi_{k})|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}|\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}}M_{k}|\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}|\,\mathrm{d}x\ldotp

Since the family
\{|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}|\}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
, it is uniformly integrable in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
. Adding the fact that
|1-\varphi_{k}|\leqslant 1
and
|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}|\to 0
we deduce that the first term in our inequality converges to
0
with
k\to\infty
, uniformly in
j
. On the other hand, Lemma
2.8
shows that the lower gradients
\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}
converge to
0
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
, thus in particular in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
. Therefore, there exists a sequence
k_{j}\to\infty
such that
\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k_{j}}}M_{k_{j}}|\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}|\,\mathrm{d}x\to 0
with
j\to\infty
. Combining the two we see that
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}((1-\varphi_{k_{j}})u_{j}\to 0
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
.

Repeating the above reasoning with
\mathrm{L}^{1}
norm replaced by
\mathrm{L}^{p}
we get

\int_{\Omega}|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}((1-\varphi_{k})u_{j})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant C\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}}(1-\varphi_{k})^{p}|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x+C\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}}M_{k}^{p}|\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x,

(3.1)

with some absolute constant
C
. The first term is now bounded uniformly in
k
and
j
, whereas for the second one we may select a sequence
{k^{\prime}}_{j}
such that it converges to
0
. Hence, adjusting the first sequence
k_{j}
(i.e., slowing it down if necessary) we obtain that
(1-\varphi_{k_{j}})u_{j}
is bounded in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
.

Combining the two we deduce that
(1-\varphi_{k_{j}})u_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
, since the sequence is bounded and the only possible limit is
0
, as the full gradient converges to
0
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
. By construction, the sequence also converges to
0
in measure, thus setting
v_{j}:=\varphi_{k_{j}}u_{j}
ends the proof of the first part of the statement.

For equiintegrability it is enough to notice that if
\{\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}\}
is
p
-equiintegrable then the first term in (
3.1
) converges to
0
with
k\to\infty
, uniformly in
j
(as
(1-\varphi_{k})
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
and
|\Omega\setminus\Omega_{k}|\to 0
). Thus, in this case,
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j}
converges to
0
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
, which proves
p
-equiintegrability of
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j}
.
∎

The following result is the key point of this section. It shows that (up to a subsequence) one may decompose a weakly convergent sequence into a
p
-equiintegrable part that carries the oscillation and a part converging to
0
in measure, which carries the concentration.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition and let
p\in(1,\infty)
. Suppose that
u_{j}\rightharpoonup u
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then, there exists a subsequence
u_{j_{k}}
and sequences
\{g_{k}\}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\{b_{k}\}\subset\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, both weakly convergent to
0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and such that the family
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}g_{k}
is
p
-equiintegrable,
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}b_{k}\to 0
in measure, and
u_{j_{k}}=u+g_{k}+b_{k}
.

Proof.

By considering
\{u_{j}-u\}
instead of
\{u_{j}\}
we reduce to the case
u\equiv 0
. Furthermore, taking a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}
generates some oscillation Young measure
\nu
. Lemma
3.4
shows that we may also take
u_{j}=v_{j}+b^{1}_{j}
with
b^{1}_{j}\to 0
in measure and
v_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
. Thus, in what follows we focus on decomposing
v_{j}
, remembering that
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j}
generates the same Young measure as
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}
.

For
l\in\mathbb{N}
recall that the standard truncation
\tau_{l}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
is given by

\tau_{l}(W):=\begin{cases}W\quad\text{if }|W|\leqslant l,\\
l\frac{W}{|W|}\quad\text{if }|W|>l\ldotp\end{cases}

Since the truncation is bounded and continuous we get

\lim_{l\to\infty}\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\tau_{l}(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}|\tau_{l}(W)|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}|\cdot|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where the first equality is due to Theorem
3.1
and the second one is an application of the Monotone Convergence Theorem. We infer that one can extract a sequence
j_{l}\to\infty
such that

\lim_{l\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\tau_{l}(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}})|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}}|\cdot|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
\mathrm{L}^{p}
boundedness of
\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}
implies equiintegrability in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
for
q\in[1,p)
, hence
\tau_{l}(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}})-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}\to 0
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
, and thus the two generate the same oscillation Young measure
\nu
. This, paired with the last equality and Theorem
3.1
, implies that the family
\{\tau_{l}(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}\}
is
p
-equiintegrable.

In this way we have constructed a sequence
w_{l}:=\tau_{l}(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}})
that is
p
-equiintegrable and generates
\nu
. The last thing we need to take care of is the fact that
w_{l}
is not necessarily a full gradient of a
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}
function. To remedy that, extend
w_{l}
by
0
to the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and do the same with
v_{j_{l}}
, keeping the same notation for the extensions. Since we had
v_{j_{l}}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
, its extension by
0
is in the space
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
Apply the canonical projection
P_{\mathbf{a}}
to
w_{l}
to get the decomposition

w_{l}=\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}g_{l}+r_{l}\ldotp

We have

\displaystyle\|r_{l}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}=

\displaystyle\|w_{l}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}g_{l}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}\leqslant\|w_{l}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}+\|\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}g_{l}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|w_{l}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}+\|P_{\mathbf{a}}(w_{l}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}\leqslant C\|w_{l}-\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}\to 0,

where we have used
P_{\mathbf{a}}(\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}})=\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j_{l}}
and the
\mathrm{L}^{q}
continuity of
P_{\mathbf{a}}
.
In particular, this gives
r_{l}\to 0
in measure.
Furthermore, Lemma
2.10
shows that
p
-equiintegrability of
\{w_{l}\}
yields the same for
\{\widetilde{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}g_{l}\}
. Lastly, we restrict
g_{l}
to
\Omega
to get
g_{l}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and apply the cut-off argument from Lemma
3.4
to end the proof.
∎

The following is a simple and useful corollary of the above.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Let
\nu
be a
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient oscillation Young measure on
\Omega
. Then there exists a sequence
u_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
generating
\nu
and such that the family
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}\}
is
p
-equiintegrable. Furthermore, if the barycentre of
\nu
is
0
at all points of
\Omega
, then the functions
u_{j}
may be chosen in the space
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

The final technical ingredient of this subsection is the following localisation result. We omit the proof and instead refer the reader to
[
72
]
where a full argument may be found. It is simply a matter of following Kristensen’s approach (see
[
54
]
) and replacing the technical tools such as decomposition of generating sequences, polynomial approximations, and scalings, by their mixed smoothness counterparts stated and proven above.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open and bounded domain.
Fix
1<p<\infty
and let
\nu=(\nu_{x})_{x\in\Omega}
be a
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure on
\Omega
. Then for
\mathcal{L}^{n}
-a.e.
x_{0}\in\Omega
,
(\nu_{x_{0}})_{y\in Q}
is a homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure on the unit cube
Q
. Its barycentre is
\overline{\nu_{x_{0}}}\mathds{1}_{Q}
.

3.3
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity

This section aims to develop the mixed smoothness equivalent of quasiconvexity introduced first by Morrey in
[
61
]
(see also
[
28
]
for a broad list of references). For now, we present only a few background results that we will need to establish the dual characterisation of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures. We will revisit
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity in more detail in the next section, when we study lower semicontinuity properties of functionals.

Definition 3.8
.

We say that a function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[-\infty,\infty)
is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex if for every
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
one has

g(V)\leqslant\inf_{u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{Q}g(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\ldotp

For functions that need not be
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex we introduce the following:

Definition 3.9
.

For a measurable function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty)
we define the function
\mathcal{Q}g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[-\infty;\infty)
by

\mathcal{Q}g(V):=\inf\left\{\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{Q}g(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\colon u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)\right\}\ldotp

(3.2)

The expression on the right-hand side of the above definition is often called Dacorogna’s formula in the standard first order gradient case (see
[
28
]
).
In what follows we will often refer to
\mathcal{Q}g
as the
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex envelope of
g
, and the next lemma justifies this terminology, by asserting that
\mathcal{Q}g
is the largest
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex function that is smaller than or equal to
g
. By definition
\mathcal{Q}g\leqslant g
, simply by testing the definition with
u\equiv 0
and, again from the definition, one sees immediately that any
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex function that is no bigger than
g
must be no bigger than
\mathcal{Q}g
. Thus it only remains to show that
\mathcal{Q}g
is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex. This can be done following the approach of Fonseca and Müller from
[
40
]
. One only needs to use the anisotropic scaling we introduced earlier instead of the classical isotropic one. Thus, for the sake of brevity, we simply state the result and refer the reader to
[
72
]
for the full argument.

Lemma 3.10
.

For a continuous function
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
we have

\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{Q}g)=\mathcal{Q}g,

that is,
\mathcal{Q}g
is indeed
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex.

3.4
Topological structure of the space of oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures

Our next aim is to obtain a dual characterisation of oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures in terms of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex functions. The root of the principal results (Theorems
3.16
and
3.17
) goes back to the seminal work of Kinderlehrer and Pedregal (see
[
46
]
and
[
47
]
). Our approach, however, is based on
[
40
]
and the proofs we give are adaptations of the techniques presented therein — for the sake of brevity we focus on the main points that require adaptation to our mixed smoothness setting, and refer the reader to
[
72
]
for the full arguments.

In the following we work with the space
\mathcal{E}_{p}
defined as

\mathcal{E}_{p}:=\left\{g\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})\colon\lim_{|W|\to\infty}\frac{g(W)}{1+|W|^{p}}\text{ exists in }\mathbb{R}\right\},

and equipped with the norm

\|g\|_{\mathcal{E}_{p}}:=\sup_{W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\frac{|g(W)|}{1+|W|^{p}}\ldotp

It is not difficult to see that
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is a separable Banach space. Furthermore, the space of probability measures with finite
p
-th moment is a subset of
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
through the pairing

\langle\nu,g\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(W)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(W),

for
\nu\in\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})\colon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}|W|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(W)<\infty\}
and
g\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
. In particular, the space of homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures is a subset of
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
.
For future use, we note the following technical result.

Lemma 3.11
.

Fix a bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a function
\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
. Suppose that for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have fixed a countable family
\{Q^{j}_{i}\}_{i}=\{Q_{x^{j}_{i}}(r^{j}_{i})\}_{i}
of pairwise disjoint anisotropic boxes contained in
\Omega
and with
\lim_{j\to\infty}\sup_{i}r^{j}_{i}=0
. Assume furthermore that
\lim_{j\to\infty}|\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}|=0
. Define

\varphi_{j}:=\sum_{i}r^{j}_{i}\varphi((r^{j}_{i})^{-1}\odot(x-x^{j}_{i})).

Then
\varphi_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
weakly in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
. The sequence
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}
is
p
-equiintegrable and generates the homogeneous Young measure
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{\#}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{N}\,\raisebox{-0.38275pt}{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\lnot$}}}\,Q}{|Q|}\right)
.

Proof.

First of all note that the function
\varphi_{j}
is well-defined as an element of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
. To see this observe that for every
\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle\leqslant 1
and every
i
we have

\|\partial^{\beta}[r^{j}_{i}\varphi((r^{j}_{i})^{-1}\odot x)]\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q^{j}_{i})}=(r^{j}_{i})^{1-\langle\beta,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle}\frac{|Q^{j}_{i}|}{|Q|}\|\partial^{\beta}\varphi\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)}.

This also shows that the sequence
\varphi_{j}
converges strongly to
0
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)
and that it is bounded in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
, thus converges weakly to
0
in that space. The
p
-equiintegrability of
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}\}
follows from the fact that for any
M>0
we have

\sup_{j}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}|^{p}\mathds{1}_{|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}|>M}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{|\Omega|}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{p}\mathds{1}_{|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|>M}\,\mathrm{d}x,

which we get by a simple change of variables on each
Q^{j}_{i}
.

To show that
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}
generates the desired Young measure let us fix arbitrary functions
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
g\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
. Then

\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i}\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}f(x)g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
f
is Lipschitz we may write

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i}\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}(f(x)-f(x^{j}_{i}))g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\right|\leqslant

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}\left|\sum_{i}\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{j}\frac{|\Omega|}{|Q|}\int_{Q}\left|g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
\varepsilon_{j}=\varepsilon_{j}(f,\sup_{i}r^{j}_{i})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
. Changing variables on each
Q^{j}_{i}
we may write that

\sum_{i}\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}f(x^{j}_{i})g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i}\frac{|Q^{j}_{i}|}{|Q|}f(x^{j}_{i})\int_{Q}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi(y))\,\mathrm{d}y.

Note that the term
\sum_{i}|Q^{j}_{i}|f(x^{j}_{i})
is a Riemann sum of
f
on the anisotropic boxes
Q^{j}_{i}
, from which we deduce that it converges, as
j\to\infty
, to the integral
\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. Thus, finally

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(x)g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\left(\int_{\Omega}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\right),

and a standard density argument on
f
and
g
ends the proof.
∎

Since every bounded open set may be covered, up to a subset of measure
0
, with arbitrarily small anisotropic boxes we get the following:

Corollary 3.12
.

For any
\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
and any bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
the measure given by
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{\#}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L}^{N}\,\raisebox{-0.38275pt}{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\lnot$}}}\,Q}{|Q|}\right)
is a homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure on
\Omega
.

We denote the space of homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures on
Q
with barycentre
0
by
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}(Q)
.

Lemma 3.13
.

The set
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
is convex.

Proof.

Fix
\nu,\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
. Let
v_{j},u_{j}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
satisfy
v_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
,
u_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
with
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}
,
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}

Pick a sequence of smooth cut-off functions

\varphi_{i}
or
(1-\varphi_{i})

\|R(i,j)\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}=\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j}^{i}-(\varphi_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}+(1-\varphi_{i})\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\leqslant C(\varphi_{i})(\|\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}v_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}+\|\widehat{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}}u_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}})\to 0

(here

is a finite constant that depends on the function
\varphi_{i}
). Therefore, we may select a subsequence

as

\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(i)}^{i}-(\varphi_{i}\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j(i)}+(1-\varphi_{i})\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j(i)})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\to 0

as

. It is straightforward to see that

generates
\mathds{1}_{(-1,-1+2\theta)\times Q^{N-1}}\nu+\mathds{1}_{(-1+2\theta,1)\times Q^{N-1}}\mu

\mathds{1}_{(-1,-1+2\theta)\times Q^{N-1}}\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j(i)}+\mathds{1}_{(-1+2\theta,1)\times Q^{N-1}}\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j(i)}

\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{i}

\mathrm{L}^{p}

Finally,

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\varphi(x)\psi(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w^{k}_{i}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\varphi(x)\left(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\psi(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{i}(y))\,\mathrm{d}y\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\ldotp

Finally, since we have already identified the measure generated by

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\varphi(x)\psi(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{i}^{k}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x(\theta\langle\nu,\psi\rangle+(1-\theta)\langle\mu,\psi\rangle)\ldotp

A standard density argument and a diagonal extraction in the separable spaces

and

\widetilde{w}_{l}\subset\{w^{k}_{i}\}\subset C^{\infty}

\mathbf{a}
-gradients generating the measure

∎

Lemma 3.14
.

The set
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
is relatively closed in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})\cap\{\mu\colon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|W|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu<\infty\}
with respect to the weak* topology on
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
\nu\in\overline{\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}}^{E^{*}}\cap\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
and let
\{f_{i}\}\subset C^{\infty}(Q)
,
\{g_{j}\}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
be countable dense subsets of
L^{1}(Q)
and
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
respectively.
Take also
f_{0}(x)\equiv 1
and
g_{0}(W):=\left|W\right|^{p}
. By definition of the weak* topology, for any fixed
g\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
there exists a
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
with

\left|\langle\nu-\nu_{k},g\rangle\right|<\frac{1}{2k}\ldotp

Through a diagonal argument we may ensure that this is satisfied simultaneously for any finite set of
g
’s, i.e., for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
such that

\left|\langle\nu-\nu_{k},g_{j}\rangle\right|<\frac{1}{2k},\text{ for all }j\in\{0,1,\ldots,k\}\ldotp

Since
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
we may find a sequence
\{w^{k}_{j}\}\subset\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
with
\mathbf{a}
-gradients generating
\nu_{k}
.

Theorem
3.1
implies that, for any
g\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
, we have
g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w^{k}_{j})\rightharpoonup\langle g,\nu_{k}\rangle
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}(Q)
. Another diagonal extraction and the triangle inequality let us establish existence of a sequence
\{w_{k}\}\subset\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
such that

\left|\langle\nu,g_{j}\rangle\int_{Q}f_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x-\int_{Q}f_{i}g_{j}(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x\right|<\frac{1}{k},\text{ for all }0\leqslant i,j\leqslant k,

(3.3)

as all
f_{i}
’s are smooth and therefore bounded, so that they are admissible test functions for weak convergence in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
.

Setting
i=j=0
shows that
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{k}\}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)
, therefore we may find a subsequence generating some
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure
\mu
. For notational simplicity we assume that the entire sequence generates
\mu
.
From (
3.3
) and the fact that
g_{j}(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{k})\rightharpoonup\langle\mu,g_{j}\rangle
in
\mathrm{L}^{1}
as

we infer that

\langle\nu,g_{j}\rangle\int_{Q}f_{i}\mathop{dx}=\int_{Q}f_{i}\langle\mu,g_{j}\rangle\mathop{dx},\text{ for all }i,j\ldotp

By density of
f_{i}
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(Q)
we deduce that
\langle\mu,g_{j}\rangle=\langle\nu,g_{j}\rangle
in
(\mathrm{L}^{p})^{*}
. In particular, they are equal almost everywhere, so that for almost every

we have
\langle\mu_{x},g_{j}\rangle=\langle\nu,g_{j}\rangle
for all

\{g_{j}\}
in

we deduce that

for almost all

is in fact homogeneous so
\nu=\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
, which ends the proof.
∎

Because bounded continuous functions are a subset of
\mathcal{E}_{p}
we immediately get:

Lemma 3.15
.

If a sequence
\{\nu_{j}\}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})\cap\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
converges to some
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})\cap\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
in the space

then it also converges in the sense of weak convergence of probability measures. In particular, by the portmanteau theorem, we have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu

for all bounded and continuous functions

, and

\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu

for all lower semicontinuous functions
g
bounded from below.

3.5
Dual characterisation of oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures

We are finally ready to show the two main results of this section:

Theorem 3.16
.

A probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
is a homogeneous oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure with mean
W_{0}
if and only if
\mu
satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}W\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)=W_{0}
,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|W\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)<\infty
and

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(W)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)\geqslant\mathcal{Q}g(W_{0})

for all
g\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
.

Theorem 3.17
.

Fix a bounded open Lipschitz domain
\Omega
satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Let
\{\nu_{x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
be a weak* measurable family of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then there exists a
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
-bounded sequence
\{v_{n}\}\subset\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
with
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{n}\}
generating the oscillation Young measure
\nu
if and only if the following conditions hold:

i) there exists
v\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
such that

\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v(x)=\langle\nu_{x},\id\rangle\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega;

ii)

\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}|W|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}(W)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty;

iii) for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and all
g\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
we have

\langle\nu_{x},g\rangle\geqslant\mathcal{Q}g(\langle\nu_{x},\id\rangle)\ldotp

The proof strategy is based on Fonseca and Müller’s approach from
[
40
]
. Having established all the necessary technical ingredients above, there are no major difficulties in adapting their arguments. Below we present a proof for the case of homogeneous measures and, for the sake of brevity, refer the reader to
[
72
]
for the full argument in the general case.

Proof.

(of Theorem
3.16
)
First observe that it is enough to consider the case
W_{0}=0
, as we may always take a translation. For the proof of this case we argue by contradiction. Suppose that
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
satisfies

\begin{cases}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}W\,\mathrm{d}\nu=0,\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|W\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu(W)<\infty,\\
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(W)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(W)\geqslant\mathcal{Q}g(0)\text{ for all }g\in\mathcal{E}_{p},\end{cases}

but
\nu\not\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
. The
p
-th moment assumption on
\nu
implies that
\nu\in\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
. By Lemmas
3.13
and
3.14
and the Hahn-Banach separation theorem, there exist
g\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\mathcal{Q}g(0)\leqslant\langle\nu,g\rangle<\alpha\leqslant\langle\mu,g\rangle\text{ for all }\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}\ldotp

For any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
the measure
(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)_{\#}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{N}\,\raisebox{-0.38275pt}{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\lnot$}}}\,Q}{|Q|}
is an element of
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
(see Corollary
3.12
) so that

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\alpha\ldotp

Taking infimum over all such
\varphi
’s yields

\mathcal{Q}g(0)=\inf_{\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\alpha,

which contradicts
\mathcal{Q}g(0)\leqslant\langle\nu,g\rangle<\alpha
and shows that
\nu
is indeed in the set
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
.

For the reverse implication let
\varphi_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}
be such that
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{k}\}
is a
p
-equiintegrable sequence generating
\nu
(which is possible thanks to Corollary
3.6
). Then for all
k
we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{k}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\mathcal{Q}g(0)

by definition of
\mathcal{Q}g
. On the other hand,
p
-equiintegrability of
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{k}\}
and the growth bound on

imply that we may use point iii) of Theorem
3.1
to deduce that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{k}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g\,\mathrm{d}\nu,

as for a continuous function the boundedness from below assumption is irrelevant, since we may simply consider the positive and negative parts of
g
separately.
This and the previous estimate finish the proof.
∎

4
Coercivity

We are now ready to move on to the core part of the paper, i.e., the study of existence of solutions to variational problems posed in Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness. To use the Direct Method we need two ingredients: compactness of sequences of minimisers and sequential lower semicontinuity of the given functional. In this section we focus on the first part and study coercivity of the relevant functionals.
Our first goal is Theorem
4.3
, which is a generalisation to the mixed smoothness framework of a recent result due to Chen and Kristensen from
[
26
]
, that was further improved by Gmeineder and Kristensen in
[
43
]
.

Here
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous integrand satisfying the growth condition

|F(X)|\leqslant C(|X|^{p}+1)

(4.1)

for some
C\in(0,\infty)
and all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. For a non-empty bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
we denote by
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)\colon\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
the mapping defined by
\mathrm{I}(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x
. To be able to properly introduce a Dirichlet problem for this functional we define, for a fixed
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the Dirichlet class

\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega):=\{g+\varphi\colon\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)\},

Observe that we require the boundary datum
g
to be defined on the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, rather than just
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, thus bypassing possible trace issues. For
q\in[1,p]
we introduce, as in
[
26
]
, the following notions:

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
if for any sequence
u_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
with
\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}\to\infty
one has
\mathrm{I}(u_{j},\Omega)\to\infty
.

Definition 4.2
.

We say that
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
if for any
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
we have

\mathrm{I}(u,\Omega)\geqslant C_{1}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}^{q}-C_{2}

for some strictly positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
independent of
u
, but not necessarily of
g
.

As in
[
26
]
(see also Proposition 3.1 in
[
43
]
), our main goal here is the following:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous integrand satisfying (
4.1
). Then, for any
q\in[1,p]
the following are equivalent:

a)

For any bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and any boundary datum

the functional
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
.

b)

There exist a non-empty bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a boundary datum
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that the functional
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
.

c)

For any bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and any boundary datum
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
the functional
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
.

d)

There exist a non-empty bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a boundary datum
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that the functional
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
.

e)

For any bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and any boundary datum
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
all
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
minimising sequences for the functional
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
are bounded in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},q}_{g}(\Omega)
.

f)

There exist a non-empty bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a boundary datum
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that all
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
minimising sequences for the functional
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega)
are bounded in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},q}_{g}(\Omega)
.

g)

There exist a constant

and a point

such that the integrand

\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex at

.

Simply put, the above asserts that
\mathrm{L}^{q}
coercivity,
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercivity, and boundedness of all minimising sequences are mutually equivalent, and it is enough to check either of these on a particular choice of domain and boundary datum. Furthermore, coercivity may be characterized in terms of
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity of
F(\cdot)-c|\cdot|^{q}
at a single point.

Lemma 4.4

(see Proposition 3.1 in
[
26
]
)
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
4.3
its point a) is equivalent to b), and point c) is equivalent to d).

Proof.

The fact that a) is equivalent to b) in the first order gradient case is the content of Proposition 3.1 in
[
26
]
. The proof of c) being equivalent to d) is similar — we will do this here omitting the first part, as all that needs to be changed in the argument of
[
26
]
is the scaling factor.

It is obvious that c) implies d), thus we only need to prove the other implication. Let
\Omega_{0}
and
g_{0}
be the domain and the boundary datum for which
\mathrm{I}(\cdot,\Omega_{0})
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercive on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g_{0}}(\Omega_{0})
. Fix an arbitrary non-empty bounded open Lipschitz set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a boundary datum
g\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Take boxes
Q_{2r}(x_{0})\Subset\Omega_{0}
(for some
x_{0}\in\Omega_{0}
) and
Q_{R}(0)\Supset\Omega
, and fix cut-off functions
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q_{2r}(x_{0}))
and
\rho\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q_{R}(0))
satisfying

\mathbf{1}_{Q_{r}(x_{0})}\leqslant\varphi\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{Q_{2r}(x_{0})}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\leqslant\rho\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{Q_{R}(0)}.

Any
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
may be seen as a function in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
if we extend it by

outside

, simply from our definition of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
. We may then cut-off outside

by setting
\widetilde{w}:=\rho u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q_{R}(0))

w(x):=R^{-1}\widetilde{w}(R\odot x)\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q_{1}(0))
and set

Clearly
v\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g_{0}}(\Omega_{0})
and we may calculate, using a simple change of variables, that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega_{0})}^{q}=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{0}\setminus Q_{r}(x_{0})}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(1-\varphi(x))g_{0}|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+(\frac{r}{R})^{|\mathbf{a}^{-1}|}\int_{Q_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(\rho g)|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x+(\frac{r}{R})^{|\mathbf{a}^{-1}|}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)}^{q},

so that
\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega_{0})}^{q}=D_{1}+D_{2}\|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\Omega)}^{q},
where
D_{i}
do not depend on
u

D_{2}>0

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{0}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v)\,\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{0}\setminus Q_{r}(x_{0})}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(1-\varphi(x))g_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+(\frac{r}{R})^{|\mathbf{a}^{-1}|}\left(\int_{Q_{R}(0)\setminus\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(\rho g))\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x\right).

Thus,
\mathrm{I}(u,\Omega)
differs from

Corollary 4.7

Lemma 4.8

\varphi^{j}_{i}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q^{j}_{i})
such that

j\left|Q^{j}_{i}\right|<\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}\left|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(u_{j}+\varphi^{j}_{i})\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x,

and

\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(u_{j}+\varphi^{j}_{i}))\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j})+1/j\,\mathrm{d}x.

Extending each
\varphi^{j}_{i}
by zero outside
Q^{j}_{i}
and letting
v_{j}:=u_{j}+\sum_{i}\varphi^{j}_{i}
yields a sequence
v_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{u}(\Omega)
that clearly still minimizes the functional, but is unbounded in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},q}
, and thus we have a contradiction, which proves that f) implies c), thus the points a) through f) are equivalent.

That g) implies d) is easy. Assuming that
F-\delta|\cdot|^{q}
is quasiconvex at some
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
we may write

F(X_{0})-\delta|X_{0}|^{q}\leqslant\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}F(X_{0}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi(x))-\delta|X_{0}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x

for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
. This is exactly
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercivity on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{g}(\Omega)
with
g
a polynomial such that
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}g=X_{0}
,
c_{1}=\delta>0
and
c_{2}=F(X_{0})-\delta|X_{0}|^{q}
.

As the last step we prove that c) implies g). Assume that
F
is
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercive, take
\Omega
to be any admissible domain, and let the boundary condition be
g\equiv 0
. If the corresponding coercivity constants are
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
then take any
\delta\in(0,c_{1})
and put
G(X):=F(X)-\delta|X|^{q}
for
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Clearly, the auxiliary function of
G
is bounded from below by
(c_{1}-\delta)t+c_{2}
and, as always,
G\geqslant\mathcal{Q}G
. From Lemma
4.10
we know that the auxiliary functions of
G
and
\mathcal{Q}G
are the same, thus we may write

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}G(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}G(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant(c_{1}-\delta)\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}x+c_{2}

for all
\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(\Omega)
. Thanks to Corollary
4.7
we may find a sequence
\varphi_{j}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
for which

\mathcal{Q}G(0)\leqslant\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}G(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}G(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\searrow\mathcal{Q}G(0).

Since we have picked
\delta<c_{1}
it is easy to conclude that
G
is still
\mathrm{L}^{q}
mean coercive. Since
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\Omega}G(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x
is bounded we infer that the sequence
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{q}
. As in
[
26
]
we consider the following probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}

\nu_{j}:=(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})_{\#}\left(\frac{\mathcal{L^{N}}\restriction\Omega}{|\Omega|}\right)

and observe that they have uniformly bounded
q
-th moments. Thus, passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that
\nu_{j}\overset{*}{\rightharpoonup}\nu
in
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})^{*}
, where
\nu
is some probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with finite
q
th moment. Setting
H(X):=G(X)-\mathcal{Q}G(X)
, which is a non-negative and lower semicontinuous function, we may use the portmanteau theorem to write

0\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}H\,\mathrm{d}\nu\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}H\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}=0,

thus
H=0
on the support of
\nu
. However, then
G=\mathcal{Q}G
on the support of
\nu
, i.e.,
F-\delta|\cdot|^{q}
is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex on the support of
\nu
, and since
\nu
is a probability measure its support is non-empty, which ends the proof.
∎

5
Lower semicontinuity

The central part of our existence theory is sequential weak lower semicontinuity of integral functionals acting on Sobolev spaces of mixed smoothness. We will show that this property is equivalent to
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity of the integrand, with the precise variant of
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity depending on the regularity of the integrand and on the space on which the functional is defined. We begin with the definitions of (closed)
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity and a short discussion of the relationship between the various notions.

Similarly to Ball and Murat in
[
9
]
we define the following:

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex if for every
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
one has

F(X)\leqslant\inf_{u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{n})}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.98003pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.26338pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.6363pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.45924pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\ldotp

Observe that this definition differs from that of
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity only by replacing the test space
C_{c}^{\infty}
by
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}
. In fact, we have the following:

Lemma 5.2

(see
[
9
]
)
.

Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and satisfies
|F(X)|\leqslant C(1+|X|^{p})
for every
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then
F
is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex if and only if it is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex.

In the classical case of first order gradients this has been shown by Ball and Murat in
[
9
]
through a simple application of Fatou’s lemma. The same proof works in our case, thus we skip it and note that the above and Lemma
3.10
immediately imply the following.

Corollary 5.3
.

Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and satisfies
|F(X)|\leqslant C(1+|X|^{p})
for every
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then
\mathcal{Q}F
is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex.

Let us note that the growth assumption on
F
cannot, in general, be removed from the statement of Lemma
5.2
. In other words,
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
and
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},q}
-quasiconvexity are different notions in general. The previously mentioned paper
[
9
]
contains an example of a function that is
\mathrm{W}^{1,p}
quasiconvex only for sufficiently large exponents
p
, thus showing the importance of the relevant test space.

Finally, for future use, we introduce a third notion (analogous to the one introduced by Pedregal in
[
66
]
and later studied by Kristensen in
[
53
]
) that will be particularly useful for dealing with extended-real valued integrands.

Definition 5.4
.

We say that a function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
is closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex if
F
is lower semicontinuous and Jensen’s inequality holds for
F
and every homogeneous oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure, i.e.,

F(X)\leqslant\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(W)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(W),

where
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X}
is the set of all homogenous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures with mean
X
.

As in Theorem
3.16
, we note that replacing
\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X}
with
\delta_{X}\ast\mu
for
\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
allows us to rewrite the inequality in the definition of closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity as

F(X)\leqslant\inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(X+W)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W),

for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.

Before we proceed, we note the following lemma which, in the case of classical gradients, was first proven by Ball and Zhang in
[
10
]
:

Lemma 5.5
.

Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous integrand satisfying
|F(X)|\leqslant C(|X|^{p}+1)
for some constant
C
and for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then
F
is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex if, and only if, it is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex and if, and only if, it is closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex.

Proof.

Under these assumptions the equivalence between
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity and
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity is the content of Lemma
5.2
. To prove the equivalence of closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity with these notions let us first observe that clearly closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity implies
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity, as for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
the measure given by
(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)_{\#}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{N}\,\raisebox{-0.38275pt}{\reflectbox{\rotatebox[origin]{-90.0}{$\lnot$}}}\,Q}{|Q|}
is a homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure (see Corollary
3.12
). Note that this part does not use any assumptions on
F
— indeed, closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity is the strongest of the three notions.

For the other direction fix a point
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and an arbitrary
\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
and let
\varphi_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
be such that the sequence
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}\}
is
p
-equintegrable and generates
\mu
, which is possible thanks to Corollary
3.6
. Due to the growth conditions on
F
we know that the family
\{F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\}
is equiintegrable as well, so that Theorem
3.1
shows that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(X+W)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}(x))\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant F(X),

where the last inequality is due to
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity of
F
. Since
X
and
\nu
were arbitrary, this ends the proof. Observe that we do not need to assume that
F
is bounded from below to apply Theorem
3.1
, as we may simply consider its positive and negative parts separately.
∎

In the following we establish an initial result that identifies closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity as a sufficient condition for lower semicontinuity of integral functionals in the mixed smoothness setting.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain. Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[0,\infty]
is a closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex integrand. Then the functional

\mathrm{I}(u):=\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x

is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and a sequence
u_{j}\rightharpoonup u
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
. We need to show that
\liminf\mathrm{I}(u_{j})\geqslant\mathrm{I}(u)
. Since
u_{j}
is weakly convergent in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
it is also bounded in that space. Passing to a subsequence if necessary, we may assume that the
\liminf
is a true limit and, passing to a further subsequence, that
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}
generates some
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure
\nu=\{\nu_{x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
. Observe that, since
p\in(1,\infty)
, the barycentre of
\nu_{x}
is
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u(x)
for almost every
x
in
\Omega
. By Theorem
3.1
we know that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{I}(u_{j})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proposition
3.7
tells us that, for Lebesgue almost every
x
, the measure
\nu_{x}
is a homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure. Hence
F
, as a closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex function, satisfies Jensen’s inequality when tested with
\nu_{x}
for almost every
x
, which ends the proof, as we now have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{I}(u_{j})\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\int_{\Omega}F(\overline{\nu_{x}})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{I}(u).

∎

Corollary 5.7
.

Since the proof above is based on localisation, we immediately get that, under the assumptions of the above lemma, the functional

\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)\ni u\mapsto\int_{A}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x

is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
for any measurable subset
A\subset\Omega
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a measurable integrand and that its associated functional
\mathrm{I}(u):=\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x
is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
. Then
F
is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
\varphi\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{0}(Q)
and a
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Let
u
be an
\mathbf{a}
-polynomial with
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u\equiv X
. For an arbitrary
k\in\mathbb{N}
we may cover
\Omega
, up to a set of measure zero, with a countable family of anisotropic boxes of the form
\{x^{j}_{i}+r^{j}_{i}\odot Q\}_{i}
, with
r^{j}_{i}<1/j
for all
j,i
. Let
\varphi^{j}_{i}(x):=(r^{j}_{i})\varphi((r^{j}_{i})^{-1}\odot(x-x^{j}))
and set
\varphi_{j}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\varphi^{j}_{i}
.
Then
\varphi_{j}
converges weakly to
0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
(see Lemma
3.11
), thus
u+\varphi_{j}\rightharpoonup u
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
, so that

\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}(u+\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x=|\Omega|F(X).

However, for every
j
we have, by a change variables,

\int_{\Omega}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x=|\Omega|\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x.

which ends the proof.
∎

Putting together the content of Lemmas
5.5
,
5.6
, and
5.8
we obtain the following:

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous integrand satisfying the
p
-growth condition
|F(X)|\leqslant C(|X|^{p}+1)
for some constant
C
and all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then the functional

\mathrm{I}(u):=\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x

is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
if and only if
F
is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex.

6
Relaxation

We have already identified
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity as an equivalent condition for lower semicontinuity of integral functionals in the mixed smoothness framework. When a functional lacks lower semicontinuity one typically studies its relaxation, defined as the sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous envelope of the original functional, i.e., through

\overline{\mathrm{I}}(u):=\inf_{u_{j}\rightharpoonup u}\left\{\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{I}(u_{j})\right\},

where the infimum is taken over all sequences
u_{j}
converging to
u
in the appropriate sense. In our case this means
u_{j}\rightharpoonup u
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
. With this definition the relaxed functional
\overline{\mathrm{I}}
is sequentially lower semicontinuous with respect to weak convergence in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
. The aim of this section is to show that, under appropriate assumptions, the relaxation is again an integral functional with integrand given by
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexification of the original one. In the classical setting of first order gradients and under
(p,p)
growth conditions on the integrand, this was first done by Dacorogna in
[
27
]
. The main results of this section are contained in Theorems
6.2
and
6.13
, which deal with the
p
-growth case and the extended-real valued case, respectively.

6.1
The
p
-growth case

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous and
\mathrm{L}^{p}
coercive integrand with
F(X)\leqslant C(|X|^{p}+1)
. Assume further that
F
is locally Lipschitz with

|F(X)-F(W)|\leqslant D(1+|X|^{p-1}+|W|^{p-1})|X-W|,

(6.1)

for all
X,W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and some
D\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\mathcal{Q}F
is continuous.

Proof.

For a continuous integrand of
p
-growth the mapping
X\mapsto\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x
is continuous for any fixed
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
. Thus, by the Dacorogna formula (
3.2
), we immediately see that the quasiconvex envelope is upper semicontinuous, as it is a pointwise infimum of a family of continuous functions.

By Theorem
4.3
we know that the functional induced by
F
is
\mathrm{L}^{p}
mean coercive with any boundary datum. However, the constants describing the coercivity may depend on the datum — if this was not the case then
F
would have to satisfy pointwise coercivity bounds of the form
F(X)\geqslant C|X|^{p}-C^{-1}
, which we do not assume. Nevertheless, under the assumption of local Lipschitz continuity of
F
, it may be shown that the constants may be chosen uniformly on compact sets. To prove that, let us take
X_{0}
to be a point at which
F-c|\cdot|^{p}
, with some positive constant
c
, is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex — such a point exists by Theorem
4.3
. For an arbitrary
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
we may, using (
6.1
), write

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\left|F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)-F(X_{0}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant

D\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\left(1+\left|X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi\right|^{p-1}+\left|X_{0}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi\right|^{p-1}\right)|X-X_{0}|\,\mathrm{d}x.

Rearranging we get

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\left|F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)-F(X_{0}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\right|\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant c_{1}+c_{2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{p-1}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where the constants
c_{i}
depend on
X
but may be chosen uniformly on compact sets. These constants also depend on
D
and
X_{0}
, but, since the integrand is fixed, this dependence is not important here. Rearranging and using the strict
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvexity at
X_{0}
given by Theorem
4.3
we may now write

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant c_{0}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x-c_{1}-c_{2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{p-1}\,\mathrm{d}x,

with a different constant
c_{1}
. Using weighted Young’s inequality we get

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant\frac{c_{0}}{2}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x-c_{1},

where, again, the constant
c_{1}
has changed, but it is still independent of
\varphi
and may be chosen locally uniformly in
X
. Thus, we have shown that for any compact set
K
there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi)\,\mathrm{d}x\geqslant c\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}x-c^{-1},

(6.2)

for all
X\in K
and all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
.

To show that
\mathcal{Q}F
is lower semicontinuous take an arbitrary point
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and a sequence
X_{j}\to X
. Assume, without loss of generality, that
\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}F(X_{j})=\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}F(X_{j})<\infty
. For every
j
we may, by the Dacorogna formula, find a function
\varphi_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
such that

\mathcal{Q}F(X_{j})>\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X_{j}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
X_{j}
is a convergent sequence, it is contained in a compact set. Similarly, the sequence
\mathcal{Q}F(X_{j})
is bounded, thus, based on what we have just proven in (
6.2
), we deduce that the family
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}\}
is bounded in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
. Using the Lipschitz bound (
6.1
), we may write

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant

\displaystyle\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X_{j}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x

(6.3)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle D\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\left(1+|X|^{p-1}+|X_{j}|^{p-1}+|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}|^{p-1}\right)|X-X_{j}|\,\mathrm{d}x.

Now,
|X-X_{j}|
converges to
0
as
j\to\infty
, and the integral

\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}\left(1+|X|^{p-1}+|X_{j}|^{p-1}+|\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}|^{p-1}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

is bounded, hence the last term in (
6.3
) goes to
0
with
j
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}F(X)

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{Q}F(X_{j}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x=\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{Q}F(X_{j}),

which ends the proof.
∎

Let us remark that the above could be easily generalised to the
\mathcal{A}
-free setting of compensated compactness due to Murat and Tartar (see
[
63
]
,
[
64
]
by Murat and
[
81
]
,
[
82
]
,
[
83
]
by Tartar). It is known that in general, when the characteristic cone of
\mathcal{A}
does not span the entire space,
\mathcal{A}
-quasiconvex envelopes of smooth functions need not be continuous (see, for example, Remark 3.5 in
[
40
]
). Our proof shows that this is not a problem if one restricts to coercive integrands satisfying the bound (
6.1
). That being said, in the context of
\mathcal{A}
-quasiconvexity, this may also be resolved in a different way (see the author’s recent collaboration with Raiţă
[
73
]
)), however that approach cannot be applied in the mixed smoothness case, and thus we do not elaborate on it further.

Our main relaxation result is the following:

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Supppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[0,\infty)
is a continuous and
\mathrm{L}^{p}
coercive integrand with
F(X)\leqslant C(|X|^{p}+1)
. Assume furthermore that
F
satisfies

F(X)\geqslant D|X|^{p}-D^{-1}

(6.4)

or

|F(X)-F(W)|\leqslant D(1+|X|^{p-1}+|W|^{p-1})|X-W|,

(6.5)

for all
X,W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
.
Then the sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous envelope of the functional
\mathrm{I}_{F}
is given by

\overline{\mathrm{I}}_{F}(u):=\inf_{u_{j}\rightharpoonup u}\left\{\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{I}_{F}(u_{j})\right\}=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{Q}F}(u),

where the infimum is taken over all sequences
u_{j}
converging to
u
weakly in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

When
F
satisfies the first of the two alternative conditions we have provided, i.e., when
F
is of
p
-growth from below as well as from above, this result is a corollary of a more general relaxation result (for extended real-valued integrands) that we will demonstrate next, thus we postpone this part of the proof.

Under the assumption that
F
is locally Lipschitz, we have shown in Lemma
6.1
, that
\mathcal{Q}F
is continuous. We also know, from Lemma
4.8
, that
\mathcal{Q}F
satisfies the same
p
-growth assumption as
F
. Lemma
3.10
tells us that
\mathcal{Q}F
is
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex, which in view of the continuity and growth bounds implies, by Lemma
5.5
, that it is
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex. Finally, Lemma
5.6
shows that the functional induced by
\mathcal{Q}F
is indeed sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous on
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
. This translates to

\overline{\mathrm{I}}_{F}(u)\geqslant\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{Q}F}(u),

and so it remains to prove the reverse inequality.

Proceeding similarly to a proof in Dacorogna’s book
[
28
]
(see Theorem 9.1 therein) let us start with the simple case of
\Omega=Q
and
u
with
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u=X
for some constant
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. By the Dacorogna formula (
3.2
) there exists a sequence
\varphi_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q)
with

\int_{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{Q}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

It only remains to show that the sequence
\varphi_{j}
may be chosen in such a way as to satisfy
\varphi_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(Q)
. The argument here is essentially a simpler version of the one in the proof of Lemma
3.11
. For a fixed
j
extend
\varphi_{j}
periodically and consider the sequence

\varphi_{j}^{k}(x):=r_{k}^{-1}\varphi_{j}(r_{k}\odot x),

with
r_{k}:=k^{a_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot a_{N}}
. Then
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}^{k}
preserves the integral, i.e.,

\int_{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j}^{k})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x,

for every
k
, and
\varphi_{j}^{k}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(Q)
with
k\to\infty
. Thus, a diagonal extraction argument ends the proof in this basic case.

For the general case let us fix an arbitrary function
u\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
. Using Proposition
2.15
we may find a sequence
v_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}_{u}(\Omega)
with
v_{j}\to u
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and such that for each
j
there exists a finite family of anisotropic boxes
\{Q^{j}_{i}\}_{i}
such that
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}
is constant on each
Q^{j}_{i}
and
\left|\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}\right|\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.
We know, from Lemma
6.1
, that
\mathcal{Q}F
is continuous, and from Lemma
4.8
that it satisfies the
p
-growth bound. Thus

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since
v_{j}
converges to
u
strongly in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
, the sequence
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}
is
p
-equiintegrable. By assumption and Lemma
4.8
we know that both
F
and
\mathcal{Q}F
satisfy the
p
-growth bound from above, so that

\int_{\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\to 0\text{ and }\int_{\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\to 0.

In particular,

\int_{\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Using the previous step we may, for any fixed
Q^{j}_{i}
, find a sequence
\varphi^{j}_{i,k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(Q^{j}_{i})
with
\varphi^{j}_{i,k}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
as
k\to\infty
and such that

\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi^{j}_{i,k})\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{Q^{j}_{i}}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\text{ as }k\to\infty.

Letting
\varphi^{j}_{k}:=\sum_{i}\varphi^{j}_{i,k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we have
\varphi^{j}_{k}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
as
k\to\infty
and

\int_{\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi^{j}_{k})\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\bigcup_{i}Q^{j}_{i}}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x.

A standard diagonal extraction argument allows us to construct a, non-relabelled, diagonal sequence
\varphi_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
with
\varphi_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and such that

\int_{\Omega}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v_{j}+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}\varphi_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\to\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{Q}F(\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Therefore, setting
u_{j}:=v_{j}+\varphi_{j}
, shows that

\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{I}_{F}(u_{j})\leqslant\mathrm{I}_{\mathcal{Q}F}(u),

and thus ends the proof.
∎

Observe that we only use the locally Lipschitz assumption on
F
in order to deduce continuity of
\mathcal{Q}F
from Lemma
6.1
. If continuity can be ensured in a different way then we can dispense with the assumption (
6.5
). In the classical setting of first order gradients it is known that quasiconvex functions are convex along rank-one directions, and these span the entire space, so that one may deduce continuity from directional convexity. We have already mentioned that, in general, there is no good analogue of rank-one convexity in the mixed smoothness setting. However, if
\mathbf{a}
is such that all multi-indices
\alpha
with
\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1
are of the same parity, this has been resolved by Kazaniecki, Stolyarov, and Wojciechowski in
[
45
]
. Under this assumption they have shown that
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex functions are convex along directions of the form

\sum_{\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1}i^{|\alpha|+|\alpha_{0}|}x^{\alpha}b^{i}e_{\alpha,i}.

(6.6)

Here
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are arbitrary vectors,
i^{2}=-1
,
e_{\alpha,i}
is the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, and
\alpha_{0}
is an arbitrary multi-index on the hyperplane of homogeneity, i.e., with
\langle\alpha_{0},\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1
. When all the multi-indices have the same parity, the coefficients in (
6.6
) are real and vectors of this form span
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, thus continuity follows from directional convexity. Thus, we obtain the following:

Corollary 6.3
.

If
\mathbf{a}^{-1}
is such that all the multi-indices
\alpha
with
\langle\alpha,\mathbf{a}^{-1}\rangle=1
have orders of the same parity then the conclusion of Theorem
6.13
holds without the additional assumptions (
6.4
) or (
6.5
).

If, on the other hand, the parities of
|\alpha|
do not match, then the coefficients in (
6.6
) complexify, and we do not get any directional convexity. It is still possible to use the calculation leading to determining the form of the vectors in (
6.6
) to show that
\mathbf{a}
-quasiconvex functions are (pluri)subharmonic in a certain sense (see the discussion in
[
45
]
), but we have not yet been able to use that to strengthen our relaxation results. We believe, however, that this should be studied further and intend to do so in future work.

6.2
Closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex envelope

The
p
-growth assumption on the integrand was crucial in the results of the previous subsections. Indeed, when
F
is not of
p
-growth the notions of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity and closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity need not coincide, and this is precisely the reason for introducing this stricter notion of closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvexity. An explicit example, in the isotropic setting of first order gradients, of an integrand that is quasiconvex but not closed quasiconvex may be found in Example 1.3 in
[
53
]
.

The first issue we run into is that, for an extended real-valued integrand, the formula (
3.2
) need not yield a closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex function. The purpose of this subsection is to establish a formula that does. We start with the natural definition of the closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex envelope.

Definition 6.4
.

For a measurable function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to(-\infty,\infty]
we define its closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex envelope by

\overline{F}(X):=\sup\{G(X)\colon G\leqslant F,\,G\text{ is closed }\mathbf{a}\text{-}p\text{ quasiconvex}\}\ldotp

Our goal is the following:

Proposition 6.5
.

For any
p\in(1,\infty)
, the closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex envelope of a lower semicontinuous function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to[0,\infty]
satisfying the growth condition
F(X)\geqslant c|X|^{p}
for some constant
c>0
is given by

\overline{F}(X)=\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}}\langle F(\cdot+X),\nu\rangle=\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X}}\langle F,\nu\rangle\ldotp

Moreover, the function
\overline{F}
is indeed closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex.

The reason we put the lower growth assumption in this result is that in a number of places in the proof we will use an argument that selects, at each
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
a measure
\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X}
which (nearly) achieves
\langle F,\nu_{X}\rangle=\overline{F}(X)
. The main idea of the proof is that then, with any fixed
\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
the measure
\mu
defined by
\,\mathrm{d}\mu:=\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\,\mathrm{d}\nu
is again a homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure. To prove that we need to ensure that it has a finite
p
-th moment, which is where coercivity comes into the picture. For now we do not know whether it is possible to relax this assumption, nevertheless it is in line with the relaxation result we prove next. As in the case of integrands of
p
-growth, to prove a relaxation formula we need some sort of a coercivity assumption on the integrand. If we were to relax the pointwise coercivity to simply
\mathrm{L}^{p}
or
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-mean coercivity we would need a Lipschitz assumption on our integrand of the form appearing in Theorem
6.13
, which we cannot have if we wish to allow
F
to take the value
+\infty
. Thus, for now at least, we content ourselves with including the lower-growth bound also in our formula for the closed quasiconvex envelope. We note that the lower growth assumption is also present in the relaxation result in Kristensen’s paper
[
53
]
, on which we base our relaxation proof.

Before we proceed to the proof let us recall the following classical result due to Kuratowski and Ryll-Nardzewski:

Theorem 6.6

(see
[
56
]
)
.

Let
X
be a metric space and
Y
be a separable and complete metric space. Fix a multi-valued function
\mathcal{G}\colon X\rightarrow 2^{Y}
. If for any closed set
K\subset Y
the set
\{x\in X\colon\mathcal{G}(x)\cap K\not=\emptyset\}
is Borel measurable then
\mathcal{G}
admits a measurable selector, i.e., there exists a Borel measurable function
g\colon X\rightarrow Y
such that for all
x\in X
we have
g(x)\in\mathcal{G}(x)
.

Proof of Proposition
6.5
.

The argument that we present closely follows the one in a recent paper by the author (see
[
71
]
), where it was employed in the context of
\mathcal{A}
-quasiconvexity. Denote

R(X):=\inf_{\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}}\langle F(\cdot+X),\nu\rangle\ldotp

Clearly for any
\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
we have
\overline{F}(X)\leqslant\langle F(\cdot+X),\nu\rangle,
therefore taking the infimum over
\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
yields

\overline{F}(X)\leqslant R(X),

hence showing that
R
is closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex will give the reverse inequality and end the proof, as one immediately gets
R\leqslant F
by testing with
\nu:=\delta_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
.

To show that
R
is lower semicontinuous fix
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, a sequence
X_{j}\rightarrow X_{0}
, and an
\varepsilon>0
. We will show that

\varepsilon+\liminf_{j\to\infty}R(X_{j})\geqslant R(X_{0})\ldotp

Without loss of generality assume that
\lim_{j\to\infty}R(X_{j})=\liminf_{j\to\infty}R(X_{j})<\infty,

and let
M
be such that
R(X_{j})+\varepsilon\leqslant M
for all
j
. By definition of
R
, for each
X_{j}
there exists
\nu_{j}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
with

M\geqslant R(X_{j})+\varepsilon\geqslant\langle F(\cdot+X_{j}),\nu_{j}\rangle\ldotp

Our growth assumption on
F
and boundedness of
\left|X_{j}\right|
(as a convergent sequence) give

M\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}c\left|X+X_{j}\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}\geqslant C\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|X\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}-1\right),

which yields
\sup_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|X\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{j}<\infty\ldotp

We see that the family
\{\nu_{j}\}
is bounded in
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
, therefore we may extract a weakly*-convergent subsequence from it — without loss of generality assume that the whole sequence converges, i.e.,
\nu_{j}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\nu_{0}
in
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
. By Lemma
3.14
we have
\nu_{0}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
. Moreover

\delta_{X_{j}}\ast\nu_{j}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\delta_{X_{0}}\ast\nu_{0}\ldotp

Since
F
is lower semicontinuous and bounded from below we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon+\liminf_{j\to\infty}R(X_{j})\geqslant

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\to\infty}\langle F,\delta_{X_{j}}\ast\nu_{j}\rangle\geqslant\langle F,\delta_{X_{0}}\ast\nu_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X_{0})\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\geqslant R(X_{0}),

where the last inequality comes from the definition of
R
and the fact that
\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary we conclude that
R
is in fact lower semicontinuous.

It now remains to show that
R
satisfies Jensen’s inequality with respect to homogeneous oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures. To that end fix
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and
\nu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X_{0}}
. We wish to show that

R(X_{0})\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}R\,\mathrm{d}\nu\ldotp

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}R\,\mathrm{d}\nu<\infty
. Fix an
\varepsilon>0
and observe that, by definition of
R
, for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
there exists
\nu_{X}\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
satisfying

\langle F(\cdot+X),\nu_{X}\rangle\leqslant\varepsilon+R(X),

so that, for now only formally,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)\leqslant\varepsilon+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}R\,\mathrm{d}\nu\ldotp

Now — if we manage to show that
\nu_{X}
may be chosen in such a way that
X\mapsto\nu_{X}
is weak* measurable and that the measure
\mu
defined by duality as

\langle g,\mu\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)

(6.7)

is a homogeneous
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure with mean
X_{0}
then the claim will follow, as by definition
\langle F,\mu\rangle\geqslant R(X_{0})
.

Note that weak* measurability of
X\mapsto\nu_{X}
only means Lebesgue measurability of
X\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}
, which need not be enough to integrate this function with respect to
\nu
. However, if we manage to get Borel measurability of the function in question then the construction is justified, as
\nu
is a Radon (hence Borel) measure — we will call such a map Borel weak* measurable. It is clear that if one makes sense of the integration on the right-hand side of (
6.7
) then it defines a linear functional on
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
. Its boundedness follows from the fact that all
\nu_{X},\nu
are probability measures, thus showing that the functional is given by some finite Radon measure
\mu
.

For the measurable selection part we define a multifunction
\mathcal{G}
given by

\mathcal{G}(X):=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}\colon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant\varepsilon+R(X)\right\}\ldotp

For the measurable selection result we intend to use (see Theorem
6.6
) we need
\mathcal{G}
to take values in
2^{Y}
for some complete metric space
Y
. For that we define, for a given
M>0
,

\Omega_{M}:=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\colon\left|X\right|<M,R(X)\leqslant M\}\ldotp

Observe that since we assumed
R
to be integrable with respect to
\nu
, we have that
X\in\bigcup_{M=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{M}
for
\nu
-a.e.
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Let us fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for any
X\in\Omega_{M}
and any
\mu\in\mathcal{G}(X)
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(W+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)\leqslant\varepsilon+R(X)\leqslant 2\varepsilon+R(X)\leqslant M+2\varepsilon\ldotp

The factor
2
in front of
\varepsilon
is not important here, we only put it there to allow for some room in later parts of the argument. Due to the growth assumption on
F
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(W+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)\geqslant

\displaystyle C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|W+X\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|W\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)-C^{-1}\left|X\right|^{p}\ldotp

Finally
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|W\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(W)\leqslant C_{M},
holds for all
\mu\in\mathcal{G}(X)
, with the constant
C_{M}
depending only on
M
(and
\varepsilon
). Therefore, we may consider our operator
\mathcal{G}
as a map
\Omega_{M}\rightarrow 2^{Y_{M}}
, where

Y_{M}:=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}\colon\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|W\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant C_{M}\right\}\ldotp

The set
Y_{M}
may be equipped with the weak* topology inherited from
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
. Since we put a uniform bound on the
p
-th moments (so also on the norm in
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
), this topology is metrisable in a complete and separable manner when restricted to
Y_{M}
. To prove that, first recall that due to Lemma
3.14

\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
is weak* closed in
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
. Since
|\cdot|^{p}\in\mathcal{E}_{p}
we know that the map
\mu\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}|W|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\mu
is weak* continuous, thus
Y_{M}
is weak* closed and bounded. The Banach-Alaoglu Theorem (see for example Theorem 3.16 in
[
20
]
) then implies that
Y_{M}
is weak* compact. Since
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is clearly separable we deduce that the weak* topology on
Y_{M}
is metrisable (see Theorem 3.28 in
[
20
]
). Finally, compact metric spaces are complete and separable, thus proving our claim.

Lemma 6.7
.

For any
X\in\Omega_{M}
the set
\mathcal{G}(X)
is non-empty and closed.

Proof.

The fact that
\mathcal{G}(X)\not=\emptyset
comes straight from the definition of
R
. To show that it is closed it is enough to show that it is sequentially closed. Let us then fix a sequence
\{\mu_{j}\}\subset\mathcal{G}(X)
and assume that it converges weak* in
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
to some
\mu\in Y_{M}
. Since the function
F
is lower semicontinuous and bounded from below we get by Lemma
3.15
that

R(X)+\varepsilon\geqslant\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu,

so
\mu\in\mathcal{G}(X)
, which ends the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.8
.

For any non-empty closed set
O\subset Y_{M}
the set

\left\{X\in\Omega_{M}\colon\mathcal{G}(X)\cap O\not=\emptyset\right\}

is Borel measurable.

Proof.

First note that we may rewrite the set in question as

\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\{X\in\Omega_{M}\colon\inf_{\mu\in O}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant R(X)+\varepsilon(1+2^{-k})\right\}\ldotp

Hence, it is enough to show that the sets

\left\{X\in\Omega_{M}\colon\inf_{\mu\in O}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant R(X)+\varepsilon(1+2^{-k})\right\}

are all Borel measurable. Define

U(X):=\inf_{\mu\in O}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\ldotp

We claim that
U
is lower semicontinuous. Let
X_{j}\rightarrow X
. We need to show that
\liminf_{j\to\infty}U(X_{j})\geqslant U(X)\ldotp

Without loss of generality assume that the
\liminf
is a true limit and that it is finite, i.e.,

\lim_{j\to\infty}U(X_{j})=\liminf_{j\to\infty}U(X_{j})<\infty.

By definition of
U
, for each
k
there exists a measure
\mu_{j}\in O
with

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}\leqslant U(X_{j})+1/k\ldotp

Therefore

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}=\lim_{j\to\infty}U(X_{j})\ldotp

Since the set
O
is a closed subset of a compact space
Y_{M}
we may extract an
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
weak* convergent subsequence from
\mu_{j}
. Without loss of generality assume that the entire sequence
\mu_{j}
converges weak* to some
\mu\in O
. This, combined with
X_{j}\rightarrow X
, implies that we have
\delta_{X_{j}}\ast\mu_{j}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\delta_{X}\ast\mu
in the sense of probability measures. Therefore, since
F
is lower semicontinuous, the portmanteau theorem yields

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X_{j})\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}=

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F\,\mathrm{d}\left(\delta_{X_{j}}\ast\mu_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F\,\mathrm{d}\left(\delta_{X}\ast\mu\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\geqslant U(X),

which shows that
U
is indeed lower semicontinuous. Since the set

\left\{X\in\Omega_{M}\colon\inf_{\mu\in O}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant R(X)+\varepsilon(1+2^{-k})\right\}

is the same as

\{X\in\Omega_{M}\colon U(X)\leqslant R(X)+\varepsilon(1+2^{-k})\},

and both
U
and
R
are lower semicontinuous (hence Borel measurable) the set in question is Borel measurable as well, which ends the proof.
∎

Now, thanks to Lemmas
6.7
and
6.8
we may use Theorem
6.6
to deduce the existence of a weak* measurable map
\nu^{M}\colon\Omega_{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
such that for any
X\in\Omega_{M}
the measure
\nu^{M}_{X}
satisfies

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)d\nu^{M}_{X}\leqslant\varepsilon+R(X)\ldotp

Finally let us define the map
\widetilde{\nu}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
by

\widetilde{\nu}_{X}:=\begin{cases}\nu^{M}_{X}\text{ for }X\in\Omega_{M}\setminus\Omega_{M-1}\\
\widetilde{\mu}\text{ for }X\not\in\bigcup_{M=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{M},\end{cases}

where
\widetilde{\mu}
is some arbitrary element of the (non-empty) set
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
. Observe that the choice of
\widetilde{\mu}
does not matter, as we have already observed that the set
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\setminus\bigcup_{M=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{M}
is of
\nu
measure
0
. This set is also Borel since we already know that
R
is Borel measurable, hence each
\Omega_{M}
is Borel. Clearly the map
\widetilde{\nu}
is Borel weak* measurable, i.e., it is a measurable map from
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra into
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
equipped with the weak* topology inherited from
\mathcal{E}_{p}^{*}
. Therefore, we may define
\mu\in(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}))^{*}
as in (
6.7
). It only remains to show that
\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
.

Positivity of
\mu
results immediately from positivity of all
\nu_{X}
and
\nu
. In the same way we show that
\mu
is a probability measure, as

\langle 1,\mu\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}1\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}1\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)=1,

since all measures considered are probability measures. To prove that
\mu
has a finite
p
-th moment we write

\langle\left|\cdot\right|^{p},\mu\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|\cdot+X\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)\ldotp

Using the growth assumption on
F
we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left|\cdot+X\right|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\leqslant C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\leqslant C(R(X)+\varepsilon),

where the last inequality is satisfied for
\nu
-a.e.
X
. Integrating with respect to
\nu
gives

\langle\left|\cdot\right|^{p},\mu\rangle\leqslant C\left(\varepsilon+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}R(X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)\right)<\infty,

since, by assumption,
R
is integrable with respect to
\nu
. Lastly, it remains to show that
\mu
satisfies the inequality in Theorem
3.16
. Fix any continuous functions
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
with
|g(v)|\leqslant C(1+|v|^{p})
for some constant
C
. We have

\displaystyle\langle\mu,g\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}g(\cdot+X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{X}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}\mathcal{Q}g(X)\,\mathrm{d}\nu(X)\geqslant\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{Q}g)(X_{0})=\mathcal{Q}g(X_{0}),

where the first inequality comes from the fact that all
\nu_{X}
’s are Young measures with mean
0
, the second one from the respective property of
\nu
, and the last equality from Lemma
3.10
.
This shows that we indeed have
\mu\in\mathbb{H}^{p}_{X_{0}}
and ends the proof, as discussed earlier (see eq. (
6.7
)).
∎

6.3
Relaxation in the extended real-valued setting

We begin by defining a relaxed notion of convergence for vector fields that are nearly (up to an
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-small error)
\mathbf{a}
-gradients of functions in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
. The notion is reminiscent of the one often used in the
\mathcal{A}
-free setting, where instead of working with sequences that satisfy the constraint exactly, i.e., with
\mathcal{A}V_{j}=0
, one only requires
\mathcal{A}V_{j}\to 0
strongly in
\mathrm{W}^{-1,p}
, see for example
[
40
]
. In the case of standard first order gradients this corresponds to the condition
\mathrm{curl}\,V_{j}\to 0
strongly in
\mathrm{W}^{-1,p}(\Omega)
investigated in
[
53
]
.

Definition 6.9
.

We say that a sequence of vector fields
V_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
is a sequence of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients if there exist sequences
u_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
v_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
such that

V_{j}=\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}+v_{j}

and
v_{j}\to 0
strongly in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
.

Following
[
54
]
we introduce the following notion of convergence:

Definition 6.10
.

We say that a sequence of vector fields
V_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
converges to
V
in the sense of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients if
V_{j}
converges to
V
weakly in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
and
(V_{j}-V)
is a sequence of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients. In such a case we write
V_{j}\to_{\mathbf{a}\text{-}p}V
.

Proposition
3.2
immediately implies the following:

Lemma 6.11
.

Assume that
\Omega
satisfies the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Suppose that a sequence
V_{j}=\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}+v_{j}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n\times m})
of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients converges weakly to
0
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
and generates an oscillation Young measure
\nu
. Then
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}u_{j}\}
generates the same Young measure
\nu
. In particular, any oscillation Young measure generated by a sequence of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients is an oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measure.

Corollary 6.12
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded open Lipschitz domain satisfying the weak
\mathbf{a}
-horn condition. Suppose that
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is bounded from below and closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex. Then the functional

\mathrm{I}(V):=\int_{\Omega}F(V)\,\mathrm{d}x

is sequentially lower semicontinuous with respect to approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradient convergence.

Proof.

Since, by Lemma
6.11
the Young measures generated by sequences of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients are
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures, the argument of Lemma
5.6
carries through unchanged.
∎

The main result here is the following:

Theorem 6.13
.

If
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a continuous integrand satisfying
F(X)\geqslant C|X|^{p}-C^{-1}
for some
C>0
then the sequentially (with respect to approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradient convergence) weakly lower semicontinuous envelope of the functional
\mathrm{I}_{F}
is given by

\overline{\mathrm{I}}_{F}[V]:=\inf_{V_{j}\to_{\mathbf{a}\text{-}p}V}\left\{\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathrm{I}_{F}[V_{j}]\right\}=\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x,

where the infimum is taken over all sequences
V_{j}
converging to
V
in the sense of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradient convergence. As before,
\overline{F}
denotes the closed
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-quasiconvex envelope of
F
.

Proof.

Corollary
6.12
guarantees that
\overline{\mathrm{I}}_{F}[V]\geqslant\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x
, thus we only need to prove the opposite inequality. If
F
is identically equal
+\infty
then there is nothing to show, so we may restrict to proper integrands.
Using a translation we may, without loss of generality, assume
F(X)\geqslant C|X|^{p}
. In any case, the fact that
F
is bounded from below immediately implies the same for
\overline{F}
. Fix any
V\in\mathrm{L}^{p}
. Without loss of generality we may assume
\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
, as otherwise there is nothing to prove. Fix an
\varepsilon>0
and observe that we must have
\overline{F}(V(x))<\infty\text{ a.e. in }\Omega\ldotp

Therefore, using Proposition
6.5
, we may find a family of homogeneous oscillation
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
-gradient Young measures
\{\nu_{x}\}_{x\in\Omega}
with mean
0
and such that, for almost every
x\in\Omega
, we have

\overline{F}(V(x))+\varepsilon\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(\cdot+V(x))\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\ldotp

(6.8)

Using exactly the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
6.5
we may ensure weak* measurability of
x\rightarrow\nu_{x}
. We intend to show that
\nu
is a suitable Young measure using Theorem
3.17
. Recall that we need to prove the following:

i) there exists
v\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
such that

\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}v(x)=\langle\nu_{x},\id\rangle\text{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega;

ii)

\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}|W|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}(W)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty;

iii) for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and all continuous functions
g\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying
|g(W)|\leqslant C(1+|W|^{p})
for some positive constant
C
one has

\langle\nu_{x},g\rangle\geqslant\mathcal{Q}g(\langle\nu_{x},\id\rangle)\ldotp

The first point is clearly satisfied, as all our measures are of mean
0
. The second one may be checked in the same way as in the already mentioned proof of Proposition
6.5
, using the growth assumption on
F
. Finally, the third point results immediately from the fact that all
\nu_{x}
’s are, by definition, elements of
\mathbb{H}^{p}_{0}
, so we may use Theorem
3.16
.

This shows that
\nu
is indeed generated by some
p
-equiintegrable family
\{\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j}\}
with
w_{j}\in\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}(\Omega)
and
w_{j}\rightharpoonup 0
in
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
. For a given
M\in\mathbb{N}
consider

F^{M}(z):=\min(F(z),M(|z|^{p}+1)).

Clearly, for each
M
, the function
F^{M}
is continuous and the family
\{F^{M}(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j})\}_{j}
is
p
-equiintegrable, due to the same property of
\{V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j}\}
. Theorem
3.1
then yields

\int_{\Omega}F^{M}(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j})\,\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F^{M}(V(x)+\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\ldotp

On the other hand, since
F^{M}\leqslant F
and
\nu_{x}
are non-negative and satisfy (
6.8
), we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F^{M}(V(x)+\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}}F(V(x)+\cdot)\,\mathrm{d}\nu_{x}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x+\varepsilon\ldotp

From this we deduce, through a diagonal extraction, that there exists a sequence
j(M)\in\mathbb{N}
with
\lim_{M\rightarrow\infty}j(M)=\infty
such that for all
M
one has

\int_{\Omega}F^{M}(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)})\,\mathrm{d}x\leqslant\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x+2\varepsilon\ldotp

(6.9)

Define the set

\mathcal{G}_{M}:=\left\{x\in\Omega\colon F(V(x)+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)}(x))\leqslant M(|V(x)+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)}(x)|^{p}+1)\right\},

and fix some
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
for which
F(X_{0})<\infty
— such a point exists, as
F
is proper. Next define a vector field
W_{M}
in such a way that

V(x)+W_{M}(x)=(V(x)+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)}(x))\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{M}}+X_{0}\mathds{1}_{\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c}}\ldotp

(6.10)

We claim that
\{V+W_{M}\}_{M}
is an admissible vector field in the
\overline{\mathrm{I}}_{F}[V]
problem.
For that it is enough to show that
\|V+W_{M}-(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0
.
By definition we have

\displaystyle\|V+W_{M}-(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|V+W_{M}-(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|X_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c})}+M^{-1}\left(\int_{\Omega}F^{M}(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p},

where the last inequality comes from the definition of the set
\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c}
and extending the integral to all of
\Omega
. Now, (
6.9
) yields

M^{-1}\left(\int_{\Omega}F^{M}(V+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)})\,\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/p}\leqslant M^{-1}\left(\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x+2\varepsilon\right)^{1/p},

thus showing the desired convergence to
0
in
\mathrm{L}^{p}
, as
\|X_{0}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c})}\to 0
results simply from the fact that clearly the Lebesgue measure of
\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c}
tends to
0
, because

F(V(x)+\nabla_{\mathbf{a}}w_{j(M)}(x))>M\text{ on }\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c},

and we have a uniform (with respect to
M
) bound on the integral of the function in question.
This implies in particular that
V+W_{M}
converges to
V
in the sense of approximate
\mathrm{W}^{\mathbf{a},p}
gradients convergence. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{I}}_{F}[V]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\liminf_{M\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}F(V+W_{M})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\liminf_{M\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathcal{G}_{M}}F^{M}(V+w_{j(M)})\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c}}F(X_{0})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\liminf_{M\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x+2\varepsilon=\int_{\Omega}\overline{F}(V(x))\,\mathrm{d}x+2\varepsilon,

where the last inequality results from (
6.9
) and the measure of
\mathcal{G}_{M}^{c}
tending to
0
. Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary the proof is complete.
∎
