On Goncharov’s conjecture in next to Milnor degree
Vasily Bolbachan

Abstract
Let 𝕂 \mathbb{K} be a field of characteristic zero. We prove that its motivic cohomology in degree m − 1 m-1 and weight m m is rationally isomorphic to the cohomology of the polylogarithmic complex. This gives a partial extension of A. Suslin theorem describing the indecomposable K 3 K_{3} of a field.



Keywords:
motivic cohomology, polylogarithmic complex, Goncharov conjecture, higher Chow groups.

1
Introduction

Let
X
be a smooth variety. S. Bloch
[
Blo86
]
defined higher Chow group of
X
. These groups are bigraded and denoted by
CH^{m}_{\Delta}(X,n)
. They are defined as
n
-th homology of the complex
Z^{m}_{\Delta}(X,*)
, where
Z^{m}_{\Delta}(X,n)
is defined as the group of codimension
m
algebraic cycles on
n
-dimensional algebraic simplex over
X
intersecting all the faces properly. Later A. Suslin and V. Voevodsky defined motivic cohomology
H^{j,m}(X,\mathbb{Z})
of
X
and showed that
H^{j,m}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong CH^{m}_{\Delta}(X,2m-j)

[
Sus00
,
FS02
,
Voe02
]
. Let
H^{j,m}(X,\mathbb{Q})=H^{j,m}(X,\mathbb{Z})\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q}
.

In this paper, we concentrate on the case when
X
is a point. Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field of characteristic zero. Everywhere we work over
\mathbb{Q}
. For instance,
\Lambda^{m}K^{\times}=\Lambda^{m}(K^{\times}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})
.

In
[
Gon95
]
, A. Goncharov defined the polylogarithmic complex
\Gamma(\mathbb{K},m)
. This complex looks as follows:

\Gamma(\mathbb{K},m)\colon\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})\xrightarrow{\delta_{m}}\mathcal{B}_{m-1}(\mathbb{K})\otimes\mathbb{K}^{\times}\xrightarrow{\delta_{m}}\dots\xrightarrow{\delta_{m}}\mathcal{B}_{2}(\mathbb{K})\otimes\Lambda^{m-2}\mathbb{K}^{\times}\xrightarrow{\delta_{m}}\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}.

This complex is concentrated in degrees
[1,m]
. The group
\mathcal{B}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
is the quotient of the free abelian group generated by symbols
\{x\}_{m},x\in\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{K})
by some explicitly defined subgroup
\mathcal{R}_{m}(\mathbb{K})
(see
[
Gon94
]
). The differential is defined as follows:
\delta_{m}(\{x\}_{k}\otimes x_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m})=\{x\}_{k-1}\otimes x\wedge x_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m}
for
k>2
and
\delta_{m}(\{x\}_{2}\otimes x_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m})=x\wedge(1-x)\wedge x_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{m}
.

As it was noted in Section 4.2 of
[
Gon94
]
the group
\mathcal{R}_{2}(\mathbb{K})
is generated by the following elements:

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{5}(-1)^{i}\{c.r.(z_{1},\dots,\widehat{z}_{i},\dots,z_{5})\}_{2},\{0\}_{2},\{1\}_{2},\{\infty\}_{2}.

In this formula
z_{i}
are five different points on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
and
c.r.(\cdot)
is
the cross ratio
defined by the formula

c.r.(a,b,c,d)=\dfrac{(a-c)(b-d)}{(a-d)(b-c)}.

A. Goncharov formulated the following conjecture:

Conjecture 1.1

(Goncharov’s conjecture)
.

For any field
\mathbb{K}
there is a functorial isomorphism
H^{j,m}(\spec\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{j}(\Gamma(\mathbb{K},m))
.

The conjecture
1.1
was formulated 30 years ago, however, it was known only in a very few cases: when
j=m

[
Tot92
,
NS90
]
and when
j=1,m=2

[
Sus91
]
. We remark that both of these cases were known before
[
Gon95
]
. The goal of this paper is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field of characteristic zero and
m\in\mathbb{Z},m\geq 2
. We have the canonical isomorphism
H^{m-1,m}(\spec\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Q})\cong H^{m-1}(\Gamma(\mathbb{K},m))
.

We believe that our result can be extended to arbitrary characteristics. However, for simplicity, we deal only with the case of characteristic zero.

Let us give a more explicit version of this theorem. Denote by
\square^{n}=({\mathbb{P}}^{1}\backslash\{1\})^{n}
the algebraic cube of dimension
n
. Subvarieties of
\square^{n}
given by the equations of the form
z_{i}=0,\infty
are called
faces.
The higher Chow groups are defined as the cohomology of a certain complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},*)
. The group
\mathcal{Z}_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},n)
is a quotient of the group of codimension
m
algebraic cycles on
\square^{n}
intersecting all faces properly by the subgroup of degenerate cycles
[
BK94
]
. The differential is given by the signed sum of restrictions to codimension one faces. We use the alternating version
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{*}
of the complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},2m-*)
which we define in Section
3
.

Let
Y
be a variety and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}
. Define a cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}
on
\square^{n}
as follows. Consider a rational map
Y\dashrightarrow\square^{n}
given by
z\mapsto(f_{1}(z),\dots,f_{n}(z))
. Denote by
Z
the closure of its image and by
\psi
the natural map
Y\dashrightarrow Z
. If
\dim Z<\dim Y
we set
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}=0
. Otherwise, we set
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}=\deg(\psi)[Z]
. When the corresponding cycle intersects faces properly, this cycle gives the canonical element in the complex
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
, where
m=n-\dim Y,j=n-2\dim Y
. We denote this element by the same symbol.

For a chain complex
C_{*}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, denote by
\tau_{\geq k}C_{*}
its
canonical truncation
. By definition, if
j>k
than
(\tau_{\geq k}C_{*})_{j}=C_{j}
. If
j=k
than
(\tau_{\geq k}C_{*})_{j}=C_{k}/(\im d)
and if
j<k
than
(\tau_{\geq k}C_{*})_{j}=0
. The differential of
\tau_{\geq k}C_{*}
is induced by the differential of
C_{*}
. Similarly, denote by
\sigma_{\geq k}
its
stupid truncation
. By definition, if
j\geq k
than
(\sigma_{\geq k}C_{*})_{j}=C_{j}
and if
j<k
than
(\sigma_{\geq k}C_{*})_{j}=0
. The differential of
\sigma_{\geq k}C_{*}
is induced by the differential of
C_{*}
.

Define a subcomplex
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)

[
GMS99
]
of the complex
\tau_{\geq m-1}\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)
as follows. The vector space
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
is generated by the elements of the form
[f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{m+1}]_{X}
, where:
X
is a complete curve, the divisors of the functions
f_{1},f_{2}
are disjoint and the functions
f_{i},i\geq 3
are constants.
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}
is defined as
d(\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1})
. One can check that for any
g_{1},g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
, the element

[g_{1}g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}-[g_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}-[g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}

belongs to
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}
.
We will show that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)
is acyclic. This essentially follows from Beilinson-Soule vanishing
H^{0,1}(\mathbb{K},\mathbb{Q})=0
which is well-known
[
Blo86
]
,
[
MVW06
]
.

Assume that
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed. Denote by
V
the
m
-th symmetric power of the complex
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},1)
. In the beginning of Section
5
we will establish the following isomorphisms

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}\cong\mathrm{coker}(d\colon V_{m-2}\to V_{m-1}),\quad\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}\cong\im(d\colon V_{m-1}\to V_{m}).

Denote
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)=(\tau_{\geq m-1}\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m))/\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)
and
\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)=\tau_{\geq m-1}\Gamma(\mathbb{K},m)
. Define a morphism of complexes

\mathcal{T}\colon\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m).

The chain complexes
\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m),\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
both concentrated in degrees
m-1
and
m
. So, to define
\mathcal{T}
it is enough to define it on the elements of the form
\{a\}_{2}\otimes c_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m}
and
c_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m}
. The element
\{a\}_{2}\otimes c_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m}
goes to

[t,(1-t),(1-a/t),c_{3},\dots,c_{m}]_{{\mathbb{P}}^{1}}.
The element
c_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m}
goes to

[c_{1},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}.
It follows easily from
[
GMS99
]
, that
\mathcal{T}
is a well-defined morphism of complexes. We will give a detailed proof in Section
7.1
. Here is a more precise version of our main result:

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field of characteristic zero and
m\geq 2
. The map
\mathcal{T}
is a quasi-isomorphism. When
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed, this map is an isomorphism.

1.1
The idea of the proof

According to
[
Rud21
,
Blo86
]
on both complexes there is a norm map with good properties. This reduces the general case to the case when
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed.

To prove the main result we will construct some intermediate complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
and a morphism of complexes
W\colon\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
. Let
\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
be
\tau_{\geq m-1}\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
. Denote by
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
induced by
\mathcal{W}
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\circ\mathcal{T}
. We will show that all maps in the following diagram are isomorphisms:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}}

(1)

Let
X
be a proper curve and
g_{1},g_{2},f_{2}\dots,f_{m+1}
are rational functions on
X
. Let
g_{3}=g_{1}g_{2}
. It was stated in
[
GMS99
]
and we will prove in Subsection 4.1, that if for any
i
the cycle
[g_{i},f_{2},\dots,f_{m+1}]_{X}
intersects faces properly, we get the following relation in the group
\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
:

[g_{3},f_{2},\dots,f_{m+1}]_{X}=[g_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{m+1}]_{X}+[g_{2},f_{2},\dots,f_{m+1}]_{X}.

(2)

However, when these cycles are not admissible, this relation does not make any sense. This suggests that there is some chain complex (we call it
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
) with the following properties:

1.

The complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
is a well-defined chain complex concentrated in degrees
m-2,m-1
and
m
.

2.

Let
Y
be a variety of dimension
\leq 2
. Any sequence
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
of non-zero rational functions on
Y
gives a well-defined element in
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{2m-n}
, where
m=n-\dim Y
. Call it
\omega(Y;f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
. The assignment
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\mapsto\omega(Y;f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
gives a well-defined homomorphism

\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{2m-n}.

3.

When the cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}
intersects faces properly, the differential of
\omega(Y;f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is closely related to the differential of the cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}
in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)
.

Let us make this idea precise. We recall that
an alteration
is a proper surjective morphism which is generically finite
[
DJ96
, Introduction]
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
j\in\{m-2,m-1,m\}.
Denote by
\widetilde{\Lambda}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
a vector space freely generated by the isomorphism classes of the pairs
(Y,a)
, where:
Y
is a complete variety over
\mathbb{K}
of dimension
m-j
and
a\in\Lambda^{2m-j}(\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times})
. Denote by
[Y,a]\in\widetilde{\Lambda}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
the corresponding element.
Denote by
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
a quotient of
\widetilde{\Lambda}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
by the following relations:

[\widetilde{Y},\varphi^{*}(a)]=(\deg\varphi)[Y,a].

[Y,a+b]=[Y,a]+[Y,b].

In this formula
\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
is any alteration. The map
\varphi^{*}
is defined by the formula

\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_{n})=\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{n})
. We remark that we write the group law in the group
\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}
additively.

We recall that we assume that
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed. In the case
j=m
we get
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}=\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. Denote by
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
the following cochain complex:

\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)\colon\quad\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-2}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}\to\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}.

The differential is given by a so-called tame symbol map defined by A. Goncharov
[
Gon95
]
.
In Section
4
we will give a precise definition of this complex and will show that it is well-defined. Besides, we will construct a morphism of complexes

\mathcal{W}\colon\sigma_{\geq m-2}\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m),

where
\sigma_{\geq m-2}
is the stupid truncation. The cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}
corresponds to
[\overline{Y},f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]
where
\overline{Y}
is the closure of
Y
in
({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{2m-j}
.

Let us emphasize that there are two main differences between
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)
and
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
:

1.

Let
Y
be a proper variety. For arbitrary non-zero functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
on
Y
, the cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{Y}
, as a cycle on
\square^{n}
, may not intersect faces properly. In this case, this cycle does not give any element in the Bloch complex. However, the element
[Y,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]
is a well-defined element in the complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},n-2\dim Y)_{n-\dim Y}
.

2.

For arbitrary non-zero rational functions
g_{1},g_{2},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}
we have

[Y,(g_{1}g_{2})\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]=[Y,g_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]+[Y,g_{2}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}].

The corresponding identity in
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)
, if true, is satisfied only up to a coboundary.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. The map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
induces the canonical isomorphism

\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m).

For a divisor
D=\sum\limits_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}[D_{\alpha}]
on
X
, denote by
\supp(D)

its support
defined by the formula
\supp D=\sum\limits_{\alpha}[D_{\alpha}]
. For some number of divisors
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
define

\supp(D_{1},\dots,D_{n})=\supp(\supp(D_{1})+\dots+\supp(D_{n})).

Theorem
1.5
will be proved in Section
5
. The idea is contained in the following statement:

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
X
be a smooth variety. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(X)
. Assume that

\supp((f_{1}),\dots,(f_{n}))

is a snc divisor. Then
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
is a linear combination of elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that 1)
\supp((g_{1}),\dots,(g_{n}))
is a snc divisor and 2) the divisors
(g_{1}),\dots,(g_{n})
have no common components.

Example
.

Let
X={\mathbb{P}}^{1}
and
f_{1}=x-a,f_{2}=x-b,a,b\in\mathbb{K},a\neq b
. The divisors
(f_{1}),(f_{2})
have a common point, namely
\infty
. Take
c\in\mathbb{K},c\not\in\{a,b\}
. We can write:

(x-a)\wedge(x-b)=1/2\left(\dfrac{x-a}{x-c}\wedge(x-b)+(x-a)\wedge\dfrac{x-b}{x-c}+\dfrac{x-a}{x-b}\wedge(x-c)\right).

It is easy to see that, for example, the divisors of the functions
(x-a)/(x-c),(x-b)
have no common components. This example was discovered by Eric Wofsey
[
Wof23
]
as an answer to the author’s question on the site math.stackexchange.

This proposition easily implies that the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is surjective. The injectivity will be proved in Section
5
.

As it was stated above, the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is an isomorphism. It remains to prove the following:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be algebraically closed. The map

This theorem will be proved in Section

\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
will follow from some generalization of the main result of

which we will prove in Section

Proposition 1.8

Let
\mathbb{K}
be algebraically closed and
m\in\mathbb{Z},m\geq 2

\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}/\im(d)
is generated by the elements of the form
[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},a]
, where

3
Preliminary definitions and
lemmas

We recall that we work over
\mathbb{Q}
. Throughout the article we assume that the field
\mathbb{K}
has characteristic zero.
An alteration

\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
is a proper surjective morphism between integral schemes which is generically finite.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a discrete valuation field. Denote
\mathcal{O}_{\nu}=\{x\in F|\nu(x)\geq 0\}
the valuation ring,
m_{\nu}=\{x\in F|\nu(x)>0\}
the maximal ideal and
\overline{F}_{\nu}=\mathcal{O}_{\nu}/m_{\nu}
the residue field. We recall that an element
a\in F^{\times}
is called
a uniformiser
if
\nu(a)=1
and
a unit
if
\nu(a)=0
. For
u\in\mathcal{O}_{\nu}
denote by
\overline{u}
its residue class in
\overline{F}_{\nu}
.

The proof of the following proposition can be found in
[
Gon95
]
:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a discrete valuation field and
n\geq 1
. There is a unique map
\ts_{\nu}\colon\Lambda^{n}(F^{\times})\to\Lambda^{n-1}\overline{F}_{\nu}^{\times}
satisfying the following conditions:

1.

When
n=1
we have
\ts_{\nu}(a)=\nu(a)
.

2.

For any units
u_{2},\dots,u_{n}\in F
we have
\ts_{\nu}(x\wedge u_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{n})=\nu(x)\cdot(\overline{u_{2}}\dots\wedge\overline{u_{n}})
.

The map
\ts_{\nu}
is called
tame symbol map
. The following lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(F,\nu)
be a discrete valuation field. Let
k,n
be two natural numbers satisfying the condition
k<n
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\in F^{\times}
and
a_{k+1},\dots,a_{n}
be
\nu
-adic units. Then:

\ts_{\nu}(a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{n})=\ts_{\nu}(a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{k})\wedge\overline{a_{k+1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\overline{a_{n}}.

Let
X
be a smooth algebraic variety and
D\subset X
be a prime divisor. Denote by
\nu_{D}
a discrete valuation corresponding to
D
. Abusing notation, we write
\ts_{D}
for
\ts_{\nu_{D}}
. Let
Y
be a variety. Denote
{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}}:=\Lambda^{n}(\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})
. Here
\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}
is the multiplicative group of the field
\mathbb{K}(Y)
considered as an abelian group. Let
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be a dominant morphism between varieties. Denote by
\varphi^{*}
the map
\mathbb{K}(Y_{2})^{\times}\to\mathbb{K}(Y_{1})^{\times}
given by the formula
\varphi^{*}(\alpha)=\alpha\circ\varphi
. Denote by the same symbol the map
{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y_{2})^{\times}}\to{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y_{1})^{\times}}
given by the formula
\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_{n})=\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{n})
. Let
D_{1}\subset Y_{1},D_{2}\subset Y_{2}
be prime divisors such that
\overline{\varphi(D_{1})}=D_{2}
. Denote by
e(D_{1},D_{2})
the multiplicity of
\varphi^{-1}(D_{2})
along
D_{1}
. This number can be computed as
\ord_{D_{1}}(\varphi^{*}(\alpha))
, where
\alpha
is any rational function on
Y_{2}
satisfying
\ord_{D_{2}}(f)=1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be a dominant morphism between smooth varieties. Let
D_{1}\subset Y_{1},D_{2}\subset Y_{2}
be two prime divisors such that
\overline{\varphi(D_{1})}=D_{2}
. Denote by
\overline{\varphi}
the natural map
D_{1}\to D_{2}
. Then for any
a\in{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}}
we have:

\ts_{D_{1}}(\varphi^{*}(a))=e(D_{1},D_{2})(\overline{\varphi})^{*}(\ts_{D_{2}}(a)).

Proof.

We can assume that
a=\xi_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\xi_{n}
such that
\ord_{D_{2}}{\xi_{1}}=1
and
\ord_{D_{2}}(\xi_{i})=0
for any
i>1
. The number
\ord_{D_{1}}(\varphi^{*}(\xi_{i}))
is equal to
e(D_{1},D_{2})
if
i=1
and is equal to
0
otherwise. Now the statement follows from Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Let
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be a morphism of finite degree and fix two prime divisors
D_{1}\subset Y_{1}
and
D_{2}\subset Y_{2}
such that
\overline{\varphi(D_{1})}=D_{2}
. We have a finite extension
\mathbb{K}(D_{2})\subset\mathbb{K}(D_{1})
. Denote its degree by
f(D_{1},D_{2})
. Denote by
Div(\varphi)_{cont}
the set of divisors contracted under
\varphi
. Let
D\subset Y_{2}
be a prime divisor. Denote by
Div(\varphi,D)
the set of prime divisors
D^{\prime}\subset Y_{1}
such that
\overline{\varphi(D^{\prime})}=D
. There is a bijection between the set
Div(\varphi,D)
and the set of extensions of the discrete valuation
\nu_{D}
to the field
\mathbb{K}(Y_{1})
. The following statement is well known:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration between smooth varieties. Let
D\subset Y_{2}
be a prime divisor. The following formula holds:

\deg\varphi=\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)}e(D^{\prime},D)f(D^{\prime},D).

3.1
Strictly regular elements

Let us recall the definition of a simple normal crossing divisor.
Let
X
be a smooth variety. For a divisor
D=\sum\limits_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}[D_{\alpha}]
on
X
, denote by
\supp(D)

its support
defined by the formula
\supp D=\sum\limits_{\alpha}[D_{\alpha}]
. For some number of divisors
D_{1},\dots,D_{n}
, we set

\supp(D_{1},\dots,D_{n})=\supp(\supp(D_{1})+\dots+\supp(D_{n})).

A divisor
D=\sum n_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}
is called
simple normal crossing
if for any closed point
z\in D
, there is a regular local system of parameters
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{d}
at
z
, such that locally
D
is given by the equation
\pi_{1}\dots\pi_{l}=0
for some
l\leq d
.
A divisor
D
is called
supported on a simple normal crossing divisor
if
\supp(D)
is a simple normal crossing divisor.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
X
be a smooth variety and
x\in X
. An element
b\in{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}}
is called
strictly regular at
x
if there is some open subset
U
containing
x
and a presentation of
b
in the form

\sum_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}f_{1}^{\alpha}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}^{\alpha}

such that for any
\alpha
, the restriction of the divisor
\supp((f_{1}^{\alpha}),\dots,(f_{n}^{\alpha}))
to
U
is supported on a simple normal crossing divisor on
U
. The element
b
is called
strictly regular
if this condition holds for any
x\in X
.

More generally, an element
b
of the group
\Gamma(\mathbb{K}(X),m)_{j}
is called
strictly regular
at
x
, if there is some open subset
U
containing
x
and a presentation of
b
in the form
\sum_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}\{f_{1}^{\alpha}\}_{k}\otimes f_{2}^{\alpha}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{r}^{\alpha}
such that for any
\alpha
the restriction of the divisor
\supp((f_{1}^{\alpha}),\dots,(f_{r}^{\alpha}))
to
U
is supported on a simple normal crossing divisor on
U
. The element
b
is called
strictly regular
if this condition holds for any
x\in X
.

We have the following simple lemma:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
X
be a proper variety and
a\in\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}
. There is a proper birational morphism
\varphi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
such that
\varphi^{*}(a)\in\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(\widetilde{X})^{\times}
is strictly regular.

Proof.

Indeed, let

a=\sum_{\alpha}l_{\alpha}f_{1}^{\alpha}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}^{\alpha}

and

D=\sup\left(\sum_{i,\alpha}\sup((f_{i}^{\alpha}))\right).

By Hironaka resolution of singularities
[
Hir64
]
, there is a proper birational morphism
\widetilde{X}\to X
such that
X
is smooth and
\varphi^{*}(D)
is supported on a snc divisor. It follows that
\varphi^{*}(a)
is strictly regular.
∎

We will need the following simple lemma:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
X
be a smooth
d
-dimensional algebraic variety and
x\in X
. Let
a\in\Gamma(k(X),m)_{j}
and assume that
a
is strictly regular at
x
. The element
a
can be represented as a linear combination of elements of the following form:

\{\pi_{1}^{n_{1}}\dots\pi_{d}^{n_{d}}u_{0}\}\otimes\pi_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\pi_{r^{\prime}}\wedge u_{r^{\prime}+1}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{r}.

Here
\pi_{i}
is a regular system of parameters at x and all the functions
u_{i}
are regular at
x
and take non-zero values at this point.

Proof.

The statement follows from the following fact, which in turn follows from the definition of a snc divisor. Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
be non-zero rational functions on
X
and
U
be some open subset containing
x
. Assume that the restriction of the divisor
\sup((f_{1}),\dots,(f_{k}))
to
U
is supported on a simple crossing divisor on
U
. Then there is a regular system of parameters
\pi_{1},\dots\pi_{d}
at
x
, rational functions
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
which are regular at
x
and take non-zero values at this point and integers
l_{i,j}
such that for any
1\leq i\leq k
, we have:
f_{i}=u_{i}\pi_{1}^{l_{i,1}}\dots\pi_{d}^{l_{i,d}}
.
∎

3.2
Higher Chow groups

We recall that two irreducible subvarieties
V,W
of some ambient variety
Y

intersect properly
, if for any irreducible component
Z
of
V\cap W
, we have
\dim Z=\dim V+\dim W-\dim Y
.

The definition of Bloch’s higher Chow group can be found in
[
BK94
]
. We use the cubical alternating version. Denote by
\square^{n}=({\mathbb{P}}^{1}\backslash\{1\})^{n}
the algebraic cube of dimension
n
. Subvarieties of
\square^{n}
given by the equations of the form
z_{i}=0,\infty
are called faces. A cycle is called
admissible
if it intersects all the faces properly. Let
z^{m}_{\square}(\mathbb{K},n)
be the free abelian group generated by codimension
m
admissible cycles on
\square^{n}
. We have the natural projections
\pi_{i,n}\colon\square^{n}\to\square^{n-1}
given by the formula
\pi_{i,n}(t_{1},\dots,t_{i},\dots,t_{n})=(t_{1},\dots\hat{t_{i}},\dots,t_{n})
. Denote by
D^{m}(\mathbb{K},n)\subset z^{m}(\mathbb{K},n)
a subgroup generated by the images of the maps
\pi_{i,n}^{*}\colon z_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},n-1)\to z^{m}_{\square}(\mathbb{K},n),i=1,\dots,n
. We set
\mathcal{Z}^{m}_{\square}(\mathbb{K},n)=z^{m}_{\square}(\mathbb{K},n)/D^{m}(\mathbb{K},n)
.

If
X
is a variety,
Z\subset X
be an equidimensional closed subset and
Z^{\prime}\subset Z
be some irreducible component of
Z
, we denote by
mult_{Z^{\prime}}(Z)

the multiplicity
of
Z^{\prime}
in
Z
(see
[
Ful13
, 1.5]
).
The cycle associated to
Z
is defined by the formula

[Z]=\sum\limits_{Z^{\prime}}\mult_{Z^{\prime}}(Z)[Z^{\prime}].

In this formula the sum is taken over all irreducible components of
Z
.

Let
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
\varepsilon\in\{0,\infty\}
. Denote by
F_{i,\varepsilon}
a face given by the equation
t_{i}=\varepsilon
. Define a map
\ts_{i,\varepsilon}\colon\mathcal{Z}_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},n)\to\mathcal{Z}_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},n-1)
given by the formula
[Z]\mapsto[Z\cap F_{i,\varepsilon}].
In this formula we identify
F_{i,\varepsilon}
with
\square^{n-1}
and consider
[Z\cap F_{i,\varepsilon}]
as a cycle on
\square^{n-1}
.

Define the differential by the following formula

d=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+1}(\ts_{i,0}-\ts_{i,\infty}).

One can show that
d^{2}=0
. Up to a sign this definition coincides with the definition given in
[
BK94
]
.

The main idea of this paper is to compare higher Chow groups with the complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
. The generators of the complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
have the form
[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]
. These elements are clearly antisymmetric with respect to
f_{i}
. For this reason, it is convenient to use antisymmetric version of higher Chow groups, which we want to recall.

Let
\mathbb{Q}[S_{n}]
be the group algebra of symmetric group. Define

alt_{n}=\dfrac{1}{n!}\sum\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}sgn(\sigma)[\sigma]\in\mathbb{Q}[S_{n}].

There is a natural action of the group
S_{n}
on
\square^{n}
by permuting coordinates. Let
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}=alt_{2m-j}(\mathcal{Z}^{m}_{\square}(\mathbb{K},2m-j))
. We consider
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{*}
as a cohomological complex. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{*}
is a subcomplex of
\mathcal{Z}_{\square}^{m}(\mathbb{K},2m-*)
. Similarly to
[
BK94
, Proposition 5.1.]
(see also p. 581 from loc.cit.) it can be shown that the natural inclusion is a quasi-isomorphism.

4
The complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)

In this section, we assume that
\mathbb{K}
is an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.

4.1
Definition of the complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)

Definition 4.1
.

Let
m\geq 0
and
j\in\{m-2,m-1,m\}
. Set

\begin{cases}p=m-j\\
n=2m-j\end{cases}

We have

\begin{cases}m=n-p\\
j=n-2p\end{cases}

Throughout the paper we will use the pairs of integers
(m,j),(n,p)
interchangeably.

Definition 4.2
.

Denote by
\widetilde{\Lambda}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
a vector space freely generated by the isomorphism classes of the pairs
(Y,a)
, where:
Y
is a complete variety over
\mathbb{K}
of dimension
p=m-j
and
a\in\Lambda^{n}(\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{Q})
. Denote by
[Y,a]\in\widetilde{\Lambda}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
the corresponding element.
Denote by
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
the quotient of
\widetilde{\Lambda}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
by the following relations:

[\widetilde{Y},\varphi^{*}(a)]=(\deg\varphi)[Y,a].

[Y,a+b]=[Y,a]+[Y,b].

In this formula
\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
is any alteration. The map
\varphi^{*}
is defined by the formula

\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\alpha_{n})=\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\varphi^{*}(\alpha_{n})
.

Let us formulate this definition in a more categorical fashion. Denote by
{\mathcal{V}ar}_{p}
the category of complete varieties over
\mathbb{K}
of dimension
p
and their proper dominant morphisms. Denote by
{\mathcal{V}ect}_{\mathbb{Q}}
the category of vector spaces over
\mathbb{Q}
. Define a contravariant functor

\mathrm{EP}^{n}\colon{\mathcal{V}ar}_{p}\to{\mathcal{V}ect}_{\mathbb{Q}}.

Its value on some variety
Y\in{\mathcal{V}ar}_{p}
is equal to the vector space
{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}}=\Lambda^{n}(\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\mathbb{Q}})
. For a morphism
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
the corresponding map
{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y_{2})^{\times}}\to{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y_{1})^{\times}}
is defined by the formula
\Lambda^{n}(\varphi)(a)=\dfrac{1}{\deg{\varphi}}(\varphi^{*}(a))
.

Definition 4.3
.

\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}=\colim\limits_{Y\in{\mathcal{V}ar}_{p}}{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}}.

As the category
{\mathcal{V}ar}_{p}
is essentially small this colimit is a well-defined vector space over
\mathbb{Q}
. It is easy to see that the two definitions above are equivalent.

For a variety
Y
and
a\in{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}}
, we denote the corresponding element in
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
by
[Y,a]
. For any alteration
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
we have
[Y_{2},a]=\dfrac{1}{\deg\varphi}[Y_{1},\varphi^{*}(a)]
.

The complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
concentrated in degrees
m-2,m-1,m
and has the following form:

\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-2}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}.

Let
j\in\{m-2,m-1\}
. Define a map
d\colon\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j+1}
as follows. Let
[Y,a]\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
. Choose a proper birational morphism
\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
with smooth
\widetilde{Y}
such that the element
\varphi^{*}(a)
would be strictly regular. (In the case
j=m-1
the condition that
\varphi^{*}(a)
should be strictly regular is trivial). Such a morphism exists by Lemma
3.6
. Define

d([Y,a])=\sum\limits_{D\subset\widetilde{Y}}[D,\ts_{D}(\varphi^{*}(a))].

In this formula the sum is taken over all prime divisors
D\subset\widetilde{Y}
. In this section, we will show that the map
d
is well-defined and satisfies
d^{2}=0
.

4.2
Some vanishing statements

To prove that
d
is well-defined we will need the following proposition:

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
S
be a smooth proper surface. Let
b\in{\Lambda^{m+2}\mathbb{K}(S)^{\times}}
be strictly regular. Then for any alteration
\varphi\colon\widetilde{Y}\to Y
and any divisor
E\subset\widetilde{Y}
contracted under
\varphi
we have
[E,\ts_{E}(\varphi^{*}(a))]=0\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j+1}
.

Remark 4.5
.

It is easy to see that the condition that
b
is strictly regular is essential. Indeed, let
S\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
be a blow-up of some point and let
E\subset S
be the exceptional divisor. It is easy to see that the tame-symbol map
\ts_{E}\colon\Lambda^{m+2}\mathbb{K}(S)^{\times}\to\Lambda^{m+1}\mathbb{K}(E)^{\times}
is surjective. Therefore, if the previous lemma were satisfied without the condition that
b
is strictly regular, then for any
a\in\Lambda^{m+1}\mathbb{K}({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{\times}
, the element
[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},a]\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
would vanish. However, by Theorem
4.16
such elements generate the group
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
. So the group
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
would vanish. And Theorem
1.7
would imply that the differential in the polylogarithmic complex
\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
would be zero. However, it is easy to see that the differential in this complex is nonzero.

The previous proposition follows from the following two lemmas:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
S
be a smooth proper surface. Let
b\in\Lambda^{m+2}L^{\times}
be strictly regular. For any alteration
\varphi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
and any divisor
E\subset\widetilde{S}
contracted under
\varphi
, the element
\ts_{E}(\varphi^{*}(b))
belongs to the subgroup generated by the elements of the form
f\wedge c_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m+1}
, where
f\in\mathbb{K}(E)^{\times}
and
c_{i}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
.

The proof of this lemma is essentially contained in the proof of Lemma 2.16 from
[
Bol23
]
. We remark that the proof of Lemma 2.16 from
[
Bol23
]
used Lemma 2.14 which proof was not correct. However, Lemma 2.14 is a particular case of Lemma
3.7
from this paper, which proof was given in Section
3
. The same remark applies to results stated in Section 4.3 and Section 7 of this paper.

Proof.

Denote by
z\subset E
the image of
E
under
\varphi
. We apply Lemma
3.7
. We can assume that
a
has the form
f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge g_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{m+2}
such that
g_{i}
are regular at
z
and take non-zero values at this point. This implies that the functions
\left.{\varphi^{*}(g_{i})}\right|_{E}
are non-zero constants. Now, the statement follows from Lemma
3.2
for
k=2
.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve,
f\in\mathbb{K}(X)
and
c_{2},\dots,c_{m+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. We have:

[X,f_{1}\wedge c_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m+1}]=0\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}.

Proof.

Consider the multiplication map
\mu\colon\Lambda(\mathbb{K},0)_{0}\otimes\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
given by the formula

[X,f]\times(a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{m})\mapsto[X,f\wedge a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge a_{m}].

The statement of this lemma says that
\mu
is zero. To prove this, it is enough to show that
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},0)_{0}=0
. So we can assume that
m=0
. We need to check that for any
f\in\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}
we have

[X,f]=0\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},0)_{0}.

Consider the following two cases:

1.

f
is constant. Choose some morphism
\psi\colon X\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
of finite degree. We get

[X,c]=(\deg\psi)[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},c].

So we can assume that
X={\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Choose some morphism
\psi_{2}\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
of degree
2
. We get:

[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},c]=(\deg\psi_{2})({\mathbb{P}}^{1},c)=2[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},c].

So
[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},c]=0
.

2.

Let
f
be non-constant. We get:

[X,f]=(\deg f)[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t].

In this formula
t
is the standard coordinate on
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Let
\psi\colon{\mathbb{P}}^{1}\to{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
be a map given by the formula
\psi(z)=z^{-1}
. We get:

[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t]=(\deg\psi)^{-1}[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},\psi^{*}(t)]=[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t^{-1}]=-[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t].

So
[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t]=0
and hence
[X,f]=0
.

∎

The following lemma will be used in Section
4.4
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
S
be a proper surface and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
be regular maps from
S
to
\mathbb{P}^{1}
considered as rational functions on
S
. Let
D_{i}^{1}=f_{i}^{-1}(1)
. Set
Z=\cup D_{i}^{1}
and
U=S\backslash Z
. Denote by
D_{i}^{0},D_{i}^{\infty}
the divisors of zeros and poles of the functions
f_{i}
on
U
. Assume that the divisors
D_{1}^{0},D_{1}^{\infty},\dots,D_{n}^{0},D_{n}^{\infty}
do not have common components and any three such divisors do not have common points. Let
\widetilde{S}
be a smooth variety and
\varphi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
be an alteration. Then for any divisor
E\subset\widetilde{S}
contracted under
\varphi
we have

[E,\ts_{E}(\varphi^{*}(f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}))]=0\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},n-2)_{n-3}.

Proof.

We can assume that
n\geq 1
. Denote by
w
the image of
E
under
\varphi
. Assume that
w\subset Z_{i}
for some
i
. Then the rational function
\left.{\varphi^{*}(f_{i})}\right|_{E}
is equal to
1
and the statement is obvious. Assume that
w
is not contained in
Z
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
w\notin D_{i}^{0},D_{i}^{1}
for any
i\geq 3
. This implies that there are elements
c_{3},\dots,c_{n}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
so that
\left.{\varphi^{*}(f_{i})}\right|_{E}=c_{i}
. Now the statement follows from Lemma
3.2
and the previous lemma.
∎

The following lemma will be used in Section
5.4
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
X
be a proper variety and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}
. Denote by
s
the transcendence degree of the extension
\mathbb{K}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})\subset\mathbb{K}(X)
. Assume that
s>0
. Then

[Y,f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]=0\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},n-\dim Y)_{n-2\dim Y}.

Proof.

Let
L=\mathbb{K}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
. There is an algebraic variety
X^{\prime}
, a rational map
\psi\colon X\dashrightarrow X^{\prime}
and rational functions
\widetilde{f}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{f}_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(X^{\prime})^{\times}
such that
f_{i}=\psi^{*}(\widetilde{f}_{i})
. Taking resolution of singularities, we can assume that
\psi
is regular.

Choose a transcendence basis
t_{1},\dots,t_{s}
of
\mathbb{K}(X)
over
\mathbb{K}(X^{\prime})
. The extension

\mathbb{K}(X^{\prime})(t_{1},\dots,t_{s})\subset\mathbb{K}(X)
is finite. This implies that there is a rational map
g\colon X\dashrightarrow X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s}
of finite degree such that
\psi=\pi\circ g
, where
\pi\colon X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s}\to X^{\prime}
is the natural projection. Taking resolution of singularities, we can assume that
g
is regular. We get:

\displaystyle[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]=(\deg g)[X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s},\widetilde{f}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{f}_{n}].

Thus we have reduced the statement to the case when
X=X\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s},s>0
and
f_{i}
are functions on
X^{\prime}
. Let
\theta
be some morphism
\theta\colon({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s}\to({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s}
of degree
2
. We get

\displaystyle[X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s},\widetilde{f}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{f}_{n}]=

\displaystyle 1/2[X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s},(id\times\theta)^{*}(\widetilde{f}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{f}_{n})]=

\displaystyle 1/2[X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s},\widetilde{f}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{f}_{n}].

It follows that
[X^{\prime}\times({\mathbb{P}}^{1})^{s},\widetilde{f}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{f}_{n}]=0
.

∎

4.3
The differential is well-defined

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
X
be a variety. The complex
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)
is well defined.

Let
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be smooth varieties and
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration. Let
D_{1}\subset Y_{1},D_{2}\subset Y_{2}
be prime divisors such that
\varphi(D_{1})=D_{2}
. We recall that
e(D_{1},D_{2})
denotes the multiplicity of
\varphi^{*}(D_{2})
along
D_{1}
and
f(D_{1},D_{2})
denotes the degree of the extension
\mathbb{K}(D_{2})\subset\mathbb{K}(D_{1})
. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be smooth varieties and
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration. Let
D_{1}\subset Y_{1},D_{2}\subset Y_{2}
be prime divisors such that
\varphi(D_{1})=D_{2}
. For any
a\in\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y_{2})^{\times}
we have

[D_{1},\ts_{D_{1}}(\varphi^{*}(a))]=e(D_{1},D_{2})f(D_{1},D_{2})[D_{2},\ts_{D_{2}}(a)].

Proof.

Denote the natural map
D_{1}\to D_{2}
by
\psi
. We have:

[D_{2},\ts_{D_{2}}(a)]=(\deg\psi)^{-1}[D_{1},\psi^{*}(\ts_{D_{2}}(a))].

As
f(D_{1},D_{2})=\deg\psi
, we get

e(D_{1},D_{2})f(D_{1},D_{2})[D_{2},\ts_{D_{2}}(a)]=e(D_{1},D_{2})[D_{1},\psi^{*}(\ts_{D_{2}}(a))].

So it remains to prove that

e(D_{1},D_{2})\psi^{*}(\ts_{D_{2}}(a))=\ts_{D_{1}}(\varphi^{*}(a)).

This is a statement of Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
j\in\{m-2,m-1\}.
The map
d\colon\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j+1}
is well-defined.

Proof.

When
j=m-1
, the statement is obvious. So we can assume that
j=m-2
. Let
a\in{\Lambda^{m+2}\mathbb{K}(S)^{\times}}
. By Lemma
3.6
there is a proper birational morphism
\varphi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
such that the element
\varphi^{*}(a)
is strictly regular.
We recall that the element
d([S,a])
is defined by the formula

\sum\limits_{D\subset\widetilde{S}}[D,\ts_{D}(\varphi^{*}(a))].

We need to prove that this element does not depend on
\varphi
and is functorial with respect to alterations between surfaces.

Let
\varphi_{1}\colon\widetilde{S}_{1}\to S
and
\varphi_{2}\colon\widetilde{S}_{2}\to S
be two such morphisms. It follows from Lemma
3.6
that there are proper birational morphisms
\varphi_{31}\colon\widetilde{S}_{3}\to S_{1},\varphi_{32}\colon\widetilde{S}_{3}\to S_{2}
such that
\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{31}=\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{32}
and the element
(\varphi_{1}\circ\varphi_{31})^{*}(a)=(\varphi_{2}\circ\varphi_{32})^{*}(a)
is strictly regular. This shows that we only need to check the following statement. Let
\varphi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
be an alteration and
a\in{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(Y)^{\times}}
. Assume that the elements
a
and
\varphi^{*}(a)
are strictly regular. Then the following formula holds:

\sum\limits_{D\subset S}[D,\ts_{D}(a)]=(\deg\varphi)^{-1}\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{S}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(a))].

We have:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{S}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(a))]=

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi)_{cont}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(a))]+\sum\limits_{D\subset S}\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(a))].

In this formula
Div(\varphi)_{cont}
is the set of divisors contracted under
\varphi
and
Div(\varphi,D)
the set of divisors whose image under
\varphi
is equal to
D
. Each term in the first sum vanishes due to Proposition
4.4
. By Lemma
4.11
and Lemma
3.4
the second term is equal to

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{D\subset S}\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)}e(D^{\prime},D)f(D^{\prime},D)[D,\ts_{D}(a)]=

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{D\subset S}[D,\ts_{D}(a)]\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)}e(D^{\prime},D)f(D^{\prime},D)=

\displaystyle\deg(\varphi)\sum\limits_{D\subset S}[D,\ts_{D}(a)].

The proposition is proved.
∎

To finish the proof of Theorem
4.10
we need to show that
d^{2}=0
. This is a direct corollary of the following theorem:

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
S
be a smooth complete surface. Let
a\in{\Lambda^{m+2}\mathbb{K}(S)^{\times}}
be strictly regular. We have

\sum\limits_{D\subset S}\sum\limits_{z\in D}\ts_{z}\ts_{D}(a)=0\in\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}.

Here the first sum is taken over all prime divisors on
S
. It is clear that in this sum there are only finitely many non-zero terms.

The point of this theorem is that for given
z
there are precisely two
D
such that
z\in D
and
\ts_{z}\ts_{D}(a)\neq 0
. Moreover, these two elements have opposite signs. The particular case of this theorem when
m=2
was proved in
[
Bol23
, Theorem 2.15]
. The proof of the general case is similar. The case
m=0
follows from classical Parshin reciprocity law
[
Par75
]
.

The proof of Theorem
4.10
.

By Proposition
4.12
we already know that
d
is well-defined. Let us prove that
d^{2}=0
. We need to show that
d^{2}([S,a])=0
. By Lemma
3.6
, there is a proper birational map
\varphi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
so that
\varphi^{*}(a)
is strictly regular. So
[S,a]=[\widetilde{S},\varphi^{*}(a)]
. This shows that we can assume that
a
is strictly regular.
We have

d^{2}([S,a])=\sum\limits_{D\subset S}d([D,\ts_{D}(a)]).

Now the statement follows from the fact that

d([D,\ts_{D}(a)])=\sum_{z\in D}[\spec\mathbb{K},\ts_{z}\ts_{D}(a)].

and Theorem
4.13
.
∎

We recall that we have given the definition of cochain complex calculated cubical higher Chow group

We recall that
\sigma_{\geq m-2}

In this formula

to

are homogenious coordinates on the

Theorem 4.14
.

The map
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
is a morphism of complexes. In particular, the map
\mathcal{W}
is a morphism of complexes as well.

Let
Y
be a variety. For a Cartier divisor
D
on
Y
, denote the corresponding Weil divisor by
[D]
.
We need the following lemma:

Lemma 4.15
.

Let
Y_{1}
,
Y_{2}
be varieties and
\varphi\colon Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
be an alteration. Then for any Cartier divisor
D
on

we have

\varphi_{*}[\varphi^{*}(D)]=(\deg\varphi)[D].

In this formula
\varphi^{*}
is pullback on Cartier divisors and
\varphi_{*}
is pushforward on Weil divisors.

Proof.

As the statement is local on
Y_{2}
, we can assume that

is principal. In this case the statement follows from
[
Ful13
, Proposition 1.4]
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
4.14
.

Denote by
\widetilde{t}_{i}\in\mathbb{K}(\overline{Z})
the restriction of the coordinate function
t_{i}
to
\overline{Z}
. Let
a=\widetilde{t}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{t}_{n}
and
a_{i}=\widetilde{t}_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{t}_{i-1}\wedge\widetilde{t}_{i+1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widetilde{t}_{n}
. Let
\varphi\colon\widetilde{Z}\to\overline{Z}
be a proper birational morphism with smooth
\widetilde{Z}
such that the element
\widetilde{a}:=\varphi^{*}(a)
is strictly regular. We have

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(Z)=[\overline{Z},a].

\displaystyle d(\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(Z))=\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{Z}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})]=\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in\Div(\varphi)_{cont}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})]+\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in\Div(\varphi)_{gen}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})].

In this formula
Div(\varphi)_{cont}
is the set of divisors contracted under
\varphi
and
Div(\varphi)_{gen}
is the set of divisors which are not contracted under
\varphi
. By Lemma
4.8
, the first sum is equal to zero. For any divisor
D\subset\overline{Z}
, the set of divisors
D^{\prime}\subset\widetilde{Z}
such that

is denoted by

. We get

\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi)_{gen}}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})]=\sum\limits_{D\subset\overline{Z}}\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in\Div(\varphi,D)}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})].

We recall that
d([Z])
is defined as

\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+1}(\ts_{i,0}([Z])-\ts_{i,\infty}([Z])),

where
\ts_{i,\varepsilon}
is the cycle associated to the closed subset
Z\cap\{x_{i}=\varepsilon\}
, considered as a cycle on
(\mathbb{P}^{1}\backslash\{1\})^{n-1}
. By the definition of a cycle associated to a closed subset, we get

\displaystyle\ts_{i,0}([Z])-\ts_{i,\infty}([Z])=\sum_{D\subset Z}\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})[\widetilde{t}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{t}_{i-1},\widetilde{t}_{i+1},\dots,\widetilde{t}_{n}]_{D}=

\displaystyle\sum_{D\subset\overline{Z}}\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})[\widetilde{t}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{t}_{i-1},\widetilde{t}_{i+1},\dots,\widetilde{t}_{n}]_{D}.

The last formula holds because if

then one of
\widetilde{t}_{i}
is identically equal to
1
and so the expression
\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})[\widetilde{t}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{t}_{i-1},\widetilde{t}_{i+1},\dots,\widetilde{t}_{n}]_{D}
vanishes. We get:

\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(d(Z))=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}D\subset\overline{Z}\\
\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})\neq 0\end{subarray}}(-1)^{i+1}\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})[D,i_{D}^{*}(a_{i})].

In this formula

is the embedding of
D
into
\overline{Z}
. As
Z
intersects all the faces properly, for any divisor
D\subset\overline{Z}
there is at most one
i
, such that
\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})\neq 0
. Fix
D
and consider the following two cases:

1.

For any
i
we have
\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})=0
. It follows from Lemma
4.15
that for any
D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)
we have
\ord_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(\widetilde{t}_{i}))=0
. So
\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})=0
and we get
[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})]=0
.

2.

There is some
i
such that
\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})\neq 0
. It remains to check the following formula:

(-1)^{i+1}\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})[D,i_{D}^{*}(a_{i})]=\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)}[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})].

Denote by
g_{D^{\prime}}
the natural map
D^{\prime}\to\overline{Z}
.
As
\ord_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(\widetilde{t}_{j}))=0
for any

, it follows from the definition of tame symbol(see Proposition
3.1
) that

\displaystyle[D^{\prime},\ts_{D^{\prime}}(\widetilde{a})]=(-1)^{i+1}\ord_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(\widetilde{t}_{i}))[D^{\prime},g_{D^{\prime}}^{*}(a_{i})]=

\displaystyle(-1)^{i+1}\ord_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(\widetilde{t}_{i}))f(D^{\prime},D)[D,i_{D}^{*}(a_{i})].

It remains to show that

\ord_{D}(\widetilde{t}_{i})=\sum\limits_{D^{\prime}\in Div(\varphi,D)}f(D^{\prime},D)\ord_{D^{\prime}}(\varphi^{*}(\widetilde{t}_{i})).

This follows from Lemma
4.15
.

Theorem 4.16

(Proposition
1.8
)
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. The group
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}/\im(d)

[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},a],

Essentially, this statement follows from

Proof.

Denote by
A
the subgroup of
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}

and
\im(d)
.

Let

. Choose a polynomial

\mathbb{K}(X)

For an irreducible polynomial

\nu(Q)

\Lambda^{m+2}F(x)\xrightarrow{(\ts_{\nu(Q)})}\bigoplus\limits_{Q}\Lambda^{m+1}(F[x]/Q)^{\times}.

with
\mathbb{K}(S)

corresponds to some curve

birationally isomorphic to

. By construction,

In this formula, we denoted by

5
The map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is an isomorphism

Define a subcomplex
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)\subset\mathcal{N}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
as follows. The vector space
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
is generated by the elements of the form
[f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}
, where:
X
is a complete curve, the divisors of the functions

are disjoint and the functions

are constants. The vector space
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}
is defined as

. In the case

this complex was defined in
[

]
. The subcomplex
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)
is defined for any field. The field
\mathbb{K}
is not necessary algebraically closed. In the next subsection we will show that
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)
is acyclic.

Assume now that
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed. We need the following lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve,
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{K}(X)
and
c_{3},\dots,c_{m+1}\in\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. We have:

[X,f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge c_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m+1}]=0\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}.

Proof.

We can assume that
m=1
. If
f_{1}
is constant then the statement follows from Lemma
4.7
. So we can assume that
f_{1}
is not constant.

The extension
\mathbb{K}(f_{1})\subset\mathbb{K}(X)
is finite. As any separable extension is contained in some finite Galois extension, there is a finite extension
\mathbb{K}(X)\subset L
so that the corresponding extension
\mathbb{K}(f_{1})\subset L
is Galois. Choose a smooth projective curve
Y
together with a morphism
\psi\colon Y\to X
such that
\mathbb{K}(Y)\cong L
and the field extension
\mathbb{K}(X)\subset L
is induced by
\psi
. We get

[X,f_{1}\wedge f_{2}]=(\deg\psi)^{-1}[Y,\psi^{*}(f_{1})\wedge\psi^{*}(f_{2})].

So we can assume that
f_{1}
is non-constant and the field extension
\mathbb{K}(f_{1})\subset\mathbb{K}(X)
is Galois. Denote the Galois group by
G
. We have

[X,f_{1}\wedge f_{2}]=1/|G|\sum\limits_{g\in G}[X,g^{*}(f_{1}\wedge f_{2})]=1/|G|\sum\limits_{g\in G}[X,f_{1}\wedge g^{*}(f_{2})]=1/|G|[X,f_{1}\wedge\widetilde{f}_{2}],

where
\widetilde{f}_{2}=\prod\limits_{g\in G}g^{*}(f_{2})
. As
\widetilde{f}_{2}
is invariant under the Galois group, it follows that there is
h\in\mathbb{K}(t)
so that
\widetilde{f}_{2}=h(f_{1})
. We get

1/|G|[X,f_{1}\wedge\widetilde{f}_{2}]=1/|G|[X,f_{1}\wedge h(f_{1})]=(\deg f_{1})/|G|[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t\wedge h(t)]=[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t\wedge h(t)].

In the last formula we used that
|G|=\deg f_{1}
.

As
h(t)
is product of linear factors, it is enough to consider the following two cases:

1.

X={\mathbb{P}}^{1},f_{1}=t,f_{2}=t-a,a\in\mathbb{K}
. Let
\varphi
be an automorphism of
\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by the formula
\varphi(t)=a-t
. We get

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t\wedge(a-t)]=[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},\varphi^{*}(t\wedge(t-a))]=

\displaystyle[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},(a-t)\wedge(-t)]=[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},(t-a)\wedge t]=-[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t\wedge(t-a)].

So
[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},t\wedge(a-t)]=0
.

2.

X={\mathbb{P}}^{1},f_{1}=t,f_{2}=c,c\in\mathbb{K}
. In this case the statement follows from Lemma
4.7
.

∎

5.1
An acyclic subcomplex

In this subsection we do not assume

Lemma 5.3
.

Assume that

[g_{1}g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}-[g_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}-[g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}

\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}

Consider the following cycle:

We claim that the boundary of this cycle is equal to the element stated in the lemma. Indeed, the cycle

. The intersection of this cycle with the face given by

[g_{1}g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}.

The intersection of
Z

[g_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}+[g_{2},c_{2},\dots,c_{m}]_{\spec\mathbb{K}}

the statement follows from the definition of the differential in the complex
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The complex
\mathcal{M}(\mathbb{K},m)
is acyclic.

Proof.

Let us first explain why higher Chow groups satisfy Galois descent. Let
\varphi\colon K\hookrightarrow F
be a (not necessarily finite) Galois extension with Galois group
G

g_{i}=\widetilde{g}_{i}/\widetilde{g}_{i}(x_{i})
.

Now we can set
g=1+(a_{2}-1)g_{2}+\dots(a_{k}-1)g_{k}
.
∎

5.4
The map
\mathcal{W}
is surjective

The goal of this subsection is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be algebraically closed and
j\in\{m-2,m-1,m\}
. The map
\mathcal{W}\colon\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}\to\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
is surjective.

We recall that the notion of an admissible cycle was introduced in Subsection
3.2
.

Definition 5.14
.

We will say that a sequence of functions
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is weakly admissible if
\supp((f_{1}),\dots,(f_{n}))
is a snc divisor.

We will say that
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is strictly admissible if it is weakly admissible and
(f_{i})
do not have common components.

We will say that a sequence of functions
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
is admissible if the cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}
is admissible.

Remark 5.15
.

A sequence which is not weakly admissible can be admissible. However, a strictly admissible sequence is admissible and weakly admissible.

We need the following lemma whose proof is deferred:

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective variety. Assume that the sequence
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is weakly admissible. Then
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
is a linear combination of the elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is strictly admissible.

The proof of Theorem
5.13
.

Let
a=[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]\in\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{j}
. We need to show that
a
lies in the image of
\mathcal{W}
. By Hironaka resolution of singularities, we can assume that
X
is smooth projective and the sequence
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
is weakly admissible. Using the previous lemma we can assume that the sequence
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is strictly admissible. Consider the map
\psi\colon X\to(\mathbb{P}^{1})^{n}
given by
z\mapsto(f_{1}(z),\dots,f_{n}(z))
. This map is undefined at points where the divisor of zeros of some
f_{i}
intersects the divisor of poles of
f_{i}
. Blowing up
X
in these codimension
2
subsets, we can assume that
\psi
is regular. Denote by
Z
its image.

Assume that
\dim Z<\dim X
. In this case it follows from Lemma
4.9
, that
[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]=0
. In particular,
[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]
lies in the image of
\mathcal{W}
.

So we can assume that
\dim Z=\dim X
. It follows that the natural map
X\to Z
is an alteration. As the sequence
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
is strictly admissible, the cycle
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}
is admissible. Now, it is easy to see that

[X,f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}]=\mathcal{W}([f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}).

∎

We proceed to the proof of Lemma
5.16
. We need the following definition.

Definition 5.17
.

Let
X
be a smooth variety and
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(X)
. Let
D
be a prime divisor. Define the set
J_{D}=J_{D}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
as follows. Let
J_{D}^{\prime}
be
\{i|\ord_{D}(f_{i})\neq 0\}
. If
|J_{D}^{\prime}|\geq 2
we set
J_{D}=J_{D}^{\prime}
. Otherwise, we set
J_{D}=\varnothing
.

Let us explain the idea of the proof of Lemma
5.16
. Let
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
be a weakly admissible sequence. Repeatedly applying identities of the form

f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{i}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge(f_{i}/h)\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n},

we will show the element
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
can be represented as a linear combination of elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is weakly admissible and (for fixed
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
) the sets
J_{D}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
are disjoint, where
D
varies among all prime divisors on
X
. So we can assume that
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is a weakly admissible sequence such that the sets
J_{D}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
are disjoint. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.18
.

Let
X
be a variety and
h,f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}
. Then:

\displaystyle f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=

\displaystyle 1/2(f_{1}/h\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}\wedge f_{2}/h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}/f_{2}\wedge h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}).

We will show that the repeated application of the above lemma allows to write element
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
as a linear combination of the elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is strictly admissible.

The proof of lemma
5.18
.

Indeed, we have

\displaystyle f_{1}/h\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}\wedge f_{2}/h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}/f_{2}\wedge h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=

\displaystyle f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}-h\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}-

\displaystyle-f_{1}\wedge h\wedge f_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}\wedge h\wedge f_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}-f_{2}\wedge h\wedge f_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=

\displaystyle 2\left(f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}\right)-h\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}-f_{2}\wedge h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=

\displaystyle 2\left(f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}\right).

∎

Proof of Lemma
5.16
.

Let
D
be an effective divisor and
D=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}n_{i}D_{i}
. Denote by
s(D)
the family of subsets
\{D_{i_{1}}\cap\dots\cap D_{i_{k}}|1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n\}
.

Let
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
be a weakly admissible sequence. Let us first show that
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
can be written as a linear combination of elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that 1)the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is weakly admissible and 2)the sets
J_{D}
are disjoint, where
D
varies among all prime divisors on
X
. Define the following invariant:

a_{1}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})=\sum\limits_{D_{1}\neq D_{2}}|J_{D_{1}}\cap J_{D_{2}}|.

The vanishing of
a_{1}
is equivalent to the fact that the sets
J_{D}
are disjoint. So, it is enough to prove that the element
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
can be represented as a linear combination of elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that 1)the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is weakly admissible and 2)
a_{1}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})<a_{1}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
.

Let us suppose that
J_{D_{1}}\cap J_{D_{2}}\neq\varnothing.
Without loss of generality we can assume that
1\in J_{D_{1}}\cap J_{D_{2}}
. Let
D=\supp((f_{1}),\dots,(f_{n}))
and
D^{\prime}=D-[D_{1}]
. By Lemma
5.11
there is
h
such that
\ord_{D_{1}}(h)=1
and
(h)
is general with respect to
s(D^{\prime})
. We have

f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=(f_{1}h^{-\ord_{D_{1}}(f_{1})})\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+(\ord_{D_{1}}(f_{1}))(h\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}).

We argue that
((f_{1}h^{-\ord_{D_{1}}(f_{1})}),f_{2},\dots,f_{n}),(h,f_{2},\dots,f_{n})
are weakly admissible and have strictly smaller value of
a_{1}
.

So we can assume that the sets
J_{D}
are disjoint. Define the following invariant:

a_{2}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})=\sum\limits_{D}\sum\limits_{i<j}|\ord_{D}(f_{i})\ord_{D}(f_{j})|.

The vanishing of
a_{2}
is equivalent to the fact that
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
is strictly admissible. So, it is enough to show that
f_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}
is a linear combination of elements of the form
g_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge g_{n}
such that 1)the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is weakly admissible, 2)the sets
J_{D}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
are disjoints and 3)
a_{2}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})<a_{2}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that for some
D_{1}
we have
\ord_{D_{1}}(f_{1}),\ord_{D_{1}}(f_{2})>0
. Let
D=\supp((f_{1}),\dots,(f_{n}))
and
D^{\prime}=D-[D_{1}]
. Choose
h
such that
\ord_{D_{1}}(h)=1
and
h
is general with respect to
s(D^{\prime})
. Such
h
exists by Lemma
5.11
. By Lemma
5.18
, we get

f_{1}\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}=1/2(f_{1}/h\wedge f_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}\wedge f_{2}/h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}+f_{1}/f_{2}\wedge h\wedge\dots\wedge f_{n}).

We argue that the sequences
(f_{1}/h,\dots,f_{n}),(f_{1},f_{2}/h\dots,f_{n}),(f_{1}/f_{2},h,\dots,f_{n})
satisfy the conditions stated above.
∎

5.5
Higher Chow groups and strictly admissible sequences

Denote by
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}^{\prime}
a subgroup of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
generated by the elements of the form
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}

is a smooth projective curve, and
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})

denotes the natural map

.

Theorem 5.19
.

The group
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
is generated by the subgroups
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}^{\prime}

Remark 5.20
.

It follows from the main result of this section that

. However we don’t know this fact yet.

Remark 5.21
.

It follows from this theorem that the group

is generated by the elements of the form

The proof of this theorem is similar to the proof of Lemma
5.16
. From here to the end of this subsection we work modulo elements from

Lemma 5.22
.

The group
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}

and by elements of the form

are disjoint, as

varies among the closed points of

Proof.

As any curve has a resolution of singularity, any irreducible cycle in

has the form

for some smooth projective curve

. By definition, the sequence

is admissible. Let

a_{1}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})=\sum\limits_{x\neq y}|J_{x}\cap J_{y}|.

We will show that the element
[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}
is a linear combination of elements of the form
[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]_{X}
such that the sequence
(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is admissible and

a_{1}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})<a_{1}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n}).

Suppose that
J_{x}\cap J_{y}\neq\varnothing
. We can assume that
1\in J_{x}\cap J_{y}
.
Let
D=\supp((f_{1}),\dots,(f_{n}))
and
D^{\prime}=D-[x]
. Choose
h
such that
h(y)=1
for any

and
\ord_{x}(h)=1
. Such function exists by Lemma
5.12
. As before, it is easy to see that the sequences
(f_{1}h^{-\ord_{x}(f_{1})},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}),(h^{\ord_{x}(f_{1})},f_{2},\dots,f_{n})
are admissible and their values of

are strictly smaller. It remains to show that the element

is contained in

.
Indeed, it is obvious that this element is contained in

. The fact that this element is contained in

5.6

∎

The proof of Theorem
5.19
.

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
be an admissible sequence such that the sets

are disjoint. Define the following invariant:

a_{2}(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})=\sum\limits_{x}\sum\limits_{i<j}|\ord_{x}(f_{i})||\ord_{x}(f_{j})|.

In this formula
x
varies among all the closed points of
X
.

[g_{1},\dots,g_{n}]_{X}

(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
is admissible 2)the sets

are disjoint and 3)

Suppose that

. Let

and

. Choose

and

for any

. It is easy to see that the following sequences are admissible:

\displaystyle(h/f_{2},f_{1},\dots,f_{n}),(h,f_{2}/f_{1},\dots,f_{n}).

\displaystyle[f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}=[h,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}+[f_{1}/h,f_{2},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}=

\displaystyle[h,f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}+[h,f_{2}/f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}+[f_{1}/h,f_{2},,\dots,f_{n}]_{X}=

∎

5.6
Generators and relations for
{\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}}

X

f_{i}

y_{\beta}=\sum_{\alpha\in A_{\beta}}n_{\alpha}l_{\alpha,\beta}\langle f_{2}^{\alpha},\dots,f_{n}^{\alpha}\rangle_{\otimes}.

We get:

\theta_{\otimes,Z,n}(x)=\sum_{\beta\in B}h_{\beta}\otimes(\theta_{\otimes,Z_{\beta},n-1}(y_{\beta})).

By previous lemma, we get
\theta_{\otimes,Z_{\beta},n-1}(y_{\beta})=0
. It follows from the inductive assumption that

.

Assume that
\beta\in B
. For any
z\in Z
we have
\ord_{z}(h)=0
. Define the map

given by the formula

\langle g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\rangle_{\otimes}\mapsto\langle h_{\beta},g_{2},\dots,g_{n}\rangle_{\otimes}.

We get

x=\sum_{\beta\in B}L_{\beta}(y_{\beta})=\sum_{\beta}L_{\beta}(0)=0.

∎

5.7
The map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is an isomorphism

We recall that
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed. It follows from the proof of Lemma
5.4
that
\widetilde{N}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}
is isomorphic to
\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}
. The group
\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}
is isomorphic to

by definition. Moreover, it is easy that the natural map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\colon\widetilde{N}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}\to\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m}
is an isomorphism. So
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
is an isomorphism in degree

.
Let us show that it is also an isomorphism in degree
m-1
.
Let
X
be a smooth projective curve. Define a map

\varphi_{X}\colon V_{n}(X)\to\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}

by the formula

\varphi_{X}(\langle f_{1},\dots,f_{n}\rangle)=[f_{1},\dots,f_{n}]_{X}
. The cycle is admissible; relations are satisfied by Proposition
5.6
.
Define a map
\lambda\colon\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}

by the formula
\lambda([X,a])=\varphi_{\widetilde{X}}(\theta_{\Lambda,n}^{-1}(\psi^{*}(a)))
, where
\psi\colon\widetilde{X}\to X
is the normalization. It is easy to check that the relations defined
\Lambda(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
are satisfied.

Denote by
\pi
the natural projection
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}\to\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
. Let us show that for any
a\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
we have
\lambda\mathcal{W}(a)=\pi(a)
. By Theorem
5.19
the group
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
is generated by the subgroups
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}^{\prime}
and
\ker\pi\cap\ker\mathcal{W}
. When

the formula follows from the definition of the map
\theta_{\Lambda,n}
. When

the formula is obvious as both sides are zero.

Let us show that
\lambda
vanishes on the image of

. We need to show that for any

we have
\lambda(d(a))=0
. By Theorem

there is

. We get

So we get a map

The goal of this section is to give some generalization of the main result of

]
. As this generalization is rather straightforward, our arguments are only sketched and we refer the reader to

]
.
We assume
\mathbb{K}
to be algebraically closed. Let

Let

be a discrete valuation field. We recall that

denotes the residue field. The following proposition was proved in

Let

\ts_{\nu}(\pi\wedge u_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge u_{m})=\overline{u_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge\overline{u_{m}}

2.

with

and any

we have

.

3.

For any unit

, any integer

and

\ts_{\nu}(\{u\}_{k}\otimes b)=-\{\overline{u}\}_{k}\otimes\ts_{\nu}(b)

The sign in the item

Denote by

. Let

For

6.1
The outline of the proof

In Section
6.2
we will show that there is the unique lifted reciprocity map on the field of rational functions
\mathbb{K}(t)
. Denote it by
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{K}(t)}
. Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}ields}_{1}
be any field. Choose some embedding
j\colon\mathbb{K}(t)\hookrightarrow F
. To define
\mathcal{H}_{F}
, we extend a lifted reciprocity map
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{K}(t)}
from the field
\mathbb{K}(t)
to the field
F
. For this we solve the more general problem: for any finite extension
j^{\prime}\colon F_{1}\hookrightarrow F_{2}
in
{\mathcal{F}ields}_{1}
we construct the canonical map
N_{F_{2}/F_{1}}\colon\mathrm{RecMaps}(F_{1})\to\mathrm{RecMaps}(F_{2})
. More precisely, we will prove the following theorem:

Theorem 6.5
.

For any embedding of fields
j\colon F_{1}\hookrightarrow F_{2}
one can define the canonical map

N_{F_{2}/F_{1}}\colon\mathrm{RecMaps}(F_{1})\to\mathrm{RecMaps}(F_{2})

satisfying the following properties:

1.

\mathrm{RecMaps}(j)\circ N_{F_{2}/F_{1}}=id
.

2.

If
F_{1}\subset F_{2}\subset F_{3}
is a tower of extension from
{\mathcal{F}ields}_{1}
then
N_{F_{3}/F_{1}}=N_{F_{3}/F_{2}}\circ N_{F_{2}/F_{1}}
.

Item
(i)
shows that
N_{F_{2}/F_{1}}
is indeed an extension, while item
(ii)
shows that this extension is functorial.

Let us explain the proof of Theorem
6.3
. The existence follows from Theorem
6.5
together with the fact that the element
N_{F/\mathbb{K}(t)}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{K}(t)})
does not depend on the embedding
j\colon\mathbb{K}(t)\hookrightarrow F
. The uniqueness essentially follows from Galois descent for Milnor
K
-theory.

Let us outline the proof of Theorem
6.5
. The proof of this theorem is in many respects similar to the construction of the norm map on Milnor
K
-theory. (See
[
BT73
,
Sus79
,
Mil70
,
Kat80
]
). That is the reason why we denote it by the letter
N
. (Note that compared to the norm map in Milnor
K
-theory, in our case, the norm map is directed in the opposite direction. The reason for this is that while Milnor
K
-theory gives a covariant functor, the functor
\mathrm{RecMaps}
is contravariant.)

We define the map
N_{F_{2}/F_{1}}
in two steps. First, for any discrete valuation
\nu
of the field
F(t)
we define the canonical map

\mathcal{N}_{\nu}\colon\mathrm{RecMaps}(F)\to\mathrm{RecMaps}(\overline{F(t)}_{\nu}).

Using this map, for any extension
F_{1}\hookrightarrow F_{2}
with a generator
a
we will define the norm map
N_{F_{2}/F_{1},a}\colon\mathrm{RecMaps}(F_{1})\to\mathrm{RecMaps}(F_{2})
(see Definition
6.18
). Using ideas from
[
Sus79
]
we will show that this map does not depend on

and will have finished the proof of Theorem
6.5
.

Let us give the outline of the construction of the map
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}
. Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}ields}_{1}
. It is useful to divide the discrete valuations of the field
F(t)
into two classes, namely the general valuations and the special ones (see Definition
6.10
). For the special valuations the definition of the map
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}
is straightforward. Let us explain how to define
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}(h)
for general valuations. We want to define
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}
so that an analog of Theorem
6.4
would hold. This means that for any
h\in\mathrm{RecMaps}(F)
and any
b\in\Lambda^{m+2}F(t)^{\times}
the following formula should hold:

\sum\limits_{\nu\in\dval(F(t))}\mathcal{N}_{\nu}(h)(b)=0.

It turns out that this formula, together with the definition of
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}
for special valuations, determines
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}
completely.

To give a formal definition, we need a notion of
lift
. Let
\nu\in\dval(F(t))
be a general valuation and
n,j\in\mathbb{N}
. A lift of the element
a\in\Gamma(\overline{F(t)}_{\nu},n)_{j}
is an element
b\in\Gamma(F(t),n+1)_{j+1}
, such that the tame-symbol
\ts_{\nu}(b)
is equal to
a
and the tame-symbol of

at any other general valuation vanishes. The set of all lifts of the element

is denoted by
\mathcal{L}(a)
. It is easy to show that for any

the set
\mathcal{L}(a)
is non-empty. Let
\nu\in\dval(F(t))
be a general valuation,
h\in\mathrm{RecMaps}(F)
and
a\in\Lambda^{m+1}\overline{F(t)}_{\nu}
. Choose some lift

and define the element
\mathcal{N}_{\nu}(h)(a)
by the following formula:

\mathcal{N}_{\nu}(h)(a)=-\sum\limits_{\mu\in\dval(F(t))_{sp}}\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(h)(\ts_{\mu}(b)).

Here
\dval(F(t))_{sp}
denotes the set of all special valuations. In this formula the lifted reciprocity maps
\mathcal{N}_{\mu}(h)
are already defined because
\mu
is special. It remains to show that this expression does not depend on the choice of
b
and for fixed

gives a lifted reciprocity map on the field
\overline{F(t)}_{\nu}
. This can be done using certain properties of the lift and some version of the Parshin reciprocity law.

6.2
Case of of the field
\mathbb{K}(t)

Let us recall the following classical statement:

Theorem 6.6

(Weil reciprocity law)
.

Let
X
be a smooth projective curve over
\mathbb{K}
and
f,g\in\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}
. We have

\prod_{x\in X(\mathbb{K})}\ts_{x}(f\wedge g)=1.

We need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
a\in\Gamma(\mathbb{K}(t),m+1)_{m}
. If
\delta_{m+1}(a)
lies in the image of the multiplication map
\Lambda^{2}\mathbb{K}(t)^{\times}\otimes\Lambda^{m-1}\mathbb{K}^{\times}\to\Lambda^{m+1}\mathbb{K}(t)^{\times}

\sum\limits_{\nu\in\val(\mathbb{K}(t))}\ts_{\nu}(a)=0\in\im(\delta_{m}).

Proof.

1.

Let
b=\delta_{m+1}(a)
. The elements of the form

\{(t-c_{1})/(t-c_{2})\}_{2}\otimes c_{3}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{m+1}

lie in the kernel of the map
Tot_{\mathbb{K}(t)}
. Subtracting from
a
a linear combination of elements of these form, we can assume that
b
lies in the image of the multiplication map
\mathbb{K}(t)^{\times}\otimes\Lambda^{m}\mathbb{K}^{\times}\to\Lambda^{m+1}\mathbb{K}(t)^{\times}
.

2.

Let
a^{\prime\prime}=(-1)^{m}\ts_{\infty}(a\wedge(1/t))
. For
x\in\mathbb{K}
denote
a^{\prime}_{x}=(-1)^{m+1}\ts_{x}(a)\wedge(t-x)
. We have
a^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}_{x}\in\Gamma(\mathbb{K}(t),m+1)_{m}
.

Since the total residue of the elements
a^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}_{x}
is equal to zero, it is enough to prove the statement for the element

\widetilde{a}=a-a^{\prime\prime}-\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{K}}a^{\prime}_{x}.

3.

Let
\widetilde{b}=\delta_{m+1}(\widetilde{a})
. We claim that for any

we have
\ts_{x}(\widetilde{b})=\ts_{\infty}(\widetilde{b}\wedge(1/t))=0
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\ts_{x}(\widetilde{b})=\ts_{x}(\delta_{m+1}(\widetilde{a}))=-\delta_{m}(\ts_{x}(\widetilde{a}))=-\delta_{m}(\ts_{x}(a-a^{\prime\prime}-\sum_{x}a_{x}^{\prime}))=

\displaystyle-\delta_{m}(\ts_{x}(a)-\ts_{x}(a_{x}^{\prime}))=-\delta_{m}(\ts_{x}(a)-\ts_{x}(a))=0.

The proof of the formula

is similar.

On the other hand, we can write the element

in the following form

\widetilde{b}=\widetilde{b}^{\prime}+\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{K}}\widetilde{b}_{x}\wedge(t-x),

with

.
As

, it follows that

.

4.

So we can assume that

. In this case the statement follows from
[

, Corollary 1.4]
. We remark that Corollary 1.4 was stated in loc.cit. only for

6.3
The construction of the lift

In the definition of norm map on Milnor

-theory the point
\infty\in{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
plays a special role. Let us call a discrete valuation on the field
\mathbb{K}(X)

special
if it corresponds to the point
\infty
and
general
otherwise. The following definition gives a two-dimensional analog of this definition.

Definition 6.10
.

Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}ields}_{1}
. A valuation
\nu\in\dval(F(t))
is called

if it corresponds to some irreducible polynomial over
F
. The set of general valuations are in bijection with the set of all closed points on the affine line over
F
, which we denote by
\mathbb{A}_{F,(0)}^{1}
. A valuation is called
special
if it is not general. Denote the set of general (resp. special) valuations by
\dval(F(t))_{gen}
(resp.
\dval(F(t))_{sp}
).

Remark 6.11
.

Let
F\in{\mathcal{F}ields}_{1}
. Let us realize
F
as a field of fractions on some smooth projective curve
X
over
k
. Set
S=X\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
. It can be checked that a valuation
\nu\in\dval(F(t))
is special in the following two cases:

1.

There is a birational morphism
\varphi\colon\widetilde{S}\to S
, and the valuation
\nu
corresponds to some prime divisor
D\subset\widetilde{S}
contracted under
\varphi
.

2.

The valuation
\nu
corresponds to some of the divisors
X\times\{\infty\},\{a\}\times\mathbb{P}^{1},a\in X
.

Otherwise, the valuation
\nu
is general.
It follows from this description that if
\nu
is a special valuation different from

, then the residue field
\overline{F(t)}_{\nu}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{K}(t)
.

Definition 6.12

Let

,

, and

.
A lift
of an element

is an element
b\in\Gamma(F(t),m+1)_{j+1}
satisfying the following two properties:

1.

\ts_{\nu}(b)=a
and

y=(t-a_{1})/(t-a_{2})\wedge(1-(t-a_{1})/(t-a_{2}))\wedge(t-a_{3})\wedge\dots\wedge(t-a_{k})\wedge c_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{n}.

By inductive assumption this implies that the element

[{\mathbb{P}}^{1},(t-a_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge(t-a_{k})\wedge c_{k+1}\wedge\dots\wedge c_{n}]

lies in
A
.

∎

It remains to show that the map
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is injective. We recall that in the previous section for any smooth complete curve over
\mathbb{K}
we constructed the canonical map
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{K}(X)}\colon{\Lambda^{m+1}\mathbb{K}(X)^{\times}}\to\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
. Define a morphism of complexes

\mathcal{CD}\colon\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)

as follows. The image of the element
[X,a]\in\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)_{m-1}
is equal to
-\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{K}(X)}(a)
. The image of the element
[\spec\mathbb{K},a]
is equal to
a
. It follows from the results of the previous section that
\mathcal{CD}
is a morphism of complexes and
\mathcal{CD}\circ\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=id
. This implies that the map
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is injective. So the map
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
is an isomorphism.

7.3
The proof of Theorem
1.3

In the beginning of this section we have shown that the map
\mathcal{T}\colon\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
is well-defined. By the result of
[
Rud21
]
and Lemma
5.5
, the groups

H^{i}(\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)),H^{i}(\Gamma_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m))

satisfy Galois descent. So we can assume that
\mathbb{K}
is algebraically closed. In this case, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{T}
is an isomorphism. In Section
5
we have constructed the map
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\colon\widetilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)\to\Lambda_{\geq m-1}(\mathbb{K},m)
and have shown that this map is an isomorphism. So it remains to prove that the map
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{W}\circ\mathcal{T}
is an isomorphism. This was proven in the previous subsection.
