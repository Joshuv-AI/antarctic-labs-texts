Approximation and Interpolation in Kolmogorov-type groups
Antonello Pesce

Abstract
We prove a real interpolation characterization for some non Euclidean Hölder spaces, built on the Lie structure induced by a class of ultra-parabolic Kolmogorov-type operators satisfying the Hörmander condition. As a by-product we also obtain an approximation property for intrinsically regular functions on the whole space.



Keywords
: Interpolation, Approximation,
Kolmogorov operators, hypoelliptic operators, Hörmander condition

MSC
: 35D99, 35K65, 35H10, 46M35.

1
Introduction

1.1
Statement of the problem

We consider the problems of approximation and interpolation for some non Euclidean Hölder spaces built on the intrinsic geometry of the following Kolmogorov operator

\mathcal{K}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}\partial_{x_{i}x_{i}}+Y,\quad Y=\langle Bx,\nabla\rangle+\partial_{t},\qquad(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1.1)

where
1\leqslant p_{0}\leqslant d
and
B
is a
d\times d
constant matrix. If
p_{0}=d
then
\mathcal{K}
is a parabolic operator while in general, for
p_{0}<d
,
\mathcal{K}
is degenerate, and not uniformly parabolic. However,
\mathcal{K}
can be a hypoelliptic operator, provided some structural assumptions on the matrix
B
are verified. This kind of PDEs appears in many linear and non-linear models in physics (see, for instance,
[
4
]
,
[
8
]
) and in mathematical finance (see, for instance,
[
2
]
,
[
7
]
).

In
[
15
]
, Lanconelli and Polidoro first studied the non-Euclidean intrinsic Lie group structure induced by the vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{p_{0}}},Y
, and from there on, many authors carried the study of general Kolmogorov operators in the framework of the theory of homogeneous groups. We recall, among many,
[
9
]
, who proved the existence of a fundamental solution under optimal regularity assumptions,
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
21
]
, who proved Schauder type estimates for the associated Cauchy and Dirichlet problems,
[
13
]
, who considered the case of integro-differential diffusions.
One basic tool in the study of regularity properties of solutions to Kolmogorov operators with variable coefficients is the notion of Hölder spaces which are
intrinsic
to the aforementioned geometric structure. Over the years, different notions of Hölder classes have been proposed by several authors, based on the Carnot-Carathéodory distance associated to the group (see, among many,
[
10
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
11
]
). Eventually Pagliarani et al. in
[
23
]
established an optimal definition of
C_{B}^{n,\alpha}
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, which is given in terms of the regularity in the directions of the vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{p_{0}}}
and
Y
with their different formal degrees: for instance, the case
n=0
consists of functions that are
C^{\alpha}
continuous in the directions
\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{p_{0}}}
and
C^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}
in
Y
. With this definition they managed to prove a Taylor-type formula with related estimates of the remainder, given in terms of the intrinsic distance.

Our main result, Theorem
1.6
, shows that these intrinsic spaces interpolate well with each other through the so called
real method
, namely that

\left(C_{B}^{n_{1},\alpha_{1}},C_{B}^{n_{2},\alpha_{2}}\right)_{\theta,\infty}=C_{B}^{n,\alpha},

(1.2)

for any
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]
, for some expected parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
In the Euclidean case, this identification is well known (see
[
20
]
,
[
27
]
), and can be proved by means of the general theory of semigroups.
Similar arguments can be adapted for a different class of Hölder spaces also associated to degenerate elliptic and parabolic differential equations, the so called
anisotropic
Hölder spaces, which, unlike the intrinsic spaces, only refer to the spatial variables (see Section
1.3
for a more in-depth discussion).
Here we choose to pursue a constructive approach based on explicit approximations of intrinsically regular functions (Theorem
3.1
), which can possibly be of independent interest.
This approach is more or less inspired by the approximation theory for Sobolev functions (see
[
1
]
, Chapter 5),
it does not rely on the theory of semigroups, but instead, it heavily exploits the aforementioned intrinsic Taylor formula.

Classically, characterizations of type (
1.2
) have remarkable applications in the study of optimal regularity in Hölder classes for linear elliptic and parabolic differential operators (see
[
14
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
) as well as approximation theory (see
[
3
]
).
In the context of Kolmogorov operators, many works already exploit some kind of interpolation inequalities to obtain different versions of Schauder estimates in different settings (see, for instance
[
12
]
,
[
10
]
and
[
22
]
among others, see also
[
17
]
for the most recent global estimate for the evolution problem, as well as a complete overview of the existing literature).
Let us also mention that our results, together with the embeddings of intrinsic Sobolev spaces of integer order in
[
25
]
could be applied to deduce Morrey-type embeddings for suitable notions of Sobolev-Slobodeckij and Besov intrinsic spaces as well, which, as far as our understanding, are yet to be explored in the literature in generality. Such studies will be subjects of future research. For the application of interpolation results to fractional embeddings in the Euclidean setting, see for instance, the references
[
1
]
or
[
26
]
.

The paper is organized as follows: in the remaining part of this Section, we specify the precise functional framework, state the structural hypothesis on the matrix
B
and our main interpolation results, Proposition
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
, and finally discuss the previous literature on the subject and justify our approach.
In Section
2
we collect some results that are preliminary to the subsequent proofs; in Section
3
we prove the core result of this work, the approximation property for intrinsically regular functions, which eventually leads to the proof of our main results in Section
4
.

1.2
Hölder spaces and real interpolation

We start by recalling the Lie group structure that is naturally associated to the differential operator (
1.1
). In
[
15
]
the authors observed that
\mathcal{K}
is invariant with respect to the left translations of the group
(\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\circ)
, where the non-commutative group law is defined by

(t,x)\circ(s,{\xi})=\ell_{(t,x)}(s,{\xi}):=(s+t,{\xi}+e^{sB}x),\qquad(t,x),\ (s,{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

(1.3)

Precisely,
\ell_{(t,x)}\circ\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{K}\circ\ell_{(t,x)}
for any
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.
Notice that
(\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\circ)
is a group with identity
\text{Id}=(0,0)
and inverse
(t,x)^{-1}=(-t,e^{-tB}x)
.

The authors also proved that the operator
\mathcal{K}
is hypoelliptic if and only if, on a certain basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
the matrix
B
takes the form

B=\begin{pmatrix}\ast&\ast&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
{\bf B}_{1}&\ast&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
0_{p_{2}\times p_{0}}&{\bf B}_{2}&\cdots&\ast&\ast\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0_{p_{r}\times p_{0}}&0_{p_{r}\times p_{1}}&\cdots&{\bf B}_{r}&\ast\end{pmatrix},

(1.4)

where
\mathbf{B}_{j}
is a
p_{j}\times p_{j-1}
matrix of rank
p_{j}
, with

p_{0}\geqslant p_{1}\geqslant\dots\geqslant p_{r}\geqslant 1,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{r}p_{j}=d,

0_{p_{i}\times p_{j}}
is a
p_{i}\times p_{j}
null matrix and the
\ast
-blocks are arbitrary. Throughout the paper we assume the following

Assumption 1.1
.

B
is a constant
d\times d
matrix as in (
1.4
), where each
\ast
-block is null.

If (and only if) Assumption
1.1
holds, then
\mathcal{K}
is homogeneus of degree two with respect to the family of dilations
(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
given by

D_{\lambda}=(\lambda^{2},\lambda I_{p_{0}},\lambda^{3}I_{p_{1}}\dots,\lambda^{2r+1}I_{p_{r}}),

where
I_{p_{j}}
are
p_{j}\times p_{j}
identity matrices, i.e.
(\mathcal{K}D_{\lambda}u)(t,x)=\lambda^{2}(\mathcal{K}u)(D_{\lambda}(t,x))
, for any
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,
\lambda>0
.
In this case, the matrix
B
uniquely identifies the
homogeneous Lie group

{\mathcal{G}}_{B}:=(\mathbb{R}^{d+1},\circ,D(\lambda))
.
We define the
D(\lambda)
-homogeneous quasi-norm on
{\mathcal{G}}_{B}
as follows: let

\bar{p}_{j}=p_{0}+\dots+p_{j},\qquad j=0,\dots,r,

(1.5)

and
\bar{p}_{-1}\equiv 0
. Then

\|(t,x)\|_{B}:=|t|^{\frac{1}{2}}+|x|_{B},\qquad|x|_{B}:=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\sum_{i=\bar{p}_{j-1}+1}^{\bar{p}_{j}}|x_{i}|^{\frac{1}{2j+1}}.

(1.6)

Next we recall the notions of
B
-intrinsic regularity and
B
-intrinsic Hölder space introduced in
[
23
]
.
We start with some useful notations:
for any
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
we denote

e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}(t,x)=(t,x+\delta e_{i}),\quad{e^{\delta Y}(t,x)=(t+\delta,e^{\delta B}x)},\qquad\delta\in\mathbb{R},

(1.7)

the (unique) integral curves of the vector fields
\partial_{x_{i}}
and
Y
respectively.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
X\in\{\partial_{x_{1}},\dots,\partial_{x_{p_{0}}},Y\}
and
\alpha\in]0,1]
. We say that
u\in C^{\alpha}_{X}
if

[u]_{C^{\alpha}_{X}}:=\sup_{z\in{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\atop\delta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left|u(e^{\delta X}z)-u(z)\right|}{|\delta|^{{\alpha}}}<\infty.

(1.8)

We say that
u
is
X
-differentiable in
z
if the function
\delta\mapsto u(e^{\delta X}z)
is differentiable in
0
and refer to the function

z\mapsto Xu(z):=\frac{d}{d\delta}u(e^{\delta X}z)\mid_{\delta=0}
as the
X
-Lie derivative of u.

Definition 1.3
.

Let
\alpha\in{[0,1]}
, then:

i)

If
\alpha=0
,
C^{0,0}_{B}\equiv C(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
is the space of bounded and continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, endowed with the sup-norm

\|u\|_{C^{0,0}_{B}}:=|u|_{\infty}.

If
\alpha\in]0,1]
we say that
u\in C^{0,\alpha}_{B}
if
u\in C^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{Y}
and
u\in C^{\alpha}_{\partial_{x_{i}}}
for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
, with norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}}

\displaystyle:=|u|_{\infty}+[u]_{C^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}_{Y}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}[u]_{C^{\alpha}_{\partial_{x_{i}}}}<\infty,\quad\alpha\in]0,1].

ii)

u\in C^{1,\alpha}_{B}
if
u\in C^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}_{Y}
and
\partial_{x_{i}}u\in C^{\alpha}_{B}
for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
, with norm

\|u\|_{C^{1,\alpha}_{B}}:=|u|_{\infty}+[u]_{C^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}_{Y}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}}<\infty.

iii)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geqslant 2
,
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
if
Yu\in C^{n-2,\alpha}_{B}
and
\partial_{x_{i}}u\in C^{n-1,\alpha}_{B}
for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
, with norm

\|u\|_{C^{n}_{B}}:=|u|_{\infty}+\|Yu\|_{C^{n-2,\alpha}_{B}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}_{B}}<\infty.

Remark 1.4
.

C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
is a Banach space. Notice also that
C^{n,\alpha}_{B}\subseteq C^{n^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}_{B}
for
0\leqslant n\leqslant n^{\prime}
and
0\leqslant\alpha^{\prime}\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1
.

For any two real Banach spaces
Z_{1},Z_{2}
, by
Z_{1}=Z_{2}
we mean that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
have the same elements with equivalent norms and by
Z_{1}\subseteq Z_{2}
we mean that
Z_{1}
is continuously embedded in
Z_{2}
. We recall that the couple
\{Z_{1},Z_{2}\}
is called
interpolation couple
if both
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
are continuously embedded in some Hausdorff topological vector space
\mathcal{Z}
; in this case, the intersection
Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}
and the sum
Z_{1}+Z_{2}=\{u_{1}+u_{2},\ u_{1}\in Z_{1},\ u_{2}\in Z_{2}\}
, endowed with the norms

\|u\|_{Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}}:=\max\{\|u\|_{Z_{1}},\|u\|_{Z_{1}}\},\quad\|u\|_{Z_{1}+Z_{2}}:=\inf\{\|u_{1}\|_{Z_{1}}+\|u_{2}\|_{Z_{1}},u_{1}\in Z_{1},\ u_{2}\in Z_{2}\},

are Banach spaces. Moreover, any Banach space
E
such that

Z_{1}\cap Z_{2}\subseteq E\subseteq Z_{1}+Z_{2},

(1.9)

is called an
intermediate space
. Among these, we have the

real interpolation space

(Z_{1},Z_{2})_{\theta,\infty}
, which can be defined, for any
\theta\in(0,1)
(see, for instance
[
20
]
), as

(Z_{1},Z_{2})_{\theta,\infty}:=\{u\in Z_{1}+Z_{2},\ \|u\|_{(Z_{1},Z_{2})_{\theta,\infty}}:=\sup_{\lambda\geqslant 0}\lambda^{-\theta}K(\lambda,u;Z_{1},Z_{2})<\infty\}

(1.10)

where

K(\lambda,u;Z_{1},Z_{2}):=\inf\left\{\|u_{1}\|_{Z_{1}}+\lambda\|u_{2}\|_{Z_{2}},\,u=u_{1}+u_{2},\,u_{1}\in Z_{1},\,u_{2}\in Z_{2}\right\}.

Our first results states that any space
C^{n,0}_{B}
belongs to a special class of
intermediate spaces
between the larger space
C^{n_{1},0}_{B}
,
n_{1}<n
and a smaller space
C^{n_{2},0}_{B}
,
n<n_{2}
, with
n,n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
n,n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n_{1}<n<n_{2}
.

i)

There exists a constant
c_{1}
such that, for any
u\in C^{n_{2},0}_{B}
, we have

\|u\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}}\leqslant c_{1}\|u\|^{\frac{n_{2}-n}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}_{C^{n_{1},0}_{B}}\|u\|^{\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}_{C^{n_{2},0}_{B}};

(1.11)

i)

There exists a constant
c_{2}
such that, for any
u\in C^{n,0}_{B}
, we have

K(\lambda,u;C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2},0}_{B})\leqslant c_{2}\lambda^{\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}\|u\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}},\quad\lambda>0.

(1.12)

Equivalently (see, for instance
[
20
]
, Proposition 1.20) it holds that

\left(C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2},0}_{B}\right)_{\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}},1}\subseteq C^{n,0}_{B}\subseteq\left(C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2},0}_{B}\right)_{\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}},\infty}.

(1.13)

Next, our main result, gives a characterization of
B
-Hölder spaces of any order as interpolation spaces.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]
, with
n_{1}+\alpha_{1}\leqslant n_{2}+\alpha_{2}
, and let
\theta\in(0,1)
. Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n_{1}\leqslant n\leqslant n_{2}
such that

\frac{n-(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})}{(n_{2}+\alpha_{2})-(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})}<\theta<\frac{n+1-(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})}{(n_{2}+\alpha_{2})-(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})}.

(1.14)

If
\alpha:=(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})+\theta[n_{2}+\alpha_{2}-(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})]-n\neq 0,1

then it holds that

\left(C^{n_{1},\alpha_{1}}_{B},C^{n_{2},\alpha_{2}}_{B}\right)_{\theta,\infty}=C^{n,\alpha}_{B}

(1.15)

Remark 1.7
.

If
\Omega
is an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, one can define the Hölder spaces
C^{n,\alpha}_{B}(\Omega)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
as in the case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, where in particular, for
X\in\{\partial_{x_{1}},\dots\partial_{x_{p_{0}}},Y\}
,

[u]_{C^{\alpha}_{X}(\Omega)}:=\sup\left\{\frac{|u(e^{\delta X}z)-u(z)|}{|\delta|^{\alpha}},\ z\in\Omega,\ \delta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\text{ s.t. }e^{\delta^{\prime}X}z\in\Omega,\ |\delta^{\prime}|<|\delta|\right\}.

Then it is likely that our results also hold for the spaces
C^{n,\alpha}_{B}(\Omega)
, provided that the domain
\Omega
is smooth enough (see
[
27
]
, Theorem 1.2.4 and Section 4.5). The construction of the appropriate extension operators under optimal regularity assumptions for
\Omega
is outside the scope of the present paper and is postponed to future research.

Remark 1.8
.

Notice that the assumption
n_{1}+\alpha_{1}\leqslant n_{2}+\alpha_{2}
is not restrictive. Indeed we can always write

\left(C^{n_{1},\alpha_{1}}_{B},C^{n_{2},\alpha_{2}}_{B}\right)_{\theta,\infty}=\left(C^{n_{2},\alpha_{2}}_{B},C^{n_{1},\alpha_{1}}_{B}\right)_{1-\theta,\infty}.

1.3
Related works and strategy of the proof

As already mentioned, in the Euclidean case, the interpolation identity (
1.2
) can be proved by means of the theory of semigroups (
[
20
]
,
[
27
]
):
let
C,C^{1}
denote the spaces of bounded continuous functions and bounded continuously differentiable functions with bounded derivatives respectively, and let
C^{\theta}
denote the space of bounded and uniformly
\theta
-Hölder continuous
functions, all endowed with the usual norms. Roughly speaking, denoting with
T_{i}
the translation semigroup of the
i
-th variable, and
{\mathcal{D}}(\partial_{x_{i}})=\{f\in C,\ \exists\partial_{x_{i}}f\in C\}
, we have

(C,C^{1})_{\theta,p}=\Big(C,\bigcap_{i=1}^{d}{\mathcal{D}}(\partial_{x_{i}})\Big)_{\theta,p}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{d}\Big(C,{\mathcal{D}}(\partial_{x_{i}})\Big)_{\theta,p},

(1.16)

for any
p\geqslant 1
, where

\big(C,{\mathcal{D}}(\partial_{x_{i}})\big)_{\theta,\infty}=\{f\in C,\ t\mapsto\phi(t):=t^{-\theta}\|T_{i}(t)f-f\|_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)\},

(1.17)

that is the space of
\theta
-Hölder continuous functions in the
i
-th variable. Therefore
(C,C^{1})_{\theta,\infty}=C^{\theta}
.
In particular (
1.17
) holds more generally for
p\geqslant 1
, and substituting
T_{i}
and
\partial_{x_{i}}
with any bounded semigroup
T
and its infinitesimal generator on a Banach space, while the second equality in (
1.16
) precisely holds because the semigroups
T_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,d
commute.

In
[
19
]
, Lunardi adapted these arguments to derive an interpolation result for anisotropic Hölder spaces associated with operators of the type (
1.1
), which are spaces of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(only the spatial variables are considered) defined with respect to the metric induced by
|\cdot|_{B}
(cfr. (
1.6
)). For instance, let us consider the degenerate prototype of (
1.1
), that is the Langevin operator

\mathcal{K}=\frac{1}{2}\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}+Y,\quad Y=x_{1}\partial_{x_{2}}+\partial_{t},

which corresponds to the matrix

B=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}.

In this case the anisotropic Hölder regularity in the space variables reads:

|u(x)-u({\xi})|\leqslant C|x-{\xi}|^{\alpha}_{B}\sim|x_{1}-{\xi}_{1}|^{\alpha}+|x_{2}-{\xi}_{2}|^{\frac{\alpha}{3}},\quad x,{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(1.18)

A regularity theory for equations with variable coefficients, built on this functional setting, is useful when one needs to consider coefficients or data that are irregular in time (see also
[
5
]
,
[
24
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
16
]
for recent developments).
This approach leads to mild or weak/distributional solutions also because it
cannot benefit from any regularizing effect of the semigroup in time.

On the other hand, for
B
as above, a function
u\in C^{0,\alpha}_{B}
satisfies (see Theorem
2.4
in the next Section), for any
(t,x),(s,{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

|u(t,x)-u(s,{\xi})|\leqslant C\|(s,{\xi})^{-1}\circ(t,x)\|^{\alpha}_{B}\sim|t-s|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}+|x_{1}-{\xi}_{1}|^{\alpha}+|x_{2}-{\xi}_{2}-(t-s){\xi}_{1}|^{\frac{\alpha}{3}}.

Notice that for points that are on the same time level, the increments are controlled as for (
1.18
); otherwise, as opposed to the standard parabolic case, the regularity in the euclidean directions is somehow entangled due to the group law associated to
B
.
This functional setting, which exploits some regularity in the full set of variables, is more suited to obtain classical solutions (see
[
10
]
,
[
21
]
and the references therein). To the best of our knowledge this is the first attempt at obtaining a general interpolation characterization for the spaces
C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
. For this purpose, notice that while it is true that

\big(C,{\mathcal{D}}(Y)\big)_{\theta,\infty}=\{f\in C,\ t\mapsto\phi(t):=t^{-\theta}\|e^{tY}f-f\|_{\infty}\in L^{\infty}(0,\infty)\}=C_{Y}^{\theta},

since the vector fields
\partial_{x_{1}}
and
Y
don’t commute, it’s not at all clear whether

\big(C,{\mathcal{D}}(\partial^{2}_{x_{1}})\cap{\mathcal{D}}(Y)\big)_{\frac{\theta}{2},\infty}=\left(C,{\mathcal{D}}(\partial^{2}_{x_{1}})\right)_{\frac{\theta}{2},\infty}\cap\left(C,{\mathcal{D}}(Y)\right)_{\frac{\theta}{2},\infty}.

(1.19)

This makes it difficult to pursue an approach based on semigroup theory.
Instead of trying to prove (
1.19
) we follow a constructive approach based on approximation: for instance, for any
u\in C^{0,\alpha}_{B}
we can define

u_{\varepsilon}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}u({\zeta})\phi\left(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)\right)\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1},

where
\phi
is a test function with unit integral, supported on
\|y\|_{B}\leqslant 1
. This approximation complies with the Lie structure induced by
B
, and indeed we prove the following expected controls

|u-u_{\varepsilon}|_{\infty}\leqslant c\varepsilon^{\alpha}\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}},\quad\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1,0}_{B}}\leqslant c\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}},

(1.20)

so that

\varepsilon^{-\alpha}K(\varepsilon,u;C,C^{1,0}_{B})\leqslant\varepsilon^{-\alpha}(|u-u_{\varepsilon}|_{\infty}+\varepsilon\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1,0}_{B}})\leqslant 2c\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}_{B}}.

This way of reasoning is fairly standard (see, for instance, Chapter 1 in
[
20
]
), though the estimates (
1.20
) are new for the intrinsic spaces and we present a generalization for any order
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in Theorem
3.1
.

2
Preliminaries

Here we collect some remarks and known preliminary results which will play a central role for the next Sections.

Remark 2.1
.

Under Assumption
1.1
the matrix
B
is a nilpotent matrix of degree
r+1
. In particular

e^{\delta B}=I_{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{r}\frac{B^{j}}{j!}\delta^{j},

is a lower triangular matrix with diagonal
(1,\dots,1)
and therefore it has determinant equal to 1.
Indeed, by Assumption
1.1
, a direct computation shows that, for any
n\leqslant r
(recalling the notation (
1.5
) for
\bar{p}_{j}
) we have

B^{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0_{\bar{p}_{n-1}\times p_{0}}&0_{\bar{p}_{n-1}\times p_{1}}&\cdots&0_{\bar{p}_{n-1}\times p_{r-n}}&0_{\bar{p}_{n-1}\times(\bar{p}_{r}-\bar{p}_{r-n}})\\
\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\bf B}_{j}&0_{p_{n}\times p_{1}}&\cdots&0_{p_{n}\times p_{r-n}}&0_{p_{n}\times(\bar{p}_{r}-\bar{p}_{r-n})}\\
0_{p_{n+1}\times p_{0}}&\prod_{j=2}^{n+1}{\bf B}_{j}&\cdots&0_{p_{n+1}\times p_{r-n}}&0_{p_{n+1}\times(\bar{p}_{r}-\bar{p}_{r-n})}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0_{p_{r}\times p_{0}}&0_{p_{r}\times p_{1}}&\cdots&\prod_{j=r-n+1}^{r}{\bf B}_{j}&0_{p_{r}\times(\bar{p}_{r}-\bar{p}_{r-n})}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\prod_{j=1}^{n}{\bf B}_{j}={\bf B}_{n}{\bf B}_{n-1}\cdots{\bf B}_{1},

(2.1)

and
{B}^{n}=0
for
n>r
.

Remark 2.2
.

A simple computation shows that, for any
z,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,

y^{-1}\circ e^{\delta Y}z=e^{\delta Y}(y^{-1}\circ z),\qquad y^{-1}\circ e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z=e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}(y^{-1}\circ z),\quad i=1,\dots,d,

(2.2)

which is a way to restate the invariance of operator (
1.1
) w.r.t the group law.

Lemma 2.3
.

The following identities hold:

D_{\lambda}e^{\delta Y}(z)=e^{\delta\lambda^{2}Y}\left(D_{\lambda}z\right),\qquad D_{\lambda}e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}(z)=e^{\delta\lambda^{2j+1}\partial_{x_{i}}}D_{\lambda}z,\quad i=\bar{p}_{j-1}+1,\dots,\bar{p}_{j},

(2.3)

for any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and
\delta,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Let
z=(t,x)
, and let
D^{0}_{\lambda}
be the spatial part of the dilation operator
D_{\lambda}
, namely
D^{0}_{\lambda}=(\lambda I_{p_{0}},\lambda^{3}I_{p_{1}},\dots,\lambda^{2r+1}I_{p_{r}})
. Denoting with
x_{\{i\}}
,
i=0,\dots,r
, the projection of
x
on
\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
defined by

x_{\{i\}}:=(x_{\bar{p}_{i-1}+1},\dots,x_{\bar{p}_{i}}),

by (
2.1
) we can write

\left(e^{\delta B}x\right)_{\{i\}}=x_{\{i\}}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\delta^{i-j}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{i}{\bf B}_{k}\right)x_{\{j\}},\qquad i=0,\dots,r.

(2.4)

Then we have

\displaystyle\left(D^{0}_{\lambda}e^{\delta B}x\right)_{\{i\}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2i-1}x_{\{i\}}+\lambda^{2i-1}\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\delta^{i-j}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{i}{\bf B}_{k}\right)x_{\{j\}}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{2i-1}x_{\{i\}}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}(\lambda^{2}\delta)^{i-j}\left(\prod_{k=j+1}^{i}{\bf B}_{k}\right)\lambda^{2j-1}x_{\{j\}}=\left(e^{\delta\lambda^{2}B}\left(D^{0}_{\lambda}x\right)\right)_{\{i\}}.

where in the last equality we used that
\lambda^{2i-1}x_{\{i\}}=(D_{\lambda}x)_{\{i\}}
and
\lambda^{2j-1}x_{\{j\}}=(D_{\lambda}x)_{\{j\}}
. Thus we get

D_{\lambda}e^{\delta Y}z=\left(\lambda^{2}(t+\delta),e^{\delta\lambda^{2}B}\left(D^{0}_{\lambda}x\right)\right)=e^{\delta\lambda^{2}Y}\left(D(\lambda)z\right).

The second equality is clear from the definitions.
∎

We conclude this Section by recalling the crucial intrinsic Taylor expansion proved in
[
23
]
. Here
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
denotes a multi-index, and as usual
\beta!:=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(\beta_{j}!)
. Moreover, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x^{\beta}=x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\dots,x_{d}^{\beta_{d}}
and
\partial^{\beta}=\partial_{x}^{\beta}=\partial_{x_{1}}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial_{x_{d}}^{\beta_{d}}
. The
B
-length
of
\beta
is defined as

|\beta|_{B}:=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(2i+1)\sum_{j=\bar{p}_{i-1}+1}^{\bar{p}_{i}}\beta_{j}.

Theorem 2.4

(Intrinsic Taylor formula)
.

If
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
then we have

Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}_{x}u\in C_{B}^{n-2k-|\beta|_{B},\alpha},\qquad 0\leqslant 2k+|\beta|_{B}\leqslant n,

(2.5)

and

|u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)|\leqslant c_{B}\|u\|_{C_{B}^{n,\alpha}}\|{\zeta}^{-1}\circ z\|^{n+\alpha}_{B},\qquad z,{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1},

(2.6)

where
c_{B}
is a positive constant which only depend on
B
and
T_{n}u({\zeta},\cdot)
is the
n
-th order
B
-Taylor polynomial of
u
around
{\zeta}=(s,{\xi})
defined as

T_{n}u({\zeta},z)=\sum_{0\leqslant 2k+|\beta|_{B}\leqslant n}\frac{1}{k!\beta!}\left(Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}_{\xi}u(s,{\xi})\right)(t-s)^{k}(x-e^{(t-s)B}{\xi})^{\beta},\qquad z=(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}.

(2.7)

Notation
: Eventually, we will frequently use the notation
\lesssim
. For two quantities
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, we mean by
Q_{1}\lesssim Q_{2}
that there exists a constant
c_{B}
, only dependent on the matrix
B
, such that
Q_{1}\leqslant c_{B}Q_{2}
.

3
Approximation of
C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
functions

This Section is mainly devoted to the proof of the following Theorem
3.1
, which establishes an approximation property for our
B
-Hölder continuous functions.

Theorem 3.1

(Approximation property)
.

Let
n,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
. There exists a constant
c_{B}
, only dependent on
B
and
d
, such that for any
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
and
0<\varepsilon\leqslant 1
, there exists
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l,0}_{B}}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{B}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}},

\displaystyle l\leqslant n,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l,0}_{B}}

\displaystyle\leqslant c_{B}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\quad

\displaystyle{l>n}.

(3.2)

The proof is based on the intrinsic Hölder expansion of Theorem
2.4
and the invariance properties of Lemma
2.3
and Remark
2.2
. We also need the following technical Lemma, which remarkably says that we can exchange the differentiation of the intrinsic Taylor polynomial with the differentiation of the inner function, taking into account the intrinsic degree of the derivatives
\partial_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
and
Y
.

To avoid ambiguity, when necessary, we will write
Y_{z}
,
Y_{\zeta}
as well as
\partial_{x_{i}}
,
\partial_{{\xi}_{i}}
to clearly distinguish the variables with respect to which the derivation is applied.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
and
z=(t,x),\,{\zeta}=(s,{\xi})\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}T_{n}u({\zeta},z)

\displaystyle=T_{n-1}(\partial_{i}u)({\zeta},z),\quad n\geqslant 1,\quad i=1,\dots,p_{0},

(3.3)

\displaystyle Y_{z}T_{n}u({\zeta},z)

\displaystyle=T_{n-2}(Yu)({\zeta},z),\quad n\geqslant 2.

(3.4)

Proof.

Equality (
3.3
) is analogous to the Euclidean case and follows by a direct computation. Indeed it suffices to notice that, for
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
,
T_{n}u({\zeta},z)
depends on
x_{i}
only in the terms
(x-e^{(t-s)B}{\zeta})_{i}
and
\partial_{x_{j}}(x-e^{(t-s)B}{\zeta})_{i}=\delta_{ij}
(
\delta_{ij}
being the Kronecker Delta) for
i,j=1,\dots,p_{0}
.
Equality (
3.4
) on the other hand, needs a more careful analysis: the directional derivative
Y_{z}
is not null when applied both to the terms
(t-s)^{k}
and
(x-e^{(t-s)B}{\zeta})_{i}
for any
i>p_{0}
and, most importantly, some commutators need to be introduced in order to exchange the order of derivation between
\partial_{{\xi}_{i}}
and
Y_{\zeta}
, and make
Y_{\zeta}u
appear in any term of the sum.

First we introduce some notations: let
v:=x-e^{(t-s)B}{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let us denote with
v^{[i]}
,
i=0,\dots,r
, the projection of
v
on
\{0\}^{\bar{p}_{i-1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{{p}_{i}}\times\{0\}^{d-\bar{p}_{i}}
; then we denote with
X_{i}
the vector field
X_{i}:=\langle v^{[i]},\nabla\rangle
, were
\nabla
represent the usual Nabla operator. Clearly we have

\displaystyle Y_{z}X_{0}

\displaystyle=0,

(3.5)

Moreover we have

\displaystyle Y_{z}X_{i}u({\zeta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=\bar{p}_{i-1}+1}^{\bar{p}_{i}}(Bv)_{j}\partial_{{\xi}_{j}}u({\zeta})=\langle(Bv)^{[i]},\nabla u({\zeta})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Bv^{[i-1]},\nabla u({\zeta})\rangle=\left[X_{i-1},Y_{{\zeta}}\right]u({\zeta})=:X^{(1)}_{i-1}u({\zeta}).

(3.6)

To prove (
3.4
) we proceed by induction. For
n=2
we directly get

\displaystyle Y_{z}T_{2}u({\zeta},z)

\displaystyle=Y_{z}\left(u({\zeta})+X_{0}u({\zeta})+\frac{1}{2!}X_{0}^{2}u({\zeta})+Y_{\zeta}u({\zeta})(t-s)\right)=Y_{z}Y_{\zeta}u({\zeta})(t-s)=Y_{\zeta}u({\zeta}).

Then we proceed in two steps, namely, the induction from
2n+1
to
2n+2
and the induction from
2n
to
2n+1
.

1: Induction from
2n+1
to
2n+2

Let
\beta=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r+1}
and let us denote just in the context of this proof

|\beta|_{B}=\sum_{i=0}^{r}(2i+1)\beta_{i},\quad\beta!=\beta_{1}!\cdots\beta_{r}!.

Then, with the notation introduced above we can conveniently write

\displaystyle T_{2n+2}u({\zeta},z)

\displaystyle=T_{2n+1}u({\zeta},z)+\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n+1)-2k}\frac{1}{k!\beta!}Y_{\xi}^{k}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X_{n-k}^{\beta_{n-k}}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=:T_{2n+1}u({\zeta},z)+\tilde{T}_{2n+1}u({\zeta},z).

(3.8)

By (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) we have

\displaystyle Y_{z}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k}(t-s)^{k}

\displaystyle=Y_{{\zeta}}^{k}(t-s)^{k-1},

(3.9)

\displaystyle Y_{z}X^{\beta_{i}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}X^{\beta_{i}-1}_{i}X^{(1)}_{i-1}

(3.10)

\displaystyle Y_{{\zeta}}X^{\beta_{i}}_{i}

\displaystyle=X^{\beta_{i}}_{i}Y_{\zeta}-\beta_{i}X^{\beta_{i}-1}_{i}X^{(1)}_{i}.

(3.11)

Therefore, applying
Y_{z}
to
\tilde{T}_{2n+1}u({\zeta},z)
we get
Y_{z}\tilde{T}_{2n+1}u({\zeta},z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}S_{k}
with

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2n+2}\frac{1}{\beta!}\sum_{i=0}^{n}{\beta_{i}}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{i}-1}_{i}\cdots X^{\beta_{n}}_{n}X^{(1)}_{i-1}u({\zeta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2n+2}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\beta_{0}!\cdots(b_{i+1}-1)!\cdots\beta_{n}!}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{i+1}-1}_{i+1}\cdots X^{\beta_{n}}_{n}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-i)-1}\frac{1}{\beta!}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{n-i-1}}_{n-i-1}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta}),

and, for
k=1,\dots,n+1
,

\displaystyle S_{k}=

\displaystyle\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n+1)-2k}\frac{(t-s)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k-1}\left(X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X_{n-k}^{\beta_{n-k}}Y_{\zeta}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\beta_{i}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X_{i}^{\beta_{i}-1}\cdots X_{n-k}^{\beta_{n-k}}X^{(1)}_{i}\right)u({\zeta})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n+1)-2k}\frac{(t-s)^{k}}{k!\beta!}Y_{\zeta}^{k}\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\beta_{i}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{i}-1}_{i}\cdots X^{\beta_{n-k}}_{n-k}X^{(1)}_{i-1}u({\zeta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{k}^{(1)}-\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-k-i)+1}\frac{1}{(k-1)!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k-1}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{n-i-k}}_{n-i-k}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-1}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-k-i)-1}\frac{1}{k!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{n-i-k-1}}_{n-i-k-1}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{k}^{(1)}+S_{k}^{(2)}+S_{k}^{(3)}.

Now notice that

S_{0}+S_{1}^{(2)}=0\qquad S_{k}^{(3)}+S_{k+1}^{(2)}=0,\quad k=1,\dots,n.

(3.12)

Moreover
S_{n}^{(3)}=S_{n+1}^{(2)}=S_{n+1}^{(3)}=0
by (
3.9
). Thus we finally get

\displaystyle Y_{z}\tilde{T}_{2n+2}u({\zeta},z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S^{(1)}_{k+1}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2n-2k}\frac{1}{k!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X_{n-k-1}^{\beta_{n-k-1}}Y_{\zeta}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k}=\tilde{T}_{2n}Yu({\zeta},z),

and therefore, using the induction hypotheses

Y_{z}T_{2n+2}u({\zeta},z)=T_{2n-1}Yu({\zeta},z)+\tilde{T}_{2n}Yu({\zeta},z)=T_{2n}Yu({\zeta},z).

2: Induction from
2n
to
2n+1

This Step only differs from the previous one by the tracking of the indexes throughout the computations and the fact that the polynomial
\tilde{T}_{2n+1}
always contains at least one field of type
X_{i}
. We write

Y_{z}\tilde{T}_{2n+1}u({\zeta},z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S_{k}
and we derive, with similar computations than in the previous step

S_{0}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-i)}\frac{1}{\beta!}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X_{n-i-1}^{\beta_{n-i-1}}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta}),

and, for any
k=1,\dots,n-1
,

\displaystyle S_{k}=

\displaystyle\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-k)+1}\frac{1}{(k-1)!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k-1}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X_{n-k}^{\beta_{n-k}}Y_{\zeta}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\quad-\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-k-i)}\frac{1}{(k-1)!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k-1}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{n-i-k}}_{n-i-k}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k-1}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-k-1}\sum_{|\beta|_{B}=2(n-k-i-1)}\frac{1}{k!\beta!}Y_{{\zeta}}^{k}X_{0}^{\beta_{0}}\cdots X^{\beta_{n-i-k-1}}_{n-i-k-1}X^{(1)}_{i}u({\zeta})(t-s)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{k}^{(1)}+S_{k}^{(2)}+S_{k}^{(3)}.

In particular,
S_{0}+S_{1}^{(2)}=0
,
S_{k}^{(3)}+S_{k+1}^{(2)}=0
for any
k=1,\dots,n-1
and
S_{n}^{(3)}=0
by (
3.5
). Therefore we can eventually derive (
3.4
).
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
and let
\phi
be a test function supported on
\|z\|_{B}\leqslant 1
with unit integral. We define our candidate approximation by

\displaystyle u^{(n)}_{\varepsilon}(z)=T_{n}u(\cdot,z)\star_{B}\phi_{\varepsilon}(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}T_{n}u({\zeta},z)\phi\left(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)\right)\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}},

where
T_{n}u({\zeta},z)
is the Taylor polynomial in (
2.7
) and
Q
is the spatial
homogeneous dimension
of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
w.r.t
(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0}
, that is the positive integer

Q=p_{0}+3p_{1}+\cdots(2r+1)p_{r}.

Notice that, by the change of variables
\bar{{\zeta}}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)
, recalling Remark
2.1
, it is easy to verify that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\phi\left(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)\right)\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}=\int_{\|{\zeta}\|_{B}\leqslant 1}\phi({\zeta})d{\zeta}=1.

(3.13)

In particular we have

\displaystyle u(z)-u^{(n)}_{\varepsilon}(z)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left(u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)\right)\phi\left(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)\right)\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}.

Observe now that the differentiation of
u^{(n)}_{\varepsilon}
falls back both on the test function and the polynomial
T_{n}u
, the latter being handled by Lemma
3.2
; for the former case, by Lemma
2.3
and Remark
2.2
we deduce

\displaystyle Y_{z}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-2}Y\phi(\bar{\zeta})\mid_{\bar{\zeta}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)},

\displaystyle\partial_{z_{j}}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{-(2i+1)}\partial_{j}\phi(\bar{\zeta})\mid_{\bar{\zeta}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)},\quad\bar{p}_{i-1}<j\leqslant\bar{p}_{i},

and, more generally, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
,

Y^{k}_{z}\partial_{z}^{\beta}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))=\varepsilon^{-2k-|\beta|_{B}}Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}\phi(\bar{z})\mid_{\bar{z}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)}.

(3.14)

1: Preliminary controls

Let us denote a general intrinsic derivative of order
m
,
{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}=\sum_{2k+|\beta|_{B}=m}Y^{k}\partial^{\beta}
, and let

I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left(u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)\right){\mathfrak{D}}_{z}^{\beta}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}.

We want to prove that

\displaystyle|I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-m}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}},

(3.15)

\displaystyle[I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-(m+1)}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}},\quad n\geqslant 1.

(3.16)

By (
3.14
), the change of variable
\bar{\zeta}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)
, and Theorem
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle|I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left|\left(u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)\right){\mathfrak{D}}^{m}_{z}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))\right|\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\|{\zeta}^{-1}\circ z\|_{B}^{n+\alpha}\varepsilon^{-m}{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{\zeta})\mid_{\bar{\zeta}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)}\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\|\bar{\zeta}\|_{B}\leqslant 1}\|D_{\varepsilon}\bar{\zeta}\|_{B}^{n+\alpha}\varepsilon^{-m}{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{\zeta})d\bar{\zeta}\lesssim\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-m}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}},

were we used that
\|D_{\varepsilon}\bar{\zeta}\|_{B}=\varepsilon\|\bar{\zeta}\|_{B}
in the last inequality, and this proves (
3.15
). On the other hand we have

\displaystyle|I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u(z)-I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u(e^{\delta Y}z)|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left|u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)\right|\left|{\mathfrak{D}}_{z^{\prime}}^{m}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z^{\prime}))-{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}_{z}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))\right|_{z^{\prime}=e^{\delta Yz}}\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},e^{\delta Y}z)-\left(u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)\right)\right|\left|{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}_{z}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))\right|\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}

\displaystyle\qquad=:\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,1}+\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}.

By (
3.14
), Theorem
3.3
, Lemma
2.3
, and the usual change of variables
\bar{\zeta}=D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z)
, we have

\displaystyle\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\|{\zeta}^{-1}\circ z\|^{n+\alpha}_{B}\varepsilon^{-m}\left|{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{{\zeta}}^{\prime})-{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{{\zeta}})\right|_{\bar{{\zeta}}^{\prime}=e^{\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}Y}\bar{{\zeta}}}\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\|\bar{\zeta}\|_{B}\leqslant 1}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-m}\left|\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right|^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\phi\|_{C^{m+1,0}_{B}}d\bar{\zeta}\lesssim|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-(m+1)}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

To control
\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}
we write

\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}=\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}\mathbb{I}_{\delta>\varepsilon^{2}}+\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}\mathbb{I}_{\delta\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}}.

We have

\displaystyle\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}\mathbb{I}_{\delta>\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left(|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},e^{\delta Y}z)|+|u(z)-T_{n}u({\zeta},z)|\right)\varepsilon^{-m}\left|{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{\zeta})\right|\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}\mathbb{I}_{\delta>\varepsilon^{2}}

(using that
{\zeta}^{-1}\circ e^{\delta Y}z=D_{\varepsilon}e^{\delta\varepsilon^{2}Y}\bar{\zeta}
by Lemma
2.3
and Remark
2.2
)

\displaystyle\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\|\bar{\zeta}\|_{B}\leqslant 1}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-m}(\|e^{\delta\varepsilon^{2}Y}\bar{\zeta}\|^{n+\alpha}_{B}+\|\bar{\zeta}\|^{n+\alpha}_{B})\left|{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{\zeta})\right|d\bar{\zeta}\mathbb{I}_{\delta>\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-m}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\mathbb{I}_{\delta>\varepsilon^{2}}\lesssim|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-(m+1)}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

Finally, assume for a moment
n\geqslant 2
.
By the standard mean value theorem and Lemma
3.2
, there exists
\lambda\in[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\Delta I^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon,2}\mathbb{I}_{\delta\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\delta|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\left|Yu(z^{\prime})-T_{n-2}Yu({\zeta},z^{\prime})\right|_{{\zeta}^{\prime}=e^{\lambda\delta Y}z}\varepsilon^{-m}\left|{\mathfrak{D}}^{m}\phi(\bar{\zeta})\right|\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}\mathbb{I}_{\delta\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\delta|\|Yu\|_{C^{n-2,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}\|{\zeta}^{-1}\circ e^{\lambda\delta Y}z\|^{n-2+\alpha}_{B}\varepsilon^{-m}\|\phi\|_{C^{m,0}_{B}}\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}\mathbb{I}_{\delta\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\delta|\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\int_{\|{\zeta}\|_{B}\leqslant 1}\varepsilon^{n-2+\alpha-m}\|e^{\lambda\delta\varepsilon^{2}Y}\bar{\zeta}\|^{n-2+\alpha}_{B}{d\bar{\zeta}}\mathbb{I}_{\delta\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\delta|\varepsilon^{n-2+\alpha-m}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}\mathbb{I}_{\delta\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}}\lesssim|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-(m+1)}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

If
n=0
or
n=1
we have
T_{1}u({\zeta},e^{\delta Y}z)=T_{1}u({\zeta},z)
and
T_{0}u({\zeta},z)=T_{0}u({\zeta},e^{\delta Y}z)=u({\zeta})
, so that the expression simplifies and it suffices to exploit the regularity of
u
w.r.t the field
Y
: this proves (
3.16
).

2: Conclusions

Let

J^{(n,m)}_{\varepsilon}u(z):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d+1}}T_{n}u({\zeta},z){\mathfrak{D}}^{m}_{z}\phi(D_{\varepsilon^{-1}}({\zeta}^{-1}\circ z))\frac{d{\zeta}}{\varepsilon^{Q+2}}.

By iterative applications of the generalized Leibniz formula and Lemma
3.2
, for
l\leqslant n
:

\displaystyle\|u-u^{(n)}_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l,0}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{l}\left|I^{(n-i,l-i)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\right|_{\infty}+\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\left[I^{(n-i,l-i-1)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\right]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}

(using that
{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\in C^{n-i,\alpha}_{B}
by Theorem
2.4
, and (
3.15
)-(
3.16
) with
n-i
instead of
n
and
m=l-i
)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{l}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\|_{C^{n-i,\alpha}_{B}}+\sum_{i=0}^{l-1}\ \varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\|_{C^{n-i,\alpha}_{B}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

(3.17)

Similarly, for
l>n
, we have

\displaystyle\|u^{(n)}_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l,0}_{\alpha}}\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\left|J^{(n-i,l-i)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\right|_{\infty}+\left[J^{(n-i,l-i-1)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\right]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}\right)

Since
l>n
any term of the first sum sports, at least, one derivative applied on
\phi
; since the integral of the mollifier (
3.13
) is constant in
z
, the cancellation property
J^{(n-i,l-i)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u=I^{(n-i,l-i)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u
holds for any
i=0,\dots,n
. Similarly for the second sum. Therefore we finally get

\displaystyle\|u^{(n)}_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{l,0}_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\left|I^{(n-i,l-i)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\right|_{\infty}+\left[I^{(n-i,l-i-1)}_{\varepsilon}{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\right]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i=0}^{n}\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|{\mathfrak{D}}^{i}u\|_{C^{n-i,\alpha}_{B}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{n+\alpha-l}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

(3.18)

The proof is completed.
∎

4
Proofs of Proposition
1.5
and Theorem
1.6

Proof of Proposition
1.5
.

Let
u\in C^{n,0}_{B}
. By Proposition
3.1
we have

K(\lambda,u;C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2},0}_{B})\lesssim\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n_{1},0}_{B}}+\lambda\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n_{2},0}_{B}}\lesssim(\varepsilon^{n-n_{1}}+\lambda\varepsilon^{n-n_{2}})\|u\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}}.

Then, taking
\varepsilon=\lambda^{\frac{1}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}
we directly get
K(\lambda,u;C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2},0}_{B})\lesssim\lambda^{\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}\|u\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}}
, which gives the set inclusion on the right. It remains to prove that, for any
u\in C^{n_{2},0}_{B}

\|u\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}}\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n_{1},0}_{B}}^{1-\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}\|u\|_{C^{n_{2},0}_{B}}^{\frac{n-n_{1}}{n_{2}-n_{1}}}.

(4.1)

We check that (
4.1
) holds for
(n_{1},n_{2})=(n-1,n+1)
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, in which case, the inequality reads

\|u\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}}\lesssim\|u\|_{C^{n-1,0}_{B}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|u\|_{C^{n+1,0}_{B}}^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(4.2)

Then it is straightforward to see that (
4.1
) follows by repeated applications of (
4.2
) with itself. We proceed by induction on
n
.
By the standard Mean Value Theorem we have

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z)-u(z)-\partial_{x_{i}}u(z)\delta|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}|\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}u|_{\infty}\delta^{2},\quad i=1,\dots,p_{0}

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta^{2}Y}z)-u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|Yu|_{\infty}\delta^{2},

and therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}|\partial_{x_{i}}u|_{\infty}+[u]_{C^{1}_{Y}}\lesssim\delta^{-1}|u|_{\infty}+\delta\left(|\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}u|_{\infty}+|Yu|_{\infty}\right).

Taking the optimal
\delta>0
we get

\|u\|_{C^{1,0}_{B}}\lesssim|u|_{\infty}+|u|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\infty}\left(|\partial^{2}_{x_{i}}u|_{\infty}+|Yu|_{\infty}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim|u|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\infty}\|u\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{C^{2,0}_{B}}.

(4.3)

This proves (
4.2
) for
n=1
.
Next, by the mean value Theorem along the vector field
Y
we have that, for every
z\in R^{d+1}
and
\delta\neq 0
, there exists
\bar{\delta}
,
|\bar{\delta}|\leqslant|\delta|
such that

u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)-\delta Yu(z)=\delta\left(Yu(e^{\bar{\delta}Y}z)-Yu(z)\right).

Then, dividing by
|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}
we easily derive

|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}|Yu|_{\infty}\lesssim[u]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}+|\delta|[Yu]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}

(4.4)

By (
4.3
) and (
4.4
), taking the optimal
\delta
, we eventually get

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{2,0}_{B}}

\displaystyle=|u|_{\infty}+|Yu|_{\infty}+\sum_{i=0}^{p_{0}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{C^{1,0}_{B}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|u|_{\infty}+[u]^{\frac{1}{2}}_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}[Yu]^{\frac{1}{2}}_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}+\sum_{i=0}^{p_{0}}|\partial_{x_{i}}u|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\infty}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{C^{2,0}_{B}}\lesssim\|u\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{C^{1,0}}\|u\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{C^{3,0}}.

This proves (
4.2
) for
n=2
. The general case simply follows by the iterative definition of the spaces and the induction hypothesis.
∎

Before we proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.6
, we recall another important tool in the theory of interpolation, that is the well known Reiteration Theorem (see
[
20
]
, Theorem 1.23 or
[
27
]
, Section 1.10.2).

Theorem 4.1

(Reiteration Theorem)
.

Let
\{Z_{1},Z_{2}\}
be an interpolation couple, and let
E_{1},E_{2}
be some intermediate spaces between
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
. If

\big(Z_{1},Z_{2}\big)_{\theta_{i},1}\subseteq E_{i}\subseteq\big(Z_{1},Z_{2}\big)_{\theta_{i},\infty},\quad i=1,2

for some
\theta_{i}
such that
0\leqslant\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}\leqslant\infty
, then

\big(E_{1},E_{2}\big)_{\alpha,p}=\big(Z_{1},Z_{2}\big)_{(1-\alpha)\theta_{1}+\alpha\theta_{2},p},\quad\alpha\in(0,1),\ p\geqslant 1.

We are finally ready to prove Theorem
1.6
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

The proof is a fairly standard application of Theorem
3.1
, Proposition
1.5
and Theorem
4.1
. We proceed in two steps:

Step 1
: We prove (
1.15
) for
\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}=0
and
n_{1}=n
,
n_{2}=n+1
. Precisely, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and
0<\alpha<1
,

\left(C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\alpha,\infty}=\left(C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n,1}_{B}\right)_{\alpha,\infty}=C^{n,\alpha}_{B}.

(\supseteq)
: This inclusion is a direct consequence of the approximation result of Theorem
3.1
.
Indeed, for any
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
and
\lambda\in[0,1)
we have

K(\lambda,u;C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B})\lesssim\|u-u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n,0}_{B}}+\lambda\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{n+1,0}_{B}}\lesssim(\varepsilon^{\alpha}+\lambda\varepsilon^{\alpha-1})\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

Therefore, taking
\epsilon=\lambda
we get

K(\lambda,u;C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B})\lesssim\lambda^{\alpha}\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}.

On the other hand, for
\lambda\geqslant 1
it suffices to take
u_{\varepsilon}\equiv 0
.

(\subseteq)
: We proceed by induction on
n
.
Let
u\in C^{0,\alpha}_{B}
, then, for any choice of
a\in C
,
b\in C^{1,0}_{B}
such that
u=a+b
we have
|u|_{\infty}\leqslant|a|_{\infty}+|b|_{\infty}
and therefore

|u|_{\infty}\leqslant K(1,u;C,C^{1,0}_{B})\leqslant\|u\|_{(C,C^{1,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}}
.
Similarly, for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}

|u(e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z)-u(z)|\leqslant 2|a|_{\infty}+[b]_{C^{1}_{\partial_{x_{i}}}}|\delta|,\quad|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)|\leqslant 2|a|_{\infty}+[b]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1},

and thus, for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z)-u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2K(|\delta|,u;C,C^{1,0}_{B})\leqslant 2|\delta|^{\alpha}\|u\|_{(C,C^{1,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}},

(4.5)

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2K(|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}},u;C,C^{1,0}_{B})\leqslant 2|\delta|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|u\|_{(C,C^{1,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}}.

(4.6)

Gathering together (
4.5
)-(
4.6
), we get
the inclusion in the case
n=0
.
Next, let
u\in C^{1,\alpha}_{B}
. Clearly
|u|_{\infty}\leqslant K(1,u;C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})
.
Moreover,for any choice of
a\in C^{1,0}_{B}
,
b\in C^{2,0}_{B}
such that
u=a+b
we have, for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z)-u(z)-\delta\partial_{x_{i}}u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2|\partial_{x_{i}}a|_{\infty}|\delta|+\delta^{2}[\partial_{x_{i}}b]_{C^{1}_{\partial_{x_{i}}}}|\delta^{2}|,

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant[a]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}+|Yb|_{\infty}|\delta|,

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{i}}u(z^{\prime})-\partial_{x_{i}}u(z)|_{z^{\prime}=e^{\delta Y}z}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2|\partial_{x_{i}}a|_{\infty}+[\partial_{x_{i}}b]_{C^{\frac{1}{2}}_{Y}}|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}},

and therefore, for any
i=1,\dots,p_{0}

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta\partial_{x_{i}}}z)-u(z)-\delta\partial_{x_{i}}u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2|\delta|K(|\delta|,u;C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})\leqslant 2|\delta|^{1+\alpha}\|u\|_{(C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}},

(4.7)

\displaystyle|u(e^{\delta Y}z)-u(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}}K(|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}},u;C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})\leqslant|\delta|^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\|u\|_{(C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}},

(4.8)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{i}}u(z^{\prime})-\partial_{x_{i}}u(z)|_{z^{\prime}=e^{\delta Y}z}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2K(|\delta|^{\frac{1}{2}},u;C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})\leqslant 2|\delta|^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|u\|_{(C^{1,0}_{B},C^{2,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}}.

(4.9)

Gathering together (
4.7
)-(
4.9
), we get
the inclusion in the case
n=1
.
Finally, for
u\in C^{n,\alpha}_{B}
,
n\geqslant 2
, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{C^{n,\alpha}_{B}}

\displaystyle=|u|_{\infty}+\|Yu\|_{C^{n-2,\alpha}_{B}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{C^{n-1,\alpha}_{B}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|u|_{\infty}+\|Yu\|_{(C^{n-2,0}_{B},C^{n-1,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}}+\sum_{i=1}^{p_{0}}\|\partial_{x_{i}}u\|_{(C^{n-1,0}_{B},C^{n,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}}\lesssim\|u\|_{(C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B})_{\alpha,\infty}},

where we used that
K(\lambda,\partial_{x_{i}}u;C^{n-1,0}_{B},C^{n,0}_{B})\leqslant K(\lambda,u;C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B})
as well as

K(\lambda,Yu;C^{n-2,0}_{B},C^{n-1,0}_{B})\leqslant K(\lambda,u;C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B})
, for any
\lambda\geqslant 0
,
i=1,\dots,p_{0}
.

Step 2
: Let now
n_{1},\,n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
n_{1}<n_{2}
, and let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in[0,1]
.

\displaystyle\left(C^{n_{1},\alpha_{1}}_{B},C^{n_{2},\alpha_{2}}_{B}\right)_{\theta,\infty}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{1}+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\alpha_{1},\infty},\left(C^{n_{2},0}_{B},C^{n_{2}+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\alpha_{2},\infty}\right)_{\theta,\infty}

\displaystyle=\left(\left(C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2}+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\frac{\alpha_{1}}{n_{2}+1-n_{1}},\infty},\left(C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2}+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\frac{n_{2}+\alpha_{2}-n_{1}}{n_{2}+1-n_{1}},\infty}\right)_{\theta,\infty}

\displaystyle=:\left(C^{n_{1},0}_{B},C^{n_{2}+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\theta^{\prime},\infty}

Taking

\displaystyle\left(C^{n_{1},\alpha_{1}}_{B},C^{n_{2},\alpha_{2}}_{B}\right)_{\theta,\infty}

\displaystyle=\left(C^{n,0}_{B},C^{n+1,0}_{B}\right)_{\theta^{\prime}(n_{2}+1-n_{1})-(n-n_{1}),\infty}=C^{n,\alpha_{1}+\theta[(n_{2}+\alpha_{2})-(n_{1}+\alpha_{1})]-(n-n_{1})}_{B}.

The proof is completed.
∎
