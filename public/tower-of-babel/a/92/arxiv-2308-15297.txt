The geometry and arithmetic of bielliptic Picard curves
Jef Laga, Ari Shnidman

Abstract
. We study the geometry and arithmetic of the curves C : y 3 = x 4 + a ​ x 2 + b C\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+b and their associated Prym abelian surfaces P P . 1.
Introduction

Let
k
be a field of characteristic neither
2
nor
3
. A
bielliptic Picard curve
over
k
is a smooth projective curve
C
with an affine model of the form

(1.1)

\displaystyle y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+b

for some
a,b\in k
. Such a curve is equipped with both a
\mu_{3}
-action
(x,y)\mapsto(x,\omega y)
and a commuting involution
\tau\colon(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)
. The induced involution
\tau^{*}
on the Jacobian variety
J=\Jac_{C}
allows us to define the Prym variety
P=\ker(1+\tau^{*})
.
The abelian surface
P/k
inherits a
\mu_{3}
-action and carries a
(1,2)
-polarization; it need not be principally polarizable over
k
.

The goal of this paper is to explore the remarkably rich geometry and arithmetic of bielliptic Picard curves and their associated Pryms.

1.1.
Basic results

We first prove some foundational results for bielliptic Picard curves. We

(1)

prove a Torelli theorem for the association
C\mapsto P
, once suitable data on both sides is fixed (Theorem
3.13
);

(2)

show
{\rm End}(P_{\bar{k}})
contains a maximal order
\mathcal{O}
in a discriminant
6
quaternion algebra (Proposition
6.1
), in other words
P_{\bar{k}}
has quaternionic multiplication (QM) by
\mathcal{O}
;

(3)

give an explicit description of the Artin representation
\rho_{{\rm End}}\colon{\rm Gal}_{k}\to{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{O})\hookrightarrow{\rm GL}_{4}({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
describing the Galois action on the endomorphism ring
\mathcal{O}
(Corollary
6.6
);

(4)

relate the moduli space of bielliptic Picard curves to a unitary Shimura curve
Y/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
and a quaternionic Shimura curve (Section
4.3
);

(5)

determine the finitely many
\bar{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-isomorphism classes of Pryms
P/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
which are geometrically non-simple (Proposition
6.18
), corresponding to the CM points on
Y
.

Our Torelli result (
1
) is perhaps surprising because Barth
[
4
]
has shown that the Prym construction on all bielliptic genus
3
curves does not satisfy a Torelli theorem, but has one-dimensional fibres. There are other instances of algebraic families of abelian surfaces with QM in the literature
[
3
,
21
]
, but (
2
) is interesting because of the very simple nature of this family.
Moreover, contrary to previous approaches, our proof constructs the quaternionic action explicitly and geometrically, using the automorphisms and the polarization of
P
.
Part (
3
) gives an interesting source of abelian surfaces with large endomorphism field and, in the notation of
[
17
]
, produces the first published examples of geometrically simple abelian surfaces with Sato–Tate group
J(E_{3})
and
J(E_{6})
(Example
6.7
).
Part (
4
) and Shimura reciprocity allow us to calculate all geometrically non-simple Prym varieties in (
5
).

1.2.
Rational torsion subgroups

To further illustrate the rich and accessible nature of these curves, we classify the finite torsion subgroups that arise in the Mordell-Weil groups
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
of Pryms of bielliptic Picard curves over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
P/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
be the Prym surface of a bielliptic Picard curve
C/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
. Then

P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}\simeq\begin{cases}{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/n{\rm\mathbb{Z}}&\mbox{ for some }n\in\{1,2,3,6\},\mbox{ or }\\
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/n{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\times{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/n{\rm\mathbb{Z}}&\mbox{ for some }n\in\{2,3\},\mbox{ or }\\
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/6{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\times{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/3{\rm\mathbb{Z}}.&\\
\end{cases}

Conversely, for each finite abelian group
G
above, there exist infinitely many
\bar{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
-isomorphism classes of bielliptic Picard Prym surfaces
P/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
with
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}\simeq G
.

Theorem
1.1
is the analogue of Mazur’s classification of rational torsion points of elliptic curves
[
33
]
in our setting.
As with elliptic curves, each finite group allowed by Theorem
1.1
can be realized by an explicit family (in fact, sometimes multiple families) of Pryms whose coefficients can be rationally parameterized.
This is in accordance with the philosophy of Mazur and Ogg, namely that torsion should only occur as a consequence of the ambient geometry of the relevant moduli space; see
[
34
]
for a recent survey. Theorem
1.1
is significantly easier to prove than Mazur’s theorem, essentially because
P
has everywhere potentially good reduction. Nonetheless, it appears to be the first classification of rational torsion on a universal abelian variety over a Shimura variety which is not a modular curve.

Remark 1.2
.

In a separate work with Schembri and Voight
[
28
]
, we significantly extend some of our arguments here to show that for any abelian surface
A/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
such that
{\rm End}(A_{\bar{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}})
is a maximal order in a non-split quaternion algebra over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, there is the uniform bound
|A({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}|\leq 18
. Theorem
1.1
shows that this bound is sharp. Contrary to
[
28
, Theorem 1.3]
, Theorem
1.1
is a complete classification, which we achieve using arguments specific to the geometry of the abelian surfaces considered here.

Example 1.3
.

By Proposition
7.16
below, for each
t\in{\rm\mathbb{Q}}\setminus\{0,1,-1\}
the Prym
P_{t}
associated to the curve

2(t^{2}-1)^{2}y^{3}=(x^{2}-1)(x^{2}-t^{2})

satisfies
P_{t}({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}\simeq{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/3{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\times{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/6{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
, achieving the maximum order allowed by Theorem
1.1
.

Remark 1.4
.

Theorem
1.1
implies that the rational torsion subgroup
J({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}
of the Jacobian of a bielliptic Picard curve is
12
-torsion.
A complete classification of the groups
J({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}
would require parameterizing points of order
4
in
J({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.

It is natural to ask how the torsion subgroup of
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
is affected by the presence of endomorphisms defined over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
. Generically, we have
{\rm End}(P)={\rm\mathbb{Z}}
, but it can also happen that
{\rm End}(P)
is an order in a quadratic field.
Such
P
are said to be
of
{\rm GL}_{2}
-type
, and are modular by work of Khare-Wintenberger
[
25
]
. After proving explicit conditions on
a
and
b
for
P
to be of
{\rm GL}_{2}
-type (see §
6.3
), we classify all the possible
{\rm End}(P)
-modules that arise as
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
P/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
be the Prym surface of a non-CM bielliptic Picard curve over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, with
{\rm End}(P)\neq{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
. Then
{\rm End}(P)\simeq{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]
for some
D\in\{2,6\}
. Furthermore, there is an isomorphism of
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]
-modules

P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}\simeq\begin{cases}\{0\}&\mbox{ or }\\
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]/\mathfrak{a_{p}}&\mbox{ for some $p\in\{2,3\}$ },\end{cases}

where
\mathfrak{a}_{p}
is the unique prime ideal of
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]
above
p
. Conversely, for each
D\in\{2,6\}
, and for each of the three cyclic
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]
-modules
G
that appear above, there are infinitely many
\overline{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
-isomorphism classes of Pryms
P/{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
such that
{\rm End}(P)\simeq{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]
and
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}\simeq G
as
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{D}]
-modules.

When
D=2
and
p=3
, Theorem
1.5
gives examples of
{\rm GL}_{2}
-type Pryms such that
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})_{\mathrm{tors}}\simeq{\rm\mathbb{F}}_{9}
, showing that the upper bound proven in
[
28
, Theorem 1.4]
for the order of the rational torsion subgroup of a QM abelian surface of
{\rm GL}_{2}
-type over
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
is in fact sharp.

1.3.
Methods

The geometry of
(1,2)
-polarized surfaces was analyzed in detail by Barth
[
4
]
and his results are crucial to our study of
C
and
P
. The key player in the story is the bigonal dual curve
\widehat{C}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+8ax^{2}+16(a^{2}-4b)
, which is the intersection of
P
with a well chosen theta divisor on
J
. The corresponding line bundle
\mathscr{M}=\mathcal{O}_{P}(\widehat{C})
represents the
(1,2)
-polarization on
P
.
The linear system
|\mathscr{M}|
is a pencil of genus
3
curves,
and the
\mu_{3}
-action on
C
induces a
\mu_{3}
-action on
P
and on the pencil.
The technical heart of this paper is to analyze this
\mu_{3}
-action on the pencil
|\mathscr{M}|
, both theoretically and explicitly.
Our Torelli theorem (
1
) boils down to showing that
\widehat{C}
is the unique bielliptic Picard curve in
|\mathscr{M}|
of the correct signature.
For the QM property (
2
), we give two different proofs. The first is indirect, relating the moduli of bielliptic Picard curves to a unitary Shimura curve (in other words, we first prove (
4
) and then deduce (
2
)). The second proof constructs the QM explicitly, by showing that a specific sextic twist of the original curve
C
also lives in
|\mathscr{M}|
, but carrying the opposite signature. This second proof allows us to prove (
3
) as well.

To prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
we must first exhibit explicit families with specified torsion subgroups, and then we must rule out all other finite abelian groups. To exhibit groups, we describe elements of
P[2]
and
P[3]
geometrically, as coming from the fixed points for the
\mu_{6}
-action. To rule out groups which are not
6
-torsion, we first use general arguments for QM abelian surfaces which are developed in greater generality in
[
28
]
. Instead of quoting the main results there, we give a simpler proof (tailored to our special family of surfaces), which also allows us to handle the geometrically non-simple case not treated in
[
28
]
.

1.4.
Previous results

Petkova–Shiga
[
37
]
considered bielliptic Picard curves from a complex analytic viewpoint.
Using period matrix computations, they show that their Pryms over
{\rm\mathbb{C}}
have quaternionic multiplication. Hashimoto-Murabayashi
[
21
]
and Baba-Granath
[
3
]
have studied genus two curves whose Jacobians
J^{\prime}
have QM by
\mathcal{O}
. Over
\overline{k}
, each surface
J^{\prime}
becomes isomorphic to a bielliptic Picard Prym
P
, but generally not over
k
. There is also work of Bonfanti-van Geemen
[
6
]
which gives an algebraic description of the embedding of the Shimura curve inside the moduli space of
(1,2)
-polarized abelian surfaces.

1.5.
Future work

There are many other avenues of research related to bielliptic Picard curves and their Pryms that are ripe for investigation. In
[
29
]
, we use our description of the
{\rm Gal}_{k}
-module
{\rm End}(P_{k^{\sep}})
to characterize the bielliptic Picard curves with torsion Ceresa cycle. One can also try to study the average rank of the Mordell-Weil group
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
; the average rank of
P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
in cubic and sextic twist families was already considered in
[
1
]
and
[
43
]
.
To help explore other arithmetic questions, it would be worthwhile to develop more robust algorithms that are tailored towards these curves, such as explicit descent algorithms, explicit addition laws, an analogue of Tate’s algorithm,
L
-functions, etc. We hope this work encourages others to study some of these topics.

2.
Bielliptic Picard curves and their Pryms

2.1.
Basic definitions

Let
k
be a field (always assumed of characteristic
\neq 2,3
).

Definition 2.1
.

Let
C/k
be a nice genus
3
curve.
We call a faithful
\mu_{6}
-action
\gamma\colon\mu_{6}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{Aut}(C)
on
C

bielliptic
if the quotient
C/\mu_{2}
is a genus one curve.
We say
C
is a
bielliptic Picard curve
over
k
if it admits a bielliptic
\mu_{6}
-action.

For
a,b\in k
, consider the projective plane curve
C_{a,b}
over
k
with affine equation

(2.1)

\displaystyle C_{a,b}\colon y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+b.

The curve
C_{a,b}
has a unique (
k
-rational) point
\infty
at infinity, and
C_{a,b}
is smooth if and only if

\Delta_{a,b}:=16b(a^{2}-4b)

is nonzero.
The
\mu_{2}
-action
\pm 1\cdot(x,y)=(\pm x,y)
and
\mu_{3}
-action
\omega\cdot(x,y)=(x,\omega y)
(where
\omega^{3}=1
) combine to a faithful bielliptic
\mu_{6}
-action
\gamma_{a,b}
, given by
\zeta\cdot(x,y)=(\zeta^{3}x,\zeta^{4}y)
, for every
\zeta\in\mu_{6}(k^{\sep})
. The unique fixed point for this action is
\infty
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
C/k
be nice genus
3
curve with faithful
\mu_{6}
-action
\gamma
.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

\gamma
is a bielliptic
\mu_{6}
-action (and consequently
C
is a bielliptic Picard curve).

(2)

\gamma
has a unique fixed point.

(3)

C/\mu_{3}
has genus zero.

(4)

C
is nonhyperelliptic.

(5)

There is an isomorphism
C\simeq C_{a,b}
for some
a,b\in k
such that
\gamma
corresponds to
\gamma_{a,b}
or
\gamma_{a,b}^{-1}
.

Proof.

For each
i
dividing
6
, let
n_{i}
be the number of
\mu_{i}
-fixed points. By Riemann-Hurwitz, we have
n_{2}\in\{0,4,8\}
,
n_{3}\in\{2,5\}
, and
n_{6}\leq 3
. Moreover,
\mu_{2}
permutes the
\mu_{3}
-fixed points and vice versa. So
n_{2}-n_{6}
is divisible by
3
and
n_{3}-n_{6}
is divisible by
2
. There are then three possibilities:

(a)

(n_{2},n_{3},n_{6})=(0,2,0)
, or

(b)

(n_{2},n_{3},n_{6})=(4,5,1)
, or

(c)

(n_{2},n_{3},n_{6})=(8,2,2)
.

The equivalence of
(1)
,
(2)
, and
(3)
follows immediately from the above cases. To show the equivalence of
(1)
and
(4)
, first observe that case
(c)
is hyperelliptic because
C/\mu_{2}
is genus
0
. Case
(a)
is also hyperelliptic since an unramified double cover of a genus two curve is again hyperelliptic
[
16
]
. It remains to show that
C
is non-hyperelliptic in case
(b)
. In that case, there is only one
\mu_{6}
-fixed point
\infty
, which is therefore
k
-rational. Moreover,
\infty
must have different local monodromy type for the
\mu_{3}
-cover
C\to C/\mu_{3}\simeq{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}
compared to the other four ramification points (which are permuted by the
\mu_{2}
-action). By Kummer theory, there is a model
C\colon y^{3}=f(x)
, for some quartic polynomial
f(x)\in k[x]
, and in particular
C
is a plane quartic curve. This shows that
(1)-(4)
are equivalent.

It is clear that
(5)
implies all the rest, so it remains to show the converse. However, we have seen that such a
C
has a model
y^{3}=f(x)
which we may assume takes the form

(2.2)

y^{3}=x^{4}+ax^{2}+cx+b

with the
\mu_{3}
-action
\omega\cdot(x,y)=(x,\omega y)
or
\omega\cdot(x,y)=(x,\omega^{-1}y)
for every
\omega\in\mu_{3}
.
We see from this model (as in the proof of
[
10
, Lemma 1.21(a)]
) that
C
admits an involution commuting with the
\mu_{3}
-action only if
c=0
and this involution is given by
(x,y)\mapsto(-x,y)
, proving
(5)
.
∎

There is one
k^{\sep}
-isomorphism class of bielliptic Picard curves that behaves so differently that it deserves its own name.

Definition 2.3
.

A bielliptic Picard curve
C
is
special
if it is
k^{\sep}
-isomorphic to
C_{0,1}:y^{3}=x^{4}+1
.

A bielliptic Picard curve
C
is special precisely when
{\rm Aut}(C_{k^{\sep}})
is larger than
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/6{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
; in that case, it is a group of order
48

[
30

, with
GAP
label
(48,33)
.
We will see in Lemma
2.9
that

is special if and only if

.

We say
C
a nice genus
3
curve is a Picard curve if it admits a faithful
\mu_{3}
-action such that the quotient
C/\mu_{3}
is isomorphic to
{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}
.
We say
C
is a bielliptic curve if it admits an involution
\tau

is a genus
1

To avoid notational conflicts, we show:

Lemma 2.4
.

If a nice genus
3

C/k
is both a Picard curve and a bielliptic curve, then it is a bielliptic Picard curve in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Proof.

Since
C
is a Picard curve, it has an affine equation of the form
y^{3}=f(x)
for some monic quartic
f(x)=x^{4}+ax^{2}+bx+c\in k[x]
with nonzero discriminant, and the
\mu_{3}
-action on
C
is given by
\omega\cdot(x,y)=(x,\omega y)
.
By assumption, there exists an involution
\tau\colon C\rightarrow C

2.4.
Twists

Let

be a marked bielliptic Picard curve over

By the twisting principle, every cocycle

Its

If

and

show that

and so every twist of

3.1.
Generalities on
(1,2)
-polarized surfaces

We recall and complement some results of Barth

Let

(1,2)
-polarized abelian surface over

We assume that

Recall our notations concerning linear systems and abelian varieties in §

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{L}\in{\rm Pic}(A_{k^{\sep}})
be a line bundle representing the polarization:
\lambda_{\mathscr{L}}=\lambda
.

(1)

\dim_{k}\HH^{0}(A,\mathscr{L})=2
.

(2)

The linear system
|\mathscr{L}|
has four base-points on which

acts simply transitively.

(3)

At most finitely many members of the pencil are singular (arithmetic) genus
3

2
with one node or the union of two genus
1

with intersection number
(X_{1}\cdot X_{2})=2
.

Proof.

See
[
4

.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a unique
\mathscr{L}\in{\rm Pic}(A_{k^{\sep}})
representing
\lambda
whose base locus is
A[\lambda]
.
This line bundle is symmetric:
[-1]^{*}\mathscr{L}\simeq\mathscr{L}
. Moreover, for every divisor
D\in|\mathscr{L}|
we have
[-1]^{*}D=D
.

Proof.

Denote the base locus of a line bundle
\mathscr{L}
by
\text{BL}(\mathscr{L})
.
Given

, let
t_{a}\colon A\rightarrow A
be the translation map and
\lambda(a)\in A^{\vee}(k^{\sep})={\rm Pic}^{0}(A_{k^{\sep}})
be its image under
\lambda
.
Then for any
\mathscr{L}
representing
\lambda
, the following identity holds:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\text{BL}(\lambda(a)\otimes\mathscr{L})=t_{a}^{*}\text{BL}(\mathscr{L}).

Indeed, by definition
\lambda(a)\otimes\mathscr{L}=t_{a}^{*}\mathscr{L}
.
Let
D,D^{\prime}
be two distinct members of the pencil
|\mathscr{L}|
. Then
t_{a}^{*}D,t_{a}^{*}D^{\prime}
are two distinct members of

So

, proving (

).
This identity, together with Lemma

(2), proves the existence and uniqueness of

.
The remainder of the lemma follows from
[
4
, Proposition 1.6]
.
∎

For the remainder of this section fix
\mathscr{L}\in{\rm Pic}(A_{k^{\sep}})
satisfying the properties of Lemma

.
Since there is a unique such line bundle up to isomorphism and

has a

Remark 3.3
.

This marks an interesting contrast with principal polarizations on abelian surfaces, which may not be represented by a
k
-rational line bundle.
Indeed, this is the case for a positive proportion of genus 2 Jacobians over

ordered by height
[

.

The next proposition shows that every smooth member of the pencil

realizes

as the dual Prym variety of a bielliptic genus

.
A

is a nice genus
3
curve together with an involution

on

with

For such a curve, let

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose that
X\in|\mathscr{L}|(k)
is smooth, and let
\tau
be the restriction of

to

Then
(X,\tau)
is a bielliptic genus

(1,2)
-polarized surfaces.

Proof.

See
[

.
∎

The abelian surface
A
can also be reconstructed from the singular members of the pencil.
By Lemma
3.1
(3), there are two cases to consider.

For the first case, let
X\in|\mathscr{L}|(k)
be a curve with one node, whose normalization
\tilde{X}\rightarrow X
is a genus
2
curve. Let
p\in X(k)
be the node and
q_{1},q_{2}
be its preimages in
\tilde{X}(k^{\sep})
.
The morphism
\tilde{X}\rightarrow X\hookrightarrow A
induces a homomorphism of abelian varieties
\phi\colon\Jac_{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow A
, sending a degree zero divisor class on

to its sum in

.

Proposition 3.5
.

The homomorphism
\phi\colon\Jac_{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow A
is an isogeny with kernel
\{0,q_{1}-q_{2}\}
.

Proof.

The image of
\phi
is an abelian subvariety containing
X
, so
\phi
is surjective.
By construction,
\phi(q_{1}-q_{2})=0
.
It suffices to prove
\phi
has degree
2
.
This follows from an intersection number calculation.
Given divisors
D,E
on a nice surface, denote by
[D],[E]
their class in the Picard group and their intersection number by
(D\cdot E)
.
The origin
0\in A(k)
lifts to a unique point
\infty\in\tilde{X}(k)
and we identify
\tilde{X}
with its image in
\Jac_{\tilde{X}}
using the point

.
Then
\phi^{*}([X])=(\deg\phi)[\tilde{X}]
and by the projection formula

\displaystyle(\deg\phi)(X,X)=(\phi^{*}X,\phi^{*}X)=(\deg\phi)^{2}(\tilde{X},\tilde{X}).

The adjunction formula tells us that
(X,X)=2\cdot 3-2=4
and
(\tilde{X},\tilde{X})=2
.
Therefore

, as claimed.
∎

For the second case, let
X=X_{1}\cup X_{2}\in|\mathscr{L}|(k)
be a pair of genus
1
curves with intersection number
2
and let
\tilde{X}=X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}\rightarrow X
be its normalization.
The map
\tilde{X}\rightarrow X\hookrightarrow A
induces a homomorphism
\phi\colon\Jac_{\tilde{X}}:=\Jac_{X_{1}}\times\Jac_{X_{2}}\rightarrow A
.

Proposition 3.6
.

The map
\phi
is an isogeny of degree
2
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of Proposition
3.5
; we omit the details.
∎

3.2.
Compatible
\mu_{3}
-actions on
(1,2)
-polarized abelian surfaces

Definition 3.7
.

A
\mu_{3}
-abelian surface
over
k
is a triple
(A,\lambda,\alpha)
, where
(A,\lambda)
is a
(1,2)
-polarized abelian surface and
\alpha\colon\mu_{3}\rightarrow{\rm Aut}(A_{k^{\sep}})
is a
{\rm Gal}_{k}
-equivariant group homomorphism such that for every primitive third root of unity
\omega\in k^{\sep}
:

(1)

\alpha(\omega)^{\vee}\circ\lambda\circ\alpha(\omega)=\lambda
;

(2)

\alpha(\omega)
has characteristic polynomial
X^{2}+X+1
on
T_{0}A=\HH^{0}(A,\Omega^{1}_{A})^{\vee}
.

The signature condition (2) is equivalent to
\alpha(\omega)^{2}+\alpha(\omega)+1=0
, and is motivated by Lemma
2.13
.
Condition (1) can be reformulated as follows: after extending
\mu_{3}\hookrightarrow{\rm Aut}(A_{k^{\sep}})
to a
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-algebra homomorphism
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\omega)\hookrightarrow{\rm End}(A_{k^{\sep}})\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
, the Rosati involution associated to
\lambda
restricts to the conjugation involution
a+b\omega\mapsto a+b\bar{\omega}
on
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}(\omega)
.

Remark 3.8
.

It would be more accurate to call such triples ‘
(1,2)
-polarized abelian surfaces with compatible
\mu_{3}
-action of signature
(1,1)
’, but we stick with
\mu_{3}
-abelian surfaces for brevity.

Let
(A,\lambda,\alpha)
be a
\mu_{3}
-abelian surface over
k
.
Assume that
(A,\lambda)
is indecomposable, so the results of §
3.1
apply.
Let
\mathscr{L}
be the unique line bundle on
A
representing
\lambda
with base locus
A[\lambda]
.
The compatibility of
\alpha
and
\lambda
and the uniqueness of
\mathscr{L}
show that
\alpha(\omega)^{*}\mathscr{L}\simeq\mathscr{L}
for all
\omega\in\mu_{3}(k^{\sep})
.
Each such isomorphism is unique up to
k^{\times}
-scaling so determines a
canonical
isomorphism
|\alpha(\omega)^{*}\mathscr{L}|\simeq|\mathscr{L}|

Via pullback this induces a
\mu_{3}
-action on the pencil
|\mathscr{L}|
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Every
\mu_{3}
-fixed point of
|\mathscr{L}|
is a smooth curve.

Proof.

We may assume that
k=k^{\sep}
.
Let
\omega\in k
be a third root of unity and for ease of notation denote
\alpha(\omega)\colon A\rightarrow A
by
\alpha
.
Let
X\in|\mathscr{L}|
be fixed by
\alpha
.
Assume for the sake of contradiction that
X
is singular, with normalization
\tilde{X}\rightarrow X
.
By Lemma
3.1
(3) and Propositions
3.5
and
3.6
, the map
\tilde{X}\rightarrow A
induces an isogeny
\phi\colon\Jac_{\tilde{X}}\rightarrow A
whose kernel
\{0,D\}
has order
2
.
Since
\alpha
preserves
X
, it induces an automorphism
\tilde{\alpha}
of
\Jac_{\tilde{X}}
and
\phi
is equivariant with respect to
\tilde{\alpha}
and
\alpha
.
It follows that
\tilde{\alpha}
fixes
D
.
Since
\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1=0
(Condition (2) in Definition
3.7
) and
\phi
is an isogeny,
\tilde{\alpha}^{2}+\tilde{\alpha}+1=0
.
Therefore the degree of
1-\tilde{\alpha}
is a power of
3
, contradicting the fact that
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/2{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\simeq\{0,D\}\subset\Jac_{\tilde{X}}[1-\tilde{\alpha}]
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Assume that
k=k^{\sep}
.
Then there are exactly
2
curves in
|\mathscr{L}|
that are preserved by the
\mu_{3}
-action.

Proof.

Let
\omega\in k
be a third root of unity.
By Proposition
3.9
, every fixed point of
\alpha(\omega)
on
|\mathscr{L}|
is smooth.
Since
|\mathscr{L}|
always contains singular curves
[
4
, §1.2]
, this implies that
\alpha(\omega)
acts nontrivially on
|\mathscr{L}|
.
By the classification of order
3
elements of
{\rm PGL}_{2}(k)
, we may choose coordinates on
{\rm\mathbb{P}}^{1}
so that
\alpha(\omega)
is given by
(x:y)\mapsto(\omega x:y)
.
This map clearly has
2
fixed points.
∎

Let
X_{1},X_{2}\in|\mathscr{L}_{k^{\sep}}|
be the members of the pencil that are preserved by the
\mu_{3}
-action
\alpha
.
By Lemma
3.2
, the involution
[-1]
also preserves
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
.
These combine to a
\mu_{6}
-action
\gamma_{i}
on
X_{i}
for each

.
The next proposition shows that each
X_{i}
is a bielliptic Picard curve and the
\mu_{6}
-actions have ‘opposite’ signature.

Proposition 3.11
.

Reordering
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
if necessary, the pairs
(X_{1},\gamma_{1})
and
(X_{2},\gamma_{2}^{-1})
are marked bielliptic Picard curves.
Moreover, these pairs are defined over
k
.

Proof.

The second sentence follows from the first, since
{\rm Gal}_{k}
preserves the
\mu_{3}
-fixed points of
|\mathscr{L}_{k^{\sep}}|
and the signatures of the
\mu_{6}
-action.
To show that
(X_{1},\gamma_{1})
and
(X_{2},\gamma_{2})
are bielliptic Picard curves of opposite signature, we may assume that
k=k^{\sep}
.

Let
(X,\gamma)=(X_{i},\gamma_{i})
for some
i=1,2
.
Proposition
3.9
shows that
X
is smooth, and Proposition
3.4
shows that
(X,[-1]|_{X})
is a bielliptic genus 3 curve.
Since
\alpha^{2}+\alpha+1=0
, the
\mu_{3}
-action on

has isolated fixed points, so the induced
\mu_{3}
-action on

is faithful.
It follows that
\gamma
is a faithful bielliptic action and hence

is a bielliptic Picard curve. Note that

is the unique
\mu_{6}
-fixed point.

To prove the claim about signatures, it suffices to show that the
\mu_{3}
-action on
T_{0}X_{1}
and
T_{0}X_{2}
acts via inverse characters.
Since
\mathscr{L}
has self-intersection

has four base points (Lemma

),

and

intersect transversally at all these base points.
Since

is such a base point,

(3.2)

\displaystyle T_{0}A=T_{0}X_{1}\oplus T_{0}X_{2}.

Condition (2) of Definition

says that the

-action on

is a direct sum of the two nontrivial characters of

.
Since the decomposition (

) is

-stable, the proposition follows.
∎

Let
(C,\gamma)
be a marked bielliptic Picard curve over

\mu_{6}
-action on

\mu_{6}
-action on

\mu_{6}
-equivariant.
This induces
\mu_{3}
-actions

\hat{\alpha}
on
3
3

3

A calculation shows that the
\mu_{3}
-actions

\mu_{3}
-equivariant) and

A
.

Lemma 3.12
.

\Prym(C,\gamma):=(P,\lambda,\alpha)
and

are indecomposable
\mu_{3}
-abelian surfaces over

Proof.

The first condition of Definition
3.7

follows from Proposition
2.15
and the fact that
\hat{\alpha}
preserves

Moreover, since

is indecomposable.
The second condition for
\Prym(C,\gamma)

.
The lemma now follows from bigonal duality (Proposition

).
∎

The next theorem is a strong Torelli statement for the association

.
To state it, we upgrade the set of marked bielliptic Picard curves (respectively
\mu_{3}
-abelian surfaces) to a category, where the morphisms are isomorphisms of curves (respectively polarized abelian surfaces) respecting the
\mu_{6}
-structure (respectively
\mu_{3}
-structure).

Theorem 3.13
.

The association
(C,\gamma)\mapsto\Prym(C,\gamma)^{\vee}

induces an equivalence of categories:

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{Bmatrix}\text{Marked bielliptic}\\
\text{Picard curves over }k\end{Bmatrix}\rightarrow\begin{Bmatrix}\text{Indecomposable}\\
\mu_{3}\text{-abelian surfaces over }k\end{Bmatrix}.

This association is well defined by Lemma

It is functorial: an isomorphism of marked bielliptic Picard curves

To a triple

Since the constructions of §

3.4
.
∎

By Lemma

4.
Shimura curves and quaternionic multiplication

We relate the moduli space of bielliptic Picard curves to certain unitary and quaternionic Shimura curves, which will allow us to deduce that these bielliptic Picard Pryms surfaces
P
have quaternionic multiplication over
\overline{k}
(Corollary
4.8
).
This will also help us study which
P
are geometrically non-simple in §
6.4
, since these correspond to CM points on the Shimura curve side.

4.1.
The quaternion order
\mathcal{O}

Let
B
be the quaternion algebra
(-3,2)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
with
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-basis
\{1,i,j,ij\}
and relations
i^{2}=-3
,
j^{2}=2
,
ij=-ji
.
This is an indefinite quaternion algebra of discriminant
6
.
Let
\omega:=\frac{1}{2}(-1+i)
, so that
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
.
Let
\mathcal{O}:={\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\omega,j]={\rm\mathbb{Z}}+{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\omega+{\rm\mathbb{Z}}j+{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\omega j
.
A discriminant calculation shows that
\mathcal{O}
is a maximal order in
B
.
As an associative ring,
\mathcal{O}
has generators
\omega,j
and relations
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
,
j^{2}=2
and
j\omega=\omega^{-1}j
.

Let
{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{O})
denote the set of ring automorphisms of
\mathcal{O}
, which we will think of as acting on the right on
\mathcal{O}
.
By the Skolem–Noether theorem, every element of
{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{O})
is given by conjugating by an element of
b\in B^{\times}
(uniquely defined up to
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
) normalizing
\mathcal{O}
; denote the conjugation action of such an element
b
by
[b]\in{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{O})
.

For reasons that will become clear in §
6.2
, we define the groups

\displaystyle{\rm Aut}_{i}^{+}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle:=\{\varphi\in{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{O})\mid i^{\varphi}=i\},

\displaystyle{\rm Aut}_{i}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle:=\{\varphi\in{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{O})\mid i^{\varphi}=\pm i\}.

Lemma 4.1
.

{\rm Aut}_{i}^{+}(\mathcal{O})=\langle[1-\omega]\rangle
is cyclic of order
6
and
{\rm Aut}_{i}(\mathcal{O})=\langle[1-\omega],[j]\rangle
is dihedral of order
12
.

Proof.

A direct calculation shows that
\langle[1-\omega],[j]\rangle\subset{\rm Aut}_{i}(\mathcal{O})
, that this subgroup is dihedral of order
12
and that
\langle[1-\omega],[j]\rangle\cap{\rm Aut}_{i}^{+}(\mathcal{O})=\langle[1-\omega]\rangle
.
Conversely, suppose
[b]\in{\rm Aut}_{i}(\mathcal{O})
.
After possibly multplying by

Definition 4.6

Let

•

\psi(R_{1}+R_{2})
, where

is the unique effective divisor on

such that

.
We interpret this geometrically: let

be the line spanned by the points

in the embedding

The condition

implies that

denotes the bielliptic involution.
Combining the above two linear equivalences shows that

.
Since

are collinear and

.
In the coordinates

∎

6.1.
Explicit quaternionic multiplication

Let
(C,\gamma)=(C_{a,b},\gamma_{a,b})
be a marked bielliptic Picard curve over
k
.
Let
\delta=\Delta_{a,b}=16b(a^{2}-4b)
, which is nonzero since

is smooth.
Consider the
{\rm Gal}_{k}
-set:

(6.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:=\{(\zeta,\varepsilon)\in k^{\sep}\times k^{\sep}\mid\zeta\text{ is a primitive sixth root of unity and }\varepsilon^{6}=\delta\}.

Consider
\mu_{3}
-abelian surfaces
\Prym(C,\gamma)=(P,\lambda,\alpha)
and
\Prym(C,\gamma)^{\vee}=(A,\widehat{\lambda},\hat{\alpha})
.
For every
(\zeta,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{S}
, we will define elements
r_{\zeta},s_{\varepsilon}\in{\rm End}(P_{k^{\sep}})
.
Since
\zeta^{2}
is a third root of unity, we may set
r_{\zeta}:=\alpha(\zeta^{2})\in{\rm End}(P_{k^{\sep}})
.

To define
s_{\varepsilon}
, we use Corollary
5.5
.
By that corollary and Remark
5.7
, the specialization of the universal isomorphism
\Phi
at the
k

(a,b)\in S(k)
is an isomorphism
\phi\colon(A,\widehat{\lambda},\hat{\alpha}^{-1})\xrightarrow{\sim}(P,\lambda,\alpha)_{\delta^{-1}}
.
The element
\varepsilon
determines an isomorphism
C_{\delta^{-1},k^{\sep}}\rightarrow C_{k^{\sep}},(x,y)\mapsto(\varepsilon^{3}x,\varepsilon^{4}y)
, hence an isomorphism
\chi_{\varepsilon}\colon(P,\lambda,\alpha)_{\delta^{-1},{k^{\sep}}}\rightarrow(P,\lambda,\alpha)_{k^{\sep}}
between their Prym varieties.
Let
s_{\varepsilon}
be the composition:

(6.2)

\displaystyle P_{k^{\sep}}\xrightarrow{\lambda}A_{k^{\sep}}\xrightarrow{\phi}(P_{\delta^{-1}})_{k^{\sep}}\xrightarrow{\chi_{\varepsilon}}P_{k^{\sep}}.

Proposition 6.1
.

For every
x=(\zeta,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{S}
the assignment
\omega\mapsto r_{\zeta},j\mapsto s_{\varepsilon}
extends to an embedding

(6.3)

\displaystyle\iota_{x}\colon\mathcal{O}\rightarrow{\rm End}(P_{k^{\sep}})={\rm End}(P_{\bar{k}}).

Proof.

We may assume
k=k^{\sep}
.
Fix
x=(\zeta,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{S}
and write
r=r_{\zeta}
,
s=s_{\varepsilon}
.
We will show that
r^{2}+r+1=0
,
s^{2}=2
and
sr=r^{-1}s
.
Since the associative ring
\mathcal{O}
from §
4.1
is generated by
\omega,j
and satisfies the same three relations, this will imply that there exists a unique ring homomorphism
\iota_{x}\colon\mathcal{O}\rightarrow{\rm End}(P_{k^{\sep}})
with
\iota_{x}(\omega)=r
and
\iota_{x}(j)=s
.
Such a homomorphism is automatically an embedding since
\mathcal{O}\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
is a simple algebra.

Equation (
2.4
) of Lemma
2.14
shows that
r^{2}+r+1=0
.
To check that
sr=r^{-1}s
, note that the surfaces
P,P_{\delta^{-1}},A
have
\mu_{3}
-actions
\alpha,\alpha_{\delta^{-1}},\hat{\alpha}
respectively.
The morphisms
\lambda
and
\chi_{\varepsilon}
from (
6.2
) are equivariant with respect to these
\mu_{3}
-actions.
On the other hand,
\phi
intertwines the
\mu_{3}
-action on the domain with the
inverse
of the
\mu_{3}
-action on the target.
The same must therefore be true for their composition
s
, so
sr=r^{-1}s
.

It suffices to prove that
s^{2}=2
; we will use the results of §
4.3
to achieve this.
Write
\psi=\chi_{\varepsilon}\circ\phi
.
The morphism
\psi
is compatible with the polarizations since
\phi
and
\chi_{\varepsilon}
are, so
\psi^{\vee}\circ\lambda\circ\psi=\widehat{\lambda}
.
Therefore

(6.4)

\displaystyle s^{2}=\psi\lambda\psi\lambda=(\psi(\psi^{\vee})^{-1})(\psi^{\vee}\lambda\psi\lambda)=(\psi(\psi^{\vee})^{-1})(\widehat{\lambda}\lambda)=2(\psi(\psi^{\vee})^{-1}).

It follows that

if and only if
\psi^{\vee}=\psi
.
To prove the latter statement, we will first show that

The identity

\lambda\circ(\psi\circ\lambda\circ\psi^{\vee}-\widehat{\lambda})\circ\lambda=0
.
Since
\lambda
is an isogeny, the inner expression must be zero, so

\psi^{\vee}
is compatible with the polarizations.
Moreover,
\psi
intertwines the
\mu_{3}
-action
\hat{\alpha}
on

with the

of the
\mu_{3}
-action

on

Applying duality to this identity and using that

,

again intertwines the

-action with the inverse of the other.
We conclude that

To show that this automorphism is the identity, we will use that its construction can be carried out in families.
More precisely, let
R={\rm\mathbb{Z}}[1/6,a,b,\Delta_{a,b}^{-1}]
and

.
Equation (
2.1
) defines a universal marked bielliptic Picard curve
(\mathcal{C},\gamma)
over

with Prym variety

and dual

On the cover
\tilde{S}={\rm Spec}(\tilde{R})\rightarrow S
given by adjoining a sixth root of

with the property that
\Psi
specializes to
\psi
for all morphisms

and that
(\Psi^{\vee})^{-1}\circ\Psi\in{\rm Aut}_{\tilde{S}}(\mathcal{A},\lambda,\alpha)
.
Since the moduli stack of
(1,2)
-polarized abelian surfaces is separated and Deligne–Mumford,

is finite and unramified.
Since finite unramified morphisms are étale locally on the target disjoint unions of closed immersions
[
45
, Tag
04HJ
]
and the base
\tilde{S}
is connected, it suffices to show that

is the identity for a

.

The specialization

defines a
{\rm\mathbb{Q}}
-point
s_{0}
of
\tilde{S}
.
Theorem
3.13
and Lemma
2.10
shows that
\mathbf{Aut}((\mathcal{A},\lambda,\alpha)_{s_{0}})=\mu_{6}
.
Since
\mu_{6}({\rm\mathbb{Q}})=\{\pm 1\}
, either
(\Psi^{\vee})^{-1}\Psi=1
or
(\Psi^{\vee})^{-1}\Psi=-1
.
Assume for the sake of contradiction that
(\Psi^{\vee})^{-1}\Psi=-1
.
Then (
6.4
) shows that
s^{2}=-2
.
This implies that
r
and
s
determine an embedding of the
definite
quaternion algebra
(-3,-2)_{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}
into

for every specialization

.
By the classification of endomorphism algebras of complex abelian surfaces
[

is isogenous to the square of a CM elliptic curve.
This is a contradiction, since there are (uncountably) many specializations

for which

is simple, using Proposition

and CM theory for the quaternionic Shimura curve

.
We conclude that

Keep the notations of the previous section.

Let
{\rm Sym}(\mathcal{S})
be the group of bijections
\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}

{\rm Gal}_{k}

\mathcal{S}

acts simply transitively on

.

Recall the subgroup
{\rm Aut}_{i}(\mathcal{O})

.

Lemma 6.4
.

For all

\iota_{x^{g}}(b^{\varphi(g)})=\iota_{x}(b)

It suffices to prove the claimed identity when

Write

Suppose first that
g=\tau

If

If

\iota_{(\zeta^{-1},\varepsilon)}(j)=s_{\varepsilon}=\iota_{x}(j)

Suppose next that

If

\displaystyle\iota_{x^{g}}(b^{\varphi(g)})=\iota_{x^{g}}(j^{[1-\omega]})=\iota_{x^{g}}(-\omega^{-1}j)=-\iota_{(\zeta,\zeta^{-1}\varepsilon)}(\omega^{-1})\iota_{(\zeta,\zeta^{-1}\varepsilon)}(j)=-r_{\zeta}^{-1}s_{\zeta^{-1}\varepsilon}.

, so

P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
. The argument for
({\rm\mathbb{Z}}/2{\rm\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
is exactly as before so we omit it.

It remains to rule out
{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/6{\rm\mathbb{Z}}\subset P({\rm\mathbb{Q}})
.
Let
I\subset\mathcal{O}
be the maximal two-sided ideal in
\mathcal{O}
above
3
(which is unique since
\mathcal{O}
is ramified at
3
), so that
\mathcal{O}/I\simeq{\rm\mathbb{F}}_{9}
. Note that the completion
I\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{3}\subset\mathcal{O}\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
is generated by any element of
\mathcal{O}\otimes{\rm\mathbb{Z}}_{3}
of minimal positive valuation. Since
\mathfrak{a}_{3}\subset{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\sqrt{6}]
is generated by the element
3+\sqrt{6}
of norm
3
, and similarly
\mathfrak{p}\subset{\rm\mathbb{Z}}[\omega]
is generated by the element
1-\zeta
of norm
3
, we have
A[\mathfrak{a}_{3}]=A[I]=A[\mathfrak{p}]
, where
A[I]
is the subgroup of
A
killed by
\iota(I)
, where
\iota\colon\mathcal{O}\rightarrow{\rm End}(P_{\bar{{\rm\mathbb{Q}}}})
is a choice of embedding satisfying the conclusion of Theorem
6.5
. There is also an exact sequence

0\to P[\mathfrak{a}_{3}]\to P[3]\to P[\mathfrak{a}_{3}]\to 0

where the surjection is multiplication by
3+\sqrt{6}
. Thus, if
P[3]({\rm\mathbb{Q}})\neq 0
,
we must also have
P[{\rm\mathfrak{p}}]({\rm\mathbb{Q}})=P[\mathfrak{a}_{3}]({\rm\mathbb{Q}})\neq 0
. It follows that
P
is a specialization of the family in Proposition
9.2
. So it suffices to show that both
b_{6}(t)
and
16(a_{6}(t)^{2}-4b(t))
are never cubes. These two quantities turn out to be inverses of each other modulo cubes, so it is enough to show
b_{6}(t)
is never a cube.
More precisely, it is enough to show that the curve
Y^{\prime}\colon y^{3}=12t^{2}(3t^{2}+1)
has no rational points with
y\neq 0
. Using the double cover
Y^{\prime}\to X
where
X\colon y^{3}=12t(3t+1)
, which is (the affine part of) an elliptic curve
\overline{X}
with
\overline{X}\simeq{\rm\mathbb{Z}}/6{\rm\mathbb{Z}}
, we check that
Y^{\prime}({\rm\mathbb{Q}})=\{(0,0)\}
, which completes the proof.
∎
