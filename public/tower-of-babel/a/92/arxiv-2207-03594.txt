Counting rotational subsets of the circle ℝ / ℤ \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} under the angle-multiplying map t ↦ d ​ t t\mapsto dt
Yee Ern Tan

Abstract
A rotational set is a finite subset A A of the unit circle 𝕋 = ℝ / ℤ \mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} such that the angle-multiplying map σ d : t ↦ d ​ t \sigma_{d}:t\mapsto dt maps A A onto itself by a cyclic permutation of its elements. Each rotational set has a geometric rotation number p / q p/q . These sets were introduced by Lisa Goldberg to study the dynamics of complex polynomial maps. In this paper we provide a necessary and sufficient condition for a set to be σ d \sigma_{d} -rotational with rotation number p / q p/q . As applications of our condition, we recover two classical results and enumerate σ d \sigma_{d} -rotational sets with rotation number p / q p/q that consist of a given number of orbits.

Introduction and preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
be the unit circle parametrized by angle measured in revolutions. Impose a total order on
\mathbb{T}
by choosing representatives in
[0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
. That is, given
s,t\in\mathbb{T}
we say that
s<t
if and only if
s^{\prime}<t^{\prime}
where
s^{\prime},t^{\prime}\in[0,1)
correspond to the equivalence classes
s,t
respectively. Likewise we impose a total order on
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
by choosing representatives in
\{0,1,\dots,q-1
}. Fix an integer
d\geq 2
and define the angle-multiplying map
\sigma_{d}
:
\mathbb{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
with
\sigma_{d}(t)=dt,\forall t\in\mathbb{T}
. The
orbit
of an element
t\in\mathbb{T}
is the set
\{\sigma_{d}^{k}(t):k=0,1,2,\dots\}
.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
A=\{t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{q-1}\}
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{T}
indexed in increasing order, where indices are viewed as elements of
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. Then
A
is
\sigma_{d}
-
rotational
if for some fixed
0\neq p\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
we have
\sigma_{d}(t_{i})=t_{i+p}
for every
t_{i}\in A.
Each rotation set is assigned a
rotation number
, which is the rational number
p/q
whose expression may be further reducible. A set is
rotational
if it is
\sigma_{d}
-rotational for some
d\geq 2
.

Rotational sets were introduced by Goldberg in [2] to study and classify polynomial maps
\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
in the setting of complex dynamics [3]. Goldberg shows in [2] that rotational sets are uniquely determined by their rotation number and
deployment sequence
, which describes the placement of the set with respect to the fixed points of
\mathbb{T}
under
\sigma_{d}
. This allows for an enumeration of
\sigma_{d}
-rotational orbits with rotation number
p/q
. (Rotational sets are rotational orbits or the union of rotational orbits.) This is generalized in [6] by Petersen and Zakeri, who enumerate periodic orbits under
\sigma_{d}
with any
combinatorial type
. That is, periodic orbits which are generally permuted under
\sigma_{d}
are considered. We continue progress on the combinatorics of rotational sets by introducing a new characterization of rotational sets (Theorem
3.1
). Two results from [2] promptly follow:

1.

There are
{{d-2+q}\choose d-2}

\sigma_{d}
-rotational orbits with rotation number
p/q
(in lowest terms).

2.

A
\sigma_{d}
-rotational set contains at most
d-1
orbits.

In Theorem
3.2
we enumerate all
\sigma_{d}
-rotational sets with rotation number
p/q
. The proof is constructive in the sense that it suggests an algorithm for generating the rotational sets.

Both smaller orbits also have rotation number

.

A subset of

consists of

\{i,i+1,i+2,\dots\}

Proposition 1.1
.

Let

A=\{t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}<\dots<t_{q-1}\}

be a

\sigma_{d}
-
rotational set with rotation number
p/q
, that is,
\sigma_{d}(t_{i})=t_{i+p}
. Then

A

is the union of

n=\text{gcd}(p,q)

orbits. Each orbit in the union is

\sigma_{d}
-
rotational and has

rotation number

p/q
.
The orbits are interlaced, meaning, given any

n

consecutive elements of

A

no two elements come from the same orbit
.

Proof.

Use the correspondence
t_{i}\leftrightarrow i
for
t_{i}\in A
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
. Then the orbit of
t_{i}
corresponds to the coset
H+i
of the subgroup
H
generated by
p
. Working over the integers, the least
m
such that
mp\equiv 0
(mod
q
) is
m=q/n
. Thus
|H|=q/n
and the index of
H
in
\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
is
n
. Equivalently,
A
is the union of
n
orbits of size
m
.

For every
a\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
, the consecutive elements
a,a+1,\dots,a+n-1\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}
come from the consecutive cosets
H,H+1,\dots,H+n-1\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})/H
(perhaps not in the same order) which are pairwise disjoint; this proves the interlacing property. Relabel the elements of
A
by the rule
t_{j}^{(k)}=t_{jn+k-1}
, where
1\leq k\leq n
, so that

A=\{t_{0}^{(1)}<t_{0}^{(2)}<\dots<t_{0}^{(n)}<t_{1}^{(1)}<t_{1}^{(2)}<\dots<t_{1}^{(n)}<\cdots\cdots<t_{m-1}^{(n)}\}.

Applying the interlacing property to the collections

and
t_{0}^{(2)},\dots,t_{1}^{(1)}
we see that
t_{0}^{(1)}
and
t_{1}^{(1)}
come from the same orbit. More generally,
t_{i}^{(k)}
and
t_{j}^{(k)}
belong in the same orbit. Picking out an orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset A
, we have

\mathcal{O}=\{t_{0}^{(k)}<t_{1}^{(k)}<\dots<t_{m-1}^{(k)}\}

and setting

\sigma_{d}(t_{j}^{(k)})=\sigma_{d}(t_{jn+k-1})=t_{jn+k-1+p}=t_{(j+m^{\prime})n+k-1}=t_{j+m^{\prime}}^{(k)}.

Therefore
\mathcal{O}
is
\sigma_{d}
-rotational with rotation number
m^{\prime}/m=(p/n)/(q/n)=p/q
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

A
\sigma_{d}
-rotational set contains at most
d-1
orbits.

Proof.

Suppose that the union of
n
orbits with rotation number
p/q
is a
\sigma_{d^{-}}
rotational set. Then there exists a
(d-1)
-sequence with length
nq
which can be unlaced to give
n
distinct
(d-1)
-sequences with length
q
. Let
s^{\prime}=b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{nq-1}
be this
(d-1)
-sequence and let

s_{i}=b_{i},b_{i+n},b_{i+2n},\dots,b_{i+(q-1)n}\qquad\text{ for }0\leq i\leq n-1.

Since the
s_{i}
are distinct, adjacent sequences in
s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}
must differ by at least one term. Then going across
s^{\prime}
we should find at least
n-1
pairs of nonequal adjacent terms. But since
s^{\prime}
is a
(d-1)
-sequence (there are only
d-1
possibilities for the terms), there can be at most
d-2
such pairs. This shows that
n-1\leq d-2
or
n\leq d-1
.
∎

Let

We know

k\in\mathbb{Z}

2\leq k\leq d-1

can be identified by a

-sequence with length

(d-1)

kq

. To find
N_{k}
, we want to subtract from

(d-1)

Now, because

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
b=[b_{1},\dots,b_{m}]^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a column vector with real entries. The formulas

n_{1}=b_{1},\quad n_{k}=b_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{{k-1}\choose{j-1}}n_{j}\text{ for }2\leq k\leq m

(9)

and

n_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(-1)^{k+j}{{k-1}\choose{j-1}}b_{j}

(10)

define the same vector
n=[n_{1},\dots,n_{m}]^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
L=(a_{ij})
and
L^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}_{ij})
be the
m\times m
lower triangular matrices defined by
a_{ij}={{i-1}\choose{j-1}}
and
a^{\prime}_{ij}=(-1)^{i+j}{{i-1}\choose{j-1}}
. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}
the binomial theorem gives

\displaystyle L[1,x,\dots,x^{m-1}]^{t}

\displaystyle=[1,x+1,\dots,(x+1)^{m-1}]^{t},

\displaystyle L^{\prime}[1,x,\dots,x^{m-1}]^{t}

\displaystyle=[1,x-1,\dots,(x-1)^{m-1}]^{t}.

It follows that
L^{\prime}=L^{-1}
(by properties of the Vandermonde matrix). Formula (
9
) produces the solution
n

using forwards substitution, whereas (
10

n=L^{-1}b
.
∎
