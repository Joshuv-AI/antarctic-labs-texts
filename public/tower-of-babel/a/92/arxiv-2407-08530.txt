Lower tail Large Deviations of the Stochastic Six Vertex Model
Sayan Das, Yuchen Liao, Matteo Mucciconi

Abstract
. In this paper, we study lower tail probabilities of the height function 𝔥 ⁡ ( M , N ) \mathfrak{h}(M,N) of the stochastic six-vertex model. We introduce a novel combinatorial approach to demonstrate that the tail probabilities ℙ ⁡ ( 𝔥 ⁡ ( M , N ) ≥ r ) \mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)\geq r) are log-concave in a certain weak sense. We prove further that for each κ > 0 \kappa>0 the lower tail of − 𝔥 ⁡ ( ⌊ κ ​ N ⌋ , N ) -\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N) satisfies a Large Deviation Principle (LDP) with speed N 2 N^{2} and a rate function Φ κ ( − ) \Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)} , which is given by the infimal deconvolution between a certain energy integral and a parabola. Our analysis begins with a distributional identity from [ BO17 ] , which relates the lower tail of the height function, after a random shift, with a multiplicative functional of the Schur measure. Tools from potential theory allow us to extract the LDP for the shifted height function. We then use our weak log-concavity result, along with a deconvolution scheme from our earlier paper [ DLM25 ] , to convert the LDP for the shifted height function to the LDP for the stochastic six-vertex model height function.

The stochastic six-vertex model (S6V) was introduced in the physics literature by Gwa and Spohn in
[

[
Pau35
]

{\Lambda}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
that satisfies the following two properties:

•

Every path begins from
x
-axis or
y
-axis and leaves the co-ordinate axes immediately.

•

Paths do not share edges, but they may share vertices.

An example of an ensemble of admissible paths is shown in

\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{2}

The main observable of interest in the S6V model is the height function
\mathfrak{h}:\Lambda\to\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
defined as follows:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{h}(M,N):=\mbox{number of paths that pass through or to the right of }(M,N).

For the step initial condition, we have
\mathfrak{h}(M,N)\leq N
since there is no path coming from the horizontal axis.

It was predicted in
[
GS92
]
and later proven by Borodin, Corwin, and Gorin
[
BCG16
]
that the model belongs to the so-called Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) universality class – a class of models that exhibit universal scaling exponents and limiting
statistics first discovered in random matrix theory
[
Qua13
,
Cor12
,
Zyg18
,
Gan21
]
.
[
BCG16
]
showed that the height function satisfies the following convergence in probability:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)}{N}=\gamma_{\kappa}:=\begin{cases}1-\kappa,&\kappa\in(0,a),\\
\frac{(1-\sqrt{a\kappa})^{2}}{1-a}&\kappa\in(a,a^{-1}),\\
0&\kappa\in(a^{-1},\infty).\end{cases}

The above convergence was upgraded to almost sure convergence in
[
DL23
]
. The choice of parameter
\kappa\in(a,a^{-1})
is known as the
liquid region
where the limit shape is curved and there
[
BCG16
]
proved that the height function has Tracy-Widom GUE fluctuations (with a negative sign) of order
N^{1/3}
. The complementary situations when parameter
\kappa\leq a
and
\kappa\geq a^{-1}
correspond to the
frozen region
where the height function is flat and one expects the fluctuations to be exponentially small. Since the work
[
BCG16
]
, there has been immense progress in understanding various aspects of this model. Connections to determinantal point processes
[
Bor18
,
BO17
]
, limit shape and fluctuation theorems
[
Agg18
,
Agg20
,
CGST20
,
Dim23
]
, and boundary-induced phase transitions
[
AB19
]
have been established. In a very recent breakthrough
[
ACH24
]
, the S6V model has been shown to converge to the directed landscape
[
DOV22
]
, a universal scaling limit in the KPZ universality class.

The present paper focuses on the study of large deviations of the S6V model: the rare events where the height function

\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)
deviates by an order
N
from its mean
\gamma_{\kappa}N
. Interestingly, we expect different speeds for the upper and lower deviations. As
N\to\infty
we expect

(Upper Tail)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)\leq sN)\asymp e^{-N\Phi_{\kappa}^{(+)}(s)}

\displaystyle s\in[0,\gamma_{\kappa}],

(Lower Tail)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)\geq sN)\asymp e^{-N^{2}\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}(s)}

\displaystyle s\in[\gamma_{\kappa},1].

Since
-\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)
converges to the Tracy-Widom GUE after centering and scaling, we refer to
\{\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)\leq sN\}
as the upper tail and
\{\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)\geq sN\}
as the lower tail, aligning with the existing literature. The asymmetry above in the speeds,
N
versus
N^{2}
, is a hallmark of the KPZ universality class. Heuristically, this can be understood from the path configurations of the S6V model. To achieve a large height at a given point, all paths need to stay low, whereas a small height can be attained if the lowest path trends upward.

We focus on the lower tail large deviations which is arguably more challenging and often requires more involved machineries. Our first main result in this direction shows that the lower-tail probabilities satisfy certain weak log-concavity.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix any
M,N\geq 8
and
r_{1},r_{2}\geq 0
. Then, we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)\geq r_{1}\big)\mathbb{P}\big(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)\geq r_{2}\big)\leq N^{2}\mathsf{C}^{(MN)^{4/5}}\left[\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)\geq\frac{r_{1}+r_{2}}{2}-4(MN)^{2/5}\Big)\right]^{2},

where
\mathsf{C}:=a^{-2}q^{-2}(1-a)^{-2}(1-q)^{-2}(1-aq)^{4}
.

The exponents
4/5
and
2/5
are not optimal. Note that in the lower-tail large deviation regime, i.e., when
M=\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor
and
r_{1},r_{2}>\gamma_{\kappa}N
, the probabilities appearing above are of order
e^{-O(N^{2})}
and thus the prefactor
N^{2}\mathsf{C}^{(MN)^{4/5}}
is negligible.

Theorem

1.1
is a key ingredient in proving a lower tail Large Deviation Principle (LDP) for the stochastic six-vertex model stated below.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
s\in[\gamma_{\kappa},1]
we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)\geq sN)=\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}(s).

\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}
is a non-decreasing, non-negative convex function with
\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}(\gamma_{\kappa})=0
and
\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}(1)=\kappa\log\frac{1-aq}{1-a}
. It has the following variational form

(1.4)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}(s):=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\left[\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y)-(\log q^{-1})\frac{(s-y)^{2}}{2}\right]

where
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y)
is a certain energy integral explained in
Section

1.1.1

While we do not address the upper tail LDP in this paper, it should be achievable using a perturbative analysis similar to
[
DT21
,
Lin21
,
DZ22
,
DLM25
]
, leveraging the Fredholm determinant formula available for the S6V model
[
Bor18
,
BO17
]
.

The lower tail case, on the other hand, is more subtle; perturbative analysis of the Fredholm determinant is no longer feasible. In this context, different techniques using connections to determinantal point processes have proven fruitful. This connection goes back to the work of Borodin and Olshanski
[
BO17
]
, where they observed that the
q
-Laplace transform of the height function of the S6V model can be viewed as an expectation of a certain multiplicative functional under Schur measures. See also
[
Bor18
]
for more general moment matching formulas between higher-spin S6V models and Macdonald measures.
It was first noted in
[
CG20b
]
that it is worthwhile to analyze such multiplicative functionals to obtain lower tail estimates of the observables. This approach was carried out in
[
CG20b
]
for the KPZ equation introduced in
[
KPZ86
]
, where the
q
-Laplace transform becomes the usual Laplace transform and the underlying point process is the
\mathrm{Airy}_{2}
point process. Such analysis is by no means trivial, and a host of sophisticated techniques, ranging from the analysis of the stochastic Airy operator to Riemann-Hilbert problems, have been developed to analyze the underlying functional of the
\mathrm{Airy}_{2}
point process
[
Tsa22
,
CC22
]
.

The approach we take in the current paper is also based on the connection from
[
Bor18
,
BO17
]
where the underlying point process is the Meixner ensemble. While the applicability of the aforementioned techniques to the Meixner ensemble is yet to be explored, the analysis of S6V model presents another challenge due to the presence of the
q
-Laplace transform instead of the usual Laplace transform. Loosely speaking, the
q
-Laplace transform of the height function observable is related to the lower tail probability of a randomly shifted height function. The shift has Gaussian tails and has a non-trivial effect in the rate function. In essence, the lower-tail rate functions of the shifted and unshifted height function
are not the same
(unlike in the case of the KPZ equation).

For the
q
-PNG model, where one faces similar challenges, we proposed a strategy in our earlier work
[
DLM25
]
to negate the effect of this shift. This approach crucially uses the fact that the tail probabilities of the
q
-PNG height function (after a shift) are log-concave, stemming from the log-concavity of the Schur polynomials
[
Oko97
,
LPP07
]
. In the present paper the same idea cannot be applied. In fact, while there still exists a precise connection between S6V and periodic Schur measures, guaranteed by a result of
[
IMS22
]
, the latter in this instance are not non-negative. Therefore their log-concavity cannot be leveraged to deduce probabilistic properties of the S6V height function.

To bypass this problem we devise an alternative approach, that proves
Theorem

1.1
without relying on special algebraic structures which are consequence of the integrability of the S6V. The proof, which is outlined in
Section

1.2.1
and fully fleshed out in
Section

2
, can be thought as a multi-dimensional variant of the Gessel-Viennot involution
[
GV85
]
, which is an elegant and simple operation commonly seen in algebraic combinatorics. Despite the inspiration coming from algebraic combinatorics, our approach primarily depends on the Gibbsian structure of the growth process. It is amenable to broad generalizations, including Gibbsian growth processes of a more general nature in arbitrary dimensions – ideas which we hope to pursue further in future works.

As already pointed out the bound (
1.2
) is not optimal since it includes (a) an exponentially diverging factor in the right hand side and (b) a sublinearly diverging shift in the argument of the right hand side probability. A valid question, to which we have no answer, is if such diverging terms can be abated or removed all together. Such a question connects the world of integrable probability with that of the raising trend in algebraic combinatorics of studying log-concavity of algebraic objects such as multiplicities of representations or special symmetric polynomials
[
Oko97
,
Oko03
,
HMMSD22
]
. In fact, it is known
[
BBW18
]
that certain multi-point distributions of the height function of the S6V is described by the Hall-Littlewood polynomials, which are one parameter deformations of the Schur polynomials. A pure log-concavity result for the tails of the height function
\mathfrak{h}
, would correspond to a log-concavity result for the Hall-Littlewood polynomials, which indeed appears as a challenging though enticing direction to pursue (see also
[
BG15
]
for similar considerations).

Soon after our work,
[
BKM24
]
proved several log-concavity results for a class of one dimensional log-gases and of Tracy-Widom type laws (see also
[
BLS17
]
). Although there is no overlap between results of this paper and
[
BKM24
]
, the construction we present could be adapted, in the case
q=0
, to give an alternative construction of some of their results, including log concavity of Tracy Widom distributions.

Recently, a variety of probabilistic techniques have emerged, successfully yielding lower tail estimates in the moderate deviations (MD) regime for models within the KPZ class
[

[

Techniques we develop in this paper could, in principle, lead to establish large deviation principle for the Asymmetric Simple Exclusion Process (ASEP), which arises as a scaling limit of the S6V. Under such scaling limit the connection between the height function of the ASEP and a multiplicative functional of a determinantal point process survives, the latter being the discrete Laguerre ensemble, which is a certain limit of the Meixner ensemble at the hard edge. For this, two approaches appear to be natural: constructing a coupling between the S6V and the ASEP strong enough to capture rare events or alternatively study the large deviations of the discrete Laguerre ensemble. Each of these approaches present issues one needs to resolve. Couplings between S6V and ASEP which have been produced in literature (such as
[
Agg16

ACH24

), while useful to control fluctuations, do not seem to be suitable to control rare events. Thus some of their modifications should be considered. On the other hand, establishing a large deviation principle of the discrete Laguerre ensemble, which is not a log gas, appears to require the use of more sophisticated techniques, e.g., the Riemann-Hilbert techniques, instead of simpler Laplace-type arguments. We leave this for future work.

1.1.1.
Description of the rate function

To describe
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
appearing in (
1.4
), we need some basic notions and results from potential theory. For context and significance on potential theory, we refer to the excellent monograph of Saff and Totik
[
ST97
]
.

Given a continuous function
V:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
(often called external field or potential) satisfying
V(x)\geq 2\log(1+x^{2})
for large enough
x
, and a probability measure on
\mu\in(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}))
, we define the logarithmic energy integral

(1.5)

\displaystyle I_{V}(\mu):=\iint_{x\neq y}k_{V}(x,y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(y),\qquad k_{V}(x,y):=-\log|x-y|+\frac{1}{2}V(x)+\frac{1}{2}V(y).

For
s\in(0,\infty]
, let
\mathcal{A}_{s}
denote the set of all
\phi\in L^{1}([0,s])
satisfying

(1.6)

\displaystyle 0\leq\phi(x)\leq 1\mbox{ for Lebesgue a.e.}~x\in[0,s],\mbox{ and }\int_{0}^{s}\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1.

Given a
\phi\in\mathcal{A}_{s}
, we will denote
\mu_{\phi}\in([0,s],\mathcal{B}([0,s]))
to be the probability measure with density
\phi
.

One of the fundamental problem of potential theory is to minimize the energy integral over all possible measures. We quote a classical result from
[
DS97
]
in this direction: There exists a unique
\phi_{V}\in\mathcal{A}_{\infty}
with compact support such that

(1.7)

\displaystyle\inf_{\phi\in\mathcal{A}_{\infty}}I_{V}(\mu_{\phi})=I_{V}(\mu_{\phi_{V}})=:F_{V}\in\mathbb{R}.

\mu_{\phi_{V}}
and
F_{V}
are known as the equilibrium measure and the equilibrium energy for the potential
V
.

We now describe
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
appearing in (
1.4
). For
y\in(-\infty,\infty]
and
\kappa\geq 1
we define the external field:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\mathpzc{V}_{y}(x)

\displaystyle=(\log a^{-1})x+(\log q^{-1})\min(x,y)-(\kappa-1)[\log(x+\kappa-1)-1]+x\log\frac{x}{x+\kappa-1}.

We have suppressed the dependency on
\kappa
above. For
\kappa\geq 1
, define

(1.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y):=(\log q^{-1})\frac{y^{2}-2y}{2}-\kappa\log(1-a)+\frac{\log(a)}{2}+C_{\kappa}+F_{\kappa}(y),

where
F_{\kappa}(y):=F_{\mathpzc{V}_{y}}
comes from (
1.7
) (with
V\mapsto\mathpzc{V}_{y}
) and

(1.10)

\displaystyle C_{\kappa}:=-\frac{(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)-\kappa^{2}\log(\kappa)+3\kappa}{2}.

When
\kappa\in(0,1)
define

(1.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y)

\displaystyle:=(\log q^{-1})\frac{y^{2}-2y\kappa+2\kappa-2\kappa^{2}}{2}-(\log q^{-1})\int_{\kappa}^{1}(y+x-1)_{+}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle-\kappa\log(1-a)+\kappa^{2}\frac{\log(a)}{2}+\kappa^{2}C_{\kappa^{-1}}+\kappa^{2}F_{\kappa^{-1}}(y\kappa^{-1}-\kappa^{-1}+1).

The following theorem summarizes the properties of
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

The function
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
is a non-decreasing, non-negative, convex, and continuously differentiable function with derivative being
(\log q^{-1})
-Lipschitz. We have
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(\gamma_{\kappa})=0
and there exists
x_{0}(a,q,\kappa)>0
such that for all
x\geq x_{0}

(1.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(x)=(\log q^{-1})\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{2}+\kappa\log\frac{1-aq}{1-a}.

1.2.1.
Log concavity of lower-tail probabilities

The proof of
Theorem

1.1
relies on an injective argument. We prove that, given a pair of height functions
h_{1},h_{2}
associated with stochastic six vertex models with step boundary conditions and such that
h_{1}(M,N)=r

\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2}

\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}-(MN)^{2/5}\leq\widetilde{h}_{1}(M,N),\widetilde{h}_{2}(M,N)\leq\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}+(MN)^{2/5}

(1.14)

\prod_{v=1}^{2}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(i,j)=h_{v}(i,j);(i,j)\in\Lambda_{M,N})\leq\mathsf{C}^{(MN)^{4/5}}\prod_{v=1}^{2}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(i,j)=\widetilde{h}_{v}(i,j);(i,j)\in\Lambda_{M,N}).

(h_{1},h_{2})\to(\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2})

\tau_{k}(h_{1})

\tau_{k}(h_{1})(M,N)=r+k

which possesses a connected component (in the topology induced by the Manhattan metric) containing

which we denote by

, this final operation defines

(\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2})=\Upsilon_{(M,N)}^{k}(h_{1},h_{2}),\qquad\text{with}\qquad\Upsilon_{(M,N)}^{k}=(\tau_{-k}\times\mathrm{Id})\circ\iota_{(M,N)}\circ(\tau_{k}\times\mathrm{Id}).

We can finally transform the height configurations back to paths configurations as done in
Figure

3
(e).

The sequence of transformations described above is not necessarily weight preserving, in the sense that it is not true that the weights corresponding to
\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2}
are just a permutation of those corresponding to
h_{1},h_{2}
. Nevertheless, it is not hard to show that differences between weights corresponding to pairs
h_{1},h_{2}
and
\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2}
are only possible along the boundaries of the region
R_{M,N}(\tau_{k}(h_{1})<h_{2})
. One can be convinced of this claim after checking in
Figure

3
. This is the point where we choose our shifting index
k
: we pick
k=k^{*}_{M,N}(h_{1},h_{2})
to be the minimal number greater than
\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}
such that the length of the boundary of
R_{M,N}(\tau_{k}(h_{1})<h_{2})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}
does not exceed
(MN)^{4/5}
. A pigeonhole principle argument (see the proof of Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
for the details) shows that such choice of
k
will satisfy the bound

\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil\leq k^{*}_{M,N}(h_{1},h_{2})\leq\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil+(MN)^{2/5},

guaranteeing that the values of the transformed heights
\widetilde{h}_{1},\widetilde{h}_{2}
satisfy the desired inequalities (
1.13
) and simultaneously (
1.14
). Here the factor
\mathsf{C}^{(MN)^{4/5}}
appears to bound the maximal difference in weights which the injection produces. Since the choice of
k
forces the number of vertices where such weight mismatch can occur to be less than
(MN)^{4/5}
, the exponent is also justified.

1.2.2.
Lower tail LDP

The starting point of our proof of
Theorem

1.2
is an identity from
[
Bor18
,
BO17
]
which, in view of an identity from
[
IMS24
]
, can be read as follows:

(1.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)-\chi-S\geq sN\big)=\left[\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+q^{j-sN}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\Big(1+q^{\lambda_{N-j}+j-sN}\Big)\right].

Here
\chi+S
is a random shift independent of
\mathfrak{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
with an explicit distribution (given in (
4.5
)) and in the right-hand side
\lambda
is distributed as a Schur measure with a certain positive specialization (see (
3.2
)). Since the work of
[
Joh00b
]
, it has been known that such Schur measures can be viewed as
discrete log-gases
. Discrete and continuous log-gases, on the other hand, are extensively studied in random matrix theory and beyond (see the books
[
AGZ10
,
Dei00
,
For10
,
Meh04
,
PS11
]
for a review), and have a close connection with potential theory. This connection allows one to derive large deviations for the empirical measure
\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\delta\big((\lambda_{N-j}+j)/N\big)
. Using a Varadhan’s lemma type argument from this point, the asymptotics of the right-hand side of (
1.15
) can be determined. This eventually leads to

(1.16)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)-\chi-S\geq sN)=\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(s),

where
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(s)
is given in (
1.9
) and (
1.11
). The details of the derivation of (
1.16
) are presented in
Section

3.1
.

To prove (
1.3
) from here, we need to negate the effect of the shift
\chi+S
. It turns out that the lower tail of
\chi+S
is Gaussian, with
\mathbb{P}(\chi+S\leq-yN)\asymp e^{-N^{2}g(y)}
where
g(y):=\big(\log q^{-1}\big)y^{2}/2
, and hence the presence of this random shift has a non-trivial effect on the limit (
1.16
). Indeed, if we define

\Phi_{\kappa,N}(y)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(\lfloor\kappa N\rfloor,N)\geq yN),

we can deduce (
Proposition

4.3
in the text) that

(1.17)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\{g(x)+\Phi_{\kappa,N}(y-x)\}.

To show that
\Phi_{\kappa,N}
has a limit, we use a deconvolution scheme from
[
DLM25
]
which relies on two key components:

•

The rate function
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y)
possesses, for
y
large, an explicit parabolic behavior
.

•

The pre-limit rate function
\Phi_{\kappa,N}
is approximately convex
, as implied by
Theorem

1.1
.

In order to establish the precise behaviour at large
y
of the rate function
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(y)
, we show that, for all large enough
y
, we have
F_{\kappa}(y)=F_{\kappa}(\infty)
, where
F_{\kappa}
is the energy integral defined below (
1.9
), and then compute
F_{\kappa}(\infty)
explicitly. This relies heavily on potential theory, and we refer the readers to
Section

3.2
for details. A result of such computation is that the exact expression in (
1.12
) in fact is equal to
g(y-1)+\Phi_{\kappa,N}(1)
. This fact is quite important; as in view of (
1.17
), it implies an approximate continuity of
\Phi_{\kappa,N}
at
1
:

\displaystyle\mbox{For any $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $\delta>0$ (free of $N$) such that }\Phi_{\kappa,N}(1)-\Phi_{\kappa,N}(1-\delta)\leq\varepsilon\mbox{ for all large }N.

Approximate convexity of
\Phi_{\kappa,N}
then allows us to upgrade the above approximate continuity to equicontinuity of
\{\Phi_{\kappa,N}\}_{N}
and hence
\Phi_{\kappa,N}
has a limit, by the Ascoli-Arzela theorem. Finally, any such limit can be expressed uniquely, using its convexity and properties of the Legendre-Fenchel transform, as the infimal deconvolution between
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
and the parabola
g
, as in (
1.4
), hence proving that it is unique. The details of this procedure are given in
Proposition

4.5
.

We mention that our deconvolution scheme bears resemblance to Seppäläinen’s deconvolution idea, used in proving the upper-tail LDP for the polynuclear growth (PNG) model
[
Sep98c
]
. Seppäläinen’s proof was based on a coupling between the PNG model and a suitable particle system that admits certain known stationary initial conditions. This coupling provides a variational relation between the upper-tail rate function of the PNG model and the stationary model (similar to (
1.17
)). The rate function in the stationary model can be computed explicitly. Using the variational relation, one then derives an explicit form for the rate function in the original model.

The coupling approach has been fruitful in obtaining upper-tail LDPs for various other models in the KPZ universality class
[
Sep98b
,
GS13
,
Jan15
,
EJ17
,
CG18
,
Jan19
]
. We stress that the existence of the upper-tail rate function in these models can be deduced from a standard subadditive argument
[
Kes86
]
. Thus, it is the explicit form of the upper-tail rate function that is of interest in these models. In our case, however, the existence of the rate function is non-trivial. Due to the lack of an explicit form for
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}
, we currently do not have an explicit form for
\Phi_{\kappa}^{(-)}
.

2.
Log-concavity bounds for the lower tail probabilities

The goal of this section is to deduce log-concavity bounds for the tail probabilities of the S6V model. As explained in the introduction, the proof relies on an injective argument that heavily utilizes surface geometry of the height function of the S6V model. In
Section

2.1

Section

2.2
, using the maps and the geometry, we prove
Theorem

1.1
.

2.1.
Surface geometry of height function

In this section, we introduce a space
\mathscr{H}
for the height functions of the S6V model and discuss some geometric properties of the space
\mathscr{H}
.

Towards this end, we define the dual lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}:=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\prime}
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{\prime}_{\geq 0}=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}+\frac{1}{2}
, which we view as the set of faces of
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{2}
; see
Figure

4
.
For every vertex
v\in\Lambda
its adjacent faces are defined as

\mathsf{NW}(v)
\mathsf{NE}(v)
\mathsf{SW}(v)
\mathsf{SE}(v)
v

(2.1)

\begin{split}\mathsf{NW}(v)=v-\frac{\mathbb{e}_{1}}{2}+\frac{\mathbb{e}_{2}}{2},\qquad&\mathsf{NE}(v)=v+\frac{\mathbb{e}_{1}}{2}+\frac{\mathbb{e}_{2}}{2}\\
\mathsf{SW}(v)=v-\frac{\mathbb{e}_{1}}{2}-\frac{\mathbb{e}_{2}}{2},\qquad&\mathsf{SE}(v)=v+\frac{\mathbb{e}_{1}}{2}-\frac{\mathbb{e}_{2}}{2}.\end{split}

For any region
R\subset\Lambda^{\prime}
, we define its boundary
\partial R\subset\Lambda
as the set of vertices
v\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}^{2}
adjacent to both faces in
R
and in
R^{\mathrm{c}}
; see
Figure

4
.

For a function
h:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\mathbb{Z}
we introduce the discrete gradients

\nabla_{1}h(p)\coloneqq h(p+\mathbb{e}_{1})-h(p),\qquad\nabla_{2}h(p)\coloneqq h(p+\mathbb{e}_{2})-h(p)

and we define the collection

(2.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{h:\Lambda^{\prime}\to\mathbb{Z}:\nabla_{1}h(p)\in\{0,-1\},\,\nabla_{2}h(p)\in\{0,1\}\Big\}.

\mathscr{H}
is the set of all possible stochastic six vertex model height functions. We also define

(2.3)

\mathscr{H}_{0}:=\left\{h\in\mathscr{H}:h(m,1/2)=0,\,h(1/2,m)=m-1/2,\,\text{for all }m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{\prime}\right\}

to denote the set of stochastic six vertex model height functions obtained from step initial condition.

In our arguments, we shall typically consider two height functions
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
simultaneously which would often require us to consider regions where one of the heights dominates the other. We introduce these regions next and then derive some of their geometric properties.

Definition 2.1
.

Given two height functions
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
we define the region of
\Lambda^{\prime}

R(h<h^{\prime})=\left\{p\in\Lambda^{\prime}:h(p)<h^{\prime}(p)\right\}.

In general the region
R(h<h^{\prime})
is the disjoint union of connected components, in the topology of
\Lambda^{\prime}
induced by the nearest neighbor metric. For any
p\in\Lambda^{\prime}
we will denote the connected component of
R(h<h^{\prime})
containing
p
by
R_{p}(h<h^{\prime})
. In case
p\notin R(h<h^{\prime})
, by agreement we set
R_{p}(h<h^{\prime})=\varnothing
. Similarly, we define

R(h\neq h^{\prime})=R(h<h^{\prime})\cup R(h^{\prime}<h)

and we denote by
R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})
the connected component of
R(h\neq h^{\prime})
containing the face
p
.

The following proposition states a simple property of the boundary of the regions
R(h<h^{\prime})
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Fix
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
. If
p,p^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}
are adjacent faces such that
p\in R(h<h^{\prime})
and
p^{\prime}\notin R(h<h^{\prime})
, then
h^{\prime}(p)=h(p)+1
and
h^{\prime}(p^{\prime})=h(p^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Consider the case
p^{\prime}=p+\mathbb{e}_{1}
. Calling
r=h(p)
and
r^{\prime}=h^{\prime}(p)
, then we have
h(p^{\prime})
is either
r
or
r-1
and
h^{\prime}(p^{\prime})
is either
r^{\prime}
or
r^{\prime}-1
as depicted below

By
Proposition

2.2
we know that, white faces
p^{\prime}
adjacent to gray faces are such that
h(p^{\prime})=h^{\prime}(p^{\prime})
, while gray faces
p
adjacent to white faces are such that
h^{\prime}(p)=h(p)+1
. This shows that in all cases (
2.4
), (
2.5
) none of the faces adjacent to
v
will belong to
R(\tau_{1}(h)<h^{\prime})
. On the other hand if faces configurations fall into case (
2.6
), then, after lifting up by 1 height
h
, around vertex
v
there could still be a single face of
R(\tau_{1}(h)<h^{\prime})
, as in (
2.4
). Then, lifting further
h
, we have that
R(\tau_{2}(h)<h^{\prime})
cannot intersect any of the vertices adjacent to
v
. Thus,
v\notin\partial R(\tau_{k}(h)<h^{\prime})
for all
k\geq 2
. This completes the proof.
∎

As a consequence of the above lemma, we observe that
\{\partial R(\tau_{2k}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\}_{k\geq 0}
is a disjoint family of sets. By a pigeonhole principle argument, this will allow us to obtain
k^{*}
where
|\partial R(\tau_{2k^{*}}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\cap([0,M]\times[0,N])|
is of smaller order than
MN
. We record a version of this consequence in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix any
p\in\Lambda^{\prime}
and
M,N\geq 8
and
r<r^{\prime}
. For any pair of height functions
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
with
h(M,N)=r
,
h^{\prime}(M,N)=r^{\prime}
, define

(2.7)

k^{*}_{M,N}(p;h,h^{\prime})=\min\left\{k\geq\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil\,:\,\left|\partial R_{p}(\tau_{k}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|\leq(MN)^{4/5}\right\},

where
\Lambda_{M,N}:=\left\{1,\dots,M\right\}\times\left\{1,\dots,N\right\}
.
Then, we have

(2.8)

\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil\leq k^{*}_{M,N}(p;h,h^{\prime})\leq\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil+(MN)^{2/5}.

Proof.

For any
p\in\Lambda^{\prime}
, pairs of height functions
h,h^{\prime}
, and for
i\in\{0,1\}
, the sets
\left\{\partial R_{p}(\tau_{2k+i}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}

form a disjoint family, by
Lemma

2.3
. Thus,

\sum_{k=\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil}^{\left\lceil\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\right\rceil+(MN)^{2/5}}\left|\partial R_{p}(\tau_{k}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|\leq 2|\Lambda_{M,N}|\leq 2MN.

Then, whenever
(MN)^{\frac{4}{5}+\frac{2}{5}-1}=(MN)^{\frac{1}{5}}>2
, which is guaranteed by our choice of
M,N
, we can be sure that not all of the
(MN)^{2/5}
terms in the above summation can be greater than
(MN)^{4/5}
, by a straightforward counting argument. This proves the bound (
2.8
).
∎

Definition 2.5

(Involution of surfaces)
.

For
p\in\Lambda^{\prime}
and two height functions
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
, we define

\iota_{p}(h,h^{\prime}):=(\overline{h},\overline{h}^{\prime})\in\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H},

where

(2.9)

(\overline{h}(z),\overline{h}^{\prime}(z)):=\begin{cases}(h^{\prime}(z),h(z))\qquad&\text{if }z\in R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})\\
(h(z),h^{\prime}(z))\qquad&\text{else}.\end{cases}

If
h(p)=h^{\prime}(p)
we set
\iota_{p}(h,h^{\prime}):=(h,h^{\prime})
.

Note that
(\overline{h},\overline{h}^{\prime})
, defined via (
2.9
), indeed lies in
\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H}
as on the faces that are not in
R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})
but are adjacent to some face in
R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})
,
h
and
h^{\prime}
are equal. In words, the
\iota_{p}
swaps the height function
h
and
h^{\prime}
in the region
R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})
. Clearly, it is an involution.

Definition 2.6

(Bijective contraction maps)
.

For
p\in\Lambda^{\prime}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and two height functions
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
, we define the map
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}:\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H}\to\mathscr{H}\times\mathscr{H}
as

\Upsilon_{p}^{k}:=(\tau_{-k}\times\mathrm{Id})\circ\iota_{p}\circ(\tau_{k}\times\mathrm{Id}),

where
(\tau_{k}\times\mathrm{Id})(h,h^{\prime})=(\tau_{k}(h),h^{\prime})
.

As
\iota_{p}
is an involution, it is clear that for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
the map
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
is a bijection on the space of pairs of height functions with inverse given by
\Upsilon_{p}^{-k}
. A closer look to the map
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
shows that, calling
(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime})=\Upsilon_{p}^{k}(h,h^{\prime})
, we have

(2.10)

\widetilde{h}(p)=h^{\prime}(p)-k,\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(p)=h(p)+k\mbox{ whenever }h^{\prime}(p)\geq h(p)+k.

Thus whenever
h^{\prime}(p)
and
h(p)
are far apart, the
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
map brings the heights at
p
closer. For this reason, we call it a
contraction
map.
A further property of the map
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
stating that it preserves the step initial condition, which will be instrumental to establish convexity properties of the law of the six vertex model height function, is stated next.

Proposition 2.7
.

Fix
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
(recall the definition from (
2.3
)) and
p\in\Lambda^{\prime}
. Suppose
h(p)=r
,
h^{\prime}(p)=r^{\prime}
with
r^{\prime}>r
. Then for all
k\in\{0,\dots,r^{\prime}-r\}
,
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
.

Proof.

Consider the left and bottom boundary of the lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}

B_{\llcorner}=\left\{(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\in\Lambda^{\prime}:\min(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})=1/2\right\}.

In case
k\in\{0,\dots,r^{\prime}-r\}
we have
h(p)+k=r+k\leq r^{\prime}=h^{\prime}(p).

On the other hand for each boundary face
v\in B_{\llcorner}
we have
h(v)+k\geq h^{\prime}(v)
. Therefore
R_{p}(\tau_{k}(h)\neq h^{\prime})
has empty intersection with the boundary
B_{\llcorner}
and calling
(\overline{h},\overline{h}^{\prime})=\iota_{p}(\tau_{k}(h),h^{\prime})
we have

\overline{h}(p)=h(p)+k,\qquad\overline{h}^{\prime}(p)=h^{\prime}(p)\qquad\text{for all }p\in B_{\llcorner}

and as a result for
(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime})=(\tau_{-k}(\overline{h}),\overline{h}^{\prime})
we have

\widetilde{h}(p)=h(p)\qquad\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(p)=h^{\prime}(p)\qquad\text{for all }p\in B_{\llcorner}.

Therefore boundary conditions of
h,h^{\prime},\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime}
are the same, completing the proof.
∎

In words,
Proposition

2.7
allows us to transform a pair
(h,h^{\prime})
of height functions in
\mathscr{H}_{0}
which assume values far apart at a fixed point
p
into a pair
(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime})
, where both heights still lie in
\mathscr{H}_{0}
, but their value at
p
is closer by
2k
as prescribed by (
2.10
).

2.2.
Log-concavity bounds

In this section, we prove weak log-concavity bounds for the tail probabilities of the stochastic six vertex model. Towards this end, we first view the S6V height function
\mathfrak{h}
as a function on the faces by setting

\mathfrak{h}(p):=\mathfrak{h}(p-\mathbb{e}_{1}/2-\mathbb{e}_{2}/2)

for all
v\in\Lambda^{\prime}
where the right-hand side is defined via (
1.1
). The Markov evolution rules for vertex configurations defining the S6V model can clearly be phrased in terms of height functions, as shown by
Figure

1
. Recall the notation of adjacent faces from (
2.1
). Given a height function
h\in\mathscr{H}
and any vertex
v\in\Lambda
we introduce the notation

w_{v}(h)=w\left(-\nabla_{1}h(\mathsf{SW}(v)),\nabla_{2}h(\mathsf{SW}(v));-\nabla_{1}h(\mathsf{NW}(v)),\nabla_{2}h(\mathsf{SE}(v))\right),

where
w(i_{1},j_{1};i_{2},j_{2})
are vertex weights from
Figure

1
.
w_{v}(h)
is precisely the stochastic weight of the vertex
v
corresponding to a given height function
h
. With a slight abuse of notation, for any finite set of vertices
V\subset\Lambda
we define

w_{V}(h)=\prod_{v\in V}w_{v}(h).

We shall call
w_{V}(h)
the Boltzmann weight of the height
h
restricted to
V
. With this notation in place, it is easy to see that for all
h\in\mathscr{H}_{0}
we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathfrak{h}(v)=h(v)\mbox{ for all }v\in\Lambda_{M,N}\big)=w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h).

Our next two results shows certain weak log-concavity of the Boltzmann weight under the
\iota_{p}
and
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
map defined in Definition
2.5
and
2.6
respectively.
Towards this end, we define the constant

(2.12)

\mathsf{C}:=a^{-2}q^{-2}(1-a)^{-2}(1-q)^{-2}(1-aq)^{4},

where
a,q
are the parameters of the S6V model. This constant has the property that

(2.13)

\frac{w_{v_{1}}(h_{1})w_{v_{2}}(h_{2})}{w_{v_{3}}(h_{3})w_{v_{4}}(h_{4})}\leq\frac{1}{w_{v_{3}}(h_{3})w_{v_{4}}(h_{4})}\leq\mathsf{C}

for all
v_{i}\in\Lambda^{\prime}
and
h_{i}\in\mathscr{H}
which can be readily checked.

Proposition 2.8

(Weak log-concavity under
\iota_{p}
map)
.

Fix
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
p\in\Lambda_{M,N}
. Consider
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
and let
(\overline{h},\overline{h}^{\prime})=\iota_{p}(h,h^{\prime})
. Then

w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h)w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{C}^{\#\left|\partial R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|}w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(\overline{h})w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(\overline{h}^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let
v\in\Lambda
be a vertex and let
\mathsf{SW}(v),\mathsf{SE}(v),\mathsf{NW}(v),\mathsf{NE}(v)\in\Lambda^{\prime}
be the quadruple of faces adjacent to
v
. To lighten the notation, we write
R_{p}
to mean
R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})
. If
\mathsf{SW}(v),\mathsf{SE}(v),\mathsf{NW}(v),\mathsf{NE}(v)
all belong to
R_{p}^{\mathrm{c}}
we have, by construction

h(p)=\overline{h}(p),\qquad h^{\prime}(p)=\overline{h}^{\prime}(p),\qquad\text{for all }p\in\{\mathsf{SW}(v),\mathsf{SE}(v),\mathsf{NW}(v),\mathsf{NE}(v)\},

which implies that
w_{v}(h)=w_{v}(\overline{h})
and
w_{v}(h^{\prime})=w_{v}(\overline{h}^{\prime}).

Similarly, if
\mathsf{SW}(v),\mathsf{SE}(v),\mathsf{NW}(v),\mathsf{NE}(v)
all belong to
R_{p}
we have, by construction

h(p)=\overline{h}^{\prime}(p),\qquad h^{\prime}(p)=\overline{h}(p),\qquad\text{for all }p\in\{\mathsf{SW}(v),\mathsf{SE}(v),\mathsf{NW}(v),\mathsf{NE}(v)\},

which implies that
w_{v}(h)=w_{v}(\overline{h}^{\prime})
and
w_{v}(h^{\prime})=w_{v}(\overline{h})
.
This shows that

w_{v}(h)w_{v}(h^{\prime})=w_{v}(\overline{h})w_{v}(\overline{h}^{\prime}),\qquad\text{if }v\notin\partial R_{p}

and hence

(2.14)

w_{\Lambda_{M,N}\setminus\partial R_{p}}(h)w_{\Lambda_{M,N}\setminus\partial R_{p}}(h^{\prime})=w_{\Lambda_{M,N}\setminus\partial R_{p}}(\overline{h})w_{\Lambda_{M,N}\setminus\partial R_{p}}(\overline{h}^{\prime}).

On the other hand, when
v\in\partial R_{p}(h\neq h^{\prime})
, in general
w_{v}(h)w_{v}(h^{\prime})\neq w_{v}(\overline{h})w_{v}(\overline{h}^{\prime})
. However, due to the property (
2.13
) we have

\frac{w_{v}(h)w_{v}(h^{\prime})}{w_{v}(\overline{h})w_{v}(\overline{h}^{\prime})}\leq\mathsf{C}.

As a result we have

(2.15)

\begin{split}&w_{\partial R_{p}\cap\Lambda_{M,N}}(h)w_{\partial R_{p}\cap\Lambda_{M,N}}(h^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{C}^{\#\left|\partial R_{p}\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|}w_{\partial R_{p}\cap\Lambda_{M,N}}(\overline{h})w_{\partial R_{p}\cap\Lambda_{M,N}}(\overline{h}^{\prime}).\end{split}

Combining (
2.14
) and (
2.15
) completes the proof.
∎

We can easily upgrade the above proposition to
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
maps defined in Definition
2.6
.

Proposition 2.9
.

Fix
M,N\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and
p\in\Lambda_{M,N}
. Consider
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathscr{H}
and let
(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime})=\Upsilon_{p}^{k}(h,h^{\prime})
. Then

w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h)w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{C}^{\#\left|\partial R_{p}(\tau_{k}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|}w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(\widetilde{h})w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(\widetilde{h}^{\prime}),

where the constant
\mathsf{C}
is defined in (
2.12
).

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of
Proposition

2.8
after noticing that for every height
h\in\mathscr{H}
and any finite set
A
, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
w_{A}(h)=w_{A}(\tau_{k}(h))
.
∎

Since the above proposition holds for all
k
, using Lemma
2.4
, we may choose a
k
for which
\#\left|\partial R_{p}(\tau_{k}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|
is of smaller order than
MN
. This will allow us to deduce a density version of
Theorem

1.1
.

Proposition 2.10
.

Fix
M,N\geq 8
and
r,r^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then, we have

(2.16)

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)=r)\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)=r^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{C}^{(MN)^{4/5}}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathfrak{h}(M,N)-\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}\right|\leq 4(MN)^{2/5}\bigg|\right)^{2},\end{split}

where the coefficient
\mathsf{C}
is given in (
2.12
).

Proof of
Proposition

2.10
.

Without loss of generality, assume
r<r^{\prime}
.
When
\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}\leq 4(MN)^{2/5}
,
the event

\left\{\left|\mathfrak{h}(M,N)-\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}\right|\leq 4(MN)^{2/5}\right\},

contains both the events
\{\mathfrak{h}(M,N)=r\}
and
\{\mathfrak{h}(M,N)=r^{\prime}\}
. Hence (
2.16
) is trivial. So, let us assume
\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}>4(MN)^{2/5}
. Set
p=(M,N)\in\Lambda
and consider the set

T_{p;r,r^{\prime}}:=\big\{(h,h^{\prime})\in\mathscr{H}_{0}\times\mathscr{H}_{0}:h(p)=r,h^{\prime}(p)=r^{\prime}\big\}.

Using (
2.11
), we may write the left-hand side of (
2.16
) as follows.

(2.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)=r)\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{h}(M,N)=r^{\prime})=\sum_{(h,h^{\prime})\in T_{p;r,r^{\prime}}}w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h)w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h^{\prime}).

Consider the
\Upsilon_{p}^{k^{*}}
map restricted to
T_{p;r,r^{\prime}}
with
k^{*}=k_{M,N}^{*}(p;h,h^{\prime})
coming from (
2.7
). Since
\frac{r^{\prime}-r}{2}>4(MN)^{2/5}
, by the bound on
k^{*}
from (
2.8
), we have that
k^{*}\leq r^{\prime}-r=h^{\prime}(p)-h(p)
for all
(h,h^{\prime})\in T_{p;r,r^{\prime}}
. Hence the condition in (
2.10
) holds. By (
2.10
) and the bound on
k^{*}
from (
2.8
) we have that

(2.18)

\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}-4(MN)^{2/5}\leq\widetilde{h}(p),\widetilde{h}^{\prime}(p)\leq\frac{r+r^{\prime}}{2}+4(MN)^{2/5}

for all
(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime})\in\Upsilon_{p}^{k^{*}}(T_{p;r,r^{\prime}})
. Moreover, by definition of
k^{*}=k_{M,N}^{*}(p;h,h^{\prime})
from (
2.7
) we have

\#\left|\partial R_{p}(\tau_{k^{*}}(h)\neq h^{\prime})\cap\Lambda_{M,N}\right|\leq(MN)^{4/5},

which shows, using
Proposition

2.9
, that

(2.19)

w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h)w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(h^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{C}^{(MN)^{4/5}}w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(\widetilde{h}^{\prime})w_{\Lambda_{M,N}}(\widetilde{h}^{\prime}).

We claim that
\Upsilon_{p}^{k^{*}}
is injective when restricted to
T_{p;r,r^{\prime}}
(note that
k^{*}
depends on
(h,h^{\prime})
). Indeed, if
(\widetilde{h},\widetilde{h}^{\prime})=\Upsilon_{p}^{k_{1}^{*}}(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})=\Upsilon_{p}^{k_{2}^{*}}(h_{2},h_{2}^{\prime})
, we have
\widetilde{h}(p)=h_{1}^{\prime}(p)-k_{1}^{*}=h_{2}^{\prime}(p)-k_{2}^{*}
. But on
T_{p;r,r^{\prime}}
,
h_{1}^{\prime}(p)=h_{2}^{\prime}(p)=r^{\prime}
. Thus
k_{1}^{*}=k_{2}^{*}
. Now injectivity of
\Upsilon_{p}^{k}
for fixed
k
leads to
(h_{1},h_{1}^{\prime})=(h_{2},h_{2}^{\prime})
. Thus,

(2.20)

3.
Large Deviation Principle for multiplicative expectation of the Schur measure

In this section, we prove the lower-tail LDP for the shifted height function and discuss some properties of the rate function

3.1.
Existence of lower tail rate function
\mathcal{F}_{\kappa}(s)

We introduce a few notations to explain the moment matching formula from
[
Bor18
]
.

A partition
\lambda
with at most
n
non-zero elements is a decreasing sequence of non-negative integers
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}\geq 0
and
\lambda_{n+1}=\lambda_{n+2}=\cdots=0

and denote the set of all such partitions by
\mathbb{Y}^{n}
. For later purposes, we also introduce the set

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}^{n}:=\left\{\ell=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n})\mid\exists\ \lambda\in\mathbb{Y}^{n},\mbox{ such that }\ell_{i}=\lambda_{i}+n-i\mbox{ for all }i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\right\}.

Given

,
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}

-measures are probability measures on

defined through

where

are Schur polynomials (c.f.
[
Mac95
]
) and in particular we have

s_{\lambda}(1^{K})=s_{\lambda}(1,1,\ldots,1)=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq K}\frac{\lambda_{i}-i-\lambda_{j}+j}{j-i}.

z
-measures were first used in the context of harmonic
analysis on the infinite symmetric group in
[
KOV93
]
. They are a special case of Schur measures introduced in
[
Oko01
]
and are related to Meixner ensembles (see
[
Joh00b
]
for more details).

It was observed by Borodin
[

that the

-Laplace transform of the height function of S6V coincides with the expectation of a certain multiplicative functional of the

-measures. We recall this result as follows.

(Proposition 8.4 in
[

]

Let

and recall the height function

We shall see in

The above formula is our starting point for the asymptotics of

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{+}_{a,b,1,1;2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\log(a+b)-\frac{\pi b}{2(a+b)}
,

(5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{c,d;1}=\frac{\pi}{2cd}
,

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{1,1;2}=\frac{\pi}{4}
.

Computation of log integral.

Let us start with computing

U(y):=-\int_{c}^{d}\log|x-y|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
-\log|y-x|=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}((y-x)\log|y-x|+x)
and

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}\phi(x)=\frac{x(p-1)(\kappa+1)+(\kappa p-1)(\kappa-1)}{2\pi x(x+\kappa-1)\sqrt{4xp(x+\kappa-1)-(x(p+1)+\kappa p-1)^{2}}}.

An integration by parts gives

U(y)=\left((y-x)\log|y-x|+x\right)\phi(x)\Big|_{x=c}^{d}-\int_{c}^{d}\left((y-x)\log|y-x|+x\right)\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
4xp(x+\kappa-1)-(x(p+1)+\kappa p-1)^{2}=(1-p)^{2}(d-x)(x-c)
. Under the change of variable
x=\frac{c+dz^{2}}{1+z^{2}}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}\left((y-x)\log|y-x|+x\right)\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi(1-p)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z\left(\left(y-\frac{c+dz^{2}}{1+z^{2}}\right)\log\left|y-\frac{c+dz^{2}}{1+z^{2}}\right|+\frac{c+dz^{2}}{1+z^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(\frac{\kappa p-1}{c+dz^{2}}+\frac{p-\kappa}{(c+\kappa-1)+(d+\kappa-1)z^{2}}\right).

Assume first that
y>d
or
y<c
. From the decomposition

y-\frac{c+dz^{2}}{1+z^{2}}=\frac{y-c+(y-d)z^{2}}{1+z^{2}}=y+\kappa-1-\frac{(c+\kappa-1)+(d+\kappa-1)z^{2}}{1+z^{2}},

we have

\displaystyle\pi(1-p)\int_{c}^{d}\left((y-x)\log|y-x|+x\right)\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(\kappa p-1)\cdot y\left(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{|y-c|},\sqrt{|y-d|},\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d};1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d};1}\right)

\displaystyle+(p-\kappa)\cdot(y+\kappa-1)\left(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{|y-c|},\sqrt{|y-d|},\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1};1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1};1}\right)

\displaystyle-(\kappa+1)(p-1)\cdot\left(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{|y-c|},\sqrt{|y-d|},1,1;1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;1}\right)

\displaystyle+(\kappa+1)(p-1)\mathcal{J}_{1,1;1}-(p-\kappa)(\kappa-1)\mathcal{J}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1};1}.

Using the identites from Lemma
B.1
, we conclude that for
y\leq c
or
y\geq d

(B.2)

\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}\left((y-x)\log|y-x|+x\right)\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=y\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(\kappa p-1)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{|y-c|}\cdot|\sqrt{\kappa p}+1|+\sqrt{|y-d|}\cdot|\sqrt{\kappa p}-1|}{|\sqrt{\kappa p}+1|+|\sqrt{\kappa p}-1|}\right)

\displaystyle-(y+\kappa-1)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{|y-c|}\cdot(\sqrt{\kappa}+\sqrt{p}))+\sqrt{|y-d|}\cdot(\sqrt{\kappa}-\sqrt{p})}{2\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)

\displaystyle+(\kappa+1)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{|y-c|}+\sqrt{|y-d|}}{2}\right)-1.

On the other hand, for
c<y<d
, applying Lemma
B.1
with
\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{y-c},{\sqrt{y-d}},*,*;1}
replaced by
\mathcal{I}^{-}_{\sqrt{y-c},{\sqrt{d-y}},*,*;1}
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}\left((y-x)\log|y-x|+x\right)\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=y\cdot\mathrm{sgn}(\kappa p-1)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{4y\sqrt{\kappa p}}}{|\sqrt{\kappa p}+1|+|\sqrt{\kappa p}-1|}\right)

\displaystyle-(y+\kappa-1)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{4(y+\kappa-1)\sqrt{\kappa p}}}{2\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)+(\kappa+1)\cdot\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{d-c}}{2}\right)-1.

Computation of potential integral.
We now compute
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{d}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. A similar integration by parts yields that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{d}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{3(\kappa-1)}{2}+\frac{\log p}{2}\int_{c}^{d}x^{2}\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{2}\int_{c}^{d}x^{2}\log x\cdot\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{c}^{d}(x+\kappa-1)^{2}\log(x+\kappa-1)\cdot\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)}{2}\mathbf{1}_{\kappa p\leq 1}.

Applying the change of variable
\displaystyle x=\frac{c+dz^{2}}{1+z^{2}}
again we have

\displaystyle\pi(1-p)\int_{c}^{d}x^{2}\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(\kappa p-1)\cdot\left(d\mathcal{J}_{1,1;1}+(c-d)\mathcal{J}_{1,1;2}\right)

\displaystyle+(p-\kappa)\cdot\left((\kappa-1)^{2}\mathcal{J}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1};1}+(d-\kappa+1)\mathcal{J}_{1,1;1}+(c-d)\mathcal{J}_{1,1;2}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi(-2\kappa p+p-1).

Similarly

\displaystyle\pi(1-p)\int_{c}^{d}x^{2}\log x\cdot\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(\kappa p-1)\cdot\left(d(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d},1,1;1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;1})+(c-d)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d},1,1;2}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;2})\right)

\displaystyle+(p-\kappa)\cdot(\kappa-1)^{2}\cdot(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d},\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1};1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1};1})

\displaystyle+(p-\kappa)\left((d-\kappa+1)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d},1,1;1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;1})+(c-d)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d},1,1;2}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;2})\right),

and

\displaystyle\pi(1-p)\int_{c}^{d}(x+\kappa-1)^{2}\log(x+\kappa-1)\cdot\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=(\kappa p-1)\cdot\left((d+2\kappa-2)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1},1,1;1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;1})+(c-d)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1},1,1;2}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;2})\right)

\displaystyle+(\kappa p-1)\cdot(\kappa-1)^{2}\cdot(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1},\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d};1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,\sqrt{c},\sqrt{d};1})

\displaystyle+(p-\kappa)\left((d+\kappa-1)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1},1,1;1}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;1})+(c-d)(\mathcal{I}^{+}_{\sqrt{c+\kappa-1},\sqrt{d+\kappa-1},1,1;2}-\mathcal{I}^{+}_{1,1,1,1;2})\right).

Combining all the terms together and using Lemma
B.1
we conclude that

(B.3)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{d}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=(\kappa-1)\log(1-p)+(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)-\kappa^{2}\log\kappa+\kappa\log\kappa+2\kappa-1

for
\kappa p>1
, and

(B.4)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{d}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\kappa^{2}-1\right)(p-1)\log(1-p)+(2\kappa p-p+1)\log(p)}{2(p-1)}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\kappa-1)(-\kappa p+(\kappa-1)(p-1)\log(\kappa-1)+2p-3)+\kappa\log(\kappa)(\kappa-\kappa p+2p)}{2(p-1)}

for
\kappa p\leq 1
.

Verification of the variational conditions.

Now we are ready to check (
3.28
) and (
3.29
). First we claim that for any
y\in(c,d)
we have (for either
\kappa p\geq 1
or
\kappa p<1
)

(B.5)

-\int_{0}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{V(y)}{2}=-\frac{\kappa\log\kappa+\log p}{2}+\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\log(1-p)+\frac{\kappa+1}{2}.

Indeed for
\kappa p\geq 1
, since
\phi(x)\equiv 0
for
x\in(0,c)
we have for any given
y\in(c,d)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{V(y)}{2}=U(y)+\frac{V(y)}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}y\log y+\frac{1}{4}y\log\kappa p+\frac{1}{2}(y+\kappa-1)\log(y+\kappa-1)+\frac{1}{4}(y+\kappa-1)\log\frac{p}{\kappa}

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\log\frac{\sqrt{\kappa p}}{1-p}+\frac{1}{2}y\log p^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}y\log y-\frac{1}{2}(y+\kappa-1)\log(y+\kappa-1)+\frac{\kappa-1}{2}+1

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa\log\kappa+\log p}{2}+\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\log(1-p)+\frac{\kappa+1}{2}.

On the other hand, if
\kappa p<1
then
\phi(x)\equiv 1
for
x\in(0,c)
and we have for any given
y\in(c,d)

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{V(y)}{2}=-\int_{0}^{c}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+U(y)+\frac{V(y)}{2}

\displaystyle=-y\log y-\int_{c}^{d}\left((y-x)\log(y-x)+x\right)\phi^{\prime}(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{V(y)}{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}y\log y+\frac{1}{4}y\log\kappa p+\frac{1}{2}(y+\kappa-1)\log(y+\kappa-1)+\frac{1}{4}(y+\kappa-1)\log\frac{p}{\kappa}

\displaystyle-\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\log\frac{\sqrt{\kappa p}}{1-p}+\frac{1}{2}y\log p^{-1}+\frac{1}{2}y\log y-\frac{1}{2}(y+\kappa-1)\log(y+\kappa-1)+\frac{\kappa-1}{2}+1

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa\log\kappa+\log p}{2}+\frac{\kappa+1}{2}\log(1-p)+\frac{\kappa+1}{2}.

Now for
\kappa p>1
note that

\int_{c}^{d}k(x,y)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=\int_{c}^{d}\frac{1}{2}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{c}^{d}-\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}V(y),

which is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)-\kappa^{2}\log\kappa+\kappa\log p+3\kappa}{2}+\kappa\log(1-p)-\frac{\log p}{2}

for
y\in(c,d)
, by (
B.5
) and (
B.3
). Moreover from (
B.2
) it is not hard to check that

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{V(y)}{2},

as a function of
y
, is increasing for
y\in(d,\infty)
and decreasing for
y\in(0,c)
if
\kappa p>1
, These complete the verification of (
3.28
). (
3.29
) is verified in a similar way using (
B.4
) instead of (
B.3
), note that for
\kappa p\leq 1
, the function
\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{V(y)}{2}
is increasing in
y
for
y\in(0,c)
.

Computation of the logarithmic energy.

Finally we compute the logarithmic energy

F_{\kappa}(\infty):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}k(x,y)\phi(x)\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

For
\kappa>1/p
, using to (
B.5
) and (
B.3
) we have

\displaystyle F_{\kappa}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{d}\int_{c}^{d}k(x,y)\phi(x)\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{d}\frac{1}{2}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{c}^{d}\left(-\int_{c}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}V(y)\right)\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)-\kappa^{2}\log\kappa+3\kappa}{2}+\kappa\log(1-p)-\frac{\log p}{2}.

The computation for
\kappa\leq 1/p
is a bit more complicated. In this case we have

\displaystyle F_{\kappa}(\infty)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{d}\int_{0}^{d}k(x,y)\phi(x)\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{d}\frac{1}{2}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{c}^{d}\left(-\int_{0}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\frac{1}{2}V(y)\right)\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{c}\left(-\int_{c}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y-\int_{0}^{c}\int_{0}^{c}\log|y-x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{c}V(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

A direct computation gives

(B.6)

\int_{0}^{c}\int_{0}^{c}\log|y-x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=-\frac{3}{2}c^{2}+c^{2}\log c,

and

(B.7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{c}V(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{c^{2}\log\frac{c}{p}-(c+\kappa-1)^{2}\log(c+\kappa-1)+(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)+3c(\kappa-1)}{2}.

To compute
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{c}\left(-\int_{c}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y
we use Fubini’s theorem:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{c}\left(-\int_{c}^{d}\log|y-x|\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{d}\left(\int_{0}^{c}-\log|y-x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{c}^{d}\left((x-c)\log(x-c)-x\log x+c\right)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

The last integral is computed in a very similar manner as
\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}V(x)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
so we only record the result here and skip the details:

(B.8)

\displaystyle\int_{c}^{d}\left((x-c)\log(x-c)-x\log x+c\right)\phi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{p\left(6\kappa+\kappa^{2}p-4\kappa\sqrt{\kappa p}-4\sqrt{\kappa p}+p\right)\log\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa p}}{1-p}\right)-3\left(\sqrt{\kappa p}-1\right)^{4}-3(p-1)\left(\sqrt{\kappa p}-1\right)^{2}}{2(p-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\kappa(\kappa+1)(p-1)p-(\kappa+1)(p-1)\sqrt{\kappa p}+3\left(\sqrt{\kappa p}-1\right)^{4}\log\left(1-\sqrt{\kappa p}\right)}{2(p-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\left(\kappa-2\sqrt{\kappa p}+p\right)^{2}\log\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{p}{\kappa}}\right)-\left(\kappa(p-1)(\kappa(p-1)+2)+(1-\sqrt{p\kappa})^{4}\right)\log(1-p)}{2(p-1)^{2}}.

Combining (
B.4
),(
B.5
),(
B.6
),(
B.7
) and (
B.8
), after some simplifications we arrive at

\int_{0}^{d}\int_{0}^{d}k(x,y)\phi(x)\phi(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{(\kappa-1)^{2}\log(\kappa-1)-\kappa^{2}\log\kappa+3\kappa}{2}+\kappa\log(1-p)-\frac{\log p}{2},

for
\kappa<1/p
as well. This completes the verification of (
3.30
).
