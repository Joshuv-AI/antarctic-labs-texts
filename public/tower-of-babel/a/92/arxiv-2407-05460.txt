Basins of Attraction in Two-Player Random Ordinal Potential Games
Andrea Collevecchio 1, Hlafo Alfie Mimun 2, Matteo Quattropani 3, Marco Scarsini 2

Abstract
. We consider the class of two-person ordinal potential games where each player has the same number of actions K K . It is well-known that each game in this class admits at least one PNE and the BRD converges to one of these PNE ; which one depends on the starting point. So, each PNE has a BoA . We pick uniformly at random one game from this class and we study the joint distribution of the sizes of the basins of attraction. We provide an asymptotic exact value for the expected BoA of each PNE , when the number of actions K K goes to infinity. Key words. Best-response dynamics, games with random payoffs, ordinal potential games, common interest games, pure Nash equilibria, discrete random structures. MSC2020 Classification : Primary 91A14, 91A05. Secondary: 91A26, 60C05.



1.
Introduction

There exists a huge literature dealing with dynamics in games and their ability to reach a
PNE
(
PNE
).
Various impossibility results have been proved, showing that no simple adaptive dynamics can reach a
PNE
in every game that has one.
See, for instance, the papers in the collection by
Hart and Mas-Colell, (2013)
and the more recent articles by
Milionis et al., (2023)
,
Hakim et al., (2024)
, among many others.
To determine the actual relevance of these impossibility results,
Johnston et al., (2023)
considered the class of ordinal games with fixed number of players and fixed action sets for each player and looked at the frequency of games for which adaptive dynamics fail to converge to a Nash equilibrium even if it exists.
They proved that this frequency vanishes as the number of players diverges, provided the number of actions for each player do not grow too fast.
Although their results are fully combinatorial, they can be framed within the literature of games with random payoffs.
Most papers about games with random payoffs focus on the number of
PNE
and its distribution.
More recently, the attention has shifted to the behavior of adaptive dynamics in games with random payoffs.
Our paper can be seen as part of this stream of literature, in the sense that we consider games with random payoffs and we study
BRD
(
BRD
) for a class of such games.
In particular, we focus on ordinal potential games.
It is well known that all games in this class admit
PNE
and
BRD
converges to one of these equilibria.
The issue we want to study in this paper is to which equilibria is a
BRD
more likely to converge.

1.1.
Our contribution

In this paper we consider a particular class of two-player normal-form games, called
OPG
.
These games always admit
PNE
, and the common learning dynamics converge to a
PNE
.
In this class of games, all the strategic features of the game are embedded in the potential function, i.e., it is sufficient to know the potential function to pinpoint the
PNE
of an
OPG
.
Therefore, if we want to pick an
OPG
at random, we can put a suitable probability measure on the space of potentials.
For the sake of simplicity, we will assume that the two players have the same number of actions
K
.
We will make the strong assumption that all outcomes in the potential are strictly comparable, i.e., no equivalences are allowed.
With this assumption, a random
OPG
is equivalent to a random permutation on of the first
K^{2}
integers.

The structure of the game implies that, for a given realization of the potential, if we start at a fixed action profile and we perform
BRD
starting with the first player (i.e., the player choosing the row), then we reach a
PNE
.

The goal of this paper is to determine the probability of reaching the
PNE
with the smallest or second smallest or third smallest potential, etc., when also the starting action profile is chosen uniformly at random.
This translates into finding the expected size of the basin of attraction of each
PNE
of the game.
Solving this problem exactly for a specific choice of
K
is a quite complicate endeavor.
We adopt an asymptotic approach and study the problem as
K\to\infty
.
We use a result of
Mimun et al., (2024)
according to which the fraction of the number of
PNE
over
K
converges to
1/2
.
Based on this, we see that the distribution of the rescaled ranking of
PNE
found by the
BRD
converges to an absolutely continuous distribution supported on the interval
[0,1/2]
, which we characterize explicitly.

1.2.
Related literature

As mentioned before, most of the initial contributions on random games, starting with
Goldman, (1957)
, assumed i.i.d. payoffs with a continuous distribution, and studied the existence of
PNE
and the distribution of their random number.
Two papers proved that, under the above hypotheses, the number of
PNE
converges in distribution to a Poisson distribution with parameter
1
as either the number of players diverges
(
Arratia et al.,, 1989
)
or the number of actions for each player diverges
(
Powers,, 1990
)
.

Rinott and Scarsini, (2000)
retained the assumption of independence for the random payoff vectors corresponding to different action profiles, but allowed for dependence of payoffs within the same action profile (modeled by an exchangeable multinormal distribution).
They proved an interesting phase transition in the correlation coefficient as either the number of players or the number of actions diverges.
When correlation is negative, the number of
PNE
converges to
0
, when the correlation is null, it converges to
\Poisson(1)
, when the correlation is positive, it diverges and a central limit theorem holds.

More recently, several papers started studying the behavior of
BRD
in games with random payoffs.

Amiet et al., 2021b ()

studied games with many players and two actions for each player.
They assumed i.i.d. payoffs, but allowed distributions to have atoms.
They proved that the number of
PNE
diverges exponentially fast in a way that depends on the parameter that corresponds to the size of the atoms.
Moreover, they studied the behavior of
BRD
and proved some phase transitions in the above parameter.
To achieve their results, they used some percolation techniques.
Their results were recently generalized by
Collevecchio et al., (2024)
.

Amiet et al., 2021a ()
compared the asymptotic behavior of
BRD
and
bRD
for two-player random games with i.i.d. payoffs with a continuous distribution.

Heinrich et al., (2023)
studied the role of the playing sequence in the convergence to equilibrium of
BRD
.

Johnston et al., (2023)
studied the model of
Amiet et al., 2021b ()
with no atoms and more than two actions per player, and allow this number to grow, although at a lower rate than the number of players.
For an extensive list of contributions on random games, we refer to the papers by
Amiet et al., 2021b ()
and
Johnston et al., (2023)
.

Pradelski and Tarbush, (2024)
introduced the concept of satisficing equilibria, which corresponds to the idea that players play one of their
k
best pure actions, not necessarily the best.
The proved that in almost all games, there exist satisficing equilibria in which all but one player use best response and the remaining player plays at least a second-best action.
Various aspects of games with random payoffs were recently studied in
Hart et al., (2008)
,
Holroyd et al., (2019)
,
Alon et al., (2021)
,
Wiese and Heinrich, (2022)
,
Pei and Takahashi, (2023)
,
Flesch et al., (2023)
,
Garnier and Ziliotto, (2023)
,
Bhasin et al., (2023)
,
Newton and Sawa, (2024)
,
Sepúlveda and Ziliotto, (2024)
.

In a paper that, similarly to ours, focuses on the quality of different equilibria,
Canyakmaz et al., (2024)
studied techniques that can be used to steer players towards one of several equilibria in a game in some specific class, e.g., coordination games.
Their approach does not consider games with random payoffs.

Hakim et al., (2024)
studied
the problem of computing the asymptotic behavior of noisy replicator dynamics as a limit distribution
over the sink equilibria of a game.
The authors generated games with random utilities to carry out randomized experiments of running noisy replicator dynamics.

Kiatsupaibul et al., (2024)
studied a variation of fictitious play called Monte Carlo fictitious play (MCFP) and proved that, with the aid of an auxiliary game, it converges to a
PNE
in games of common interest.

The problem of learning equilibria in repeated games with a large number of actions was studied by
Chen et al., (2024)
in the setting of AI Safety via Debate.

The idea of ordinal games goes back at least to
Fishburn, (1978)
, who used the term “stochastic dominance games” to identify them.
They were then studied by several authors
(
Cruz and Simaan,, 2000
,
Bonanno,, 2008
,
Gafarov and Salcedo,, 2015
, see, e.g., )
.
The computational complexity of their equilibria was recently studied by
Conitzer, (2024)
.

Pieroth and Bichler, (2024)
proposed a new solution concept, called
\alpha
-Rank-collections, for games where ordinal preferences are known, but there is uncertainty concerning players’ cardinal utilities.

Ordinal potential games were introduced and studied by
Monderer and Shapley, (1996)
, and then characterized by
Voorneveld and Norde, (1997)
and
Norde and Patrone, (2001)
.

2.
The game

2.1.
Ordinal games

Throughout the paper, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we let
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,n\}
.
We study two-person normal form finite ordinal games where each player
i\in\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}\}
has the same action set
[K]
and a preference relation
\prec_{i}
over the outcomes of the game.
We assume these preferences to be strict, i.e., for all pairs of outcomes
\Theta,\Theta^{\prime}
, either
\Theta\prec_{i}\Theta^{\prime}
or
\Theta^{\prime}\prec_{i}\Theta
.

A strategy profile
(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})
is a
NE

(
NE
) of the game if, for all
a,b\in[K]
, we have

(a,b^{\ast})\prec_{\mathrm{A}}(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})\quad\text{and}\quad(a^{\ast},b)\prec_{\mathrm{B}}(a^{\ast},b^{\ast}).

A two-person normal form game is called
SOP

(
SOP
) if there exists a
potential function

\Psi\colon[K]\times[K]\to\mathbb{R}
such that, for each player
i\in\{\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B}\}
, we have

(2.1)

\begin{split}\Theta(a,b)&\prec_{\mathrm{A}}\Theta(a^{\prime},b)\iff\Psi(a,b)>\Psi(a^{\prime},b),\\
\Theta(a,b)&\prec_{\mathrm{B}}\Theta(a,b^{\prime})\iff\Psi(a,b)>\Psi(a,b^{\prime}).\end{split}

To allow us to express asymptotic results in a simple way, the inequality in the potential is reversed with respect to the preference orders:
given the action of the other player, an outcome with a lower potential is better than an outcome with a higher potential.
For our class of
SOP
(
SOP
) games, w.l.o.g, the potential function can be chosen to be
1
-
1
with values in
[K^{2}]
.
In other words, the outcome whose potential is
1
is the best, the one whose potential is
2
is the second best, etc, all the way to the worst, whose potential is
K^{2}
.
The class of
1
-
1
potentials with values in
[K^{2}]
will be denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{K}
.
In the sequel we identify the function
\Psi\in\mathcal{P}_{K}
with the
[K]\times[K]
matrix of its values.

Two
SOP
games having the same potential are strategically equivalent, i.e., they have the same set of
NE
.
Any
SOP
game is strategically equivalent to a game where
\prec_{\mathrm{A}}\equiv\prec_{\mathrm{B}}
.
The potential identifies the set of
NE
of any
SOP
game.
Therefore, we identify the equivalence class of strategically equivalent
SOP
with their potential
\Psi\in\mathcal{P}_{K}
.
Each
NE
(
NE
) of a
SOP
game is a local minimum of its potential.
The set of
NE
of
\Psi\in\mathcal{P}_{K}
will be denoted by
\NE_{K}
.
Its cardinality
\left\lvert\NE_{K}\right\rvert
will be denoted by
W_{K}
.
The set

(2.2)

\Psi^{\ast}\coloneqq\left\{\Psi(\boldsymbol{\eta})\colon\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\NE_{K}\right\}\subset\left[K^{2}\right]

is the set of potential values corresponding to equilibrium profiles (in the sequel, they will be called equilibrium potentials).
The
NE
in a
SOP
can be ordered according to their potential, so that the equilibrium
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{1}
is the one with the smallest potential,
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{2}
is the one with the second smallest potential, etc.
The ranking of equilibrium
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is denoted by
\Lambda(\boldsymbol{\eta})
.

3.
Random
SOP
games

We now study random
SOP
games.
To achieve this, for every fixed
K
, we consider a uniform distribution over the set
\mathcal{P}_{K}
.
A simple way to generate a uniform distribution on
\mathcal{P}_{K}
is to draw
K^{2}
i.i.d. random variables with a continuous distribution function (w.l.o.g, a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
) and to consider their rankings, which, with probability
1
, are all different.

A random
SOP
game has a random number of
NE
, which is a random variable with values in
[K]
, as each column and raw can contain at most one
NE
.
This random number is positive, since the potential always has a global minimum and cannot be larger than
K
because the preferences are strict, i.e., the values that the potential can take are all distinct.

It is easy to compute the average number of
NE
in a random
SOP
.
Each action profile is a
NE
with probability
1/(2K-1)
because it is a
NE
if the value of its potential is larger than all the values on the same row and column.
Therefore, the expected number of
NE
is

(3.1)

\Expect\left[W_{K}\right]=\frac{K^{2}}{2K-1}.

The following concentration result was proved by
Mimun et al., (2024, corollary 3.3)
.

Theorem 3.1

(
Mimun et al., (2024, corollary 3.3)
)
.

For all
\delta>0
,

(3.2)

\lim_{K\to\infty}\Prob\left(\left\lvert\frac{W_{K}}{K}-\frac{1}{2}\right\rvert<\delta\right)=1.

In what follows, we will often use the classical asymptotic notation: for positive sequences
f_{n}
and
g_{n}
we write
f_{n}=\mathcal{O}(g_{n})
to mean that the ratio
f_{n}/g_{n}
is asymptotically bounded; similarly, we write
f_{n}=o(g_{n})
to mean that the limiting ratio
f_{n}/g_{n}
is zero. Finally,
f_{n}\sim g_{n}
denotes the fact that the limiting ratio is one.

4.
BRD

The
BRD

(
BRD
) is a learning algorithm taking as input a potential
\Psi
and a starting action profile
(a_{0},b_{0})
.
For each
t\geq 0
we consider the process
\BRD(t)
on
[K]\times[K]
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\BRD(0)

\displaystyle=(a_{0},b_{0})

and, if
\BRD(t)=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
, then, for
t
even,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\BRD(t+1)

\displaystyle=(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime}),

where

a^{\prime\prime}\in\argmin_{a\in[K]}\Psi(a,b^{\prime})\setminus\left\{a^{\prime}\right\}
, if the latter set is not empty, otherwise

(4.3)

\displaystyle\BRD(t+1)

\displaystyle=\BRD(t);

for
t
odd,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\BRD(t+1)

\displaystyle=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime}),

where

b^{\prime\prime}\in\argmin_{b\in[K]}\Psi(a^{\prime},b)\setminus\left\{b^{\prime}\right\}
, if the latter set is not empty, otherwise

(4.5)

\displaystyle\BRD(t+1)

\displaystyle=\BRD(t).

It is easy to see that, when, for some
positive
\hat{t}
, we have

(4.6)

\BRD(\hat{t})=\BRD(\hat{t}+1)=(a^{\ast},b^{\ast}),

then
\BRD(t)=(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})
for all
t\geq\hat{t}
and
(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})
is a
NE
of the game.

The fact that the game is
SOP
implies that a
NE
is always reached by the
BRD
.
When the algorithm visits an action profile for the second time, that profile is a
NE
.
Notice that a
BRD
never visits a row or column more than twice (once by the row player and once by the column player), so it reaches a
PNE
in at most
2K
steps. For this reason, we will often employ the notation
\BRD(2K)
to denote the action profile in which the
BRD
will eventually get absorbed in.

Once a starting point
\BRD(0)=(a_{0},b_{0})
is chosen, the
BRD
will reach (deterministically) one
NE
.
For each
NE

(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})
, we define its
BoA

(
BoA
) as follows:

(4.7)

\BoA(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})\coloneqq\left\{(a,b)\colon\text{if }\BRD(0)=(a,b),\text{ then }\BRD(2K)=(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})\right\}

Remark 4.1
.

Given the way the process
\BRD(\,\cdot\,)
is defined, we have that
(a,b)\in\BoA(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})
implies
(a^{\prime},b)\in\BoA(a^{\ast},b^{\ast})
for all
a^{\prime}\in[K]
.
This is because the very first step of the
BRD
is along the row.

Remark 4.2
.

Notice that if the potential
\Psi^{\prime}
is obtained from
\Psi
by permuting rows and columns, then the
PNE
of
\Psi^{\prime}
are just the corresponding permutations of the
PNE
of
\Psi
.
Moreover, the
BoA
of the
PNE
in
\Psi^{\prime}
are obtained by permuting the columns of the corresponding
BoA
in
\Psi
.

5.
The incremental construction

Theorem

4.4
will be proved using what we call the
incremental construction
of the game.
This construction provides the potential of a random
SOP
game that does not have a uniform distribution, but has the same set of equilibrium potentials that a uniformly distributed potential has.

For a fixed integer
K
, we will construct a random potential function
\Psi\in\mathcal{P}_{K}
by adding entries sequentially according to the algorithm described below.

Set
C_{0}=R_{0}=0
and, for
t\in\left[K^{2}\right]
,

(a)

we call
R_{t}
the number of non-empty rows,
C_{t}
the number of non-empty columns, and
G_{t}
the number of green entries after adding the first
t
entries of
\Psi
;

(b)

we call
M_{t}
the sub-matrix of
\Psi
composed of rows
\left[R_{t}\right]
and columns
\left[C_{t}\right]
;

(c)

we call
\mathfrak{R}_{t}
a Bernoulli random variable such that

(5.1)

\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t}=1)=\rho_{t}\coloneqq\frac{(K-R_{t-1})K}{K^{2}-t-1};

(d)

we call
\mathfrak{C}_{t}
a Bernoulli random variable such that

(5.2)

\Prob(\mathfrak{C}_{t}=1)=\kappa_{t}\coloneqq\frac{(K-C_{t-1})}{K}.

The incremental construction will be described by
Algorithm

1
.

\Psi(1,1)=1
.

(2)

(1,1)

(3)

, the

(a)

\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=1
, then set

.

(a.i)

\mathfrak{C}_{t+1}=1
, then set

,

(a.ii)

If
\mathfrak{C}_{t+1}=0
, then set
C_{t+1}=C_{t}
, sample
Z_{t+1}
uniformly at random in
\left[C_{t}\right]
, and set
\Psi(R_{t+1},Z_{t+1})=t+1
.

(b)

If
\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0
, then set
R_{t+1}=R_{t}

and draw one entry uniformly at random among the empty entries in the rows
\left\{1,\dots,R_{t}\right\}
.
Call this entry
(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\in[R_{t}]\times[K]
.

(b.i)

If
(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\not\in M_{t}
, then set
C_{t+1}=C_{t}+1
and
\Psi(X_{t+1},C_{t+1})=t+1
.

(b.ii)

If
(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})\in M_{t}
, then set
C_{t+1}=C_{t}
and
\Psi(X_{t+1},Y_{t+1})=t+1
.

(4)

The output of the algorithm will be called
\Psi
.

In the incremental construction call
\Psi^{t}
the set of all entries added at times
s\leq t
and

\mathcal{F}_{t}\coloneqq\sigma(\Psi^{t})
.
So
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\right\}_{t\in\{0,\dots,K^{2}\}}
is the natural filtration.

6.
Asymptotic analysis of the incremental construction

6.1.
\varepsilon
-stopped incremental construction

Definition 6.1
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
. An
\varepsilon
-stopped incremental construction
of a potential
\Psi\in\mathcal{P}_{K}
is obtained as follows: perform the incremental construction of
Algorithm

1
up to the stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon K}
defined as

(6.1)

\tau_{\varepsilon K}\coloneqq\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon G_{t}=\lfloor\varepsilon K\rfloor\right\}.

After
\tau_{\varepsilon K}
continue the construction by placing the remaining integers
\tau_{\varepsilon K}+1,\dots,K^{2}
uniformly at random on the remaining empty entries.

Notice that the set of best
\lfloor\varepsilon K\rfloor
equilibria and the value of their potential are measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{\tau_{\varepsilon K}}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
.

(a)

The stopping time
\tau_{\varepsilon K}
satisfy

(6.2)

\frac{\tau_{\varepsilon K}}{K}\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\Prob}\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\right).

(b)

The quantities
R_{\tau_{\varepsilon K}}
and
C_{\tau_{\varepsilon K}}
satisfy

(6.3)

\left(\frac{R_{\tau_{\varepsilon K}}}{K},\frac{C_{\tau_{\varepsilon K}}}{K}\right)\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\Prob}\left(1-\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon},1-\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}\right).

The proof of
Proposition

6.2
requires the introduction of a new process,
(\widetilde{C}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, defined below.

In
Algorithm

1
3(b)
let
Y_{t+1}
be sampled as follows: draw a cell at random in the set of rows
\left\{1,\dots,R_{t}\right\}
.
If the cell is not empty, draw another cell, and keep drawing until an empty cell is selected.
Call
\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}
a Bernoulli random variable that takes value
1
if no resamples are needed.
We have

(6.4)

\Prob(\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=1)=u_{t+1}=1-\frac{t}{R_{t}K}.

The process
\widetilde{C}_{t}
is constructed as follows:

(a)

Set
\widetilde{C}_{0}=0
.

(b)

If
\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=1
, then set
\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}=C_{t+1}-C_{t}
.

(c)

If
\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0
and
\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=1
, then
set
\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}=C_{t+1}-C_{t}
.

(d)

If
\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0
and
\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=0
, then
set
\widetilde{C}_{t+1}=C_{t}
.

Call
D_{t}
the number of steps where
(d)
occurs up to time
t
.
It follows that, a.s.,

(6.5)

0\leq C_{t}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\leq D_{t}.

Moreover,

(6.6)

\Prob\left(D_{t+1}=D_{t}+1\right)=\Prob\left(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=0\right)=\frac{R_{t}K-t}{K^{2}-t}\frac{t}{R_{t}K}\leq\frac{t}{K^{2}},

where the inequality stems from the fact that
R_{t}\leq K
, which implies
(R_{t}K-t)/(K^{2}-t)\leq R_{t}K/K^{2}
.

The next two results provide a control on the difference between the processes
C_{t}
and
\widetilde{C}_{t}
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and choose
t=\lfloor K^{1+\alpha}\rfloor
.
Then

(6.7)

\lim_{K\to\infty}\Prob\left(D_{t}<6K^{2\alpha}\right)=1.

Proof.

Consider a sequence of independent Bernoulli random variables
\left(L_{j}\right)
such that
\Prob\left(L_{j}=1\right)=j/K^{2}
.
Then
Eq.

6.6
implies

(6.8)

\Prob\left(D_{t}\geq 6K^{2\alpha}\right)\leq\Prob\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t}L_{j}\geq 6K^{2\alpha}\right).

Since

(6.9)

\Expect\left[\sum_{j=1}^{t}L_{j}\right]\leq\frac{t^{2}}{K^{2}}=K^{2\alpha},

the multiplicative Chernoff bound

(see, e.g.,
Mitzenmacher and Upfal,, 2017
, theorem 4.4)

implies that

(6.10)

\Prob\left(D_{t}\geq 6K^{2\alpha}\right)\leq 2^{-6K^{2\alpha}}.

∎

Corollary 6.4
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
and
t\leq K^{1+\alpha}
, we have

(6.11)

\frac{C_{t}-\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K}\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\Prob}0.

Similarly to what was done with the process
C_{t}
, we introduce a simplified process
\widetilde{G}_{t}
which serves as an approximation of
G_{t}
. More precisely, we set
\widetilde{G}_{0}=0
and define, for all
t\in[K^{2}]

(6.12)

\widetilde{G}_{t}-\widetilde{G}_{t-1}=\mathds{1}_{R_{t}-R_{t-1}=1}\,\mathds{1}_{\widetilde{C}_{t}-\widetilde{C}_{t-1}=1}.

Notice that the evolution of
G_{t}
can be obtained by replacing
\widetilde{C}_{t}
and
\widetilde{C}_{t-1}
with
C_{t}
and
C_{t-1}
, respectively, in
Eq.

6.12
.

6.2.
An
ODE
(
ODE
) approximation

We now want to show that the triple
\left(R_{t},\widetilde{C}_{t},\widetilde{G}_{t}\right)
, when properly re-scaled (in space-time) by
K
can be approximated by the solution of an
ODE
system.
To do this, we need the following lemma:

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\colon[0,1]^{3}\to[0,1]
be defined as follows:

(6.13)

\alpha(r,c,g)=1-r,\quad\beta(r,c,g)=1-c,\quad\gamma(r,c,g)=(1-r)(1-c).

Then, for every
t\leq K^{3/2}
, we have

(6.14)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Expect\left[R_{t+1}-R_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\alpha\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}},

(6.15)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\beta\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}},

(6.16)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Expect\left[\widetilde{G}_{t+1}-\widetilde{G}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\gamma\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{K}}.

Proof.

To prove
Eq.

6.14
notice that, by
Eq.

5.1
,

(6.17)

\Expect\left[R_{t+1}-R_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]=\frac{(K-R_{t})K}{K^{2}-t}.

For
0\leq t\leq K^{3/2}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Expect\left[R_{t+1}-R_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\alpha\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\frac{(K-R_{t})K}{K^{2}-t}-\left(1-\frac{R_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert=\frac{K-R_{t}}{K}\cdot\frac{t}{K^{2}-t}\leq\frac{t}{K^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\,.

To prove
Eq.

6.15
recall
Eq.

5.2
and note that

\displaystyle\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=1,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\quad+\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=1,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle\quad+\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=0,\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=0\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})

\displaystyle=\kappa_{t+1}\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})+\kappa_{t+1}\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=1\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})+0

\displaystyle=\kappa_{t+1}(1-\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=0\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}))\,.

Hence, by (
6.6
), for
0\leq t\leq K^{\frac{3}{2}}
we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\beta\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\kappa_{t+1}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\Prob(\mathfrak{R}_{t+1}=0,\mathfrak{U}_{t+1}=0\mid\mathcal{F}_{t})\leq\frac{t}{K^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\,.

Finally,
Eq.

6.16
follows from
Eq.

6.12
. Indeed, being the sequences
(R_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\widetilde{C}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
independent, we have

\displaystyle\left\lvert\Expect\left[\widetilde{G}_{t+1}-\widetilde{G}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\gamma\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert=

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\Expect\left[R_{t+1}-R_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\alpha\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\beta\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\Expect\left[R_{t+1}-R_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\alpha\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\Expect\left[\widetilde{C}_{t+1}-\widetilde{C}_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t}\right]-\beta\left(\frac{R_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K},\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}\right)\right\rvert\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{K}}\,.

∎

At this point, the main approximation tools which we will exploit in the following is summarized in the following result.

Proposition 6.6
.

Let
\left(r_{s},c_{s},g_{s}\right)_{s\geq 0}
be the solution of the Cauchy system

(6.18)

\begin{cases}\dot{r}_{s}=1-r_{s},\\
\dot{c}_{s}=1-c_{s},\\
\dot{g}_{s}=(1-r_{s})(1-c_{s}),\\
r_{0}=c_{0}=g_{0}=0,\end{cases}

that is,

(6.19)

r_{s}=c_{s}=1-\expo^{-s},\quad g_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\expo^{-2s}\right).

Then, for any constant
T>0
, we have

(6.20)

\Prob\left(\forall t\leq TK,\left\lvert\frac{R_{t}}{K}-r_{t/K}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{\widetilde{C}_{t}}{K}-c_{t/K}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\frac{\widetilde{G}_{t}}{K}-g_{t/K}\right\rvert\leq 18\expo^{2T}K^{-1/3}\right)\geq 1-6\exp\left\{-\frac{K^{1/3}}{8T}\right\}.

Proof.

The result can be deduced using the so-called
Wormald differential equation method

(
Wormald,, 1995
, see)
.
For simplicity, we adopt here the same formulation as in

Warnke, (2019, theorem 2)
, and use the bounds proved therein.
With this method, the evolution of the space-time rescaled random process can be uniformly first-order approximated by the solution of an associated Cauchy problem.

The main requirement is that the expected increment of the original process be controlled by certain function (which plays the role of the derivative in the associated Cauchy problem) up to a vanishingly small error, as we showed in

Lemma

6.5
.
∎

We can now prove
Proposition

6.2
.

Proof of
Proposition

6.2
.

Fix
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
. If we define

(6.21)

\widetilde{\tau}_{\varepsilon K}\coloneqq\inf\left\{t\geq 0\colon\widetilde{G}_{t}=\lfloor\varepsilon K\rfloor\right\},

then, by
Proposition

6.6
, we obtain

(6.22)

\frac{\widetilde{\tau}_{\varepsilon K}}{K}\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\Prob}\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-2\varepsilon}\right).

In light of (
6.22
), to prove

(a)
it suffices to show that, for all
t\leq K^{7/6}

(6.23)

\frac{\left\lvert\widetilde{G}_{t}-G_{t}\right\rvert}{K}\xrightarrow[K\to\infty]{\Prob}0.

Notice that
Eq.

6.23
follows by
Corollary

6.4
and
Eq.

6.12
.
At this point, the convergence in
(b)
is a consequence of
(a)
,
Proposition

6.6
, and
Corollary

6.4
.
∎

Lemma 7.2
.

For all
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
and for all

(7.8)

we have

\lim_{K\to\infty}1+\sum_{\ell=0}^{2K-2}\frac{K-c}{(\ell+1)K-h(\ell+1)}\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{K-r\mathds{1}_{\left\{t\textup{ odd}\right\}}-c\mathds{1}_{\left\{t\textup{ even}\right\}}-\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor}{tK-h(t)}=\exp\left\{\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}\right\},

where we omitted the explicit dependence of

and

from

.

Proof.

Define, for
\zeta\in\mathbb{N}
,

(7.10)

L_{K}(\zeta)\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=0}^{2K-2}\frac{\zeta K}{(\ell+1)K-h(\ell+1)}\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{\zeta K-\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor}{tK-h(t)}\,.

We have

(7.11)

\begin{split}L_{K}\left(\left\lfloor(1-\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}-K^{-1/4})\right\rfloor\right)&\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{2K-2}\frac{K-c}{(\ell+1)K-h(\ell+1)}\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{K-r\mathds{1}_{\left\{t\textup{ odd}\right\}}-c\mathds{1}_{\left\{t\textup{ even}\right\}}-\left\lfloor\frac{t}{2}\right\rfloor}{tK-h(t)}\\
&\leq L_{K}\left(\left\lceil(1-\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}+K^{-1/4})\right\rceil\right)\,.\end{split}

Fixing a sequence
T_{K}
such that
\lim_{K\to\infty}T_{K}=\infty

and
T_{K}=o(K)
, we get

(7.12)

\begin{split}L_{K}(\zeta)&=\sum_{\ell=0}^{2K-2}\frac{\zeta}{(\ell+1)-\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2}/K)}\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{\zeta-\mathcal{O}(t^{2}/K)}{tK-\mathcal{O}(t/K)}\\
&=\sum_{\ell=0}^{T_{K}}\frac{\zeta}{(\ell+1)-\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2}/K)}\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{\zeta-\mathcal{O}(t^{2}/K)}{tK-\mathcal{O}(t/K)}\\
&\quad+\sum_{\ell=T_{K}+1}^{2K-2}\frac{\zeta}{(\ell+1)-\mathcal{O}(\ell^{2}/K)}\prod_{t=1}^{\ell}\frac{\zeta-\mathcal{O}(t^{2}/K)}{tK-\mathcal{O}(t/K)}\\
&\sim\sum_{\ell=0}^{T_{K}}\frac{\zeta^{\ell+1}}{(\ell+1)!}+o(1)\\
&\sim\expo^{\zeta}-1+o(1),\end{split}

which concludes the proof by choosing
\zeta=\sqrt{1-2\varepsilon}\pm K^{-1/4}
and taking the limit
K\rightarrow\infty
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4.6
.

The process
\left(R_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
can be seen as a coupon-collector processes, where
R_{t}
stands for the number of coupons collected by time
t
.
If we call
T_{k}
the time that is needed to collect the
k
-th coupon, then we have

(7.22)

\Expect\left[T_{k}\right]=K(H_{k}-H_{K-k}),

where
H_{k}
is the
k
-th harmonic number

(7.23)

H_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{i}.

Moreover, for each
k\in[K]
, we have

(7.24)

\Var\left[T_{k}\right]\leq 2K^{2}.

If, for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
k=K-K^{\varepsilon}
, then

(7.25)

\Expect[T_{k}]\sim(1-\varepsilon)K\log(K),

and Chebyshev inequality implies that, for all
\delta>0

(7.26)

\lim_{K\to\infty}\Prob\left(\left|\frac{T_{k}}{(1-\varepsilon)K\log(K)}-1\right|>\delta\right)=0\,,

Notice that, the probability of having a Nash equilibrium with ranking larger than
t
is bounded above by the probability that the incremental construction in
Algorithm

1
does not stop before time
t
, i.e.,

(7.27)

\displaystyle\Prob\left(\max\left\{C_{\left\lfloor(1+\delta)K\log K\right\rfloor},R_{\left\lfloor(1+\delta)K\log K\right\rfloor}\right\}=K\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Prob\left(R_{\left\lfloor(1+\delta)K\log K\right\rfloor}=K\right)

(7.28)

\displaystyle=\Prob\left(T_{K}\leq\left\lfloor(1+\delta)K\log K\right\rfloor\right)\to 1,

where the asymptotic follows by (
7.26
). This concludes the proof.
∎
