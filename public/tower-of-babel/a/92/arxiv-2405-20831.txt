Strong propagation of chaos for systems of interacting particles with nearly stable jumps
Eva Löcherbach ??, Dasha Loukianova ??, Elisa Marini ??

Abstract
We consider a system of N N interacting particles, described by SDEs driven by Poisson random measures, where the coefficients depend on the empirical measure of the system. Every particle jumps with a jump rate depending on its position. When this happens, all the other particles of the system receive a small random kick which is distributed according to a heavy tailed random variable belonging to the domain of attraction of an α − \alpha- stable law and scaled by N − 1 / α , N^{-1/\alpha}, where 0 < α < 2 . 0<\alpha<2. We call these jumps collateral jumps . Moreover, in case 0 < α < 1 , 0<\alpha<1, the jumping particle itself undergoes a macroscopic, main jump. Such systems appear in the modeling of large neural networks, such as the human brain. The particular scaling of the collateral jumps implies that the limit of the empirical measures of the system is random and equals the conditional distribution of one typical particle in the limit system, given the source of common noise. Thus the system exhibits the conditional propagation of chaos property . The limit system turns out to be solution of a non-linear SDE, driven by an α − \alpha- stable process. We prove strong unique existence of the limit system and introduce a suitable coupling to obtain the strong convergence of the finite to the limit system, together with precise error bounds for finite time marginals. Introduction

In the present paper we study the large population limit of the Markov process
X^{N}=(X^{N}_{t})_{t\geq 0},

X^{N}_{t}=(X^{N,1}_{t},\ldots,X^{N,N}_{t}),
which takes values in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and has generator
A^{N}
given by

A^{N}\varphi(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{x^{i}}\varphi(x)b(x^{i},\mu^{N,x})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x^{i})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\nu(du)\left(\varphi\left(x+\psi(x^{i},\mu^{N,x})e_{i}+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{u}{N^{1/\alpha}}e_{j}\right)-\varphi(x)\right),

(1)

for any smooth test function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}.
In the above formula,
x=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

\mu^{N,x}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{x^{j}}
is the associated
empirical measure, and
e_{j}
denotes the
j-
th unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}.
Moreover,
b(x^{i},\mu^{N,x})
is a bounded drift function depending both on the position
x^{i}
of a fixed particle and on the empirical measure
\mu^{N,x}
of the total system,
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a Lipschitz continuous bounded rate function, and
\nu
is the law of a heavy-tailed random variable belonging to the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law (see Definition
2.1
below);
\nu
is supposed to be centered if
\alpha\in(1,2)
.
Since
f
is bounded,
X^{N}
is a piecewise deterministic Markov process. In between jumps, any particle
X^{N,i}
follows a deterministic flow having drift
b.
Each particle jumps at rate
f(x^{i})
whenever its current position is
x^{i}.
When jumping, it receives an additional kick
\psi(x^{i},\mu^{N,x})
which is added to its position. Moreover, at the same time, all the other particles in the system receive the same
collateral jump
(
[
2
]
). This collateral jump is random, it has law
\nu,
and it is renormalized by
N^{1/\alpha},
where
N
is the system size. Therefore in our system there is coexistence of main jumps - the jumps of size
\psi(x^{i},\mu^{N,x})
- and of random and common small kicks that are received synchronously by all the other particles. Such systems have originally been introduced to model large biological neural nets such as the brain where the collateral jumps correspond to the synaptic weight of a neuron on its postsynaptic partners and the main jumps to the hyperpolarization of a neuron after a spike, see for instance
[
4
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
28
]
, see also the recent monograph
[
20
]
.

In a series of papers
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
, we have already studied the mean field limit of such systems in a diffusive scaling, that is, when
\alpha=2
and
\nu
is a centered probability measure on
\mathbb{R}
having a second moment. In this case, the central limit theorem implies that the large population limit of the system having generator (
1
) is given by an infinite exchangeable system evolving according to

X^{i}_{t}=X^{i}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X^{i}_{s},\mu_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\psi(X^{i}_{s-},\mu_{s-})d{Z}^{i}_{s}+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{\mu_{s}(f)}dW_{s},\;t\geq 0,i\in\mathbb{N}^{*},

(2)

where
\sigma^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\nu(du)
and where
W
is a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion which is common to all particles.
In (
2
) above,
{Z}^{i}
is the counting process associated to the jumps of particle
i,
having intensity
t\mapsto f(X^{i}_{t^{-}}).

The presence of the Brownian motion
W
is a source of common noise in the limit system and implies that
the
conditional propagation of chaos
property holds: in the limit system, particles are conditionally independent, if we condition on
W.
In particular, we have shown in
[
14
]
that the limit empirical measure
\mu_{s}
is the directing measure of the infinite limit system (see Def. (2.6) in
[
1
]
for the precise definition), which is necessarily given by
\mu_{s}={\mathcal{L}}(X^{i}_{s}|W_{u},u\leq s),
such that the stochastic integral term appearing in (
2
) is given by

\int_{0}^{t}\sqrt{I\!\!E(f(X^{i}_{s})|W_{u},u\leq s)}dW_{s}.

It is a natural question to ask what happens in the situation when
\nu
does not belong to the domain of attraction of a normal law but of a stable law of index
\alpha<2.
The present paper gives an answer to this question. Not surprisingly, the limit Brownian motion will be replaced by a stable process
S^{\alpha}
of index
\alpha
such that the limit equation is now given by

\bar{X}^{i}_{t}=\bar{X}^{i}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(\bar{X}^{i}_{s},\bar{\mu}_{s})ds+\int_{0}^{t}\psi(\bar{X}^{i}_{s-},\bar{\mu}_{s-})d\bar{Z}^{i}_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{\mu}_{s-}(f))^{1/\alpha}dS^{\alpha}_{s},\;t\geq 0,i\in\mathbb{N}^{*},

(3)

with
\bar{\mu}_{s}={\mathcal{L}}(\bar{X}^{i}_{s}|S^{\alpha}_{u},u\leq s).
In the case
1<\alpha<2,
we exclude main jumps; that is, we suppose that
\psi(\cdot)\equiv 0.
This is due to the fact that the stochastic integral with respect to

\bar{Z}^{i}
has to be treated in the
L^{1}-
norm which is a norm not suited for the integral with respect to
S^{\alpha},
see Remark
3.2
below.

The present paper establishes the proof of the strong convergence of the finite system to the limit system, with respect to a convenient distance. This is done by proposing a coupling of the finite system with the limit one. More precisely we will construct a particular version
S^{N,\alpha}
of the stable process which is defined on an extension of the same probability space on which the finite system
X^{N}
is defined, and then we consider the limit system driven by
S^{N,\alpha}.
So we have to ensure first the existence of a unique strong solution of (
3
).
This is relatively straightforward, when considering the system before the first big jump of the driving stable process, bigger than
K
for some fixed large
K
. This strategy is inspired by the study of classical SDEs driven by Lévy noise proposed by
[
17
]
, it has been used in the framework of non-conditional McKean-Vlasov equations by
[
7
]
, and it can be very easily extended to the present framework of conditional McKean-Vlasov equations.

In a second step, we then prove the strong convergence of the finite system to the limit system.
The main ingredient of this step is an explicit construction of the stable process
S^{N,\alpha}
based on the random nearly stable heights of the collateral jumps present in the finite particle system. We discretize time and freeze the jump rate during small time intervals of length
\delta.
Let us suppose for simplicity that
\nu
is already the law of a strictly stable random variable (this assumption will not be needed in the sequel of the text). Then the total contribution of collateral jumps during one such interval is a random sum, renormalized by
N^{1/\alpha},
constituted of independent stable random variables, each representing one collateral jump. The total number of terms in the sum is Poisson distributed (conditionally). Thus we are able to use the self-similarity property of the
\alpha-
stable law: we know that, if
Y_{n}
are i.i.d. strictly
\alpha-
stable random variables, then

Y_{1}+\ldots+Y_{n}\sim n^{1/\alpha}Y_{1}.

The following trivial but very useful result says that this property survives for random sums, and it is the main argument of our coupling construction.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
Y_{n}
be i.i.d. strictly
\alpha-
stable random variables. Let
P
be an integer-valued random variable, independent of
(Y_{n})_{n}.
Then the following equality holds.

\sum_{n=1}^{P}Y_{n}=P^{1/\alpha}\tilde{Y}_{1},\;\;\;\tilde{Y}_{1}\sim Y_{1},

where
P
and
\tilde{Y}_{1}
are independent.

We apply this result on each time interval
[k\delta,(k+1)\delta[,

k\geq 0,
such that the total number of jumps during this time interval,
P,
follows a Poisson distribution with parameter
\delta\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x^{i})
, conditionally to
\mathcal{F}_{k\delta}
. Here,
f(x^{i})
is the frozen jump rate of particle
i
at time
k\delta.
We then use the law of large numbers for the Poisson random variable
P
to replace
P
by its intensity
\delta\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(x^{i})=N\delta\int fd\mu^{N,x}
(where
\mu^{N,x}
is the empirical measure
N^{-1}\sum\delta_{x^{i}}
), so that
N^{-1/\alpha}P^{1/\alpha}\sim(\delta\int fd\mu^{N,x})^{1/\alpha}
. So the contribution of collateral jumps, over one time interval, is approximately given by
(\int fd\mu^{N,x})^{1/\alpha}\delta^{1/\alpha}\tilde{Y}_{1},
and since
\delta^{1/\alpha}\tilde{Y}_{1}\sim S^{\alpha}_{\delta},
it is then reasonable to expect that, as
\delta\to 0,
the joint contribution of all small intervals gives rise to the stochastic integral term
\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{\mu}_{s-}(f))^{1/\alpha}dS^{\alpha}_{s}
that appears in (
3
) above, and this is precisely the strategy of our proof, up to technical details.

Our main result Theorem
2.14
then shows that for all
t>0,
for all
i=1,\ldots,N,

in the case
\alpha>1
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}I\!\!E[|X^{N,i}_{t}-\bar{X}^{i}_{t}|]=0,

and, in the case
\alpha<1
, for any
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}I\!\!E[|X^{N,i}_{t}-\bar{X}^{i}_{t}|\wedge|X^{N,i}_{t}-\bar{X}^{i}_{t}|^{{\alpha_{-}}}]=0.

Moreover, for all
K>0
a precise control on
I\!\!E[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}|X^{N,i}_{t}-\bar{X}^{i}_{t}|]
in the case
\alpha>1
and
I\!\!E[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}|X^{N,i}_{t}-\bar{X}^{i}_{t}|\wedge|X^{N,i}_{t}-\bar{X}^{i}_{t}|^{{\alpha_{-}}}]
in the case
0<\alpha<1
is given
in terms of
\alpha,N,t
and of the truncation level
K,
where
T^{N}_{K}
is the first moment when the driving
\alpha
-stable process in (
3
) executes a jump exceeding
K
(see Theorem
2.14
below for the precise form of this control).

Comments on the norms we have used

Our error bounds contain three main contributions, and each of these contributions has to be treated using a different norm. The final strong error is then obtained by balancing all these error terms.

1.

The error due to the stable central limit theorem is treated using the
L^{1}-
norm in case
\alpha>1,
and the norm induced by
|\cdot|\wedge|\cdot|^{\alpha_{-}}
in case
\alpha<1.

2.

We need to study stochastic integral terms of the type
\int_{0}^{t}(\mu^{N}_{s-}(f))^{1/\alpha}dS^{N,\alpha}_{s},
with
\mu^{N}
the empirical measure of the finite particle system
(X^{N,i})_{1\leq i\leq N},

\int_{0}^{t}(\bar{\mu}_{s-}(f))^{1/\alpha}dS^{N,\alpha}_{s}
and their convergence. Since small and big jumps of
S^{N,\alpha}
are not integrable in the same norm, as usual, we cut big jumps and work on the event
\{t<T^{N}_{K}\},
for some fixed
K.
To control the dependency on
K
in the case
\alpha>1
we then work in the
L^{\alpha_{+}}-
norm, for some
\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}<2\lx@nobreakspace.
For technical reasons, this trick does not work in the case
\alpha<1
, such that we work with the
L^{1}-
norm then.

3.

Finally, most error terms that do only concern the finite particle system (such as time discretization errors) have to be controlled in the
L^{\alpha_{-}}-
norm, since
X^{N,i}_{t}\in L^{\alpha_{-}}
does not belong to
L^{\alpha}.

Bibliographical comments

The property of
conditional propagation of chaos
is related to the existence of a common noise in the limit system and has been a lot studied in the literature; see for instance
[
5
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
13
]
. In these papers the common noise, which is most often a common, maybe infinite dimensional, Brownian motion, is already present at the level of the finite particle system, the mean field interactions act on the drift of each particle, and the scaling is the classical one in
N^{-1}.
On the contrary to this, in our model, the common noise is only present in the limit, and it is created by the
\alpha-
stable limit theorem as a consequence of the joint action of the collateral jumps of the finite particle system and of the scaling in
N^{-1/\alpha}.

In the classical setting of
unconditional propagation of chaos
, mean field limits for particle systems driven by general Lévy noise have also been extensively studied. We refer the interested reader to the
paper of Graham
[
22
]
who considers equations driven by (possibly compensated) Poisson random measures. He works under Lipschitz and integrability conditions with the
L^{1}
-norm - which is not possible when the driving noise is an
\alpha-
stable process with
\alpha<1
.
[
23
]
have worked with general Lévy noise, but mostly under
L^{2}
-conditions. They consider also the case when the driving process does only possess moments of order
\alpha<1,
and in that situation they are only able to obtain weak existence of the limit without proving uniqueness in law. In the recent paper
[
6
]
, the author obtains a quantitative propagation of chaos result for systems driven by
\alpha-
stable subordinators, in the case
\alpha\in]1,2[.
There is no measure dependent term within the stochastic integral term in
[
6
]
, and the author is mostly interested in relaxing the regularity assumptions on the coefficients and works only under the assumption of Hölder continuity. Finally, several papers are devoted to the well-posedness of the limit equation. Let us mention
[
19
]
which treats the general case
0<\alpha<2,
under mild regularity assumptions, by the means of an associated non-linear martingale problem. Finally,
[
7
]
treats the case
1<\alpha<2,
including a measure dependent term in the stochastic integral term, under general Lipschitz assumptions. All these papers are devoted to the unconditional framework.

Let us come back to the discussion of our present paper, which deals with the conditional propagation of chaos property in a situation where the driving Lévy process appears only in the limit system. We have already mentioned that it continues and extends the diffusive setting studied in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
to the framework of the
\alpha-
stable limit theorem. As in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
, the basic strategy is to discretize time and construct a coupling with the driving noise of the limit equation explicitly within each time interval
[k\delta,(k+1)\delta).
This strategy is actually inspired by the approach proposed in
[
12
]
.

We now quickly discuss the main differences with respect to the former diffusive setting. In the bounded variation regime
0<\alpha<1,
the error due to time discretization is now of order
\delta,
as opposed to
\sqrt{\delta}
which was the leading order in the diffusive scaling. Contrarily to the diffusive case, the main contribution to the error comes now from the error made when replacing the Poisson variable
P
by its expectation and from the quantified error in the stable limit theorem. The first error gives rise to a term of order
(N\delta)^{-\alpha/2},
and this is a main contribution to the error. In the diffusive case this error was negligible. Concerning the quantified rate of convergence in the stable limit theorem, we suppose that the law of the collateral jumps is heavy-tailed and rely on recent results of
[
10
]
. Finally, there is the error which is due to the bound on the Wasserstein
-{\alpha_{-}}
distance between the empirical measure
\bar{\mu}^{N}_{s}
of the limit system and
\bar{\mu}_{s}.
We rely on results obtained by
[
18
]
to control this error.

In case
1<\alpha<2,
to control the error is more complicated than in the original diffusive case. Indeed, the presence of the stochastic integral imposes that we have to deal with the small jumps of the driving stable process with respect to at least the
L^{\alpha_{+}}-
norm. And this is what we do. However, all errors related to the finite particle system can only be treated with respect to the
L^{\alpha_{-}}-
norm. So we use Hölder’s inequality repeatedly – and each time we do this, we loose with respect to the original convergence rate. The error due to time discretization is now of order
\delta^{1/{\alpha_{-}}},
which gives another important contribution. Concerning the quantified rate of convergence in the stable limit theorem, we rely on recent results of
[
9
]
.

Our paper is accompanied by a companion paper
[
25
]
which studies the case
0<\alpha<1,
in a particular framework where all jumps are positive and where big jumps do not need to be cut.

2.1
The model

Throughout this article,
S^{\alpha}=(S^{\alpha}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes an
\alpha-
stable Lévy process given by (
[
3
]
,
[
29
]
)

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}_{t}=\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{\ast}}z\tilde{M}(ds,dz),\mbox{ if }\alpha>1

(6)

\displaystyle S^{\alpha}_{t}=\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{\ast}}zM(ds,dz),\mbox{ if }\alpha<1.

Its jump measure

is a Poisson random measure on

having intensity

\nu^{\alpha}(dz)=\frac{a_{+}}{z^{\alpha+1}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z>0\}}dz+\frac{a_{-}}{|z|^{\alpha+1}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z<0\}}dz,

In what follows we will consider random variables which are distributed according to a heavy-tailed law which belongs to the strong domain of attraction of a stable law, according to the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Following Example 2 in
[
9
]
and
[
10
]

for some

,

, and

.
In particular, such a law belongs to the domain of attraction of an

Following Chapter IX, Section 8 of

of the limit stable process by

Remark 3.2
.

It is difficult to include main jumps in the case
\alpha>1.
This is due to the fact that the natural way of controlling them is by using the
L^{1}
-norm, while the small jumps of the stable integral need to be controlled in
L^{2}.
Such a difficulty has already been remarked in the paper by Carl Graham,
[
22
]
. It is possible to deal with main jumps in the presence of martingale terms which are not of bounded variation using other techniques, see e.g.
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
, where we applied a space transform and worked in
L^{1},
using the technique of
[
22
]
who uses the Burkholder-Davies-Gundy inequality in
L^{1}
to deal with the martingale term. In our present framework, the martingale is discontinuous such that this approach does not seem to be feasible. Therefore we have decided to disregard main jumps in the case
\alpha>1.

5
Convergence to the limit system

5.1
Some technical results

We provide some technical lemmas that we
state in this subsection for generic processes
X
and
\tilde{X}
with associated measures
\mu
and
\tilde{\mu}
which can be either the associated empirical distributions or the respective conditional laws given
S^{\alpha}.

In the sequel,
M
denotes the jump measure of
S^{\alpha}
and, as before,
T_{K}=\inf\{t\geq 0:|\Delta S_{t}^{\alpha}|>K\}.

Moreover, we will always assume that Assumptions
2.4
–
2.10
are satisfied.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let

M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\coloneqq\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{*}}\left(\mu^{1/\alpha}_{s-}(f)-\tilde{\mu}^{1/\alpha}_{s-}(f)\right)z\tilde{M}(ds,dz)\quad\mbox{ if }\quad\alpha>1,

and

M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\coloneqq\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{*}}\left(\mu^{1/\alpha}_{s-}(f)-\tilde{\mu}^{1/\alpha}_{s-}(f)\right)zM(ds,dz)\quad\mbox{ if }\quad\alpha<1.

i) For all
1<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}\leq 2,
we have for any
K>0,

I\!\!E\left[|M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]\leq C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[|\mu_{s}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}(f)|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds\\
\leq C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[W_{1}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

(42)

ii) For all
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<1,
we have for any
K>0,

I\!\!E\left[|M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]\leq C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[|\mu_{s}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}(f)|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds\\
\leq C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[W_{d_{\alpha_{-}}}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

(43)

Proof.

We write

M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})\eqqcolon M_{t}^{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})+M_{t}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}),

where
M_{t}^{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})
corresponds to the integral over
[0,t]\times B^{\ast}_{K}
and
M_{t}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})
to that over
[0,t]\times B^{c}_{K}.

Let us first consider the case
1<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}\leq 2.

Using first the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality and then the sub-additivity of
x\to x^{{\alpha_{+}}/2},
we obtain

I\!\!E[|M_{t}^{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq I\!\!E\left[\left|\int_{[0,t\wedge T_{K}]\times B^{\ast}_{K}}\left(\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}^{1/\alpha}_{s}(f)\right)z\tilde{M}(ds,dz)\right|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\right]\\
\leq CI\!\!E\left[\left(\int_{[0,t\wedge T_{K}]\times B^{\ast}_{K}}\left(\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)\right)^{2}z^{2}M(ds,dz)\right)^{{\alpha_{+}}/2}\right]\\
\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[|\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds\int_{B^{\ast}_{K}}\frac{|z|^{{\alpha_{+}}}}{|z|^{1+\alpha}}dz\\
=C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}]ds.

As for
M_{t}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})
, using Jensen’s inequality,

I\!\!E[|M_{t}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq I\!\!E\left[\left|\int_{0}^{t}(\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f))\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}ds\int_{B^{c}_{K}}\frac{z}{|z|^{1+\alpha}}dz\right|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\right]\\
\leq C_{t}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[|\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

Now, in both previous bounds we use the Lipschitz property of
z\mapsto z^{1/\alpha}
on
[\underline{f},\infty)
to obtain the first inequality in (
42
) and we use (
9
) to obtain the second bound in (
42
):

I\!\!E\left[|M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]\leq CI\!\!E\left[|M_{t}^{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]+CI\!\!E\left[|M_{t}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]\\
\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|\mu_{s}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}(f)|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}]ds\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[W_{1}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

Let now
0<\alpha<1
so that
M_{t}^{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}),
being a non-compensated integral, equals zero on the event
\{t<T_{K}\}.
Using that
z\to z^{1/\alpha}
is Lipschitz on
[0,|\!|f|\!|_{\infty}],
we obtain

\displaystyle I\!\!E[|M_{t}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I\!\!E[|M_{t}^{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle I\!\!E\left[\int_{[0,t\wedge T_{K}]\times B^{\ast}_{K}}\left|\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}^{1/\alpha}_{s}(f)\right||z|M(ds,dz)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle CI\!\!E\left[\int_{[0,t]}\left|\mu_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}^{1/\alpha}(f)\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}ds\int_{B^{\ast}_{K}}\frac{|z|}{|z|^{\alpha+1}}dz\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}I\!\!E\left[\int_{[0,t]}\left|\mu_{s}(f)-\tilde{\mu}_{s}(f)\right|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}ds\right].

Finally, using (
10
), this last expression is in turn upper bounded by

C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[W_{d_{\alpha_{-}}}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

B_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}[b(X_{s},\mu_{s})-b(\tilde{X}_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})]ds.

i) If
1<\alpha<2,
then for any
1<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}\leq 2
and for any
K>0,

I\!\!E[|B_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq\\
C_{t}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}I\!\!E\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\left(|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}|^{{\alpha_{+}}}+W_{1}^{{\alpha_{+}}}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\right)ds.\\
I\!\!E\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}\left(|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}|+W_{1}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\right)ds.\end{array}\right.

ii) If
0<\alpha<1,
then for any
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha
and for any
K>0,

I\!\!E[|B_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq CI\!\!E\int_{0}^{t}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}(d_{\alpha_{-}}(X_{s},\tilde{X}_{s})+W_{d_{\alpha_{-}}}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s}))ds.

In particular, if
X=X^{N,i},

\tilde{X}=\tilde{X}^{N,i},
where
(X^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
(\tilde{X}^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
are two systems defined on the same probability space and
(X^{N,i},\tilde{X}^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
is exchangeable, and if moreover
\mu=\mu^{N,X}
,
\tilde{\mu}=\tilde{\mu}^{N,X}
are the empirical measures of
X
and
\tilde{X},
then, for any
1<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}\leq 2
and for any
K>0,

I\!\!E[|B_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}]ds,\\
\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|X_{s}-\tilde{X}_{s}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}]ds,\end{array}\right.

(44)

and, for any
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<1
and for any
K>0,

I\!\!E[|B_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[d_{\alpha_{-}}(X_{s},\tilde{X}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T_{K}\}}]ds.

(45)

Proof.

The inequalities of item i) and item ii) follow immediately from the Lipschitz property of
b,
using moreover the fact that
b
is also bounded in case
\alpha>1.

Equations (
44
) and (
45
) follow from the fact that

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(X^{N,i}_{s},\tilde{X}^{N,i}_{s})}
is a coupling of
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X^{N,i}_{s}}
and
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\tilde{X}_{s}^{N,i}}
and the exchangeability of
(X^{N,i},\tilde{X}^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}.

Of course both inequalities hold also without indicator, but in the sequel we need them in this form.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\alpha<1
, let

\Psi_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})\coloneqq\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left[\psi(X_{s-},\mu_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq f(X_{s-})\}}-\psi(\tilde{X}_{s-},\tilde{\mu}_{v-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq f(\tilde{X}_{s-})\}}\right]\bar{\pi}(ds,dz).

Then, for any
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha
and any
K>0,

I\!\!E[|\Psi_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[(d_{\alpha_{-}}(X_{s},\tilde{X}_{s})+W_{d_{\alpha_{-}}}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s}))\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

(46)

In particular, if
X=X^{N,i},

\tilde{X}=\tilde{X}^{N,i},
where
(X^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
(\tilde{X}^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
are two systems defined on the same probability space and
(X^{N,i},\tilde{X}^{N,i})_{i=1}^{N}
is exchangeable, and if moreover
\mu=\mu^{N,X}
,
\tilde{\mu}=\tilde{\mu}^{N,X}
are the empirical measures of
X
and
\tilde{X},

I\!\!E[|\Psi_{t}(X,\tilde{X},\mu,\tilde{\mu})|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[d_{\alpha_{-}}(X_{s},\tilde{X}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T_{K}\}}\right]ds.

(47)

Proof.

The proof is analogous to the one of Lemma
5.2
, using Assumption
2.9
and using the boundedness and Lipschitz continuity of
f
and the Kantorovitch-Rubinstein duality.

∎

5.2
Introducing an auxiliary process

In what follows, to clearly distinguish the empirical measures of the respective auxiliary processes that we shall consider, we write
\mu^{N,X}

for the empirical measure of the finite system
(X^{N,i})_{1\leq i\leq N}
(see (
7
)).

Based on Theorem
4.1
, we
introduce for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{\ast}
,
i=1,\dots,N,
the auxiliary process

Y^{N,i}_{t}=X^{i}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X^{N,i}_{s},\mu^{N,X}_{s})ds+\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(X^{N,i}_{s-},\mu^{N,X}_{{s-}})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq f(X^{N,i}_{s-})\}}\bar{\pi}^{i}(ds,dz)\\
+\int_{[0,t]}\left(\mu^{N,X}_{s-}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}dS^{N,\alpha}_{s},

(48)

where we recall that
\psi(\cdot)\equiv 0
in case
\alpha>1.
Let
T_{K}^{N}=\inf\{t\geq 0:|\Delta S_{t}^{N,\alpha}|>K\}.
In what follows,
C_{t}
denotes a constant (that may change from line to line) which is non-decreasing as a function of
t
, and
r_{t}(N,\delta)
is given in Theorem
2.14
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Grant Assumptions
2.4
–
2.10
. Then, on an extension of
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbf{P})
depending on
N
and
\delta,

i)

if
\alpha>1,
then we have for any
{\alpha_{-}},{\alpha_{+}}
such that
1<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}<2,

I\!\!E[|X_{t}^{N,i}-Y^{N,i}_{t}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C_{t}r_{t}^{X,Y},

where

r_{t}^{X,Y}\coloneqq\left(\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\right)^{1/{\alpha_{+}}}\left(N^{1-{\alpha_{-}}/\alpha}\delta\right)^{1/{\alpha_{+}}}+r_{t}(N,\delta);

ii)

if
0<\alpha<1,
then we have for any
{\alpha_{-}}
such that
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<1,
for any
K>0,

I\!\!E[d_{\alpha_{-}}(X_{t}^{N,i},Y^{N,i}_{t})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}r_{t}(N,\delta).

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

Clearly,

X_{t}^{N,i}=Y_{t}^{N,i}+R_{t}^{N}-E_{t}^{N,i}-M_{t}^{N},

where
R_{t}^{N}
is defined in Theorem
4.1
,
M_{t}^{N}\coloneqq M_{t}(\mu^{N,X},\mu_{\tau}^{N,X})
as in Lemma
5.1
and

E^{N,i}_{t}\coloneqq\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}\frac{u}{N^{1/\alpha}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq f(X^{N,i}_{s-})\}}\pi^{i}(ds,dz,du).

(49)

Since
f
is bounded, in case
\alpha>1,
we upperbound

I\!\!E[|E^{N,i}_{t}|]\leq\frac{C}{N^{1/\alpha}}I\!\!E\left[\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}|u|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\}}\pi^{i}(ds,dz,du)\right]\leq CtN^{-1/\alpha},

(50)

whereas if
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<1,
using sub-additivity,

I\!\!E[|E^{N,i}_{t}|^{\alpha_{-}}]\leq\frac{C}{N^{{\alpha_{-}}/\alpha}}I\!\!E\left[\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}}|u|^{\alpha_{-}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\}}\pi^{i}(ds,dz,du)\right]\leq CtN^{-{\alpha_{-}}/\alpha},

(51)

Finally, since
N\delta\to\infty,
it is easy to see that both upper bounds (
50
),(
51
) are bounded by
Cr_{t}(N,\delta).

Let
1<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}<2.
Then using Lemma
5.1
, Jensen’s inequality, exchangeability, the boundedness and Lipschitz continuity of
f
and item ii) of Proposition
4.4
,

\displaystyle I\!\!E\left[|M^{N}_{t}|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[\left|\mu_{s}^{N,X}(f)-\mu_{\tau_{s}}^{N,X}(f)\right|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\right]ds

(52)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|f(X^{N,1}_{s})-f(X^{N,1}_{\tau_{s}})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}]ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|X^{N,1}_{s}-X^{N,1}_{\tau_{s}}|^{\alpha_{-}}]ds

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{t}\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}N^{1-{\alpha_{-}}/\alpha}\delta.

To go from the second line above to the third line, we have used that, since
f
is bounded,
|f(X^{N,1}_{s})-f(X^{N,1}_{\tau_{s}})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\leq C|f(X^{N,1}_{s})-f(X^{N,1}_{\tau_{s}})|^{{\alpha_{-}}},
which is in turn upper bounded by

C\|f\|_{Lip}|X^{N,1}_{s}-X^{N,1}_{\tau_{s}}|^{{\alpha_{-}}}.

Remember that Theorem
4.1
gives

I\!\!E[|R_{t}^{N}|]\leq C_{t}r_{t}(N,\delta)
in case
\alpha>1.

Using this bound and collecting (
52
), (
50
) we obtain the claim
i).

Whereas if
0<\alpha<1,
the claim
ii)
follows from upper bounding
d_{\alpha_{-}}(X_{t}^{N,i},Y^{N,i}_{t})\leq|\!|R_{t}^{N}|\!|_{d_{\alpha_{-}}}+|E_{t}^{N,i}|^{\alpha_{-}}+|M_{t}^{N}|,
using then Theorem
4.1
, (
51
), Lemma
5.1
, exchangeability and Proposition
4.4
:

I\!\!E\left[|M_{t}^{N}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}\right]\leq C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[\left|\mu_{s}^{N,X}(f)-\mu_{\tau_{s}}^{N,X}(f)\right|\right]ds\\
\leq C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|f(X^{N,1}_{s})-f(X^{N,1}_{\tau_{s}})|]ds\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}N^{1-{\alpha_{-}}/\alpha}\delta,

noticing that this last term is upper bounded by
\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}r_{t}(N,\delta).

∎

Proposition 5.5
.

Grant Assumptions
2.4
–
2.10
. Let
Y^{N}
given by (
48
) and

r_{t}^{X,Y}
given by Proposition
5.4
. Write
\mu^{N,Y}
for the empirical measure of
Y^{N}.
Then
Y^{N}
can be represented as

Y^{N,i}_{t}=X^{i}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(Y^{N,i}_{s},\mu^{N,Y}_{s})ds+\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(Y^{N,i}_{s-},\mu^{N,Y}_{s-})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq f(Y^{N,i}_{s-})\}}\bar{\pi}^{i}(ds,dz)\\
+\int_{[0,t]}\left(\mu^{N,Y}_{s-}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}dS^{N,\alpha}_{s}+R_{t}^{N,Y},\quad\mbox{and}\quad

i)
if
1<\alpha<2,
then for any
1<\alpha<{\alpha_{+}}\leq 2,

I\!\!E[|R_{t}^{N,Y}|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}r_{t}^{X,Y};

ii) if
0<\alpha<1,
then for any
K>0,

I\!\!E[|R_{t}^{N,Y}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C_{t}{\left(\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right)^{2}r_{t}(N,\delta)}.

Proof.

Let
1<\alpha<2.
Then
R_{t}^{N,Y}=B^{N}_{t}+M^{N}_{t},

with
M^{N}_{t}\coloneqq M_{t}(\mu^{N,X},\mu^{N,Y})

and

B^{N}_{t}=B_{t}(X^{N,i},Y^{N,i},\mu^{N,X},\mu^{N,Y}).

Using first (
44
) of Lemma
5.2
and then Proposition
5.4
, we obtain

I\!\!E[|B^{N}_{t}|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|X^{N,i}_{s}-Y^{N,i}_{s}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}]ds\leq Ctr_{t}^{X,Y}.

(53)

Moreover, using Lemma
5.1
, Jensen’s inequality, the exchangeability of
(X^{N,i},Y^{N,i})_{i}
and finally once more the boundedness and the Lipschitz continuity of
f,

\begin{split}I\!\!E\left[|M^{N}_{t}|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}\right]&\leq C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[\left|\mu^{N,X}_{s}(f)-\mu^{N,Y}_{s}(f)\right|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}\right]ds\\
&\leq C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|f(X^{N,i}_{s})-f(Y^{N,i}_{s})|^{{\alpha_{+}}}\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}]ds\\
&\leq C\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[|X^{N,i}_{s}-Y^{N,i}_{s}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}]ds\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}}{{\alpha_{+}}-\alpha}r_{t}^{X,Y}.\end{split}

(54)

Finally, collecting (
53
) and (
54
) we obtain the claim
i).

Let now
0<{\alpha_{-}}<\alpha<1
. Clearly
R_{t}^{N,Y}=B^{N}_{t}+\Psi^{N}_{t}+M^{N}_{t},

with
M^{N}_{t}\coloneqq M_{t}(\mu^{N,X},\mu^{N,Y}),

\Psi^{N}_{t}\coloneqq\Psi_{t}(X^{N,i},Y^{N,i},\mu^{N,X},\mu^{N,Y})

and

B^{N}_{t}\coloneqq B_{t}(X^{N,i},Y^{N,i},\mu^{N,X},\mu^{N,Y}).

Now we have, by (
45
) of Lemma
5.2
and (
47
) of Lemma
5.3
respectively,

I\!\!E[|B_{t}^{N}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[d_{{\alpha_{-}}}(X_{s}^{N,i},Y_{s}^{N,i})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}]ds

and

I\!\!E[|\Psi^{N}_{t}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E[d_{{\alpha_{-}}}(X_{s}^{N,i},Y_{s}^{N,i})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}]ds,

so that, using Proposition
5.4
in both the last inequalities, we obtain

I\!\!E[(|B_{t}^{N}|+|\Psi^{N}_{t}|)\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C_{t}\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}r_{t}(N,\delta).

Finally, (
43
) implies

I\!\!E[|M^{N}_{t}|\boldsymbol{1}_{\{t<T^{N}_{K}\}}]\leq C\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\int_{0}^{t}I\!\!E\left[W_{d_{\alpha_{-}}}(\mu_{s},\tilde{\mu}_{s})\boldsymbol{1}_{\{s<T^{N}_{K}\}}\right]ds\leq C_{t}\left(\frac{K^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right)^{2}r_{t}(N,\delta),

concluding the proof.
∎

5.3
Representation for the limit system

In this subsection we prove the following representation result.

Proposition 5.6
.

Grant Assumptions
2.4
–
2.10
.
Let
\bar{X}^{N}
denote the unique solution to the limit system (
8
) driven by
S^{N,\alpha}
, and let
(\bar{X}^{N,i})_{i=1,\dots,N}
be the first
N
coordinates of this solution. Write
\mu^{N,\bar{X}^{N}}
for the empirical measure of these first
N
coordinates. Then

\bar{X}^{N,i}_{t}=X^{i}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(\bar{X}^{N,i}_{s},\mu^{N,\bar{X}^{N}}_{s})ds+\int_{[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(\bar{X}^{N,i}_{s-},\mu^{N,\bar{X}^{N}}_{s-}(f))\boldsymbol{1}_{\{z\leq f(\bar{X}^{N,i}_{s-})\}}\bar{\pi}^{i}(ds,dz)\\
+\int_{[0,t]}\left(\mu^{N,\bar{X}^{N}}_{s-}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}dS_{s}^{N,\alpha}+R_{t}^{N,\bar{X}},

where

5.6
Discussion of the convergence rate obtained in Theorem
2.14

We close this section with a discussion of the different ingredients that constitute our rates in (
11
) and (
12
). Taking formally
\alpha_{-}=\alpha,
it is clear that the main contribution to the error arises from
r_{t}(N,\delta)
. The term that involves
g(N\delta)
comes from the quantitative version of the stable central limit theorem and can presumably not be improved. The terms
(N\delta)^{-1/2}
(if
\alpha>1
) and
(N\delta)^{-\alpha_{-}/2}
(if
\alpha<1
) come from the fluctuations in the law of large numbers for the Poisson number of jumps per time interval. So they cannot be improved neither. The
\delta-
power
\delta^{1/\alpha}
(or
\delta^{\alpha_{-}/\alpha}
) comes from the fact that we discretize time and that we have to deal with the “overlap” coming from the stochastic integral against the stable process (that is, the integral between
K\delta
and
t,
where
K
is such that
K\delta\leq t<(K+1)\delta
).

Finally, the very first term in the definition of
r_{t}(N,\delta)
comes from the necessity of approximating the interaction term
A_{t}^{N}
of the finite particle system by its time-discretized version. In the
\alpha_{-}\to\alpha
limit, it is basically of order
\delta^{-1/\alpha}
if
\alpha>1
or
\delta,
if
\alpha<1,
which is reasonable.

To summarize, we believe that the term
r_{t}(N,\delta)
is rather optimal due to the above reasons.

There are other terms that come into play, like for instance the error that is due to the Wasserstein distance between the empirical measure of the limit system and its theoretical counterpart – we believe that this cannot be improved neither.

However, we see that in the case
\alpha>1,
it is
r_{t}(N,\delta)^{1/\alpha}
(in the
\alpha_{+}\to\alpha-
limit) that appears as a final rate. This is because we want to compare the finite system, which is of bounded variation, to the limit one, which is not. Roughly speaking, there are errors that have to be considered in the
L^{1}-
norm (such as the one that we control in Proposition
4.2
, in the case
\alpha>1
), and other errors that cannot be controlled in
L^{1},
but need to be controlled in another norm, for example
L^{\alpha_{+}},
for
\alpha_{+}>\alpha>1
– a control that we need to use e.g. in Lemma
5.1
to deal with the stochastic integral terms, when integrating against the stable process. To switch from one norm to another, using Hölder’s inequality, we loose with respect to the accuracy of our error. And so it seems rather clear that the final rate of convergence has no reason to be optimal in the case
\alpha>1.

\displaystyle X^{[n],K}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X^{[n-1],K}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(X^{[n-1],K}_{s},\mu^{[n-1],K}_{s})ds+\int_{[0,t]\times B^{\ast}_{K}}\!\!\left(\mu^{[n-1],K}_{s-}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}z\tilde{M}(ds,dz)

(57)

\displaystyle-M_{K}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu^{[n-1],K}_{s}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}ds,

where
\mu^{[n],K}_{s}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(X^{[n],K}_{s}\,|\,S^{\alpha})
.

Using similar arguments as in the proof of the uniqueness, we have the a priori bound

\begin{split}I\!\!E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{[n],K}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]&\leq C_{K,T}(1+I\!\!E[|X_{0}|^{2}])\end{split}

(58)

for some constant
C_{K,T}
which does only depend on the truncation level
K
and on
T,
but not on
n
.

We show now that, for any
T>0
and
t\in[0,T]
, the sequence
(X^{[n],K}_{t})_{n\geq 0}
defined in (
57
) converges a.s. to a limit
\bar{X}^{K}_{t}
.

First notice that, analogously to what we obtained in the uniqueness proof (see (
19
)),

\begin{split}I\!\!E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]&\leq C_{K,T}\int_{0}^{T}I\!\!E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,s]}\left|X^{[n],K}_{t}-X^{[n-1],K}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]ds\end{split}

(59)

for some constant
C_{K,T}
non-decreasing with respect to
T
.

Introduce for any
n\geq 0

u^{[n],K}_{T}\coloneqq I\!\!E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]

(60)

and iterate (
59
) to obtain

u^{[n],K}_{T}\leq C_{K,T}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}ds_{1}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}u^{[n-2],K}_{s_{2}}ds_{2}\leq C_{K,T}^{n}\int_{0}^{T}ds_{1}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}ds_{2}\dots\int_{0}^{s_{n-1}}ds_{n}u^{[0],K}_{s_{n}}\\
\leq\frac{T^{n}}{n!}C_{K,T}^{n}u^{[0],K}_{T},

where
u^{[0],K}_{T}=I\!\!E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{[1],K}_{t}-X^{[0],K}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C_{T}
is bounded thanks to (
58
).

This implies that

I\!\!E\left[\sum_{n\geq 0}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right|\right]\leq\sum_{n\geq 0}\sqrt{I\!\!E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left|X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right|^{2}\right]}\\
=\sum_{n\geq 0}\sqrt{u^{[n],K}_{T}}\leq\sqrt{C_{T}}\sum_{n\geq 0}C_{K,T}^{n/2}\frac{T^{n/2}}{\sqrt{n!}}<+\infty,

such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sum_{n\geq 0}\left|X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right|<+\infty\qquad\text{a.s..}

Hence the series
\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right)
converges a.s. and we can define a.s.

\bar{X}^{K}_{t}\coloneqq X_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(X^{[n+1],K}_{t}-X^{[n],K}_{t}\right).

The next step is to prove that the a.s. limit of
(X^{[n],K}_{t})_{n\geq 0}
,
\bar{X}^{K}_{t}
, solves the same equation as the process
\bar{X}_{t}
on
[0,T_{K}[,
that is, that almost surely,

\displaystyle\bar{X}^{K}_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b(\bar{X}^{K}_{s},\bar{\mu}^{K}_{s})ds+\int_{[0,t]\times B^{\ast}_{K}}\left(\bar{\mu}^{K}_{s-}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}z\tilde{M}(ds,dz)

(61)

\displaystyle\qquad-M_{K}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\bar{\mu}^{K}_{s}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}ds,

where
\bar{\mu}^{K}_{s}\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(\bar{X}^{K}_{s}\,|\,S^{\alpha})
.
This follows by taking the
n\to+\infty
limit in (
57
), since

•

by Assumption
2.4
b)
,
I\!\!E[|b(X^{[n],K}_{t},\mu^{[n],K}_{t})-b(\bar{X}^{K}_{t},\bar{\mu}^{K}_{t})|]\leq CI\!\!E[|X^{[n],K}_{t}-\bar{X}^{K}_{t}|]
.
On the other hand,
I\!\!E[|X^{[n],K}_{t}-\bar{X}^{K}_{t}|]\to 0
by dominated convergence.
Hence, from this
L^{1}
convergence of
X^{[n],K}_{t}
to
\bar{X}^{K}_{t}
, we obtain
L^{1}
convergence of
b(X^{[n],K}_{t},\mu^{[n],K}_{t})
to
b(\bar{X}^{K}_{t},\bar{\mu}^{K}_{t})
. This latter yields in turn a.s. convergence, up to a subsequence.

•

\mu^{[n],K}_{t}(f)=I\!\!E\left[f(X^{[n],K}_{t})\,|\,S^{\alpha}\right]\to I\!\!E\left[f(\bar{X}^{K}_{t})\,|\,S^{\alpha}\right]\eqqcolon\bar{\mu}^{K}_{t}(f)
a.s.
Moreover, we have that
|\mu^{[n],K}_{t}(f)|\leq|\!|f|\!|_{\infty}
by Assumption
2.5
b)
, hence we obtain the
L^{2}
-convergence of the stochastic integrals against
\tilde{M},
whence the almost sure convergence, once again for a subsequence. The convergence of
\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mu^{[n],K}_{s}(f)\right)^{1/\alpha}ds
follows similarly.

As a consequence of the above construction we dispose of a family of processes
(\bar{X}^{K})_{K\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
such that

(i)

for any
K
,
\bar{X}^{K}
solves (
61
);

(ii)

\bar{X}^{K+1}_{t}=\bar{X}^{K}_{t}
a.s. for all
t\in[0,T_{K}[,
since both are solution of the same equation on
[0,T_{K}[.

So, letting
T_{0}=0
, the following process is well-defined

\bar{X}_{t}\coloneqq\sum_{K\geq 1}\boldsymbol{1}_{[T_{K-1},T_{K}[}(t)\bar{X}^{K}_{t},

and it solves (
8
) on
[0,T]
for any
T\geq 0
.

The same construction thanks to a Picard iteration works also in the case
\alpha<1,
using
L^{1}
-norm instead of
L^{2}
-norm now. Details are omitted.

6.4
Proof of Remark
2.16

Case
\boldsymbol{1<\alpha<2}
.

According to Theorem
2.14
, the error term is given, up to a constant, by (
11
), where we have to choose
\delta=\delta(N)
such that
N\delta\to\infty.

To understand formally what is the leading term in our error, we let
{\alpha_{-}}\uparrow\alpha
and
{\alpha_{+}}\downarrow\alpha
. In the limit
{\alpha_{-}}={\alpha_{+}}=\alpha
, we are left with an error term given by (up to a constant and to the common power
1/\alpha
)

\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}}+\delta^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left(g(N\delta)+(N\delta)^{-1/2}\right).

Since
g(x)=x^{-B}
for some
B>0
, if we suppose
\delta=N^{-\eta}
for some
\eta\in(0,1)
, we can write

\delta^{\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}}\left(g(N\delta)+(N\delta)^{-1/2}\right)=N^{\eta(1-1/\alpha+B)}g(N)+N^{\eta(3/2-1/\alpha)}N^{-1/2},

which is an increasing function of
\eta.

On the other hand,
\delta^{\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}}
is a decreasing function of
\eta,
and so we have to choose
\eta
such that the two terms which are left are equal, that is,

\delta^{\frac{1-\alpha+\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}=g(N\delta)+(N\delta)^{-1/2}.

The leading term between
g(N\delta)
and
(N\delta)^{-1/2}
will asymptotically behave as
(N\delta)^{-C}
, with either
C=B
or
C=1/2
.
Then we have to solve

\delta^{\frac{1-\alpha+\alpha^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}}=N^{-C}\delta^{-C},\mbox{ which gives }\delta=N^{-\frac{C\alpha^{2}}{1-\alpha+C\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{2}}}.

By the equality we imposed, and re-introducing the
1/\alpha
power, the rate will be

\delta^{1/\alpha^{3}}=N^{-\frac{C}{(1-\alpha+C\alpha^{2}+\alpha^{2})\alpha}}.

Consider now the explicit form of the function
g
given in (
26
). We give the explicit rate (i.e. we choose
C
) in the two cases:

Case 1:

\gamma<2-\alpha
. Then, if
\gamma<\alpha/2
,
C=\gamma/\alpha
and the rate is

N^{-\frac{\gamma}{\alpha^{2}(1-\alpha+\gamma\alpha+\alpha^{2})}},

whereas, if
\gamma\in\left(\frac{\alpha}{2},2-\alpha\right)
,
C=1/2,
such that the rate is

N^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha\left(1-\alpha+\frac{3}{2}\alpha^{2}\right)}}.

Case 2:

\gamma>2-\alpha
. Then, for
\alpha\in(1,\frac{4}{3}),

C=1/2,
such that the rate is once more

N^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha\left(1-\alpha+\frac{3}{2}\alpha^{2}\right)}}.

If
\alpha>\frac{4}{3}
, then
C=\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha}
, such that the rate is

N^{-\frac{2-\alpha}{\alpha^{2}(1+\alpha)}}.

Case
\boldsymbol{\alpha<1}
.

According to Theorem
2.14
, the error term equals, up to a constant, the expression (

). Taking
{\alpha_{-}}=\alpha

given in (

Hence we are left with an error proportional to

Take now
\delta=N^{-\eta}

is an increasing function of

whose leading order has the form

Therefore, we impose

We conclude by choosing

If
