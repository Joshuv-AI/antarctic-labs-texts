A New Fractional Derivative and its Fractional Integral with some applications
Fahed Zulfeqarr, Amit Ujlayan, Priyanka Ahuja

Abstract
. A new derivative, called deformable derivative, is introduced here which is equivalent to ordinary derivative in the sense that one implies other. The deformable derivative is defined using limit approach like that of ordinary one but with respect to a parameter varying over unit interval. Thus it could also be regarded as a fractional derivative. Reason of calling it as deformable derivative is because of its intrinsic property of continuously deforming function to derivative. This is substantiated by its linear connection to function and its derivative. Besides discussing some of its basic properties, we discover the forms of Rolle’s, Mean Value and Taylor’s theorems. The fundamental theorem of calculus for this fractional derivative could be taken as definition of its fractional integral. As a theoretical application some fractional differential equations are solved.

Definition 0.1
.

Let
\displaystyle f(t)

\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant 1

(*)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{(1+\epsilon\beta)f(t+\epsilon\alpha)-f(t)}{\epsilon},\quad\mbox{where}\;\;\alpha+\beta=1.

If this limit exists, we denote it by
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(t)

\displaystyle\alpha=0,1

\displaystyle\alpha=0

\displaystyle D^{0}f(t)=f(t)

\displaystyle\alpha=1

\displaystyle Df(t)=f^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle 0<\alpha\leqslant 1.
The deformable derivative with a given

1.
Preliminary Results

This section exhibits a relation of deformable derivative with function and its derivative. This is where the name deformable derivative is taken from. The relation further exposes the interesting fact about the graph of deformable derivative lying linearly between that of function and derivative.

The first result is quite natural and asserts that
differentiability implies
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiability
. The proof connects both operators.

Theorem 1.1
.

A differentiable
\displaystyle f
at a point
\displaystyle t\in(a,b)
is always
\displaystyle\alpha
- differentiable at that point for any
\displaystyle\alpha
. Moreover in this case we have

(**)

D^{\alpha}f(t)=\beta f(t)+\alpha Df(t),\quad\mbox{where\;\;}\alpha+\beta=1.

Proof.

By definition we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left(1+\epsilon\beta\right)f(t+\alpha\epsilon)-f(t)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{f(t+\alpha\epsilon)-f(t)}{\epsilon}-\beta f(t+\alpha\epsilon)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha\cdot Df(t)-\beta\cdot\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}f(t+\alpha\epsilon).

Both the terms exist as
\displaystyle f
, being differentiable at
\displaystyle t
is continuous as well. Hence theorem follows.
∎

The second result of the section that is also natural is about
Does
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiability imply continuity
? Answer is affirmative. However to prove the claim we need an auxiliary result concerning to locally bounded function. A function is said to be
locally bounded
at a point if it is bounded in some neighbourhood of that point. Precisely a function
\displaystyle f
defined on
\displaystyle(a,b)
is locally bounded at
\displaystyle t
if there are positive numbers
\displaystyle M\;\&\;\delta
such that

\bigl\lvert f(t+\epsilon)\bigr\rvert\leqslant M\quad\mbox{whenever}\;\;\bigl\lvert\epsilon\bigr\rvert<\delta.

Here
\displaystyle\delta
is chosen sufficiently small so that
\displaystyle~t+\epsilon\in(a,b)
.

Lemma 1.2
.

Suppose
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable in
\displaystyle(a,b)
with respect to some
\displaystyle\alpha
. Then
\displaystyle f
is locally bounded there.

Proof.

Suppose
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable at
\displaystyle t
, there exist a number
\displaystyle\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\bigl\lvert(1+\epsilon\beta)f(t+\epsilon\alpha)-f(t)-\epsilon\cdot D^{\alpha}f(t)\bigr\rvert\leqslant\bigl\lvert\epsilon\bigr\rvert,\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{whenever}\;\;\bigl\lvert\epsilon\bigr\rvert<\delta

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\bigl\lvert(1+\epsilon\beta)f(t+\epsilon\alpha)\bigr\rvert\leqslant|\epsilon|+\bigl\lvert f(t)+\epsilon\cdot D^{\alpha}f(t)\bigr\rvert,\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{whenever}\;\;\bigl\lvert\epsilon\bigr\rvert<\delta

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leqslant|\epsilon|\left(1+\bigl\lvert D^{\alpha}f(t)\bigr\rvert\right)+\bigl\lvert f(t)\bigr\rvert,\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{whenever}\;\;\bigl\lvert\epsilon\bigr\rvert<\delta

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad\bigl\lvert f(t+\epsilon\alpha)\bigr\rvert\leqslant\frac{|\epsilon|\left(1+\bigl\lvert D^{\alpha}f(t)\bigr\rvert\right)+\bigl\lvert f(t)\bigr\rvert}{\bigl\lvert 1+\beta\epsilon\bigr\rvert}\quad

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mbox{whenever}\;\;\bigl\lvert\epsilon\bigr\rvert<\delta

This yields that
\displaystyle f
is locally bounded at
\displaystyle t
.
∎

The next theorem establish the claim that
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiability implies continuity.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\displaystyle f
be
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable at a point
\displaystyle t
for some
\displaystyle\alpha
. Then it is continuous there.

Proof.

For continuity, it suffices to prove the following:

\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(f(t+\epsilon\alpha)-f(t)\right)=0.

The left hand side can also be written as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left(1+\epsilon\beta\right)f(t+\epsilon\alpha)-f(t)-\epsilon\beta f(t+\epsilon\alpha)}{\epsilon}\epsilon

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left(\frac{\left(1+\epsilon\beta\right)f(t+\epsilon\alpha)-f(t)}{\epsilon}\cdot\epsilon-\beta\epsilon\cdot f(t+\epsilon\alpha)\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=D^{\alpha}f(t)\cdot 0-\beta\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\epsilon f(t+\epsilon\alpha)=0,\quad(\mbox{by hypothesis})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=-\beta\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\epsilon f(t+\epsilon\alpha)=0.\quad(\mbox{by using lemma \ref{locally.bounded}})

This completes theorem.
∎

A strong version of the theorem
1.3

Corollary 1.4
.

An
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable function
\displaystyle f
defined in
\displaystyle(a,b)
is differentiable as well.

Proof.

For the existence of derivative we use its definition

\displaystyle\displaystyle Df(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(t+\alpha\epsilon)-f(t)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left(1+\epsilon\beta\right)f(t+\alpha\epsilon)-f(t)-\epsilon\beta f(t+\alpha\epsilon)}{\epsilon}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad Df(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left(1+\epsilon\beta\right)f(t+\alpha\epsilon)-f(t)}{\epsilon}-\beta\cdot\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}f(t+\alpha\epsilon)\right)

By using hypothesis and theorem
1.3
, we get the result done.
∎

Theorem 1.5

Let
\displaystyle f

\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable if and only if it is differentiable.

We make a remark here that over the interval
\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle\alpha
-derivative with respect to one particular value of

\displaystyle\alpha
-derivative with respect to all other values of
\displaystyle\alpha

Though the most important case for

\displaystyle\alpha\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\alpha\in(n,n+1]

\displaystyle n

Definition 1.7
.

Suppose
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle n
-times differentiable at
\displaystyle t\in(a,b).
For given
\displaystyle\alpha\in(n,n+1]

D^{\alpha}f(t)=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\left(1+\epsilon\{\beta\}\right))D^{n}f\left(t+\epsilon\{\alpha\}\right)-D^{n}f(t)}{\epsilon}

Theorem 2.1

The operator
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}

Linearity:
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}\left(af+bg\right)=aD^{\alpha}f+bD^{\alpha}g

\displaystyle(

Commutativity:
\displaystyle D^{\alpha_{1}}\cdot D^{\alpha_{2}}=D^{\alpha_{2}}\cdot D^{\alpha_{1}}

3.
Some Useful Theorems on deformable Derivative

In this section we extend
\displaystyle Rolle^{\prime}s
, Mean Value and Taylor’s theorems to deformable derivative with respect to
\displaystyle\alpha
.

Theorem 3.1
.

(Rolle’s theorem on deformable derivative)

Let
\displaystyle f:[a,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function satisfying:

\displaystyle(
i
\displaystyle)

\displaystyle f
is continuous on
\displaystyle[a,b]

\displaystyle(
ii
\displaystyle)

\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable in
\displaystyle(a,b)

\displaystyle(
iii
\displaystyle)

\displaystyle f(a)=f(b).

Then, there exists a point
\displaystyle c\in(a,b)
such that
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(c)=\beta f(c)
.

Proof.

By applying corollary
1.5
,
\displaystyle f
is differentiable in
\displaystyle(a,b)
. Thus
\displaystyle f
satisfies all conditions of classical Rolle’s theorem. Then there is a point
\displaystyle c\in(a,b)
such that
\displaystyle Df(c)=0
. Hence using equation (**) of theorem
1.1
, we have
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(c)=\beta f(c)
.
∎

Mean Value theorem is a consequence of Rolle’s theorem so is case with deformable derivative.

Theorem 3.2
.

(Mean Value theorem on deformable derivative)

Let
\displaystyle f:[a,b]\longrightarrow R
be a function satisfying:

\displaystyle(
i
\displaystyle)

\displaystyle f
is continuous on
\displaystyle[a,b]

\displaystyle(
ii
\displaystyle)

\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle\alpha
- differentiable in
\displaystyle(a,b)
.

Then, there exists a point
\displaystyle c\in(a,b)
such that

D^{\alpha}f(c)=\beta f(c)+\alpha\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.

Proof.

We consider a function
\displaystyle g
defined by:

g(x)=f(t)-f(a)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}t.

Notice that
\displaystyle g(t)
satisfies all the conditions of Rolle’s Theorem
3.1
, there exists
\displaystyle c\in(a,b)
such that
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}g(c)=\beta g(c).
This yields the desired expression in the theorem.
∎

Theorem 3.3
.

(Taylor’s theorem)
Suppose
\displaystyle f
is
\displaystyle n
-times
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable such that all
\displaystyle\alpha
-derivatives are continuous on
\displaystyle[a,a+h].
Then

f(a+h)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{h^{k}}{k!\alpha^{k}}\left(D^{\alpha}_{k}f(a)-\beta\frac{(1-\theta)^{k-n+1}h}{\alpha n}D^{\alpha}_{k}f(a+\theta h)\right)+\frac{h^{n}}{n!\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{n}f(a+\theta h)

where
\displaystyle D_{k}^{\alpha}=D^{\alpha}D^{\alpha}\cdots D^{\alpha}
,
\displaystyle(k
-times
\displaystyle)
,
\displaystyle 0<\theta<1
.

Proof.

Consider a function
\displaystyle\phi
defined by:

(1)

\phi(t)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{(a+h-t)^{k}}{k!\alpha^{k}}D^{\alpha}_{k}f(t)+\frac{A}{n!\alpha^{n}}(a+h-t)^{n},

where
\displaystyle A
is a constant to be chosen
\displaystyle A
such that
\displaystyle\phi(a+h)=\phi(a)
. This yields

(2)

\frac{A}{n!\alpha^{n}}h^{n}=f(a+h)-\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{h^{k}}{k!\alpha^{k}}D^{\alpha}_{k}f(a)

Now by hypothesis,
\displaystyle\phi
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable in
\displaystyle(a,a+h)
. Using part (d) of theorem
2.1
, the
\displaystyle\alpha
-derivative
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}\phi
is given by

(3)

D^{\alpha}\phi(t)=\frac{(a+h-t)^{n-1}}{\alpha^{n-1}(n-1)!}D^{\alpha}_{n}f(t)+\frac{A}{\alpha^{n}n!}\left(\beta(a+h-t)^{n}-\alpha n(a+h-t)^{n-1}\right)

Hence
\displaystyle\phi
satisfies all the conditions of Rolle’s Theorem
3.1
. So there is some
\displaystyle\theta\in(0,1)
such that

D^{\alpha}\phi(a+\theta h)=\beta\phi(a+\theta h).

Using equations (
\displaystyle 1
), (
\displaystyle 2
) and (
\displaystyle 3
), we have

f(a+h)=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{h^{k}}{k!\alpha^{k}}\left(D^{\alpha}_{k}f(a)-\beta\frac{(1-\theta)^{k-n+1}h}{\alpha n}D^{\alpha}_{k}f(a+\theta h)\right)+\frac{h^{n}}{n!\alpha}D^{\alpha}_{n}f(a+\theta h)

This completes the theorem.
∎

4.
Fractional Integral

Fractional integral, being an inverse operator to fractional derivative, plays equally important role as that of fractional derivative in the field of fractional calculus. The section defines a fractional integral as an inverse operator for deformable derivative. Some basic properties of this fractional integral are also discussed. All functions considered in this section are assumed to be continuous.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle f
be a continuous function defined on
\displaystyle[a,b]
. We define
\displaystyle\alpha
-fractional Integral
of
\displaystyle f,~
denoted by
\displaystyle I^{\alpha}_{a}f,
by the integral

(#)

I^{\alpha}_{a}f(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\frac{-\beta}{\alpha}t}\int_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}x}f(x)dx,\quad\mbox{where}\;\alpha+\beta=1,\;\alpha\in(0,1].

Some basic properties of this fractional integral are contained in next theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

The operator
\displaystyle I^{\alpha}_{a}
possesses the following properties:

\displaystyle(
a
\displaystyle)

Linearity:
\displaystyle I^{\alpha}_{a}\left(bf+cg\right)=bI^{\alpha}_{a}f+cI^{\alpha}_{a}g.

\displaystyle(
b
\displaystyle)

Commutativity:

\displaystyle I_{a}^{\alpha_{1}}I_{a}^{\alpha_{2}}=I_{a}^{\alpha_{2}}I_{a}^{\alpha_{1}}
,
where
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}=1
,
\displaystyle i=1,2.

Proof.

Linearity readily follows from definition (#). For commutativity, we consider

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{a}^{\alpha_{1}}I_{a}^{\alpha_{2}}f(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{a}^{\alpha_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\theta}f(\theta)d\theta\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}x}\left(\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}x}\int\limits_{a}^{x}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\theta}f(\theta)d\theta\right)dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}\int\limits_{a}^{x}e^{(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}})x}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\theta}f(\theta)\;d\theta dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}\int\limits_{\theta}^{t}e^{(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}})x}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\theta}f(\theta)\;dxd\theta

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}}e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\theta}f(\theta)\left(\int\limits_{\theta}^{t}e^{(\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}})x}dx\right)d\theta

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}}\left(e^{-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}\theta}f(\theta)d\theta-e^{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}t}\int\limits_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\theta}f(\theta)d\theta\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{1}{\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}-\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}}\left(\alpha_{2}I^{\alpha_{2}}_{a}-\alpha_{1}I^{\alpha_{1}}_{a}\right)f(t)

Interchanging the role of
\displaystyle\alpha_{1}
and
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}
, we have

I_{a}^{\alpha_{2}}I_{a}^{\alpha_{1}}f(t)=\frac{1}{\beta_{2}\alpha_{1}-\beta_{1}\alpha_{2}}\left(\alpha_{1}I^{\alpha_{1}}_{a}-\alpha_{2}I^{\alpha_{2}}_{a}\right)f(t)=I_{a}^{\alpha_{1}}I_{a}^{\alpha_{2}}f(t)

This completes the proof
∎

The next theorem is a version of fundamental theorem of calculus that roughly says that fraction integral
\displaystyle I_{a}^{\alpha}
is an inverse operation of
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiation
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

(Inverse Property)
Let
\displaystyle f
be a continuous function defined on
\displaystyle[a,b].
Then
\displaystyle I_{a}^{\alpha}f
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable in
\displaystyle(a,b)
. In fact, we have
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}\left(I_{a}^{\alpha}f(x)\right)=f(x).
Conversely Suppose
\displaystyle g
is a continuous anti-
\displaystyle\alpha
-derivative of
\displaystyle f
over
\displaystyle(a,b)
, that is
\displaystyle g=D^{\alpha}f
. Then we have

I^{\alpha}_{a}\left(D^{\alpha}f(t)\right)=I^{\alpha}_{a}\left(g(t)\right)=f(t)-e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}(a-t)}f(a).

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle f
is given to be continuous so in view of theorem
1.5
,
\displaystyle I^{\alpha}_{a}f
is
\displaystyle\alpha
-differentiable. If we set
\displaystyle g=I^{\alpha}_{a}f
then we have

D^{\alpha}\left(I_{a}^{\alpha}f(t)\right)=D^{\alpha}g(t)=\alpha Dg(t)+\beta g(t)

We know that a particular solution of the differential equation:
\displaystyle\alpha Dg+\beta g=f
is given as

g(t)=\frac{1}{\alpha}e^{\frac{-\beta}{\alpha}t}\int_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}x}f(x)dx.

Thus first part of theorem is complete. For the second part, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle D^{\alpha}f(t)=\alpha Df(t)+\beta f(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad I^{\alpha}_{a}g(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha I^{\alpha}_{a}\left(Df(t)\right)+\beta I^{\alpha}_{a}f(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle e^{\frac{-\beta}{\alpha}t}\int_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}x}f^{\prime}(x)dx+\beta I^{\alpha}_{a}f(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle e^{\frac{-\beta}{\alpha}t}\left(\left[e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}x}f(x)\right]_{a}^{t}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\int_{a}^{t}e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}x}f(x)dx\right)+\beta I^{\alpha}_{a}f(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad I^{\alpha}_{a}g(t)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(t)-e^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha}(a-t)}f(a).

This completes the second part.

5.
Applications to Fractional Differential Equations

We solve some simple linear fractional differential equations using deformable derivative as
\displaystyle D^{\alpha}
operator. In first example we discuss method of solving homogeneous linear, while in second non-homogeneous linear fractional differential equations.

Example 5.1
.

Consider the fractional differential equation:

D^{\alpha}y(t)+P(t)y(t)=0,

where
\displaystyle P(t)
is continuous. Using expression (**), the equation get transformed to

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha Dy+\beta y+P(t)y

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad Dy+\frac{\left(\beta+P(t)\right)}{\alpha}y

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle 0.

Which is simple first order linear ordinary differential equation whose general solution is given by

y=Ce^{\displaystyle{\frac{-\left(\beta t+\int P(t)dt\right)}{\alpha}}}

where
\displaystyle C
is arbitrary constant.

Example 5.2
.

We now consider a non-homogeneous linear fractional equation:

D^{1/2}y+y=te^{-t}.

This can be written as

\frac{1}{2}y+\frac{1}{2}Dy+y=te^{-t}\quad\Rightarrow\quad Dy+3y=2te^{-t}

Whose general solution is given by

y(t)=Ce^{-3t}+\left(t-\frac{1}{2}\right)e^{-t}

where
\displaystyle C
is a constant.

Example 5.3
.

The fractional differential equation :

D^{\alpha_{2}}[D^{\alpha_{1}}y(t)]=0

is equivalent to the following second order homogeneous differential equation:

\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}D^{2}y+(\alpha_{1}\beta_{2}+\alpha_{2}\beta_{1})Dy+\beta_{1}\beta_{2}y=0.

The roots its auxiliary equation are:

\frac{-\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}\;\mbox{and}\;\frac{-\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}.

Hence in case of distinct roots the general solution of the fractional differential equation is

y=C_{1}e^{\displaystyle{-\frac{\beta_{1}}{\alpha_{1}}t}}+C_{2}e^{\displaystyle{-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\alpha_{2}}t}},

and in case of repeated roots, we have

y=(C_{1}+C_{2}t)e^{\displaystyle{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}t}}
