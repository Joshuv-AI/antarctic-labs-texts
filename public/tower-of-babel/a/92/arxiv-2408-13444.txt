Abstract
In this correspondence, we analyze the performance of a reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)-aided communication system that involves a fluid antenna system (FAS)-enabled receiver. By applying the central limit theorem (CLT), we derive approximate expressions for the system outage probability when the RIS has a large number of elements. Also, we adopt the block-correlation channel model to simplify the outage probability expressions, reducing the computational complexity and shedding light on the impact of the number of ports. Numerical results validate the effectiveness of our analysis, especially in scenarios with a large number of RIS elements.

On the other hand, research on reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS) is going strong. RIS is particularly important in deep-fading scenarios where obstacles such as thin walls may obstruct the direct link between the transmitter and receiver

Integrating the capabilities of FAS and RIS presents a robust approach to enhance wireless communication performance, so the synergy of FAS and RIS offers a promising direction for advancing communication systems. However, the performance of FAS-RIS is not well understood, and remains unexplored. Motivated by this, this correspondence explores a RIS-assisted communication system that includes a FAS-enabled receiver. Here, FAS is used to improve the quality of received signals, while RIS is employed to re-establish the LoS link when it is originally blocked. Using the central limit theorem (CLT), we derive the system outage probability when the number of RIS elements is large. Also, the block-correlation approximation technique in

Communication Model

In the communication progress, the BS emits the RF signal
\displaystyle s\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,1)
, and then the RIS reflects the transmitted signal to the receiver. In this work, the Rayleigh fading channel is considered, which is suitable for non-light-of-sight scenarios, such as BS-to-vehicle scenarios and vehicle-to-vehicle scenarios.
Given the absence of the direct link between the BS and the receiver, the receiver relies on the signals solely from the reflections of the RIS. Consequentially, at the
\displaystyle k
-th FAS port of the user, the received signal is given by
[
26
]

\displaystyle\displaystyle y_{k}=\sqrt{P_{S}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}h_{m}v_{m,k}e^{-2i\pi\theta_{m}}s+n_{k},

(1)

where
\displaystyle h_{m}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\epsilon_{1})
denotes the complex channel coefficient from the BS to the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element, and
\displaystyle v_{m,k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\epsilon_{2})
denotes the channel from the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element to the
\displaystyle k
-th FAS port,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{1}
and
\displaystyle\epsilon_{2}
are the average channel gain of the BS-to-RIS link and RIS-to-receiver link, respectively.
\displaystyle P_{S}
is the transmit power,
\displaystyle n_{k}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\sigma^{2}_{n})
is the additional white Gaussian noise,
\displaystyle\theta_{m}
denotes the reflecting phase of the
\displaystyle m
-th RIS element, and the RIS is assumed to have full knowledge of the phases of
\displaystyle h_{m}
and
\displaystyle v_{m,k}

\displaystyle\theta_{m}=-\arg(h_{m}v_{m,k})

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\sum_{m=1}^{M}|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|.

(2)

It can be readily observed that both

and
\displaystyle|v_{m,k}|

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|)=\mathbf{E}(|h_{m}|)\mathbf{E}(|v_{m,k}|)=\pi\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}/4

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|)=\mathbf{E}(|h_{m}|^{2}|v_{m,k}|^{2})-(\mathbf{E}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|))^{2}=\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}(1-\pi^{2}/16)

III
Outage Performance Analysis

In this section, the outage performance of the system is analyzed. First, let us denote
\displaystyle R
as the target transmission rate, and the outage probability is accordingly given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle P_{\rm{out}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=F_{\gamma^{*}}(\Lambda_{\rm{th}})=\Pr\left(\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{S}(\gamma^{*})^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right)\leq R\right),

(9)

where
\displaystyle\Lambda_{\rm{th}}=((2^{R}-1)\sigma_{n}^{2}/P_{S})^{1/2}
. To proceed with the outage probability analysis, it is necessary to derive the CDF of
\displaystyle\gamma^{*}
, i.e.,
\displaystyle F_{\gamma^{*}}(y)
. Given the fact that the exact expression for
\displaystyle F_{\gamma^{*}}(y)
is intractable, the following subsections will develop several approximations for
\displaystyle F_{\gamma^{*}}(y)
. Besides, it should be noted that the results presented are developed based on the CLT, and a multivariate Gaussian distribution is assumed. Consequently, the mean and variance of
\displaystyle\gamma_{k}
, as well as the correlation coefficient between ports
\displaystyle k
and
\displaystyle l
, must be computed to facilitate further derivations.

III-A

CLT Approximation

In practice, the number of RIS elements,
\displaystyle M
, is supposed to be very large, and thus the CLT can be applied.
Consequentially,
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}=[\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}]
can be approximated by
\displaystyle\mathbf{\bar{\Gamma}}=[\bar{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\bar{\gamma}_{N}]
, which follows a multivariate Gaussian distribution with identical correlation coefficient matrix and mean vector to those of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}
. Also, the PDF and CDF of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}
are similar to those of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\bar{\Gamma}}
.
Furthermore, the mean and variance of
\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{k}
are identical to those of
\displaystyle\gamma_{k}
, which can be computed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle E_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle M\mathbf{E}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|)=\frac{M\pi\sqrt{\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}}}{4},

(10)

\displaystyle\displaystyle V_{\gamma}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle M\mathbf{Var}(|h_{m}||v_{m,k}|)=M\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\big(1-\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}\big).

(11)

Moreover, as
\displaystyle\gamma_{k}
for
\displaystyle k=1,\dots,N
share the same
\displaystyle|h_{m}|
for
\displaystyle m=1,\dots,M
, and
\displaystyle|v_{m,k}|
are correlated for different
\displaystyle k
, it can be easily demonstrated that
\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}_{k}
for
\displaystyle k=1,\dots,N
are correlated. Hence, given the calculation of Pearson correlation coefficient and (
2
), the correlation coefficient between
\displaystyle\gamma_{k}
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{l}
, which indicates the degree of correlation between RVs
\displaystyle\gamma_{k}
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{l}
and falls in the range of
\displaystyle[-1,1]
, can be formulated as

\eta(g_{k,l})=\frac{\mathbf{E}(\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l})-E_{\gamma}^{2}}{V_{\gamma}}=\frac{M\epsilon_{1}\mathbf{E}(|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|)-E_{\gamma}^{2}/M}{V_{\gamma}},

(12)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}xyf_{|v_{m,k}|,|v_{m,l}|}(x,y)dxdy,

(13)

and

f_{|v_{m,k}|,|v_{m,l}|}(x,y)=\frac{4x^{2}y^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}(1-g_{k,l})}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}}}e^{-\frac{y^{2}+g_{k,l}x^{2}}{\epsilon_{2}^{2}(1-g_{k,l})}}\\
\times I_{0}\left(\frac{2g_{k,l}xy}{\epsilon_{2}(1-g_{k,l})}\right),

(14)

in which
\displaystyle f_{|v_{m,k}|,|v_{m,l}|}(x,y)
is the joint PDF of RVs
\displaystyle|v_{m,k}|
and
\displaystyle|v_{m,l}|
,
\displaystyle I_{0}(\cdot)
denotes the zeroth order of Bessel function of the first kind. Note that
\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(|v_{m,k}||v_{m,l}|)
can be computed via numerical integration.

Based on the correlation coefficient in (
12
), we can formulate the correlation coefficient matrix of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\bar{\Gamma}}
as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}=\mathbf{toeplitz}\left(\eta(g_{1,1}),\dots,\eta(g_{1,N})\right),

(15)

and the joint PDF of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\bar{\Gamma}}
is written as

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{E}_{\gamma})^{T}(V_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{\Omega})^{-1}(\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{E}_{\gamma})}}{(2\pi V_{\gamma})^{N/2}(\mathrm{det}\mathbf{\Omega})^{1/2}},

(16)

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{N}]^{T},~\mbox{and}~\mathbf{E}_{\gamma}=[E_{\gamma},\dots,E_{\gamma}]^{T}.

(17)

Utilizing the PDF of
\displaystyle\mathbf{\bar{\Gamma}}
in (
16
), the CDF of RV,
\displaystyle\bar{\gamma}^{*}
, can be expressed via the joint CDF of
\displaystyle N
-dimensional Gaussian distribution, i.e.,
\displaystyle\Phi(y,V_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{\Omega},E_{\gamma})
, as

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\bar{\gamma}^{*}}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Phi(y,V_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{\Omega},E_{\gamma})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{-\infty}^{y}\cdots\int_{-\infty}^{y}}_{N}f_{\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})dx_{1}\cdots dx_{N},

(18)

where the calculation of
\displaystyle\Phi(y,V_{\gamma}\cdot\mathbf{\Omega},E_{\gamma})
can be implemented via mathematical tools, such as Matlab and Maple. However, for large values of
\displaystyle N
, say
\displaystyle N\geq 25
, the complexity of the calculation of
\displaystyle F_{\bar{\gamma}^{*}}(y)
is prohibitive as an
\displaystyle N
-dimensional integral is required. To overcome the difficulty, we will provide approximate expressions of
\displaystyle F_{\bar{\gamma}^{*}}(y)
in the next subsections.

III-B

Block-Correlation Channel Approximation

In this part, we resort to a block-correlation approximation model in
[
22
]
. It is important to note that as the dimension
\displaystyle N
increases, the correlation coefficient matrix becomes predominantly influenced by a small fraction of eigenvalues. To efficiently capture the significant eigenvalues, we employ a block matrix, which simplifies the representation of the original matrix. Consequently, we derive an approximate distribution for the FAS model, characterized by a correlation coefficient matrix configured in block form. The number of blocks and the sizes of each block are determined based on the principal eigenvalues of
\displaystyle{\bf\Sigma}
.
Specifically, the random vector
\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{m}
can be approximated by
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{m}
, and the correlation coefficient matrix of
\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{v}}_{m}
can be presented in the following form:

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{A}_{1}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{A}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{A}_{D}\\
\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d}\times L_{d}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&\mu&\cdots&\mu\\
\mu&1&\cdots&\mu\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mu&\mu&\cdots&1\\
\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\mu
denotes a constant close to
\displaystyle 1
, and
\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{d=1}^{D}L_{d}=N
with
\displaystyle D=|S\{\lambda_{th}\}|
being determined by the number of principal eigenvalues of
\displaystyle{\bf\Sigma}
. That is, there exists
\displaystyle S\{\lambda_{\rm{th}}\}=\{\lambda_{n}|\lambda_{n}\geq\lambda_{\rm{th}},n=1,\dots,N\}
, and
\displaystyle D
equals to the cardinal number of
\displaystyle S\{\lambda_{th}\}
, where
\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm{th}}
is a small number to ensure that enough eigenvalues are included in
\displaystyle S\{\lambda_{\rm{th}}\}
, which can be dynamically adjusted. After identifying the principal eigenvalues of
\displaystyle{\bf\Sigma}
, which dictate the number of blocks, we proceed to ascertain the size of each block. Specifically,
\displaystyle L_{d}
represents the size of the
\displaystyle d
-th block, which is determined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\underset{L_{1},\dots,L_{d},\dots,L_{D}}{\arg\min}~~\mathrm{dist}(\hat{\bf\Sigma},{\bf\Sigma}),

(27)

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(\cdot)
is a distance metric between two matrices, which is determined by the difference of their eigenvalues and the detailed procedure can be found in
[
22
]
.
The procedure outlined in reference
[
22
]
involves determining the integer
\displaystyle L_{d}
for each block sequentially, based on (
27
). This process continues until
\displaystyle\sum_{d=1}^{D}L_{d}=N
, ensuring that the sum of the eigenvalues of the approximated matrix
\displaystyle\hat{\bf\Sigma}
matches that of the original matrix
\displaystyle{\bf\Sigma}
.

Based on the block-correlation model, we can approximate
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}
with
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}=[\hat{\gamma}_{1},\dots,\hat{\gamma}_{N}]
. Applying the Pearson correlation coefficient calculation, the correlation coefficient matrix of
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
is given as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathbf{B}_{1}&\mathbf{C}&\cdots&\mathbf{C}\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{B}_{2}&\cdots&\mathbf{C}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{C}&\mathbf{C}&\cdots&\mathbf{B}_{D}\\
\end{array}\right),

where all the elements in the submatrix within
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}
are given as
\displaystyle\rho_{0}=\eta(0)
, and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{d}\times L_{d}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&\rho_{1}&\cdots&\rho_{1}\\
\rho_{1}&1&\cdots&\rho_{1}\\
\vdots&&\ddots&\vdots\\
\rho_{1}&\rho_{1}&\cdots&1\\
\end{array}\right),

where
\displaystyle\rho_{1}=\eta(\mu)
.

Based on the structure of the correlation coefficient matrix
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Omega}}
, which indicates that the elements in
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
can be divided into
\displaystyle D
parts, and each part contains
\displaystyle L_{d}
identically distributed RVs, for
\displaystyle d=1,\cdots,D
, we can then rearrange the subscript and rewrite
\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{\Gamma}}
into
\displaystyle[\hat{\gamma}_{1,1},\dots,\hat{\gamma}_{L_{1},1},\dots,\hat{\gamma}_{k,d},\dots,\hat{\gamma}_{L_{D},D}]
.
Thanks to the block-correlation model, where the correlation coefficient within each block is a constant, the analysis becomes tractable for the FAS-RIS communication and other complicated FAS-enabled communications.
Here,
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{k,d}
can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{k,d}=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\rho_{1}}z_{2,k,d}+\sqrt{\rho_{1}-\rho_{0}}z_{1,d}+\sqrt{\rho_{0}}z_{0}+E_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle a_{k,d}+r_{d}+w,

(36)

for
\displaystyle k=1,\dots,L_{d}
, and
\displaystyle d=1,\dots,D
, and
\displaystyle z_{2,k,d}
,
\displaystyle z_{1,d}
and
\displaystyle z_{0}
are independently and identically distributed (i.i.d.) Gaussian RVs with mean
\displaystyle 0
and variance
\displaystyle V_{\gamma}
. Also,
\displaystyle w\sim\mathcal{N}(E_{\gamma},V_{\gamma}\rho_{0})
,
\displaystyle r_{d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\gamma}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0}))
and

\displaystyle a_{k,d}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1}))
for
\displaystyle k=1,\dots,L_{d}
and
\displaystyle d=1,\dots,D
, are independently distributed.

The maximum of the channel parameter
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{k,d}
in each block is
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{*}=\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,L_{d}\}}\hat{\gamma}_{k}^{*}
for
\displaystyle d=1,\dots,D
, and the CDF of RV
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}^{*}=\max_{d\in\{1,\dots,D\}}\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{*}
can be computed as

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\hat{\gamma}^{*}}(y)=\mathbf{E}_{w}\left[\prod_{d=1}^{D}F_{\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{*}|w}(y)\right].

(37)

Applying the PDFs of RVs
\displaystyle a_{k,d}
,
\displaystyle r_{d}
and
\displaystyle w
, that is

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{a_{k,d}}(x)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1})}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1})}},

(38)

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{r_{d}}(x_{d})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi V_{\gamma}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})}}e^{-\frac{x_{d}^{2}}{2V_{\gamma}(\rho_{1}-\rho_{0})}},

(39)

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{w}(x_{0})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi V_{\gamma}\rho_{0}}}e^{-\frac{(x_{0}-E_{\gamma})^{2}}{2V_{\gamma}\rho_{0}}},

(40)

we can obtain the conditional PDF of
\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{k,d}
for
\displaystyle k=1,\dots,L_{d}
and
\displaystyle d=1,\dots,D

\displaystyle r_{d}=x_{d}
and
\displaystyle w=x_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle f_{\hat{\gamma}_{k,d}|r_{d}=x_{d},w=x_{0}}(x)=\frac{e^{-\frac{(x-x_{d}-x_{0}))^{2}}{2V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1})}}}{\sqrt{2\pi V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1})}},

(41)

and the conditional CDF as

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\hat{\gamma}_{k,d}|r_{d}=x_{d},w=x_{0}}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{y}f_{\gamma_{k,d}|r_{d}=x_{d},w=x_{0}}(x)dx

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{y-x_{d}-x_{0})}{\sqrt{2\pi V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1})}}\right)\right],

(42)

where
\displaystyle{\rm erf}(x)
is the error function. Taking the expectation of
\displaystyle F_{\gamma_{k,d}|r_{d}=x_{d},w=x_{0}}(y)

\displaystyle r_{d}

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{*}|w=x_{0}}(y)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big(F_{\gamma_{k,d}|r_{d}=x_{d},w=x_{0}}(y)\big)^{L_{d}}f_{r_{d}}(x_{d})dx_{d}.

(43)

\displaystyle\displaystyle F_{\hat{\gamma}^{*}}(y)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{d=1}^{D}F_{\hat{\gamma}_{d}^{*}|w=x_{0}}(y)f_{w}(x_{0})dx_{0}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\prod_{d=1}^{D}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{L_{d}}}\left[1+\mathrm{erf}\left(\frac{y-x_{d}-x_{0}}{\sqrt{2\pi V_{\gamma}(1-\rho_{1})}}\right)\right]^{L_{d}}

V
Conclusions

In this correspondence, we analyzed the outage probability for FAS-RIS systems. Applying the CLT and block-correlation channel model, we derived the expressions of outage probability. Numerical results showed the effectiveness of the proposed analysis, as the derived analytical results become close to the simulation results when the number of RIS elements is large.
