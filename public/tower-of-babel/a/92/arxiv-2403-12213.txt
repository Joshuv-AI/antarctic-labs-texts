Private graphon estimation via sum-of-squares
Hongjie Chen, Jingqiu Ding, Tommaso d’Orsi, Yiding Hua, Chih-Hung Liu, David Steurer

Abstract
We develop the first pure node-differentially-private algorithms for learning stochastic block models and for graphon estimation with polynomial running time for any constant number of blocks. The statistical utility guarantees match those of the previous best information-theoretic (exponential-time) node-private mechanisms for these problems. The algorithm is based on an exponential mechanism for a score function defined in terms of a sum-of-squares relaxation whose level depends on the number of blocks. The key ingredients of our results are (1) a characterization of the distance between the block graphons in terms of a quadratic optimization over the polytope of doubly stochastic matrices, (2) a general sum-of-squares convergence result for polynomial optimization over arbitrary polytopes, and (3) a general approach to perform Lipschitz extensions of score functions as part of the sum-of-squares algorithmic paradigm.

Introduction

Differential privacy is an appealing and intensely studied theoretical framework for privacy preserving data analysis
[
DMNS06
,
DR+14
]
.
In the context of graph data, researchers investigate two natural versions of differential privacy:
For
edge-differential privacy
, nodes in the input graph represent public information and only individual relationships, encoded by edges, represent sensitive information that the released data must not reveal.
For
node-differential privacy
, individual nodes themselves (along with all their incident edges) represent sensitive information that we require the algorithm to protect.
(See
Definition

3.4
for a precise definition of node-differential privacy.)

Under edge-differential privacy, it is feasible to release accurate approximations of both global properties of the graph (e.g. the degree distribution)
[

,
HLMJ09
,
BBDS12
,
GRU12
,
KRSY14
,
XCT14
]
and local properties of the nodes (e.g. whether two particular nodes belong to the same community or not)
[
MNVT22
,
CCAd+23
]
.

On the other hand, node-differential privacy is significantly more stringent.
Here, we can only hope to release global statistics of the graph.
But even for the simplest such properties, private algorithms turn out to be challenging to design
[
BBDS13
,
KNRS13a
,
BCS15
,
RS16
,
BCSZ18
,
US19a
,
KRST23
]

The reason is that global statistics often are highly sensitive to modifications of even a single node.
For example, in a
n
-vertex graph, the number of connected components can change by an additive factor of
\Omega(n)
when we modify a single vertex.
In contrast, it can change by at most
1
when we add or remove a single edge.

Parameter estimation and privacy

Given the above picture, results
[

,
BCS15
,
BCSZ18
,
US19a
]
in node differential privacy adheres to the following blueprint:

(i)
they preserve privacy on all graphs,

(ii)
they consider a specific graph model,

(iii)
they argue how, from typical instances of that model, they can accurately estimate its parameters.
The quality of the result then depends on the accuracy of the estimation, the significance of the models considered and the running time of the underlying algorithm.

In terms of (statistical) utility guarantees and privacy trade-offs, one of the best known node-private mechanisms is the mechanism for graphon estimation (closely related to parameter-learning for stochastic block model, see

This mechanism summarizes the global structure of the input graph as a small

A major drawback of this mechanism is its high computational cost: for every value of

So far researchers have been able to turn this mechanism into a computationally efficient one only for edge-density estimation, roughly corresponding to the case

In this work, we show that for

Constant average degree

The utility analysis outlined so far provides an upper bound on the error no better than
\tfrac{k\log n}{d}
.
This error bound is meaningful only if
d\gg k\log n
.
In particular, we cannot get a meaningful guarantee in this way for graphs with large constant degree.
This logarithmic factor in the error bound comes from the spectral norm bound for the centered adjacency matrix of our input graph.
(Indeed, due to the presence of vertices with degree at least
\sqrt{\log n}
, this logarithmic factor is required for the spectral norm of the centered adjacency matrix.)
There are (at least) two approaches in the literature for avoiding this logarithmic factor in the final error bound.

The first approach is to use an analysis based on the cut-norm (as a substitute for the spectral norm) and Grothendieck’s inequality (e.g.,
[
GV14
]
).
While this approach yields meaningful guarantees for constant average degree, the error bounds achieved with this approach in the literature are substantially worse than
\tfrac{k}{d}
as far as we are aware
(similar to the distinction between fast and slow error bounds of lasso for sparse linear regression).

The second approach to deal with constant average degree is to prune high-degree vertices (e.g.,
[
Coj05
]
) and to redo the analysis based on spectral norms for the remaining graph.
A recent iteration of this approach shows the following remarkable property:
after removing the rows and columns of vertices with average degree larger than
20dR
, the centered adjacency matrix has spectral norm bounded by
O(\sqrt{Rd})
— smaller by a factor
\sqrt{\log n}
than before pruning.

By virtue of being node-private, our algorithm turns out to be robust to pruning a small fraction of vertices.
That robustness allows us to carry out the utility analysis on the pruned graph (where the relevant spectral norm is nicely bounded) and conclude that utility also holds for the original graph before pruning.

Node-adjacent graphs

Two graphs are

Definition 3.4

Since all our algorithms on graphs have an output distribution that is invariant under permutations of the vertices of the input graph, we can assume node-adjacent input graphs to be identical up to rewiring a single vertex.

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z),f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{20.53386pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z}$}}}$}}}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-Y_{0}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim k\cdot\lVert Y_{1}-Y_{0}\rVert^{2}+\lVert Y_{2}-Y_{1}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+R\lVert Y_{\mathrm{in}}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\textnormal{sum}}+\nu\,,

where

.

Proof.

Since

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{20.53386pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z}$}}}$}}}0\leqslant ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\leqslant R\cdot\mathbb{1}_{n}{\mathbb{1}_{n}}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

Consequently, by

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{20.53386pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z}$}}}$}}}f(Z;B,Y_{2})-f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})=\langle ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},Y_{2}-Y_{\mathrm{in}}\rangle\geqslant-R\cdot\lVert Y_{\mathrm{in}}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\textnormal{sum}}\,.

(sos version of Cauchy–Schwarz),

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{20.53386pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z}$}}}$}}}f(Z;B,Y_{1})-f(Z_{0};B_{0},Y_{1})\geqslant

\displaystyle f(Z;B,Y_{2})-f(Z_{0};B_{0},Y_{2})

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{16}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-{Z_{0}}{B_{0}}Z_{0}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}-4\lVert Y_{1}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z),f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{20.53386pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z}$}}}$}}}

\displaystyle+f(Z_{0};B_{0},Y_{2})-f(Z;B,Y_{2})

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{16}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-Y_{0}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+4\lVert Y_{1}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+R\cdot\lVert Y_{\mathrm{in}}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\textnormal{sum}}

\displaystyle+v

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z),f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{20.53386pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-20.53386pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[20.53386pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z}$}}}$}}}

For the sum-of-squares proof above, it is important that in the constraint
f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t
only

is a variable for the proof system and that, in particular,

is a constant for the proof system.
However, the polynomial system
\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)
that we consider for our algorithms only contains a constraint
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{f(Y,Z;B)\geqslant t}}\right\}
where
Y
is a variable for the proof system.
The following lemma shows that the system
\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)
satisfies a monotonicty property that allows us to sos-derive
f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t
from it.

Lemma 4.3
.

For all matrices
Y_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and every matrix
B
with nonnegative entries,

\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{17.33401pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-17.33401pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[17.33401pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-17.33401pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[17.33401pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Y,Z}$}}}$}}}f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t\,.

Proof.

Since

f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})-t=f(Y,Z;B)-t+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},Y_{\mathrm{in}}-Y}}\right\rangle

and we have the degree-4 constraint
f(Y,Z;B)-t\geqslant 0
, it suffices to show
\langle ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},Y_{\mathrm{in}}-Y\rangle\geqslant 0
.
For all
i,j
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{7.98607pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2}$}}}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})(i,j)\geqslant 0
as

(ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})(i,j)=\sum_{a,b}B(a,b)Z(i,a)Z(j,b)
.
Combining the degree-2 sos proof
ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\geqslant 0
with the degree-1 constraint
Y_{\mathrm{in}}\geqslant Y
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y;Y_{\mathrm{in}})
,

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y;Y_{\mathrm{in}})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{7.98607pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{3}$}}}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},Y_{\mathrm{in}}-Y}}\right\rangle\geqslant 0\,.

Therefore

\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{7.98607pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}f(Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant t\,.

∎

The final part of our utility analysis is the following connection between the Frobenius norm distance between
n
-by-
n
matrices
ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and
{Z_{0}}{B_{0}}Z_{0}^{\top}
and the graphon
\delta_{2}
distance of the
k
-by-
k
matrices
B
and
{B_{0}}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
B,B_{0}
be
k
-by-
k
matrices with nonnegative entries and let
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
be a scalar.
Suppose the following polynomial system in
Z
is sos-consistent up to level
k^{O(1)}
,

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\cup\mathcal{A}_{1}({Z_{0}})\cup\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-Z_{0}B_{0}{Z_{0}}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant t}}\right\}\,.

Then,
\delta_{2}^{2}(B,B_{0})\leqslant 1.1\cdot t
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

6.1
, the
\delta_{2}
distance has the following alternative characterization in terms of
k
-by-
k
doubly-stochastic matrices,

\delta_{2}^{2}(B,B_{0})=\min_{S\in B_{k}}p(S;B,B_{0})\,.

Here,
B_{k}
denotes the set of all
k
-by-
k
doubly-stochastic matrices (also known as the Birkhoff polytope), and
p(S;B,B_{0})
denotes the following quadratic polynomial in
S
with coefficients depending on
B,B_{0}
,

p(S;B,B_{0})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}\sum_{a,a^{\prime},b,b^{\prime}\in[k]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B(a,b)-{B_{0}}(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})}}\right)^{2}\cdot S(a,a^{\prime})\cdot S(b,b^{\prime})\,.

Relatedly, we can embed the set of community membership matrices
\mathcal{Z}(n,k)
into the Birkhoff polytope
B_{k}
, by the following quadratic map
Z\mapsto S(Z)
,

S(Z)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{k}{n}{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}{Z_{0}}\,.

Furthermore, this map has the property that
p\big(S(Z);B,B_{0}\big)=\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-{Z_{0}}{B_{0}}Z_{0}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}
for all
Z,Z_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}(n,k)
.

We show in
Lemma

D.14
that these properties of the map
S(Z)
also have sos proofs,

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z),\mathcal{A}_{1}({Z_{0}})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{21.61389pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-21.61389pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[21.61389pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{O(1)}$}}}\hskip-21.61389pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[21.61389pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{Z,{Z_{0}}}$}}}$}}}\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ds}}(S(Z)),p\big(S(Z);B,B_{0}\big)=\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-{Z_{0}}{B_{0}}Z_{0}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,,

where
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ds}}(S)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{S\geqslant 0,\penalty\ S\mathbb{1}_{k}=\mathbb{1}_{k},\penalty\ {S}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbb{1}_{k}=\mathbb{1}_{k}}}\right\}
is the set of linear constraints describing
B_{k}
.

Thus, since the system
\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\cup\mathcal{A}_{1}({Z_{0}})\cup\{\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\lVert ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-{Z_{0}}{B_{0}}Z_{0}^{\top}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant t\}
is sos-consistent up to level
k^{O(1)}
, then so is the system
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ds}}(S)\cup\{p(S;B,B_{0})\leqslant t\}
(with a small additive loss in the exact number of levels).

In
Section

7
, we show the sum-of-squares provides good multiplicative approximations for the problem of minimizing quadratic polynomials with nonnegative coefficients over integral polytopes.
Concretely,
Corollary

7.2
shows that the sos-consistency of the system
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{ds}}(S)\cup\{p(S;B,B_{0})\leqslant t\}
up to level
k^{O(1)}
implies that there exists a doubly-stochastic matrix
S^{*}\in B_{k}
with
p(S^{*};B,B_{0})\leqslant 1.1t
.
We conclude
\delta_{2}^{2}(B,B_{0})\leqslant 1.1t
as desired.
∎

The following lemma allows us to bound the sensitivity of our score functions, which is the main ingredient for the privacy analysis of our algorithms.

Lemma 4.5

(Sensitivity bound)
.

For every two matrices
Y_{\mathrm{in}},Y_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
that differ in at most one row and one column, there exist linear polynomials
L=(L_{ij})
such that for every polynomial inequality
p(Y,Z)\geqslant 0
in
\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime},t-\Delta)
,

\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{26.3307pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-26.3307pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[26.3307pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\deg(p)}$}}}\hskip-26.3307pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[26.3307pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}p(L(Y),Z)

where
\Delta=40nR\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}
.

In particular, by
Lemma

8.1
, the property established in the above lemma implies that, whenever the system
\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)
is sos-consistent up to level
\ell
, then so is the system
\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime},t-\Delta)
(under the conditions of the lemma).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
Y_{\mathrm{in}}
and
Y_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime}
differ on the first row and column.
Consider the following linear functions

L_{ij}(Y)=\begin{cases}Y(i,j)&\text{if }i,j>1\,,\\
0&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Sos proofs for inequalities in
\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y;Y_{\mathrm{in}}^{\prime})
are straightforward.
For
f(L(Y),Z;B)\geqslant t-\Delta
, observe that

\displaystyle f(L(Y),Z;B)-(t-\Delta)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(Y,Z;B)-t}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},Y-L(Y)}}\right\rangle}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(f(Y,Z;B)-t}}\right)+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta-2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle(ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})(1,\cdot),Y(1,\cdot)}}\right\rangle}}\right)\,.

For all
i,j
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{7.98607pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{2}$}}}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}(ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})(i,j)\leqslant\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}
as

\displaystyle(ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})(i,j)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}B(a,b)Z(i,a)Z(j,b)\leqslant\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\cdot\sum_{a,b}Z(i,a)Z(j,b)

\displaystyle=\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{a}Z(i,a)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{b}Z(j,b)}}\right)=\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\,.

Then,

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z)\cup\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y;Y_{\mathrm{in}})\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{7.98607pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{3}$}}}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle(ZB{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})(1,\cdot),Y(1,\cdot)}}\right\rangle\leqslant\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\cdot\sum_{j}Y(1,j)\leqslant 20nR\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\,.

where the first inequality uses
Y\geqslant 0
and the second uses
Y\mathbb{1}\leqslant 20nR\cdot\mathbb{1}
in
\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y;Y_{\mathrm{in}})
.
Since we have degree-4 constraint
f(Y,Z;B)-t\geqslant 0
, then

\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{7.98607pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-7.98607pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[7.98607pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}f(L(Y),Z;B)\geqslant t-\Delta\,.

∎

Theorem 4.6
.

For every
R\geqslant 1
and
k\geqslant 2
, there exists an
\varepsilon
-differentially private algorithm with the following utility guarantee:
For every input matrix
Y_{\mathrm{in}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{n\times n}
and all matrices
Y_{0},Y_{1},Y_{2}
satisfying
\mathcal{A}_{2}(Y_{2};Y_{\mathrm{in}})
and
Y_{0}={Z_{0}}{B_{0}}{{Z_{0}}}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
for some
{Z_{0}}\in\mathcal{Z}(n,k)
and
{B_{0}}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}^{k\times k}
with
\lVert{B_{0}}\rVert_{\max}\leqslant R

of the algorithm satisfies with probability at least

\delta_{2}^{2}(\hat{\bm{B}},B_{0})\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k\cdot\lVert Y_{0}-Y_{1}\rVert^{2}+\lVert Y_{1}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+R\cdot\lVert Y_{2}-Y_{\mathrm{in}}\rVert_{\textnormal{sum}}}}\right)+\frac{R^{2}k^{2}\log(n)}{\varepsilon n}\,.

Proof.

For any symmetric matrix
B\in[0,R]^{k\times k}
, consider the score function

which is defined as

\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\text{ level-}k^{10}\text{ pseudo-distribution }\tilde{\mu}\text{ satisfying }\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;B,Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)}}\right\}

\hat{\bm{B}}\sim\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\varepsilon\cdot\text{Score}(B;Y_{\mathrm{in}})}{\Delta}}}\right)\,,

where
\Delta=40R^{2}n
.

By
Lemma

4.5
, the sensitivity of the score function is upper bounded by
40R^{2}n
.
This sensitivity bound together with
Theorem

3.5
implies that the exponential mechanism is
\varepsilon
-differentially node private.
Moreover, for each matrix
B
, it takes

time to evaluate the score function
\text{Score}(B;Y_{\mathrm{in}})
.
Therefore by taking
\eta=n^{-100}
in

3.5
(exponential mechanism), our algorithm runs in time

.

It remains to analyze the utility of this exponential mechanism.
To this end let
\hat{\bm{B}}
denote the randomized output of the mechansim for input
Y_{\mathrm{in}}
.
Since our score function is
L
-Lipschitz with
L\leqslant O(n^{10})
.
By taking net size
\eta=n^{-100}
and probability bound

, in

3.5
(utility guarantees of exponential mechanism), the difference of the scores

and

is at most
O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{nk^{2}\log(n)}{\varepsilon}}}\right)
with probability at least
1-n^{-\Omega(k^{2})}
.

Let
\ell\geqslant k^{10}
and
\mu
be the level-
\ell
pseudo distribution that witnesses that the system
\mathcal{A}(Y,Z;\bm{B},Y_{\mathrm{in}},t)
is sos-consistent up to level
\ell
.
Let
Y_{2}
be the matrix obtained from
Y_{\mathrm{in}}
by removing the rows and columns averaging to more than
20R
.
By
Lemma

4.3
, the pseudo-distribution
\mu
satisfies the constraint
f(Z;\hat{\bm{B}},Y_{\mathrm{in}})\geqslant\text{Score}(\hat{\bm{B}};Y_{\mathrm{in}})=\bm{t}
.
Therefore, by
Lemma

4.2
, the pseudo-distribution

also satisfies the constraint

\lVert Z\hat{\bm{B}}{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-Y_{0}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\lesssim{k\cdot\lVert Y_{0}-Y_{1}\rVert^{2}+\lVert Y_{1}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\lVert Y_{2}-Y_{\mathrm{in}}\rVert_{\textnormal{sum}}+(t_{0}-\bm{t})}\,.

\mathcal{A}_{1}(Z),\lVert Z\hat{\bm{B}}{Z}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}-{Z_{0}}{B_{0}}{{Z_{0}}}{}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant O\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k\cdot\lVert Y_{0}-Y_{1}\rVert^{2}+\lVert Y_{1}-Y_{2}\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\lVert B\rVert_{\textnormal{max}}\lVert Y_{2}-Y_{\mathrm{in}}\rVert_{\textnormal{sum}}+(t_{0}-\bm{t})}}\right)\,.

4.2.1
Preliminaries for graphon estimation

A graphon is a bounded and measurable function
W:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
W(x,y)=W(y,x)
, which is said to be normalized if
\int W=1
.
Given a target edge density
\rho
, and a normalized graphon
W\in[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, a
(W,\rho,n)
-random graph on
n
vertices is sampled in the following way.
For each vertex
i\in[n]
in the graph, a real value
\bm{x}_{i}
is sampled uniformly at random from
[0,1]
.
Then each pair of vertices
i,j
is connected independently with probability
\bm{Q_{0}}(i,j)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\rho\cdot W(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})
.
We call
\bm{Q_{0}}
the edge connection probability matrix.

For any given graphon
W
and a measure-preserving mapping
\phi:[0,1]\to[0,1]
(with regard to the Lebesgue measure),
W^{\phi}(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=W(\phi(x),\phi(y))
define the same distribution on graphs as
W(x,y)
.
Thus we consider the following distance between graphons.

Definition 4.13

(
\delta_{2}
distance between graphons)
.

The
\delta_{2}
distance between two graphons
W_{1},W_{2}
is

\delta_{2}(W_{1},W_{2})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\colon[0,1]\to[0,1]\\
\text{measure preserving}\end{subarray}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert W_{1}^{\phi}-W_{2}}}\right\rVert_{2}\,,

where
W_{1}^{\phi}(x,y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=W_{1}(\phi(x),\phi(y))
.

Block graphons

Given a symmetric and nonnegative
k\times k
matix
B
, we can define a
k
-block graphon
W[B]
as follows.
Let
(I_{1},I_{2},\ldots,I_{k})
be the partition of
[0,1]
into adjacent and disjoint intervals of lengths
1/k
.
Let
W[B]:[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
to be the step function that equals
B(i,j)
on
I_{i}\times I_{j}
for every
i,j\in[k]
.

Given an arbitrary graphon
W
, when we approximate it by
k
-block graphons, the approximation error is given by

\varepsilon_{k}^{(O)}(W)\coloneqq\min_{B\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{k\times k}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert W[B]-W}}\right\rVert_{2}\,.

(4.9)

It is easy to see
\varepsilon_{k}^{(O)}(W)\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

In this section, we prove the extended sum-of-squares spectral Hölder inequality in

Lemma 5.1
.

Consider the following polynomial constraint system with respect to
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
,
V_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
M\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

\mathcal{A}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{V^{\top}V=\mathrm{Id}_{k};{V_{0}^{\top}}V_{0}=\mathrm{Id}_{k};(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-VV^{\top})M(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}})=0}}\right\}\,.

For any symmetric matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, there is a sum-of-squares proof that

\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{8}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}\langle M,W\rangle\leqslant\tfrac{3}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert M}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

Proof.

First, expanding
(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-VV^{\top})M(\mathrm{Id}_{n}-V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}})=0
and rearranging terms, we have

\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{8}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}M=VV^{\top}M+MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}-VV^{\top}MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}\,.

Plugging this into
\langle M,W\rangle
, we get

\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{8}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}\langle M,W\rangle=\langle VV^{\top}M,W\rangle+\langle MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}},W\rangle-\langle VV^{\top}MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}},W\rangle\,.

Now, we bound the three terms on the right hand side separately. First, by AM-GM inequality we have

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}\langle VV^{\top}M,W\rangle=\langle M,VV^{\top}W\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert M}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

For the same reason, we have

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}\langle MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}},W\rangle=\langle M,V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}W\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert M}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

For the third term, we have

\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{8}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}-\langle VV^{\top}MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}},W\rangle=-\langle M,VV^{\top}WV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}\rangle\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert M}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}WV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

Notice that, by the cyclicity of the matrix trace and AM-GM inequality, we have the following degree-
8
sum-of-squares inequality for the term
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}WV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{8}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}WV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\Tr\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(WVV^{\top}WV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle VV^{\top}W,WV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}\rangle

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

Combining everyting together, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{31.9737pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{8}$}}}\hskip-31.9737pt\raisebox{3.32222pt}{\makebox[31.9737pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{M,V,V_{0}}$}}}$}}}\langle M,W\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle VV^{\top}M,W\rangle+\langle MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}},W\rangle-\langle VV^{\top}MV_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}},W\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{3}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert M}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert V_{0}{V_{0}^{\top}}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\,.

∎

Lemma 5.2
.

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{V^{\top}V=\mathrm{Id}_{k}}}\right\}\,.

\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{10.44441pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-10.44441pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[10.44441pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-10.44441pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[10.44441pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{V}$}}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}\leqslant k\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert W}}\right\rVert^{2}\,.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{10.44441pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-10.44441pt\raisebox{-6.47223pt}{\makebox[10.44441pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{4}$}}}\hskip-10.44441pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[10.44441pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{V}$}}}$}}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert VV^{\top}W}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}

Lipschitz extensions within sum-of-squares

In this section, we demonstrate how to incorporate Lipschitz extensions into our sum-of-squares framework for private graphon estimation.
To this end, we first set up the framework for Lipschitz extensions in the context of differential privacy.

Lipschitz extensions as a privacy tool

Lipschitz extensions are basic mathematical objects that can be defined on general metric space (see the lecture note by Naor
[
Nao15
]
for more background).
Here we focus on the space of graphs (equivalently, adjacency matrices) equipped with node distance
16
16

16

Recall the node distance between two
n
-vertex graphs
G,G^{\prime}
is the minimum number of vertices of
G
that need to be rewired to obtain
G^{\prime}
.

d_{\mathrm{node}}

Let

denote the set of adjacency matrices of
n

\mathbb{G}_{n}
be the abbreviation of
\mathbb{G}_{n,n}

A graph function

\lvert f(G)-f(G^{\prime})\rvert\leqslant c\cdot d_{\mathrm{node}}(G,G^{\prime})
for all
G,G^{\prime}\in\mathbb{G}_{n}
.
In differential privacy, the smallest Lipschitz constant of

is also known as the sensitivity of

Functions with lower sensitivity can be approximated more accurately by private algorithms.
For example, the Laplace mechanism releases a private approximation of
f(G)
by adding Laplace noise proportinal to the sensitivity of
f
to
f(G)
.
However, there are many cases where the function of interest can only have low sensitivity when its input space is restricted to a subset
\mathbb{G}^{\prime}_{n}
of
\mathbb{G}_{n}

For example, the sensitivity of the number of edges in a graph is
d
when restricted to
\mathbb{G}_{n,d}

Given a function

of

\hat{f}(G)=f(G)
for

Towards efficient Lipschitz extensions

Lipschitz extensions always exist for real-valued functions

]

There is a line of work on constructing efficient Lipschitz extensions for designing node-DP algorithms

Let us first abstract a common thread in this line of work.

\hat{f}(A)=\max_{C}f(C)\text{ such that }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}C\in[0,1]^{n\times n}\text{ is symmetric}\,,\\

To see

Sensitivity

To bound sensitivity of functions defined in terms of sos-consistency like
f_{\mathrm{sos}}
and
\hat{f}_{\mathrm{sos}}
, we introduce the following general lemma that relates sos-consistency of two polynomial systems.

Lemma 8.1
.

Consider two polynomial systems
\mathcal{P}(x)
and
\mathcal{Q}(x)
in variables
x=(x_{i})
.
Suppose there exists a linear function
L(x)
such that for every polynomial inequality
q(x)\geqslant 0
in
\mathcal{Q}(x)
, we have
\mathcal{P}(x)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{26.1247pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-26.1247pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[26.1247pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\deg(q)}$}}}\hskip-26.1247pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[26.1247pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}q(L(x))
.
Then, if
\mathcal{P}(x)
is sos-consistent up to level
\ell
,
\mathcal{Q}(x)
is also sos-consistent up to level
\ell
.

Proof.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}(x)
is sos-consistent up to level
\ell
, which is witnessed by pseudo-distribution
\mu
.
In the following, we construct a pseudo-distribution
\mu^{\prime}
that witnesses the level-
\ell
sos-consistency of
\mathcal{Q}(x)
.
For each polynomial
q(x)
of degree at most
\ell
, let
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu^{\prime}}q(x)=\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}q(L(x))
.
Note
\mathop{\tilde{\mathbb{E}}}_{\mu}q(L(x))
is well-defined as
L
is linear and thus
q(L(x))
is a polynomial of degree at most
\ell
.
Then it is a routine work to verify
\mu^{\prime}\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-1.23135pt]{8.00003pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.00003pt\rule[0.75348pt]{8.00003pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-8.00003pt\raisebox{-2.95354pt}{\makebox[8.00003pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}\hskip-8.00003pt\raisebox{2.95354pt}{\makebox[8.00003pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}\mathcal{Q}(x)
. ∎

, as shown in

.

As a corollary of

, we can bound sensitivity of functions defined in terms of sos-consistency.

Corollary 8.2
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a space of datasets,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\Delta\geqslant 0
.
Consider a function
h:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

h(Y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\max t\text{ s.t. $\mathcal{P}(x;Y,t)$ is sos-consistent up to level $\ell$,}

where
\mathcal{P}(x;Y,t)
is a polynomial system in variables
x=(x_{i})
.
Suppose for every
t
and every pair of neigboring datasets
Y,Y^{\prime}
,
there exists a linear function
L(x)
such that any polynomial inequality
p(x)\geqslant 0
in
\mathcal{P}(x;Y^{\prime},t-\Delta)
has an sos proof
\mathcal{P}(x;Y,t)\mathrel{\hbox{\raisebox{3.44444pt}{$\rule[-6.45831pt]{0.47787pt}{12.91663pt}\rule[-0.23894pt]{26.3307pt}{0.47787pt}\hskip-26.3307pt\raisebox{-7.21112pt}{\makebox[26.3307pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\deg(p)}$}}}\hskip-26.3307pt\raisebox{1.96112pt}{\makebox[26.3307pt]{\hbox{$\scriptstyle{}$}}}$}}}p(L(x))
.
Then,

\max_{Y\sim Y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert h(Y)-h(Y^{\prime})}}\right\rvert\leqslant\Delta\,.

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary pair of neigboring datasets
Y,Y^{\prime}
.
Due to symmetry of
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
, it suffices to prove
h(Y^{\prime})\geqslant h(Y)-\Delta
.
Suppose
h(Y)=t
. Then
\mathcal{P}(x;Y,t)
is sos-consistent up to level
\ell
.
By
Lemma

8.1
,
\mathcal{P}(x;Y^{\prime},t-\Delta)
is also sos-consistent up to level
\ell
.
Thus
h(Y^{\prime})\geqslant t-\Delta=h(Y)-\Delta
.
∎
