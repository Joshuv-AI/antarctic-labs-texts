Few-particle scattering from localized quantum systems in spatially structured bosonic baths
Rahul Trivedi, Kevin Fischer, Shanhui Fan, Jelena Vuckovic

Abstract
Understanding the dynamics of localized quantum systems embedded in engineered bosonic environments is a central problem in quantum optics and open quantum system theory. We present a formalism for studying few-particle scattering in a localized quantum system interacting with an bosonic bath described by an inhomogeneous wave-equation. In particular, we provide exact relationships between the quantum scattering matrix of this interacting system and frequency domain solutions of the inhomogeneous wave-equation thus providing access to the spatial distribution of the scattered few-particle wave-packet. The formalism developed in this paper paves the way to computationally understanding the impact of structured media on the scattering properties of localized quantum systems embedded in them without simplifying assumptions on the physics of the structured media.

In this paper, we present a framework for exactly calculating the scattering matrix of a localized quantum system interacting with a bosonic bath whose dynamics are described by an inhomogeneous wave equation. There are two key ingredients that go towards making this framework possible —

1.

A second quantized description of the inhomogeneous bosonic bath in terms of both Langevin noise operators and plane-wave annihilation operators (Section
II

2.

Calculation of few-particle scattering matrices that builds on this second quantized description (Section

first

II.1
First quantization

A quantum theory for such a bosonic bath can easily be developed by applying Dirac’s quantization
weinberg1995quantum
procedure on its classical Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{\text{bath}}[\psi]

\mathcal{L}_{\text{bath}}[\psi]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})\bigg|\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}\bigg|^{2}-|\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x},t)|^{2}\bigg)d^{3}\mathbf{x}.

(2)

From this lagrangian, the canonically conjugate field to
\psi(\mathbf{x},t)
is easily identified to be

\displaystyle\pi(\mathbf{x},t)=\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})\frac{\partial\psi(\mathbf{x},t)}{\partial t}.

(3)

Dirac’s quantization then promotes both of these fields to operators,
\psi(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi(\mathbf{x})
, with commutator
[\psi(\mathbf{x}),\pi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]=i\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, the dynamics of the quantum state of the bath is governed by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\text{bath}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg(\frac{\pi^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{2\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})}+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\psi(\mathbf{x})|^{2}\bigg)d^{3}\mathbf{x}

(4)

It can be readily verified that the Heisenberg equations of motion for the operators
\psi(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi(\mathbf{x})
reproduce the equations of motion for the classical fields as given by Eqs.
1
and
3
.

II.2
Second quantization

While the first quantized description provides a complete quantum theory for the bosonic bath, it does not allow for an easy and physically meaningful description of incident and scattered quantum states. It is, therefore, of interest to perform second quantization and introduce particle annihilation and creation operators. We accomplish a second quantization of an inhomogeneous bosonic bath in two steps.
First
, we consider a more general case of lossy inhomogeneous media and introduce a second quantization based on Langevin noise formalism.
Second
, we treating lossless inhomogeneous media as a limiting case of lossy inhomogeneous media, we construct a second quantization in which an annihilation operator is associated with each possible plane-wave incident on the inhomogeneous media.

II.2.1
Langevin noise formalism

In this subsection, we build upon the formalism introduced in Refs.
dung1998three
;
gruner1996green
which quantize the wave-equation in lossy inhomogeneous Kramer-Kronig dielectrics using a Langevin noise-operator approach.
However, since we are fundamentally interested in lossless inhomogeneous baths, to make use of this formalism we fictitiously introduce loss in the bosonic bath, and consider a frequency-dependent permittivity distribution
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
given by

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})+i\alpha(\omega),

(5)

where
\alpha(\omega)>0
is the loss in the permittivity distribution. For simplicity, we assume the loss to be position independent — the permittivity distribution
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
is thus that of a lossy inhomogeneous permittivity distribution in a homogeneous background with permittivity
\varepsilon_{0}+i\alpha(\omega)
. While we could work with a position dependent loss as well, since the objective is to study the limiting case of a lossless permittivity distribution, the position dependence of the loss does not matter.

With the introduction of this loss, following Ref.
dung1998three
we introduce a bosonic noise operator
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
for every position
\mathbf{x}
and for every frequency
\omega>0
which satisfy
[\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega),\phi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})]=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
. Furthermore, the bath’s Hamiltonian is expressible as

II.2.2
Second quantization with plane-wave operators

Since the physics of an inhomogeneous bath can fundamentally be captured by the fields scattered from the inhomogeneity when excited by incident plane-waves, we seek its second quantized description in terms of incident plane-wave annihilation operators.
This can be derived from the noise-operator description by taking the limit of
\alpha(\omega)\to 0
. In order to take this limit, we express the Green’s function
\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
as

\displaystyle\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=

\displaystyle\quad\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\text{S}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y},

(11)

where
\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
is the Green’s function corresponding to the (lossy) background i.e. it is the solution of the partial differential equation

\displaystyle\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+\omega^{2}(\varepsilon_{0}+i\alpha(\omega))\big]\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(12)

with the boundary condition that
\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
is an outgoing spherical wave when
|\mathbf{x}|\to\infty
and
\text{S}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)
is the position-domain representation of the classical scattering operator of the (lossy) inhomogeneous bath.
The scattering operator is the operator that maps an incident field to the corresponding fields scattered from the inhomogeneous permittivity distribution. Substituting this expansion into Eq.
II.2.1

\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\psi^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\text{S}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)\psi^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y},

(13a)

where

\psi^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}.

(13b)

Physically, Eq.
13a
expresses the fields inside the inhomogeneous bath as the sum of an incident field and a scattered field. Furthermore, since the operator
\psi^{0}(\mathbf{x};\omega)
describes the excitations incident on the scattering region, it is expressible as a sum of plane-wave modes propagating in the background media. Formally, this is accomplished by introducing the plane-wave decomposition of
\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)

\text{G}_{\alpha}^{0}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\\
k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{ik\Omega\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\ k^{2}d^{2}\Omega\ dk}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{0}-k^{2}+i\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)},

(14)

into Eq.
13b

\psi^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\omega)
as

\psi^{0}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=N_{0}(\omega)\int_{\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)e^{ik_{0}(\omega)\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}\ d^{2}\Omega,

(15)

where
N_{0}(\omega)=({\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}/{16\pi^{3}})^{1/2}
and
k_{0}(\omega)=\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}
and

\displaystyle a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\omega)=\bigg(\frac{16\pi^{2}\omega\alpha(\omega)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\bigg)^{1/2}e^{-ik_{0}(\omega)\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\\
{k\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\end{subarray}}\frac{e^{ik\Omega\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{0}-k^{2}+i\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega)\ d^{3}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\ dk.

(16)

It is shown in appendix
B
that taking the limit of
\alpha(\omega)\to 0
makes

independent of
\mathbf{x}
, enabling us to define
a(\Omega,\omega)=\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\omega)

a(\Omega,\omega)
satisfies the commutator

\displaystyle[a(\Omega,\omega),a^{\dagger}(\Omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})]=\delta^{2}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime}),

(17)

and Eq.

\alpha(\omega)\to 0

\psi^{0}(\mathbf{x},\omega)=N_{0}(\omega)\int_{\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}a(\Omega,\omega)e^{ik_{0}(\omega)\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}d^{2}\Omega.

(18)

The operators

\psi(\mathbf{x},\omega)=N_{0}(\omega)\int_{\Omega\in\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)a(\Omega,\omega)d^{2}\Omega,

III
Scattering from a localized two-level system

In this section, we consider the problem of embedding a localized quantum system, which for simplicity is assumed to be a two-level system (TLS) with ground state
\ket{g}

\ket{e}

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}(n)},\Omega_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{N}(n)},\nu_{\mathcal{D}_{k}(n)})\delta\big(\omega_{\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}(n)}-\nu_{\mathcal{D}_{k}(n)}\big)\bigg)\times

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\Omega_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{N}(n)},\nu_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{N}_{k}(n)})\bigg)\mathcal{G}^{N-k}\big(\vec{\omega}_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}^{N}_{k}};\vec{\nu}_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{N}_{k}}\big)\bigg],

(30)

where the notation used in Eq.
III
is

1.

\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}
and
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{N}
are two (independent) unordered
k-
element subsets of
\{1,2\dots N\}
,

2.

\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{N}
and
\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{N}
are complements of
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}
and
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{N}
respectively,

3.

\mathcal{P}_{k}
is a
k-
element permutation,

4.

\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}
(
\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{D}_{k}^{N}
) is a permutation of the
k-
element set
\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}
(
\mathcal{D}_{k}^{N}
),

5.

For any set
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}(i)
is its
i^{\text{th}}
element and

6.

\vec{\omega}_{\bar{B}_{k}^{N}}=(\omega_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{N}(1)},\omega_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{N}(2)}\dots\omega_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{N}(N-k)})
(similarly for
\bar{D}_{k}^{N}\vec{\nu}
).

Furthermore,
\mathcal{G}^{N}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})
is the
N-
excitation Green’s function for the two-level system and is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{N}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})=

\displaystyle\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1},t_{2}\dots t_{N}\in\mathbb{R}\\
s_{1},s_{2}\dots s_{N}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sigma(t_{i})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{i})\bigg]\ket{\text{vac},g}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\prod_{j=1}^{N}e^{i(\omega_{j}t_{j}-\nu_{j}s_{j})}dt_{j}ds_{j}.

(31)

We note that the spatial dependence of the scattering matrix-element in Eq.
III
depends on
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\omega)
, which are the fields produced by the permittvity distribution on excitation by an incident plane-wave, as well
\text{Im}\big[\text{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)]
that captures the fields radiated by the two-level system. However, while
\text{Im}[\text{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)]
has contributions from both incoming and outgoing waves, when considering the action of the scattering-matrix on a normalizable
N-
particle wave-packet, only the outgoing waves have a non-zero contribution. Mathematically, this follows from the fact that
\text{G}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
is analytic in the lower-half of the complex plane and thus

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}f(\omega)\text{G}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)e^{-i\omega t}d\omega=0,

(32)

for any smooth and square-integrable function
f(\omega)
. Therefore, we can only retain the outgoing part of
\text{Im}\big[\text{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)]
in Eq.
III
and obtain

\displaystyle S^{N}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega};\vec{\Omega},\vec{\nu})=\bigg(\prod_{n=1}^{N}N_{0}(\nu_{n})\bigg)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\bigg[\sum_{\mathcal{B}_{k},\mathcal{D}_{k}}\bigg(\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}\prod_{n=1}^{k}

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}(n)},\Omega_{\mathcal{D}_{k}^{N}(n)},\nu_{\mathcal{D}_{k}(n)})\delta\big(\omega_{\mathcal{P}_{k}\mathcal{B}_{k}^{N}(n)}-\nu_{\mathcal{D}_{k}(n)}\big)\bigg)\times

\displaystyle\quad\bigg(\prod_{n=1}^{N-k}\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{2\pi i}\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{N}(n)},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{N}(n)})\times

\displaystyle\quad\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\Omega_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{N}(n)},\nu_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{N}_{k}(n)})\bigg)\mathcal{G}^{N-k}\big(\vec{\omega}_{\bar{\mathcal{B}}^{N}_{k}};\vec{\nu}_{\bar{\mathcal{D}}^{N}_{k}}\big)\bigg],

(33)

Equation
III
is one of the key results of this paper — to compute the quantum scattering matrix, we need to compute
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
and
\text{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
, which can be obtained by solving the (classical) inhomogeneous wave equation, and
\mathcal{G}^{N}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})
, the Green’s function of the quantum emitter.
Furthermore, the
N-
particle scattering matrix in Eq.
III
, consistent with the cluster decomposition principle
weinberg1995quantum
, comprises of terms where
k<N
particles are not scattered by the two-level system (which results in the
\delta(\cdot)
functions conserving the individual frequencies) while the remaining
N-k
particles interact with the quantum emitter (which is captured by the
N-k
excitation Green’s function
\mathcal{G}^{N-k}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})
).

In the following subsections, we explicitly calculate and study the properties of single- and two-particle scattering matrices. We provide analytical results for single and two-particle scattering when the two-level system is coupled to a homogeneous bath (i.e.
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})=\varepsilon_{0}
for all
\mathbf{x}
). We also numerically study properties of the single and two-particle scattering when the inhomogeneous bath is a 2D particleic crystal with a point defect. While we restrict our analysis to two-level emitters throughout this paper, in appendix
E
we extend the formalism of this paper to an emitter with a general level structure but under the Markovian approximation.

III.1
Single-particle scattering

We first consider scattering of a single particle propagating in the inhomogeneous bath from the localized two-level system. The spectral properties of the single-particle scattering matrix are governed by the single-excitation Green’s function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega;\nu)=

\displaystyle\int_{t,s\in\mathbb{R}}\bra{g,\text{vac}}\mathcal{T}\big[\sigma(t)\sigma^{\dagger}(s)\big]\ket{g,\text{vac}}e^{i(\omega t-\nu s)}dtds.

(34)

Since
\sigma(t)\ket{\text{vac},g}=0
, it follows that
\bra{g,\text{vac}}\mathcal{T}\big[\sigma(t)\sigma^{\dagger}(s)\big]\ket{g,\text{vac}}=A_{e}(t-s)\Theta(t\geq s)
where
A_{e}(t)=\bra{e,\text{vac}}e^{-iHt}\ket{e,\text{vac}}
. It then follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega;\nu)=2\pi\delta(\omega-\nu)\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{e}(t)e^{i\nu t}dt}_{\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu)}.

(35)

As is shown in appendix
D.1
,
A_{e}(t)
can be computed by solving the following (non-Markovian) ODE

\displaystyle\frac{dA_{e}(t)}{dt}=-i\omega_{0}A_{e}(t)+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}t^{\prime}\in[0,t]\\
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\end{subarray}}\text{Im}\big[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}A_{e}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\ d\omega.

(36)

subject to the boundary condition
A_{e}(t)=0
for
t\leq 0
. Specializing Eq.
III
to the case of single-particle scattering (
N=1
), we obtain

\displaystyle S^{1}\big(\mathbf{x},\omega;\Omega,\nu\big)=N_{0}(\nu)\bigg(e^{ik_{0}(\nu)\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}+\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\nu)-

\displaystyle\qquad iV_{0}^{2}\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\Omega,\nu)\text{G}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{d};\nu)\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu)\bigg)\delta(\omega-\nu).

(37)

This form of the single-particle scattering matrix is immediately physically interpretable — we notice that as a consequence of energy conservation, the frequencies of the incoming and outgoing single-particles are constrained to be equal. The spatial dependence of the scattering matrix can be seen to be the sum of three terms — first is the incident plane-wave
e^{ik_{0}(\nu)\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}
, second is the fields produced by direct scattering of the incident plane-wave from the inhomogeneous bath,
\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\nu)
, and third are dipolar fields radiated by the two-level system due to excitation by the incident plane-wave. We note that the strength of the dipolar field depends on the coupling strength
V_{0}
, as well as the total fields at the position of the dipole
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\Omega,\nu)
.

We first consider a homogeneous bath, for which
\mathcal{E}_{s}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\nu)=0
, and assume that
\mathbf{x}_{d}=0
and
\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\Omega,\nu)=1
. The spatial dependence of the single-particle scattering matrix is thus only determined by interference between the incident plane-wave and an outgoing spherical waves emitted by the two-level system at
\textbf{x}=\textbf{x}_{d}

\displaystyle S^{1}\big(\mathbf{x},\omega;\Omega,\nu\big)=

\displaystyle\qquad N_{0}(\nu)\bigg(e^{ik_{0}(\nu)\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}+iV_{0}^{2}\frac{e^{ik_{0}(\nu)r}}{4\pi r}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu)\bigg)\delta(\omega-\nu).

(38)

Furthermore, the Weisskopf-Wigner theory
wang1974wigner
can be used to approximate the single-excitation Green’s function by a complex Lorentzian (refer to appendix
D.1
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu)=\frac{1}{i(\omega_{0}-\nu)+\gamma/2},

(39)

where
\gamma=2V_{0}^{2}\text{G}(\mathbf{x}=0,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=0;\omega_{0})=V_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}
. Our result for the single-particle scattering matrix Eq.
III.1
can be used to calculate the single-particle scattering cross-section by integrating the square of the amplitude of the scattered field over a spherical surface to obtain

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{3D}}(\nu)=\frac{V_{0}^{4}}{4\pi^{2}}|\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu)|^{2}=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}\omega_{0}^{2}\varepsilon_{0}}\bigg[\frac{\gamma^{2}/4}{(\nu-\omega_{0})^{2}+\gamma^{2}/4}\bigg].

(40)

This result agrees with the classical scattering cross-section for a dipole scatterer, and the single-particle scattering cross-section for a dipole two-level system coupled to a homogeneous bath derived in Ref.
liu2016quantum
. A similar analysis yields the following result for the scattering cross-section for a 2D problem

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{2D}}(\nu)=\frac{4}{\omega_{0}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\bigg[\frac{\gamma^{2}/4}{(\nu-\omega_{0})^{2}+\gamma^{2}/4}\bigg],

(41)

where
\gamma=V_{0}^{2}/2
. We note that at resonance (
\omega=\omega_{0}
), both
\sigma_{\text{3D}}(\nu)
and
\sigma_{\text{2D}}(\nu)
are independent of the coupling strength
V_{0}
, and are completely determined by the resonant frequency of the two-level system.

(42b)

III.2
Two-particle scattering

The two-particle spectrum of the scattered wave-packet is governed by the two-excitation Green’s function
\mathcal{G}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})
, which is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})=

\displaystyle\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\\
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\bra{g,\text{vac}}\mathcal{T}\big[\sigma(t_{1})\sigma(t_{2})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{1})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{2})\big]\ket{g,\text{vac}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\prod_{i=1}^{2}e^{i(\omega_{i}t_{i}-\nu_{i}s_{i})}dt_{i}ds_{i}.

(43)

As is shown in Appendix
D.2
, following the procedure introduced in Ref.
shi2015multiphoton
, this Green’s function can be obtained entirely from the single-excitation Green’s function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega_{1};\nu_{\mathcal{P}_{2}(1)})\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega_{2};\nu_{\mathcal{P}_{2}(2)})

\displaystyle\quad\underbrace{-\pi\frac{\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu_{1})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu_{2})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega_{1})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega_{2})}{\Gamma(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}\delta\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\big(\omega_{j}-\nu_{j}\big)\bigg)}_{\mathcal{G}^{2}_{\text{C}}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})},

(44a)

where
\mathcal{P}_{2}
is a two element permutation and

\displaystyle\Gamma(E)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega)\mathcal{G}_{0}(E-\omega)\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}.

(44b)

Specializing Eq.
III
provides the following expression for the two-particle scattering matrix

\displaystyle S^{2}\big(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega};\vec{\Omega},\vec{\nu}\big)=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}\bigg(S\big(\mathbf{x}_{1},\omega_{1};\Omega_{\mathcal{P}(1)},\nu_{\mathcal{P}_{2}(1)}\big)\times

\displaystyle\qquad S\big(\mathbf{x}_{2},\omega_{2};\Omega_{\mathcal{P}(2)},\nu_{\mathcal{P}_{2}(2)}\big)\bigg)+S_{\text{C}}^{2}\big(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega};\vec{\Omega},\vec{\nu}\big),

(45a)

where
\mathcal{P}
is a permutation of the set
\{1,2\}
,
S^{1}\big(\mathbf{x},\omega;\Omega,\nu)
is the single-particle scattering matrix and
S_{\text{C}}^{2}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega};\vec{\Omega},\vec{\nu})
is the connected part of the two-particle scattering matrix that is given by

\displaystyle S_{\text{C}}^{2}\big(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega};\vec{\Omega},\vec{\nu}\big)=-\frac{V_{0}^{4}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{2}N_{0}(\nu_{i})\mathcal{E}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\Omega_{i},\nu_{i})\times

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega_{i})\bigg)\mathcal{G}_{\text{C}}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu}).

(45b)

We point out that Eq.
45
is consistent with the form of the two-particle scattering matrix as expected by the cluster-decomposition principle
weinberg1995quantum
, with the connected part of the scattering matrix capturing the particle-particle interactions induced by the nonlinearity of the two-level system. As can be seen from Eq.
45
, the spectral properties of the entanglement generated in the scattered two-particles due to such particle-particle interactions is captured by the connected part of the two-excitation Green’s function, while the spatial distribution of the entanglement is simply given by the Green’s function corresponding to the permittivity distribution.

For a homogeneous bath (
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})=\varepsilon_{0}
) and assuming
\mathbf{x}_{d}=0
, the connected part of the scattering matrix reduces to a product of two outgoing radially symmetric spherical waves

\displaystyle S_{\text{C}}^{2}\big(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega};\vec{\Omega},\vec{\nu}\big)=-\frac{V_{0}^{4}}{4\pi^{2}}\bigg(\prod_{i=1}^{2}N_{0}(\nu_{i})\frac{e^{ik_{0}(\omega_{i})r_{i}}}{4\pi r_{i}}\bigg)\mathcal{G}_{\text{C}}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu}),

(46)

where, in the Weisskopf-Wigner approximation,
\mathcal{G}_{\text{C}}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})
is given by (refer to appendix
D.2
for details)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\text{C}}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})=-\frac{i\pi(\sum_{j=1}^{2}\omega_{j}-2\omega_{0}-i\gamma)\delta(\sum_{j=1}^{2}(\omega_{j}-\nu_{j}))}{\prod_{j=1}^{2}(\nu_{j}-\omega_{0}-i\gamma/2)(\omega_{j}-\omega_{0}-i\gamma/2)},

(47)

where
\gamma=V_{0}^{2}\omega_{0}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}
. We point out that this result for the connected part of the two-excitation Green’s function is identical to the one obtained in Waveguide QED under a Markovian approximation to the waveguide two-level system interaction
fan2010input
.

\psi_{\text{out,C}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega})
can then express it as a superposition of two-particle fock states

\displaystyle\psi_{\text{out,C}}(\vec{\mathbf{x}},\vec{\omega})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\omega_{1})\psi_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{2},\omega_{2}),

(50)

with
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\lambda_{3}\dots
. Figures

for a two-level system strongly coupled to the photonic crystal bath. The frequency spectrum of each Schmidt vector (shown in Fig.

IV
Conclusion

In conclusion, we presented an approach to exactly analyze spatial and spectral properties of few-particle scattering from a localized system embedded in a an inhomogeneous bath. We develop exact solutions for the single and two-particle scattering matrices when the localized system is a two-level system. The formalism presented in this paper paves the way for computationally understanding the impact of structured media on quantum scattering from embedded localized systems. While the formalism in this paper assumes that the bosonic bath can be described by an inhomogeneous scalar wave equation, it can be extended to quantum optical systems where the bosonic bath (i.e. the photonic field) interacting with the localized system is described by a vector wave-equation.

Appendix A
Second quantization with noise operators

We begin by outlining some useful properties of the scalar Green’s function corresponding to the inhomogeneous wave-equation, followed by showing that the noise-operator based quantum theory presented in the main text satisfies the canonical commutation relations stemming from Dirac’s quantization.

A.1
Properties of the scalar inhomogeneous Green’s function

Throughout this section,
\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
will refer to the scalar’s green’s function which satisfies

\displaystyle\big[\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}+\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\big]\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

(51)

where
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\omega)=\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})+i\alpha(\omega)
. Here
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})
is the inhomogeneous permittivity distribution corresponding to the inhomogenuous media and
\alpha(\omega)
governs the loss in the media.
We point out that while physically
\omega>0
, it is convenient to consider the solution of Eq.
51
for
\omega<0
as well. We assume that for
\omega>0,\alpha(-\omega)=-\alpha(\omega)
— this ensures that for
\omega<0,\alpha(\omega)<0
and hence the outgoing solution to Eq.
51
for
\omega<0
decays exponentially as
|\mathbf{x}|\to\infty
. Furthermore, we also make a physically reasonable assumption of
\alpha(\omega)\to 0
as
|\omega|\to\infty
.

Property 1
:

\displaystyle\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};-\omega).

(52)

Proof
: This immediately follows by conjugating Eq.
51
and by noting that
\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},-\omega)=\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
.

Property 2
:

\displaystyle 2i\omega^{2}\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y}=\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)-\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega).

(53)

Proof
: We note that the scalar Green’s function is necessarily reciprocal i.e.
\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)=\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x};\omega)
. Therefore, it follows from Eq.
51
that

\displaystyle\big[\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{y}}+\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y};\omega)\big]\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}).

(54)

Multiplying this equation with
\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)
and integrating with respect to
\mathbf{y}
we obtain

\displaystyle\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y}+\omega^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varepsilon_{\alpha}(\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y}.

(55)

Conjugating Eq.
55
followed by swapping
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y}+\omega^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\varepsilon_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y}.

(56)

Subtracting Eq.
56
from Eq.
55
, we obtain Eq.
53
.

Property 3
: Assuming that
\exists\ \varepsilon_{\text{ub}}
such that
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})<\varepsilon_{\text{UB}}\ \forall\ \mathbf{x}
and
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})>0\ \forall\ \mathbf{x}
,

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)\ d\omega=-\frac{i\pi}{\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(57)

Proof
: Defining
\theta(\mathbf{x})=\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}-\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})
, Eq.
51
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\big[\nabla^{2}_{\mathbf{x}}+\omega^{2}(\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}+i\alpha(\omega))\big]\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\omega^{2}\theta(\mathbf{x})\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)+\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(58)

A Dyson series for
\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
can be deduced from Eq.
58
to obtain

\displaystyle\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega^{2k}\int_{\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}\dots\mathbf{y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\bigg[\prod_{k=0}^{n}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{y}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k+1};\omega)\bigg]_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}_{0}=\mathbf{x}\\
\mathbf{y}_{n+1}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\bigg[\prod_{k=1}^{n}\theta(\mathbf{y}_{k})d^{3}\mathbf{y}_{k}\bigg],

(59)

where
\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
satisfies

\displaystyle\big[\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}+\omega^{2}(\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}+i\alpha(\omega))\big]\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(60)

It can easily be seen from Eq.
60
that

\displaystyle\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}-|\mathbf{k}|^{2}+i\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}.

(61)

We next evaluate the integral in Eq.
57
using the expansion for
\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)
in Eq.
59
. Consider the following integral

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega^{2k+1}

\displaystyle\bigg[\prod_{k=0}^{n}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{y}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k+1};\omega)\bigg]d\omega=\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbf{k}_{0},\mathbf{k}_{1}\dots\mathbf{k}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\omega^{2k+1}\bigg[\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{e^{i\mathbf{k}_{k}\cdot(\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{y}_{k+1})}}{\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}-|\mathbf{k}_{k}|^{2}+i\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}\frac{d^{3}\mathbf{k}_{k}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\bigg]d\omega.

(62)

Noting that the integrand is analytic as a function of
\omega
in the upper complex half-plane, it follows that the integral with respect to
\omega
can be evaluated along
\omega=Re^{i\theta}
, with
R\to\infty
with
\theta
going from
\pi
to
0
. This immediately yields

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega^{2k+1}\bigg[\prod_{k=0}^{n}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{y}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k+1};\omega)\bigg]d\omega=-\frac{i\pi}{\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}}\prod_{k=0}^{n}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{y}_{k}-\mathbf{y}_{k+1}).

(63)

It thus follows from Eq.
59
that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega\text{G}_{\alpha}^{\text{ub}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)=-i\pi\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\theta^{k}(\mathbf{x})}{\varepsilon_{\text{ub}}^{k+1}}=-\frac{i\pi}{\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(64)

A.2
Verifying canonical commutation relations between
\psi(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi(\mathbf{x})

We begin by deriving the commutator
[\psi(\mathbf{x},\omega),\psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})]

\displaystyle[\psi(\mathbf{x},\omega),\psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]=\frac{1}{\pi}\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y};\omega)\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{y}.

(65)

Using Eq.

\displaystyle[\psi(\mathbf{x},\omega),\psi^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}{2\pi i}\big[\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)-\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)\big],

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})}{2\pi}\text{Im}\big[\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)\big].

(66)

Consider now the commutator
[\psi(\mathbf{x}),\pi(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})]

9a

Appendix B
Evaluating
a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega,\omega)
as
\alpha(\omega)\to 0

In this appendix, we detail the evaluation of the
\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
, where
a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
is defined in Eq.
II.2.2
. In particular, we show that in the limit of
\alpha(\omega)\to 0
,
a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
becomes independent of
\mathbf{x}
. Since
a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
are, by construction, bosonic annihilation operators, in order to show that
\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
is independent of
\mathbf{x}
, it is sufficient to show that
\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}[a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega),a_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]
is independent of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
. Using Eq.
II.2.2
and
[\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x};\omega),\phi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]=\delta^{3}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})
, we can explicitly evaluate this commutator to obtain

\displaystyle[a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\Omega;\omega),a_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(\omega)\omega}{4\pi^{4}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\\
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\end{subarray}}\frac{k^{2}k^{\prime 2}e^{i(k\Omega-k^{\prime}\Omega^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{y}}e^{i(k-k_{0}(\omega))\Omega\cdot\mathbf{x}}e^{-i(k^{\prime}-k_{0}(\omega))\Omega^{\prime}\cdot\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}}{(\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{0}-k^{2}+i\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega))(\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{0}-k^{\prime 2}-i\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega))}dkdk^{\prime}d^{3}\mathbf{y}.

(68)

Noting that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{i(k\Omega-k^{\prime}\Omega^{\prime})\cdot\mathbf{y}}d^{3}\mathbf{y}=\frac{8\pi^{3}\delta(k-k^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})}{k^{2}},

(69)

we obtain

\displaystyle[a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega),a_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]=\frac{2\alpha(\omega)\omega}{\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\frac{k^{2}e^{i(k-k_{0}(\omega))\Omega\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}}{(\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{0}-k^{2})^{2}+\omega^{4}\sigma^{2}(\omega)}dk.

(70)

We note that in the limit of
\alpha(\omega)\to 0
, the integral in the above equation can be approximated as follows

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\frac{k^{2}e^{i(k-k_{0}(\omega))\Omega\cdot(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}}{(\omega^{2}\varepsilon_{0}-k^{2})^{2}+\omega^{2}\sigma^{4}(\omega)}dk\approx\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{+}}\frac{1}{(k-\omega\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}})^{2}+\omega^{2}\sigma^{2}(\omega)/4\varepsilon_{0}}\approx\frac{\pi\sqrt{\varepsilon_{0}}}{2\omega\alpha(\omega)}.

(71)

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}[a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega),a_{\alpha}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\Omega^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}).

(72)

Thus, we conclude that the operators
a_{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
are independent of
\mathbf{x}
in the limit of
\alpha(\omega)\to 0
. Defining
a(\Omega;\omega)
via
a(\Omega;\omega)=\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}a_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\Omega;\omega)
, we thus obtain the following commutator

\displaystyle[a(\Omega;\omega),a^{\dagger}(\Omega^{\prime};\omega^{\prime})]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\delta^{2}(\Omega-\Omega^{\prime}).

(73)

Appendix D
Computing single and two-excitation Green’s functions

In this section, we outline the numerical procedures used for computing the single and two-excitation Green’s function that are needed for calculating the single and two-excitation scattering matrices.

D.1
Single-excitation Green’s function

The single-excitation Green’s function in time-domain,
\mathcal{G}(t;t^{\prime})
, is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,s)=\bra{g,\text{vac}}\mathcal{T}\big[\sigma(t)\sigma^{\dagger}(s)\big]\ket{g,\text{vac}},

(76)

where
\sigma(t)
and
\sigma^{\dagger}(t)
are the Heisenberg picture operators corresponding to the two-level system’s lowering and raising operators. Since
\sigma(t)\ket{g,\text{vac}}=0
and
\bra{g,\text{vac}}\sigma^{\dagger}(t)=0
for all
t
, we obtain an alternative representation of the single-excitation Green’s function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}(t,s)=\bra{e,\text{vac}}e^{-iH(t-s)}\ket{e,\text{vac}}\Theta(t\geq t^{\prime}),

(77)

i.e. it can be obtained by solving for the dynamics of an initially excited emitter emitting into the bosonic bath. To this end, we consider the system to initially be in the state
\ket{\psi(0)}=\sigma^{\dagger}\ket{G}
— using the noise operator representation of the bath, the following ansatz can be assumed for the state of the system

\ket{\psi(t)}=\bigg[A_{e}(t)\sigma^{\dagger}+\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}A_{g}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)\phi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)d^{3}\mathbf{x}\ d\omega\bigg]\ket{G},

(78)

with
A_{e}(0)=1
and
A_{g}(\mathbf{x},\omega;0)=0
. From the Schroedinger’s equation, it then immediately follows that

\displaystyle i\frac{dA_{e}(t)}{dt}=\omega_{0}

\displaystyle A_{e}(t)+iV_{0}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x};\omega)A_{g}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)d^{3}\mathbf{x}d\omega,

(79)

\displaystyle i\frac{dA_{g}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\omega A_{g}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)-iV_{0}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x};\omega)A_{e}(t).

(80)

Integrating the second equation, we obtain

A_{g}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)=-V_{0}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x};\omega)\int_{0}^{t}A_{e}(t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}dt^{\prime}.

(81)

Substituting this back into Eq.
79
and using Eq.
53
, we thus obtain

\frac{dA_{e}(t)}{dt}=-i\omega_{0}A_{e}(t)+\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}\int_{\omega=0}^{\infty}\text{Im}\big[\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}A_{e}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\ d\omega.

(82)

We note that while the above analysis assumes a loss
\alpha(\omega)
in the dielectric distribution under consideration, it can be taken to
0
in Eq.
82
since
\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}\text{Im}\big[\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)]
exists.

Numerical solution
: For a given permittivity distribution
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})
, Im
\big[G(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]
can be computed using various numerical methods (such as FDTD or FDFD). To compute
A_{e}(t)
, we approximate Im
\big[G(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]
as a sum of Lorentzians within a sufficiently large bandwidth around the emitter frequency

\text{Im}\big[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]=-\sum_{n}\frac{p_{n}}{(\omega-\omega_{n})^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}}.

(83)

Eq.
82
can the be expressed as

\displaystyle\frac{dA_{e}(t)}{dt}=-i\omega_{0}A_{e}(t)-\frac{V_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\sum_{n}\xi_{n}(t),

(84)

where

\displaystyle\xi_{n}(t)=\int_{t^{\prime}=0}^{t}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p_{n}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}}{(\omega-\omega_{n})^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}}A_{n}(t^{\prime})\ dt^{\prime}d\omega.

(85)

Assuming that
\omega_{n}/\gamma_{n}\gg 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p_{n}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}}{(\omega-\omega_{n})^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}}d\omega\approx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{p_{n}e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}}{(\omega-\omega_{n})^{2}+\gamma_{n}^{2}}d\omega=\frac{\pi p_{n}}{\gamma_{n}}e^{-i\omega_{n}(t-t^{\prime})}e^{-\gamma_{n}|t-t^{\prime}|},

(86)

which immediately yields

\displaystyle\xi_{n}(t)=\frac{\pi p_{n}}{\gamma_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i\omega_{n}(t-t^{\prime})}e^{-\gamma_{n}(t-t^{\prime})}A_{n}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

(87)

A differential equation for
\xi_{n}(t)
can be derived from this expression by differentiating it with respect to
t

\displaystyle\frac{d\xi_{n}(t)}{dt}=-(i\omega_{n}+\gamma_{n})\xi_{n}(t)+\frac{\pi p_{n}}{\gamma_{n}}A_{e}(t).

(88)

In frequency-domain scattering matrix calculations, the quantity of interest is the one-sided fourier transform of
A_{e}(t)

\displaystyle A_{e}(\omega)=\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{e}(t)e^{i\omega t}dt.

(89)

Taking the one-sided fourier transform of Eqs.
84
and
88
, we obtain an expression for
A_{e}(\omega)

\displaystyle A_{e}(\omega)=\bigg[i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+V_{0}^{2}\sum_{n}\frac{p_{n}/\gamma_{n}}{i(\omega_{n}-\omega)+\gamma_{n}}\bigg]^{-1}.

(90)

Weisskopf-Wigner approximation
: For permittivity distribution
\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})
with a frequency-response much broader than the line-width of the emitter, Eq.
82
can be approximated using the Weisskopf-Wigner formalism

\displaystyle\frac{dA_{e}(t)}{dt}=-\bigg(i\omega_{0}+\frac{\gamma}{2}\bigg)A_{e}(t),

(91)

where
\gamma=-2V_{0}^{2}\text{Im}\big[G(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega_{0})]
is the spontaneous emission decay rate of the emitter into the inhomogeneous bath. This yields an exponentially decaying solution for
A_{e}(t)
and a complex Lorentzian for
A_{e}(\omega)

\displaystyle A_{e}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{0}t}e^{-\gamma t/2}\text{ and }A_{e}(\omega)=\frac{1}{i(\omega_{0}-\omega)+\gamma/2}.

(92)

D.2
Two-excitation Green’s function

The evaluation of the two-excitation Green’s function for the two-level system can be done by following the procedure outlined in Ref.
shi2015multiphoton
. As an initial step, the two-level system is replaced with a boson with an onsite repulsion as described by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\text{0}}=\omega_{0}\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma+\frac{U_{0}}{2}(\sigma^{\dagger})^{2}\sigma^{2},

(93)

instead of
\omega_{0}\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma
and the commutation relation
[\sigma,\sigma^{\dagger}]=\sigma_{z}
replaced by the bosonic commutation
[\sigma,\sigma^{\dagger}]=1
. The two-level system is recovered from this model by taking
U_{0}\to\infty
. Next, we decompose the full hamiltonian of the TLS coupled to the inhomogeneous bath,
H
, as

\displaystyle H=H_{\text{L}}+H_{\text{NL}},

(94)

where
H_{\text{L}}=H_{\text{bath}}+H_{\text{int}}+\omega_{0}\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma
and
H_{\text{NL}}=U_{0}(\sigma^{\dagger})^{2}\sigma^{2}/2
. We point out that
H_{\text{L}}
is a quadratic hamiltonian when expressed in terms of the bosonic annihilation operators of the bath and
\sigma
, and consequently its dynamics are completely captured by two-point correlations between the different bosonic operators. We now consider the time-ordered expectation
\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\big[\sigma(t_{1})\sigma(t_{2})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{1})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{2})\big]\ket{\text{vac},g}
and rewrite it as

\displaystyle\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\big[\sigma(t_{1})\sigma(t_{2})

\displaystyle\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{1})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{2})\big]\ket{\text{vac},g}=

\displaystyle\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\bigg[\tilde{\sigma}(t_{1})\tilde{\sigma}(t_{2})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{1})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{2})\exp\bigg({-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde{H}_{\text{NL}}(t)dt}\bigg)\bigg]\ket{\text{vac},g},

(95)

where, for an operator
O
,
\tilde{O}(t)=e^{iH_{\text{L}}t}Oe^{-iH_{\text{L}}t}
. The exponential in Eq.
D.2
can be expanded to obtain

\displaystyle\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\big[

\displaystyle\sigma(t_{1})\sigma(t_{2})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{1})\sigma^{\dagger}(s_{2})\big]\ket{\text{vac},g}=\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\big[\tilde{\sigma}(t_{1})\tilde{\sigma}(t_{2})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{1})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{2})\big]\ket{\text{vac},g}+

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\dots\tau_{k}\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{k!}\bigg(-\frac{iU_{0}}{2}\bigg)^{k}\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\bigg[\tilde{\sigma}(t_{1})\tilde{\sigma}(t_{2})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{1})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{2})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\big(\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\tau_{k}))^{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(\tau_{k})\bigg]\ket{\text{vac},g}\prod_{i=1}^{k}d\tau_{i}.

(96)

Each of the time-ordered expectations in the summation in the above equation can be evaluated through an application of the Wick’s theorem since the operators involved in the expectation are Heisenberg operators with respect to a quadratic Hamiltonian
H_{\text{L}}

\displaystyle\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\big[\tilde{\sigma}(t_{1})\tilde{\sigma}(t_{2})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{1})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{2})\big]\ket{\text{vac},g}=g(t_{1};s_{1})g(t_{2};s_{2})+g(t_{1};s_{2})g(t_{2};s_{1}),

(97a)

\displaystyle\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\bigg[\tilde{\sigma}(t_{1})\tilde{\sigma}(t_{2})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{1})\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s_{2})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\big(\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\tau_{k}))^{2}\tilde{\sigma}^{2}(\tau_{k})\bigg]\ket{\text{vac},g}=

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ 2^{k-1}\sum_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}g(t_{1};\tau_{\mathcal{P}_{k}(1)})g(t_{2};\tau_{\mathcal{P}_{k}(1)})g(s_{1};\tau_{\mathcal{P}_{k}(k)})g(s_{2};\tau_{\mathcal{P}_{k}(k)})\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}g^{2}(\tau_{\mathcal{P}_{k}(i)};\tau_{\mathcal{P}_{k}(i+1)}).

(97b)

where
\mathcal{P}_{k}
is a
k-
element permutation of
\{1,2\dots k\}
and
g(t;s)=\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}[\tilde{\sigma}(t)\tilde{\sigma}^{\dagger}(s)]\ket{\text{vac},g}
. We note that
H_{\text{NL}}
does not impact the dynamics of the system within the single-excitation subspace, and consequently
g(t;s)=\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}[\sigma(t)\sigma^{\dagger}(s)]\ket{\text{vac},g}=A_{e}(t-s)\Theta(t\geq s)
where
A_{e}(t)
is defined in appendix
D.1
. Finally, we compute the two-excitation Green’s function
\mathcal{G}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
defined in Eq.
III.2
by Fourier transforming the time-ordered expectation in Eq.
D.2
and using Eqs.
D.2
and
97

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})=\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega_{1};\nu_{1})\mathcal{G}^{2}(\omega_{2};\nu_{2})+\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega_{1};\nu_{2})\mathcal{G}^{2}(\omega_{2};\nu_{1})+\mathcal{G}_{C}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu}),

(98)

where
\mathcal{G}^{1}(\omega;\nu)
is the single-excitation Green’s function and
\mathcal{G}^{2}_{C}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})
is the connected part of the two-excitation Green’s function

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{C}^{2}(\vec{\omega};\vec{\nu})=-i\pi\frac{U_{0}\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu_{1})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu_{2})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega_{1})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega_{2})}{1+iU_{0}\Gamma(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}\delta(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}),

(99)

where
\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega)
is defined in Eq.
35
and

\displaystyle\Gamma(E)=\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{e}^{2}(t)e^{iEt}dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{G}_{0}(E-\omega)\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega)d\omega.

(100)

Finally, in order to obtain the result for a two-level system, we take the limit of

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{C}^{2}(\omega_{1},\omega_{2};\nu_{1},\nu_{2})=-\pi\frac{\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu_{1})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\nu_{2})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega_{1})\mathcal{G}_{0}(\omega_{2})}{\Gamma(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})}\delta(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}-\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}).

Appendix E
Extension to Markovian localized systems with general level structures

The results presented in Section
III
can easily be generalized to localized quantum systems with complex level structures (e.g.
\Lambda
-systems, V-level systems etc.) under the Markovian approximation. We consider a localized system with Hamiltonian
H_{s}
interacting with the inhomogeneous bosonic bath with an interaction Hamiltonian identical to that in Eq.
III
with
\sigma
being the operator through which the low-dimensional system couples to the bosonic bath. Since the Heisenberg equations of motion for the bath operators (Eq.
29
) are independent of the level structure of the localized system, the
N-
particle scattering matrix element defined in Eq.
26
can still be related to the Green’s function of the low-dimensional system using Eq.
III
. In this section, we show that under the Markovian approximation, the low dimensional system’s Green’s functions are completely determined by the propagator corresponding to a dissipative (non-Hermitian) Hamiltonian defined entirely within the system’s Hilbert space. This is an extension of a similar well known result in waveguide QED
xu2015input
;
trivedi2018few
;
caneva2015quantum
to the case of an inhomogeneous high-dimensional bath.

For notational convenience and generality, we restrict our attention to evaluating the following time-ordered expectation value

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t_{1},t_{2}\dots t_{M})=\bra{\text{vac},g}\mathcal{T}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}(t_{i})\bigg]\ket{\text{vac},g},

(105)

where
c_{i}
are operators defined on the localized system’s Hilbert space and
\ket{g}
is the ground state of the localized system. We note that the
N-
excitation Green’s function defined in Eq.
III
is simply the Fourier transform of such a time-ordered expectation with the system operators
c_{i}
being either
\sigma
or
\sigma^{\dagger}
. Choosing to describe the Hilbert space of the bath via the noise operators
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega)
, this expectation can be expressed as the following path integral
weinberg1995quantum

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t_{1},t_{2}\dots t_{M})=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t_{i})=0\\
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t_{f})=0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}[\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t),\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)]\mathcal{D}s\ \bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}(t_{i})\bigg]e^{-i(\mathcal{S}_{\text{sys}}+\mathcal{S}_{\text{bath}}+\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}})},

(106)

where
t_{i}\to-\infty
,
t_{f}\to\infty
,
\mathcal{D}[\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t),\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)]
is the path integral measure over the Hilbert space of the bath,
\mathcal{D}s
is the path integral measure over the Hilbert space of the localized system,
\mathcal{S}_{\text{sys}}
is the classical action for the localized system from
t=t_{i}
to
t=t_{f}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{bath}}=\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\bigg(\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)-\omega\big|\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)\big|^{2}\bigg)dt\ d^{3}\mathbf{x}\ d\omega,

(107a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}=-iV_{0}\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\bigg(\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\omega;t)\sigma^{*}(t)-\psi_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\omega;t)\sigma(t)\bigg)dt\ d\omega,

(107b)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)=\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\int\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime};\omega)\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\omega;t)d^{3}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}.

(107c)

We note that the integrand in Eq.
106
is gaussian in
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
and consequently the integral with respect to
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
can be evaluated exactly using the stationary phase method. The stationary point of the integrand in Eq.
106
with respect to
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
is given by the classical path
\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
that minimizes the action
\mathcal{S}_{\text{bath}}+\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}
, which is governed by

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\phi}_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)=\omega\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)+iV_{0}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\sigma(t).

(108)

Since we are interested in evaluating expectations in which the bath is in the vacuum state,
\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t_{i})=0
— the above equation of motion can then be integrated to obtain

\displaystyle\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)=V_{0}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\text{G}_{\alpha}^{*}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\int_{t_{i}}^{t}\sigma(t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}dt^{\prime}.

(109)

Next, we perform a change of variables in the path integral from
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
to
\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)=\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)+\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
. Since
\mathcal{S}_{\text{bath}}+\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}
is quadratic in
\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
and
\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
is a stationary point of this action, it is expressible as a quadratic in
\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)
without any linear terms in
\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{bath}}+\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}=\mathcal{S}_{0}+\delta\mathcal{S},

(110)

where

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{S}=\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\bigg(\delta\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)-\omega\big|\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)\big|^{2}\bigg)dt\ d\mathbf{x}\ d\omega,

(111)

and
\mathcal{S}_{0}
is the action
\mathcal{S}_{\text{bath}}+\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}
evaluated at
\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}

\displaystyle=-iV_{0}\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\int_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\bigg(\frac{\omega^{2}\alpha(\omega)}{\pi}\bigg)^{1/2}\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x};\omega)\phi_{\text{cl}}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)\sigma^{*}(t)dt\ d^{3}\mathbf{x}\ d\omega,

\displaystyle=\frac{iV_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\int_{t^{\prime}=t_{i}}^{t}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\text{Im}\big[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]\sigma^{*}(t)\sigma(t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}dt\ dt^{\prime}\ d\omega,

(112)

wherein in the second step we have used the fact that
\lim_{\alpha(\omega)\to 0}\text{Im}[\text{G}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)]
exists and have explicitly taken this limit. The expectation
\mathcal{E}(t_{1},t_{2}\dots t_{N})
then evaluates to

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t_{1},t_{2}\dots t_{M})=\mathcal{A}\int\mathcal{D}s\ \bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}(t_{i})\bigg]e^{-i\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}}

(113)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{sys}}+\frac{iV_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\int_{t^{\prime}=t_{i}}^{t}\int_{\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\text{Im}\big[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)\big]\sigma^{*}(t)\sigma(t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}dt\ dt^{\prime}\ d\omega,

(114)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t_{i})=0\\
\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t_{f})=0\end{subarray}}\mathcal{D}[\delta\phi_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t),\delta\phi^{*}(\mathbf{x},\omega;t)]e^{-i\delta\mathcal{S}}.

(115)

We immediately notice that
\mathcal{A}
is just the expectation
\bra{\text{vac}}e^{-iH_{\text{bath}}(t_{f}-t_{i})}\ket{\text{vac}}
and thus evaluates to 1. This yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(t_{1},t_{2}\dots t_{M})=\int\mathcal{D}s\ \bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}(t_{i})\bigg]e^{-i\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}}.

(116)

We note that the effective action
\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}
is, in general, a nonlocal action consequently making the integral in Eq.
116
difficult to evaluate directly. However, within the Markovian approximation
carmichael2009open
, this effective action becomes local. This immediately follows from Eq.
114
by noting that the Markovian approximation around a (system resonance) frequency
\omega_{0}
approximates
\text{Im}[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega)]
by
\text{Im}[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega_{0})]
and extends the integral with respect to
\omega
from
-\infty
to
\infty
to obtain:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}=\mathcal{S}_{\text{sys}}+\frac{i\gamma}{2}\int_{t=t_{i}}^{t_{f}}|\sigma(t)|^{2}dt

(117)

where
\gamma=V_{0}^{2}\text{Im}[\text{G}(\mathbf{x}_{d},\mathbf{x}_{d};\omega_{0})]
. From this local lagrangian, we immediately obtain that the expectation in Eq.
116
can be evaluated by evolving the localized system with respect to a dissipative (non-Hermitian) Hamiltonian
H_{\text{eff}}
defined by:

\displaystyle H_{\text{eff}}=H_{\text{sys}}-\frac{i\gamma}{2}\sigma^{\dagger}\sigma

(118)
