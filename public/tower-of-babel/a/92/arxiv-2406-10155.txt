On Twisted Spacetimes: a new class of Galilean cosmological models
Daniel de la Fuente, Rafael María Rubio, Jose Torrente

Abstract
Within the generalized Newton-Cartan theory, Galilean Twisted spacetimes are introduced as dual models of the well-known relativistic twisted spacetimes. As a natural generalization, torqued vector fields in Galilean spacetimes are defined, showing that the local structure of a Galilean spacetime admitting a timelike torqued vector field is given by a Twisted spacetime. In addition, several results assuring the global splitting as Twisted spacetime are obtained. On the other hand, completeness of free falling observers is studied, as well as general geodesic completeness.

Introduction

The geometric formulation of Newton’s theory of gravity began with E. Cartan in the early twentieth century
[
10
,
11
]
. But it was only in the second half of the century that the Newton-Cartan theory was given significant attention by various authors.
In the 1960s Malament studied the Lorentzian and Galilean groups, proving that Newtonian gravity constitutes a limit of General Relativity and how free particles are represented by geodesic curves of certain connections in both theories.
Additionally, Trautman showed the existence of a symmetric connection in both theories
[
39
]
, arising due to the local nature of physical laws and simultaneously encoding the inertia principle and gravitational forces. Concretely, classical Newtonian gravitation is formulated as a covariant theory, showing that certain results previously considered characteristic or singular of the theory of Relativity are also shared by the (geometric) Newton-Cartan Gravitational Theory. In fact, Newtonian gravity is explained by the curvature of a connection in the spacetime, although in this case it no longer arises from any semi-Riemannian metric. Furthermore, in the geometric formulation of Newtonian Gravity Theory, the spacetime structure is dynamic in the sense that it participates in the development of physics rather than being a fixed backdrop (see
[
24
]
and references therein).

Later in that century, several steps were taken towards the generalization of this theory, such as the introduction of new Newtonian models satisfying the cosmological principle
[
30
]
, obtaining the Galilean analogue of relativistic Robertson-Walker spacetimes. In
[
18
]
, Newtonian theory achieves the status of a gauge theory and is shown to be a limit of the Einstein-Klein-Gordon theory. This view of the theory as a limit of General Relativity continued to develop during these years
[
22
]
, concluding also that the results obtained from Newton-Cartan cosmology were similar on not too large scales to those derived from Einstein’s theory
[
34
,
35
]
.

In the last few decades, the simplicity of certain Newtonian models in Newton-Cartan theory, in contrast to the computational complexity of General Relativity, has led to new applications of this non-relativistic theory in Cosmology
[
8
]
, Hydrodynamics
[
19
]
, quantum collapse
[
32
]
, AdS/CFT correspondence
[
23
]
, condensed matter systems
[

]
, quantum Hall effect
[
20
]
and other related phenomena. The many applications of Newton-Cartan theory have motivated a review of the geometric structures associated with generalized Newton-Cartan theory
[

and new extensions such as Newton-Cartan Supergravity
[

]
and non-relativistic Strings
[

. Furthermore, new Newtonian models have been obtained, including Galilean Generalized Robertson-Walker spacetimes
[

, the Newtonian Gödel spacetime
[

, standard stationary Galilean spacetimes
[

, as well as the embedding of these geometries in 5-dimensional Lorentzian manifolds

.

In this manuscript, a wide family of Galilean spacetimes is presented, the Galilean Twisted spacetimes (Sect.
3
), which constitute a generalization of the classical Galilean Robertson-Walker spacetimes (RW), and even of the Galilean Generalized Robertson-Walker spacetimes (GGRW). In fact, Definition
3.1
states

Given a real interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, a Riemannian manifold
(F,h)
and a positive smooth function
f\in C^{\infty}(I\times F)
, a Galilean Twisted (GT) spacetime is given by the tuple
(I\times F,\Omega=\mathrm{d}t,g=f^{2}\,\pi_{F}^{*}h,\nabla)
where
t\coloneq\pi_{I}
and
\nabla
is the only symmetric Galilean connection on
I\times F
such that
\nabla_{\pt}\pt=0
and
\rot\pt=0
.

According to the cosmological principle, RW spacetimes model a spatially homogeneous and isotropic universe composed of matter (galaxies) subject only to the action of its own gravity. Thus, the “absolute space at each moment in absolute time” must be locally Euclidean with constant sectional curvature. Furthermore, as large-scale experimental measurements (redshift) indicate, the expansion is assumed to be homogeneous, i.e., the expansion rate is the same at all points at a fixed (absolute) time.
However, while the hypothesis of spatial homogeneity and isotropy may be a reasonable first approximation to the large scale structure of the universe, it could not be appropriate when we consider a more accurate scale. This led to the introduction of the family of GGRWs spacetimes, where the absolute space is modelled by an arbitrary Riemannian manifold, which does not necessarily have to be of constant curvature. Finally, the Twisted spacetimes presented here assume a non-homogeneous expansion rate, which means that it depends not only on the moment in time but also on the point in space. In other words, the “Hubble constant” is no longer constant.

Twisted products have been studied in the relativistic setting
[
12
,
13
,
25
,
36
]
. They have been analyzed in relation to geometric models of fluids, resulting in a characterization in terms of the stress-energy tensor of an imperfect fluid
[
26
]
and obtaining the properties of static perfect fluids in Twisted Lorentzian spacetimes
[
21
]
. Similar results could be explored in the non-relativistic setting introduced here.

A Galilean Twisted (GT) spacetime possesses an infinitesimal symmetry given by the existence of a timelike irrotational and (spatially) conformally Leibnizian field of observers. Several geometrical properties, as well as physical interpretations of this family of spacetimes, are given in Sections
4
and
5

From a physical perspective, this result is relevant since the completeness of the timelike trajectories is crucial in order to have a consistent theory (without observers or with observers that disappear or appear suddenly, it would not be possible to do Physics).

Let

A

\Omega

is a smooth, bilinear, symmetric and positive definite 2-covariant tensor

Given a Leibnizian spacetime

, the points

are called

. If
p\in M

\Omega_{p}
is known as the
absolute clock

is called the

at

[
6

\Omega_{p}(v)=0
(resp.
\Omega_{p}(v)\neq 0

(resp.

(resp.
\Omega_{p}(v)<0

On the other hand, an

is defined as a smooth curve

standard timelike unit

In this Leibnizian setting, the synchronizability of observers is intrinsic to the Leibnizian structure, in contrast to the relativistic notions, which is relative to each field of observers. In fact, if
\Omega\wedge\mathrm{d}\Omega=0
(i.e., the smooth distribution

[
40

\Omega=\lambda\mathrm{d}T

\{T=\text{constant}\}

T

\mathrm{d}\Omega=0

proper time locally synchronizable
. Under this assumption, locally we can express
\Omega=\mathrm{d}T

[

Let
(M,\Omega,g)
and
(\widetilde{M},\widetilde{\Omega},\widetilde{g})
be two Leibnizian spacetimes. A diffeomorphism
\lambda\colon M\to\widetilde{M}
is called

\lambda^{*}\widetilde{\Omega}=\Omega
and
\lambda^{*}\widetilde{g}=g

To give complete physical meaning to a Leibnizian spacetime, the principle of inertia has to be included via an affine connection
\nabla

\nabla
compatible with the absolute clock
\Omega

(1)

\nabla\Omega=0

X(\Omega(Y))=\Omega(\nabla_{X}Y)

,

(2)

\nabla g=0
, that is,
Z(g(V,W))=g(\nabla_{Z}V,W)+g(V,\nabla_{Z}W)
, for all
Z\in\Gamma(TM)
and
V,W\in\Gamma(\An)
.

Note that, thanks to condition (1), the right-hand side of item (2) is well defined.
Any such
\nabla
is called a
Galilean connection
. Consequently, the Leibnizian spacetime does not determine a canonical Galilean connection. The tuple
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
is known as a

. Recall that if its torsion tensor vanishes, a connection is said to be

\text{Tor}(X,Y)=\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X-[X,Y]=0,\quad\forall X,Y\in\Gamma(TM).

From a physical viewpoint, it is often convenient to choose a symmetric connection because it is determined by the associated geodesics
[
38

[
7

\Omega\circ\tor=\mathrm{d}\Omega\,.

(1)

From now on, we will only consider symmetric Galilean connections, then using equation (
1

\mathrm{d}\Omega=0

Given two Galilean spacetimes

On the other hand, given a Galilean spacetime

2.1
Spatially conformally Leibnizian spacetimes

Let us recall the notion of spatially conformally Leibnizian vector field, which appears in several classes of cosmological models in the context of the generalized Newton-Cartan theory (see
[
16
]
) and will be key to obtaining some of our results.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g)
be a Leibnizian spacetime and
K
a vector field satisfying

\Omega([K,V])=0\quad\mathrm{for}\;\mathrm{all}\quad V\in\Gamma(\mathrm{An}(\Omega)).

(3)

The vector field
K
is called
spatially conformally Leibnizian
if the Lie derivative of the absolute space metric satisfies

{\cal L}_{{}_{K}}g=2\rho\,g,

(4)

for some smooth function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(M)
. The function
\rho
is called conformal factor of
g
associated to
K
.

Notice that condition (
3
) ensures that the previous notion is well defined. As a direct consequence, we have

K(g(V,W))=2\rho\,g(V,W)+g([K,V],W)+g([K,W],V),\,

(5)

for all
V,W\in\Gamma(\An)
.

The next result provides another condition to guarantee that (
3
) holds for a vector field.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a Galilean spacetime with symmetric connection
\nabla
. Then, a vector field
K
satisfies equation
(
3
)
if and only if the function
\Omega(K)
is spatially invariant, i.e.,
V(\Omega(K))=0
,
\forall V\in\Gamma\big(\mathrm{An}(\Omega)\big)
.

Finally,
K\in\Gamma(TM)
is called a
spatially Leibnizian
vector field if the conformal factor
\rho
vanishes identically, or equivalently, if its local flows
\Phi_{s}
preserve the absolute space, i.e.,
\Phi_{s}^{*}g=g
. If, in addition, they also preserve the absolute clock, i.e.,
\Phi_{s}^{*}\Omega=\Omega
, then
K
is called
Leibnizian
.

2.2
Spacelike differential map and spacelike gradient

To conclude the preliminaries section, let us introduce some notions that will be used later. Given a smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
on a Leibnizian spacetime
(M,\Omega,g)
, we define the
spacelike gradient
of
\varphi
, as the section
\grad\varphi\in\Gamma(\An)
, determined by

g(\grad\varphi,V)=\mathrm{d}\varphi(V),\quad\forall V\in\Gamma(\An)\,.

Implicitly
\Omega(\grad\varphi)=0
. This notion is a generalization of the gradient in semi-Riemannian manifolds.

When a smooth manifold
M
splits as a product
M=I\times F
, for
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
an open interval and
F
certain smooth manifold, the differential of a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(I\times F)
can be canonically split. We can identify
T_{(t,p)}(I\times F)\equiv T_{t}I\times T_{p}F\equiv\mathbb{R}\times T_{p}F
. Then, define

\begin{split}\varphi_{t}\in C^{\infty}(I\times F),\quad{}&\varphi_{t}(t,p)\coloneq\mathrm{d}\varphi_{(t,p)}(\pt)\,,\\
\mathrm{d}^{F}\varphi_{(t,p)}\colon T_{p}F\to\mathbb{R},\quad{}&\mathrm{d}^{F}\varphi_{(t,p)}(v)\coloneq\mathrm{d}\varphi_{(t,p)}(\overline{v})\,,\end{split}

where
\pt
is the canonical vector field associated to the projection onto the first component
t=\pi_{I}
and
\overline{v}\in\mathbb{R}\times T_{p}F
is the horizontal lift of a vector
v\in T_{p}F
. If
(F,h)
is a Riemannian manifold, the usual Riemannian gradient
\grad^{h}
applies. Thus, we will denote
\mathrm{d}^{h}=\mathrm{d}^{F}
to unify the notation although the differential map only depends on the differential structure. From now on, the horizontal lift of a vector field
W\in\Gamma(TF)
to a product
I\times F
will be denoted by
\overline{W}\in\Gamma(T(I\times F))\equiv\Gamma(\mathbb{R}\times TF)
. Moreover, the induced differential on
F
can be lifted as

\overline{\mathrm{d}^{h}}\varphi_{(t,p)}\colon\mathbb{R}\times T_{p}F\to\mathbb{R},\quad\overline{\mathrm{d}^{h}}\varphi_{(t,p)}(a\pt+\overline{v})\coloneq\mathrm{d}^{h}\varphi_{(t,p)}(v)\,,

for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
v\in T_{p}F
.

On the other hand, we say that
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(I\times F)
is bounded from below (resp. from above)
along finite times
if,
\forall[s_{1},s_{2}]\subset I
, there exists a constant
C_{s_{1},s_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\varphi>C_{s_{1},s_{2}}
(resp.
\varphi<C_{s_{1},s_{2}}
) in
[s_{1},s_{2}]\times F
. A smooth function
\varphi
is
bounded along finite times
if it is bounded from below and from above along finite times. Obviously,
\inf\varphi>A
,
A\in\mathbb{R}
, along finite times is equivalent to
\varphi
being bounded from below along finite times for constants greater than
A
.

3
Galilean Twisted Spacetimes

We introduce a new class of Galilean spacetimes, which extends the family of cosmological models known as Galilean Generalized Robertson-Walker spacetimes (see
[
16
,
30
]
).

Definition 3.1
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
,
0\in I
, be an open real interval,
(F,h)
be an n-dimensional connected Riemannian manifold, and
f\in C^{\infty}(I\times F)
be a positive smooth function. A Galilean spacetime
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
is called
Galilean Twisted
(GT) spacetime if
M=I\times F
,
\Omega=\mathrm{d}\pi_{I}
,
g
is the restriction to the vector bundle
\An
of the following (degenerate) metric on M:

\overline{g}=f^{2}\pi_{F}^{*}h\,,

(6)

where
\pi_{I},\pi_{F}
are the canonical projections onto the open interval I and the manifold
F
, respectively, and
\nabla
is the only symmetric Galilean connection on
M
such that

\nabla_{\partial_{t}}\partial_{t}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{Rot}\partial_{t}=0\,,

(7)

where
\partial_{t}=\partial/\partial t
is the global coordinate vector field associated with
t\coloneq\pi_{I}
.

Abusing the notation, we will denote
g=f^{2}\pi_{F}^{*}h
.
Analogously to the Riemannian twisted product, the Riemannian manifold
(F,h)
is called fiber.

The vector field
\pt
is a field of observers in
M
, and the observers in
\partial_{t}
are called
commovil observers
in analogy with both the Galilean and relativistic Generalized Robertson-Walker spacetimes
[
1
,
16
]
. Notice that the conditions (
7
) determine the Galilean connection
\nabla
(see equation (
2
)).

Example 3.2
.

Consider the GT spacetime
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathrm{d}\pi_{1},g=f^{2}\pi_{2}^{*}\langle\,,\rangle,\nabla)
, where
\pi_{1}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and
\pi_{2}\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
are the canonical projections onto the first and second factors, respectively,
\langle\,,\rangle
is the usual Euclidean product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If
f
does not depend on the fiber
F=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e.,
f=f(t)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, then we have a GGRW spacetime
[
16
]
. In the simplest case where
f=constant
, the Galilean connection is just the standard flat connection of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

4
Geodesic Completeness of GT spacetimes

In this section we are going to study the completeness of the geodesics in a GT spacetime. In particular, this will allow us to know if the free falling observers live forever.

Remark 4.1
.

From the compatibility condition of a Galilean connection
\nabla
with the absolute clock
\Omega
in a Galilean spacetime, it is clear that the geodesics
\gamma
maintain their causal character. That is,
\Omega(\gamma^{\prime})
is constant along the trajectory of
\gamma
. Then, the two relevant cases to consider will be spacelike geodesics (
\Omega(\gamma^{\prime})=0
) and free falling observers (
\Omega(\gamma^{\prime})=1
).

Given a geodesic
\gamma:J\longrightarrow M
in a GT spacetime
(M=I\times F,\Omega,g,\nabla)
, where
J\subset\mathbb{R}
denote an interval in the real line, we will be able to derive the corresponding equation of its projection
\pi_{F}(\gamma)
on the fiber
F
. The following lemma gives us the necessary computations.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(M=I\times F,\Omega=\mathrm{d}\pi_{I},g=f^{2}\pi_{F}^{*}h,\nabla)
be a GT spacetime. If
W_{1},W_{2}\in\Gamma(TF)
, then

\begin{split}\nabla_{\pt}\overline{W}={}&\nabla_{\overline{W}}\pt=u_{t}\overline{W}\,,\\
\nabla_{\overline{W_{1}}}\overline{W_{2}}={}&\overline{\nabla^{h}_{W_{1}}W_{2}}+\mathrm{d}^{h}u(W_{1})\overline{W_{2}}+\mathrm{d}^{h}u(W_{2})\overline{W_{1}}-h(W_{1},W_{2})\overline{\grad^{h}u}\,,\end{split}

(8)

where
u\coloneq\log f
and
\grad^{h}u
denotes the usual gradient on the Riemannian manifold
(F,h)
.

Proof.

It is enough to apply equation (
2
) for
V=\overline{U}
,
U\in\Gamma(TF)
, since a local reference frame
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in
TF
lift to a local reference frame
\{\pt\}\cup\{\overline{E_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in
TM
.
∎

The previous formulae (
8
) allow us to obtain the equations of the projections of the geodesics on the fiber of a GT spacetime.

Remark 4.3
.

Let
\gamma
be a geodesic
\gamma:J\longrightarrow M
in a GT spacetime
(M=I\times F,\Omega,g,\nabla)
, where
J\subset\mathbb{R}
denote an interval in the real line. If
\gamma
is a free falling observer, we have

\gamma(s)=(s,\sigma(s))\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma^{\prime}(s)=(\pt\circ\gamma)(s)+\overline{\sigma^{\prime}}(s)\,,

where
\sigma

Galilean Torqued Vector Fields

Along this section, we will see that there exists a natural generalization of GT spacetimes in a rich geometrical structure characterized by the existence of a distinguished vector field on the Galilean spacetime. In fact, the main theorem will state that these Galilean spacetimes are locally GT spacetimes.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
(M=I\times F,\Omega=\mathrm{d}t,g=f^{2}\pi_{F}^{*}h,\nabla)
be a GT spacetime. Then, the vector field
K=f\pt
satisfies

\nabla_{X}K=f_{t}X+\alpha(X)K\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(K)=0,\quad\forall X\in\Gamma(TM)\,,

(24)

where
\alpha=\overline{\mathrm{d}^{h}}(\log f)\in\bigwedge^{1}(M)
(see subsection

Proof.

Let
X\in\Gamma(TM)
be a vector field. Consider
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
a local reference frame in
TF
that lifts to a local reference frame
\{\pt\}\cup\{\overline{E_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
in
TM

such that

X=g_{0}\pt+\sum_{i}g_{i}\overline{E_{i}}\,.

Then, if

, we have

\begin{split}\nabla_{X}K={}&X(f)\pt+f\nabla_{X}\pt\\
={}&X(u)K+f\sum_{i}g_{i}u_{t}\overline{E_{i}}\\
={}&X(u)K+fu_{t}(X-g_{0}\pt)\\
={}&f_{t}X+\overline{\mathrm{d}^{h}}u(X)K\,.\end{split}

∎

As we announced, a generalization of GT spacetimes is defined by means of a vector field satisfying an analogous expression to equation (
24
).

Definition 6.2
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
a Galilean spacetime. A future-pointing timelike vector field
K\in\Gamma(TM)
satisfying

\nabla_{X}K=\rho X+\alpha(X)K\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(K)=0,\quad\forall X\in\Gamma(TM)\,,

(25)

for some
\rho\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\alpha\in\bigwedge^{1}(M)
, is called a
Galilean torqued vector field
. The function
\rho
is called the
torqued function
and
\alpha
the
torqued form
.

The torqued function and form are fully determined by the function
\Omega(K)
, as we prove in the following lemma.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a spacetime and suppose it admits a Galilean torqued vector field
K\in\Gamma(TM)
with torqued function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(M)
and torqued form
\alpha\in\bigwedge^{1}(M)
. Then,

\alpha(V)=\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}V\big(\Omega(K)\big),\quad\forall V\in\Gamma(\An)\,.

(26)

and

\rho=\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}K\big(\Omega(K)\big)\,.

(27)

Proof.

Consider the field of observers
Z=\frac{1}{\Omega(K)}K
. Then, for any
V\in\Gamma(\An)
,

\begin{split}\nabla_{V}Z={}&V\big(\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}\big)K+\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}\nabla_{V}K\\
={}&\tfrac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}V+\left[\tfrac{\alpha(V)}{\Omega(K)}+V\big(\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}\big)\right]K\,.\end{split}

(28)

Now, making use of the compatibility of the connection with
\Omega
we have
\Omega(\nabla_{V}Z)=V(\Omega(Z))=0
. Then, from (
28
) we get (
26
).

On the other hand, from equation (
25
) we have
\nabla_{K}K=\rho K
. Consequently,

K(\Omega(K))=\Omega(\nabla_{K}K)=\rho\Omega(K)\,.

Thus, it follows expression (
27
).
∎

Remark 6.4
.

Let
K
be a Galilean torqued vector field in a Galilean spacetime
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
with torqued function
\rho\in C^{\infty}(M)
and torqued form
\alpha\in\bigwedge^{1}(M)
. Consider the associated field of observers
Z\coloneq\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}K
. Note that we have obtained in the proof of the lemma the following identity,

\nabla_{V}Z=\tfrac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}V,\quad\forall V\in\Gamma(\An)\,.

(29)

Since
V(\Omega(Z))=0
for all
V\in\Gamma(\An)
, from equation (
29
) we can compute

\begin{split}L_{Z}g(V,W)={}&Zg(V,W)-g([Z,V],W)-g(V,[Z,W])\\
={}&g(\nabla_{V}Z,W)+g(V,\nabla_{W}Z)\\
={}&2\tfrac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}g(V,W)\,,\end{split}

for all
V,W\in\Gamma(\An)
(see subsection
2.1
).
Therefore,
Z
is a spatially conformally Leibnizian vector field.

\diamond

Finally, we obtain the main theorem of this section, which determines the local structure of Galilean spacetimes admitting a Galilean torqued vector field.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a Galilean spacetime. If it admits a torqued vector field
K\in\Gamma(TM)
, then for each
p\in M
, there exists an open neighborhood of p,
\mathcal{U}
, and a Galilean diffeomorphism
\Psi\colon N\to\mathcal{U}
, where
N
is a GT spacetime.

Proof.

This is in fact a generalization of the corresponding proof of the local structure Theorem for ICL spactimes
[
16
, Th. 12]
. In order to
make the paper self-contained we give the details.

Suppose there exists a torqued vector field
K
. Let
\rho\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\alpha\in\bigwedge^{1}(M)
be the associated torqued function and one-form, and
Z\coloneq\frac{1}{\Omega(K)}K
be the associated field of observers. Given an event
p\in M
, take a neighbourhood
U_{p}
of
p
in the leaf
\mathcal{F}_{p}
of the foliation induced by
\Omega
, and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
,
0\in I
, a suitable interval such that the local flow of
Z
,

\begin{split}\Psi\colon I\times U_{p}\to{}&M\\
(s,q)\longmapsto{}&\phi_{s}(q)\,,\end{split}

is well-defined and one-to-one. It satisfies

\mathrm{d}\Psi_{(s,q)}(1,0)=Z_{\phi_{s}(q)},\quad\mathrm{d}\Psi_{(s,q)}(0,v)=\big(\mathrm{d}\phi_{s}\big)_{q}(v)\,,

for all
(s,q)\in I\times U_{p}
and
v\in T_{p}U_{p}
. Now, if we identify
t\equiv\pi_{I}
and
\pt\equiv(1,0)
, it follows by construction

\Omega(\mathrm{d}\Psi(\pt))=\Omega(Z)=1=\mathrm{d}t(\pt)\,.

Moreover, the flow
\phi_{s}
of
Z
preserves
\Omega
on spacelike vector fields,
L_{Z}\Omega(V)=0
,
\forall V\in\Gamma(\An)
. Then,

\Omega\left(\mathrm{d}\Psi_{(s,q)}(0,v)\right)=\Omega\left((\mathrm{d}\phi_{s})_{q}(v)\right)=(\phi_{s}^{*}\Omega)_{q}(v)=\Omega_{q}(v)=0.

Consequently,
\Psi^{*}\Omega=\mathrm{d}t
, and each level set of
t
corresponds with certain open set
\mathcal{U}\cap\mathcal{F}_{a}
of some leaf of the foliation
\mathcal{F}
induced by
\Omega
.

From Remark
6.4
,
Z
is spatially conformally Leibnizian:

L_{Z}g(V,W)=\tfrac{2\rho}{\Omega(K)}g(V,W),\quad\forall V,W\in\Gamma(\operatorname{An}(\Omega))\,.

(30)

Consider
v,w\in T_{q}U_{p}
. If we define

\eta_{q}(s)\coloneq\Psi^{*}g_{(s,q)}((0,v),(0,w))=g_{\phi_{s}(q)}\left(\big(\mathrm{d}\phi_{s}\big)_{q}(v),\big(\mathrm{d}\phi_{s}\big)_{q}(w)\right),

it follows from equation (
30
),

\eta_{q}^{\prime}(s)=\left.\tfrac{2\rho}{\Omega(K)}\right|_{\phi_{s}(q)}\eta_{q}(s)\,.

Then, taking

f(s,q)\coloneq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}\left(\phi_{l}(q)\right)\mathrm{d}l\right)\,,

(31)

we obtain
\eta_{q}(s)=f^{2}(s,q)g_{q}(v,w)
. Therefore,
\Psi^{*}g(s,q)=f^{2}(s,q)g_{q}
.

Finally, from equation (
29
), and any arbitrary tensor field
V\in\Gamma(\An)
, we have

\operatorname{Rot}(Z)(V,W)=g\left(\nabla_{V}Z,W\right)-g\left(\nabla_{W}Z,V\right)=0\,,

and

\nabla_{Z}Z=\tfrac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}Z+Z\big(\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}\big)K=0\,.

Thus,
Z
is an irrotational field of observers. Since
\mathrm{d}\Psi(\pt)=Z
, from
[
7
, Cor. 28]
, the Galilean connection
\nabla
is
\Psi-
related to the corresponding GT connection
\nabla^{GT}
in
I\times U_{p}
. Therefore,
\Psi
is a Galilean diffeomorphism on

\big(I\times U_{p},\mathrm{d}\pi_{I},f^{2}\,\pi_{U_{p}}^{*}g,\nabla^{GT}\big)\,.

∎

Note that the conclusions are similar when we consider ICL spacetimes
[
16
]
. In this case, the distinction lies in the fact that the torqued factor is not necessarily spatially uniform.

7
Global splitting

This section is devoted to give natural geometric conditions to characterize a Galilean spacetime as a global GT spacetime. We will use strongly the geometric properties satisfied by a Galilean torqued vector field.

In the following Lemma, we define an auxiliary semi-Riemannian metric in terms of the Galilean structure of a Galilean spacetime. The spacelike gradient defined in subsection
2.2
allows us to write the associated Levi-Civita connection in terms of the Galilean connection.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a Galilean spacetime and let
Z\in\Gamma(TM)
be an irrotational spatially conformally Leibnizian field of observers. Given a smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\epsilon\in\{-1,+1\}
, define on
M
the semi-Riemannian metric

\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\coloneq\epsilon\Omega\otimes\Omega+e^{2\varphi}g(P^{Z}\cdot,P^{Z}\cdot)\,.

Then, the associated Levi-Civita connection
\overline{\nabla}
of
\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}
is given by

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y=

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y+\mathrm{d}\varphi(X)P^{Z}Y+\mathrm{d}\varphi(Y)P^{Z}X-\Omega(X)\Omega(Y)\mathcal{G}^{Z}

(32)

\displaystyle-\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y)\grad\varphi-\epsilon\big(Z(\varphi)+\tilde{\rho}\big)\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)Z\,,

for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(TM)
, where
\mathcal{G}^{Z}
is the gravitational field of
Z
,
\tilde{\rho}
is the conformal factor associated to
Z
and
\grad\varphi
is the spacelike gradient of
\varphi
in the Galilean structure.

Proof.

Consider
X,Y\in\Gamma(TM)
and
V\in\Gamma(\An)
. Koszul formula
[
31
, Th. 11]
gives

\displaystyle 2\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y,V\right)=

\displaystyle X\left(\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(Y,V)\right)+Y\left(\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(X,V)\right)-V\left(\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(X,Y)\right)

(33)

\displaystyle+\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}([X,Y],V)-\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}([X,V],Y)-\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}([Y,V],X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2\varphi}\left\{2X(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}Y,V\right)+X\left(g\left(P^{Z}Y,V\right)\right)+2Y(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}X,V\right)\right.

\displaystyle+Y\left(g\left(P^{Z}X,V\right)\right)-2V(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)-V\left(g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)\right)

\displaystyle+g\left(\big[P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\big],V\right)-\Omega(X)g\left(\big[P^{Z}Y,Z\big],V\right)+\Omega(Y)g\left(\big[P^{Z}X,Z\big],V\right)

\displaystyle-g\left(\big[P^{Z}X,V\big],P^{Z}Y\right)+\Omega(X)g\left([V,Z],P^{Z}Y\right)

\displaystyle\left.-g\left(\big[P^{Z}Y,V\big],P^{Z}X\right)+\Omega(Y)g\left([V,Z],P^{Z}X\right)\right\}

Considering
\omega(Z)=0
, we can derive from equation (
2
) the following expression:

\displaystyle 2\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\nabla_{X}Y,V\right)={}

\displaystyle 2e^{2\varphi}g\left(P^{Z}\left(\nabla_{X}Y\right),V\right)

(34)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e^{2\varphi}\left\{X\left(g\left(P^{Z}(Y),V\right)\right)+Y\left(g\left(P^{Z}(X),V\right)\right)-V\left(g\left(P^{Z}(X),P^{Z}(Y)\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle+2\Omega(X)\Omega(Y)g\left(\mathcal{G}^{Z},V\right)+\Omega(X)\left(g\left(\big[Z,P^{Z}(Y)\big],V\right)-g\left([Z,V],P^{Z}(Y)\right)\right)

\displaystyle-\Omega(Y)\left(g\left(\big[Z,P^{Z}(X)\big],V\right)+g\left([Z,V],P^{Z}(X)\right)\right)+g\left(\big[P^{Z}(X),P^{Z}(Y)\big],V\right)

\displaystyle-g\left(\big[P^{Z}(Y),V\big],P^{Z}(X)\right)\left.-g\left(\big[P^{Z}(X),V\big],P^{Z}(Y)\right)\right\}\,.

Substituting (
34
) in (
33
), we obtain

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y,V\right)={}

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\nabla_{X}Y,V\right)+\mathrm{d}\varphi(X)\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(Y,V)+\mathrm{d}\varphi(Y)\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(X,V)

\displaystyle-e^{2\varphi}V(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)-\Omega(X)\Omega(Y)\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\mathcal{G}^{Z},V\right)\,.

On the other hand, applying (
2
) again and the relation
L_{Z}g=2\tilde{\rho}g
, we have

\displaystyle 2\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y,Z\right)={}

\displaystyle X\left(\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(Y,Z)\right)+Y\left(\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(X,Z)\right)-Z\left(\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(X,Y)\right)

(35)

\displaystyle+\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}([X,Y],Z)-\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}([X,Z],Y)-\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}([Y,Z],X)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\epsilon X(\Omega(Y))+\epsilon Y(\Omega(X))-\epsilon Z(\Omega(X))\Omega(Y)-\epsilon\Omega(X)Z(\Omega(Y))

\displaystyle-2e^{2\varphi}Z(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)-e^{2\varphi}Z\left(g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)\right)+\epsilon\Omega([X,Y])

\displaystyle-\epsilon\Omega([X,Z])\Omega(Y)-e^{2\varphi}g\left(P^{Z}[X,Z],P^{Z}Y\right)

\displaystyle-\epsilon\Omega([Y,Z])\Omega(X)-e^{2\varphi}g\left(P^{Z}[Y,Z],P^{Z}X\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 2\epsilon X(\Omega(Y))-2e^{2\varphi}Z(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)-2e^{2\varphi}\tilde{\rho}g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right).

Using
\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\nabla_{X}Y,Z\right)=\epsilon\Omega\left(\nabla_{X}Y\right)=\epsilon X(\Omega(Y))
, equation (
35
) gives

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\overline{\nabla}_{X}Y,Z\right)={}

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}\left(\nabla_{X}Y,Z\right)-e^{2\varphi}Z(\varphi)g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)-e^{2\varphi}\tilde{\rho}g\left(P^{Z}X,P^{Z}Y\right)\,.

∎

We will describe how the semi-Riemannian induced structure on a Galilean spacetime can be split into a semi-Riemannian twisted product
I\times_{f}\mathcal{F}
, for an interval
I
, a Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{F}
and certain positive smooth function
f\in C^{\infty}(I\times\mathcal{F})
. This requires a slight adaptation of
[
33
, Th. 1]
, in which the geodesic completeness of the leaves
L
is replaced in the following special case.

Consider a smooth
r
-dimensional manifold
N
and suppose
N
admits two smooth transverse foliations
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{K}
,
1
-dimensional the first one and
(r-1)
-dimensional the second one. We can assume that
\mathcal{L}
is defined by a smooth vector field
X_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Definition 7.2
.

The foliation
\mathcal{L}
admits a
uniform global generator

X_{\mathcal{L}}
if there exists a transverse leaf
\mathcal{K}_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
and an open real interval
I
, such that the maximal flow
\phi
of
X_{\mathcal{L}}
is well-defined and onto on
I\times\mathcal{K}_{0}
.

The proof of
[
33
, Th. 1]
remains valid in the case of the following lemma.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
(M,g)
be a simply connected semi-Riemannian manifold endowed with two orthogonal foliations
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{K}
. Suppose
\mathcal{L}
is one-dimensional and admits a uniform global generator
X\in\Gamma(TM)
with associated interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and transverse leaf
\mathcal{K}_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
. If
\mathcal{L}
is totally geodesic and
\mathcal{K}
is totally umbilic, then
(M,g)
is isometric to a (semi-Riemannian) twisted product
I\times_{f}\mathcal{K}_{0}
.

The above lemma allows us to prove

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a simply connected Galilean spacetime and let
K\in\Gamma(TM)
be a Galilean torqued vector field. Given a smooth function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
and
\epsilon\in\{-1,+1\}
, if the field of observers
Z\coloneq K/\Omega(K)
is a uniform global generator with associated interval
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and transverse leaf
\mathcal{F}
in the foliation induced by
\Omega
, then
the semi-Riemannian manifold

(M,\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}),\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}=\epsilon\Omega\otimes\Omega+e^{2\varphi}g(P^{Z}\cdot,P^{Z}\cdot)\,,

is isometric to the (semi-Riemannian) twisted product

(I\times\mathcal{F},\overline{g}),\quad\text{with}\quad\overline{g}=\epsilon\,\pi_{I}^{*}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+f^{2}\pi_{\mathcal{F}}^{*}\big(g|_{\mathcal{F}}\big)\,,

where
g|_{\mathcal{F}}
is the restriction of
g
to
\mathcal{F}
and
f
is a positive smooth function on
I\times\mathcal{F}
.

Moreover,
Z
is a spatially conformal vector field of
\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}
.

Proof.

Note first that we are in the hypothesis of Lemma
7.1
with a conformal factor
\tilde{\rho}=\rho/\Omega(K)
(cf. equation (
29
)).

The induced foliation by
Z
is parameterized by its integral curves
\phi_{p}(t)
, for
t\in I
and
p\in\mathcal{F}
. From equation (
32
) and using
\nabla_{Z}Z=0
, we obtain
\overline{\nabla}_{Z}Z=0
. Therefore, the integral curves
\phi_{p}(t)
are geodesics for both
\nabla
and
\overline{\nabla}
, that is, the associated foliation is totally geodesic.

Moreover, for all vector fields
V,W\in\Gamma(\An)
, we have

\begin{split}2\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(\overline{\nabla}_{V}W,Z)={}&-2\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(W,\overline{\nabla}_{V}Z)\\
={}&-Z\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,W)-\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,[W,Z])+\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(W,[Z,V])\\
={}&e^{2\varphi}\big[-2Z(\varphi)g(V,W)-g(V,\nabla_{W}Z)-g(\nabla_{V}Z,W)\big]\\
={}&-2\big(Z(\varphi)+\tfrac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}\big)e^{2\varphi}g(V,W)\\
={}&-2\big(Z(\varphi)+\tfrac{\rho}{\Omega(K)}\big)\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,W)\,.\end{split}

Then,
\overline{II}(V,W)=-\tfrac{K(\varphi)+\rho}{\Omega(K)}\,\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,W)Z
; hence, the leaves of the foliation induced by
\An
are totally umbilic for the semi-Riemannian metric
\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}
. From Lemma
7.3
,
(M,\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi})
is isometric to a (semi-Riemmanian) twisted product
I\times_{f}\mathcal{F}
.

Furthermore, from equation (
32
) we have

\overline{\nabla}_{V}Z=\tfrac{1}{\Omega(K)}(\rho+K(\varphi))V\,,

for all spacelike vector fields
V
.
Then,

\displaystyle L_{Z}\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,W)={}

\displaystyle\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(\overline{\nabla}_{V}Z,W)+\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,\overline{\nabla}_{W}Z)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\tfrac{2}{\Omega(K)}(\rho+K(\varphi))\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}(V,W)\,,

for all spacelike vector fields
V,W
, that is,
Z
is spatially conformal for
\overline{g}_{\epsilon,\varphi}
.

∎

Finally, we are in the position to obtain a converse for Proposition
6.1
.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a simply connected Galilean spacetime. It admits a global decomposition as a GT spacetime if and only if there exists a Galilean torqued vector field
K\in\Gamma(TM)
such that the associated field of observers,
Z\coloneq K/\Omega(K)
, is a uniform global generator relative to the foliation defined by
\An
.

Proof.

From Proposition
6.1
, the necessary condition is obvious.
Now, if the flow of
Z
is well-defined and onto on
I\times\mathcal{F}
, for
I\subset\mathbb{R}
an open interval and
\mathcal{F}
a leaf of the foliation induced by
\Omega
, Theorem
7.4
states that there exists a semi-Riemannian isometry map

\Psi\colon\big(M,\overline{g}_{-1,0}\big)\longrightarrow\big(I\times\mathcal{F},-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+f^{2}\,g|_{\mathcal{F}}\big)\,,

where we take
\epsilon=-1
and
\varphi=0
.
Consider
\Psi
as a map between Galilean spacetimes,

\Psi\colon\big(M,\Omega,g,\nabla\big)\longrightarrow\big(I\times\mathcal{F},\mathrm{d}t,f^{2}\,g|_{\mathcal{F}},\nabla^{0}\big)\,,

where
\nabla^{0}
is the corresponding GT connection. It suffices to prove that
\Psi
is a Galilean diffeomorphism.

First,
\Psi
maps the foliation induced by
Z
to the canonical foliation induced by
\pt
in
I\times\mathcal{F}
, with
t=\pi_{I}\colon I\times\mathcal{F}\to I
. Consequently, if
\phi_{q}(s)
is the flow of
Z
for
q\in\mathcal{F}
, we have

\Psi(\phi_{q}(s))=(\beta(s),q^{\prime}),\quad\forall s\in I\,,

for certain
q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}
and a smooth function
\beta\in C^{\infty}(I)
. Since
\Psi
preserves the causal character of vector fields for the semi-Riemannian structures,
\mathrm{d}\Psi(Z)=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}(\Psi\circ\phi_{q})
must be timelike unitary. Thus,
(\beta^{\prime})^{2}=1
.

On the one hand, if
\beta^{\prime}=-1
, we may redefine the isometry map as follows,

\widetilde{\Psi}\colon M\to I\times\mathcal{F},\quad p\longmapsto\widetilde{\Psi}(p)=\big(-\psi_{1}(p),\psi_{2}(p)\big)\,,

for all
p\in M
, where
\psi_{i}
are the component functions of
\Psi
,
\Psi(p)=\big(\psi_{1}(p),\psi_{2}(p)\big)
with
\psi_{1}(p)\in I
and
\psi_{2}(p)\in\mathcal{F}
. Hence, the orientation of the isometry
\Psi
is reversed and the previous argument gives
\mathrm{d}\widetilde{\Psi}(Z)=\widetilde{\beta^{\prime}}=1
.

Now, if
\beta^{\prime}=1
, we have

(\Psi\circ\phi_{q})(s)=(s+c,q^{\prime}),\quad\forall s\in I\,,

with
c\in I
, and
\mathrm{d}\Psi(Z)=\pt
. The manifold
M
is simply connected and
\mathrm{d}\Omega=0
, then there exists a function
T\in C^{\infty}(M)
, determined except for a constant, such that
\Omega=\mathrm{d}T
. It turns out
(t\circ\Psi)(\phi_{q}(s))=s+c
and
T(\phi_{q}(s))=s+\tilde{c}
, with
\tilde{c}\in\mathbb{R}
. If necessary, redefine
T
via a translation to get
t\circ\Psi=T
. As a consequence,
\Psi^{*}\mathrm{d}t=\mathrm{d}T
. Using
\Psi
preserves the semi-Riemannian structures, from Theorem
7.4
we obtain
\Psi^{*}(f^{2}g|_{\mathcal{F}})=g
.

Note that
\mathrm{d}\Psi(Z)=\pt
and take into account

\mathrm{d}\Psi(P^{Z}X)=\mathrm{d}\Psi(X)-\Omega(X)\mathrm{d}\Psi(Z)=P^{\pt}\mathrm{d}\Psi(X)\,,

it is straightforward that formula ’à la Koszul’ (
2
) gives

2g|_{\mathcal{F}}(P^{\pt}\nabla^{0}_{\mathrm{d}\Psi(X)}\mathrm{d}\Psi(Y),\mathrm{d}\Psi(V))=2g(P^{Z}\nabla_{X}Y,V)\,,

for all
X,Y\in\Gamma(TM)
and all
V\in\Gamma(\An)
. Therefore
\nabla
and
\nabla^{0}
are
\Psi-
related.
∎

When the spacelike leaves are compact, we can assure the existence of a global splitting.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
(M,\Omega,g,\nabla)
be a simply connected Galilean spacetime. If there exists a Galilean torqued vector field
K\in\Gamma(TM)
and the leaves of the distribution induced by
\An
are compact, then
M
is a GT spacetime.

Proof.

It is enough to show that the vector field
Z=\frac{K}{\Omega(K)}
is a uniform global generator. Indeed, let
\Phi\colon\mathcal{D}\to M
be the maximal local flow of
Z
.

For any
p\in\mathcal{F}
, we have
I_{p}\times U_{p}\subset\mathcal{D}
, with
I_{p}\subset\mathbb{R}
an open interval and
U_{p}
an open set in
\mathcal{F}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is compact,
\mathcal{F}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}U_{i}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then,
\Phi
is well-defined on some domain
I\times\mathcal{F}
, for
I\subset\mathbb{R}
maximal interval.

In the case
I=\mathbb{R}
, the flow is complete and the proof is ended using Theorem
7.5
. Suppose now
I=]a,b[
, with
b<+\infty
. Given
q\in\mathcal{F}
and
\epsilon>0
, if
\Phi(\cdot,q)
is defined on
]a,b+\epsilon[
, then there exists
\delta>0
such that
]-\delta,\delta[\times\mathcal{F}_{\Phi(b,q)}\subset\mathcal{D}
. Therefore, the flow can be extended,

\overline{\Phi}(t,q)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}\Phi(t,q)&\text{ if }\quad t\in(a,b-\delta),\\
\Phi\big(t-b+\tfrac{\delta}{2},\Phi(b-\tfrac{\delta}{2},q)\big)&\text{ if }\quad t\in(b-\delta,b+\delta).\end{array}\right.

This is a contradiction.

Finally, we prove that
\Phi\colon I\times\mathcal{F}\to M
is onto. Suppose again by contradiction, that there exists
q\in M\setminus\Phi(I\times\mathcal{F})
and consider the maximal interval
J\subset\mathbb{R}
where
\Phi\colon J\times\mathcal{F}_{q}\to M
is defined. If
\Phi(I\times\mathcal{F})\cap\Phi(J\times\mathcal{F}_{q})
is not empty, the flow could be extended towards
I
. Therefore, since
I
is maximal,
\Phi(I\times\mathcal{F})
is open and closed in a connected manifold
M
. Hence,
\Phi(I\times\mathcal{F})=M
, and the proof is ended using Theorem
7.5
.
∎
