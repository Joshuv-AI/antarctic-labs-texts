Tree-Packing Revisited: Faster Fully Dynamic Min-Cut and Arboricity
Tijn de Vos, ⓡ, Aleksander B. G. Christiansen

Abstract
Tree-packings – collections of spanning trees of a graph – are a fundamental tool in the study of minimum cut and related graph parameters. They have played a central role in the design of algorithms across static, dynamic, and distributed settings. In this paper, we study both tree-packings themselves and their structural connections to min-cut and arboricity. Our results lead to faster dynamic algorithms for both problems. For dynamic min-cut, [Thorup, Comb. 2007] used tree-packings to obtain his dynamic min-cut algorithm with O ~ ​ ( λ 14.5 ​ n ) \tilde{O}(\lambda^{14.5}\sqrt{n}) worst-case update time. We reexamine this relationship, showing that we need to maintain fewer trees for such a result; we show that we only need to pack Θ ⁡ ( λ 3 ​ log ⁡ m ) \Theta(\lambda^{3}\log m) greedy trees to guarantee either a 1-respecting cut or a trivial cut in some contracted graph. Based on this structural result, we then provide a deterministic algorithm for fully dynamic exact min-cut that has O ~ ​ ( λ 5.5 ​ n ) \tilde{O}(\lambda^{5.5}\sqrt{n}) worst-case update time, for graphs with min-cut value at most λ \lambda . In particular, this also yields an algorithm for fully dynamic exact min-cut with O ~ ​ ( m 1 − 1 / 12 ) \tilde{O}(m^{1-1/12}) amortized update time, improving upon O ~ ​ ( m 1 − 1 / 31 ) \tilde{O}(m^{1-1/31}) [Goranci et al., SODA 2023]. We also give the first fully dynamic algorithm that maintains a ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation of the fractional arboricity. Our algorithm is deterministic and has O ⁡ ( α ​ log 6 ​ m / ε 4 ) O(\alpha\log^{6}m/\varepsilon^{4}) amortized update time, for graphs with arboricity at most α \alpha . We extend these results to a Monte Carlo algorithm with O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ m , ε − 1 ) ) O(\operatorname{\text{{\rm poly}}}(\log m,\varepsilon^{-1})) amortized update time against an adaptive adversary. Our algorithms work on multi-graphs as well. Our structural results on tree-packing also include a lower bound for greedy tree-packing, which – to the best of our knowledge – is the first progress on this topic since [Thorup, Comb. 2007].

Introduction

A
tree-packing

\mathcal{T}
is a collection of spanning trees of a graph.
Often one is interested in a tree-packing satisfying certain requirements, e.g., greedy, disjoint, which we detail where applicable.
In particular, tree-packing first appeared in the seminal works of Nash-Williams
[
46
]
and Tutte
[
57
]

They studied sufficient and necessary conditions for when a graph can be decomposed into
k

k

They provided a sufficient and necessary condition by considering partition values:
given a partition
\mathcal{P}
of the vertex set of a graph
G

of

is then
\tfrac{|E(G/\mathcal{P})|}{|V(G/\mathcal{P})|-1}
, where

is the graph obtained by contracting
\mathcal{P}
in
G
.
In particular, they showed that

k

k
.
Not long after, Nash-Williams noted
[
47

that his techniques extend to answer a question with a very similar flavor: what is the smallest number of trees needed to cover all edges of a graph?
This number is now known as the
arboricity
of the graph, and Nash-Williams showed that the arboricity is the ceiling of the
fractional arboricity
defined as
\alpha:=\max_{S\subseteq V}\frac{|E(S)|}{|S|-1}
.

Subsequently, tree-packing has also been studied for its algorithmic applications, in particular for computing the value of a minimum cut and related problems

,

The

Such a collection of edges forms a

1

As per convention, we often simply write min-cut to refer to the size/value of a min-cut. Note that there can be multiple min-cuts, although there is a unique min-cut value.

In many of these applications,

Section

We are still far from fully understanding ideal relative loads and their limits.
In this paper, we hope to contribute to a better understanding.
In particular, we study ideal relative loads by giving new upper and lower bounds on the size of tree-packings that approximate the ideal relative loads.
Moreover, we show two structural results on ideal relative loads and min-cut/arboricity respectively.
This leads to a faster fully dynamic exact min-cut algorithm and the first fully dynamic algorithm to maintain a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of the fractional arboricity.
As such, we categorize our results in three parts: 1) tree-packings and ideal relative loads, 2) min-cut, and 3) arboricity.

1.1
Tree-Packings and Ideal Relative Loads

Let
G=(V,E)
be an unweighted undirected graph. A
tree-packing

\mathcal{T}
of
G
is a family of spanning trees, where edges can appear in multiple trees. The
load
of an edge
e
, denoted by
L^{\mathcal{T}}(e)
, is defined by the number of trees that contain
e
. The
relative load
is defined as
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)=L^{\mathcal{T}}(e)/|\mathcal{T}|
. Whenever the tree-packing is clear from the context, we omit the superscript
\cdot^{\mathcal{T}}
. The
packing value
of a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}
is

\rm{pack\_val}(\mathcal{T}):=\frac{1}{\max_{e\in E}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)}.

Dual to tree-packing we have a concept for partitions. For a partition
\mathcal{P}
of the vertex set
V
, we define the
partition value
as

\rm{part\_val}(\mathcal{P}):=\frac{|E(G/\mathcal{P})|}{|\mathcal{P}|-1}.

We now have that (see e.g.
[
46
,
57
]
)

\Phi_{G}:=\max_{\mathcal{T}}\rm{pack\_val}(\mathcal{T})=\min_{\mathcal{P}}\rm{part\_val}(\mathcal{P}).

We omit the subscript and simply write
\Phi
when the graph is clear from the context.

Next, we introduce
ideal relative loads
, following Thorup
[
54
]
. These loads, denoted by
\ell^{*}(e)
, are defined recursively.

1.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{*}
be a partition with
\rm{part\_val}(\mathcal{P}^{*})=\Phi
.

2.

For all
e\in E(G/\mathcal{P}^{*})
, set
\ell^{*}(e):=1/\Phi
.

3.

For each
S\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
, recurse on the subgraph
G[S]
.

By definition, we have that
\Phi_{G}=\tfrac{1}{\max_{e\in E}\ell^{*}(e)}
. We show an analogous result for the fractional arboricity
\alpha(G)
and the minimum ideal relative load:

Theorem 1
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be an undirected, unweighted (multi-)graph, then

\alpha(G)=\frac{1}{\min_{e\in E}\ell^{*}(e)}.

In
Section

1.3
, we demonstrate how this leads to novel dynamic algorithms to approximate the fractional arboricity.

We obtain a second structural result, which regards tree-packing and the min-cut. We see a first connection between these two concepts as follows: let
\lambda
denote the min-cut of
G
, then
\lambda/2<\Phi\leq\lambda

[
37
]

\Phi=\tfrac{1}{\max_{e\in E}\ell^{*}(e)}
, we see that the min-cut relates to the ideal relative loads. We investigate this relation further in
Section

1.2

Before we discuss these two structural results, we consider how to approximately compute ideal relative loads.

Approximating Ideal Relative Loads.

Computing the ideal relative loads exactly seems to be a challenging question. Giving a tree-packing such that its relative loads approximate the ideal relative loads seems easier. More precisely, the goal is to find a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}
such that

|\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|\leq\varepsilon/\lambda,

(1)

for all
e\in E

\mathcal{T}
is
small

In general, such a tree-packing needs

Theorem 2
.

Let
G
be an unweighted, undirected (multi-)graph. There exists a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}
with
|\mathcal{T}|=\Theta(\lambda/\varepsilon)
trees that satisfies

|\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|\leq\varepsilon/\lambda,

for all
e\in E
.

Although our algorithm is constructive, it is not efficient. Instead, we consider
greedy tree-packings
.

Greedy Tree-Packing.

Thorup
[
54
]
showed that a

approximates the ideal packing well.
Here a greedy tree-packing is defined as follows: let the weight of an edge be the number of trees an edge belongs to, and require that the spanning trees in the packing form successive minimum weight spanning trees.
Thorup showed that a greedy tree-packing
\mathcal{T}
with
|\mathcal{T}|\geq 6\lambda\log m/\varepsilon^{2}

2

Equation

1

The fact that greedy tree-packings approximate ideal packings will be the basis for our dynamic min-cut and arboricity algorithms, since greedy tree-packings are easy to compute – even in a dynamic setting.

To make any algorithm based on

Theorem 3
.

Let

Although a greedy tree-packing is easy to compute and maintain, this lower bound combined with

1.2
Min-Cut

In a seminal paper, Gabow
[
23
]
showed how to compute both a packing of disjoint trees and a min-cut. Subsequently, Karger
[
37
]
used the fact that any large enough greedy tree-packing contains trees that
2
-respect some min-cut, i.e., it contains a spanning tree which crosses some min-cut at most twice.
To arrive at his near-linear time min-cut algorithm for computing a min-cut, Karger used this fact by showing that one can efficiently compute the size of all cuts that
2
-respects a tree.
This observation has also been proven useful for computing
k
-cuts

12
]

This ‘semi-duality’ between tree-packing and min-cut has additionally found applications in other models of computation such as distributed computing
[

16
]

54
]

Dynamic algorithms maintain a solution to a problem – for instance the size of a min-cut – as the input graph undergoes deletions and insertions of edges.
In this setting, it is not known how to maintain the smallest
2
-respecting cut in a dynamic forest. Instead, Thorup
[
54
]
showed that packing
|\mathcal{T}|=\Omega(\lambda^{7}\log^{3}m)

\mathcal{T}

1
-respects a min-cut, provided that the size of the min-cut is at most
\lambda

He then shows that one can dynamically maintain this packing in
\tilde{O}(|\mathcal{T}|^{2}\sqrt{\lambda n})

1

\lambda
.

This dependency on

One immediate way of improving the dependency on

This prompted Thorup to ask if it is possible to show that an even smaller tree-packing always

Our Structural Result.

In this paper, we show that a better

We do not attain this by improving directly on Thorup’s bound that
\Omega(\lambda^{7}\log m)
greedy trees suffice.
Instead, we find that in the dynamic setting, focusing exclusively on
1
-respecting cuts may be too limiting.
In particular, we prove that in the cases where one might need to pack many trees to ensure that at least one packed tree
1
-respects a min-cut, one can instead consider a corresponding approximate partition. We show that whenever we cannot guarantee a
1
-respecting min-cut, we can instead guarantee that the approximate partition contains a
trivial
min-cut. Here, a trivial min-cut is a cut where one side consists of a single vertex.
With this approach, we need to pack only
\Omega(\lambda^{3}\log m)
trees, thus resulting in a much smaller final dependency on
\lambda

Theorem 4
.

Let
G
be an unweighted, undirected (multi-)graph, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a greedy tree-packing on
G
. Suppose
|\mathcal{T}|=\Omega(\lambda^{3}\log m)
, then at least one of the following holds

1.

Some
T\in\mathcal{T}

1
-respects a min-cut of
G
; or

2.

Some trivial cut in
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}\}
corresponds to a min-cut of
G
.

1.3
Arboricity

As mentioned, the
fractional arboricity

\alpha
of a graph is defined as

\displaystyle\alpha:=\max_{S\subseteq V}\frac{|E(S)|}{|S|-1}.

The
(integral) arboricity
is defined as
\lceil\alpha\rceil

[
46
]
.
As stated in
Theorem

1
, we prove that the arboricity can be expressed in terms of the ideal relative loads.
We use this equality to maintain an approximation of the arboricity in the dynamic setting.

Our Dynamic Results.

We obtain the first dynamic
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of the fractional arboricity. Our algorithm is deterministic, hence also works naturally against an adaptive adversary.

Theorem 7
.

There exists a deterministic dynamic algorithm that, given an unweighted, undirected (multi-)graph
G=(V,E)

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of the fractional arboricity
\alpha

\alpha\leq\alpha_{\max}

O(\alpha_{\max}\log^{6}m/\varepsilon^{4})
amortized update time or a Las Vegas algorithm with
O(\alpha_{\max}^{2}m^{o(1)}/\varepsilon^{4})
worst-case update time.

This improves the approximation factor in the state-of-the-art: Chekuri et al.
[
11
]
give a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation of the fractional arboricity. It has
O(\log\alpha_{\max}\log^{2}m/\varepsilon^{4})
amortized update time or
O(\log\alpha_{\max}\log^{3}m/\varepsilon^{6})
worst-case update time. In fact, for simple graphs, the value of the densest subgraph is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for large values of

Theorem 8

There exists a deterministic dynamic algorithm that, given a simple, unweighted, undirected graph

-approximation of the fractional arboricity

O(\log^{6}m/\varepsilon^{5})

For multi-graphs, the value of the densest subgraph remains a

1.4
Further Related Work.

Tree-Packing.

Tree-packing can be formulated as a (packing) LP. One way of solving LPs is via the
Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU) Method
, see e.g.,
[
2
]
. In particular, this method needs at most
O(\rho\log m/\varepsilon^{2})
iterations for a packing/covering LP with width
\rho

[
50
]

\rho=\Theta(\lambda)

The MWU method and

tree-packing are strongly related. In particular, Harb, Quanrud, and Chekuri
[
27
]
note that if one uses (a particular version of) the MWU method then the resulting algorithm would also be greedy tree-packing. Chekuri, Quanrud, and Xu
[
13
]
conjecture that greedy tree-packing is in fact equivalent to approximating the LP via the standard MWU method.

We note that in both greedy tree-packing and in the MWU method, there is still freedom in the update step: in greedy tree-packing there are often multiple minimum spanning trees (e.g., the first tree can be
any
spanning tree) and in the MWU method we can select any of the violated constraints.
For example, we could select a minimum spanning tree at random or select a violated constraint at random. Such adaptation of the standard approach might lead to better greedy tree-packing or MWU algorithms.

Tree-packing is also used for minimum

-cut

,
13
]

, use out-orientations to compute the approximate fractional arboricity. Although this works well for crude approximations, it does not seem to lead to high precision approximations.

1.5
Technical Overview

In this section, we give an overview of our techniques, starting with the results whose proofs are most extensive. However, we first define the model our dynamic results are part of.

Dynamic Graph Algorithms.

The goal is to maintain a data structure for a changing graph, that maintains the solution to some problem, e.g., the value of the min-cut or the arboricity. The input graph undergoes a series of updates, which are either edge insertions or edge deletions. If the sequence of updates is fixed from the start, we say we have an
oblivious
adversary. If the sequence of updates can be based upon the algorithm or the state of the data structure, we say we have an
adaptive
adversary. We say the algorithm has
amortized update time
equal to
t
, if it spends
\sigma t
time for a series of
\sigma
updates. We say the algorithm has
worst-case update time
equal to
t
if it spends at most
t
time after every update.

In some cases, the data structure does not store the solution to the problem explicitly, but it can be retrieved by a query, e.g., in case of the
edges
of a min-cut. In this case we state the required time to answer such a query.

1.5.1
Min-Cut

To obtain our dynamic min-cut result,
Theorem

5
, we have three technical contributions. The first is to prove
Theorem

4
. Our algorithm then consists of maintaining such a tree-packing, its 1-respecting cuts, and the corresponding trivial cuts. Our second contribution is to show how to maintain such trivial cuts, and the third is a new technique to decrease the recourse of tree-packing.

Min-Cut and Tree-Packing.

First, let us sketch the proof of
Theorem

4
. We remark that
\lambda
and
\Phi
only differ by a constant factor, which we will use throughout.

Lemma 10
.

\lambda/2<\Phi\leq\lambda
.

The main observation that allows one to show that some tree
1
-respects a min-cut is that if some min-cut
C
has

\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<2,

then it must be
1
-respected by some tree.
Indeed, then the average number of times a tree in
\mathcal{T}
crosses
C
is

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{e\in C}L^{\mathcal{T}}(e)=\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<2,

so some tree must
1
-respect
C
.
Hence, if
\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{*}(e)
is small enough, i.e., far enough below
2
, then the tree-packing does not need to be very large to also concentrate the sum
\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)
below
2
.

Hence, we can restrict ourselves to the case where every min-cut
C
has
\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{*}(e)\approx 2
.
In this case, one can show that in fact every edge
e
participating in a min-cut has
\ell^{*}(e)\geq\bar{a}\approx\tfrac{2}{\lambda}
for some appropriately chosen
\bar{a}
.
This in turn means that for a different value
\hat{a}
only slightly smaller than
\bar{a}
, the graph
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\}
must contain trivial min-cuts.
To show this we assume this is not the case for contradiction, and then count the edges of the graph in two different ways: once using the
\ell^{*}
values and once by summing the degrees.
The first way of counting implies that
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\}
contains roughly
\hat{a}^{-1}|V(G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\})|\approx\tfrac{\lambda}{2}|V(G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\})|
edges, and the second way of counting shows via the Hand-Shaking Lemma that
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\}
contains at least
\tfrac{\lambda+1}{2}|V(G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\})|
– a contradiction.

Now that we know a feasible
\hat{a}
exists, we need to show that we do not need a very large tree packing
\mathcal{T}
to obtain the result. Here, the next step is to show that, for an arbitrary trivial min-cut
X
of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{*}(e)<\hat{a}\}
,
\Phi_{G[X]}
is sufficiently large.
We claim that this finishes the proof, because this implies that we do not need to pack too many trees in order to concentrate
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}
values of the edges in a min-cut far above the
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}
values of edges in
G[X]
.
In particular, one can ensure that all edges
e
in a min-cut have
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)>\hat{a}
and all edges in
G[X]
has
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}
.
This then implies that
X
is a trivial min-cut of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
.

To show this, we lower bound the value of
\Phi_{G[X]}
via the objective value of an optimization problem, which encodes the size of every trivial cut in the partition
\mathcal{P}
realizing
\Phi_{G[X]}
.
The key property needed is that no min-cut edges are in
E(G[X])
and so very few trivial cuts of
G[X]/\mathcal{P}
have size smaller than
\lambda+1
.
This allows us to bound the objective value of the optimization problem significantly above
\tfrac{\lambda}{2}
, which in turn implies that all
\ell^{*}
values of edges in
G[X]
are significantly below
\tfrac{2}{\lambda}
, thus yielding the required property. For more details, see
Section

2.1
.

Maintaining Trivial Cuts.

Second, we describe how we maintain the trivial cuts efficiently.
The key challenge in maintaining the size of the trivial cuts, is that we do not know how to maintain an explicit representation of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
.
The difficult part is that dynamically maintaining contractions and un-contractions explicitly can be very time consuming, as we might need to look at many edges in order to assign them the correct endpoints after the operation is performed.
To circumvent this issue, we maintain a weaker, implicit representation of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
.
Instead of maintaining the graph explicitly, we only maintain the vertices of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
as well as their degrees.
This weaker representation turns out to be much easier to maintain.

We maintain a dynamic connectivity data structure on the graph induced by all edges
e
with
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}
.
The connected components of this graph correspond exactly to the vertices of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
.
In order to calculate the degree of these vertices, we maintain the number of edges
e
with
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)\geq\hat{a}
incident to each vertex
v\in V(G)
.
Since the connectivity data structure supplies us with a connectivity witness in the form of a spanning forest, we can maintain a spanning tree of each connected component as a top tree
[
1
]
.
By weighting each vertex
v\in V(G)
by the number of incident edges
e
with
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)\geq\hat{a}
, we can use the top trees to dynamically maintain the sum of vertex weights of each tree.
Since loops are counted twice, the sum of vertex weights in a spanning tree corresponds exactly to the degree of the corresponding vertex in
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
.
By using a min-heap, we can then maintain the minimum degree of
G/\{e\in E:\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<\hat{a}\}
, which can only over-estimate the size of the corresponding trivial cut.
To report the min-cut, we can then retrieve the cut by searching each vertex with weight strictly greater than
0
in only
\log m
time, and then report each edge with
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)\geq\hat{a}
incident to the vertex in
\log m
time per edge. For more details, see
Section

2.2
.

Recourse in Tree-Packing.

Third, we turn to the tree-packing. We note that the most expensive step in our algorithm is to maintain
1
-respecting cuts on
|\mathcal{T}|=\Theta(\lambda_{\max}^{3}\log m)
minimum spanning trees. Every time such a spanning tree changes, we need to update its
1
-respecting cuts in
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})
time. Therefore, it is important to bound the
recourse
in these spanning trees: the number of changes to the tree-packing due to an edge update. Naively, we have
O(|\mathcal{T}|^{2})=O(\lambda_{\max}^{6}\log^{2}m)
recourse (see e.g.,
[
53
]
), to see this consider an edge deletion. This edge is contained in at most
|\mathcal{T}|
trees. In each of these trees it needs to be replaced, which leads to a weight increase for the replacement edge. This change needs to be propagated in all following trees, so can lead to a series of
|\mathcal{T}|
changes. We show that we can get away with a recourse of
O(\lambda_{\max}^{5}\log^{2}m)
. See
Lemma

22
for a formal statement. This improvement is independent of the improvement from
Theorem

4
in the number of trees we need to pack, and might have applications in other instances where a greedy tree-packing is used.

The first observation is that if the graph has min-cut
\lambda
, then an edge will be in roughly
|\mathcal{T}|/\lambda
trees, not in all
|\mathcal{T}|
. If
\lambda=\lambda_{\max}
throughout the update sequence, then this gives the result. However,
\lambda
can become arbitrary small. To mitigate this fact, we keep
\log\lambda_{\max}
copies of the tree-packing,
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2},\dots
, where
\mathcal{T}_{i}
corresponds to the case where the min-cut value is in
[2^{i},2^{i+1})
. If
\lambda\geq 2^{i}
, then we have
\mathcal{T}_{i}
as a normal tree-packing on
G=(V,E)
. However, if
\lambda<2^{i}
, then
\mathcal{T}_{i}
is a tree-packing on
(V,E\cup E_{\rm{virtual}})
, where
E_{\rm{virtual}}
is some set of virtual edges ensuring that the min-cut stays large. These edges get added as
\lambda
shrinks and deleted as
\lambda
grows.

When using this global view of the connectivity, we obtain an algorithm with
O(\lambda_{\max}^{5}\log^{2}m)
amortized recourse. However, by inspecting the
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}
values, we have a much more refined view of the connectivity. With a careful analysis, this means we can obtain the same bound on the worst-case recourse. This comes down to deleting a virtual edge when it is no longer needed, and not waiting until the global min-cut has increased. We refer to
Section

2.3
for the details.

We revisit the relationship between ideal tree-packing and the partition values. We recall that

Simple Graphs.

First, we consider simple graphs, to obtain a result independent of

This means that for large values of

Multi-Graphs.

For multi-graphs, the same argument does not hold: even in subgraphs of size
|S|=2
we can have many parallel edges, so the density is only a
2
-approximation of the arboricity, even for large values of
\alpha
. Instead, we use a sampling approach to reduce the case of large

[

Section

3.4

However, an adaptive adversary poses a problem: such an adversary can for example delete sampled edges to skew the outcome. Of course it is too costly to resample all edges after every update. A first idea is to only resample the edges incident to an updated edge. Although this would give us the needed probabilistic guarantee, it has high update time: a vertex can have degree in the sampled graph as big as
n/\alpha
. To combat this, we work with ownership of edges: each edge belongs to one of its endpoints. When an edge is updated, we resample all edges of its owner. When we assign arbitrary owners, this does not give us any guarantees on the degree yet. We remark that ownership can be seen as an orientation of the edge, where the edge is oriented away from the owner. Now we can guarantee that each vertex owns at most
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
edges by using an out-orientation algorithm
[
11
]
.
For more details, see
Section

3.5

Following the earlier discussion, we now want to argue that

\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)<2.

This follows from the fact that we pack
|\mathcal{T}|=\Omega(\tfrac{\lambda\log m}{\varepsilon^{2}})
trees, and so we achieve concentration when
\varepsilon<\tfrac{3}{8c\lambda}
and thus we can write:

\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)\leq\sum_{e\in C}\ell^{*}(e)+\lambda\cdot{}\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda}\leq\lambda\cdot{}\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{\lambda}{2}(\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}-\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}})+\varepsilon\leq 2-\frac{3}{8c\lambda}+\varepsilon<2,

which per the previous discussion implies that
1
holds.
∎

Following the proof outline in the beginning of this section, we next show that if
\overline{a}
is greater than or equal to the cut-off value of
\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}
, then it is sufficiently close to
\tfrac{2}{\lambda}
for

to hold.
We begin by showing that
G/E^{*}_{<\overline{a}}
must contain at least one trivial min-cut that corresponds to a min-cut of
G
.

Lemma 14
.

Suppose that
\gamma\geq 4c>4
and that
\overline{a}
is as outlined above, then
G/E^{*}_{<\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
contains at least one trivial min-cut that corresponds to a min-cut of
G
.

Proof.

As hinted at earlier, we will assume that this is not the case for contradiction. We will then count the edges of
G^{\prime}=G/E^{*}_{<\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
twice to reach the contradiction.
To do so, let
\mathcal{P}_{0}
be the partition for
G
with
\rm{part\_val}(\mathcal{P}_{0})=\Phi
. Next, we recurse on the subgraphs
G[S]
for each
S\in\mathcal{P}_{0}
.
To keep notation simple, we do not introduce double indices, but just continue numbering: first, we write
\mathcal{P}_{0}=S_{1},\dots,S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|}
. Then, for the partition
\mathcal{P}_{1}
of
S_{1}
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{1}=\{S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|+1},\dots,S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|+|\mathcal{P}_{1}|}\}
. Next, for the partition
\mathcal{P}_{2}
of
S_{2}
, we write
\mathcal{P}_{2}=\{S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|+|\mathcal{P}_{1}|},\dots,S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|+|\mathcal{P}_{1}|+|\mathcal{P}_{2}|}\}
. We continue until we reach the partition of
S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|}
, and then go down a level to
S_{|\mathcal{P}_{0}|+1}
. Formally, we partition the next set
S_{i}
, with minimal
i
.

The above process gives us a total decomposition with
\mathcal{P}_{i}
the partition on
G[S_{i}]
, where each
S_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}
for some
j<i
, for
i\in\mathcal{I}
for some index set
\mathcal{I}
. We are only interested in parts of the decomposition, and so we will only recurse on
S_{i}\in\mathcal{P}_{j}
if
\Phi_{G[S_{i}]}\leq(\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}})^{-1}
.

Now we have the containment:

\displaystyle E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}\subset\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}E(G[S_{i}]/\mathcal{P}_{i}).

Indeed, any edge in
E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
will be included since by
Lemma

11
, we have that
\Phi_{G[S]}=(\max_{e\in G[S_{i}]}\ell^{*}(e))^{-1}
, and so the recursion will not terminate until every edge in
E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
belongs to some subgraph.
In fact, the containment is an equality.
Next, we have

\displaystyle|E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}|E(G[S_{i}]/\mathcal{P}_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\Phi_{i}(|V_{i}|-1)

\displaystyle\leq(\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}})^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(|V_{i}|-1),

where
\Phi_{i}
is the packing value of
\mathcal{P}_{i}
, and
V_{i}=V(G[S_{i}]/\mathcal{P}_{i})
.
Next, we show that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(|V_{i}|-1)\leq|V(G^{\prime})|-1.

We divide
\mathcal{I}
in different depths of recursion: let
\mathcal{I}_{j}
be such that for
i\in\mathcal{I}_{j}
we have
S_{i}
in recursion depth
j
(i.e., it is in
j

\{v\}
again partition into
\{v\}
. This has no effect on the sum over
|V_{i}|-1
, but ensures that all vertices make it to the lowest depth. Let

be the total recursion depth.
With this notation, we can write for
0\leq j<r

\sum\limits_{i\in I_{j}}|V_{i}|=|I_{j+1}|

since we must recurse exactly once on each vertex in
V_{i}
in the next level.
Let the partition
\hat{\mathcal{P}}
of
G
be obtained by letting
S\in\hat{\mathcal{P}}
if and only if
S\in\mathcal{P}_{i}
for some
i\in I_{r}
.
Then, similarly to above, the introduced notation implies that

\sum\limits_{i\in I_{r}}|V_{i}|=|V(G/\hat{\mathcal{P}})|.

We have
E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}\subset E(G/\hat{\mathcal{P}})
by before.
Thus, we note that any edge contracted in
G/\hat{\mathcal{P}}
is also contracted in
G^{\prime}
, and therefore we have
|V(G/\hat{\mathcal{P}})|\leq|V(G^{\prime})|
. Indeed, contracting an edge can never increase the size of the vertex set.
Finally, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(|V_{i}|-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}(|V_{i}|-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{j}}|V_{i}|\right)-|I_{j}|

\displaystyle=|V(G/\hat{\mathcal{P}})|-|I_{r}|+\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}|\mathcal{I}_{j+1}|-|\mathcal{I}_{j}|

\displaystyle=|V(G/\hat{\mathcal{P}})|-|I_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq|V(G^{\prime})|-1.

Now we upper bound as follows:

\displaystyle|E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(|V_{i}|-1)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}\right)^{-1}(|V(G^{\prime})|-1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{\lambda}{2}}{1-\frac{1}{2c\lambda}}(|V(G^{\prime})|-1)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(1+\frac{1}{c\lambda}\right)(|V(G^{\prime})|-1)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2c}\right)(|V(G^{\prime})|-1),

where we used the well-known fact that
\tfrac{1}{1-x}\leq 1+2x
if
x<1
.

To reach a contradiction, we let
\mathcal{P}
be the final partition obtained in the above procedure and apply the classical hand-shaking lemma to obtain:

|E^{*}_{\geq\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{X\in\mathcal{P}}d_{G^{\prime}}(X)\geq\frac{\lambda+1}{2}|V(G^{\prime})|,

which is a contradiction for
c>1
. Here we used the assumption that
G^{\prime}
contains no trivial cut of size
\lambda
. Note that a cut of
G^{\prime}
corresponds to a cut in
G
containing the exact same edges.
∎

Finally, we show that if one was to recurse the idealized load decomposition on a partition in
G/E^{*}_{<\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
, then the recursive call on a trivial cut will produce a new set

for which

is sufficiently far away from

to establish a separation.

Lemma 15
.

Suppose
\overline{a}\geq\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}
, and let
\mathcal{P}
be the partition induced in
G/E^{*}_{<\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
, and let
S\in\mathcal{P}
be any trivial min-cut in this partition. Then we have

\Phi_{G[S]}\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2}.

Proof.

We will exploit the fact that no edge belonging to
E(G[S])
is contained in any min-cut. We consider the minimum partition of
G[S]
, call it
\mathcal{P}_{S}
, and let
\tilde{G}=G[S]/\mathcal{P}_{S}
. Then it must be the case that any vertex of degree
d\leq\lambda
in
\tilde{G}
is incident to at least
\lambda+1-d
edges in
G/E^{*}_{<\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
. Indeed, otherwise an incident edge in
G[S]
belongs to some min-cut in
G
, which contradicts the choice of
\overline{a}
.
It now follows that any choice of
\tilde{G}
with
k

\Phi_{\tilde{G}}
:

\displaystyle\!\min_{\mathbf{\alpha},\mathbf{\beta}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2(k-1)}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda+1-\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})

subject to

\displaystyle\forall i:\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}\geq 0,

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\leq\lambda.

Indeed, for an arbitrary numbering of the vertices of
\tilde{G}
, we pick the unique feasible choice of
\mathbf{\alpha}
and
\mathbf{\beta}
that satisfies
\deg(v_{i})=\lambda+1-\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}
with
\deg_{G/E^{*}_{<\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}}(v_{i})+\deg(v_{i})=\lambda+1+\beta_{i}
. It then follows by the hand-shaking lemma and the definition of the partition value that:

\Phi_{\tilde{G}}=\frac{E(\tilde{G})}{|V(\tilde{G})|-1}=\frac{\sum\limits_{v\in V(\tilde{G})}\deg(v_{i})}{2(k-1)}=\frac{1}{2(k-1)}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda+1-\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}).

Furthermore, by the above, we know that
\sum\limits_{v\in V(\tilde{G})}\max\{0,\lambda+1-d(v)+\beta(v)\}\leq\lambda
since any vertex
v
with degree
d\leq\lambda
in
\tilde{G}
must be incident to at least
\lambda+1-d+\beta(v)
edges in
G/E^{*}_{<\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}}
. By assumption, we know that
S
has at most
\lambda
such edges.

In particular, we find that the solution to the above optimization problem lower bounds the value of
\Phi_{\tilde{G}}
.
Since we are optimizing over a closed and bounded subset of

it follows by the Extreme Value Theorem that an optimal solution exists. To find it, simply note that if any
\beta_{i}>0
, we can reduce the objective value by setting
\beta_{i}=0
, and if
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}<\lambda
, we can reduce the objective value by increasing any
\alpha_{i}
. Hence, we can assume that any solution to the optimization problem has
\mathbf{\beta}=\mathbf{0}
and
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}=\lambda
. Now simple calculations yield the result:

\Phi_{G[S]}\geq\frac{1}{2(k-1)}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda+1-\alpha_{i})=\frac{k(\lambda+1)-\lambda}{2(k-1)}=\frac{(k-1)\lambda+k}{2(k-1)}=\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{k}{2(k-1)}>\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2},

for any integer

.
∎

If
\overline{a}\leq\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}

1
holds by Lemma
13

\overline{a}\geq\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{c\lambda^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{\gamma\lambda^{2}}

Note that

.
Therefore we have

Now by this inequality,

\max_{e\in G[S]}\ell^{*}(e)\leq\Phi_{G[S]}^{-1}\leq\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^{-1}\leq\frac{\frac{2}{\lambda}}{1+\frac{1}{\lambda}}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}(1-\frac{1}{2\lambda})=\frac{2}{\lambda}-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}},

since for

. Now, any greedy tree-packing

Hence, if

For any

2.2
Estimating Trivial Cuts in
G_{a}

In this section, we design a data structure that takes a parameter

as input and is able to report an estimate of the size of the smallest trivial cut in
G_{a}
.
Depending on the current loads,
G_{a}
might only contain one vertex, in which case the data structure returns
\infty
.
Formally, we show the following lemma.

Lemma 16
.

Let
G
be a dynamic unweighted, undirected (multi-)graph, and assume we have access to a black-box dynamic algorithm that maintains a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}
on
G
with loads
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(\cdot{})
.
Suppose an update to
G
results in
P(n)

\mathcal{T}
.
Then there is a deterministic data structure which reports a value
\mu
such that:

•

If
|V(G_{a})|=1
, then
\mu=\infty
.

•

Else
\mu=\min_{X\in V(G_{a})}d_{G_{a}}(X)
.

The algorithm has
O((1+P(n))\log m)

Both data structures can list the edges incident to some vertex

with

in
O(\log m)
worst-case time per edge.
Here loops are listed twice.

We briefly note that if
G
has min-cut
\lambda

\bar{a}\geq\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{3}{8\lambda^{2}}
at some time

\mu_{t}=\lambda
at time

a=\tfrac{2}{\lambda}-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}

Indeed, when

, the set of edges incident to a single vertex of

are super-sets of cuts in

upper bounds the size of cuts in

\mu_{t}\geq\lambda
.
However, under these assumptions if follows by

that some trivial cut of

, will be a min-cut of

. Since the proof of

has no loops in

\mu_{t}\leq\lambda
.
Note also that for any choice of

since the edges incident to any vertex of

form a super-set of a cut, as argued above.

We briefly discuss the intuition behind the proof.
Since it can be expensive to support contractions and un-contractions, we will not maintain an explicit representation of
G_{a}
.
Instead, we will maintain the graph
\Gamma=G[E^{\mathcal{T}}_{<a}]

For each connected component of
\Gamma
, we maintain a spanning tree.
For any connected component

in
\Gamma
, we let the

of

be the number of endpoints of edges in

We observe that the external degree of a connected component

Thus we can maintain the degrees of vertices in

\Gamma

This can be achieved by storing the spanning tree of each component as a top tree. By storing some additional information, the top trees can compute the external degrees exactly.
Finally, we note that we only have to perform updates to
\Gamma

Description of data structure.

The data structure updates as follows:

•

We maintain
\Gamma
as well as a connectivity data structure on
\Gamma

After an update to

For each vertex

•

For each connected component

2.3
Recourse in Tree-Packing

The goal of this section is to show that we can bound the recourse to
\sim\lambda_{\max}^{5}
when maintaining
\sim\lambda_{\max}^{3}
greedy spanning trees.
A standard argument gives that we can maintain
|\mathcal{T}|
greedy spanning trees with
O(|\mathcal{T}|^{2})
recourse (see e.g.,
[
53
]
), but with a more careful analysis, we can shave a factor
\lambda_{\max}
. We start with the following lemma, which showcases the main idea.

Lemma 21
.

Let
|\mathcal{T}|\geq\lambda\log m
. We can maintain
|\mathcal{T}|
greedy spanning trees
\mathcal{T}
with a recourse of
O(|\mathcal{T}|^{2}/\lambda)
.

Proof.

First, let us consider a deletion of an edge
e
. We observe that it can appear in at most a limited number of trees since by
Lemma

12
(with
\eta=1)

\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)\leq\ell^{*}(e)+1/\lambda\leq\max_{e^{\prime}}\ell^{*}(e^{\prime})+1/\lambda\leq 1/\Phi+1/\lambda\leq 3/\lambda,

where the third inequality follows by
Lemma

11
, and the last inequality holds by
Lemma

10
. So we have
L^{\mathcal{T}}(e)=|\mathcal{T}|\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)=O(|\mathcal{T}|/\lambda)
. In each tree where
e
appears, we need to do an update that can lead to a chain of
|\mathcal{T}|
updates. Hence we get total recourse
O(|\mathcal{T}|^{2}/\lambda)
.
∎

This lemma by itself does not help us yet: we are maintaining
\sim\lambda_{\max}^{3}
trees, and
\lambda_{\max}^{6}/\lambda
can be as big as
\lambda_{\max}^{6}
, when
\lambda
becomes small.
The trick is to maintain
O(\log\lambda_{\max})
different tree-packings
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, each of different size.
Now, we only need the tree-packing
\mathcal{T}_{i}
to be correct when
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
, and so some of the packings can be much smaller. For
i
such that
\lambda_{t}\geq 2^{i+1}
, the tree-packing can just be seen as a truncated packing from a larger
i
and will be correct as well. For larger
i
, i.e., when
\lambda<2^{i}
, we can add fake input edges to keep the min-cut larger and hence the update time smaller. First, we observe that in
Lemma

21
we do not actually use the min-cut, but we have a more local argument: we need
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)
to be small enough. We will exploit this by locally adding edges, such that all
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)
stay small.

Lemma 22
.

We can maintain a tree-packing of size
\Theta(\lambda_{t}^{3}\log m)
in the following manner.
We maintain
\log m
tree-packings
\mathcal{T}_{i}
of various graphs of size
|\mathcal{T}_{i}|=\Theta(2^{3i}\log m)
, with
O(2^{5i}\log^{2}m)
worst-case recourse in tree-packing
\mathcal{T}_{i}
, using any minimum spanning tree algorithm with
O(1)
worst-case recourse to maintain the individual trees. We then have that
\mathcal{T}_{i}
for
i
such that
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
is a tree-packing on
G
of the required size.

Proof.

We will maintain tree-packings
\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{\log\lambda_{\max}}
, where
|\mathcal{T}_{i}|=O(2^{3i}\log m)
. We only need the packing
\mathcal{T}_{i}
with
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
. The other
\mathcal{T}_{i}
will not necessarily correspond to a tree-packing of
G
, but we maintain all of them simultaneously with the stated recourse.

First, note that for
\mathcal{T}_{i}
with
2^{i}\leq\lambda_{t}
the results directly holds by
Lemma

21
. In fact, we do not need to compute these separately, but we can just use the truncated tree-packing below the cut-off value. For the rest of the proof, we focus on
\mathcal{T}_{i}
with
2^{i}>\lambda_{t}
.

Next, we consider an initialization step for the tree-packings
\mathcal{T}_{i}
with
2^{i}>\lambda_{0}
. For each of these, we do not start with only
G
, but we add a path graph where each edge appears with multiplicity
2^{i-4}
. We call these added edges

. Next, we compute a tree-packing for the resulting graph
G_{i}
. Clearly this graph now has min-cut at least
2^{i-4}
. More precisely, it also means that
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)\leq|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/{2^{i-4}}=16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, hence the recourse of updates to this graph is now bounded by
O(2^{5i}\log^{2}m)
. Next, we delete all virtual edges with
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)\leq 8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
. These edges were not necessary, in the sense that
G
already guarantees the right connectivity.

Claim 1.

Deleting a virtual edge
e
cannot lead to
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e^{\prime})>16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
for any
e^{\prime}
.

Proof.
The last time
e
was not picked, there must be some path separating the end points of
e
where each edge has load at most
8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
.
In the worst case we add the entire load of
8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
to this path. Since all edges had load at most
8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, so they now have load at most
16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
.
Note that the load of
e
will be distributed along this path before being added to an edge
e^{\prime}
with
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e^{\prime})=16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, as any tree previously containing
e
missed at least one edge from this path.

This initialization time is
O(n\cdot 2^{i-4}\cdot 2^{5i}\log^{2}m)
. This can be divided in a worst-case manner over insertions in the graph, carrying out at most
O(2^{5i}\log^{2}m)
operations per insertion. The reason is that to reach
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
, we need at least
n\cdot 2^{i}
edges in
G
.

Now after every update, whenever a virtual edge satisfies
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)\leq 8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, we delete it. Note that by Claim 1, this does not lead to any edge
e^{\prime}
with
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e^{\prime})>16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
. So we do not get a chain of insertions and deletions.

However, one edge insertion can lead to multiple deletions. In fact, it can lead to
2^{i}
deletions, which would take more than the stated worst-case update time. Instead of actually deleting it right away, we add each virtual edge that needs to be deleted to a deletion queue and delete one edge from the queue after any update. Note that these delayed deletions only have a positive effect on the min-cut (and hence update time). Moreover, if a virtual edge in the deletion queue later increases its load above the threshold, then it is removed from the deletion queue.

Further, if an edge deletion
e
leads to
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e^{\prime})>16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
for any
e^{\prime}
, then we keep
e
as a virtual edge. This guarantees that at any point in time we have
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)\leq 16|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
for all edges
e
, hence our recourse stays bounded.
In particular, the min-cut of
G_{i}
is always at least
\tfrac{2^{i}}{16}
.
Indeed, for any
e
in
G_{i}
, we have
\ell^{*}(e)\leq\max_{f}\ell^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(f)\leq 16/2^{i}
.
This implies that
\lambda(G_{i})\geq\Phi\geq\tfrac{1}{\max_{f}\ell^{*}(f)}\geq\tfrac{2^{i}}{16}
.
Finally, we show correctness.

Claim 2.

If
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
, then all virtual edges have been placed in the deletion queue.

Proof.
Since
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
, we have

\displaystyle L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{T}_{i}|\ell^{*}(e)+|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\lambda_{t}\leq|\mathcal{T}_{i}|\max_{e^{\prime}}\ell^{*}(e^{\prime})+|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\Phi+|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\lambda_{t}\leq 3|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\lambda_{t}\leq 3|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}\leq 8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}.

Next, we need to show that the deletion queue will be empty by this time. This shows that when
\lambda_{t}\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
, the tree-packing is correct. In Claim 3, we show that if there are
k
virtual edges
e
with
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)\geq 8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, then we need to insert at least
k
edges to obtain
\lambda\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
. Hence whenever an edge gets moved to the deletion queue, we know that enough insertions will be performed later, at which time we can carry out the deletion.

Claim 3.

Suppose there are
k
virtual edges
e
with
L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)\geq 8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, then we need to insert at least
k
edges to obtain
\lambda\in[2^{i},2^{i+1})
.

Proof.
Consider the idealized load packing of this graph (including the virtual edges). We write
L^{*}(e):=|\mathcal{T}_{i}|\ell^{*}(e)
.
We observe that for each of our
k
edges

L^{*}(e)\geq L^{\mathcal{T}_{i}}(e)-|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}\geq 8|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}-|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}\geq 4|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i},

where the first inequality uses
Lemma

12
with
\eta=1/16
so that the maximum error in the estimate is bounded by
1/2^{i}
.
When
\lambda\geq 2^{i}
, we have that

2^{i}\leq\lambda\leq 2\Phi=2\min_{\mathcal{P}}\rm{part\_val}(\mathcal{P}),

where the second inequality holds by
Lemma

10
and equality holds by the definition of
\Phi
. Recall that
\mathcal{P}^{*}
denote the packing satisfying this minimum and defining the ideal loads, so we have
L^{*}(e)\leq|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\Phi\leq 2|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/\lambda\leq 2|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
for any
e
.

Consider the partition
\mathcal{P}
defined by the vertices of
G/\{e\in E:L^{*}(e)<4|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}\}
.
Whenever
\lambda\geq 2^{i}
any edge
e
across this partition must have
L^{*}(e)\leq 2|\mathcal{T}_{i}|/2^{i}
, meaning that we must have at least doubled the no. of edges across this partition.
Since this partition used to contain the
k
virtual edges, we need to insert at least
k
edges to do so.

At any time the deletion queue is empty, we can directly apply Claim 3 for the current virtual edges. If it is nonempty, we delete at least one edge from the queue, which decreases the number of virtual edges. We note that the number of virtual edges can increase by 1 when a deleted edge becomes virtual. In that case, that there will be an additional insertion across the partition
\mathcal{P}^{*}
on this level.
∎

For different choices of

Returning the cut edges

Observation 25

3.2
Dynamic Algorithm for Bounded
\alpha

In this section, we show how to dynamically maintain the estimate
\tfrac{1}{\min_{e\in E}\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)}
, giving us the arboricity estimate. We start with a warm-up giving the result almost directly by plugging in a deterministic minimum spanning tree algorithm.

3.2.1
Warm-Up

Lemma 27
.

There exists a deterministic dynamic algorithm that, given an unweighted, undirected (multi-)graph
G=(V,E)

(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of the fractional arboricity
\alpha

\alpha\leq\alpha_{\max}
in
O(\alpha_{\max}^{4}\log^{6}m/\varepsilon^{4})

O(\alpha_{\max}^{4}m^{o(1)}/\varepsilon^{4})

Proof.

By

26
, all we need to do is maintain
\Theta(\tfrac{\alpha_{\max}^{2}\log m}{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}})=O(\tfrac{\alpha_{\max}^{2}\log m}{\varepsilon^{2}})
greedy spanning trees, and then maintain the minimum over
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)
.
The latter can simply be done by maintaining a min-heap, which has an update time of
O(\log m)

For the former, we note that any edge insertion or deletion can lead to overall
O\left(\left(\tfrac{\alpha_{\max}^{2}\log m}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)
updates to the spanning trees
[
53
]
.
We can use a deterministic dynamic minimum spanning tree algorithm with

•

O(\log^{4}m)

[
31
]
; or

•

m^{o(1)}
worst-case update time (Las Vegas algorithm)
[
44
]
.

Multiplying these update times with the number of spanning tree updates per insertion/deletion gives the result. Note that both subsume the

update time for maintaining the min-heap.

3.2.2
Recourse in Tree-Packing

Next, we show how we can bound the recourse in the tree-packing to shave a factor

See
7

Proof.

By
Lemma

\Theta(\tfrac{\alpha^{2}\log m}{\lambda\varepsilon^{2}})

Next, we show that the virtual edges do not interfere with the arboricity if

Proof.

The algorithm is as follows:

Note that we always have

Using that

\displaystyle=\frac{|E(S^{*})|}{|S^{*}|-1}-\max_{S\subseteq V}\frac{|E(S)|}{|S|}\leq\frac{|E(S^{*})|}{|S^{*}|-1}-\frac{|E(S^{*})|}{|S^{*}|}

\displaystyle=|E(S^{*})|\frac{|S^{*}|-(|S^{*}|-1)}{|S^{*}|(|S^{*}|-1)}=\frac{\alpha}{|S^{*}|}\leq\varepsilon\alpha.

Rearranging gives us

As shown in

3.5
Downsampling Against an Adaptive Adversary

For our algorithm against an adaptive adversary, we use the same set-up as before: again, we have
\log m
sampled graphs
H_{i}
for different regimes of
\alpha
. However, we need to resample more often to fend of adversarial attacks. We first consider a naive way of doing this, and then describe a more involved process. A similar effort has been made in
[
7
]
for dynamic matching. However, their algorithm only works against an output-adaptive adversary, that allows updates to depend only on the algorithm’s output. Furthermore, we believe the techniques of this section can be used to simplify the dynamic matching result of
[
58
]
.

Naive Resampling.

An adaptive adversary can attack our sampling, for example by deleting our sampled edges. This forces us to introduce some form of resampling. The most straightforward way to do this, is that for any inserted or deleted edge
uv
. We resample all edges adjacent to either
u
or
v
. This guarantees that we cannot over- or under-sample the edges of one vertex, and one can show that this is enough to preserve the fractional arboricity. The downside is that this approach is slow: each vertex can have up to degree
n
, even in a graph with bounded fractional arboricity. Instead, we will assign ownership of each edges to a vertex, such that we can sample more efficiently.

Fancy Resampling.

First we compute out-orientations such that each vertex has at most
\alpha(1+\varepsilon)
out-edges. To each vertex, we assign its out edges. So every edge is assigned to a vertex, and each vertex has at most
\alpha(1+\varepsilon)
edges assigned to it. Now upon an update to
uv
, we recompute the out-orientation, and then resample all out-edges of each vertex for which its set of out-edges changed.

There is one more complication that we address in the next paragraph where we provide the full description of the algorithm: we cannot afford to resample in each
H_{i}
. However, where resampling is too costly, it is also unnecessary.

Algorithm Description.

The main algorithm consists of the following steps.

1.

Maintain out-orientation with maximum out-degree
(1+\varepsilon)\alpha
.

2.

Maintain the algorithm of
Theorem

7
with
\alpha_{\max}=\Theta(\log m/\varepsilon^{4})
.

3.

Upon update to
uv
, i.e., an out-edge of
u
, do for each
i=1,2,\dots,\log m
:

(a)

Resample for
H_{i}
all out-edges of
u
with probability
p_{i}:=8(c+3)\tfrac{\log m}{2^{i}\varepsilon^{4}}
, using e.g.
[
9
]
.

(b)

If this leads to at most
\Theta(\tfrac{\log m}{\varepsilon^{4}})
changes to
H_{i}
, process them as updates in
H_{i}
’s fractional arboricity algorithm,
Theorem

7
with
\alpha_{\max}=\Theta(\log m/\varepsilon^{4})
. If it leads to more changes, do nothing.

We note that if resampling leads to more than
\Theta(\tfrac{\log m}{\varepsilon^{4}})
changes, that
p_{i}\alpha>p_{i}d^{+}_{H_{i}}(u)=\Omega(\tfrac{\log m}{\varepsilon^{4}})
, so

As before, in

we should use for the output after the next update.

Next, we show that the given algorithm always maintains a correct estimate of the fractional arboricity.

Lemma 37
.

Let
G
be a graph on vertex set
V(G)=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
such that the trivial partition
\mathcal{P}=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
achieves the minimum partition value
\Phi_{G}
of
G
. Then, there exists a disjoint packing with
\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor
spanning trees and one forest
F
on exactly
(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(n-1)
edges.

Proof.

The idea is the following: pick a
k
-decomposition that first of all contains
\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor
disjoint spanning trees and (at most) one disjoint spanning subgraph
F
on
(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(n-1)
edges, that subject to these constraints maximize the partial order
\prec
. Note that this is a
k
-decomposition for
k=\mathopen{}\lceil\Phi_{G}\rceil\mathclose{}
, and that there is only a forest
F
iff
\Phi_{G}
is non-integer. If
\Phi_{G}
is integer the statement is exactly the tree-packing theorem, so follows by e.g.
[
35
]
. So we assume it is not. In that case
F=T_{k}
.

The claim now is that
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}_{\infty}
has at least
(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(n-1)
non-parallel, inter-partition edges. If
F
is a forest, we are done.

We first argue that if
F
is not a forest, then
F
must contain a cycle in
G/\mathcal{P}
. To see this, consider
G/\mathcal{P}
. Since
T_{i}
and
F
all induce trees inside each partition of
\mathcal{P}
, we conclude that
F
must have at least
(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(|\mathcal{P}|-1)
edges in
G/\mathcal{P}
.
F
contains a tree on each partition
P\in\mathcal{P}
so we have at least
n-|\mathcal{P}|
edges inside the partitions.
Now we see that

(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(n-1)=|E(F)|\geq(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(|\mathcal{P}|-1)+(n-|\mathcal{P}|).

Rearranging gives

(\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor)(n-|\mathcal{P}|)\geq n-|\mathcal{P}|,

and thus for
|\mathcal{P}|\neq n
,
\Phi_{G}-\lfloor\Phi_{G}\rfloor\geq 1
, a contradiction. We conclude that
|\mathcal{P}|=n
, and thus any cycle in
F
is a cycle in
F/\mathcal{P}
.

Let
e
be an edge in a cycle of
F=T_{k}
of minimum level, and set
m=\lev(e)
. Let
P
be the class of
\mathcal{P}_{m}
containing both endpoints of
e
. Since
e
joins different components of
T_{c_{m}}[P]
, we have
c_{m}\neq k
, and the unique cycle
C
in
T_{c_{m}}+e
contains an edge with only one endpoint in
P
. Thus for some edge
e^{\prime}\in C
we have
\lev(e^{\prime})<m
. Let
e^{\prime}
be such an edge of lowest level. Let
Q
be the class of
\mathcal{P}_{\lev(e^{\prime})}
containing both endpoints of
e^{\prime}
. Observe that
V(C)\subseteq Q
. We now create a new
k
-decomposition with
e
and
e^{\prime}
swapped: let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be the
k
-decomposition obtained from
\mathcal{T}
by replacing
T_{c_{m}}
with
T_{c_{m}}+e-e^{\prime}
and
T_{k}
with
T_{k}-e+e^{\prime}
.

Next, it is easy to check that
\mathcal{T}\prec\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
, contradicting that
\mathcal{T}
was maximal. First, we show that for
i\leq m
we have
\mathcal{P}_{i}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}_{i}
and
c_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}
. We do this by induction. For
i=0
we have
\mathcal{P}_{0}^{\prime}=\{V(G)\}=\mathcal{P}_{0}
and
c_{0}^{\prime}=k=c_{0}
, so the base case is clear. Now we assume that the statement holds for
0\leq i<m
, and we prove it for

Let
S
be an arbitrary class of
\mathcal{P}_{i+1}

T_{c_{i}}[S]
is connected. We want to show that

T^{\prime}_{c_{i}^{\prime}}[S]=T_{c_{i}^{\prime}}[S]=T_{c_{i}}[S]

Lemma 38
.

Let
G
be an unweighted, undirected (multi-)graph, where the trivial partition
\mathcal{P}=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
achieves the minimum partition value
\Phi_{G}
of
G
.
There exists a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}
that needs
|\mathcal{T}|=\Theta(\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon)
trees to satisfy

|\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|\leq\varepsilon/\Phi_{G},

for all
e\in E
.

Proof.

W.l.o.g., assume that
1/\varepsilon
is an integer. We replace every edge in
G
by
1/\varepsilon
copies to obtain
G^{\prime}
. Note that
\ell^{*}_{G^{\prime}}(e)=\varepsilon\ell^{*}_{G}(e)
and
\Phi_{G^{\prime}}=\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon
. By
Lemma

37
, we see that we can pack
\lfloor\Phi_{G^{\prime}}\rfloor
spanning trees and one forest on exactly
(\Phi_{G^{\prime}}-\lfloor\Phi_{G^{\prime}}\rfloor)(n-1)
edges.
Now let
\mathcal{T}
consists of these
\lfloor\Phi_{G^{\prime}}\rfloor
spanning trees, together with one tree that is the forest extended to a tree in an arbitrary way. Now we have that
L^{\mathcal{T}}(e)=1/\varepsilon
or
L^{T}(e)=1/\varepsilon+1
. We see that in the first case that

\displaystyle|\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1/\varepsilon}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil}-\frac{1}{\Phi_{G}}\right|=\frac{1}{\Phi_{G}}-\frac{1/\varepsilon}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil}=\frac{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil-\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil\Phi_{G}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil\Phi_{G}}\leq\varepsilon/\Phi_{G}.

And in the second case we have

\displaystyle|\ell^{\mathcal{T}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1/\varepsilon+1}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil}-\frac{1}{\Phi_{G}}\right|=\left|\frac{\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon+\Phi_{G}-\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil\Phi_{G}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon-\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil\Phi_{G}}\right|+\left|\frac{\Phi_{G}}{\lceil\Phi_{G}/\varepsilon\rceil\Phi_{G}}\right|\leq 2\varepsilon/\Phi_{G},

where for the first inequality we use the triangle inequality, and for the second inequality we use the first case. Now by setting
\varepsilon\leftarrow\varepsilon/2
, we obtain the result.
∎

At last, we are ready to show the general case.

Proof of
Theorem

2
.

We recall the definition of the ideal relative loads:
\ell^{*}(e)
is defined recursively as follows.

1.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{*}
be a packing with
\rm{pack\_val}(\mathcal{P}^{*})=\Phi
.

2.

For all
e\in E(G/\mathcal{P})
, set
\ell^{*}(e):=1/\Phi
.

3.

For each
S\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
, recurse on the subgraph
G[S]
.

We prove the lemma by induction on the depth of this recursive definition. If the depth is
0
then the minimum partition value
\Phi_{G}
is exactly achieved by the trivial partition
\mathcal{P}=\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, so the base case is immediate. Suppose it holds up to depth
i
. Then we have for each component
X\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
that there exists a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}_{X}
such that
|\mathcal{T}_{X}|=\Theta(\Phi/\varepsilon)
and

|\ell^{\mathcal{T}_{X}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|\leq\varepsilon/\Phi,

for all
e\in E(G[X])
. Note that this follows from the induction hypothesis since
\Phi_{G[X]}\geq\Phi
, so we can set
\varepsilon\leftarrow\tfrac{\Phi_{G[X]}}{\Phi}\varepsilon
. We also have a tree-packing
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}*}
on
G/\mathcal{P}^{*}
such that
|\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}|=\Theta(\Phi/\varepsilon)
and

|\ell^{\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}}(e)-\ell^{*}(e)|\leq\varepsilon/\Phi,

for all
e\in E(G/\mathcal{P}^{*})
.
Clearly
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}^{*}}\cup\left(\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{P}^{*}}\mathcal{T}_{X}\right)
, where each tree is a union of the respective trees, is a tree-packing of
G
that satisfies the bounds on
\ell^{\mathcal{T}}
.
∎
