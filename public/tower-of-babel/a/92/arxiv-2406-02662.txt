Generalised symmetries and state-operator correspondence for nonlocal operators


Abstract
We provide a one-to-one correspondence between line operators and states in four-dimensional CFTs with continuous 1-form symmetries. In analogy with 0 0 -form symmetries in two dimensions, such CFTs have a free photon realisation and enjoy an infinite-dimensional current algebra that generalises the familiar Kac–Moody algebras. We construct the representation theory of this current algebra, which allows for a full description of the space of states on an arbitrary closed spatial slice. On 𝕊 2 × 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1} , we rederive the spectrum by performing a path integral on 𝔹 3 × 𝕊 1 \mathbb{B}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{1} with insertions of line operators. This leads to a direct and explicit correspondence between the line operators of the theory and the states on 𝕊 2 × 𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1} . Interestingly, we find that the vacuum state is not prepared by the empty path integral but by a squeezing operator. Additionally, we generalise some of our results in two directions. Firstly, we construct current algebras in ( 2 ​ p + 2 ) (2p+2) -dimensional CFTs, that are universal whenever the theory has a p p -form symmetry, and secondly we provide a non-invertible generalisation of those higher-dimensional current algebras.

The idea can be summarised as follows. Any Riemann surface can be viewed as a collection of discs and pair-of-pants geometries sewn together along circles. The view from the algebra

Let us now match
equation

3.15
, starting from the Kac–Moody algebra,
equation

2.26
and considering its representations. For simplicity we will discuss the quantisation on a rectangular torus, with complex structure,
\uptau=\mathrm{i}\beta
. We will reinstate the generic complex structure at the end of the section. The Hamiltonian of the free scalar, on the spatial
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, takes the Sugawara form

H=L_{0}^{+}+E_{0}^{+}+L_{0}^{-}+E_{0}^{-},

(3.17)

with

L_{0}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{k}}\sum_{\mathsf{n}\geqslant 0}J_{-\mathsf{n}}^{\pm}\,J_{\mathsf{n}}^{\pm}\quad\mbox{and}\quad E_{0}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\upzeta(-1)=-\frac{1}{24},

(3.18)

where
\upzeta(s)
is the Riemann zeta function, regularising the zero-point energy, and
\mathrm{k}
, the Kac–Moody level, is
\frac{\mathrm{g}^{2}}{2}
. With this Hamiltonian, the non-zero-modes of the Kac–Moody algebra,
J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}\neq 0}
are just ladder operators, as can be seen by computing their commutation relations with the Hamiltonian. The negative modes are creation operators, while the positive modes annihilation operators:

\displaystyle\commutator{H}{J^\pm_{-\sfn}}

\displaystyle=\phantom{+}\mathsf{n}\,J^{\pm}_{-\mathsf{n}}\qquad\mathsf{n}>0

(3.19)

\displaystyle\commutator{H}{J^\pm_{\sfn}}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{n}\,J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}}\qquad\mathsf{n}>0.

(3.20)

The zero-modes,
J_{0}^{\pm}
, commute among themselves and with the Hamiltonian, and they label the various fixed momenutm and winding sectors. Their eigenstates,

J_{0}^{\pm}\ket{j^+,j^-}=j^{\pm}\ket{j^+,j^-},

(3.21)

are primary states, or in other words, the ground state on each sector:

J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}}\ket{j^+,j^-}=0,\qquad\mathsf{n}>0.

(3.22)

To obtain the charges,
j^{\pm}
, we simply have to invoke momentum and winding flux quantisation on the spatial
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, i.e.

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}f^{\phi}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\check{f}^{\phi}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z},

(3.23)

where
\check{f}^{\phi}
is the widning, or magnetic dual of
f^{\phi}
, defined as
\check{f}^{\phi}=\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{k}}\star f^{\phi}
.
13
13

13

The factor of
\mathrm{i}
is so that the action, written in the dual frame is positive semidefinite in Euclidean signature.
With this, we immediately get, with a convenient overall normalisation of the current:

j^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi}}\quantity(2\pi m \pm\g^2 n),\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

(3.24)

The ground states are, then, labelled by two integers,
n
and
m
, and we will denote them as

\ket{j^+,j^-}\mathrel{=\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}\hbox to0.0pt{\hss\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}}\ket{n,m}.

(3.25)

Over each of the two families of ground states sits a Verma module,
\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{\pm}
, generated by acting with the creation operators
J^{\pm}_{-\mathsf{n}}
. To each Verma module corresponds a character,

\opbraces{\character}_{\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{\pm}}[q]\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\tr_{\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{\pm}}q^{H}.

(3.26)

Having the explicit form of the Hamiltonian, and the algebra in our disposal, it is trivial task to compute the characters. They read:

\opbraces{\character}_{\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{\pm}}[q]=q^{h^{\pm}-\frac{1}{24}}\quantity(\prod_{\sfn= 1}^\infty\sum_{N_\sfn= 0}^\infty q^{\sfn\, N_\sfn})=\frac{q^{h^{\pm}}}{\upeta(q)},

(3.27)

where we recognise the form of the Dedekind eta function, upon performing the geometric sum. In the above,
h^{\pm}=\quantity(j^\pm)^{2}
, is the eigenvalue of
L_{0}^{\pm}
acting on the ground states.

On each sector we have left- and right-moving states, i.e. a generic state on top of
\ket{n,m}
lives in
\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{+}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{-}
. The full Hilbert space of the theory is, then, a direct sum over all possible sectors:

\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}=\bigoplus_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{+}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{-}.

(3.28)

A generic state of this Hilbert space is

\hskip-20.00003pt\ket{n,m;\set{N_{\sfn}^+,N_{\sfm}^-, \cdots}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\quantity(J_{-\sfn}^+)^{N_{\mathsf{n}}^{+}}\quantity(J_{-\sfn}^-)^{N_{\mathsf{m}}^{-}}\cdots\ket{n,m}.

(3.29)

As such, we see that taking a trace, i.e. computing an extended character of all the Verma modules, lands us on the torus partition function,
equation

3.15
(with
\uptau=\mathrm{i}\beta
):

\opbraces{\boldsymbol{\character}}[q]\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\opbraces{\character}_{\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{+}\otimes\mathcal{V}_{n,m}^{-}}[q]=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{\upeta(q)}^{2}}\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{\Delta_{n,m}},

(3.30)

with

\Delta_{n,m}=h^{+}+h^{-}=\frac{\mathrm{g}^{2}n^{2}}{4\pi}+\frac{m^{2}\pi}{\mathrm{g}^{2}}.

(3.31)

Reinstating the generic complex structure,
\uptau\in\mathbb{H}
, we obtain the well-appreciated, yet remarkable result:

\mathcal{Z}\quantity[\T^2_\uptau]=\opbraces{\boldsymbol{\character}}[q]\quad\mbox{with}\quad q=\mathrm{e}^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\uptau}.

(3.32)

3.2
The state-operator correspondence

As a preparatory exercise for the four-dimensional story of the later sections, we will review here the well-established state-operator correspondence for local operators. In particular we will show that the states in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}
, as in
equation

3.28
, can be prepared by a path integral, with insertions of local operators, on the disc,
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, whose boundary is the spatial
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We will do so for the compact scalar, where both sides of the state operator correspondence can be explicitly identified and checked. Moreover, a crucial role is played by the Kac–Moody algebra of the compact scalar, organising the local operators into primaries and descendants. This will serve as an analogy for the later sections, where, as we will see, the ideas will be similar.

The general strategy towards a state-operator map for local operators (illustrated in
figure

1
), common to all CFTs is the following. We take a spherical slice (here
\mathbb{S}^{1}
), of the Euclidean cylinder (here
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
) at
t=0
. Each state on this slice is prepared by a choice of boundary conditions at
t=-\infty
. Mapping the Euclidean cylinder to the disc (rather,
d
-ball in higher dimensions) with a Weyl transformation, this choice gets mapped to a boundary condition at the centre of the disc.
14
14

14

We will parametrise the disc by a radial coordinate,
r\in\mathopen{[}0,1\mathclose{]}
, and an angle
\theta\in\mathopen{[}0,2\pi\mathclose{)}
, i.e.
\differential{s^2_{\D^2}}=\differential{r}^{2}+r^{2}\differential{\theta^2}
.
This boundary condition can be satisfied by the insertion of a local operator. Then the path integral on the disc, with this operator insertion prepares the state that we are after. More precisely, the path integral on the disc, with boundary conditions

\Phi\quantity(\pd\D^2)=\Phi_{\partial}
(where
\Phi
denotes all dynamical fields) and an insertion
\mathcal{O}\quantity[\Phi(0)]
at the centre of the disc produces a wavefunctional:

\Psi_{\mathcal{O}}[\Phi_{\partial}]=\int_{\mathcal{C}\quantity[\Phi_\pd]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\Phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\Phi]}\ \mathcal{O}[\Phi(0)],

(3.33)

where
\mathcal{C}\quantity[\Phi_\pd]
denotes an appropriate functional space over the disc, with boundary conditions
\Phi_{\partial}
. We will write the state produced by
\mathcal{O}
as

\ket{\cO}=\int_{\mathcal{C}\quantity[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\Phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\Phi]}\ \mathcal{O}[\Phi(0)],

(3.34)

to indicate that we need to provide a boundary condition to get out a number.

Here we note that it would be much easier to work in complex coordinates, and exploit the power of Cauchy’s theorem, as is commonly done in two-dimensional conformal field theories. We will not do so, however, as a preparatory exercise for the four-dimensional case where we cannot afford that luxury. For a discussion in a very similar vein, written in complex variables see e.g.
[
63
]
.

Before discussing the states and the operators, it will be useful to obtain an integral expression for the ladder operators, in terms of the currents, that we can insert at an intermediate point in the path integral. To that end, let
i_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}}:\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{D}^{2}
be the map embedding a circle of radius
r

J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{m}}=\frac{\mp\mathrm{i}r^{\mathsf{m}+1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}}\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\mathsf{m}\theta}\ i_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}}^{*}J^{\pm},

(3.35)

where
i_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}}^{*}J^{\pm}
is the pullback of
J^{\pm}\in\Omega^{2}\quantity(\D^2)
along the map
i_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}}
. This is arrived at by expanding
J^{\pm}
in Fourier modes, solving the closedness condition,
\differential J^{\pm}=0
and then inverting the Fourier transform. As a sanity check, we can verify that
J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{m}}
, given by
equation

3.35
, obey the Kac–Moody algebra,
equation

2.26
:

\commutator{J^\pm_\sfn}{J^\pm_\sfm}=\mathrm{k}\,\mathsf{n}\,\delta_{\mathsf{n}+\mathsf{m},0},

(3.36)

while the mixed commutators are zero.

Let us start by discussing the ground states,
\ket{n,m}
. They are prepared by inserting a vertex operator,
{\text{V}}_{n,m}
, of momentum
n
and winding
m

{\text{V}}_{n,m}(x)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\, n\,\phi(x) + \ii\, m\, \dual{\phi}(x)),

(3.37)

where
x
is a point on the disc and
\check{\phi}
is the winding dual of
\phi
, i.e. the field whose curvature is
\check{f}^{\phi}
, as defined around
equation

3.23
. The states are then obtained as:

\ket{n,m}=\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\phi]}\ {\text{V}}_{n,m}(0).

(3.38)

In this case,
\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]
is the space of smooth functions on the disc. To verify that these are indeed the ground states, it suffices to show that they are indeed annihilated by the lowering operators,
J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}>0}
. To illustrate the argument, let us first focus on the state
\ket{0,0}
, which is the zero-momentum, zero-winding state, corresponding to the identity operator. There we have

J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}}\ket{0,0}\sim\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\phi]}\quantity(\int_{\S^1_r} r^{\sfn+1} \ex{\pm\ii\sfn\theta} \ i_{\S^1_r}^* J^\pm),

(3.39)

where the twiddle indicates that we are ignoring purely numerical factors. Since we are integrating over smooth functions, and
J^{\pm}=\mathbb{P}_{\pm}f^{\phi}
, the integrand is smooth inside the disc, for
\mathsf{n}\geqslant 0
. Therefore, we have that

J_{\mathsf{n}}^{\pm}\ket{0,0}=0,\qquad{{}^{\forall}\!}\,\mathsf{n}>0.

(3.40)

In this case, we also have that
J_{0}^{\pm}\ket{0,0}=0
, as
\ket{0,0}
is a state of zero momentum and winding. To show that the states
\ket{n,m}
are annihilated by all positive modes the argument is essentially the same, save for the fact that before assessing the integrand one needs to look at the operator product between
i_{\mathbb{S}^{1}_{r}}^{*}J^{\pm}
and
{\text{V}}_{n,m}
. Since
J^{\pm}
is a current and
{\text{V}}_{n,m}
is a primary operator, their OPE goes like
\frac{1}{r}
. Therefore,
\ket{n,m}
are annihilated by all positive modes, but not by the zero-modes, as expected.

We have, so far, argued, that
equation

3.38
produces all the highest-weight states, of our Verma module discussion in
section

3.1
. To complete the picture, what remains is a construction of the descendants. It suffices to consider the states
\ket{n,m;\set{1_\sfn^\pm}}
; a generic state is straightforward to arrive at, afterwards. The claim is, then, that these states are prepared by:

\ket{n,m; \set{1_\sfn^\pm}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\phi]}\ \ \partial_{\pm}^{\mathsf{n}}\phi\,{\text{V}}_{n,m}(0),\qquad\mathsf{n}>0,

(3.41)

where
\partial_{\pm}
is the
“(anti-)self-dual derivative”
15
15

15

Intuitively
\partial_{+}
is just the holomorphic derivative and
\partial_{-}
the antiholomorphic derivative. If we insist on avoiding complex analysis language we can also define them as follows. First define bases of the (anti-)self-dual one-forms,
\differential{x}^{\pm}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}\quantity(\dd{r}\pm\ii r\dd{\theta})
. Then dualise these to vectors fields, as
(\differential{x^\pm})^{\sharp}
and take an interior product with the exterior derivative, so
\partial_{\pm}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\quantity(\dd{x^\pm})^{\sharp}\mathbin{\lrcorner}\differential
.
and
\partial_{\pm}^{\mathsf{n}}\phi{\text{V}}_{n,m}(0)
denotes the operator obtained upon performing the OPE of
\partial_{\pm}^{\mathsf{n}}\phi
with
{\text{V}}_{n,m}
and placing it at
0
. To verify the claim
equation

3.41
, observe what happens if we act with a lowering operator. As before, we treat the descendants of the state dual to the identity operator,
\ket{0,0}
, first.

J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{m}}\ket{0,0; \set{1_\sfn^\pm}}\sim\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\phi]}\ \quantity(\int_{\S^1_r} \dd{\theta} r^{\sfm+1} \ex{\pm\ii(\sfm+1) \theta} \ \pd_\pm\phi(r,\theta)\, \pd_\pm^\sfn\phi(0)),

(3.42)

where we have also used the fact that, for all that matters here,

is proportional to
\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\theta}\partial_{\pm}\phi\differential{\theta}

-th (anti-)self-dual derivative of a current. So we can invoke the current-current OPE

\partial_{\pm}\phi(r,\theta)\;\partial_{\pm}^{\mathsf{n}}\phi(0)\sim\frac{\mathrm{k}\,\mathsf{n}!}{r^{\mathsf{n}+1}}\ \mathrm{e}^{\mp\mathrm{i}(\mathsf{n}+1)\theta}.

(3.43)

This, in turn, implies:

J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{m}}\ket{0,0; \set{1_\sfn^\pm}}\sim\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\phi]}\ \quantity(\int_{0}^{2\pi} \dd{\theta} r^{\sfm-\sfn} \ex{\pm\ii(\sfm-\sfn)\theta})\sim\mathrm{k}\;\delta_{\mathsf{m},\mathsf{n}}\ket{0,0}.

(3.44)

The factor of
\mathsf{n}
in the Kac–Moody algebra

2.26
is absorbed into the proportionality symbol, and is related to a rescaling of the operator insertion by
\frac{1}{(\mathsf{n}-1)!}

Inserting back
{\text{V}}_{n,m}

J^{\pm}_{\mathsf{m}}\ket{n,m;\set{1_\sfn^\pm}}=\mathrm{k}\,\mathsf{m}\,\delta_{\mathsf{m},\mathsf{n}}\ket{n,m}.

(3.45)

{\text{V}}_{n,m}[\phi]\ \partial_{\pm}^{\mathsf{n}}\phi

\ket{n,m;\set{1_\sfn^\pm}}
. We therefore have all the ingredients to write down the generic state. It is given by:

\ket{n,m;\set{N_{\sfn}^+,N_{\sfm}^-,\cdots}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}\phi}\mathrm{e}^{-S[\phi]}\ \quantity{\qty(\pd_+^\sfn\phi)^{N_\sfn^+} \qty(\pd_-^\sfm\phi)^{N_\sfm^-}\cdots\t{V}_{n,m}(0)}.

(3.46)

Four-dimensional Maxwell theory

Having dealt with the two-dimensional case in sufficient detail we now turn to the main focus of this paper: 4d and the nonlocal state operator correspondence. We will illustrate this in four dimensions, although the results extend to generic even dimensions easily. We therefore, consider a unitary four-dimensional CFT, with a
{\mathrm{U}(1)}^{\quantity[1]}
symmetry. As we have explained in
section

2.24

4.1
The Maxwell path integral

We consider Maxwell theory on a closed, compact four-dimensional manifold
X
.
16
16

16

We will take
X

[

.
Since we are interested in topologically non-trivial manifolds we must also allow for a theta angle. The action is:

S[a]\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}\int_{X}f\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}\star f-\frac{\mathrm{i}\theta}{16\pi\,^{2}}\int_{X}f\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}f,

(4.1)

\differential{a}

S[a]=\underset{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.5352,0.7656}\smallmatrixquantity{\lx@physics@smallmatrix\rotcoloneqq\\ S^{\inst}[f_\t{harm}]\endlx@physics@smallmatrix}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.5352,0.7656}\underbrace{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}\norm{f_\t{harm}}^{2}-\frac{\mathrm{i}\theta}{16\pi\,^{2}}\int_{X}f_{\text{harm}}\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}f_{\text{harm}}}}}}+\underset{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.4883,0.1445}\smallmatrixquantity{\lx@physics@smallmatrix\rotcoloneqq\\ S^{\osc}[a]\endlx@physics@smallmatrix}}}{{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.4883,0.1445}\underbrace{\vphantom{\int_{X}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}\norm{\dd{a}}^{2}}}}}.

(4.2)

With a theta angle turned on, it is useful to introduce the complexified coupling constant:

\mathrm{t}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{\theta}{4\pi}+\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}}{\mathrm{g}^{2}}.

(4.3)

We refrain from using the more common

(4.4)

4.2

Of special importance to our discussion are manifolds of the form

5.1
Line operators

First we outline the possible line operators in our disposal. Since in pure Maxwell theory there are no local charged operators, the only gauge-invariant line operators must be supported either on closed loops or infinitely extending lines. In what follows we will only consider closed loops (which we will denote as
\gamma
), as we are dealing with compact spaces. Infinitely extending lines can be obtained as a limit. The basic kind of line operator there is, is a Wilson loop:

\mathrm{W}_{n}(\gamma)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\, n\, \int_\gamma a),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(5.3)

These operators are electrically charged under the one-form symmetry of Maxell theory, which served as the starting point of photonisation (cf.
section

2
). In particular, if

U^{\text{elec}}_{C}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\int_C \star f),

(5.4)

is the generator of the electric one-form symmetry, where
C
is a closed two-dimensional surface,
\mathrm{W}_{n}(\gamma)
carries charge
n
:

U^{\text{elec}}_{C}\cdot\mathrm{W}_{n}(\gamma)=\exp(\frac{2\pi\,\ii\, n}{\Im\tt}\; \t{Link}\qty(C,\gamma))\mathrm{W}_{n}(\gamma),

(5.5)

where
{\text{Link}}\quantity(C,\gamma)
denotes the linking number between the closed surface
C
and the loop
\gamma
.

Furthermore, there exist magnetically charged line operators. As we explained in
section

2
, and as is well-understood in Maxwell theory
[
17
]
, there is also a magnetic one-form symmetry, with generators (in the absense of a theta term)

U^{\text{mag}}_{C}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\int_C f).

(5.6)

The operators charged under this symmetry are ’t Hooft loops. In the electric presentation of the theory these are described by excising a loop from spacetime, providing boundary conditions for the gauge-fields that fix the value of
\int f
on a thin tube surrounding the loop. They can be written more compactly, however, in the magnetic presentation, where we exchange the gauge-field for a magnetic gauge-field,
\check{a}
, whose curvature is
\check{f}=\real\mathrm{t}\,f+\mathrm{i}\imaginary\mathrm{t}\star f
. The ’t Hooft loops become, in this presentation, Wilson loops for the magnetic photon:

{\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\, m\int_\gamma\check{a}),\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}.

(5.7)

and carry “electric” charge in the magnetic presentation:

\exp(\ii\int_C \star\check{f})\cdot{\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma)=\exp(\frac{2\pi\,\ii\, m\, \abs{\tt}^2}{\Im\tt} \t{Link}\qty(C,\gamma))\ {\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma).

(5.8)

In the original, electric, presentation, they carry magnetic charge:

U^{\text{mag}}_{C}\cdot{\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma)=\exp(2\pi\,\ii\, m\;\t{Link}\qty(C,\gamma))\,{\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma).

(5.9)

In the presence of a theta term, they also carry electric charge, due to the Witten effect. This is given by:

U^{\text{elec}}_{C}\cdot{\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma)=\exp(2\pi\, \ii\, m\,\frac{\Re\tt}{\Im\tt}\;\t{Link}\qty(C,\gamma))\,{\text{'tH}}_{m}(\gamma).

(5.10)

More generally, we can consider dyonic, or Wilson–’t Hooft, line operators labelled both by an electric and a magnetic charge:

\mathrm{W}_{n,m}(\gamma)\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\, n \int_\gamma a + \ii\,m \int_\gamma\check{a}),\qquad n,m\in\mathbb{Z}.

(5.11)

Simultaneously, we will combine the one-form symmetries into their irreducible, self- and anti-self-dual, incarnations, as in
section

2
:

U^{\pm}_{C}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\exp(\ii\int_C J^\pm).

(5.12)

The Wilson–’t Hooft lines carry charge under
U^{\pm}_{C}
as:

U^{\pm}_{C}\cdot\mathrm{W}_{n,m}=\exp(\pm 2\pi\frac{n+\tt^\pm m}{\Im\tt} \t{Link}(C,\gamma))\mathrm{W}_{n,m}.

(5.13)

These are the basic, or primary, in a sense to be made precise below, line operators. But this is not the complete story. We can obtain composite, or descendant (in the same to-be-made-precise sense) line operators by smearing functions of gauge-invariant local operators on
\gamma
. The only gauge-invariant local operators in our disposal are polynomials of (derivatives of)
J^{\pm}

\gamma

\mathbb{S}^{2}_{\varepsilon}\times\gamma

\gamma

J^{\pm}
on a three-dimensional surface

4.58

(5.45)

where
\rho_{\mathsf{n}},\xi_{\mathsf{n}},\psi_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
are related to
v^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}}
as

\displaystyle v^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\psi_{\mathsf{n}\pm}}\cosh(\rho_\sfn)

(5.46)

\displaystyle v^{\pm}_{\mathsf{n}\mp}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\quantity(\psi_{\sfn\pm}+\xi_\sfn)}\sinh(\rho_\sfn).

Apart from the phases
\psi_{\mathsf{n}\pm}
— which are nevertheless necessary to capture the correct vectors
v_{\mathsf{n}}^{\pm}
— this corresponds to an
\mathfrak{su}(1,1)
squeezing transformation.

A salient feature of squeezing transformations is that applying the squeezing operator to the a ground state of the original set of operators,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}}}=0,

(5.47)

yields a ground state of the new set of operators. Namely, the state

\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{n}}(v_{\mathsf{n}})\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}}},

(5.48)

satisfies

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}}}=0.

(5.49)

These states are known as
squeezed vacua
. Excited states can be built in the standard way, by applying creation operators of the new set of modes on the squeezed vacuum. They are related to the excited states of the original set of modes by a squeezing transformation:

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}}}=\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{n}}(v_{\mathsf{n}})\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}^{\dagger}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}}}

(5.50)

In other words, they remain squeezed.

Finally, recall that our setup involves infinitely many modes. Demanding that the new ground states are annihillated by
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}
for all
\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*}
, identifies it with an all-mode squeezed state that is only pairwise non-separable. In other words there exists a squeezing operator acting on all modes defined as:

\mathfrak{S}\underset{{\text{\tiny for}}}{\overset{{\text{\tiny shorthand}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\joinrel=\joinrel=\joinrel=\joinrel=}}\mathfrak{S}\quantity(\set{v_\sfn}_{\sfn\in\sN_\perp^*})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\prod_{\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*}}\mathfrak{S}_{\mathsf{n}}\quantity(v_\sfn),

(5.51)

Note here, that there is no ordering amgiguity in defining
\mathfrak{S}
, as modes at different levels commute. It is for the same reason that it also acts on each energy level separately, i.e.

\mathfrak{S}\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}{\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}=\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm},\qquad{{}^{\forall}\!}\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*},

(5.52)

without mixing ladder operators at different levels. As a consequence, there are all-mode squuezed vacua

\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{B}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\mathfrak{S}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{A}},

(5.53)

annihillated by all
\mathcal{B}
modes,

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{B}}=0,\qquad{{}^{\forall}\!}\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*},

(5.54)

where
\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{A}}
is the ground state annihilated by all
\mathcal{A}
modes,

\mathcal{A}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{\vec{0}}_{\mathcal{A}}=0,\qquad{{}^{\forall}\!}\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*}.

(5.55)

As an explicit illustration of this construction, let us focus again on the family without momentum on the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, corresponding to
\mathsf{n}=(\ell,m,0)
, and governed by the evolution matrix
equation

5.28
. The magnetic quantum number,
m
, does not enter any of the formulas, so we will suppress it, denoting the modes just by the angular momentum number,
\ell
. The Bogoliubov coefficients are given by

\displaystyle v^{+}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{2\quantity(\ell(\ell+1))^{1/4}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\sqrt{\ell}+\sqrt{\ell+1} \\[0.5em] \sqrt{\ell}-\sqrt{\ell+1}\endlx@physics@matrix),

(5.56)

\displaystyle v^{-}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{-\mathrm{i}}{2\quantity(\ell(\ell+1))^{1/4}}\matrixquantity(\lx@physics@matrix\sqrt{\ell}-\sqrt{\ell+1} \\[0.5em] \sqrt{\ell}+\sqrt{\ell+1}\endlx@physics@matrix),

(5.57)

corresponding to the squeezing parameters

\rho_{\ell}=\frac{1}{4}\log(\frac{\ell+1}{\ell}),\quad\xi_{\ell}=\pi,\quad\psi_{\ell+}=\pi,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\psi_{\ell-}=\frac{3\pi}{2}.

(5.58)

This yields a two-mode squeezing operator as:

\mathfrak{S}_{\ell}(v_{\ell})=\exp(\frac{1}{4}\log(\frac{\ell+1}{\ell})\qty(\cA_{\ell+}^\dagger\cA_{\ell-}^\dagger-\cA_{\ell-}^\dagger\cA_{\ell-}))\exp(\ii\pi\qty(\cA_{\ell+}^\dagger\cA_{\ell+} + \frac{3}{2}\cA_{\ell-}^\dagger\cA_{\ell-})).

(5.59)

5.4
The correspondence

After this interlude on squeezed states, we return to line operators, and the states they prepare, via the path integral. First we consider the states of the Wilson–’t Hooft operators,
equation

5.11
,

\ket{\W_{n,m}}\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}a}\mathrm{e}^{-S[a]}\ \mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}).

(5.60)

Let us see the action of the charges on these states.

Starting wit the zero-mode, we have:

Q_{0}^{\pm}\ket{\W_{n,m}}=\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}a}\mathrm{e}^{-S[a]}\ \int_{\Sigma_{r}}\frac{r}{r_{0}}\phi_{0}(r)\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}J^{\pm}_{\Sigma_{r}}\times\mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}).

(5.61)

We will need the OPE between
J^{\pm}_{\Sigma_{r}}
and the Wilson–’t Hooft loop. In fact, here we will only need the Wick contraction, as all the regular terms are bound to vanish, by regularity of the rest of the zero-mode integrand. The desired OPE is:

J^{\pm}_{\Sigma_{r}}\times\mathrm{W}_{n,m}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\quantity{0})\sim\quantity(\ii\, n \ev{J^\pm_{\Sigma_r} \int_{\S^1} a} + \ii\, m \ev{J^\pm_{\Sigma_r} \int_{\S^1} \check{a}})\mathrm{W}_{n,m}(\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\quantity{0}),

(5.62)

where the twiddle indicates that it is considered up to regular terms. To continue on we will need the two-point function of the gauge-fields on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
24
24

24

Remember, we are taking this OPE in the limit
r\to 0
, so the three-ball,
\mathbb{B}^{3}_{r_{0}}
is identical to the whole
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Parametrising
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by coordinates
x
and the circle by an angle,
\eta
, the two-point function reads:

\expectationvalue{a_\mu(x,\eta)a_\nu(0,0)}=\frac{4}{\imaginary\mathrm{t}}\;\frac{g_{\mu\nu}\;\sinh\norm{x}}{\norm{x}\quantity(\cosh\norm{x}-\cos\eta)}+{\text{gauge-dependent terms}}.

(5.63)

Integrating one of the gauge fields on the circle, to get the holonomy, and differentiating the other one to get the current yields immediately:

\expectationvalue{\qty(\int_{\Sigma_r} \frac{r}{r_0} \phi_0(r)\w J^\pm_{\Sigma_r})\qty(\int_{\S^1} a)}=\pm\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}\,r_{0}}\frac{1}{\imaginary\mathrm{t}}.

(5.64)

From here, electric–magnetic duality implies

\expectationvalue{\qty(\int_{\Sigma_r} \frac{r}{r_0} \phi_0(r)\w J^\pm_{\Sigma_r})\qty(\int_{\S^1} \check{a})}=\pm\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2}\,r_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{t}^{\mp}}{\imaginary\mathrm{t}},

(5.65)

and therefore, finally:

Q_{0}^{\pm}\ket{\W_{n,m}}=\pm\pi\mathrm{i}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}r_{0}}\frac{n+\mathrm{t}^{\mp}m}{\imaginary\mathrm{t}}\ket{\W_{n,m}}.

(5.66)

Compare this to
equation

4.86
. The state
\ket{\W_{n,m}}
has exactly the same zero-mode charges, as the Kac–Moody primaries
\ket{n,m}
. It is tempting, therefore, to identify the Wilson–’t Hooft state with
\ket{n,m}
.

This is almost the right answer. To iron out that almost we need the action of the non-zero modes. First, note that the OPE
equation

5.62
cannot give any singular contribution upon integrated against any of the higher-modes. The reason is, simply, that
\expectationvalue{J^\pm_{\Sigma_r} \int_{\S^1} a}
and
\expectationvalue{J^\pm_{\Sigma_r} \int_{\S^1} a}
are proportional to the volume form on the sphere. But the one-forms
\phi_{\mathsf{n}}(r)
, that it will be integrated against, are periodic on the circle. Hence the net answer is zero. Therefore, as far as non-zero modes are concerned, the OPE produces only regular contributions. We are therefore in the same territory as for the empty state that we discussed previously. Hence again we have that

\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{\W_{n,m}}=0,\qquad{{}^{\forall}\!}\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*}.

(5.67)

This is the precise sense, in which Wilson–’t Hooft lines are
primary operators
. They have definite one-form charge, and the states they generate are primary states of the Kac–Moody algebra
equation

5.43
, sitting at the bottom of the Verma module labelled by
n
and
m
.

Reconciling the above fact with our digression on squeezed states, the Wilson–’t Hooft lines correspond to
squeezed primary states
:

\ket{\W_{n,m}}=\mathfrak{S}\ket{n,m}.

(5.68)

Inverting this relation, we have, equivalently that the vacua
\ket{n,m}
correspond to:

\ket{n,m}={\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\ket{\W_{n,m}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}a}\mathrm{e}^{-S[a]}\ {\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}),

(5.69)

where
{\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\mathrm{W}_{n,m}
denotes the line operator obtained by shrinking
{\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}
— expressed in terms of the currents — onto the Wilson–’t Hooft loop, à la
figure

3
, or equivalently via the OPE:

{\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0})\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\lim_{r\to 0}{\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}[J^{\pm}_{\Sigma_{r}}]\times\mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}).

(5.70)

Let us pause here to comment on the ground state, i.e. the state
\ket{0,0}
, of zero energy. By the above discussion, it corresponds to the operator

\ket{0,0}\quad\leftrightsquigarrow\quad{\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}).

(5.71)

This is clearly not the identity operator. In one sense, it is almost the identity operator, as this is the only primary line operator it sees. It is completely transparent to the one-form charges. However, it is also as far as one can get from the identity operator as it contains photon excitations of arbitrary frequency — indeed, of all frequencies. This was, to some extent, anticipated in
[
26
]
, where it was shown that, in a generic CFT, the identity operator, cannot prepare the vacuum state on the torus (or generally, on any spatial slice other than the sphere). Our result is consistent with that statement, while still tractable in this example.

Continuing on, and moving up in the Verma module, all the other states can be reached by acting with
\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}^{\dagger}
on the squeezed primaries, i.e.

\displaystyle\ket{\W_{n,m};\set{N_{\sfn\sigma}}_{\sfn\in\sN_\perp^*}^{\sigma=\pm}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathsf{n}\in\mathscr{N}_{\perp}^{*}\\

\sigma=\pm\end{subarray}}\quantity(\cB^\dagger_{\sfn\sigma})^{N_{\mathsf{n}\sigma}}\mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}).

(5.72)

The modes

and

can be written, in terms of the local operators

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\Sigma_{r}}v_{\mathsf{n}\sigma}^{\pm}\quantity[\bbU^\pm_{\sfn}(r,r_0)]_{\sigma\tau}^{-1}\phi_{\mathsf{n}\tau}(r)\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}J^{\pm}_{\Sigma_{r}},

(5.73)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}^{\dagger}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0}\int_{\Sigma_{r}}{\mkern 1.3mu\overline{\mkern-1.3muv\mkern-1.3mu}\mkern 1.3mu}_{\mathsf{n}\sigma}^{\pm}\quantity[\bbU^\mp_{\sfn}(r,r_0)]_{\sigma\tau}^{-1}\phi_{\mathsf{n}\tau}(r)\mathbin{\scalebox{0.8}{$\wedge$}}J^{\pm}_{\Sigma_{r}},

respectively. Acting them on Wilson–’t Hooft operators gives precisely the

that we discussed in
section

5.1

4.75

\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{n, m}={\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}\ket{\W_{n,m}}=\int_{\mathcal{C}[\,\cdot\,]}\mathinner{\mathrm{D}a}\mathrm{e}^{-S[a]}\ {\mathfrak{S}^{\dagger}}\mathcal{B}_{\mathsf{n}\pm}^{\dagger}\mathrm{W}_{n,m}\quantity(\S^1\times\set{0}),

(5.75)

Energies and overlaps

In this last paragraph, we will, briefly, compare the squeezed states we have arrived at to the energy eigenstates. We begin by calculating the average energy of the squeezed primary states
\ket{\W_{n,m}}
. A quick computation shows that their average energy is

\matrixelement{\W_{n,m}}{H_{\Sigma_{r_0}}}{\W_{n,m}}=\Delta_{n,m}+E_{0,\mathfrak{S}}(r_{0}),

(5.76)

where

E_{0,\mathfrak{S}}(r_{0})=\sum_{\mathsf{n},\sigma}\sinh[2](\rho_\sfn)\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathsf{n}}(r_{0})},

(5.77)

is essentially measuring a zero-point energy, which has to do with the fact that the Hamiltonian
H_{\Sigma_{r_{0}}}
is not normal-ordered with respect to the new set of ladder operators. More importantly,

\Delta_{n,m}=\frac{\pi}{2r_{0}^{2}}\frac{\absolutevalue{n+\tt\,m}^{2}}{\imaginary\mathrm{t}}

(5.78)

is the same as the energy of the (unsqueezed) primaries
equation

4.87
, as well as the instanton weights in the path integral
equation

4.38
. This property reflects and refines the observation of Verlinde
[
34
]
and the argument of Kapustin
[
33
]
, that the Wilson–’t Hooft lines have a quantum number
\Delta_{n,m}
akin to a scaling weight.
25
25

25

Do notice, however, that these are not the eigenvalues of the dilation operator of the theory under consideration.

For the first excited states, an elementary computation reveals their average energy

\matrixelement{\W_{n,m}}{\cB_{\sfn\sigma} H_{\Sigma_{r_0}} \cB^\dagger_{\sfn\sigma}}{\W_{n,m}}=\Delta_{n,m}+\quantity(\cosh[2](\rho_\sfn)+\sinh[2](\rho_\sfn))\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathsf{n}}(r_{0})}+E_{0,\mathfrak{S}}(r_{0}).

(5.79)

In the no-squeezing limit,
\rho_{\mathsf{n}}\to 0
, this becomes precisely the energy of the first excited energy eigenstates.

Finally we can also compute overlaps between the squeezed and the unsqueezed primaries. This is very much facilitated by the disentangling formula of
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{u}(1,1)

\displaystyle\exp(\alpha_\sfn\cX_\sfn^+ -\conj{\alpha}_\sfn\cX_\sfn^-)

\displaystyle\phantom{=~}{\color[rgb]{0.6,0.6,0.6}\times}\exp(-2\log\cosh\abs{\alpha_\sfn} \cX_\sfn^0)\exp(-\ex{-\ii\arg(\alpha_\sfn)}\tanh\abs{\alpha_\sfn} \cX_\sfn^-),

(5.80)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{n}}^{+}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}\hss}\raisebox{-1.29167pt}{$\cdot$}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\frac{\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{\mathsf{n}+}\mathcal{A}^{\dagger}_{\mathsf{n}-}}{\mathrm{k}\sqrt{\lambda_{\mathsf{n}}}},

(5.81)

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{n}}^{-}

6
Discussion

In this paper, we studied CFTs with continuous generalised global symmetries. We showed that an invertible continuous
(p+1)
-form symmetry in a
(2p+2)
-dimensional unitary CFT automatically enhances to an infinite collection of codimension-one, i.e. zero-form, conserved charges, labelled by (anti-)chiral
p
-forms. The algebra of these charges is spectrum-generating (up to decoupled neutral factors) and characterises completely the CFT. The dynamics of the CFT are those of free
p
-forms. Along a similar vein, we showed that a non-invertible continuous
(p+1)
-form symmetry, leads to a non-invertible current algebra, which we describe in terms of the fusion rules of the symmetry generators. As before, it characterises completely the dynamics, leading, in this case, to an
\mathrm{O}(2)

p
-form gauge theory. In the invertible case, and focussing on
p=1
, hence in four-dimensional CFTs, we constructed the representation theory of this algebra (for the invertible case), which we showed reproduces the path integral calculation, as a non-trivial check. This allowed us to describe explicitly the Hilbert space of Maxwell theory on arbitrary closed spatial manifolds. On non-trivial topologies, the full Hilbert space consists of the usual photon Hilbert space, as well as of Verma modules, built on top of states with one-form symmetry charge. Additionally, we showed that the full spectrum of states on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
can be obtained by a path integral on
\mathbb{B}^{3}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, with various operator insertions. The radial evolution on the ball acts as a squeezing transformation between the path integral states and the energy eigenstates, requiring that line operators are subsequently dressed with a squeezing operator, containing all photon frequencies, to reach the energy eigenstates. Notably, the vacuum state of Maxwell theory on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}

Other topologies

The choice of
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
for our state-operator correspondence is a natural one for that the unique one-cycle allows line operators wrapping it and the unique two-cycle allows a single flux. It is not clear that this is the only choice, though. For example, states on a
\mathbb{T}^{3}
topology are prepared via a four-dimensional path integral with some two-cycle filled in. It is likely that to get the complete set of states we have to consider a mixture of states prepared by filling in every two-cycle. Therefore, the state-operator map, would, in that case, include a sum over topologies, with a given boundary, in the path integral. This is not the case here, because of the requirement that the
\mathbb{S}^{1}
remains non-trivial.

Surgery and overlaps

Given our state-operator correspondence, we now have a resolution of the identity on
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
, in terms of line operators:

\text{1}=\sum_{n,m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{\quantity{N_{\sfn\sigma}}}\outerproduct{\W_{n,m};\set{N_{\sfn\sigma}}}{\W_{n,m};\set{N_{\sfn\sigma}}}.

(6.8)

We can use this to simplify correlation functions on manifolds that can be cut along an
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Relatedly, states can be glued to produce partition functions or correlation functions on manifolds surgered along
\mathbb{S}^{2}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. This fact opens up a new perspective on partition functions. Take, for example, partition functions in
d
Euclidean dimensions with the topology of the sphere. These are known to be related to counting problems through F-theorem arguments
[
93
]
, at least in
d=3
. On the other hand, by the logic above, this partition function should be related to the overlap between states prepared by computing path integrals on some half spaces which do not necessarily produce the vacuum of the theory. An example would amount to considering the Hopf fibration of the sphere and consider states defined on the
\mathbb{T}^{2}
gluing surface. Now we know those states, while having a simple path integral construction, are described by complicated superpositions of high-energy eigenstates. Therefore, these partition functions could have a more natural counting interpretation shedding light on the exact nature of the F-theorem. At this point this is just a plausibility argument, as we have only focused on theories with higher-form symmetries in even dimensions. It would be interesting to study this in more depth.
