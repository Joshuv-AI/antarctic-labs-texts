Jacopo Bellazzini, Matteo Nesi

Abstract
. In this note, we prove a new uncertainty principle for functions with radial symmetry by differentiating a radial version of the Stein-Weiss inequality. The difficulty is to prove the differentiability in the limit of the best constant that, unlike the general case, it is not known.

1.
Introduction

In this note, we will be differentiating inequalities, so we start by summarizing the general reasoning scheme. Let us suppose that we are given a family of inequalities

F_{t}\leq k_{t}G_{t}

parametrized by
t>0
. We choose to keep separate the
families
F_{t},G_{t}
from the family
k_{t}
, since in later applications

F_{t},G_{t}
will be given by some functionals computed on a fixed function
f
, while
k_{t}
are the (optimal) constants in the inequalities.
Suppose further that

•

the functions
F_{t},G_{t},k_{t}
are actually defined and differentiable for
t\geq 0
;

•

F_{0}=k_{0}G_{0}
.

Then we can write

\frac{F_{t}-F_{0}}{t}\leq\frac{k_{t}G_{t}-k_{0}G_{0}}{t}

and passing to the limit for
t\to 0^{+}
we get

(1)

\partial_{t}F_{t}|_{t=0}\leq G_{0}\partial_{t}k_{t}|_{t=0}+k_{0}\partial_{t}G_{t}|_{t=0}.

These computations will be carried out, in each case, for functions in a suitable class which allows differentiation under the integral sign; in our case it is convenient to consider the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is made of regular and decaying functions and is well-behaved with respect to the Fourier transform.

For a first example, we start with the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f(x)g(y)}{|x-y|^{\lambda}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|\lesssim||f||_{p}||g||_{q},

which, in the case
f=g\geq 0
(
p=q
and
0<\lambda<d
), becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f(x)f(y)}{|x-y|^{\lambda}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq k_{\lambda}||f||^{2}_{\frac{2d}{2d-\lambda}},

where

k_{\lambda}=\pi^{\frac{\lambda}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2})}{\Gamma(d-\frac{\lambda}{2})}\left(\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma(d)}\right)^{-1+\frac{\lambda}{d}}

and the inequality becomes an
equality
when
\lambda=0.
By observing that

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\lambda}|x-y|^{-\lambda}|_{\lambda=0}=-\log|x-y|

and following the above reasoning scheme, we get the logarithmic Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality (cf.
[
6
]
):

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\!-\log|x-y|\ f(x)f(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\leq\frac{1}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f\log f\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+C_{0},

for regular
f\geq 0
with
||f||_{1}=1
, where
C_{0}=\partial_{\lambda}k_{\lambda}|_{\lambda=0}
.

Also, a quantity similar to the LHS of the previous inequality can be estimated differentiating the sharp Young inequality: as in
[
7
]
(ch. 8, p. 223), we find, for any
a>0
, the logarithmic Sobolev inequality
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{a^{2}}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla f|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-d(1+\log a),

which holds for every
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\left\|f\right\|_{2}=1
.

Concerning the Fourier transform, a classical inequality is the

Hausdorff-Young inequality for
1\leq p\leq 2

||\widehat{f}||_{p^{\prime}}\lesssim||f||_{p},

from which Beckner
[
2
]
derived a logarithmic uncertainty principle for
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\left\|f\right\|_{2}=1

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\widehat{f}|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\leq\frac{d}{2}(\log(2)-1)<0.

So far, we have examples in which we get inequalities involving logarithmic terms, in the form of
\log(f)
or the kernel
\log|x-y|
. Now we recall three families of inequalities which give also logarithmic weights in physical
(\log|x|)
and Fourier
(\log|\xi|)
domains. These are
Sobolev, Hardy and Stein-Weiss inequalities, the latter being a generalization of the first two ”endpoint” inequalities. The results of the differentiation are summarized as follows: the Sobolev and Hardy inequalities give, respectively, (
\left\|f\right\|_{2}=1
)
[
3
]

\displaystyle||f||_{\frac{2d}{d-2s}}

\displaystyle\lesssim||f||_{\dot{H}^{s}}\ \longrightarrow\ \frac{2}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\hat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+c

\displaystyle\left\||\cdot|^{-s}f\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim||f||_{\dot{H}^{s}}\ \longrightarrow\quad-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\hat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+c

and, by interpolating, the Stein-Weiss inequality

\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\lesssim||f||_{\dot{H}^{s}}

yields (cf.
4
)

(2)

\begin{split}\frac{2(1-t)}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}&|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+c\end{split}

for all
t\in[0,1]
. In the above inequalities,
c
is a constant that differs from line to line.

The aim of the note is to prove an improvement of the logarithmic Stein-Weiss inequality (
eq.

2
) when restricted to radially symmetric functions. We denote by
\dot{H}_{rad}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the space of radial functions which belong to
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The starting point is an improvement of Stein-Weiss inequality for radial functions, which is due to Rubin.

Theorem 1

(Rubin inequality,
[
8
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
)
.

Given
f\in\dot{H}_{rad}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
0\leq s<d/2
and
p,\beta
with the relations
(1/0:=+\infty)

\displaystyle 2\leq p\leq\frac{2}{(1-2s)_{+}}

\displaystyle\beta=s+\frac{d}{p}-\frac{d}{2},

one has the inequality

\left\|\ |\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\leq C(p,s)||f||_{\dot{H}^{s}}.

Remark 2
.

The latter theorem is an improvement of Stein-Weiss inequality on radial functions, meaning that the range of parameters
p
extends further on the right and for big values of
p
even positive exponents
-\beta
are allowed.

We point out that in
1
the optimal constants
C(p,s)
are not known, but via an interpolation argument we get an estimate which is sufficient to carry out the differentiation, so as to get:

Theorem 3
.

There exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that, given
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
radial and with
\left\|f\right\|_{2}=1

(3)

\begin{split}2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}&\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+(d-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+c\end{split}

Remark 4
.

The argument in the proof holds, in particular, in the restricted range of parameters
2\leq p\leq 2d/(d-2s)
or equivalently
0\leq\beta\leq s
. For this range of exponent, the Rubin inequality holds even for non-radial functions (thanks to Stein-Weiss inequality) and the differentiation gives, for any
0\leq t\leq 1
, again

\frac{2(1-t)}{d}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\ -\ t\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+c.

Notice that the relevant difference w.r.t. the radial case is only the sign of the constant in front of the second term.

Remark 5
.

Stein-Weiss inequality can be improved in the radial case (Rubin inequality), while Sobolev and Hardy cannot.

1.1.
Notations.

L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with
p\in[1,\infty],
denote the usual Lebesgue spaces,
\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
stands for the usual homogeneous Sobolev space, namely the space of tempered distribution
u
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Fourier transform of which belongs
L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and satisfies

||u||_{\dot{H}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\xi|^{2s}|\widehat{u}|^{2}d\xi<+\infty.

In this paper, we adopt the following definition for the Fourier Transform:

\mathcal{F}[f](\xi):=\widehat{f}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-d/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\ e^{-i\xi\cdot x}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x,

so that, defining

\widehat{|D|f}:=|\xi|\widehat{f},

one has

\left\||D|f\right\|_{2}=\left\|\nabla f\right\|_{2}.

For references on the properties of homogeneous and nonhomogeneous Sobolev spaces, we refer to
[
1
]
.

2.
Proof of main results

In order to prove the estimate on the growth of the constant of the Rubin inequality near
s=0
we need an interpolation result due to Stein, so we start with a preliminary definition

Definition 6
.

Let
(M,\mathcal{M},\mu),(N,\mathcal{N},\nu)
be two
\sigma
-finite measure spaces and
\{T_{z}\}_{z\in\overline{\Sigma}}
a family of operators defined on the space of simple functions on
M
and taking values in the space of measurable functions on
N
. The family of operators is said to be
admissible
if, for every couple of simple functions
f,g
defined on
M,N
respectively, the function

z\mapsto\langle\overline{T_{z}f},g\rangle:=\int_{N}T_{z}f\ g\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu

is holomorphic in
\Sigma:=(0,1)\times i\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{C}
and continuous up to the boundary and for some
a<\pi

\sup_{z\in\overline{\Sigma}}\ e^{-a|y|}\log|\langle T_{z}f,g\rangle|<+\infty.

Theorem 7

(Stein interpolation,
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
)
.

Given
p_{0},p_{1},q_{0},q_{1}\in[1,+\infty]
and an admissable family of operators
\{T_{z}\}_{z\in\overline{\Sigma}}
satisfying

||T_{iy}||_{L^{p_{0}}\to L^{q_{0}}}\leq M_{0}(y),\quad||T_{1+iy}||_{L^{p_{1}}\to L^{q_{1}}}\leq M_{1}(y)

and the growth condition

\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\ e^{-a|y|}\log M_{j}(y)<+\infty\quad\text{ for }j=0,1

with
a<\pi
, it holds

||T_{t}||_{L^{p_{t}}\to L^{q_{t}}}\leq M_{t},

for
0<t<1
, where

\displaystyle M_{t}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{M_{0}(y)}{\cosh(\pi y)-\cos(\pi t)}+\frac{M_{1}(y)}{\cosh(\pi y)+\cos(\pi t)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{t}}:=\frac{1-t}{p_{0}}+\frac{t}{p_{1}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q_{t}}:=\frac{1-t}{q_{0}}+\frac{t}{q_{1}}.

We are interested in the case where the functions
M_{0},M_{1}
can be chosen to be bounded; the previous theorem becomes:

Corollary 8

(Stein interpolation - weak version)
.

Given
p_{0},p_{1},q_{0},q_{1}\in[1,+\infty]
and an admissable family of operators
\{T_{z}\}_{z\in\overline{\Sigma}}
satisfying

||T_{iy}||_{L^{p_{0}}\to L^{q_{0}}}\leq M_{0},\quad||T_{1+iy}||_{L^{p_{1}}\to L^{q_{1}}}\leq M_{1},

it holds

||T_{t}||_{L^{p_{t}}\to L^{q_{t}}}\leq M_{t},

for
0<t<1
, where

\displaystyle M_{t}:=M_{0}^{1-t}M_{1}^{t},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{p_{t}}:=\frac{1-t}{p_{0}}+\frac{t}{p_{1}},

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q_{t}}:=\frac{1-t}{q_{0}}+\frac{t}{q_{1}}.

Remark 9
.

In the following, we will need to interpolate some inequalities that hold only for functions belonging to a proper subspace of an
L^{p}
space, for example only for radial functions, so we notice that if
\mathcal{G}
is a family of simple functions on
M
such that the hypotheses of one of the above interpolation theorems hold for
f\in\mathcal{G}
, for instance

||T_{j+iy}f||_{q_{j}}\leq M_{j}(y)||f||_{p_{j}}\quad(j=0,1)\quad\forall f\in\mathcal{G},

then the corresponding interpolation result states that

||T_{t}f||_{q_{t}}\leq M_{t}||f||_{p_{t}}

for every
f
in the closure of
\mathcal{G}
as a subset of
L^{p_{t}}(\mu)
.

Lemma 10
.

If
C(p,s)
is the optimal constant in Rubin’s inequality and
0\leq s\leq s_{1}<d/2
, then we have the estimate

C(p_{\theta},s)\leq C(p_{1},s_{1})^{s/s_{1}},

where
\theta=s/s_{1}
and
1/p_{\theta}=(1-\theta)/2+\theta/p_{1}
.

Proof.

Setting
f=|D|^{-s}\varphi
, the Rubin inequality reads

\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}\ |D|^{-s}\varphi\right\|_{p}\leq C(p,s)||\varphi||_{2},

so we are led to consider the family of operators

\widetilde{T}_{s,\beta}:\varphi\mapsto|\cdot|^{-\beta}\ |D|^{-s}\varphi.

If
s_{1},p_{1}
are fixed, to apply the interpolation result we use
\theta=s/s_{1}
as a parameter and we set

\frac{1}{p_{\theta}}=\frac{1-\theta}{2}+\frac{\theta}{p_{1}}

and, by the relation between the parameters in Rubin inequality, we have

\beta_{\theta}=\theta s_{1}+\frac{d}{p_{\theta}}-\frac{d}{2}=\theta\left(\frac{d}{p_{1}}-\frac{d}{2}+s_{1}\right)=\theta\beta_{1}.

Now we have a new family of operators that depend on a single parameter

T_{\theta}:\varphi\mapsto|\cdot|^{-\beta_{\theta}}\ |D|^{-s_{\theta}}\varphi=|\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}\theta}\ |D|^{-s_{1}\theta}\varphi

that can be defined on the whole strip
\overline{\Sigma}
. On the left line
\{x=0\}
we have

||T_{iy}\varphi||_{2}=\left\||D|^{-s_{1}iy}\varphi\right\|_{2}=\left\||\cdot|^{-s_{1}iy}\widehat{\varphi}\right\|_{2}=||\varphi||_{2}

so that

||T_{iy}||_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq 1.

On the left line
\{x=1\}

\displaystyle||T_{1+iy}\varphi||_{p_{1}}

\displaystyle=\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}(1+iy)}|D|^{-s_{1}(1+iy)}\varphi\right\|_{p_{1}}=

\displaystyle=\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}}|D|^{-s_{1}}\left(|D|^{-s_{1}iy}\varphi\right)\right\|_{p_{1}}

and applying Rubin’s inequality

||T_{1+iy}\varphi||_{p_{1}}\leq C(p_{1},s_{1})\left\||D|^{-s_{1}iy}\varphi\right\|_{2}=C(p_{1},s_{1})||\varphi||_{2}.

Given
\varphi,\psi
simple functions,

h(z):=\langle\overline{T_{z}\varphi},\psi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{-\beta_{1}z}\ |D|^{-s_{1}z}\varphi(x)\ \psi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

is a continuous function on
\overline{\Sigma}
and holomorphic in
\Sigma
; indeed consider the two variable function

\displaystyle H(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x|^{-\beta_{1}z_{1}}\ |D|^{-s_{1}z_{2}}\varphi(x)\ \psi(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{F}\left[|x|^{-\beta_{1}z_{1}}\psi(x)\right](\xi)\ |\xi|^{-s_{1}z_{2}}\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

The first expression for
H
shows that, fixing
z_{2}
,
\frac{\partial H(z_{1},z_{2})}{\partial\bar{z}_{1}}=0
, while the second form gives,
z_{1}
being fixed,
\frac{\partial H(z_{1},z_{2})}{\partial\bar{z}_{2}}=0
. Hence
H
is holomorphic in
\Sigma\times\Sigma
(Osgood’s Lemma) and

z\mapsto H(z,z)=h(z)

is a composition of holomorphic functions, so it is holomorphic itself. Moreover, the function
h
is bounded on the strip, since

\displaystyle|h(z)|

\displaystyle=|\langle|D|^{-s_{1}\bar{z}}\overline{\varphi},|\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}\bar{z}}\psi\rangle|=|\langle|\cdot|^{-s_{1}\bar{z}}\widehat{\overline{\varphi}},\mathcal{F}(|\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}\bar{z}}\psi))\rangle|\leq

\displaystyle\leq|\langle|\cdot|^{-s_{1}x}\widehat{\overline{\varphi}},\mathcal{F}(|\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}\bar{z}}\psi))\rangle|=|\langle|D|^{-s_{1}x}\overline{\varphi},|\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}\bar{z}}\psi\rangle|\leq

\displaystyle\leq|\langle|D|^{-s_{1}x}\overline{\varphi},|\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}x}\psi\rangle|=|\langle\overline{T_{x}\varphi},\psi\rangle=|h(x)|,

where we have used repeatedly Plancherel identity. This proves that
|h|
is bounded on the strip if and only if it is bounded in
[0,1]
; by continuity, the function
|h|
is indeed bounded there. Thus we apply Stein interpolation to get

||T_{\theta}\varphi||_{p_{\theta}}\leq\left(C(p_{1},s_{1})\right)^{\theta}||\varphi||_{2},

which means

\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}s/s_{1}}|D|^{-s}\varphi\right\|_{p_{\theta}}\leq\left(C(p_{1},s_{1})\right)^{\theta}||\varphi||_{2},

for every
\varphi\in L_{rad}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and substituting back
\varphi=|D|^{s}f

\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta_{1}s/s_{1}}f\right\|_{p_{\theta}}\leq\left(C(p_{1},s_{1})\right)^{\theta}||f||_{\dot{H}^{s}}

for every
f\in|D|^{-s}(L_{rad}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))=\dot{H}_{rad}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Proof of Main Theorem.

We start by differentiating all the relevant quantities: for the weighted
L^{p}
norm

\displaystyle\partial_{p}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}=\partial_{p}\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p}=

\displaystyle=\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\partial_{p}\left(\frac{1}{p}\log\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)\right)=

\displaystyle=\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\left(-\frac{1}{p^{2}}\log\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)+\frac{1}{p}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{p}\left(|f|^{p}|x|^{-\beta p}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}}\right)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}=\partial_{\beta}\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p-1}\partial_{\beta}\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\left(\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}^{p}\right)^{1/p-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{p}|x|^{-\beta p}\left(-p\log|x|\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Computing the above quantities for
p=2,\beta=0

\displaystyle\partial_{p}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p=2}\\
{\beta=0}\end{subarray}}=||f||_{2}\left(-\frac{1}{4}\log||f||_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}||f||_{2}^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

and similarly

\partial_{\beta}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p=2}\\
{\beta=0}\end{subarray}}=-||f||_{2}^{-1}\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

This means that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}|_{s=0}=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}|_{s=0}\ \partial_{p}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p=2}\\
{\beta=0}\end{subarray}}+

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}|_{s=0}\ \partial_{\beta}\left\||\cdot|^{-\beta}f\right\|_{p}\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}{p=2}\\
{\beta=0}\end{subarray}}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}|_{s=0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}|_{s=0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

Now we choose
s_{1},p_{1},\beta_{1}
in the permitted range of values and, referring to
lemma

10
, define

p(s):=p_{s/s_{1}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\beta(s):=\beta_{s/s_{1}}.

With this choice

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}p}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}|_{s=0}=\frac{4}{s_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)=\frac{4}{d}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{1}}{s_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}|_{s=0}=1-\frac{d}{s_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)=\frac{\beta_{1}}{s_{1}}.

Since we are considering the Rubin inequality with a constant given by
lemma

10
, we need to compute

\partial_{s}\left(C(p_{1},s_{1})\right)^{s/s_{1}}|_{s=0}=\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log C(p_{1},s_{1})

and recalling that

\partial_{s}||f||_{\dot{H}^{s}}|_{s=0}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi

we get the inequality

(4)

\begin{split}\frac{2}{d}\left(1-\frac{\beta_{1}}{s_{1}}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-\frac{\beta_{1}}{s_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\\
\leq\frac{1}{s_{1}}\log C(p_{1},s_{1})+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.\end{split}

In particular we can choose the values
p_{1}=2/(1-2s_{1})
and
\beta_{1}=s_{1}(1-d)
to get

2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|f|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+(d-1)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f|^{2}\log|x|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\widehat{f}|^{2}\log|\xi|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+c

for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
∎
