Chevalley groups over ℤ {\mathbb{Z}} : A representation–theoretic approach
Abid Ali, Lisa Carbone and Scott H. Murray

Abstract
. Let G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) be a simply connected Chevalley group over ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} corresponding to a simple Lie algebra 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} over ℂ {\mathbb{C}} . Let V V be a finite dimensional faithful highest weight 𝔤 \mathfrak{g} -module and let V ℤ V_{\mathbb{Z}} be a Chevalley ℤ {\mathbb{Z}} -form of V V . Let Γ ⁡ ( ℤ ) \Gamma({\mathbb{Z}}) be the subgroup of G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) that preserves V ℤ V_{{\mathbb{Z}}} and let G ⁡ ( ℤ ) G({\mathbb{Z}}) be the group of ℤ {\mathbb{Z}} -points of G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) . Then G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) is integral if G ⁡ ( ℤ ) = Γ ⁡ ( ℤ ) G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}}) . Chevalley’s original work constructs a scheme-theoretic integral form of G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) which equals Γ ⁡ ( ℤ ) \Gamma({\mathbb{Z}}) . Here we give a representation-theoretic proof of integrality of G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) using only the action of G ⁡ ( ℚ ) G({\mathbb{Q}}) on V V , rather than the language of group schemes. We discuss the challenges and open problems that arise in trying to extend this to a proof of integrality for Kac–Moody groups over ℚ {\mathbb{Q}} .

1.
Introduction

In his seminal work
[
Che55
,
Che05
]
, Chevalley gave a unified construction of semisimple linear algebraic groups over arbitrary fields by constructing (what was his notion of) an affine group scheme
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
, corresponding to a semisimple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
over
{\mathbb{C}}
with root system
\Delta
.
Chevalley’s affine group scheme was later replaced by the modern definition due to Demazure and Grothendieck
[
ABD+66
]
.

Let
V
be a faithful finite dimensional highest weight
\mathfrak{g}
-module with representation
\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to{\rm End}(V)
and
let
L
be the lattice generated by
\wts(V)
, the weights of
V
.
Then
L
satisfies
Q\subseteq L\subseteq P
, where
Q
is the root lattice and
P
is the weight lattice of
\mathfrak{g}
. Chevalley

[
Che55
]

associated a group scheme
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
to
(\Delta,L)
and an integral form
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
of
V
, which has the property that
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
is stable under a Cartier–Kostant integral form
\mathcal{U}_{\mathbb{Z}}
of the universal enveloping algebra
\mathcal{U}
of
\mathfrak{g}
. Such a lattice in
V
is called an
admissible lattice
.
Let
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
be the group of
{\mathbb{Q}}
-rational points of
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
and let

\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})=\{g\in G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})\mid g\cdot V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\}

be the subgroup of
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
that preserves
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Chevalley
[
Che55
]
showed that, up to canonical isomorphism, the group
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
depends only on (
\Delta
,
L
) and not on the choice of admissible lattice
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

In this work, we are interested in those properties of the groups
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Q}})
,
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}})
and
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
that are determined by their actions on
V
and
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Let
\mathfrak{h}
be a Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
with dual
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
. Let
\Delta\subseteq{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
be the root system of
{\mathfrak{g}}
with set of positive roots (respectively negative roots)
\Delta_{+}
(respectively
\Delta_{-}
) and simple roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}
.
An essential component of Chevalley’s work (Theorem
2.3
) was to prove the existence of a basis

\mathcal{B}=\{x_{\alpha},h_{i}\mid\alpha\in\Delta,\ h_{i}\in{\mathfrak{h}},\ i\in I\}

for
\mathfrak{g}
such that the structure constants with respect to
\mathcal{B}
are integers.

One can verify (see Section
4
) that for each root
\alpha\in\Delta
, the endomorphism
\rho(x_{\alpha})^{n}
is zero for
n
sufficiently large. It follows that for
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}

(1)

\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(t):=\exp(t\cdot\rho(x_{\alpha}))=I+t\rho(x_{\alpha})+\frac{t^{2}}{2}(\rho(x_{\alpha}))^{2}+\dots

is a finite sum and hence a well defined automorphism of
V
.
Using the weight space decomposition
V=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\wts(V)}V_{\mu}
, we can also define automorphisms

h_{i}(t)
for all
i\in I
and
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
by

(2)

\displaystyle h_{i}(t)\cdot v:=t^{\langle\mu,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle}v

for all
v\in V_{\mu}
.

Following
[
Car93
]
,
[
CMT04
]
and
[
Ste16
]
, we define Chevalley groups in the following way.
Given an integral domain
{\mathbb{F}}
, the Chevalley group
G({\mathbb{F}})
is

G({\mathbb{F}})=\left\langle\chi_{\alpha}(u),h_{i}(t)\mid\alpha\in\Delta,u\in{\mathbb{F}},i\in I,t\in{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}\right\rangle.

Defining relations for
G({\mathbb{F}})
were given by Steinberg
[
Ste16
]
. If
L=Q
, then
G({\mathbb{F}})
is called the
adjoint Chevalley group
and if
L=P
,
G({\mathbb{F}})
is called the
simply connected Chevalley group
.

Taking
{\mathbb{F}}={\mathbb{Q}}
, we say that a subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is
generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
if it has a generating set of the form
\{\chi_{\alpha}(u),h_{i}(t)\mid\alpha\in S,\ u\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ i\in I,\ t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}\}

for some subset
S\subseteq\Delta
.

We let
G({\mathbb{Z}})
denote the subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
by the elements

\{\chi_{\alpha}(u),\ h_{i}(t)\mid\alpha\in\Delta,\ u\in{\mathbb{Z}},i\in I,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}\}.

Then

G({\mathbb{Z}})=\langle\chi_{\alpha}(u),\ h_{i}(-1)\mid\alpha\in\Delta,\ u\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ i\in I\rangle.

For a fixed
V
and
V_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we say that
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is
integral
if

G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}}).

Chevalley’s original work constructs a scheme-theoretic integral form
G_{\mathbb{Z}}({\mathbb{Z}})
of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
which equals
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Our approach differs from that of Chevalley in that we use the definition of Chevalley groups as in
[
Car93
]
,
[
CMT04
]
,
[
Ste16
]
described above. In our representation-theoretic setting, the equality
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
must be proven explicitly.

In certain cases, it is straightforward to verify this equality using properties of the Chevalley group and the underlying representation. See for example Section
5
where there is a simple proof that
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
is the stabilizer of the standard lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}\oplus{\mathbb{Z}}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}\oplus{\mathbb{Q}}
. See also
[
Sou07
]
, where Soulé showed that the Chevalley group
{\mathbb{E}}_{7}({\mathbb{Z}})
(non-compact form) coincides with
{\mathbb{E}}_{7}({\mathbb{R}})\cap\Sp_{56}({\mathbb{Z}})
where
\Sp_{56}({\mathbb{Z}})
is the stabilizer of the standard lattice, with the canonical symplectic form, in the fundamental representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{e}_{7}
of dimension 56.

Here we give a proof that
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
for simply connected Chevalley groups
G({\mathbb{Q}})
using only properties of the action of the group
G({\mathbb{Q}})
on the representation
V
. We first prove that (Theorem
6.8
)

U({\mathbb{Q}})\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})=U({\mathbb{Z}})

where

\displaystyle U({\mathbb{Q}})

\displaystyle:=\langle\chi_{\alpha}(t)\mid\alpha\in\Delta^{+},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\rangle

is the positive unipotent subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
associated to a root system
\Delta
and simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, and

\displaystyle U({\mathbb{Z}})

\displaystyle:=\langle\chi_{\alpha}(t)\mid\alpha\in\Delta^{+},\ t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\rangle

is the subgroup of
U({\mathbb{Q}})
generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

We then use a decomposition of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
as
G({\mathbb{Q}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
(
[
Ste16
, Theorem 18]
and Theorem
8.6
) to show that
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
(Theorem
9.1
).
Here
B({\mathbb{Q}})
denotes the Borel subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
. The decomposition
G({\mathbb{Q}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
is an analog of the Iwasawa decomposition of
G({\mathbb{Q}}_{p})
, where
G({\mathbb{Z}})
plays the role of the maximal compact subgroup
G({\mathbb{Z}}_{p})
.

Part of the motivation for this work is to extend the proof of integrality given here to integrality of representation theoretic Kac–Moody groups
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. Here,
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
is constructed with respect to an integrable highest weight module
V=V^{\lambda}
for the underlying symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, with dominant integral highest weight
\lambda
.

Over a general commutative ring
R
, there is still no widely agreed upon definition of a Kac–Moody group
G(R)
, except in the affine case (
[
All16
]
,
[
Gar80
]
).
A proof of integrality for
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
would give a unique definition of representation theoretic Kac–Moody groups over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
There are certain difficulties and open questions in answering this question for Kac–Moody groups. The issues that arise are discussed in Section
12
.

In
[
ACLM24
]
, we addressed the question of integrality of certain subgroups of a Kac–Moody group
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
associated to a symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A)
with generalized Cartan matrix
A
, using only the definition of
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
acting on an integrable highest weight module
V
. In the Kac–Moody case, the construction of the group
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
depends on the choice of integrable highest weight representation
V=V^{\lambda}
corresponding to a dominant integral weight
\lambda
.
In this setting, we called a subgroup
M
of
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})

integral
if
M\cap G_{V}({\mathbb{Z}})=M\cap\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
, where
G_{V}({\mathbb{Z}})
is the subgroup of
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
generated over
{\mathbb{Z}}
. This is equivalent to showing that for all
g\in M
,
g\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq V_{\mathbb{Z}}
implies that
g\in G_{V}({\mathbb{Z}})
. Let
U_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
be the positive unipotent subgroup of
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
. In
[
ACLM24
]
, we proved that, for all
w\in W
, the
inversion subgroup

U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle U_{\beta}({\mathbb{Q}})\mid\beta\in\Phi_{(w)}\rangle,

is integral, where
\Phi_{(w)}
is a subset of the set of positive real roots
\Delta^{\re}_{+}
, defined as follows

\Phi_{(w)}=\{\beta\in\Delta^{\re}_{+}\mid w^{-1}\beta\in\Delta^{-}\},

where
U_{\beta}({\mathbb{Q}})
is the root group corresponding to
\beta\in\Delta^{\re}_{+}
. We briefly discuss this approach in Section
11
.

Unfortunately, we don’t currently know how to extend this to a proof of integrality for unipotent subgroups of Kac–Moody groups in general.
However, this method gives a proof of integrality of
U_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
when the generalized Cartan matrix
A
has
finite type
. That is, when
A
is a Cartan matrix and
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
coincides with the simply connected semisimple algebraic group associated to
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(A)
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. To summarize this alternate approach to proving integrality, we discuss this proof briefly in Section
11
.
We believe that an analog of Theorem
9.1
is needed to address the integrality question for Kac–Moody groups.

The authors are grateful to Shrawan Kumar for his interest in this work and for helpful discussions.

We make frequent use of the concepts of

Let
A
denote an algebra, an
A

{\mathbb{C}}
.
Then
B\subseteq A

2.1.
Complex semisiple Lie algebras

In this subsection we review some basic theory of complex semisimple Lie algebras.
Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a semisimple Lie algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
.
We fix a
Cartan subalgebra
, that is, a nilpotent subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}
with
[{\mathfrak{h}},{\mathfrak{h}}]\subseteq{\mathfrak{h}}
.
Since
{\mathfrak{g}}
is semisimple,
{\mathfrak{h}}
is abelian.
We write
\ell=\dim{\mathfrak{h}}
for the
rank
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

For
\alpha\in\mathfrak{h}^{*}
, we set

\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}:=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid[h,x]=\alpha(h)x,\text{ for all
$h\in\mathfrak{h}$}\}.

We have
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}={\mathfrak{h}}
.
If
\alpha\neq 0
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}\neq 0
, then
we
call
\alpha
a
root
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
the corresponding
root space
.
Let
\Delta
denote the set of all roots.

Let
(\circ,\circ)
denote the Killing form on
\mathfrak{g}
. This is a nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form whose restriction to
\mathfrak{h}
is also nondegenerate. We also use
(\circ,\circ)
to denote the nondegenerate form induced on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
.
If we restrict the form on
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\ast}
to the
the
{\mathbb{R}}
-vector space
E:={\mathbb{R}}\Delta
, then
E

is a Euclidean space and an
{\mathbb{R}}
-form of
{\mathfrak{h}}^{*}
.
For a root
\alpha
, we define a
root reflection

w_{\alpha}:\quad E\rightarrow E,\quad x\mapsto x-\frac{2(\alpha,x)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}\alpha

and we have
w_{\alpha}(\Delta)\subseteq\Delta
, for all
\alpha\in\Delta
.
Hence the set of roots
\Delta
forms a
root system
in
E
.
Furthermore,
\Delta
is
crystallographic
, that is,

\frac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}
is an integer for every
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
.
The matrix

A:=\left(\dfrac{2(\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j})}{(\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j})}\right)_{i,j\in I}

is called the
Cartan matrix
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.

Let
\pi:E\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a fixed map such that
\ker(\pi)
is a hyperplane that does not intersect
\Delta
.
We say
\alpha\in\Delta
is
positive
(respectively
negative
) if
\pi(\alpha)
is positive
(respectively negative).
The set of positive (respectively negative) roots is denoted
\Delta^{\pm}
.
A positive root
\alpha
is called a
simple root
if it cannot be written as the sum of two positive roots.
The simple roots form a basis of
E
and are denoted

\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{\ell}
.
For convenience we write
I=\{1,\dots,\ell\}
for the indexing set of the simple roots.
Hence every
\alpha\in\Delta^{+}
has an expression of the form

\alpha=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}\alpha_{i}
where the
a_{i}\geq 0
are integers.
The
height
of the root
\alpha=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}\alpha_{i}
is

\htt(\alpha):=\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}.

The reflections
w_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in\Delta
, generate a
reflection group

W=\langle w_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\Delta\rangle\subseteq\GL(E),

called the
Weyl group
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Using the Cartan matrix
A=(A_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
, we define the
Coxeter matrix

(c_{ij})_{i,j\in I}
by

c_{ij}:=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i=j$,}\\
2&\text{if $A_{ij}A_{ji}=0$,}\\
3&\text{if $A_{ij}A_{ji}=1$,}\\
4&\text{if $A_{ij}A_{ji}=2$,}\\
6&\text{if $A_{ij}A_{ji}=3$.}\\
\end{cases}

If we write
w_{i}:=w_{\alpha_{i}}
, then
W
is a
Coxeter group
with presentation

W=\langle w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell}\mid(w_{i}w_{j})^{c_{ij}}=1\text{ for $i,j\in I$}\rangle.

We can write
w\in W
as a word of minimum length in the generators
w_{1},\dots,w_{\ell}
of the form

w=w_{j_{1}}w_{j_{2}}\dots w_{j_{m}}
. This is called a
reduced word
for
w
and

\ell(w):=m
is called the
length
of
w
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any root
\alpha\in\Delta
, we have
\alpha=w\alpha_{i}
for some
w\in W
and some simple root
\alpha_{i}
.

The semisimple Lie algebras over
{\mathbb{C}}
are classified by their corresponding root systems.
The root systems are classified by Dynkin diagram or, equivalently, by Cartan type (
A_{n}
,
B_{n}
and so on).
This also gives
{\mathfrak{g}}
a
root space decomposition

{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{h}}\oplus\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta}{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}

where each
{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}
is one dimensional, and a
triangular decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}^{-}\oplus\mathfrak{h}\oplus\mathfrak{n}^{+},

where

\mathfrak{n}^{+}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
and
\mathfrak{n}^{-}=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Delta^{-}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
are nilpotent subalgebras.

2.2.
Root lattice and weight lattice

Since
\Delta
is crystallographic, the roots generate a lattice

Q:={\mathbb{Z}}\Delta=\bigoplus_{i\in I}{\mathbb{Z}}\alpha_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}

called the
root lattice
with
WQ=Q
.
The Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a
Q
-graded algebra since
[{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha},{\mathfrak{g}}_{\beta}]\subseteq{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha+\beta}
, for all
\alpha,\beta\in Q
.

Lemma 2.2

(
[
Ste16
]
, Lemma 1)
.

For each root
\alpha\in\Delta
, there is a unique

h_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{h}}
such that

(h,h_{\alpha})=\frac{2\alpha(h)}{(\alpha,\alpha)}

for all
h\in{\mathfrak{h}}
. If we write
h_{i}:=h_{\alpha_{i}}
,
then each
h_{\alpha}
is a
{\mathbb{Z}}
-linear combination of the
h_{i}
.

The
coroot

h_{\alpha}
is also written
\alpha^{\vee}
, and
\Delta^{\vee}=\{\alpha^{\vee}\mid\alpha\in\Delta\}
.
We take
\langle\circ,\circ\rangle
to be the natural pairing
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}\times{\mathfrak{h}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
of
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}
with
{\mathfrak{h}}
, that is,

\langle\varphi,h\rangle:=\varphi(h)

for all
\varphi\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{\star}
and
h\in{\mathfrak{h}}
.
For
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
, we have

\langle\alpha,h_{\beta}\rangle=\dfrac{2(\beta,\alpha)}{(\alpha,\alpha)},

and so
\langle\alpha_{i},h_{j}\rangle=A_{ij}
, for all
i,j\in I.

We denote the set of coroots by
\Delta^{\vee}
.
The
coroot lattice
is

Q^{\vee}={\mathbb{Z}}\Delta^{\vee}=\bigoplus_{i\in I}{\mathbb{Z}}h_{i}.

The
weight lattice

P
is the dual of the coroot lattice
Q^{\vee}
under the pairing
\langle\circ,\circ\rangle
.
That is,

P=(Q^{\vee})^{*}=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{h}^{\ast}\mid\langle\lambda,h\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ for all $h\in Q^{\vee}$}\}.

The weight lattice has a basis of
fundamental weights

\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{\ell}
such that

\langle\omega_{i},h_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
,where

\delta_{ij}:=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }i=j\\
0&\mbox{if }i\neq j\end{cases}

is the Kronecker delta.

Similarly the
coweight lattice

P^{\vee}=Q^{*}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}\mid\langle\lambda,h\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ for all $\lambda\in Q$}\}

has a basis of fundamental coweights

\omega^{\vee}_{1},\dots,\omega^{\vee}_{\ell}
such that

\langle\alpha_{i},\omega^{\vee}_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
.
If
\alpha\in Q
then
\alpha=\sum_{i\in I}\langle\alpha,\omega^{\vee}_{i}\rangle\alpha_{i}
,
and if
\lambda\in P
then

\lambda=\sum_{i\in I}\langle\lambda,h_{i}\rangle\omega_{i}
.

Since
\langle\alpha_{j},\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle=A_{ij}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
for
i,j\in I
, we have

\alpha_{i}\in P
,
so roots are weights and
Q\leq P
.
Given a lattice
L
such that
Q\subseteq L\subseteq P
, we can define a dual lattice

L^{*}=\{h\in\mathfrak{h}\mid\langle h,y\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ for all $y\in L$}\}.

Given lattices
Q\subseteq L\subseteq M\subseteq P
we have
M^{*}\subseteq L^{*}
.
It is straightforward to show that

P/Q
is a finite abelian group of order
\left|\det A\right|
where
A
is the Cartan matrix.

.

2.3.
Chevalley basis

If
\alpha
is a root, then the root space
\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}
has dimension one.
For roots
\alpha
and
\beta
,
recall that the constant
p_{\alpha\beta}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
is defined so that

\beta,\beta+\alpha,\dots,\beta+p_{\alpha\beta}\alpha\in\Delta
but
\beta+(p_{\alpha\beta}+1)\beta\notin\Delta
.
Chevalley showed that there is a basis for
\mathfrak{g}
with the following properties.

Theorem 2.3
.

Every semisimple Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
over
{\mathbb{C}}

has a basis

\mathcal{B}=\{x_{\alpha},h_{i}\mid\alpha\in\Delta,\ i\in I\}

such that

\displaystyle h_{i}

\displaystyle\in{\mathfrak{h}},

\displaystyle[h_{i},x_{\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\alpha(h_{i})

\displaystyle[x_{\alpha},x_{-\alpha}]

\displaystyle=h_{\alpha}\in\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\ell}{\mathbb{Z}}h_{i},

\displaystyle[x_{\alpha},x_{\beta}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}n_{\alpha\beta}x_{\alpha+\beta}&\mbox{if }\alpha+\beta\in\Delta,\ \alpha\neq-\beta\\
0&\mbox{otherwise, }\end{cases}

where
n_{\alpha\beta}=\pm(p_{\alpha\beta}+1)
,
and such that the linear map

\theta:\quad\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g},\quad h_{i}\mapsto-h_{i},\quad x_{\alpha}\mapsto x_{-\alpha}

is a Lie algebra automorphism

Hence the the structure constants with respect to
\mathcal{B}
are integers.
Such a basis is called a
Chevalley basis
and
\theta
is called the
Chevalley involution
. The structure constants
n_{\alpha,\beta}\in{\mathbb{C}}
depend only on the roots
\alpha,\beta
and a choice of sign. See

Lemma 3.4

By assuming the inductive hypothesis for

\displaystyle[x^{k}_{\alpha},{x_{-\alpha}}]

\displaystyle=x_{\alpha}{x^{k}_{-\alpha}}-{x^{k}_{-\alpha}}x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=x^{k}_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}-{x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}x_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}+{x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}x_{\alpha}{x_{-\alpha}}-{x^{k}_{-\alpha}}x_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(k-1){x^{k-2}_{-\alpha}}(h_{\alpha}x_{-\alpha})-(k-1)(k-2){x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}+{x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}h_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(k-1){x^{k-2}_{-\alpha}}(x_{-\alpha}h_{\alpha}-2x_{-\alpha})-(k-1)(k-2){x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}+{x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}h_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=k{x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}h_{\alpha}-k(k-1){x^{k-1}_{-\alpha}}

\displaystyle=kx^{k-1}_{-\alpha}(h_{\alpha}-(k-1)).

Secondly, we note that

\displaystyle u\cdot v_{\mu}\in V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}.

We expand
u\cdot v_{\mu}=(\chi_{\beta_{j_{1}}}(s_{{j_{1}}})u^{\prime\prime}x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})})\cdot v_{\nu}
further and write it in its weight space decomposition

\displaystyle(\chi_{\beta_{j_{1}}}(s_{{j_{1}}})u^{\prime\prime}x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})})\cdot v_{\nu}

\displaystyle=x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})}\cdot v_{\nu}+(s_{{j_{1}}}x_{\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(1)}x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})})\cdot v_{\nu}+\dots+(s_{{j_{1}}}^{n}x_{\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})}x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})})\cdot v_{\nu}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\text{weight vectors of lower depth than the depth of $\nu$}.

Set
m=\langle\lambda,h_{\beta_{j_{1}}}\rangle
. Since
\lambda
is regular,
\langle\lambda,h_{\beta_{j_{i}}}\rangle\neq 0
for all

. Then

for all

, since the height of each
\beta_{j_{i}}
is greater than or equal to the height of
\beta_{j_{1}}
. We have

\{\chi_{\beta_{j_{1}}}(s_{{j_{1}}})u^{\prime\prime}\cdot x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})}v_{\nu}\}\cap V_{\nu}=\{(s_{{j_{1}}}^{n^{\prime}}x_{\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})}x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})})\cdot v_{\nu}\}

Combining this with Equation (
11
), we have

(s_{{j_{1}}}^{n^{\prime}}x_{\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})}x_{-\beta_{j_{1}}}^{(n^{\prime})})\cdot v_{\nu}\in V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\cap V_{\nu}.

By Lemma
6.7

\mu
is the lowest weight of an
\mathfrak{sl}_{\beta_{j_{1}}}
-module,
w_{\beta_{j_{1}}}\mu=\nu
is the highest weight of this module. More precisely

is not a weight of the
\mathfrak{sl}_{\beta_{j_{1}}}
-module and hence by Lemma

,
s_{{j_{1}}}^{n^{\prime}}\cdot v_{\nu}\in V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. This implies

and thus

. So, we get

u^{\prime\prime}\cdot V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=\chi_{\beta_{1}}(-t_{\beta_{j_{1}}})u\cdot V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\subseteq V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}

and hence by the inductive hypothesis
t_{\beta_{j_{i}}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, for all

This completes the induction and the proof of the statement follows.
∎

7.
Toral subgroup

Recall from Equation (
2
) that for
\mu\in{\rm wts}(V)
, the elements
h_{i}(t)

act on weight vectors
v\in V_{\mu}
as

h_{i}(t)\cdot v=t^{\langle\mu,h_{\alpha_{i}}\rangle}v.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional faithful
\mathfrak{g}
-module.
Let
H({\mathbb{Q}})\leq G({\mathbb{Q}})
be the subgroup generated by the elements
h_{i}(t)
,
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
,
i\in I
.

(1)

The group
H({\mathbb{Q}})
is abelian.

(2)

Every element of
H({\mathbb{Q}})
may be expressed in the form
\prod_{i\in I}h_{i}(t_{i})
, for
t_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
.

Proof.

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Delta
. Let
\mu\in{\rm wts}(V)
. For each weight
\mu
of
V
, and
s,t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
,

h_{\alpha}(s)h_{\beta}(t)
acts on the weight space
V_{\mu}
as
s^{\langle\mu,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle}t^{\langle\mu,\beta^{\vee}\rangle}
. Thus
h_{\alpha}(s)h_{\beta}(t)=h_{\beta}(t)h_{\alpha}(s)
. Since this is true for each representation
V
, we obtain

h_{\alpha}(s)h_{\beta}(t)=h_{\beta}(t)h_{\alpha}(s)
. Similarly
h_{\alpha}(s)h_{\alpha}(t)=h_{\alpha}(st)
, which proves (1).

For (2), let
\alpha^{\vee}=\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}
. Then for
t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
and each weight
\mu
of
V
,

t^{\langle\mu,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle}=t^{\langle\mu,\sum_{i\in I}c_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle}=\prod_{i\in I}t^{c_{i}\langle\mu,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle}.

But
h_{\alpha}(t)
acts as
t^{\langle\mu,\alpha^{\vee}\rangle}
on
V^{\mu}
and
\prod_{i\in I}h_{i}(t^{c_{i}})
acts as
\prod_{i\in I}t^{c_{i}\langle\mu,\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle}
on
V^{\mu}
. Since this is true for every
V
, we obtain

h_{\alpha}(t)=\prod_{i\in I}h_{i}(t^{c_{i}}),

so every
h_{\alpha}(t)
can be written in the desired form. Since
H({\mathbb{Q}})
is generated by the
h_{i}(t)
and is abelian, every element of
H({\mathbb{Q}})
can be written in the desired form.
∎

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional faithful
\mathfrak{g}
-module such that the set of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. Then the map
t\mapsto h_{i}(t)
is an injective homomorphism from
{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times}
into
H({\mathbb{Q}})
, for each
i\in I
.

Proof.

Let
h_{i}(t)\in H({\mathbb{Q}})
. Since each fundamental weight
\omega_{i}
,
i\in I
, is a weight of
V
,
h_{i}(t)
acts as scalar multiplication on the weight space
V_{\omega_{i}}
by

t^{\langle\omega_{i},\alpha_{i}^{\vee}\rangle}=t.

Hence for each
i\in I
, if
h_{i}(t)=1
then
t=1
.∎

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
V
be a
\mathfrak{g}
-module such that the set of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. Then the map

({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{\ell}\longrightarrow H({\mathbb{Q}})

(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{\ell})\mapsto\prod_{i\in I}h_{i}(t_{i})

is an injective homomorphism.

Proof.

For each
i\in I
, the restriction
t_{i}\mapsto h_{i}(t_{i})
of this map to the
i
-th factor of
({\mathbb{Q}}^{\times})^{\ell}
is injective.
∎

We note that the hypothesis of Corollary
7.3
, that the set of weights contains all the fundamental weights, is satisfied when
G
is a simply connected Chevalley group or whenever the root lattice
Q
is isomorphic to the weight lattice
P
.

8.
Iwasawa decomposition of
G

We recall the Bruhat decomposition of
G
, which holds over any field
[
Ste16
]
:

G=BWB=\bigsqcup_{w\in W}BwB

where
B=HU
is
the Borel subgroup of
G
. We also write
B=B({\mathbb{Q}})
.

As before, let
V
denote a finite dimensional faithful
\mathfrak{g}
-module with highest weight
\lambda
. We assume that
L_{V}=P
. Let
G({\mathbb{Q}})
be the corresponding simply connected Chevalley group.

Lemma 8.1
.

We have
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})=\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})B(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}))
, where
B(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}))
denotes the Borel subgroup of
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
.

Proof.

Let
\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
and let
\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\
r&s\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
. Consider the product

\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\
r&s\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}).

We may choose
c,d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
with
\gcd(c,d)=1
and
cp+dr=0
. This determines
a,\ b\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
ad-bc=1
. Then

\left(\begin{matrix}p&q\\
r&s\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{matrix}\right)^{-1}\left(\begin{matrix}\alpha&\beta\\
0&\gamma\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})B(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})).\ \qed

See also
[
Ste16
]
, Lemma 43(c), p. 101.

Lemma 8.2

(
[
Ste16
]
, Lemmas 48 and 49)
.

For fixed
i
,

\varphi_{i}(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}))=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(s),\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\rangle.

Now let
Y_{i}
be a system of coset representatives for
Bw_{i}B/B
. A natural choice is
[
Ste16
, Lemma 43(a)]

Y_{i}=\{\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)w_{i}\mid t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\}.

Lemma 8.3
.

The
Y_{i}
can be chosen with integral coordinates. That is, we may choose coset representatives
Y_{i}=\{\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)w_{i}\}
for
Bw_{i}B/B
such that

Y_{i}\subseteq\varphi_{i}(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}))
.

Proof.

In
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
we have

\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(t)w_{i}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&t\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}-t&~1\\
-1&~0\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}).

By Lemma
8.1
, we can write an element of
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
in
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})B(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}))
:

\left(\begin{matrix}-t&~1\\
-1&~0\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}-t&~t+1\\
-1&~1\end{matrix}\right)\cdot\left(\begin{matrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right).

Modulo
B
, the
Y_{i}
can thus be chosen such that
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and so

Y_{i}=\varphi_{i}\left(\left(\begin{matrix}1&t\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right)\cdot\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{matrix}\right)\right)\subseteq\varphi_{i}(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})).\qed

It follows that for each
i
,

Y_{i}\subseteq\Delta_{i}(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}}))=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(s),\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}\rangle\leq G({\mathbb{Z}}).

Lemma 8.4
.

For each
i\in I
,

Bw_{i}B=Y_{i}B,

where
Y_{i}
is a system of coset representatives for
Bw_{i}B/B
.

See also
[
Ste16
]
, Theorem 15, p 99.

Corollary 8.5
.

For each
i\in I
, we have
Bw_{i}B=Y_{i}B\leq G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}}).

For example, an element
\left(\begin{matrix}1/2&~0\\
3/4&~2\end{matrix}\right)\in\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})
that is not in upper triangular form can be written in
\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})B(\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Q}}))
:

\left(\begin{matrix}1/2&~0\\
3/4&~2\end{matrix}\right)=\left(\begin{matrix}2&1\\
3&2\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}1/4&-2\\
0&~4\end{matrix}\right).

The following theorem gives an analog of Lemma
8.1
for
G({\mathbb{Q}})
.

Theorem 8.6

(
[
Ste16
]
, Theorem 18, p. 114)
.

We have

G({\mathbb{Q}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
.

Proof:
We prove Theorem
8.6
using the Bruhat decomposition. That is, we prove by induction on the length of
w\in W
that each Bruhat cell
B({\mathbb{Q}})wB({\mathbb{Q}})
is contained in
G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
. The case
\ell(w)=0
gives

B({\mathbb{Q}})\leq G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
. The case
\ell(w)=1
follows from Corollary
8.5
, which gives a base for induction.

Assume inductively that

B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}B({\mathbb{Q}})\leq G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}}),

for all
k\geq 1
, where
w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}
has minimal length, and each
w_{i_{j}}
is a simple root reflection.

Let
w_{i_{k+1}}
be a simple root reflection and assume that
w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}w_{i_{k+1}}
has minimal length. Then the rule B3 gives

B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}w_{i_{k+1}}B({\mathbb{Q}})=B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{k+1}}B({\mathbb{Q}}).

By the inductive hypothesis,
B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}B({\mathbb{Q}})\leq G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
. Since
w_{i_{k+1}}
is a simple root reflection, the inductive hypothesis also implies that

B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{k+1}}B({\mathbb{Q}})\leq G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}}).

Then

B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{k+1}}B({\mathbb{Q}})

is the product in
G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
of the images of
B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\dots w_{i_{k}}B({\mathbb{Q}})
and
B({\mathbb{Q}})w_{i_{k+1}}B({\mathbb{Q}})
. Thus this product also lies in

. Hence each each Bruhat cell
B({\mathbb{Q}})wB({\mathbb{Q}})
is contained in
G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
.

\square

9.
Integrality of
G(\mathbb{Q})

Recall that
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})=\{g\in G({\mathbb{Q}})\mid g\cdot V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}\}

is the subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
that preserves
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
.

Theorem 9.1
.

In
G({\mathbb{Q}})
we have
G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap U({\mathbb{Q}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap U({\mathbb{Q}})
.
Thus any element of
U({\mathbb{Q}})
that stabilizes
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
lies in
G({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Proof:
We have
G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap U({\mathbb{Q}})\subseteq\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap U({\mathbb{Q}})
, since by Lemma
6.4
,
G({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
. For the reverse inclusion, let
\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap U({\mathbb{Q}})
. So
\gamma\in U({\mathbb{Q}})
and
\gamma\cdot V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
. Since
\gamma\in U({\mathbb{Q}})
, we have
\gamma=\prod_{\alpha\in\Delta^{+}}\chi_{\alpha}(t_{\alpha})
. By Theorem
6.8
, all
t_{\alpha}
are integers. Hence
\gamma\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
.
\square

Theorem 9.2
.

Suppose that the set
{\rm wts}(V)
of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. In
G({\mathbb{Q}})
we have

G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap H({\mathbb{Q}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap H({\mathbb{Q}})=H({\mathbb{Z}})=\langle h_{i}(-1)\mid i\in I\rangle.

Proof.

It is immediate that
G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap H({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle h_{i}(-1)\mid i\in I\rangle
. The generator
h_{i}(t_{i})
acts on the weight space
V_{\omega_{i}}
as scalar multiplication by
t_{i}
. Hence
h_{i}(t_{i})
preserves
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
when
t\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{\times}
. Thus
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap H({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle h_{i}(-1)\mid i\in I\rangle
.
∎

See also
[
Ste16
]
, Lemma 49(c), p. 114.

Corollary 9.3
.

Suppose that the set
{\rm wts}(V)
of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. In
G({\mathbb{Q}})
we have
G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap B({\mathbb{Q}})=\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap B({\mathbb{Q}})
.
Thus any element of
B({\mathbb{Q}})
that stabilizes

lies in
G({\mathbb{Z}})
.

This allows us to prove our main result.

Theorem 9.4
.

Suppose that the set
{\rm wts}(V)
of weights of
V
contains all the fundamental weights. We have
\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})
.
That is, any element of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
that stabilizes
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
lies in the group
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(s),\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(t)\mid s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}},\ i\in I\rangle
.

Proof.

We have
G({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq G({\mathbb{Q}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})
by Theorem
8.6
. Let
\gamma\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
. Then

\gamma=\gamma_{0}b\in G({\mathbb{Q}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})B({\mathbb{Q}})

with
\gamma_{0}\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
and

. Thus

and by Lemma

,
G({\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})
, so

\gamma_{0}^{-1}\gamma\in\Gamma({\mathbb{Z}})\cap B({\mathbb{Q}})=G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap B({\mathbb{Q}})

by Corollary

. Thus
\gamma_{0}^{-1}\gamma\in G({\mathbb{Z}})

\gamma_{0}\in G({\mathbb{Z}})
.
∎

10.
Generating sets for
G({\mathbb{Q}})
and
G({\mathbb{Z}})

Our simply connected Chevalley group
G({\mathbb{Q}})
is generated by

\{\chi_{\alpha}(u),\ h_{i}(t)\mid\alpha\in\Delta,u\in{\mathbb{Q}},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times},\ i\in I\}.

This generating set is redundant and we may reduce it using Steinberg’s group relations
[
Ste16
]
.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
G({\mathbb{Q}})
be a simply connected Chevalley group. Then

\displaystyle G({\mathbb{Q}})

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(s),\ \widetilde{w}_{\alpha_{i}}(t),\ h_{i}(t)\mid s\in{\mathbb{Q}},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times},\ i\in I\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(s),\ \chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(u),\ h_{i}(t)\mid s,u\in{\mathbb{Q}},\ t\in{\mathbb{Q}}^{\times},\ i\in I\rangle.

Proof.

For all
\alpha\in\Delta
there exists
w\in W
such that
\alpha=w\alpha_{i}
for some
i\in I
. We have

\chi_{\alpha}(t)=\chi_{w\alpha_{i}}=\widetilde{w}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\pm t)\widetilde{w}^{-1}

for a lifting
\widetilde{w}\in\widetilde{W}
of
w\in W
under the natural projection
\widetilde{W}\longrightarrow W
. The
\widetilde{w}_{\alpha_{i}}
generate
\widetilde{W}
, thus the first equality follows. The second inequality is obvious since each
\widetilde{w}_{\alpha_{i}}
is a product of
\chi_{\alpha_{i}}
’s and
\chi_{-\alpha_{i}}
’s.
∎

Corollary 10.2
.

The group
G(\mathbb{Z})
has the following generating sets:

(1)

\{\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(1),\ \chi_{-\alpha_{i}}(1),\ h_{i}(-1)\mid i\in I\}
, or

(2)

\{\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(1),\ \widetilde{w}_{\alpha_{i}},\ h_{i}(-1)\mid i\in I\}
.

Proof.

(1) is an immediate consequence of the exponential rule

\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(s)=\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(1+1+\dots+1)=\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(1)^{s}

for
s\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. (2) follows immediately from (1).
∎

In some cases (such as
G({\mathbb{Z}})=\SL_{2}({\mathbb{Z}})
), the elements
h_{i}(-1)
, for
i\in I
, are not required as explicit generators of
G({\mathbb{Z}})
.

11.
Integrality of inversion subgroups of Kac–Moody groups

For the remaining two sections, we assume that
{\mathfrak{g}}
is a symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra over
{\mathbb{C}}
and that
(V,\rho)
is an integrable
{\mathfrak{g}}
-module. That is,
V
is a weight module and the Chevalley–Serre generators of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, denoted
e_{i}
and
f_{i}
for
i\in I
, act locally nilpotently on any
x\in V
. That is, for each
x\in V
, there exists a natural number
n=n(i,x)
such that

\rho(e_{i})^{n}\cdot x=\rho(f_{i})^{n}\cdot x=0.

In order to extend our representation-theoretic results to a proof of integrality for Kac–Moody groups, we considered an alternate approach in
[
ACLM24
]
. We proved integrality of inversion subgroups of
U({\mathbb{Q}})
, which are defined as follows.
For
w
in the Weyl group
W
, the
inversion subgroup

U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Q}})\leq U({\mathbb{Q}})
is

U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Q}})=\langle U_{\beta}({\mathbb{Q}})\mid\beta\in\Phi_{(w)}\rangle,

where

\Phi_{(w)}=\{\beta\in\Delta^{\re}_{+}\mid w^{-1}\beta\in\Delta_{-}\}

and
U_{\beta}({\mathbb{Q}})=\{\chi_{\beta}(t)\mid t\in{\mathbb{Q}}\}
where

\chi_{\beta}(t)=\widetilde{w}\chi_{\alpha_{i}}(\pm t)\widetilde{w}^{-1}
and
\beta=w\alpha_{i}
for some
w\in W
. If
w\in W
has the reduced expression
w_{i_{1}}w_{i_{2}}\cdots w_{i_{k}}
then
\Phi_{(w)}
has cardinality
k=\ell(w)
. We set

U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Z}})=U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Q}})\cap U({\mathbb{Z}}).

Theorem 11.1

(
[
ACLM24
]
)
.

Let
U({\mathbb{Q}})
be the positive unipotent subgroup of
G({\mathbb{Q}})
. For any
w\in W
,

U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Q}})
is integral. That is for all
g\in U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Q}})
,
g\cdot V_{\mathbb{Z}}\subseteq V_{\mathbb{Z}}
implies that
g\in U_{(w)}({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Theorem
11.1
holds in the case that
\mathfrak{g}
is a semisimple Lie algebra and
V
is any highest weight
\mathfrak{g}
-module. In this case, the Weyl group
W
is finite and
W
contains the longest element, denoted
w_{0}
, which flips all positive roots to negative roots. That is
\Phi_{(w_{0})}=\Delta^{+}
, which gives
U_{(w_{0})}=U({\mathbb{Q}})
.
It follows that
G({\mathbb{Z}})\cap U({\mathbb{Q}})=U({\mathbb{Z}})
.

Unfortunately, Kac–Moody algebras have infinite Weyl groups and there is no analog of the longest element which flips all positive roots to negative roots. Hence this method cannot be used to prove integrality of unipotent subgroups in Kac–Moody groups.

12.
Integrality of Kac–Moody groups over
{\mathbb{Q}}

Part of the motivation for this work is to extend the proof of integrality of Chevalley groups given here to integrality of representation theoretic Kac–Moody groups
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
over
{\mathbb{Q}}
. The group
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
is constructed with respect to an integrable highest weight module
V=V^{\lambda}
for the underlying symmetrizable Kac–Moody algebra
\mathfrak{g}
, with dominant integral highest weight
\lambda
.

Over a general commutative ring
R
, there is still no widely agreed upon definition of a Kac–Moody group
G(R)
, except in the affine case (
[
All16
]
,
[
Gar80
]
). It is expected be some sort of generalization of the notion of a Chevalley–Demazure group scheme. The groundwork for the notion of a Kac–Moody group over an arbitrary commutative ring was provided by Tits who defined a functor
\widetilde{\mathfrak{G}}
from commutative rings to groups
[
Tit87
]
.

There are two natural candidates for a representation theoretic Kac–Moody group over
{\mathbb{Z}}
:

•

A representation theoretic construction
G_{V}({\mathbb{Z}})
of the value of the Tits functor
\widetilde{\mathfrak{G}}
over
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

•

The subgroup
\Gamma_{V}({\mathbb{Z}})=\{g\in G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})\mid g\cdot V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}=V_{\mathbb{Z}}\}
preserving an integral lattice
V_{{\mathbb{Z}}}
in
V
.

A proof of integrality for
G_{V}({\mathbb{Q}})
would therefore give a unique definition of representation theoretic Kac–Moody groups over
