Cohomologies of difference Lie groups and van Est theorem
Jun Jiang, Yunnan Li, Yunhe Sheng

Abstract
. A difference Lie group is a Lie group equipped with a difference operator, equivalently a crossed homomorphism with respect to the adjoint action. In this paper, first we introduce the notion of a representation of a difference Lie group, and establish the relation between representations of difference Lie groups and representations of difference Lie algebras via differentiation and integration. Then we introduce a cohomology theory for difference Lie groups and justify it via the van Est theorem. Finally, we classify abelian extensions of difference Lie groups using the second cohomology group as applications.

1.
Introduction

Crossed homomorphisms on groups first appeared in Whitehead’s earlier work
[
28
]
and were later applied to study non-abelian Galois cohomology
[
25
]
. Recently, crossed homomorphisms were used to study Hopf-Galois structures
[
26
]
and construct representations of mapping class groups of surfaces
[
4
,
12
]
.
In the definition of a crossed homomorphism
\mathcal{D}:G\to H
on groups, there is an action of the group
G
on the group
H
. A crossed homomorphism on a group
G
with respect to the adjoint action is called a difference operator in this paper. Meanwhile, a group together with a difference operator is called a difference group. Difference operators on groups were studied in
[
8
]

as the inverse of Rota-Baxter operators on groups introduced there with the motivation from factorization problems and integrable systems
[
23
,
24
]
. In the category of Hopf algebras, similar structures are called bijective 1-cocycles, and applied to construct solutions of the quantum Yang-Baxter equation
[
2
]
.

In the Lie algebra context, the concept of crossed homomorphisms was introduced in
[
16
]
in the study of non-abelian extensions of Lie algebras. Crossed homomorphisms on Lie algebras with respect to the adjoint representation are in fact difference operators (also called differential operators of weight 1), abstracted from original instance in numerical analysis to algebraic settings of associative and Lie algebras
[
7
,
13
,
15
]
. Lie’s third theorem holds for crossed homomorphisms on Lie groups and Lie algebras
[
8
,
11
,
18
]
. Crossed homomorphisms have various applications, e.g. in the study of post-Lie algebras and post-Lie Magnus expansion
[
18
]
and representations of Cartan type Lie algebras
[
20
]
.

In this paper, we study representations and cohomologies of difference Lie groups, and give applications in the study of abelian extensions of difference Lie groups.

A representation of a Lie group is a smooth homomorphism from this Lie group to the general linear Lie group of a vector space.
A basic tool to study representations of Lie groups is the usage of the corresponding “infinitesimal” representations of Lie algebras.
The representation theory of connected compact Lie groups parallels to
that of semisimple Lie algebras. We introduce the notion of representations of difference Lie groups and establish the relation with representations of difference Lie algebras via differentiation and integration. More precisely, one can obtain a representation of the difference Lie algebra by differentiating a representation of a difference Lie group, and conversely one can also obtain a representation of the difference Lie group by integrating a representation of a difference Lie algebra.

A classical approach to study a mathematical structure is to associate to it invariants. Among these, cohomology theories occupy a central position as they enable for example to control deformations or extension problems. The cohomology theory of difference Lie algebras was given in
[
11
]
, and it was shown that infinitesimal deformations of a difference Lie algebra are classified by the second cohomology group. In this paper, we establish the cohomology theory for difference Lie groups with coefficients in arbitrary representations. To justify its correctness, we show that the van Est theorem holds for cohomologies of difference Lie groups and cohomologies of difference Lie algebras given in
[
11
]
. The classical van Est isomorphism
[
27
]
gives the relation between the differentiable cohomology of Lie groups and the cohomology of Lie algebras. See
[
1
,
3
,
5
,
10
,
14
,
17
,
21
,
22
]

for various van Est type theorems and applications.

Finally we study abelian extensions of difference Lie groups as applications. We show that abelian extensions of difference Lie groups are classified by the second cohomology group given above. As a byproduct, we classify difference operators on the semidirect product Lie group via certain quotient of the second cohomology group of the difference operator. See
[
19
]
for more details of abelian extensions of infinite dimensional Lie groups.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notion of representations of difference Lie groups, and establish its relation with representations of difference Lie algebras via differentiation and integration. In Section
3
, we introduce a cohomology theory for difference Lie groups. To do that, first we give the cohomology of a difference operator, and then combine the cohomology of a difference operator and the cohomology of a Lie group to obtain the cohomology of a difference Lie group. The relation between these cohomology groups are given by a long exact sequence (Theorem
3.6
). In Section
4
, we show that the van Est theorem holds for cohomologies of difference Lie groups and cohomologies of difference Lie algebras. In Section
5
, we classify abelian extensions of difference Lie groups in terms of the second cohomology group introduced in Section
3
.

2.
Representations of difference Lie groups and difference Lie algebras

In this section, we introduce the notion of a representation of a difference Lie group, and establish the relation between representations of difference Lie groups and representations of difference Lie algebras via differentiation and integration.

2.1.
Representations of difference Lie groups

We introduce the notion of a representation of a difference Lie group, which gives rise to the semidirect product difference Lie group.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
G
be a Lie group. A smooth map
\mathcal{D}:G\,\rightarrow\,G
is called a
difference operator
on
G
if the following equality holds:

(1)

\mathcal{D}(gh)=\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h)g^{-1},\quad\forall g,h\in G.

A
difference Lie group

(G,\mathcal{D})
is a Lie group
G
equipped with a difference operator
\mathcal{D}
.

Example 2.2
.

Let
G
be an abelian Lie group. Then a Lie group homomorphism
\mathcal{D}:G\,\rightarrow\,G
is a difference operator.

Example 2.3
.

Let
G
be a Lie group. Then the inverse map
(\cdot)^{-1}:G\,\rightarrow\,G
is a difference operator and
(G,(\cdot)^{-1})
is a difference Lie group.

Example 2.4
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{B})
be a Rota-Baxter Lie group, i.e.
G
is a Lie group and
\mathcal{B}:G\to G
is a smooth map satisfying

\mathcal{B}(g)\mathcal{B}(h)=\mathcal{B}(g\mathrm{Ad}_{\mathcal{B}(g)}h),\quad\forall g,h\in G.

If
\mathcal{B}
is invertible, then
(G,\mathcal{B}^{-1})
is a difference Lie group.

Example 2.5
.

Let
G
be the real matrix Lie group
GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Then the adjugate map
(\cdot)^{*}:G\,\rightarrow\,G
is a difference operator and
(G,(\cdot)^{*})
is a difference Lie group.

Example 2.6
.

Let
G
be the complex matrix Lie group
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Then
G
endowed with the map taking any
g\in G
to
\overline{g}g^{-1}
is a difference Lie group, where
\overline{g}
is the complex conjugate of
g
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{D})
be a difference Lie group. Then
\mathcal{D}(e_{G})=e_{G}
, where
e_{G}
is the unit of
G
, and

(2)

\mathcal{D}(g^{-1})=(\mathcal{D}(g)g)^{-1}g,\quad\forall g\in G.

Proof.

By (
1
), we have
\mathcal{D}(e_{G})=e_{G}
. Since
e_{G}=\mathcal{D}(gg^{-1})=\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(g^{-1})g^{-1}
, it implies that
\mathcal{D}(g^{-1})=(\mathcal{D}(g)g)^{-1}g.

∎

Lemma 2.8
.

If
\mathcal{D}
is a difference operator, then it induces a Lie group homomorphism
\mathcal{D}_{+}:G\,\rightarrow\,G,
defined by
\mathcal{D}_{+}(g)=\mathcal{D}(g)g
, for all
g\in G
.

Proof.

By
(
1
)
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{+}(gh)=\mathcal{D}(gh)gh=\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h)h=\mathcal{D}_{+}(g)\mathcal{D}_{+}(h)
, Thus
\mathcal{D}_{+}:G\,\rightarrow\,G
is a Lie group homomorphism.
∎

Definition 2.9
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{D})
and
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
be two difference Lie groups. A
homomorphism
from
(G,\mathcal{D})
to
(G^{\prime},\mathcal{D}^{\prime})
consists of a Lie group homomorphism
\Psi:G\rightarrow G^{\prime}
such that

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\circ\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi\circ\mathcal{D}.

Definition 2.10
.

A
representation
of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
on a vector space
V
with respect to a linear map
T:V\,\rightarrow\,V
is a Lie group homomorphism
\Theta
from
G
to
GL(V)
such that

(4)

T(\Theta(g)u)+\Theta(g)u=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)T(u)+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)u,\quad\forall g\in G,u\in V.

We denote a representation by
(V,T,\Theta)
.

Example 2.11
.

For the difference Lie group
(GL_{n}(\mathbb{R}),(\cdot)^{*})
given in Example
2.5
, let
V=(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{\otimes n}
and
(V,\Theta)
be the tensor representation of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
. Let
\tilde{T}
be the anti-symmetrization map on
V
defined by

\tilde{T}(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{n})=\sum_{\epsilon\in S_{n}}(-1)^{|\epsilon|}u_{\epsilon(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{\epsilon(n)},\quad\forall u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Denote
T=\tilde{T}-{\rm id}_{V}
. Then
(V,T,\Theta)
is a representation of the difference Lie group
(GL_{n}(\mathbb{R}),(\cdot)^{*})
. Indeed, for any
g\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(\Theta(g)(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{n}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(gu_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes gu_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\epsilon\in S_{n}}(-1)^{|\epsilon|}gu_{\epsilon(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes gu_{\epsilon(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(g)\sum_{\epsilon\in S_{n}}(-1)^{|\epsilon|}u_{\epsilon(1)}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{\epsilon(n)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(g^{*}g)\tilde{T}(u_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes u_{n}).

Hence, Eq. (
4
) holds for
(V,T,\Theta)
.

Example 2.12
.

For the difference Lie group
(GL_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\overline{(\cdot)}(\cdot)^{-1})
given in Example
2.6
, let
V=\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
(V,\Theta)
be the vector representation of
GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. Let
T
be an
\mathbb{R}
-linear operator on
V
defined by

T(u)=\overline{u}-u,\quad\forall u\in V.

Then
(V,T,\Theta)
is a real representation of
(GL_{n}(\mathbb{C}),\overline{(\cdot)}(\cdot)^{-1})
. In fact, for any
g\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

\displaystyle T(\Theta(g)u)+\Theta(g)u=\overline{gu}=\Theta(\overline{g})\overline{u}=\Theta((\overline{g}g^{-1})g)(T(u)+u).

Hence, Eq. (
4
) holds for
(V,T,\Theta)
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and
\mathfrak{g}
be the corresponding Lie algebra of
G
. Since
\mathrm{Ad}(g)\in\mathrm{Aut}(G)
for all
g\in G
and
\mathrm{Ad}(g)e_{G}=e_{G}
, it follows that
\mathrm{Ad}(g)_{*e_{G}}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}
is an isomorphism of Lie algebras. By
\mathrm{Ad}(g_{1}g_{2})=\mathrm{Ad}(g_{1})\mathrm{Ad}(g_{2})
, we have
\mathrm{Ad}(g_{1}g_{2})_{*e_{G}}=\mathrm{Ad}(g_{1})_{*e_{G}}\mathrm{Ad}(g_{2})_{*e_{G}}
. Thus we obtain a Lie group homomorphism from the Lie group
G
to
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
, which is also denoted by
\mathrm{Ad}:G\,\rightarrow\,\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{D})
be a difference Lie group. Then
(\mathfrak{g},D,\mathrm{Ad})
is a representation of
(G,\mathcal{D})
, where
D=\mathcal{D}_{*e_{G}}
.

Proof.

For any
g\in G
and
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, since
\mathrm{Ad}:G\,\rightarrow\,\mathrm{Aut}(\mathfrak{g})
is a Lie group homomorphism and
\mathrm{Ad}(g)x=\frac{d}{dt}\big|_{t=0}g\exp(tx)g^{-1}
, by (
2
), we have

\displaystyle D(\mathrm{Ad}(g)x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\mathcal{D}(g\exp(tx)g^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\mathcal{D}(g\exp(tx))g\exp(tx)\mathcal{D}(g^{-1})\exp(-tx)g^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx))\exp(tx)\mathcal{D}(g^{-1})\exp(-tx)g^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Big((\mathcal{D}(g)g)\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx))(\mathcal{D}(g)g)^{-1}\Big)\Big((\mathcal{D}(g)g)\exp(tx)(\mathcal{D}(g)g)^{-1}\Big)\Big(g\exp(-tx)g^{-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(\mathcal{D}(g)g)\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx))(\mathcal{D}(g)g)^{-1}+\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}(\mathcal{D}(g)g)\exp(tx)(\mathcal{D}(g)g)^{-1}+\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}g\exp(-tx)g^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ad}(\mathcal{D}(g)g)(D(x))+\mathrm{Ad}(\mathcal{D}(g)g)x-\mathrm{Ad}(g)x.

Thus
(\mathfrak{g},D,\mathrm{Ad})
is a representation of the difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
.
∎

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
(V,T,\Theta)
be a representation of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
. Then
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes})
is a difference Lie group, where
G\ltimes_{\Theta}V
is the semidirect product Lie group, in which the multiplication
\cdot_{\ltimes}
is given by

(g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(h,v)=(gh,u+\Theta(g)v),\quad\forall g,h\in G,u,v\in V,

and
\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}
is given by

\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}(g,u)=(\mathcal{D}(g),T(u)+u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u),\quad\forall g\in G,u\in V.

Proof.

Since
(V,T,\Theta)
is a representation of the difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
, by (
1
) and (
4
), for all
g,h\in G
and
u,v\in V
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}((g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(h,v))\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}(gh,u+\Theta(g)v)\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{D}(gh),T(u)+T(\Theta(g)v)+u+\Theta(g)v-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(gh))u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(gh))\Theta(g)v\Big)\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{D}(gh)g,T(u)+T(\Theta(g)v)+u+\Theta(g)v-\cancel{\Theta(\mathcal{D}(gh))u}-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(gh))\Theta(g)v+\cancel{\Theta(\mathcal{D}(gh))u}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h),T(u)+T(\Theta(g)v)+u+\Theta(g)v-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h))v\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h),T(u)+u+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)T(v)+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)v-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h))v\Big)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}(g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}(h,v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}(g),T(u)+u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(\mathcal{D}(h),T(v)+v-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(h))v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}(g)g,T(u)+u-\cancel{\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)u)}+\cancel{\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u})\cdot_{\ltimes}(\mathcal{D}(h),T(v)+v-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(h))v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h),T(u)+u+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)T(v)+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)v-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h))v\Big),

which implies

\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}((g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(h,v))=\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}(g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)\cdot_{\ltimes}\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes}(h,v)\cdot_{\ltimes}(g,u)^{-1}.

Thus
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes})
is a difference Lie group.
∎

2.2.
Differentiation and integration of representations

In this subsection, we establish the relationship between representations of difference Lie groups and representations of difference Lie algebras via differentiation and integration. Recall representations of difference Lie algebras as following.

Definition 2.15
.

A
difference operator on a Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}
is a linear map
D:\mathfrak{g}\,\rightarrow\,\mathfrak{g}
such that

D([x,y])=[D(x),y]+[x,D(y)]+[D(x),D(y)],\quad\forall x,y\in\mathfrak{g}.

A difference Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},D)
is a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
equipped with a difference operator
D:\mathfrak{g}\,\rightarrow\,\mathfrak{g}
.

Definition 2.16
.

[
11
]

A
representation of a difference Lie algebra

(\mathfrak{g},D)
on a vector space
V
with respect to a linear map
T:V\,\rightarrow\,V
is a Lie algebra representation
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
such that

T(\theta(x)u)=\theta(D(x))u+\theta(x)T(u)+\theta(D(x))T(u),\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{g},u\in V.

Denote a representation by
(V,T,\theta)
.

It was proved in
[
8
]
that the differentiation of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
is the difference Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},D)
, where
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie algebra of the Lie group
G
and
D=\mathcal{D}_{*e_{G}}
. A difference Lie algebra can also be integrated to a difference Lie group
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.17
.

Let
(V,T,\Theta)
be a representation of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
. Then
(V,T,\theta)
is a representation of the difference Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},D)
, where
\theta=\Theta_{*e_{G}}
.

Proof.

Since
T:V\,\rightarrow\,V
is a linear map, we have
T_{*}=T
. Denote the exponential map of the Lie group
G
by
\exp
. For any
x\in\mathfrak{g},u\in V
, by (
4
), we have

\displaystyle T(\theta(x)u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}T(\Theta(\exp(tx))u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Big(\Theta(\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx))\exp(tx))T(u)+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx))\exp(tx))u-\Theta(\exp(tx))u\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Theta(\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx)))T(u)+\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Theta(\exp(tx))T(u)+\frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0}\Theta(\mathcal{D}(\exp(tx)))u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\theta(D(x))T(u)+\theta(x)T(u)+\theta(D(x))u.

Thus
(V,T,\theta)
is a representation of the difference Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},D)
.
∎

Let
(\mathfrak{g},D)
be a difference Lie algebra and
(V,T,\theta)
be a representation. We denote the integration of
(\mathfrak{g},D)
by
(G,\mathcal{D})
, where
G
is a connected and simply connected Lie group. See
[
11
]
for explicit construction of
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\Theta:G\,\rightarrow\,GL(V)
be the Lie group homomorphism integrating the Lie algebra homomorphism
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\,\rightarrow\,\mathfrak{gl}(V)
. Then we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.18
.

With the above notations, if
(V,T,\theta)
is a representation of a difference Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},D)
, then
(V,T,\Theta)
is a representation of the integrated difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
.

Proof.

Since
\Theta
is already a Lie group homomorphism, we only need to show that (
4
) holds.

Define
D_{+}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
by

D_{+}(x)=x+D(x).

Then
D_{+}
is a Lie algebra homomorphism, and the graph of
D_{+}
, which is denoted by
\mathrm{Gr}(D_{+})=\{(x,D(x)+x)\,|\,\forall x\in\mathfrak{g}\}

is a Lie subalgebra of the direct sum Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\oplus\mathfrak{g}
. Denote by

\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}}(V,T)=\{(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\,|\,\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\mathfrak{gl}(V),\text{and}~~(T+{\rm{Id}})\circ\phi_{1}=\phi_{2}\circ(T+{\rm{Id}})\},

which is a Lie subalgebra of the direct sum Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}(V)\oplus\mathfrak{gl}(V)
. Define
\Lambda:\mathrm{Gr}(D_{+})\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)\oplus\mathfrak{gl}(V)
by

\Lambda(x,D(x)+x)=(\theta(x),\theta(D(x))+\theta(x)),\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{g}.

Since
(V,T,\theta)
is a representation of the difference Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},D)
, it follows that

(\theta(x),\theta(D(x))+\theta(x))\in\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}}(V,T)
, and
\Lambda
is a Lie algebra homomorphism from
\mathrm{Gr}(D_{+})
to
\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}}(V,T)
.

By Lemma
2.8
,
\mathcal{D}_{+}
is a Lie group homomorphism. So the graph of
\mathcal{D}_{+}
, which is denoted by
\mathrm{Gr}(\mathcal{D}_{+})=\{(g,\mathcal{D}(g)g)\,|\,\forall g\in G\},
is a connected and simply connected Lie subgroup of the direct product Lie group
G\times G
. It is straightforward to see that the tangent map of
\mathcal{D}_{+}
at the identity is exactly
D_{+}
, and the Lie algebra of
\mathrm{Gr}(\mathcal{D}_{+})
is
\mathrm{Gr}(D_{+})
. This can be summarized by the following commutative diagram:

\textstyle{G\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathcal{D}_{+}}
\textstyle{G}
\textstyle{\mathfrak{g}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\exp}
\scriptstyle{D_{+}}
\textstyle{\mathfrak{g}.\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\exp}

Denote by

\widetilde{GL}(V,T)=\{(\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2})\,|\,\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}\in GL(V),\text{and}~~(T+{\rm{Id}})\circ\Phi_{1}=\Phi_{2}\circ(T+{\rm{Id}})\},

which is obviously a Lie subgroup of the direct product Lie group
GL(V)\times GL(V)
, whose Lie algebra is
\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}}(V,T)
.

Let
\Xi:\mathrm{Gr}(\mathcal{D}_{+})\,\rightarrow\,\widetilde{GL}(V,T)
be the integration of the Lie algebra homomorphism
\Lambda:\mathrm{Gr}(D_{+})\,\rightarrow\,\widetilde{\mathfrak{gl}}(V,T)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\Xi(\exp(x),\exp(D(x)+x))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathrm{Exp}(\theta(x)),\mathrm{Exp}(\theta(D(x))+\theta(x)))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\Theta(\exp(x)),\Theta(\exp(D(x)+x))),

where
\mathrm{Exp}
is the exponential map from
\mathfrak{gl}(V)
to
GL(V)
.
Since
\exp(D(x)+x)=\mathcal{D}(\exp(x))\exp(x)
, we have

\Xi(\exp(x),\mathcal{D}(\exp(x))\exp(x))=(\Theta(\exp(x)),\Theta(\mathcal{D}(\exp(x))\exp(x))).

Since
\mathrm{Gr}(\mathcal{D}_{+})
is diffeomorphic to
G
and
G
is a connected Lie group, any
g\in G
can be written as products of elements near the identity. Thus it follows that

\Xi(g,\mathcal{D}(g)g)=(\Theta(g),\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g))\in\widetilde{GL}(V,T).

Therefore, we have

(T+{\rm{Id}})\circ\Theta(g)=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g))\circ(T+{\rm{Id}}),

which implies that (
4
) holds, and

\Theta
is a representation of
(G,\mathcal{D})
on
V
with respect to
T
.
∎

3.
Cohomologies of difference Lie groups

In this section, we introduce cohomology theories for difference operators on Lie groups as well as difference Lie groups. The relation between various cohomologies are given by a long exact sequence.

First we recall the normalized cohomology of a Lie group
G
with coefficients in a representation

\Theta:G\to GL(V)
(see e.g.
[
6
]
). A smooth map
\alpha_{n}:\underbrace{G\times\cdots\times G}_{n}\,\rightarrow\,V
is called an
n
-normalized cochain
if
\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})=0
when any one of elements

. Denote the space of

-normalized cochains by

, which is an abelian group. The coboundary operator

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}(\alpha_{n})(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n},g_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(g_{1})\alpha_{n}(g_{2},\cdots,g_{n},g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{i-1},g_{i}g_{i+1},g_{i+2},\cdots g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n+1}\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}).

The corresponding
n
-th cohomology group is denoted by

.

Remark 3.1
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let

be a representation of a difference Lie group

. Define

(5)

\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}(g)u=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)u,\quad\forall g\in G,u\in V.

G
on

.

For all

1
), we have

\displaystyle\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}(gh)u=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(gh)gh)u=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g\mathcal{D}(h)h)u=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)\Theta(\mathcal{D}(h)h)u=\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}(g)\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}(h)u,

which implies that
\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}
is a representation of
G
on
V
.
∎

-normalized cochains

0
. For

, define the space of

-normalized cochains

by

.

3.2.
Cohomologies of difference Lie groups

Let
(V,T,\Theta)
be a representation of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
. Define the space of
1
-cochains
C^{1}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
to be
C^{1}(G,V)
. For
n\geq 2
, we define the space of
n
-cochains
C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
by

C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)=C^{n}(G,V)\oplus\mathfrak{C}^{n}(\mathcal{D},T).

Define the coboundary operator

\delta:C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)\,\rightarrow\,C^{n+1}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)

by

(6)

\delta(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n-1})=(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n},\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\beta_{n-1})+\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n})),

where
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}
and
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}
are the coboundary operators of the Lie group
G
with coefficients in the representation
(V,\Theta)
and
(V,\Theta_{\mathcal{D}})
respectively, and

\mathfrak{K}:C^{n}(G,V)\,\rightarrow\,\mathfrak{C}^{n+1}(\mathcal{D},T)
is defined by
\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n})=\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n})+\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{n})
, where

(7)

\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{n})(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})=\begin{cases}-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}))\alpha_{1}(g_{1})+\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})-\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})),&n=1;\\
\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}),g_{1})-\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}),g_{1}g_{2})+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}),g_{2}),&n=2;\\
0,&n\geq 3,\end{cases}

and

(8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{n})(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Big(\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n})g_{n})-T(\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}))-\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})\Big).

Since
\alpha_{n}
is an
n
-normalized cochain, it follows that
\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n})\in\mathfrak{C}^{n+1}(\mathcal{D},T)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

With the above notations,
(\oplus_{n=1}^{\infty}C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T),\delta)
is a cochain complex, i.e.

\delta\circ\delta=0.

Proof.

For any
(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n-1})\in C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
, by (
6
), we have

\delta\circ\delta(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n-1})=\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n}),\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\beta_{n-1})+\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n})\Big)+\mathfrak{K}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n})\Big).

Since
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}
and
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}
are the coboundary operators of the Lie group
G
with coefficients in the representations
(V,\Theta)
and
(V,\Theta_{\mathcal{D}})
respectively, we get
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}=0
and
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}=0
.
Thus we only need to prove

\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\circ\mathfrak{K}+\mathfrak{K}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}=0.

For
n\geq 1
and
g_{1},\cdots,g_{n+1}\in G
, by (
1
), (
4
) and (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{n}))(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{n})(g_{2},\cdots,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i}\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{n})(g_{1},\cdots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,g_{n+1})+(-1)^{n+1}\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{n})(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2})g_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n+1})g_{n+1})

\displaystyle-(-1)^{n}\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})T(\alpha_{n}(g_{2},\cdots,g_{n+1}))-(-1)^{n}\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{n}(g_{2},\cdots,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+n}\Big(\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{i}g_{i+1})g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n+1})g_{n+1})

\displaystyle-T(\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,g_{n+1}))-\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,g_{n+1})\Big)

\displaystyle-\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n})g_{n})+T(\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}))+\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Big((-1)^{n+1}\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2})g_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n+1})g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{i+n+1}\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{i})g_{i}\mathcal{D}(g_{i+1})g_{i+1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n+1})g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n})g_{n})-\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n+i+1}\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots,g_{n+1})

\displaystyle+(-1)^{n}\Theta(g_{1})\alpha_{n}(g_{2},\cdots,g_{n+1})+(-1)^{n}T(\Theta(g_{1})\alpha_{n}(g_{2},\cdots,\mathfrak{g}_{n+1}))

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(-1)^{n+i}T(\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{i}g_{i+1},\cdots g_{n+1}))-T(\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n}))\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{D}(g_{n+1})g_{n+1})-T(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n+1}))-\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n}(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n+1})\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}(\alpha_{n}))(g_{1},\cdots,g_{n+1}),

which implies that
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\circ\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}+\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}=0
. Since when
n\geq 3
, we have

\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\circ\mathfrak{K}+\mathfrak{K}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}=\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\circ\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}+\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}.

Thus
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\circ\mathfrak{K}+\mathfrak{K}\circ\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}=0
, when
n\geq 3
.

When
n=1
,

\displaystyle\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{1}))+\mathfrak{K}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{1})\Big)(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{1}))+\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{1})\Big)(g_{1},g_{2})+\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{1}))+\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{1})\Big)(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}))\alpha_{1}(g_{2})+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2})g_{2})-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}))

\displaystyle+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}))\alpha_{1}(g_{1}g_{2})-\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2})g_{1}g_{2})+\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}))-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}))\alpha_{1}(g_{1})+\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})

\displaystyle-\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}))+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}))\alpha_{1}(g_{1})-\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})+\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}))-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}))\alpha_{1}(g_{1}g_{2})

\displaystyle+\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2})g_{1}g_{2})-\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}))+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}))\alpha_{1}(g_{2})-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2})g_{2})

\displaystyle+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{1}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}))+\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{1}))(g_{1},g_{2})+\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{1})(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

When
n=2
, since
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{2}\in C^{3}(G,V)
, then
\mathfrak{K}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{2})=\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{2})
. Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{2}))+\mathfrak{K}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{2})\Big)(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2}))(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})+\Big(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}(\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\alpha_{2}))+\widehat{\mathfrak{K}}(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{2})\Big)(g_{1},g_{2},g_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})(g_{2},g_{3})-\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})(g_{1}g_{2},g_{3})+\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})(g_{1},g_{2}g_{3})-\mathfrak{K}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})(g_{1},g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}),g_{2})-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}g_{3}),g_{2}g_{3})

\displaystyle+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{2})g_{2})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{3}),g_{3})-\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}),g_{1}g_{2})+\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}),g_{1}g_{2}g_{3})

\displaystyle-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2})g_{1}g_{2})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{3}),g_{3})+\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}),g_{1})-\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}g_{3}),g_{1}g_{2}g_{3})

\displaystyle+\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}g_{3}),g_{2}g_{3})-\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}),g_{1})

\displaystyle+\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{1}g_{2}),g_{1}g_{2})-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g_{1})g_{1})\alpha_{2}(\mathcal{D}(g_{2}),g_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

Thus for all
n\geq 1
,
\delta\circ\delta=0
, which implies that
(\oplus_{n=1}^{\infty}C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T),\delta)
is a cochain complex.
∎

Definition 3.5
.

The cohomology of the cochain complex
(\oplus_{n=1}^{\infty}C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T),\delta)
is called
the cohomology of the difference Lie group
with coefficients in the representation
(V,T,\Theta)
. The corresponding
n
-th cohomology group is denoted by
\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
.

The relation between various cohomologies are given by the following theorem, which is resemblance of the Mayer-Vietoris sequence.

Theorem 3.6
.

There is a short exact sequence of the cochain complexes:

0\longrightarrow(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathfrak{C}^{n}(\mathcal{D},T),\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathfrak{i}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T),\delta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathfrak{p}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}C^{n}(G,V),\mathrm{d}^{\Theta})\longrightarrow 0,

where
\mathfrak{i}(\beta_{n-1})=(0,\beta_{n-1})
and
\mathfrak{p}(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n-1})=\alpha_{n}
for all
\beta_{n-1}\in\mathfrak{C}^{n}(\mathcal{D},T)
and
\alpha_{n}\in C^{n}(G,V)
.

Consequently,
there is a long exact sequence of the cohomology groups:

\cdots\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{n}(\mathcal{D},T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathfrak{i}_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathfrak{p}_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathfrak{k}^{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{H}^{n+1}(\mathcal{D},T)\longrightarrow\cdots,

where the connecting map
\mathfrak{k}^{n}
is defined by

(9)

\mathfrak{k}^{n}([\alpha_{n}])=[\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n})],\quad\forall[\alpha_{n}]\in\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,V).

Proof.

By (
6
), we have the short exact sequence of cochain complexes which induces a long exact sequence of cohomology groups. Moreover, if
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n}=0
, then we can chose
(\alpha_{n},0)\in C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
such that
\mathfrak{p}(\alpha_{n},0)=\alpha_{n}
. Since
\delta(\alpha_{n},0)=(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha_{n},\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n}))
, it follows that
\mathfrak{k}^{n}([\alpha_{n}])=[\mathfrak{K}(\alpha_{n})]
.
∎

4.
The van Est theorem for cohomologies of difference Lie groups

In this section, we establish the van Est theorem for cohomologies of difference Lie groups and cohomologies of difference Lie algebras, which can viewed as a justification of our
cohomology theory for difference Lie groups.

4.1.
Cohomologies of difference Lie algebras

Let
(\mathfrak{g},D)
be a difference Lie algebra. A cohomology theory of difference Lie algebras was introduced in
[
11
]
as follows. Let
(V,T,\theta)
be a representation of
(\mathfrak{g},D)
. Define the space of
1
-cochains
C^{1}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)
to be
\mathrm{Hom}(\mathfrak{g},V)
. For
n\geq 2
, define the space of
n
-cochains
C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)
by

C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)=C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)\oplus\mathfrak{C}^{n}(D,T),

where
C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)=\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n}\mathfrak{g},V)
, and
\mathfrak{C}^{1}(D,T)=0,\mathfrak{C}^{n}(D,T)=\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1}\mathfrak{g},V)
for
n\geq 2
.

Define the coboundary operator

\delta_{\theta}:C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)\,\rightarrow\,C^{n+1}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)

by

(10)

\delta_{\theta}(\zeta_{n},\xi_{n-1})=(\mathrm{d}^{\theta}\zeta_{n},\mathrm{d}^{\theta_{D}}\xi_{n-1}+K(\zeta_{n})),

for all
\zeta_{n}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n}\mathfrak{g},V),\xi_{n-1}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1}\mathfrak{g},V)
,
where
\mathrm{d}^{\theta}
and
\mathrm{d}^{\theta_{D}}
are the Chevalley-Eilenberg coboundary operators of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
with coefficients in the representation
(V,\theta)
and
(V,\theta_{D})
respectively. Here
\theta_{D}:\mathfrak{g}\,\rightarrow\,\mathfrak{gl}(V)
is the representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
V
defined by

\theta_{D}(x)u=\theta(x)u+\theta(D(x))u,

and
K:\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n}\mathfrak{g},V)\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n}\mathfrak{g},V)
is defined by

\displaystyle K(\zeta_{n})(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n}\zeta_{n}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{i-1},D(x_{i_{1}}),\cdots,D(x_{i_{k}}),\cdots,x_{n})-T(\zeta_{n}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}))\Big).

The corresponding
n
-th cohomology group is denoted by
H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)
. Moreover, we denote
n
-th cohomology groups of the cochain complexes
(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V),\mathrm{d}^{\theta})
and
(\oplus_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathfrak{C}^{n}(D,T),\mathrm{d}^{\theta_{D}})
by
H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)
and
H^{n}(D,T)
respectively. Then there is the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

With the above notations, there is a short exact sequence of the cochain complexes:

0\longrightarrow(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathfrak{C}^{n}(D,T),\mathrm{d}^{\theta_{D}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\iota}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T),\delta_{\theta})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\oplus_{n=1}^{+\infty}C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V),\mathrm{d}^{\theta})\longrightarrow 0,

where
\iota(\xi_{n-1})=(0,\xi_{n-1})
and
p(\zeta_{n},\xi_{n-1})=\zeta_{n}
for all
\xi_{n-1}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1}\mathfrak{g},V)
and
\zeta_{n}\in\mathrm{Hom}(\wedge^{n}\mathfrak{g},V)
.

Consequently,
there is a long exact sequence of the cohomology groups:

\cdots\longrightarrow H^{n}(D,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\iota_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p_{*}}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k^{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}H^{n+1}(D,T)\longrightarrow\cdots,

where the connecting map
k^{n}
is defined by

(11)

k^{n}([\zeta_{n}])=[K(\zeta_{n})],\quad\forall[\zeta_{n}]\in H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V).

Proof.

By (
10
), we have the short exact sequence of cochain complexes which induces a long exact sequence of cohomology groups. Moreover, if
\mathrm{d}^{\theta}\zeta_{n}=0
, then we can chose
(\zeta_{n},0)\in C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)
such that
p(\zeta_{n},0)=0
, which implies that
\delta_{\theta}(\zeta_{n},0)=(\mathrm{d}^{\theta}\zeta_{n},K(\zeta_{n}))
. Thus
k^{n}([\zeta_{n}])=[K(\zeta_{n})]
.
∎

4.2.
The van Est theorem

Let
G
be a Lie group and
\mathfrak{g}
its Lie algebra. Let

\Theta:G\to GL(V)
be a representation of
G
and
\theta:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{gl}(V)
the induced representation of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Define

\mathrm{VE}_{n}:C^{n}(G,V)\,\rightarrow\,C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)

by

\displaystyle\mathrm{VE}_{n}(\alpha_{n})(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\epsilon\in S_{n}}(-1)^{|\epsilon|}\frac{d}{dt_{\epsilon(1)}}\cdots\frac{d}{dt_{\epsilon(n)}}\bigg|_{t_{\epsilon(1)}=\cdots=t_{\epsilon(n)}=0}\alpha_{n}\Big(\exp(t_{\epsilon(1)}x_{\epsilon(1)}),\cdots,\exp(t_{\epsilon(n)}x_{\epsilon(n)})\Big),\quad\forall x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\in\mathfrak{g}.

From the classical argument for the cohomologies of Lie groups and Lie algebras,

\mathrm{VE}:\oplus_{n=1}^{\infty}C^{n}(G,V)\,\rightarrow\,\oplus_{n=1}^{\infty}C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)

is a cochain map, which induces homomorphisms
{\mathrm{VE}_{n}}_{*}
from the cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{n}(G,V)
to
H^{n}(\mathfrak{g},V)
. Moreover, under certain conditions, the cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{k}(G,V)
and
H^{k}(\mathfrak{g},V)
are isomorphic.

Theorem 4.2
.

(
[
27
]
)
Let
G
be a connected Lie group and its homotopy groups are trivial in
1,\cdots,n
, then for all
1\leq i\leq n
, the cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{i}(G,V)
is isomorphic to the cohomology group
H^{i}(\mathfrak{g},V)
.

Let
(G,\mathcal{D})
be a difference Lie group and
(\mathfrak{g},D)
be the corresponding difference Lie algebra. Let
(V,T,\Theta)
be a representation of
(G,\mathcal{D})
. By Theorem
2.17
,
(V,T,\theta)
is a representation of
(\mathfrak{g},D)
. By Theorem
3.2
,
\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}
defined by
\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}(g)u=\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g)g)u
is a representation of
G
on
V
. Moreover, we have

(\Theta_{\mathcal{D}})_{*e_{G}}(x)u=\theta(D(x))u+\theta(x)u=\theta_{D}(x)u,\quad\forall x\in\mathfrak{g},u\in V,

which is a representation of
\mathfrak{g}
on
V
.

Define
\widetilde{\mathrm{VE}}_{n}:C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)\,\rightarrow\,C^{n}(\mathfrak{g},D,V,T)
by

\widetilde{\mathrm{VE}}_{n}(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n-1})=(\mathrm{VE}_{n}(\alpha_{n}),\mathrm{VE}_{n-1}(\beta_{n-1})),\quad\forall(\alpha_{n},\beta_{n-1})\in C^{n}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T).

Then we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3

With the above notations,

for
\eta\in C^{1}(G,V)
. Then we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(g,h)-\alpha(g,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(g)\cdot_{\Pi}s^{\prime}(h)\cdot_{\Pi}(s^{\prime}(g\cdot_{G}h))^{-1}-s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}s(h)\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g\cdot_{G}h))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(g)\cdot_{\Pi}s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}\eta(h)\cdot_{\Pi}s(h)\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g\cdot_{G}h))^{-1}\cdot_{\Pi}(\eta(g\cdot_{G}h))^{-1}

\displaystyle-s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}s(h)\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g\cdot_{G}h))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(g)+s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}\eta(h)\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g))^{-1}+\cancel{(s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}s(h))\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g\cdot_{\Pi}h))^{-1}}

\displaystyle-\eta(g\cdot_{G}h)-\cancel{s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}s(h)\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g\cdot_{G}h))^{-1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta(g)+\Theta(g)\eta(h)-\eta(g\cdot_{G}h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\eta(g,h),

\displaystyle\beta^{\prime}(g)-\beta(g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}(s^{\prime}(g))\cdot_{\Pi}(s^{\prime}(\mathcal{D}(g)))^{-1}-\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}(s(g))\cdot_{\Pi}(s(\mathcal{D}(g)))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}(\eta(g))\cdot_{\Pi}\eta(g)\cdot_{\Pi}\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}(s(g))\cdot_{\Pi}(\eta(g))^{-1}\cdot_{\Pi}(s(\mathcal{D}(g))^{-1}(\eta(\mathcal{D}(g)))^{-1}

\displaystyle-\mathcal{D}_{\Pi}(s(g))\cdot_{\Pi}(s(\mathcal{D}(g)))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T(\eta(g))+\eta(g)-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))\eta(g)-\eta(\mathcal{D}(g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathfrak{K}(\eta)(g),

which implies that
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})-(\alpha,\beta)=\delta(\eta)
. Thus,
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
and
(\alpha,\beta)
are in the same cohomology class.
∎

Definition 5.5
.

Let
(\hat{\Pi},\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\hat{\Pi}})
and
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
be two abelian extensions of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
by
(V,T)
. They are said to be
isomorphic
if there exists an isomorphism
\sigma:\Pi\,\rightarrow\,\hat{\Pi}
of difference Lie groups such that
\sigma\circ i=\hat{i}
and
\hat{p}\circ\sigma=p
.

Theorem 5.6
.

For a given representation
(V,T,\Theta)
of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
, abelian extensions of
(G,\mathcal{D})
by
(V,T)
relative to the representation
(V,T,\Theta)
are classified by the second cohomology group
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
.

Proof.

Let
(\hat{\Pi},\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\hat{\Pi}})
and
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
be two isomorphic abelian extensions via an isomorphism
\sigma
. Assume that
s
is a section of
(\Pi,\mathcal{D})
. Define
s^{\prime}
by

s^{\prime}=\sigma\circ s.

Then, it is obvious that
s^{\prime}
is a section of
(\hat{\Pi},\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\hat{\Pi}})
.
Note that any two isomorphic abelian extensions
(\hat{\Pi},\hat{\mathcal{D}}_{\hat{\Pi}})
and
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
of
(G,\mathcal{D})
by
(V,T)
are relative to the same representation
(V,T,\Theta)
of
(G,\mathcal{D})
. Actually, for any
g\in G,\,u\in V
, we have

\displaystyle s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}u\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(s(g)\cdot_{\Pi}u\cdot_{\Pi}(s(g))^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(s(g))\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}\sigma(u)\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}(\sigma(s(g)))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(g)\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}u\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}(s^{\prime}(g))^{-1},

which implies that the representation
(V,T,\Theta)
given in Proposition
5.3
are the same.

Denote by
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
the corresponding
2
-cocycle given in Theorem
5.4
respectively. Then we have

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}(g,h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle s^{\prime}(g)\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}s^{\prime}(h)\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}(s^{\prime}(g\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}h))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(s(g))\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}\sigma(s(h))\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}(\sigma(s(g\cdot_{\hat{\Pi}}h)))^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(\alpha(g,h))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(g,h).

Similarly, we have
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
. By Theorem
5.4
, isomorphic abelian extensions give rise to the same cohomological class in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
.

Conversely, given a
2
-cocycle
(\alpha,\beta)
, we define a group multiplication
\cdot_{\alpha}
on
G\times V
by

(g,u)\cdot_{\alpha}(h,v)=(g\cdot_{G}h,u+\Theta(g)v+\alpha(g,h)),\quad\forall g,h\in G,u,v\in V.

By
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\alpha=0
, it is straightforward to deduce that
(G\times V,\cdot_{\alpha})
is a Lie group. Define a smooth map
\mathcal{D}_{\beta}:G\times V\,\rightarrow\,G\times V
by

(14)

\mathcal{D}_{\beta}(g,u)=(\mathcal{D}(g),T(u)+u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u+\beta(g)),\quad\forall g\in G,u\in V.

Since
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta_{\mathcal{D}}}\beta+\mathfrak{K}(\alpha)=0
, it is straightforward to deduce that
\mathcal{D}_{\beta}
is a difference operator on the difference Lie group
(G\times V,\cdot_{\alpha})
. Thus
(G\times V,\cdot_{\alpha},\mathcal{D}_{\beta})
is a difference Lie group, which is an abelian extension of
(G,\mathcal{D})
by
(V,T)
.

Choose another
2
-cocycle
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
, such that
(\alpha,\beta)
and
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
are in the same cohomology class, i.e.

(\alpha-\alpha^{\prime},\beta-\beta^{\prime})=\delta(\eta)=(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\eta,\mathfrak{K}(\eta)),

where
\eta\in C^{1}(G,V)
, and denote the corresponding difference Lie group by
(G\times V,\cdot_{\alpha^{\prime}},\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}})
. Define a smooth map
\sigma:G\times V\,\rightarrow\,G\times V
by

\sigma(g,u)=(g,u+\eta(g)),

for all
g\in G,u\in V
. Since

\displaystyle\sigma((g,u)\cdot_{\alpha}(h,v))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(g\cdot_{G}h,u+\Theta(g)v+\alpha(g,h))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g\cdot_{G}h,u+\Theta(g)v+\alpha(g,h)+\eta(g\cdot_{G}h))

and

\displaystyle\sigma((g,u))\cdot_{\alpha^{\prime}}\sigma((h,v))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g,u+\eta(g))\cdot_{\alpha^{\prime}}(h,v+\eta(h))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(g\cdot_{G}h,u+\eta(g)+\Theta(g)v+\Theta(g)\eta(h)+\alpha^{\prime}(g,h)),

we deduce that
\sigma
is a Lie group isomorphism. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}}(\sigma(g,u))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}}(g,u+\eta(g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}(g),T(u)+T(\eta(g))+u+\eta(g)-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))\eta(g)+\beta^{\prime}(g)),

and

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{D}_{\beta}(g,u))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{D}(g),T(u)+u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u+\beta(g))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}(g),T(u)+u-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))u+\beta(g)+\eta(\mathcal{D}(g))).

Since
\beta(g)-\beta^{\prime}(g)=T(\eta(g))+\eta(g)-\Theta(\mathcal{D}(g))\eta(g)-\eta(\mathcal{D}(g))
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}}\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ\mathcal{D}_{\beta}
. Therefore,
\sigma
is a difference Lie group isomorphism. Then it is straightforward to deduce that the two abelian extensions are isomorphic.
∎

Note that for any
abelian extension
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
of
(G,\mathcal{D})
by
(V,T)
relative to the representation
(V,T,\Theta)
, the Lie group
\Pi
is isomorphic to the semidirect product Lie group
G\ltimes_{\Theta}V
, if and only if there exists a section
s
of
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
being a group homomorphism from
G
to
\Pi
. Indeed, for the
2
-cocycle
(\alpha,\beta)
obtained by such a section
s
, we clearly have
\alpha=0
by Eq. (
12
).

In this situation, we have the following result as a byproduct of Theorem
3.6
and Theorem
5.6
.

Corollary 5.7
.

For a given representation
(V,T,\Theta)
of a difference Lie group
(G,\mathcal{D})
,
the difference operators on the semidirect product Lie group
G\ltimes_{\Theta}V
are classified by the quotient
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{D},T)/\mathfrak{k}^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,V))
, where
\mathfrak{k}^{1}
is given by (
9
).

Proof.

Let
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\beta})
and
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}})
be two abelian extensions associated with 2-cocycles
(0,\beta)
and
(0,\beta^{\prime})
respectively in
C^{2}(G,\mathcal{D},V,T)
, where
\mathcal{D}_{\beta}
is given by (
14
). It is obvious that
\beta
and
\beta^{\prime}
are 2-cocycles in
\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\mathcal{D},T)
. By Theorem
5.6
, if
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\ltimes})
and
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{\ltimes})
are isomorphic, then
(0,\beta)
and
(0,\beta^{\prime})
are in the same cohomology class, i.e.

(0,\beta-\beta^{\prime})=\delta(\eta)=(\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\eta,\mathfrak{K}(\eta))

for
\eta\in C^{1}(G,V)
. Therefore,
\mathrm{d}^{\Theta}\eta=0
and
\beta-\beta^{\prime}=\mathfrak{K}(\eta)
. This means that
[\beta]=[\beta^{\prime}]
in
\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{D},T)/\mathfrak{k}^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,V))
.

Conversely, for any 2-cocycle
\beta
in
\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\mathcal{D},T)
, define
\mathcal{D}_{\beta}
by (
14
). Then
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\beta})
is a difference Lie group. Let
\beta^{\prime}
be another 2-cocycle such that

[\beta]-[\beta^{\prime}]=[\mathfrak{K}(\eta)]=\mathfrak{k}^{1}([\eta]),

for some 1-cocycle
\eta
in
C^{1}(G,V)
. Denote by
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}})
the corresponding difference Lie group. Then the smooth map
\sigma:G\times V\,\rightarrow\,G\times V
defined by

\sigma(g,u)=(g,u+\eta(g))

gives rise to an isomorphism from the difference Lie group
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\beta})
to the difference Lie group
(G\ltimes_{\Theta}V,\mathcal{D}_{\beta^{\prime}})
.

As a result, the difference operators on
G\ltimes_{\Theta}V
are classified by

\mathcal{H}^{2}(\mathcal{D},T)/\mathfrak{k}^{1}(\mathcal{H}^{1}(G,V))
.
∎

Further, if there exists a section
s
of
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
being a difference Lie group homomorphism from
(G,\mathcal{D})
to
(\Pi,\mathcal{D}_{\Pi})
, then the corresponding
2
-cocycle
(\alpha,\beta)=0
by Eqs. (

), (
13
), and

.
