Classical certification of quantum gates under the dimension assumption
Jan Nöller, Nikolai Miklin, Martin Kliesch, Mariami Gachechiladze

Abstract
We develop an efficient method for certifying single-qubit quantum gates in a black-box scenario, assuming only the dimension of the quantum system is known. The method is based on testing the outcomes of exact quantum computations on a single qubit, with no auxiliary systems required. We prove that the method’s sample complexity grows as O ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) \LandauO(\varepsilon^{-1}) with respect to the average gate infidelity ε \varepsilon for the certification of a relevant single-qubit gate, which experimentally corresponds to a π / 2 \pi/2 -pulse. Furthermore, we show that the proposed method can be used to certify a gate set universal for single-qubit quantum computation. Our approach takes a first step in bridging the gap between strong notions of certification from self-testing and practically highly relevant approaches from quantum system characterization.

Introduction

One of the most fundamental operations on a quantum computer is the preparation of an equal superposition of the basis states of a qubit.
In textbooks, this operation is achieved by applying the Hadamard gate to a qubit’s ground state.
However, in practice, it is often more natural to perform a partial qubit flip, which experimentally corresponds to a
\pi/2
-pulse
[
Leibfried et al.(2003)Leibfried, Blatt, Monroe, and Wineland
]
.
Testing the successful preparation of a superposition state of a qubit is one of the simplest experiments in which one can observe the quantum nature of the system.
For this reason, such tests are frequently used to calibrate quantum devices
[
Leibfried et al.(2003)Leibfried, Blatt, Monroe, and Wineland
]
.
Here, we demonstrate that this simple calibration task can be developed into a rigorous certification protocol for single-qubit quantum operations, which notably does not require the estimation of any transition probabilities.

The problem of certifying the correct functioning of quantum devices is crucial for developing quantum hardware and has naturally evolved into a field of study known as quantum system characterization (see Refs.
[
Eisert et al.(2020)Eisert, Hangleiter, Walk,
Roth, Markham, Parekh,
Chabaud, and Kashefi
,
Kliesch and Roth(2021)
]
for reviews).
Particularly challenging is assessing the quality of quantum gates due to unavoidable
SPAM
(
SPAM
) errors.
They are a limiting factor in standard quantum process tomography
[
Chuang and Nielsen(1997)
,
Mohseni et al.(2008)Mohseni, Rezakhani, and Lidar
]
and direct certification methods
[
Liu et al.(2020)Liu,
Shang, Yu, and Zhang
,
Zhu and Zhang(2020)
,
Zeng et al.(2020)Zeng,
Zhou, and Liu
]
.

Two broad families of characterization methods have been developed to address this challenge:
GST
(

)
[
Merkel et al.(2013)Merkel, Gambetta, Smolin,
Poletto, Córcoles, Johnson, Ryan, and Steffen
,
Blume-Kohout et al.()Blume-Kohout, King Gamble, Nielsen,
Mizrahi, Sterk, and Maunz
,
Brieger et al.(2023)Brieger, Roth, and Kliesch
]
and
RB
(

)
[
Emerson et al.(2005)Emerson, Alicki, and Życzkowski
,
Lévi et al.(2007)Lévi, López, Emerson, and Cory
,
Dankert et al.(2009)Dankert, Cleve, Emerson, and Livine
,
Emerson et al.(2007)Emerson, Silva, Moussa, Ryan, Laforest, Baugh, Cory, and Laflamme
,
Knill et al.(2008)Knill, Leibfried, Reichle,

,
Magesan et al.(2012)Magesan, Gambetta, and Emerson
,
Helsen et al.(2022)Helsen, Roth, Onorati, Werner, and Eisert
]
with its many variants
(see Refs.
[
Helsen et al.(2022)Helsen, Roth, Onorati, Werner, and Eisert

Both types of protocols can be used to estimate gate errors in a
SPAM
-robust manner by executing sequences of gates with varying lengths.
However, neither of these methods is suitable for certification, as they cannot definitively rule out the implementation of incorrect gates.

GST
provides a set of compatible descriptions of the underlying experiment, some of which may not be connected to the target quantum model by any physical gauge.

RB
, in turn, already requires that the implemented gates are close to the target ones in order to interpret the output decay parameters
[
Helsen et al.(2022)Helsen, Roth, Onorati, Werner, and Eisert
]
.

Currently, there is no method in the quantum characterization literature that is both resilient to
SPAM
errors and is capable of certifying
uniqueness
of the quantum model compatible with the observed statistics.
Such a strong notion of certification is known as
self-testing

[
Šupić and Bowles(2020)
]
, a concept first introduced for the certification of entangled states and incompatible measurements in the Bell experiment
[
Mayers and Yao(2004)
]
.
However, achieving this strong notion of assumption-free certification necessitates two space-like separated quantum devices
[
Magniez et al.(2006)Magniez, Mayers, Mosca, and Ollivier
,
Sekatski et al.(2018)Sekatski, Bancal, Wagner, and Sangouard
]
.
This requirement is experimentally challenging and is misaligned with the practically motivated setup with a quantum server and classical users, which is inherent to foreseeable quantum computation.

An alternative avenue of research
[
Mahadev(2018)
,
Metger and Vidick(2021)
]
, designed for certifying a single quantum computer in a black-box scenario, relies on computational assumptions, providing the potential for classical certification of quantum computers.
However, this method is currently out of reach for quantum hardware due to its demanding requirements on the number of qubits and the fidelity of implemented gates
[
Stricker et al.(2022)Stricker, Carrasco, Ringbauer,
Postler, Meth, Edmunds,
Schindler, Blatt, Zoller,
Kraus, and Monz
]
.
Recently, a related question of learnability of a quantum model was raised in Ref.
[
Huang et al.(2022)Huang,
Flammia, and Preskill
]
.
There, the authors proposed a framework to investigate the learnability of the intrinsic descriptions of quantum experiments – the quantum model – without relying on any prior knowledge.
However, the general result obtained there relies on more assumptions than are desirable for a certification task.

In this work, our objective is to achieve
SPAM

This investigation is particularly relevant in a practical server-user configuration (see

Since achieving this goal without any assumptions is impossible, we adopt the commonly used assumption on the quantum system’s dimension
[
Mohan et al.(2019)Mohan,
Tavakoli, and Brunner
,
Miklin et al.(2020)Miklin,
Borkała, and Pawłowski
,

Since we assume that no part of the quantum apparatus, such as the measurement device, is characterized prior to the test, certification is only possible up to the degrees of freedom inherent to quantum mechanics, i.e., unitary or anti-unitary transformation (a unitary and the complex conjugation)
[
Wigner(1931)
]
.
It is important to note that this is the absolute minimum degree of freedom that cannot be excluded in black-box tests, as it corresponds to the simultaneous change of bases.

Our method is based on a very intuitive idea of testing outcomes of exact quantum computation for quantum gate sequences that can be resolved efficiently classically.
Examples include gate sequences that compose into the identity gate or simply a zero-length sequence, for which the system is measured directly after the state preparation.
Certification is then enabled by invoking the dimension assumption, in our case one qubit, which limits the probability that a noisy quantum computer will produce the correct result.
For certification of quantum gates, only the input-output correlations are used and no entanglement with an auxiliary system is required.
Here, we prove that a universal gate set for single qubit quantum operations can be certified within our framework, and analyze in detail the certification of a relevant single-qubit gate, which corresponds to the application a
\pi/2
-pulse.
The latter is routinely implemented on a quantum computer for creating an equal superposition of qubit basis states
[
Leibfried et al.(2003)Leibfried, Blatt, Monroe, and Wineland
]
.
We show that the required
sample complexity
, measured in terms of the number of individual runs of the experiment or the number of tested system copies
[
Kliesch and Roth(2021)
]
, grows as
\LandauO(\varepsilon^{-1})
with respect to the average gate infidelity
\varepsilon
.
This favorable scaling sets our work apart from self-testing results, which typically require much higher experimental effort.

A quantum system is prepared in some initial state, after which a sequence of quantum gates is applied to it, and it is finally measured in some fixed basis.
For each gate in a given sequence, a label, chosen from some finite set

The certification protocol relies on a particular finite subset of sequences

In a single repetition of the protocol, a random string

The certification protocol decides to proceed or abort, depending on the deterministic outcome

The length

Given our emphasis on the minimal nature of the assumptions in our method, we explicitly enumerate them below.

(i)

The dimension assumption.

We assume that the underlying quantum model is mathematically described by a density operator
\rho
, a set of
CPTP
(
CPTP
) maps
(\Lambda_{x})_{x\in X}
, and a
POVM
(
POVM
)
(M_{a})_{a\in A}
, all defined over a Hilbert space of a specified dimension.

(ii)

Context independence.

We assume that in each repetition of our protocol, for each label
x
the quantum computer implements the corresponding quantum channel
\Lambda_{x}
in the same way, irrespective of the sequence
\mathbf{x}
in which the gate
x
appears or the order in which it appears in this sequence.
In the certification literature, this assumption would be referred to as existence of a single-shot implementation function.

(iii)

Independence of repetitions.

We assume that quantum models in different repetitions of the protocol are independent.
Note, that we do not need the assumption that quantum models in different repetitions of the protocol are identical.

Other minor assumptions include error-free functioning of the classical part of the quantum computer, such as control circuits, and our ability to randomly select the gate sequences.
Importantly, our protocol is robust against deviations from the target distribution
\mu(\mathbf{x})
according to which we sample the random sequences
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathcal{X}
.

We call the above set of assumptions
minimal
, because if we lift any of them, the results of the paper can no longer be obtained.
The dimension assumption is critical, because a single quantum system, with which a classical user is interacting, can be simulated by a classical system of a larger dimension
[
Miklin and Oszmaniec(2021)
,
Miklin et al.(2020)Miklin,
Borkała, and Pawłowski
]
.
The assumption of context independence cannot be removed, because for each gate sequence one can always assign a
POVM
that reproduces the required statistics, and a quantum computer can simply perform this measurement on the preparation state, ignoring the structure of the gate sequence.
Finally, the independence assumption can be regarded as a part of the dimension assumption.
Indeed, without this assumption, there is always a possibility of a global unitary gauge applied to several copies of state, measurements, and quantum gates in a way that makes these objects entangled across the protocol repetitions, but does not change the observed statistics.

From

It is possible to modify

by including sequences such as, e.g.,

However, this means that the target statistics will stop being deterministic, and we will need to estimate the corresponding outcome probabilities up to some precision.
At the same time, it does not mean that the overall sampling complexity should change drastically, because, at least in the ideal case, we will only need to distinguish between the two cases

\lvert\langle+|\mathrm{Z}\mathrm{T}|+\rangle\rvert^{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}

4
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we propose an efficient method for classical certification of single-qubit quantum gates under the dimension assumption.
For a relevant single-qubit gate, the
\mathrm{S}
gate, we show that the sample complexity of our method scales like
\LandauO(\varepsilon^{-1})
with respect to the average gate infidelity
\varepsilon
.
To prove this result, we derive a robust self-testing argument for the
\mathrm{S}
gate and its inverse, as well as the initially prepared state and the measurement.
This setup captures many single qubit experiments with
\pi/2
-pulse gates.
Moreover, we show that with our protocol we can certify an implementation of a gate set that is universal for single-qubit quantum computation.

In a bigger spectrum of quantum device characterization, with the proposed method, we aim to bridge the gap between a theoretical abstraction of self-testing a quantum computer in the Bell test and the assumptions-heavy certification methods that are used in practice.
Self-testing in the Bell test takes an idealistic approach, assuming only space-like separation, an assumption that is hard to ensure within a single quantum processor.
In contrast, the certification tools used in practice necessitate a number of assumptions, with the dimension assumption being the least of them, in order to provide guarantees on the protocols’ output.
We argue that the proposed certification method is not intended to replace either idealistic or pragmatic approaches but offers an intermediary perspective that appears to be lacking currently.

Our preliminary results suggest that the proposed certification method can be extended to multi-qubit quantum computation.
However, this requires new techniques for the case of entangling gates, and, thus, deserves a separate study.
In particular, it is interesting to see if the representation theory techniques, commonly used in the
RB
literature, can be employed here.
It also seems possible to translate some of the ideas from Ref.
[
van Dam et al.(2000)van
Dam, Magniez, Mosca, and Santha
]
to the framework of the dimension assumption, removing the requirement on the ideal preparation of the computational basis states, assumed therein.
Finally, we find the connection between the classical simulability of quantum computation and the types of quantum gates which can be efficiency certified with deterministic measurement outcomes intriguing, which also deserves a separate investigation.

From

From now on, we express the bounds using the Big-O notation, because we are interested in the scaling w.r.t.
\varepsilon
, and we estimate the constants in our numerical studies in
Section

.
Using the expansion in
Eq.

36
, we can reduce the condition in

\Tr[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})\psi]+2\operatorname{Re}\left[d_{3}\Tr[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi^{\perp}|]\right]\geq 1-\LandauO(\varepsilon).

\LandauO(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
on the second term in

, but instead carefully analyze both terms.
We use the expansion of
\phi^{\perp}
in the basis of

in the basis

\displaystyle[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})]_{\mathrm{ONB}_{2}}

\displaystyle=2\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}&-c_{3}\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\\

\displaystyle+2\begin{bmatrix}a_{1}\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}-2\operatorname{Re}[b_{2}]\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert&b_{1}^{\ast}(\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}-\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2})-a_{3}^{\ast}\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\\

The first summand in the above expression can be safely ignored, because its contribution is of the order of

On the other hand, the matrix representations of

-\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}&-\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\end{bmatrix}.\end{split}

(40)

Using
Eq.

39
and

as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})\psi]&\leq\LandauO(\varepsilon)+2(a_{1}+a_{2}+2\operatorname{Re}[a_{3}])\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}\\

Similarly, the second term on the left-hand side of

37

\displaystyle\begin{split}\operatorname{Re}\left[d_{3}\Tr[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi^{\perp}|]\right]\leq\LandauO(\varepsilon)+2\operatorname{Re}\Big[d_{3}\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\Big((a_{1}+a_{3}^{\ast})\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}-(a_{2}+a_{3})\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}\\

To simplify the estimates of the quantities in

41
and
Eq.

42
, we introduce the last bit of notation, namely two functions

and

\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}=\frac{1-f(\varepsilon)}{2},\quad\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}=\frac{1+f(\varepsilon)}{2},\quad\operatorname{Re}[a_{3}]=\frac{1-g(\varepsilon)}{2}.

(43)

Note, that even though we use
\varepsilon
as the argument for functions

and

In particular, we can take

, because from
Eq.

29
, we know that
\lvert a_{3}\rvert\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Due to the same reason, we can upper-bound the absolute value of the imaginary part of
a_{3}

\lvert\operatorname{Im}[a_{3}]\rvert\leq\sqrt{\frac{g(\varepsilon)}{2}}
.

Using the new notations in
Eq.

43

\Tr[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})\psi]\leq\frac{1}{2}(1-f(\varepsilon)^{2})(2-g(\varepsilon))+\sqrt{1-f(\varepsilon)^{2}}\sqrt{g(\varepsilon)}\sqrt{2\varepsilon}+2\lvert f(\varepsilon)\rvert\sqrt{\varepsilon}+\LandauO(\varepsilon).

(44)

Similarly, the bound in

\operatorname{Re}\left[d_{3}\Tr[\Lambda^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{s}}(\phi^{\perp})|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi^{\perp}|]\right]\leq\LandauO(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\sqrt{1-f(\varepsilon)^{2}}\left(\lvert f(\varepsilon)\rvert+\sqrt{g(\varepsilon)}\right)+\LandauO(\varepsilon).

(45)

Combining these two bounds together and inserting them back to the condition in
Eq.

37
, we finally arrive at

\left(\lvert f(\varepsilon)\rvert-\LandauO(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{1-f(\varepsilon)^{2}}\sqrt{g(\varepsilon)}-\LandauO(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}\leq\LandauO(\varepsilon).

(46)

This allows us to deduce that
\lvert f(\varepsilon)\rvert\leq\LandauO(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
and
g(\varepsilon)\leq\LandauO(\varepsilon)
.

As the final part of the proof, we choose the gauge unitary
U
to be

U=|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}+|-\mathrm{i}|\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}-|.

(47)

Up to this gauge, the ideal gate
\mathrm{S}
takes the form

U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\psi+\psi^{\perp}+|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi^{\perp}|-|\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|\right).

(48)

Consequently, we find the Choi state vector
|\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})\rangle
, which we define as
|\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})|\coloneqq\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})
,

\displaystyle|\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger}\otimes\mathds{1}\left(|0\rangle|0\rangle+|1\rangle|1\rangle\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}}{2}\Big((\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert+\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert)(|\psi\rangle|\phi\rangle^{\ast}+|\psi^{\perp}\rangle|\phi^{\perp}\rangle^{\ast})+(\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert-\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert)(|\psi\rangle|\phi^{\perp}\rangle^{\ast}-|\psi^{\perp}\rangle|\phi\rangle^{\ast})\Big).

Its matrix representation in the
\mathrm{ONB}_{1}
is

\left[|\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})\rangle\right]_{\mathrm{ONB}_{1}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4}}}{2}\begin{bmatrix}\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert+\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\\
\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert+\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\\
\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert-\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert\\
-(\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert-\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert)\end{bmatrix}.

(50)

Having the explicit forms of the Choi states in

and
Eq.

allows us to estimate their inner product,

\displaystyle\tr\left[\Choi(\Lambda_{\mathrm{s}})\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Big((\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert+\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert)^{2}(a_{1}+a_{2}+2\operatorname{Re}[a_{3}])

(51)

\displaystyle+(\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}-\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert^{2})(2\operatorname{Re}[2b_{1}-b_{2}+b_{3})\Big)+\LandauO(\varepsilon)

(52)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\Big((1+\sqrt{1-f(\varepsilon)^{2}})(1-\varepsilon+1-g(\varepsilon))-\lvert f(\varepsilon)\rvert\LandauO(\sqrt{\varepsilon})\Big)+\LandauO(\varepsilon).

(53)

Inserting the bounds

and

leads to the lower bound of

on the inner product of the Choi states of

and the target unitary channel with the unitary operator

This inner product is sometimes referred to as the entanglement fidelity

]

\mathrm{F}_{\mathrm{avg}}(\Lambda_{\mathrm{s}},U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})=\frac{2}{3}\Tr\left[\Choi(\Lambda_{\mathrm{s}})\Choi(U\mathrm{S}U^{\dagger})\right]+\frac{1}{3}.

(54)

21
.

The proof for channel

follows exactly the same steps as for channel
\Lambda_{\mathrm{s}}
.
It is important, however, that the lower bound on

has the leading terms in the block

rather than the block

, if we look at

-(\lvert\langle\psi\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert+\lvert\langle\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt|\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi\rangle\rvert)\\

again with the leading terms in the lower half of the vector.

Lemma 6
.

For a
PSD
matrix
\Gamma=\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
B^{\dagger}&C\end{bmatrix}
, with
A,C,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
it holds that

\lvert\langle v|B|w\rangle\rvert^{2}\leq\langle v|A|v\rangle\langle w|C|w\rangle,\quad\forall|v\rangle,|w\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

(56)

Proof.

Let
K\coloneqq|0\rangle|v\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle|w\rangle\langle 1|\in\mathbb{C}^{2n\times 2}
.
We can write
\Gamma=|0\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}0|\otimes A+|0\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}1|\otimes B+|1\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}0|\otimes B^{\dagger}+|1\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}1|\otimes C
, and express
K^{\dagger}\Gamma K
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}K^{\dagger}\Gamma K&=(|0\rangle\langle 0|\langle v|+|1\rangle\langle 1|\langle w|)(|0\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}0|\otimes A+|0\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}1|\otimes B+|1\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}0|\otimes B^{\dagger}+|1\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}1|\otimes C)(|0\rangle|v\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle|w\rangle\langle 1|)\\
&=|0\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}0|\langle v|A|v\rangle+|0\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}1|\langle v|B|w\rangle+|1\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}0|\langle w|B^{\dagger}|v\rangle+|1\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}1|\langle w|C|w\rangle.\end{split}

(57)

The matrix representation of
K^{\dagger}\Gamma K
in the computational basis is therefore
K^{\dagger}\Gamma K=\begin{bmatrix}\langle v|A|v\rangle&\langle v|B|w\rangle\\
\langle w|B^{\dagger}|v\rangle&\langle w|C|w\rangle\end{bmatrix}
.
Since
\Gamma\geq 0
, then also
K^{\dagger}\Gamma K\geq 0
, since
K^{\dagger}({\,\cdot\,})K
is
CP
(
CP
).
The claim of the lemma then follows from non-negativity of the determinant of
K^{\dagger}\Gamma K
.
∎

In Ref.
[
Horn and Johnson(1985)
]
, the above lemma is stated as part of a theorem (Theorem 7.7.7), which holds for
A>0
and
C>0
.
Therefore, we preset the proof above for completeness, to account for the cases of non-invertible
A
and
C
.

Lemma 7
.

Given a qubit channel
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
, if
\Lambda\circ\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)=|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|
, for any two
|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, then
\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)
is a pure state.

Proof.

Assume the opposite, that is
\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)=\lambda|\theta\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\theta|+(1-\lambda)|\theta^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\theta^{\perp}|
for some
|\theta\rangle\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Then, from linearity it must hold that
\lambda\Lambda(|\theta\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\theta|)+(1-\lambda)\Lambda(|\theta^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\theta^{\perp}|)=|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|
, which is only possible if
\Lambda(|\theta\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\theta|)=\Lambda(|\theta^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\theta^{\perp}|)
, and hence
\Lambda(\mathbbm{1})=2|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|
, which means that
\Lambda
is a measure-and-prepare channel, and, in particular,
\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)=|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|
.
Indeed, if
\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)\neq|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|
, then due to
\Lambda(\mathbbm{1})=2|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|
, we would have that
\Lambda(|\psi^{\perp}\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi^{\perp}|)=2|\phi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\phi|-\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)
is not
PSD
.
We reach the contradiction, because we assumed that
\Lambda(|\psi\kern 0.6458pt\rangle\langle\kern 0.6458pt\mathopen{}\psi|)
is not pure.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
be a qubit channel which maps an
