Generalized group designs: constructing novel unitary 2-, 3- and 4-designs
Ágoston Kaposi, Zoltán Kolarovszki, Adrián Solymos, Zoltán Zimborás

Abstract
Unitary designs are essential tools in several quantum information protocols. Similarly to other design concepts, unitary designs are mainly used to facilitate averaging over a relevant space, in this case, the unitary group U ⁡ ( d ) \mathrm{U}(d) . While it is known that exact unitary t t -designs exist for any degree t t and dimension d d , the most appealing type of designs, group designs (in which the elements of the design form a group), can provide at most 3 3 -designs. Moreover, even group 2 2 -designs can exist only in limited dimensions. In this paper, we present novel construction methods for creating exact generalized group designs based on the representation theory of the unitary group and its finite subgroups that overcome the 4 4 -design-barrier of unitary group designs. Furthermore, a construction is presented for creating generalized group 2 2 -designs in arbitrary dimensions.

Preliminaries

This section provides the essential definitions and statements employed in the proposed constructions. A basic familiarity with representation theory is assumed; a brief recap on the basic concepts is provided in Appendix

Several different but equivalent definitions can be found for unitary designs and group designs in the literature

,

-designs, where the summation over the finite set is weighted with a certain weight function, is crucial for this paper since the constructions presented are generally of this type.

Definition 1

(Unitary
t
-design)
.

A finite set
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathrm{U}(d)
with weight function
w:\mathcal{V}\to[0,1]
is called a unitary
t
-design if the following equation holds for any linear transformation
M
on
(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}
:

\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}w(V)\,V^{\otimes t}M\left(V^{\otimes t}\right)^{\dagger}=\overset{}{\underset{U\in\mathrm{U}(d)}{\int}}U^{\otimes t}M\left(U^{\otimes t}\right)^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}U,

(1)

where the integral on the right-hand side is taken over all elements in
\mathrm{U}(d)
with respect to the Haar measure. The number
t
is called the degree of the design.

In this definition and the rest of the paper
\mathrm{U}(d)
can be naturally identified with its defining representation.
More concretely,
U^{\otimes t}=\Pi_{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{&\opttoksa@YT={\font@YT}\getcolor@YT{\save@YT{\opttoksb@YT}}\nil@YT\getcolor@YT{\startbox@@YT\the\opttoksa@YT\the\opttoksb@YT}#\endbox@YT\cr\lower 0.31883pt\vbox{\kern 0.00002pt\kern 0.15941pt\hbox{\kern 0.31883pt\vbox to2.71053pt{\vss\hbox to2.3917pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-2.71053pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt}\kern-0.15941pt\kern-2.71053pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt}\cr}}\kern 345.4pt}}(U)^{\otimes t}
for
U\in\mathrm{U}(d)
, where
\Pi_{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{&\opttoksa@YT={\font@YT}\getcolor@YT{\save@YT{\opttoksb@YT}}\nil@YT\getcolor@YT{\startbox@@YT\the\opttoksa@YT\the\opttoksb@YT}#\endbox@YT\cr\lower 0.31883pt\vbox{\kern 0.00002pt\kern 0.15941pt\hbox{\kern 0.31883pt\vbox to2.71053pt{\vss\hbox to2.3917pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-2.71053pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt}\kern-0.15941pt\kern-2.71053pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt}\cr}}\kern 345.4pt}}
is the defining representation of
\mathrm{U}(d)
.
Moreover, the weight function of a
t
-design
\mathcal{V}
is the constant function
w\equiv 1/\absolutevalue{\mathcal{V}}
unless otherwise stated. Finally, a unitary
t
-design is called a
unitary group
t
-design
when it possesses a group structure. In our proposals, however, we will construct
t
-designs which are generalizations of group
t
-designs in the sense that they are constructed from products of finite groups:

Definition 2

(Generalized group
t
-design)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{1},\dotsc,\mathcal{V}_{\ell}<\mathrm{U}(d)
be finite subgroups. We call
\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\mathcal{V}_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathcal{V}_{\ell}
a generalized group
t
-design if
\mathcal{V}
forms a unitary
t
-design with weight function given by

w(U)\coloneqq\frac{\left|\left\{(h_{1},\dotsc,h_{\ell})\in\mathcal{V}_{1}\times\dotsc\times\mathcal{V}_{\ell}:\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}h_{j}=U\right\}\right|}{\absolutevalue{\mathcal{V}_1}\cdots\absolutevalue{\mathcal{V}_\ell}}.

(2)

Remark 1
.

The above careful definition of the weight function is needed as, in general, the product of finite subgroups of a group does not form a set whose cardinality is equal to the product of the cardinalities of the constituent subgroups.

3
Constructing higher-degree generalized group designs

Construction of unitary designs for general
t
and
d
is a mathematically challenging task
[
30
]
.
A well-known example of a unitary design is the Clifford group, which constitutes a group
3
-design for qubit systems
[
21
,
20
,
22
]
, but this specific example does not generalize for higher-degree designs. Fortunately, using the representation theory of finite groups, one may discover group
t
-designs with
t=1,2,3
for specific dimensions.
However, the construction of higher-degree group designs based on representation theory is impossible because of the nonexistence of group
4
-designs in dimensions greater than
2

[
23
]
. The resulting
4
-design-barrier for group designs compelled us to search for higher-degree designs that are not group designs.
In this section, we present a construction that pushes the
4
-design-barrier further for unitary designs by introducing an extended notion of group designs, which we will refer to as generalized group designs (see Def.
2
).

First, another characterization of unitary
t
-designs (equivalent to Def.
1
) is presented based on representation theory (see also, Appendix of
[
15
]
). Consider the
t
-fold tensor product of the defining representation of
\mathrm{U}(d)
: the underlying vector space
(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}
splits up under the action of
\mathrm{U}(d)
into the different isotypic components labeled by Young diagrams as

(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\;\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\gamma},

(3)

where
\Gamma
is the set of Young diagrams with
t
number of boxes and at most
d
rows,
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
carries the
\mathrm{U}(d)
-irrep labeled by the Young diagram
\gamma
and
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
is the multiplicity space where
\mathrm{U}(d)
acts trivially
[
31
]
. Let us denote by
P_{\gamma}

the projection corresponding to the
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
subspace. As previously mentioned, we present another equivalent characterization of unitary
t
-designs, which may be easily justified using Schur’s lemma:

Proposition 1
.

A finite set
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathrm{U}(d)
and a weight function
w:\mathcal{V}\to[0,1]
forms a unitary
t
-design if and only if the following equation is true for all linear transformations
M
on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}
:

\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}w\left(V\right)V^{\otimes t}M\left(V^{\otimes t}\right)^{\dagger}=\bigoplus_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}(P_{\gamma}MP_{\gamma})}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}},

(4)

where we used the notation as before, and
\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}
is the partial trace over
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
of operators supported on the subspace
\mathcal{W}_{\gamma}
.

Based on Proposition
1
the condition for a finite subgroup of the unitary group to be a unitary
t
-design can be reformulated to require that the appropriate irreducible subspaces of the unitary group remain irreducible when restricted to this finite subgroup.
In light of this, the 4-design-barrier can be formulated in the following way: there is no finite subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(d)
such that all irreps of
\mathrm{U}(d)
labeled by Young diagrams with 4 boxes, restricted to this subgroup, remain irreducible if
d>2
.
The idea behind our construction is that for each Young diagram with
t
boxes and at most
d
rows we find a finite subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(d)
, such that the irrep of
\mathrm{U}(d)
corresponding to this Young diagram remains irreducible when restricted to this subgroup.
From these irreps, one can attempt to create a generalized unitary group
t
-design for
t\geq 4
.
The fly in the ointment is that the irreducible subspace corresponding to such irreps might appear elsewhere in the irreducible decomposition of the
t
-fold tensor product of
\mathrm{U}(d)
upon restriction. This might lead to a non-trivial intertwiner between these irreducible subspaces when averaging, ruining the construction. To rescue this idea, we aim to exclude these situations, in a carefully crafted theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\varPi_{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{&\opttoksa@YT={\font@YT}\getcolor@YT{\save@YT{\opttoksb@YT}}\nil@YT\getcolor@YT{\startbox@@YT\the\opttoksa@YT\the\opttoksb@YT}#\endbox@YT\cr\lower 0.33772pt\vbox{\kern 0.00002pt\kern 0.16885pt\hbox{\kern 0.33772pt\vbox to2.87111pt{\vss\hbox to2.53339pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-2.87111pt\vrule height=2.87111pt,width=0.33772pt\kern 2.53339pt\vrule height=2.87111pt,width=0.33772pt}\kern-0.16885pt\kern-2.87111pt\hrule width=3.20883pt,height=0.33772pt\kern 2.53339pt\hrule width=3.20883pt,height=0.33772pt}\cr}}\kern 345.4pt}}
denote the defining representation of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
. Let
\Gamma
denote the set of all Young diagrams with t boxes and at most d rows, corresponding to the irreducible representations appearing in the irreducible decomposition of
\varPi_{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{&\opttoksa@YT={\font@YT}\getcolor@YT{\save@YT{\opttoksb@YT}}\nil@YT\getcolor@YT{\startbox@@YT\the\opttoksa@YT\the\opttoksb@YT}#\endbox@YT\cr\lower 0.33772pt\vbox{\kern 0.00002pt\kern 0.16885pt\hbox{\kern 0.33772pt\vbox to2.87111pt{\vss\hbox to2.53339pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-2.87111pt\vrule height=2.87111pt,width=0.33772pt\kern 2.53339pt\vrule height=2.87111pt,width=0.33772pt}\kern-0.16885pt\kern-2.87111pt\hrule width=3.20883pt,height=0.33772pt\kern 2.53339pt\hrule width=3.20883pt,height=0.33772pt}\cr}}\kern 345.4pt}}^{\otimes t}
.
Let
G_{1},\dotsc,G_{\ell}<\mathrm{U}(d)
be finite unitary subgroups such that:

1.

For each Young diagram
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there is a
j\in\left\{1,\dotsc,\ell\right\}
such that the representation
\varPi_{\gamma}|_{G_{j}}
remains an irreducible representation;

2.

For any
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
, there is a
j\in\left\{1,\dotsc,\ell\right\}
such that there is no non-trivial intertwiner between the representations
\varPi_{\gamma}|_{G_{j}}
and
\varPi_{\eta}|_{G_{j}}
.

Then the product
G_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot G_{\ell}
forms a generalized group
t
-design.

Proof.

Let us denote twirling with a subgroup
G<\mathrm{U}(d)
as

\mathcal{T}_{G}(X)\coloneqq\int_{G}U^{\otimes t}X(U^{\otimes t})^{\dagger}\,\differential U,

(5)

where the integration is done over the Haar measure of
G
.
Moreover, let us denote the consecutive twirls with
G_{1},\dotsc,G_{\ell}
by

\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\mathcal{T}_{G_{1}}\dotsc\mathcal{T}_{G_{\ell}},

(6)

and let us have the following notations

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}(X)\coloneqq P_{\gamma}XP_{\eta},\hskip 56.9055pt\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\gamma},

(7)

where
X
is a linear operator on
(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}
.
Considering a fixed linear operator
M
on
(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}
,
we want to show that

\mathcal{T}(M)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(M))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}.

(8)

For this, let us decompose
\mathcal{T}(M)
as

\mathcal{T}(M)=\sum_{\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}(M)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}(M)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma\\
\gamma\neq\eta\end{subarray}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}(M)\text{.}

(9)

Let
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
.
Firstly, we prove that
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}(M)=0
if
\gamma\neq\eta
.
We can use the fact that for all
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
and any subgroup
G<\mathrm{U}(d)

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}_{G}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}_{G}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta},

(10)

which is trivial by using Schur’s lemma.
Hence, the following is true:

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}(M)=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}_{G_{1}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\dotsc\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}\mathcal{T}_{G_{\ell}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma,\eta}(M)\text{.}

(11)

Let
j
be the index for which there is no non-trivial intertwiner between
\varPi_{\gamma}|_{G_{j}}
and
\varPi_{\eta}|_{G_{j}}
as required by the second condition.
It is easy to see that
\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}(X)
is an intertwiner of the
t
-fold diagonal action of
G_{j}
for any linear operator
X
on
(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}
. Furthermore, given that
G_{j}
is a subgroup of the unitary group,
P_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}(X)P_{\eta}
is also an intertwiner for the same representation as the product of intertwiners is also an intertwiner. Given that
P_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}(X)P_{\eta}
would be an intertwiner exactly between the subrepresentations
\varPi_{\gamma}|_{G_{j}}
and
\varPi_{\eta}|_{G_{j}}
(with multiplicities), it has to be zero by the second condition. Moreover, because of the linearity of the above equation, if it transforms into the zero map at any point it will stay zero after subsequent twirls.

Secondly, considering the case when
\gamma=\eta
, we can write Eq. (
11
) as

\displaystyle P_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}(M)P_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{1}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\dotsc\mathcal{T}_{G_{\ell}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(M).

(12)

By the first assumption in the Theorem, for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there exists a group
G_{j}
such that
\varPi_{\gamma}|_{G_{j}}
is irreducible. Note that from Proposition
1
it follows that:

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}(X)=\mathbb{0}\oplus\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\text{,}

(13)

where
X
is any linear transformation on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}
and where we denoted the zero operator on the orthogonal complement of
P_{\gamma}
by
\mathbb{0}
.

As a consequence, it is clear that
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}
for
k=j+1,\dotsc,\ell
since

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}(X)=\mathbb{0}\oplus\frac{\int_{G_{k}}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\left([\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}\otimes\varPi_{\gamma}(U)]\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X)[\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}\otimes\varPi_{\gamma}(U)^{\dagger}]\right)\,\differential U}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}},\end{split}

(14)

where the integration is done over the Haar measure of
G_{k}
. The partial trace is cyclic on the vector space it is tracing over, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
, and the operators on
\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}
commute. Therefore this partial trace can be treated as cyclic to get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}(X)=\mathbb{0}\oplus\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}(X).\end{split}

(15)

Moreover, the twirlings
\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}
for
k=1,\,\dotsc,\,j-1
leave
\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}(X)
invariant, since the above operator is invariant to the adjoint action of any subgroup.
More concretely, let
k\in\left\{1,\dotsc,j-1\right\}
. Then we can write for any linear transformation
X
on
(\mathbb{C}^{d})^{\otimes t}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}&\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X)=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}\left(\mathbb{0}\oplus\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\right)\\
&=\mathbb{0}\oplus\int_{G_{k}}(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}\otimes\varPi_{\gamma}(U))\left(\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\right)(\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{K}_{\gamma}}\otimes\varPi_{\gamma}(U))^{\dagger}\differential U\\
&=\mathbb{0}\oplus\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(X))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}},\end{split}

(16)

hence,
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}
. By combining this with the fact that

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}\mathcal{T}_{G_{k}}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}\quad(k=j+1,\ldots,\ell)

by Eq. (
15
),
we get that

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}\mathcal{T}_{G_{j}}.

(17)

As a result, one gets

\mathcal{T}(M)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\frac{\Tr_{\mathcal{H}{\gamma}}(\mathcal{P}_{\gamma}(M))}{\dim\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}}.

(18)

which proves the Theorem by Proposition
1
.

Theorem
1
enables us to search for examples of unitary
t
-designs using character theory. In particular, for some low-dimensional
2
- and
3
-design constructions a similar technique was used in Ref.
[
32
]
.
To be explicit, given a finite unitary subgroup
G<\mathrm{U}(d)
, one can examine if the representation labeled by the Young diagram
\gamma
with
t
boxes and at most
d
rows remains irreducible when restricted to
G
. Hence, one can search for a set of finite subgroups of
\mathrm{U}(d)
such that the first condition is fulfilled.
For the second condition, consider two Young diagrams
\gamma,\eta
.
When
\dim\varPi_{\gamma}\neq\dim\varPi_{\eta}
, consider
\dim\varPi_{\gamma}>\dim\varPi_{\eta}
without loss of generality. By the first condition, there is a finite subgroup
G
for which the representation
\varPi_{\gamma}|_{G}
remains irreducible, hence, the second condition is automatically fulfilled.
When
\dim\varPi_{\gamma}=\dim\varPi_{\eta}
, further investigation is needed to verify that the representations are inequivalent when restricted to some finite subgroup
G
.
To verify the irreducibility and inequivalence of the representations,
the method described in Appendix
B.1
is used.

]
:

4.1
Monomial reflection group

The monomial reflection group
G(m,1,d)
is the group of
d\times d
matrices with a single non-zero entry in each row and column, where the only possible values for the non-zero entries are the
m
-th root of unity.
More generally, given
p
such that
p\,|\,m
,
G(m,p,d)
is the subgroup of
G(m,1,d)
in which the product of the non-zero entries in each matrix is an
\frac{m}{p}
-th root of unity.
However, for the purposes of this paper, the group
G(3,1,d)
is sufficient.
The group has also been utilized for randomized benchmarking in Ref.
[
34
]
.

We know that
G(3,1,d)\cong S_{d}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{d}
, since
each element in
G(3,1,d)
can be completely characterized by

•

an element in
S_{d}
corresponding to the places of the nonzero entries in the
d\times d
matrix
through its natural representation, and

•

an element in
\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{d}
(corresponding to the
3
rd roots of unity in these entries).

The group product in
S_{d}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{d}
is given by

(\omega_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{1})\cdot(\omega_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2})=(\omega_{1}\omega_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2}+\Gamma_{\text{nat}}(\omega_{2})\bm{\alpha_{1}})\qquad(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in S_{d},\;\bm{\alpha}_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{3}),

(26am)

where
\Gamma_{\text{nat}}
is the natural representation of the symmetric group
S_{d}
defined by
\Gamma_{\text{nat}}(\omega)\ket{j}\coloneqq\ket{\omega(j)}
.

In the following, we will consider the
natural representation

\Gamma:G(3,1,d)\cong S_{d}\ltimes\mathbb{Z}_{3}^{d}\to\text{GL}(d,\mathbb C)

\Gamma(\omega,\bm{\alpha})\coloneqq\Gamma_{\text{nat}}(\omega)D(\bm{\alpha})\qquad(\omega\in S_{d},\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{3}),

(26an)

where, as before,
\Gamma_{\text{nat}}

S_{d}

\mathbb Z_{3}^{d}

\zeta

\displaystyle\Gamma(\omega_{1},\bm{\alpha}_{1})\Gamma(\omega_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2})=\Gamma_{\text{nat}}(\omega_{1}\omega_{2})D(\omega_{2}(\bm{\alpha}_{1}))D(\bm{\alpha}_{2})=\Gamma(\omega_{1}\omega_{2},\bm{\alpha}_{2}+\omega_{2}(\bm{\alpha}_{1})),

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J(\omega)}\zeta^{\alpha_{j}},

(26av)

where
J(\omega)
is the set of indices representing the fixed points of
\omega
.
With this equation, the inner product can be calculated as follows:

\begin{split}\left\langle\chi_{\Gamma^{\otimes 2}},\chi_{\Gamma^{\otimes 2}}\right\rangle&=\left\langle\chi_{\Gamma}^{2},\chi_{\Gamma}^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{G(3,1,d)}}\sum_{g\in G(3,1,d)}\Tr(\Gamma(g))^{2}\overline{\Tr(\Gamma(g))}^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{d!3^{d}}\sum_{\omega\in S_{d}}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb Z_{3}^{d}}\sum_{j,k\in J(\omega)}\sum_{\ell,m\in J(\omega)}\zeta^{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{\ell}-\alpha_{m}}\\
&=\frac{1}{3^{d}}\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\left[\sum_{j,k,\ell,m\in J(\omega)}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb Z_{3}^{d}}\zeta^{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{\ell}-\alpha_{m}}\right].\end{split}

(26aw)

It is clear that the innermost sum for fixed indices
j,k,\ell,m
and fixed
\omega
only depends on the equality of the indices
\left\{j,k\right\}=\left\{\ell,m\right\}
as sets. We can write

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb Z_{3}^{d}}\zeta^{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{\ell}-\alpha_{m}}=\begin{cases}3^{d}&\text{if }\left\{j,k\right\}=\left\{\ell,m\right\},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(26ax)

Let
f(\omega)\coloneqq\absolutevalue{J(\omega)}
.
Hence, for fix
\omega
, we get the following:

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k,\ell,m\in J(\omega)}\sum_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb Z_{3}^{d}}\zeta^{\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{\ell}-\alpha_{m}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k,\ell,m\in J(\omega)\\
\left\{j,k\right\}=\left\{\ell,m\right\}\end{subarray}}3^{d}=\left(2f(\omega)^{2}-f(\omega)\right)3^{d}

(26ay)

Proceeding with Eq. (
26aw
), in order to evaluate the average over
S_{d}
, we define
\iota_{j}(\omega)
to be the characteristic function of
j
being a fixed point of
\omega
.
Then, the number of fixed points can be written as
f(\omega)=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\iota_{j}(\omega)
.
The expectation values of
f(\omega)
and
f^{2}(\omega)
are the following:

\displaystyle\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\iota_{j}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\omega\in S_{d}}\iota_{j}(\omega)=\frac{(d-1)!}{d!}=\frac{1}{d},

(26aza)

\displaystyle\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\iota_{j}(\omega)\iota_{k}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-2)!}{d!}=\frac{1}{d(d-1)}\qquad\text{for }j\neq k,

(26azb)

\displaystyle\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}f(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\iota_{j}(\omega)=1,

(26azc)

\displaystyle\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}f^{2}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=0}^{d-1}\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\iota_{j}(\omega)\iota_{k}(\omega)=\sum_{j}^{d-1}\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\iota_{j}(\omega)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,k=0\\
j\neq k\end{subarray}}^{d-1}\underset{\omega\in S_{d}}{\mathbb E}\iota_{j}(\omega)\iota_{k}(\omega)=2.

(26azd)

Finally, the inner product of the character follows from Eq. (
26aw
) and Eq. (
26ay
), and can be calculated as

\displaystyle\left\langle\chi_{\Gamma^{\otimes 2}},\chi_{\Gamma^{\otimes 2}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d!}\sum_{\omega\in S_{d}}\left(2f(\omega)^{2}-f(\omega)\right)=2\cdot 2-1=3.

(26ba)

The only possible way to get 3 as the sum of positive integer squares is by three
1

\Gamma\otimes\Gamma
decomposes into three inequivalent irreducible representations.
∎

4.2
Constructing generalized unitary
2
-design

As a consequence of Schur’s lemma, twirling over a finite unitary subgroup yields a quantity that depends on the irreducible subspaces of the subgroup.
In this subsection, two consecutive twirlings are used to get the same result as twirling by the entire unitary group, resulting in an exact unitary
2
-design by Proposition
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\geq 2
and let
\mathcal{G}
be the image of the representation
\Gamma
of the monomial reflection group
G(3,1,d)
and suppose that
Q
is a unitary matrix with the following property:

\Tr\left(P_{1}^{Q^{\otimes 2}}P_{1}\right)=\frac{2d}{d+1}.

(26bg)

Then
\mathcal{G}^{Q}\cdot\mathcal{G}
is a generalized unitary
2
-design, where
\mathcal{G}^{Q}=\left\{QgQ^{\dagger}\;|\;g\in\mathcal{G}\right\}
.

Proof.

Using that
\Tr P_{1}=d
and
\Tr P_{\hbox{$\vbox{\hbox{\vtop{\halign{&\opttoksa@YT={\font@YT}\getcolor@YT{\save@YT{\opttoksb@YT}}\nil@YT\getcolor@YT{\startbox@@YT\the\opttoksa@YT\the\opttoksb@YT}#\endbox@YT\cr\lower 0.31883pt\vbox{\kern 0.00002pt\kern 0.15941pt\hbox{\kern 0.31883pt\vbox to2.71053pt{\vss\hbox to2.3917pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-2.71053pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt}\kern-0.15941pt\kern-2.71053pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt}&\lower 0.31883pt\vbox{\kern 0.00002pt\kern 0.15941pt\hbox{\kern 0.31883pt\vbox to2.71053pt{\vss\hbox to2.3917pt{\hss$$\hss}\vss}\kern-2.71053pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\vrule height=2.71053pt,width=0.31883pt}\kern-0.15941pt\kern-2.71053pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt\kern 2.3917pt\hrule width=3.02936pt,height=0.31883pt}\cr}}\kern 690.4pt}}$}}=d(d+1)/2
, the statement is a trivial consequence of
lemma

1
.
∎

Theorem

2
asserts the existence of a matrix
Q
which fulfills the relation given by Eq. (
26bg
).
In the next part of this section, we construct a matrix that satisfies the required condition.
The search for such a matrix follows the idea of defining the function

\displaystyle f:\mathrm{U}(d)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}

(26bi)

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle\mapsto\Tr\left(P_{1}^{U^{\otimes 2}}P_{1}\right),

(26bj)

whose maximum value is

d
, attained at the identity. The main idea is to find a matrix
B
such that
f(B)\leq 2d/(d+1)
, and then to search for the optimal matrix
Q
along a continuous path in
\mathrm{U}(d)
between the identity and
B
.

Let
F
denote the matrix of the (normalized) discrete Fourier transform (DFT), with matrix elements given by

F_{jk}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\exp\left(-\frac{2\pi i}{d}jk\right).

(26bk)

This is a well-known transformation, but it is difficult to connect with a continuous path to the identity which is also analytically tractable.
In order to acquire a simpler matrix, we can transform the DFT matrix as follows:

B\coloneqq\frac{1+i}{2}F+\frac{1-i}{2}F^{\dagger},

(26bl)

which is real valued and its eigenvalues are
\pm 1
.
Importantly, since
B^{2}=\mathbbm{1}
, we can give a parameterized path of unitaries that connects
B
to the identity in
\mathrm{U}(d)
:

\displaystyle Q(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\left(itB\right)=\cos(t)\mathbbm{1}+i\sin(t)B.

(26bm)

According to the next proposition, the path
Q(t)
will contain at least one specific matrix
Q(t^{\ast})
that satisfies Eq. (
26bg
).
To keep this proposition compact, we define

q(d)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&d\equiv 1,2,4,5\pmod{8},\\[4.0pt]
\sqrt{d},&d\equiv 7\pmod{8},\\[4.0pt]
-\sqrt{d},&d\equiv 3\pmod{8},\\[4.0pt]
\sqrt{2d},&d\equiv 0,6\pmod{8}.\end{cases}

(26bn)

Proposition 3
.

The overlap of
P_{1}
and
P_{1}^{Q(t)^{\otimes 2}}
is given by the following formula:

\displaystyle\Tr\left(P_{1}P_{1}^{Q(t)^{\otimes 2}}\right)=a(d)\sin^{4}(t)+b(d)\sin^{2}(t)+d,

(26bo)

where

\displaystyle a(d)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{2d-1}{2}-\frac{2q(d)}{d}-\frac{\operatorname{gcd}\left(4,d\right)}{2d},

(26bpa)

\displaystyle b(d)

\displaystyle\coloneqq-2(d-1)+\frac{2q(d)}{d}.

(26bpb)

The desired value
\frac{2d}{d+1}
can be reached by a specific
t
given by

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle=\pm\arcsin(\sqrt{x^\ast})+k\pi,\>k\in\mathbb{Z},

(26bqa)

\displaystyle x^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{-b(d)-\sqrt{\Delta(d)}}{2a(d)},

(26bqb)

with determinant
\Delta(d)=b(d)^{2}-4a(d)c(d)
with
c(d)\coloneqq d-\frac{2d}{d+1}
the constant term in the
0

Proof in Appendix

5.1
Orthogonal designs

The concept of a unitary
t
-design, which is based on the unitary group, may be naturally defined for other groups as well, see, e.g., Ref.
[
35
]
.
For our purposes, the orthogonal group
\mathrm{O}(d)
will be of main concern.

Definition 3

(Orthogonal
t
-design)
.

A finite set
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathrm{O}(d)
with weight function
w:\mathcal{V}\to[0,1]
is called a weighted orthogonal
t
-design if the following equation holds for any linear transformation
M
on
(\mathbb C^{d})^{\otimes t}
:

\sum_{V\in\mathcal{V}}w(V)\,V^{\otimes t}M\left(V^{\otimes t}\right)^{\dagger}=\overset{}{\underset{O\in\mathrm{O}(d)}{\int}}O^{\otimes t}M\left(O^{\otimes t}\right)^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}O,

(26br)

where the integral on the right-hand side is taken over all elements in
\mathrm{O}(d)
with respect to the Haar measure. The number
t
is called the degree of the design.

In this section, let us use the notation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{O}(d)}^{(t)}(M)&\coloneqq\int_{\mathrm{O}(d)}O^{\otimes t}M(O^{\otimes t})^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}O,\\
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{U}(d)}^{(t)}(M)&\coloneqq\int_{\mathrm{U}(d)}U^{\otimes t}M(U^{\otimes t})^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}U.\end{split}

(26bs)

Moreover, for a finite subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathrm{U}(d)
with weight function
w
, we define

\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{V}}^{(t)}(M)\coloneqq\sum_{U\in\mathcal{V}}w(U)U^{\otimes t}M(U^{\otimes t})^{\dagger}.

(26bt)

Here, we are considering approximate
t
-designs, since the main statement of this section holds in this general setting for
t\leq 3
.
In this paper, we are considering approximate designs using the relative error as follows
[
36
,
37
]
.

Definition 4

(
\epsilon
-approximate unitary
t
-design)
.

We call a finite subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathrm{U}(d)
an
\epsilon
-approximate unitary
t
-design for some
\epsilon>0
when

\left(1-\epsilon\right)\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{V}}^{(t)}\preceq\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{U}(d)}^{(t)}\preceq\left(1+\epsilon\right)\mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{V}}^{(t)},

(26bu)

where
\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
means that the
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}-\mathcal{A}
is a completely positive map.

Remark 2
.

The definition of
\epsilon
-approximate unitary design can be generalized to
\epsilon
-approximate orthogonal design naturally.

Appendix B
Recap of representation theory

The representation theory of finite and compact groups is a vast topic, and the interested reader is directed to
[
31
,
41
,
42
,
43
]
for more thorough introductions. In this section, only the basic notions are presented.

In quantum theory, we are usually interested in operations that can be described by unitary matrices. Thus, every representation we encounter in this paper is a unitary representation, i.e., a representation
\varPi:G\to\mathrm{U}(d)
.
The dimension of the representation is the dimension of the underlying vector space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
on which these matrices act, i.e.,
\dim\varPi=d
.

Two unitary representations are considered the same (i.e., unitarily equivalent) if there is a unitary basis transformation that links them. In general, a group can have many different representations. Sometimes, one talks about
natural, standard
or
defining
representations when the representation is based on some native properties of the group itself. Thus, in the case of matrix groups such as
\mathrm{U}(d)
, the defining representation is the representation when every unitary matrix in
\mathrm{U}(d)
is represented by itself.

To get a handle on representations, we usually turn to representations that are, in some sense, indivisible into smaller ones:

Definition 5

(Irreducible representation)
.

A representation
\varPi:G\to\mathrm{U}(d)
is called
irreducible
(or irrep for short) if there is no non-trivial subspace in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that every matrix
\varPi(g)
leaves it invariant. That is, a non-trivial subspace
V\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
\varPi(g)v\in V
for all
g\in G
and
v\in V
.

A nice property of unitary representations is the following:

Proposition 9
.

Every unitary representation of any group
G
is completely reducible. That is, every unitary representation
\varPi:G\to\mathrm{U}(d)
can be written as the direct sum of irreducible representations. In such a direct sum decomposition we call the isotypic component of an irrep
\varPi_{\gamma}
the sum of all subrepresentations isomorphic to that given irrep.

For example, if a unitary representation
\varPi:G\to\mathrm{U}(d)
splits up into four irreps, out of which two are isomorphic, then we have three isotypic components:

\varPi\cong\varPi_{1}\oplus\left(\varPi_{2}\oplus\varPi_{2}\right)\oplus\varPi_{3}\text{.}

(26hl)

This means, that the underlying vector space
\mathbb{C}^{d}
splits into direct summands under the action of
\varPi
as:

\mathbb{C}^{d}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\oplus\left(\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}\right)\oplus\mathbb{C}^{d_{3}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}}\oplus\left(\mathbb{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d_{2}}\right)\oplus\mathbb{C}^{d_{3}}\text{,}

(26hm)

where on the right-hand side of the above equation we have used a simple transformation into tensor products.

Given two representations, one may ask about matrices that link them in a structure preserving way similar to how a matrix between two vector spaces preserves linearity.

Definition 6

(Intertwiner)
.

Let
\varPi_{1}:G\to\mathrm{U}(d_{1})
and
\varPi_{2}:G\to\mathrm{U}(d_{2})
be two representations of the group
G
. A matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}
is called an
intertwiner
or
G
-homomorphism
if it has the following property for all group elements
g
:

\varPi_{1}(g)A=A\varPi_{2}(g)\text{.}

(26hn)

There is a powerful lemma that gives the exact form of all such matrices in the case of irreducible representations:

Lemma 3

(Schur’s lemma)
.

Given two irreducible representations
\varPi_{1}:G\to\mathrm{U}(d_{1})
and
\varPi_{2}:G\to\mathrm{U}(d_{2})
of the group
G
, all the possible intertwiners between them are:

1.

\mathbb{0}
if the two irreps are not equivalent,

2.

c\mathbb{1}
for some
c\in\mathbb{C}
if they are equivalent.

One way to construct new representations from existing ones is by taking the tensor product of two representations:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varPi_{1}\otimes\varPi_{2}:G&\to\mathrm{U}(d_{1})\otimes\mathrm{U}(d_{2})\text{,}\\
g&\mapsto\varPi_{1}(g)\otimes\varPi_{2}(g)\text{.}\end{split}

(26ho)

These representations are usually not irreducible even if
\varPi_{1}
and
\varPi_{2}
were irreps. The decomposition of such representations into irreps is governed by so-called fusion rules, which are connected to the group structure.

Let us continue our discussion with the representation theory of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)

d

A Young diagram of
t

k

t

B.1
Recap of character theory

For a given finite group
G
, the representation theory is well explicable: There are exactly as many inequivalent irreps of
G
as the number of conjugacy classes in
G
.
Based on this notion, any representation of
G
can be described by the multiplicities of the specific irreps.
Usually, it is hard to compute the irreducible decomposition of a specific representation of
G
, to handle this problem, character theory is used.
The character can be defined for any representation of a group in the following way.
In the following, we shall use
\varPi
and
\rho
, respectively, to differentiate between representations of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
and the finite group
G
.

Definition 8
.

The
character
of a representation
\rho
, denoted by
\chi_{\rho}
, is defined as:

\chi_{\rho}(g)\coloneqq\operatorname{Tr}(\rho(g)),\quad g\in G.

(26ii)

The character
\chi_{\rho}
is a
class function
on
G
, because it is constant in each conjugacy class due to the cyclicity of the operator trace.
A character
\chi_{\rho}
is
irreducible
if the representation
\rho
is irreducible.

Characters encode significant information about the representations of any group (even infinite) and are useful in distinguishing inequivalent representations or checking the irreducibility of a representation.
The irreducible characters of a finite group
G
form a
character table
which encodes useful information about
G
in a compact form.
The character table consists of
n
rows and
n
columns where
n
is the number of conjugacy classes of
G
.
Each row corresponds to an irreducible character and each column to a conjugacy class. The elements are the values of a specific irreducible character on a specific conjugacy class.
The character table also carries orthogonality properties.

The
inner product
of two class functions
\alpha,\beta
of
G
is defined as

\left\langle\alpha,\beta\right\rangle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\left|G\right|}\sum_{g\in G}\alpha(g)\overline{\beta(g)}.

(26ij)

Proposition 11

(Orthogonality relation 1.)
.

For any finite group
G
and its irreducible characters
\chi_{i},\chi_{j}
the following holds:

\left\langle\chi_{i},\chi_{j}\right\rangle=\left\{\begin{matrix}0\;&\text{if}\kern 5.0pti\neq j,\\
1\;&\text{if}\kern 5.0pti=j.\end{matrix}\right.

(26ik)

In our research, we investigate finite subgroups of the unitary group, with particular focus on their behavior under tensor powers. Given any finite group
G
and a unitary representation
\rho
, the image of
\rho
can be thought of as a subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(d)
. Therefore, in the following, we shall consider
G
a subgroup of
\mathrm{U}(d)
by
\rho
. Given the unitary group
\mathrm{U}(d)
, we consider the
t
-fold tensor power of its defining representation, and analyze how its irreducible components decompose when restricted to a finite subgroup
G<\mathrm{U}(d)
.
For each irreducible component appearing in the tensor power representation (which are labeled by Young diagrams), we study the restriction to
G
and determine its decomposition into irreps of
G
.
To compute this decomposition, we utilize the character table of
G
as well as its power map, which encodes how the powers of conjugacy classes map to other conjugacy classes. These tools allow us to determine precisely how a given representation of the full unitary group decomposes into irreducible components upon restriction to the finite subgroup.

Let
G
be a finite group with a representation
\rho
in
d
dimensions and
\gamma
be a Young diagram with
t
boxes in at most
d
rows.
The irreducible decomposition of the representation
\rho_{\gamma}
of
G
which is the restriction of
\varPi_{\gamma}
to
\rho(G)
can be calculated by taking the inner product of its character
\chi_{\gamma}(\cdot)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\gamma}(\cdot))
with each irreducible character
\chi_{j}
.
The inner product
\left\langle\chi_{\gamma},\chi_{j}\right\rangle
gives the multiplicity of the representation corresponding to
\chi_{j}
in the irreducible decomposition of
\rho_{\gamma}
.
The character
\chi_{\gamma}
can be calculated as

\chi_{\gamma}(g)=\frac{1}{t!}\sum_{\pi\in\mathrm{S}_{t}}\gamma(\pi)\prod_{k=1}^{t}\chi_{\rho}(g^{k})^{a_{k}(\pi)},

(26il)

where
\gamma(\pi)
is the character of the irrep indexed by the Young diagram
\gamma
of the
t
-degree symmetric group
\mathrm{S}_{t}
evaluated at permutation
\pi
and
a_{k}(\pi)
is the number of cycles of length
k
in
\pi
.
This result is derived from Theorem 2.7.9 in Ref.
[
44
]
.
Using this equation, characters can be calculated using the GAP system for any specific group
G
and the irreducibility of the representation
\rho_{\gamma}
can be calculated.
Thus, the conditions of Theorem

G_{1},\ldots,G_{\ell}
can be found.
