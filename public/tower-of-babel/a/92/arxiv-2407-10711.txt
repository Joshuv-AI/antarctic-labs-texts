Rigorous derivation of damped-driven wave turbulence theory
Ricardo Grande, Zaher Hani

Abstract
. We provide a rigorous justification of various kinetic regimes exhibited by the nonlinear Schrödinger equation with an additive stochastic forcing and a viscous dissipation. The importance of such damped-driven models stems from their wide empirical use in studying turbulence for nonlinear wave systems. The force injects energy into the system at large scales, which is then transferred across scales, thanks to the nonlinear wave interactions, until it is eventually dissipated at smaller scales. The presence of such scale-separated forcing and dissipation allows for the constant flux of energy in the intermediate scales, known as the inertial range, which is the focus of the vast amount of numerical and physical literature on wave turbulence. Roughly speaking, our results provide a rigorous kinetic framework for this turbulent behavior by proving that the stochastic dynamics can be effectively described by a deterministic damped-driven kinetic equation, which carries the full picture of the turbulent energy dynamic across scales (like cascade spectra or other flux solutions). The analysis extends previous works in the unperturbed setting [ 10 ] – [ 12 ] to the above empirically motivated damped driven setting. Here, in addition to the size L L of the system, and the strength λ \lambda of the nonlinearity, an extra thermodynamic parameter has to be included in the kinetic limit ( L → ∞ , λ → 0 L\to\infty,\lambda\to 0 ), namely the strength ν \nu of the forcing and dissipation. Various regimes emerge depending on the relative sizes of L L , λ \lambda and ν \nu , which give rise to different kinetic equations. Two major novelties of this work is the extension of the Feynman diagram analysis to additive stochastic objects, and the sharp asymptotic development of the leading terms in that expansion.



1.
Introduction

In an informal manner of expression, wave turbulence theory can be described as
Boltzmann meets Kolmogorov in a wavy setting.
This concise slogan surprisingly captures the essence of the matter, as wave turbulence lies at an intersection of kinetic theory, a legacy of Boltzmann, and Kolmogorov’s vision of turbulence in the context of nonlinear interacting waves. This striking intersection of ideas was first uncovered by Zakharov in 1960’s
[
33
]
, when he showed that the kinetic theory for wave systems, a theory that has been developed since the late 1920s to parallel Boltzmann’s kinetic theory for particle systems, can be used to give a rather systematic formulation of Kolmogorov’s ideas of turbulence, such as energy cascades and their power-type spectra, in the context of nonlinear wave systems.

Like in Boltzmann’s particle kinetic theory, the central object in wave kinetic theory is a kinetic PDE, known as the
wave kinetic equation
, which plays the role of the Boltzmann equation to describe the lowest order statistics of the wave interactions, in the limit where the number of waves goes to infinity and the interaction strength goes to zero. More precisely, the microscopic system in this wave setting is given by a wave-type nonlinear Hamiltonian PDE posed, for example, on a large box of size
L
, which we shall denote by
\mathbb{T}^{d}_{L}
, and with a nonlinearity of strength
\lambda
. The parameter
L
encodes the number of interacting waves (which is
\mathcal{O}(L^{d})
) and is sent to infinity in the thermodynamic limit, whereas
\lambda
is sent to zero. The wave kinetic equation appears as the effective equation for the second order moments of the solution
u(x,t)
, that can be described in Fourier space by
\mathbb{E}|\widehat{u}(k,t)|^{2}
. Just like in the classical Boltzmann theory, the higher order statistics are supposed to reduce to products of the lowest order ones (here second order), a phenomenon that is known as
propagation of chaos

[
32
,
28
]
.

Zakharov discovered that such wave kinetic equations sustain stationary in time, power-type, solutions that exhibit a
constant flux
of energy across scales, like forward or backward cascades. Such solutions are exact analogs to Kolmogorov’s spectra in hydrodynamic turbulence, and as such they offer a deep insight into the out-of-equilibrium statistical physics of a dispersive system including its turbulent features. It is safe to say that the vast majority of the physical and empirical literature on wave kinetic theory has focused on exhibiting, either numerically, experimentally, or empirically, such power-type solutions. While Zakharov’s solutions can be formally obtained from the unperturbed wave kinetic equation, they are only observed in practice (such as in numerical simulations or empirical experiments) in the presence of forcing and dissipation in the system that are separated
1
1

1

While it is permissible for the forcing and dissipation to coexist on certain scales, the effect of each should be negligible on the scales where the other is significant.
by a large stretch of intermediate scales, known as the
inertial range
, over which the system is left basically unperturbed. The presence of such scale-separated forcing and dissipation allows for the constant flux of energy in the
inertial range
, on which the wave kinetic equation is supposed to hold. Of course, such constant flux of energy across scales cannot be sustained in a conservative system (i.e. without forcing and dissipation), at least not for finite energy solutions.

Mathematically speaking, a lot needs to be done to get to, or even make sense of, this exciting combination of ideas presented by the Kolmogorov-Zakharov spectra. Naturally, the first step would be to justify the wave kinetic theory and establish its rigorous mathematical foundations. This has been an active avenue of research over the past ten years. We will review this progress in more detail later in the introduction, but that justification has been achieved successfully for some semilinear models (like the nonlinear Schrödinger equation), and even over arbitrarily long time intervals
[
15
]
, which was the first result of its kind, even compared to the particle setting which only recently featured a similar progress
[
16
]
. Nonetheless, all the recent progress on rigorous wave kinetic theory has been restricted to situations where either the equation is left conservative or in the presence of phase-randomizing forces that don’t introduce energy into the system, and act on all scales. In particular, this literature does not cover the setting that is most relevant to observing the Kolmogorov-Zakharov spectra, namely the one we described above in which an additive force injects energy into the system which is eventually dissipated by damping. This is precisely the setting we consider in this manuscript, where we study a forced-dissipated nonlinear Schrödinger model introduced by Zakharov and L’vov
[
31
]
for that same purpose.

Roughly speaking, our results provide a rigorous kinetic framework for the turbulent behavior of the damped-driven stochastic dynamics. More precisely, we show that the stochastic dynamics can be effectively described by a deterministic damped-driven kinetic equation, in a similar spirit to the recently established results in the unperturbed setting by Deng and the second author
[
10

[
15
]

This is despite the different randomness structures in the two settings: in the unperturbed setting, the randomness is coming from the initial data, whereas in our setting here, it is mainly coming from the stochastic force (especially when the initial data is zero). We do not attempt in this manuscript to match the progress achieved by the most recent results in the unperturbed setting, namely
[

Finally, we iterate that the importance of our results derives mainly from the fact that they show that
one can reduce the formulation and proof of practically any turbulence conjecture for waves to the study of a single deterministic damped-driven kinetic equation
. We believe that this equation is the right theoretical framework to understand the, up until now formal and non-rigorous, turbulent statistics (like cascade spectra) of nonlinear wave systems. Moreover, it allows to draw parallels, and exchange insights, with hydrodynamic turbulence theory, where such kinetic formulation of turbulence is not present. The latter was the aim of the recent work of Bedrossian

1.1.
Setup

We consider the following forced and damped NLS equation:

(1.1)

{\partial}_{t}u+i\Delta u=i\lambda\,\left(|u|^{2}-\frac{2}{L^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}|u|^{2}\right)\,u-\nu\,(1-\Delta)^{r}u+\sqrt{\nu}\,\dot{\beta}^{\omega},\qquad u(t=0,x)=u_{\mathrm{in}}(x),

on the torus
\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}/(L\mathbb{Z}^{d})
, where
\lambda,\nu>0
and
r\in(0,1]
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}=(L^{-1}\mathbb{Z})^{d}
be the dual of
\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}
, with the Fourier transform and its inverse defined by

(1.2)

\widehat{u}(t,k)=u_{k}(t)={L^{-d/2}}\,\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}u(x)\,e^{-2\pi ik\cdot x}\,dx\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad u(t,x)=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}}u_{k}(t)\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}.

Randomness comes into the system through the additive stochastic force
\beta^{\omega}
, and possibly the initial data
u_{\mathrm{in}}
if the latter is nonzero. More precisely, the initial data and the forcing are taken as follows:

(1.3)

\begin{split}u^{\omega}(t,x)|_{t=0}&=u_{\mathrm{in}}^{\omega}(x)=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}c_{k}\,\eta_{k}^{\omega}\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}\\
\beta^{\omega}(t,x)&=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}b_{k}\,\beta_{k}^{\omega}(t)\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}.\end{split}

Here, the family
\{\eta_{k}^{\omega}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
are independent complex standard Gaussian random variables, while

\{\beta_{k}^{\omega}(t)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
are independent complex standard Wiener processes, which are also independent of
\{\eta_{k}^{\omega}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
. The coefficients
c_{k}=c(k)
and
b_{k}=b(k)
,
c,b:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, are sufficiently smooth and decaying functions of
k
, which we take to be Schwartz functions to simplify the statement of our main results (although a finite amount of decay and regularity given by the first
100d
Schwartz seminorms is sufficient for all our results).

In Fourier space, the dissipation operator appearing in (
1.1
) is a multiplier with coefficients given by:

(1.4)

\gamma_{k}:=(1+|k|^{2})^{r},\quad\mbox{where}\quad|k|^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|k^{(j)}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for}\quad k=(k^{(1)},\ldots,k^{(d)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

1.1.1.
The model

The normalization prefactor
L^{-d/2}
in (
1.3
) guarantees that, for each
x\in\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}
, the initial datum and the forcing have average size which is
asymptotically independent of

(1.5)

\mathbb{E}|u_{\mathrm{in}}(x)|^{2}=L^{-d}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}c_{k}^{2}\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c(k)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}k=\mathcal{O}(1)\qquad\mbox{as }L\rightarrow\infty.

As a result, the nonlinearity in (
1.1
) has size
\lambda
, and the
kinetic timescale
(or Van-Hove timescale) is given by

(1.6)

T_{\mathrm{kin}}:=\lambda^{-2}.

The parameters
\nu

\sqrt{\nu}
in the forcing balance input/output of energy in the system

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}-\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}c_{k}^{2}=-2\nu\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(1-\Delta)^{r/2}u(t^{\prime})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\,dt^{\prime}+2\nu\,t\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}b_{k}^{2}

which, by the Grönwall inequality, implies that

(1.8)

It follows that the timescale for the forcing to input enough energy into the system, known as

(1.9)

We remark here that this choice of additive stochastic forcing that is balanced by dissipation is standard in the study of turbulent systems, both wave-type and hydrodynamic. It allows for the necessary scale separation between the needed source and sink of energy, in a way that leaves an inertial range, where Hamiltonian interactions dominate, to form. 1.1.2.
Scaling laws

Our goal is to study the dynamics of (
1.1
) as
\nu\rightarrow 0
,
\lambda\rightarrow 0
and
L\rightarrow\infty
. These parameters are related by
scaling laws
that specify the relative rates of their convergence to the limits. In particular, we assume that
\lambda=L^{-\kappa_{1}}
and
\nu\sim L^{-\kappa_{2}}
for some
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}>0
. In the absence of forcing (
\kappa_{2}=\infty
), there are natural restrictions on
\kappa_{1}
for one to obtain a useful kinetic description in the thermodynamic limit. This was precisely explained in
[
14
, Section 1.3]
(see also
[
13
]
), where it is shown that the optimal range of
\kappa_{1}
on a general torus is the interval
(0,1)
(for example, this is the optimal range on the square torus), which can be extended to
(0,d/2)
in the case of a generically irrational torus. The same limitations carry over to our forced-dissipated setting, so we shall restrict ourselves to the same ranges of
\kappa_{1}
. Note that
\kappa_{1}
dictates the size of the kinetic timescale
T_{\mathrm{kin}}=L^{2\kappa_{1}}
in terms of the system size
L
, and the mentioned range of
\kappa_{1}
limits
T_{\mathrm{kin}}
to the range
1\ll T_{\mathrm{kin}}\ll L^{2}
on a general torus, and
1\ll T_{\mathrm{kin}}\ll L^{d}
for a generically irrational torus.

1.1.3.
Heuristics

Heuristically, one expects that the limiting dynamics of (
1.1
) to depend on whether the ratio

(1.10)

\varrho:=\frac{T_{\mathrm{kin}}}{T_{\mathrm{for}}}=\nu\lambda^{-2}\sim L^{2\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}}

tends to a fixed constant in
(0,\infty)
, to 0, or to infinity in the limit.
This trichotomy leads to the following asymptotic regimes:

(i)

If
2\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}=0
, nonlinear wave interactions and forcing/dissipation are comparable, and we expect a forced/damped wave kinetic equation in the kinetic limit.

(ii)

If
2\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}<0
, nonlinear interactions dominate over the forcing and dissipation, and thus we expect similar behavior as in the case of the unforced and undamped cubic Schrödinger equation.

(iii)

If
2\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{2}>0
, the forcing and dissipation dominate over nonlinear interactions, and we expect a kinetic limit entirely determined by forcing and dissipation.

Roughly speaking, our results confirm these heuristics by deriving the adequate kinetic limit in each regime. But before stating the result, let us review the kinetic theory for the NLS equation in the absence of forcing and dissipation.

Finally, we would like to draw a contrast between the kinetic and non-equilibrium paradigm explored in this paper, and the vast literature in stochastic PDE focused on constructing invariant measures for damped-driven equations. At a philosophical level, one can think of the kinetic paradigm derived in this paper as describing an explicit (in terms of its description of the turbulent features) transient regime that would hold on the way to the convergence of the dynamics to its unique invariant measure, if such measure exists. At the point of this supposed convergence, the solution of the kinetic equation would converge to one of its stationary solutions.

1.4.
Strategy of proof

We explain briefly here some of the ideas in the proof, focusing mainly on the novelties and differences with respect to the
\nu=0

1.4.1.
Picard iterates with stochastic terms

The main strategy consists of expanding the solution in terms of Picard iterates (also referred to as Dyson series expansion)

(1.28)

u=u^{(0)}+u^{(1)}+\ldots+u^{(N)}+R_{N+1},

up to some large order
N
to be determined. The key is to obtain effective estimates for the iterates as well as the error, which will require taking
N
large enough in order for the error contribution to be negligible.

Each
u^{(n)}
can be written as a sum over ternary trees of order
n
, which admit similar combinatorics to those in
[
10
]
. The main difference with the case of the cubic NLS equation (
\nu=0
) is the fact that
u^{(0)}
contains both the linear evolution of the initial data and a stochastic term, namely

(1.29)

u^{(0)}(t)=e^{-it\Delta-\nu\,t(1-\Delta)^{r}}u_{\mathrm{in}}^{\omega}+\sqrt{\nu}\,\int_{0}^{t}e^{-i(t-s)\Delta-(t-s)\,\nu\,(1-\Delta)^{r}}d\beta^{\omega}(s)

This means that the solution profile
e^{it\Delta}u^{(0)}(t)
now has a nontrivial time dependence compared to the
\nu=0
case, which leads to new difficulties at the level of estimating the higher iterates
u^{(n)}
, which are basically iterative self-interactions of the zeroth iterate
u^{(0)}
. In the case of the cubic NLS equation (
\nu=0
case), this means that
u^{(n)}(t)
can be written as a sum over expressions involving a product of
2n+1
Gaussian random variables
(\eta^{\omega}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
, which are
time-independent
. In our setting, however, the stochastic terms in
u^{(n)}
require a different treatment, due to their nontrivial time dependence coming from the stochastic integral in (
1.29
). In fact,
u^{(n)}
can be seen as an integral expression involving a product of
2n+1
copies of
u^{(0)}(t_{j})
for different times
t_{j}\in[0,t]
.
As such, obtaining sharp bounds on
u^{(n)}
requires precise bounds on the two-point correlations
\mathbb{E}[u^{(0)}(t)\,\overline{u^{(0)}(t^{\prime})}]
, or rather, their Fourier transform in time (to capture their time oscillation). To make this comparison explicit, it suffices to compare the contributions of the two terms in (
1.29
). Let us rescale time by setting
t=sT_{\mathrm{kin}}
, let
\chi(s)
be a smooth cut-off in time supported in
[0,2]
such that
\chi=1
in
[0,1]
, and define

\begin{split}v^{(0)}(s)&=\chi(s)\,e^{-\varrho\,s(1-\Delta)^{r}}u_{\mathrm{in}}^{\omega}\\
w^{(0)}(s)&=\chi(s)\,\int_{0}^{s}\sqrt{\varrho}\,e^{-(s-s^{\prime})\,\varrho\,(1-\Delta)^{r}}d\beta^{\omega}(s^{\prime}),\end{split}

so that
u^{(0)}(t)=e^{-it\Delta}[v^{(0)}(\frac{t}{T_{\mathrm{kin}}})+w^{(0)}(\frac{t}{T_{\mathrm{kin}}})]
for times
t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{kin}}]
. Denoting by
\widetilde{f_{k}}(\tau)
the spacetime Fourier transform at
(k,\tau)
, one can show that the two-point correlations of the Fourier transform of the terms coming from the initial data, i.e.
v^{(0)}(s)
, admit bounds of the form,

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{v_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)\,\overline{\widetilde{v_{k^{\prime}}}^{(0)}(\tau^{\prime})}]\lesssim_{N}c_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\tau^{\prime}\rangle^{-N}\,\delta_{k-k^{\prime}}\qquad\qquad\forall N\in\mathbb{N}.

On the other hand, those coming from the stochastic forcing are only small when they they are sufficiently separated in temporal frequency, e.g.

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{w_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)\,\overline{\widetilde{w_{k^{\prime}}}^{(0)}(\tau^{\prime})}]\lesssim_{N}b_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau-\tau^{\prime}\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\tau\rangle^{-1}\,\langle\tau^{\prime}\rangle^{-1}\,\delta_{k-k^{\prime}}\qquad\qquad\forall N\in\mathbb{N}\ \mbox{and for}\ \varrho=1.

We note that the two-point correlations between
\widetilde{v}^{(0)}
and
\widetilde{w}^{(0)}
vanish on account of the independence between forcing and initial datum. This is a crucial property in the computation of correlations between Picard iterates in (
1.28
):

(1.30)

\mathbb{E}[u^{(n_{1})}_{k}(t)\overline{u^{(n_{2})}_{k}(t)}],\qquad n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

For instance, beyond terms coming exclusively from the initial datum or the forcing,
\mathbb{E}|u^{(1)}_{k}(t)|^{2}
is also made of cross-terms given by integrals over
s_{1},s_{2}\in[0,t]
of

\mathbb{E}[v_{k_{1}}^{(0)}(s_{1})\overline{w^{(0)}_{k_{2}}(s_{1})}v^{(0)}_{k_{3}}(s_{1})\overline{v^{(0)}_{k_{1}^{\prime}}(s_{2})}w^{(0)}_{k_{2}^{\prime}}(s_{2})\overline{v^{(0)}_{k_{3}^{\prime}}(s_{2})}]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm{ind.}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[v_{k_{1}}^{(0)}(s_{1})v^{(0)}_{k_{3}}(s_{1})\overline{v^{(0)}_{k_{1}^{\prime}}(s_{2})}\overline{v^{(0)}_{k_{3}^{\prime}}(s_{2})}]\,\mathbb{E}[\overline{w^{(0)}_{k_{2}}(s_{1})}w^{(0)}_{k_{2}^{\prime}}(s_{2})].

Whenever the initial datum is nonzero
6
6

6

When
c_{k}=0
in (
1.3
),
v^{(0)}\equiv 0
and the only terms that give rise to the kinetic integral come from the forcing
w^{(0)}
.

As can be seen from this example, the new input coming from the stochastic forcing –which is decoupled from the input coming from the initial datum via independence– requires a new analysis to manage its contribution to the Dyson series.
In the case of top-order contributions such as (

1.4.2.
Convergence to WKE

As a consequence of the analysis mentioned above, one obtains that, with overwhelming probability, the Picard iterates

in (

) decay like a geometric sequence for
n\leq N
. An estimate on the remainder
R_{N+1}
can be obtained by analyzing the linearization of the (NLS) equation around the first
N
iterates; this is done in Section

. From this, it follows that

(1.31)

\mathbb{E}|u_{k}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[|u_{k}^{(0)}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}+|u_{k}^{(1)}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}+2\mbox{Re}\,\overline{u_{k}^{(0)}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})}u_{k}^{(2)}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})\right]+o\left(s\right).

The terms in the braces should coverge to the term
n_{\mathrm{app}}
in (
1.15
) which give rise to the kinetic kernel
\mathcal{K}
in (
1.13
). This is another place where there is a major departure with the
\nu=0
setting. Indeed, the convergence to the kinetic kernel is very different in the regimes
\varrho\ll 1
and
\varrho\gtrsim 1
. In order to illustrate this, let us describe the computation in the case of
\mathbb{E}|u_{k}^{(1)}(t)|^{2}
for
t=s\,T_{\mathrm{kin}}
with
0\leq s\leq 1
. As shown in
Section

4
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}|u_{k}^{(1)}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}=4\,\left(\frac{1}{\lambda\,L^{d}}\right)^{2}\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}\\
\end{subarray}}\int_{0}^{s}\int_{s_{2}}^{s}&\ e^{-\varrho\gamma_{k}(2s-s_{1}-s_{2})-\varrho(s_{1}-s_{2})\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{k_{j}}}\\
&\cos\left(T_{\mathrm{kin}}\Omega\,(s_{1}-s_{2})\right)\,\prod_{j=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}|u_{k_{j}}^{(0)}(s_{2}T_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}\,ds_{2}\,ds_{1}+o(s).\end{split}

where
\Omega=|k_{1}|_{\zeta}^{2}-|k_{2}|_{\zeta}^{2}+|k_{3}|_{\zeta}^{2}-|k|_{\zeta}^{2}
.

When
\varrho\rightarrow 0
, one may exploit oscillations by integrating by parts the cosine in order to recover the top order terms which give rise to the Wave Kinetic Equation (
1.12
). This critically exploits the fact that we gain powers of
\varrho
in the remainder terms from integrating by parts.

For
\varrho\gtrsim 1
, however, such terms are at least as important, and they even dominate as
\varrho\rightarrow\infty
. For that reason, a different approach is needed. Upon integration in
s_{1}
, we obtain

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}|u_{k}^{(1)}(sT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}=L^{-2d}&\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}\\
\end{subarray}}\left(\int_{0}^{s}e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}(s-s^{\prime})}\,\nu^{-1}\,h_{0}(\nu^{-1}\Omega,\Gamma_{-})\,\prod_{j=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}|u_{k_{j}}^{(0)}(s^{\prime}T_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}\,ds^{\prime}\right.\\
&-\left.\int_{0}^{s}e^{-2\varrho\Gamma_{+}(s-s^{\prime})}\,\nu^{-1}\,[h_{1}(\nu^{-1}\Omega,\Gamma_{-})-h_{2}(\nu^{-1}\Omega,\Gamma_{-})]\,\prod_{j=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}|u_{k_{j}}^{(0)}(s^{\prime}T_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}\,ds^{\prime}\right),\end{split}

where
\Gamma_{\pm}=\pm\gamma_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{k_{j}}
and

\begin{split}h_{0}(x,y)=\frac{4y}{y^{2}+x^{2}},\qquad h_{1}(x,y)=\frac{4y\,\cos(\varrho(s-s^{\prime})x)}{y^{2}+x^{2}},\qquad h_{2}(x,y)=\frac{4x\,\sin(\varrho(s-s^{\prime})x)}{y^{2}+x^{2}}.\end{split}

In the limit as
L\to\infty
, and hence
\nu\to 0
, all three kernels
h_{0},h_{1},h_{2}
should behave like an approximation to the identity in their first variable and lead to
\delta(\Omega)
factors as a leading contribution. While the term coming from
h_{0}
leads to a key contribution towards the WKE, the contribution of the terms in
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
should vanish in the limit. This vanishing is the result of an exact cancellation of their respective contributions in the limit, which have to be computed precisely.
Interestingly, in the complementary regime
\varrho\rightarrow 0
we described above, the delta function coming from
h_{2}
is the one leading to the WKE, while the deltas arising from
h_{0}
and
h_{1}
cancel in this asymptotic regime. This sharp asymptotic development of the oscillatory kernels in
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
as
\nu\rightarrow 0
in the whole regimes of
\varrho\gtrsim 1
constitutes one of the main novelties of this work, as they do not fall under the umbrella of the kernels studied in the previous works (e.g. Proposition 6.1 in
[
12
]
). Note that
h_{2}
is not even absolutely integrable in the
x
variable, which requires exploiting its oscillations to reveal its approximation to the identity behavior.

1.5.
Future perspectives

1.5.1.
Getting to the kinetic timescale

It should be clear from our discussion in Section
1.4
that the bulk of the analysis in this paper goes on two fronts: The first is reducing the combinatorial estimates on the Feynman diagrams to their analogs in the
\nu=0
case, by proving and effectively utilizing the time correlations estimates of the stochastic integral terms. The second front is obtaining the exact asymptotics of the first iterates (here
u^{(n)}
for
n\leq 2
) that converge to the corresponding iterate of the wave kinetic equation. Here, the analysis was quite different and more involved compared to the
\nu=0
case.

We believe that those same ideas can be extended, in combination to the more sophisticated analytical and combinatorial machinery in
[
12
,
14
]
, to obtain a justification of the approximation (
1.14
) all the way to time intervals that reach the kinetic timescale, say of the form
[0,\delta\cdot T_{\mathrm{kin}}]
, or even longer time intervals as was done recently in
[
15
]
.

1.5.2.
Analysis of the damped/driven WKE

As we explained earlier, one of the main postulates in wave turbulence theory is that of scale separation: the random force operates at large scales, the dissipation acts on small scales, and there are intermediate scales (known as inertial range) in which the effect of the viscosity and the force are negligible. In this inertial range, physical arguments, as well as various experiments, suggest that
\mathbb{E}|u_{k}(t)|^{2}
should resemble a power law in
k
, in the spirit of the stationary-in-time power-law solutions to (
1.12
) that were discovered by Zakharov. Unfortunately, the highly singular behavior of those power-law solutions and the very formal fashion in which they satisfy the equation pose substantial blocks to their rigorous study, and as a result, our understanding of the turbulence aspects of wave turbulence theory.

This was a major motivation for us to study the forced-dissipated equation, as we believe that the analogous turbulent solutions to (
1.17
) should be relatively better behaved. This raises the highly important open problem of investigating the existence of such solutions to (
1.17
) that resemble the Kolmogorov-Zakharov power-law spectra at least within the inertial range.

Clearly, the analogy with hydrodynamic turbulence is quite strong here. In a recent manuscript, Bedrossian explored this analogy and formulated some conjectures on turbulence and anomalous dissipation for the damped-driven NLS equation
[
4
]
. While these conjectures were formulated in terms of invariant measures and averages of solutions to the damped-driven NLS, they can now be rigorously and concretely checked at the level of solutions of the (
1.17
) (or more generally (
1.27
) with
\widetilde{\nu}_{2}\gg\widetilde{\nu}_{1}
). This provides yet another motivation to study the dynamics of the latter kinetic equations.

1.5.3.
Admissible dissipations

One of the basic questions in turbulence is that of universality: do the statistics of the problem depend on the external forcing or the internal friction? Which features are common to different turbulent systems?

A key property satisfied by our chosen dissipation function
\gamma_{k}
in (
1.4
) is that

(1.32)

\left\{|k_{1}|^{2}-|k_{2}|^{2}+|k_{3}|^{2}-|k|^{2}=0\right\}\cap\{-\gamma_{k}+\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{k_{j}}=0\}=\emptyset,

see
Claim

4.7
. From a technical viewpoint, this condition plays an important role in our derivation of the kinetic equation. However, we believe that our argument also allows to deal with other dissipation functions that satisfy weaker versions of (
1.32
). For example, in
Remark

4.14
, we explain how one can modify the argument in
Section

4
to deal with the case of the dissipation given by the Laplacian
\gamma_{k}=|k|_{\zeta}^{2}
, which violates condition (
1.32
). More precisely, we show that it suffices to prove that the number of points in a neighborhood of the intersection (
1.32
) is sufficiently small (see Lemma
4.13
). Interestingly enough, our analysis suggests that one might always need a condition in the spirit of (
1.32
) (or a quantitative version of it in the spirit of Lemma
4.13
) where the equality in the second set is replaced by
<
.

Further study on the necessity of such criteria, as well as deriving quantitative estimates on the number of points in a neighborhood of the intersection of these manifolds for more general dissipations
\gamma_{k}
are interesting open problems.

2.
Setup of the problem

Consider the Zakharov-L’vov stochastic model for wave turbulence:

(2.1)

{\partial}_{t}u+i\Delta_{\zeta}u=i\lambda\,\left(|u|^{2}-2M(u)\right)\,u-\nu\,(1-\Delta_{\zeta})^{r}u+\sqrt{\nu}\,\dot{\beta}^{\omega}

where
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}
, where
\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}=\frac{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{L\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
and
L\gg 1
is the size of the large box,
\lambda,\nu>0
are parameters that will be discussed later,
r\in(0,1]
and

(2.2)

\beta^{\omega}(t,x):=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}b_{k}\beta_{k}^{\omega}(t)\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x},

where
\{\beta_{k}(t)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
are standard independent complex Wiener processes and
b_{k}=b(k)
is a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The normalized mass is defined as follows:

(2.3)

\begin{split}M(u)(t)&:=\fint_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}|u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx=L^{-d}\,\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}|u(t,x)|^{2}\,dx.\end{split}

Note that, unlike in the case of the cubic NLS equation with random initial data, the mass is not conserved. However, we will show that
M(u)
is uniformly bounded in time.

The rescaled Laplacian is given by:

(2.4)

\Delta_{\zeta}:=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\zeta_{j}\,{\partial}_{j}^{2},\qquad\zeta=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{d})\in[1,2]^{d},

where
\zeta
controls the aspect ratios of the torus, and thus whether it is rational or irrational. The dissipation operator appearing in (
2.1
) is a Fourier multiplier with coefficients given by:

(2.5)

\gamma_{k}:=(1+|k|_{\zeta}^{2})^{r},\quad\mbox{where}\quad|k|^{2}_{\zeta}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\zeta_{j}|k^{(j)}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for}\quad k=(k^{(1)},\ldots,k^{(d)})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The choice of normalization
L^{-d/2}
in (
2.2
) guarantees that that the size of the forcing at each point in the torus is asymptotically independent on the size of the torus
L
. Indeed, we define

(2.6)

B:=\left\lVert b\right\rVert_{\ell^{2}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L})}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}}b_{k}^{2},

which has size
L^{d}
since

(2.7)

L^{-d}\,B\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|b(\xi)|^{2}\,d\xi\sim 1\quad\mbox{as}\quad L\rightarrow\infty.

Together with (
2.1
), let us prescribe random initial data at
t=0
as follows:

(2.8)

u(t,x)|_{t=0}=u_{0}(x)=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}c_{k}\,\eta_{k}^{\omega}\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}

where
c_{k}=c(k)
is a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{\eta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
are iid complex Gaussian random variables with zero mean,
\mathbb{E}\eta_{k}\eta_{j}=0
, and
\mathbb{E}\eta_{k}\overline{\eta_{j}}=\delta_{kj}
. Moreover, we will assume that
\eta_{k}
is independent of
\beta_{k}(t)
for all
t\geq 0
.

We may now interpret the stochastic PDE in (
2.1
) in the integral sense, i.e.

u(t)=u_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\left[-i\Delta_{\zeta}u(t^{\prime})+i\lambda\,\left(|u(t^{\prime})|^{2}-2M(u(t^{\prime}))\right)\,u(t^{\prime})-\nu\,(1-\Delta_{\zeta})^{r}u(t^{\prime})\right]\,dt^{\prime}+\sqrt{\nu}\,\beta^{\omega}(t).

2.1.
Balance of energy

The choice of the coefficients
\nu
and
\sqrt{\nu}
for the dissipation and the forcing terms, respectively, is to balance the energy in the system. Using the standard notation for Itô processes, we write

du=\left(-i\Delta_{\zeta}u+i\lambda\,\left(|u|^{2}-2M(u)\right)\,u-\nu\,(1-\Delta_{\zeta})^{r}u\right)dt+\sqrt{\nu}\,d\beta(t):=\mu_{t}dt+\sqrt{\nu}\,d\beta(t).

Then one can apply the Itô formula, which yields:

d(|u(t)|^{2})=\left(\overline{u(t)}\,\mu_{t}+u(t)\,\overline{\mu_{t}}+2\nu\,L^{-d}B\right)\,dt+\sqrt{\nu}\,\overline{u(t)}\,d\beta(t)+\sqrt{\nu}\,u(t)\,d\overline{\beta(t)}.

By integrating in time and in space, we are led to the balance of energy equation:

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}=-2\nu\,\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(1-\Delta_{\zeta})^{r/2}u(t^{\prime})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\,dt^{\prime}+2\nu\,B\,t.

Here we see that our choice of coefficients
\nu
and
\sqrt{\nu}
led to the terms of the right-hand side having comparable size and opposite signs, in such a way that the dissipation and the forcing terms balance each other.
Moreover, we find

(2.10)

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}+2\nu B\,t,

which is a good estimate for times
t\lesssim\nu^{-1}
. In view of (
2.7
)-(
2.8
),

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\sim L^{d}\ \mbox{and}\ B\sim L^{d},

and therefore
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\sim L^{d}
for times
t\lesssim\nu^{-1}
. This
a priori
bound still holds for times
t\gtrsim\nu^{-1}
, but we must perform a more careful analysis of (
2.9
).
Using the fact that

\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert(1-\Delta_{\zeta})^{r/2}u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})},

equation (
2.9
) yields the following inequality:

(2.11)

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(0)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\leq-2\nu\,\int_{0}^{t}\,\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t^{\prime})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\,dt^{\prime}+2\nu\,B\,t.

An application of Grönwall’s inequality yields:

(2.12)

\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\leq B+(\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}-B)\,e^{-2\nu t}.

Note that at times
t\gtrsim\nu^{-1}
the effect of
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}
fades off and the forcing takes over, while keeping
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}^{2}\sim L^{d}
at all times.

2.2.
Two timescales

There are two natural timescales that are important in this problem. As suggested by (
2.10
), the timescale in which the forcing truly starts to operate is proportional to
\nu^{-1}
, and therefore we define

(2.13)

T_{\mathrm{for}}=\nu^{-1}.

On the other hand, we have the kinetic timescale given by the typical size of the cubic nonlinearity in (
2.1
). For all times
t
where the solution exists, we know that
\mathbb{E}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{L})}^{2}\sim L^{d}
thanks to (
2.12
) and (
2.7
). As a consequence,
|u|
has size
1
on average, so the strength of the cubic nonlinearity is
\lambda
. The kinetic timescale is the inverse of the square of this number:

(2.14)

T_{\mathrm{kin}}=\lambda^{-2}.

Given the two natural timescales in this problem, there are three different scenarios depending on the relative size between
T_{\mathrm{kin}}
and
T_{\mathrm{for}}
.

(1)

If
T_{\mathrm{kin}}\ll T_{\mathrm{for}}
, the forcing is so weak that it has no effect in the limit. The limiting dynamics will be governed by the WKE for the cubic NLS equation with random initial data, as in
[
10
]
.

(2)

If
T_{\mathrm{kin}}\sim T_{\mathrm{for}}
, there is a balance between forcing and the cubic nonlinearity. In the limit, we obtain a damped/driven wave kinetic equation.

(3)

If
T_{\mathrm{kin}}\gg T_{\mathrm{for}}
, the forcing is so strong that the limiting dynamics are dominated by it. This is a somewhat uninteresting scenario, where we expect the forcing and dissipation to entirely determine the dynamics.

Let us highlight that our techniques may handle a third timescale connected to the dissipation, should one consider different parameters
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
for the dissipation and the forcing. Such considerations lead to additional possible kinetic equations, see
Remark

1.4
for more detailes.

We will study (
2.1
) for times
t\in[0,T]
where we wish to take
T
to be as close to
T_{\mathrm{kin}}
as possible. Given the heuristics above, we define

(2.15)

\vartheta:=\nu T=\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{for}}}.

Let us highlight that
\vartheta\leq\varrho
for
\varrho
introduced in (
1.10
).

2.3.
Fourier formulation and rescaled time

Given that we are interested in times
t\in[0,T]
for some
T
as close as possible to
T_{\mathrm{kin}}
, we rescale time so that we may work on the interval
[0,1]
. In order to do so, it is convenient to work at the level of the Fourier coefficients. Recall that

(2.16)

u(x)=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}}u_{k}\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad u_{k}=L^{-d/2}\,\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}u(x)\,e^{-2\pi ik\cdot x}\,dx.

By the Plancherel theorem, we have that

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{L})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}|u(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}=\left(L^{-d}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}}|u_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

We rewrite (
2.1
) in terms of the Fourier coefficients:

(2.17)

\begin{split}{\partial}_{t}u_{k}-i|k|_{\zeta}^{2}u_{k}&=\frac{i\lambda}{L^{d}}\,\sum_{k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}=k}\epsilon_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\,u_{k_{1}}\overline{u_{k_{2}}}u_{k_{3}}-\nu\gamma_{k}u_{k}+\sqrt{\nu}\,b_{k}\dot{\beta}_{k}^{\omega}\end{split}

where
\gamma_{k}:=(1+|k|_{\zeta}^{2})^{r}
, and

(2.18)

\epsilon_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}=\begin{cases}+1&\mbox{if}\ k_{2}\notin\{k_{1},k_{3}\};\\
-1&\mbox{if}\ k_{1}=k_{2}=k_{3};\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We set
v(t):=e^{it\Delta_{\zeta}}u(t)
so that
v_{k}=e^{-it|k|_{\zeta}^{2}}u_{k}
, which yields

(2.19)

{\partial}_{t}v_{k}=\frac{i\lambda}{L^{d}}\,\sum_{k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}=k}\epsilon_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\,v_{k_{1}}\overline{v_{k_{2}}}v_{k_{3}}\,e^{it\Omega_{k2}^{13}}-\nu\gamma_{k}v_{k}+\sqrt{\nu}\,b_{k}\dot{\beta}_{k}^{\omega}

and

(2.20)

\Omega_{k2}^{13}=\Omega(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k)=|k_{1}|_{\zeta}^{2}-|k_{2}|_{\zeta}^{2}+|k_{3}|_{\zeta}^{2}-|k|_{\zeta}^{2}.

Note that in (
2.19
) we use the fact that
e^{-it|k|_{\zeta}^{2}}\,\dot{\beta}_{k}^{\omega}(t)
is a white noise with the same distribution as
\dot{\beta}_{k}^{\omega}(t)
.

Finally, we rescale the time variable
t\mapsto Tt
. Using the fact that
T^{-1/2}\,\beta(Tt)
is a Wiener process with the same distribution as
\beta(t)
, we find that

(2.21)

{\partial}_{t}w_{k}+\vartheta\,\gamma_{k}\,w_{k}=\frac{i\lambda\,T}{L^{d}}\,\sum_{k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}=k}\epsilon_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\,w_{k_{1}}\overline{w_{k_{2}}}w_{k_{3}}\,e^{iTt\Omega_{k2}^{13}}+\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\dot{\beta_{k}}

with
t\in[0,1]
and
\vartheta=\nu T
as introduced in (
2.15
).

2.4.
Picard iterates

At this stage it is convenient to introduce some additional notation. For functions
\bm{a}(t)=(a_{k}(t))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
,
\bm{b}
and
\bm{c}
we define

(2.22)

\mathcal{W}(\bm{a},\bm{b},\bm{c})_{k}(t)=\frac{i\lambda T}{L^{d}}\,\sum_{k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}=k}\epsilon_{k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}}\,a_{k_{1}}\overline{b_{k_{2}}}c_{k_{3}}e^{iTt\Omega_{k2}^{13}}.

Fix a smooth cutoff
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mbox{supp}(\varphi)\subset(-2,2)
such that
\varphi(t)=1
if
t\in[-1,1]
. Then we define

(2.23)

(\mathcal{I}\bm{a})_{k}(t)=\varphi(t)\,\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}a_{k}(t^{\prime})\,\varphi(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime},

with
\vartheta
as in (
2.15
).
Writing
\bm{w}(t)=(w_{k}(t))_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}
, we may rewrite (
2.21
) as

(2.24)

\dot{\bm{w}}+\vartheta\,\bm{\gamma}\cdot\bm{w}=\mathcal{W}(\bm{w},\bm{w},\bm{w})+\sqrt{\vartheta}\,\bm{b}\cdot\dot{\bm{\beta}},

which, in integral form, reads

(2.25)

\bm{w}(t)-e^{-\vartheta\bm{\gamma}t}\bm{c}\cdot\bm{\eta}=\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w},\bm{w},\bm{w})(t)+\sqrt{\vartheta}\,\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{I}\dot{\bm{\beta}},

for times
t\in[0,1]
. Note also that we need to properly interpret
\mathcal{I}\dot{\bm{\beta}}
as an Itô integral, which we detail in (
3.4
) below.

Next we write an expansion for
\bm{w}
based on Picard iteration:

(2.26)

\bm{w}=\bm{w}^{(0)}+\bm{w}^{(1)}+\ldots+\bm{w}^{(N)}+\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},

so that

(2.27)

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=e^{-\vartheta\bm{\gamma}t}\bm{c}\cdot\bm{\eta}+\sqrt{\vartheta}\,\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{I}\dot{\bm{\beta}},

(2.28)

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(0)},\bm{w}^{(0)},\bm{w}^{(0)}),

(2.29)

\displaystyle\bm{w}^{(N)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=N-1}\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})},\bm{w}^{(n_{3})}),

(2.30)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1}

\displaystyle=\bm{w}-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\bm{w}^{(n)}.

In order to derive an equation for
\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1}
, we plug (
2.30
) into (
2.25
)

(2.31)

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})+\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})+\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\leq n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}\\
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})},\bm{w}^{(n_{3})})

where

(2.32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N}2\,\mathcal{IW}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})})+\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})}),

(2.33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\leq n_{1}\leq N}2\,\mathcal{IW}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{w}^{(n_{1})})+\mathcal{IW}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1}),

(2.34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{IW}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})

From now on, we let
w^{(n)}(t,x)=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}w_{k}^{(n)}(t)e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}
be the function corresponding to the sequence
\bm{w}^{(n)}(t)
. Below we state our main results regarding the size of the Picard iterates and the remainder term.

2.5.
Main estimates

For
s,b\geq 0
, consider the spaces

\begin{split}\left\lVert a\right\rVert_{h^{b}}&=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\tau\rangle^{2b}\,|\widehat{a}(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau\right)^{1/2},\\
\left\lVert\bm{a}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}&=\left(L^{-d}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle\tau\rangle^{2b}\,\langle k\rangle^{2s}\,|\widehat{\bm{a}}_{k}(\tau)|^{2}\,d\tau\right)^{1/2}.\end{split}

In order to state our main estimates, let us introduce the quantity

(2.35)

\rho:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T&\mbox{if }1\leq T\leq L,\\
L&\mbox{if }L\leq T\leq L^{2},\\
T\,L^{-1}&\mbox{if }L^{2}\leq T\mbox{ and }\zeta\ \mbox{is generic}.\end{array}\right.

Note that the assumptions in
Theorem

1.2
guarantee that
\rho\lesssim L^{-\delta}\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{kin}}}
. The definition of
\rho
is motivated by a combinatorial result which we record in
Appendix

A
.

Our first result provides an upper bound on the size of the Picard iterates:

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
n\geq 1
,
s\geq 0
, and
b>\frac{1}{2}
. For any
0<\theta\ll 1
, we have that

(2.36)

\begin{split}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}\left\lVert\bm{w}_{k}^{(0)}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}}&\lesssim L^{\theta}\\
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}\langle k\rangle^{s}\left\lVert\bm{w}_{k}^{(n)}\right\rVert_{h^{b}}&\lesssim L^{\theta+c(b-1/2)}\,\left(\frac{\rho}{\sqrt{T_{\mathrm{kin}}}}\right)^{n-1}\,\sqrt{\frac{T}{T_{\mathrm{kin}}}}\end{split}

L
-certainly (i.e. on an event with probability
\geq 1-C\,e^{-L^{\theta^{\prime}}}
for some
0<\theta^{\prime}\ll 1
and
C>0
depending on
\theta^{\prime}
).

Our next result allows us to control the size of the remainder in (
2.30
).

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that
T
and
T_{\mathrm{kin}}
are as in
Theorem

1.2
, and let
s>d/2
, and
b>\frac{1}{2}
.
Then the remainder in (
2.30
) satisfies

\left\lVert\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}\leq L^{-\delta N}

L
-certainly, where
\delta>0
is as in (
1.25
).

The proof of
Proposition

2.1
will be given in
Section

3
, while
Section

5
is devoted to the proof of
Proposition

2.2
.

3.
Estimates on the iterates

In this section we prove
Proposition

2.1
. We start our analysis with the case
n=0
and then develop a general approach to handle any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.1.
Zeroth iterate

Our first result corresponds to the statement about
w^{(0)}
in
Proposition

2.1
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For any
s\geq 0
and any
0<\theta\ll 1
, we have that

\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}|w_{k}^{(0)}(t)|\leq L^{\theta}

L
-certainly.

Proof.

We write
w^{(0)}=c^{(0)}+b^{(0)}
, where

(3.1)

\begin{split}c^{(0)}(t,x)&=L^{-d/2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}c_{k}\eta_{k}\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x-\vartheta\gamma_{k}t},\\
b^{(0)}(t,x)&=L^{-d/2}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}b^{(0)}_{k}(t)\,e^{2\pi ik\cdot x},\quad\mbox{for}\qquad b^{(0)}_{k}(t)=\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime}).\end{split}

We first study
c^{(0)}
. We claim that it suffices to show that, for
c_{k}\neq 0
,

(3.2)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|c_{k}^{(0)}(t)|>L^{\theta}\right)\leq C\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\,c_{k}^{2}}\,L^{2\theta}\right).

Indeed, assuming (
3.2
), and using the fact that
c_{k}^{2}\leq C\,\langle k\rangle^{-2}
, we have that

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}|c_{k}^{(0)}(t)|>L^{\theta}\right)&\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|c_{k}^{(0)}(t)|>L^{\theta}\right)\lesssim\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}e^{-C\,\langle k\rangle^{2}\,L^{2\theta}}\\
&\lesssim e^{-L^{2\theta}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}e^{-C\,\langle k\rangle^{2}}\lesssim e^{-L^{2\theta}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}\langle k\rangle^{-2}\lesssim L^{d}\,e^{-L^{2\theta}}\lesssim e^{-L^{\theta}},\end{split}

where we allow the constant
C
to change from line to line.

Let us thus focus on proving (
3.2
). To do so, we note that
\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|c_{k}^{(0)}(t)|\leq c_{k}|\eta_{k}|
and therefore (
3.2
) follows from an elementary tail bound for a Gaussian random variable.

Next consider
b^{(0)}
. By the same reasoning as before, we claim that it suffices to show that, for
b_{k}\neq 0
,

(3.3)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|b_{k}^{(0)}(t)|>L^{\theta}\right)\leq C\,\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2\,b_{k}^{2}}\,L^{2\theta}\right).

for some
C
independent of
k
. In order to prove this, we first note that we may write

b_{k}^{(0)}(t)=\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,V_{k}(t)
, where
V_{k}(t)
is an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process satisfying

dV_{k}(t)=-\vartheta\gamma_{k}V_{k}(t)+d\beta_{k}(t).

By
[
22
, Theorem 2.5]
, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|V_{k}(t)|\right]\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\vartheta\,\gamma_{k}}}\,\log(1+\vartheta\gamma_{k}),

for a universal constant
C
. Since
\vartheta\leq L^{d}
, this yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|b_{k}^{(0)}(t)|\right]\leq\frac{C\,b_{k}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}}\,[\log(1+\gamma_{k})+\log(1+\vartheta)]\leq L^{\theta}b_{k}.

The Borell-TIS inequality
[
1
, Theorem 2.1.1]

then implies

\begin{split}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|b_{k}^{(0)}(t)|>L^{\theta}(1+b_{k})\right)&\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|b_{k}^{(0)}(t)|>L^{\theta}+\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}|b_{k}^{(0)}(t)|\right]\right)\\
&\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2b_{k}^{2}(1-e^{-2\vartheta\gamma_{k}})}\,L^{2\theta}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2b_{k}^{2}}\,L^{2\theta}\right),\end{split}

as desired.
∎

Despite the fact that
w^{(0)}
is relatively rough, we would like to place higher iterates in a better space such as
H^{b}([0,1],H^{s}(\mathbb{T}^{d}_{L}))
for
b>1/2
,
s>d/2
.

We begin by computing the Fourier transform of
w^{(0)}
. In order to do so, it is convenient to fix a smooth cutoff
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\mbox{supp}(\varphi)\subset(-2,2)
such that
\varphi(t)=1
if
t\in[-1,1]
. Next we redefine

w^{(0)}(t,x):=L^{-d/2}\,\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}}e^{2\pi ik\cdot x}w_{k}^{(0)}(t)

where

(3.4)

w_{k}^{(0)}(t)=c_{k}^{(0)}(t)+b_{k}^{(0)}(t):=c_{k}\eta_{k}e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}t}\varphi(t)+\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\varphi(t)\,\mathbbm{1}_{t\geq 0}\,\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime}).

Given that
\varphi=1
in the interval
[0,1]
, we can focus on studying this function as long as we restrict ourselves to such times. Moreover,
Proposition

3.1
and the continuity of
\varphi
guarantee that (
3.4
) is continuous almost surely.

We compute the temporal Fourier transform of
w_{k}^{(0)}
. First of all,

(3.5)

\widetilde{c_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)=c_{k}\eta_{k}\,\widetilde{\varphi}(\tau-i\vartheta\gamma_{k}),

where
\widetilde{\varphi}
is the Fourier-Laplace transform of
\varphi
. Similarly,

(3.6)

\begin{split}\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)&=\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\tau}\varphi(t)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime})\,dt\\
&=\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\vartheta\gamma_{k}t^{\prime}}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{\infty}\varphi(t)\,e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau)t}\,dt\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime})=:\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,Y_{k}(\tau).\end{split}

For fixed
k
and
\tau
, it is easy to show that
Y_{k}(\tau)
is a normal random variable with mean zero and variance
\mathbb{E}|Y_{k}(\tau)|^{2}\lesssim 1
.

On the other hand, we may integrate by parts to obtain some decay in
\tau
:

(3.7)

\begin{split}\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)&=\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-it\tau}\varphi(t)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime})\,dt=\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\vartheta\gamma_{k}t^{\prime}}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{\infty}\varphi(t)\,e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau)t}\,dt\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime})\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}}{\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau}\,\int_{0}^{2}\left(e^{-i\tau t^{\prime}}\varphi(t^{\prime})+e^{\vartheta\gamma_{k}t^{\prime}}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau)t}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right)\,d\beta_{k}(t^{\prime})=\frac{\sqrt{\vartheta}\,b_{k}}{\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau}\,X_{k}(\tau).\end{split}

For fixed
k
and
\tau
,
X_{k}(\tau)
is a Gaussian random variable with mean zero and variance:

(3.8)

\mathbb{E}|X_{k}(\tau)|^{2}=2\,\int_{0}^{2}\left|e^{-i\tau t^{\prime}}\varphi(t^{\prime})+e^{\vartheta\gamma_{k}t^{\prime}}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau)t}\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,dt\right|^{2}\,dt^{\prime}\lesssim 1.

When
\vartheta<1
, the best possible bounds are:

(3.9)

\mathbb{E}|\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)|^{2}\lesssim\begin{cases}\vartheta\,b_{k}^{2}&\mbox{if}\ \tau<1\\
\vartheta\,|\tau|^{-2}\,b_{k}^{2}&\mbox{if}\ \tau>1\end{cases}\lesssim\vartheta\,b_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau\rangle^{-2}.

Note that
b^{(0)}
cannot live in
H^{b}H^{s}
for
b>1/2
due to the decay
\langle\tau\rangle^{-2}
, which captures the roughness of the Wiener process.

When
\vartheta>1
, the bound coming from integration by parts is best and we have that:

(3.10)

\mathbb{E}|\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)|^{2}\lesssim\vartheta^{-1}\,b_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau/\vartheta\rangle^{-2}.

Despite the fact that the variance of
\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau)
only decays like
|\tau|^{-2}
, we can show that covariances have additional decay. That is the content of the next result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}
and
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

(i)

If
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
, or if
k_{1}=k_{2}
and
\imath=+
, we have that

(3.11)

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{w_{k_{1}}}^{(0)}(\tau_{1})\,\widetilde{w_{k_{2}}}^{(0)}(\tau_{2})^{\imath}\right]=0,

where
\imath\in\{-,+\}
indicates conjugation.

(ii)

If
\vartheta>2
, we have that

(3.12)

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{w_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{1})\,\overline{\widetilde{w_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{2})}\,\right]\Big|\lesssim_{N}&\ \vartheta^{-1}\,b_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\tau_{1}/\vartheta\rangle^{-1}\,\langle\tau_{2}/\vartheta\rangle^{-1}+c_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau_{1}\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\tau_{2}\rangle^{-N}\end{split}

for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}
and any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

If
\vartheta<2
, we have that

(3.13)

\begin{split}\Big|\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{w_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{1})\,\overline{\widetilde{w_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{2})}\,\right]\Big|\lesssim_{N}&\ \vartheta\,b_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\rangle^{-N}\,\left\langle\tau_{1}\right\rangle^{-1}\,\left\langle\tau_{2}\right\rangle^{-1}+c_{k}^{2}\,\langle\tau_{1}\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\tau_{2}\rangle^{-N}\end{split}

for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{L}
and any
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

(i)
Writing
\widetilde{w_{k}}^{(0)}=\widetilde{c_{k}}^{(0)}+\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}
we have three types of terms in (
3.11

is independent of

for all times and all

.

Next we account for the second summand in (

3.13
).
The fact that
\widetilde{c_{k}}^{(0)}
is independent from
\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}
implies that we need only estimate

Since

The rest of the proof consists of estimating
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{1})\,\overline{\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{2})}]
.

(ii)
Suppose that
\vartheta\gtrsim 1
. Using (

), we write:

(3.14)

\mathbb{E}\left[\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{1})\,\overline{\widetilde{b_{k}}^{(0)}(\tau_{2})}\,\right]=\frac{\vartheta b_{k}^{2}}{(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau_{1})\,(\vartheta\gamma_{k}-i\tau_{2})}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(\tau_{1})\overline{X_{k}(\tau_{2})}].

Given that

, we have that

\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}[X_{k}(\tau_{1})\overline{X_{k}(\tau_{2})}]=\int_{0}^{2}\Psi(\tau_{1},s)\,\overline{\Psi(\tau_{2},s)}\,ds

where

\displaystyle\Psi(\tau,s)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\tau s}\varphi(s)+e^{-i\tau s}\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau)s^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}(s+s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}.

It is easy to show that

\displaystyle\Psi(\tau_{1},s)\,\overline{\Psi(\tau_{2},s)}=

\displaystyle\ e^{i(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})s}\,\varphi(s)^{2}+e^{i(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})s}\,\varphi(s)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}-i\tau_{2})s^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}(s+s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+e^{i(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})s}\,\varphi(s)\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau_{1})s^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}(s+s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}

\displaystyle+e^{i(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})s}\,\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}-i\tau_{2})s^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}(s+s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}\right)\,\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau_{1})s^{\prime}}\varphi^{\prime}(s+s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\ e^{i(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})s}\,F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2};s).

In the last step, we factor out

\int_{0}^{2}e^{i(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})s}\,F(\tau_{1},\tau_{2};s)\,ds=\mathcal{O}_{N}\left(\langle\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}\rangle^{-N}\right).

(iii)
Suppose that

If

Finally, suppose that

The first step is to write the Fourier transform of

Our first result allows us to understand the temporal Fourier transform of this operator.

Lemma 3.3
.

We have that

(\widetilde{\mathcal{I}F})_{k}(\tau)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}[I_{0,k}(\tau,\sigma)+I_{1,k}(\tau,\sigma)]\,\widetilde{F}(\sigma)\,d\sigma

where

\begin{split}|I_{0,k}(\tau,\sigma)|&\lesssim_{N}\langle\tau+i\vartheta\gamma_{k}\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\sigma\rangle^{-1}\lesssim_{N}\langle\tau\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\sigma\rangle^{-1}\\
|I_{1,k}(\tau,\sigma)|&\lesssim_{N}\langle(\tau-\sigma)+i\vartheta\gamma_{k}\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\sigma\rangle^{-1}\lesssim_{N}\langle\tau-\sigma\rangle^{-N}\,\langle\sigma\rangle^{-1}\end{split}

for any
N\in\mathbb{N}

Proof.

We first write

(3.17)

\int_{0}^{t}f(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\,(f\ast\mbox{sign})(t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t^{\prime})\,\mbox{sign}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}.

We multiply this equality by
e^{-\vartheta\gamma_{k}t}\,\varphi(t)
and take the temporal Fourier transform. Next we substitute
f(t^{\prime}):=e^{\vartheta\gamma_{k}t^{\prime}}\,\varphi(t^{\prime})\,F(t^{\prime})
. The second term in (
3.17
) becomes

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t^{\prime})\,\mbox{sign}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-(\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau)t}\varphi(t)\,dt

which has the desired decay
\langle\vartheta\gamma_{k}+i\tau\rangle^{-N}
. Next note that the Plancherel theorem implies

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t^{\prime})\,\mbox{sign}(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\vartheta\gamma_{k}t^{\prime}}\varphi(t^{\prime})\,\mbox{sign}(t^{\prime})\,F(t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sigma-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-(is-\vartheta\gamma_{k})s^{\prime}}\varphi(s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}ds\right)\widetilde{F}(\sigma)\,d\sigma,

where we interpret
(\sigma-s)^{-1}
in the principal value sense. The integrals in parenthesis yield the decay
\langle\sigma\rangle^{-1}
. This completes the bound on
I_{0,k}(\tau,\sigma)
.

The term

corresponds to the first term in (
3.17

∎

4.1.
First reductions

In this section we want to find the equation satisfied by

Lemma 4.1
.

Let the (finite) intersection of all
L
-certain events in

2.1
and

2.2

Proof.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\mathbb{E}(\mathbbm{1}_{A^{c}}|u_{k}(t)|^{2})\leq(\mathbb{E}|u_{k}(t)|^{4})^{1/2}\,{\mathbb{P}}(A^{c})^{1/2}\lesssim(\mathbb{E}|u_{k}(t)|^{4})^{1/2}\,e^{-cL^{\theta}}.

is at most polynomial in

uniformly in

and

We repeat the argument that led to (

\begin{split}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}-\left\lVert u(0)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}=&\ -2\nu\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert(1-\Delta_{\zeta})^{r/2}u(t^{\prime})\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d})}\!dt^{\prime}+2\nu Bt+2\nu\mbox{Re}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}\overline{u(t^{\prime},x)}\,dx\,d\beta(t^{\prime})\\
\leq&\ 2\nu Bt+2\nu\mbox{Re}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}\overline{u(t^{\prime},x)}\,dx\,d\beta(t^{\prime}).\end{split}

Using the Plancherel theorem, we find that

(4.2)

|u_{k}(t)|^{2}\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}}|u_{k}(0)|^{2}+2\nu Bt+2\nu\mbox{Re}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}_{L}^{d}}\overline{u(t^{\prime},x)}\,dx\,d\beta(t^{\prime}).

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we estimate the expectation of the fourth moment:

Finally, we estimate the rightmost term in (

y\neq 0
.
Away from
\eta=T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau
, we have that

(4.66)

|{\partial}_{\eta}^{j}(e^{-\nu|y|\,|\eta-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|})|=(\nu|y|)^{j}e^{-\nu|y|\,|\eta-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}

Since
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are a linear combination of similar exponential terms,
(K.4)
follows.

Consider next
(K.5)
. As before, we may assume
y\neq 0
since the derivative vanishes when
y=0
.
Using (
4.66
) we have that

\int_{\eta>T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau+\nu^{\theta}}(\nu|y|)^{j}\,e^{-\nu|y|\,(\eta-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)}\,d\eta+\int_{\eta<T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau-\nu^{\theta}}(\nu|y|)^{j}\,e^{-\nu|y|\,(T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau-\eta)}\,d\eta=2\,(\nu|y|)^{j-1}\,e^{-\nu^{1+\theta}|y|}\lesssim(\nu\,|y|)^{j-1},

which implies
(K.5)
.

We now turn to
(K.6)
. Consider
h_{1}
first:

(4.67)

\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}y}\left[e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}+e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi+T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}-2e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}\right]\\
=\mbox{sign}(y)\,\left(-|\nu\xi-\varrho\tau|e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}-|\nu\xi+\varrho\tau|e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi+T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}+2\varrho\tau e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}\right).

We separate this expression into two summands and exploit the following cancellation between each of them:

(4.68)

|\varrho\tau e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}-|\nu\xi-\varrho\tau|e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}|\lesssim\nu|\xi|.

The above bound follows immediately if
|\xi|>T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau
(since it implies
\nu|\xi|>\varrho\tau
). If
\xi\in(-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau,T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)
we write

\varrho\tau e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}-(\varrho\tau-\nu\xi)\,e^{-\nu|y|\,(T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau-\xi)}=\frac{1}{|y|}\,[F(\eta_{1})-F(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})]

where
F(z)=ze^{-z}
,
\eta_{1}=|y\,\varrho\tau|>\eta_{2}=\nu|y\xi|
. Note that
F
is bounded for all
z\geq 0
, and thus

(4.69)

\frac{1}{|y|}\,|F(\eta_{1})-F(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})|\leq\frac{1}{|y|}\,\left|\int_{\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}}^{\eta_{1}}(e^{-z}-F(z))\,dz\right|\lesssim\frac{|\eta_{2}|}{|y|}=\nu|\xi|.

It follows that (
4.68
) holds for all
\xi
. Since
\widehat{h_{1}}(\nu\xi,y)-\widehat{h_{1}}(0,y)
is a sum of two such terms,
(K.6)
holds for the kernel
h_{1}
.

Let us now prove
(K.6)
for
h_{2}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that
y>0
. Then

(4.70)

\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}y}\left[\widehat{h_{2}}(\nu\xi,y)-\widehat{h_{2}}(0,y)\right]=\frac{{\partial}}{{\partial}y}\left[-e^{-\nu y\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}\,\mbox{sign}(\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)+e^{-\nu y\,|\xi+T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}\,\mbox{sign}(\xi+T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)-2e^{-\varrho\tau y}\right]\\
=\nu\,(\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)\,e^{-\nu y\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}-\nu\,(\xi+T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)\,e^{-\nu y\,|\xi+T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}+2\varrho\tau\,e^{-\varrho\tau y}.

The result then follows from applying (
4.68
) twice.

Let us now show
(K.7)
for both
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
. For
y\neq 0
,

\widehat{h_{1}}(0,y)=4\pi\,e^{-|y|\varrho\tau}=\mbox{sign}(y)\,\widehat{h_{2}}(0,y),

thus it suffices to note that

|{\partial}_{y}^{j}e^{-|y|\varrho\tau}|=(\varrho\tau)^{j}\,e^{-|y|\varrho\tau}=|y|^{-j}\,\left[(\varrho\tau|y|)^{j}\,e^{-|y|\varrho\tau}\right]\lesssim|y|^{-j}

given that
\eta^{j}e^{-\eta}=\mathcal{O}_{j}(1)
.

Finally, we set out to prove
(K.8)
. In order to prove (
4.48
), note that for any
R>R_{0}=\max\{T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau,\nu^{-1}\}
,

\int_{|\xi|>R}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{j}}\,(\nu\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|)^{j}e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}\,d\xi\lesssim\nu^{j}\,\int_{|\xi|>R}e^{-\nu|y|\,(\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)}\,d\xi=|y|^{-1}\,\nu^{j-1}\,e^{-\nu|y|\,(R-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau)}.

Both
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are a linear combination of such exponential terms, and thus (
4.48
) follows.

Let us turn to (
4.49
). Recall that
R_{0}=\max\{T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau,\nu^{-1}\}
. In view of the cancellation exploited in (
4.67
) and (
4.70
), it suffices to study the
y
-derivative of the expression in (
4.68
). Assuming, without loss of generality, that
y>0

{\partial}_{y}\left(\varrho\tau e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}-|\nu\xi-\varrho\tau|e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}\right)=-(\varrho\tau)^{2}e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}+|\nu\xi-\varrho\tau|^{2}e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}.

We want to integrate this quantity in the region where
|\xi|\geq\nu^{\theta}
,
|\nu\xi-\varrho\tau|\geq\nu^{\theta}
and
|\nu\xi|\leq\max(\rho\tau,1)
. We claim that

(4.71)

\Big||\nu\xi-\varrho\tau|^{2}e^{-\nu|y|\,|\xi-T_{\mathrm{kin}}\tau|}-(\varrho\tau)^{2}e^{-|y\,\varrho\tau|}\Big|\lesssim\begin{cases}\frac{\nu|\xi|}{|y|}\quad\mbox{when}\ |\nu\xi|<\varrho\tau,\\
\nu|\xi|\quad\mbox{when}\ 1>|\nu\xi|\geq\varrho\tau.\end{cases}

The first inequality follows from an argument such as (
4.69
) with
F(z)=z^{2}e^{-z}
, while the second inequality follows from a direct bound.

Using the fact that
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are linear combinations of such terms, we find for
h\in\{h_{1},h_{2}\}
and
y\neq 0
that

\begin{split}\int_{\{\nu^{\theta}<|\xi|<R_{0}\}-\nu^{-1}\mathcal{A}(h)}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\,|{\partial}_{y}^{2}[\widehat{h}(\nu\xi,y)-\widehat{h}(0,y)]|d\xi&\lesssim\max\{1,|y|^{-1}\}\,\int_{\nu^{\theta}<|\xi|<R_{0}}\frac{\nu}{|\xi|}\,d\xi\\
&\lesssim\nu\,\max\{1,|y|^{-1}\}\,\log(\nu^{-\theta}R_{0}).\end{split}

Using (
4.68
) also yields:

\int_{\{\nu^{\theta}<|\xi|<R_{0}\}-\nu^{-1}\mathcal{A}(h)}\frac{1}{|\xi|^{2}}\,|{\partial}_{y}[\widehat{h}(\nu\xi,y)-\widehat{h}(0,y)]|d\xi\lesssim\int_{\nu^{\theta}<|\xi|<R_{0}}\frac{\nu}{|\xi|}\,d\xi\lesssim\nu\,\log(\nu^{-\theta}R_{0}).

This completes the proof
(K.8)
and the claim.
∎

We are ready to give the main convergence results in the case
\varrho\gtrsim 1
.

Corollary 4.12
.

Let
T,T_{\mathrm{kin}}
be as in
Theorem

1.2
and let
0\leq tT_{\mathrm{kin}}\leq T
. Then

(i)

If
\varrho\in(0,\infty)
, then there exists some
0<\theta\ll 1
such that

(4.72)

\mathbb{E}|u_{k}(tT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}=f(t,k)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\mathcal{K}(f(t^{\prime},\cdot))(k)\,dt^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}(L^{-\theta}\,t),

where
\mathcal{K}
is as in (
1.13
) and
f
is defined in (
4.6
). As a result,
\mathbb{E}|u(k,tT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}
is well-approximated by the solution
n(t,k)
to

(4.73)

{\partial}_{t}n=\mathcal{K}(n)-2\varrho\gamma_{k}\,n+2\varrho\,b_{k}^{2},\qquad n|_{t=0}=c_{k}^{2}.

(ii)

If
\varrho\sim L^{\kappa}
for some
\kappa>0
, then there exists some
0<\theta\ll 1
such that

(4.74)

\mathbb{E}|u(k,tT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}=c_{k}^{2}\,e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}t}+\frac{b_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\left(1-e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}t}\right)+\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}(L^{-\kappa}+L^{-\theta}\,t),

which is the exact solution to

(4.75)

{\partial}_{t}n=-2\varrho\gamma_{k}\,n+2\varrho\,b_{k}^{2},\qquad n|_{t=0}=c_{k}^{2}.

Proof.

We recall that
f
defined in (
4.6
) is given by

\mathbb{E}|w_{k}^{(0)}(t)|^{2}=c_{k}^{2}\,e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}t}+\frac{b_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\left(1-e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}t}\right).

(i)
By
Lemma

4.4
, (
4.24
),
Proposition

4.5
and
Theorem

4.8
, there exists some
0<\theta\ll 1
such that

\mathbb{E}|w_{k}^{(0)}(t)+w_{k}^{(1)}(t)+w_{k}^{(2)}(t)|^{2}=f(t,k)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\mathcal{K}(f(t^{\prime},\cdot))(k)\,dt^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}\left(L^{-\theta}\,t\right).

By
Lemma

4.1
and (
4.5
), we have that if
0\leq tT_{\mathrm{kin}}\leq T
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}|u_{k}(tT_{\mathrm{kin}})|^{2}&=f(t,k)+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\mathcal{K}(f(t^{\prime},\cdot))(k)\,dt^{\prime}+\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}\left(L^{-\theta}t\right)\end{split}

Notice that the right-hand side is the first Picard iterate of the kinetic equation (
4.73
), and thus the result follows.

(ii)
If

(4.76)

f(t,k)=c_{k}^{2}\,e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}t}+\frac{b_{k}^{2}}{\gamma_{k}}\left(1-e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}t}\right)=\begin{cases}c_{k}^{2}+\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}(L^{-N})&\mbox{if}\ t\ll\varrho^{-1},\\

for

\int_{0}^{t}e^{-2\varrho\gamma_{k}(t-t^{\prime})}\,\mathcal{K}(f(t^{\prime},\cdot))(k)\,dt^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}(L^{-\kappa}).

Combining

4.5
and

\mathbb{E}|w_{k}^{(0)}(t)+w_{k}^{(1)}(t)+w_{k}^{(2)}(t)|^{2}=f(t,k)+\mathcal{O}_{\ell^{\infty}_{k}}\left(L^{-\kappa}+L^{-\theta}\,t\right).

Finally, we use

4.1

∎

4.4.
Admissible dissipations

A key tool in the proof of
Proposition

4.5
was the fact that
\Gamma_{-}^{2}+(\nu^{-1}\Omega)^{2}\geq 1
for our choice of dissipation
\gamma_{k}=(1+|k|_{\zeta}^{2})^{r}
,
r\in(0,1]
. In particular, this means that there are no points in the intersection of a neighborhood of the manifolds
\Gamma_{-}=0
and
\Omega=0
. This requirement may be weakened provided this intersection remains small:

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
T\leq L^{2}
,
L^{-2}<\alpha<1
and fix a small
\theta\in(0,1)
. Consider the dissipation
\gamma_{k}=|k|_{\zeta}^{2}
. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d}
satisfy
|k|\leq L^{\theta}
and consider the set:

\mathfrak{S}(k)=\{(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d})^{3}\ |\ k=k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3},\ k\neq k_{1},k_{3},\ |k_{j}|\leq L^{\theta},\ |\Omega_{13}^{2k}|<T^{-1},\ \Big|\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{k_{j}}-\gamma_{k}\Big|\leq\alpha\}.

Then the number of points in
\mathfrak{S}(k)
satisfies the following bound

|\mathfrak{S}(k)|\lesssim L^{d\theta}\,L^{2d}T^{-1}\,(\alpha+T^{-1})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}.

Proof.

Given that
|\Omega_{13}^{2k}|<T^{-1}
and
\gamma_{k}=|k|_{\zeta}^{2}
,

2|k_{2}|_{\zeta}^{2}-T^{-1}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{3}\gamma_{k_{j}}-\gamma_{k}=\Omega_{13}^{2k}+2|k_{2}|_{\zeta}^{2}.

This implies that
\mathfrak{S}(k)\subseteq\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}(k)
, where

\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}(k)=\{(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{L}^{d})^{3}\ |\ k=k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3},\ k\neq k_{1},k_{3},\ |k_{j}|\leq L^{\theta},\ |\Omega_{13}^{2k}|<T^{-1},\ |k_{2}|_{\zeta}^{2}\leq\frac{T^{-1}+\alpha}{2}\}\ .

It thus suffices to estimate
|\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}(k)|
. In view of the equality
\Omega_{13}^{2k}=-2(k_{1}-k)\cdot(k_{3}-k)
, we introduce the variables
p=L\,(k_{1}-k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
q=L\,(k_{3}-k)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Suppose that
p_{i}q_{i}=0
for
1\leq i\leq j
and
p_{i}q_{i}\neq 0
for
j+1\leq i\leq d
, where
j\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
. The condition
|\Omega_{13}^{2k}|<T^{-1}
implies that

(4.77)

\zeta_{d}p_{d}q_{d}=-\sum_{i=j+1}^{d-1}\zeta_{i}p_{i}\,q_{i}+\mathcal{O}(L^{2}T^{-1}).

The conditions
|k_{1}|,|k_{3}|\leq L^{\theta}
imply that each component of
p
and
q
belong to an interval of size
L^{1+\theta}
. Moreover, the condition on
|k_{2}|_{\zeta}
implies that

(4.78)

|p+q-k|_{\zeta}\lesssim L\,\sqrt{\alpha+T^{-1}}.

In particular, there are
\mathcal{O}(\min\{L^{(1+\theta)\,j},L^{j}\,(\alpha+T^{-1})^{j/2}\})
choices for the first
j
components of
p
and
q
(since
p_{i}q_{i}=0
). If
j=d
, we are done since
T\leq L^{2}
.

If
j=d-1
, (
4.77
) yields
\mathcal{O}(L^{2}T^{-1})
choices for the product
p_{d}q_{d}
. It is well known that the number of integer pairs
(p_{d},q_{d})
, each smaller than
n
, such that an
p_{d}q_{d}
is fixed is bounded by
n^{\theta}
for
\theta\in(0,1)
as small as desired (this is a consequence of the divisor bound in
\mathbb{Z}
). As a result, there are
\mathcal{O}(L^{2+\theta}T^{-1})
choices for the pair
(p_{d},q_{d})
. All in all,
|\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}(k)|=\mathcal{O}(L^{d-1}(\alpha+T^{-1})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}L^{2+\theta}T^{-1})
which concludes the proof in the case
j=d-1
.

If
j\leq d-2
, let us consider the remaining
d-j
components of
p
and
q
. For
j+1\leq i\leq d-1
, we fix
p_{i}
freely (there are
\mathcal{O}(L^{(1+\theta)(d-j-1)})
such choices). By (
4.78
),
q_{i}
lives in an interval of size
L\,\sqrt{\alpha+T^{-1}}
and thus there are
\mathcal{O}(L^{d-j-1}\,(\alpha+T^{-1})^{\frac{d-j-1}{2}})
choices for the remaining components of
q
. Finally, there are
\mathcal{O}(L^{2+\theta}T^{-1})
choices for
(p_{d},q_{d})
in view of (
4.77
). All in all,

\begin{split}|\widetilde{\mathfrak{S}}(k)|&\lesssim L^{j}\,(\alpha+T^{-1})^{j/2}\,L^{(1+\theta)(d-j-1)}\,L^{d-j-1}\,(\alpha+T^{-1})^{\frac{d-j-1}{2}}\,L^{2+\theta}T^{-1}\\
&\lesssim L^{d\theta}\,L^{2d-j}\,T^{-1}\,(\alpha+T^{-1})^{\frac{d-1}{2}}\end{split}

which is maximal when
j=0
, and yields the desired estimate.
∎

Remark 4.14
.

Using this result, we may extend cases (i) and (ii) of
Theorem

1.1
to the dissipation given by
\gamma_{k}=|k|^{2}_{\zeta}
with forcing

at

with

towards, say,

In particular, the leading terms towards the kinetic equation are given by (

) in the region
\mathfrak{S}(k)^{c}
. This is equivalent to adding a smooth cut-off
\chi(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
supported in

in all remaining calculations. Finally, one needs to verify that the term (

.

5.
Analysis of the remainder terms

In this section we prove
Proposition

2.2
. As derived in (
2.31
), the remainder satisfies the following equation:

(5.1)

\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1}=\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})+\mathcal{Q}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})+\mathcal{C}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\leq n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}\\
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})},\bm{w}^{(n_{3})})

The proof of
Proposition

2.2
is an application of the contraction mapping theorem.
The worst terms on the right-hand side of (
5.1
) are the linear ones, which are essentially given by the operators

\mathcal{IW}(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{N+1},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})}),

where
n_{1},n_{2}\leq N
. In particular, we would like to estimate the operator norm of

\bm{v}\in h^{s,b}\ \longmapsto\mathcal{IW}(\bm{v},\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})})\in h^{s,b}\ .

By
Proposition

3.6
, it suffices to study the operators with
w^{(n_{1})}
and
w^{(n_{2})}
substituted by
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{j}
is a ternary tree of order
n_{j}
,
j=1,2
. We have the following result:

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
T
and
T_{\mathrm{kin}}
are as in (
1.24
)- (
1.25
). Let
0\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N
and
\mathcal{T}_{j}
be a ternary tree of order
n_{j}
satisfying (
3.19
)-(
3.26
). Then the operators

(5.2)

\mathcal{P}_{+}:\bm{v}\mapsto\mathcal{IW}(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}},\bm{v}),\qquad\mathcal{P}_{-}:\bm{v}\mapsto\mathcal{IW}(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}},\bm{v},\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}})

satisfy the following bounds
L
-certainly

(5.3)

\left\lVert\mathcal{P}_{\pm}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}\rightarrow h^{s,b}}\leq L^{\theta}\,L^{-\delta(n_{1}+n_{2}+\frac{1}{2})}

where
0<\theta\ll 1
is as small as desired.

Let us now prove
Proposition

2.2
assuming
Proposition

5.1
.

Proof of
Proposition

2.2
.

Based on (
5.1
), it suffices to prove that the mapping

(5.4)

\bm{v}\longmapsto\mathcal{L}(\bm{v})+\mathcal{Q}(\bm{v})+\mathcal{C}(\bm{v})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\leq n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}\\
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\leq N\end{subarray}}\mathcal{IW}(\bm{w}^{(n_{1})},\bm{w}^{(n_{2})},\bm{w}^{(n_{3})})

is a contraction from the set

\left\{\bm{v}\in h^{s,b}:\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}<L^{-\delta N}\right\}

to itself. To handle the last term in (
5.4
), we note that we can replace
\bm{w}^{(n_{j})}
by
\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{j}}
for trees
\mathcal{T}_{j}
of order
n_{j}
. These trees uniquely determine a larger tree
\mathcal{T}
of order
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1
, thus
Proposition

2.1
yields:

\left\lVert\mathcal{IW}(\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}},\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}_{3}})\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}=\left\lVert\mathcal{J}_{\mathcal{T}}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}\lesssim L^{\theta+c(b-1/2)}L^{-\delta(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1)}

L
-certainly. As a consequence, the
h^{s,b}
-norm of the rightmost term in (
5.4
) is
L
-certainly bounded by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N\leq n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}\\
n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}\leq N\end{subarray}}L^{\theta+c(b-1/2)}L^{-\delta(n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}+1)}\lesssim L^{\theta+c(b-1/2)}L^{-\delta(N+1)}\ll L^{-\delta N}.

By
Proposition

5.1
, the linear term admits the following bound
L
-certainly:

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}(\bm{v})\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}\lesssim\sum_{0\leq n_{1},n_{2}\leq N}L^{\theta}\,L^{-\delta(n_{1}+n_{2}+\frac{1}{2})}\left\lVert\bm{v}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}\lesssim L^{\theta}\,L^{-\delta/2}\,L^{-\delta N}\ll L^{-\delta N}.

Finally, the quadratic and cubic terms are easy to estimate using the simple bound:

\left\lVert\mathcal{IW}(\bm{f}_{1},\bm{f}_{2},\bm{f}_{3})\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}\lesssim L^{\theta}\lambda T\,\prod_{j=1}^{3}\left\lVert\bm{f}_{j}\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}

L
-certainly for
\bm{f}_{j}\in\{\bm{v},\,\bm{w}^{(n)}\,:\,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
,
j=1,2,3
. Using this bound together with the fact that
\lambda T\leq L^{d}
, we have that,
L
-certainly,

\left\lVert\mathcal{Q}(\bm{v})\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}+\left\lVert\mathcal{C}(\bm{v})\right\rVert_{h^{s,b}}\lesssim L^{\theta+c(b-1/2)+d}L^{-2\delta N}\ll L^{-\delta N}

provided we choose
N>d/\delta
.

This shows that the operator (
5.4
)
L

Appendix A
Some useful lemmas

Let
H
be a Gaussian Hilbert space where our standard complex Wiener processes,

, and the Gaussian variables
\eta_{k}
live. We define

\mathcal{P}_{n}(H)=\{p(X_{1},\ldots,X_{m})\mid\mbox{$p$ is a polynomial of degree}\leq n;\ X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}\in H;m\in\mathbb{N}\}.

We will often consider the closure
\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}(H)}
with respect to the topology given by the inner product in

We start with a corollary of Nelson’s hypercontractivity estimate (see Theorem 5.10 and Remark 5.11 in
[
25
]
).

Lemma A.1
.

For any
1\leq p,q<\infty
we have that

(A.1)

\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq c(p,q)^{n}\,\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{p}\right)^{1/p}

for all
X\in\overline{\mathcal{P}_{n}(H)}
,
n\geq 0
.

Remark A.2
.

It can be shown that
c(2,q)\leq(q-1)^{1/2}
and
c(1,2)\leq e
, see
[
25
]
. We will often combine these and use the estimate

(A.2)

\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq(q-1)^{n/2}\,\left(\mathbb{E}|X|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq(q-1)^{n/2}\,e^{n}\,\mathbb{E}|X|.

Another important result is Isserlis’ theorem (also known as Wick’s theorem). We record it below as stated in

25
]
, Theorem 1.28.

Theorem A.3
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be centred jointly normal random variables. Then

(A.3)

\mathbb{E}\left[X_{1}\cdots X_{n}\right]=\sum\prod_{k}\mathbb{E}[X_{i_{k}}X_{j_{k}}],

where we sum over all partitions of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
into disjoint pairs
\{i_{k},j_{k}\}
.

Beyond the combinatorial objects introduced in

Proposition A.5
