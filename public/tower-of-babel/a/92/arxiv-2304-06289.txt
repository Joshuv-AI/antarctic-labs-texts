Resonances at the Threshold for Pauli Operators in Dimension Two Thanks: ©2023 by the authors. Faithful reproduction of this article, in its entirety, by any means is permitted for non-commercial purposes.
Jonathan Breuer, Hynek Kovařík

Abstract
. It is well-known that, due to the interaction between the spin and the magnetic field, the two-dimensional Pauli operator has an eigenvalue 0 0 at the threshold of its essential spectrum. We show that when perturbed by an effectively positive perturbation, V V , coupled with a small parameter ε \varepsilon , these eigenvalues become resonances. Moreover, we derive explicit expressions for the leading terms of their imaginary parts in the limit ε ↘ 0 \varepsilon\searrow 0 . These show, in particular, that the dependence of the imaginary part of the resonances on ε \varepsilon is determined by the flux of the magnetic field. The cases of non-degenerate and degenerate zero eigenvalue are treated separately. We also discuss applications of our main results to particles with anomalous magnetic moments.



Keywords:
resonances, spin, threshold eigenvalue, anomalous magnetic moment.

35Q40, 35P05, 81Q10

1.
Introduction and outline of the main results

1.1.
Basic set up

In this paper we consider Hamiltonians associated to fermions with spin
\frac{1}{2}
interacting with a magnetic
field perpendicular to the plane in which the particle is confined. Such a magnetic field can be identified with a function

B:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
. If
A:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a magnetic vector potential satisfying
{\rm curl}\,A=B
, then the corresponding non-relativistic Hamiltonian is
given by the Pauli operator

P(A)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)&0\\
0&P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(A)\end{pmatrix}\,\qquad\text{with}\quad P_{\scriptscriptstyle\pm}(A):=(i\nabla+A)^{2}\pm B,

(1.1)

acting in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})
. This operator is essentially self-adjoint on
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})
under mild regularity assumptions on
B
.
The operator
P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(A)
(
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
), acting on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, denotes the restriction of the Pauli operator to the spin-down (respectively,
spin-up) subspace.
We refer to
[
21
]
for further reading.

When
B
vanishes at infinity, which will be the case under our assumptions, then

\sigma\,(P(A))=\sigma\,_{\rm es}(P(A))=[0,\infty).

(1.2)

The latter follows e.g. from
[
2
, Thm. 6.5]
. Moreover, the well-known Aharonov-Casher Theorem,
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
16
]
, states that if

B\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
if the flux

\alpha:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}B(x)\,dx\,

(1.3)

satisfies
|\alpha|>1
, then zero is an eigenvalue of
P(A)
. More precisely, zero is an eigenvalue of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
if
\alpha>1
and of

P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(A)

if
\alpha<-1
, see Section
2.5
for details. The multiplicity
N
of this eigenvalue is given by

N=[\,|\alpha|\,]\quad\text{if}\ \ \alpha\not\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad N=|\alpha|-1\quad\text{if}\ \ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z},

(1.4)

where
[x]
denotes the integer part of
x
.

Now let
V_{1},V_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
be polynomially decaying functions, and let
\varepsilon>0
. We denote by

P_{\varepsilon}(A)=P(A)+\varepsilon\mathcal{V},\qquad\mathcal{V}=\begin{pmatrix}V_{1}&0\\
0&V_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

the perturbed Pauli operator. Since
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
is a diagonal matrix operator and we focus on only one of its entries, there is no loss of
generality if we set

V_{1}=V_{2}=V.

If the perturbation
V
is negative, then the threshold eigenvalue of
P(A)
gives rise to discrete negative eigenvalues of
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
.
Asymptotic expansion of such eigenvalues in the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0
for radial
B
and
V
was found by variational methods in
[
7
]
,
see also
[
3
]
.

In this paper we address a different question. Namely, what happens with the zero eigenvalue of
P(A)
when the perturbation
V
is positive?
Clearly,
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
has no negative eigenvalues in this case, and under very mild decay and regularity conditions on
B
and
V
it cannot
have positive eigenvalues either, see
[
9
,
2
]
. Hence if zero does not remain an eigenvalue of
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
, then it is natural to expect
that it turns into a
resonance
.
It is well-known, however, that there is no unique definition of the latter. Here we adopt the dynamical approach of
[
15
]
, see also

[
8
,
10
,
20
,
17
,
18
,
22
]
, in which a resonance manifests itself by a (quasi)-exponential decay of the associated survival
probability.

More precisely, by a
resonance
of
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
we understand a
pair

(\Psi,\lambda_{\varepsilon})
such that
\|\Psi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})}=1
,
\lambda_{\varepsilon}=x_{\varepsilon}-i\gamma_{\varepsilon}
with
\gamma_{\varepsilon}>0
, and such that

\mathcal{\big\langle}\Psi,\,e^{-itP_{\varepsilon}(A)}\,\Psi\mathcal{\big\rangle}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})}=e^{-it\lambda_{\varepsilon}}+\delta(t,\varepsilon)\qquad\forall\,t>0,

(1.5)

where

\delta_{\varepsilon}:=\sup_{t>0}|\delta(t,\varepsilon)|\to 0\qquad\text{as}\qquad\varepsilon\searrow 0.

(1.6)

We call
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
the
resonance position
and
\Psi
the
resonance eigenstates
.
Owing to the Aharonov-Casher Theorem, the natural candidates for the latter are

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
0\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{if}\ \ \alpha>0,\qquad\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\psi\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{if}\ \ \alpha<0,

(1.7)

where
\psi
is a zero eigenfunction of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
respectively
P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(A)
. Note that equation (
1.5
) cannot hold without the error
term
\delta(t,\varepsilon)
since the survival probability can decay exponentially only for
t
neither too small nor too large,
cf.
[
18
, Sec. 2]
.

1.2.
Main results

Our goal is to prove the existence of resonances, in the sense of equation (
1.5
), and to derive an explicit expression for the
leading term in the asymptotic expansion of
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Our results are described precisely in Theorems
3.1
,
3.10
,
3.16
,
4.3
,
4.5
, and
4.6
. We describe them briefly here.

In view of first order perturbation theory it is not
surprising that the real part of
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

x_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\Psi,\mathcal{V}\,\Psi\mathcal{\big\rangle}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})}+o(\varepsilon),

where the first term on the right hand side is positive by assumption.

A much more important quantity is the imaginary part of
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
, also known as the width,
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, since it determines the rate
of
decay of the survival probability
|\mathcal{\big\langle}\Psi,\,e^{-itP_{\varepsilon}(A)}\,\Psi\mathcal{\big\rangle}|^{2}
, see equation (
1.5
), and consequently the mean
lifetime of the resonance.

It turns out that the dependence of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
on
\varepsilon
is determined by the flux (
1.3
). In order to describe its asymptotic
behavior more in detail, it is convenient to denote by

\mu=\min_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\alpha-k|\in[0,1/2\,]\\

(1.8)

the distance between
\alpha
and
\mathbb{Z}
. Since the operator
P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(-A)
is unitarily equivalent to
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
, we may assume that

\alpha>0.

(1.9)

We now distinguish various cases of asymptotic behavior of
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
depending on the value of
\alpha
.

Non-degenerate threshold eigenvalue

If zero is a simple eigenvalue of
P(A)
, then by equation (
1.4
) we must
have

\alpha\leq 2
. Our result in the case of non-integer flux, i.e. for
1<\alpha<2
, then says that
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
has a resonance which satisfies

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\Gamma_{1}\,\varepsilon^{\alpha}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\mu})\big)+\Gamma_{2}\,\varepsilon^{3-\alpha}\,\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\mu})\big),

(1.10)

where the coefficients
\Gamma_{1}>0
and
\Gamma_{2}\geq 0
are computed in Theorem
3.1
, see in particular equation
(
3.20
). The corresponding error term (
1.6
) satisfies
\delta_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\mu})
.
Although generically
\Gamma_{2}>0
, in certain cases
\Gamma_{2}
may vanish. This happens for example when both
B
and
V
are radially
symmetric, cf. Remark
3.9
. Notice that if
\Gamma_{2}=0
, then the power of
\varepsilon
in (
1.10
) covers, by varying

\alpha
, the whole interval
]1,2[
.
On the other hand, if
\Gamma_{2}>0
, then the power of
\varepsilon
of the leading term in (
1.10
) attains its maximum for

\alpha=\frac{3}{2}
, in which case we have
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\,\sim\,\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}
. The same coupling constant
dependence, i.e.
\varepsilon^{\frac{3}{2}}
, is generically manifested by resonances arising from threshold eigenvalues of non-magnetic Schrödinger
operators in
dimension three,
[
10
, Thm. 5.1]
. For comparison, resonances arising from embedded eigenvalues of
Schrödinger operators typically satisfy the asymptotical law
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\,\sim\,\varepsilon^{2}
predicted by the Fermi Golden Rule, see
e.g.
[
17
, Sec. 4]
.

In the borderline case
\alpha=2
one has
\mu=0
and equation (
1.10
) is replaced by

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\Gamma_{3}\,\varepsilon^{2}+\Gamma_{4}\,\frac{\varepsilon}{(\log\varepsilon)^{2}}\,\big[1+\mathcal{O}\big(|\log\varepsilon|^{-1}\big)\big],

(1.11)

with
\Gamma_{3}>0,\ \Gamma_{4}\geq 0
, and with an error term which satisfies
\delta_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{O}(|\log\varepsilon|^{-1})
if
\Gamma_{4}>0,

and
\delta_{\varepsilon}=o(1)

if
\Gamma_{4}=0
. Explicit expressions for the coefficients
b_{3}
and
\Gamma_{4}
are given in Theorems
3.10
and
3.16
.
Similarly as in equation (
1.10
) the
coefficient
\Gamma_{4}
vanishes when both
B
and
V
are radial. The resonance width then satisfies
\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\Gamma_{3}\,\varepsilon^{2}
without
any error term, cf. Remark
3.17
.

Remark 1.1
.

We do not make any statement about the optimality of the upper bounds on the error term
\delta_{\varepsilon}
in the above results. In general it is
possible to
improve
\delta_{\varepsilon}

by replacing
\Psi
in equation (
1.5
) with a suitable sequence
\Psi_{\varepsilon}
satisfying
\|\Psi_{\varepsilon}-\Psi\|_{2}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. Such an analysis was carried out for Schrödinger operators with dilation-analytic potentials in

[
8
]
, and for more general potentials in
[
4
]
. We do not address this issue in the present paper.

An interesting application of our main results concerns particles with
anomalous magnetic moments
. The standard value of the magnetic
moment of a fermion with spin
\frac{1}{2}
predicted by the Dirac equation is equal to
2
. However, there exist fermions whose magnetic
moment differs slightly from this value. It follows from our results that
the Pauli operator then may have a resonance even if
V=0
, see Section
3.4
.

Degenerate threshold eigenvalue

The situation in the case in which zero is a degenerate eigenvalue of
P(A)
is, as expected, much more delicate. As suggested in
[
11
]
,
the natural candidates for the resonance
eigenstates are the eigenfunctions of the operator
{\rm P_{0}}V{\rm P_{0}}
, where
{\rm P_{0}}
is the projection onto the zero eigenspace. The crucial hypothesis
on
V
now requires that this operator admits an eigenfunction
\psi_{0}^{(V)}
with a simple
and positive eigenvalue. In Section
4
we show that, under certain additional assumptions,
\psi_{0}^{(V)}
is a resonance
eigenstate of the operator
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
,
and we calculate the asymptotic formula for its position
\lambda_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
, see Theorems
4.3
,
4.5
and

4.6
.

Moreover, in Section
4.4
we treat an explicit example in which zero is a doubly degenerate eigenvalue of
P(A)
, and in which
the operator
P_{\varepsilon}(A)
admits two resonances
whose widths have different orders of magnitude in
\varepsilon
, see equations (
4.28
), (
4.29
) and (
4.30
). For general
values of
\alpha
, when
0
is
an eigenvalue of
P(A)
of multiplicity
N
, see (
1.4
), the results of Section
4
guarantee, under suitable
assumptions on the eigenfunction of
{\rm P_{0}}V{\rm P_{0}}
, existence of
N
resonances, cf. Remark
4.7
.

Remark 1.2
.

The approach of this paper can be applied also in the relativistic description of the problem under consideration, i.e. when the Hamiltonian
is given by the two-dimensional Dirac operator. Resonances of the latter will be treated in a forthcoming paper.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

In the next section we state our main hypothesis on
B
and collect some preliminary results which will be needed later. The main results and

non-degenerate threshold eigenvalue are presented in Section
3
. Resonances arising from a degenerate eigenvalue are treated in

.
In Section

2.
Preliminaries and notation

2.1.
Hypothesis

We will work under the following decay condition on
B
.

Assumption 2.1
.

The function
B:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and satisfies

|B(x)|\ \lesssim\ (1+|x|^{2})^{-\rho}.

(2.1)

for some
\rho>7/2
.

Let us comment on the decay condition stated in Assumption
2.1
. The upper bound (
2.1
) turns out to be a sufficient
condition for the resolvent expansion of the Pauli
operator
P(A)
at threshold, cf.
[
12
, Sec. 5]
, which is one of the main tools of our proofs. It is natural to expect, though, that
the results presented here should hold
under weaker decay assumptions on
B
, in particular for any magnetic field with finite flux.

2.2.
Notation

We will need the resolvent expansion of the operator
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
in the topology of weighted Sobolev spaces
{\rm H}^{k,s}

equipped with the norm

\|u\|_{{\rm H}^{k,s}}=\|\,\langle x\rangle^{s}(1-\Delta)^{k/2}\,u\|_{\textsf{L}\!^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.\qquad k\in\mathbb{Z},\ s\in\mathbb{R},

(2.2)

where
\langle x\rangle=(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}
. By

\mathscr{B}(k,s;k^{\prime},s^{\prime})=\mathscr{B}({\rm H}^{k,s};{\rm H}^{k^{\prime},s^{\prime}})

we denote the space of bounded linear operators from
{\rm H}^{k,s}
into
{\rm H}^{k^{\prime},s^{\prime}},
and for
k=0
we use the shorthands

\|u\|_{{\rm H}^{0,s}}=\|u\|_{2,s}=\|\,\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle^{s}\,u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})},\qquad L^{2,s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\,={\rm H}^{0,s},\qquad\mathscr{B}(0,s;0,s^{\prime})=\mathscr{B}(s;s^{\prime})\,,

where
\langle x\rangle=(1+|x|^{2})^{1/2}
. We define the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to be linear in the second argument, i.e. we set

\mathcal{\big\langle}f,g\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bar{f}\,g\,.

Given a set
M
and two functions
f_{1},\,f_{2}:M\to\mathbb{R}
, we write
f_{1}(m)\lesssim f_{2}(m)
if there exists a numerical constant
c
such that

f_{1}(m)\leq c\,f_{2}(m)
for all
m\in M
. The symbol
f_{1}(m)\gtrsim f_{2}(m)
is defined analogously. Moreover, we use the notation

f_{1}(m)\,\sim\,f_{2}(m)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f_{1}(m)\lesssim f_{2}(m)\ \wedge\ f_{2}(m)\lesssim f_{1}(m),

Finally, by
\mathds{1}_{M}
we denote the characteristic function of
M
.

2.3.
The SLFG formula

Identity (
2.5
) below is known in the literature as Schur-Livsic-Feshbach-Grushin (shortly SLFG) formula, see e.g.
[
10
,
19
]
.
Since we will use it on various occasions, we briefly recall it. Assume that a Hilbert space
\mathscr{H}
is a direct sum of two Hilbert
spaces;
\mathscr{H}=\mathscr{H}_{1}\oplus\mathscr{H}_{2}
. Let
a
be a closed operator on
\mathscr{H}
. Then
a
can be represented in the
matrix form

a=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}.\\

(2.3)

Suppose moreover that
a_{22}
is boundedly invertible on
\mathscr{H}_{2}
. Then
a
is invertible if and only if its Schur complement

S=a_{11}-a_{12}\,a_{22}^{-1}\,a_{21}

(2.4)

is invertible. In that case we have

a^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}\\
b_{21}&b_{22}\end{pmatrix},\\

(2.5)

where

\displaystyle b_{11}

\displaystyle=S^{-1},\qquad b_{12}=-S^{-1}\,a_{12}\,a_{22}^{-1}\,,\qquad b_{21}=-a_{22}^{-1}\,a_{21}\,S^{-1},\qquad b_{22}=a_{22}^{-1}-a_{22}^{-1}\,a_{21}\,S^{-1}\,a_{12}\,a_{22}^{-1}\,.

2.4.
The gauge

Since the definition of the resonance is gauge invariant, see equation (
1.5
), we may choose any vector
potential
A
which satisfies
{\rm curl}\,A=B
. In order to make a suitable choice of
A
we let

h(x)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}B(y)\log|x-y|\,dy.\\

(2.6)

We then have
-\Delta h=B
, which implies that the vector potential

A_{h}=\big(\partial_{2}h\,,\,-\partial_{1}h\big)\,

(2.7)

satisfies

{\rm curl}\,A_{h}=-\Delta h=B.

(2.8)

Moreover, we define
the reference vector potential

A_{0}(x)=\alpha\,(-x_{2},x_{1})\min\big\{\,|x|^{-1},\,|x|^{-2}\big\}\,.

(2.9)

Obviously,
{\rm curl}\,A_{0}\neq B
, but a short calculation shows that
A_{0}
generates a magnetic field with the same flux as
B
. For later purposes
we need to choose
A
which is close enough to
A_{0}
at infinity.
To do so we make use of the gauge transformation function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
constructed in
[
12
, Prop. 2.2]
, for which

|A_{h}(x)+\nabla\chi(x)-A_{0}(x)|\ \lesssim\ \langle x\rangle^{-\rho+1}

(2.10)

holds whenever
B
satisfies Assumption
2.1
. In the sequel we thus put

A=A_{h}+\nabla\chi,

(2.11)

so that
{\rm curl}\,A=B
, see equation (
2.8
). Notice also that for radial
B
one may choose
\chi=0
,
[
12
, Sec. 3.4]
.

Remark 2.2
.

In the proofs of our results we will use the vector potential given by (
2.11
). Recall however that the survival probability is
gauge-invariant.

2.5.
The Aharonov-Casher states

Let us describe the structure of the zero-eigenspace of the Pauli operator in the gauge
A
defined in (
2.11
). The
Aharonov-Casher Theorem implies that
\Psi
is a zero-eigenfunction
of
P(A)
if and only if

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\psi(x)=e^{i\chi(x)+h(x)}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,c_{j}\,(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{j-1}\qquad\text{for}\ \ \alpha>0

(2.12)

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\psi\end{pmatrix},\qquad\psi(x)=e^{i\chi(x)-h(x)}\,\sum_{j=1}^{N}\,c_{j}\,(x_{1}-ix_{2})^{j-1}\qquad\text{for}\ \ \alpha<0,

see
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
16
]
. Here
N
is given by (
1.4
), and
c_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
are arbitrary. From (
2.6
) we deduce
that

h(x)=-\alpha\log|x|+\mathcal{O}(|x|^{-1})\,,\qquad|x|\to\infty.

(2.13)

This shows, in particular, that
\Psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
Apart from the zero-eigenfunctions we need also the so-called
zero virtual bound states, i.e. solutions of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)\,\varphi=0
which satisfy
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\setminus L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. For
\alpha>0
these solutions are given by

\varphi(x)=e^{i\chi(x)+h(x)}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\,c_{j}\,(x_{1}-ix_{2})^{j-1}\quad\text{if}\ \alpha\not\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\varphi(x)=e^{i\chi(x)+h(x)}\sum_{j=1}^{N+2}\,c_{j}\,(x_{1}-ix_{2})^{j-1}\quad\text{if}\ \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2.14)

Since the flux is assumed to be positive, the Aharonov-Casher Theorem says that
P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(A)
has no eigenvalues, and we may thus restrict our
attention to the operator
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
.
This means that we will study a resonance behavior of the operator

P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)=(i\nabla+A)^{2}-B+\varepsilon V

in the limit
\varepsilon\searrow 0
.

In what follows we denote by
{\rm P_{0}}
the orthogonal projection on the zero
eigenspace of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
.

2.6.
Resolvent expansion of

With this notation we state

.

in
\mathscr{B}(-1,s;1,-s),\,s>3,

.
Here

is a constant which is equal to

,

{\alpha^{\prime}}=\alpha-N

(2.17)

is the fractional part of
\alpha
,

,
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a specific zero eigenfunction of

, and

Note that, in order to avoid confusion, we have slightly changed the notation in

\psi=d\,\|e^{h}\|_{2}\,\psi^{\scriptscriptstyle-},

Remark 2.4
.

If

3.
Resonances arising from a non-degenerate threshold eigenvalue

In this section we assume that the zero eigenvalue of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
is simple. By equation (
1.4
) this is equivalent
to the
condition

1<\alpha\leq 2.

(3.1)

Equation (
2.12
) implies that the associated zero-eigenfunction is, in the gauge (
2.11
), given by

\psi_{0}=c_{0}\,e^{h+i\chi}\ ,

(3.2)

where
c_{0}
is chosen so that
\|\psi_{0}\|_{2}=1
.
Obviously, we then have

{\rm P_{0}}=\psi_{0}\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}\,,\,\cdot\,\mathcal{\big\rangle}.

3.1.
Survival probability

To analyze the amplitude of the survival probability we use the representation

\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,e^{-itP_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\lim_{y\to 0+}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-itx}\,{\rm Im}\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,(P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)-x-iy)^{-1}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\,dx,

(3.3)

and show that if we replace the integrand in the above equation by the Lorentzian peak

\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\,,

with suitably chosen
x_{\varepsilon}
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
,
then we make an error
\delta(t,\varepsilon)
which tends to zero as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
uniformly in
t>0
. As noted in the Introduction we
work under a positivity assumption on
V
. In fact, we
do not require that
V>0
hold pointwise. It suffices to assume that

\beta:=\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},V\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}>0.

(3.4)

We also factorize
V
as follows;

V=vUv,\qquad\text{where}\ \ v=\sqrt{|V|}\,,\quad\text{and}\ \ \ U(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad}l}-1&\quad\text{if} \ \ V(x)<0\ ,\\
1&\quad\text{elsewhere}\ .\end{array}\right.

(3.5)

We will use the SLFG formula in order to express
\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,(P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)-z)^{-1}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}
as the resolvent of an effective

z
-dependent (one-dimensional) operator, by taking
\mathscr{H}_{1}=P_{0}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\mathscr{H}_{2}=Q_{0}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proceeding as in
[
10
, Sec. 2]
, we let
Q_{0}=I-P_{0}
and denote by

\displaystyle R(z)

\displaystyle=Q_{0}(Q_{0}P_{-}(A,\varepsilon)Q_{0}-z)^{-1}Q_{0}

\displaystyle R_{0}(z)

\displaystyle=Q_{0}(Q_{0}P_{-}(A)Q_{0}-z)^{-1}Q_{0}=Q_{0}(P_{-}(A)-z)^{-1}Q_{0}

the inverses of the restrictions of
P_{-}(A,\varepsilon)
and
P_{-}(A)
(respectively) to the orthogonal subspace to
\psi_{0}
. The resolvent
equation says

\begin{split}R(z)&=R_{0}(z)-\varepsilon R_{0}(z)VR(z)=R_{0}(z)-\varepsilon R(z)VR_{0}(z)=R_{0}(z)-R_{0}(z)\left(\varepsilon V-\varepsilon^{2}VR(z)V\right)R_{0}(z)\\
&=R_{0}(z)-\varepsilon R_{0}(z)v\left(U-\varepsilon UvR(z)vU\right)vR_{0}(z),\end{split}

(3.6)

and this can be combined with

\left(U+\varepsilon vR_{0}(z)v\right)\left(U-\varepsilon UvR(z)vU\right)=I

(which can be seen by multiplying the first equality in (
3.6
) by
\varepsilon vU
on the right and remembering that

U^{2}=I
) to show that

R(z)=R_{0}(z)-\varepsilon R_{0}(z)v\left(U+\varepsilon vR_{0}(z)v\right)^{-1}vR_{0}(z).

(3.7)

The Schur complement, see equation (
2.4
), is then given by

S(z)=F(z,\varepsilon)\,P_{0},

(3.8)

where

\displaystyle F(z,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\beta\varepsilon-z-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},vU\big(G(z)-\varepsilon G(z)[U+\varepsilon G(z)]^{-1}G(z)\big)Uv\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\,,

(3.9)

and

G(z)=vR_{0}(z)v=v(P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)-z)^{-1}\,v+z^{-1}\,v\,{\rm P_{0}}\,v\,.

(3.10)

Equation (
2.5
) thus gives

\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,(P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)-z)^{-1}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\frac{1}{F(z,\varepsilon)}.

(3.11)

3.2.
Non-integer flux

We recall the resolvent expansion presented in Proposition
2.3
. Since

1<\alpha<2
and
{\rm P_{0}}=\psi_{0}\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}\,,\,\cdot\,\mathcal{\big\rangle}
, equation (
2.16
) takes the form

(P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)-z)^{-1}=-z^{-1}{\rm P_{0}}+\frac{\omega(\alpha)\,z^{\alpha-2}}{1+\omega(\alpha)\,z^{\alpha-1}}\,\psi_{0}\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}\,,\,\cdot\,\mathcal{\big\rangle}-\frac{\zeta(\alpha-1)\,z^{1-\alpha}}{1+\varrho\,\zeta(\alpha-1)\,z^{2-\alpha}}\,\varphi\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\varphi,\,\cdot\,\mathcal{\big\rangle}\ +S_{0}+o(1)\\

(3.12)

in
\mathscr{B}(-1,s;1,-s),\,s>3,
as
z\to 0
. To simplify the notation in what follows let us denote

\eta\,=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{4^{\alpha}\,\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)\,\|e^{h}\|_{2}^{2}}\ ,\qquad\ \sigma\,=\frac{4^{\alpha}}{16\,\Gamma^{2}(2-\alpha)}\\
,

(3.13)

and

\omega=\omega(\alpha),\qquad\zeta=\zeta(\alpha-1),

We then define the function
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

g(x)=\frac{\eta\,\beta^{2}x^{\alpha-2}}{|1+\omega\,x^{\alpha-1}|^{2}}+\frac{\sigma\,|{\rm w}|^{2}x^{1-\alpha}}{|1+\varrho\,\zeta\,x^{2-\alpha}|^{2}}\,,

(3.14)

where

{\rm w}=\mathcal{\big\langle}V\psi_{0},\varphi\mathcal{\big\rangle}\,,

(3.15)

and where
\varrho
is the constant in Proposition
2.3
.
The asymptotic behavior of
g(x)
for
x\searrow 0
depends on whether
{\rm w}=0
or not. Therefore we set

\nu=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad}l}\mu&\quad\text{if} \ {\rm w}\neq 0\ ,\\[4.0pt]
\alpha-1&\quad\text{if}\ \ {\rm w}=0\ .\end{array}\right.

(3.16)

Now we can state the result in the case of non-integer flux.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
1<\alpha<2
, let
B
satisfy Assumption (
2.1
) and assume that
V\lesssim\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle^{-\rho}
for
some
\rho>6
. Suppose moreover that (
3.4
) holds. Then there exists a constant
C
such that for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
we
have

\sup_{t>0}\big|\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,e^{-itP_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}-e^{-it(x_{\varepsilon}-i\gamma_{\varepsilon})}\big|\,\leq\,C\,\varepsilon^{\mu},

(3.17)

where
x_{\varepsilon}
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
satisfy

\displaystyle x_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\beta\,\varepsilon\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\nu})\big)

(3.18)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}g(x_{\varepsilon})\,.

(3.19)

Equation (
3.19
) gives

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\eta\,\beta^{\alpha}\varepsilon^{\alpha}\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\alpha-1})\big)+\sigma\,\beta^{1-\alpha}\,|{\rm w}|^{2}\,\varepsilon^{3-\alpha}\,\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2-\alpha})\big).

(3.20)

Hence
generically, i.e. when
{\rm w}\neq 0
, the resonance width satisfies
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\,\sim\,\varepsilon^{1+\mu}
.

Remark 3.2
.

The decay condition on
V
in Theorem
3.1
, and all the subsequent theorems, is dictated by the condition
s>3
in Proposition

2.3
. Indeed,
since
v\lesssim\langle\,\cdot\,\rangle^{-\rho}
with
\rho>3
, see (
3.5
), we can insert the asymptotic expansion
(
3.12
) into equation (
3.10
) and expand
G(z)
for
z\to 0
in the uniform operator topology on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Remark 3.3
.

Condition (
3.4
) is in fact necessary for the existence of a resonance. Indeed, if
\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},V\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\leq 0
, then zero turns
into a negative discrete eigenvalue of

P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)
. This follows by a simple test function argument.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
will be subdivided into a series of auxiliary lemmas. First, we denote

J_{\varepsilon}:=\Big(\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{2}\,,\,\frac{3\beta\varepsilon}{2}\Big),

(3.21)

and following
[
10
]
we split the integration in (
3.3
) by setting

p_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\,\lim_{y\to 0+}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,{\rm Im}\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,(P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)-x-iy)^{-1}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\,dx,

(3.22)

where
I_{\varepsilon}\subset J_{\varepsilon}
is an interval which will be specified later. The essential idea of the proof is to show that the above
contribution to (
3.3
) will give rise to the exponential
e^{-it(x_{\varepsilon}-i\gamma_{\varepsilon})}
plus an error term which will be absorbed,
together with the
integral over
\mathbb{R}\setminus I_{\varepsilon}
, into
\delta(\varepsilon,t)
.
Combining (
3.11
) and (
3.22
) we get

p_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,\lim_{y\to 0+}\,{\rm Im}\,\Big(\,\frac{1}{F(x+iy,\varepsilon)}\,\Big)\,dx.

(3.23)

Let

\displaystyle K(z,\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\beta\varepsilon-z-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},VH(z)V\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\beta\varepsilon-z-\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\,\frac{\omega\,\beta^{2}\,z^{\alpha-2}}{1+\omega\,z^{\alpha-1}}-\frac{\zeta\,|{\rm w}|^{2}\,z^{1-\alpha}}{1+\varrho\,\zeta\,z^{2-\alpha}}\Big),

(3.24)

where

H(z)=\frac{\omega\,z^{\alpha-2}}{1+\omega\,z^{\alpha-1}}\ {\rm P_{0}}-\frac{\zeta\,z^{1-\alpha}}{1+\varrho\,\zeta\,z^{2-\alpha}}\ \varphi\,\mathcal{\big\langle}\varphi,\,\cdot\,\mathcal{\big\rangle}\ .\\

(3.25)

Convention

The absence of positive eigenvalues of
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)
and the limiting absorption principle imply the existence of the limit

G(x)=\lim_{y\to 0+}G(x+iy),\qquad x\in(0,\infty)

in the uniform operator topology on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Consequently, in view of (
3.23
), we define

F(x,\varepsilon)=\lim_{y\to 0+}F(x+iy,\varepsilon)\,,\qquad x>0.

(3.26)

The same notation will be adapted for the quantity
K(x,\varepsilon)
defined by
\lim_{y\to 0+}K(x+iy,\varepsilon)
, cf. (
3.24
).

The first step in the proof of Theorem
3.1
is to show that
K(x,\varepsilon)
is a good approximation of
F(x,\varepsilon)
for
x
belonging to the interval
J_{\varepsilon}
.
In fact,
from (
3.9
) and (
3.24
) we deduce that

F(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{3}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},vUG(z)[U+\varepsilon G(x)]^{-1}G(x)\,Uv\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

(3.27)

We then have

Lemma 3.4
.

For
\varepsilon
small enough

\sup_{x\in J_{\varepsilon}}|F(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)|\ \lesssim\ \varepsilon^{1+2\nu}+\varepsilon^{2}\,.

(3.28)

Proof.

As explained in Remark
3.2
the decay assumption on
V
allows to combine equations (
3.10
) and (
3.12
). This
gives

\varepsilon\|G(x)\|_{2\to 2}=\varepsilon\|vH(x)v\|_{2\to 2}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\alpha-1}+\varepsilon^{2-\alpha}.

Hence
U+\varepsilon G(x)
is boundedly invertible for
x\in J_{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon
small enough.
From Proposition
2.3
we then get

\varepsilon^{3}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},vUG(x)[U+\varepsilon G(x)]^{-1}G(x)\,Uv\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2\alpha-1})+|{\rm w}|^{2}\,\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{5-2\alpha})\,.

In view of (
3.27
) this implies the claim.
∎

Next we separate
K
into real and imaginary parts which we denote by
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
respectively;

K(x,\varepsilon)=K_{1}(x,\varepsilon)+iK_{2}(x,\varepsilon).

The identity

\Gamma(1-z)\,\Gamma(z)=\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi z)}\qquad z\not\in\mathbb{Z}

(3.29)

and equation (
2.15
) imply

{\rm Im}\Big(\frac{\omega\,x^{\alpha-2}}{1+\omega\,x^{\alpha-1}}\Big)=\frac{\eta\,x^{\alpha-2}}{|1+\omega\,x^{\alpha-1}|^{2}}\,,\qquad{\rm Im}\Big(\frac{\zeta\,x^{1-\alpha}}{1+\varrho\,\zeta\,x^{2-\alpha}}\Big)=\frac{-\sigma\,x^{1-\alpha}}{|1+\varrho\,\zeta\,x^{2-\alpha}|^{2}}\,.

From (
3.14
) and (
3.24
) we thus get

K_{2}(x,\varepsilon)=-\varepsilon^{2}g(x).

(3.30)

As for the real part of
K
, we note the following result.

Lemma 3.5
.

We have

\sup_{x\in J_{\varepsilon}}|\,K_{1}(x,\varepsilon)-\beta\varepsilon+x\,|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1+\nu}).

(3.31)

Furthermore, for
\varepsilon
small enough there exits a unique
x_{\varepsilon}\in J_{\varepsilon}
such that
K_{1}(x_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon)=0
.

Proof.

By (
3.24
) we have

K_{1}(x,\varepsilon)=\beta\varepsilon-x-\varepsilon^{2}f(x),

(3.32)

where

f(x)=\beta^{2}\ \frac{{\rm Re}(\omega)\,x^{\alpha-2}+|\omega|^{2}\,x^{2\alpha-3}}{|1+\omega\,x^{\alpha-1}|^{2}}-|{\rm w}|^{2}\,\frac{{\rm Re}(\zeta)\,x^{1-\alpha}+\varrho|\zeta|^{2}\,x^{3-2\alpha}}{|1+\varrho\,\zeta\,x^{2-\alpha}|^{2}}\,.

(3.33)

One easily verifies that

\sup_{J_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}\,|f^{(j)}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1+\nu-j})\,,\qquad j=0,1,2.

(3.34)

This implies (
3.31
). Hence for
\varepsilon
small enough we have
K_{1}(\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{2},\varepsilon)>0
,
K_{1}(\frac{3\beta\varepsilon}{2},\varepsilon)<0
, and

\partial_{x}K_{1}(x,\varepsilon)\leq-\frac{1}{2}\ ,\qquad\forall\,x\in J_{\varepsilon}.

(3.35)

This proves the second part of the claim.
∎

Lemma
3.5
obviously implies that

x_{\varepsilon}=\beta\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1+\nu}).

Therefore we set

I_{\varepsilon}=\Big[x_{\varepsilon}-\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4},\,x_{\varepsilon}+\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4}\,\Big]\\

(3.36)

and note that for
\varepsilon
sufficiently small

.

Lemma 3.6
.

For
\varepsilon
small enough,

\sup_{t>0}\Big|\,p_{\varepsilon}(t)-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,{\rm Im}\,\Big[\,\frac{1}{K(x,\varepsilon)}\,\Big]\,dx\,\Big|\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\mu}.

Proof.

By (
3.28
)

\Big|\frac{1}{F(x,\varepsilon)}-\frac{1}{K(x,\varepsilon)}\Big|\ \lesssim\ \frac{\varepsilon^{1+2\nu}+\varepsilon^{2}}{|F(x,\varepsilon)K(x,\varepsilon)|}\ \lesssim\ \frac{\varepsilon^{1+2\nu}+\varepsilon^{2}}{|K(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}}\,.\\

On the other hand, equations (
3.30
) and (
3.14
) imply that

|K_{2}(x,\varepsilon)|\,\gtrsim\,\varepsilon^{1+\nu}

and, by (
3.35
),

|K_{1}(x,\varepsilon)|\,\gtrsim\,\,|x-x_{\varepsilon}|\,.

(3.37)

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\Big|\frac{1}{F(x,\varepsilon)}-\frac{1}{K(x,\varepsilon)}\Big|\,dx

\displaystyle\ \lesssim\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varepsilon^{1+2\nu}+\varepsilon^{2}}{|K(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}}\,dx\ \lesssim\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varepsilon^{1+2\nu}+\varepsilon^{2}}{x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2+2\nu}}\ dx\ \lesssim\ \varepsilon^{\mu},

and the result follows from (
3.23
).
∎

Now we write

L(x,\varepsilon)=L_{1}(x,\varepsilon)+iL_{2}(x,\varepsilon)=x_{\varepsilon}-x-i\varepsilon^{2}g(x_{\varepsilon})=x_{\varepsilon}-x-i\gamma_{\varepsilon}.

where
L_{1}
and
L_{2}
stand for the real and imaginary part of
L
. Recall that
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is given by equation (
3.19
).

Lemma 3.7
.

For
\varepsilon
small enough

\sup_{t>0}\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\Big[\,\frac{1}{K(x,\varepsilon)}\,-\frac{1}{L(x,\varepsilon)}\,\Big]\,dx\,\Big|\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\nu}\,.

(3.38)

Proof.

Note that by (
3.32
)

x_{\varepsilon}=\beta\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{2}f(x_{\varepsilon})\,.

Hence

L_{1}(x,\varepsilon)-K_{1}(x,\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2}(f(x)-f(x_{\varepsilon})).

Similarly, it follows from (
3.30
) that

L_{2}(x,\varepsilon)-K_{2}(x,\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{2}(g(x)-g(x_{\varepsilon})).

The function
g
obviously satisfies the same bound (
3.34
) as the function
f
:

\sup_{I_{\varepsilon}}\varepsilon^{2}\,|g^{(j)}|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1+\nu-j})\,,\qquad j=0,1,2.

(3.39)

The mean value theorem thus gives

|L(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)|\,\leq\,\varepsilon^{2}\sup_{I_{\varepsilon}}|f^{\prime}+ig^{\prime}||x-x_{\varepsilon}|\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\nu}|x-x_{\varepsilon}|

(3.40)

Moreover, applying (
3.34
) for
f
and
g
with

and Taylor’s Theorem we obtain

\displaystyle\big|L(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)-\varepsilon^{2}[f^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon})+ig^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon})]\,(x-x_{\varepsilon})\big|\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\nu-1}\,(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}\,.

(3.41)

The last two bounds, which hold for all

, will be our main tools in estimating the integral in (

). Following

[
10
]
we decompose

\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\Big[\,\frac{1}{K(x,\varepsilon)}\,-\frac{1}{L(x,\varepsilon)}\,\Big]\,dx=\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\Big[\,\frac{L(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)}\,+\frac{(L(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon))^{2}}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)K(x,\varepsilon)}\,\Big]\,dx\,.

(3.42)

To estimate the first integral on the right hand side we use (
3.41
) and write

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,\frac{L(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)}\,dx\Big|\,

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{2}\,|f^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon})+ig^{\prime}(x_{\varepsilon})\big|\,\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,\frac{x-x_{\varepsilon}}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)}\,dx\,\Big|+\varepsilon^{\nu-1}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{|L(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}}\,dx

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{\nu}\,\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,\frac{x-x_{\varepsilon}}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)}\,dx\Big|+\varepsilon^{\nu}\,,

where we have used equations (
3.34
) and (
3.39
), and the fact that

\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{|L(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}}\,dx=\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}}{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\gamma^{2}(\varepsilon)}\,dx=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon),

see (
3.19
), (
3.36
). For the remaining integral we find,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,\frac{x-x_{\varepsilon}}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)}\,dx\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}e^{-itx}\,\Big(\frac{1}{L(x,\varepsilon)}+\frac{i\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{L^{2}(x,\varepsilon)}\Big)\,dx\Big|\,\leq\,\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{e^{-itx}}{L(x,\varepsilon)}\,dx\Big|+\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\gamma^{2}(\varepsilon)}\,dx\,.

(3.43)

The second integral on the right hand side of (

) satisfies

\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\gamma^{2}(\varepsilon)}\,dx\ \leq\ \int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}{(x-x_{\varepsilon})^{2}+\gamma^{2}(\varepsilon)}\,dx\,=\pi.

In view of Lemma
B.1
the first term on the right hand side of (

) is bounded, for

sufficiently small, by

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{e^{-itx}}{L(x,\varepsilon)}\,dx\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\int_{-\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4}}^{\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4}}\,\frac{e^{-it\xi}}{\xi+i\gamma_{\varepsilon}}\,d\xi\,\Big|=\Big|\int_{-\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4\gamma_{\varepsilon}}}^{\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4\gamma_{\varepsilon}}}\,\frac{e^{-it\gamma_{\varepsilon}y}}{y+i}\,dy\,\Big|\,\leq\,C,

(3.44)

where
C
is a constant independent of
\varepsilon
and
t
. Here we have used the fact that
\frac{\varepsilon}{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\searrow 0
. This finishes our discussion of the first term on the right hand side of (
3.42
). To control the second term in (
3.42
) we
use the bounds (
3.37
) and (
3.40
), and compute

\displaystyle\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{|L(x,\varepsilon)-K(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}}{|L(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}|K(x,\varepsilon)|}\,dx\,

\displaystyle\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{2\nu}\int_{I_{\varepsilon}}\frac{|x-x_{\varepsilon}|}{|L(x,\varepsilon)|^{2}}\,dx=\varepsilon^{2\nu}\int_{-\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4}}^{\frac{\beta\varepsilon}{4}}\ \frac{|y|}{y^{2}+\gamma^{2}(\varepsilon)}\,dy=\varepsilon^{2\nu}\!\! \log\Big(1+\frac{\beta^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{16\,\gamma^{2}(\varepsilon)}\Big)=o(\varepsilon^{\nu}),

cf. (
3.20
). This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

For
\varepsilon

3.4.
Resonances induced by anomalous magnetic moment

The two dimensional Pauli operator of an elementary fermion with spin
1/2
and magnetic moment
g
is given by

P(A,g)=\begin{pmatrix}P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,g)&0\\
0&P_{\scriptscriptstyle+}(A,g)\end{pmatrix},\qquad P_{\pm}(A,g)=(i\nabla+A)^{2}\pm\frac{g}{2}B\\

in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})

1/2
should have magnetic moment

. Note that
P(A,2)=P(A)
, see
equation (
1.1
). However, magnetic moments of certain particles may slightly differ from this value. One can identify the
anomaly of the magnetic moment with the parameter

by writing

. Then

P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,g)=(i\nabla+A)^{2}-(1-\varepsilon)B.

and we have

Corollary 3.20
.

Let
1<\alpha<2
and let
B
satisfy Assumption (
2.1
) . Suppose that
\varepsilon=\frac{2-g}{2}>0
. Then there exists a constant
C
such
that for
\varepsilon
small enough

\sup_{t>0}\big|\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,e^{-itP_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,g)}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}-e^{-it(x_{\varepsilon}-i\gamma_{\varepsilon})}\big|\,\leq\,C\,\varepsilon^{\mu},

(3.70)

where
x_{\varepsilon}
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
satisfy

\displaystyle x_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\beta\varepsilon\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\nu})\big)

\displaystyle\gamma_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\eta\,\beta^{\alpha}\,\varepsilon^{\alpha}\,\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{\alpha-1})\big)+\sigma\,|{\rm w}|^{2}\,\beta^{1-\alpha}\,\varepsilon^{3-\alpha}\,\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2-\alpha})\big)

with
\eta\,,\sigma\,,\mu
given by (
3.13
), (
1.8
) and (
3.16
), and with

\beta=\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},B\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle},\qquad{\rm w}=\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},B\,\varphi\mathcal{\big\rangle}\,.

(3.71)

Proof.

Since
P_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A)\psi_{0}=(i\nabla+A)^{2}\psi-B\,\psi_{0}=0
, the Hardy inequality

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|u|^{2}}{1+|x|^{2}\,\log^{2}|x|}\,dx\ \lesssim\ \|(i\nabla+A)u\|^{2}\,\qquad u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),

(3.72)

which holds for any
A\in L^{2}_{\rm\,loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
\text{rot}\,A\not\equiv 0
, see
[
14
,
23
,
13
]
,
implies

\beta=\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},(i\nabla+A)^{2}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\|(i\nabla+A)\psi_{0}\|_{2}^{2}>0.

We can thus apply Theorem
3.1
with
V=B
, and the claim follows.
∎

Corollary 3.21
.

Let
\alpha=2
. Let
B
satisfy Assumptions (
2.1
) and let
\beta
be given by (
3.71
). Then

\lim_{g\searrow 2}\,\big|\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},\,e^{-itP_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,g)}\,\psi_{0}\mathcal{\big\rangle}-e^{-it(x_{\varepsilon}-i\gamma_{\varepsilon})}\big|=0

(3.73)

uniformly in

, where
\varepsilon=\frac{2-g}{2},\ x_{\varepsilon}=\beta\varepsilon\big[1+\mathcal{O}\big(|\log\varepsilon|^{-1}\big)\big]
, and

\displaystyle\gamma_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon\,|\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},B\,\varphi_{2}\mathcal{\big\rangle}|^{2}}{(\log\varepsilon)^{2}}\,\big[1+\mathcal{O}\big(|\log\varepsilon|^{-1}\big)\big]\qquad\ \ \text{if}\quad\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0},B\,\varphi_{2}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\neq 0,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\varepsilon}

Since

3.10

∎

4.
Resonances arising from a degenerate eigenvalue

In this section we treat the resonances arising when the unperturbed operator has a degenerate eigenvalue at the threshold.
By (
1.4
), zero is no longer a simple eigenvalue of
P(A)
if
\alpha>2
. We need to isolate a zero-eigenfunction in order to
study
resonance behavior. It is natural to consider eigenfunctions of
P_{0}VP_{0}
. Thus, we consider resonances arising as

\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},\,e^{-itP_{\scriptscriptstyle-}(A,\varepsilon)}\,\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}

simple

we
apply the SLFG formula twice: we first obtain the restriction of the resolvent to
P_{0}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and then apply the formula again to obtain
the desired matrix element.

Explicitly, the operator
P_{0}VP_{0}
, as an operator on
P_{0}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

N
(recall (
1.4
)), and
we work under the following assumption.

Assumption 4.1
.

The operator
P_{0}VP_{0}
has a simple positive eigenvalue,
\kappa>0
, with (normalized) eigenfunction
\psi_{0}^{(V)}
. We let
P_{1}
denote the
projection onto the subspace spanned by
\psi_{0}^{(V)}
and
Q_{1}=P_{0}-P_{1}
.

4.1.
The application of the SLFG formula in the degenerate case

As in the non-degenerate case, we take
\mathscr{H}_{1}=P_{0}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\mathscr{H}_{2}=Q_{0}L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})

S(z)=\varepsilon P_{0}VP_{0}-zP_{0}-\varepsilon^{2}P_{0}vU\big(G(z)-\varepsilon G(z)\left(U+\varepsilon G(z)\right)^{-1}G(z)\big)UvP_{0}

with

as defined in (

\widetilde{S}(z)=P_{0}vU\big(G(z)-\varepsilon G(z)\left(U+\varepsilon G(z)\right)^{-1}G(z)\big)UvP_{0}.

(4.1)

We now write

S(z)=\begin{pmatrix}s_{11}(z)&s_{12}(z)\\
s_{21}(z)&s_{22}(z)\end{pmatrix}\\

where

s_{11}(z)=\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},S(z)\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle},

so that, by the SLFG formula applied twice,

\begin{split}&\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},\left(P_{{\scriptscriptstyle-}}(A,\varepsilon)-z\right)^{-1}\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}=\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},S(z)^{-1}\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\\
&\quad=\left(s_{11}(z)-s_{12}(z)s_{22}(z)^{-1}s_{21}(z)\right)^{-1}\\
&\quad=\Big(\kappa\varepsilon-z-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},\widetilde{S}(z)\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}-\varepsilon^{4}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},\widetilde{S}(z)Q_{1}\left(\varepsilon Q_{1}VQ_{1}-zQ_{1}-\varepsilon^{2}Q_{1}\widetilde{S}(z)Q_{1}\right)^{-1}Q_{1}\widetilde{S}(z)\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\Big)^{-1}\\
&\quad=\widetilde{F}(z,\varepsilon)^{-1}.\end{split}

(4.2)

4.2.
Non-integer flux

For
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Z}

in mind, we denote

(4.3)

and

For

Proof.

Note that

\begin{split}\widetilde{F}(x,\varepsilon)-\widetilde{K}(x,\varepsilon)&=\varepsilon^{3}\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},vUG(x)\left(U+\varepsilon G(x)\right)^{-1}G(x)Uv\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\\

We treat the two terms above separately. The first term is treated in the same way as in Lemma

&\lesssim\varepsilon^{1+2\widetilde{\nu}}+\varepsilon^{2}.\end{split}

As for the second term, note that

\varepsilon^{2}\sup_{x\in\widetilde{J}_{\varepsilon}}\|\widetilde{S}(x)\|_{2\to 2}\lesssim\ \varepsilon^{1+\mu}

and that for sufficiently small

which implies

\begin{split}\varepsilon^{4}\,\big|\mathcal{\big\langle}\psi_{0}^{(V)},\widetilde{S}(x)Q_{1}\big(\varepsilon Q_{1}VQ_{1}-xQ_{1}-\varepsilon^{2}Q_{1}\widetilde{S}(x)Q_{1}\big)^{-1}Q_{1}\widetilde{S}(x)\psi_{0}^{(V)}\mathcal{\big\rangle}\big|&\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{3}\big\|\widetilde{S}(x)\psi_{0}^{(V)}\big\|^{2}.\\
\end{split}

Using again the fact that

small enough, a straightforward computation shows that for such

\begin{split}\varepsilon^{3}\big\|\widetilde{S}(x)\psi_{0}^{(V)}\big\|^{2}&\,\lesssim\,\varepsilon^{3}\big\|V\widetilde{H}(x)V\psi_{0}^{(V)}\big\|^{2}+\varepsilon^{4}\big\|V\widetilde{H}(x)V\psi_{0}^{(V)}\big\|\big\|V\widetilde{H}(x)v(U+\varepsilon G(z))^{-1}v\widetilde{H}(x)V\psi_{0}^{(V)}\big\|\\[4.0pt]

This finishes the proof.
∎

Hence if we define

\widehat{K}(x,\varepsilon)=\kappa\varepsilon-x+\varepsilon^{2}\,\widetilde{a}_{1}\,(\log x-i\pi)-\varepsilon^{2}\,\widetilde{a}_{2},

(4.21)

then

\sup_{x\in\widetilde{J}_{\varepsilon}}\,|\widetilde{F}(x,\varepsilon)-\widehat{K}(x,\varepsilon)|=o(\varepsilon^{2}).

Setting
x_{\varepsilon}
to be the unique zero point of
{\rm Re}\big(\widehat{K}(x,\varepsilon)\big)=\kappa\varepsilon-x+\varepsilon^{2}\,\widetilde{a}_{1}\,\log x-\varepsilon^{2}\,\widetilde{a}_{2}
in the interval

\widetilde{J}_{\varepsilon}
and

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\widetilde{a}_{1}\pi\varepsilon^{2}

we can thus follow the proof of Theorem
3.16
and conclude the proof of the theorem.
∎

Appendix B

The following technical result is not new, see
[
10
, Eq. (3.56)]
. For the sake of completeness, we give a short proof.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
a>1
. Then

\sup_{s>0}\,\sup_{R>a}\,\Big|\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{e^{-is\frac{y}{R}}}{y+i}\,dy\,\Big|\,<\,\infty.

Proof.

By the residue theorem,

\int_{-R}^{R}\frac{e^{-is\frac{y}{R}}}{y+i}\,dy\ =-2\pi i\,e^{-\frac{s}{R}}-\int_{\Gamma}\frac{e^{-is\frac{z}{R}}}{z+i}dz

where
\Gamma
is the semi-circle of radius
R
in the lower complex half-plane centered in the origin and directed from
(R,0)
to
(-R,0)
.
The first term on the hand side is obviously bounded
uniformly in
s>0
and
R>a
. As for the second term, parametrizing
\Gamma
in the usual way we find

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\Gamma}\frac{e^{-is\frac{z}{R}}}{z+i}dz\,\Big|

\displaystyle=R\ \Big|\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{e^{-is\cos\theta-s\sin\theta}e^{i\theta}}{R\cos\theta-iR\sin\theta+i}\,d\theta\,\Big|\leq\frac{\pi R}{R-1}\leq\frac{\pi a}{a-1}\,,

since
|R\cos\theta-iR\sin\theta+i|\geq R-1
.
∎
