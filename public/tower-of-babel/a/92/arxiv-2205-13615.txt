Abstract
. We study branching Markov chains on a countable state space (space of types) 𝒳 \mathscr{X} with the focus on the qualitative aspects of the limit behaviour of the evolving empirical population distributions. No conditions are imposed on the multitype offspring distributions at the points of 𝒳 \mathscr{X} other than to have the same average and to satisfy a uniform L ​ log ⁡ L L\log L moment condition. We show that the arising population martingale is uniformly integrable. Convergence of population averages of the branching chain is then put in connection with stationary spaces of the associated ordinary Markov chain on 𝒳 \mathscr{X} (assumed to be irreducible and transient). This is applied, in particular, to the boundaries of appropriate compactifications of 𝒳 \mathscr{X} . Final considerations consider the general interplay between the measure theoretic boundaries of the branching chain and the associated ordinary chain.

Introduction

There is a large body of literature devoted to the quantitative aspects of branching random
walks on the additive group of real numbers and to the behaviour of the associated martingales,
e.g., see
Shi

[
Shi15
]
and the references therein. In what concerns more general
state spaces rich enough to have a non-trivial topological boundary at infinity (like,
for instance, infinite trees), it is natural to ask about the limit behaviour of the
branching populations in geometric terms. Non-trivial limit sets of random population
sequences were first exhibited by
Liggett

[
Lig96
]
for branching random
walks on regular trees. This was pursued further for branching random walks on free groups by

Hueter – Lalley

[
HL00
]
, on more general free products by

Candellero – Gilch – Müller

[
CGM12
]
, and very recently to
random walks on hyperbolic groups by

Sidoravicius – Wang –Xiang

[
SWX20
]
. See also

Benjamini – Müller

[
BM12
, Section 4.1]
for a number
of conjectures concerning the trace and limit sets of branching random walks. Some
answers were given by

Candellero – Roberts

[
CR15
]
and
Hutchcroft

[
Hut20
]
.

We are looking at branching random walks from a different and apparently novel angle.
We are interested in the random limit boundary measures arising from the empirical
distributions of sample populations. Unlike with the limit sets, the very existence
of the limit measures is already a non-trivial problem. We consider and solve this
problem in two different setups: in the topological one (when the boundary of the
state space is provided by a certain compactification) and in the measure-theoretical
one (when we are dealing with the Poisson or exit boundary of the underlying Markov
chain on the state space).

\Pi\cong\pi=\aug(\Pi)
on
\mathscr{M}\cong{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
. Therefore, it is nothing but the usual
Galton – Watson branching process
determined by
\pi
.

Example 1.19
.

For a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathscr{X}
, we denote by
\mu^{k}\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})
the image of the product measure
\mu^{\otimes k}
on
\mathscr{X}^{k}
under the map

\mathscr{X}^{k}\to\mathscr{M}\,,\qquad(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\mapsto\delta_{x_{1}}+\dots+\delta_{x_{k}}\,.

Given a distribution
\pi\in\Prob({{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
and a Markov chain on
\mathcal{X}
with the transition
probabilities
p_{x}\in\Prob(\mathcal{X})
, the family of branching distributions

\Pi_{x}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\pi(k)\cdot p_{x}^{\otimes k}

(1.20)

determines then a branching Markov chain with
independent branching and displacement
, for which
all offspring distributions
\pi_{x}
coincide with
\pi
, and the displacement distributions
are
p_{x}\,
. Any particle occupying a position
x\in\mathcal{X}
fissions into a
\pi
-distributed
random number of new particles,
and each new particle moves to a new
p_{x}
-distributed position independently of all
other particles. Branching and displacement can be fully decoupled by first generating a
random Galton – Watson tree
T
with the offspring distribution
\pi
, and then running
the
T
-indexed Markov chain
with the transition probabilities
p_{x}

(e.g., see
Aldous

[
Ald91
, Section 6, p. 64]
and

Benjamini – Peres

[
BP94
]
).

Example 1.21
.

In case all offspring distributions
\pi_{x}
of a branching Markov chain coincide with a
common distribution
\pi
, this does in no way imply that offspring and displacement are
independent. It just means that all branching distributions
\Pi_{x}
have the form

\Pi_{x}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\pi(k)\cdot\Pi_{x}^{k}\;,

where
\Pi_{x}^{k}
are probability measures on size
k
populations. For instance, if we let

m_{y}^{k}=k\cdot\delta_{y}
be the population with
k
particles at
y
and none elsewhere, then we
can consider the branching distributions

\Pi_{x}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\pi(k)\sum_{y\in\mathscr{X}}p_{x}(y)\cdot\delta_{m_{y}^{k}}\,.

(1.22)

The corresponding transition distributions are again
p_{x}\,
. In the branching Markov chains determined
by both (
1.20
) and (
1.22
) first one samples a Galton – Watson tree with the offspring distribution
\pi
and then equips this tree with the transitions sampled from the appropriate transition distributions
p_{x}
. However, in Example
1.19
the independently sampled transitions are parameterised by the
edges
of the tree, whereas for the chain determined by (
1.22
) they are parameterised by the
vertices
of the tree (so that the transition is the same for all edges issued from the same vertex in the direction away from the root). It might be interesting to look at the branching Markov chains determined by convex combinations of the measures (
1.20
) and (
1.22
).

Example 1.23
.

One can also consider a more general situation than in Example
1.19
with the offspring distributions
\pi_{x}
being
space dependent
, although the displacement is still governed by the transition probabilities
p_{x}
of an underlying Markov chain on
\mathscr{X}
(e.g., see
Menshikov – Volkov

[
MV97
]
and
Gantert – Müller

[
GM06
]
). In this case the resulting branching Markov chain is determined by the branching distributions

\Pi_{x}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\pi_{x}(k)\cdot p_{x}^{\otimes k}\;.

In the context of this example, the dependence of
\pi_{x}
on
x
is often referred to as an
environment
; if it is random, then one talks about
branching Markov chains in random environment
, see
Comets – Menshikov – Popov

[
CMP98
]
. The term “environment” is also used to describe the generalisation of the Galton – Watson process that allows the offspring distribution to depend on the generation number, see e.g.
Athreya – Ney

[
AN72
, Section VI.5]
. By passing to the space-time process (see Section
4.B
) the latter model becomes a particular case of the former one.

Example 1.24
.

If
\mathscr{X}
is a group, then it makes sense to consider the assignments
x\mapsto\Pi_{x}

equivariant
with respect to the natural action of
\mathscr{X}
on the population space
\mathscr{M}={{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}[\mathscr{X}]
by translations, i.e., such that all branching distributions
\Pi_{x}
are the translates of a
single
probability measure
\Pi\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})
(the branching distribution at the group identity). By analogy with ordinary
random walks on groups
, we then talk about
branching random walks
. In particular, in this case the offspring distributions
\pi_{x}
all coincide with the size distribution
\pi
of the measure
\Pi
, the branching ratios (offspring averages)
\overline{\pi}_{x}
all coincide with
\overline{\pi}
, and the transition probabilities
p_{x}
are the translates of the displacement distribution
\mu=\overline{\Pi}/\overline{\pi}
, the
law
of the random walk on the group:
p_{x}(y)=\mu(x^{-1}y)
. In the same vein one can also consider the situation when
\mathscr{X}
is endowed with a group action (transitive, quasi-transitive, or a more general one), and the map
x\mapsto\Pi_{x}
is equivariant with respect to this action (cf.
Kaimanovich – Woess

[
KW02
]
and Subsection
3.D
).

1.E.
Limit sets vs. limit measures

Before plunging into
medias res
we outline the earlier approach to the boundary behaviour of branching Markov chains which served as our motivation. For the branching Markov chain

{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{n})
, its
trace

\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\operatorname{supp}M_{n}\subset\mathscr{X}

is the random set of all points from the state space which are charged (or visited) by at least one of the populations
M_{n}
. Assuming that the state space
\mathscr{X}
is endowed with a
compactification

\overline{\mathscr{X}}=\mathscr{X}\cup\partial\mathscr{X}
(see Section
3.E
below for definitions and examples), one can then define, in the usual way, the
limit set
of a sample path as the boundary of its trace with respect to this compactification:

\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})=\overline{\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}}\setminus\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}=\overline{\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}}\cap\partial\mathscr{X}\;.

Notions of of
recurrence and transience

for branching Markov chains with independent branching and displacement have been studied by
Benjamini – Peres

[
BP94
]
,
Müller

[
Mü08
]
,
Bertacchi – Zucca

[
BZ08
]
(in continuous time) and
Benjamini – Müller

[
BM12
]
; see also

Woess

[
Woe09
, §5.C]
for a simplified approach.
If the branching Markov chain is recurrent in the sense that
\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}=\mathscr{X}
for almost
all sample paths, then obviously the limit set
\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})
coincides almost surely with the whole boundary

\mathscr{X}
. Otherwise, proper traces
\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}\neq\mathscr{X}
may lead to proper limit sets
\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})\neq\partial\mathscr{X}
, and
it makes sense to look at their properties.

Regarding non-trivial limit sets, see the referenes given at the beginning of the introduction.
Note that in those papers only branching random walks with independent branching
and displacement (as described in Example
1.19
) were considered.

Free groups have served as the “true touchstone” in the non-commutative random walk theory for the last 60 years, so let us describe the situation with them in more detail (for instance, see
Ledrappier

[
Led01
]
and the references therein for more background). Let
\mathscr{A}
be a finite
alphabet
of cardinality
d\geq 2
, and
\mathscr{F}
be the
free group
of rank
d
generated by
\mathscr{A}
. We fix a symmetric probability measure
\mu
with support
\mathscr{A}\cup\mathscr{A}^{-1}
; the simplest case is when
\mu
is equidistributed on
\mathscr{A}\cup\mathscr{A}^{-1}
, so that the random walk

(\mathscr{F},\mu)
is just the simple random walk on the homogeneous Cayley tree of the free group. Further, let
\pi
be the geometric distribution on
\mathbb{N}
with parameter
p\in(0,1)
and mean
\rho=1/p
. We can now consider the branching random walk with independent branching and displacement determined by the underlying random walk
(\mathscr{F},\mu)
and the offspring distribution
\pi
.

If
\rho r>1
, where
r=r(\mathscr{F},\mu)
is the spectral radius (
3.39
) of the random walk

(\mathscr{F},\mu)
, then almost surely
\operatorname{supp}{\mathbf{M}}=\mathscr{F}
and
\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})=\partial\mathscr{F}
. In the case
\rho r\leq 1
, Hueter and Lalley, extending the above cited result by Liggett related to simple random walk, proved that the Hausdorff dimension
\operatorname{HD}\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})
of the limit set with respect to a natural metric on
\partial\mathscr{F}
is almost surely constant and obtained an explicit formula for it
[
HL00
, Theorem 1]
. In particular, it satisfies the inequality

\operatorname{HD}\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{HD}\partial\mathscr{F}\;,

and
\operatorname{HD}\Lambda({\mathbf{M}})\to 0
as
\rho\to 1
from above. This result was extended to branching random walks on free products of finitely generated groups under less restrictive conditions by
Candellero – Gilch – Müller

[
CGM12
, Theorems 3.5 and 3.10]
, and very recently to
random walks on hyperbolic groups by

Sidoravicius – Wang –Xiang

[
SWX20
]
.

As outlined in the Introduction, our goal here is different; we are interested in
random
limit boundary measures
arising from the sequences (
0.2
), resp.
(
0.5
). Unlike with the limit sets, the very existence of the limit measures is a non-trivial
problem. In many cases there is a phase (regarding the branching ratio
\rho
) where the branching
Markov chain is strongly recurrent in the sense that with probability 1, each state
x\in\mathscr{X}
is visited
by the population infinitely often, see the references of the present subsection. Nevertheless,
the empirical distributions
always
move their mass to infinity, as the following lemma shows,
providing a simple motivation for our goals.

Lemma 1.25
.

Under assumptions (
BR
) and (
TC
), for any
y\in\mathscr{X}

\lim_{n}\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{n}(y)=0\quad\text{almost surely.}

Proof.

It suffices to prove this for the situation when the branching chain starts with
one particle at a generic
x\in\mathscr{X}
. In view of (
0.4
),

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\bigl(M_{n}(y)\bigr)=p^{(n)}(x,y)\,\rho^{n}\,.

Therefore

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\biggl(\sum_{n}\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}M_{n}(y)\biggr)=G(x,y)<\infty\,.

Therefore
M_{n}(y)/\rho^{n}\to 0
and thus also
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{n}(y)\to 0
almost surely under
\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{\!x}\,
.
∎

2.
Uniform integrability and positivity of the population martingale

2.A.
The population martingale

Recall that we assume (
BR
): the offspring averages satisfy
\overline{\pi}_{x}=\rho<\infty
for all

x\in\mathscr{X}
. In terms of the augmentation map (
1.1
), the barycentre map (
1.3
), and the transition operator
\mathcal{P}
(
1.9
) this condition means that

\bigl\|\overline{\Theta\mathcal{P}}\bigr\|=\rho\bigl\|\overline{\Theta}\bigr\|\qquad\forall\,\Theta\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})\;,

or, equivalently,

\overline{\aug(\Theta\mathcal{P})}=\rho\cdot\overline{\aug(\Theta)}\qquad\forall\,\Theta\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})\;.

In other words, after one step of the branching Markov chain the average size of populations is always
multiplied by the same constant
\rho
. This is the case, for instance, for the branching Markov chains
from examples
1.19
,
1.21
, and
1.24
; in the setup of Example
1.23
, condition (
BR
) was used by
Gantert – Müller

[
GM06
, Section 3.1]
.
Recall the Definition
0.6
of the population martingale. The sequence
(W_{n})
is indeed a
martingale with respect to the increasing coordinate filtration on the path space, because by
Corollary
1.16
condition (
BR
) implies that the lift
\widetilde{\mathds{1}}(m)=\|m\|
(
1.12
) of the
constant function
{\mathds{1}}
from
\mathscr{X}
to
\mathscr{M}
is
\rho
-harmonic; see Section
4.D
below for a more
general discussion.

A priori
the expectation of the limit population ratio

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{\Theta}W_{\infty}=\langle\,\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{\!\Theta},W_{\infty}\rangle

may be
strictly smaller
than the expectations

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{\Theta}W_{n}=\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{\Theta}W_{0}=\sum_{m\in\mathscr{M}}\Theta(m)\,\|m\|

of the population martingale with respect to the measure
\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{\!\Theta}
on the path space corresponding to an initial distribution
\Theta\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})
. Their equality means that the population martingale is
uniformly integrable
on the path space
(\mathscr{M}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}},\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{\!\Theta})
(e.g., see
Meyer

[
Mey66
, Chapter V]
for the basics of martingale theory). When talking about uniform integrability without specifying a measure on the path space we mean that it holds for
any
initial distribution
\Theta\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})
, i.e., with respect to the full initial support measure class (
FS
). In order to guarantee this property it is enough to take for
\Theta
just the delta measures concentrated at singletons
\delta_{x},\;x\in\mathscr{X}
, i.e., to require that

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}W_{\infty}=1\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathscr{X}\,.

2.B.
Uniform
L\log L
moment condition

For the ordinary Galton – Watson processes (Example
1.18
) the equivalence of the uniform integrability of the population martingale to the
L\log L
moment condition

\sum_{k}\pi(k)\cdot k\log k<\infty\;.

(2.1)

on the offspring distribution
\pi
is the classical theorem of

Kesten – Stigum

[
KS66
]
(see also

Lyons – Pemantle – Peres

[
LPP95
]
and the references therein).
Although this criterion is directly applicable to the situation when the offspring distributions

\pi_{x}
are the same for all
x\in\mathscr{X}
, in particular, to branching random walks on groups
(see Example
1.24
), this is not the case for general branching Markov chains.

In order to formulate an analogous result in the general setup we need
tightness
of the
offspring distributions, as follows.

Definition 2.2
.

Given two probability distributions
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
on
{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
, we say that
\pi

dominates

\pi^{\prime}
(notation:
\pi^{\prime}\preceq\pi
) if

\pi^{\prime}[n,\infty)\leq\pi[n,\infty)\qquad\forall\,n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\,.

A family of probability measures on
{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
satisfies the
uniform first moment condition
(resp.,
the
uniform
L\log L
moment condition
) if it is dominated by a probability measure with a finite
first moment (resp., by a measure that satisfies the
L\log L
moment condition).

The uniform moment condition was used, for example,
by
Kaimanovich – Woess

[
KW92
, Lemma 1]
for random walks on graphs, and
by
D’Souza – Biggins

[
DB92
, p. 40]
for branching processes.

Theorem 2.3
.

If the offspring distributions of a branching Markov chain satisfy the uniform
L\log L
moment condition, then the population martingale is uniformly integrable.

It is known since
Levinson

[
Lev59
, Section 4]
that for the ordinary
Galton – Watson processes the
L\log L
condition implies that the limit population
ratio is almost surely strictly positive on non-extinction. A consequence of the
Kesten – Stigum theorem is the equivalence (on non-extinction) of the following two conditions:

(i)

the population martingale
(W_{n})
is uniformly integrable;

(ii)

the limit population ratio
W_{\infty}
is almost surely strictly positive.

However, for branching processes in varying environment it may well happen that the limit population ratio
vanishes with positive probability in spite of the uniform integrability of the population martingale (see
the example constructed in
MacPhee – Schuh

[
MS83
]
and the discussion in

D’Souza – Biggins

[
DB92
, p. 41]
). We do not know whether in our setup the
uniform integrability of the population martingale would always imply that the limit population ratio is
almost surely positive. Still, we can show that this is the case under the same uniform
L\log L
condition as in Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If the offspring distributions of a branching Markov chain satisfy the uniform
L\log L
moment condition, then the limit population ratio is almost surely strictly positive for any initial population.

Our proofs of Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
2.4
below are self-contained and follow the approach of
D’Souza – Biggins

[
DB92
]
to the Galton – Watson processes in varying environment. Theorem
2.3
can also be deduced from the general criterion of uniform integrability of the martingales of multi-type branching processes (
\equiv
branching Markov chains in our terminology)
associated with “mean-harmonic functions” due to
Biggins – Kyprianou

[
BK04
, Theorem 1.1 and the discussion on p. 547]
, cf. Remark
4.16
below.

2.C.
Laplace transforms and their remainders

We denote by

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(s)=\sum_{k}\theta(k)e^{-sk}={\mathbf{E}}_{\theta}e^{-sX}

the
Laplace transform
of a probability measure
\theta\in\Prob({{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
(which can be thought of as
the distribution of a
{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
-valued random variable
X
). The linear part of the power series expansion
of
\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(e^{-s})
is equal to
1-\overline{\theta}s
, where
\overline{\theta}
is the expectation of
\theta
(assumed to be finite), and we denote the arising
remainder
by

\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(s)=\mathcal{L}_{\theta}(s)-1+\overline{\theta}s=\sum_{k}\theta(k)\,\psi(sk)={\mathbf{E}}_{\theta}\psi(sX)

(2.5)

with

\psi(t)=e^{-t}-1+t\geq 0\;.

(2.6)

We also use the above notation with the subscript
\Theta
in the situation when

\theta=\aug(\Theta)

is the image of a measure
\Theta\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})
under the augmentation map
\aug
(
1.1
), so that

\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}(s)=\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{\Theta}e^{-s\|M\|}\;,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\Theta}(s)=\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{\Theta}\psi(s\|M\|)\,.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any measure
\theta\in\Prob({{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
with a finite first moment

(i)

the function
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
is non-decreasing on the positive ray
\mathbb{R}_{+}
;

(ii)

the ratio
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(s)/s
is non-decreasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and

\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(s)}{s}=0\;;

(iii)

the integral

\int_{0}^{C}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(s)}{s^{2}}\,ds

is convergent for any
C>0
if and only if the measure
\theta
satisfies the
L\log L
moment condition (
2.1
).

Further, if a measure
\theta\in\Prob({{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
with a finite first moment dominates another measure

\theta^{\prime}\in\Prob({{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
, then

(iv)

\mathcal{R}_{\theta^{\prime}}(s)\leq\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(s)\qquad\forall\,s\geq 0\;.

(2.8)

Proof.

(i) and (ii) immediately follow from the same properties of the functions
\psi
(
2.6
) and
s\mapsto\psi(s)/s
, respectively, whereas (iv) is a consequence of (i). Property (iii) is well-known, e.g., see
Athreya – Ney

[
AN72
, Lemma I.10.1]
. Since our setup is somewhat different, for the sake of completeness we include its elementary proof.

The function
\mathcal{R}_{\theta}
being non-negative, by exchanging the order of summation and integration one arrives at

\int_{0}^{C}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\theta}(s)}{s^{2}}\,ds=\int_{0}^{C}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\psi(sk)}{s^{2}}\theta(k)\,ds=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\theta(k)\int_{0}^{C}\frac{\psi(sk)}{s^{2}}\,ds\;,

where

\int_{0}^{C}\frac{\psi(sk)}{s^{2}}\,ds=k\int_{0}^{kC}\frac{\psi(s)}{s^{2}}\,ds\;.

Since
\psi(s)/s\to 1
as
s\to\infty
, the latter integral asymptotically behaves as
k\log(kC)
, whence the claim.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

If the offspring distributions
\pi_{x}
of a branching Markov chain satisfy the uniform first moment condition, then there exists
s_{0}>0
such that for any measure
\Theta\in\Prob(\mathscr{M})

\mathcal{L}_{\Theta\mathcal{P}}(s)\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}(\rho s)+\overline{\theta}\,\mathcal{R}(s)\qquad\text{for all }\;s\in[0,s_{0}]\;,

(2.10)

where
\rho
is the common branching ratio from condition (
BR
),
\overline{\theta}
is the expectation of the measure
\theta=\aug(\Theta)
, and
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\pi}
is the remainder function (
2.5
) associated with the measure
\pi\in\Prob({{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
that dominates the distributions
\pi_{x}
.

Proof.

To begin with, let
\Theta
be the delta measure at the singleton
\delta_{x}\in\mathscr{M},\;x\in\mathscr{X}
. Then

\Theta\mathcal{P}=\Pi_{x}
, see Definition
0.1
, whence
\aug(\Theta\mathcal{P})=\pi_{x}
.
We recall that
\overline{\pi}_{x}=\rho
for all
x\in\mathscr{X}
by our standing assumption (
BR
). Therefore, by Lemma
2.7
(iv) for any
s\geq 0

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\Theta\mathcal{P}}(s)=\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{x}}(s)

\displaystyle=1-\overline{\pi}_{x}s+\mathcal{R}_{\pi_{x}}(s)

(2.11)

\displaystyle=1-\rho s+\mathcal{R}_{\pi_{x}}(s)\leq 1-\rho s+\mathcal{R}(s)\;.

Now, let
\Theta=\delta_{m}
for
m\in\mathscr{M}
, so that
\Theta\mathcal{P}=\Pi_{m}
. Then by (
1.8
) and (
2.11
),

\mathcal{L}_{\Theta\mathcal{P}}(s)=\mathcal{L}_{\Pi_{m}}(s)=\prod_{x\in m}\mathcal{L}_{\pi_{x}}(s)\leq(1-\rho s+\mathcal{R}(s))^{\|m\|}

(counting as always multiplicities in the product).
By Lemma
2.7
(ii) we can choose
s_{0}>0
in such a way that

\mathcal{R}(s)\leq\rho s\leq 1\qquad\forall\,s\in[0,s_{0}]\;.

Since the derivative of the function
t\mapsto t^{\|m\|}
on the interval
[0,1]
does not exceed
\|m\|
, we then have

\displaystyle(1-\rho s+\mathcal{R}(s))^{\|m\|}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho s)^{\|m\|}+\|m\|\mathcal{R}(s)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\rho s\|m\|}+\|m\|\mathcal{R}(s)\qquad\qquad\forall\,s\in[0,s_{0}]\;,

and therefore (
2.10
) is satisfied, because
\theta=\aug(\Theta)=\delta_{\|m\|}
, so that
\mathcal{L}_{\Theta}(z)=z^{\|m\|}
and
\overline{\theta}=\|m\|
.

Finally, the general case follows from the linearity of the both sides of (
2.10
) with respect to
\Theta
.
∎

2.D.
Proof of Theorem
2.3

We denote by

\Theta_{n}=\delta_{\delta_{x}}\mathcal{P}^{n}\,,\qquad t\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\,,

the one-dimensional distributions of the associated measure
\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{\!x}
on the space of sample paths of the branching Markov chain
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{0},M_{1},\dots)
with
M_{0}=\delta_{x}\in\mathscr{M}
. Then

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\,e^{-sW_{\infty}}=\lim_{n}\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\,e^{-sW_{n}}=\lim_{n}\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\,e^{-s\,\|M_{n}\|/\rho^{n}}=\lim_{n}\mathcal{L}_{\Theta_{n}}\Bigl(s/\rho^{n})\;.

Condition (
BR
) implies that

\overline{\aug(\Theta_{n})}=\rho^{n}\;,

whence by Lemma
2.9
for
s\leq s_{0}

\mathcal{L}_{\Theta_{n}}(s/\rho^{n})\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Theta_{n-1}}(s/\rho^{n-1})+\rho^{n-1}\mathcal{R}(s/\rho^{n})\,,

and by telescoping

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\,e^{-sW_{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{L}_{\Theta_{0}}(s)+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\rho^{n-1}\mathcal{R}(s/\rho^{n})

(2.12)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-s}+\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\infty}\rho^{\tau}\mathcal{R}(s/\rho^{\tau})\,d\tau=e^{-s}+\frac{s}{\rho\log\rho}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\mathcal{R}(\sigma)}{\sigma^{2}}\,d\sigma\,.

Thus,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\,W_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\lim_{s\to 0}\frac{1-\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}\,e^{-sW_{\infty}}}{s}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{s\to 0}\left\{\frac{1-e^{-s}}{s}-\frac{1}{\rho\log\rho}\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\mathcal{R}(\sigma)}{\sigma^{2}}\,d\sigma\right\}=1\,.

2.E.
Proof of Theorem

Let

By inequality (

3.
Topological convergence of populations

3.A.
Harmonic systems of measures and stationary spaces

In this and the next subsection, we set up the needed background on boundary behaviour
for transient Markov chains, to be applied to the base Markov chain of our branching
chain and subsequently the branching Markov chain itself.

The action of the transition operator of a countable state space Markov chain (see Section
1.C
)
naturally extends from the “ordinary” real valued functions to the ones taking values in an arbitrary
affine space (provided infinite convex combinations are well-defined — this is needed if not all
transition probabilities are finitely supported), in particular, to measure valued functions. By

\Prob(\mathscr{K})
we denote the space of probability measures on a measurable space
\mathscr{K}
, and in the same way as for real functions we can formulate

Definition 3.1
.

A map

\kappa:\mathscr{X}\to\Prob(\mathscr{K})\;,\qquad x\mapsto\kappa_{x}\;,

(3.2)

— in other words, a system
(\kappa_{x})
of probability measures on
\mathscr{K}
indexed by a countable space
\mathscr{X}
—,is
harmonic
with respect to a Markov operator
P
on
\mathscr{X}
, if
P\kappa=\kappa,

i.e., if
\kappa
satisfies the mean value property

\kappa_{x}=\langle\mu_{x},\kappa\rangle=\sum_{y}p_{x}(y)\,\kappa_{y}\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathscr{X}\,,

(3.3)

where
p_{x}
are the transition probabilities of the operator
P
. One also uses the term

stationary
(or,
P
-stationary
), cf. Remark
3.4
and Example
3.12
.

We shall refer to the couple
(\mathscr{K},\kappa)
as a
measurable
P
-stationary space
. In the situation
when
\mathscr{K}
is a topological space endowed with the Borel sigma-algebra, we call it a
topological

P
-stationary space
.

As follows from our irreducibility assumption (
TC
), all measures
\kappa_{x}
in a harmonic system

\kappa=(\kappa_{x})
are pairwise equivalent. Therefore one can talk about their common measure class
and we use the notation
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K},\kappa)=L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K})
for the corresponding Banach space of essentially bounded measurable functions.

Remark 3.4
.

Given a map
\kappa
(
3.2
), we use the same notation for its extension

\kappa_{\theta}=\sum_{x}\theta(x)\,\kappa_{x}\;,\qquad\theta\in\Meas(\mathscr{X})\;.

(3.5)

Then the
P
-harmonicity of a map
\kappa
is equivalent to its invariance with respect to the
action of the operator
P
on
\Meas(\mathscr{X})
, that is,

\kappa_{\theta}=\kappa_{\theta P}
for all
\theta\in\Meas(\mathscr{X})\,
.

The dual statement is that (
3.3
) holds if and only if
for any test function
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K})
, or from
the Banach space
C(\mathscr{K})
of real valued continuous functions when
\mathscr{K}
is a topological space,
the function
n the measurable case

f^{\varphi}(x)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle\;,\qquad x\in\mathscr{X}\;,

(3.6)

is
P
-harmonic in the usual sense. In particular, a
non-constant
harmonic system
exists only if there are non-constant bounded
P
-harmonic functions on
\mathscr{X}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

If
(\mathscr{K},\kappa)
is a compact separable
P
-stationary space, then with probability
1
, the Markov chain

{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{n})
has a random weak* limit

\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}=\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{X_{n}}\in\Prob(\mathscr{K})\;,

and the barycentre of the arising family of measures
\{\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}\}
on
\mathscr{K}
with respect to any distribution
\mathbf{P}_{\!x},\;x\in\mathscr{X}
, on the path space is the measure

{\mathbf{E}}_{x}(\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}})=\kappa_{x}\,\quad\text{i.e.,}\quad{\mathbf{E}}_{x}(\langle\upkappa_{{\mathbf{X}}}\,,\varphi\rangle)=\langle\kappa_{x}\,,\varphi\rangle\quad\forall\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})\,,x\in\mathscr{X}.

(3.8)

Proof.

As we have already mentioned, for any
\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
the function
f^{\varphi}
(
3.6
)
on
\mathscr{X}
is
P
-harmonic and obviously bounded. Therefore, the sequence of its values

f^{\varphi}(X_{n})
along the sample paths of the chain is a bounded martingale with respect
to the coordinate filtration of the path space (see Section
4.B
below for more details),
whence the limit

{\mathbf{K}}(\varphi)=\lim_{n}f^{\varphi}(X_{n})=\lim_{n}\langle\kappa_{X_{n}},\varphi\rangle

(3.9)

exists for almost every sample path, and

{\mathbf{E}}_{x}\bigl({\mathbf{K}}(\varphi)\bigr)={\mathbf{E}}_{x}\bigl(f^{\varphi}(X_{0})\bigr)=f^{\varphi}(x)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle\quad\forall\,x\in\mathscr{X}\;.

(3.10)

If
\Phi\subset C(\mathscr{K})
is a countable dense subset, then by discarding the exceptional
sets for each function
\varphi\in\Phi
one obtains a co-negligible subset
\Omega
of the path
space such that the limit (
3.9
) exists
on
\Omega
for
all
functions
\varphi\in\Phi
, hence, by the density assumption, for

all

\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
. Hence, this limit determines a non-negative normalised linear functional on

,
i.e., a Borel probability measure
\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}
on
\mathscr{K}
such that

\int\varphi\,d\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}={\mathbf{K}}(\varphi)\quad\text{on }\;\Omega\,,

(3.11)

and convergence in (

)

\langle\kappa_{X_{n}},\varphi\rangle\to{\mathbf{K}}(\varphi)\qquad\forall\,\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})

is precisely the weak
∗
convergence of the measures
\kappa_{X_{n}}
to
\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}
on
\Omega
.
Now, in terms of the limit measures
\kappa_{\mathbf{X}}
(
3.11
) formula (
3.10
) takes the form

{\mathbf{E}}_{x}\bigl(\langle\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}},\varphi\rangle\bigr)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle\qquad\forall\,\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})\;,

which proves the statement on the barycentre.
∎

3.B.
Compactifications and the Dirichlet problem

A priori
the stationary space
\mathscr{K}
in Definition
3.1
and Proposition
3.7
does not have to be “attached” to the state space
\mathscr{X}
in any way. Let us now look at the situation when
\mathscr{K}
is the
boundary

\partial\mathscr{X}
of a
compactification

\overline{\mathscr{X}}=\mathscr{X}\cup\partial\mathscr{X}
of the state space
\mathscr{X}
.

We only consider
compactifications for which

\partial\mathscr{X}
is separable.

(
SC
)

(Since
\mathscr{X}
is countable, the compactification space
\overline{\mathscr{X}}
is always separable. Still, the boundary

\partial\mathscr{X}
need not be separable in general,— such as, e.g., the
Stone – Čech
compactification
).

Definition 3.15
.

A compactification of the state space
\mathscr{X}
of a Markov chain is
stochastically resolutive
with respect to this chain if
X_{n}
converges almost surely to the compactification boundary, i.e., with probability
1

X_{\infty}=\lim_{n}X_{n}\in\partial\mathscr{X}\;.

The resulting images
\kappa_{x}
of the measures
\mathbf{P}_{\!x}
under the limit map are called the
hitting distributions
of the Markov chain.

This definition alludes to the notion of
resolutivity
from classical potential theory (e.g., see
Lukeš – Netuka - Veselý

[
LNV02
, Section 4]
), cf. the remark at the beginning of Section 8 in
Woess

[
Woe96
]
.
By the Markov property the system of hitting measures
\kappa_{x}
of a stochastically resolutive compactification is
P
-harmonic in the sense of Definition
3.1
.

Proposition 3.16
.

The boundary
\partial\mathscr{X}
of a stochastically resolutive compactification endowed with the family of the
hitting measures
(\kappa_{x})
is a
P
-boundary, and

\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{X_{n}}=\delta_{X_{\infty}}\qquad\text{almost surely.}

This is a consequence of a general result on the identification of
P
-boundaries with the quotients of the Poisson boundary (Proposition
4.6
) which we relegate to Section
4.B
.

Definition 3.17
.

A compactification of the state space
\mathscr{X}
of a Markov chain with the transition operator
P
is

Dirichlet regular
with respect to this chain if for any function
\varphi\in C(\partial\mathscr{X})
there
is a unique
P
-harmonic function
f^{\varphi}
on
\mathscr{X}
(the
solution of the Dirichlet problem

with the boundary data
\varphi
) that provides a continuous extension of
\varphi
to all of
\overline{\mathscr{X}}
. In
this situation for any

\varphi\mapsto f^{\varphi}(x)

is a norm 1 positive linear functional on
C(\partial\mathscr{X})
represented by a Borel probability
measure
\kappa_{x}
on
\partial\mathscr{X}
(
\equiv
the
harmonic measure
with pole at
x
) as

f^{\varphi}(x)=\int\varphi(\xi)\,d\kappa_{x}(\xi)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle\;.

(3.18)

The system of harmonic measures from Definition
3.17
is
P
-harmonic in the sense of
Definition
3.1
(cf. Remark
3.4
).

Proposition 3.19

(
Woess

[
Woe96
, Theorem 2.2]
,
[
Woe00
, Theorem 20.3]
)
.

A compactification satisfying (
SC
)
\overline{\mathscr{X}}=\mathscr{X}\cup\partial\mathscr{X}
of the state space

\mathscr{X}
of a transient Markov chain is Dirichlet regular if and only if the following two conditions hold:

(i)

the compactification is stochastically resolutive;

(ii)

the system of the hitting measures
\kappa_{x}
has the property that

\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{x\to\xi}\kappa_{x}=\delta_{\xi}\qquad\forall\,\xi\in\partial\mathscr{X}\;.

In this situation the measures arising from the solvability of the Dirichlet problem coincide
with the hitting measures
\kappa_{x}
.

Remark 3.20
.

In terms of the boundary convergence the difference between stochastic resolutivity and Dirichlet
regularity is that in the latter case the harmonic measures
\kappa_{x_{n}}
converge to the delta measure

\delta_{x_{\infty}}
at the limit point
x_{\infty}=\lim x_{n}\in\partial\mathscr{X}
for
any
boundary convergent
sequence
(x_{n})
, whereas in the latter case
\kappa_{X_{n}}\to\delta_{X_{\infty}}
just almost surely, i.e., along
almost all
sample paths of the chain. Stochastic resolutivity on its own does by no means imply Dirichlet regularity. For instance, see
Benjamini – Peres

[
BP92
, Example 3]
and
Kaimanovich – Woess

[
KW92
, pp. 461-462]
for examples of this kind with random walks on trees.

Remark 3.21
.

If a
P
-stationary space
(\mathscr{K},\kappa)
is compact, then the map
\kappa:x\mapsto\kappa_{x}

(
3.2
) provides an
embedding
of the discrete space
\mathscr{X}
into the
compact space
\Prob(\mathscr{K})
of Borel probability measures on
\mathscr{K}
endowed with the
weak* topology, and therefore it gives rise to a
compactification
of
\mathscr{X}

whose boundary is the collection of all weak* limit points of the system

.
This idea goes back to
Furstenberg

[
Fur63b
, Chapter II]
who
used it to define a compactification of Riemannian symmetric spaces. Proposition

then implies that this compactification is stochastically resolutive.

Our various preliminary considerations lead to the following, which is going to be a basic
tool for proving a.s. convergence of the empirical distributions
to a random distribution on the boundary.

Proposition 3.22
.

Let
\overline{\mathscr{X}}=\mathscr{X}\cup\mathscr{X}
be a Dirichlet regular compactification of the state space
\mathscr{X}
of a transient Markov chain. If

\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{n\to\infty}\theta_{n}=\theta_{\infty}\in\Prob(\partial\mathscr{X})\;,

for a sequence of measures
\theta_{n}\in\Prob(\mathscr{X})
, then also

\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{n\to\infty}\kappa_{\theta_{n}}=\theta_{\infty}

for the sequence of the associated harmonic measures
\kappa_{\theta_{n}}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi\in C(\partial\mathscr{X})
be a continuous test function on
\partial\mathscr{X}
, and let
f^{\varphi}\in C\left(\overline{\mathscr{X}}\right)
be its harmonic extension to the whole of
\overline{\mathscr{X}}
. Then by the definitions of the harmonic measures and of the weak* convergence

\langle\kappa_{\theta_{n}},\varphi\rangle=\langle\theta_{n},f^{\varphi}\rangle\to\langle\theta_{\infty},f^{\varphi}\rangle=\langle\theta_{\infty},\varphi\rangle\;,

whence the claim.
∎

Corollary 3.23
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
3.22
, let
\theta_{n}\in\Prob(\mathscr{X})
be a sequence of measures
escaping to infinity on
\mathscr{X}
(i.e., such that
\theta_{n}(x)\to 0
for any
x\in\mathscr{X}
).
Then the sequence
\theta_{n}
converges if and only if the sequence of the harmonic
measures
\kappa_{\theta_{n}}
converges, and the limits of these two sequences coincide.

Proof.

The claim follows from the compactness of the space
\Prob\left(\overline{\mathscr{X}}\right)
in the
weak* topology. Indeed, if
\kappa_{\theta_{n}}
is convergent, whereas
\theta_{n}
is not, then
by the compactness the sequence
\theta_{n}
has at least two different limit measures

\theta_{\infty}^{1},\theta_{\infty}^{2}
which by the escape assumption are supported by
\partial\mathscr{X}
.
By taking sub-sequences of
\theta_{n}
converging to
\theta_{\infty}^{1}
and to
\theta_{\infty}^{2}
,
respectively, one then arrives at a contradiction with Proposition
3.22
.
∎

3.C.
Population convergence

We finally come to the application, resp. extension of the results from subsection
3.A

and subsection
3.B
to the setup of branching Markov chains (see subsection
1.B
).
We recall that, given a map
\kappa:\mathscr{X}\to\Prob(\mathscr{K})
, we denote by

\kappa_{m}=\sum_{x\in m}\kappa_{x}\;,\qquad m\in\mathscr{M}\;,

its extension (
3.5
) to the population space
\mathscr{M}={{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}(\mathscr{X})
over
\mathscr{X}
, so that, in particular,

\|\kappa_{m}\|=\|m\|\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathscr{M}\;.

(3.24)

The
normalisation

\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\kappa}_{m}=\frac{1}{\|m\|}\,\kappa_{m}\in\Prob(\mathscr{K})

(3.25)

is then the
average
of the measures
\kappa_{x}
over a population
m
(where
m
, as always, is treated as a multiset).

Theorem 3.26
.

If

(1)

a branching Markov chain on the state space
\mathscr{X}
has constant branching ratio
\rho>1
, and
its offspring distributions satisfy the uniform
L\log L
moment condition,

(2)

(\mathscr{K},\kappa)
is a separable compact stationary space for the underlying Markov chain on
\mathscr{X}
,

then

(I)

for almost every sample path
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{n})
of the branching Markov chain there exists the limit

\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}=\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,\kappa_{M_{n}}\,,

which is a positive finite Borel measure on
\mathscr{K}
;

(II)

the barycentre of the measures
\{\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\}
with respect to any distribution
\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{\!x},\;x\in\mathscr{X}
, on the path space of the branching Markov chain is
\kappa_{x}\,
:

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}(\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}})=\kappa_{x}\,.

In particular, if

(3)

\overline{\mathscr{X}}=\mathscr{X}\cup\partial\mathscr{X}
is a compactification of the state space
\mathscr{X}
which satisfies (
SC
) and
is stochastically resolutive with respect to the underlying Markov chain,

then
(I)
and
(II)
hold for the associated family of hitting distributions on
the boundary
\partial\mathscr{X}
.
Furthermore, if in addition

(4)

the compactification is Dirichlet regular for the underlying Markov chain,

then

(III)

For almost every sample path of the branching Markov chain,

\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}=\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,M_{n}\,.

Proof.

The argument for the proof of (I) and (II) is essentially the same as in the proof of
Proposition
3.7
(which could potentially be generalised to allow the measures from a harmonic
family to be not necessarily normalised and to depend on time, cf. Section
4.D
). The
only difference is that the arising martingales of the branching Markov chain are not bounded.
Still, they are dominated by the uniformly integrable
population martingale
.

Let us first take a test function
\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
, let

f(x)=f^{\varphi}(x)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle

(3.27)

be the corresponding harmonic function of the underlying Markov chain on
\mathscr{X}
, and let

\widetilde{f}(m)=\langle m,f\rangle=\langle\kappa_{m},\varphi\rangle

be its lift to
\mathscr{M}
. Then by Corollary
1.16
and Remark
3.4
the function
\widetilde{f}
is
\rho
-harmonic for the branching Markov chain, whence the sequence of random variables

W_{n}^{f}=\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,\widetilde{f}(M_{n})=\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,\langle\kappa_{M_{n}}\,,\varphi\rangle

on the path space of the branching Markov chain is a martingale with

\bigl|W_{n}^{f}\bigr|\leq\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,\|\kappa_{M_{n}}\|\cdot\|\varphi\|=\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,\|M_{n}\|\cdot\|\varphi\|=W_{n}\cdot\|\varphi\|\,,

(3.28)

where
W_{n}=W_{n}({\mathbf{M}})
is the population martingale of Definition
0.6
.
Then Theorem
2.3
on the uniform integrability of
(W_{n})
implies the uniform integrability of the martingale
(W_{n}^{f})
as well, so that the limit

W_{\infty}^{f}({\mathbf{M}})=W_{\infty}^{f}=\lim_{n}W_{n}^{f}

(3.29)

exists for almost all sample paths and has the property that

\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}W_{\infty}^{f}=\boldsymbol{\matheu{E}}_{x}W_{0}^{f}=f(x)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathscr{X}\;.

(3.30)

If
\Phi\subset C(\mathscr{K})
is a countable dense subset, then there is a common co-negligible subset
\Omega
of the path space such that the limit (
3.29
) exists and satisfies (
3.30
) for all
{\mathbf{M}}\in\Omega
and any
\varphi\in\Phi
. By (
3.28
)

\bigl|W_{\infty}^{f_{1}}-W_{\infty}^{f_{2}}\bigr|\leq W_{\infty}({\mathbf{M}})\cdot\|\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\|\qquad\forall\,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}\in\Phi\,,

where
f_{i}=f^{\varphi_{i}},\;i=1,2,
are the harmonic functions (
3.27
) associated with the
functions
\varphi_{i}
. Therefore, the limit (
3.29
) exists on
\Omega
for all
\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
and
satisfies condition (
3.30
). For any fixed realisation of
{\mathbf{M}}
on
\Omega
, it defines a
positive linear functional on
C(\mathscr{K})
whose norm is
W_{\infty}^{\mathds{1}}({\mathbf{M}})=W_{\infty}({\mathbf{M}})
, i.e.,
a non-negative Borel measure
\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}
with total mass

\|\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\|=W_{\infty}({\mathbf{M}})

(3.31)

which is strictly positive by Theorem
2.4
. The identity (II) is then precisely the fact that (
3.30
) is satisfied for all
\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
.
Finally, the existence of a stochastically resolutive compactification obviously implies the transience of the underlying chain, and therefore (I) implies (III) in view of Corollary
3.23
.
∎

In the course of the proof of Theorem
3.26
we have seen, in formula (
3.31
), that the norm of the limit measure
\kappa_{\mathbf{M}}
is the limit
W_{\infty}({\mathbf{M}})
of the population martingale, whence we get the following.

Theorem 3.32
.

Under conditions
(1)
and
(2)
of Theorem
3.26
for almost all sample paths

3.D.
Adaptedness conditions

Having in mind the above boundary convergence results, we are now going to list several compactifications of the state space
\mathscr{X}
of a discrete Markov chain (to be thought of as the underlying chain of a branching Markov chain) and comment upon the key properties of these compactifications required in Theorem
3.26
: stochastic resolutivity and Dirichlet regularity.
Suppose that
\mathscr{X}
carries a certain geometric, algebraic or combinatorial structure, and that the
transition operator
P
is adapted in some way (to be specified in more detail) to that
structure. In this situation the Markov chain is usually called
random walk
(so
that the corresponding branching Markov chain becomes a
branching random walk
,
cf. Example
1.24
.)
How does its adaptedness affect the behaviour of the chain?
For the next considerations, we assume that
\mathscr{X}
carries the structure of an unoriented
infinite graph which is

locally finite
, i.e., for every vertex
x\in\mathscr{X}
the cardinality

\deg(x)
of its set of neighbours
\mathcal{N}(x)
is finite, and
connected
.

(
LFC
)

We denote by
\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{X})\subset\mathscr{X}\times\mathscr{X}
the
edge set
of
\mathscr{X}
and write
d(x,y)
for the
graph distance
on
\mathscr{X}\,
.
We recall that the transition operator
P
is always assumed to satisfy condition (
TC
), i.e.,
to be transient and to have pairwise communicating states. Here is a list of different
basic
geometric

adaptedness conditions. The random walk
(\mathscr{X},P)
with the transition probabilities

p(x,y)=p_{x}(y)
is said to be

•

simple
, if for any
x\in\mathscr{X}
the transition measure
p_{x}
is equidistributed of its set of neighbours
\mathcal{N}(x)
, i.e.,
p(x,y)=1/\deg(x)
for
[x,y]\in E(\mathscr{X})
, and
p(x,y)=0
, otherwise.

•

nearest neighbour
, if

p(x,y)>0
only when
[x,y]\in E(\mathscr{X})
.

•

of
bounded range
, if there is
R<\infty
such that
p(x,y)>0
only when
d(x,y)\leq R
.

•

uniformly irreducible
, if there are
N<\infty
and
\varepsilon>0
such that for any pair

(x,y)\in\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{X})
there is a time
n\leq N
with
p^{(n)}(x,y)\geq\varepsilon
.

One can also impose various
tightness
or
moment
conditions on the distributions of the
distances
d(x,y)
with respect to the transition probabilities
p(x,y)
, e.g., the
uniform first
moment
condition from Definition
2.2
(see
Kaimanovich – Woess

[
KW92
, Section 3]
for a detailed discussion).

We now consider
algebraic
adaptedness.
A graph
\mathscr{X}
is called
vertex transitive
if the action of its

group of automorphisms

\operatorname{\sf Aut}(\mathscr{X})
on the vertex set acts transitively on the
vertex set. This is the case for the
Cayley graph
of any finitely generated group with respect
to a finite symmetric set of generators
S
(i.e.,
[x,y]
is an edge if and only if
x^{-1}y\in S
).
There are also vertex transitive graphs which are
not
Cayley graphs.

Given a transition operator
P
on a state space
\mathscr{X}
(not necessarily endowed with a graph structure), one can define the
automorphism group
of the Markov chain
(\mathscr{X},P)
as

\operatorname{\sf Aut}(\mathscr{X},P)=\{g\in\Perm(\mathscr{X}):p(gx,gy)=p(x,y)\quad\forall\,x,y\in\mathscr{X}\}\;.

where
\Perm(\mathscr{X})
denotes the group of all permutations (not necessarily finitely supported!) of
\mathscr{X}
.
A natural algebraic adaptedness condition in this situation is to require that

\operatorname{\sf Aut}(\mathscr{X},P)
(or a subgroup) act transitively on
\mathscr{X}
, or at least
quasi-transitively
,
which consists in requiring that the action of the corresponding automorphism group have finitely
many orbits. This condition is satisfied for so-called
random walks with internal degrees of freedom
(also known under numerous other names, in particular, as
semi-Markov, covering,
or
coloured
chains), e.g., see
Kaimanovich – Woess

[
KW02
]
and the references therein.

3.E.
A zoo of compactifications

We now display several “geometric” compactifications of a graph
\mathscr{X}
satisfying conditions (
LFC
) and discuss if and how Theorem
3.26
applies.

Example 3.35

(end compactification)
.

This definition goes back to Freudenthal
[
Fre45
]
and Halin
[
Hal64
]
.
We denote by
{\mathcal{C}}(F)
the (finite!) collection of all infinite connected components of
the graph
\mathscr{X}\setminus F
obtained from
\mathscr{X}
by removing a finite set of edges
F\subset\mathcal{E}(\mathscr{X})
.
The
end compactification

\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{E}=\mathscr{X}\cup\partial_{E}\mathscr{X}
is the unique (up to homomorphisms)
minimal compactification of
\mathscr{X}
to which all the indicator functions
{\mathds{1}}_{C}
of connected components

C\in{\mathcal{C}}(F)
extend continuously. The
space of ends

\partial_{E}\mathscr{X}
is the projective limit of the
discrete spaces
{\mathcal{C}}(F)
as
F\to\mathscr{X}
. There is also a more explicit graph-theoretical description.

Example 3.36

(hyperbolic compactification)
.

A graph
\mathscr{X}
is called
hyperbolic
, if it is a
Gromov-hyperbolic metric space
with respect to the standard graph metric. We refrain from re-stating all features of hyperbolic spaces and groups.
See the original paper by
Gromov

[
Gro87
]
(and its numerous renditions), or, in the context
of random walks on graphs and groups,
Woess

[
Woe00
, §22]
. A hyperbolic graph
\mathscr{X}
has a

hyperbolic compactification

\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{H}
with the
hyperbolic boundary

\partial_{H}\mathscr{X}
.

Example 3.37

(Floyd compactification)
.

Let
\mathsf{f}:{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\to(0,\infty)
be a summable function such that there is
0<c<1
with

c\,\mathsf{f}(n)\leq\mathsf{f}(n+1)\leq\mathsf{f}(n)\qquad\forall\,n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\;.

We define the
\mathsf{f}
-length

\ell_{\mathsf{f}}(\pi)
of any finite path
\pi
in
\mathscr{X}
as the sum of the
\mathsf{f}
-lengths

\ell_{\mathsf{f}}(e)=\min\{\mathsf{f}(|x|),\mathsf{f}(|y|)\}

of its edges
e=[x,y]
, where as usual
|x|=d(x,o)
denotes the graph distance between
x
and a fixed root vertex
o
. The
Floyd distance
on
\mathscr{X}
is the resulting path metric

d_{\mathsf{f}}(x,y)=\inf\{\ell_{\mathsf{f}}(\pi):\pi\textrm{ is a finite path from $x$ to $y$ }\}\;.

We denote by
\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{\mathsf{f}}
the completion of
\mathscr{X}
with respect to this metric, with the resulting

Floyd boundary

\partial_{\mathsf{f}}\mathscr{X}=\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{\mathsf{f}}\setminus\mathscr{X}
. The space
\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{\mathsf{f}}
is compact and does not
depend on the choice of the root
o
(the identity map on
\mathscr{X}
extends to a homeomorphisms between the
compactifications corresponding to different roots), see
Floyd

[
Flo80
]
and
Karlsson

[
Kar03a
,
Kar03b
,
Kar03c
]
.

The end, the hyperbolic (provided the graph is hyperbolic), and the Floyd compactifications have the following common features (see the aforementioned references):

•

The action of the group of automorphisms
\operatorname{\sf Aut}(\mathscr{X})
on
\mathscr{X}
extends to its action on the whole compactification by homeomorphisms;

•

If the graph is vertex-transitive, then the boundary of the compactification consists of one, two, or uncountably many points;

•

All these compactifications are
contractive
\operatorname{\sf Aut}(\mathscr{X})
-compactifications
in the sense of
Woess

[
Woe93
]
.

The Floyd compactification is
finer
than the end one, i.e., there exists a (necessarily surjective)
continuous map
\pi:\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{\mathsf{f}}\to\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{E}
(an extension of the identity map on
\mathscr{X}
) such that the embedding
\mathscr{X}\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{E}
is the composition of the embedding
\mathscr{X}\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{\mathsf{f}}
with
\pi
. If the
graph
\mathscr{X}
is hyperbolic, then the hyperbolic compactification is intermediate between the other two, i.e.,
it is coarser than the Floyd one and finer than the end one (the latter fact was, to our knowledge, first
explicitly stated by
Pavone

[
Pav89
]
):

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{\mathsf{f}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{E}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{H}}

Note that in general even the vertex-transitive graphs with infinitely many ends may be quite far from being
hyperbolic.

The following result from
Woess

[
Woe93
, Section 4]
provides sufficient conditions for the
applicability of Theorem
3.26
:

Proposition 3.38
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a
graph satisfying (
LFC
), and let
\overline{\mathscr{X}}
be one of its

is hyperbolic), or the Floyd one — with

. If the group

The case when

fixes a boundary point is a very special one; we omit the details here.

Next, we review the situation when no group invariance is assumed. We recall that the
spectral radius
of a transition operator

is defined as

under condition (

(see, e.g.

[

]
).

.

Suppose that (

) holds.
The end compactification is stochastically resolutive if one of the following two conditions holds:

(i)

(ii)

Moreover, it is Dirichlet regular if, in addition to

, the following respective conditions hold:

)

(ii

)

.

Boundary convergence of Markov chains is a vast and active area, and the purpose of the examples above is just to convey its flavour as a backdrop for Theorem
3.26
rather than to provide any comprehensive overview. Without going into further details, let us mention, for instance, the related work on the visual compactification of Cartan – Hadamard manifolds, the Busemann (or horospheric) compactification of metric spaces, various compactifications of Riemannian symmetric spaces, boundaries of planar graphs, the Thurston compactification of Teichmüller space, etc. etc.

Let us finally discuss a compactification of the state space
\mathscr{X}
intrinsically determined just by the transition operator
P
. The latter, as always, is assumed to satisfy condition (
TC
), and therefore a normalisation of the Green kernel produces the
Martin kernel

K_{o}(x,y)=\frac{G(x,y)}{G(o,y)}\;,\qquad x,y\in\mathscr{X}\;,

where
o\in\mathscr{X}
is a fixed reference point. The
Martin compactification
is the unique (up to homeomorphisms) minimal compactification
\overline{\mathscr{X}}_{M}=\mathscr{X}\cup\partial_{M}\mathscr{X}
of the state space
\mathscr{X}
to which each function
K_{o}(x,\cdot),\;x\in\mathscr{X}
, extends continuously in the second variable (e.g., see Woess
[
Woe09
]
for a detailed exposition).
The Martin compactification of a
bounded range
Markov operator
P
on a graph
\mathscr{X}
is known to be comparable with the aforementioned geometric compactifications of
\mathscr{X}
in the following situations:

(i)

it is finer than the end compactification;

(ii)

it coincides with the hyperbolic compactification — if
\mathscr{X}
is hyperbolic and
r(P)<1
;

(iii)

it is finer than the Floyd compactification — if
(\mathscr{X},P)
is a random walk on (the Cayley graph of) a finitely generated group.

For (i) and (ii), see
Woess

[
Woe00
, Chapter IV]
and the references therein; (iii) is very
recent and due to
Gekhtman, Gerasimov, Potyagailo and Yang

[
GGPY21
]
.

Every positive harmonic function
h
has an integral representation

h(x)=\int_{\partial_{M}\mathscr{X}}K_{o}(x,\xi)\,d\nu_{o}^{h}(\xi)

for a unique Borel measure
\nu_{o}^{h}
on
\partial_{M}\mathscr{X}
. The Martin compactification is stochastically
resolutive, and the hitting distribution
\nu_{o}
issued from the reference point

is precisely the
representing measure
\nu_{o}^{\mathds{1}}
of the constant harmonic function
{\mathds{1}}(x)\equiv 1
. There are various classes
of Markov chains for which the Martin compactification is Dirichlet regular, but there are also many classes
for which it is not. In any case, at least claim (iii) of Theorem
3.26
always applies to the Martin
compactification.

After mentioning that the Martin boundary considered as a measure space endowed with the family of the representing measures
\nu_{o}^{\mathds{1}}
is isomorphic to the
Poisson boundary
of the chain, we shall now switch to discussing the boundary behaviour of branching Markov chains in the measure-theoretic setting.

4.
Boundary correspondence

4.A.
Motivation: topological case

In the topological setup, as we saw in the previous Section (Theorem
3.26
and Theorem
3.32
),
under suitable conditions there is a natural map

\;{\mathbf{M}}\mapsto\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\,,

which assigns to almost every sample path of the branching Markov chain
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{n})
a
finite positive weak* limit measure

\;\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\;

on a stationary space
\mathscr{K}
. The total mass

\;\|\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\|\;

is the limit of the population martingale (
0.7
), and its normalisation

\;\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}}=\frac{1}{\|\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\|}\,\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}\;

is a random probability measure on
\mathscr{K}
which can be interpreted as a limit of the population averages.
In particular, if
\mathscr{K}=\partial\mathscr{X}
is the boundary of a Dirichlet regular compactification
of the state space
\mathscr{X}
, then
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}}
is the weak* limit of the empirical distributions

\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{n}=\frac{1}{\|M_{n}\|}\,M_{n}

on the populations
M_{n}\,
.

The purpose of this section is to show that the limit measures associated with the sample paths of the branching Markov chain can also be defined by entirely measure-theoretical means not involving any topological convergence, as the transition probabilities of a certain Markov transfer operator acting between two measure spaces. This will allow us to define the limit distributions of the branching Markov chain on the measure-theoretical boundaries of the underlying chain.

Before proceeding further, let us return to Theorem
3.32
. It provides a measurable family of probability
measures
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}}
on the stationary space
\mathscr{K}
parameterised by the sample paths of the branching Markov chain
{\mathbf{M}}
. Considered as a Markov kernel from the path space
\mathscr{M}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
to
\mathscr{K}
, this family gives rise to a positive norm 1 linear operator

\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{K}}:\varphi\mapsto\mathscr{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}}\varphi\;,\qquad\text{where}\quad\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K}),\quad\mathscr{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}}\varphi=\langle\,\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}},\varphi\rangle\;,

(4.1)

from
C(\mathscr{K})
to the space
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{M}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}})
of bounded measurable functions on the path space of the branching chain with respect to the default measure class (
FS
).

We will now go in the opposite direction by first defining an appropriate transfer operator and then using it to produce the associated family of boundary measures.

4.B.
Tail and Poisson boundaries

We begin with reminding the basic definitions and facts from the measurable boundary theory of Markov chains, see
Kaimanovich

[
Kai92
]
and the references therein for more details. Given a transition operator
P
on a countable state space
\mathscr{X}
(or, equivalently, the corresponding family of transition probabilities), this theory provides an integral representation of bounded harmonic functions, or, more generally, of bounded
harmonic sequences
(
\equiv

space-time harmonic functions
)

f_{n}=Pf_{n+1}\qquad\forall\,n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\;.

By
{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}^{\infty}
we denote the
\sigma
-algebra on the path space
\mathscr{X}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}
determined by the positions of the chain at times
\geq n
. The intersection

{\mathfrak{A}}^{\infty}=\bigcap_{n}{\mathfrak{A}}_{n}^{\infty}

is the
tail
\sigma
-algebra
of the Markov chain
(\mathscr{X},P)
, and it gives rise to the
tail boundary

\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
defined in the measure category by using
Rokhlin
’s correspondence between (complete) sub-
\sigma
-algebras of Lebesgue measure spaces and their quotient spaces; see e.g.

Coudène

[
Cou16
, Chapter 15]
.
We denote the corresponding quotient map by

\tail=\tail_{P}:\mathscr{X}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\to\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}\;.

(4.2)

The tail boundary is endowed with the
harmonic measure class
, which is the
\tail
image of the
default measure class (
FS
) on the path space, and the notation
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
refers to the
harmonic measure class. Any initial position
(n,x)
from the
space-time

{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\times X
determines
the associated
harmonic
probability measure
\eta_{(n,x)}
on
\mathcal{T}_{P}^{\mathscr{X}}
absolutely continuous with
respect to the harmonic measure class, and

f(n,x)=f_{n}(x)=\bigl\langle\eta_{(n,x)},\widehat{f}\;\bigr\rangle

(4.3)

is a space-time
P
-harmonic function for any
\widehat{f}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
. Equivalently,
f
is a
harmonic function of the
space-time Markov chain
on
{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\times X
(for which the spatial
transitions are accompanied by increasing the time coordinate by one). Conversely, a space-time
function
f=(f_{n})
is
P
-harmonic if and only if the sequence of its values
f_{n}(X_{n})
along
the sample paths of the Markov chain is a
martingale
with respect to the increasing
coordinate filtration of the path space. In particular, if
f
is bounded, then the limit

\lim_{n}f_{n}(X_{n})=\widehat{f}(\tail{\mathbf{X}})

(4.4)

exists and is measurable with respect to the tail
\sigma
-algebra, i.e., it determines a function
\widehat{f}

in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
. Formulas (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) establish an isometric
isomorphism of
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
and of the space of bounded space-time
P
-harmonic functions
endowed with the
\sup
norm.

In the same way one defines (also in the measure category) the
Poisson boundary

\partial_{P}X

responsible for an integral representation of bounded
P
-harmonic functions (whence the name
alluding to the classical Poisson formula for harmonic functions on the unit disk). It is the
quotient of the path space under the
boundary map

\bnd=\bnd_{P}:\mathscr{X}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\to\partial_{P}X\;,

determined by the
exit
\sigma
-algebra
(the sub-algebra of the tail
\sigma
-algebra consisting of shift invariant sets — this is why this
\sigma
-algebra is also sometimes called
invariant
). The following commutative diagram illustrates the relationship between the path space, the tail and the Poisson boundaries:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathscr{X}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\partial_{P}\mathscr{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bnd}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\tail}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\matheu{p}}

(4.5)

The Poisson boundary can be interpreted as the
space of ergodic components
of the transformation
T
of the tail boundary induced by the time shift on the path space, and the resulting projection is the map
\matheu{p}:\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}\to\partial_{P}\mathscr{X}
in the above diagram. Formulas (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) restricted to the space of bounded
P
-harmonic functions (i.e., of space-time harmonic functions constant in time) establish its isometric isomorphism with the subspace of
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
that consists of
T
-invariant functions. In terms of the Poisson boundary this isomorphism takes the form of the
Poisson formula

f(x)=\bigl\langle\nu_{x},\widehat{f}\;\bigr\rangle\;,

where
\widehat{f}\in L^{\infty}(\partial_{P}X)
, and
\nu_{x}
are the
harmonic measures
on the Poisson boundary, i.e., the images of the measures
\mathbf{P}_{\!x}
on the path space under the boundary map
\bnd
—  or, equivalently, the images of the measures
\eta_{x}=\eta_{(0,x)}
on
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
under the quotient map
\matheu{p}:\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}\to\partial_{P}\mathscr{X}\,
.

We now provide a characterisation of the topological
P
-boundaries of a Markov chain in terms of quotients of the Poisson boundary mentioned after Definition
3.14
.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
P
be a Markov operator on a countable state space
\mathscr{X}
, and let
(\mathscr{K},\kappa)
be a compact separable
P
-stationary space. It is a
P
-boundary if and only if, as a measure space, it is a quotient of the Poisson boundary
\partial_{P}\mathscr{X}
, i.e., there exists a measurable map
{\matheu q}:\partial_{P}\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{K}
such that
{\matheu q}(\nu_{x})=\kappa_{x}
for all
x\in\mathscr{X}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{K}
be a
P
-boundary. Then for almost every sample path of the Markov chain
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{n})

the weak* limit

\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}=\operatorname*{\matheu w*-{\lim}}_{t\to\infty}\kappa_{X_{t}}

is a delta measure, which provides a map from the path space to
\mathscr{K}
which is measurable with respect
to the exit
\sigma
-algebra, i.e., a sought for measurable map
{\matheu q}:\partial_{P}\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{K}
, and by formula (
3.8
) from Proposition
3.7

{\matheu q}(\nu_{x})=\kappa_{x}
for all
x\in\mathscr{X}
.

Conversely, let
\mathscr{K}
, as a measure space, be a quotient of the Poisson boundary. Then any test function
\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
, considered as an element of
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K})
, can be lifted to a function
\widehat{f}\in L^{\infty}(\partial_{P}\mathscr{X})
. Let
f=f^{\varphi}
be the associated bounded harmonic function:

f(x)=\langle\nu_{x},\widehat{f}\rangle=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle\;,\qquad x\in\mathscr{X}\;.

Then for almost every sample path of the Markov chain
{\mathbf{X}}=(X_{n})
,

\langle\kappa_{X_{n}},\varphi\rangle=f(X_{n})\to\widehat{f}(\bnd{\mathbf{X}})=\varphi({\matheu q}\circ\bnd{\mathbf{X}})\;,

i.e.,

\upkappa_{\mathbf{X}}=\delta_{{\matheu q}\circ\bnd{\mathbf{X}}}\;.

∎

4.C.
Boundaries of branching Markov chains

Now we pass to the branching chain on
\mathscr{M}
determined by the transition probabilities
\Pi_{m}
(
1.8
), or, equivalently, by the transition operator
\mathcal{P}
(
1.9
).

We denote the tail boundary of the branching Markov chain by
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
, and the Poisson boundary by
\partial_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
. By
\upeta_{(t,m)}
and
\upnu_{m}
we denote the harmonic measures on the respective tail and Poisson boundaries corresponding to an initial population
m\in\mathscr{M}
(replacing, as in §
1.B
, the subscript
\delta_{x}
with
x
if the initial population is a singleton
\delta_{x}
).

We recall that in what concerns
random walks on countable groups

(cf. Example
1.24
), the difference between the tail and the Poisson boundaries is not very significant. They do coincide in the aperiodic case (
Derriennic

[
Der76
]
); otherwise the fibres of the projection
\matheu{p}
(
4.5
) of the tail boundary onto the Poisson boundary are parameterised by the periodicity classes of the random walk (
Jaworski

[
Jaw95
]
), in particular,
\matheu{p}
is always a bijection with respect to a one-point initial distribution (the latter fact plays a key role in the entropy theory of random walks on groups, see
Derriennic

[
Der80
]
and
Kaimanovich – Vershik

[
KV83
]
). The situation is similar for
random walks on graphs
under the
uniform ellipticity
condition (the transition probabilities between any two neighbouring vertices are bounded away from 0): in this case the tail and the Poisson boundaries also coincide with respect to any one-point initial distribution, and the cardinality of the fibres of the projection
\matheu{p}
is at most 2, see
Kaimanovich

[
Kai92
, Corollary 2 on p. 162]
.

Branching Markov chains are manifestly space inhomogeneous, and the difference between their tail and
Poisson boundaries is much more pronounced. It can be illustrated already by the simplest example:

Example 4.7
.

Consider the
Galton – Watson process (Example
1.18
). Let
\rho>1
be the mean of a non-degenerate offspring
distribution
\pi\in\Meas(\mathbb{N})
that satisfies the
L\log L
moment condition. Then, assuming the non-
extinction condition (
NE
) (as always in this paper), for almost every sample path of the Galton
– Watson process
(Z_{n})
there exists the limit

W_{\infty}=\lim_{n}Z_{n}/\rho^{n}>0

(cf. Section
2.A
and Section
4.D
) which is clearly tail measurable. It was proved by

Lootgieter

[
Loo77
, Corollaire 2.3.II, Corollaire 3.3.II]
that the limit
W_{\infty}

completely describes the tail behaviour of the Galton – Watson process with respect to any one-point
initial distribution, or, in the aperiodic case, with respect to any initial distribution.
(According to
Cohn

[
Coh79
, pp. 420-421]
, this result was also independently obtained by
B. M. Brown in an unpublished 1977 manuscript “The tail
\sigma
-field of a branching process”.)
Therefore, under the
L\log L
moment condition the tail boundary of the Galton – Watson process
coincides with the product of the positive ray
\mathbb{R}_{+}
by the finite set of periodicity classes, or just with
\mathbb{R}_{+}
in the aperiodic
case. The arising limit measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(the distribution of
W_{\infty}
) is actually absolutely
continuous, and its support is the whole ray
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, see
Athreya – Ney

[
AN72
, Theorem I.10.4]
. The time shift on the path space amounts to the multiplication of the limit
W_{\infty}
by
\rho
. The corresponding action of the group
{\mathbb{Z}}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is dissipative, and therefore its space of
ergodic components (
\equiv
the Poisson boundary of the Galton – Watson process) can be identified with
the fundamental interval
[1,\rho)\,
.

This identification of the Poisson boundary of Galton – Watson processes was first obtained — in somewhat different terms — by
Dubuc

[
Dub71
, Theorem 2]
under the finite second moment condition.

Remark 4.8
.

It seems plausible that the tail and the Poisson boundaries admit a similar description for branching Markov chains over any
finite
state space (
\equiv
multi-type branching processes with a finite number of types). As far as we know, this question has not been addressed in the literature.

If one passes to branching Markov chains over an infinite state space, then the problem of identification
of the tail and the Poisson boundaries appears to be horizon-less. This is indicated by the abundance
of various martingales already in the simplest case of branching random walks on
{\mathbb{Z}}
(e.g., see

Shi

[
Shi15
, Chapter 3]
). To the best of our knowledge, this problem has not been
formulated even in the aforementioned
{\mathbb{Z}}
case. We are now going
to provide links between measure-theoretic boundaries of a branching
Markov chain and that of the underlying chain.

4.D.
Harmonic martingales

Given two functions
f
and
g
on the state space
\mathscr{X}
, their respective lifts
\widetilde{f}
and
\widetilde{g}
to
\mathscr{M}
(see Section
1.C
) have the property that
\mathcal{P}\widetilde{f}=\widetilde{g}
if and only if
\overline{\pi}\cdot Pf=g
,
see Proposition
1.14
and recall that
x\mapsto\overline{\pi}_{x}
is the assignment of the branching ratio at

x
. We immediately get the following.

Proposition 4.9
.

The lifts
\widetilde{f}_{n}
of a sequence of functions
f_{n}
on
\mathscr{X}
form a space-time
\mathcal{P}
-harmonic function
on
\mathscr{M}
, i.e.,

\widetilde{f_{n}}=\mathcal{P}\,\widetilde{f}_{n+1}\qquad\forall\,n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}

if and only if on
\mathscr{X}

f_{n}=\overline{\pi}\cdot Pf_{n+1}\qquad\forall\,n\in{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\;.

(4.10)

Corollary 4.11
.

If condition (
BR
) is satisfied, then for any space-time
P
-harmonic function
(f_{n})
on
the state space
\mathscr{X}
the sequence

\left(\frac{1}{\rho^{n}}\,\widetilde{f}_{n}\right)

is a space-time
\mathcal{P}
-harmonic function on the population space
\mathscr{M}
.

Definition 4.12
.

The
harmonic martingale
determined by a space-time
P
-harmonic function
f=(f_{n})
on
\mathscr{X}

is the sequence of random variables

W^{f}_{n}({\mathbf{M}})=\frac{\widetilde{f}_{n}(M_{n})}{\rho^{n}}=\frac{\left\langle M_{n},f_{n}\right\rangle}{\rho^{n}}

(4.13)

on the path space of the branching Markov chain
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{n})
.

We denote the pointwise (almost everywhere) limit of the harmonic martingale (
4.13
) by

W^{f}_{\infty}({\mathbf{M}})=\lim_{n}W^{f}_{n}({\mathbf{M}})\;.

The random variable
W^{f}_{\infty}
is tail measurable, and therefore it can be presented as the composition

W^{f}_{\infty}=\matheu w^{f}\circ\tail_{\mathcal{P}}

(4.14)

of the quotient map
\tail_{\mathcal{P}}:\mathscr{M}^{{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{X}
(
4.2
) with the arising measurable
function
\matheu w^{f}
on the tail boundary
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
of the branching Markov chain.

In the particular case when
f={\mathds{1}}
the associated harmonic martingale
(W_{n}^{\mathds{1}})
is precisely the

population martingale

(W_{n})
introduced in Definition
0.6
and studied in Section
2
. We denote by
\matheu w=\matheu w^{\mathds{1}}
the function on the tail boundary determined by the pointwise limit
W_{\infty}=W_{\infty}^{\mathds{1}}
of the population martingale (the
limit population ratio
, see Definition
0.6
).

If the limit population ratio is positive (by Theorem
2.4
this is almost surely the case under the uniform
L\log L
moment condition), then

\displaystyle\frac{\matheu w^{f}}{\matheu w}(\tail{\mathbf{M}})

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{f}_{\infty}}{W_{\infty}}({\mathbf{M}})=\lim_{n}\frac{W^{f}_{n}}{W_{n}}({\mathbf{M}})

(4.15)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}\frac{\left\langle M_{n}\,,f_{n}\right\rangle}{\|M_{n}\|}=\lim_{n}\Bigl\langle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{n}\,,f_{n}\Bigr\rangle

is nothing but the
limit empirical average
of the functions
f_{n}
along the branching Markov chain.

Remark 4.16
.

In the context of branching Markov chains the term “harmonic martingale” was used by

Biggins – Cohn – Nerman

[
BCN99
]
for the sequence (in our notation)

\widetilde{\varphi}_{n}(M_{n})
, where
(\varphi_{n})
is a sequence of functions on
\mathscr{X}
such that its lift to the space of populations
(\widetilde{\varphi}_{n})
is a space-time
\mathcal{P}
-harmonic function. Actually, in the setup of

[
BCN99
]
the state space
\mathscr{X}
is endowed with a
space-time partition

into pairwise disjoint
levels

\mathscr{X}_{n},\;n\geq 0
such that
\mathscr{X}_{0}
consists of a single
state
x_{0}
, the branching chain starts at time 0 from a single particle sitting at
x_{0}
, and at each
step the population moves to the next level, so that the time
n
random population
M_{n}
is concentrated
on
\mathscr{X}_{n}
. Therefore, the sequence
(\varphi_{n})
can be considered as a
single
function
\varphi
on the
state space
\mathscr{X}
with the property that its lift
\widetilde{\varphi}
is a
\mathcal{P}
-harmonic function on the population
space
\mathscr{M}
. Such functions on
\mathscr{X}
are called
mean-harmonic
by
Biggins – Kyprianou

[
BK04
]
. Our setup is slightly more general as we deal with the space-time harmonic
functions which do not necessarily come from a space-time partition of the state space. We feel that it is
more consistent to deal with the space-time harmonic functions (instead of the time constant ones) from the
very beginning. The reason is that martingales, by their very nature, are linked with the
tail

\sigma
-algebra (rather than with the exit one), cf. Section
4.B
and Theorem
4.17
.

4.E.
Boundary transfer operator

Below we are going to use the standard facts from the measurable theory of Markov operators,
e.g., see
Foguel

[
Fog80
]
. We recall that, given two measure spaces
(X,\mu_{X})
and
(Y,\mu_{Y})
, a linear operator

B:L^{\infty}(Y,\mu_{Y})\to L^{\infty}(X,\mu_{X})

is called
Markov
if it preserves constants, is positive and order continuous, i.e.,

(i)

B{\mathds{1}}_{Y}={\mathds{1}}_{X}
,

(ii)

B\varphi\geq 0
for any
\varphi\geq 0
,

(iii)

B\varphi_{k}\downarrow 0
for any sequence
\varphi_{k}\downarrow 0
.

As we have explained in Section
4.B
, formulas (
4.3
) and (
4.4
) establish an isometric isomorphism
\widehat{f}\mapsto f=(f_{n})
of
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
and of the Banach space of bounded space-time
P
-harmonic functions on the state space
\mathscr{X}
endowed with the
\sup
norm.

Theorem 4.17
.

If the offspring distributions of a branching Markov chain with property (
NE
)
satisfy the uniform
L\log L
moment condition, then the map

B:\widehat{f}\mapsto\frac{\matheu w^{f}}{\matheu w}\;,\qquad L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})\;,

(4.18)

is a Markov operator. Here
\matheu w^{f}/\matheu w
is the function (
4.15
) on the tail boundary
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
that represents the limit empirical averages of the space-time
harmonic function
f=(f_{n})
determined by
\widehat{f}
.

Proof.

Property (i) from the definition of a Markov operator follows from Theorem
2.4
, whereas (ii) is obvious. We just have to verify property (iii), i.e., the order continuity of
B
. It is here that we use the uniform integrability of the population martingale which implies that the operator
B
preserves the integrals with respect to appropriately chosen measures on the tail boundaries
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
.

The first observation is that the uniform integrability of the population martingale (Theorem
2.3
) implies the uniform integrability of the harmonic martingale
\bigl(W^{f}_{n}\bigr)
for any
bounded
space-time harmonic function
f=(f_{n})
on
\mathscr{X}
. Thus, for any initial population
m\in\mathscr{M}

\langle\,\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{m},W^{f}_{0}\rangle=\langle\,\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{m},W^{f}_{\infty}\rangle\;,

or, in view of (
4.13
) and (
4.14
),

\langle m,f_{0}\rangle=\langle\upeta_{m},\matheu w^{f}\rangle\;,

where
\upeta_{m}=\tail(\boldsymbol{\matheu{P}}_{m})
is the harmonic measure on the tail boundary
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
corresponding to the initial distribution
\delta_{m}
. In terms of the boundary function
\widehat{f}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
representing
f
we then have

\bigl\langle\eta_{m},\widehat{f}\,\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\matheu w\!\cdot\!\upeta_{m},B\widehat{f}\,\bigr\rangle\;,

(4.19)

where

\eta_{m}=\sum_{x\in m}\eta_{x}\;,

and
\matheu w\!\cdot\!\upeta_{m}
is the measure on
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
with the density
\matheu w
with respect to the harmonic measure
\upeta_{m}\,
, so that

\|\eta_{m}\|=\|\matheu w\!\cdot\!\upeta_{x}\|=\|m\|\;.

In view of the monotone convergence theorem, identity (
4.19
) then implies that if

{\widehat{f}}^{\,(k)}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
with
{\widehat{f}}^{\,(k)}\downarrow 0
almost everywhere, then

B{\widehat{f}}^{\,(k)}\downarrow 0
almost everywhere with respect to all measures
\matheu w\!\cdot\!\upeta_{m}\,,\;m\in\mathscr{M}
, which by Theorem
2.4
is the same as the almost everywhere convergence with respect to the harmonic measure class on
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}
.
∎

If a measurable space
\mathscr{K}
is a quotient of the tail boundary
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
, then the precomposition of the transfer operator
B
(
4.18
) constructed in Theorem
4.17
with the lift

L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K})\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})

provides a Markov operator

B_{\mathscr{K}}:L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K})\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})\;,

(4.20)

which we are going to compare with the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{K}}
(
4.1
). Since the map
{\mathbf{M}}\mapsto\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}
is tail measurable by the definition of the measures
\upkappa_{\mathbf{M}}
in Theorem
3.26
, the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{K}}
produces tail measurable functions on the path space, and therefore its range can be identified with the space
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
4.17
, if
\mathscr{K}
be a compact separable
P
-boundary of the underlying Markov chain
(\mathscr{X},P)
, then the restriction of the operator
B_{\mathscr{K}}
(
4.20
) to the space
C(\mathscr{K})
coincides with the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{K}}
(
4.1
).

Proof.

Take a test function
\varphi\in C(\mathscr{K})
. Then by the definition of the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{K}}
and by Theorem
3.32
for almost every sample path
{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{t})

\mathscr{B}_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}}\varphi(\tail{\mathbf{M}})=\langle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}},\varphi\rangle=\lim_{t}\langle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\kappa}_{M_{t}},\varphi\rangle\;,

whereas by (
4.15
)

B_{\mathchoice{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\displaystyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\textstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}{\raisebox{-2.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathscr{K}$}}}\varphi(\tail{\mathbf{M}})=\lim_{t}\Bigl\langle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{t},f\Bigr\rangle\;,

where
f(x)=\langle\kappa_{x},\varphi\rangle
, and therefore
\Bigl\langle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{t},f\Bigr\rangle=\langle\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\kappa}_{M_{t}},\varphi\rangle
.
∎

4.F.
Boundary measures

Theorem 4.22
.

If the offspring distributions of a branching Markov chain satisfy the uniform
L\log L
moment condition,
then the tail boundary of the underlying chain
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
is endowed with a family of probability
measures
\eta^{\,\xi}
indexed by the points
\xi\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}
from the tail boundary of the branching
chain with the following properties:

(i)

The family
\{\eta^{\,\xi}\}
is measurable in the sense that for any function
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
the integrals
\langle\eta^{\,\xi},\varphi\rangle
depend on
\xi
measurably.

(ii)

If
\mathscr{K}
is a compact separable
P
-boundary, then for almost every sample path of the branching chain

{\mathbf{M}}=(M_{n})
, the limit measure
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}}
on
\mathscr{K}
from Theorem
3.32
is the image

{\matheu q}(\eta^{\,\xi})
of the measure
\eta^{\,\xi},\;\xi=\tail{\mathbf{M}},
under the quotient map
{\matheu q}:\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{K}
.

(iii)

In particular, if
\partial\mathscr{X}
is the boundary of a Dirichlet regular compactification of the state space

\mathscr{X}
, then the empirical distributions
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{M}_{n}
almost surely weak* converge to the image of the
measure
\eta^{\,\xi},\;\xi=\tail{\mathbf{M}},
under the quotient map from the tail boundary
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
to
\partial\mathscr{X}
.

Proof.

We will construct the measures
\eta^{\,\xi}
as the transition probabilities of the Markov operator
B
from Theorem
4.17
.

We denote by
L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
the Banach space of finite measures absolutely continuous with respect to the
harmonic measure class on the tail boundary
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
of the underlying chain and endowed with the total
variation norm. We emphasise that this space — in the same way as its dual
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
— is
defined “coordinate free”, just in terms of the harmonic
measure class
. If one takes a reference
measure
\lambda
from this class, then the elements of
L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
can be identified with their densities
with respect to
\lambda
, after which
L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
becomes the “usual” space
L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X},\lambda)
.
Likewise, we denote by
L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{M})
the analogous space associated with the tail boundary of the
branching Markov chain.

Being Markov, the operator

B:L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})

from Theorem
4.17
is dual to an operator

\lambda\mapsto\lambda B\;,\qquad L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})\to L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})\;,

and, as we saw in the course of the proof of Theorem
4.17
, formula (
4.19
),

\eta_{m}=(\matheu w\!\cdot\!\upeta_{m})B\qquad\forall\,m\in\mathscr{M}\;.

For any initial probability measure
\lambda\in L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})
the operator
B
gives rise to the associated
joint distribution
\lambda^{B}
on the product
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{X}
whose marginal distributions are the
measures
\lambda
and
\lambda B
. Since all involved measure spaces are Lebesgue spaces, the conditional measures

\eta^{\,\xi},\;\xi\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M},
on the fibres
\{\xi\}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{X}\cong\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{X}
of the
projection

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{X}\to\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M}

are well-defined and their dependence on
\xi
is measurable. Their system does not depend (mod 0) on the choice of
\lambda
and provides the transition probabilities that determine the operator
B
, so that

\lambda B=\int\eta^{\,\xi}\,d\lambda(\xi)

for any
\lambda\in L^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{P}}\mathscr{M})
.

Claim (ii) then follows from Theorem
4.21
. Indeed, the operator
B_{\mathscr{K}}
(
4.20
) being the result of the precomposition of the operator
B
(
4.18
) with the lift
L^{\infty}(\mathscr{K})\to L^{\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X})
determined by the quotient map
{\matheu q}:\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{K}
, its transition probabilities are the
{\matheu q}
-images of the transition probabilities
\eta^{\,\xi}
of the operator
B
. On the other hand, by Theorem
4.21
the operator
B_{\mathscr{K}}
has the same transition probabilities as the operator
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{K}}
(
4.1
), whereas the latter ones are, by definition, the measures
\overset{\mathchoice{}{}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.50694pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}{\mbox{\raisebox{-1.05487pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle\bm{\frown}$}}}}{\upkappa}_{\mathbf{M}}\,
.

Finally, claim (iii) now follows from Theorem
3.32
.
∎

Remark 4.23
.

Although the measure
\lambda B
on
\mathcal{T}_{P}\mathscr{X}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the harmonic measure class, the measures
\eta^{\,\xi}
need
not
be absolutely continuous. An extreme example is provided by the situation when there is no branching (
\rho=1
), and the branching Markov chain is reduced to an ordinary one (Example
1.17
).
