A note on one-dimensional Poincaré inequalities by Stein-type integration
Gilles Germain, Yvik Swan

Abstract
. We study the weighted Poincaré constant C ⁡ ( p , w ) C(p,w) of a probability density p p with weight function w w using integration methods inspired by Stein’s method. We obtain a new version of the Chen-Wang variational formula which, as a byproduct, yields simple upper and lower bounds on C ⁡ ( p , w ) C(p,w) in terms of the so-called Stein kernel of p p . We also iterate these variational formulas so as to build sequences of nested intervals containing the Poincaré constant, sequences of functions converging to said constant, as well as sequences of functions converging to the solutions of the corresponding spectral problem. Our results rely on the properties of a pseudo inverse operator of the classical Sturm-Liouville operator. We illustrate our methods on a variety of examples: Gaussian functionals, weighted Gaussian, beta, gamma, Subbotin, and Weibull distributions.



1.
Introduction and overview of main results

Given a measure with density
p
on the real line, and a weight function

w>0
, we say that
p
satisfies a weighted Poincaré inequality with
weight
w
if there exists
C>0
such that

\mathrm{Var}_{p}[h]\leq C\,\mathbb{E}_{p}[|h^{\prime}|^{2}w]

(1.1)

for all
h
in the Sobolev space
H^{1}(p,w)
which we will define in Section
2.1
. We abbreviate this by

\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
. The smallest constant for which (
1.1
) holds
is called the Poincaré constant of
p
with weight
w
, and denoted by

C(p,w)
. When
C(p,w)<\infty
, we say that a test function
h
saturates

\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
if (
1.1
) is an equality. Estimation of
C(p,w)
and of the corresponding saturating functions is of theoretical importance because of its connections with concentration of measure (see
[
25
,
22
]
), isoperimetric inequalities (see
[
8
,
7
]
) and quantitative central limit theorems (see
[
18
]
). This problem is also of practical importance, with uses ranging from the study of convergence properties of MCMC algorithms (see
[
4
,
3
,
35
]
) to sensitivity analysis
[
30
]
.

Exact solutions are known for some classical measures; for example
C(p,1)=1
for
p
the standard Gaussian,
C(p,1)=4
for
p
the (double) exponential and
C(p,1)=(b-a)^{2}/\pi^{2}
for
p
the uniform on
[a,b]
.
A general bound on
C(p,1)
is provided by the so-called Muckenhoupt criterion (see e.g.
[
4
, Section 4.5.1]
): letting
P
be the cumulative distribution function of
p
,
and
\bar{P}=1-P
the corresponding survival function, it holds that

B/2\leq C(p,1)\leq 4B,

(1.2)

where, letting
m
be a median of
p
,
B=\max(B_{+},B_{-})
with
B_{+}=\sup_{x>m}\bar{P}(x)\int_{m}^{x}1/p(t)\mathrm{d}t
and
B_{-}=\sup_{x<m}{P}(x)\int_{x}^{m}1/p(t)\mathrm{d}t
. In particular,
C(p,1)<\infty
if and only if
B<\infty
. Bound (
1.2
) is, however, not sharp when applied to specific choices of
p
; for instance it yields
0.239406\leq C(p,1)\leq 1.91525
in the standard Gaussian case and
0.5\leq C(p,1)\leq 4
in the exponential case.
Another elegant universal bound (obtained by a transport argument) is

C(p,1)\leq 4\left(\sup_{x\in]a,b[}\frac{P(x)\bar{P}(x)}{p(x)}\right)^{2}\leq 4\left(\sup_{x\in]a,b[}\frac{\min(P(x),\bar{P}(x))}{p(x)}\right)^{2}

(1.3)

where the first inequality is due to
[
30
]
and the second to
[
7
]
. Again these bounds are generally not sharp, for instance in the Gaussian case they yield
C(p,1)\leq\pi/2\approx 1.57
and in the exponential case
C(p,1)\leq 4
.

Sharp bounds on
C(p,w)

can be obtained by couching the problem within the context Sturm-Liouville theory. More precisely, consider the Sturm-Liouville operator for
p
with weight

w
defined for smooth functions
f
as

\mathcal{L}f=f^{\prime\prime}w+f^{\prime}(-V^{\prime}w+w^{\prime})

(1.4)

where
V=-\ln p
. One important property of this operator is that the inverse of its
smallest non-zero eigenvalue is
C(p,w)
(see
[
11
]
). In their paper
[
15
]
, Chen and Wang showed that
C(p,w)

satisfies the following variational upper bound (see
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
):

\frac{1}{C(p,w)}\geq\inf-\frac{(\mathcal{L}f)^{\prime}}{f^{\prime}}

(1.5)

for all smooth increasing functions
f
. Note that the authors of
[
9
]
obtained the same formula through a totally different approach linked to intertwining relations (see
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
).
In
[
14
]
, Chen introduced an iteration of (
1.5
) which allows to approximate both from above and from below the weighted Poincaré constant of a density on the positive real line to arbitrary precision; this in particular leads to a strict improvement on (
1.2
). Chen’s method of proof relies on an operator which we will define in (
1.11
). Such iterative schemes also apply in a discrete context, and are of importance for birth-and-death processes, see e.g.
[
34
]
and the many references therein.

Bounds such as (
1.2
), (
1.3
), (
1.5
), and their variations (or iterations) are useful for
obtaining numerical estimates (and sometimes even exact values) for
explicit densities on the real line. Aside from the above references, we also refer to
[
9
,
10
]
as well as
[
30
]
who give a nice overview of some literature on the topic and also propose an efficient algorithm (and
R
-package) that approximates
C(p,1)
for any one-dimensional density
p
with compact support.

Chen and Wang extend their formula (
1.5
) to higher dimensions using a coupling approach (see
[
15
, Theorem 4.6]
). Other upper bounds are available in particular cases like uniform and radial measures, convolution of measures or when a Lyapunov function exists (see
[
5
,
11
,
17
]
and
[
2
]
), but much remains to be done in this context. However, it is not the object of our paper and we will focus on dimension 1. The extension of our method to higher dimensions seems challenging.

As mentioned above, Poincaré inequalities have been mostly studied through their relationship with
\mathcal{L}
. For specific examples of distributions, it may be possible to use classical Sturm-Liouville methods directly to obtain the corresponding solutions. In this paper, we use another approach, originating in the theory surrounding Stein’s method which, as it turns out, nicely generalises the approach from
[
15
,
14
]
.

Before proceeding to the statement of our results, we first fix some notations. Let
a\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
,

b\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
, and
p\in L^{1}(]a,b[)
be such that
p>0

almost everywhere (a.e.) and the Lebesgue integral of
p
on the
interval
]a,b[
is
\int_{a}^{b}p=1
. The function
p
is thus the
density of a probability measure on the interval
]a,b[
, absolutely
continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. A weight is any function
w\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
such that
w>0
a.e. and
pw\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
. Throughout, we let

X
be distributed according to density
p
and we write the integral of a
function
f
with respect to the corresponding measure as

\mathbb{E}[f(X)]=\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]=\int_{a}^{b}fp=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)p(x)\mathrm{d}x
where the
choice of one notation over the other will be dictated by convenience reasons.
There is no loss of generality in assuming that
p>0
a.e. on
]a,b[
. Indeed, if
p=0
a.e. on
]a,a_{1}]\cup[b_{1},b[
for some
a<a_{1}<b_{1}<b
, we can restrict the domain of
p
to
]a_{1},b_{1}[
without changing the value of
C(p,w)
and if
p=0
a.e. on an interior interval of
]a,b[
, we must have
C(p,w)=\infty
.

We shall need four operators. The first is the Sturm-Liouville operator defined in (
1.4
). The second is the canonical density Stein operator
\mathcal{T}
, defined for a density
p
on the real line by

\mathcal{T}h=\frac{(hp)^{\prime}}{p}

for any function
h
such that
hp
is weakly differentiable on
]a,b[
.
This operator is related to
\mathcal{L}
by the identity
\mathcal{L}h=\mathcal{T}(h^{\prime}w)
. The third operator is the pseudo-inverse Stein operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
(see
[
19
]
) defined, for
f\in L^{1}(p)
, by

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}f(x)=\frac{1}{p(x)}\int_{a}^{x}(f-\mathbb{E}_{p}[f])

(the denomination pseudo-inverse will be explained in Section
2.1
). Our fourth and final operator is the pseudo-inverse Sturm-Liouville operator which we define as

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f=-\frac{1}{w}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\left(\int_{c}^{\cdot}f\right),

(1.6)

for some arbitrary
c\in]a,b[
and
f\in L^{2}(pw)
(the terminology will be explained in Section
2.2
); this operator can be seen to be equivalent to Chen’s operator
II
from
[
14
]
.

As already stated above, Sturm-Liouville theory, through the spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}
and formulas such as (
1.5
), provides a blueprint for (approximately) solving
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
with given
p,w
. Similarly, it is known that properties of
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
allow to obtain explicit Poincaré constants for well chosen weights. Indeed, letting
\mathrm{id}
be the identity function and setting
\tau=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{id}
, we have

\mathrm{Var}_{p}[h]\leq\mathbb{E}_{p}[|h^{\prime}|^{2}\tau]

(1.7)

with equality if
h
is an affine transformation of
\tau
(see e.g.
[
31
,
19
]
). The function
\tau
is called the Stein kernel of
p
, and it thus follows in particular that
C(p,\tau)=1
for any density
p
with finite variance admitting a Stein kernel. As already noted in
[
19
]
, it is not hard to tweak the theory of Stein operators to make appear a connection between “Stein-type variance bounds” such as (
1.7
) and “Chen-Wang-type variance bounds” such as (
1.5
). The purpose of our paper is to study this connection in more detail.

1.1.
Overview of the main results

In order to make the paper easier to read and to use, we now present a streamlined overview of our main results. We refer to later sections for more complete statements along with the corresponding proofs, as well as intermediary side-results that may also be of independent interest.

We begin by introducing two kernels whose properties are at the heart of our approach.

Definition 1.

Let
P=\int_{a}^{\cdot}p
be the cumulative distribution function of
p
,
and
\bar{P}=1-P
the corresponding survival function.
For almost all
x\in]a,b[
we define

K(x,y)=P\big(x\wedge y\big)\bar{P}\big(x\lor y\big)\mbox{ and }k(x,y)=\frac{K(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)}

with
x\wedge y=\min(x,y)
and
x\lor y=\max(x,y)
. For any weight
w
, we set

k_{w}(x,y)={k(x,y)}/{(w(x)w(y))}
.

With this notation in hand, our first main result is a variational formula for Poincaré constants (see Theorem
3.1
).

Theorem 1 (Chen-Wang formula).

For all weakly
differentiable functions
h_{1}\in L^{1}(p)
and
h_{2}\in L^{2}(p)
which are a.e. strictly monotone throughout
]a,b[
it holds that

\inf_{]a,b[}\left\{\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[k(x,X)h_{1}^{\prime}(X)\right]}{w(x)h_{1}^{\prime}(x)}\right\}\leq C(p,w)\leq\sup_{]a,b[}\left\{\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[k(x,X)h_{2}^{\prime}(X)\right]}{w(x)h_{2}^{\prime}(x)}\right\}

(1.8)

(the notations
\inf
and
\sup
must be understood as the essential infimum and supremum).

Upon closer inspection, the upper bound in (
1.8
) is seen to be a equivalent to Chen-Wang’s bound (
1.5
), using (
2.1
) (see
[
19
, Lemma 2.21]
). The main difference is that in (
1.8
) we restrict to functions with mean 0, hereby achieving a minor improvement as illustrated in Proposition
3.2
below (see specifically (
3.2
)).
As already noted in
[
15
]
, the freedom of choice in the functions
h_{1},h_{2}
allows to easily produce non trivial bounds on
C(p,1)
. A quite natural choice, related to Muckenhoupt criterion (
1.2
) and to
[
15
,
14
]
, is
h(x)=\psi(h_{0}(x))
where
h_{0}^{\prime}(x)=1/(pw)(x)
and
\psi
chosen so as to ensure integrability (for instance
\psi(x)=\sqrt{x}
suffices, see
[
14
]
). Another interesting choice is

h_{2}^{\prime}(x)=-p(x)/(P(x)\bar{P}(x))
(which always satisfies
\mathrm{Var}_{p}[h_{2}]=\pi^{2}/3
irrespective of
p
; we will prove this curiosity in Appendix
B
). This yields

\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[k(x,X)h_{2}^{\prime}(X)\right]}{h_{2}^{\prime}(x)}=\frac{P(x)\bar{P}(x)}{p(x)^{2}}\psi(P(x))

with
\psi(y)=-y\log(y)-(1-y)\log(1-y).
It follows that

C(p,w)\leq\sup_{x\in]a,b[}\frac{P(x)\bar{P}(x)}{w(x)p(x)^{2}}\psi(P(x)).

(1.9)

Although
\psi(u)\leq 4u(1-u)
over nearly the whole interval
[0,1]
, it can be seen that (
1.9
) does not imply (and is not implied by) (
1.3
). For instance, in the case of the Gaussian, we have already mentioned that the latter yields
C(p,1)\leq\pi/2\approx 1.57
while (
1.9
) gives
C(p,1)\leq\pi\log(\sqrt{2})\approx 1.09
, whereas for the exponential distribution (
1.3
) yields
C(p,1)\leq 4
and (
1.9
) only produces a trivial bound. We refer to Section
4
for more illustrations.

Another natural choice in (
1.8
) is
h_{1}^{\prime}=h_{2}^{\prime}=-1
. If
p
has finite second moment, noting that
\mathbb{E}\left[k(x,X)\right]=\tau(x)
is the aforementioned Stein kernel of
p
, it then follows that

\inf_{]a,b[}\frac{\tau(x)}{w(x)}\leq C(p,w)\leq\sup_{]a,b[}\frac{\tau(x)}{w(x)}

(1.10)

(only the upper bound holds if
p
only has finite first moment). In particular we immediately read that
C(p,\tau)=1
when
p
has finite second moment, hereby confirming (
1.7
).
This already gives nontrivial bounds for densities with bounded Stein kernel; for instance if
p
is the Gaussian density then
\tau(x)=1
which leads us back to the known value of the Poincaré constant
C(p,1)
in this case. Bound (
1.10
) is not useful for densities with unbounded Stein kernel, as e.g. for the exponential distribution (in which case
\tau(x)=x
). Again, we refer to Section
4
for more illustrations.

Starting from (
1.8
), it is intuitively appealing to iterate the reasoning that leads to Theorem 1 by replacing
h^{\prime}
with
\mathbb{E}[k(\cdot,X)h^{\prime}(X)]/w
; this, as we shall see in Theorem 2 below, indeed leads to sharper bounds on
C(p,w)
. Moreover, since

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}h^{\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{p(x)w(x)}\int_{a}^{b}K(x,y)h^{\prime}(y)dy=\frac{1}{w(x)}\mathbb{E}[k(x,X)h^{\prime}(X)]

(1.11)

for all weakly differentiable function
h\in L^{1}(p)
(see Lemma 2.21 in
[
19
]
), we witness how pseudo-inverse Sturm-Liouville operator introduced in (
1.6
) now comes into play.
As we shall show in Section
2.2
,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is a continuous, self-adjoint, and positive operator whose norm is
C(p,w)
. Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{0}f=f
and define by recurrence
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n})
. Replacing iteratively
h^{\prime}
by
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}h^{\prime}
in (
1.8
), leads to a nested sequence of intervals containing
C(p,w)
, as follows (see Theorem
3.4
).

Theorem 2 (A sequence of nested intervals).
Assume that
C(p,w)<\infty
and
L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
. For all
g_{0}\in L^{2}(pw)
such that
g_{0}>0
a.e. the sequence of intervals

I_{n}=\left[\inf_{]a,b[}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}},\sup_{]a,b[}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}\right]

satisfies
I_{n+1}\subset I_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
C(p,w)\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}
.

When
a=0
, one readily verifies that
[
14
, Theorem 1.4]
follows from Theorem 2, by considering
g_{0}(x)=(pw)(x)^{-1}(\int_{0}^{x}1/(pw))^{-1/2}
. We note that there
is a priori no guarantee that
\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}=\left\{C(p,w)\right\}
. For
example, in the case of the exponential measure on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
with

g_{0}=\text{id}
, we have
I_{n}=[0,\infty[
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In order to obtain convergence results, we have to assume that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact, which is true when
k_{w}\in L^{2}(pw\otimes pw)
(see Proposition
2.13
). Under this condition, the first eigenvector
e_{1}
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is the derivative of the function that saturates
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
(see Proposition
2.8
) and we can recover
e_{1}
by applying recursively
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}/C(p,w)
to any starting function
g_{0}\in L^{2}(pw)
. More precisely, the following holds (see Theorem
3.6
).

Theorem 3 (A sequence converging to
e_{1}
).

Assume that
C(p,w)<\infty
,
L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact. Let
e_{1}
be the first eigenfunction of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
,
g_{0}\in L^{2}(pw)
and
a_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]
. Then

\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}{C(p,w)^{n}}\to a_{1}e_{1},

where the convergence holds in
L^{2}(pw)
.

In some particular cases, Theorem 3 provides
C(p,w)
as well as the corresponding saturating function (see Examples
4.4
and
4.5
). In general, however, one cannot guess
e_{1}
. The result remains useful because it provides sequences converging to
C(p,w)
, as follows (see Theorem
3.7
).

Theorem 4 (A sequence converging to
C(p,w)
).
Assume that
C(p,w)<\infty
,
L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact. For all
g_{0}\in L^{2}(pw)
, it holds that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}(x)}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}(x)}=C(p,w)

for all
x\in]a,b[

such that
g_{0}>0
and
k_{w}(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(pw)
.

We conclude this overview of our main results by noting how

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}(x)=\int_{a}^{b}\ldots\int_{a}^{b}\frac{K(x,x_{1})}{p(x)w(x)}\frac{K(x_{1},x_{2})}{p(x_{1})w(x_{1})}\ldots\frac{K(x_{n-1},x_{n})}{p(x_{n-1})w(x_{n-1})}g_{0}(x_{n})dx_{n}\ldots dx_{1}.

If moreover
\int_{a}^{b}pw<\infty
, we can normalize
w
in order that
pw
is
the density of a probability measure. Hence, the previous equality can
be reformulated as

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}(x)=\mathbb{E}\left[k_{w}(x,X_{1})k_{w}(X_{1},X_{2})\ldots k_{w}(X_{n-1},X_{n})g_{0}(X_{n})\right]

where
X_{1},\ldots X_{n}
are independent and identically distributed (iid) with density
pw
; this last formula leads, for any reasonable choice of starting function
g_{0}
, to easily implemented and numerically stable approximations of
C(p,w)
.

Structure of the paper

The rest of the paper is as follows. In Section

2.
Preliminaries

Most of the results in this section are extensions (or particularizations) of material already available from the literature. In order to keep the paper self-contained (and also because, in some instances, our assumptions are different from those in the literature), we propose bespoke proofs in Appendix
B
.

2.1.
The pseudo-inverse Stein operator

Let
V=-\ln p
. Following

[
19
]
, we define the canonical Stein operators for
p
as

\mathcal{T}f=\frac{(fp)^{\prime}}{p}=f^{\prime}-Vf\mbox{ and }\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h=\frac{1}{p}\int_{a}^{\cdot}(h-\mathbb{E}_{p}[h])p

for a function
f
such that
fp
is weakly differentiable on the
one hand, and
h\in L^{1}(p)
on the other hand.
As shown in
[
19
, Lemma 2.6]
,

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
is a pseudo-inverse of
\mathcal{T}
in the
sense that
(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\circ\mathcal{T})f=f
if
\mathbb{E}_{p}[(fp)^{\prime}]=0

while
(\mathcal{T}\circ\widetilde{\mathcal{T}})h=h-\mathbb{E}_{p}[h]
. Moreover, if
h
itself is
furthermore also weakly differentiable,
it holds that

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h(x)=-\frac{1}{p(x)}\int_{a}^{b}K(x,y)h^{\prime}(y)dy=-\mathbb{E}\left[k(x,X)h^{\prime}(X)\right]

(2.1)

where
X\sim p
and the kernels
K
and
k
above are defined in Definition 1 (see
[
19
, Lemma 2.21]
).
We say that a weakly differentiable function
h
is increasing if
h^{\prime}\geq 0
a.e. The same convention holds for a monotone function.
We recall that
L^{2}(p)
and
L^{2}(pw)
are
separable Hilbert spaces (see
[
23
, Section 3.2 and Proposition
3.5.5]
) and define the Sobolev space

H^{1}(p,w)=\left\{h\in L^{2}(p):h\text{ is weakly differentiable and
}h^{\prime}\in L^{2}(pw)\right\}.

Note how, if
h\in H^{1}(p,w)
, then both

h
and
h^{\prime}
belong to
L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
. The following holds.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
C(p,w)<\infty
, every
f\in H^{1}(p,w)
can be written
f=f_{1}-f_{2}
for some increasing functions
f_{1},\,f_{2}\in H^{1}(p,w)
.

One of the most remarkable facts about
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
is that it appears in the following Hoeffding-type covariance
representation taken from
[
32
, Corollary 2.4]
.

Theorem 2.2

(Hoeffding-type covariance identity)
.

Let
g,h\in L^{1}(p)
be weakly differentiable and increasing. Then

\mathrm{Cov}_{p}[g,h]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[g^{\prime}(-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h)\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[h^{\prime}(X)k(X,Y)g^{\prime}(Y)\right]

(2.2)

where

X,Y\sim p
are taken independent. These equalities also hold if

g\in H^{1}(p,w_{1})
and
h\in H^{1}(p,w_{2})
for some weights
w_{1},w_{2}

such that
C(p,w_{i})<\infty
for
i=1,2
.

We will only use this result in the case of two functions
in
H^{1}(p,w)
for the same
w
, but we emphasize that it holds in a
more general setting.

Using the Hoeffding covariance identity, we can design a set of weights with
finite Poincaré constants (see
[
19
, Corollary 3.6]
).
For this purpose, we need the next lemma which says that the maximization in
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
can be restricted to increasing functions (such a result is not new and can, for instance, be read from
[
26
]
).

Lemma 2.3

(Monotonicity)
.

For all non monotone functions
h\in H^{1}(p,w)
, we can find an increasing function
g\in H^{1}(p,w)
such that

\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{p}[h]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}[|h^{\prime}|^{2}]}<\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{p}[g]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}[|g^{\prime}|^{2}]}.

Theorem 2.4

(Papathanasiou-type upper bound)
.

Let
h\in L^{1}(p)
be weakly differentiable such that
h^{\prime}<0
a.e. and define
w_{h}={-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h}/{h^{\prime}}
. Then

C(p,w_{h})\leq 1
. In other words,

\mathrm{Var}_{p}[g]\leq\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\frac{-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h}{h^{\prime}}\left|g^{\prime}\right|^{2}\right]

for all
g\in H^{1}(p,w_{h})
.
Equality holds if and only if
h\in L^{2}(p)

and
g
is an affine transformation of
h
. Hence,
C(p,w_{h})=1
if
h\in L^{2}(p)
.

Aside from the requirement of monotonicity, there is near total freedom of choice for the
function
h
in Theorem
2.4
; the choice

h=-\text{id}
(recall that
id
is the identity function) is intuitively a most natural one, and as we
now show, whenever this choice is allowed then it is
optimal in the following sense.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
X\sim p
. If
\mathrm{Var}[X]<\infty
then

\tau:=-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\mathrm{id}
is the optimal weight with respect to
the
L^{1}(p)
norm, in the sense that

\mathrm{Var}[X]=\|\tau\|_{L^{1}(p)}\leq\|w\|_{L^{1}(p)}
for all
weights
w
such that
C(p,w)=1
.

The corresponding weight
\tau=-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\text{id}
is called the
Stein kernel of
p
. It has long been known to be an important handle
on the density
p
and multivariate extensions are a topic of active
research; see
[
31
,
27
,
18
]
for more detail and further
references.

2.2.
The pseudo-inverse Sturm-Liouville operator

In a spirit similar to the Stein operator
\mathcal{T}
and its pseudo-inverse
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
, we want to define the pseudo-inverse of the Sturm-Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}
. First, we need to find a proper domain for this operator.
We write
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)=\left\{h\in H^{1}(p,w):\mathbb{E}_{p}[h]=0\right\}
, with
H^{1}(p,w)
as in Section

2.1
, and we endow it with the scalar
product
(f,h)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{p}[f^{\prime}h^{\prime}w]
. If
C(p,w)<\infty
, then

\left\|h\right\|_{L^{2}(p)}^{2}\leq C(p,w)\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}^{2}=C(p,w)\left\|h\right\|_{H^{1}_{c}(p,w)}^{2}

so that the resulting norm is equivalent to the usual norm

\left\|h\right\|^{2}_{H^{1}(p,w)}=\left\|h\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(p)}+\left\|h^{\prime}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}^{2}
.
We define the space of functions

E^{2}(p,w)=\left\{f\in L^{2}(pw)\cap L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[):x\mapsto\int_{x_{0}}^{x}f\in L^{2}(p)\right\}

(2.3)

where
a<x_{0}<b
is finite and
arbitrary.
We endow
E^{2}(p,w)
with the norm of
L^{2}(pw)
. Finally we denote the
integral and differential operators by

If=\int_{x_{0}}^{\cdot}f-\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\int_{x_{0}}^{\cdot}f\right]\mbox{ and
}Dh=h^{\prime}

for
f\in E^{2}(p,w)
and
h\in H^{1}(p,w)
, respectively. Note how neither the
definition of
E^{2}(p,w)
nor the definition of
I
depend on the
choice of
x_{0}\in]a,b[
.

Proposition 2.6
.

The operators
I:E^{2}(p,w)\rightarrow H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
and

D:H^{1}_{c}(p,w)\rightarrow E^{2}(p,w)
are continuous and satisfy
I=D^{-1}
.
If
C(p,w)<\infty
, then
E^{2}(p,w)=L^{2}(pw)\cap L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)

and the following statements are equivalent :

(1)

E^{2}(p,w)
is a Hilbert space,

(2)

H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
is a Hilbert space,

(3)

L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
,

(4)

E^{2}(p,w)=L^{2}(pw)
.

The inclusion
L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
is true whenever
pw
is
continuous and strictly positive on
]a,b[
, which is the case in many
classical examples.
For the remainder of this section, we assume that
p
and
w
are
chosen so that

(H1)

C(p,w)<\infty
,

(H2)

L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
.

Those hypotheses ensure that
E^{2}(p,w)
is a Hilbert space by Proposition
2.6
. The same proposition says that
E^{2}(p,w)=L^{2}(p,w)
, so the notation
E^{2}(p,w)
is a bit superficial, which is why we dropped it from the statements of the results in the Introduction. We shall nevertheless continue using this notation in the current section so as to emphasize the relation with
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
.
With these notations, we recall the pseudo-inverse Sturm-Liouville operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}:E^{2}(p,w)\rightarrow E^{2}(p,w)
defined by

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f=-\mathcal{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}}If=\frac{1}{p(x)w(x)}\int_{a}^{b}K(x,y)f(y)dy=\frac{1}{w(x)}\mathbb{E}[k(x,X)f(X)]

where
X\sim p
. The following then holds.

Proposition 2.7
.

Under (H1)-(H2),
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is well-defined, continuous, self-adjoint, positive and satisfies

\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\right\|_{E^{2}(p,w)\rightarrow E^{2}(p,w)}=C(p,w).

Proposition 2.8
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. If
e\in E^{2}(p,w)
is an eigenvector of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, its eigenvalue is
C(p,w)
if and only if
e>0
a.e. Further, if such a eigenvector exists, it is unique and
Ie
saturates
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
.

Another characteristic that may be interesting is compactness, because compact self-adjoint operators have strong spectral properties.

Proposition 2.9
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. The operator
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact if and only if

(A1)

the eigenvalues
\left\{\kappa_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}
of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
verify
\kappa_{i}>0
,
\kappa_{i+1}\leq\kappa_{i}
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\kappa_{i}=0
,

(A2)

there exists a countable Hilbert basis
\left\{e_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}
of
E^{2}(p,w)
made up of eigenvectors of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
.

Moreover, if
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact, we have
\kappa_{1}=C(p,w)
.

In the sequel, we won’t use explicitly the compactness of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, but we will refer repeatedly to (A1)-(A2). Our convergence results rely on these properties.

Example 2.10
.

Consider the uniform measure on
[0,1]
. From the proof of forthcoming Example
4.3
, we know that the eigenvectors of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
are
e_{i}(x)=\sin((2i-1)\pi x)
and the eigenvalues are
\kappa_{i}={1}/({(2i-1)^{2}\pi^{2}})
.

We can also show that the converse of Proposition
2.8
holds if
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact.

Proposition 2.11
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. If
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact,
h\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
saturates
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
if and only if
h^{\prime}
is an eigenvector of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
associated to the eigenvalue
\kappa_{1}=C(p,w)
.

It remains to be seen when
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact. By Proposition
2.7
, we know that if
C(p,w)<\infty
or, equivalently, if the injection of
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
in
L^{2}(p)
is continuous, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is continuous. Actually, the same relation holds for compactness.

Proposition 2.12
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. If
H^{1}(p,w)
is dense in
L^{2}(p)
and the injection of
H^{1}(p,w)
in
L^{2}(p)
is compact, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact.

The hypotheses of this proposition have already been studied and we can find in the literature more explicit conditions on
p
under which they hold (see
[
24
]
when
w=1
). They mean that
H^{1}(p,w)
shares some properties of the classical Sobolev space
H^{1}(]a,b[)
. In particular, the second one is the counterpart for
H^{1}(p,w)
of the Rellich-Kondrachov Theorem (see Theorem IX.16 in
[
12
]
).
Nevertheless, they are uneasy to verify. As
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is a kernel operator, there is another sufficient condition to check its compactness.

Proposition 2.13
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. If
k_{w}\in L^{2}(pw\otimes pw)
, then
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact and

C(p,w)^{2}<\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\kappa_{i}^{2}=\left\|k_{w}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw\otimes pw)}^{2}.

3.
Statements and proofs of the main results

3.1.
Variational bounds on
C(p,w)

From here onward, we use the notations
\inf
and
\sup
to denote the essential
infimum and supremum over
]a,b[
. Using Theorem
2.4
we obtain the following.

Theorem 3.1

(Chen-Wang variational formula)
.

Let
h_{1}\in L^{1}(p)
and
h_{2}\in L^{2}(p)
be weakly differentiable
and such that
h_{1}^{\prime},h_{2}^{\prime}<0
a.e. Then

\inf-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h_{2}}{h_{2}^{\prime}w}\leq C(p,w)\leq\sup-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h_{1}}{h_{1}^{\prime}w}.

Furthermore, if
-{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h_{2}}/{(h_{2}^{\prime}w)}
is constant,
h_{2}
saturates
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Let
h\in L^{1}(p)
be such that
h^{\prime}<0
a.e. on
]a,b[
and set
w_{h}:={-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h}/{h^{\prime}}
. We start with the upper bound. If
\sup w_{h}/w=\infty
, there is nothing to prove. Assume that
\sup w_{h}/w<\infty
. As
w_{h},w>0
a.e. we have
\inf w/w_{h}=(\sup w_{h}/w)^{-1}>0
. Using this and Theorem
2.4
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[|g^{\prime}|^{2}w\right]\geq\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[|g^{\prime}|^{2}w_{h}\right]\inf\frac{w}{w_{h}}\geq\mathrm{Var}_{p}[g]\inf\frac{w}{w_{h}}

(3.1)

for all
g\in H^{1}(p,w)\subset H^{1}(p,w_{h})
. Hence
C(p,w)\leq\sup w_{h}/w
.

Now, we look at the lower bound. Assume by contradiction that

h\in L^{2}(p)
and
C(p,w)<\inf{w_{h}}/{w}
.
Then there exists some
\epsilon>0

such that
C(p,w)+\epsilon\leq{w_{h}}/{w}
a.e. on

]a,b[
. Since
w>0
a.e., we have
(C(p,w)+\epsilon)w\leq w_{h}

a.e. so that
H^{1}(p,w_{h})\subset H^{1}(p,w)
and

\mathrm{Var}_{p}[g]\leq C(p,w)\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[|g^{\prime}|^{2}w\right]\leq\frac{C(p,w)}{C(p,w)+\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[|g^{\prime}|^{2}w_{h}\right]

for all
g\in H^{1}(p,w_{h})
. This is a contradiction since Theorem
2.4
states that
C(p,w_{h})=1
.

Finally, assume that
h\in L^{2}(p)
and
w_{h}/w
is constant.
We have
w_{h}=C(p,w)w
by the first part of the proof. By Theorem
2.4
, we know that
h
saturates
\mathrm{PI}(p,w_{h})
and thus
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
.
∎

Taking
h=\mathcal{L}f
for a smooth function
f
, we obtain a similar result for the Sturm-Liouville operator (see Appendix
B
for a proof).

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume that
p,w\in C^{2}(]a,b[)
. Let

f_{i}\in C^{\infty}(]a,b[)
be such that
-(\mathcal{L}f_{i})^{\prime}>0
on
]a,b[
and

\mathcal{T}(f_{i}^{\prime}w)\in L^{i}(p)
for
i=1,2
. We have

\inf\frac{f_{2}^{\prime}w-\Phi(f_{2}^{\prime}w)}{-(\mathcal{L}f_{2})^{\prime}w}\leq C(p,w)\leq\sup\frac{f_{1}^{\prime}w-\Phi(f_{1}^{\prime}w)}{-(\mathcal{L}f_{1})^{\prime}w}

where

\Phi g(x)={\bar{P}(x)}/{p(x)}\lim_{t\to a}g(t)p(t)+{P(x)}/{p(x)}\lim_{t\to b}g(t)p(t)
.

One obvious benefit of Proposition
3.2
over Theorem
3.1
is that it’s easier to take derivatives than to integrate.
If we take
f\in C^{\infty}(]a,b[)
such that
h:=\mathcal{L}f\in L^{1}(p)
and
h^{\prime}<0
, we have
f^{\prime}w-\Phi(f^{\prime}w)=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h>0
(see the proof of Proposition
3.2
for details).
Thus, we can inverse the upper bound of Proposition
3.2
to obtain

\frac{1}{C(p,w)}\geq\inf\frac{-(\mathcal{L}f)^{\prime}w}{f^{\prime}w-\Phi(f^{\prime}w)}.

(3.2)

If we also assume
f^{\prime}>0
on
]a,b[
, we have
\Phi(f^{\prime}w)\geq 0
. Hence, we can recover the spectral gap inequality (
1.5
) from (
3.2
).
We conclude with an immediate corollary which will be useful for proving the results given in the examples.

Corollary 3.3
.

Assume that
p,w\in C^{2}(]a,b[)
. If

f\in C^{\infty}(]a,b[)\cap L^{2}(p)
is such that
f^{\prime}>0
,
-\mathcal{L}f=\lambda f
on
]a,b[
and
\lim_{t\to a}f^{\prime}(t)w(t)p(t)=0=\lim_{t\to b}f^{\prime}(t)w(t)p(t)
, then
C(p,w)=\lambda^{-1}
and
f
saturates
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
.

3.2.
Recursive approximation of
C(p,w)

In this Subsection, we assume that
C(p,w)<\infty
and
L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
.
We now propose, using properties of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, various methods allowing to estimate

C(p,w)
. We recall that
\{\kappa_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
and
\{e_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
are respectively the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
. According to Proposition
2.9
, if
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact, we have
\kappa_{1}=C(p,w)
. Hence, our goal is to find a way of
extracting the first eigenvalue of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
.
We begin by constructing a sequence of nested intervals containing
C(p,w)
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. For all
g_{0}\in E^{2}(p,w)
such that
g_{0}>0
, the sequence of intervals

I_{n}=\left[\inf\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}},\sup\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}\right]

satisfies
I_{n+1}\subset I_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
C(p,w)\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Set
g_{n}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}
and
M_{n}=\sup{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}}/{g_{n}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For a.e.
x\in]a,b[
, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n+1}}{g_{n+1}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g_{n+1}(x)}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{K(x,y)}{p(x)w(x)}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{g_{n+1}(x)}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{K(x,y)g_{n}(y)}{p(x)w(x)}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}(y)}{g_{n}(y)}\,dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{M_{n}}{g_{n+1}(x)}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{K(x,y)g_{n}(y)}{p(x)w(x)}\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{M_{n}}{g_{n+1}(x)}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}(x)=M_{n}.

With the same reasoning, we can get
\inf{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}}/{g_{n}}\leq\inf{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n+1}}/{g_{n+1}}
. Hence, we have
I_{n+1}\subset I_{n}
. Remark that
g_{n+1}=\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. So, we can choose
h=Ig_{n}
in Theorem
3.1
, which entails that
C(p,w)\in I_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
since

-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h}{h^{\prime}w}=-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}Ig_{n}}{wg_{n}}=\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n}}{g_{n}}.\qed

As mentioned in the Introduction, there is no guarantee that
\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}I_{n}=\left\{C(p,w)\right\}
. To obtain convergence results, we need
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
to be compact, which allows us to use the spectral properties studied in Section
2
. First, we show that

I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}
is a minimising sequence for
\mathrm{PI}(p,w)
,
irrespective of the starting function
g_{0}\in E^{2}(p,w)
(see Appendix
B
for a proof).

Proposition 3.5
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact. For all
g_{0}\in E^{2}(p,w)
such that
\mathbb{E}[g_{0}e_{1}w]\neq 0
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}_{p}\left[I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}\right]}{\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\left|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}\right|^{2}\right]}=C(p,w).

Proposition
2.8
provides an easy way of checking that

\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]\neq 0
. Indeed, since we have either
e_{1}>0
on

]a,b[
or
e_{1}<0
on
]a,b[
, it suffices that
g_{0}>0
to ensure

\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]\neq 0
. Proposition
3.5
gives us a
theoretical way of estimating
C(p,w)
but is difficult to use from a
computational point of view. However, we can establish other results
of convergence to

C(p,w)
. We recall that
\left\{e_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}
is a Hilbert basis of
E^{2}(p,w)
by (A2). Thus, any
g_{0}\in E^{2}(p,w)
can be written as
g_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}e_{i}
with
a_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{i}w]
.
We introduce the auxiliary operator
A:={\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}}/{\kappa_{1}}
, along with its iterations
A^{1}=A
,
A^{n+1}=A(A^{n})
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. The following holds.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact. Let
g_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}e_{i}\in E^{2}(p,w)
. The sequence
(A^{n}g_{0})

satisfies

\left\|A^{n}g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}\leq\left(\frac{\kappa_{2}}{C(p,w)}\right)^{n}\left\|g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}.

In particular,
g_{n}
converges to
a_{1}e_{1}
in
L^{2}(pw)
.

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Let
\left\{\mu_{i}={\kappa_{i}}/{\kappa_{1}}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}
be the eigenvalues of
A
.
By (A1) and Proposition
2.8
, we know that
\mu_{1}=1
,
\mu_{i}>0
and
\mu_{i+1}\leq\mu_{i}<1
for all
i\geq 2
.
As
A
is continuous, we have

\displaystyle A^{n}g_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}A^{n}e_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}=a_{1}e_{1}+\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}.

So, we can compute using the orthonormality of
\left\{e_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle\left\|A^{n}g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(pw)}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(pw)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\mu_{i}^{2n}a_{i}^{2}\leq\mu_{2}^{2n}\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\leq\mu_{2}^{2n}\left\|g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}^{2}.

Since
\mu_{2}={\kappa_{2}}/{C(p,w)}
, we get the desired inequality.
∎

Remark 1
.

For any functions
f,g\in L^{2}(pw)
, the expression

pw(x)\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f(x)=\int_{a}^{b}K(x,\cdot)f\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g}(x)=\frac{\int_{a}^{b}K(x,\cdot)f}{\int_{a}^{b}K(x,\cdot)g}

makes sense for all
x\in]a,b[
such that
{K(x,\cdot)}/{pw}\in L^{2}(pw)
, despite the fact that
f
,
g
and
pw
are not necessarily well defined in
x
. To make the expressions shorter, we will sometimes write
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}f(x)
or
e_{1}(x)
by abuse of notation, but all our computations make sense if we multiply by
pw(x)
.

Theorem 3.7
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact and

k_{w}(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(pw)
for some
x\in]a,b[
.
For all
g_{0}\in E^{2}(p,w)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]\neq 0
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}\right)(x)=C(p,w).

Proof of Theorem
3.7
.

Set
g_{n}=A^{n}g_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Assume that
a_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]=1
without loss of generality. We have

|g_{n}-e_{1}|(x)=\left|\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}g_{n-1}}{\kappa_{1}}-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}e_{1}}{\kappa_{1}}\right|(x)\leq\frac{1}{\kappa_{1}(pw)(x)}\int_{a}^{b}K(x,\cdot)|g_{n-1}-e_{1}|.

With the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the assumption on
k_{w}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{(pw)(x)}\int_{a}^{b}\frac{K(x,\cdot)}{\sqrt{pw}}|g_{n-1}-e_{1}|\sqrt{pw}\leq\left\|k_{w}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}\left\|g_{n-1}-e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}.

We conclude using Theorem
3.6
that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{n}(x)=e_{1}(x)
. Now, remark that
e_{1}(x)=\kappa_{1}^{-1}\int_{a}^{b}k_{w}(x,\cdot)e_{1}pw\neq 0
since
e_{1}>0
a.e. by Proposition
2.8
. Finally, we just have to observe how

\frac{1}{C(p,w)}\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}(x)=\frac{g_{n+1}}{g_{n}}(x)\rightarrow\frac{e_{1}}{e_{1}}(x)=1,

as required.
∎

We learn from the proof of this proposition that

|A^{n}g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}|(x)\leq\frac{\left\|k_{w}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}}{C(p,w)}\left\|A^{n-1}g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}.

(3.3)

If
k_{w}\in L^{2}(pw\otimes pw)
, the condition
k_{w}(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(pw)
is verified for almost every
x\in]a,b[
and (
3.3
) entails simple convergence of
A^{n}g_{0}
to
a_{1}e_{1}
and of
{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}/{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}
to
C(p,w)
.
Putting together Theorem
3.6
and (
3.3
) provides an idea of the convergence rate, as follows (see Appendix
B
for a proof).

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is compact and
k_{w}(x,\cdot)\in L^{2}(pw)
for a certain
x\in]a,b[
. For all
g_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}e_{i}\in E^{2}(p,w)
with
\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]\neq 0
, we have

\left|\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}(x)-C(p,w)\right|\leq 2\left(\frac{|a_{1}e_{1}(x)|}{B_{n}(x)}-\frac{1}{C(p,w)}\right)^{-1}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
B_{n}(x)<C(p,w)|a_{1}e_{1}(x)|
, where

B_{n}(x):=\left\|k_{w}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}\left\|g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}\left(\frac{\kappa_{2}}{C(p,w)}\right)^{n-1}.

Of course, we don’t know
C(p,w)
,
\kappa_{2}
and
a_{1}e_{1}(x)
, so this
convergence rate can’t be used in practice. At least, it emphasises
how the speed of convergence depends on the parameter.
It tells us also that the convergence of
{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n+1}g_{0}}/{\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}}
to
C(p,w)
is uniform when
k_{w}\in L^{2}(pw\otimes pw)
and
e_{1}(x)\geq\alpha\left\|k_{w}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}
for some
\alpha>0
and almost every
x\in]a,b[
. In such cases, the sequence of nested intervals
I_{n}
from Theorem
3.4
converges to
\left\{C(p,w)\right\}
.

Appendix A
Relation between
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
and
\mathcal{L}

In this section, we show that
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
has, in some sense, the inverse spectral properties of
\mathcal{L}
. This will be helpful in the proof of Proposition
2.12
. We assume that
C(p,w)<\infty
and
L^{2}(pw)\subset L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(]a,b[)
.
The Sturm-Liouville operator
\mathcal{L}
has already been defined on the space of twice
differentiable functions in the introduction. Now, we give a weak version
valid on the whole space
H^{1}(p,w)
. We define

\mathcal{L}:H^{1}(p,w)\rightarrow H^{1}_{c}(p,w)^{*}
by

\mathcal{L}h:H^{1}_{c}(p,w)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:v\mapsto-\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[h^{\prime}v^{\prime}w\right]

where
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)^{*}
is the dual space of
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
. We can define its pseudo-inverse operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}:L^{2}(p)\rightarrow H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
by associating to
f\in L^{2}(p)
the solution
h\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
of

-\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[h^{\prime}v^{\prime}w\right]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[fv\right],\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w).

(A.1)

This solution exists and is unique by the Riesz representation
theorem. Indeed, using Cauchy-Schwarz and Poincaré inequalities, we
can see that the form
v\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w)\mapsto\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[fv\right]
is
in
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)^{*}
. If
h\in H^{1}(p,w)
, we have by Theorem
2.2

\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}h^{\prime}\,v^{\prime}w\right]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[-\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h\,v^{\prime}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[hv\right]=-\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[(\mathcal{L}^{-1}h)^{\prime}v^{\prime}w\right]

for all
v\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
. By uniqueness of the solution of (
A.1
), it follows that
-I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}h^{\prime}=\mathcal{L}^{-1}h
. The operators

\mathcal{L}^{-1}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
are thus related by
the following identity:

-\mathcal{L}^{-1}=I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}D

(A.2)

on
H^{1}(p,w)
. We say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is an eigenvalue
of
-\mathcal{L}
if there exists
h\in H^{1}(p,w)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[h^{\prime}v^{\prime}w\right]=\lambda\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[hv\right],\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w).

(A.3)

First, observe that
h\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
is an eigenvector of
-\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if
h
is an eigenvector of
-\mathcal{L}^{-1}
with eigenvalue
\lambda^{-1}
. Then, remark that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}h^{\prime}=D(-\mathcal{L}^{-1})Ih^{\prime}=D(-\mathcal{L}^{-1})h=D(\frac{1}{\lambda}h)=\frac{1}{\lambda}h^{\prime}.

This leads us to the following.

Corollary A.1
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. A function
h\in H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
is an eigenvector of
-\mathcal{L}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if and only if
h^{\prime}
is an eigenvector of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda^{-1}
.

We end this section by recalling a classical result.

Proposition A.2
.

Assume that (H1)-(H2) hold. If
H^{1}(p,w)
is dense in
L^{2}(p)
and the injection of
H^{1}(p,w)
in
L^{2}(p)
is compact, the eigenvalues of
-\mathcal{L}
form an increasing sequence
\left\{\lambda_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
of real positive numbers converging to infinity and such that
\lambda_{1}=C(p,w)^{-1}
. Moreover, there exists a Hilbert basis of
H^{1}(p,w)
of associated eigenvectors
\left\{v_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.

Proof of Proposition
A.2
.

The symmetric bilinear form
a(h,v):=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left[h^{\prime}v^{\prime}w\right]
is continuous and coercive on
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
. The hypotheses (C1)-(C2) ensure that
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
is dense in
L^{2}_{c}(p)
and that the injection of
H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
in
L^{2}_{c}(p)
is compact. So, we can apply theorem 7.3.2 in
[
1
]
with
H=L^{2}_{c}(p)
and
V=H^{1}_{c}(p,w)
. It tells us exactly our statement except the equality
\lambda_{1}=C(p,w)^{-1}
which follows from proposition 7.3.4 in
[
1
]
.

Appendix B
Further proofs

Proof of curious identity.

Let
h_{2}(x)=\int_{c}^{x}{p}/{(P\bar{P})}
for some
c\in]a,b[
. First, we compute using the Fubini-Tonelli Theorem

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}[|h_{2}|]

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{c}p(x)\int_{x}^{c}\frac{p(y)}{P(y)\bar{P}(y)}dy\,dx+\int_{c}^{b}p(x)\int_{c}^{x}\frac{p(y)}{P(y)\bar{P}(y)}dy\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{c}\frac{p(y)}{P(y)\bar{P}(y)}\int_{a}^{y}p(x)dx\,dy+\int_{c}^{b}\frac{p(y)}{P(y)\bar{P}(y)}\int_{y}^{b}p(x)dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{c}\frac{p(y)}{\bar{P}(y)}dy+\int_{c}^{b}\frac{p(y)}{P(y)}dy

\displaystyle=-\int_{a}^{c}\log(\bar{P}(y))^{\prime}dy+\int_{c}^{b}\log(P(y))^{\prime}dy

\displaystyle=-\log(\bar{P}(c))-\ln(P(c))=-\log(P(c)\bar{P}(c)).

As the last expression is finite, we have
h_{2}\in L^{1}(p)
. Since
h_{2}
is increasing, we can apply the second identity in (
2.2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{p}\left[h_{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int\int\frac{p(x)}{P(x)\bar{P}(x)}P(x\wedge y)\bar{P}(x\lor y)\frac{p(y)}{P(y)\bar{P}(y)}dxdy

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int_{a}^{x}\frac{p(x)}{P(x)}\frac{p(y)}{\bar{P}(y)}dydx+\int_{a}^{b}\int_{x}^{b}\frac{p(x)}{\bar{P}(x)}\frac{p(y)}{P(y)}dydx

\displaystyle=-\int_{a}^{b}\log(P(x))^{\prime}\log(\bar{P}(x))dx+\int_{a}^{b}\log(P(x))\log(\bar{P}(x))^{\prime}dx

\displaystyle=-\left[\log(P(x))\log(\bar{P}(x))\right]_{a}^{b}+2\int_{a}^{b}\log(P(x))\log(\bar{P}(x))^{\prime}dx

Hence

Proof of Lemma

[d,b[

Proof of Proposition
2.13
.

Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is a kernel operator with kernel

,
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
is a continuous Hilbert-Schmidt operator on

by Theorem VI.23 in
[
29

[
29
]
ensures that every Hilbert-Schmidt operator is compact. As
L^{2}(pw)=E^{2}(p,w)
by Proposition
2.6
, we reap the first statement.

The first inequality is obvious since
C(p,w)=\kappa_{1}
.
Theorems VI.22 and VI.23 in
[
29
]
tell us that

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}(]a,b[)
be such that
-(\mathcal{L}f)^{\prime}>0
on
]a,b[
and
\mathcal{T}(f^{\prime}w)\in L^{2}(p)
.
We set
h=\mathcal{T}(f^{\prime}w)=\mathcal{L}f
. As
p,w,f^{\prime}\in C^{2}(]a,b[)
,
h
is differentiable. For a differentiable function

g
such that
\mathcal{T}g\in L^{1}(p)
, we necessarily have

\mathbb{E}_{p}[\mathcal{T}g]=\lim_{t\to\infty}g(t)p(t)-\lim_{t\to-\infty}g(t)p(t)
so that, after some straightforward
simplifications,

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}g(x)=\frac{1}{p(x)}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\left(\mathcal{T}g-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}g]\right)p=g(x)-\Phi g(x)

and thus

\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h=\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{T}(f^{\prime}w)=f^{\prime}w-\Phi(f^{\prime}w)
.
By assumption, we have
h\in L^{2}(p)
.
Hence, we can use Theorem
3.1
to obtain

\inf\frac{f^{\prime}w-\Phi(f^{\prime}w)}{-(\mathcal{L}f)^{\prime}w}=\inf-\frac{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}h}{h^{\prime}w}\leq C(p,w).

The upper bound on
C(p,w)
can be deduced in the same way.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.5
.

As
g_{0}\in E^{2}(p,w)
, we write
g_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}e_{i}
for some
a_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
by (A2). By assumption, we have
a_{1}=\mathbb{E}_{p}[g_{0}e_{1}w]\neq 0
.
Using the continuity of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}
, (A2) and (
B.5
), we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\left\|I\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(p)}}{\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}g_{0}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(pw)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|I\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}e_{i}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(p)}}{\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{n}e_{i}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(pw)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|I\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\kappa_{i}^{n}e_{i}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(p)}}{\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}\kappa_{i}^{n}e_{i}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(pw)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\kappa_{i}^{2n+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\kappa_{i}^{2n}}=\kappa_{1}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)^{2n+1}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)^{2n}}.

By Proposition
2.8

\kappa_{1}\frac{a_{1}^{2}}{a_{1}^{2}}=\kappa_{1}=C(p,w)
as
n\to\infty

∎

Proof of Proposition
3.8
.

Set
g_{n}=A^{n}g_{0}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Putting together Theorem
3.6
and (
3.3
), we get

C(p,w)|g_{n}-a_{1}e_{1}|(x)\leq\left\|k_{w}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}\left\|g_{0}-a_{1}e_{1}\right\|_{L^{2}(pw)}\left(\frac{\kappa_{2}}{C(p,w)}\right)^{n-1}=B_{n}(x).

We have

\left|\frac{g_{n+1}(x)}{g_{n}(x)}-1\right|\leq\frac{|g_{n+1}-a_{1}e_{1}|(x)}{|g_{n}|(x)}+\frac{|g_{n}-a_{1}e_{1}|(x)}{|g_{n}|(x)}\leq\frac{2B_{n}(x)}{C(p,w)|g_{n}|(x)}.

Further, it holds that

\displaystyle|g_{n}|(x)=\left|a_{1}e_{1}-(a_{1}e_{1}-g_{n})\right|(x)

\displaystyle\geq\left|a_{1}e_{1}\right|(x)-\left|a_{1}e_{1}-g_{n}\right|(x)

\displaystyle\geq\left|a_{1}e_{1}\right|(x)-\frac{B_{n}(x)}{C(p,w)}.

We have gathered everything needed to conclude

\displaystyle=C(p,w)\left|\frac{g_{n+1}}{g_{n}}(x)-1\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2B_{n}(x)}{\left|a_{1}e_{1}\right|(x)-C(p,w)^{-1}B_{n}(x)}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{\left|a_{1}e_{1}\right|(x)}{B_{n}(x)}-\frac{1}{C(p,w)}\right)^{-1}.\qed
