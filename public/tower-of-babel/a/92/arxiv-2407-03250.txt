Abstract
The article concerns low-rank approximation of matrices generated by sampling a smooth function of two m m -dimensional variables. We identify several misconceptions surrounding a claim that, for a specific class of analytic functions, such n × n n\times n matrices admit accurate entrywise approximation of rank that is independent of m m and grows as log ⁡ ( n ) \log(n) —colloquially known as “big-data matrices are approximately low-rank”. We provide a theoretical explanation of the numerical results presented in support of this claim, describing three narrower classes of functions for which function-generated matrices can be approximated within an entrywise error of order ε \varepsilon with rank 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ ( n ) ​ ε − 2 ​ log ⁡ ( ε − 1 ) ) \mathcal{O}(\log(n)\varepsilon^{-2}\log(\varepsilon^{-1})) that is independent of the dimension m m : (i) functions of the inner product of the two variables, (ii) functions of the Euclidean distance between the variables, and (iii) shift-invariant positive-definite kernels. We extend our argument to tensor-train approximation of tensors generated with functions of the “higher-order inner product” of their multiple variables. We discuss our results in the context of low-rank approximation of (a) growing datasets and (b) attention in transformer neural networks.

Introduction

Datasets are often arranged as matrices
\bm{\mathrm{F}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
and obtained by (or at least modeled as the result of) sampling a bi-variate function
f:\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
so that

\bm{\mathrm{F}}(i,j)=f(\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i},\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{j}),\quad\{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\subset\Omega_{1},\quad\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\subset\Omega_{2}.

We shall say that such
\bm{\mathrm{F}}
is a function-generated matrix and
f
is its generating function. Given the sheer volume of modern datasets, it is necessary to use low-parametric representation formats for matrices, the most prominent being approximate low-rank decompositions:

\bm{\mathrm{F}}\approx\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\intercal},\quad\bm{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times r},\quad\bm{\mathrm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times r},\quad r\ll\min\{n_{1},n_{2}\}.

When a matrix admits accurate low-rank approximation, its storage and processing become more efficient, noise can be filtered out from its entries, and missing entries can be imputed.

How does the accuracy of low-rank approximation of a matrix depend on the properties of its generating function? Since we treat the matrix as a dataset, we consider the error in the maximum norm
\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}\|_{\max}=\max_{i\in[n_{1}],j\in[n_{2}]}|\bm{\mathrm{F}}(i,j)|
. Small approximation error in the maximum norm guarantees that each entry is perturbed only slightly. The maximum norm goes hand in hand with the
\mathrm{L}_{\infty}
norm of bounded functions
\|f\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}=\sup_{\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\Omega_{1},\bm{\mathrm{y}}\in\Omega_{2}}|f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})|
. Indeed, if
\bm{\mathrm{F}}
is generated with
f
and
\bm{\mathrm{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
is generated with
g:\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, but with the same sampling points, then
\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}\|_{\max}\leq\|f-g\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega_{1}\times\Omega_{2})}
.

1.4
Contributions: fast attention

The transformer neural-network architecture
[
41
]

3
Functions of the inner product

The theory developed in
[
40
]
does not guarantee the logarithmic growth of
\varepsilon
-ranks for the Gaussian kernel. Nevertheless, the numerical experiment of
[
40
]
shows that matrices generated with the Gaussian kernel for
m=1000
can be approximated with slowly growing ranks. In this section, we provide a theoretical explanation of the observed behavior.

In this numerical experiment, the points
\{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n_{1}}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n_{2}}
are drawn independently at random from the uniform distribution on the
m
-dimensional unit sphere. As a consequence, the Gaussian kernel can be rewritten as
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\exp(-2+2\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{y}})
—this is a function of the inner product. Let us focus on this property and consider functions
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=h(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{y}})
, where
h
depends on a single scalar variable. It follows that we can represent matrices
\bm{\mathrm{F}}
generated with the function
f
as
\bm{\mathrm{F}}=h(\bm{\mathrm{X}}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}^{\intercal})
, where
h
is applied to
\bm{\mathrm{X}}\bm{\mathrm{Y}}^{\intercal}
entrywise. Here, the rows of
\bm{\mathrm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times m}
and
\bm{\mathrm{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times m}
are the
m
-dimensional sampling points:

\bm{\mathrm{X}}^{\intercal}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathrm{Y}}^{\intercal}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{1}&\cdots&\bm{\mathrm{y}}_{n_{2}}\end{bmatrix}.

We begin the analysis by extending
theorem

3
to the compression of the Hadamard product (i.e., entrywise product) of several factorized matrices.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
be given by
eq.

2
. For every
t\in\mathbb{N}
, any
m_{1},\ldots,m_{t}\in\mathbb{N}
, and every collection of matrices
\{\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{t}
and
\{\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{s}\}_{s=1}^{t}
of sizes
\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times m_{s}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times m_{s}}
, there exists
\bm{\mathrm{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
of
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})\leq r
such that

\|(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}^{\intercal})\mathbin{\ast}\cdots\mathbin{\ast}(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{t}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{t}^{\intercal})-\bm{\mathrm{G}}\|_{\max}\leq\varepsilon\prod_{s=1}^{t}\|\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{s}\|_{2,\infty}\|\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{s}\|_{2,\infty}.

Proof.

By the properties of the Hadamard product
[
21
, §2.6]
, we can write

(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{1}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}^{\intercal})\mathbin{\ast}\cdots\mathbin{\ast}(\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{t}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{t}^{\intercal})=\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\intercal},

where
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times(m_{1}\ldots m_{t})}
and
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times(m_{1}\ldots m_{t})}
are defined via the Khatri–Rao product
eq.

5
:

\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbin{\odot}\cdots\mathbin{\odot}\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{t}^{\intercal},\quad\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\intercal}=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}^{\intercal}\mathbin{\odot}\cdots\mathbin{\odot}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{t}^{\intercal}.

Applying
theorem

3
, we obtain the following error bound of rank-
r
approximation:

\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}^{\intercal}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}\|_{\max}\leq\varepsilon\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}\|_{2,\infty}\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}\|_{2,\infty}.

The rows of
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}
and
\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}
are Kronecker products of the corresponding rows of the initial matrices,

\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}(i,:)=\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{1}(i,:)\mathbin{\otimes}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes}\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{t}(i,:),\quad\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(j,:)=\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{1}(j,:)\mathbin{\otimes}\cdots\mathbin{\otimes}\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{t}(j,:),

so that
\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{A}}}(i,:)\|_{2}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\|\bm{\mathrm{A}}_{s}(i,:)\|_{2}
and
\|\tilde{\bm{\mathrm{B}}}(j,:)\|_{2}=\prod_{s=1}^{t}\|\bm{\mathrm{B}}_{s}(j,:)\|_{2}
.

In the following lemma, we show how the particular structure of the generating function
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=h(\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{y}})
paves the way for the low-rank approximation of the corresponding function-generated matrices with rank that is independent of the dimension
m
.

Assumption 9
.

h:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is in
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
for a sufficiently large interval
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
be given by
eq.

2
. Let
h:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy

9
with
0\in\Omega
. For any
t,m\in\mathbb{N}
and every pair of
\bm{\mathrm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times m}
and
\bm{\mathrm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}\times m}
such that
\mathcal{I}=[\min\{\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\intercal}\},\max\{\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\intercal}\}]\subseteq\Omega
, there exists
\bm{\mathrm{G}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}
of
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})\leq 1+(t-1)r
such that

\|h(\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\intercal})-\bm{\mathrm{G}}\|_{\max}\leq\frac{\|\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\intercal}\|_{\max}^{t}}{t!}\|h^{(t)}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega)}+\varepsilon\sum_{s=1}^{t-1}\frac{|h^{(s)}(0)|}{s!}\|\bm{\mathrm{A}}\|_{2,\infty}^{s}\|\bm{\mathrm{B}}\|_{2,\infty}^{s}.

Proof.

The truncated Taylor series
h_{t}(x)=\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}\frac{h^{(s)}(0)}{s!}x^{s}
approximates

\|h-h_{t}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\mathcal{I})}\leq\frac{\|\bm{\mathrm{A}}\bm{\mathrm{B}}^{\intercal}\|_{\max}^{t}}{t!}\|h^{(t)}\|_{\mathrm{L}_{\infty}(\Omega)}.

Note that we cannot use

4
Functions of the squared Euclidean distance

When restricted to the unit sphere, the Gaussian kernel
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\exp(-\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}-\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{2}^{2})
depends only on the inner product
\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{y}}
. Should the variables belong to the unit ball,
f
ceases to be a function of the inner product since
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\exp(-\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}\|_{2}^{2})\exp(-\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{2}^{2})\exp(2\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{y}})
. A matrix generated with a product of functions is the Hadamard product of the corresponding function-generated matrices; in our case,
\bm{\mathrm{F}}=\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{1}\mathbin{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{2}\mathbin{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{3}
with
f_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\exp(-\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}\|_{2}^{2})
,
f_{2}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\exp(-\|\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{2}^{2})
, and
f_{3}(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=\exp(2\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\intercal}\bm{\mathrm{y}})
. The first two functions depend on a single variable, so
\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{1}
and
\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{2}
are rank-one matrices, and
\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{3}
can be approximated with
\bm{\mathrm{G}}
of
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\mathcal{O}(\log(n_{1}n_{2})\varepsilon^{-2}\log(\varepsilon^{-1}))
by
theorem

15
. By the properties of the Hadamard product
[
19
, §5.1]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{1}\mathbin{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{2}\mathbin{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{G}})\leq\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{1})\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{2})\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{G}})=\mathrm{rank}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}),

\displaystyle\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}-\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{1}\mathbin{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{2}\mathbin{\ast}\bm{\mathrm{G}}\|_{\max}\leq\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{1}\|_{\max}\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{2}\|_{\max}\|\bm{\mathrm{F}}_{3}-\bm{\mathrm{G}}\|_{\max}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon).

For matrices generated with Gaussian kernels, it does not matter whether the variables are sampled from a sphere or from a ball. In proving this, we relied on the properties of the exponential. What happens in the more general case of
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=h(\|\bm{\mathrm{x}}-\bm{\mathrm{y}}\|_{2}^{2})
?

Analytical low-rank approximation of such functions was studied in
[
43

5
Positive-definite kernels

Previously, we relied on the smoothness of the generating function to approximate it via the truncated Taylor series. With different analytical assumptions come other approaches to low-rank approximation of functions.

Kernel functions are actively used in machine learning. A function
f:\Omega^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is called a
positive-definite
kernel if it is symmetric,
f(\bm{\mathrm{x}},\bm{\mathrm{y}})=f(\bm{\mathrm{y}},\bm{\mathrm{x}})
for all

\{\bm{\mathrm{x}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\Omega

Many of the kernels used in practice (such as the Gaussian, Cauchy, and exponential kernels) possess an additional property of being

such that

Many datasets are naturally arranged as tensors with

Given the success of inner-product generating functions in the matrix case, we turn to generating functions that depend only on the “

(6)

Let us describe the tensor that, upon the entrywise application of

\bm{\mathcal{P}}=\llbracket\bm{\mathrm{X}}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\mathrm{X}}_{d}\rrbracket\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times\cdots\times n_{d}}

(7)

We need to specify a particular tensor decomposition with which to approximate

The tuple

Numerical experiments

7.1
Algorithm

While the truncated singular value decomposition (SVD) of a matrix provides its optimal low-rank approximation in the Frobenius norm, optimal low-rank approximation in the maximum norm is substantially more difficult—to the extent that the problem is NP-hard even in the simplest rank-one case
[
16
,
45
,
27
]
. It might still be possible to find “good” entrywise approximations, and we focus on a heuristic method based on
alternating projections
as developed in
[
8
, §7.5]
and further used in
[
9
,
10
]
. This approach applies to both matrices and tensors and, to the best of our knowledge, is the only currently available specialized algorithm of entrywise low-rank approximation for tensors. Find an overview of the known matrix algorithms in
[
9
]
.

The method of alternating projections aims to find a point in the intersection of two sets by computing successive projections in the Frobenius norm. Consider the sets of low-rank tensors and tensors that are close to
\bm{\mathcal{F}}
in the maximum norm:

\mathbb{M}_{\bm{r}}=\{\bm{\mathcal{T}}:\mathrm{rank_{TT}}(\bm{\mathcal{T}})=\bm{r}\},\quad\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathrm{F}})=\{\bm{\mathcal{E}}:\|\bm{\mathcal{F}}-\bm{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\max}\leq\varepsilon\}.

The projection of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}
onto
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon}(\bm{\mathcal{F}})
amounts to clipping the entries of
\bm{\mathcal{T}}-\bm{\mathcal{F}}
whose absolute value exceeds
\varepsilon
. The projection onto
\mathbb{M}_{\bm{r}}
is given by the truncated SVD for matrices. It cannot be computed for tensors, but the TT-SVD algorithm
[
29
,
28
]
acts as a quasi-optimal low-rank projection, and the iterations of quasi-optimal alternating projections with TT-SVD instead of the optimal projection still converge
[
39
,
8
]
and can be accelerated with randomized low-rank projections
[
26
,
39
]
. In
[
8
,
10
,
9
]
, (quasi-optimal) alternating projections were combined with a binary search over
\varepsilon
to obtain an upper bound on the optimal low-rank approximation error in the maximum norm for a matrix or tensor.

8
Conclusion

So are “big data” approximately low-rank? The question is too vague to have a concise, definite answer. In this article, we presented three classes of function-generated matrices and a class of function-generated tensors whose
\varepsilon
-ranks tend to grow slowly with the number of samples
n
(once
n
is big enough) when the underlying latent dimension
m
is large. It is unlikely that these specific types of function-generated data represent the whole of “big data”, and we encourage further investigations in this area: seeking other classes of approximately low-rank matrices and tensors and tying them with the real-world datasets.

Appendix A
Multi-index notation

Consider a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, a point
\bm{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and a multi-index
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}_{0}
. The following notation is standard in multi-variate calculus and partial differential equations:

\bm{\gamma}!=\gamma_{1}!\cdots\gamma_{m}!,\quad\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\bm{\gamma}}=\bm{\mathrm{x}}(1)^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\bm{\mathrm{x}}(m)^{\gamma_{m}},\quad\partial^{\bm{\gamma}}f=\partial_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\partial_{m}^{\gamma_{m}}f.

With its help, we can write down the Taylor series of an analytic function in a concise way:

f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})=\sum\nolimits_{\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}_{0}}\partial^{\bm{\gamma}}f(\bm{\mathrm{0}})\frac{\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\bm{\gamma}}}{\bm{\gamma}!}.

Using
|\bm{\gamma}|=\gamma_{1}+\cdots+\gamma_{m}
, we can also write the truncated Taylor series:

f(\bm{\mathrm{x}})\approx\sum\nolimits_{|\bm{\gamma}|<\rho}\partial^{\bm{\gamma}}f(\bm{\mathrm{0}})\frac{\bm{\mathrm{x}}^{\bm{\gamma}}}{\bm{\gamma}!}.
