Noise-Induced Quantum Synchronization
Finn Schmolke, Eric Lutz

Abstract
Synchronization is a widespread phenomenon in science and technology. We here study noise-induced synchronization in a quantum spin chain subjected to local Gaussian white noise. We demonstrate stable (anti)synchronization between the endpoint magnetizations of a quantum X ​ Y XY model with transverse field of arbitrary length. Remarkably, we show that noise applied to a single spin suffices to reach stable (anti)synchronization, and find that the two synchronized end spins are entangled. We additionally determine the optimal noise amplitude that leads to the fastest synchronization along the chain, and further compare the optimal synchronization speed to the fundamental Lieb-Robinson bound for information propagation.

We here investigate noise-induced synchronization in an isolated quantum many-body system. By locally applying Gaussian white noise to a quantum spin chain of arbitrary length, we show that stable (anti)synchronization between local spin observables may be achieved when a given condition, on the length of the chain and on the sites at which noise is applied, is satisfied. Synchronization time.
The speed of signal propagation in discrete quantum systems with local interactions is upper bounded by the Lieb-Robinson velocity
lie72
, which in the XY model with transverse field is given by
v_{\mathrm{LR}}=2J
.
This finite group velocity defines an effective light cone beyond which the amount of transferred information decays exponentially. Consequently, a minimal time is needed for information to travel along a quantum spin chain. We here investigate the minimal time it takes to fully (anti)synchronize the two edges of the quantum XY model (
1
) of arbitrary length
N
, as a function of the noise strength
\gamma
, and compare the result to the Lieb-Robinson bound. To that end, we consider single-site noise applied at site
u=3
, and solve the quantum Liouville equation (
3
) numerically for varying
N
and
\gamma
. We compute the eigenvalues
-\mu_{\alpha}(N,\gamma)+i\lambda_{\alpha}(N,\gamma)
, with real part
\mu_{\alpha}(N,\gamma)\geq 0
and imaginary part
\lambda_{\alpha}(N,\gamma)
. The eigenvalue with smallest real and nonvanishing imaginary part
\mu^{\mathrm{s}}=\min\{\mu_{\beta}|\lambda_{\beta}\neq 0\}
sets the decay of the synchronized mode. Consequently,
r(N,\gamma)=\mathrm{min}\{\mu_{\alpha}>\mu^{\mathrm{s}}|\lambda_{\alpha}\neq 0\}
, sets the relaxation time to the (anti)synchronized state, as seen in
Figs.

1
and
2
(orange lines), as well as in
Fig.

2
b for the Pearson coefficients (dashed lines).
We thus define the synchronization time as
\tau_{\mathrm{s}}=5/r
.

Figure

3
a displays the decay rate
r
as a function of the noise amplitude
\gamma
for different chain lengths
N
. We observe that
r
first sharply increases with increasing noise strength: intensifying small noise hence significantly speeds up the relaxation, and accordingly reduces the synchronization time. However, beyond an optimal noise amplitude
\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}
, the decay rate progressively decreases, and the relaxation is slowed down. This slowing down is related to the phenomenon of noise-induced quantum Zeno effect
whi09
;
bur20
. The maximum decay rate
r_{\mathrm{max}}=r(\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}})
scales as
1/N^{3}
, as seen in
Fig.

3
b, in agreement with the scaling of the inverse gap of the Liouvillian of the XY model with boundary dissipation
zni15
. The synchronization time
\tau_{\mathrm{s}}
therefore grows like the third power of the number
N
of lattice sites, indicating that bigger systems need longer to (anti)synchronize. This dependence is stronger than the
1/N
scaling of decay rates set by the Lieb-Robinson bound
zni15
;
com5
. At the same time, the optimal noise strength
\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}
decreases as
1/N
, like the related decay rates
m^{u}_{kl}
in Eq. (
7
), before saturating at an asymptotic nonzero value
\gamma_{\mathrm{opt}}^{\infty}
, independent of the length
N
(blue dashed line).

Conclusions.
We have demonstrated the occurrence of stable (anti)synchronization of the endpoints of an isolated quantum spin chain exposed to Gaussian white noise. We have obtained (equivalent) stable synchronization conditions,
Eq.

8
for one-site noise and
Eq.

12
for two-site noise, for this noise-induced phenomenon to happen in the quantum domain. We have additionally determined the optimal noise amplitude corresponding to the shortest synchronization time, and shown that the latter grows cubically with the system size, hence stronger than the linear Lieb-Robinson bound. Remarkably, noise applied at a single spin is enough to synchronize a chain of arbitrary length and synchronized edge spins are nonclassically correlated. This opens up the possibility to employ them for synchronization-based
arg05
;
cho17
quantum communication systems.

I
Averaged evolution equation in Liouville space

We first derive the evolution equation of the averaged density operator
\rho(t)
in Liouville space for a generic (possibly time dependent) quantum system subjected to white noise.
We start with a general Hamiltonian of the form

\displaystyle H(t)=H_{0}(t)+\xi(t)V(t),

(S1)

where the total Hamiltonian
H(t)
consists of a non-fluctuating part
H_{0}(t)
and a stochastic part
\xi(t)V(t)
, with
\xi(t)
a delta-correlated Gaussian noise with zero mean and amplitude
\Gamma
,

\langle\xi(t)\rangle=0
,
\langle\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})\rangle=\Gamma\delta(t-t^{\prime})
.
For notational convenience, we drop the explicit time dependence of the Hamiltonian parts in the following.

In Liouville space, the stochastic evolution of the superket
\mathop{\lvert\rho_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}
is described by the Stratonovich stochastic differential equation
gya20

\displaystyle\mathop{\lvert\dot{\rho}_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=-i(\mathcal{L}_{0}+\xi(t)\mathcal{V})\mathop{\lvert\rho_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}\qquad\text{(Stratonovich)},

(S2)

with the supercommutator for the
non-fluctuating
part
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{L}_{0}}=\text{[\negthinspace[}H_{0},\mathds{1}\text{]\negthinspace]}=H_{0}\otimes\mathds{1}-\mathds{1}\otimes H_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}
and the
stochastic
part
\mathcal{V}=\text{[\negthinspace[}V,\mathds{1}\text{]\negthinspace]}
.
In differential form this equation reads

\displaystyle\differential{\vb{x}}=-i\mathcal{L}_{0}\mathbf{x}\differential{t}-i\sqrt{\Gamma}\mathcal{V}\mathbf{x}\differential{W(t)}\qquad\text{(Stratonovich)},

(S3)

where we have used the shorthand notation
\mathbf{x}=\mathop{\lvert\rho_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}
;
W(t)
here denotes a Wiener process.
Equation

S3
is of Langevin type with multiplicative noise and represents a generalized Kubo-oscillator model
Kubo_1963
;
Kubo_1969
. We can hence identify the drift and diffusion coefficients for this process: the superket
\mathbf{x}
undergoes Brownian motion in the complex plane with a constant drift given by
\mathcal{L}_{0}
and diffusion coefficient given by
\Gamma\mathcal{V}^{2}
. As with the classical Kubo-oscillator, the noise thus leads to phase diffusion.

The Stratonovich equation (
S3
) can be converted ito an equivalent Itô equation describing the same process
Gardiner_2004

\displaystyle\differential{\vb{x}}=(-i\mathcal{L}_{0}-\Gamma\mathcal{V}^{2}/2)\mathbf{x}\differential{t}-i\sqrt{\Gamma}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{x}\differential{W(t)}\qquad\text{(It\^{o})}.

(S4)

Transformation between Itô and Stratonovich always comes at the expense of an additional drift term
vanKampen_1981
, which here is given by half the diffusion coefficient. We exploit the properties of Itô calculus to directly evaluate the average of (
S4
). By definition of the Itô integral,
\mathbf{x}
is always taken at the left endpoint of the interval, that is before a jump in
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\differential{W(t)}}
. Thus,
\mathbf{x}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\differential{W(t)}}
are statistically independent and their average vanishes,
\langle\mathbf{x}\differential{W(t)}\rangle=0
. We accordingly arrive at Eq. (3) of the main text:

\displaystyle\mathop{\lvert\dot{\rho}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=-(i\mathcal{L}_{0}+\Gamma\mathcal{V}^{2}/2)\mathop{\lvert\rho(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle},

(S5)

with
\mathop{\lvert\rho(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=\langle\mathop{\lvert\rho_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}\rangle
. We note that the above Liouville space formalism has also been recently employed to investigate a noisy quantum Ising chain (with global instead of local coupling) in the context of quantum phase transitions
dut16
and quantum simulations
che17
.

II
Computation of the decay rates for the
XY
model

We next detail the computation of the decay rates for the
XY
model with transverse field with Hamiltonian
tak99

\displaystyle H=J\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(\sigma^{+}_{j}\sigma^{-}_{j+1}+\sigma^{-}_{j}\sigma^{+}_{j+1})+h\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma^{z}_{j}\,.

(S6)

We first perform a Jordan-Wigner transformation to reduce the dimensions, thereby mapping the interacting spin-1/2 systems to a chain of non-interacting spinless fermions
tak99
. The transformed Hamiltonian is tridiagonal and reads

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{JW}}=J\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j+1}+c^{\dagger}_{j+1}c_{j})+h\sum_{j=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}N}}(2c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{j}-\mathds{1}),

(S7)

where
c^{\dagger}_{j}
and
c_{j}
are the respective fermionic ladder operators. They are related to the usual Pauli operators by

\displaystyle c_{j}=\exp(i\pi\sum^{j-1}_{n=1} c^\dagger_nc_n)\sigma^{-}_{j},\qquad c_{j}^{\dagger}=\exp(i\pi\sum^{j-1}_{n=1} c^\dagger_nc_n)\sigma^{+}_{j}.

(S8)

In the
c_{j}
basis, the evolution of the two-point correlations,
Z=\langle(c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{k})_{1\leq j,k\leq N}\rangle
, follow a von Neumann-type equation

\displaystyle\dot{Z}(t)=i[\Omega,Z(t)],

(S9)

where
\Omega=\mathrm{diag}(J;2h;J)
contains the interactions on the off-diagonals and the
populations
on the diagonal.
The diagonal elements of
Z(t)
therefore give the time evolution of the populations from which we obtain the magnetizations by simple rescaling,
\langle\sigma^{z}_{j}\rangle=2Z_{jj}-1
.

Let us now apply Gaussian white noise
\xi(t)

to
a single spin at site
u
by choosing
V=\sigma^{z}_{u}
. In the Jordan-Wigner representation, the equation of motion then becomes

\displaystyle\dot{Z}_{\xi}(t)=i[\Omega+2\xi(t)Y,Z_{\xi}(t)],

(S10)

with
Y=\outerproduct{\hat{\vb{e}}_u}{\hat{\vb{e}}_u}
in the canonical basis.
In Liouville space, we accordingly have

\displaystyle\mathop{\lvert\dot{Z}_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=(i\mathcal{Q}+2\xi(t)\mathcal{Y})\mathop{\lvert Z_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle},

(S11)

with
\mathcal{Q}=\text{[\negthinspace[}\Omega,\mathds{1}\text{]\negthinspace]}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\text{[\negthinspace[}Y,\mathds{1}\text{]\negthinspace]}
.
Equation (
S11
) is of the same form as (
S2
). Averaging over the noise, we therefore find

\displaystyle\mathop{\lvert\dot{Z}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=(i\mathcal{Q}-2\Gamma\mathcal{Y}^{2})\mathop{\lvert Z(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle},

(S12)

with
\mathop{\lvert Z(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=\langle\mathop{\lvert Z_{\xi}(t)\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}\rangle
.
The spin chain possesses an intrinsic time scale given by the interaction strength
J
. To arrive at statements independent of the specific value of
J
, we introduce the normalized time
\tau=Jt
.

Since
Eq.

S12
is of the form of a Schrödinger equation (with
“Hamiltonian”

i\mathcal{Q}
and
“perturbation”

-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2}\Gamma\mathcal{Y}^{2}
), we can apply standard perturbation theory to evaluate the decay rates for small noise amplitude (with reduced strength
\gamma=\Gamma/J
). The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the tridiagonal Toepliz matrix
\Omega
are respectively given by
Reichel_2013

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}_{k}

\displaystyle=2{h}/J+2\cos(\frac{\pi k}{N+1}),

(S13)

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\sin(\frac{\pi k}{N+1}),\sin(\frac{2\pi k}{N+1}),\ldots,\sin(\frac{N\pi k}{N+1})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{N+1}}\,.

(S14)

The (unperturbed) eigenfrequencies and normal modes of the noise-free system immediately follow as

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}_{kl}

\displaystyle=2\left(\cos(\frac{\pi k}{N+1})-\cos(\frac{\pi l}{N+1})\right),

(S15)

\displaystyle\mathop{\lvert\varphi_{k},\varphi_{l}\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}

\displaystyle=\left(\sin(\frac{\pi k}{N+1})\sin(\frac{\pi l}{N+1}),\ldots,\sin(\frac{N \pi}{N+1})\sin(\frac{N \pi}{N+1})\right)^{\mathrm{T}}\frac{2}{N+1}.

(S16)

\tilde{\Lambda}_{kl}
and
\mathop{\lvert\varphi_{k},\varphi_{l}\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}
are the eigenfrequencies and eigenmodes of the magnetizations
\langle\sigma^{z}_{j}\rangle

\{\tilde{\Lambda}_{kl}\}\subset\{\Lambda_{kl}\}
and the magnetization modes are a subspace of the full eigensystem

.
There are
\#\tilde{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{deg}}=\lfloor N^{2}/(N/2-1)\rfloor
twofold degenerate eigenfrequencies with

\displaystyle\tilde{\Lambda}_{rs}=\tilde{\Lambda}_{N+1-s,N+1-r},

(S17)

the others are nondegenerate. For every degenerate eigenfrequency there are two orthonormal eigenvectors
\mathop{\lvert a\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=\mathop{\lvert\varphi_{r},\varphi_{s}\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}
and

We obtain the decay rates

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}m^{u}_{kl}|_{\mathrm{non}}}

(S18)

(S19)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{N+1}\left[\sin(\frac{uk\pi}{N+1})^{2}+\sin(\frac{ul\pi}{N+1})^{2}\right]-\frac{16}{(N+1)^{2}}\left[\sin(\frac{uk\pi}{N+1})^{2}\sin(\frac{ul\pi}{N+1})^{2}\right],

For the degenerate frequencies, we have to compute the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the perturbation matrix

\displaystyle P=\begin{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{aa}}&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}2(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}}\\

(S22)

\displaystyle v^{+}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}}}{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}}|},1\right)^{\mathrm{T}},\qquad v^{-}=\left(-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}}}{|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}}|},1\right)^{\mathrm{T}}.

(S23)

From

\displaystyle\mathop{\lvert a^{\pm}\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\mathop{\lvert a\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}\pm\mathrm{sgn}\{(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}\}\mathop{\lvert a\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}).

The projection onto the magnetization eigenbasis produces the same magnetization eigenmode for
\mathop{\lvert a\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}
and

\mathop{\lvert b\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}
. That is,

We therefore finally obtain for the zeroth-order eigenmode

\displaystyle\mathop{\langle\negthinspace\negthinspace\langle\sigma^{z}_{j}|a^{\pm}\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1\pm\mathrm{sgn}\{(\mathcal{Y}^{2})_{ab}\})\underbrace{\mathop{\langle\negthinspace\negthinspace\langle\sigma^{z}_{j}|a\rangle\negthinspace\negthinspace\rangle}}_{\mathclap{\equiv\epsilon_{j,a},\text{ c.f. \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:magmode}}}}.

Depending on the sign of

one of the corrections will always vanish while the other gains a factor of

Evaluating

(S26)

The case of two-site noise with

\displaystyle m^{u,v}_{kl}|_{\mathrm{non}}=

for degenerate eigenfrequencies. The only possible configuration such that

III
Stable synchronization condition

In this Section, we derive the stable synchronization condition given by Eq. (8) of the main text. To this end, we try to find a configuration
l,k,N,u
for which every mode except a single one decays. We require that only a single pair
(k,l)
fulfills
m^{u}_{kl}=0
. By examining
Eq.

S26
, we find that this is achieved when

\displaystyle\sin(\frac{uk\pi}{N+1})\overset{!}{=}0\land\sin(\frac{ul\pi}{N+1})\overset{!}{=}0.

However, if
k
solves the above equation, every integer multiple of
k
is also a solution (the same holds for

. We further require that

, but we are also constrained by

. As result, this condition becomes

.

The configuration

is the one with the smallest possible

that solves
Eq.

S30
. Because the value for

cannot exceed the length of the chain

, we additionally have

\displaystyle k

\displaystyle<N,

(S31)

\displaystyle l=2k

\displaystyle<N,

(S32)

\displaystyle 3k

\displaystyle>N.

The remaining task is to find

. Finally, we obtain
