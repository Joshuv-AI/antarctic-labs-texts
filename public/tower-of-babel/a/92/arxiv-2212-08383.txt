Analytic K K -semistability and wall-crossing
Lars Martin Sektnan and Carl Tipler

Abstract
. For a small polarised deformation of a constant scalar curvature Kähler manifold with abelian reduced automorphism group, we prove that K K -polystability along nearby polarisations implies the existence of a constant scalar curvature Kähler metric. In this setting, we reduce K K -polystability to the computation of the classical Futaki invariant on the cscK degeneration. Our result holds on specific families and provides a first step towards wall-crossing phenomena for the moduli of cscK manifolds when the polarisation varies.



1.
Introduction

One of the central themes of Kähler geometry is the search for canonical Kähler metrics. A natural notion of canonical metric is constant scalar curvature Kähler (cscK) metrics. Such metrics may exist in some, all or no Kähler classes and the goal is to determine which classes on a given manifold admit such metrics. The Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture (
[
40
,
38
,
14
]
) predicts that the existence of a cscK metric should be equivalent to an algebro-geometric notion of stability, called
K
-polystability. The conjecture has had great progress, but still remains open.

Classical results of LeBrun–Simanca (
[
29
]
) say that a cscK manifold will admit a cscK metric in all nearby Kähler classes, and on all nearby complex deformations, provided the reduced automorphism group of
X
is trivial. In general, while (
[
28
]
) says that a cscK manifold will still admit a more general type of canonical metric, called extremal Kähler metrics, in all nearby Kähler classes, the presence of automorphisms may prevent such openness when deforming the complex structure (
[
16
,
35
,
31
]
).

Our work focuses on the YTD conjecture when we
vary both the Kähler class and the complex structure simultaneously, in the presence of symmetries. Our setting is working with polarised manifolds
(X,[\omega])
that are small deformations of a cscK manifold
(X_{0},\omega)
. This in particular implies that
(X,[\omega])
is
K
-semistable (
[
15
]
), and covers as a special case of interest
analytically
K
-semistable
manifolds, when
(X_{0},\omega)
is the central fiber of a smooth
test configuration
for
(X,[\omega])
(see Section
2.2
for the definitions). In the latter case, unless it is isomorphic to
X_{0}
,
X
will then not admit a cscK metric in
[\omega]
.
We then investigate what happens as one varies this Kähler class, where
(X,[\omega])
may or may not admit a cscK metric.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
(X,[\omega])
is a small polarised complex deformation of a cscK manifold
(X_{0},[\omega])
. Assume that
h^{0,2}(X_{0})=0
,
H^{2}(X_{0},TX_{0})=0
and that the reduced automorphism group of
X_{0}
is abelian. Then for any class
[\alpha]\in H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})\cap H^{1,1}(X)
, there exists an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
,
(X,[\omega+\varepsilon\alpha])
is
K
-polystable if and only if
X
admits a cscK metric in
[\omega+\varepsilon\alpha]
. Moreover, in the analytically
K
-semistable case, to test for
K
-polystability in these classes, it suffices to check on the test configurations produced from holomorphic vector fields on
X_{0}
.

This is a local verification of the Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture, near a cscK manifold. Moreover, the final condition really says that the verification of
K
-polystability is a finite condition in these classes, which we now explain.

The natural candidate for the obstruction to
K
-polystability of
X
in the perturbed classes can be understood algebro-geometrically. In general,
K
-polystability asks one to verify that a certain numerical invariant, the
Donaldson–Futaki invariant
(see Section
2.2
), is non-negative for all test configurations of
X
, with equality if and only if the test configuration is a product test configuration, constructed from a
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
X
. Since
X_{0}
admits a cscK metric, it is
K
-polystable (
[
33
,
7
]
), and the only test configurations with vanishing Donaldson–Futaki invariant are therefore product test configurations. Morally, test configurations on
X_{0}
induce ones for
X
and the Donaldson–Futaki invariant will have an expansion in the Kähler class whose leading order term is the one of the initial class. In particular, the natural candidate to test
K
-polystability on is the test configurations on
X
induced from product test configurations on
X_{0}
.

Our main result shows that this is precisely what one needs to test
K
-polystability for
X
on, in classes near the initial semistable class. The precise test configurations needed to test
K
-polystability of
X
are obtained from the action of the reduced automorphism group of
X_{0}
on its Kuranishi slice, and the associated invariants to be computed are explicitly described in Section
4.5
. We should highlight that it is a very difficult problem to test
K
-polystability in general, and our work provides a new instance where this can be achieved with a finite number of explicit test configurations. As an illustration, in Section
5
, we apply Theorem
1.1
to small deformations of a
4
-point blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
, completing the picture developed in
[
6
,
31
]
.

Remark 1.2
.

We comment on the three technical hypothesis that appear in the statement of Theorem
1.1
. The first two,
h^{0,2}(X_{0})=0
and
H^{2}(X_{0},TX_{0})=0
, are mainly cosmetic. The first implies that the classes
[\omega+\varepsilon\alpha]
are of type
(1,1)
on both
X
and
X_{0}
, which is essential in our construction. We can drop that assumption by considering some specific perturbation classes
[\alpha]
. The second one can be relaxed by considering
unobstructed
families of deformations. We refer to Remark
3.5
for a more general setting where our results apply. On the other hand, the hypothesis on the reduced automorphism group of
X_{0}
to be a torus seems to be purely technical. While we were not able to remove it, we believe that an adaptation of the work of Sjamaar (
[
32
]
) should be the key to extend Theorem
1.1
to general reductive groups (see Remark
4.7
).

Our result exhibits some local wall-crossing phenomena for
K
-stability, as one varies the polarisation. Such phenomena arise when a moduli space parametrising families of stable objects undergo transformations when the stability condition varies. As a leading example, in
[
12
]
, the cone of linearisations for a given reductive group action is divided into chambers, where crossing a wall results in a birational transformation of the associated GIT quotient. While there are deep results on wall-crossings for moduli of stable bundles on surfaces (see
[
37
, Chapter 4, Section C]
and reference therein), moduli of curves (e.g.
[
23
]
), or moduli of log Fano pairs (see e.g.
[
3
]
), far less seems to be known about variations of cscK moduli, as built in
[
19
,
9
]
. While a broader approach for wall-crossings of moduli of varieties is developed in
[
8
]
, in this work we focus on local variations of the cscK moduli under perturbations of the polarisation. As we explain in Section
4.5
, our result holds uniformly in specific families of polarised manifolds, and shows that the sign of a finite number of Futaki invariants on
X_{0}
governs whether or not those families will contribute to the corresponding cscK moduli. This provides an evidence that wall-crossing phenomena should be observed for such moduli. While the main difficulties are yet to come to understand those wall-crossings fully, we expect our construction to be a first step in that direction.

Remark 1.3
.

It would be very interesting to address the issues and results of the present work purely algebro-geometrically, for example using the newly developed non-Archimedean framework for
K
-stability of Boucksom–Jonsson (
[
4
]
). In particular, they prove the (uniform)
K
-stability analogue of the LeBrun–Simanca openness theorem (
[
28
]
). Note, however, that it is crucial to allow for automorphisms in the current situation. Indeed, while the strictly
K
-semistable manifold might have discrete automorphism group, the
K
-polystable central fibre it is a small deformation of will
always
have non-trivial reduced automorphism group. The work of Boucksom–Jonsson is under the assumption that the reduced automorphism group is trivial and thus their techniques cannot immediately be applied to give purely algebro-geometric counterparts to our results. On the other hand, with an extension of their work to allow for automorphisms, it is more likely their techniques also work in situations where the
K
-polystable central fibre is singular.
The above is thus one potential application of extending their techniques to allow for non-trivial reduced automorphism groups.
Note however that due to the lack of the resolution of the Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture in general, this would not give the same implications about existence of cscK metrics.

Finally, let us describe the main steps in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The first one is the construction of a family of Kuranishi slices parametrised by
\varepsilon
. They satisfy the constraints that the almost complex structures in the
\varepsilon
-slice are compatible with
\omega+\varepsilon\alpha
while the restriction of the scalar curvature operator on those slices lives in a finite dimensional space of hamiltonians. This construction is performed in Section
3
, and is a version of
[
35
, Section 3]
with parameters, largely relying on ideas from
[
29
,
16
,
35
]
. With this at hand, we reduce the problem to finding zeros of a finite dimensional moment map as in
[
35
]
, with the additional difficulties coming from the perturbation of the Kähler class. While we borrowed the idea of perturbing finite dimensional moment maps to solve a perturbative problem in Kähler geometry from
[
8
,
10
]
, we stress that our method to solve the resulting finite dimensional problem is fairly different, and does not use the quantitative implicit function theorem. Instead, we use a more geometric approach in Section
4.3
where we develop a local rigidity result for families of moment maps. This clearly and explicitly produces the numerical invariants needed to test stability. This new method seems to be quite robust, and we give a general and abstract presentation of this section, that could be of independent interest for other perturbative problems related to infinite dimensional moment maps.

2.
Extremal metrics and stability

In this section, we will introduce notations and recall classical results about extremal Kähler metrics and
K
-stability. We refer the reader to
[
22
]
and
[
36
]
for a detailed treatment.

2.1.
Extremal metrics and the moment map picture

Let
(X_{0},[\omega])
be a polarised Kähler manifold.
We denote
M
the underlying smooth manifold and
J_{0}
the almost complex structure, so that
X_{0}=(M,J_{0})
. Recall that
\omega
is said to be
extremal
if its scalar curvature function is a hamiltonian (with respect to
\omega
) of a holomorphic vector field. Equivalently,

\mathrm{scal(J_{0},\omega)}
is in the kernel of the operator

(2.1)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mathcal{D}:&\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})&\to&\Omega^{0,1}(T^{1,0})\\
&f&\mapsto&\overline{\partial}(\nabla_{\omega}^{1,0}f),\end{array}

where
\nabla_{\omega}^{1,0}f
is defined by
\overline{\partial}f=\omega(\nabla_{\omega}^{1,0}f,\cdot)
. The kernel of
\mathcal{D}
is the Lie algebra of
\mathrm{Isom}_{0}(M,J_{0},\omega)
, the connected component of the group of hamiltonian biholomorphism of
(M,J_{0},\omega)
. A special case of extremal metrics are given by constant scalar curvature metrics (cscK metrics for short), for which, by Matsushima and Lichnerowicz, the
reduced automorphism group
is the complexification of the compact Lie group
\mathrm{Isom}_{0}(M,J_{0},\omega)
.

Independently, Fujiki (
[
18
]
) and Donaldson (
[
13
]
) provided an infinite dimensional moment map interpretation of cscK metrics (see
[
22
, Chapter 9]
for a detailed treatment including the extremal metric case). Let
\mathcal{J}
be the space of almost-complex structures on
M
and denote
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
the subspace of
\omega
-compatible elements. The group
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
of hamiltonian symplectomorphisms acts on
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
via the standard action of diffeomorphisms on
\mathcal{J}
, and
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
carries a formal symplectic structure
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}_{\omega}}
such that the
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
-action is hamiltonian, with moment map

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{\mathcal{J}_{\omega}}:&\mathcal{J}_{\omega}&\to&\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(M,\mathbb{R})\\
&J&\mapsto&\mathrm{scal}(J,\omega)-\hat{s}.\end{array}

Here
\hat{s}
is the average of the hermitian scalar curvature
\mathrm{scal}(J,\omega)
of
(J,\omega)
and
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(M,\mathbb{R})
is the space of mean value zero function (with respect to the volume form
\omega^{n}
), identified with the dual of the Lie algebra of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
via the pairing

(2.2)

\langle f,h\rangle_{\omega}=\int_{M}fg\;\frac{\omega^{n}}{n!}.

While the complexification of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
does not exist, a key fact is that one can make sense of the leaf of a “
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}
-foliation” in the following manner. The operator
\mathcal{D}
gives the infinitesimal action of
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
on
T_{J_{0}}\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
. We can extend this operator
\mathbb{C}
-linearly, and we will say that
J_{1}
is in the
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
J_{0}
if there are paths
(J_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
in
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
and
(\phi_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
in
\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{C})
such that

\frac{d}{dt}J_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}\phi_{t},

where
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is the operator (
2.1
) at
J_{t}
. Locally, we can consider small variations of almost complex structures along “complexified orbits” as follows.

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
sufficiently small in some appropriate
L^{2,k}
-norm, we can, using Moser’s trick, find a diffeomorphism
F_{\omega,\phi}
such that
F_{\omega,\phi}^{*}(\omega-dJ_{0}d\phi)=\omega
. We then set

(2.3)

J_{\phi}=F_{\omega,\phi}^{*}J_{0}.

We then see that varying in a complexified orbit in
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
amounts to varying the Kähler form in a Kähler class for a fixed complex structure, and we will take the former point of view to look for cscK (or extremal) metrics within a fixed Kähler class.

2.2.
K
-stability

The Yau–Tian–Donaldson conjecture predicts that an extremal metric should exist on
(X_{0},[\omega])
if and only if it satisfies a relative
K
-stability condition (
[
40
,
38
,
14
,
34
]
). While recent progress shows that the right notion to consider should be a uniform version of
K
-stability, we will only need a weaker notion in the present work.

Definition 2.1
.

A smooth test configuration for a polarised Kähler manifold
(X,[\omega])
is given by
a relatively polarised
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-equivariant family of Kähler manifolds
\pi:(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})\to\mathbb{C}
for the natural
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
\mathbb{C}
with
(\mathcal{X}_{1},\mathcal{A}_{1})\simeq(X,[\omega])
.

In this definition, by
family
we mean that the map
\pi
must be surjective and a proper submersion. For any such test configuration
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
, the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on the central fiber
(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
provides the Donaldson–Futaki invariant :

\mathrm{DF}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A}):=-\int_{\mathcal{X}_{0}}H_{u}\,(\mathrm{scal}(J_{0},\omega_{0})-\hat{s})\;\frac{\omega_{0}^{n}}{n!},

where
(M,J_{0},[\omega_{0}])\simeq(\mathcal{X}_{0},\mathcal{A}_{0})
, and where
H_{u}
is the hamiltonian of the generator
u\in\Gamma(T^{1,0}\mathcal{X}_{0})
of the
S^{1}
-action on the central fiber with respect to
\omega_{0}
.

Definition 2.2
.

We will say that
(X,[\omega])
is
K
-polystable
if for all smooth test configurations
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})
for
(X,[\omega])
, the associated Donaldson–Futaki invariant satisfies

\mathrm{DF}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})\geq 0,

with equality only for product test configurations
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{A})\simeq(X\times\mathbb{C},[\omega_{0}])
.

Remark 2.3
.

K
-polystability usually requires one to also consider test configurations that are not smooth. In general, this is needed, but we will only require this weaker notion of
K
-polystability in the present work.

3.
Deformation theory and the perturbed slices

Let
(X_{0},[\omega])
be a polarised Kähler manifold. We denote
M
the underlying smooth manifold and
J_{0}
the almost complex structure, so that
X_{0}=(M,J_{0})
.

3.1.
Deformations of polarised Kähler manifolds after Kuranishi, Fujiki and Schumacher

In
[
19
, Section 2]
, a deformation complex for the pair
(X_{0},[\omega])
is introduced. First, the usual Kuranishi complex for deformation of complex structures on
X_{0}=(M,J_{0})
is (
[
26
]
)

\ldots\to\Omega^{0,k}(T^{1,0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\overline{\partial}}}{{\to}}\Omega^{0,k+1}(T^{1,0})\to\ldots,

where the extension of
\overline{\partial}
to
(0,p)
-forms is given in local coordinates, for
\beta=\sum_{j}\beta_{j}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{j}}
, by

\overline{\partial}\beta=\sum_{j}\overline{\partial}\beta_{j}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{j}}.

We wish to consider deformations that are compatible with
\omega
. Following
[
19
]
, we define maps

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\iota^{k}_{\bullet}:&\Omega^{0,k}(T^{1,0})&\to&\Omega^{0,k+1}\\
&\beta&\mapsto&\iota_{\beta}\omega\end{array}

where
\iota_{\beta}\omega
is obtained by the composition of first contraction and then alternation operators. For
k\geq 1
, we then set :

\Omega_{\omega}^{0,k}(T^{1,0}):=\ker\iota^{k}_{\bullet},

while we define

\Omega_{\omega}^{0,0}(T^{1,0}):=\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{C}).

Together with the restriction of
\overline{\partial}
, and the map
\overline{\partial}^{0}:=\mathcal{D}
defined by (
2.1
), we obtain an elliptic complex
(\Omega_{\omega}^{0,\bullet}(T^{1,0}),\overline{\partial}^{\bullet})
.
We denote by
H^{0,k}_{\omega}(T^{1,0})
the associated cohomology groups. Then, from this complex, one can build a slice for the
\mathcal{G}^{\mathbb{C}}
-action on
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
around
J_{0}
. As in
[
11
]
, setting
K:=\mathrm{Isom}_{0}(M,J_{0},\omega)
, this construction can actually be made
K
-equivariantly, and even locally
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-equivariantly, for the natural
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-actions on
H^{0,1}_{\omega}(T^{1,0})
and
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
induced by
K\subset\mathcal{G}_{\omega}\subset\mathrm{Diff}(M)
. To sum up:

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a holomorphic
K
-equivariant map

\Phi:B\to\mathcal{J}_{\omega}

from a ball around the origin
B\subset H^{0,1}_{\omega}(T^{1,0})
such that

(1)

\Phi(0)=J_{0}
;

(2)

Z:=\Phi^{-1}(\mathcal{J}_{\omega}^{\mathrm{int}})
is a complex subspace of
B
;

(3)

if
(b,b^{\prime})\in Z^{2}
lie in the same
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, then
(M,\Phi(b))
and
(M,\Phi(b^{\prime}))
are isomorphic complex manifolds;

(4)

The
\mathcal{G}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of any small complex deformation of
X_{0}
polarised by
[\omega]
intersects
\Phi(Z)
.

From deformation theory (
[
27
, Chapter 8]
), this map induces a
K
-equivariant semi-universal family of polarised deformations of
(X_{0},[\omega])

\pi:\mathcal{X}\to Z

such that any two points
(b,b^{\prime})
in the same
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit on
Z
correspond to isomorphic polarised manifolds
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega])\simeq(\mathcal{X}_{b^{\prime}},[\omega])
.

3.2.
Perturbing the slice

We are now interested in perturbing the Kähler classes and building a family of slices for the perturbed classes, following ideas from
[
10
]
and
[
35
]
. For simplicity of the exposition, we will assume that
h^{0,2}(X_{0})=0
, so that for any small complex deformation
X
of
X_{0}
, one has
h^{0,2}(X)=0
(
[
39
, Chapter 9]
) and

H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})=H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})=H^{1,1}(X_{0},\mathbb{R}),

where we denote
H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R}):=H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{C})\cap H^{2}(X,\mathbb{R})
. We will also assume
H^{2}(X_{0},TX_{0})=0
so that
Z=B
in Proposition
3.1
. Those hypotheses can be relaxed, see Remark
3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{H}^{1,1}(X_{0},\mathbb{R})
be the space of
(\omega,J_{0})
-harmonic real
(1,1)
-forms on
(X_{0},\omega)
and let
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})
be a basis of this vector space. Note that by uniqueness of harmonic representatives of Dolbeault cohomology classes, the
\alpha_{i}
’s are
K
-invariant. For any
\underline{\varepsilon}:=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{r})
, we set

\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}:=\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varepsilon_{i}\alpha_{i}.

This defines a Kähler form on
X_{0}
for all
\underline{\varepsilon}
in a sufficiently small neighbourhood
U
of zero in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
. For any
b\in Z
and
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
, we have
[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}]\in H^{1,1}(\mathcal{X}_{b},\mathbb{R})
. However,
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
itself might not be of type
(1,1)
with respect to
\Phi(b)
. For this reason we perform a first perturbation of the slice
\Phi
, based on Moser’s trick.

Lemma 3.2
.

Up to shrinking
U
and
B
, there exists a smooth map

\Upsilon:U\times B\to\mathcal{J}

such that

(1)

For all
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
,
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},0)=J_{0}
;

(2)

For all
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)\in U\times B
,
(M,\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b))
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{X}_{b}
;

(3)

For all
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
, the map
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},\cdot)
is a
K
-equivariant map from
B
to
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
whose image is a symplectic submanifold.

Note that by the properties of
\pi:\mathcal{X}\to B
, we have in particular that if
(b,b^{\prime})
lie in the same
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, then the almost-complex structures
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
and
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b^{\prime})
are equivalent for all
\underline{\varepsilon}
.
The symplectic structure on
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
in point
(3)
is the one of Fujiki and Donaldson as defined in Section
2.1
, while the
K
-action on
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
is the standard one on almost-complex structures through diffeomorphisms, which restricts to this subspace as
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
is
K
-invariant.

Proof.

The starting point is a map
\Phi
as in Proposition
3.1
.
Note that from the assumption
H^{2}(X_{0},TX_{0})=0
,
\Phi(b)
is integrable for all
b\in B
. We use
b
as a subscript to emphasise that the almost-complex structure considered is
\Phi(b)
. Then the
(1,1)_{b}
-component of a
2
-form
\alpha
is the fixed part of
\alpha
under the map
\alpha\mapsto\alpha(\Phi(b)\cdot,\Phi(b)\cdot)
. Thus, its
(2,0)_{b}+(0,2)_{b}
-component is given by

(3.1)

\alpha^{(2,0)_{b}+(0,2)_{b}}=\frac{\alpha-\alpha(\Phi(b)\cdot,\Phi(b)\cdot)}{2}.

In particular, for fixed
\alpha
, we see that this is smooth in
b
.

Now, consider the forms
\alpha_{i}
. They are
d
-closed, and thus satisfy
\overline{\partial}_{b}\alpha_{i}^{(0,2)_{b}}=0
for all
b
. From
h^{0,2}(X_{0})=0
, we deduce that there is
\theta_{i}(b)^{\prime}\in\Omega_{b}^{(0,1)}(M,\mathbb{R})
such that
\overline{\partial}_{b}\theta_{i}(b)^{\prime}=\alpha_{i}^{(0,2)_{b}}
. Denoting
G_{b}
the Green operator of
\Delta_{b}:=\overline{\partial}^{*}_{b}\overline{\partial}+\overline{\partial}\overline{\partial}_{b}^{*}
with respect to the Kähler metric
(\omega,J_{b})
, we can actually choose
\theta_{i}(b)^{\prime}=G_{b}\overline{\partial}^{*}_{b}\alpha_{i}^{(0,2)_{b}}
. From this expression, we see that
\theta_{i}(b)^{\prime}
varies smoothly with
b
. Similarly, we solve
\alpha_{i}^{(2,0)_{b}}=\partial_{b}\theta_{i}(b)^{\prime\prime}
using a smooth map
b\mapsto\theta_{i}(b)^{\prime\prime}
, and set
\theta_{i}=\theta_{i}^{\prime}+\theta_{i}^{\prime\prime}
. Finally, we define

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}&=&\displaystyle\omega+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varepsilon_{i}(\alpha_{i}-d\theta_{i}(b))\\
&=&\displaystyle\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}-d\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varepsilon_{i}\theta_{i}(b)\right)\\
&=&\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}-d\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)\end{array}

where

\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\varepsilon_{i}\theta_{i}(b).

As for all
b
,
\omega
is
\Phi(b)
-compatible, and by construction of the
\theta_{i}
’s, we see that
(\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}},\Phi(b))
defines a Kähler structure on
M
for
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
small enough. Moreover,
[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}]=[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}]
in
H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
.

We now use Moser’s trick and consider the time one flow
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}
of the vector field
v_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}
defined as the
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
-symplectic dual of
\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)
. From the defining formula
\iota_{v_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}=\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)
, we see that

\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b):=F_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}^{*}\Phi(b)

is a smooth map. By construction, points
(1)
and
(2)
in the statement of the lemma are clearly satisfied.

It remains to show point
(3)
. Since
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}^{*}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}=\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
,
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
is
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
-compatible. From Equation (
3.1
), one deduces that
b\mapsto\alpha^{(0,2)_{b}}
is
K
-equivariant from the
K
-invariance of the
\alpha_{i}
’s and
K
-equivariance of
\Phi
. Arguing as in
[
11
, Lemma 4.1]
, the operators
G_{b}
and
\overline{\partial}^{*}_{b}
are
K
-equivariant. Thus the forms
\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
are
K
-equivariant. As
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
is
K
-invariant, we deduce that for
f\in K
,
v_{\underline{\varepsilon},f(b)}
is conjugated to
v_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}
via the adjoint action of
f
, and hence
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},f(b)}
is conjugated to
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}
via
f
. The equivariance of
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},\cdot)
follows.

Finally, the image
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},B)
defines a symplectic submanifold of
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
for all
\underline{\varepsilon}
. Indeed, from the construction of
\Phi
, we know that
\Phi(B)
is a complex submanifold of
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
and hence a symplectic submanifold. In particular, the image of
\Phi
is embedded in
\mathcal{J}
. As this is an open property, up to shrinking
U\times B
, we can assume that for all
\underline{\varepsilon}
, the image of
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},\cdot)
is embedded into
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\subset\mathcal{J}
. Moreover, from the definition of
\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)
, we see that

||\theta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)||_{\omega_{0}}\leq C\,||\underline{\varepsilon}||,

for a constant
C
that can be taken uniformly in
b\in B
. Thus
v_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}=\mathcal{O}(||\underline{\varepsilon}||)
and hence

F_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}=\Id+\mathcal{O}(||\underline{\varepsilon}||).

As
\Omega_{\mathcal{J}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}=\Omega_{\mathcal{J}_{\omega}}+\mathcal{O}(||\underline{\varepsilon}||)
, we deduce that
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},\cdot)^{*}\Omega_{\mathcal{J}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
remains non-degenerate, up to shrinking
U\times B
again.
∎

From now on, we assume that
(M,J_{0},\omega)
is cscK.
The aim is to find cscK metrics on
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
for small
\underline{\varepsilon}
. In order to reduce to a finite dimensional problem, we further perturb the slice, following an idea by Donaldson (
[
16
]
), developed in
[
35
,
5
]
. Recall that
K
is a maximal compact subgroup of the group of reduced automorphisms of
X_{0}
. As
(M,J_{0},\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})
is Kähler for
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
, with
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
invariant under the action of
K
, we deduce that
K
is actually a group of hamiltonian transformations for
(M,\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})
for every
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
. Hence we can see
K\subset\mathcal{G}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, where we include
\underline{\varepsilon}
in the notation for
\mathcal{G}
to remember the dependence on
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
. Hence, for each
\underline{\varepsilon}
, the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{k}
of
K
can be seen as a subspace of
\mathrm{Lie}(\mathcal{G}_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\simeq\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{0}(M,\mathbb{R})
. We will denote by
\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
this subspace, namely, the space of hamiltonians of
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
induced by
\mathfrak{k}
, normalised to have mean value zero with respect to the volume form
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{n}
. We will denote this correspondence by

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathfrak{k}&\to&\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\\
u&\mapsto&H_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}.\end{array}

Proposition 3.3
.

Up to shrinking
U
and
B
, there exists a smooth map

\Psi:U\times B\to\mathcal{J}

satisfying properties (2) and (3) of the map
\Upsilon
from Lemma
3.2
, and in addition

(4)

for all
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)\in U\times B,\>\mathrm{scal}(\Psi(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\in\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}.

The proof below follows the one of
[
35
, Proposition 7]
, where the modifications due to the variations of the Kähler classes are following the ideas from
[
28
]
.

Proof.

For each
\underline{\varepsilon}
, and each
\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{0}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
with sufficiently small
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-norm (note that up to shrinking
U
we can assume the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-norms induced by the
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
to be equivalent), we denote by
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}J
the complex structure obtained through the action of
\phi\in\mathrm{Lie}(\mathcal{G}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\underline{\varepsilon}})
on
J\in\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
as defined in Equation (
2.3
). Starting from the slices
\Upsilon
of Lemma
3.2
, we would like to find, for each sufficiently small
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
, a
\phi=\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
such that

(3.2)

\mathrm{scal}(F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\in\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}.

We thus introduce the projections

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\Pi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}:&\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})&\to&\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\\
&\phi&\mapsto&\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{dim}(K)}\langle\phi,H_{u_{i},\underline{\varepsilon}}\rangle_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\;H_{u_{i},\underline{\varepsilon}}\end{array}

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
is defined by Equation (
2.2
) using
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
and
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{\mathrm{dim}(K)})
is a fixed basis of
\mathfrak{k}
.

Note that the variation of this map in
\underline{\varepsilon}
is smooth. Indeed, the potential
H_{u_{i},\underline{\varepsilon}}
is given by

H_{u_{i},\underline{\varepsilon}}=G_{\underline{\varepsilon}}d^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\iota_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}

where
G_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
and
d^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
are the Green operator of
dd^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}+d^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}d
and the adjoint of
d
for the metric given by
(J_{0},\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})
, and
\hat{u}_{i}
stands for the vector field associated to
u_{i}\in\mathfrak{k}
. This formula can be checked from the properties of the Green operator:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}dG_{\underline{\varepsilon}}d^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\iota_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}&=&G_{\underline{\varepsilon}}dd^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\iota_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\\
&=&G_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\Delta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\iota_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\\
&=&\iota_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}},\end{array}

where in the second line we used
\mathcal{L}_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}=0
and in the third line we used that
\iota_{\hat{u}_{i}}\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
is orthogonal to harmonic
1
-forms (see e.g.
[
22
, Chapter 2]
). From this we see that
H_{u_{i},\underline{\varepsilon}}
is smooth in
\underline{\varepsilon}
, and hence so is
\Pi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
.

From this we deduce that for small
\underline{\varepsilon}
,
\Pi_{0}:\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\to\mathfrak{k}_{0}
is an isomorphism, and hence, up to shrinking
U
, solving

(\Id-\Pi_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\left(\mathrm{scal}(F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\right)=0

is equivalent to solving

(\Id-\Pi_{0})(\Id-\Pi_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\left(\mathrm{scal}(F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\right)=0.

We then introduce the operator

\begin{array}[]{cccc}A:&U\times B\times\mathcal{U}&\to&\mathfrak{k}_{0}^{\perp}\\
&(\underline{\varepsilon},b,\phi)&\mapsto&(\Id-\Pi_{0})(\Id-\Pi_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\left(\mathrm{scal}(F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\right),\end{array}

where
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
is a small neighbourhood of zero for the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
norm.

Note that by the definition of
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}
,

\mathrm{scal}\left(F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\right)=\mathrm{scal}\left(\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}+d\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b)d\phi\right).

With this formulation, we see that
A
can be extended, for
k
large enough, to a small neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}^{2,k}
of zero in the Sobolev
L^{2,k}
completion of
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
as a map

A:U\times B\times\mathcal{U}^{k}\to\mathfrak{k}_{0}^{\perp,k-4}

where
\mathfrak{k}_{0}^{\perp,k-4}
is the orthognal of
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
in
L^{2,k-4}
. Because
(J_{0},\omega)
is cscK, the differential of
A
with respect to
\phi
at
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)=(0,0)
is nothing but
(\Id-\Pi_{0})\mathcal{D}_{0}^{*}\mathcal{D}_{0}
. As the Lichnerowicz operator
\mathcal{D}_{0}^{*}\mathcal{D}_{0}
has cokernel
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
,
(\Id-\Pi_{0})\mathcal{D}_{0}^{*}\mathcal{D}_{0}
is surjective and we can apply the implicit function theorem to
A
. We obtain
\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
such that (
3.2
) holds for all
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
small enough. Note that actually
\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
is a-priori only
L^{2,k}
, but by Sobolev embeddings, we can assume it lies in
\mathcal{C}^{l}(M,\mathbb{R})
, for
l
as large as desired. In fact, if we start with a sufficiently large value of
k
, then from elliptic bootstrapping,
\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
has to be smooth since the scalar curvature determined by
\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
lies in
\mathfrak{k}_{0}
and is thus smooth.

Now we consider the perturbed slice

\Psi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)=F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi}^{*}\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b).

It is clear by construction that it satisfies
(2)
and
(4)
. It remains to prove
(3)
, namely that for fixed
\underline{\varepsilon}
,
\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}:=\Psi(\underline{\varepsilon},\cdot)
is
K
-equivariant and its image is a symplectic submanifold of
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
. As we perturbed
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},B)
in
\mathcal{G}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbits, the image of
\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
still lies in
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
. To prove
K
-equivariance, as in the proof of Lemma
3.2
, it is enough to show that
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},f(b))}
and
F_{\underline{\varepsilon},\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)}
are conjugated by
f
for
f\in K
. By definition,
\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
is a solution to

\mathrm{scal}\left(\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}+d\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b)d\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)\right)\in\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}.

But by
K
-equivariance of
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
, we see that for
f\in K
,

\mathrm{scal}\left(\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},f(b)),\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}+d\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},f(b))df^{*}\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)\right)\in\mathrm{Ad}_{f}(\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}})=\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}},

so that
f^{*}\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)
and
\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},f(b))
both cancel
A(\underline{\varepsilon},f(b),\cdot)
. By restricting the operator
A
to
\mathfrak{k}_{0}^{\perp}\cap\mathcal{U}^{k}
, we can ensure that there is only one solution in applying the implicit function theorem above, so that
f^{*}\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},b)=\phi(\underline{\varepsilon},f(b))
, from which we deduce
K
-equivariance of
\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
. Finally, for fixed
\underline{\varepsilon}
, we had that
\Upsilon(\underline{\varepsilon},B)
was a symplectic submanifold of
\mathcal{J}_{\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
. As this is an open property, this will remain true for
\Psi(\underline{\varepsilon},B)
, up to shrinking
U\times B
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

It is worth noticing that for
\underline{\varepsilon}=0
, we have by construction
\Upsilon(0,\cdot)=\Phi(\cdot)
and then the slice
\Psi_{0}
is the same as Székelyhidi’s in
[
35
]
.

Remark 3.5
.

Our methods for proving Proposition
3.3
also work if we relax the hypothesis
h^{0,2}(X_{0})=0
and
H^{2}(X_{0},TX_{0})=0
. Consider the slice
\Phi
from Proposition
3.1
and fix a point
b\in B
with
(M,\Phi(b))
isomorphic to
X
, the deformed manifold of interest. Fix a class
[\alpha]\in H^{2}(M,\mathbb{R})
that is of type
(1,1)
with respect to
both

X_{0}
and
X
. We will then consider perturbed classes
[\omega_{\varepsilon}]=[\omega]+\varepsilon[\alpha]
for small
\varepsilon
. Assume that there is a submanifold
B_{X,\alpha}\subset B
that contains
0
and the orbit closure of
b
under the
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-action, and such that for any

, the class

is of type
(1,1)
with respect to
(M,\Phi(b^{\prime}))
, with

integrable. Then, we can produce a family of slices from

to

, satisfying conclusions of Proposition
3.3
, for the symplectic forms
\omega_{\varepsilon}
. There are two extreme cases when such

can be found easily. The first is when

and deformations of

4.
Solving the finite dimensional problem

Fix a map
\Psi
as in Proposition
3.3
, and denote

J_{\underline{\varepsilon},b}:=\Psi(\underline{\varepsilon},b).

Consider the pulled back symplectic forms
\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}:=\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}\Omega_{\mathcal{J}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
on
B
. By the properties
(3)
and
(4)
of Proposition
3.3
(see also the statement of Lemma
3.2
), this defines a family of symplectic forms on
B
, with respect to which the
K
-action is hamiltonian. The associated moment maps are given by

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}:&B&\to&\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\\
&b&\mapsto&\mathrm{scal}(J_{\underline{\varepsilon},b})-\hat{s}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\end{array}

for appropriate constants
\hat{s}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, where
\mathrm{scal}(J_{\underline{\varepsilon},b})
stands for
\mathrm{scal}(J_{\underline{\varepsilon},b},\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})
and we have identified
\mathfrak{k}^{*}
with
\mathfrak{k}\simeq\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
through the pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, given, for
(u,v)\in\mathfrak{k}
, by

(4.1)

\langle u,v\rangle_{\underline{\varepsilon}}=\int_{M}H_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}H_{v,\underline{\varepsilon}}\,\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{n}.

We thus reduced the problem to finding zeros of
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
on
B
.

4.1.
Hamiltonians and stabilisers

Note that by definition of the moment map for the
\mathcal{G}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
-action on
\mathcal{J}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, the hamiltonians for the
K
-action on
(B,\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})
are given, for
u\in\mathfrak{k}
, by

(4.2)

h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}(b)=\int_{M}H_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}\,(\mathrm{scal}(J_{\underline{\varepsilon},b})-\hat{s}_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\,\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{n},

where we recall that
H_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}
denotes the hamiltonian with respect to
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
of the vector field
\hat{u}
induced by
u
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)\in U\times B
. If there exists
b^{\prime}
in
(K^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b)\cap B
such that for all
u\in\mathfrak{k}
,

h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})=0,

then the manifold
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
carries a cscK metric.

Proof.

This is simply a reformulation of the previous results. First, the properties of
\Phi
imply
\mathcal{X}_{b}\simeq\mathcal{X}_{b^{\prime}}
for any
b^{\prime}\in(K^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b)\cap B
. Then, by construction of
\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
,
\mathcal{X}_{b}
is isomorphic to
(M,\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime}))
for any
b^{\prime}
in the local complexified orbit of
b
. Thus,
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
carries a cscK metric as soon as there is
b^{\prime}
in its
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit that satisfies
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})=0
. As for all
u\in\mathfrak{k}
one has

h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})=\langle u,\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})\rangle_{\underline{\varepsilon}}

the result follows from non-degeneracy of the Futaki–Mabuchi bilinear form.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We should warn the reader that it is not clear whether the converse holds true. Indeed,
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
could admit a cscK metric corresponding to an almost complex structure away from
\Psi_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(B)
. This was first noticed in
[
25
]
, see also
[
9
]
.

Our goal now is to relate the vanishing of
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, equivalently of the hamiltonians
h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}
, to
K
-polystability. The first observation we make is that the hamiltonians of the stabiliser of a point
b
corresponding to a polystable variety vanish. For each
b\in B
, denote by
K_{b}
(resp.
\mathfrak{k}_{b}
) the stabiliser of
b
under the
K
-action (resp. its Lie algebra).

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\underline{\varepsilon},b)\in U\times B
such that
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
is
K
-polystable. Then, for all
b^{\prime}\in(K^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b)\cap B
, and for all
u\in\mathfrak{k}_{b^{\prime}}
, one has
h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})=0
.

Proof.

If
b^{\prime}\in(K^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b)\cap B
, then
\mathcal{X}_{b}
and
\mathcal{X}_{b^{\prime}}
are isomorphic, so
\mathcal{X}_{b}
is
K
-polystable if and only if
\mathcal{X}_{b^{\prime}}
is. Thus it is enough to show that for all
u\in\mathfrak{k}_{b}
, the hamiltonian
h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}
vanishes at
b
. But
h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}
is the classical Futaki invariant of the holomorphic vector field
\hat{u}
, which therefore vanishes by the polystability assumption.
∎

By Lemmas
4.1
and
4.3
, we see that our problem boils down to finding zeros of the functions
h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}
in a local
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, and for
u
in a complementary space of the stabiliser.

Remark 4.4
.

In fact, one has a similar interpretation for extremal metrics, where the only difference is that one does not require the vanishing of the hamiltonians in
\mathfrak{k}_{b^{\prime}}
. Since the classical Futaki invariant does not vanish, one has to make a particular choice of complementary space in this situation, however, namely the orthogonal complement taken with respect to the inner product determined by
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
.

4.2.
Focusing on an orbit

We start, in this section, to restrict ourselves to a single orbit. We will reduce the problem to a simpler one in several steps, adding some technical assumptions on the way.

From now on, we fix a point
b\in B
.

By gathering results from Székelyhidi and LeBrun–Simanca, we obtain:

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume that the
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
b
is closed in
B
. Then, there is a neighbourhood
U_{b}
of zero in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that for any
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U_{b}
, if
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
is
K
-polystable then
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
carries a cscK metric.

In fact, in the situation of this proposition, it is enough to check for the vanishing of the Futaki invariant to ensure the existence of a cscK metric.

Proof.

If the
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
b
is closed in
B
, by
[
35
]
, we know that
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega])
admits a cscK metric. Then, by the LeBrun–Simanca openness theorem (
[
28
, Theorem A]
), there is a neighbourhood
U_{b}
of zero in
\mathbb{R}^{r}
such that for any
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U_{b}
,
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
carries an extremal metric. As noticed in Lemma
4.3
, if we further assume that
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
is
K
-polystable, then the Futaki invariant of
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}])
vanishes, and the extremal metric is actually cscK.
∎

We will now turn to the case when the
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
b
is not closed. Then, by the Hilbert–Mumford criterion, there is a one parameter subgroup
\rho:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to K^{\mathbb{C}}
such that

\lim_{t\mapsto 0}\rho(t)\cdot b=b_{0}\in B

and
b_{0}
has a closed
K^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit. By
[
35
]
,
(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},[\omega])
admits a cscK metric. Moreover,
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega])
is a small complex polarised deformation of
(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},[\omega])
. Note that
h^{0,2}(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}})=h^{0,2}(\mathcal{X}_{0})
, and that by
[
9
, Corollary 3.2]
,
\Aut_{0}(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},[\omega])\simeq K_{b_{0}}
. In particular, if
K
is abelian, so is
K_{b_{0}}
. Hence,
(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},[\omega])
satisfies the same hypothesis as
(\mathcal{X}_{0},[\omega])
so we might, and we will, up to performing Kuranishi–Fujiki–Schumacher–Székelyhidi’s slice starting from
(\mathcal{X}_{b_{0}},[\omega])
, assume that
b_{0}=0
. We will keep the notations
K
,
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, etc for the group
K_{b_{0}}
, the associated moments maps, etc.

From now on, we assume that
K=T
is a torus.

If
T_{b}
is not trivial, then it is a subtorus of
T
. As elements in
T_{b}
fix all the points in
B\cap\overline{T^{\mathbb{C}}}\cdot b
, and as by Lemma
4.3
the hamiltonians
h_{u,\underline{\varepsilon}}
for
u\in\mathfrak{t}_{b}:=\mathrm{Lie}(T_{b})
vanish if we assume
K
-polystability, elements in
\mathfrak{t}_{b}
will play no role in the arguments that follow. Hence, working instead with the torus
T/T_{b}
, and composing the maps
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
with the orthogonal projection to
\mathfrak{t}_{b}^{\perp_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
, we may, and we will, assume that
T_{b}
is trivial.

The next step is to reduce to a family of moment maps from
B
to
\mathfrak{t}
. Recall that
\mathfrak{k}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}=\mathfrak{t}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
stands for the space of hamiltonians with respect to
\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
for elements in
\mathfrak{t}
. Those are functions, and we can identify

\mathfrak{t}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\simeq\mathfrak{t}

by sending
\theta\in\mathfrak{t}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
to
\overline{\partial}_{0}^{\sharp_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\theta\in\mathfrak{t}
, defined by the equation

\overline{\partial}_{0}\theta=\omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(\overline{\partial}_{0}^{\sharp_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\theta,\cdot).

Using the family of isomorphisms

\overline{\partial}_{0}^{\sharp_{\underline{\varepsilon}}}:\mathfrak{t}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\to\mathfrak{t},

we have a family of moment maps going to the same space:

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}:B\to\mathfrak{t}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\simeq\mathfrak{t}.

For
any
(
\mathrm{Ad}_{T}
-invariant) inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}
, we might see those maps as maps

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}:B\to\mathfrak{t}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\simeq\mathfrak{t}^{*}.

We will see later how to choose this inner product.

We now set
V:=H^{0,1}_{\omega}(T^{1,0})
. There is a weight decomposition under the
T
-action

(4.3)

V:=\bigoplus_{m\in M}V_{m}

for
M\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}
the lattice of characters of
T
. This means that if we decompose
b\in V
accordingly as

b=\sum_{i}b_{m_{i}},

then the action of a one-parameter subgroup generated by
v\in\mathfrak{t}
is given by

(4.4)

t\in\mathbb{C}^{*}\mapsto\sum_{i}t^{\langle m_{i},v\rangle}b_{m_{i}},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the natural duality pairing on
\mathfrak{t}^{*}\times\mathfrak{t}
.
Finally, we will restrict our setting to

B\cap\bigoplus_{b_{m_{i}\neq 0}}V_{m_{i}},

which we still denote by
B
. That is, we only consider weight spaces actually appearing in the decomposition of
b
.

4.3.
A local rigidity result for families of moment maps

We next prove a local rigidity result for the images of a complexified torus orbit under a continuous family of moment maps. Here is the setting of last section. We have :

(
R_{1}
)

An effective and holomorphic action of a compact torus
T
on a complex vector space
V
;

(
R_{2}
)

A continuous family of symplectic forms
(\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}})_{\underline{\varepsilon}\in U}
on a ball
B\subset V
around the origin, with respect to which the
T
-action is hamiltonian;

(
R_{3}
)

A point
b\in B
with trivial stabiliser,
0
in its
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit closure, and such that for all weight
m\in M
appearing in the weight space decomposition of
V
,
b_{m}\neq 0
.

Note that we do not require the forms
\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
to be Kähler with respect to the complex structure on
V
, and in fact this may not be true. However, by construction, the slice
\Psi_{0}
induces an embedding of
B\cap(T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b)
into a
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit inside
\mathcal{J}_{\omega}
in such a way that the
\mathfrak{t}
-directions in the
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit are mapped to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,\mathbb{R})
-directions in the
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, while the
i\mathfrak{t}
-directions are sent to
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(M,i\mathbb{R})
directions. Then, by non-degeneracy of the restriction of Donaldson–Fujiki’s symplectic form to this
\mathcal{G}_{\omega}^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit, we have that:

(
R_{4}
)

The restriction of the symplectic form
\Omega_{0}
to the
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
b
is non-degenerate.

We still denote the equivariant moment mappings
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}:B\to\mathfrak{t}^{*}
, and we set
\mathcal{Z}:=B\cap(T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b)
and
\overline{\mathcal{Z}}:=B\cap(\overline{T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b})
. We also introduce

\sigma:=\sum_{b_{m_{i}}\neq 0}\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}

with
\{m_{i}\}
the weights of the decomposition (
4.3
) under the torus action,
and for
\eta>0

\sigma_{\eta}:=\sum_{b_{m_{i}}\neq 0}[0,\eta)\cdot m_{i}\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}.

The goal of this section is to prove, under the assumptions
(R_{1})-(R_{4})
:

Proposition 4.6
.

Up to shrinking
B
and
U
, there exists
\eta>0
such that for all
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
,

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)+\sigma_{\eta}\subset\mu^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}}).

Note that by the local version of Atiyah and Guillemin–Sternberg convexity theorem, combining the equivariant Darboux Theorem (
[
17
, Theorem 3.2]
) and the local description of linear hamiltonian torus actions (
[
17
, Section 7.1]
), there are
\eta_{\underline{\varepsilon}}>0
and open neighbourhoods
0\in B_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\subset B
such that for all
\underline{\varepsilon}
,

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(B_{\underline{\varepsilon}})=\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)+\sum_{i}[0,\eta_{\underline{\varepsilon}})\cdot m_{i}.

By continuity of
\underline{\varepsilon}\mapsto\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
, we can find a uniform
\eta
(up to shrinking
B
and
U
) so that for all
\underline{\varepsilon}\in U
,

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)+\sum_{i}[0,\eta)\cdot m_{i}\subset\mu^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(B).

Hence, the content of Proposition
4.6
is to preserve this inclusion when restricting to
\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
.

Remark 4.7
.

Note that we cannot use the results in
[
32
, Sections 4 and 6]
directly, as we do not require our forms
\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
to be compatible with the complex structure on
V
. Instead, we use a more direct argument, that requires an explicit knowledge of the
T
-action. This is the main reason for our hypothesis
K=T
. We expect however that a generalisation of Proposition
4.6
should hold for general compact connected groups
K
, considering the intersection of the images of the moment maps with a fixed positive Weyl chamber, and replacing
\sigma
with an appropriate cone, as in
[
32
, Theorem 6.5]
.

We start with the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

The cone
\sigma\subset\mathfrak{t}^{*}
is a strongly convex rational polyhedral cone of maximal dimension
\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{t}^{*})
. Hence, so is its dual cone
\sigma^{\vee}
.

Recall that by definition,

\sigma^{\vee}=\{v\in\mathfrak{t}\,|\>\forall m\in\sigma,\;\langle m,v\rangle\geq 0\}.

Proof.

The cone
\sigma
is a rational polyhedral cone by definition. As
0\in\overline{T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b}
, and as the action of
t\in T^{\mathbb{C}}
on
b
is given by

(4.5)

\sum_{i}t^{\langle m_{i},\cdot\rangle}b_{m_{i}},

we know that there is
v\in\mathfrak{t}
such that the action of
v
sends
b
to
0
. That is, there is
v\in\mathfrak{t}
such that for all
i
,

\langle m_{i},v\rangle>0.

But then
\sigma
cannot contain any line, as any point
x\in\sigma\cap(-\sigma)
would give
\langle x,v\rangle>0
and
\langle-x,v\rangle>0
, which is absurd. Finally,
\mathrm{dim}(\sigma)=\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{t}^{*})
follows from the fact that any element
v\in\sigma^{\perp}
belongs to the stabiliser of
b
by the description of the
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-action at
b\in B
. As
b
is supposed to have trivial stabiliser, we deduce that
\mathrm{dim}(\sigma^{\perp})=0
, and thus
\mathrm{dim}(\sigma)=\mathrm{dim}(\mathfrak{t}^{*})
by duality. The statement about
\sigma^{\vee}
follows from general theory of polyhedral cones, see e.g.
[
30
, Appendix]
.
∎

The previous lemma has several interesting consequences. First, there is a finite set of rational elements
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\}\in\mathfrak{t}^{\ell}
such that

\sigma^{\vee}=\sum_{j}\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot v_{j}.

Then, the facets of
\sigma
(resp.
\sigma^{\vee}
) are of the form
\sigma\cap H_{v_{i}}
(resp.
\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}
), for some of the
m_{i}
’s (resp.
v_{i}
’s), where we set
H_{v}=\{m\in\mathfrak{t}^{*},\>\langle m,v\rangle=0\}
(resp.
H_{m}=\{v\in\mathfrak{t},\>\langle m,v\rangle=0\}
). In general, a face of
\sigma^{\vee}
is of the form

(\cap_{i}H_{m_{i}})\cap\sigma^{\vee}

for some
m_{i}
’s. Finally, note by construction that the rays
\rho\in\sigma(1)
(that is
1
-dimensional faces of
\sigma
) are all of the form
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}
, for the
m_{i}
’s such that
\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}
is a facet of
\sigma^{\vee}
.

Proof of Proposition
4.6
.

First, recall from
[
17
, Theorem 3.2 and Section 7.1]
that
B
is chosen so that
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
is given by

(4.6)

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime})=\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)+\sum_{i}||b_{i}^{\prime}||^{2}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}\cdot m_{i},

for some norm
||\cdot||_{\underline{\varepsilon}}
that might depend on
{\underline{\varepsilon}}
. From Equation (
4.5
), this implies in particular that for all
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
,
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime})\in\mathrm{Int}(\sigma)
, the interior of
\sigma
. Similarly, if
b^{\prime}
is in the boundary of
\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
, then its image
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime})
is in the boundary of
\sigma
. To see this, let
b^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
be a boundary point, so that there is
v\in\mathfrak{t}
such that

b^{\prime}=\lim_{t\mapsto 0}\sum_{i}t^{\langle m_{i},v\rangle}b_{m_{i}}.

As the limit exists,
v\in\sigma^{\vee}
. Then

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime})=\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)+\sum_{\langle v,m_{i}\rangle=0}||b_{m_{i}}||_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{2}\cdot m_{i}.

This is a boundary point of
\sigma
, unless
\{m_{i},\>\langle m_{i},v\rangle=0\}
spans
\mathrm{Vect}(\sigma)=\mathfrak{t}^{*}
. This is impossible, as this would imply
v\in(\mathfrak{t}^{*})^{\perp}=\{0\}
.

The proof is now done in
3
steps.

Step 1 : The restriction of
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
to
\mathcal{Z}
is a submersion.
Let
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}
. From the fact that
b^{\prime}
has trivial stabiliser we deduce that the map

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathfrak{t}\oplus i\mathfrak{t}&\to&T_{b^{\prime}}\mathcal{Z}\\
v+iw&\mapsto&\check{v}(b^{\prime})+i\check{w}(b^{\prime})\end{array}

is an isomorphism, where for
v\in\mathfrak{t}
,
\check{v}
denotes the associated vector field on
B
. We then identify

T_{b^{\prime}}\mathcal{Z}\simeq\mathfrak{t}\oplus i\mathfrak{t}

and

T_{\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime})}\mathfrak{t}^{*}\simeq\mathfrak{t}^{*},

so that the differential of
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
restricted to the complexified orbit at
b^{\prime}
is a map

d\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime}):\mathfrak{t}\oplus i\mathfrak{t}\to\mathfrak{t}^{*}

given by

d\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(b^{\prime})(v+iw)=\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})((\check{v}+i\check{w})(b^{\prime}),\cdot).

From (
R_{4}
), the form
\Omega_{0}(b^{\prime})
is non-degenerate on
\{\check{v}+i\check{w}(b^{\prime}),\;v+iw\in\mathfrak{t}\oplus i\mathfrak{t}\}
. Up to shrinking
U
, we can assume that
\Omega_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(b^{\prime})(\cdot,\cdot)
is non-degenerate on this space as well, so that
d\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
has maximal rank on
\mathcal{Z}
. Then,
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
restricted to
\mathcal{Z}
is a submersion, and thus in particular is an open map.

Step 2 : Each ray
\rho\in\sigma(1)
intersects
\mu^{*}_{\underline{\varepsilon}}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)
.
Indeed, such a ray
\rho
can be written
\rho=\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}
with
H_{m_{i}}\cap\sigma^{\vee}
a facet of
\sigma^{\vee}
. Pick now
v\in H_{m_{i}}\cap\sigma^{\vee}
such that
v
doesn’t belong to any of the proper faces of
H_{m_{i}}\cap\sigma^{\vee}
. Then, by the choice of
v
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cccc}\langle m_{i},v\rangle&=&0,&\\
\langle m_{j},v\rangle&\geq&0,&j\neq i.\end{array}\right.

Assume there is a
j\neq i
such that
\langle m_{j},v\rangle=0
. Then
v\in\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}\cap H_{m_{j}}
. As we assumed that
v
was not in a proper face of
\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}
, necessarily
\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}\cap H_{m_{j}}=\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}
. As
\sigma^{\vee}
is maximal dimensional, and as
\sigma^{\vee}\cap H_{m_{i}}
is a facet of
\sigma^{\vee}
(because
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}
is a ray),
m_{j}
must be a positive multiple of
m_{i}
. Hence,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\langle m_{j},v\rangle&=&0&\textrm{ if }&m_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}\\
\langle m_{j},v\rangle&>&0&\textrm{ if }&m_{j}\notin\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}.\end{array}\right.

From the action (
4.5
) of
T^{\mathbb{C}}
on
B
at
b
, we have that the limit point under the action generated by
v
starting from
b
is

\lim_{t\mapsto 0}\sum_{i}t^{\langle m_{i},v\rangle}b_{m_{i}}=\sum_{m_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}}b_{m_{j}}\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}}.

But from (
4.6
) the image of that point by
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}
is of the form

\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)+\sum_{m_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}}\lambda_{j}m_{j}=\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)+\lambda m_{i}

for positive
\lambda_{j}
’s and
\lambda>0
. This proves the claim of Step
2
.

Step 3 : Conclusion of the proof.
From Step
2
,
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})
contains an element of the form
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)+\lambda_{i}m_{i}
,
\lambda_{i}>0
, for each ray
\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}
of
\sigma
. It also contains
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)
as
0\in\overline{T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b}
. Denote by
\Delta
the convex hull of
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)
and the points
(\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)+\lambda_{i}m_{i})_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot m_{i}\in\sigma(1)}
. It is enough to show that
\Delta\subset\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})
to conclude, as for
\eta
small enough,

\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(0)+\sigma_{\eta}\subset\Delta
.

To prove that
\Delta\subset\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})
, it is enough, by convexity, to prove that for any
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\Delta\cap\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})
, the segment
[x_{1},x_{2}]
lies in
\Delta\cap\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(\overline{\mathcal{Z}})
. This can be done by showing that

\{t\in[0,1],\>tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{2}\in\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}(\mathcal{Z})\}

is open and closed. Step
1
implies that this set open. As
\Delta
is closed, there is a closed ball
D
such that
(\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*})^{-1}(\Delta)\subset D\subset B
. Note that for any sequence
(b_{i})\in T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b\cap D
, we can extract a converging subsequence with limit in
\overline{T^{\mathbb{C}}\cdot b}\cap D\subset\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
. A classical argument using extractions of convergent subsequences and continuity of
\mu_{\underline{\varepsilon}}^{*}
then implies the desired closedness.
∎

4.4.
Solving the problem

We can now conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
. We restrict to variations in a single direction in the Kähler cone. Let
\alpha=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\alpha_{i}\in\mathcal{H}^{1,1}(X_{0},\mathbb{R})
. We set
\omega_{\varepsilon}=\omega+\varepsilon\alpha
. There is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for
\varepsilon\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
,
(\varepsilon\lambda_{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}\in U
. We denote by a subscript
\varepsilon
all quantities depending on
\underline{\varepsilon}:=(\varepsilon\lambda_{i})_{1\leq i\leq r}
. Our goal is to prove that up to shrinking
\varepsilon_{0}
, for all
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
, if
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
is
K
-polystable, then it carries a cscK metric.

From the previous sections, to produce a cscK metric, it is enough to find
b^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
such that
\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(b^{\prime})=0
. Indeed, if such a
b^{\prime}
is in
\mathcal{Z}
, then
(M,J_{\varepsilon,b^{\prime}},\omega_{\varepsilon})
is cscK and isomorphic to
\mathcal{X}_{b}
by the properties of the slices in Lemma
3.2
and Proposition
3.3
. If
b^{\prime}
is in the boundary of
\overline{\mathcal{Z}}
, then
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
is a small polarised deformation of
(\mathcal{X}_{b^{\prime}},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
. As the latter is cscK, if the former is
K
-polystable, we can conclude from
[
35
, Theorem 2]
that
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
carries a cscK metric.

We fix
\eta
as in Proposition
4.6
. Then, it is is enough to prove that
K
-polystability implies
0\in\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)+\sigma_{\eta}
to complete the proof. But this is equivalent to

-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)\in\sigma_{\eta}.

Up to shrinking
\varepsilon_{0}
, and as
\mu_{0}^{*}(0)=0
, we only need to prove that
K
-polystability implies

-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)\in\sigma.

By the duality Theorem (
[
30
, Appendix, Theorem A.1]
), we have

\sigma^{\vee\vee}=\sigma,

where
\sigma^{\vee}=\sum_{j}\mathbb{R}_{+}\cdot v_{j}
is the dual of
\sigma
, for some finite set of rational elements
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{\ell}\}\in\mathfrak{t}^{\ell}
. Hence, we need to show that
K
-polystability implies

-\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(0)^{*},v_{j}\rangle\geq 0,

for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}.
But any such
v_{j}
lies in
\sigma^{\vee}
, and thus satisfies
\langle m_{i},v_{j}\rangle\geq 0
for all
m_{i}
such that
b_{m_{i}}\neq 0
. By Equation (
4.4
), each
v_{j}
then generates a
1
-parameter subgroup
\rho_{j}:\mathbb{C}^{*}\to T^{\mathbb{C}}
with

\lim_{t\mapsto 0}\rho_{j}(t)\cdot b=b_{j}\in\overline{\mathcal{Z}}.

From
[
35
, proof of Theorem 2]
, one can produce out of
\rho_{j}
a smooth test configuration
(\mathcal{X}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j,\varepsilon})
for
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
with Futaki invariant given by

\mathrm{DF}(\mathcal{X}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j,\varepsilon})=-\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(b_{j}),H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon},

where recall
H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}
is the hamiltonian of
v_{j}\in\mathfrak{t}
with respect to
\omega_{\varepsilon}
. If
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
is
K
-polystable, then we must have

\mathrm{DF}(\mathcal{X}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j,\varepsilon})\geq 0.

Moreover, the quantity
\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(b_{j}),H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
is the Futaki invariant of
v_{j}
that belongs to the stabiliser
\mathfrak{t}_{b_{j}}
. Hence, from the independence of the Futaki invariant on the metric in a fixed Kähler class (
[
20
]
), the map
b^{\prime}\mapsto\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(b^{\prime}),H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
is invariant along the
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit of
b_{j}
. Note that
0
belongs to the
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-orbit closure of
b_{j}
(we can use the same
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action that sends
b
to
0
to send any
b_{j}
to
0
, as shown by Equation (
4.4
)). Thus, from these two observations, we see that

\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(0),H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}=-\mathrm{DF}(\mathcal{X}_{j},\mathcal{A}_{j,\varepsilon})\leq 0.

Remember that we had some flexibility on the choice of inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathfrak{t}}
in the end of Section
4.2
. We fix it to be
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0}
, the Futaki-Mabuchi pairing with respect to
\omega_{0}
, using the isomorphism
\overline{\partial}_{0}^{\sharp_{0}}:\mathfrak{t}_{0}\simeq\mathfrak{t}
. Unravelling the various isomorphisms, we then see that

\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0),v_{j}\rangle=\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},0},H_{v_{j},0}\rangle_{0},

where
H_{w_{\varepsilon},0}
is the hamiltonian, with respect to
\omega_{0}
, of the vector field
\overline{\partial}_{0}^{\sharp_{\varepsilon}}\mu_{\varepsilon}(0)\in\mathfrak{t}
. By definition,
H_{w_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon}=\mu_{\varepsilon}(0)
, and thus

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(0),H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}&=&\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},\varepsilon},H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}\\
&=&\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},0},H_{v_{j},0}\rangle_{0}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{q+1})\end{array}

where
\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},0},H_{v_{j},0}\rangle_{0}
admits an expansion of some order
q
in
\varepsilon
. Note that those expansions exist, as the map
\Psi
from Proposition
3.3
is smooth in its variables. Then, for
\varepsilon
small enough, the sign of
\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}(0),H_{v_{j},\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
will be given by the sign of
\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},0},H_{v_{j},0}\rangle_{0}
, if the latter is not zero. But then,
K
-polystability excludes
\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},0},H_{v_{j},0}\rangle_{0}>0
. Hence

\langle\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0),v_{j}\rangle=\langle H_{w_{\varepsilon},0},H_{v_{j},0}\rangle_{0}\leq 0,

and
-\mu_{\varepsilon}^{*}(0)\in\sigma^{\vee\vee}=\sigma
, which concludes the proof.

4.5.
Futaki invariant and wall-crossing

In this section, we describe the local wall-crossing phenomena alluded to in the introduction. First, from the previous proof, it follows that for
\varepsilon
small enough,
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
is
K
-polystable if and only if
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(\mathcal{X}_{b},\cdot)=0
and for
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, one has
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{j})>0
, where
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X,\cdot)
denotes the classical Futaki invariant of
(X,[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
. By invariance and continuity of the Futaki invariant, this is equivalent to

(4.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v})&=&0&\mathrm{for}&v\in\mathfrak{t}_{b},\\
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v})&>&0&\mathrm{for}&v\in\sigma^{\vee}.\end{array}\right.

While
K
-polystability immediately implies those conditions, the converse follows as those conditions implies the existence of a cscK metric from our proof of Theorem
1.1
, which then ensures
K
-polystability (
[
33
,
7
]
).

There are several interesting consequences from such a characterisation of
K
-polystability. First, observe that conditions
(R_{1})-(R_{4})
required for Proposition
4.6
are satisfied for
any
point
b^{\prime}=\sum_{m_{i}}b^{\prime}_{m_{i}}
with same non-vanishing coordinates
b^{\prime}_{m_{i}}
as
b
in the weight space decomposition. Hence, the result of Proposition
4.6
holds true uniformly in
b
for any compact family of such points. Since
T
is a torus, the stabilisers
\mathfrak{t}_{b^{\prime}}
of those points are all equal to
\mathfrak{t}_{b}
(this is simply
(\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{m_{i},\;b_{m_{i}}\neq 0\})^{\perp}\subset\mathfrak{t}
), and our proof works uniformly in
b
for
b
varying in a compact set
S
of the above kind. More precisely, for such a compact family of polarised manifolds
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])_{b\in S}
, there is
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
,
K
-polystability of
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
implies the existence of a cscK metric and is equivalent to the System (
4.7
). But conditions (
4.7
) are independent of
b
in this compact family. Hence, we deduce that if
one
of the
(\mathcal{X}_{b},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
is
K
-polystable for
b\in S
, then
all
of them are. For
\mathfrak{t}_{b}=\{0\}
, this is simply openness of
K
-stability, and is implied by openness of the existence of a cscK metric when the reduced automorphism group is trivial.

Another interesting phenomenon is wall-crossing for those families. Assume that the system (
4.7
) holds true for
\varepsilon>0
, and that some of the
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{j})
change sign with
\varepsilon
. Then we see that for
\varepsilon>0
, the quotient of
S
by the
T^{\mathbb{C}}
-action will correspond to points in the associated cscK moduli, while for
\varepsilon=0
, this quotient should appear in the boundary of the moduli space, to finally disappear from it for negative
\varepsilon
. We illustrate this in the next section.

5.
An example

Consider the toric surface
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
with torus action

\begin{array}[]{ccc}(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}&\to&\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}\\
(\alpha,\beta),([x_{1},y_{1}],[x_{2},y_{2}])&\mapsto&([\alpha x_{1},y_{1}],[\beta x_{2},y_{2}]).\end{array}

Denote by
\check{\omega}
the product of the Fubini–Study metrics on each factor
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. Let
\pi:X_{0}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
be the blow-up at the four torus fixed points. By the work of Arezzo–Pacard (
[
1
,
2
]
), we know that for small
\delta>0
,
X_{0}
carries a cscK metric in the class

[\omega_{0}]:=[\pi^{*}\check{\omega}]-\delta(E_{0,0}+E_{\infty,0}+E_{0,\infty}+E_{\infty,\infty}),

wher
E_{i,j}
is (the Poincaré dual of) the exceptional divisor associated to the blow-up of the point
(i,j)\in\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

From
[
31
]
, following works of Ilten and Vollmert
[
24
]
, we know that the reduced automorphism group of
X_{0}
is isomorphic to the
2
-dimensional torus, acting on
H^{1}(X_{0},T^{1,0})\simeq H^{0,1}_{\omega_{0}}(X_{0},T^{1,0})\simeq\mathbb{C}^{4}
through the representation

\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\alpha&0&0&0\\
0&\alpha^{-1}&0&0\\
0&0&\beta&0\\
0&0&0&\beta^{-1}\end{array}\right],

where the choice of coordinates
b=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{4})\in\mathbb{C}^{4}
is such that the complex deformation associated to
b\in B
, for
B
a small neighbourhood of the origin, corresponds to the blow-up of
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}
along the points
([b_{1}:1],[b_{3}:1])
,
([1:b_{2}],[1:b_{4}])
,
(0,\infty)
and
(\infty,0)
. As
X_{0}
is toric,
h^{0,2}(X_{0})=0
(
[
30
, Section 3.3]
), and we can consider the polarised complex deformation
(X,[\omega_{0}])
corresponding to the point
b=(0,0,b_{3},0)
, for small
b_{3}
. Clearly,
(X,[\omega_{0}])
is analytically
K
-semistable, and degenerates to
(X_{0},[\omega_{0}])
via
\beta\mapsto(0,0,\beta\,b_{3},0)
. The stabiliser of
b
in the torus is the first factor
\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\{1\}
.

Consider the class

[\omega_{\varepsilon}]:=[\omega_{0}]-\varepsilon(E_{0,\infty}+E_{\infty,\infty}).

From our construction, it follows that
(X,[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
will admit a cscK metric for all
|\varepsilon|\ll 1
if and only if the following two conditions are satisfied:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{\alpha})&=&0,\\
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{\beta})&>&0,\end{array}\right.

where
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}
stands for the Futaki invariant,
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
is a generator for the
\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\{1\}
-action and
\hat{v}_{\beta}
is a generator for the
\{1\}\times\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action. We can compute those invariants by using the polytope description of
(X_{0},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
, pictured in
[
6
, Figure 1 page 468]
, for
\varepsilon>0
.

The vanishing of
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{\alpha})
follows from the symmetry along the
x
-axis, as the affine function corresponding to the hamiltonian of
\hat{v}_{\alpha}
is, up to scale, given by
(x,y)\mapsto x.
On the other hand, a direct computation, using the toric description of the Futaki invariant (
[
14
, Lemma 3.2.9]
), shows that
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{\beta})<0
for
\varepsilon>0
and
\mathrm{Fut}_{[\omega_{\varepsilon}]}(X_{0},\hat{v}_{\beta})>0
for
\varepsilon<0
. This provides an example of wall crossing for
(X,[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
that is cscK for
\varepsilon<0
, analytically
K
-semistable for
\varepsilon=0
and
K
-unstable for
\varepsilon>0
small enough. On the other hand,
(X_{0},[\omega_{\varepsilon}])
is cscK precisely at
\varepsilon=0
.
