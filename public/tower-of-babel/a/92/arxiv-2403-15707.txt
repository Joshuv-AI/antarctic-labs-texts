Role of Locality and Weight Sharing in Image-Based Tasks: A Sample Complexity Separation between CNNs, LCNs, and FCNs
Aakash Lahoti, Stefani Karp, Ezra Winston, Aarti Singh & Yuanzhi Li

Abstract
Vision tasks are characterized by the properties of locality and translation invariance. The superior performance of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) on these tasks is widely attributed to the inductive bias of locality and weight sharing baked into their architecture. Existing attempts to quantify the statistical benefits of these biases in CNNs over locally connected convolutional neural networks (LCNs) and fully connected neural networks (FCNs) fall into one of the following categories: either they disregard the optimizer and only provide uniform convergence upper bounds with no separating lower bounds, or they consider simplistic tasks that do not truly mirror the locality and translation invariance as found in real-world vision tasks. To address these deficiencies, we introduce the Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD) classification task that models an image as consisting of k k patches, each of dimension d d , and the label is determined by a d d -sparse signal vector that can freely appear in any one of the k k patches. On this task, for any orthogonally equivariant algorithm like gradient descent, we prove that CNNs require O ~ ​ ( k + d ) \tilde{O}(k+d) samples, whereas LCNs require Ω ⁡ ( k ​ d ) \Omega(kd) samples, establishing the statistical advantages of weight sharing in translation invariant tasks. Furthermore, LCNs need O ~ ​ ( k ​ ( k + d ) ) \tilde{O}(k(k+d)) samples, compared to Ω ⁡ ( k 2 ​ d ) \Omega(k^{2}d) samples for FCNs, showcasing the benefits of locality in local tasks. Additionally, we develop information theoretic tools for analyzing randomized algorithms, which may be of interest for statistical research.

Introduction

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) exhibit state-of-the-art performance across computer vision tasks, including Image Classification, Object Detection, and Out of Distribution Detection (
Liu et al. 2022
;
Fang et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2022
).
This efficacy is commonly attributed to the biases of locality and weight sharing encoded into CNNs’ short convolutions.
The rationale is that these biases align with the properties of vision tasks, where local and mobile signals determine the output (
Gens & Domingos 2014
;
Marcus 2018
). In contrast, Locally Connected Neural Networks (LCNs) encode only locality, while Fully Connected Neural Networks (FCNs) encode neither locality nor weight sharing, thus resulting in a larger sample complexity compared to CNNs.

Previous works have attempted to quantify the statistical benefit of these architectural biases in CNNs.
For example,
Vardi et al. 2022
,
Du et al. 2018
and
Long & Sedghi 2020
derived Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) bounds for CNNs which are tighter than that for FCNs.
However, they do not
provide separating lower bounds for FCNs on the same task, and cannot rule out the possibility that FCNs can adaptively yield better bounds when the input satisfies locality and translation invariance.
In fact, as noted in
Li et al. 2021
, without taking the training algorithm into consideration, standard lower bound techniques cannot be used to show a separation between the three models.
This is because an algorithm can simulate CNNs and LCNs within FCNs. Thus, if the algorithm is unconstrained, the minimax lower bound for FCNs cannot be greater than any upper bound for CNNs or LCNs.

Recently,
Li et al. 2021
established a sample complexity separation between CNNs and FCNs that were trained on the restricted class of equivariant algorithms like gradient descent

Wang & Wu 2023
further extended this line of work to show a separation between FCNs, LCNs and CNNs.
However, the data models employed in these works are not truly reflective of the locality and translation invariance of vision tasks.
Typically in such tasks, the output is determined by some local pattern, also known as “signal”.
For example, a cat within images labeled “cat”.
Often, this signal is embedded within uninformative background, also known as “noise”, and can freely translate within the image, i.e. it can appear in any patch within the image, without changing the label (as illustrated in figure

In contrast, in both
Li et al. 2021
and
Wang & Wu 2023
, the data model considered is as follows: the input
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{4d})

Both of these data models fail to capture the aforementioned desiderata of a model for a vision task.
Additionally, they lack the requisite structure to demonstrate how sample complexity varies with the
“degree” of locality and translation invariance within the input, or establish conditions on the input under which the differences between CNNs, LCNs, and FCNs are more pronounced.

Furthermore, it is worth noting that the driving force for their separation results is the interaction between two halves of the input.
Specifically, their lower bound selects “hard instances” from the class of functions
{\mathcal{H}}=\{{\bm{x}}_{1:d}^{\top}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}_{d+1:2d}\}
, where
{\mathbf{U}}
is a
d\times d
orthonormal matrix, learning which results in a lower bound of
\Omega(d^{2})
.
While the interaction between the patches is an interesting phenomenon, it is not the primary characteristic of locality and translation invariance found in images.

We introduce the Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD) task, which is inspired by the setting in
Karp et al. 2021
, as our data model for vision tasks.
The input
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{kd}
is comprised of
k
consecutive patches, each of dimension
d
.
From amongst these
k
patches, one of them is randomly filled with a noisy signed signal.
The remaining patches are filled with isotropic Gaussian noise of variance
\sigma^{2}
.
The binary label is set as the sign of the signal, so that all images with the same signal in any one of the patches have the same label.
By encapsulating concepts of signal, noise, locality, and translation invariance, the DSD task offers a higher fidelity to the complexities found in real-world vision tasks.

On this task, we establish a sample complexity separation of
\Omega(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d)
vs
\tilde{O}(\sigma^{2}k(k+d))
samples between FCNs and LCNs, as well as a separation of
\Omega(\sigma^{2}kd)
vs
\tilde{O}(\sigma^{2}(k+d))
samples between LCNs and CNNs.
Our analysis indicates that due to no architectural biases, FCNs incur a multiplicative cost factor of
k
for each of the two reasons: identifying the location of the
k
patches, and learning the signal vector for each patch. The factor of
d
arises due to learning the signal which is
d
dimensional.
For LCNs, we can eliminate the
k
cost for identification of the patches since the location of all the patches is baked into the architecture.
Finally for CNNs both these costs are removed as the architecture not only localizes all the patches, it also allows the signal to be jointly learnt across all patches via weight sharing.
It is noteworthy that both the LCN and the CNN upper bound feature a
k+d
factor instead of the expected factor of
d
. This is an artifact of the gradient descent analysis, and is suggestive of being a potential cost for the algorithmic efficiency of gradient descent.

Our approach diverges from
Li et al. 2021
;
Wang & Wu 2023
, because in our task, the marginal over the input
is not a
\mathbf{0}
-mean Gaussian, but a mixture of
k
Gaussians, which is not an orthogonally invariant distribution.
As a consequence, for deriving lower bounds, we cannot apply the Benedek-Itai bound from
Benedek & Itai 1991
as done in
Li et al. 2021
, nor can we directly use Fano’s Theorem as done in
Wang & Wu 2023
owing to the absence of the semi-metricness of
l_{2}
loss under an invariant distribution, and analyzing the expected risk under a mixture of Gaussians is analytically difficult.
Instead, we utilize a novel technique that leverages the randomness of the training algorithm to break the original minimax lower-bound problem into
k
simpler problems using a simulation-style argument.
In case of FCNs, we prove sample complexity lower bounds for the
k
the simpler problems using a novel boosting technique to derive a reduction to the Gaussian mean estimation problem on the unit sphere.
To prove sample complexity lower bounds for the simpler problems in the case of LCNs, we prove a variant of Fano’s Theorem that can be used for randomized algorithms.
Distinctively, our variant does not require the semi-metric property to hold on the entire space of output functions, as is needed in the "Fano’s Theorem for Random Estimators" developed in
Wang & Wu 2023
.

Our sample complexity upper bounds depend on the analysis of an equivariant gradient descent style algorithm on LCNs and CNNs. This is unlike the separation proved in
Wang & Wu 2023
, where they use covering number-based arguments for ERM analysis.
The advantage of doing a gradient descent analysis over an ERM analysis is two fold:
First, it demonstrates a sample complexity separation for computationally-efficient (poly time) equivariant algorithms. This distinction is crucial because while a separation may exist for computationally inefficient algorithms, the separation might disappear under constraints of computational efficiency.
Second, for a valid separation, it is important to ensure that both the upper and lower bounds are derived for equivariant algorithms since non-equivariant algorithms could potentially be more sample-efficient than their equivariant counterparts.
Furthermore, our approach differs significantly from
Karp et al. 2021
, which analyzes population gradients by assuming enough (
\text{poly}(k,d)
) samples at each iteration to yield a representational gap between CNNs and CNTKs (Convolutional Neural Tangent Kernels).
Since we are interested in sample complexity separation, we adopt a more direct analysis of empirical gradients.

Our Setting

We introduce the Dynamic Signal Distribution, an image-like task which is inspired from
Karp et al. 2021
. We also specify the FCN, LCN, and CNN architectures that we consider for our analysis.

4.1
Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD)

In many vision-based tasks, the output often relies on a local "signal" in the image, a property referred to as locality. Often, this signal is enveloped in random noise, and satisfies translation invariance, that is its movement within the image does not alter the output. The DSD task is designed to capture the both locality and translation invariance properties into an analyzable task.

We define the input space as
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{kd}
and the output space as
{\mathcal{Y}}={\mathbb{R}}
. Any input vector
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}
is structured as
({\bm{x}}^{(1)},..,{\bm{x}}^{(k)})

{\bm{x}}^{(i)}
being a vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

i^{\text{th}}
patch of
{\bm{x}}

k
consecutive patches of dimension
d
.
To model the local signal, we employ an unknown unit vector
{\bm{w}}^{\star}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, with
\lVert{\bm{w}}^{\star}\rVert=1
.
To include translation invariance in the task, this signal
{\bm{w}}^{\star}
can reside within any one of the
k
patch locations, described above. Specifically, for each
i

{\bm{\mu}}_{i}[(i-1)d+1:id]={\bm{w}}^{\star}

The noise is chosen to be isotropic Gaussian, with variance
\sigma^{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

5.2
Equivariant Algorithms

We introduce the concept of equivariant algorithms, originally presented in
Li et al. 2021
. To keep it concise, we provide a simplified version which is sufficient for our purposes.

To motivate the definition of equivariant algorithms, we review the following thought experiment. Consider a neural network parameterized as
f({\mathbf{A}}{\bm{x}},{\bm{b}})
, where
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q\times p}
is the parameter of the first linear layer, while
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q}
encapsulates the remaining parameters.
We initialize the parameters as
({\mathbf{A}}^{0},{\bm{b}}^{0})
and use gradient descent, with learning rate
\eta
, to train the network on the dataset
\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n}
. In parallel, we train another network initialized as
({\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\mathbf{U}}^{T},{\bm{b}}^{0})
, with the dataset
\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n}
. Here,
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{O}}(p)
such that
{\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}
and
{\mathbf{A}}^{0}
are identically distributed.

Observe that at the first iteration, the output of the first hidden layer for both networks is the same,
{\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}={\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\bm{x}}
. This implies that the gradients with respect to the pre-activations of the first layer are also equal. Consequently, the gradients with respect to the matrix parameters satisfy the relation,
\tfrac{d}{d{\mathbf{A}}^{0}}\text{loss}({\mathbf{A}}^{0}){\mathbf{U}}^{T}=\tfrac{d}{d{\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}}\text{loss}({\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\mathbf{U}}^{T})\coloneqq\Delta{\mathbf{U}}^{T}
. Thus, after the first iteration, the parameter sets for the two neural networks are,

\displaystyle({\mathbf{A}}^{1},{\bm{b}}^{1})=({\mathbf{A}}^{0}-\eta\Delta,{\bm{b}}^{1}),\hskip 28.45274pt({\mathbf{A}}^{1}{\mathbf{U}}^{T},{\bm{b}}^{1})=({\mathbf{A}}^{0}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}-\eta\Delta{\mathbf{U}}^{T},{\bm{b}}^{1}),

(9)

respectively. By induction, this property is preserved across all iterations

and

The key idea is that the risk of a network parameterized as

on any data

is the same as its counterpart with parameters

on the transformed data

.
Now since

and
{\mathbf{A}}^{0}
have the same distribution, we can infer that the expected risk of this network trained with gradient descent is invariant to the transformation

of the input distribution.
In other words, the network learns the original distribution and the transformed distribution equally well. Formally,

We present the minimax framework by closely following the notation established in

We now present our Fano’s Theorem for Randomized Algorithms to lower bound the minimax risk

FCNs vs LCNs Separation Results

We now present the separation result between FCNs and LCNs, along with an outline of the proof. Specifically, we establish that FCNs, when trained with any equivariant algorithm, require
\Omega(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d)
samples to learn DSD upto some constant risk
\delta
.
Conversely, there exists an equivariant algorithm that can train LCNs with
\tilde{O}(\sigma^{2}k(k+d))
samples, to achieve a risk less than
\delta
.

Theorem 6.1

(Sketched)
.

Consider the group
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{O}}(kd)
, then any
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant algorithm that is used to train FCNs, requires
\Omega(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d)
samples to achieve some constant risk
\delta
.

Proof.

We justify the choice of
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{O}}(kd)
, in light of the intuition for equivariance presented in section
5.2
.
Note that the parameter of the first layer of FCNs,
{\mathbf{A}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k\times kd}
, is given by
({\bm{w}}_{1};\ldots;{\bm{w}}_{k})
.
We establish equivariance if, for every transformation
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
,
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}
corresponds to a valid FCN, and if there exists an initialization such that

{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}
and
{\mathbf{A}}
are identically distributed.
Indeed,
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}=({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{w}}_{1};\ldots;{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{w}}_{k})
, corresponds to a FCN with the parameter vectors
{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{w}}_{k}
.
And, if each
{\bm{w}}_{i}
is initialized as
{\bm{w}}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{I}}_{kd})
, then
{\mathbf{A}}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}
and
{\mathbf{A}}
share the same distribution.

Our proof proceeds in two steps.
First, we establish that learning
{\mathbf{U}}\circ\text{DSD}
with
m
samples requires learning
k
"nearly independent" subtasks,
\{{\mathbf{U}}\circ\text{SSD}_{t}\}_{k}
, with
m/k
samples each. The underlying rationale of this result is that learning
{\mathbf{U}}\circ\text{DSD}
entails recovering each mean vector
\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\}_{k}
.
Note that these mean vectors are pair-wise orthogonal,
({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i})^{T}({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{j})={\bm{\mu}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{\mu}}_{j}=0
.
Therefore, even with the knowledge of
\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\}_{t\neq i}
, the only information we have about
{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}
is the
kd-k+1\simeq kd
dimensional subspace in which it lies. Thus, to learn DSD, we have to recover all the means vectors,
\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}\}_{k}
, "nearly independently" from each other.

In the second step, we reduce the problem of learning
\text{SSD}_{t}
, into a problem of Gaussian mean estimation.
For this, we show that if there exists an algorithm that learns
\text{SSD}_{t}
, then
we can extract a weakly aligned mean estimate of
{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}
from the FCN returned by the algorithm.
We propose a scheme that reliably boosts this estimate, to generate a strongly aligned mean estimate.
This is necessary because standard information theoretic tools do not work with weakly aligned mean estimates.
We then bound the sample complexity for any algorithm that is able to return a strongly aligned Gaussian mean estimate using our Fano’s Theorem for Randomized Estimators
5.1
as
m/k=\Omega(\sigma^{2}kd)
. This implies that
m=\Omega(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d)
, proving the result.

8
Conclusion And Future Work

In this paper, we established a sample complexity separation between FCNs, LCNs, and CNNs that are trained using equivariant algorithms on the Dynamic Signal Distribution (DSD) task.
Unlike previous works, this task encodes the concepts of
signal, noise, locality, and translation invariance, thus incorporating the salient characteristics of vision-based tasks. We quantify the benefits of locality and weight sharing on the DSD task. Specifically, we show that
FCNs incur an extra multiplicative cost of
k^{2}
because they lacks both architectural biases,
LCNs incur a
k
cost because of the absence of weight sharing,
whereas
CNNs avoid these costs because it exhibits both locality and weight sharing.

In future work, we plan to incorporate second-order characteristics of images into the data model.
For instance, allowing multiple signals to appear across different patches simultaneously would mirror real-world scenarios where multiple objects occur.
Additionally, an interesting direction would be to analyze the role of depth in a CNN in capturing dependency between different patches.

Theorem
5.1

(Fano’s Theorem for Randomized Algorithms)
.

Under the notation established above, let
{\mathcal{V}}
be an index set of finite cardinality of some chosen subset of
{\mathcal{P}}
. Then, we define
{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq\{P_{v}\mid\forall v\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
, and
{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq\{\theta^{\star}(P_{v})\mid\forall v\in{\mathcal{V}}\}
.
For some fixed parameter
\delta>0
, let
\rho
satisfy the condition that,
for all
f_{u}\neq f_{v}\in{\mathcal{F}}_{\mathcal{V}}
and
f\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
if
\rho(f,f_{u})<\delta
, then
\rho(f,f_{v})>\delta
.
And, for all
P_{u},P_{v}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{V}}
,
u\neq v
,
let the KL divergence satisfy
\text{KL}(P_{u}\parallel P_{v})\leq D
for some

. Then,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{n}(\Theta)\geq\delta\left(1-\tfrac{nD+\ln(2)}{\ln(\mid{\mathcal{V}}\mid)}\right).

Proof.

From the definition of minimax risk,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{n}({\Theta})

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}}\sim P(\Xi)}\left[\rho(\theta(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}}),\theta^{\star}(P))\right],

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{V}}}\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}}\sim P(\Xi)}\left[\rho(\theta(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}}),\theta^{\star}(P))\right],

\displaystyle=\inf_{{\theta}\in\Theta}\sup_{Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{n}}\E_{(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}})\sim Q}\left[\rho(\theta(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}}),\theta^{\star}(Q))\right],

where

{\mathcal{Q}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{n}\coloneqq\{Q(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}})\coloneqq P^{n}(S^{n})*P_{\Xi}({\mathbf{\xi}})\mid P\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{V}}\}
,
and
we overload the target mapping notation and set
\theta^{\star}(Q)=\theta^{\star}(P)
,
where
P
is the distribution corresponding to
Q
.
First, observe that for all
Q_{u},Q_{v}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{n}
,
u\neq v
, the KL divergence between the two distributions is given by,

\displaystyle\text{KL}(Q_{u}\parallel Q_{v})

\displaystyle=\E_{(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}})\sim Q_{u}}\tfrac{Q_{u}(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}})}{Q_{v}(S^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}})}=\E_{S^{n}\sim P_{u}^{n},{\mathbf{\xi}}\sim P_{\Xi}}\tfrac{P_{u}^{n}(S^{n})P_{\Xi}({\mathbf{\xi}})}{P_{v}^{n}(S^{n})P_{\Xi}({\mathbf{\xi}})},

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim P_{u}^{n}}\tfrac{P_{u}^{n}(S^{n})}{P_{v}^{n}(S^{n})}=\text{KL}(P_{u}^{n}\parallel P_{v}^{n})=n\text{KL}(P_{u}\parallel P_{v})=nD.

We follow in the footsteps of the proof of Fano’s Theorem [Prop 7.3
Duchi 2021
].
For any
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathcal{V}}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\E_{Q}[\rho(\theta_{n},\theta^{\star}(Q))]\geq\E_{Q}[\delta\,\bm{1}\{\rho(\theta_{n},\theta^{\star}(Q))\geq\delta\}]\geq\delta\,{\mathbb{P}}[\rho(\theta_{n},\theta^{\star}(Q))\geq\delta].

We define the testing function,

\Psi\colon{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{V}}
as,

\displaystyle\Psi(f)\coloneqq\argmin_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\{\rho(f,\theta^{\star}(Q_{v}))\},

where ties can be broken arbitrarily and the analysis would still hold.
Let
{\bm{v}}
be the uniform random variable over
{\mathcal{V}}
.
Recall the assumption on
\rho
that,
if
\rho(f,Q_{u})<\delta
, then
\rho(f,Q_{v})>\delta
,

\displaystyle\sup_{Q\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\mathcal{V}}}^{n}}{\mathbb{P}}[\rho(\theta_{n},\theta^{\star}(Q))\geq\delta]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mid{\mathcal{V}}\mid}\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathbb{P}}[\rho(\theta_{n},\theta^{\star}(Q_{v}))\geq\delta\mid{\bm{v}}=v],

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\mid{\mathcal{V}}\mid}\sum_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}{\mathbb{P}}[\Psi(\theta_{n})\neq v\mid{\bm{v}}=v],

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\Psi}{\mathbb{P}}[\Psi(\theta_{n})\neq{\bm{v}}].

From the above,
B
, and Prop 7.10 and Eq 7.4.5 from
Duchi 2021
, we have the result,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}_{n}({\Theta})\geq\delta\left(1-\tfrac{nD+\ln(2)}{\ln(|{\mathcal{V}}|)}\right).

∎

{\bm{w}}^{0}\sim W
, for some distribution
W
.
If the algorithm,

\bar{\theta}_{n}(S^{n},{\bm{w}}^{0};{\mathcal{M}}_{F}[{\mathcal{W}}],\{F_{t}\}_{T})
,

{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant, such that

, then for large enough

\displaystyle n_{\delta}(\bar{\theta}_{n})=\max(\Omega\left(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d\right),40k),

(46)

\delta=0.25\times 10^{-2}
,

.

Proof.

For simplicity we refer to the distribution
DSD
by
P
, and the distribution
\text{SSD}_{t}
by
Q_{t}
, for
t\in[k]
.
Since the algorithm
\bar{\theta}_{n}
is
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant, lemma
5.1
gives us that for all
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
,

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\bar{\theta}(\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}),

(47)

\displaystyle\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}}),y\right)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}),y\right),

(48)

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\E_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim P}\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}}),y\right)

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\E_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim P}\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}),y\right),

(49)

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},P\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ P\right)\right],

(50)

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}\right)\right],

(51)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}\right)\right].

(52)

To simplify
52
, we begin by showing that the expected risk incurred by the algorithm is the same for every distribution
{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}
. Specifically, for all
i,j\in[k]
,

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}\right)\right]=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{j}\right)\right].

(53)

For
i=j
, the
53
trivially holds. So we can assume that
i\neq j
.
From the definition of DSD, note that for any
\alpha,\beta\in[k]
, we have that
{\bm{\mu}}_{\alpha}^{T}{\bm{\mu}}_{\beta}=\bm{1}[\alpha=\beta]
.
Consequently, for any
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, we observe that,

\displaystyle({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\alpha})^{T}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\beta}={\bm{\mu}}_{\alpha}^{T}{\mathbf{U}}^{T}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\beta}={\bm{\mu}}_{\alpha}^{T}{\bm{\mu}}_{\beta}=\bm{1}[\alpha=\beta]

(54)

The above fact ensures that for all
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, there exists a
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\in{\mathcal{U}}
, such that for all
\alpha\in[k]\setminus\{i,j\}
,

{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\alpha}={\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{\alpha}
,
and that

{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}={\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{j}

and
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{j}={\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}
.
In other words, the map
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
swaps the vectors
{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}
, and
{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{j}
, and keeps the other vectors unchanged.
We again use the
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariance of
\bar{\theta}_{n}
to infer from lemma
5.1
that,

\displaystyle\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\bar{\theta}(\{{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\bm{x}}),

(55)

\displaystyle\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}}),y\right)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\bm{x}}),y\right),

(56)

\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\E_{{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}}\left[\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}}),y\right)\right]=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\E_{{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}}\big[\text{err}(\bar{\theta}(\{{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})\\
({\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\bm{x}}),y)\big],

(57)

\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\E_{{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}}\left[\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})({\bm{x}}),y\right)\right]=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\E_{{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}}\big[\text{err}\big(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{n})\\
({\bm{x}}),y\big)\big].

(58)

From construction of
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}
, we know that
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}\circ P\overset{d}{=}{\mathbf{U}}\circ P
, and

{\mathbf{U}}_{1}{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}\overset{d}{=}{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{j}
,

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\E_{{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}}\left[\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{i})({\bm{x}}),y\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\E_{{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{j}}\big[\text{err}\left(\bar{\theta}(\{{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i}\}_{i})({\bm{x}}),y\right)\big],

(59)

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{j}\right)\right].

(60)

This proves the claim
53
. Substituting it back into
52
,

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},P\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\right],

(61)

\displaystyle=\sup_{{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}}\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\right],

(62)

where, we define

\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}
such that,
for all
{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}
,
t\in\{2,\ldots,k\}
,

{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}=e_{kd-k+t}
.
Let

\Xi={\mathcal{W}}
,

P_{\Xi}=W
,
and

\Theta=\{{\theta}\mid{\theta}\colon(({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})^{n},\Xi)\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\}
be the set of
{\mathcal{O}}(kd)
equivariant algorithms, such that
b^{T}\geq b_{\min}
.
It is easy to note that
\bar{\theta}_{n}\in\Theta
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},P\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\theta_{n}\in\Theta}\sup_{{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}}\E_{S^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ P)^{n}}\left[R\left({\theta}_{n},{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\right],

(63)

We will now perform a series of reductions to lower bound the above minimax problem, with the minimax problem of learning
\text{SSD}_{1}
. The central concept behind these reductions is to demonstrate that a given minimax problem can be ‘simulated’ by a more tractable one, and thus the tractable problem serves as a lower bound on the original problem.

Let

\Theta_{1}=\{{\theta}\mid{\theta}\colon((({\mathcal{X}},{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}),{\mathcal{Y}})^{n},\Xi)\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\}
be the set of algorithms that are
{\mathcal{U}}_{1}=\{\text{Block}({\mathbf{U}},{\mathbf{I}}_{1})\mid{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{O}}(kd)\}
equivariant, and
b^{T}\geq b_{\min}
.
Define the set
\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}\subseteq{\mathcal{U}}_{1}
,
\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}=\{\text{Block}({\mathbf{U}},{\mathbf{I}}_{1})\mid{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}\}
.
Define

\tilde{P}
to be the indexed distribution with
the generative story:
Sample
j\sim\text{Unif}[k]
,
then sample
({\bm{x}},y)\sim Q_{j}
,
and return
(({\bm{x}},j),y)
.
We can then lower bound the minimax expression
63
as,

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{1}}\sup_{{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}}\E_{{\bm{w}}\sim W}\E_{(({\bm{x}},j),y)^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}_{1}\circ\tilde{P})^{n}}\left[R\left(\theta((({\bm{x}},j),y)^{n},{\bm{w}}),{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\right].

(64)

The inequality follows from the fact that for every
\theta^{a}_{n}\in\Theta
, there exists
\theta^{b}_{n}\in\Theta_{1}
, that discards the index
j
and returns the output of
\theta^{a}_{n}
.

Let
n_{1}
be the random variable that denotes the number of samples drawn from
{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\circ\tilde{P}
when
j=1
.
Using Berstein’s inequality, we get that,

\tfrac{n}{2k}\leq n_{1}\leq m\coloneqq\tfrac{3n}{2k}
,
with probability

\geq c\coloneqq 1-2\exp(\tfrac{-n}{10k})
.
We will refer to the event,

\tfrac{n}{2k}\leq n_{1}\leq m
,
as

.
Then, we can lower bound
64

\displaystyle\geq c\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{1}}\sup_{{\mathbf{U}}_{1}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}_{1}}\E_{{\bm{w}}\sim W}\E_{(({\bm{x}},j),y)^{n}\sim({\mathbf{U}}_{1}\circ\tilde{P})^{n}}\left[R\left(\theta((({\bm{x}},j),y)^{n},{\bm{w}}),{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\mid E\right].

(65)

For the next reduction, we generalize the definition of
n_{1}
, and define
n_{i}
to be the random variable corresponding to the number of samples drawn from the distribution
{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{i}
, for all
i\in[k]
.
Let
{\bm{y}}\sim(\text{Unif}[{\mathcal{Y}}])^{n}

be a uniform random vector over
\{+1,-1\}
of size
n
, and

{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0}_{nkd},\mathbf{I}_{nkd})

be a vector of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables.
Let

\Theta_{2}=\{{\theta}\mid{\theta}\colon(({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})^{m}\times(({\mathbb{R}}^{kd})^{k-1}\times({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})^{k}\times({\mathbb{R}})^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{nkd}\times{\mathcal{W}}))\rightarrow{\mathcal{F}}\}

be a set of
{\mathcal{O}}(kd)
equivariant algorithms that take as input the training data,
\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}\}_{i=2}^{k}
mean vectors, the number of samples to be drawn from each mean, pre-sampled values of
{\bm{y}}
and
{\bm{\epsilon}}
, and the parameter initialization respectively. It subsequently returns a function within
{\mathcal{F}}
. Then we can bound
65
as,

\displaystyle\geq c\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{2}}\sup_{{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}}\E_{{\bm{w}}\sim W}\E_{\{n_{i}\}_{1}^{k}}\E_{{\bm{y}},{\bm{\epsilon}}}\E_{S^{m}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1})^{m}}\left[R\left(\theta(S^{m},(\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}\}_{2}^{k},\{n_{i}\}_{1}^{k},{\bm{y}},{\bm{\epsilon}},{\bm{w}})),{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\mid E\right].

(66)

The last inequality follows from the fact that for every
\theta^{a}_{n}\in\Theta_{1}
, there exists
\theta^{b}_{n}\in\Theta_{2}
, that first deterministically creates the indexed dataset using

S^{m},\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}\}_{2}^{k},\{n_{i}\}_{1}^{k},{\bm{y}},{\bm{\epsilon}}
and then runs
\theta^{a}_{n}
.

For notational brevity, we define

\Xi_{1}\coloneqq({\mathbb{R}}^{kd})^{k-1}\times({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\})^{k}\times({\mathbb{R}})^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{nkd}\times{\mathcal{W}}
, to encapsulate the randomness in
\{{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{i}\}_{2}^{k}
,

\{n_{i}\}_{1}^{k},{\bm{y}},{\bm{\epsilon}}
, and
{\bm{w}}
.
We denote its associated product distribution by
P_{\Xi_{1}}
.
Recall that this distribution must be independent of the input data distribution. To see this, we recall that from the construction of
\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}
, that for all
{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}
,
t\in\{2,\ldots,k\}
,

{\mathbf{U}}{\bm{\mu}}_{t}=e_{kd-k+t}
, which are fixed deterministic quantities.
Rewriting
66
, we get,

\displaystyle=c\inf_{\theta\in\Theta_{2}}\sup_{{\mathbf{U}}\in\tilde{{\mathcal{U}}}}\E_{{\mathbf{\xi}}\sim P_{\Xi_{1}}}\E_{S^{m}\sim({\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1})^{m}}\left[R\left(\theta(S^{m},{\mathbf{\xi}}),{\mathbf{U}}\circ Q_{1}\right)\right],

(67)

We have effectively reduced solving the original problem
P
into solving its constituent problem
Q_{1}
with approximately
n/k
samples.
We have already proven a lower bound for the SSD problem in Theorem
4
. Using that result, we have,

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},P\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq c*0.5*10^{-2},

(68)

iff

, which implies that
n=\Omega(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d)
. Since,

c\geq\left(1-2\exp(\tfrac{-n}{10k})\right)
,
using
n\geq 40k
, we can bound

.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\E_{S^{n}\sim P^{n}}\left[R\left(\bar{\theta}_{n},P\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq 0.25*10^{-2}

(69)

iff
n=\Omega(\sigma^{2}k^{2}d)
, proving the result.

C.2
LCN Upper Bound

Theorem

(Formal)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
denote the class of functions represented by the set of locally connected neural network models
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{L}}
, defined in equation
5
.
Let the input data samples be drawn from the DSD distribution,
S^{n}\sim(\text{DSD})^{n}
, with
\sigma=\tilde{O}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}})
, and
k=O(\exp(d))
.
Define the following groups:

{\mathcal{U}}_{1}\coloneqq\{\text{Block}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\right)\mid{\mathbf{U}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}(d)\}
,

\>{\mathcal{U}}_{2}\coloneqq\{{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{O}}_{p}(kd)\mid\text{idx}_{kd}({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{e}}_{(i-1)d+1})+j-1=\text{idx}_{kd}({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{e}}_{(i-1)d+j}),\>\forall i\in[k],\>\forall j\in[d]\}
,
and

{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{U}}_{1}\star{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
.
Then, there exist update functions
\{F_{t}\}_{T}
, and a model parameter initialization distribution
W
,
such that

\bar{\theta}_{n}({\mathcal{M}}_{L},\{F_{t}\}_{T},W,S^{n})

is a
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant algorithm, and
for large enough

,

\displaystyle n_{\delta}(\bar{\theta}_{n})=\max(O\left(\sigma^{2}k(d+k)\ln(kd)\right),80k\ln(kd)),

(70)

for
\delta=O(1)
.

Proof.

We begin by defining the algorithm
\bar{\theta}_{n}
, we then establish that
\bar{\theta}_{n}
is a
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant algorithm,
and then we analyze each iteration of the algorithm to prove the required sample complexity bound.

1. Algorithm Definition

To define the algorithm
\bar{\theta}_{n}({\mathcal{M}}_{L},\{F_{t}\}_{T},W,S^{n})
,
we need to specify
the initialization distribution
W
, and
the update functions
\{F_{t}\}_{T}
.
At iteration
t=0
, we initialize the model parameter

{\bm{v}}^{0}=[{\bm{w}}_{1}^{0},..,{\bm{w}}_{k}^{0},b^{0}]
as follows:
for each
i\in[k]
, the vector
{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}
is independently sampled from the distribution
{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\gamma{\mathbf{I}}_{d})
, where
\gamma^{-1}=100k^{2}d^{2}
, and bias is set as
b^{0}=0
.
The superscript denotes the iteration number.
We define the empirical loss function for
N\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
data samples as,

\displaystyle l\colon({\mathcal{W}},({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})^{N})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(y_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j})\big)^{2}.

(71)

The algorithm proceeds in
T=2

For simpler analysis, we split the input dataset
S^{n}
into two equal sized datasets
S^{m}_{1}
, and
S^{m}_{2}
, with
m\coloneqq\tfrac{n}{2}

Then, for each

\displaystyle F_{t}({\bm{v}},S^{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left[\tfrac{{\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})}{\lVert{\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})\rVert},\ldots,\tfrac{{\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})}{\lVert{\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})\rVert}\>,\>b_{t}\right],

2. Algorithm is Equivariant

To establish that
\bar{\theta}_{n}

{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant, we only need to show that it is both
{\mathcal{U}}_{1}

{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
-equivariant, because, every element in
{\mathcal{U}}
is a finite matrix product of elements from
{\mathcal{U}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
.
We define groups
{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\coloneqq\{\text{Block}({\mathbf{V}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{V}}_{k},{\mathbf{I}}_{1})\mid{\mathbf{V}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}(d),i\in[k]\}

{\mathcal{V}}_{2}\coloneqq\{\text{Block}({\mathbf{U}},{\mathbf{I}}_{1})\mid{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{2}\}
, and
{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{V}}_{1}\star{\mathcal{V}}_{2}
.

To prove
{\mathcal{U}}_{1}
-equivariance,
we need to verify the three conditions in Definition
6
.
For any data sample
{\bm{x}},y\in({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{1}
,
{\bm{w}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d};i\in[k]
,

, choose
{\mathbf{V}}=\text{Block}(\{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)},{\mathbf{I}}_{1}\})\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
Then, the first property
1

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{L}[{\bm{v}}]({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}_{i}^{T}({\mathbf{U}}^{(i)})^{T}({\mathbf{U}}^{(i)}){\bm{x}}^{(i)})={\mathcal{M}}_{L}[{\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}}]({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}).

(73)

For each iteration

, and

\displaystyle F_{t}\left({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}},{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})}{\lVert{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})\rVert},\ldots,

(74)

\displaystyle{}\hskip 56.9055pt\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}{\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})}{\lVert{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}{\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})\rVert}\>,\>b_{t}\bigg],

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}\left({\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)}{\lVert{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}\left({\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)\rVert},\ldots,\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}\left({\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)}{\lVert{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}\left({\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)\rVert}\>,\>b_{t}\right],

(75)

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}\left({\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})\right)}{\lVert{\bm{w}}_{1}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{1}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})\rVert},\ldots,\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}\left({\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})\right)}{\lVert{\bm{w}}_{k}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{k}}l({\bm{v}};S_{t}^{m})\rVert}\>,\>b_{t}\right],

(76)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{V}}F_{t}\left({\bm{v}},S^{n}\right).

(77)

And property
3
can be affirmed by observing that, for all
{\mathbf{V}}\in{\mathcal{V}}_{1}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}}^{0}

\displaystyle=[{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}_{1}^{0},\ldots,{\mathbf{U}}^{(k)}{\bm{w}}_{k}^{0},b^{0}],

(78)

\displaystyle\overset{d}{=}[{\bm{w}}_{1}^{0},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{k}^{0},b^{0}]={\bm{v}}.

(79)

This proves that
\bar{\theta}_{n}
is
{\mathcal{U}}_{1}
-equivariant.
We now establish
{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
-equivariance.
Observe that action of any matrix
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}_{2}
is to permute the
k
patches of the input.
Let
\pi\colon[k]\rightarrow[k]
be the permutation function corresponding to
{\mathbf{U}}
.
For this given
{\mathbf{U}}
, we choose
{\mathbf{V}}=\text{Block}({\mathbf{U}},{\mathbf{I}}_{1})\in{\mathcal{V}}
.
For any
{\bm{x}},y\in({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
{\bm{w}}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},i\in[k]
,

, property

of equivariance holds as,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{L}[{\bm{v}}]({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}_{\pi(i)}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{\pi(i)})={\mathcal{M}}_{L}[{\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}}]({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}).

(80)

For each iteration
t\in[2]
, and
S^{n}\in({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})^{n}
, the second property

\displaystyle F_{t}\left({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}},{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\bigg[\tfrac{{\bm{w}}_{\pi(1)}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{\pi(1)}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})}{\lVert{\bm{w}}_{\pi(1)}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{\pi(1)}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})\rVert},\ldots,

(81)

3. Algorithm Analysis

We analyze each iteration of
\bar{\theta}_{n}
,
with
n=\max(2\sigma^{2}k(k+d)\ln(kd),80k\ln(kd))

samples,
and establish that
\bar{\theta}_{n}
achieve an expected risk of at most
\delta=2.5\times 10^{-3}
.
Since
k=O(\exp(d))
and
\sigma=\tilde{O}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}})
, we assume that
\sqrt{d}\geq 20\sqrt{\ln(k)}
and
100\sqrt{k\ln(kd)^{3}}\sigma\leq 1
.

The outline of the analysis is as follows: We show that after the first update step, we reliably recover the unknown signal vector, upto an alignment of
\Omega(\sqrt{\tfrac{k+d}{kd}})
.
In the second step, we show that this alignment is enough to threshold out the "noise" patches while only letting the "signal" patch pass through the first hidden layer.
This enables us to recover the signal upto an alignment of
\Omega(1)
, which results in the expected risk of the learned LCN being smaller than
\delta
.

3a. Update Step 1

For each
i\in[k]

\tilde{{\bm{w}}}_{i}^{1}={\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}-\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}}l({\bm{w}}_{i}^{0},0;S_{1}^{m})

{\bm{w}}_{i}^{1}

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}}l([{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0},0];S_{1}^{m})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}}\left(y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{0}(({\bm{w}}_{i}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j})\right)^{2},

(86)

(87)

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}}l([{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0},0];S_{1}^{m})=\tfrac{-2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}{\bm{\beta}}_{ij},

\alpha_{j}

j\in[m]

{\bm{w}}_{i}^{0}\overset{d}{=}\gamma{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}

{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{I}}_{d})

DSD

{\bm{x}}_{j}^{(i)}=y_{j}r_{ij}{\bm{w}}^{\star}+\sigma{\bm{\epsilon}}_{j}^{(i)}

in

i

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j}({\bm{w}}_{i}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j},

(92)

\displaystyle 1+|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma r_{ij}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{w}}^{\star}|+|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma\sigma{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{j}^{(i)}|\geq\alpha_{j}\geq 1-|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma r_{ij}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{w}}^{\star}|-|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma\sigma{\bm{\epsilon}}_{i}^{T}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{j}^{(i)}|.

Substituting
94

\displaystyle\max_{i\in[k],j\in[m]}|\sigma({\bm{w}}_{i}^{1})^{T}{\bm{\epsilon}}_{j}^{(i)}|

D.2
CNN Upper Bound

Theorem
7.2

(Formal)
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
denote the class of functions represented by the set of locally connected neural network models,
{\mathcal{M}}_{\mathcal{C}}[{\mathcal{W}}],
as defined in
4.2
.
Let the input data be drawn from the DSD distribution,
S^{n}\sim(\text{DSD})^{n}
, with
\sigma=\tilde{O}(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}})
.
We define the group

{\mathcal{U}}\coloneqq\{\text{Block}\left({\mathbf{U}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{U}}_{k}\right)\mid{\mathbf{U}}_{i}\in{\mathcal{O}}(d),{\mathbf{U}}_{i}={\mathbf{U}}_{j}\}
.
Then there exists a weight initialization distribution
W

and update functions
\{F_{t}\}_{T}
such that

\bar{\theta}_{n}({\mathcal{M}}_{C}[{\mathcal{W}}],\{F_{t}\}_{T},W,S^{n})

is an
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant algorithm and,
if
k,d
are large enough, then

\displaystyle n_{\delta}(\bar{\theta}_{n})=\max(O\left(\sigma^{2}(d+k)\ln(kd)\right),10),

(217)

for some constant
\delta=O(1)
.

Proof.

The outline of the proof will run parallel to the approach taken in the proof of Theorem
6.2
.
We will first present the algorithm
\bar{\theta}_{n}
, then show it is a
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant algorithm and then derive the required sample complexity bound upper bound.

1. Algorithm Definition

To define the algorithm
\bar{\theta}_{n}
,
we need to specify its components: the model
{\mathcal{M}}_{C}[{\mathcal{W}}]
,
the initialization distribution
W
, and
the update functions
\{F_{t}\}_{T}
.
At iteration
t=0
, we initialize the model parameter

{\bm{v}}^{0}=[{\bm{w}}^{0},b^{0}]
as
{\bm{w}}^{0}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},\gamma{\mathbf{I}}_{d})
, where
\gamma^{-1}=100k^{2}d^{2}
, and bias is set as
b^{0}=0
.
The superscript denotes the iteration number.
To specify the update functions, we define the empirical loss function,

\displaystyle l\colon({\mathcal{W}},({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})^{n})\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j})\right)^{2}.

(218)

The algorithm has
T=2
iterations. For simpler analysis, we divide the dataset,
S^{n}
, into two equal sized datasets
S^{m}_{1}
, and
S^{m}_{1}
, with
m\coloneqq\tfrac{n}{2}
samples each. The update function for each
t\in\{1,2\}
is,

\displaystyle F_{t}({\bm{v}},S_{t}^{m})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left[\tfrac{{\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}l({\bm{w}},b;S_{t}^{m})}{\lVert{\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}l({\bm{w}},b;S_{t}^{m})\rVert}\>;\>b_{t}\right],

(219)

where
\eta_{1}=1,\eta_{2}=10^{3},b_{1}=\tfrac{1}{100}\sqrt{\tfrac{kd}{(k+d)\ln(kd)}},b_{2}=10^{-4}
.

2. Algorithm is Equivariant

To establish that
\bar{\theta}_{n}
is
{\mathcal{U}}
-equivariant, we verify the three conditions specified in Definition
6
.
We define the group,
{\mathcal{V}}\coloneqq\{\text{Block}({\mathbf{V}},{\mathbf{I}}_{1})\mid{\mathbf{V}}\in{\mathcal{O}}(d)\}
, where
{\mathbf{I}}_{1}
is the identity matrix of size
1
.

For
{\bm{x}},y\in({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
,
{\mathbf{U}}\in{\mathcal{U}}
,
{\bm{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
b\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, choose
{\mathbf{V}}=\text{Block}(\{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)},{\mathbf{I}}_{1}\})\in{\mathcal{V}}
, without loss of generality, as for all
i,j
,
{\mathbf{U}}^{(i)}={\mathbf{U}}^{(j)}
.
Then, the property
1
of equivariance holds as,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{C}[{\bm{v}}]({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{b}({\bm{w}}^{T}({\mathbf{U}}^{(1)})^{T}{\mathbf{U}}^{(i)}{\bm{x}}^{(i)})={\mathcal{M}}_{C}[{\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}}]({\mathbf{U}}{\bm{x}}).

(220)

For all
t\in[2]
and
S_{t}^{m}\in({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})^{m}
the second property
2
follows as,

\displaystyle F_{t}\left({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}},{\mathbf{U}}\circ S_{t}^{m}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})}{\lVert{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}}l({\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}};{\mathbf{U}}\circ S^{m}_{t})\rVert}\>;\>b_{t}\right],

(221)

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}\left({\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)}{\lVert{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}\left({\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)\rVert}\>;\>b_{t}\right],

(222)

\displaystyle=\left[\tfrac{{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}\left({\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\right)}{\lVert{\bm{w}}-\eta_{t}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}}l({\bm{v}};S^{m}_{t})\rVert}\>;\>b_{t}\right],

(223)

\displaystyle={\mathbf{V}}F_{t}\left({\bm{v}},S^{m}_{t}\right).

(224)

And as for property
3
, observe that,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{V}}{\bm{v}}^{0}=[{\mathbf{U}}^{(1)}{\bm{w}}^{0}b^{0}],\overset{d}{=}[{\bm{w}}^{0},b^{0}]={\bm{v}},

(225)

holds for all
{\mathbf{V}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
.

3. Algorithm Analysis

We analyze the algorithm,
with
n=\max(2\sigma^{2}(k+d)\ln(kd),10)

samples,
to establish that
\bar{\theta}_{n}
achieve an expected risk of at most
\delta=2.5\times 10^{-3}
.
We set
\sigma\leq\tfrac{1}{100\sqrt{k\ln(kd)^{3}}}
.
The outline of the proof is as follows:
we first prove that after the first update step, the alignment of
{\bm{w}}^{1}
with unknown signal vector
{\bm{w}}^{\star}
is
\Omega(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{k}})
.
In the second step, we use this alignment is reliably threshold out the "noise" patches, while letting the "signal" patch pass through the first hidden layer.
We then show that this denoising effect, enables us to recover the signal with an alignment of
\Omega(1)
, which would imply that the risk of the CNN on the task
\leq\delta
.

3a. Update Step 1

We define

\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{1}={\bm{w}}^{0}-\nabla_{{\bm{w}}^{0}}l({\bm{w}}^{0},0;S_{1}^{m})

to be the unnormalized parameter vector
{\bm{w}}^{1}
,
and therefore the alignment with the signal is given by

({\bm{w}}^{1})^{T}{\bm{w}}^{\star}=\tfrac{(\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{1})^{T}{\bm{w}}^{\star}}{\lVert\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{1}\rVert}
.
To analyze
\hat{{\bm{w}}}^{1}
,
we first evaluate the gradient with respect to
{\bm{w}}^{0}
,
\nabla_{{\bm{w}}^{0}}l({\bm{w}}^{0},0;S_{1}^{m})
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{{\bm{w}}^{0}}l({\bm{w}}^{0},0;S_{1}^{m})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\nabla_{{\bm{w}}^{0}}\left(y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{0}(({\bm{w}}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j})\right)^{2},

(226)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{-2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(y_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{0}(({\bm{w}}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j}))\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j}\phi^{\prime}_{0}(({\bm{w}}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j}))\right),

(227)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{-2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j}({\bm{w}}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j})\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j}\right),

(228)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\tfrac{-2}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\beta_{j},

(229)

where
\phi^{\prime}_{0}(x)\coloneqq\tfrac{d}{dx}\phi_{0}(x)
.
We have used the facts that
\phi_{0}
is the identity function, and
\phi_{0}^{\prime}
is the constant function
1
.
And,

\alpha_{j}\coloneqq 1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j}({\bm{w}}^{0})^{T}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j}
,

\beta_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{j}{\bm{x}}^{(i)}_{j}
.

To further analyze
229
, we first prove high probability bounds for
\alpha_{j}
,
j\in[m]
.
From the initialization distribution
W
, we know that
{\bm{w}}^{0}\overset{d}{=}\gamma{\bm{\epsilon}}
, where
{\bm{\epsilon}}
is the Gaussian random vector defined as
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{I}}_{d})
.
And from the input distribution
DSD
, we know that

{\bm{x}}_{j}^{(i)}=y_{j}r_{ij}{\bm{w}}^{\star}+\sigma{\bm{\epsilon}}_{j}^{(i)}
, for all
i
in
[k]
.
Here,
{\bm{\epsilon}}_{j}^{(i)}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(\mathbf{0},{\mathbf{I}}_{d})
is also a Gaussian random vector, and

r_{ij}=1
, if the signal patch appears in the
j
-th data sample appears in the
i
-th patch, and
0
otherwise.

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}

Appendix E
Experiments

In this section, we validate our theoretical bounds with empirical results. We begin by presenting the test-error experiments, where we evaluate the test error of the three models across various training sample sizes. The results for these experiments show an order-of-magnitude decrease in the sample efficiency when comparing CNNs to LCNs, and comparing LCNs to FCNs.

We then present our sample complexity experiments, wherein we explicitly calculate the sample complexity of CNNs and LCNs for various
(k,d)

While the computational demands are manageable for CNNs, they increase significantly for LCNs and become prohibitively large for FCNs. This is primarily because FCNs require at least

times more samples than LCNs.
Nonetheless, for both CNNs and LCNs, we successfully verify that the empirical sample complexity satisfies the respective theoretical bounds.
Specifically, for CNNs, we show a

d
and a
O(d)
growth with a fixed
k
