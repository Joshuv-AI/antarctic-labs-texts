Hamiltonian stationary maps with infinitely many singularities
Filippo Gaia

Abstract
. For any k ∈ ℕ k\in\mathbb{N} we construct an Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian map from a disc to ℂ 2 \mathbb{C}^{2} with infinitely many Schoen-Wolfson singularities which is of class C k C^{k} up to the boundary and has smooth trace.

1.
Introduction

In this notes we give a positive answer to the following question, raised in
[
4
]
:

Is it possible to construct Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian discs with locally finite area but infinitely many Schoen-Wolfson singularities accumulating at the boundary?

More precisely we will prove the following result:

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
p<2
,
j\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}
there exists a weakly conformal, Hamiltonian stationary, Lagrangian map
\Phi\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2},{C}^{2})
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

\Phi
has infinitely many, isolated Schoen-Wolfson singularities,

(2)

\Phi
is continuous up to the boundary and its trace is smooth,

(3)

The Lagrangian angle
g
of
\Phi
lies in
W^{1,p}\cap W^{1,(2,\infty)}_{\text{loc}}(D^{2})
.

Moreover
\Phi
is a smooth branched immersion away from the singularities.

For the precise meaning of the concepts appearing in Theorem
1.1
we refer to Section 2.

In
[
10
]
, R. Schoen and J. Wolfson showed that in a compact symplectic
4
-manifold, the Lagrangian homology is generated
by classes that can be represented by Lagrangian Lipschitz maps which
are branched immersions except at finite number of singular points, having Schoen-Wolfson cones as tangent maps.
They also provide examples of Lagrangian homology classes where the minimizer for the area
must have singularities of this kind (see also
[
12
]
). Moreover the authors say that they expect the regularity result to hold for Lagrangian stationary maps as well.

In
[
6
]
and
[
5
]
, A. Pigati and T. Rivière studied critical points of the area functional among lagrangian surfaces by means of parametrized integer legendrian varifolds, and conjectured that any such critical point should be realised by a smooth branched immersion away from isolated Schoen-Wolfson conical singularities.

Theorem
1.1
, in contrast, shows that an hamiltonian stationary map
\Phi
from a disc to
\mathbb{C}^{2}
which is a branched immersion away from isolated singularities might have accumulation of singular points at the boundary, even if its trace is smooth and even if
\Phi
is assumed to be in
C^{k}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It remains open if there exist a weakly conformal map
\Phi
minimizing the area (or at least stable) among lagrangian maps with a given trace and satisfying the properties listed in Theorem
1.1
.

In
[
4
]
, G. Orriols, T. Rivière and the author introduced a variational method to construct Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian surfaces with prescribed singularities, by which several examples of Hamiltonian stationary discs in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with finitely many singularities can be constructed. It seems difficult, however, to apply directly the method of
[
4
]
to construct examples of Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian discs with infinitely many singularities in
W^{1,2}(D^{2})
.
Therefore the question we reported at the beginning of the Introduction remained open.

The construction we propose here is based on the following observation from
[
4
]
: let
g
be a map in
W^{1,1}(D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and let
u\in W^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
be a solution of

(1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)=0;

then there exists
v\in W^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{C})
such that
\nabla^{\perp}v=g\nabla u
(here
\nabla^{\perp}:=(-\partial_{x_{2}},\partial_{x_{1}})^{T}
) and

(2)

\displaystyle\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
-\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}

is a weakly conformal, Lagrangian map with Lagrangian angle
\overline{g}
. In particular, if
g
satisfies

(3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0,

then
\Phi
is Hamiltonian stationary (for the details of this argument see the proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section 3).

As observed in
[
4
]
(Section 3), this method allows to construct weakly conformal Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian maps in
W^{1,p}(D^{2})
for
p\in[1,2)
which have infinitely many singularities, and even examples which are everywhere discontinuous: let
g
be an everywhere discontinuous
\mathbb{S}^{1}
–harmonic map in
W^{1,p}(D^{2})
, as constructed by L. Almeida in
[
1
]
. Then
u=\overline{g}
solves (
1
), and by the discussion above the map
\Phi
defined in (
2
) satisfies the desired properties.
The map
\Phi
, however, does not lie in
W^{1,2}(D^{2})
and the surface described by
\Phi
has infinite area (
\Phi
is an infinite covering of the cylinder
\mathbb{S}^{1}\times 0\times\mathbb{R}
).

Geometrically, our construction will produce a branched immersion of a disc in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
passing infinitely many times through the origin–where it will have its singularities– and smooth outside of the origin. Its trace will not be an immersed smooth curve. It remains open if it is possible to construct a map as in Theorem
1.1
, whose trace parametrizes an immersed smooth curve (this would be interesting for the study of the Plateau problem in the Lagrangian setting).

To obtain Theorem
1.1
we will combine the method described above with the following analytic result.

Theorem 1.2
.

For any
p<2
,
j\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}
there exists a map
g\in W^{1,p}\cap W_{\text{loc}}^{1,(2,\infty)}(D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1})\cap C^{0}(\overline{D^{2}})
satisfying

(4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla^{\perp}g)=2\pi\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\delta_{p_{k}}\text{ in }D^{2}

for isolated points
p_{k}\in D^{2}
, and a map
u\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2},\mathbb{C})
satisfying

(5)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)=0\text{ in }D^{2}.

Moreover,
u
is smooth away from the points
p_{i}
,
\nabla u
vanishes at isolated points and the trace of
u
on
\partial D^{2}
is smooth.

We remark that the statement of Theorem
1.2
is optimal in the following sense: if
g
is an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued map with
\nabla g\in L^{2,q}(D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1})
for some
q<\infty
and satisfies
\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0
, then
g
has no singularities (see for instance Appendix
4
in
[
1
]
). The case
\nabla g\in L^{2,\infty}(D^{2})
remains open (in
[
1
]
, L. Almeida conjectures that if
\nabla g\in L^{2,\infty}(D^{2})
, then
g
has only finitely many singularities).

To prove Theorem
1.2
we will use the following observation from
[
4
]
(Lemma V.2): if
g\in W^{1,1}
takes values in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
G
is a function such that

(6)

\displaystyle-i\overline{g}\nabla g=\nabla^{\perp}G,

then for any
j\in\mathbb{Z}

(7)

\displaystyle g^{j}e^{-\sqrt{j^{2}+j}\lvert G\rvert}

is a solution of (
5
).
In our construction,
g
and
G
will be respectively the angular part and the logarithm of the modulus of an holomorphic function
\phi
on
D^{2}
, which will be chosen to be smooth up to the boundary in
D^{2}
and to have infinitely many zeros (which will correspond to the singularities of
u
and
g
).

More generally, this method allows to construct Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian maps starting from any holomorphic function on
D^{2}
.

Finally we remark that our method do not allow to construct maps as in Theorem
1.1
satisfying in addition

(8)

\displaystyle i\overline{g}\partial_{\nu}g=0\text{ on }\partial D^{2},

as we will see in Section 4 (where we will also clarify the meaning of this condition when
g\in W^{1,p}(D^{2})
). The existence of such maps would be of interest because (
8
) is the boundary condition satisfied by Lagrangian free-boundary surfaces when the constraint manifold is a complex surface (see
[
8
]
).

2.
Preliminaries

In the present document, we will denote by
D^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the open ball of radius
1
centered at the origin.

Let
\omega=dx_{1}\wedge dy_{1}+dx_{2}\wedge dy_{2}
be the standard symplectic form on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. A map
\Phi\in W^{1,1}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is
Lagrangian
if
\Phi^{\ast}\omega=0
a.e..

A map
\Phi\in W^{1,1}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is said to be
weakly conformal
if
\langle\partial_{x}\Phi,\partial_{y}\Phi\rangle=0
and
\lvert\partial_{x}\Phi\rvert^{2}=\lvert\partial_{y}\Phi\rvert^{2}
a.e..

If
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is Lagrangian and weakly conformal, there exists a measurable map
g:D^{2}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that

(9)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\ast}(dz_{1}\wedge dz_{2})=e^{2\lambda}\overline{g}\,dx\wedge dy\text{ a.e.},

where
e^{2\lambda}=\lvert\partial_{x}\Phi\rvert^{2}=\lvert\partial_{y}\Phi\rvert^{2}

(see for instance p. 3 in
[
11
]
). The map
\overline{g}
is called the
Lagrangian angle
of
\Phi
.

If
\Phi\in C^{1}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
is a weakly conformal Lagrangian map such that
\nabla\Phi
vanishes at isolated points, we say that
p\in D^{2}
is a
branch point
of
\Phi
if
\nabla\Phi(p)=0
and the Lagrangian angle
\overline{g}
of
\Phi
is continuous at
p
. If
\Phi
is an immersion away from isolated points in
D^{2}
which are branch points of
\Phi
, we say that
\Phi
is a
branched immersion
.

It will be convenient to identify
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with the algebra of quaternions
\mathbb{H}
with basis elements
1,I,J,K
. In this identification,
I
corresponds to the complex number
i
.
For any weakly conformal, Lagrangian
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
there holds

\partial_{\theta}\Phi\in\operatorname{span}\{J\partial_{r}\Phi,K\partial_{r}\Phi\}
a.e.. Thus at a.e.
x\in D^{2}
there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}\Phi=(aJ\partial_{r}\Phi+bK\partial_{r}\Phi).

Now (
9
) implies

(11)

\displaystyle e^{2\lambda}\overline{g}=

\displaystyle\partial_{r}\Phi_{1}(a(J\partial_{r}\Phi)_{2}+b(K\partial_{r}\Phi)_{2})-\partial_{r}\Phi_{2}(a(J\partial_{r}\Phi)_{1}+b(K\partial_{r}\Phi)_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(a+ib)\lvert\partial_{r}\Phi\rvert^{2},

where the indices
1
and
2
refer to the complex coordinates in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
.
Therefore

(12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}\Phi=-\overline{g}J\partial_{r}\Phi\text{ a.e.,}

so that

(13)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla\Phi)=0.

Let
\Phi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{D}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
be a smooth Lagrangian embedding with Lagrangian angle
g
;
\Phi
is said to be
Hamiltonian stationary
if for any smooth function
f:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
supported away from
\Phi(\partial D^{2})
there holds

(14)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\langle d\Phi;d(I(\nabla f)\circ\Phi)\rangle=0.

By (
13
) this implies

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\langle d\Phi;d(I(\nabla f)\circ\Phi)\rangle=-\int_{D^{2}}\langle\Delta\Phi,I(\nabla f)\circ\Phi\rangle=-\int_{D^{2}}\langle i\overline{g}dg\cdot d\Phi,\nabla f\circ\Phi\rangle

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{D^{2}}\langle i\overline{g}dg;d(f\circ\Phi)\rangle=\int_{D^{2}}\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)f\circ\Phi.

As
\Phi
is an embedding and (
15
) holds for any smooth
f
supported away from
\Phi(\partial D^{2})
, we have
\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0
in
D^{2}
.

More generally, for a Lagrangian map
\Phi\in W^{1,1}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
we say that
\Phi
is
Hamiltonian stationary
if its Lagrangian angle
\overline{g}
satisfies

(16)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0.

In
[
10
]
, R. Schoen and J. Wolfson classified all the two-dimensional Hamiltonian stationary Lagrangian cones:
for any relatively prime positive integers
p
and
q
, up to unitary rotation, the
Schoen-Wolfson cone
\Sigma_{p,q}

has the following weakly conformal parametrization

(17)

\displaystyle\Phi_{p,q}:\overline{D^{2}}\to\mathbb{C}^{2},\quad re^{i\theta}\mapsto\frac{r^{\sqrt{pq}}}{\sqrt{p+q}}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{q}e^{ip\theta}\\
i\sqrt{p}e^{-iq\theta}\end{pmatrix}

and its Lagrangian angle is given by
e^{i(p-q)}
. Notice that
\Phi_{p,q}\in C^{\sqrt{pq}}(D^{2})
, meaning that
\Phi_{p,q}\in C^{\lfloor\sqrt{pq}\rfloor,\sqrt{pq}-\lfloor\sqrt{pq}\rfloor}(D^{2})
.

We remark (see Lemma V.2 in
[
4
]
) that if
g\in W^{1,(2,\infty)}_{\text{loc}}(D^{2})
(meaning that
\nabla g\in L^{2,\infty}_{\text{loc}}(D^{2})
) solves
\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0
and has a singularity of degree
1
in
p
, and
\Phi\in W^{1,2}(D^{2})
is a solution of
\operatorname{div}(g\nabla\Phi)=0
, then
\Phi
has the following form around
p
:

(18)

\displaystyle\Phi=U\cdot\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}a_{j}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{j+1}e^{\sqrt{j(j+1)}\,G}g^{j}\\
i\sqrt{j}e^{\sqrt{j(j+1)}\,G}g^{-j-1}\end{pmatrix}+\Phi_{0},

where
U
is a unitary matrix,
G
satisfies
-i\overline{g}\nabla g=\nabla^{\perp}G
,
\Phi_{0}
is a constant vector and
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}\lvert a_{j}\rvert^{2}<\infty
.

We say that
\Phi
has a
Schoen-Wolfson singularity of type
\Sigma_{j,j+1}
at
p
if
a_{j}\neq 0
and
a_{k}=0
for all
k<j
.

3.
Proof of the theorems

We first prove Theorem
1.2
. Let
p<2
and
j\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}
. In Step 1 we will construct an holomorphic map
\phi
on
D^{2}
which is smooth up to the boundary and has infinitely many zeros. In Step 2 we will construct, starting from
\phi
, an
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued map
g\in W^{1,p}\cap W_{\text{loc}}^{1,(2,\infty)}(D^{2})
satisfying (
4
). In Step 4 we will construct (again starting from
\phi
) a map
u\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2},\mathbb{C})
whose gradient only vanishes at isolated points in
D^{2}
, and such that its trace on
\partial D^{2}
is smooth. Finally in Step 5 we will show that
u
and
g
satisfy (
5
).

Let
s\in(0,\frac{2}{p}-1)
.
For any
k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}
set
p_{k}:=-1+e^{-k}
. Set

(19)

\displaystyle\phi:=e^{-\frac{1}{(z+1)^{s}}}\prod_{k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{z-p_{k}}{1-p_{k}z}\right)

in
D^{2}
, where
z^{s}
denotes the branch of the
s
-power with branch cut
(-\infty,0)
and equal to the real
s
-power on
[0,\infty)
.

Step 1
:

\phi
is a well defined, holomorphic function on
D^{2}
satisfying the following properties:

(1)

the zeros of
\phi
in
D^{2}
are precisely the points
p_{i}
, and all zeros are of order
1
,

(2)

\phi
extends to a continuous function on
\overline{D}^{2}
which is smooth up to the boundary, its trace on
\partial D^{2}
has only one zero at
-1
, which has infinite order.

Proof of Step 1.

First we show that the Blaschke product

(20)

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}:=\prod_{k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{z-p_{k}}{1-p_{k}z}\right)

defines a holomorphic function in
D^{2}
:
for any
z\in D^{2}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}

(21)

\displaystyle 1-\frac{z-p_{k}}{1-p_{k}z}=e^{-k}\frac{1-z}{1+z-e^{-k}z}.

Thus the series

(22)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}\left\lvert 1-\frac{z-p_{k}}{1-p_{k}z}\right\rvert

converges uniformly on compact subsets of
D^{2}
. We deduce that
\mathscr{P}
defines an holomorphic function on
D^{2}
with zeros of order one at the points
p_{k}
(see for instance Theorem 15.6 in
[
7
]
). Since
e^{-(z+1)^{-s}}
is holomorphic and different from zero in
D^{2}
, we conclude that the same properties hold for
\phi
. Notice also that the argument above shows that
\phi
can be extended to an analytic function in a neighbourhood of any point of
\partial D^{2}
but
-1
, so that
\phi
is smooth up to the boundary away from
-1
, and its extension on
\partial D^{2}\smallsetminus\{-1\}
is nowhere zero. On the other hand, since
\lvert\mathscr{P}\rvert\leq 1
on
D^{2}
, we have

(23)

\displaystyle\lvert\phi(z)\rvert\leq\left\lvert\exp\left(-(z+1)^{-s}\right)\right\rvert\leq\exp\left(-\cos\left(s\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\lvert z+1\rvert^{-s}\right),

therefore
\phi
extends continuously on
\overline{D}^{2}
and is equal to zero at
-1
.
We can repeat the same argument for the derivatives of
\phi
: for instance we compute

(24)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\phi=

\displaystyle e^{-(z+1)^{-s}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}\left(\frac{2e^{-k}-e^{-2k}}{(1-p_{k}z)^{2}}\right)\prod_{i\neq k}\left(\frac{z-p_{i}}{1-p_{i}z}\right)

\displaystyle+s(z+1)^{-s-1}e^{-(z+1)^{-s}}\mathscr{P}.

Notice that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for any
z\in D^{2}

there holds

(25)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{e^{-(z+1)^{-s}}}{(1-p_{k}z)^{n}}\right\rvert=

\displaystyle\frac{\lvert e^{-(z+1)^{-s}}\rvert}{\lvert p_{k}\rvert^{n}\lvert z+(1-e^{-k})^{-1}\rvert^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(-\cos\left(s\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\lvert z+1\rvert^{-s}\right)}{\lvert p_{k}\rvert^{n}\lvert z+1\rvert^{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(s,n)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\cos\left(s\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\lvert z+1\rvert^{-s}\right).

Therefore
\lvert\partial_{z}\phi\rvert
tends to zero as
z
approaches
-1
, so that
\phi
extends to a
C^{1}
function in
\overline{D}^{2}
. A similar argument can be performed for any derivative of
\phi
, showing that
\phi
is smooth up to the boundary and that
\phi
has a zero of infinite order at
-1
.
∎

Let’s define the following functions on
\overline{D}^{2}
:

(26)

\displaystyle\rho:=\lvert\phi\rvert;\quad g:=\frac{\phi}{\lvert\phi\rvert}.

Step 2
:
g
belongs to
W^{1,(2,\infty)}_{\text{loc}}(D^{2})
and to
W^{1,p}(D^{2})
, and satisfies

(27)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(-i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{div}(-i\overline{g}\nabla^{\perp}g)=-2\pi\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\delta_{p_{k}}\text{ in }D^{2}.

Proof of Step 2.

First notice that
\rho
and
g
are smooth away from the zeros of
\phi
. Around any zero
p_{k}
of
\phi
we can write
\phi(z)=(z-p_{k})h(z)
for some holomorphic
h
not vanishing at
p_{k}
. Thus

(28)

\displaystyle g(z)=\frac{z-p_{k}}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert}\frac{h(x)}{\lvert h(z)\rvert}

and

(29)

\displaystyle\log\rho(z)=\log\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert+\log\lvert h(z)\rvert

around
p_{k}
. Since
\frac{h(x)}{\lvert h(z)\rvert}
and
\log\lvert h(z)\rvert
are smooth around
p_{k}
we conclude that
g
and
\rho
belong to
W^{1,(2,\infty)}_{\text{loc}}(D^{2})
.

As
\phi
is holomorphic, by the Cauchy-Riemann equations there holds

(30)

\displaystyle-i\overline{g}\nabla g=\nabla^{\perp}\log\rho.

In particular we have

(31)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(-i\overline{g}\nabla g)=\operatorname{div}(\nabla^{\perp}\log\rho)=0.

To deduce the second identity in (
27
), notice that locally away from the singularities
g
can be written as
g=e^{i\beta}
for some harmonic function
\beta
. Thus

(32)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(-i\overline{g}\nabla^{\perp}g)=\operatorname{div}(\nabla^{\perp}\beta)=0

away from the zeros of
\phi
.
Around any zero
p_{k}
of
\phi
, writing again
\rho(z)=\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert\lvert h(z)\rvert
for
h
holomorphic with
h(p_{k})\neq 0
, we get

(33)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(-i\overline{g}\nabla^{\perp}g)(z)=-\operatorname{div}(\nabla\log\rho)(z)=-\Delta\log\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert-\Delta\log\lvert h(z)\rvert

around
p_{k}
. As
h
is holomorphic and different from zero,
\log\lvert h\rvert
is harmonic around
p_{k}
, therefore

(34)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(-i\overline{g}\nabla^{\perp}g)=-\Delta\log\lvert z-p_{i}\rvert=-2\pi\delta_{p_{k}}

around
p_{k}
. This completes the proof of (
27
).

Next we show that
g\in W^{1,p}(D^{2})
: observe that

(35)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla\left(\frac{\mathscr{P}}{\lvert\mathscr{P}\rvert}\right)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}}\left\lvert\frac{z-p_{k}}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert^{2}}-\frac{z-\frac{1}{p_{k}}}{\lvert z-\frac{1}{p_{k}}\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert

and that for any
i\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}

(36)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\frac{z-p_{k}}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert^{2}}-\frac{z-\frac{1}{p_{k}}}{\lvert z-\frac{1}{p_{k}}\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert\leq 3\frac{\lvert p_{k}-\frac{1}{p_{k}}\rvert}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert^{2}}

for any
z\in D^{2}
.
A direct computation (considering separately the integral over
B_{2e^{-k}}(-1)\cap D^{2}
and the one over
B_{2e^{-k}}(-1)^{c}\cap D^{2}
, where we use (
36
)) shows that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}

(37)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\left\lvert\frac{z-p_{k}}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert^{2}}-\frac{z-\frac{1}{p_{k}}}{\lvert z-\frac{1}{p_{k}}\rvert^{2}}\right\rvert^{p}\leq 16e^{-(2-p)k}.

Therefore
\frac{\mathscr{P}}{\lvert\mathscr{P}\rvert}\in W^{1,p}(D^{2})
.

On the other hand

(38)

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla\left(\frac{\exp(-(z+1)^{-s})}{\lvert\exp(-(z+1)^{-s})\rvert}\right)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\nabla\exp(-i\Im(z+1)^{-s})\right\rvert=\lvert\nabla\Im(z+1)^{-s}\rvert\leq s\lvert z+1\rvert^{-1-s},

so that

(39)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\left\lvert\nabla\left(\frac{\exp((-z+1)^{-s})}{\lvert\exp(-(z+1)^{-s})\rvert}\right)\right\rvert^{p}\leq s^{p}\int_{B_{2}(-1)}\lvert z+1\rvert^{-p(1+s)}=s^{p}\frac{2^{2-p(1+s)}}{2-p(1+s)}

(notice that
2-p(1+s)>0
). We conclude that

(40)

\displaystyle g=\frac{\mathscr{P}}{\lvert\mathscr{P}\rvert}\frac{\exp(-(z+1)^{-s})}{\lvert\exp(-(z+1)^{-s})\rvert}\in W^{1,p}(D^{2}).

∎

Step 3
: The functions
\rho
and
\rho^{\delta}g
are smooth on
\partial D^{2}
for any
\delta>0
.

Proof of Step 3.

Both the angular and radial parts of
e^{(z+1)^{-s}}
are clearly smooth and different from zero on
\partial D^{2}
away from
-1
. Near
-1
we have

(41)

\displaystyle\exp(-(z+1)^{-s})=\exp(-\lvert z+1\rvert^{-s}e^{-is\varphi}),

where

(42)

\displaystyle\varphi=\begin{cases}\frac{\theta}{2}&\text{ if }\theta\in(\frac{\pi}{2},\pi)\\
\frac{\theta}{2}-\pi&\text{ if }\theta\in(\pi,\frac{3}{2}\pi).\end{cases}

Since
\lvert\mathscr{P}\rvert=1
on
\partial D^{2}
, near
-1
we have (for
0\leq\theta<\pi
, the other case being analogous)

(43)

\displaystyle\rho(e^{i\theta})=\exp\left(-(2+2\cos\theta)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\cos\left(\frac{s\theta}{2}\right)\right)

and

(44)

\displaystyle\rho^{\delta}g(e^{i\theta})=\mathscr{P}\exp\left(i(2+2\cos\theta)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\sin\left(s\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\right)\exp\left(-\delta(2+2\cos\theta)^{-\frac{s}{2}}\cos\left(\frac{s\theta}{2}\right)\right).

Arguing as in Step 1 we see that
\rho|_{\partial D^{2}}
and
\rho^{\delta}g|_{\partial D^{2}}
are smooth also around
-1
.
∎

Step 4
: Set

(45)

\displaystyle u:=\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-j}\phi^{j}

Then
u\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2})
and away from the points
p_{i}
,
u
is smooth and
\nabla u
vanishes at isolated points.
Moreover
u
extends to a continuous map on
\overline{D^{2}}
and its trace on
\partial D^{2}
is smooth.

Proof of Step 4.

Notice first that the map

(46)

\displaystyle\mu_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad x\mapsto\lvert x\rvert^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-j}x^{j}

belongs to
C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Since
\phi
is smooth up to the boundary, we have
u=\mu_{j}\circ\phi\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2})
. As
\mu_{j}
is smooth away from zero and
\phi
is holomorphic,
u
is smooth away from the points
p_{i}
.
Moreover we notice that away from the points
p_{i}
,
\nabla u
vanishes if and only if
\nabla\phi
vanishes. As
\phi
is holomorphic and non-constant, its gradient only vanishes at isolated points, therefore the same holds for
u
.

Finally we notice that by Step 3,
u=\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-j}g^{j}
is smooth on
\partial D^{2}
.
∎

Step 5
: There holds

(47)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)=0\text{ in }D^{2}.

Proof of Step 5.

Let
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D^{2},\mathbb{C})
. As the points
p_{k}
are isolated in
D^{2}
, there are only finitely many of them in
\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)
. Let
I\subset\mathbb{N}
be the finite index set of the points
p_{k}
in
\operatorname{supp}(\varphi)
. As
u\in W^{1,2}(D^{2})
there holds

(48)

\displaystyle\langle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u),\varphi\rangle=

\displaystyle-\int_{D^{2}}g\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi=-\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{D^{2}\smallsetminus\bigcup_{k\in I}B_{\varepsilon}(p_{k})}g\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\bigcup_{k\in I}\partial B_{\varepsilon}(p_{k})}g\partial_{\nu}u\varphi+\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{D^{2}\smallsetminus\bigcup_{k\in I}B_{\varepsilon}(p_{k})}\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)\varphi.

Here
\nu
denotes the unit outward pointing vector of
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(p_{k})
.
Recall that around any
p_{k}
we have
u(z)=(z-p_{k})^{j}\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-j}w(z)
for some smooth
w
with
w(p_{k})\neq 0
.
Thus for any
\varepsilon>0
, on
\partial B_{\varepsilon}(p_{k})
we have

(49)

\displaystyle\partial_{\nu}u=\sqrt{j^{2}+j}\left(\frac{z-p_{k}}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert}\right)^{j}\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-1}w(z)+\left(\frac{z-p_{k}}{\lvert z-p_{k}\rvert}\right)^{j}\lvert z-p_{i}\rvert^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\partial_{\nu}w(z),

so that

(50)

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(p_{i})}g\partial_{\nu}u\varphi=0

and the first term on the right hand side of (
48
) vanishes. To evaluate the second term on the right hand side of (
48
) we compute, away from the points
p_{i}
,

(51)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)=

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(g^{j+1}\nabla\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}+j\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}g^{j}\nabla g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(j+1)\sqrt{j^{2}+j}\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-1}g^{j}\nabla g\cdot\nabla\rho+g^{j+1}\Delta\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}

\displaystyle+j\sqrt{j^{2}+j}\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-1}\nabla\rho\cdot g^{j}\nabla g+j\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\operatorname{div}(g^{j}\nabla g).

Notice that since
\nabla g\cdot\nabla\rho=0
, the first and the third terms vanish.
We have

(52)

\displaystyle g^{j+1}\Delta\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}=

\displaystyle g^{j+1}\operatorname{div}(\sqrt{j^{2}+j}\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\nabla\log\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(j^{2}+j)g^{j+1}\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\lvert\nabla\log\rho\rvert^{2}+\sqrt{j^{2}+j}g^{j+1}\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\Delta\log\rho.

The second term vanishes because
\log\rho
is harmonic away from the points
p_{k}
(since
\phi
is holomorphic).
On the other hand, since
\operatorname{div}(\overline{g}\nabla g)=0
we have

(53)

\displaystyle j\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\operatorname{div}(g^{j}\nabla g)=

\displaystyle j\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}\operatorname{div}(g^{j+1}\overline{g}\nabla g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-j(j+1)\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}g^{j+1}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)\cdot(i\overline{g}\nabla g)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(j^{2}+j)\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}g^{j+1}\lvert\nabla\log\rho\rvert,

where we used that by (
30
)
\lvert i\overline{g}\nabla g\rvert=\lvert\nabla\log\rho\rvert
.
Combining (
51
), (
52
) and (
53
) we
see that
\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)=0
away from the points
p_{k}
. Thus
(
48
) implies that
\langle\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u),\varphi\rangle=0
. As this holds for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(D^{2})
, the claim follows.
∎

This concludes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
Next we apply Theorem
1.2
to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
p<2
and
j\in\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus\{0\}
and let
g
and
u
be as in Theorem
1.2
. Since
\operatorname{div}(g\nabla u)=0
, there exists
v\in W^{1,\infty}(D^{2},\mathbb{C})
such that
\nabla^{\perp}v=g\nabla u
.
Set

(54)

\displaystyle\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
-\overline{v}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\Phi
belongs to
W^{1,\infty}(D^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})
and it is smooth away from the points
p_{k}
.
Recall that we are identifying
\mathbb{C}^{2}
with the algebra of quaternions with basis elements
1,I,J,K
. Then the identity
\nabla^{\perp}v=g\nabla u
implies that

(55)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Phi=\overline{g}J\partial_{y}\Phi.

We deduce that
\Phi
is weakly conformal (as
\langle\partial_{x}\Phi,\partial_{y}\Phi\rangle=0
and
\lvert\partial_{x}\Phi\rvert^{2}=\lvert\partial_{y}\Phi\rvert^{2}
a.e.) and Lagrangian (as
\langle\partial_{x}\Phi,I\partial_{y}\Phi\rangle=0
a.e.).

We claim that the Lagrangian angle of
\Phi
is given by
\overline{g}
. With the help of (
9
) we compute (for any point where
\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert\neq 0)

(56)

\displaystyle e^{-2\lambda}\det[\partial_{x}\Phi,\partial_{y}\Phi]=

\displaystyle e^{-2\lambda}(-\partial_{x}u\partial_{y}\overline{v}+\partial_{x}\overline{v}\partial_{y}u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-2\lambda}\left(\partial_{x}u\overline{g}\partial_{x}\overline{u}+\overline{g}\partial_{y}\overline{u}\partial_{y}u\right)=\overline{g},

where
e^{2\lambda}=\lvert\partial_{x}\Phi\rvert^{2}=\lvert\partial_{y}\Phi\rvert^{2}=2\lvert\partial_{x}u\rvert^{2}=2\lvert\partial_{y}u\rvert^{2}
.
Since
\overline{g}
satisfies
\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0
,
we conclude that
\Phi
is Hamiltonian stationary.

As the gradient of the map
u
only vanishes at isolated points, the same holds for
\Phi
. Since the Lagrangian angle is continuous away from the points
p_{k}
, we conclude that
\Phi
is a branched immersion away from the points
p_{k}
.
Next we observe that the map
v
, determined up to an additive constant, can be chosen to be

(57)

\displaystyle v=i\frac{\sqrt{j}}{\sqrt{j+1}}\rho^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}g^{j+1}=i\frac{\sqrt{j}}{\sqrt{j+1}}\nu_{j}\circ\phi,

where

(58)

\displaystyle\nu_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2},\quad x\mapsto\lvert x\rvert^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}-(j+1)}x^{j+1}.

Observe that
\nu_{j}\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. As
\phi
is smooth up to the boundary,
v\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2})
. Since
u
also lies in
C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2})
we conclude that
\Phi\in C^{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}(D^{2})
.
Moreover Step 4 in the proof of Theorem
1.2
implies that
\rho^{\frac{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}{j}}g
is smooth on
\partial D^{2}
, therefore also
v=i\sqrt{\frac{j}{j+1}}(\rho^{\frac{\sqrt{j^{2}+j}}{j}}g)^{j}
is smooth on
\partial D^{2}
. Since also
u
has a smooth trace on
\partial D^{2}
, we conclude that
\Phi
has a smooth trace on
\partial D^{2}
.

Finally, comparing the explicit expression for
\Phi
(given by (
45
) and (
57
)) with (
18
), we see that at any point
p_{k}
,
\Phi
has a singularity of type
\Sigma_{j,j+1}
.
∎

4.
Related questions

In this section we discuss condition (
8
) and how it relates to our construction.

First we recall that if a map
g\in W^{1,p}(D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1})
for some
p>1
satisfies

(59)

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}(i\overline{g}\nabla g)=0,

then there exists
G\in W^{1,p}(D^{2},\mathbb{R})
such that

(60)

\displaystyle-i\overline{g}\nabla g=\nabla^{\perp}G.

The condition

i\overline{g}\partial_{\nu}g=0
on
\partial D^{2}
can be given the following meaning: we require the map
G
to have constant trace on
\partial D^{2}
.

As observed in
[
8
]
, this condition arises naturally in Lagrangian free-boundary problems where the constraint manifold is a complex surface (see also Remark IV.4 in
[
4
]
).
Originally we were hoping to obtain a map as in Theorem
1.1
satisfying in addition
\overline{g}\partial_{\nu}g=0
on
\partial D^{2}
in the sense specified above.
Unfortunately our method does not allow to obtain such an example.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
in Section 3, in fact, is based on the construction of a non-zero holomorphic function
\phi
on
D^{2}
with infinitely many zeros and finite Dirichlet energy. The
\mathbb{S}^{1}
-valued map
g
is then obtained as
g=\frac{\phi}{\lvert\phi\rvert}
, so that the function
G
appearing in (
60
) can be chosen to be
\log\lvert\phi\rvert
. To get an example satisfying
\overline{g}\partial_{\nu}g=0
,
we would need to have
\lvert\phi\rvert
constant on
\partial D^{2}
, but this is not possible, because of the next lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

If an holomorphic map
\phi
on
D^{2}
satisfies the following properties

(1)

\phi
has infinitely many zeros,

(2)

\displaystyle\int_{D^{2}}\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert^{2}<\infty
,

(3)

\lvert\phi\rvert
is constant on
\partial D^{2}
,

then
\phi\equiv 0
.

Lemma
4.1
is a consequence of the following result, based on
[
9
]
, Section 4, and whose idea has been widely used in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\psi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1})
and let
\phi
be its harmonic
extension in
D^{2}
. Then
\lvert\phi(z)\rvert\to 1
uniformly as
\lvert z\rvert\to 1
.

Proof.

Since
{\phi}
is harmonic,
\phi(r,\theta)=P_{r}\ast\psi(\theta)
, where for any
r\in(0,1)
the Poisson kernel
P_{r}
is given by

(61)

\displaystyle P_{r}(\theta)=\frac{1-r^{2}}{2\lvert re^{i\theta}-1\rvert^{2}}.

Let
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
,
r\in(0,1)
. We estimate (exploiting the fact that
\lvert\phi\rvert=1
on
\partial D^{2}
)

(62)

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}^{2}(\phi(r,\theta),\mathbb{S}^{1})\leq

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\lvert P_{r}\ast\psi(\theta)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\left\lvert\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{r}(\alpha-\theta)(\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma))d\alpha\right\rvert^{2}d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{r}(\alpha-\theta)\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}d\alpha d\sigma,

where
B_{1-r}(\theta)
denotes a ball in
\mathbb{R}
.

Notice that
1-r<\lvert re^{i\varphi}-1\rvert
for any
\varphi
, therefore
\lvert e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\sigma}\rvert^{2}<9\lvert re^{i(\alpha-\sigma)}-1\rvert^{2}
if
\sigma\in B_{1-r}(\theta)
,
\alpha\in B_{2(1-r)}(\theta)
. Thus

(63)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{B_{2(1-r)}(\theta)}\lvert P_{r}(\alpha-\theta)(\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}d\alpha d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{9}{2}(1-r^{2})\fint_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{B_{2(1-r)}(\theta)}\frac{\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\sigma}\rvert^{2}}d\alpha d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{9}{4}(1+r)\int_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{B_{2(1-r)}(\theta)}\frac{\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\sigma}\rvert^{2}}d\alpha d\sigma.

On the other hand
there holds
r\lvert e^{i\varphi}-1\rvert\leq\lvert re^{i\varphi}-1\rvert
for any
\varphi
, therefore

(64)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{B_{2(1-r)}^{c}(\theta)}P_{r}(\alpha-\theta)\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}d\alpha d\sigma

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1-r^{2}}{8r^{2}(1-r)}\int_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{B_{2(1-r)}^{c}(\theta)}\frac{\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert e^{i(\alpha-\theta)}-1\rvert^{2}}d\sigma d\alpha

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1+r}{8r^{2}}\int_{B_{1-r}(\theta)}\int_{B_{2(1-r)}^{c}(\theta)}\frac{\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\sigma}\rvert^{2}}d\alpha d\sigma,

where
B_{2(1-r)}^{c}(\theta)
denotes the complement of
B_{2(1-r)}(\theta)
in
{{}^{\textstyle\mathbb{R}}\big/_{\textstyle 2\pi\mathbb{Z}}}
, and in the last step we used the fact that if
\alpha\in B_{2(1-r)}^{c}(\theta)
and
\sigma\in B_{1-r}(\theta)
, then

(65)

\displaystyle\lvert e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\sigma}\rvert\leq\lvert\alpha-\sigma\rvert\leq\frac{3}{2}\lvert\alpha-\theta\rvert\leq\frac{3\pi}{4}\lvert e^{i(\alpha-\theta)}-1\rvert.

Notice that the expressions in (
63
) and (
64
) tend to zero uniformly as
r
approaches 1, in fact
\frac{\lvert\psi(\alpha)-\psi(\sigma)\rvert^{2}}{\lvert e^{i\alpha}-e^{i\sigma}\rvert^{2}}
lies in
L^{1}(\partial D^{2}\times\partial D^{2})
and thus is uniformly integrable, as
\psi\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D^{2})
. Therefore (
62
) implies that
\operatorname{dist}(\phi(r,\theta),\mathbb{S}^{1})
tends to zero uniformly (as a function of
\theta
) as
r
tends to
1
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Assume by contradiction that
\phi
is a non-zero holomorphic map on
D^{2}
satisfying (1), (2) and (3).
Multiplying
\phi
by a constant we may assume that
\lvert\phi\rvert\equiv 1
on
\partial D^{2}
. Since
\phi\in W^{1,2}(D^{2})
, its trace on
\partial D^{2}
lies in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D^{2})
and therefore defines a map in
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial D^{2},\mathbb{S}^{1})
as in Lemma
4.2
. We deduce that there exists some
r\in(0,1)
such that
\lvert\phi\rvert>\frac{1}{2}
in
D^{2}\smallsetminus B_{r}(0)
, thus all the zeros of
\phi
must lie in
B_{r}(0)
. But since
\phi
is holomorphic in
D^{2}
, its zeros are isolated (they can only accumulate at the boundary of
D^{2}
), therefore
\phi
can only have finitely many zeros in
\overline{B_{r}(0)}
. This contradicts property (1).
∎
