Uniqueness for Riccati equations with unbounded operator coefficients
Paolo Acquistapace, Francesca Bucci

Abstract
. In this article we address the issue of uniqueness for differential and algebraic operator Riccati equations, under a distinctive set of assumptions on their unbounded coefficients. The class of boundary control systems characterized by these assumptions encompasses diverse significant physical interactions, all modeled by systems of coupled hyperbolic/parabolic partial differential equations. The proofs of uniqueness provided tackle and overcome the obstacles raised by the peculiar regularity properties of the composite dynamics. These results supplement the theories of the finite and infinite time horizon linear-quadratic problem devised by the authors jointly with Lasiecka, as the unique solution to the Riccati equation enters the closed loop form of the optimal control.



1.
Introduction

Well-posedness of Riccati equations is a fundamental question within control theory of Partial Differential Equations (PDE).
While the issues of existence and uniqueness for the corresponding solutions are both natural to be
addressed and significant in themselves, when seen in the context of linear-quadratic optimal control uniqueness proves particularly relevant, not exclusively from a theoretical perspective.
This is because it brings about
in a univocal manner
the (optimal cost, or Riccati) operator which occurs in the feedback representation of the optimal control, thereby allowing its synthesis.

In the present work focus is on the differential and algebraic Riccati equations
arising from optimal control problems with quadratic functionals for the class of infinite dimensional
abstract control systems dealt with in our earlier works
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
,
joint with Lasiecka.
The basic characteristics of these linear systems – which read as
y^{\prime}=Ay+Bu
, according to a standard notation – are the following: the free dynamics operator
A
is the infinitesimal generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup
\{e^{At}\}_{t\geq 0}
on the state space
Y
, while the control operator
B
is
unbounded
,
meaning that
B
maps continuously the control space
U
into a larger functional space than
Y
,
that is the extrapolation space
[{\mathcal{D}}(A^{*})]^{\prime}
; see the Assumptions
2.1
.
It is well known that the latter is an intrinsic feature of differential systems describing evolutionary PDE with boundary (and also point) control, already recognized by the end of the sixties in the pioneering work of Fattorini
[
18
]
.

We require in addition and more specifically that several assumptions on the operators
A,B
are fulfilled,
recorded in Section
2
as Assumptions
2.4
.
These are regularity properties that pertain to the operator
B^{*}e^{A^{*}t}
, with
respective PDE counterparts.
It is worth emphasizing here that not only the aforesaid control-theoretic properties do not prescribe analyticity of the semigroup
e^{At}
, in accordance with the fact that the class of systems under consideration – introduced by these authors with Lasiecka in
[
1
]
–
is inspired by and tailored on systems of coupled hyperbolic-parabolic PDE, subjected to
boundary/interface control.
They are also weaker (and somewhat trickier) than the full
singular estimates
for
e^{At}B
which are known to be equally effective for the study of the quadratic optimal control problem on both a finite and infinite time horizon, as proved in
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
25
,
26
]
.
In this respect, we remark that it was first discovered in
[
10
]
that a very same
thermoelastic system may or may not yield a singular estimate for the corresponding operator
e^{At}B
, depending on which
combination of boundary conditions/actions (e.g., clamped/Neumann
vs
clamped/Dirichlet)
is taken into consideration.

Another distinguishing feature of the coefficients of the Riccati equations under study,
which when the unknown
P
is time-independent read as

(1.1)

(Px,Az)_{Y}+(Ax,Pz)_{Y}-(B^{*}Px,B^{*}Pz)_{U}+(Rx,Rz)_{Z}=0\,,\quad x,z\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)\,,

is that
R
– that is the
observation
operator in the optimization problem – does not need to be smoothing; see (
2.7
), that is iiib) of the Assumptions
2.4
.

Working in the framework described above, a theory for both the finite and infinite time horizon
LQ-problem has been devised in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
, the latter under the Assumptions
2.9
(replacing Assumptions
2.4
).
The strenght of these theories is confirmed by the trace regularity results that have been established for the solutions to significant PDE systems comprising hyperbolic and parabolic components, over the years.
Indeed, the novel class introduced in
[
1
]
has proven successful in describing a diverse range of physical interactions such as mechanical-thermal, acoustic-structure, fluid-elasticity ones.
And above all, successful in order to attain solvability of the associated optimization problems;
see
[
2
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
11
,
12
]
,

[
3
]
, and the recent
[
9
]
.

We recall that since in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
we followed a variational approach,
by using the optimality conditions a bounded operator – to wit,
P(t)
or
P
, in accordance with
either a finite or infinite time interval – is constructed in terms of the optimal state and only subsequently shown to satisfy the corresponding Riccati equations.
For this reason the works
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
provide existence for Riccati equations, but not uniqueness.

By contrast and as it is well-known, in a (sometimes called) ‘direct’ approach,
the well-posedness of the nonlinear Riccati equation is studied in a first step, independently from the minimization problem.
For the Cauchy problems associated with the differential Riccati equations,
uniqueness is established along with existence,
as joint outcomes of a method of proof based on fixed point theorems.
Then, in order to achieve the actual feedback representation of the optimal control, that is the ultimate goal from a mathematical as well as a practical perspective, further steps are necessary.
(This is not the case here.)

Thus, our aim with the present work is to provide
uniqueness
results for both the differential and algebraic Riccati equations, thereby completing the complex of findings of
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
, respectively.
These results – our main results – are stated as Theorems
2.7
and
2.11
.
Uniqueness holds in appropriate (respective) classes of linear bounded operators, which are consistent
with the distinctive property of the
gain operator
emerged in
[
1
,
3
]
;
see the statements S4. and A4. in Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.10
, respectively.

We feel it is important to emphasize that although often following approaches and arguments previously used in the past literature, our proofs of uniqueness need to face novel challenges.
This is due to the specific assumptions on the operator coefficients of the Riccati equations,
notably weaker than the ones which characterize the parabolic class (and its generalization),
somewhat a little bit stronger than mere admissibility which is typical of the hyperbolic class,
yet not requiring smoothness of the observations.

We find it useful to devote a separate section to several comments and points concerning our analysis.

1.1.
An insight into the mathematical proofs

We provide two proofs of Theorem
2.7
, that pertains to uniqueness for the
differential Riccati equations (
2.13
) (DRE, in short).
This result is relevant for the optimal control problem on a finite time horizon (i.e. Problem
2.3
with
T<+\infty
), under the Assumptions
2.4
.

The first proof, given in Section
3
, follows the method employed
by Lasiecka and Triggiani in
[
27
, Vol. I, Theorem 1.5.3.3; Vol. II, Theorems 8.3.7.1]
, up to a certain point.
The basic rationale is standard: one proceeds by contradiction, assuming there exists another solution

P_{1}(t)
to the DRE, besides the optimal cost operator
P(t)
.
(In our case, that
P(t)
solves the DRE has been proved in
[
1
]
; see the statement S6. of
Theorem
2.6
.)
On the basis of the integral form of the DRE – in the present case two forms, derived in Lemma
3.1
–, one finds that the difference
Q(t)=P_{1}(t)-P(t)
solves a suitable integral equation.
It is in the estimates performed afterwards, that the paths diverge, with iiic) of the
Assumptions
2.4
playing a major role here, together with the class of operators

P(t)
and
P_{1}(t)
belong to.
The proofs carried out in the aforementioned results instead take advantage of either the enhanced regularity of the analytic semigroup
e^{At}
that describes the free dynamics, or the additional regularity assumed on
the operators
Re^{At}
and
Re^{At}B
.

It is unlikely that the method of proof described above could be adjusted in order to establish uniqueness
for the algebraic Riccati equation, relevant for Problem
2.3
with
T=+\infty
.
This owing to the argument employed when
T<+\infty
:
Q(t)
is shown to be zero on some subinterval
of
[0,T]
, with the soughtafter goal attained in a finite number of steps.
Other methods of proof are certainly worth to be explored.
One might attempt to proceed along the lines of the proof of
[
27
, Vol. I, Theorem 2.4.5]
, despite the absence of analyticity of the semigroup
e^{At}
, as well as of the optimal state semigroup

\Phi(t)
.
If this were the case, a preliminary analysis which appears unavoidable would pertain to issues
connected to a given solution
P_{1}
to the ARE (a priori, distinct from the optimal cost operator
P
),
such as the possible generation of a
C_{0}
-semigroup on
Y
by the operator
A-BB^{*}P_{1}
,
in turn to be suitably defined.
It is unclear in this respect whether a
full
description of the domain of the generator itself
A_{P}

of
\Phi(t)
, would be required.
Improving the statement A5. of Theorem
2.10
is an independent interesting subtlety;
in any case, we leave this question open.

Thus, in order to prove Theorem
2.11
we choose to return to
the dynamic programming approach to the LQ-problem, and borrow from it a key element in attaining
that the optimal control admits a (pointwise in time) feedback representation.
This element is fulfilled by the so called
fundamental identity
.
In a direct approach, the fundamental identity builds a bridge between the nonlinear Riccati equation – whose well-posedness is studied in a first step, independently from the minimization problem, as recalled above – and the actual closed loop form of the optimal control.
The latter goal (i.e. the feedback representation of the optimal control) was already attained in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
; see the statements
S4. of in Theorem
2.6
and A4. of Theorem
2.10
, respectively.
And yet, the identities we establish in Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.3

constitute a major (and technically nontrivial) step in our analysis, allowing to achieve uniqueness for both differential and algebraic Riccati equations, respectively.
Theorem
2.7
and Theorem
2.11
are thus established,
via methods of proof which are akin to each other.

1.2.
Riccati equations: a historical synopsis, and a few open questions

Historically, the appearance of
matrix
Riccati equations – named after Jacopo Riccati (XVII century) – as a research subject is recognized to date back to the sixties, with the independent contributions of Kalman
[
20
]
in the USA and of Letov
[
30
]
in the former Soviet Union.
Their study has already lasted for more than half a century, owing to its connections to a wide variety of topics such as stability and stabilization problems, the linear-quadratic (LQ) optimal control problem, differential games, just to name a few.
We refer the reader to the introductory monograph
[
38
]
, along with its references.

We begin by briefly mentioning a broader problem,
and then we focus on well-posedness of Riccati equations with unbounded operator coefficients.
We make an attempt to retrace the main stages of its evolution, which spans over decades, thereby
arriving at the problem at hand.

1.2.1.
The non-standard LQR-problem, matrix/operator inequalities

A related significant problem which sees its development around the same years is the one referred
to in the literature as the
non-standard
(linear) quadratic regulator problem.
Associated with the Cauchy problems for the linear control system
y^{\prime}=Ay+Bu
is a quadratic
functional, which may not be coercive;
it is indeed the integral of a general quadratic form

F(y,u)=\langle Qy,y\rangle+2\text{Re}\langle Su,y\rangle+\langle Ru,u\rangle\,,

where for instance
Q
is allowed to be negative definite.
The goal, in short, is then to characterize – given an arbitrary initial state
y_{0}
– the condition

\inf_{u\in L^{2}(0,T;U)}\int_{0}^{T}F(y(t),u(t))\,dt>-\infty\,,

where
y(\cdot)
is the solution to the Cauchy problem corresponding to
y_{0}
and to the control function

u(\cdot)
.
As it is well known, one is led to solving matrix/operator
inequalities
, rather than
(matrix/operator) Riccati
equations
;
in addition, when
T=+\infty
the analysis moves from the
time domain
to the
frequency domain
.

It is far beyond the scope of this article to go into any detail of this problem, whose key result is
the celebrated Kalman-Popov-Yakubovich Lemma.
However, we find it fitting to include in this synopsis a mention of the above described scenario, owing to the variety of its motivations and applications – some of them dating back to much earlier times than the sixties –, as well as of the extensions of Yakubovich’s results to the PDE realm that have been attained in the nineties.
We refer the interested reader to
[
33
]
and
[
14
]
, which include historical comments and pertinent references.

1.2.2.
Operator Riccati equations

During the seventies, the theories of the LQ-problem and of Riccati equations is extended to the infinite dimensional setting.
The abstract formulation of initial-boundary value problems (IBVP) for PDE in bounded domains, in the presence of
distributed
control,
brings about control systems of the usual form
y^{\prime}=Ay+Bu
, where

A
is the infinitesimal generator of a
C_{0}
-semigroup in a certain Banach space
Y
, while

B
is a
bounded
operator from a (control) space
U
to
Y
; see
[
7
]
.
(The same framework encompasses also other kinds of differential problems such as, e.g., delay systems.)
The first contributions to the study of Riccati equations with unbounded operator coefficients
(still, with
B
bounded) are due to the works of Da Prato
[
16
]
, Tartar,
Curtain and Pritchard, Zabczyk; see
[
38
, Part IV, Ch. 4]
and
[
7
]
.

1.2.3.
Riccati equations with unbounded
B

The proof of well-posedness performed by Da Prato in
[
16
]
paves the way for subsequent direct studies of Riccati equations with an
unbounded
(control) operator
B
.
These extensions were initially focused on mixed problems for
parabolic
PDE.
Hence, they could exploit the regularity properties brought about by the analytic semigroup that describes the free dynamics.
Although well known to those acquainted with the subject, it is important to emphasize
that the transition to infinite-dimensional systems describing PDE
with
boundary
or
point
control (as opposed to PDE with
distributed
control),
has drastically impacted on the mathematical analysis of the corresponding Riccati equations.
Whatever approach and method of proof is chosen, the actual meaning of the term
B^{*}P

which appears in the quadratic term of the equation – or lack thereof –
is the central issue that must be tackled.
It is here that the parabolic and the hyperbolic analyses split apart.

Even more notably, when a Riccati theory
in connection with optimal boundary control of hyperbolic-like PDE is ultimately attained,
greater mathematical challenges come from the trace regularity results that are essential
in order to invoke it, with respective proofs on an
ad hoc
basis.

We refer the reader to
[
24
]
for a useful overview of the main results on the subject
until the early nineties, and to
[
7
]
for a broader picture.
The in-depth monograph
[
27
]
goes into much more detail;
the notes at the end of any chapter provide a roadmap as well as bibliographical information.
Principal contributions to the Riccati theory for parabolic (and parabolic-like) PDEs are
due to Balakrishnan (1977), Lasiecka and Triggiani (1983),
Pritchard and Salamon (1984), Flandoli (1984), Da Prato and Ichikawa (1985).
The unifying functional-analytic approach such as the one of Salamon
[
34
]
appears upweighed by
the Riccati theory developed specifically for hyperbolic (hyperbolic-like, including Petrovskiĭ-type) PDE.
We recall explicitly the direct study of differential Riccati equations by Da Prato, Lasiecka and Triggiani

[
17
]
; the most conclusive results on algebraic Riccati equations
are due to Flandoli (1988) and Barbu (2000), both joint with Lasiecka and Triggiani.

1.2.4.
A glimpse of peculiarities and challenges relevant to hyperbolic-like PDE

The hyperbolic character of the dynamics – in the present work, of one component of the coupled PDE system –, combined with the unboundedness of the control operator, can raise obstacles to the well-posedness of the Riccati equations
corresponding to the associated optimal control problems.
It will suffice to highlight that under the
abstract trace regularity
assumption or

admissibility condition

\exists C_{T}>0\colon\quad\int_{0}^{T}\|B^{*}e^{A^{*}t}x\|_{U}^{2}\leq C_{T}\|x\|_{Y}^{2}\qquad\forall x\in Y

which is characteristic of hyperbolic-like dynamics,

•

well-posedness of differential Riccati equations holds true provided the observation operator

R
has appropriate
smoothing properties
;

•

given the optimal cost operator
P
for the infinite time horizon problem, it turns out that the gain operator
B^{*}P
that occurrs in the algebraic Riccati equation may be even
not densely defined
.
(Then, appropriate extensions of
B^{*}P
are called for.)

A simple illustration of the latter weak point is given by Weiss and Zwart
[
37
]
.
This work exhibits a first-order hyperbolic PDE (in one space dimension), with
point
control;
given a certain quadratic functional, the optimal cost operator
P
is computed explicitly,
and then it is shown that
B^{*}
is intrinsically not defined on
Py
,
y\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)
.
In this connection we remark that, to the best of our knowledge, the question as to whether there
actually exist examples of hyperbolic PDE with boundary (rather than point) control which give rise to the same ‘failure’, is open.

We like to point out that in the case of systems of hyperbolic-parabolic PDEs (with boundary control) which fulfil the requirements of our Assumptions
2.4
(or Assumptions
2.9
, when time
varies in the positive half line), the highlighted difficulties are overcome, as the theory of the Riccati
equations devised in
[
1
,
3
]
and in the present work shows.

The (full) Bolza problem – that is a finite time horizon problem where the quadratic functional to be minimized includes a penalization of the state at the final time
T
–
renders the study of well-posedness of Riccati equations more intricate.
A technical discussion of this subject is beyond the scope of this synopsis.
We refer the reader to
[
27
]
and its references.

We just remark that the case of functionals of Bolza type is not addressed in
[
1
]
;
the Bolza problem for the class of systems characterized by the Assumptions
2.4

is indeed open.

1.2.5.
The non-autonomous case

The case of time dependent (operator) coefficients in the Riccati equation is considerably more difficult.
The major contributions, that all pertain to the parabolic case, are due to Acquistapace and Terreni,
in part jointly with Flandoli; see
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
.

1.2.6.
Approaching the present scenario

We turn now our attention to PDE systems comprising two (or more) evolutionary PDE of different type, which further display a
strong coupling
and are subject to boundary/interface/point control actions.
We owe to Avalos and Lasiecka the understanding of the role played by certain (local in time) estimates for the kernel
e^{At}B
for the purpose of boundedness of the gain operator
B^{*}P(t)
, even in the absence of
analyticity of the semigroup
e^{At}
.
It was the theoretical study of the quadratic optimal control problem for a PDE model of an acoustic-structure interaction, successfully carried out in
[
6
]
, to
motivate the subsequent introduction of the class of control systems which yield
the said
singular estimates
.
The theory relative to the corresponding (differential as well as algebraic) Riccati equations is well recognized:
after the former works
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
25
,
26
]
,
a great deal of attention has been devoted to the Bolza problem by Lasiecka and Tuffaha
(see
[
28
]
,
[
29
]
,
[
36
]
).
It should be pointed out that the question of uniqueness of Riccati equations appear to be
left out of the aforementioned works, with the exception of
[
36
]
.

The class of control systems at hand here – introduced by these authors, jointly with Lasiecka, in
[
1
]
– is instead characterized by more elaborate regularity properties of the adjoint operator
B^{*}e^{A^{*}t}
, recorded in the next section as Assumptions
2.4
(these assume the slightly strenghtened form of Assumptions
2.9
for the problem on the infinite time interval).
These assumptions have proved effective in describing significant systems of strongly coupled hyperbolic/parabolic PDE which do not fall in the previous class, as has been pointed out already.
Within this framework, complete theories of the quadratic optimal control problem on a finite and infinite time horizon have been devised in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
, which bring about existence for the corresponding differential and algebraic Riccati equations,
with the distinctive feature that
B^{*}P
is defined on a
dense
set.
An insightful comparison among all the mentioned abstract classes, along with their
respective Riccati theories, is provided by
[
1
, Section 2.1]
.

Thus, focus of the present work is on uniqueness of Riccati equations, an issue which was not discussed and is absent in the aforesaid works
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
.
While benefiting from the already established existence, our proofs of uniqueness tackle (and overcome)
the challenges brought about by the peculiar regularity properties of the composite dynamics,
singled out by the Assumptions
2.4
and
2.9
.

2.
Abstract framework, main results

2.1.
The LQ problem: abstract dynamics and setting

Let
Y
and
U
be two separable Hilbert spaces, the
state
and
control
space, respectively.
We consider the abstract (linear) control system
y^{\prime}=Ay+Bu
and the corresponding Cauchy problem

(2.1)

\begin{cases}y^{\prime}(t)=Ay(t)+Bu(t)\,,&\quad 0\leq t<T\\[5.69054pt]
y(0)=y_{0}\in Y\,,&\end{cases}

under the following basic Assumptions.

Assumptions 2.1

(
Basic Assumptions).

Let
Y
,
U
be separable complex Hilbert spaces.

•

The closed linear operator
A:{\mathcal{D}}(A)\subset Y\to Y
is the infinitesimal generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
\{e^{At}\}_{t\geq 0}
on
Y
;

•

B\in{\mathcal{L}}(U,[{\mathcal{D}}(A^{*})]^{\prime})
.

Remarks 2.2
.

The apparent weakness of the basic assumptions on the operators
A
and
B
which characterize the
initial/boundary value problems described by the abstract equation in (
2.1
)
is a reflection of the modeling of boundary control problems for systems of coupled hyperbolic/parabolic PDEs.
It is worth recalling its most prominent features:
(i) first, the control operator
B
will
not
be bounded from the control space
U
into the state space
Y
; (ii) secondly, the semigroup
e^{At}
will
not
be analytic.

We remind the reader that (i) is intrinsic to the mathematical modeling of control actions on the boundary of the domain (or on some part of it), as first shown in
[
18
]
.
We also note that the presence of control actions concentrated on points (in
1
-D) or on curves (in
2
-D) in the interior of domain results in unboundedness of the control operator as well;
illustrations of both situations are found in
[
24
]
,
[
7
]
and
[
27
]
.
As for the simple requirement in (ii), it is a natural feature of composite dynamics comprising solely a parabolic component: analiticity of the overall semigroup should not be expected.

Thus, given
y_{0}\in Y
, the Cauchy problem (
2.1
) possesses a unique
mild
solution given by

(2.2)

y(t)=e^{At}y_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds\,,\qquad t\in[0,T)\,,

where

L\colon u(\cdot)\longrightarrow(Lu)(t):=\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}Bu(s)\,ds

is the
input-to-state mapping
, that is the operator which associates to any control function

u(\cdot)
the solution to the Cauchy problem (
2.1
) with
y_{0}=0
, and (
2.2
)
makes sense at least in the extrapolation space
[{\mathcal{D}}(A^{*})]^{\prime}
;
see
[
27
, § 0.3, p. 6, and Remark 7.1.2, p. 646]
.

We will use the notation
L
throughout the paper.
We point out here the definition (
A.1
) of the operator
L_{s}
, which will occur
later; the symbol
L_{0}
, in place of
L
, is avoided for the sake of simplicity.

To the state equation (
2.1
) we associate the quadratic functional

(2.3)

J(u)=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\|Ry(t)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(t)\|_{U}^{2}\right)dt\,,

where
Z
is a third separable Hilbert space – the so called observation space (possibly,

Z\equiv Y
) – and at the outset the
observation
operator
R
simply satisfies

(2.4)

R\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y,Z)\,.

The formulation of the optimal control problem under study is classical. The adjectives finite or infinite time horizon problem refer to the cases
T<+\infty
or
T=+\infty
,
respectively.

Problem 2.3

(
The optimal control problem).

Given
y_{0}\in Y
, seek a control function
u\in L^{2}(0,T;U)
which minimizes the
cost functional (
2.3
), where
y(\cdot)=y(\cdot\,;y_{0},u)
is the solution to
(
2.1
) corresponding to the control function
u(\cdot)
(and with initial state
y_{0}
)
given by (
2.2
).

It is well known that aiming at solving Problem
2.3
, certain

3.
A first proof of uniqueness for the DRE

In this Section we derive integral forms of both the differential and algebraic Riccati equations,
and present a first proof of Theorem

The argument employed in this proof is pretty standard: the difference between another possible solution

to the DRE and the Riccati operator

is shown – in a series of steps – to be identically zero on the interval
[0,T]

The final goal is attained fully exploiting the Assumptions
2.4
and more specifically iiic),
having taken as a starting point the integral form of the DRE.

By contrast, in the next Section

a unified approach and method of proof will prove effective in showing uniqueness for both cases.

3.1.

In this subsection we make reference to the optimal control problem (
2.1
)–(
2.3
),
with

.
We address the issue of uniqueness of solutions to the Cauchy problem (

) for the Riccati equation corresponding to problem (

)–(

We begin by relating the differential form (
2.13
) of the Riccati equation to
an integral form of it, which in turn can be further interpreted.

Lemma 3.1

(Integral forms of the Riccati equation)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
be the class defined in (
2.14
), and let

Q(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
be a solution to the DRE (
2.13
).
Then the following assertions hold true.

1.

Q(\cdot)
solves the integral Riccati equation (in short, IRE), that is

(3.1)

\begin{split}&\big(Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,e^{A(t-s)}y\big)_{Y}=(Q(s)x,y)_{Y}-\int_{s}^{t}\big(Re^{A(r-s)}x,Re^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Z}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{U}\,dr\,,\end{split}

with
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
and
x,y\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
.

2.

B^{*}Q(\cdot)e^{A(\cdot-s)}\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y,L^{2}(s,T;U))
.

3.

The IRE (
3.1
) can be rewritten in the form

(3.2)

\begin{split}&\big(e^{A^{*}(t-s)}Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,y\big)_{Y}=(Q(s)x,y)_{Y}-\int_{s}^{t}\big(e^{A^{*}(r-s)}R^{*}Re^{A(r-s)}x,y\big)_{Y}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{s}^{t}\big(e^{A^{*}(r-s)}Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,y\big)_{Y}\,dr\,,\end{split}

valid for any
x,y\in Y
and with
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
.

Proof.

1.

Let
x,y\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)
: then
e^{A\cdot}x
,
e^{A\cdot}y
are differentiable, and therefore,
using (
2.13
), there exists

\begin{split}&\frac{d}{dr}\big(Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Y}\\[5.69054pt]
&\quad=-\big(Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,Ae^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Y}-\big(Ae^{A(r-s)}x,Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Y}\\[5.69054pt]
&\qquad-\big(Re^{A(r-s)}x,Re^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Z}\,+\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{U}\\[5.69054pt]
&\qquad+\big(Q(r)Ae^{A(r-s)}x,e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Y}+\big(Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,Ae^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Y}\\[5.69054pt]
&\quad=-\big(Re^{A(r-s)}x,Re^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{Z}\,+\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{U}\,.\end{split}

Integrating the above identity in
r\in[s,t]
, one readily obtains the IRE (
3.1
),
valid for
x,y\in{\mathcal{D}}(A)
.
In view of Lemma
A.5
, the validity of the IRE is extended to all

A first proof of Theorem

.

We follow the proof of Theorem 1.5.3.3 in

We know already that the optimal cost operator

Assume there exists another operator in
{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}

, which solves (

By construction

By Lemma

).
Then, taking in particular

, we find that
Q(s)
satisfies

(3.3)

\begin{split}(Q(s)x,y)_{Y}&=-\int_{s}^{T}(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}P_{1}(r)e^{A(r-s)}y)_{U}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]

To render the computations cleaner, set

The same observations apply to
[B^{*}P_{1}(r)]^{*}

We may now rewrite (

\begin{split}(Q(s)x,y)_{Y}&=-\int_{s}^{T}(e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}[B^{*}P_{1}(r)]^{*}V(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,A^{\epsilon}y)_{Y}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]

which tells us that

(3.4)

\begin{split}Q(s)x&=-{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\int_{s}^{T}\Big[e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}[B^{*}P_{1}(r)]^{*}V(r)e^{A(r-s)}\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}V(r)^{*}B^{*}P(r)e^{A(r-s)}\Big]x\,dr\,,\end{split}

valid for every

, where, as pointed out above, the right hand side is an element of

As

B^{*}
to both sides

\begin{split}V(s)x&=-B^{*}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\int_{s}^{T}\Big[e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}[B^{*}P_{1}(r)]^{*}V(r)e^{A(r-s)}\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}V(r)^{*}B^{*}P(r)e^{A(r-s)}\Big]x\,dr\,.\end{split}

It is here where iiic) of Assumptions

becomes crucially important: indeed, it yields as well

We return to (

), and highlight a few blocks within its right hand side,
as follows:

\begin{split}V(s)x&=-\int_{s}^{T}\big[B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\big]\,{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}[B^{*}P_{1}(r)]^{*}V(r)e^{A(r-s)}x\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad-\int_{s}^{T}\big[B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\big]{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}V(r)^{*}B^{*}P(r)e^{A(r-s)}x\,dr\,;\end{split}

multiply next both members by

\begin{split}(V(s)x,w)_{U}&=-\int_{s}^{T}\big(V(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,[B^{*}P_{1}(r)A^{-\epsilon}]\,\big[B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\big]^{*}w\big)_{U}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]

We now proceed to estimate either summand in the right hand side, making use of (
3.6
);
this leads to

\begin{split}&|(V(s)x,w)_{U}|\\
&\;\leq M\,\|V(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),C([s,T];U))}\|x\|_{{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}\|B^{*}P_{1}(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),C([s,T];U))}\|w\|_{U}(T-s)^{1/q^{\prime}}\\
&\quad+M\,\|B^{*}P(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),C([s,T];U))}\|x\|_{{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}\|V(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),C([s,T];U))}\|w\|_{U}(T-s)^{1/q^{\prime}}\,.\end{split}

which establishes

(3.7)

\|V(s)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),U)}\leq C\,\|V(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),C([s,T];U))}(T-s)^{1/q^{\prime}}\,,

for any

.

The argument is now pretty standard: set

by density we obtain (

\begin{split}&\hskip-5.69054pt\big(P_{1}e^{A(t-s)}x,e^{A(t-s)}y\big)_{Y}=(P_{1}x,y)_{Y}+\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}P_{1}e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}P_{1}e^{A(r-s)}y\big)_{U}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]

4.1.
Finite time interval, differential Riccati equations

In this subsection we focus on the optimal control problem (

with

In approaching the second proof of Theorem

Despite being a standard element in classical optimal control theory, the identity should not be taken for granted in the absence of evident beneficial regularity properties
of the kernel

Achieving the said equality requires that the Assumptions

The delicate, careful computations are carried out in the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.1

(Fundamental identity)
.

Let
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
be a solution to the integral Riccati equation (
3.1
).
With
u\in L^{2}(s,T;U)
and
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
, let
y(\cdot)
be the semigroup solution to the state equation in (
2.1
) corresponding to
u(\cdot)
, with
y(s)=x
, that is

y(t)=e^{A(t-s)}x+\int_{s}^{t}e^{A(t-r)}Bu(r)\,dr=e^{A(t-s)}x+L_{s}u(t)\,,\quad t\in[s,T]\,.

Then, the following identity is valid: for
t\in[s,T]

(4.1)

\begin{split}&(Q(t)y(t),y(t))_{Y}-(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}=-\int_{s}^{t}\left[\|Ry(r)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(r)\|_{U}^{2}\right]\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{s}^{t}\|u(r)+B^{*}Q(r)y(r)\|_{U}^{2}\,dr\,.\end{split}

Proof.

Assume initially that
u\in L^{\infty}(s,T;U)
.
We examine the right hand side of the identity (
4.1
).
For the first term we have

\displaystyle-\int_{s}^{t}\|Ry(r)\|_{Z}^{2}\,dr

\displaystyle=-\int_{s}^{t}\big\|Re^{A(r-s)}x\big\|_{Z}^{2}\,dr-\int_{s}^{t}\big\|RL_{s}u(r)\big\|_{Z}^{2}\,dr

\displaystyle\quad-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(Re^{A(r-s)}x,RL_{s}u(r)\big)_{Z}\,dr=:\sum_{j=1}^{3}R_{j}\,.

We note that each summand
R_{j}
makes sense, just considering the space regularity originally singled out in
[
1
]
and here recalled in Proposition
A.1
; more specifically,
u\in L^{\infty}(s,T;U)
implies
L_{s}u\in C([s,T];Y)
by its fourth assertion.
We consider next the remainder

-\int_{s}^{t}\|u(r)\|_{U}^{2}\,dr+\int_{s}^{t}\|u(r)+B^{*}Q(r)y(r)\|_{U}^{2}\,dr\,.

Computing the square in the second integral, discarding additive inverses and replacing
again the expression of
y(r)
, we get

\displaystyle-\int_{s}^{t}\|u(r)\|_{U}^{2}\,dr+\int_{s}^{t}\|u(r)+B^{*}Q(r)y(r)\|_{U}^{2}\,dr

\displaystyle\quad=2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)L_{s}u(r),u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{s}^{t}\big\|B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x\big\|_{U}^{2}\,dr+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}Q(r)L_{s}u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{s}^{t}\big\|B^{*}Q(r)L_{s}u(r)\big\|_{U}^{2}\,dr=:\sum_{j=1}^{5}C_{j}\,.

That each summand
C_{j}
makes sense as well is justified by the following observations:

B^{*}Q(\cdot)e^{A(\cdot-s)}x\in L^{2}(s,T;U)

because of item 2. of Lemma
3.1
;
in addition, since
L^{\infty}(s,T;U)\subset L^{q^{\prime}}(s,T;U)
, Lemma
A.2
yields the improved regularity
L_{s}u\in C([s,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}))
, which in turn implies

B^{*}Q(\cdot)L_{s}u(\cdot)\in C([s,T];U)
, as shown in Lemma
A.5
.

By using the original form (
3.1
) of the integral Riccati equation (IRE), with
x=y
, we find that

(4.2)

\begin{split}R_{1}+C_{3}&=-\int_{s}^{t}\|Re^{A(r-s)}x\|_{Z}^{2}\,dr+\int_{s}^{t}\big\|B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x\big\|_{U}^{2}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&=\big(Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,e^{A(t-s)}x\big)_{Y}-(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}\,.\end{split}

Next,

\begin{split}R_{3}+C_{4}+C_{1}&=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(Re^{A(r-s)}x,RL_{s}u(r)\big)_{Z}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\hskip-28.45274pt\quad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,B^{*}Q(r)L_{s}u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\hskip-28.45274pt\quad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\hskip-28.45274pt=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\Big(R^{*}Re^{A(r-s)}x,\int_{s}^{r}e^{A(r-\sigma)}Bu(\sigma)\,d\sigma\Big)_{Y}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\hskip-28.45274pt\quad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\Big\langle Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,\int_{s}^{r}e^{A(r-\sigma)}Bu(\sigma)\,d\sigma\Big\rangle_{[{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})]^{\prime},{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\hskip-28.45274pt\quad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr\,,\end{split}

where the duality in the penultimate term is based on the membership
Q(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
(along with the estimate (
A.2
))
which yields
Q(r)B\in{\mathcal{L}}(U,[{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})]^{\prime})
,
combined as before with
L_{s}u\in C([s,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}))
.
The above leads to

\begin{split}R_{3}+C_{4}+C_{1}&=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{r}\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-\sigma)}R^{*}Re^{A(r-s)}x,u(\sigma)\big)_{U}\,d\sigma\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\quad+\,2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{r}\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-\sigma)}Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-s)}x,u(\sigma)\big)_{U}\,d\sigma dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\quad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}(B^{*}Q(\sigma)e^{A(\sigma-s)}x,u(\sigma)\big)_{U}\,d\sigma\,,\end{split}

which can be rewritten, exchanging the order of integration, as follows:

(4.3)

\begin{split}R_{3}+C_{4}+C_{1}=&-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\Big(B^{*}\Big\{\int_{\sigma}^{t}e^{A^{*}(r-\sigma)}R^{*}R\big[e^{A(r-s)}x\big]\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\quad-\int_{\sigma}^{t}e^{A^{*}(r-\sigma)}Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-\sigma)}\big[e^{A(\sigma-s)}x\big]\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\quad-Q(\sigma)\big[e^{A(\sigma-s)}x\big]\Big\},u(\sigma)\Big)_{U}\,d\sigma\,.\end{split}

Let us focus on the expression inside the curly bracket.
Because
Q(\cdot)
solves the IRE (
3.1
), as well as its second form (
3.2
) valid for any pair
x,y\in Y
, then the following identity – a
strong
form of the IRE, when

is unknown – holds true:

\begin{split}&e^{A^{*}(t-\sigma)}Q(t)e^{A(t-\sigma)}z=Q(\sigma)z-\int_{\sigma}^{t}e^{A^{*}(r-\sigma)}R^{*}Rz\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{\sigma}^{t}e^{A^{*}(r-\sigma)}Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)e^{A(r-\sigma)}z\,dr\,,\quad 0\leq\sigma\leq t\leq T\,,\;z\in Y\,.\end{split}

Thus, returning to (
4.3
) with this information and setting in particular

z=e^{A(\sigma-s)}x
,
we find that
R_{3}+C_{4}+C_{1}
simply reads as follows:

(4.4)

\begin{split}R_{3}+C_{4}+C_{1}&=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-\sigma)}Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,u(\sigma)\big)_{U}\,d\sigma\\[2.84526pt]
&=-2\textrm{Re}\big(Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,L_{s}u(t)\big)_{Y}\,.\end{split}

We examine next the sum

R_{2}+C_{5}=-\int_{s}^{t}\|RL_{s}u(r)\|_{Z}^{2}\,dr+\int_{s}^{t}\big\|B^{*}Q(r)L_{s}u(r)\big\|_{U}^{2}\,dr\,,

where, again, since

,
we know from Lemma
A.2
that
L_{s}u\in C([s,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}))
.
Consequently, one gets

(4.5)

\begin{split}&R_{2}+C_{5}=-\int_{s}^{t}\big\langle\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]L_{s}u(r),L_{s}u(r)\big\rangle_{[{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})]^{\prime},{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\;=-\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\Big({A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]A^{-\epsilon}\,\int_{s}^{r}A^{\epsilon}e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda)\,d\lambda,\\[1.42262pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\int_{s}^{r}A^{\epsilon}e^{A(r-\mu)}Bu(\mu)\,d\mu\Big)_{Y}\,dr\,.\end{split}

It is important to emphasize that in going from the duality to the inner product in (
4.5
),
two facts have been crucially used, besides
Q(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
:
the hypothesis (
2.7
) on the observation operator
R
(that is iiib) of the Assumptions
2.4
), and once again, Lemma
A.2
.
Further handling of the right hand side of (
4.5
) leads to the triple integral

R_{2}+C_{5}=-\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}I(r,s)\,dr\,,

having set

I(r,s)=\int_{s}^{r}\!\!\int_{s}^{r}\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-\mu)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),u(\mu)\big)_{U}\,d\lambda\,d\mu\,.

Let us focus on the inner double integral
I(r,s)
.
We note that this integral pertains to a symmetric function of
(\lambda,\mu)
,
and hence the integral over the square
[s,r]\times[s,r]
can be replaced by twice the integral
over the triangle

\{(\lambda,\mu)\colon s\leq\mu\leq\lambda\leq r\}\,.

It follows that

\begin{split}&I(r,s)=2\,\int_{s}^{r}\!\!d\lambda\int_{s}^{\lambda}\!\!d\mu\,\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-\mu)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),u(\mu)\big)_{U}\\[2.84526pt]
&\,=2\,\int_{s}^{r}\!\!\Big[\int_{s}^{\lambda}\!\!\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(\lambda-\mu)}\,e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),u(\mu)\big)_{U}\,d\mu\,\Big]\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\,=2\,\int_{s}^{r}\!\!\big(\,e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),\int_{s}^{\lambda}e^{A(\lambda-\mu)}Bu(\mu)\,d\mu\big)_{Y}\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\,=2\,\int_{s}^{r}\!\!\big(\,e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),L_{s}u(\lambda)\big)_{Y}\,d\lambda\,.\end{split}

Inserting the expression of
I(r,s)
obtained above in the outer integral yields

\begin{split}&R_{2}+C_{5}\\[2.84526pt]
&\;=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{s}^{r}\big(\,e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),L_{s}u(\lambda)\big)_{Y}d\lambda\,dr\,;\end{split}

next we exchange the order of integration and also move the first argument of the inner product,
to achieve

(4.6)

\begin{split}&R_{2}+C_{5}\\[2.84526pt]
&\;=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{\lambda}^{t}\big(\,e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\,\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda),L_{s}u(\lambda)\big)_{Y}dr\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\;=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{\lambda}^{t}\big(u(\lambda),B^{*}e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}L_{s}u(\lambda)\big)_{Y}dr\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\;=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(u(\lambda),B^{*}\int_{\lambda}^{t}e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}\,L_{s}u(\lambda)\,dr\big)_{U}\,d\lambda\,.\end{split}

It is apparent that the second form (
3.2
) of the IRE (with
\lambda
in place of
s
)
– in fact, a strong form of it –
provides once more the tool, just like in deriving (
4.4
) from (
4.3
).
With
z=L_{s}u(\lambda)
, replace the integral

\int_{\lambda}^{t}e^{A^{*}(r-\lambda)}\big[R^{*}R-Q(r)BB^{*}Q(r)\big]e^{A(r-\lambda)}z\,dr

by
[Q(\lambda)-e^{A^{*}(t-\lambda)}Q(t)e^{A(t-\lambda)}]z
, to find

(4.7)

\begin{split}&R_{2}+C_{5}=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(u(\lambda),B^{*}\big[Q(\lambda)-e^{A^{*}(t-\lambda)}Q(t)e^{A(t-\lambda)}\big]L_{s}u(\lambda)\big)_{U}\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}\big[Q(\lambda)-e^{A^{*}(t-\lambda)}Q(t)e^{A(t-\lambda)}\big]L_{s}u(\lambda),u(\lambda)\big)_{U}\,d\lambda\,.\end{split}

Thus, adding
C_{2}
to (
4.7
), we see that a useful simplification occurs, as detailed below:

\begin{split}R_{2}+C_{5}+C_{2}&=-2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(\lambda)L_{s}u(\lambda),u(\lambda)\big)_{U}\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-\lambda)}Q(t)e^{A(t-\lambda)}L_{s}u(\lambda),u(\lambda)\big)_{U}\,d\lambda\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad+2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\big(B^{*}Q(r)L_{s}u(r),u(r)\big)_{U}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&=2\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{s}^{\lambda}\big(Q(t)e^{A(t-\sigma)}Bu(\sigma),e^{A(t-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda)\big)_{Y}\,d\sigma\,d\lambda\,.\end{split}

Owing to the simmetry of the latter integrand in
(\sigma,\lambda)
, we may replace twice the integral over
the triangle
\{(\lambda,\sigma)\colon s\leq\lambda\leq\sigma\leq t\}
by the integral over the square

[s,t]\times[s,t]
, and finally get

(4.8)

\begin{split}R_{2}+C_{5}+C_{2}&=\textrm{Re}\int_{s}^{t}\!\!\int_{s}^{t}\big(Q(t)e^{A(t-\sigma)}Bu(\sigma),e^{A(t-\lambda)}Bu(\lambda)\big)_{Y}\,d\lambda\,d\sigma\\[2.84526pt]
&=\textrm{Re}\big(Q(t)L_{s}u(t),L_{s}u(t)\big)_{Y}=\big(Q(t)L_{s}u(t),L_{s}u(t)\big)_{Y}\,.\end{split}

Combining (
4.8
) with (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), we finally obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{3}R_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{5}C_{j}&=\big(Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,e^{A(t-s)}x\big)_{Y}-(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}\\[-2.84526pt]
&\qquad+2\textrm{Re}(Q(t)e^{A(t-s)}x,L_{s}u(t))_{Y}+(Q(t)L_{s}u(t),L_{s}u(t))_{Y}\\[5.69054pt]
&=(Q(t)y(t),y(t))_{Y}-(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}\,,\end{split}

which establishes the fundamental identity (
4.1
) in the case
u\in L^{\infty}(s,T;U)
.
Finally, the identity extends to
u\in L^{2}(s,T;U)
by density, which concludes the proof of
Lemma
4.1
.

∎

We next introduce an integral equation that involves a given operator solution
Q(t)
to the Riccati equation corresponding to optimal control problem (
2.1
)–(
2.3
).
Once uniqueness for the DRE (
2.13
) is established, so that
Q(t)
must coincide with the Riccati operator
P(t)
, then it will be clear that the said integral equation ((
4.9
) below)
is nothing but the well known
closed-loop equation
, of central importance for the synthesis of the optimal control.
(This justifies the use of the term “closed-loop equation” for (
4.9
)).

As we shall see, the following Lemma
4.2
and (the independent) Lemma
4.1

constitute the core elements for the proof of Theorem
2.7
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\epsilon
be as in iii) of Assumptions
2.4
.
Let
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
, where
{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
is the class defined by (
2.14
).
Then, for every
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
, the closed loop equation

(4.9)

y(t)=e^{At}x-\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}BB^{*}Q(\sigma)y(\sigma)\,d\sigma\,,\qquad t\in[0,T]\,,

has a unique solution in the space

(4.10)

X=\Big\{y\in C([0,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}))\colon\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\big(e^{-rt}\|y(t)\|_{{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\varepsilon})})<\infty\Big\}

endowed with the norm

\|y\|_{X,r}=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}e^{-rt}\|y(t)\|_{{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}\,,\qquad y\in X\,,

provided
r>0
is chosen sufficiently large.

Proof.

With
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
, we set
E(t)=e^{At}x
.
By semigroup theory we know that
E(\cdot)\in C([0,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}))
; even more,
since
e^{At}
is exponentially stable, it holds
E(\cdot)\in X
provided
r
is sufficiently large.
As the integral equation (
4.9
) has the clear form

y(t)+\big[LB^{*}Q(\cdot)y(\cdot)\big](t)=E(t)\,,\quad t\in[0,T]\,,

we appeal to a classical argument of functional analysis: we will prove that
LB^{*}Q(\cdot)
is a contraction mapping in
X
, having chosen
r
sufficiently large.
This will in turn imply that
I+LB^{*}Q(\cdot)
is invertible in
X
, thus providing the sought unique solution
to (
4.9
).

For each
y\in X
,
z\in{\mathcal{D}}({A^{*}}^{\epsilon})
,
t\in[0,T]
, we have by Lemma
A.5

\displaystyle\big|(e^{-rt}LB^{*}Q(\cdot)y(\cdot),{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z)_{Y}\big|

\displaystyle\quad=\Big|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r(t-s)}\,\big(B^{*}Q(s)e^{-rs}y(s),B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\big)_{U}\,ds\Big|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r(t-s)}\|B^{*}Q(\cdot)e^{-r\cdot}y(\cdot)\|_{C([0,T];U)}\,\big\|B^{*}e^{(t-s)A^{*}}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\big\|_{U}\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|B^{*}Q(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(C([0,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})),C([0,T];U))}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r\sigma}\|B^{*}e^{A^{*}\sigma}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\|_{U}\,d\sigma

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|B^{*}Q(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(C([0,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})),C([0,T];U))}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\,\Big[\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r\sigma q^{\prime}}\,d\sigma\Big]^{1/q^{\prime}}\|B^{*}e^{\cdot A^{*}}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\|_{L^{q}(0,T;U)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|B^{*}Q(\cdot)\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(C([0,T];{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})),C([0,T];U))}\,\frac{1}{(rq^{\prime})^{1/q^{\prime}}}\,\|B^{*}e^{A^{*}\cdot}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(Y,L^{q}(0,T;U))}\,\|z\|_{Y}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\,.

We note that in going from the antepenultimate to the penultimate estimate we used iiic) of the Assumptions
2.4
.
Therefore, there exist positive constants
c,c^{\prime}
such that

\big\|e^{-rt}\big[L\,B^{*}Q(\cdot)y(\cdot)\big](t)\big\|_{{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}\leq\frac{c}{(rq^{\prime})^{1/q^{\prime}}}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\leq\frac{c^{\prime}}{r^{1/q^{\prime}}}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\,,

so that by taking a sufficiently large
r
we see that
LB^{*}Q(\cdot)
is a contraction mapping
in
X
.
The conclusion of the Lemma follows.
∎

Uniqueness for the DRE is now a consequence of Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
,
its proof following a somewhat familiar path.

Proof of Theorem
2.7
.

For the optimal pair
(\hat{y},\hat{u})
corresponding to the initial state
x\in Y

it holds

(P(s)x,x)_{Y}=J(\hat{u})=\int_{s}^{T}\Big(\|R\hat{y}(r)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|\hat{u}(r)\|_{U}^{2}\Big)\,dr\,,\quad 0\leq s\leq T\,,

where
P(\cdot)
is the Riccati operator defined in (
2.9
), i.e.

P(t)x=\int_{t}^{T}e^{A^{*}(r-t)}R^{*}R\Phi(r,t)x\,dr\,,\qquad x\in Y\,,

while
\Phi(r,t)
denotes the evolution operator

\Phi(r,t)x=e^{A(r-t)}x+L_{t}\hat{u}(r)\,,\qquad r\in[t,T]\,.

Let
Q(\cdot)\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{T}
be another solution to the DRE (
2.13
): by Lemma
3.1

Q(\cdot)

solves the IRE (
3.1
) as well; then, with
u\in L^{2}(s,T;U)
and
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
,
the identity (
4.1
) holds true by Lemma
4.1
.
With
t=T
, since
Q(T)=0
, from (
4.1
) we see that

\begin{split}(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}&=\int_{s}^{T}\left[\|Ry(r)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(r)\|_{U}^{2}\right]\,dr-\int_{s}^{T}\|u(r)+B^{*}Q(r)y(r)\|_{U}^{2}\,dr\\[2.84526pt]
&\leq\int_{s}^{T}\left[\|Ry(r)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(r)\|_{U}^{2}\right]\,dr=J(u)\,.\end{split}

In particular, when
u=\hat{u}
, we establish

(4.11)

(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}\leq J(\hat{u})=(P(s)x,x)_{Y}\qquad\forall s\in[0,T]\,,\;\forall x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})\,.

Conversely, let
y(\cdot)
be the solution to the closed-loop equation (
4.9
) corresponding to
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
, guaranteed by Lemma
4.2
, and
let
u(\cdot)=-B^{*}Q(\cdot)y(\cdot)
.
By construction
u\in L^{2}(s,T;U)
, and the fundamental identity becomes

(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}=\int_{s}^{t}\Big(\|Ry(r)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(r)\|_{U}^{2}\Big)\,dr+(Q(t)y(t),y(t))_{Y}\,,

which in turn gives, for
t=T
,

(4.12)

(Q(s)x,x)_{Y}=J(u)\geq J(\hat{u})=(P(s)x,x)_{Y}\qquad\forall s\in[0,T]\,,\;\forall x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})\,.

The inequality (
4.12
), combined with (
4.11
), establishes
– via the usual polarization (first) and density (next) arguments –
Q(s)\equiv P(s)
on
[0,T]
,
as desired.

∎

.
Instrumental results are the counterparts of Lemmas
4.1
and

, along with the integral form (

see Lemma

The first Lemma is the infinite time horizon version of the fundamental identity established

.

Lemma 4.3

(Fundamental identity (
T=+\infty
))
.

Recall the class
{\mathcal{Q}}
defined in (
2.20
).
Let
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
be a solution to the integral Riccati equation (
3.8
).
With
u\in L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(0,\infty;U)
and
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
, let
y(\cdot)

be the semigroup solution to the state equation (
2.1
) corresponding to
u(\cdot)
,
with initial state
x
, given by (
2.2
).
Then, the following identity holds true, for any
t\geq 0
:

(4.13)

\begin{split}&(Qy(t),y(t))_{Y}-(Qx,x)_{Y}\\[5.69054pt]
&\qquad=-\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|Ry(s)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(s)\|_{U}^{2}\Big)\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\big\|u(s)+B^{*}Qy(s)\big\|_{U}^{2}\,ds\,.\end{split}

Proof.

It suffices to proceed along the lines of the proof of Lemma
4.1
, replacing the interval

[s,t]
by
[0,t]
and assuming initially

; the proof is
actually slightly simpler, since here

is independent of

The details are omitted for the sake of conciseness.
∎

The next Lemma is the infinite time horizon version of Lemma
4.2
, dealing with
an integral equation which – once uniquenss for the ARE is ascertained – will turn out to be
the closed-loop equation.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\epsilon
be as in the Assumptions
2.9
.
Recall the class
{\mathcal{Q}}
defined by (
2.20
), and let
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
.
For every
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
and a suitably large
r>0
there exists a unique solution

y(\cdot)
to the closed loop equation

(4.14)

y(t)=e^{At}x-\int_{0}^{t}e^{A(t-s)}BB^{*}Qy(s)\,ds\,,\qquad t>0\,,

in the space

(4.15)

X=\big\{y\in C([0,\infty);{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}))\colon\quad\sup_{t\geq 0}e^{-rt}\|y(t)\|_{{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})}<\infty\big\}

endowed with the norm

\|y\|_{X,r}=\sup_{t>0}e^{-rt}\|y(t)\|_{D(A^{\epsilon})}\quad\forall y\in X\,,\quad r>0\,.

Proof.

The argument is pretty much the same employed in the proof of Lemma
4.2
.
A technically decisive (distinct) element here comes from the extended (and enhanced) regularity in time of the operator
B^{*}e^{A^{*}\cdot}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}
over the half line
[0,\infty)
, which is guaranteed by

[
3
, Proposition 3.2]
, recalled here as Proposition
A.6
.
The computation is included for the reader’s convenience.

Let
x\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
be given.
By setting
E(t)=e^{At}x
, and recalling the input-to-state map
L
, the integral equation
(
4.14
) reads as
\big([I+LB^{*}Q]y(\cdot)\big)(t)=E(t)
, in short.
For any function
y(\cdot)\in X
and any
z\in{\mathcal{D}}({A^{*}}^{\epsilon})
, we have

\begin{split}&|(e^{-rt}LB^{*}Qy(t),A^{*\epsilon}z)_{Y}=\left|\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r(t-s)}(B^{*}Qy(s)e^{-rs},B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z)_{Y}\,ds\right|\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-r(t-s)}\|B^{*}Q\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),U)}\|y\|_{X,r}\,e^{-\delta(t-s)}\,\|e^{\delta(t-s)}B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\|_{U}\,ds\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\leq\|B^{*}Q\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),U)}\|y\|_{X,r}\,\Big(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(r+\delta)(t-s)q^{\prime}}\,ds\Big)^{1/q^{\prime}}\,\cdot\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\|e^{\delta(t-s)}B^{*}e^{A^{*}(t-s)}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\|_{U}^{q}\,ds\Big)^{1/q}\\[2.84526pt]
&\qquad\leq\frac{1}{[(r+\delta)q^{\prime}]^{1/q^{\prime}}}\,\|B^{*}Q\|_{{\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon}),U)}\,\|e^{\delta\cdot}B^{*}e^{A^{*}\cdot}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(Y,L^{q}(0,\infty;U))}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\,\|z\|_{Y}\,,\end{split}

where
\delta
belongs to the interval
(0,\omega\wedge\eta)
(
\omega
and
\eta
being like in the Assumptions
2.9
).
The above estimate implies readily that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\|LB^{*}Qy\|_{X,r}\leq\frac{C}{(r+\delta)^{1/q^{\prime}}}\,\|y\|_{X,r}\|e^{\delta\cdot}B^{*}e^{A^{*}\cdot}A^{*\epsilon}\|_{{\mathcal{L}}(Y,L^{q}(0,\infty;U))}

so that

\|LB^{*}Qy\|_{X,r}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|y\|_{X,r}\,,

provided
r
is sufficiently large.
The conclusive argument is standard.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.11
.

Let
y_{0}\in Y
, and let
(\hat{y},\hat{u})
the optimal pair of the optimal control problem
(
2.1
)-(
2.3
) (with
T=+\infty
), corresponding to the initial state
y_{0}
.
Recall that

(Py_{0},y_{0})_{Y}=J(\hat{u})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\|R\hat{y}(s)\|^{2}_{Z}\,ds+\int_{0}^{\infty}\|\hat{u}(s)\|_{U}^{2}\,ds\,,

where the (optimal cost) operator
P
is defined in terms of the optimal state semigroup

\Phi(t)
via (
2.18
).
In addition,
P
belongs to the class
{\mathcal{Q}}
and solves the ARE (
1.1
); consequently,
by Lemma
3.2

P
solves the integral form (
3.8
) of the ARE.

Let now
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
be another solution to the ARE.
By Lemma
4.3
, we know that for any given
y_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
,
and any admissible control
u(\cdot)
, the identity (
4.13
) holds true (with
x
replaced by

y_{0}
), where
y(\cdot)
is the solution to the state equation corresponding to the control
u
and
the initial state
y_{0}
.
Consequently,

(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\leq(Qy(t),y(t))_{Y}+J(u)\qquad\forall u\in L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(0,\infty;U)\,,\;\forall t>0\,;

by choosing in particular the admissible pair
(y_{T},u_{T})
defined as follows,

u_{T}=\hat{u}\cdot\chi_{[0,T]}\,,\qquad y_{T}(t)=\begin{cases}\hat{y}(t)&\textrm{if $t\leq T$}\\[5.69054pt]

we find
(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\leq(Qy_{T}(t),y_{T}(t))_{Y}+J(u_{T})
, valid for arbitrary

.
By letting

in the previous inequality, one obtains readily

(4.16)

(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\leq J(u_{T})\qquad\forall y_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})\,\quad\forall T>0\,,

in view of the fact that the semigroup

\|y_{T}(t)\|_{Y}\longrightarrow 0
, as

.

Observe now that

\begin{split}J(u_{T})&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\|Ry_{T}(s)\|^{2}_{Z}\,ds+\int_{0}^{\infty}\|u_{T}(s)\|_{U}^{2}\,ds\\[5.69054pt]
&=\int_{0}^{T}\|R\hat{y}(s)\|^{2}_{Z}\,ds\,+\int_{T}^{\infty}\big\|R\big(e^{sA}y_{0}+e^{(s-T)A}L\hat{u}(T)\big)\big\|^{2}\,ds+\int_{0}^{T}\|\hat{u}(s)\|_{U}^{2}\,ds\,,\end{split}

so that
J(u_{T})\longrightarrow J(\hat{u})
, as
T\rightarrow+\infty
.
Keeping this in mind, return to (
4.16
) and let
T\to+\infty
to find

(4.17)

(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\leq J(\hat{u})=(Py_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\qquad\forall y_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})\,.

On the other hand, given
y_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})
(and still with
Q\in{\mathcal{Q}}
another solution
to the ARE), let
y(\cdot)
be the solution to the closed loop equation guaranteed
by Lemma
4.4
; by construction
y\in L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(0,\infty;Y)
.
Take now the control
u(\cdot)=-B^{*}Qy(\cdot)
, which belongs to
L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(0,\infty;U)
.
Then, the identity (
4.13
) holds true for any positive
t
, that is

(4.18)

\begin{split}&(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}=(Qy(t),y(t))_{Y}+\int_{0}^{t}\Big(\|Ry(s)\|_{Z}^{2}+\|u(s)\|_{U}^{2}\Big)\,ds\\[5.69054pt]
&\qquad-\cancel{\int_{0}^{t}\big\|u(s)+B^{*}Qy(s)\big\|_{U}^{2}\,ds}\geq\int_{0}^{t}\|Ry(s)\|_{Z}^{2}\,ds+\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|_{U}^{2}\,ds\,.\end{split}

As
t\to+\infty
, this shows that
Ry\in L^{2}(0,\infty;Z)
,
u\in L^{2}(0,\infty;U)
,
as well as
(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\geq J(u)
.
By minimality we find

(4.19)

(Qy_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\geq J(u)\geq J(\hat{u})=(Py_{0},y_{0})_{Y}\qquad\forall y_{0}\in{\mathcal{D}}(A^{\epsilon})\,.

On the basis of (
4.17
) and (
4.19
), a standard polarization (first)
and density (next) argument confirms that
Q=P
, thereby concluding the proof of Theorem
2.11
.
∎

Lemma A.7
.

Under the Assumptions
2.9
, the following regularity results are valid,
for any

:

(A.4)

\begin{split}&a)\quad e^{\delta\cdot}A^{\epsilon}e^{A\cdot}B\in{\mathcal{L}}(L^{q^{\prime}}(0,\infty;U),Y)\,,\\[2.84526pt]
&b)\quad e^{\delta\cdot}B^{*}e^{A^{*}\cdot}{A^{*}}^{-\epsilon}\in{\mathcal{L}}(L^{r}(0,\infty;Y),U)\qquad\forall r>\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\,.\end{split}

The respective actions of the operators in (
A.4
) are made explicit by (
A.5
) and (
A.6
).

Proof.

The regularity results in (
A.4
) are, in essence, dual properties of the regularity result
in Proposition
A.6
and of assertion A6. in Theorem
2.10
, respectively.
To infer (a), we introduce the notation
S
for the mapping from
Y
into
L^{q}(0,\infty;U)

defined by

Y\ni z\longrightarrow[Sz](t):=e^{\delta t}B^{*}e^{A^{*}t}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\,,\qquad\;t>0\,.

For any
z\in Y
and any

, it must be
S^{*}\in{\mathcal{L}}(L^{q^{\prime}}(0,\infty;U),Y)

and more precisely,

\begin{split}\langle S^{*}h,z\rangle_{Y}&=\langle h,Sz\rangle_{L^{q^{\prime}}(0,\infty;U),L^{q}(0,\infty;U)}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big\langle h(t),e^{\delta t}B^{*}e^{A^{*}t}{A^{*}}^{\epsilon}z\big\rangle_{U}\,dt\\[2.84526pt]

Therefore,

(A.5)

S^{*}h=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{\delta t}A^{\epsilon}e^{At}Bh(t)\,dt\,,\qquad h\in L^{q^{\prime}}(0,\infty;Y)\,.

To achieve (b) of (
A.4
), we recall instead the assertion A6. in Theorem

which further tells us that

e^{\delta\cdot}A^{-\epsilon}e^{A\cdot}B\in{\mathcal{L}}(U,L^{p}(0,\infty;Y))\,,\quad\text{for any $p$ such that $1\leq p<\frac{1}{\gamma}$.}

for the mapping from

More precisely, for any

which establishes
