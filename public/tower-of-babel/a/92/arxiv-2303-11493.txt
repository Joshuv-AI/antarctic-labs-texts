Abstract
. The Fibonacci numbers are the prototypical example of a recursive sequence, but grow too quickly to enumerate sets of integer partitions. Introduction

An integer partition of a positive integer
n
is a weakly decreasing sequence of positive integers whose sum is
n
. The positive integers in the sequence are called parts. The set of partitions of
n

is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(n)
and the number of partitions of

For example, the five partitions of

As usual,

Given an integer sequence

2.
Preliminaries and notation

If
\lambda
is a partition of
n
, we write
\lambda\vdash n
. The weight (or size) of a partition
\lambda
, denoted by
|\lambda|
, is the sum of its parts. Thus, if
\lambda\vdash n
, then
|\lambda|=n
. The length of a partition
\lambda
, denoted by
\ell(\lambda)
, is the number of parts of
\lambda
. We write a partition as a list, a formal sum, or a concatenation when unambiguous. For example, the partition
\lambda=(4,4,3,2,2,1)
of
16
can also be written as
4+4+3+2+2+1
or
443221
. We denote the empty partition by
(\ )
. We also view a partition as the multiset of its parts and write
a\in\lambda
to mean that
a
is a part of
\lambda
. The number of times an integer
i
occurs as a part in the partition
\lambda
is denoted by
m_{\lambda}(i)
and is referred to as the multiplicity of
i
in
\lambda
. For example, if
\lambda=(4,4,3,2,2,1)
, then
m_{\lambda}(4)=2
.
For more on the theory of partitions, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
.

An overpartition of
n
is a partition of
n
in which the first occurrence of a part may be overlined
[
7
]
. For example,
(4,4,3,2,2,1),(\overline{4},4,3,2,2,1)
, and
(\overline{4},4,\overline{3},2,2,1)
are some of the overpartitions of
16
.

We define several operations on partitions.

If
\lambda
and
\mu
are partitions, then
\lambda\sqcup\mu
denotes the partition whose multiset of parts is the union of the multisets of parts of
\lambda
and
\mu
. For example, if
\lambda=(2,1,1)
and
\mu=(3,2,1,1)
, we have
\lambda\sqcup\mu=(3,2,2,1,1,1,1)
. Similarly, if
\mathcal{S}
is a set of partitions, define
{\mathcal{S}\sqcup\mu=\{\lambda\sqcup\mu\mid\lambda\in\mathcal{S}\}}
. If
\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
, then
\mathcal{S}\sqcup\lambda=\emptyset
.

If each part of
\mu
occurs in
\lambda
with equal or greater multiplicity, we say that
\mu
is contained in
\lambda
, or a subpartition of
\lambda
, and write
\mu\subseteq\lambda
. If
\mu\subseteq\lambda
, we denote by
\lambda\setminus\mu
the partition obtained from
\lambda
by removing the multiset of parts of
\mu
. For example, if
\lambda=(7,4,4,4,2,1,1)
and
\mu=(4,2,1,1)
, then
\lambda\setminus\mu=(7,4,4)
.

Let

be a positive integer. For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
, we define
m\lambda:=(m\lambda_{1},m\lambda_{2},\ldots,m\lambda_{\ell})
. Moreover, if every part of
\lambda
is divisible by

, we define
\displaystyle\lambda/m:=(\lambda_{1}/m,\lambda_{2}/m,\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}/m)
. Occasionally, we also write
\frac{1}{m}\lambda
for
\lambda/m
.

For a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
, we define
\mathrm{add}_{1,1}(\lambda):=(\lambda_{1}+1,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
, the partition obtained by adding
1
to the first part of
\lambda
. Vacuously,
\mathrm{add}_{1,1}((\ ))=(\ )
. If
\lambda
has an odd part, we denote by
\lambda^{o+2}
the partition obtained from
\lambda
by adding
2
to the largest odd part.

Again, we extend this notation to sets. Given a set of partitions
\mathcal{S}
, define
{m\mathcal{S}=\{m\lambda\mid\lambda\in\mathcal{S}\}}
,
{\mathcal{S}^{o+2}=\{\lambda^{o+2}\mid\lambda\in\mathcal{S}\}}
(assuming each partition in

has an odd part), and
\mathrm{add}_{1,1}(\mathcal{S})=\{\mathrm{add}_{1,1}(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\mathcal{S}\}
.

A binary partition has only powers of
2
for parts. We denote the set of binary partitions of
n
by
\mathcal{B}(n)
.

As mentioned in the previous section,
\mathcal{OB}(n)
denotes the subset of
\mathcal{B}(n)
consisting of partition in which all parts have odd multiplicities.
We denote by
\overline{\mathcal{OB}}(n)
the set of odd binary overpartitions of
n
; removing the overlines in

gives an odd binary partition. For example, the binary overpartitions of

are

Throughout the paper, if
\mathfrak{f}
is a function on a set of partitions, we omit double parentheses and write
\mathfrak{f}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell})
instead of
\mathfrak{f}((\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}))
.

We define the weight-preserving function
\mathrm{rep}_{2}:\mathcal{P}(n)\to\mathcal{B}(n)
as follows. If
n\geq 1
and
\mathrm{val}_{2}(n)=k
, define
\mathrm{rep}_{2}(n)
to be the partition with

parts all equal to

. Then define

\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}):=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\lambda_{i}).

For example,
\mathrm{rep}_{2}(12,6,1,1,1)=(4,4,4,2,2,2,1,1,1)
. Note that
\mathrm{rep}_{2}
is not injective. For example,
\mathrm{rep}_{2}(12,6,2,1,1,1)=(4,4,4,2,2,2,1,1,1)=\mathrm{rep}_{2}(12,6,3,2).

As mentioned above, in

.

We define the weight-preserving function
\mathrm{bin}:\mathcal{P}(n)\to\mathcal{B}(n)
as follows. First, for
n\geq 1
,
\mathrm{bin}(n)
is the unique partition of

into distinct powers of

\mathrm{bin}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}):=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathrm{bin}(\lambda_{i}).

For example,
\mathrm{bin}(7,2)=(4,2,2,1)
. Note that
\mathrm{bin}
is not injective on the set of partitions of

. For example,
\mathrm{bin}(6,3)=(4,2,2,1)=\mathrm{bin}(7,2)

The next theorem emphasizes the difference between
\mathrm{rep}_{2}
and

Theorem 2.1
.

The partition
\lambda
is binary if and only if
\mathrm{bin}(\lambda)=\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\lambda)
.

Proof.

If
n
is a power of 2,
\mathrm{bin}(n)=(n)=\mathrm{rep}_{2}(n)
.

So if
\lambda
is a binary partition,

(1)

\mathrm{bin}(\lambda)=\lambda=\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\lambda).

If
\lambda
is not a binary partition, let
\beta
be the subpartition of
\lambda
consisting of the parts that are powers of 2 and let
\gamma=\lambda\setminus\beta
. By (
1
), to prove
\mathrm{bin}(\lambda)\neq\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\lambda)
, it is sufficient to prove that
\mathrm{bin}(\gamma)\neq\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\gamma)
.

Let
m
be the largest power of 2 that divides all the parts of
\gamma
. Since the parts of
\gamma
are not powers of
2
, it follows that
m
is not a part of
\gamma
. The partition
\gamma/m
has at least one odd part and all of its odd parts of are greater than 1. The number of 1s in
\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\gamma/m)
is the sum
S
of the odd parts of
\gamma/m
; the number of 1s in
\mathrm{bin}(\gamma/m)
is the number
N
of odd parts of
\gamma/m
. But
S>N
, so
\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\gamma/m)\neq\mathrm{bin}(\gamma/m)
. Then, since
m
is a power of
2
, we have
\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\gamma)=m\,\mathrm{rep}_{2}(\gamma/m)\neq m\,\mathrm{bin}(\gamma/m)=\mathrm{bin}(\gamma)
, which completes the proof.
∎

We aim to keep the notation intuitive throughout the paper. A self-similar sequence is denoted by one or two lowercase letters prefixed by the letter “s” (for “semi”); for example,
\mathrm{sp}(n)
denotes the semi-Pell sequence and
\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(n)
denotes the semi-Padovan sequence. The corresponding set of partitions is denoted by the same letters in capital calligraphy style; for example, the set of partitions enumerated by
\mathrm{sp}(n)
is denoted by
\mathcal{SP}(n)
, which are the semi-Pell partitions. (Sometimes we need to add a lowercase letter, as in
\mathcal{SP}a
, the semi-Padovan partitions.) For the total number of parts in all partitions in
\mathcal{SP}(n)
, for example, we use
\mathrm{psp}(n)
; that is, we the same lower case letters preceded by
\mathrm{p}
.

In general, to avoid repetition, we omit the proofs of propositions; they can be proved from some combination of using the recurrences, induction, or previous results. The omitted proofs are similar to those we present in detail for some theorems.

3.
The generating function of a self-similar sequence

In this section, we begin by considering a general self-similar sequence with a recurrence of order at most
3
at positive odd arguments and use analytic methods similar to those in
[
3
]
and
[
9
]
to find its generating function. In subsequent sections, specializing that recurrence gives combinatorial interpretations for self-similar sequences based on well-known Fibonacci-like sequences.

Consider the sequence
\{f(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
defined recursively by:
f(0)=a_{0},f(1)=a_{1}
, and, for
n\geq 1
, by

\displaystyle f(2n)

\displaystyle=f(n),

\displaystyle f(2n+1)

\displaystyle=c_{1}f(2n)+c_{2}f(2n-1)+c_{3}f(2n-2).

Let
F(x)
be the generating function for
\{f(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
. Thus,

F(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}f(n)x^{n}.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n)x^{2n}=a_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 1}f(2n)x^{2n}=a_{0}+\sum_{n\geq 1}f(n)x^{2n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}f(n)x^{2n}=F(x^{2}),

and

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n+1)x^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}x

\displaystyle+\sum_{n\geq 1}(c_{1}f(2n)+c_{2}f(2n-1)+c_{3}f(2n-2))x^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=a_{1}x

\displaystyle+c_{1}x\sum_{n\geq 1}f(2n)x^{2n}+c_{2}x^{2}\sum_{n\geq 1}f(2n-1)x^{2n-1}

\displaystyle+c_{3}x^{3}\sum_{n\geq 1}f(2(n-1))x^{2(n-1)}

\displaystyle=a_{1}x

\displaystyle+c_{1}x\sum_{n\geq 1}f(2n)x^{2n}+c_{2}x^{2}\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n+1)x^{2n+1}

\displaystyle+c_{3}x^{3}\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n)x^{2n}.

Thus,

\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n+1)x^{2n+1}=\frac{1}{1-c_{2}x^{2}}\left(a_{1}x+c_{1}x(F(x^{2})-a_{0})+c_{3}x^{3}F(x^{2})\right).

Then

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n)x^{2n}+\sum_{n\geq 0}f(2n+1)x^{2n+1}

\displaystyle=F(x^{2})+\frac{1}{1-c_{2}x^{2}}\left(a_{1}x+c_{1}x(F(x^{2})-a_{0})+c_{3}x^{3}F(x^{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(a_{1}-a_{0}c_{1})x}{1-c_{2}x^{2}}+\left(1+\frac{c_{1}x+c_{3}x^{3}}{1-c_{2}x^{2}}\right)F(x^{2}).

Let

A(x):=\frac{(a_{1}-a_{0}c_{1})x}{1-c_{2}x^{2}}\text{\ and \ }B(x):=1+\frac{c_{1}x+c_{3}x^{3}}{1-c_{2}x^{2}}.

Then

(2)

F(x)=A(x)+B(x)F(x^{2}).

Iterating (
2
), leads to

(3)

F(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}A(x^{2^{i}})\prod_{r=0}^{i-1}B(x^{2^{r}})+F(x^{2^{k+1}})\prod_{r=0}^{k}B(x^{2^{r}}),

with the convention that an empty product such as
\prod_{r=0}^{-1}B(x^{2^{r}})
is taken to be
1
.

In (
3
), we let
k\to\infty
. Using
\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}F(x^{2^{k+1}})=a_{0}
we get

(4)

F(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}A(x^{2^{i}})\prod_{r=0}^{i-1}B(x^{2^{r}})+a_{0}\prod_{r=0}^{\infty}B(x^{2^{r}}).

It is easily checked that (
4
) satisfies equation (
2
) and the initial coefficients agree with the initial terms of the sequence.

Case 1:
If
a_{1}-a_{0}c_{1}\neq 0
, then
A(x^{2^{i}})\neq 0
for all
i\geq 0
and we have

(5)

\displaystyle F(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1}-a_{0}c_{1})x^{2^{i}}}{1-c_{2}x^{2^{i+1}}}

\displaystyle\prod_{r=0}^{i-1}\left(1+\frac{c_{1}x^{2^{r}}+c_{3}x^{3\cdot 2^{r}}}{1-c_{2}x^{2^{r+1}}}\right)

\displaystyle+a_{0}\prod_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{c_{1}x^{2^{r}}+c_{3}x^{3\cdot 2^{r}}}{1-c_{2}x^{2^{r+1}}}\right).

Case 2:
If
a_{1}-a_{0}c_{1}=0
, then
A(x^{2^{i}})=0
for all
i\geq 0
and we have

(6)

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=a_{0}\prod_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{c_{1}x^{2^{r}}+c_{3}x^{3\cdot 2^{r}}}{1-c_{2}x^{2^{r+1}}}\right).

In particular, if
f(n)
is the semi-Fibonacci sequence, we have
a_{0}=a_{1}=1
,
c_{1}=c_{2}=1
, and

. Thus, we are in Case 2. and (

F(x)=\prod_{r=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{x^{2^{r}}}{1-x^{2^{r+1}}}\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{OB}(n)|q^{n},

the generating function for the sequence

To understand the combinatorial interpretation of (

\displaystyle\frac{x^{2^{r}}}{1-x^{2^{r+1}}}
keeps track of parts equal to

keeps track of parts equal to

Thus, we have the following combinatorial interpretation for
\mathrm{st}(n)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
n\geq 0
,
|\overline{\mathcal{OB}}^{{\dagger}}_{\overline{\mathrm{R}}}(n)|=\mathrm{st}(n)
for
n\geq 0
.

Remark 1
.

The odd binary partitions of
n
, which are enumerated by
\mathrm{sf}(n)
, are precisely the overpartitions in
\overline{\mathcal{OB}}^{{\dagger}}_{\overline{\mathrm{R}}}(n)
that have no overlined part. Hence,
\mathrm{sf}(n)\leq\mathrm{st}(n)
for all

.

We next define a subset of partitions of
n
enumerated by
\mathrm{st}(n)
.

Definition 3
.

The semi-tribonacci partitions are defined recursively by

\mathcal{ST}(0)=\emptyset
,
\mathcal{ST}(1)=\{(1)\}
,
\mathcal{ST}(3)=\{(3),(2,1)\}
and for

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{ST}(2n)

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{ST}(n),

\displaystyle\mathcal{ST}(2n+3)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{ST}(2n+2)\sqcup(1)\bigcup\mathcal{ST}(2n+1)^{o+2}\bigcup\mathcal{ST}(2n)\sqcup(1,1,1).

.

Remark 2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{ST}(n)
. Clearly, if
n
is even,
\lambda
has no odd parts. If
n
is odd, it is easily seen by induction that
\lambda
has one or three odd parts and in the latter case at least two of the odd parts equal

.

The recursive definition of semi-tribonacci partitions together with Remark

leads to the following corollary to Theorem

.

Corollary 4.2
.

For
n\geq 0
,
|\mathcal{ST}(n)|=|\overline{\mathcal{OB}}^{{\dagger}}_{\overline{\mathrm{R}}}(n)|.

Next, we give a non-recursive description of the semi-tribonacci partitions that will allow us give a combinatorial proof of Corollary
4.2
.

Given a partition
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell(\lambda)})
let
v(\lambda)=(v_{1}(\lambda),v_{2}(\lambda),\ldots,v_{\ell(\lambda)}(\lambda))
be the sequence of non-negative integers obtained by arranging
\mathrm{val}_{2}(\lambda_{i})
,
1\leq i\leq\ell(\lambda)
, in non-increasing order. Thus,
v(\lambda)
is the partition whose parts are the
2
-adic valuations of the parts of
\lambda
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
n>0
. The set
\mathcal{ST}(n)
consists of all partitions
\lambda
of
n
satisfying the following conditions.

(a)

v_{1}(\lambda)
has multiplicity one in
v(\lambda)
(i.e.
v_{1}(\lambda)>v_{2}(\lambda)
);

(b)

if
1<j\leq\ell(\lambda)
, then
v_{j}(\lambda)
has multiplicity one or three;

(c)

if
v_{j}(\lambda)
has multiplicity three, then
\lambda
has at least two parts equal to
2^{v_{j}(\lambda)}
.

Proof.

We prove the theorem by induction.
We first show that all partitions in
\mathcal{ST}(n)
satisfy conditions (a), (b), and (c).
By inspection, this is true for
n=1,2,3
. Let
n\geq 4
be an integer and assume that, for all
m<n
, all partitions in
\mathcal{ST}(m)
satisfy (a), (b), and (c). Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{ST}(n)
.

If
n=2t
for some
t>1
, then
\lambda=2\mu
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{ST}(t)
. Then,
v(\lambda)=(v_{1}(\mu)+1,v_{2}(\mu)+1,\ldots,v_{\ell(\mu)}(\mu)+1)
. Since
\mu
satisfies (a), (b), and (c), and the parts of
\lambda
with
2
-adic valuation equal to
v_{i}(\mu)+1
are precisely twice the parts of
\mu
with
2
-adic valuation equal to
v_{i}(\mu)
, it follows that
\lambda
satisfies (a), (b), and (c).

If
n=2t+1
for some
t>1
, then we have three cases.

(1)

\lambda=\mu\sqcup(1)
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{ST}(2t)
. Then,
v_{\ell(\mu)}(\mu)>0
and

v(\lambda)=(v_{1}(\mu),v_{2}(\mu),\ldots,v_{\ell(\mu)}(\mu),0).

Since
\mu
satisfies (a), (b), and (c), so does
\lambda
.

(2)

\lambda=\mu\sqcup(1,1,1)
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{ST}(2t-2)
. Then,
v_{\ell(\mu)}(\mu)>0
and

v(\lambda)=(v_{1}(\mu),v_{2}(\mu),\ldots,v_{\ell(\mu)}(\mu),0,0,0).

The only parts with
2
-adic valuation zero are the three parts equal to
1
. Since
\mu
satisfies (a), (b), and (c), so does
\lambda
.

(3)

\lambda=\mu^{o+2}
for some
\mu\in\mathcal{ST}(2t-1)
. Then
v(\lambda)=v(\mu)
and from Remark
2
and the induction hypothesis,
\lambda
satisfies (a), (b), and (c).

Next, we show by induction that all partitions of
n
satisfying (a), (b), and (c) are in
\mathcal{ST}(n)
. By inspection, for
n=1,2,3
, the partitions in
\mathcal{ST}(n)
are the only partitions satisfying conditions (a), (b), and (c). Let
n\geq 4
be an integer and assume that if
m<n
all partitions of
m
satisfying (a), (b), and (c) are in
\mathcal{ST}(m)
. Let
\lambda
be a partition of
n
satisfying (a), (b), and (c).

If
n=2t
for some
t>1
, by (b),
\lambda
cannot have odd parts. The partition
\lambda{/2}
has
v(\lambda{/2})=(v_{1}(\lambda)-1,v_{2}(\lambda)-1,\ldots,v_{\ell(\lambda)}(\lambda)-1)
and thus
\lambda{/2}
satisfies conditions (a) and (b). If
v_{i}(\lambda{/2})
has multiplicity three, then
v_{i}(\lambda)
has multiplicity three and
\lambda
has at least two parts equal to
2^{v_{i}(\lambda)}
. Then
\lambda{/2}
has at least two parts equal to
2^{v_{i}(\lambda)-1}
and
\lambda{/2}
satisfies (c). By induction,
\lambda{/2}\in\mathcal{ST}(t)
and, by definition,
\lambda\in\mathcal{ST}(2t)
.

If
n=2t+1
for some
t>1
,
\lambda
must have odd parts, i.e,
v_{\ell(\lambda)}(\lambda)=0
. By (b),
\lambda
has one or three odd parts and, by (c), if
\lambda
has three odd parts, at least two odd parts must equal
1
. We transform
\lambda
into a partition
\mu
as follows.

(i)

If
m_{\lambda}(1)=1
, let
\mu=\lambda\setminus(1)
. Then
\mu\vdash 2t
.

(ii)

If
m_{\lambda}(1)=3
, let
\mu=\lambda\setminus(1,1,1)
. Then
\mu\vdash 2t-2
.

(iii)

If
m_{\lambda}(1)=2
, then
\mu
is the partition obtained from
\lambda
by subtracting two from the unique odd part greater than
1
. Then,
\mu\vdash 2t-1
.

It is easily seen that in each case the partition
\mu
satisfies conditions (a), (b), and (c). By the induction hypothesis,
\mu
is a semi-tribonacci partition. By the definition of
\mu
from
\lambda
, it follows that
\lambda\in\mathcal{ST}(n)
.
∎

Theorem
4.3
allows us to prove Corollary

combinatorially.

If

4.1.
Parity Results for
\mathrm{st}(n)

We establish parity results for the sequence
\mathrm{st}(n)
, and therefore for the sequences
|\overline{\mathcal{OB}}^{{\dagger}}_{\overline{\mathrm{R}}}(n)|
and
|\mathcal{ST}(n)|
. Here and throughout, we write
\equiv
to mean congruence modulo
2
.

Theorem 4.5
.

For
n\geq 0
,
\mathrm{st}(2n+1)+\mathrm{st}(4n+1)\equiv 0
.

Proof.

We prove the statement by induction. The statement is true for
n=0
by inspection. Let
n\geq 1
and assume that
\mathrm{st}(2k+1)+\mathrm{st}(4k+1)\equiv 0
for all
k<n
. From the recurrence, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{st}(2n+1)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{st}(2n)+\mathrm{st}(2n-1)+\mathrm{st}(2n-2)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{st}(4n)+\mathrm{st}(4n-2)+\mathrm{st}(4n-4).

Also,

\displaystyle\mathrm{st}(4n+1)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{st}(4n)+\mathrm{st}(4n-1)+\mathrm{st}(4n-2)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{st}(4n)+\mathrm{st}(4n-2)+\mathrm{st}(4n-3)+\mathrm{st}(4n-4)+\mathrm{st}(4n-2).

Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{st}(2n+1)+\mathrm{st}(4n+1)

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{st}(4n-3)+\mathrm{st}(4n-2)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{st}(2(n-1)+1)+\mathrm{st}(4(n-1)+1)\equiv 0.

∎

Corollary 4.6
.

For
n\geq 0
,
\mathrm{st}(16n+7)\equiv\mathrm{st}(16n+8)\equiv\mathrm{st}(16n+4)
.

Proof.

The congruence follows from the recurrence relation and the statement of Theorem
4.5
. We omit the details.
∎

Theorem 5.6

For

if and only if

Proof.

We say that two finite sequences are congruent modulo
2
if and only if the corresponding terms are congruent. We use complete induction to prove that for all
n\geq 0

\{\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(7n+k)\}_{k=0,1,\ldots,6}\equiv(1,0,0,1,0,1,1).

We work with

\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(n)

Let

be an integer. Assume that, for all

is even if and only if

For the seven odd arguments, we use the recurrences and induction one value at a time to find the parities. The first value is

\displaystyle\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(14M+1)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(14M-1)+\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(14M-2)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(14(M-1)+13)+\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(14(M-1)+12)

The other six are similar; we get

The seven even arguments are easier; we use induction once for all the values:

\{\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(14M+2j)\}_{j=0,1,\ldots,6}=\{\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(7M+j)\}_{j=0,1,\ldots,6}\equiv y.

Interleaving

(ii)

If
\lambda=(2\eta_{1}-1)\sqcup 2(\eta\setminus(\eta_{1}))
for some
\eta\in\mathcal{SB}(t+1)
, we have

\mathrm{bin}(\lambda_{1}-1)=\mathrm{bin}(2(\eta_{1}-1))=2\mathrm{bin}(\eta_{1}-1).

Then

\lambda\setminus(\lambda_{1})=2(\eta\setminus(\eta_{1}))\subseteq 2\mathrm{bin}(\eta_{1}-1)=\mathrm{bin}(\lambda_{1}-1).

Conversely, let
\mu\vdash m
be such that
\mu\setminus(\mu_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)
.

Case I:

m=2t
,
t\geq 2
. Note that
1\not\in\mu
since otherwise
\mu_{1}
is odd and
\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)
does not contain
1
as a part. Thus all the parts of
\mu
are even. Consider the partition
\nu=\mu/2
. Then
\nu\setminus(\nu_{1})=(\mu\setminus(\mu_{1}))/2
. Since
1\not\in\mu
, we have
\mu\setminus(\mu_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)\setminus(1)
.
Using Corollary
10.3
, we get

\mathrm{bin}(\nu_{1}-1)=\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}/2-1)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)\setminus(1))\supseteq(\mu\setminus(\mu_{1}))/2=\nu\setminus(\nu_{1}).

By the induction hypothesis
\nu\in\mathcal{SB}(t)
, so
\mu=2\nu\in\mathcal{SB}(m)
.

Case II:

m=2t+1
,
t\geq 2
. Then either (i) the smallest part of
\mu
is
1
and there are no other odd parts, or (ii) the largest part of
\mu
is odd. The latter includes the case
\lambda=(m)
.

(i)

In the first subcase,
\mu_{1}
is even and
\mu\setminus(\mu_{1},1)\subseteq\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)\setminus(1)
. Let
\nu=(\mu\setminus(1))/2
.
As in Case I, using Corollary
10.3
, we get

\mathrm{bin}(\nu_{1}-1)=\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}/2-1)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)\setminus(1))\supseteq(\mu\setminus(\mu_{1},1))/2=\nu\setminus(\nu_{1}).

By the induction hypothesis,
\nu\in\mathcal{SB}(t)
and hence
\mu=2\nu\sqcup(1)\in\mathcal{SB}(m)
.

(ii)

In the second subcase,
\mu_{1}
is odd and since
t\geq 2
,
\mu_{1}\geq 3
. If
\ell(\lambda)=1
, by property (c) above,
\lambda\in\mathcal{SB}(m)
. If
\ell(\lambda)\geq 2
, let
\nu=(\mu_{1}+1,\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{\ell})/2
. We have

\mathrm{bin}(\nu_{1}-1)=\mathrm{bin}((\mu_{1}-1)/2)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{bin}(\mu_{1}-1)\supseteq(\mu\setminus(\mu_{1}))/2=\nu\setminus(\nu_{1}).

By the induction hypothesis,
\nu\in\mathcal{SB}(t+1)
. Hence
\mu=(2\nu_{1}-1,2\nu_{2},\ldots,2\nu_{\ell})\in\mathcal{SB}(m)
.

∎

Remark 9
.

We define
\theta:\mathcal{SB}(n)\to\mathcal{HB}(n-1)
by
\theta(\lambda)=\mathrm{bin}(\lambda_{1}-1)\sqcup\lambda\setminus(\lambda_{1})
. By Theorem
10.4
,
\theta(\lambda)
is a binary partition whose parts have multiplicity at most 2. As
\mathrm{bin}
preserves the sum of parts,
\theta(\lambda)\in\mathcal{HB}(n-1)
. For any partition
\mu
, let
\mu^{d}
be the partition containing one copy of each part of
\mu
. Then
\theta^{-1}(\mu)=(1+|\mu^{d}|)\sqcup\mu\setminus\mu^{d}
, which is in
\mathcal{SB}(n)
, again by Theorem
10.4
.

We now give a recursive definition of hyperbinary partitions that lets us find the total number of parts in
\mathcal{HB}(n)
recursively.

Definition 18
.

We define the set
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)
recursively by
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(1)=\{(1)\}
,
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2)=\{(2),(1,1)\}
, and for
n\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2n)

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)\bigcup 2HB^{\prime}(n-1)\sqcup(1,1),

\displaystyle\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2n+1)

\displaystyle=2\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)\sqcup(1).

The union is disjoint in the definition of
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2n)
.

Theorem 10.5
.

For
n\geq 1
,
|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)|=\mathrm{sb}(n+1)
.

Proof.

By inspection,
|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(1)|=\mathrm{sb}(2)=1
and
|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2)|=\mathrm{sb}(3)=2
. Let
n\geq 2
and assume that for all
m<n
we have
|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(m)|=\mathrm{sb}(m+1)
.
If
n=2t
is even, using the recurrence in the definition of
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)
and the induction hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)|=|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2t)|

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(t)|+|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(t-1)|

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sb}(t+1)+\mathrm{sb}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sb}(2t+1)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sb}(n+1).

Similarly, if
n=2t+1
is odd, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)|=|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(2t+1)|=|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(t)|=\mathrm{sb}(t+1)=\mathrm{sb}(2(t+1))=\mathrm{sb}(n+1).

∎

Theorem 10.6
.

For
n\geq 1
,
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)=\mathcal{HB}(n)
.

Proof.

The definition of
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)
implies that
\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)\subseteq\mathcal{HB}(n)
. Since
|\mathcal{HB}^{\prime}(n)|=|\mathcal{HB}(n)|
, the result follows.
∎

12.
Concluding remarks

We defined recursively seven families of sets of partitions enumerated by self-similar sequences. In each case, we found a non-recursive description of the partitions, usually in terms of
2
-adic valuations of parts. Using generating functions, we also found
subsets of odd binary partitions or overpartitions enumerated by the sequences. We then found bijections between the corresponding sets of partitions enumerated by the same sequence.

It might be interesting to study other recursively defined sets of partitions, whether enumerated by self-similar sequences or not.

The sub-sequences at odd indices
\mathrm{sf}(2n+1)
,
\mathrm{st}(2n+1)
,
\mathrm{sl}(2n+1)
,
\mathrm{sp}(2n+1)
, and
\mathrm{sp{\small a}}(2n+1)
are increasing, although very slowly. It would be interesting to compare their asymptotic growth rates.

A natural generalization of the recursion
a(2n)=a(n)
is
a(mn)=a(n)
, leading to recursively defined sets of partitions enumerated by
a(n)
as before. Alanazi, Munagi, and Nyirenda did this for semi-
m
-Fibonacci
partitions
[
4
]
; the same could be done for the
m
-ary analogs of
\mathrm{st}(n)
,
\mathrm{sl}(n)
, and the other sequences presented here.
