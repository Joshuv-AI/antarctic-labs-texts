Renormalization of the Hutchinson Operator
Yann DEMICHEL

Abstract
One of the easiest and common ways of generating fractal sets in ℝ D {\mathbb{R}}^{D} is as attractors of affine iterated function systems (IFS). The classic theory of IFS’s requires that they are made with contractive functions. In this paper, we relax this hypothesis considering a new operator H ρ H_{\rho} obtained by renormalizing the usual Hutchinson operator H H . Namely, the H ρ H_{\rho} -orbit of a given compact set K 0 K_{0} is built from the original sequence ( H n ​ ( K 0 ) ) n \big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n} by rescaling each set by its distance from 0 0 . We state several results for the convergence of these orbits and give a geometrical description of the corresponding limit sets. In particular, it provides a way to construct some eigensets for H H . Our strategy to tackle the problem is to link these new sequences to some classic ones but it will depend on whether the IFS is strictly linear or not. We illustrate the different results with various detailed examples. Finally, we discuss some possible generalizations.

Introduction and notation

The theory and the use of fractal objects, introduced and developed by Mandelbrot (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
), still play an important role today in scientific areas as varied as physics, medicine or finance (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
and references therein). Exhibit theoretical models or solve practical problems requires to produce various fractal sets. There is a long history of generating fractal sets using Iterated Function Systems. After the fundamental and theoretical works by Hutchinson (see
[
17
]
), this method was popularized and developed by Barnsley in the 80s (see
[
1
,
2
]
). Since these years very numerous developments and extensions were made (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
) making even more enormous the literature related to these topics. Indeed, the simplicity and the efficiency of this approach have contributed to its success in a lot of domains, notably in image theory (see, e.g.,
[
13
]
) and shape design (see, e.g.,
[
15
]
).

1.1
Background

Let us recall the mathematical context and give the main notation used throughout the paper. Let
(M,\text{d})
be a metric space. For any map
f\colon M\to M
, we define the
f
-orbit of a point
x_{0}\in M
as the sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
given by

\displaystyle x_{n}=(f\circ\cdots\circ f)(x_{0})=f^{n}(x_{0}),

where
f^{n}
is the
n
th iterate of
f
with the convention that
f^{0}
is the identity function
\id
. In particular, one has
x_{n+1}=f(x_{n})
hence, if
f
is continuous and if
(x_{n})_{n}
converges to
z\in M
, then
z
is an invariant point for
f
, i.e.,
f(z)=z
.

We denote by
{\cal K}_{M}
the set of all non-empty compact subsets of
M
. We obtain a metric space endowing it with the Hausdorff metric
\dha
defined by

\displaystyle\forall\,K,K^{\prime}\in{\cal K}_{M},\qquad\dha(K,K^{\prime})=\inf\big\{\varepsilon>0\,|\,K\subset K^{\prime}(\varepsilon)\text{ and }K^{\prime}\subset K(\varepsilon)\big\},

where
K(\varepsilon)
is the set of points at a distance from
K
less than
\varepsilon
.

For every
K\subset M
we define the set
f(K)=\{f(x)\colon x\in K\}
and we will assume in the sequel that
f(K)\in{\cal K}_{M}
if
K\in{\cal K}_{M}
.

Let us consider
p\geqslant 1
maps
f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}
with
f_{i}\colon M\to M
. Then we can define a new map
H\colon{\cal K}_{M}\to{\cal K}_{M}
setting

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in{\cal K}_{M},\qquad H(K)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(K).

We say that
H
is the
Hutchinson operator
associated with the
iterated function system
(IFS in short)
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}\}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
,
12
,
17
]
).

Basic questions about an IFS are the following: Does the orbit
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converge for any compact set
K_{0}
? Does its limit depend on
K_{0}
? What are the geometrical properties of the limit sets?

The classic theory of IFS’s is based on the contractive mapping principle (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
12
,
17
]
). Let us recall that a map
f\colon M\rightarrow M
is contractive if

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}=\sup\left\{\frac{\text{d}(f(x),f(y))}{\text{d}(x,y)}\colon x,y\in M\text{ with }x\neq y\right\}<1.

Let us assume that
(M,\text{d})
is a complete metric space. Then, any contractive map is continuous, has a unique invariant point
z\in M
, and the
f
-orbit of any
x_{0}\in M
converges to
z
with the basic estimate

\displaystyle\forall\,n\geqslant 0,\qquad\text{d}\big(f^{n}(x_{0}),z\big)\leqslant\lambda_{f}^{n}\text{d}(x_{0},z).

If
f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}
are contractive then the associated Hutchinson operator
H
is also contractive because of

\displaystyle\lambda_{H}=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\lambda_{f_{i}}\}.

Since
({\cal K}_{M},\dha)
inherites the completeness of
(M,\text{d})
, the map
H
has then a unique invariant point
L\in{\cal K}_{M}
, called the
attractor
of
H
, and for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}_{M}
the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to
L
. One of the interests is that such sets
L
are generally fractal sets.

In the sequel, the space
M
will be essentially
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
,
D\geqslant 1
, endowed with the metric induced by the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|
. Writing simply
{\cal K}
for
{\cal K}_{M}
, a subset
K\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
belongs to
{\cal K}
if and only if it is closed and bounded. In particular, the closed ball with center
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and radius
r>0
will be denoted by
B(x,r)
.

In this paper, we are interested in affine IFS’s, i.e., when
f_{i}
is defined by
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x+b_{i}
with
A_{i}
a
D\times D
matrix and
b_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
a vector. Such a map satisfies
\lambda_{f_{i}}=\|A_{i}\|
where
\|A_{i}\|
is the norm of
A_{i}
given by

\displaystyle\|A_{i}\|=\sup\big\{\|A_{i}x\|\colon x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\text{ with }\|x\|=1\big\}=\inf\big\{r>0\,|\,\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D},\,\|A_{i}x\|\leqslant r\|x\|\big\}.

In particular, classic IFS’s consist of transformations involving rotations, symmetries, scalings and translations. In this case, if
H
is contractive, the corresponding attractor
L
is called a self-affine set.
One obtains a nice subclass of such IFS’s when the
f_{i}
’s are homotheties, i.e., when
f_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}x+b_{i}
with
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
. Indeed, contrarily to general affine maps,
f_{i}
contracts the distances with the same ratio
\alpha_{i}
in all directions. This enables a precise description of
L
. For example, if the sets
f_{i}(K_{0})
are mutually disjoints then
L
is a Cantor set whose fractal dimension is the solution of a very simple equation (see
[
12
,
21
]
). Cantor sets are fundamental and come naturally when one studies IFS’s. A simple family of Cantor sets in
{\mathbb{R}}
is
\big\{\Gamma_{a}\colon 0<a<\frac{1}{2}\big\}
where
\Gamma_{a}
is the attractor of the IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
with
f_{1}(x)=ax
and
f_{2}(x)=ax+(1-a)
. For example,
\Gamma_{\frac{1}{3}}
is the usual triadic Cantor set (see
[
10
,
12
,
17
]
). When
\frac{1}{2}\leqslant a<1
, the attractor of the previous IFS becomes the whole interval
[0,1]
. These basic examples will be extensively used in the sequel.

1.2
Motivation

Let us point out two specific situations:

–

When
\lambda_{H}\geqslant 1
the previous results become false: typical orbits fail to converge. Basically, the orbits of some points
x_{0}\in K_{0}
may then satisfy
\|f_{i}^{n}(x_{0})\|\to\infty
for some
i
, preventing the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
from being bounded.

–

When all the
f_{i}
’s are contractive linear maps, the attractor of
H
is always
\{0\}
so does not depend on the fine structure of the
A_{i}
’s but only on their norms.

However, in these two degenerate situations we can observe an intriguing geometric structure of the sets
H^{n}(K_{0})
. For example, let us consider the IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
where the
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are the linear maps given by their canonical matrices

\displaystyle A_{1}=\left[\begin{matrix}a&a\\
a&0\end{matrix}\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{2}=\left[\begin{matrix}a&-a\\
-a&0\end{matrix}\right]

with
a>0
. We focus on the
H
-orbit of the unit ball
B(0,1)
. For all
a
large enough we have
\|A_{1}\|=\|A_{2}\|>1
and the sequence
\big(H^{n}(B(0,1))\big)_{n}
is not bounded: the diameter
d_{n}
of
H^{n}(B(0,1))
grows to infinity. At the contrary, for all
a
small enough we have
\|A_{1}\|=\|A_{2}\|<1
. Thus
H
is now contractive and
\big(H^{n}(B(0,1))\big)_{n}
converges to
\{0\}
:
d_{n}
vanishes to
0
. Nevertheless, whatever is
a
, one can observe that the sets
H^{n}(B(0,1))
tend to a same limit shape looking like a ‘sea urchin’-shaped set (see Fig.
1
). So one can wonder if there exists a critical value
\overline{a}
for which
d_{n}
do not degenerate so makes possible to observe this asymptotic set.

(a)
H^{2}(B(0,1))

(b)
H^{5}(B(0,1))

(c)
H^{10}(B(0,1))

H
-orbit of
B(0,1)
where
H
is the Hutchinson operator associated with the IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
. Maps
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
are given above. Since the maps are linear, changing the parameter
a
gives the same sets for each
n
up to a scaling factor. Thus an adequate renormalization should reveal a common asymptotic limit-shape.

In this paper, we aim to modify the original Hutchinson operator to annihilate these two degenerate behaviors. We wish to obtain a limit set even if the IFS is not contractive, and a non zero limit set for contractive linear IFS’s. Moreover, we would like this new operator to exhibit the typical ‘limit shape’ observed above.

1.3
Renormalization with the radius function

Our strategy is to rescale each set
H^{n}(K_{0})
by dividing it by its size. The idea of rescale a sequence of sets
(K_{n})_{n}
to get its convergence to a non degenerate compact limit is not new and is particularly used in stochastic modeling (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
22
]
for famous examples of random growth models and more recently
[
18
,
20
]
in the context of random graphs and planar maps). Probabilists usually consider the
a posteriori
rescaled sets
\frac{1}{d_{n}}K_{n}
where
d_{n}
estimates the size of
K_{n}
, often its diameter.

Here we proceed differently. First, in order to keep dealing with the orbit of an operator, we will do an
a priori
renormalization. Secondly, we will measure the size of a compact set with its distance from
0
. Precisely, we consider the radius function
\rho
defined on
{\cal K}
by

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in{\cal K},\qquad\rho(K)=\sup\{\|x\|\colon x\in K\}

and we denote by
H_{\rho}
the operator defined by

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in{\cal K},\qquad H_{\rho}(K)=\frac{1}{\rho(H(K))}H(K).

(1.1)

The radius function
\rho
satisfies the three following basic properties:

–

continuity:
\rho
is continuous with respect to
\dha
;

–

monotonicity: If
K\subset K^{\prime}
then
\rho(K)\leqslant\rho(K^{\prime})
;

–

homogeneity: For all
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\rho(\alpha K)=|\alpha|\rho(K)
.

Actually
\rho
is a very nice function because it enjoys an additional stability property:

\displaystyle\forall\,K,K^{\prime}\in{\cal K},\qquad\rho(K\cup K^{\prime})=\max\{\rho(K),\rho(K^{\prime})\}.

(1.2)

The subject of interest of the paper is then the
H_{\rho}
-orbit of sets
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
. For simplicity, we will write in the sequel
K_{n}=H_{\rho}^{n}(K_{0})
so that

\displaystyle\forall\,n\geqslant 0,\qquad K_{n+1}=\frac{1}{d_{n}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(K_{n})

(1.3)

with

\displaystyle d_{n}=\rho\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(K_{n})\right)=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\rho(f_{i}(K_{n})).

(1.4)

We will assume that
d_{n}>0
, i.e.,
K_{n}\neq\{0\}
.

Observe that
\rho(K_{n})=1
for all
n\geqslant 1
, thus:

–

K_{n}\subset B(0,1)
so that the orbit of any set
K_{0}
is bounded;

–

there exist at least one
x_{n}\in K_{n}
such that
\|x_{n}\|=1
so that
(K_{n})_{n}
cannot vanish to
\{0\}
.

In particular, if
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to a set
K
then
\rho(K)=1
and
K\neq\{0\}
.

This new operator
H_{\rho}
is then a good candidate to solve the problems discussed in Section
1.2
. It will act by freezing the geometrical structure of
H^{n}(K_{0})
at each step
n
of the construction of the orbit.

1.4
Eigen-equation problem

Let us point out a very strong connection with the ‘eigen-equation problem’ recently studied in
[
3
]
for affine IFS’s. Indeed, if
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to a set
K
then
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d>0
and taking the limit in (
1.3
) leads to
H(K)=dK
. Hence
d
is an eigenvalue of
H
and
K
a corresponding eigenset. Existence of solutions for this equation is discussed and proved in
[
3
]
. The values for
d
are closely related to the joint spectral radius
\sigma_{{\cal M}}
of the
A_{i}
’s (see (
2.1
)). In particular, for linear IFS’s,
\sigma_{{\cal M}}
was interpreted as a transition value for which exists a corresponding eigenset
K
whose structure is similar to the one described in Section
1.2
. Unfortunately, these results don’t hold for every IFS. In particular it rules out simple IFS’s only made up with homotheties or some more interesting ones made up with stochastic matrices. However, the results stated in
[
3
]
provide important clues to determine and study the possible limits of both sequences
(d_{n})_{n}
and
(K_{n})_{n}
.

When studying the eigen-equation problem, an interesting question is to approximate any couple
(d,K)
of solutions of equation
H(K)=dK
. Let us look at the special case when the IFS consists in only one linear map with matrix
A
and set
K_{0}=\{x_{0}\}
. Then
K_{n}=\{x_{n}\}
with

\displaystyle\forall\,n\geqslant 0,\qquad x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\|Ax_{n}\|}\,Ax_{n}.

One recognizes the famous
power iteration algorithm
. With suitable assumptions it gives a simple way to approximate the unit eigenvector associated with the dominant eigenvalue
\sigma_{{\cal M}}
of
A
, this eigenvalue being the limit of
d_{n}=\|Ax_{n}\|
. Therefore, iterating the operator
H_{\rho}
from a set
K_{0}
is nothing but a generalization of this algorithm and then provides a natural procedure to approximate both an eigenvalue of
H
and one of its associated eigenset.

From now on we are then interested in the convergence of
(K_{n})_{n}
and the geometric properties of its limit. Typically,
H_{\rho}
is not contractive and the classic theory may not be applied. In particular,
H_{\rho}
may have different invariant points so that the limit of
(K_{n})_{n}
may be no longer unique but deeply depend on
K_{0}
. Furthermore, it is clear that the
H_{\rho}
-orbits of
K_{0}
may diverge for some
K_{0}
(for example when the
A_{i}
’s are only rotations). We will expose different ways to state the convergence of
(K_{n})_{n}
depending on whether the IFS is affine (Section
2
) or strictly linear (Section
3
). Finally, some generalizations will be shown in the last section (Section
4
).

2
Results for affine IFS’s

We suppose in this section that the IFS consists in
p\geqslant 1
affine maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
defined by
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x+b_{i}
. We denote by
{\cal M}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}\}
the set of their canonical matrices. Let us recall that the joint spectral radius of
{\cal M}
is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma_{{\cal M}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\sup_{1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant p}\{\alpha(A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{n}})\}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\sup_{1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant p}\{\|A_{i_{1}}\cdots A_{i_{n}}\|\}\big)^{\frac{1}{n}},\!\!\!

(2.1)

where
\alpha(M)
denotes the usual spectral radius of the matrix
M
(see
[
24
]
). Finally, we denote by
\spec(M)
the set of the eigenvalues of
M
so that
\alpha(M)=\max\{|\alpha|\colon\alpha\in\spec(M)\}
.

2.1
Strategy: a general result

Our strategy consists in linking the convergence of
(K_{n})_{n}
to the asymptotic behavior of the sequence of positive numbers
(d_{n})_{n}
. If
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to a set
K
then
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d>0
and the eigen-equation
H(K)=dK
shows that
K
may be seen as an invariant set of the classical Hutchinson operator
H_{d}=\frac{1}{d}H
associated with the IFS
\{\frac{1}{d}f_{1},\ldots,\frac{1}{d}f_{p}\}
. In particular, if
d>\lambda_{H}
then
K
is unique: it is the attractor
L_{d}
of this contractive operator
H_{d}
.

Conversely, if
(d_{n})_{n}
is a constant sequence, say
d_{n}=d
, one has
K_{n}=H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})
so that
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to
L_{d}
if
d>\lambda_{H}
. Actually, when
(d_{n})_{n}
is no longer constant, but converges to a positive number
d>\lambda_{H}
, then the convergence of
(K_{n})_{n}
to
L_{d}
still happen.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
. Assume that the sequence
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to a positive number
d>\lambda_{H}
. Then the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to the attractor
L_{d}
of the
\mathrm{IFS}

\big\{\frac{1}{d}f_{1},\ldots,\frac{1}{d}f_{p}\big\}
.

Proof.

Let us set
K^{\prime}_{n}=H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})
. We have to prove that
\varepsilon_{n}=\dha(K_{n},K^{\prime}_{n})
converges to
0
. We can write

\displaystyle\dha(K_{n+1},K^{\prime}_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leqslant\dha\left(\frac{1}{d_{n}}H(K_{n}),\frac{1}{d_{n}}H(K^{\prime}_{n})\right)+\dha\left(\frac{1}{d_{n}}H(K^{\prime}_{n}),\frac{1}{d}H(K^{\prime}_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\lambda_{H}}{d_{n}}\dha(K_{n},K^{\prime}_{n})+\left|\frac{1}{d_{n}}-\frac{1}{d}\right|\rho(H(K^{\prime}_{n})).

Since
(K^{\prime}_{n})_{n}
converges, there exists
B\in{\cal K}
such that
K^{\prime}_{n}\subset B
for all
n\geqslant 0
. Then let us fix
\eta>0
and
N\geqslant 0
such that
0<\lambda_{H}<d-\eta\leqslant d_{n}\leqslant d+\eta
for all
n\geqslant N
. We obtain
0\leqslant\varepsilon_{n+1}\leqslant\mu\varepsilon_{n}+m_{n}
where
\mu=\frac{\lambda_{H}}{d-\eta}
and
m_{n}=\big|\frac{1}{d_{n}}-\frac{1}{d}\big|\rho(H(B))
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\forall\,n>N,\qquad 0\leqslant\varepsilon_{n}\leqslant\mu^{n-N}\varepsilon_{N}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-N-1}\mu^{k}m_{n-1-k}.

Since
\mu\in[0,1)
and
m_{n}\rightarrow 0
it follows that
\varepsilon_{n}\rightarrow 0
.
∎

Let us emphasize that we did not use the definition of
(d_{n})_{n}
nor the fact that the
f_{i}
’s are affine. Hence the result is valid for
any
pairs of sequences
(K_{n})_{n}
and
(d_{n})_{n}
satisfying (
1.3
).

Let us notice that the sequence
(d_{n})_{n}
depends on
K_{0}
, so that the two limits
d
and
L_{d}
may also depend on
K_{0}
. If
d\leqslant\lambda_{H}
, the asymptotic behavior of
(K_{n})_{n}
is more delicate to derive directly from the one of
(d_{n})_{n}
. Therefore, in view of Theorem
2.1
, we ask the following questions: Does the sequence
(d_{n})_{n}
always converge? Does its limit may not depend on
K_{0}
or may be smaller than
\lambda_{H}
?

2.2
Convergence of
\boldsymbol{(d_{n})_{n}}

Except for very special cases it is impossible to obtain the exact expression of
d_{n}
. Therefore we rather seek for bounds for
d_{n}
and
d
. Let us begin with a basic result.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(d_{n})_{n}
be the sequence defined in (
1.4
). Then,

\displaystyle\forall\,n\geqslant 1,\qquad\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\|b_{i}\|-\|A_{i}\|\}\leqslant d_{n}\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\|A_{i}\|+\|b_{i}\|\}.

(2.2)

In particular, if
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d
, then
d
also satisfies (
2.2
).

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant 1
. One has
f_{i}(K_{n})\subset f_{i}(B(0,1))\subset B(b_{i},\|A_{i}\|)
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Thus, any
x\in f_{i}(K_{n})
satisfies
|\|x\|-\|b_{i}\||\leqslant\|x-b_{i}\|\leqslant\|A_{i}\|
, that is

\displaystyle\|b_{i}\|-\|A_{i}\|\leqslant\|x\|\leqslant\|A_{i}\|+\|b_{i}\|.

Since
d_{n}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\|x\|\colon x\in f_{i}(K_{n})\}
we obtain (
2.2
).
∎

The next result provides non trivial bounds for the possible limit
d
.

Proposition 2.3
.

If
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d
, then

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\{1,\ldots,p\},\qquad 0\leqslant\|b_{i}\|\leqslant\|d\id-A_{i}\|.

(2.3)

Moreover, if
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
is such that
d\notin\spec(A_{i})
, then

\displaystyle 0\leqslant\big\|(d\id-A_{i})^{-1}b_{i}\big\|\leqslant 1.

In particular, if
d>\lambda_{H}
then

\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\big\{\big\|(d\id-A_{i})^{-1}b_{i}\big\|\big\}\leqslant 1.

(2.4)

Proof.

Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and consider the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
defined by
x_{1}\in K_{1}
and
x_{n+1}=\frac{1}{d_{n}}(A_{i}x_{n}+b_{i})
. One has
x_{n}\in K_{n}
and
b_{i}=d_{n}x_{n+1}-A_{i}x_{n}
. By summation we get

\displaystyle nb_{i}=(d_{n}x_{n+1}-A_{i}x_{1})+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(d_{k}\id-A_{i})x_{k+1}.

(2.5)

Therefore, for all
n>1
,

\displaystyle\|b_{i}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\|d_{n}x_{n+1}-A_{i}x_{1}\|+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\|(d_{k}\id-A_{i})x_{k+1}\|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2}{n}(d_{n}+\|A_{i}\|)+\left(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\|d_{k}\id-A_{i}\|\right).

The first term in the sum above goes to
0
when
n\to\infty
and Cesàro’s lemma implies that the term into brackets goes to
\|d\id-A_{i}\|
. That gives (
2.3
).

Now assume that
i
is such that
d\notin\spec(A_{i})
. Then the matrix
M_{i}=d\id-A_{i}
is invertible and (
2.5
) yields

\displaystyle n(M_{i}^{-1}b_{i})=M_{i}^{-1}(d_{n}x_{n+1}-A_{i}x_{1})+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}M_{i}^{-1}(d_{k}\id-A_{i})x_{k+1}.

Thus we obtain in a similar way

\displaystyle\big\|M_{i}^{-1}b_{i}\big\|\leqslant\frac{2}{n}\big\|M_{i}^{-1}\big\|(d_{n}+\|A_{i}\|)+\left(\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\big\|M_{i}^{-1}(d_{k}\id-A_{i})\big\|\right).

We conclude as above using that
\big\|M_{i}^{-1}(d_{k}\id-A_{i})\big\|\rightarrow\|\id\|=1
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.

Finally, since
\|M\|\geqslant\alpha(M)
holds for every matrix
M
, inequality
d>\lambda_{H}
implies that
d\notin\cap_{i=1}^{p}\spec(A_{i})
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We will now show that (
2.4
) is an equality when the
A_{i}
’s are homotheties. Actually, we will prove again (
2.4
) but with a very different approach which can be generalized (see Theorem
4.1
(i)
). We need the following result. We denote by
\ch(K)
the convex hull of a non-empty set
K
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Assume that
\|A_{i}\|<1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Denote by
z_{i}
the unique invariant point of
f_{i}
and by
L
the attractor of the
\mathrm{IFS}

\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{p}\}
. If
f_{j}(z_{i})\in\ch(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\})
for all
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, then the convex hull of
L
is the polytope
\ch(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\})
.

Proof.

Let us write
C=\ch(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\})
. Let
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Since
f_{i}(z_{i})=z_{i}
, one has
z_{i}\in L\subset\ch(L)
and then
C\subset\ch(L)
. To prove the reverse inclusion we have to state that
L\subset C
. It is enough to prove that
H(C)\subset C
, i.e., that
f_{i}(C)\subset C
. So let
z=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}t_{j}z_{j}
,
t_{j}\geqslant 0
and
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}t_{j}=1
, a point in
C
. We have

\displaystyle f_{i}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}t_{j}\big(A_{i}z_{j}+b_{i}\big)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}t_{j}f_{j}(z_{i}),

thus
f_{i}(z)\in C
.
∎

Proposition 2.5
.

If
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d
with
d>\lambda_{H}
then
d
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\rho\big(\big\{(d\id-A_{1})^{-1}b_{1},\ldots,(d\id-A_{p})^{-1}b_{p}\big\}\big)\leqslant 1.

(2.6)

Moreover, if
A_{i}=\alpha_{i}\id
with
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, then (
2.6
) is an equality. In this case, there is at least one
b_{i}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
i\!\in\!\{1,{\ldots},p\}
. First,
d>\lambda_{H}
implies that
d\id-A_{i}
is invertible and
z_{i}\!=\!(d\id-A_{i})^{-1}b_{i}
is the unique invariant point of
\frac{1}{d}f_{i}
. Secondly,
d>\lambda_{H}
implies that
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to
L_{d}
so
z_{i}\in L_{d}
. Therefore
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\}\subset L_{d}
, and, by monotonicity,
\rho(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\})\leqslant\rho(L_{d})=1
. That gives (
2.6
).

Now, if all the
A_{i}
’s are homotheties, one has

\displaystyle\forall\,j\in\{1,\ldots,p\},\qquad\frac{f_{j}}{d}(z_{i})=\frac{\alpha_{j}}{d}\,z_{i}+\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{j}}{d}\right)z_{j}.

Since
0\leqslant\alpha_{j}<d
, one has
\frac{f_{j}}{d}(z_{i})\in\ch(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\})
. Thus, it follows from Lemma
2.4
applied to the IFS
\big\{\frac{1}{d}f_{1},\ldots,\frac{1}{d}f_{p}\big\}
that
\ch(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\})=\ch(L_{d})
. Since
\rho(\ch(K))=\rho(K)
for all
K\in{\cal K}
, we obtain

\displaystyle 1=\rho(L_{d})=\rho(\ch(L_{d}))=\rho(\ch(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\}))=\rho(\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\}),

hence (
2.6
) becomes an equality. Finally, if
b_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
then the left-hand side of (
2.6
) is zero, hence a contradiction.
∎

Notice that using the stability property of
\rho
, (
2.6
) gives (
2.4
).

We conclude now by giving another non trivial bounds for
d
valid for a particular class of IFS’s. The next result is only a rephrasing of Theorems 2 and 3 in
[
3
]
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Assume that the
A_{i}
’s have no common invariant subspaces except
\{0\}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\!
. If
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to
K\in{\cal K}
, then
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to

\displaystyle d=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\rho(f_{i}(K))\geqslant\sigma_{{\cal M}}

and equality holds if
b_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
.

The determination of
\sigma_{{\cal M}}
is delicate but the basic estimates

\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha(A_{i})\}\leqslant\sigma_{{\cal M}}\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\|A_{i}\|\}=\lambda_{H}

always hold (see
[
24
]
). In particular for homotheties, i.e., when
A_{i}=\alpha_{i}\id
with
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
, one obtains
\sigma_{{\cal M}}=\lambda_{H}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}\}
. Unfortunately, this simple case does not fulfill the hypotheses of Proposition
2.6
.

2.3
Case of homotheties

We can give a complete answer when all the
A_{i}
’s are homotheties: the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
always converges and its limit may be explicited. First, we show that
(d_{n})_{n}
converges and we give the possible value for its limit
d
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Assume that
A_{i}=\alpha_{i}\id
with
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Let
j
be an index such that
\alpha_{j}=\lambda_{H}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}\}
. Then
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to a number
d>0
. If
d=\alpha_{j}
then
b_{j}=0
else
d\neq\alpha_{j}
and satisfies

\displaystyle d=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}+\|b_{i}\|\}&\text{if $d>\alpha_{j}$},\\
\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|&\text{if $d<\alpha_{j}$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

For all
n\geqslant 1
we can find
y_{n}\in K_{n}
and
i_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that
d_{n}=\|\alpha_{i_{n}}y_{n}+b_{i_{n}}\|
. Then,
u_{n}=\frac{1}{d_{n}}(\alpha_{i_{n}}y_{n}+b_{i_{n}})
satisfies
u_{n}\in K_{n+1}
and
\|u_{n}\|=1
. Since
\|y_{n}\|\leqslant 1
we obtain

\displaystyle d_{n+1}\geqslant\|\alpha_{i_{n}}u_{n}+b_{i_{n}}\|\geqslant\|\alpha_{i_{n}}u_{n}+d_{n}u_{n}\|-\|d_{n}u_{n}\negthickspace-b_{i_{n}}\|=(\alpha_{i_{n}}\negthickspace+d_{n})\|u_{n}\|-\alpha_{i_{n}}\|y_{n}\|\geqslant d_{n}.

Thus
(d_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is increasing and bounded (see (
2.2
)), so it converges. Let
d
be its limit.

For all
n\geqslant 1
, choosing
x_{n}\in K_{n}
such that
\|x_{n}\|=1
we get

\displaystyle d\geqslant d_{n}\geqslant\|\alpha_{j}x_{n}+b_{j}\|\geqslant\|\alpha_{j}x_{n}\|-\|b_{j}\|=\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|.

(2.7)

Inequality (
2.3
) with
i=j
writes
\|b_{j}\|\leqslant|d-\alpha_{j}|
so
d=\alpha_{j}
implies
b_{j}=0
. If
d<\alpha_{j}
then
d\leqslant\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|
. In addition with (
2.7
) we obtain
d=\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|
.

If
d>\alpha_{j}
, it follows from Proposition
2.5
that
d
is a solution of
\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\frac{\|b_{i}\|}{|t-\alpha_{i}|}=1
. We can consider only the
b_{i}\neq 0
. Then, since
d>\lambda_{H}
and the functions
t\mapsto\frac{\|b_{i}\|}{|t-\alpha_{i}|}
are strictly decreasing on
(\lambda_{H},+\infty)
, the unique solution is
\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}+\|b_{i}\|\}
.
∎

We can state now the precise result. We denote by
\cl(K)
the closure of a non-empty set
K
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Assume that
A_{i}=\alpha_{i}\id
with
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Let
j
,
k
two indices such that
\alpha_{j}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}\}
and
\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}+\|b_{i}\|\}
. Then, for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
, the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to a set
K\in{\cal K}
. Precisely,

(i)

if
b_{j}\neq 0
then

(a)

either
\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|>0
,
f_{i}\big({-}\frac{1}{\|b_{j}\|}b_{j}\big)=(\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|)\big({-}\frac{1}{\|b_{j}\|}b_{j}\big)
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
K_{0}=H_{\rho}^{-1}\big(\big\{{-}\frac{1}{\|b_{j}\|}b_{j}\big\}\big)
: in this case
K=\big\{{-}\frac{1}{\|b_{j}\|}b_{j}\big\}
,

(b)

or else
K
does not depend on
K_{0}
: it is the attractor
L_{d}
with
d=\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
and
\frac{1}{\|b_{k}\|}b_{k}\in L_{d}
;

(ii)

if
b_{j}=0
then

(a)

either
\alpha_{j}\geqslant\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
and then
K=\cl\big({\bigcup}_{n\geqslant 1}K_{n}\big)
,

(b)

or else
\alpha_{j}<\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
and then
K
does not depend on
K_{0}
: it is the attractor
L_{d}
with
d=\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
and
\frac{1}{\|b_{k}\|}b_{k}\in L_{d}
.

Proof.

(i) Assume that
b_{j}\neq 0
. Hence by Lemma
2.7
we have
d\neq\alpha_{j}
.

(a) Suppose first that
d<\alpha_{j}
. Then, use of Lemma
2.7
again shows that
d=\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|
. In particular
\alpha_{j}-\|b_{j}\|\neq 0
and
\alpha_{j}\neq 0
. Moreover, it follows from (
2.7
) that
d_{n}=d
for all
n\geqslant 1
. Let
x_{n}\in K_{n}
and consider the sequence
(x_{n+k})_{k}
defined by
x_{n+k+1}=\frac{1}{d_{n+k}}(\alpha_{j}x_{n+k}+b_{j})=\frac{1}{d}f_{j}(x_{n+k})
for all
k\geqslant 0
. Notice that
x_{n+k}\in K_{n+k}
so in particular
\|x_{n+k}\|\leqslant 1
. Let us introduce
u=-\frac{1}{\|b_{j}\|}b_{j}
the unique point such that
f_{j}(u)=du
. Noticing that
x_{n+k+1}-u=\frac{\alpha_{j}}{d}(x_{n+k}-u)
we obtain by induction that

\displaystyle\forall\,k\geqslant 0,\qquad 2\geqslant\|x_{n+k}-u\|=\left(\frac{\alpha_{j}}{d}\right)^{k}\|x_{n}-u\|\geqslant 0.

Since
d<\alpha_{j}
we must have
\|x_{n}-u\|=0
. It follows that
K_{n}=\{u\}
for all
n\geqslant 1
. Thus
f_{i}(u)=du
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
K=\{u\}
. Therefore conditions of (a) are all fulfilled. Conversely, if they are satisfy we have obviously
K_{n}=K_{1}
for all
n\geqslant 0
and the result.

(b) Suppose now that
d>\alpha_{j}
. Then Lemma
2.7
implies that
d=\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
. Since
d>\lambda_{H}
the convergence to
L_{d}
follows from Theorem
2.1
. Since
L_{d}
is the attractor of the IFS
\big\{\frac{1}{d}f_{1},\ldots,\frac{1}{d}f_{p}\big\}
, it contains the invariant point
z_{k}
of
\frac{1}{d}f_{k}
which is
z_{k}=\frac{1}{\|b_{k}\|}b_{k}
.

(ii) Assume that
b_{j}=0
. Hence by (
2.7
) we have
d\geqslant\alpha_{j}
.

(a) Suppose first that
\alpha_{j}\geqslant\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
. Then it follows from Lemma
2.2
that
d=\alpha_{j}
and then by (
2.7
) that
d_{n}=d
. Therefore, for all

n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle K_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(K_{n})=K_{n}\cup\bigcup_{i\neq j}^{p}f_{i}(K_{n}).

Thus
(K_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is increasing. Since it is bounded it converges to
\cl(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 1}K_{n})
.

(b) Suppose now that
\alpha_{j}<\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
. Assume that
d=\alpha_{j}
. Then inequality (
2.3
) with
i=k
yields either
d\geqslant\alpha_{k}+\|b_{k}\|
or
d\leqslant\alpha_{k}-\|b_{k}\|
. This latter being the unique possibility we obtain
\alpha_{k}\leqslant\alpha_{j}\leqslant\alpha_{k}-\|b_{k}\|
. Thus
b_{k}=0
and
\alpha_{j}=\alpha_{k}
which is a contradiction. Therefore
d>\alpha_{j}
and we conclude along the same lines as for (i)(b).
∎

Let us note that, when
D=1
, the unit sphere being finite, we can prove that
(d_{n})_{n}
is always stationary.

Example 2.9
.

Let us consider the IFS

where the

are given by

with

\displaystyle b_{1}=\left[\begin{matrix}0\\

2\\
\end{matrix}\right].

Then, for all

, the sequence

converges to the attractor of the IFS
\big\{\frac{1}{4}f_{1},\frac{1}{4}f_{2},\frac{1}{4}f_{3}\big\}
. It is a classical Sierpinski gasket (see Fig.

Proof.

We apply Theorem
2.8
with
\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 3}\{\alpha_{i}+\|b_{i}\|\}=4
and
\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 3}\{\alpha_{i}\}=2
(notice here that indices

and

are not unique). Whatever is the choice of

and

, we are here in the case (b). We have

and
(1,0)\in L_{d}

.

Example 2.10
.

Let us consider the IFS

We apply Theorem

with
\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant 3}\{\alpha_{i}+\|b_{i}\|\}=5

Proposition 2.11
.

Assume that
f_{p}(x)=\alpha x+b
with
\alpha\geqslant 0
and that

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\},\qquad f_{i}(B(0,1))\subset B(0,|\alpha-\|b\||).

Then, for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
, the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to a set
K\in{\cal K}
. Precisely,

(i)

if
b\neq 0
then

(a)

either
\alpha-\|b\|>0
,
f_{i}\big({-}\frac{1}{\|b\|}b\big)=(\alpha-\|b\|)\big({-}\frac{1}{\|b\|}b\big)
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
K_{0}=H_{\rho}^{-1}\big(\big\{{-}\frac{1}{\|b_{j}\|}b_{j}\big\}\big)
: in this case
K=\big\{{-}\frac{1}{\|b\|}b\big\}
,

(b)

or else
K
does not depend on
K_{0}
: it is the attractor
L_{d}
with
d=\alpha+\|b\|
and
\frac{1}{\|b\|}b\in L_{d}
;

(ii)

if
b=0
then
K=\cl\big({\bigcup}_{n\geqslant 1}K_{n}\big)
.

Proof.

Actually the proof is very similar to the one of Theorem
2.8
thus we will only detail the key-points.
The first point is to show that
d_{n}
is always obtained with the function
f_{p}
. Indeed, let
n\geqslant 1
and
x_{n}\in K_{n}
with
\|x_{n}\|=1
. Then, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}
,

\displaystyle\|f_{p}(x_{n})\|=\|\alpha x_{n}+b\|\geqslant|\|\alpha x_{n}\|-\|b\||=|\alpha-\|b\||\geqslant\rho(f_{i}(B(0,1)))\geqslant\rho(f_{i}(K_{n})).

It follows that

for some

and

.

Considering now

,
\|u_{n}\|=1
and, since
\|y_{n}\|\leqslant 1
,

\displaystyle d_{n+1}\geqslant\|\alpha u_{n}+b\|\geqslant\|\alpha u_{n}+d_{n}u_{n}\|-\|d_{n}u_{n}-b\|=(\alpha+d_{n})\|u_{n}\|-\alpha\|y_{n}\|\geqslant d_{n}.

(d_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is increasing, bounded, so it converges. Let

be its limit.

In particular, we have proved that

\displaystyle\alpha-\|b\|\leqslant|\alpha-\|b\||\leqslant d_{n}\leqslant d\leqslant\alpha+\|b\|.

(2.8)

Besides, inequality (

) with
i=p
writes

hence either

or

. Finally,

.

(i) Assume that
b\neq 0
. Hence

.

(a) Suppose first that

. This case is similar to the case (i)(a) of Theorem
2.8
.

(b) Suppose now that

. First,

. Next, for

,

\displaystyle\|A_{i}\|\leqslant\sup_{\|x\|=1}\{\|A_{i}x+b_{i}\|\}\leqslant|\alpha-\|b\||<\alpha+\|b\|.

It follows that

and we conclude as for the case (i)(b) of Theorem

.

(ii) Assume that
b=0
. It follows from (
2.8
) that

for all

. This case is then similar to the case (ii)(a) of Theorem
2.8
.
∎

Results for linear IFS’s

We suppose in this section that the IFS consists in
p\geqslant 1
linear maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
defined by
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x
. We still denote by
{\cal M}=\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{p}\}
the set of their canonical matrices.

3.1
New strategy

If
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d
, it follows from Lemma
2.2
that
d\leqslant\lambda_{H}
so we cannot apply Theorem
2.1
. Actually the convergence of
(d_{n})_{n}
may not imply the convergence of
(K_{n})_{n}
. Consider for example
D=2
and the functions
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
defined by

\displaystyle A_{1}=\left[\begin{matrix}2&0\\
0&-3\end{matrix}\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{2}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{matrix}\right].

If
K_{0}=\{(1,0)\}
then
K_{n}=\big\{\big(2^{-k},0\big)\colon 0\leqslant k\leqslant n\big\}
hence converges to
\cl\big(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}K_{n}\big)
and
(d_{n})_{n}
is constant to
2<3=\lambda_{H}
. If
K_{0}=\{(0,1)\}
then
K_{n}=(-1)^{n}\big\{\big(0,3^{-k}\big)\colon 0\leqslant k\leqslant n\big\}
hence diverges but
(d_{n})_{n}
is constant to
\lambda_{H}
.

Therefore we adopt here a new strategy, taking advantage of both the linearity of the
f_{i}
’s and the homogeneity of
\rho
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
. Assume that there exists
d>0
such that
\big(H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to a set
L\in{\cal K}
with
\rho(L)\neq 0
. Then,

(i)

(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d
and
d\leqslant\sigma_{{\cal M}}
,

(ii)

(K_{n})_{n}
converges to
K=\frac{1}{\rho(L)}\,L
.

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant 1
. We obtain by linearity

\displaystyle K_{n}=\frac{1}{d_{0}\cdots d_{n-1}}H^{n}(K_{0}).

Since
\rho(K_{n})=1
, it follows by homogeneity that
\rho\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)=d_{0}\cdots d_{n-1}
. Using linearity again we observe that
H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})=\frac{1}{d^{n}}H^{n}(K_{0})
. Thus
\rho\big(H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})\big)=\prod\limits_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{d_{k}}{d}
. By hypothesis, this last sequence is a proper convergent product, hence
\frac{d_{k}}{d}\to 1
. Moreover, if
d>\sigma_{{\cal M}}
then the joint spectral radius of
\big\{\frac{1}{d}A_{1},\ldots,\frac{1}{d}A_{p}\big\}
is
\frac{\sigma_{{\cal M}}}{d}<1
, hence
\big(H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to
\{0\}
(see
[
3
]
) which is a contradiction. Thus we get
(i)
. Finally,

\displaystyle K_{n}=\frac{1}{\rho\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)}H^{n}(K_{0})=\frac{1}{\rho\big(H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})\big)}H_{d}^{n}(K_{0}).

Hypotheses and continuity of
\rho
allow us to take the limit in the right-hand side above. That gives (ii).
∎

As we saw in Proposition
2.6
if such a
d
exists then
d=\sigma_{{\cal M}}
for a large class of IFS’s. We can expect that this is true in general. However, the first example in this section shows that the strict inequality is possible even for very simple IFS’s. Actually the hypothesis on the common invariant subspaces of the
A_{i}
’s is essential. Notice that the
A_{i}
’s share a common non trivial invariant subspace if and only if there exists an invertible matrix
P
such that, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

\displaystyle A_{i}=P\left[\begin{matrix}A^{\prime}_{i}&M_{i}\\
0&A^{\prime\prime}_{i}\end{matrix}\right]P^{-1}

with
A^{\prime}_{i}
and
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}
square and some matrix
M_{i}
(see
[
24
]
). This in particular the case of diagonal matrices, where the numerous invariant spaces will provide very special behaviors for
(K_{n})_{n}
. In the rest of this section, we will look at such IFS’s focusing on the convergence of
\big(H_{d}^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
especially for
d=1
.

3.2
LCP sets of matrices

We say that
{\cal M}
is a
left convergent product set of matrices
(LCP set in short) if the infinite products
A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}
converge for all sequences
(i)=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots)\in{\cal I}=\{1,\ldots,p\}^{\infty}
. In this case, we set
A_{(i)}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}
(see
[
9
,
16
]
). The theory of LCP sets was popularized in the 90s (see
[
9
]
) and it is still of interest nowadays (see
[
16
]
) for example in the study of inhomogeneous Markov chains (see, e.g.,
[
23
]
). One can always associate a canonical IFS with a LCP set. The next result gives sufficient conditions to obtain its convergence.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that

(i)

{\cal M}
is a
\mathrm{LCP}
set,

(ii)

There exists a sequence
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n}
of positive numbers such that
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
and

\displaystyle\forall\,(i)=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots)\in{\cal I},\qquad\forall\,n\geqslant 1,\qquad\|A_{(i)}-A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}\|\leqslant\varepsilon_{n}.

(3.1)

Then,
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
to the limit set

\displaystyle L=\cl\left(\bigcup_{(i)\in{\cal I}}A_{(i)}(K_{0})\right).

(3.2)

Proof.

Let us write
K_{n}^{\prime}=H^{n}(K_{0})
and
L^{\prime}=\bigcup_{(i)\in{\cal I}}A_{(i)}(K_{0})
. Hypothesis (i) implies that
{\cal M}
is product bounded (see
[
5
]
), then there exists
R>0
such that
\|A_{(i)}\|\leqslant R
for all
(i)\in{\cal I}
. Since
K_{0}
is compact, it follows that
L^{\prime}
is bounded, hence
L
is compact. We claim that
\dha(K_{n}^{\prime},L^{\prime})\leqslant C\varepsilon_{n}
for all
n\geqslant 1
,
C>0
. Let
n\geqslant 1
be fixed. We have

\displaystyle K_{n}^{\prime}=\{A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}(x_{0})\colon x_{0}\in K_{0}\text{ and }1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant p\}.

Let
x^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}
. One has
x^{\prime}=A_{(i)}(x_{0})
with
x_{0}\in K_{0}
and
(i)=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n},\dots)\in{\cal I}
. Let
x=A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}(x_{0})
. One has
x\in K_{n}^{\prime}
and
\|x^{\prime}-x\|\leqslant\|A_{(i)}-A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}\|\|x_{0}\|\leqslant C\varepsilon_{n}
where
C=\rho(K_{0})
. Thus
L^{\prime}\subset K_{n}^{\prime}(C\varepsilon_{n})
. We prove in a similar way that
K_{n}^{\prime}\subset L^{\prime}(C\varepsilon_{n})
, hence
\dha(K_{n}^{\prime},L^{\prime})\leqslant C\varepsilon_{n}
. It follows that
\dha(K_{n}^{\prime},L^{\prime})\to 0
and, since
\dha(K_{n}^{\prime},L^{\prime})=\dha(K_{n}^{\prime},L)
, that
K_{n}^{\prime}\to L
.
∎

We illustrate this result with the family of positive stochastic matrices in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. In this case, we can give a precise description of the limit set
L
. Let us recall that a positive stochastic matrix is a matrix whose rows consist of positive real numbers, with each row summing to
1
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
linear maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of the form
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x
where
A_{i}
is a positive stochastic matrix. Then, for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
, the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to the set

\displaystyle L=\cl\left(\bigcup_{v_{0}\in K_{0}}\big\{(x,x)\colon x\in h_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)\big\}\right),

where
h_{v_{0}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is an affine map which depends on
v_{0}
and
f_{i}
, and
\Gamma
is the attractor of an
\mathrm{IFS}

\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}
where
g_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is an affine map which only depends on
f_{i}
.

Proof.

We will apply Lemma
3.2
. First, since each product
A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}
contains a positive stochastic matrix then it converges (see
[
7
]
) and
{\cal M}
is a LCP set. Next, there exists a matrix
P
of the form

\displaystyle P=\left[\begin{matrix}1&u\\
1&v\end{matrix}\right]\qquad\text{with}\quad u,v\in{\mathbb{R}},

such that, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
A_{i}=PT_{i}P^{-1}
where
T_{i}
is a matrix of the form

\displaystyle T_{i}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&a_{i}\\
0&b_{i}\end{matrix}\right]\qquad\text{with}\quad a_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{i}\in[0,1).

Notice that it is also proved in
[
9
]
that
\{T_{1},\ldots,T_{p}\}
is a LCP set with a continuous limit function. Here we want more and describe precisely the limit set of matrices. Let us define
g_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
g_{i}(x)=b_{i}x+a_{i}
and set
b=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}b_{i}<1
. We obtain by induction that, for all sequences of indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}
,
n\geqslant 2
,

\displaystyle T_{i_{n}}\cdots T_{i_{1}}=\left[\begin{matrix}1&(g_{i_{1}}\circ g_{i_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{i_{n-1}})(a_{i_{n}})\\
0&b_{i_{1}}\cdots b_{i_{n}}\end{matrix}\right].

Hence, considering the contractive Hutchinson operator
G
associated with the IFS
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}
, its attractor
\Gamma
, and the orbit
\big(G^{n}(A)\big)_{n}
of the compact set
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}\}
, we obtain, for all
n\geqslant 2
,

\displaystyle\big\{T_{i_{n}}\cdots T_{i_{1}}\big\}_{1\leqslant i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}\leqslant p}=\left\{\left[\begin{matrix}1&c_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}\\
0&b(c_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}})\end{matrix}\right],\,c_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}}\in G^{n-1}(A)\right\}

with
0\leqslant b(c_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}})\leqslant b^{n}
and
\text{d}(c_{i_{1}\cdots i_{n}},\Gamma)\leqslant Cb^{n}
for a constant
C>0
(see
[
17
]
).

It follows first that, for all
(i)\in{\cal I}
and all
n\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\|A_{(i)}-A_{i_{n}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}\|=\big\|P\big(T_{(i)}-T_{i_{n}}\cdots T_{i_{1}}\big)P^{-1}\big\|\leqslant\|P\|\|T_{(i)}-T_{i_{n}}\cdots T_{i_{1}}\|\big\|P^{-1}\big\|\leqslant C^{\prime}b^{n}

with
C^{\prime}>0
. Hence (
3.1
) and all the hypotheses of Lemma
3.2
are satisfied with
\varepsilon_{n}=C^{\prime}b^{n}
.

Moreover, letting
n
goes to
\infty
we obtain the following set of limit matrices:

\displaystyle\left\{T_{(i)}\right\}_{(i)\in{\cal I}}=\left\{\left[\begin{matrix}1&c\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right],\,c\in\Gamma\right\}.

Therefore, if
v_{0}=(x_{0},y_{0})\in K_{0}
we get

\displaystyle A_{(i)}(v_{0})=\left(P\left[\begin{matrix}1&c\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right]P^{-1}\right)\left[\begin{matrix}x_{0}\\
y_{0}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}\dfrac{y_{0}-x_{0}}{v-u}c+\dfrac{x_{0}v-y_{0}u}{v-u}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\dfrac{y_{0}-x_{0}}{v-u}c+\dfrac{x_{0}v-y_{0}u}{v-u}\end{matrix}\right].

The result follows by taking
h_{v_{0}}(x)=\frac{y_{0}-x_{0}}{v-u}x+\frac{x_{0}v-y_{0}u}{v-u}
and using (
3.2
).
∎

Notice that if
K_{0}\subset\Span\{(1,1)\}
then
f_{i}(v_{0})=v_{0}
and
L=K_{0}
. Actually,
\Span\{(1,1)\}
is a common invariant space of the
A_{i}
’s so that
K_{n}=K_{0}
for all
n\geqslant 0
. In particular, one cannot apply Proposition
2.6
but it follows from the decomposition of the
A_{i}
’s that
d=1=\sigma_{{\cal M}}
.

Example 3.4
.

Let us consider the IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2}\}
where the
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
are the linear maps given by their canonical matrices

\displaystyle A_{1}=\frac{1}{4}\left[\begin{matrix}1+3a&3-3a\\
1-a&3+a\end{matrix}\right]\qquad\text{and}\qquad A_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\begin{matrix}1+a&1-a\\

with

. With

Thus
g_{1}(x)=ax
,

and
\Gamma
is the Cantor set

when

and the interval
[0,1]
when

. For example, when

,

if

if

(see Fig.

(b)). More

contains points then more complicated is the limit set

, with unions of overlapping Cantor sets.

3.3
Identity-block matrices

Hypothesis (
3.1
) implies that the address function
{\cal A}\colon(i)\mapsto A_{(i)}
is continuous. Unfortunately, very simple LCP sets may not fulfill this condition preventing from applying Lemma
3.2
. This situation happens for example adding the matrix
\id
to a LCP set with a continuous function
{\cal A}
(see
[
9
]
). However this simple case can be solved directly.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
A_{p}=\id
and
\|A_{i}\|\leqslant 1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}
. Then, for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
, the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to the set

\displaystyle L=\cl\left(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}H^{n}(K_{0})\right).

Moreover, if
\|A_{i}\|<1
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\}
, then denoting by
L^{\prime}
the attractor of the contractive
\mathrm{IFS}

\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{p-1}\}
, we have
L=L^{\prime}
as soon as
K_{0}\subset L^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Since
f_{p}=\id
, the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
is clearly increasing. So it is enough to prove that it is bounded to get the convergence to
L
. Let
R>0
be large enough to ensure
K_{0}\subset B(0,R)
. Then,
f_{i}(B(0,R))\subset B(0,R)
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Therefore
H^{n}(K_{0})\subset B(0,R)
for all
n\geqslant 0
. Now, observe that, for all
K\in{\cal K}
,
H(K)=H^{\prime}(K)\cup K
where
H^{\prime}
is the Hutchinson operator associated with the
\mathrm{IFS}

\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{p-1}\}
. Assume that
K_{0}\subset L^{\prime}
. Then,
H(K_{0})\subset H(L^{\prime})=H^{\prime}(L^{\prime})\cup L^{\prime}=L^{\prime}\cup L^{\prime}=L^{\prime}
. By induction it follows that
H^{n}(K_{0})\subset L^{\prime}
for all
n\geqslant 0
. Taking the limit we get
L\subset L^{\prime}
. Next, we have
L=H(L)=H^{\prime}(L)\cup L\supset H^{\prime}(L)
. By induction it follows that
L\supset(H^{\prime})^{n}(L)
for all
n\geqslant 0
. Taking the limit we get
L\supset L^{\prime}
, and finally
L=L^{\prime}
.
∎

These latter sequences are related to the so-called inhomogeneous IFS’s (see, e.g.,
[
14
]
). Indeed, one has
H^{n}(K_{0})=H_{0}^{n}(K_{0})
where
H_{0}
is the Hutchinson operator associated with the contractive IFS
\{f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{p-1}\}
where
f_{0}\colon K\in{\cal K}\mapsto K_{0}
.

We can generalize the previous result to matrices
A_{i}
which contain an identity-block, that is when the restriction of
f_{i}
to a certain subspace of
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
is the identity function. We will state two results dealing with two special cases of such families.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that there exists two subspaces
V,W\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
satisfying
V\oplus W={\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

(i)

f_{i}(V)\subset V
and the linear function
f_{i,V}\colon V\to V
induced by
f_{i}
is a contraction or the identity function
\id
,

(ii)

f_{i}(W)\subset W
and the linear function
f_{i,W}\colon W\to W
induced by
f_{i}
is a contraction.

Then,
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
to a set
L
. Precisely,

–

either there is at least one
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that
f_{i,V}=\id
, then

\displaystyle L=\cl\left(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}p_{V,W}\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)\right),

where
p_{V,W}
is the projection onto
V
along
W
,

–

or else
L=\{0\}
.

Moreover,
L
is the limit of the sequence
\big(H_{V}^{n}(p_{V,W}(K_{0}))\big)_{n}
where
H_{V}
is associated with the
\mathrm{IFS}

\{f_{1,V},\ldots,f_{p,V}\}

Proof.

Let us write
V_{0}=p_{V,W}(K_{0})
,
W_{0}=p_{W,V}(K_{0})
where
p_{W,V}
is the projection onto
W
along
V
, and set
\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\|f_{i,W}\|<1
. Finally let us write
K^{\prime}_{n}=H_{V}^{n}(V_{0})
.

(a) Let
x=v+w\in V\oplus W
. Since
f_{i}(x)=f_{i,V}(v)+f_{i,W}(w)
, we can write

\displaystyle\forall\,n\geqslant 0,\qquad H^{n}(K_{0})=\bigcup_{v\in K^{\prime}_{n}}v+L_{n}(v),

where
L_{n}(v)\subset W
satisfies
p_{V,W}(L_{n}(v))=v
. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\dha\big(H^{n}(K_{0}),K^{\prime}_{n}\big)=\dha\left(\bigcup_{v\in K^{\prime}_{n}}v+L_{n}(v),\bigcup_{v\in K^{\prime}_{n}}\{v\}\right)\leqslant\sup_{v\in K^{\prime}_{n}}\dha(v+L_{n}(v),\{v\}).

Let
v\in K^{\prime}_{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\dha(v+L_{n}(v),\{v\})=\sup_{z\in L_{n}(v)}\|z-v\|=\sup_{z\in L_{n}(v)}\|z-p_{V,W}(z)\|.

Let
z\in L_{n}(v)
. We can write

\displaystyle z=(f_{i_{n}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1}})(z_{0})=(f_{i_{n},V}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1},V})(v_{0})+(f_{i_{n},W}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1},W})(w_{0})

with
z_{0}=v_{0}+w_{0}\in V_{0}\oplus W_{0}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\|z-p_{V,W}(z)\|=\|(f_{i_{n},W}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1},W})(w_{0})\|\leqslant\|f_{i_{n},W}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1},W}\|\|w_{0}\|\leqslant\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}^{n}\sup_{w_{0}\in W_{0}}\|w_{0}\|.

It follows that
\dha\big(H^{n}(K_{0}),K^{\prime}_{n}\big)\leqslant\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}^{n}\sup\limits_{w_{0}\in W_{0}}\|w_{0}\|\to 0
when
n\to\infty
.

(b) Therefore, it is now enough to prove the convergence of
(K^{\prime}_{n})_{n}
.

Assume first that
f_{i,V}\neq\id
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Then
H_{V}
is contractive and we get
K^{\prime}_{n}\to\{0\}
. Thus
K_{n}\to L=\{0\}
.

Assume now that there is at least one
j\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that
f_{j,V}=\id
. Since
\|f_{i,V}\|\leqslant 1
, it follows from Lemma
3.5
that
K^{\prime}_{n}\to L=\cl\big(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}K^{\prime}_{n}\big)
. Let us prove by induction that
K^{\prime}_{n}=p_{V,W}\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)
. We have
K^{\prime}_{0}=H_{V}^{0}(V_{0})=V_{0}=p_{V,W}(K_{0})
and

\displaystyle K^{\prime}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=H_{V}(K^{\prime}_{n})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i,V}(K^{\prime}_{n})=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i,V}\big(p_{V,W}\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)\big)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}p_{V,W}\big(f_{i}\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)\big)=p_{V,W}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)\right)=p_{V,W}\big(H^{n+1}(K_{0})\big).

Hence the result. Therefore
K_{n}\to L=\cl\big(\bigcup_{n\geqslant 0}p_{V,W}\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)\big)
that ends the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.7
.

Assume that there exists two subspaces
V,W\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
satisfying
V\oplus W={\mathbb{R}}^{D}
and, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,

(i)

(f_{i}-\id)(V)\subset W
,

(ii)

f_{i}(W)\subset W
and the linear function
f_{i,W}\colon W\to W
induced by
f_{i}
is a contraction.

Then,
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
to the set

\displaystyle L=\cl\left(\bigcup_{v_{0}\in p_{V,W}(K_{0})}v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0})\right),

where
p_{V,W}
is the projection onto
V
along
W
and
\widetilde{L}(v_{0})
is the attractor of the
\mathrm{IFS}

\big\{\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{p}\big\}
where
\widetilde{f}_{i}\colon W\to W
is defined by
\widetilde{f}_{i}(w)=f_{i,W}(w)+(f_{i}-\id)(v_{0})
.

In particular,
p_{V,W}(L)=p_{V,W}(K_{0})
.

Proof.

Let us write
V_{0}=p_{V,W}(K_{0})
,
W_{0}=p_{W,V}(K_{0})
where
p_{W,V}
is the projection onto
W
along
V
, and set
\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\|f_{i,W}\|=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\|\widetilde{f}_{i}\|<1
.

(a) First let
z_{0}\in K_{0}
with
z_{0}=v_{0}+w_{0}\in V_{0}+W_{0}\subset V\oplus W
. We have

\displaystyle\forall\,i\in\{1,\ldots,p\},\qquad f_{i}(z_{0})=v_{0}+\big((f_{i}(v_{0})-v_{0})+f_{i}(w_{0})\big)=v_{0}+\widetilde{f}_{i}(w_{0})\in V_{0}+W.

We then prove by induction that

\displaystyle\forall\,n\geqslant 0,\qquad H^{n}(\{z_{0}\})=v_{0}+\widetilde{H}^{n}(\{w_{0}\}),

where
\widetilde{H}
is the Hutchinson operator associated with the contractive IFS
\big\{\widetilde{f}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{f}_{p}\big\}
. Therefore the sequence
\big(\widetilde{H}^{n}(w_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to a set
\widetilde{L}(v_{0})
which only depends on
v_{0}
. It follows that
\big(H^{n}(\{z_{0}\})\big)_{n}
converges to
v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0})
with the estimate

\displaystyle\dha\big(v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0}),v_{0}+\widetilde{H}^{n}(w_{0})\big)=\dha\big(\widetilde{L}(v_{0}),\widetilde{H}^{n}(w_{0})\big)\leqslant\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}^{n}\dha\big(\widetilde{L}(v_{0}),w_{0}\big).

(b) Let

\displaystyle R=\frac{1}{1-\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\big\{\rho((f_{i}-\id)(V_{0}))\big\}.

We have
\widetilde{f}_{i}(B(0,R)\cap W)\subset B(0,R)\cap W
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
so that
\widetilde{H}(B(0,R)\cap W)\subset B(0,R)\cap W
. It follows that
\widetilde{L}(v_{0})\subset B(0,R)\cap W
for all
v_{0}\in V_{0}
. Thus

\displaystyle\forall\,v_{0}\in V_{0},\qquad\rho\big(v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0})\big)\leqslant\|v_{0}\|+R\leqslant\rho(V_{0})+R.

We then have proved that the set
\,\bigcup_{v_{0}\in V_{0}}v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0})
is bounded, i.e.,
L\in{\cal K}
.

Furthermore, since
\widetilde{L}(v_{0})
does not depend on
w_{0}
, we can write

\displaystyle\bigcup_{v_{0}\in V_{0}}v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0})=\bigcup_{v_{0}+w_{0}\in K_{0}}v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0}).

(c) Finally, writing
H^{n}(K_{0})=\bigcup_{v_{0}+w_{0}\in K_{0}}v_{0}+\widetilde{H}^{n}(w_{0})
and using (a) and (b), we obtain

\displaystyle\dha\big(L,H^{n}(K_{0})\big)

\displaystyle=\dha\left(\bigcup_{v_{0}+w_{0}\in K_{0}}v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0}),\bigcup_{v_{0}+w_{0}\in K_{0}}v_{0}+\widetilde{H}^{n}(w_{0})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sup_{v_{0}+w_{0}\in K_{0}}\dha\big(v_{0}+\widetilde{L}(v_{0}),v_{0}+\widetilde{H}^{n}(w_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant\widetilde{\lambda}_{H}^{n}\sup_{v_{0}+w_{0}\in K_{0}}\dha\big(\widetilde{L}(v_{0}),w_{0}\big).

Since this latter supremum is finite, we obtain the result by letting
n
goes to
\infty
.
∎

Notice that hypotheses of Theorem
3.7
mean that, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
, the maps
f_{i}
’s have a block matrix with respect to the sum

of the form

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\id&0\\
M_{i}&\widetilde{A}_{i}\end{matrix}\right]

with
\widetilde{A}_{i}
contractive and some matrix

. We deduce that
\sigma_{{\cal M}}=1
.

In particular, when

for all

hence

for all

and the limit set is simply

3.6

3.4
Orbit of the unit ball

To avoid the various behaviors due to the different invariant subspaces of the
A_{i}
’s, we propose to take into account all the directions of
{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
by focusing on the
H
-orbit of the unit ball
B(0,1)
.

Proposition 3.9
.

Assume that

(i)

For all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
,
\,\|A_{i}\|\leqslant 1
,

(ii)

There exists
N\geqslant 1
indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{N}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
such that the matrix
A_{i_{N}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}
has eigenvalue
1
.

Then, the sequence
(H^{n}(B(0,1)))_{n}
is decreasing and converges to the set

\displaystyle L=\bigcap_{n\geqslant 0}H^{n}(B(0,1))

(3.3)

with
\rho(L)=1
. Moreover,
H_{\rho}^{n}(B(0,1))=H^{n}(B(0,1))
and
d_{n}=1
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Hypothesis (i) implies that
H(B(0,1))\subset B(0,1)
, then
\big(H^{n}(B(0,1))\big)_{n}
is decreasing and converges to
L
. Let
v\in B(0,1)
with
\|v\|=1
and such that
A_{i_{N}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}v=v
. One has
v\in H^{kN}(K_{0})
for all
k\geqslant 0
, thus
v\in L
. It follows
L\neq\{0\}
and
v\in H^{n}(B(0,1))
for all
n\geqslant 0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle 1=\|v\|\leqslant\rho(L)\leqslant\rho\big(H^{n}(B(0,1))\big)\leqslant\rho(B(0,1))=1.

This yields
H_{\rho}^{n}(B(0,1))=H^{n}(B(0,1))
and
d_{n}=1
.
∎

Notice that the two hypotheses imply that
\alpha(A_{i_{N}}\cdots A_{i_{1}})=\|A_{i_{N}}\cdots A_{i_{1}}\|=1
, hence
\sigma_{{\cal M}}=1
. Notice also that
{\cal M}
need not to be a LCP set. One can for example consider matrices with rotations or symmetries.

3.5
Angular structure of the limit set in
\boldsymbol{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
3.9
, the set
L
defined by (
3.3
) is an eigenset for
H
associated with the eigenvalue
\sigma_{{\cal M}}=1
. It is straightforward to see that, as the ball
B(0,1)
, the set
L
is symmetric and star-shaped with respect to the origin, i.e., if
x\in L
then
rx\in L
for any
r\in[-1,1]
. Such a property was also discussed in
[
3
]
. More generally, if
K_{0}
is symmetric and star-shaped with respect to the origin, then the same holds for every set
H^{n}(K_{0})
and thus for its limit set
L
in case of convergence. Therefore, such a limit set
L
admits a polar representation and it would be especially interesting to know its angular structure. In this section we state a result in this direction for IFS’s in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}=\big\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\colon x\geqslant 0\big\}\backslash\{0\}
. Every point
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{P}}
may be written with polar coordinates as
(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta)
with
R>0
and
\theta\in\big[{-}\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big]
. Let
K\in\cal{K}
with
K\neq\{0\}
. Assume moreover that
K
is symmetric with respect to the origin. Then
K\cap{\mathbb{P}}\neq\varnothing
and we can define its
set of slopes

S_{K}\subset[-\infty,\infty]
by

\displaystyle S_{K}=\big\{\tan\theta\,|\,\exists\,(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta)\in K\cap{\mathbb{P}}\big\}

with the convention
\tan\big({\pm}\frac{\pi}{2}\big)=\pm\infty
.

The following result provides a description of the set of slopes of
L
for particular IFS’s.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
linear maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
given by their canonical matrices

A_{i}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a_{i}&b_{i}\\
c_{i}&d_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\right]

such that
\det(A_{i})\neq 0
and
f_{i}({\mathbb{P}})={\mathbb{P}}
. Let
K_{0}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a symmetric and star-shaped with respect to the origin set. Assume that the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to a set
L\neq\{0\}
. Then
L
is symmetric and star-shaped with respect to the origin and its set of slopes
S_{L}
is a non-empty invariant set of the Hutchinson operator
\widehat{H}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\widehat{f}_{i}
where
\widehat{f}_{i}\colon[-\infty,\infty]\to[-\infty,\infty]
is the homographic function defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{i}(z)=\frac{d_{i}z+c_{i}}{b_{i}z+a_{i}}.

Proof.

Notice that
S_{L}
is well-defined. Writing
L\cap{\mathbb{P}}=\{(R\cos\theta,R\sin\theta)\colon R>0,\theta\in\Theta\}
we get
S_{L}=\tan\Theta
. Observing that
L\cap{\mathbb{P}}=H(L)\cap{\mathbb{P}}
, we have
S_{L}=S_{H(L)}
. We deduce now from the hypothesis that

\displaystyle H(L)\cap{\mathbb{P}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\big(f_{i}(L)\cap{\mathbb{P}}\big)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\big(f_{i}(L)\cap f_{i}({\mathbb{P}})\big)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(L\cap{\mathbb{P}})=H(L\cap{\mathbb{P}}).

Hence
H(L)\cap{\mathbb{P}}
is the set of the points
f_{i}(s)
for all
s\in L\cap{\mathbb{P}}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. Since

\displaystyle f_{i}(s)=A_{i}\left[\begin{matrix}R\cos\theta\\
R\sin\theta\end{matrix}\right]=R\left[\begin{matrix}a_{i}\cos\theta+b_{i}\sin\theta\\
c_{i}\cos\theta+d_{i}\sin\theta\end{matrix}\right],

the set of slopes of
H(L)
is exactly the set of all the points of the form

\displaystyle\frac{c_{i}\cos\theta+d_{i}\sin\theta}{a_{i}\cos\theta+b_{i}\sin\theta}=\widehat{f}_{i}(\tan\theta),\qquad\theta\in\Theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}.

Therefore,
S_{H(L)}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}\widehat{f}_{i}(\tan\Theta)
. The results follows.
∎

The angular structure of
L
is then known as soon as we can describe the invariant sets of the Hutchinson operator
\widehat{H}
. This operator may have several invariant sets
S
, not necessarily closed. However, there is a useful way to determine them. Indeed, if
\widehat{H}
is contractive then every bounded invariant set
S
of
\widehat{H}
satisfies
\cl(S)=\widehat{L}
where
\widehat{L}
is the attractor of
\widehat{H}
. It is possible to determine such attractors of IFS made up with homographic functions (see for example
[
12
, p. 136]
). Notice that
[-\infty,\infty]
is always a compact set and then would be an invariant set. Thus, it will be often necessary to consider a restriction of
\widehat{H}
to obtain a contractive operator.

4
Other renormalizations

We have renormalized the sets
H^{n}(K_{0})
by dividing them by their radius. As we mentioned in the introduction, one usually rather uses the diameter
to rescale a sequence of compact sets. Thus, we can wonder what a such renormalization would yield. More generally, we want to study in this section the iteration of more general operators
H_{\varphi}
which will provide various ways to approximate the solutions of the eigen-equation
H(K)=dK
. We keep using the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
but choosing a well-adapted operator according to the form of the matrices
A_{i}
’s.

4.1
Renormalization with a size function

We are interested in functions
\varphi
that describe the size of a compact set and the way it occupies the space. Following the example of the radius function
\rho
, we will say that a function
\varphi\colon{\cal K}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
is a
size function
if it is continuous with respect to
\dha
, monotonic and homogeneous (see Section
1.3
). For example, the max-radius function
\rho_{\infty}
and the diameter
\delta
respectively defined on
{\cal K}
by

\displaystyle\rho_{\infty}(K)=\max_{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{D})\in K}\{|x_{j}|\colon 1\leqslant j\leqslant D\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\delta(K)=\max\{\|x-y\|\colon x,y\in K\}

are two size functions.

We define an associated operator
H_{\varphi}
in the same way as (
1.1
) setting

\displaystyle\forall\,K\in{\cal K},\qquad H_{\varphi}(K)=\frac{1}{\varphi(H(K))}H(K)

and consider the
H_{\varphi}
-orbit of some set
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
. Let us keep all the notation of the previous sections, easily adapted by replacing
\rho
with
\varphi
. In particular
K_{n}=H_{\varphi}^{n}(K_{0})
(see (
1.3
)) and
d_{n}=\varphi\big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(K_{n})\big)
(see the first equality in (
1.4
)). We will assume that
K_{n}
is always well-defined, i.e.,
\varphi(K_{n})\neq 0
.

We are still interested in the convergence of the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
and the description of its limit. The key-points are Theorem
2.1
and Lemma
3.1
which provide general conditions of convergence. We summarize here the main results which still hold for any size functions.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
affine maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
of the form
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x+b_{i}
.

(i)

If
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d>\lambda_{H}
, then
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to the attractor
L_{d}
and
d
satisfies the inequality

\displaystyle\varphi\big(\big\{(d\id-A_{1})^{-1}b_{1},\ldots,(d\id-A_{p})^{-1}b_{p}\big\}\big)\leqslant 1.

(ii)

Assume that
b_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
. If
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to a set
L\in{\cal K}
such that
\varphi(L)\neq 0
, then
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to
K=\frac{1}{\varphi(L)}L
.

In particular, when all the
f_{i}
’s are linear, the choice of
\varphi
is not important. Therefore, all the results of Section
3
providing the convergence of the sequence
\big(H^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
(e.g., Lemma
3.2
, Lemma
3.5
, Theorem
3.7
) and the description of its limit
L
(Proposition
3.11
) may be used. In case of convergence, the limit set
K
will only depend on
\varphi
through the scaling factor
\varphi(L)
.

4.2
Renormalization with the max-radius function

We consider here the size function
\rho_{\infty}
, which is nothing but the ‘radius function’ associated with the usual maximum norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. In particular, Property (
1.2
), Lemma
2.2
and Proposition
2.3
still work
mutatis mutandis
. Therefore it should be possible to obtain a result similar to Theorem
2.8
. Since the Euclidean norm is isotropic we assumed in Theorem
2.8
the
A_{i}
’s were homotheties. Here, the maximum norm allows us to deal with more general diagonal matrices. However, many complicated particular situations may happen. For the sake of simplicity, we only state a simpler result with an additional hypothesis avoiding these special behaviors.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{D}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
of the form
f_{i}(x)=A_{i}x+b_{i}
where
A_{i}=\diag(a_{i,1},\ldots,a_{i,D})
is a diagonal matrix with non-negative entries and
b_{i}=(b_{i,1},\ldots,b_{i,D})
. Let
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
. Assume that

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant p\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant D\end{subarray}}\left\{|a_{i,j}x_{j}+b_{i,j}|\colon x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{D})\in H_{\rho_{\infty}}(K_{0})\right\}>\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant p\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant D\end{subarray}}\big\{|a_{i,j}|\big\}.

(4.1)

Then, the sequence
\big(H_{\rho_{\infty}}^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to
L_{d}
where
d=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}d_{n}
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of Theorem
2.8
. First we claim that
(d_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
always converges. Indeed, for all
n\geqslant 1
we can find
x_{n}\in K_{n}
,
i_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
and
j_{n}\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
such that
d_{n}=|a_{i_{n},j_{n}}x_{n,j_{n}}+b_{i_{n},j_{n}}|
. Then,
u_{n}=\frac{1}{d_{n}}(A_{i_{n}}x_{n}+b_{i_{n}})
satisfies
u_{n}\in K_{n+1}
and
|u_{n,j_{n}}|=1
. Since
|x_{n,j_{n}}|\leqslant 1
we obtain

\displaystyle d_{n+1}\geqslant\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant D}\{|a_{i_{n},j}u_{n,j}+b_{i_{n},j}|\}

\displaystyle\geqslant\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant D}\{|a_{i_{n},j}u_{n,j}+d_{n}u_{n,j}|-|d_{n}u_{n,j}-b_{i_{n},j}|\}

\displaystyle\geqslant|a_{i_{n},j_{n}}u_{n,k_{n}}+d_{n}u_{n,j_{n}}|-|d_{n}u_{n,j_{n}}-b_{i_{n},j_{n}}|

\displaystyle\geqslant(a_{i_{n},j_{n}}+d_{n})|u_{n,j_{n}}|-a_{i_{n},j_{n}}|x_{n,j_{n}}|

\displaystyle\geqslant d_{n}+a_{i_{n},j_{n}}(1-|x_{n,j_{n}}|)\geqslant d_{n}.

Hence,
(d_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is increasing and bounded so it converges to a number
d\geqslant 0
. Notice that the left-hand side of (
4.1
) is
d_{1}
and the right-hand side of (
4.1
) is
\lambda_{H}
. Thus one has
d\geqslant d_{1}>\lambda_{H}
and the result follows from Theorem
2.1
.
∎

4.3
Renormalization with the diameter function

We consider now the diameter function
\delta
. The situation is more complicated than the previous ones, even if the matrices
A_{i}
’s are homotheties. The stability property (
1.2
) of
\rho
was a key-point in the proof of Theorem
2.8
but unfortunately it is no longer satisfied. We will only deal with the one dimensional case
D=1
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
affine maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
of the form
f_{i}(x)=\alpha_{i}x+b_{i}
with
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
. Let us define the function

\displaystyle F(x)=\frac{\min\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}x+b_{i}\}}{\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}(x+1)+b_{i}\}-\min\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}x+b_{i}\}}.

(4.2)

Assume that the sequence
\big(F^{n}(u)\big)_{n}
starting at
u=\min(H_{\delta}(K_{0}))
converges to a number
c\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then,

(i)

(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}(c+1)+b_{i}\}-\min\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}c+b_{i}\}
,

(ii)

If
d>\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}\}
then
\big(H_{\delta}^{n}(K_{0})\big)_{n}
converges to
L_{d}
whose convex hull is
[c,c+1]
.

Proof.

Let us write
\ch(K_{n})=[a_{n},a_{n}+1]
for any
n\geqslant 1
.

(i) Let
d(x)=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}(x+1)+b_{i}\}-\min\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}x+b_{i}\}
. Since
\alpha_{i}\geqslant 0
one checks that
d_{n}=d(a_{n})
and
a_{n+1}=F(a_{n})
where
F
is given by (
4.2
). The result follows.

(ii) Since
\lambda_{H}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant p}\{\alpha_{i}\}
the result is obtained by applying Theorem
2.1
. Finally, since
\ch(L_{d})=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\ch(K_{n})
, we get the last part of the assertion.
∎

This Proposition gives a very simple and practical tool to prove the convergence of
(K_{n})_{n}
. It is enough to study the orbits of
F
which is just a piecewise homographic function.

Example 4.4
.

Let us consider the IFS
\{f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}\}
where the
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are given by

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)=2x+1,\qquad f_{2}(x)=3x-4\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{3}(x)=x+2.

Then, for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
, the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to the attractor
L_{d}
with
d=5+2\sqrt{3}
. In particular its convex hull is the interval
\big[1-\sqrt{3},2-\sqrt{3}\big]
.

Proof.

First we determine the function
F
. We obtain

\displaystyle F(x)=\left(\frac{3x-4}{7-2x}\right)1\negthickspace\text{I}_{(-\infty,0]}(x)+\left(\frac{3x-4}{7-x}\right)1\negthickspace\text{I}_{(0,3]}(x)

\displaystyle\hphantom{F(x) =}{}+\left(\frac{x+2}{x+1}\right)1\negthickspace\text{I}_{(3,4]}(x)+\left(\frac{x+2}{2x-3}\right)1\negthickspace\text{I}_{(4,+\infty)}(x).

The only invariant point of
F
is
c=1-\sqrt{3}
and one checks that
(F^{n}(u))_{n}
converges to
c
for every
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Thus
(d_{n})_{n}
converges to
d=f_{3}(c+1)-f_{2}(c)=5+2\sqrt{3}
. Since
d>\max\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\}=5
, the result follows from Proposition
4.3
.
∎

Example 4.5
.

Let
p\geqslant 1
affine maps
f_{i}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
of the form
f_{i}(x)=\alpha x+b_{i}
with
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
b_{1}<\cdots<b_{p}
. Then, for all
K_{0}\in{\cal K}
, the sequence
(K_{n})_{n}
converges to
L_{d}
with
d=\alpha+(b_{p}-b_{1})
.

Proof.

We get

\displaystyle F(x)=\frac{\alpha x+b_{1}}{\alpha+(b_{p}-b_{1})}.

Thus
F
is a contraction,
(a_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
converges to the invariant point
c=\frac{b_{1}}{b_{p}-b_{1}}
and
(d_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
is constant to
d=\alpha+(b_{p}-b_{1})
. Since
d>\alpha=\lambda_{H}
, the result follows from Proposition
4.3
.
∎
