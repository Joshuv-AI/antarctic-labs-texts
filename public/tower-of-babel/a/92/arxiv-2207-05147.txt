Asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry for the Fisher-KPP equation
François Hamel, Luca Rossi

Abstract
Let u u be a solution of the Fisher-KPP equation ∂ t u = Δ ​ u + f ⁡ ( u ) , t > 0 , x ∈ ℝ N . \partial_{t}u=\Delta u+f(u),\quad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}. (0.2) We address the following question: does u u become locally planar as t → + ∞ t\to+\infty ? Namely, does u ( t n , x n + ⋅ ) u(t_{n},x_{n}+\cdot) converge locally uniformly, up to subsequences, towards a one-dimensional function, for any sequence ( ( t n , x n ) ) n ∈ ℕ ((t_{n},x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}} in ( 0 , + ∞ ) × ℝ N (0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N} such that t n → + ∞ t_{n}\to+\infty as n → + ∞ n\to+\infty ? This question is in the spirit of a conjecture of De Giorgi for stationary solutions of Allen-Cahn equations. The answer depends on the initial datum u 0 u_{0} of u u . It is known to be affirmative when the support of u 0 u_{0} is bounded or when it lies between two parallel half-spaces. Instead, the answer is negative when the support of u 0 u_{0} is “V-shaped”. We prove here that u u is asymptotically locally planar when the support of u 0 u_{0} is a convex set (satisfying in addition a uniform interior ball condition), or, more generally, when it is at finite Hausdorff distance from a convex set. We actually derive the result under an even more general geometric hypothesis on the support of u 0 u_{0} . We recover in particular the aforementioned results known in the literature. We further characterize the set of directions in which u u is asymptotically locally planar, and we show that the asymptotic profiles are monotone. Our results apply in particular when the support of u 0 u_{0} is the subgraph of a function with vanishing global mean.



1
Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the large time description of solutions of the Fisher-KPP reaction-diffusion equation

\partial_{t}u=\Delta u+f(u),\quad t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

(1.1)

with
N\geq 2
. The Fisher-KPP condition of
[
10
,
18
]
is

\begin{cases}f(0)=f(1)=0,\\
f(s)>0\hbox{ for all }s\in(0,1),\\
\displaystyle s\mapsto\frac{f(s)}{s}\hbox{ is nonincreasing in $(0,1]$}.\end{cases}

(1.2)

As long as the regularity is concerned, we assume that
f\in C^{1}([0,1])
. These assumptions on
f
will always be understood to hold.

We consider the Cauchy problem associated with (
1.1
). The initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}
is assumed to be a characteristic function
\mathbbm{1}_{U}
of a measurable set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, i.e.

u_{0}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\hbox{if }x\in U,\\
0&\hbox{if }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\!\setminus\!U.\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

This Cauchy problem is well posed and, given
u_{0}
, there is a unique bounded classical solution
u
of (
1.1
) such that
u(t,\cdot)\to u_{0}
as
t\to 0^{+}
in
L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Furthermore,
0\leq u(t,x)\leq 1
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, from the maximum principle. For mathematical convenience, we extend
f
by
0
in
\mathbb{R}\setminus[0,1]
, and the extended function, still denoted
f
, is then Lipschitz continuous in
\mathbb{R}
.

Instead of initial conditions
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
, we could also have considered multiples
\alpha\mathbbm{1}_{U}
of characteristic functions, with
\alpha>0
, or even other more general initial conditions
0\leq u_{0}\leq 1
for which the upper level set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:u_{0}(x)\geq h\}
is at bounded Hausdorff distance from the support of
u_{0}
, for some
h\in(0,1)
(see Section
8
below). But we preferred to keep the assumption
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
for the sake of simplicity of the presentation of the statements.

The goal of the paper is to understand whether, and under which condition on the initial datum, the solution of (
1.1
) eventually becomes locally planar as time goes on. To express this property in a rigorous way, we consider the notion of the
\Omega
-limit set
of a given bounded function
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\hskip-8.0pt\Omega(u)\!:=\!\big\{&\!\!\!\!\!\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\,:\,u(t_{n},x_{n}+\cdot)\to\psi\text{ in $L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$ as $n\to+\infty$,}\\
&\!\!\!\!\!\text{for some sequences $(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ diverging to $+\infty$, and $(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}\big\}.\end{array}

(1.4)

Roughly speaking, the
\Omega
-limit set contains all possible asymptotic profiles of the function as
t\to+\infty
. Notice that, for any bounded solution
u
of (
1.1
), the set
\Omega(u)
is not empty and is included in
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, from standard parabolic estimates. We say that
u
is
asymptotically locally planar
if every
\psi\in\Omega(u)
is
one-dimensional
, that is, if
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, for some
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|x|=1\}
,
|\cdot|
denotes the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and “
\cdot
” denotes the Euclidean scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Furthermore, we say that
\psi
is
one-dimensional and
(
strictly
)
monotone
if
\psi
is as above with
\Psi
(strictly) monotone.

This property reclaims the De Giorgi conjecture about bounded solutions of the Allen-Cahn equation (that is, bounded stationary solutions of the reaction-diffusion equation
\Delta u+u(1-u)(u-1/2)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, obtained after a change of unknown from the original Allen-Cahn equation), see
[
6
]
.

Let us review the results in the literature about the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry. Consider, as before, solutions emerging from indicator functions of a set
U
. First, the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry is known to hold when

is bounded, as a consequence of
[
17
]
. The case of unbounded sets

has been much less studied in the literature. However, the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry is known to hold when

is the subgraph of a bounded function, by
[
3
,
4
,
14
,
20
,
28
]
. Conversely, the property fails when
U
is “V-shaped”, i.e. when
U
is the union of two half-spaces with non-parallel boundaries, as follows from the methods developed in
[
13
]
, see Proposition
4.4
below for further details. The properties listed in this paragraph are known to hold for other types of function

as well, see
[
3
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
26
]
and Section

.

A possible interpretation of these results is that the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry holds provided

is “not too far” from a convex set. This is indeed what we will show.

2
Statement of the main results

In order to state our main results, we define the notion of positive-distance-interior of a set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
as

U_{\delta}:=\big\{x\in U:\dist(x,\partial U)\geq\delta\big\},\quad\delta>0,

where
\dist(x,A):=\inf\big\{|x-y|:y\in A\big\}
for a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, with the convention
\dist(x,A)=+\infty
if
A=\emptyset
. Throughout the paper, we denote

B_{r}(x):=\big\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:|x-y|<r\big\}\ \hbox{ and }\ B_{r}:=B_{r}(0),

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
r>0
. We will also make use of the Hausdorff distance between subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which is defined, for
A,B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, by

d_{\mathcal{H}}(A,B):=\max\Big(\sup_{x\in A}\dist(x,B),\sup_{y\in B}\dist(y,A)\Big),

with the conventions that

d_{\mathcal{H}}(A,\emptyset)=d_{\mathcal{H}}(\emptyset,A)=+\infty\hbox{ \ if $A\neq\emptyset\ $ \ and \ $\ d_{\mathcal{H}}(\emptyset,\emptyset)=0$}.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) with an initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
such that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies

\exists\,\delta>0,\quad d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})<+\infty.

(2.1)

Assume moreover that
U
is convex or nearly convex, that is, that there exists a convex set
U^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U^{\prime})<+\infty
. Then any function in
\Omega(u)
is one-dimensional and, in addition, it is either constant or strictly monotone.

Theorem
2.1
extends the known results about the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry for the Fisher-KPP equation that, we recall, have been established under the assumption that
U
is bounded
[
17
]
, or it is the subgraph of a bounded function
[
3
,
4
,
14
,
20
,
28
]
.

Condition (
2.1
) means that there exists some
R>0
such that, for any
x\in U
, there is a ball
B_{\delta}(x_{0})
contained in
U
of radius
\delta
and centered at a point
x_{0}
such that
|x-x_{0}|<R
. It is fulfilled in particular if
U
satisfies a uniform interior ball condition. It is not hard to see that, in dimension
N=2
, for a convex set
U
, property (
2.1
) is equivalent to require that
U
has nonempty interior. The role of assumption (
2.1
) is cutting off regions of
U
which play a negligible role in the large-time behavior of the solution of the Cauchy problem. This assumption is necessary, otherwise one could take a set
U^{\prime}
for which the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry fails (for instance V-shaped), then consider the set
U:=U^{\prime}\,\cup\,\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}B_{e^{-|k|^{2}}}(k)
: the set
U
is nearly convex, being at finite Hausdorff distance from the convex set
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, but it does not satisfy (
2.1
) and the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry fails for
U
, see Proposition
4.6
below for further details.

Let us describe the idea of the proof of Theorem
2.1
, in relation with the previous proofs in the literature. First, when
U
is the subgraph of a bounded function, the one-dimensional symmetry is derived combining
[
4
]
or
[
14
,
20
,
28
]
with the Liouville-type result of
[
3
]
, which asserts that an entire solution trapped between two translations of a planar traveling front is necessary a planar traveling front – hence one-dimensional at every time. The Liouville result is proved using the sliding method. The proof of
[
17
]
concerning the case where
U
is bounded is much quicker. It relies on the same reflection argument as in the moving plane method. This very elegant proof works for arbitrary nonlinear terms
f
, but unfortunately fails as soon as
U
is unbounded. To circumvent this difficulty, we develop an argument that allows us to extend the technique of
[
17
]
even when
U
is unbounded. This is the main technical contribution of the present paper. The idea is to approximate the solution through a suitable truncation of the initial support
U
. The choice of the truncation cannot be made once and for all, but it rather depends on the time at which we want to approximate the solution. In order to control the approximation error, we exploit a new family of retracting supersolutions obtained as a superposition of a large number of traveling fronts, see Lemma
3.2
below.

As a matter of fact, the convex-proximity assumption on
U
in Theorem
2.1
is a very special case of a geometric hypothesis under which we prove the one-dimensional symmetry, that we now introduce. For a given nonempty set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
and a given point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we let
\pi_{x}
denote the set of orthogonal projections of
x
onto
\overline{U}
, i.e.,

\pi_{x}:=\big\{\xi\in\overline{U}:|x-\xi|=\dist(x,U)\big\},

(2.2)

and, for
x\notin\overline{U}
, we define the
opening
function as follows:

\mathcal{O}(x):=\sup_{\xi\in\pi_{x},\,y\in U\setminus\{\xi\}}\,\frac{x-\xi}{|x-\xi|}\cdot\frac{y-\xi}{|y-\xi|},

(2.3)

with the convention that
\mathcal{O}(x)=-\infty
if
U=\emptyset
or
U
is a singleton (otherwise
-1\leq\mathcal{O}(x)\leq 1
). Namely, when
\mathcal{O}(x)\neq-\infty
, one has
\mathcal{O}(x)=\cos\alpha
, where
\alpha
is the infimum among all
\xi\in\pi_{x}
of half the opening of the largest exterior cone to
U
at
\xi
having axis
x-\xi
, see Figure
1
.

Here is our most general asymptotic symmetry result.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) with an initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
such that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies (
2.1
) and moreover

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\bigg(\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)=R}\mathcal{O}(x)\bigg)\leq 0.

(2.4)

Then any function in
\Omega(u)
is one-dimensional and, in addition, it is either constant or strictly monotone.

It is understood that the left-hand side in condition (
2.4
) is equal to
-\infty
(hence the condition is fulfilled) if
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\dist(x,U)<+\infty
(and indeed in such case the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry trivially holds because condition (
2.1
) yields that
u(t,x)\to 1
uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as
t\to+\infty
, see Proposition
3.1
below). We remark that the limit in (
2.4
) always exists, because the involved quantity is nonincreasing with respect to
R
, see Lemma
4.3
below.

The optimality of hypotheses (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) is discussed in Section
4.4
below. Hypothesis (
2.4
) means that the angle
\alpha
in Figure
1
tends to a value larger than or equal to
\pi/2
(which means that the exterior cone contains a half-space) as
\dist(x,U)\to+\infty
. Theorem
2.2
yields Theorem
2.1
because, firstly, convex sets satisfy
\mathcal{O}(x)\leq 0
for every
x\notin\overline{U}
(actually, they are characterized by such condition in the class of closed sets) and, secondly, if (
2.4
) holds for a given set, then it holds true for any set at finite Hausdorff distance from it, as stated by Lemma
4.3
. However, the class of sets satisfying (
2.4
) is wider. It contains for instance the subgraphs of functions with
vanishing global mean
, i.e.,

U=\big\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ x_{N}\leq\gamma(x^{\prime})\big\},

(2.5)

with
\gamma\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1})
such that

\frac{\gamma(x^{\prime})-\gamma(y^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|}\longrightarrow 0\quad\text{as }\;|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\to+\infty.

(2.6)

As a matter of fact, when
U
is given by (
2.5
)-(
2.6
), we derive a more precise characterization of the

\Omega
-limit set, see Corollary
2.5
below.

Theorems
2.1
and
2.2
are concerned with locally uniform convergence properties along sequences of times
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverging to
+\infty
and sequences of points
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We now assert an asymptotic property which is satisfied
uniformly
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It is expressed in terms of the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrices
D^{2}u(t,x)
(with respect to the
x
variables). For a symmetric real-valued matrix
A
of size
N\times N
, let
\lambda_{1}(A)\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{N}(A)
denote its eigenvalues, and let

\sigma_{k}(A):=\sum_{1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq N}\lambda_{j_{1}}(A)\times\cdots\times\lambda_{j_{k}}(A),\ \ \ \ 1\leq k\leq N,

be the elementary symmetric polynomials of eigenvalues of
A
(
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u(t,x))
is also called
k
-Hessian).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
u
be as in Theorem
2.2
. Then,

\forall\,2\leq k\leq N,\quad\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u(t,x))\to 0\ \hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$ uniformly in $x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$.}

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is based on the asymptotic local one-dimensional symmetry given in Theorem
2.2
, and on standard parabolic estimates. We point out that, if
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and one-dimensional, then
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\psi(x))=0
for all
2\leq k\leq N
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, since the quantities
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\psi(x))
involve sums of products of at least two eigenvalues of
D^{2}\psi(x)
(but
\sigma_{1}(D^{2}\psi(x))\neq 0
in general). However, the converse property is immediately not true (for instance, the function
\psi:(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}
satisfies
\sigma_{2}(D^{2}\psi(x_{1},x_{2}))=0
for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, but it is not one-dimensional).

Once the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry and monotonicity properties are established, it is natural to ask what are the directions in which the solution actually becomes locally one-dimensional. Namely, we investigate the set

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}:=\big\{\,&e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\,:\,\exists\,\psi\in\Omega(u)\text{ such that $\,\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)$}\\
&\text{for some strictly decreasing function $\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})$}\big\}.\end{split}

Under the assumptions of Theorems
2.1
or
2.2
, the set
\mathcal{E}
is then the set of the directions of decreasing monotonicity of all non-constant elements of
\Omega(u)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let

\begin{split}\mathcal{E}=\Big\{\,&e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\ :\ \displaystyle\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|}\to e\ \hbox{as $n\to+\infty$, for some sequences $(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$, $(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$}\\

In particular,
\mathcal{E}=\emptyset
if and only if
U
is relatively dense in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
or

.

We remark that, without the assumption (
2.1
), the last statement of Theorem

U=\{0\}
then

. When

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
u
be the solution of (

) with an initial datum

, where

can also be deduced from
[

,

It is possible to relax the uniform mean condition (

3
A uniform spreading speed result

It is well known since
[
18
]
that, for equation (
1.1
), propagation occurs with an
asymptotic speed of spreading
equal to
c^{*}:=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
, and that the latter coincides with the minimal speed
c
of
traveling fronts
, i.e., solutions of the type

u(t,x)=\varphi(x\cdot e-ct),\ \ 0=\varphi(+\infty)<\varphi<\varphi(-\infty)=1,\ \ c\in\mathbb{R},\ \ e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1},

(3.1)

where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,1)
is of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and decreasing. The precise result is derived in
[
2
]
and asserts that, for any solution
u
to (
1.1
) with an initial condition
0\leq u_{0}\leq 1
which is compactly supported and fulfills
\inf_{B}u_{0}>0
for some ball
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
with positive measure, it holds that

\forall\,c\in(0,c^{*}),\quad\inf_{|x|\leq ct}u(t,x)\to 1\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty,

(3.2)

\forall\,c>c^{*},\quad\sup_{|x|\geq ct}u(t,x)\to 0\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty,

(3.3)

with
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
.

In the sequel, we will need the following uniform version of the above properties for initial data of the form
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
with
U
unbounded.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) emerging from an initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
with
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Then, for any
\delta>0
such that
U_{\delta}\neq\emptyset
, the following convergences hold:

\displaystyle\forall\,c\in(0,c^{*}),\quad

\displaystyle\ \displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U_{\delta})\leq ct}u(t,x)\to 1\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\forall\,c>c^{*},\quad

\displaystyle\ \displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)\geq ct}u(t,x)\to 0\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty.

(3.5)

The reason why (
3.4
) involves
U_{\delta}
instead of
U
is to neglect subsets of
U
(such as isolated points) which do not affect the solution
u
at positive times. The role of hypothesis (
2.1
) in our main results is precisely that it allows us to replace
U_{\delta}
with
U
in (
3.4
).

The uniform “invasion property” (
3.4
) will be immediately deduced from (
3.2
). Instead, property (
3.5
) does not follow from (
3.3
). In order to prove it we construct a family of supersolutions whose upper level sets are given by the exterior of balls retracting with a speed larger, but arbitrarily close, to
c^{*}
. These supersolutions will also directly be used to prove Theorem
2.2
. Here is their construction.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
c>c^{*}
and
\lambda>0
, there exist
R>0

(
depending on
N,f,c
and
\lambda
)
and a family of positive functions
(v^{T})_{T>0}
of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that, for each
T>0
,
v^{T}
is a supersolution to (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
and satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v^{T}(0,x)\geq 1,&\text{for all $x$ such that }|x|\geq R+cT,\\
v^{T}(t,0)<\lambda,&\text{for all }t\in[0,T].\end{array}\right.

(3.6)

Proof.

The functions
v^{T}
will be constructed as the sums of finitely many positive solutions to (
1.1
), hence they will be supersolutions to (
1.1
) due to the following standard consequence of the Fisher-KPP condition (
1.2
):

\forall\,a,b\geq 0,\quad f(a+b)\leq f(a)+f(b).

(3.7)

To show the above inequality, assume to fix the ideas that
a\leq b
, with
b>0
(otherwise the inequality trivially holds because
f(0)=0
). Then, observing that the function
f
, which we recall is extended by
0
outside
[0,1]
, fulfills the third condition in (
1.2
) on the whole half-line
(0,+\infty)
, we get

f(a+b)\leq\frac{f(b)}{b}(a+b)\leq f(a)+f(b).

Now, let
c>c^{*}
and
\lambda>0
. Take
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
small enough to have that
(1-\varepsilon)c>c^{*}
. Consider a finite subset
\mathcal{S}
of the unit ball
\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
such that

\forall\,e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1},\quad\dist(e,\mathcal{S})\leq\varepsilon.

The set
\mathcal{S}
only depends on
N
and
\varepsilon
, which in turn depends on
f,c
. We define the family of positive functions
(v^{T})_{T>0}
, of class
C^{2}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N})
, by

v^{T}(t,x):=2\sum_{e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\varphi\big(x\cdot e^{\prime}-c^{*}(t-T)+R/2\big),

where
\varphi
is the traveling front with speed
c^{*}
, as in (
3.1
), normalized by
\varphi(0)=1/2
, and
R>0
is such that
\varphi(R/2)<\lambda/(2n)
, with
n
being the number of elements of
\mathcal{S}
. With this choice of
R
(which only depends on
N,f,c,\lambda
) we have, as desired,
v^{T}(t,0)<\lambda
for all
T>0
,
t\in[0,T]
. Observe that each term of the sum in the definition of
v^{T}
is a positive solution to (
1.1
), hence
v^{T}
is a positive supersolution to (
1.1
) as discussed at the beginning of the proof.

It remains to check that each
v^{T}
fulfills the first property in (
3.6
). Let
T>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfy
|x|\geq R+cT
, and consider
e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}
such that

\left|e^{\prime}+\frac{x}{|x|}\right|\leq\varepsilon.

We have that

v^{T}(0,x)\geq 2\varphi(x\cdot e^{\prime}+c^{*}T+R/2).

Since
x\cdot e^{\prime}\leq-|x|+\varepsilon|x|
, we deduce

\begin{split}x\cdot e^{\prime}+c^{*}T+R/2&\leq(\varepsilon-1)(R+cT)+c^{*}T+R/2\\
&\leq(\varepsilon-1/2)R+[(\varepsilon-1)c+c^{*}]T,\end{split}

which is negative because
\varepsilon<1/2
and
(1-\varepsilon)c>c^{*}
. It follows that
v^{T}(0,x)\geq 2\varphi(0)=1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

We can now prove the uniform spreading speed result.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

We start with (
3.4
). Let
\delta>0
be such that
U_{\delta}\neq\emptyset
and let
v
be the solution to (
1.1
) emerging from the initial datum
v_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\delta}}
. Take
c\in(0,c^{*})
. For any
x_{0}\in U_{\delta}
, we have that
u_{0}(x_{0}+\cdot)\geq v_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and therefore
u(t,x_{0}+x)\geq v(t,x)
for all
t\geq 0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
thanks to the parabolic comparison principle. Applying (
3.2
) to
v
we deduce that

1\geq\inf_{x_{0}\in U_{\delta},\,|x|\leq ct}u(t,x_{0}+x)\geq\inf_{|x|\leq ct}v(t,x)\to 1\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty.

This is property (
3.4
).

Let us turn to (
3.5
). Take
c>c^{*}
,
c^{\prime}\in(c^{*},c)
,
\lambda>0
, and consider the family of supersolutions
(v^{T})_{T>0}
given by Lemma
3.2
, associated with the speed
c^{\prime}
. Namely, there exists
R>0
such that they fulfill (
3.6
) with
c^{\prime}
instead of
c
. For
T\geq R/(c-c^{\prime})
it holds that
cT\geq R+c^{\prime}T
and thus, if
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is such that
\dist(x_{0},U)\geq cT
, then
\dist(x_{0},U)\geq R+c^{\prime}T
and (
3.6
) implies that
0\leq u_{0}(x_{0}+\cdot)\leq v^{T}(0,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, hence by comparison
0\leq u(T,x_{0})\leq v^{T}(T,0)<\lambda
. This shows that

\forall\,T\geq\frac{R}{c-c^{\prime}},\quad 0\leq\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x_{0},U)\geq cT}u(T,x_{0})\leq\lambda.

From this, property (
3.5
) follows by the arbitrariness of
c>c^{*}
and
\lambda>0
.
∎

We first prove our most general symmetry result, Theorem

. Next, we derive the proof of Theorem
2.1
in Section
4.3
, by showing that its hypotheses imply the ones of Theorem
2.2
. Lastly, Section
4.4

4.1
An approximation result by truncation of the initial datum

The cornerstone of the proof of Theorem
2.2
, hence of the whole paper, is an approximation result. Before stating it, let us introduce some notation. We recall that
B_{R}(x_{0})
and
B_{R}
stand for the balls in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
of radius
R
and center
x_{0}
and
0
respectively. A generic point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
will sometimes be denoted by
(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}
, and the ball in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
of radius
R
and center
x_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
is denoted by
B_{R}^{\prime}(x_{0}^{\prime})
, or just
B_{R}^{\prime}
if
x_{0}^{\prime}=0
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
u
be a solution to (
1.1
) with an initial condition
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
, where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies, for some
\delta>0
,
L>0
, and
\sigma\in(0,c^{*}/2)
,

U_{\delta}\cap B_{L}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{ \ and \ }\quad U\setminus(B^{\prime}_{L}\times\mathbb{R})\,\subset\,\Big\{(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ x_{N}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2c^{*}}|x^{\prime}|\Big\}.

(4.1)

Let
(u^{R})_{R>0}
be the solutions to (
1.1
) emerging from the initial data
(u_{0}^{R})_{R>0}
defined by

u_{0}^{R}=\mathbbm{1}_{U\cap(B_{R}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R})}.

Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\tau_{\varepsilon}>0
, only depending on
N,f,\delta,L,\sigma,\varepsilon
, such that

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon},\quad\big\|u(\tau,\cdot)-u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau,\cdot)\big\|_{C^{1}(B^{\prime}_{\sigma\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}<\varepsilon.

(4.2)

Proof.

In order to get the
C^{1}
estimate (
4.2
), it is sufficient to show an
L^{\infty}
estimate at a later time, that is,

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon},\quad\big\|u(\tau+1,\cdot)-u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,\cdot)\big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{\tau})}<\varepsilon,

(4.3)

where
\tau_{\varepsilon}>0
depends on
N,f,\delta,L,\sigma
and
\varepsilon
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
is the half-cylinder

\mathcal{C}_{\tau}:={B^{\prime}_{\sigma\tau}}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}={B^{\prime}_{\sigma\tau}}\times(0,+\infty).

Indeed, once (
4.3
) is proved, observing that
u-u^{3\sigma\tau}
is nonnegative (by the comparison principle) and it solves a parabolic equation that can be written in linear form, one infers from the parabolic Harnack inequality and interior estimates, given for instance by
[
19
]
, that (
4.2
) holds with
\varepsilon
replaced by
C\varepsilon
, where
C
only depends on
f
and
N
. Then, to prove the lemma it is sufficient to derive (
4.3
) for an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Consider the family of solutions
(w^{R})_{R>0}
to (
1.1
) emerging from the initial data
(w_{0}^{R})_{R>0}
given by

w_{0}^{R}=\mathbbm{1}_{W^{R}}\quad\text{with}\quad W^{R}:=\Big\{(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ |x^{\prime}|\geq R,\ x_{N}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2c^{*}}|x^{\prime}|\Big\}.

By (
4.1
) it holds that
u_{0}\leq\min(u_{0}^{R}+w_{0}^{R},1)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
for every
R\geq L
. Hence, since the KPP condition (
1.2
) yields (
3.7
), the minimum between
1
and the sum of two solutions ranging in
[0,1]
is a supersolution. We infer by comparison that, for
R\geq L
,

0\leq u^{R}\leq u\leq\min(u^{R}+w^{R},1)\ \hbox{ in }[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Thus, property (
4.3
) holds for some
\tau_{\varepsilon}\geq L/(3\sigma)
if we show that

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon},\quad\sup_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}}\big[\min\big(u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x)+w^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x)\,,\,1\big)-u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x)\big]<\varepsilon.

(4.4)

In order to prove (
4.4
), we consider a value
c\in(2\sigma,c^{*})
, that will be specified later, and divide the half-cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
into the subsets

\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{i}:=\big(B^{\prime}_{\sigma\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\big)\cap B_{c\tau},\qquad\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e}:=\big(B^{\prime}_{\sigma\tau}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\big)\setminus B_{c\tau}.

Let us first deal with the set
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{i}
, with arbitrary
c\in(2\sigma,c^{*})
. We want to show that
u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,\cdot)>1-\varepsilon
there for
\tau
large. By hypothesis, there exists a ball
B_{\delta}(x_{0})\subset U
with
|x_{0}|<L
, hence
B_{\delta}(x_{0})\subset U\cap(B_{L+\delta}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R})
. It follows by comparison that
u^{L+\delta}(t,x)\geq v(t,x-x_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
v
is the solution to (
1.1
) with initial datum
\mathbbm{1}_{B_{1}}
. Then, applying the spreading property (
3.2
) to
v
we infer that, for
\tau_{1}>0
large enough, depending on
N,f,c,\delta,\varepsilon
(recall that
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
), it holds that

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{1},\quad\inf_{x\in B_{\frac{c^{*}+c}{2}\,\tau}(x_{0})}u^{L+\delta}(\tau+1,x)>1-\varepsilon.

Since the family
(u^{R})_{R>0}
is nondecreasing with respect to
R
, by the parabolic comparison principle, up to increasing
\tau_{1}
so that
3\sigma\tau_{1}\geq L+\delta
, we derive

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{1},\quad\inf_{x\in B_{\frac{c^{*}+c}{2}\,\tau}(x_{0})}u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x)>1-\varepsilon

(with
\tau_{1}
also depending on
L
and
\sigma
). Finally, from the inclusions
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{i}\subset B_{c\tau}\subset B_{c\tau+L}(x_{0})
, which hold for all
\tau>0
, and
B_{c\tau+L}(x_{0})\subset B_{\frac{c^{*}+c}{2}\tau}(x_{0})
, which holds if
\frac{c^{*}-c}{2}\tau\geq L
, we find a quantity
\tau_{2}>0
depending on
N,f,c,\delta,L,\sigma,\varepsilon
such that

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{2},\quad\inf_{x\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{i}}u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x)\geq\inf_{x\in B_{\frac{c^{*}+c}{2}\tau}(x_{0})}u^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x)>1-\varepsilon.

This shows that, for any choice of
c\in(2\sigma,c^{*})
, the estimate (
4.4
) holds when
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
is replaced by
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{i}
and
\tau_{\varepsilon}
is equal to the above quantity
\tau_{2}
.

Let us consider now the set
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e}
. We want to show that
w^{3\sigma\tau}<\varepsilon
there by estimating the distance between
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e}
and
W^{3\sigma\tau}
and then applying Lemma
3.2
. In this paragraph,
\tau>0
is arbitrary and
c\in(2\sigma,c^{*})
will be fixed at the end of the paragraph. Take two arbitrary points
x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e}
and
y=(y^{\prime},y_{N})\in W^{3\sigma\tau}
. There holds

|x^{\prime}|<\sigma\tau\leq\frac{1}{3}|y^{\prime}|\quad\text{ and }\quad x_{N}>\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau,\quad y_{N}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2c^{*}}|y^{\prime}|.

We compute

|x-y|^{2}=|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|^{2}+(x_{N}-y_{N})^{2}\geq\frac{4}{9}|y^{\prime}|^{2}+(x_{N}-y_{N})^{2}.

If
\sigma|y^{\prime}|/(2c^{*})\geq\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau
, neglecting
(x_{N}-y_{N})^{2}
in the above inequality we get

|x-y|^{2}\geq\frac{16}{9}(c^{*})^{2}\Big(\frac{c^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-1\Big)\tau^{2}\geq\frac{16}{3}(c^{*})^{2}\tau^{2}

since
c>2\sigma>0
. Instead, in the opposite case
\sigma|y^{\prime}|/(2c^{*})<\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau
, one has
y_{N}\leq\sigma|y^{\prime}|/(2c^{*})<\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau<x_{N}
, whence

\displaystyle|x-y|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{4}{9}|y^{\prime}|^{2}+\Big(\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau-\frac{\sigma}{2c^{*}}|y^{\prime}|\Big)^{2}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{9}|y^{\prime}|^{2}+(c^{2}-\sigma^{2})\tau^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4(c^{*})^{2}}|y^{\prime}|^{2}-\frac{\sigma}{c^{*}}\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau|y^{\prime}|,

(4.6)

and we estimate the negative terms by observing that

\frac{4}{9}|y^{\prime}|^{2}-\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}-\frac{\sigma}{c^{*}}\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau|y^{\prime}|\geq|y^{\prime}|\Big(\frac{1}{3}|y^{\prime}|-\frac{\sigma}{c^{*}}\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\,\tau\Big)\geq 0

since
|y^{\prime}|/3\geq\sigma\tau\geq\sigma\sqrt{c^{2}-\sigma^{2}}\tau/c^{*}
. Thus, in such case one has

|x-y|^{2}\geq c^{2}\tau^{2}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4(c^{*})^{2}}|y^{\prime}|^{2}\geq c^{2}\tau^{2}+\frac{9\sigma^{4}\tau^{2}}{4(c^{*})^{2}},

which is larger than
(c^{*})^{2}\tau^{2}
for
c\in(2\sigma,c^{*})
close enough to
c^{*}
, depending on
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
and
\sigma
only. Summing up, we have shown the existence of some
c\in(2\sigma,c^{*})
and
c^{\prime}>c^{*}
, depending on
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
and
\sigma
, such that

\forall\,\tau>0,\ \forall\,x\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e},\quad\dist(x,W^{3\sigma\tau})\geq c^{\prime}\tau.

(4.7)

At this point, we invoke the supersolutions
(v^{T})_{T>0}
provided by Lemma
3.2
, associated with
\widetilde{c}:=(c^{*}+c^{\prime})/2
and
\lambda=\varepsilon
; they satisfy (
3.6
) with
\widetilde{c}
instead of
c
and a quantity
R
depending on
N,f,\widetilde{c},\varepsilon
(hence,
R
depends on
N,f,\sigma,\varepsilon
, since
\widetilde{c}
depends on
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
and
c^{\prime}
and the latter depends on
c^{*},\sigma
). Take
\tau_{3}>0
large enough (depending on
R,\widetilde{c},c^{\prime}
, hence on
N,f,\sigma,\varepsilon
) so that
R+\widetilde{c}(T+1)\leq c^{\prime}T
for all
T\geq\tau_{3}
. Thus, on one hand,
v^{T+1}(0,x)\geq 1
for
|x|\geq c^{\prime}T
and
T\geq\tau_{3}
. On the other hand, for all
\tau>0
and
x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e}
, we know from (
4.7
) that
B_{c^{\prime}\tau}(x_{0})\cap W^{3\sigma\tau}=\emptyset
, which implies that
w_{0}^{3\sigma\tau}(x+x_{0})=0
for
|x|<c^{\prime}\tau
. This means that, for
\tau\geq\tau_{3}
,
w^{3\sigma\tau}(0,\cdot+x_{0})\leq v^{\tau+1}(0,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and thus
w^{3\sigma\tau}(t,\cdot+x_{0})\leq v^{\tau+1}(t,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
for all
t\geq 0
by comparison. We conclude by (
3.6
) that

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{3},\ \forall\,x_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e},\quad w^{3\sigma\tau}(\tau+1,x_{0})\leq v^{\tau+1}(\tau+1,0)<\varepsilon.

This yields that (
4.4
) holds in the set
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}^{e}
too, for a suitable choice of
c
depending on
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
and
\sigma
, and for all
\tau\geq\tau_{3}>0
with
\tau_{3}
depending on
N,f,\sigma,\varepsilon
. Therefore (
4.4
) holds true in the whole
\mathcal{C}_{\tau}
, for some
\tau_{\varepsilon}\geq\max(\tau_{2},\tau_{3})>0
depending on
N,f,\delta,L,\sigma,\varepsilon
. The proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

4.2
Proof of Theorem
2.2

Lemma
4.1
allows us to derive Theorem
2.2
by applying the reflection argument “à la Jones”
[
17
]
to the solution with the truncated initial datum. This actually yields an additional information about the direction in which
u
becomes locally one-dimensional, that will be used to prove one inclusion of the characterization of the set
\mathcal{E}
in Theorem
2.4
. Here is the description of the
\Omega
-limit set that shows in particular Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) with an initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
such that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies (
2.1
) and (
2.4
). Let
\psi\in\Omega(u)
and let
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding sequences given by definition (
1.4
). The following properties hold:

(i)

if
\ \displaystyle\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\dist(x_{n},U)}{t_{n}}<c^{*}
then
\,\psi\equiv 1
;

(ii)

if
\ \displaystyle\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\dist(x_{n},U)}{t_{n}}>c^{*}
then
\,\psi\equiv 0
;

(iii)

if
\ \displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{\dist(x_{n},U)}{t_{n}}=c^{*}
and if
(\xi)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is any sequence such that
\xi_{n}\in\pi_{x_{n}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then, up to extraction of a subsequence,

\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|}\to e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}\ \hbox{ as }n\to+\infty,

and
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
for some function
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which is either constant or strictly decreasing.

Hence, in any case,
\psi
is one-dimensional, and it is either constant or strictly monotone.

Proof.

Statement
(ii)
is a direct consequence of property (
3.5
), because in such a case there is
c>c^{*}
such that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\dist(x_{n}+x,U)>ct_{n}
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the case
(i)
, there is
c\in(0,c^{*})
such that
\dist(x_{n},U)<ct_{n}
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, since
\dist(\cdot,U_{\delta})\leq\dist(\cdot,U)+d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})
, for given
c^{\prime}\in(c,c^{*})
it follows from hypothesis (
2.1
) that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
\dist(x_{n}+x,U_{\delta})<c^{\prime}t_{n}
for infinitely many
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The invasion property (
3.4
) eventually yields that
\psi\equiv 1
.

We are left with statement
(iii)
, that is, calling for short
k_{n}:=\dist(x_{n},U)
, we now have

\frac{k_{n}}{t_{n}}\to c^{*}\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty,

(4.8)

In order to show that
\psi
is one-dimensional, we proceed in several steps: we first define some new convenient coordinate systems; next, assuming by contradiction that
\psi
is not one-dimensional, we show that a line orthogonal to a level set of
u
at time
t_{n}
is far from a suitable half-cylinder with radius of order
t_{n}
, which in the following step is used to define an approximation of
u
through a truncation of its initial support
U
. Then Lemma
4.1
will ensure that the error in this approximation is small (this is where the geometric assumption (
2.4
) is used), and this in turn will allow us to obtain a contradiction by applying Jones’ reflection argument to the solution with the truncated initial support. In the final step of the proof, we will derive the monotonicity of
\psi
in the desired direction by using again the reflection argument together with Lemma
4.1
.

Step 1: coordinates transformations.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\xi_{n}
belong to the set
\pi_{x_{n}}
of the projections of
x_{n}
onto
\overline{U}
(i.e.,
\xi_{n}\in\overline{U}
and
|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|=k_{n}
). Up to extraction of a subsequence, we have by (
4.8
) that
k_{n}>0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We set

e_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{k_{n}}=\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|}.

Next, we consider a family of
N\times N
orthogonal transformations
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
M_{n}(\mathrm{e}_{N})=e_{n}
, with
\mathrm{e}_{N}:=(0,\cdots,0,1)
. Up to subsequences,
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(M_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge respectively to some direction
e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
and some orthogonal transformation
M
, with
M(\mathrm{e}_{N})=e
. We define, for
t\geq 0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

u_{n}(t,x):=u(t,\xi_{n}+M_{n}(x)).

These are still solutions to (
1.1
), because the equation is invariant under isometry. It follows that

u_{n}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+x)=u(t_{n},x_{n}+M_{n}(x))\longrightarrow\psi(M(x))=:\widetilde{\psi}(x)\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty,

(4.9)

locally uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Moreover,
u_{n}(0,\cdot)=\mathbbm{1}_{U_{n}}
with

U_{n}:=M_{n}^{-1}(U)-\{M_{n}^{-1}(\xi_{n})\}

The set
U_{n}
is a rigid transformation of
U
and it is constructed in a way that
0\in\overline{U_{n}}
is an orthogonal projection of
k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}
onto
\overline{U_{n}}
, whence
\dist(k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N},\overline{U_{n}})=k_{n}\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
by (
4.8
) and therefore the geometric assumption (
2.4
) (which, being invariant by isometries, is fulfilled by
U_{n}
too) yields

U_{n}\subset\big\{(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ x_{N}\leq\alpha_{n}|x^{\prime}|\big\}\quad\text{with $\alpha_{n}\to 0$ as $n\to+\infty$}.

(4.10)

Step 2: the choice of the truncation.
Firstly, by interior parabolic estimates, the
L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
convergence (
4.9
) holds true in
C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. We claim that

\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}\equiv 0\ \hbox{ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$},

(4.11)

which will yield, in the original coordinate system,

\partial_{e^{\prime}}\psi\equiv\nabla\widetilde{\psi}\cdot(M^{-1}(e^{\prime}))\equiv 0\ \hbox{ in }\mathbb{R}^{N}

for any direction
e^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
such that
M^{-1}(e^{\prime})\perp\mathrm{e}_{N}
, that is,
e^{\prime}\perp M(\mathrm{e}_{N})=e
. This will imply that
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
for some
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Assume by contradiction that the above claim (
4.11
) fails, that is, that
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}\widetilde{\psi}(\bar{x})\neq 0
for some
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let us call for short
\beta:=\nabla\widetilde{\psi}(\bar{x})
, hence
\beta=(\beta^{\prime},\beta_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}
with
\beta^{\prime}\neq 0
, and it holds that

\nabla u_{n}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x})\to\beta\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty.

(4.12)

Take a real number
\vartheta>0
, that will be fixed at the end of this paragraph. Let
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the family of closed half-cylinders in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
defined by

H_{n}:=\overline{B^{\prime}_{\vartheta t_{n}}}\times(-\infty,\vartheta t_{n}].

Consider also the conical sets
(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
given by

V_{n}:=\big\{k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x}+s(\beta+\zeta):s\in\mathbb{R},\,\zeta\in B_{\vartheta}\big\}.

(4.13)

We look for
\vartheta>0
small enough so that

H_{n}\cap V_{n}=\emptyset\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

(4.14)

To this end, consider a generic point
P=(P^{\prime},P_{N})\in V_{n}
. It can be written as
P=k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x}+s(\beta+\zeta)
for some
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\zeta\in B_{\vartheta}
. Suppose that
P_{N}\leq\vartheta t_{n}
, thus

\vartheta t_{n}\geq k_{n}-|\bar{x}|-(|\beta_{N}|+\vartheta)|s|.

By (
4.8
), for
n
large enough we have
k_{n}>(c^{*}/2)t_{n}
and therefore

|s|\geq\frac{(c^{*}/2-\vartheta)t_{n}-|\bar{x}|}{|\beta_{N}|+\vartheta}.

(4.15)

The component
P^{\prime}
is estimated as follows:

|P^{\prime}|\geq(|\beta^{\prime}|-\vartheta)|s|-|\bar{x}|.

We impose
\vartheta<|\beta^{\prime}|
and, for
n
sufficiently large, we can invoke (
4.15
) and get

|P^{\prime}|-\vartheta t_{n}\geq\left(\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|-\vartheta}{|\beta_{N}|+\vartheta}(c^{*}/2-\vartheta)-\vartheta\right)t_{n}-\left(\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|}{|\beta_{N}|}+1\right)|\bar{x}|.

Thus, one can find
\vartheta\in(0,\min(|\beta^{\prime}|,c^{*}/2))
sufficiently small, only depending on
\beta^{\prime}
,
\beta_{N}
and
c^{*}
, such that, for
n
large,
|P^{\prime}|>\vartheta t_{n}
, i.e.
P\notin H_{n}
. This means that, with this choice of
\vartheta
, condition (
4.14
) holds. This fixes our choice of
\vartheta
and thus of the family of half-cylinders
(H_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Step 3: the approximation procedure.
We now apply Lemma
4.1
to the sequence of solutions
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Take
\delta>0
from hypothesis (
2.1
), and call

\sigma:=\frac{\vartheta}{3}>0,\qquad L:=d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})+1>0,\qquad\varepsilon:=\frac{\vartheta}{2}>0,

(4.16)

where
\vartheta>0
is given in the previous step. One has
0<\sigma<\vartheta<c^{*}/2
and
0<L<+\infty
by (
2.1
). We further have, on the one hand, that

(U_{n})_{\delta}\cap B_{L}\neq\emptyset,

(4.17)

because
0\in\overline{U}_{n}
and
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U_{n},(U_{n})_{\delta})=d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})<L
. On the other hand, it follows from (
4.10
) that, for
n
large,

U_{n}\subset\,\Big\{(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ x_{N}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2c^{*}}|x^{\prime}|\Big\}.

This means that the sets
U_{n}
fulfill the hypotheses (
4.1
) of Lemma
4.1
for
n
large enough. Therefore, for such values of
n
, considering the solution
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
of (
1.1
) whose initial datum is given by the indicator function of the set

U_{n}\cap(B_{\vartheta t_{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R}),

the estimate (
4.2
) implies

\big\|u_{n}(t_{n},\cdot)-u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},\cdot)\big\|_{C^{1}(B^{\prime}_{\vartheta t_{n}/3}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}<\frac{\vartheta}{2},

(4.18)

provided that
t_{n}
is larger than a quantity depending on
\vartheta
but not on
n
. This means that the above estimate holds for all
n
sufficiently large. Furthermore, (
4.17
) implies that, for all
n
large enough,

U_{n}\cap(B^{\prime}_{\vartheta t_{n}}\times\mathbb{R})\ \supset\ B_{\delta}(y_{n}),

(4.19)

for some
y_{n}\in B_{L}
. Since
u_{n}
fulfills (
4.12
), one then infers from (
4.18
) that

\big|\nabla u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x})-\beta\big|<\vartheta\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

(4.20)

This entails that, for such values of
n
, the line
\Gamma_{n}
passing through the point
k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x}
and directed as
\nabla u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x})
is contained in the set
V_{n}
defined in (
4.13
), and therefore, by (
4.14
),

\big(\Gamma_{n}\cap H_{n}\big)\subset\big(V_{n}\cap H_{n}\big)=\emptyset\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

Next, owing to (
4.10
), we also have that
\overline{U_{n}}\cap(\overline{B_{\vartheta t_{n}}^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R})\subset H_{n}
for all
n
sufficiently large, hence

\supp u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(0,\cdot)\subset H_{n}\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large},

(4.21)

where
\supp u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(0,\cdot)
denotes the support of
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
(one has
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(0,\cdot)\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus H_{n}
).

Step 4: the reflection argument.
Let
H_{n},V_{n},\Gamma_{n}
and
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
be as in the previous steps. For
n
large enough, the half-cylinder
H_{n}
and the line
\Gamma_{n}
are convex, closed and disjoint; we can then separate them with an hyperplane, which, up to translation, can be assumed without loss of generality to contain
\Gamma_{n}
. Namely, for
n
large, there exists an open half-space
\Omega_{n}
such that

\Gamma_{n}\subset\partial\Omega_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad H_{n}\subset\Omega_{n}.

(4.22)

Let
\mathcal{R}_{n}
denote the affine orthogonal reflection with respect to
\partial\Omega_{n}
. Then define the function
v^{n}
in
[0,+\infty)\times\overline{\Omega_{n}}
by

v^{n}(t,x):=u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t,\mathcal{R}_{n}(x)).

The function
v^{n}
coincides with
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
on
[0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{n}
. Furthermore
v_{n}(0,\cdot)
vanishes identically in
\Omega_{n}
, while
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}=1
in
B_{\delta}(y_{n})\subset U_{n}\cap(B^{\prime}_{\vartheta t_{n}}\times\mathbb{R})\subset H_{n}\subset\Omega_{n}
, provided
n
is large enough for (
4.19
), (
4.21
) and (
4.22
) to hold. Then, for such values of
n
, it follows from the comparison principle that
v^{n}\leq u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
in
(0,+\infty)\times\Omega_{n}
, and moreover, by the Hopf lemma, that
\partial_{\nu_{n}}v^{n}>\partial_{\nu_{n}}u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
on
(0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{n}
, where
{\nu_{n}}
is the exterior normal to
\Omega_{n}
. Since clearly
\partial_{\nu_{n}}v^{n}=-\partial_{\nu_{n}}u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
on
(0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{n}
, this means that
\partial_{\nu_{n}}u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}<0
on
(0,+\infty)\times\partial\Omega_{n}
, and thus in particular that
\partial_{\nu_{n}}u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x})<0
, because
k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x}\in\Gamma_{n}\subset\partial\Omega_{n}
. This is however impossible because
\nabla u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\bar{x})
is parallel to
\Gamma_{n}
and thus orthogonal to
\nu_{n}
. We have reached a contradiction. This therefore shows (
4.11
), and then
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
for some function
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.

Step 5: the large monotonicity property.
Let us now show that
\Psi
is nonincreasing, that is,
\partial_{x_{N}}\widetilde{\psi}\leq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We fix an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and an arbitrary
\widehat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. As in Step 3, we apply Lemma
4.1
to the sequence of solutions
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Namely, we take
\delta>0
from hypothesis (
2.1
) and call

\sigma:=\frac{c^{*}}{6},\qquad L:=d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})+1.

(4.23)

As seen before, with these values, we have that the sets
U_{n}
fulfill (
4.1
) for
n
large enough. Therefore, calling
u_{n}^{R}
the solution with initial datum given by the indicator function of the set

U_{n}\cap(B_{R}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R}),

for such large values of
n
, one can apply Lemma
4.1
and infer that

\forall\,\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon},\quad\big\|u_{n}(\tau,\cdot)-u_{n}^{c^{*}\tau/2}(\tau,\cdot)\big\|_{C^{1}(B^{\prime}_{c^{*}\tau/6}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}<\varepsilon,

where
\tau_{\varepsilon}
only depends on
N,f,\delta,L,\varepsilon
. As a consequence, for
n
large enough such that the above holds, and in addition
t_{n}>\tau_{\varepsilon}
, we deduce that

\big\|u_{n}(t_{n},\cdot)-u_{n}^{c^{*}t_{n}/2}(t_{n},\cdot)\big\|_{C^{1}(B^{\prime}_{c^{*}t_{n}/6}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}<\varepsilon\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

(4.24)

Next, consider the half-space

\Omega_{n}:=\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times(-\infty,k_{n}+\widehat{x}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{N}],

so that
k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\widehat{x}\in\partial\Omega_{n}
. By (
4.8
) and (
4.10
) it holds that
U_{n}\cap(B_{c^{*}t_{n}/2}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R})\subset\Omega_{n}
for
n
sufficiently large (also depending on
\widehat{x}
), that is, the support of the initial datum of
u_{n}^{c^{*}t_{n}/2}
is contained in
\Omega_{n}
. The same reflection argument as in Step 4 eventually yields

\partial_{x_{N}}u_{n}^{c^{*}t_{n}/2}(t_{n},k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}+\widehat{x})\leq 0\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

Combining this with (
4.24
) we infer that
\partial_{x_{N}}\widetilde{\psi}(\widehat{x})\leq\varepsilon
, and thus
\partial_{x_{N}}\widetilde{\psi}\leq 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon>0
and
\widehat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Step 6: the strict monotonicity property.
We have shown in the above steps that
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
with
\Psi
nonincreasing. It remains to show that
\Psi
is either constant or strictly decreasing in the whole
\mathbb{R}
. To do so, consider the functions
(t,x)\mapsto u(t_{n}+t,x_{n}+M_{n}(x))
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which satisfy the same equation (
1.1
) as
u
, but for
t>-t_{n}
. From standard parabolic estimates, there is a solution
u_{\infty}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,1]
of (
1.1
), which is a solution also for
t\leq 0
, and such that, up to extraction of a subsequence,

u(t_{n}+t,x_{n}+M_{n}(x))\to u_{\infty}(t,x)\ \hbox{ as $n\to+\infty$ locally uniformly in $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.

We apply the results derived in the Steps 1-5 to the sequences
(t_{n}-1)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Observe that also these sequences fulfill the condition of statement
(iii)
. Moreover, the direction
e
associated with these new sequences, as defined at the beginning of Step 1, only depends on
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, hence it is the same before. We deduce the existence of a nonincreasing function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
such that
u_{\infty}(-1,x)\equiv\Phi(x\cdot e)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Notice also that
u_{\infty}(0,x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Now, if the function
\Phi
is constant in
\mathbb{R}
, which means that
u_{\infty}(-1,\cdot)
is constant in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, then so is
u_{\infty}(0,\cdot)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(because
u_{\infty}
solves (
1.1
) for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
), that is,
\Psi
is constant in
\mathbb{R}
. On the other hand, if
\Phi
is not constant in
\mathbb{R}
, then, for each
h>0
,

u_{\infty}(-1,x+he)\leq u_{\infty}(-1,x)\ \hbox{ and }\ u_{\infty}(-1,x+he)\not\equiv u_{\infty}(-1,x)\ \hbox{ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}$},

hence
u_{\infty}(0,x+he)<u_{\infty}(0,x)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
from the strong parabolic maximum principle, yielding
\Psi(s+h)<\Psi(s)
for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. As a conclusion,
\Psi
is either constant or strictly decreasing in
\mathbb{R}
. The proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

4.3
Proof of Theorem
2.1

Let us turn to Theorem
2.1
that, as we now show, is a special case of Theorem
2.2
. For this, we need to check that the geometric condition (
2.4
) is invariant among sets having finite Hausdorff distance from one another. This is done in the following geometric lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, consider the function
\mathcal{O}(x)
defined in (
2.3
). Then the map

R\mapsto\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)=R}\,\mathcal{O}(x)

is nonincreasing in
(0,+\infty)
. Moreover, for any
U^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U^{\prime})<+\infty
, then
U
fulfills (
2.4
) if and only if
U^{\prime}
does
(
with the corresponding
\mathcal{O}
defined as in (
2.3
) with
U^{\prime}
instead of
U)
.

Proof.

The monotonicity property involving
\mathcal{O}
is readily derived. Consider indeed any

0<R^{\prime}<R.

If the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\dist(x,U)=R\}
is empty, then
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)=R}\mathcal{O}(x)=-\infty
and the inequality
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)=R}\mathcal{O}(x)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)=R^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}(x)
is trivially true. Assume now that the set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:\dist(x,U)=R\}
is not empty, and consider any
x
in this set and any
\xi\in\pi_{x}
, that is,
\xi\in\overline{U}
and
|x-\xi|=\dist(x,U)=R
. If
U=\{\xi\}
then
\mathcal{O}(x)=-\infty
by our convention, hence this case is trivial too.

Assume then that
U\neq\{\xi\}
. Consider the point
x^{\prime}:=\xi+(R^{\prime}/R)(x-\xi)
. Its unique projection onto
\overline{U}
is
\xi
, that is,
\pi_{x^{\prime}}=\{\xi\}
. Furthermore,
\dist(x^{\prime},U)=|x^{\prime}-\xi|=R^{\prime}
. One also observes that, for any
y\in U\!\setminus\!\{\xi\}
,

\frac{x-\xi}{|x-\xi|}\cdot\frac{y-\xi}{|y-\xi|}=\frac{x^{\prime}-\xi}{|x^{\prime}-\xi|}\cdot\frac{y-\xi}{|y-\xi|}\leq\mathcal{O}(x^{\prime})\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(z,U)=R^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}(z).

Since
x
with
\dist(x,U)=R
, together with
\xi\in\pi_{x}
and
y\in U\!\setminus\!\{\xi\}
, were arbitrary, this shows that

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(z,U)=R}\mathcal{O}(z)\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(z,U)=R^{\prime}}\mathcal{O}(z).

Let us turn to the second statement of the lemma. One considers any two subsets
U
and
U^{\prime}
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfying
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U^{\prime})<+\infty
. Denote
\pi^{\prime}_{x}
and
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}(x)
the objects defined as in (
2.2
)-(
2.3
) with
U^{\prime}
instead of
U
.

Assume by way of contradiction that
U
fulfills (
2.4
) and
U^{\prime}
does not. Then there are
\varepsilon>0
and a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\!\setminus\!\overline{U^{\prime}}
such that

0<R^{\prime}_{n}:=\dist(x_{n},U^{\prime})\to+\infty\hbox{ as $n\to+\infty,\ $ and $\ \mathcal{O}^{\prime}(x_{n})\geq 2\varepsilon>0$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

(4.25)

Calling
d:=d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U^{\prime})<+\infty
, one then has
R_{n}:=\dist(x_{n},U)\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
, and moreover

R_{n}-d\leq R^{\prime}_{n}\leq R_{n}+d\ \hbox{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.26)

Without loss of generality, one has
R_{n}>0
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Since
U
is assumed to satisfy (
2.4
), there holds
\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\mathcal{O}(x_{n})\leq 0
, that is,

\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}:=\max\big(\mathcal{O}(x_{n}),0\big)\to 0\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty.

(4.27)

Now, from (
4.25
), for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are
\xi^{\prime}_{n}\in\pi^{\prime}_{x_{n}}
, that is,
\xi^{\prime}_{n}\in\overline{U^{\prime}}
and
|x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|=\dist(x_{n},U^{\prime})=R^{\prime}_{n}>0
, and
y^{\prime}_{n}\in U^{\prime}\!\setminus\!\{\xi^{\prime}_{n}\}
such that

\frac{x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\cdot\frac{y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\geq\varepsilon>0.

(4.28)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider any
\xi_{n}\in\pi_{x_{n}}
, that is,
\xi_{n}\in\overline{U}
and
|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|=\dist(x_{n},U)=R_{n}>0
, and then there is a point
y_{n}\in U
such that

|y_{n}-y^{\prime}_{n}|\leq d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U^{\prime})+1=d+1.

(4.29)

We estimate from above the quantities in (
4.28
) by writing:

\frac{x_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|x_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\cdot\frac{y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\!\leq\!\underbrace{\Big|\frac{x_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|x_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\!-\!\frac{x_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}\Big|}_{=:I_{1,n}}\!+\!\underbrace{\Big|\frac{y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\!-\!\frac{y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}}{|y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}\Big|}_{=:I_{2,n}}\!+\!\underbrace{\frac{x_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}\!\cdot\!\frac{y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}}{|y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}}_{=:I_{3,n}}\!.

(4.30)

This inequality is understood to hold whenever
y_{n}\neq\xi_{n}
, which we will show to occur for
n
sufficiently large. We will then prove that
I_{1,n}
,
I_{2,n}
,
I_{3,n}

\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
, which will eventually contradict (
4.28
). In order to estimate
I_{1,n}
, we take
z_{n}\in\overline{U}
such that
|z_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\leq d
and we compute

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(x_{n}-\xi_{n})\cdot(x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n})&=&R_{n}^{2}+(x_{n}-\xi_{n})\cdot(\xi_{n}-z_{n})+(x_{n}-\xi_{n})\cdot(z_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n})\\
&\geq&R_{n}^{2}-\mathcal{O}(x_{n})R_{n}|z_{n}-\xi_{n}|-R_{n}d\\
&\geq&R_{n}\big(R_{n}-2(R_{n}+d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}-d\big),\end{array}

(4.31)

where the last inequality follows from

|z_{n}-\xi_{n}|\leq|z_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|+|\xi^{\prime}_{n}-x_{n}|+|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|\leq d+R^{\prime}_{n}+R_{n}\leq 2(R_{n}+d).

One then derives from (
4.26
) and (
4.31
) that

0\leq I_{1,n}\leq\sqrt{2-\frac{2\big(R_{n}-2(R_{n}+d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}-d\big)}{R^{\prime}_{n}}}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{(R_{n}+d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}+d}{R^{\prime}_{n}}}.

(4.32)

Together with (
4.25
)-(
4.27
), one gets that

I_{1,n}\to 0\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty.

(4.33)

Next, let us check that
y_{n}\neq\xi_{n}
for
n
large. We first control
|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|
from below. We write

|y^{\prime}_{n}-x_{n}|^{2}=|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|^{2}+(R^{\prime}_{n})^{2}-2(y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n})\cdot(x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}),

which together with (
4.28
) and the inequality
|y^{\prime}_{n}-x_{n}|\geq\dist(x_{n},U^{\prime})=R^{\prime}_{n}
yields

|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\big(|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|-2\varepsilon R^{\prime}_{n}\big)\geq|y^{\prime}_{n}-x_{n}|^{2}-(R^{\prime}_{n})^{2}\geq 0.

Since
y^{\prime}_{n}\neq\xi^{\prime}_{n}
, this means that

|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\geq 2\varepsilon R^{\prime}_{n}.

(4.34)

Now, using (
4.31
) and
R^{\prime}_{n}\leq R_{n}+d
, one infers

|\xi_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|^{2}=R_{n}^{2}+(R^{\prime}_{n})^{2}-2(x_{n}-\xi_{n},x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n})\leq 4R_{n}d+d^{2}+4R_{n}(R_{n}+d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}.

(4.35)

Gathering together the inequalities (
4.29
), (
4.34
) and (
4.35
) shows that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|y_{n}-\xi_{n}|&\geq&|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|-|y_{n}-y^{\prime}_{n}|-|\xi^{\prime}_{n}-\xi_{n}|\\
&\geq&2\varepsilon R^{\prime}_{n}-(d+1)-\sqrt{4R_{n}d+d^{2}+4R_{n}(R_{n}+d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}}.\end{array}

(4.36)

The right-hand side is positive for all
n
large enough and is equivalent to
2\varepsilon R^{\prime}_{n}
as
n\to+\infty
, because of (
4.25
)-(
4.27
). This means that
y_{n}\neq\xi_{n}
for
n
large enough. Let us estimate
I_{2,n}
. One has, for
n
large,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}0\leq I_{2,n}&\!\!\!=&\displaystyle\sqrt{2-2\frac{(y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n})\cdot(y_{n}-\xi_{n})}{|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\times|y_{n}-\xi_{n}|}}\\
&\!\!\!=&\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{|(y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n})-(y_{n}-\xi_{n})|^{2}-(|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|-|y_{n}-\xi_{n}|)^{2}}{|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\times|y_{n}-\xi_{n}|}}\\
&\!\!\!\leq&\displaystyle\frac{|(y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n})-(y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n})|}{\sqrt{|y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\!\times\!|y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}}\leq\frac{|y_{n}\!-\!y^{\prime}_{n}|+|\xi_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}{\sqrt{|y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\!\times\!|y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}}\leq\frac{d\!+\!1\!+\!|\xi_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}{\sqrt{|y^{\prime}_{n}\!-\!\xi^{\prime}_{n}|\!\times\!|y_{n}\!-\!\xi_{n}|}},\end{array}

(4.37)

where the last inequality follows from (
4.29
). Putting together (
4.34
)-(
4.37
) leads to

0\leq I_{2,n}\leq\frac{d+1+\sqrt{4R_{n}d+d^{2}+4R_{n}(R_{n}+d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}}}{\sqrt{2\varepsilon R^{\prime}_{n}}\times\sqrt{2\varepsilon R^{\prime}_{n}-(d\!+\!1)-\sqrt{4R_{n}d\!+\!d^{2}\!+\!4R_{n}(R_{n}\!+\!d)\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}}}}

for all
n
large enough. Using again (
4.25
)-(
4.27
), it follows that

I_{2,n}\to 0\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty.

(4.38)

Finally, one has that
0\leq I_{3,n}\leq\mathcal{O}(x_{n})\leq\mathcal{O}(x_{n})^{+}
for all

, hence
I_{3,n}\to 0
as

, by (

). Together with (

), (

) and (

), one gets that

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\frac{x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\cdot\frac{y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}}{|y^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|}\leq 0,

a contradiction with (
4.28
). The conclusion of the lemma then follows by changing the roles of
U
and
U^{\prime}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

If the set

is convex, then the quantity
\mathcal{O}(x)
defined by (

) satisfies
\mathcal{O}(x)\leq 0
for all

, hence condition (

) is immediately true in this case. Condition (

4.4
Counterexamples without the conditions (
2.1
) or (
2.4
)

This section essentially consists of two propositions, which assert that the conclusions of the main results do not hold in general without the assumptions (
2.1
) or (
2.4
).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) with an initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
, where
U
is the union of two half-spaces with non-parallel boundaries. The set
U
satisfies (
2.1
) but not (
2.4
), and
\Omega(u)
contains some elements which are not one-dimensional.

Proof.

We consider the dimension
N=2
only, since the general case
N\geq 3
follows by trivially extending the functions in the variables
x_{3},\cdots,x_{N}
. Since the equation (
1.1
) is invariant by rigid transformations, one can assume without loss of generality that

U=\big\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x_{2}\leq\beta\,|x_{1}|\big\},

for some
\beta>0
. Denote
\alpha:=\arctan\beta\in(0,\pi/2)
. Notice immediately that
U
satisfies (
2.1
), but it does not satisfy (
2.4
). Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) in dimension
N=2
, with initial condition
u_{0}:=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
. Let
v
be the solution of (
1.1
) in dimension
N=1
, with Heaviside initial condition
v_{0}:=\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,0]}
. As follows from
[
4
,
14
,
18
,
20
,
28
]
, there is a function
t\mapsto\zeta(t)
such that

v(t,x)-\varphi(x-\zeta(t))\to 0\ \hbox{ as $t\to+\infty$ uniformly in $x\in\mathbb{R}$},

(4.39)

where
\varphi
is the (decreasing) profile of the traveling front
\varphi(x-c^{*}t)
solving (
3.1
), with
N=1
,
e=1
, and minimal speed
c=c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
(furthermore, it is known that
\zeta(t)=c^{*}t-(3/c^{*})\ln t+x_{\infty}+o(1)
as
t\to+\infty
, for some real number
x_{\infty}
). Since (
1.1
) is invariant by rigid transformations and since
f(a+b)\leq f(a)+f(b)
for all
a,b\geq 0
by (
3.7
),
it follows from the definition of
U
and the maximum principle that

\begin{array}[]{l}\max\big(v(t,x_{2}\cos\alpha-x_{1}\sin\alpha),v(t,x_{2}\cos\alpha+x_{1}\sin\alpha)\big)\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq u(t,x_{1},x_{2})\leq v(t,x_{2}\cos\alpha-x_{1}\sin\alpha)+v(t,x_{2}\cos\alpha+x_{1}\sin\alpha)\end{array}

(4.40)

for all
t\geq 0
and
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Together with (
4.39
), one gets that

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}u\Big(t,0,\frac{\zeta(t)+\varphi^{-1}(1/2)}{\cos\alpha}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad\limsup_{t\to+\infty}u\Big(t,0,\frac{\zeta(t)+\varphi^{-1}(1/8)}{\cos\alpha}\Big)\leq\frac{1}{4},

where
\varphi^{-1}:(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the reciprocal of the decreasing function
\varphi
. Consider now any sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverging to
+\infty
. Up to extraction of a subsequence, the functions
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto u(t_{n},x_{1},\zeta(t_{n})/\cos\alpha+x_{2})
converge in
C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to a function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to[0,1]
, which then belongs to
\Omega(u)
. One has

\psi\Big(0,\frac{\varphi^{-1}(1/2)}{\cos\alpha}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2}>\frac{1}{4}\geq\psi\Big(0,\frac{\varphi^{-1}(1/8)}{\cos\alpha}\Big),

hence there exists
y\in(\varphi^{-1}(1/2)/\cos\alpha,\varphi^{-1}(1/8)/\cos\alpha)
such that
1/4<\psi(0,y)<1/2
and
\partial_{x_{2}}\psi(0,y)<0
. Furthermore, since
U
is symmetric with respect to the axis
\{x_{1}=0\}
, the function
u_{0}
is even in
x_{1}
, and so are
u(t,\cdot)
for every
t>0
, and then
\psi
. Thus,
\partial_{x_{1}}\psi(0,x_{2})=0
for all
x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. From the previous observations, the gradient of
\psi
at the point
(0,y)
is a non-zero vector parallel to the vector
(0,1)
. If the function
\psi
were one-dimensional, it would then necessarily be written as
\psi(x_{1},x_{2})\equiv\Psi(x_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, for some
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function
\Psi
. In particular, one would have that
\psi(x_{1},y)=\psi(0,y)\in(1/4,1/2)
for all
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
. But (
4.39
)-(
4.40
) yield

\begin{array}[]{r}\displaystyle 1\geq\psi(x_{1},y)=\lim_{n\to+\infty}u\Big(t_{n},x_{1},\frac{\zeta(t_{n})}{\cos\alpha}+y\Big)\geq\lim_{n\to+\infty}v(t_{n},\zeta(t_{n})+y\cos\alpha-x_{1}\sin\alpha)\\
=\varphi(y\cos\alpha-x_{1}\sin\alpha),\end{array}

hence
\psi(x_{1},y)\to 1
as
x_{1}\to+\infty
, leading to a contradiction. As a conclusion, the element
\psi
of
\Omega(u)
is not one-dimensional. Notice finally that all shifts
\psi(\cdot+a_{1},\cdot+a_{2})
of
\psi
belong to
\Omega(u)
, and are not one-dimensional either.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

In the example considered in the above proof, the set
\Omega(u)
nevertheless contains some elements which are one-dimensional (apart from the constant elements
0
and
1
, which belong to
\Omega(u)
by Proposition
3.1
). Indeed, it follows from (
4.39
)-(
4.40
) and
\varphi(+\infty)=0
that, for any
\varrho:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\varrho(t)\to+\infty
as
t\to+\infty
, one has

u\Big(t,\varrho(t)+x_{1},\varrho(t)\tan\alpha+\frac{\zeta(t)}{\cos\alpha}+x_{2}\Big)\to\varphi(x_{2}\cos\alpha-x_{1}\sin\alpha)\ \hbox{ as }t\to+\infty,

locally uniformly in
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Therefore, the one-dimensional non-constant function
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\varphi(x_{2}\cos\alpha-x_{1}\sin\alpha)
belongs to
\Omega(u)
. So does the one-dimensional non-constant function
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\varphi(x_{2}\cos\alpha+x_{1}\sin\alpha)
, by choosing
\varrho
such that
\varrho(+\infty)=-\infty
and adapting the above limit.

The second result shows that the conclusions of the main results do not hold in general without the assumption (
2.1
).

Proposition 4.6
.

In any dimension
N\geq 2
, there are measurable sets
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which satisfy (
2.4
) but not (
2.1
), such that
\Omega(u)
contains some elements that are not one-dimensional, where
u
is the solution of (
1.1
) with initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
.

Proof.

Consider
\beta>0
and
U:=U_{1}\cup U_{2}
, with

U_{1}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:x_{2}\leq\beta|x_{1}|\big\}\ \hbox{ and }\ U_{2}:=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\overline{B_{e^{-|k|^{2}}}(k)}.

(4.41)

The set
U_{1}
is the same as in the proof of Proposition
4.4
, and as before we call
\alpha:=\arctan\beta\in(0,\pi/2)
. Notice that
U
satisfies (
2.4
) (it is at finite Hausdorff distance from
\mathbb{R}^{N}
), but not (
2.1
). Let
u
,
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
denote the solutions of (
1.1
) with initial conditions
\mathbbm{1}_{U}
,
\mathbbm{1}_{U_{1}}
and
\mathbbm{1}_{U_{2}}
, respectively. As in the proof of Proposition
4.4
, one has

\max(u_{1}(t,x),u_{2}(t,x))\leq u(t,x)\leq u_{1}(t,x)+u_{2}(t,x)\ \hbox{ for all $t\geq 0$ and $x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$}.

(4.42)

Furthermore, since
f(s)\leq f^{\prime}(0)s
for all
s\geq 0
, there holds that

0\!\leq\!u_{2}(1,x)\!\leq\!\frac{e^{f^{\prime}(0)}}{(4\pi)^{N/2}}\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\!\int_{B_{\!e^{-|k|^{2}}}\!(k)}\!\!\!\!e^{-|x-y|^{2}/4}dy\leq\!\frac{e^{f^{\prime}(0)-|x|^{2}/8}}{(4\pi)^{N/2}}\!\!\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\!\int_{B_{\!e^{-|k|^{2}}}\!(k)}\!\!\!\!e^{|y|^{2}/4}dy\!\leq\!A\,e^{-|x|^{2}/8}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, for some positive real number
A
. Therefore, for any
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, one has
0\leq u_{2}(1,x)\leq A\,e^{-\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}\,x\cdot\xi+2f^{\prime}(0)}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and using again that
f(s)\leq f^{\prime}(0)s
for all
s\geq 0
, the maximum principle implies that

0\leq u_{2}(t,x)\leq A\,e^{-\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}\,x\cdot\xi+2f^{\prime}(0)t}\ \hbox{ for all }t\geq 1\hbox{ and }x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Since
\xi\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
was arbitrary, this means that
0\leq u_{2}(t,x)\leq A\,e^{-\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}\,|x|+2f^{\prime}(0)t}
for all
t\geq 1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In particular,
\sup_{|x|\geq ct}u_{2}(t,x)\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
, for any
c>c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
.

On the other hand, from the proof of Proposition
4.4
(and the trivial extension of all functions in the variables
(x_{3},\cdots,x_{N})
), there is a sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverging to
+\infty
such that the functions
x\mapsto u_{1}(t_{n},x_{1},\zeta(t_{n})/\cos\alpha+x_{2},x_{3},\cdots,x_{N})
converge locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
to a function
\psi_{1}\in\Omega(u_{1})
that is not one-dimensional. We also recall that
\zeta(t)\sim c^{*}t
as
t\to+\infty
, hence
\zeta(t_{n})/\cos\alpha\sim(c^{*}/\cos\alpha)t_{n}
as
n\to+\infty
, with
c^{*}/\cos\alpha>c^{*}
. It then follows from (
4.42
) and the conclusion of the previous paragraph that the functions
x\mapsto u(t_{n},x_{1},\zeta(t_{n})/\cos\alpha+x_{2},x_{3},\cdots,x_{N})
still converge locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
to the function
\psi_{1}
.
Therefore,
\Omega(u)
contains the element of
\psi_{1}
, which is not one-dimensional, as well as all its shifts.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The sets

given in Propositions

are actually

Proof of Theorem
2.3

Let
u
be the solution of (
1.1
) with an initial condition
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
and a set
U
satisfying (
2.1
) and (
2.4
). Assume, by way of contradiction, that the conclusion of Theorem
2.3
does not hold. Then, there are
k\in\{2,\cdots,N\}
, a sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of positive real numbers diverging to
+\infty
and a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, such that

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}|\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u(t_{n},x_{n}))|>0.

(4.43)

u(t_{n},x_{n}+\cdot)
converge in

, to an element
\psi
of
\Omega(u)
. By Theorem

(because

). On the other hand, since
(-1)^{k}\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u(t_{n},x_{n}))

\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u(t_{n},x_{n}))

. It then follows from the convergence

in

that
\sigma_{k}(D^{2}u(t_{n},x_{n}))\to\sigma_{k}(D^{2}\psi(0))=0
as

. This contradicts (
4.43
), and the proof of Theorem
2.3
is complete.
\Box

With Theorem

Proof of Theorem

.

The set on the right-hand side of the equivalence stated in the theorem is empty if and only if

Let us show the double inclusion between the sets, as stated in the theorem. The inclusion “

Let us turn to the inclusion “
\supset

,
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{U}
and
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
be such that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\dist(x_{n},U)=+\infty,\ \ \lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|}=e,\ \hbox{ and }\ \xi_{n}\in\pi_{x_{n}}\hbox{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

We need to show that
e\in\mathcal{E}
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we set for short

k_{n}:=|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|=\dist(x_{n},U)\quad\text{ and }\quad e_{n}:=\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|}\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}.

We start with showing that

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\Big(\,\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N},\,\lambda\in(0,1)}\partial_{e_{n}}u(t,\lambda x_{n}+(1-\lambda)\xi_{n})\Big)<0.

(5.1)

Assume by contradiction that (
5.1
) does not hold. Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\tau_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

\forall\,t\geq\tau_{\varepsilon},\ \forall\,n\in\mathbb{N},\ \forall\,\lambda\in(0,1),\quad\partial_{e_{n}}u(t,\lambda x_{n}+(1-\lambda)\xi_{n})>-\varepsilon.

(5.2)

Hypothesis (
2.1
) implies the existence of two constants
\delta,R>0
such that
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})<R
. Moreover, by parabolic estimates, there is
K>0
, only depending on
f
and
N
, such that

\forall\,t\geq 1,\ \forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad|\nabla u(t,x)|\leq K.

(5.3)

Call
\delta^{\prime}:=\min(\delta,1/(8K))
. By (
3.2
) there exists
\tau>0
, only depending on
f
,
N
,
\delta
and
R
, such that the solution
v
to (
1.1
) with initial datum
v_{0}=\frac{1}{8}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}}
satisfies
v(t,x)\geq 1/2
for all
t\geq\tau
and
x\in B_{R}
. We can assume without loss of generality that
\tau\geq 1
.

Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Because
d_{\mathcal{H}}(\overline{U},U_{\delta})=d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})<R
, there exists
\zeta_{n}\in U_{\delta}
such that
|\zeta_{n}-\xi_{n}|<R
. It follows that
u_{0}(x)\geq v_{0}(x-\zeta_{n})
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and therefore, by comparison,

\forall\,t\geq\tau,\quad u(t,\xi_{n})\geq v(t,\xi_{n}-\zeta_{n})\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Using (
5.2
) we then deduce

u\Big(\tau_{\varepsilon}+\tau,\xi_{n}+\min\Big(\frac{1}{4\varepsilon},k_{n}\Big)e_{n}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{4}.

We now start an iterative argument. By (
5.3
) (recall that
\tau\geq 1
) we get

\forall\,x\in B_{\frac{1}{8K}},\quad u\Big(\tau_{\varepsilon}+\tau,\xi_{n}+\min\Big(\frac{1}{4\varepsilon},k_{n}\Big)e_{n}+x\Big)\geq\frac{1}{8}.

Since
\delta^{\prime}\leq 1/(8K)
, this allows us to compare
u(\tau_{\varepsilon}+\tau+\cdot,\xi_{n}+\min(\frac{1}{4\varepsilon},k_{n})e_{n}+\cdot)
with
v
and obtain

u\Big(\tau_{\varepsilon}+2\tau,\xi_{n}+\min\Big(\frac{1}{4\varepsilon},k_{n}\Big)e_{n}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{2},

whence by (
5.2
)

u\Big(\tau_{\varepsilon}+2\tau,\xi_{n}+\min\Big(\frac{2}{4\varepsilon},k_{n}\Big)e_{n}\Big)\geq\frac{1}{4}.

We iterate
j_{n}
times this procedure, where
j_{n}
is the smallest
j\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
j/(4\varepsilon)\geq k_{n}
. Namely, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have shown that

u(\tau_{\varepsilon}+j_{n}\tau,x_{n})\geq\frac{1}{4}\quad\text{with $j_{n}\in\mathbb{N}$ such that}\;j_{n}-1<4\varepsilon k_{n}\leq j_{n}.

(5.4)

We compute

\frac{\dist(x_{n},U)}{\tau_{\varepsilon}+j_{n}\tau}=\frac{k_{n}}{\tau_{\varepsilon}+j_{n}\tau}>\frac{k_{n}}{\tau_{\varepsilon}+(4\varepsilon k_{n}+1)\tau}\to\frac{1}{4\varepsilon\tau}\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty.

Take
c>c^{*}
and
\varepsilon<1/(4\tau c)
. It follows from the above estimate that
\dist(x_{n},U)\!>\!c(\tau_{\varepsilon}+j_{n}\tau)
for
n
large enough, but then (
5.4
) contradicts (
3.5
) because
(j_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverges to
+\infty
since
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does. This proves (
5.1
).

We can now conclude. By (
5.1
) there exist
\varepsilon>0
, a diverging sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a sequence
(n_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{N}
and a sequence
(\lambda_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in
(0,1)
such that

\partial_{e_{n_{k}}}u(t_{k},y_{k})<-\varepsilon,\quad\text{where }\;y_{k}:=\lambda_{k}x_{n_{k}}+(1-\lambda_{k})\xi_{n_{k}}.

Therefore, by parabolic estimates, the sequence of functions
(u(t_{k},y_{k}+\cdot))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in
C^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
(up to subsequences) towards a function
\psi\in\Omega(u)
satisfying
\partial_{e}\psi(0)\leq-\varepsilon
. Moreover, since
\xi_{n_{k}}\in\pi_{x_{n_{k}}}
, there also holds by definition of
y_{k}
that
\xi_{n_{k}}\in\pi_{y_{k}}
and

\frac{y_{k}-\xi_{n_{k}}}{|y_{k}-\xi_{n_{k}}|}=e_{n_{k}}\to e\quad\text{as }\;k\to+\infty.

We deduce from Theorem
4.2
that
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x\cdot e)
for some nonincreasing function
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. We further know that
\Psi^{\prime}(0)\leq-\varepsilon<0
. Theorem
4.2
then implies that
\Psi
is actually strictly decreasing in
\mathbb{R}
, hence
e\in\mathcal{E}
.
∎

6
The subgraph case: proof of Corollary
2.5

We now turn to Corollary
2.5
, which is a consequence of Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
. In order to check the geometric condition (
2.4
) of these two theorems, we will make use of the following simple property of functions with vanishing global mean.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy (
2.6
). Then

M:=\sup_{{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}}\frac{|\gamma(x^{\prime})-\gamma(y^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|+1}<+\infty.

(6.1)

In particular,
|x_{N}-\gamma(x^{\prime})|\leq M
for all
(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\partial U
.

Proof.

By (
2.6
), there exists
L>0
such that

\sup_{x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1},\,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1},\,|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\geq L}\frac{|\gamma(x^{\prime})-\gamma(y^{\prime})|}{|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|}\leq 1.

Consider
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
. Let
z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
be such that
|z^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|=L
and
|z^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\geq L
. We have that

|\gamma(x^{\prime})-\gamma(y^{\prime})|\leq|\gamma(x^{\prime})-\gamma(z^{\prime})|+|\gamma(z^{\prime})-\gamma(y^{\prime})|\leq L+|z^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|\leq 2L+|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|,

from which the desired estimate immediately follows. The last statement of Lemma
6.1
is an immediate consequence of (
6.1
).
∎

Proof of Corollary
2.5
.

The proof consists in showing that the assumptions of this corollary entail, on one hand, that
U
fulfills the hypotheses (
2.1
), (
2.4
) of Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
, and, on the other hand, that

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{x_{n}-\xi_{n}}{|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|}=\mathrm{e}_{N},

(6.2)

for any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\dist(x_{n},U)\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
and any sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\xi_{n}\in\pi_{x_{n}}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

By (
2.6
), there exists
L>0
such that

\forall\,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1},\ \forall\,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\!\setminus\!B_{L}^{\prime}(x^{\prime}),\quad\gamma(y^{\prime})\geq\gamma(x^{\prime})-|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|.

(6.3)

It follows that, for any
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
,

U\supset\big\{(y^{\prime},y_{N})\in(\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\!\setminus\!B_{L}^{\prime}(x^{\prime}))\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ y_{N}\leq\gamma(x^{\prime})-|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|\big\}.

Take
\delta>0
. Since the set in the right-hand side above contains the
N
-dimensional ball
{B_{\delta}((x^{\prime}+y^{\prime},\gamma(x^{\prime})+y_{N}))}
, for any
(y^{\prime},y_{N})
with
|y^{\prime}|=L+\delta
and
y_{N}\leq-L-3\delta
, we find that

\partial B_{L+\delta}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\times(-\infty,\gamma(x^{\prime})-L-3\delta)\subset U_{\delta}\quad\text{for all $x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}$}.

From this inclusion, and the fact that

\forall(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in U,\quad\dist\big((x^{\prime},x_{N})\,,\,\partial B_{L+\delta}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\times(-\infty,\gamma(x^{\prime})-L-3\delta)\big)\leq\sqrt{(L+\delta)^{2}+(L+3\delta)^{2}},

we deduce that

d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\delta})\leq\sqrt{(L+\delta)^{2}+(L+3\delta)^{2}},

that is, (
2.1
) holds for any
\delta>0
.

Next, we claim that

\sup_{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,U)=R,\,\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{N})\in\pi_{x}}\,\frac{|x^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|}{R}\to 0\ \hbox{ as }R\to+\infty.

(6.4)

To show (
6.4
), take
R>0
, consider any point
x_{R}=(x^{\prime}_{R},x_{R,N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\dist(x_{R},U)=R
and let
\xi_{R}=(\xi^{\prime}_{R},\xi_{R,N})\in\pi_{x_{R}}
(remember that
\pi_{x_{R}}\subset\partial U
). The quantity
h_{R}:=x_{R,N}-\gamma(x^{\prime}_{R})
satisfies
h_{R}\geq R
. We compute

R^{2}=|(x_{R}^{\prime},\gamma(x_{R}^{\prime})+h_{R})-(\xi_{R}^{\prime},\xi_{R,N})|^{2}\geq|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|^{2}+h_{R}^{2}-2h_{R}|\gamma(x_{R}^{\prime})-\xi_{R,N}|.

(6.5)

If
x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}
stays bounded as
R\to+\infty
then the limit in (
6.4
) trivially holds. Suppose instead that (up to subsequences)
|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|\to+\infty
as
R\to+\infty
. Then, by hypothesis (
2.6
) and Lemma
6.1
, it follows that
|\gamma(x_{R}^{\prime})-\xi_{R,N}|\leq|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|/2
for
R
large, and thus, for such values of
R
, (
6.5
) yields

R^{2}\geq|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|^{2}+h_{R}^{2}-h_{R}|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}h_{R}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}h_{R}^{2},

that is,
h_{R}\leq\sqrt{2}R
. Recalling that
h_{R}\geq R
, we then derive from (
6.5
) and Lemma
6.1
that

\frac{|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|}{R}\leq 2\frac{h_{R}|\gamma(x_{R}^{\prime})-\xi_{R,N}|}{R|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\frac{|\gamma(x_{R}^{\prime})-\xi_{R,N}|}{|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|}\leq 2\sqrt{2}\,\frac{|\gamma(x^{\prime}_{R})-\gamma(\xi^{\prime}_{R})|+M}{|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi_{R}^{\prime}|},

which tends to
0
as
R\to+\infty
by (
2.6
). This shows that property (
6.4
) holds.

Now, consider any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\overline{U}
such that
\dist(x_{n},U)\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
, and any sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\xi_{n}\in\pi_{x_{n}}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
6.1
, one has that

|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|\geq x_{n,N}-\xi_{n,N}\geq x_{n,N}-\gamma(\xi^{\prime}_{n})-M\geq x_{n,N}-\gamma(x^{\prime}_{n})-2M-M|x^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. But
x_{n,N}-\gamma(x^{\prime}_{n})=|x_{n}-(x^{\prime}_{n},\gamma(x^{\prime}_{n}))|\geq|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|
since
\xi_{n}\in\pi_{x_{n}}
. Hence
|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|\geq x_{n,N}-\xi_{n,N}\geq|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|-2M-M|x^{\prime}_{n}-\xi^{\prime}_{n}|
, and the last quantity is equivalent to
|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|=\dist(x_{n},U)
as
n\to+\infty
, by (
6.4
). As a consequence,
x_{n,N}-\xi_{n,N}\sim|x_{n}-\xi_{n}|
as
n\to+\infty
and, together with (
6.4
) again, the property (
6.2
) follows.

We are left to show (
2.4
). As before, let
x_{R}=(x^{\prime}_{R},x_{R,N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
such that
\dist(x_{R},U)=R>0
. We want to estimate
\mathcal{O}(x_{R})
defined by (
2.3
) when
R
is large, i.e.

\mathcal{O}(x_{R})=\sup_{\xi\in\pi_{x_{R}},\,y\in U\setminus\{\xi\}}\,\frac{x_{R}-\xi}{R}\cdot\frac{y-\xi}{|y-\xi|}.

We first consider the set of points
\xi,y
satisfying
|y-\xi|<\sqrt{R}
. Since at any
\xi\in\pi_{x_{R}}
and
y\in U
it holds that

R^{2}\leq|x_{R}-y|^{2}=R^{2}+|\xi-y|^{2}+2(x_{R}-\xi)\cdot(\xi-y),

we derive

\sup_{\xi\in\pi_{x_{R}},\,y\in U,\,0<|y-\xi|<\sqrt{R}}\,\frac{x_{R}-\xi}{R}\cdot\frac{y-\xi}{|y-\xi|}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{R}}\to 0\quad\text{as }\;R\to+\infty.

(6.6)

It remains to estimate the above scalar product when
|y-\xi|\geq\sqrt{R}
. We first observe that, for
\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{N}^{\prime})\neq y=(y^{\prime},y_{N})
,

\frac{x_{R}-\xi}{R}\cdot\frac{y-\xi}{|y-\xi|}\leq\left(\frac{|x_{R}^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|}{R}+\frac{(x_{R,N}-\xi_{N})(y_{N}-\xi_{N})}{R|y-\xi|}\right).

(6.7)

Let
\xi\in\pi_{x_{R}}
and
y\in U\setminus\{\xi\}
. The first term of the right-hand side is handled by (
6.4
). As for the second term, we notice that (
6.2
) and Lemma
6.1
imply that
x_{R,N}-\xi_{N}\geq 0
for
R
large enough, and
\xi_{N}\geq\gamma(\xi^{\prime})-M
. Therefore, since
y_{N}\leq\gamma(y^{\prime})
, it follows that

\frac{(x_{R,N}-\xi_{N})(y_{N}-\xi_{N})}{R|y-\xi|}\leq\frac{(x_{R,N}-\xi_{N})(\gamma(y^{\prime})-\gamma(\xi^{\prime})+M)}{R|y-\xi|}\leq\frac{|\gamma(y^{\prime})-\gamma(\xi^{\prime})|+M}{|y-\xi|}

(6.8)

for all
R
large enough. From this, on one hand, restricting to
|y^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|\geq\sqrt[3]{R}
, it follows from (
2.6
) that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\dist(x_{R},U)=R,\,\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{N}^{\prime})\in\pi_{x_{R}}\\
y=(y^{\prime},y_{N})\in U,\,|y^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|\geq\sqrt[3]{R}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\frac{(x_{R,N}\!-\!\xi_{N})(y_{N}\!-\!\xi_{N})}{R|y-\xi|}\leq\!\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\\
|y^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|\geq\sqrt[3]{R}\end{subarray}}\!\!\!\frac{|\gamma(y^{\prime})\!-\!\gamma(\xi^{\prime})|\!+\!M}{|y^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|}\!\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{R\to+\infty}0.

(6.9)

On the other hand, when
|y^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|<\sqrt[3]{R}
, we deduce from (
6.8
) that

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\dist(x_{R},U)=R,\,\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi_{N}^{\prime})\in\pi_{x_{R}}\\
y=(y^{\prime},y_{N})\in U\,|y^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime}|<\sqrt[3]{R},\ |y-\xi|\geq\sqrt{R}\end{subarray}}\frac{(x_{R,N}-\xi_{N})(y_{N}-\xi_{N})}{R|y-\xi|}\leq\frac{2M+\sqrt[3]{R}}{\sqrt{R}}\mathop{\longrightarrow}_{R\to+\infty}0.

(6.10)

Summing up, (
2.4
) follows from the estimates (
6.6
) and (
6.7
), (
6.4
), (
6.9
), and (
6.10
).
∎

7
Directional asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry

The arguments of the proof of Theorem
2.2
can somehow be localized. Loosely speaking, if one focuses on the asymptotic one-dimensional property around a given direction, the global geometric assumption (
2.4
) can be restricted to the points
x
around that direction, and hypothesis (
2.1
) can be relaxed too. Under such weaker assumptions, we derive the one-dimensional symmetry for functions belonging to the
directional
\Omega
-limit set
of the solution, which is defined as follows.

Definition 7.1
.

For a given bounded function
u:\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and for any direction
e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
, the set

\begin{array}[]{ll}\Omega_{e}(u):=\big\{&\!\!\!\!\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\ :\ u(t_{n},x_{n}+\cdot)\to\psi\text{ in $L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})$}\\
&\!\!\!\!\text{for some sequences $(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{+}$ diverging to $+\infty$},\\
&\!\!\!\!\text{and $(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in $\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus\{0\}$ such that $x_{n}/|x_{n}|\to e$ as $n\to+\infty$}\,\big\}\end{array}

is called the
\Omega
-limit set in the direction
e
of
u
. Notice that
\Omega_{e}(u)\subset\Omega(u)
.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
u
be a solution of (
1.1
) with an initial condition
u_{0}\!=\!\mathbbm{1}_{U}
, where
U\!\subset\!\mathbb{R}^{N}
has nonempty interior and satisfies

U\subset\big\{(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ x_{N}\leq\gamma(x^{\prime})\big\},

(7.1)

for a function
\gamma\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1})
such that

\limsup_{|x^{\prime}|\to+\infty}\frac{\gamma(x^{\prime})}{|x^{\prime}|}\leq 0.

(7.2)

Then, any function
\psi\in\Omega_{\mathrm{e}_{N}}(u)
is one-dimensional and satisfies
\psi(x^{\prime},x_{N})\equiv\Psi(x_{N})
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
with
\Psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
either constant or strictly decreasing. In particular it holds that

\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\to 0\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty,\text{ locally in $x^{\prime}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N-1}$ and uniformly in $x_{N}\!\in\![R,+\infty)$},

for any
R\in\mathbb{R}
, and moreover if the inclusion is replaced by an equality in (
7.1
), then

\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\to 0\quad\text{as }\;t\to+\infty,\text{ locally in $x^{\prime}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N-1}$ and uniformly in $x_{N}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}$}.

Proof.

We prove the result showing that, when restricted to the directional
\Omega
-limit set
\Omega_{\mathrm{e}_{N}}(u)
, the arguments of the proof of Theorem
4.2
can be performed with hypotheses (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) replaced by the assumptions that
U
has nonempty interior and fulfills (
7.1
)-(
7.2
). We will also show that the functions in
\Omega_{\mathrm{e}_{N}}(u)
are one-dimensional precisely in the direction
\mathrm{e}_{N}
. The situation is simpler here and we do not need to introduce any coordinates transformation.

Assume by contradiction that there exists
\psi\in\Omega_{\mathrm{e}_{N}}(u)
satisfying
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}\psi(\bar{x})\neq 0
for some
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Let
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\!\setminus\!\{0\}
be the associated sequences given in Definition
7.1
, that is,

t_{n}\to+\infty\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{x_{n}}{|x_{n}|}\to\mathrm{e}_{N}\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty.

(7.3)

Since
U
has nonempty interior, the invasion property (
3.2
) applies and yields

\forall\,c\in(0,c^{*}),\quad|x_{n}|\geq ct_{n}\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large},

(7.4)

because otherwise
\psi\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. In particular,
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
needs to be unbounded. Therefore, up to replacing
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
(x_{n}+\bar{x})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we can assume without loss of generality that
\bar{x}=0
. Namely, the sequences
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy (
7.3
) and (
7.4
), and by parabolic estimates it holds that

\nabla u(t_{n},x_{n})\to\beta\quad\text{as }\;n\to+\infty,

(7.5)

with
\beta=(\beta^{\prime},\beta_{N})
,
\beta^{\prime}\neq 0
. We write, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{n}=(x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n,N})
. Properties (
7.3
), (
7.4
) immediately imply

\forall\,c\in(0,c^{*}),\quad x_{n,N}=x_{n}\cdot\mathrm{e}_{N}\geq ct_{n}\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

(7.6)

Similarly, (
3.5
) implies that
\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\dist(x_{n},U)/t_{n}\leq c^{*}
(because otherwise
\psi\equiv 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
). Furthermore, it follows from (
7.1
)-(
7.3
) and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}|x_{n}|=+\infty
, that
\dist(x_{n},U)\sim x_{n,N}
as
n\to+\infty
. Therefore,
\limsup_{n\to+\infty}x_{n,N}/t_{n}\leq c^{*}
, hence

|x^{\prime}_{n}|=o(t_{n})\ \hbox{ as }n\to+\infty,

(7.7)

by using (
7.3
) again.

For given
\vartheta>0
, define the sets

H_{n}:=\overline{B^{\prime}_{\vartheta t_{n}}}\times(-\infty,\vartheta t_{n}],\qquad V_{n}:=\big\{x_{n}+s(\beta+\zeta)\ :\ s\in\mathbb{R},\,\zeta\in B_{\vartheta}\big\}.

Consider a point
P=(P^{\prime},P_{N})\in V_{n}
satisfying
P_{N}\leq\vartheta t_{n}
. Namely,
P=x_{n}+s(\beta+\zeta)
for some
\zeta\in B_{\vartheta}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\vartheta t_{n}\geq x_{n,N}-(|\beta_{N}|+\vartheta)|s|.

As a consequence, if
\vartheta<|\beta^{\prime}|
we derive

|P^{\prime}|\geq(|\beta^{\prime}|-\vartheta)|s|-|x_{n}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{|\beta^{\prime}|-\vartheta}{|\beta_{N}|+\vartheta}(x_{N,n}-\vartheta t_{n})-|x_{n}^{\prime}|.

Since
\beta^{\prime}\neq 0
and
|x^{\prime}_{n}|/x_{n,N}\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
due to (
7.3
), using (
7.6
) one can find
\vartheta\in(0,\min(|\beta^{\prime}|,c^{*}/2))
sufficiently small, only depending on
\beta^{\prime}
,
\beta_{N}
and
c^{*}
, such that, for
n
large,
|P^{\prime}|>\vartheta t_{n}
, i.e.
P\notin H_{n}
. With this choice it holds that
H_{n}\cap V_{n}=\emptyset
for
n
sufficiently large.

We then set

\sigma:=\frac{\vartheta}{3}>0,\qquad\varepsilon:=\frac{\vartheta}{2}>0.

(7.8)

We further take
\delta>0
such that
U_{\delta}\neq\emptyset
and finally
L>0
large enough so that (
4.1
) holds, the latter being possible due to (
7.1
)-(
7.2
). Observe that
0<\sigma<\vartheta<c^{*}/2
. This means that
U
fulfills the hypotheses of Lemma
4.1
. Therefore, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the solution
u^{\vartheta t_{n}}
of (
1.1
) whose initial datum is equal to the indicator function of the set
U\cap(B_{\vartheta t_{n}}^{\prime}\times\mathbb{R})
satisfies (
4.2
), i.e.

\big\|u(t_{n},\cdot)-u^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},\cdot)\big\|_{C^{1}(B^{\prime}_{\vartheta t_{n}/3}\times\mathbb{R}^{+})}<\frac{\vartheta}{2},

provided
n
is sufficiently large. By (
7.5
)-(
7.7
), one then derives
|\nabla u^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})-\beta|<\vartheta
for all
n
sufficiently large. This means that, for such values of
n
, the line
\Gamma_{n}
passing through the point
x_{n}
and directed as
\nabla u^{\vartheta t_{n}}(t_{n},x_{n})
is contained in the set
V_{n}
defined before, whence

\big(\Gamma_{n}\cap H_{n}\big)\subset\big(V_{n}\cap H_{n}\big)=\emptyset\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

On the other hand, we have that

\supp u^{\vartheta t_{n}}(0,\cdot)\subset\overline{U}\cap(\overline{B_{\vartheta t_{n}}^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R})\subset H_{n}\quad\text{for all $n$ sufficiently large}.

(7.9)

We are thus in a position to apply the reflection argument. Namely, proceeding as in Step 4 of the proof of Theorem
4.2
, with
u_{n}^{\vartheta t_{n}}
replaced by
u^{\vartheta t_{n}}
, we reach a contradiction thanks to the Hopf lemma. This proves that any
\psi\in\Omega_{\mathrm{e}_{N}}(u)
satisfies
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}\psi\equiv 0
, that is, there is a
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function
\Psi
such that
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x_{N})
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The monotonicity of
\Psi
can then be shown as in Step 5 of the proof of Theorem
4.2
, with
u_{n}
replaced by
u
,
k_{n}\mathrm{e}_{N}
by
x_{n}
, and
k_{n}
by
x_{n,N}
. Furthermore, similarly as in Step 6 of the proof of Theorem
4.2
, the function
\Psi
is either constant or strictly decreasing.

Let us deal now with the last part of the theorem concerning the convergences of
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}u
towards
0
. Consider a diverging sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, a bounded sequence
(x_{n}^{\prime})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
and a sequence
(x_{n,N})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}
. By parabolic estimates, as
n\to+\infty
, the function
u(t_{n},(x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n,N})+\cdot)
converge in
C^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, up to extraction of a subsequence, towards a function
\psi
. On one hand, if up to extraction of another subsequence,
x_{n,N}\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
, then
\psi\in\Omega_{\mathrm{e}_{N}}(u)
and thus, by the first part of the theorem proved above, there is a
C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
function
\Psi
such that
\psi(x)\equiv\Psi(x_{N})
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. On the other hand, if
(x_{n,N})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded then
\psi\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
due to (
3.2
). Summing up, we have
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}u(t_{n},x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n,N})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
when
(x_{n,N})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded from below. This proves the first convergence of
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}u
stated in the theorem. We are left with the case where, up to subsequences,
x_{n,N}\to-\infty
as
n\to+\infty
and the inclusion is replaced by an equality in (
7.1
). In such a case, even if it means replacing
U
by a measurable set
U^{\prime}\supset U
such that
U^{\prime}\setminus U
has zero Lebesgue measure, the set
U
contains, for given
\delta>0
, the half-cylinder
B_{\delta}^{\prime}\times(-\infty,\essinf_{B_{\delta}^{\prime}}\gamma)
, where
\essinf_{B_{\delta}^{\prime}}\gamma>-\infty
because
\gamma\in L^{\infty}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N-1})
. We deduce

U_{\delta}\supset\{0\}\times(-\infty,\essinf_{B_{\delta}^{\prime}}\gamma-\delta),

where
0
above stands for the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
. It follows from property (
3.4
) of Proposition
3.1
that
u(t,x^{\prime},x_{N})\to 1
as
t\to+\infty
locally in
x^{\prime}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
and uniformly in
x_{N}\!\in\!(-\infty,R]
, for any
R>0
. This implies that
\psi\equiv 1
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, hence
\nabla_{\!x^{\prime}}u(t_{n},x_{n}^{\prime},x_{n,N})\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
up to subsequences. The proof of the theorem is complete.
∎

8
Extensions and open questions

We list in this last section some extensions of the main results for more general initial data, as well as some open questions and conjectures.

Some extensions of the main results

First of all, we point out that the conclusions of the main results of Section
2
still hold for the solutions to (
1.1
) with measurable initial conditions
u_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,1]
more general than characteristic functions. To be more precise, if there are
h\in(0,1]
and
\delta>0
such that (
2.1
) is replaced by

d_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\{u_{0}\geq h\},\supp u_{0}\big)<+\infty\ \hbox{ and }\ d_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\{u_{0}\geq h\},\{u_{0}\geq h\}_{\delta}\big)<+\infty,

(8.1)

and if (
2.4
) is replaced by

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\bigg(\,\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\dist(x,\supp u_{0})=R}\mathcal{O}(x)\bigg)\leq 0,

(8.2)

then the conclusion of Theorem
4.2
– and thus of Theorem
2.2
– holds true. Indeed, first of all, one checks that Proposition
3.1
holds with
U
and
U_{\delta}
replaced by
\supp u_{0}
and
\{u_{0}\geq h\}_{\delta}
respectively, where, for the proof of (
3.4
), one defines
v
as the solution with initial datum
v_{0}=h\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\delta}}
. Then Lemma
4.1
still holds with
u_{0}^{R}:=u_{0}\,\mathbbm{1}_{B^{\prime}_{R}\times\mathbb{R}}
and with the assumption (
4.1
) replaced by

\{u_{0}\geq h\}_{\delta}\cap B_{L}\neq\emptyset\ \text{ \ and \ }\ \supp u_{0}\setminus(B^{\prime}_{L}\times\mathbb{R})\,\subset\,\Big\{(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times\mathbb{R}\ :\ x_{N}\leq\frac{\sigma}{2c^{*}}|x^{\prime}|\Big\}

(but now in the conclusion (
4.2
) the time
\tau_{\varepsilon}
depends on
h
too). Next, one repeats the arguments of the proof of Theorem
4.2
with the
U_{n}
defined as rigid transformations of
\supp u_{0}
in place of
U
, and
L:=d_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\{u_{0}\geq h\},\{u_{0}\geq h\}_{\delta}\big)+1
in (
4.16
) and (
4.23
).

As a consequence of Theorem
2.2
, the conclusion of Theorem
2.3
still holds for initial conditions
u_{0}
satisfying (
8.1
)-(
8.2
) instead of
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
with (
2.1
) and (
2.4
). Similarly, the conclusion of Theorem
2.4
holds for such
u_{0}
’s, with
U
replaced by
\supp u_{0}
in the statement, while the conclusion of Corollary
2.5
holds when
d_{\mathcal{H}}\big(\{u_{0}\geq h\},\supp u_{0}\big)<+\infty
and
d_{\mathcal{H}}\big(U,\supp u_{0}\big)<+\infty
, with
U
still satisfying (
2.5
)-(
2.6
). Finally, the conclusion of Theorem
2.1
is satisfied when
u_{0}
fulfills (
8.1
) instead of (
1.3
) and (
2.1
), and when the convexity – or convex proximity – of
U
is replaced by that of
\supp u_{0}
.

Some open questions and conjectures

To complete the paper, we propose a list of open questions and conjectures related to our results. First of all, let us call
\varphi
the traveling front profile with minimal speed, that is, for each
e\in\mathbb{S}^{N-1}
,
\varphi(x\cdot e-c^{*}t)
satisfies (
1.1
) with
0=\varphi(+\infty)<\varphi<\varphi(-\infty)=1
and
c^{*}=2\sqrt{f^{\prime}(0)}
. Based on Theorems
2.2
and
4.2
, and according to the definition (
2
) of
\mathcal{E}
, we propose the following.

Conjecture 8.1
.

Let
u
be as in Theorem
2.2
. Then,

\Omega(u)=\big\{\,0,\ 1,\ \varphi(x\cdot e+a)\ :\ e\in\mathcal{E},\ a\in\mathbb{R}\,\big\}.

(8.3)

This conjecture is known to hold when
U
is bounded with non-empty interior, by
[
8
,
25
]
, and when
U
is the subgraph of a bounded function, or more generally when there are two half-spaces
H
and
H^{\prime}
–necessarily with parallel boundaries– such that
H\subset U\subset H^{\prime}
, by
[
3
,
4
,
14
,
20
,
28
]
.

We have shown in Lemma
4.3
that the assumption (
2.4
) of Theorem
2.2
is stable by bounded perturbations of the sets
U
. We could then wonder whether the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry is also stable with respect to bounded perturbations of the initial support. Namely, if the solution to (
1.1
) with an initial datum
\mathbbm{1}_{U}
satisfying (
2.1
) is asymptotically locally planar, and if
U^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies (
2.1
) and
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U^{\prime},U)<+\infty
, then is the solution to (
1.1
) with initial datum
\mathbbm{1}_{U^{\prime}}
asymptotically locally planar as well ?

One can also wonder whether the reciprocal of Theorem
2.2
is true, in the following sense: if the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry holds for a solution
u
of (
1.1
) with initial datum
\mathbbm{1}_{U}
and
U
satisfying (
2.1
), does necessarily
U
fulfill (
2.4
) ? The answer is immediately seen to be negative in general: take for instance
U
given by

U=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}[2^{n},2^{n}+1]\times\mathbb{R}^{N-1},

which fulfills (
2.1
) but not (
2.4
), while
u
– hence any element of
\Omega(u)
– is one-dimensional, depending on the variable
x_{1}
only. However, the question is open if
U
is connected.

Our study concerns the Fisher-KPP equation, with functions
f
satisfying (
1.2
). However, the same question of asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry can be asked for more general reaction terms
f
, still with
f(0)=f(1)=0
. First of all, the hypothesis (
2.1
) should be strengthened, by requiring
\delta>0
to be large enough. Indeed, if
f
is for instance of the bistable type

f^{\prime}(0)<0,\ \ f^{\prime}(1)<0,\ \ f<0\hbox{ in }(0,\alpha),\ \ f>0\hbox{ in }(\alpha,1),\ \ \int_{0}^{1}f(s)ds>0

(8.4)

for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then by
[
7
,
29
]
there is
\delta_{0}>0
such that the solution to (
1.1
) with initial condition
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\delta_{0}}}
converges uniformly as
t\to+\infty
to a ground state, that is, a positive radial solution converging to
0
as
|x|\to+\infty
, hence
u
is not asymptotically locally planar. However, if
u_{0}:=\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\delta}}
with
\delta>\delta_{0}
, then
u(t,x)\to 1
as
t\to+\infty
locally uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. We then say that the
invasion property
holds if there is
\rho
such that the solution
u
to (
1.1
) with initial condition
\mathbbm{1}_{B_{\rho}}
satisfies
u(t,x)\to 1
as
t\to+\infty
locally uniformly in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For general functions
f
for which the invasion property holds, if
U
is bounded and
U_{\rho}\neq\emptyset
, then the solutions to (
1.1
) with initial condition
\mathbbm{1}_{U}
are known to be asymptotically locally planar, by
[
17
]
. The same conclusion holds for bistable functions
f
of the type (
8.4
) if there are two half-spaces
H
and
H^{\prime}
–necessarily with parallel boundaries– such that
H\subset U\subset H^{\prime}
, by
[
3
,
9
,
21
,
22
]
(see also
[
24
]
for the case of more general functions
f
). On the other hand, still for bistable functions
f
of the type (
8.4
), the solutions
u
to (
1.1
) with initial condition
\mathbbm{1}_{U}
are not asymptotically locally planar if
U
is V-shaped, that is, if it is the union of two half-spaces with non-parallel boundaries, by
[
11
,
12
,
23
,
26
]
. These known results lead us to formulate the following De Giorgi type conjecture for the solutions of the reaction-diffusion equation (
1.1
) beyond the Fisher-KPP case.

Conjecture 8.2
.

Assume that the invasion property holds for some
\rho>0
. Let
u
be the solution to (
1.1
) with an initial datum
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
such that
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
satisfies
d_{\mathcal{H}}(U,U_{\rho})<+\infty
and (
2.4
). Then any function in
\Omega(u)
is one-dimensional and, in addition, it is either constant or strictly monotone.

Let us also mention another natural question related to the preservation of the convexity of the upper level sets of
u
when
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
and
U
is convex. It is known from
[
5
,
16
]
that, if
U
is convex, then the solution of the heat equation
\partial_{t}u=\Delta u
is quasi-concave at each
t>0
, that is, for each
t>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the upper level set
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}:u(t,x)>\lambda\}
is convex. The same conclusion holds for (
1.1
) set in bounded convex domains instead of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and under some additional assumptions on
f
, by
[
15
]
. A natural question is to wonder for which class of functions
f
this property still holds for (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Notice finally that, for any solution
u
to (
1.1
), for any sequence
(t_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
diverging to
+\infty
, and for any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the functions
u(t_{n}+\cdot,x_{n}+\cdot)
converge locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}
, up to extraction of a subsequence, to an entire solution to (
1.1
) (that is, solution for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
). Remembering Theorem
2.1
on the asymptotic one-dimensional symmetry for the solutions to (
1.1
) with
u_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{U}
and
U
convex, and having in mind the question of the previous paragraph on the convexity of the upper level sets, it is then natural to ask the following: if an entire solution
v:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to[0,1]
to (
1.1
) is quasi-concave for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
, is
v(t,\cdot)
necessarily one-dimensional for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
?
