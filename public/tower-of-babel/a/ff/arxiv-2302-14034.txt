Asymptotic theory for quadratic variation of harmonizable fractional stable processes
Andreas Basse-O’Connor, Mark Podolskij

Abstract
In this paper we study the asymptotic theory for quadratic variation of a harmonizable fractional α \alpha -stable process. We show a law of large numbers with a non-ergodic limit and obtain weak convergence towards a Lévy-driven Rosenblatt random variable when the Hurst parameter satisfies H ∈ ( 1 / 2 , 1 ) H\in(1/2,1) and α ⁡ ( 1 − H ) < 1 / 2 \alpha(1-H)<1/2 . This result complements the asymptotic theory for fractional stable processes investigated in e.g. [ 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 17 , 21 ] . Key words : fractional processes, harmonizable processes, limit theorems, quadratic variation, stable Lévy motion. AMS 2010 subject classifications : 60F05, 60F15, 60G22, 60G48, 60H05.

Introduction

Since the seminal work of Mandelbrot and Van Ness
[
20
]
fractional stochastic processes and fields have gained a lot of attention in the probabilistic and statistical literature. One of the key results of
[
20
]
was the introduction of the

fractional Brownian motion
, the most famous fractional stochastic process. The authors show that, up to scaling, fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\in(0,1)
is the unique self-similar centered Gaussian process with stationary increments.
When the Gaussianity assumption is replaced by
\alpha
-stable distributions
the aforementioned class becomes much richer: Rosinski
[
24
]
shows that it consists of (mixed)
moving average
\alpha
-stable processes
,
\alpha
-stable harmonizable processes
and processes of a third kind (which lack an explicit description). The most well-known representative of the first class is the
linear fractional stable motion
(lfsm), whose probabilistic and statistical properties have been studied in numerous articles. We refer to
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
17
,
22
,
23
]
for a detailed exposition of limit theorems
for functionals of lfsm, and to
[
1
,
10
,
12
,
18
,
19
,
21
]
for statistical estimation of lfsm and related processes.

In the paper
[
9
]
the authors introduced the
\alpha
-stable harmonizable fractional motion, defined as

X_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\exp(its)-1}{is}|s|^{1-H-1/\alpha}\,dL_{s},

(1.1)

where
i:=\sqrt{-1}
,
H\in(0,1)
is the Hurst parameter and

L_{t}:=L_{t}^{1}+iL_{t}^{2}
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
, denotes an isotropic
complex-valued
\alpha
-stable Lévy motion with
\alpha\in(0,2)
. The processes
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is self-similar with index
H
, i.e.
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}(a^{-H}X_{at})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, and possesses stationary increments. Probabilistic properties of model (
1.1
) and their extensions have been studied in numerous articles, see e.g.
[
2
,
8
,
9
,
11
,
15
]
among others. However, to the best of our knowledge, asymptotic theory for statistics of harmonizable fractional
\alpha
-stable processes has not yet being investigated in the literature. In contrast to lfsm, harmonizable fractional
\alpha
-stable processes are not ergodic, which indicates potential difficulties when studying limit theorems.

This paper aims to derive asymptotic results for the quadratic statistic

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2},

(1.2)

and our main statement is the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the
\alpha
-stable harmonizable fractional motion introduced in (
1.1
) with parameters
\alpha\in(0,2)
and
H\in(0,1)
.

(i)

As
n\to\infty
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}U:=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s|^{-2H-2/\alpha}\left(1-\cos(s)\right)d([L^{1}]_{s}+[L^{2}]_{s}),

(1.3)

where
[L^{k}]
denotes the quadratic variation of
L^{k}
for
k=1,2
.

(ii)

For
H>1/2

and
\alpha(1-H)<1/2
we obtain the weak convergence

\displaystyle n^{2-2H}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2}-U\right)

(1.4)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-1.0pt}{{\tiny d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}2{\textrm{Re}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1-\exp(i(s-u))}{i(s-u)}|su|^{1-H-1/\alpha}1_{\{u<s\}}\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}\Big).

(1.5)

Remark 1.2
.

(A)
The result of Theorem
1.1
(i) is in sharp contrast to the setting of short memory processes. To make this comparison concrete, let us consider a sequence
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent
\alpha
-stable isotropic random variables with
\alpha\in(0,2)
. According to the
Stable Central Limit Theorem, cf.
[
13
, Theorem 5.25]
,

\frac{1}{n^{2/\alpha}}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|Y_{j}\|^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-1.0pt}{{\tiny d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}Z,

(1.6)

where
Z
is an
(\alpha/2)
-stable random variable. That is,
for
independent
\alpha
-stable random variables, the quadratic variation

\sum_{j=0}^{n}\|Y_{j}\|^{2}
is of the order
n^{\alpha/2}
, whereas

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2}

is of the order
n
, cf. Theorem
1.1
(i). This difference may be viewed
as an indication of the strong dependence between the random variables

(X_{j}-X_{j-1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
.

(B) We believe that the randomness of the limit in (
1.3
)
is connected to the non-ergodicity of the process
X
.

(C) The weak convergence result of (
1.4
) is similar in spirit to the Gaussian case. Indeed, the quadratic variation of the fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H
is asymptotically distributed according to the Rosenblatt random variable when
H\in(3/4,1)
with convergence rate
n^{2H-2}
; the classical Rosenblatt random variable possesses the same representation as the right hand side of (
1.4
) where
L
is replaced by a complex-valued Gaussian measure. We also recall that there is no convergence regime of the form (
1.4
) in the setting of lfsm
(cf.
[
4
,
5
]
).

(D) The proof of Theorem
1.1
heavily relies on the quadratic form of the statistic. Extensions to more general functional forms and statistical estimation of the process
X
are therefore far from obvious.
\diamond

.

2
Some background

In this section we will collect some important results on
stochastic integration theory.
A complex-valued random variable
Z
is called isotropic if it holds that
\exp(i\theta)Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}Z
for any
\theta\in(0,2\pi)
. Similarly, a stochastic process
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

is isotropic when any linear combination
\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}Z_{t_{j}}
is isotropic. It is easy to see
that a complex-valued Lévy process
L
with characteristic triplet
(0,0,\nu)
, where
\nu
denotes the Lévy measure, is isotropic if and only if

\nu\circ(x\mapsto x\exp(i\theta))^{-1}=\nu\qquad\forall\theta\in(0,2\pi).

(2.1)

In particular,
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
both have the same symmetric
\alpha
-stable distribution. For any measurable function

f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
we define

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)\,dL_{s}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\quad:=\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Re}(f(s))\,dL^{1}_{s}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}(f(s))\,dL^{2}_{s}\Big)+i\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Re}(f(s))\,dL^{2}_{s}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\text{Im}(f(s))\,dL^{1}_{s}\Big),

(2.3)

whenever the four real-valued integrals on the right-hand side exist.
We note that the integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(s)\,dL_{s}
is well defined if and only if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(s)\|\,dL^{1}_{s}
is well defined, which holds if

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|f(s)\|^{\alpha}\,ds<\infty
. The next result, which follows by
[
25
, Theorem 2]
, gives a criterion for the existence
of the double integral:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
be a measure function satisfying
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(s)^{\alpha}\,ds=1
, and suppose that
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is a two-dimensional symmetric
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with
Z_{t}=(Z^{1}_{t},Z^{2}_{t})
. The double integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u,v)\,dZ^{1}_{u}\,dZ^{2}_{v}
of a measurable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
vanishing outside
\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}:u<v\}

exists when the following condition holds:

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(s,t)|^{\alpha}\Big[1+\log_{+}\Big(\frac{|f(s,t)|}{\psi(s)\psi(t)}\Big)\Big]\,ds\,dt<\infty,

(2.4)

where
\log_{+}(x):=\log(x)
for
x\geq 1
and
\log_{+}(x):=0
for
x<1
.

We remark that
[
25
, Theorem 2]
is only stated in the case where
Z^{1}=Z^{2}
; however, the extension to
Z^{1}\neq Z^{2}
follows directly using a polarization techniques similar to the polarization equality for real numbers
xy=((x+y)^{2}-(x-y)^{2})/4
. Proposition
2.4
extends directly to complex-valued functions and processes as well.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
is measurable function with

\|g\|_{\alpha}^{\alpha}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|g(x)\|^{\alpha}dx<\infty
and set
f(u,v)=\overline{g}(u)g(v)1_{\{u<v\}}
for all
u,v\in\mathbb{R}
. Then we have the following statements.

(A) The function
f
satisfies condition (
2.4
), and hence the
double integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u,v)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{v}
is well defined,
cf. Proposition
2.1
. Indeed, (
2.4
) follows by choosing

\psi(s)=\|g(s)\|/\|g\|_{\alpha}
.

(B) The following identity holds

\Big\|\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(s)\,dL_{s}\Big\|^{2}=2\text{Re}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{g}(u)g(s)1_{\{u<s\}}\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}\Big)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|g(s)\|^{2}\,d([L^{1}]_{s}+[L^{2}]_{s}).

(2.5)

To show (
2.5
) we note that

Y_{t}:=\int_{-\infty}^{t}g(u)\,dL_{u}
exists for all
t\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.
For all
w<t
in
\mathbb{R}
we have by integration by parts that

\displaystyle Y_{t}\overline{Y}_{t}-Y_{w}\overline{Y}_{w}={}

\displaystyle\int_{w}^{t}Y_{s-}\,d\overline{Y}_{s}+\int_{w}^{t}\overline{Y}_{s-}\,dY_{s}+[Y,\overline{Y}]_{t}-[Y,\overline{Y}]_{w}

(2.6)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle 2\text{Re}\Big(\int_{u}^{t}\overline{Y}_{s-}\,dY_{s}\Big)+[Y,\overline{Y}]_{t}-[Y,\overline{Y}]_{w},

(2.7)

where
[Z,\tilde{Z}]
denotes the quadratic variation of two complex-valued semimartingales
Z
and
\tilde{Z}
.
If we let
t\to\infty
and
w\to-\infty
in (
2.7
) we obtain

Y_{\infty}\overline{Y}_{\infty}=2\text{Re}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\overline{Y}_{s-}\,dY_{s}\Big)+[Y,\overline{Y}]_{\infty}.

(2.8)

By reconizing the integral on the right-hand side of (
2.8
)
as a double integral and calculating the
quadratic variation
[Y,\overline{Y}]
we
derive the identity

\|Y_{\infty}\|^{2}=Y_{\infty}\overline{Y}_{\infty}=2\text{Re}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\overline{g}(u)g(v)1_{\{u<v\}}\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{v}\Big)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\|g(s)\|^{2}\,d([L^{1}]_{s}+[L^{2}]_{s}),

(2.9)

where we have used that
[L,\overline{L}]_{t}=[L^{1}]_{t}+[L^{2}]_{t}
.
Equation (
2.9
) implies (
2.5
).

\diamond

We will also use the dominated convergence result

[
14
, Theorem 6.2(c)]
, which reads as follows.

Proposition 2.3
.

Assume that
(h_{n})_{n\geq 1}
,
h
and
g
are deterministic measurable functions
\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
supported on
\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\!:u<v\}
all satisfying condition (
2.4
). Suppose moreover that
(Z_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is a two-dimensional symmetric
\alpha
-stable Lévy process with
Z_{t}=(Z^{1}_{t},Z^{2}_{t})
. If
h_{n}(u,v)\to h(u,v)
for Lebesgue almost all points
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\sup_{n}\|h_{n}\|\leq g
, then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h_{n}(u,v)\,dZ^{1}_{u}\,dZ^{2}_{v}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h(u,v)\,dZ_{u}^{1}\,dZ^{2}_{v}\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(2.10)

3
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Throughout this section all positive constants are denoted by
C
, although they may change from line to line. We start with the identity

X_{j}-X_{j-1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\exp(ijs)r(s)\,dL_{s}\qquad\text{with}\qquad r(s):=\frac{1-\exp(-is)}{is}|s|^{1-H-1/\alpha}.

(3.1)

In particular, we have that

\|r(s)\|\leq C\left(|s|^{1-H-1/\alpha}1_{\{|s|\leq 1\}}+|s|^{-H-1/\alpha}1_{\{|s|>1\}}\right),

(3.2)

which implies the statement

\|r\|_{\alpha}<\infty.

(3.3)

By using the representations (
3.1
) and (
2.5
) on the function
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
g(s)=\exp(ijs)r(s)
we deduce the identity

\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2}=V_{j}+U,

(3.4)

where

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle:=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s|^{-2H-2/\alpha}\left(1-\cos(s)\right)d([L^{1}]_{s}+[L^{2}]_{s})\qquad\text{and}

\displaystyle V_{j}

\displaystyle:=2\text{Re}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\exp(ij(s-u))r(s)\overline{r}(u)1_{\{u<s\}}\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}\Big).

(3.5)

3.1
Proof of convergence in (
1.3
)

By the decomposition (
3.4
) we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2}=U+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}V_{j},

(3.6)

and thus we only need to prove that

n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}V_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. We first note that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\exp(ij(s-u))=\frac{1-\exp(in(s-u))}{n(1-\exp(i(s-u)))}\to 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

(3.7)

for any
s-u\not\in\{2\pi m:~m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, using the geometric series. On the other hand, we have

\|\exp(ij(s-u))r(s)r(u)\|\leq\|r(s)\|\|r(u)\|.

(3.8)

In view of (
3.3
) and Remark
2.2
(A), we can apply Proposition
2.3
to conclude that

n^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}V_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

(3.9)

which implies (
1.3
).

3.2
Proof of weak convergence

We will divide the proof into several steps. We write
g_{n}(x):=(1-\exp(inx))/(1-\exp(ix))

and define the following complex-valued functions

\displaystyle h_{n}(s,u)

\displaystyle:=n^{1-2H}g_{n}(s-u)r(s)\overline{r}(u)1_{\{u<s\}},

(3.10)

\displaystyle h(s,u)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1-\exp(i(s-u))}{i(s-u)}|su|^{1-H-1/\alpha}1_{\{u<s\}}.

(3.11)

By the representation (
3.4
) we have

n^{2-2H}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|X_{j}-X_{j-1}\|^{2}-U\right)=n^{1-2H}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}V_{j}=2\textrm{Re}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h_{n}(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}\Big),

(3.12)

where the last equality follows from the identity in (
3.7
) and the definition of
h_{n}
. Thus, to
show the limit theorem (
1.4
) it is enough to show the convergence

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h_{n}(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-1.0pt}{{\tiny d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}.

(3.13)

The following subsections will be denoted to the proof of (
3.13
), and to this aim we define

\gamma:=1-H-1/\alpha.

3.2.1
Existence of the limit

In this subsection we will show that the limiting integral
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}
from (
3.13
) is well-defined
by proving that
h
satisfies condition (
2.4
).
Set

\psi(s):=c\left(|s|^{-r}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|s|>1\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{|s|\leq 1\}}\right)

(3.14)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, where
r
is any real number satisfying
r>\alpha^{-1}
and
c=c_{r}>0
is chosen such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\psi(s)^{\alpha}\,ds=1
. To show condition (
2.4
) with respect to the function
\psi
we will use the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}
be a complex-valued function satisfying the inequality

\|f(s,u)\|\leq C|su|^{-r_{1}}\left(1_{[s-1,s)}(u)+|s-u|^{-r_{2}}1_{(-\infty,s-1)}(u)\right),

(3.15)

where the numbers
r_{1},r_{2}
satisfy the conditions
r_{1}\in(1/2,1)
and
2r_{1}+r_{2}>2
.
Then it holds that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|f(s,u)\|\,du\,ds<\infty.

(3.16)

Proof.

According to condition (
3.15
) we have that
\|f(s,u)\|\leq f_{1}(s,u)+f_{2}(s,u)
and hence
we need to show the integrability of the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. We have that

\int_{s-1}^{s}|u|^{-r_{1}}du\leq C\left(1_{[-1,1]}(s)+s^{-r_{1}}1_{[-1,1]^{c}}(s)\right)

since
r_{1}\in(1/2,1)
. Hence, we conclude

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{1}(s,u)\,du\,ds\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}|s|^{-r_{1}}\left(1_{[-1,1]}(s)+s^{-r_{1}}1_{[-1,1]^{c}}(s)\right)ds

and the latter is finite as
r_{1}\in(1/2,1)
.

Now we turn our attention to the function
f_{2}
. We use the substitution
s=xu
to obtain
the bound

\displaystyle\int_{u+1}^{\infty}|s|^{-r_{1}}|s-u|^{-r_{2}}ds

\displaystyle=|u|^{1-r_{1}-r_{2}}\int_{\frac{u+1}{u}}^{\infty}|x|^{-r_{1}}|x-1|^{-r_{2}}dx

(3.17)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1_{[-1,1]}(u)+\left(|u|^{-r_{1}}+|u|^{1-r_{1}-r_{2}}\right)1_{[-1,1]^{c}}(u)\right)

(3.18)

Indeed, the latter integral is finite since
r_{1}+r_{2}>2r_{1}+r_{2}-1>1
. Thus, we deduce

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f_{2}(s,u)\,ds\,du\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u|^{-r_{1}}\left(1_{[-1,1]}(u)+\left(|u|^{-r_{1}}+|u|^{1-r_{1}-r_{2}}\right)1_{[-1,1]^{c}}(u)\right)ds<\infty

due to assumptions
r_{1}\in(1/2,1)
and
2r_{1}+r_{2}>2
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we proceed with the proof of existence. First of all, we observe that the function

\|h(s,u)\|^{\alpha}
satisfies the condition (
3.15
) with

r_{1}=\alpha\gamma\qquad\text{and}\qquad r_{2}=\alpha.

It is easy to see that assumptions
r_{1}\in(1/2,1)
and
2r_{1}+r_{2}>2
are satisfied due to

H>1/2
and
\alpha(1-H)<1/2
. On the other hand, for any
\varepsilon>0
we readily deduce that

\log_{+}\left(\frac{\|h(s,u)\|}{\psi(s)\psi(u)}\right)\leq C\left(|s|^{\varepsilon}+|s|^{-\varepsilon}\right)\left(|u|^{\varepsilon}+|u|^{-\varepsilon}\right).

(3.19)

When we choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we conclude that the function

\|h(s,u)\|^{\alpha}\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{\|h(s,u)\|}{\psi(s)\psi(u)}\right)\right)

also satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.1
. This proves the existence of the double integral

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}
, cf. Proposition
2.1
.

3.2.2
Main decomposition

Now, we show the weak limit theorem in (
1.4
). For this purpose, we introduce the sets

\mathcal{Z}:=\{2\pi j:~j\geq 0\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:~|x-\mathcal{Z}|<\delta\}

for some fixed
\delta\in(0,1)
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we also use the notation
[x]:=2\pi j
where

2\pi j\in\mathcal{Z}
is the closest number to
x
. We obtain the following estimate for the
function
g_{n}
:

\displaystyle\|g_{n}(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|\frac{1-\exp(in(x-[x]))}{i(x-[x])}\right\|1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}}(x)+\delta^{-1}1_{\mathcal{Z}^{c}_{\delta}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(n1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{1/n}}+\frac{1}{|x-[x]|}1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}\cap\mathcal{Z}_{1/n}^{c}}+\delta^{-1}1_{\mathcal{Z}^{c}_{\delta}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(n^{1-a}|x-[x]|^{-a}1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}}+\delta^{-1}1_{\mathcal{Z}^{c}_{\delta}}(x)\right)

(3.20)

for any
a\in(0,1)
.
We decomposition the double integral on the right-hand side of
(
3.12
) as follows

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h_{n}(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}=Z_{n}^{(1)}+Z_{n}^{(2)}+Z_{n}^{(3)},

(3.21)

with

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{(1)}

\displaystyle:=\int_{(-\delta,\delta)^{2}}h_{n}(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}

(3.22)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{(2)}

\displaystyle:=\int_{(-\delta,\delta)^{c}\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}}h_{n}(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}

(3.23)

\displaystyle Z_{n}^{(3)}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\big((-\delta,\delta)^{c}\times(-\delta,\delta)\big)\cup\big((-\delta,\delta)\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}\big)}h_{n}(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}.

(3.24)

In the following, we will prove that
Z_{n}^{(1)}
is the dominating term and show its convergence, while we will prove that
\|Z_{n}^{(2)}\|+\|Z_{n}^{(3)}\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. The following identity in distribution

\int_{A}f(s,u)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}n^{-2/\alpha}\int_{nA}f(s/n,u/n)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s},

(3.25)

where
nA:=\{(ns,nu)\!:~(s,u)\in A\}
, will be used in our proof. The identity (
3.25
) follows from the self-similarity of
L
; more precisely, from the fact that
(L_{t/n})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
equals
(n^{-1/\alpha}L_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
in finite dimensional distributions.

3.2.3
Term
Z_{n}^{(1)}

We apply the distributional identity (
3.25
) to deduce that

Z_{n}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}n^{-2/\alpha}\int_{(-\delta n,\delta n)^{2}}h_{n}(s/n,u/n)\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}.

(3.26)

For any fixed
(s,u)
with
s-u\not\in\mathcal{Z}
we obtain the convergence

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{\gamma}r(s/n)=|s|^{\gamma}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-1}g_{n}(s-u)=\frac{1-\exp(i(s-u))}{i(s-u)}.

(3.27)

Hence, we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}n^{-2/\alpha}h_{n}(s/n,u/n)=h(s,u).

(3.28)

On the other hand, we have that

\|n^{-2/\alpha}h_{n}(s/n,u/n)\|^{\alpha}\leq C|su|^{\gamma}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{s-1\leq u<s\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{u<s-1\}}|s-u|^{-\alpha}\right)=:f(s,u).

(3.29)

As in the proof of existence we deduce that the function
f
satisfies the condition (
2.4
). Thus,

Z_{n}^{(1)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\raisebox{-1.0pt}{{\tiny d}}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1-\exp(i(s-u))}{i(s-u)}|su|^{1-H-1/\alpha}1_{\{u<s\}}\,d\overline{L}_{u}\,dL_{s}

(3.30)

due to Proposition
2.3
.

3.2.4
Term
Z_{n}^{(2)}

We will use the approximation (
3.20
). First of all,
we have that

\|h_{n}(s,u)\|1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}^{c}}(s-u)\leq C\delta^{-1}n^{1-2H}|su|^{\gamma-1}1_{\{u<s\}}.

(3.31)

Note that
n^{1-2H}\to 0

since
H>1/2
. On the other hand, the function
f(s,u):=|su|^{\gamma-1}1_{\{u<s\}}
satisfies the condition

\int_{(-\delta,\delta)^{c}\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}}f(s,u)^{\alpha}\left(1+\log_{+}\left(\frac{f(s,u)}{\psi(s)\psi(u)}\right)\right)du\,ds<\infty

where we use an estimate of the type (
3.19
) for the log term.
For all
a\in(0,1)
we have

\displaystyle\|h_{n}(s,u)\|1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}}(s-u)

\displaystyle\leq Cn^{2-2H-a}|su|^{\gamma-1}\left|(s-u)-[s-u]\right|^{-a}1_{\{u<s\}}.

(3.32)

It is easy to show that the function
(s,u)\mapsto|su|^{\gamma-1}|(s-u)-[s-u]|^{-a}1_{((-\delta,\delta)^{c})^{2}}(s,u)1_{\{u<s\}}
satisfies the condition (
2.4
) when
a\alpha<1
. Choosing
a=1/\alpha-\varepsilon
we deduce that

n^{2-2H-a}=n^{2-2H-1/\alpha+\varepsilon}\to 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty,

if we choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough since
\alpha(1-H)<1/2
. We, therefore, conclude that there exists a measurable function
\phi
satisfying (
2.4
) such that
|h_{n}(u,v)|\leq\phi(u,v)
for all

(u,v)\in(-\delta,\delta)^{c}\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}
. Since
h_{n}(u,v)\to 0
Lebesgue almost surely, cf. (
3.31
)–(
3.32
), Proposition
2.3
yields
Z_{n}^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
.

3.2.5
Term
Z_{n}^{(3)}

The proof is similar to the one in Subsection
3.2.4
. For simplicity we only consider the integration region
(-\delta,\delta)\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}
; the

(-\delta,\delta)^{c}\times(-\delta,\delta)
case is analogue. For all
(u,s)\in(-\delta,\delta)\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}
:

\|h_{n}(s,u)\|1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}^{c}}(s-u)\leq C\delta^{-1}n^{1-2H}|u|^{\gamma}|s|^{\gamma-1}1_{\{u<s\}}.

(3.33)

We have
n^{1-2H}\to 0
and the function
f(s,u):=|u|^{\gamma}|s|^{\gamma-1}1_{(-\delta,\delta)}(u)1_{(-\delta,\delta)^{c}}(s)1_{\{u<s\}}
satisfies the condition (
2.4
).
On the other hand, we have

\|h_{n}(s,u)\|1_{\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}}(s-u)\leq Cn^{2-2H-a}|u|^{\gamma}|s|^{\gamma-1}\left|(s-u)-[s-u]\right|^{-a}1_{\{u<s\}}.

(3.34)

The bounds (
3.33
) and (
3.34
) imply the existence of
a measurable function
f
satisfying (
2.4
) such that
|h_{n}(u,v)|\leq f(u,v)
for all
(u,v)\in(-\delta,\delta)\times(-\delta,\delta)^{c}
. Since a similar bound holds on

(-\delta,\delta)^{c}\times(-\delta,\delta)
we conclude, cf. Proposition
2.3
, that
Z_{n}^{(3)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
since
h_{n}\to 0

(cf. (
3.33
)–(
3.34
)).

By the decomposition (
3.21
) and the
convergence results of Subsections
3.2.3
–
3.2.5

we obtain the limit theorem (
3.13
). This completes the proof of
(
1.4
) and of Theorem
1.1
.
