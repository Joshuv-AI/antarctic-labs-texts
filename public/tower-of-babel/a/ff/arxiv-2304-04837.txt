Abstract

This partition problem has connections to a diverse range of topics, including deterministic rounding schemes, pseudodeterminism, replicability, as well as Sperner/KKM-type results. In this work, we establish near-optimal relationships between k k and ε \varepsilon . We show that, for any bounded measure partitions and for any d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 , it must be that k ≥ ( 1 + 2 ​ ε ) d k\geq(1+2\varepsilon)^{d} . We also provide new constructions of secluded partitions that work for a broad spectrum of k ⁡ ( d ) k(d) and ε ⁡ ( d ) \varepsilon(d) parameters. Our Contributions

Our work makes four key contributions. Firstly, we establish a lower bound on the degree parameter

Theorem 1.7

Given a coloring of

Theorem 1.8

4.1
Prerequisite Results

In this section, we will deal with arbitrary norms of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We point out the well-known fact that all norms on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are equivalent in the sense that they all generate the same topology on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given two norms
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{a}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert^{b}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists fixed constants
c_{d},C_{d}\in(0,\infty)
such that for all vectors
\vec{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that
c_{d}\lVert\vec{x}\rVert^{a}\leq\lVert\vec{x}\rVert^{b}\leq C_{d}\lVert\vec{x}\rVert^{a}
. Thus the collection of open sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the same no matter which norm we are using. This also means that the Borel and Lebesgue
\sigma
-algebras on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are the same no matter which norm is used, and thus balls with respect to any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
are measurable.

We begin with four simple facts. All have straightforward proofs which we provide in
\Autoref
*sec:other
and
\Autoref
sec:minkowski-sums. The first fact will later allow us to pass a result through a limit since the answer will be an integer.

Fact 4.1
.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\gamma<\alpha
and
\lceil\gamma\rceil=\lceil\alpha\rceil
.

The next fact says that the Minkowski sum of a set
X
and an open ball at the origin can be viewed as a union of open balls positioned at each point of
X
.

Fact 4.2
.

For any normed vector space, given a set
X
and
\varepsilon\in[0,\infty)
, then

X+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})=\bigcup_{\vec{x}\in X}B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{x}).

The same can be said replacing open balls with closed balls.

The third fact says that we can decompose a ball into a Minkowski sum of two smaller balls.

Fact 4.3
.

For any normed vector space, and any
\alpha,\beta\in(0,\infty)
, it holds that
B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\alpha,\vec{0})+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\beta,\vec{0})=B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\alpha+\beta,\vec{0})
.

The fourth fact, while also very simple, is the key change of perspective that allowed us to prove the main results of this section. It says that if we are checking which sets
X
in our partition intersect an
\varepsilon
-ball located at
\vec{p}
(in order to see how many there are), we can instead enlarge each member of the partition by taking its Minkowski sum with the origin-centered
\varepsilon
-ball, and check which of these enlarged members contain the point
\vec{p}
.

Fact 4.4
.

For any normed vector space, for any set
X
, for any vector
\vec{p}
, and any
\varepsilon>0
, the following are equivalent:

1.

B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{p})\cap X\not=\emptyset

2.

\vec{p}\in X+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})

The same can be said replacing both open balls with closed balls.

Now we introduce the result which is the connection to the above-mentioned key change of perspective. The result says to consider a bounded, (measurable) subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
(so it has finite measure) and a collection
\mathcal{A}
of (measurable) subsets of
S
. If we compute the sum of measures of all members in the collection
\mathcal{A}
(i.e. intuitively the total volume that they take up), and compare this to the measure/volume of
S
, then whatever this ratio is, we can find a point in
S
covered by that many members of the collection
\mathcal{A}
.
For example, in the simplest case that the total measure of members of
\mathcal{A}
is larger than the measure of
S
, then there is no way for all of the members of
\mathcal{A}
to be disjoint, so there has to be some point covered by two members. This simple case can be viewed as a continuous version of the pigeonhole principle.

In the more generic case, this result should be intuitively true by an averaging argument: if every point of
S
is covered only
n
times, then the total measure of members in
\mathcal{A}
is at most
n\cdot m(S)
, so if the ratio of total measure in
\mathcal{A}
to the measure of
S
is large, then
n
must also be large. This more general version is a sort of continuous multi-pigeonhole principle.

The next ingredient that we need is a way to measure how large the Minkowski sum in
\Autoref

Theorem 4.5

(Generalized Brunn-Minkowski Inequality)
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be Lebesgue measurable such that
A+B
is also Lebesgue measurable. Then

m(A+B)\geq\left[m(A)^{\frac{1}{d}}+m(B)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right]^{d}.

This version of the statement can be obtained from
[
Gar02
, Equation 11]
; in that survey, Gardner states this theorem with a requirement that the sets be bounded, but in the following paragraph notes that this is not necessary and the requirement is only stated for convenience of the presentation in that survey.

In the theorem, the requirement that
A+B
is Lebesgue measurable is not a triviality; Gardner discusses that there exist known Lebesgue measurable sets
A
and
B
such that the Minkowski sum
A+B
is not Lebesgue measurable as shown in
[
Sie20
]
. The next result gives us a way to circumvent this issue in our application even if the members of our partition are not measurable by taking
B
to be an open set so that the sum
A+B
is open (and thus measurable), and using the outer measure of
A
so that we don’t need the assumption that
A
is measurable.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathbb{R}^{d}
be equipped with any norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
. Let
Y\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
. Then
Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})
is open (and thus Borel measurable), and
m(Y+B^{\circ}(\varepsilon,\vec{0}))\geq\left(m_{out}(Y)^{\frac{1}{d}}+\varepsilon\cdot(v_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert},d})^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}
.

Proof.

By
\Autoref
minkowski-bubble-union, for any
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\in(0,\infty)
,
Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},\vec{0})=\bigcup_{\vec{y}\in Y}B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime\prime},\vec{y})
which is a union of open sets, so is itself open and thus Borel measurable. Now, for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, observe that by
\Autoref

B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})=B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})
and thus, this sum is measurable because it is an open ball. Using this equality and the associativity of the Minkowski sum, we have

Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})=Y+\left[B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right]=\left[Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right]+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0}).

Thus, we have the following chain of inequalities (each justified after it is stated):

\displaystyle m\left(Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})\right)

\displaystyle=m\left(\left[Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right]+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right)

(Open, measurable, equality above)

\displaystyle\geq\left(m\left(Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+m\left(B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}

The above comes from the
Theorem 4.5

(Generalized Brunn-Minkowski Inequality)
.
(
\Autoref
*generalized-brunn-minkowski-inequality)
noting that as demonstrated above, terms of the sum
\left[Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right]
and
B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})
are open and thus measurable, and the same holds for the sum itself
\left(Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})\right)
. We continue.

\displaystyle\geq\left(m_{out}\left(Y\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+m\left(B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}

The above inequality comes from the definition of the outer measure of
Y
being the infimum of the measures of all measurable supersets of
Y
. Since
Y\subseteq Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\vec{0})
, we get the inequality above. Continuing, we have the following:

\displaystyle=\left(m_{out}\left(Y\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+m\left(\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(1,\vec{0})\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}

(Scaling of norm-based balls)

\displaystyle=\left(m_{out}\left(Y\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+\left[(\varepsilon^{\prime})^{d}\cdot m\left(B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(1,\vec{0})\right)\right]^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}

(Scaling for Lebesgue measure)

\displaystyle=\left(m_{out}\left(Y\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot(v_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert},d})^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}

(Algebra and
v_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert},d}\defeq m\left(B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(1,\vec{0})\right)
)

Since the inequality above holds for all
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, it must also hold in the limit (keeping
d
and
Y

m\left(Y+B^{\circ}_{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert}(\varepsilon,\vec{0})\right)\geq\lim_{\varepsilon^{\prime}\to\varepsilon}\left[\left(m_{out}\left(Y\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot(v_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert},d})^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}\right]=\left(m_{out}\left(Y\right)^{\frac{1}{d}}+\varepsilon\cdot(v_{{\scriptscriptstyle\lVert\cdot\rVert},d})^{\frac{1}{d}}\right)^{d}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Fact A.1

(
\ell_{\infty}

.

Let

and

be a bounded set with diameter

(with respect to
\ell_{\infty}
). Then there exists
\vec{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

denotes outer Lebesgue measure.

Proof Sketch.

For each coordinate

, consider the set
X_{i}=\left\{\pi_{i}(\vec{x})\colon\vec{x}\in X\right\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
of the
i
th coordinates of each point in
X
. The infimum and supremum are distance at most
D
apart, because otherwise there would be points

\lVert\vec{z}-\vec{y}\rVert_{\infty}>D

Fact A.2
.

If
\mu
is a measure and
\mathcal{A}
is a (possibly uncountable) family of pairwise disjoint measurable sets, then

\mu(\bigsqcup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}A)\geq\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A).

Proof.

By definition of the arbitrary summation (c.f.
[
Fol99
, p. 11]
) we have

\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)\defeq\sup\left\{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\mu(A):\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{A},\;\mathcal{F}\text{ finite}\right\}

and for any
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
we have

\mu(\bigsqcup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}A)\geq\mu(\bigsqcup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\mu(A).

Thus
\mu(\bigsqcup_{A\in\mathcal{A}}A)
is an upper bound for the set
\left\{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\mu(A):\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{A},\;\mathcal{F}\text{ finite}\right\}
and thus greater than or equal to the supremum.
∎

Fact A.3

(Interchange of Countable Sums with Non-negative Terms)
.

If
I,J
are countable sets, and

for all
(i,j)\in I\times J
, then

\sum_{i\in I}\sum_{j\in J}a_{i,j}=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{i\in I}a_{i,j}

This can be proved directly via basic analysis methods if
I
and

and the definition of the infinite sum as a limit of finite sums is used. Alternatively, viewing the summation as an integral over a countable measure space, this can be viewed as a corollary to Tonelli’s theorem.

Lemma A.4

(Exact Measure of Multiplicity)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
X
be a measurable set in some measure space (the measure being denoted by
\mu
) and
\mathcal{A}
a countable family of measurable subsets of
X
such that for each
x\in X
,
x
belongs to exactly
n
members of
\mathcal{A}
. Then

\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)=n\cdot\mu(X).

Proof.

We note that if
n=0
, then the statement is trivially true because
\mathcal{A}
is either empty or contains just the empty set, and in either case
\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)=0=0\cdot\mu(X)
if we use the standard convention that the empty sum is
0
.

For any
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, let
G_{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A
noting that this is a countable intersection of measurable sets, so it is measurable (mnemonically, the
G
represents an intersection as it does in the notation for
G_{\delta}
sets).

Let
\mathcal{A}\choose n
denote all subsets of
\mathcal{A}
of size
n
noting that because
\mathcal{A}
is countable, so is
\mathcal{A}\choose n
. Observe that for distinct
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}
, the sets
G_{\mathcal{F}}
and
G_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}
are disjoint because

G_{\mathcal{F}}\cap G_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}=\left(\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A\right)\cap\left(\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}A\right)=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}A

and since
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
are distinct and each contain
n
items,
\lvert\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\rvert\geq n+1
, and by assumption no point in
X
belongs to
n+1
members, so
\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}A=\emptyset
. Furthermore, for each
x\in X
, since
x
belongs to exactly
n
members
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
of
\mathcal{A}
, taking
\mathcal{F}=\left\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}\right\}
we have
x\in G_{\mathcal{F}}
which shows that
\left\{G_{\mathcal{F}}\colon\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}\right\}
is a partition of
X
into countably many measurable sets (allowing that some
G_{\mathcal{F}}
might be empty).

The last observation we need is that for any
\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}
and any
A\in\mathcal{A}
, it holds that if
A\in\mathcal{F}
, then
A\supseteq G_{\mathcal{F}}
and if
A\not\in\mathcal{F}
then
A\cap G_{\mathcal{F}}=\emptyset
. To see this, note that for any
x\in G_{\mathcal{F}}
,
x
belongs to each of the
n
members of
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, and since by assumption
x
belongs to exactly
n
members of
\mathcal{A}
, it does not belong to any other members of
\mathcal{A}
.

Now we have the following chain of equalities:

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A\cap X)

(
A\subseteq X
so
A\cap X=A
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(A\cap\left[\bigsqcup_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}G_{\mathcal{F}}\right]\right)

(Set equality; the
G_{\mathcal{F}}
partition
X
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(\bigsqcup_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\left[A\cap G_{\mathcal{F}}\right]\right)

(Set equality)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\mu\left(A\cap G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)\right]

(Countable additivity of measures)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\left[\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(A\cap G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)\right]

(Interchange sums by
\Autoref
interchange-of-countable-sums)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\left[\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\begin{cases}\mu\left(A\cap G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)=\mu\left(G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)&A\in\mathcal{F}\\
\mu\left(A\cap G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)=\mu(\emptyset)=0&A\not\in\mathcal{F}\end{cases}\right]

(Previous paragraph)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\left[\sum_{A\in\mathcal{F}}\mu\left(G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)\right]

(Remove
0
terms from summation)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\left[n\cdot\mu\left(G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)\right]

(
\lvert\mathcal{F}\rvert=n
)

\displaystyle=n\sum_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}\mu\left(G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)

(Linearity of summation)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\mu\left(\bigsqcup_{\mathcal{F}\in{\mathcal{A}\choose n}}G_{\mathcal{F}}\right)

(Countable additivity of measures)

\displaystyle=n\cdot\mu\left(X\right)

(Set equality; the
G_{\mathcal{F}}
partition
X
)

This proves the result.
∎

Lemma A.5

(Upper Bound Measure of Multiplicity)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
X
be a measurable set in some measure space (the measure being denoted by
\mu
) and
\mathcal{A}
a countable family of measurable subsets of
X
such that for each
x\in X
,
x
belongs to at most
n
members of
\mathcal{A}
. Then

\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)\leq n\cdot\mu(X).

Proof.

As in the last proof, for any
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, let
G_{\mathcal{F}}=\bigcap_{A\in\mathcal{F}}A
noting that this is a countable intersection of measurable sets, so it is measurable (mnemonically, the
G
represents an intersection as it does in the notation for
G_{\delta}
sets). And for any
k\in[n]\cup\left\{0\right\}
, let
\mathcal{A}\choose k
denote all subsets of
\mathcal{A}
of size
k
noting that because
\mathcal{A}
is countable, so is
\mathcal{A}\choose k
.

For each

, let

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in X\colon\text{$x$ belongs to {exactly} $k$ members of $\mathcal{A}$}\right\}

\displaystyle S_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in X\colon\text{$x$ belongs to {at least} $k$ members of $\mathcal{A}$}\right\}

We will show that
S_{k}
and
S_{k}^{\prime}
are measurable.

To show that the

are measurable, note that for any

,

can be expressed as

. This is because for any
x\in X
, if
x
belongs to at least
k

\mathcal{A}
, then there is a subset

with

) such that

, so
x
belongs to at least
k
members of
\mathcal{A}
. Thus, since
\mathcal{A}
is countable, so is
\mathcal{A}\choose k
(for each
k
) implying that each
S_{k}^{\prime}
is a countable union of measurable sets, so is itself measurable.

To show the measurability of each
S_{k}
, first consider
k=n
. Observe that
S_{n}=S_{n}^{\prime}
because by assumption each
x\in X
belongs to at most
n
members of
\mathcal{A}
, so it belongs to exactly
n

n
members. Thus,
S_{n}
is also measurable.

Now for

if and only if it belongs to at least
k

\mathcal{A}
. Thus, for

is the set difference of two measurable sets, so is itself measurable.

Finally, note that
\left\{S_{k}\colon k\in[n]\cup\left\{0\right\}\right\}
is a partition of

(allowing the possibility that some

are empty) because every point of

belongs to some number of members of
\mathcal{A}
, and that number is (by assumption) between
0
and
n

Now we have the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A\cap X)

(
A\subseteq X
so
A\cap X=A
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(A\cap\left[\bigsqcup_{k\in[n]\cup\left\{0\right\}}S_{k}\right]\right)

(Set equality; the
S_{k}
partition
X
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(\bigsqcup_{k\in[n]\cup\left\{0\right\}}\left[A\cap S_{k}\right]\right)

(Set equality)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\left[\sum_{k\in[n]\cup\left\{0\right\}}\mu\left(A\cap S_{k}\right)\right]

(Countable additivity of measures)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[n]\cup\left\{0\right\}}\left[\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu\left(A\cap S_{k}\right)\right]

(Interchange sums by
\Autoref

Corollary A.6

(Lower Bound Cover Number)
.

Let
X
be a measurable set in some measure space (the measure being denoted by
\mu
) such that
0<\mu(X)<\infty
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a countable family of measurable subsets of
X
such that
\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)<\infty
. Then there exists
x\in X
such that
x
belongs to at least
\left\lceil\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}\right\rceil
-many members of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

First observe that by hypothesis,
\lceil\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}\rceil
is finite. Suppose for contradiction that each
x\in X
belongs to strictly less than
\lceil\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}\rceil
-many members of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
n=\lceil\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}\rceil-1
(noting that
n<\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}
). Then each
x\in X
belongs to at most
n
-many members of
A
, so we have

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\mu(X)

(
\Autoref
upper-bound-measure-of-multiplicity)

\displaystyle<\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}\mu(X)

(
0<\mu(X)<\infty
and
n<\frac{\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)}{\mu(X)}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(A)

which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark A.7
.

In
\Autoref

, but this is in general not true (see

, there exists a point

that is contained in at least

-many sets of
\mathcal{A}
. The distinction is that this point might have to depend on the choice of

.
