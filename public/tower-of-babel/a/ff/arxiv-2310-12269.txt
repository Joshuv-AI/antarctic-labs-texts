Weakly-Popular and Super-Popular Matchings with Ties and Their Connection to Stable Matchings
Gergely Csáji (HUN-REN KRTK KTI)

Abstract
In this paper, we study a slightly different definition of popularity in bipartite graphs G = ( U , W , E ) G=(U,W,E) with two-sided preferences, when ties are present in the preference lists. This is motivated by the observation that if an agent u u is indifferent between his original partner w w in matching M M and his new partner w ′ ≠ w w^{\prime}\neq w in matching N N , then he may probably still prefer to stay with his original partner, as change requires effort, so he votes for M M in this case, instead of being indifferent. We show that this alternative definition of popularity, which we call weak-popularity, allows us to guarantee the existence of such a matching and also to find a weakly-popular matching in polynomial time that has size at least 3 4 \frac{3}{4} the size of the maximum weakly popular matching. We also show that this matching is at least 4 5 \frac{4}{5} times the size of the maximum (weakly) stable matching, so may provide a more desirable solution than the current best (and tight under certain assumptions) 2 3 \frac{2}{3} -approximation for such a stable matching. We also show that unfortunately, finding a maximum size weakly popular matching is NP-hard even with one-sided ties, and that assuming some complexity-theoretic assumptions, the 3 4 \frac{3}{4} -approximation bound is tight. Next, we study a model more general than weak-popularity, where for each edge, we can specify for both endpoints the size of improvement the endpoint needs to vote in favor of a new matching N N . We show that even in this more general model, a so-called γ \gamma -popular matching always exists and that the same positive results still hold. Finally, we define another, stronger variant of popularity, called super-popularity, where even a weak improvement is enough to vote in favor of a new matching. We show that for this case, even the existence problem is NP-hard.

Introduction

In preference-based markets, each
market participant expresses their preferences as a strict ordering of some possible scenarios, and the aim is to find solutions that are fair or optimal according to some fairness or optimality notion. The area is extensively used and studied in economics, mathematics and computer science, originating from the seminal paper of Gale and Shapley

[
10
]
about stable matchings. Stability means that we aim for solutions, where there is no pair of agents that could mutually improve by dropping their partners and getting matched together. Their model for the stable matching problem has been applied to countless real word scenarios, from resident-allocation to school-choice and even kidney exchange programs. For an overview of the area of preference based markets, see for example the book of Manlove

[
21
]
. A relaxation of stability, called popularity, which gained significant theoretical interest in the literature recently, was introduced by Gärdenfors

[
11
]
. Popular matchings translate the simple majority voting rule into the world of matchings under preferences, i.e a matching is popular if it does not lose a head-to-head comparison to any other matching. A very attractive property of popular matchings is that they still always exist in bipartite markets with two-sided preferences (in fact, every stable matching is popular), and their size may even be two times the size of stable matchings. For an overview of popular matchings, see Cseh

[
5
]
.

A large drawback of popular matchings however, is that they do not necessarily exist when ties are allowed in the preferences of the agents and even worse, deciding if an instance with ties admits a popular matching or not is NP-complete, even if ties occur on one side of the market only, as shown in
[
6
]
. In contrast, stable matchings still always exist in this model; however, a maximum size stable matching becomes NP-hard to find (
[
17
]
) and the current best approximation ratio is
\frac{2}{3}
(
[
23
]
,
[
20
]
) which holds even in significantly more general settings (
[
3
]
). In this paper, we show that with a natural alternative definition of popularity in the presence of ties, we can guarantee the existence of a popular matching and also find a
\frac{3}{4}
-approximation of a maximum size such matching in polynomial time, that is also a
\frac{4}{5}
-approximation for the maximum size stable matching.

We also study a strengthened variant called super-popularity, where we show that even deciding if such a matching exists is NP-hard.

1.2
Our contributions

In this paper, we define new notions of popularity, called weak-popularity, super-popularity and
\gamma
-popularity, where the first is inspired by weak-stability, the second by super-stability and the last is inspired by
\gamma
-minstability. We prove that weakly-popular and
\gamma
-popular matchings always exist, and we can find a near-maximum size such matching in linear time. In particular, we show that in both the weak-popularity model and the more general
\gamma
-popularity model, there exists a
\frac{3}{4}
-approximation algorithm. We also show that the size of the output of this algorithm is at least
\frac{4}{5}
times the size of the maximum size stable matching and
\gamma
-minstable matching, respectively.

We use the edge duplication technique that has proven to be a very efficient technique in problems related to stable matchings. While this technique is very similar to the idea of promoting agents, it allows us to handle, analyze and solve problems much more easily. Our algorithm combines and extends the one of Király
[
20
]
for
Max-SMTI
(generalized in
[
3
]
for the maximum size
c\gamma
-stable matching ) and the one in Kavitha
[

]
for the maximum size popular matching with strict preferences.

On the hardness side, we show that finding a maximum size weakly popular matching in an instance with ties is NP-hard, even if ties occur on one side of the market only, so an exact polynomial algorithm for the problem is not very likely. We also show that the
\frac{3}{4}
approximation ratio is tight under the strong-Unique Games Conjecture or the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis.

2
Preliminaries

We investigate matching markets, where the set of agents with the possible set of contracts is given by a (not necessarily simple) bipartite graph
G=(U,W;E)
. For each agent
v\in U\cup W
, we denote by
E(v)
the edges that are incident to
v
. We assume that for each agent
v
, there is a
preference valuation

p_{v}:E(v)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, which defines a weak ranking over the adjacent edges of
v
. This is a strictly more general setting than ordinary weak preference lists, where only a weak order
\succeq_{v}
is given for each agent
v\in U\cup W
over
E(v)
. We note that we assume that the vertices rank their adjacent edges instead of the adjacent agents on the other side, because we allow parallel edges in our model. We also assume that
p_{v}(\emptyset)\leq 0
, which means that an agent always weakly prefers to be matched to any acceptable partners rather than being unmatched.

We say that an edge set
M\subset E
is a
matching
, if
|M\cap E(v)|\leq 1
for each
v\in U\cup W
. For a vertex
v
, let
M(v)
denote the edge incident to
v
in
M
, if there is any, otherwise
M(v)
is
\emptyset
. A matching
M
is
maximal
if no edge can be added to it. A matching is
maximum
if it is largest among all matchings.

Stability.

We start by defining weakly stable matchings.
We say that a matching
M
is
weakly stable
, or simply
stable
, if there is no edge
e=(u,w)\notin M
, such that
p_{u}(e)>p_{u}(M(u))
and
p_{w}(e)>p_{w}(M(w))
. Otherwise, such an edge is called a
blocking edge
.

In the context of ordinary weak preference orders
\succeq_{v}
for
v\in U\cup W
this is just equivalent to saying that there should be no edge
(u,w)
where
u
and
w
strictly prefer each other to their partners in
M
.

Super Stability.

A stronger version of stability, called super-stability, is the following. Let
M
be a matching. We say that
M
is
super-stable
, if there is no edge
e=(u,w)\notin M
such that
p_{u}(e)\geq p_{u}(M(u))
and
p_{w}(e)\geq p_{w}(M(w))
, so here even a weak improvement is enough to block
M
.

\gamma
-min stability.

Now we define
\gamma
-min stability.
Suppose we are given
\gamma_{e}^{u}>0,\gamma_{e}^{w}>0
values for each edge
e=(u,w)\in E
. They specify the size of improvement an agent
u
needs in order to prefer the edge
e
over his partner
M(u)
. Let
M
be a matching. We say that an edge
e=(u,w)\notin M

\gamma
-min blocks
M
, if
p_{u}(e)\geq p_{u}(M(u))+\gamma_{e}^{u}
and
p_{w}(e)\geq p_{w}(M(w))+\gamma_{e}^{w}
. We say that
M
is
\gamma
-min stable
, if there is no
\gamma
-min blocking edge to
M
. The case of simple stability corresponds to the special case where every
\gamma_{e}^{v}
value is sufficiently small.

Popularity.

Now, we define popular matchings with their original definition. Let
M
be a matching, and
N
be another matching. Each agent
v\in U\cup W
compares these two matchings and casts a vote as follows. If
p_{v}(M(v))=p_{v}(N(v))
, then we define
\textit{vote}_{v}(M,N)=0
. If
p_{v}(M(v))>p_{v}(N(v))
, then
\textit{vote}_{v}(M,N)=+1
, and finally if
p_{v}(M(v))<p_{v}(N(v))
, then
\textit{vote}_{v}(M,N)=-1
. Then, we aggregate the votes of each agent and obtain
\Delta(M,N)=\sum_{v\in U\cup W}\textit{vote}_{v}(M,N)
. Clearly, with this definition we have
vote_{v}(M,N)+vote_{v}(N,M)=0
.
We say that a matching
M
is
popular
, if for any matching
N
, it holds that
\Delta(M,N)\geq 0
.

Weak popularity.

We provide an alternative definition of popularity as follows. Let
M,N
be two matchings. We define
\textit{vote}^{weak}_{v}(M,N)
in a slightly different manner. If
p_{v}(M(v))>p_{v}(N(v))
or
p_{v}(M(v))>p_{v}(N(v))
, then
\textit{vote}^{weak}_{v}(M,N)
is
+1
and
-1
respectively, same as before. Also, if
M(v)=N(v)
, then again
\textit{vote}^{weak}_{v}(M,N)=0
. However, when
M(v)\neq N(v)
, but
p_{v}(M(v))=p_{v}(N(v))
, we define
\textit{vote}^{weak}_{v}(M,N)=+1
.

This is motivated by the fact that if an agent
v
does not improve in the matching
N
compared to
M
, but has to change partners, then he would probably prefer to keep his original partner and not go through the additional effort to obtain a similarly liked new partner.

Then, we define a matching
M
to be
weakly-popular
, if for any matching
N
, it holds that
\Delta^{weak}(M,N)=\sum_{v\in U\cup W}\textit{vote}^{weak}_{v}(M,N)\geq 0
.

This is also related to the definition of weakly stable matchings, where in order for a pair
(u,w)
to block, both must strictly improve.

\gamma
-popularity.

We give a generalization of weak popularity, inspired by
\gamma
-min stability.
Suppose again that we are given
\gamma_{e}^{u}>0,\gamma_{e}^{w}>0
values for each edge
e=(u,w)\in E
.

Let
M,N
be two matchings. We define
\textit{vote}^{\gamma}_{v}(M,N)
as follows. First, if
M(v)=N(v)
, then
\textit{vote}^{\gamma}_{v}(M,N)=0
. If
p_{v}(N(v))\geq p_{v}(M(v))+\gamma_{N(v)}^{v}
, then
\textit{vote}^{\gamma}_{v}(M,N)=-1
. Finally, if
p_{v}(N(v))<p_{v}(M(v))+\gamma_{e}^{v}
and
M(v)\neq N(v)
, then
\textit{vote}^{\gamma}_{v}(M,N)=+1
.

Then, we define a matching
M
to be
\gamma
-popular
, if for any matching
N
, it holds that
\Delta^{\gamma}(M,N)=\sum_{v\in U\cup W}\textit{vote}^{\gamma}(M,N)\geq 0
.

Super popularity.

Motivated by super-stable matchings, we can introduce a stronger variant of popularity, called super-popularity. In the super-stable matching model, even a partner that is equally good is considered as good enough to block.
Hence, now we define the votes as follows.
Let
M,N
be two matchings. If
p_{v}(M(v))>p_{v}(N(v))
, then
\textit{vote}^{super}_{v}(M,N)=+1
. If
N(v)=M(v)
, then
\textit{vote}^{super}_{v}(M,N)=0
. Finally, if
p_{v}(N(v))\geq p_{v}(M(v))
and
M(v)\neq N(v)
, then
\textit{vote}^{super}_{v}(M,N)=-1
.

Then, we say that
M
is
super-popular
if for any other matching
N
, it holds that
\Delta^{super}(M,N)=\sum_{v\in U\cup W}\textit{vote}^{super}_{v}(M,N)\geq 0
.

The studied problems.

Now we define the optimization problem we investigate, called
Maximum
\gamma
-popular matching with ties and incomplete preferences
abbreviated as
max-
\gamma
-PM
for short.

max-
\gamma
-PM

Input:

A bipartite graph
G=(U,W;E)
,
p_{v}()
preference valuations for each
v\in U\cup W
, numbers
0<\gamma_{e}^{v}
for each pair
(e,v)\in E\times(U\cup W)
such that
v\in e
.

Output:

A maximum size
\gamma
-popular matching
M
.

For the restricted case of weak-popularity, which corresponds to the case when each
\gamma_{e}^{v}
is sufficiently small, we call the optimization problem
max-weak-pm
.

We define the following existence problem too.

\gamma
-
super-pm

Input:

A bipartite graph
G=(U,W;E)
,
p_{v}()
preference valuations for each
v\in U\cup W
, numbers
0<\gamma_{e}^{v}
for each pair
(e,v)\in E\times(U\cup W)
such that
v\in e
.

Output:

Is there a super-popular matching
M
?

The restriction of the problem to ordinary weak preferences is denoted by
super-pm
.

Before we move on to our main results, let us show a simple theorem that connects
\gamma
-popular and
c\gamma
-stable matchings.

Theorem 1
.

Any
\gamma
-min stable matching
M
is also
\gamma
-popular.

Proof.

Let
M
be a
\gamma
-min stable matching. Then, for any matching
N
, it holds that the sum of votes on each edge
e=(u,w)
of
N
is at least 0. Indeed, by the definition of the votes, either both are 0 or neither are. Hence, if the two votes of the endpoints are negative, then both votes with
-1
, but by the definition of the votes, such an edge is a blocking edge to
M
, contradiction.
∎

Hardness of finding maximum weakly popular matchings

In this section, we give a simple proof of the NP-hardness of
max-weak-pm
, even in very restricted settings.

Theorem 2
.

max-weak-pm
is NP-hard, even with one-sided ties.

Proof.

We reduce from

, where we are given an instance

of the stable matching problem with ties and a number

, and the question is whether there exists a stable matching with size at least

(
[

Let

be such an instance of

from

to

to get

to

for each

is

We show that there is a stable matching of size

+1
, but we do not count them on the edges of

. Also, there are

, who vote with
+1
. Also, if such an

agent has a partner

in

, then

also votes with
+1
, as otherwise the edge

would have blocked

, as

was unmatched in

Any edge of

, where both endpoints vote with

must be of the form

. Let the number of these be

. Then, these edges together give us

aggregate votes, but the original partners of the

in this case. Our goal is to show that

. In this case, the

and the

Each agent from the set

originally had a partner in

. As the agents in

can only be matched to agents from

or one less agent in

. Therefore, we do indeed have

.

\blacksquare

Hence, it is NP-hard to decide if there is a weakly-popular matching of size at least

.
∎

Let

\gamma

1. Create an instance

We describe the algorithm in more detail as follows. Let

\gamma
-PM

Then, we create strict preferences as follows. For a vertex

a\succeq^{\gamma}b\succ c\succ x\succeq^{\gamma}y\succ z

For a vertex

z\succeq^{\gamma}y\succ x\succ c\succeq^{\gamma}b\succ a
.

For any non-

p_{v}()
by breaking the ties arbitrarily. Here,

denotes that for any two edges

is always ranked higher than the copy

. The notion

means that
\alpha(e)\succ_{v}\beta(e)
for any edge

\beta(f)\succ_{v}\alpha(e)
, if and only if

.

This ranking can be obtained in the following way. For each vertex

rank the

by breaking the ties arbitrarily. Then we can insert the

and

for any edge

For the

If there are still some ties remaining, we break them arbitrarily.

We remark that this definition allows the

Approximation analysis and inapproximability

First, we give some examples to illustrate that our approximation analysis is tight in all three cases, even in the case of ordinary weak preferences and one-sided ties.

Example 1
.

Consider an instance with agents
\{w_{1},u_{1},w_{2},u_{2},w_{3},u_{3}\}
. Let the edges be
E\cup F
, where
E=\{e_{i}=(u_{i},w_{i})\mid i=1,2,3\}

All agents with degree

to

\frac{2}{3}|N|=|M|
.

Example 2
.

Claim 8
.

Suppose that there is a weakly-popular matching
M^{\prime}
in
I^{\prime}
of size at least
\frac{5}{2}n-k
. Then, there is a maximal matching in
G

.

Proof:

Let
M
be the restriction of
M^{\prime}
to
G
. Suppose that
|M|=k^{\prime}>k
. Then, we get that the size of
M^{\prime}
is at most
(n-k^{\prime})+k^{\prime}+(n-k^{\prime})+\frac{n}{2}=\frac{5}{2}n-k^{\prime}<\frac{5}{2}n-k
, contradiction.

Therefore, it is enough to show that

is maximal. Suppose that
(u_{i},w_{j})
is an edge of

. If
u_{i}
or
w_{j}
is unmatched in
M^{\prime}
, then at most one of them leaves a partner behind if they get matched together and strictly improve, so we obtain a matching that dominates

, contradiction.

Hence,
(w_{j},w_{j}^{\prime})\in M^{\prime}
and
(u_{i},u_{l}^{\prime})\in M^{\prime}

and
z_{l}^{\prime}
vote with

. Hence,

\blacksquare

Claim 9
.

If there is a maximal matching of size
k
in
G
, then there is a weakly-popular matching of size at least
\frac{5}{2}n-k
in
I^{\prime}
.

Proof:

Let
M
be such a matching of
G
. Extend it to a matching
M^{\prime}
of
I^{\prime}
as follows. For each
i\in[n]
, such that
w_{i}
is unmatched in
M
, add
(w_{i},w_{i}^{\prime})
. Let
\{u_{i_{1}},\dots,u_{i_{n-k}}\}
be the unmatched agents in
U
by
M
. Then, we add the edges
(u_{i_{j}},u_{j}^{\prime}),(z_{j},z_{j}^{\prime})
for
j\in[n-k]
. As
n-k\leq\frac{n}{2}
, we can do this. Finally, if
n-k<\frac{n}{2}
, then for each
n-k<j\leq\frac{n}{2}
we add
(u_{j}^{\prime},z_{j})
. Clearly
|M^{\prime}|=(n-k)+k+2(n-k)+(\frac{n}{2}-(n-k))=\frac{5}{2}n-k
.

We show that
M^{\prime}
is weakly-popular. From the fact that
M
is inclusionwise maximal, we get that the only blocking edges to
M^{\prime}
are the edges
(u_{j}^{\prime},z_{j})
,
j\in[n-k]
. Therefore, these are the only possible
(-1,-1)
voting edges in another matching.

Suppose that a matching
N^{\prime}
dominates
M^{\prime}
. Take the components of the symmetric difference
N^{\prime}\triangle M^{\prime}
. Then, there is a component such that within this component,
N^{\prime}
also dominates
M^{\prime}
. In such a component, there must be an edge
e
of
N^{\prime}
, such that both of its endpoints vote with
-1
. As we have seen, such an edge must be
(u_{j}^{\prime},z_{j})
, for some
j\in[n-k]
. As for a vertex
z_{j}
, his only other neighbor is
z_{j}^{\prime}
, which is a leaf vertex, we get that at most two such edges can be in any component and the component cannot be a cycle. Also, if
(u_{j}^{\prime},z_{j})\in N^{\prime}
, then
z_{j}^{\prime}
is unmatched in
N^{\prime}
, but is matched in
M^{\prime}
, so such edges can only be in components in
M^{\prime}\triangle N^{\prime}
, where they are adjacent to an
M^{\prime}
-edge, whose other endpoint is only covered by
M^{\prime}
.

Let
(u_{j}^{\prime},z_{j})\in N^{\prime}
. If
u_{i_{j}}
is unmatched in
N^{\prime}
, then he and
z_{j}^{\prime}
vote with
+1
in the component and only
u_{j}^{\prime}
and
z_{j}
vote with
-1
, contradiction.

If
(u_{i_{j}},u_{l}^{\prime})\in N^{\prime}
, then both of them vote with
+1
, so there is a
(+1,+1)
edge. Therefore, even if there are two
(-1,-1)
edges of
N^{\prime}
in the component, the
(+1,+1)
edge and the two
+1
votes by the endpoints only covered by
M^{\prime}
cancel those, contradiction.

So
(u_{i_{j}},w_{i})\in N^{\prime}
for some
i\in[n]
. As
M
was maximal and
(u_{i_{j}},u_{j}^{\prime})\in M^{\prime}
, we get that
w_{i}
was matched in
M
, so
(u_{l},w_{i})\in M^{\prime}
for some
l\in[n]
. This implies that
u_{l}
and
w_{i}
both vote with
+1
in any case. Now, if
u_{l}
is unmatched in
N^{\prime}
, then there is only one
(-1,-1)
edge in the component, but both endpoints of the component vote with
+1
, contradiction. If
u_{l}
and his partner in
N^{\prime}
both vote with
+1
, then we have a
(+1,+1)
edge and we get a contradiction by a similar reasoning as before. Otherwise, the partner of
u_{l}
in
N^{\prime}
must strictly improve from
M^{\prime}
, hence
(u_{l},w_{m})\in N^{\prime}
for an agent
w_{m}
who was unmatched in
M
, so
(w_{m},w_{m}^{\prime})\in M^{\prime}
. As
w_{m}^{\prime}
is a leaf, we get that in this case this is the whole component. However, as both endpoints are only covered by
M^{\prime}
, we get that those two
+1
votes cancel the

Hence we conclude that
M^{\prime}
is weakly-popular.

\blacksquare

Now suppose for contradiction that there is a
(\frac{3}{4}+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for
max-weak-pm
. We may assume that
n
is larger than
\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
. Now, if every maximal matching of
G
has size at least
(1-\varepsilon)n
edges, then any weakly-popular matching in
I^{\prime}
has size at most
\frac{5}{2}n-(1-\varepsilon)n
by Claim
8
. Also, if there is a maximal matching of size at most
(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon)n>\frac{n}{2}+1
edges, then there is one with
\frac{n}{2}\leq k<(\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon)n

k<\frac{n}{2}
, then we increase it by one via an alternating path and get a larger maximal matching, as we may assume that
G
has a matching of size at least
\frac{n}{2}
, otherwise distinguishing the cases is easy) and by Claim

we have that there is a weakly popular matching of size at least
\frac{5}{2}n-k\geq(2-\varepsilon)n

Now, because
(2-\varepsilon)n\cdot(\frac{3}{4}+\varepsilon)=\frac{3}{2}n-\frac{3}{4}\varepsilon n+2\varepsilon n-\varepsilon^{2}n>(\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon)n=\frac{5}{2}n-(1-\varepsilon)n

6
Super-popular matchings

In this final section, we show that even deciding if a super-popular matching exists is NP-hard.

Theorem 6
.

Deciding if an instance
I
of
super-pm
admits a super-popular matching is NP-hard even with ordinary weak preferences, where only two agents have ties, and both have just a single tie of length two.

Proof.

We reduce from the NP-hard problem
(0,1,1,0)-pm
, which is the problem of deciding whether an instance
I
of the popular matching problem with strict preferences contains a popular matching
M
, that does not contain a given forbidden edge
(x,y)
and covers a given forced vertex
t\neq x,y
. By the reduction of Faenza et al.
[
8
]
, we may assume that
x
is a leaf, and
y
has only one other neighbor
z
(other than
x
), such that
y
and
z
are each others’ first ranked agents.

Let
I
be an instance of
(0,1,1,0)-pm
.
We create an instance
I^{\prime}
of
super-pm
as follows. We keep each vertex and edge from
I
. Furthermore, we add vertices
d_{x},x^{\prime},d_{t},t^{\prime}
along with edges
(x,d_{x}),(x^{\prime},d_{x}),(t,d_{t}),(t^{\prime},d_{t})
. Then, the graph is still clearly bipartite.

We let the preference of
d_{x}
be
x\succ x^{\prime}
and the preference of
d_{t}
be
t\sim t^{\prime}
, where
\sim
denotes a tie. Furthermore, we extend
t
’s preference list by adding
d_{t}
to the end and also
x
’s preference list from
y
to
d_{x}\sim y
.

Claim 10
.

If there is a super-popular matching in
I^{\prime}
, then there is a popular matching in
I
, which excludes
(x,y)
and covers
t
.

Proof:

Let
M^{\prime}
be a super-popular matching in
I^{\prime}
. Let
M
be the restriction of
M^{\prime}
to
I
.

We first show that
t
is covered in
M
. Suppose otherwise. Then, if
(t,d_{t})\in M^{\prime}
, we get that
M^{\prime}\setminus\{(t,d_{t})\}\cup\{(t^{\prime},d_{t})\}
dominates
M^{\prime}
, as
d_{t}
and
t^{\prime}
vote with
-1
and only
t
votes with
+1
. Similarly, if
(t,d_{t})\notin M^{\prime}
, then
M^{\prime}\setminus\{(t^{\prime},d_{t})\}\cup\{(t,d_{t})\}
dominates
M^{\prime}
, contradiction.

Next, we show that
(x,y)\notin M
. Suppose that
(x,y)\in M^{\prime}
. Then
(x^{\prime},d_{x})\in M^{\prime}
. Also,
M(z)\neq\emptyset
, because otherwise
M^{\prime}\setminus\{(x,y)\}\cup\{(y,z)\}
dominates
M^{\prime}
. However, the matching
M^{\prime}\setminus\{(x^{\prime},d_{x}),(x,y),M(z)\}\cup\{(x,d_{x}),(y,z)\}
dominates
M^{\prime}
, because
x,y,z
and
d_{x}
all vote with
-1
and only
x^{\prime}
and
z
’s partner in
M^{\prime}
vote with
+1
.

These observations also imply that
(t^{\prime},d_{t})\in M^{\prime}
and
(x,d_{x})\in M^{\prime}
.

Finally, we show that
M
is popular. Suppose that a matching
N
dominates
M
. Create
N^{\prime}
from
N
by adding
(t^{\prime},d_{t})
and
(x,d_{x})
, if
N(x)=\emptyset
and
(x^{\prime},d_{x})
otherwise. In both cases, the sum of the votes of
t^{\prime},d_{t},x^{\prime},d_{x}
is
0
. Also, no original agent may have a smaller vote, because apart from
x
, each of them has the same situation in
M^{\prime}
and
M
; and also
N^{\prime}
and
N
. For agent
x
, if
(x,d_{x})\in N^{\prime}
, then he votes with 0 in both
M
versus
N
in
I
and
M^{\prime}
versus
N^{\prime}
in
I^{\prime}
. Otherwise, his vote is
-1
in the former and also
-1
in the latter case. Hence, we get that the sum of votes in the comparison of
M^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}
is negative, contradicting
M^{\prime}
is super-popular.

\blacksquare

Claim 11
.

If there is a popular matching

in

Proof:

Let

be such a popular matching in

to

in

by adding the edges

Let

be the restriction of

to

. Each original agent other than

and

Consider agent

Consider agent

is matched in

, so

and
(x,y)\in N^{\prime}

was unmatched in

in both instances. If

and

It is easy to check that the sum of the votes of

\blacksquare

∎

7
Conclusion and future work

In this paper, we introduced a new natural notion of popularity in two-sided matching markets with ties. We have shown that with our definition, a weakly-popular matching always exists. We also provided an algorithm that finds a
\frac{3}{4}
-approximation to the maximum size
\gamma
-popular matching problem, which is also a
\frac{4}{5}
-approximation for the maximum size
\gamma
-min stable matching problem. We also proved that the ratio of
\frac{3}{4}
is tight assuming that either the strong-UGC or the Small Set Expansion Hypothesis holds.

For super popularity, we have shown that even the existence problem is NP-hard. However, our proof only works for two-sided ties, so with one-sided ties, the problem may become solvable, which we leave as an open question.
