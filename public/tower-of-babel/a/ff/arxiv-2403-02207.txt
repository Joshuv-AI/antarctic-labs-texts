Throughout the paper,

A map

(1)

That is,

Definition 1.1

For

[

The anti-linear operator

The study of conjugations has its roots in physics and is also essential in operator theory and operator algebras.
Conjugations and the extension theory for unbounded symmetric operators are closely related concepts. A survey article titled “Mathematical and physical aspects of complex symmetric operators” by Garcia, Prodan, and Putinar

3.
Factorization and range inclusion theorem for anti-linear operators

This section is devoted to application of Douglas theorem. Here we prove the range inclusion and factorization theorem for anti-linear operators and also, we prove the polar decomposition of anti-linear operators.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
S,T\in\mathcal{B}_{a}(\mathcal{H})
, and
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
. Then the fallowing are equivalent:

(1)

\text{ran}(T)\subseteq\text{ran}(S)
.

(2)

TT^{\#}\leq SS^{\#}
.

(3)

T=SR
, where
R=CDC
for some
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

Moreover, for such an operator
R
, we have
\ker R=\ker T
and
\overline{\text{ran}}(R)=(\ker S)^{\perp}.

Proof.

(
1
)
\implies
(
3
)
Let
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
. Set
A=TC
and
B=SC
. Then
A
and
B
are linear operators. Since
C\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}
, we have

\text{ran}(A)=\text{ran}(TC)=\text{ran}(T)\subseteq\text{ran}(S)=\text{ran}(SC)=\text{ran}(B).

Then by Douglas theorem, there is a
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

\displaystyle A=BD

\displaystyle\Rightarrow TC

\displaystyle=SCD

\displaystyle\Rightarrow T

\displaystyle=SCDC=SR,\text{ where }R=CDC.

Note that
R\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(
3
)
\implies
(
1
)
Using the fact that
C\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}
, we get
T\mathcal{H}=SR\mathcal{H}\subseteq S\mathcal{H}
and this implies
R(T)\subseteq R(S)
.

(
2
)
\implies
(
3
) Observe that
AA^{*}=TCCT^{\#}=TT^{\#}
and
BB^{*}=SS^{\#}
. Therefore,
TT^{\#}\leq SS^{\#}
implies
AA^{*}\leq BB^{*}
. By Douglas theorem,
A=BD
and hence
T=SR
.

(
3
)
\implies
(
2
) If
T=SR
, then

\begin{split}SS^{\#}-TT^{\#}&=SS^{\#}-SCDCCD^{*}CS^{\#}\\
&=SS^{\#}-SCDD^{*}CS^{\#}=SC\left(I-DD^{*}\right)CS^{\#}=SC\left(I-DD^{*}\right)(SC)^{*}.\end{split}

Thus
TT^{\#}\leq SS^{\#}
.

Since
\ker D=\ker A
and
\overline{\text{ran}}(D)=(\ker B)^{\perp}
, we have

\ker D=\ker(TC)=C\ker T\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\text{ran}}(D)=(\ker(SC))^{\perp}=(C\ker S)^{\perp}=C(\ker S)^{\perp}.

Thus

\ker R=\ker(CDC)=\ker(DC)=C\ker D=C\ker(TC)=\ker T,

and

\overline{\text{ran}}(R)=\overline{\text{ran}}(CDC)=(\ker(CD^{*}C))^{\perp}=(\ker(D^{*}C))^{\perp}=C\overline{\text{ran}}(D)=(\ker S)^{\perp}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
S,T\in\mathcal{B}_{a}(\mathcal{H})
such that
S^{\#}S=T^{\#}T
. Then there exists a partial isometry
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with initial space
\overline{\text{ran}}(T)
and final space
\overline{\text{ran}}(S)
such that
S=DT
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a conjugation. Then
S^{\#}S=T^{\#}T
implies
CS^{\#}SC=CT^{\#}TC
. Now set
A=CS^{*}
and
B=CT^{*}
. Then
AA^{*}=BB^{*}
. By Douglas Theorem,

A=BD^{*},

where
D
is a linear partial isometry with initial Space
\overline{\text{ran}}\left(B^{*}\right)
and final space
\overline{\text{ran}}\left(A^{*}\right)
.

This implies

\begin{split}CS^{*}&=CT^{\#}D^{*}\\
\quad S^{\#}&=T^{\#}D^{*}\\
S&=DT.\end{split}

Since
\overline{\text{ran}}\left(B^{*}\right)=\ker(B)^{\perp}=\ker\left(CT^{*}\right)^{\perp}=\ker\left(T^{*}\right)^{\perp}=\overline{\text{ran}}\left(T\right)
and
\overline{\text{ran}}\left(A^{*}\right)=\overline{\text{ran}}(S).
The operator
D
is linear partial isometry with initial space
\overline{\text{ran}}(T)
and final space
\overline{\text{ran}}(S)
.
∎

Next we give the polar decomposition of anti-linear operators which is also available in
[
20
]
but here we establish this as a consequence of Douglas theorem.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}_{a}(\mathcal{H})
and
C
be a conjugation. Then there exists an unique anti-linear partial isometry
J
such that
A=J|A|
such that
\ker J=\ker A
.

Proof.

Since
A
is an anti-linear operator and
C
is a conjugation, the operator
CA
is bounded linear. Then note that
|CA||CA|=(CA)^{*}(CA)
and
|A|=|CA|
. Set
\tilde{A}=(CA)^{*}
and
\tilde{B}=|A|
and then by applying Douglas theorem, we get

\begin{split}\tilde{A}&=\tilde{B}D\quad\text{which implies}\\
A&=CD^{*}|A|\\
A&=J|A|,\end{split}

where
J=CD^{*}
for some
D\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker D=\ker\tilde{A}
and
\overline{\text{ran}}D=(\ker\tilde{B})^{\perp}
. Therefore,

\ker J=\ker(CD^{*})=\ker D^{*}=(\text{ran}D)^{\perp}=\ker\tilde{B}=\ker|A|=\ker A.

Also,
J
is anti-linear and isometry on
\overline{\text{ran}}(|A|)=(\ker J)^{\perp}.

∎

Below we give a proof of polar decomposition of
C
-normal operators (cf. Theorem
2.6

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and
C
be a conjugation. Then
T
is
C
-normal operator if and only if there exists an partial anti-unitary operator
J
such that
T=CJ|T|
with
J|T|=|T|J
.

Proof.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be
C
-normal operator that is,
CT^{*}TC=TT^{*}
equivalently,
C|T|C=|T^{*}|
. Then by setting
A=CT^{*}C
and
B=|T^{*}|
, we have
AA^{*}=BB^{*}
. Now apply Douglas theorem, we get
A=BD^{*}
for some
D\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\overline{\text{ran}}(B^{*})\rightarrow\overline{\text{ran}}(A^{*})
such that
DB^{*}x=A^{*}x
, and
Dx=0
for all
x\in\overline{\text{ran}}(B^{*})^{\perp}=\ker B
. Thus we have

\begin{split}CT^{*}C&=|T^{*}|D^{*}\\
T&=CD|T^{*}|C\\
T&=CDC|T|.\end{split}

By defininig
J=DC
, we get
T=CJ|T|
. Note that

\begin{split}\ker J=\ker DC=C\ker D&=C\ker B=C\ker|T^{*}|\\
&=\ker(|T^{*}|C)=\ker(C|T|)=\ker|T|=\overline{\text{ran}}(|T|)^{\perp},\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\overline{\text{ran}}(J)=\overline{\text{ran}}(DC)=\overline{\text{ran}}(D)&=\overline{\text{ran}}(CTC)=C\overline{\text{ran}}(T)=C(\ker T^{*})^{\perp}\\
&=(\ker|T^{*}|C)^{\perp}=(\ker C|T|)^{\perp}=\overline{\text{ran}}(|T|)\end{split}

Since
\overline{\text{ran}}(B^{*})=\overline{\text{ran}}(|T^{*}|)=C\overline{\text{ran}}(|T|)
, and
\overline{\text{ran}}(A^{*})=\overline{\text{ran}}(|T|)
,
it is evident that
J=DC
is anti-linear unitary operator supported on
\overline{\text{ran}}(|T|)
.

Also,

|T^{*}|^{2}=TT^{*}=CJ|T||T|J^{\#}C=CJ|T|J^{\#}CCJ|T|J^{\#}C

This implies
|T^{*}|=CJ|T|J^{\#}C
and consequently,
J|T|=|T|J
.

The converse follows trivially.
∎

Lemma 4.6

Let
C
be a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
, and
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be such that
T=A+iB
, where
A
is
C
-symmetric and
B
is
C
-skew-symmetric. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

T
is
C
-normal.

(2)

\left\|Tx\right\|^{2}=\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
.

(3)

A^{*}B=B^{*}A
.

(4)

AB^{*}=BA^{*}
.

In this case,
T^{*}T=A^{*}A+B^{*}B
and
TT^{*}=AA^{*}+BB^{*}
with
(A^{*}A)(B^{*}B)=(B^{*}B)(A^{*}A)
, and
\max\{\|A\|^{2},\|B\|^{2}\}\leq\|T\|^{2}\leq\|A\|^{2}+\|B\|^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
C
be a conjugation, and
T=A+iB
, where
A
is
C
-symmetric and
B
is
C
-skew-symmetric (cf.
[
18
, Theorem 2.3]
). Therefore, for every
x\in\mathcal{H}

(5.1)

\begin{split}\left\|Tx\right\|^{2}=\left\|(A+iB)x\right\|^{2}=\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}-2\Re(i\langle Ax,Bx\rangle),\end{split}

and

\begin{split}\left\|T^{*}x\right\|^{2}=\left\|(A^{*}-iB^{*})x\right\|^{2}=\left\|A^{*}x\right\|^{2}+\left\|B^{*}x\right\|^{2}+2\Re(i\langle A^{*}x,B^{*}x\rangle).\end{split}

Since
T
is
C
-normal, we have
\left\|Tx\right\|=\left\|T^{*}Cx\right\|
, and this implies

(5.2)

\begin{split}\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}-2\Re(i\langle Ax,Bx\rangle)&=\left\|A^{*}Cx\right\|^{2}+\left\|B^{*}Cx\right\|^{2}+2\Re(i\langle A^{*}Cx,B^{*}Cx\rangle)\\
&=\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}-2\Re(i\langle CAx,CBx\rangle)\\
&=\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}-2\Re(i\langle Bx,Ax\rangle)\\
&=\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}+2\Re(i\langle Ax,Bx\rangle)\quad(\Re z=\Re\bar{z}).\end{split}

Thus for every
x\in\mathcal{H}
, we have
T
is
C
-normal if and only if
\left\|Tx\right\|=\left\|T^{*}Cx\right\|
if and only if
\Re(i\langle Ax,Bx\rangle)=0
if and only if
\left\|Tx\right\|^{2}=\left\|Ax\right\|^{2}+\left\|Bx\right\|^{2}
.

Now observe that
\Re(i\langle Ax,Bx\rangle)=0
if and only if
\langle Ax,Bx\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
for all
x\in\mathcal{H}
if and only if
(B^{*}A)^{*}=B^{*}A
, that is,
A^{*}B=B^{*}A
. Since
A
is
C
-symmetric and
B
is
C
-skew-symmetric, we have

A^{*}B=B^{*}A\iff CA^{*}B=CB^{*}A\iff ACB=-BCA\iff-AB^{*}C=-BA^{*}C\iff AB^{*}=BA^{*}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We characterize
C
-normal finite-dimensional truncated weighted shifts as an application of Cartesian decomposition of
C
-normal operators.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
C
be the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

(5.3)

\displaystyle C(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{n})=(\widebar{z_{n}},\widebar{z_{n-1}},\dots,\widebar{z_{2}},\widebar{z_{1}}).

Then

(5.4)

\displaystyle T=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{j}e_{j}\otimes e_{j+1}

is
C
-normal if and only if
|\lambda_{j}|=|\lambda_{n-j}|
for
j=1,2,\dots,n-1
.

Proof.

To prove this, we use the Cartesian decomposition of
C
-normal operators (See Theorem
5.1
). We write
T=A+iB
, where

\displaystyle A=\frac{T+CT^{*}C}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\lambda_{n-j}+\lambda_{j})e_{j}\otimes e_{j+1}

and

\displaystyle B=\frac{T-CT^{*}C}{2i}=\frac{1}{2i}\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{n-j})e_{j}\otimes e_{j+1}.

Also note that

AB^{*}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{(\lambda_{n-j}+\lambda_{j})(\bar{\lambda_{n-j}}-\bar{\lambda_{j}})}{4i}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}

and

BA^{*}=\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{n-j})(\bar{\lambda_{n-j}}+\bar{\lambda_{j}})}{4i}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}.

The condition
AB^{*}=BA^{*}
implies

\displaystyle(\lambda_{n-j}+\lambda_{j})(\bar{\lambda_{n-j}}-\bar{\lambda_{j}})=(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{n-j})(\bar{\lambda_{n-j}}+\bar{\lambda_{j}})

and this implies
|\lambda_{n-j}|=|\lambda_{j}|
for all
1\leq j\leq n-1
. Hence the conclusion follows.
∎

Example 5.3
.

Let
C
be conjugation defined by
Cf(t)=\overline{f(1-t)}
for all
t\in[0,1]
and consider the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
for all
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
. Then
M_{\varphi}\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}[0,1])
for
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
. Then

A=\frac{1}{2}M_{\psi_{1}}\quad\text{and}\quad B=\frac{1}{2i}M_{\psi_{2}},

where
\psi_{1}(t)=\varphi(t)+\varphi(1-t)
and
\psi_{2}(t)=\varphi(t)-\varphi(1-t)
. Therefore, by Theorem
5.1
,
M_{\varphi}
is
C
-normal if and only if
A^{*}B=B^{*}A
that is
\bar{\psi_{1}}\psi_{2}=\bar{\psi_{2}}\psi_{1}
which implies
|\varphi(1-t)|^{2}=|\varphi(t)|^{2}
.

C
-normal operators.

5.1.
Singular value inequalities for compact
C
-normal operators

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a compact operator. Then the singular
values of
T
, denoted by
s_{1}(T),s_{2}(T),\dots
are the eigenvalues of the positive operator
|T|=(T^{*}T)^{\frac{1}{2}}

enumerated as
s_{1}(T)\geq s_{2}(T)\geq\dots
, and repeated according to multiplicity. Note that
s_{j}(T)=s_{j}(T^{*})=s_{j}(|T|)
for
j=1,2,\dots
. It follows by Weyl’s monotonicity principle (cf.
[
1
, p. 63]
) that
if
S,T
are positive compact operators and
S\leq T
(that is,
T-S
is a positive operator), then
s_{j}(S)\leq s_{j}(T)
for
j=1,2,\dots
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
C
be a conjugation, and
T
be a compact
C
-normal operator, where
T=A+iB
be the Cartesian decomposition of
T
. Then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}s_{j}(|A|+|B|)\leq s_{j}(T)\leq s_{j}(|A|+|B|),

for
j=1,2,\dots
.

Proof.

Since
T
is a compact
C
-normal operator, it follows from
Theorem

5.1
that
T=A+iB
, where
A
is
C
-symmetric and
B
is
C
-skew-symmetric such that
A^{*}B=B^{*}A
and
|T|=\sqrt{|A|^{2}+|B|^{2}}
with
|A||B|=|B||A|
. Thus we have

(5.5)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(||A|+|B||)\leq\sqrt{|A|^{2}+|B|^{2}}=|T|\leq|A|+|B|.

Hence, by using Weyl’s monotonicity principle the conclusion follows.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The equality holds in the right hand side of the inequality in
Theorem

5.4
if either
A=0
, or
B=0
.

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a compact
C
-normal with the Cartesian decomposition
T=A+iB
. Then
2s_{j}(BA^{*})=2s_{j}(AB^{*})\leq s_{j}((T^{*}T)\oplus(TT^{*}))
for all
j=1,2,\dots
.

Proof.

It is well known that if
A,B
are compact operators, then

(5.6)

s_{j}(AB^{*}+BA^{*})\leq s_{j}((A^{*}A+B^{*}B)\oplus(AA^{*}+BB^{*})),

for
j=1,2,\dots
(cf.
[
2
]
).
Therefore, if
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is compact
C
-normal with the Cartesian decomposition
T=A+iB
, by using
Theorem

5.1
, we have
T^{*}T=A^{*}A+B^{*}B
and
TT^{*}=AA^{*}+BB^{*}
, and hence by
Eq.

5.6
, we have
2s_{j}(BA^{*})=2s_{j}(AB^{*})\leq s_{j}((T^{*}T)\oplus(TT^{*}))
for
j=1,2,\dots
.
∎

5.2.
Some inequalities for self-commutator

Let

be a
C
-normal operator with the Cartesian decomposition

as in

5.1
. Then the following are true:

(1)

\|T^{*}T-TT^{*}\|\leq 2\|A\|\min\{\|A-A^{*}\|,\|A+A^{*}\|\}+2\|B\|\min\{\|B-B^{*}\|,\|B+B^{*}\|\}

5

[
5
