On evolution of corner-like gSQG patches
Junekey Jeon, In-Jee Jeong

Abstract
. We study the evolution of corner-like patch solutions to the generalized SQG equations. Depending on the angle size and order of the velocity kernel, the corner instantaneously bents either downward or upward. In particular, we obtain the existence of strictly convex and smooth patch solutions which become immediately non-convex.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Patch solutions

We consider patch solutions to the generalized surface quasi-geostrophic (gSQG) equations, which are given by

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\theta+u\cdot\nabla\theta=0,&\\
u=-\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{-1+\frac{\alpha}{2}}\theta,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\theta(\,\cdot\,,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and
u(\,\cdot\,,t):\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Here,
0\leq\alpha<2
is a parameter, and the special cases
\alpha=0,1
correspond to the two-dimensional incompressible Euler and SQG equations, respectively. In this paper, by
a patch solution
to (
1.1
) we mean the indicator function

\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}
over a moving bounded open set
\Omega(t)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
whose boundary is given by a simple closed curve
\gamma(\,\cdot\,,t)\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying the following
contour dynamics equation
:

(1.2)

\partial_{t}\gamma(\xi,t)=\begin{cases}c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)\ln\left|\gamma(\xi,t)-\gamma(\eta,t)\right|\,d\eta+\lambda(\xi,t)\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\xi,t)&\textrm{if $\alpha=0$,}\\
c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\xi,t)-\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)}{\left|\gamma(\xi,t)-\gamma(\eta,t)\right|^{\alpha}}\,d\eta+\lambda(\xi,t)\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\xi,t)&\textrm{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
c_{\alpha}>0
is a constant and
\lambda
is any scalar function that can depend on
\gamma
. It is shown in
[
13
,
21
]
that as long as the curve
\gamma
solves (
1.2
), remains to be a simple closed curve, and retains certain regularity, then the function
(x,t)\mapsto\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(t)}(x)
defines a weak solution to (
1.1
). (The mentioned references only talk about the case
0<\alpha\leq 1
but the argument extends to the whole range as well.) Moreover, with a careful choice of the function
\lambda
, it is known that (
1.2
) admits a unique solution locally in time if the initial patch boundary is sufficiently regular.

In the specific case of
\alpha=0
(the Euler equations), taking
\lambda\equiv 0
gives that (
1.2
) is nothing but the characteristic equation restricted to the patch boundary, which is known to have a unique solution by Yudovich theory (
[
27
]
). The resulting patch solution is also known to exists globally in time and preserves its boundary regularity (
[
4
,
1
,
25
]
). When
0<\alpha<1
, the characteristic equation does not necessarily admit a unique solution, but (
1.2
) still does with
\lambda\equiv 0
(
[
13
]
). When
1\leq\alpha<2
, the velocity field
u
may not be even defined on the boundary, but if we select

(1.3)

\begin{split}\lambda(\xi,t)&=c_{\alpha}\frac{\xi+\pi}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)}{\left|\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)\right|^{2}}\cdot\partial_{\eta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)-\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\zeta,t)}{\left|\gamma(\eta,t)-\gamma(\zeta,t)\right|^{\alpha}}\,d\zeta\right)d\eta\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad-c_{\alpha}\int_{-\pi}^{\xi}\frac{\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)}{\left|\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)\right|^{2}}\cdot\partial_{\eta}\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,t)-\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\zeta,t)}{\left|\gamma(\eta,t)-\gamma(\zeta,t)\right|^{\alpha}}\,d\zeta\right)d\eta,\end{split}

then (
1.2
) still admits a unique solution (
[
14
,
3
,
7
,
13
]
). From now on, we implicitly assume that
\lambda
is chosen to be identically zero when
0\leq\alpha<1
, or as in (
1.3
) if
1\leq\alpha<2
.

1.2.
Main results

In the current work, we are interested in the evolution of patch solutions which locally look like a
corner
at the initial time. To be more precise, we assume that the initial patch
\Omega_{0}
has two length scales
0<\delta\ll M
such that

(1.4)

\begin{split}\Omega_{0}\cap\left\{x\in[-M,M]^{2}\colon\left|x\right|>\delta\right\}=\left\{x\in(0,\infty)^{2}\colon\beta x_{2}<x_{1}\right\}\cap\left\{x\in[-M,M]^{2}\colon\left|x\right|>\delta\right\}\end{split}

for some
0\leq\beta<\infty
, see Figure
1
. For simplicity, we assume the following on the area
|\Omega_{0}|
:

(1.5)

\begin{split}|\Omega_{0}|\leq 100M^{2}.\end{split}

The case
\beta=0
corresponds to corners with the right angle. We call the intersections of
\partial\Omega_{0}
with the line segments
\left\{x\in(0,M)^{2}\colon\left|x\right|>\delta,\ \beta x_{2}=x_{1}\right\}
and
(\delta,M)\times\left\{0\right\}
as
the upper edge
and
the lower edge
of
\Omega_{0}
, respectively.

It turns out that generically, during the evolution, parts of both of the edges of
\Omega_{0}
sufficiently close to the origin instantaneously “bents” either downward or upward. Our main result gives a precise criterion on
\alpha
and
\beta
that determines which one of the two is the case.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume that we are given a patch
\Omega_{0}
with smooth boundary satisfying (
1.4
)–(
1.5
).

Then, the boundary of the corresponding unique patch solution
\Omega(t)
becomes instantaneously convex downward (upward, resp.) in the region
A\cap\Omega(t)
if

(1.6)

\begin{split}F(\alpha,\beta):=\frac{\beta}{\beta^{2}+1}-\frac{\alpha}{(\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}}\int_{-\tan^{-1}\beta}^{\pi/2}\cos^{\alpha}\theta\,d\theta>0\qquad(\mbox{resp.}<0),\end{split}

where the annular region
A
is defined as

(1.7)

\begin{split}A\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:{C_{\alpha}^{-1}}|F(\alpha,\beta)|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta<|x|<C_{\alpha}|F(\alpha,\beta)|^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}M\right\}\end{split}

with some
C_{\alpha}>0
depending only on
\alpha
.

F(\alpha,\cdot)

with
\alpha=0,0.5,1,1.5
(from top to bottom).

Remark 1.2
.

As one can see from Figure
2
,
F(\alpha,\beta)
takes both positive and negative signs. Indeed, one can compute that

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\left((\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}F(\alpha,\beta)\right)=-\frac{\alpha-1}{(\beta^{2}+1)^{\alpha/2}}

holds. Based on this formula, we have the following:

•

In the Euler case
\alpha=0
, we have
F(0,\beta)>0
for all
\beta>0
, which means that for any angle of the corner in the range
\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
, the patch boundary near the corner always bents downward. This is consistent with the numerical simulations from
[
2
]
(their sign convention for the velocity is the opposite from ours).

•

In the SQG case
\alpha=1
and the more singular case
\alpha>1
, we have
F(\alpha,\beta)<0
for all
\beta\geq 0
, which means that for any angle of the corner in the range
\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
, the patch boundary near the corner always bents upward.

•

When
0<\alpha<1
(corresponding to the equations interpolating Euler and SQG),
F(\alpha,0)<0
and
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}(\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}F(\alpha,\beta)=\infty
, so
(\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}F(\alpha,\beta)
strictly monotonically increases from some negative value to
\infty
. In particular, there is a unique zero
\beta^{*}(\alpha)
of
F(\alpha,\,\cdot\,)
. Consequently, the patch boundary near the corner bents upward for large corner angle (
\beta<\beta^{*}(\alpha)
) while it bents downward for small corner angle (
\beta>\beta^{*}(\alpha)
). This is consistent with our own numerical simulations shown in Figure
3
.

As a consequence of the above, we obtain the existence of initially convex and smooth patches which become non-convex at a later time. To the best of our knowledge, such a result has not been proved even in the case of Euler.

Corollary 1.3
.

For any
0\leq\alpha<2
, there exists a strictly convex patch
\Omega_{0}
with smooth boundary such that the corresponding unique patch solution becomes non-convex immediately after the initial time.

1.3.
Background

To put the above results into context, let us review relevant works which have inspired our investigation. To begin with, it is a natural question to ask what happens to patches with a corner for the gSQG equations, since in contrast to the local well-posedness results in the smooth patch regime, not much is known without the smoothness assumption.

In the exceptional case of the Euler equations where
\alpha=0
, there is an existence and uniqueness result for patch solutions without a smoothness assumption (
[
27
]
). When the initial patch has a corner, numerical computations of
[
2
]
and formal asymptotic analysis of
[
11
]
suggest that it instantaneously evolves into a cusp, although rigorously proving it seems to be an open problem. Our main theorem can be applied
without
the local smoothing parameter
\delta
in the Euler case, and it shows that the corner patch immediately bents downward for any corner angle strictly between
0
and
\pi/2
, confirming the results of
[
2
]
. In particular, we deduce that the patch is not convex for any small enough
t
.

The main step in our proof is to compute the precise asymptotic behavior of the normal velocity field associated with the corner-type patch. Then, the change in convexity of the patch boundary can be derived from the curvature evolution equation in the very recent work of Kiselev–Luo
[
18
]
. A particularly interesting feature revealed by our computation is that in the case of
0\leq\alpha<1
, there is a special value of
\beta^{*}=\beta^{*}(\alpha)
such that the corner evolution is the “opposite” for
\beta<\beta^{*}
and
\beta>\beta^{*}
. In fact, it turns out that in the regime
M\to\infty
and
\delta\to 0
(that is, when the corner is infinitely long and perfectly sharp), the normal velocity on both of the edges becomes a constant if the corner angle is equal to
\beta^{*}
; see Remark
2.2
. This shows that, formally, infinitely long corners with angle
\beta^{*}
are steady solutions for the gSQG equations relative to the frame moving together with the corner. In particular, we have
\beta^{*}(0)=0
, which is consistent with the (conjectured) existence of relative equilibria of Euler patches containing
90^{\circ}
corners (
[
22
,
16
,
8
,
28
,
26
]
).

In general, numerical simulations on patch solutions have revealed very complicated dynamics of the boundary, involving repeated filamentation and fast curvature growth (
[
2
,
10
,
9
,
24
,
23
]
). Most notably, a recent work of Scott and Dritschel (
[
23
]
) shows the possibility of locally self-similar finite time singularity formation for gSQG patches. Very recently, the existence of self-similar solutions which exhibit similar behavior was proved by García–Gómez-Serrano
[
15
]
. When the domain is the upper half-plane, rapid small scale creation for the patch boundary including finite time blow-up for patch solutions have been established (
[
17
,
21
,
20
,
14
]
), thanks to the stability of the instability provided by the boundary. To the best of our knowledge, such a dramatic growth for sufficiently regular patches has not been proved to exist for domains without boundary like
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, but see
[
5
,
6
,
12
]
for some results in this direction. On the other hand, in the aforementioned work
[
18
]
, the authors observe a remarkable structure in the curvature equation, and in particular applied it to prove strong ill-posedness of the patch problem with the boundary regularity of
C^{2}
for 2D Euler. Namely, it is possible for a patch with a
C^{2}
boundary (which in particular implies finite curvature) to instantaneously have infinite curvature for
t\neq 0
.

2.
Proofs

2.1.
Curvature evolution equation

We denote the unit tangent and normal vectors as
{\bf T}
and
{\bf N}=-{\bf T}^{\perp}
, respectively, defined on the patch boundary. Then, we define the (signed) curvature by

(2.1)

\begin{split}\kappa=-\partial_{s}{\bf T}\cdot{\bf N}={\bf T}\cdot\partial_{s}{\bf N},\end{split}

where
\partial_{s}
is the differentiation with respect to the arc-length parameter
s
. Given a simple closed smooth curve
\gamma
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, it is well-known that the domain enclosed by
\gamma
is convex if and only if either
\kappa
is non-positive or non-negative everywhere on the curve, depending on the orientation of the parametrization. We will always assume counterclockwise orientation, and in that case the domain is convex if and only if
\kappa\geq 0
everywhere.

Now, consider a parametrized simple closed curve
\gamma(\,\cdot\,,t)\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
evolving over time, whose evolution is governed by a velocity field
v(\,\cdot\,,t)\colon\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
along the curve in the sense that

(2.2)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\gamma(\xi,t)=v(\xi,t)\end{split}

holds. Assuming that
v
is at least twice continuously differentiable with respect to the curve parameter
\xi
, Kiselev and Luo
[
19
]
have derived the evolution equation

(2.3)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\kappa=-2\kappa\partial_{s}v\cdot{\bf T}-\partial_{s}^{2}v\cdot{\bf N}\end{split}

for the curvature
\kappa
of the curve
\gamma
evaluated at a fixed
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
, where
\partial_{s}
can be computed using the formula
\partial_{s}=\frac{1}{\left|\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\xi,t)\right|}\partial_{\xi}
. Substituting the right-hand side of (
1.2
) to
v
, we obtain the evolution equation for the curvature in the contour dynamics equation.

2.2.
The main estimate and the proof of Theorem
1.1

We now state our main estimate, which implies Theorem
1.1
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Consider the gSQG patch solution
\Omega(\cdot)
with the initial data
\Omega_{0}
having smooth boundary and satisfying (
1.4
)–(
1.5
). Then, the corresponding velocity at
t=0
satisfies the estimate

(2.4)

\begin{split}\left|\partial_{s}^{2}v_{2}((a,0),0)-\frac{c_{\alpha}F(\alpha,\beta)}{a^{1+\alpha}}\right|\leq C_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{M^{1+\alpha}}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{a^{3+\alpha}}\right)\end{split}

for
2\delta\leq a\leq M/2
, where
C_{\alpha}>0
is a constant depending only on
\alpha
.

Proof.

Assume that we have chosen a parametrization
\gamma(\,\cdot\,,0)
of
\partial\Omega(0)
such that
\gamma(\pi,0)=(a,0)
for some
2\delta\leq a\leq M/2
. We will derive a sharp condition on
\alpha
and
\beta
which determines the sign of the right-hand side of (
2.3
) evaluated at
((a,0),0)
, when
\delta\ll a\ll M
.

Note that the vertical velocity at
((a,0),0)
is given as

v_{2}(\pi,0)=c_{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{(\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\pi,0)-\partial_{\xi}\gamma(\eta,0))\cdot(0,1)}{\left|(a,0)-\gamma(\eta,0)\right|^{\alpha}}\,d\eta=c_{\alpha}\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{\delta}}\frac{a-y_{1}}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{1+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2}.

We shall obtain an asymptotic formula for the second derivative of the above with respect to
a
. Define

\displaystyle A(a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathrm{p.v.}\int_{\Omega_{0}\cap[-M,M]^{2}}\frac{a-y_{1}}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{1+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2},

\displaystyle B(a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega_{0}\setminus[-M,M]^{2}}\frac{a-y_{1}}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{1+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2},\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle C(a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Omega_{0}\setminus\Omega_{0}^{\delta}}\frac{y_{1}-a}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{1+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2},

so that
v_{2}(\pi,0)=c_{\alpha}(A(a)+B(a)+C(a))
.

We estimate
B^{\prime\prime}(a)
first. From the expression

B^{\prime}(a)=\int_{\Omega_{0}\setminus[-M,M]^{2}}\frac{1}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{1+\alpha/2}}-\frac{(\alpha+2)(a-y_{1})^{2}}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{2+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2},

we easily derive

\displaystyle|B^{\prime}(a)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega_{0}\setminus[-M,M]^{2}}\frac{C_{\alpha}}{(a-y_{1})^{2+\alpha}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{(M-M/2)^{2+\alpha}}\left|\Omega_{0}\right|\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{M^{2+\alpha}}\left|\Omega_{0}\right|

for some constant
C_{\alpha}
that may change from side to side. In a similar manner, one can show

(2.5)

|B^{\prime\prime}(a)|\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{M^{3+\alpha}}\left|\Omega_{0}\right|.

In the same way, we obtain

(2.6)

|C^{\prime\prime}(a)|\leq\int_{B_{0}(\delta)}\frac{C_{\alpha}}{\left|a-y_{1}\right|^{3+\alpha}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{a^{3+\alpha}}\delta^{2}.

Next, we rewrite
A(a)
as

\displaystyle A(a)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{M/\max(\beta,1)}\int_{\beta y_{2}}^{M}\frac{a-y_{1}}{\left((a-y_{1})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{1+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{1}\,dy_{2}=\int_{0}^{M/\max(\beta,1)}f(M,y_{2})-f(\beta y_{2},y_{2})\,dy_{2}

where we define

\displaystyle f(t,y_{2})\coloneqq\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\ln\left((a-t)^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)&\textrm{if $\alpha=0$,}\\
\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{1}{\left((a-t)^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{\alpha/2}}&\textrm{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Differentiating twice by
a
, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\prime\prime}(a)=\int_{0}^{M/\max(\beta,1)}\frac{(\alpha+1)(a-M)^{2}-y_{2}^{2}}{\left((a-M)^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{2+\alpha/2}}+\frac{y_{2}^{2}-(\alpha+1)(a-\beta y_{2})^{2}}{\left((a-\beta y_{2})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{2+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{2}\eqqcolon A_{1}(a)+A_{2}(a).

Since
a\leq\frac{M}{2}
, we have

\left|\frac{(\alpha+1)(a-M)^{2}-y_{2}^{2}}{\left((a-M)^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{2+\alpha/2}}\right|\leq\frac{(\alpha+1)M^{2}}{(M/2)^{4+\alpha}},

thus
\left|A^{\prime\prime}(a)-A_{2}(a)\right|\leq C_{\alpha}M^{-1-\alpha}
.
We further divide
A_{2}(a)
into the sum of

\displaystyle A_{3}(a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{y_{2}^{2}-(\alpha+1)(a-\beta y_{2})^{2}}{\left((a-\beta y_{2})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{2+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{2}\quad\textrm{and}

\displaystyle A_{4}(a)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{M/\max(\beta,1)}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha+1)(a-\beta y_{2})^{2}-y_{2}^{2}}{\left((a-\beta y_{2})^{2}+y_{2}^{2}\right)^{2+\alpha/2}}\,dy_{2}.

For the integrand in
A_{4}(a)
, if
\beta\geq 1
, then for
y_{2}
in the domain of integration, we have
\beta y_{2}\geq M\geq 2a
,
so
\beta y_{2}-a\geq\frac{\beta}{2}y_{2}\geq\frac{y_{2}}{2}
, which implies

\left|A_{4}(a)\right|\leq\int_{M/\beta}^{\infty}\frac{4}{(\beta y_{2}-a)^{2+\alpha}}\,dy_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{M^{1+\alpha}}.

On the other hand, if
\beta<1
, then for
y_{2}
in the domain of integration, we have
\max(a,\beta y_{2})\leq y_{2}
, which implies

\left|A_{4}(a)\right|\leq\int_{M}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha+1}{y_{2}^{2+\alpha}}\,dy_{2}\leq\frac{C_{\alpha}}{M^{1+\alpha}}.

To evaluate
A_{3}(a)
, note that the denominator of the integrand of
A_{3}(a)
is equal to

\left((\beta^{2}+1)\left(y_{2}-\frac{\beta a}{\beta^{2}+1}\right)^{2}+\frac{a^{2}}{\beta^{2}+1}\right)^{2+\alpha/2},

so by applying the change of variable
y_{2}=\frac{a(\beta+\tan\theta)}{\beta^{2}+1}

and the identity
a-\beta y_{2}=\frac{a(1-\beta\tan\theta)}{\beta^{2}+1}
,
we can rewrite
A_{3}(a)
as

\displaystyle A_{3}(a)=\frac{1}{(\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}}\frac{1}{a^{1+\alpha}}\int_{-\tan^{-1}\beta}^{\pi/2}\left((\beta+\tan\theta)^{2}-(\alpha+1)(1-\beta\tan\theta)^{2}\right)\cos^{2+\alpha}\theta\,d\theta.

The integrand can be rewritten as the product of
\cos^{\alpha}\theta
and

\displaystyle\left((\beta+\tan\theta)^{2}-(\alpha+1)(1-\beta\tan\theta)^{2}\right)\cos^{2}\theta

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=\left(1-(\alpha+1)\beta^{2}\right)\sin^{2}\theta+2\beta(\alpha+2)\sin\theta\cos\theta+(\beta^{2}-(\alpha+1))\cos^{2}\theta

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad=(\alpha+2)\left(\frac{\beta^{2}-1}{2}\cos 2\theta+\beta\sin 2\theta\right)-\frac{\alpha(\beta^{2}+1)}{2}.

By integration by parts, one can obtain the formulae

(\alpha+2)\int_{s}^{t}\cos 2\theta\cos^{\alpha}\theta\,d\theta-\alpha\int_{s}^{t}\cos^{\alpha}\theta\,d\theta=\left.2\sin\theta\cos^{\alpha+1}\theta\right|_{\theta=s}^{t}=\left.\frac{2\tan\theta}{(1+\tan^{2}\theta)^{1+\alpha/2}}\right|_{\theta=s}^{t}

and

\int_{s}^{t}\sin 2\theta\cos^{\alpha}\theta\,d\theta=2\int_{\cos t}^{\cos s}u^{\alpha+1}\,du=\left.\frac{2u^{\alpha+2}}{\alpha+2}\right|_{u=\cos t}^{\cos s}=\left.\frac{2}{\alpha+2}\frac{1}{(1+\tan^{2}\theta)^{1+\alpha/2}}\right|_{\theta=t}^{s},

so applying these we get

\displaystyle A_{3}(a)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}}\frac{1}{a^{1+\alpha}}\left(\left.\frac{(\beta^{2}-1)u}{(1+u^{2})^{1+\alpha/2}}\right|_{u=-\beta}^{\infty}-\alpha\int_{-\tan^{-1}\beta}^{\pi/2}\cos^{\alpha}\theta\,d\theta+\left.\frac{2\beta}{(1+u^{2})^{1+\alpha/2}}\right|_{u=-\beta}^{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta^{2}+1}-\frac{\alpha}{(\beta^{2}+1)^{1-\alpha/2}}\int_{-\tan^{-1}\beta}^{\pi/2}\cos^{\alpha}\theta\,d\theta\right)\frac{1}{a^{1+\alpha}}.

Consequently, we arrive at the estimate (
2.4
).
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We assume the case
F(\alpha,\beta)>0
; the other case is almost identical. From (
2.3
), we have

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(e^{2\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}v\cdot{\bf T}}\kappa\right)=-e^{2\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{s}v\cdot{\bf T}}\partial_{s}^{2}v\cdot{\bf N}.\end{split}

Note that the estimate (
2.4
) provides a uniform positive lower bound
b\leq\partial_{s}^{2}v_{2}((a,0),0)
for all
(a,0)\in A
, where
A
is defined as in (
1.7
) with an appropriately modified
C_{\alpha}
. At
t=0
and
x=(a,0)\in A
, we see that
\partial_{s}^{2}v\cdot{\bf N}=-\partial_{s}^{2}v_{0,2}=-\partial_{a}^{2}v_{0,2}\leq-b
. In particular, it follows that
\kappa(t,(a,0))
is bounded below by a positive constant for all
(a,0)\in A
and for all sufficiently small
t
, using the Taylor expansion of
\kappa
in time. By symmetry,
\partial_{s}^{2}v\cdot{\bf N}
must admit a uniform positive lower bound at
t=0
on the intersection of
A
and the upper edge, thus
\kappa
is bounded above by a negative constant there for all sufficiently small
t
. Therefore, both of the edges must bent downward.
∎

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

We only need to ensure the existence of a single point where
\kappa
becomes negative; the idea is to simply regularize
\Omega_{0}
in a way that its boundary is
C^{\infty}
-smooth everywhere and has strictly positive curvature except at a single point. To this end, we consider the following one-parameter family of modifications of
\Omega_{0}
, denoted as
\Omega_{0}^{\epsilon}
; we require that

•

the curvature of
\partial\Omega_{0}^{\epsilon}
is uniformly bounded in
\epsilon
, and is strictly positive except at a single fixed point
(a,0)\in\partial\Omega_{0}^{\epsilon}\cap A
where the curvature is zero,

•

\partial\Omega_{0}^{\epsilon}
is tangent to
\partial\Omega_{0}
at
(a,0)
, and

•

|\Omega_{0}^{\epsilon}\triangle\Omega_{0}|<\epsilon
.

It can be shown that, in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
,

\begin{split}\partial_{a}^{2}v_{0,2}^{\epsilon}(a,0)\to\partial_{a}^{2}v_{0,2}(a,0).\end{split}

In particular, for all
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, we can arrange that

\begin{split}\partial_{a}^{2}v_{0,2}^{\epsilon}(a,0)<0,\end{split}

where
v_{0,2}^{\epsilon}

\Omega_{0}^{\epsilon}
. Then, we have that for all sufficiently small

\partial\Omega^{\epsilon}(t)

∎

Remark 2.2
.

By performing a computation similar to what is shown in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, one can show that

\left|\partial_{s}v((a,0),0)+\frac{c_{\alpha}F(\alpha,\beta)}{\alpha a^{\alpha}}\right|\leq C_{\alpha}\left(\frac{1}{M^{\alpha}}+\frac{\delta^{2}}{a^{2+\alpha}}\right)

holds for all
2\delta\leq a\leq M/2
(with a possibly different
C_{\alpha}>0
). Therefore, when
0<\alpha<1
and
\beta=\beta^{*}(\alpha)
so that
F(\alpha,\beta)=0
, we obtain that
\partial_{s}v((\,\cdot\,,0),0)\to 0
locally uniformly on
(0,\infty)
when
M\to\infty
and
\delta\to 0
. This means that when the corner becomes infinitely long and perfectly sharp, then the normal velocity on both of the edges of
\Omega_{0}
must become a constant, which formally shows that the infinitely long and perfectly sharp corner with the angle
\beta^{*}(\alpha)
is a steady solution to the gSQG equations with the parameter
\alpha
, relative to the frame moving together with the corner.
