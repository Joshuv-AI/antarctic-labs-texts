Mikael De La Salle

Abstract
. We show that a partition of the unity (or POVM) on a Hilbert space that is almost orthogonal is close to an orthogonal POVM in the same von Neumann algebra. This generalizes to infinite dimension previous results in matrix algebras by Kempe-Vidick and Ji-Natarajan-Vidick-Wright-Yuen. Quantitatively, our result are also finer, as we obtain a linear dependance, which is optimal. We also generalize to infinite dimension a duality result between POVMs and minimal majorants of finite subsets in the predual of a von Neumann algebra.



1.
Orthonormalization of partitions of unity in infinite dimensional Hilbert space

Stability is a term coined in
[
11
]
to describe a situation when mathematical objects that almost satisfy certain properties are close to objects exactly satisfying these properties. It has been recently much studied for groups, mainly motivated by the question of whether every group is hyperlinear/sofic. The same phenomena but for quantum strategies for two-player non-local games are also central in the recent work by Ji-Natarajan-Vidick-Wright-Yuen
[
4
,
6
]
. The goal of this note is to explore a small portion of
[
4
]
and discuss its possible generalizations to infinite dimension and some consequence in terms of stability.

The objects that we study in this note are finite families
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
of positive operators on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
which sum to the identity:
t_{1}+\dots+t_{n}=1_{\mathcal{H}}
. They are called
partitions of unity
by operator algebraists, and
Positive Operator Valued Measures
(POVM) by quantum information theorists. We will use POVM here as it is shorter, and the integer
n
is called the number of outputs. And we talk about Projection Valued Measures (PVM) if in addition all the
t_{i}
’s are projections (
t_{i}^{2}=t_{i}
). The main result of this note is the following. The question whether a form of this result holds in infinite dimension answers a question asked by Henry Yuen (private communication). This result is used in the subsequent work
[
5
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
be a POVM on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, let
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
be a unit vector and
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
satisfying
\sum_{i}\|a_{i}\xi\|^{2}>1-\varepsilon
.

There exists an orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{H}_{n}
such that

(1)

if
\xi_{i}
denotes the orthogonal projection on
\mathcal{H}_{i}
, then
\sum_{i}\|a_{i}\xi-\xi_{i}\|^{2}<9\varepsilon
,

(2)

every operator
b\in B(\mathcal{H})
which commutes with each
a_{i}
preserves each
\mathcal{H}_{i}
.

In other words, the condition
\sum_{i}\|a_{i}\xi\|^{2}>1-\varepsilon
implies that: (
1
)
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
is
9\varepsilon
-close on
\xi
to a PVM
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
(
2
) which preserves the symmetries of the original POVM.

This theorem generalizes to infinite dimension and slightly strengthens a result from
[
4
]
(see also
[
8
, Lemma 19]
), which proves a form of this theorem with finite dimensional Hilbert spaces. More precisely, we can rephrase
[
4
, Theorem 5.2]
as follows: if
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
is a tensor product of
finite dimensional
Hilbert spaces and the
a_{i}
are of the form
A_{i}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{B}
, then the theorem holds, but with
9\varepsilon
in (
1
) (which is essentially optimal, see Remark
1.3
) replaced by
100\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{4}}
. Formally, the conclusion (
2
) is replaced by a slightly weaker conclusion, as it only requires that the orthogonal projections on each factor in the orthogonal decomposition are of the same tensor product form
P_{i}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{B}
, or equivalently that the decomposition is of the form

\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\oplus\dots\oplus(\mathcal{H}_{n}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}).

But the main contribution here is to deal with infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces.

If one only requires the conclusion (
1
), Theorem
1.1
becomes a small exercise in euclidean geometry, and the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
is irrelevant as everything happens in the space spanned by the
n
vectors
a_{i}\xi
. It is the conclusion (
2
) that makes the statement dependant on the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
and of the structure of the algebra of operators commuting with
a_{i}
. So, although it is stated in Hilbert-space vocabulary, Theorem
1.1
is a result about von Neumann algebras and states. This paper might be read by non-experts in von Neumann algebras, so we will try to recall basic definitions in section
2
, and to give complete proofs or precise references for the statements we need. We refer to standard textbooks such as
[
12
]
for more background.

The following is an equivalent reformulation of Theorem
1.1
in von Neumann algebraic language.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a von Neumann algebra with a normal state
\varphi
, and let
(a_{i})
be a POVM in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\varphi(\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2})>1-\varepsilon
.

Then there is a PVM
(p_{i})\subset\mathcal{M}
(made of projections) such that
\varphi(\sum_{i}|a_{i}-p_{i}|^{2})<9\varepsilon
.

Remark 1.3
.

Conversely, if there is a PVM
(p_{i})
such that
\sum_{i}\varphi(|a_{i}-p_{i}|^{2})\leq\delta
, then by the triangle inequality

\varphi(\sum\nolimits_{i}a_{i}^{2})\geq\bigg(1-\sqrt{\varphi(\sum\nolimits_{i}|a_{i}-p_{i}|^{2})}\bigg)^{2}\geq 1-2\sqrt{\delta}.

This could have suggested that the upper bound
9\varepsilon
in Theorem
1.2
is not optimal and can be replaced by
O(\varepsilon^{2})
. This is not the case, and the
9\varepsilon
cannot be replaced by anything smaller than
\varepsilon
, as the following simple example illustrates.

Consider
\mathcal{M}=\ell_{\infty}^{2}
(
\mathbf{C}^{2}
with the
\ell_{\infty}
norm),
a_{1}=(1,\frac{1}{2})
and
a_{2}=(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and
\varphi
is the state
\varphi(x,y)=(1-c)x+cy
, then we have

\varphi(a_{1}^{2}+a_{2}^{2})=1-\frac{1}{2}c,

and for every PVM
(p_{1},p_{2})
we have

\varphi(|p_{1}-a_{1}|^{2})+\varphi(|p_{2}-a_{2}|^{2})\geq\frac{1}{2}c.

Before we prove the main Theorem
1.2
, let us state one consequence, which says that almost commuting PVMs are close to commuting PVMs.

Let us denote, for an element
a
in a von Neumann algebra and a normal state
\varphi
,
\|a\|_{\varphi}=\sqrt{\varphi(a^{*}a)}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(p_{i})_{i}
and
(q_{j})_{j}
be two PVMs in a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
and
\varphi
be a normal state on
\mathcal{M}
. If
\sum_{i,j}\|p_{i}q_{j}-q_{j}p_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}<\varepsilon
, then there is another PVM
(p_{i}^{\prime})_{i}
in
\mathcal{M}
such that
[p^{\prime}_{i},q_{j}]=0
for every
i,j
and

\sum_{i}\|p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}<10\varepsilon.

By Fourier transform (Pontryagin duality), PVM’s with
n
outputs are in one-to-one correspondence with unitaries
u
of order
n
(
u^{n}=1
): to
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
corresponds
u=\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{\frac{2ik\pi}{n}}p_{k}
. The inverse maps
u
to
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
where
p_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k}e^{-\frac{2ijk\pi}{n}}u^{k}
. Therefore, the previous theorem is formally equivalent to the following. It is a new form of a statement asserting that almost commuting unitaries are close to unitaries, that does not seem to be comparable with existing results, even when
\varphi
is a trace. Comparing with the probabilistic results in
[
2
]
for permutation actions (that Michael Chapman kindly pointed out to me) raises the question whether there is a form of Corollary
1.5
that is valid for arbitrary amenable groups and not just
\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}/m\mathbf{Z}
.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
(\mathcal{M},\varphi)
be a von Neumann algebra with a normal state, and
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
be unitaries of finite order
n,m
.

If
\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|u^{i}v^{j}-v^{j}u^{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}<\varepsilon
, then there is a unitary
v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
that commutes with
u
and satisfies

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\|v^{i}-{v^{\prime}}^{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}<10\varepsilon.

The proof of the main result is not very involved and follows the same general strategy as in
[
4
]
, but it requires a bit of familiarity with von Neumann algebras and some adaptations to obtain the optimal order in the constants. We present the necessary background in Section
2
, and then prove the Theorem by decomposing it into three different cases. We deduce Theorem
1.4
in Section
6
. Finally, Section
7
generalizes to infinite dimensional von Neumann algebras a semidefinite program considered in
[
4
]
.

2.
Facts on von Neumann algebras

A von Neumann algebra is a self-adjoint subalgebra of the algebra
B(\mathcal{H})
of bounded operators on a complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
that is equal to its bicommutant. Here the
commutant
of a subset
F\subset B(\mathcal{H})
is the algebra
F^{\prime}
of operators that commute with all elements of
F
, and its
bicommutant
is the commutant of its commutant. The von Neumann bicommutant theorem
[
12
, Theorem II.3.9]
is a fundamental result of the theory, which asserts that the bicommutant of a self-adjoint subset
F\subset B(\mathcal{H})
coincides with the weak-* closure of the self-adjoint unital algebra generated by
F
, where we see
B(\mathcal{H})
as the dual of the trace-class operators on
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, a von Neumann algebra is a dual space. Another fundamental theorem
[
12
, Corollary III.3.9]
asserts that a von Neumann algebra admits a unique predual. This allows to talk about
the
weak-* topology on
\mathcal{M}
; it coincides with the ultraweak operator topology, the smallest topology making continuous all linear maps of the form
x\mapsto\sum_{k}\langle x\xi_{k},\eta_{k}\rangle
for sequences
\xi_{k},\eta_{k}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying
\sum_{k}\|\xi_{k}\|\|\eta_{k}\|<\infty
.

A
normal state
on a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}\subset B(\mathcal{H})
is a linear map
\varphi\colon\mathcal{M}\to\mathbf{C}
that is positive (
\varphi(x^{*}x)\geq 0
for every
x\in\mathcal{M}
), normalized by
\varphi(1)=1
and weak-* continuous. The typical example of a state is a vector state
x\mapsto\langle a\xi,\xi\rangle
for a unit vector
\xi\in\mathcal{H}
. The Gelfand-Naimark-Segal (GNS) construction asserts that (up to changing the Hilbert space), every normal state can be realized as a vector state.

We will prove the theorem by a reduction to two main cases (finite and type III). To state the reduction we need to recall some basic definitions on the type of a von Neumann algebra (see
[
12
, Chapter V]
). Throughout this note, by projection we always mean self-adjoint projection:
p=p^{*}=p^{2}
. Given a von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
, we say that

•

\mathcal{M}
is finite if for every
u\in\mathcal{M}
,
u^{*}u=1
implies
uu^{*}=1
. A projection
p\in\mathcal{M}
is finite if the von Neumann algebra
p\mathcal{M}p
is finite.

•

\mathcal{M}
is of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
if it is finite and if
0
is the only projection
p\in\mathcal{M}
such that
p\mathcal{M}p
is commutative.

•

\mathcal{M}
is semi-finite if every nonzero projection
p\in\mathcal{M}
majorizes a non-zero finite projection.

•

\mathcal{M}
has type III if it does not contain any nonzero finite projection.

We know from general theory
[
12
, Theorem V.1.19]
that every von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{M}
can be written as a direct sum of a semifinite and a type III von Neumann algebra. So it is enough to separately prove Theorem
1.2
when
\mathcal{M}
is semi-finite and when
\mathcal{M}
has type III. The semi-finite case can easily be reduced to the finite case, which is the most interesting one.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
when
\mathcal{M}
is finite

Let
\mathcal{M},\varphi,(a_{i})
be as in Theorem
1.2
, with
\mathcal{M}
finite. By
[
12
, Theorem V.2.6]
, the finiteness assumption is equivalent to the existence of a
normal center-valued trace
, that is a normal conditional expectation
E\colon\mathcal{M}\to Z(\mathcal{M})
onto the center of
\mathcal{M}
such that
E(ab)=E(ba)
for every
a,b\in\mathcal{M}
.

The first lemma contains all the difficulty in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There are projections
q_{i}
commuting with
a_{i}
such that

(3.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}E(q_{i})=1,

(3.2)

\varphi\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}a_{i}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Before we prove the Lemma, let us observe that it is not possible to replace (
3.1
) by the stronger condition
\sum_{i}q_{i}=1
. Indeed, if
\mathcal{M}=M_{2}(\mathbf{C})
and if the
a_{i}^{\prime}s
do not have any common eigenvector, then the only families
(q_{i})
of projections commuting with
a_{i}
satisfying
\sum_{i}q_{i}=1
are when one of the
q_{i}
’s is the identity and the other are
0
, and so (
3.2
) would become
\max_{i}\varphi(a_{i})\geq 1-\varepsilon
. A concrete example is given by
n=3
,
\varphi
the normalized trace on
M_{2}(\mathbf{C})
and

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+6\delta}\begin{pmatrix}1+4\delta&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle a_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+6\delta}\begin{pmatrix}\delta&\sqrt{3}\delta\\
\sqrt{3}\delta&1+3\delta\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle a_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+6\delta}\begin{pmatrix}\delta&-\sqrt{3}\delta\\
-\sqrt{3}\delta&3\delta\end{pmatrix}.

This example satisfies
\varphi(\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2})\geq 1-5\delta+o(\delta)
, but
\max_{i}\varphi(a_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the subset
C\subset\mathcal{M}^{n}

C=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n}\mid\forall i,0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,x_{i}a_{i}=a_{i}x_{i},\sum\nolimits_{i}E(x_{i})=1\}.

C
is a convex subset in the unit ball of
\mathcal{M}^{n}
, and contains
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
. It is clearly weak-* closed, and therefore compact as the unit ball of
\mathcal{M}^{n}
is weak-* compact. By the Krein-Milman theorem,
C
is the closure of the convex hull of its extreme points, and in particular the continuous affine map

f\colon(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in C\mapsto\varphi(\sum\nolimits_{i}x_{i}a_{i})

attains its maximum (which is
\geq f(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\geq 1-\varepsilon
) at an extreme point.

So all we have to do is to show that

(3.3)

if
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is an extreme point of
C
, then each
x_{i}
is a projection.

We know from general theory
[
12
, Theorem V.1.19 and V.1.27]
that there is a sequence
(z_{d})_{d\in\mathbf{N}}
of orthogonal projections in
Z(\mathcal{M})
such that
z_{d}\mathcal{M}
is isomorphic to
M_{d}(\mathbf{C})\otimes z_{d}Z(\mathcal{M})
and
(1-\sum_{d}z_{d})\mathcal{M}
is of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
. If
x_{i}
was not a projection, then either there is
d\in\mathbf{N}
such that
z_{d}x_{i}
is not a projection, or
(1-\sum_{d}z_{d})x_{i}
is not a projection. So we are reduced to showing (
3.3
) when
\mathcal{M}=M_{d}(\mathbf{C})\otimes Z
for an abelian von Neumann algebra
Z
, or when
\mathcal{M}
is of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
.

The latter case is easier, as the extremality of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
in
C
in particular implies that, for each
i
,
x_{i}
is extremal inside

C_{i}:=\{y_{i}\in\mathcal{M}\mid 0\leq y_{i}\leq 1,y_{i}a_{i}=a_{i}y_{i},E(y_{i})=E(x_{i})\},

and this weaker condition already implies that
x_{i}
is a projection. Indeed, if
x_{i}
was not a projection, there would exist
\delta>0
such that the spectral projection
p:=\chi_{[\delta,1-\delta]}(x_{i})
is nonzero and commutes with
a_{i}
. By the definition of
\mathcal{M}
being of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
, we know that
p\mathcal{M}p
is not abelian. In particular, there is a self-adjoint element
b\in p\mathcal{M}p
which commutes with
pa_{i}
but does not belong to
pZ(\mathcal{M})
(we can take
b=pa_{i}
if
pa_{i}\notin pZ(\mathcal{M})
and an arbitrary selfadjoint element of
p\mathcal{M}p\setminus pZ(\mathcal{M})
otherwise). We can moreover assume that
0\leq b\leq p
. Using that the center valued trace
E:\mathcal{M}\to Z(\mathcal{M})
is (completely) positive, we obtain that
0\leq E(b)\leq E(p)
, and in particular there is
z\in Z(\mathcal{M})
such that
E(b)=zE(p)
and
0\leq z\leq 1
. Then
b^{\prime}:=b-zp
is a nonzero selfadjoint element of
(\{pa_{i}\}^{\prime}\cap p\mathcal{M}p)\setminus pZ(\mathcal{M})
, which moreover satisfies
E(b^{\prime})=E(b)-zE(p)=0
. It has norm
\leq 1
. So we can write
x_{i}
as the midpoint between
x_{i}+\delta b^{\prime}
and
x_{i}-\delta b^{\prime}
, which both belong to
C_{i}
. This contradicts the extremality of
x_{i}
in
\mathcal{M}
and proves (
3.3
) when
\mathcal{M}
is of type
\mathrm{II}_{1}
.

In the first case, we can write equivalently
\mathcal{M}=M_{d}(\mathbf{C})\otimes L_{\infty}(\Omega,\mu)
for a measure space
(\Omega,\mu)

[
12
, Theorem
III.1.18]
. We first consider the simpler situation when
\mathcal{M}=M_{d}(\mathbf{C})
. Then (
3.3
) becomes that any
extreme point in
\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in M_{d}(\mathbf{C})\mid 0\leq x_{i}\leq 1,[x_{i},a_{i}]=0,\Tr(\sum_{i}x_{i})=d\}
is made of projections. Assume
for a contradiction that this is not the case, and that

(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is an extreme point not entirely made of
projections. If, for some
i
,
x_{i}
has at least two nonzero
eigenvalues different from
0
and
1
(counting with multiplicities),
then we can do as in the
\mathrm{II}_{1}
case, choose orthogonal rank one
projections
p_{1},p_{2}
corresponding to these eigenvalues of
x_{i}
and
commuting with
a_{i}
, and for
\delta>0
small enough the
decomposition

x_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\left((x_{i}+\delta p_{1}-\delta p_{2})+(x_{i}-\delta p_{1}+\delta p_{2})\right)

will contradict the
extremality. Otherwise, using that
\sum_{k}\Tr(x_{k})
is an integer,
there are at least two indices
i\neq j
such that
x_{i}
and
x_{j}

both have exactly one eigenvalue not in
\{0,1\}
, counting
multiplicities. In that case, if
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
are the corresponding
rank one projections, they necessarily commute with
a_{i}
and
a_{j}

respectively, and we can define for
\delta\in[-1,1]

x_{k}(\delta)=\begin{cases}x_{i}+\delta p_{i}&\textrm{if }k=i\\
x_{j}-\delta p_{j}&\textrm{if }k=j\\
x_{k}&\textrm{if }k\notin{i,j}.\end{cases}.

For
|\delta|
small enough
(x_{1}(\delta),\dots,x_{n}(\delta))
belongs to
C
, and the expression
x_{k}=\frac{1}{2}(x_{k}(\delta)+x_{k}(-\delta))
also contradicts the extremality of
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
.

To summarize, when
\mathcal{M}=M_{d}(\mathbf{C})
we have constructed, for every
x\in C
that is not made of projections, two distinct points
x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}\in C
such that
x=\frac{1}{2}(x^{\prime}+x^{\prime\prime})
. An inspection of the proof reveals that the map
x\mapsto(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
can be made Borel-measurable. As a consequence, the proof applies also to
\mathcal{M}=M_{d}(\mathbf{C})\otimes L_{\infty}(\Omega,\mu)
.
∎

We shall use the following elementary fact about finite von Neumann algebras.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a finite von Neumann algebra with center-valued trace
E
, and
x\in\mathcal{M}
. If
p
and
q\in\mathcal{M}
are projections such that
E(p)=E(q)
and

xp=qx=x,

then we can decompose
x=u|x|
where
u^{*}u=p
and
uu^{*}=q
.

Proof.

Denote by
p_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
the left support of
x
and
q_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
the right support of
x
(that is, if
\mathcal{M}\subset B(\mathcal{H})
,
p_{0}
and
q_{0}
are the smallest projection in
B(\mathcal{H})
such that
xp_{0}=x
and
q_{0}x=x
respectively). Write
x=u_{0}|x|
be the usual polar decomposition of
x
, where
|x|=(x^{*}x)^{1/2}
, and
u_{0}\in\mathcal{M}
is a partial isometry with
u_{0}^{*}u_{0}=p_{0}
and
u_{0}^{*}u_{0}=q_{0}
(see
[
1
, Proposition 2.2.4]
). By definition, we have
p_{0}\leq p
and
q_{0}\leq q
. Moreover,

E(p-p_{0})=E(p)-E(u_{0}^{*}u_{0})=E(q)-E(u_{0}u_{0}^{*})=E(q-q_{0}).

By
[
1
, Proposition 9.1.8]
, we have that
p-p_{0}\sim q-q_{0}
. That is, there is a partial isometry
v
such that
v^{*}v=p-p_{0}
and
vv^{*}=q-q_{0}
. The lemma holds with
u=v+u_{0}
.
∎

We can now prove the main result. With Lemma
3.1
and Lemma
3.2
in hand, the proof is very close to
[
4
]
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
when
\mathcal{M}
is finite.

Let
q_{i}
be given by Lemma
3.1
. Consider the matrix
x=\sum_{i}e_{i,1}\otimes q_{i}a_{i}^{1/2}
, that we see in
M_{n}(\mathcal{M})
. What is important for us is that
M_{n}(\mathcal{M})
is a finite von Neumann algebra. Specifically, its center is
1_{n}\otimes Z(\mathcal{M})
and the corresponding central-valued trace is

E_{n}:(a_{i,j})\mapsto 1_{n}\otimes(\frac{1}{n}\sum\nolimits_{i}E(a_{i,i})).

Let
p=\sum_{i}e_{i,i}\otimes q_{i}
and
q=e_{1,1}\otimes 1
. Then
E_{n}(p)=1_{n}\otimes\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}E(q_{i})=\frac{1}{n}=E_{n}(q)
, so by Lemma
3.2
we can write
x=u|x|
with
uu^{*}=\sum_{i}e_{i,i}\otimes q_{i}
and
u^{*}u=e_{1,1}\otimes 1
. In the following, we identify
\mathcal{M}
with
\{e_{1,1}\otimes a\mid a\in\mathcal{M}\}
. If
t_{i}=e_{i,i}\otimes q_{i}
, then
u^{*}t_{i}u
is a projection
p_{i}
in
\mathcal{M}
(formally it is of the form
e_{1,1}\otimes p_{i}
, but we decided to identify this with
p_{i}
) for projections
p_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
which sum to
1
. Moreover, we have

|x|p_{i}|x|=|x|u^{*}t_{i}u|x|=x^{*}t_{i}x=q_{i}a_{i}.

Let us denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\varphi}
the norm on
\mathcal{M}^{n}
given by

\|(B_{i})_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}=\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(B_{i}^{*}B_{i}).

By the triangle inequality, decomposing
a_{i}-p_{i}=a_{i}-q_{i}a_{i}+(|x|-1)p_{i}|x|+p_{i}(|x|-1)
, we obtain

\|(a_{i}-p_{i})_{i}\|_{\varphi}\leq\|(a_{i}-q_{i}a_{i})_{i}\|_{\varphi}+\|((1-|x|)p_{i}|x|)_{i}\|_{\varphi}+\|(p_{i}(1-|x|))_{i}\|_{\varphi}.

We shall bound each term. The first term is easy:

\|(a_{i}-q_{i}a_{i})_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}=\sum_{i}\varphi((1-q_{i})a_{i}^{2})\leq\sum_{i}\varphi((1-q_{i})a_{i})\leq\varepsilon.

The third term is also easy:

\|(p_{i}(1-|x|))_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}=\sum_{i}\varphi((1-|x|)p_{i}(1-|x|))=\varphi((1-|x|)^{2})\leq\varphi(1-|x|^{2})\leq\varepsilon.

We used that
(1-|x|)^{2}\leq 1-|x|^{2}
, which is true because
0\leq|x|\leq 1
. For the second term we proceed similarly but with more care:

\displaystyle\|((1-|x|)p_{i}|x|)_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\varphi(|x|p_{i}(1-|x|)^{2}p_{i}|x|)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\varphi(|x|p_{i}(1-|x|^{2})p_{i}|x|)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\varphi(|x|p_{i}|x|-(|x|p_{i}|x|)^{2}).

Using that
|x|p_{i}|x|=q_{i}a_{i}
, we obtain

\|((1-|x|)p_{i}|x|)_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}\leq\sum_{i}\varphi(q_{i}(a_{i}-a_{i}^{2}))\leq\sum_{i}(a_{i}-a_{i}^{2})\leq\varepsilon.

To conclude, we obtain

\|(a_{i}-p_{i})_{i}\|_{\varphi}\leq 3\sqrt{\varepsilon},

which is the desired conclusion.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
when
\mathcal{M}
is semi-finite

We deduce easily the case when
\mathcal{M}
is semi-finite from the finite case, thanks to the following basic fact.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
(\mathcal{M},\varphi)
is a von Neumann algebra with a normal state and
(p_{\alpha})
is a net of projections tending to
1
, then for every finite family
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
, we have

\lim_{\alpha}\varphi(p_{\alpha}C_{1}p_{\alpha}C_{2}\dots p_{\alpha}C_{k}p_{\alpha})=\varphi(C_{1}C_{2}\dots C_{k}).

Proof.

This follows simply from the fact that, on the unit ball
B
of
\mathcal{M}
equipped with the strong operator topology, multiplication
B\times B\to B
is continuous.

∎

So let
\mathcal{M},\varphi,(a_{i})
be as in Theorem
1.2
, with
\mathcal{M}
semi-finite. By
[
12
, Theorem V.1.37]
), there is an increasing net
p_{\alpha}
of finite projections in
\mathcal{M}
such that
\lim_{\alpha}p_{\alpha}=1
. Let
\varphi_{\alpha}
be the state
\frac{1}{\varphi(p_{\alpha})}\varphi
on
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}:=p_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}p_{\alpha}
, and define a POVM
a_{i,\alpha}=p_{\alpha}a_{i}p_{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{M}_{\alpha}
. It follows from Lemma
4.1
that for every
\alpha
large enough,
\varphi_{\alpha}(\sum_{i}a_{i,\alpha}^{2})>\varepsilon
, and the (already proven) finite case of Theorem
1.2
provides us with a PVM
p_{i,\alpha}
in
p_{\alpha}\mathcal{M}p_{\alpha}
satisfying the conclusion of the theorem. Then for
\alpha
large enough, the PVM
(1-p_{\alpha}+p_{1,\alpha},p_{2,\alpha},\dots,p_{n,\alpha})
satisfies the conclusion of Theorem
1.2
.

5.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
when
\mathcal{M}
is type III

The type III case will be proven with the same strategy as the finite case, but the details are simpler, and the constants are a bit better (the
9
can be replaced by
1
in that case). We shall need the following. Recall that the central support of an element
x\in\mathcal{M}
is the smallest projection
z(x)
in the center of
\mathcal{M}
such that
z(x)x=x
. We shall use the standard terminology on comparision of projections
[
12
, Chapter V]
: we say that two projections
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
are equivalent and write
p\sim q
if there is
u\in\mathcal{M}
such that
u^{*}u=p
,
uu^{*}=q
. We write
p\prec q
if there is a projection
p^{\prime}
equivalent to
p
such that
q-p^{\prime}
is positive.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a type III von Neumann algebra, then there is a net
(q_{\alpha})
of projections in
\mathcal{M}
converging weak-* to
1
and such that
1-q_{\alpha}\sim 1
for every
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
be a state. In the first step of the proof, we shall construct an increasing sequence
(q_{k})_{k\in\mathbf{N}}
of projections such that
\psi(q_{k})\geq 1-2^{-k}
and
(1-q_{k})\sim 1
. The construction is by induction. Define
q_{0}=0
. If
q_{k}
is defined, then
(1-q_{k})\mathcal{M}(1-q_{k})
is of type III, so by
[
12
, Proposition V.1.36]
, there is projection
e_{k}\in(1-q_{k})\mathcal{M}(1-q_{k})
such that, if
f_{k}=1-q_{k}-e_{k}
, then
e_{k}\sim f_{k}\sim 1-q_{k}
. In particular, both
f_{k}
and
e_{k}
are equivalent to
1
in
\mathcal{M}
. Moreover, we have
\psi(e_{k})+\psi(f_{k})=\psi(1-q_{k})\leq 2^{-k}
, so
\min(\psi(e_{k}),\psi(f_{k}))\leq 2^{-k-1}
. It remains to define
q_{k+1}=1-e_{k}
if
\psi(e_{k})\leq\psi(f_{k})
and
q_{k+1}=1-f_{k}
otherwise.

This sequence
q_{k}
depends on
\psi
, so let us denote it
q_{k,\psi}
.

Consider the set
A
of all finite sets of normal states on
\mathcal{M}
, ordered by inclusion. For every
\alpha=\{\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{d}\}\in A
, we define
q_{\alpha}
to be
q_{d,\psi}
where
\psi=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\psi_{i}
. It satisfies
\psi_{i}(1-q_{\alpha})\geq 1-d2^{-d}
for every
1\leq i\leq d
. In other words, the net
(q_{\alpha})
satisfies
\lim_{\alpha}\psi(q_{\alpha})=1
for every normal state
\psi
. That is, it converges to
1
weak-*.
∎

So let
\mathcal{M},\varphi,(a_{i})
be as in Theorem
1.2
, with
\mathcal{M}
type III, and let
q_{\alpha}
be as in the previous lemma. Consider
v_{\alpha}=\sum_{i}e_{i,1}\otimes a_{i}^{1/2}q_{\alpha}
, that we see in
M_{n}(\mathcal{M})
. Observe that
v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}=\sum_{i}e_{1,1}\otimes q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha}=e_{1,1}\otimes q_{\alpha}
, so
v_{\alpha}
is a partial isometry. It is well-known that the projections
e_{1,1}\otimes 1
and
1_{n}\otimes 1
are equivalent in
M_{n}(\mathcal{M})
. Indeed, it follows from a repeated use of
[
12
, Proposition V.1.36]
that there are isometries
u_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
(that is
u_{i}^{*}u_{i}=1
) such that
1=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}u_{i}^{*}
. Then
u:=\sum_{i}e_{1,i}\otimes u_{i}\in\mathcal{M}
realizes the equivalence between
u^{*}u=1_{n}\otimes 1
and
uu^{*}=e_{1,1}\otimes 1
. So by the properties of
q_{\alpha}
given in Lemma
5.1
, we have

e_{1,1}\otimes 1-v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}=e_{1,1}\otimes(1-q_{\alpha})\sim e_{1,1}\otimes 1\sim 1\textrm{ in }M_{n}(\mathcal{M}).

In particular, we have

1_{n}\otimes 1-v_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}^{*}\prec e_{1,1}\otimes 1-v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha},

and there is
w_{\alpha}\in M_{n}(\mathcal{M})
such that
w_{\alpha}w_{\alpha}^{*}=1_{n}\otimes 1-v_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}^{*}
and
w_{\alpha}^{*}w_{\alpha}\leq e_{1,1}\otimes 1-v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}
. Letting
u_{\alpha}=v_{\alpha}+w_{\alpha}
, we therefore have

u_{\alpha}^{*}u_{\alpha}\leq e_{1,1}\otimes 1,\ \ u_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}^{*}=1_{n}\otimes 1.

We can therefore define
p_{i,\alpha}\in\mathcal{M}
by
u_{\alpha}^{*}(e_{i,i}\otimes 1)u_{\alpha}=e_{1,1}\otimes p_{i,\alpha}
. The fact that
u_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}^{*}=1
implies that
p_{i,\alpha}
are pairwise orthogonal projections, but a priori we only have
\sum_{i}p_{i,\alpha}\leq 1
. However, the sum is close to
1
as

(5.1)

e_{1,1}\otimes(\sum_{i}p_{i,\alpha})=u_{\alpha}^{*}u_{\alpha}\geq v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}=e_{1,1}\otimes q_{\alpha}.

Moreover, by the definition of
p_{i,\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle e_{1,1}\otimes q_{\alpha}p_{i,\alpha}q_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}u_{\alpha}^{*}(e_{i,i}\otimes 1)u_{\alpha}v_{\alpha}^{*}v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=v_{\alpha}^{*}(e_{i,i}\otimes 1)v_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e_{1,1} \otimes q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha}.

That is,

(5.2)

q_{\alpha}p_{i,\alpha}q_{\alpha}=q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha}.

As in the finite case, let us denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\varphi}
the norm on
\mathcal{M}^{n}
given by

\|(b_{i})_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi(b_{i}^{*}b_{i}).

Remembering (
5.2
), we can decompose
a_{i}-p_{i,\alpha}=a_{i}-q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha}-(1-q_{\alpha})p_{i,\alpha}q_{\alpha}-p_{i,\alpha}(1-q_{\alpha})
and obtain by the triangle inequality

\|(a_{i}-p_{i,\alpha})_{i}\|_{\varphi}\leq\|(a_{i}-q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha})_{i}\|_{\varphi}+\|((1-q_{\alpha})p_{i,\alpha}q_{\alpha})_{i}\|_{\varphi}+\|(p_{i,\alpha}(1-q_{\alpha}))_{i}\|_{\varphi}.

It follows from Lemma
4.1
that the first term goes to
0
as
\alpha\to\infty
. The last term is straightforward to bound:

\|(p_{i,\alpha}(1-q_{\alpha}))_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}=\varphi((1-q_{\alpha})(\sum_{i}p_{i,\alpha})(1-q_{\alpha}))\leq\varphi(1-q_{\alpha})\to 0.

The middle term is bounded as follows

\displaystyle\|((1-q_{\alpha})p_{i,\alpha}q_{\alpha})_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\varphi(q_{\alpha}p_{i,\alpha}(1-q_{\alpha})p_{i,\alpha}q_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\varphi(q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha}-(q_{\alpha}a_{i}q_{\alpha})^{2}),

which goes to
\sum_{i}\varphi(a_{i}-a_{i}^{2})<\varepsilon
. All in all, this implies that

\limsup_{\alpha}\|(a_{i}-p_{i,\alpha})_{i}\|_{\varphi}<\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

We are not completely done yet, as
p_{i,\alpha}
do not sum to
1
. But almost. Indeed, by (
5.1
), we have
\sum_{i}p_{i,\alpha}
converges to
1
and in particular

\lim_{\alpha}\varphi(\sum_{i}p_{i,\alpha})=1.

This implies that if we replace
p_{1,\alpha}
by
p_{1,\alpha}+(1-\sum_{i}p_{i,\alpha})
, we obtain a PVM in
\mathcal{M}
which still satisfies

\limsup_{\alpha}\|(a_{i}-p_{i,\alpha})_{i}\|_{\varphi}<\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

This concludes the proof of the Theorem in the type III case.

6.
Almost commuting PVMs are close to commuting PVMS

This short section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Denote by
(a_{i})
the POVM
a_{i}=\sum_{j}q_{j}p_{i}q_{j}
. We can compute

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle>\sum_{i,j}\|p_{i}q_{j}-q_{j}p_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\sum_{i,j}p_{i}q_{j}p_{i}+q_{j}p_{i}q_{j}-(p_{i}q_{j})^{2}-(q_{j}p_{i})^{2})

\displaystyle=2-2\Re(\sum_{i}\varphi(a_{i}p_{i}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\|p_{i}-a_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}+(1-\varphi(\sum_{i}a_{i}^{2})).

We can apply Theorem
1.2
to the
a_{i}
in the von Neumann algebra
\mathcal{N}
generated by the
a_{i}
’s, and obtain a PVM
p^{\prime}_{i}
belonging to
\mathcal{N}
such that

\sum_{i}\|a_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}<9(\varepsilon-\sum_{i}\|p_{i}-a_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}).

But since
a_{i}
belongs to the commutant of
\{q_{j}\}
, the same is true for
\mathcal{N}
, so
[p^{\prime}_{i},q_{j}]=0
for all
i,j
. Using the triangle inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we conclude as follows

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{i}\|p_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{i}\|p_{i}-a_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\sum_{i}\|a_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{10}\left(\sum_{i}\|p_{i}-a_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}+\frac{1}{9}\|a_{i}-p^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\varphi}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle<\sqrt{10\varepsilon}.

7.
Hahn-Banach

We conclude this note with a quite unrelated subject, except that it is also an infinite dimensional generalization of a key result in
[
4
]
, and that is is also used in
[
5
]
.

Lemma 9.2 in
[
4
]
states that for any finite collection
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of positive matrices,

(7.1)

\min\{\tr(z)\mid z\geq a_{i}\forall i\}=\max\{\sum_{i}\tr(a_{i}t_{i})\mid 0\leq t_{i}\leq 1,\sum_{i}t_{i}=1\},

and that moreover any pair of minimizer
z
and maximizer
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
satisfies

(7.2)

z=\sum_{i}t_{i}a_{i}.

The equality in (
7.1
) is known to be true more generally in any semifinite von Neumann algebra, as a particular case of a duality for Pisier’s operator-space valued non-commutative
L_{p}
spaces
L_{p}(\mathcal{M};\ell_{\infty})

[
9
]
. Formula (
7.1
) corresponds to the case
p=1
and
\mathcal{M}=M_{n}(\mathbf{C})
in
[
7
, Proposition 2.1 (iii)]
. To the author’s knowledge, (
7.2
) has not been observed or used earlier in operator space theory.

It turns out that the preceding is true more generally in arbitrary von Neumann algebras, as follows. For
t\in\mathcal{M}
and
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
, we use the standard notation
t\varphi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
and
\varphi t\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
to denote the linear forms
x\mapsto\varphi(xt)
and
x\mapsto\varphi(tx)
respectively.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a von Neumann algebra, and
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in(\mathcal{M}_{*})_{+}
be normal positive linear forms. Then

(7.3)

\inf\{\psi(1)\mid\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{*},\psi\geq\varphi_{i}\forall i\}=\sup\{\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(t_{i})\mid(t_{i})\textrm{POVM in }\mathcal{M}\}.

Moreover, the infimum and the supremum are both attained, and any pair of a minimizer
\psi
and a maximizer
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
satisfies
t_{i}\psi=t_{i}\varphi_{i}
,
\psi t_{i}=\varphi_{i}t_{i}
and
\psi=\sum_{i}t_{i}\varphi_{i}=\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}t_{i}
.

Corollary 7.2
.

For every
\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{n}\in(\mathcal{M}_{*})_{+}
, there is a unique element of
\mathcal{M}_{*}
of minimal norm such that
\psi\geq\varphi_{i}
for all
i
.

Moreover, there is a POVM
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\psi=\sum_{i}t_{i}\varphi_{i}
.

Proof of Proposition
7.1
.

The inequality
\geq
in (
7.3
) is clear: if
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
is any POVM and
\psi\geq\varphi_{i}
for all
i
, then

\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(t_{i})\leq\sum_{i}\psi(t_{i})=\psi(1).

The converse relies on Hahn-Banach. We rather use the variant given in
[
10
, Lemma A.16]
. Define

m:=\inf\{\psi(1)\mid\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{*},\psi\geq\varphi_{i}\forall i\}.

Consider the weak-* closed convex subset of
\mathcal{M}^{n+1}

S=\{(t_{0},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n+1}\mid 0\leq t_{i}\leq 1\forall i\},

and for every self-adjoint
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
, define
f_{\psi}\in\ell_{\infty}(S)
by

f_{\psi}(t_{0},\dots,t_{n})=\psi(t_{0})-m+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\varphi_{i}-\psi)(t_{i}).

We claim that
\sup_{S}f_{\psi}\geq 0
. Indeed, it is a general fact that if a self-adjoint element
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
has Jordan decomposition
\rho=\rho_{+}-\rho_{-}
(see
[
12
, Theorem III.4.2]
), then
\sup_{0\leq t\leq 1}\rho(t)=\|\rho_{+}\|=\rho_{+}(1)
. In our situation, we obtain

\sup_{S}f_{\psi}=\left(\psi_{+}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\varphi_{i}-\psi)_{+}\right)(1)-m.

For every
1\leq j\leq n
, using that
\psi_{+}\geq\psi
,
(\varphi_{j}-\psi)_{+}\geq\varphi_{j}-\psi
and
(\varphi_{i}-\psi)_{+}\geq 0
for
i\neq j
, we see that

\psi_{+}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\varphi_{i}-\psi)_{+}\geq\varphi_{j}.

By the definition of
m
, this implies
\sup_{S}f_{\psi}\geq 0
as claimed.

Consider now the convex cone
\mathcal{F}\subset\ell_{\infty}(S)
generated by the
convex
set
\{f_{\psi}\mid\psi=\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{*}\}
:

\mathcal{F}=\{\lambda f_{\psi}\mid\psi=\psi^{*}\in\mathcal{M}_{*},\lambda\in(0,\infty)\}.

The elements of
\mathcal{F}
are affine weak-* continuous maps on
S
, and we have just proved that
\forall f\in\mathcal{F}
,
\sup_{S}f\geq 0
. We can therefore apply
[
10
, Lemma A.16]
and obtain
(t_{0},\dots,t_{n})\in S
such that
f_{\psi}(t_{0},\dots,t_{n})\geq 0
for every self-adjoint
\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{*}
. Equivalently,

\psi(t_{0}-\sum_{1}^{n}t_{i})+\sum_{1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(t_{i})\geq m.

This implies that
t_{0}=\sum_{1}^{n}t_{i}
, and that
\sum_{1}^{n}\varphi_{i}(t_{i})\geq m
. In other words, we have obtained positive elements
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathcal{M}
such that
\sum_{i}t_{i}\leq 1
and
\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(t_{i})\geq m
. A fortiori (say replacing
t_{n}
by
t_{n}+(1-\sum_{1}^{n}t_{i})
), there are positive
t_{i}
with
\sum_{i}t_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i}\varphi_{i}(t_{i})\geq m
. This proves at the same time the inequality
\leq
in (
7.3
) and that the supremum in (
7.3
) is attained.

Let us justify that the infimum is also attained. By the weak-* compactness of the unit ball of
\mathcal{M}^{*}
, we have that the infimum of
\psi(1)
over all
\psi\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
such that
\psi\geq\varphi_{i}
for all
i
is attained at some
\psi\in\mathcal{M}^{*}
. But using that
\mathcal{M}_{*}
is
L
-embedded in
\mathcal{M}^{*}

[
12
, Theorem III.2.14]
, we obtain that
\psi
necessarily belongs to
\mathcal{M}_{*}
.

Consider now
\psi
attaining the infimum, and
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})
attaining the supremum in (
7.3
). We then have

\sum_{i}(\psi-\varphi_{i})(t_{i})=0.

This implies (since
(\psi-\varphi_{i})(t_{i})\geq 0
is clear) that
(\psi-\varphi_{i})(t_{i})=0
for all
i
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that for every
x\in\mathcal{M}
,

|(\psi-\varphi_{i})(xt_{i})|^{2}=|(\psi-\varphi_{i})(xt_{i}^{1/2}t_{i}^{1/2})|^{2}\leq(\psi-\varphi_{i})(t_{i})(\psi-\varphi_{i})(xt_{i}x^{*})=0.

Hence
t_{i}(\psi-\varphi_{i})=0
. Summing over
i
we also obtain
\sum_{i}t_{i}\varphi_{i}=\sum_{i}t_{i}\psi=\psi
. Taking the adjoints we deduce
\psi t_{i}=\varphi_{i}t_{i}
.
∎
