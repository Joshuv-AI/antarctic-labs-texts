Finer-grained Reductions in Fine-grained Hardness of Approximation
Elie Abboud Noga Ron-Zewi, eliabboud1000@gmail.com, noga@cs.haifa.ac.il

Abstract
We investigate the relation between δ \delta and ϵ \epsilon required for obtaining a ( 1 + δ ) (1+\delta) -approximation in time N 2 − ϵ N^{2-\epsilon} for closest pair problems under various distance metrics, and for other related problems in fine-grained complexity. Specifically, our main result shows that if it is impossible to (exactly) solve the (bichromatic) inner product (IP) problem for vectors of dimension c ​ log ⁡ N c\log N in time N 2 − ϵ N^{2-\epsilon} , then there is no ( 1 + δ ) (1+\delta) -approximation algorithm for (bichromatic) Euclidean Closest Pair running in time N 2 − 2 ​ ϵ N^{2-2\epsilon} , where δ ≈ ( ϵ / c ) 2 \delta\approx(\epsilon/c)^{2} (where ≈ \approx hides polylog \mathrm{polylog} factors). This improves on the prior result due to Chen and Williams (SODA 2019) which gave a smaller polynomial dependence of δ \delta on ϵ \epsilon , on the order of δ ≈ ( ϵ / c ) 6 \delta\approx(\epsilon/c)^{6} . Our result implies in turn that no ( 1 + δ ) (1+\delta) -approximation algorithm exists for Euclidean closest pair for δ ≈ ϵ 4 \delta\approx\epsilon^{4} , unless an algorithmic improvement for IP is obtained. This in turn is very close to the approximation guarantee of δ ≈ ϵ 3 \delta\approx\epsilon^{3} for Euclidean closest pair, given by the best known algorithm of Almam, Chan, and Williams (FOCS 2016). By known reductions, a similar result follows for a host of other related problems in fine-grained hardness of approximation. Our reduction combines the hardness of approximation framework of Chen and Williams, together with an MA communication protocol for IP over a small alphabet, that is inspired by the MA protocol of Chen (Theory of Computing, 2020).

Introduction

Traditionally, the approach to determine whether a computational problem is tractable was to find out whether
it has a polynomial-time algorithm. Finding such an algorithm implies that the problem is in
\mathrm{P}
,
and thus it was considered efficiently computable. Otherwise, if one is interested
in proving that the problem is intractable, we usually lack
the tools to prove lower bounds; instead one relies on hardness assumptions
which allow us to prove conditional lower-bounds.
In the classical theory of
\mathrm{NP}
-hardness, the hardness assumption is that
\mathrm{P}\neq\mathrm{NP}
, which is known to imply that no polynomial-time algorithm exists for many central computational problems.

In fine-grained complexity, one is interested in pinning down the
precise
complexity
of
tractable
computational problems. In particular, a central objective in fine-grained complexity is to
determine the exact exponent in the time complexity of
problems already known to be in
P
. More concretely, given a problem with input length
n
known
to be solvable in
t(n)
-time, is it possible to solve the problem
in time
t(n)^{1-\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
?
This is motivated by the fact that despite rigorous study of many central
computational problems in
P
, we have failed to improve on the running time of their best-known algorithms (see for example the survey
[
Wil18
]
for a list of such problems). This motivates the question of whether there is an inherent difficulty in the problem that prevents us from finding faster algorithms.

Once more, we typically lack the tools to prove lower bounds, and we thus instead rely on hardness assumptions to obtain conditional lower bounds for problems in
\mathrm{P}
.
One popular such conjecture has been the
Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
(SETH), which postulates that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an integer
k=k(\epsilon)
so that
it is impossible to solve
k
-SAT on
n
variables in time
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}

[
IP01
]
.

Another popular conjecture is the
Orthogonal Vector Conjecture
(OVC) which in the low-dimensional regime posits that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
c_{\mathrm{ov}}=c_{\mathrm{ov}}(\epsilon)
such that given a pair of sets
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of cardinality
N
each and of dimension
d=c_{\mathrm{ov}}\cdot\log N
, it is impossible to determine whether there exists a pair
(a,b)\in A\times B

satisfying that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=0
in
N^{2-\epsilon}
time
[
GIKW19
]
. It is known that SETH implies OVC
[
Wil05
]
, and so OVC is at least as plausible as SETH. In terms of algorithms, it is known how to solve the OV problem in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
with
c=\exp(1/\epsilon)

[
AWY15
,
CW21
]
, which implies that
c_{\mathrm{ov}}\geq\exp(1/\epsilon)
.

A related assumption is the
inner product
(IP)
assumption
which postulates that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
c_{\mathrm{ip}}=c_{\mathrm{ip}}(\epsilon)
such that given a pair of sets
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of cardinality
N
each and of dimension
d=c_{\mathrm{ip}}\cdot\log N
, and an integer
\sigma\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, it is impossible to determine whether there exists a pair
(a,b)\in A\times B

satisfying that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma
in
N^{2-\epsilon}
time. Once more, since the OV problem is a special case of the IP problem, the IP assumption is at least as plausible as OVC. Indeed, the best known algorithms for the IP problem are only able to solve this problem in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
with
c\approx 1/\epsilon

[
AW15
]
, and this only imposes that
c_{\mathrm{ip}}\gtrapprox 1/\epsilon
.
1
1

1

We use
\approx
,
\gtrapprox
,
\lessapprox
to hide
\mathrm{polylog}
factors.

In recent years, there has been a flurry of work showing fine-grained lower bounds for many central computational problems in P, based on the above assumptions. A main challenge in showing such fine-grained lower bounds based on these assumptions is that one must carefully design the reductions so that they run fast enough as not to supersede the lower bound assumptions.

One fundamental problem for which such fine-grained reductions were shown is the

Closest Pair
(CP)
problem
. In this problem, given a distance metric
\mathrm{dist}:\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and given a pair of sets
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
, the goal is to find a pair
(a,b)\in A\times B
which minimizes
\mathrm{dist}(a,b)
.
This problem was studied for various metrics such as Hamming,
\ell_{p}
, and edit distance, and it has many applications, for example in computational geometry, geographic
information systems
[
Hen06
]
, clustering
[
Zah71
,
Alp14
]
,
and matching problems
[
WTFX07
]
, to name a few.
For concreteness, in what follows we restrict our attention only to the Euclidean
\ell_{2}
metric, though many of the results we mention hold also for other metrics.

In the low-dimensional regime
d=c\log N
, the trivial algorithm for (Euclidean) closest pair runs in time
\tilde{O}(N^{2})
, while for small values of
c
,
the best known algorithm runs in time
N^{O(c)}

[
Rab76
,
KM95
]
(so a truly sub-quadratic algorithm is only known for small values of
c
close to zero).
On the other hand, in
[
AW15
]
it was shown that assuming OVC, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
c=c(\epsilon)
so that
no algorithm can solve this problem in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
.

1.1
Fine-grained hardness of approximation

Given the above state of affairs, it is natural to ask whether relaxing the requirements and settling
for an approximate “close-enough” answer can help in designing faster fine-grained algorithms. For example, it is known that for the (Euclidean) CP problem, one can obtain a
(1+\delta)
-approximation with running time
N^{2-\epsilon}
for
\delta\approx\epsilon^{3}
(for any dimension
d\leq N^{1-\epsilon}
)
[
ACW16
]
, which is much faster than the best-known exact algorithm.

In terms of impossibility results, known fine-grained reductions can typically be adapted to the approximate setting, based on appropriate

gap assumptions
, such as Gap-SETH.
2
2

2

The Gap-SETH assumption asserts that for any
\epsilon>0
, there are
k
and
\delta>0
, so that no
2^{(1-\epsilon)n}
-time algorithm can, given a
k
-CNF on
n
variables, distinguish between the case that it is satisfiable, and the case that any assignment satisfies at most an
(1-\delta)
-fraction of its clauses.

In the theory of NP-hardness, it is often possible to base hardness of gap-problems on hardness of exact problems using PCPs. However, a major barrier in applying this approach in the fine-grained setting (for example for the purpose of reducing SETH to Gap-SETH) is the large (super-constant) blow-up in the length of existing PCPs, which translates into a large (super-constant) blow-up in the number of variables
n
in the reduction.

Nevertheless, in a recent breakthrough, Abboud, Rubinstein, and Williams
[
ARW17
]
have shown how to utilize PCP machinery (specifically, the sumcheck protocol) for showing fine-grained hardness of approximation results based on
non-gap assumptions
. Since then,
many works have utilized this framework for showing fine-grained hardness of approximation results for many central problems in P, based on non-gap assumptions such as SETH or OVC
(see the recent surveys
[
RW19
,
FKLM20
]
for a description of this line of work).

In particular, for the CP problem, Rubinstein
[
Rub18
]
has shown that assuming OVC, for any
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)
such that there is no

(1+\delta)
-approximation algorithm for (Euclidean) CP running in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
. This rules out truly sub-quadratic approximation algorithms, running, say, in time
f(\delta)\cdot N^{1.99}
. However, the obtained dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
is far from optimal, specifically

\delta=\exp(-c_{\mathrm{ov}}/\epsilon)
, where
c_{\mathrm{ov}}=c_{\mathrm{ov}}(\epsilon)\geq\exp(1/\epsilon)
is the constant guaranteed by the OVC conjecture.

In a follow-up work, Chen and Williams
[
CW19
]
have shown an improved hardness of approximation result for CP in which
\delta
only depends polynomially on
\epsilon
. Specifically, they showed that if the IP assumption holds, then for any
\epsilon>0
there is no

(1+\delta)
-approximation algorithm for (Euclidean) CP running in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
, where
\delta=\mathrm{poly}(\epsilon/c_{\mathrm{ip}})
and
c_{\mathrm{ip}}=c_{\mathrm{ip}}(\epsilon)\gtrapprox 1/\epsilon
is the constant guaranteed by the IP assumption.
However, the obtained dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
was still quite small, on the order of
\delta\approx(\epsilon/c_{\mathrm{ip}})^{6}\lessapprox\epsilon^{12}
. This is still quite far from the dependence obtained by the best known approximation algorithm for CP which gives an
(1+\delta)
-approximation in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
for
\delta\approx\epsilon^{3}
.

In this work, we investigate the question of whether the dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
can even be further improved, potentially to match the best known approximation algorithm.

1.2
Our results

Recall that by the discussion above, the best known approximation algorithm for (Euclidean) CP gives an
(1+\delta)
-approximation in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
for
\delta\approx\epsilon^{3}
, while the best-known hardness of approximation result shows that if the IP assumption holds, then no
(1+\delta)
-approximation algorithm running in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
exists for

\delta\approx(\epsilon/c_{\mathrm{ip}})^{6}\lessapprox\epsilon^{12}
, where
c_{\mathrm{ip}}=c_{\mathrm{ip}}(\epsilon)\gtrapprox 1/\epsilon
is the constant guaranteed by the IP assumption.
Thus there remains a large polynomial gap between the upper and lower bounds, and our main result narrows this gap.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that the IP assumption holds, i.e., for any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, there exists a
c_{\mathrm{ip}}=c_{\mathrm{ip}}(\epsilon^{\prime})
such that given a pair of sets
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of cardinality
N
each and of dimension
d=c_{ip}(\epsilon^{\prime})\cdot logN
, and an integer
\sigma\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, it is impossible to find a pair
(a,b)\in A\times B

satisfying that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma
in
N^{2-\epsilon^{\prime}}
time.

Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there is
\delta=\tilde{\Theta}((\frac{\epsilon}{c_{\mathrm{ip}}(\epsilon/2)})^{2})
, so that any algorithm running in time

N^{2-\epsilon}
cannot

(1+\delta)
-approximate Euclidean CP.

Recall that it is known how to solve the IP problem in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
for dimension
d=c\log N
with

c\approx 1/\epsilon
, and so it must hold that
c_{\mathrm{ip}}\gtrapprox 1/\epsilon
. If we assume that
c_{\mathrm{ip}}\approx 1/\epsilon
, then the above theorem gives a dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
of the form
\delta\approx\epsilon^{4}
, which is very close to the dependence of
\delta\approx\epsilon^{3}
given by the best known algorithm. Moreover, improving the dependence in the above theorem to
\delta\approx\frac{\epsilon}{c_{\mathrm{ip}}}
would imply an algorithmic improvement on the IP problem. We leave the question of determining the exact dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
as an interesting open problem for future research.

By known reductions, the above theorem gives a similar improvement for a host of other problems in fine-grained hardness of approximation such as closest pair with respect to other metrics such as Hamming,
\ell_{p}
-norm for any constant
p>0
, and edit distance, Furthest Pair and approximate nearest neighbor in these metrics, and additive approximations to Max-IP and Min-IP, see Section
5
for more details.

Finally, we remark that the above theorem also holds under OVC (or SETH), but is less meaningful, since as discussed above, for the OV problem we have that
c_{\mathrm{ov}}\geq\exp(1/\epsilon)
.

1.3
Proof overview

Next we give an overview of our proof method, and how it improves on prior work. To this end, we first describe the general framework presented in
[
ARW17
]
for obtaining fine-grained hardness of approximation results based on MA communication protocols. Then we discuss the work of Rubinstein
[
Rub18
]
who relied on this framework to give the first fine-grained hardness of approximation result for CP, albeit with an exponential dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
, and the work of Chen and Williams
[
CW19
]
who improved this dependence to polynomial. Following this, we turn to discuss our proof method that obtains a tighter polynomial relation.

Fine-grained hardness of approximation via MA communication
[
ARW17
]
:

In a Merlin-Arthur (MA) communication protocol for a function
f:\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}
, two players
Alice and Bob wish to compute

f(a,b)
, where Alice is given as input only

a\in\{0,1\}^{d}
, and Bob is given as input only
b\in\{0,1\}^{d}
.
To this end, Alice and Bob engage in a randomized (public coin) communication protocol, where their goal is to use as little communication as possible.
To aid them with this task, there is also a (potentially malicious)
prover Merlin who sees Alice’s and Bob’s inputs, and before any communication
begins Merlin sends Alice a short message
m
, which can be thought of as a “proof” or “advice”.
The requirement is that if
f(a,b)=1
, then there must exist
some message
m
from Merlin on which Alice accepts with probability

1
. Otherwise, if
f(a,b)=0
, then for any possible message
\tilde{m}
from Merlin, Alice accepts with probability
at most
\frac{1}{2}
on
\tilde{m}
.
3
3

3

The rejection probability can be increased by executing the communication phase between Alice and Bob independently for multiple times and rejecting if and only if all invocations reject.

In
[
ARW17
]
, it was shown that an efficient MA communication protocol for
set disjointness
4
4

4

Recall that
set disjointness
is the function
\mathrm{disj}:\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\to\{0,1\}

\mathrm{disj}(a,b)=1
if and only if the supports of
a
and
b

\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=0
.
implies a fine-grained reduction from OV to an approximate version of Max-IP in which given two sets
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of cardinality
N
each, the goal is to output a number sufficiently close to
M:=\max_{a\in A,b\in B}\left\langle a,b\right\rangle
.

To see how a reduction as above can be constructed, suppose that there exists an MA communication protocol for set disjointness with Merlin’s message length
L
, communication complexity
\mathrm{cc}
between Alice and Bob, and randomness complexity
R
. Suppose furthermore that we are given an instance
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of OV, where
|A|=|B|=N
. Then for each possible Merlin’s message
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, we construct an instance
A_{m},B_{m}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{2^{\mathrm{cc}+R}}
of Max-IP, where
|A_{m}|=|B_{m}|=N
.

Fix
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}
. Then the set
A_{m}
is obtained from
A
by mapping each element
a\in A
to a binary vector
a_{m}
that contains an entry for each possible transcript
\Gamma\in\{0,1\}^{\mathrm{cc}}
and randomness string
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
(so
a_{m}
has length
2^{\mathrm{cc}+R}
), and whose
(\Gamma,r)
-entry equals
1
if and only if
\Gamma

r

a
, and Alice accepts on input
a
, randomness string
r
, and transcript
\Gamma
.
The set
B_{m}
is obtained analogously from
B
.

Then the main observation is that for some
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, we have that the
(\Gamma,r)
-entry of both
a_{m}
and
b_{m}
equals
1
if and only if Alice accepts on Merlin’s message
m
, inputs
a
and
b
, and randomness string
r
. Consequently, if there exists
(a,b)\in A\times B
so that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=0
(i.e.,
f(a,b)=1
), then there exists a Merlin’s message
m
on which Alice accepts with probability
1

a
and
b
, and consequently we have that the corresponding vectors

(a_{m},b_{m})\in A_{m}\times B_{m}
satisfy that
\left\langle a_{m},b_{m}\right\rangle=2^{R}
.
On the other hand, if

\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq 0
(i.e.,
f(a,b)=0
) for any
(a,b)\in A\times B
, then for any Merlin’s message
\tilde{m}
, and on any inputs

\frac{1}{2}

\left\langle a_{\tilde{m}},b_{\tilde{m}}\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{R}

(a_{\tilde{m}},b_{\tilde{m}})\in A_{\tilde{m}}\times B_{\tilde{m}}

\mathrm{cc}

and

are not too large,
so that the total construction time of the reduction is at most

MA communication protocol for set disjointness
[
AW09
]
:

The MA communication protocol for set disjointness of Aaronson and Wigderson
[
AW09
]
relies on the influential sumcheck protocol of
[
LFKN92
]
, and it proceeds as follows.

Let
a\in\{0,1\}^{d}
be Alice’s input.
Slightly abusing notation, we view
a
as a
\sqrt{d}\times\sqrt{d}
binary matrix in the natural way, and we let
\hat{a}
denote the
p\times\sqrt{d}
matrix obtained by encoding each column of
a
with a systematic Reed-Solomon code
\mathrm{RS}_{\sqrt{d},p}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\sqrt{d}}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{p}
of degree
\sqrt{d}
over a prime field of size
p\approx 4\sqrt{d}
.
5
5

5

The systematic Reed-Solomon code
\mathrm{RS}_{d,p}:\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}\to\mathbb{F}_{p}^{p}
of degree
d
over a prime field of size
p>d
is a linear map, defined as follows. To encode a message
m=(m(0),\ldots,m(d-1))\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{d}
, one finds the (unique) degree
d-1
polynomial
P_{m}(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{p}[X]
which satisfies that
P_{m}(i)=m(i)
for any
i=0,\ldots,d-1
, and lets
\mathrm{RS}_{d,p}(m)=(P_{m}(0),\ldots,P_{m}(p-1))
. The code is called
systematic
since the message is a prefix of its encodings.
The code has distance at least
p-d+1
since any pair of distinct degree
d-1
polynomials can agree on at most
d-1
points.

Let
\hat{b}
be defined analogously.

In the protocol, Merlin first computes the pointwise product
\hat{a}\star\hat{b}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{p\times\sqrt{d}}
, and then sends Alice the sum
m\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{p}
of the columns of
\hat{a}\star\hat{b}
(where arithemtic is performed mod
p
). Alice first checks that
m
is a codeword of
\mathrm{RS}_{2\sqrt{d},p}
, and that the first
\sqrt{d}
entries of
m
are all zero, otherwise she rejects and aborts.
Then Alice and Bob jointly sample a random index
i\in[p]
, Bob sends Alice the
i
’th row of
\hat{b}
, Alice computes its inner product with the
i
’th row of
\hat{a}
, and accepts if and only if this product equals
m(i)
(where once more, arithmetic is performed mod
p
).

To see that the protocol is complete, note first that if
a
and
b
are disjoint, then
a\star b
is the all-zero matrix. Consequently, by the systematic property of the Reed-Solomon encoding, the first
\sqrt{d}
rows of
\hat{a}\star\hat{b}
are also identically zero, which implies in turn that the first
\sqrt{d}
entries of
m
are identically zero. Furthermore, since the product of
two polynomials of degree at most
\sqrt{d}
is a polynomial of degree at most
2\sqrt{d}
, it follows that
m
is a codeword of
\mathrm{RS}_{2\sqrt{d},p}
. Thus, both Alice’s checks will clearly pass. It can also be verified that by construction, the inner product of the
i
’th rows of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
equals
m(i)
, and so Alice accepts with probability
1
.

To show soundness, suppose that
a
and
b
intersect, and let
\tilde{m}
denote Merlin’s message. We may assume that
\tilde{m}
is a codeword of
\mathrm{RS}_{2\sqrt{d},p}
, and that the first
\sqrt{d}
entries of
\tilde{m}
are all zero, since otherwise Alice clearly rejects. But on the other hand, since
a
and
b
intersect, then
a\star b
has a
1
-entry, say in the
j
-th row, and since
p>\sqrt{d}
, the sum of entries in the
j
’th row of
a\star b
is non-zero mod
p
, which implies in turn that
m(j)\neq 0
. Thus, we conclude that
\tilde{m}
and
m
are distinct codewords of
\mathrm{RS}_{2\sqrt{d},p}
– a code of distance at least
\frac{p}{2}
– and so they must differ by at least
\frac{1}{2}
of their entries. But this implies in turn that with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
over the choice of
i
, it holds that

\tilde{m}(i)\neq m(i)
, in which case the inner product of the
i
’th row of
\hat{a}
and
\hat{b}
will be different than
\tilde{m}(i)
, which will cause Alice to reject.

Finally, it can also be verified that in this protocol,
\mathrm{cc}
,
R
, and
L
are all upper bounded by
\approx\sqrt{d}
.

AW09
]
described above only gives
\mathrm{cc}\approx\sqrt{d}
which is super-constant for a super-constant dimension
d
, and consequently only yields a
sub-constant

\delta
.

To deal with this,
[
Rub18
]
first utilized the fact (previously utilized also in
[
ARW17
]
) that the
[
AW09
]
protocol described above works equally well on skewed matrices of dimensions
\frac{d}{T}\times T
, in which case we have that
L=\frac{d}{T}\cdot\log p
and
\mathrm{cc}=T\cdot\log p
.
Thus, assuming
d=c\log N
, to achieve
2^{L}\leq N^{\epsilon}
, one can set
T=\frac{c}{\epsilon}\cdot\log p
, which gives in turn
\mathrm{cc}=\frac{c}{\epsilon}\cdot\log^{2}p
.

However, this is still not quite enough since the MA protocol of
[
AW09
]
requires setting
p>\sqrt{d}
because of the use of Reed-Solomon codes that are only defined over a large alphabet, and consequently the communication complexity is still super-constant. However, the main observation in
[
AW09
]
is that the protocol can actually be executed using any error-correcting code with a
multiplication property
7
7

7

Informally, we say that a linear code
C:\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
has a
multiplication property
if the set
\mathrm{span}\{C(m)\star C(m^{\prime})\mid m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}^{k}\}
has sufficiently large distance.
.
Relying on this observation, Rubinstein replaced the Reed-Solomon codes in the protocol of
[
AW09
]
with algebraic-geometric (AG) codes that satisfy the multiplication property over a constant-size alphabet.
This reduced the communication complexity to
\approx c/\epsilon
, yielding in turn an approximation factor of
\delta=2^{-\tilde{\Theta}(c/\epsilon)}
.

Polynomial dependence
[
CW19
]
:

While
[
Rub18
]
gave the first non-trivial hardness of approximation result for CP, a downside of this result was that the approximation factor
\delta
depended exponentially on the running time parameter
\epsilon
. In the follow-up work
[
CW19
]
, Chen and Williams showed how to reduce this dependence to just polynomial.

The main observation of Chen and Williams was that instead of thinking of the output of the MA protocol of
[
Rub18
]
as being just accept or reject, one can view the output as being
short vectors

a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{T}
(namely, the
i
’th row of
\hat{a},\hat{b}
, respectively), and
\sigma^{\prime}\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
(namely, the
i
’th entry of Merlin’s message
m
), where
a^{\prime}
only depends on Alice’s input
a
and the randomness string,
b^{\prime}
only depends on Bob’s input
b
and the randomness string, and
\sigma^{\prime}
only depends on Merlin’s message and the randomness string. The requirement then is that if
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=0
for some
(a,b)\in A\times B
,
then for some Merlin’s message
m
,

\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sigma^{\prime}
with probability
1
, while if
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq 0
for any
(a,b)\in A\times B
,
then for any Merlin’s message
\tilde{m}
,
then
\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle\neq\sigma^{\prime}
with probability at least
\frac{1}{2}
.

Chen and Williams then suggested to create an instance
A_{m},B_{m}
for any Merlin’s message
m
, where the set
A_{m}
is obtained from
A
by simply mapping each element
a\in A
to a vector
a_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{T\cdot 2^{R}}
that is the concatenation of all possible output vectors
a^{\prime}
for all possible randomness strings, and analogously for
B_{m}
. The advantage is that now
the dimension of the vectors in
A_{m}
and
B_{m}
is much shorter than in

[
Rub18
]
. However, a disadvantage is that now the alphabet is not binary anymore, and an even more serious problem is that the soundness guarantee is only that
\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle\neq\sigma^{\prime}
, so the reduction does not seem to produce any gap.

To deal with these issues, Chen and Williams use an
encoding lemma
which gives mappings
g,h
and a value
\Gamma
, where
g,h
, and
\Gamma
only depend on
p
and
T
, so that
g(a^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
and
h(b^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
are binary vectors of length
\mathrm{poly}(p,T)
satisfying that if
\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sigma^{\prime}
then
\left\langle g(a^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}),h(b^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\right\rangle=\Gamma
, while if
\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle\neq\sigma^{\prime}
then
\left\langle g(a^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime}),h(b^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\right\rangle<\Gamma
(see Lemma
4.2
for a formal statement). This produces the desired additive gap, on the order of
\Omega(2^{R})
. Since the encoding lemma increases the dimension of the vectors only by a factor of
\mathrm{poly}(p,T)
, this yields an approximation factor of
\delta=\frac{1}{\mathrm{poly}(p,T)}=\mathrm{poly}(\frac{\epsilon}{c})
.

We note that a delicate issue that should be dealt with in the reduction is that the encoding lemma works over the integers, while the protocol works over finite fields, and in particular, over non-prime fields, as AG codes are only known to exist over non-prime fields. Additionally, Chen and Williams show that the reduction works equally well when using the IP problem instead of OV as its starting point, and using an MA communication protocol for IP similar to that of
[
AW09
]
. This can potentially allow for a smaller value of
c
as it is only known how to solve (exact) IP in time

N^{2-\epsilon}
up to a dimension of
c\log N
for
c\approx 1/\epsilon
.

This work - tighter polynomial dependence:

While
[
CW19
]
obtained a polynomial dependence of
\delta
on
\epsilon
, the dependence was quite small, on the order of
\delta\approx(\frac{\epsilon}{c})^{6}
, and in the current work we show how to improve the dependence to
\delta\approx(\frac{\epsilon}{c})^{2}
.

To this end, we first observe that one reason for the small polynomial dependence obtained in
[
CW19
]
was the large polynomial dependence of the dimension of the resulting vectors in the encoding lemma on the alphabet size
p
. While in the protocol of
[
Rub18
]
the field size
p
can be made constant using AG codes, the field still needs to be of characteristic at least
T
, since otherwise the sum of entries in a non-zero row of
a\star b
may sum to zero over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
, and consequently the soundness analysis will not go through.

To reduce the alphabet size, we first design a new MA protocol in which the alphabet size is only
polylogarithmic
in
T
(see Theorem
3.1
).
This protocol is inspired by the MA protocol of
[
Che20
]
for IP which achieved communication complexity
O(\sqrt{d\log d\log\log d})
, improving on the communication complexity of
O(\sqrt{d}\log d)
of
[
AW09
]
.
In a nutshell, Chen’s idea was to execute the original MA protocol of
[
AW09
]
multiple times over different small prime fields, hoping that if
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq 0
, then
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle
is also non-zero modulo many of the primes, and so the protocol will be executed correctly.
Chen showed that this is indeed possible to achieve using
O(\log d)
distinct primes of cardinality at most
\mathrm{polylog}(d)
each.

We observe that for skewed matrices of dimensions
\frac{d}{T}\times T
, it in fact suffices to execute the protocol with
O(\log T)
distinct primes of cardinality at most
\mathrm{polylog}(T)
each. While this choice does not necessarily guarantee the property above that if
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq 0
, then
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle
is also non-zero modulo many of the primes, this turns out to still suffice for a correct execution of the protocol.

We then further observe that such a protocol can be used in the framework of
[
CW19
]
to obtain an improved hardness of approximation result for Max-IP. Once more, a delicate issue is how to use the encoding lemma in the presence of many different non-prime fields.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first use of the techniques underlying the improved MA protocol of
[
Che20
]
for showing a fine-grained hardness of approximation result.

Paper organization.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We begin in Section 2 below with the required notation and terminology with respect to fine-grained complexity problems and error-correcting codes. Then in Section
3
we present our improved MA protocol over a small alphabet, while in Section

4
we show how to use this protocol for obtaining an improved reduction from IP to approximate Max-IP. Finally, in Section
5
we show implications of our latter result to showing hardness of approximation results for closest pair, as well as other related problems in fine-grained complexity.

2.2
Error-correcting codes

Our reduction will make use of error-correcting codes. In what follows, we first present some general notation and terminology with respect to error-correcting codes, and then describe the kind of codes we shall use for our reduction.

Let
\Sigma
be a finite alphabet, and
k,n
be positive integers (the
message length
and the

codeword length
, respectively). An
(error-correcting) code
is an injective map
C:\Sigma^{k}\to\Sigma^{n}
. The elements in the domain of
C
are called
messages
, and the elements in the image of
C
are called
codewords
.
We say that
C
is
systematic
if the message is a prefix of
the corresponding codeword, i.e., for every
x\in\Sigma^{k}
there
exists
z\in\Sigma^{n-k}
such that
C(x)=(x,z)
.
The
rate
of a code
C:\Sigma^{k}\to\Sigma^{n}
is the ratio

\rho:=\frac{k}{n}
. The
relative distance

\mathrm{dist}(C)
of

C
is the maximum
\delta>0
such that for every pair of distinct
messages
x,y\in\Sigma^{k}
it holds that
\Delta(C(x),C(y))\geq\delta
.

If
\Sigma=\mathbb{F}
for some finite field
\mathbb{F}
, and
C
is a linear map
between the vector spaces
\mathbb{F}^{k}
and
\mathbb{F}^{n}
then we say that
C
is

linear
. The
generating matrix
of a linear code

C:\mathbb{F}^{k}\to\mathbb{F}^{n}
is a matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}^{n\times k}
such that

C(x)=G\cdot x
for any
x\in\mathbb{F}^{k}
. We say that a linear code
C
is
explicit
if

G
can be generated in time
\mathrm{poly}(n)
.

For our reduction, we shall require linear codes over a small (constant-size, independent of the codeword length) alphabet, satisfying the
multiplication property
, which informally says that all pointwise products of pairs of codewords span a code of large distance.
Such codes can be obtained from the AG codes of
[
SAK+01
]
(see also
[
Rub18
, Theorem 2.4]
).

Theorem 2.6

(
[
SAK+01
]
;
[
Rub18
]
, Theorem 2.4)
.

There exists a constant integer
p_{0}
so that for any prime
p\geq p_{0}
, there exist two explicit code families
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\star}=\{(C_{\star})_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
so that the following hold for any

•

are systematic linear codes over

of relative distance at least

(MA Protocol for

The protocol:

The protocol proceeds as follows:

1.

(a)

Merlin sends

m_{0}:=\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathrm{col}_{j}(a)\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b)\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}^{d/T}.

(b)

For
\ell=1,\ldots,10t

2.

(a)

Alice checks that
\sum_{i=1}^{d/T}m_{0}(i)=\sigma
.

(b)

Alice checks that

and

(c)

For
\ell=1,\ldots,10t

3.

\ell_{*}\in[10t]

\alpha_{*},\beta_{*}\in\{0,1\}

It can be verified that the protocol has the required structure, and that the running times of Alice and Bob are as claimed. Next we show completeness and soundness.

\displaystyle m_{\ell}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m_{\ell,0,0}+(m_{\ell,0,1}+m_{\ell,1,0})\cdot X+m_{\ell,1,1}\cdot X^{2}\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\bigg[\mathrm{col}_{j}(a^{(\ell,0)})\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell,0)})

\displaystyle+\left(col_{j}(a^{(\ell,0)})\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell,1)})+col_{j}(a^{(\ell,1)})\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell,0)})\right)\cdot X

\displaystyle+col_{j}(a^{(\ell,1)})\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell,1)})\cdot X^{2}\bigg]\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}(\mathrm{col}_{j}(a^{(\ell,0)})+\mathrm{col}_{j}(a^{(\ell,1)})\cdot X)\star(\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell,0)})+\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell,1)})\cdot X)\;\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{T}\mathrm{col}_{j}(a^{(\ell)})\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell)})\;\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell}).

Now, since each column of
a^{(\ell)}
and
b^{(\ell)}
is a codeword of
C^{(\ell)}
, we have that
\mathrm{col}_{j}(a^{(\ell)})\star\mathrm{col}_{j}(b^{(\ell)})\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell})
is a codeword of
C_{\star}^{(\ell)}
for any
j\in[T]
. By linearity
of
C_{\star}^{(\ell)}
, this implies in turn that
m_{\ell}
is a codeword of
C_{\star}^{(\ell)}
,
and so Alice’s check on Step
2c
will also pass.

Thus, we conclude that all of Alice’s checks on Step
2
pass.
Furthermore, we clearly have that

\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rangle=\left\langle\mathrm{row}_{i_{*}}(a^{(\ell_{*},\alpha_{*})}),\mathrm{row}_{i_{*}}(b^{({\ell_{*}},\beta_{*})})\right\rangle=m_{\ell_{*},\alpha_{*},\beta_{*}}(i)=\sigma^{\prime}.

We conclude that in the case that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma
, we have that
\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sigma^{\prime}
with probability
1
, as required.

Soundness:

Assume that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq\sigma
, and let
\tilde{m}_{0}
and
\tilde{m}_{\ell,\alpha,\beta}
for
\ell=1,\ldots,10t
and
\alpha,\beta\in\{0,1\}
be Merlin’s messages on Step

. We shall show that in this case Alice and Bob output
a^{\prime}
,

, and
\sigma^{\prime}
so that
\left\langle a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\right\rangle=\sigma^{\prime}
with probability at most
0.98
.

To this end, first note that we may assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{d/T}\tilde{m}_{0}(i)=\sigma

.
On the other hand, by (

) and by assumption that

\sum_{i=1}^{d/T}m_{0}(i)=\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq\sigma.

Consequently, there exists

so that
m_{0}(i)\neq\tilde{m}_{0}(i)
.

Moreover, since
m_{0}(i),\tilde{m}_{0}(i)\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\}
, we have that
|m_{0}(i)-\tilde{m}_{0}(i)|\leq T
. Recalling that

t^{t}=T

p_{\ell}\geq t
for any

of the

’s can divide
|m_{0}(i)-\tilde{m}_{0}(i)|
.
Thus, with probability at least
0.9
over the choice of
\ell_{*}
, it holds that
p_{\ell*}
does not divide
|m_{0}(i)\neq\tilde{m}_{0}(i)|
, and so
m_{0}(i)\neq\tilde{m}_{0}(i)\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;p_{\ell_{*}})
. In what follows, assume that this event holds.

Let
\tilde{m}_{\ell_{*}}\in(\mathbb{F}_{p_{\ell_{*}}^{2}})^{n_{\ell_{*}}}
be given by

\tilde{m}_{\ell_{*}}=\tilde{m}_{\ell_{*},0,0}+(\tilde{m}_{\ell_{*},0,1}+\tilde{m}_{\ell^{*},1,0})\cdot X+\tilde{m}_{\ell^{*},1,1}\cdot X^{2}\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell_{*}}).

Next observe that we may assume that for any

since otherwise Alice clearly rejects on Step
2b
.
On the other hand, by (

) and (

)

m_{\ell_{*}}(i)=m_{\ell_{*},0,0}(i)+(m_{\ell_{*},0,1}(i)+m_{\ell^{*},1,0}(i))\cdot X+m_{\ell^{*},1,1}(i)\cdot X^{2}\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;Q_{\ell_{*}})=m_{0}(i)\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;p_{\ell_{*}}).

Consequently, by assumption that
m_{0}(i)\neq\tilde{m}_{0}(i)\;\;(\mathrm{mod}\;p_{\ell_{*}})
for some

\tilde{m}_{\ell_{*}}(i)\neq m_{\ell_{*}}(i)
.

Finally, note that we may assume that
\tilde{m}_{\ell_{*}}

, and by assumption that

, we have that
\tilde{m}_{\ell^{*}}

4
From IP to Apx-Max-IP

In this section we use Theorem
3.1
from the previous section which gives an MA protocol for IP over a small alphabet to give a fine-grained reduction from IP to Apx-Max-IP with a tighter polynomial dependence of the approximation parameter
\delta
on the running time parameter
\epsilon
.

Lemma 4.1

(From
\mathrm{IP}
to
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{Max}
-
\mathrm{IP}
)
.

The following holds for any
\epsilon>0
and integer
c\geq 1
.
Suppose that
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d}
cannot be solved in time
N^{2-\epsilon}
for
d=c\log N
. Then
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{Max}
-
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d^{\prime}}
cannot be solved in time
N^{2-2\epsilon}
for
\delta=\tilde{\Theta}((\frac{\epsilon}{c})^{2})
.

To prove the above lemma, we shall use the following encoding lemma from
[
CW19
]
, which can be used to turn the (non-binary) vectors
a^{\prime},b^{\prime}
from the protocol given in Theorem
3.1
into (binary) vectors, whose inner product exhibits a gap.

Lemma 4.2

(Encoding Lemma,
[
CW19
]
, Lemma 6.3)
.

For any non-negative integers
T
and
q
, there exist mappings
g,h:\{0,1,\ldots,q\}^{T}\times\{0,1,\ldots,T\cdot q^{2}\}\rightarrow\{0,1\}^{O(T^{2}q^{4})}
and an integer
\Gamma\leq O(T^{2}\cdot q^{4})
, so that for any
a,b\in\{0,1,\ldots,q\}^{T}
and
\sigma\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\cdot q^{2}\}
:

•

If
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma\Rightarrow\left\langle g(a,\sigma),h(b,\sigma)\right\rangle=\Gamma
.

•

If
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq\sigma\Rightarrow\left\langle g(a,\sigma),h(b,\sigma)\right\rangle<\Gamma
.

Moreover,
g,h
can be computed in time
\mathrm{poly}(T,q)
.

The reduction:

We shall show a reduction from
\mathrm{IP}
to many instances of
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{Max}
-
\mathrm{IP}
, based on our
\mathrm{MA}
protocol for
\mathrm{IP}
over a small alphabet given in Theorem
3.1
, and the above encoding Lemma
4.2
.

Let
A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
and
\sigma\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
be an instance of
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d}
. Let
T
be a sufficiently large integer, to be determined later on, and let
\pi
be the protocol guaranteed by Theorem
3.1
for
T
,
q=O(\log^{2}T)
, and
d
(without loss of generality assume that
T
divides
d
).
Let
g,h
, and
\Gamma
be the mappings and the integer guaranteed for
T
and
q
by Lemma
4.2
.

Let
\mathrm{Rej}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{L}
denote the subset of Merlin’s messages
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}
in
\pi
on which Alice rejects on input
\sigma
.
For
b\in B
and
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
, let
b^{\prime}_{r}
denote the string output by Bob in the protocol
\pi
on input
b
and randomness string
r
.
Similarly, for
a\in A
and
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
, let
a^{\prime}_{r}
denote the string output by Alice in the protocol
\pi
on input
a
and randomness string
r
. For
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
and
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
, let
\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}
denote the integer output by Alice on Merlin’s message
m
and randomness string
r
.

For any
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
, we create an instance
A_{m},B_{m}
of
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{Max}
-
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d^{\prime}}
, given by

A_{m}:=\{(g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}))_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}\mid a\in A\},

and

B_{m}:=\{(h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}))_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}\mid b\in B\},

where

d^{\prime}=O(2^{R}\cdot T^{2}\cdot q^{4}).

Let
\delta:=\frac{0.01\cdot 2^{R}}{d^{\prime}}
.
Given an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{Max}
-
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d^{\prime}}
, we show an algorithm
\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
for
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d}
:
Given an instance
A,B,\sigma
for
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d}
, the algorithm
\mathcal{A^{\prime}}

generates all instances
A_{m},B_{m}
for
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
, and
runs
\mathcal{A}
on any of these instances.
If on any of the instances the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
outputs a value at least
2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.01)
then the algorithm
\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
accepts, otherwise it rejects.

Correctness:

Correctness relies on the following claim.

Claim 4.3
.

•

If there exists
(a,b)\in A\times B
so that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma
, then there exist
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
and
(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in A_{m}\times B_{m}
so that
\left\langle a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle=2^{R}\cdot\Gamma
.

•

If
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq\sigma
for any
(a,b)\in A\times B
, then
\left\langle a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle\leq 2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.02)
for any
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
and
(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in A_{m}\times B_{m}
.

Proof.

For the first item, suppose that there exists
(a,b)\in A\times B
so that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma
. Let
m
be Merlin’s message in the protocol
\pi
on inputs
a,b
, and
\sigma
, and let
(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in A_{m}\times B_{m}
be given by

a^{\prime\prime}=(g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}))_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}
and
b^{\prime\prime}=(h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}))_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}
. By the completeness property of
\pi
, we have that
m\notin\mathrm{Rej}
, and

\left\langle a^{\prime}_{r},b^{\prime}_{r}\right\rangle=\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}
for any
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
. Consequently, by Lemma
4.2
,
\left\langle g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}),h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r})\right\rangle=\Gamma
for any
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
. But this implies in turn that

\left\langle a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle=\sum_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}\left\langle g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}),h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r})\right\rangle=2^{R}\cdot\Gamma.

For the second item, suppose that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq\sigma
for any
(a,b)\in A\times B
. Fix
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
and

(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in A_{m}\times B_{m}
. Then by construction,
a^{\prime\prime}=(g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}))_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}
and
b^{\prime\prime}=(h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}))_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}
. By the soundness property of
\pi
, for at least a
0.02
-fraction of the randomness strings
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
, it holds that
\left\langle a^{\prime}_{r},b^{\prime}_{r}\right\rangle\neq\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}
. Consequently, by Lemma
4.2
for at least a
0.02
-fraction of the randomness strings
r\in\{0,1\}^{R}
, it holds that
\left\langle g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}),h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r})\right\rangle\leq\Gamma-1
. But this implies in turn that

\left\langle a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle=\sum_{r\in\{0,1\}^{R}}\left\langle g(a^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r}),h(b^{\prime}_{r},\sigma^{\prime}_{m,r})\right\rangle\leq 0.98\cdot 2^{R}\cdot\Gamma+0.02\cdot 2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-1)=2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.02).

∎

Now, if there exists
(a,b)\in A\times B
so that
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle=\sigma
, then by the above claim there exists
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
so that
\max_{a^{\prime\prime}\in A_{m},b^{\prime\prime}\in B_{m}}\left\langle a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle\geq 2^{R}\cdot\Gamma
. Consequently, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
will output a value greater than
2^{R}\cdot\Gamma-\delta\cdot d^{\prime}=2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.01)
on the instance
A_{m},B_{m}
, and so the algorithm
\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
will accept.

If on the other hand,
\left\langle a,b\right\rangle\neq\sigma
for any
(a,b)\in A\times B
, then by the above claim
\max_{a^{\prime\prime}\in A_{m},b^{\prime\prime}\in B_{m}}\left\langle a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\right\rangle\leq 2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.02)
for any
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}\setminus\mathrm{Rej}
. Consequently, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
will output a value at most
2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.02)<2^{R}\cdot(\Gamma-0.01)
on any of the instances, an so the algorithm

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
will reject.

Running time:

Suppose that the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{Max}
-
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d^{\prime}}
runs in time
N^{2-2\epsilon}
, we shall show that for an appropriate choice of
T
, the running time of the algorithm
\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
for

\mathrm{IP}_{N,d}
is at most
N^{2-\epsilon}
.

The algorithm
\mathcal{A^{\prime}}
enumerates over all possible Merlin’s messages
m\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, and for each such message checks whether Alice rejects
m
in
\pi
, which takes time
\mathrm{poly}(d)
, and if she does not reject, it generates the instance
A_{m},B_{m}
which takes time
N\cdot 2^{R}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(d)\cdot\mathrm{poly}(T,q)\leq N\cdot\mathrm{poly}(d)
, and runs the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
on
A_{m},B_{m}
which takes time
N^{2-2\epsilon}
.

Hence the total running time of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is at most

\displaystyle 2^{L}\cdot(N\cdot\mathrm{poly}(d)+N^{2-2\epsilon})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{O(\frac{d}{T}\cdot\log^{2}T)}\cdot(N\cdot\mathrm{poly}(d)+N^{2-2\epsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{O(\frac{c\log N}{T}\cdot\log^{2}T)}\cdot(N\cdot\mathrm{poly}(c\log N)+N^{2-2\epsilon}).

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{O(\frac{c\log N}{T}\cdot\log^{2}T)}\cdot N^{2-2\epsilon}.

Finally, it can be verified that the latter expression is at most
N^{2-\epsilon}
for choice of
T=\tilde{\Theta}(c/\epsilon)
which divides
d
.

Approximation parameter:

By choice of

\delta=\frac{0.01\cdot 2^{R}}{d^{\prime}}
,
d^{\prime}=O(2^{R}\cdot T^{2}\cdot q^{4})
,
T=\tilde{\Theta}(c/\epsilon)
,
and
q=O(\log^{2}T)
, we have that

\delta=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{T^{2}q^{4}}\right)=\tilde{\Theta}\left(\left(\frac{\epsilon}{c}\right)^{2}\right).

Data structure setting.

Our results extend to the data structure setting.

Definition 5.7

(Approximate Nearest Neighbor (
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{NN}
))
.

Let
\mathrm{dist}:\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a distance function, and let
\delta>0
be a parameter. In the
Approximate Nearest Neighbor
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{NN}_{N,d,dist}
problem
, given a set
A\subseteq\{0,1\}^{d}
of cardinality
N
, the goal is to pre-process the set, so that given a vector
b\in\{0,1\}^{d}
it is possible to quickly output a number in
[M,(1+\delta)M]
, where
M:=\min_{a\in A}\mathrm{dist}(a,b).

It is known that
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{CP}
can be reduced to
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{NN}

[
WW18
]
(see also proof of Corollary 1.4 in
[
ARW17
]
).

Lemma 5.8

(From
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{CP}_{\Delta}
to
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{NN}
,
[
WW18
]
)
.

Let
\mathrm{dist}:\{0,1\}^{d}\times\{0,1\}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a distance function. Suppose that
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{CP}_{N,d,dist}
cannot be solved in
N^{2-\epsilon}
time. Then for any
r>0
,
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{NN}_{N,d,dist}
cannot be solved with
N^{r}
preprocessing time and
N^{1-2r\epsilon}
time.

(From
\mathrm{IP}
to
\mathrm{Apx}
-
\mathrm{NN}
)
.

Suppose that
\mathrm{IP}_{N,d}
cannot be solved in time

for

. Then for any distance function
\mathrm{dist}\in\{\Delta,\ell_{p},\mathrm{ED}\}
and

,
\delta
-
\mathrm{Apx}
-
