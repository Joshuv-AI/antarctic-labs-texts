Quantifying the uncertainty in the time-redshift relationship
Michael S. Turner

Abstract
The age of the Universe at a given redshift is a fundamental relationship in cosmology. For many years, the uncertainties in it were dauntingly large, close to a factor of 2. In this age of precision cosmology, they are now at the percent level and dominated by the uncertainty in the Hubble constant. The uncertainties due to the parameters that describe the current cosmological model, Λ \Lambda CDM, are much less important. In decreasing order they are: uncertainty due to the matter density Ω M \Omega_{M} around 0.9% for z > 3 z>3 ; uncertainty due to the dark energy equation-of-state parameter w w , less than 0.3% for z > 3 z>3 ; and uncertainty due to the curvature parameter Ω k \Omega_{k} , at most 0.07%.



With only the assumption of the Robertson-Walker metric it is simple to show that the age of the Universe at a given redshift is given by (see e.g., Ref.
[
1
]
)

\displaystyle t(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{z}^{\infty}{dz\over(1+z)H(z)}

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}^{-1}\int_{z}^{\infty}{dz\over(1+z)\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})(1+z)^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}(1+z)^{2}+\Omega_{M}(1+z)^{3}\right]^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{0}^{-1}\int_{(1+z)}^{\infty}{dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{1/2}}

where
x\equiv 1+z
,
\Omega_{M}
is the fraction of critical density in matter,
w
is the dark energy equation-of-state parameter, and
\Omega_{k}
is the curvature parameter. Here it is assumed that the Universe comprises matter and dark energy and the small, about 0.01% fraction of critical density in radiation has been neglected here (see footnote below).

Twenty-five years ago, the uncertainties in the age-redshift were very large, both because of uncertainty in the Hubble constant and in the cosmological model. Until 2001, when the HST Key Project pinned down
H_{0}
with a reliable error budget
[
2
]
, one could argue for any value between 50 km/sec/Mpc and 100 km/sec/Mpc (or even beyond
[
3
]
) and the use of “little
h
” was universal,
h\equiv H_{0}/
100/km/sec/Mpc. Likewise, values for the density parameter
\Omega_{0}
ranged from
0.1
to
1.0
and even above. Together, this meant that
t(z)
was uncertain by around a factor of two, making quantifying the uncertainty in the age-redshift relationship a fool’s errand.

In the current era of precision cosmology
[
4
]
, the situation couldn’t be more different: We have a standard model,
\Lambda
CDM, well supported by data, with a small set of cosmological parameters that are well determined. Likewise, the Hubble constant is known to a precision of better than 5%
[
8
,
9
]
. For
\Lambda
CDM,
\Omega_{k}=0
and
w=-1
, the age-redshift relationship is given by:

t(z)={1\over 3}H_{0}^{-1}\Omega_{\Lambda}^{-1/2}\ln\left[{[1+x^{3}\Omega_{M}/\Omega_{\Lambda}]^{1/2}+1\over[1+x^{3}\Omega_{M}/\Omega_{\Lambda}]^{1/2}-1}\right]\\

where as before
x=1+z
.

The purpose of this note is to estimate the uncertainty in the age-redshift relationship for the
\Lambda
CDM model. In particular, with the exception of
H_{0}
(see below), I will be guided by the cosmological parameters and uncertainties from Planck
[
5
]
, supplemented by measurements of
w
by Pantheon
[
6
]
and the Dark Energy Survey
[
7
]
:

•

\Omega_{M}=0.3111\pm 0.0056

•

\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.6889\pm 0.0056

•

\Omega_{k}=0.0007\pm 0.0019

•

w=-1\pm 0.05

where I have inflated the quoted uncertainty in
w
by a factor of 2.

Adopting these cosmological parameters, i.e.,
\Omega_{M}=0.3111
,
\Omega_{k}=0
and
w=-1
, the age-redshift relationship above is

\displaystyle t(z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.402H_{0}^{-1}\ln\left[{(1+0.452x^{3})^{1/2}+1\over(1+0.452x^{3})^{1/2}-1}\right]

(2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 5.616(0.7/h)\,{\rm Gyr}\ln\left[{(1+0.452x^{3})^{1/2}+1\over(1+0.452x^{3})^{1/2}-1}\right]

The determination of the Hubble constant is once again of pressing interest and controversy, with apparent discrepancies between the value directly measured and that inferred from CMB anisotropy measurements and the assumption of
\Lambda
CDM
[
8
,
9
]
. Since the Hubble constant factors out in determining the age-redshift relationship it is easy to separate its contribution to the uncertainty in
t(z)
from the other cosmological parameters, remembering that there may be correlations with the other parameters. Without making a judgment on the current Hubble tension, and for our purposes here, I think it is fair to say the current uncertainty is in the range of 1% to 5%.

Taking as the independent cosmological parameters,
H_{0}
,
\Omega_{M}
,
w
and
\Omega_{k}
, it follows by differentiation that

\displaystyle{\delta t\over t(z)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{\delta H_{0}\over H_{0}}

(3)

\displaystyle{\delta t\over\delta\Omega_{M}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}H_{0}^{-1}\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{(x^{3}-1)dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{3/2}}

(4)

\displaystyle{\delta t\over\delta w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{3\over 2}H_{0}^{-1}\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{\ln(x)(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{3/2}}

(5)

\displaystyle{\delta t\over\delta\Omega_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}H_{0}^{-1}\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{(x^{2}-1)dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{3/2}}

(6)

Bringing everything together, the uncertainty in the age-redshift relationship can be written as:

{\delta t\over t(z)}=\left\{-{\delta H_{0}/H_{0}},\ \alpha_{M}\delta\Omega_{M},\ \alpha_{w}\delta_{w},\ \alpha_{k}\delta\Omega_{k}\right\},

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{M}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 2\alpha_{0}}\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{(x^{3}-1)dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{3/2}}

(7)

\displaystyle\alpha_{w}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{3\over 2\alpha_{0}}\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{\ln(x)(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{3/2}}

(8)

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{1\over 2\alpha_{0}}\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{(x^{2}-1)dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{3/2}}

(9)

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{1+z}^{\infty}{dx\over x\left[(1-\Omega_{M}+\Omega_{k})x^{3(1+w)}-\Omega_{k}x^{2}+\Omega_{M}x^{3}\right]^{1/2}}

(10)

I have not added the uncertainties in quadrature, which would require a choice for the
H_{0}
uncertainty and taking proper account of correlated error in the cosmological parameters. Rather, I have shown the individual contributions separately to illustrate their relative importance and to allow the reader to re-scale if they feel it is necessary.

z>3
;
\Omega_{M}
, around 0.9% for
z>3
; and
H_{0}
, between 1% and 5% (which follows directly from its assumed uncertainty).

\Omega_{R}\simeq 0.00008
whose major uncertainty owes to that in

The redshift dependence of the uncertainty upon
\Omega_{M}
and
w
is easy to understand. It is most dependent upon
w
at
z<1
where dark energy dominates and the age is close to that of a dark energy dominated universe. The uncertainty due to
w
drops rapidly with redshift because for
z>1
the Universe is increasingly matter-dominated and the age approaches that of a matter only universe. In particular,
\delta t(z)/t(z)\rightarrow-{1\over 2}\delta\Omega_{M}/\Omega_{M}=0.93\%
for
z\gg 1
..

Two final comments. First, other than a variation in the dark energy EOS parameter, I did not consider modifications to the
\Lambda
CDM paradigm. The effect of early dark energy on the age-redshift relationship was discussed in detail in Ref.
[
10
]
. As I mentioned above, I did not combine all the parameter uncertainties, because of the correlations between the values determined for the parameters. But more importantly, the Hubble tension raises the issue of a possible missing element in the current cosmological model or a systematic error in
H_{0}
, which could impact
t(z)
significantly.

This note was inspired by a question asked of my UCLA colleague Professor Alice Shapley at the International Space Science Institute’s
Symposium on Cosmic Beginnings: Unveiling the first billion years with JWST
at the University of Bern on 14 March 2024. The question was, what is the uncertainty in the age-redshift relationship. She responded that it was small but she didn’t know precisely how small. I thought it would make a nice problem for my UCLA Cosmology class’ final problem set. Unfortunately, I had just given out that problem set and so I did the problem myself to share with the class.
