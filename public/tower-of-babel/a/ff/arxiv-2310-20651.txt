The Quantum Decoding Problem
André Chailloux, Jean-Pierre TillichInria de Paris, { \{ andre.chailloux,jean-pierre.tillich } \} @inria.fr

Abstract
One of the founding results of lattice based cryptography is a quantum reduction from the Short Integer Solution problem to the Learning with Errors problem introduced by Regev. It has recently been pointed out by Chen, Liu and Zhandry that this reduction can be made more powerful by replacing the learning with errors problem with a quantum equivalent, where the errors are given in quantum superposition. In the context of codes, this can be adapted to a reduction from finding short codewords to a quantum decoding problem for random linear codes. We therefore consider in this paper the quantum decoding problem, where we are given a superposition of noisy versions of a codeword and we want to recover the corresponding codeword. When we measure the superposition, we get back the usual classical decoding problem for which the best known algorithms are in the constant rate and error-rate regime exponential in the codelength. However, we will show here that when the noise rate is small enough, then the quantum decoding problem can be solved in quantum polynomial time. Moreover, we also show that the problem can in principle be solved quantumly (albeit not efficiently) for noise rates for which the associated classical decoding problem cannot be solved at all for information theoretic reasons. We then revisit Regev’s reduction in the context of codes. We show that using our algorithms for the quantum decoding problem in Regev’s reduction matches the best known quantum algorithms for the short codeword problem. This shows in some sense the tightness of Regev’s reduction when considering the quantum decoding problem and also paves the way for new quantum algorithms for the short codeword problem.

Decoding and looking for short codewords are problems that have been conjectured to be extremely close. They have been studied for a long time, and the best algorithms for solving these two problems

. A reduction from decoding to the problem of finding short codewords is known but in an

,

,

,

]
. However, until recently and even in an

]
which gave a quantum reduction from

which followed the path of the breakthrough result of
[

which reduced the problem of sampling short lattice vectors to

Later on, it was shown in

There is a fundamental difficulty of reducing the search of low weight codewords to decoding a linear code which is due to the fact that the nature of these two problems is very different. Decoding concentrates on a region of parameters where there is typically just one solution, whereas finding low weight codewords concentrates on a region of parameters where there are many solutions (and typically an exponential number of solutions). This makes these problems inherently very different. This was also the case for the reduction of

It is also worthwhile to notice that all these problems,

,

1.2
Regev’s quantum reduction and follow-up work

Regev’s quantum reduction
[
Reg05
]
is at the core of complexity reductions for these problems, which with
[
Ajt96
]
essentially started lattice-based cryptography. His approach when rephrased in the coding context is based on the following observation. Suppose that we were able to construct a quantum superposition
\sqrt{\frac{1}{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(e)}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
of noisy codewords of a code
\mathcal{C}
of dimension
k
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, for a normalization factor
Z
. If we would apply the quantum Fourier transform on such a state, then because of the periodicity property of such a state we would get a superposition concentrating solely on the codewords of the dual
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
of
\mathcal{C}
, that is
\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\perp}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}\sqrt{\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{c}}^{\perp})}|{\boldsymbol{c}}^{\perp}\rangle
. Here
\widehat{f}
is the (classical) Fourier transform of
f
that we will properly define in the technical part of the paper. Recall that the dual code is defined as

Definition 1

(dual code)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a linear code over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, i.e. a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
for some
k
and
n
. The dual code
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
is an
(n-k)
dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
defined by

\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\{\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}:\boldsymbol{d}\cdot{\boldsymbol{c}}=0,\;\forall{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}\},

where
{\boldsymbol{x}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{y}}=\sum_{i}x_{i}y_{i}
stands for the inner product between the vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
.

Now, we can expect that if
f
concentrates on fairly small weights, then
\widehat{f}
would also concentrate on rather small weights and therefore we would have a way of sampling low weight
(dual) codewords and solve
SCP
for the dual code. The point is now that
\sqrt{\frac{1}{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(e)}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
could be obtained
by solving the
DP
problem on states that are easy to construct. This is the main idea of Regev’s reduction. More precisely, the whole algorithm works as follows

Step 1.

Creation of the tensor product of a uniform superposition of codewords and a quantum superposition of noise

|\phi_{1}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{q^{k}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}|{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(e)}|\boldsymbol{e}\rangle.

Step 2.

Entangling the codeword with the noise by adding the first register to the second one and then swapping the two registers

|\phi_{2}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{q^{k}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(e)}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle.

Step 3.

Disentangling the two registers by decoding
{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}
and therefore finding
{\boldsymbol{c}}
which allows to erase the second register

|\phi_{2}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(e)}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle.

(The different normalizing factor
Z
arises when the above decoding procedure is imperfect and we condition on measuring
\mathbf{0}
in the last register.)

Step 4.

Applying the quantum Fourier transform on the first register and get

\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}}\sqrt{\widehat{f}(\boldsymbol{d})}|\boldsymbol{d}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle

Step 5.

Measure the first register and get some
\boldsymbol{d}
in
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
.

This approach is at the heart of the quantum reductions obtained in
[
Reg05
,
SSTX09
,
DRT23
]
. It is also a crucial ingredient in the paper
[
YZ22
]
proving verifiable quantum advantage by constructing - among other things - one-way functions that are even collision resistant against classical adversaries but are easily invertible quantumly.
In
[
Reg05
,
SSTX09
,
DRT23
]
, the crucial erasing/disentangling step is performed with the help of a
classical
decoding algorithm. Indeed any (classical or quantum) algorithm that can recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
from
{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}
can be applied coherently to erase the last register in step
3
2
2

2

Indeed, having such an algorithm means we can construct the unitary
U:|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle|0\rangle\rightarrow|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
. Applying the inverse of this unitary will give the erasure operation.
.

A key insight observed in
[
CLZ22
]
is that it is actually enough to recover
|{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
from the state
\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(\boldsymbol{e})}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
so we are given a superposition of all the noisy codewords
{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}
and not a fixed one. This means we have to solve the following problem

Problem 3

(
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,f)
)
.

The quantum decoding problem with positive integer parameters
q,n,k
and a probability distribution
f
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is defined as:

•

Input: Take
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
sampled uniformly at random over their domain. Let
{\boldsymbol{c}}=\boldsymbol{u}\mathbf{G}
and
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(e)}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
. The (quantum) input to this problem is
(\mathbf{G},|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle)
.

•

Goal: given
(\mathbf{G},|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle)
, find
{\boldsymbol{c}}
.

It’s not clear a priori whether this is helpful or not. If one measures the state
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
then one recovers a noisy codeword and we are back to the classical decoding problem.

However, having improvements by directly solving the (
LWE
variant of the) above problem has been proposed in
[
CLZ22
]
where a polynomial time quantum algorithm based on Regev’s approach solving
SIS
is proposed for the
l_{\infty}
norm (and not the euclidean norm as is standard there) for extremely high rate codes. Here the decoding problem is obtained by measuring the qudits in an appropriate basis allowing to rule out certain values for the code-symbols, and then they use the Arora-Ge algorithm
[
AG11
]
for recovering completely the codeword by solving an algebraic system which for the parameters that are considered there, is of polynomial complexity. Despite the fact that the parameters of the
SIS
problem are highly degenerate, no efficient classical algorithm performing this task is known. This paper puts forward the
S-LWE
and the
C-LWE
problems. Informally the first problem is the one we solve in Step 3 above and the second one is just to create directly the uniform superposition of noisy codewords obtained at Step 3.

1.3
Contributions

Our work has
2
starting points. First, the quantum reduction of
[
DRT23
]
between the short codeword problem and the decoding problem in the regime relevant for code-based cryptography
i.e.
a constant code rate
\frac{k}{n}
and constant error rate. Then, the key insight of
[
CLZ22
]
that one requires to solve the quantum decoding problem in the above reduction which can make it more efficient. Instead on focusing too much on the reduction, our aim is first to study here the quantum decoding problem for its own sake. Indeed, the problem is already interesting as a quantum generalization of the decoding problem and the fact it is used in the above reduction creates strong motivation for studying it.

In this work, we focus only on the Bernoulli noise of parameter
p
. This means we consider the error function

f(\boldsymbol{e})=(1-p)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}\left(\frac{p}{q-1}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}.

which in turn means that for any
{\boldsymbol{c}}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, we can rewrite

|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{f(\boldsymbol{e})}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sqrt{1-p}|c_{i}\rangle+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\sqrt{\frac{p}{q-1}}|c_{i}+\alpha\rangle\right).

For this Bernoulli noise with parameter
p
, the associated quantum decoding problem is written
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,p)
. We show that indeed, the complexity of the quantum decoding problem significantly differs from its classical counterpart. Our contributions can be summarized as

A polynomial time algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}
when the noise is low enough (but still of constant rate).

We will show that the quantum problem
\mathrm{QDP}
defined here is probably much easier than its classical counterpart
DP
. Indeed, for fixed rate
R\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\frac{k}{n}
only exponential-time algorithms are known for
DP
for natural noise models, for instance the Bernoulli i.i.d model where
q=2
,
\Pr(e_{i}=1)=p
for which all algorithms solving it are
exponential for
p
in
(0,1)
. This is not the case for the associated
\mathrm{QDP}
problem, where we will show that by using Unambiguous State Discrimination (USD) together with linear algebra we can solve
the problem in
polynomial time
up to some limiting value of
p
which is strictly between
0
and
1
for a fixed rate
R
. We generalize this result for any
q
by generalizing existing bounds on USD and also present an algorithm for partial binary unambiguous state discrimination which could be of independent interest.

A problem which can be solved above capacity.

There is an information theoretic limit for
any algorithm
solving classically or quantumly the classical decoding problem

DP
. When the rate exceeds the capacity of the noisy channel specified by
f
(and if this is an i.i.d. noise) then above the capacity of the noisy channel it is just impossible to solve
with say polynomial error probability the decoding problem just because there are exponentially many candidates at least as likely as the right candidate. The problem becomes intractable just because of this reason. For instance in the Bernoulli model above, the rate
R=k/n
has to be smaller than
1-h(p)
where
h(x)
is the binary entropy function,
h(x)=-x\log_{2}(x)-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)
.
Somewhat surprisingly, it turns out that we can go above the Shannon capacity for the
\mathrm{QDP}
problem. Moreover, with the help of the Pretty Good Measurement (PGM) we can fully characterize the noise range where the problem is tractable.

Applying
\mathrm{QDP}
solvers in Regev’s reduction.

Both algorithms (the one using USD and the other one based on PGM) can be applied to sample small weight
dual codewords and solve
SCP
. By applying the quantum reduction steps above, together with our polynomial time solving
\mathrm{QDP}
we obtain non-zero codewords of relative weight
\omega\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}w/n
satisfying
\omega\leq\frac{(q-1)(1-R)}{q}
. Interestingly enough, this is precisely the smallest weight that can be reached by the best known polynomial time algorithm, namely a minor variant of the Prange algorithm
[
Pra62
]
.
On the other hand, we will show that there is no hope to have a proper general reduction of
SCP
to
\mathrm{QDP}
, by providing examples showing that we can solve
\mathrm{QDP}
in a certain noise regime and still get nothing useful for
SCP
after using it in Regev’s reduction. However, we can adapt the PGM to still have some small codewords up to the tractability bound. Our examples really show that we have to analyze properly the state that we have at Step 3. of the reduction on a case by case basis.

We now perform a detailed description of our contributions.

1.3.1
Using USD as a means of improving quantum algorithms for
\mathrm{QDP}

The binary setting.

Our first idea, which extends naturally the work of
[
CLZ22
]
is to apply USD for the quantum decoding problem.

We first consider the binary setting,
i.e.

q=2
. This means the states
|\Psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}\sqrt{f(\boldsymbol{e})}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
for which we want to recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
are of the form

|\Psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{1-p}|c_{i}\rangle+\sqrt{p}|1-c_{i}\rangle.

Consider a fixed coordinate
i
for which we have the state
\sqrt{1-p}|c_{i}\rangle+\sqrt{p}|1-c_{i}\rangle
which we call
|\psi^{p}_{c_{i}}\rangle
. By measuring this state in the computational basis we get
c_{i}
wp.
1-p
and
1-c_{i}
wp.
p
. This measurement is actually the measurement that distinguishes best
|\psi_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi_{1}\rangle
.

Another measurement of interest is unambiguous state discrimination.
Here, the goal is not to distinguish optimally between
|\psi^{p}_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi^{p}_{1}\rangle
but to make sure that our guess is always correct but allowing for some abort. In this setting, we have the following

Proposition 1

(Unambiguous state discrimination)
.

For any
p\in[0,1]
, there exists a quantum measurement that on input
|\psi^{p}_{c_{i}}\rangle
outputs
c_{i}
wp.
1-2\sqrt{p(1-p)}
and outputs
\bot

Using this measurement, the probability of guessing correctly
c_{i}
is always smaller than
1-p
for
p\leq\frac{1}{2}
. However, we know exactly when we succeed in guessing
c_{i}
. This will be extremely useful for decoding. Indeed, if we recover
k

c_{i}
we recover the complete codeword
{\boldsymbol{c}}
with good probability by linear algebra by using the fact that

{\boldsymbol{c}}=\boldsymbol{m}\mathbf{G}
with
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{k\times n}
. This will lead to

Theorem 1
.

Let
R\in[0,1]
. For any

1.3.2
Determining exactly the tractability of the quantum decoding problem

We are now interested in the tractability of
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,p)
meaning when is it possible from an information theoretic perspective to solve this problem. In order to study this problem, a fundamental quantity is
\delta_{\textup{min}}(R)
defined below, sometimes referred to as the Gilbert-Varshamov distance

Notation 2
.

Let

\delta_{\textup{min}}(R)\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}h_{q}^{-1}(1-R)

For the classical setting, it is well understood that

o(1)
.

We would like now to understand what happens in the quantum setting.
Techniques based on (partial) unambiguous state discrimination will not work in the regime

. Since we are only interested in the tractability of the problem, we can consider optimal quantum algorithms for discriminating between the states

This means that if the problem is tractable then

\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}=\Omega(1)

\textrm{P}_{\textrm{OPT}}=o(1)

\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}=o(1)

The pretty good measurement associated to this distinguishing problem actually has a a lot of structure. It is actually a projective measurement on an orthonormal basis corresponding which can be seen as a Fourier basis involving the shifted dual codes of the code

Definition 13

For

\omega\in[0,1]

sampled as follows:
\mathbf{G}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k\times n},\ \boldsymbol{m}\leftarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k},{\boldsymbol{c}}=\boldsymbol{m}\mathbf{G},\ |\psi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in F_{q}^{n}}\sqrt{\omega^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}(1-\omega)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle

2.2.3
Punctured codes and Prange’s algorithm

We will use in what follows the notion of punctured and shortened code.

Definition 17

(Punctured and shortened code)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a linear code over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
of length
n
. Let
J\subseteq[n]
be a subset of code positions. The punctured code
\mathcal{C}_{J}
with respect to
J
is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{J}=\{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}:{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}\}.
The shortened code
C^{J}
with respect to
J
is defined as

\mathcal{C}^{J}=\{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}:{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\;{\boldsymbol{c}}_{[n]\setminus J}={\boldsymbol{0}}\}
(i.e. the set of codewords of
\mathcal{C}
where we keep only the positions in
J
and which are zero outside
J
).

It is readily seen that these two operations commute when taking the dual

Lemma 2
.

For any linear code
\mathcal{C}
and any subset
J
of positions of this code

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}_{J}\right)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\right)^{J}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}^{J}\right)^{\perp}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{C}^{\perp}\right)_{J}.

A variation of the Prange algorithm.

We recall here a result which is essentially folklore in coding theory, namely that there is a probabilistic polynomial time algorithm for finding short codewords in a random linear code of dimension
k
and length
n
over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
which produces short codewords of weight
\left\lfloor\frac{(q-1)(n-k)}{q}\right\rfloor
. It simply uses linear algebra. For this, consider a parity-check matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(n-k)\times n}
of
\mathcal{C}
and run
\Theta\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
times the following procedure

1.

Choose uniformly at random subset
J
of
k
positions of
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\bar{J}=[n]\setminus J
.

2.

If
\mathbf{H}_{\bar{J}}
is not of rank
n-k
, abort and else choose
{\boldsymbol{c}}
on
J
as a random vector of Hamming weight
1
.

3.

Find the remaining entries of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
by solving the linear system

\mathbf{H}_{\bar{J}}{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\bar{J}}}^{\intercal}=-\mathbf{H}_{J}{{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}}^{\intercal}

4.

If
|{\boldsymbol{c}}|=\left\lfloor\frac{(q-1)(n-k)}{q}\right\rfloor
output
{\boldsymbol{c}}
.

The rationale behind this algorithm is that the expected weight of such a
{\boldsymbol{c}}
is
1+\frac{(q-1)(n-k)}{q}
and that it can be proved that it takes the right weight with probability

\Omega\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
. Note that all the known (be they classical or quantum) algorithms that produce asymptotically relative weights
\omega<\frac{(1-q)(1-R)}{q}
where
R=\frac{k}{n}
is the code rate have
exponential complexity.

2.3
Distinguishing quantum states

Proposition 6

(Helstrom’s measurement)
.

Let
|\psi_{0}\rangle,|\psi_{1}\rangle
be
2
quantum pure states s.t.
|\langle\psi_{0}|\psi_{1}\rangle|=u
. There exists a quantum projective measurement
\Pi=\{\Pi_{0},\Pi_{1}\}
s.t.
\forall c\in\{0,1\},\ \tr(\Pi_{c}|\psi_{c}\rangle\langle\psi_{c}|)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{2}
.

In the above measurement, the measurement gives the correct answer w.p.
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{2}
and gives the opposite answer w.p.
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{1-u^{2}}}{2}
. Another measurement of interest is the one arising in the context of unambiguous state discrimination. Here we allow the measurement to answer “I don’t know” (which corresponds to outcome
2
). What we require from the measurement is that if the measurement does not answer
2
then it always answers the correct value. The optimal unambiguous measurement is given by the proposition below.

Proposition 7

(Unambiguous State Discrimination)
.

Let
|\psi_{0}\rangle,|\psi_{1}\rangle
be
2
quantum pure states s.t.
|\langle\psi_{0}|\psi_{1}\rangle|=u
. There exists a POVM
F=\{F_{0},F_{1},F_{2}\}
s.t.
\forall c\in\{0,1\},\tr(F_{c}|\psi_{c}\rangle\langle\psi_{c}|)=1-u
and
\tr(F_{2}|\psi_{c}\rangle\langle\psi_{c}|)=u
.

The optimal unambiguous measurement is not known when there are more than
2
states. We present a detailed analysis of USD with
q
states in Section
4.1
, where we give known results and also provide some new ones.

The final measurement of interest is the Pretty Good Measurement, which is a generic measurement to distinguish
n
quantum states.

Definition 18

(Pretty Good Measurement)
.

Consider an ensemble
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in[n]}
of
n
quantum pure states. The Pretty Good Measurement associated to this ensemble is the
POVM

\{M_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
with

M_{i}=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\quad\textrm{ given }\quad\rho=\sum_{i\in[n]}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|.

One can easily check that each
M_{i}\succcurlyeq 0
and that
\sum_{i}M_{i}=\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}\rho\rho^{-\frac{1}{2}}=I
.

Proposition 8
.

[
BK02
,
Mon06
]

Consider an ensemble
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}_{i\in[n]}
of
n
quantum pure states and
\{M_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
the associated pretty good measurement. We consider the setting where
i
is chosen at random and we want to recover
i
from
|\psi_{i}\rangle
. Let
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}
be the probability of success using the PGM and
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{OPT}}
be the optimal success probability. This means

\displaystyle\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\tr\left(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|M_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\textrm{P}_{\textrm{OPT}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\{N_{i}\}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\tr(|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|N_{i})

where the maximum is over all POVMs

. We have

\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}\leq\textrm{P}_{\textrm{OPT}}\leq\sqrt{\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}}.

The classical and quantum Fourier transform on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

In this article, we will use the quantum Fourier transform on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
where
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
is the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Definition and basic properties.

It is based on the characters of the group
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},+)
which are defined as follows (for more details see
[
LN97
, Chap 5,
\mathsf{S}
1]
, in particular a description of the characters in terms of the trace function is given in
[
LN97
, Ch. 5,
\mathsf{S}
1, Th. 5.7]
).

Definition 19
.

Fix
q=p^{s}
for a prime integer
p
and an integer
s\geq 1
. The characters of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are the functions
\chi_{y}:\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
indexed by elements
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
defined as follows

\displaystyle\chi_{y}(x)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}

\displaystyle e^{\frac{2i\pi\tr(x\cdot y)}{p}},\quad\text{with}

\displaystyle\tr(a)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}

\displaystyle a+a^{p}+a^{p^{2}}+\dots+a^{p^{s-1}}.

where the product
x\cdot y
corresponds to the product of elements in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We extend the definition to vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
as follows:

\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\Pi_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{y_{i}}(x_{i}).

When
q
is prime, we have
\chi_{y}(x)=e^{\frac{2i\pi xy}{q}}
. In the case where
q
is not prime, the above definition is not necessarily easy to handle for computations. Fortunately, characters have many desirable properties that we can use for our calculations.

Proposition 9
.

The characters
\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
have the following properties

1.

(Group Homomorphism).
\forall{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}
is a group homomorphism from
(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},+)
to
(\mathbb{C},\cdot)
meaning that
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})=\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\cdot\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})
.

2.

(Symmetry).
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},\ \chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})

3.

(Orthogonality of characters). The characters are orthogonal functions meaning that
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\overline{\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})}=q^{n}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}}
. In particular
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}=q
and
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\setminus\{0\},\ \sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
.

Notice that these imply some other properties on characters. For instance
\chi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\mathbf{0})=1
or
|\chi_{\boldsymbol{y}}({{\boldsymbol{x}}})|=1
for any
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
The orthogonality of characters, allows to define a unitary transform which is is nothing but the classical or the quantum Fourier transform on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

Definition 20
.

For a function
f:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, we define the (classical) Fourier transform
\hat{f}
as

\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})f({\boldsymbol{y}}).

The quantum Fourier transform
\QFTt
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
is the quantum unitary satisfying
\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\QFTt|{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle.

We will also write
|\widehat{\psi}\rangle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\QFTt|\psi\rangle
.

Note that when
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle
we have

|\widehat{\psi}\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle.

The Fourier transform can also be viewed as expressing the coefficients of a state in the Fourier basis

\left\{|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle,{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\right\}
as shown by

Fact 1
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}f({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle
, then

|\psi\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\widehat{f}(-{\boldsymbol{x}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle.

This follows on the spot from the fact that if
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}c_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle
, then

c_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\overline{\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(y)}f(y)=\widehat{f}(-{\boldsymbol{x}}).

Translations amount to multiplication by a phase in the Fourier basis.

It will be convenient for what follows to bring in the shift and phase operators which are defined by

Definition 21

(shift and phase operators)
.

For
{\boldsymbol{b}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

X_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}
be the shift operator
X_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}|{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle=|{\boldsymbol{x}}+{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle
and

The main properties of the Fourier transform follow from the fact that the characters are the common eigenbasis of all shift operators (and therefore all convolution operators). In the quantum setting,
this amounts to the fact that the quantum states
\{|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle,{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}
form an eigenbasis of the shift operators as shown by

Proposition 10
.

We have for all
{\boldsymbol{b}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

is an eigenstate of

associated to the eigenvalue
\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})
and

\displaystyle X_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}\cdot\QFTt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\QFTt\cdot Z_{-{\boldsymbol{b}}}

(2)

\displaystyle\QFTt\cdot X_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Z_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}\cdot\QFTt.

(3)

Proof.

Let
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{b}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle|C|\;\;\text{if ${\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\perp}$}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\;\;\text{otherwise,}

which follows by a similar reasoning by noticing that
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
can be vieved as the set of trivial characters acting on
\mathcal{C}
:

\{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}:\chi_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}({\boldsymbol{c}})=1,\;\forall{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}\}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}.

∎

3
Algorithms for the binary quantum decoding problem

3.1
Quantum polynomial time algorithm using unambiguous state discrimination

We present our first quantum algorithm that directly uses unambiguous state discrimination.

Theorem 4
.

Let
R\in(0,1)
. For any
\omega<\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{\perp}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\frac{1}{2}-\sqrt{\frac{R}{2}(1-\frac{R}{2})}
, there exists a quantum algorithm that solves
\mathrm{QDP}(2,n,\lfloor Rn\rfloor,\omega)
w.p.
1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
.

Proof.

We fix
R,\omega
, as well as
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k=\lfloor Rn\rfloor
. We consider an instance of
QDP(2,R,k,\omega)
so we have a random matrix
\mathbf{G}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k\times n}
,
{\boldsymbol{c}}=\boldsymbol{m}\mathbf{G}
for a randomly chosen
\boldsymbol{m}\leftarrow\{0,1\}^{k}
and the state

|\Psi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\psi^{\omega}_{c_{i}}\rangle
where
|\psi^{\omega}_{c_{i}}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\omega}|c_{i}\rangle+\sqrt{\omega}|1-c_{i}\rangle
.
We consider the following algorithm for solving our Quantum Decoding Problem

Quantum algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}
using USD

1.

Start from
|\Psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\psi_{c_{i}}^{\omega}\rangle
. Notice that
|\langle\psi^{\omega}_{0}|\psi^{\omega}_{1}\rangle|=2\sqrt{\omega(1-\omega)}
.

2.

Perform the optimal unambiguous measurement from Proposition
7
on each qubit of
|\Psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
in order to guess
c_{i}
, which can be done w.p.
p=1-|\langle\psi^{\omega}_{0}|\psi^{\omega}_{1}\rangle|=1-2\sqrt{\omega(1-\omega)}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}2\omega^{\perp}
. Let
J\subseteq[n]
be the set of indices where this measurement succeeds. The algorithm recovers here
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}
.

3.

If
G_{J}\in\{0,1\}^{k\times|J|}
is of rank
k
, recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
from
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}
by computing
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}G_{J}^{-1}G
.

Let
p=2\omega^{\perp}
. Since
\omega<\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{\perp}
, we have
2\omega^{\perp}>R
and there exists an absolute constant
\gamma>0
s.t.

p=R+\gamma
. Let
X_{i}
be the random variable s.t.
X_{i}(i\in J)=1
and
X_{i}(i\notin J)=0
. The

are independent random Bernoulli variables with parameter

\displaystyle P_{1}=\Pr\left[|J|\geq k+\frac{\gamma n}{2}\right]\geq\Pr\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq pn-\frac{\gamma n}{2}\right]\geq 1-2^{-\frac{\gamma^{2}n}{2}}

Then, using Proposition
4
we compute

\displaystyle P_{2}=\Pr\left[\rank(G_{J})=k\left|\ |J|\geq k+\frac{\gamma n}{2}\right.\right]\geq 1-2^{-\gamma n}.

Notice that the algorithm recovers
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}
so from Proposition

then the algorithm successfully recovers
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. If we define

to be the probability of success of the algorithm, we therefore have

∎

Using complex phases.

It is also possible to put complex phases in front of the error. This means we consider the states

3.2
Reduction between quantum decoding problems in the binary setting

The above algorithm is interesting as it presents an polynomial time algorithm for the quantum decoding problem in a regime where its classical counterpart requires - with our current knowledge - an exponential classical or quantum algorithm. However, it completely fails when
\omega>\left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^{\perp}
and the best algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}(2,n,\lfloor Rn\rfloor,\omega)
is still by first measuring and then solving
\mbox{DP}(2,n,\lfloor Rn\rfloor,\omega)
. Is there a way to improve the best algorithms
\mathrm{QDP}(2,n,\lfloor Rn\rfloor,\omega)
by using ideas of the previous section? The answer is yes. Instead of using USD, we use what we call partial Unambiguous State Discrimination. Our measurement will still abort with some probability but when it does not abort, we still allow a small probability failure but which will typically be smaller than if we used Helstrom’s measurement. With this technique we can actually show a general reduction theorem for
\mathrm{QDP}
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
R\in(0,1)
. Let
\omega\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
and
\omega^{\prime}\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
satisfying:
\omega^{\prime}\leq\omega
and
\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}}>R
.
Let any
p>\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}}
. Let also
k=\lfloor Rn\rfloor
.
Then
\mathrm{QDP}(2,n,k,\omega)\preccurlyeq\mathrm{QDP}(2,\lfloor pn\rfloor,k,\omega^{\prime})
meaning that if we have an algorithm that solves
\mathrm{QDP}(2,\lfloor pn\rfloor,k,\omega^{\prime})
, we can use it to solve
\mathrm{QDP}(2,n,k,\omega)
.

In order to prove our theorem, we first present our partial unambiguous state discrimination protocol. As a special case, we obtain our previous algorithm by taking
\omega^{\prime}=0
(the theorem can then be applied when
R<2\omega^{\perp}
).

3.2.1
Partial unambiguous state discrimination

We define
|\psi_{b}^{\omega}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\omega}|b\rangle+\sqrt{\omega}|1-b\rangle
. Recall that
\langle\psi_{0}^{\omega}|\psi_{1}^{\omega}\rangle=2\sqrt{\omega(1-\omega)}=1-2\omega^{\perp}
. Fix
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
with
\omega^{\prime}\leq\omega
. We use the following lemma

Lemma 5
.

Let
\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}}}
and
\beta=\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}
.
There exists a unitary
U
operation acting on
\myspan\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}
s.t.

\displaystyle U|\psi_{0}^{\omega}\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha|\psi_{0}^{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle+\beta|2\rangle

\displaystyle U|\psi_{1}^{\omega}\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha|\psi_{1}^{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle+\beta|2\rangle

Proof.

With the choice of
\alpha
that was made the hermitian product
\langle\psi_{0}^{\omega}|\psi_{1}^{\omega}\rangle
and their image is preserved. As a matter of fact

\langle\psi_{0}^{\omega}|\psi_{1}^{\omega}\rangle=2\sqrt{\omega(1-\omega)}=1-2\omega^{\perp}.

(6)

Now, if we let

|\psi^{\prime}_{b}\rangle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\alpha|\psi_{b}^{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle+\beta|2\rangle
for
b\in\{0,1\}
, then we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi^{\prime}_{0}|\psi^{\prime}_{1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}\langle\psi^{\omega^{\prime}}_{0}|\psi^{\omega^{\prime}}_{1}\rangle+|\beta|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\alpha|^{2}(1-2\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp})+|\beta|^{2}\quad\text{( by \eqref{eq:firsthermitproduct})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2|\alpha|^{2}\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}\quad\text{(by using $|\beta|^{2}=1-|\alpha|^{2}$)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-2\omega^{\perp}\quad\quad\text{(with our choice of }\alpha).

By definition of
\beta
,
|\psi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi^{\prime}_{1}\rangle
are both of norm
1
. This together with the equality
\langle\psi_{0}^{\omega}|\psi_{1}^{\omega}\rangle=\langle\psi^{\prime}_{0}|\psi^{\prime}_{1}\rangle
we just proved shows that
U
as defined above preserves the hermitian product on
\myspan\{\psi_{0}^{\omega},\psi_{1}^{\omega}\}=\myspan\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
. It suffices to choose

U|2\rangle
of norm
1
and orthogonal to both
|\psi^{\prime}_{0}\rangle
and
|\psi^{\prime}_{1}\rangle
to obtain a unitary transform since by construction it preserves the hermitian product on

\myspan\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}
.
∎

Proposition 12
.

Let
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
with
\omega^{\prime}<\omega
. There exists a quantum measurement s.t. when it is applied on
|\psi_{b}^{\omega}\rangle
, the resulting state is
|\psi_{b}^{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle
w.p.
\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}}
and
|2\rangle
w.p.
1-\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}}
.

Proof.

Start from
|\psi_{b}^{\omega}\rangle
and apply the unitary
U
from Lemma
5
. Then, perform the two outcomes projective measurement
\left\{\left(|0\rangle\langle 0|+|1\rangle\langle 1|\right),|2\rangle\langle 2|\right\}
on the state
U|\psi_{b}^{\omega}\rangle=\alpha|\psi_{b}^{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle+\beta|2\rangle
. We obtain the first outcome w.p.
|\alpha|^{2}=\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{\left(\omega^{\prime}\right)^{\perp}}
and the resulting state is
|\psi_{b}^{\omega^{\prime}}\rangle
and the second outcome w.p.
|\beta|^{2}
and the resulting outcome is
|2\rangle
.
∎

Unambiguous state discrimination can be seen as a special case of this operation by taking
\omega^{\prime}=0
, which gives
\alpha=\sqrt{2\omega^{\perp}}
and the probability of success is
\alpha^{2}=2\omega^{\perp}=1-\langle\psi_{0}^{\omega}|\psi_{1}^{\omega}\rangle
.

Proof of Theorem

3.2.3
Interpretation of the above as changing the noise model

In this section, we show how performing (partial) unambiguous state discrimination on a state
|\psi_{b}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\omega}|b\rangle+\sqrt{\omega}|1-b\rangle
can be seen as a way to change the noise model applied on the bit
b
. We first define different notions of noisy channels in the binary setting.

Definition 22
.

For a bit
b
, an error probability
\omega
and abort probability
p
, we define the distributions of the Binary Symmetric Channel
\mbox{BSC}(b,\omega)
, of the Binary Erasure Channel
BEC(b,p)
and of the Binary Symmetric with Errors and Erasures Channel
\mbox{BSEEC}(b,\omega,p)
sampled as follows:

\displaystyle BSC(b,\omega)

\displaystyle:\mbox{ return }b\mbox{ wp. }(1-\omega)\mbox{ and }(1-b)\mbox{ wp. }\omega.

\displaystyle BEC(b,p)

\displaystyle:\mbox{ return }b\mbox{ wp. }(1-p)\mbox{ and }\bot\mbox{ wp. }p.

\displaystyle BSEEC(b,\omega,p)

\displaystyle:\mbox{ return }b\mbox{ wp. }(1-p)(1-\omega),(1-b)\mbox{ wp. }(1-p)\omega\mbox{ and }\bot\mbox{ wp. }p.

For a bit
b
, flipping it w.p.
\omega
can be seen as passing
b
through a binary symmetric channel
BSC(\omega)
. Having this error in superposition means that we have access to the quantum state.
Our results can be interpreted as follows

Proposition 13
.

From
|\psi_{b}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\omega}|b\rangle+\sqrt{\omega}|1-b\rangle
it is possible to:

1.

Generate
y\leftarrow BSC(b,\omega)
simply by measuring
|\psi_{b}\rangle
.

2.

Generate
y\leftarrow BEC(b,1-2\omega^{\perp})
by performing unambiguous state discrimination on
|\psi_{b}\rangle
.

3.

Generate
y\leftarrow BSEEC(b,(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{1-p})^{\bot},p)
for any abort probability
p\in[0,1-2\omega^{\perp}]
, by performing partial unambiguous state discrimination on
|\psi_{b}\rangle
.

Notice that the third case generalizes the
2
first cases by respectively taking
p=0
and
p=1-2\omega^{\perp}
. This shows the advantage of having the noise in quantum superposition. It is possible to change the noise from the one coming from a Binary Symmetric Channel to the one coming from a Binary Erasure Channel or a Binary Symmetric with Errors and Erasures Channel.

4
Polynomial time algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}
in the
q
-ary setting

As we saw in the previous section, unambiguous state discrimination is crucial for polynomial time algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}
. While this task is very well understood in the binary case, we do not have any general formula in the
q
-ary setting. Fortunately, the states we consider will have enough structure so that we can fully characterize the optimal unambiguous state discrimination algorithm. We first present this characterization, which is essentially a generalization of the work of
[
CB98
]
. We then use this unambiguous state discrimination in the

-ary setting to derive our quantum algorithm for

in the

.

4.1
Unambiguous state discrimination in the
q
-ary setting

Definition 23
.

An unambiguous state discrimination measurement associated to some states
|\psi_{0}\rangle,\dots,|\psi_{N-1}\rangle
is a POVM
\{E_{0},\dots,E_{N-1},E_{F}\}
(where
E_{F}
stands for the failure outcome) s.t.

\forall i,j\neq i\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket,\ \tr(E_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|)=0.

To such a POVM, we associate the quantities

P_{j}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\tr(E_{j}|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|)
(the probability of correctly guessing
j
when given
|\psi_{j}\rangle
, as well as the average success probability
\overline{P_{D}}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}P_{j}
.

The optimal unambiguous measurement is not known when there are more than
2
states, however it is known in a case where the states we want to distinguish are linearly independent, have the same

a priori
probabilities and are symmetric in the following sense
[
BKMH97
]

Definition 24

(symmetric states)
.

A set
\left\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,\cdots,|\psi_{N-1}\rangle\right\}
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
of dimension
N
is symmetric if and only if there exists a unitary transformation
U
of order
N
on
\mathcal{H}
such that for any
i
and
j

in
\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket
we have
|\psi_{j}\rangle=U^{j-i}|\psi_{i}\rangle
.

In such a case, the optimal unambiguous measurement is known
[
CB98
]

Proposition 14

(Unambiguous State Discrimination of Symmetric States)
.

Let
\left\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,\cdots,|\psi_{N-1}\rangle\right\}
be a set of
N
symmetric states associated to a unitary transform
U
. Let
\{E_{0},\dots,E_{N-1},E_{F}\}
be any unambiguous state discrimination measurement associated to these states and let
P_{j}
and
\overline{P_{D}}
be the associated success probabilities.
\overline{P_{D}}
always satisfies

\overline{P_{D}}\leq N\min_{r\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket}|c_{r}|^{2},

(7)

where
c_{r}
are the coordinates of
|\psi_{0}\rangle
in the eigenbasis
\left\{|\gamma_{r}\rangle,r\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket\right\}
of
U
, i.e.
|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}c_{r}|\gamma_{r}\rangle
. There is a POVM which meets (
7
) with equality.

A corollary of this result is obtained by taking the Hilbert space of dimension a prime number
p
and take

U
as the shift operator
U|x\rangle=|x+1\rangle
where addition is performed in
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. It is easy to verify
that in this case, the maximal average probability of discrimination
\overline{P_{D}}^{\text{max}}
is given by

\overline{P_{D}}^{\text{max}}=p\min_{r\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}|\widehat{f}(r)|^{2}

when
|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{e\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}f(e)|e\rangle
. This is a consequence of the fact
that an eigenbasis of
U
is given by
\left\{|\widehat{x}\rangle,x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}\right\}
(this is implied by Proposition
10
)
and from

|\psi_{0}\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{p}}\widehat{f}(-x)|\widehat{x}\rangle.

The last equation follows from Fact
1
.
We will actually use and prove a slightly more general result, where in particular the dimension of the Hilbert space is not prime anymore (in which case we can not apply
Proposition
14
)

Proposition 15
.

Let
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}f({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle
for some function

s.t.
\lvert\kern-1.0pt\lvert f\rvert\kern-1.0pt\rvert_{2}=1
and for

|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}X_{\boldsymbol{b}}|\psi\rangle

are all linearly independent, unambiguous state discrimination of the states

is possible and has a maximal average probability of discrimination given by

\overline{P_{D}}^{\text{max}}=q^{n}\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}.

The proof of this statement borrows many ideas from
[
CB98
]
. Before giving it, we have to recall a few points (see

\mathsf{S}

Unambiguous state discrimination of linearly independent states.

Let
{\mathcal{H}}
be the Hilbert space spanned by the

{\boldsymbol{b}}
ranging over
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

where

is the probability of detecting

given that the input state was of this form and the

Since

. From these considerations, we see that if we bring in

gives an unambiguous discrimination for the set of states

\{|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle,{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}
. We call this POVM, the original POVM shifted by
{\boldsymbol{a}}
. However now the operator
X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{\boldsymbol{b}}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}
detects the state
|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{a}}+{\boldsymbol{b}}}\rangle
. Let
P_{\boldsymbol{b}}
be the discrimination probability of
|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}\rangle
by the operator

E_{\boldsymbol{b}}
, that is
P_{\boldsymbol{b}}=\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}|E_{\boldsymbol{b}}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle
and
P_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}
be the discrimination probability of the same state, but this time by the POVM
\{X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{\boldsymbol{b}}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}\cup\{X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{F}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}\}
. Since
|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}\rangle
is now detected by
X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{{\boldsymbol{b}}-{\boldsymbol{a}}}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}
, we have for all
{\boldsymbol{b}}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

P_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}^{\boldsymbol{a}}=P_{{\boldsymbol{b}}-{\boldsymbol{a}}}.

(9)

From these considerations, we clearly see that
E_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\text{ave}}=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{{\boldsymbol{b}}-{\boldsymbol{a}}}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}
detects
|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}\rangle
with probability

P_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\text{ave}}=\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}P_{{\boldsymbol{b}}-{\boldsymbol{a}}}=\overline{P}_{D}
. However, we also have to show that
\{E^{\text{ave}}_{\boldsymbol{b}},{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}\cup\{E_{F}^{\text{ave}}\}
defines a POVM.
All the
E_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\text{ave}}
are
clearly positive semi-definite, it remains to check that
E_{F}^{\text{ave}}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\mathbbm{1}-E_{D}^{\text{ave}}
is also positive semi-definite.
For this, we observe that

\displaystyle E_{D}^{\text{ave}}

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}E_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\text{ave}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{{\boldsymbol{b}}-{\boldsymbol{a}}}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{{\boldsymbol{b}}}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{D}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}

where
E_{D}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}E_{\boldsymbol{b}}
. By convexity of the maximum eigenvalue on the space of Hermitian operators on
\mathcal{H}
we have

\lambda_{\text{max}}(E_{D}^{\text{ave}})\leq\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{a}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\lambda_{\text{max}}(E_{D}^{\boldsymbol{a}})

(10)

where
E_{D}^{\boldsymbol{a}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}X_{\boldsymbol{a}}E_{D}X_{-{\boldsymbol{a}}}
.
The shifted POVM by
{\boldsymbol{a}}
is indeed a POVM and we have therefore
\lambda_{\text{max}}(E_{D}^{\boldsymbol{a}})\leq 1
. This together with (
10
) shows that
\lambda_{\text{max}}(E_{D}^{\text{ave}})\leq 1
and
that therefore
E_{F}^{\text{ave}}=\mathbbm{1}-E_{D}^{\text{ave}}
is indeed positive semi-definite.
∎

Choosing the appropriate basis.

The appropriate basis which simplifies a lot the computation is the common diagonalization basis of all the
X_{\boldsymbol{b}}
’s. It is given by the “character” basis

\{|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle,{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\}
(see Proposition
10
) and we have

X_{\boldsymbol{b}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle=\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle.

(11)

From this, we deduce that for all
{\boldsymbol{b}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
we have

X_{\boldsymbol{b}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|

(12)

If we express
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle
in this basis, we obtain

|\psi_{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}c_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle

then all the other ones are given by

|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle=X_{\boldsymbol{b}}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}c_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle.

(13)

It is readily verified that the reciprocal states are given by

|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\perp}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\frac{1}{\overline{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})|\widehat{x}\rangle

(14)

where
Z=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|c_{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{-2}
. Indeed, we observe that for any
{\boldsymbol{a}}
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\perp}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\overline{\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{a}})}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{x}}}({\boldsymbol{a}}-{\boldsymbol{b}})=\frac{q^{n}}{\sqrt{Z}}\delta({\boldsymbol{a}},{\boldsymbol{b}})

(15)

where
\delta({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
is the Kronecker function which is equal to
1
iff
{\boldsymbol{x}}={\boldsymbol{y}}
and to
0
otherwise.

We have now all the tools we need to prove Proposition
15
.

Proof of Proposition
15
.

From Lemma
6
we can choose the
E_{\boldsymbol{b}}
of the optimal POVM as

E_{\boldsymbol{b}}=\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{|\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\perp}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle|^{2}}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\perp}\rangle\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\perp}|.

(16)

By (
15
) we know that

|\langle\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\perp}|\psi_{\boldsymbol{b}}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{q^{2n}}{Z}
, and therefore by plugging
this expression in (
16
) and using (
13
) and (
14
) we obtain

\displaystyle E_{\boldsymbol{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Z}{q^{2n}}\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{Z}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\overline{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}c_{\boldsymbol{y}}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{x}}(-{\boldsymbol{b}})\overline{\chi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(-b)}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{q^{2n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\overline{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}c_{\boldsymbol{y}}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}(-{\boldsymbol{x}})\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{y}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{q^{2n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\overline{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}c_{\boldsymbol{y}}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}|

From this we infer that

\displaystyle E_{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}E_{\boldsymbol{b}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{q^{2n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\overline{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}c_{\boldsymbol{y}}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{q^{2n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\\
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\end{subarray}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\frac{1}{\overline{c_{\boldsymbol{x}}}c_{\boldsymbol{y}}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{y}}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{q^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\frac{1}{|c_{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|,

where in the last line we used that
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{b}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{b}}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}})=q^{n}\delta({\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}})
by Proposition
9
.
Since the
|\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|
’s form an orthonormal set of projectors we have that
\lambda_{\text{max}}(E_{D})=\frac{\overline{P_{D}}}{q^{n}\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|c_{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}
.
From the fact that we should have
\lambda_{\text{max}}(E_{D})\leq 1
in order
E_{F}
to be positive semi-definite, we have

\overline{P_{D}}\leq q^{n}\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|c_{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}.

Clearly the optimum is attained when we have equality here and therefore

\displaystyle\overline{P_{D}}^{\text{max}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{n}\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|c_{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle q^{n}\min_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}|\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}

where we used Fact
1
for the last point which gives
c_{\boldsymbol{x}}=\widehat{f}(-{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
∎

Remark 1
.

It is readily seen that the two crucial ingredients of the proof are that (i) we can take an “average” of an optimal solution to show that there is an optimal
solution where all states are discriminated with the same probability, (ii) a basis which simplifies the computation. (i) holds in a more general case where
the set of states is of the form
\{U|\psi\rangle,U\in G\}
where
G
is a finite group of unitaries. On top of that, (ii) holds for instance if the group
G
is Abelian, the nice basis
is then provided by the common diagonalization basis of the
U
’s. It other words, it is straightforward to generalize Proposition
14
in the case where the set of states
is of the form
\{U|\psi\rangle,U\in G\}
where
G
is a finite Abelian group.

4.2
Quantum polynomial time algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}
in the
q
-ary setting

The goal of the previous subsection was to extend unambiguous state discrimination to our
q
-ary setting. When we apply Proposition
15
in our case we obtain

Proposition 16

(Unambiguous state discrimination,
q
-ary case)
.

Let
\omega\leq\frac{q-1}{q}
. For each
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we define
|\psi_{a}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\omega}|a\rangle+\sum_{b\neq a}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{q-1}}|b\rangle
. There exists a POVM
\{\{E_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}},E_{F}\}
s.t.

\displaystyle\forall a\in\mathbb{F}_{q},\ \tr(E_{a}|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\textrm{P}_{\textrm{usd}}={\frac{q\cdot\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}

\displaystyle\forall a,b\neq a\in\mathbb{F}_{q},\ \tr(E_{b}|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|)

\displaystyle=0

Notice that since
\{\{E_{a}\}_{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}},E_{F}\}
is a POVM, this implies for each
a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}

\tr(E_{F}|\psi_{a}\rangle\langle\psi_{a}|)=1-\textrm{P}_{\textrm{usd}}=1-{\frac{q\cdot\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}

Proof.

We define
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}f(x)|x\rangle
with
f(0)=\sqrt{1-\omega}
and
f(x)=\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{q-1}}
for
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. With this definition,
|\psi_{a}\rangle=X_{a}|\psi\rangle
. As computed in Lemma
3
, we have

\widehat{f}(0)=\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}\quad;\quad\widehat{f}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}\ \textrm{ for }y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}.

with
\omega^{\perp}=\frac{\left(\sqrt{(q-1)(1-\omega)}-\sqrt{\omega}\right)^{2}}{q}.
One can check that for
\omega\in[0,\frac{q-1}{q}]
, we have
\hat{f}(0)\geq\hat{f}(y)
for
y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
. We use Proposition
15
with
n=1
to immediately get

\textrm{P}_{\textrm{usd}}=q\cdot\min_{y}|\hat{f}(y)|^{2}={\frac{q\cdot\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}

∎

It also turns that this operation can be implemented efficiently in poly-log time (in
q
) as shown by

Proposition 17
.

Consider the unitary
U
acting on
|\psi_{a}\rangle|0\rangle
as

\displaystyle U|\widehat{0}\rangle|0\rangle

\displaystyle=|\widehat{0}\rangle\left(u|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}|1\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\textrm{ with }u=\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{(1-\omega^{\perp})(q-1)}}

\displaystyle U|\widehat{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle

\displaystyle=|\widehat{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle

\displaystyle\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}

With our choice of function
f
, the above unambiguous state discrimination quantum measurement can be done by applying
U
on
|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle
and
and then measuring the output state in the computational basis.
This can be done in time
O({\mathsf{polylog}}(q))
.

Proof.

Let us start the proof by writing
|{\psi_{\alpha}}\rangle
in the Fourier basis
\{\widehat{x},\;x\in{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\}
. This can be done by observing that

\displaystyle|{\psi_{\alpha}}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}\cdot\QFTt\cdot\QFTt^{\dagger}|\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}\cdot\QFTt\left(\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|0\rangle+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{Q}^{*}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}|\gamma\rangle\right)\;\text{(by Lemma \ref{lemma:QFTpsi} and $\left(\omega^{\perp}\right)^{\perp}=\omega$)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\QFTt\cdot Z_{-\alpha}\left(\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|0\rangle+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{Q}^{*}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}|\gamma\rangle\right)\text{(by Lemma \ref{lemma:QFTpsib})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\QFTt\left(\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|0\rangle+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{Q}^{*}}\chi_{-\alpha}(\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}|\gamma\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|\widehat{0}\rangle+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{Q}^{*}}\chi_{-\alpha}(\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}|\widehat{\gamma}\rangle.

Applying
U
on
|\psi_{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle
, we obtain

|\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha}\rangle:=U|\Psi_{\alpha}\rangle|0\rangle=\left(u\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|\widehat{0}\rangle+\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}}\chi_{-\alpha}(\gamma)\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}|\widehat{\gamma}\rangle\right)|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|\widehat{0}\rangle|1\rangle.

Notice that
u\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}=\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}
and that
|\alpha\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\chi_{-\alpha}(\gamma)|\widehat{\gamma}\rangle
(by Fact
1
).
From there, we can rewrite

|\phi^{\prime}_{\alpha}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{q\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}|\alpha\rangle|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-u^{2}}\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}}|\widehat{0}\rangle|1\rangle.

We now measure all the qubits in the computational basis. If the last qubit is
0
, the measurement outputs the value
\alpha
in the first register. If the last qubit is
1
, we output Fail. The measurement succeeds and outputs the correct value
\alpha
w.p.
\frac{q\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}
. The time to perform
U
is essentially the time to perform two Quantum Fourier Transforms so
U
can be efficiently computed in time
O({\mathsf{polylog}}(q))
, the whole measurement can be done in time
O({\mathsf{polylog}}(q))
.
∎

We can now present our polynomial time algorithm in the
q
-ary setting:

Theorem 6
.

Let
R>0
and
\omega\in(0,\frac{q-1}{q})
satisfying
\frac{q\cdot\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}>R
. There exists a quantum algorithm that solves
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,\lfloor Rn\rfloor,\omega)
in time
{\mathsf{poly}}(n,\log(q))
.

Proof.

We fix
R>0,k=\lfloor Rn\rfloor
and
\omega\in(0,\frac{q-1}{q})
satisfying
\frac{q\omega_{\perp}}{q-1}>R
. We are given a random generating matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
with associated code
\mathcal{C}
as well as a state
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\psi_{c_{i}}\rangle
for a randomly chosen
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
, where

|\psi_{c_{i}}\rangle=\sqrt{1-\omega}|c_{i}\rangle+\sum_{x\neq c_{i}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{q-1}}|x\rangle.

As in Section
3.1
, we consider the following algorithm.

Quantum algorithm for
\mathrm{QDP}
using
q
-ary USD

1.

Perform the optimal unambiguous measurement given in Proposition
16
from Proposition
7
on each register
i
in order to guess
c_{i}
, which can be done w.p.
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{usd}}=\frac{q\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}
. Let
J\subseteq[n]
be the set of indices where this measurement succeeds. The algorithm recovers here
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}
.

2.

If
G_{J}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times|J|}
is of rank
k
, recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
from
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}
by computing
{\boldsymbol{c}}_{J}G_{J}^{-1}G
.

By our choice of
\omega
, we have
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{usd}}>R
which means that there exists an absolute constant
\gamma>0
s.t.
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{usd}}=R+\gamma
. This in turn implies that the success probability of this algorithm is
1-o(1)
, using the same arguments as in Section
3.1
.
∎

5
(In)tractability of the quantum decoding problem

In this section we provide a full characterization of the tractability of
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,\omega)
. We show that the problem is tractable
i.e.
there exists a quantum algorithm that solves the problem w.p.
1-o(1)
(as
n\rightarrow+\infty
and
q=\Omega(1)
) for any absolute constant
\omega<\left(\delta_{\textup{min}}(q,1-k/n)\right)^{\perp}
. We will simplify the notation as alluded in Subsection
2.1
and write from now on just
\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-k/n)
instead of
\delta_{\textup{min}}(q,1-k/n)
. Moreover,
R
denotes in the whole section the rate
\frac{k}{n}
of the code we decode:

R\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}k/n.

Notice here that we do not put here any restriction on the running time of the algorithm. On the other hand, we show that the problem is intractable
i.e.
all quantum algorithms solve the problem w.p. at most
o(1)
for any absolute constant
\omega>\left(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)\right)^{\perp}
.

In order to prove our results, we will focus on a single quantum algorithm: the one that performs a pretty good measurement. Recall that in the quantum decoding problem, we have to recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
from
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}}\sqrt{(\frac{\omega}{q-1})^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}(1-\omega)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}}|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
. In order to prove our results, we will focus on a single quantum algorithm performing the pretty good measurement on the Fourier transforms of these states. For the tractability result, we show that the PGM recovers
{\boldsymbol{c}}
w.p.
1-o(1)
. For the intractability result, we show that the PGM recovers
{\boldsymbol{c}}
w.p.
o(1)
. But we know from Proposition
8
that this implies that any quantum algorithm will recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
w.p.
o(1)
hence the intractability result.

We first study the PGM for any error function
f
and then apply our results to
f(\boldsymbol{e})=\sqrt{(\frac{\omega}{q-1})^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}(1-\omega)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}}
in order to show our (in)tractability results.

5.1
Computing the PGM associated to the quantum decoding problem

We fix a generating matrix
\mathbf{G}
and an associated code
\mathcal{C}
. In order to study our PGM, we define the shifted dual codes of
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}:\mathbf{G}{{\boldsymbol{x}}}^{\intercal}={\boldsymbol{s}}\}

Notice that
\mathcal{C}^{\bot}_{\mathbf{0}}=\mathcal{C}^{\bot}
where
\mathcal{C}^{\bot}
is the dual code of
\mathcal{C}
. For each shifted dual code
\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}
, we fix an element
u_{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}
. We have
\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}=\{u_{\boldsymbol{s}}+\boldsymbol{d}:\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathcal{C}^{\bot}\}
. This means that for all
{\boldsymbol{c}}
in
\mathcal{C}
and all
{\boldsymbol{y}}
in
\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}

\displaystyle\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})

Moreover, for any
{\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
s.t.
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}\neq{\boldsymbol{s}}
, since
u_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}+u_{{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}}\notin\mathcal{C}^{\bot}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}}+u_{{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}})=0

(17)

Now fix any error function
f:\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n} \rightarrow\mathbb{C}
s.t.
\lvert\kern-1.0pt\lvert f\rvert\kern-1.0pt\rvert_{2}=1
, and consider the states
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
. The goal is to recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. Actually, we will start from
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle=\QFTt{|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle}
instead of
|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
and apply the Pretty Good Measurement on the ensemble of states
\{|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle\}
. The distinguishing problem is equivalent since applying
\QFTt
is a unitary operation.
We first define the states

\displaystyle|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\bot}}\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle\quad\textrm{ (not normalized)}

\displaystyle|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\frac{|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle}{\lvert\kern-1.0pt\lvert|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\rvert\kern-1.0pt\rvert}

as well as
n_{\boldsymbol{s}}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\ \lvert\kern-1.0pt\lvert|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\rvert\kern-1.0pt\rvert=\sqrt{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\bot}}|\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}}
. We first write
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
in the
\{|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\}
basis.

Lemma 7
.

|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle
.

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}},\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}}({\boldsymbol{y}})f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{n}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{e}}({\boldsymbol{x}})f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{{\boldsymbol{s}}})\sum_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}}\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{x}})|{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle.

∎

We can now explicit the PGM associated to the states
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
.

Proposition 18
.

The PGM associated to the ensemble of states
\{|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle\}_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in C}
is the projective measurement
\{|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\langle Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}|\}_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}
where
|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle
.

Proof.

We write the PGM
\{M_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\}
associated to the states
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
using Definition
18
.

\displaystyle M_{\boldsymbol{c}}=\rho^{-1/2}|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}|\rho^{-1/2}\quad\textrm{ given }\rho=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}|

We now write

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle\langle\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}|=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}},{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}-u_{{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}})|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\langle W_{{\boldsymbol{s}}^{\prime}}|=q^{k}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\langle W_{\boldsymbol{s}}|

where we use Equation
17
as well as
\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\mathbf{0})=1
for the last equality.
Using the fact that the
|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle
are pairwise orthogonal (since they have disjoint support in the computational basis), the
|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\langle\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}|
’s are pairwise orthogonal projectors and we have

\displaystyle\rho=q^{k}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n^{2}_{\boldsymbol{s}}|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\langle\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}|\ \ \textrm{hence}\ \ \rho^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\frac{1}{{n_{\boldsymbol{s}}}}|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\langle\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}|

and

\displaystyle\rho^{-1/2}|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle:=|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle.

(18)

Here
|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
is a pure state of norm
1
. Also, notice that these states are pairwise orthogonal. So
M_{\boldsymbol{c}}=|Y_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle\langle Y_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}|
and the PGM is just the projective measurement
\{|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\langle Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}|\}_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}
.
∎

Finally, we can explicit the probability that the PGM succeeds on the states
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
.

Proposition 19
.

The PGM succeeds to recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
from
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
w.p.

\frac{1}{{q^{k}}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{\boldsymbol{s}}\right)^{2}
.

Proof.

From the previous proposition, the PGM we use is the projective measurement
\{|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\langle Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}|\}_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}
with
|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}\chi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle
.
For each
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
, we write using Lemma
7
as well as the expression of
|Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
the probability
p_{\boldsymbol{c}}
that this measurement succeeds

\displaystyle p_{\boldsymbol{c}}\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}|\langle Y_{\boldsymbol{c}}|\widehat{\psi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left|\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}\langle\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}|W_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{s}\right)^{2}

(19)

which immediately gives us the result.
∎

Remark 2
.

Since
|\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
is of norm
1
, we have immediately from Lemma
7
that
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{\boldsymbol{s}}^{2}=1
. In the case where all the norms are equal, we have
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}=\sqrt{q^{-k}}
which gives indeed
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}=1
. On the other hand, if these norms are highly unbalanced the probability that the PGM succeeds is very low.

5.2
(In)tractability results

5.2.1
First computations and probabilistic arguments on random codes

We go back to our quantum decoding problem. Our error function corresponds to the
q
-ary symmetric channel so
f(\boldsymbol{e})=\left(\sqrt{1-\omega}\right)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{q-1}}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}

we have (see Lemma
3
)

\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})=(\sqrt{1-\omega^{\perp}})^{n-|{\boldsymbol{y}}|}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}}\right)^{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|},

with

\omega^{\perp}=\frac{\left(\sqrt{(q-1)(1-\omega)}-\sqrt{\omega}\right)^{2}}{q}.
For a fixed
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
and associated code
\mathcal{C}
(we will not make this dependency explicit in the notation to simplify it), we define

\displaystyle n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}

\displaystyle\lvert\kern-1.0pt\lvert\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}}\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle\rvert\kern-1.0pt\rvert

\displaystyle a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)

\displaystyle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}

\displaystyle\left|\{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\bot}_{\boldsymbol{s}}:|{\boldsymbol{y}}|=t\}\right|

We also define
S(t)\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\frac{(q-1)^{t}\binom{n}{t}}{q^{k}}
. Notice that
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}
corresponds exactly to
n_{\boldsymbol{s}}
defined in the previous section but we made the dependency in
\mathcal{C}
explicit. Our goal is to compute the success probability of the PGM on average on
\mathbf{G}
so using Proposition
19
, we want to bound the quantity

\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}\right].

We first write

\displaystyle n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}}|\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}=\sum_{t=0}^{n}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}

(20)

and recall from Remark
2
that
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=1
. We see that to compute
P_{PGM}
, we have to say something about the terms
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(t,\mathcal{C})
. We first have the following, which was proven for example in
[
Deb23
]
:

Proposition 20
.

\forall t\neq 0,\ {\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}(t,\mathcal{C})\right]=S(t)
.

But the expected value will not be enough. We will need concentration bounds coming from the second moment technique

Proposition 21

(Second moment technique, Proposition 3 from
[
Deb23
]
)
.

Fix any
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
and
t\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\Pr_{G}\left[|a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)-S(t)|\geq\varepsilon S(t)\right]\leq\frac{q-1}{\varepsilon^{2}S(t)}.

In particular, take
\varepsilon=S(t)^{-1/3}
, we have

\Pr_{G}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq S(t)(1-\frac{1}{S(t)^{1/3}})\right]\leq\frac{q-1}{S(t)^{1/3}}.

We can now observe two things

1.

From the above proposition combined with Equation
20
, we have that when
S(t)
is exponential, which happens when
t=\gamma n
with
\gamma\in(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R),\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R))

n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}\approx\sum_{t=\lfloor\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)n\rfloor}^{\lceil\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R)n\rceil}S(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}.

(21)

2.

In order to estimate the above sum, first notice that

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{n}S(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\bot}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\bot})^{n-t}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}\sum_{t=0}^{n}\binom{n}{t}(\omega^{\bot})^{t}(1-\omega^{\bot})^{n-t}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}.

(22)

But the above sum is actually the cumulative sum
of the binomial distribution with parameters
n
and
\omega^{\perp}
. It concentrates around the weight
n\omega^{\perp}
. This is formalized by the following proposition

Proposition 22
.

For any absolute constant
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{t=\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}-\varepsilon)n\rfloor}^{\lceil(\omega^{\perp}+\varepsilon)n\rceil}S(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left(1-o(1)\right).

(23)

We now have all the tools for our (in)tractability proofs. The main idea is the following: when
t=\omega n
with
\omega<\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)^{\bot}
,
we have
\omega^{\perp}\in(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R),\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R))
and so we can combine Equations
21
,
23
to show that for most
\mathbf{G}
,
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}(1-o(1))
. On the other hand, when
\omega>\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)^{\bot}
,
we have
\omega^{\perp}\notin(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R),\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R))
and so we can combine Equations
21
,
22
,
23
to show that for most
\mathbf{G}
,
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=o(1)
. The next sections will make these arguments formal and show how this allows us to conclude.

5.2.2
Tractability

We use the notations previously defined in Section
5.2.1
.
\omega
will be considered as a fixed constant in
(0,1)
. Our main claim is the following

Proposition 23
.

If
\omega<(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R))^{\bot}

then
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}=\frac{1}{q^{k}}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}\right]=1-o(1)
.

Proof.

Using (
20
)
we know that

n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=\sum_{t=0}^{n}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}.

Since
\omega<\left(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)\right)^{\perp}
, we have
\omega^{\perp}>\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)
so we fix
\delta>0
s.t.
\omega^{\perp}-\delta>\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R).
We therefore write

n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}\geq\sum_{t=\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}-\delta)n\rfloor}^{\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}+\delta)n\rfloor}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}.

We define
t_{0}=\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}-\delta)n\rfloor
and
t_{1}=\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}+\delta)n\rfloor
.
Recall that
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)
is typically close to
S(t)
as shown by Lemma
21
.
This gives for
t\in\left(\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}-\delta)n\rfloor,\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}+\delta)n\rfloor\right)

\displaystyle\Pr_{G}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq S(t)(1-\frac{1}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{G}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq S(t)(1-\frac{1}{S(t)^{1/3}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{q-1}{S(t)^{1/3}}\leq\frac{q-1}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}}.

and

\Pr_{G}\left[\forall t\in\llbracket t_{0},t_{1}\rrbracket,\ a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\geq S(t)(1-\frac{1}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}})\right]\geq 1-\frac{(q-1)(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}}

This implies

\displaystyle\Pr_{G}\left[n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}\geq\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}S(t)(1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3})\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}\right]\geq 1-\frac{(q-1)(t_{1}-t_{0}+1)}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}}.

(24)

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}S(t)(1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3})\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}

\displaystyle=(1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3})\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}S(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3})}{q^{k}}K(n)

where
K(n)\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\sum_{t=t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\binom{n}{t}\left(\omega^{\perp}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}
and
K(n)=1-o(1)
as
n
tends to infinity
by Proposition
22
.
By plugging this equality in the left-hand side of (
24
) we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{G}\left[n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3}}{q^{k}}K(n)}\right]\geq 1-\frac{(q-1)(t_{1}-t_{0})}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}}.

Since
t_{0}=\lfloor(\omega^{\perp}-\delta)n\rfloor
with
\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)<(\omega^{\perp}-\delta)<\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R)
, we have
S(t_{0})=q^{\Omega(n)}
which implies

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{G}[n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}]

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3}}{q^{k}}K(n)}\cdot\Pr_{G}\left[n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3}}{q^{k}}K(n)}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\sqrt{\frac{1-S(t_{0})^{-1/3}}{q^{k}}K(n)}\cdot\left(1-\frac{(q-1)(t_{1}-t_{0})}{S(t_{0})^{1/3}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\left(1-o(1)\right)

which gives

\displaystyle{\mathsf{E}}_{G}[\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}]\geq\sqrt{q^{k}}(1-o(1))

(25)

In order to conclude, we use Jensen’s inequality
{\mathsf{E}}_{G}(X^{2})\geq({\mathsf{E}}_{G}(X))^{2}

and Equation
25
to get

\displaystyle P_{PGM}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{k}}{\mathsf{E}}_{G}\left[\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left({\mathsf{E}}_{G}\left[\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right]\right)^{2}\geq 1-o(1)

∎

5.2.3
Intractability

Again, we use the same notation as in the previous sections with a fixed
\omega\in(0,1)
. We show that if
\omega
is too large
then

P\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}(\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}})
is an
o(1)
as shown by

Theorem 7
.

Let
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
be a random generating matrix and
\mathcal{C}
be the associated code. Let
\omega>\left(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)\right)^{\perp}
and let the states
|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
with

f(\boldsymbol{e})=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\omega}{q-1}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}\left(1-\omega\right)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}}.

The pretty good measurement distinguishes the states
|\psi_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
w.p.
P=o(1)
.

Again, we will heavily build on the expression of
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}
given by (
20
) in terms of the
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)
’s. The proof is based on the following steps

Step 1.

Let us start by giving an upper-bound on
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)
which holds with probability close to
1
for large values of
K

Lemma 8
.

For any
K>0
, any
t
in
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
and any
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, we have

\Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\geq K{\cdot}S(t)\right]\leq\frac{1}{K}

which directly implies

\Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq K{\cdot}S(t)\right]\geq 1-\frac{1}{K}.

(26)

Proof.

This is just Markov’s inequality

\Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq K{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}(a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t))\right]\leq\frac{1}{K}
by recalling that

{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}(a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t))=S(t)
.
∎

A rather immediate corollary of this result is that

Corollary 1
.

For any
\delta>0
,
{\boldsymbol{s}}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
and
t
in

\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\setminus[(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)-\delta)n,(\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R)+\delta)n]
, we have with probability
1-q^{-\Omega\left(n\right)}
that
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)=0
.

Proof.

In such a case we have
S(t)=q^{-\Omega\left(n\right)}
, since

S(t)\leq q^{n(h_{q}(t/n)-k/n)}
and we use Lemma
8
with
K=\frac{1}{\sqrt{S(t)}}
to obtain

\Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq\sqrt{S(t)}\right]\geq 1-\sqrt{S(t)}=1-q^{-\Omega(n)}.

We can conclude by using the fact that
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)
is an non negative integer so if
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\leq\sqrt{S(t)}<1
then necessarily
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)=0
.
∎

Step 2.

The previous results allow to show that

Lemma 9
.

Let
\omega>\left(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)\right)^{\perp}
. There exists an
\varepsilon>0
such that for any
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
which is non zero we have

\Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\geq q^{-k/2-\varepsilon n}\right]=q^{-\Omega\left(n\right)}.

Proof.

Let us recall (
20
)

n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{C}_{{\boldsymbol{s}}}^{\perp}}|\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|^{2}=\sum_{t=0}^{n}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}.

By using Corollary
1
and
a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(0)=0
for
{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq 0
, we obtain that for any absolute constant
\delta>0
,

\forall{\boldsymbol{s}}\in F_{q}^{k}\backslash{\{\mathbf{0}\}},\ \Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[n^{2}_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}=\sum_{t=\left\lfloor(\delta_{\textup{min}}-\delta)n\right\rfloor}^{\left\lceil(\delta_{\textup{max}}+\delta)n\right\rceil}a_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}\right]\geq 1-q^{\Omega(n)},

(27)

where to simplify notation we simply write
\delta_{\textup{min}}
and
\delta_{\textup{max}}
for
\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-R)
and
\delta_{\textup{max}}(1-R)
respectively.
Then by using Lemma
8
we also deduce that for any
\delta,\delta^{\prime}>0
,

\forall{\boldsymbol{s}}\in F_{q}^{k}\backslash{\{\mathbf{0}\}},\ \Pr_{\mathbf{G}}\left[n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}\leq\sum_{t=\left\lfloor(\delta_{\textup{min}}-\delta)n\right\rfloor}^{\left\lceil(\delta_{\textup{max}}+\delta)n\right\rceil}q^{\delta^{\prime}n}S(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}\right]\geq 1-q^{-\Omega(n)}

We observe now that

\sum_{t=\left\lfloor(\delta_{\textup{min}}-\delta)n\right\rfloor}^{\left\lceil(\delta_{\textup{max}}+\delta)n\right\rceil}q^{\delta^{\prime}n}S(t)\left(\frac{\omega^{\perp}}{q-1}\right)^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}=q^{\delta^{\prime}n-k}\sum_{t=\left\lfloor(\delta_{\textup{min}}-\delta)n\right\rfloor}^{\left\lceil(\delta_{\textup{max}}+\delta)n\right\rceil}p(t)

(28)

where
p(t)\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\binom{n}{t}({\omega^{\perp}})^{t}(1-\omega^{\perp})^{n-t}
is the probability that a binomial variable of parameters
n
and
\omega^{\perp}
takes the
value
t
. By using the fact that
\omega^{\perp}\leq(\delta_{\textup{min}}-\delta")n
for some
\delta">0
and the Hoeffding inequality (see Lemma
1
) we
deduce that for
\delta=\delta"/2
, it holds that

\sum_{t=\left\lfloor(\delta_{\textup{min}}-\delta)n\right\rfloor}^{\left\lceil(\delta_{\textup{max}}+\delta)n\right\rceil}p(t)\leq q^{-\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}n}
for some
\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}>0
. By choosing
\delta^{\prime}<\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}
, we obtain that
n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}
is less than
q^{-k/2-\frac{\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}-\delta^{\prime}}{2}n}
with probability
1-q^{-\Omega\left(n\right)}
. We just have to choose
\varepsilon=(\delta^{\prime\prime\prime}-\delta^{\prime})/2
to finish the proof.
∎

We are ready now to prove Theorem
7
.

Proof of Theorem
7
.

For
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}
, let
G_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\{\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}:n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\geq q^{-k/2-\varepsilon n}\}
. Also, for
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
, let
S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})=\{{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq 0:n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\geq q^{-k/2-\varepsilon n}\}
. The previous lemma tells us that
\forall{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq\mathbf{0},|G_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{s}})|=o(|G|)
where
|G|=q^{nk}
is the total number of possible matrices
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
. Now, notice that

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq 0}|G_{\varepsilon}({\boldsymbol{s}})|=\left|\{(\mathbf{G},{\boldsymbol{s}}):n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\geq q^{-k/2-\varepsilon n}\}\right|=\sum_{\mathbf{G}}|S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})|.

This implies that
\sum_{G\in\mathbf{G}}|S_{\varepsilon}(G)|=o(|G|q^{k})
and
{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}[|S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})|]=o(q^{k})
. Now fix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
. We write

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(n_{\mathbf{0,\mathcal{C}}}+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\notin S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}\leq 3n^{2}_{\mathbf{0},\mathcal{C}}+3\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}+3\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\notin S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}

(29)

\displaystyle\leq 3n^{2}_{\mathbf{0},\mathcal{C}}+3|S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})|\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq\mathbf{0}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}+3\left(q^{k/2-\varepsilon n}\right)^{2}

(30)

\displaystyle\leq 3|S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})|+o(q^{k})

(31)

Here (
29
) follows from the inequality
(x+y+z)^{2}\leq 3x^{2}+3y^{2}+3z^{2}
(which can be proved by noticing that

3x^{2}+3y^{2}+3z^{2}-(x+y+z)^{2}=(x-y)^{2}+(y-z)^{2}+(x-z)^{2}
). (
30
) follows from the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and
(
31
) is a consequence of
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}^{2}=1
which also gives
n^{2}_{\mathbf{0},\mathcal{C}}\leq 1
.
In order to conclude, we write

\displaystyle P={\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}\left[\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\in F_{q}^{k}}n_{{\boldsymbol{s}},\mathcal{C}}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{q^{k}}\left(3{\mathsf{E}}_{\mathbf{G}}[|S_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{G})|]+o(q^{k})\right)=o(1).

∎

6
From the quantum decoding problem to the short codeword problem

In this section, we show how to apply our algorithm for the quantum decoding problem into Regev’s reduction in order to obtain quantum algorithms for the short codeword problem.

We fix
n,k^{\prime},q\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\geq 2
as well as
\omega^{\prime}\in(0,1)
. We start from a random instance
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k^{\prime}\times n}
of
\mbox{SCP}(q,n,k^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the code associated to
\mathbf{G}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})^{\perp}
the dual code of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. The idea will be to solve a quantum decoding problem associated to
\mathcal{C}
,
i.e.
from the state

|\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle
where
{\boldsymbol{c}}
belongs to
\mathcal{C}
, we want to recover
{\boldsymbol{c}}
. Then we apply the quantum Fourier transform and measure in the computational basis to obtain a short codeword of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. We also define
k=n-k^{\prime}
and

\displaystyle\omega\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}(\omega^{\prime})^{\perp}=\frac{\left(\sqrt{(q-1)(1-{\omega^{\prime}})}-\sqrt{{\omega^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}}{q}\quad;\quad f(\boldsymbol{e})\mathop{=}\limits^{\triangle}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\omega}{q-1}}\right)^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}\left(\sqrt{1-{\omega}}\right)^{n-|\boldsymbol{e}|}

Recall also, using
\omega^{\perp}={\omega^{\prime}}
that

\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\omega^{\prime}}}{q-1}}\right)^{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|}\left(\sqrt{1-{{\omega^{\prime}}}}\right)^{n-|{\boldsymbol{y}}|}.

Remark.

We use this notation

so that the problem we reduce to is a
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,\omega)
with a generating matrix
\mathbf{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{k\times n}
. This allows us to keep notation consistent with the previous section but be aware that the Short Codeword problem we are solving is on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
.

6.1
Regev’s reduction for codes

We now describe Regev’s reduction for codes. As we will see, this does not necessarily give a reduction from the short codeword problem to the quantum decoding problem because of the small error in the quantum decoding algorithm. We consider the formulation of this reduction from

We first construct

This follows from Proposition

Finally, we measure this state in the computational basis and hope to find a small codeword.

•

1.

(b)

(c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{C}|}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathsf{S}}}\beta_{{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{y}}}|{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle|Z_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle

\displaystyle|\Omega_{4}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{C}|}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathsf{S}}}\beta_{{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{y}}}|{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle|Z_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\rangle|{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle=\left(\sqrt{P_{PGM}}-\frac{n_{0}}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\right)|{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{C}|}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}|Z_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{C}|}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathsf{S}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\neq{\boldsymbol{c}}}\beta_{{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{y}}}|{\boldsymbol{c}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle|Z_{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle

where in the last line, we dropped in the last equality the third register and we used that
\beta_{{\boldsymbol{c}},{\boldsymbol{c}}}=\left(\sqrt{P_{PGM}}-\frac{n_{0}}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\right)
for each
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}
. This means that when we measure the first register, we obtain
\mathbf{0}
w.p.
\left(\sqrt{\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}}-\frac{n_{0}}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\right)^{2}
, and the resulting state is
|\Omega_{5}\rangle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C}}|Z_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle=|\bot\rangle
which shows that the reduction entirely fails in this case.

6.3.2
A measurement that works

Finally, we show a measurement that will make the reduction work when
\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}=1-o(1)
. The idea is similar to the one of Section
6.3.1
. We add an extra outcome
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\backslash\mathcal{C}
and define
{\mathsf{S}}=\mathcal{C}\cup\{\boldsymbol{u}\}
. We now define

\displaystyle|U_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:{\boldsymbol{y}}\neq\mathbf{0}}\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle}{\lvert\kern-1.0pt\lvert\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:{\boldsymbol{y}}\neq\mathbf{0}}\widehat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle\rvert\kern-1.0pt\rvert}

\displaystyle\forall{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\ |{Z}_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}\left(|U_{0}\rangle+\sum_{{\boldsymbol{s}}\neq\mathbf{0}}\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(u_{\boldsymbol{s}})|\widetilde{W}_{\boldsymbol{s}}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle|Z_{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle

\displaystyle=|\mathbf{0}\rangle

The
|Z_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle
are exactly the states
|Y_{{\boldsymbol{c}}}\rangle
of the pretty good measurement but we removed the
|\mathbf{0}\rangle
component of
|\widetilde{W}_{0}\rangle
. As in the previous subsection, the
|Z_{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle
are orthogonal. In order to make the measurement complete, we added the extra basis element
|Z_{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle=|{\boldsymbol{0}}\rangle
. We therefore have a projective measurement
\{|Z_{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\rangle\}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathsf{S}}}
.
Again, we have

\langle\widehat{\psi_{\boldsymbol{c}}}|{Z}_{\boldsymbol{c}}\rangle\geq\sqrt{\textrm{P}_{\textrm{PGM}}}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q^{k}}}

and independent of
{\boldsymbol{c}}
so w.p. at least

, we get the state
|U_{0}\rangle
. Then, if we measure this state
|U_{0}\rangle
, we will get a codeword of weight
t
w.p.

p(t)=\frac{a(t)|\widehat{f}(t)|^{2}}{\sum_{t\neq 0}a(t)|\widehat{f}(t)|^{2}},\ \forall t\neq 0

and
p(0)=0
, where

is the number of codewords of weight

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
. Recall that

and we are in the regime where

\omega<(\delta_{\textup{min}}(1-\frac{k}{n}))^{\bot}

\omega^{\prime}>\delta_{\textup{min}}(\frac{n-k}{n})=\delta_{\textup{min}}(\frac{k^{\prime}}{n})

Using Proposition
22

\widehat{f}(t)

Discussion.

These
3
examples above show that it is very easy to slightly modify the algorithm for solving the quantum decoding problem and drastically change the result after Regev’s reduction. We therefore cannot have proper reduction theorems between the quantum decoding problem and the short codeword problem but we have to analyze on a case by case basis whether an algorithm for the quantum decoding problem can be used for finding a short codeword. On the positive side of the reduction, we can summarize our results as follows:

Proposition 24
.

Let
q,n,k\in\mathbb{N}
with
q\geq 2
and
\omega\in(0,1)
. Let also
R=\lfloor\frac{k}{n}\rfloor
,
\omega^{\prime}=\omega^{\perp}
and
k^{\prime}=n-k
.

•

For
\omega<(\frac{q-1R}{q})^{\perp}
, there exists a quantum algorithm running in time
{\mathsf{poly}}(n,\log(q))
that solves
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,\omega)
w.p.
1-o(1)
(Theorem
6
) . Moreover, this algorithm can be used using Regev’s reduction to solve
\mbox{SCP}(q,n,k^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
in time
poly(n,\log(q))
(Section
6.2
).

•

For
\omega<(\delta_{\min}(1-R))^{\perp}
, there exists a quantum algorithm (for which we don’t specify the running time but which could be exponential in
n
) that solves
\mathrm{QDP}(q,n,k,\omega)
w.p.
1-o(1)
(Proposition
23
). This algorithm can be (slightly tweaked but with success probability still
1-o(1)
) and used in Regev’s reduction to solve
\mbox{SCP}(q,n,k^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
w.p.
\Theta(1)
(Section
6.3.2
).

Appendix A
General phases

We consider more general error functions
f(\boldsymbol{e})=\sqrt{1-t}^{(n-|\boldsymbol{e}|)}(e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t})^{|\boldsymbol{e}|}
with
t\in[0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, which means we consider the states

|\psi_{f}({\boldsymbol{c}})\rangle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{e}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{1-t}|c_{i}\rangle+e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}|1-c_{i}\rangle.

Again, we consider unambiguous states discrimination between the following two states

|\zeta_{0}\rangle=\sqrt{1-t}|0\rangle+e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}|1\rangle\quad;\quad|\zeta_{1}\rangle=e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-t}|1\rangle.

We have
|\langle\zeta_{0}|\zeta_{1}\rangle|=|\sqrt{t(1-t)}(e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta})|=2\sqrt{t(1-t)}|\cos(\theta)|
. From there, we have that decoding with this unambiguous measurement is possible w.h.p as long as

\displaystyle 1-2\sqrt{t(1-t)}|\cos(\theta)|>\frac{k}{n}

(34)

Now, what do we get in the dual? Let

|\psi_{f}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in\mathcal{C},\boldsymbol{e}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}f(\boldsymbol{e})|{\boldsymbol{c}}+\boldsymbol{e}\rangle

where
Z
is a normalizing constant.
We write

\widehat{|\psi_{f}\rangle}=\frac{2^{k}}{\sqrt{2^{n}\cdot Z}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathcal{C}^{\bot}}\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})|{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle.

Moreover, we write

\displaystyle\widehat{|\zeta_{0}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}+e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right)|0\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}-e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right)|1\rangle

\displaystyle\widehat{|\zeta_{1}\rangle}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}+e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right)|0\rangle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}-e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right)|1\rangle

This means that
\hat{f}({\boldsymbol{y}})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}+e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right)^{(n-|{\boldsymbol{y}}|)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sqrt{1-t}-e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right)^{|{\boldsymbol{y}}|}
.
The probability
p(t,\theta)
to measure
1
on each coordinate in the above is given by

\displaystyle p(t,\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sqrt{1-t}-e^{i\theta}\sqrt{t}\right|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left((\sqrt{1-t}-\sqrt{t}\cos(\theta))^{2}+t\sin^{2}(\theta)\right)

(35)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-2\sqrt{t(1-t)}\cos(\theta)\right)

(36)

In the case Equation
34
is saturated, meaning
1-2\sqrt{t(1-t)}|\cos(\theta)|\approx\frac{k}{n}
, we have
p(t,\theta)=\frac{k}{2n}
as long as
\cos(\theta)\geq 0
(otherwise, we have the symmetric for large weights) which is Prange’s bound. Notice that the above only works when Equation
34
can be saturated, so we can not take
\theta=\pi/2
for example.
