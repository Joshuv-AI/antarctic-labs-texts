Special polynomials, interlacing, and new real-rootedness results
Aurelien Gribinski Jussieu, Inria (Paris)

Abstract
. In this paper, we exhibit new monotonicity properties of roots of families of orthogonal polynomials P n ( z ) ​ ( x ) P_{n}^{(z)}(x) depending polynomially on a parameter (Laguerre and Gegenbauer). We show that P n ( z ) ​ ( x ) P_{n}^{(z)}(x) are realrooted in z z for x x in the support of orthogonality. As an application we show realrootedness in x x and interlacing properties of ∂ z k P n ( z ) ​ ( x ) \partial_{z}^{k}P_{n}^{(z)}(x) for k ≤ n k\leq n and z ≥ 0 z\geq 0 , establishing a dual approach to orthogonality.



1.
Introduction

Orthogonal families of polynomials like generalized Laguerre and Gegenbauer polynomials have long been studied, and show up in all fields of mathematics and physics. But little has been said about the properties of such polynomials when we vary the underlying parameter (see Chapter 6 in
[
8
]
for a review). Asymptotic density of generalized Laguerre roots when parameters are negative was recently investigated in
[
5
]
, pseudo orthogonality properties of Gegenbauer polynomials with negative parameters were considered in
[
1
]
, and asymptotic results on root densities with sliding parameter were obtained in
[
6
]
. However, we believe that a bivariate approach was missing until now to try and understand adequately the behaviour of roots when the parameters evolves.

We study families of generalized orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}^{(z)}(x)
depending on a parameter
z
from a new angle, that is, as bivariate polynomials
P_{n}(x,z)
. We fix the usual variable
x
and consider them instead as polynomials in
z
, and in the following
P_{n}^{(z)}(x)
will refer to generic orthogonal polynomials, either Gegenbauer or Laguerre. This multivariate approach as well as the interlacing techniques we develop stem from the work of Borcea and Branden on real stable polynomials (see
[
2
]
).
Our results and approach extends the seminal works of Stieljes, Sturm and many others on basic properties of roots of fundamental polynomials.

Let us recall standard definitions.

Definition 1.1

(Generalized Laguerre and Gegenbauer families)
.

We define the generalized Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{(z)}(x)
(alternatively referred to as
L_{n}(x,z)
in the paper) with parameter
z
as the degree
n
solution of Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric differential equation :

xy^{\prime\prime}(x)+(z+1-x)y^{\prime}(x)+ny(x)=0.

Alternatively, we define the Gegenbauer(ultraspehrical) polynomials
G_{n}^{(z)}(x)
(alternatively referred to as
G_{n}(x,z)
in the paper) with parameter
z
as the degree
n
solution of the hypergeometric differential equation:

(1-x^{2})y^{\prime\prime}(x)-(2z+1)xy^{\prime}(x)+n(n+2z)y(x)=0.

These families share many strong properties as polynomials in
x
due to the orthogonality aspect.

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem 3.3.1 in
[
8
]
)
.

The
P_{n}^{(z)}(x)
form families of orthogonal polynomials with simple roots on respectively
[0,+\infty]
(Laguerre) and
[-1,1]
(Gegenbauer), with respective orthogonality weight densities
e^{-x}x^{z}
(Laguerre) and
(1-x^{2})^{z-1/2}
(Gegenbauer).

We can relate different degree polynomials of the family through the concept of interlacing; let us recall it’s meaning:

Definition 1.3
.

Assume that a polynomial
p
is of degree
n
and has real roots
p_{1}\geq p_{2}\dots\geq p_{n}
, and
q
of degree
n
or
n-1
has roots
q_{1}\geq q_{2}\dots\geq q_{n-1}(\geq q_{n})
.We say that they are interlacing if

p_{1}\geq q_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq q_{2}\dots.\geq p_{n-1}\geq q_{n-1}\geq p_{n}(\geq q_{n})

or if the equivalent inequality permuting the roles of
p
and
q
holds in case the polynomials have the same degree. We also say that the interlacing is strict when all inequalities above are strict, meaning that they have distinct simple roots.

It is a classical result that:

Theorem 1.4

(Interlacing in
x
, see Theorem 3.3.2 in
[
8
]
)
.

P_{n}^{(z)}(x)
and
P_{n-1}^{(z)}(x)
interlace strictly, as well as
P_{n}^{(z)}(x)
and
\partial_{x}P_{n-1}^{(z)}(x)
.

We will see in this paper a dual version of this result when we consider polynomials in
z
and fix
x
as a parameter instead.
We start by recalling a well-known monotonicity result.

Theorem 1.5

(Monotonicity of the roots with respect to the parameter, see Theorem 6.12.2 and Theorem 6.21.1 in
[
8
]
)
.

Take
z\geq 0
.
If
x_{i}(z)
are the roots of
L_{n}^{(z)}(x)
, then they are increasing with
z
:
\frac{d}{dz}x_{i}(z)>0
,
i\leq n
. Also, the positive roots
y_{i}(z)
of
G_{n}^{(z)}(x)
are decreasing with
z
:
\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}(z)<0
(the negative ones are increasing by symmetry). More precisely (see Remarks
4
and
6
in Section 2 below for a proof):
\lim_{z\to+\infty}x_{i}(z)=+\infty
and
\lim_{z\to+\infty}y_{i}(z)=0
.

This result gives us partial information on roots in
z
, see Remarks
1
and
2
. However it is not sufficient and we will reprove this monotonicity in a different way without using this monotonicity in

.

In Section 2, we show that
P_{n}^{(z)}(x)
are real-rooted in
z
for
x
in the support of the underlying measure of orthogonality, and we exhibit remarkable monotonicity.The whole difficulty of the problem lies in the fact that little is known about regularity properties (as orthogonality fails) when

.

Theorem 1.6
.

For fixed
x_{0}\in[0,+\infty[
,
L_{n}^{(z)}(x_{0})
is a real-rooted polynomial in
z
of degree
n
with simple roots. Furthermore, its roots in
z
are increasing to

when
x_{0}\to+\infty
.

Remark 1
.

According to Theorem
1.5
) the roots
x_{i}(z)
of
L_{n}^{(z)}(x)
are strictly increasing (to infinity) when the parameter
z
increases starting at

. So for a fixed
x_{0}>\mathrm{maxroot}\left\{L_{n}^{(0)}\right\}
(largest root of the polynonial), then we can trivially conclude that
L_{n}^{(z)}(x_{0})
will have
n
distinct roots in
z
that are positive (we just have to follow the flow of the roots

starting at the roots of
L_{n}^{(0)}
,
x_{i}(0)
, when

is increasing and each one will at some point by the intermediate value theorem cross

while increasing to infinity, yielding some corresponding

roots). However, our theorem applies to the nontrivial case when

, and in this case there are negative roots in

which can’t be found using such a monotonicity.

Theorem 1.7
.

For fixed
x_{0}\in[-1,1]
,
G_{n}^{(z)}(x_{0})
is a real-rooted polynomial in
z
of degree at most
n
(exactly

and with simple roots except for

). Furthermore, its roots in

are increasing for

, and decreasing for

when

).

Remark 2
.

In this case, the roots in
x
split in two groups, the positive and negative ones, which behave monotonously for

(half increasing, half decreasing, see Theorem

) . When

increases to infinity, roots move towards zero and therefore for

close to zero we can conclude that half of the roots in

will be positive again (and half negative by symmetry) using the intermediate value theorem as in Remark

when moving

and following a fixed root flow in

. We can again easily get realrootedness in

but it is only true for

(by definition, the minimum positive root). When
|x_{0}|
is close to

, this reasoning fails.

Note that the intervals on which we prove that they are realrooted are exactly the support of orthogonality of these polynomials.
These results are obtained in Section 2 through a whole range of new interlacing properties in both variables. Such results are intriguing dual versions of the classical Theorems
1.4
.

Theorem 1.8

(Dual Laguerre interlacing)
.

For
x\in[0,+\infty[
,
L_{n}(x,z)
has
n
simple roots in
z
and it interlaces strictly with
L_{n-1}(x,z)
in

. The same holds for
L_{n}(x,z)
and
\partial_{x}L_{n}(x,z)
.

For the Gegenbauer case, we need to isolate

.

Theorem 1.9

(Dual Gegenbauer interlacing)
.

For
x\in 0\in[-1,0[\cup]0,1]
,
G_{n}(x,z)
has
n
simple real roots in
z
and interlaces strictly
G_{n-1}(x,z)
and
\partial_{x}G_{n}(x,z)
in

. At

, one of the two polynomials is identically zero, so depending on the conventions we could still say that interlacing happens.

To prove

We show in Section 3 how to use such results to analyze
P_{n}(x,z)
as bivariate polynomials and consider new derived families of polynomials. More specifically, we prove that when we differentiate these orthogonal polynomials with respect to
z\geq 0
and consider them as polynomials in
x
, then they are realrooted in
x
. Such families of polynomials (derivatives with respect to the parameter) seem to have many nice properties similar to their corresponding orthogonal polynomials from which they are derived and yet have never been studied.

Theorem 1.10
.

Consider
z\geq 0
.

\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
and more generally
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
for all
k\leq n
are real-rooted in
x
; additionally their roots are increasing in
z
and they form a strict interlacing family of decreasing degree, in the sense that
\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}(x,z)
interlaces strictly
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
.

The strict analogue holds for Gegenbauer families:

Theorem 1.11
.

Consider again
z\geq 0
.

\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
and more generally
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
for all
k\leq n
are real-rooted in
x
; additionally the positive roots are decreasing in
z
, their symmetric negative counterpart increasing in
z
, and they form an interlacing family of decreasing degree:
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
interlaces
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
.

Remark 3
.

Let us remark that taking
k\leq n
is not restrictive, as
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
and
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
are identically zero if
k>n
.

We are certain that such a phenomenon is quite general and doesn’t restrict to these two families of hypergeometric functions, but the proof might need to be adapted (especially for discrete orthogonal polynomials). As we will see for the Laguerre and Gegenbauer cases, proofs are similar but not identical and there are some differences that made it impossible to prove both at the same time. Which suggests that a more general proof could be replicated from our proof, using interlacings and solving ordinary differential equations on roots of polynomials(see Section 2). We should also point out that this bivariate point of view on such polynomials came up while studying a continuous extension of polynomial convolutions considered in
[
4
]
themselves extending the ones from
[
7
]
. Gegenbauer and generalized Laguerre polynomials are extremal polynomials in finite free probability (see
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
), and differentiating with respect to

has a meaning in the framework of finite free probability. But we chose to keep such a connexion for a future paper, as these results themselves are relevant in the theory of orthogonal polynomials independently.

2.
Realrootedness in
z
, from interlacing monotonicity to ODE on the roots

The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.6
and Theorem
1.7
jointly.
Consider
P_{n}(x,z)
, a family of polynomials depending on a parameter
z
. We want to show that they are real-rooted in
z
for a fixed
x
in a given interval.

The strategy goes as follows:

•

We check that for
x
at one of the extreme point of the interval it is real-rooted.

•

We show that locally around this extreme point the roots in
z
are all monotonous.

•

We show that there can’t be a shared zero in
z
for
\partial_{x}P_{n}(x,z)
and
P_{n}(x,z)
or equivalently for
P_{n-1}(x,z)
and
P_{n}(x,z)
.

•

We show that the roots in
z
of
P_{n}(x,z)
and simultaneously
P_{n-1}(x,z)
and
\partial_{x}P_{n}(x,z)
interlace and are monotonous as long as they are well-defined.

•

We extend the local properties by exhibiting an ODE to which all roots in
z
are solutions and show that there has to be explosion at the other extreme point of the interval, all roots evolving monotonously along the way.

2.1.
Set up and Initialization: monotonicity and realrootedness around the extreme points

2.2.
Set up and notations

Let us first recall the explicit expansions of our families of polynomials.

Let’s start with the Laguerre hypergeometric confluent definition.

\displaystyle L_{n}^{(z)}(x)

\displaystyle:=\binom{n+z}{n}M(-n,z+1,x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{z+n+1-l}{l}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}n!}{(n-k)!\prod_{l=0}^{k-1}(z+1+k)}x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\prod_{l=1}^{n}(z+l)}{(n-k)!\prod_{l=1}^{k}(z+l)}x^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(z+j)}{(n-k)!}x^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n!}z^{n}+\Big(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}j}{n!}-\frac{x}{(n-1)!}\Big)z^{n-1}+R_{n-2}(z,x)

where
R_{n-2}(z,x)
is of degree lower than
n-2
in
z
.

Remark 4
.

We can prove using this expansion that
\lim_{z\to+\infty}x_{i}(z)=+\infty
for all
i
. Indeed, by contradiction and by monotonicity of
x_{i}(z)
, it would mean that
x_{i}(z)
is bounded. But then in the expansion above we get
L_{n}^{(z)}(x)\sim_{z\to\infty}\frac{1}{n!}z^{n}
as the lower orders are negligible ( powers in
x_{i}(z)
are bounded), and we get a contradiction as the left hand side is zero and the right hand side diverges.

We will henceforth write
L_{n}(x,z)
as it is clearly a bivariate polynomial of degree
n
in
z
with front coefficient
\frac{1}{n!}
.
Let us furthermore decompose
L_{n}(x,z)
using a priori complex roots
\lambda_{i}(x)
:

L_{n}(x,z)=\frac{1}{n!}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\big(z-\lambda_{i}(x)\big)

where we order the roots by decreasing module:
|\lambda_{1}(x)|\geq|\lambda_{2}(x)|\geq\dots\geq|\lambda_{n}(x)|
.

As the Gegenbauer polynomials are even or odd in
x
, that is
G_{n}^{(z)}(-x)=(-1)^{n}G_{n}^{(z)}(x)
, it is enough to prove our statement for
x\in[-1,0[
: all monotonicity properties get reversed for
x\in]0,1]
. Let us recall the hypergeometric expression:

\displaystyle G_{n}^{(z)}(x)

\displaystyle:=\prod_{l=0}^{n-1}(2z+l)\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{1}{k!(n-k)!}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(2z+n+i)}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(z+1/2+i)2^{k}}(1-x)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\frac{{2^{n}\binom{n}{k}}}{n!}\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{n+k-1}(z+i/2)}{\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(z+(2i+1)/2)}(1-x)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{{2^{n}\binom{n}{k}}}{n!}\prod_{i=2k}^{n+k-1}(z+i/2)\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(z+i)(x-1)^{k}.

We will henceforth write
G_{n}(x,z)
as it is clear from the previous expression that it is indeed a bivariate polynomial and not a rational fraction in
z
.

We start dealing with the extreme boundary. We have

(5)

G_{n}(x,z)=(-1)^{n}G_{n}(-x,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{{2^{n}\binom{n}{k}}}{n!}(-1)^{n+k}\prod_{i=2k}^{n+k-1}(z+i/2)\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}(z+i)(x+1)^{k}.

So that
G_{n}(-1,z)=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(z+i/2)
, which is clearly realrooted in
z
with simple roots. We can also check this property for
x=0
:

\displaystyle G_{n}(0,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(n/2+z)}{\Gamma(z)\Gamma(n/2+1)}=\frac{1}{(n/2)!}\prod_{j=0}^{j=n/2-1}(z+j)\text{ \; when $n$ is even and}

\displaystyle G_{n}(0,z)

\displaystyle=0\text{ \; when $n$ is odd}.

Also, each bivariate polynomial in the sum is of degree
n
in
z
so the sum is of degree at most
n
in
z
. It is in fact of degree exactly
n
for
x\neq 0
by inspection of the coefficient of
z^{n}
in the sum, which is equal to

(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-1)^{k}(1+x)^{k}=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\big(1-(1+x)\big)^{n}=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}(-x)^{n}=\frac{(2x)^{n}}{n!}.

Remark 6
.

We can prove using this expansion that
\lim_{z\to\infty}y_{i}(z)=0
for all
i
. Indeed, by contradiction and by monotonicity of
y_{i}(z)
, it would mean that
y_{i}(z)
converges to
\epsilon_{i}\neq 0
. But then in the expansion above we get
G_{n}^{(z)}(x)\sim_{z\to\infty}z^{n}\frac{(2\epsilon_{i})^{n}}{n!}
as the lower orders are negligible ( powers in
y_{i}(z)
are bounded), and we get a contradiction as the left hand side is zero and the right hand side diverges.

Notice that we can write, if
n
is even,

G_{n}(x,z)=\Big[\prod_{j=0}^{n/2-1}(z+j)\Big]\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)

and if
n
is odd,

G_{n}(x,z)=\Big[\prod_{j=0}^{(n-1)/2}(z+j)\Big]\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z),

where
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
is a bivariate polynomial.That is, approximately half of the roots in
z
, depending on the oddness, are constant when
x
is moving. We can therefore investigate instead the evolution of the roots of
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
to avoid considering roots that remain constant (and real).

Definition 2.1
.

In all cases for
x\neq 0
, we can define a bivariate polynomial
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
of degree
n
in
x
and of degree
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
in
z
as:

\displaystyle\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{G_{n}(x,z)}{\Big[\prod_{j=0}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}(z-\mu_{j})\Big]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2x)^{n}}{n!}\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\big(z-\gamma_{i}(x)\big)

where
\mu_{j}=-j
and the
\gamma_{i}(x)
are a priori complex roots (defined only for
x\neq 0
),where we order the roots by nondecreasing module:
|\gamma_{1}(x)|\leq|\gamma_{2}(x)|\leq\dots\leq|\gamma_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(x)|
.

It simplifies greatly for
x=-1
:

\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)_{|_{x=-1}}=\frac{2^{n}}{n!}(-1)^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(z+1/2+i-1)

so that
\gamma_{i}(-1)=-1/2-(i-1)
for
i=1,2\dots\lfloor n/2\rfloor
are all real and distinct.

2.3.
Initialization

We derive a way to locally prove the existence of real-rootedness if it is known at some extreme point of the interval.

Lemma 2.2

(Local existence of the roots and smoothness)
.

Take
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Assume
P(x,z)
is a bivariate polynomial such that
P(a,z)
is realrooted in
z
of degree
j
and has only simple roots in
z
(let us call them
z_{i}(a)
for
i=1\dots.j
). Assume that
P(x,z)
has degree less than
j
for all
x
.Then for
x
in a neighborhood of
a
,
P(x,z)
is also realrooted in
z
of degree
j
with simple roots
z_{i}(x)
. Furthermore, the roots
z_{i}(x)
are
C^{\infty}
functions of
x
.

Proof.

Consider the equation
P(x,z)=0
, around the points
\big(a,z_{i}(a)\big)
.

We have
\partial_{z}P(x,z)_{|_{x=a,z=z_{i}(a)}}\neq 0
as the roots are simple (they can’t be roots of the derivative in
z
). Then using the implicit function theorem, we can find in a neighborhood of each point
z_{i}(a)
a
C^{\infty}
function
z_{i}(x)
which will be the only solution to the equation
P(x,z)=0
in this neighborhood. Therefore we have found
j
roots, and it is the maximal number of roots for a fixed
x
, as the polynomial is of degree less than
j
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

L_{n}(x,z)
is real rooted of degree
n
in
z
with simple roots
\lambda_{i}(x)
in a neighborhood of
x=0
.

Proof.

We have:

L_{n}(0,z)=\frac{\prod_{l=1}^{n}(z+l)}{n!},

so that we can apply Lemma
2.2
with
a=0
.
∎

We can characterize the local behaviour of roots more precisely.

Lemma 2.4
.

The roots
\lambda_{i}(x)
of
L_{n}(x,z)
are all strictly increasing when
x
is, in a neighborhood of
x=0
.

Proof.

To prove this, we need some information on the derivatives with respect to
x
of the
\lambda_{i}(x)
in the neighborhood of
0
(which roots we know are going to be real by the previous lemma).

Lemma 2.5
.

\frac{d^{l}\lambda_{i}(x)}{dx^{l}}_{|_{x=0}}=0
for
1\leq l<i
, and
\frac{d^{i}\lambda_{i}(x)}{dx^{i}}_{|_{x=0}}>0
.

Proof.

We get, for all
1\leq l\leq n
,

\partial_{x}^{l}L_{n}(x,z)_{|_{x=0}}=l!(-1)^{l}\frac{\prod_{j=l+1}^{n}(z+j)}{(n-l)!}.

Notice that
\lambda_{i}(0)=-i
for
i=1\dots n
, so we see that for
l\leq i-1
,

\partial_{x}^{l}L_{n}\big(0,\lambda_{i}(0)\big)=l!(-1)^{l}\frac{\prod_{j=l+1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}(0)+j)}{(n-l)!}=0

and

\partial_{x}^{i}L_{n}\big(0,\lambda_{i}(0)\big)=i!(-1)^{i}\frac{\prod_{j=i+1}^{n}(\lambda_{i}(0)+j)}{(n-i)!}=i!(-1)^{i}.

On the other hand,

\partial_{z}L_{n}\big(0,\lambda_{i}(0)\big)=\frac{1}{n!}\prod_{l=1,l\neq i}^{n}(-i+l)=\frac{i!(-1)^{i-1}(n-i)!}{n!}.

Now we have
L_{n}\big(x,\lambda_{i}(x)\big)=0
for all
i=1\dots n
, by definition, so differentiating with respect to
x
, we get

\frac{d\lambda_{i}(x)}{dx}=-\frac{\partial_{x}L_{n}}{\partial_{z}L_{n}}\big(x,\lambda_{i}(x)\big).

Note that the denominator is nonzero as the roots in
z
are simple at
0
( so they won’t be roots of the derivative in
z
). Using Leibniz’s formula and induction on
l
, we get for
i>l\geq 1
,

\frac{d^{l}\lambda_{i}(x)}{dx^{l}}_{|_{x=0}}=0,

and

\frac{d^{i}\lambda_{i}(x)}{dx^{i}}_{|_{x=0}}=-\frac{\partial_{x}^{i}L_{n}}{\partial_{z}L_{n}}\big(-1,\lambda_{i}(0)\big)=\frac{n!}{(n-i)!}>0.

∎

We conclude by a Taylor expansion around
0
as

\lambda_{i}(x)=-i+\frac{x^{i}}{i!}\frac{n!}{(n-i)!}+o(x^{i}).

∎

Let us investigate the analogous property for Gegenbauer polynomials.

Lemma 2.6
.

\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
is real rooted of degree
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
in
z
with simple roots in a neighborhood of
x=-1
.

Proof.

We have:

\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)_{|_{x=-1}}=\frac{2^{n}}{n!}(-1)^{n}\prod_{i=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(z+1/2+i-1)

which has
\lceil n/2\rceil
simple roots in
z

Lemma 2.7
.

The roots
\gamma_{i}(x)
of

are all strictly increasing when

Proof.

We will prove this fact by first proving the following.

Lemma 2.8
.

We have:
\frac{d^{l}\gamma_{i}(x)}{dx^{l}}_{|_{x=-1}}=0
for
1\leq l<i
, and
\frac{d^{i}\gamma_{i}(x)}{dx^{i}}_{|_{x=-1}}>0

Proof.

Using Equation
5
we get that

\partial_{x}^{l}G_{n}(x,z)_{|_{x=-1}}=l!\frac{{2^{n}\binom{n}{l}}}{n!}(-1)^{n+l}\prod_{j=2l}^{n+l-1}(z+j/2)\prod_{j=0}^{l-1}(z+j)

so that

\partial_{x}^{l}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)_{|_{x=-1}}=\frac{{2^{n}\binom{n}{l}}l!}{n!}(-1)^{n+l}\prod_{j=l}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}(z+1/2+j)\prod_{j=n+1}^{n+l-1}(z+j/2).

We then see that

\partial_{x}^{l}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)\big(-1,\gamma_{i}(-1)\big)=0\text{ for $i\geq l+1$}

and

(-1)^{n+i}\partial_{x}^{i}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)\big(-1,\gamma_{i}(-1)\big)=\frac{{2^{n}\binom{n}{i}}i!}{n!}\prod_{j=i}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}\big(j-(i-1)\big)\prod_{j=n+1}^{n+i-1}\big((j-1)/2-(i-1)\big)>0

as

.

Now we have
\tilde{G_{n}}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)=0
for all
i
, by definition, so differentiating with respect to

, we get:

\frac{d\gamma_{i}(x)}{dx}=-\frac{\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}}{\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big).

Note that the denominator is nonzero as the roots in
z
are simple at

( so they won’t be roots of the derivative in

). Using Leibniz’s formula and induction on

\frac{d^{i}\gamma_{i}(x)}{dx^{i}}_{|_{x=-1}}=-\frac{\partial_{x}^{i}\tilde{G_{n}}}{\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}}\big(-1,\gamma_{i}(-1)\big).

Now, we have
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)_{|_{x=-1}}=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\prod_{j=0}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}(z+1/2+j)

\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)\big(-1,\gamma_{i}(-1)\big)=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\prod_{j=0,j\neq i-1}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}\big(j-(i-1)\big)=(-1)^{n}\frac{2^{n}}{n!}(-1)^{i-1}\prod_{j=0}^{i-2}\big((i-1)-j\big)\prod_{j=i}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}\big(j-(i-1)\big)

Then Lemma

∎

In this subsection, we find invariants that enable us to characterize realrootedness. We start by analyzing the Laguerre family.

Lemma 2.9

\displaystyle=\Big[\prod_{j=0}^{\lceil(n-1)/2\rceil-1}(z+j+1)\Big]\tilde{G}_{n-1}(x,z+1),

we get

\partial_{x}\tilde{G}_{n}(x,z)=\frac{\prod_{j=0}^{\lceil(n-1)/2\rceil-1}(z+j+1)}{\prod_{j=0}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}(z+j)}2z\tilde{G}_{n-1}(x,z+1)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\tilde{G}_{n}(x,z)

\displaystyle=2(z+n/2)\tilde{G}_{n-1}(x,z+1)\text{\; \;if $n$ is even,}

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)

\displaystyle=2\tilde{G}_{n-1}(x,z+1)\text{\; \; if $n$ is odd}.

So as
-n/2<\min_{i,x}\gamma_{i}(x)
for all
i
and
x
, it amounts to proving that
\tilde{G_{n-1}}(x,z+1)
and
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
interlace.
We want to show that for all
x\in I
, with
x>-1
, all
i\leq\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor
:

\gamma_{i}^{n}(x)>\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1>\gamma_{i+1}^{n}(x).

First we check this in a neighborhood of
-1
. We can verify that the inequality
\gamma_{i}^{n}(x)>\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1
is true in a neighborhood of
-1
as
\gamma_{i}^{n}(-1)=\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(-1)
. So the nontrivial one is the other one,
\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1>\gamma_{i+1}^{n}(x)
for
x>-1
. We have equality at the origin as
\gamma_{i}^{n}(-1)=\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(-1):=\gamma_{i}(-1)
and
\gamma_{i}(-1)-1=\gamma_{i+1}(-1)
. Then we look at the Taylor expansions around
x=-1
:

\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1=\gamma_{i+1}(-1)+\frac{(x+1)^{i}}{i!}\frac{d^{i}\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)}{dx^{i}}_{|_{x=-1}}+o((x+1)^{i}),

\gamma_{i+1}^{n}(x)=\gamma_{i+1}(-1)+\frac{(x+1)^{i+1}}{(i+1)!}\frac{d^{i+1}\gamma_{i+1}^{n}(x)}{dx^{i+1}}_{|_{x=-1}}+o((x+1)^{i+1}).

It is clear then that locally
\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1>\gamma_{i+1}^{n}(x)
as
(x+1)^{i+1}<<(x+1)^{i}
.

We extend the inequality to the whole interval
I
by noticing again that if the inequalities where not valid anymore, then there would have to be some equality
\gamma_{i}^{n}(x)=\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1
or
\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x)-1=\gamma_{i+1}^{n}(x)
, which would mean
\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x))=0
and as
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,\gamma_{i}^{n-1}(x))=0
, we would again get a contradiction by Lemma
2.13
.

∎

2.5.
Ordinary differential equation on the roots and global extension

Lemma 2.17

(Global extension through ODE, Laguerre case)
.

The local Laguerre property is in fact true over the whole interval:
L_{n}(x,z)
is real rooted in
z
with simple (distinct) roots for
x\in[0,+\infty[
, and the roots are all increasing to
+\infty
when
x\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Denote by
F_{n}(x,z):=-\frac{\partial_{x}L_{n}}{\partial_{z}L_{n}}\big(x,z\big)
. Consider a rectangular domain
D
such that
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
is nonzero on the domain.

F_{n}
is continuous in
x
and
z
in the the domain
D
.
Indeed, it is a rational fraction whose denominator is nonzero and it is therefore
C^{\infty}
in both variables by theorem of composition. As
L_{n}(0,z)
is realrooted in
z
with simple roots,
\partial_{z}L_{n}\big(0,\lambda_{i}(0)\big)\neq 0
and by continuity we can find small rectangles
D_{i}:=[0,\epsilon]\times[\lambda_{i}(0)-\delta,\lambda_{i}(0)+\delta]
such that
L_{n}(x,z)
is nonzero on
D_{i}
. A strong version of Picard’s theorem tells us that there is a maximal interval
I_{i}^{max}=[0,\eta^{max}_{i}[
(where
\eta^{max}_{i}\in\mathbb{\bar{R^{+}}}
) for which the roots
\lambda_{i}(x)
(
i=1,2\dots n
) are the unique solutions of the initial value ODE

\frac{dz}{dx}=F_{n}(x,z),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;z(-1)=-i.

Note that on
I_{i}^{max}
,
\partial_{z}L_{n}\big(x,\lambda_{i}(x)\big)\neq 0
(the denominator is nonzero, so that the differential equation is well defined).

Let us consider the smallest interval
I^{max}=I_{i_{0}}^{max}=]0,b[
, with
b=\inf_{i=1\dots n}\eta^{max}_{i}
, so that
I^{max}\subset I_{i}^{max}
for all
i
and prove that
b=+\infty
.
By Corollary
2.10
,
F_{n}(x,\lambda_{i}(x))>0
on
I_{max}
as all roots are distinct (nonzero denominator) and real (by definition of the intervals
I^{i}_{max}
) .
According to Picard’s theorem, we either have
\lambda_{i}(x)\rightarrow_{x\to\eta^{max}_{i}}+\infty
(explosion), or
\eta^{max}_{i}
is such that
\lim_{x\to\eta^{max}_{i}}F_{n}(x,\lambda_{i}(x))
is not well defined (we leave the domain of definition).
Now, explosion can’t happen if
b<+\infty
. Indeed, we have using the hypergeometric expansion above

\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\lambda_{i}(x)}=-n!\big(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\frac{1}{n!}-\frac{x}{(n-1)!}\big)=n\big(-\frac{n+1}{2}+x\big),

so the sum of roots, all increasing, is bounded above for
x<b
and there can be no explosion of the sum and therefore of the increasing roots separately when
x\to b
(necessarily to
+\infty
by monotonicity).

We can leave the domain of definition only if
\lim_{x\to b}\partial_{z}L_{n}\big(x,\lambda_{i_{0}}(x)\big)=0
. If this is the case and if by contradiction
b<+\infty
,
\partial_{z}L_{n}(b,z)
would be of degree
n
in
z
. Therefore it means that
\lim_{x\to b}\lambda_{i_{0}}(x)=\mu
exists and is finite (by monotonicity) where
\mu
is a root of
\partial_{z}L_{n}(b,z)
. But then it means that we can extend by continuity
\lambda_{i_{0}}(x)
at
x=b
with
\lambda_{i_{0}}(b)=\mu
. We check by continuity that
L_{n}\big(b,\lambda_{i_{0}}(b)\big)=\partial_{z}L_{n}\big(b,\lambda_{i_{0}}(b)\big)=0
so that in fact
\lambda_{i_{0}}(b)
is a real double root in
z
of
L_{n}\big(b,z\big)
.

As there is a root of
\partial_{x}L_{n}(x,z)
between any two roots of
L_{n}(x,z)
in
z
on
I^{max}
using Lemma
2.12
, it follows that:

\lim_{x\to b}\partial_{x}L_{n}\big(x,\lambda_{i_{0}}(x)\big)=0,

and by continuity,
\partial_{x}L_{n}\big(b,\lambda_{i}(b)\big)=0
. But this is impossible according to Lemma
2.13
.

Therefore, we have necessarily
b=+\infty
, that is,
\eta^{max}_{i}=+\infty
for all
i=1\dots n
.

Furthermore, assume by contradiction that there is no explosion for some index
i
at
+\infty
, which means that there exists a
\lambda_{i}(x)
which is bounded. As
\lambda_{i}(x)
is monotonous for
x\in[0,+\infty[
, then we have necessarily that
\lim_{x\to+\infty}\lambda_{i}(x)=\mu
exists and is finite. By continuity we have
L_{n}(x,\mu)\sim_{x\to\infty}(-1)^{n}x^{n}
, and
\partial_{x}L_{n}(x,\mu)\sim_{x\to\infty}n(-1)^{n}x^{n-1}
, as well as
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,\mu)\sim_{x\to\infty}(-1)^{n-1}x^{n-1}
, using:

L_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(z+j)}{(n-k)!}x^{k}.\\

So that

\frac{d\lambda_{i}(x)}{dx}\to_{x\to+\infty}n,

and clearly we would have
\lambda_{i}(x)\to+\infty
, which is a contradiction.

∎

Lemma 2.18

(Global extension through ODE, Gegenbauer case)
.

The local Gegenbauer property is in fact true over the whole interval:
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
is real rooted in
z
with simple (distinct) roots for
x\in[-1,0[
, and they are all increasing to
+\infty
when
x\to 0
.

Proof.

Denote by
F_{n}(x,z):=-\frac{\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}}{\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}}\big(x,z\big)
. Consider a rectangular domain
D
such that
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
is nonzero on the domain.

F_{n}
is continuous in
x
and
z
in the the domain
D
.
Indeed, it is a rational fraction whose denominator is nonzero and it is therefore
C^{\infty}
in both variables by theorem of composition. As
\tilde{G_{n}}(-1,z)
is realrooted in
z
with simple roots,
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(-1,\gamma_{i}(-1))\neq 0
and by continuity we can find small rectangles
D_{i}:=[-1,-1+\epsilon]\times[\gamma_{i}(-1)-\delta,\gamma_{i}(-1)+\delta]
such that
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
is nonzero on
D_{i}
. A strong version of Picard’s theorem tells us that there is a maximal interval
I_{i}^{max}=]-1,0[\cap[-1,\eta^{max}_{i}[
for which the roots
\gamma_{i}(x)
(
i=1,2\dots\lfloor n/2\rfloor
) are the unique solutions of the initial value ODE

\frac{dz}{dx}=F_{n}(x,z),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;z(-1)=-1/2-(i-1).

Note that on
I_{i}^{max}
,
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,\gamma_{i}(x))\neq 0
(the denominator is nonzero, so that the differential equation is well defined).

Let us consider the smallest interval
I^{max}=I_{i_{0}}^{max}=]-1,b[
, with
b=\eta^{max}_{i_{0}}=\inf_{i=1\dots n}\eta^{max}_{i}
, so that
I^{max}\subset I_{i}^{max}
for all
i
; and let us prove that
b=0
.

By Corollary
2.14
,
F_{n}(x,\gamma_{i}(x))>0
for all
i
on
I_{max}
as all roots are distinct (nonzero denominator) and real (by definition of the intervals
I^{i}_{max}
) .
According to Picard’s theorem, we either have
\gamma_{i_{0}}(x)\rightarrow_{x\to b}+\infty
(explosion), or
b
is such that
\lim_{x\to b}F_{n}(x,\gamma_{i_{0}}(x))
is not well defined (we leave the domain of definition).
Let us prove that
I^{max}=[-1,0[
and that there is explosion when
x\to 0
(roots going to infinity).

Now, explosion can’t happen if
b<0
. Indeed, we have that

\sum_{i}{\gamma_{i}(x)}+\sum_{j}{\mu_{j}}=\frac{-P_{n-1}(x)}{\frac{(2x)^{n}}{n!}}

where
P_{n-1}(x)
is a polynomial of degree
n-1
as well as the coefficient of
z^{n-1}
in the expansion of
G_{n}(x,z)
. So the sum of roots is bounded above by a constant when
x\to b
, and there can be no explosion (necessarily to
+\infty
by monotonicity).

We can leave the domain of definition only if
\lim_{x\to b}\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(x,\gamma_{i_{0}}(x)\big)=0
. If this is the case and if by contradiction
b<0
, we have seen that
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(b,z)
would be of degree
\lfloor n/2\rfloor-1
in
z
. Therefore it means that
\lim_{x\to b}\gamma_{i_{0}}(x)=\mu
where
\mu
is a root of
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}(b,z)
. But then it means that we can extend by continuity
\gamma_{i_{0}}(x)
at
x=b
with
\gamma_{i_{0}}(b)=\mu
. We check by continuity that
\tilde{G_{n}}\big(b,\gamma_{i_{0}}(b)\big)=\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(b,\gamma_{i_{0}}(b)\big)=0
so that in fact
\gamma_{i_{0}}(b)
is a real double root in
z
of
\tilde{G_{n}}\big(b,z\big)
.

Using Lemma
2.16
, as there is a root of
\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
between any two roots of
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
in
z
by interlacing, it follows that necessarily
\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(\eta^{max}_{i},\gamma_{i}(\eta_{max})\big)=0
.
As there is a root of
\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
between any two roots of
\tilde{G_{n}}(x,z)
in
z
on
I^{max}
using Lemma
2.16
, it follows that:

\lim_{x\to b}\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(x,\lambda_{i_{0}}(x)\big)=0,

and by continuity,
\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(b,\lambda_{i}(b)\big)=0
.
But this is impossible according to Lemma
2.13
. Therefore, we have necessarily
b=0
and
\eta^{max}_{i}=0
for all
i=1\dots\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.

Let us prove that
\gamma_{i}(x)\to_{x\to 0}+\infty
.Assume by contradiction that there is no explosion for some index
i
at
0
. As
\gamma_{i}(x)
is monotonous for
x\in]-1,0[
, then we have necessarily that
\lim_{x\to 0}\gamma_{i}(x)=\mu
exists and is finite. By continuity we have
\tilde{G_{n}}(0,\mu)=0
.

Let us distinguish according to the parity of
n
.

If
n
is even, we have
\tilde{G_{n}}(0,z)=\frac{G_{n}(0,z)}{\prod_{j=0}^{n/2-1}(z+j)}=\frac{1}{(n/2)!}
for all
z
, which combined with
\tilde{G_{n}}(0,\mu)=0
leads to a contradiction.

If
n
is odd, then by definition, we will have, as :

\displaystyle\tilde{G_{n}}\big(x,z\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=0}^{\lceil n/2\rceil-1}(z+j)}\sum_{k=0}^{(n-1)/2}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(n-k+z)}{\Gamma(z)k!(n-2k)!}(2x)^{n-2k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=0}^{(n-1)/2}(z+j)}\big(x\prod_{j=0}^{(n-1)/2}(z+j)\frac{2(-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{\big((n-1)/2\big)!}+\sum_{k=0}^{(n-1)/2}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(n-k+z)}{\Gamma(z)k!(n-2k)!}(2x)^{n-2k}\big)

\displaystyle=2x\frac{(-1)^{(n-1)/2}}{\big((n-1)/2\big)!}+\big(z+(n+1)/2\big)4x^{3}\frac{(-1)^{(n-3)/2}}{\big((n-3)/2\big)!}+o(x^{3}),

so that:

\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(x,z\big)=_{x\to 0}4x^{3}\frac{(-1)^{(n-3)/2}}{\big((n-3)/2\big)!}+o(x^{3}),

and we see that we can factor out
x^{3}
and write:
\partial_{z}\tilde{G_{n}}\big(x,z\big)=x^{3}Q_{n}\big(x,z\big)
where
Q_{n}(x,z)
is a bivariate polynomial such that
Q_{n}(0,z)=\frac{4(-1)^{(n-3)/2}}{\big((n-3)/2\big)!}
.

The remainder

is indeed negligible because the coefficients depend on
\gamma_{i}(x)
which are by assumption bounded as functions of
x
(this asymptotic approximation would fail without the boundedness assumption).
For
x\in[-1,0]
,
x^{3}F_{n}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)=-\frac{\partial_{x}\tilde{G_{n}}}{Q_{n}}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)
is therefore bounded above as a continuous function on a compact. Indeed, its denominator
Q_{n}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)
can’t be zero on
[-1,0[
by simplicity of the roots in
z
, and we also have :
\lim Q_{n}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)\to_{x\to 0}Q_{n}(0,\mu)\neq 0
. The function
x^{3}F_{n}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)
is nonpostive on
[-1,0]
, and the maximum can’t be zero because it would mean that for an
x\in[-1,0]
,
\partial_{x}G_{n}\big(x,\gamma_{i}(x)\big)=0
, which is impossible according to Lemma
2.13
. Therefore it is always smaller than
-K<0
. It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{d\gamma_{i}(x)}{dx}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x^{3}}x^{3}F_{n}(x,\gamma_{i}(x))>\frac{-K}{x^{3}}

and

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}(x)-\gamma_{i}(-1)

\displaystyle>K\big(\frac{1}{2x^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\big),

by integrating the inequality on
[-1,x]
.
It leads to
\gamma_{i}(x)\to_{x\to 0}+\infty
, which is again a contradiction.

We conclude that there is explosion for all
i=1\dots\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
∎

3.
Applications to realrootedness in
x
of derivatives in
z

The main application of realrootedness in
z
will show up through the famous Laguerre inequality.

Lemma 3.1

(Laguerre’s inequality)
.

For a polynomial
p(z)
with simple real roots, then

p^{\prime\prime}(z)p(z)-p^{\prime}(z)^{2}<0.

3.1.
Laguerre case

The goal of this subsection is to prove Theorem
1.10
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a fixed
z
, we have that the roots of
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
in
x
(of degree
n-1
) are real and interlace strictly those of
L_{n}(x,z)
in
x
.

Proof.

We have

(8)

\displaystyle L_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{k}\prod_{j=k+1}^{n}(z+j)}{(n-k)!}x^{k}.

From this expression it follows that
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
is of degree
n-1
in
x
.
Also by differentiating
L_{n}(x_{i}(z),z)=0
with respect to
z
, we get

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}L_{n}(x_{i}(z),z)=-\partial_{x}L_{n}(x_{i}(z),z)\frac{d}{dz}x_{i}.

\partial_{x}L_{n}(x_{i}(z),z)
changes sign when we increment
i
because
L_{n}(x_{i}(z),z)=0
so the derivative changes sign when we go from one root to the next. As
\frac{d}{dz}x_{i}>0
, We get that
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
changes sign
n-1
times and therefore we have
n-1
real zeros strictly between zeros of
L_{n}
.
Therefore all the zeros of
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
have been found and are interlacing with the zeros of
L_{n}(x,z)
.
∎

let us now proceed to proving Theorem
1.10
.
We show inductively the following property:
\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}(x,z)
is realrooted, the roots of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}(x,z)
interlace strictly the roots of
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
and are increasing in
z
. We start with the initial condition. Using Lemma
3.2
, we get the real-rootedness and strict interlacing property for
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
. Now we need to prove that the roots
\tilde{x}_{i}(z)
(
i=1\dots n-1
) of
\partial_{z}L_{n}(x,z)
also share the strict monotonicity property. As by strict interlacing
L_{n}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)\neq 0
,

\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)=0,

which leads to

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{d(\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)\Big)}{dz}=\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)\Big)\frac{d\tilde{x_{i}}}{dz}+\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)\Big)=0.

We want to show that for all
i
,

\frac{d\tilde{x_{i}}}{dz}>0.

On the one hand,

\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{\partial_{x}L_{n}}(x_{j},z)\frac{1}{\tilde{x_{i}}-x_{j}},

so that

(11)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)\Big)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{\partial_{x}L_{n}}(x_{j},z)\frac{1}{(\tilde{x_{i}}-x_{j})^{2}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{dx_{j}}{dz}\frac{1}{(\tilde{x_{i}}-x_{j})^{2}}>0.

On the other hand, using the real-rootedness in
z
, as
\tilde{x_{i}}\in[0,+\infty[
by interlacing of the roots, then

\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}(\tilde{x_{i}}(z),z)\Big)<0

as Laguerre’s inequality (
3.1
) applied to
L_{n}(\tilde{x_{i}},z)
implies that
[\partial_{zz}L_{n}L_{n}-(\partial_{z}L_{n})^{2}](\tilde{x_{i}},z)<0
.
We conclude by gathering the two inequalities.

The induction is proven using exactly the same method. Assume
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
is realrooted of degree
n-k
with simple positive roots for all
z\geq 0
, and with roots increasing strictly in
z
. Then, we prove like in Lemma
3.2
that

Lemma 3.3
.

The roots of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}(x,z)
in
x
(of degree
n-k-1
) are real and interlace strictly those of
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
.

Eventually, we show exactly like in the case
k=0
that the roots of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}(x,z)
are strictly increasing in
z
using the partial fraction decomposition
\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}}
instead of
\frac{\partial_{z}L_{n}}{L_{n}}
, as we also have

\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}L_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}}\Big)<0

because
\partial_{z}^{k}L_{n}(x,z)
is realrooted in
z
with simple roots as the
k^{th}
derivative of a realrooted polynomial with simple roots (
x
is in the appropriate interval
[0,+\infty]
) and Laguerre’s inequality applies. Therefore the induction follows and Theorem
1.10
is proven.

3.2.
Gegenbauer case

In this subsection we prove Theorem
1.11
.
We recall that as
G_{n}(x,0)=0
, the canonical way to extend the definition of
G_{n}(x,z)
to
z=0
is to look at the roots of
G_{n}(x,z)/z
at
z=0
(removing the singularity in
z
). Equivalently, we can just look at the limiting distribution of the roots of
G_{n}(x,z)
when
z
converges to
0
.
We have

(12)

\displaystyle G_{n}(-x,z)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}G_{n}(x,z)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}G_{n}(-x,z)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{n}\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z).

So that if we have a root
\tilde{y(z)}
of
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
in
x
, then its symmetric counterpart
-\tilde{y(z)}
will also be a root of
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
. In other terms,
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
and more generally ,
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
have symmetric roots in
x
.

Lemma 3.4
.

The roots of
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
in
x
(of degree
n
) are real and the positive ones interlace strictly those of
G_{n}(x,z)
by below (that is the largest root in module belongs to
G_{n}(x,z)
).

Proof.

We have

(13)

\displaystyle G_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(n-k+z)}{\Gamma(z)k!(n-2k)!}(2x)^{n-2k}.

From this expression it follows that
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
is of degree
n
in
x
, as the coefficient of
x^{n}
is
\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\partial_{z}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(z+i)\big]
.

Let us denote the roots of
G_{n}(x,z)
by
y_{i}
, by decreasing order. Then by differentiating the equality
G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)=0
with respect to
z
we get

(14)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)=-\partial_{x}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}.

\partial_{x}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)
changes sign when we increment
i
because the derivative changes sign when we go from one root to the next (the roots are simple). Let us distinguish between the even and odd cases.

In the even case,
\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}<0
when
i=1..n/2
, and we have
n/2
positive roots for
G_{n}
,
y_{1}>y_{2}\dots>y_{n/2}>0
. They give us, by the sign rule, using Equation
14
and the fact that
\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}<0
,
n/2-1
positive roots
\tilde{y_{1}}>\tilde{y_{2}}\dots>\tilde{y_{n/2-1}}>0
for
\partial_{z}G_{n}
. Now there is still one root missing on the positive side. the sign of
\partial_{x}G_{n}(y_{1},z)
is
+1
(at the largest root the derivative is positive), and
sign\big[\partial_{x}G_{n}(y_{n/2}(z),z)\big]=(-1)^{n/2-1}
by the alternance of signs of the derivative from root to root. From there we get that
sign\big[\partial_{z}G_{n}(y_{n/2}(z),z)\big]=(-1)^{n/2-1}
using Equation
14
. On the other hand,
sign\big[\partial_{z}G_{n}(0,z)\big]=(-1)^{n/2}
by easy inspection as
\partial_{z}G_{n}(0,z)=(-1)^{n/2}\frac{1}{n/2!}\partial_{z}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n/2-1}(z+i)\big]
.
As
\partial_{z}G_{n}(y_{n/2}(z),z)
and
\partial_{z}G_{n}(0,z)
have opposite signs and are nonzero, there must exist an additional zero of
\partial_{z}G_{n}
in
]0,y_{n/2}(z)[
.
As
\partial_{z}G_{n}
is symmetric in
x
, there is also another symmetric negative zero. We get
n
roots overall.

In the odd case
n=2n_{0}+1
, denote the positive roots of
G_{n}
by
y_{1}>y_{2}\dots>y_{n_{0}}>0
. If we collect as above the roots we get by the change of signs in Equation
14
, we get
n_{0}-1
positive roots for
\partial_{z}G_{n}
. We can’t use directly the sign changes between
0
and
y_{n_{0}}
as
0
is a root, but we can look for such a sign change in the neighborhood . Indeed, we have using Equation
13
,
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)=_{x=0}x(-1)^{n_{0}}\frac{2}{n_{0}!}\partial_{z}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}(z+i)\big]+o(x)
. For
x>0
,
sign\big[\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)\big]=(-1)^{n_{0}}
, and on the other hand
sign\big[\partial_{z}G_{n}(y_{n_{0}},z)\big]=sign\big[\partial_{x}G_{n}(y_{n_{0}},z)\big]=(-1)^{n_{0}-1}
therefore there is an additional zero for
\partial_{z}G_{n}
in
]0,y_{n_{0}}[
. By symmetry, we also have another negative zero, and in total we get
n_{0}
positive roots,
n_{0}
negative roots, and
0
which gives
n
roots.
Therefore all the zeros of
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
have been found and the positive ones are interlacing the zeros of
G_{n}(x,z)
(strictly).

∎

Let us now proceed to the inductive proof of Theorem
1.11
.
We show inductively the following property:

\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
is realrooted, the positive roots of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
interlace the positive roots of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
strictly (that is no shared positive root) and the positive roots of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
are strictly decreasing with
z
.

We start with the initial condition,
k=0
. Using Lemma
3.4
, we get the real-rootedness and interlacing property for
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
. Now we need to prove that the positive roots
\tilde{y}_{i}(z)
of
\partial_{z}G_{n}(x,z)
also share the monotonicity property. By assumption, as
G_{n}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\neq 0
(strict interlacing),

\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)}{G_{n}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)}=0,

which leads to

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{y_{i}}}{dz}=-\frac{\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)}{\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)}

We want to show that for the positive roots,

\frac{d\tilde{y_{i}}}{dz}<0.

On the one hand,

\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{\partial_{x}G_{n}}(y_{j},z)\frac{1}{\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j}}

so that

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{\partial_{x}G_{n}}(x_{j},z)\frac{1}{(\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{dy_{j}}{dz}\frac{1}{(\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j})^{2}}

(17)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{dy_{j}}{dz}\big[\frac{1}{(\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(\tilde{y_{i}}+y_{j})^{2}}\big]

(18)

\displaystyle=4\tilde{y_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{\frac{dy_{j}}{dz}y_{j}}{(\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j})^{2}(\tilde{y_{i}}+y_{j})^{2}}

As we have
\tilde{y_{i}}>0
,
\frac{dy_{j}}{dz}<0
as well as
y_{j}>0
we get

\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)<0.

On the other hand, as
\tilde{y_{i}}\in[-1,1]
by interlacing, then

\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}G_{n}}{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)<0

using Laguerre’s inequality for
{G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}},z)
, stating that
[\partial_{zz}G_{n}G_{n}-(\partial_{z}G_{n})^{2}](\tilde{y_{i}},z)<0
.
We conclude by gathering the two inequalities.

The induction goes as follows. Assume
k<n
. We first extend Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 3.5
.

The roots of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
in
x
(of degree
n
) are real and the positive ones interlace those of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
by below (that is the largest root in module belongs to
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
) and the interlacing is strict amongst positive roots (no shared positive root for the two polynomials). For
k+1>n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, zero roots add two by two when
k
increases by one.

Proof.

We have

(19)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(-1)^{j}\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n-j-1}(z+i)\big]}{j!(n-2j)!}(2x)^{n-2j}.

From this expression it follows that
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
is of degree
n
in
x
(like
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
) , as the coefficient of
x^{n}
is
\frac{2^{n}}{n!}\partial_{z}^{k+1}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(z+i)\big]
and
\partial_{z}^{k+1}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}(z+i)\big]>0
(nonzero) as
k<n
.

Let’s assume first that
k+1\leq n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor
. Then the proof will be almost identical to the
k=0
case (Lemma
3.4
). We still make it explicit for completeness sake.
Let us denote the positive roots of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
by
y_{i}
, in decreasing order. Then by differentiating the equality
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)=0
with respect to
z
we get

(20)

\displaystyle\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)=-\partial_{x}\big[\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)\big]\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}.

\partial_{x}G_{n}(y_{i}(z),z)
changes sign when we increment
i
because the derivative changes sign when we go from one root to the next (the roots are simple). Let us distinguish between the even and odd cases.

In the even case,
\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}<0
when
i=1..n/2
, and we have
n/2
positive roots for
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}
,
y_{1}>y_{2}\dots>y_{n/2}>0
. They give us, by the sign rule, using Equation
20
and the fact that
\frac{d}{dz}y_{i}<0
,
n/2-1
positive roots
\tilde{y_{1}}>\tilde{y_{2}}\dots>\tilde{y_{n/2-1}}>0
for
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}
. Now there is still one root missing on the positive side. the sign of
\partial_{x}\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(y_{1},z)
is
+1
(at the largest root the derivative is positive), and
sign\big[\partial_{x}\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(y_{n/2}(z),z)\big]=(-1)^{n/2-1}
by the alternance of signs of the derivative from root to root. From there we get that
sign\big[\partial_{z}\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(y_{n/2}(z),z)\big]=(-1)^{n/2-1}
using Equation
20
. On the other hand,
sign\big[\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(0,z)\big]=(-1)^{n/2}
(and is non zero) as
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(0,z)=(-1)^{n/2}\frac{1}{n/2!}\partial_{z}^{k+1}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n/2-1}(z+i)\big]
, and
k+1\leq n/2
by assumption.
As
\partial_{z}G_{n}(y_{n/2}(z),z)
and
\partial_{z}G_{n}(0,z)
have opposite signs and are nonzero, there must exist an additional zero of
\partial_{z}G_{n}
in
]0,y_{n/2}(z)[
.
As
\partial_{z}G_{n}
is symmetric in
x
, there is also another symmetric negative zero. We get
n
roots overall.

In the odd case
n=2n_{0}+1
, denote the positive roots of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}
by
y_{1}>y_{2}\dots>y_{n_{0}}>0
. If we collect as above the roots we get by the change of signs in Equation
20
, we get
n_{0}-1
positive roots for
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}
. We can’t use directly the sign changes between
0
and
y_{n_{0}}
as
0
is a root, but we can look for such a sign change in the neighborhood . We have, using Equation
13
,
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)=_{x=0}x\big[(-1)^{n_{0}}\frac{2}{n_{0}!}\partial_{z}^{k+1}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}(z+i)\big])\big]+o(x)
. As
k+1\leq n_{0}
by assumption , this quantity is nonzero. For
x>0
,
sign\big[\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)\big]=(-1)^{n_{0}}
, and on the other hand
sign\big[\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(y_{n_{0}},z)\big]=sign\big[\partial_{x}\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(y_{n_{0}},z)\big]=(-1)^{n_{0}-1}
therefore there is an additional zero for
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}
in
]0,y_{n_{0}}[
. By symmetry, we also have another negative zero, and in total we get
n_{0}
positive roots,
n_{0}
negative roots, and one zero root which gives
n
roots.
Therefore all the zeros of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
have been found and the positive ones are interlacing with the zeros of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
.

In the case where
k+1>n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, it is actually easier. For
l=max(k-[n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor],0)
,

\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-l}(-1)^{j}\frac{\partial_{z}^{k}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n-j-1}(z+i)\big]}{j!(n-2j)!}(2x)^{n-2j}

and

\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor-l-1}(-1)^{j}\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}\big[\prod_{i=0}^{n-j-1}(z+i)\big]}{j!(n-2j)!}(2x)^{n-2j}.

In the case
n
is even, then the number of (distinct) positive zeros for
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
is
n/2-l
, the number of negative zeros as well ( the multiplicity of
0
is
2l
). Use of the sign rule (through Equation
20
) leads to
n/2-l-1
positive zeros and
n/2-l-1
negative zeros for
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}
. But as the multiplicity of
0
is
2(l+1)
(we cancel a term in the summation when differentiating with respect to z) , then we get overall
n
zeros, and the positive ones interlace those of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
(with an accumulation point at
0
).

In the odd case,
n=2n_{0}+1
, the number of (distinct) positive zeros for
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
is
n_{0}-l
, the number of negative zeros also
n_{0}-l
( and the multiplicity of
0
is
2l+1
). Again, use of the sign rule (through Equation
20
) leads to
n_{0}-l-1
positive zeros and
n_{0}-l-1
negative zeros for
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}
. as the multiplicity of
0
is
2(l+1)+1
, we also get overall
n
zeros whose positive ones interlace those of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(x,z)
.

∎

Now we need to finish the induction by proving that the positive roots
\tilde{y}_{i}(z)
of
\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(x,z)
share the monotonicity property.
By assumption, as
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\neq 0
(strict interlacing for positive roots),

\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)}=0

which leads to

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{y_{i}}}{dz}=-\frac{\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)}{\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)}.

We want to show that

\frac{d\tilde{y_{i}}}{dz}<0.

On the one hand, if we consider
y_{j}
to be the roots of
\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}
and
y_{j}

\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{x}\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(y_{j},z)\frac{1}{\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j}}

so that as before, symmetrizing and keeping only the positive roots (zero ones go away in the sum anyway),

(22)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)=4\tilde{y_{i}}\sum_{j=1,s.t.y_{j}>0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{\frac{dy_{j}}{dz}y_{j}}{(\tilde{y_{i}}-y_{j})^{2}(\tilde{y_{i}}+y_{j})^{2}}.

As we have
\tilde{y_{i}}>0
, and for all
j
such that
y_{j}>0
,
\frac{dy_{j}}{dz}y_{j}<0
(the existence of at least one of them is guaranteed by
k<n
),

\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)<0.

On the other hand, as
\tilde{y_{i}}\in[-1,1]
by interlacing, then using Laguerre’s inequality for
{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}},z)
(which is realrooted in
z
with simple roots as the
k^{th}
derivative in
z
of a polynomial with simple real roots), stating that
[\partial_{z}^{k+2}G_{n}\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}-(\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n})^{2}](\tilde{y_{i}},z)<0
; we get

\partial_{z}\Big(\frac{\partial_{z}^{k+1}G_{n}}{\partial_{z}^{k}G_{n}}(\tilde{y_{i}}(z),z)\Big)<0.

We conclude as before, and we proved Theorem
1.11
by induction.
