A Petrov-Galerkin method for nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problems
Yu Leng, Xiaochuan Tian, Leszek Demkowicz, Hector Gomez, John T. Foster

Abstract
We present a Petrov-Gelerkin (PG) method for a class of nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problems. There are two main ingredients in our approach. First, we define the norm on the test space as induced by the trial space norm, i.e., the optimal test norm, so that the inf-sup condition can be satisfied uniformly independent of the problem. We show the well-posedness of a class of nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problems under the optimal test norm with general assumptions on the nonlocal diffusion and convection kernels. Second, following the framework of Cohen et al. (2012), we embed the original nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problem into a larger mixed problem so as to choose an enriched test space as a stabilization of the numerical algorithm. In the numerical experiments, we use an approximate optimal test norm which can be efficiently implemented in 1d, and study its performance against the energy norm on the test space. We conduct convergence studies for the nonlocal problem using uniform h h - and p p -refinements, and adaptive h h -refinements on both smooth manufactured solutions and solutions with sharp gradient in a transition layer. In addition, we confirm that the PG method is asymptotically compatible.

Introduction

In this work, we are motivated to study the numerical solution of the nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion model. Mathematical analysis and numerical methods have been developed for nonlocal diffusion models

We refer the readers to the monograph

The time-dependent linear nonlocal convection-diffusion models with volumetric constraints are studied in

An important feature for these models is that the nonlocal convection-diffusion model converges to the classical convection-diffusion model as the nonlocal interaction vanishes.
The nonlocal convection terms used in these works are slightly different, with which physical properties such as maximum principle and mass conservation may or may not be preserved.
The well-posedness of the weak form of the nonlocal convection-diffusion problem in all these works, however, is essentially based on the assumption of coercivity of the bilinear form. This assumption limits the model to the diffusion-dominated regime.

It is well-known for the classical convection-diffusion problem that, as the convection becomes dominant over the diffusion, the solution of the standard Galerkin method or central finite difference method deteriorates and unphysical oscillations emerge in the solution. Stabilizing numerical techniques, such as the streamline upwind/Petrov-Galerkin method

For nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion models, it was also shown in
[

[

Discontinuous Petrov-Galerkin (DPG) method has been developed in a series of work
[

[

There are two major contributions of this work. The first contribution is that we show the well-posedness result of the nonlocal convection-diffusion model where the convection could be dominant.
Such result is achieved by choosing the norm on the test space in an optimal way such that the inf-sup condition or the Ladyzhenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi condition for well-posedness of the variational problem is satisfied. However, the optimal test space norm is not practical in computation, the second contribution is that we present an approximation of the optimal test norm in one dimension and conduct numerical experiments to demonstrate the effectiveness of the PG method for the convection-dominated diffusion model.

2
The Petrov-Galerkin method

In this section, we briefly introduce the ingredients of our PG method following the expositions in
[
13
,
17
,
19
]
.
Let us consider the abstract variational problem,

\begin{cases}\textnormal{Find }u\in U,\,\textnormal{ such that:}\\
b(u,v)=l(v),\quad\forall v\in V,\end{cases}

(1)

where
U
(the “trial" space) and
V
(the “test" space) are Hilbert spaces with norm,
\|\cdot\|_{U}
and
\|\cdot\|_{V}
respectively,
l(\cdot)
is a given real-valued continuous linear functional on
V
, and
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is a continuous bilinear form defined on
U\times V
that satisfies the inf-sup condition

\inf_{u\in U}\sup_{v\in V}\frac{|b(u,v)|}{\|u\|_{U}\|v\|_{V}}\geq\alpha>0\iff\sup_{v\in V}\frac{|b(u,v)|}{\|v\|_{V}}\geq\alpha\|u\|_{U}.

(2)

We also assume that the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
is definite, i.e.,

\text{if }b(u,v)=0,\quad\forall u\in U,\text{ then }v=0.

(3)

Equation

1
is well-posed by the Banach-Nečas-Babuška theorem (see e.g.
[
33
,
47
]
).
It is worth mentioning that the inf-sup constant
\alpha
is
1
if
U
and
V
form a
duality pairing
. Namely if we define
\|\cdot\|_{V}
as the norm induced by
\|\cdot\|_{U}
, we can recover
\|\cdot\|_{U}
by the norm induced by
\|\cdot\|_{V}
, i.e.,

\|v\|_{V}:=\sup_{u\in U}\frac{|b(u,v)|}{\|u\|_{U}}\quad\implies\quad\|u\|_{U}:=\sup_{v\in V}\frac{|b(u,v)|}{\|v\|_{V}}.

For this reason and following the nomenclature in
[
17
]
, we will call the norm on
V
induced by
\|\cdot\|_{U}
the
optimal test norm
, denoted by

\|v\|_{\textnormal{opt},V}:=\sup_{u\in U}\frac{|b(u,v)|}{\|u\|_{U}}.

(4)

In this paper, we will explore the effectiveness of our Petrov-Galerkin method with
\|\cdot\|_{V}
defined in different ways.

In computation, PG methods in general take finite-dimensional trial and test spaces
U_{h}\subset U
and
V_{h}\subset V
with
\text{dim}U_{h}=\text{dim}V_{h}
and solve the approximate problem

\begin{cases}\textnormal{Find }u_{h}\in U_{h},\,\textnormal{ such that:}\\
b(u_{h},v_{h})=l(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}.\end{cases}

(5)

Equation (
5
) is well-posed only if the discrete inf-sup condition

\inf_{u_{h}\in U_{h}}\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{|b(u_{h},v_{h})|}{\|u\|_{U_{h}}\|v\|_{V_{h}}}\geq\beta>0\iff\sup_{v_{h}\in V_{h}}\frac{|b(u_{h},v_{h})|}{\|v_{h}\|_{V_{h}}}\geq\beta\|u_{h}\|_{U_{h}}

(6)

is satisfied for some
\beta>0
.
In practice, it is notoriously hard to find stable and robust discretizations in the form of (
5
) for accurate simulations of the convection-dominated problems.
An
ideal PG method
by choosing
V_{h}
with optimal test functions that automatically guarantees the numerical stability, is presented in
[
19
]
.
This is done by letting
V_{h}=T(U_{h})
where
T:U\to V
is the trial-to-test operator defined by

(Tu,v)_{V}=b(u,v),\quad\forall v\in V,

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{V}
denotes the inner product on the test space
V
.
It is also shown in
[
19
]
that the ideal PG method generates numerical solutions that are orthogonal projections of the exact solution in the norm induced by the test norm (the same as the trial norm if
U
and
V
form a duality pairing).
However, the implementation of the ideal PG method in general is difficult.
Therefore, we follow a different strategy suggested by
[
13
]
and solve the discrete mixed problem

\begin{cases}\textnormal{Find }\psi_{h}\in V_{h},u_{h}\in U_{h},&\textnormal{ such that:}\\
(\psi_{h},v_{h})_{V}+b(u_{h},v_{h})&=l(v_{h}),\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},\\
b(w_{h},\psi_{h})&=0,\qquad\forall w_{h}\in U_{h}.\end{cases}

(7)

Equation (
7
) allows us to choose test spaces of much larger dimension, i.e.,
\text{dim}V_{h}\gg\text{dim}U_{h}
, such that the discrete inf-sup condition (
7
) is much easier to satisfy. This is critical for the numerical stability of the convection-dominated problems, which are the major interest of this work.
In addition,
\psi_{h}
serves as a natural a-posteriori error indicator for the adaptive
h
-refinements in the trial space.
We remark that if the test space is not approximated, i.e.,
V_{h}=V
in (
7
), (
7
) is equivalent to the ideal PG method presented by
[
19
]
.

3
Application to the nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion

In this section, we introduce the nonlocal convection-diffusion model. We derive the optimal test space norm, and show the well-posedness of the model problem utilizing the optimal test space norm.
In the existing literature, the well-posedness of the weak form nonlocal convection-diffusion equation is always based on the assumption of coercivity
[
15
,
55
]
. This means that the results in the existing literature only apply to the diffusion-dominated regime. We establish in this section the well-posedness of the convection-dominated problem, and this is done by the PG approach presented in the previous section.

3.1
Model equations

Following
[
14
,
25
]
, we define the
nonlocal diffusion operator
with the nonlocal length scale parameter
\delta>0
in dimension
{\textnormal{d}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
by

\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(\bm{x})=2\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\gamma_{\delta}^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))d\bm{x}\,,

(8)

where
B_{\delta}(\bm{x})\subset\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}}
denotes the Euclidean ball of radius
\delta
centered at a point
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}}
, and

{\gamma}_{\delta}^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{s}|)
is the nonlocal diffusion kernel supported on
\overline{B_{\delta}(\bm{0})}
with the following scaling

\gamma^{\text{diff}}_{\delta}(|\bm{s}|)=\frac{1}{\delta^{{\textnormal{d}}+2}}\gamma^{\text{diff}}\left(\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\delta}\right).

(9)

We assume in this work that
\gamma^{\text{diff}}(r)
is a non-negative and non-increasing function defined on
[0,1]
, and
\gamma^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{s}|)
has a bounded second order moment, i.e.,

\int_{B_{1}(\bm{0})}{\gamma}^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{s}|)|\bm{s}|^{2}d\bm{s}={\textnormal{d}}\,.

(10)

We introduce the
nonlocal gradient operator

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
following by

\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma_{\delta}^{\text{conv}}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))d\bm{y}\,.

(11)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})\subset B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
is an influence region surrounding
\bm{x}
, and
\gamma_{\delta}^{\text{conv}}(|\bm{s}|)
is the nonlocal convection kernel supported on
\overline{B_{\delta}(\bm{0})}
with the scaling

\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{s}|)=\frac{1}{\delta^{{\textnormal{d}}+1}}\gamma^{\text{conv}}\left(\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\delta}\right).

(12)

We assume that
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
is a sector of the ball
B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
and

\frac{|\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})|}{|B_{\delta}(\bm{x})|}=\eta,\quad\forall\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}},

(13)

where
\eta>0
is independent of
\bm{x}
, and
|\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})|
and
|B_{\delta}(\bm{x})|
denote the volume of the sets
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
and
B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
respectively.
The most common choices of
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
are either the full ball
B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
(
\eta=1
)
[
15
,
31
,
32
,
46
]
or a hemispherical subregion of
B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
(
\eta=1/2
)
[
38
,
54
,
55
]
. More detailed discussions on the choices of
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
will be given in
Section

3.3
.
Now with
eq.

13
, we assume that
\gamma^{\text{conv}}(r)
is a non-negative function on
[0,1]
, and that
\gamma^{\text{conv}}(|\bm{s}|)
satisfies

\int_{B_{1}(\bm{0})}{\gamma}^{\text{conv}}(|\bm{s}|)|\bm{s}|d\bm{s}={\textnormal{d}}/\eta.

(14)

As a result,
eq.

11
is a nonlocal analogue of the classical gradient operator
\nabla u
.
In this work, for reasons that will be explained later, we also assume the following relation between the diffusion kernel and the convection kernel:

{\gamma}^{\textnormal{conv}}(|\bm{s}|)=\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\eta}{\gamma}^{\textnormal{diff}}(|\bm{s}|).

(15)

It is worth noting that
eq.

15
is compatible with
eqs.

10
and
14
. Namely, if
eq.

15
is satisfied, we also have

{\gamma}_{\delta}^{\textnormal{conv}}(|\bm{s}|)=\frac{1}{\delta^{{\textnormal{d}}+1}}\gamma^{\text{conv}}\left(\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\delta}\right)=\frac{1}{\delta^{{\textnormal{d}}+2}}\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\eta}{\gamma}^{\textnormal{diff}}\left(\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\delta}\right)=\frac{|\bm{s}|}{\eta}{\gamma}_{\delta}^{\textnormal{diff}}(|\bm{s}|).

For a vector field
\bm{b}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}};\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}})
, the nonlocal convection-diffusion model problem, defined on an open bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{{\textnormal{d}}}
, is formulated as

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\epsilon\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u(\bm{x})+\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u(\bm{x})&=f_{\delta}(\bm{x}),\quad&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
u(\bm{x})&=0,\qquad&\bm{x}\in\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(16)

where
\epsilon
is a positive parameter, and
\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}
is the interaction domain given by

\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\textnormal{d}}}\backslash\Omega:\text{dist}(\bm{x},\partial\Omega)<\delta\}\,.

(17)

We denote
\Omega_{\delta}:=\Omega\cup\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}
for the rest of the work.
We are interested in the convection-dominated regime, namely,
0<\epsilon\ll\|\bm{b}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
. Then,
eq.

16
is called a nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion model, and is a nonlocal analogue of the classical convection-diffusion problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\epsilon\Delta u(\bm{x})+\bm{b}\cdot\nabla u(\bm{x})&=f(\bm{x}),\quad&\bm{x}\in\Omega,\\
u(\bm{x})&=0,\qquad&\bm{x}\in\partial\Omega\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(18)

Remark
3.1
.

The nonlocal convection term can also be formulated in a different way analogous to the differential operator
\nabla\cdot(\bm{b}u)

[
15
,
54
]
.
For this, we need to define the nonlocal divergence operator
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
acting on the function
\bm{b}u
by

\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}u)(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\bm{b}(\bm{x})u(\bm{x})1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y})+\bm{b}(\bm{y})u(\bm{y})1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})}(\bm{x}))\cdot\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\textnormal{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}.

(19)

The analysis in this work would be similar if we replace
\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u(\bm{x})
with
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}u)(\bm{x})
in
eq.

16
mainly because of the integration by parts formula (
20
).

One can show that by defining
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
in (
19
),
-\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
forms an adjoint of
\mathcal{G}_{\delta}
in the sense that

(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u,v)=-(u,\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}v)),

(20)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the inner product in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta})
with
u|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=v|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=0
.
Indeed, since
u|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=v|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=0
, we have

\begin{split}&(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{b}(\bm{x})\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{x})}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))v(\bm{x})d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\\
=&\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y})\frac{\bm{b}(\bm{x})\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{x})}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))v(\bm{x})d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\\
=&-\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\left(1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y})\bm{b}(\bm{x})u(\bm{x})v(\bm{x})+1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})}(\bm{x})\bm{b}(\bm{y})u(\bm{x})v(\bm{y})\right)\cdot\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\\
=&-\int_{\Omega}u(\bm{x})\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\left(1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y})\bm{b}(\bm{x})v(\bm{x})+1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})}(\bm{x})\bm{b}(\bm{y})v(\bm{y})\right)\cdot\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\\
=&-(u,\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}v)).\end{split}

With a similar reasoning which we omit here, we can show that
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
is a self-adjoint operator, i.e.,

(\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u,v)=(u,\mathcal{L}_{\delta}v),

(21)

if
u,v\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta})
and
u|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=v|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=0
.

3.2
Weak formulation

We present the weak formulation of
eq.

16
in this subsection.
We define the natural energy space
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
associated with
eq.

16
by

\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega_{\delta}):\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}{\gamma}_{\delta}^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))^{2}d\bm{y}d\bm{x}<\infty,u|_{\Omega_{\mathcal{I}_{\delta}}}=0\}\,.

(22)

The norm on
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
is given as

\|u\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+|u|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)},

where
|u|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}
is the semi-norm on
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
, defined as

|u|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}{\gamma}_{\delta}^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))^{2}d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\right)^{1/2}\,.

It is shown in
[
43
,
44
]
that
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product

a(u,v):=\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}{\gamma}_{\delta}^{\text{diff}}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))(v(\bm{y})-v(\bm{x}))d\bm{y}d\bm{x}.

Because of the nonlocal Poincaré-type inequalities
[
25
,
43
,
44
]
, the semi-norm
|u|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}
is also a norm on
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
. For the rest of the paper, we will simply let
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}=|\cdot|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}
and call it the energy norm.

Now we can define the bilinear form,
b(\cdot,\cdot)
, associated with
eq.

16
by

b(u,v)=\epsilon a(u,v)+\left(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u,v\right)\,,\quad\forall u,v\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega).

(23)

It is trivial to show that
a(u,v)=(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u,v)
for
u,v\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
, see
[
57
]
.
Then,
eq.

16
can be recast into the weak form

\begin{cases}\textnormal{Find }u\in U,\,\textnormal{ such that}:\\
b(u,v)=(f_{\delta},v),\quad\forall v\in V,\end{cases}

(24)

where
U
and
V
are both spaces of all the functions in
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
. In this work, we always assume that
U
is equipped with the energy norm on
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)
, i.e.,
\|\cdot\|_{U}=\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}
.
The test space
V
, however, can be endowed with different norms. In the next subsection, we will show
V
can be equipped with the optimal test norm in the sense of
eq.

4
.

3.3
Optimal test space norm

Before identifying the optimal test space norm, we need to make some assumptions on the velocity field
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
.
We distinguish two cases. In the first case, we assume that the velocity field is a constant and the influence region
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
that defines the integral in
eq.

11

coincides with
B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
, see
3.2
. In the second case, we allow the velocity field to be a variable with additional assumptions on
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
, see
3.3
.

Assumption 3.2
.

Assume the velocity field
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
is a constant, i.e.,

\bm{b}(\bm{x})\equiv\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\textnormal{d}}},

and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})=B_{\delta}(\bm{x})
.

3.2
leads to the central nonlocal convection-diffusion model termed in
[
54
]
which refers to the fact that the nonlocal gradient operator (
11
) takes a full spherical influence region. It was shown in
[
54
]
that the standard Galerkin approach for such central model leads to significant instabilities. We will establish, however, that such model is indeed well-posed by the PG approach with optimal test norms.

Assumption 3.3
.

Assume the velocity field
\bm{b}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}};\mathbb{R}^{\textnormal{d}})
with
-\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b})\geq 0
, where

\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b})(\bm{x})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\bm{b}(\bm{x})1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y})+\bm{b}(\bm{y})1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})}(\bm{x})\right)\cdot\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\textnormal{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}.

(25)

In addition,
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
is the hemisphere defined by

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})=\left\{\bm{y}\in B_{\delta}(\bm{x}):-\bm{b}(\bm{x})\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{x})>0\right\}.

Equation

Remark
3.4
.

In

, if
\bm{b}(\bm{x})
is a constant, then

. Thus, we only need to assume

. Indeed, if
\bm{b}(\bm{x})\equiv\bm{b}
, then

\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b})(\bm{x})=\bm{b}\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y})+1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})}(\bm{x}))\cdot\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\textnormal{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y},

and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})
and
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})
become

\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})=\left\{\bm{y}\in B_{\delta}(\bm{x}):-\bm{b}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{x})>0\right\},\quad\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})=\left\{\bm{x}\in B_{\delta}(\bm{y}):-\bm{b}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{y})>0\right\}.

For any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{{\textnormal{d}}}
such that
|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|<\delta
, we have

1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{y})}(\bm{x})=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if }-\bm{b}\cdot(\bm{x}-\bm{y})>0\\
0\quad\text{if }-\bm{b}\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{x})>0\end{cases}=1-1_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}(\bm{y}).

Then,

\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b})(\bm{x})=\bm{b}\cdot\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\textnormal{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}=0,

where the last line is understood in the sense of principle value.

Now recall that for problem (

),

and

with the trial norm

To show that

Suppose

From the definition of the energy space

For the second term in

\begin{split}&\left|\left(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u,v\right)\right|=\left|\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{b}(\bm{x})\cdot(\bm{y}-\bm{x})}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}{\gamma}^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)(u(\bm{y})-u(\bm{x}))v(\bm{x})d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\right|,\\

Proof.

Assume there is

By rewriting

\begin{split}(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\psi,u)&={\epsilon}(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta}u,v)+\left(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}u,v\right)=(u,-{\epsilon}\mathcal{L}_{\delta}v)-\left(u,\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}v)\right),\\

3.4
Well-posedness of the nonlocal convection-diffusion problem

In order to establish the well-posedness of

given by

Lemma 3.7
.

Suppose that

or

defined by (

\text{if }b(u,v)=0,\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega),\text{ then }v=0.

Proof.

Since
b(u,v)=0
for all

, taking

, we have

0=b(v,v)={\epsilon}a(v,v)+(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}v,v).

(33)

From the nonlocal Poincaré inequality on
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)

[

43
,
44
]
, we know that

a(v,v)\geq C\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq 0.

Herein and for the rest of the work,

is a generic constant. Next, we address the second term on the right hand side of
eq.

33
based on
3.2
and
3.3
.

If
3.2
is satisfied, then we want to show

\begin{split}0=(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}v,v)&=\bm{b}\cdot\int_{\Omega}\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)v(\bm{y})v(\bm{x})d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\\
&\quad-\bm{b}\cdot\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}\right)v^{2}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}.\end{split}

(34)

The first term on the right hand side of
eq.

34
is zero, because

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)v(\bm{y})v(\bm{x})d\bm{y}d\bm{x}\\

where we have used the antisymmetry of the integrand in the last line.
The second term on the right hand side of

is also zero, since

\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{y}-\bm{x}}{|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{y}-\bm{x}|)d\bm{y}\right)v^{2}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{B_{\delta}(\bm{x})}\frac{\bm{s}}{|\bm{s}|}\gamma^{\text{conv}}_{\delta}(|\bm{s}|)d\bm{s}\right)v^{2}(\bm{x})d\bm{x}=0,

by the antisymmetry of the function

. Therefore, under
3.2
, we have
b(v,v)\geq C\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\geq 0
, and the equality holds only if

.

If
3.3
is satisfied, using

20

\begin{split}&(\bm{b}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\delta}v,v)=-(v,\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}v))\\

For hemispherical influence regions as given in

4.1
Approximations to the optimal test space norm in one dimension

The main difficulty for computing with the optimal test norm given in
3.6
is the term
\left(\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}v),(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{\delta}(\bm{b}v)\right)
, which involves the inverse operator
(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta})^{-1}
. We show next that
(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta})^{-1}
can be approximated using its local limit,
(-\mathcal{L}_{0})^{-1}
.
Since
{\textnormal{d}}=1
and
b(x)\equiv 1
, the local limit of the operators
\mathcal{D}_{\delta}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\delta}
are
\frac{d}{dx}
and
\frac{d^{\,2}}{dx^{2}}
respectively.
In this case, the explicit expression of local limit of the term can be computed as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
{\textnormal{d}}=1
and
\Omega=(0,1)
. Assume that
(-\frac{d^{\,2}}{dx^{2}})^{-1}
is the inverse of
-\frac{d^{\,2}}{dx^{2}}
understood in the sense of homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition, then we have

\left(\frac{d}{dx}v,\left(-\frac{d^{\,2}}{dx^{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}v\right)=\|v-\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2},\quad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,,

where
\overline{v}
denotes the average of
v
on
\Omega
, i.e.,

\overline{v}=\int_{\Omega}v.

Proof.

By letting
\frac{d}{dx}v=w
and
(-\frac{d^{\,2}}{dx^{2}})^{-1}w=g
, we have

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}g&=w=\frac{d}{dx}v,\quad\textnormal{in }\Omega=(0,1)\,,\\
g(0)&=g(1)=0\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(36)

where we have used the fact that
(-\frac{d^{\,2}}{dx^{2}})^{-1}
is understood with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition.
From
eq.

36
, we have

-\frac{d}{dx}g=v+C_{0}\,,

where
C_{0}
is a constant to be determined. From the boundary conditions in
eq.

36
, we have

0=-\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dx}g=\int_{0}^{1}v+C_{0},

which implies
C_{0}=-\overline{v}
. Therefore, we obtain

\begin{split}&\left(\frac{d}{dx}v,\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}v\right)=-\left(v,\frac{d}{dx}\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}v\right)\\
&=-\left(v,\frac{d}{dx}g\right)=(v,v-\bar{v})=(v-\bar{v},v-\bar{v})=\|v-\bar{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\,,\end{split}

where we have used
\int_{\Omega}(v-\overline{v})=0
in the last line.
∎

Using the fact that

\left(\mathcal{D}_{\delta}v,(-\mathcal{L}_{\delta})^{-1}\mathcal{D}_{\delta}v\right)\approx\left(\frac{d}{dx}v,\left(-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\right)^{-1}\frac{d}{dx}v\right)=\|v-\overline{v}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

in one dimension, we arrive at an approximate optimal test norm
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{app},V}
on
V
given by

\|v\|_{\textnormal{app},V}:=\left({\epsilon}^{2}\|v\|^{2}_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}+\|v-\overline{v}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}\,.

(37)

A second choice of the test norm is simply the energy norm, i.e.,

\|v\|_{\textnormal{eng},V}:=\|v\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}(\Omega)}\,.

(38)

In the rest of the paper, we conduct numerical experiments to study how the two test space norms,
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{app},V}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{eng},V}
as defined in
eqs.

37
and
38
, perform in solving the nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problem.

. The finite dimensional trial space

\tilde{p}=p+\delta p>p

V_{h}=\{v\in V:v|_{K}\in\mathcal{Q}_{\tilde{p}}(K),\forall K\in\Omega_{h}\}.

(40)

Therein,
\delta p\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
is the degree of enrichment in the test space compared with the polynomial order in the trial space.

For simplicity, we choose the following nonlocal kernels

\gamma^{\textnormal{diff}}\left(|s|\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{3}{2},\quad&|s|\leq 1\,,\\
0,\quad&|s|>1\,,\end{cases}\textnormal{ and }\gamma^{\textnormal{conv}}\left(|s|\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}|s|,\quad&|s|\leq 1\,,\\
0,\quad&|s|>1\,.\end{cases}

(41)

The parameters are chosen such that

10
and
14
are satisfied. In this case,
\eta=1
since we let
\mathcal{H}_{\delta}(x)=B_{\delta}(x)
. As a result, the nonlocal diffusion and convection kernels are given as

\gamma^{\textnormal{diff}}_{\delta}\left(|s|\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\delta^{3}},\quad&|s|\leq\delta\,,\\
0,\quad&|s|>\delta\,,\end{cases}\textnormal{ and }\gamma^{\textnormal{conv}}_{\delta}\left(|s|\right)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\delta^{3}}|s|,\quad&|s|\leq\delta\,,\\
0,\quad&|s|>\delta\,.\end{cases}

(42)

It is immediate that
eq.

15
is also satisfied. Since the nonlocal kernels are discontinuous over
\mathbb{R}

\Omega_{h}
with balls of radius
\delta

We remark that the intersection of an element and horizon in one dimension,
\Omega_{j}\cap B_{\delta}(x_{p})

[
11
,
16
]
. To reduce the integration error, we have used
p\,(\tilde{p})+N_{over}
quadrature points. Therein,

is the number of extra quadrature points.

Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a PG method for the nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problem using optimal test functions in a general space dimension.
The well-posedness of the nonlocal convection-dominated diffusion problem is established for two types of nonlocal convection kernels, with spherical or hemispherical interaction regions. The optimal test space norm is identified in both abstract and explicit forms. However, the optimal test space noem is not practical in computation as it involves inverting the nonlocal diffusion operator. We instead propose an approximate optimal test norm
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{app},V}
which is easy to implement in one dimension. Using manufactured solutions, convergence results in the nonlocal energy norm and the performance of the approximate optimal test norm are tested in comparison with the simple nonlocal energy norm on the test space
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{eng},V}
.

For a manufactured smooth solution, uniform
h
- and
p
-refinements, and adaptive
h
-refinements are carried out, and the convergence results in
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}
are similar for the two test space norms.
For the uniform
h
-refinements, second-order convergence rates are observed when
\delta
is large while we have obtained first-order convergence rates in when
\delta
is small. The variations in convergence rates for different sizes of
\delta
are due to the characteristics of the nonlocal energy space
\mathcal{S}_{\delta}
. For large
\delta
,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}
is equivalent to the
L^{2}
norm
[
25
]
, and as
\delta
approaches to zero,
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{S}_{\delta}}
converges to the local energy norm,
{H^{1}}
-semi norm
[
4
]
.
Exponential rates have been observed for the uniform
p
-refinements. For the adaptive
h
-refinements, first- and second-order convergence rates are obtained when
\delta=0.1
and
0.00001
, respectively. Moreover, first-order convergence rates are recovered when both
\delta
and
h
go to zero and the observed convergence is independent of the coupling between
\delta
and
h
. This shows that the proposed PG method is asymptotically compatible.

For a manufactured solution with a sharp transition region near the boundary, uniform and adaptive
h
-refinements are considered. For the uniform
h
-refinements, convergence results in the asymptotic regime agree with what we have observed for a manufactured smooth solution, and they are similar for the two test space norms. For the adaptive
h
-refinement, optimal convergence rates are recovered in the asymptotic regime for both test space norms. The superiority of
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{app},V}
over
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{eng},V}
is observed in the pre-asymptotic regime.
While numerical solutions using
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{eng},V}
suffer from significant oscillations on coarse meshes, we observe little (for larger
\delta
) or no (for smaller
\delta
) oscillations of the numerical solutions by using
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{app},V}
.

There are many challenging topics remaining to be addressed in the future. The numerical experiments in this work have been limited to 1d and it would be meaningful to extend this work to higher dimensions.
In higher dimensions, it would be more reasonable to look at the ultra-weak formution
[
17
,
21
]
of the nonlocal problem to avoid the inversion of the diffusion operator in the expression of the optimal test norm. The integration error plays a significant role in the convergence analysis. The discussions on integration rules for more general kernels and higher dimensions are also critical to guarantee the performance of the PG method as predicted by the theory.
Moreover, the perturbation,
\epsilon
, of the diffusion is limited to
0.01
and the polynomial order in test space is chosen ad hoc. Reducing
\epsilon
to smaller values and increasing
\delta
require better practical approximations of the optimal test space norm and more sophisticated strategies, such as the double adaptivity algorithm
[
17
]
, to choose the polynomial order in the test space so as to guarantee numerical stability.
