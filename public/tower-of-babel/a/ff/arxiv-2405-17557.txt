Almost no experiments have classical Kirkwood-Dirac representations
Christopher Langrenez, Wilfred Salmon, Stephan De Bièvre, Jonathan J. Thio, Christopher K. Long, David R. M. Arvidsson-Shukur

Abstract
A central problem in quantum information is determining quantum-classical boundaries. In the quasiprobability framework, a state is called classical if it is represented by a quasiprobability distribution that is positive, and thus a probability distribution. In recent years, the Kirkwood-Dirac (KD) distributions have gained much interest due to their numerous applications in modern quantum-information research. A particular advantage of the KD distributions is that they can be defined with respect to arbitrary observables. Here, we show that if two d d -dimensional observables are picked at random, the set of classical (positive) states of the resulting KD distribution is a minimal polytope of dimension 2 ​ ( d − 1 ) 2(d-1) with 2 ​ d 2d explicitly known vertices. This implies minimality of the sets of KD-real observables, of KD-positive measurement elements and of KD-positivity-preserving unitaries. We show how these results have implications on robust observations of nonclassical phenomena, on classical simulations of quantum circuits, and on foundations of quantum theory.

The KD representation of quantum theory:—

The KD distributions, as do other quasiprobability distributions, provide useful alternatives to the standard Schrödinger picture of quantum mechanics. If
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}:=\min|\innerproduct{a_i}{b_j}|>0
(as we assume henceforth), the KD distribution is informationally complete
[
24
]
: Knowledge of
Q(\rho)
uniquely determines
\rho
. The marginals of
Q(\rho)
reproduce the Born rule:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}Q_{ij}(\rho)=\langle a_{i}|\rho|a_{i}\rangle,\quad\sum_{i}Q_{ij}(\rho)=\langle b_{j}|\rho|b_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}Q_{ij}(\rho)=1.

(2)

Therefore,
Q(\rho)
behaves like a joint probability distribution for the observables
A
and
B
, except that it may take complex values.

To extend the KD formalism to a full representation of quantum mechanics, we introduce the KD symbol
W(F)
of an operator
F
:

W_{ij}(F)=\frac{\matrixelement{a_i}{F}{b_j}}{\innerproduct{a_i}{b_j}}=\frac{Q_{ij}(F)}{|\innerproduct{a_i}{b_j}|^{2}}.

(3)

Here, we extended Eq.(
1
) by linearity to all operators
F
on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
Then, the expectation value
\Tr(F^{\dagger}\rho)
of any operator
F
with respect to any state
\rho
is
[
29
,
4
,
48
]

\Tr(F^{\dagger}\rho)=\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}^{*}(F)Q_{ij}(\rho).

(4)

Mathematically, each matrix element
W_{ij}(F)
is a
weak value
of
F

[
2
]
.
We call an operator KD real if its KD symbol is real. The real vector space of KD-real observables is denoted by
V_{\mathrm{KDr}}
:
F\in V_{\mathrm{KDr}}
provided that
F=F^{\dagger}
and that
W_{ij}(F)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
i,j
. The set
V_{\mathrm{KDr}}
of KD-real observables is in general equally difficult to characterize as the set
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
of KD-positive states.

A generalized measurement (also known as a positive-operator-valued measure) is described by a set
\{M_{k}\}
, the elements of which are positive semi-definite operators that satisfy
\sum_{k}M_{k}=I_{d}
.
We denote by
M_{\textrm{KD}+}
the space of measurement elements
M
with
W_{ij}(M)\geq 0
.

Together, the KD distribution
Q
and the KD symbol
W
provide a quasiprobability representation of quantum mechanics. Their restrictions to
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
, respectively
M_{\textrm{KD}+},
provide a proper probability representation of a (classically describable) “fragment” of quantum mechanics. The states of this fragment belong to
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
and its observables are modeled with generalized measurements with elements in
M_{\textrm{KD}+}
.

We provide a proof in Appendix

(which contains references
[

,

,

,
27
,
39
,
46
,
3
,
1
]
therein). The proof relies on the fact that the zero sets of real analytic functions on

Discussion:—
We have shown that the convex set of KD-positive states,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
, is, with probability
1
, simple and of minimal size. That is,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
is almost always the polytope of all mixtures of the states in
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, of which the supporting hyperplanes are known
[
29
]
.
Also the space of KD-real observables, the set of KD-positivity-preserving unitary transformations, and the set of KD-positive measurement elements are almost always minimal. Below, we discuss four applications of our results, with details in Appendices
D
,
E
and
F
.

First, consider the task of preparing a state
\rho
with the purpose of generating nonclassical phenomena in any of the numerous fields where KD nonpositivity signals quantum advantages. Naturally, one would want
\rho
to be far from
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
, i.e., far from the shaded areas in Fig.
1
.
In general, it can be a formidable task to characterize the region outside
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
. For example, the KD distribution naturally associated to
n
qubits (
d=2^{n}
) has
\mathcal{O}(d^{\log_{2}(d)})
KD-positive pure extremal states and additional elusive mixed extremal states. Then
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
is not necessarily a polytope and determining the distance of
\rho
to this set is often unfeasible. However, our results show that almost all KD distributions have only
2d
explicitly known positive extremal states. For such KD distributions, one can lower bound the distance of any given
\rho
to
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
through the known witnesses associated with the facets of the simple polytope
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}=\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
.

Second, our theorem has applications to the foundational concept of generalized contextuality
[
52
]
. Generalizing Bell nonlocality, generalized contextuality describes the nonclassical phenomenon wherein experimentally indistinguishable procedures cannot be represented identically on a hidden-variable level. Prior works have established that experiments measuring the weak values in Eq. (
3
) are noncontextual (classical) if, and only if, the input state
\rho
is KD-positive
[
44
,
28
,
55
]
. Applying Theorem
1
, we find that the set of input states that yield classical behavior is minimal and well-known for almost all observables
A
and
B
.

Third, certain forms of nonclassical phenomena
[
55
]
stem from
mixed
exotic
states: states that have a positive KD distribution but cannot be decomposed in terms of KD-positive pure states. [See Fig.
1
(b).] Our results show that such states can exist only in rare and tailored KD distributions, such as those associated to certain spin-1
[
29
]
or
n
-qubit systems.

Fourth, Theorem
1
has implications for the prospect of employing the KD distribution for efficient classical simulation of amenable quantum circuits. Quasiprobability distributions can be used to simulate quantum circuits using Monte Carlo sampling
[
43
]
. The runtime of such techniques scales polynomially with the accuracy but exponentially with the total KD nonpositivity of the circuit’s input state, unitary gates, and measurements.
Our results immediately imply that, with probability 1, the only KD-positive circuit is an initialization in
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
followed immediately by a measurement in
M_{\textrm{KD}+}
. Consequently, only rare KD distributions, such as the KD distribution in Appendix
D

[
56
]
, can be expected to be of use in standard protocols for efficient quantum-circuit simulation.

To summarize, our results prove that the characterization of nonclassical features connected with KD distributions is almost always vastly simpler than it can be. Our results also show that rare KD distributions must be tailored to unveil certain aspects of quantum theory. Such KD tailoring will benefit from further studies of the map
C^{U}
.

On the zeroes of a non-zero polynomial function on
{\mathcal{U}}(d)

For convenience, we recall here Proposition
3
. We expect this result to be well known, but we didn’t find a proof in the literature.

Proposition 4
.

Let
f:{\mathcal{U}}(d)\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial in the real and imaginary matrix elements of
U
. Suppose that there exists at least one unitary matrix
{U_{\star}}
for which
f({U_{\star}})\not=0
; then the zero-set of
f
has vanishing Haar measure.

We first recall some known properties of the unitary group
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
.
We then show that the zero set of an analytic function on a connected analytic manifold has zero measure,
implying Proposition
3
. Note that throughout this Appendix, we call a function analytic if it is a real analytic function.

First, we need some basic facts on the unitary group
{\mathcal{U}}(d)=\left\{U\in\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{C}),U^{{\dagger}}U=I_{d}\right\}
that we collect here. We view a matrix
{\mathcal{U}}\in U(d)
as a tuple of
d
columns:

U=(U_{1},\dots,U_{d}).

(A35)

The
U_{j}
satisfy the orthonormality relations

U_{j}^{\dagger}U_{k}=\delta_{jk}.

(A36)

We write

U=R+iS,\quad\textrm{and}\quad U_{j}=R_{j}+iS_{j},

(A37)

for real and imaginary parts, and thus identify
U
with an element of
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
.

The orthonormality relations of equation (
A36
) become, for all
j,k
:

R_{j}^{T}R_{k}+S_{k}^{T}S_{j}=\delta_{jk},\quad R_{j}^{T}S_{k}-S_{j}^{T}R_{k}=0.

(A38)

Proposition 5
.

{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is compact and connected. Moreover, it is a
d^{2}
dimensional analytic submanifold of
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
.

The proof that
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is compact and connected is standard, see for example
[
18
]
. We did not find a proof of the analytic part of the proposition in the literature, although it is certainly well known. We provide an argument below. Let us first recall that a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is analytic if it has, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a convergent Taylor series expansion (in some neighbourhood of
x
). A submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a
k
-dimensonal analytic submanifold if it can be locally parametrized with analytic coordinates
[
58
]
.

Theorem
6
states that a level surface of an analytic function
f
is an analytic submanifold, provided
f
has no critical points on the level surface. Theorem
6
is the adaptation to the analytic setting of a well-known result in the
C^{\infty}
setting, based on the analytic implicit function theorem; we omit the details (see Theorem 5.3 in
[
54
]
and Theorem 6.3.2 in
[
27
]
).

Theorem 6
.

Let
(n,m)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that
m<n
. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be an analytic function and
k\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We denote by
\Sigma_{k}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)=k\right\}
. Suppose that for all
x\in\Sigma_{k}
,
Df(x)
has rank
m
(i.e. is of maximal rank). Then,
\Sigma_{k}
is an analytic submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with dimension
n-m
.

We can now prove Proposition
5
.

Proof of Proposition
5
.

To prove that
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is an analytic manifold of dimension
d^{2}
, we consider the map
f:\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{S}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
, with the notations used in Eq.(
A38
):

f(R,S)=\left(R^{T}R+S^{T}S,R^{T}S-S^{T}R\right).

(A39)

Here,
\mathcal{S}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
is the set of real symmetric
d
by
d
matrices and
\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
the set of real antisymmetric
d
by
d
matrices. Clearly,
f
is analytic since it is a polynomial in the matrix elements of
R
and
S
.
Consequently, using the notation in Theorem
6
, one sees that
{\mathcal{U}}(d)=\Sigma_{k}
with
k=(I_{d},0_{d,d})
where
0_{d,d}
is the zero matrix of dimension
d\times d
and
f
is an analytic function.

Now, for a fixed unitary
U=R_{U}+iS_{U}
, we can define
g_{U}:\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})

g_{U}(R,S)=\left(R_{U}R-S_{U}S,R_{U}S+S_{U}R\right).

(A40)

With this definition, one can check that
f\circ g_{U}=f
for all
U\in{\mathcal{U}}(d)
, meaning that
f
is invariant under the left action of unitary matrices. This implies that it is enough to prove that the derivative of
f
at
(R,S)=(I_{d},0_{d,d})
,
Df(I_{d},0_{d,d})
, is of full rank. We find, for all
(H,K)\in\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R}),\;

Df(I_{d},0_{d,d})(H,K)=(H+H^{T},K-K^{T}).

As any matrix
A
in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
can be written as
\frac{1}{2}A+\frac{1}{2}A^{T}
and any matrix
B\in\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
can be written as
\frac{1}{2}B-\frac{1}{2}B^{T}
, this proves that
Df(I_{d},0_{d,d})
is surjective in
\mathcal{S}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{A}_{d}(R)
,
i.e.

f
is of full rank.

As
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{2d^{2}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{A}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
, we can apply Theorem
6
to conclude that
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is an analytic submanifold of
\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathcal{M}_{d}(\mathbb{R})
of dimension
d^{2}
.
∎

We now turn to the results we need on the zeroes of analytic functions on compact, connected and analytic manifolds.

Theorem 7
.

Let
\Omega
be an open connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{C}
be an analytic function. Let
\mathcal{Z}(f)=\left\{x\in\Omega\mid f(x)=0\right\}
be the set of zeroes of
f
. Then either
\mathcal{Z}(f)
has zero Lebesgue measure or
f
vanishes identically on
\Omega
, meaning that
\mathcal{Z}(f)=\Omega
.

A proof of this theorem can be found in
[
39
]
. To generalise this theorem, we define a set of zero measure on a manifold as follows. A subset
N
of a manifold
M
of dimension
m
is said to have measure zero if on each local chart
\phi:V\to\Omega
,
\phi(V\cap N)
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with vanishing Lebesgue measure. This definition leads to the following theorem on connected and analytic manifolds.

Theorem 8
.

Let
M
be a connected and analytic manifold of dimension
m
. Let
f:M\to\mathbb{C}
be an analytic function and
\mathcal{Z}(f)=\left\{x\in M\mid f(x)=0\right\}
be the set of zeroes of f. Then, either
\mathcal{Z}(f)
has zero measure or
f
vanishes identically on
M
, meaning that
\mathcal{Z}(f)=M
.

Proof.

As
M
is a manifold, it is second-countable and thus
M
is covered by a countable set of charts
\{V_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, i.e.
\cup_{{n\in\mathbb{N}}}V_{n}=M
. Let
\phi_{n}:V_{n}\to\Omega_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
denote the coordinate functions on the charts
V_{n}
.
Note that, if we fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
f\circ\phi_{n}^{-1}:\Omega_{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is an analytic function on an open set of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
(because
M
is analytic). Consequently, by applying Theorem
7
, either
\mathcal{Z}(f\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})
has a zero Lebesgue measure or
\mathcal{Z}(f\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})=\Omega_{n}
, meaning that
f
vanishes on
V_{n}
.

We define two sets:
I
is the set of
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
\mathcal{Z}(f\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})
has zero Lebesgue measure and
J
is the set of
n\in\mathbb{N}
for which
f
vanishes on
V_{n}
. Let
W=\cup_{i\in I}V_{i}
and
Z=\cup_{j\in J}V_{j}
.
Note that
W,Z
are the union of open sets, so are open.
Moreover, as the sets
V_{n}
cover
M
, we find
M=W\cup Z
. Additionally, suppose there exists some
p\in W\cap Z
, so that
p\in V_{i}\cap V_{j}
for some
i\in I
and
j\in J
. Note that, by definition of
J
,
f\circ\phi_{i}^{-1}
vanishes on
\phi_{i}(V_{i}\cap V_{j})\subseteq\Omega_{i}
. However,
\phi_{i}(V_{j}\cap V_{i})
is open in
\Omega_{i}
and non-empty. Thus, it has positive measure in
\Omega_{i}
, so that
f\circ\phi_{i}^{-1}
vanishes on a positive measure subset of
\Omega_{i}
. This contradicts the definition of
I
, and we deduce
W\cap Z=\emptyset
.

Therefore, as
M
is connected, either
W=M
or
Z=M
. If
Z=M
, then
f
vanishes identically on
M
. If
M=W
, then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\phi_{n}(\mathcal{Z}(f)\cap V_{n})=\mathcal{Z}(f\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})
has zero Lebesgue measure. Then, for any chart
\phi:V\to\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\phi(\mathcal{Z}(f)\cap V)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
V_{n}\cap V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\phi(\mathcal{Z}(f)\cap V\cap V_{n})

and thus,

\phi(\mathcal{Z}(f)\cap V)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{N}\\
V_{n}\cap V\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(\phi\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})[\mathcal{Z}(f\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})\cap\phi_{n}(V\cap V_{n})].

Moreover, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(\phi\circ\phi_{n}^{-1})
is analytic, so differentiable, and thus maps measure zero sets to measure zero sets (see
[
46
]
Lemma 7.25). Since the countable union of measure zero sets is itself measure zero, we deduce that
\phi(\mathcal{Z}(f)\cap V)
is measure zero. Since this is true for any chart
(V,\phi)
, we conclude that
\mathcal{Z}(f)
is measure zero in
M
.

∎

Applying Theorem
8
to the special case of
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
, we obtain Proposition
3
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is a compact group, it has a unique measure that is left invariant, called the Haar measure
[
3
]
. Moreover,
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is a compact Lie group
[
58
]
. From Proposition 4.1.14 in
[
1
]
, the Haar measure on
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
is a volume form, meaning that the Haar measure is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on each local coordinate patch. Thus, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{Z}(f):=\{U\in{\mathcal{U}}(d):f(U)=0\}
has zero measure in the sense of Theorem
8
.

Note that the matrix entries
U_{ij},U_{ij}^{*}
are analytic functions on
{\mathcal{U}}(d)
(by the implicit function theorem, Theorem 6.3.2 in
[
27
]
). Since a polynomial of analytic functions is also analytic, we deduce that
f:{\mathcal{U}}(d)\to\mathbb{R}
is analytic. Finally, by assumption
f\neq 0
and we apply Theorem
8
.
∎

As a final remark, we explain how our results imply that the simple structure of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
is robust under small perturbations of the bases. Recall that
\Omega
is defined as the set of all unitary matrices
U
that contain no zeroes. Then, with
\Gamma
as defined in Eq.(
27
), the set
\Omega\cap\Gamma
is open. Consequently, if
U

has no zeroes and is a “good” unitary (
U\in\Gamma
), then all sufficiently small perturbations of
U
are also “good”. Thus, the simple structure of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
is robust under small perturbations of the bases. Conversely, since
\Omega\cap\Gamma
has full measure, it is also dense and thus if two set of basis projectors
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
are such that
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)\subsetneq{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
, then there exist nearby sets of basis projectors
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}
for which
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup{\mathcal{B}}^{\prime}\right)={\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
. In other words, there exists a small perturbation of the sets of basis projectors
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
that do have a simple associated
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
.

Proof that
{U_{\star}}

Appendix C
On the absence of KD-positivity preserving unitaries.

Let
(|a_{i}\rangle)_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}
and
(|b_{j}\rangle)_{j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}
be two orthonormal bases of a Hilbert space of dimension
d
. We denote by
U
the transition unitary matrix between the two bases and by
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
the set of KD positive states associated to the unitary matrix
U
. We say that
T\in\mathcal{U}(d)
is a KD-positivity preserving unitary if and only if
T{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}T^{\dagger}={\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
.
The goal of this section is to prove the following proposition, regarding KD-preserving unitaries.

Proposition 9
.

In any dimension
d\geqslant 3
, there exists a set
\mathcal{V}(d)
of full Haar measure among the set of unitaries
\mathcal{U}(d)
for which the only KD-positivity preserving unitaries
T
are given by the family
\left(T(\theta)=e^{i\theta}I_{d}\right)_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}
.

We now suppose that
U\in\mathcal{W}(d)
, where
\mathcal{W}(d)=\Omega\cap\Gamma
. Here
\Omega
is the set of all unitaries that do not have zeroes, and
\Gamma
is defined in Eq.(
30
). We thus have that
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}=\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
. This implies that
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}^{\mathrm{pure}}={\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}
. As
T
preserves the extreme points of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
,
T
realises a permutation of the set of
2d
elements
{\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}
. The following lemma shows that only specific permutations are allowed.

Lemma 10
.

Suppose that
(|a_{i}\rangle)_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}
and
(|b_{j}\rangle)_{j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}
are two orthonormal bases such that
m_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}>0
and
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}=\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
. If
T
is KD-positivity preserving, then one of the two alternatives occurs:

1.

There exist permutations
(\sigma,\tau)
and phases
(\eta_{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket},(\theta_{j})_{j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
such that

\displaystyle\forall i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\quad T|a_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\eta_{i}}|a_{\sigma(i)}\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\quad T|b_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\theta_{j}}|b_{\tau(j)}\rangle;

(C48)

2.

There exist permutations
(\sigma,\tau)
and phases
(\eta_{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket},(\theta_{j})_{j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
such that

\displaystyle\forall i\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\quad T|a_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\eta_{i}}|b_{\sigma(i)}\rangle,

\displaystyle\forall j\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket,\quad T|b_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{i\theta_{j}}|a_{\tau(j)}\rangle.

(C49)

Proof.

Suppose that we are not in case

We have shown that, with probability one, the set of KD-positive states

We consider for that purpose a KD distribution naturally associated to an
n

n=1

\mathbf{A}_{1}=\left(|0\rangle,|1\rangle\right)
and the basis
\mathbf{B}_{1}=\left(|+\rangle,|-\rangle\right)

If

n

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\varepsilon_{i}\rangle\mid\varepsilon_{i}\in\left\{0,1\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}|\eta_{i}\rangle\mid\eta_{i}\in\left\{-,+\right\}\right\}.

(D68)

This KD representation can be viewed as being associated to the group
\left(\mathbb{Z}_{2}\right)^{n}

8

Appendix E
Witnesses of
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)

In this section, we show how the simple structure of
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
can be used to construct estimates of the distance of a state
\rho_{\star}
to
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
. In
[
29
]
, the authors proved that the facets of
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
are given by
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}_{i}\cup{\mathcal{B}}_{j}\right)
for all
(i,j)\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket^{2}
where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{|a_{\ell}\rangle\bra{a_\ell}\mid\ell\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\backslash\{i\}\right\}\

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{|b_{k}\rangle\bra{b_k}\mid k\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\backslash\{j\}\right\}.

For a fixed
(i,j)\in\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket^{2}
, the bounding plane associated to the facet is given by

\displaystyle|a_{p}\rangle\bra{a_p}+

\displaystyle{\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}}{\left\{|a_{\ell}\rangle\bra{a_\ell}-|a_{p}\rangle\bra{a_p},|b_{k}\rangle\bra{b_k}-|a_{p}\rangle\bra{a_p}\right\}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\neq i\\
k\neq j\end{subarray}}

where
p=\min\llbracket 1,d\rrbracket\backslash\{i\}
. This set is an affine hyperplane inside the set
E={\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}})}\cap\left\{F\in\mathcal{S}_{d}(\mathbb{C})\mid\Tr(F)=1\right\}
and a bounding plane of
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
. Thus, there exists
\eta_{ij}\in E
such that :

\forall\rho\in\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right),\Tr(\eta_{ij}\rho)\leqslant\Tr(\eta_{ij}\cket{a_p}\bra{a_p}).

(E72)

Such witnesses can be explicitly constructed thanks to the simple structure of
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
. As an example, when the transition matrix is the DFT matrix in dimension
3
, the witnesses are given by

\forall(i,j)\in\llbracket 1,3\rrbracket^{2},\eta_{ij}=I_{3}-|a_{i}\rangle\bra{a_i}-|b_{j}\rangle\bra{b_j}.

(E73)

Suppose that such a witness
\eta_{ij}
is given and that
\rho_{\star}
is a quantum state satisfying that
\Tr(\eta_{ij}\rho_{\star})>\Tr(\eta_{ij}\cket{a_p}\bra{a_p})
. Then,

0<\frac{\Tr\left(\eta_{ij}\left(\rho_{\star}-|a_{p}\rangle\bra{a_p}\right)\right)}{\left|\left|\eta_{ij}\right|\right|_{2}}\leqslant\mathrm{d}\left(\rho_{\star},\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)\right)

(E74)

where
\mathrm{d}\left(\rho_{\star},A\right)
denotes the distance of
\rho_{\star}
to the set
A
.

One can then use Theorem
1
from the paper in the following manner. If the transition matrix is in
\Gamma\cap\Omega
, we know
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}=\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)
, so that we obtain a lower bound on the distance of
\rho_{\star}
to
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
, given by

\mathrm{d}\left(\rho_{\star},{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}\right)\geqslant\frac{\Tr(\eta_{ij}\rho_{\star})-\Tr(\eta_{ij}\cket{a_p}\bra{a_p})}{\left|\left|\eta_{ij}\right|\right|_{2}}>0.

(E75)

Obtaining such a bound whenever
\mathrm{conv}\left({\mathcal{A}}\cup{\mathcal{B}}\right)\subsetneq{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
is considerably more difficult because in that case
{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathrm{KD+}}
may not be a polytope, and even when it is, it may be very difficult to determine its bounding planes.

Appendix F
Simulability

A KD-positive quantum circuit is composed of a KD-positive input state, KD-positive unitary gates and KD-positive measurement elements. KD-positive states and KD-positive measurement elements were defined in the main text. KD-positive gates are defined as follows. Let
T
be a unitary on
{\mathcal{H}}
, then there exist
K_{ij:i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{C}
so that

Q(T\rho T^{\dagger})_{ij}=\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}K_{ij:i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}Q_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}(\rho),

(F76)

with

\sum_{ij}K_{ij:i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}=1,\forall i^{\prime},j^{\prime}.

A unitary
T
is said to be KD-positive if all
K_{ij:i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\geqslant 0
. As explained in the Discussion section, such circuits can be efficiently simulated classically.

To see how Theorem
1
impacts the problem of efficient simulability, we first note that, clearly, a KD-positive unitary is KD-positivity preserving. Hence Theorem
1
implies that, with probability one, the only quantum circuits that are KD-positive are those that have as input states (convex mixtures of) the basis states, followed immediately by a KD-positive measurement.
