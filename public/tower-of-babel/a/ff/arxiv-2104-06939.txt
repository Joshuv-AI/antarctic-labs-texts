Abstract
. Recently a continuous description of the particle swarm optimization (PSO) based on a system of stochastic differential equations was proposed by Grassi and Pareschi in [ 27 ] where the authors formally showed the link between PSO and the consensus-based optimization (CBO) through zero-inertia limit. This paper is devoted to solving this theoretical open problem proposed in [ 27 ] by providing a rigorous derivation of CBO from PSO through the limit of zero inertia, and a quantified convergence rate is obtained as well. The proofs are based on a probabilistic approach by investigating both the weak and strong convergence of the corresponding stochastic differential equations (SDEs) of Mckean type in the continuous path space and the results are illustrated with some numerical examples.



Keywords:
Swarm optimization, consensus-based optimization, Laplace’s principle, tightness.

1.
Introduction

Over the last decades, large systems of interacting particles are widely used in the investigation of complex systems that
model collective behavior (or swarming), an area that has attracted a great deal of attention; see for instance
[
14
,
5
,
29
,
46
]
and references therein.
Such complex systems frequently appear in modeling phenomena such as biological swarms
[
17
]
, crowd dynamics
[
6
]
, self-assembly of nanoparticles
[
30
]
, and opinion formation
[
46
]
. In the field of global optimization, similar particle models are also used in
metaheuristics

[
1
,
4
,
8
,
26
]
, which provide empirically robust solutions to tackle hard optimization problems with fast algorithms. Metaheuristics are methods that orchestrate an interaction between local improvement procedures and global/high level strategies, and combine random and deterministic decisions,
to create a process capable of escaping from local optima and performing a robust search of a solution space. In the sequel, we consider the following optimization problem

(1.1)

x^{*}\in\mbox{argmin}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{E}(x)\,,

where
\mathcal{E}(x):~\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given continuous cost function.

One noble example of metaheuristics is the so-called Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), which was initially introduced to model the intelligent collective behavior of complex biological systems such as flocks of birds or schools of fish
[
38
,
39
,
51
]
, and it is now widely recognized as an efficient method for tackling complex optimization problems
[
48
,
43
]
. Certain convergence and stability analysis of PSO may be found, for instance, in
[
10
,
53
,
50
,
49
]
and readers would be referred to
[
54
]
for a review on the PSO method and its variants and applications.
The PSO method solves optimization problem (
1.1
) by considering a group of candidate solutions, which are represented by particles. Then the algorithm moves those particles in the search space according to certain mathematical relationships on the particle position and velocity. Each particle is driven to its best known local location, which is updated once the particles find better positions.
However, the mathematical understanding of PSO is still in its infancy. Recently Grassi and Pareschi
[
27
]
took a significant first step towards a mathematical theory for PSO based on a continuous description in the form of a system of stochastic differential equations:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}^{i,m}=V_{t}^{i,m}dt,\\
dV_{t}^{i,m}=-\frac{\gamma}{m}V_{t}^{i,m}dt+\frac{\lambda}{m}(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m})-X_{t}^{i,m})dt+\frac{\sigma}{m}D(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m})-X_{t}^{i,m})dB_{t}^{i},\quad i=1,\cdots,N\,,\end{cases}

where the
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued functions
X_{t}^{i,m}
and
V_{t}^{i,m}
denote the position and velocity of the
i
-th particle at time
t
,
m>0
is the inertia weight,
\gamma=1-m\geq 0
is the friction coefficient,
\lambda>0
is the acceleration coefficient,
\sigma>0
is the diffusion coefficient, and
\{(B_{t}^{i})_{t\geq 0}\}_{i=1}^{N}
are
N
independent
d
-dimensional Brownian motions.
We also use the notations for the diagonal matrix

D(X_{t}):=\mbox{diag}\{(X_{t})_{1},\dots,(X_{t})_{d}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\,,

where
(X_{t})_{k}
is the
k
-th component of
X_{t}
, and the weighted average is given by

(1.3)

{X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m}):=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho^{N,m}(t,dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho^{N,m}(t,dx)}

with the empirical measure
\rho^{N,m}(t,dx):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i,m}}(dx)
. So we can rewrite

(1.4)

D(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m})-X_{t}^{i,m})dB_{t}^{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m})-X_{t}^{i,m})_{k}d(B_{t}^{i})^{k}e_{k}\,,

where
e_{k}
is the unit vector in the
k
-th dimension for
k=1,\dots,d
. Noise of the form (
1.4
) is called the anisotropic component-wise noise, which was used in
[
15
,
23
]
to remove the dimensionality dependence for the CBO method.
Furthermore, the initial data
(X_{0}^{i},V_{0}^{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) with the common distribution
f_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, where
\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
denotes the space of probability measures with finite fourth moment, endowed with the Wasserstein distance
[
3
]
. The choice of the weight function

\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x):=\exp(-\alpha\mathcal{E}(x))

comes from the well-known Laplace’s principle
[
45
,
19
]
, a classical asymptotic method for integrals, which states that for any probability measure
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there holds

(1.5)

\lim\limits_{\alpha\to\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\log\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho(dx)\right)\right)=\inf\limits_{x\in\rm{supp}(\rho)}\mathcal{E}(x)\,.

Thus for
\alpha
large enough, one expects that

{X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m})\approx\mbox{argmin }\{\mathcal{E}(X_{t}^{1,m}),\dots,\mathcal{E}(X_{t}^{N,m})\}\,,

which means that
{X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N,m})
is a global best location at time
t
.

Before starting our analysis of the PSO dynamics (
1.2
), let us illustrate
numerically the behavior of the dynamics for the benchmark Ackley function

\mathcal{E}(x)=-20\exp\left(-\frac{0.2}{\sqrt{d}}\left|x-x^{*}\right|\right)-\exp\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\cos\left(2\pi\left(x_{k}-x_{k}^{*}\right)\right)\right)+e+20

in the case of

.
In Figures

x^{*}
and then apply a discretization scheme (which will be explained in Section

) to the system (
1.2

x^{*}
, and particles’ velocity converges to zero.

As it has been shown in
[
27
]
, in the zero-inertia limit (
m\to 0
), one may expect to obtain the recent developed Consensus-Based Optimization (CBO) dynamics
[
12
,
28
,
47
,
25
]
satisfying

(1.6)

dX_{t}^{i}=\lambda(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N})-X_{t}^{i})dt+\sigma D(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N})-X_{t}^{i})dB_{t}^{i},\quad i=1,\cdots,N\,,

where

{X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{N}):=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho^{N}(t,dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho^{N}(t,dx)}\mbox{ with }\rho^{N}(t,dx):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{X_{t}^{i}}(dx)\,.

It has been proved that CBO is a powerful and robust method to solve many interesting non-convex high-dimensional optimization problems in machine learning
[
15
]
. By now, CBO methods have also been generalized to optimization over manifolds
[
22
,
24
,
40
,
23
]
. The objective of the present paper is to complete a theory gap suggested in
[
27
]
by providing a rigorous proof of the zero-inertia limit.

On the one hand, as
N\to\infty
, the mean-field limit results (see
[
9
,
31
,
11
,
52
,
35
,
33
,
32
]
for instance) indicate that our PSO dynamics (
1.2
) converges to the solutions of the following mean-field nonlinear Mckean systems:

(1.7a)

\displaystyle d\overline{X}_{t}^{m}=\overline{V}_{t}^{m}dt,

(1.7b)

\displaystyle d\overline{V}_{t}^{m}=-\frac{\gamma}{m}\overline{V}_{t}^{m}dt+\frac{\lambda}{m}(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})-\overline{X}_{t}^{m})dt+\frac{\sigma}{m}D(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})-\overline{X}_{t}^{m})dB_{t}\,,

where

(1.8)

X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}x\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho^{m}(t,dx)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}^{\mathcal{E}}(x)\rho^{m}(t,dx)},\quad\rho^{m}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{m}(t,x,dv)\,,

and the initial data
(\overline{X}_{0},\overline{V}_{0})
is the same as in (
1.2
). Here
f^{m}(t,x,v)
is the distribution of
(\overline{X}_{t}^{m},\overline{V}_{t}^{m})
at time
t
, which makes the set of equations (
1.7
) nonlinear. We refer to
[
33
]
for a proof the well-posedness of the PSO particle system (
1.2
) and its mean-field dynamic (
1.7
). A direct application of the Itô-Doeblin formula yields that the law
f_{t}^{m}:=f^{m}(t,\cdot,\cdot)
at time
t
is a weak solution to the following nonlinear Vlasov-Fokker-Plank equation

(1.9)

\partial_{t}f^{m}_{t}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f^{m}_{t}=\nabla_{v}\cdot\left(\frac{\gamma}{m}vf^{m}_{t}+\frac{\lambda}{m}\left(x-{X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})\right)f_{t}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2m^{2}}\left[D\left(x-{X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})\right)\right]^{2}\nabla_{v}f^{m}_{t}\right),

with the initial data
f^{m}_{0}(x,v)=\mbox{Law}(\overline{X}_{0},\overline{V}_{0})
. On the other hand, taking
N\to\infty
in (
1.6
) leads to the mean-field CBO dynamic of the form

(1.10)

\displaystyle d\overline{X}_{t}=\lambda(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho)-\overline{X}_{t})dt+\sigma D(X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho)-\overline{X}_{t})dB_{t}

with
\rho_{t}=\mbox{Law}(\overline{X}_{t})
satisfying the corresponding CBO equation

(1.11)

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\lambda\nabla_{x}\cdot(\rho_{t}({X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho)-x))=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\left(\rho_{t}\left(x_{j}-({X}_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho))_{j}\right)^{2}\right)\,.

In this paper, we prove that in the zero-inertia limit, as
m\to 0^{+}
, the processes
\{\overline{X}^{m}\}
satisfying SDEs (
1.7
) converge to the solution
\overline{X}
to the SDE (
1.10
) in the continuous path space
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. A convergence rate is obtained and the generalization to the case with memory effects is also addressed. This is related to the study of the overdamped limit
[
41
,
16
,
20
]
, or large friction limit
[
13
,
36
,
21
]
for Vlasov type equations. However, the nonlinear term
X_{t}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})
here makes our model very different from theirs, which is nonstandard in the literature. Moreover, all of those results mentioned earlier are obtained through the investigation of PDEs like (
1.9
) and (
1.11
), while in the present paper we adopt a probabilistic approach by investigating the convergence of the non-Markovian stochastic processes
\{\overline{X}^{m}\}
satisfying the SDE (
1.7
) to the solution
\{\overline{X}\}
to SDE (
1.10
)) in the continuous path space.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Section 2 we verify the tightness of the PSO model (
1.7
) through Aldous criteria, which allows us to obtain the zero-inertia limit from the PSO model (
1.7
) towards the CBO model (
1.10
) as
m\to 0^{+}
; see Theorem
2.4
. Then in Section 3 we generalize the result to the PSO model with memory effects of the local best positions. Lastly we conclude this paper in Section 4 by reporting a few instructive numerical experiments that aim to validating the zero-inertia limit.

2.
Zero-inertia limit

Throughout this work, the letter
C
denotes a generic constant whose value may vary from line to line and its dependence on certain model parameters will be specified whenever needed. We start this section with the standing assumption on the cost function
\mathcal{E}
.

Assumption 1
.

The given cost function
\mathcal{E}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is locally Lipschitz continuous and satisfies the properties:

(1)

There exists some constant
L>0
such
|\mathcal{E}(x)-\mathcal{E}(y)|\leq L(|x|+|y|)|x-y|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(2)

\mathcal{E}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.
-\infty<\underline{\mathcal{E}}:=\inf\mathcal{E}\leq\mathcal{E}\leq\sup\mathcal{E}=:\overline{\mathcal{E}}<+\infty
, and define
C_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}:=e^{\alpha(\overline{\mathcal{E}}-\underline{\mathcal{E}})}
.

The following theorem gives the well-posedness of the mean-field PSO dynamics (
1.7
) whose proof is analogous to
[
33
, Theorem 2.3]
and
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
, and thus omitted.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. If
(\overline{X}^{m}_{0},\overline{V}^{m}_{0})=(\overline{X}_{0},\overline{V}_{0})
is distributed according to
f_{0}
with
f_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
, then for each
m\in(0,1]
and
T>0
, the nonlinear SDE (
1.7
) admits a unique strong solution up to time
T
with the initial data
(\overline{X}^{m}_{0},\overline{V}^{m}_{0})
, and it holds further that

(2.1)

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}_{t}^{m}|^{4}+|\overline{V}_{t}^{m}|^{4}\right]\leq e^{CT}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}_{0}|^{4}+|\overline{V}_{0}|^{4}\right]\,,

where
C
depends only on
\lambda,m,\sigma
, and
C_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}}
.

Solving (
1.7b
) for
\overline{V}_{t}^{m}
gives

\overline{V}_{t}^{m}=e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}t}\left(\overline{V}_{0}+\frac{\lambda}{m}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\frac{\gamma s}{m}}(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m})ds+\frac{\sigma}{m}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\frac{\gamma s}{m}}D(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m})dB_{s}\right)\,,

which implies that

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{t}^{m}

\displaystyle=\overline{X}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\overline{V}_{\tau}d\tau=\overline{X}_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}\tau}\overline{V}_{0}d\tau+\frac{\lambda}{m}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{\frac{\gamma}{m}s}(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m})dsd\tau

(2.2)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\sigma}{m}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m}\tau}\int_{0}^{\tau}e^{\frac{\gamma}{m}s}D(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m})dB_{s}d\tau\,.

Then
\overline{X}_{t}^{m}
has the law
\rho^{m}_{t}
for each
t\geq 0
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, denote by
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{k})
the space of all
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued continuous functions on
[0,T]
equipped with the usual uniform norm
\|\cdot\|_{0}
.
Each continuous stochastic process
\overline{X}^{m}
may be seen as a
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
-valued random function and it induces a probability measure (or law, denoted by
\rho^{m}
) on
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We shall use the convergence in the space of probability measures on
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In what follows, we write
\overline{X}^{m}\rightharpoonup\overline{X}
or
\rho^{m}\rightharpoonup\rho
with
\rho
being the law of
\overline{X}
, if
\left\{\rho^{m}\right\}_{m>0}
, as a sequence of probability measures, converges weakly to
\rho
, i.e.,
for each bounded continuous functional
\Phi
on
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there holds
\lim_{m\rightarrow 0^{+}}\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(\overline{X}^{m})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\Phi(\overline{X})\right]
. The weak convergence
\overline{X}^{m}\rightharpoonup\overline{X}
is stronger than, and actually implies the convergence of
\{\rho^{m}_{t}\}_{m>0}
to
\rho_{t}
with
\rho_{t}
being the law of
\overline{X}_{t}
for each
t\geq 0
, while the converse need not hold. Moreover, due to the separability and completeness of the space
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
, Prohorov’s theorem implies that the relative compactness is equivalent to the tightness; see
[
7
]
for more details.

The proof of zero-inertia limit will proceed in two steps:

•

The tightness of the sequence of probability distributions
\{\rho^{m}\}_{0<m\leq 1}
of
\{\overline{X}^{m}\}_{0<m\leq 1}
is justified by using Aldous tightness criteria.

•

We will check that all the limit points of
\{\overline{X}^{m}\}_{0<m\leq 1}
as
m\to 0
satisfy mean-field CBO dynamic (
1.10
) which in fact admits a unique strong solution.

For the sake of completeness, we recall a result concluded directly from the Aldous tightness criteria
[
37
, Theorem 4.5]
; it might have been existing somewhere which, however, we did not find, so its straightforward proof is sketched below.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\{X^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and valued in
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The sequence of probability distributions
\{\mu_{X^{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{X^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight if the following two conditions hold.

(Con1)
For all
t\in[0,T]
, the set of distributions of
X_{t}^{n}
, denoted by
\{\mu_{X_{t}^{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, is tight in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(Con2)
For all
\varepsilon>0
,
\eta>0
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}
and for all
(\sigma(X^{n}_{s};s\in[0,t]))_{t\geq 0}
-stopping times
\beta
satisfying
0\leq\beta+\delta_{0}\leq T
, it holds that

(2.3)

\sup_{\delta\in[0,\delta_{0}]}\mathbb{P}\left(|X^{n}_{\beta+\delta}-X^{n}_{\beta}|\geq\eta\right)\leq\varepsilon\,.

Sketched proof.

First, we note that the Aldous tightness criteria is normally stated for the tightness of stochastic processes valued in the space of all càdlàg (right continuous with left limits) functions under the so-called Skorokhod topology (see
[
2
, Theorem 1]
,
[
7
, Theorem 16.10, Page 178]
or
[
37
, Theorem 4.5, Page 356]
). In fact, by the assertion at the beginning of the proof of
[
2
, Theorem 1]
, the condition
(Con2)
implies Hypothesis (A) therein (see
[
2
, Pages 335 & 338]
). Therefore, applying
[
2
, Theorem 1]
directly gives the tightness of
\{X^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the space of all càdlàg functions under the Skorokhod topology.

Noteworthily, the condition
(Con1)
implies that

(2.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}^{n}|>\eta)=0,\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T],

Indeed, for each
t\in[0,T]
and
\epsilon>0
, the tightness in condition
(Con1)
indicates that there exists a compact set
\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(X_{t}^{n}\notin\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon})<\epsilon
. Choosing
\eta_{\epsilon}>0
to be so big that
\mathcal{K}_{\epsilon}\subset\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,|x|\leq\eta_{\epsilon}\}
, we have
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}^{n}|>\eta_{\epsilon})<\epsilon
, which by the arbitrariness of

and
\epsilon
implies
(
2.4
).

Further, by
[
37
, Proposition 3.26 (i) & (iii), Page 351]
, because each
X^{n}
herein is valued in
\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d})
having continuous trajectories, we have the
\mathcal{C}
-tightness of
\{X^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
which means the time-continuity of the paths of the limit(s), whereas this is still under the Skorokhod topology. However, in view of the equivalence of
\mathcal{C}
-tightness in
[
37
, Proposition 3.26 (i) & (ii), Page 351]
, the
\mathcal{C}
-tightness implies further that for all
\epsilon>0
and
\eta>0
, there are
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in(0,T]
such that

(2.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq N_{0}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{r,s\in[0,T],\,|r-s|\leq\theta}|X^{n}_{s}-X^{n}_{r}|>\eta\right)\leq\epsilon.

Combining (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), we may use the tightness criteria for probability measures on
\left(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\|\cdot\|_{0}\right)
in
[
7
, Theorem 7.3, Page 82]
to obtain the tightness of
\{\mu_{X^{n}}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

on
\left(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\|\cdot\|_{0}\right)
that is endowed with the uniform norm.

Theorem 2.2

(Tightness)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and
(X_{t}^{m},V_{t}^{m})_{t\in[0,T]}
satisfy the system (
1.7
). For each countable subsequence
\{m_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,1]
with
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}m_{k}=0
, the sequence of probability distributions
\{\rho^{m_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\{\overline{X}^{m_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is tight on
\left(\mathcal{C}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{d}),\,\|\cdot\|_{0}\right)
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to justify conditions
(Con1)
and
(Con2)
in Lemma
2.1
.

\bullet

Step 1: Checking
(Con1)
.

First, for
0<m\leq\frac{1}{2}
, recalling (
2
), we have by Fubini’s theorem (see
[
18
, Theorem 4.33]
for the stochastic version)

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{t}^{m}

Proof.

For the unique strong solution

(by (

\displaystyle\leq C\,m,\quad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

\displaystyle 8\,\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in[0,t]}\left|\xi_{s}\right|\right]

\displaystyle|\theta_{t}|^{4}

Theorem 2.4

(Zero-inertia limit)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\overline{X}^{m_{k}}-\widehat{X}\|_{0}=0\right)=1,

a.s. In the following, we shall verify that the limit

Recall that the unique strong solution to an SDE may be regarded as a function of the initial value and the driving Wiener process (see

\displaystyle\overline{X}_{t}^{m_{k}}

\displaystyle=\overline{X}_{0}+\frac{m_{k}}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\frac{\gamma}{{m_{k}}}t})\overline{V}_{0}+\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}\int_{0}^{t}(1-e^{-\frac{\gamma}{{m_{k}}}(t-s)})(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}})ds

(2.39)

\displaystyle\leq C\,m_{k},

(2.47)

\displaystyle\xi_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}(Z^{k}_{s})^{\prime}D(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}})dB_{s},\quad t\in[0,T]\,

with

being the transpose of

defines a continuous martingale with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{s\in[0,T]}\left|\xi_{s}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\int_{0}^{T}|Z^{k}_{s}|^{2}\left|X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}}\right|^{2}ds\Big)^{1/2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}|Z^{k}_{s}|^{2}ds\right]\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\left|X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}}\right|^{4}\right]\right)^{1/4}

(2.48)

(by (
2.24
), (
2.40
), and (
2.46
))

\displaystyle\leq C\,|m_{k}|^{1/2}.

Using the Itô-Doeblin formula as in (

(2.49)

\displaystyle|Z^{k}_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{2\gamma(t-s)}{m_{k}}}\left|X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}}\right|^{2}ds+2\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{2\gamma(t-s)}{{m_{k}}}}d\xi_{s}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{2\gamma(t-s)}{m_{k}}}ds\cdot\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}}\right|^{2}+2\xi_{t}-\frac{4\gamma}{m_{k}}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{2\gamma(t-s)}{m_{k}}}\xi_{s}\,ds

(2.50)

\displaystyle\leq C{m_{k}}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left|X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}}\right|^{2}+4\max_{s\in[0,t]}|\xi_{s}|,\quad\text{a.s.}

Putting (
2.24
), (
2.40
), (
2.48
), and (
2.50
) together, we obtain that

(2.51)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\tau\in[0,t]}\left|Z^{k}_{\tau}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C\,\sqrt{m_{k}}.

In addition, it is obvious that

(2.52)

\displaystyle\left|\frac{m_{k}}{\gamma}(1-e^{-\frac{\gamma}{m_{k}}t})\overline{V}_{0}\right|\leq Cm_{k}\left|\overline{V}_{0}\right|.

As the constants
C
s are independent of
(k,t)
, combining the estimates (
2.44
), (
2.45
), (
2.51
) and (
2.52
), letting

2.39
) and recalling

and the relation (

\displaystyle\widehat{X}_{t}=\overline{X}_{0}+\lambda\int_{0}^{t}(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho)-\widehat{X}_{s})ds+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}D(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho)-\widehat{X}_{s})dB_{s}.

Therefore, the limit

Meanwhile, in view of the continuity of

. Thus, we must have

) with
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t\in[0,T]}|\overline{X}_{t}|^{4}\right]\leq C_{2}
. Further, due to the arbitrariness of the subsequence
\{\overline{X}^{m_{k}}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\overline{X}
to SDE (
2.35
).

Finally, to measure the distance between
\overline{X}^{m}
and the limit
\widehat{X}=\overline{X}

\widehat{X}
with
\overline{X}

), (

), (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\tau\in[0,t]}|\overline{X}^{m}_{\tau}-\overline{X}_{\tau}|^{2}\right]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}^{m}_{s}-\overline{X}_{s}|^{2}]ds+C\,\sqrt{m},\quad t\in[0,T],

where the constant

is independent of
(m,t)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}^{m}_{t}-\overline{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}^{m}_{s}-\overline{X}_{s}|^{2}]ds+C\,m,\quad t\in[0,T],

with

(2.53)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t\in[0,T]}|\overline{X}^{m}_{t}-\overline{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\,\sqrt{m},\quad\max_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[|\overline{X}^{m}_{t}-\overline{X}_{t}|^{2}\right]\leq C\,m,

where the constants

\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{0}|^{4}],\mathbb{E}[|\overline{V}_{0}|^{4}],C_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}},\lambda,\sigma,d

Remark 2.2
.

When proving the convergence of
\{\overline{X}^{m}\}
satisfying SDEs (
1.7
) to the solution
\overline{X}
of (
1.10
), we cannot expect the convergence of the associated velocity processes
\{\overline{V}^{m}\}
due to the indifferentiability of the limit
\{\overline{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with respect to time
t
if
\sigma\neq 0
. Therefore, we do not investigate the convergence of the joint Markovian process
\{(\overline{X}^{m},\overline{V}^{m})\}
and consider instead solely the process
\{\overline{X}^{m}\}
which satisfies a stochastic equation (
2.39
) of Volterra type, being path-dependent and thus non-Markovian. This non-Markovianity prevents us from using the usual techniques for weak convergence with martingale problems but prompts us to identify the limit by measuring directly the distance between
\overline{X}^{m_{k}}
and
\widehat{X}=\overline{X}
in the above proof.

Regarding to the rate of convergence in (
2.53
), the
L^{2}
-estimate of the uniform norm of the paths of
\overline{X}^{m}-\overline{X}
is on the order of
\sqrt{m}
while the
L^{2}
-estimate for each time
t\in[0,T]
has order of
m
. Such a difference stems from the relation (
2.49
) which also reads

|Z^{k}_{t}|^{2}=\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{2\gamma(t-s)}{m_{k}}}\left|X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}}\right|^{2}ds+2\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\frac{2\gamma(t-s)}{{m_{k}}}}(Z^{k}_{s})^{\prime}D(X_{s}^{\alpha}(\rho^{m_{k}})-\overline{X}_{s}^{m_{k}})dB_{s}.

Here the stochastic integral has mean zero and taking expectation on both sides can directly give us the order of
m
as seen in the first term on the right hand side of (
2.50
); however, when considering the uniform norm, we cannot ignore the stochastic integral and its
linear
dependence on
(Z^{k}_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
leads to the order
\sqrt{m}
. In addition, in the above proof, because both
\overline{X}^{m_{k}}
and
\overline{X}
are the strong solutions to associated SDEs with pathwise uniqueness and a strong solution is a functional of the initial value and the driving Wiener process (see
[
34
, Chapter IV]
), the following convergence

(2.54)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\overline{X}^{m_{k}}-\overline{X}\|_{0}=0\right)=1,

actually holds for strong solutions to SDEs (
1.7
) and (
2.35
) on any given probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
equipped with augmented filtration generated by a
d
-dimensional Wiener process
B
. Indeed, some experiments conducted at the end of Section 4 (see Figures
9
,
10
, and
11
) numerically support the convergences established here.

3.
Generalization to the case with memory effects

In
[
27
]
, the authors considered a PSO model which involves the memory of the local best positions, and it is of the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle d\overline{X}_{t}^{m}=\overline{V}_{t}^{m}dt,

(3.2)

\displaystyle d\overline{Y}_{t}^{m}=\nu\left(\overline{X}_{t}^{m}-\overline{Y}_{t}^{m}\right)S^{\beta}\left(\overline{X}_{t}^{m},\overline{Y}_{t}^{m}\right)dt,

\displaystyle d\overline{V}_{t}^{m}=-\frac{\gamma}{m}\overline{V}_{t}^{m}dt+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{m}\left(\overline{Y}_{t}^{m}-\overline{X}_{t}^{m}\right)dt+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{m}\left({Y}_{t}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}^{m})-\overline{X}_{t}^{m}\right)dt

(3.3)

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt\hskip 18.49988pt+\frac{\sigma_{1}}{m}D\left(\overline{Y}_{t}^{m}-\overline{X}_{t}^{m}\right)dB_{t}^{1}+\frac{\sigma_{2}}{m}D\left({Y}_{t}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}^{m})-\overline{X}_{t}^{m}\right)dB_{t}^{2}\,,

where
B^{1}
and
B^{2}
are two mutually independent d-dimensional Wiener processes, and similarly to the previous section, we introduce the following regularization of the global best
position

(3.4)

{Y}_{t}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho}^{m})=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\omega_{\alpha}(y){\overline{\rho}}^{m}(t,dy)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\omega_{\alpha}(y){\overline{\rho}}^{m}(t,dy)},\quad{\overline{\rho}}^{m}(t,y)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{m}(t,dx,y,dv)\,.

Here the equation (
3.2
) of
\overline{Y}^{m}
is the time continuous approximation to the evolution of the local best position, and

S^{\beta}
with
\beta\gg 1
is the hyperbolic tangent
S^{\beta}(x,y)=1+\tanh(\beta(\mathcal{E}(y)-\mathcal{E}(x))
. The corresponding mean-field PSO equation is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{t}^{m}+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f_{t}^{m}

\displaystyle+\nabla_{y}\cdot(\nu(x-y)S^{\beta}(x,y)f_{t}^{m})=\nabla_{v}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\gamma}{m}vf_{t}^{m}+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{m}(x-y)f_{t}^{m}+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{m}(x-{Y}_{t}^{\alpha}({\overline{\rho}}^{m}))f_{t}^{m}

(3.5)

\displaystyle+\Big(\frac{\sigma_{2}^{2}}{2m^{2}}D(x-{Y}_{t}^{\alpha}({\overline{\rho}}^{m}))^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{1}^{2}}{2m^{2}}D(x-y)^{2}\Big)\nabla_{v}f_{t}^{m}\bigg)\,.

We want to prove that the zero-inertia limit
(m\to 0)
leads to the following mean-field CBO dynamic

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\overline{X}_{t}=\overline{X}_{0}+\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{t}(\overline{Y}_{s}-\overline{X}_{s})ds+\sigma_{1}\int_{0}^{t}D(\overline{Y}_{s}-\overline{X}_{s})dB_{s}^{1}+\lambda_{2}\int_{0}^{t}(Y_{s}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho})-\overline{X}_{s})ds+\sigma_{2}\int_{0}^{t}D(Y_{s}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho})-\overline{X}_{s})dB_{s}^{2},\\
\overline{Y}_{t}=\overline{Y}_{0}+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\left(\overline{X}_{s}-\overline{Y}_{s}\right)S^{\beta}\left(\overline{X}_{s},\overline{Y}_{s}\right)ds\,,\end{cases}

and its corresponding partial differential equation is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(\lambda_{1}(y-x)+\lambda_{2}({Y}_{t}^{\alpha}({\overline{\rho}})-x)\rho_{t}\right)+\nabla_{y}\cdot\left(\nu(x-y)S^{\beta}(x,y))\rho_{t}\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}\left(\rho_{t}(\sigma_{1}^{2}(x-y)_{j}^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}(x-{Y}_{t}^{\alpha}({\overline{\rho}}))_{j}^{2})\right)\,,

where
\overline{\rho}(t,y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\rho(t,dx,y)
.

Since the proof of the zero-inertia limit for the PSO dynamics with memory effects follows similar arguments as developed in the previous section and no essential innovation is needed to be explained, we only sketch the proof for the tightness.

Theorem 3.1

(Tightness)

Let Assumption
1

\displaystyle=\overline{X}_{0}+\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{t}(\overline{Y}_{s}-\overline{X}_{s})ds+\sigma_{1}\int_{0}^{t}D(\overline{Y}_{s}-\overline{X}_{s})dB_{s}^{1}+\lambda_{2}\int_{0}^{t}(Y_{s}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho})-\overline{X}_{s})ds+\sigma_{2}\int_{0}^{t}D(Y_{s}^{\alpha}(\overline{\rho})-\overline{X}_{s})dB_{s}^{2},

\displaystyle\overline{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\overline{Y}_{0}+\nu\int_{0}^{t}\left(\overline{X}_{s}-\overline{Y}_{s}\right)S^{\beta}\left(\overline{X}_{s},\overline{Y}_{s}\right)ds\,.

Moreover, we have the following convergences

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{t\in[0,T]}\left(\big|\overline{X}^{m}_{t}-\overline{X}_{t}\big|^{2}+\big|\overline{Y}^{m}_{t}-\overline{Y}_{t}\big|^{2}\right)\right]\leq C\,\sqrt{m},\quad\max_{t\in[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\big|\overline{X}^{m}_{t}-\overline{X}_{t}\big|^{2}+\big|\overline{Y}^{m}_{t}-\overline{Y}_{t}\big|^{2}\right]\leq C\,{m}.

where the constants

s depend on
\mathbb{E}[|\overline{X}_{0}|^{4}+|\overline{Y}_{0}|^{4}+|\overline{V}_{0}|^{4}],\lambda_{1},\sigma_{2},\lambda_{2},\sigma_{2},\beta,T,C_{\alpha,\mathcal{E}},d
, and
\nu
.
