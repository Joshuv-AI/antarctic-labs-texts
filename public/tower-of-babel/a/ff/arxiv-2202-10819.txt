Giry algebras for standard measurable spaces
Kirk Sturtz

Abstract
. The notion of “super convex spaces” generalizes the idea of convex spaces by replacing finite affine sums with countable affine sums. Using this notion permits a very elegant approach for analysis of the Giry monad on standard measurable spaces and identifying the 𝒢 \mathcal{G} -algebras for that monad. We use Isbell duality and restrict the adjunction 𝐒𝐩𝐞𝐜 ⊣ 𝒪 \mathbf{Spec}\dashv\mathcal{O} to a proper subcategory of super convex spaces and separated standard measurable spaces. Keywords. Giry algebras, Giry monad, standard measurable spaces, super convex spaces.

Lemma 3.2
.

A function

is a countably affine map if and only if

f(i)\leq f(j)
.

Proof.

Necessary condition
Suppose that
f:\mathbbmss{N}\rightarrow\mathbbmss{N}
is a countably affine map. Let
i<j
. By the super convex space structure on
\mathbb{N}
it follows, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, that
\alpha i+(1-\alpha)j=i
. If
f
is not monotone then there exist a pair of elements
i,j\in\mathds{N}
such that
i<j
with
f(j)<f(i)
. This implies, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
, that
f(j)=\alpha f(i)+(1-\alpha)f(j)<f(\alpha i+(1-\alpha)j)=f(i)
, which contradicts our hypothesis that
f
is a countably affine map.

Sufficient condition
Suppose
f
is a monotone function, and
that we are given an arbitrary countably affine sum
\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}p_{i}i=n
in
\mathbbmss{N}
, so that for all
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
we have
p_{i}=0
. Since the condition defining the super convex structure is conditioned on the property “for
p_{i}\neq 0
”, the countably affine sum is not changed by removing any number of terms
i
in the countable sum whose coefficient
p_{i}=0
. Hence
for all
j
such that
n<j
it follows that
f(n)\leq f(j)
so that

f(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}p_{i}\,i)=f(n)=\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}p_{i}f(i)

where the last equality follows from the definition of the super convex space structure on
\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

For every countably affine map
\phi:\mathbbmss{N}\rightarrow\mathbbmss{N}
the property
\phi\circ\epsilon_{\mathbb{N}}=\epsilon_{\mathbb{N}}\circ\mathcal{P}{(\mathbf{\Sigma}\phi)}

A direct verification gives

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\big(\phi\circ\epsilon_{\mathbb{N}}\big)(\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}p_{i}\delta_{i})&=&\phi\big(\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}p_{i}\underline{i}\big)\\

Suppose
\min_{i}\{i\,|\,p_{i}>0\}=k
which implies
p_{i}=0
for all

p_{k}>0

\big(\phi\circ\epsilon_{\mathbb{N}}\big)(\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}p_{i}\delta_{i})=\phi(k)

On the other hand we have

and

∎

We can classify a super convex space into one of three types.

(1)

A space that can be embedded into a vector space is of
geometric type
.

(2)

Let
\Delta_{\mathbb{N}}^{\dagger}=\{\mathbf{p}\in(0,1)^{\mathds{N}}\,|\,\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}p_{i}=1\}
. A space
A
, consisting of at least two points, is of
discrete type
if, for each fixed sequence
\mathbf{a}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow A
, the function

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\Delta_{\mathbb{N}}^{\dagger}&\longrightarrow&A\\
\mathbf{p}&\mapsto&\sum_{i\in\mathds{N}}p_{i}a_{i}\end{array}

is a constant function.

(3)

A space which is neither of discrete type or geometric type is of
mixed type
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
D
a discrete type space and
G
a geometric type space the set
\operatorname{\mathbf{SCvx}}(D,G)
consist of constant maps.

Proof.

Since
D
is a discrete space, it follows that for all
d_{1},d_{2}\in D
and all
r\in(0,1)
, that the property
rd_{1}+(1-r)d_{2}\in\{d_{1},d_{2}\}
holds, which implies that the condition
m(rd_{1}+(1-r)d_{2})=rm(d_{1})+(1-r)m(d_{2})

can only be satisfied if
m
is a constant function.
∎

Lemma
3.5
shows that no useful information can be obtained about a geometric space
G
by looking at maps from a discrete type space
D
into
G
. On the other hand, the maps
\operatorname{\mathbf{SCvx}}(G,D)
can provide quite useful information.

Example 3.6
.

The set
\mathbbmss{2}
with the super convex structure defined by

There is also a super convex space

5.
Standard measurable spaces

The two defining characteristics of a standard measurable space
X
are

(1)

Its
\sigma
-algebra
\Sigma_{X}
is asymptotically generated by a countably generated field
\mathcal{F}
, so that
\Sigma_{X}=\sigma(\mathcal{F})
.
Let
\{F_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be the sequence of finite fields which asymptotically generate the field
\mathcal{F}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}
.

Each such field
F_{n}
itself is generated by a partition of
X
, and we take
F_{n}
as the field generated by the partition
Atoms_{n}:X\rightarrow\mathbf{n}
which specifies the atoms of the finite field

9.
Remarks

We comment on two different aspects of this work: (1) Other research directly related to this article, and (2) The advantage of representing
\operatorname{\mathbf{Std}}^{\mathcal{G}}
as
\operatorname{\mathbf{SCvx}}_{\star}
.

(1)By using codensity monads, useful qualitative information about the Giry monad was obtained by Ruben Van Belle
[
1
]
and presented
in the article
Probability monads as codensity monads
. Belles’ research showed that we could limit our focus of attention on
countability
.
That article shows the Giry monad for
\mathbf{Meas}
can be viewed as arising from the codensity monad of a functor
\mathbf{G}:\mathbf{Set}_{c}\rightarrow\mathbf{Meas}
, where
\mathbf{Set}_{c}
is the category of countable sets. It is equivalent to say that
\mathbf{G}
is the Giry monad restricted to countable measurable spaces with the powerset
\sigma
-algebra. He employs the countable-dimensional simplexes in defining the functor, and it is clear that the finite-dimensional simplexes can be viewed as subspaces of
\Delta_{\mathds{N}}
. Consequently he could have chosen his functor
\mathbf{G}:\mathbf{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbf{Meas}
where
\mathbf{\Omega}
is the category with the one object
\Delta_{\mathds{N}}
, and with arrows as he defined in the article,
\Delta_{f}:\Delta_{\mathds{N}}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathds{N}}
, which is the pushforward map induced by a function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
. While the pushforward maps are countably affine maps, most countably affine maps
\Delta_{\mathds{N}}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathds{N}}
are not pushforward maps.

A comparison between the
\mathcal{G}
-algebras on
\mathbf{Std}
and the
\mathcal{G}
-algebras on complete metric spaces,
\mathbf{CMet}
with Lipshitz-1 functions (short maps), using the Kantorovich monad illustrate an important distinction.
The Kantorovich monad
[
7
]
on
\mathbf{CMet}
has algebras that are equivalent to barycenter maps
\mathcal{G}(C)\rightarrow C
sending
P\mapsto\int_{C}id_{C}\,dP
, where
C
is a closed convex subset of a Banach space. Hence the codomain of these algebras are geometric spaces, i.e., embeddable into a vector space. This issue is the same shortcoming that Doberkat
[
2
,
3
]
recognized in analyzing the algebras for Polish spaces with continuous maps. Discrete spaces (with at least two points) have no algebras because the maps are required to be continuous. Short maps in
\mathbf{CMet}
force the same continuity requirement, and hence discrete spaces have no algebras.

(2) The advantage of working with
\operatorname{\mathbf{SCvx}}_{\star}
rather than
\operatorname{\mathbf{Std}}^{\mathcal{G}}
resides in the fact that we can answer questions about existence of algebras, and obtaining knowledge about properties of
\operatorname{\mathbf{SCvx}}_{\star}
is easier that trying to figure out such properties using
\operatorname{\mathbf{Std}}^{\mathcal{G}}
.
Conceptually, an advantage of using super convex spaces is that we can, with a minor adjustment in the definition of a super convex space, employ “probability amplitudes”. To do this it is only necessary to define

\mathcal{G}{\mathbb{N}}=\{\textrm{all sequences }\mathbf{p}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbf{D}_{2}\,\textrm{ such that }\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}p_{i}p_{i}^{\star}\}=1\}

where
\mathbf{D}_{2}=\{re^{\imath\theta}\in\mathbb{C}\,|r\in[0,1],\textrm{ and }\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}
and
p_{i}^{\star}
is the complex conjugate of
p_{i}
.
The three axioms of a super convex space are unchanged, and countably affine sums are defined accordingly using probability amplitudes. Thus the super convex space structure on
\mathcal{P}{X}
is given by “
(\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i}P_{i})(U)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i}p_{i}^{\star}P_{i}(U)
for all measurable sets
U
in
X
”, in other words, whenever we evaluate a probability measure we use the
\ell_{2}
-norm in evaluating the countable affine sum,
which is precisely what is done in quantum mechanics.
That same principal can be applied to any countably affine sum. For example, the super convex space structure of
\mathbb{N}
reads as “
\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}}p_{i}\,i=\min_{i}\{i\,|\,p_{i}p_{i}^{\star}>0\}
”. Nowhere in any of our theorems or lemmas do we use any special properties arising from the unit interval. We only use the property of “countably affine sums” which can be defined using either the
\ell_{2}
