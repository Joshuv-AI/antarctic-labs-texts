Long-time dynamics of the Cahn–Hilliard equation with kinetic rate dependent dynamic boundary conditions
Harald Garcke, Patrik Knopf, Sema Yayla

Abstract
We consider a Cahn–Hilliard model with kinetic rate dependent dynamic boundary conditions that was introduced by Knopf, Lam, Liu and Metzger (ESAIM Math. Model. Numer. Anal., 2021) and will thus be called the KLLM model. In the aforementioned paper, it was shown that solutions of the KLLM model converge to solutions of the GMS model proposed by Goldstein, Miranville and Schimperna (Physica D, 2011) as the kinetic rate tends to infinity. We first collect the weak well-posedness results for both models and we establish some further essential properties of the weak solutions. Afterwards, we investigate the long-time behavior of the KLLM model. We first prove the existence of a global attractor as well as convergence to a single stationary point. Then, we show that the global attractor of the GMS model is stable with respect to perturbations of the kinetic rate. Eventually, we construct exponential attractors for both models, and we show that the exponential attractor associated with the GMS model is robust against kinetic rate perturbations.

Introduction

The Cahn–Hilliard equation was originally introduced in
[
7
]
to describe spinodal decomposition in binary alloys.
Meanwhile, it has become one of the most popular models to describe various kinds of phase separation phenomena arising, for instance, in materials science, life sciences and image processing. The standard Cahn–Hilliard equation as proposed in
[
7
]
reads as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=m_{\Omega}\Delta\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }Q:=\Omega\times(0,\infty),

(1.1a)

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta u+\varepsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }Q,

(1.1b)

\displaystyle u|_{t=0}=u_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Omega.

(1.1c)

Here,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
stands for a bounded domain (usually
d\in\{2,3\}
) with boundary
\Gamma
,
\Delta
stands for the Laplace operator acting on
\Omega
, and
m_{\Omega}
denotes a mobility parameter. For simplicity, it is assumed to be a non-negative constant, although non-constant mobilities find a use in some situations (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
).

In order to describe a binary mixture, the phase-field variable
u
represents the difference in volume fractions of both materials.
After a short period of time, the solution
u
will attain values
close to
\pm 1
in most parts of the domain
\Omega
. These regions, which correspond to the pure phases of the materials, are
separated by a diffuse interface whose thickness is proportional to the parameter
\varepsilon>0
appearing in (
1.1b
). In most applications this interface will be very thin and thus, the parameter
\varepsilon
is usually chosen to be very small.
The time evolution of the mixture described by
u
is driven by the chemical potential
\mu
in the bulk (i.e., in
\Omega
).
It is given as the derivative of the following Ginzburg–Landau type free energy:

\displaystyle E_{\text{bulk}}(u)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(u)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1.2)

The bulk potential
F
is usually double-well shaped. Typically, it attains its minimum at
-1
and
1
and has a local maximum in between at
0
. From a mathematical point of view,
F
is responsible for the phase separation since it is energetically favorable for
u
to attain values close to
\pm 1
.
A physically relevant choice is the
logarithmic potential

\displaystyle F_{\text{log}}(s)=\frac{\vartheta}{2}\big((1+s)\ln(1+s)+(1-s)\ln(1-s)\big)-\frac{\vartheta_{c}}{2}s^{2},\hskip 10.00002pts\in(-1,1)

(1.3)

with constants
0<\vartheta<\vartheta_{c}
.
It is often approximated by the
smooth double-well potential

\displaystyle F_{\text{sdw}}(s)=\frac{1}{4}(s^{2}-1)^{2},\hskip 10.00002pts\in\mathbb{R}

(1.4)

which is easier to handle in terms of mathematical analysis. In this paper, we will deal with general smooth potentials satisfying certain polynomial growth conditions such that (
1.4
) fits into our setting. However, singular potentials like (
1.3
) cannot be taken into account in our approach.

To ensure well-posedness of the system (
1.1
), certain boundary conditions on
u
and
\mu
need to be imposed. The classical choices are the homogeneous Neumann conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\;\Sigma:=\Gamma\times(0,\infty),

(1.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on}\;\;\Sigma.

(1.6)

The
no-flux condition

(
1.6
)
implies mass conservation in the bulk, meaning that (sufficiently regular) solutions satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(0)\,\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[0,\infty).

(1.7)

Moreover, due to (
1.5
) and (
1.6
), we obtain the following energy dissipation law:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{\text{bulk}}\big(u(t)\big)+m_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mu(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x=0,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[0,\infty).

(1.8)

Furthermore,
(
1.5
)
can be regarded as a
contact angle condition
as it enforces the diffuse interface separating the regions of pure materials to intersect the boundary at a perfect right angle.
The Cahn–Hilliard equation (
1.1
) with the homogeneous Neumann conditions (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) is already very well understood and has been studied from many different viewpoints (see, e.g.,

[
1
,
6
,
11
,
22
,
23
]
). In particular, we refer to
[
61
,
56
,
49
,
34
,
21
]
for the investigation of long-time behavior.

However, in many situations the contact angle condition (
1.5
) turned out to be very restrictive as in many applications the contact angle of the interface will not only deviate from ninety degrees but also change dynamically over the course of time.
In certain situations (e.g., in hydrodynamic applications), it also turned out to be essential to model short-range interactions between the mixture of materials and the solid wall of the container more precisely. To this end physicists (see
[
26
,
27
,
40
]
) proposed that the total free energy should contain an additional contribution on the surface being also of Ginzburg–Landau type:

E_{\text{surf}}(u)=\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\kappa\delta}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}u\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}G(u)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

(1.9)

Here,
\nabla_{\Gamma}
denotes the surface gradient operator, the constant
\kappa\geq 0
acts as a weight for surface diffusion effects and the parameter
\delta>0
is related to the thickness of the diffuse interface on the boundary.
Moreover, the function
G
is an additional surface potential. If phase separation processes are expected to also occur on the boundary it makes sense to assume that
G
exhibits a double-well structure similar to
F
.
In this paper, we will thus impose similar conditions on
G
as on
F
such that the choice
G=F_{\mathrm{sdw}}
is admissible.
The total free energy
E=E_{\text{bulk}}+E_{\text{surf}}
then reads as

E(u)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}F(u)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\kappa\delta}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}u\right|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta}G(u)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

(1.10)

Associated with this total free energy, various Cahn-Hilliard type systems with dynamic boundary conditions have been proposed and investigated in the literature (see,e.g.,

[
16
,
18
,
31
,
32
,
19
,
45
,
48
,
51
,
52
,
55
,
60
,
28
,
59
,
30
,
8
,
9
,
17
,
50
]
).
In recent times, dynamic boundary conditions which also exhibit a Cahn–Hilliard type structure have become very popular.
Therefore, we want to highlight the Cahn–Hilliard equation subject to a class of dynamic boundary conditions of Cahn–Hilliard type depending on a parameter
L\in[0,\infty]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=m_{\Omega}\Delta\mu,

\displaystyle\mu=-\varepsilon\Delta u+\varepsilon^{-1}F^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }Q,

(1.11a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=m_{\Gamma}\Delta_{\Gamma}\theta-\beta m_{\Omega}\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu,\hskip 10.00002pt

\displaystyle\theta=-\delta\kappa\Delta_{\Gamma}v+\delta^{-1}G^{\prime}(v)+\varepsilon\partial_{\bm{n}}u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma,

(1.11b)

\displaystyle u|_{\Sigma}=v

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma,

(1.11c)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mu|_{\Sigma}&=\beta\theta\\
L\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu|_{\Sigma}&=\beta\theta-\mu|_{\Sigma}\\
\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu|_{\Sigma}&=0\\
\end{aligned}\right.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\text{if}\;L=0,\\
&\text{if}\;L\in(0,\infty),\\
&\text{if}\;L=\infty,\\
\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma,

(1.11d)

\displaystyle(u,v)|_{t=0}=(u_{0},v_{0})

\displaystyle\text{with}\;u_{0}|_{\Sigma}=v_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Omega\times\Gamma.

(1.11e)

The chemical potentials
\mu
and
\theta
which are coupled by the boundary condition
(
1.11d
)
describe the chemical interaction between the materials in the bulk and the materials on the surface. The constant
1/L
is related to a
kinetic rate
, and the term
L\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu
describes adsorption and desorption processes. Here, the mass flux
-m_{\Omega}\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu
(which describes the motion of the materials towards and away from the boundary) is directly influenced by differences in the chemical potentials through the condition (
1.11d
). As the models corresponding to the cases
L=0
,
L=\infty
and
0<L<\infty
were introduced separately in different articles in the literature, we are now going to briefly highlight their most important features.

The case
L=0
(GMS model).

The system (
1.11
) was first introduced with
L=0
in (
1.11d
) by G. Goldstein, A. Miranville and G. Schimperna
[
36
]
. In view of the authors’ initials we will refer to this system as the
GMS model
.

It can be regarded as an extension of a model that was previously introduced by Gal
[
31
]
where the equation
\partial_{t}v=-\beta\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu+\gamma\mu
on
\Sigma
(for some additional constant
\gamma
) was proposed instead of
(
1.11b
)
.
Here, the chemical potentials in the bulk and on the boundary can differ only by the factor
\beta
, i.e., they are directly proportional.
In other words, this means that the chemical potentials
\mu
and
\theta
are always in chemical equilibrium.
It is worth mentioning that in
[
36
]
,
\beta
is even allowed to be a uniformly positive function in
L^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. However, in this paper we restrict ourselves to the case where
\beta
is a positive constant.

We observe that any (sufficiently regular) solution to the GMS system satisfies the mass conservation law

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\Omega}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}v(t)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\beta\int_{\Omega}u(0)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}v(0)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[0,\infty),

(1.12)

meaning that the parameter
\beta
can be interpreted as a weight for the bulk mass compared to the surface mass. Moreover, the energy dissipation law

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\big(u(t),v(t)\big)+m_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mu(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+m_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=0

(1.13)

is satisfied for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. In particular, we observe that the dissipation rate is strongly influenced by the values of the mobilities
m_{\Omega}
and
m_{\Gamma}
.

The weak well-posedness and some results on long-time behavior were established in
[
36
]
. We will present the well-posedness result as well as some additional important properties of weak solutions in Section
3.2
. A summary of the results on long-time behavior is given in Section
4.2
.

We further point out that numerical analysis for the GMS model as well as some numerical simulations can be found in
[
37
]
. A nonlocal variant of the GMS model (including a nonlocal dynamic boundary condition) was proposed and analyzed in
[
44
]
.

The case
L=\infty
(LW model).

Some time after the introduction of the GMS model, the system (
1.11
) with
L=\infty
in (
1.11d
) was derived by C. Liu and H. Wu
[
46
]
via an energetic variational approach. We will thus call this system the
LW model
.

The crucial difference to the GMS model is that the Dirichlet type boundary condition
\beta\theta=\mu
is replaced by the no mass flux condition
\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu=0
. This means that the chemical potentials
\mu
and
\theta
are not directly coupled. However, mechanical interactions between the bulk and the surface materials are still taken into account through the trace condition (
1.11c
) for the phase-field variable. Mathematically speaking, the elliptic subproblems
\big(\eqref{CH:INT:1}_{1},\eqref{CH:INT:3}\big)
and
\eqref{CH:INT:2}_{1}
are coupled only through the trace relation (
1.11c
).

Compared to (
1.12
), we obtain the very different mass conservation law

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u(t)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}u(0)\,\mathrm{d}x\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\int_{\Gamma}v(t)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\int_{\Gamma}v(0)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\hskip 10.00002ptt\in[0,\infty),

(1.14)

meaning that the bulk mass and the surface mass are conserved
separately
. However, the energy dissipation law (
1.13
) is still satisfied by solutions of this system.

The well-posedness of the LW model was addressed in
[
46
,
33
]
, and its long-time behavior was investigated in
[
46
,
50
]
.
For numerical analysis and some simulations we refer to
[
47
,
4
]
.

Moreover, a variant of the LW model (
1.11
) was proposed and investigated in
[
41
]
where the relation between
u
and
v
is given by the Robin type transmission condition

\displaystyle K\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u=H(v)-u\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ on }\Sigma

with
K>0
and a function
H\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
satisfying suitable growth conditions. In particular, it was rigorously established in
[
41
]
that in the case
H(s)=s
, solutions of this model converge to solutions of the LW model in the limit
K\to 0
in some suitable sense.

The case
0<L<\infty
(KLLM model).

For
0<L<\infty
, the system (
1.11
) was recently proposed and analyzed by the second author in collaboration with K.F. Lam, C. Liu and S. Metzger
[
42
]
. It will thus be referred to as the
KLLM model
.

The Robin type condition (
1.11c
) establishes a connection between the GMS model ((
1.11
) with
L=0
) and the LW model ((
1.11
) with
L=\infty
) despite their very different chemical and physical properties. Suppose that
\beta>0
and that
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
is a solution of the system (
1.11
) corresponding to the parameter
L>0
.
Let
(u^{0},v^{0},\mu^{0},\theta^{0})
denote its formal limit as
L\to 0
and let
(u^{\infty},v^{\infty},\mu^{\infty},\theta^{\infty})
denote its formal limit as
L\to\infty
.
Passing to the limit in the Robin boundary condition, we deduce that

\displaystyle\beta\theta^{0}=\mu^{0}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\partial_{\mathbf{n}}\mu^{\infty}=0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma.

This corresponds to the limit cases of instantaneous relaxation to chemical equilibrium (
1/L\to\infty
), and the absence of adsorption and desorption (
1/L\to 0
).
We infer that
(u^{0},v^{0},\mu^{0},\theta^{0})
is a solution to the GMS model while
(u^{\infty},v^{\infty},\mu^{\infty},\theta^{\infty})
is a solution to the LW model. These formal considerations are rigorously verified in
[
42
]
. In this regard, the Cahn–Hilliard system (
1.11
) with
L\in(0,\infty)
can be interpreted as an interpolation between the GMS model and the LW model where the interpolation parameter
L
corresponds to positive but finite kinetic rates.

We observe that solutions of the KLLM model satisfy the same mass conservation law (
1.12
) as solutions of the GMS model. However, we obtain an additional term in the dissipation rate depending on the relaxation parameter
L
. To be precise, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E\big(u(t)\big)+m_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\mu(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+m_{\Gamma}\int_{\Gamma}|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta(t)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma+\frac{m_{\Omega}}{L}\int_{\Gamma}\big(\beta\theta(t)-\mu(t)\big)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=0

(1.15)

for all
t\in[0,\infty)
. In particular, this implies that the total free energy
E
is decreasing along solutions, and as
E
is bounded from below (at least for reasonable choices of
F
and
G
), we infer that
\frac{d}{dt}E(u(t))
tends to zero as
t\to\infty
. As a consequence, the chemical potentials will converge to the chemical equilibrium
\mu=\beta\theta
as
t\to\infty
.

We further point out that weak and strong well-posedness of the KLLM model was established in
[
42
]
. As already mentioned above, the asymptotic limits
L\to 0
and
L\to\infty
were also discussed in
[
42
]
. We will exploit some of these results for the limit
L\to 0
in Section 5, Lemma
5.2
.

For simulations and numerical analysis concerning the KLLM model, we refer to
[
42
,
5
]
.
A nonlocal variant of the KLLM model (including a nonlocal dynamic boundary condition) was proposed and investigated in
[
44
]
.

Contents of this paper.

This paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
, we first introduce some notation and assumptions which will be used throughout the paper. We further define some suitable spaces and operators and we introduce several important interpolation inequalities. In Section
3
, we present the well-posedness results for both the KLLM model and the GMS model for the reader’s convenience. Furthermore, we establish some properties of the weak solutions to both models which are essential to investigate their long-time behavior. Subsection
4.1
is devoted to the long-time analysis of the KLLM model. We first characterize the set of stationary points containing the time-independent solutions to the KLLM model. Next, we establish the existence of a (unique) global attractor and we prove convergence of weak solutions to a single stationary point as
t\to\infty
by means of a Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality. Eventually, we show that the global attractor can be represented as the union of the unstable manifolds of all stationary points. In Subsection
4.2
, supported by the results on long-time behavior that have already been established in
[
36
]
, we sketch how all results we proved in Subsection
4.1
for the KLLM model can be obtained also for the GMS model in a similar fashion. Next, in Section
5
, we show that the global attractor of the GMS model (i.e.,
L=0
) is stable with respect to perturbations of the kinetic rate (i.e.,
L>0
being small). Ultimately, in Section
6
, we show that there exists a family
\{\mathfrak{M}^{L}\}_{L\geq 0}
of exponential attractors for the system (
1.11
) such that the attractor
\mathfrak{M}^{0}
associated with the GMS model is robust in some certain sense against perturbations of the kinetic rate (i.e.,
L>0
being small).

Comparison of the GMS/KLLM dynamics with the LW dynamics.

We point out that we do not see any possibility of transferring the results of Section
5
and Section
6
to the scenario
L\to\infty
. This is mainly due to the different mass conservation law of the LW model. Roughly speaking, the mass conservation law (
1.12
) of the GMS/KLLM model fixes only one degree of freedom, whereas the mass conservation law (
1.14
) of the LW model already fixes two degrees of freedom. As a consequence, the GMS/KLLM model and the LW model exhibit a different behavior regarding energy dissipation. Namely, as only one degree of freedom is fixed by the mass conservation law, the free energy can usually be decreased much further by the GMS/KLLM model than by the LW model. This phenomenon can also be observed in numerical simulations, see, e.g.,
[
42
, Fig. 4]
or
[
5
, Fig. 16]
. This already indicates that the GMS/KLLM model and the LW model differ in their long-time behavior.
For more details why our analysis in Section
5
and Section
6
fails in the situation
L\to\infty
, we refer to Remark
5.6
.

Preliminaries and important tools

We will now fix some notation and assumptions that are supposed to hold throughout this paper.

Notation.

(\mathrm{N1})

Sometimes, we will use the notation
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}:=[0,\infty)
. The symbol
\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the set of natural numbers including zero and
\mathbb{N}=\mathbb{N}_{0}\setminus\{0\}
.

(\mathrm{N2})

For any real numbers
k\geq 0
and
1\leq p\leq\infty
, the standard Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces over a domain
\Omega
are denoted as
L^{p}(\Omega)
and
W^{k,p}(\Omega)
. We write
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}
and
\|\cdot\|_{W^{k,p}(\Omega)}
to denote the standard norms on these spaces. If
p=2
, these spaces are Hilbert spaces and we use the notation
H^{k}(\Omega)=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
. We point out that
H^{0}(\Omega)
can be identified with
L^{2}(\Omega)
. An analogous notation is used for Lebesgue and Sobolev spaces on
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
, provided that the boundary is sufficiently regular.

(\mathrm{N3})

For any Banach space
X
, we write
X^{\prime}
to denote its dual space. The associated duality pairing of elements
\phi\in X^{\prime}
and
\zeta\in X
is denoted as
\langle\phi,\zeta\rangle_{X}
. If
X
is a Hilbert space, we write
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
to denote its inner product.

(\mathrm{N4})

We define

\displaystyle\langle u\rangle_{\Omega}:=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\langle u,1\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)}&\text{ if }u\in H^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime},\\
\frac{1}{\left|\Omega\right|}\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}x&\text{ if }u\in L^{1}(\Omega)\end{cases}

to denote the (generalized) spatial mean of
u
. Here,
\left|\Omega\right|
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure of
\Omega
. The spatial mean of a function
v\in H^{1}(\Gamma)^{\prime}
(or
v\in L^{1}(\Gamma)
, respectively) is defined analogously.

(\mathrm{N5})

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. Then for any sets
A,B\subset X
, the
Hausdorff semidistance
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{X}(A,B):=\underset{a\in A}{\sup}\;\underset{b\in B}{\inf}\;d\left(a,b\right),

(2.1)

and the
symmetric Hausdorff distance
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{sym},X}(A,B):=\max\big(\mathrm{dist}_{X}(A,B)\,{,}\,\mathrm{dist}_{X}(B,A)\big).

(2.2)

For any compact set
K\subset X
, the
fractal dimension of
K
is defined as

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{frac},X}(K):=\underset{r\to 0}{\lim\sup}\frac{\ln\big(\mathcal{N}_{r}(K;X)\big)}{-\ln(r)},

(2.3)

where
\mathcal{N}_{r}(K;X)
denotes the minimal number of balls in
X
with radius
r
that are necessary to cover the set
K
.

Assumptions.

We make the following general assumptions.

(\mathrm{A1})

We assume that
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d\in\{2,3\}
is a bounded domain whose boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
is of class
C^{3}
. We further use the notation

\displaystyle Q:=\Omega\times(0,\infty),\hskip 10.00002pt{\Sigma}:=\Gamma\times(0,\infty).

(\mathrm{A2})

In general, we assume that the constants occurring in the system (
1.11
) satisfy
T
,
\beta
,
\kappa
,
m_{\Omega}
,
m_{\Gamma}>0
and
L\in[0,\infty)
.
Since the choice of
\delta
,
\varepsilon
,
\kappa
,
m_{\Omega}
and
m_{\Gamma}
has no impact on the mathematical analysis, we will simply set
\delta=\varepsilon=\kappa=m_{\Omega}=m_{\Gamma}=1
for convenience in the mathematical analysis.

(\mathrm{A3})

We assume that the potentials
F
and
G
are non-negative functions which can be written as
F=F_{1}+F_{2}
and
G=G_{1}+G_{2}
with
F_{1},F_{2},G_{1},G_{2}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that the following properties hold:

(\mathrm{A3.1})

There exist exponents
2<p\leq 4
and

,
as well as constants

\displaystyle a_{F}\left|s\right|^{p}-b_{F}

(2.4a)

(2.4b)

\displaystyle a_{F^{\prime}}\left|s\right|^{p-1}-b_{F^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{F^{\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{p-1}),

(2.4c)

\displaystyle a_{G^{\prime}}\left|s\right|^{q-1}-b_{G^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq c_{G^{\prime}}(1+\left|s\right|^{q-1}).

(2.4d)

(\mathrm{A3.2})

The functions

and

are convex and non-negative. There further exist positive constants

and

such that the second order derivatives satisfy the growth conditions

\displaystyle 0\leq F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq c_{F^{\prime\prime}}(1+|s|^{p-2}),\hskip 10.00002pt0\leq G_{1}^{\prime\prime}(s)\leq c_{G^{\prime\prime}}(1+|s|^{q-2})

(2.5)

(\mathrm{A3.3})

(2.6)

\displaystyle\left|F_{2}(s)\right|\leq d_{F}(1+\left|s\right|^{2}),\hskip 10.00002pt

Preliminaries.

We next introduce several function spaces, products, norms and operators that will be used throughout this paper.

(\mathrm{P1})

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and any real number
p\in[1,\infty]
, we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{p}:=L^{p}(\Omega)\times L^{p}(\Gamma),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\mathcal{H}^{k}:=H^{k}(\Omega)\times H^{k}(\Gamma),

and we identify
\mathcal{L}^{2}
with
\mathcal{H}^{0}
. Note that
\mathcal{H}^{k}
is a Hilbert space with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\big((\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\big(\phi,\zeta\big)_{H^{k}(\Omega)}+\big(\psi,\xi\big)_{H^{k}(\Gamma)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{k}$,}

and its induced norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}^{1/2}
.

(\mathrm{P2})

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the Hilbert space

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}^{k}}:=\big\{(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{k}\;|\;\phi|_{\Gamma}=\psi\;\text{a.e.~on}\;\Gamma\big\}

endowed with the inner product
\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{{\mathcal{V}^{k}}}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}
and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{V}^{k}}}:=\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}
.

(\mathrm{P3})

For any

m\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{k}}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(\phi,\psi)\in{\mathcal{V}^{k}}\;|\;\beta\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\psi\rangle_{\Gamma}=m\big\}.

Endowed with the inner product

\displaystyle\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{k}}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{k}}

and the induced norm, the space
\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{k}
is a Hilbert space.

(\mathrm{P4})

For
\beta>0
, we introduce the subspace

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}:=\left\{(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}\;\big|\;\phi|_{\Gamma}=\beta\psi\;\;\text{a.e. on}\;\Gamma\right\}\subset\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Endowed with the inner product

\displaystyle\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

and its induced norm,
the space
{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}
is a Hilbert space. Moreover, we define the product

\displaystyle\big<(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big>_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}:=\left(\phi,\zeta\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\psi,\xi\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

for all
(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{L}^{2}
. By means of the Riesz representation theorem, this product can be extended to a duality pairing on
({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}})^{\prime}\times{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}
, which will also be denoted as
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}
.

In particular, the spaces
\big({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}},\mathcal{L}^{2},({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}})^{\prime}\big)
form a Gelfand triplet, and the operator norm on
({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}})^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle\|(\phi,\psi)\|_{({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}})^{\prime}}:=\sup\Big\{\,\big|\langle(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\rangle_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}\big|\;\Big|\;(\zeta,\xi)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}\text{ with }\|(\zeta,\xi)\|_{{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}}=1\Big\},

for all
(\phi,\psi)\in({\mathcal{D}_{\beta}})^{\prime}
. Note that the mapping

\displaystyle\mathfrak{I}:{\mathcal{V}}^{1}\to{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}},\hskip 10.00002pt(\phi,\psi)\mapsto(\beta\phi,\psi)

is an isomorphism.

(\mathrm{P5})

Let
L\geq 0
,
\beta>0
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary. We define the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\left\{(\phi,\psi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}\;|\;\beta\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\psi\rangle_{\Gamma}=0\right\},&\text{if}\;L>0,\\[8.61108pt]
\left\{(\phi,\psi)\in{\mathcal{D}_{\beta}}\;|\;\beta\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\psi\rangle_{\Gamma}=0\right\},&\text{if}\;L=0,\end{cases}

along with the inner product

\displaystyle\big((\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{L,\beta}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\psi\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\sigma(L)\int_{\Gamma}(\beta\psi-\phi)(\beta\xi-\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,

for all
(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
, where

\displaystyle\sigma(L)=\begin{cases}L^{-1}&\text{if}\;L>0,\\
0&\text{if}\;L=0.\end{cases}

(2.8)

This is indeed an inner product since
\left((\phi,\psi),(\phi,\psi)\right)_{L,\beta}=0
already entails that
\phi
and
\psi
are constant almost everywhere and satisfy
\phi|_{\Gamma}=\beta\psi
almost everywhere on
\Gamma
. Then, the mean value constraint of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{L,\beta}
directly yields
(\phi,\psi)=(0,0)
almost everywhere.
The induced norm is given as
\|\,\cdot\,\|_{L,\beta}:=\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{L,\beta}^{1/2}
.

(\mathrm{P6})

Let
L,\beta>0
be arbitrary. We define the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\beta}^{-1}

\displaystyle:=\big\{(\phi,\psi)\in(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}\;|\;\beta\left|\Omega\right|\langle\phi\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle\psi\rangle_{\Gamma}=0\big\}\subset(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}.

We conclude from

43
, Thm. 3.3]
that for any

to the elliptic problem

\displaystyle-\Delta\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\phi

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(2.9a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Gamma}+\beta\partial_{\bm{n}}\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\psi

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma,

(2.9b)

\displaystyle L\partial_{\bm{n}}\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=(\beta\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Gamma}-\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Omega})

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma.

(2.9c)

This means that

satisfies the weak formulation

\displaystyle\big(\mathfrak{S}^{L}(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big)_{L,\beta}=-\big<(\phi,\psi),(\zeta,\xi)\big>_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

for all test functions

As in

.

(\mathrm{P7})

Let
\beta>0
be arbitrary. We define the space

Well-posedness and further essential properties

In this section, we present the weak well-posedness results for the problem (

By this choice, the system (

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u=\Delta\mu,

\displaystyle\mu=-\Delta u+F^{\prime}(u)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }Q,

(3.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v=\Delta_{\Gamma}\theta-\beta\partial_{\bm{n}}\mu,

\displaystyle\theta=-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+G^{\prime}(v)+\partial_{\bm{n}}u

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma,

(3.1b)

\displaystyle u|_{\Gamma}=v

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma,

(3.1c)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \mu|_{\Sigma}&=\beta\theta\\

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\begin{aligned} &\text{if}\;L=0,\\
&\text{if}\;L\in(0,\infty),\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{on }\Sigma,

\displaystyle(u,v)|_{t=0}=(u_{0},v_{0})

\displaystyle\text{with}\;u_{0}|_{\Sigma}=v_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Omega\times\Gamma.

(3.1e)

The total free energy associated with this system is given as

\displaystyle E(u,v)=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla u\right|^{2}+F(u)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\frac{1}{2}\left|\nabla_{\Gamma}v\right|^{2}+G(v)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

3.1

(Weak well-posedness and smoothing property for the KLLM model)

Suppose that (

)–(

. Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary and let

(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
be any initial datum.

Then there exists a unique global weak solution
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
of the system (
3.1
) existing on the whole time interval
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
and having the following properties:

(\mathrm{i})

For every
T>0
, the solution has the regularity

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \big(u^{L},v^{L}\big)&\in C^{0,\frac{1}{4}}([0,T];\mathcal{L}^{2})\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;{\mathcal{V}}^{1})\\
&\qquad\cap L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{3})\cap H^{1}\big(0,T;(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}\big),\end{aligned}

(3.3a)

\displaystyle\big(\mu^{L},\theta^{L}\big)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{1}).

(3.3b)

(\mathrm{ii})

The solution satisfies

\displaystyle\big<\partial_{t}u^{L},w\big>_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mu^{L}\cdot\nabla w\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\tfrac{1}{L}(\beta\theta^{L}-\mu^{L})w\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,

(3.4a)

\displaystyle\big<\partial_{t}v^{L},z\big>_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta^{L}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}z\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma-\int_{\Gamma}\tfrac{1}{L}(\beta\theta^{L}-\mu^{L})\beta z\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,

(3.4b)

a.e. on
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
for all test functions
(w,z)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
, and

\displaystyle\mu^{L}=-\Delta u^{L}+F^{\prime}(u^{L})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in Q},

(3.4c)

\displaystyle\theta^{L}=-\Delta_{\Gamma}v^{L}+G^{\prime}(v^{L})+\partial_{\mathbf{n}}u^{L},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in $\Sigma$},

(3.4d)

\displaystyle u^{L}|_{\Sigma}=v^{L},

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in $\Sigma$},

(3.4e)

\displaystyle(u^{L},v^{L})|_{t=0}=(u_{0},v_{0})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in }\Omega\times\Gamma.

(3.4f)

(\mathrm{iii})

The solution satisfies the global mass conservation law

\displaystyle\beta\int_{\Omega}u^{L}(t)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}v^{L}(t)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\beta\int_{\Omega}u_{0}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}v_{0}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=m\hskip 10.00002pt

(3.5)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
, i.e.,
\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.

(\mathrm{iv})

The solution satisfies the energy inequality

\displaystyle E\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\big\|\big(\mu^{L}(s),\theta^{L}(s)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\leq E(u_{0},v_{0})

(3.6)

for almost all
t\geq 0
.

(\mathrm{v})

The solution satisfies the smoothing property, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{*}>0
depending only on
\Omega
,
\beta
,
F
,
G
and
\|(u_{0},v_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
, such that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3}}\leq C_{*}\left(\dfrac{1+t}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(3.7)

for almost all
t\geq 0
.

Remark 3.2
.

We point out that the above results of Theorem
3.1
hold true, if the bounds from below in the conditions (
2.4a
) and (
2.4b
) are omitted. This means, it would be sufficient to demand that

\displaystyle F(s)\leq c_{F}(1+\left|s\right|^{p})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptG(s)\leq c_{G}(1+\left|s\right|^{q})

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
, even though the stronger assumptions were used in
[
42
]
to which we will refer in the subsequent proof. This is because instead of
[
42
, Lem. 2.1]
, the stronger Poincaré type inequality

\displaystyle\|(u,v)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq C_{P}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}$}

(3.8)

could be used to prove the existence of a weak solution. The proof of (
3.8
) can be done similarly to the proof of
[
42
, Lem. 2.1]
via a contradiction argument. It relies on the fact that if
\nabla u=0
a.e. in
\Omega
, then the functions
u
and
v=u|_{\Gamma}
must be constant a.e. in
\Omega
and a.e. on
\Gamma
, respectively. Then, the mean value condition
\beta\left|\Omega\right|\langle u\rangle_{\Omega}+\left|\Gamma\right|\langle v\rangle_{\Gamma}=0
ensures that
u=0
a.e. in
\Omega
and
v=0
a.e. on
\Gamma
.

Nevertheless, in Assumption

A3

, we stick to the stronger conditions as demanded in
[
42
]
since such kind of estimates are very useful in the
proof of Lemma
4.4
(see (
4.13
) and (
4.14
)).

We further point out that the above comments also apply for the GMS model (
L=0
) whose well-posedness will be discussed in Subsection
3.2
.

The existence and uniqueness of a global unique weak solution which satisfies the assertions (i)–(iv) has already been established in
[
42
, Thm. 3.1]
.

Hence, only the smoothing property (v) remains to be proved.
In the following, we omit the superscript
L
to provide a cleaner presentation.
Let us fix arbitrary test functions
(w,z)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
be arbitrary.
Adding (
3.4a
) and (
3.4b
), we obtain

\displaystyle\big\langle\partial_{t}u,w\big\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\big\langle\partial_{t}v,z\big\rangle_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}=-\big((\mu,\theta),(w,z)\big)_{L,\beta}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~on $\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$}

(3.9)

for all test functions
(w,z)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
.

In the following, let
C
denote a generic positive constant depending only on
\Omega
,
\beta
,
F
,
G
and
\|(u_{0},v_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
that may change its value from line to line.
For any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
and
h>0
, we will write

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{h}f(\tau)=\frac{1}{h}\big(f(\tau+h)-f(\tau)\big)

(3.10)

to denote the forward-in-time difference quotient of a suitable function
f
.

From (
3.9
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\big\langle\partial_{t}^{h}\partial_{t}u(\tau),w\big\rangle_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\big\langle\partial_{t}^{h}\partial_{t}v(\tau),z\big\rangle_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}=-\Big(\big(\partial_{t}^{h}\mu(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}\theta(\tau)\big),(w,z)\Big)_{L,\beta}

(3.11)

for almost all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
where

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{h}\mu(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\Delta\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)+\frac{1}{h}\Big(F^{\prime}\big(u(\tau+h)\big)-F^{\prime}\big(u(\tau)\big)\Big)

a.e. in
\Omega
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{h}\theta(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)+\frac{1}{h}\Big(G^{\prime}\big(v(\tau+h)\big)-G^{\prime}\big(v(\tau)\big)\Big)+\partial_{n}\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)

a.e. on
\Gamma

(3.13)

due to (
3.4c
) and (
3.4d
).
Plugging
(w,z)=\mathfrak{S}^{L}\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)
into (
3.11
), recalling (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), and using integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}=-\Big(\big(\partial_{t}^{h}\mu(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}\theta(\tau)\big),\mathfrak{S}^{L}\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\Big)_{L,\beta}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\big(\partial_{t}^{h}\mu(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\big)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big(\partial_{t}^{h}\theta(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\big\|\nabla\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\Big(F^{\prime}\big(u(\tau+h)\big)-F^{\prime}\big(u(\tau)\big)\Big)\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}+\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Gamma}\Big(G^{\prime}\big(v(\tau+h)\big)-G^{\prime}\big(v(\tau)\big)\Big)\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma

for almost all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}+\big\|\nabla\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\Big(F_{1}^{\prime}\big(u(\tau+h)\big)-F_{1}^{\prime}\big(u(\tau)\big)\Big)\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Gamma}\Big(G_{1}^{\prime}\big(v(\tau+h)\big)-G_{1}^{\prime}\big(v(\tau)\big)\Big)\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Omega}\Big(F_{2}^{\prime}\big(u(\tau+h)\big)-F_{2}^{\prime}\big(u(\tau)\big)\Big)\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt-\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\Gamma}\Big(G_{2}^{\prime}\big(v(\tau+h)\big)-G_{2}^{\prime}\big(v(\tau)\big)\Big)\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.
Recall that
F_{2}
,
G_{2}
are Lipschitz continuous, and that
F_{1}
and
G_{1}
are convex which directly implies that
F_{1}^{\prime}
and
G_{1}^{\prime}
are monotone. Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}+\big\|\nabla\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\\
&\quad\leq C\big(\big\|\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\|\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\big)\end{aligned}

(3.14)

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.
Using Lemma
2.3
we conclude that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}+\big\|\nabla\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\\
&\quad\leq C\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\end{aligned}

(3.15)

for all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.
Multiplying both sides by
\tau
and integrating the resulting inequality with respect to
\tau
from
0
to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle t\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(t),\partial_{t}^{h}v(t)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\big(C\tau+1\big)\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau

(3.16)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(\tau),\partial_{t}^{h}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+h}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u(s),\partial_{t}v(s)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\dfrac{1}{h}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+h}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u(s),\partial_{t}v(s)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s=\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u(\tau),\partial_{t}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}.

(3.18)

Recalling the energy inequality (
3.6
) and the fact that the energy
E
is non-negative (since the potentials
F
and
G
are non-negative), we derive the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\big\|\nabla\mu(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\frac{1}{L}\big\|\beta\theta(\tau)-\mu(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C.

(3.19)

By the definition of the solution operator
\mathfrak{S}^{L}
, we infer from (
3.4a
) and (
3.4b
) that

\displaystyle\mu(\tau)=\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Omega}\big(\partial_{t}u(\tau),\partial_{t}v(\tau)\big)+c(\tau)\beta,\hskip 10.00002pt\theta(\tau)=\mathfrak{S}^{L}_{\Gamma}\big(\partial_{t}u(\tau),\partial_{t}v(\tau)\big)+c(\tau)

(3.20)

for almost all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
and some function
c=c(\tau)
.
Testing (
3.4a
) and (
3.4b
) with
\mathfrak{S}^{L}\big(\partial_{t}u(\tau),\partial_{t}v(\tau)\big)
, and adding the resulting equations, we infer that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u(\tau),\partial_{t}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}=\big\|\big(\mu(\tau),\theta(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta}^{2}\\
&\quad=\big\|\nabla\mu(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\frac{1}{L}\big\|\beta\theta(\tau)-\mu(\tau)\big\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\end{aligned}

(3.21)

for almost all
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
.
Consequently, after integrating with respect to
\tau
from
0
to
t
, (
3.19
) and (
3.21
) imply

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u(\tau),\partial_{t}v(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$.}

(3.22)

Therefore, recalling (
3.17
), (
3.18
) and (
3.22
), we obtain from (
3.16
) that

\displaystyle t\big\|\big(\partial_{t}^{h}u(t),\partial_{t}^{h}v(t)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\leq C\big(t+1\big)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$.}

Invoking Lebesgue’s convergence theorem and passing to the limit
h\to 0
, we get

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u(t),\partial_{t}v(t)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{2}\leq C\left(\dfrac{t+1}{t}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$},

(3.23)

which in turn yields

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla\mu(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leq C\left(\dfrac{t+1}{t}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for almost all $t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$}

(3.24)

because of (
3.21
). Proceeding as in
[
42

we obtain the estimates

\displaystyle\big\|\mu(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\big\|\nabla\mu(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\big),

(3.25)

\displaystyle\big\|\theta(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C\big(1+\big\|\nabla\mu(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}\big)

(3.26)

for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
. This directly implies that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(\mu(t),\theta(t)\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C\left(\dfrac{t+1}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(3.27)

for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
. Furthermore, recalling the growth assumptions on
F
and
G
imposed in (

A3

), we conclude from the energy inequality (
3.6
) that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u(t),v(t)\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C+CE\big(u(t),v(t)\big)\leq C+CE\big(u_{0},v_{0}\big)\leq C

(3.28)

for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.
Since
(u,v)\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathcal{H}^{3})
for all
T>0
, we have

\displaystyle-\Delta u(t)

\displaystyle=\mu(t)-F^{\prime}\big(u(t)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in $\Omega$},

(3.29)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v(t)+\partial_{n}u(t)

\displaystyle=\theta(t)-G^{\prime}\big(v(t)\big)

a.e. on
\Gamma

(3.30)

for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
.
Invoking the growth assumptions on
F
and
G
in (

A3

), recalling that
p\leq 4
,
and using the continuous embeddings

\displaystyle\big\|G^{\prime}\big(v(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|v(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{q-1}\leq C,

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime\prime}\big(u(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|u(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{p-2}\leq C,

\displaystyle\big\|G^{\prime\prime}\big(v(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{3}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|v(t)\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{q-2}\leq C.

), and Young’s inequality, we thus infer that for any

\displaystyle\big\|F^{\prime}\big(u(t)\big)\big\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}=\big\|F^{\prime}\big(u(t)\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\big\|F^{\prime\prime}\big(u(t)\big)\nabla u(t)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}

\alpha\in(0,1)
and for any
\alpha>0

\displaystyle\big\|G^{\prime}\big(v(t)\big)\big\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\alpha}+\alpha\big\|v(t)\big\|_{H^{3}(\Gamma)}^{2}.

(3.32)

Furthermore, a regularity estimate for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
43

) implies that for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
,

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u(t),v(t)\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\big\|\mu(t)-F^{\prime}\big(u(t)\big)\big\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+C\big\|\theta(t)-G^{\prime}\big(u(t)\big)\big\|_{H^{1}(\Gamma)}^{2}

Eventually, invoking the estimates (
3.27
), (
3.1
) and (
3.32

\alpha\in(0,1)

t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u(t),v(t)\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3}}^{2}\leq C+C\big\|\big(\mu(t),\theta(t)\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}\leq C\left(\frac{t+1}{t}\right),

which verifies the smoothing property (
3.7
).
Thus, the proof of Theorem
3.1
is complete.

Using the interpolation inequalities from Section

and the energy inequality (

Proposition 3.3

(Improved continuity results)
.

Suppose that (

A1

)–(

A3

) hold and that
L\in(0,\infty)
. Let
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary,
let
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
be any initial datum, and let
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
denote the corresponding weak solution of the system (
3.1
).
Then it holds that

\displaystyle(u^{L},v^{L})\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{1})\cap C((0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{2}).

(3.33)

Step 1.

We first prove that
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C((0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{2})
. Therefore, we fix an arbitrary time
t_{0}\in(0,\infty)
. Let further
t\in(0,\infty)
with
\frac{1}{2}t_{0}<t<t_{0}+1
be arbitrary. We already know from Theorem
3.1
that

(u^{L},v^{L})\in L^{\infty}\big(\tfrac{1}{2}t_{0},t_{0}+1;\mathcal{H}^{2}\big)\cap C\big([\tfrac{1}{2}t_{0},t_{0}+1];\mathcal{L}^{2}\big).

This entails that
(u^{L},v^{L})(t)\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
for all
t\in[\tfrac{1}{2}t_{0},t_{0}+1]
(see, e.g.,
[
57
, Chap. III, §1, Lem. 1.4.]
).
Using Lemma
2.4
and the smoothing property (
3.7
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{0}),v^{L}(t_{0})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\big\|\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{0}),v^{L}(t_{0})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{3}}^{\frac{4}{3}}\big\|\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{0}),v^{L}(t_{0})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{3}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\left[\left(\frac{2(2+t_{0})}{t_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\left(\frac{1+t_{0}}{t_{0}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{\frac{4}{3}}\big\|\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{0}),v^{L}(t_{0})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{\frac{1}{3}}.

We now choose
T:=1+t_{0}
and recall that
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C([0,T];\mathcal{L}^{2})
.
Hence, the last line of the above estimate tends to zero as
t\to t_{0}
which proves
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C((0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{2})
.

Step 2.

We now want to show that
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{1})
. As
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C((0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{1})
is already established, only the continuity at
t=0
needs to be investigated.
To this end, let
\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
be an arbitrary sequence with
t_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

From the growth conditions on
F
and
G
in (

A3

), we deduce that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u(t_{k}),v(t_{k})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq C+CE\big(u(t_{k}),v(t_{k})\big)\leq C+CE(u_{0},v_{0})\leq C

(3.34)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, there exists a subsequence
\{t_{k}^{*}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
of
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and a pair
(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
such that

\displaystyle\big(u^{L}(t_{k}^{*}),v^{L}(t_{k}^{*})\big)\rightharpoonup(u_{*},v_{*})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\mathcal{H}^{1}$.}

(3.35)

Since
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{L}^{2})
, we also know that

\displaystyle\big(u^{L}(t_{k}),v^{L}(t_{k})\big)\to(u_{0},v_{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\mathcal{L}^{2}$}

(3.36)

and thus
(u_{*},v_{*})=(u_{0},v_{0})
a.e. in
\Omega\times\Gamma
due to uniqueness of the limit. This means that the weak limit in (
3.35
) does not depend on the subsequence extraction and thus, (
3.35
) holds true for the
whole
sequence, that is

\displaystyle\big(u^{L}(t_{k}),v^{L}(t_{k})\big)\rightharpoonup(u_{0},v_{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\mathcal{H}^{1}$.}

(3.37)

With some abuse of notation, we will write

E(t):=E\big(u(t),v(t)\big)
for all
t\geq 0
.
We further recall that
F_{1}
and
G_{1}
are convex, and that
F_{2}
and
G_{2}
have at most quadratic growth. Using Lebesgue’s general convergence theorem (see
[
2
, Sect. 3.25]
) for the non-convex contributions, and lower-semicontinuity of the convex contributions, we obtain

\displaystyle E(t_{0})\leq\underset{k\to\infty}{\lim\inf}\;E(t_{k}).

(3.38)

Moreover, we infer from the energy inequality (
3.6
) that the sequence
(E(t_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-decreasing and bounded from above by
E(t_{0})
. Hence, the limit
k\to\infty
exists, and in combination with (
3.38
) we conclude that

\displaystyle E(t_{k})\to E(t_{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as $k\to\infty$.}

(3.39)

By the definition of the energy functional
E
(see (
3.2
)), this implies that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2}\big\|\big(\nabla u^{L}(t_{k}),\nabla_{\Gamma}v^{L}(t_{k})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\big\|\big(\nabla u_{0},\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{0}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{2}\\
&=E(t_{k})-E(t_{0})+\int_{\Omega}F\big(u_{0}\big)-F\big(u^{L}(t_{k})\big)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}G\big(v_{0}\big)-G\big(v^{L}(t_{k})\big)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.\end{aligned}

(3.40)

Since
(u(t_{k}),v(t_{k}))\to(u_{0},v_{0})
strongly in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, and
(u(t_{k}),v(t_{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, we also have
(u(t_{k}),v(t_{k}))\to(u_{0},v_{0})
strongly in
L^{p}(\Omega)\times L^{q}(\Gamma)
via interpolation.
We can thus use Lebesgue’s general convergence theorem (see
[
2
, Sect. 3.25]
) to infer that the right-hand side of (
3.40
) tends to zero as
k\to\infty
. Along with (
3.36
), this yields

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}(t_{k}),v^{L}(t_{k})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}-\big\|\big(u_{0},v_{0}\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{2}\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{$k\to\infty$.}

(3.41)

Combining (
3.37
) and (
3.41
), we eventually conclude that

(u^{L}(t_{k}),v^{L}(t_{k}))
converges to
(u_{0},v_{0})
strongly in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
as
k\to\infty
. As the null sequence
(t_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
was arbitrary, this proves
(u^{L},v^{L})\in C([0,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{1})
.

Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

4

4.1

In this section, we investigate the long-time behavior of the KLLM model (
3.1
) for any fixed
L\in(0,\infty)
. We prove the existence of a global attractor for weak solutions of this system, and we establish the convergence of each weak solution to a single stationary point as

To this end, we first analyze the set of stationary points of the problem (

) with

4.1.1
The set of stationary points

For the semigroup
{\left\{S_{m}^{L}(t)\right\}}_{t\geq 0}
on
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
corresponding to the KLLM model with fixed

, the corresponding set of stationary points of the problem (

) can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{m}^{L}=\left\{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}:S_{m}^{L}(t)(u,v)=(u,v)\;\text{for all}\;t\geq 0\right\}.

We aim to show that the set
\mathcal{N}_{m}^{L}
is a nonempty and bounded subset of
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}
.

To this end, let
(u,v)\in\mathcal{N}_{m}^{L}
be arbitrary, and let
\mu
and
\theta
denote the corresponding chemical potentials given by (
3.4c
) and (
3.4d
).
Then the energy inequality (
3.6
) entails that

\int_{0}^{T}\left(\left\|\nabla\mu\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\left\|\nabla\theta\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma\right)}^{2}+\dfrac{1}{L}\left\|\beta\theta-\mu\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma\right)}^{2}\right)dt\leq 0.

Hence, there exists a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\mu=\beta\lambda
a.e. in
\Omega
and
\theta=\lambda
a.e. on
\Gamma
.
Integrating (
3.4c
) over
\Omega
and (
3.4d
) over

\lambda=\dfrac{\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}\left(u\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}G^{\prime}\left(v\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\beta\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|}\text{.}

(4.1)

Therefore, we obtain that
\mathcal{N}_{m}^{L}
consists of solutions to the following stationary problem.

\displaystyle-\Delta u+F^{\prime}\left(u\right)

\displaystyle=\beta\lambda

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ in }\Omega,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle-\Delta_{\Gamma}v+G^{\prime}\left(v\right)+\partial_{n}u

\displaystyle=\lambda

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ on }\Gamma,

(4.2b)

\displaystyle u|_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=v

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ on }\Gamma,

(4.2c)

\displaystyle\textstyle\beta\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}v\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma

\displaystyle=m.

(4.2d)

\mathcal{N}_{m}^{L}

4.2

Since the problem (
4.2

In the following, we interpret the total free energy as a continuous functional

\displaystyle E:{\mathcal{V}}^{1}\to[0,\infty),\hskip 10.00002pt(u,v)\mapsto E(u,v)

(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}

has a global minimizer on the space
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}

on
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
is actually a strong solution of the system
(\eqref{5.33},\eqref{5.34})

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

) hold. Then the functional
E|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}}
has at least one minimizer
(u_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
, i.e.,

E(u_{*},v_{*})=\inf_{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}}E\left(u,v\right).

To prove the assertion, we employ the direct method of calculus of variations.
From the assumptions (

A3

) on
F
and
G
, we obtain the lower bound

E\left(u,v\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla u\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\nabla_{\Gamma}v\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\Gamma\right)}^{2}+a_{F}\left\|u\right\|_{L_{p}\left(\Omega\right)}^{p}+a_{G}\left\|v\right\|_{L_{q}\left(\Gamma\right)}^{q}-b_{F}-b_{G}

(4.4)

for all
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
.
Hence, the infimum

{E}_{*}:=\underset{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}}{\inf}E\left(u,v\right)

exists and thus, we can find a minimizing sequence
\left\{(u_{k},v_{k})\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset

{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}

such that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E\left(u_{k},v_{k}\right)={E}_{*}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptE\left(u_{k},v_{k}\right)\leq{E}_{*}+1\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $k\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(4.5)

Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), it follows that
\left\{\left(u_{k},v_{k}\right)\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
{\cal H}^{1}
. Then, according to the Banach-Alaoglu theorem,
it holds that

(u_{k},v_{k})\rightharpoonup({u}^{*},{u}^{*})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in $\mathcal{H}^{1}$}

(4.6)

along a non-relabeled subsequence.
Invoking Sobolev’s embedding theorem we further deduce that

\displaystyle\left(u_{k},v_{k}\right)\rightarrow\left({u}^{*},{v}^{*}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }L^{p}\left(\Omega\right)\times L^{q}\left(\Gamma\right),\text{ and a.e.~in }\Omega\times\Gamma

(4.7)

after another subsequence extraction, where
p
and
q
are the exponents introduced in (

A3

).
Since
F
and
G
are continuous, (
4.7
) implies

F\left(u_{k}\right)\rightarrow F\left({u}^{*}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in }\Omega,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptG\left(v_{k}\right)\rightarrow G\left({v}^{*}\right)\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in }\Gamma.

Recalling assumption (

A3

), it follows from Lebesgue’s generalized convergence theorem
[
2
, Sect. 3.25]
that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}F\left(u_{k}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}F\left({u}^{*}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Gamma}G\left(v_{k}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\int_{\Gamma}G\left({v}^{*}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

As all the other terms in the energy functional are convex, they are lower semicontinuous, and we thus conclude that

E\left(u^{*},v^{*}\right)\leq\underset{m\rightarrow\infty}{\lim\inf}\;E\left(u_{k},v_{k}\right)={E}_{*}.

This proves that
(u^{*},v^{*})
is
a minimizer of the functional
E
on
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
.
∎

We further show that the energy functional is of class
C^{2}
.

Lemma 4.2

The energy functional

\displaystyle\left\langle E^{\prime}(u,v),(\zeta,\eta)\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\zeta+F^{\prime}(u)\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}v\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta+G^{\prime}(v)\eta\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

(4.8)

For every
(u,v),(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1}),(\zeta_{2},\eta_{2})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{1}
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\langle E^{\prime\prime}(u,v)(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1}),(\zeta_{2},\eta_{2})\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}^{1}}&=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\zeta_{1}\cdot\nabla\zeta_{2}+F^{\prime\prime}(u)\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\\
&\quad+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta_{1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta_{2}+G^{\prime\prime}(v)\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.\end{aligned}

(4.9)

The proof of this lemma is straightforward and will thus not be presented. For a very similar result including a detailed proof, we refer to
[
13
, Lem. 6.2]
.

Lemma 4.3

(Critical points of
E
are steady states)

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

(u_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(\mathrm{i})

(\mathrm{ii})

(u_{*},v_{*})

(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}

We first notice that (i) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left\langle E^{\prime}(u_{*},v_{*}),(\zeta,\xi)\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{V}}^{1}}\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u_{*}\cdot\nabla\zeta+F^{\prime}\left(u_{*}\right)\zeta\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\left(\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{*}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\xi+G^{\prime}\left(v_{*}\right)\xi\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=0\end{aligned}

(4.10)

for all
(\zeta,\xi)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
.
Now, for any
(\overline{\zeta},\overline{\xi})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{1}
, we define

\gamma=\dfrac{\beta\int_{\Omega}\overline{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\overline{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\beta\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|}.

Hence,
(\zeta,\xi)=(\overline{\zeta}-\gamma,\overline{\xi}-\gamma)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}\,
, and thus (
4.10
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\int_{\Omega}\left(\nabla u_{*}\cdot\nabla\overline{\zeta}+F^{\prime}\left(u_{*}\right)\overline{\zeta}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\left(\nabla_{\Gamma}v_{*}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\overline{\xi}+G^{\prime}\left(v_{*}\right)\overline{\xi}\right)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma\\
&\qquad=\int_{\Omega}F^{\prime}\left(u_{*}\right)\gamma\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}G^{\prime}\left(v_{*}\right)\gamma\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\int_{\Omega}\beta\lambda\,\overline{\zeta}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\lambda\,\overline{\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma\end{aligned}

(4.11)

for all
(\overline{\zeta},\overline{\xi})\in{\mathcal{V}}^{1}
.
This means that
(u_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
is
a weak solution of the problem ((
4.1
),(
4.2
)). Moreover, recalling the growth assumption (

A3

), we can use elliptic regularity theory for problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
43
, Thm. 3.3]
) to conclude that
(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{H}^{2}
. Therefore,
(u_{*},v_{*})
is actually a strong solution of the problem ((
4.1
),(
4.2
)) which directly yields
(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}
. We conclude that (i) and (ii) are equivalent and thus, the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

)-(

4.1.2
Existence of a global attractor

We first prove the following asymptotic compactness property by exploiting the smoothing estimate (

).

Lemma 4.5

(Asymptotic compactness)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

) are satisfied, and let
B
be a bounded subset
of
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
.
Then for any sequences
\left\{(u_{k},v_{k})\right\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset B

\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
with

as

{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}
.

We first obtain from the smoothing property (

) that for all

with

,

. Hence, there exists

Lemma 4.6

(Gradient system)
.

Suppose that (

A1

)-(

A3

) hold.
Then the energy functional
E
that was defined in (
3.2
) is a strict Lyapunov function for the dynamical system
({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},\{S_{m}^{L}\left(t\right)\}_{t\geq 0})
. This means that the dynamical system is a so-called
gradient system
.

Let
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
denote an arbitrary weak solution of the system (
3.1
) on
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
to the initial datum
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
in the sense of Theorem
3.1
. In particular, this means that
\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)=S_{m}^{L}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})
for all
t\geq 0
.
Interpreting
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
as a weak solution starting at any time
s\geq 0
(instead of zero), the energy inequality (
3.6
)
yields that for almost all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
with
t\geq s
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &E\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)\\
&\;\;+\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}\big\|\nabla\mu^{L}(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+\big\|\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta^{L}(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\frac{1}{L}\big\|\beta\theta^{L}(\tau)-\mu^{L}(\tau)\big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\\
&\leq E\left(u^{L}(s),v^{L}(s)\right).\end{aligned}

(4.17)

This implies that
E

is nonincreasing along weak solutions of the system (
3.1
). Hence, the energy functional
E
is a Lyapunov function for the dynamical system
\{S_{m}^{L}\left(t\right)\}_{t\geq 0}
.

To prove that the Lyapunov function
E
is actually strict, we assume that there exists a weak solution
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
as well as
s,t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
with
s<t
such that
(u^{L},v^{L})\notin\mathcal{N}_{m}
and

E\left(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\right)=E\left(u^{L}(s),v^{L}(s)\right).

Since,
E
is nonincreasing along weak solutions, this directly implies that
E(u^{L}(\cdot),v^{L}(\cdot))
is constant on the time interval
[s,t]
.
Thus, the inequality (
4.17
) entails that

\displaystyle\nabla\mu^{L}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~in }\Omega\times[s,t],

(4.18a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\Gamma}\theta^{L}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~on }\Gamma\times[s,t],

(4.18b)

\displaystyle\beta\theta^{L}

\displaystyle=\mu^{L}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\text{a.e.~on }\Gamma\times[s,t].

(4.18c)

Plugging these relations into the weak formulations (
3.4a
) and (
3.4b
), we infer that
u^{L}
and
v^{L}
are constant in time on
[s,t]
. It immediately follows that
\mu^{L}
and
\theta^{L}
are also constant on
[s,t]
, and from the uniqueness of weak solutions, we eventually conclude that
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
is constant on the whole time interval
\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
. This means that
(u^{L},v^{L})
solves (
4.2
) and thus,
(u^{L},v^{L})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}
, which contradicts the assumptions.

We have thus proven that the energy functional
E
is strictly decreasing along non-stationary weak solutions of the system (
3.1
) and thus,

E
is a strict Lyapunov function for the dynamical system
({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},\{S_{m}^{L}\left(t\right)\}_{t\geq 0})
.
∎

To prove the existence of a global attractor which is bounded in
\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{3}
, we first recall the following definitions.

Definition 4.7

(Global attractor, cf.
[
15
, Def. 7.2.1.]
)
.

Let
\left(X,d\right)
be a metric space and let
\left\{S(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
be a semigroup on
X
.
A set
\mathcal{A}\subset X
is called a
global attractor for the dynamical system
(X,\{S(t)\}_{t\geq 0})
, if

(\mathrm{i})

\mathcal{A}
is a compact subset of
X
,

(\mathrm{ii})

\mathcal{A}
is invariant, i.e.
S(t)\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}
for all
t\geq 0
,

(\mathrm{iii})

\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\lim}\mathrm{dist}_{X}(S(t)B,\mathcal{A})=0
for every bounded set
B\subset X
.

In particular, it follows directly from this definition that a global attractor is always unique.

Definition 4.8

(Unstable manifold, cf.
[
15
, Def. 7.5.1.]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{N}
be the set of stationary points of the dynamical system
\left(X,\{S(t)\}_{t\geq 0}\right)
.
We define the unstable manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\mathcal{N}\right)

emanating from the set
\mathcal{N}
as the set of all
y\in X
such that
there exists a full trajectory
\gamma=\{u(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
where
u
satisfies the properties

u(0)=y\text{ and }\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}\mathrm{dist}_{X}(u(t),\mathcal{N})=0.

From the results established above, we now deduce the existence of a unique global attractor.

Theorem 4.9

(Existence of a unique global attractor)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

) are satisfied. Then, the
semigroup
\left\{S_{m}^{L}(t)\right\}_{t\geq 0}
generated by
the weak solutions of the problem (
3.1
) possesses a unique global attractor
{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{L}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
, and it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}=\mathcal{M}^{L}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\mathcal{N}_{m}\right).

(4.19)

Moreover, the global attractor
{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{L}
is a bounded subset of
\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{3}
.

Due to Lemma
4.4
, Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
, the existence of a unique global attractor
{\mathcal{A}}_{m}^{L}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
to the dynamical system
({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},S_{m}^{L}\left(t\right))
which can be expressed as
\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}=\mathcal{M}^{L}_{\mathrm{u}}\left(\mathcal{N}_{m}\right)
follows directly from
[
15
, Cor. 7.5.7]
.
The fact that
\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{3}
is a direct consequence of the invariance property in Definition
4.7
(ii) and the smoothing property (
3.7
).
∎

4.1.3
Convergence to stationary points

The invariance property of the global attractor ensures that for any
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}
, the corresponding weak solution
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
exists on
\mathbb{R}
and it holds that

\displaystyle(u_{0},v_{0})\in\left\{(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t))\,\big|\,t\in\mathbb{R}\right\}\subset\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}.

A detailed proof can be found, e.g., in
[
14
, Lem. 6.1]
.
In particular, due to Proposition

(b), this weak solution satisfies

.
This allows the following definition:

Definition 4.10

(

-limit set and
\alpha
-limit set)
.

For any initial datum
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}\,
, let

).
We define the set

\displaystyle\omega^{L}\left(u_{0},v_{0}\right):=\left\{(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}\;\middle|\;\begin{aligned} &\text{$\exists\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}$ with $t_{k}\to\infty$ such that}\\
&\text{$S^{L}_{m}\left(t_{k}\right)(u_{0},v_{0})\to(u_{*},v_{*})$ in ${\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}$}\\
\end{aligned}\right\},

which is called the
\omega
-limit set
of
(u_{0},v_{0})
. If
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}
, we further define

\end{aligned}\right\},

which is referred to as the

-limit set
of

.

For our purposes, the following properties will be essential.

Lemma 4.11

(Properties of the limit sets)
.

The limit sets have the following properties:

(\mathrm{a})

For any
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}\,
, the set

(4.20)

(\mathrm{b})

For any
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}\,
, the set
\alpha^{L}\left(u_{0},v_{0}\right)
is nonempty, compact in
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}
, and invariant in the sense of Definition

(ii).
Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to-\infty}\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}\big(S^{L}_{m}(t)(u_{0},v_{0}),\alpha^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})\big)=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\alpha^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})\subset\mathcal{N}_{m}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}.

The assertions in (a) follow directly from
[

[

\displaystyle\alpha^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{\phantom{\Big|}\big\{\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)\;\big|\;t\leq-n\big\}}^{\mathcal{H}^{2}},

We will now show that for every

(u_{-\infty},v_{-\infty})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}
as

Arguing as in
[

we next establish a Łojasiewicz–Simon type inequality.
Here, we restrict ourselves to dealing only with the case

. To see that this is not really a restriction, we
introduce the constant

Lemma 4.12

(Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality)
.

Suppose that (

A5

) holds, and that
(u_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
is a critical point of the functional
E|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}
. Then, there exist constants
\gamma\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
and
C,\sigma>0
depending only on

,
\beta
,
\Omega
and
(u_{*},v_{*})
such that for all
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
with
\|(u,v)-(u_{*},v_{*})\|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}<\sigma
, the Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality

\displaystyle\left|E(u,v)-E(u_{*},v_{*})\right|^{1-\gamma}\leq C\|E^{\prime}(u,v)\|_{({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}}

(4.22)

is satisfied.

Comment.
According to Lemma
4.3
, any critical point
(u_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
of the energy
E
is actually a stationary point, i.e., the regularity
(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{N}_{0}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
holds automatically.

To prove the lemma, we will show that the abstract result in
[
12
, Cor. 3.11]
can be applied. In the following, we consider the restriction of the energy functional to the linear subspace
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
of
{\mathcal{V}}^{1}
, and we write
\bar{E}:=E|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}
,
\bar{E}^{\prime}:=E^{\prime}|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}

\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}:=E^{\prime\prime}|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}
.

We first notice that Sobolev’s embedding theorem yields
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}\subset\mathcal{L}^{\infty}
. Hence, the restriction of
\bar{E}^{\prime}
to
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
is analytic with values in
\mathcal{L}^{2}
. For more a more detailed reasoning in a similar situation we refer to
[
12
, Cor. 4.6]
.
For arbitrary
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
, we regard the second-order Fréchet derivative
\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}
as the linearization of
\bar{E}^{\prime}
which means

\displaystyle\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v):{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}\to({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime},\hskip 10.00002pt(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1})\mapsto\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1}).

In view of (
4.9
),

\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
can be interpreted as a continuous, symmetric, elliptic differential operator associated with the bilinear form

\displaystyle\left\langle\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1}),(\zeta_{2},\eta_{2})\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\zeta_{1}\cdot\nabla\zeta_{2}+F^{\prime\prime}(u)\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta_{1}\cdot\nabla_{\Gamma}\eta_{2}+G^{\prime\prime}(v)\eta_{1}\eta_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma,\hskip 10.00002pt(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1}),(\zeta_{2},\eta_{2})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}},

which results from (
4.9
).
We further interpret the restriction of
\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
to
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
as

\displaystyle\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}}:{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}\to\mathcal{L}^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1})\mapsto\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)(\zeta_{1},\eta_{1}).

Invoking the Lax–Milgram theorem, we deduce that the operator

\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
has a nontrivial resolvent set. In particular, there exists at least one
\lambda>0
such that the linear map

\displaystyle\big(\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)+\lambda\text{id}\big)^{-1}:({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}\to({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}

is well-defined. As the embedding
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}\hookrightarrow({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}

is compact, we conclude that this linear operator is compact.

By regularity theory for elliptic problems with bulk-surface coupling (see
[
43
, Thm. 3.3]
), we conclude that the kernel

\text{Ker}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
belongs to
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
. We further recall that the embedding
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{L}^{2}
is compact.

Now, proceeding as in
[
24
, Sect. 6.2, Thm. 4]
, the Fredholm alternative implies that the kernel

\text{Ker}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
is finite dimensional, and that the ranges

\text{Rg}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
and

\text{Rg}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)\big|\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scriptsize${\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}$}

are closed in
({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{L}^{2}
, respectively.
Furthermore,

({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}
can be expressed as the orthogonal sum of

\text{Ker}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
and

\text{Rg}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
,
whereas

\mathcal{L}^{2}
can be expressed as the orthogonal sum of

\text{Ker}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
and

\text{Rg}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)\big|\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scriptsize${\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}$}
.
In particular, we can define a continuous orthogonal projection

P:{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
with
\text{Rg}\,P=\text{Ker}\,\bar{E}^{\prime\prime}(u,v)
.

Given these considerations, we can conclude the proof by applying the results in
[
12
, Cor. 3.11]
with
X={\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
,
V={\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
,
Y=\mathcal{L}^{2}
, and
W=({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}
.
∎

Theorem 4.13

(Convergence to a single stationary point as
t\to\infty
)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

) and (

A5

) are satisfied. Then, for any
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
there exists a unique stationary point
(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\big\|S_{m}^{L}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})-(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}=0.

(4.23)

In particular, this means that
\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})=\{(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{m}
.

As discussed above, it suffices to handle the case
m=0
.
Let
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
be arbitrary, and let
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
denote the corresponding weak solution of the system (
3.1
). To prove the existence of a unique limit
(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\in\mathcal{N}_{0}
, it suffices to show that the
\omega
-limit set of
(u_{0},v_{0})
consists of one single point.

Since
E
is bounded from below and decreasing along weak solutions, we know that the limit

\displaystyle E_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}E(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t))

exists. Hence, recalling the definition of
\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
, we infer that

\displaystyle E(u_{*},v_{*})=E_{\infty}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all}\;(u_{*},v_{*})\in\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0}).

(4.24)

Since
E
is nonincreasing along weak solutions, it holds that
E(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t))\geq E_{\infty}
for all
t\geq 0
.

If there exists a time
t_{*}\geq 0
such that
E(u^{L}(t_{*}),v^{L}(t_{*}))=E_{\infty}
, then the existence of a unique limit
(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}
is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.6
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\big(u_{0},v_{0}\big)=\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)=\big(u^{L}(t_{*}),v^{L}(t_{*})\big)=\big(u_{\infty},v_{\infty}\big)

for all
t\geq 0
.

In the following, we will thus assume that
E(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t))>E_{\infty}
for all
t\geq 0
. From Lemma
4.12
we infer that for every critical point
(u_{*},v_{*})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
of
E
there exist constants
\gamma(u_{*},v_{*})\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
and
C(u_{*},v_{*})
,
\sigma(u_{*},v_{*})>0
such that the the Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality (
4.22
) holds for all

\displaystyle(u,v)\in B_{\sigma(u_{*},v_{*})}(u_{*},v_{*})=\Big\{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}\,:\,\|(u,v)-(u_{*},v_{*})\|_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}}<\sigma(u_{*},v_{*})\Big\}.

Obviously, the set
\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
is covered by the union of all open balls
B_{\sigma(u_{*},v_{*})}(u_{*},v_{*})
with
(u_{*},v_{*})\in\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
.
Since
\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
is compact in
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
, we can select finitely many points
(u_{n},v_{n})\in\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
,
n=1,...,N
such that

\displaystyle\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})\subset\left(\,\bigcup_{n=1}^{N}B_{\sigma(u_{n},v_{n})}(u_{n},v_{n})\right)=:\mathcal{U}.

Recalling (
4.24
) and arguing as in
[
13
, Theorem 2.3]
,
we can now find constants

\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
and
C>0
depending only on
L
,
\beta
and
\Omega
and
(u_{0},v_{0})
but not on
n
or
N
such that for all
(u,v)\in\cal U
,

\displaystyle\left|E(u,v)-E_{\infty}\right|^{1-\gamma}\leq C\|E^{\prime}(u,v)\|_{({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}}\;.

(4.25)

We point out that
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
can be assumed without loss of generality.
Since
\mathcal{U}
is an open subset of
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
, and
\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})\subset\mathcal{U}
, there exists a time
t_{0}\geq 1
such that
\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)\subset\mathcal{U}
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. In particular, this means that
\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)
satisfies the inequality (
4.25
) for all
t\geq t_{0}
.

Let now
s\geq t_{0}
be arbitrary, and without loss of generality we assume that
\mu^{L}
and
\theta^{L}
can be evaluated at the time
s
with
\big(\mu^{L}(s),\theta^{L}(s)\big)\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
. Now, applying integration by parts on (
4.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle E^{\prime}\big(u^{L}(s),v^{L}(s)\big),(\zeta,\xi)\right\rangle_{{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}}=\int_{\Omega}\mu^{L}(s)\,\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\theta^{L}(s)\,\xi\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma

(4.26)

for all
(\zeta,\xi)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
. We now define

\displaystyle c:=\frac{\beta\int_{\Omega}\mu^{L}(s)\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\theta^{L}(s)\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma}{\beta^{2}\left|\Omega\right|+\left|\Gamma\right|},\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\mu}:=\mu^{L}(s)-\beta c,\hskip 10.00002pt\bar{\theta}:=\theta^{L}(s)-c,

meaning that
(\bar{\mu}(s),\bar{\theta}(s))\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
. For any
(\zeta,\xi)\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}}
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mu^{L}(s)\,\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\theta^{L}(s)\,\xi\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma=\int_{\Omega}\bar{\mu}\zeta\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Gamma}\bar{\theta}\xi\,\mathrm{d}\Gamma.

(4.27)

Applying the bulk-surface Poincaré type inequality presented in
[
43
, Lem. 7.1]
(with
K:=L
and
\alpha:=\beta
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\big\|E^{\prime}\big(u^{L}(s),v^{L}(s)\big)\big\|_{({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{1}})^{\prime}}\leq\|(\bar{\mu},\bar{\theta})\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}\leq c_{P}\|(\bar{\mu},\bar{\theta})\|_{L,\beta}=c_{P}\big\|\big(\mu^{L}(s),\theta^{L}(s)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta}\;,

(4.28)

where the constant
c_{P}>0
depends only on
L
,
\beta
and
\Omega
.

Recalling that
(u,v)\in C([1,\infty);\mathcal{H}^{2})
and interpreting
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
as a weak solution starting at time
s
(instead of zero), the energy inequality (
3.6
) yields that (
4.17
) holds for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
with
t\geq s
. As the right-hand side of this inequality does not depend on
t
, we may pass to the limit
t\to\infty
on the left-hand side, which gives

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{\infty}\big\|\big(\mu^{L}(\tau),\theta^{L}(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq E(u^{L}(s),v^{L}(s))-E_{\infty}.

(4.29)

Hence, combining (
4.25
), (
4.28
) and (
4.29
), we infer that

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{\infty}\big\|\big(\mu^{L}(\tau),\theta^{L}(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C\big\|\big(\mu^{L}(s),\theta^{L}(s)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta}^{1/(1-\gamma)}

for some constant
C\geq 0
depending only on
L
,
\beta
and
\Omega
, and almost all
s\geq t_{0}
.
We recall that
\gamma<\frac{1}{2}
was assumed without loss of generality.
As the expression on the left hand side can also be bounded by a constant via the energy inequality (
3.6
), we invoke
[
25
, Lem. 7.1]

to deduce that

\displaystyle\nabla\mu^{L}\in L^{1}(t_{0},\infty;L^{2}(\Omega)),\text{ \ }\nabla\theta^{L}\in L^{1}(t_{0},\infty;L^{2}(\Gamma)),\text{ \ }\beta\theta^{L}-\mu^{L}\in L^{1}(t_{0},\infty;L^{2}(\Gamma)).

(Alternatively, one could also argue as in
[
13
, Proof of Thm. 2.3]
to obtain the same result.)
Recalling the weak formulations (
3.4a
) and (
3.4b
), we further obtain

(\partial_{t}u^{L},\partial_{t}v^{L})\in L^{1}\big(t_{0},\infty;\mathcal{H}_{\beta}^{-1}\big)
.
Consequently, for any
t_{0}<t_{1}<t_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}(t_{2}),v^{L}(t_{2})\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{1}),v^{L}(t_{1})\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}\leq\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\big\|\big(\partial_{t}u^{L}(\tau),\partial_{t}v^{L}(\tau)\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}\,\mathrm{d}\tau\;,

and the right-hand side converges to zero as
t_{2}>t_{1}\to\infty
.
Using the interpolation inequalities from Lemma
2.4
and Corollary
2.5
along with the smoothing property (
3.7
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}(t_{2}),v^{L}(t_{2})\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{1}),v^{L}(t_{1})\big)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\left(\frac{t+1}{t}\right)^{\frac{3}{8}}\big\|\big(u^{L}(t_{2}),v^{L}(t_{2})\big)-\big(u^{L}(t_{1}),v^{L}(t_{1})\big)\big\|_{L,\beta,*}^{\frac{1}{4}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\longrightarrow 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as $t_{2}>t_{1}\to\infty$}.

Invoking the Cauchy criterion, this implies the existence of a unique limit
(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
such that the convergence assertion (
4.23
) is satisfied. In particular, it holds that
(u_{\infty},v_{\infty})\in\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})\subset\mathcal{N}_{0}
which completes the proof.
∎

We will now see that solutions starting in
\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}
converge to a stationary point also for
t\to-\infty
.

Theorem 4.14

(Convergence to a single stationary point as
t\to-\infty
)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

) and (

A5

) hold. Then, for any
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}
, the corresponding state
(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t))
exists for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and there exists a unique stationary point
(u_{-\infty},v_{-\infty})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to-\infty}\|\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)-(u_{-\infty},v_{-\infty})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{2}}=0.

(4.30)

In particular, this means that
\alpha^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})=\{(u_{-\infty},v_{-\infty})\}\subset\mathcal{N}_{m}
.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.13
, it suffices to address the case
m=0
.
Let
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{A}_{0}^{L}
be arbitrary, and let
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
denote the corresponding weak solution of the system (
3.1
).

We recall that the state
\big(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t)\big)
associated with
(u_{0},v_{0})
exists for all times
t\in\mathbb{R}
and lies in
A^{L}_{0}
.
According to Definition
4.7
and Theorem
4.9
, the global attractor
\mathcal{A}^{L}_{0}
is a compact subset of
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,0}^{2}}
. Hence, since
E
is continuous, we know that
E(\mathcal{A}^{L}_{0})\subset\mathbb{R}
is compact. As
E
is nonincreasing along weak solutions, we infer that the limit

\displaystyle E_{-\infty}:=\lim_{t\rightarrow-\infty}E(u^{L}(t),v^{L}(t))

exists. Recalling the definition of
\alpha^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
, we conclude that

\displaystyle E(u_{*},v_{*})=E_{-\infty}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $(u_{*},v_{*})\in\alpha^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})$}.

Based on this, the claim of Theorem
4.14
can be established by proceeding analogously to the proof of Theorem
4.13
.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following result which provides further information of the geometric structure of the global attractor.

Corollary 4.15
.

Under the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A3

) and (

A5

), the global attractor
{\cal{A}}_{m}^{L}
of the dynamical system
({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},S_{m}^{L}(t))
can be defined as the union of the unstable manifolds of all stationary points, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\cal{A}}_{m}^{L}=\underset{(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathcal{N}_{m}}{\bigcup}\mathcal{M}^{L}_{\text{u}}\big(\{(u_{*},v_{*})\}\big).

(4.31)

The assertion follows directly from Theorem
4.14
and Definition
4.8
.

Remark 4.16
.

If the set of stationary points were finite, Corollary
4.15
could also be established without the Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality. However, as the set of stationary points is usually infinite, we would merely get
{\cal{A}}_{m}^{L}=\mathcal{M}^{L}_{\text{u}}(\mathcal{N}_{m})
(see (
4.19
)) instead of (
4.31
) if we disregard the Łojasiewicz–Simon inequality.

4.2
Long-time dynamics of the GMS model

Based on a result from
[
53
]
on closed semigroups, the existence of a global attractor in the “finite-energy phase space” has already been established for the GMS model (
L=0
) in
[
36
, Cor. 3.11]
.

However, it is also possible to obtain the existence of a global attractor for the dynamical system
\big({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},S_{m}^{0}(t)\big)
by using the same arguments as in Section
4.1
.
It is worth mentioning that the GMS model has the same set of stationary points
\mathcal{N}_{m}
as the KLLM model. Hence, we already know from Subsection
4.1.1
that the stationary point set of the GMS model is nonempty and bounded in
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
.
The smoothing property (
3.66
) implies the asymptotic compactness of the dynamical system
\big({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},S_{m}^{0}(t)\big)
, meaning that an analogue of Lemma
4.5
can be established.
Furthermore, it can be shown that the energy functional
E
is a strict Lyapunov function for the dynamical system
\big({\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}},S_{m}^{0}(t)\big)
and consequently, the existence of a unique global attractor
\mathcal{A}_{m}^{0}
can be proved analogously as in Subsection
4.1.2
.

It was further established in
[
36
, Thm. 3.22]
that the
\omega
-limits set consists of one single stationary point. We point out that Theorem
4.14
could be adapted to the case
L=0
and thus, Corollary
4.15
remains true for the GMS model.

5
Stability of the global attractor for the GMS model

We intend to study the stability of the global attractor
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{m}
of the GMS model (
L=0
) with respect to perturbations
\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}
with small
L>0
.
At least formally, this means we have to investigate the asymptotic limit
L\to 0
of the family
\{\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}\}_{L>0}
of global attractors for the KLLM model.
To provide a rigorous notion of such a limit, we recall the following definition which is included in
[
15
, Thm. 7.2.8]
.

Definition 5.1

(Upper semicontinuity of global attractors)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
\Lambda
be a metric space.
Suppose that for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
(X,\{S^{\lambda}(t)\}_{t\geq 0})
is a dynamical system possessing a global attractor
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}\subset X
.
Then, the family
\{\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\geq 0}
is called
upper semicontinuous at the point
\lambda_{*}\in\Lambda
if

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\lambda_{*}}\mathrm{dist}_{X}\big(\mathcal{A}^{\lambda},\mathcal{A}^{\lambda_{*}}\big)=0.

To prove that the family
\{\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}\}_{L\geq 0}
is upper semicontinuous at
L=0
, we need to know how a weak solution
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
to the KLLM model behaves in the asymptotic limit
L\to 0
.

Lemma 5.2

(The asymptotic limit
L\to 0
)
.

Suppose that (

A1

)–(

A3

) hold, let
L>0
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
be any initial datum.
Let
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
denote the corresponding unique weak solution to the KLLM model,
and let
(u^{0},v^{0},\mu^{0},\theta^{0})
denote the corresponding weak solution of the GMS model. Then, for any
T_{*}>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle(u^{L},v^{L})\to(u^{0},v^{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $C([0,T_{*}];\mathcal{L}^{2})$ as $L\to 0$}.

(5.1)

Moreover, there exist constants
A,B>0
depending (monotonically increasingly) on
\|(u_{0},v_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
but not on
L
such that for all
L>0
,

\displaystyle\big\|(u^{L},v^{L})-(u^{0},v^{0})\big\|_{C([1,T_{*}];\mathcal{L}^{2})}\leq Ae^{BT_{*}}\,L^{\frac{1}{4}}.

(5.2)

The convergence (
5.1
) has already been established in
[
42
, Thm. 4.1]
. Moreover, is was also shown that there exists a positive constant
c(T_{*})
depending (monotonically increasingly) on
\|(u_{0},v_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
and
T_{*}
but not on
L
such that for all
L>0
,

\displaystyle\big\|(u^{L},v^{L})-(u^{0},v^{0})\big\|_{C([1,T_{*}];\mathcal{L}^{2})}\leq c(T_{*})\,L^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Studying the proof of
[
42
, Thm. 4.1]
carefully, we find that the constant
c(T_{*})
depends (at most) exponentially on
T_{*}
. This results from the application of Gronwall’s lemma. We can thus find constants
A,B>0
independent of
L
such that
c(T_{*})=Ae^{BT_{*}}
. Thus, the proof is complete.
∎

By means of the smoothing properties (
3.7
) and (
3.66
), we can further generalize this convergence result to higher order norms.

Corollary 5.3

(Convergence rates for the limit
L\to 0
)
.

Suppose that (

A1

)–(

A4

) hold, let
L>0
and
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary, and let
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
be any initial datum.
Let
(u^{L},v^{L},\mu^{L},\theta^{L})
denote the corresponding unique weak solution to the KLLM model,
and let
(u^{0},v^{0},\mu^{0},\theta^{0})
denote the corresponding weak solution of the GMS model. Then, for any
T_{*}>1
,

\displaystyle(u^{L},v^{L})\to(u^{0},v^{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{strongly in $C([1,T_{*}];\mathcal{H}^{2})$ as $L\to 0$}.

(5.3)

Moreover, for any
T_{*}>1
, there exist constants
C_{1}(T_{*}),C_{2}(T_{*})>0
depending (monotonically increasingly) on
\|(u_{0},v_{0})\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
but not on
L
such that

\displaystyle\big\|(u^{L},v^{L})-(u^{0},v^{0})\big\|_{C([1,T_{*}];\mathcal{H}^{1})}\leq C_{1}(T_{*})\,L^{\frac{1}{6}},

(5.4)

\displaystyle\big\|(u^{L},v^{L})-(u^{0},v^{0})\big\|_{C([1,T_{*}];\mathcal{H}^{2})}\leq C_{2}(T_{*})\,L^{\frac{1}{12}}.

(5.5)

Let
T_{*}>1
be arbitrary.
We first recall that
(u^{L},v^{L}),(u^{0},v^{0})\in C([1,T_{*}];\mathcal{H}^{2})
according to Proposition
3.4
and Proposition
3.9
. Using the interpolation inequality (
2.20
) from Lemma
2.4
, and the smoothing properties (
3.7
) and (
3.66
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\underset{t\in[1,T_{*}]}{\sup}\;\|(u^{L},v^{L})(t)-(u^{0},v^{0})(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\underset{t\in[1,T_{*}]}{\sup}\;\|(u^{L},v^{L})(t)-(u^{0},v^{0})(t)\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}^{\frac{2}{3}}\left[(C_{*}+C_{0})(1+T_{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}.

Invoking the convergence rate (
5.2
) we directly conclude (
5.4
) from the above estimate.
The estimate (
5.5
) can be established similarly. In particular, this proves (
5.3
) and thus, the proof is complete.
∎

We now intend to establish the following stability result which is the main result of this section. Here, the term “stability” is to be understood as semicontinuity of the family of perturbed global attractors.

Theorem 5.4

(Stability of the global attractor
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{m}
)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A5

) hold and let
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary. Then, the family of global attractors
\{\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}\}_{L\geq 0}
is upper semicontinuous at
L=0
in the sense of Definition
5.1
.

To prove this theorem, we will exploit the following abstract result which can be found in
[
15
, Thm. 7.2.8]
.

Proposition 5.5

(A criterion for upper semicontinuity of global attractors)
.

Let
X
be a Banach space, and let
\Lambda
be a metric space.
Suppose that for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
(X,\{S^{\lambda}(t)\}_{t\geq 0})
is a dynamical system possessing a global attractor
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}\subset X
.
We further assume that the following conditions hold:

\mathrm{(i)}

There exists a compact set
K\subset X
such that
\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}\subset K
for all
\lambda\geq 0
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

If
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
and
\lambda_{k}\in\Lambda
are sequences satisfying

•

x_{k}\in\mathcal{A}^{\lambda_{k}}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

x_{k}\to x_{*}
as
k\to\infty
,

•

\lambda_{k}\to\lambda_{*}
as
k\to\infty
,

then there exists
t_{*}>0
such that
S^{\lambda_{k}}(t)x_{k}\rightarrow S^{\lambda_{*}}(t)x_{*}
in
X
for all
t>t_{*}
.

Then the family
\{\mathcal{A}^{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\geq 0}
is upper semicontinuous at the point
\lambda_{*}
.

In order to apply Proposition
5.5
, we need to verify the conditions (i) and (ii) imposed therein.

Step 1:
To verify Condition (i), we show that there exists a compact set
\mathcal{K}_{m}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
independent of
L
such that
\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}
for all
L\geq 0
.

For the KLLM model (
L>0
), Lemma
4.11
implies that for every
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
, the set
\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})
is included in the set of stationary points. As discussed Subsection
4.2
, the same holds true for the GMS model (
L=0
). We further recall that the set
\mathcal{N}_{m}
is a bounded subset of
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}
which does not depend on the parameter
L
.
Hence, we can choose an open, bounded set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathcal{H}^{2}
such that for all
L\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\omega^{L}(u_{0},v_{0})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{m}\subset\mathcal{U}

for all
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
.
Consequently, for every
L\geq 0
and every initial datum
(u_{0},v_{0})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
, there exists a time

t^{L}_{(u_{0},v_{0})}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle S^{L}_{m}\left(t\right)(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathcal{U}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\geq t^{L}_{(u_{0},v_{0})}$.}

(5.6)

Now, we define the set

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{m}:=\overline{\underset{L\in[0,\infty)}{\bigcup}\;\underset{t\geq 1}{\bigcup}\;S^{L}_{m}\left(t\right)\mathcal{U}}^{\mathcal{H}^{1}}.

(5.7)

Since
\mathcal{U}
is bounded in
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{2}}
uniformly in
L\geq 0
, the smoothing property (
3.7
) of the KLLM model implies that
S^{L}_{m}\left(t\right)\mathcal{U}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{3}
uniformly in
L>0
and
t\geq 1
. Moreover, using the smoothing property (
3.66
) of the GMS model, we conclude that
S^{\infty}_{m}\left(t\right)\mathcal{U}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{3}
uniformly in
t\geq 1
. This entails that
\mathcal{K}_{m}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
is bounded in
\mathcal{H}^{3}
, closed in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, and thus compact in
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
.
We further infer from (
5.6
) that
\mathcal{K}_{m}
is an
absorbing set
for every semigroup
S^{L}_{m}(t)
with
L\geq 0

(i.e., for every
L\geq 0
and every bounded set
B\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
, there exists a time
t_{B}^{L}\geq 1
such that
S^{L}_{m}(t)B\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}
for all
t\geq t_{B}^{L}
).
Recall that for all
L\geq 0
, the global attractor
A_{m}^{L}
is a bounded subset of
{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
. We thus conclude that there exists a time
t^{L}\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}=S_{m}^{L}(t^{L})\mathcal{A}_{m}^{L}\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}

due to the invariance property of the global attractor. This directly proves that
\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}
for all
L\geq 0
.

Step 2:
To verify Condition (ii) of Proposition
5.5
, let
\{(u_{k},v_{k})\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
be any sequence with
(u_{k},v_{k})\in\mathcal{A}^{L_{k}}_{m}
and
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(u_{*},v_{*})
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
as
k\to\infty
, and let
\{L_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
be any sequence with
L_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Let now
t\geq t_{*}:=1
and
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary. Using the continuous dependence estimate from Proposition
3.9
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\big\|S^{L_{k}}_{m}(t)(u_{k},v_{k})-S^{0}_{m}(t)(u_{*},v_{*})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\big\|S^{L_{k}}_{m}(t)(u_{k},v_{k})-S^{0}_{m}(t)(u_{k},v_{k})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}+\big\|S^{0}_{m}(t)(u_{k},v_{k})-S^{0}_{m}(t)(u_{*},v_{*})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\underset{(u,v)\in\mathcal{K}_{m}}{\sup}\;\big\|S^{L_{k}}_{m}(\cdot)(u,v)-S^{0}_{m}(\cdot)(u,v)\big\|_{C([0,t];\mathcal{H}^{1})}+\Lambda_{1}^{0}(t)\big\|(u_{k},v_{k})-(u_{*},v_{*})\big\|_{L,\beta,*}.

(5.8)

Since
L_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, and since
\mathcal{K}_{m}
is bounded, Corollary
5.3
implies the existence of a number
K_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq K_{1}
, the first summand in (
5.8
) is smaller than
\varepsilon/2
. Furthermore, the convergence
(u_{k},v_{k})\to(u_{*},v_{*})
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
directly implies that

\displaystyle\big\|(u_{k},v_{k})-(u_{*},v_{*})\big\|_{L,\beta,*}\to 0\hskip 10.00002pt\text{as $k\to\infty$}.

Hence, there exists a number
K_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\geq K_{2}
, the second summand in (
5.8
) is smaller than
\varepsilon/2
. In summary, we get

\displaystyle\big\|S^{L_{k}}_{m}(t)(u_{k},v_{k})-S^{0}_{m}(t)(u_{*},v_{*})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}<\varepsilon\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $k\geq K:=\max\{K_{1},K_{2}\}$,}

and since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, this verifies Condition (ii) of Proposition
5.5
.

Eventually, Proposition
5.5
can be applied on the family
\{\mathcal{A}^{L}_{m}\}_{L\geq 0}
which proves the assertion of Theorem
5.4
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

We point out that Proposition
5.5
cannot be used to prove the stability of the global attractor associated with
L=\infty
. This because the semigroups associated with
L<\infty
and the semigroup associated with
L=\infty
would have to be defined on the same domain
X
. However, due to the different mass conservation law of the LW model, its solution operator
S^{0}_{(m_{1},m_{2})}
would have to be defined on the linear subspace

\displaystyle{\mathcal{V}}_{(m_{1},m_{2})}=\big\{(u,v)\in{\mathcal{V}}^{1}\;|\;\langle u\rangle_{\Omega}=m_{1}\;\text{and}\;\langle v\rangle_{\Gamma}=m_{2}\big\}.

This entails that the only reasonable choice for
X
in Proposition
5.5
would be
X:={\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}\cap{\mathcal{V}}_{(m_{1},m_{2})}
. Thus,
X
is nonempty only if
m=\beta m_{1}+m_{2}
. However, even in this case, we cannot ensure that
S^{L}_{m}X\subseteq X
for any
L<\infty
, which means that
S^{L}_{m}
does not define a semigroup on the space
X
. Consequently, Proposition
5.5
is not applicable and thus, the results of Section
5
cannot be transferred to the scenario
L\to\infty
.

In particular, for the aforementioned reasons, we cannot find a compact set
\mathcal{K}_{m}
which acts as an absorbing set the semigroup corresponding to
L=\infty
but also for the semigroups associated with
L<\infty
. (Note that if
\mathcal{K}_{m}
were a compact absorbing set, it also would have to absorb itself.)
As such a set
\mathcal{K}_{m}
will play a crucial role in the subsequent section, it is also not possible to adapt the results of Section
6
to the situation
L\to\infty
.

6
Existence of a robust family of exponential attractors

In this section, for every
L\in[0,1]
, we intend to construct an exponential attractor for the dynamical system
(\mathcal{W}^{1}_{\beta,m},\{S^{L}_{m}\left(t\right)\}_{t\geq 0})
. We further show that the exponential attractor associated with
L=0
is robust against perturbations
L>0
in some certain sense.

We first recall the definition of exponential attractors (see, e.g.,
[
21
, Def. 4.1]
in the discrete case and
[
15
, Def. 7.4.4]
in the continuous case).

Definition 6.1

(Exponential attractors)
.

Let
M
be a metric space, let
I=[0,\infty)
or
I=\mathbb{N}_{0}
, and let

\{S(i)\}_{i\geq 0}
be a semigroup on
M
. Then, a compact set
{\mathfrak{M}}\subset M
is called an exponential attractor for the dynamical system
\big(M,\{S(i)\}_{i\in I}\big)
if the following properties hold:

\mathrm{(i)}

The set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is compact in
M
and has finite fractal dimension, i.e.,

\dim_{\mathrm{frac},M}(\mathfrak{M})<\infty.

\mathrm{(ii)}

The set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is forward invariant under the semigroup
\{S(i)\}_{i\geq 0}
, i.e.,

S(i){{\mathfrak{M}}}\subset{{\mathfrak{M}}}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $i\in I$.}

\mathrm{(iii)}

The set
{\mathfrak{M}}
is an exponentially attracting set for the dynamical system
\big(M,\{S(i)\}_{i\in I}\big)
, i.e., for every bounded subset
B\subset M
, there exist constants
C,a>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{M}\left(S(i)B,{{\mathfrak{M}}}\right)\leq Ce^{-ai}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $i\in I$.}

In the proof of Theorem
5.4
, we obtained the existence of a compact absorbing set
\mathcal{K}_{m}
(that does not depend on
L
) such that for all
L\geq 0
and every bounded set
B\subset{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
there exists a time
t_{0}^{L}(B)\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle S_{m}^{L}(t)B\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}^{L}(B)$.}

As for any
t\geq 0
the state
S_{m}^{L}(t)
depends continuously on the parameter
L
, we infer that also the time
t_{0}^{L}(B)
depends continuously on
L
. We thus obtain

\displaystyle\bigcup_{L\in[0,1]}S_{m}^{L}(t)B\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}(B):=\underset{L\in[0,1]}{\max}\,t_{0}^{L}(B)$.}

It is worth mentioning that the set
\mathcal{K}_{m}
might not be forward invariant under the semigroup
\{S_{m}^{L}(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
for any
L\geq 0
. However, as
\mathcal{K}_{m}
is a bounded absorbing set, it also absorbs itself. Thus, defining
T_{0}:=t_{0}(\mathcal{K}_{m})\geq 1
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\bigcup_{L\in[0,1]}S_{m}^{L}(t)\mathcal{K}_{m}\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\geq T_{0}$.}

(6.1)

The next step is to construct a family
\{\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}\}_{L\in[0,1]}
of exponential attractors for the discrete dynamical systems

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{D_{m}^{L}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\right),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{where}\hskip 10.00002ptD^{L}_{m}(n):=S^{L}_{m}(nT_{0})\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$},

(6.2)

with
L\in[0,1]
.
We further show that the exponential attractor associated with
L=0
is robust against perturbations of the parameter
L
.
Eventually, we will extend these results to the continuous dynamical systems
\big(\mathcal{K}_{m},S_{m}^{L}(t)\big)
with
L\in[0,1]
.

It obviously holds that for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{K}_{m}
,

\displaystyle D^{L}_{m}(n)(u,v)=\big[\underbrace{D^{L}_{m}(1)\circ...\circ D^{L}_{m}(1)}_{\text{$n$ times}}\big](u,v)=:\big[D^{L}_{m}(1)\big]^{n}(u,v).

(6.3)

Therefore, the operator
D^{L}_{m}(1)=S^{L}_{m}(T_{0})
will play a crucial role in the analysis.

6.1
Robust exponential attractors for discrete dynamical systems

To prove the existence of a robust family of exponential attractors for the discrete dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{D_{m}^{L}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}})
we first present a simple generalization of the abstract result
[
21
, Thm. 4.4.]
, which can be established by a rescaling argument.

Lemma 6.2

(Robust exponential attractors for general discrete dynamical systems)
.

Suppose that
X
and
Y
are Banach spaces such that
Y
is compactly embedded in
X
, and let
M
be a bounded subset of
X
. Let
L_{*}>0
be arbitrary. We further assume that there exists a family
\{\mathcal{F}^{L}\}_{L\in[0,L_{*}]}
of operators
\mathcal{F}^{L}:M\to M\cap Y
which satisfies the following assumptions.

\mathrm{(i)}

There exist a constant
\Lambda\geq 0
such that for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in M
and all
L\in[0,L_{*}]
,

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{F}^{L}(x_{1})-\mathcal{F}^{L}(x_{2})\big\|_{Y}\leq\Lambda\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|_{X}\;.

(6.4)

\mathrm{(ii)}

There exists constants
\Theta>0
and
\zeta\in(0,1]
such that for all
x\in M
,
L\in[0,L_{*}]
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\big\|\big[\mathcal{F}^{L}\big]^{n}(x)-\big[\mathcal{F}^{0}\big]^{n}(x)\big\|_{X}\leq\Theta^{n}\,L^{\zeta}\;,

(6.5)

where
\big[\mathcal{F}^{L}\big]^{n}
denotes the
n
-fold composition
\mathcal{F}^{L}\circ...\circ\mathcal{F}^{L}
.

Then, for every
L\in[0,L_{*}]
, there exists an exponential attractor
\mathcal{M}^{L}\subset M
for the discrete semigroup
(M,\{\mathcal{D}^{L}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}})
with
\mathcal{D}^{L}(n):=[\mathcal{F}^{L}]^{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in the sense of Definition
6.1
.

Moreover, these exponential attractors can be chosen in such a way that the following properties hold:

\mathrm{(a)}

There exists constants
C_{1}>0
and
0<\gamma<1
such that for all
L\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{sym},X}(\mathcal{M}^{L},\mathcal{M}^{0})\leq C_{1}L^{\zeta\gamma}.

(6.6)

\mathrm{(b)}

The fractal dimension of
\mathcal{M}^{L}
is uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that for all
L\in[0,L_{0}]
:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{frac},X}(\mathcal{M}^{L})\leq C_{2}.

(6.7)

\mathrm{(c)}

There exist constants
C_{3},A>0
such that for all
L\in[0,L_{*}]
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{X}\big(\mathcal{D}^{L}(n)M\,,\,\mathcal{M}^{L}\big)\leq C_{3}e^{-An},

(6.8)

meaning that the rate of convergence to these attractors is uniform in
L
.

We first assume that (ii) holds with
\zeta=1
. In this case, the assertions of Lemma
6.2
have already been established in
[
21
, Thm. 4.4.]
. We point out that the assertion (c) is not explicitly stated in
[
21
, Thm. 4.4.]
but follows directly from its proof, in which the authors showed that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{X}\big(\mathcal{D}^{L}(n)M\,,\,\mathcal{M}^{L}\big)=\mathrm{dist}_{X}\big([\mathcal{F}^{L}]^{n}M\,,\,\mathcal{M}^{L}\big)\leq 2^{1-n}R\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}$.}

Here,
R>0
denotes the radius of a fixed ball
B_{R}(x_{0})
in
X
with some center
x_{0}\in M
such that
M\subset B_{R}(x_{0})
. Hence, choosing
C_{3}=2R
and
A=\ln(2)
, we obtain (c).

Let us now assume that (ii) holds with
\zeta\in(0,1)
. In this case, the assertions can be established by slightly modifying the proof of
[
21
, Thm. 4.4.]
. Alternatively, the change of variables
L\mapsto K:=L^{\zeta}
could be used to transform back to the already known situation
\zeta=1
.
∎

We now want to apply this abstract result on the discrete dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{D^{L}_{m}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}})
. To this end, we first need a compact Lipschitz estimate that is uniform in
L\in[0,1]
.

Lemma 6.3

(Uniform compact Lipschitz estimate)
.

Suppose that (

A1

)–(

A4

) hold, and let
m\in\mathbb{R}
and
L\in[0,1]
be arbitrary.
Moreover, for any
i\in\{1,2\}
, let
(u_{0,i},v_{0,i})\in{\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
be an arbitrary initial datum,
and let
(u_{i}^{L},v_{i}^{L},\mu_{i}^{L},\theta_{i}^{L})
denote the corresponding weak solution of the system (
3.1
).

Then there exists a positive, non-decreasing function
\Lambda\in C(\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+})
depending only on
\Omega
,
\beta
,
F
and
G
, such that for all
L\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}_{2},v_{2}^{L}\big)(t)-\big(u^{L}_{1},v_{1}^{L}\big)(t)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq\Lambda(t)\big\|(u_{0,2},v_{0,2})-(u_{0,1},v_{0,1})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}\,,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for all $t\geq 1.$}

Recall that the functions
\Lambda_{1}^{*}
in Proposition
3.4
and
\Lambda_{1}^{0}
in Proposition
3.9
are independent of
L
. Along with Lemma
2.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big\|\big(u^{L}_{2},v_{2}^{L}\big)(t)-\big(u^{L}_{1},v_{1}^{L}\big)(t)\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\Lambda^{*}_{1}(t),\Lambda^{0}_{1}(t)\}\,\big\|(u_{0,2},v_{0,2})-(u_{0,1},v_{0,1})\big\|_{L,\beta,*}

\displaystyle\leq C\max\{\Lambda^{*}_{1}(t),\Lambda^{0}_{1}(t)\}\,\big\|(u_{0,2},v_{0,2})-(u_{0,1},v_{0,1})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}},

for all
L\in[0,1]
and all
t\geq 1
. This directly proves the claim.
∎

Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
6.3
can now be used to establish the following result.

Corollary 6.4

(Robust exponential attractors for
(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{D^{L}_{m}(n)\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}})
)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A4

) are satisfied and let
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.

Then, for every
L\in[0,1]
, there exists an exponential attractor
{\mathfrak{M}}^{L}_{D}\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}
for the dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}_{m},D_{m}^{L}(n))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in the sense of Definition
6.1
, where
M=\mathcal{K}_{m}
is to be understood as a metric subspace of
X=(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}
.

Moreover, these exponential attractors can be chosen in such a way that the following properties hold:

\mathrm{(a)}

The fractal dimension of
\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}
is uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
c_{1}\geq 0
such that for all
L\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{frac},{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}}(\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D})\leq c_{1}.

(6.9)

\mathrm{(b)}

There exist constants
c_{2}>0
and
0<\gamma<1
such that for all
L\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{sym},{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}}(\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D},\mathfrak{M}^{0}_{D})\leq c_{2}L^{\gamma/4}.

(6.10)

\mathrm{(c)}

There exist constants
c_{3},a>0
such that for all
L\in[0,1]
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}}\big(D^{L}(n)\mathcal{K}_{m}\,,\,\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}\big)\leq c_{3}e^{-an},

(6.11)

meaning that the rate of convergence to these attractors is uniform in
L
.

To prove the assertion we intend to apply Lemma
6.2
with
Y={\mathcal{W}_{\beta,m}^{1}}
,
X=(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}
,
M=\mathcal{K}_{m}
,
\mathcal{F}^{L}=D^{L}_{m}(1)
and
\mathcal{D}^{L}=D^{L}_{m}
. This means that only the conditions (i) and (ii) of Lemma
6.2
need to be verified.

Condition (i):

We first recall that
D^{L}_{m}(1)=S^{L}_{m}(T_{0})
with
T_{0}\geq 1
as constructed in (
6.1
). Hence, Lemma
6.3
implies that

\displaystyle\big\|D^{L}_{m}(1)(u_{2},v_{2})-D^{L}_{m}(1)(u_{1},v_{1})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda(T_{0})\big\|(u_{2},v_{2})-(u_{1},v_{1})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}

for all
L\in[0,1]
.
This verifies condition (i) of Lemma
6.2
.

Condition (ii):

Furthermore, using the continuous embedding
\mathcal{L}^{2}\hookrightarrow(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}
and applying Lemma
5.2
with
T_{*}=nT_{0}
, we infer that for all
L\in[0,1]
and
(u,v)\in\mathcal{K}_{m}
,

\displaystyle\big\|D^{L}_{m}(n)(u,v)-D^{0}_{m}(n)(u,v)\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}\leq C\big\|D^{L}_{m}(n)(u,v)-D^{0}_{m}(n)(u,v)\big\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq Ce^{CnT_{0}}\,L^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq\big[(C+1)e^{CT_{0}}\big]^{n}\,L^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Here,
C
is a positive constant independent of
L
.
Hence, condition (ii) of Lemma
6.2
with
\zeta=1/4
and
\Theta=(C+1)e^{CT_{0}}
is fulfilled.

This allows us to apply Lemma
6.2
as described above which directly proves the assertions and thus, the proof is complete.
∎

6.2
Robust exponential attractors for the continuous dynamical system

We are now ready to present the main result of this section, which is the existence of a robust family of exponential attractors for the continuous dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{S^{L}_{m}(t)\}_{t\geq 0})
.

Theorem 6.5

(Robust exponential attractors for
(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{S^{L}_{m}(t)\}_{t\geq 0})
)
.

Suppose that the assumptions (

A1

)-(

A4

) are satisfied, and let
m\in\mathbb{R}
be arbitrary.

Then, for every
L\in[0,1]
, there exists an exponential attractor
{\mathfrak{M}}^{L}\subset\mathcal{K}_{m}
for the dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}_{m},\{S^{L}_{m}(t)\}_{t\geq 0})
in the sense of Definition
6.1
, where
M=\mathcal{K}_{m}
is to be understood as a metric subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
.

Moreover, these exponential attractors can be chosen in such a way that the following properties hold:

\mathrm{(a)}

The fractal dimension of
\mathfrak{M}^{L}
is uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists a constant
C_{1}\geq 0
such that for all
L\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{frac},\mathcal{H}^{1}}(\mathfrak{M}^{L})\leq C_{1}.

(6.12)

\mathrm{(b)}

There exist constants
C_{2}>0
and
0<\gamma<1
such that for all
L\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{sym},\mathcal{H}^{1}}(\mathfrak{M}^{L},\mathfrak{M}^{0})\leq C_{2}\big(L^{1/6}+L^{\gamma/4}).

(6.13)

\mathrm{(c)}

There exists constants
C_{3},\alpha>0
such that for all
L\in[0,1]
and all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\big(S^{L}_{m}(t)\mathcal{K}_{m},\mathfrak{M}^{L}\big)\leq C_{3}\,e^{-\alpha t},

(6.14)

meaning that the rate of convergence to these attractors is uniform in
L
.

To establish the assertion, we proceed similarly as in the proof of
[
21
, Thm. 5.1]
(which in turn is based on the proof of the general result
[
20
, Thm. 3.1]
).
Let
T_{0}\geq 1
denote the number introduced in (
6.1
).
Since
\mathcal{K}_{m}
is a compact subset of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
, we can find a radius
K>0
(independent of
L
) such that
\|(u,v)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\leq K
for all
(u,v)\in\mathcal{K}_{m}
.
Let now
C
denote a generic positive constant, depending only on
T_{0}
and
K
that may change its value from line to line.
For any
L\in[0,1]
, we set

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}^{L}:=\bigcup_{t\in[T_{0},2T_{0}]}S_{m}^{L}(t)\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}.

(6.15)

We now intend to show that
\{\mathfrak{M}^{L}\}_{L\in[0,1]}
is a family of exponential attractors with respect to the
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-metric which satisfies the assertions (a), (b) and (c).

Invoking the compact Lipschitz estimate from Lemma
6.3
, we infer that
\mathfrak{M}^{L}
is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{1}
for every
L\in[0,1]
.
We next show that the fractal dimension of
\mathfrak{M}^{L}
is bounded uniformly in
L
.
To this end, we consider the mapping

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{L}:[T_{0},2T_{0}]\times\mathcal{K}_{m}\to\mathcal{K}_{m},\hskip 10.00002pt(t,u,v)\mapsto S^{L}(t)(u,v).

It obviously holds that
\mathfrak{M}^{L}=\mathcal{F}^{L}([T_{0},2T_{0}]\times\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D})
.
Hence, invoking Proposition
3.4
, Proposition
3.5
, Proposition
3.9
, Proposition
3.10
and Lemma
6.3
, we infer that there exists some constant
h\geq 0
depending only on
T_{0}
and
K
, such that

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{F}^{L}(t_{1},u_{1},v_{1})-\mathcal{F}^{L}(t_{2},u_{2},v_{2})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}=\big\|S_{m}^{L}(t_{1})(u_{1},v_{1})-S_{m}^{L}(t_{2})(u_{2},v_{2})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\big(\big\|(u_{1},v_{1})-(u_{1},v_{2})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}+\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|^{\frac{3}{16}}\big)\leq h\big\|(t_{1},u_{1},v_{1})-(t_{2},u_{1},v_{2})\big\|_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{H}^{1}}^{\frac{3}{16}}

for all
t_{1},t_{2}\in[T_{0},2T_{0}]
and all
(u_{1},v_{1}),(u_{2},v_{2})\in\mathcal{K}_{m}
, where
\mathbb{R}
is endowed with the Euclidean norm. This means that
\mathcal{F}^{L}
is Hölder continuous with exponent
3/16
and Hölder constant
h
.

Note that
hr^{3/16}\leq r^{1/8}
if
r>0
is sufficiently small. Hence, recalling the definition of the fractal dimension (see (
2.3
)), we obtain

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathrm{frac},\mathcal{H}^{1}}\big(\mathfrak{M}^{L}\big)=\dim_{\mathrm{frac},\mathcal{H}^{1}}\big(\mathcal{F}^{L}([T_{0},2T_{0}]\times\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D})\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=\underset{r\to 0}{\lim\sup}\;\frac{\mathcal{N}_{hr^{3/16}}\big(\mathcal{F}^{L}([T_{0},2T_{0}]\times\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D})\,;\,\mathcal{H}^{1}\big)}{-\ln\big(h\,r^{3/16}\big)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\underset{r\to 0}{\lim\sup}\;\frac{\mathcal{N}_{r}\big([T_{0},2T_{0}]\times\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}\,;\,\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{H}^{1}\big)}{-\ln\big(r^{1/8}\big)}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=8\dim_{\mathrm{frac},\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{H}^{1}}\big([T_{0},2T_{0}]\times\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq 8\big(\dim_{\mathrm{frac},\mathcal{H}^{1}}\big(\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}\big)+1\big)\leq 8\big(c_{1}+1\big),

where
c_{1}>0
is the constant from Corollary
6.4
(a). This verifies (a).

Proceeding as in the proof of
[
20
, Thm. 3.1]
, it is straightforward to check that the set
\mathfrak{M}^{L}
is forward invariant, i.e.,
S^{L}(t)\mathfrak{M}^{L}\subset\mathfrak{M}^{L}
for all
t\geq 0
.

Let now
L\in(0,1]
,
t>0
and
(u,v)\in\mathcal{K}_{m}
be arbitrary. Without loss of generality, we assume that
t\geq T_{0}
. Then there exist
s\in[T_{0},2T_{0})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
t=nT_{0}+s
.
Due to Corollary
6.4
, and since
\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}
is compact in
(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}
, we can further find
(\bar{u},\bar{v})\in\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}
such that

\displaystyle\big\|D^{L}(n)(u,v)-(\bar{u},\bar{v})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}\leq c_{3}e^{-an}.

Then, recalling
1\leq T_{0}\leq s<2T_{0}
and
n=(t-s)/T_{0}
, we can use the compact Lipschitz estimate from Lemma
6.3
to conclude that

\displaystyle\big\|S^{L}_{t}(u,v)-S^{L}_{s}(\bar{u},\bar{v})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}=\big\|S^{L}_{s}D^{L}(n)(u,v)-S^{L}_{s}(\bar{u},\bar{v})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq C\big\|D^{L}(n)(u,v)-(\bar{u},\bar{v})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}\leq Ce^{-an}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt=Ce^{-at/T_{0}}e^{as/T_{0}}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}

with
\alpha:=a/T_{0}
. Since
S_{s}^{L}(\bar{u},\bar{v})\in\mathfrak{M}^{L}
, this implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{X}\big(S^{L}_{m}(t)\mathcal{K}_{m},\mathfrak{M}^{L}\big)\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}

which proves (c). In particular, we have thus shown that for all
L\in[0,1]
,
\mathfrak{M}^{L}
is indeed an exponential attractor for the dynamical system
(\mathcal{K}_{m},S_{m}^{L}(t))
with respect to the
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-metric.

It remains to prove (b). Therefore, let
L\in(0,1]
and
(u_{*},v_{*})\in\mathfrak{M}^{L}
be arbitrary. Hence, there exist
t\in[T_{0},2T_{0}]
and
(u_{0},v_{0})\in\mathfrak{M}^{L}_{D}
such that
(u_{*},v_{*})=S^{L}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})
. According to Corollary
6.4
(b), there exists
(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{v}_{0})\in\mathfrak{M}^{0}_{D}
such that

\displaystyle\big\|(u_{0},v_{0})-(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{v}_{0})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}\leq c_{2}L^{\gamma/4}.

We now set
(\bar{u}_{*},\bar{v}_{*}):=S^{0}(t)(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{v}_{0})\in\mathfrak{M}^{0}
.
Hence, using the Hölder estimate from Proposition
3.9
, the convergence estimate from Corollary
5.3
, and Lemma
2.2
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\big\|(u_{*},v_{*})-(\bar{u}_{*},\bar{v}_{*})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}=\big\|S^{L}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})-S^{0}(t)(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{v}_{0})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\big\|S^{L}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})-S^{0}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}+\big\|S^{0}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})-S^{0}(t)(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{v}_{0})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq\big\|S^{L}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})-S^{0}(t)(u_{0},v_{0})\big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}+C\big\|(u_{0},v_{0})-(\bar{u}_{0},\bar{v}_{0})\big\|_{(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\leq CL^{1/6}+CL^{\gamma/4}.

This directly implies

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}\big(\mathfrak{M}^{L},\mathfrak{M}^{0}\big)\leq C\big(L^{1/6}+L^{\gamma/4}\big).

Proceeding analogously, we derive the same estimate for
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}(\mathfrak{M}^{0},\mathfrak{M}^{L})
. Combining both estimates, we obtain (b).
Thus, the proof is complete.
∎
