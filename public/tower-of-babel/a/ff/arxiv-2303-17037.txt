1.
Introduction

Let
f(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}z^{-k}
be a convergent power series. The
n
-th diagonal Padé approximant of
f(z)
is a rational function
[n/n]_{f}(z)=P_{n}(z)/Q_{n}(z)
, where
\deg(P_{n}),\deg(Q_{n})\leq n
and
Q_{n}(z)
is not identically zero, such that the linearized error function
R_{n}(z)
satisfies

(1.1)

R_{n}(z):=(Q_{n}f-P_{n})(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-(n+1)}\right)

as
z\to\infty
. One can readily check that the above condition is nothing but a system of linear equations on the coefficients of the polynomials of
P_{n}(z),Q_{n}(z)
that always has a non-trivial solution. This system is not necessarily uniquely solvable, but it is known that the rational function
[n/n]_{f}(z)
is indeed unique. Hereafter, we shall understand that
Q_{n}(z)
in (
1.1
) is monic and of minimal possible degree, which does make it unique.

Our goal is to understand convergence properties of
\smash{[n/n]_{f}(z)}
. It was shown by Herbert Stahl
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
that if
f(z)
can be meromorphically continued along any path
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus A
for a polar set
A
and there exists a point in
\mathbb{C}\setminus A
with at least two distinct continuations, then there exists a compact set
\Delta_{f}
such that
f(z)
has a single-valued meromorphic continuation into the complement of
\Delta_{f}
(which is a branch cut for
f(z)
) and the diagonal Padé approximants converge to this continuation in logarithmic capacity. The set
\Delta_{f}
is uniquely characterized as the branch cut of smallest logarithmic capacity that is set-theoretically minimal (if
K
is a branch cut with the same logarithmic capacity, then
\Delta_{f}\subseteq K
).

The minimal capacity contour
\Delta_{f}
can also be characterized from the point of view of quadratic differentials. Assume for simplicity that
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
,
m\geq 2
, is a finite set. Suppose that every element of
A
is a branch point of
f(z)
. Then there exist auxiliary points
b_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,m-2\}
, sometimes called Chebotarëv centers
1

Suppose that all the points

are disjoint from

\Delta_{f}
(a generic situation). Assume further that

has either logarithmic or power branching at each

(i.e., behaves like
\log(z-a_{i})
or
(z-a_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}
,

). Then the strong asymptotics of the corresponding diagonal Padé approximants was investigated by Aptekarev and the second author in

. Our overarching goal is to remove all the assumptions on contours

corresponding to finite sets

around the auxiliary points

In this work, we shall consider the following class of functions. Orient each arc
\Delta_{i}
towards
a_{0}

a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}

Definition
.

We are interested in functions
f(z)
of the form

(1.4)

f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Delta}\frac{\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s}{s-z},

where there exist exponents
\alpha_{i}>-1
,
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, and branches of
(z-a_{i})^{\alpha_{i}}
holomorphic across
\Delta\setminus\{a_{i}\}
for which the restriction
\rho_{i}(s)
of
\rho(s)
to
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}:=\Delta_{i}\setminus\{a_{0},a_{i}\}
is such that
\rho_{i}(s)(s-a_{0})^{-\alpha_{0}}(s-a_{i})^{-\alpha_{i}}
extends to a holomorphic and non-vanishing function in some neighborhood of
\Delta_{i}
.

Notice that
\rho(s)
is not defined at
a_{0}
even if
\alpha_{0}=0
. However, in the latter case the values
\rho_{i}(a_{0})
are well-defined. It was assumed in
[
2
]
that
\rho_{1}(z)+\rho_{2}(z)+\rho_{3}(z)\equiv 0
in some neighborhood of
a_{0}
(no branching assumption). No such supposition is made here, neither in some neighborhood of
a_{0}
nor at
a_{0}
itself. The main advantage of the class of functions introduced in (
1.4
) is that the denominator polynomials
Q_{n}(z)
can be equivalently characterized as non-Hermitian orthogonal polynomials satisfying

(1.5)

\int_{\Delta}s^{k}Q_{n}(s)\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s=0,\quad k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}.

This paper is organized as follows. The next section is devoted to the description of the main term of the asymptotics of the polynomials

3

5

6

Q_{n}(z)
around
a_{0}
is described by certain special functions that come from a
2\times 2
matrix function solving the so-called Painvlevé XXXIV Riemann-Hilbert problem with Stokes parameters that depend on the weight
\rho(s)
in a transcendental way. This connection is described in Appendices
A
and
B

C

\Delta

Q_{n}(z)
can be very different for certain classes of weights from the rest of the cases.

2.
Nuttall-Szegő Functions

Similarly to orthogonal polynomials on an interval, the asymptotics of the polynomials
Q_{n}(z)
is described by the term that captures the geometric rate of their growth, see (
2.4
), and a Szegő function of the weight
\rho(s)
, see (
2.11
). Both of these functions naturally live on a genus one Riemann surface associated with the contour
\Delta
, see (
2.1
). However, the nature of the theory of functions on a Riemann surface leads to the introduction of the third term, in fact, a normal family of functions that depends on
n
, which are essentially ratios of the Riemann theta functions, see (
2.17
) (the necessity of these terms was already demonstrated by Akhiezer
[
1
]
).

\mathfrak{S}

2.1.
Riemann surface

Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a genus one two-sheeted Riemann surface defined by

(2.1)

\mathfrak{S}:=\left\{{\boldsymbol{z}}=(z,w):~w^{2}=(z-a_{0})(z-a_{1})(z-a_{2})(z-a_{3})\right\}.

We realize
\mathfrak{S}
as a ramified cover of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}
constructed in the following manner. Two copies of the extended complex plane are cut along each arc
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}
. These copies are glued together along the cuts in such a manner that the right (resp. left) side of the arc
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}
belonging to one copy is joined with the left (resp. right) side of the same arc
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}
only belonging to the other copy. Let

\pi,w:\mathfrak{S}\to\overline{\mathbb{C}},\quad\pi({\boldsymbol{z}})\mapsto z\quad\text{and}\quad w({\boldsymbol{z}})\mapsto w.

We call
\pi
the canonical projection and label the sheets
\mathfrak{S}^{(0)}
and
\mathfrak{S}^{(1)}
(cut copies of the extended complex plane) so that
w({\boldsymbol{z}})=w
behaves like
z^{2}
as
{\boldsymbol{z}}\to\infty^{(0)}
. In our definition, the sheets are topologically closed and their intersection is equal to
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, where

\boldsymbol{\Delta}:=\pi^{-1}(\Delta),\quad\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}:=\pi^{-1}(\Delta_{i}),\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{A}:=\{\boldsymbol{a}_{0},\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\boldsymbol{a}_{2},\boldsymbol{a}_{3}\},\quad\pi(\boldsymbol{a}_{l})=a_{l},

i\in\{1,2,3\}
and
l\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. Thus,
\boldsymbol{A}
is the set of the ramification points of
\mathfrak{S}
and
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
is a union of three simple cycles that all intersect each other at
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}
. We orient each
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}
so that
\mathfrak{S}^{(0)}
remains on the left when
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}
is traversed in the positive direction. We write

z^{(k)}:=\pi^{-1}(z)\cap\mathfrak{S}^{(k)},\quad z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta,

where
k\in\{0,1\}
, and use bold lower case letters such as
{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{t}},{\boldsymbol{s}}
to indicate generic points on
\mathfrak{S}
with canonical projections
z,t,s
. We designate the symbol
\cdot^{*}
to stand for the conformal involution that sends
z^{(k)}
into
z^{(1-k)}
,
k\in\{0,1\}
(we then extend this notion to
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
by continuity). Since
\mathfrak{S}
has genus
1
, we need to select a homology basis. We choose cycles
\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}
to be involution-symmetric, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{s}}^{*}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, and such that
\pi(\boldsymbol{\alpha}),\pi(\boldsymbol{\beta})
are smooth Jordan arcs joining
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{1},a_{3}
respectively, while
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is oriented towards
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
away from
\boldsymbol{a}_{1}
in
\mathfrak{S}^{(0)}
, see Figures
1
and
2
. In what follows it will be sometimes convenient to set

\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}:=\mathfrak{S}\setminus\boldsymbol{\alpha}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}:=\mathfrak{S}\setminus(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cup\boldsymbol{\beta}).

Finally, given a function
F({\boldsymbol{z}})
defined on
\mathfrak{S}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, or on its subset, we shall set
F(z):=F(z^{(0)})
and
F^{*}(z):=F(z^{(1)})
. That is, with a slight abuse of notation we use the same letter for a function on the top sheet as well as its pull-back to the complex plane while we use the involution symbol
∗
to mark the pull-back to the complex plane from the bottom sheet. In particular,

(2.2)

w(z)=\sqrt{(z-a_{0})(z-a_{1})(z-a_{2})(z-a_{3})},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta,

is the branch such that
w(z)=z^{2}+\mathcal{O}(z)
as
z\to\infty
and
w^{*}(z)=-w(z)
.

As it happens, the asymptotics of the polynomials
Q_{n}(z)
as well as of the linearized error functions
R_{n}(z)
, see (
1.1
), is described by the solution of the following boundary value problem on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
.

Boundary Value Problem: BVP-
\Psi
.

Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and a function
\rho(s)
on
\Delta
as described after (
1.4
), find a function
\Psi({\boldsymbol{z}})
that is holomorphic in
\mathfrak{S}\setminus\big(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cup\big\{\infty^{(0)}\big\}\big)
, has a pole of order
n
at
\infty^{(0)}
and a zero of order at least
n-1
at
\infty^{(1)}
, and whose traces on
\boldsymbol{\Delta}
are continuous away from
\boldsymbol{A}
and satisfy

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\Psi_{+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\Psi_{-}({\boldsymbol{s}})/(\rho w_{+})(s),\quad{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta},\\
\big|\Psi(z)\big|=\mathcal{O}\left(|z-a_{l}|^{-(2\alpha_{l}+1)/4}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad z\to a_{l},\;\;l\in\{0,1,2,3\}.\end{array}\right.

The solution of this boundary value problem is given in Theorem
1
further below. It is rather explicit and constructed as a product of three terms that are introduced in the following three subsections.

2.2.
Geometric Term

Let

(2.4)

\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\exp\left\{\int_{\boldsymbol{a}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{z}}G\right\},\quad G({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\frac{(z-a_{0})\mathrm{d}z}{w({\boldsymbol{z}})},

for
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, where the path of integration lies entirely in
\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
and
G({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a meromorphic differential on
\mathfrak{S}
having two simple poles at
\infty^{(1)}
and
\infty^{(0)}
with respective residues
1
and
-1
, whose period over any cycle on
\mathfrak{S}
is purely imaginary (the latter claim follows from (
1.2
)). The function
\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})
is holomorphic and non-vanishing on
\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
except for a simple pole at
\infty^{(0)}
and a simple zero at
\infty^{(1)}
. Define (real) constants

(2.5)

\omega:=-\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}G\quad\text{and}\quad\tau:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}G.

One can readily check that
\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})
possesses continuous traces on both sides of each cycle of the canonical basis (in fact, it extends to a multiplicatively multi-valued function on the entire surface) that satisfy

(2.6)

\Phi_{+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\Phi_{-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\omega},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\},\\
e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}\tau},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\beta}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\}.\end{array}\right.

Since
w(z)=-w^{*}(z)
, it follows from (
2.4
) that
\Phi(z)\Phi^{*}(z)\equiv 1
. Moreover, since the constants in (
2.5
) are real, it also holds that
\log|\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})|
is harmonic on
\mathfrak{S}\setminus\{\infty^{(0)},\infty^{(1)}\}
. Furthermore, since
\Delta
is the set of critical trajectories of the quadratic differential
-(z-a_{0})^{2}w^{-2}(z)\mathrm{d}z^{2}
and the domain
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
contains a single critical point of this differential, namely, double pole at infinity, it follows from Basic Structure Theorem
[
16
, Theorem 3.5]
that
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
is a circle domain for this differential, see
[
16
, Definition 3.9]
. In particular, it must be true that

(2.7)

-\log|\Phi^{*}(z)|=\log|\Phi(z)|>0,\quad z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta.

In particular, this means that
\log|\Phi(s)|\equiv 0
,
s\in\Delta
, and that
\log|\Phi(z)|
is the Green function for
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta
with pole at infinity.

2.3.
Szegő Function

Since
\mathfrak{S}
has genus one, it has one (up to a constant factor) holomorphic differential. Since
\mathfrak{S}
is a two-sheeted surface, it is a standard fact of the theory of Riemann surfaces that

(2.8)

\Omega({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\left(\oint_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{w({\boldsymbol{s}})}\right)^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{w({\boldsymbol{z}})},\quad\mathsf{B}:=\oint_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Omega,

is the holomorphic differential on
\mathfrak{S}
normalized to have unit period on the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
cycle of the homology basis (it was shown by Riemann that the constant
\mathsf{B}
has positive imaginary part). In particular, the function

(2.9)

a({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\int_{\boldsymbol{a}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{z}}\Omega,\quad{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}},

where the path of integration lies entirely in
\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, extends to additively multi-valued analytic function on the entire surface
\mathfrak{S}
. Denote further by
\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{s}})
the meromorphic differential with two simple poles at
{\boldsymbol{z}}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}
with respective residues
1
and
-1
normalized to have a zero period on the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-cycle. When
{\boldsymbol{z}}
does not lie on top of the point at infinity, it can be readily verified that

(2.10)

\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{s}})=\dfrac{w(\boldsymbol{z})}{s-z}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}s}{w({\boldsymbol{s}})}-\left(\oint_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\dfrac{w({\boldsymbol{z}})}{t-z}\dfrac{\mathrm{d}t}{w({\boldsymbol{t}})}\right)\Omega({\boldsymbol{s}}).

The third kind differential
\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{s}})
can be thought of as a discontinuous Cauchy kernel on
\mathfrak{S}
, see
[
28
]
. The following proposition elaborates this point.

Proposition 1
.

For a function
\rho(s)
as described after (
1.4
) fix a branch of
\log(\rho_{i}w_{+})(s)
that is continuous on
\Delta_{i}^{\circ}
,
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Put

(2.11)

S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\exp\left\{-\dfrac{1}{4\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\log(\rho w_{+})(s)\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{s}})\right\}.

Then
S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is a holomorphic and non-vanishing function in
\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}
with continuous traces on
(\boldsymbol{\Delta}\cup\boldsymbol{\alpha})\setminus\boldsymbol{A}
that satisfy

(2.12)

S_{\rho+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=S_{\rho-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\exp\big\{2\pi\mathrm{i}c_{\rho}\big\},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}\setminus\boldsymbol{A},\\
1/(\rho w_{+})(s),&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\Delta}\setminus\boldsymbol{A},\end{array}\right.

where the constant
c_{\rho}
is given by

(2.13)

c_{\rho}:=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}}\log(\rho w_{+})\Omega.

It also holds that
S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}})S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}}^{*})\equiv 1
,
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\setminus\boldsymbol{\Delta}
, and

(2.14)

\big|S_{\rho}(z)\big|\sim|z-a_{l}|^{-(2\alpha_{l}+1)/4}\quad\text{as}\quad z\to a_{l},\quad l\in\{0,1,2,3\}.

We call
S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}})
the Szegő function of the weight
\rho(s)
. The Cauchy kernel
\Omega_{{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{z}}^{*}}({\boldsymbol{s}})
is called discontinuous due to the presence of the jump of
S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}})
across
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. We prove Proposition
1
in Section
4.1
.

2.4.
Adjustment Terms

Due to the presence of the jumps of both
\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})
and
S_{\rho}({\boldsymbol{z}})
across the cycles of the homology basis, we need to introduce an adjustment term that will cancel out these jumps. To this end, let

\theta(u):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\big\{\pi\mathrm{i}\mathsf{B}n^{2}+2\pi\mathrm{i}nu\big\},\quad u\in\mathbb{C},

be the Riemann theta function associated with
\mathsf{B}
. Further, let
\mathsf{Jac}(\mathfrak{S}):=\mathbb{C}/\{\mathbb{Z}+\mathsf{B}\mathbb{Z}\}
be the Jacobi variety of
\mathfrak{S}
, where
\mathsf{B}
is given by (
2.8
). We shall represent elements of
\mathsf{Jac}(\mathfrak{S})
as equivalence classes
[s\,]=\{s+l+\mathsf{B}m:l,m\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, where
s\in\mathbb{C}
. It is known that Abel’s map

(2.15)

{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathfrak{S}\mapsto\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})\in\mathsf{Jac}(\mathfrak{S}),\quad\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\left[\int_{\boldsymbol{a}_{0}}^{\boldsymbol{z}}\Omega\right],

is a bijection (clearly, it holds that
[a({\boldsymbol{z}})]=\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{z}})
for
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, see (
2.9
)). Inverting Abel’s map (
2.15
) is known as the Jacobi inversion problem, see
[
7
, Section III.6]
. Since
\mathfrak{S}
has genus one, every Jacobi inversion problem has a unique solution.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\omega,\tau
and
c_{\rho}
be as in (
2.5
) and (
2.13
), respectively. Set

(2.16)

x_{n}:=n\omega-\lfloor n\omega\rfloor,\quad y_{n}:=n\tau-\lfloor n\tau\rfloor,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathsf{A}:=(1+\omega)/2+\mathsf{B}(1+\tau)/2,

where
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor
is equal to the largest integer smaller or equal to its argument. Define

(2.17)

T_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=\exp\big\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}y_{n}a({\boldsymbol{z}})\big\}\dfrac{\theta\big(a({\boldsymbol{z}})-c_{\rho}-x_{n}-\mathsf{B}y_{n}+\mathsf{A}\big)}{\theta\big(a({\boldsymbol{z}})+\mathsf{A}\big)},\quad{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Each function
T_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
is meromorphic in the domain of its definition with continuous traces on
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cup\boldsymbol{\beta})\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\}
that satisfy

(2.18)

T_{n+}({\boldsymbol{s}})=T_{n-}({\boldsymbol{s}})\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\exp\big\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}\big(c_{\rho}+n\omega\big)\big\},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\alpha}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\},\\
\displaystyle\exp\big\{-2\pi\mathrm{i}n\tau\big\},&{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\boldsymbol{\beta}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{a}_{1}\}.\end{array}\right.

In fact,
T_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}})
can be holomorphically continued across each of these cycles. It has precisely one pole, namely
\infty^{(1)}
, and exactly one zero, namely

2.6.
Local Obstruction

To prove our main results on asymptotic behavior of orthogonal polynomials we utilize matrix Riemann-Hilbert analysis. As typical for such an approach, we need to construct a local parametrix around

that models the behavior of the polynomials and their functions of the second kind there. In our case, this is done with the help of the matrix that solves Riemann-Hilbert problems that characterizes solutions of Painlevé XXXIV equation with the Painlevé variable equal to zero. It is known that such a matrix does not exist when the corresponding Painlevé function has a pole at zero. In this case we need to modify our construction and this modification requires placing an additional requirement on the sequence of allowable indices. More precisely, let us write

and

and lying in the sector between
\Delta_{1}
and
\Delta_{3}
. Notice that the coefficients
t_{l}({\boldsymbol{x}})
are continuous functions of
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
(the change of
x({\boldsymbol{x}})
by an integer does not affect
T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}})
) that continuously extend to both sides of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.

Recall (
2.9
). Given
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathfrak{S}
, let
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\pm 1}\in\mathfrak{S}
be the unique points such that

(4.16)

\mathfrak{a}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\pm 1})=\left[a({\boldsymbol{x}})\pm\big(\omega+\mathsf{B}\tau\big)\right].

Clearly,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1}
continuously depend on
{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Notice that
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})_{-1}={\boldsymbol{x}}
and that
({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n})_{\pm 1}={\boldsymbol{z}}_{n\pm 1}
. Define

\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\begin{pmatrix}2t_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}})-h_{1}t_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}})&2t_{2}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1})-h_{1}t_{1}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1})\vskip 6.0pt plus 2.0pt minus 2.0pt\\
t_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})&t_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1})\end{pmatrix},

where the constant
h_{1}
is the same as in (
2.29
), whatever it might be. Consider the function

d({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\left|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}))\right|+\left|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}))\right|.

As we have explained, this is a continuous function of
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
that continuously extends to both sides of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
. Our next goal is to prove that
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{a}_{0}
is the only possible zero of
d({\boldsymbol{x}})
(it might happen that this function does not vanish at all).

Assume that
d({\boldsymbol{x}})=0
, in which case
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}))=\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}))=0
. This means that rows of
\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})
are linearly dependent and since these matrices share a column, there exists a constant
c
such that

2t_{2}(\boldsymbol{y})-h_{1}t_{1}(\boldsymbol{y})+ct_{0}(\boldsymbol{y})=0,\quad\boldsymbol{y}\in\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\}.

Hence, the matrix
[t_{l}(\boldsymbol{y})]
has zero determinant, where
l
is the column index and
\boldsymbol{y}\in\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1},{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}\}
is the row one. By taking linear combinations of rows rather than columns, we get that there exist constants
c_{-1},c_{0},c_{1}
, not all equal to zero, such that

(4.17)

c_{-1}t_{l}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1})+c_{0}t_{l}({\boldsymbol{x}})+c_{1}t_{l}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})=0,\quad l\in\{0,1,2\}.

In another connection, since the integrand in (
2.4
) behaves like
\sqrt{z-a_{0}}
around
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}
, it holds that

(4.18)

\Phi(z)=1+\mathcal{O}\left((z-a_{0})^{3/2}\right)

as
z\to a_{0}
. Consider the function

\displaystyle F({\boldsymbol{z}})

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle c_{-1}\frac{T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1})}{\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}})}+c_{0}+c_{1}\frac{\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})}{T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{-1}T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-1})+c_{0}\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}})+c_{1}\Phi^{2}({\boldsymbol{z}})T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1})}{\Phi({\boldsymbol{z}})T({\boldsymbol{z}};{\boldsymbol{x}})}.

It follows from (
2.6
), (
4.15
), and (
4.16
) that this is a rational function on the entire surface
\mathfrak{S}
. Moreover, the first representation readily yields that
F({\boldsymbol{z}})
has exactly three poles, all simple, at
{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
\infty^{(0)}
, and
\infty^{(1)}
, while the second formula together with (
4.17
) and (
4.18
) clearly shows that it has at least a triple zero at
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}
(unless
{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{a}_{0}
, in which case it is at least a double zero). Since
F({\boldsymbol{z}})
must have equal number of poles and zeros, its zero/pole divisor is equal to

3\boldsymbol{a}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}-\infty^{(0)}-\infty^{(1)}.

However, this means that the function
F({\boldsymbol{z}})/(z-a_{0})
has a zero/pole divisor
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Since there are no rational functions with at most one pole besides constants,
F({\boldsymbol{z}})=C(z-a_{0})
for some constant
C
and it must hold that
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}={\boldsymbol{x}}
as claimed.

Now, it follows from (
2.29
) and (
4.14
) that

\big|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}_{n})\big|=Y_{n-1}Y_{n}\big|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n}))\big|,\quad Y_{n}=\begin{cases}1,&y({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n})=y_{n},\\
e^{\pi\mathsf{Im}((2-2y_{n}+\tau)\mathsf{B}-2c_{\rho})},&y({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n})=y_{n}-1.\end{cases}

Since
\mathsf{Im}(\mathsf{B})>0
,
\tau\in(-1,0)
, and
y_{n}\in[0,1)
, we have that
Y_{n}\geq Y:=e^{-\pi\mathsf{Im}(2c_{\rho}+\mathsf{B})}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
\boldsymbol{V}_{\varepsilon}:=d^{-1}([0,2Y^{-2}\varepsilon))
. Then for all
\varepsilon
small enough this set is either empty (when
d({\boldsymbol{x}})
does not vanish) or it is a neighborhood of
\boldsymbol{a}_{0}
by continuity of
d({\boldsymbol{x}})
. Then if
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n}\notin\boldsymbol{V}_{\varepsilon}
, it holds that

|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}_{n})|+|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}_{n+1})|\geq Y^{2}d({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n})\geq 2\varepsilon,

which yields that
|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}_{m})|\geq|\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{T}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}))|\geq\varepsilon
for either
m=n
or
m=n+1
.

5.1.

Recall the definition of

in (

) as well as the definition of

in (

). It follows from the known behavior of Cauchy integrals around the endpoints of arcs of integration

(we can consider

\log|z|,&\alpha=0,\\

(it was pointed out in

]

(5.1)

\deg(Q_{n})=n\quad\text{and}\quad R_{n-1}(z)=k_{n}^{-1}z^{-n}+\mathcal{O}\left(z^{-n-1}\right),

Then
\boldsymbol{Y}(z)
solves the following Riemann-Hilbert problem (RHP-
{\boldsymbol{Y}}
):

(a)

\boldsymbol{Y}(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta
and
\displaystyle\lim_{z\to\infty}\boldsymbol{Y}(z)z^{-n\sigma_{3}}=\boldsymbol{I}
, where

(b)

,

, that satisfy

\boldsymbol{Y}_{+}(s)=\boldsymbol{Y}_{-}(s)\left(\begin{matrix}1&\rho_{i}(s)\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),\quad s\in\Delta_{i}^{\circ};

(c)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Y}(z)=\mathcal{O}\left(\begin{matrix}1&\psi_{\alpha_{l}}(z-a_{l})\\
1&\psi_{\alpha_{l}}(z-a_{l})\end{matrix}\right)
as

,

.

It is by now standard to show, see for example
[

,

, that if a solution of

) and (

) holds. Notice that

is holomorphic in

at infinity, is continuous across

.

Appendix A
Riemann-Hilbert Problem for Painlevé II

A.1.
Lax Pair and the Corresponding Riemann-Hilbert Problem

The material of this section originates in
[
8
]
. However, we essentially follow the presentation in
[
9
, Section 5.0]
, see also
[
9
, Section 1.0]
for the relevant facts of the general theory of differential equations. Let
q(s)
be a solution of Painlevé II equation

(A.1)

q^{\prime\prime}(s)=sq(s)+2q^{3}(s)-\nu,\quad\nu>-1/2.

It is known that
q(s)
is a meromorphic function in the entire complex plane. For each
s
, which is not a pole of
q
, consider the following system of differential equations:

(A.2)

\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\tau;s)=\boldsymbol{A}^{FN}(\tau;s)\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\tau;s),

where

(A.3)

\boldsymbol{A}^{FN}(\tau;s):=\left(\begin{matrix}-\mathrm{i}(4\tau^{2}+s+2q^{2}(s))&4\tau q(s)+2\mathrm{i}q^{\prime}(s)+\nu/\tau\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
4\tau q(s)-2\mathrm{i}q^{\prime}(s)+\nu/\tau&\mathrm{i}(4\tau^{2}+s+2q^{2}(s))\end{matrix}\right)

and

(A.4)

\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\tau;s)=\boldsymbol{U}^{FN}(\tau;s)\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\tau;s),\quad\boldsymbol{U}^{FN}(\tau;s):=\left(\begin{matrix}-\mathrm{i}\tau&q(s)\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
q(s)&\mathrm{i}\tau\end{matrix}\right).

In general, such a system would be overdetermined, but the compatibility condition

(A.5)

\partial_{s}\boldsymbol{A}^{FN}(\tau;s)-\partial_{\tau}\boldsymbol{U}^{FN}(\tau;s)+\big[\boldsymbol{A}^{FN}(\tau;s),\boldsymbol{U}^{FN}(\tau;s)\big]=0

of (
A.2
)–(
A.4
) exactly reduces to the fact that
q(s)
solves (
A.1
). The general theory of differential equations implies that equation (
A.2
)–(
A.3
) has a set of canonical solutions
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\tau;s)
,
k\in\{1,\ldots,6\}
, that are uniquely determined by the conditions
\mathrm{det}(\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\tau;s))\equiv 1
and

(A.6)

\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{k}(\tau;s)=\left(\boldsymbol{I}+\boldsymbol{\mathcal{O}}\left(\tau^{-1}\right)\right)e^{-\mathrm{i}(\frac{4}{3}\tau^{3}+s\tau)\sigma_{3}}\quad\text{as}\quad\tau\to\infty,~~\mathrm{arg}(\tau)\in\left(\frac{(k-2)\pi}{3},\frac{k\pi}{3}\right).

Given any solution
\boldsymbol{\Psi}(\tau;s)
of (
A.2
)–(
A.3
) one can readily check by using (

In this appendix we discuss a group of examples of weights in

and therefore our results do not apply. As follows from Proposition
5
, this can happen only on

C.1.
Orthogonal Polynomials on a Segment

Let
\widehat{\rho}(z)
be an analytic and non-vanishing function in some neighborhood of
[-1,0]
and
\widehat{Q}_{n}(z)
be the non-identically zero monic polynomial of smallest degree, which is necessarily at most
n
, such that

(C.1)

\int_{-1}^{0}x^{k}\widehat{Q}_{n}(x)\widehat{\rho}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0,\quad k\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}

(we could also introduce Jacobi-type singularities at
-1
and
0
into the weight
\widehat{\rho}(x)
, but choose not to for simplicity of the exposition). Such polynomials were studied in
[
18
]
using Riemann-Hilbert approach under an additional assumption of positivity on
[-1,0]
(this assumption is really not needed for the approach to work). To describe the asymptotics of these polynomials, let

\widehat{\Phi}(z):=2z+1+2\widehat{w}(z),\quad\widehat{w}(z):=\sqrt{z(z+1)},

where the branch of the square root is chosen so that
\widehat{w}(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-1,0]
and
\widehat{w}(z)=z+\mathcal{O}(1)
as
z\to\infty
, while
\widehat{\Phi}(z)
is nothing but the conformal map of
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[-1,0]
onto
\big\{|z|>1\big\}
such that
\widehat{\Phi}(\infty)=\infty
and
\widehat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\infty)>0
. Further, let

(C.2)

D_{\widehat{\rho}}(z):=\exp\left\{\frac{\widehat{w}(z)}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{-1}^{0}\frac{\log\widehat{\rho}(x)}{z-x}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\widehat{w}_{+}(x)}\right\},\quad z\not\in[-1,0],

which is simply the Szegő function of
\widehat{\rho}(x)
(one must choose a continuous determination of
\log\widehat{\rho}(x)
on
[-1,0]
; Szegő functions for different continuous determinations will either coincide or differ by a sign), i.e., it is a non-vanishing and holomorphic function in the domain of its definition with continuous traces on
[-1,0]
that satisfy
D_{\widehat{\rho}+}(x)D_{\widehat{\rho}-}(x)=1/\widehat{\rho}(x)
. Lastly, let

D(z):=\left(\widehat{\Phi}(z)/\widehat{w}(z)\right)^{1/2}

be the branch holomorphic in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[-1,0]
that is positive for
z>0
(this is the Szegő function of
\widehat{w}_{+}(x)
, that is,
D(z)
is analytic and non-vanishing in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[-1,0]
and
D_{+}(x)D_{-}(x)=1/\widehat{w}_{+}(x)
for
x\in(-1,0)
). Then it holds that

(C.3)

\widehat{Q}_{n}(z)=\big(1+\mathcal{O}\big(1/n\big)\big)\widehat{\gamma}_{n}\widehat{\Phi}^{n}(z)\big(DD_{\widehat{\rho}}\big)(z)

locally uniformly in
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[-1,0]
, where
\widehat{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}=4^{n}(DD_{\widehat{\rho}})(\infty)
is the normalizing constant that makes the right-hand side of (
C.3
) behave like
z^{n}+\mathcal{O}(z^{n-1})
around infinity. The reader should see clear parallels between (
C.3
) and (
2.20
), (
3.4
) (the product
(DD_{\widehat{\rho}})(z)
also can be defined via an integral representation (
C.2
) with
\widehat{\rho}(x)
replaced by
(\widehat{\rho}\widehat{w}_{+})(x)
).

C.2.
Rotationally Symmetric Weights

Let
\Delta_{\mathsf{sym}}
be given by (

). In this case we let
a_{1}:=-1
,

, and

, where

Indeed, as all the legs of

The above expression is equal to

. When

\int_{\Delta_{\mathsf{sym}}}s^{3m+2}\widehat{Q}_{n}\big(s^{3}\big)\rho(s)\mathrm{d}s=3\int_{-1}^{0}x^{3m+2}\widehat{Q}_{n}\big(x^{3}\big)\widehat{\rho}\big(x^{3}\big)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-1}^{0}x^{m}\widehat{Q}_{n}(x)\widehat{\rho}(x)\mathrm{d}x=0,

where the last conclusion holds by (
C.1
) and one needs to observe that
3m+2=k\leq 3n+1
implies that

. Thus, it follows from (
C.3
