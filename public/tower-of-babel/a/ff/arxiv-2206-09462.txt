Fast Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann algorithm with a convergence rate of the fixed point iteration of o ⁡ ( 1 k ) o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right) Thanks: Received by the editors June 21, 2022; accepted for publication (in revised form) August 22, 2023;
Radu Ioan Boţ, Dang-Khoa Nguyen

Abstract
The Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann (KM) algorithm is the most fundamental iterative scheme designed to find a fixed point of an averaged operator in the framework of a real Hilbert space, since it lies at the heart of various numerical algorithms for solving monotone inclusions and convex optimization problems. We enhance the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann algorithm with Nesterov’s momentum updates and show that the resulting numerical method exhibits a convergence rate for the fixed point residual of o ⁡ ( 1 k ) o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right) while preserving the weak convergence of the iterates to a fixed point of the operator. Numerical experiments illustrate the superiority of the resulting so-called Fast KM algorithm over various fixed point iterative schemes, and also its oscillatory behavior, which is a specific of Nesterov’s momentum optimization algorithms.

Introduction

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real Hilbert space with inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
and induced norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
.
In this paper we are interested in formulating a fast numerical method for solving the fixed point problem

\textrm{ Find }x\in\mathcal{H}\textrm{ such that }x=T\left(x\right),

(1)

where
T\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is a
\theta
-averaged operator with
\theta\in\left(0,1\right]
. Recall that an operator
R\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is
nonexpansive
if it is
1
-Lipschitz continuous, that is

\left\lVert R\left(x\right)-R\left(y\right)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

Then
T
is said to be
averaged with constant
\theta
or
\theta
-averaged
if there exists a nonexpansive operator
R\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

T=\left(1-\theta\right)\mathrm{Id}+\theta R,

where
\mathrm{Id}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
denotes the identity mapping on
\mathcal{H}
. Obviously, an operator
T
is nonexpansive if it is at least
1
-averaged.
We denote the set of all
fixed points
of
T
by
\mathsf{Fix}T:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{H}\ |\ x=T\left(x\right)\right\}
.

The most naive approach when looking for a fixed point of
T
is the following process, also called
Banach-Picard iteration
,

x_{k+1}:=T\left(x_{k}\right)\quad\forall k\geq 0,

(2)

where
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
is a starting point.

According to the Banach-Picard fixed point theorem, if
T
is a
contraction
, namely,
T
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus
\delta\in\left[0,1\right)
, then the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
generated by (
2
) converges strongly to the unique fixed point of
T
with linear convergence rate. If
T
is just nonexpansive, then this statement is no longer true. To illustrate this, it is enough to choose
T=-\mathrm{Id}
and
x_{0}\neq 0
, in which case the Banach-Picard iteration not only fails approach a fixed point of
T
, but also generates a sequence that does not satisfy the
asymptotic regularity property
. We say that
the sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfies the asymptotic regularity property if the difference
x_{k}-T\left(x_{k}\right)
converges strongly to
0
as
k
tends to
+\infty
. This property is crucial for guaranteeing the convergence of the iterates, as we will see later.

In order to overcome the restrictive contraction assumption on
T
, Krasnosel’skiĭ proposed in
[
26
]
to apply the Banach-Picard iteration (
2
) to the operator
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Id}+\frac{1}{2}T
instead of
T
. Following on the idea of using convex combinations the so-called
Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann (KM) iteration

x_{k+1}:=\left(1-s_{k}\right)x_{k}+s_{k}T\left(x_{k}\right)\quad\forall k\geq 0,

(3)

where
\left(s_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
is a sequence in
\left(0,1\right]
, emerged. It turned out that a fundamental step in proving the convergence of the iterates of (
3
) is to show that
x_{k}-T\left(x_{k}\right)\to 0
as
k\to+\infty
, as it was done by Browder and Petryshyn in
[
11
]
in the constant case
s_{k}\equiv s\in\left(0,1\right)
. The extension to nonconstant sequences was achieved by Groetsch in
[
20
]
who proved that, if
\sum_{k\geq 0}s_{k}\left(1-s_{k}\right)=+\infty
, then the asymptotic regularity property is satisfied. The weak convergence of the iterates was then studied in various settings in
[
20
,
24
,
37
,
8
,
3
]
. Tikhonov regularization based techniques to improve the convergence of the iterates from weak to strong have been recently studied in
[
5
,
7
]
.

By considering convex combinations with a fixed so-called
anchor point

x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
one obtains the
Halpern iteration

[
21
]

x_{k+1}:=\left(1-s_{k}\right)x_{0}+s_{k}T\left(x_{k}\right)\quad\forall k\geq 0,

(4)

a method that has recently attracted a lot of interest
[
29
,
39
,
36
,
35
]
. The asymptotic regularity property of this iterative scheme has been studied in
[
42
,
43
]
.

Despite having ubiquitous applications in various fields, the study of the computational complexity of fixed point iteration schemes is still limited. One natural measure to quantify this is by means of the rate of convergence of the
fixed point residual

\left\lVert x_{k}-T\left(x_{k}\right)\right\rVert
. Notice that the asymptotic regularity property does not automatically provide an explicit convergence rate.

Sabach and Shtern proved in
[
38
]
for a general form of the Halpern iteration that the rate of convergence of the fixed point residual is of
O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
. Lieder tightened this results in
[
29
]
by a constant factor, for the Halpern iteration with
s_{k}:=1-\frac{1}{k+2}
for every
k\geq 0
, whereas Park and Ryu proved in
[
35
]
that the convergence rate of
O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
is optimal for this iterative scheme, which means that it can not be improved in general. On the other hand, the convergence of the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration expressed in terms of the fixed point residual was in the nineties proved to be of
o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
in
[
2
]
in the case of a constant sequence
\left(s_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. Later on, in the case of a nonconstant sequence, it was proved to be of
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
in
[
12
,
28
]
, and of
o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
in
[
9
,
15
,
32
]
, whereas in
[
4
]
it was shown that the asymptotic rate of convergence for the fixed point residual of the continuous time counterpart of the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration is of
o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right)
. Recently, Fierro, Maulén and Peypouquet proved in
[
18
]
that the rate of convergence of the fixed point residual of a general
inertial Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann algorithm
is also of
o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
. Noticeably, Contreras and Cominetti showed in
[
13
]
that in the
Banach space setting
the lower bound of the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration is
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
, which does not say anything about the lower bound in the Hilbert space setting.

For a family of general approaches aimed to “accelerate” the convergence of sequences relying on Shanks transformation and including Anderson acceleration, which can be applied also to fixed point problems, we refer to
[
10
]
.

In this paper we introduce an iterative method for solving the fixed point problem (
1
) which exhibits a rate of convergence for the fixed point residual of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
and guarantees the weak convergence of the iterates to a fixed point of
T
. The method is obtained by enhancing the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration with Nesterov’s momentum updates and follows via the temporal discretization of the second order dynamical system with vanishing damping term proposed in
[
6
]
for solving monotone equations. The iterative scheme exploits the coercivity of the operator
\mathrm{Id}-T
and has consequently a much more simple formulation than the Fast OGDA algorithm introduced in
[
6
]
for solving monotone equations, which requires the construction of auxiliary sequences. Numerical experiments show that the resulting so-called Fast KM algorithm outperforms various fixed point iterative schemes including recently introduced ones using anchoring. The numerical experiments also illustrate the oscillatory behavior of the method, which is a specific of algorithms with Nesterov’s momentum updates.

As a by-product of our proposed approach we obtain several fast splitting methods for solving monotone inclusions. It is well-known that some of the most prominent splitting schemes result as particular instances of the Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration, since they can be reduced to the solving of a fixed point problem governed by an average operator. This is the case for the Douglas-Rachford splitting
[
17
,
30
]
, the forward-backward splitting
[
30
]
, and the three operator splitting
[
16
,
14
]
. For a comprehensive study of operator splitting schemes we refer to
[
3
]
. Recent contributions to the acceleration of the convergence of splitting methods have been made in
[
25
,
39
,
27
,
41
,
35
]
.

2
A fast Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration

In our approach, we rely on the simple observation that

x_{*}\in\mathsf{Fix}T\Leftrightarrow\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{*}\right)=0,

which allows us to benefit from the recent development on a continuous fast method for solving monotone equations in
[
6
]
. To be more specific, we have that
T
is
\theta
-averaged if and only if
\mathrm{Id}-T
is
\frac{1}{2\theta}
-cocoercive
[
3
, Proposition 4.39]
, that is

\left\langle x-y,\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(y\right)\right\rangle\\
\geq\dfrac{1}{2\theta}\left\lVert\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(y\right)\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H}.

(5)

From here one can immediately see that it follows immediately that
\mathrm{Id}-T
is monotone. Furthermore, from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we can see that
\mathrm{Id}-T
is at most
2\theta
-Lipschitz continuous.

As a direct consequence of (
5
) we have that for every
x_{*}\in\mathsf{Fix}T
it holds

\left\langle x-x_{*},x-T\left(x\right)\right\rangle\geq\dfrac{1}{2\theta}\left\lVert x-T\left(x\right)\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0\quad\forall x\in\mathcal{H}.

The dynamical system studied in
[
6
]
, formulated for the monotone equation
\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\right)=0
and for constant time scaling term
\beta\left(t\right)\equiv 1
, reads

\begin{cases}\ddot{x}\left(t\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{t}\dot{x}\left(t\right)+\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\left(t\right)\right)+\dfrac{\alpha}{2t}\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\left(t\right)\right)=0\\
x\left(t_{0}\right)=x_{0}\textrm{ and }\dot{x}\left(t_{0}\right)=\dot{x}_{0},\end{cases}

(6)

where
\alpha\geq 2
,
\left(x_{0},\dot{x}_{0}\right)\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{H}
. The Lipschitz continuity of
\mathrm{Id}-T
guarantees the existence and uniqueness of a strong global solution
x:[t_{0},+\infty]\rightarrow\mathcal{H}
, which means that
x
and
\dot{x}
are locally absolutely continuous,
x\left(t_{0}\right)=x_{0}
,
\dot{x}\left(t_{0}\right)=\dot{x}_{0}
and
x
fulfills the first equation in (
6
) almost everywhere.

We set the time scaling term equal to one since our aim is to derive via temporal discretization an explicit iterative fixed point scheme, whereas nonconstant time scaling terms are known to positively impact the convergence rates of implicit numerical algorithms; see
[
6
, Remark 2]
for a detailed discussion on this issue.

Consider the first-order formulation of the first equation in (
6
)

\begin{cases}\dot{u}\left(t\right)&=\left(2-\alpha\right)\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\left(t\right)\right)\\
u\left(t\right)&=2\left(\alpha-1\right)x\left(t\right)+2t\dot{x}\left(t\right)+2t\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\left(t\right)\right).\end{cases}

(7)

We fix a time step
s>0
, set
s_{k}:=s\left(k+1\right)
for every
k\geq 1
, and approximate
x\left(s_{k}\right)\approx x_{k+1}
, and
u\left(s_{k}\right)\approx u_{k+1}
. The explicit finite-difference scheme for (
7
) at time
t:=s_{k}
gives for every
k\geq 1

\begin{cases}\dfrac{u_{k+1}-u_{k}}{s}&=\left(2-\alpha\right)\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)\\
u_{k+1}&=2\left(\alpha-1\right)x_{k+1}+2\left(k+1\right)\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)+2s\left(k+1\right)\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right),\end{cases}

(8)

with the initialization
u_{0}:=x_{0}-s\dot{x}_{0}
and
u_{1}:=x_{0}
. Different to
[
6
]
, where for the discretization of the argument of
\mathrm{Id}-T
we used an auxiliary sequence, this time we can use
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. This is thanks to the stronger property of cocoercivity the operator
\mathrm{Id}-T
is enhanced with and which will be reflected in the convergence analysis. We will see that this allows us not only to design a simpler algorithm, but also to consider larger step sizes than for the one proposed in
[
6
]
.

Next we will simplify the sequence
\left(u_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. The second equation in (
8
) gives for every
k\geq 1

u_{k}=2\left(\alpha-1\right)x_{k}+2k\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+2sk\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right).

Taking the difference we obtain for every
k\geq 1

\displaystyle u_{k+1}-u_{k}=

\displaystyle\ 2\left(k+\alpha\right)\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)-2k\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+2s\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle+2sk\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \left(2-\alpha\right)s\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right),

(9)

where the last relation comes from the first equation in (
8
). After rearranging (
9
), we obtain for every
k\geq 1

\displaystyle 2\left(k+\alpha\right)\left(x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=2k\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)-\alpha s\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle\quad-2sk\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right).

From here, we deduce that for every
k\geq 1

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle=x_{k}+\dfrac{k}{k+\alpha}\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)-\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\quad-\dfrac{sk}{k+\alpha}\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\right)x_{k}+\dfrac{\left(1-s\right)k}{k+\alpha}\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}T\left(x_{k}\right)+\dfrac{sk}{k+\alpha}\left(T\left(x_{k}\right)-T\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right).

Summing up, the algorithm we propose in this paper for solving (
1
) has the following formulation.

Algorithm 1

Fast KM algorithm

Let
\alpha>2,x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
and
0<s\leq\frac{1}{\theta}
.

for

k=1,2,\cdots

Compute

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\left(1-\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\right)x_{k}+\dfrac{\left(1-s\right)k}{k+\alpha}\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}T\left(x_{k}\right)+\dfrac{sk}{k+\alpha}\left(T\left(x_{k}\right)-T\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right).

(11)

end

for

Remark 1
.

For the step size choice
s:=1
which is allowed for every
\theta
-averaged operator
T
with
\theta\in(0,1]
, our iterative scheme becomes

x_{k+1}:=\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\right)x_{k}+\dfrac{\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}T\left(x_{k}\right)+\dfrac{k}{k+\alpha}\left(T\left(x_{k}\right)-T\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right)\quad\forall k\geq 1.

(12)

Notice that for a
nonexpansive
operator
T
, which corresponds to the case
\theta=1
, the value
s:=1
is the largest step size that can be taken.

The numerical algorithm (
12
) can be interpreted as a Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration enhanced with the extrapolation term
\frac{k}{k+\alpha}\left(T\left(x_{k}\right)-T\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right)
which proves to have an accelerating effect on the convergence of the fixed point residual. We learn form here that, in order to improve the convergence rate while preserving the convergence of the iterates, one must address iterative schemes that go beyond the classical
Mann iteration

[
31
]
. The latter allows in the update rule only nonnegative coefficients for both the previous iterates and the operator evaluations at the previous iterates.

Remark 2
.

A direct application of the explicit Fast OGDA method in
[
6
]
to the solving of the monotone equation
\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\right)=0
leads for given
\alpha>2,x_{0},x_{1},y_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
,
0<s<\max\left\{\frac{1}{4\theta},\frac{1-\theta}{2\theta}\right\}
to the following iterative scheme: for every
k\geq 1
set

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle:=x_{k}+\left(1-\dfrac{\alpha}{k+\alpha}\right)\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)-\dfrac{\alpha s}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(y_{k-1}\right)

(13a)

\displaystyle x_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=y_{k}-\dfrac{s}{2}\left(1+\dfrac{k}{k+\alpha}\right)\left(\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(y_{k}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(y_{k-1}\right)\right).

(13b)

If
T
is
\theta
-averaged, then
\mathrm{Id}-T
is
L
-Lipschitz with
L:=\min\left\{2\theta,\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right\}
(here we make the convention
\frac{1}{0}:=+\infty
), thus the upper bound of the step size is
\frac{1}{2L}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{4\theta},\frac{1-\theta}{2\theta}\right\}
. Indeed, we already noticed that, since
\mathrm{Id}-T
is
\frac{1}{2\theta}
-cocoercive, it is at most
2\theta
-Lipschitz continuous. On the other hand,
T
is
\theta
-averaged if and only if (see
[
3
, Proposition 4.35]
)

\left\lVert T\left(x\right)-T\left(y\right)\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{2}-\dfrac{1-\theta}{\theta}\left\lVert\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(y\right)\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{H},

which implies that
\mathrm{Id}-T
is Lipschitz continuous with modulus at most
\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}
.

Noticeably, the step size upper bound for algorithm (
13
) is more restrictive compared to the one in the Fast KM iteration (
11
). In addition, (
11
) has an obviously simpler formulation than (
13
). Even if one rewrites (
13
) in terms of a single sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
, it would require
y_{k},y_{k-1}
and
y_{k-2}
to compute
y_{k+1}
. In comparison, (
11
) requires
x_{k}
and
x_{k-1}
to compute
x_{k+1}
.

3
Convergence analysis

The fundamental tool of the convergence analysis is the following discrete energy function which, for fixed
x_{*}\in\mathsf{Fix}T
and
0\leq\lambda\leq\alpha-1
, is defined for every
k\geq 1
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda,k}:=

\displaystyle\ \dfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert 2\lambda\left(x_{k}-x_{*}\right)+2k\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(3\alpha-2\right)sk\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle+2\lambda\left(\alpha-1-\lambda\right)\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{*}\right\rVert^{2}+\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(\alpha-2\right)\lambda sk\left\langle x_{k}-x_{*},\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)s^{2}k^{2}\left\lVert\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}.

The discrete energy function is defined in analogy with the Lyapunov energy functions used in the study of continuous time dynamical systems associated with convex minimization problems and monotone equations (
[
1
,
6
]
). While in convex minimization, the distance from the objective function at the current iterate to its minimal objective value plays the prominent role in the definition of the discrete energy function, in the present setting, this role is taken by
\left\lVert\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2}
. The coefficient
k^{2}
in front of this term suggests the rate at which we expect this term to converge, provided the energy sequence converges as
k\rightarrow+\infty
. The same reasoning applies to the third summand in the discrete energy function, while the first two summands will play an important role in proving the convergence of the iterates.

The properties of the discrete energy function are presented in the following lemma, with the proof deferred to the
appendix

B
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
x_{*}\in\mathsf{Fix}T
and
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by
algorithm

1
.
Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

for
0\leq\lambda\leq\alpha-1
and every
k\geq 1
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda,k+1}-\mathcal{E}_{\lambda,k}

(14)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle 2\left(2-\alpha\right)\lambda s\left\langle x_{k}-x_{*},\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)\right\rangle+\omega_{1}k\left\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+s\left(\omega_{2}k+\omega_{3}\right)\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{k},\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)\right\rangle+\omega_{4}s^{2}k\left\lVert\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{1}{\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(s-\dfrac{1}{\theta}\right)sk^{2}\left\lVert\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k}\right)-\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert^{2},

where

\displaystyle\omega_{1}

\displaystyle:=4\left(\lambda+1-\alpha\right)\leq 0,

(15a)

\displaystyle\omega_{2}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\Bigl(4\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(\lambda+1-\alpha\right)+\alpha\left(2-\alpha\right)\Bigr)\leq 0,

(15b)

\displaystyle\omega_{3}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(2\alpha\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(\lambda+1-\alpha\right)+\alpha-2\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}+2\left(2-\alpha\right)\left(\alpha-1\right)\right),

(15c)

\displaystyle\omega_{4}

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{2\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(2-\alpha\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)\leq 0;

(15d)

\rm{(ii)}

for

Remark 8
.

In
[
35
]
, Park and Ryu established a fixed point residual lower bound of
O(\frac{1}{k})
for various fixed point iterations designed to find a fixed point of a
1
-Lipschitz continuous operator. In the following, we will explain that this statement is not in contradiction with the convergence rate statement in
theorem

7
.

According to
[
35
, Theorem 4.6]
, for given
K\geq 2
,
d\geq K
and every initial point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exists an
1
-Lipschitz continuous operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x_{*}\in\mathsf{Fix}T
such that the inequality

\|x_{K-1}-T(x_{K-1})\|\geq\frac{2\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|}{K},

(24)

holds for every iterates
(x_{k})_{k=0,...,K-1}
satisfying

x_{k}\in x_{0}+\textrm{span}\{x_{0}-T(x_{0}),...,x_{k-1}-T(x_{k-1})\}

(25)

for
k=1,...,K-1
. It is evident that the sequence generated by the Fast KM algorithm with
x_{1}:=x_{0}
fulfills (
25
). Consequently, there exists such an
1
-Lipschitz continuous operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
that fulfills (
24
) for the sequence of iterates generated by the Fast KM algorithm with
x_{1}:=x_{0}
. In contrast, according to
theorem

7
, for the same operator (as it is the case for every
1
-Lipschitz continuous operator), it holds
k\|x_{k-1}-T(x_{k-1})\|\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow+\infty
, which means that there exists
k_{0}>K
such that

\|x_{k-1}-T(x_{k-1})\|<\frac{2\|x_{0}-x_{*}\|}{k}

for every
k\geq k_{0}
.

Remark 9
.

In
[
13
]
, Contreras and Cominetti presented an example of a
1
-Lipschitz continuous operator defined on a Banach space with the property that the fixed point residual of the Mann iteration is bounded from below by
O(\frac{1}{k})
. For given initial points
y_{0}
and
x_{0}
, the Mann iterates are defined for every
k\geq 0
recursively as follows

x_{k+1}:=s^{k+1}_{0}y_{0}+\sum_{i=0}^{k}s^{k+1}_{i+1}T(x_{i}),

where
s_{i}^{k+1}\geq 0
for every
i=0,...,k+1
and
\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}s_{i}^{k+1}=1
.

As observed in
remark

1
, the Fast KM algorithm is not a Mann iteration since not all the weights are nonnegative, although they do sum up to
1
. This observation suggests that in order to obtain a fixed point residual rate of
o(\frac{1}{k})
in Banach spaces, one might have to go beyond general Mann iterations and allow, for instance, negative weights in the iterative scheme.

4
Application to several splitting algorithms

In the light of the fact that fixed point methods lie at the heart of important splitting iterative schemes for monotone inclusions, we will discuss in this section how the Fast KM algorithm impacts the latter. In addition, we will review some recent acceleration approaches of splitting algorithms from the literature.

Consider the following monotone inclusion problem

\mbox{Find}\ x\in\mathcal{H}\ \mbox{such that}\ 0\in A\left(x\right)+B\left(x\right)+C\left(x\right),

(26)

where
A,B\colon\mathcal{H}\to 2^{\mathcal{H}}
are set-valued maximally monotone operators and
C:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is a
\beta
-cocoercive operator with
\beta>0
.

Davis and Yin introduced in
[
16
]
the following operator

T_{DY}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H},\quad T_{DY}:=J_{\gamma A}\circ\left(2J_{\gamma B}-\mathrm{Id}-\gamma C\circ J_{\gamma B}\right)+\mathrm{Id}-J_{\gamma B},

(27)

where
0<\gamma\leq 2\beta
and
J_{\gamma A}:=\left(\mathrm{Id}+\gamma A\right)^{-1}
denotes the
resolvent operator
of
\gamma A
with constant
\gamma
. The set of zeros of
A+B+C
, denoted by
\textrm{Zer}\left(A+B+C\right)
, can be characterized in terms of
T_{DY}
by (see
[
16
, Lemma 2.2]
)

\textrm{Zer}\left(A+B+C\right)=J_{\gamma B}\left(\mathsf{Fix}T_{DY}\right).

The Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration applied to
T_{DY}
gives rise to the
three-operator splitting method

x_{k+1}:=\left(1-s_{k}\right)x_{k}+s_{k}T_{DY}\left(x_{k}\right)\quad\forall k\geq 0,

where
x_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\left(s_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}\subseteq\left(0,2-\frac{\gamma}{2\beta}\right]
. The operator
T_{DY}
is
\frac{2\beta}{4\beta-\gamma}
-averaged, here we use the convention
\beta:=+\infty
whenever
C\equiv 0
, in which case the operator is
\frac{1}{2}
-averaged. According to
[
23
]
, this constant is tight. It has been shown in
[
14
]
that the convergence rate of three-operator splitting method is, as expected, of
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
.

The theoretical statements of the previous section applied to this particular setting lead to the following result.

Corollary 10
.

Let
\alpha>2,x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
,
0<\gamma\leq 2\beta
and
0<s\leq 2-\frac{\gamma}{2\beta}
.
For every
k\geq 1
we set

\displaystyle x_{k+1}:=

\displaystyle\left(1-\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}\right)x_{k}+\dfrac{\left(1-s\right)k}{k+\alpha}\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{s\alpha}{2\left(k+\alpha\right)}T_{DY}\left(x_{k}\right)+\dfrac{sk}{k+\alpha}\left(T_{DY}\left(x_{k}\right)-T_{DY}\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right).

Then the following statements are true:

\rm{(i)}

\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
converges weakly to an element
x_{*}
in
\mathsf{Fix}T_{DY}
such that
J_{\gamma B}\left(x_{*}\right)
is a solution of (
26
);

\rm{(ii)}

it holds

\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\left\lVert x_{k-1}-T_{DY}\left(x_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert=o\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right)\textrm{ as }k\to+\infty.

In the following we will revisit some of the particular formulations of (
26
) and of the corresponding underlying operator
T_{DY}
also in order to emphasize the broad applicability of
corollary

10
.

Resolvent operator

For
B\equiv C\equiv 0
, the problem (
26
) reduces to

\mbox{Find}\ x\in\mathcal{H}\ \mbox{such that}\ 0\in A\left(x\right),

and, for
\gamma>0
,

T_{DY}=J_{\gamma A},

which is
\frac{1}{2}
-averaged.

The fixed point residual of the classical proximal point algorithm

x_{k+1}=J_{\gamma A}\left(x_{k}\right)\quad\forall k\geq 0,

is known to be in general of
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
, whereas Gu and Yang have shown in
[
19
]
that, for
\mathcal{H}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it can be tightened to

\left\lVert J_{\gamma A}\left(x_{k}\right)-x_{k}\right\rVert=\begin{cases}O\left(\dfrac{1}{k}\right),&\textrm{ if }n=1,\\
O\left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k}k}}\right),&\textrm{ if }n\geq 2.\end{cases}

In the same setting of finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, Kim proposed in
[
25
]
(see also
[
35
]
) the following
accelerated proximal point method
, which, given
y_{1}=x_{0}=x_{1}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\gamma>0
, reads: for every
k\geq 1
set

\displaystyle\begin{split}y_{k+1}&:=J_{\gamma A}\left(x_{k}\right),\\
x_{k+1}&:=y_{k+1}+\dfrac{k}{k+2}\left(y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right)-\dfrac{k}{k+2}\left(y_{k}-x_{k-1}\right).\end{split}

(28)

Using the performance estimation approach, the the author proved that the method exhibits a convergence rate of the fixed point residual of
O\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
.

By comparison, the algorithm in
corollary

10
for
\gamma>0
,
0<s\leq 2
and
T_{DY}=J_{\gamma A}
exhibits a convergence rate of the fixed point residual of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
.

Forward-backward operator

For
B\equiv 0
, the problem (
26
) reduces to

\mbox{Find}\ x\in\mathcal{H}\ \mbox{such that}\ 0\in A\left(x\right)+C(x),

and, for
0<\gamma\leq 2\beta
,

T_{DY}:=T_{FB}=J_{\gamma A}\circ(\mathrm{Id}-\gamma C),

which is
\frac{2\beta}{4\beta-\gamma}
-averaged.

The Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration gives rise in this case to the classical
forward-backward
algorithm, which is known to exhibit a convergence rate of the fixed point residual of
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
. By comparison, the algorithm in
corollary

10
for
T_{DY}=T_{FB}
exhibits a convergence rate of the fixed point residual of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
.

Douglas-Rachford operator

For
C\equiv 0
, the problem (
26
) reduces to

\mbox{Find}\ x\in\mathcal{H}\ \mbox{such that}\ 0\in A\left(x\right)+B(x),

and, for
\gamma>0
,

T_{DY}:=T_{DR}=J_{\gamma A}\circ(2J_{\gamma B}-\mathrm{Id})+\mathrm{Id}-J_{\gamma B},

which is
\frac{1}{2}
-averaged.

The Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration gives rise in this case to the classical
Douglas-Rachford
algorithm (see
[
17
]
,
[
30
]
), which is known to exhibit a convergence rate of the fixed point residual of
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\right)
(see
[
22
]
). By comparison, the algorithm in
corollary

10
for
\gamma>0
,
0<s\leq 2
and
T_{DY}=T_{DR}
exhibits a convergence rate of the fixed point residual of
o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)
.

Recent contributions to acceleration approaches

The idea of the Halpern iteration of considering in the iterative schemes convex combinations with an anchor point has been recently extensively exploited as it led to convergence rate improvements of numerical methods. This has been first done for fixed point iterations (
[
38
]
,
[
29
]
), then for algorithms for solving monotone equations and minimax problems (
[
39
]
,
[
27
]
), and later on for variants of splitting algorithms like the forward-backward, the Douglas-Rachford and the three-operator splitting method (
[
36
,
41
,
40
,
44
]
).

Our method, however, relies on the continuous time approach from
[
6
]
and uses the idea of Nesterov’s momentum updates (
[
33
]
).

and that

-averaged, therefore we take as step size

5.2
Douglas-Rachford type methods

For the second family of numerical experiments we consider the following feasibility problem

\mbox{Find }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mbox{ such that }x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}\cap H_{u,\nu},

where
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}
is the set of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
with nonnegative entries and, for a given vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
and real number
\nu\in\mathbb{R}
,

H_{u,\nu}:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\colon\left\langle x,u\right\rangle=\nu\right\}.

If
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}\cap H_{u,\nu}\neq\emptyset
, then (see, for instance,
[
3
, Corollary 27.6]
)

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}\cap H_{u,\nu}=\textrm{Zer}\left(N_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}}+N_{H_{u,\nu}}\right)\neq\emptyset,

where
N_{D}:\mathbb{R}^{2n}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{R}^{2n}
denotes the normal cone operator of a nonempty closed convex set
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The normal cone operator
N_{D}
is a maximally monotone and its resolvent
J_{N_{D}}
is nothing else than the projection
\mathrm{Proj}_{D}
onto the set
D
.

The Douglas-Rachford (DR) algorithm is known as one of the most successful numerical method for solving such feasibility problems. In this concrete case it reads

x_{k+1}:=(1-s_{k})x_{k}+s_{k}T_{DR}(x_{k})\quad\forall k\geq 0,

where
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}^{2n}},(s_{k})_{k\geq 0}\subseteq(0,2]
and
T_{DR}:=\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}}\circ(2\mathrm{Proj}_{H_{u,\nu}}-\mathrm{Id})+\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{H_{u,\nu}}
.

In the following we compare the performances of the DR algorithm for various choices for
\left(s_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}\subseteq(0,2]
with the ones of the Halpern algorithm (
[
21
]
) and of the Fast KM algorithm, which make also use of the Douglas-Rachford operator
T_{DR}
. For the Fast KM algorithm we consider
\alpha\in\left\{5,10,30,100,500\right\}
and as a step size
s:=2
.

In the numerical experiments we generate for different values for the dimension
n\geq 1
a number of
\mathtt{N_{test}}
pairs
(u,\nu)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that the intersection of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}
and
H_{u,\nu}
is nonempty, and a number of
\mathtt{N_{init}}
normally distributed starting points
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, which we scale then by
100
. For each generated hyperplane
H_{u,\nu}
and starting point
x_{0}
we run several variants of the DR algorithm, the Fast KM method and the Halpern algorithm. The algorithms terminate either after
\mathtt{k_{\max}}
iterations or once the following condition is fulfilled

\left\lVert\mathrm{Proj}_{H_{u,\nu}}\left(x_{k}\right)-\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}}\left(\mathrm{Proj}_{H_{u,\nu}}\left(x_{k}\right)\right)\right\rVert\leq\mathtt{Tol},

(29)

where
\mathtt{Tol}
denotes the tolerance error. This condition is motivated by the fact that for the Douglas-Rachford methods the so-called shadow sequence is the one that converges to a solution; in other words, (
29
) guarantees that
\mathrm{Proj}_{H_{u,\nu}}\left(x_{k}\right)
is close to the intersection
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2n}\cap H_{u,\nu}

iterations will be counted as a successful attempt. In

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{1}{4\left(3\alpha-2\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\bigg\|4\lambda\left(x_{k}-x_{*}\right)+2k\left(x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt+\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\left(3\alpha-2\right)sk\left(\mathrm{Id}-T\right)\left(x_{k-1}\right)\bigg\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\dfrac{1}{3\alpha-2}\left(\alpha-2\right)k^{2}\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{k-1}\right\rVert^{2}+2\lambda\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(1-\dfrac{4\lambda}{3\alpha-2}\right)\left\lVert x_{k}-x_{*}\right\rVert^{2},

This shows that for
0\leq\lambda\leq\frac{3\alpha}{4}-\frac{1}{2}
all terms in the expression (

) are nonnegative, thus the sequence
(\mathcal{E}_{\lambda,k})_{k\geq 1}
is nonnegative, too.

Proof of
lemma

4
.

For the quadratic expression in
R_{k}
we calculate

\dfrac{\Delta_{k}}{s^{2}}:=\left(\omega_{2}k+\omega_{3}\right)^{2}-\dfrac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}k^{2}\\
=\left(\omega_{2}^{2}-\dfrac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}\right)k^{2}+2\omega_{2}\omega_{3}k+\omega_{3}^{2}.

It suffices to guarantee that
\omega_{2}^{2}-\frac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}<0
in order to be sure that there exits some integer
k\left(\lambda\right)\geq 1
such that
\Delta_{k}\leq 0
for every
k\geq k\left(\lambda\right)
and to obtain from here, due to
lemma

14
, that
R_{k}\leq 0
for every
k\geq k\left(\lambda\right)
.

We will show that there exists a nonempty open interval contained in
[0,\alpha-1]
with the property that
\omega_{2}^{2}-\dfrac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}<0
holds when
\lambda
is chosen within this open interval. To this end we set
\xi:=\lambda+1-\alpha\leq 0
and get

\omega_{2}=\dfrac{1}{\alpha-1}\Bigl(4\left(\alpha-1\right)\xi-\alpha\left(\alpha-2\right)\Bigr)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\omega_{1}\omega_{4}=-\dfrac{2}{\alpha-1}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)\xi.

Written in terms of
\xi
, we have first to guarantee that

\displaystyle\omega_{2}^{2}-\dfrac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left(\left(4\left(\alpha-1\right)\xi-\alpha\left(\alpha-2\right)\right)^{2}+4\left(5\alpha-2\right)\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(\alpha-2\right)\xi\right)

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left(16\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}\xi^{2}+4\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)\xi+\alpha^{2}\left(\alpha-2\right)^{2}\right)<0.

A direct computation shows that

\Delta_{\xi}=16\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}\left(2-\alpha\right)^{2}\left(\left(3\alpha-2\right)^{2}-4\alpha^{2}\right)=16\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}\left(\alpha-2\right)^{3}\left(5\alpha-2\right)>0.

Hence, in order to get (
B
), we have to choose
\xi
between the two roots of the quadratic function arising in this formula, in other words

\displaystyle\xi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle:=\dfrac{1}{32\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left(-4\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)-\sqrt{\Delta_{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2+\sqrt{\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(5\alpha-2\right)}\right)

\displaystyle<\xi=\lambda+1-\alpha<\xi_{2}\left(\alpha\right):=\dfrac{1}{32\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}\left(-4\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)+\sqrt{\Delta_{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2-\sqrt{\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(5\alpha-2\right)}\right).

Obviously
\xi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)<0
and from Viète’s formula
\xi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)\cdot\xi_{2}\left(\alpha\right)=\frac{\alpha^{2}\left(\alpha-2\right)^{2}}{16\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}}
, it follows that we must have
\xi_{2}\left(\alpha\right)<0
as well.

Therefore, going back to
\lambda
, in order to be sure that
\omega_{2}^{2}-\frac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}<0
this must be chosen such that

\alpha-1+\xi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)<\lambda<\alpha-1+\xi_{2}\left(\alpha\right).

Next we will show that

0<\alpha-1-\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)<\dfrac{3\alpha}{4}-\dfrac{1}{2}.

(43)

Indeed, the inequality on the left-hand side follows immediately, since

\displaystyle\alpha-1-\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(5\alpha^{2}-8\alpha+4\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha^{2}+4\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}\right)>0.

Using this relation, one can notice that the inequality on the right-hand side of (
43
) can be equivalently written as

5\alpha^{2}-8\alpha+4<2\left(\alpha-1\right)\left(3\alpha-2\right)\Leftrightarrow 0<\alpha^{2}-2\alpha=\alpha\left(\alpha-2\right),

which is true as
\alpha>2
.

From (
43
) we immediately deduce that

0<\alpha-1+\xi_{2}\left(\alpha\right)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\alpha-1+\xi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)<\dfrac{3\alpha}{4}-\dfrac{1}{2}.

This allows us to choose

\displaystyle\underline{\lambda}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle:=\alpha-1+\xi_{1}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}+\dfrac{\alpha-1}{2}-\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\sqrt{\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(5\alpha-2\right)}

\displaystyle<\overline{\lambda}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{\dfrac{3\alpha}{4}-\dfrac{1}{2},\alpha-1+\xi_{2}\left(\alpha\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\dfrac{3\alpha}{4}-\dfrac{1}{2},\dfrac{\alpha^{2}}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}+\dfrac{\alpha-1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\sqrt{\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(5\alpha-2\right)}\right\},

since

\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\alpha^{2}+\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\alpha-1\right)-\dfrac{1}{8\left(\alpha-1\right)}\left(\alpha-2\right)\sqrt{\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(5\alpha-2\right)}>0.

Indeed, as
\left(\alpha-1\right)\sqrt{\alpha-1}>\left(\alpha-2\right)\sqrt{\alpha-2}
and
4\sqrt{\alpha-1}>\sqrt{5\alpha-2}
we can easily deduce that

\alpha^{2}+4\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}>4\left(\alpha-1\right)^{2}>\left(\alpha-2\right)\sqrt{\left(\alpha-2\right)\left(5\alpha-2\right)}

and the claim follows.

In conclusion, choosing
\lambda
to satisfy
\underline{\lambda}\left(\alpha\right)<\lambda<\overline{\lambda}\left(\alpha\right)
, we have

\omega_{2}^{2}-\frac{2(5\alpha-2)}{3\alpha-2}\omega_{1}\omega_{4}<0

and therefore there exists some integer
k\left(\lambda\right)\geq 1
such that
R_{k}\leq 0
for every
k\geq k\left(\lambda\right)
.
