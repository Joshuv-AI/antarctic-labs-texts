Uniform linear inviscid damping and enhanced dissipation near monotonic shear flows in high Reynolds number regime (I): the whole space case Thanks: Supported in part by NSF grant DMS-1945179.
Hao Jia

Abstract
. We study the dynamics of the two dimensional Navier Stokes equations linearized around a strictly monotonic shear flow on 𝕋 × ℝ \mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R} . The main task is to understand the associated Rayleigh and Orr-Sommerfeld equations, under the natural assumption that the linearized operator around the monotonic shear flow in the inviscid case has no discrete eigenvalues. We obtain precise control of solutions to the Orr-Sommerfeld equations in the high Reynolds number limit, using the perspective that the nonlocal term can be viewed as a compact perturbation with respect to the main part that includes the small diffusion term. As a corollary, we give a detailed description of the linearized flow in Gevrey spaces (linear inviscid damping) that are uniform with respect to the viscosity, and enhanced dissipation type decay estimates. The key difficulty is to accurately capture the behavior of the solution to Orr-Sommerfeld equations in the critical layer. In this paper we consider the case of shear flows on 𝕋 × ℝ \mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R} . The case of bounded channels poses significant additional difficulties, due to the presence of boundary layers, and will be addressed elsewhere.



1.
Introduction and main results

The study of stability problems in mathematical analysis of fluid dynamics has a long and distinguished history, dating back to the work of Kelvin
[
36
]
, Orr
[
46
]
and Rayleigh
[
47
]
among many others, and continuing to the present day. Hydrodynamical stability problems can be considered in both two and three dimensions. In this paper we work with two dimensional flows. An important early theme was to understand when physically relevant steady flows may become unstable by studying discrete eigenvalues of the corresponding linearized operator, the presence of which often leads to exponential instability. When the linearized operator is spectrally stable, it is natural to ask if we can prove nonlinear stability, which is the case for many parabolic equations.

The answer to this question turns out to be complicated due to many reasons. The main issue is that very often the most interesting physical settings involve high Reynolds number (or equivalently the viscosity has to be taken as very small), and the Navier Stokes equations degenerate to Euler equations where continuous spectrum plays a dominant role. It is therefore important to understand mathematically the property of the linearized operator in the high Reynolds number regime, including the limiting inviscid problem.

For the Euler equations, there are significant recent progresses on the asymptotic stability of shear flows and vortices, assuming spectral stability, see for example

When the viscosity
\nu>0

Roughly speaking, the background flow mixes the vorticity field which makes the viscous effect more powerful in averaging the vorticity function, leading to faster decay of the vorticity than when viscous effect acts alone. The enhanced dissipation can be used to establish improved nonlinear asymptotic stability results for perturbations of the order
O(\nu^{\gamma})

In view of the results on linear inviscid damping for the Euler equation, it is natural to expect that even for Navier Stokes equations with small viscosity we should have the same inviscid damping results with explicit rates of decay in time of the stream functions and velocity fields. Surprisingly, results in this direction are very few, as pointed out in
[

[

[

]
for the spectrally restricted stream function for “mixing layer” type shear flows (but a description of the full solution without spectral restrictions is not available).

The main reason for this gap is that the Navier Stokes equations in high Reynolds number regime is a singular perturbation of the Euler equations, and the corresponding analysis for establishing precise inviscid damping is significantly more complicated. As a consequence, the problem remains largely open for more general shear flows than the Couette flow.

To bridge this gap, we take a first step and study the linear asymptotic stability of monotonic shear flows
(b(y),0)
on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
, and obtain precise inviscid damping estimates for the linearized flow that are uniform with respect to viscosity, together with enhanced dissipation estimates. Strictly speaking,
(b(y),0)
is not a steady state for the Navier Stokes equations, and becomes steady state only with a small external force
f=(-\nu b^{\prime\prime}(y),0)
. In our setting, we consider the viscosity
\nu
to be very small. The effect of diffusion on the background shear flow is negligible, at least up to the diffusion time scale
T\ll\frac{1}{\nu}
. After the diffusion time, due to the enhanced dissipation effect, the flow is essentially dominated by a heat evolution, at least heuristically. Therefore our analysis below still captures the main dynamics of the Navier Stokes equations near shear flows, even when we do not add the external forcing. We hope the methods introduced here can be applied to establish uniform inviscid damping in high Reynolds number regime for more complicated and physically relevant flows, such as the Poiseuille flow in a periodic channel and monotone vortices in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

1.3.
Main ideas and further problems

1.3.1.
Main ideas of proof

We briefly describe our main ideas to establish Theorem
1.3
. With the representation formula (
1.24
)-(
1.25
), the proof of the bounds (
1.28
)-(
1.29
) is reduced to the study of the Orr-Sommerfeld equations (

) (in

) (in

2
2

2

The study of the Orr-Sommerfeld equation has a long history. We refer to
[
24
,
25
]
for an introduction to other important aspects of the equation.
The main task is to establish the bound (
1.30
) on the profile of the spectral density function
\Theta_{k,\nu}(v,w)
.

Our argument, on a conceptual level, can be divided into two steps. The first important step is to obtain a very detailed understanding of the fundamental solution of the main Airy part
\frac{\nu}{k}\partial_{y}^{2}+i(b(y_{0})-b(y))
of the Orr-Sommerfeld equation, in Gevrey spaces. The characterization of the fundamental solution we need consists of both pointwise bounds, see (
4.11
)-(
4.12
), and estimates in Gevrey regularity spaces after suitable renormalizations, see Proposition
4.1
.

The second main step is to show that the full Orr-Sommerfeld equation can be viewed as a relatively compact perturbation of the main Airy part, and can be treated using a limiting absorption principe, see Proposition
5.3
, under the spectral assumption that the linearized operator in the inviscid case has no discrete eigenvalues, see Assumption
1.1
.

Therefore, the general line of our argument is analogous to the study of the Rayleigh equation, except that we need to take the small-in-size but highest-in-order diffusion term as part of the main term. The essential complication is that we can no longer solve the main Airy equation explicitly, and instead need to rely on various pointwise bounds and regularity estimates on the fundamental solution.

On a more technical level, to establish the required pointwise bounds on the fundamental solution
k^{\ast}_{\epsilon}(y,z;y_{0})
with
y,z,y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
of the main Airy part, defined for
y,z,y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
as

(1.31)

\Big[\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}+i(b(y_{0})-b(y))\Big]k^{\ast}_{\epsilon}(y,z;y_{0})=\delta(y-z),

we use several energy estimates which achieve different levels of control, see section
3
. The most interesting energy estimate is perhaps the one which we call “entanglement inequalities”, see Lemma
3.4
and the remark below it, as these inequalities provide bounds on the fundamental solution in one region by its behavior in another, possibly far away, region. Such inequalities are not new, and can be found for example in
[
57
]
, although our setting is slightly different.

The pointwise bounds alone are not sufficient for our purposes, due to the strong singularity of the fundamental solution at
y=y_{0}
or equivalently
v=w
. To obtain a more accurate description of the singularity, as in
[
29
,
35
]
, we perform a renormalization by making the change of variable
(v,w)\to(v+w,w)
after reformulating the fundamental solution in
v,w
variables, see (
4.1
), which shifts all singularity to the
v
variable and the resulting kernel becomes very smooth in
w
.

The main remaining difficulty is to capture the singular behavior of the kernel in
v
, which is quite complicated since the kernel is both oscillatory and singular. It appears that there is no simple way to renormalize such complicated behavior. To overcome this difficulty, we derive various characterizations of the singular behavior in
v
, see for example (
5.7
) and the decomposition (
4.7
).

Once we have sufficient understanding on the kernel, we can establish the limiting absorption principle, see Proposition
5.3
, and obtain a preliminary bound on
\Theta_{k,\nu}(v,w)
in low regularity Sobolev spaces. We then apply the Fourier multiplier associated with the symbol
e^{\delta\langle k,\eta\rangle^{1/2}}
and use a commutator argument to get higher regularity bounds, as in
[
29
,
35
]
.

To obtain the enhanced dissipation bound (
1.27
), in section
8
we generalize an important result from
[
57
]
which gives sharp decay bounds on the semigroup using resolvent estimates (that was inspired by the earlier work of Helffer and Sjöstrand
[
26
]
). Our main observation here is that the assumption in
[
57
]
on the generator of the semigroup being accretive may be replaced by an a priori bound on the semigroup, which can be obtained by a more detailed analysis of the spectral density function. This new formulation appears applicable for a wide range of singular perturbation problems where sharp semigroup bounds are useful.

After the completion of this work, we learnt about the recent work of Helffer and Sjöstrand
[
27
]
which contained a more general version of our estimate (see Theorem
8.1
) on the decay of semigroups using resolvent bounds and a priori semigroup bounds, with sharp constants. We decide to keep the statement and proof of our semigroup bounds for their simplicity and for the sake of completeness, and refer to
[
27
]
for the more general version.

1.3.2.
Further problems

A natural next step is to study the uniform inviscid damping near monotonic shear flows inside a bounded periodic channel, such as
\mathbb{T}\times[0,1]
. In this setting, an essential new difficulty is the appearance of a “boundary layer” near
y\in\{0,1\}
, due to the mismatch between non-slip and non-penetration boundary conditions for the Navier Stokes and Euler equations, respectively.

Another interesting problem is to consider shear flows that are not necessarily monotonic, such as Kolmogorov flows. For such flows on the linear inviscid level, there is an additional physical phenomenon called “vorticity depletion” which refers to the asymptotic vanishing of vorticity as
t\to\infty
near the critical point where the derivative of the shear flow is zero, first predicted in
[
8
]
and proved in
[
56
]
. A similar phenomenon was proved in
[
5
]
for the case of vortices. See also
[
32
]
for a refined description of the dynamics as a step towards proving nonlinear vortex symmetrization. It is an important and very intriguing problem to understand, as precisely as possible, the interaction of inviscid damping, enhanced dissipation, vorticity depletion and boundary layers, in the high Reynolds number regime.

In the above problems, although our method here will not apply in a straightforward fashion due to various additional difficulties, the general idea of a direct comparison between Orr-Sommerfeld equation in the high Reynolds number regime and Rayleigh equation through the limiting absorption principle may be applicable.

It is also natural to ask whether the linear asymptotic stability results in the high Reynolds number regime can be extended to the full nonlinear problem, as in
[
7
]
for the Couette flow on
\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
. This is a very subtle and difficult problem in general, already at the inviscid level. In the setting considered in this paper, we believe that with additional work one can extend the nonlinear inviscid damping result to Navier Stokes equations with high Reynolds number, using the techniques developed here and in
[
29
]
.

3.
Pointwise bounds on the kernel of generalized Airy operators

In this section, we prove the following bounds on the kernel for the generalized Airy operator
\partial_{Y}^{2}+iV(Y)
where
V(Y)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is real valued and “non-stationary” in the sense that
|V^{\prime}(Y)|\in[\sigma,1/\sigma]
for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}
and some
\sigma\in(0,1)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that
\sigma\in(0,1)
,
V(Y)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
V(0)=0
and
|V^{\prime}(Y)|\in[\sigma,1/\sigma]
for all
Y\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
. Let
K(Y,Z)
be the fundamental solution to the generalized Airy operator
\partial_{Y}^{2}-\alpha+iV(Y)
. More precisely, for
Y,Z\in\mathbb{R}
, in the sense of distributions,

(3.1)

\partial_{Y}^{2}K(Y,Z)-\alpha K(Y,Z)+iV(Y)K(Y,Z)=\delta(Y-Z).

Then there exists
c_{0}\in(0,\infty)
depending only on
\sigma
such that

(3.2)

|K(Y,Z)|\lesssim_{\sigma}\frac{1}{\langle\alpha,Z\rangle^{1/2}}e^{-c_{0}\langle\alpha,Y,Z\rangle^{1/2}|Y-Z|},

and

(3.3)

|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|\lesssim_{\sigma}\frac{\langle\alpha,Y\rangle^{1/2}}{\langle\alpha,Z\rangle^{1/2}}e^{-c_{0}\langle\alpha,Y,Z\rangle^{1/2}|Y-Z|}.

In addition, for all
Z\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3.4)

|K(Z,Z)|\approx_{\sigma}\langle\alpha,Z\rangle^{-1/2}.

The rest of the section is devoted to the proof of Proposition
3.1
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
V^{\prime}(Y)\in[\sigma,1/\sigma]
.

3.1.
Global energy estimates

We begin with the following energy estimates.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have the energy bounds for all

we obtain that

(3.25)

\begin{split}&-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY\\
&-2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(Y)\varphi^{\prime}(Y)\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)\overline{K(Y,Z)}\,dY+i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)V(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY=0,\end{split}

and

(3.26)

\begin{split}&-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)V(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)V(Y)|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY\\
&-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big[2\varphi(Y)\varphi^{\prime}(Y)V(Y)+\varphi^{2}(Y)V^{\prime}(Y)\Big]\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)\overline{K(Y,Z)}\,dY\\
&+i\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)V^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY=0.\end{split}

We get from (
3.25
)-(
3.26
) that

(3.27)

\begin{split}&\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\\
&\leq 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(Y)\big|\varphi^{\prime}(Y)||\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)\overline{K(Y,Z)}|\,dY,\\
&\int_{\mathbb{R}}V^{2}(Y)\varphi^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big[2|\varphi(Y)\varphi^{\prime}(Y)V(Y)|+\varphi^{2}(Y)|V^{\prime}(Y)|\Big]\big|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)\overline{K(Y,Z)}\big|\,dY.\end{split}

It follows from equation (
3.1
) and the definition (
3.22
) that

(3.28)

\|\partial_{Y}^{2}K(Y,Z)\|_{L^{2}(Y\in[A,A+\langle\alpha,A\rangle^{-1/2}])}\lesssim_{\sigma}\langle\alpha,A\rangle\beta_{+}(A,Z),

and thus

(3.29)

\|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)\|_{L^{2}(Y\in[A,A+\langle\alpha,A\rangle^{-1/2}])}\lesssim_{\sigma}\langle\alpha,A\rangle^{1/2}\beta_{+}(A,Z).

We then conclude from (
3.27
) that

(3.30)

\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\lesssim_{\sigma}\langle\alpha,A\rangle(\beta_{+}(A,Z))^{2},

and from (
3.27
), (
3.30
), and using also Poincaré inequality that

(3.31)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)\langle Y\rangle^{2}|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\\
&\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)\langle Y\rangle^{2}|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY+C(\sigma)\Big[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\varphi^{2}(Y)}{\langle Y\rangle^{2}}|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY+\langle\alpha,A\rangle^{2}\big(\beta_{+}(A,Z)\big)^{2}\Big],\end{split}

which implies that

(3.32)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)\langle Y\rangle^{2}|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\lesssim_{\sigma}\langle\alpha,A\rangle^{2}\big(\beta_{+}(A,Z)\big)^{2}.

The desired bounds (
3.23
) follow from (
3.30
) and (
3.32
).
∎

3.3.
Entanglement inequalities

The following “entanglement inequalities” play an important role in our argument.

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the conditions that
A_{1}\leq A_{2}
, and either
[A_{1},A_{2}]\subseteq[0,\infty)
or
[A_{1},A_{2}]\subseteq(-\infty,0]
. Fix a nonnegative cutoff function
\varphi\in C^{1}([A_{1},A_{2}])
such that
\varphi(A_{1})=\varphi(A_{2})=0
. We extend the domain of
\varphi
to
\mathbb{R}
by setting
\varphi(A)=0
for
A\not\in[A_{1},A_{2}]
. Then we have the following bounds for some
c_{0}:=c_{0}(\sigma)\in(0,1)
,

(3.33)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big[|\varphi^{\prime}(Y)|^{2}-c^{2}_{0}\,\langle\alpha,Y\rangle|\varphi(Y)|^{2}\Big]|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\geq 0.

Remark 3.5
.

In applications below, we shall choose the cutoff function
\varphi
so that
|\varphi^{\prime}(Y)|^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\,\langle\alpha,Y\rangle|\varphi(Y)|^{2}=0
on
[A_{1},A_{2}]
except when

Y=A_{1}+O(\langle\alpha,A_{1}\rangle^{-1/2})\quad{\rm or}\quad Y=A_{2}+O(\langle\alpha,A_{2}\rangle^{-1/2}).

Then (
3.33
) implies the control of
K(Y,Z)
near
A_{1}
by
K(Y,Z)
around
A_{2}
, and vice versa. Since
[A_{1},A_{2}]
is a possibly very large interval, there is an “entanglement” in the behavior of
K(Y,Z)
near the two end points
Y=A_{1}
and
Y=A_{2}
, which inspires our choice of the terminology.

Proof.

Using energy estimates similar to (
3.25
) and noting that
V
does not change sign on
[A_{1},A_{2}]
, we obtain that

(3.34)

\begin{split}&\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY+\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)V(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\Big|\\
&\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(Y)\big|\varphi^{\prime}(Y)\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)\overline{K(Y,Z)}\big|\,dY.\end{split}

Therefore,

(3.35)

\begin{split}&\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|K(Y,Z)|^{2}dY+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)\langle Y\rangle|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}(Y)|\partial_{Y}K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\\
&\lesssim_{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi^{\prime}(Y)|^{2}|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY.\end{split}

It follows from (
3.35
) that for some
c_{0}(\sigma)\in(0,1)
,

(3.36)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big[|\varphi^{\prime}(Y)|^{2}-c^{2}_{0}\,\langle\alpha,Y\rangle|\varphi(Y)|^{2}\Big]|K(Y,Z)|^{2}\,dY\geq 0.

∎

4.

In this section, we prove precise estimates on the kernel of the generalized Airy operator in Gevrey spaces, in the high Reynolds number regime. To obtain the refined regularity structure in the study of the main Orr-Sommerfeld equation (

\widehat{\,Af\,}(\xi):=e^{\delta_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}},\qquad{\rm for}\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

We decompose, using the Gevrey cutoff function

\begin{split}&e^{\delta_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}}\big|\widehat{\,\,k^{1}_{\epsilon,w_{0}}}(v,\rho;\xi)\big|\\

Define for
\gamma,\alpha,w\in\mathbb{R}

(4.41)

E_{xp}(\gamma,\alpha,w):=e^{-\epsilon/3(B^{\ast}(w))^{2}[\gamma^{3}-\alpha^{3}]+i(\epsilon/2)B^{\ast}\partial_{w}B^{\ast}(w)[\gamma^{2}-\alpha^{2}]}.

\begin{split}&\widetilde{\,\,k_{\epsilon}^{2}}(\alpha,\beta;w)=-\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}(B^{\ast}(w))^{2}\Big[\widehat{\,\,\Psi_{1}}(\gamma+\beta)+i\epsilon^{1/3}\widehat{\,\Psi\,}(\gamma+\beta)\Big]E_{xp}(\gamma,\alpha,w)\,d\gamma\\

5.
Limiting absorption principle for the Orr-Sommerfeld equation

In this section we prove the limiting absorption principle. We begin with the following bounds for solutions to the equation

(5.1)

\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w-\alpha w+i(b(y_{0})-b(y))w=f(y),\quad y\in\mathbb{R}.

In the above, we assume that
0<|\epsilon|<1/8
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
\epsilon\alpha\geq 0
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
0<|\epsilon|<1/8
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
\epsilon\alpha\geq 0
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
,
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Suppose that
w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(\cdot,y_{0}),\psi_{\epsilon,\alpha}(\cdot,y_{0})\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
are the solution to the equations for
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.2)

\begin{split}&\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-\alpha w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})+i(b(y_{0})-b(y))w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})=f(y),\\
&-(k^{2}-\partial_{y}^{2})\psi_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})=w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0}).\end{split}

Then we have the following conclusions.

(i) We have the energy estimates

(5.3)

\|(y-y_{0})w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}+\epsilon^{1/3}\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}+\epsilon^{2/3}\|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})};

(ii) Denoting
m:=\|h\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}
, we have the pointwise bounds for
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.4)

\begin{split}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|&\lesssim|\epsilon|^{-1/3}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-1}m,\\
|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|&\lesssim\Big[|\epsilon|^{-1/2}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-3/4}+|\epsilon|^{-2/3}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-2}\Big]m,\\
|\partial^{2}_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|&\lesssim\Big[|\epsilon|^{-5/6}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-1/4}+|\epsilon|^{-1}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-3/2}\Big]m;\end{split}

(iii) We have the bounds for the “stream function”
\psi_{\epsilon,\alpha}
,

(5.5)

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\Big[\langle k,\xi\rangle^{2}\big|\widehat{\,\,\psi_{\epsilon,\alpha}}(\xi,y_{0})\big|\Big]\lesssim\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})};

(iv) In addition, we have the limiting behaviors

•

If
\alpha_{0}\neq 0
,

(5.6)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0,\,z\to y_{0},\,\alpha\to\alpha_{0},\,\epsilon\alpha>0}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)=\frac{-f(y)}{\alpha_{0}+i(b(y)-b(y_{0}))};

•

(5.7)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+,\,\alpha\to 0+,\,z\to y_{0}}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)={\rm P.V.}\frac{-f(y)}{i(b(y)-b(y_{0}))}-\pi\frac{f(y_{0})}{b^{\prime}(y_{0})}\delta(y-y_{0}),

for
y\in\mathbb{R}
, in the sense of distributions.

•

Similarly for
y\in\mathbb{R}
, in the sense of distributions for
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.8)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0-,\,\alpha\to 0-,\,z\to y_{0}}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)={\rm P.V.}\frac{-f(y)}{i(b(y)-b(y_{0}))}+\pi\frac{f(y_{0})}{b^{\prime}(y_{0})}\delta(y-y_{0}).

Remark 5.2
.

We note that the bound

(5.9)

|\epsilon||\partial^{2}_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|\lesssim\Big[|\epsilon|^{1/6}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-1/4}+\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-3/2}\Big]\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}

implies that the singular perturbation term
\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w
is “negligible” outside the “critical region”
|y-y_{0}|\lesssim|\epsilon|^{1/3}
, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
.

Proof.

For concreteness, we assume that
0<\epsilon<1/8
and correspondingly
\alpha\geq 0
, as the other case is completely analogous. We organize the proof into several steps.

Step 1: Proof of (
5.3
).
We first give the proof of (
5.3
). By integration by parts, we have

(5.10)

\begin{split}&-\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}\,dy+i\int_{\mathbb{R}}(b(y_{0})-b(y))|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)\overline{w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})}\,dy,\\
&-\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}(b(y_{0})-b(y))|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy-\alpha\int_{\mathbb{R}}(b(y_{0})-b(y))|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\\
&+\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})b^{\prime}(y)\overline{w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})}\,dy+i\int_{\mathbb{R}}|b(y)-b(y_{0})|^{2}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\\

It follows from (

\begin{split}&\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\leq\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})},\\
&\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y-y_{0}|^{2}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\\
&\lesssim\epsilon\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})||w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|\,dy+\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\||y-y_{0}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}.\end{split}

\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\||y-y_{0}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}\|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})},

\begin{split}\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}&\lesssim\||y-y_{0}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}\|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}\\
&\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/4}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/4}\||y-y_{0}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}^{1/4},\end{split}

which implies that

(5.14)

\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/3}\||y-y_{0}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}^{2/3}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/3}.

It follows from (

) and (

(5.15)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y-y_{0}|^{2}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy+\epsilon^{4/3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\\

&\qquad+\epsilon^{1/3}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\||y-y_{0}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|^{1/2}_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}\|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\|^{1/2}_{L^{2}(y\in\mathbb{R})}\\
&\lesssim_{\sigma}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}|y-y_{0}|^{2}|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy+\epsilon^{4/3}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\partial_{y}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|^{2}dy\bigg]^{1/2}.\end{split}

(5.17)

\begin{split}&\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-\alpha w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-ib^{\prime}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\\
&=f(y)-i\big[b^{\prime}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})-(b(y)-b(y_{0}))\big]w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0}),\end{split}

and decompose for
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.18)

w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0}):=w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})+w_{2\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0}),

where
w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}
and
w_{2\epsilon,\alpha}
solve the equations

(5.19)

\begin{split}&\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-\alpha w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-ib^{\prime}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})=f(y_{0}),\\
&\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w_{2\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-\alpha w_{2\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})-ib^{\prime}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})w_{2\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})\\
&=f(y)-f(y_{0})-i\big[b^{\prime}(y_{0})(y-y_{0})-(b(y)-b(y_{0}))\big]w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0}).\\
\end{split}

It follows from the kernel estimates (
4.11
),
\|f\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=1
, and the bound (
5.4
) that for
y\in\mathbb{R}
with
|y-y_{0}|<1
,

(5.20)

|w_{2\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/6}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}(y-y_{0}),\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha\rangle^{-1/2}.

By rescaling, we have for
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.21)

w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y)=\epsilon^{-1/3}f(y_{0})[b^{\prime}(y_{0})]^{-2/3}W\big(\epsilon^{-1/3}(b^{\prime}(y_{0}))^{1/3}(y-y_{0})\big),

where
W
satisfies the equation for
Y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.22)

\partial_{Y}^{2}W(Y)-\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha(b^{\prime}(y_{0}))^{-2/3}W(Y)-iYW(Y)=1.

In the above we have suppressed the dependence of
W
on
\epsilon,\alpha
. Equation (
5.22
) can be solved explicitly using Fourier transforms, and we have

(5.23)

\widehat{W}(\xi)=-\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{\xi^{3}/3+\epsilon^{-1/3}\alpha(b^{\prime}(y_{0}))^{-2/3}\xi}\,{\bf 1}_{(-\infty,0]}(\xi),\qquad{\rm for}\,\,\xi\in\mathbb{R}.

The bounds (
5.3
)-(
5.4
), the decomposition (
5.18
) and the bounds (
5.20
), the identity (
5.21
) and the bound (
5.23
) imply that

(5.24)

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}|\widehat{\,\,w_{\epsilon,\alpha}}(\xi)|\lesssim_{\sigma}1.

The desired bound (
5.5
) follows from (
5.24
).

Step 4: Proof of (
5.6
)-(
5.8
).
We first note that (
5.6
) follows from the bound (
5.4
) and the equation (
5.2
).

We now turn to the proof of the limit (
5.7
). By equation (
5.2
) and the bounds (
5.4
), we only need to consider the region
|y-z|<1/2
. We fix an even cutoff function
\Phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(-2,2)
with
\Phi\equiv 1
on
[-1,1]
. For any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, we calculate, using the bound (
5.4
), equation (
5.2
) and (
5.18
)-(
5.23
) that for any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(-1,1)
,

(5.25)

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon,\alpha\to 0+,z\to y_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)\varphi(y)\,dy=\lim_{\epsilon,\alpha\to 0+,z\to y_{0}}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{-f(y)\varphi(y)}{i(b(y)-b(z))}(1-\Phi((y-z)/(\epsilon+\alpha)^{1/4}))\,dy\\
&\hskip 224.03743pt+\varphi(y_{0})\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)\Phi((y-z)/(\epsilon+\alpha)^{1/4})\,dy\bigg],\end{split}

and therefore,

(5.26)

\begin{split}&\lim_{\epsilon,\alpha\to 0+,z\to y_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)\varphi(y)\,dy-{\rm P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{-f(y)\varphi(y)}{i(b(y)-b(y_{0}))}\,dy\\
&=\varphi(y_{0})\lim_{\epsilon,\alpha\to 0+,z\to y_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}w_{1\epsilon,\alpha}(y,z)\Phi((y-z)/(\epsilon+\alpha)^{1/4})\,dy\\
&=\varphi(y_{0})f(y_{0})[b^{\prime}(y_{0})]^{-2/3}\lim_{\epsilon,\alpha\to 0+,z\to y_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\epsilon^{-1/3}W\big(\epsilon^{-1/3}(b^{\prime}(z))^{1/3}(y-z)\big)\Phi\big((y-z)/(\epsilon+\alpha)^{1/4}\big)\,dy\\
&=-\sqrt{2\pi}\,\varphi(y_{0})\frac{f(y_{0})}{b^{\prime}(y_{0})}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\widehat{\,\Phi\,}(\xi)\,d\xi=-\pi\varphi(y_{0})\frac{f(y_{0})}{b^{\prime}(y_{0})}.\end{split}

We note that the cutoff length we used,
\epsilon^{1/4}
, is somewhat arbitrary, and we can use any cutoff scale
d
with
\max\{\epsilon^{1/3},\alpha\}\ll d\ll 1
, to separate the critical region which is of the size
\epsilon^{1/3}
from the more regular region. Since
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(-1,1)
is arbitrary, we obtain the desired identity (
5.25
).
∎

For
\epsilon\in(-1/8,1/8)\backslash\{0\}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
\epsilon\alpha\geq 0
and
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, we define the operator
T_{\epsilon,\alpha,y_{0}}:H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
as follows. For any
f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, suppose
w_{\epsilon,\alpha},\psi_{\epsilon,\alpha}
are given as in (
5.2
). Then we define

(5.27)

T_{\epsilon,\alpha,y_{0}}f(y):=\psi_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0}),\qquad{\rm for}\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}.

The bounds (
5.4
)-(

) imply that

is compact.

We are now ready to establish the following limiting absorption principle which plays a fundamental role in controlling the solutions to the Orr-Sommerfeld equation in the high Reynolds number regime.

Proposition 5.3
.

There exist
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
\kappa>0
, such that for all
\epsilon\in(-\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{0})\backslash\{0\}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with
\epsilon\alpha\geq 0
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
,
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we have the bound

(5.28)

\|\psi+T_{\epsilon,\alpha,y_{0}}(ib^{\prime\prime}\psi)\|_{H_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\geq\kappa\|\psi\|_{H_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

Proof.

For the sake of concreteness, we assume that
\epsilon>0
, as the other case is completely analogous. By the bound (
5.5
) we can assume that
k=k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
. For
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, assume that
w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(\cdot,y_{0})\in H^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is the solution to

\epsilon\partial_{y}^{2}w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})+i(b(y_{0})-b(y))w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})=-ib^{\prime\prime}(y)\psi(y),\quad{\rm for}\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}.

The kernel estimates (
4.11
) and the support assumption
{\rm supp}\,b^{\prime\prime}\Subset[-1/\sigma_{0},1/\sigma_{0}]
imply that for some
c_{0}=c_{0}(\sigma)\in(0,1)
and all
|y_{0}|>2/\sigma_{0}
,

(5.29)

|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}(y,y_{0})|\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/2}\langle y_{0}\rangle^{-1/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-c_{0}\epsilon^{-1/2}|y_{0}|^{1/2}|y-z|}|\psi(z)|\,dz,\quad y\in\mathbb{R}.

It follows from (
5.29
) that

(5.30)

\|w_{\epsilon,\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim\langle y_{0}\rangle^{-1}\|\psi\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R})}.

The desired bounds (
5.28
) then follow if
|y_{0}|\gg 1/\sigma_{0}
. Thus we can assume that
|y_{0}|\lesssim 1
. Similarly, by the bound (
5.4
) we can assume that
0<\alpha\lesssim 1
. In summary, it suffices to consider the case

(5.31)

k=k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\},\quad|y_{0}|\lesssim 1,\quad 0<\alpha\lesssim 1.

Suppose the inequality (
5.28
) does not hold, then we can find a sequence
\epsilon_{j}\to 0+
,
0<\alpha_{j}\lesssim 1
,
y_{0j}\in\mathbb{R}
with
|y_{0j}|\lesssim 1
, and
\psi_{j}\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|\psi_{j}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=1
for
j\geq 1
, such that

(5.32)

\|\psi_{j}+T_{\epsilon_{j},\alpha_{j},y_{0j}}(ib^{\prime\prime}\psi_{j})\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\to 0+,\qquad{\rm as}\,\,j\to\infty.

By (
5.31
) and the bound (
5.5
), we can assume (by passing to a subsequence) that for some
\psi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|\psi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=1
,

(5.33)

y_{0j}\to y_{0},\quad\alpha_{j}\to\alpha\geq 0,\quad{\rm and}\quad\lim_{j\to\infty}\|\psi_{j}-\psi\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=0.

We conclude from (
5.32
)-(
5.33
) that

(5.34)

\psi(y)+i\lim_{j\to\infty}T_{\epsilon_{j},\alpha_{j},y_{0j}}(b^{\prime\prime}\psi)(y)=0,\qquad{\rm for}\,\,y\in\mathbb{R}.

We distinguish two cases,
\alpha=0
and
\alpha>0
, and focus first on the case
\alpha=0
.
Applying the differential operator
-k^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2}
to (
5.34
), and using the identity (
5.7
), we get that in the sense of distributions,

(5.35)

(-k^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\psi(y)-{\rm P.V.}\frac{b^{\prime\prime}(y)\psi(y)}{b(y)-b(y_{0})}-i\pi\frac{b^{\prime\prime}(y_{0})}{b^{\prime}(y_{0})}\psi(y_{0})\delta(y-y_{0})=0.

Multiplying
\overline{\psi}
, integrating over
\mathbb{R}
and taking the real part, we conclude that

(5.36)

b^{\prime\prime}(y_{0})\psi(y_{0})=0.

Hence

(5.37)

w:=(-k^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}),

and

(5.38)

(b(y)-b(y_{0}))w-b^{\prime\prime}(y)\psi=0.

(
5.38
) implies that
w\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
is an eigenfunction for the eigenvalue
b(y_{0})
of the operator
L_{k}
, which is a contradiction to our assumption that there are no discrete eigenvalues for
L_{k}
in our main assumption
1.1
.

The case
\alpha>0
is similar, and we can obtain a contradiction, using the identity (
5.6
) to get an eigenvalue
b(y_{0})+i\alpha
for
L_{k}
, and our assumption that there is no discrete eigenvalues for
L_{k}
. The theorem is now proved.

Recall the definitions (

Proposition 6.1

Proof.

Lemma 6.2

Lemma 6.3

The spectral representation formula

In this section, we justify the representation formula (
1.8
) for
\omega_{0k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. The basic idea is to use the general theory of sectorial operators, and we refer to section 4, Chapter II of
[
17
]
for the well known approach. The main properties we need to establish are the following.

Proposition 7.1
.

Assume that
k\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
and
\omega_{0k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. There exists
\nu_{\ast}\in(0,1)
, such that the following statement holds. Define

(7.1)

\Sigma:=\big\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\,k\,\Re\lambda\leq 0\big\}.

For any
\lambda\in\Sigma
and
\nu\in(0,\nu_{\ast})
, the following system of equations for

,

(7.2)

\begin{split}\frac{\nu}{k}\partial_{y}^{2}h(y,\lambda)-(ib(y)-\lambda)h(y,\lambda)+ib^{\prime\prime}(y)\varphi(y,\lambda)&=\omega_{0k}(y),\\
(-k^{2}+\partial_{y}^{2})\varphi(y,\lambda)&=h(y,\lambda),\end{split}

is solvable for a unique solution

which is holomorphic for
\lambda
in the interior of
\Sigma
and continuous on
\Sigma

(7.3)

\big\|h(\cdot,\lambda)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{\nu,\omega_{0k}}\frac{1}{\langle\lambda\rangle},

(7.4)

\big\|\partial_{\lambda}h(\cdot,\lambda)\big\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{\nu,\omega_{0k}}\frac{1}{\langle\lambda\rangle^{2}}

We note that in the bounds (
7.3
)-(
7.4
) we allow the implied constants to depend on the small viscosity
\nu>0
and an unspecified norm on
\omega_{0k}
, which is not a problem for us as we only use these bounds in a qualitative way, to justify the identity (
1.8
).

Proof.

Assume for the sake of concreteness
k>0
and correspondingly
\Re\lambda\leq 0
. By Propositions
5.1
and
5.3
, the equation (
7.2
) is solvable and we have the bound

(7.5)

\|\varphi(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{\omega_{0k}}1,\quad\|h(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{\nu,\omega_{0k}}1.

4.2
and the assumption on the compact support of

\omega_{0k}

\|h(\cdot,\lambda)\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim_{\nu,\omega_{0k}}\langle\lambda\rangle^{-1},

\omega_{0k}

(7.7)

|h(y,\lambda)|\lesssim_{\nu,\omega_{0k}}e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\lambda\rangle^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/3}\langle y\rangle}.

8.
Decay of semigroups using resolvent bounds

In this section we prove a quantitative bound on the decay of bounded semigroups using resolvent estimates, which is of independent interest. The main theorem and its proof are modifications of a similar result in Wei
[
57
]
(see also the earlier work of Helffer and Sjöstrand
[
26
]
, and the more recent
[
27
]
which contained a more general form the estimates below, with sharp constants). The key difference with
[
57
]
is that we do not assume the operator which generates the semigroup to be “accretive”, and instead require the semigroup to be bounded to begin with. The formulation allows a wider range of applications than semigroups generated by accretive operators.

Theorem 8.1
.

Assume that
\mu>0,M\geq 1
. Suppose that
X
is a separable Hilbert space, and the linear operator
A:\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq X\to X
is densely defined and closed. Assume that
A
generates the bounded semigroup
e^{At}
,
t\geq 0
with the bound

(8.1)

\left\|e^{At}\right\|_{X\to X}\leq M.

Assume that for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
i\lambda-A:\mathcal{D}(A)\to X
is invertible and we have the resolvent bound

(8.2)

\left\|(i\lambda-A)^{-1}\right\|_{X\to X}\leq\mu^{-1}.

Then we have the decay estimates for the semigroup
e^{At},t\geq 0
,

(8.3)

\left\|e^{At}\right\|_{X\to X}\leq C_{0}M^{2}e^{-\mu t},

for a universal constant
C_{0}>0
.

Proof.

By renormalization, we can assume that
\mu=1
. Assume that
C_{0}\geq 10
is sufficiently large, then in view of the bound (
8.1
) it suffices to consider
t\geq 20
. We choose a positive number
L\geq 2
whose value is to be determined below. For
t\geq 5
, fix
\varphi\in C^{1}([0,t+L])
with
\varphi(0)=\varphi(t+L)=0
. For any
x\in\mathcal{D}(A)
with
\|x\|_{X}=1
, set

(8.4)

g(t):=e^{At}x.

Direct computation shows
\varphi(t)g(t)
satisfies the equation

(8.5)

\partial_{t}\big(\varphi(t)g(t)\big)-A\big(\varphi(t)g(t)\big)=\varphi^{\prime}(t)g(t).

Taking Fourier transform in
t\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain that

(8.6)

(i\tau-A)\widehat{\,\varphi g\,}(\tau)=\widehat{\,\varphi^{\prime}g\,}(\tau),\qquad\tau\in\mathbb{R}.

From (
8.6
) we conclude that for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}

(8.7)

\left\|\widehat{\,\varphi g\,}(\tau)\right\|_{X}=\left\|(i\tau-A)^{-1}\widehat{\,\varphi^{\prime}g\,}(\tau)\right\|_{X}\leq\left\|\widehat{\,\varphi^{\prime}g\,}(\tau)\right\|_{X},

which implies (by Parseval’s identity) that

(8.8)

\int_{0}^{t+L}|\varphi(s)|^{2}\|g(s)\|_{X}^{2}\,ds\leq\int_{0}^{t+L}|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\|g(s)\|_{X}^{2}\,ds.

The inequality (
8.8
), which is analogous to the entanglement inequality (
3.33
), is very powerful in the sense that by appropriately choosing
\varphi
, it allows to connect the bounds on
g(s)
for
s
near
t

with bounds for
g(s)
near the initial time
s=0
.

Define
\varphi(s)
for
s\in[0,t+L]
as

(8.9)

\varphi(s)=s{\bf 1}_{[0,1]}(s)+e^{s-1}{\bf 1}_{[1,t]}(s)+e^{t-1}\big(1-\frac{s-t}{L}\big){\bf 1}_{[t,t+L]}(s).

By (
8.8
), we have

(8.10)

\int_{t}^{t+L}\big(|\varphi(s)|^{2}-|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\big)\|g(s)\|_{X}^{2}\,ds\leq M^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s^{2})\,ds\leq M^{2}.

For
s\in[t,t+L]
,

(8.11)

|\varphi(s)|^{2}-|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}=e^{2(t-1)}\Big[\big(1-\frac{s-t}{L}\big)^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\Big],

therefore using the semigroup bound (
8.1
), we have the bound

(8.12)

\begin{split}&\int_{t}^{t+L}\big(|\varphi(s)|^{2}-|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\big)\|g(s)\|_{X}^{2}\,ds\\
&\geq\int_{t}^{t+1}M^{-2}\|g(t+1)\|^{2}_{X}\big(|\varphi(s)|^{2}-|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\big)\,ds-\int_{t+L-1}^{t+L}\big||\varphi(s)|^{2}-|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\big|M^{2}\|g(t+1)\|_{X}^{2}\,ds\\
&\geq\|g(t+1)\|_{X}^{2}e^{2(t-1)}\Big\{\frac{1}{M^{2}}\Big((1-\frac{1}{L})^{2}-\frac{1}{L^{2}}\Big)-\frac{M^{2}}{L^{2}}\Big\}.\end{split}

Taking
L\geq 10M^{2}
, then we obtain that

(8.13)

\int_{t}^{t+L}\big(|\varphi(s)|^{2}-|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|^{2}\big)\|g(s)\|_{X}^{2}\,ds\geq\frac{1}{20M^{2}}\|g(t+1)\|_{X}^{2}e^{2(t-1)},

which together with (
8.10
) implies that

(8.14)

\|g(t+1)\|_{X}^{2}e^{2(t-1)}\leq 20M^{4}.

Consequently we obtain that

(8.15)

\|g(t+1)\|_{X}^{2}\leq 20e^{2}M^{4}e^{-2t}.

The theorem is proved.

∎

9.
Proof of the main theorem

In this section we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
, using Proposition
1.4
. Denote
\epsilon=\nu/k
. For the sake of concreteness, we assume that
\epsilon>0
, as the other case is completely analogous.

We begin with the proof of (
1.27
)-(
1.28
). Due to the similarity in the argument, we shall focus only on the proof of (
1.28
) which is slightly more complicated. We use the representation formula (
1.16
), the definition (
4.1
) for the kernel
k_{\epsilon}
and equation (
1.23
) to obtain that

(9.1)

\begin{split}F_{k}(t,v)=&-\frac{i}{2\pi}e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ik(v-w)t}k_{\epsilon}(v-w,\rho;w)(\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}/B^{\ast})(\rho+w)\frac{\Theta_{k,\nu}(\rho,w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw\\
&+\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ik(v-w)t}k_{\epsilon}(v-w,\rho;w)\frac{1}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+w))^{2}}\frac{F_{0k}(\rho+w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw\\
=&-\frac{i}{2\pi}e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikw^{\prime}t}k_{\epsilon}(w^{\prime},\rho;v-w^{\prime})(\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}/B^{\ast})(\rho+v-w^{\prime})\frac{\Theta_{k,\nu}(\rho,v-w^{\prime})}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw^{\prime}\\
&+\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikw^{\prime}t}k_{\epsilon}(w^{\prime},\rho;v-w^{\prime})\frac{1}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+v-w^{\prime}))^{2}}\frac{F_{0k}(\rho+v-w^{\prime})}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw^{\prime},\end{split}

for
v\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
. Recall the Gevrey cutoff function
\Psi
from (
4.2
) and
\Psi^{\ast}
from (
6.9
). Define the localization functions for
w,\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.2)

q_{N}(w,\rho):=1-\Psi(\rho),\quad q_{L}(w,\rho):=(1-\Psi^{\ast}(w))\Psi(\rho),\quad q(w,\rho):=\Psi^{\ast}(w)\Psi(\rho).

We define for
\dagger\in\{N,L\}
and
v\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0
,

(9.3)

S_{k}^{\dagger}(t,v):=-\frac{ie^{-\nu k^{2}t}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}k_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)(\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}/B^{\ast})(\rho+v-w)q_{\dagger}(w,\rho)\frac{\Theta_{k,\nu}(\rho,v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw,

and for
\dagger\in\{1,2\}
and
v\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0

(9.4)

S_{k}^{\dagger}(t,v):=-\frac{ie^{-\nu k^{2}t}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}k^{\dagger}_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)(\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}/B^{\ast})(\rho+v-w)q(w,\rho)\frac{\Theta_{k,\nu}(\rho,v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw.

Define also for
v\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0
,

(9.5)

I_{k}^{N}(t,v):=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}k_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)\frac{1-\Psi(\rho-w)}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+v-w))^{2}}\frac{F_{0k}(\rho+v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw,

and for
v\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0

(9.6)

I_{k}^{L}(t,v):=\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}k_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)\frac{\Psi(\rho-w)}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+v-w))^{2}}\frac{F_{0k}(\rho+v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw.

We can decompose

(9.7)

F_{k}(t,v)=\sum_{\dagger\in\{N,L,1,2\}}S_{k}^{\dagger}(t,v)+\sum_{\dagger\in\{N,L\}}I_{k}^{\dagger}(t,v).

It suffices to prove that for
h_{k}\in\{S_{k}^{\dagger}:\dagger\in\{N,L,1,2\}\}
and
h_{k}\in\{I_{k}^{N},I_{k}^{L}\}
,

(9.8)

\left\|e^{\delta\langle k,\xi\rangle^{1/2}}\widehat{\,\,h_{k}}(t,\xi)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\left\|e^{\delta\langle k,\xi\rangle^{1/2}}\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}}(\xi)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

For notational conveniences, we denote for
w,\rho\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.9)

E_{\epsilon,\delta_{0}}(\rho,w):=e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho,\epsilon^{-1/3}w\rangle^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/3}|w-\rho|}

For
h_{k}\in\{S_{k}^{N},S_{k}^{L},S_{k}^{1}\}
, we can bound, using (
4.5
), (
4.8
), and the fact that
\partial_{v}B^{\ast}
is compactly supported, that for
t\geq 0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.10)

\begin{split}|\widehat{\,\,h_{k}}(t,\xi)|\lesssim e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\epsilon^{-1/3}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho,\epsilon^{-1/3}w\rangle^{1/2}E_{\epsilon,\delta_{0}}(\rho,w)e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\alpha\rangle^{1/2}}\frac{|\widehat{\,\,\,\Theta_{k,\nu}}(\rho,\xi-\alpha)|}{\langle\rho\rangle}d\rho dwd\alpha.\end{split}

The desired bounds (
9.8
) for
h_{k}\in\{S_{k}^{N},S_{k}^{L},S_{k}^{1}\}
then follow from (
9.10
) and the bounds (
1.30
).

For
h_{k}=S_{k}^{2}
, we take Fourier transform and obtain that

(9.11)

|\widehat{\,\,h_{k}}(t,\xi)|\lesssim e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\left|\widetilde{\,\,k_{\epsilon,0}^{2}}(\xi-kt,\alpha_{1};\beta)\right||\widetilde{(B^{\ast}\partial B^{\ast})}(\gamma)|\left|\widetilde{\,\,\Theta_{k,\nu}}(-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}-\gamma,\xi-\beta-\gamma)\right|d\Xi,

where
d\Xi=d\alpha_{1}d\alpha_{2}d\beta d\gamma
. The desired bound (
9.8
) then follows from the bounds (
1.30
) and (
4.9
).

We now turn to the terms
h_{k}\in\{I_{k}^{N},I_{k}^{L}\}
. Fix a nonnegative Gevrey-regular partition of unity function
\Psi^{p}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(-2,2)
. More precisely we impose

(9.12)

\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Psi^{p}(v-j)\equiv 1,\quad{\rm for}\,\,v\in\mathbb{R},

and

(9.13)

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\Big[e^{\langle\xi\rangle^{3/4}}|\widehat{\,\,\Psi^{p}}(\xi)|\Big]\lesssim 1.

Denote for
v\in\mathbb{R}
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(9.14)

F_{0k}^{j}(v):=F_{0k}(v)\Psi^{\ast\ast}(v-j)/(B^{\ast}(v))^{2}.

We first treat the case when
h_{k}=I_{k}^{N}
. Define for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t\geq 0
and
v\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.15)

\begin{split}&2\pi e^{\nu k^{2}t}I_{k,j}^{N}(t,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}k_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)\Psi^{p}(v-j)\frac{1-\Psi(\rho-w)}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+v-w))^{2}}\frac{F_{0k}(\rho+v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw\\
&=\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}k_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)\Psi^{p}(v-j)\big[1-\Psi(\rho-w)\big]\Psi(\rho-w-\ell)\frac{F^{j+\ell}_{0k}(\rho+v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw.\end{split}

Taking Fourier transform in
v
in (
9.15
) and using the bound (
4.5
), we obtain that for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\geq 0
,

(9.16)

\begin{split}&e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\big|\widehat{\,\,I_{k,j}^{N}}(t,\xi)\big|\\
&\lesssim\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}|\widehat{\,\,k_{\epsilon,j+\ell-\rho}}(w,\rho;\alpha)|\big[1-\Psi(\rho-w)\big]\Psi(\rho-w-\ell)\frac{|\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j+\ell}}(\xi-\alpha)|}{|\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}|}d\rho dwd\alpha\\
&\lesssim\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}e^{-(\delta_{0}/2)\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}w,\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho\rangle^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/3}-\delta_{0}\langle\alpha\rangle^{1/2}}{\bf 1}_{[-10,10]}(\rho-w-\ell)\big|\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j+\ell}}(\xi-\alpha)\big|\,d\rho dwd\alpha\end{split}

The desired bound (
9.8
) then follow from (
9.16
).

Finally we treat the most difficult case when
h_{k}=I_{k}^{L}
. We need a more precise understanding of the singularity of
k_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)
. Decompose for
v,\rho,w\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.17)

k_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w):=m_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)+n_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w),

where
m_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)
satisfies for
v,\rho,w\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.18)

\begin{split}&\Big[\epsilon\partial_{v}^{2}+\epsilon\frac{\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}{B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}\partial_{v}-i\frac{v}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+w))^{2}}\Big]m_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)\\
&=\Big[\epsilon\frac{\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}{B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}\partial_{v}-\epsilon\frac{\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}(v+w)}{B^{\ast}(v+w)}\partial_{v}-i\frac{v}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+w))^{2}}+i\frac{v}{(B^{\ast}(v+w))^{2}}\Big]k_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w),\end{split}

and
n_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)
satisfies for
v,\rho,w\in\mathbb{R}

(9.19)

\begin{split}\Big[\epsilon\partial_{v}^{2}+\epsilon\frac{\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}{B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}\partial_{v}-i\frac{v}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+w))^{2}}\Big]n_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)=(\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3})\delta(v-\rho).\end{split}

For any
w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
v,\rho,w\in\mathbb{R}
, we set

(9.20)

m_{\epsilon,w_{0}}(v,\rho;w):=m_{\epsilon}(v,\rho;w)\Psi(w-w_{0}).

It follows from the bound (
4.5
) that for
v,\rho,w,w_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.21)

\big|\widehat{\,\,m_{\epsilon,w_{0}}}(v,\rho;\xi)\big|\lesssim\epsilon^{-1/3}|v-\rho|\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho\rangle^{1/2}e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho,\epsilon^{-1/3}v\rangle^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1/3}|v-\rho|}e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\xi\rangle^{1/2}}.

To study the property of
n_{\epsilon}
, we take Fourier transform in
v
in the equation (
9.19
) and obtain that for
\rho,w,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.22)

\Big[-\epsilon\xi^{2}+i\epsilon\xi\frac{\partial_{\rho}B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}{B^{\ast}(\rho+w)}+\frac{\partial_{\xi}}{(B^{\ast}(\rho+w))^{2}}\Big]\widehat{\,\,n_{\epsilon}}(\xi,\rho;w)=(\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3})e^{-i\rho\xi}.

Therefore, recalling the definition (
4.41
), we get that for
\rho,w,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.23)

\begin{split}\widehat{\,\,n_{\epsilon}}(\xi,\rho;w)&=-(\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3})\int_{\xi}^{\infty}e^{-(\epsilon/3)(\gamma^{3}-\xi^{3})(B^{\ast}(\rho+w))^{2}+i(\epsilon/2)(\gamma^{2}-\xi^{2})(B^{\ast}\partial B^{\ast})(\rho+w)}e^{-i\rho\gamma}\,d\gamma\\
&=-(\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3})\int_{\xi}^{\infty}E_{xp}(\gamma,\xi,\rho+w)e^{-i\rho\gamma}\,d\gamma.\end{split}

Define for
q\in\{m,n\}
,

(9.24)

I_{k}^{q}(t,v):=\frac{e^{-\nu k^{2}t}}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{ikwt}q_{\epsilon}(w,\rho;v-w)\Psi(\rho-w)\frac{F_{0k}(\rho+v-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\,d\rho dw.

We decompose for
t\geq 0,v\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.25)

I_{k}^{L}(t,v)=I_{k}^{m}(t,v)+I_{k}^{n}(t,v),

and set for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(9.26)

I_{k,j}^{m}(t,v):=I_{k}^{m}(t,v)\Psi(v-j),\quad I_{k,j}^{n}(t,v):=I_{k}^{n}(t,v)\Psi(v-j).

It suffices to prove (
9.8
) for
h_{k}\in\{I_{k}^{m},I_{k}^{n}\}
.

Taking Fourier transform in
v
in (
9.24
) and using the estimates (
9.21
), we obtain that for
t\geq 0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(9.27)

\begin{split}&e^{\nu k^{2}t}\big|\widehat{\,\,I_{k,j}^{m}}(t,\xi)\big|\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\big|\widehat{\,\,m_{\epsilon,j-\rho}}(w,\rho;\alpha)\big|\Psi(\rho-w)\frac{\big|\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j}}(\xi-\alpha)\big|}{|\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}|}\,d\rho dwd\alpha\\
&\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\epsilon^{-2/3}|\rho-w|\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho\rangle^{-1/2}\Psi(\rho-w)E_{\epsilon,\delta_{0}}(\rho,w)e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\alpha\rangle^{1/2}}\big|\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j}}(\xi-\alpha)\big|\,d\rho dwd\alpha.\end{split}

It follows from (
9.27
) that for
t\geq 0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,

(9.28)

\big|\widehat{\,\,I_{k,j}^{m}}(t,\xi)\big|\lesssim e^{-\nu k^{2}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\langle\epsilon^{-1/3}\rho\rangle^{-3/2}e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\alpha\rangle^{1/2}}\big|\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j}}(\xi-\alpha)\big|\,d\rho d\alpha.

The desired bounds follow from (
9.28
) in the case of
I_{k}^{m}
.

We finally turn to the term
I^{n}_{k}
, which (using (
9.23
)) can be reformulated as

(9.29)

\begin{split}&2\pi e^{\nu k^{2}t}I_{k,j}^{n}(t,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\widehat{\,\,n_{\epsilon}}(\alpha,\rho;v-w)e^{ikwt+i\alpha w}\frac{\Psi(\rho-w)}{\rho+i\epsilon^{1/3}}\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j}}(\beta)e^{i\beta(\rho+v-w)}\,d\rho dwd\alpha d\beta\\
&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\int_{\alpha}^{\infty}\Psi^{\ast}(v-j)E_{xp}(\gamma,\alpha,\rho+v-w)e^{-i\rho\gamma}e^{ikwt+i\alpha w}\Psi(\rho-w)\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j}}(\beta)e^{i\beta(\rho+v-w)}\,d\Xi,\end{split}

where
d\Xi=d\gamma d\rho dwd\alpha d\beta
. Define for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\gamma,\alpha,w\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.30)

E_{xp}^{j}(\gamma,\alpha,w):=E_{xp}(\gamma,\alpha,w)\Psi^{\ast\ast}(w-j).

We have the bounds for
\gamma,\alpha,a\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.31)

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}E_{xp}^{j}(\gamma,\alpha,w)e^{-iwa}\,dw\Big|\lesssim e^{-\delta_{0}\epsilon(\gamma^{3}-\alpha^{3})}e^{-\delta_{0}\langle a\rangle^{1/2}}.

It follows from (
9.29
) and (
9.31
) that for
t\geq 0,\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,

(9.32)

\big|\widehat{\,\,I_{k,j}^{L}}(t,\xi)\big|\lesssim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-\delta_{0}\langle\xi-\eta-\beta,\eta\rangle^{1/2}}\big|\widehat{\,\,F_{0k}^{j}}(\beta)\big|\,d\beta d\eta.

The desired bounds (
9.8
) for
I_{k}^{L}
follow from (
9.32
).

The enhanced dissipation estimates (
1.27
) then follow from (
1.28
), Proposition
5.3
, bound (
5.3
), and Theorem
8.1
.

Finally we notice that (
1.29
) follows from the formula for
v\in\mathbb{R},t\geq 0
,

(9.33)

\Phi_{k}(t,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{G}_{k}(v,v^{\prime})F_{k}(t,v^{\prime})e^{-ikt(v^{\prime}-v)}\,dv^{\prime}

and the bounds on the Green’s function
\mathcal{G}_{k}(v,v^{\prime})
, see Lemma
2.2
. The proof of Theorem
1.3
is now complete.
