When is Mean-Field Reinforcement Learning Tractable and Relevant? Conference: Proc. of the 23rd International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2024); May 6 – 10, 2024; Auckland, New Zealand 1045
Batuhan Yardim, Artur Goldman, Niao He

Abstract
. Mean-field reinforcement learning has become a popular theoretical framework for efficiently approximating large-scale multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problems exhibiting symmetry. However, questions remain regarding the applicability of mean-field approximations: in particular, their approximation accuracy of real-world systems and conditions under which they become computationally tractable. We establish explicit finite-agent bounds for how well the MFG solution approximates the true N N -player game for two popular mean-field solution concepts. Furthermore, for the first time, we establish explicit lower bounds indicating that MFGs are poor or uninformative at approximating N N -player games assuming only Lipschitz dynamics and rewards. Finally, we analyze the computational complexity of solving MFGs with only Lipschitz properties and prove that they are in the class of PPAD -complete problems conjectured to be intractable, similar to general sum N N player games. Our theoretical results underscore the limitations of MFGs and complement and justify existing work by proving difficulty in the absence of common theoretical assumptions.

1.
Introduction

Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) finds numerous impactful applications in the real world
(
Shavandi and
Khedmati 2022
;
Wiering 2000
;
Samvelyan
et al. 2019
;
Rashedi
et al. 2016
;
Matignon
et al. 2007
;
Mao et al. 2022
)
.
Despite the urgent need in practice, MARL remains a fundamental challenge, especially in the setting with large numbers of agents due to the so-called “curse of many agents”
(
Wang
et al. 2020
)
.

Mean-field games (MFG), a theoretical framework first proposed by
Lasry and Lions 2007
and
Huang
et al. 2006
, permits the theoretical study of such large-scale games by introducing mean-field simplification.
Under certain assumptions, the mean-field approximation leads to efficient algorithms for the analysis of a particular type of
N
-agent competitive game where there are symmetries between players and when
N
is large. Such games appear widely in for instance auctions
(
Iyer
et al. 2014
)
, and cloud resource management
(
Mao et al. 2022
)
.
For the mean-field analysis, the game dynamics with
N
-players must be
symmetric
(i.e., each player must be exposed to the same rules) and
anonymous
(i.e., the effect of each player on the others should be permutation invariant).
Under this simplification, works such as
(
Perrin et al. 2020
;
Anahtarci
et al. 2022
;
Guo
et al. 2019
;
Yardim
et al. 2023b
;
Pérolat et al. 2022
;
Xie
et al. 2021
;
Cui and Koeppl 2021
)
and many others have analyzed reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms in the MFG limit
N\rightarrow\infty
to obtain a tractable approximation of many agent games, providing learning guarantees under various structural assumptions.

Being a simplification, MFG formulations should ideally satisfy two desiderata:
(1) they should be
relevant
, i.e., they are good approximations of the original MARL problem and
(2) they should be
tractable
, i.e., they are at least easier than solving the original MARL problem.
In this work, we would like to understand the extent to which MFGs satisfy these two requirements, and
we aim to answer two natural questions that remain understudied:

•

When are MFGs good approximations of the finite player games, when are they not?

In particular, are polynomially many agents always sufficient for mean-field approximation to be effective?

•

Is solving MFGs always computationally tractable, or more tractable than directly solving the
N
-player game?

In particular, can MFGs be solved in polynomial or pseudo-polynomial time?

1.1.
Related Work

Mean-field RL has been studied in various mathematical settings.
In this work, we focus on two popular formulations in particular: stationary mean-field games (Stat-MFG, see e.g.
(
Anahtarci
et al. 2022
;
Guo
et al. 2019
)
) and finite-horizon MFG (FH-MFG, see e.g.
(
Perrin et al. 2020
;
Pérolat et al. 2022
)
).
In the Stat-MFG setting the objective is to find a stationary policy that is optimal with respect to its induced stationary distribution, while in the FH-MFG setting, a finite-horizon reward is considered with a time-varying policy and population distribution.

Existing results on MFG relevance/approximation.

The approximation properties of MFGs have been explored by several works in literature, as summarized in Table
1
.
Finite-agent approximation bounds have been widely analyzed in the case of stochastic mean-field differential games
(
Carmona and
Delarue 2013
;
Carmona
et al. 2018
)
, albeit in the differential setting and without explicit lower bounds.
Recent works
(
Anahtarci
et al. 2022
;
Cui and Koeppl 2021
)
have established that Stat-MFG Nash equilibria (Stat-MFG-NE) asymptotically approximate the NE of
N
-player symmetric dynamic games under continuity assumptions.
The result by
Saldi
et al. 2018
, as the basis of subsequent proofs, shows asymptotic convergence for a large class of MFG variants and only requires continuity of dynamics and rewards as well as minor technical assumptions such as compactness and a form of local Lipschitz continuity.
However, such asymptotic convergence guarantees leave the question unanswered if the MFG models are realistic in real-world games.
Many games such as traffic systems, financial markets, etc. naturally exhibit large
N
, however, if
N
must be astronomically large for good approximation, the real-world impact of the mean-field analysis will be limited.
Recently,
(
Yardim
et al. 2023a
)
provided finite-agent approximation bounds of a special class of stateless MFG, which assumes no state dynamics.
We complement existing work on approximation properties of both Stat-MFG and FH-MFG by providing explicit upper and lower bounds for approximation.

Existing results on MFG tractability.
The tractability of solving MFGs as a proxy for MARL has been also heavily studied in the RL community under various classes of structural assumptions.
Since finding approximate Nash equilibria for normal form games is
PPAD
-complete, a class believed to be computationally intractable
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
;
Chen
et al. 2009
)
, solving the mean-field approximation in many cases can be a tractable alternative.
We summarize recent work for computationally (or statistically) solving the two types of MFGs below, with an in-depth comparison also provided in Table
2
.

For Stat-MFG, under a contraction assumption RL algorithms such as Q-learning
(
Zaman
et al. 2023
;
Anahtarci
et al. 2022
)
, policy mirror ascent
(
Yardim
et al. 2023b
)
, policy gradient methods
(
Guo
et al. 2022b
)
, soft Q-learning
(
Cui and Koeppl 2021
)
and fictitious play
(
Xie
et al. 2021
)
have been shown to solve Stat-MFG with statistical and computational efficiency.
However, all of these guarantees require the game to be heavily regularized as pointed out in
(
Cui and Koeppl 2021
;
Yardim
et al. 2023b
)
, inducing a non-vanishing bias on the computed Nash.
Moreover, in some works the population evolution is also implicitly required to be contractive under all policies (see e.g.
(
Guo
et al. 2019
;
Yardim
et al. 2023b
)
), further restricting the analysis to sufficiently smooth games.
While
(
Guo
et al. 2022a
)
has proposed a method that guarantees convergence to MFG-NE under differentiable dynamics, the algorithm converges only when initialized sufficiently close to the solution.
To the best of our knowledge, there are neither RL algorithms that work without regularization nor evidence of difficulty in the absence of such strong assumptions: we complement the line of work by showing that unless dynamics are sufficiently smooth, Stat-MFG is both computationally intractable and a poor approximation.

A separate line of work analyzes the finite horizon problem.
In this case, when the dynamics are population-independent and the payoffs are monotone the problem is known to be tractable.
Algorithms such as fictitious play
(
Perrin et al. 2020
)
and mirror descent
(
Pérolat et al. 2022
)
have been shown to converge to Nash in corresponding continuous-time equations.
Recent work has also focused on the statistical complexity of the finite-horizon problem in very general FH-MFG problems
(
Huang
et al. 2023
)
, however, the algorithm proposed is in general computationally intractable.
In terms of computational tractability and the approximation properties, our work complements these results by demonstrating that (1) when dynamics depend on the population as well an exponential approximation lower bound exists, and (2) in the absence of monotonicity, the FH-MFG is provably as difficult as solving an
N
-player game.

Finally, we note that there are several other settings and MFG solution concepts have been analyzed.
For instance, a certain class of infinite horizon MFG has been shown to be equivalent to concave utility RL, proving finite-time computational guarantees
(
Geist et al. 2022
)
.

1.2.
Our Contribution

In this work, we formalize and provide answers to the two aforementioned fundamental questions, first focusing on the approximation properties of MFG in Section
3
and later on the computational tractability of MFG in Section
4
.
Our contributions are summarized as follows.

Firstly, we introduce explicit finite-agent approximation bounds for finite horizon and stationary MFGs (Table
1
) in terms of exploitability in the finite agent game.
In both cases, we prove explicit upper bounds which quantify how many agents a symmetric game must have to be well-approximated by the MFG, which has been absent in the literature to the best of our knowledge.
Our approximation results only require a minimal Lipschitz continuity assumption of the transition kernel and rewards.
For FH-MFG, we prove a
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(1-L^{H})H^{2}}{(1-L)\sqrt{N}}\right)
upper bound for the exploitabilty where
L
is the Lipschitz modulus of the population evolution operator: the upper bound exhibits an exponential dependence on the horizon
H
.
For the Stat-MFG we show that a
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(1-\gamma)^{-3}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
approximation bound can be established, but only if the population evolution dynamics are non-expansive.
Next, for the first time, we establish explicit lower bounds for the approximation proving the shortcomings of the upper bounds are fundamental.
For the FH-MFG, we show that unless
N\geq\Omega(2^{H})
, an exploitability linear in horizon
H
is unavoidable when deploying the MFG solution to the
N
player game: hence in general the MFG equilibrium becomes irrelevant quickly as the problem horizon increases.
For Stat-MFG we establish an
\Omega(N^{\log_{2}\gamma})
lower bound when the population dynamics are not restricted to non-expansive population operators, showing that a large discount factor
\gamma
also rapidly deteriorates the approximation efficiency.
Our lower bounds indicate that in the worst case, the number of agents required for the approximation can grow exponentially in the problem parameters, demonstrating the limitations of the MFG approximation.

Finally, from the computational perspective, we establish that both finite-horizon and stationary MFGs can be
PPAD
-complete problems in general, even when restricted to certain simple subclasses (Table
2
).
This shows that both MFG problems are in general as hard as finding a Nash equilibrium of
N
-player general sum games.
Furthermore, our results imply that unless
PPAD
=
P
there are no polynomial time algorithms for solving FH-MFG and Stat-MFG, a result indicating computational intractability.

2.
Mean-Field Games: Definitions, Solution Concepts

Notation.

Throughout this work, we assume
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A}
are finite sets.
For a finite set
\mathcal{X}
,
\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
denotes the set of probability distributions on
\mathcal{X}
.
The norm used will not fundamentally matter for our results, we choose to equip
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}},\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
.
We define the set of Markov policies
\Pi:=\{\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\}
,
\Pi_{H}:=\{\{\pi_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}:\pi_{h}\in\Pi,\forall h\}
and
\Pi_{H}^{N}:=\{\{\pi_{h}^{i}\}_{h=0,i=0}^{H-1,N}:\pi_{h}^{i}\in\Pi,\forall h\}
.
For policies
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
denote
\|\pi-\pi^{\prime}\|_{1}=\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\|\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\|_{1}
.
We denote
d(x,y):=\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\neq y\}}
for
x,y
in
\mathcal{A}
or
\mathcal{S}
.
For
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi^{N},\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
, we define
(\pi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-i})\in\Pi^{N}
as the policy profile where the
i
-th policy has been replaced by
\pi^{\prime}
.
Likewise, for
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi^{N}_{H},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\Pi_{H}
, we denote by
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{-i})\in\Pi^{N}_{H}
the policy profile where the
i
-th player’s policy has been replaced by
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
.
For any
N\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
,
[N]:=\{1,\ldots,N\}
.

MFGs introduce a dependence on the population distribution over states of the rewards and dynamics.
We will strictly consider Lipschitz continuous rewards and dynamics, which is a common assumption in literature
(
Guo
et al. 2019
;
Anahtarci
et al. 2022
;
Yardim
et al. 2023b
;
Xie
et al. 2021
)
, formalized below.

Definition 2.1 (Lipschitz dynamics, rewards).

For some
L\geq 0
, we define the set of
L
-Lipschitz reward functions and state transition dynamics as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{L}:=\Big\{R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow

\displaystyle[0,1]\,:\,|R(s,a,\mu)-R(s,a,\mu^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq L\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},\forall s,a,\mu,\mu^{\prime}\Big\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{L}:=\Big\{P:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\rightarrow

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\,:\,\|P(s,a,\mu)-P(s,a,\mu^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq L\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},\forall s,a,\mu,\mu^{\prime}\Big\}.

Moreover, we define the set of Lipschitz rewards and dynamics as
\mathcal{R}:=\bigcup_{L\geq 0}\mathcal{R}_{L},\quad\mathcal{P}:=\bigcup_{L\geq 0}\mathcal{P}_{L}
respectively.

We note that there are interesting MFGs with non-Lipschitz dynamics and rewards, however, even the existence of Nash is not guaranteed in this case.
Lipschitz continuity is a minimal assumption under which solutions to MFG always exist, and as our aim is to prove lower bounds and difficulty we will adopt this assumption.
Solving MFG with non-Lipschitz dynamics is more challenging than Lipschitz continuous MFG (the latter being a subset of the former), hence our difficulty results will apply.

Operators.

We will define the useful population operators
\Gamma_{P}:\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\times\Pi\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
,
\Gamma_{P}^{H}:\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\times\Pi\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
, and

\Lambda^{H}_{P}:\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\times\Pi_{H}\rightarrow\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{H}
as

\displaystyle\Gamma_{P}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}}\mu(s)\pi(a|s)P(\cdot|s,a,\mu),

\displaystyle\Gamma^{H}_{P}(\mu,\pi)

\displaystyle:=\underbrace{\Gamma_{P}(\dots\Gamma_{P}(\Gamma_{P}(\mu,\pi),\pi)\dots),\pi)}_{H\text{ times}},

\displaystyle\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi})

\displaystyle:=\big\{\underbrace{\Gamma_{P}(\dots\Gamma_{P}(\Gamma_{P}(\mu_{0},\pi_{0}),\pi_{1})\dots,\pi_{h-1})}_{h\text{ times}}\big\}_{h=0}^{H-1}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0},\pi\in\Pi,\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{\pi_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}\in\Pi_{H},P\in\mathcal{P},\mu_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Finally, we will need the following Lipschitz continuity result for the
\Gamma_{P}
operator.

Lemma 2.2.

(
Yardim
et al. 2023b
, Lemma 3.2)

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{K_{\mu}}
for
K_{\mu}>0
and

\displaystyle K_{s}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,s^{\prime}\\
a,\mu\end{subarray}}\left\|P(s,a,\mu)-P(s^{\prime},a,\mu)\right\|_{1},K_{a}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,a^{\prime}\\
s,\mu\end{subarray}}\left\|P(s,a,\mu)-P(s,a^{\prime},\mu)\right\|_{1}.

Then it holds for all
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}},\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
that:

\|\Gamma_{P}(\mu,\pi)-\Gamma_{P}(\mu^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})\|_{1}\leq L_{pop,\mu}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1}+\frac{K_{a}}{2}\|\pi-\pi^{\prime}\|_{1},

where
L_{pop,\mu}:=(K_{\mu}+\frac{K_{s}}{2}+\frac{K_{a}}{2})
for all
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi
,
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.

In particular, in our settings, Lemma
2.2
indicates that
\Gamma_{P}
is always Lipschitz continuous if
P\in\mathcal{P}
, a property which will become significant for approximation analysis.

We will be interested in two classes of MFG solution concepts that lead to different analyses: infinite horizon stationary MFG Nash equilibrium (Stat-MFG-NE) and finite horizon MFG Nash equilibrium (FH-MFG-NE).
The first problem widely studied in literature is the stationary MFG equilibrium problem, see for instance
(
Anahtarci
et al. 2022
;
Yardim
et al. 2023b
;
Guo
et al. 2019
;
Guo
et al. 2022b
;
Xie
et al. 2021
)
.
We formalize this solution concept below.

Definition 2.3 (Stat-MFG).

A stationary MFG (Stat-MFG) is defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)
for Lipschitz dynamics and rewards
P\in\mathcal{P},R\in\mathcal{R}
, discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
.
For any
(\mu,\pi)\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\times\Pi
, we define the
\gamma
-discounted infinite horizon expected reward as

\displaystyle V^{\gamma}_{P,R}(\mu,\pi):=\Exop\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t},\mu)\middle|\begin{subarray}{c}s_{0}\sim\mu,\hskip 7.97224pta_{t}\sim\pi(s_{t})\\
s_{t+1}\sim P(s_{t},a_{t},\mu)\end{subarray}\right].

A policy-population pair
(\mu^{*},\pi^{*})\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}\times\Pi
is called a Stat-MFG Nash equilibrium if the two conditions hold:

Stability:

\displaystyle\mu^{*}=\Gamma_{P}(\mu^{*},\pi^{*}),

(Stat-MFG-NE)

Optimality:

\displaystyle V^{\gamma}_{P,R}(\mu^{*},\pi^{*})=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}V^{\gamma}_{P,R}(\mu^{*},\pi).

The second MFG concept that we will consider has a finite time horizon, and is also common in literature
(
Perolat
et al. 2015
;
Perrin et al. 2020
;
Laurière
et al. 2022
;
Huang
et al. 2023
)
.
In this case, the population distribution is permitted to vary over time, and the objective is to find an optimal non-stationary policy with respect to the population distribution it induces.
We formalize this problem and the corresponding solution concept below.

Definition 2.4 (FH-MFG).

A finite horizon MFG problem (FH-MFG) is determined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},H,P,R,\mu_{0})
where
H\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
P\in\mathcal{P},R\in\mathcal{R},\mu_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.
For
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{\pi_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H}\in\Pi_{H},\bm{\mu}=\{\mu_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}^{H}
, define the expected reward and exploitability as

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}\left(\bm{\mu},\boldsymbol{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle:=\Exop\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}R(s_{h},a_{h},\mu_{h})\middle|\begin{subarray}{c}s_{0}\sim\mu_{0},\hskip 7.97224pta_{h}\sim\pi_{h}(s_{h})\\
s_{h+1}\sim P(s_{h},a_{h},\mu_{h})\end{subarray}\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,R}^{H}(\boldsymbol{\pi})

\displaystyle:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\Pi^{H}}V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi}).

Then, the FH-MFG Nash equilibrium is defined as:

Policy

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}=\{\pi^{*}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}\in\Pi_{H}\text{ such that }

(FH-MFG-NE)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,R}^{H}(\{\pi^{*}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1})=0.

3.
Approximation Properties of MFG

As established in literature, the reason the FH-MFG and Stat-MFG problems are studied is the fact that they can approximate the NE of certain symmetric games with
N
players, establishing the main relevance of the formulations in the real world.
Such results are summarized in Table
1
.

In this section, we study how efficient this convergence is and also related lower bounds.
For these purposes, we first define the corresponding
finite-player
game of each mean-field game problem: to avoid confusion, we call these games
symmetric anonymous dynamic games
(SAG).
Afterwards, for each solution concept, we will first establish (1) an upper bound on the approximation error (i.e. the exploitability) due to the mean-field, and (2) a lower bound demonstrating the worst-case rate.
We will present the main outlines of proofs, and postpone computation-intensive derivations to the supplementary material of the paper.

3.1.
Approximation Analysis of FH-MFG

Firstly, we define the finite-player game that is approximately solved by the FH-MFG-NE.

Definition 3.1 (
N
-FH-SAG).

An
N
-player finite horizon SAG (
N
-FH-SAG) is determined by the tuple
(N,\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},H,P,R,\mu_{0})
such that
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0},H\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,

4.
Computational Tractability of MFG

The next fundamental question for mean-field reinforcement learning will be whether it is always computationally easier than finding an equilibrium of a
N
-player general sum normal form game.
We focus on the computational aspect of solving mean-field games in this section, and not statistical uncertainty: we assume we have full knowledge of the MFG dynamics.
We will show that unless additional assumptions are introduced (as typically done in the form of contractivity or monotonicity), solving MFG can in general be as hard as finding
N
-player general sum Nash.

We will prove that the problems are
PPAD
-complete, where
PPAD
is a class of computational problems studied in the seminal work by
Papadimitriou 1994
, containing the complete problem of finding
N
-player Nash equilibrium in general sum normal form games and finding the fixed point of continuous maps
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
;
Chen
et al. 2009
)
.
The class
PPAD
is conjectured to contain difficult problems with no polynomial time algorithms
(
Beame et al. 1995
;
Goldberg 2011
)
, hence our results can be seen as a proof of difficulty.
Our results are significant since they imply that the MFG problems studied in literature are in the same complexity class as general-sum
N
-player normal form games or
N
-player Markov games
(
Daskalakis
et al. 2023
)
.
Once again, several computation-intensive aspects of our proofs will be postponed to the supplementary material.

Due to a technical detail, we will prove the complexity results for a subset of possible reward and transition probability functions.
We formalize this subset of possible rewards and dynamics as “simple” rewards/dynamics and also linear rewards, defined below.

Definition 4.1 (Simple/Linear Dynamics and Rewards).

R\in\mathcal{R}
and
P\in\mathcal{P}
are said to be
simple
if for any
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}
,
P(s^{\prime}|s,a,\mu)
and
R(s,a,\mu)
are functions of
\mu
that are expressible as finite combinations of arithmetic operations
+,-,\times,\frac{\cdot}{\cdot}
and functions
\max\{\cdot,\cdot\},\min\{\cdot,\cdot\}
of coordinates of
\mu
.
They are called
linear
if
P(s^{\prime}|s,a,\mu)
and
R(s,a,\mu)
are linear functions of
\mu
for all
s,a,s^{\prime}
.
The set of simple rewards and dynamics are denoted by
\mathcal{R}^{\text{Sim}}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\text{Sim}}
respectively, and the set of linear rewards and transitions are denoted
\mathcal{R}^{\text{Lin}},\mathcal{P}^{\text{Lin}}
respectively.

A note on simple functions.

We define simple functions as above as in general there is no known efficient encoding of a Lipschitz continuous function as a sequence of bits.
This is significant since a Turing machine accepts a finite sequence of bits as input.
To solve this issue, we prove a slightly stronger hardness result that even games where
P(s^{\prime}|s,a,\mu),R(s,a,\mu)
are Lipschitz functions with strong structure are
PPAD
-complete.
Since we are proving hardness, other larger classes of
P,R
including
\mathcal{P}^{\text{Sim}},\mathcal{R}^{\text{Sim}}
will have similar intractability.
See also arithmetic circuits with
\max,\min
gates
(
Daskalakis and
Papadimitriou 2011
)
for a similar idea.

4.1.
The Complexity Class
PPAD

The
PPAD
class is defined by the complete problem

(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
)
, whose formal definition we defer to the appendix as it is not used in our proofs.

Definition 4.2 (
PPAD
,
PPAD
-hard,
PPAD
-complete).

The class
PPAD
is defined as all search problems that can be reduced to

in polynomial time.
If

can be reduced to a search problem

in polynomial time, then
\mathcal{S}
is called
PPAD
-hard.
A search problem
\mathcal{S}
is called

-complete if it is both a member of PPAD and it is

-hard.

While
End-of-the-Line
defines the problem class

, it is hard to construct direct reductions to it.
We will instead use two problems that are known to be
PPAD
-complete (and hence can be equivalently used to define

-player general sum game.

Definition 4.3 (Generalized Circuits
(

;

).

A generalized circuit
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})
is a finite set of nodes
\mathcal{V}

.
Each gate
G\in\mathcal{G}
is characterized by the tuple
G(\theta|v_{1},v_{2}|v)
where
G\in\{G_{\leftarrow},G_{\times,+},\mathcal{G}_{<}\}

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\star}
is a parameter (possibly of length 0),
v_{1},v_{2}\in V\cup\{\perp\}
are the input nodes (with
\perp
indicating an empty input) and
v\in V

The collection of gates
\mathcal{G}
satisfies the property that if
G_{1}(\theta|v_{1},v_{2}|v),G_{2}(\theta^{\prime}|v^{\prime}_{1},v^{\prime}_{2}|v^{\prime})\in G
are distinct gates, then
v\neq v^{\prime}
.

Such circuits define a set of constraints on values assigned to each gate, and finding such an assignment will be the associated computational problem for such a circuit desription.
We formally define the
\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
problem to this end.

\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
is a standard complete problem for the class

We will use the shorthand notation

for

.

Definition 4.4 (

-

(

)
).

Given a generalized circuit
\mathcal{C}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})

•

For every gate

•

of the form

\displaystyle p(v)\in[\max\{\min\{0,\alpha p(v_{1})+\beta p(v_{2})\}\}]\pm\varepsilon,

•

For every gate

of the form

\displaystyle p(v)=\begin{cases}&1\pm\varepsilon,\quad p(v_{1})\leq p(v_{2})-\varepsilon,\\

The
\varepsilon

GCircuit

\displaystyle\textit{Given generalized circuit }\mathcal{C},\textit{ find an }\varepsilon\textit{-satisfying assignment of $\mathcal{C}$.}

\varepsilon

5.
Discussion and Conclusion

We provided novel results on when mean-field RL is relevant for real-world applications and when it is tractable from a computational perspective.
Our results differ from existing work by provably characterizing cases where MFGs might have practical shortcomings.
From the approximation perspective, we show clear conditions and lower bounds on when the MFGs efficiently approximate real-world games.
Computationally, we show that even simple MFGs can be as hard as solving
N
-player general sum games.

We emphasize that our results do not discard MFGs, but rather identify potential bottlenecks (and conditions to overcome these) when using mean-field RL to compute a good approximate NE.

A.1.
Preliminaries

To establish explicit upper bounds on the approximation rate, we will use standard concentration tools.

Definition A.1 (Sub-Gaussian).

Random variable
\xi
is called sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
if
\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\quad\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda(\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right])}\right]\leq e^{\frac{\lambda^{2}\sigma^{2}}{2}}
.
In this case, we write
\xi\in SG(\sigma^{2})
.

It is easy to show that if
\xi\in SG(\sigma^{2})
, then
\alpha\xi\in SG(\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2})
for any constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
Furthermore, if
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are independent random variables with
\xi_{i}\in SG(\sigma_{i}^{2})
, then
\sum_{i}\xi_{i}\in SG(\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2})
.
Finally, if
\xi
is almost surely bounded in
[a,b]
, then
\xi_{i}\in SG(\nicefrac{{(b-a)^{2}}}{{4}})
.
We also state the well-known Hoeffding concentration bound and a corollary, Lemma
A.3
.

Lemma A.2 (Hoeffding inequality
(
McDiarmid
et al. 1989
)
).

Let
\xi\in SG(\sigma^{2})
.
Then for any
t>0
it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(|\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]|\geq t\right)\leq 2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}
.

Lemma A.3.

Let
\xi\in SG(\sigma^{2})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]|\right]\leq\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right])^{2}\right]\leq 4\sigma^{2}

Proof.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]|\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]|\geq t)dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(I)}}{{\leq}}2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}dt=\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}

Inequality
(I)
is true due to
Lemma

A.2
.
Likewise,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right])^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}((\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right])^{2}\geq t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|\xi-\mathbb{E}\left[\xi\right]|\geq\sqrt{h})dt

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(II)}}{{\leq}}2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\frac{h}{2\sigma^{2}}}dt=4\sigma^{2}

∎

Establishing lower bounds for the mean-field approximation of the
N
-player game will be more challenging as it will require different tools.
To establish lower bounds, we will need to use the following anti-concentration result for the binomial distribution.

Lemma A.4 (Anti-concentration for binomial).

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and
X\sim\operatorname{Binom}(N,p)
be drawn from a binomial distribution for some
p\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},1]
.
Then,
\Prob\left[X\geq\frac{N}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{20}.

Proof.

For
k_{0}:=\left\lceil\frac{N}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}\right\rceil
, we will lower bound
\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{N}\binom{N}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{N-k}
when
N
is large enough.
If
k_{0}<\lceil Np\rceil
, then the probability in the statement above is bounded below trivially by
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
since
\lfloor Np\rfloor
lower bounds the median of the binomial
(
Kaas and Buhrman 1980
)
.
Otherwise, if
k_{0}\geq\lceil Np\rceil
, then the function
\widebar{p}\rightarrow\widebar{p}^{k}(1-\widebar{p})^{N-k}
is increasing in
\widebar{p}
in the interval
[0,p]
.
As
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\in[0,p]
, it is then sufficient to assume
p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, and to upper bound
\Prob\left[\frac{N}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}<X<\frac{N}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}\right]
by
\nicefrac{{9}}{{10}}
as the binomial probability mass is symmetric around
\frac{N}{2}
when
p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

First assuming
N
is even, we obtain by monotonicity
\binom{N}{k}\leq\binom{N}{\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}}
.
Using the Stirling bound
\sqrt{2\pi}k^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-k}\leq k!\leq\mathrm{e}k^{k+\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-k}
, we further upper bound
\binom{N}{\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}}\leq\frac{e}{\pi}\frac{2^{N}}{\sqrt{N}}
, resulting in the bound
\Prob\left[\frac{N}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}<X<\frac{N}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}\right]\leq 2^{-N}\sqrt{N}\binom{N}{\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}}\leq\frac{e}{\pi}\leq\nicefrac{{9}}{{10}}
, since there are at most
\sqrt{N}
binomial coefficients being summed.
Finally, assume
N=2m+1
is odd, then by the binomial formula
\binom{2m+1}{m+1}=\binom{2m}{m+1}+\binom{2m}{m}\leq 2\binom{2m}{m}\leq\frac{2e}{\pi}\frac{2^{2m}}{\sqrt{2m}}
.
Hence we have the bound on the sum
\Prob\left[\frac{N}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}<X<\frac{N}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}\right]\leq\frac{e\sqrt{N}}{\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{N-1}}
.
It is easy to verify that for

,
\frac{e\sqrt{N}}{\pi\sqrt{N-1}}\leq\nicefrac{{9}}{{10}}
, and the case when
N<16
and
N
is odd follows by manual computation.
∎

h=0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{0}-\mu_{0}\|_{1}\right]=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{0}^{i}=s\}}-\mu_{0}(s))\right|\right]\leq|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N}},

where the last line is due to
Lemma

A.3
and the fact that
\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{0}^{i}=s\}}
are bounded (hence subgaussian) random variables, and that in the finite state space we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{0}^{i}=s\}}\right]=\mu_{0}(s)
.

Next, denoting the
\sigma
-algebra induced by the random variables
(\{s^{i}_{h}\})_{i,h^{\prime}\leq h}
as
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}-\mu_{h+1}^{\widebar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\|_{1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]-\Gamma_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{h},\widebar{\pi}_{h})\|_{1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]}_{(\square)}

(3)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]\|_{1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]}_{(\triangle)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Gamma_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{h},\widebar{\pi}_{h})-\mu_{h+1}^{\widebar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\|_{1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]}_{(\heartsuit)}

We upper bound the three terms separately.
For
(\triangle)
, it holds that

\displaystyle(\triangle)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]\|_{1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}\left[|\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}(s)-\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}(s)\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]|\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]\leq|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N}},

since each
\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}(s)
is an average of independent subgaussian random variables given
\mathcal{F}_{h}
.
Specifically, each indicator is bounded
\mathbbm{1}_{\{s^{i}_{h+1}=s\}}\in[0,1]
a.s. and therefore is sub-Gaussian with
\mathbbm{1}_{\{s^{i}_{h+1}=s\}}\in SG(1/4)
. Thus we get
\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}(s)\in SG(1/(4N))
and apply bound on expected value discussed in
Section

A.1
.

Next, for
(\square)=\|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]-\Gamma_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{h},\widebar{\pi}_{h})\|_{1}
, we note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}(s)\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{h+1}^{i}=s\}}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{P}(s|s_{h}^{i},\pi_{h}^{i}(s_{h}^{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{h}),

therefore

\displaystyle(\square)

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\overline{P}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i},\pi_{h}^{i}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{h})-\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s^{\prime})\overline{P}(\cdot|s^{\prime},\pi_{h}(\cdot|s^{\prime}),\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\overline{P}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i},\pi_{h}^{i}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{h})-\overline{P}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i},\pi_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\overline{P}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i},\pi_{h}^{i}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{h})-\overline{P}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i},\pi_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i}),\widehat{\mu}_{h})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(I)}}{{\leq}}\frac{K_{a}}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\pi_{h}^{i}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i})-\pi_{h}(\cdot|s_{h}^{i})\|_{1}\leq\frac{K_{a}}{2}\Delta_{h},

where (I) follows from the Lipschitz property (
1
).
Finally, the last term
(\heartsuit)
can be bounded using:

\displaystyle(\heartsuit)=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\Gamma_{P}(\widehat{\mu}_{h},\widebar{\pi}_{h})-\Gamma_{P}(\mu_{h}^{\widebar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}},\widebar{\pi}_{h})\|_{1}\left|\mathcal{F}_{h}\right.\right]\leq L_{pop,\mu}\|\widehat{\mu}_{h}-\mu_{h}^{\widebar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\|_{1}.

To conclude, merging the bounds on the three terms in Inequality (
3
) and taking the expectations we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h+1}-\mu_{h+1}^{\widebar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\|_{1}\right]\leq L_{pop,\mu}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h}-\mu_{h}^{\widebar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}\|_{1}\right]+|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N}}+\frac{K_{a}\Delta_{h}}{2}.

Induction on
h
yields the statement of the lemma.

∎

Step 2: Bounding difference of
N
agent value function.

Next, we bound the difference between the
N
-player expected reward function
J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}
and the infinite player expected reward function
V_{P,R}^{H}
.
For ease of reading, expectations, probabilities, and laws of random variables will be denoted
\Exop_{\infty},\Prob_{\infty},\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
respectively over the infinite player finite horizon game and
\Exop_{N},\Prob_{N},\mathcal{L}_{N}
respectively over the
N
-player game.
We use the regular notation
\Exop[\cdot],\Prob[\cdot],\mathcal{L}(\cdot)
without subscripts if the underlying randomness is clearly defined.
We state the main result of this step in the following lemma.

Lemma A.8.

Suppose
N
-FH-MFG agents follow the same sequence of policies
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{\pi_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
.
Then

\left|J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\dots,\boldsymbol{\pi})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi})\right|\\
\leq(L_{\mu}+\frac{L_{s}}{2})|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2N}}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{h+1}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}.

Proof.

Due to symmetry in the
N
agent game, any permutation
\sigma:[N]\rightarrow[N]
of agents does not change their distribution, that is
\mathcal{L}_{N}(s_{h}^{1},\dots,s_{h}^{N})=\mathcal{L}_{N}(s_{h}^{\sigma(1)},\dots,s_{h}^{\sigma(N)})
.
We can then conclude that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[R(s_{h}^{i},a_{h}^{i},\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s)\overline{R}(s,\pi_{h}(s),\widehat{\mu}_{h}).\right]

Therefore, we by definition:

\displaystyle J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\dots,\boldsymbol{\pi})=\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s)\overline{R}(s,\pi_{h}(s),\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right].

Next, in the FH-MFG, under the population distribution
\{\mu_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}=\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi})
we have that for all
h\in 0,\ldots,H-1
,

\displaystyle\Prob_{\infty}(s_{0}=\cdot)

\displaystyle=\mu_{0},

\displaystyle\Prob_{\infty}(s_{h+1}=\cdot)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Prob_{\infty}(s_{h}=s)\Prob_{\infty}(s_{h}=\cdot|s_{h}=s)

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{P}(\Prob_{\infty}(s_{h}=\cdot),\pi_{h}),

so by induction
\Prob_{\infty}(s_{h}=\cdot)=\mu_{h}
.
Then we can conclude that

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}R(s_{h},\pi_{h}(s_{h}),\mu_{h})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu_{h}(s)R(s,\pi_{h}(s),\mu_{h}).

Merging the two equalities for
J,V
, we have the bound:

\displaystyle|J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi},\dots,\boldsymbol{\pi})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s)\overline{R}(s,\pi_{h}(s),\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]-\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu_{h}(s)R(s,\pi_{h}(s),\mu_{h})\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\left|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s)\overline{R}(s,\pi_{h}(s),\widehat{\mu}_{h})-\mu_{h}(s)R(s,\pi_{h}(s),\mu_{h})\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{N}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\left(\frac{L_{s}}{2}\|\mu_{h}-\widehat{\mu}_{h}\|_{1}+L_{\mu}\|\mu_{h}-\widehat{\mu}_{h}\|_{1}\right)\right].

The statement of the lemma follows by an application of Lemma
A.7
.
∎

Step 3: Bounding difference in policy deviation.

Finally, to conclude the proof of the main theorem of this section, we will prove that the improvement in expectation due to single-sided policy changes are at most of order
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
.

Lemma A.9.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{\pi_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}\in\Pi^{H}
and

\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}=\{\pi_{h}^{\prime}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}\in\Pi^{H}
arbitrary policies, and
\bm{\mu}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi})
is the population distribution induced by
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
Then

\left|J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi},\dots,\boldsymbol{\pi})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime})\right|\\
\leq\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\biggl(\frac{L_{\mu}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h}-\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}\|_{1}\right]+K_{\mu}\sum_{h^{\prime}=0}^{h-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h^{\prime}}-\mu_{h^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\|_{1}\right]\biggr).

Proof.

Define the random variables
\{s^{i}_{h},a_{h}^{i}\}_{i,h},\{\widehat{\mu}_{h}\}_{h}
as in the definition of
N
-FH-SAG (Definition
3.1
).
In addition, define the random variables
\{s_{h},a_{h}\}_{h}
evolving according to the FH-MFG with population
\bm{\mu}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}:=\{\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}\}_{h}:=\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi})
and representative policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}
, independent from the random variables
\{s^{i}_{h},a_{h}^{i}\}_{i,h}
.
Hence
s_{0}\sim\mu_{0},a_{h}\sim\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s_{h}),s_{h+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})
.
Define also for simplicity

\displaystyle E_{N}:=\left|J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi},\dots,\boldsymbol{\pi})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0}\boldsymbol{\pi}),\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime})\right|.

With these definitions, we have

\displaystyle E_{N}

\displaystyle=\left|\Exop\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}R(s_{h},a_{h},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})-\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]\right|

(4)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\left|\Exop\left[R(s_{h},a_{h},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})-R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]\right|.

Furthermore, for any
h\in\{0,\ldots,H-1\}
,

\displaystyle|\Exop

\displaystyle\left[R(s_{h},a_{h},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})-R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\Exop\left[R(s_{h},a_{h},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})-R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})\right]\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\Exop\left[R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})-R(s_{h}^{1},a_{h}^{1},\widehat{\mu}_{h})\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\Exop\left[R(s_{h},\pi^{\prime}_{h}(s_{h}),\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})-R(s_{h}^{1},\pi^{\prime}_{h}(s_{h}^{1}),\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})\right]\right|

\displaystyle+L_{\mu}\Exop\left[\|\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}-\widehat{\mu}_{h}\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\Prob[s_{h}=\cdot]-\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=\cdot]\|_{1}+L_{\mu}\Exop\left[\|\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}-\widehat{\mu}_{h}\|_{1}\right],

where the last line follows since
R
is bounded in
[0,1]
.
Replacing this in Equation (
4
),

(5)

\displaystyle E_{N}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{h}\|\Prob[s_{h}=\cdot]-\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=\cdot]\|_{1}+L_{\mu}\sum_{h}\Exop\left[\|\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}-\widehat{\mu}_{h}\|_{1}\right].

The first sum above we upper bound in the rest of the proof inductively.

Firstly, by definitions of
N
-FH-SAG and FH-MFG, both
s_{0}^{1}
and
s_{0}
have distribution
\mu_{0}
, hence
\|\Prob[s_{0}=\cdot]-\Prob[s_{0}^{1}=\cdot]\|_{1}=0
.
Assume that
h\geq 1
.
We note that
P
takes values in
\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
and the random vector
\widehat{\mu}_{h}
takes values in the discrete set
\{\frac{1}{N}u:u\in\{0,\ldots,N\}^{\mathcal{S}},\sum_{s}u(s)=N\}\subset\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
, hence we have the bounds:

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle\Prob[s_{h+1}=\cdot]-\Prob[s^{1}_{h+1}=\cdot]\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{s,\mu}P(s,\pi^{\prime}_{h}(s),\mu)\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=s,\widehat{\mu}_{h}=\mu]-\sum_{s}P(s,\pi_{h}^{\prime}(s),\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})\Prob[s_{h}=s]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{s}P(s,\pi_{h}^{\prime}(s),\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=s]-\sum_{s}P(s,\pi_{h}^{\prime}(s),\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})\Prob[s_{h}=s]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\left\|\sum_{s,\mu}\left(P(s,\pi_{h}^{\prime}(s),\mu)-P(s,\pi_{h}^{\prime}(s),\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h})\right)\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=s,\widehat{\mu}_{h}=\mu]\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=\cdot]-\Prob[s_{h}=\cdot]\right\|_{1}+\sum_{s,\mu}K_{\mu}\left\|\mu-\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}\right\|_{1}\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=s,\widehat{\mu}_{h}=\mu]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\Prob[s_{h}^{1}=\cdot]-\Prob[s_{h}=\cdot]\right\|_{1}+K_{\mu}\Exop\left[\left\|\widehat{\mu}_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}\right\|_{1}\right]

where the last two lines follow from the fact that
P
is
K_{\mu}
Lipschitz in
\mu
and stochastic matrices are non-expansive in the total-variation norm over probability distributions.
By induction, we conclude that for all
h\geq 0
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\|

\displaystyle\Prob[s_{h}=\cdot]-\Prob[s^{1}_{h}=\cdot]\|_{1}\leq K_{\mu}\sum_{h^{\prime}=0}^{h}\Exop\left[\left\|\widehat{\mu}_{h^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}-\mu^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h^{\prime}}\right\|_{1}\right].

Placing this result into Equation (
5
), we obtain the statement of the lemma.

∎

Since
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{h^{\prime}}-\mu_{h^{\prime}}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\|_{1}\right]
above in the theorem is of the order of
\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{N}}}\right)
by the result in step 1, the result above allows us to bound exploitability in the
N
-FH-SAG.

Conclusion and Statement of Result.

Finally, we can merge the results up until this stage to upper bound the exploitability.
By definition of the FH-MFG-NE, we have:

\delta\geq\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\Pi^{H}}V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\delta}),\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\mu_{0},\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\delta}),\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\delta})

The upper bounds on the deviation between
V_{P,R}^{H}
and
J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}
from the previous steps directly yields the statement of the theorem.
We state it below for completeness.

Theorem A.10.

It holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\delta},\dots,\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\delta})\leq 2\delta+\frac{C_{1}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{C_{2}}{N}=O\left(\delta+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

where
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\delta}
is a
\delta
-FH-MFG Nash equilibrium and

\displaystyle C_{1}=|\mathcal{S}|\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left((2L_{\mu}+\frac{L_{s}}{2})\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{h+1}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}+K_{\mu}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{i+1}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}\right)

\displaystyle C_{2}=L_{\mu}K_{a}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{h}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}+K_{a}K_{\mu}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\sum_{i=0}^{h-1}\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{i}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}},

where we use shorthand notation
\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{k}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}:=k-1
when
L_{pop,\mu}=1
.

A note on constants.

Note that constants
C_{1},C_{2}
in
Theorem

A.10
depend on horizon with
\frac{H^{2}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}
if
L_{pop,\mu}<1
, with
H^{3}
if
L_{pop,\mu}=1
and with
H^{2}\frac{1-L_{pop,\mu}^{H+1}}{1-L_{pop,\mu}}
if
L_{pop,\mu}>1
.

A.3.
Lower Bound for FH-MFG: Extended Proof of Theorem
3.3

The proof will be by construction: we will explicitly define an FH-MFG where the optimal policy for the
N
-agent game diverges quickly from the FH-MFG-NE policy.

Preliminaries.

We first define a few utility functions.
Define
\mathbf{g}:\Delta_{2}\rightarrow B_{\infty,+}^{2}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\|\mathbf{x}\|_{\infty}=1,\ x_{1},x_{2}\geq 0\}
and
\mathbf{h}:\Delta_{2}\rightarrow[0,1]^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{g}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\\
\mathbf{g}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{x_{1}}{\max\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}\\
\frac{x_{2}}{\max\{x_{1},x_{2}\}}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})\\
\mathbf{h}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\max\{4x_{2},1\}\\
\max\{4x_{1},1\}\end{pmatrix}.

Furthermore, for any
\epsilon>0
we define
\omega_{\epsilon}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
as:

\displaystyle\omega_{\epsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\quad x>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\epsilon\\
0,\quad x<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\epsilon\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{x-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}{2\epsilon},\quad x\in[\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}-\epsilon,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\epsilon]\end{cases}.

\epsilon\in(0,\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})
will be specified later.

It is straightforward to verify that
\mathbf{g}
has an inverse in its domain given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}^{-1}(x_{1},x_{2})=\left(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}+x_{2}},\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}\right),\forall(x_{1},x_{2})\in B^{2}_{\infty,+}.

Furthermore, it holds for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})\in B^{2}_{\infty,+},\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},y_{2})\in B^{2}_{\infty,+}

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}^{-1}

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{g}^{-1}(\mathbf{y})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{x_{1}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}-\frac{y_{1}}{y_{1}+y_{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{x_{2}}{x_{1}+x_{2}}-\frac{y_{2}}{y_{1}+y_{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{x_{1}(y_{2}-x_{2})+x_{2}(x_{1}-y_{1})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}\right|+\left|\frac{x_{2}(y_{1}-x_{1})+x_{1}(x_{2}-y_{2})}{(x_{1}+x_{2})(y_{1}+y_{2})}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|_{1},

and likewise for
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\Delta_{2}
, letting
u_{+}:=\max\{u_{1},u_{2}\},v_{+}:=\max\{v_{1},v_{2}\},

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{v})\|_{1}=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{u_{1}}{u_{+}}-\frac{v_{1}}{v_{+}}\right|+\left|\frac{u_{2}}{u_{+}}-\frac{v_{2}}{v_{+}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{u_{1}v_{+}-v_{1}u_{+}}{u_{+}v_{+}}\right|+\left|\frac{u_{2}v_{+}-u_{+}v_{2}}{u_{+}v_{+}}\right|\leq 2\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{1}.

This follows from considering cases and observation that
u_{+}\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
,
v_{+}\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.
Then for all
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\Delta_{2}
,
\mathbf{g},\mathbf{h}
have the bi-Lipschitz and Lipschitz properties:

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{1}\leq\|\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{v})\|_{1}\leq 2\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{1},

(7)

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{u})-\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{v})\|_{1}\leq 4\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|_{1}.

Likewise,
\omega_{\epsilon}
, being piecewise linear, also satisfies the Lipschitz condition:
|\omega_{\epsilon}(x)-\omega_{\epsilon}(y)|\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon}|x-y|,\quad\forall x,y\in[0,1].

Defining the FH-MFG.

We take a particular FH-MFG with 6 states, 2 actions.
Define the state-actions sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}},s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LB}},s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}}\},\quad\mathcal{A}=\{a_{\text{A}},a_{\text{B}}\}.

Intuitively, the “main” states of the game are
s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}
and the
4
states
s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LB}},s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}}
are dummy states that keep track of which actions were taken by which percentage of players used to introduce a dependency of the rewards on the distribution of agents over actions as well as states.
Define the initial probabilities
\mu_{0}
by:

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{0}(s_{\text{Left}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{0}(s_{\text{Right}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{0}(s_{\text{LA}})

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{0}(s_{\text{RA}})=\bm{\mu}_{0}(s_{\text{RA}})=\bm{\mu}_{0}(s_{\text{RB}})=0.

When at the states
s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}
, the transition probabilities are defined for all
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
by:

\displaystyle P(s_{\text{LA}}|s_{\text{Left}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)

\displaystyle=1,\quad P(s_{\text{LB}}|s_{\text{Left}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)=1,

\displaystyle P(s_{\text{RA}}|s_{\text{Right}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)

\displaystyle=1,\quad P(s_{\text{RB}}|s_{\text{Right}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)=1.

That is, the agent transitions to one of
\{s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}},s_{\text{LB}}\}
to remember its last action and left-right state.
When at states
\{s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}},s_{\text{LB}}\}
, the transition probabilities are:

\displaystyle\text{If }s

\displaystyle\in\{s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LB}},s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}}\}:

\displaystyle P(s^{\prime}|s,a,\mu)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\omega_{\epsilon}(\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}})),\text{ if }s^{\prime}=s_{\text{Left}}\\
\omega_{\epsilon}(\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}})),\text{ if }s^{\prime}=s_{\text{Right}}\end{cases},\forall\mu,a.

The other non-defined transition probabilities are of course
0
.

Finally, let
\alpha,\beta>0
such that
\alpha+\beta<1
(to be also defined later).
The reward functions are defined for all
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
as follows:

\displaystyle R(s_{\text{Left}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)=

\displaystyle R(s_{\text{Left}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)=0,

\displaystyle R(s_{\text{Right}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)=

\displaystyle R(s_{\text{Right}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)=0,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}R(s_{\text{LA}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)\\
R(s_{\text{LB}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha-\beta)\mathbf{g}\big(\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}}),\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}})\big)

\displaystyle+\alpha\mathbf{h}(\mu(s_{\text{LA}}),\mu(s_{\text{LB}}))

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}R(s_{\text{LA}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)\\
R(s_{\text{LB}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha-\beta)\mathbf{g}\big(\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}}),\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}})\big)

\displaystyle+\alpha\mathbf{h}(\mu(s_{\text{LA}}),\mu(s_{\text{LB}}))+\beta\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}R(s_{\text{RA}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)\\
R(s_{\text{RB}},a_{\text{A}},\mu)\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha-\beta)\mathbf{g}\big(\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}}),\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}})\big)

\displaystyle+\alpha\mathbf{h}(\mu(s_{\text{RA}}),\mu(s_{\text{RB}}))

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}R(s_{\text{RA}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)\\
R(s_{\text{RB}},a_{\text{B}},\mu)\end{pmatrix}=

\displaystyle(1-\alpha-\beta)\mathbf{g}\big(\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}}),\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}})\big)

\displaystyle+\alpha\mathbf{h}(\mu(s_{\text{RA}}),\mu(s_{\text{RB}}))+\beta\mathbf{1}

Note that only at odd steps do the agents get a reward, and at this step, it does not matter which action the agent plays, only the state among
\{s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}}\}
and the population distribution.
The parameters
\epsilon,\alpha,\beta
of the above FH-MFG are “free” parameters to be specified later.

A minor remark.

The arguments of
\mathbf{g}
above will be with probability one in the set
\Delta_{2}
at odd-numbered time steps, but to formally satisfy the Lipschitz condition
R\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
one can for instance replace
\mathbf{g}\big(\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}}),\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}})\big)
with
\mathbf{g}\big(\mu(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu(s_{\text{RB}})+\mu(s_{\text{Left}}),\mu(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu(s_{\text{LB}})+\mu(s_{\text{Right}})\big)
in the definitions, which will not impact the analysis since at odd timesteps
\mu(s_{\text{Right}})=\mu(s_{\text{Left}})=0
for both the FH-MFG and
N
-FH-SAG.

Note that with these definitions,
P\in\mathcal{P}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\epsilon}}},R\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
since only
\forall\ s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A},\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
, we have by the definitions:

(8)

\displaystyle\|P(\cdot|s,a,\mu)-P(\cdot|s^{\prime},a^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2d(s,s^{\prime})+2d(a,a^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},

(9)

\displaystyle|R(s,a,\mu)-R(s^{\prime},a^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq d(s,s^{\prime})+d(a,a^{\prime})+2\|\mu-\mu^{\prime}\|_{1},

for any
\alpha,\beta>0
with
\alpha+\beta<1
and
\alpha<\frac{1}{4}
,
using the Lipschitz conditions in (
6
), (
7
).

Step 1: Solution of the FH-MFG.

Next, we solve the infinite player FH-MFG and show that the policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}_{H}:=\{\pi^{*}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}
given by:

\displaystyle\pi_{h}^{*}(a|s):=\begin{cases}1,\text{if $h$ odd and $a=a_{\text{B}}$}\\
\frac{1}{2},\text{if $h$ even}\\
0,\text{if $h$ odd and $a=a_{\text{B}}$}\end{cases}

It is easy to verify in this case that, if
\bm{\mu}^{*}:=\{\mu^{*}_{h}\}_{h}
is induced by
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
:

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{LA}})

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{LB}})=\mu^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{RA}})=\mu^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{RB}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}},\text{ if $h$ odd},

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{Left}})

\displaystyle=\mu^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{Right}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\text{ if $h$ even}.

In this case, the induced rewards in odd steps are state-independent (it is the same for all states
s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}},s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LB}}
), therefore the policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is the optimal best response to the population and a FH-MFG.

In fact,
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
is unique up to modifications in zero-probability sets (e.g., modifying
\pi^{*}_{h}(s_{\text{Left}})
for odd
h
, for which
\Prob[s_{h}=s_{\text{Left}}]=0
).
To see this, for
any
policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi_{H}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mu_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(s_{\text{Left}})

\displaystyle=\mu_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(s_{\text{Right}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\text{ if $h$ even},

\displaystyle\mu_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(s_{\text{LA}})+\mu_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(s_{\text{LB}})

\displaystyle=\mu_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(s_{\text{RA}})+\mu_{h}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}(s_{\text{RB}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},\text{ if $h$ odd},

as the action of the agent does not affect transition probabilities between
s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}
in even rounds.
Moreover, as odd stages, the action rewards terms only depend on the state apart from the positive additional term
\beta\mathbf{1}
, so the only optimal action will be
a_{\text{B}}
.
Finally, for
\alpha>0
, the actions
a_{\text{A}},a_{\text{B}}
must be played with equal probability as otherwise the term
\alpha\mathbf{h}(\mu(s_{\text{RA}}),\mu(s_{\text{RB}}))
will lead to the action with lower probability assigned by being optimal.

Step 2: Population divergence in
N
-FH-MFG.

We will analyze the empirical population distribution deviation from
\bm{\mu}^{*}
, namely, we will lower bound
\Exop[\|\mu_{h}^{*}-\widehat{\mu}_{h}\|_{1}]
.
The results in this step will be valid for
any
policy profile
(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{N})\in\Pi
: we emphasize that at even
h
,
\widehat{\mu}_{h}
is independent of agent policies in the
N
player game.
In this step, we also fix
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\varepsilon}}=8
.

We will analyze
\widehat{\mu}_{h}
at all even steps
h=2m
where
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
.
Define the sequence of random variables for all
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
as
X_{m}:=\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Left}})
.
Define
\mathcal{G}:=\{\frac{k}{N}:k=0,\ldots,N\}
.
Note that for all even
h=2m
, it holds almost surely that
\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s_{\text{Left}}),\widehat{\mu}_{h}(s_{\text{Right}})\in\mathcal{G}
.
By the definition of the MFG, it holds for any
m\geq 0,k\in[N]
that

\displaystyle\Prob[NX_{0}=k]=\binom{N}{k}2^{-N},

\displaystyle\Prob[NX_{m+1}=k|X_{m}]=\binom{N}{k}(\omega_{\varepsilon}(X_{m}))^{k}(1-\omega_{\varepsilon}(X_{m}))^{k},

that is, given
X_{m}
,
NX_{m+1}
is binomially distributed with
NX_{m+1}\sim\operatorname{Binom}(N,\omega_{\epsilon}(X_{m}))
without any dependence on the actions played by agents.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[X_{m+1}|X_{m}\right]=\omega_{\epsilon}(X_{m}),\quad\Varop\mathopen{}\left[X_{m+1}|X_{m}\right]\leq\frac{1}{4N}.

We define the following set
\mathcal{G}_{*}:=\{0,1\}\subset\mathcal{G}
.
By the definition of the mechanics, if
x\in\mathcal{G}_{*},m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
, it holds for all
m^{\prime}>m
that
\Prob[X_{m^{\prime}}=X_{m}|X_{m}=x]=1
, that is once the Markovian random process
X_{m}
hits
\mathcal{G}_{*}
, it will remain in
\mathcal{G}_{*}
.
Furthermore, for
K:=\lfloor\log_{5}\sqrt{N}\rfloor
, and for
k=0,\ldots,K
define the level sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{-1}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G},\quad\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}:\left|x-\frac{1}{2}\right|\geq\frac{5^{k}}{2\sqrt{N}}\right\}.

For all
k\geq K
, define
\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\mathcal{G}_{*}.

Firstly, we have that

\displaystyle\Prob[X_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}]=

\displaystyle\Prob\left[\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{0}^{i}=s_{\text{Left}}\}}-\frac{1}{2}\right|\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{N}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Prob\left[\left|\sum_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{0}^{i}=s_{\text{Left}}\}}-\frac{N}{2}\right|\geq\frac{\sqrt{N}}{2}\right]\geq\frac{1}{10},

where in the last line we applied the anti-concentration result of Lemma
A.4
on the sum of independent Bernoulli random variables
\mathbbm{1}_{\{s_{0}^{i}=s_{\text{Left}}\}}
for
i\in[N]
.

Next, assume that for some
m\in 1,\ldots,K-1
we have
p\in\mathcal{G}_{m}
.
If
\omega_{\epsilon}(p)\in\{0,1\}
, it holds trivially that
\Prob[X_{m+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{m+1}|X_{m}=p]=1
.
Otherwise, if
\omega_{\epsilon}(p)\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\Prob

\displaystyle[X_{m+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{m+1}|X_{m}=p]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Prob\left[|X_{m+1}-\frac{1}{2}|\geq\frac{5^{m+1}}{2\sqrt{N}}\,\middle|\,X_{m}=p\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Prob\left[|\omega_{\epsilon}(p)-\frac{1}{2}|-|X_{m+1}-\omega_{\epsilon}(p)|\geq\frac{5^{m+1}}{2\sqrt{N}}\middle|X_{m}=p\right].

Since in this case
|\omega_{\epsilon}(X_{m})-\frac{1}{2}|=|\omega_{\epsilon}(X_{m})-\omega_{\epsilon}(\frac{1}{2})|\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\epsilon}}|X_{m}-\omega_{\epsilon}(\frac{1}{2})|
, we have

\displaystyle\Prob

\displaystyle[X_{m+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{m+1}|X_{m}=p]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Prob\left[|\omega_{\epsilon}(p)-\frac{1}{2}|-|X_{m+1}-\omega_{\epsilon}(p)|\geq\frac{5^{m+1}}{2\sqrt{N}}\middle|X_{m}=p\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Prob\left[\Big|X_{m+1}-\omega_{\epsilon}(p)\Big|\leq\left|\omega_{\epsilon}(p)-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{5^{m+1}}{2\sqrt{N}}\middle|X_{m}=p\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Prob\left[\Big|X_{m+1}-\omega_{\epsilon}(p)\Big|\leq 8\frac{5^{m}}{2\sqrt{N}}-\frac{5^{m+1}}{2\sqrt{N}}\middle|X_{m}=p\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Prob\left[\Big|X_{m+1}-\omega_{\epsilon}(p)\Big|\leq 3\frac{5^{m}}{2\sqrt{N}}\middle|X_{m}=p\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-2\exp\left\{-\frac{9}{50}25^{m+1}\right\}

where in the last line we invoked the Hoeffding concentration bound (Lemma
A.2
).

Using the above result inductively for
m\in 0,\ldots,K
it holds that

\displaystyle\Prob[X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m}|X_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}]\geq

\displaystyle\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{m}\Prob[X_{m^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}}|X_{m^{\prime}-1}\in\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}-1}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\prod_{m^{\prime}=1}^{m}\left(1-2\exp\left\{-\frac{9}{50}25^{m^{\prime}}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-2\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{9}{50}25^{m^{\prime}+1}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-2\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{9}{2}m^{\prime}-\frac{9}{2}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2e^{-\nicefrac{{9}}{{2}}}}{1-e^{-\nicefrac{{9}}{{2}}}}\right)\geq\frac{9}{10}.

Since for
k>K
,
\Prob[X_{k+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{*}|X_{k}\in\mathcal{G}_{*}]=1
and
\Prob[X_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}]\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{10}}
, it also holds that

\displaystyle\Prob[X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m},\forall m\geq 0]\geq\frac{9}{100}.

Finally, we use the above lower bound on the probability to lower bound the expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{2m}-\mu_{2m}\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Prob[X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m}]\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{\mu}_{2m}-\mu_{2m}\right\|_{1}\left|X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m}\right.\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Prob[X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m}]\,\mathbb{E}\left[2|X_{m}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}|\,\,\left|X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{m}\right.\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{9}{100}\,\min\left\{\frac{5^{m}}{\sqrt{N}},1\right\}.

For odd
h=2m+1
, we also have the inequality

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{2m+1}-\mu_{2m+1}\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\widehat{\mu}_{2m}-\mu_{2m}\|_{1}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{9}{100}\,\min\left\{\frac{5^{m}}{\sqrt{N}},1\right\}.

which completes the first statement of the theorem (as
5^{H/2}=\Omega(2^{H})
).

Step 3: Hitting time for
\mathcal{G}_{*}
.

We will show that the empirical distribution of agent states almost always concentrates on one of
s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}
during the even rounds in the
N
-player game, and bound the expected waiting time for this to happen.
The distributions of agents over states
s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}
in the even rounds are policy independent (they are not affected by which actions are played): hence the results from Step 2 still hold for the population distribution and the expected time computed in this step will be valid for any policy.

For simplicity, we define the FH-MFG for the non-terminating infinite horizon chain, and we will compute value functions up to horizon
H
.
Define the (random) hitting time
\tau
as follows:

\displaystyle\tau:=\inf\{m\geq 0:\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Left}})\in\mathcal{G}_{*}\}=\inf\{m\geq 0:X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{*}\}.

Note that for any
p\in\mathcal{G}
, it holds that
\Prob[X_{m+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{*}|X_{m}=p]=\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Left}})^{N}+\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Right}})^{N}=p^{N}+(1-p)^{N}\geq 2^{-N}.

Therefore for all
m
it holds that
\Prob[\widehat{\mu}_{2m}\notin\mathcal{G}_{*}]\leq\left(1-2^{-N}\right)^{m-1}
.
By the Borel-Cantelli lemma, we can conclude that
\tau<\infty
almost surely, and in particular
T_{\tau}:=\Exop[\tau|X_{0}=x]<\infty
for any
x\in\mathcal{G}
.

Next, we compute the expected value
T_{\tau}
.
Define the following two quantities:

\displaystyle T_{-1}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{-1}}\{\Exop[\tau|X_{0}=x]\}

\displaystyle T_{0}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\{\Exop[\tau|X_{0}=x]\}.

First, we compute an upper bound for
T_{0}
.
Define the event:

\displaystyle E_{0}:=\bigcap_{m^{\prime}\in[K]}\{X_{m^{\prime}}\in\mathcal{G}_{m^{\prime}}\}.

Then,
T_{0}
is upper bounded by:

\displaystyle T_{0}=

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\Exop[\tau|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\Exop[\tau|E_{0},X_{0}=x]\Prob[E_{0}|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle+\Exop[\tau|E_{0}^{c},X_{0}=x]\Prob[E_{0}^{c}|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{0}}\Exop[\tau|E_{0},X_{0}=x]\Prob[E_{0}|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle+\Exop[\tau|E_{0}^{c},X_{0}=x]\Prob[E_{0}^{c}|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\frac{9}{10}+\left(K+T_{-1}\right)\frac{1}{10}=K+\frac{T_{-1}}{10}

where in the last step we used the lower bound on
\Prob[E_{0}]
from Step 2.
Similarly for
T_{-1}
, from the one-sided anti-concentration bound (Lemma
A.4
) it holds that:

\displaystyle T_{-1}\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{G}_{-1}}\Exop[\tau|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Exop[\tau|x\in\mathcal{G}_{0},X_{0}=x]\Prob[x\in\mathcal{G}_{0}|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle+\Exop[\tau|x\notin\mathcal{G}_{0},X_{0}=x]\Prob[x\notin\mathcal{G}_{0}|X_{0}=x]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{20}(T_{0}+1)+\frac{19}{20}(T_{-1}+1),

the last line following since
T_{-1}>T_{0}
by definition.
Solving the two inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle T_{\tau}\leq T_{-1}\leq\frac{200}{9}+\frac{10K}{9}\leq 23+\frac{5}{9}\log_{5}{N}.

Step 4: Ergodic optimal response to
N
-players.

Next, we formulate a policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}}=\{\pi^{\text{br}}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{H-1}\in\Pi^{H}
that is ergodically optimal for the
N
-player game and can exploit a population that deploys the unique FH-MFG-NE.
For all
h
, the optimal policy will be defined by:

\displaystyle\pi^{\text{br}}_{h}(a|s)=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $s=s_{\text{Left}}$, $a=a_{\text{A}}$}\\
1,\text{ if $s=s_{\text{Right}}$, $a=a_{\text{B}}$}\\
1,\text{ if $s\notin\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}\}$, $a=a_{\text{B}}$}\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Intuitively,
\pi^{\text{br}}_{h}
becomes optimal once all the agents are concentrated in the same states during the even rounds, which happens very quickly as shown in Step 3.
Assume that agents
i=2,\ldots N
deploy the unique FH-MFG-NE
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{i}=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
, and for agent
i=1
,
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{1}=\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}}
.
We decompose the three components of the rewards for the first agent, as defined in the construction of the MFG (Step 1):

\displaystyle J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\text{ odd}\\
0\leq h\leq H\end{subarray}}(1-\alpha-\beta)R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}+\alpha R^{1,\mathbf{h}}_{h}+\beta\mathbbm{1}_{\{a^{1}_{h}=a_{B}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha-\beta)\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\text{odd }h=0}^{H-1}R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}\right]+\beta\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor

as by definition clearly
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{a^{1}_{h}=a_{B}\}}\right]=1
for all odd
h
and
R^{\mathbf{h}}_{h}\geq 0
almost surely.

We analyze the terms
R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}
when the first agent follows
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}}
.
By the definition of the dynamics and
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}=g_{1}(\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}(s_{h-1}^{1}),\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}(\widebar{s}_{h-1}^{1}))

where
\widebar{s}_{h-1}^{1}:=s_{\text{Left}}
if
s_{h-1}^{1}=s_{\text{Right}}
and
\widebar{s}_{h-1}^{1}:=s_{\text{Right}}
if
s_{h-1}^{1}=s_{\text{Left}}
.
As
\Prob[s^{1}_{h-1}=\cdot,\ldots,s^{N}_{h-1}=\cdot]
at even step
h-1
is permutation invariant, it holds that
\Prob[s^{1}_{h-1}=\cdot|\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}=\mu]=\mu(\cdot)
for any
\mu\in\mathcal{G}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Exop[R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}]=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\in\mathcal{G}\\
s\in\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}\}\end{subarray}}\Prob[\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}=\mu]\Prob[s^{1}_{h-1}=s|\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}=\mu]

\displaystyle\Exop[R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}|s^{1}_{h-1}=s,\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}=\mu]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\in\mathcal{G}\\
s\in\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}\}\end{subarray}}\Prob[\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}=\mu]\mu(s)g_{1}(\mu(s),\mu(\widebar{s}))\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},

as for any
\mu
, if
s
is such that
\mu(s)\geq\mu(\widebar{s})
then
g_{1}(\mu(s),\mu(\widebar{s}))=1
.
Furthermore, by the definition of the hitting time
\tau
, for any odd
h\geq 1
,
\mathbb{E}\left[R^{\mathbf{g}}_{h}\left|2\tau<h\right.\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[R^{\mathbf{g}}_{h}\left|\widehat{\mu}_{h-1}(s_{\text{Left}})\in\mathcal{G}_{*}\right.\right]=1
,
as after time
2\tau
the action
a_{\text{A}}
will be optimal with reward
R^{\mathbf{g}}_{h}=1
almost surely, as
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{br}
chooses action
a_{\text{A}}
at even steps.

Finally, using the lower bound of
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for
R^{\mathbf{g}}_{h}
when
h<2\tau
and that
R^{\mathbf{g}}_{h}=1
when
h>2\tau
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\text{ odd}\\
0\leq h\leq H\end{subarray}}R^{\mathbf{g}}_{h}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\text{ odd}\\
0\leq h\leq\min\{2\tau,H\}\end{subarray}}R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}h\text{ odd}\\
\min\{2\tau,H\}+1\leq h<H\end{subarray}}R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{h}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\min\left\{\tau,\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}+\left(\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor-\min\left\{\tau,\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\min\left\{\tau,\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\tau\right]=\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor-\frac{T_{\tau}}{2}

Merging the inequalities above, we obtain

\displaystyle\,J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\geq(1-\alpha-\beta)\left(\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor-\frac{T_{\tau}}{2}\right)+\beta\left\lfloor\frac{H}{2}\right\rfloor.

Step 5: Bounding exploitability.

Finally, we will upper bound also the expected reward of the FH-MFG-NE policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
and hence lower bound the exploitability.
Our conclusion will be that
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
suffers from a non-vanishing exploitability for large
H
, as
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}}
becomes the best response policy after
H\gtrsim\log N

by the Hoeffding inequality.
Under the event
\widebar{E}
, it holds that
\widehat{\mu}_{t}(s_{\text{LA}})+\widehat{\mu}_{t}(s_{\text{LA}})+\widehat{\mu}_{t}(s_{\text{LA}})+\widehat{\mu}_{t}(s_{\text{LA}})>\nicefrac{{4}}{{9}}
almost surely at all

.

Fix
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that
\left|\nicefrac{{N_{0}}}{{N}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\right|\leq\nicefrac{{1}}{{18}}
, in this step we will condition on
E_{0}:=\{\widebar{N}:=N_{0}\}
.
Once again define the random process
X_{m}
for

such that

\displaystyle X_{m}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Left}})}{\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Left}})+\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Right}})},\text{ if $m$ odd}\\
\frac{\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Right}})}{\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Left}})+\widehat{\mu}_{2m}(s_{\text{Right}})},\text{ if $m$ even}\end{cases}

with the modification at odd

(oscillating between

) from the FH-SAG case.
It still holds that

is Markovian, and given

N_{0}X_{m+1}\sim\operatorname{Binom}(N_{0},\omega_{\epsilon}(X_{m}))
.
As before,

is independent from the policies of agents.

Define
K:=\lfloor\log_{2}\sqrt{N_{0}}\rfloor
,
\mathcal{G}:=\{\nicefrac{{k}}{{N_{0}}}:k=0,\ldots,N_{0}\}
,
\mathcal{G}_{*}:=\{0,1\}\subset\mathcal{G}
and the level sets once again as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{-1}:=\mathcal{G},\quad\mathcal{G}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in\mathcal{G}:\left|x-\frac{1}{2}\right|\geq\frac{2^{k}}{2\sqrt{N_{0}}}\right\}\text{ when $k\leq K$},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{K+1}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{G}_{*}.

As before, using the Markov property, Hoeffding, and the fact that
|\omega_{\epsilon}(x)-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}|\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2\epsilon}}|x-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}|
we obtain
\forall k\in 0,\ldots,K-1
,
\forall m
that

\displaystyle\Prob[X_{m+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{0}|X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{-1},E_{0}]

\displaystyle\geq\nicefrac{{1}}{{20}}

\displaystyle\Prob[X_{m+1}\in\mathcal{G}_{k+1}|X_{m}\in\mathcal{G}_{k},E_{0}]

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{k}:=1-2\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{8}4^{k+1}\right\},

hence from the analysis before we have the lower bound

\displaystyle\Exop[|X_{m}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}|\,|E_{0}]\geq C_{1}\,\min\left\{\frac{2^{m}}{\sqrt{N_{0}}},1\right\},

for some absolute constant

.

Step 3. Exploitability lower bound.

As in the case of FH-MFG, the ergodic optimal policy is given by

\displaystyle\widebar{\pi}(a|s)=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $s=s_{\text{Left}}$, $a=a_{\text{A}}$}\\
1,\text{ if $s=s_{\text{Right}}$, $a=a_{\text{A}}$}\\
1,\text{ if $s\notin\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}\}$, $a=a_{\text{B}}$}\\
0,\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

We define the shorthand functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{*}:=\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}\},\quad

\displaystyle Q(\mu):=(Q_{L}(\mu),Q_{R}(\mu)),

\displaystyle Q_{\text{min}}(\mu):=\min\{Q_{L}(\mu),Q_{R}(\mu)\},\quad

\displaystyle Q_{\text{max}}:=\max\{Q_{L}(\mu),Q_{R}(\mu)\}.

We condition on
E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}:=\{s^{1}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\}
, that is the first agent starts from states
\{s_{\text{Left}},s_{\text{Right}}\}
, the analysis will be similar under event
E^{c}_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
.
As in the case of FH-MFG, due to permutation invariance, it holds for any odd
t
and
\mu\in\{\mu^{\prime}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}:N_{0}\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}^{2}\}
that

\displaystyle\Prob[s_{t}^{1}\in\{s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LB}}\}|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},Q(\widehat{\mu}_{t})=\mu]=Q_{L}(\mu)

\displaystyle\Prob[s_{t}^{1}\in\{s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}}\}|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},Q(\widehat{\mu}_{t})=\mu]=Q_{R}(\mu),

therefore expressing the error component due to
\mathbf{g}
as
R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{t}
and expressing some repeating conditionals as
\bullet
:

\displaystyle\widebar{G}_{t}^{\mu}:=

\displaystyle\Exop\left[R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{t}\middle|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},Q(\widehat{\mu}_{t})=\mu,a_{t}^{1}\sim\widebar{\pi}(s_{t}^{1}),\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{when $i\neq 1$}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}^{*}}\Prob[s^{1}_{t}=s|Q(\widehat{\mu}_{t})=\mu,\bullet]\Exop[R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{t}|s^{1}_{t}=s,Q(\widehat{\mu}_{t})=\mu,\bullet]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)+\frac{Q_{\text{min}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}Q_{\text{min}}(\mu).

Similarly, since
\pi^{*}(a|s)=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
for any
s\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, it holds that

\displaystyle G_{t}^{\mu}:=

\displaystyle\Exop\left[R^{1,\mathbf{g}}_{t}\middle|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},Q(\widehat{\mu}_{t})=\mu,\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{ $\forall i$}\end{subarray}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{Q_{\text{min}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}.

Therefore, given the population distribution between
s_{\text{LA}},s_{\text{LB}}
and
s_{\text{RA}},s_{\text{RB}}
, the expected difference in rewards for the two policies is

\displaystyle\widebar{G}_{t}^{\mu}-G_{t}^{\mu}=

\displaystyle\left(Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(Q_{\text{min}}(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{Q_{\text{min}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{2}-Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)\right)\frac{Q_{\text{min}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(1-\frac{Q_{\text{min}}(\mu)}{Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\left(Q_{\text{max}}(\mu)-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}.

Therefore from above, we conclude that

\displaystyle\Exop[\widebar{G}_{t}^{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}-G_{t}^{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\,|E_{0}]

\displaystyle\geq\Exop[2|X_{\frac{t-1}{2}}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}|^{2}\,|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}]\geq 2C_{1}^{2}\,\min\left\{\frac{2^{t}}{2N_{0}},1\right\}.

Using the lower bound above, the conditional expected difference in discounted total reward is

\displaystyle\Exop

\displaystyle\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s^{1}_{t},a^{1}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t})|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},a_{t}^{1}\sim\widebar{\pi}(s_{t}^{1}),\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{when $i\neq 1$}\end{subarray}\big]

\displaystyle\quad-\Exop\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s^{1}_{t},a^{1}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t})|E_{0},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{$\forall i$}\end{subarray}\big]

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha-\beta)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2C_{1}^{2}\,\gamma^{2k+1}\min\left\{\frac{2^{2k}}{N_{0}},1\right\}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{2}}{N_{0}}\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor\log_{4}N_{0}\rfloor}(4\gamma^{2})^{k}+\frac{C_{3}}{N_{0}}\sum_{k=\lfloor\log_{4}N_{0}\rfloor}^{\infty}\gamma^{2k}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{4}((4\gamma^{2})^{\log_{4}N_{0}}-1)}{N_{0}}+C_{5}\frac{(\gamma^{2})^{\log_{4}N_{0}}N_{0}^{-1}}{1-\gamma^{2}}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}

\displaystyle\geq C_{6}N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma}+C_{7}\frac{N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma-1}}{1-\gamma}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}.

Taking expectation over
N_{0}
(using
\Exop[\widebar{N}|E^{*}]=\nicefrac{{N}}{{2}}
and Jensen’s):

\displaystyle\Exop

\displaystyle\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s^{1}_{t},a^{1}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t})|E^{*},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},a_{t}^{1}\sim\widebar{\pi}(s_{t}^{1}),\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{when $i\neq 1$}\end{subarray}\big]

\displaystyle-\Exop\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s^{1}_{t},a^{1}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t})|E^{*},E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}},\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{$\forall i$}\end{subarray}\big]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{6}N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma}+C_{7}\frac{N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma-2}}{1-\gamma}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}

While the analysis above assumes event
E_{\mathcal{S}^{*}}
, the same analysis lower bound follows with a shift between even and odd steps when
s^{1}_{0}\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}
, hence

\displaystyle\Exop

\displaystyle\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s^{1}_{t},a^{1}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t})|E^{*},a_{t}^{1}\sim\widebar{\pi}(s_{t}^{1}),\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{when $i\neq 1$}\end{subarray}\big]

\displaystyle-\Exop\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s^{1}_{t},a^{1}_{t},\widehat{\mu}_{t})|E^{*},\begin{subarray}{c}a_{t}^{i}\sim\pi^{*}(s_{t}^{i}),\\
\text{$\forall i$}\end{subarray}\big]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{6}N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma}+C_{7}\frac{N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma-2}}{1-\gamma}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}

Finally, we conclude the proof with the observation

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}J_{P,R}^{\gamma,N,(1)}(\pi,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})-J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle J_{P,R}^{\gamma,N,(1)}(\widebar{\pi},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})-J_{P,R}^{H,N,(1)}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C_{6}N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma}+C_{7}\frac{N_{0}^{\log_{2}\gamma-2}}{1-\gamma}-\frac{2\alpha}{1-\gamma}-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\Prob[\widebar{E}^{c}],

where
\Prob[\widebar{E}^{c}]=O(e^{-N^{2}})
and we pick
\alpha=\mathcal{O}(e^{-N})
.

Appendix B
Intractability Results

B.1.
Fundamentals of PPAD

We first introduce standard definitions and tools, mostly taken from
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
;
Goldberg 2011
;
Papadimitriou 1994
)
.

Notations.

For a finite set
\Sigma
, we denote by
\Sigma^{n}
the set of tuples
n
elements from
\Sigma
, and by
\Sigma^{*}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\Sigma^{n}
the set of finite sequences of elements of
\Sigma
.
For any
\alpha\in\Sigma
, let
\alpha^{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
denote the
n
-tuple
(\underbrace{\alpha,\ldots,\alpha}_{\text{$n$ times}})
.
For
x\in\Sigma^{*}
, by
|x|
we denote the length of the sequence
x
.
Finally, the following function will be useful, defined for any
\alpha>0
:

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}:

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\alpha]

\displaystyle u_{\alpha}(x)

\displaystyle:=\max\{0,\min\{\alpha,x\}\}=\begin{cases}\alpha,\text{ if $x\geq\alpha$},\\
x,\text{ if $0\leq x\leq\alpha$},\\
0,\text{ if $x\leq 0$}.\end{cases}

We define a search problem
\mathcal{S}
on alphabet
\Sigma
as a relation from a set
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}\subset\Sigma^{*}
to
\Sigma^{*}
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{S}}
, the image of
x
under
\mathcal{S}
satisfies
\mathcal{S}_{x}\subset\Sigma^{|x|^{k}}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, and given
y\in\Sigma^{|x|^{k}}
m whether
y\in\mathcal{S}_{x}
is decidable in polynomial time.

Intuitively speaking, PPAD is the complexity class of search problems that can be shown to always have a solution using a “parity argument” on a directed graph.
The simplest complete example (the example that defines the problem class) of PPAD problems is the computational problem
End-of-The-Line
.
The problem, formally defined below, can be summarized as such: given a directed graph where each node has in-degree and out-degree at most one and given a node that is a source in this graph (i.e., no incoming edge but one outgoing edge), find another node that is a sink or a source.
Such a node can be always shown to exist using a simple parity argument.

Definition B.1 (
End-of-The-Line

(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
)
).

The computational problem
End-of-The-Line
is defined as follows: given two binary circuits
S,P
each with
n
input bits and
n
output bits such that
P(0^{n})=0^{n}\neq S(s^{n})
, find an input
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that
P(S(x))\neq x\text{ or }S(P(x))\neq x\neq 0^{n}
.

The obvious solution to the above is to follow the graph node by node using the given circuits until we reach a sink: however, this can take exponential time as the graph size can be exponential in the bit descriptions of the circuits.
It is believed that
End-of-The-Line
is difficult
(
Goldberg 2011
)
, that there is no efficient way to use the bit descriptions of the circuits
S,P
to find another node with degree 1.

B.2.
Proof of Intractability of Stat-MFG

We reduce any
\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
problem to the problem
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
for some simple transition function
P\in\mathcal{P}^{\text{Sim}}
.

Let
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})
be a generalized circuit to be reduced to a stable distribution computation problem.
Let
V=|\mathcal{V}|\geq 1
.
We will define a game that has at most
V+1
states and
|\mathcal{A}|=1
actions, that is, agent policy will not have significance, and it will suffice to determine simple transition probabilities
P(s^{\prime}|s,\mu)
for all
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.

The proposed system will have a base state
s_{\text{base}}\in\mathcal{S}
and 1 additional state
s_{v}
associated with the gate whose output is
v\in\mathcal{V}
.
Our construction will be sparse: only transition probabilities in between states associated with a gate and
s_{\text{base}}
will take positive values.
We define the useful constants
\theta:=\frac{1}{8V},B:=\frac{1}{4}
.

Given an (approximately) stable distribution
\mu^{*}
of
P
, for each vertex
v
we will read the satisfying assignment for the
\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
problem by the value
u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(s_{v}))
.
For each possible gate, we define the following gadgets.

Binary assignment gadget.

For a gate of the form
G_{\leftarrow}(\zeta||v)
, we will add one state
s_{v}
such that

If
\zeta=1:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{v},\mu)=1,\\
P(s_{v}|s_{v},\mu)=0,\\
P(s_{v}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)=\frac{\theta}{\max\{B,\,\mu(s_{\text{base}})\}}\end{cases}

If
\zeta=0:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{v},\mu)=1,\\
P(s_{v}|s_{v},\mu)=0,\\
P(s_{v}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)=0\end{cases}

Weighted addition gadget.

Next, we implement the addition gadget
G_{\times,+}(\alpha,\beta|v_{1},v_{2}|v)
for
\alpha,\beta\in[-1,1]
.
In this case, we also add one state
s_{v}
to the game, and define the transition probabilities:

\displaystyle P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{v},\mu)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle P(s_{v}|s_{v},\mu)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle P(s_{v}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{u_{\theta}(\alpha u_{\theta}(\mu(v_{1}))+\beta u_{\theta}(\mu(v_{2})))}{\max\{B,\,\mu(s_{\text{base}})\}}

Brittle comparison gadget.

For the comparison gate
G_{<}(|v_{1},v_{1}|v)
, we also add one state
s_{v}
to the game.
Define the function
p_{\delta}:[-1,1]\rightarrow[0,1]

\displaystyle p_{\delta}(x,y):=u_{1}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\delta^{-1}(x-y)\right),

for any
\delta>0
.
In particular, if
x\geq y+\delta
, then
p_{\delta}(x,y)=1
, and if
x\leq y-\delta
, then
p_{\delta}(x,y)=0
.
We define the probability transitions to and from
s_{v}
as

\displaystyle P(s_{v}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta p_{8\varepsilon}(\theta^{-1}u_{\theta}(\mu(s_{1})),\theta^{-1}u_{\theta}(\mu(s_{2})))}{\max\{B,\,\mu(s_{\text{base}})\}},

\displaystyle P(s_{v}|s_{v},\mu)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{v},\mu)

\displaystyle=1.

Finally, after all
s_{v}
have been added, we complete the definition of
P
by setting

\displaystyle P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)=1-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}},\mu).

We first verify that the above assignment is a valid transition probability matrix for any
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.
It is clear from definitions that for any
\mu,s\neq s_{\text{base}}

is a valid probability distribution as long as
8\varepsilon<1
.
Moreover, for any
s\neq s_{\text{base}}

, and it also holds that

\displaystyle P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)=1-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}P(s^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}},\mu)\geq 1-\frac{V\theta}{B}\geq 0

so
P(\cdot|s_{\text{base}},\mu)
is a valid probability transition matrix.
Finally, the defined transition probability function
P
is Lipschitz in the components of
\mu
, and
P
can be defined as a composition of simple functions, hence

.
Finally, in this defined MFG, it holds that
V+1=|\mathcal{S}|
, since for each gate in the generalized circuit we defined one additional state.

Error propagation.

We finally analyze the error propagation of the stationary distribution problem in terms of the generalized circuit.
Without loss of generality we assume
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{8}
.
First, for any solution of the
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
problem
\mu^{*}
, whenever
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{8}

\displaystyle\left|\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mu^{*}(s)P(s_{\text{base}}|s,\mu^{*})\right|\leq\frac{1}{8|\mathcal{S}|},

hence (using

) we have the lower bound on

given by:

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu^{*}(s)P(s_{\text{base}}|s,\mu^{*})-\frac{1}{8V}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})P(s_{\text{base}}|s_{\text{base}},\mu^{*})+\sum_{s\neq s_{\text{base}}}\mu^{*}(s)P(s_{\text{base}}|s,\mu^{*})-\frac{1}{8V}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})\left(1-\frac{V\theta}{B}\right)+\sum_{s\neq s_{\text{base}}}\mu^{*}(s)-\frac{1}{8V}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})\left(1-\frac{V\theta}{B}\right)+(1-\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}}))-\frac{1}{8V}

\displaystyle\implies\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})\geq\frac{1-\frac{1}{8V}}{1+\frac{V\theta}{B}}\geq B=\frac{1}{4}.

We will show that a solution of the
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
can be converted into a
\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle v\rightarrow u_{1}\left(\frac{\mu^{*}(s_{v})}{\theta}\right),

for some appropriate

Case 1: Binary assignment error.

First, assume
G_{\leftarrow}(\zeta||v)\in\mathcal{G}

If

\displaystyle|\mu^{*}(s_{v})-\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})P(s_{v}|s_{\text{base}},\mu^{*})|

\displaystyle\left|\mu^{*}(s_{v})-\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})\frac{\theta}{\max\{B,\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})\}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|}

\displaystyle|\mu^{*}(s_{v})|

Case 2: Weighted addition error.

\displaystyle|\mu^{*}(s_{v})-\mu^{*}(s_{\text{base}})P(s_{v}|s_{\text{base}},\mu^{*})|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|}

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|}

u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(v_{1}))\geq u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(v_{2}))+8\varepsilon
and
u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(v_{1}))\leq u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(v_{2}))-8\varepsilon
.
In the first case, we obtain

\displaystyle\theta^{-1}u_{\theta}(\mu^{*}(v_{1}))\geq\theta^{-1}u_{\theta}(\mu^{*}(v_{2}))+8\varepsilon,

which implies by the definition of
p_{8\varepsilon}

\displaystyle|\mu^{*}(s_{v})-\theta|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|}

\displaystyle|u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(s_{v}))-1|\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|\theta}

\displaystyle u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(s_{v}))\geq

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|\theta}\geq 1-8\varepsilon.

In the second case
u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(v_{1}))\leq u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(v_{2}))-8\varepsilon
, it follows by a similar analysis that

\displaystyle u_{1}(\theta^{-1}\mu^{*}(s_{v}))\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{|\mathcal{S}|\theta}\leq 8\varepsilon.

Hence, in the above, we reduced the
8\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
problem to the
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
problem, completing the proof that
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
is
PPAD
-hard.
The fact that
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
is in
PPAD
on the other hand easily follows from the fact that
\varepsilon
-
StatDist
is the fixed point problem for the (simple) operator
\Gamma_{P}
, reducing it to the
End-of-the-Line
problem by a standard construction
(
Daskalakis et al. 2009
)
.

B.3.
Proof of Intractability of FH-MFG

As in the previous section, we reduce any
\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
problem
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{V})
to the problem
(\text{$\varepsilon^{2}$},\text{$2$})
-
FH-Nash
for some simple reward
R\in\mathcal{R}^{\text{Sim}}
.
Once again let
V=|\mathcal{V}|
.

Associated with each
v\in\mathcal{V}
we define
s_{v,1},s_{v,0},s_{v,\text{base}}\in\mathcal{S}
.
The initial distribution is defined as

\displaystyle\mu_{0}(s_{v,\text{base}})=\frac{1}{V},\forall v\in\mathcal{V},

and we define two actions for each state:
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{1},a_{0}\}
.
The state transition probability matrix is given by

\displaystyle P(s|s_{v,\text{base}},a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if }a=a_{1},s=s_{v,1},\\
1,\text{ if }a=a_{0},s=s_{v,0},\\
0,\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle P(s_{v,\text{base}}|s,a)

\displaystyle=0,\forall v\in\mathcal{V},s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A},

and an
\varepsilon
satisfying assignment
p:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow[0,1]
will be read by
p(v)=\pi_{1}^{*}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})
for the optimal policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}=\{\pi_{h}\}_{h=0}^{1}
.
We will specify population-dependent rewards
R\in\mathcal{R}^{\text{Simple}}
, since
R
will not depend on the particular action but only the state and population distribution, we will concisely denote
R(s,a,\mu)=R(s,\mu)
.
It will be the case that

\displaystyle R(s_{v,\text{base}},\mu)=0,\forall v\in\mathcal{V},\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}.

We assign
R(s_{v,1},\mu)=R(s_{v,0},\mu)=0,\forall\mu
for any vertex
v
of the generalized circuit that is not the output of any gate in
\mathcal{G}
.

Binary assignment gadget.

For any binary assignment gate
G_{\leftarrow}(\zeta||v)
, we assign

\displaystyle R(s_{v,1},\mu)

\displaystyle=\zeta,

\displaystyle R(s_{v,0},\mu)

\displaystyle=1-\zeta,\forall\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}.

Weighted addition gadget.

For any gate
G_{\times,+}(\alpha,\beta|v_{1},v_{2}|v)
,

\displaystyle R(s_{v,1},\mu)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(u_{1}(\alpha V\mu(s_{v_{1},1})+\beta V\mu(s_{v_{2},1}))-V\mu(s_{v,1})),

\displaystyle R(s_{v,0},\mu)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(V\mu(s_{v,1})-u_{1}(\alpha V\mu(s_{v_{1},1})+\beta V\mu(s_{v_{2},1}))),

for all
\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Brittle comparison gadget.

For any gate
G_{<}(|v_{1},v_{2}|v)
, we define the rewards for states
s_{v,1},s_{v,0}
as

\displaystyle R(s_{v,1},\mu)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(V\mu(s_{v_{2},1})-V\mu(s_{v_{1},1})),

\displaystyle R(s_{v,0},\mu)

\displaystyle=u_{1}(V\mu(s_{v_{1},1})-V\mu(s_{v_{2},1})),\forall\mu\in\Delta_{\mathcal{S}}.

Now assume that
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}=\{\pi^{*}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{1}
is a solution to the
(\text{$\varepsilon^{2}$},\text{$2$})
-
FH-Nash
problem and
\bm{\mu}^{*}=\Lambda_{P,\mu_{0}}^{2}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})
, that is, assume that for all
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi^{2}
,

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{V}.

Firstly, if
\mu_{1}^{*}
is induced by
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}
, it holds that
\forall v\in\mathcal{V}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v,\text{base}})=0,\quad

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v,1})=\frac{1}{V}\pi_{0}^{*}(s_{v,1}|s_{v,\text{base}}),

\displaystyle\mu_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle(s_{v,0})=\frac{1-\pi_{0}^{*}(s_{v,1}|s_{v,\text{base}})}{V}.

Furthermore, a policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}}\in\Pi_{2}
that is the best response to
\bm{\mu}^{*}:=\{\mu^{*}_{0},\mu^{*}_{1}\}
can be always formulated as:

\displaystyle\pi_{0}^{\text{br}}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $R(s_{v,1},\mu_{1}^{*})>R(s_{v,1},\mu_{1}^{*})$},\\
0,\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\pi_{0}^{\text{br}}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})

\displaystyle=1-\pi_{0}^{\text{br}}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}}),

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\text{br}}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\pi_{1}^{\text{br}}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})

\displaystyle=0.

By the optimality conditions, we will have

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{V}.

Furthermore, for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
it holds that

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\text{br}})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mu_{0}(s_{v,\text{base}})[\max_{s\in\{s_{v,1},s_{v,0}\}}R(s,\mu^{*}_{1})

\displaystyle\qquad-\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})R(s_{v,1},\mu^{*}_{1})-\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})R(s_{v,0},\mu^{*}_{1})]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\max_{s\in\{s_{v,1},s_{v,0}\}}R(s,\mu^{*}_{1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})R(s_{v,1},\mu^{*}_{1})-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})R(s_{v,0},\mu^{*}_{1})

as the summands are all positive.
We prove that all gate conditions are satisfied case by base.
Without loss of generality, we assume
\varepsilon<1
below.

Case 1.

It follows that for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
such that
G_{\leftarrow}(\zeta||v)\in\mathcal{G}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})\zeta-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})(1-\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{V}

\displaystyle 1-\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})\zeta-(1-\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}}))(1-\zeta)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle\zeta(1-2\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}}))+\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})\leq\varepsilon^{2}\leq\varepsilon.

The above implies
\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})\geq 1-\varepsilon
if
\zeta=1
, and if
\zeta=0
, it implies
\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})\leq\varepsilon
.

Case 2.

For any
v\in\mathcal{V}
such that
G_{\times,+}(\alpha,\beta|v_{1},v_{2}|v)\in\mathcal{G}
, denoting in short

\displaystyle\square

\displaystyle:=u_{1}(\alpha V\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v_{1},1})+\beta V\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v_{2},1}))

\displaystyle=u_{1}(\alpha\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v_{1},1})+\beta\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v_{2},1})),

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})

we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}

\displaystyle\max\big\{u_{1}(V\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v,1})-\square),u_{1}(\square-V\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v,1}))\big\}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})u_{1}(\square-V\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v,1}))

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})u_{1}(V\mu_{1}^{*}(s_{v,1})-\square)\leq\varepsilon^{2},

or equivalently

\displaystyle\max\big\{u_{1}(p_{1}-\square),

\displaystyle u_{1}(\square-p_{1})\big\}-p_{1}u_{1}(\square-p_{1})-p_{0}u_{1}(p_{1}-\square)\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

First, assume it holds that
p_{1}\leq\square
, then:

\displaystyle u_{1}(\square-p_{1})-p_{1}u_{1}(\square-p_{1})\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle(1-p_{1})(\square-p_{1})\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}.

The above implies that either
p_{1}\geq 1-\varepsilon
or
u_{1}(\square-p_{1})\leq\varepsilon
, both cases implying
|\square-p_{1}|\leq\varepsilon
since we assume
\square\geq p_{1}
.
To conclude case 2, assume that
\square<p_{1}
, then

\displaystyle u_{1}(p_{1}-\square)-(1-p_{1})u_{1}(p_{1}-\square)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2},

\displaystyle p_{1}(p_{1}-\square)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2},

then either
p_{1}\leq\varepsilon
or
p_{1}-\square\leq\varepsilon
, either case implying once again
|\square-p_{1}|\leq\varepsilon
.

Case 3.

Finally, for any
v\in\mathcal{V}
such that
G_{<}(|v_{1},v_{2}|v)\in\mathcal{G}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}

\displaystyle\max\Big\{u_{1}(\mu(s_{v_{2},1})-\mu(s_{v_{1},1})),u_{1}(\mu(s_{v_{1},1})-\mu(s_{v_{2},1}))\Big\}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})u_{1}(\mu(s_{v_{1},1})-\mu(s_{v_{2},1}))

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{V}\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})u_{1}(\mu(s_{v_{2},1})-\mu(s_{v_{1},1}))\leq\varepsilon

hence once again using the shorthand notation:

\displaystyle\triangle

\displaystyle:=V\mu^{*}_{1}(s_{v_{2},1})-V\mu^{*}_{1}(s_{v_{1},1})=\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v_{2},1})-\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v_{1},1})

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})

\displaystyle p_{0}

\displaystyle:=\pi^{*}_{0}(a_{0}|s_{v,\text{base}})

we have the inequality:

\displaystyle u_{1}(|\triangle|)-p_{1}u_{1}(\triangle)-p_{0}u_{1}(-\triangle)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle u_{1}(|\triangle|)-p_{1}u_{1}(\triangle)-(1-p_{1})u_{1}(-\triangle)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

First assume
\triangle\geq\varepsilon
, then

\displaystyle u_{1}(\triangle)(1-p_{1})\leq\varepsilon^{2}\implies 1-\varepsilon\leq p_{1},

and conversely if
\triangle\leq-\varepsilon
,

\displaystyle u_{1}(-\triangle)p_{1}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\implies p_{1}\leq\varepsilon,

concluding that the comparison gate conditions are
\varepsilon
satisfied for the assignment
v\rightarrow\pi_{0}^{\text{br}}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})
.

The three cases above conclude that
v\rightarrow\pi_{0}^{\text{br}}(a_{1}|s_{v,\text{base}})
is an
\varepsilon
-satisfying assignment for the generalized circuit
(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{G})
, concluding the proof that
(\text{$\varepsilon_{0}$},\text{$2$})
-
FH-Nash
is
PPAD
-hard for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
.
The fact that
(\text{$\varepsilon_{0}$},\text{$2$})
-
FH-Nash
is in
PPAD
follows from the fact that the NE is a fixed point of a simple map on space
\Pi_{2}
, see for instance
(
Huang
et al. 2023
)
.

B.4.
Proof of Intractability of
2
-
FH-Linear

Our reduction will be similar to the previous section, however, instead of reducing a
\varepsilon
-
GCircuit
to an MFG, we will reduce a
2
player general sum normal form game,
2
-
Nash
, to a finite horizon mean field game with linear rewards with horizon
H=2
(
2
-
FH-Linear
).
Let
\varepsilon>0,K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0},A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{1},K_{2}}
be given for a
2
-
Nash
problem.
We assume without loss of generality that
K_{1}>1
, as otherwise, the solution of
2
-
Nash
is trivial.

This time, we define finite horizon game with
K_{1}+K_{2}+2
states, denoted
\mathcal{S}:=\{s_{\text{base}}^{1},s_{\text{base}}^{2},s^{1}_{1},\ldots,s^{1}_{K_{1}},s^{2}_{1},\ldots,s^{2}_{K_{2}}\}
.
Without loss of generality, we can assume
K_{1}\leq K_{2}
.
The action set will be defined by
\mathcal{A}=[K_{2}]=\{1,\ldots,K_{2}\}
.
The initial state distribution will be given by
\mu_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{1})=\mu_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, with
\mu_{0}(s)=0
for all other states.
We define the transitions for any
s\in\mathcal{S},a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
as:

\displaystyle P(s|s_{\text{base}}^{1},a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $s=s^{1}_{a}$ and $a\leq K_{1}$},\\
1,\text{ if $s=s^{1}_{a}$ and $a>K_{1}$},\\
0,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle P(s|s_{\text{base}}^{2},a)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $s=s^{2}_{a}$},\\
0,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle P(s|s^{1}_{a},a^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $s=s^{1}_{a}$},\\
0,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\qquad P(s|s^{2}_{a},a^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $s=s^{2}_{a}$},\\
0,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Finally, we will define the linear reward function as for all
a\in[K_{2}]
:

\displaystyle R(s_{\text{base}}^{1},a,\mu)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle R(s_{\text{base}}^{2},a,\mu)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle R(s^{1}_{a},a,\mu)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,\text{if $a>K_{1}$},\\
\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\mu(s^{2}_{a^{\prime}})A_{a,a^{\prime}}\end{cases}

\displaystyle R(s^{2}_{a},a,\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{1}]}\mu(s^{1}_{a^{\prime}})B_{a^{\prime},a}.

In words, the states
s_{\text{base}}^{1},s_{\text{base}}^{2}
represent the two players of the
2
-
Nash
, and an agent starting from one of the initial base states
s_{\text{base}}^{1},s_{\text{base}}^{2}
of the FH-MFG at round
h=0
will be placed at
h=1
at a state representing the (pure) strategies of each player respectively.

Given the game description above, assume
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}=\{\pi_{h}^{*}\}_{h=0}^{1}
is an
\varepsilon
solution of the
2
-
FH-Linear
.
Then, it holds for the induced distribution
\bm{\mu}^{*}:=\{\mu^{*}_{h}\}_{h=0}^{1}=\Lambda^{H}_{P}
that:

\displaystyle\mu_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu_{0},

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{1}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime},a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\mu_{0}(s^{\prime})\pi^{*}(a^{\prime}|s^{\prime})P(s|s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\pi_{0}(i|s_{\text{base}}^{1}),\text{ if $s=s^{1}_{i}$, for some $i\in[K_{1}]$},\\
\frac{1}{2}\pi_{0}(i|s_{\text{base}}^{2}),\text{ if $s=s^{2}_{i}$, for some $i\in[K_{2}]$},\\
\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[K_{1}]}\pi_{0}(i|s_{\text{base}}^{1}),\text{ if $s=s_{\text{base}}^{1}$,}\\
0,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

By definition of the
\varepsilon
finite horizon Nash equilibrium,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P,R}^{H}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})

\displaystyle:=\max_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\Pi^{H}}V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}),\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\Lambda^{H}_{P}(\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*}),\boldsymbol{\pi})\leq\varepsilon,

in particular, it holds for any
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\Pi_{2}
that

(12)

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi})-V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{*})\leq\varepsilon.

By direct computation, the value functions
V_{P,R}^{H}
can be written directly in this case for any
\pi
:

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\pi_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\mu_{1}^{*}(s^{2}_{a^{\prime}})A_{a,a^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\mu_{1}^{*}(s^{1}_{a})B_{a,a^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\pi_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})A_{a,a^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})\pi^{*}_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})B_{a,a^{\prime}}

We analyze two different cases, accounting for a possible imbalance between the strategy spaces of the two players,
[K_{1}]
and
[K_{2}]
.

Case 1.
Assume
K_{1}=K_{2}
.
Then,
V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi})
simplifies to

\displaystyle V_{P,R}^{H}(\bm{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{\pi})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})A_{a,a^{\prime}}

(13)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8}\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})\pi^{*}_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})B_{a,a^{\prime}}.

Take an arbitrary mixed strategy
\sigma_{1}\in\Delta_{[K_{1}]}
and define the policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{A}=\{\pi_{A,h}\}_{h=0}^{1}\in\Pi^{2}
so that

\displaystyle\pi_{A,0}(s_{\text{base}}^{1})=\sigma_{1},\quad\pi_{A,0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2})=\pi^{*}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2}),\quad\pi_{A,1}=\pi^{*}_{1}.

Then, placing
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{A}
in equations (
13
) and (
12
), it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\sigma_{1}(a)\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})A_{a,a^{\prime}}

(14)

\displaystyle-\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})A_{a,a^{\prime}}\leq 8\varepsilon.

Similarly, for any
\sigma_{2}\in\Delta_{[}K_{2}]
, replacing
\boldsymbol{\pi}
in equations (
13
) and (
12
) with a policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{B}
such that

\displaystyle\pi_{B,0}(s_{\text{base}}^{1})=\pi^{*}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{1}),\quad\pi_{B,0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2})=\sigma_{2},\quad\pi_{B,1}=\pi^{*}_{1},

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}

\displaystyle\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\sigma_{2}(a)\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{1})B_{a,a^{\prime}}

(15)

\displaystyle-\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})\pi^{*}_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})B_{a,a^{\prime}}\leq 8\varepsilon.

Hence, the resulting equations (
14
), (
15
) imply that in this case the strategy profile
(\pi^{*}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{1}),\pi^{*}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2}))
is a
8\varepsilon
-Nash equilibrium for the normal form game defined by matrices
A,B
.

Case 2.

Next, we analyze the case when
1<K_{1}<K_{2}
.
If
\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{1}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{1})=0
, then the policy

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}_{0}(1|s_{\text{base}}^{1})=1,\quad\pi^{\prime}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2})=\pi^{*}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2}),\quad\pi^{\prime}_{1}=\pi^{*}_{1}.

yields an exploitability of at least
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
, so by taking
\varepsilon
smaller than
\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
we can discard this possibility.

Otherwise, we define a policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{C}=\{\pi_{C,h}\}_{h=0}^{1}\in\Pi^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\pi_{C,0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi^{*}_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{1}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{1})},\text{ if $a\in[K_{1}]$},\\
0,\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\pi_{C,0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2})=\pi^{*}_{0}(s_{\text{base}}^{2}),\quad\pi_{C,1}=\pi^{*}_{1},

and replace
\boldsymbol{\pi}
in Equation (
12
) with
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{C}
to obtain:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}-\frac{1}{4}S

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{8}\left(S^{-1}-1\right)\sum_{a\in[K_{1}]}\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{2})A_{a,a^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon

where
S:=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{1}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{1})<1
, hence

\displaystyle 1-S=\sum_{a^{\prime}\in[K_{2}]-[K_{1}]}\pi^{*}_{0}(a^{\prime}|s_{\text{base}}^{1})\leq 4\varepsilon.

Now for some
\sigma_{1}\in\Delta_{[K_{1}]}
, once again take the policy
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{A}
defined in Case 1, and use Inequality (
