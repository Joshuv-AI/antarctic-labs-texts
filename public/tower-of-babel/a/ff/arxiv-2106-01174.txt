Nitsche’s Finite Element Method for Model Coupling in Elasticity
Peter Hansbo, Mats G. Larson

Abstract
We develop a Nitsche finite element method for a model of Euler–Bernoulli beams with axial stiffness embedded in a two–dimensional elastic bulk domain. The beams have their own displacement fields, and the elastic subdomains created by the beam network are triangulated independently and are coupled to the beams weakly by use of Nitsche’s method in the framework of hybridization.



1
Introduction

In this paper we continue our work on coupling of elastic models
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
Unlike previous coupling models
[
3
,
5
]
, we take as our starting point
the hybridized approach of Burman et al.
[
6
]
, where an auxiliary interface variable is introduced in the Nitsche framework. The hybridized formulation conveniently supports solution and preconditioning based on substructuring
where the bulk variables are eliminated resulting in a system for the hybrid variable. Furthermore, the hybrid variable
may be used to model interfaces with mechanical properties such as bending and membrane stiffness. We consider in particular embedded interfaces made up by beams-trusses embedded in a two dimensional elastic membrane with both strong and cohesive coupling between the beam-truss interface and the membrane. Using the hybridized Nitsche framework we easily derive a weak formulation, which directly leads
to a finite element method by replacing the function spaces with conforming finite dimensional finite element spaces.
The cohesive formulation is designed in such a way that we may let the stiffness in the coupling tend to infinity without loss of stability or convergence. We focus our attention on fitted meshes, that are not required to match on
the interface, but the approach can directly be extended to cut finite element formulations.

The outline of the paper is as follows: In Section 2 we introduce the hybridized formulation for an elastic interface problem, in Section 3 we consider interfaces with bending and membrane stiffness as well as strong and cohesive coupling to the elastic problem. In Section 4 we present some numerical examples illustrating the method and we observe optimal order convergence properties.

2
The Elastic Interface Problem

We begin by extending the hybridized Nitsche method proposed by Burman et al.
[
6
]
to the case of linearized
elasticity. Let
\Omega
denote a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For ease of presentation, we consider only the case where

\Omega
is divided into two non-overlapping subdomains

\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{2}
,
\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
, with
interface
\Gamma=\overline{\Omega}_{1}\cap\overline{\Omega}_{2}
; the case of several domains is a straightforward extension.
We further assume that the subdomains are polygonal
so that
\Gamma
is piecewise linear. In each subdomain, we assume plane stress linearized elasticity with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions, i.e.,
we seek displacement fields
({\boldsymbol{u}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma})
that are zero on
\partial\Omega
. The problem takes the form: find

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}:\Omega_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}:\Gamma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})

\displaystyle=2\mu{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\lambda~\nabla\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{I}}

in
\Omega_{i}

(2.1)

\displaystyle-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot\nabla

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}

in
\Omega_{i}

(2.2)

\displaystyle\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket

\displaystyle={\bf 0}

on
\Gamma

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle={\bf 0}

on
\Gamma\cap\partial\Omega_{i}

(2.4)

Here we used the notation

\llbracket{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}\rrbracket:={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{1}+{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{2})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{2}

(2.5)

where
{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}
denotes the outward pointing normal to
\Omega_{i}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})
is the stress
tensor,
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\left({\boldsymbol{u}}\right)=\left[\varepsilon_{ij}({\boldsymbol{u}})\right]_{i,j=1}^{2}

is the strain tensor with components

\varepsilon_{ij}({\boldsymbol{u}})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial u_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial u_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

(2.6)

{\boldsymbol{I}}=\left[\delta_{ij}\right]_{i,j=1}^{2}
with
\delta_{ij}=1

if
i=j
and
\delta_{ij}=0
if
i\neq j
, and
\lambda
and
\mu
are the
Lamé parameters in plane stress, so in terms of Young’s modulus,
\rm{E}
, and Poisson’s ratio,
\nu
, we have

\lambda=\frac{\rm{E}\,\nu}{1-\nu^{2}},\quad\mu=\frac{\rm{E}}{2\,(1+\nu)}.

(2.7)

Now, multiplying (
2.2
) by test functions
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}={\bf 0}
on
\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
, integrating by parts over
\Omega_{i}
, and using (
2.4
) we find

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{f}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}(-{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot\nabla,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}

(2.8)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\partial\Omega_{i}}

(2.9)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(2.10)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}+(\gamma_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(2.11)

where
\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
are arbitrary. Using the interface condition (
2.3
), in the form
(\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket,{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}=0
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{f}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(2.12)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}+(\gamma_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(2.13)

To formulate a finite element method we let
V^{h}_{i}
,
i=1,2
, and
V^{h}_{\Gamma}
be conforming finite element spaces, such that
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}^{h}\in V^{h}_{i}
and
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}^{h}\in V^{h}_{\Gamma}
. We then obtain the hybridized Nitsche method from Burman et al.
[
6
]
, extended to linear elasticity: find
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{1},{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{2},{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{\Gamma})\in V^{h}_{1}\oplus V^{h}_{2}\oplus V^{h}_{\Gamma}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{f}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(2.14)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(2.15)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}(\gamma_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{\Gamma}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(2.16)

for all
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})\in V^{h}_{1}\oplus V^{h}_{2}\oplus V^{h}_{\Gamma}
.

Remark 2.1

With local meshsize
h_{i}
on
\Omega_{i}
and the choice

\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{0,i}h_{i}^{-1}

(2.17)

it is possible to
show that the bilinear form is coercive on the finite element space, provided the parameters
\gamma_{0,i}
are taken large enough, which together with Galerkin orthogonality and approximation properties of the finite element spaces leads to optimal order a priori error estimates. We refer to Burman et al.
[
6
]
for details.

3
Interfaces with Bending and Membrane Stiffness

3.1
Strong Coupling

We now add bending and membrane stiffness of the interface to our functional in the vein of model coupling in creeping flow
[
7
]
.
We assume that we are given and arclength parameter
s
and a unit tangent vector
{\boldsymbol{t}}
along
\Gamma
, which creates an orthonormal system with the unit normal
{\boldsymbol{n}}
to
\Gamma
. For definiteness we assume that
{\boldsymbol{n}}={\boldsymbol{n}}_{1}=-{\boldsymbol{n}}_{2}
, and
{\boldsymbol{t}}={\boldsymbol{t}}_{1}=-{\boldsymbol{t}}_{2}
.
We further split
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}
into a normal and a tangential part

{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}=u_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}}+u_{t}{\boldsymbol{t}}

(3.1)

with
u_{t}={\boldsymbol{t}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}
and
u_{n}={\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}
.

The equilibrium equations on
\Gamma
are then assumed as follows

\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\text{EI}\frac{d^{2}u_{n}}{ds^{2}}\right)=f_{n}-{\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket

(3.2)

and

-\frac{d}{ds}\left(\text{EA}\frac{du_{t}}{ds}\right)=f_{t}-{\boldsymbol{t}}\cdot\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket

(3.3)

where
f_{n}
and
f_{t}
are given external loads. Here
EI
denotes bending stiffness (with
I
the second moment of inertia) and
EA
axial stiffness (with
A
the cross section area), both possibly varying with position.
These two equilibrium equations now replace the interface equilibrium (
2.3
) which no longer holds.

Again, multiplying (
2.2
) by test functions
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}
,
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}={\bf 0}
on
\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma
, integrating by parts over
\Omega_{i}
, and using (
2.4
) we find

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{f}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}-({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot\nabla,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\partial\Omega_{i}}

(3.5)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.6)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}+(\gamma_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.7)

Writing
{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma}=v_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}}+v_{t}{\boldsymbol{t}}
we see that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=(\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket,{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\left({\boldsymbol{t}}\cdot\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket,v_{t}\right)_{\Gamma}+\left({\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot\llbracket{{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}}\rrbracket,v_{n}\right)_{\Gamma}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\left(f_{t}+\frac{d}{ds}\left(\text{EA}\frac{du_{t}}{ds}\right),v_{t}\right)_{\Gamma}

(3.10)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(f_{n}-\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\left(\text{EI}\frac{d^{2}u_{n}}{ds^{2}}\right),v_{n}\right)_{\Gamma}

(3.11)

Our Nitsche method thus takes the form: find
({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{1},{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{2},u_{n}^{h},u_{t}^{h})\in V^{h}_{1}\oplus V^{h}_{2}\oplus V^{h}_{n}\oplus V^{h}_{t}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-v_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}}-v_{t}{\boldsymbol{t}})_{\Gamma}

(3.12)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}-u_{n}^{h}{\boldsymbol{n}}-u_{t}^{h}{\boldsymbol{t}},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.13)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{i}(\gamma_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}-u_{n}^{h}{\boldsymbol{n}}-u_{t}^{h}{\boldsymbol{t}}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-v_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}}-v_{t}{\boldsymbol{t}})_{\Gamma}

(3.14)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\left(\text{EA}\frac{du^{h}_{t}}{ds},\frac{dv_{t}}{ds}\right)_{\Gamma}+\left(\text{EI}\frac{d^{2}u^{h}_{n}}{ds^{2}},\frac{d^{2}v_{n}}{ds^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma}

(3.15)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}+(f_{t},v_{t})_{\Gamma}+(f_{n},v_{n})_{\Gamma}

(3.16)

for all
({\boldsymbol{v}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{2},v_{n},v_{t})\in V^{h}_{1}\oplus V^{h}_{2}\oplus V^{h}_{n}\oplus V^{h}_{t}
.

•

In this setting,
V^{h}_{n}
must be a space of
C^{1}(\Gamma)-
continuous polynomials, whereas
V^{h}_{t}
can be
C^{0}(\Gamma)-
continuous. Thus it is reasonable to choose different discretizations for
u_{t}^{h}
and
u^{h}_{n}
.

•

For an interface that have a corner or bifurcates in a points we can not use
(u_{n}^{h},u^{h}_{t})
as global degrees of freedom since these are not continuous if the segments meet at an angle. Thus we must transform the variables
back to Cartesian coordinates. We give details for our chosen discretization below.

For brevity let us define

A({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}}):=\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Omega_{i}}+\left(\text{EA}\frac{du_{t}}{ds},\frac{dv_{t}}{ds}\right)_{\Gamma}+\left(\text{EI}\frac{d^{2}u_{n}}{ds^{2}},\frac{d^{2}v_{n}}{ds^{2}}\right)_{\Gamma}

(3.17)

L({\boldsymbol{v}}):=\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})_{\Omega_{i}}+(f_{t},v_{t})_{\Gamma}+(f_{n},v_{n})_{\Gamma}

(3.18)

The finite element method takes the form: find
{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}:=({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{1},{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{2},u_{n}^{h},u_{t}^{h})\in V^{h}:=V^{h}_{1}\oplus V^{h}_{2}\oplus V^{h}_{n}\oplus V^{h}_{t}

such that

\displaystyle L({\boldsymbol{v}})={}

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(3.19)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}^{h},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.20)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}(\gamma_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{\Gamma}),{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma},\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V^{h}

(3.21)

where we recall that

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{u}}^{h}_{\Gamma}=u_{n}^{h}{\boldsymbol{n}}+u_{t}^{h}{\boldsymbol{t}},\qquad{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma}=v_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}}+v_{t}{\boldsymbol{t}}

(3.22)

3.2
Cohesive Coupling

To model a weaker coupling we proceed in the spirit of Juntunen and Stenberg
[
8
]
and Hansbo and Hansbo
[
9
]
.
The cohesive model, replacing the strong condition
{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}={\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
, is given by

{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{S}}_{i}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma})={\bf 0}\quad\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\Gamma

(3.23)

where
{\boldsymbol{S}}_{i}
are coupling stiffness matrices, assumed to be of the form

{\boldsymbol{S}}_{i}=\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}+\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}{\boldsymbol{t}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{t}}

(3.24)

where
1/\alpha_{i}
and
1/\beta_{i}
are stiffness parameters normal and tangential to the interface, respectively.
We are interested in the case where
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
can be arbitrarily small, so we write (
3.23
) as

{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}={\bf 0}

(3.25)

where
{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}={\boldsymbol{S}}_{i}^{-1}=\alpha_{i}{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}+\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{t}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{t}}
. We then find

\displaystyle L({\boldsymbol{v}})={}

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(3.26)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.27)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(3.28)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.29)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i},{\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{v}}_{\Gamma})_{\Gamma}

(3.30)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i})_{\Gamma}

(3.31)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}({\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma},{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}({\boldsymbol{C}}_{i}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}+{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{u}}_{\Gamma}))_{\Gamma}

(3.32)

where the last two terms are zero due to the interface condition and the resulting form on the right hand side is symmetric. Furthermore,
{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}
is a stabilization matrix of the form

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{i}=\tau_{n}^{i}{\boldsymbol{n}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{n}}+\tau_{t}^{i}{\boldsymbol{t}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{t}},\qquad\tau_{n}^{i}=\frac{1}{h_{i}/\gamma_{0,i}+\alpha_{i}},\quad\tau_{t}^{i}=\frac{1}{h_{i}/\gamma_{0,i}+\beta_{i}}

(3.33)

where
\gamma_{0,i}
is sufficiently large (cf. Remark
2.1
).

3.3
One-Sided Cohesive Coupling

It is clear that the cohesive model is unphysical in that the beams are allowed to penetrate the domains. Thus we need to
enforce strong continuity of contact type in such situations.
We then enforce the contact constraints by way of
{\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}\cdot({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}-u_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}})\leq 0
.
With
\sigma_{n}:={\boldsymbol{n}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}
,
\sigma_{t}:={\boldsymbol{t}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{n}}
,
\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket:={\boldsymbol{n}}_{i}\cdot({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-v_{n}{\boldsymbol{n}})
, and

\llbracket{v_{t}^{i}}\rrbracket:={\boldsymbol{t}}_{i}\cdot({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}-v_{t}{\boldsymbol{t}})
our contact conditions on
\Gamma
can then be formulated, following Burman and Hansbo
[
10
]
:

\displaystyle\beta_{i}\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\llbracket{u_{t}^{i}}\rrbracket={}

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\Gamma

(3.34)

\displaystyle\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket\leq{}

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\Gamma,

(3.35)

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket\leq{}

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\Gamma

(3.36)

\displaystyle(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket={}

\displaystyle 0\quad\text{on}\quad\partial\Omega_{i}\cap\Gamma

(3.37)

where we recognise (
3.35
)–(
3.37
) as the Kuhn–Tucker conditions. We begin by rewriting (
3.32
)
in normal and tangential components, writing :

\displaystyle L({\boldsymbol{v}})

\displaystyle=A({\boldsymbol{u}},{\boldsymbol{v}})+\sum_{i}(\beta_{i}\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Gamma}

(3.38)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{i}(\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\beta_{i}\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\llbracket{v_{t}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

(3.39)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{i}(\beta_{i}\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\llbracket{u_{t}^{i}}\rrbracket,\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Gamma}

(3.40)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i}(\beta_{i}\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\llbracket{v_{t}^{i}}\rrbracket,\tau_{t}^{i}(\beta_{i}\sigma_{t}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\llbracket{u_{t}^{i}}\rrbracket))_{\Gamma}

(3.41)

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

(3.42)

where we did not introduce the normal component of the cohesive law.

We now turn to the alternative formulation of the Kuhn–Tucker conditions due to Rockafellar
[
11
]
, introduced in a Nitsche formulation for contact
analysis by Chouly and Hild
[
12
]
. We recognize that

p_{i}:=\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket

(3.43)

act as multipliers (cf. Burman and Hansbo
[
10
]
, Section 5.1), and
the Kuhn–Tucker conditions are then equivalent to the relation

p_{i}=-\frac{1}{\epsilon_{i}}[\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket-\epsilon_{i}p_{i}]_{+}

(3.44)

where
[x]_{+}=\max(x,0)
and
\epsilon_{i}>0
but arbitrary.
We then write

\displaystyle(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}={}

\displaystyle(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}-(\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

(3.45)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket-\epsilon_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket))_{\Gamma}

(3.46)

\displaystyle+(\epsilon_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket),\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

(3.47)

\displaystyle-(\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

(3.48)

Thus we have that

\displaystyle-(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}=(\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle+\left(\epsilon_{i}^{-1}\left[\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket-\epsilon_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)\right]_{+},\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket-\epsilon_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)\right)_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle-(\epsilon_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket),\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

To obtain well conditioned systems for small
\alpha_{i}
, we examine the case when
p_{i}=0
and
p_{i}\neq 0
.

•

If
p_{i}\neq 0
we are in contact and

\displaystyle-(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}={}

\displaystyle-(\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}+(\epsilon_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle-\left(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket\right)_{\Gamma}-(\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Gamma}

which, with
\epsilon_{i}^{-1}=\gamma_{0,i}/h_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{-1}
gives a scheme of the type (
2.16
).

•

If
p_{i}=0
we have the cohesive law and

\displaystyle-(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}={}

\displaystyle(\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle-(\epsilon_{i}(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle-(\alpha_{i}\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket,\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle-(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\alpha_{i}\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}+(\alpha_{i}\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}),\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i}))_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle+((\alpha_{i}-\epsilon_{i})(\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{u}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{u_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket),\sigma_{n}({\boldsymbol{v}}_{i})+\alpha_{i}^{-1}\llbracket{v_{n}^{i}}\rrbracket)_{\Gamma}

and using again
\epsilon_{i}^{-1}=\gamma_{0,i}/h_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{-1}
we find the standard method for cohesive laws with penalty parameter

(\alpha_{i}-\epsilon_{i})/\alpha_{i}^{2}=1/(h_{i}/\gamma_{0,i}+\alpha_{i})=\tau_{n}^{i}
.

Thus the parameter
\epsilon_{i}
does not change in contact and out of contact and the schemes become insensitive to small
\alpha_{i}

5
Concluding Remarks

We have introduced a hybridized Nitsche method for linearized elasticity which uses an auxiliary interface displacement, modelled independently of the domains. This allows for easy modeling of different stiffness models at the interface. We have focused
here on Euler–Bernoulli beam bending and membrane stiffness, but other models can be easily accommodated; the only requirement is
that continuity of displacements between the interface field and the domain fields can be represented. We have also
suggested weaker couplings between the interface and domains in the form of a cohesive interface law, with no-penetration fulfilled.
This leads to a nonlinear contact problem which fits straighforwardly in the general framework of Nitsche’s method. Some numerical examples
are provided to show how different parameter choices affect the solution in bending and in stretching of a plane elasticity problem.
