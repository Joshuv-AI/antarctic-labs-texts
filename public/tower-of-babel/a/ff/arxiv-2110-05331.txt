Abstract
. The weak-strong uniqueness for Maxwell–Stefan systems and some generalized systems is proved. The corresponding parabolic cross-diffusion equations are considered in a bounded domain with no-flux boundary conditions. The key points of the proofs are various inequalities for the relative entropy associated to the systems and the analysis of the spectrum of a quadratic form capturing the frictional dissipation. The latter task is complicated by the singular nature of the diffusion matrix. This difficulty is addressed by proving its positive definiteness on a subspace and using the Bott–Duffin matrix inverse. The generalized Maxwell–Stefan systems are shown to cover several known cross-diffusion systems for the description of tumor growth and physical vapor deposition processes.

1.
Introduction

The Maxwell–Stefan equations describe the diffusive transport of the components
of gaseous mixtures. Applications arise in, e.g., sedimentation, dialysis,
electrolysis, and ion exchange
[
34
]
.
They were suggested in 1866 by James Maxwell
[
31
]
for dilute gases
and in 1871 by Josef Stefan
[
33
]
for fluids.
While there are several works on the existence of
local-in-time smooth solutions
[
4
,
20
,
21
]

and global-in-time weak solutions
[
27
]
in the case of vanishing barycentric
velocity, the problem of the uniqueness of solutions is basically unsolved.
The uniqueness of strong solutions has been shown in
[
21
,
24
]
,
and uniqueness results for weak solutions in a very special case can be found
in
[
10
]
. In this paper, we make a step forward in the uniqueness problem
by showing that strong solutions are unique in the class of weak solutions
to Maxwell–Stefan systems.

1.1.
Setting

We consider an ideal gaseous mixture consisting of
n
components with
volume fractions or concentrations
c_{i}(x,t)
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. The dynamics
of the mixture is given by the mass balance equations and
the relations between the driving forces and the fluxes,

(1)

\partial_{t}c_{i}+\operatorname{div}(c_{i}u_{i})=0,\quad\nabla c_{i}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
u_{i}(x,t)
are the partial velocities and
D_{ij}=D_{ji}>0
are
diffusion coefficients.
The equations are solved in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
),
supplemented by the initial and no-flux boundary conditions

(2)

c_{i}(0)=c_{i}^{0}\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\quad\nabla c_{i}\cdot\nu=0\quad\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\ i=1,\ldots,n,

where
\nu
is the exterior unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
.

We assume that the barycentric velocity vanishes, which implies
that the sum of all fluxes vanishes,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}u_{i}=0
. Then, supposing
that
c_{i}^{0}\geq 0
and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{0}=1
in
\Omega
, we deduce from
mass conservation that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1\quad\mbox{in $\Omega$ for all $t>0$.}

This constraint is
necessary to invert the force-flux relations in (
1
), i.e. to express
the flux
c_{i}u_{i}
as a linear combination of the driving forces
\nabla c_{j}
.

The global existence analysis for (
1
)–(
2
) is based on the
property that the system is endowed with the entropy functional

(3)

H(\bm{c})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}(\log c_{i}-1)dx,

where
\bm{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
solves (
1
)–(
2
) and satisfies
the entropy dissipation inequality

[
27
, (1.14)]

(4)

\frac{dH}{dt}(\bm{c})+C\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}|^{2}dx\leq 0,

with
C>0
depending only on
(D_{ij})
. The aim of this paper is to prove the
weak-strong uniqueness for (
1
)–(
2
) and generalized systems.
Weak-strong uniqueness means that any weak solution coincides with a strong
solution emanating from the same initial data as long as the latter exists.
In other words, the strong solutions must be unique within the class of weak solutions.
To achieve this aim, we use ideas from our previous work
[
23
]
and
establish a relative entropy inequality. This leads to a stability estimate for
the difference of a weak and a strong solution and eventually to the
weak-strong uniqueness property. Here, the relative entropy functional is given by

(5)

H(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(c_{i}\log\frac{c_{i}}{\bar{c}_{i}}-(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})\bigg)dx,

where
\bm{c}
and
\bar{\bm{c}}
are suitable solutions to (
1
)–(
2
).

In the literature, relative entropies are known to be useful to prove the weak-strong
uniqueness of solutions. First results were achieved for systems of hyperbolic
conservation laws
[
14
]
and later for the compressible Navier–Stokes
equations
[
16
,
17
]
and general hyperbolic-parabolic systems endowed
with an entropy
[
13
]
.
The relative entropy technique was applied to,
for instance, entropy-dissipating reaction-diffusion equations
[
18
]
,
reaction-cross-diffusion systems
[
11
]
,
energy-reaction-diffusion systems
[
22
]
,
nonlocal cross-diffusion systems
[
26
]
,
and quantum Euler systems
[
8
,
19
]
.
Compared to the results of, e.g.
[
11
,
22
]
, the diffusion matrix
in these works is assumed to be positive definite
if
c_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, which is
not satisfied for the Maxwell–Stefan system.

1.2.
Definitions and assumptions

We impose the following assumptions:

(A1)

Domain:
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 1
is a bounded domain.

(A2)

Coefficients:
D_{ij}>0
and
D_{ij}=D_{ji}
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,n
,
i\neq j
.

(A3)

Initial data:
0\leq c_{i}^{0}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,

H(\bm{c}^{0})<\infty
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}^{0}=1
in
\Omega
.

Next, we define the concept of weak and strong solutions employed in this paper.

We call
\bm{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})
a
weak solution
to (
1
)–(
2
)
if
\bm{c}
satisfies the initial condition (
2
),

c_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1
in
\Omega\times(0,\infty)
,

\sqrt{c_{i}}\in L^{2}_{\rm loc}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\Omega)),\quad c_{i}\in C_{\rm loc}^{0}([0,\infty);{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
is the dual space of
{\mathcal{V}}=\{w\in H^{2}(\Omega):\nabla w\cdot\nu=0
on
\partial\Omega\}
, and
\bm{c}

satisfies (
1
)–(
2
) in the weak sense, i.e., for any

\phi_{i}\in C_{\rm loc}^{1}([0,\infty);C^{1}(\overline{\Omega}))
satisfying

\nabla\phi_{i}\cdot\nu=0
on
\partial\Omega
and any
T>0
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, we have

\int_{\Omega}c_{i}(T)\phi_{i}(T)dx-\int_{\Omega}c_{i}^{0}\phi_{i}(0)dx-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}\partial_{t}\phi_{i}dxdt-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}u_{i}\cdot\nabla\phi_{i}dxdt=0,

where
u_{i}
satisfies the force-flux relations in (
1
).
The last integral is well defined, since the gradient bound for
\sqrt{c_{i}}

implies that
\sqrt{c_{i}}u_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
(see Lemma

7
below) and thus, because of the property
0\leq c_{i}\leq 1
,

c_{i}u_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
Finally, a weak solution is required to satisfy
the entropy inequality

(6)

H(\bm{c}(t))+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{D_{ij}}|u_{i}-u_{j}|^{2}dxds\leq H(\bm{c}^{0}).

For the Maxwell-Stefan system this is not an additional requirement
as it is guaranteed by the existence theory of
[
27
]
;
see Section
3.1
.

We will use the term
strong solution
to (
1
)–(
2
)
to mean that
\bar{\bm{c}}=(\bar{c}_{i},\ldots,\bar{c}_{n})

with
0<\bar{c}_{i}<1
is a weak solution satisfying additional
regularity properties. The necessary regularity is
stated precisely in context. In certain cases,
\bar{c}_{i}
satisfies
(
1
)–(
2
) pointwise,
as is the traditional notion of strong solutions.

1.3.
Main results and key ideas of the proofs

Our first main result is concerned with the Maxwell–Stefan system
(
1
)–(
2
).

Theorem 1

(Weak-strong uniqueness)
.

Let Assumptions (A1)–(A2) hold. Let
\bm{c}
be a weak solution to
(
1
)–(
2
) and let
\bar{\bm{c}}
be a strong solution
to (
1
)–(
2
) satisfying
0<\bar{c}_{i}<1
in
\Omega
,
t>0
,
the regularity properties

\log\bar{c}_{i}\in H_{\rm loc}^{1}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),\quad\bar{u}_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),

and
\bar{c}_{i}
does not have anomalous dissipation, i.e., it satisfies
the entropy identity

H(\bar{\bm{c}}(t))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{\bar{c}_{i}\bar{c}_{j}}{D_{ij}}|\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j}|^{2}dxds=H(\bar{\bm{c}}^{0})\quad\mbox{for }t>0.

The initial data for
\bm{c}
and
\bar{\bm{c}}
satisfy Assumption (A3).
Then for any
t>0
, there exists a constant
C(t)>0
, depending on
t
,
\Omega
,

n
, and
(D_{ij})
, such that

(7)

H(\bm{c}(t)|\bar{\bm{c}}(t))+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}|u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i}|^{2}dxds\leq C(t)H(\bm{c}^{0}|\bar{\bm{c}}^{0}).

If the initial data coincide, i.e.
\bm{c}^{0}=\bar{\bm{c}}^{0}
in
\Omega
, then

\bm{c}(t)=\bar{\bm{c}}(t)
in
\Omega
for
t>0
.

We verify in Section
3.1
that solutions with the stated regularity exist.
To prove Theorem
1
we develop a relative entropy identity and use it
as a yardstick to control the distance between two solutions.
First, it is shown that the relative entropy (
5
) satisfies the inequality

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{dH}{dt}(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{D_{ij}}|(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{i}}{D_{ij}}(c_{j}-\bar{c}_{j})(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j})dx.

(see Section
3.2
).
Next, we study how the frictional dissipation
(the second term in (
8
)) controls the
L^{2}
norm of
u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i}
.
The quadratic form in (
8
) captures the dissipative effect of friction
in the following way:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}

\displaystyle\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{D_{ij}}|(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{D_{ij}}c_{j}\big((u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})\big)

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(c)(\sqrt{c_{i}}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i}))\cdot(\sqrt{c_{j}}(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j}))=\bm{Y}^{T}A(\bm{c})\bm{Y},

where the matrix

A(\bm{c})=(A_{ij}(\bm{c}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
is defined by

(10)

A_{ij}(\bm{c})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{k=1,\,k\neq i}^{n}c_{k}/D_{ik}&\quad\mbox{if }i=j,\\
-\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}}/D_{ij}&\quad\mbox{if }i\neq j,\end{array}\right.

and
\bm{Y}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})
with
Y_{i}=\sqrt{c_{i}}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})
.
The matrix
A(\bm{c})
is singular and thus not positive definite. However,
we can show that it is positive definite on the subspace

L:=\{\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\sqrt{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{z}=0\}
(here,
\sqrt{\bm{c}}
is the
vector with components
\sqrt{c_{i}}
) and the quadratic form satisfies

\bm{Y}^{T}A(\bm{c})\bm{Y}\geq\mu|P_{L}\bm{Y}|^{2},

where
\mu>0
is a uniform lower bound for the positive eigenvalues of
A(\bm{c})

and
P_{L}
is the projection on
L
. This inequality and a careful estimate
of the right-hand side of (
8
) implies (
7
) and the
weak-strong uniqueness property.

The
L^{\infty}
bound on the partial velocities
\bar{u}_{i}
in Theorem
1

can be avoided at the expense of assuming
\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}\in L^{\infty}
and

\bar{c}_{i}
is uniformly bounded from below by a positive constant.
The uniform lower bound is not needed in
Theorem
1
, where only positivity is required.

Corollary 2
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold, replacing
\bar{u}_{i}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
by
\nabla\sqrt{\bar{c}_{i}}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega\times(0,\infty))
,

i=1,\ldots,n
.
Suppose additionally that there exists
m>0
such that
\bar{c}_{i}(t)\geq m
in

\Omega
,
t>0
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then there exist constants
C_{1}>0

and
C_{2}(t)>0
(depending on

\Omega

(D_{ij})
)
such that the following inequality holds for

(11)

H(\bm{c}(t)|\bar{\bm{c}}(t))+C_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(\sqrt{c_{i}}-\sqrt{\bar{c}_{i}})|^{2}dxds\leq C_{2}(t)H(\bm{c}^{0}|\bar{\bm{c}}^{0}).

The relative entropy inequality (
11
) is the analogue of the
entropy estimate (
4
). It can be achieved by working with the square
roots
\sqrt{c_{i}}
as the main variables. More precisely, we write the force-flux
relations in (
1

(12)

2\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}u_{j},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

subject to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}u_{i}=0
, where
A(\bm{c})
is defined in (
10
).
This system cannot be directly inverted,
since
\ker A(\bm{c})=\operatorname{span}\{\sqrt{\bm{c}}\}
. However,
introducing the Bott–Duffin inverse
A^{BD}(\bm{c})
of

with respect to

L:=(\operatorname{span}\{\sqrt{\bm{c}}\})^{\perp}
(see Section

(13)

\sqrt{c_{i}}u_{i}=-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\nabla\sqrt{c_{j}},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

and system (
1
) can be formulated in the concise form

(14)

\partial_{t}c_{i}=2\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\nabla\sqrt{c_{j}}\bigg),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

The Bott–Duffin inverse
A^{BD}(\bm{c})
equals the group inverse
studied in
[
6
]
, since
L=\operatorname{ran}A(\bm{c})
. Compared to
[
6
]
,
we work here with the square roots
\sqrt{c_{i}}
instead of the chemical
potentials
\log c_{i}
(see
[
6
, (4.25)]
).
The relative entropy inequality (
8
) is rewritten in the form
(see Lemma

)

(15)

\displaystyle\frac{dH}{dt}(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}})

\displaystyle+4\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}dx

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}Z_{i}\cdot\nabla\sqrt{\bar{c}_{j}}\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{c_{i}}}{\sqrt{\bar{c}_{i}}}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bar{\bm{c}})-A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\frac{\sqrt{c_{j}}}{\sqrt{\bar{c}_{j}}}\bigg)dx,

where
Z_{i}=\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}-\sqrt{c_{i}/\bar{c}_{i}}\nabla\sqrt{\bar{c}_{i}}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
.
We prove in Lemma

that the Bott–Duffin inverse is symmetric and
positive definite on
L
,

\bm{Z}^{T}A^{BD}(\bm{c})\bm{Z}\geq\lambda|P_{L}\bm{Z}|^{2},\quad\bm{Z}=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}),

where
\lambda>0
is a uniform lower bound for the positive eigenvalues of

. Inequality (

The above-mentioned techniques can be extended to a class of generalized
Maxwell–Stefan systems, which includes several examples of
cross-diffusion systems occurring in applications (see Section
5
):

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}+\operatorname{div}(c_{i}u_{i})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}u_{j}=0,

(17)

\displaystyle-\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}(\bm{c})c_{j}u_{j}

\displaystyle=c_{i}\nabla\frac{\delta H}{\delta c_{i}}(\bm{c})-c_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\nabla\frac{\delta H}{\delta c_{j}}(\bm{c}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

Theorem 3

(Weak-strong uniqueness for the generalized system)

Hypothesis (H).

and the entropy identity

H(\bar{\bm{c}}(t))+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{\bar{c}_{i}\bar{c}_{j}}B_{ij}^{BD}(\bar{\bm{c}})\nabla h^{\prime}_{i}(\bar{c}_{i})\cdot\nabla h^{\prime}_{j}(\bar{c}_{j})dxds=H(\bar{\bm{c}}^{0})

If the initial data coincide, then

The strategy of the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle{}+C(m)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\chi(\bm{c})|\nabla(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})|^{2}dx\leq CH(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}}),

2.
Properties of the matrix
A(\bm{c})

The properties of the matrix
A(\bm{c})
, defined in (
10
), have been
studied in
[
4
,
21
,
27
]
under the assumption
c_{i}>0
for all

i=1,\ldots,n
. Our results are valid for nonnegative concentrations
c_{i}\geq 0
,
including vacuum.

Let
\bm{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{+}
. Since
(D_{ij})
is symmetric, we have for all
\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

0=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}A_{ij}(\bm{c})z_{j}=\sum_{i,j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}\frac{c_{j}}{D_{ij}}z_{i}-\sum_{i,j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}}}{D_{ij}}z_{j}=\sum_{i,j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}\frac{\sqrt{c_{j}}}{D_{ij}}(\sqrt{c_{j}}z_{i}-\sqrt{c_{i}}z_{j}),

showing that
\operatorname{span}\{\sqrt{\bm{c}}\}=\ker A(\bm{c})
. We set

\displaystyle\operatorname{ran}A(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=L:=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\sqrt{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{x}=0\},

\displaystyle\ker A(\bm{c})

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{ran}A(\bm{c}))^{\perp}=L^{\perp}=\operatorname{span}\{\sqrt{\bm{c}}\},

and note that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1
implies that

|\sqrt{\bm{c}}|^{2}=c_{1}+\cdots+c_{n}=1
.
The projection matrices
P_{L}
on
L
and
P_{L^{\perp}}
on
L^{\perp}
are given by

(22)

(P_{L})_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}},\quad(P_{L^{\perp}})_{ij}=\delta_{ij}-(P_{L})_{ij}=\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}},\quad i,j=1,\ldots,n.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\bm{c}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{n}
be such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1
. Then

(23)

\bm{z}^{T}A(\bm{c})\bm{z}\geq\mu|P_{L}\bm{z}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

where
\mu=\min_{i\neq j}(1/D_{ij})
. Moreover, the Bott–Duffin inverse

A^{BD}(\bm{c})=P_{L}(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}

is well defined, symmetric, and satisfies

(24)

\bm{z}^{T}A^{BD}(\bm{c})\bm{z}\geq\lambda|P_{L}\bm{z}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

where
\lambda=(2\sum_{i\neq j}(1/D_{ij}+1))^{-1}
.

Proof.

We first prove (
23
).
Let
0<\alpha\leq\mu
and suppose that
c_{i}\neq 0

for
i=1,\ldots,M
and
c_{i}=0
for
i=M+1,\ldots,n
.
If necessary, we may rearrange the indices to achieve this ordering.
Since
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1
, it holds that
M>0
.
Thus, we can write
-A(\bm{c})-\alpha P_{L^{\perp}}
in block diagonal form as

-A(\bm{c})-\alpha P_{L^{\perp}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}\widetilde{A}&0&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&a_{M+1}&&0\\
\vdots&0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&&a_{n}\end{array}\right),

where
\widetilde{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}
has the coefficients

\widetilde{A}_{ii}=-A_{ii}(\bm{c})-\alpha c_{i}
and

\widetilde{A}_{ij}=(1/D_{ij}-\alpha)\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}}
for

i,j=1,\ldots,M
,
i\neq j
,
and
a_{j}=-\sum_{k=1,\,k\neq j}^{n}c_{k}/D_{kj}
for
j=M+1,\ldots,n
.
Because of
\alpha\leq\mu
, the matrix
\widetilde{A}
is quasi-positive and
irreducible. Hence, by the Perron–Frobenius theorem
[
32
, Chapter 8]
,
the spectral radius of
\widetilde{A}
is less than or equal to
the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue that is a simple eigenvalue of

\widetilde{A}
associated with a strictly positive eigenvector,
and all other eigenvalues of
\widetilde{A}
have no positive eigenvector.
In the present case, the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue is given by
\lambda_{\rm PF}=-\alpha
and is associated with the eigenvector
(\sqrt{c_{1}},\ldots,\sqrt{c_{M}})
,
recalling that
c_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\ldots,M
(also see the proof of Lemma 2.1
in
[
27
]
). Since all eigenvalues of
\widetilde{A}
are not larger than

\lambda_{\rm PF}
, we have

\widetilde{\bm{z}}^{T}(-\widetilde{A})\widetilde{\bm{z}}\geq\alpha|\widetilde{\bm{z}}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for }\widetilde{\bm{z}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{M})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}.

This leads, for any
\alpha\leq\mu
and
\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, to the inequality

\displaystyle\bm{z}^{T}

\displaystyle(A(\bm{c})+\alpha P_{L^{\perp}})\bm{z}=\widetilde{z}^{T}(-\widetilde{A})\widetilde{\bm{z}}+\sum_{i=M+1}^{n}\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}\frac{c_{j}}{D_{ij}}z_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\alpha|\widetilde{\bm{z}}|^{2}+\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,\ell=1,\ldots,M\\
k\neq\ell\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{D_{k\ell}}\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}c_{j}\sum_{i=M+1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}\geq\alpha|\widetilde{\bm{z}}|^{2}+\alpha\sum_{i=M+1}^{n}z_{i}^{2}=\alpha|\bm{z}|^{2},

where we have used the fact that
\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}c_{j}=1
for

i=M+1,\ldots,n
, since
c_{i}=0
for exactly these indices.
This inequality implies that for all
\bm{z}=P_{L}\bm{z}+P_{L^{\perp}}\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\bm{z}^{T}A(\bm{c})\bm{z}+\alpha|P_{L^{\perp}}\bm{z}|^{2}=\bm{z}^{T}(A(\bm{c})+\alpha P_{L^{\perp}})\bm{z}\geq\alpha|P_{L}\bm{z}|^{2}+\alpha|P_{L^{\perp}}\bm{z}|^{2},

which shows (
23
).

The invertibility of
A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
is a consequence of Lemma

17
in the appendix. Consequently, the Bott–Duffin inverse

A^{BD}(\bm{c})=P_{L}(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}
exists.

It remains to show (
24
).
The spectral radius
r(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})

is bounded by the Frobenius norm. Thus, because of
A(\bm{c})P_{L}=A(\bm{c})

(see Lemma
17
in Appendix
A
) and
0\leq c_{i}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle r(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})

\displaystyle\leq\|A(\bm{c})+P_{L^{\perp}}\|_{F}=\bigg(\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(A_{ij}(\bm{c})+\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}})^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigg(\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{n}\frac{c_{j}}{D_{ij}}+c_{i}\bigg)^{2}+\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{D_{ij}}\bigg)^{2}c_{i}c_{j}\bigg\}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}\bigg(\frac{1}{D_{ij}}+1\bigg)=\frac{1}{\lambda}.

We infer that the eigenvalues of
(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}
are larger than
or equal to
\lambda
. Thus, in view of (
81
), we find that for all

\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,

\bm{z}^{T}A^{BD}(\bm{c})\bm{z}=(P_{L}\bm{z})^{T}(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}P_{L}\bm{z}\geq\lambda|P_{L}\bm{z}|^{2},

finishing the proof.
∎

(\nabla\sqrt{c_{1}},\ldots,\nabla\sqrt{c_{n}})\in L

)
can be expressed via the formula (

.

3.1.
Existence theory

We discuss the existence of weak and strong solutions to the Maxwell–Stefan system

). First, we recall

Theorem 5

(Global existence for Maxwell–Stefan systems)
.

\displaystyle H(\bm{c}(t))+4\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}A^{BD}_{ij}(\bm{c})\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}\cdot\nabla\sqrt{c_{j}}dxds

The existence of strong solutions was proved in

Theorem 6

(Strong solutions for Maxwell–Stefan systems)
.

Let

and let

and a unique

to (

)–(

for

.

The strong solution of Theorem

has the property of immediate

in

\Omega

for

depends on
\bm{c}^{0}
. Moreover, if the initial

Let

If

\Omega

and

of Corollary

The assumption

m\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}|^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}|u_{i}|^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}|^{2}
.

Lemma 7
.

Let

and let

be given by the force-flux relations in

\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}|u_{i}|^{2}\leq C\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}|^{2}.

Proof.

It follows from (

3.2.
Relative entropy inequality

We first derive a relative entropy inequality via a formal computation.
Using (
5
) and (
1
), we obtain

(25)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\log\frac{c_{i}}{\bar{c}_{i}}\partial_{t}c_{i}+\bigg(1-\frac{c_{i}}{\bar{c}_{i}}\bigg)\partial_{t}\bar{c}_{i}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\nabla\log\frac{c_{i}}{\bar{c}_{i}}\cdot(c_{i}u_{i})-\nabla\log\frac{c_{i}}{\bar{c}_{i}}\cdot(c_{i}\bar{u}_{i})\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}c_{i}\nabla(\log c_{i}-\log\bar{c}_{i})\cdot(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})dx.

To reformulate the integrand of the right-hand side, we insert the second equation
of (
1
), and use the symmetry of
(D_{ij})
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle c_{i}\nabla(\log c_{i}-\log\bar{c}_{i})\cdot(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{1}{D_{ij}}\big(c_{j}(u_{i}-u_{j})-\bar{c}_{j}(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{c_{j}}{D_{ij}}\big((u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})\big)

(26)

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}-\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\frac{1}{D_{ij}}c_{i}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot\big((c_{j}-\bar{c}_{j})(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j})\big)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{2D_{ij}}\big|(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})\big|^{2}-\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\frac{c_{i}}{D_{ij}}(c_{j}-\bar{c}_{j})(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j}).

This shows that

(27)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}H

\displaystyle(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{D_{ij}}\big|(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})-(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j})\big|^{2}dx

\displaystyle{}=-\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{i}}{D_{ij}}(c_{j}-\bar{c}_{j})(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j})dx.

Our aim is to make this computation rigorous. Since the computation in
(
26
) is purely algebraic, it holds without any regularity restrictions.
In principle, one would expect that (
27
) holds under the condition
that all the terms are well defined, which would cover the class of weak solutions
subject to the condition
u_{i}\in L^{\infty}
(to ensure integrability of the right-hand
side). However, we have not been able to establish (
25
) for such a
class of solutions, and stricter conditions on one of the solutions are
required.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\bm{c}
be a weak solution to (
1
)–(
2
) and let
\bar{\bm{c}}

be a strong solution to (
1
)–(
2
) satisfying
0<\bar{c}_{i}(t)<1

in
\Omega
, the regularity

\log\bar{c}_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{2}(0,\infty;H^{1}(\Omega)),\ \partial_{t}\log\bar{c}_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{2}(\Omega\times(0,\infty)),\ \bar{u}_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}(\Omega)),

and the entropy identity

(28)

H(\bar{\bm{c}}(t))+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\bar{c}_{i}\bar{c}_{j}}{D_{ij}}|u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i}|^{2}dxds=H(\bar{\bm{c}}^{0})\quad\mbox{for }t>0.

Then

\displaystyle H(\bm{c}(t)

\displaystyle|\bar{\bm{c}}(t))+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}A_{ij}(\bm{c})(\sqrt{c_{i}}(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i}))\cdot(\sqrt{c_{j}}(u_{j}-\bar{u}_{j}))dxds

(29)

\displaystyle{}\leq H(\bm{c}^{0}|\bar{\bm{c}}^{0})-\sum_{i,j=1,\,i\neq j}^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{c_{i}}{D_{ij}}(c_{j}-\bar{c}_{j})(u_{i}-\bar{u}_{i})\cdot(\bar{u}_{i}-\bar{u}_{j})dxds.

Proof.

Since

H(\bm{c}|\bar{\bm{c}})=H(\bm{c})-H(\bar{\bm{c}})-\int_{\Omega}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})\log\bar{c}_{i}dx,

we need to formulate the time evolution of each of these terms.
According to Theorem
5
, the weak solution
\bm{c}
satisfies

\nabla\sqrt{c_{i}}
,
\sqrt{c_{i}}u_{i}\in L_{\rm loc}^{2}(0,\infty;L^{2}(\Omega))
and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

Let
s>0
. Then the elements

•

The matrix

satisfies for all

Proof.

Assumption (B1) and Lemma

Hence,
B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
is invertible and the Bott–Duffin inverse
is well defined and symmetric.

We continue by studying the eigenvalues of
B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
.
A computation shows that for

\sqrt{\bm{c}}\in\ker(B(\bm{c})P_{L})=L^{\perp}
we have

(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})\sqrt{\bm{c}}=P_{L^{\perp}}\sqrt{\bm{c}}=\sqrt{\bm{c}}
,
i.e.,
\sqrt{\bm{c}}
is an eigenvector of

with
eigenvalue 1. Let
\xi\notin L^{\perp}
,
\xi\neq 0
, be another eigenvector,

(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})\xi=\rho\xi.

Then
\rho\neq 0
. Applying

on both sides, we obtain

with eigenvalue

Due to Assumption (B4), we conclude that

We claim that the elements

are bounded and Lipschitz

\bm{c}\in[s,1]^{n}
. Indeed, observe that

(46)

B^{BD}(\bm{c})=P_{L}(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}=\frac{P_{L}\operatorname{adj}(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})}{\det(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})},

and the denominator in
(

are bounded.
Hence, all elements

are bounded.
Since the product of Lipschitz continuous functions is Lipschitz continuous,

are

\bm{c}\in[s,1]^{n}
for any
s>0
.

We wish to verify (

is bounded by its Frobenius norm

and the Frobenius norm is

\displaystyle r(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})

\displaystyle\leq\|B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}\|_{F}\leq\|B(\bm{c})P_{L}\|_{F}+\|P_{L^{\perp}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\|B(\bm{c})\|_{F}\|P_{L}\|_{F}+\|P_{L^{\perp}}\|_{F}.

\displaystyle\|P_{L}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\delta_{ij}-\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}})^{2}\leq\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}1=n^{2},

\displaystyle\|P_{L^{\perp}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}(\sqrt{c_{i}c_{j}})^{2}=\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\bigg)^{2}=1.

.

. We infer that
the smallest eigenvalue of
(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}
is larger than

\lambda(m):=1/({\gamma}(m)n+1)

44
).

It remains to prove (
45
). First,
we show that the nonzero eigenvalues of
B(\bm{c})
and
B^{BD}(\bm{c})

are reciprocal to each other.
Let

be a nonzero eigenvalue of

.
Then the corresponding eigenvector
\bm{y}\in L
satisfies

, which is

P_{L}(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}\bm{y}=\ell\bm{y}
.
Hence,

satisfies

on both sides

Thus,

is an eigenvector of
B(\mathbf{c})
with eigenvalue

.
Similarly, we can reverse the above argument and verify that if
\bm{z}
is a
nonzero eigenvector of
B(\bm{c})
with eigenvalue

(B(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})\bm{z}

B^{BD}(\bm{c})

is the reciprocal of the smallest eigenvalue of

,
and Assumption (B4) implies that

\bm{z}^{T}B^{BD}(\bm{c})\bm{z}\leq|\bm{z}|^{2}/\mu
for all
\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

Lemma 13

\bm{c}

Proof of (i).

Before we can estimate the right-hand side, we need some preparations.

We define the vector

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}z_{i}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}E_{ij}(\bm{c})(z_{j}-z_{n})+z_{n}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}E_{nj}(\bm{c})(z_{j}-z_{n})

Choosing

\displaystyle(P_{L})_{ij}Z_{i}\cdot Z_{j}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{n-1}E_{ij}(\bm{c})Q_{ik}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})\nabla c_{k}\cdot Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})\nabla c_{\ell}.

We claim that there exists

Consider next the

,
with

is the unit matrix on

are symmetric and positive definite, we deduce that

Proof of (ii).
Inequality (

\displaystyle(P_{L})_{ij}Y_{i}\cdot Y_{j}=\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{n-1}E_{ij}(\bm{c})\big(Q_{ik}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})\nabla c_{k}-Q_{ik}(\widetilde{\bar{\bm{c}}})\nabla\bar{c}_{k}\big)\cdot\big(Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})\nabla c_{\ell}-Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bar{\bm{c}}})\nabla\bar{c}_{\ell}\big)

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}\times\big(Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})\nabla(c_{\ell}-\bar{c}_{\ell})+(Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})-Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bar{\bm{c}}}))\nabla\bar{c}_{\ell}\big).

\displaystyle+\bm{z}_{2})^{T}E(\bm{z}_{1}+\bm{z}_{2})=\bm{z}_{1}^{T}E\bm{z}_{1}+\bm{z}_{1}^{T}E\bm{z}_{2}+\bm{z}_{2}^{T}E\bm{z}_{1}+\bm{z}_{2}^{T}E\bm{z}_{2}

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}-\sum_{i,j,k,\ell=1}^{n-1}E_{ij}(\bm{c})\big((Q_{ik}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})-Q_{ik}(\widetilde{\bar{\bm{c}}}))\nabla\bar{c}_{k}\big)\cdot\big((Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bm{c}})-Q_{j\ell}(\widetilde{\bar{\bm{c}}}))\nabla\bar{c}_{\ell}\big)=:J_{5}+J_{6}.

5.1.
A cross-diffusion system for thin-film solar cells

Thin-film crystalline solar cells can be fabricated by the so-called physical

on electrically conductive materials as a thin coating. It is shown in

[

\partial_{t}c_{i}=\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{ij}(u_{j}\nabla u_{i}-u_{i}\nabla u_{j})\bigg),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

for

.
This model can be formulated
as a generalized Maxwell–Stefan system. Indeed, let

and

for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
,
where

is given by (
10
) with

.
Then

(see (
18

. Equation (
43
) becomes

(72)

\partial_{t}c_{i}=\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}\nabla\log c_{j}\bigg),\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

Because of (
10
), the mobility matrix
(\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}})

reads as

\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{k\neq i}a_{ik}c_{i}c_{k}&\quad\mbox{if }i=j,\\
-a_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}&\quad\mbox{if }i\neq j,\end{array}\right.

and an elementary computation shows that (

).

Although it can be checked that the matrix

satisfies Assumptions (B1)–(B4),

5.2.
A tumor-growth model

The growth of a symmetric avascular tumor can be modeled on the mechanical level
by diffusion fluxes of the tumor cells, the extracellular matrix (ECM), and
the interstitial fluid (water, nutrients, etc.). The model was suggested in

[
25
]
and analyzed in
[
27
]
. The evolution of the volume fractions

c_{i}
of the tumor cells, ECM, and interstitial fluid is given by
(see, e.g.,
[
29
, Section 4.2]
)

(73)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}+\operatorname{div}(c_{i}u_{i})

\displaystyle=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,3,

(74)

\displaystyle\nabla(c_{i}P_{i})+c_{i}\nabla p

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}(u_{i}-u_{j}),\quad i=1,2,

(75)

\displaystyle c_{3}\nabla p

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}k_{ij}c_{i}c_{j}(u_{i}-u_{j}),

where
k_{ij}=k_{ji}>0
for
i,j=1,2,3
,
the partial pressures
P_{1}
,
P_{2}
and the phase pressure
p
are given by

P_{1}=c_{1},\quad P_{2}=\beta c_{2}(1+\theta c_{1}),\quad p=-c_{1}P_{1}-c_{2}P_{2},

\beta>0
,
\theta>0
are suitable parameters, and it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{3}c_{i}=1
.

We claim that (
73
)–(
75
) can be formulated
as a generalized Maxwell–Stefan system. We define the entropy densities
as in the previous example,
h_{i}(c_{i})=c_{i}(\log c_{i}-1)
,
i=1,2,3
.
With the matrix

W(\bm{c})=\begin{pmatrix}2c_{1}(1-c_{1})-\beta\theta c_{1}c_{2}^{2}&-2\beta c_{1}c_{2}(1+\theta c_{1})&0\\
-2c_{1}c_{2}+\beta\theta(1-c_{2})c_{2}^{2}&2\beta c_{2}(1-c_{2})(1+\theta c_{1})&0\\
-2c_{1}c_{3}-\beta\theta c_{3}c_{2}^{2}&-2\beta c_{3}c_{2}(1+\theta c_{1})&0\end{pmatrix},

the left-hand side of (
74
)–(
75
) can be written in a
more concise form:

\begin{pmatrix}\nabla(c_{1}P_{1})+c_{1}\nabla p\\
\nabla(c_{2}P_{2})+c_{2}\nabla p\\
c_{3}\nabla p\end{pmatrix}=W(\bm{c})\begin{pmatrix}\nabla c_{1}\\
\nabla c_{2}\\
\nabla c_{3}\end{pmatrix}.

Let the matrix
A(\bm{c})
be given by (
10
) with
D_{ij}=1/k_{ij}
.
Then the right-hand side of
(
74
)–(
75
) equals (also see (
12
))

-\sum_{j=1,\,j\neq i}^{3}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j})=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}u_{j}.

Thus, inverting

\sum_{j=1}^{n}W_{ij}(\bm{c})\nabla c_{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{3}\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}u_{j},\quad i=1,2,3,

(which is the same as (
74
)–(
75
)) yields

\sqrt{c_{i}}u_{i}=-\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}W_{jk}(\bm{c})\nabla c_{k},\quad i=1,2,3,

and system (
73
)–(
75
) can be written for
i=1,2,3
as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}W_{jk}(\bm{c})\nabla c_{k}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{j,k,\ell=1}^{3}\sqrt{c_{i}}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}W_{jk}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{k}}(P_{L})_{k\ell}\sqrt{c_{\ell}}\nabla\log c_{\ell}\bigg),

recalling definition (
22
) of
P_{L}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}c_{i}=\operatorname{div}\bigg(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sqrt{c_{i}}R_{i\ell}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{\ell}}\nabla\log c_{\ell}\bigg),\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle R_{i\ell}(\bm{c}):=\sum_{j,k=1}^{3}A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}W_{jk}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{k}}(P_{L})_{k\ell},\quad i,\ell=1,2,3.

Also in this case, it is more convenient to check the statements of Lemma
11

instead of Assumptions (B1)–(B4). Notice that
W(\bm{c})
is not symmetric,
so
R(\bm{c})
is not symmetric either. The elements
R_{ij}(\bm{c})

are bounded and Lipschitz continuous, since
A_{ij}^{BD}(\bm{c})
and

W_{ij}(\bm{c})
have these properties. The second statement of Lemma
11
,
namely property (
44
), is verified only for a special example that
was considered in
[
27
]
.

Lemma 15
.

Let
k_{ij}=1
for
i,j=1,2,3
and
0\leq\theta<4/\sqrt{\beta}
. Then,
with
m=\min_{i=1,2,3}c_{i}>0
, there exists
\lambda(m)>0
such that

\bm{z}^{T}R(\bm{c})\bm{z}\geq\lambda(m)|P_{L}\bm{z}|^{2}\quad\mbox{for all }\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

Proof.

The assumption
k_{ij}=1
implies that
A(\bm{c})=P_{L}
and hence

A^{BD}(\bm{c})=P_{L}(A(\bm{c})P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}=P_{L}
. Suppose that
for any
\bm{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
satisfying
\bm{y}\in L
(i.e.
\sqrt{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{y}=0
),
we have

(76)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}W_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}\geq\lambda(m)|\bm{y}|^{2}.

Then, for
\bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\bm{y}=P_{L}\bm{z}\in L
,

\bm{z}^{T}R(\bm{c})\bm{z}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}(P_{L}\bm{z})_{i}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}W_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}(P_{L}\bm{z})_{j}\geq\lambda(m)|P_{L}\bm{z}|^{2},

which proves the lemma.

It remains to verify (
76
). Using

y_{3}=-(\sqrt{c_{1}}y_{1}+\sqrt{c_{2}}y_{2})/\sqrt{c_{3}}
, we calculate

\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}W_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}=2\beta(1+\theta c_{1})(\sqrt{c_{2}}y_{2})^{2}+\beta\theta c_{2}(\sqrt{c_{1}}y_{1})(\sqrt{c_{2}}y_{2})+2(\sqrt{c_{1}}y_{1})^{2}.

Since

\kappa>0
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{3}\frac{1}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}W_{ij}(\bm{c})\sqrt{c_{j}}y_{i}y_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\kappa(c_{1}y_{1}^{2}+c_{2}y_{2}^{2})\geq\frac{\kappa}{3}\big(c_{1}y_{1}^{2}+c_{2}y_{2}^{2}+(\sqrt{c_{1}}y_{1}+\sqrt{c_{2}}y_{2})^{2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa}{3}(c_{1}y_{1}^{2}+c_{2}y_{2}^{2}+c_{3}y_{3}^{2})\geq\frac{\kappa}{3}\Big(\min_{i=1,2,3}c_{i}\Big)(y_{1}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+y_{3}^{2}),

proving the claim with
\lambda(m)=\kappa m/3
.
∎

We deduce from the previous lemma that the weak-strong uniqueness property
holds for the tumor-growth model if
k_{ij}=1
for
i,j=1,2,3
and

0\leq\theta<4/\sqrt{\beta}

[
27
]
for details.

5.3.
A multi-species porous-medium-type model

Another model is a generalization of the first example to illustrate that
also non-logarithmic entropies may be considered. We choose

with
\gamma>1
and
A(\bm{c})
as
in (
10
). The partial pressure

5.4.
Maxwell-Stefan system with different molar masses

In equations (
1
), we have implicitly assumed that all molar masses
of the species are the same. We show that the weak-strong uniqueness property also
holds for the model proposed in
[
3
,
5
]
without this assumption. In the
case of different molar masses
M_{i}
, we need to distinguish between the mass
densities
\rho_{i}
and the molar concentrations
c_{i}=\rho_{i}/M_{i}
.
The Maxwell–Stefan equations read for
i=1,\ldots,n
as

(77)

\partial_{t}\rho_{i}+\operatorname{div}(\rho_{i}u_{i})=0,\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{c^{2}D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j})=\rho_{i}\nabla\frac{\delta H}{\delta\rho_{i}}(\bm{\rho})-\rho_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{j}\nabla\frac{\delta H}{\delta\rho_{j}}(\bm{\rho}),

where
c=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}
.
As before, the restriction
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{j}=1
inherited from the initial data
is imposed. The second equation can be rewritten as

-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\rho_{i}\rho_{j}}{\widetilde{D}_{ij}(\bm{\rho})}(u_{i}-u_{j})=\rho_{i}\nabla\frac{\delta H}{\delta\rho_{i}}(\bm{\rho})-\rho_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\rho_{j}\nabla\frac{\delta H}{\delta\rho_{j}}(\bm{\rho}),

where
\widetilde{D}_{ij}(\bm{\rho})=c^{2}M_{i}M_{j}=(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\rho_{k}/M_{k})^{2}M_{i}M_{j}
.
Due to

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\rho_{k}}{M_{k}}\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\rho_{k}}{\max_{\ell=1,\ldots,n}{M_{\ell}}}=\frac{1}{\max_{\ell=1,\ldots,n}M_{\ell}},

the coefficients
\widetilde{D}_{ij}(\bm{\rho})
are uniformly bounded from below.
Since the proof of Lemma
4
only relies on the uniform boundness of
D_{ij}
,
Lemma
4
also holds for the following matrix
\widetilde{A}(\bm{c})
,
defined similarly as in (
10
):

\widetilde{A}_{ij}(\bm{\rho})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sum_{k=1,\,k\neq i}^{n}\rho_{k}/\widetilde{D}_{ik}(\bm{\rho})&\quad\mbox{if }i=j,\\
-\sqrt{\rho_{i}\rho_{j}}/\widetilde{D}_{ij}(\bm{\rho})&\quad\mbox{if }i\neq j.\end{array}\right.

Therefore, the weak-strong uniqueness holds if
H(\bm{c})
satisfies the assumptions of
Theorem
3
.

Recalling that
H(\bm{\rho})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}h_{i}(\rho_{i})dx
, we may
formulate the entropy in terms of the concentrations
\bm{c}
as

\eta(\bm{c})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\eta_{i}(c_{i})dx,
where

\eta_{i}(c_{i})=h_{i}(\rho_{i}/M_{i})
. Then we can rewrite the second equation in
(
77
) as

-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{c^{2}D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j})=c_{i}\nabla\mu_{i}-c_{i}M_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\nabla\mu_{j},

where
\mu_{i}=\eta_{i}^{\prime}(c_{i})
is the molar-based chemical potential.
Using the Gibbs–Duhem equation
\nabla p=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{j}\nabla\mu_{j}
, where
p

is the pressure, the above equation can be put into the form

-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{c^{2}D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j})=c_{i}\nabla\mu_{i}-\rho_{i}\nabla p,

which is
[
5
, Formula (203)]
. Yet another formulation in terms of the
molar fractions
X_{i}=c_{i}/c
is

-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{c^{2}D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j})=c_{i}\nabla_{p}\widetilde{\mu}_{i}+(\phi_{i}-\rho_{i})\nabla p,

where
\widetilde{\mu}_{i}
is given by
\widetilde{\mu}_{i}(p,X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mu_{i}(c_{i})
,
\nabla_{p}\widetilde{\mu}_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial\widetilde{\mu}_{i}/\partial X_{j})\nabla X_{j}
, and
\phi_{i}:=\partial\widetilde{\mu}_{i}/\partial p
is the
volume fraction.

A simple choice is the entropy

\eta_{i}(c_{i})=c_{i}\log c_{i}-c_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

corresponding to
h_{i}(\rho_{i})=(\rho_{i}/M_{i})(\log(\rho_{i}/M_{i})-1)
.
It leads to
\mu_{i}=\log c_{i}
,
p=c
, and the model

-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{c_{i}c_{j}}{c^{2}D_{ij}}(u_{i}-u_{j})=\nabla c_{i}-\rho_{i}\nabla c.

The existence of local strong solutions to this model can be proved as in

[
4
]
, while the existence of global weak solutions was shown in

[
9
]
.

Appendix A
The Bott–Duffin inverse

For the convenience of the reader, we recall the definition and some properties
of the Bott–Duffin inverse. Let
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n}
be an arbitrary matrix and

L\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a subspace.
The Bott–Duffin inverse is introduced in connection
to the solution of the constrained inversion problem
(see
[
7
]
,
[
2
, Ch 2.10]
)

(78)

Ax+y=b,\quad x\in L,\quad y\in L^{\perp}.

Let
P_{L}
and
P_{L^{\perp}}
be
the projection operators onto
L
and
L^{\perp}
, respectively.
The set of solutions of (
78
) is the same as the set of solutions to

(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})z=b
,
and
(x,y)
solves (
78
) if and only if

x=P_{L}z
and
y=P_{L^{\perp}}z=b-AP_{L}z
.
Then, if the matrix
AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
is invertible,
we define the
Bott–Duffin inverse
of
A
with respect to
L
by

(79)

A^{BD}:=P_{L}(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}.

and the solution to (
78
) is expressed in the form

(80)

x=A^{BD}b\,,\quad y=b-Ax.

If
L=\operatorname{ran}(A)
and
A
is symmetric, the Bott–Duffin inverse
is the same as the group inverse, which was investigated in the context of
Maxwell–Stefan systems in
[
6
]
.

Let
A
be symmetric. We call
A

L
-positive definite
if

\bm{z}^{T}A\bm{z}>0
for all
\bm{z}\in L\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
.
For this class of matrices, a generalized Bott–Duffin inverse is defined in

[
35
]
, which coincides with the classical Bott–Duffin inverse when

AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
is invertible. The following result is proved in

[
35
, Lemma 2c and 1b]
.

Lemma 16
.

Let
A
be symmetric and
L
-positive definite. Then

\displaystyle{\rm(i)}

\displaystyle\ A^{BD}P_{L^{\perp}}=0,

\displaystyle{\rm(ii)}

\displaystyle\ \operatorname{ran}(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})=P_{L}\operatorname{ran}(A)\otimes L^{\perp},\quad\ker(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})=\ker(AP_{L})\cap L.

It follows from property (i) that
A^{BD}
can be formulated as

(81)

A^{BD}=P_{L}(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}(P_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})=P_{L}(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}P_{L}=A^{BD}P_{L}.

Lemma 17
.

Let
A
be symmetric and
L=\operatorname{ran}A
,
L^{\perp}=\ker A
.
Then
AP_{L}=A
,
P_{L}A=A
,
A^{BD}
is well defined and symmetric.

Proof.

The identities
AP_{L}=A
and
P_{L}A=A
follow immediately from
L=\operatorname{ran}A
,
We infer from property (ii) that

\ker(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})=\ker(AP_{L})\cap L=\ker(A)\cap L=L^{\perp}\cap L=\{0\},

showing that
AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
is invertible. The matrix
AP_{L}=P_{L}AP_{L}
is symmetric,
since
P_{L}
and
A
are symmetric. Also
P_{L^{\perp}}
is symmetric,
so
AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
and its inverse are symmetric too. Taking into account
(
81
), this implies that

A^{BD}=P_{L}(AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}})^{-1}P_{L}
is also symmetric.
∎

In our context, we are interested in the constrained inversion

Ax=b,\quad x\in L,

where
A
is a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix,
with
L=\operatorname{ran}(A)
and thus
L^{\perp}=\ker(A)
, and
b\in L
.
Lemma
17
implies that
AP_{L}+P_{L^{\perp}}
is invertible and

A^{BD}
is well defined by (
79
).
Because of (
80
), we can express the inverse as

x=A^{BD}b
if
b\in L
.

Appendix B
Pointwise estimates for entropy functions

For the convenience of the reader, we recall the following lower bounds.

Lemma 18
.

The following estimates hold for any
c
,
\bar{c}\in[0,1]
:

c\log\frac{c}{\bar{c}}-(c-\bar{c})\geq\frac{1}{2}(c-\bar{c})^{2},\quad c\log\frac{c}{\bar{c}}-(c-\bar{c})\geq(\sqrt{c}-\sqrt{\bar{c}})^{2}.

Proof.

Let
f(c)=c\log c
. Then

f(c)-f(\bar{c})=f(\theta(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})\big|_{\theta=0}^{1}=(c-\bar{c})\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(\theta(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})d\theta

and

\displaystyle c\log

\displaystyle\frac{c}{\bar{c}}-(c-\bar{c})=f(c)-f(\bar{c})-f^{\prime}(\bar{c})(c-\bar{c})

\displaystyle=(c-\bar{c})\int_{0}^{1}\big(f^{\prime}(\theta(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})-f^{\prime}(\bar{c})\big)d\theta

\displaystyle=(c-\bar{c})\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(s(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})\big|_{s=0}^{\theta}d\theta=(c-\bar{c})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\theta}f^{\prime\prime}(s(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})dsd\theta.

The first inequality follows after observing that
f^{\prime\prime}(s(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})=1/(s(c-\bar{c})+\bar{c})\geq 1
.

For the second inequality, we define
g(c)=(c\log c-c+1)/(\sqrt{c}-1)^{2}
for

c\neq 1
and
g(1)=2
. Then
g
is continuous and increasing, which implies that

g(c)\geq g(0)=1
and proves the statement.
∎

Lemma 19
.

Let
\bm{c},\bar{\bm{c}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
satisfy
0\leq c_{i}\leq 1
,

m\leq\bar{c}_{i}\leq 1
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
and suppose that
h_{i}\in C([0,1])\cap C^{2}((0,1])
satisfies

h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(c_{i})>0\quad\mbox{for }0<c_{i}\leq 1.

Then, for some
\kappa_{m}>0
,

(82)

h_{i}(c_{i}|\bar{c}_{i})=h_{i}(c_{i})-h_{i}(\bar{c}_{i})-h_{i}^{\prime}(\bar{c}_{i})(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})\geq\kappa_{m}(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})^{2}.

Proof.

By Taylor expansion, the relative entropy density satisfies

\lim_{c_{i}\to\bar{c}_{i}}\frac{h_{i}(c_{i}|\bar{c}_{i})}{(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})^{2}}=\lim_{c_{i}\to\bar{c}_{i}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\theta}h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(s(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})+\bar{c}_{i})dsd\theta=\frac{1}{2}h_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\bar{c}_{i})>0.

Therefore,
h_{i}(c_{i}|\bar{c}_{i})/(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})^{2}
is a continuous function with
a positive minimum:

\kappa_{m}:=\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\min_{c_{i}\in[0,1],\,\bar{c}_{i}\in[m,1]}\frac{h_{i}(c_{i}|\bar{c}_{i})}{(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})^{2}}>0.

This shows that
h_{i}(c_{i}|\bar{c}_{i})\geq\kappa_{m}(c_{i}-\bar{c}_{i})^{2}
for

c_{i}\in[0,1]
,
\bar{c}_{i}\in[m,1]
and proves (
82
).
∎

Appendix C
Thermodynamic derivation of the generalized Maxwell–Stefan system

The aim of this section is to derive (
16
)–(
17
) from
elementary thermodynamic principles. We assume that the evolution of the gaseous
mixture is given by the conservation of mass and energy (without chemical reactions),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho c_{i})+\operatorname{div}(\rho c_{i}v+J_{i})

\displaystyle=0,

(83)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho U)+\operatorname{div}(\rho Uv+q)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\operatorname{div}(\rho v)

\displaystyle=0,\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

where
\rho_{i}
is the partial density of the
i
th species,
\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}

the total density,
c_{i}=\rho_{i}/\rho
the concentration of the
i
th species,

v
the barycentric velocity,
J_{i}
the
i
th flux,
q
the
heat flux, and the internal energy
U
is given by the first law of thermodynamics
in differential form by

(84)

dU=TdS-pdV+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}dc_{i},

where
S
is the entropy,
V=1/\rho
the volume, and
\mu_{i}=\partial U/\partial c_{i}

the
i
th chemical potential. By definition, it holds that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}=1
.
Adding the first and last equation in (
83
), we see that

\operatorname{div}\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}=0
, which motivates us to assume that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}=0
.

The sum of the fluxes should vanish,
\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}=0
, to be consistent
with the conservation laws.

With the material derivative
D_{t}f=\partial_{t}f+v\cdot\nabla f
, the conservation laws
can be simplified to

\rho D_{t}c_{i}+\operatorname{div}J_{i}=0,\quad\rho D_{t}U+\operatorname{div}q=0,\quad D_{t}\rho+\rho\operatorname{div}v=0.

Inserting these equations into equation (
84
), formulated as

D_{t}U=TD_{t}S-pD_{t}V+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}D_{t}c_{i}
, yields the entropy balance

\displaystyle\rho D_{t}S

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho}{T}D_{t}U+\frac{\rho}{T}pD_{t}\bigg(\frac{1}{\rho}\bigg)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mu_{i}}{T}D_{t}c_{i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{T}\operatorname{div}q+\frac{p}{T}\operatorname{div}v+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mu_{i}}{T}\operatorname{div}J_{i}=-\operatorname{div}J_{S}+r_{S},

where

J_{S}=\frac{q}{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\mu_{i}}{T}J_{i},\quad r_{S}=q\cdot\nabla\frac{1}{T}+p\operatorname{div}v+\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}\cdot\nabla\frac{\mu_{i}}{T}

are the entropy flux and entropy production, respectively.

In our Maxwell–Stefan model, we assume that
v=0
and
T=1
. Then the
entropy production simplifies to
r_{S}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}\cdot\nabla\mu_{i}
.
It can be reformulated by taking into account that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}J_{i}=0

and hence
J_{i}/\sqrt{c_{i}}\in L=\{\bm{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\sqrt{\bm{c}}\cdot\bm{x}=0\}
:

r_{S}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{J_{i}}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\cdot\sqrt{c_{i}}\nabla\mu_{i}=-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{J_{i}}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\cdot(P_{L})_{ij}\sqrt{c_{j}}\nabla\mu_{j}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{J_{i}}{\sqrt{c_{i}}}\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}(P_{L})_{ij}\sqrt{c_{j}}\nabla\mu_{j},

where the projection
P_{L}
on
L
is defined in (
22
).
By the second law of thermodynamics, it should hold that
r_{S}\geq 0
.
To guarantee this property, we introduce
a positive semidefinite matrix
B(\bm{c})
such that

(85)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}(P_{L})_{ij}\sqrt{c_{j}}\nabla\mu_{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{ij}(\bm{c})\frac{J_{j}}{\sqrt{c_{j}}},\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

We claim that these equations correspond to the generalized Maxwell–Stefan
equations (
17
)
after setting
J_{i}=c_{i}u_{i}
and
K_{ij}(\bm{c})=\sqrt{c_{i}}B_{ij}(\bm{c})/\sqrt{c_{j}}

(see (
18
)). Indeed, the left-hand side of (
85
), multiplied by

\sqrt{c_{i}}
, becomes

\sqrt{c_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(P_{L})_{ij}\sqrt{c_{j}}\nabla\mu_{j}=c_{i}\nabla\mu_{i}-c_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}\nabla\mu_{j},

and the right-hand side of (
85
), multiplied by
\sqrt{c_{i}}
, equals

-\sqrt{c_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{ij}(\bm{c})\frac{J_{j}}{\sqrt{c_{j}}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}(\bm{c})J_{j}=-\sum_{j=1}^{n}K_{ij}(\bm{c})c_{j}u_{j}.

Hence, observing that
\mu_{j}=\partial U/\partial c_{j}
corresponds to
\delta H/\delta c_{j}
,
(
85
) equals (
17
).
