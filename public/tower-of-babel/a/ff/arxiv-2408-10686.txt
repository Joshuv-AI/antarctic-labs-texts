Abstract
We study the gradient wild bootstrap-based inference for instrumental variable quantile regressions in the framework of a small number of large clusters in which the number of clusters is viewed as fixed, and the number of observations for each cluster diverges to infinity. For the Wald inference, we show that our wild bootstrap Wald test, with or without studentization using the cluster-robust covariance estimator (CRVE), controls size asymptotically up to a small error as long as the parameter of endogenous variable is strongly identified in at least one of the clusters. We further show that the wild bootstrap Wald test with CRVE studentization is more powerful for distant local alternatives than that without. Last, we develop a wild bootstrap Anderson-Rubin (AR) test for the weak-identification-robust inference. We show it controls size asymptotically up to a small error, even under weak or partial identification for all clusters. We illustrate the good finite-sample performance of the new inference methods using simulations and provide an empirical application to a well-known dataset about US local labor markets. Keywords: Gradient Wild Bootstrap, Weak Instruments, Clustered Data, Randomization Test, Instrumental Variable Quantile Regression. JEL codes: C12, C26, C31

Introduction

The instrumental variable (IV) regression is one of the five most widely used methods for causal inference, as highlighted by
Angrist and
Pischke (2008)
, and it is often employed in analyses involving clustered data. For instance,
Young (2022)
examines 1,359 IV regressions across 31 papers published by the American Economic Association (AEA), with 24 of these papers accounting for the clustering of observations. In the context of quantile regression models, where endogeneity may be present,
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2004)
;
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2005)
;
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2006)
;
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2008)
(hereafter referred to as CH) developed an instrumental variables quantile regression (IVQR) method. This approach offers a general IV procedure to address the endogeneity of regressors in quantile regressions, and it has been widely adopted by empirical researchers to capture the distributional effects of endogenous variables.

However, three difficulties arise when running IVQR with clustered data.
First, the number of clusters is small in many empirical applications with IVs.
For instance,
Acemoglu et al. (2011)
cluster the standard errors at the country/polity level, resulting in 12-19 clusters,
Glitz and
Meyersson (2020)
cluster at the sectoral level with 16 sectors, and
Rogall (2021)
clusters at the province (district) level with 11 provinces (30 districts), respectively.
Autor
et al. (2013)
study the effects of Chinese imports on local labor markets in the US by clustering at the state level. However, if we focus on the estimation and inference for the effects on specific regions such as the South region designated by the US Census Bureau, there are only 16 states. Furthermore,
Bester
et al. (2011)
and
Leung (2023)
partition spatial and network data into clusters, respectively. Both papers consider the asymptotic setting in which the number of clusters is small and, thus, treated as fixed. When the number of clusters is small, conventional cluster-robust inference procedures may be unreliable for IVQR.

Second, in many applications, the strength of IVs may be relatively heterogeneous across clusters, with a few clusters providing the main identification power. For instance, Figure
1
reports the estimated first-stage coefficients for each cluster (state) from the South region in
Autor
et al.
’s (
2013
) dataset, which suggests that there exists substantial variation in the IV strength among states. Specifically, the first-stage coefficients of some states are relatively large compared with the rest in the region. In contrast, some other states have coefficients that are rather close to zero and, thus, potentially subject to weak identification. Some states even have opposite signs for their first-stage coefficients. However, there is no existing proven-valid inference method for IVQR with few clusters, where identification may be weak in some clusters.

Third, it is also possible that IVs are weak in all clusters, in which case researchers need to use weak-identification-robust inference methods for IVQR
(
Chernozhukov and
Hansen, 2008
;
Chernozhukov et al., 2009
;
Andrews
et al., 2019
)
.

Motivated by these challenges, this paper investigates inference for IVQR with a small, fixed number of clusters and weak within-cluster error dependence, accommodating significant cluster-level heterogeneity in IV strength. We define clusters where the structural parameter (i.e., for the endogenous variable) is strongly identified as “strong IV clusters.” We propose a gradient wild bootstrap procedure for IVQR with clustered data. Our findings show that a bootstrap Wald test, whether studentized by the cluster-robust variance estimator (CRVE) or not, asymptotically controls size, given at least one strong IV cluster. The gradient wild bootstrap tests demonstrate power against local alternatives at the 10% and 5% significance levels with at least five and six strong IV clusters, respectively. Additionally, the CRVE-based bootstrap Wald test proves more powerful for distant local alternatives. We also develop a gradient wild bootstrap
Anderson and
Rubin (1949, AR)
test for IVQR that controls size regardless of instrument strength. Compared to analytical methods using HAC estimators, all our bootstrap inference methods are agnostic to within-cluster dependence structure, avoiding complex covariance matrix estimation and making it applicable to datasets with various weak dependence structures, such as cross-sectional, serial, network, and spatial dependence.

Setup, Estimation, and Inference Procedure

2.1
Setup

Throughout the paper, we observe clustered data where the clusters are indexed by
j\in[J]
and units in the
j
-th cluster are indexed by
i\in I_{n,j}=\{1,...,n_{j}\}
. For the
i
-th unit in the
j
-th cluster, we observe
y_{i,j}\in\textbf{R}
,
X_{i,j}\in\textbf{R}
, and
W_{i,j}\in\textbf{R}^{d_{w}}
as an outcome of interest, a scalar endogenous regressor, and exogenous regressors, respectively.
Furthermore, we let
Z_{i,j}\in\textbf{R}^{d_{z}}
be the exogenous variables that are excluded from the outcome equation defined through conditional CDF:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(y_{i,j}\leq X_{i,j}\beta_{n}(\tau)+W_{i,j}^{\top}\gamma_{n}(\tau)|W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})=\tau,\hskip 11.74988pt\tau\in\Upsilon,

(1)

where
\Upsilon
is a compact subset of
(0,1)
.

Throughout the paper, we focus on the setting with a single endogenous variable, as it is the most common case in empirical applications involving IVs. For instance, 101 out of 230 specifications in
Andrews
et al.
’s (
2019
) sample and 1,087 out of 1,359 in
Young
’s (
2022
) sample feature one endogenous regressor and one IV. Similarly,
Lee
et al. (2022)
find that 61 out of 123 IV papers published in
AER
between 2013 and 2019 use single-IV regressions. While our setting also accommodates multiple IVs, most IVQR applications involve only one endogenous variable and one IV, as seen in studies by
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2004)
;
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2006)
;
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2008)
, and
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2013)
. In our empirical application, we revisit the influential study by
Autor
et al. (2013)
, which also employs a single endogenous variable and one IV.

We allow the parameter of interest
\beta_{n}(\tau)
(and the coefficient
\gamma_{n}(\tau)
for the exogenous controls) to shift with respect to (w.r.t.) the sample size, which incorporates the analyses of size and local power in a concise manner:

\beta_{n}(\tau)=\beta_{0}(\tau)+\mu_{\beta}(\tau)/r_{n}
, and
\gamma_{n}(\tau)=\gamma_{0}(\tau)+\mu_{\gamma}(\tau)/r_{n}
, where
\mu_{\beta}(\tau)\in\textbf{R}
and
\mu_{\gamma}(\tau)\in\textbf{R}^{d_{w}}
are the local parameters and

is the convergence rate of the score defined later. Throughout the paper, for a generic function
g
of data
D_{i,j}=(y_{i,j},X_{i,j},W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})
, we let
\mathbb{P}_{n}g(D_{i,j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}g(D_{i,j})
,
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n}g(D_{i,j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\mathbb{E}g(D_{i,j})
,
\mathbb{P}_{n,j}g(D_{i,j})=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}g(D_{i,j})
, and
\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n,j}g(D_{i,j})=\frac{1}{n_{j}}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\mathbb{E}g(D_{i,j})
.

2.2
Estimation

Following
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2006)
, we construct instrumental variables
\Phi_{i,j}(\tau)\in\textbf{R}^{d_{\phi}}
from
(W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})
, that is,
\Phi_{i,j}(\tau)=\Phi(W_{i,j},Z_{i,j},\tau)
. For the validity of our bootstrap inference with a fixed number of clusters, we further require the instruments to be orthogonal to the control variables in the quantile regression context. Therefore, the function
\Phi(\cdot)
may be unknown but can be estimated as
\hat{\Phi}(\cdot)
. The corresponding feasible IVs are defined as
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)=\hat{\Phi}(W_{i,j},Z_{i,j},\tau)
. We will provide more details about the construction of
\hat{\Phi}(\cdot)
in Section
3.3
. Additionally, a scalar nonnegative weight is defined as
V_{i,j}(\tau)
, which also may be unknown, and its estimator is defined as
\hat{V}_{i,j}(\tau)
. Then, the estimation of
\beta_{n}(\tau)
can be implemented via a profiled method described below. For a given value
b
of
\beta_{n}(\tau)
, we first compute

\displaystyle(\hat{\gamma}(b,\tau),\hat{\theta}(b,\tau))=\arg\inf_{r,t}\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\rho_{\tau}(y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}b-W_{i,j}^{\top}r-\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tau)t)\hat{V}_{i,j}(\tau),

(2)

where
\rho_{\tau}(u)=u(\tau-1\{u\leq 0\})
.
Under appropriate conditions for strong identification, which will be made clear later, we can estimate
\beta_{n}(\tau)
by
\hat{\beta}(\tau)
defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}(\tau)=\arg\inf_{b\in\mathcal{B}}||\hat{\theta}(b,\tau)||_{\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)},

(3)

where
\mathcal{B}
is a compact subset of
R
,
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)
is some
d_{\phi}\times d_{\phi}
weighting matrix, and the notation
||u||_{A}
for a compatible vector
u
and matrix
A
means
(u^{\top}Au)^{1/2}
. Last, we define
\hat{\gamma}(\tau)=\hat{\gamma}(\hat{\beta}(\tau),\tau)
and
\hat{\theta}(\tau)=\hat{\theta}(\hat{\beta}(\tau),\tau)
. Following the lead of
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2006)
, in practice, we suggest setting
\hat{V}_{i,j}(\tau)=1
and
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)=\mathbb{I}_{d_{\phi}}
or
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}_{n}\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tau)
. When the instrument is a scalar so that the model is just identified, the choice of
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)
becomes irrelevant.
In addition, we note that the case of just identification is always achievable because even if the original IV
Z_{i,j}
is multi-dimensional, we can construct
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}
as the prediction of
X_{i,j}
using
Z_{i,j}
and
W_{i,j}
from a first-stage linear regression, which is again a scalar (see Section
3.3
for further details on the construction of
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)
).

2.3
Gradient Wild Bootstrap Inference

2.3.1
Inference Procedure for Wald Statistics

In this section, we consider the null and local alternative hypotheses defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:\beta_{n}(\tau)=\beta_{0}(\tau),\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ \tau\in\Upsilon\hskip 11.74988ptv.s.\hskip 11.74988pt\mathcal{H}_{1,n}:\beta_{n}(\tau)\neq\beta_{0}(\tau),\penalty\ \exists\penalty\ \tau\in\Upsilon,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mu_{\beta}(\tau)=0,\penalty\ \forall\penalty\ \tau\in\Upsilon\hskip 11.74988ptv.s.\hskip 11.74988pt\mathcal{H}_{1,n}:\mu_{\beta}(\tau)\neq 0,\penalty\ \exists\penalty\ \tau\in\Upsilon,

(4)

where
\Upsilon
is a compact subset of
(0,1)

\hat{A}_{2}(\tau)

\displaystyle T_{n}=\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||(\hat{\beta}(\tau)-\beta_{0}(\tau))||_{\hat{A}_{2}(\tau)}

:

Let

\displaystyle(\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*}(b,\tau),\hat{\theta}_{g}^{*}(b,\tau))=

\displaystyle\arg\inf_{r,t}\biggl[\sum_{j\in[J]}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\rho_{\tau}(y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}b-W_{i,j}^{\top}r-\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tau)t)\hat{V}_{i,j}(\tau)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in[J]}g_{j}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\hat{f}^{\top}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{0}(\tau),\hat{\gamma}^{r}(\tau),0)\begin{pmatrix}r\\
t\end{pmatrix}\biggr],

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{g}^{*}(\tau)=

\displaystyle\arg\inf_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\left[||\hat{\theta}_{g}^{*}(b,\tau)||_{\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)}\right],\hskip 11.74988pt\text{and}\hskip 11.74988pt\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*}(\tau)=\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*}(\hat{\beta}_{g}^{*}(\tau),\tau),

(6)

where the null-restricted estimator

Remark 2.1
.

Several remarks regarding the choice of estimators in the above algorithm are in order. Specifically, in

\hat{\gamma}^{r}(\tau)

\hat{\beta}(\tau)

Canay
et al. (2021)

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{CR}(\tau)=\left[\hat{G}^{\top}(\tau)\hat{\Omega}(\tau,\tau)\hat{G}(\tau)\right]^{-1},

The normalization factor

\hat{G}(\tau)

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\top}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left[\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{X,\Phi}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{X,\Phi}^{\top}(\tau)\right]^{-1}\left[\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{X,\Phi}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)\right],

\displaystyle\hat{G}^{\top}(\tau)=\left[\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{X,\Phi}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{X,\Phi}^{\top}(\tau)\right]^{-1}\left[\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{X,\Phi}(\tau)\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)\right].

In addition, even if we use a consistent estimator of the Jacobian

as

To define an appropriate gradient bootstrap analogue of

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\tau,g}^{*}(D_{i,j})=g_{j}\hat{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{0}(\tau),\hat{\gamma}^{r}(\tau),0)+\hat{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\hat{\beta}_{g}^{*}(\tau),\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*}(\tau),0)-\hat{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\hat{\beta}(\tau),\hat{\gamma}(\tau),0),

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{g}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[J]}\omega\left\{\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\hat{f}_{\tau,g}^{*}(D_{i,j})\right\}\left\{\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\hat{f}_{\tau^{\prime},g}^{*}(D_{i,j})\right\}^{\top}\omega^{\top}.

(10)

Then, we let
\hat{A}_{2,g}^{*}(\tau)
in
Step 3
of the previous bootstrap algorithm equal
\hat{A}_{CR,g}^{*}(\tau)
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{CR,g}^{*}(\tau)=\left[\hat{G}^{\top}(\tau)\hat{\Omega}_{g}^{*}(\tau,\tau)\hat{G}(\tau)\right]^{-1},

where
\hat{\beta}_{g}^{*}(\tau)
and
\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*}(\tau)
are defined in (
6
).
The bootstrap counterpart of
T_{CR,n}
and the critical value are defined as

T_{CR,n}^{*}(g)=\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||\hat{\beta}_{g}^{*}(\tau)-\hat{\beta}(\tau)||_{\hat{A}_{CR,g}^{*}(\tau)}\hskip 11.74988pt\text{and}\hskip 11.74988pt\hat{c}_{CR,n}(1-\alpha),\hskip 11.74988pt\text{respectively}.

Remark 2.2
.

Several remarks are in order regarding our design of
\hat{\Omega}_{g}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
in (
10
). First,
when
\mathcal{H}_{0}
is true,
\hat{\Omega}_{g}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
has the same limit distribution as
\hat{\Omega}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
, which is needed for the asymptotic validity of the bootstrap Wald test with CRVE. Second, we design
\hat{\Omega}_{g}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
in such a way so that under
\mathcal{H}_{1,n}
, the local parameter
\mu_{\beta}(\tau)
defined in (

\hat{\Omega}_{g}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
in sufficiently many randomization draws.
By contrast,
\mu_{\beta}(\tau)
does not enter the limit distribution of
\hat{\Omega}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
in the original Wald statistic

.
This leads to a further power improvement for our bootstrap test studentized by CRVE (more details are given in Theorem
3.3
and Remark

below).

Remark 2.3
.

We note that if we are in case (1) and
\Upsilon
defined in (
4
) is a singleton, then
\hat{A}_{2}(\tau)
(which has a deterministic limit) shows up in both the test statistic

and its bootstrap counterpart

, and thus, the critical value

so that

In addition, if we use CRVE to studentize the test statistic (i.e., in case (2)),

and the critical value

Such an invariance property is one of the advantages of using the bootstrap tests.

2.3.2
Inference Procedure for Weak-instrument-robust Statistics

This section considers the weak-instrument-robust inference for
\beta_{n}(\tau)
when it may be weakly or partially identified. Recall
\beta_{n}(\tau)=\beta_{0}(\tau)+\mu_{\beta}(\tau)/r_{n}
. Under the null, we have
\mu_{\beta}(\tau)=0
, or equivalently,
\beta_{n}(\tau)=\beta_{0}(\tau)
. Our test statistic follows the construction by
Chernozhukov and
Hansen (2008)
. Specifically, we let

\displaystyle AR_{n}=\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||\hat{\theta}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau)||_{\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)},

where
\hat{\theta}(b,\tau)
is defined in (
2
) and
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
is a
d_{\phi}\times d_{\phi}
weighting matrix, which will be specified later. We differentiate the weighting matrix used here (denoted as
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
) with that used for the estimation of
\beta_{n}(\tau)
in (
3
) (denoted as
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)
) because of their different usages: the former is for the construction of the weak-instrument-robust test statistic while the later is for the estimation under strong identification. Theoretically, we require
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
to have a deterministic limit, same as
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)
.

Next, the bootstrap procedure for the weak-instrument-robust inference is defined as follows.

Step 1
:

Recall
\textbf{G}=\{-1,1\}^{J}
and for any
g=(g_{1},\cdots,g_{J})\in\textbf{G}
, the null-imposed bootstrap estimators
(\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*r}(\tau),\hat{\theta}_{g}^{*r}(\tau))=(\hat{\gamma}_{g}^{*}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau),\hat{\theta}_{g}^{*}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau))
for
(\gamma,\theta)
are defined in (
6
).

Step 2
:

The bootstrap test statistic is then defined as
AR_{n}^{*}(g)=\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||\hat{\theta}_{g}^{*r}(\tau)-\hat{\theta}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau)||_{\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)}
.

Step 3
:

Let
\hat{c}_{AR,n}(1-\alpha)
denote the
1-\alpha
quantile of
\{AR_{n}^{*}(g)\}_{g\in\textbf{G}}
,
and we reject the null hypothesis when
AR_{n}>\hat{c}_{AR,n}(1-\alpha)
.

It is also possible to studentize
\hat{\theta}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau)
by a (null-imposed) CRVE, i.e., replace
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
with
\tilde{A}_{CR}(\tau)
, where

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{CR}(\tau)=\left[\hat{H}(\tau)\tilde{\Omega}(\tau,\tau)\hat{H}(\tau)\right]^{-1},

\hat{H}(\tau)\in\Re^{d_{\phi}\times d_{\phi}}
is some symmetric matrix, and

\displaystyle\tilde{\Omega}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in[J]}\omega\left[\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\hat{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{0}(\tau),\hat{\gamma}^{r}(\tau),0)\right]\left[\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\hat{f}_{\tau^{\prime}}(D_{i,j},\beta_{0}(\tau^{\prime}),\hat{\gamma}^{r}(\tau^{\prime}),0)\right]^{\top}\omega^{\top}.

Note that different from
\hat{\Omega}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
of the Wald test statistic in the previous section, we impose the null hypothesis in the form of
\tilde{\Omega}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})
.
This is essential for the validity of the test under weak/non-identification for all clusters. Additionally, note that
\tilde{A}_{CR}(\tau)
admits a random limit in our setting, which distinguishes it from
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
used for
AR_{n}
.

We define the corresponding test statistic, its bootstrap counterparts, and critical value as

\displaystyle AR_{CR,n}=\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||\hat{\theta}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau)||_{\tilde{A}_{CR}(\tau)},\hskip 11.74988ptAR_{CR,n}^{*}(g)=\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||\hat{\theta}_{g}^{*r}(\tau)-\hat{\theta}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau)||_{\tilde{A}_{CR}(\tau)},

and
\hat{c}_{AR,CR,n}(1-\alpha)
, respectively. Naturally, for
\hat{H}(\tau)
, we want to use a consistent estimator for the Jacobian of
\hat{\theta}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau)
, which would again involve a kernel density estimation and parameter tuning. Therefore, similar to the bootstrap Wald inference, we instead suggest setting

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\tau)=\mathbb{P}_{n}\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tau)\hat{V}_{i,j}(\tau).

Then, we reject the null hypothesis when
AR_{CR,n}>\hat{c}_{AR,CR,n}(1-\alpha)
.
Note that different from the Wald statistic, we do not need to bootstrap
\tilde{A}_{CR}(\tau)
here because
\tilde{\Omega}(\tau,\tau)
is invariant to sign changes. Also, when the IV
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)
is a scalar and
\Upsilon
is a singleton, the choice of
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
becomes irrelevant as it gets canceled in both the test statistic and the bootstrap critical value. Therefore, the bootstrap tests based on
AR_{n}
and
AR_{CR,n}
are numerically equivalent in this case.

Computation

Furthermore, we note that given a value of

and

. Denote them as

and

(W_{i,j}^{\top},\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}^{\top}(\tau))

. Last, denote
\eta^{\top}=(r^{\top},t^{\top})
, and

\displaystyle S=\sum_{j\in[J]}g_{j}\sum_{i\in I_{n,j}}\hat{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{0}(\tau),\hat{\gamma}^{r}(\tau),0).

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\rho_{\tau}(\mathcal{Y}_{i}-\mathcal{X}_{i}^{\top}\eta)\mathcal{V}_{i}-S^{\top}\eta=\tau\mathcal{V}^{\top}u+(1-\tau)\mathcal{V}^{\top}v-S^{\top}\eta,

. Therefore, the gradient-based wild bootstrap estimator

\displaystyle(\hat{\eta},\hat{u},\hat{v})

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\eta,u,v}\tau\mathcal{V}^{\top}u+(1-\tau)\mathcal{V}^{\top}v-S^{\top}\eta

\displaystyle s.t.\hskip 11.74988pt\mathcal{Y}-\mathcal{X}^{\top}\eta=u-v,\hskip 11.74988ptu\in\Re^{n}_{+},\hskip 11.74988ptv\in\Re^{n}_{+},\hskip 11.74988pt\text{and}\hskip 11.74988pt\eta\in\Re^{d_{w}+d_{\phi}}.

\displaystyle\sup_{j\in[J],\tau\in\Upsilon}||r_{n}\mathbb{P}_{n,j}\tilde{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{n}(\tau),\gamma_{n}(\tau),0)-\mathcal{Z}_{j}(\tau)||_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

Remark 3.3
.

Assumption

(i) introduces Neyman orthogonality between the estimators of the coefficients of the endogenous and control variables, which is the key to connecting the gradient bootstrap with the randomization test with sign changes. This is in line with the results in
Canay
et al. (2021)
and
Wang and
Zhang (2024)
for their residual-based bootstrap procedures. In Section

below, we propose both parametric and nonparametric approaches to construct IVs that satisfy Assumption

Remark 3.4
.

Consistently estimating

in Assumption

(ii) requires further assumptions on the within-cluster dependence structure and potential tuning parameters; see, for example,
Yoon and Galvao (2020)
and
Galvao and Yoon (2023)
. Instead, the key benefit of our bootstrap inference approach
is that it is fully agnostic about the expression of the covariance matrices.

Remark 3.5
.

We notice that (

Example 3

(Network Dependence)
.

Suppose we observe
n

\ell\in[n]

\displaystyle y_{\ell}=\delta_{0}(U_{\ell})+\beta(U_{\ell})\frac{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}:A_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=1}y_{\ell^{\prime}}}{n_{\ell}}+\delta_{1}(U_{\ell})B_{\ell}+\delta_{2}(U_{\ell})\frac{\sum_{\ell^{\prime}:A_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=1}B_{\ell^{\prime}}}{n_{\ell}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(y_{\ell}\leq X_{\ell}\beta(\tau)+W_{\ell}^{\top}\gamma(\tau)|\{B_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in[n]}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(U_{\ell}\leq\tau|\{B_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in[n]}\right)=\tau.

Example 4

(Factor Structure)
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, our asymptotic framework, which treats the number of clusters as fixed, follows previous studies such as IM, BCH, and CRS. We emphasize that the main restriction of such an asymptotic framework is it requires the within-cluster dependence to be sufficiently weak for some CLT to hold within each cluster (as illustrated in Examples
1
-
3
).
For example, as pointed out by
MacKinnon
et al. (2023, Sections 3.1, 3.2, and 4.2)
, this requirement rules out the case where the error follows a factor structure, i.e., for
\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)=y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}\beta_{n}(\tau)-W_{i,j}^{\top}\gamma_{n}(\tau)
in the current IVQR model,

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)=\lambda_{i,j}(\tau)f^{s}_{j}(\tau)+u_{i,j}(\tau),

(14)

where
u_{i,j}(\cdot)
denotes the idiosyncratic error,
f^{s}_{j}(\cdot)
denotes the cluster-wide shock, and
\lambda_{i,j}(\cdot)
is the factor loading. By contrast, such a dependence structure can be handled under the asymptotic framework that lets the number of clusters
J
diverge to infinity. However, we conjecture that our gradient wild bootstrap procedure is also valid under the alternative asymptotic framework with a large number of small clusters (more discussions are provided in Remark
3.12
).

Example 5

(Cluster Fixed Effects)
.

If cluster fixed effects exist in the IVQR model, we can add cluster dummies into the control variables
W
. In our setting, the number of clusters is fixed so that even
W
includes cluster dummies, it still has a fixed dimension, and all our assumptions can still hold. We also note that in the case with linear regressions, adding cluster dummies is equivalent to first projecting out the fixed effects so that
(y_{i,j},X_{i,j},W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})
is expressed as deviations from cluster means, which is also recommended by
Djogbenou
et al. (2019, Section 2.2)
and
MacKinnon
et al. (2023, Section 3.2)
. However, we emphasize that in the current setting with quantile regressions, cluster-level demeaning and adding cluster dummies are not equivalent to each other.

Example 6

(Heterogeneous IV Strength Across Clusters)
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, we allow for cluster-level heterogeneity with regard to IV strength. For instance, we can consider the following first-stage regression:

\displaystyle X_{i,j}=Z_{i,j}^{\top}\Pi_{z,j,n}+W_{i,j}^{\top}\Pi_{w,j,n}+E_{i,j},

Then, our model (
1
) allows for both
\Pi_{z,j,n}
and
\Pi_{w,j,n}
to vary across clusters.
In this case, the Jacobian
Q_{\Phi,X,j}(\tau)
for the
j
-th cluster takes the form of

\displaystyle Q_{\Phi,X,j}(\tau)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n,j}f_{\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)}(0|W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})\Phi_{i,j}(\tau)Z_{i,j}^{\top}\Pi_{z,j,n}V_{i,j}(\tau).

In particular, our bootstrap Wald tests (i.e.,
T_{n}
and
T_{CR,n}
) are still valid even when
\Pi_{z,j,n}
, and thus,
Q_{\Phi,X,j}(\tau)
decay to or are zero for some of the clusters. Our bootstrap AR tests (i.e.,
AR_{n}
and
AR_{CR,n}
) control asymptotic size even when
\Pi_{z,j,n}
decay to or are zero for all clusters.

Example 7

(Heterogeneous Slope for the Endogenous Variable)
.

Similar to
Canay
et al. (2021, Example 2)
, we cannot allow for
\beta_{n}(\tau)
in (
1
) to be heterogeneous across clusters, denoted as
(\beta_{n,j}(\tau))_{j\in[J]}
. More specifically, in this case, the IVQR estimator based on the full sample will estimate

(\beta_{n,j}(\tau))_{j\in[J]}
. Then, in Assumption

, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{n}(\tau),\gamma_{n}(\tau),0)=(\tau-1\{X_{i,j}(\beta_{j}(\tau)-\tilde{\beta}(\tau))+\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)\leq 0\})\Phi_{i,j}(\tau)V_{i,j}(\tau),

so that Assumption

Example 8

(Cluster-level Endogenous Variable)
.

If
X_{i,j}
is a cluster-level variable (say,
X_{j}
), then the within-cluster limiting Jacobian

may be random and potentially correlated with the within-cluster score component

(as

3.3

In Assumption
3
(i) above, we require the IVs to satisfy the following condition: for

\displaystyle Q_{W,\Phi,j}(\tau)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n,j}f_{\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)}(0|W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})W_{i,j}\Phi_{i,j}^{\top}(\tau)V_{i,j}(\tau)=0,

where
\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)=y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}\beta_{n}(\tau)-W_{i,j}^{\top}\gamma_{n}(\tau)
and

is the conditional PDF of
\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)
given
(W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})
and evaluated at
0

Remark 3.6

(Parametric Approach)
.

Suppose

such that

is a finite dimensional parameter,

is a known

is a random shock such that

\hat{\lambda}
be the regression coefficient of

in the linear (first-stage) regression of

and
W_{i,j}
using the full sample and

\displaystyle\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}=\hat{\lambda}^{\top}(Z_{i,j}-G(W_{i,j},\hat{\pi})),

where
\hat{\pi}
is a consistent estimator of
\pi
.
8
8

8

When

as the coefficient of

in the linear regression of

W_{i,j}
using observations in the full sample.
We can see that, if

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n,j}f_{\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)}(0|W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})W_{i,j}\Phi_{i,j}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n,j}f_{\varepsilon_{i,j}(\tau)}(0|W_{i,j},Z_{i,j})W_{i,j}U_{i,j}^{\top}\lambda

The key benefit of this approach is that it does not require nonparametric estimation of the conditional density and, thus, the tuning parameters. The way to convert the potentially multi-dimensional IVs into one via the least squares estimator

Remark 3.7

\displaystyle\hat{\underline{\varepsilon}}_{i,j}(\tau)=Y_{i,j}-X_{i,j}\beta_{0}(\tau)-W_{i,j}^{\top}\hat{\gamma}(\beta_{0}(\tau),\tau),

Remark 3.10

To examine the performance of the gradient wild bootstrap inference for IVQR, we consider two data-generating processes (DGPs). The first DGP is inspired by those designed by

4.1
Simulation Designs

DGP 1.
Let
a_{k,j}=(a_{k,1,j},\cdots,a_{k,n_{j},j})^{\top}
for
k=1,\cdots,d_{z}
, where
d_{z}
is the dimension of
Z
, and
u_{j}=(u_{l,1,j},\cdots,u_{l,n_{j},j})^{\top}
for
l=1,2
. Then,
(a_{1,j},\cdots,a_{d_{z},j},u_{1,j},u_{2,j})
are independent, and each of them is independent across
j\in[J]
and follows an
n_{j}\times 1
multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and covariance
\Sigma(\rho_{j})
, where
\Sigma(\rho_{j})
is an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
toeplitz matrix with coefficient
\rho_{j}
and
\rho_{j}=0.2+0.5j/J
for
j\in[J]
. We define the original instruments as

\displaystyle Z_{k,i,j}=1\{F_{N}(a_{k,i,j})>0.5\},\hskip 11.74988pt\forall k\in[d_{z}],

where
F_{N}(\cdot)
is the standard normal CDF. Then, the endogenous variable
X_{i,j}
is generated as

\displaystyle X_{i,j}=1\left\{0.1+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{z}}\Pi_{k,j}(F_{N}(a_{k,i,j})-0.5)+0.5(F_{N}(u_{i,j})-0.5)>0\right\},

where
\Pi_{k,j}
determines the identification strength. To allow for first-stage heterogeneity in the identification strength across clusters, for all
k\in[d_{z}]
, we let

\displaystyle\Pi_{k,j}=\begin{cases}&\pi\hskip 11.74988pt\text{if}\hskip 11.74988pt1\leq j\leq J/3\\
&0\hskip 11.74988pt\text{if}\hskip 11.74988ptJ/3<j\leq 2J/3\\
&2\pi\hskip 11.74988pt\text{if}\hskip 11.74988pt2J/3<j\leq J\end{cases},

where
\pi\in(1,1/2,1/4)
.

The outcome
Y_{i,j}
is generated as

\displaystyle Y_{i,j}=X_{i,j}\beta(u_{1,i,j})+\underline{W}_{i,j}\gamma+\sqrt{0.1}u_{i,j},

where
\underline{W}_{i,j}
is distributed following
\frac{1}{2}\chi^{2}_{1}
and independent of
(a_{1,j},\cdots,a_{d_{z},j},u_{1j},u_{2j})
, and
W_{i,j}=(1,\underline{W}_{i,j})^{\top}
. We set
\beta(u)=1+F_{N}(u)
,
\gamma=0
, and the total number of observations
n=500
. The correlation between
X_{i,j}
and
u_{i,j}
is about 0.77, indicating the endogeneity level.

To allow for unbalanced clusters, we follow
Djogbenou
et al. (2019)
and
MacKinnon (2023)
and set the cluster sizes as

\displaystyle n_{j}=\left[n\frac{\exp(r\cdot j/J)}{\sum_{j\in[J]}\exp(r\cdot j/J)}\right],\;\;\text{for}\;\;j=1,...,J-1,

and
n_{J}=n-\sum_{j\in[j]}n_{j}
.
We let
r=4
when
J=9
and
J=18
to generate substantial heterogeneity in cluster sizes. The parameter
r
corresponds to the heterogeneity parameter considered in the simulations of
Djogbenou
et al. (2019)
and
MacKinnon
et al. (2023)
, and
r=4
is the most heterogeneous setting considered in those papers.
When
J=9
, the cluster sizes are
(5,8,12,19,30,48,75,117,186)
. When
J=18
, the resulting cluster sizes are
(2,2,3,4,5,7,8,10,13,17,21,26,33,41,52,65,81,110)
. We test the following hypothesis for
d_{z}\in\{1,3\}
and
\tau\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9\}
:

\displaystyle H_{0}:\beta(\tau)=1+\tau\hskip 11.74988ptv.s.\hskip 11.74988ptH_{1}:\beta(\tau)=0.5+\tau.

DGP 2.
We consider the linear-in-mean social interaction model detailed in Example
3
. Specifically, following
Leung (2023)
, we generate the network
\mathcal{A}
with
n=500
nodes as
\mathcal{A}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=1\{||\eta_{\ell}-\eta_{\ell^{\prime}}||_{2}\geq(7/(\pi n))^{1/2}\}
, where
\eta_{\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\text{Uniform}[0,1]^{2}
.

Then, we generate
B_{\ell}
in (
13
), the
\ell
-th node’s background characteristic, as follows:

\displaystyle B_{\ell}=\begin{cases}0&\text{ with probability $1/2$}\\
\exp(-\log(2)+(\log(4))^{1/2}\varepsilon_{\ell}^{\prime})&\text{ with probability $1/2$}\end{cases},

where
\varepsilon_{\ell}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then, the control variables
W
and IV
Z
can be constructed as in Example
3
.

Further denote
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
a normalized adjacency matrix with a typical entry
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}=\mathcal{A}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}/d_{\ell}
, where
d_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell}{\mathcal{A}}_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}
is the degree of node
\ell
. Then, we generate the outcome variable as (
13
) in which
U=\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in[n]}
is a sequence of i.i.d. uniform (0,1) random variables and

\displaystyle y=\left(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\text{diag}(\beta(U))\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\right)^{-1}\left(\delta_{0}(U)+\text{diag}(\delta_{1}(U))B+\text{diag}(\delta_{2}(U))\tilde{\mathcal{A}}B\right),

where
\text{diag}(v)
for a
n\times 1
vector
v
denotes an
n\times n
diagonal matrix with
v
as the diagonal,
y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})^{\top}
,
B=(B_{1},\cdots,B_{n})^{\top}
,
\beta(U)=(\beta(U_{1}),\cdots,\beta(U_{n}))^{\top}
, and
(\delta_{0}(U),\delta_{1}(U),\delta_{2}(U))
are similarly defined. In addition, we set
\delta_{0}(u)=0.7683+0.25(u-0.5)
,
\beta(u)=0.4666+0.2(u-0.5)
,
\delta_{1}(u)=0.0834+0.1(u-0.5)
, and
\delta_{2}(u)=0.1507+0.2(u-0.5)
. For comparison, the values
(\delta_{0}(0.5),\beta(0.5),\delta_{1}(0.5),\delta_{2}(0.5))=(0.7683,0.4666,0.0834,0.1507)
are set according to the calibration study by
Bramoullé et al. (2009)
. Given the outcome variable, we can set the endogenous variable as
X=\tilde{\mathcal{A}}y
, where
X=(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n})^{\top}
. Finally, we note that (
1
) holds because coefficients
(\delta_{0}(\cdot),\beta(\cdot))
are monotone increasing and their corresponding regressors are positive and
\{U_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in[n]}\perp\!\!\!\perp\{B_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in[n]}
. The correlation between
X_{\ell}
and
U_{\ell}
is about 0.16, which indicates the endogeneity level. As discussed in Example
3
, we let
W=(\iota_{n},B,\tilde{A}B)
and
Z=\tilde{A}^{2}B
.

We follow the classification procedure proposed by
Leung (2023)
to obtain the clusters.

1.

Input: a positive integer
L
and network
\mathcal{A}
.

2.

Compute all separated components (no links between two components) of the network denoted as
\{\mathcal{V}_{h}\}_{h\in\{0\}\cup[H]}
, where
\{\mathcal{V}_{h}\}_{h\in\{0\}\cup[H]}
is a partition of
[n]
(n vertexes) and they are sorted in ascending order according to their sizes. We keep all the components whose sizes are greater than 5. Denote the number of components left as
L^{\prime}+1
for some
L^{\prime}\geq 0
.

3.

Suppose the biggest component
\mathcal{V}_{0}
has size
\tilde{n}_{0}
. By permuting labels, we suppose
\mathcal{V}_{0}=[\tilde{n}_{0}]
and denote its adjacency matrix as
\mathcal{A}_{0}
. Then, we compute the graph Laplacian as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}=\mathbb{I}_{\tilde{n}_{0}}-D_{0}^{-1/2}\mathcal{A}_{0}D_{0}^{-1/2},

where
D_{0}=\text{diag}(\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}_{0}]}A_{0,1,i},\cdots,\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}_{0}]}A_{0,\tilde{n}_{0},i})
is an
\tilde{n}_{0}\times\tilde{n}_{0}
diagonal matrix of degrees.

4.

Obtain the top
L
eigenvector matrix of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
corresponding to its
L
largest eigenvalues and denote it as
V=[V_{1}^{\top},\cdots,V_{\tilde{n}_{0}}^{\top}]^{\top},

where
V_{i}\in\Re^{L}
for
i\in[\tilde{n}_{0}]
.

5.

Apply the k-means algorithm to
V
and divide
\mathcal{V}_{0}=[\tilde{n}_{0}]
into
L
groups, denoted as
\mathcal{V}_{0,1},\cdots,\mathcal{V}_{0,L}
.

6.

Output: we obtain
J=L+L^{\prime}
clusters
(\mathcal{V}_{0,1},\cdots,\mathcal{V}_{0,L},\mathcal{V}_{1},\cdots,\mathcal{V}_{L^{\prime}})
.

We let
L
be
(10,20)
. The number of clusters (
J
) depends on
L^{\prime}
, which varies across simulation replications. Note that different from DGP 1, there is no cluster heterogeneity in the identification strength under DGP 2.
We test the following hypothesis for
\tau\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9\}
:

\displaystyle H_{0}:\beta(\tau)=0.4666+0.2(\tau-0.5)\hskip 11.74988ptv.s.\hskip 11.74988ptH_{1}:\beta(\tau)=1.2166+0.2(\tau-0.5).

4.2
Inference Procedure

We compare the performance of our three bootstrap inference methods
T_{CR,n}
,
T_{n}
, and
AR_{n}
(denoted as T_CR, T, and AR in Tables
1
-
5
) with the conventional asymptotic Wald inference based on CRVE and two alternative methods based on cluster-level IVQR estimators available in the literature.

1.

T_STD: This inference method is based on the same test statistic as
T_{CR,n}
but with the conventional normal critical value.

2.

IM: This inference method is proposed by
Ibragimov and
Müller (2010)
, which compares their group-based
t
-test statistic with the critical value of a
t
-distribution with
J-1
degrees of freedom. The test statistic is constructed by separately running IVQR using the samples in each cluster.

3.

CRS: The approximate randomization inference method proposed by
Canay
et al. (2017)
, which compares IM’s group
t
-test statistic with the critical value of the sign changes-based randomization distribution of the statistic.

We construct the instrument using the nonparametric approach outlined in Remark
3.7
. The tuning parameters are set based on the rule-of-thumb provided in Remark
3.7
. Because this approach eventually produces a scalar
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)
regardless of the dimension of the original IVs
Z_{i,j}
, our
AR_{n}
and
AR_{CR,n}
tests are numerically equivalent. Therefore, we only report the performance of
AR_{n}
. In addition, because we focus on inference point-wise in
\tau
and
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}(\tau)
is a scalar, the choices of
\hat{A}_{1}(\tau)
in the estimation of
\hat{\beta}(\tau)
,
\hat{A}_{2}(\tau)
in the Wald inference based on
T_{n}(\tau)
,
\hat{G}(\tau)
in the Wald inference based on
T_{CR,n}(\tau)
, and
\hat{A}_{3}(\tau)
in the AR inference based on
AR_{n}(\tau)
will not affect the performance of corresponding inference methods, and are set to 1 without loss of generality.

Several key observations emerge from the results. First, T_CR, T, and AR effectively control size across various settings, while T_STD, IM, and CRS have size distortions, particularly in DGP 1, which includes both weak and strong IV clusters (i.e., there exists substantial cluster heterogeneity in the IV strength under DGP 1). The size distortions of T_STD, IM, and CRS also typically increase when
\pi
becomes small in Tables
1
-
4
.
Second, for both DGPs, T_CR demonstrates greater power than T across various settings, aligning with our theoretical expectations.
Third, we observe that T_STD typically has larger size distortion when the number of clusters is small, while the size distortions of IM and CRS tend to increase with the number of clusters.
Fourth, the power of CRS and IM is comparable, and in DGP 2, their power is similar to that of T_CR, although they display slightly higher size distortion.
Overall, T_CR has the best size and power performance among the six methods, and is thus our recommended inference procedure.

Empirical Applications

In an influential study,
Autor
et al. (2013)
analyzes the effect of rising Chinese import competition on wages and employment in US local labor markets between 1990 and 2007, when the share of total US spending on Chinese goods increased substantially from 0.6% to 4.6%. In this section,
we further analyze the region-wise distributional effects of such import exposure by applying IVQR and the proposed gradient wild bootstrap procedures to the Census Bureau-designated South region with 16 states and total number of observations equal to 578.
12
12

12

We focus on the South region because it has the highest IV strength.

For the IVQR model, we let the outcome variable
(y_{i,j})
denote the decadal change in the average individual log weekly wage in a given CZ.
The endogenous variable
(X_{i,j})
is the change in Chinese import exposure per worker in a CZ, which is instrumented by
(Z_{i,j})
Chinese import growth in other high-income countries.
13
13

13

See Sections I.B and III.A in
Autor
et al. (2013)
for a detailed definition of these variables.
We follow the nonparametric approach in Remark
3.7
to construct
\hat{\Phi}_{i,j}
used in IVQR.
In addition, the exogenous variables
(W_{i,j})
include the characteristic variables of commuting zones (CZs) and decade specified in
Autor
et al. (2013)
as well as state fixed effects.
Our IV quantile regressions are based on the CZ samples in the South region, and the samples are clustered at the state level, following
Autor
et al. (2013)
.
Besides the results for the full sample, we also report those for male and female samples separately.

The main results are given in Table
6
. Specifically, for
\tau\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.9\}
, we report the point IVQR estimate and the 90% bootstrap confidence sets (CSs) constructed by inverting the corresponding
AR_{n}
,
T_{n}
, and
T_{CR,n}
-based tests. The computation of the bootstrap CSs was conducted over the parameter space
[-3,1]
with a step size of 0.01, and the number of bootstrap draws is set at 300 for each step. We can draw three key observations from the results in Table

. First, the impact of Chinese imports on wages shows distributional heterogeneity. Specifically, the three types of bootstrap CSs reveal that the effects are relatively significant at the high quantiles, followed by the median quantile, but not at the lower quantiles, across the full, male, and female samples. Second, the
T_{CR,n}
-based CSs are generally shorter than those of
AR_{n}
and
T_{n}

T_{CR,n}

In addition, we observe that the effects of Chinese imports are relatively more substantial for the male samples.

and

) in the main text and
\omega=(0_{d_{\phi}\times d_{w}},\mathbb{I}_{d_{\phi}})
.

is a block matrix, so is

, and thus

\displaystyle\omega Q_{\Psi,\Psi}^{-1}(\tau)=(0_{d_{\phi}\times d_{w}},Q^{-1}_{\Phi,\Phi}(\tau)).

Then, we have

\displaystyle Q_{\Psi,X}^{\top}(\tau)Q_{\Psi,\Psi}^{-1}(\tau)\omega^{\top}A_{1}(\tau)\omega Q_{\Psi,\Psi}^{-1}(\tau)Q_{\Psi,X}(\tau)=Q_{\Phi,X}^{\top}(\tau)Q_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)A_{1}(\tau)Q_{\Phi,\Phi}^{-1}(\tau)Q_{\Phi,X}(\tau)

\displaystyle\Gamma(\tau)f_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{n}(\tau),\gamma_{n}(\tau),0)=\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau)\tilde{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{n}(\tau),\gamma_{n}(\tau),0).

(25)

Therefore, by Lemma
G.2
, we have

\displaystyle r_{n}\left(\hat{\beta}(\tau)-\beta_{n}(\tau)\right)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau)\left[\sum_{j\in[J]}\xi_{j}r_{n}(\mathbb{P}_{n,j}-\overline{\mathbb{P}}_{n,j})\tilde{f}_{\tau}(D_{i,j},\beta_{n}(\tau),\gamma_{n}(\tau),0)\right]+o_{p}(1),

where the

term holds uniformly over
\tau\in\Upsilon
.

Then we have

\displaystyle r_{n}(\hat{\beta}(\tau)-\beta_{0}(\tau))

\displaystyle=r_{n}\left(\hat{\beta}(\tau)-\beta_{n}(\tau)\right)+\mu_{\beta}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau)\left[\sum_{j\in[J]}\xi_{j}\mathcal{Z}_{j}(\tau)\right]+\mu_{\beta}(\tau)+o_{p}(1),

where the
o_{p}(1)
term holds uniformly over
\tau\in\Upsilon
.

By Lemma
G.4
, we have

\displaystyle r_{n}(\hat{\beta}_{g}^{*}(\tau)-\hat{\beta}(\tau))=\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau)\left[\sum_{j\in[J]}g_{j}\xi_{j}\mathcal{Z}_{j}(\tau)\right]+\overline{a}_{g}^{*}(\tau)\mu_{\beta}(\tau)+o_{p}(1),

(26)

where
o_{p}(1)
term holds uniformly over
\tau\in\Upsilon
,
\overline{a}_{g}^{*}(\tau)=\sum_{j\in[J]}\xi_{j}g_{j}a_{j}(\tau)

\displaystyle a_{j}(\tau)=\Gamma(\tau)Q_{\Psi,X,j}(\tau)=\tilde{\Gamma}(\tau)Q_{\Phi,X,j}(\tau).

Let

\displaystyle\{r_{n}T_{n},\{r_{n}T_{n}^{*}(g)\}_{g\in\textbf{G}}\}\rightsquigarrow(T_{\infty}(\iota_{J}),\{T_{\infty}(g)\}_{g\in\textbf{G}}),

\displaystyle(T_{n}^{*})^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq(T_{n}^{*})^{(|\textbf{G}|)}\hskip 11.74988pt\text{and}\hskip 11.74988pt(T_{\infty})^{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq(T_{\infty})^{|\textbf{G}|}.

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{n}(1-\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle p}}{{\longrightarrow}}(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\{T_{n}\geq\hat{c}_{n}(1-\alpha)\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{T_{\infty}(\iota_{J})\geq(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}\right\}\leq\alpha+\frac{1}{2^{J-1}},

(

Wellner, 1996
)
, Theorem 1.3.4(iii)), and the third inequality is due to the properties of randomization tests (see, e.g.,
(
Lehmann and
Romano, 2006
)
, Theorem 15.2.1) and the facts that the distribution of

is invariant w.r.t.
g

T_{\infty}(g)=T_{\infty}(-g)
, and
T_{\infty}(g)\neq T_{\infty}(g^{\prime})
if

. Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\{T_{n}>\hat{c}_{n}(1-\alpha)\}=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\{T_{n}>(T_{n}^{*})^{(k^{*})}\}\geq\mathbb{P}\left\{T_{\infty}(\iota_{J})>(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}\right\}\geq\alpha-\frac{1}{2^{J-1}}.

T_{\infty}(g)=T_{\infty}(-g)
, and

. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in\textbf{G}}1\{T_{\infty}(g)\leq(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}\}\leq k^{*}+1.

|\textbf{G}|=2^{J}
, we have

\displaystyle|\textbf{G}|\mathbb{E}1\{T_{\infty}(\iota_{J})>(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\sum_{g\in\textbf{G}}1\{T_{\infty}(g)>(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}\}

\displaystyle=|\textbf{G}|-\mathbb{E}\sum_{g\in\textbf{G}}1\{T_{\infty}(g)\leq(T_{\infty})^{(k^{*})}\}\geq|\textbf{G}|-(k^{*}+1)

\displaystyle\geq\lfloor|\textbf{G}|\alpha\rfloor-1\geq|\textbf{G}|\alpha-2,

\mathcal{H}_{1,n}

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\{T_{n}>\hat{c}_{n}(1-\alpha)\}

\displaystyle\max_{g\in\textbf{G}_{s}}T_{\infty}(g)\leq\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon,g\in\textbf{G}_{s}}|\sum_{j\in[J]}g_{j}\xi_{j}a_{j}(\tau)|\sup_{\tau\in\Upsilon}||\mu_{\beta}(\tau)||_{A_{2}(\tau)}+O_{p}(1).

This concludes the proof.
