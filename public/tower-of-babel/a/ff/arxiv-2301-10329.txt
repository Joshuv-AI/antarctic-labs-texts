Abstract




Superstrata microstate geometries furnish some of the most successful laboratories, to date, for probing black hole microstructure in a geometric setting. This paper extends the
(1,m,n)
family of superstrata, to allow for flat R
1,5
asymptotics. Previous constructions utilized the decoupling regime, where the geometry is asymptotically AdS
{}_{3}\times
S
3

Brief comments are made on the additional complexity introduced by the flat space coupling, how this obscures holomorphicity and breaks the consistent truncation to which the decoupled solutions belong. Holomorphicity and consistent truncation were key simplifications assisting previous studies of the
(1,m,n)
superstrata, undertaken in the decoupling regime. Further, these results open a window for future projects to determine how previous analysis on the decoupled geometries extend or are modified once flat space asymptotics are imposed. Since our universe is almost flat on cosmological scales, this represents progress towards more phenomenologically relevant microstate geometries. This work can be considered a continuation of that in

These geometries provide families of smooth supergravity solutions, that can arbitrarily closely approximate the geometry of a black hole outside the horizon, before capping off smoothly at the would be horizon scale in a multitude of ways. \displaystyle ds_{6}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}}\,(dv+\beta)\big(du+\omega+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{F}\,(dv+\beta)\big)+\sqrt{\mathcal{P}}\,ds_{4}^{2}(\mathcal{B})\,.

BPS equations

The BPS-equations can be arranged and solved in the

•

\displaystyle d_{4}\beta=*_{4}d_{4}\beta\,.

(2.10)

•

Find

\displaystyle*_{4}\mathcal{D}\dot{Z}_{I}=-\eta_{IJ}\mathcal{D}\Theta^{J}\,,\qquad\mathcal{D}*_{4}\mathcal{D}Z_{I}=\eta_{IJ}\Theta^{J}\wedge d\beta\,,\qquad\Theta^{I}=*_{4}\Theta^{I}\,.

(2.11)

•

Find

\displaystyle(1+*_{4})\mathcal{D}\omega+\mathcal{F}\,d_{4}\beta

\displaystyle=Z_{I}\Theta^{I}\,,

\displaystyle*_{4}\mathcal{D}*_{4}\left(\dot{\omega}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{F}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{4}\eta_{IJ}\left[4\ddot{Z}^{I}Z^{J}+2\dot{Z}^{I}\dot{Z}^{J}-*_{4}\left(\Theta^{I}\wedge\Theta^{J}\right)\right]\,.

6
Discussion and conclusions

Having constructed the full
(1,m,n)
superstrata in Section
3
, considered the decoupled AdS limit in Section
4
and illustrated various limits of the asymptotically flat geometries in Section
5
, it is now worth revisiting the two main research questions of this paper:

•

What features of microstate geometries, in the decoupling limit, are altered or retained when more realistic asymptotics are imposed?

•

To what extent are the currently accepted results or intuition, in the microstate geometry program, artefacts of working in the decoupling limit?

To first order, an answer comes from comparing
(\mathcal{F},\omega)
, key data in the metric expansion (
2.6
), between the full solution (
3.22
)-(
3.23
) and the decoupled solution, given by setting
\epsilon_{1,2}=0
. One sees immediately that the “addition modes” in
\omega
, make the full solution’s geometry vastly more involved than the decoupled solution’s geometry, especially when many more modes are considered in the superposition.

To second, the explicit implications this additional geometric structure should be explored. Some straightforward implications demonstrated in this paper include:

•

The full solution is no longer simply written in terms of the holomorphic formalism.

•

The full solution does not belong to the consistent truncation to which the decoupled solution does.

•

Changing the asymptotics has effects on the geometry, even deep within the long throat.

Beyond these straightforward observations, it is now possible to revisit many of the calculations previously performed in the decoupling limit, to determine the corrections due to the flat space coupling. We leave such intriguing analysis to future projects.

6.1
Holomorphic considerations

The holomorphic formalism traces its origins to the coiffuring relations, required to ensure regularity of the decoupled solution. However, coupling the solutions to flat space alters the coiffuring relations, see (
3.33
)-(
3.34
). So it is not obvious that the holomorphic formalism survives the transition. The simplest way to probe this is by considering (
3.16
):

Z_{1}=C_{1}+\frac{Q_{1}}{\Sigma}+\frac{R_{y}}{2Q_{5}}\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}}\left(\frac{b_{1,m_{1},n_{1}}b_{1,m_{2},n_{2}}}{1+\epsilon_{1,2}}\right)z^{(o)}_{2,m_{1}+m_{2},n_{1}+n_{2}}\,,

(6.1)

where (
3.15
):

\epsilon_{i,j}=\frac{C_{1}a^{2}}{2Q_{5}}\left(m_{i}+m_{j}+n_{i}+n_{j}\right)\,.

(6.2)

When trying to reformulate this in the complex variables, one notes that:

z^{(o)}_{2,m,n}=\chi^{2}\mu^{m}\xi^{n}+\bar{\chi}^{2}\bar{\mu}^{m}\bar{\xi}^{n}\,,

(6.3)

but then the
\epsilon_{1,2}
in (
6.1
) becomes very cumbersome. This can not be generated cleanly from some holomorphic functions.

On the other hand, in the decoupling limit,
Z_{1}
does has the very simple holomorphic form (
4.7
):

Z_{1}=\frac{Q_{1}}{\Sigma}+\frac{R_{y}^{2}}{4Q_{5}\Sigma}(F^{2}+\bar{F}^{2})\,,

(6.4)

with (
4.6
):

F(\chi,\mu,\xi)\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{1}\sum_{m=0}^{1}\sum_{n}b_{k,m,n}\chi^{k}\mu^{m}\xi^{n}\,.

(6.5)

Thus, it appears that the utility of the holomorphic formalism identified in
[
11
]
, was a consequence of working in the decoupling regime. Rather than reflecting an underlying structure, such as supersymmetry.

6.2
Consistent truncation considerations

From the expansion given of the asymptotically flat
(1,m,n)
solution in the long BTZ throat region of Section
5.3
, it is clear that the full solution can not belong to a consistent truncation on a three sphere. To see this, consider the external three dimensional part of the metric (
5.21
):

\displaystyle ds_{3,\text{external}}^{2}\equiv\left(\frac{\Delta}{\det m_{AB}}\right)^{1/2}ds_{3}^{2}+ds^{2}_{3,C_{1}}\,.

(6.6)

Considering the form of
ds_{3,C_{1}}^{2}
in (
5.15
), it is clear that the dependence on the spherical coordinates,
(\theta,\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})
, can not be factored out into a simple “warp factor.” Thus the dynamics of the sphere can not couple in the requisite way, to the external part of the geometry, for a consistent truncation to be valid.

Although this removes one of the most powerful supergravity tools for simplifying analysis, it also opens a window into uncovering new physics. For instance, it shows that the consistent truncation is missing some supergravity degrees of freedom, necessary to couple to flat space. Can these degrees of freedom be identified in some convenient manner? It is conceivable that the full
(1,m,n)
solution presented herein could be used to answer such a question.

6.3
UV/IR mixing considerations

An intriguing feature of quantum gravity is the possibility for subtle UV/IR mixing effects. Wherein the short distance, UV physics, may qualitatively alter the long distance, IR physics. A result that runs counter to the effectiveness of re-normalization in quantum field theories, where the UV degrees of freedom can be discarded.

In the context of microstate geometries, the UV region corresponds to the asymptotic region, while the IR region corresponds to the region deep down the long throat, including the cap. With this in mind, consider the conceptual processes of producing asymptotically flat superstrata:

1.

Change UV structure, from AdS to flat asymptotics.

2.

Regularity of superstrata requires altered coiffuring relations.

3.

Altered coiffuring leads to new structure deep down the long throat and at the cap.

Thus, the additional structure, relative to the decoupled solutions, presented in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
. Might be suitably interpreted as artifacts of UV/IR mixing in the microstate geometry program.

Appendix A
Constructing the solutions

This appendix gives details on how the full
(1,m,n)
asymptotically flat solutions, of Section
3.2
, were generated. Schematically it involves:

•

Developing an ansatz for the first BPS layer data, with enough modes to allow for regularity of the final solution.

•

Decomposing the second layer sources into “basic” parts.

•

Identifying solutions to the basic second layer sources that already appear in the literature.

•

Solving the second layer for the basic sources that don’t already appear in the literature.

•

Utilizing the semi-linear property of the BPS equations to superpose the basic solutions.

•

Tuning the constants in the first layer ansatz such that the full solution is regular, a process known as
coiffuring
.

In practice, for some basic sources, the last three points can only be completed simultaneously. This is in contrast to the solutions purely in the decoupled limit, where each step can be completed independently, explicitly.
