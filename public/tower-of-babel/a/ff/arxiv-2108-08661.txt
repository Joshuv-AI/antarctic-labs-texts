Asymptotic behaviour of the first positions of uniform parking functions
Etienne Bellin CMAP - Ecole Polytechnique

Abstract
In this paper we study the asymptotic behavior of a random uniform parking function π n \pi_{n} of size n n . We show that the first k n k_{n} places π n ​ ( 1 ) , … , π n ​ ( k n ) \pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k_{n}) of π n \pi_{n} are asymptotically i.i.d. and uniform on { 1 , 2 , … , n } \{1,2,\dots,n\} , for the total variation distance when k n = o ⁡ ( n ) k_{n}=o(\sqrt{n}) , and for the Kolmogorov distance when k n = o ⁡ ( n ) k_{n}=o(n) , improving results of Diaconis & Hicks. Moreover we give bounds for the rate of convergence, as well as limit theorems for some statistics like the sum or the maximum of the first k n k_{n} parking places. The main tool is a reformulation using conditioned random walks.

Introduction

A
parking function
of size
n
is a function
\pi_{n}:\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket\to\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
such that, if
\pi_{n}^{\prime}(1)\leq\dots\leq\pi_{n}^{\prime}(n)
is the nondecreasing rearrangement of
(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(n))
, then
\pi_{n}^{\prime}(i)\leq i
for all
i
. Konheim and Weiss
[
KW66
]
first introduced parking functions, in a context of information storing, to study hashing functions and they have shown that there are
(n+1)^{n-1}
parking functions of size
n
. Since then, parking functions
became a subject of interest in the fields of combinatorics, probability, group theory and computer science. More precisely, parking functions are linked to the enumerative theory of trees and forests
[
CM01
]
, to coalescent processes
[
CL02
]

[
BM16
]
, to the analysis of set partitions
[
Sta97
]
, hyperplane arrangements
[
Sta98
]

[
Shi86
]
, polytopes
[
SP02
]

[
CP10
]
and sandpile groups
[
CR00
]
. Finally, the study of probabilistic properties of parking functions has recently attracted some interest
[
DH17
]

[
KY21
]

[
Yin21
]
. We refer to
[
Yan86
]
for an extensive survey. Our initial interest for parking functions comes from the study of minimal factorisations of cycles
[
Bia02
]
.

For all
n\geq 1
consider a random parking function
(\pi_{n}(i))_{1\leq i\leq n}
chosen uniformly among all the
(n+1)^{n-1}
possible parking functions of size
n
. For all
1\leq k\leq n
denote by

d_{TV}(k,n):=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}^{n}\left|\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)=i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)=i_{k})-\frac{1}{n^{k}}\right|

(1)

the total variation distance between
(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k))
and
(U_{n}(1),\dots,U_{n}(k))
where
(U_{n}(i))_{1\leq i\leq n}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed in
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. Diaconis and Hicks
[
DH17
, Corollary 6]
have shown that
d_{TV}(1,n)
tends to 0 as
n
tends to infinity and conjectured that for any fixed
k
,
d_{TV}(k,n)
should be a
O(k/\sqrt{n})
. In the same paper the authors studied the Kolmogorov distance

d_{K}(k,n):=\max_{1\leq i_{1}\dots i_{k}\leq n}\left|\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)\leq i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)\leq i_{k})-\frac{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}{n^{k}}\right|

(2)

and have shown that
[
DH17
, Theorem 3]
for
1\leq k\leq n
:

d_{K}(k,n)=O\left(k\sqrt{\frac{\log{n}}{n}}+\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right).

They also discuss the growth threshold of
k
at which
d_{K}
doesn’t converge towards 0 anymore and find that for
k
of order
n
the convergence fails. We prove a stronger version of Diaconis’ and Hicks’ conjecture when
k
is allowed to grow with

at rate at most
\sqrt{n}
. Moreover the Kolmogorov distance converges towards 0 when
k=o(n)
, namely:

Theorem 1
.

(i)

If
k_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
then

d_{TV}(k_{n},n)=O\left(\frac{k_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(3)

(ii)

If
k_{n}=o(n)
and
\sqrt{n}=o(k_{n})
then

d_{K}(k_{n},n)=O\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{k_{n}}+\left(\frac{k_{n}}{n}\right)^{0.19}\right).

(4)

.

In Theorem

\sqrt{n}
is assumed to be a

Bijection between parking functions and Cayley trees

Here the goal is to use the bijection found by Chassaing and Marckert in
[

between parking functions of size

and
Cayley trees
with

n+1
vertices labeled from 0 to

\mathfrak{C}_{n+1}
be the set of Cayley trees with
n+1
vertices labeled from 0 to
n
where the vertex labeled 0 is distinguished from the others (we call it the
root
of the tree). Also let

be the set of parking functions of size

[

]
for a detailed definition and see figure

, define

in

. The bijection of Chassaing and Marckert is described in the following theorem.

.

.

Let

We are now able to state and prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 6
.

Fix
1\leq k\leq n
and
1\leq i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\leq n
. Let
j_{1}<\dots<j_{r}
be such that
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{r}\}
and define
m_{s}=\#\{u:i_{u}=j_{s}\}
for all
1\leq s\leq r
. Then

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)=i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)=i_{k})=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\prod_{s=1}^{r}(X_{j_{s}})_{m_{s}}\right]

(6)

where
(x)_{m}:=x(x-1)\cdots(x-m+1)
.

Proof.

Let
T_{n+1}
be a random Cayley tree in
\mathfrak{C}_{n+1}
with uniform distribution. Denote by
\mathfrak{S}(k,n)
the set of all injections between
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
and
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)=i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)=i_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(k,n)}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(\sigma(1))=i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(\sigma(k))=i_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(k,n)}\mathds{1}_{r(\sigma(1),T_{n+1})=i_{1},\dots,r(\sigma(k),T_{n+1})=i_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{s=1}^{r}(\#\{1\leq j\leq n:r(j,T_{n+1})=j_{s}\})_{m_{s}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{s=1}^{r}(X_{j_{s}})_{m_{s}}\right].

The first equality comes from the fact that any permutation of a parking function is still a parking function, thus any permutation induces a bijection in
P_{n}
. The second equality comes from Theorem
4
and the last from Corollary
5
. This completes the proof.
∎

Convergence for the total variation distance

In this section we suppose that
k_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
. We will write
k
instead of
k_{n}
to make notation lighter but keep in mind that
k
depends on
n
. The goal of this section is to show item (i) of Theorem
1
.

3.1
Probability that the parking places are distinct

The first step is to reduce the problem to distinct parking places, in this case Equation (
6
) becomes easier. To this end we introduce the set of distinct indices
D_{n}:=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket^{k}:i\neq j\Rightarrow u_{i}\neq u_{j}\}
. We also introduce the set
G_{n}:=\{(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket^{k}:\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)=u_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)=u_{k})\geq(n-k)!/n!\}
and the quantity

\delta(k,n):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\\
\in D_{n}\cap G_{n}\end{subarray}}\left[\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)=i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)=i_{k})-\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\right].

(7)

The next Lemma shows that the first
k
parking places of a uniform parking function are all distinct with high probability. It also shows that if
\delta(k,n)
is a
O(k/\sqrt{n})
then so is
d_{TV}(k,n)
. Recall that
(U_{n}(i))_{1\leq i\leq n}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed in
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
.

Lemma 7
.

We have

(i)

\mathbb{P}((U_{n}(1),\dots,U_{n}(k))\in D_{n})=1+O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
,

(ii)

\mathbb{P}((\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k))\in D_{n})=1+O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
,

(iii)

\delta(k,n)=O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\implies d_{TV}(k,n)=O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\mu_{n}
be the law of
(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k))
and
\nu_{n}
the law of
(U_{n}(1),\dots,U_{n}(k))
, with support on the same finite space
E_{n}:=\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket^{k}
. First we check (i). By Markov’s inequality

\nu_{n}(D_{n}^{\mathrm{c}})=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{r<s}\mathds{1}_{U_{n}(r)=U_{n}(s)}\geq 1\right)\leq\sum_{r<s}\mathbb{P}\left(U_{n}(r)=U_{n}(s)\right)=\sum_{r<s}\frac{1}{n}=\frac{1}{n}\frac{k(k-1)}{2}.

Since
k=o(\sqrt{n})
we have that

\frac{1}{n}\frac{k(k-1)}{2}=O\left(\frac{k^{2}}{n}\right)=O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Now we check (ii). To do so, we will use the Prüfer encoding (or a slight variant thereof) of a rooted Cayley tree
t\in\mathfrak{C}_{n}
into a sequence
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n-2})\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket^{n-2}
, which we now explain. For
a\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
, define
p(t,a)
to be the label of the parent of the vertex labeled
a
in
t
. Also, define
\ell(t)
as the biggest leaf label of
t
, and
t^{*}
the tree
t
obtained after removing the leaf labeled
\ell(t)
and its adjacent edge. Finally we define the sequence of trees,
t_{1}:=t
,
t_{i}:=t_{i-1}^{*}
for
2\leq i\leq n-2
. The Prüfer encoding of
t
is then defined as
p_{i}:=p(t_{i},\ell(t_{i}))
. For example, the Prüfer encoding of the tree in figure
1
is
(8,8,6,0,3,0,3,3)
. The key property of this encoding is that it is a bijection between the sets
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
and
\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket^{n-2}
. Now, let
T_{n+1}
be a uniform Cayley tree in
\mathfrak{C}_{n+1}
. Theorem
4
implies that
\mu_{n}(D_{n})
is equal to the probability that the vertices labeled 1 to
k
in
T_{n+1}
have distinct parents. Let
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
be a random vector with uniform distribution in
D_{n}
independent of
T_{n+1}
. Since the distribution of
T_{n+1}
is invariant under permutation of the labels, the previous probability is also equal to the probability that the vertices labeled
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
have distinct parents in
T_{n+1}
. Let
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n-1})
be the Prüfer encoding of the tree
T_{n+1}
. We complete this vector with
p_{n}:=0
(this comes from the fact that
t_{n-2}
has two vertices, one of them being the root labeled 0). Since
T_{n+1}
is uniformly distributed in
\mathfrak{C}_{n+1}
, the vector
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n-1})
is uniformly distributed in
\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket^{n-1}
. From the previous discussion and the definition of the Prüfer encoding we deduce that

\mu_{n}(D_{n})=\mathbb{P}((p_{v_{1}},\dots,p_{v_{k}})\in D_{n}).

Consider the event
Z_{n}:=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{k}\neq n\}
. Under this event, it is easy to see that
(p_{v_{1}},\dots,p_{v_{k}})
has the same law as
k
i.i.d random variables uniformly distributed in
\llbracket 0,n\rrbracket
. So from (i) we have

\mu_{n}(D_{n})\geq\mathbb{P}((p_{v_{1}},\dots,p_{v_{k}})\in D_{n}\,|\,Z_{n})\mathbb{P}(Z_{n})=\left(1+O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right)\mathbb{P}(Z_{n}).

To conclude, notice that
\mathbb{P}(Z_{n})=1-k/n
.

Finally we show (iii). Assume that
\delta(k,n)=O\left(k/\sqrt{n}\right)
. For all
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}
denote by
\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
the quantity
(\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)=i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)=i_{k})-(n-k)!/n!)
. Notice that
n^{k}(n-k)!/n!-1=O(k/\sqrt{n})
so

d_{TV}(k,n)=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}^{n}\left|\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\right|+O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Denote by
d^{+}_{n}
the sum of
\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}
over the indices in
G_{n}
and
d^{-}_{n}
the opposite of the sum over the indices in
E_{n}\setminus G_{n}
. We have that

d^{+}_{n}-d^{-}_{n}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}=1}^{n}\Delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}=1-\frac{n^{k}(n-k)!}{n!}=O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

The last two equalities imply that

d_{TV}(k,n)=d^{+}_{n}+d^{-}_{n}+O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=2d^{+}_{n}+O\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

In conclusion we just need to show that
d^{+}_{n}
is a
O(k/\sqrt{n})
. Notice that

\displaystyle d^{+}_{n}=\delta(k,n)+\mu_{n}(D_{n}^{\mathrm{c}}\cap G_{n})-\nu_{n}(D_{n}^{\mathrm{c}}\cap G_{n})\times\frac{n^{k}(n-k)!}{n!}.

From (i), (ii) and the assumption on
\delta(k,n)
we deduce that
d^{+}_{n}
is indeed a
O(k/\sqrt{n})
.
∎

3.2
A monotonicity argument

In this section we bound the terms
\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}}\right]
that appear in (
6
) when

are distinct with terms involving

since the latter are more manageable. More precisely, the aim of this section is to prove the following result.

Proposition 8
.

Fix
i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
pairwise distinct. We have

\displaystyle i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\,\mathbb{E}_{n}[X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{n}[(S_{i_{1}}+i_{1})\cdots(S_{i_{k}}+i_{k})]

(8)

To prove Proposition
8
we first state a really useful lemma which, put in simple terms, says that the steps of the random walk
S
tend to decrease under
\mathbb{P}_{n}
.

Lemma 9
.

Fix
n\geq k\geq 1
and
m_{1},\dots,m_{k}\geq 1
. Let
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq n
,
1\leq j_{1}<\dots<j_{k}\leq n
be such that
j_{r}\leq i_{r}
for all
1\leq r\leq k
. Finally let
f:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}\mapsto[0,\infty)
be a nonnegative function such that
f(0,m)=0
, then

\mathbb{E}_{n}[f(X_{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(X_{i_{k}},m_{k})]\leq\mathbb{E}_{n}[f(X_{j_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(X_{j_{k}},m_{k})].

(9)

Proof of Lemma
9
.

Let
s:=\min\{r\geq 1:j_{r}<i_{r}\}
. We only need to treat the case where
i_{r}=j_{r}
for all
r\neq s
and
j:=j_{s}=i_{s}-1
(the general result can then be obtained by induction). Let
\sigma=(j\penalty\ j+1)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the permutation that transposes
j
and
j+1
. Let
\mathcal{E}_{n}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}:(x_{1}-1)+\dots+(x_{t}-1)=-1\text{ iff }t=n\}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n}=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{n}:x_{j+1}>0\text{ or }(x_{1}-1)+\dots+(x_{j-1}-1)>0\}
. Notice that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mapsto(x_{\sigma(1)},\dots,x_{\sigma(n)})
is a bijection on
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(X_{i_{k}},m_{k})\mathds{1}_{\tau_{-1}=n+1}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}f(x_{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(x_{i_{k}},m_{k})\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n}}f(x_{\sigma(i_{1})},m_{1})\cdots f(x_{\sigma(i_{k})},m_{k})\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}]

\displaystyle+\sum_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n}}f(x_{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(x_{i_{k}},m_{k})\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}].

Notice that if
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{E}^{\prime}_{n}
then
f(x_{j+1},m_{j+1})=f(0,m_{j+1})=0
; in particular, since
f
is nonnegative,
f(x_{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(x_{i_{k}},m_{k})\leq f(x_{\sigma(i_{1})},m_{1})\cdots f(x_{\sigma(i_{k})},m_{k})
. Finally

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(X_{i_{k}},m_{k})\mathds{1}_{\tau_{-1}=n+1}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{E}_{n}}f(x_{\sigma(i_{1})},m_{1})\cdots f(x_{\sigma(i_{k})},m_{k})\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=x_{1},\dots,X_{n}=x_{n}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{\sigma(i_{1})},m_{1})\cdots f(X_{\sigma(i_{k})},m_{k})\mathds{1}_{\tau_{-1}=n+1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[f(X_{j_{1}},m_{1})\cdots f(X_{j_{k}},m_{k})\mathds{1}_{\tau_{-1}=n+1}].

∎

Remark
.

In Lemma

we can for instance take

or
f(x,m)=(x)_{m}
. Notice that in Lemma

the indices

must be pairwise distinct as well as the indices

. In the proof of Proposition

we extend the result, when

, to the case where only the

are pairwise distinct.

Proof of Proposition

.

First we show the following inequality. Fix

. Let

,

be such that

for all

. Then

\mathbb{E}_{n}[X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{k}}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{n}[X_{j_{1}}\cdots X_{j_{k}}].

(10)

To show (

) it is actually enough to show the following result: let

and

such that

and

do not belong to

for

. Then

3.3
Bounding the moments of a random walk conditioned to be an excursion

The goal of this section is to find bounds for the moments of the random walk
S
conditioned to be an excursion. More precisely, the aim of this section is to show the following result.

Proposition 10
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n\geq 2
,
1\leq k\leq n-1
and
d\geq 1

(i)

we have

\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[S_{k}^{d}\right]\leq\left(Cdn\right)^{d/2},

(12)

(ii)

and

\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[S_{n-k}^{d}\right]\leq\left(\frac{n}{n-k}\right)^{3/2}(Cdk)^{d/2},

(13)

(iii)

as well as

\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[S_{k}^{d}\right]\leq\left(\frac{n}{n-k}\right)^{3/2}\left(Cd\sqrt{k}\right)^{d}.

(14)

Remark
.

Proposition
10
(i) and (ii) actually hold true for any random walk
(S_{n})_{n\geq 0}
starting from 0 with i.i.d. increments
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
all having the distribution
\mu
whose support is
\mathbb{N}\cup\{-1\}
and such that
\mu
has mean 0 and finite variance. However, (iii) uses in addition the fact that a Poisson random walk has a sub-exponential tail (see e.g.
[
Wel18
, Example 3]
), namely, for all
k\geq 1
and
x\geq 0
:

\mathbb{P}(S_{k}\geq x)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(k+x/3)}\right)\leq C\exp\left(-\frac{x}{4k}\right).

(15)

To prove Proposition
10
we will use the following lemma whose proof is postponed at the end of this section. This lemma is similar to the cyclic lemma in spirit but instead of conditioning the walk to be an excursion we only condition it to stay positive.

Lemma 11
.

Let
n\geq 1
and
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,+\infty)
be invariant under cyclic shifts. Then

\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{n}>0}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})(S_{n}\wedge n)\mathds{1}_{S_{n}>0}\right].

(16)

Proof of Proposition
10
.

We recall that
C
denotes a constant which may vary line to line. For (i), according to
[
ABDJ13
, Eq. (32)]
the maximum of the excursion of
S
has a sub-Gaussian tail, namely there exist constants
C,\alpha>0
such that for all
n\geq 1
and
x\geq 0
:

\mathbb{P}_{n}\left(M_{n}\geq x\right)\leq Ce^{-\alpha x^{2}/n}

where
M_{n}:=\max\{S_{0},\dots,S_{n}\}
is the maximum of the walk
S
on
[0,n]
. So we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[n^{-d/2}S_{k}^{d}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[n^{-d/2}M_{n}^{d}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx^{d-1}\mathbb{P}_{n}\left(M_{n}\geq\sqrt{n}x\right)dx\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}Cdx^{d-1}e^{-\alpha x^{2}}dx\leq C^{d}d^{d/2}.

This shows (i).

The following computation is a common starting point to show both (ii) and (iii). Let
H(S_{k},x)
be either the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{S_{k}=x}
or
\mathds{1}_{S_{k}\geq x}
with
x>0
. Using the fact that
\mathbb{P}(\tau_{-1}=n+1)
is equivalent to a constant times
n^{-3/2}
(see e.g.
[
Gal05
, Eq. (10)]
), then the Markov property and finally the cyclic lemma (see e.g.
[
Pit06
, Section 6.1]
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[H(S_{k},x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\,n^{3/2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[H(S_{k},x)\,\mathds{1}_{\tau_{-1}=n+1}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\,n^{3/2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[H(S_{k},x)\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{-1-S_{k}}=n-k\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\,n^{3/2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[H(S_{k},x)\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,\frac{1+S_{k}}{n-k}\,\mathbb{P}\left(S^{\prime}_{n-k}=-1-S_{k}\right)\right]

where
S^{\prime}
is independent of
S
and has the same distribution. Now we use Janson’s inequality
[
Jan06
, Lemma 2.1]
which states that
\mathbb{P}(S_{r}=-m)\leq Cr^{-1/2}e^{-\lambda m^{2}/r}
for all
r\geq 1
and
m\geq 0
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[H(S_{k},x)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\,n^{3/2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[H(S_{k},x)\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,\frac{1+S_{k}}{(n-k)^{3/2}}\,e^{-\lambda\frac{(1+S_{k})^{2}}{n-k}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{n}{n-k}\right)^{3/2}\mathbb{E}\left[H(S_{k},x)\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,S_{k}\,e^{-\lambda\frac{S_{k}^{2}}{n-k}}\right].

(17)

To prove (ii) we first define
T_{k}:=\max\{0\leq i\leq k:S_{i}=0\}
, thus, using the Markov property, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=x,\,S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=x,\,S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0,\,T_{k}=i\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k-i}=x,\,S_{1},\dots,S_{k-i}>0\right).

We apply Lemma
11
and the local limit theorem (see e.g.
[
IL71
, Theorem 4.2.1]
), which gives a constant
C>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(S_{k-i}=x)\leq C(k-i)^{-1/2}
for every
k>i
and
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=x,\,S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)\frac{x}{k-i}\mathbb{P}(S_{k-i}=x)

\displaystyle\leq C\,x\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)\frac{1}{(k-i)^{3/2}}.

Notice that

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)=e\,\mathbb{P}(\tau_{-1}=i+1)\leq\frac{C}{(i+1)^{3/2}}.

So, finally we have

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{k}=x,\,S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0\right)\leq C\,x\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{((i+1)(k-i))^{3/2}}=\frac{C\,x}{(k+1)^{3/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{1}{i+1}+\frac{1}{k-i}\right)^{3/2}\leq\frac{C\,x}{k^{3/2}}.

Putting the previous inequality in (
3.3
) with
H(S_{k},x)=\mathds{1}_{S_{k}=x}
and replacing
k
by
n-k
gives

\mathbb{P}_{n}(S_{n-k}=x)\leq C\left[\frac{n}{k(n-k)}\right]^{3/2}x^{2}e^{-\lambda\frac{x^{2}}{k}}.

We can now bound the
d
-th moment of
S_{n-k}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}[S_{n-k}^{d}]\leq C\left[\frac{n}{k(n-k)}\right]^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d+2}e^{-\lambda\frac{x^{2}}{k}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C^{d}k^{d/2}\left[\frac{n}{n-k}\right]^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d+2}e^{-x^{2}}dx

\displaystyle\leq C^{d}k^{d/2}\left[\frac{n}{n-k}\right]^{3/2}d^{d/2}.

This concludes the proof of (ii).

To prove (iii) we follow the same principle. Using the Markov property and Lemma
11
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k}\geq x}\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,S_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k}\geq x}\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,S_{k}\,\mathds{1}_{T_{k}=i}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k-i}\geq x}\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k-i}>0}\,S_{k-i}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)\frac{1}{k-i}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k-i}\geq x}\,S_{k-i}^{2}\right].

Then, we apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k-i}\geq x}\,S_{k-i}^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}(S_{k-i}\geq x)^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{k-i}^{4}\right]^{1/2}\leq C(k-i)\mathbb{P}(S_{k-i}\geq x)^{1/2}.

The last inequality comes from an explicit computation of the fourth central moment of a Poisson distribution. We combine the last inequality with (
15
) to get

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k-i}\geq x}\,S_{k-i}^{2}\right]\leq C(k-i)e^{-\frac{x}{8(k-i)}}\leq C(k-i)e^{-\frac{x}{8k}}.

Putting everything together we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{S_{k}\geq x}\,\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{k}\geq 0}\,S_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-\frac{x}{8k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{P}\left(S_{1},\dots,S_{i}\geq 0,\,S_{i}=0\right)

\displaystyle\leq Ce^{-\frac{x}{8k}}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}e\,\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{-1}=i+1\right)\leq Ce^{-\frac{x}{8k}}.

We combine the last inequality with (
3.3
) to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}[k^{-d/2}S_{k}^{d}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}dx^{d-1}\mathbb{P}_{n}(S_{k}\geq\sqrt{k}x)dx\leq C\left[\frac{n}{n-k}\right]^{3/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}dx^{d-1}e^{-\frac{x}{8}}dx\leq\left[\frac{n}{n-k}\right]^{3/2}C^{d}d^{d}.

This concludes the proof of (iii).

∎

We now prove Lemma
11

Proof of Lemma
11
.

For
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
i\in\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
denote by
x^{i}
the
i
-th cyclic permutation of
x
, namely
x^{i}:=(x_{1+i},\dots,x_{n},x_{1},\dots,x_{i})
. Set
A_{n}:=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in(\mathbb{N}\cup\{-1\})^{n}:\forall k\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket,\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}>0\}
. We also set
\xi:=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\mathds{1}_{S_{1},\dots,S_{n}>0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi)\mathds{1}_{\xi\in A_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi^{i})\mathds{1}_{\xi^{i}\in A_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi)\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\mathds{1}_{\xi^{i}\in A_{n}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[F(\xi)(S_{n}\wedge n)\mathds{1}_{S_{n}>0}\right].

The inequality comes from the fact that the number of cyclic shifts of
\xi
such that
\xi^{i}\in A_{n}
is almost surely bounded by
(S_{n}\wedge n)\mathds{1}_{S_{n}>0}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
(i)

Recall we want to show that

. In section

we have shown that it is enough to show

where the definition of

is given by (

). Thanks to Proposition

this quantity can be rewritten as

where

. As was already mentioned in section
3.1

n^{k}(n-k)!/n!=1+O(k/\sqrt{n})
so, for our purpose, it is sufficient to bound the integral:

Using inequality (
8
) we obtain

\displaystyle I(k,n)\leq

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{k}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\frac{S_{\lceil nt_{1}\rceil}}{\lceil nt_{1}\rceil}+1\right)\cdots\left(\frac{S_{\lceil nt_{k}\rceil}}{\lceil nt_{1}\rceil}+1\right)\right]-1\right]dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k}.

(18)

Notice that Proposition
10
(i) and (iii) imply that for all
0\leq k\leq n

\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[S_{k}^{d}\right]\leq\left(Cd\sqrt{k}\right)^{d}

Hölder’s inequality then shows that for

\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[S_{i_{1}}\cdots S_{i_{d}}\right]\leq(Cd)^{d}\sqrt{i_{1}\cdots i_{d}}.

(19)

Expanding the products in (

) and using (

) gives

I(k,n)\leq\sum_{d=1}^{k}\binom{k}{d}(Cd)^{d}n^{-d/2}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\frac{dt_{1}\cdots dt_{d}}{(t_{1}\cdots t_{d})^{1/2}}.

Notice that

is integrable so

,

4
Convergence for the Kolmogorov distance

In this section we suppose that
k_{n}=o(n)
and
\sqrt{n}=o(k_{n})
. We will write
k
instead of
k_{n}
to make notation lighter but keep in mind that
k
depends on
n
. The goal of this section is to show Theorem
1
(ii). The following Lemma allows us to replace the cumulative probability in (
2
) with the term
\mathbb{E}_{n}[(S_{i_{1}}+i_{1})\cdots(S_{i_{n}}+i_{n})]
which is more manageable.

Lemma 12
.

There is a constant
C>0
such that

d_{K}(k,n)\leq\frac{1}{n^{k}}\max_{1\leq i_{1}\dots i_{k}\leq n}\left|\mathbb{E}_{n}[(S_{i_{1}}+i_{1})\cdots(S_{i_{k}}+i_{k})]-i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\right|+\frac{Ck}{n}.

(20)

Before proving this Lemma we show how it implies Theorem

(ii). We will also need the following simple Lemma which extends
[
Bil12
, Eq. (27.5)]

Lemma 13
.

Let
r\geq 1
,

and

be complex numbers of modulus smaller than, respectively,

and

. Then

Proof of Lemma

.

The result readily follows from the identity

\prod_{i=1}^{r}w_{i}-\prod_{i=1}^{r}z_{i}=(w_{1}-z_{1})\prod_{i=2}^{r}w_{i}+z_{1}\left(\prod_{i=2}^{r}w_{i}-\prod_{i=2}^{r}z_{i}\right).

∎

Proof of Theorem
1
(ii).

Let
1/2<\alpha<1
. We define a sequence of intervals

(depending on

) in the following way.

where

is the biggest integer such that

so in particular

. Let

(with

depending on what intervals the

\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\left(\frac{S_{i_{1}}+i_{1}}{n}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{S_{i_{k}}+i_{k}}{n}\right)-\frac{i_{1}}{n}\cdots\frac{i_{k}}{n}\right]\leq\sum_{m=1}^{M+3}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\prod_{i_{j}\in I_{m}}\frac{S_{i_{j}}+i_{j}}{n}-\prod_{i_{j}\in I_{m}}\frac{i_{j}}{n}\right].

(22)

Fix

and denote by
\iota_{1},\dots,\iota_{r_{m}}
the

’s that belong to

. If

then by Lemma

and Proposition

(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\prod_{i_{j}\in I_{1}}\frac{S_{i_{j}}+i_{j}}{n}-\prod_{i_{j}\in I_{1}}\frac{i_{j}}{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{1}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[S_{\iota_{j}}]\left(\frac{n-k}{n}\right)^{j-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\sqrt{n}}{n}\frac{n}{k}=\frac{C\sqrt{n}}{k}.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Ck^{\alpha^{m-2}/2}}{n}\frac{n}{k^{\alpha^{m-1}}}=\frac{C}{k^{(\alpha-1/2)\alpha^{m-2}}}.

And finally if

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{M+3}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[S_{\iota_{j}}]

\displaystyle\leq r_{M+3}\frac{C}{n}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{1/2}\leq C\frac{\log\log(n)}{(nk)^{1/2}}.

Notice that

so for all

,

. Summing the preceding bounds for

gives

\displaystyle\sum_{m=2}^{M+2}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[\prod_{i_{j}\in I_{m}}\frac{S_{i_{j}}+i_{j}}{n}-\prod_{i_{j}\in I_{m}}\frac{i_{j}}{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{k^{(\alpha-1/2)\alpha^{m}}}=C\sum_{m=0}^{M}\frac{1}{k^{(\alpha-1/2)\alpha^{M-m}}}\leq C\sum_{m=0}^{M}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{(\alpha-1/2)\alpha^{-m}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{(\alpha-1/2)}+C\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{-(\alpha-1/2)e\ln(\alpha)}

where we used the fact that

for all

. If we take

O(k/n)^{0.19}
. Putting everything together we finally obtain that

\frac{1}{n^{k}}\max_{1\leq i_{1}\dots i_{k}\leq n}\left|\mathbb{E}_{n}[(S_{i_{1}}+i_{1})\cdots(S_{i_{k}}+i_{k})]-i_{1}\cdots i_{k}\right|\leq C\frac{\sqrt{n}}{k}+C\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{0.19}+C\frac{\log\log(n)}{(nk)^{1/2}}.

Combining the last display with Lemma
12
gives the desired result.
∎

Now we prove Lemma
12
.

Proof of Lemma
12
.

Define the empirical distribution function of
\pi_{n}
, namely, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
:

F_{n}(i):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\pi_{n}(j)\leq i}.

As suggested in
[
DH17
]
we can use a result of Bobkov
[
Bob05
, Theorem 1.1]
. The sequence
(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(n))
is an exchangeable extension of
(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k))
meaning that the distribution of
(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(n))
stays the same after any permutation. So by Theorem 1.1 of
[
Bob05
]
we have that

\max_{1\leq i_{1}\dots i_{k}\leq n}\left|\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1)\leq i_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}(k)\leq i_{k})-\mathbb{E}[F_{n}(i_{1})\cdots F_{n}(i_{k})]\right|\leq C\frac{k}{n}

where
C
is a universal constant. Using Proposition
6
and Corollary
5
we find

\mathbb{E}[F_{n}(i_{1})\cdots F_{n}(i_{k})]=\frac{1}{n^{k}}\mathbb{E}_{n}[(S_{i_{1}}+i_{1})\cdots(S_{i_{k}}+i_{k})].

Indeed, Proposition
6
implies that
(F_{n}(1),\dots,F_{n}(n))
has the same distribution as
(G_{n}(1),\dots,G_{n}(n))
with

G_{n}(i):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{r(j,T_{n+1})\leq i}

where
T_{n+1}
is a uniform random tree of
\mathfrak{C}_{n+1}
. Then, Corollary
5
shows that

G_{n}(i)=\frac{1}{n}\#\{1\leq j\leq n:r(j,T_{n+1})\leq i\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\frac{1}{n}(S_{i}+i)

jointly for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. This concludes the proof.

∎

5
Sum and maximum of the first parking places

We begin this section with the proof of Corollary
2
. Then we finish by proving Proposition
3
.

Proof of Corollary
2
.

(i) Recall that
(U_{n}(i))_{1\leq i\leq n}
are i.i.d. uniformly distributed in
\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
. By the central limit theorem, the convergence

\sqrt{\frac{12}{k_{n}}}\left(\frac{U_{n}(1)+\dots+U_{n}(k_{n})}{n}-\frac{k_{n}}{2}\right)\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,1)

holds in distribution. Using the first item of Theorem
1
we deduce that the total variation distance between the distributions of
U_{n}(1)+\dots+U_{n}(k_{n})
and
\pi_{n}(1)+\dots+\pi_{n}(k_{n})
tends to 0. Thus the above convergence still holds when
U_{n}(i)
is replaced with
\pi_{n}(i)
.

(ii) Let
x>0
, then

\mathbb{P}\left[k_{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\max\{U_{n}(1),\dots,U_{n}(k_{n})\}\right)\geq x\right]=0\vee\frac{1}{n}\left\lfloor n\left(1-\frac{x}{k_{n}}\right)\right\rfloor^{k_{n}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}e^{-x}.

Using Theorem
1
(ii) we deduce that the above convergence still holds when
U_{n}(i)
is replaced with
\pi_{n}(i)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

In this proof we write
k
instead of
k_{n}
to make notation lighter. For every
a\geq 0
:

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k)\leq n-a)=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}_{n}\left[(S_{n-a}+n-a)_{k}\right].

(23)

Indeed, following the same computation as in the proof of Proposition
6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k)\leq n-a)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(k,n)}\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(\sigma(1)),\dots,\pi_{n}(\sigma(k))\leq n-a)

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(k,n)}\mathds{1}_{r(\sigma(1),T_{n+1}),\dots,r(\sigma(k),T_{n+1})\leq n-a}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n-a})_{k}\right]

which leads to (
23
) since
X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n-a}=S_{n-a}+n-a
. Let
\tau_{n}
be a Bienaymé-Galton-Watson tree with a critical Poisson offspring distribution
\mu
conditionned on having
n
vertices and define
S^{n}
the associated Łukasiewicz path. More precisely, if
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
are the vertices of
\tau_{n}
ordered according to the lexicographic order (see e.g.
[
Gal05
, Section 1.1]
), then for all
0\leq k\leq n
:

S^{n}_{k}:=\#\left\{e:e\text{ is an edge adjacent to a vertex }v_{i}\text{ with }i\leq k\right\}-k.

In the previous definition,
S^{n}_{k}
is deduced from the first
k
vertices
v_{1},\dots,v_{k}
but it is possible to see
S^{n}_{k}
in terms of the last
n-k
vertices
v_{k+1},\dots,v_{n}
:

S^{n}_{k}=n-1-k-\#\left\{e:e\text{ is an edge between two vertices }v_{i}\text{ and }v_{j}\text{ with }i,j>k\right\}.

It is known that
S^{n+1}
and
S
under
\mathbb{P}_{n}
have the same distribution. Thus, equality (
23
) can be rewritten in the following way

\mathbb{P}(\pi_{n}(1),\dots,\pi_{n}(k)\leq n-a)=\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_{n-a}^{n+1}+n-a)_{k}\right].

(24)

Let
\tau^{*}
be the so-called Kesten’s tree associated with
\mu
(see e.g.
[
AD20
, Section 2.3]
). Denote by
\preceq
the lexicographic order on the set of vertices of
\tau^{*}
. It is always possible to find a unique infinite sequence
u_{1},u_{2},\dots
of distinct vertices of
\tau^{*}
such that for all
i\geq 1
,
\{u:u\text{ is a vertex of }\tau^{*}\text{ such that }u_{i}\preceq u\}=\{u_{1},\dots,u_{i}\}
. In other words,
u_{1},u_{2},\dots
are the last vertices of
\tau^{*}
for the lexicographic order, which, necessarily, lay on the right of the infinite spine. Similarly to the Łukasiewicz path we can define the quantity

S_{a}^{*}:=a-\#\left\{e:e\text{ is an edge between two vertices }u_{i}\text{ and }u_{j}\text{ with }i,j\leq a+1\right\}.

It is known that
\tau_{n}
converges in distribution, for the local topology, towards
\tau^{*}
(see e.g.
[
AD20
, Section 3.3.5]
). Making use of Skorokhod’s representation theorem, suppose that the latter convergence holds almost surely. Thus,
S^{n+1}_{n-a}
converges almost surely towards
S_{a}^{*}
. Consequently, the convergence

\frac{(n-k)!}{n!}(S_{n-a}^{n+1}+n-a)_{k}=\frac{(n-k)!}{(n-k+S_{n-a}^{n+1}-a)!}\frac{(n+S_{n-a}^{n+1}-a)!}{n!}\sim(n-cn)^{a-S_{a}^{*}}\frac{1}{n^{a-S_{a}^{*}}}\longrightarrow(1-c)^{a-S_{a}^{*}}

holds almost surely. Since the above sequence is bounded by 1 we deduce that the convergence of the expectation holds which concludes the proof.
∎
