Interleaving Distances, Monoidal Actions and 2-Categories
Patrick K. McFaddin and Tom Needham

Abstract
. Interleaving distances are used widely in Topological Data Analysis (TDA) as a tool for comparing topological signatures of datasets. The theory of interleaving distances has been extended through various category-theoretic constructions, enabling its usage beyond standard constructions of TDA, while clarifying certain observed stability phenomena by unifying them under a common framework. Inspired by metrics used in the field of statistical shape analysis, which are based on minimizing energy functions over group actions, we define three new types of increasingly general interleaving distances. Our constructions use ideas from the theories of monoidal actions and 2-categories. We show that these distances naturally extend the category with a flow framework of de Silva, Munch and Stefanou and the locally persistent category framework of Scoccola, and we provide a general stability result. Along the way, we give examples of distances that fit into our framework which connect to ideas from differential geometry, geometric shape analysis, statistical TDA and multiparameter persistent homology.

The category-theoretic viewpoint also allows for conceptually simple proofs of important theoretical properties of TDA such as the stability of invariants—roughly, if two datasets are similar with respect to, say, Hausdorff distance, then their topological signatures are guaranteed to be similar with respect to interleaving distance.

This paper constructs further generalizations of the notion of interleaving distance, inspired by ideas rooted in differential geometry and statistical shape analysis. Our main motivation was an analogy between interleaving distances and metrics which arise through group actions: namely, suppose that a group
\mathcal{G}
endowed with a left-invariant metric

acts transitively from the left on a topological space
\mathcal{X}
; we can then define the distance between points
x
and

\mathcal{X}
as

(1.3)

\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\{d_{\mathcal{G}}(e,g)\mid gx=y\},

where
e
denotes the identity element of
\mathcal{G}
. It is straightforward to show that this defines a pseudometric, and that it defines a true metric under further mild assumptions on the action. Such metrics arise in the context of homogeneous spaces, where they are geodesic distances with respect to natural Riemannian metrics, and in medical imaging and shape analysis, where the space
\mathcal{X}
represents a collection of medical images and the group
\mathcal{G}
is a group of diffeomorphisms of the image domain which acts by deforming the images
[
25
,
3
]
(see Section
3.3

Consider the following interpretation of the distance defined in (
1.3

d_{\mathcal{G}}(e,g)
is taken to represent the “energy” of the group element
g\in\mathcal{G}
, then (
1.3
) measures the distance between points
x
and
y
by determining the minimum energy required to “align”
x
to
y
via the group action. It is natural to consider a relaxation of this idea which doesn’t require a perfect alignment—for example, such a relaxation is necessary to obtain a meaningful distance if the group action is not transitive. The interleaving distance between persistence modules can be viewed as such a relaxation. Indeed, the group in question is
{\mathbb{R}}
(under addition), and a real number
t
acts on a persistence module
M
to produce the persistence module
t\cdot M
with
(t\cdot M)(s)=M(s+t)
. The computation of interleaving distance between modules
M
and
N
can be viewed as finding a “rough alignment” between
M
and
t\cdot N
, and between
N
and
t\cdot M
, consisting of the collections of linear maps
(\phi_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{R}}}
and
(\psi_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{R}}}
, respectively. The diagrams (
1.1
) can be understood to say that these collections of linear maps are consistent with the persistence structures of
M
and
N
(so that the collections are instances of an appropriate notion of morphism in the category of persistence modules), while the diagrams (
1.2
) tell us that
M
and
t\cdot N
are roughly isomorphic, in the sense that
\phi_{s}
and
\psi_{s+t}
are close to being inverses (likewise for
N
and
t\cdot M
). The energy of a group element in this setting is simply
|t|
, so that we may finally interpret the interleaving distance between
M
and
N
to be the minimum energy required to find a rough alignment between them.

The goal of this paper is to identify a framework for interleaving distances which captures the notion of the energy of a rough alignment, as described above, and which generalizes the notions of interleaving that have appeared previously in the literature. To achieve this, we use ideas from the theories of
actegories
(actions of monoidal categories) and
2-categories
(categories endowed with “morphisms between morphisms”).

Stability.

A general theme in TDA is the notion of stability—in this context, this means that the maps which transform datasets into topological descriptors should be Lipschitz with respect to appropriate metrics. The categorical perspective lends itself well to this sort of question, and one can derive quite general and conceptually simple stability results in this setting. We prove the following general result.

Theorem

(Theorem
6.1
)
.

A 2-functor between 2-weighted 2-categories which is weight non-increasing yields a Lipschitz map with respect to the associated interleaving distances.

Due to the very general structure of 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distances, the proof of this result is almost obvious. However, as we specialize to more specific settings, we obtain corollaries which are less so. We give the details of a specialization to the setting of
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distances (Proposition
6.3
) and apply this to give a generalization of the famous sublevel set persistent homology stability result of
[
20
]
to the setting of group actions and generalized persistence modules (Proposition
6.4
).

2.
Background on 2-Categories and Monoidal Actions

This section introduces the categorical terminology and notation that will be used for the rest of the paper. We assume that the reader is familiar with the basic notions of category theory, such as categories, functors and monoidal categories.

Remark 2.5

(Bicategories)
.

A more concise definition of a 2-category is that it is a 1-category such that each set of (1-)morphisms is itself a small category, subject to certain compatibility axioms. That is, a 2-category is a category enriched in

and
g
given by
f\otimes g
, and

(3)

\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}_{2}(f,g)=\mathsf{G}_{1}(f,g)
, with vertical composition of
\alpha\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(f,g)
and
\beta\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(g,h)
given by composition in
\mathsf{G}
,
\beta\alpha
, and with horizontal composition of
\alpha\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(f,g)
and
\beta\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(h,k)
given by
\beta\otimes\alpha\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(h\otimes f,k\otimes g)
(i.e.,
\beta\bullet\alpha=\beta\otimes\alpha)
.

In fact, 2-categories with a single object correspond exactly to deloopings of strict monoidal categories (see
[
29
]
, Examples 2.1.19 and 2.3.11).

Remark 2.7
.

The notation
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
is somewhat standard, intended to evoke notation used in the context of classifying spaces.

If
\mathcal{G}
is a group, we also use
\mathsf{B}\mathcal{G}
to denote the 1-category obtained by
delooping
the group. That is,
\mathsf{B}\mathcal{G}_{0}
contains a single abstract object
\star
,
\mathsf{B}\mathcal{G}_{1}(\star,\star)=\mathcal{G}
and compositions are given by the group law.

We now describe two useful 1-categories determined by any 2-category.

Definition 2.8

(Associated 1-Categories)
.

For a 2-category
\mathsf{C}
, the
underlying 1-category of
\mathsf{C}
is the 1-category determined by the data
\mathsf{C}_{0}
and
\mathsf{C}_{1}
. By abuse of notation, we may continue to denote the underlying 1-category as
\mathsf{C}
. Given
A,B\in\mathsf{C}_{0}
, the data
\mathsf{C}_{1}(A,B)
and
\{\mathsf{C}_{2}(f,g)\}_{f,g\in\mathsf{C}_{1}(A,B)}
determines a 1-category (using vertical compositions) called the
associated 1-category determined by
(A,B)
. This is denoted
\mathsf{C}(A,B)
.

Example 2.9
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a 2-category with a single object
\star
. Then
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
for some monoidal category
\mathsf{G}
, and
\mathsf{C}(\star,\star)=\mathsf{G}
.

2.1.2.
2-Functors

The notion of a functor between categories generalizes to the 2-category setting.

Definition 2.10

(2-Functor)
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
and
\mathsf{D}
be 2-categories. A
2-functor

\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{D}
consists of:

(1)

For all
A\in\mathsf{C}_{0}
, an object
\Delta(A)\in\mathsf{D}_{0}
.

(2)

For each 1-morphism
A\xrightarrow{f}B
in
\mathsf{C}_{1}
, a 1-morphism
\Delta(f)=:\Delta_{f}:\Delta(A)\to\Delta(B)
in
\mathsf{D}_{1}
. Composition and identities must be preserved; i.e.,
\Delta_{gf}=\Delta_{g}\Delta_{f}
and
\Delta_{1_{A}}=1_{\Delta(A)}
.

(3)

For each 2-morphism
f\xRightarrow{\alpha}g
in
\mathsf{C}_{2}
, a 2-morphism
\Delta(\alpha):\Delta_{f}\Rightarrow\Delta_{g}
in
\mathsf{D}_{2}
. Identities and (horizontal and vertical) compositions must be preserved; that is,
\Delta(1_{f})=1_{\Delta_{f}}
,
\Delta(1_{1_{A}})=1_{1_{\Delta(A)}}
,
\Delta(\beta\alpha)=\Delta(\beta)\Delta(\alpha)
and
\Delta(\delta\bullet\gamma)=\Delta(\delta)\bullet\Delta(\gamma)
.

Points (1) and (2) say that
\Delta
determines a functor from the underlying 1-category of
\mathsf{C}
to the underlying 1-category of
\mathsf{D}
. Points (2) and (3) say that for all
A,B\in\mathsf{C}_{0}
,
\Delta
determines a functor
\mathsf{C}(A,B)\to\mathsf{C}(\Delta(A),\Delta(B))
(see Definition
2.8
), such that horizontal compositions are also preserved.

Remark 2.11

(Lax 2-Functors)
.

It is frequently convenient to relax the definition of a 2-functor by allowing diagrams to commute only up to natural transformations. This results in the definition of a
lax 2-functor
. Most of our constructions and results can be extended to the lax setting. In order to simplify the exposition, we work with
strict
2-functors (i.e., 2-functors as described in Definition
2.10
) in this paper.

2.2.
Basics of Actegories

Let
\mathsf{X}
be a small (1-)category. The category
\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
of
endomorphisms of
\mathsf{X}
has objects given by functors from
\mathsf{X}
to itself, and morphisms given by natural transformations. This category has a monoidal structure: using the notation of Example
2.4
and Definition
2.8
,
\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{Cat}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{X})
, and its monoidal structure comes from composition laws of 1- and 2-morphisms in
\mathsf{Cat}
. Let
1_{\mathsf{X}}
denote the identity functor on
\mathsf{X}
, which serves as the identity object for the monoidal structure.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be a (strict) monoidal category with bifunctor denoted
\otimes
and identity object
e\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
. A
(strict) monoidal functor

T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
is a functor which also preserves the monoidal structure; i.e.,
T(e)=1_{\mathsf{X}}
, for objects
g,h\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
,
T(g\otimes h)=T(g)T(h)
and for morphisms
g\xrightarrow{\alpha}h
and
k\xrightarrow{\beta}\ell
,
T(\beta\otimes\alpha)=T(\beta)\bullet T(\alpha)
. We will typically write
T(g)=T_{g}
, so that the first condition becomes
T(g\otimes h)=T_{g}T_{h}
.

Remark 2.12

(Lax Monoidal Functors)
.

Similar to the 2-category setting described in Remark
2.11
, it can be useful to relax the notion of a monoidal functor to that of a
lax monoidal functor
, where diagrams commute up to natural transformation. For the sake of simplicity, we focus on the strict case, although most of what follows can be extended to the lax setting.

A monoidal functor of the form
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
is referred to as an
action of
\mathsf{G}
on
\mathsf{X}
, and
\mathsf{X}
is sometimes referred to as a
\mathsf{G}
-actegory
; these structures (as well as lax versions) have been widely studied, going back at least to the 1970s
[
7
,
40
,
30
,
28
,
35
]
.

A monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
determines a 2-functor
\mathsf{B}T:\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{Cat}
defined on objects by
\mathsf{B}T(\star)=\mathsf{X}
, and on 1- and 2-morphisms by observing that
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}(\star,\star)=\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{Cat}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{X})=\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
and setting
\mathsf{B}T=T
on these associated 1-categories. By
[
29
, Examples 2.1.19 and 2.3.11]
, this association is a bijection onto the collection of 2-functors
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
whose domain 2-category has a single object. That is, we have the following.

Proposition 2.13
.

Any monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
gives rise to a 2-functor
\Delta=\mathsf{B}T:\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{Cat}
. Conversely, if
\mathsf{C}
is a 2-category with a single object and
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
is a 2-functor, then
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
for some strict monoidal category
\mathsf{G}
and
\Delta=\mathsf{B}T
for a monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\Delta(\star))
.

2.2.1.
Category with a Flow

A particular type of actegory was studied in
[
22
,
45
]
: a
category with a flow
is a category
\mathsf{X}
, together with a (strict or lax) monoidal functor
T:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
(i.e., an action of
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
on
\mathsf{X}
). For the sake of simplicity, let us focus on the case of a strict monoidal functor; the lax case can be easily adapted. Here,
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is considered as a poset category (with respect to
\leq
), with monoidal structure given by addition.

In
[
22
,
45
]
, a category with a flow
\mathsf{X}
is endowed with an extended pseudometric on
\mathsf{X}_{0}
. The distance is called
interleaving distance
in the original paper (as it generalizes interleaving distances used previously in TDA
[
20
,
15
,
13
]
), but we will refer to it as
flow interleaving distance
to distinguish it from the generalized interleaving distances which are introduced in the following subsections. For
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, we write
T_{t}=T(t)\in\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
. A
t
-flow interleaving of
X,Y\in\mathsf{X}_{0}
is a pair of morphisms
\phi:X\to T_{t}(Y)
and
\psi:Y\to T_{t}(X)
such that the diagrams

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{2t}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{t}(Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(0\leq 2t)_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{t}(\psi)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{2t}(Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{t}(X)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(0\leq 2t)_{Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{t}(\phi)}

commute. The

is given by

(2.2)

d^{\mathrm{Fl}}_{T}(X,Y)=\inf\{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\mid\mbox{there exists a $t$-flow interleaving of $X$ and $Y$}\}.

It is shown in
[
22
, Theorem 2.7]
that

defines an extended pseudometric on
\mathsf{X}_{0}
.

3.
Interleaving Distances from Actegories and 2-Functors

We now give generalizations of the flow interleaving distances described in Section
2.2.1
. Our constructions require the data of an actegory (Section
3.1
), or more generally a 2-functor from some 2-category into
\mathsf{Cat}
(Section
3.2
).

3.1.
Interleaving Distance Associated to an Actegory

Let
\mathsf{G}
be an arbitrary monoidal category. We generally omit the tensor product notation for objects and write
gh=g\otimes h
for
g,h\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
from now on. Let
\mathsf{X}
be a
\mathsf{G}
-actegory, with monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
. We generalize the flow interleaving distance to an interleaving distance on
\mathsf{X}_{0}
depending on a certain metric-like structure on
\mathsf{G}
.

Definition 3.1

(Monoidal Weight)
.

A
monoidal weight
on
\mathsf{G}
is a function
\mathrm{W}:\mathsf{G}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

for all
g\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
,
\mathrm{W}(g)\geq 0
and
\mathrm{W}(e)=0
;

(2)

for all
f
and
g
in
\mathsf{G}_{0}
,

\mathrm{W}(gf)\leq\mathrm{W}(g)+\mathrm{W}(f).

Remark 3.2
.

This structure is closely related to a well-known categorical construction due to Lawvere
[
32
]
. This connection is explained in detail below in Remark
3.14
.

Example 3.3
.

For
\mathsf{G}={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
(see Section
2.2.1
),
\mathrm{W}(t)=t
is a monoidal weight.

Remark 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a monoid. A function
\mathrm{W}:\mathcal{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying axioms analogous to those of Definition
3.1
will, by abuse of terminology, also be referred to as a
monoidal weight
.

We now define interleavings and interleaving distances induced by a monoidal functor.

Definition 3.5

(Interleaving)
.

Let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
be a monoidal functor and let
X,Y\in\mathsf{X}_{0}
. For
g,h\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
, we say that
X
and
Y
are
(g,h)
-interleaved
if there exist a pair of morphisms
\phi:X\to T_{g}(Y)
,
\psi:Y\to T_{h}(X)
in
\mathsf{X}
and a pair of morphisms
\alpha:e\to gh
and
\beta:e\Rightarrow hg
in
\mathsf{G}
such that the following diagrams commute:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{gh}(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{g}(Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(\alpha)_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{g}(\psi)}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{hg}(Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{h}(X)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(\beta)_{Y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{h}(\phi)}

In this case, we say that the pair
(\phi,\psi)
is a
(g,h)
-interleaving of
X
and
Y
.

Definition 3.6

(
\mathsf{G}
-Interleaving Distance)
.

Let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
be a monoidal functor and
\mathrm{W}
a monoidal weight on
\mathsf{G}
.
The
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance
determined by this data is the function

d_{T}=d_{T,\mathrm{W}}:\mathsf{X}_{0}\times\mathsf{X}_{0}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}

defined by

d_{T}(X,Y):=\inf\{\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(h)\}\mid\mbox{$X$ and $Y$ are $(g,h)$-interleaved}\}.

Remark 3.7

(Intuition Behind the Definition)
.

We can intuit the definition of
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance by considering the monoidal functor
T
as a framework for measuring the “energy” that is required for the “registration” of two objects
X,Y\in\mathsf{X}_{0}
. To elaborate on this:

•

We can think of
T_{g}(Y)
as using
g
to register
Y
to
X
, and likewise,
T_{h}(X)
uses
h
to register
X
to
Y
—preservation of compositions by
T
means that registrations roughly behave like an action of a transformation group.

•

The natural transformations
T(\alpha)
and
T(\beta)
can be understood to control which registrations are admissible; e.g., the existence of
\alpha:e\Rightarrow gh
tells us that
gh
is sufficiently comparable to an identity transformation.

•

The weight
\mathrm{W}
allows us to the measure the “cost” of a registration as
\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(h)\}
.

Thus the
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance
d_{T}(X,Y)
, at an intuitive level, measures the minimum weight that it takes for the objects
X
and
Y
to be admissibly registered to one another.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
be a monoidal functor and
\mathrm{W}
a monoidal weight on
\mathsf{G}
. The associated
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance
d_{T}
is an extended pseudometric on
\mathsf{X}_{0}
.

We will prove Theorem
3.8
below in Section
3.2
by deriving it as a corollary of a more general theorem built around an interleaving distance constructed from the data of a 2-functor. First, we prove formally the intuitively obvious fact that the notion of
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance generalizes that of flow interleaving distance.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\mathsf{G}={\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
, considered as a monoidal category as in Section
2.2.1
and let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
be a monoidal functor. Then the associated flow interleaving distance
d^{\mathrm{Fl}}_{T}
is equal to the associated
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance
d_{T,\mathrm{W}}
, where
\mathrm{W}
is the monoidal weight
\mathrm{W}(t)=t
.

Proof.

We first observe that the flow interleaving distance can be written as

d^{\mathrm{Fl}}_{T}(X,Y)=\inf\{t\geq 0\mid\mbox{$X$ and $Y$ are $(t,t)$-interleaved}\},

where
(t,t)
-interleaved is meant in the sense of Definition

. Now suppose that
X,Y\in\mathsf{X}_{0}
are

-interleaved and, without loss of generality, that

. It then suffices to show that

and

are

-interleaved. Write the diagrams for the

It is shown in
[

]
that many familiar metrics, such as Hausdorff distance and

distance between scalar functions defined on a common space, can be realized as flow interleaving distances for appropriately chosen (strict or lax) monoidal functors. These results show that the examples of

]
are, in turn, realized as 2-functor interleaving distances, at least in the strict case. The same result in the lax setting can also be proved by a similar argument, which we omit for the sake of simplicity.

3.2.
Interleaving Distance Associated to a 2-Functor into
\mathsf{Cat}

Although the focus of this paper is the
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance construction, one can easily generalize this construction for input data consisting of a 2-functor of the form
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
, where
\mathsf{C}
is an arbitrary 2-category. We believe that it is conceptually simpler to show that these more general interleaving distances are extended pseudometrics, and from this we derive Theorem

as an immediate corollary.

Suppose that

is a 2-functor. To make the 2-functor conditions in this setting more explicit, we have:

(1)

For all

For all

. It is assumed that, for all

,

. For

means that for each

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta_{f}(X)}

,

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta(\alpha)_{X}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}

Definition 3.12

(Lawvere Weight)
.

Let
\mathsf{C}
be a (1 or 2)-category. A
Lawvere weight
on
\mathsf{C}
is a function
\mathrm{W}:\mathsf{C}_{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying the following axioms:

(1)

for all
g\in\mathsf{C}_{1}(A,B)
,
\mathrm{W}(g)\geq 0
and
\mathrm{W}(g)=0
if
A=B
and
g=1_{A}
;

(2)

for all composable
f
and
g
in
\mathsf{C}_{1}
,

\mathrm{W}(gf)\leq\mathrm{W}(g)+\mathrm{W}(f).

A (1 or 2)-category endowed with a weight will be called a
weighted (1 or 2)-category
.

Remark 3.13
.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be a monoidal category and
\mathrm{W}
a monoidal weight (Definition
3.1
). Let
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
be the 2-category obtained by delooping (Example
2.6
). There is a natural Lawvere weight on
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
induced via
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}_{1}=\mathsf{G}_{0}
.

Remark 3.14
.

Said differently, a Lawvere weight on
\mathsf{C}
is an enrichment of (the underlying 1-category of)
\mathsf{C}
over the monoidal category whose objects are elements of
[0,\infty]
, such that there exists a morphism
s\rightarrow t
if and only if
s\leq t
, and with monoidal structure given by addition. Lawvere first considered structures of this form in the influential paper
[
32
]
, where he showed that the notion of a category endowed with a Lawvere weight is equivalent to the notion of an extended quasi-pseudo-metric space (a class
\mathcal{X}
endowed with a kernel
d:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
satisfying
d(x,x)=0
and the triangle inequality), also known as a
Lawvere metric space
.

The terminology
weight
is not entirely standard; the structure described in Definition
3.12
has been alternatively referred to as a
metric
or
norm

[
32
]
or
length

[
39
]
on the category. The
weight
terminology is used in, for example,
[
14
]
.

Definition 3.15

(2-Functor Interleaving Distance)
.

Let
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be a 2-functor and
\mathrm{W}
a Lawvere weight on
\mathsf{C}
.
The
2-functor interleaving distance
determined by this data is the function

d_{\Delta}=d_{\Delta,\mathrm{W}}:\mathrm{Im}(\Delta)\times\mathrm{Im}(\Delta)\to{\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}

defined by

d_{\Delta}(X,Y):=\inf\{\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(h)\}\mid\mbox{$X$ and $Y$ are $(g,h)$-interleaved}\}.

3.3.
Examples of Interleaving Distances

The main examples of interest in this paper come from variations on persistent homology; these will be treated in detail below in Section
4
. For now, we give a few simple examples of commonly used distances, outside the traditional realm of TDA, which can be encoded in this framework.

3.3.1.
Group Actions

As was mentioned in the introduction, an inspiration for the definition of
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance was an analogy between interleaving distances and induced metrics on homogeneous spaces. The examples presented in this subsection make precise the connection between these ideas.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group. We denote its elements as
f,g,h,\ldots
, with the identity denoted
e
, and we use multiplicative notation for the group law. This notation coincides with the notation used for monoidal categories (and 2-categories) in anticipation of the following construction of an associated monoidal category.

Definition 3.19

(Associated Monoidal Category)
.

Let
\mathsf{G}
be the (1-)category with objects
\mathsf{G}_{0}=\mathcal{G}
and a unique morphism
\alpha_{g,h}\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(g,h)
for every pair
g,h\in\mathcal{G}
. This defines a monoidal category with tensor product
\otimes
defined by
g\otimes h=gh
and
\alpha_{f,g}\otimes\alpha_{h,k}=\alpha_{fh,gk}
. We refer to
\mathsf{G}
as the
monoidal category associated to
\mathcal{G}
.

Suppose that the group
\mathcal{G}
acts on a set
\mathcal{X}
(say, from the left); write the action as
\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}:(g,x)\mapsto gx
. There is a natural groupoid (1-category whose morphisms are all invertible) associated to this action, which we define below. This is, in fact, one of the prototypical constructions of a groupoid—see
[
11
, Example 3]
for a discussion on terminology and further references.

Definition 3.20

(Action Groupoid)
.

The
action groupoid
associated to the action of
\mathcal{G}
on
\mathcal{X}
is the (1-)category
\mathsf{X}
with
\mathsf{X}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
and

\mathsf{X}_{1}(x,y)=\{\beta_{g,x,y}\mid\mbox{$g\in\mathcal{G}$ satisfies $gx=y$}\}.

That is, we add a unique 1-morphism for every group element taking
x
to
y
via the action. Compositions are then defined by
\beta_{h,y,z}\beta_{g,x,y}:=\beta_{hg,x,z}
. Note that if the action of
\mathcal{G}
is not transitive, then
\mathsf{X}_{1}(x,y)
may be empty. However,
\mathsf{X}_{1}(x,x)
always contains an identity morphism
\beta_{e,x,x}
(and this is the unique morphism if and only if
x
has trivial stabilizer).

We define a monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
as follows:

(1)

T_{g}:\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{X}
is defined on objects by
T_{g}(x)=gx
and on morphisms by
T_{g}(\beta_{h,x,y}):=\beta_{ghg^{-1},gx,gy}
,

(2)

T(\alpha_{g,h}):T_{g}\Rightarrow T_{h}
is defined to be the natural transformation with component at
x
given by
T(\alpha_{g,h})_{x}=\beta_{hg^{-1},gx,hx}
. Then, for any
\beta_{k,x,y}:x\to y
, diagram (
3.1
) reads

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces gx}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces hx}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces gy}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces hy}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{hg^{-1},gx,hx}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{gkg^{-1},gx,gy}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{hkh^{-1},hx,hy}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{hg^{-1},gy,hy}}

and this diagram commutes, so that

is a natural transformation.

One can check that all compositions are preserved under these definitions.

Definition 3.21

(Invariant Metric)
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group. A
right-invariant
(respectively
left-invariant
) metric on
\mathcal{G}
is a metric
d:\mathcal{G}\times\mathcal{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}
with the property that
d(fh,gh)=d(f,g)
(respectively,
d(hf,hg)=d(f,g)
) for all
f,g,h\in\mathcal{G}
.

In what follows, we make statements mainly for right-invariant metrics, but similar statments hold for left-invariant metrics.

Example 3.22
.

Any finite-dimensional Lie group
\mathcal{G}
admits a right-invariant metric, defined by first choosing an inner product on its Lie algebra, extending this to a Riemannian metric via right translations, then taking
d
to be the associated geodesic distance.

Proposition 3.23
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group with right-invariant metric
d
and let
\mathrm{W}:\mathcal{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the map
\mathrm{W}(g):=d(e,g)
, where
e\in\mathcal{G}
is the identity. Then
\mathrm{W}(e)=0
and

(3.5)

\mathrm{W}(gh)\leq\mathrm{W}(g)+\mathrm{W}(h).

Proof.

That
\mathrm{W}(e)=0
is obvious and (
3.5
) is a simple consequence of right-invariance and the other metric properties of
d
:

\mathrm{W}(gh)=d(e,gh)=d(h^{-1},g)\leq d(h^{-1},e)+d(e,g)=d(e,h)+d(e,g)=\mathrm{W}(g)+\mathrm{W}(h).

∎

Now suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is endowed with a right-invariant metric
d_{\mathcal{G}}
. Then Proposition
3.23
says that the function
\mathrm{W}:\mathcal{G}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
\mathrm{W}(g)=d_{\mathcal{G}}(e,g)
is a monoidal weight on
\mathsf{G}
. The following result characterizes the associated
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance.

Proposition 3.24
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a group with right-invariant metric

. Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
acts transitively on a set
\mathcal{X}
and let

be the monoidal functor defined above. Let
\mathrm{W}

. Then, for all

This is a rather natural metric on
\mathcal{X}
: intuitively,
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance gives a measurement of the weight required to move one element of the set to another via the group action (cf. Remark

). This was the distance (

) described in the introduction as a motivation for our interleaving distance framework.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
. Since the action of

is transitive, an interleaving of

and

always exists. Indeed, for any

such that

we have the diagrams

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{g}T_{g^{-1}}(x)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{g}(y)=gy=x}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(\alpha_{e,e})_{x}=\beta_{e,x,x}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{e,x,x}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{e,x,x}}

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{g^{-1}}T_{g}(y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T_{g^{-1}}(x)=g^{-1}x=y}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T(\alpha_{e,e})_{y}=\beta_{e,y,y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{e,y,y}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta_{e,y,y}}

On the other hand, suppose that

\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(h)\}
of the
(g,h)

\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(g^{-1})\}
of the

. The formula for interleaving distance claimed in (

∎

Remark 3.25
.

Going through the proof, one sees that the requirement that
\mathrm{W}
be defined with respect to a right-invariant metric is not necessary. In fact, all that we really used is that
\mathrm{W}(g)=\mathrm{W}(g^{-1})
for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
. If
\mathrm{W}
is any monoidal weight with this property, then the proof shows that

d_{\Delta,\mathrm{W}}(x,y)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\{\mathrm{W}(g)\mid gx=y\}.

The following two subsections give some more specific examples of this result.

3.3.2.
Homogeneous Spaces

Consider a compact connected Lie group
\mathcal{G}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{G}
acts properly and transitively on a smooth manifold
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{X}
by first choosing a Riemannian structure on
\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{K}
so that the quotient map
\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{K}
is a submersion, and then transferring to
\mathcal{X}
via the above diffeomorphism. It is a fact

3.3.3.
Shape Analysis on Spaces of Landmarks and Embedded Submanifolds.

A central object of study in the fields of statistical shape shape analysis and medical imaging is the space

{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(or subgroups with various prescribed regularity conditions), together with the action of this space on
\mathrm{Emb}(\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})

is a discrete set of
k
points,
\mathrm{Emb}(\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is also known as the
configuration space
of
k
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In this subsection, we view shape analysis metrics on this configuration space through the lens of
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distances. Similar comparisons exist for more general embedding spaces, but we focus on the configuration space in order to keep the discussion more concrete.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{Diff}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
which is rich enough to act transitively on the set of configurations of
k
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(e.g., the group of compactly supported diffeomorphisms) and suppose that we have chosen a right-invariant Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{G}
. For technical reasons, such metrics are typically chosen in practice via a Hilbert space of time-dependent vector fields. That is, given smooth a path
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{G}
, the space derivative
\frac{d}{dx}\gamma
is a time-dependent vector field, so one can consider tangent vectors to
\mathcal{G}
as being determined by time-dependent vector fields; a Hilbert space structure on this space of vector fields can therefore be used to define a Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{G}
. The details of such constructions are beyond the scope of this paper, and are not necessary in what follows, but we refer the reader to
[
3
, Section 8]
for an overview of the topic. Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the associated Hilbert space with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
. We define the
geodesic weight
of a piecewise smooth path
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{G}
to be

\widehat{\mathrm{W}}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{d}{dx}\gamma(t)\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\;dt.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{Emb}(\mathcal{M},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, where
\mathcal{M}
is a collection of
k
points. Then
\mathcal{G}
acts on
\mathcal{X}
transitively. Let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{X}
be the monoidal functor from the monoidal category associated to
\mathcal{G}
to the action groupoid (Definitions
3.19
and
3.20
). We define a monoidal weight
\mathrm{W}
on
\mathsf{G}
by

\mathrm{W}(g)=\inf\left\{\widehat{\mathrm{W}}(\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}}\mid\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{G},\;\gamma(0)=e,\;\gamma(1)=g\right\}.

Then
[
3
, Equation (23)]
says that the geodesic distance between configurations
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
with respect to the Riemannian metric induced by the Riemannian metric on
\mathcal{G}
is given by

d(x,y)=\inf_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\{\mathrm{W}(g)\mid gx=y\}.

Observing that
\mathrm{W}(g)=\mathrm{W}(g^{-1})
, Remark
3.25
implies the following.

Corollary 3.27
.

The geodesic distance on the configuration space of
k
points in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, defined as above, can be expressed as a
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance.

3.3.4.
Fundamental Groupoid and Length Metrics

We now give an example of an interleaving distance arising in the more general 2-functor setting. We recover a familiar metric construction in an admittedly convoluted manner; this section is mainly intended to illustrate how the pieces of the 2-functor definition come together.

Fix a path connected topological space
\mathcal{X}
and let
\mathsf{G}=\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X})
denote its fundamental groupoid. We consider this as a 1-category by setting
\mathsf{G}_{0}=\mathcal{X}
and, for
x,y\in\mathcal{X}
, setting
\mathsf{G}_{1}(x,y)
to be the collection of homotopy classes of continuous paths from
x
to
y
in
\mathcal{X}
; we use the notation
x\xrightarrow{p}y
for such a homotopy class. Composition of two homotopy classes is the homotopy class of the concatenation of the underlying paths. We promote this to a 2-category through the following general construction.

Definition 3.28

(Indiscrete 2-Category)
.

Let
\mathsf{D}
be a 2-category. The
indiscrete 2-category associated to
\mathsf{D}
is the 2-category

\mathsf{E}\mathsf{D}

for each

. Horizontal and vertical composition laws are forced by uniqueness.

Let
\mathsf{C}=\mathsf{E}\mathsf{G}
be the indiscrete 2-category associated to the fundamental groupoid of

. We define a 2-functor

as follows:

(1)

For all

4.
Induced 2-Functors and Generalized Persistence Modules

We now provide new examples of interleaving distances in the context of TDA, arising from actions of monoids on posets. Doing so will require a Yoneda-like construction, which may be interesting in more general settings. Our main interest is in the
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving distance setting, with input data consisting of a monoidal functor. For the sake of generality, we give the Yoneda-like construction at the level of general 2-functors.

4.1.
Generalized Persistence Modules

Early work in TDA considered persistent homology primarily from a computational perspective, with the barcode invariant of a function defined in a rather combinatorial fashion
[
24
]
(although earlier works such as
[
17
]
already considered categorical aspects). The perspective that the fundamental objects in persistent homology theory are persistence modules was subsequently developed in
[
20
]
, and precisely formalized in categorical language in
[
15
]
. Much of the theory of TDA can be expressed at the level of
generalized persistence modules

[
13
]
, which are functors of the form
M:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Y}
, where
\mathsf{Y}
is an arbitrary category and
\mathsf{P}
is a
preorder category
; that is
\mathsf{P}_{0}=\mathcal{P}
, where
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
is a preordered set, or
proset
, and there is a unique morphism in
\mathsf{P}_{1}(p,q)
if and only if
p\leq q
. For convenience, we denote this morphism simply as
p\leq q
.

In the following, let
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{C},\mathsf{D})
denote the
1-category of functors
from a category
\mathsf{C}
to a category
\mathsf{D}
(i.e., objects are functors and morphisms are natural transformations). Then the objects of
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Y})
, with
\mathsf{P}
a preorder category, are generalized persistence modules. In
[
13
]
, two notions of distance are defined on the space of generalized persistence modules
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},{Y})_{0}
—more details of this construction are given in Section
4.4.1
. The main result of this section (Theorem
4.5
) shows that any monoid action on a poset gives rise to a family of interleaving distances on the space of generalized persistence modules (with fixed target category). To state and prove the result, we introduce a general construction for inducing new 2-functors from old.

4.2.
2-Functors Associated to a Fixed 2-Functor Together with an Auxiliary Category

Let
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be a 2-functor and fix a category
\mathsf{Y}\in(\mathsf{Cat})_{0}.
Let
\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}
denote the 2-category whose 1-morphisms are reversed (but whose 2-morphisms are not). We define an associated 2-functor
\Delta^{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
according to the following proposition. We leave the proof, which is straightforward, to the reader.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be a 2-functor and let
\mathsf{Y}
be some fixed 1-category. Then one obtains a well-defined 2-functor
\Delta^{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
with:

(1)

for
A\in\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathrm{op}}=\mathsf{C}_{0}
,
\Delta^{\mathsf{Y}}(A)=\mathsf{Fun}(\Delta(A),\mathsf{Y})
;

(2)

for
f\in\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\mathrm{op}}(A^{\prime},A)=\mathsf{C}_{1}(A,A^{\prime})
,

\Delta_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(H:\Delta(A^{\prime})\rightarrow\mathsf{Y})=H\circ\Delta_{f}:\Delta(A)\to\mathsf{Y}

and

\Delta_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(\lambda:H\Rightarrow H^{\prime})=\lambda\bullet\Delta_{f}:\Delta_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(H)\Rightarrow\Delta_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(H^{\prime});

(3)

for
\alpha\in\mathsf{C}_{2}^{\mathrm{op}}(f,g)=\mathsf{C}_{2}(f,g)
,
f,g\in\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\mathrm{op}}(A^{\prime},A)
,
H:\Delta(A^{\prime})\to\mathsf{Y}
,
X\in\Delta(A)_{0}
,

\big(\Delta^{\mathsf{Y}}(\alpha)_{H}\big)_{X}=H\circ\Delta(\alpha)_{X}.

Recall the correspondence between monoidal functors and 2-functors given by identifying
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
with
\mathsf{B}T:\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{Cat}
(Proposition
2.13
). Using the fact that
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}^{\mathrm{op}}=\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
, Proposition
4.1
has an immediate corollary in the monoidal functor setting.

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
be a monoidal functor and let
\mathsf{Y}
be some fixed 1-category. Then one obtains a well-defined monoidal functor
T^{\mathsf{Y}}:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}))
with:

(1)

for
f\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
,
T_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}\in\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{X},\mathsf{Y}))
is defined by

T^{\mathsf{Y}}_{f}(H:\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{Y})=H\circ T_{f}:\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{Y}

and

T_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(\lambda:H\Rightarrow H^{\prime})=\lambda\bullet T_{f}:T_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(H)\Rightarrow T_{f}^{\mathsf{Y}}(H^{\prime});

(2)

for
\alpha\in\mathsf{G}_{1}(f,g)
,
H:\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{Y}
,
X\in\mathsf{X}_{0}
,

\big(T^{\mathsf{Y}}(\alpha)_{H}\big)_{X}=H\circ T(\alpha)_{X}.

4.3.
Proset 2-Functors and Induced Interleaving Distances.

Returning to the setting of interleavings of generalized persistence modules, let
\mathcal{G}
be a monoid acting on a proset
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
. That is, for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
and
p\in\mathcal{P}
, there is an associated
g(p)\in\mathcal{P}
such that
h(g(p))=(hg)(p)
and
e(p)=p
, and the maps are
monotone
in the sense that
p\leq q
implies
g(p)\leq g(q)
.

Definition 4.3

(Proset Monoidal Category)
.

The
proset monoidal category associated to
\mathcal{G}
is the monoidal category
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
with:

(1)

\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}_{0}=G
, with product given by the monoid law of
G
.

(2)

For 1-morphisms
g,h
, let

\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}_{1}(g,h)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\{\alpha_{g,h}\}&\mbox{if $g(p)\leq h(p)$ for all $p\in\mathcal{P}$,}\\
\emptyset&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Composition is given by
\alpha_{h,k}\alpha_{g,h}=\alpha_{g,k}
; this is well-defined, since the existence of
\alpha_{g,h}
and
\alpha_{h,k}
imply
g(p)\leq h(p)\leq k(p)
for all
p
. The tensor product is given by
\alpha_{f,g}\otimes\alpha_{h,k}=\alpha_{fh,gk}
. To see that this is well-defined, observe that
h(p)\leq k(p)
and
f(p)\leq g(p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
. This implies that
fh(p)\leq gh(p)
and that
gh(p)\leq gk(p)
(since
g
is order-preserving), so we have
fh(p)\leq gk(p)
.

Definition 4.4

(Proset Action Monoidal Functor)
.

Let
\mathsf{P}
be the proset category associated to
\mathcal{P}
. We define the
proset action
monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{P})
as follows:

(1)

For
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
T_{g}:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{P}
is the functor defined on objects by
T_{g}(p)=g(p)
and on morphisms by
T_{g}(p\leq q)=g(p)\leq g(q)
.

(2)

For a morphism
\alpha_{g,h}
,
T(\alpha_{g,h}):T_{g}\Rightarrow T_{h}
is the natural transformation with component at
p\in\mathcal{P}
given by
T(\alpha_{g,h})_{p}=g(p)\leq h(p)
.

This leads to the main result of this section.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a proset with associated proset category
\mathsf{P}
and let
\mathsf{Y}
be some other category. Any action on
\mathcal{P}

.

Proof.

Let

be a monoid acting on

4.4.
Examples of 2-Functor Interleaving Distances for Generalized Persistence Modules

We now present some examples of interleaving distances arising from Theorem
4.5
.

4.4.1.
Bubenik-de Silva-Scott Interleaving Distances

We now fill in some details of the interleaving constructions of Bubenik, de Silva and Scott in
[
13
]
, first mentioned at the beginning of this section.

Definition 4.7

(
\omega
-Interleaving Distance
[
13
]
)
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
be a proset. A function
g:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}
is called
monotone
if
p\leq q
implies
g(p)\leq g(q)
. A monotone function
g
is called a
translation
if
p\leq g(p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
. The collection of translations of
\mathcal{P}
is denoted
\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}
; it is easy to show that
\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}
is a monoid. The identity is the identity map
e_{\mathcal{P}}
. A
sublinear projection
is a map
\omega:\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}\to[0,\infty]
such that
\omega(e_{\mathcal{P}})=0
and
\omega(h\circ g)\leq\omega(h)+\omega(g)
.

The above data leads to an extended pseudometric on
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},{Y})_{0}
. Given
M\in\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Y})_{0}
and
g\in\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}
, let
gM\in\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Y})_{0}
be the functor with
gM(p)=M(g(p))
and
gM(p\leq q)=M(g(p)\leq g(q))
. There is a natural transformation
\eta^{M}_{g}:M\Rightarrow gM
with component
(\eta^{M}_{g})_{p}:M(p)\rightarrow M(g(p))
induced by
p\leq g(p)
. We say that functors
M,N:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Y}
are
t
-interleaved with respect to
\omega
if there are translations
g
and
h
of
\mathcal{P}
with
\omega(g),\omega(h)\leq t
and natural transformations
\phi:M\Rightarrow gN
and
\psi:N\Rightarrow hM
such that the diagrams

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M(p)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M(gh(p))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{N(p)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{N(hg(p))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{N(g(p))}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{M(h(p))}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{p}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{g(p)}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M(p\leq gh(p))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{p}}

.

, the following result becomes clear.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
be a proset and let
\omega
be a sublinear family for
\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}
. Let
\mathsf{Y}
be an arbitrary category. Then the 2-functor interleaving distance
d_{T^{\mathsf{Y}},\mathrm{W}}
from Theorem

is equal to the
\omega
-interleaving distance

on
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Y})_{0}
when we take the monoid to be
\mathcal{G}=\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}
and the monoidal weight to be
\mathrm{W}=\omega
.

Remark 4.9
.

It is shown in
[
22
, Theorem 3.5]
that another Bubenik-de Silva-Scott construction of interleaving distance, depending on a structure called a
superlinear family of translations
, can be realized as a flow interleaving distance. While the interleaving distances induced by sublinear projections and superlinear families are sometimes related
[
13
, Theorem 3.24]
, the concepts are, in general, distinct.
We showed in Proposition
3.9
that flow interleaving distances can always be realized as 2-functor interleaving distances. Putting all of this together, we see that the
\mathsf{G}
-interleaving framework fully generalizes the interleaving distances of Bubenik, de Silva and Scott.

4.4.2.
Multiplication Group

Let
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
be the poset of non-negative real numbers. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the group of positive real numbers under multiplication. Then
\mathcal{G}
acts on
\mathcal{P}
(but does not contain and is not contained in the monoid of translations
\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}
). We define a monoidal weight
\mathrm{W}
on
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
by

\mathrm{W}(t)=|\log(t)|.

Then we obtain an interleaving distance on
d_{T^{\mathsf{Y}},\mathrm{W}}
on any space of generalized persistence modules
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Y})_{0}
.

Example 4.10
.

To see that the behavior of the induced interleaving distance is qualitatively different than that of classical interleaving distance, let us consider a basic example, where
\mathsf{Y}=\mathsf{Vec}
, the category of vector spaces over (say)
{\mathbb{R}}
. Functors
\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Vec}
are herein referred to as
classical persistence modules
, as they are the basic objects in the foundational theory of TDA
[
20
]
. Taking the sublinear projection
\omega:\mathrm{Trans}_{\mathcal{P}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
\omega(g)=\sup\{g(p)-p\mid p\in\mathcal{P}\}
yields the
classical interleaving distance
for classical persistence modules, as studied in
[
20
]
. Under mild tameness assumptions, this distance agrees with the
bottleneck distance

d_{\mathrm{B}}
between persistence diagrams (see, e.g.,
[
20
,
33
,
5
]
), which is defined as a more straightforward optimization problem and was an essential component in the early research on TDA
[
21
]
.

For
a\leq b
, let
M_{[a,b)}:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Vec}
denote the
interval module
with

M_{[a,b)}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}{\mathbb{R}}&\mbox{if $r\in[a,b)$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

and where
M_{[a,b)}(r\leq s)
is the identity map on
{\mathbb{R}}
whenever possible and is otherwise the zero map. If
a=b
,
M_{[a,a)}=M_{\emptyset}
, the persistence module which is identically zero. It is well-known that the classical interleaving distance (or bottleneck distance) between
M_{[a,b)}
and
M_{\emptyset}
is given by
d_{\mathrm{B}}(M_{[a,b)},M_{\emptyset})=\frac{b-a}{2}
. On the other hand, one can show that the
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
-interleaving distance is

d_{T^{\mathsf{Y}},\mathrm{W}}(M_{[a,b)},M_{\emptyset})=\frac{1}{2}(\log(b)-\log(a)).

The classical interleaving distance and
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
-interleaving distance behave qualitatively differently; for example

d_{\mathrm{B}}(M_{[a,a+1)},M_{\emptyset})=\frac{1}{2}

for all
a
, whereas

\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}d_{T^{\mathsf{Y}},\mathrm{W}}(M_{[a,a+1)},M_{\emptyset})=0.

Remark 4.11
.

We end this subsection with an informal remark, geared towards researchers dealing with practical applications of TDA. The above example is meant to be a simple expository one, and we do not advocate it as a serious replacement for standard metrics in TDA. However, even this toy example suggests the potential of exploring alternate metrics in a data analysis context. An interval module
M_{[a,b)}
is typically interpreted in a TDA setting as a persistent topological feature in a data set, subject to some notion of filtration (e.g., persistent homology barcodes of a Vietoris-Rips complex associated to point cloud data; see
[
18
]
). The distance from one of these interval modules to the zero module with respect to the bottleneck distance—i.e.,
\frac{1}{2}(b-a)
—can be understood as the “importance” of a feature. The behavior exhibited above by
d_{T^{\mathsf{Y}},\mathrm{W}}
says that the importance of a feature with respect to this metric not only depends on its persistence (i.e., distance between endpoints), but on the temporal location of the feature—that is, the “birth time”, or lower limit of the interval. The interleaving distance induced by the multiplication group says that features born near zero become infinitely important, while features with a fixed length have importance decaying to zero as the birth time increases. This agrees with the assertion of the recent paper
[
8
]
that the ratio
b/a
is a more relevant indicator of statistical significance of a topological feature
M_{[a,b)}
than the traditional persistence
\frac{1}{2}(b-a)
.

We believe that it would be useful to incorporate different importance dependencies into metrics, depending on the data analysis context. Such a focus on learning appropriate TDA metrics based on data structure is advocated for in, e.g.,
[
19
]
, and our framework gives a rich theory-backed source of such metrics. Designing data-driven metrics based on these generalized interleaving distances will be the subject of future research.

4.4.3.
Diffeomorphism Group of
{\mathbb{R}}

We now consider the case where
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
is the real line, but extend the class of monoids under consideration to diffeomorphism groups; these are well-studied by certain communities in geometric data science
[
43
,
3
,
44
,
38
,
4
]
, and the ideas presented here are meant to serve as a bridge between these techniques in TDA and geometric shape analysis (see also Section
3.3.3
).

Let
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
denote the group of orientation-preserving, compactly supported (i.e., differing from the identity map only on a compact set) smooth diffeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{R}}
and let
\mathcal{G}
be a subgroup of
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
. Then
\mathcal{G}
acts on
\mathcal{P}
(by
g\cdot p=g(p)
), so Theorem
4.5
implies that we obtain an interleaving distance on the space of generalized persistence modules once we have chosen a monoidal weight on
\mathcal{G}
. Recall from Proposition
3.23
that such a weight is induced by any right-invariant metric on
\mathcal{G}
. This discussion is summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
be the real line and let
\mathcal{G}
be a subgroup of the compactly-supported orientation-preserving diffeomorphism group
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
. Any choice of right-invariant metric on the diffeomorphism group gives rise to an interleaving distance on the space of generalized persistence modules
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Y})_{0}
, where
\mathsf{Y}
is any category.

Remark 4.13
.

A natural question is whether any right-invariant metrics on
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
exist, and, if so, whether they are computationally feasible to work with—this matter was discussed briefly for diffeomorphisms of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
in Section
3.3.3
, but we add more discussion here, since there are simpler right-invariant metrics in this one-dimensional setting. The details of the construction of these metrics and their computational approximation require substantial knowledge of functional analysis and optimization theory, and are beyond the scope of the present paper. We sketch briefly the main ideas below, but their development remains a direction of future research.

The group
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
is a Lie group, but it is infinite-dimensional, modeled locally on a Fréchet vector space
[
26
]
. There are several candidates for right-invariant metrics in the literature, and we describe one family of them here. Consider the weak (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
) Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
defined at a basepoint
g\in\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
and tangent vectors
x,y\in T_{g}\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
(the tangent space is isomorphic to the space of smooth maps
{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
which are equal to zero off a compact set
[
31
, Section 43.1]
) by

\langle x,y\rangle_{g}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}a_{j}\cdot D_{g}^{j}x(t)\cdot D_{g}^{j}y(t)\cdot g^{\prime}(t)dt,

where
D_{g}
is the operator
\frac{1}{g^{\prime}(t)}\frac{d}{dt}
,
k
is a nonnegative integer and the
a_{j}
’s are non-negative real number hyperparameters. Riemannian metrics of this form are referred to as
Sobolev-type
metrics, and are studied intensely by researchers interested in applications of Riemannian geometry to shape analysis
[
37
,
3
,
12
]
, although these works tend to focus on similar metrics on spaces of immersions of
{\mathbb{R}}
or the unit circle into a higher-dimensional Euclidean space. It is straightforward to check that geodesic distance with respect to this metric is a right-invariant metric on
\mathrm{Diff}_{c}({\mathbb{R}})
, as desired. Moreover, there has been substantial development of computational pipelines for computing geodesic distances for Sobolev-type metrics
[
2
]
(once again focusing on the setting of immersions into higher-dimensional spaces). The existence of theory and numerical pipelines for shape analysis applications suggest the potential for developing the application of these metrics to interleaving distances in future work.

Now consider the indiscrete 2-category
\mathsf{E}\mathsf{C}
(Definition
3.28
) with Lawvere 2-weight
\widehat{\mathrm{W}}
defined by setting
\widehat{\mathrm{W}}_{1}=\mathrm{W}
and setting
\widehat{\mathrm{W}}_{2}
to be identically zero. It is straightforward to prove that this recovers the symmetrized Lawvere construction:

Proposition 5.4
.

Using the notation above, the interleaving distance
d_{\mathsf{E}\mathsf{C},\widehat{\mathrm{W}}}
is equal to the symmetrized Lawvere metric
\hat{d}^{\mathrm{Law}}_{\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}}
.

Since any extended pseudometric can be realized via the Lawvere construction for some weighted 1-category, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.5
.

Any extended pseudometric can be represented as an interleaving distance associated to a 2-weighted 2-category.

Remark 5.6
.

In fact, any extended pseudometric space can be realized as a 2-functor interleaving distance or as an interleaving distance associated to a Locally Persistent Category (LPC)
[
41
]
(see Section
5.4
below for the definition)—constructions which we show in the following subsections to be generalized by 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance. We echo a sentiment expressed in
[
41
]
in the setting of LPCs: although any extended pseudometric can be realized as an interleaving distance in this somewhat trivial way, the interest in the interleaving distance construction comes from situations where it arises in a natural context or where it leads to notions of distance which are not obviously constructed in some other manner.

On the other hand, given a 2-weighted 2-category
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})
, there is an associated 1-category coming from the underlying 1-category, denoted (by an abuse of notation) as
\mathsf{C}
, with the underlying weight
\mathrm{W}_{1}
. The following result is obvious.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})
be a 2-weighted 2-category with associated weighted 1-category
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}_{1})
. Then, for any
A,B\in\mathsf{C}_{0}
,

\hat{d}_{\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}_{1}}^{\mathrm{Law}}(A,B)\leq d_{\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}}(A,B).

If
\mathrm{W}_{2}
is identically zero, then this is an equality.

Remark 5.8
.

Propositions
5.4
and
5.7
support our intuitive understanding of the interleaving distance associated to a 2-weighted 2-category: it is a generalization of the Lawvere categorical representation of a metric space, with the inclusion of additional obstructions on allowable morphisms between objects (in the form of weighted 2-morphisms).

We show below that other interleaving constructions can be realized in the 2-weighted 2-category framework; namely 2-functor interleaving distances in Section
5.3
and interleaving distances for Locally Persistent Categories in Section
5.4
. Before we move on to these generalities, we provide some specific examples of 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distances which illustrate the ideas above, focusing on the theme of Gromov-Hausdorff-type distances.

5.2.1.
Altered Gromov-Hausdorff Distance

Let us recall the basic construction of Gromov-Hausdorff distance. Let
\mathcal{X}=(\mathcal{X},d_{\mathcal{X}})
and
\mathcal{Y}=(\mathcal{Y},d_{\mathcal{Y}})
be metric spaces. The
Gromov-Hausdorff (GH) distance

[
16
]
between
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
is given by

(5.1)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{f,g}\max\{\mathrm{dis}(f),\mathrm{dis}(g),\mathrm{codis}(f,g)\},

where the infimum is defined over (not necessarily continuous) maps
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
, the
distortion
of
f
is defined as

\mathrm{dis}(f):=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}|d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))|,

with
\mathrm{dis}(g)
defined similarly, and the
codistortion
of
f
and
g
is

\mathrm{codis}(f,g):=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}}|d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,g(y))-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,f(x))|.

Intuitively, the distortion terms measure how much the maps distort the metric, whereas the codistortion measures how far
f
and
g
are from being inverses.

The Gromov-Hausdorff distance was characterized as an interleaving distance in a locally persistent category (see Section
5.4
) in
[
41
, Section 6.2]
. On the other hand, one might observe that the structure of GH distance is quite similar to that of an interleaving distance on a 2-weighted 2-category. Here, we make this observation precise by showing that a slight variant of GH distance is naturally realized in this context. We begin by altering the definition of GH distance to more directly reflect the intuition for its definition described above.

Definition 5.9
.

The
altered Gromov-Hausdorff distance
between metric spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
is

\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}):=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{f,g}\max\left\{\mathrm{dis}(f),\mathrm{dis}(g),\widetilde{\mathrm{codis}}(f,g)\right\},

where the infimum is over (not necessarily continuous) maps
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
,
\mathrm{dis}(f)
and
\mathrm{dis}(g)
are defined as above, and

\widetilde{\mathrm{codis}}(f,g)=\widetilde{\mathrm{codis}}_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}(f,g):=\max\left\{\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,gf(x)),\sup_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,fg(y))\right\}.

The altered version of GH distance is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to the classical version, as we now demonstrate.

Proposition 5.10
.

For any metric spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
,

\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq 2\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}).

Proof.

Let
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathrm{dis}(f)
,
\mathrm{dis}(g)
and
\mathrm{codis}(f,g)
are all less than or equal to
r
for some
r\geq 0
. Then, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}
,

d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,gf(x))=d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,gf(x))-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(f(x),f(x))\leq r,

where we have used the codistortion bound, taking
y=f(x)
. Likewise,
d_{\mathcal{Y}}(y,fg(y))\leq r
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, and these statements impy that
\widetilde{\mathrm{codis}}(f,g)\leq r
. This proves that
\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.

To show the other inequality, suppose we have
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{X}
with
\mathrm{dis}(f)
,
\mathrm{dis}(g)
and
\widetilde{\mathrm{codis}}(f,g)
all bounded above by
r
. Then, for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,g(y))

\displaystyle\leq d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,gf(x))+d_{\mathcal{X}}(gf(x),g(y))

\displaystyle\leq r+d_{\mathcal{Y}}(f(x),y)+r,

where we have used the bounds on distortion and the altered codistortion. It follows that
d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,g(y))-d_{\mathcal{Y}}(f(x),y)\leq 2r
and a similar argument shows that
d_{\mathcal{Y}}(f(x),y)-d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,g(y))\leq 2r
, so that
\mathrm{codis}(f,g)\leq 2r
. Therefore
d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})\leq 2\tilde{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.
∎

We now realize the altered Gromov-Hausdorff distance as a 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance. The 2-category

(2)

(note that this is different than the usual categorical structure on the space of metric spaces, where one typically considers Lipschitz maps);

(3)

for functions

,

(a)

if

,
\mathsf{GH}_{2}(f,f)
contains a single abstract identity 2-morphism

;

(b)

if

and

contains a single abstract 2-morphism, denoted

—if this coincides with the previous case, we have

;

(c)

in any other case,
\mathsf{GH}_{2}(f,g)
is empty.

(together with Propositions

and

).

5.2.2.
The Gromov-Hausdorff Bicategory

The Gromov-Hausdorff distance is categorified and generalized in
[
14
]
, using the formalism of bicategories (see Remark
2.5
). Describing the construction in detail would require a significant detour, so we only very briefly summarize it here, and then remark on the obvious connection to the notion of 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance.

It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
) that the GH distance can alternatively be realized as

(5.2)

d_{\mathrm{GH}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})=\inf_{\mathcal{Z},f,g}d^{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{H}}(f(X),g(Y)),

where the infimum is over all metric spaces
\mathcal{Z}
and isometric embeddings
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
and
g:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
, and where
d^{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathrm{H}}
denotes the Hausdorff distance between subsets of
\mathcal{Z}
.

Inspired by the formulation (
5.2
) of GH distance, the
Gromov-Hausdorff bicategory

[
14
, Section 5]

\mathsf{WEmb}
is defined to be a bicategory whose objects are small categories endowed with Lawvere weights. A 1-morphism between
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}),(\mathsf{C}^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime})\in\mathsf{WEmb}_{0}
is an
embedding pair
, which is a triple
\big((\mathsf{C}^{\prime\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime\prime}),F,F^{\prime}\big)
, where
(\mathsf{C}^{\prime\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime\prime})
is a weighted category,
F:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{C}^{\prime\prime}
and
F^{\prime}:\mathsf{C}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{C}^{\prime\prime}
are full and faithful functors which are injective on objects, and which preserve weights. A generalized notion of Hausdorff distance can be associated to an embedding pair, and this is used as a Lawvere weight for
\mathsf{WEmb}
. There is a natural notion of a morphism between embedding pairs, and these form the 2-morphisms for
\mathsf{WEmb}
, but we omit a detailed description here.

The Lawvere metric resulting from the construction of
\mathsf{WEmb}
described above gives a generalized, categorified notion of Gromov-Hausdorff distance between weighted 1-categories. We remark that, in light of Proposition
5.7
, this generalized GH distance can essentially be viewed as an interleaving distance associated to a 2-weighted 2-category (by extending the Lawvere weight to be zero on 2-morphisms). The qualification in this statement could be removed by extending the definitions and results above to handle 2-weighted bicategories.

\left((\nabla\circ F)(\alpha)\bullet\eta_{A}\right)_{X}=(\nabla\circ F)(\alpha)_{\eta_{A}(X)}.
Similarly, the natural transformation on the right-hand side of Diagram
5.5
may be realized as the whiskering of
\Delta(\alpha)
and
\eta_{B}
:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Delta(A)}

For
X\in\Delta(A)_{0}
, the

5.3.2.
Naturality of
(\mathsf{C}^{\Delta},\mathrm{W}^{\Delta})

We now show that the map
\Delta\mapsto(\mathsf{C}^{\Delta},\mathrm{W}^{\Delta})
defined in Definition
5.13
is functorial and induces an isometry of interleaving distances. We first introduce an appropriate notion of a morphism between (1 or 2)-weighted (1 or 2)-categories.

Definition 5.15

(Lipschitz Functors)
.

A
Lipschitz 2-functor
from one 2-weighted 2-category
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})
to another
(\mathsf{C}^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime})
is a 2-functor
\Theta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{C}^{\prime}
such that

(5.7)

\mathrm{W}_{1}^{\prime}(\Theta(g))\leq\mathrm{W}_{1}(g)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{W}_{2}^{\prime}(\Theta(\alpha))\leq\mathrm{W}_{2}(\alpha)

for each 1-morphism
g
and 2-morphism
\alpha
of
\mathsf{C}
. A
Lipschitz functor
of 1-categories endowed with Lawvere weights is defined similarly.

Let
\mathsf{W2Fun}(\cdot,\mathsf{Cat})
denote the category whose objects are 2-functors
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
from a weighted 2-category
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})
to
\mathsf{Cat}
, and whose morphisms are pairs
(F,\eta)
as in Definition
5.14
, such that
F
is Lipschitz. Let
\mathsf{W2Cat}
denote the category whose objects are 2-weighted 2-categories and whose morphisms are Lipschitz 2-functors.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})
be a weighted category. The map taking a 2-functor
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
to the 2-weighted 2-category
(\mathsf{C}^{\Delta},\mathrm{W}^{\Delta})
defines a functor
\mathsf{W2Fun}(\cdot,\mathsf{Cat})\to\mathsf{W2Cat}
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
and
\nabla:\mathsf{D}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be 2-functors,
F:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{D}
a 2-functor, and
\eta:\Delta\Rightarrow\nabla\circ F
a 2-natural transformation, satisfying the conditions of Definition
5.14
.
From the data
(F,\eta)
, we produce a 2-functor
F_{*}:\mathsf{C}^{\Delta}\to\mathsf{D}^{\nabla}
.

Beginning with objects, we have
\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\Delta}=\text{Im}(\Delta)
and
\mathsf{D}^{\nabla}_{0}=\text{Im}(\nabla)
. Let
X\in\Delta(A)_{0}
for
A\in\mathsf{C}_{0}
. We have a functor
\eta_{A}:\Delta(A)\to\nabla(F(A))
, so define
F_{*}(X)=\eta_{A}(X)
.

To extend
F_{\ast}
to 1-morphisms, let
X,Y\in\text{Im}(\Delta)
, so that
X\in\Delta(A)_{0}
and
Y\in\Delta(B)_{0}
. Let
(g,\phi):X\to Y
be a morphism in
\mathsf{C}^{\Delta}
, with
g:B\to A
and
\phi:X\to\Delta_{g}(Y)
. Then
F(g):F(B)\to F(A)
and

\eta_{A}(\phi):\eta_{A}(X)\to\eta_{A}(\Delta_{g}(Y))=(\nabla\circ F)_{g}(\eta_{B}(Y))=\nabla_{F(g)}(\eta_{B}(Y)).

Using the definition of
\mathsf{D}^{\nabla}
, the corresponding map is
(F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi)):\eta_{A}(X)\to\eta_{B}(Y)
, and we define

F_{*}(g,\phi)=(F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi)).

Using the commutativity properties given in the diagrams above, one checks that
F_{*}
preserves compositions of 1-morphisms. If
(g,\phi):X\to Y
and
(h,\psi):Y\to Z
are morphisms in
\mathsf{C}^{\Delta}
, their composition is given by
(gh,\Delta_{g}(\psi)\circ\phi)
, and thus

\begin{array}[]{rl}F_{*}(h,\psi)\circ F_{*}(g,\phi)&=(F(h),\eta_{B}(\psi))\circ(F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi))\\
&=\left(F(g)F(h),\nabla_{F(g)}(\eta_{B}(\psi))\circ\eta_{A}(\phi)\right)\\
&=\left(F(gh),\nabla_{F(g)}(\eta_{B}(\psi))\circ\eta_{A}(\phi)\right)\\
&=\left(F(gh),(\nabla\circ F)_{g}(\eta_{B}(\psi))\circ\eta_{A}(\phi)\right)\\
&=\left(F(gh),\eta_{A}(\Delta_{g}(\psi))\circ\eta_{A}(\phi)\right)\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{by }(\ref{diagm:1-comm})\\
&=\left(F(gh),\eta_{A}(\Delta_{g}(\psi)\circ\phi)\right)\\
&=F_{*}(gh,\Delta_{g}(\psi)\circ\phi)\end{array}

We treat the definition of
F_{*}
on 2-morphisms by cases, following , part (
3
) of Definition
5.13
. In the first case, we have a single abstract 2-morphism
\widehat{\psi}\in\mathsf{C}_{2}^{\Delta}\left((1_{A},1_{X}),(h,\psi)\right)
. Mapping to
\mathsf{D}^{\nabla}
, we have
F_{*}(1_{A},1_{X})=(F(1_{A}),\eta_{A}(1_{X}))=(1_{F(A)},1_{\eta_{A}(X)})
and
F_{*}(h,\psi)=(F(h),\eta_{A}(\psi)).
By definition, there is a single abstract 2-morphism
\widehat{\eta_{A}(\psi)}\in\mathsf{D}^{\nabla}_{2}((1_{F(A)},1_{\eta_{A}(X)}),(F(h),\eta_{A}(\psi))).
We thus define

F_{*}(\widehat{\psi})=\widehat{\eta_{A}(\psi)}.

In the second case, we have a single abstract element
1_{g,\phi}\in\mathsf{C}_{2}^{\Delta}((g,\phi),(g,\phi))
, and a single abstract element
1_{F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi)}\in\mathsf{D}^{\nabla}_{2}((F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi),(F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi))
. Define

F_{*}(1_{g,\phi})=1_{F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi)}.

Composition of 2-morphisms is preserved by
F_{*}
. As stated in Definition
5.13
, it suffices to treat horizontal compositions. Let
\widehat{\phi}:(1_{A},1_{X})\Rightarrow(g,\phi)
and
\widehat{\psi}:(1_{A},1_{X})\Rightarrow(k,\psi)
be 2-morphisms. We wish to show that
F_{*}(\widehat{\psi}\bullet\widehat{\phi})=F_{*}(\widehat{\psi})\bullet F_{*}(\widehat{\phi})
. The horizontal composition law for 2-morphisms given in Definition
5.13
, determined by Equation
5.3
, yields

(5.8)

\widehat{\Delta(\beta)_{X}}\bullet\widehat{\Delta(\alpha)_{X}}=(\Delta_{g}(\Delta(\beta))_{X})\circ\Delta(\alpha)_{X})^{\wedge},

(5.9)

((\nabla\circ F)(\beta)_{X})^{\wedge}\bullet((\nabla\circ F)(\alpha)_{X})^{\wedge}=((\nabla\circ F)_{g}((\nabla\circ F)(\beta)_{\eta_{A}(X)})\circ(\nabla\circ F)(\alpha)_{\eta_{A}(X)})^{\wedge}.

Using these facts along with Equation
5.6
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}F_{*}(\widehat{\psi}\bullet\widehat{\phi})&=F_{*}(\widehat{\omega})\\
&=\widehat{\eta_{A}(\omega)}\\
&=\eta_{A}(\Delta_{g}(\Delta(\beta)_{X})\Delta(\alpha)_{X})^{\wedge}\hskip 116.65646pt\mbox{by }(\ref{eqn:2morph_comp})\\
&=(\eta_{A}(\Delta_{g}(\Delta(\beta)_{X}))\circ\eta_{A}(\Delta(\alpha)_{X}))^{\wedge}\\
&=((\nabla\circ F)_{g}(\eta_{A}(\Delta(\beta)_{X}))\circ(\nabla\circ F)_{g}(\alpha)_{\eta_{A}(X)})^{\wedge}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{by }(\ref{eqn:2morcomp})\\
&=((\nabla\circ F)_{g}((\nabla\circ F)(\beta)_{\eta_{A}(X)})\circ(\nabla\circ F)(\alpha)_{\eta_{A}(X)})^{\wedge}\hskip 10.81218pt\mbox{by }(\ref{eqn:2morcomp})\\
&=((\nabla\circ F)(\beta)_{\eta_{A}(X)})^{\wedge}\bullet((\nabla\circ F)(\alpha)_{\eta_{A}(X)})^{\wedge}\hskip 43.2483pt\mbox{by }(\ref{eqn:2morph_comp_nabF})\\
&=(\eta_{A}(\Delta(\beta)_{X}))^{\wedge}\bullet(\eta_{A}(\Delta(\alpha)))^{\wedge}\hskip 94.17854pt\mbox{by }(\ref{eqn:2morcomp})\\
&=\widehat{\eta_{A}(\psi)}\bullet\widehat{\eta_{A}(\phi)}\\
&=F_{*}(\widehat{\psi})\bullet F_{*}(\widehat{\phi})\end{array}

The association
(F,\eta)\mapsto F_{*}
is functorial. Let
\Delta_{1}:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
,
\Delta_{2}:\mathsf{D}\to\mathsf{Cat}
and
\Delta_{3}:\mathsf{E}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be 2-functors, and let
(F,\eta):\Delta_{1}\to\Delta_{2}
and
(G,\nu):\Delta_{2}\to\Delta_{3}
be morphisms. The composition
(G,\nu)\circ(F,\eta)=(G\circ F,(\nu\bullet F)\circ\eta)
determines the 2-functor
(G\circ F)_{*}:\mathsf{C}^{\Delta_{1}}\to\mathsf{E}^{\Delta_{3}}
, and we have
(G\circ F)_{*}=G_{*}\circ F_{*}
.
Indeed, we verify this on objects

\begin{array}[]{rl}(G\circ F)_{*}(X)&=((\nu\bullet F)\circ\eta)_{A}(X)=\nu_{F(A)}(\eta_{A}(X))=G_{*}(\eta_{A}(X))=G_{*}(F_{*}(X)),\end{array}

on 1-morphisms

\begin{array}[]{rl}(G\circ F)_{*}(g,\phi)&=((G\circ F)(g),((\nu\bullet F)\circ\eta)_{A}(\phi))\\
&=(G(F(g)),\nu_{F(A)}(\eta_{A}(\phi)))\\
&=G_{*}(F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi))=G_{*}(F_{*}(g,\phi)),\end{array}

and on 2-morphisms

\begin{array}[]{rl}(G\circ F)_{*}(\widehat{\psi})&=(((\nu\bullet F)\circ\eta)_{A}(\psi))^{\wedge}\\
&=((\nu\bullet F)_{A}\circ\eta_{A}(\psi))^{\wedge}\\
&=((\nu_{F(A)}\circ\eta_{A})(\psi))^{\wedge}=G_{*}(\widehat{\eta_{A}(\psi)})=G_{*}(F_{*}(\widehat{\psi})).\end{array}

We have established that
(F,\eta)
naturally defines a 2-functor
F_{*}
, so it only remains to show that this 2-functor is Lipschitz. Since
F
is (1-)Lipschitz, we have
\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{D}}(F(g))\leq\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{C}}(g)
for all
g\in\mathsf{C}_{1}
. Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rl}\left(\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{D}}^{\nabla}\right)_{1}(F_{*}(g,\phi))&=\left(\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{D}}^{\nabla}\right)_{1}((F(g),\eta_{A}(\phi)))\\
&=\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{D}}(F(g))\leq\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{C}}(g)=\left(\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{C}}^{\Delta}\right)_{1}((g,\phi)).\end{array}

By definition,
\left(\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{C}}^{\Delta}\right)_{2}=\left(\mathrm{W}_{\mathsf{D}}^{\nabla}\right)_{2}=0,
so there is nothing to check.
∎

Lemma 5.17
.

Let
\Delta:\mathsf{C}\to\mathsf{Cat}
be a 2-functor, let
\mathrm{W}
be a Lawvere weight on
\mathsf{C}
and let
\mathsf{C}^{\Delta}
and
\mathrm{W}^{\Delta}
be defined as above. Then the 2-functor interleaving distance on
\mathrm{Im}(\Delta)=\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\Delta}
agrees with the 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance,
d_{\Delta,\mathrm{W}}=d_{\mathsf{C}^{\Delta},\mathrm{W}^{\Delta}}
.

Proof.

Let
X,Y\in\mathrm{Im}(\Delta)=\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\Delta}
. Unpacking the definitions, there is a
(g,h)
-interleaving (in the sense of 2-functor interleaving) of
X
and
Y
if and only if there is a
(g,h,\alpha,\beta)
-interleaving (in the sense of weighted 2-categories). Moreover,

\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(h)\}=\max\{\mathrm{W}^{\Delta}_{1}(g),\mathrm{W}^{\Delta}_{1}(h),\mathrm{W}^{\Delta}_{2}(\alpha),\mathrm{W}^{\Delta}_{2}(\beta)\},

so this completes the proof.
∎

From Lemmas
5.16
and
5.17
, we immediately deduce the following result.

Theorem 5.18
.

The map from Definition
5.13
induces a functor
\mathsf{W2Fun}(\cdot,\mathsf{Cat})\to\mathsf{W2Cat}
and an isometry at the level of interleaving distances.

5.4.
Locally Persistent Categories

In
[
41
]
, Scoccola introduces an alternate perspective on interleaving distances through the lens of enriched categories. We will show that these interleaving distances can be realized in our 2-category setting. In what follows, we use the font
\mathfrak{D}
to denote an enriched 1-category.

Definition 5.19

(Locally Persistent Category
[
41
]
)
.

A
locally persistent category (LPC)
is a 1-category
\mathfrak{D}
enriched in
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{Set})
, where
\mathsf{R}
is the poset category associated to the nonnegative reals
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
and
\mathsf{Set}
is the category of sets. Explicitly, this entails the following data:

(1)

A class of objects
\mathfrak{D}_{0}
.

(2)

For each
A,B\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}
and each
s\geq 0
, a set of morphisms
\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}
.

(a)

For
A=B
and
s=0
, there is a distinguished identity morphism
1_{A}\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,A)_{0}
.

(b)

Compositions of morphisms respect the
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
-grading in the sense that
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}
and
h\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(B,C)_{t}
compose to give
hg\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,C)_{s+t}
.

(3)

For
s\leq t
, there is a
shift operation

\mathrm{S}_{s,t}:\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}\rightarrow\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{t}
.

This data is required to satisfy various natural axioms; we refer to
[
41
, Definition 3.11]
for details.

Interleaving distance in the LPC setting is defined as follows.

Definition 5.20

(LPC Interleaving Distance
[
41
]
)
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}
be an LPC and let
A,B\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}
. We say that
A
and
B
are
(s,t)
-interleaved
if there exist
f\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}
and
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(B,A)_{t}
such that
gf=\mathrm{S}_{0,s+t}(1_{A})
and
fg=\mathrm{S}_{0,s+t}(1_{B})
.

The
LPC interleaving distance
between
A
and
B
is

d_{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)=\inf\{\max\{s,t\}\mid\mbox{$A$ and $B$ are $(s,t)$-interleaved}\}.

Next we will show that
d_{\mathfrak{D}}
is equivalent to a 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance. We will show that the connection between these distances is natural, in a precise sense.

5.4.1.
From LPCs to 2-Categories

We now fit the notion of LPCs into the framework of 2-categories, using a construction similar to that of
\mathsf{GH}
above.

Definition 5.21

(2-Category Associated to an LPC)
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}
be an LPC. We define a 2-category
\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}
consisting of the following data:

(1)

\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{0}=\mathfrak{D}_{0}
.

(2)

For
A,B\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}
,

\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}(A,B)=\{(g,s)\mid g\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}\}.

Compositions are defined by

(h,t)\circ(g,s)=(hg,s+t)

and the identity morphism is
(1_{A},0)
.

(3)

For
(g,s),(h,t)\in\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}(A,B)
, the class of 2-morphisms
\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}(g,h)
contains a unique 2-morphism, denoted
\alpha_{g,h}
, if and only if
s\leq t
and
\mathrm{S}_{s,t}(g)=h
. Vertical and horizontal compositions are defined, respectively, by the formulas

\alpha_{g,h}\alpha_{f,g}=\alpha_{f,h}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\alpha_{h,k}\bullet\alpha_{f,g}=\alpha_{hf,kg}.

One can use the axioms of an LPC
[
41
, Definition 3.11]
to deduce that these compositions are well-defined.

The 2-category defined above inherits a Lawvere 2-weight.

Lemma 5.22
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}
be an LPC with associated 2-category
\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}
. Let
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}:\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the function
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}((g,s))=s
and let
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}:\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be constantly zero. Then
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}=(\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1},\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{2})
is a Lawvere 2-weight.

Proof.

The properties of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{2}
are trivial, so we only need to check the properties of
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}
.
By definition,
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}((1_{A},0))=0
. For
(g,s)\in\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\mathfrak{D}}(A,B)
and
(h,t)\in\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\mathfrak{D}}(B,C)
, we have that

\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}((h,t)\circ(g,s))=\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}((gh,s+t)=s+t=\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}((g,s))+\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}((h,t)).

∎

Similar to the treatement we gave in the 2-functor setting, we now wish to show that the construction of Definition
5.21
is functorial. Let
\mathfrak{D}
and
\mathfrak{E}
be locally persistent categories. Following
[
41
, Definition 3.16]
, we define the appropriate notion of morphism between LPCs as follows. A
locally persistent functor

F:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{E}
is a functor which is compatible with the
\mathbb{R}
-grading on morphism sets and which commutes with the shift functor (Defintion
5.19
). More precisely, for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the functor
F
induces a map on morphism sets

\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}\to\mathfrak{E}_{1}(F(A),F(B))_{s},

and for any
s\leq t
and
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}
, we have
\mathrm{S}_{s,t}(F(g))=F(\mathrm{S}_{s,t}(g))
. Let
\mathsf{LPC}
denote the category whose objects are LPC’s and whose morphisms are locally persistent functors.

Lemma 5.23
.

The map taking an LPC
\mathfrak{D}
to the 2-weighted 2-category
(\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}},\mathrm{W}^{\mathsf{D}})
defines a faithful functor
\mathsf{LPC}\to\mathsf{W2Cat}
.

Proof.

Given LPC’s
\mathfrak{D}
,
\mathfrak{E}
, and a LP-functor
F:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{E}
, we define
F_{*}:\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}\to\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{E}}
as follows. On objects,
F_{*}
is given by
F
since
\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{0}=\mathfrak{D}_{0}
and
\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{E}}_{0}=\mathfrak{E}_{0}
. Given a morphism
(g,s):A\to B\in\mathsf{C}_{1}^{\mathfrak{D}}
, so that
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}
, we have
F(g)\in\mathfrak{E}_{1}(F(A),F(B))_{s}
. We thus define

F_{*}((g,s))=(F(g),s):F(A)\to F(B)\in\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}.

Composition of 1-morphisms is preserved. Indeed, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}F_{*}((h,t)\circ(g,s))&=F_{*}(hg,s+t)=(F(hg),s+t)\\
&=(F(h)\circ F(g),t+s)=(F(h),t)\circ(F(g),s)=F_{*}((h,t))\circ F_{*}((g,s)).\end{array}

There is a 2-morphism
\alpha_{g,h}\in\mathsf{C}_{2}^{\mathfrak{D}}((g,s),(h,t))
if and only if
s\leq t
and
\mathrm{S}_{s,t}(g)=h
. Since
F
commutes with shifts, this yields
\mathrm{S}_{s,t}(F(g))=F(\mathrm{S}_{s,t}(g))=F(h),
and so we have a 2-morphism
\alpha_{F(g),F(h)}\in\mathsf{C}_{2}^{\mathfrak{E}}((F(g),s),(F(h),t))
. We define

F_{*}(\alpha_{g,h})=\alpha_{F(g),F(h)}.

Then vertical composition and horizontal compositions are preserved:

\begin{array}[]{rl}F_{*}(\alpha_{g,h}\alpha_{f,g})&=F_{*}(\alpha_{f,h})=\alpha_{F(f),F(h)}=\alpha_{F(g),F(h)}\alpha_{F(f),F(g)}=F_{*}(\alpha_{g,h})F_{*}(\alpha_{f,g})\end{array}

and

\begin{array}[]{rl}F_{*}(\alpha_{h,k}\bullet\alpha_{f,g})&=F_{*}(\alpha_{hf,kg})=\alpha_{F(hf),F(kg)}\\
&=\alpha_{F(h)F(f),F(k)F(g)}=\alpha_{F(h),F(k)}\bullet\alpha_{F(f),F(g)}=F_{*}(\alpha_{h,k})\bullet F_{*}(\alpha_{f,g}).\end{array}

This association is functorial; that is, given
F:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{E}
and
G:\mathfrak{E}\to\mathfrak{F}
, we have
(G\circ F)_{*}=G_{*}\circ F_{*}
. Indeed, this is clear on objects, on 1-morphisms, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}(G\circ F)_{*}(g,s)&=(G(F(g)),s)=G_{*}(F(g),s)=G_{*}(F_{*}(g,s))=(G_{*}\circ F_{*})(g,s)\end{array}

and on 2-morphisms, we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}(G\circ F)_{*}(\alpha_{g,h})&=\alpha_{G(F(g)),G(F(h))}=G_{*}(\alpha_{F(g),F(h)})=(G_{*}\circ F_{*})(\alpha_{g,h}).\end{array}

Moreover, this functor is faithful. To see this, let
F,G:\mathfrak{D}\to\mathfrak{E}
be LP-functors with
F_{*}=G_{*}:\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}\to\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{E}}
. Then
F(A)=G(A)
for all
A\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}=\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{0},
and
F_{*}((g,s))=G_{*}((g,s))
for all
(g,s)\in\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}.
This second statement implies
(F(g),s)=(G(g),s)
, so that
F(g)=G(g)
. Since
F
and
G
agree on objects and morphisms, we must have
F=G
.

It only remains to check that
F_{\ast}
is Lipschitz. Indeed, a stronger condition holds, as we have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}(F_{*}(g,s))&=\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{E}}_{1}((F(g),s))=s=\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{1}((g,s)).\end{array}

∎

Lemma 5.24
.

Let
\mathfrak{D}
be an LPC and let
\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}}
and
\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}
be defined as above. Then the LPC interleaving distance on
\mathfrak{D}_{0}=\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{D}}
agrees with the 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance,
d_{\mathfrak{D}}=d_{\mathsf{C}^{\mathfrak{D}},\mathrm{W}^{\mathfrak{D}}}
.

Proof.

This follows immediately from the construction, as
A,B\in\mathfrak{D}_{0}=\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{D}}
are
(s,t)
-interleaved via
f\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(A,B)_{s}
and
g\in\mathfrak{D}_{1}(B,A)_{t}
, in the sense of LPC interleaving distance, if and only if they are
\big((f,s),(g,t),\alpha_{f,g},\alpha_{g,f}\big)
-interleaved, in the sense of 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distance.
∎

From Lemmas
5.23
and
5.24
, we deduce the following result.

Theorem 5.25
.

The map from Definition
5.21
induces a faithful functor
\mathsf{LPC}\to\mathsf{W2Cat}
and an isometry at the level of interleaving distances.

6.
Stability

An important property of the topological invariants of datasets studied in TDA is that they are stable under perturbations of the data. The prototypical quantification of this statement is the main theorem of
[
21
]
, stated here informally: for a topological space
\mathcal{X}
, continuous maps
f,g:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, and persistence diagrams
D(f)
and
D(g)
obtained from degree-
k
sublevel set persistent homology, we have (under mild tameness assumptions)

(6.1)

d_{\mathrm{B}}(D(f),D(g))\leq\|f-g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})},

where
d_{\mathrm{B}}
denotes bottleneck distance (see Example
4.10
).

A common theme in the literature on interleaving distances is the idea that certain stability results of TDA can be viewed naturally from the categorical perspective; for example, see
[
22
, Theorem 4.3]
,
[
41
, Theorem 4.2.2]
,
[
13
, Theorem 3.16]
and
[
14
, Theorem 4.6]
. Stability in the setting of 2-weighted 2-categories is very natural, and more general than other notions in the literature, in light of the results of the previous section. We give a brief overview of stability and some applications in this section.

6.1.
Stability for 2-Weighted 2-Categories

Recall that
\mathsf{W2Cat}
denotes the 1-category whose objects are small 2-weighted 2-categories and whose morphisms are Lipschitz 2-functors. Let
\mathsf{EPMet}
denote the 1-category whose objects are extended pseudometrics and whose morphisms are Lipschitz functions.

Theorem 6.1

(Stability)
.

The map taking a 2-weighted 2-category
(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})
to the extended pseudometic space
(\mathsf{C}_{0},d_{\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}})
defines a functor from
\mathsf{W2Cat}
to
\mathsf{EPMet}
. In particular, for any Lipschitz 2-functor of 2-weighted 2-categories
\Theta:(\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W})\to(\mathsf{C}^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime})
, we have

d_{\mathsf{C}^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime}}(\Theta(A),\Theta(B))\leq d_{\mathsf{C},\mathrm{W}}(A,B)

for all
A,B\in\mathsf{C}_{0}
.

Proof.

We only need to check the Lipschitz inequality. Given a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces h}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1_{A}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces hg}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces gh}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1_{B}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\alpha}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\beta}

in
\mathsf{C}
, we obtain a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(B)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(A)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(B)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(g)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(h)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(1_{A})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(hg)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(gh)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(1_{B})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(\alpha)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Theta(\beta)}

or, equivalently,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Theta(A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\Theta(B)}}

in

. By the Lipschitz property (

) of

The claim follows.

6.2.
Stability for Monoidal Functors

Since most examples of interest of interleaving distances in this paper arise from monoidal functors, let us show how to apply Theorem
6.1
in this context. Let
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
and
T^{\prime}:\mathsf{G}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X}^{\prime})
be monoidal functors and suppose that
\mathsf{G}
and
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
are endowed with monoidal weights
\mathrm{W}
and
\mathrm{W}^{\prime}
, respectively.

Definition 6.2
.

An
equivariant Lipschitz functor pair
is a pair
(H,K)
consisting of a Lipschitz monoidal functor
H:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
and a functor
K:\mathsf{X}\to\mathsf{X}^{\prime}
such that, for all
g\in\mathsf{G}_{0}
,
K\circ T_{g}=T^{\prime}_{H(g)}\circ K
.

Recall from Proposition
2.13
that a monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
can be promoted to a 2-functor into
\mathsf{Cat}
via the delooping construction. Suppose that
\mathsf{G}
is endowed with a monoidal weight
\mathrm{W}
; this can then be considered as a weight on the 1-category
\mathsf{B}\mathsf{G}
. We slightly abuse notation and denote the 2-weighted 2-category associated to this 2-functor data (Definition
5.13
) as
(\mathsf{C}^{T},\mathrm{W}^{T})
.

Proposition 6.3
.

An equivariant Lipschitz functor pair
(H,K)
from
T:\mathsf{G}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X})
to
T^{\prime}:\mathsf{G}^{\prime}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{X}^{\prime})
induces a Lipschitz 2-functor from
(\mathsf{C}^{T},\mathrm{W}^{T})
to
(\mathsf{C}^{T^{\prime}},\mathrm{W}^{T^{\prime}})
. It follows that, for all
X,Y\in\mathsf{X}_{0}
, we have the Lipschitz bound on interleaving distances

d_{T^{\prime},\mathrm{W}^{\prime}}(K(X),K(Y))\leq d_{T,\mathrm{W}}(X,Y).

Proof.

We define a 2-functor
\Theta:\mathsf{C}^{T}\to\mathsf{C}^{T^{\prime}}
by first defining it on objects via the observation that
\mathsf{C}^{T}_{0}=\mathsf{X}_{0}
, hence we may define
\Theta(X)=K(X)
. The definition of
\Theta
on 1 and 2-morphisms requires more work, and follows below.

Let
(g,\phi):X\to Y
be a morphism of
\mathsf{C}^{T}
; that is, we have
\phi:X\to T_{g}(Y)
in
\mathsf{X}
. Define
\Theta((g,\phi)):\Theta(X)\to\Theta(Y)
by
\Theta((g,\phi))=(H(g),K(\phi))
. This is well-defined: indeed, it is the case that
K(\phi)
is a morphism from
\Theta(X)=K(X)
to
T_{H(g)}(\Theta(Y))=T_{H(g)}(K(Y))
, since, definitionally,
K(\phi):K(X)\to K(T_{g}(Y))
, and
K(T_{g}(Y))=T_{H(g)}^{\prime}(K(Y))
, by equivariance. Finally, we need to show that this preserves identities and compositions. Identity preservation follows immediately by the assumption that
H
is a monoidal functor and
K
is a functor, since
\Theta((e,1_{X}))=(H(e),K(1_{X}))=(e^{\prime},1_{K(X)})
, where
e^{\prime}
denotes the identity object in
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
. To establish preservation of compositions, consider
(g,\phi):X\to Y
and
(h,\psi):Y\to Z
. Then, using (monoidal) functoriality and equivariance, we have

\displaystyle\Theta((h,\psi)\circ(g,\phi))

\displaystyle=\Theta((gh,T_{g}(\psi)\circ\phi))=(H(gh),K(T_{g}(\psi)\circ\phi))

\displaystyle=(H(g)H(h),K(T_{g}(\psi))\circ K(\phi))=(H(g)H(h),T_{H(g)}^{\prime}(K(\psi))\circ K(\phi))

\displaystyle=(H(h),K(\psi))\circ(H(g),K(\phi)).

Now consider a 2-morphism in
\mathsf{C}^{T}_{2}((g,\phi),(h,\psi))
. There are two cases to consider:
(g,\phi)=(h,\psi)
and the 2-morphism is of the form
1_{g,\phi}
, or
g=e
,
\phi=1_{X}
, and the 2-morphism is of the form
\hat{\psi}
. In the former case, define
\Theta(1_{g,\phi})=1_{H(g),K(\psi)}
, and in the latter, define
\Theta(\hat{\psi})=\widehat{K(\psi)}
. Verification that this is well-defined, and that it preserves identities and compositions is similar to the work done in the previous paragraph.

It is obvious, by definition, that
\Theta
satisfies the Lipschitz property (
5.7
), so this completes the proof. The last statement of the proposition then follows from Lemma
5.17
and Theorem
6.1
.
∎

6.3.
Sublevel Set Persistent Homology

A common application of category-theoretic metrics in TDA is to illustrate the stability under perturbations of various flavors of persistent homology; see, for example, the statement at the beginning of this section, from
[
21
]
, or related results in
[
20
,
13
,
22
]
. We extend these results to the setting of prosets with monoid actions that we considered in Section
4
. Our general approach is similar to those previously explored in the literature, so we give a somewhat terse exposition here; please see the above references for more details.

Let
(\mathcal{P},\leq)
be a proset with associated category
\mathsf{P}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
is endowed with an action by a group
\mathcal{G}
with associated proset monoidal category
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
and proset action monoidal functor
T:\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{P})
(Definitions
4.3
and
4.4
). Finally, suppose that
\mathcal{G}
is endowed with a monoidal weight
\mathrm{W}
.

Consider the (1-)category of functions from some fixed topological space
\mathcal{X}
into
\mathcal{P}
, denoted
\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})
—the objects of this category are functions
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}
, and there is a unique morphism from
\phi
to
\psi
, denoted
\phi\leq\phi
, if
\phi(x)\leq\psi(x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Let
\mathsf{Top}
denote the category of topological spaces with continuous maps and let
\mathsf{Vec}=\mathsf{Vec}_{\mathbb{k}}
denote the category of vector fields over some fixed category
\mathbb{k}
with linear maps. We now define several additional monoidal functors; we leave it to the reader to check that these are well-defined.

(1)

Let
T^{\prime}:\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P}))
be defined as follows:

(a)

for
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}
,
T^{\prime}_{g}(\phi):\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}
is the map
T^{\prime}_{g}(\phi)(x)=g(\phi(x))
—in other words,
T^{\prime}_{g}(\phi)=g\circ\phi
;

(b)

for a morphism
\alpha_{g,h}
in
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
(i.e.,
g(p)\leq h(p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
),
T^{\prime}(\alpha_{g,h}):T^{\prime}_{g}\Rightarrow T^{\prime}_{h}
has component at
\phi
given by
T^{\prime}(\alpha_{g,h})_{\phi}=g\circ\phi\leq h\circ\phi
.

(2)

Let
\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}
denote the opposite category of
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}
; there is a unique morphism denoted
\overline{\alpha}_{g,h}:g\to h
if and only if
g(p)\geq h(p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
T^{\prime\prime}:\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Top}))
be defined as follows:

(a)

for
g\in\mathcal{G}
and a functor
F:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Top}
,
T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(F)
is the functor defined by
T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(F)(p)=F(g^{-1}(p))
and
T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(F)(p\leq q)=F(g^{-1}(p)\leq g^{-1}(q))
; for a natural transformation
\beta:F\Rightarrow G
of functors
F,G:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Top}
,
T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(\beta)
has component at
p
given by
T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(\beta)_{p}=\beta_{g^{-1}(p)}
, the component of
\beta
at
g^{-1}(p)
;

(b)

for a morphism
\overline{\alpha}_{g,h}
in
\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}
,
T^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\alpha}_{g,h}):T^{\prime\prime}_{g}\Rightarrow T^{\prime\prime}_{h}
has component at
F:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Top}
given by the natural transformation

T^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\alpha}_{g,h})_{F}:T_{g}^{\prime\prime}(F)\Rightarrow T_{h}^{\prime\prime}(F)

with component at
p\in\mathcal{P}
given by

\big(T^{\prime\prime}(\overline{\alpha}_{g,h})_{F}\big)_{p}=F(g^{-1}(p)\leq h^{-1}(p))

(note that
g(p)\geq h(p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
implies
g^{-1}(p)\leq h^{-1}(p)
).

(3)

Let
T^{\prime\prime\prime}:\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Vec}))
be defined similarly to
T^{\prime\prime}
(observe that the definition of
T^{\prime\prime}
didn’t use the assumption that the target in the functor category was
\mathsf{Top}
in any meaningful way, so that the definition extends in general to a monoidal functor
\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathsf{End}(\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{C}))
for any category
\mathsf{C}
).

We then define two functors which link the constructions above.

(1)

The
sublevel set filtration functor

S:\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})\to\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Top})
is defined as follows.

(a)

For
\phi\in\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})_{0}
,
S(\phi):\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Top}
is the functor with

(i)

for
p\in\mathcal{P}=\mathsf{P}_{0}
,
S(\phi)(p)=\phi^{-1}(\{r\in\mathcal{P}\mid r\leq p\})\subset\mathcal{X}
, endowed with the subspace topology, so that
S(\mathcal{X},f)(p)\in\mathsf{Top}_{0}
;

(ii)

For the morphism
p\leq q
in
\mathsf{P}_{1}(p,q)
,

S(\phi)(p\leq q):\phi^{-1}(\{r\in\mathcal{P}\mid q\leq p\})\hookrightarrow\phi^{-1}(\{q\in\mathcal{P}\mid r\leq q\})

is the inclusion map.

(b)

For a morphism
\phi\leq\psi
in
\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})
,
S(\phi\leq\psi):S(\phi)\Rightarrow S(\psi)
is the natural transformation with component at
p\in\mathcal{P}
given by inclusion

S(\phi\leq\psi)_{p}:\phi^{-1}(\{r\in\mathcal{P}\mid r\leq p\})\to\psi^{-1}(\{r\in\mathcal{P}\mid r\leq p\});

this is well-defined due to the assumption that
\phi\leq\psi
and it is easy to see that the requisite diagram commutes in order for this to define a natural transformation.

(2)

For any choice of degree
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
H_{n}:\mathsf{Top}\to\mathsf{Vec}
denote the
degree-
n
homology functor over the field
\mathbb{k}
. This induces the
degree-
n
persistent homology functor

PH_{n}:\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Top})\to\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{P},\mathsf{Vec})
:

(a)

for a functor
F:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Top}
, we define
PH_{n}(F)=H_{n}\circ F

(b)

for a natural transformation
\beta:F\Rightarrow G
,
H_{n}(\beta):H_{n}(F)\Rightarrow H_{n}(G)
has component at
p\in P
given by
H_{n}(\beta)_{p}=H_{n}(\beta_{p})
.

We now apply these constructions to generalize the classical TDA stability result (
6.1
). Let
1_{\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}}
denote the identity functor on
\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}
and let
O:\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\to\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}
be the
opposite functor
which is the identity on objects and which takes
\alpha_{g,h}
to
\overline{\alpha}_{h,g}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

With the notation above, we have the following statements.

(1)

(O,S)
is an equivariant Lipschitz functor pair for
T^{\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime}
.

(2)

(1_{\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}}},PH_{n})
is an equivariant Lipschitz functor pair for
T^{\prime\prime}
and
T^{\prime\prime\prime}
, for any degree
n
.

(3)

Interleaving distance for sublevel set persistent homology is stable, in the sense that for any functions
\phi,\psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{P}
,

d_{T^{\prime\prime\prime}}(PH_{n}\circ S(\phi),PH_{n}\circ S(\psi))\leq d_{T^{\prime\prime}}(S(\phi),S(\psi))\leq d_{T^{\prime}}(\phi,\psi).

(4)

The interleaving distance on
\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})
can be expressed as

d_{T^{\prime}}(\phi,\psi)=\inf\{\max\{\mathrm{W}(g),\mathrm{W}(h)\}\mid\phi(x)\leq g(\psi(x)),\;\psi(x)\leq h(\phi(x))\;\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X}\}.

(5)

When
\mathcal{P}={\mathbb{R}}
with
\mathrm{W}(p)=|p|
, the above results recover classical stability of sublevel set persistent homology (
6.1
).

Proof.

To prove point (1), we need to show that for all
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
S\circ T^{\prime}_{g}=T^{\prime\prime}_{g}\circ S
. First, let
\phi\in\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{P})_{0}
. For any
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

S(T^{\prime}_{g}(\phi))(p)=S(g\circ\phi)(p)=(g\circ\phi)^{-1}\big(\{r\in\mathcal{P}\mid r\leq p\}\big)=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid g(\phi(x))\leq p\}

and

\displaystyle T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(S(\phi))(p)=S(\phi)(g^{-1}(p))

\displaystyle=\phi^{-1}\big(\{r\in\mathcal{P}\mid r\leq g^{-1}(p)\}\big)

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid\phi(x)\leq g^{-1}(p)\}=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid g(\phi(x))\leq p\},

so that
S\circ T^{\prime}_{g}=T^{\prime\prime}_{g}\circ S
at the object level. A similar computation shows that the equivariance property holds at the morphism level. Since
\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}_{0}=(\big(\mathsf{G}^{\mathcal{P}}\big)^{\mathrm{op}})_{0}=\mathcal{G}
share the same monoidal weight, the Lipschitz property is obvious, so this proves point (1).

Point (2) also only requires us to show the equivariance property. For a functor
F:\mathsf{P}\to\mathsf{Top}
and point
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we have

PH_{n}(T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(F))(p)=H_{n}\big(T^{\prime\prime}_{g}(F)(p)\big)=H_{n}\big(F(g^{-1}(p))\big)

and

T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{g}(PH_{n}(F))(p)=PH_{n}(F)(g^{-1}(p))=H_{n}\big(F(g^{-1}(p))\big).

Equivariance at the level of morphisms follows similarly.

Point (3) is a direct corollary of points (1) and (2) and Proposition
6.3
. Point (4) follows by unpacking the definitions.

Finally, we prove point (5). By point (4), the interleaving distance on
\mathsf{Map}(\mathcal{X},{\mathbb{R}})
is given by

d_{T^{\prime}}(\phi,\psi)=\inf\{\max\{|t|,|s|\}\mid\phi(x)\leq\psi(x)+t,\,\psi(x)\leq\phi(x)+s\,\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X}\},

and this is equal to
\|\phi-\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}
by an elementary argument. The objects
PH_{n}\circ S(\phi)
and
PH_{n}\circ S(\psi)
are standard (one parameter) persistent homology modules, and we claim that
d_{T^{\prime\prime\prime}}
is the standard interleaving distance on persistence modules in
\mathsf{Fun}(\mathsf{R},\mathsf{Vec})
(i.e., the one described in the introduction in equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
)), where
\mathsf{R}
is the poset category associated to
{\mathbb{R}}
. Indeed, an argument similar to the one used in the proof of Proposition
3.9
shows that it suffices to consider
(u,u)
-interleavings (rather than general
(u,v)
-interleavings). Such an interleaving of persistence modules
M,N:\mathsf{R}\to\mathsf{Vec}
consists of natural transformations
\phi:M\to T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{u}N
and
\psi:N\to T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{u}M
such that the diagrams

(6.2)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{2u}M}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{u}N}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\alpha}_{0,2u})_{M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{u}(\psi)}
and
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{2u}N}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{u}M}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{\prime\prime\prime}(\overline{\alpha}_{0,2u})_{N}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{\prime\prime\prime}_{u}(\phi)}

commute. The existence of
\overline{\alpha}_{0,2u}
means that
u\leq 0
; let us make the change of variables
t=-u\geq 0
. The fact that
\phi
and
\psi
are natural transformations then imply the existence of commutative diagrams (
1.1
) for all
r\leq s
. Moreover, after unraveling the definitions, we see that the diagrams (
6.2
) imply the existence of diagrams (
1.2
) for all

. Putting this together, we get that standard interleaving distance between the sublevel set persistent homology modules is bounded above by
\|f-g\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})}
, which yields an upper bound on bottleneck distance under reasonable tameness conditions, by standard results of TDA
[
20
]
.
∎

7.
Discussion

This paper establishes the fundamental theory for some novel generalizations of interleaving distance, suggesting several directions for future research; we briefly discuss some of them here. First, we note that this work concerns the theory of interleaving distance, but was inspired by ideas coming from applied fields such as TDA and statistical shape analysis. A major question is whether any of the metrics considered here lead to computationally feasible methods. For example, can interleaving distances based on diffeomorphism group actions on 1-parameter persistence modules be computed using barcode decompositions and numerical methods from shape analysis? One of our initial motivations for developing this framework was to relate these distances to
p
-Wasserstein distances between barcodes, with a view toward a categorical proof of
p
-Wasserstein stability (a version of which was recently proved via combinatorial and algebraic methods in
[
42
]
). At this point, however, this remains an open question. There are also many possibilities for theoretical extensions of these ideas. One obvious direction is to generalize our constructions and results to lax or bicategory settings. Another natural idea is to extend the 2-weighted 2-category interleaving distances to higher categories. These generalizations should be motivated by natural applications, which we believe could be useful for studying (bounded, coherent) derived categories of schemes and their higher-categorical counterparts. Work of Neeman
[
39
]
highlights the utility of metrics in relation to categorical constructions in the triangulated setting.
