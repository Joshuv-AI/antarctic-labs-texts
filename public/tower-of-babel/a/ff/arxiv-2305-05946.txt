On the impact of noise on quennching for a nonlocal diffusion model driven by a mixture of Brownian and fractional Brownian motions
Nikos I. Kavallaris, Christos V. Nikolopoulos, Athanasios N. Yannacopoulos

Abstract
. In this paper, we study a stochastic parabolic problem involving a nonlocal diffusion operator associated with nonlocal Robin-type boundary conditions. The stochastic dynamics under consideration are driven by a mixture of a classical Brownian and a fractional Brownian motions with Hurst index H ∈ ( 1 2 , 1 ) . H\in(\frac{1}{2},1). We first establish local in existence result of the considered model and then explore conditions under which the resulting SPDE exhibits finite-time quenching. Using the probability distribution of perpetual integral functional of Brownian motion as well as tail estimates of fractional Brownian motion we provide analytic estimates for certain statistics of interest, such as quenching times and the corresponding quenching probabilities. The existence of global in time solutions is also investigated and as a consequence a lower estimate of the quenching time is also derived. Our analytical results demonstrate the non-trivial impact of the considered noise on the dynamics of the system. Next, a connection of a special case of the examined model is drawn in the context of MEMS technology. Finally, a numerical investigation of the considered model for a fractional Laplacian diffusion and Dirichlet-type boundary conditions is delivered.



Key words:
Nonlocal diffusion, Brownian motion, fractional Brownian motion, exponential functionals, quenching, global existence, SPDEs, MEMS.

Mathematics Subject Classification:
Primary: 60G22, 60G65, 60H15, 35R60; Secondary: 65M06, 35A01, 60J60.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we consider the following nonlocal stochastic semilinear parabolic problem

(1.1)

\displaystyle dz=\left(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}z-g(x,z)\right)dt-zdN_{t},\quad x\in D,\;t>0,\quad

(1.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}z(x,t)+\beta(x)z(x,t)=0,\quad x\in D^{c}:=\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus D,\;t>0,

(1.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq z(x,0)=z_{0}(x)<1,\quad x\in D,

where
\mathscr{L}
is an integral (nonlocal) operator of the fractional Laplacian type and
\mathscr{N}
is the corresponding nonlocal Neumann-type operator whose forms are specified in Section
2
. Also,
N_{t}
is a stochastic process which is a mixture of the Wiener process and the fractional Brownian motion with a Hurst index of
H>1/2
and
g
is a nonlinear function such that
g(x,z)\to-\infty
as
z\to 0
whereas
g(x,z)>0
for
z
large enough. A possible function
g
with this behavior can be
g(x,z)=\lambda\zeta(x)z^{-2}-\gamma z
for
\gamma,\lambda,\zeta(x)>0
. The choice of this asymptotic behaviour for
g
in the limit as
z\to 0
is so that the model, in its deterministic form induces quenching behaviour whereas for
z
sufficiently large non vanishing solutions can be maintained.

System (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) is a rather general model that may be used to study the effects of spatio-temporal nonlocality and noise on quenching in nonlinear problems.
The motivation for the introduction of the integral (nonlocal) diffusion operator and the noise terms is so that we may study the combined effects of nonlocal spatio-temporal processes on the quenching behaviour of nonlinear stochastic PDEs; the considered nonlocal diffusion term modelling spatial nonlocal effects and the noise term
N_{t},
as a mixture of the Wiener process and the fractional Brownian motion, modelling the combined effects of stochasticity and temporal nonlocal effects.
Furthermore, the choice of the noise term is so that in the limit where quenching happens
z\to 0
, the noise term will not dominate over the dynamics of the model and sweep them away under the effects of noise. On the other hand, before quenching is about to happen, i.e., when
z
is not close to
0
, the effects of the noise term may be such as to drive the system away from the quenching regime (note that
N_{t}
has a symmetric distribution around
0
).

In Section

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The nonlocal operators
\mathscr{L}
and
\mathscr{N}

By
\mathscr{L}
we denote the nonlocal operator

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}u(x):=p.v.\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(u(x)-u(y))k(x,y)dy,\,\,\,\,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

where
k:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus{\rm diag}\to[0,\infty)
is measurable kernel satisfying

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Lambda}^{-1}\nu(x-y)\leq k(x,y)\leq\widetilde{\Lambda}\nu(x-y),\,\,\,\,\,x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\;\widetilde{\Lambda}>0,

for some
\nu:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\to[0,\infty)
that is the density of a symmetric Lévy measure.
Moreover,
{\mathscr{N}}
is the nonlocal analogue of the Neumann operator, defined by

(2.2)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{N}}u(y):=\int_{\Omega}(u(y)-u(x))k(x,y)dx,\,\,\,\,\,y\in D^{c}.

In the special case where
k(x,y)=\nu(x-y)
the operator
\mathscr{L}
is translation invariant and the generator of a symmetric Lévy process. This is the case we will focus on for this work. For the choice
k(x,y)=\nu(x-y)=C_{d,\alpha}|x-y|^{-d-2\alpha}
, for
\alpha\in(0,1)
the corresponding operator
\mathscr{L}
reduces to the fractional Laplacian,
\mathscr{L}=(-\Delta)^{\alpha}
. The usual choice of the constant
C_{d,\alpha}
is

C_{d,\alpha}:=\frac{\alpha 2^{2\alpha}\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}+\alpha\right)}{\pi^{2\alpha+\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
with
\Gamma
being the Euler function. For this choice the operator
\mathscr{L}
converges (in the appropriate sense) to the standard Laplacian operator
-\Delta
in the limit as
\alpha\to 1
.

2.1.1.
Variational formulation and relevant function spaces

Consider the bilinear forms

\displaystyle{\mathscr{E}}(u,v):=\int_{(D^{c}\times D^{c})^{c}}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))k(x-y)dxdy,

and

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,v):={\mathscr{E}}(u,v)+\int_{D^{c}}\beta(y)u(y)v(y)dy.

As shown in
[
26
]
, the bilinear form
Q_{\beta}
is related to the nonlocal Robin problem

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}u=f,\,\,\,\mbox{in}\,D

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}u+\beta u=0,\,\,\,\mbox{on}\,\,D^{c}.

The connection between the two comes in terms of the variational formulation of (
2.3
)-(
2.4
)

(2.5)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,v)=\int_{D}f(x)v(x)dx,\,\,\,\,\forall\,\,v\in V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\cap L^{2}(D^{c};\beta),

where

\displaystyle V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d}):=\left\{u:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}\,\,\,mes.\,\,:\,\,\left.u\right|_{D}\in L^{2}(D),\,\,\,|u|_{V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<\infty\right\}

and

\displaystyle|u|_{V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}=\int_{D}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(u(x)-u(y))^{2}\nu(x-y)dxdy.

This space can be turned into a Hilbert space when equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}^{2}+|u|_{V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{2}.

Concerning the Robin coefficient
\beta(x)
we will assume that
\beta(x)>0
and that
\beta\tilde{\nu}^{-1}:D^{c}\to[0,\infty)
is essentially bounded and non-trivial (as in
[
26
]
).
The above weak formulation follows from the nonlocal Gauss-Green formula

\displaystyle\int_{D}\mathscr{L}u(x)v(x)dx={\mathscr{E}}(u,v)-\int_{D^{c}}{\mathscr{N}}u(y)v(y)dy,\,\,\,\,\forall\,u,v\in C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d}),

and the density of
C_{c}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
in
V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
(for
D
such that
\partial D
is compact and Lipschitz; see in
[
26
, Theorem 2.11]
).

2.1.3.
The semigroup generated by
\mathscr{L}

The operator
\mathscr{L}
with the corresponding nonlocal boundary conditions can be shown to generate a strongly continuous semigroup on an appropriate
L^{2}
space. There are two possible approaches to the choice of the required
L^{2}
space.

In the first approach (see e.g.
[
9
]
for the case of the fractional Laplacian) we may modify the boundary condition (
1.2
) to

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}u+\beta u=0,\,\,\,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\bar{D}

and then we define an extension of a function
u:D\to{\mathbb{R}}
to a function
\bar{u}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in such a way so that the external boundary condition is automatically satisfied. Note that the modified boundary condition carries no information as to what happens for
x\in\partial D
but this is of little importance if we are only interested in considering
u
as an
L^{2}(D)
object since
meas(\partial D)=0
.
For the case of the Robin boundary condition studied here the corresponding extension can be defined as

(2.10)

\displaystyle\bar{u}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}u(x)&x\in D,\\
\frac{1}{\rho(x)+\beta(x)}\int_{D}u(y)k(x,y)dy&x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus D,\end{array}\right.

where
\rho(x):=\int_{D}k(x,y)dy
and let
\mathscr{I}_{R}
be the corresponding extension operator defined by
\mathscr{I}_{R}u=\bar{u}
. It can easily be seen (see e.g. in
[
9
, Lemma 3.15]
for the case of the fractional Laplacian) the
\bar{u}
satisfies the exterior boundary condition on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\setminus\bar{D}
, for any
u
. Moreover,
u
is well defined for
u\in L^{2}(D)
(note again that
meas(\partial D)=0
). We can then define the bilinear form

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{R}(u,v)=Q_{\beta}(\bar{u},\bar{v})

which is well defined on

\displaystyle D(\mathscr{E}_{R})=\{u\in L^{2}(D)\,\,\mid\,\,\bar{u}\in V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\}.

To this bilinear form we can associate an operator
\mathscr{L}_{R}
defined as in terms of the weak formulation of the problem (
2.3
) with the (modified) Robin boundary condition (
2.9
) as follows: Consider any
u\in L^{2}(D)
, such that its Robin extension
\bar{u}=\mathscr{I}_{R}u
as defined by (
2.10
) satisfies the weak formulation of (
2.3
) with boundary condition (
2.9
) for some function
f\in L^{2}(D)
: Then we define the operator
\mathscr{L}_{R}
in terms of

\displaystyle L^{2}(D)\ni u\mapsto\mathscr{L}_{R}u:=f,\,\,\,\mbox{where}\,\,\,\bar{u}=\mathscr{I}_{R}u\,\,\,\mbox{solves}\,\,\,Q_{\beta}(\bar{u},\bar{v})=\langle f,v\rangle,\,\,\,\forall\,v\in L^{2}(D),\,\,\bar{v}=\mathscr{I}_{R}v.

The above definition indicates that the appropriate domain for this operator will be

\displaystyle D(\mathscr{L}_{R})=\{u\in L^{2}(D)\,\,|\,\,\bar{u}\in V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\,\,\exists\,f\in L^{2}(D)\,\,s.t.\,\,\bar{u}\,\,\mbox{is a weak solution of}\,\,\eqref{A1}\,\mbox{with}\,\eqref{BC-MOD}\}.

It can be shown (see in
[
9
, Theorem 3.18]
for the fractional Laplacian) that the form
\mathscr{E}_{R}
is closed, symmetric and densely defined on
L^{2}(D)
, hence the corresponding operator
\mathscr{L}_{R}
is a self adjoint operator generating a strongly continuous semigroup
\mathscr{T}_{t}^{R}=e^{-t{\mathscr{L}}_{R}}
on
L^{2}(D)
which moreover is positivity preserving and can be extended to a contraction semigroup on
L^{p}(D)
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
, and is strongly continuous for
p\in[1,\infty)
.

In the second approach (see
[
26
]
) we do not consider directly the idea of a Robin extension but rather consider the problem on the whole of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and making sure that the exterior condition holds in terms of the weak formulation (
2.5
). It can then be shown (see in
[
26
, Theorem 2.25]
) that the bilinear form
(\mathscr{E},V_{\nu}(D|{\mathbb{R}}^{d}))
is a regular Dirichlet form on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};\nu^{\prime})
where
\nu^{\prime}
is any of the measures

\displaystyle\tilde{\nu}(x)=\int_{B}\min(1,\nu(x-y))dy,

\displaystyle\bar{\nu}(x)=ess\inf_{y\in B}\nu(x-y),

\displaystyle\nu^{*}(x)=\nu(R(1+|x|)),

where
B\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is bounded and open and
R>1
is an arbitrary fixed number. The need for the weight is so that the asymptotic behaviour of the function is controlled, since now we consider the function
u
as a function on the whole of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and not just on
D
as above.
Here again, by the properties of Dirichlet forms the operator
\mathscr{L}
generates an operator semigroup
\mathscr{T}_{t}=e^{-t\mathscr{L}}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};\nu^{\prime})
, which is strongly continuous and can be extended to a contraction semigroup on
L^{p}(D;\nu^{\prime})
for all
p\in[1,\infty]
(by the Beurling-Deny conditions).

For the needs of this work we may consider either of the above semigroups which will be invariably denoted by
\mathscr{T}^{*}_{t}
acting on
L^{2}
(which may be either
L^{2}(D)
or
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d};\nu^{\prime})
).

Moreover, we note that here we are interested in the family of operators
-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}
. This family of operators generates a evolution family as follows:

Define the functions

(2.11)

\displaystyle K(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}k^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau,\;A(t):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau,

(2.12)

\displaystyle K(t,s):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}k^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau,\quad A(t,s):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{s}^{t}a^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau

We may then define the evolution family
\mathscr{T}_{t,s}
by its action

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}_{t,s}f=\mathscr{T}^{*}_{K(t,s)}f,

on any
f\in L^{2}
. Using this evolution family we may express the solution of the nonhomogeneous system

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}u+f,

\displaystyle u(0)=\phi,

in terms of

\displaystyle u(t)=\mathscr{T}_{t}\phi+\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{T}_{t,s}f(s)ds,

where we use the simplified notation
\mathscr{T}_{t}=\mathscr{T}_{t,0}
.

2.2.
The noise term

The noise term
N_{t}
is of mixed form and it is defined as follows:

(2.13)

\displaystyle N_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}a(s)dB_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)dB^{H}_{s},

for some positive functions
a(s),b(s),
where
B_{t}
and
B^{H}_{t}
stand for the one-dimensional, real-valued Brownian and fractional Brownian motions, defined on a stochastic basis
\{\Omega,\,{\mathscr{F}},\,{\mathscr{F}}_{t},\,\mathbb{P}\}
with filtration
\left({\mathscr{F}}_{t}\right)_{t\in[0,T]}
and Hurst index
\frac{1}{2}<H<1
.

2.2.1.
Fractional Brownian motion and its properties

Recall that the fractional Brownian motion
(B_{t}^{H},\,\,t\geq 0)
is a Gaussian process with the properties

(i)

B_{0}^{H}=0
a.s.  ,

(ii)

{\mathbb{E}}[B_{t}^{H}]=0,

(iii)

{\mathbb{E}}[B_{s}^{H}B_{t}^{H}]=\frac{1}{2}(t^{2H}+s^{2H}-|t-s|^{2H})=:R_{H}(t,s)
,
t,s>0.

The above properties uniquely characterize the fractional Brownian motion in terms of its characteristic function

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda;t)={\mathbb{E}}[e^{i\langle\lambda,t\rangle}]={\mathbb{E}}[e^{i\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k},t_{k}}]=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle C_{t}\lambda,\lambda\rangle},

where the matrix
C_{t}:=\bigg({\mathbb{E}}[B_{t_{i}}^{H}B_{t_{j}}^{H}]\bigg)_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. For the choice
H=1/2
we recover the standard Brownian motion. The fractional Brownian motion is a self similar process (in the sense that for any
a>0
the processes
(B_{t}^{H},\,\,t\geq 0)
and
(a^{-H}B_{at}^{H},\,\,\,t\geq 0)
have the same distribution) and has stationary increments, which are uncorrelated for
H=1/2
, positively correlated for
H\in(1/2,1)
and negatively correlated for
H\in(0,1/2)
. In this work we will focus on positively corellated regime
H\in(1/2,1)
in which the process
(X_{n}\,\,:n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{:}=(B_{n+1}^{H}-B_{n}^{H},\,\,\,n\in{\mathbb{N}})
displays long-range dependence, which corresponds to an aggregation behaviour useful for describing clustering phenomena in various applications ranging from physical phenomena to economics (see e.g.
[
57
,
29
]
).
Note that for fractional Brownian motions with
\frac{1}{2}<H<1
it holds that
d\langle B_{t}^{H},B_{t}^{H}\rangle=0
. On the other hand, for the standard Brownian motion
d\langle B_{t},B_{t}\rangle=dt
.

2.2.2.
Stochastic integration with respect to fractional Brownian motion for
H>1/2

The fractional Brownian motion
(B_{t}^{H},\,\,\,t\geq 0)
is not a semimartingale for
H\neq 1/2
. For the process
(B_{t}+B_{t}^{H},\,\,\,t\geq 0)
though, it is interesting to note that when
H\in(3/2,1)
it is equivalent in law to Brownian motion.

Eventhough the fractional Brownian motion is not a semimartingale a theory for stochastic integration can be developed in parallel to the corresponding theory for the Wiener process. The development of stochastic integration differs between the cases where
H<1/2
and
H>1/2
; here we will focus in the latter case. We assume throughout the rest of the paper that
H>1/2
.
Moreover, there are different notions available for stochastic integration over the fBM, depending on the type of integrand in consideration. Various notions of stochastic integration over fBM include the pathwise integration, or the Maliavin calculus approach involving the Skorokhod integral. Different notions of stochastic integration result to integrals with different properties so we need to specify the particular concept of stochastic integration used to define the corresponding SPDE since the begining. In this paper we will use the pathwise approach.

The pathwise stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}dB_{t}^{H}
can be defined for a process
(u_{t}\,\,\,t\geq 0)
with
\beta
-Hölder continuous trajectories, for
\beta>1-H
in the sense of Young integration as the limit of the Riemann-Stieltjes sums. Let us consider the sequence of partitions
\pi_{n}=\{0=t_{0}^{n}<t_{1}^{n}<\cdots<t_{k_{n}}^{n}=T\}
such that
|\pi_{n}|\to 0
for
n\to\infty
. The pathwise stochastic integral is defined as

(2.14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}u_{t}dB^{H}_{t}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=1}^{k_{n}}u_{t_{k-1}^{n}}\left(B_{t_{k}^{n}}-B_{t_{k-1}^{n}}\right).

The pathwise approach follows a simple transformation rule for pointwise transformations of stochastic integrals in terms of smooth functions
F
.
In particular (see
[
51
, Lemma 2.7.3 page 182]
) if
F\in C^{2}
then

(2.15)

\displaystyle F(B_{t}^{H})=F(0)+\int_{0}^{t}F^{\prime}(B_{s}^{H})dB_{s}^{H}.

Throughout this work, we will use the Banach space
\mathscr{B}^{\beta,2}\left(\left[0,t\right],L^{2}(D)\right)
, which consists of all measurable functions
u:[0,t]\rightarrow L^{2}(D)
for which the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\beta,2}
is defined, i.e.,

\|u\|_{\beta,2}^{2}=\left(\esup_{s\in[0,t]}\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{2}\right)^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{0}^{s}\frac{\|u(\cdot,s)-u(\cdot,r)\|_{2}}{(s-r)^{\beta+1}}dr\right)^{2}ds<+\infty,

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the usual norm in
L^{2}(D)
, cf.
[
50
,
51
,
68
]
. The requirement that
u\in\mathscr{B}^{\beta,2}\bigl([0,\tau],L^{2}(D)\bigr)
for some
\beta\in(1-H,1/2)

ensures that the stochastic integral with respect to
B_{t}^{H}
in (
2.14
) exists as a generalized Stieltjes integral in the sense of
[
68
]
; see also
[
56
, Proposition 1]
.

As far as the mixed process
N_{t}
is concerned, the stochastic integral with respect to
B_{t}^{H}
will be considered in the above pathwise sense, whereas the stochastic integral with respect to
B_{t}
will be considered in the Itô sense, i.e. in the Riemann-Stieltjes sum (
2.14
) we must set
H=1/2
and moreover, the convergence of the Riemann sum in (
2.14
) must be considered in the
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
sense. This leads to a modification to the Itô formula (
2.15
) to

(2.16)

\displaystyle F(B_{t})=F(0)+\int_{0}^{t}F^{\prime}(B_{s})dB_{s}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}F^{\prime\prime}(B_{s})ds.

Note that the last term arises because of the interpretation of the limit for the Itô integral for
H=1/2
in the
L^{2}
sense.

The Itô lemma can be generalized for composition of a smooth function
F
with more complicated processes of the form
Y_{t}^{i}=\int_{0}^{t}u_{i}(s)dB_{s}^{H_{i}}
, for
H_{i}\in[1/2,1)
or linear combinations of such processes.
The following Itô’s lemma will be used (see
[
51
, Theorem 2.7.3 page 184]
): Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}^{k}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a
C^{2}-
function and let
Y^{(i)}
,
i=1,\cdots,k
be Itô processes driven by the mixed process
N_{t}
. Then,

(2.17)

\displaystyle F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\cdots,Y^{(k)}_{t})

\displaystyle=F(Y^{(1)}_{0},\cdots,Y^{(k)}_{0})+\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y_{i}}(Y^{(1)}_{s},\cdots,Y^{(k)}_{s})dY^{(i)}_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{i,j=1}^{k}\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}(Y^{(1)}_{s},\cdots,Y^{(k)}_{s})d\langle Y^{(i)}_{s},Y^{(j)}_{s}\rangle.

In the above formula the rules
d\langle B_{t}^{H},B_{t}^{H}\rangle=0
,
d\langle B_{t},B_{t}\rangle=dt
and
d\langle B_{t}^{H},B_{t}\rangle=0
will be used for the calculation of the quadratic covariation processes
d\langle Y^{(i)}_{s},Y^{(j)}_{s}\rangle
. For example, if
Y^{(i)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}a_{i}(s)dB_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{i}(s)dB^{H}_{s}
, then
d\langle Y^{(i)}_{s},Y^{(j)}_{s}\rangle=a_{i}(s)a_{j}(s)ds
since all other covariation processes vanish except for
d\langle B_{t},B_{t}\rangle=dt
. This is because we consider the case
H>1/2
for the fractional Brownian motion.

2.2.3.
The Malliavin derivative

We close this section by defining the Malliavin derivative that will be used in section
4
.

To define this we will consider as
\Omega:=C_{0}([0,T],{\mathbb{R}})
the space of continuous functions
f:[0,T]\to{\mathbb{R}}
vanishing at
0
, equipped with the supremum norm, and set
\mathbb{P}
the unique probability measure on
\Omega
such that the canonical process
(B_{t}^{H},\,\,\,t\in[0,T])
is the fBM with Hurst exponent
H
.

We define the square integrable Volterra kernel

G^{H}
for
H>\frac{1}{2}
by

(2.18)

\displaystyle G^{H}(t,s):=\begin{cases}C_{H}s^{1/2-H}\int_{s}^{t}(\sigma-s)^{H-3/2}\sigma^{H-1/2}\,d\sigma\quad\mbox{if}\quad t>s,\\
0\quad\mbox{if}\quad t\leq s,\end{cases}

where

\displaystyle C_{H}=\left[\frac{H(2H-1)}{\mathscr{B}(2-2H,H-1/2)}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

and
\mathscr{B}
stands for the usual beta function, c.f. section 5.1.3 in
[
55
]
for more details. Note that in this case
B
and
B^{H}
are dependent since
B^{H}
can be represented as

\displaystyle B_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{t}G^{H}(t,s)dB_{s}.

We then express the auto-covariance function of the fBM in terms of

\displaystyle R_{H}(t,s)=\int_{0}^{\min(s,t)}G_{H}(t,r)G_{H}(s,r)dr.

Let
\mathscr{S}
be the space of step functions on
[0,T]
and let
\mathscr{H}
be the closure of this space with respect to the scalar product
\langle{\bf 1}_{[0,s]},{\bf 1}_{[0,t]}\rangle_{H}=R_{H}(s,t)
. We define the mapping
{\bf 1}_{[0,t]}\mapsto B_{t}^{H}
on
\mathscr{S}
and its extension to an isometry between
\mathscr{H}
and the Gaussian space
H_{1}(B^{H})
spanned by
B^{H}
, denoted by
\phi\mapsto B^{H}(\phi)
.

We can now define the Malliavin derivative
D
with respect to fBM. Consider any smooth cylindrical random function of the form
F=f(B^{H}(\phi))
where
\phi\in\mathscr{H}
, and
f\in C_{b}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
. The Malliavin derivative
DF
is the random variable in
\mathscr{H}
defined by

\displaystyle\langle DF,h\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}:=\frac{df}{dx}(B^{H}(\phi))\langle\phi,h\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}=\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}f(B^{H}(\phi)+\epsilon\langle\phi,h\rangle_{\mathscr{H}})\right|_{\epsilon=0},

which can be interpreted as the generalization of the directional derivative along the possible paths of the fBM.
The derivative operator
D
is a closable operator from
L^{p}(\Omega)
into
L^{p}(\Omega;\mathscr{H})
for any
p\geq 1
. It can be used to define generalizations of Sobolev spaces. The most commonly used of this spaces is
{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}
which is the closure of the space of cylidrical smooth random variables with respect to the norm

\displaystyle\|F\|_{{\mathbb{D}}^{1,2}}=\bigg({\mathbb{E}}[|F|^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}[\|DF\|_{\mathscr{H}}^{2}]\bigg)^{1/2}.

In the present setting the Malliavin derivative will be intepreted as a stochastic process
(D_{t}F,\,\,\,:\,\,t\in[0,T])
(see
[
55
]
Section 1.2.1 and Chapter 5).

3.
Local solutions

We start by providing a precise definition of the notion of quenching time that was alluded to in the introduction.

Definition 3.1
.

A stopping time
\tau:\Omega\to(0,\infty)
with respect to the filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{t},t\geq 0\}
is a quenching time of a solution
z
of (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) if

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\tau}\inf_{x\in D}|z(x,t)|=0,\quad\mbox{a.s. on the event}\quad\{\tau<\infty\},

or equivalently

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\inf_{(x,t)\in D\times[0,\tau)}|z(x,t)|>0\right]=1,\quad\mbox{a.s. on the event}\quad\{\tau<\infty\}.

We will write
\tau=+\infty
if
\tau>t
for all
t>0.

Next we introduce the closely related to (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) RPDE problem

(3.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}(x,t)=\left(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}v(x,t)-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(t)v(x,t)-e^{N_{t}}g(x,e^{-N_{t}}v(x,t))\right),\quad x\in D,\;t>0,\qquad

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}v(x,t)+\beta(x)v(x,t)=0,\quad x\in D^{c},\;t>0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq v(x,0)=z_{0}(x)<1,\quad x\in D.

Definition 3.2

(Weak solutions)
.

(i)

The random field
\{z(x,t)\,\,:\,\,t\geq 0,\,\,x\in D\}
with values in
L^{2}(D)
is a weak solution of (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) if

\displaystyle\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}

\displaystyle=\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\langle z(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}^{*}\phi\rangle_{D}-\langle g\left(\cdot,z(\cdot,s)\right),\phi\rangle_{D}\bigg)ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\langle z(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}dN_{s},\,\,\,\,\forall\,\phi\in D(\mathscr{L}^{*}),

where
\mathscr{L}^{*}
stands for the adjoint operator of
\mathscr{L}^{.}

(ii)

The random field
\{v(x,t)\,\,:\,\,t\geq 0,\,\,x\in D\}
with values in
L^{2}(D)
is a weak solution of (
3.1
)–(
3.3
) if

\displaystyle\langle v(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}=\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\left\langle v(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}^{*}\phi\right\rangle_{D}-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(s)\langle v(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}

(3.4)

\displaystyle-e^{N_{t}}\left\langle g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right),\phi\right\rangle_{D}\bigg)ds,\,\,\,\,\forall\,\phi\in D(\mathscr{L}^{*}).\qquad

Note that for the present case
\mathscr{L}=\mathscr{L}^{*}.

The mild solutions for the above equations can be defined in terms of the evolution family
\mathscr{T}
generated by the family of operators
-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}.

Definition 3.3

(Mild solutions)
.

(i)

The random field
\{z(x,t)\,\,:\,\,t\geq 0,\,\,x\in D\}
with values in
L^{2}(D)
is a mild solution of (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) if

\displaystyle z(x,t)=[\mathscr{T}_{t}z_{0}](x)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\mathscr{T}_{t,s}\left[-g\left(\cdot,z(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)\right)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}z(\cdot,s)\right](x)dN_{s},\,\,\,\,\forall\,(x,t)\in D\times[0,T].

(ii)

The random field
\{v(x,t)\,\,:\,\,t\geq 0,\,\,x\in D\}
with values in
L^{2}(D)
is a mild solution of (
3.1
)–(
3.3
) if

\displaystyle v(\cdot,t)=\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathscr{T}_{t,s}\left[-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(s)v(\cdot,s)-e^{N_{s}}g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)\right](\cdot)ds.

Note that in Definition
3.3
(ii) we may rewrite the mild solution in terms of the evolution family
\widetilde{\mathscr{T}}
generated by the family of operators
{\mathscr{L}}(t)=\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(t)\mathscr{I},
where
\mathscr{I}
represents the unit operator. This will lead to the equivalent mild form

(3.5)

\displaystyle v(x,t)=e^{-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)](x)ds,

where
A(t,s),A(t)

are defined by (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) respectively.

The following proposition allows us to reduce the SPDE problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) to the RPDE problem (
3.1
)–(
3.3
).

Proposition 3.4
.

z
is a weak solution of the SPDE problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) if and only if
v:=e^{N_{t}}z
is a weak solution of the RPDE problem (
3.1
)–(
3.3
).

Proof.

To show that we simply need to apply Itô’s formula (
2.17
) to the process
Z_{t}:=e^{N_{t}}\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}
, for
z
being a weak solution of (
1.1
)–(
1.3
). Using Definition
3.3
(i)
we note that we may express

Z_{t}=F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t}),

for
F(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4},y_{5})=e^{y_{1}+y_{2}}(y_{3}-y_{4}-y_{5})
, with

\displaystyle Y^{(1)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}a(s)dB_{s},\,\,\,\,\,Y^{(4)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\langle z(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}a(s)dB_{s},

\displaystyle\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}b(s)dB^{H}_{s},\,\,\,\,\,\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\langle z(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}b(s)dB^{H}_{s},

\displaystyle\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t}

\displaystyle=\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\langle z(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}\phi\rangle_{D}-\langle g(z(\cdot,s)),\phi\rangle_{D}\right)ds.

In order to clarify the exposition we use the following scheme in the notation: We denote the terms depending on the Wiener process by
Y^{(i)}
, the terms depending on the fractional Brownian motion by
\bar{Y}^{(i)}
and by
\hat{Y}^{(i)}
the process that can be expressed as a Riemann integral over
t
. Clearly, (a) by independence there is no quadratic covariation between the processes
Y^{(i)}
and
\bar{Y}^{(i)}
, (b) by the fact that
H>1/2
there is no quadratic covariation between the processes
\bar{Y}^{(i)}
and
\bar{Y}^{(j)}
- even for
i=j
, and (c) by the standard rules of stochastic calculus there is no quadratic covariation between the processes
Y^{(i)},\hat{Y}^{(i)}
and
\bar{Y}^{(i)},\hat{Y}^{(i)}
. The only contributions in quadratic coveriation will be from the processes
Y^{(i)},Y^{(j)}
(including
i=j
). Hence, only the second derivatives
\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial y_{1}^{2}}
and
\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial y_{1}\partial y_{4}}
will contribute to the Itô formula.

A straightforward application of (
2.17
) taking into account the above comments yields that

\displaystyle F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})=F(Y^{(1)}_{0},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{0},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{0},Y^{(4)}_{0},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{0})

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})(dY^{(1)}_{t}+d\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t})+e^{Y^{(1)}_{t}+\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}}d(\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t}-Y^{(4)}_{t}-\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})\right)

(3.6)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})d\langle Y^{(1)}_{t},Y^{(1)}_{t}\rangle-2e^{Y^{(1)}_{t}+\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}}d\langle Y^{(1)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t}\rangle\right).

Moreover, since
Y^{(1)}_{t}+\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}=N_{t}
, we see that

\displaystyle e^{Y^{(1)}_{t}+\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}}d(\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t}-Y^{(4)}_{t}-\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})

\displaystyle=e^{N_{t}}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\langle z(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}\phi\rangle_{D}-\langle g(z(\cdot,s)),\phi\rangle_{D}\bigg)dt-e^{N_{t}}\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}dN_{t}.

Note that since
z
is a weak solution of the SPDE,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t}-Y^{(4)}_{t}-\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t}=\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D},

it holds that

\displaystyle F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})(dY^{(1)}_{t}+d\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t})=e^{N_{t}}\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}dN_{t},

hence, in the contribution of the first order terms in the Itô formula, the stochastic integral over
N_{t}
is eliminated to yield

\displaystyle\bigg(F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})(dY^{(1)}_{t}+d\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t})+e^{Y^{(1)}_{t}+\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}}d(\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t}-Y^{(4)}_{t}-\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})\bigg)

\displaystyle=e^{N_{t}}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\langle z(\cdot,t),\mathscr{L}\phi\rangle_{D}-\langle g(z(\cdot,t)),\phi\rangle_{D}\bigg)dt.

Noting that
d\langle Y_{t}^{(1)},Y_{t}^{(1)}\rangle=a^{2}(t)dt
and
d\langle Y_{t}^{(1)},Y_{t}^{(4)}\rangle=a^{2}(t)\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}dt
, we see that (using again (
3.7
))

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(F(Y^{(1)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t},\hat{Y}^{(3)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t},\bar{Y}^{(5)}_{t})d\langle Y^{(1)}_{t},Y^{(1)}_{t}\rangle-2e^{Y^{(1)}_{t}+\bar{Y}^{(2)}_{t}}d\langle Y^{(1)}_{t},Y^{(4)}_{t}\rangle\bigg)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}e^{N_{t}}a^{2}(t)\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}dt.

Substituting the above in (
3
) and using once more (
3.7
) to express the first and second term as
e^{N_{t}}\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}
and
\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}
respectively we obtain that

\displaystyle e^{N_{t}}\langle z(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}=\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(e^{N_{s}}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\langle z(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}\phi\rangle_{D}-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(s)\langle z(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}-\langle g(z(\cdot,s)),\phi\rangle_{D}\bigg)\bigg)ds.

Expressing the above in terms of
v(x,t)=e^{N_{t}}z(x,t)
yields

\displaystyle\langle v(\cdot,t),\phi\rangle_{D}=\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\langle v(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}\phi\rangle_{D}-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(s)\langle v(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}-\langle e^{N_{s}}g(e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)),\phi\rangle_{D}\bigg)ds,

which proves that
v
is a weak solution of the RPDE problem (
3.1
)–(
3.3
).

To prove the converse, we follow a similar route, starting from a
v
satisfying Definition
3.3

(ii)
and considering the process
z=e^{-N_{t}}v
using Itô’s formula for the process
W_{t}=Q(Z^{(1)}_{t},Z^{(2)}_{t},Z^{(3)}_{t})
where

\displaystyle Z^{(1)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}a(t)dB_{t},\quad Z^{(2)}_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}b(t)dB_{t}^{H},

\displaystyle Z^{(3)}_{t}=\langle z_{0},\phi\rangle_{D}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(s)\langle v(\cdot,s),\mathscr{L}\phi\rangle_{D}-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(s)\langle v(\cdot,s),\phi\rangle_{D}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\langle e^{N_{s}}g(e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)),\phi\rangle_{D}\right)ds,

and
Q(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=e^{-z_{1}-z_{2}}z_{3}
. The details are left to the reader.
∎

Following the same reasoning as in
[
16
, Theorem 2.2]
we can derive the equivalence between weak and mild solutions for the random problem (
3.1
)–(
3.3
). Therefore the proof is omitted.

Proposition 3.5

(Equivalence of weak and mild solutions)
.

v
is a weak solution of (
3.1
)–(
3.3
) in
[0,T]
if and only
v
is a mild solution of (
3.1
)–(
3.3
) in
[0,T].

Proposition 3.6

(Existence of local weak solutions)
.

There exists
T>0
such that (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) has a mild solution in
L^{\infty}\left([0,T)\times D\right).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
it suffices to show the existence of a weak local in time solution for the related RPDE problem (
3.1
)–(
3.3
).

The proof is broken up in three steps.

1. We introduce the mild form for (
3.1
)–(
3.3
)

(3.8)

\displaystyle v(x,t)=e^{-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)](x)ds,

recalling that
\mathscr{T}
is the evolution family generated by the operators
-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}
while
A(t,s),A(t)
are defined by (
2.11
) and (
2.12
) respectively.

By Proposition
3.5
we have that the weak form and the mild form are equivalent. Hence, showing existence for the mild form guerantees existence for the weak form as well.

2. To prove existence we need to handle the singular behaviour of
g
at
v=0
. To this end we consider the approximating sequence
(g_{n})
defined by
g_{n}(\cdot,v)=g\left(\cdot,\max(v,\frac{1}{n})\right)
. Assuming the
g
is decreasing in a neighbourhood of
0
we see that
-g_{n}
are bounded below by
-g\left(\cdot,\frac{1}{n}\right)
and moreover for each
n
the functions
g_{n}
are locally Lipschitz. To ease notation we will denote by
C_{n}
the bound of
|g_{n}|
and
L_{n}(R)
the Lipschitz constant for
g_{n}
in
(0,R)
.

We note that for any
n
,
g_{n}(\cdot,v)=g(\cdot,v)
as long as
v>\frac{1}{n}
. Moreover, if
n<m
then
g_{n}(\cdot,v)\leq g_{m}(\cdot,v)
(equiv.
-g_{n}(\cdot,v)\geq-g_{m}(\cdot,v)
).

Using the approximation we define the random fields
v_{n}
as the mild solutions for the approximate problems

(3.9)

\displaystyle v_{n}(x,t)=e^{-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{n}(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)ds,

and define the stopping times
\tau_{n}=\min(\bar{\tau}_{n},T)
, where

\displaystyle\bar{\tau}_{n}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\,\,:\,\,\inf_{x\in D}v_{n}(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{n}\right\}.

We will show that problem (
3.9
) admits a unique solution in
L^{\infty}([0,T)\times D)
for
T
small enough, for every
n
, so that the stopping times
\tau_{n}
are well defined. This is due to the local Lipschitz property of
g_{n}
and is shown in step 3 below.

Note that
\tau_{n}\leq\tau_{m}
if
n<m
. Indeed, if
v>\frac{1}{n}
then
g_{n}(v)=g_{m}(v),
hence by uniqueness of the solution of (
3.9
) we have that
v_{n}=v_{m}
as long as both are above
\frac{1}{n}
, that is as long as
t\leq\tau_{n}
. This means that up to time
\tau_{n}
neither
v_{n}
nor
v_{m}
are below
\frac{1}{n}
, hence
v_{m}
will go below
\frac{1}{m}<\frac{1}{n}
at a time
\tau_{m}\geq\tau_{n}
. Therefore the sequence
(\tau_{n})
defined above is an increasing and bounded (by
T
) sequence of stopping times.

Since up to
\tau_{n}
, it holds that
g_{n}=g,
then
v_{n}=v
in
[0,\tau_{n})
where
v
is the solution of
(
3.5
). We will then construct the solution
v
of (
3.5
) via

\displaystyle v(x,t)=v_{n}(x,t),\,\,\,\forall\,\,n\geq 1,\,\,(x,t)\in D\times[0,\tau_{n}).

The solution can be continued up to
\tau=\sup_{n}\tau_{n}>0
, and is the local solution of (
3.9
).

3. We now prove the existence of a local in time mild solution for (
3.9
) in
L^{\infty}([0,T_{n})\times D)
for
T_{n}
small enough.
We will use a fixed point scheme for the operator
S_{n}
defined by

(3.10)

\displaystyle(S_{n}v)(x,t):=e^{-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)ds,

on the Banach space
X:=\{v:[0,T_{n}]\times D\to L^{\infty}(D)\,\,\mid\,\,\|v\|_{*}<\infty\}
where the norm
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is defined by
\|v\|_{*}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T_{n}]}\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{\infty}
, where
T
is a suitable time (to be determined).

We will show that the operator
S_{n}
leaves invariant and is a contraction on the subset
X_{R}:=\{v\in X\,\,\mid\,\,v\geq 0,\,\,\|v\|_{*}\leq R\}
for a suitable
R
hence guaranteeing the existence of a unique non negative solution.

We first consider the invariance property for
X_{R}
. Consider any
v\in X_{R}
and take
Sv
. We have that

\displaystyle\|S_{n}v\|_{*}=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)ds\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}\left\|\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)\right\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}\left\|\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)\right\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}\left\|g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right)(\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}+T_{n}A_{T_{n}}C_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}+T_{n}A_{T_{n}}C_{*},

where
A_{T_{n}}=\sup_{t\in[0,T_{n}]}e^{|N_{t}|}
and
C_{*}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n}.
Note that since
v_{n}(x,0)=v_{0}(x)>0,
for
n=1,2,\dots
then by a continuity argument we have
C_{*}>0.

We will choose
R
and
T_{n}
so that

\displaystyle\|v_{0}\|_{\infty}+T_{n}A_{T_{n}}C_{*}<R,

and thus
X_{R}
is invariant under the operator
S_{n}.

We now consider the contraction property for
S_{n}.

We start by noting that

(3.11)

\displaystyle\|S_{n}v_{1}-S_{n}v_{2}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}-A(t,s)}\left\|\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{1}(\cdot,s)\right)-\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{2}(\cdot,s)\right)\right\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}\left\|\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{1}(\cdot,s)\right)-\mathscr{T}_{t,s}g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{2}(\cdot,s)\right)\right\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}\left\|g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{1}(\cdot,s)\right)-g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{2}(\cdot,s)\right)\right\|_{\infty}ds,

where we first used the fact that
e^{-A(t,s)}\leq 1
and then the contractivity of the evolution family
\mathscr{T}
. To proceed further we must make use of the local Lipschitz properties of the nonlinearity
g_{n}
. Let
g_{n}
be locally Lipschitz, satisfying the property

\displaystyle|g_{n}(\cdot,z_{1})-g_{n}(\cdot,z_{2})|\leq L_{n}(R)|z_{1}-z_{2}|,\,\,\,\,\forall\,\,z_{1},z_{2}\in(0,R),

for some
L_{n}(R)>0
. Then as long as
|e^{-N_{s}}|\,\|v_{i}(\cdot,s)\|_{\infty}<R
we may estimate

\displaystyle\left\|g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{1}(\cdot,s)\right)-g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{2}(\cdot,s)\right)\right\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in D}|g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{1}(x,s)\right)-g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{2}(x,s)\right)|

\displaystyle\leq L_{n}(R)e^{N_{s}}\sup_{x\in D}|v_{1}(x,s)-v_{2}(x,s)|=L_{n}(R)e^{-N_{s}}\|v_{1}(\cdot,s)-v_{2}(\cdot,s)\|_{\infty}.

Using the above estimate we may further estimate
(
3.11
) by

\displaystyle\|S_{n}v_{1}-S_{n}v_{2}\|_{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}\left\|g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{1}(\cdot,s)\right)-g_{n}\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v_{2}(\cdot,s)\right)\right\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}L_{n}(R)e^{-N_{s}}\|v_{1}(\cdot,s)-v_{2}(\cdot,s)\|_{\infty}ds

\displaystyle\leq T_{n}L_{n}(R)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|v_{1}(\cdot,s)-v_{2}(\cdot,s)\|_{\infty}=T_{n}L_{n}(R)\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{*}.

Hence we conclude that

\displaystyle\|S_{n}v_{1}-S_{n}v_{2}\|_{*}\leq T_{n}L_{n}(R)\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{*},\,\,\,\mbox{as long as}\,\,\,sup_{s\in[0,T]}e^{N_{s}}\|v_{i}(\cdot,s)\|_{\infty}\leq R.

Choosing
T
so that

(3.12)

\displaystyle T_{n}L_{n}(R)<1,

it can be seen that
S_{n}
is a contraction.
∎

4.
Upper bounds for quenching time and quenching probability

The current section is focused on the derivation of upper bounds for the quenching time as well as the quenching probability for problem (
1.1
)–(
1.3
). Along this section we consider the following growth condition for the nonlinearity
g,

(4.1)

\displaystyle g(x,u)\geq\eta_{1}\left(\lambda\zeta(x)u^{-2}-\gamma u\right)

for some positive constant
\eta_{1}.

4.1.
An upper bound for the quenching time

Define:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\zeta_{m}:=\min_{x\in\bar{D}}\zeta(x)>0,\;\zeta_{M}:=\max_{x\in\bar{D}}\zeta(x)>0.

Consider now the following eigenvalue problem

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\psi=\mu\psi,\quad x\in D,

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}\psi(x)+\beta(x)\psi=0,\quad x\in\partial D^{c},

then it is known, see Appendix, that for the first eigenpair
(\mu_{1},\psi_{1})
we have
\mu_{1}>0
whilst the corresponding eigenfunction
\psi
could be taken also positive.

Theorem 1
.

Consider the random (stopping) time

(4.5)

\displaystyle\tau^{*}:=\inf\left\{t>0:\int_{0}^{t}e^{-3(\eta_{1}\gamma s-\mu_{1}K(s)-A(s))+3N_{s}}\,ds\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right\},

where the functions
K(s),A(s)
are defined by (
2.11
) while the constant
\zeta_{m}
is given by (
4.2
). Then, on the event
\{\tau^{*}<\infty\}
the solution
v
of problem (
3.1
)–(
3.3
) and thus the solution
z
of (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) quench in finite
\tau,
where
\tau\leq\tau^{*}\,\mathbb{P}-a.s.

Proof.

By choosing as a test function into the weak formulation (
(ii)
) the principal eigenfunction
\psi_{1}
to (
4.3
)-(
4.4
) normalized as

(4.6)

\displaystyle\int_{D}\psi_{1}(x)\,dx=1,

then

(4.7)

\displaystyle\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}

\displaystyle=\langle z_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\mu_{1}k^{2}(s)}{2}+\frac{a^{2}(s)}{2}\right)\langle v,\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}\,ds

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}e^{N_{s}}\left\langle g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right),\psi_{1}\right\rangle_{D}\,ds.

Next by (
4.7
) and for any
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\frac{\langle v(\cdot,t+\varepsilon),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}-\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}}{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{\mu_{1}k^{2}(s)}{2}+\frac{a^{2}(s)}{2}\right)\langle v,\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}\,ds

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{t}^{t+\varepsilon}e^{N_{s}}\left\langle g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right),\psi_{1}\right\rangle_{D}\,ds.

Letting now
\varepsilon\to 0

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}=-\left(\frac{\mu_{1}k^{2}(t)}{2}+\frac{a^{2}(t)}{2}\right)\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}-e^{N_{t}}\left\langle g\left(\cdot,e^{-N_{s}}v(\cdot,s)\right),\psi_{1}\right\rangle_{D}.

By virtue of (
4.1
) and Jensen’s inequality, taking also into account (
4.6
), we finally derive

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}\leq\left(\eta_{1}\gamma-\frac{\mu_{1}k^{2}(t)}{2}-\frac{a^{2}(t)}{2}\right)\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}-\eta_{1}\lambda\zeta_{m}e^{3N_{t}}\left\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\right\rangle^{-2}_{D}.

Thus via comparison we deduce that
\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}\leq I(t)
where
I(t)
solves the following random initial value problem

\displaystyle\frac{dI}{dt}=\left(\eta_{1}\gamma-\frac{\mu_{1}k^{2}(t)}{2}-\frac{a^{2}(t)}{2}\right)I(t)-\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}e^{3N_{t}}I^{-2}(t),\;0<t<\tau^{*},

\displaystyle I(0)=\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}.

Hence

\displaystyle I(t)=e^{\eta_{1}\gamma t-\mu_{1}K(t)-A(t)}\left[\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}-3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\int_{0}^{t}e^{-3\left(\eta_{1}\gamma s-\mu_{1}K(s)-A(s)\right)+3N_{s}}\,ds\right]^{1/3},

for
0<t<\tau^{*},
where
\tau^{*}
is defined by (
4.5
).

Therefore,
I(t)
hits
0
(quenches) in finite time on the event
\left\{\tau^{*}<+\infty\right\}.
Since
I(t)\geq\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D},
then
\tau^{*}
is an upper bound
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
for the quenching (stopping) time of
\langle v(\cdot,t),\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}
and thus for
v(x,t).
The latter thanks to transformation
v=e^{N_{t}}z
implies that
\tau^{*}
is also an upper bound
\mathbb{P}-a.s.
for the solution
z
of (
1.1
)- (
1.3
).
∎

4.2.
A tail estimate leading to an upper bound of quenching time

In the current subesction, our main purpose is to provide a tail estimate for the upper bound
\tau^{*}
defined by (
4.5
), making use of a tail probability estimate for exponential estimates of fBM given in
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
. To this end we consider that the stochastic process is defined as follows

(4.8)

\displaystyle B_{t}^{H}:=\int_{0}^{t}G^{H}(t,s)dB_{s},

where the Volterra kernel
G^{H}
is given by (
2.18
).

Henceforth, for simplicity, we drop the superscript
H
from the notation of the kernel
G^{H}(t,s).

Theorem 4.1
.

Assume that
B^{H}
is given by (
4.8
) and let

\nu(T):=\int_{0}^{T}\exp\left[-3(\gamma\eta_{1}t-\mu_{1}K(t)-A(t))\right]\mathbb{E}(3N_{t})\,dt.

Then, for any
T>0
such that

\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}>\nu(T),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}\leq T\right]\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{\ln^{2}\left[\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{-3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\nu(T)}\right]}{2M(T)}\right),

where

(4.9)

\displaystyle M(T):=18\int_{0}^{T}a^{2}(s)\,ds+36\,HT^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{T}b^{2}(s)\,ds.

Proof.

Consider the stochastic process

\displaystyle X_{t}:=-3\left(\gamma\eta_{1}t-\mu_{1}K(t)-A(t)\right)+3N_{t},

then thanks to (
4.8
) we have the representation

(4.10)

\displaystyle X_{t}=-3\left(\gamma\eta_{1}t-\mu_{1}K(t)-A(t)\right)+3\left(\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}b(r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}G(r,s)\,drdB_{s}\right).

Now from
[
20
, Theorem 3.1]
it folows that for any
T\geq 0
and
w>\nu(T),
it holds

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{X_{t}}dt\geq w\right)\leq 2\exp\left[-\frac{(\ln w-\ln\nu(T))^{2}}{2M(T)}\right],

where
\nu(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{X_{t}}\right]\,dt
and
M(T)
is such that

(4.12)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{T}|D_{r}X_{t}|^{2}dr\leq M(T)\quad\mathbb{P}-a.s.\,,

and
D_{r}X_{t}
stands for the Malliavin derivative of
X_{t}.

So in order to obtain the desired tail estimate we are working towards finding an upper bound of
M(T)
such that (
4.12
) holds.

We first note, using the representation (
4.10
), that for
s<t

\displaystyle D_{r}X_{t}=3\left(a(r)+\int_{r}^{t}b(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,r)\,ds\right),

and thus

(4.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}|D_{r}X_{t}|^{2}dr\leq 18\int_{0}^{t}a^{2}(r)dr+18\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{t}b(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,r)\,ds\right)^{2}dr.

Next we are working towards the derivation of a bound of the second integral of the right-hand side of (
4.13
). Indeed, via Fubini’s theorem and following the same calculations like in
[
16
, page 15]
we have

(4.14)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{t}b(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,r)\,ds\right)^{2}dr=2\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{0}^{s}b(s)b(s^{\prime})\Psi(s,s^{\prime})\,ds^{\prime}\right)\,ds,

where

\displaystyle\Psi(s,s^{\prime}):=\int_{0}^{\min\{s,s^{\prime}\}}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,r)\frac{\partial}{\partial s^{\prime}}G(s^{\prime},r)\,dr.

Since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,r)=C_{H}r^{1/2-H}(s-r)^{H-3/2}s^{H-1/2}

then using
[
55
, relation (5.7) ]
for
s^{\prime}<s
we deduce

\displaystyle\Psi(s,s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=C_{H}^{2}\,(ss^{\prime})^{H-1/2}\int_{0}^{\min\{s,s^{\prime}\}}r^{1-2H}(s-r)^{H-3/2}(s^{\prime}-r)^{H-3/2}dr

\displaystyle=H(2H-1)(s-s^{\prime})^{2H-2}.

Next by virtue of (
4.14
) and following the same calculations as in
[
16
, page 16]
yields

(4.15)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{r}^{t}b(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial s}G(s,r)\,ds\right)^{2}dr

\displaystyle\leq 2H(2H-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s}|b(s)b(s^{\prime})|(s-s^{\prime})^{2H-2}\,ds^{\prime}ds

\displaystyle\leq 2Ht^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{t}b^{2}(s)ds.

Then (
4.13
) infers

(4.16)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int_{0}^{T}|D_{r}X_{t}|^{2}dr\leq 18\int_{0}^{T}a^{2}(r)dr+36HT^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{T}b^{2}(s)ds:=M(T).

Since by (
4.5
)

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}\leq T\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{-3(\gamma\eta_{1}t-\mu_{1}K(t)-A(t))+3N_{t}}\,dt\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}\exp\left[X(t)\right]\,dt\geq w\right],

then the desired result follows by virtue of (
4.11
) and (
4.16
) for
w=\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}.

∎

Theorem 4.2
.

(1)

Assume that

(4.18)

\displaystyle B_{t}^{H}=\int_{0}^{t}G(t,s)dW_{s},

where
G
is the Volterra kernel given by (
2.18
) and

is a Brownian motion defined in the same probability space, and adapted to the same filtration as the Brownian motion

Then

(4.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}\leq T\right]\leq\frac{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(\mu_{1}K(t)+A(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t)}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}e^{6A(t)+36Ht^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{t}b^{2}(s)\,ds}\,dt\right)}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}.

(2)

Assume that
B_{t}
and
B_{t}^{H}
are independent then

(4.20)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}\leq T\right]\leq 3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}e^{3(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+4A(t)+3Ht^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{t}b^{2}(s)\,ds)}\,dt}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}.

Proof.

(1)

Making use first of Hölder’s and then Chebichev’s inequalities then (
4.5
) yields

(4.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}\leq T\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{3(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})+3(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\left.\times\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt\right)^{1/2}\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})}\,dt\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\delta t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})}\,dt\right]}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt\right]}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\left(\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\delta t+A(t))}\,dt+\int_{0}^{T}e^{6A(t)}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{6\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\right]\,dt\right)}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}.

Note that in order to pass from the second to the third inequality above we have used the inequality

for the choice

\displaystyle A=\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})}\,dt>0\;\mbox{and}\;B=\int_{0}^{T}e^{6(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt>0.

Indeed, since for any

\{A^{1/2}B^{1/2}\geq s\}\subset\left\{\frac{1}{2}(A+B)\geq s\right\}\subset\{A+B\geq s\},

by the monotonicity and the subadditivity of the probability measure we pass from the second to the third inequality.
Moroever, note that in the last inequality has been used that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\delta\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s}}\right]=\exp\left[\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}a^{2}(s)\,ds\right]=e^{\delta^{2}A(t)}.

Furthemore thanks to (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{6\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{6\int_{0}^{t}\int_{s}^{t}b(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}G(r,s)\,dr\,dW_{s})}\right]=e^{18\int_{0}^{t}\left[\int_{s}^{t}b(s)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}G(r,s)\,dr\right]^{2}\,ds}.

Using now (
4.15

(4.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{6\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s}}\right]\leq\exp\left(36Ht^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{t}b^{2}(s)ds\right).

Therefore (
4.21
) in conjunction with (
4.22
) implies estimate (
4.19
).

(2)

In this case using again Chebichev’s inequality reads

(4.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}\leq T\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{3(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})+3(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt\geq\frac{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}{3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{T}e^{3(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})+3(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\,dt\right]}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}

\displaystyle=3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{3(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\,dB_{s})}\right]\times\mathbb{E}\left[e^{3(A(t)+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\,dB^{H}_{s})}\right]\,dt}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}}

\displaystyle\leq 3\lambda\eta_{1}\zeta_{m}\frac{\int_{0}^{T}e^{3(\mu_{1}K(t)-\gamma\eta_{1}t+4A(t)+3Ht^{2H-1}\int_{0}^{t}b^{2}(s)\,ds)}\,dt}{\langle v_{0},\psi_{1}\rangle_{D}^{3}},

where the third equality is an immediate consequence of the independence of
B_{t}
and
B_{t}^{H},
whilst the last inequality follows from the same calculations employed in the previous case.

∎

5.
Lower bounds for quenching probability

Along this section we also assume that the nonlinearity

Consider

(1)

(2)

If

) – (

Proof.

By Theorem
1

4.5
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}=\infty\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{t}e^{-3(\gamma\eta_{1}s-\mu_{1}K(s)-A(s))+3\widetilde{N}_{s}}\,ds<w\quad\mbox{for all}\quad t>0\right]

for

Under the change of variable

we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\tau^{*}=\infty\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-3(\gamma\eta_{1}s-\mu_{1}K(s)-A(s))+3\widetilde{B}_{2A(s)}}\,ds\leq w\right]

where

Next we distinguish the following cases:

(1)

\mathscr{R}(U)(x,t)\leq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x).
The latter in conjunction with (

(6.9)

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(U)(x,t)

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(\zeta(\cdot)U_{r}^{-3}(\cdot)U_{r}(\cdot)\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle\geq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(U_{r}^{-3}(\cdot)U_{r}(\cdot)\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle\geq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(\mathscr{G}_{1}^{-3}(\cdot,r)U_{r}(\cdot)\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle\geq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{G}^{-3}(r)\mu^{-3}(r)\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(U_{r}(\cdot)\right)(x)\,dr.\qquad\qquad

Next, using once more (
6.8
) and applying the semigroup property
[
59
, Definition 2.3, page 106]
into the last inequality of (
6.9
)
reads

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}(U)(x,t)

\displaystyle\geq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{G}^{-3}(r)\mu^{-3}(r)\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(U_{r}(\cdot)\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle\geq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{G}^{-3}(r)\mu^{-3}(r)\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(\mathscr{G}_{2}(\cdot,r)\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{G}^{-3}(r)\mu^{-3}(r)\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(\mathscr{T}_{r}v_{0}(x)\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)\left[1-\lambda\eta_{2}\zeta_{M}\int_{0}^{t}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{G}^{-3}(r)\mu^{-3}(r)\,dr\right]

Accordingly,

(6.11)

\displaystyle e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)\mathscr{G}(t)\leq\mathscr{R}(U)(x,t)\leq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x),\quad 0\leq t<\tau^{*},\,x\in D.

Next, we consider the iteration scheme

\displaystyle v_{0}(x,t):=e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad v_{n+1}(x,t)=\mathscr{R}(v_{n})(x,t),\quad n=0,1,2,\dots.

Due to (
6.11

v_{0}(x,t)\geq\mathscr{R}(v_{0})(x,t)=v_{1}(x,t).
If we assume that
v_{n-1}(x,t)\geq v_{n}(x,t)
for some
n\geq 1
and for every
x\in D
and

then since
\zeta(x)>0

\displaystyle v_{n+1}(x,t)

\displaystyle=\mathscr{R}(v_{n})(x,t)

\displaystyle=e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{T}_{t-r}\left({\zeta v_{n}^{-2}}\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle\leq e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-r)-A(t,r)}e^{3N_{r}}\mathscr{T}_{t,r}\left(\zeta v_{n-1}^{-2}\right)(x)\,dr

\displaystyle=\mathscr{R}(v_{n-1})(x,t)

\displaystyle=v_{n}(x,t).

The latter implies by induction that

Consequently, using the version of the monotone convergence theorem for decreasing functions reads

\displaystyle\widetilde{v}(x,t)=\mathscr{R}(\widetilde{v})(x,t)\quad\mbox{for}\quad x\in D\quad\mbox{and}\quad 0\leq t<\tau_{*},

and hence

of the following random problem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v_{1}}{\partial t}(x,t)=-\frac{1}{2}k^{2}(t)\mathscr{L}v_{1}(x,t)+\left(\gamma\eta_{2}-\frac{1}{2}a^{2}(t)\right)v_{1}(x,t)-\lambda\eta_{2}e^{3N_{t}}\frac{\zeta(x)}{v^{2}_{1}(x,t)},\quad x\in D,\;t>0,

\displaystyle\mathscr{N}v_{1}(x,t)+\beta(x)v_{1}(x,t)=0,\quad x\in D^{c},\;t>0,

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{1}(x,0)=z_{0}(x)<1,\quad x\in D.

which is given as the following integral representation

\displaystyle v_{1}(x,t)=e^{\gamma\eta_{2}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}z_{0}(x)-\lambda\eta_{2}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\gamma\eta_{2}(t-s)-A(t,s)}e^{3N_{s}}\left[\mathscr{T}_{t,s}\left(\zeta(\cdot)v_{1}^{-2}(\cdot,s)\right)\right](x)ds.

Furthermore, by virtue of (

\displaystyle 0<e^{\gamma\eta_{1}t-A(t)}\mathscr{T}_{t}v_{0}(x)\mathscr{G}(t)\leq v_{1}(x,t)\leq v(x,t)\leq 1,\;x\in D,\;0\leq t<\tau_{*}\leq\infty.\qquad

Now, (

7.
Applications: MEMS models

Deterministic versions of model (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) with local diffusion have been introduced the last decade to describe the operation of
certain types of micro-electromechanical systems (MEMS), cf
[
24
,
40
,
44
,
60
]
. MEMS devices are precision devices which integrate mechanical processes
with electrical circuits. Their size ranges from
millimetres down to microns, and involve precision mechanical
components which can be constructed using semiconductor
manufacturing technologies
[
44
,
60
,
65
]
. MEMS devices are commonly employed in biomedical engineering applications, including the design of micro-scale drug delivery devices and the development of micropumps for microfluidic diagnostic tools, among others
[
8
,
58
,
64
]
.

The key part of such an electrostatically actuated
MEMS device usually consists of an
elastic plate (or membrane) suspended above a rigid ground one.
Regularly the elastic plate is held
fixed at two ends while the other two edges remain free to move (see Figure
1
).

Figure 1.
Schematic representation of a MEMS device

A potential difference

V
is applied between the elastic membrane and the rigid ground plate,
leading to a deflection of the membrane towards the plate. Considering the width
d
of the intermediate gap, i.e the gap between the membrane
and the bottom plate, to be small compared to the
device length
L
, then
the deformation of the elastic membrane
u,
after proper scaling, is given by the dimensionless equation

(7.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u+\frac{\lambda\,q(x)}{(1-u)^{2}},\quad x\in D,\;t>0,

see
[
44
,
60
,
61
]
, where the laplacian term
\Delta u
describes to the spread of the deformation, whilst the term
(1-u)^{-2}
arises as a consequence of the electrostatic features of the MEMS device. Here, the term
q(x)
describes the varying dielectric properties of the membrane and for some elastic materials can be taken to be constant;
for simplicity, henceforth we assume that
q(x)\equiv 1.

Furthermore, the parameter
\lambda
appearing in (
7.1
) equals to

\lambda=\frac{V^{2}L^{2}\varepsilon_{0}}{2\widetilde{T}\ell^{3}},

and is the
tuning
parameter of the device.
Note that
\widetilde{T}
stands for the tension of the elastic membrane,

\ell
is the characteristic width of the gap
between the membrane and the fixed ground plate (electrode),
whilst
\varepsilon_{0}
is the permittivity of free space. For extending further the stable operation of the MEMS device then a capacitance connected in series with MEMS is introduced to the underlying electrical circuit. Then we are led, via the application of Kirchoff’s laws, into versions of model (
7.1
) involving nonlocal reaction terms, cf.
[
44
,
60
,
61
]
.

MEMS engineers are commonly interested in identifying the conditions under which the elastic membrane can touch the rigid plate, a mechanical phenomenon usually referred to as
touching down
and one that can potentially lead to the destruction of the device. Touching down can be described via model (
7.1
), and its nonlocal variations. It actually corresponds to the case when the deformation
u
reaches the value
1;
such a situation is known as
quenching (or extinction)
in the mathematical literature.

Experimental observations (see
[
65
]
) show a significant uncertainty regarding the values of
V
and
\widetilde{T}.
In that case, incorporating this uncertainty into the tunning parameter
\lambda
we can obtain some a stochastic model with a multiplicative white noise involving
[
18
,
39
]
. In case of a MEMS device with a long-range dependence uncertainty for
V
and
\widetilde{T}
it seems reasonable to consider a stochastic model with multiplicative fractional noise with Hurst index
1/2<H<1,
cf.
[
19
]
.

A more complex configuration of the MEMS device described in Figure
1
incorporates the case where two edges of the membrane are attached to a pair of torsional and translational springs, modeling a flexible, non-ideal support, see
[
17
,
65
]
. In that case homogeneous Robin boundary conditions should be assigned to equation (
7.1
), cf.
[
17
,
19
]
.

Now consider the case we would also like to model nonlocal effects of the elastic membrane, maybe due to material discontinuities. In that case the spread of the membrane’s deflection should be modeled, into equation (
7.1
), by a nonlocal diffusion operator
\mathscr{L}
of the form (
2.1
), with a possibly time-dependent diffusion coefficient, incorporating the natural treatment of balance laws on and off material discontinuities, cf.
[
11
,
22
]
.
To integrate any possible material anomalies on the boundary a nonlocal Robin boundary condition of the form (
1.2
) should be assigned to the underlying nonlocal equation. A rather simple choice is to take
\mathscr{L}=(-\Delta)^{\alpha},\,0<\alpha<1,
whilst the nonlocal operator
\mathscr{N}
given by (
2.2
), should have kernel of the form
|x-y|^{-d-2\alpha}.
Finally, in order to include elastic membranes could generate long-range and short-range voltage and tension fluctuations (occuring as clustering) we could embody a noise of the form (
2.13
).

For such a model with extended Robin conditions the analysis and the analytical results and estimates of sections
3
-
6
give an insight about the quenching behaviour of the model. For the case of the extended Dirichlet conditions, where no similar analytical results are available, we present a preliminary numerical treatment of the problen in the following section
8
.

8.
Numerical solution

In the current section we deliver a numerical study of the following problem

(8.1)

\displaystyle du=\left(-(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u+\frac{\lambda}{\left(1-u\right)^{2}}\right)dt+\pi(u)dN_{t},\quad x\in D,\;t>0,\;0<\alpha<1,\quad

(8.2)

\displaystyle u(x,t)=0,\;x\in D^{c},\;t>0,

(8.3)

\displaystyle 0\leq u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)<1,\quad x\in D,

for the one-dimensional case
d=1.
The considered time-dependent noise is a combination of standard and fractional Brownian motions. This case corresponds to a simpler version of the more general problem studied in the previous sections and it is closely related to the MEMS application described in the preceding section.
In relation to problem (
1.1
)-(
1.3
), here we consider,
\mathscr{L}=(-\Delta)^{\alpha},\,0<\alpha<1,
,
u=1-z,

D=[-1,1],

k(t)=2
,
\gamma=0
,
\zeta(x)=1
and extended Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The considered noise term is of a multiplicative form, that is
\pi(u)\,dN_{t}
for

\pi(u)=(1-u)
. In particular we take the noise being of the form
\pi(u)\,dN_{t}=\kappa_{1}(1-u)\,dB_{t}+\kappa_{2}(1-u)\,dB^{H}_{t}
for some positive constants
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2};
that is a mixture of the standard and fractional Brownian motion.

Here we focus in the case of homogeneous Dirichlet conditions in the complement of the interval
[-1,\,1].

Note that homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
u=0
in
[-1,\,1]^{c}
corresponds in having
z=1
for
x\in[-1,\,1]^{c},
and this case is not actually covered by the analysis in the previous sections.

The consideration of extended Dirichlet boundary conditions is important, since those conditions
are quite relevant to the MEMS application (see for example
[
24
,
44
,
60
]
) considered in the previous section. On the other hand, since as for the case of the standard Laplacian operator the analytical methods for estimating the quenching probability are more delicate (see
[
18
,
19
]
) an initial numerical study of the Dirichlet problem is a valuable contribution. So we attempt a numerical study of the conisdered stochastic nonlocal model in that case. We intend to derive an initial estimation of the dynamics of our nonlocal stochastic problem under the infuence of extended Dirichlet boundary conditions.

8.1.
Finite Differences approximation

In order to approximate numerically the problem (
8.1
)-(
8.3
)
we proceed with a finite difference semi–implicit Euler in time scheme, cf.
[
23
,
49
]
.

We set a discretization in
[0,T]\times[-1,1]
,
0\leq t\leq T
,
0\leq x\leq 1
with

t_{n}=n\delta t
,
\delta t=\left[{T}/{N}\right]
for
N
the number of time steps and we also introduce the grid points in
[-1,1]
,
x_{j}=-1+j\delta x
, for
\delta x=2/M
and
j=0,1,\ldots,M
.

Then, initially we apply a finite differences approximation for the fractional Laplacian term
(-\Delta)^{\alpha}u
in (
8.1
). We follow the finite differences approximation approach given in
[
23
]
.

Following this approach we obtain

(-\Delta)^{\alpha}_{\rho}u\simeq Au
, for
A
an
(M-1)\times(M-1)
matrix having the form:

\displaystyle A_{i,j}=C_{1,\alpha}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sum_{k=2}^{M-1}\frac{(k+1)^{\chi}-(k-1)^{\chi}}{k^{\rho}}+(\frac{(M+1)^{\chi}-(M-1)^{\chi}}{M^{\rho}})+\left(2^{\chi}+\kappa_{\rho}-1\right)+\frac{\chi}{\alpha M^{2\alpha}},&&i=j,\\
&&\\
-\frac{\left(|j-1|+1\right)^{\chi}-\left(|j-1|-1\right)^{\chi}}{2|j-1|^{\rho}},&&j\neq i,\,i\pm 1,\\
&&\\
-\frac{1}{2}\left(2^{\chi}+\kappa_{\rho}-1\right),&&j=i\pm 1,\end{array}\right.

where here we set
\chi=\rho-2\alpha
,
\kappa_{\rho}=1+2\alpha
for
2\alpha\in(1,2)
and
\kappa_{\rho}=1
for
2\alpha=1
. Also the parameter
\rho\in(2\alpha,2]
and in our simulations we take
\rho=1+\alpha
. For more details about the derivation of the scheme and the choices of the various parameters we refer to
[
23
]
.

We then apply a semi-implicit Euler method in time. We denote by

U_{h}^{n}=(u_{0}^{n},u_{1}^{n},\ldots,u_{M}^{n})
the finite difference approximation of
u(x,t)
, i.e.
u(x,t^{n})\simeq U_{h}^{n}
, and by
u_{h}^{n}=(u_{1}^{n},\ldots,u_{M-1}^{n})
since due to Dirichlet boundary conditions
u_{0}^{n}=u_{M}^{n}=0
.

By a standard discretization in time we obtain

\displaystyle du(t_{n})\simeq\frac{\left(u_{h}^{n+1}-u_{h}^{n}\right)}{\delta t}=Au_{h}^{n+1}+g\left(u_{h}^{n}\right)+\pi\left(u_{h}^{n}\right)\left(\kappa_{1}b_{s}(t_{n})+\kappa_{2}b_{s}^{H}(t_{n})\right),

or

\displaystyle\left(I-\delta tA\right)u_{h}^{n+1}=u_{h}^{n}+\delta t\,g\left(u_{h}^{n})\right)+\delta t\,B_{h}(t_{n}),

where we denote by
B_{h}

\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1}
the vector

B=\pi\left(u_{h}^{n}\right)\left(\kappa_{1}b_{s}(t_{n})+\kappa_{2}b_{s}^{H}(t_{n})\right)
.

.
Similarly we sample the fractional Brownian motion by considering

9.
Discussion

In the current work, we investigated the behaviour of certain stochastic partial differential equations, including nonlocal diffusion operators assigned to nonlocal Robin conditions. The dynamics were driven by a mixture of a Wiener process and fractional Brownian motion with Hurst index
H>1/2.
The considered model can describe the operation of MEMS devices embeded to materials with discontinuities. This model can exibit the phenomenon of
finite time quenching
, which is closely related to the mechanical phenomenon of
touching down
in MEMS devices. Similar models, related also to MEMS systems, driven by either single Brownian or fractional Brownian motions have been investigated in
[
18
,
19
,
39
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first time in the literature that the quenching behaviour of such nonlocal model with mixed noises is investigated.

Our theoretical analysis enabled us to provide upper bounds for the
quenching probability
and for the
quenching time
. To this end, we employed estimates of perpetual integral functionals of Brownian motion as wells as recently obtained tale estimates of fractional Brownian motion. Moreover,
we were able to estimate the probability of global existence, and as a by-product we also derived lower estimates of the quenching time.
The produced analytical results exhibit the strong impact of the noise to the long time dynamics of the model. In particular, as alluded to in Theorem
5.4
, the form of the nonlinear term in problem (
1.1
) – (
1.3
) forces the solution towards quenching almost surely. This is in stark contrast with the dynamics of the corresponding deterministic local diffusion system, cf.
[
40
,
44
]
. A key auxiliary result for our analytical approach is the positivity of the principal Robin eigenpair of the considered nonlocal operator. It is worth emphasizing, that since such a result was not available in the literature we had to prove it, see Appendix.

A complementary numerical study of the underlying nonlocal model has been employed for the case of fractional Laplacian
\left(-\Delta\right)^{\alpha},\;0<\alpha<1,
and for homogeneous extended Dirichlet boundary conditions. The presented numerical experiments shed light to the impact of the mixed noise to the dynamics of the model. More interestingly, the numerical simulations illuminate how the dispersal coefficient
0<\alpha<1
affects the quenching behaviour of the underlying model,
an effect cannot be observed by means of an analytical approach.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

The proof (that follows the strategy of
[
25
]
) can be broken into the following steps:

1. We first now that by the Green-Gauss formula, for any
u,v
it follows that

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle={\mathscr{E}}(u,v)+\int_{D^{c}}\beta u(y)v(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{D}{\mathscr{L}}u(x)v(x)dx+\int_{D^{c}}{\mathscr{N}}u(y)v(y)dy+\int_{D^{c}}\beta(y)u(y)v(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{D}{\mathscr{L}}u(x)v(x)dx,

as long as
u
satisfies the nonlocal Robin-type boundary conditions.

Hence, for any
u
satisfying Robin-type boundary conditions

(10.1)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,v)=\int_{D}{\mathscr{L}}u(x)v(x)dx=({\mathscr{L}}u,v)_{L^{2}(D)}.

2. We now take any
u\in L^{2}(D)
, such that
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
. Since the set of Robin eigenfunctions for
{\mathscr{L}}
forms an orthonormal basis (see Theorem 4.21 in
[
26
]
) we expect an expansion for
u
of the form
u=\sum_{k\in{{\mathbb{N}}}}d_{k}\psi_{k}
, with
d_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})_{L^{2}(D)}
, and the expansion converging in
L^{2}(D)
and
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}=1
.

Moreover, for any two eigenfunctions
\psi_{k}
,
\psi_{n}
, we have (using (
10.1
)) that

(10.2)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(\psi_{k},\psi_{n})=(\mathscr{L}\psi_{k},\psi_{n})_{L^{2}(D)}=\mu_{k}(\psi_{k},\psi_{n})_{L^{2}(D)}=\mu_{k}\delta_{k,n},

where we also used the orthonormality of
\{\psi_{n}\}
in
L^{2}(D)
.

Using arguments related to the Lax-Milgram lemma, we can consider the bilinear form
Q_{\beta}
as forming an inner product
(u,v)_{\beta}:=Q_{\beta}(u,v)
on the fractional Sobolev space
V_{\nu}(D\mid{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, which will subsequently be denoted by
H_{\nu}^{1}(D)
to indicate the resemblance with the standard Sobolev space used for the local problem.
To this end, motivated by (
10.2
), we note that the set
\{\psi_{k}\}:=\{\psi_{k}/\mu_{k}^{1/2}\}
forms a basis for
H^{1}_{\nu}(D).
Indeed, (
10.2
) shows that
\{\psi_{k}\}
is orthonormal with respect to this new inner product.
To show that this is a basis for
H^{1}_{\nu}(D)
it suffices to show that
(\psi_{k},v)_{\beta}=0
for all
k
implies that
v=0
. To see this we simply have to use (
10.1
), to observe that

\displaystyle 0=(\psi_{k},v)_{\beta}=Q_{\beta}(\psi_{k}/\mu_{k}^{1/2},v)=\mu_{k}^{1/2}(\psi_{k},v)_{L^{2}(D)},\,\,\,\forall\,\,k\in{\mathbb{N}},

and by the fact that
\{\psi_{k}\}
is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(D)
, we see that
v=0
as required.

Hence,
\{\psi_{k}\}=\{\psi_{k}/\mu_{k}^{1/2}\}
is an orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space
H^{1}_{\nu}
, endowed with the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\beta}=Q_{\beta}(\cdot,\cdot)
. This implies that for any
u\in H^{1}_{\nu}(D)
, the series
u=\sum_{k\in N}c_{k}\psi_{k}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}c_{k}\psi_{k}/\mu_{k}^{1/2}
, for
c_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})_{\beta}=Q_{\beta}(u,\psi_{k}/\mu_{k}^{1/2})
converges in
H^{1}_{\nu}(D)
. We easily see that
Q_{\beta}(u,\psi_{k}/\mu_{k}^{1/2})=(u,\psi_{k})_{L^{2}(D)}
. By the observation the
H^{1}_{\nu}(D)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(D)
, so that
u
also admits an expansion
u=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}\psi_{k}
,
d_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})_{L^{2}(D)}
in
L^{2}(D)
, and upon direct comparison with the corresponding expansion for
u
in
H^{1}_{\nu}
, we see that the two expansions coincide.

3. We are now ready to show the variational formula (
2.8
).

Consider any
u\in H^{1}_{\nu}(D)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(D)
, such that
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
. By the results of step 2, it admits an expansion of the form
u=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}\psi_{k}
, with
d_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})_{L^{2}(D)}
and
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}=1
. We now calculate
Q_{\beta}(u,u)
using this expansion and the bilinearity of
Q_{\beta}
:

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,u)=Q_{\beta}\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}\psi_{k},\sum_{k^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k^{\prime}}\psi_{k^{\prime}}\right)=\sum_{(k,k^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}d_{k^{\prime}}Q_{\beta}(\psi_{k},\psi_{k^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{D})}}{{=}}\sum_{(k,k^{\prime})\in{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}}\mu_{k}d_{k}d_{k^{\prime}}\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}\geq\mu_{1}(\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2})=\mu_{1},

where we used the fact that
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}=1
and
0<\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots
.

Hence, for any
u\in H^{1}_{\nu}(D)
such that
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
it holds that
Q_{\beta}(u,u)\geq\mu_{1}
. Choosing,
u=\psi_{1}
, the first Robin eigenfunction we see that
Q_{\beta}(u,u)=Q_{\beta}(\psi_{1},\psi_{1})=\mu_{1}
. Hence, (
2.8
) is proven.

4. We now show that for any
u\in H^{1}_{\nu}(D)
such that
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
the following are equivalent:

(10.3)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}u=\mu_{1}u

(10.4)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{N}}u+\beta u=0

and

(10.5)

Q_{\beta}(u,u)=\mu_{1}

Clearly (
10.3
)–(
10.4
) implies (
10.5
) by a simple application of the Green-Gauss formula. It thus remains to prove the reverse implication.

Suppose that (
10.5
) holds for some
u\in H^{1}_{\nu}(D)
such that
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
. By step 2,
u
admits an expansion of the form
u=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}\psi_{k}
with
d_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})
, and
\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}=1
. Moreover, by the bilinearity of
Q_{\beta}
, and the above expansion we have that (essentially as in step 3) that

(10.6)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,u)=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}\mu_{k}.

Note that

(10.7)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}\mu_{1}=\left(\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}\right)\mu_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1}}{{=}}\mu_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{E2}}}{{=}}Q_{\beta}(u,u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{F}}}{{=}}\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}\mu_{k},

which upon rearrangement yields,

(10.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}d_{k}^{2}(\mu_{k}-\mu_{1})=0,

and in return (recalling the fact that
0<\mu_{1}\leq\mu_{2}\leq\cdots
) yields

(10.9)

\displaystyle d_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})_{L^{2}(D)}=0,\,\,\,\mu_{k}>\mu_{1}.

Combining that with the expansion for
u
we see that the only contribution on
u
comes from projections of
u
on the solutions of the Robin eigenvalue problem, for
\mu_{1}
.

There is no guarantee for uniqueness (up to constant multiplications) for the solution of problem
{\mathscr{L}}u=\mu_{1}u
, subject to Robin boundary conditions. However, using results from the Fredholm theory (for the inverse of
\mathscr{L}
) we know that
\mu_{1}
has finite multiplicity, hence there exists
m
solutions of problem
\mathscr{L}u=\mu_{1}u
, subject to Robin boundary conditions,
\{\psi_{1}^{(1)},\cdots,\psi_{1}^{(m)}\}
.

Taking into account that
d_{k}=(u,\psi_{k})_{L^{2}(D)}=0
for all
k>1
and the finite multiplicity of the first eigenfunction we conclude that
u=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(u,\psi_{1}^{(\ell)})_{L^{2}(D)}\psi_{1}^{(\ell)}
, with
\mathscr{L}\psi_{1}^{(\ell)}=\mu_{1}\psi_{1}^{(\ell)}
,
\ell=1,\cdots,m
, with Robin boundary conditions. Then,

\displaystyle{\mathscr{L}}u

\displaystyle={\mathscr{L}}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(u,\psi_{1}^{(\ell)})_{L^{2}(D)}\psi_{1}^{(\ell)}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(u,\psi_{1}^{(\ell)})_{L^{2}(D)}{\mathscr{L}}\psi_{1}^{(\ell)}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(u,\psi_{1}^{(\ell)})_{L^{2}(D)}\psi_{1}^{(\ell)}\right)=\mu_{1}u,

and with a similar calculation
{\mathscr{N}}u+\beta u=0
. Hence, (
10.5
) implies (
10.3
) and the proof of this step is complete.

Step 5. We now show that if
u
is an eigenfunction of
\mathscr{L}
(with Robin boundary conditions and
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
) with eigenvalue
\mu_{1}
, then so it is
|u|
.

To show that note that since
u
is an eigenfunction of
{\mathscr{L}}
we have by (
2.8
) that

(10.10)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(u,u)=\mu_{1}=\min\{Q_{\beta}(w,w)\,\,\mid\,\,\|w\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1\}.

We recall the elementary inequality
||a|-|b||\leq|a-b|
which we apply for
a=u(x)
,
b=u(y)
to see that
{\mathscr{E}}(|u|,|u|)\leq{\mathscr{E}}(u,u)
, hence,

(10.11)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(|u|,|u|)\leq Q_{\beta}(u,u)=\mu_{1},

where we also used (
10.10
).

But since
\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
also implies that
\||u|\|_{L^{2}(D)}=1
, once more by (
10.10
) and (
10.11
)
we see that

(10.12)

\displaystyle Q_{\beta}(|u|,|u|)=\mu_{1},

which by the results of step 4 yields that
|u|
is also an eigenfunction.

We note that by the same argument, and since
u^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(|u|+u)
, by a convexity argument the same holds for
u^{+}
, and subsequently for
u^{-}
.

Step 6. By the results of step 5 we have that
\mathscr{L}|u|=\mu_{1}|u|
in
D
, which in turn implies that
\mathscr{L}|u|\geq 0
in
D
, with
|u|\geq 0
. With the comment above, same applies for
u^{+}
. By the strong maximum principle (see Theorem 1.1, or Theorem 1.2 in
[
36
]
) this implies that either
u^{+}
is strictly positive or identically zero. Exactly the same applies for
|u|
. Since having
u^{+}
and
|u|
identically zero will lead to the trivial solution, we conclude that
u^{+}>0
hence
u>0
.
