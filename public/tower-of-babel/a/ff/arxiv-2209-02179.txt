Decentralized Natural Policy Gradient with Variance Reduction for Collaborative Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning 0 0 footnotetext: Jinchi Chen and Jie Feng contribute equally to this work.
Jinchi Chen, Jie Feng, Weiguo Gao, Ke Wei

Abstract
This paper studies a policy optimization problem arising from collaborative multi-agent reinforcement learning in a decentralized setting where agents communicate with their neighbors over an undirected graph to maximize the sum of their cumulative rewards. A novel decentralized natural policy gradient method, dubbed Momentum-based Decentralized Natural Policy Gradient (MDNPG), is proposed, which incorporates natural gradient, momentum-based variance reduction, and gradient tracking into the decentralized stochastic gradient ascent framework. The 𝒪 ⁡ ( n − 1 ​ ϵ − 3 ) {\cal O}(n^{-1}\epsilon^{-3}) sample complexity for MDNPG to converge to an ϵ \epsilon -stationary point has been established under standard assumptions, where n n is the number of agents. It indicates that MDNPG can achieve the optimal convergence rate for decentralized policy gradient methods and possesses a linear speedup in contrast to centralized optimization methods. Moreover, superior empirical performance of MDNPG over other state-of-the-art algorithms has been demonstrated by extensive numerical experiments.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a sequential decision-making task in which an agent seeks a strategy that maximizes the long-term return received from the environment via interaction with the system. Recent years have witnessed considerable theoretical and empirical advances in RL, see for example

Markov decision processes (MDPs) are widely used to model how agents interact with an environment. An MDP can be defined as a tuple
\left\langle{\cal S},{\cal A},P,r,\gamma\right\rangle
, where
{\cal S}
is a finite state space,
{\cal A}
is a finite action space,
P:{\cal S}\times{\cal A}\rightarrow\Delta({\cal S})
is the state transition model which determines the probability from
(\bm{s},\bm{a})
to state
\bm{s}^{\prime}
,
r

{\cal S}\times{\cal A}\times{\cal S}\rightarrow[-1,1]
is the immediate reward function associated with the transition from
(\bm{s},\bm{a})
to
\bm{s}^{\prime}
, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor. Moreover, a policy, denoted
\pi:{\cal S}\rightarrow\Delta({\cal A})

is the probability of executing action
\bm{a}
at state
\bm{s}

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\left\{V(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\pi)}\left\{R(\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right\},

(1.1)

There are several classical categories of RL algorithms. Model-based approaches, such as policy iteration and value iteration (see e.g.,
[
8
]
) find the optimal policy based on the ideas of of fixed point iteration. Whereas in model-free settings, value-based methods, like temporal difference learning and Q-learning (see e.g.,

10

12

Coupled with neural networks, they have gained tremendous success in many applications due to their flexibility and adaptability. Moreover, the theoretical guarantees for gradient-based methods are relatively more complete, even in conjunction with simple function approximations

,
4

In this paper, we restrict our attention to policy optimization based methods. Using a parameterized policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{V(\bm{\theta}):=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{R(\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right\}.

(1.2)

After parameterization, the distribution of the trajectories, denoted

\displaystyle p(\tau|\bm{\theta}):=\rho(\bm{s}^{0})\prod_{h=0}^{H-1}\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})P(\bm{s}^{h+1}|\bm{s}^{h},\bm{a}^{h}),

(1.3)

where we recall that

A direct method for solving problem (

,
16
,
17

18

utilize the intrinsic distance between policies, i.e., the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, to modify the search direction so that parameterization invariance can be preserved. As two variants of NPG methods, trust region policy optimization (TRPO)

combines NPG with a line search procedure to guarantee improvement, whereas proximal policy optimization (PPO)

uses a simplified objective with a penalty term or a clipped ratio rather than the KL constraint.

Collaborative reinforcement learning.

•

Let

i

\bm{s}

\bm{s}

\displaystyle\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s}):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{[i]}}(\bm{a}_{i}|\bm{s}).

(1.5)

In this scenario,

\bm{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\theta}_{[1]}^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\bm{\theta}_{[n]}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

not only relies on the local parameter but also relies on the parameters of other agents.

Multi-task reinforcement learning.

Multi-task reinforcement learning (MTRL) refers to the problem in which different agents learn a shared policy in different but similar environments
[
29
]
. MTRL can utilize similarities across different environments to enhance learning efficiency and generalization. Such an approach has received a lot of attention in recent years
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. In the MTRL setting, the MDP for the
i
-th agent is expressed as
\left\langle{\cal S}_{i},{\cal A},P_{i},r_{i},\gamma_{i}\right\rangle
. The setup for different agents can differ in terms of:

•

{\cal S}_{i}
, the state space for the
i
-th environment (similar or overlapping),

•

P_{i}:{\cal S}_{i}\times{\cal A}\rightarrow\Delta({\cal S}_{i})
, the transition model for the
i
-th environment,

•

r_{i}:{\cal S}_{i}\times{\cal A}\times{\cal S}_{i}\rightarrow[-1,1]
, the reward function for the
i
-th agent,

•

\gamma_{i}
, the discount factor for the
i
-th agent.

Note that in order for the agents to share a common policy
\pi

{\cal A}
and the states in each state space
{\cal S}_{i}
must have the same format. In MTRL, though agents independently select actions in their own environments, the policy should be parameterized using a single parameter
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, yielding the parameterized policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
.

Roughly speaking, collaborative RL shares a global state space, while MTRL shares a common action space. For conciseness, we refer to both of the aforementioned settings as collaborative multi-agent reinforcement learning. It should be easy to see whether “collaborative” refers to two tasks or the particular one task from the context.

1.2
Decentralized optimization setup

Since each agent only has access to local information, solving problem (
1.4
) needs to aggregate all local computations to update the learning parameter. The centralized optimization method uses a central coordinator for data collection and information transmission, inevitably leading to high communication costs. Moreover, the central coordinator does not exist or may be too expensive to deploy in real applications. By contrast, in a decentralized framework that is considered in this paper each agent only communicates with its neighbors through a communication network. Let
{\cal G}=({\cal N},{\cal E})
be the communication network which is indeed an undirected graph, where
{\cal N}=\{1,\cdots,n\}
is the set of agents, and
{\cal E}\subseteq{\cal N}\times{\cal N}
is the collection of edges. Note that a pair
(i,j)\in{\cal E}
represents that
i
can communicate with
j
. For the
i
-th agent, define the set of its neighbors as
{\cal N}(i)=\{j\in{\cal N}|(i,j)\in{\cal E}\text{ or }i=j\}
. In addition, we can associate a weight matrix
\bm{W}=[W_{ij}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with the graph
{\cal G}
, where
W_{ij}>0
if
(i,j)\in{\cal E}
, and
W_{ij}=0
otherwise.
Assuming
{\cal G}
is a connected graph, it is not hard to see that problem (
1.4
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}_{1},\cdots,\bm{\theta}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}V_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i}),

subject to

\displaystyle\quad\bm{\theta}_{i}=\bm{\theta}_{j},\quad\text{for all }(i,j)\in{\cal E}.

1.3
Main contributions and outline of this paper

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

•

We develop a Momentum-based Decentralized Natural Policy Gradient (MDNPG) method for the collaborative MARL problem. MDNPG combines natural gradient with momentum-based variance reduction and gradient tracking to solve the decentralized optimization problem. In a nutshell, natural gradient is a gradient method with suitably chosen preconditioning. Extensive numerical experiments show that introducing this preconditioning in the decentralized setting is also able to improve the empirical performance for collaborative MARL.

•

Theoretical guarantees for MDNPG have been obtained, showing that MDNPG is able to converge to an
\epsilon
-stationary point in
{\cal O}(n^{-1}\epsilon^{-3})
iterations provided a mini-batch initialization. Even though the variance reduced decentralized policy gradient has been studied in the collaborative MARL scenario, the existing analysis does not apply directly to MDNPG due to the requirement for the consensus of the precondition matrices. To overcome this difficulty, a novel stochastic ascent inequality (see Lemma
4.1
) has been established to handle the preconditioning for the non-convex objective in the decentralized setting. This intermediate technical result is of independent interest and may be applied to the analysis of other preconditioned stochastic first order methods in decentralized non-convex optimization.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we present a complete description of MDNPG and provide theoretical guarantees for it. In addition, more closely related works are reviewed.
In Section
3
, we compare MDNPG with other state-of-the-art algorithms in single-agent and multi-agent experiments, which demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed method.
The proofs of the main results and key lemmas are presented in Section
4
.
Finally, in Section
6
, we conclude this paper with future research directions.

2.1
Three pillars in MDNPG algorithm

2.1.1
Pillar I: Decentralized optimization

To solve problem (
1.6
), each agent can first perform a local gradient update and then seek consensus with its neighbors in order to fulfill the equality constraint. This is the basic idea behind the decentralized gradient ascent method which can be expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}=\sum_{j\in{\cal N}(i)}W_{ij}(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t}+\eta\nabla V_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t})),

(2.1)

where
\eta
represents the learning rate,
\nabla V_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t})
represents the gradient of
V_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j})
with respect to
\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t}
, and
W_{ij}
are the elements of the weight matrix
\bm{W}
associated with the communication network
{\cal G}
. Note that
\bm{W}
here plays a role of weighted average for consensus which should satisfy certain properties (see Assumption
2.1
).
Despite its simplicity, the original decentralized gradient ascent suffers from slow convergence. To address this issue, a gradient tracking technique has been developed in
[
34
,
35
]
, of which the central idea is to correct biases between local copies of
\bm{\theta}
via tracking the average gradient, i.e.,
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla V_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i})
. The modified version of update (
2.1
) with gradient tracking consists of the following two steps:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{y}_{i}^{t+1}&=\sum_{j\in{\cal N}(i)}W_{ij}\left(\bm{y}^{t}_{j}+\nabla V_{j}(\bm{\theta}^{t}_{j})-\nabla V_{j}(\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{j})\right),\\
\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}&=\sum_{j\in{\cal N}(i)}W_{ij}\left(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t}+\eta\bm{y}_{j}^{t+1}\right),\end{split}

(2.2)

where
\bm{y}_{i}
denotes the gradient tracker for agent
i
. Simple calculation shows that (
2.2
) satisfies the dynamic average consensus property:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{y}_{i}^{t+1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla V_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i}),\quad t\geq 1,

which implies that the average of
\nabla V_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})
is dynamically tracked by the average of
\bm{y}_{i}^{t+1}
. Further, it can be proved that decentralized optimization methods equipped with gradient tracking can achieve better convergence rate
[
34
]
.

2.1.2
Pillar II: Variance reduction

Consider the optimization problem

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left\{f(\bm{\theta};\xi)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\},

where
\xi
represents a random variable drawn from an unknown distribution
{\cal D}
. The stochastic gradient ascent at the
t
-th iteration is given as

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{t}+\eta\cdot\bm{g}(\bm{\theta}^{t};\xi^{t}),

where
\eta
is the learning rate and
\bm{g}(\bm{\theta}^{t};\xi^{t})=\nabla f(\bm{\theta}^{t};\xi^{t})
is the gradient estimator with
\xi^{t}
being independently sampled from
{\cal D}
. Due to the high variance incurred by the stochastic evaluation of the gradient, vanilla stochastic gradient methods suffer from slow convergence. Thus, in order to accelerate the methods, various variance reduction methods have been proposed and studied in the past decades, such as SVRG
[
36
]
, SAGA
[
37
]
, SARAH
[
38
]
, and SPIDER
[
39
]
. More recently, a momentum-based variance reduction method
[
40
,
41
]
is proposed, in which the gradient estimator is given by

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{t}=\beta\underbrace{\bm{g}(\bm{\theta}^{t};\xi^{t})}_{\text{SGD}}+(1-\beta)\underbrace{\left(\bm{v}^{t-1}+\bm{g}(\bm{\theta}^{t};\xi^{t})-\bm{g}(\bm{\theta}^{t-1};\xi^{t})\right)}_{\text{SARAH}},

(2.3)

where
\beta\in(0,1]
is the momentum parameter. A key feature of the momentum-based method is that it is a single-loop algorithm which leverages the benefits of both the unbiased SGD estimator
[
42
]
and the novel SARAH estimator
[
38
]
. Thus it can avoid the high computational cost of batch gradients to reduce variance.

In this work, we will adopt the momentum-based variance reduction method for the policy gradient estimation. The gradient of
V_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i})
in (
1.6
) with respect to
\bm{\theta}_{i}
can be computed as follows

\displaystyle\nabla V_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{i}\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}_{i})}\left\{R(\tau_{i})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau_{i}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}p(\tau_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i})R(\tau_{i})d\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau_{i}}p(\tau_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i})\frac{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}p(\tau_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i})}{p(\tau_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i})}R(\tau_{i})d\tau_{i}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{i}\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}_{i})}\left\{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\log p(\tau_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i})R(\tau_{i})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

(2.4)

The commonly used gradient estimators of policy gradient include REINFORCE
[
11
]
or GPOMDP
[
43
]
. For the
i
-th agent, we adopt REINFORCE with a baseline
b_{i}
as the policy gradient estimator:

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{i}(\tau_{i}|\bm{\theta}_{i})=\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right]\cdot\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}r^{h}_{i}-b_{i}\right],

(2.5)

where
\tau_{i}
denotes a trajectory generated under policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
. Without loss of generality, we use the same
\gamma
for all agents.

Note that in the momentum-based gradient estimator (
2.3
) for the ordinary stochastic optimization, the
\xi^{t}
sampled from
\mathcal{D}
is independent of
\bm{\theta}^{t}
. However, in (
2.5
), the sampled trajectory
\tau^{t}_{i}
is determined by the distribution
p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})
. It is easily seen that
\mathbf{g}_{i}(\tau^{t}_{i}|\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i})
is a biased estimator for
\nabla V(\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i})
. To ensure the unbiased property, we can utilize the importance sampling technique,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau_{i}^{t}\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})}\left\{\omega(\tau_{i}^{t}|\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})\mathbf{g}_{i}(\tau_{i}^{t}|\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=\nabla V_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i}),

where
\omega(\tau^{t}_{i}|\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})
represents the importance weight defined as

\displaystyle\omega(\tau^{t}_{i}|\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i},\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})=\frac{p(\tau^{t}_{i}|\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i})}{p(\tau^{t}_{i}|\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i})}=\prod_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{\pi_{\bm{\theta}^{t-1}_{i}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})}{\pi_{\bm{\theta}^{t}_{i}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})}.

(2.6)

Therefore, the momentum-based variance reduction (
2.3
) for the policy gradient of the
i
-th agent, denoted
\bm{v}^{t}_{i}
, is given by
[
44
]
:

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{i}^{t}=\beta\bm{g}_{i}(\tau_{i}^{t}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t})+(1-\beta)\left(\bm{v}_{i}^{t-1}+\bm{g}_{i}(\tau_{i}^{t}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t})-\omega(\tau_{i}^{t}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t-1},\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t})\cdot\bm{g}_{i}(\tau_{i}^{t}|\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t-1})\right).

2.1.3
Pillar III: Natural policy gradient

In order to introduce the natural policy gradient method, we consider the policy optimization problem (
1.2
):

\displaystyle\max_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{V(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{R(\tau)\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right\},

where
\tau
is the
H
-horizon trajectory and
p(\tau|\bm{\theta})
given by (
1.3
). The PG update
\bm{\theta}^{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{t}+\eta\nabla V(\bm{\theta}^{t})
is a gradient ascent method over the parameter space, which is also the minimizer of the following problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\left\langle-\nabla V(\bm{\theta}^{t}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{t}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}.

(2.7)

However, since the objective function essentially relies on the distributions of
\tau
, it is more natural to conduct a search over distribution space, leading to the following sub-problem for updating
\bm{\theta}^{t}
:

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\left\langle-\nabla V(\bm{\theta}^{t}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta}\KL(p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t});p(\tau|\bm{\theta})),

(2.8)

where the KL divergence is used to enable the search around
p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t})
over the distribution space.

Since
\KL(p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t});p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t}))=0
and
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}^{t}}\KL(p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t});p(\tau|\bm{\theta}))=\bm{0}
, one can approximate
\KL(p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t});p(\tau|\bm{\theta}))
by its second order information and thus approximate (
2.8
) by

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\theta}}\left\langle-\nabla V(\bm{\theta}^{t}),\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{t}\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2\eta}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{t})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{F}(\bm{\theta}^{t})(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{t}),

(2.9)

where
\bm{F}(\bm{\theta}^{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta}^{t})}\left\{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}
is the Fisher information matrix (FIM) of
p(\tau|\bm{\theta}^{t})
and
\bm{F}(\bm{\theta}^{t})^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\bm{F}(\bm{\theta}^{t})
. It can be easily seen that the optimal solution to (
2.9
) is given by

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}^{t+1}=\bm{\theta}^{t}+\eta\bm{F}(\bm{\theta}^{t})^{\dagger}\nabla V(\bm{\theta}^{t}),

(2.10)

which yields the natural policy gradient update.

Given the definition of
p(\tau|\bm{\theta})
in (
1.3
), the FIM can be further expressed as

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\left(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)\left(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

(2.11)

Here the second line has used the fact that the cross term is equal to
0
, which can be easily verified. When
H\rightarrow\infty
, the FIM may not be well determined. There are two typical ways to deal with this issue:

•

Averaged case. Let
\tau
be a trajectory induced by
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
up to horizon
H
. The FIM in the average case is given by

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\lim_{H\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{H}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\},

(2.12)

It has been shown by
[
16
,
45
]
that (
2.12
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}\sim d^{\pi_{\theta}},\bm{a}\sim\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\bm{s})}\left\{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\},

where
d^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}
is the stationary distribution of state.

•

Discounted case. On the other hand, one can consider infinite horizon but introduce a discounted factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
. In this situation, the FIM is given by

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\sum_{h=0}^{+\infty}\gamma^{h}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}.

(2.13)

Moreover, letting
\tau
be the
H
-horizon trajectory induced by
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
, where
H
obeys the geometric distribution with parameter
1-\gamma
, then
F(\bm{\theta})
in (
2.13
) is indeed the FIM associated with the random-length trajectory
\tau
. That is
[
16
,
45
]
,

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{H\sim\Geo(1-\gamma)}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\Bigg|H\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}\sim d_{\rho}^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}},\bm{a}\sim\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\cdot|\bm{s})}\left\{\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\},

where
d_{\rho}^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\bm{s})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{s}^{0}\sim\rho}\left\{(1-\gamma)\sum_{h=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{h}P(\bm{s}^{h}=\bm{s}|\bm{s}^{0},\pi_{\bm{\theta}})\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}
is the discounted state visitation distribution under the initial distribution
\rho
. Such formulation has been widely used in the literature
[
15
,
18
,
4
]
.

With a slight abuse of notation, we will use the following definition of FIM in this paper:

\displaystyle\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\},

(2.14)

which agrees with (
2.11
) up to a scale.

In contrast to PG, NPG can be approximately viewed as a second-order method since the FIM serves as a structured preconditioner based on the underlying structure of the parameterized policy space
[
46
,
47
]
. Such a preconditioner can adaptively adjust the update direction to improve the convergence rate. In Algorithm
1
, we have extended the NPG update (
2.10
) to the decentralized multi-agent setting (
1.6
). For the
i
-th agent at the
t
-th iteration, we have

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{i}^{t+1}=\sum_{j\in{\cal N}(i)}W_{ij}\left(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t}+\eta\bm{H}_{j}^{t}\nabla V_{j}(\bm{\theta}^{t}_{j})\right),

where
\bm{H}_{j}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of
\bm{F}_{j}(\bm{\theta}_{j}^{t})
.
Namely, each agent searches along the (preconditioned) natural gradient direction of its own before the consensus.

In addition, the following lemma establishes that the FIM in the collaborative RL setting mentioned in Section
1.1
is indeed a block diagonal matrix for each agent due to the product structure of the joint policy, see (
1.5
).

Lemma 2.1
.

In collaborative RL, let
\tau_{i}
be the
H
-horizon trajectory induced by the policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}
for agent
i
. The FIM of the
i
-th agent
\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is given by

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i})=\diag\left(\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{[1]}),\cdots,\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{[n]})\right),

where

\displaystyle\bm{F}_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{[j]})=\frac{1}{H}E_{\tau_{i}\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{[j]}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{[j]}}(\bm{a}^{h}_{j}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{[j]}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}_{[j]}}(\bm{a}^{h}_{j}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{T}\right\}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}\times d_{j}},

(2.15)

for
j=1,\cdots,n
.

2.2
Theoretical result

Before stating the main convergence result in Theorem
2.4
, we first introduce some standard assumptions.

Assumption 2.1
.

The weight matrix
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
associated with the communication graph
{\cal G}
is doubly stochastic, i.e.,
\bm{W}\bm{1}_{n}=\bm{1}_{n}
and
\bm{1}^{\mathsf{T}}_{n}\bm{W}=\bm{1}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, one can show that
[
48
]

\displaystyle\rho:=\left\|\bm{W}-\frac{1}{n}\bm{1}_{n}\bm{1}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|\in[0,1).

(2.16)

Assumption 2.2
.

The objective function
V(\bm{\theta})
is upper bounded, i.e.,
V^{\star}:=\sup_{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}V(\bm{\theta})<+\infty
.

Assumption 2.3
.

Let
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})
be the policy parameterized by
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. There are constants
G
and
M
such that the gradient and Hessian of the log-density of the policy function obey that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\leq G\text{ and }\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}|\bm{s})\right\|\leq M

for any
\bm{a}\in{\cal A}
and
\bm{s}\in{\cal S}
.

Assumption 2.4
.

The variance of
\omega(\tau|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta})
, the importance sampling weight defined in (
2.6
), is bounded,

\displaystyle\Var(\omega(\tau|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}))\leq W,

for any
\bm{\theta},\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\tau\sim p_{i}(\cdot|\bm{\theta})
.

The following two auxiliary lemmas can be obtained from Assumptions
2.3
and
2.4
.

Lemma 2.2

(Proposition 5.2 in
[
49
]
)
.

Under Assumption
2.3
, one has the following facts:

•

The objective function
V(\bm{\theta})
is
L
-smooth with
L=H(M+HG)/(1-\gamma)
.

•

Let
\bm{g}_{i}(\tau;\bm{\theta})
be the gradient estimator defined in (
2.5
). Then for all
\bm{\theta},\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, one has

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{g}_{i}(\tau;\bm{\theta})-\bm{g}_{i}(\tau;\widetilde{\bm{\theta}})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}\leq L_{g}\left\|\bm{\theta}-\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}

and
\left\|\bm{g}_{i}(\tau;\bm{\theta})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}\leq C_{g}
for all
i\in[n]
, where
L_{g}=HM(1+|b|)/(1-\gamma)
and
C_{g}=HG^{1/2}(1+|b|)/(1-\gamma)
.

Lemma 2.3

(Lemma 3 in
[
28
]
)
.

Under Assumption
2.3
and
2.4
, one has

\displaystyle\Var(\omega(\tau|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta}))=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\left(\omega(\tau|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}},\bm{\theta})-1\right)^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}=C_{\omega}^{2}\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\theta}}-\bm{\theta}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2},

(2.17)

where
C_{\omega}^{2}=H(2HG+M)(W+1)
.

The next assumption states that the variance of the gradient estimator is bounded, which is commonly used in stochastic optimization.

Assumption 2.5
.

There exists a constant
\nu_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\Var(g_{i}(\tau;\bm{\theta}))=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\bm{g}_{i}(\tau;\bm{\theta})-\nabla V_{i}(\bm{\theta})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{i}^{2}

(2.18)

for all policy
\pi_{\bm{\theta}}
, where
\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})
. Define
\bar{\nu}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}^{2}
.

Assumption 2.6
.

Let
\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be the FIM defined in (
2.14
).
There exists a constant
\mu_{F}>0
such that
\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})\succeq\mu_{F}\bm{I}_{d}
for all
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The positive definiteness on FIM is fairly standard in the analysis of single-agent NPG algorithm
[
50
]
and is often made in both convex and nonconvex optimizations for establishing the convergence of preconditioned algorithms
[
51
,
52
,
53
,
54
]
. Moreover, this assumption can be always satisfied if we use
\bm{F}+\epsilon\bm{I}_{d}
for
\epsilon>0
instead of
\bm{F}
as the precondition matrix.

Let
\bm{H}_{i}
be the inverse FIM
\bm{F}(\bm{\theta}_{i})
for the
i
-th agent with policy parameter
\bm{\theta}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then Assumptions
2.3
and
2.6
imply that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{G}\bm{I}_{d}\preccurlyeq\bm{H}_{i}^{t}\preccurlyeq\frac{1}{\mu_{F}}\bm{I}_{d},

(2.19)

where the lower bound holds since the fact
\left\|\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})\right\|\leq G
. Moreover, this fact can be proved as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{F}(\bm{\theta})\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim p(\cdot|\bm{\theta})}\left\{\left\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\left(\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\log\pi_{\bm{\theta}}(\bm{a}^{h}|\bm{s}^{h})\right)^{\mathsf{T}}\right\|\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq G,

where the last line is due to Assumption
2.3
.

We are in position to present the main result of this paper.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\bm{\theta}_{\out}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the output of Algorithm
1
. Suppose that

\displaystyle 0<\eta<\frac{\mu_{F}(1-\rho^{2})^{3}}{\kappa_{F}\sqrt{1632000(L^{2}+\Phi^{2})}}

and choose
\beta
such that
\frac{1632000(L^{2}+\Phi^{2})\kappa_{F}^{2}\eta^{2}}{n\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{6}}\leq\beta<\frac{1}{n}
, where
\Phi^{2}=L_{g}^{2}+C_{g}^{2}C_{\omega}^{2}
and
\kappa_{F}=G/\mu_{F}
.
Then under Assumptions
2.1
-
2.6
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\nabla V(\bm{\theta}_{\out})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8G^{2}\Delta}{T\eta\mu_{F}}+\frac{76\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{nT\beta B}+\frac{152\beta\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{n}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{44\rho^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{TB(1-\rho^{2})}+\frac{352\beta^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}+\frac{352\beta\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{TB(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}+\frac{704\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}\beta^{3}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}+\frac{44\rho^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{nT(1-\rho^{2})}\left\|\widetilde{\nabla V}(\bm{\theta}^{0})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}.

(2.20)

Remark 2.2
.

If we choose
\eta
and
\beta
according to Theorem
2.4
, then the mean squared stationary gap
\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\nabla V(\bm{\theta}_{\out})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}
converges to a steady-state error as
T\rightarrow\infty
at a rate of
{\cal O}(1/T)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\nabla V(\bm{\theta}_{\out})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\rightarrow{\cal O}\left(\frac{\beta\bar{\nu}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\beta^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}\right),\quad T\rightarrow\infty.

It can be seen that the steady-state error will decrease as the number of agents increases. Moreover, the second term in the steady-state error indicates that the impact of communication graph
{\cal G}
through
\rho
can be reduced with small
\beta
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Choose step size
\eta
, momentum parameter
\beta
, and batch size
B
in initialization such that

\displaystyle\eta=\frac{\mu_{F}n^{2/3}}{\kappa_{F}\sqrt{L^{2}+\Phi^{2}}T^{1/3}},\beta=\frac{n^{1/3}}{T^{2/3}}\text{ and }B=\left\lceil\frac{T^{1/3}}{n^{2/3}}\right\rceil.

Then for all
T>\frac{1632000^{3/2}n^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{9}}
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\nabla V(\bm{\theta}_{\out})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8\Delta\kappa_{F}^{3}\sqrt{L^{2}+\Phi}+228\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{(nT)^{2/3}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{44\rho^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}\left\|\widetilde{\nabla V}(\bm{\theta}^{0})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})}\cdot\frac{1}{nT}+\frac{396\kappa_{F}^{2}\bar{\nu}^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{n^{2/3}}{T^{4/3}}+\frac{1056\bar{\nu}^{2}\kappa_{F}^{2}}{(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{T^{2}}.

Remark 2.3
.

Corollary
2.5
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\nabla V(\bm{\theta}_{\out})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}={\cal O}((nT)^{-2/3})

when
T
is large enough. Thus one can achieve
\varepsilon
-stationary, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\nabla V(\bm{\theta}_{\out})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
, in
{\cal O}(n^{-1}\varepsilon^{-3})
iteration complexity, which shows that MDNPG also enjoys the linear speedup convergence rate.

2.3
Related work

In this section, we discuss the recent progress that is mostly related to our work, especially those gradient based methods in reinforcement learning and decentralized stochastic optimization.

Single-agent (natural) policy gradient.

Inspired by stochastic optimization, there has been extensive research in designing variance reduction methods for policy gradient estimator
[
55
,
56
,
49
,
57
,
44
]
. For instance, Papini et al.
[
55
]
show that SVRPG achieves an
\epsilon
-stationary point given
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-4})
trajectories. Xu et al.
[
49
]
improve this sample complexity to
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-10/3})
. Moreover, SRVR-PG
[
56
]
and HAPG
[
57
]
can obtain an
\epsilon
-stationary point provided
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-3})
trajectories, both of which are nearly optimal in the sample complexity
[
58
]
. However, these methods require large batches or double-loop updates. Recently,
[
44
]
incorporates the momentum-based variance reduction technique in policy gradient methods and achieve the sample complexity of
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-3})
with a single trajectory at each iteration. The global convergence of policy gradients with variance reduction has also been studied in
[
59
,
50
]
.

As already mentioned, NPG
[
15
]
and its generalizations, such as TRPO
[
19
]
and PPO
[
20
]
, are widely used in RL. There has been a lot of interest in understanding the theoretical performance of this class of methods, see
[
4
,
60
,
61
]
and the references therein. Different variance reduction techniques have also been utilized in NPG. For example, SRVR-NPG is proposed in
[
50
]
which reaches a sample complexity of
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-3})
. In addition, two variance reduced mirror ascent methods, named VRMPO and VR-BGPO, are developed in
[
62
]
and
[
63
]
, respectively. These methods reduces to NPG with variance reduction if a special mirror mapping is used.

Multi-agent policy gradient.

For collaborative RL problem, many decentralized policy gradient algorithms have been developed. Lu et al.
[
64
]
study a decentralized policy gradient method in safe MARL and show that an
\epsilon
-stationary point can be achieved from
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-4})
iterations. Zhao et al.
[
33
]
study the convergence of decentralized policy gradient with variance reduction and gradient tracking in collaborative RL and establish the sample complexity of
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-3})
. However, the method in
[
33
]
requires very large batch gradients to obtain this optimal complexity. In contrast, Jiang et al.
[
28
]
adopt the momentum-based variance reduction technique for decentralized policy gradient which also achieves the optimal sample complexity but only uses a single trajectory in each iteration. For the MTRL problem, various policy gradient methods have been developed and studied. In
[
65
,
66
]
, a distributed framework is used to solve the learning problem. However, in these works, each agent collects local data, which are then shared to a centralized coordination. In a subsequent work, a decentralized policy gradient method is proposed in
[
29
]
. However, the proposed method only adopts the vanilla gradient ascent without gradient tracking and variance reduction, thus resulting in a sample complexity of
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-4})
. In addition, decentralized optimization methods have also been studied in the framework of policy evaluation
[
67
,
68
,
69
]
.

Decentralized optimization.

In general, decentralized online optimization has been extensively studied for non-convex problems. There are many algorithms developed toward this line of research, including DSGD
[
70
]
, EXTRA
[
71
]
, and Exact Diffusion
[
72
]
. To achieve the lower oracle complexity, various kinds of variance reduced techniques have been utilized. The D-GET proposed in
[
73
]
is built upon gradient tracking and the SARAH gradient estimator and achieves an oracle complexity of
{\cal O}(\epsilon^{-3})
. The same oracle complexity is also obtained by D-SPIDER-SFO
[
74
]
which uses SPIDER in the variance reduction step. Recently, built on the hybrid variance reduction scheme introduced in
[
40
,
75
]
, the GT-HSGD is developed in
[
76
]
which can achieve an
\epsilon
-approximate ﬁrst-order stationary point within
{\cal O}(n^{-1}\epsilon^{-3})
samples for each node. It is worth noting that these approaches are designed for oblivious objective functions, where the randomness of data samples is independent of the optimized parameters.

3.1
Single-agent experiments

When
n=1
, MDNPG reduces to the single-agent NPG with momentum-based variance reduction, which can also be viewed as BGPO
[
63
]
with special mirror mappings. In this subsection, we compare the single-agent version of MDNPG with the momentum-based policy gradient
[
44
]
, PPO
[
20
]
, and SRVR-NPG (NPG with variance reduction via SRVR)
[
50
]
over two single-agent environments: GridWorld and MountainCar.

In a
10\times 10
GridWorld, an agent at a random initial position try to reach the grid labeled as “goal” and at the same time avoid grids labeled as “obstacle” in a minimum number of steps. Five obstacle grids are set up in our experiments. The agent can select one of four discrete actions (up, down, right, and left) to move to another grid, and the state is simply the location of the agent. The received reward is
-0.1\times\mbox{(distance to the goal)}\pm 10
, up to whether the goal is reached or the agent falls into an obstacle. The other environment, called MountainCar, is a continuous control task from OpenAI Gym
[
77
]
, in which the goal of an agent is to reach the top of the hill. A detailed description of the environment is provided in
[
77
]
.

In our implementation, a one-hidden-layer (of size 128) neural network with ReLU activation function is used to parameterize the policy. For the GirdWorld task, the parameterized policy can be obtained via a softmax layer. For the MountainCar task, the outputs of the network are
(\mu_{\bm{\theta}}(s),\sigma_{\bm{\theta}}(s))
, the mean and standard deviation of a Gaussian distribution. Moreover, we also use a value network (one hidden layer of size 128, with ReLU as the activation function) for the estimation of value functions. All the parameters in the algorithms are finely tuned for the pursuit of better performance.

The plots of average return and standard deviation over five random instances against the number of iterations are presented in Figure
1
. It can be observed that overall the momentum-based NPG method displays better convergence and stability than the other algorithms for both tasks. Note that even though SRVR-NPG is competitive with the momentum-based NPG method, the former one costs significantly more time and memory due to its double-loop nature for variance reduction.

We first introduce some convenient notations. Letting

be the local variable for
i

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\theta}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&\bm{\theta}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}.

Let

1

\displaystyle\bm{y}^{t+1}=\left(\bm{W}\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\right)(\bm{y}^{t}+\bm{v}^{t}-\bm{v}^{t-1})\text{ and }\bm{\theta}^{t+1}=\left(\bm{W}\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\right)(\bm{\theta}^{t}+\eta\bm{d}^{t}).

(4.1)

Let

be the average of

over all the agents, i.e,

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\theta}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{\theta}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}(\bm{1}_{n}^{\mathsf{T}}\otimes\bm{I}_{d})\bm{\theta}.

Similarly,

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{y}}^{t+1}=\bar{\bm{v}}^{t}\text{ and }\bar{\bm{\theta}}^{t+1}=\bar{\bm{\theta}}^{t}+\eta\bar{\bm{d}}^{t}.

\displaystyle\left\|\left((\bm{W}-n^{-1}\bm{J})\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\right)\bm{a}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}=\left\|\left((\bm{W}-n^{-1}\bm{J})\otimes\bm{I}_{d}\right)(\bm{a}-\bm{1}_{n}\otimes\bar{\bm{a}})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16A_{1}\eta^{2}}{\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}+2\left(\frac{4G^{2}\eta^{2}}{\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{3}}+\frac{8\eta^{2}}{\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{960\Phi^{2}G^{2}\eta^{2}}{\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}\right)\sum_{t=0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\bm{d}^{t}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16A_{1}\eta^{2}}{\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{2}}+\frac{10G^{2}\eta^{2}}{\mu_{F}^{2}(1-\rho^{2})^{3}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\bm{d}^{t}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\rule{0.0pt}{8.53581pt}\right\},

which completes the proof.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we propose a novel decentralized algorithm named MDNPG for MARL. We have established the sample complexity for local convergence of MDNPG, which achieves the best available rate. The key ingredient to our development is a new stochastic ascent inequality for non-convex objectives, which could be of independent interest. Numerical results have demonstrated the efficiency of the proposed method.

There are several interesting directions for future research. Firstly, it is natural to study the global convergence of MDNPG and extend our framework to the class of entropy-regularized natural policy gradient methods in MARL.
Secondly, the Fisher information matrix in this paper is empirically estimated by sample averaging, which may incur large variance. Thus, we may also consider variance reduction for the estimation of the precondition matrix. Lastly, though importance sampling is widely used to address the varying data distribution issue when developing variance reduced policy gradient methods, there are also a few recent works
[
57
,
79
]
which instead use a hessian-based technique in the single-agent setting. Therefore, it is also interesting to investigate whether importance sampling can be removed in the multi-agent setting when developing decentralized (natural) policy gradient methods.
