Physical constraints in intracellular signaling: the cost of sending a bit
Samuel J. Bryant, Benjamin B. Machta

Abstract
One of the primary computational requirements of a cellular system is the ability to transfer information between spatially separated components. To accomplish this, biology uses diverse physical channels including production or release of second-messengers molecules and electrical depolarization of the plasma membrane. To send reliable information, these processes must dissipate energy to compete with thermal noise, in some cases consuming a substantial fraction of the cellular energy budget. Here we bound the energetic efficiency of several physical strategies for communication, using tools from information theory and the fluctuation dissipation relations to quantify communication through a channel corrupted by thermal noise. We find a minimum energetic cost, in k B k_{B} T/bit for sending information as a function of the size of the sender and receiver, their spatial separation, and the communication latency. From these calculations construct a phase diagram indicating where each strategy is most efficient. In addition, these calculations provide an estimate for the energy costs associated with information processing arising from the physical constraints of the cellular environment.

All life must couple entropically unfavorable changes to the dissipation of energy.
Some of this energy is consumed to perform mechanical tasks for which thermodynamics provides a conceptual framework for understanding energy consumption.

However, biological systems are subject to constraints often not captured in these theoretical abstractions.
In particular, information processing networks are by nature distributed in space, and therefore it is necessary to send information over physical distances under specific time constraints.
For example, chemoreceptors in bacteria measure environmental information which must then travel

In neurons, information arriving at synapses in distant dendrites must travel across the cell body, perhaps a millimeter in length, in timescales of milliseconds.

The schemes that have evolved to move information across space quickly are varied, not just in their molecular details, but in their underlying physics.
In neurons, signals are transmitted electrically via the opening of ion channels which depolarize the membrane, causing distant changes in electrical potential.
All cells signal through the creation, release or modification of second messenger molecules which diffuse to distant targets.
At the organism level, pressure waves transmit information over longer distances in the form of sound.

While moving information is not a process that has a fundamental energetic cost, the practical costs can be substantial - a large fraction

2
Overview of Work

In this manuscript we estimate bounds for this energetic cost of sending information. We examine several physical communication strategies used by biology): 1) electrical signaling via the depolarization of membranes through ion channels, 2) diffusive signaling in 2D and 3D, and 3) acoustic signaling.
The resulting energetic costs, in
k_{B}T
per bit, depend on four key parameters: the distance the signal is sent
r
, the signal frequency
\omega
, and the size of the sender/receiver
\sigma_{I}
/
\sigma_{O}
.
The bounds we find do not represent fundamental costs associated with information processing, but instead represent costs associated with the constraints that biology faces, subject to life’s practical existence in a watery buffer.
As such our bounds contain not just pure numbers and the thermal energy
k_{B}T
, but also physical diffusion constants, the plasma membrane’s capacitance, and the cytoplasm’s electrical conductivity and viscosity.

We find that each physical strategy is characterized by a functional scaling form that describes the cost of sending information over a distance, with particular dependence on the size of the sensors.
Each strategy also has a characteristic lengthscale
\ell
, which depends on its associated physical constants and the frequency of the signal, setting an upper limit on how far it can be used efficiently.
When the transmission distance
r
exceeds this characteristic lengthscale
\ell
, the signal becomes exponentially costly to send.
From these characteristic lengthscales, we can determine when certain signaling schemes are optimal and when they are forbidden (fig.
2
b).

Like previous work, we have used thermodynamic tools to place energetic bounds on information processing at the cellular level.
However, our approach is novel in considering the practical constraints imposed by the physical environments available to biology.
Our results show that there are large energetic costs associated with biological function that cannot be obtained from purely abstract thermodynamic and information theoretic considerations.
We believe that this approach will help bridge the gap between the experimentally observed energetic usage and the weak energetic bounds found in the theoretical community.

Energetic cost of sending information

Here we introduce the energetic-information framework (summarized in fig.
1
a-b) we use to compute the cost of sending information across physical space.
We suppose that there is a sender,
I
, and a receiver,
O
, embedded in a background medium
\lambda({\bf x},t)
subject to thermal fluctuations.
The sender transmits a time-varying signal
I(t)
by locally perturbing the medium
\lambda
.
The receiver then measures the signal by observing
O(t)
, the local deviation of the medium
\lambda
from equilibrium.
From this measurement, the receiver is able to extract information about the state of the sender.
We refer to
I(t)
and
O(t)
as the input and output.
The locality of the input and output behavior is quantified by a Gaussian density profile, as defined in fig.
1
a.

\lambda
is a density field, whose nature and dynamics depend on the specific communication medium (see table
1
).

We define the rate of information transfer between the sender and receiver as the time-series mutual information rate between
I(t)
and
O(t)

tostevin_mutual_2009
, measured in bits per second.
Following
tostevin_mutual_2009
, we assume that we are in the weak signal regime and further that noise is dominated by thermal fluctuations in
\lambda
.
With this assumption, the rate that information is sent from the input to the output (in bits/sec) is given by:

R(I,O)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\log 2}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\frac{|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}S^{I}(\omega)}{S^{O}(\omega)}

(1)

where
|\chi^{OI}|^{2}
and
S^{O}(\omega)
correspond to the transmission gain and noise, as defined in fig.
1
b, and where
S^{I}(\omega)
is the power spectrum of the signal process, chosen by the sender, and thus external to the network itself.

To compute the energetic cost of signaling, we need to compare the information rate to the rate of work required to produce the input signal. We characterize this dissipation rate
\dot{W}
by a dissipation kernel function
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
, describing the rate of energy dissipation at each signal frequency (fig.
1
b). If we consider signals localized in frequency space, we can describe the overall cost per bit to send a signal at frequency
\omega
as the ratio of the dissipation rate integrand to the information rate integrand (see methods sec.
8.1
):

\mathcal{C}(\omega)\equiv\frac{\textnormal{Cost}}{\textnormal{bit}}\equiv\frac{\mathrm{d}\dot{W}}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\left/\frac{\mathrm{d}R}{\mathrm{d}\omega}\right.=4\pi\log 2\frac{\mathcal{D}(\omega)S^{O}(\omega)}{|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}}

(2)

To compute this quantity for a given model, we need the equilibrium noise spectrum
S^{O}(\omega)
, the dissipation kernel
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
, and the transmission coefficient
\chi^{OI}(\omega)
.
In section
4
, we sketch this analysis explicitly for the case of electrical signaling in membranes to illustrate the techniques.
The analysis for the other systems (diffusive and acoustic signaling) follows similar procedures and details are provided in the methods section with key intermediates summarized in table
1
.
A complete derivation of this result for all systems, including asymptotic behavior, can be found in appendix
B
-
D
.

Application to Electrical Signaling Between Ion Channels

We suppose there is a signaling process between two membrane-bound ion channels embedded in a 2D membrane, as depicted in fig.
1
c.
The membrane is an infinite 2D capacitor with capacitance
c
(F/m
2
) and surface charge density
\lambda(x,y)
.
It’s embedded within a bulk conductor extending into the
z
direction, with conductance
\alpha
(
\Omega
/m).
Free charges can accumulate along the membrane but may not exist in the bulk.
Following fig
1
a, the input of the system,
I(t)
, is the time-varying flow of current through the sender ion channel located at the origin, spread out over a 2D Gaussian disk of radius
\sigma_{I}
.
The output of the system,
O(t)
, is the excess charge measured over a Gaussian disk of radius
\sigma_{O}
at the receiver ion channel located at
\bm{r}
.
Here we assume an infinite flat membrane, though the geometry of specific systems is likely important, for example in roughly cylindrical axons and dendrites.

4.1
Linearized dynamics

To compute the quantities needed for eq. (
2
), we first need a minimal model for the dynamics of
\lambda
.
The behavior of the bulk is described by the potential
V(x,y,z)
obeying Laplace’s equation
\nabla^{2}V=0
for
z>0
.
At the membrane, the voltage is given by the local capacitance equation:
V(x,y,z=0)=\lambda(x,y)/c-h(x,y)
where
h
is an artificial external field useful in calculating the spectrum of thermal charge fluctuations.
Bulk current flows according to
-\alpha\nabla V
, which is divergenceless everywhere except at the membrane.
Thus, the rate of change of charge at the membrane is given by the sum of the injected current,
J(x,y)
, and the rate that charge accumulates from bulk currents:
\partial_{t}\lambda(x,y)=J(x,y)+\alpha\partial_{z}V(x,y,z)|_{z=0}
.
These equations are linear, and in
xy
-Fourier space they close in terms of
\lambda
and applied fields
h
and
J
yielding:

\partial_{t}\lambda(\bm{k},t)=-\alpha k\left[\frac{\lambda(\bm{k},t)}{c}-h(\bm{k},t)\right]+J(\bm{k},t)

(3)

Here
\bm{k}=(k_{x},k_{y})
is the
xy
-momentum vector and all given quantities are implicit Fourier transforms.
This result will be used to derive the transmission coefficient,
\chi^{OI}
(the linear response function of
O
to
I

zwanzig_nonequilibrium_2001
;
tong_lectures_2012
), the dissipation kernel,
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
, and, in conjunction with the fluctuation dissipation theorem, the noise in the output signal,
S^{O}(\omega)

zwanzig_nonequilibrium_2001
;
tong_lectures_2012
.

4.2
Calculating Transmission Strength

The transmission strength is characterized by the linear response function
\chi^{OI}
which indicates how the mean output
\langle O(t)\rangle
responds to the input
I(t)
.
As an intermediary step, we first compute the response of the charge density to the input signal,
\chi^{\lambda J}
in frequency space by reading off the frequency-space Fourier transform of eq. (
3
):
\chi^{\lambda J}(k,\omega)=c/\alpha(|k|+i\omega c/\alpha)
.
By then integrating
\chi^{\lambda J}
over the sensor area, and making the assumption that
r\gg\sigma_{O}
,
\sigma_{I}
, we get the transmission coefficient
\chi^{OI}
:

\displaystyle|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}=\frac{c^{2}\sigma_{O}^{4}}{\alpha^{2}r^{2}}U_{S}\left(\frac{r}{\ell(\omega)}\right)

\displaystyle\ell(\omega)=\frac{\alpha}{\omega c}

(4)

where
U_{S}
is a universal function of its argument that goes to
1
when
r/\ell(\omega)\ll 1
and then exponentially decays (see appendix
B
for a more complete derivation including its explicit form and asymptotic behavior).
Very importantly, we have expressed this universal function in terms of the lengthscale
\ell(\omega)=\alpha/\omega c
which sets an upper limit on the viability of transmission.
When the transmission distance

exceeds
\ell(\omega)

U_{S}
falls off exponentially.
The origin of this lengthscale is related to the RC timescale found in basic RC circuits.
The 3D bulk resistance and 2D membrane capacitance together define an RC (inverse)

4.3
Calculating Dissipation

In the linear response regime the instantaneous dissipation associated with powering a trans-membrane current is given by a spatial integral of the injected current density multiplied by the voltage across the membrane. In frequency space this can be calculated from the response function, yielding:

\mathcal{D}(\omega)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{3/2}\alpha\sigma_{I}}U_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{I}}{\ell(\omega)}\right)

(5)

where
U_{D}
is another universal function which goes to
1
when
\sigma_{I}\ll\ell(\omega)
.

4.4
Calculating Noise

We define the noise
S^{O}(\omega)
to be the power spectrum of the equilibrium fluctuations in the output
O(t)
in the absence of an input signal.
As with
\chi^{OI}
, we first compute
\chi^{\lambda h}
, the susceptibility of the charge density field to the external field
h
, by reading off eq. (
3
):
\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)=\alpha k/(\alpha k/c+i\omega)
.
The fluctuation dissipation theorem

;
tong_lectures_2012
then tells us that the equilibrium fluctuations of
\lambda
are related to the imaginary part of
\chi^{\lambda h}
:
S^{\lambda}(\omega)=-(2k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT/\omega)\textnormal{Im}(\chi^{\lambda h})

We then integrate

over the sensor area to obtain

:

S^{O}=\frac{2\pi^{3/2}k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptTc^{2}\sigma_{O}^{3}}{\alpha}U_{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell(\omega)}\right)

(6)

where
U_{N}
is another universal function which goes to
1

\sigma_{O}\ll\ell(\omega)
.

4.5
Energetic Cost per Bit

Plugging these results into the cost per bit (eq. (
2

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{El}}=\pi\log 2\frac{r^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}}\,U^{\textnormal{El}}\left(\frac{r}{\ell(\omega)}\right)\qquad(k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT/\textnormal{bit})

We call

(the universal functions

Thus, when the transmission distance is

However, as illustrated in fig.

We thus say that the characteristic lengthscale

5
Results

In the previous section (and methods sections
8.2
,
8.3
) we computed the energetic cost of sending information across physical distances through four biologically relevant mediums: electrical signaling, diffusive signaling in 2D and 3D, and acoustic signaling.
Using these calculations we can construct a phase diagram (fig.
2
a) with respect to frequency and distance indicating where each method of signaling is energetically preferred (for sensor sizes fixed at
5
nm).

As summarized in table
1
(and plotted in fig.
2
b), each of these systems has a characteristic lengthscale
\ell(\omega)
which determines its limits of viability.
When the transmission distance exceeds this lengthscale, the energetic cost of sending information no longer follows the scaling forms
\mathcal{C}
, instead becoming exponentially expensive.
Below their characteristic lengthscales, the cost of communication for 3D diffusion, electrical and acoustic signaling scales with distance squared.
Therefore the transition lines between the phases in fig.
2
a can be determined by their characteristic lengthscales, which sets the cutoff in energetic efficiency.
Diffusive signaling in 2D has a unique scaling form and is preferred over 3D diffusive signaling except in the regime where diffusion in 2D would be too slow; typical membrane diffusion constants are 2-3 orders of magnitude slower than those for small molecules in the cytoplasm.

For diffusive and ion-channel signaling, by examining the frequency-dependent characteristic lengthscales, we can see that the frequency
\omega
can be interpreted as measuring the speed of signal transmission.
These two physical mediums do not support coherent waves and so a signal must reach the receiver before it phase shifts and is strongly attenuated.
In diffusive signaling in
d
dimensions, for example, this means that the transmission distance is limited to the half-period diffusive travel distance
r^{2}<\langle\Delta x^{2}\rangle=2dDt=2d\pi D/\omega
, explaining the appearance of the lengthscale
\ell(\omega)=\sqrt{D/\omega}
.
Acoustic signaling is different because it permits coherent waves. Its speed is set by the speed of sound, which is large with respect to biological purposes at sub-cellular scales.
In this case, the characteristic lengthscale
\ell
(the inverse acoustic attenuation coefficient) is instead a measure of the viscous damping of acoustic waves.

Acoustic and electrical mediums both have characteristic lengthscales which are large relative to biological lengthscales, even at high frequencies (see appendices
B.2
and
D.3
).
Thus, the regimes in which they are efficient (sub-exponential) overlap significantly.
A comparison of their cost functions (
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{El}}\propto r^{2}/\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{Ac}}\propto\ell_{\sigma}^{2}r^{2}/\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}^{3}
, ) shows that electrical signaling is preferred when the receiver size is smaller than the acoustic lengthscale
\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}
.
This lengthscale,
\ell_{\sigma}
, depends on the speed of sound
c
, the viscous timescale
\tau
, and the damping power
\eta
within cytoplasm and other biological media.
Based on ultrasound measurements in blood
holm_waves_2019
, we can estimate that the smallest value of
\ell_{\sigma}
in the frequency ranges considered is
\ell_{\sigma}=7

\mu
m (appendix
D.3
), which is much larger than a single protein complex
phillips_physical_2013
.
Thus, at the sub-cellular level where
\sigma_{O}\sim 5
nm, electrical signaling is favored by a factor of
\sim 10^{6}
.
This provides a possible energetic explanation for why acoustic signaling is absent at the cellular level, despite its omnipresence in the animal kingdom.

6
Outlook

The energetic costs we have obtained are lower bounds which hold regardless of the molecular mechanism being used to power the communication channel, which can be quite varied.
For example, diffusive signaling can be driven by futile cycling of reactions (e.g. in CheY phosphorylation/dephosphorylation cycles which in net hydrolyze ATP), or by chemical pumps which concentrate signal molecules for controlled release (e.g. neurotransmitter concentration in synaptic vesicles).
For each of these processes, the total dissipation required to power the channel may be larger than the bounds we derive, but it cannot be smaller.

While an important first step, there are some limitations in this analysis.
Our results often assume a simplified geometry. In particular, for electrical signaling, many biological examples take place in roughly cylindrical axons and dendrites rather than 2D sheets, qualitatively changing results when signals are sent a distance farther than the cylinder radius.
Our setup also doesn’t capture some physical strategies such as the manipulation of stresses in fiber networks and directed transport by motor proteins.
Lastly, the noise in this calculation is assumed to be thermal noise arising from equilibrium fluctuations.
Equilibrium provides a well-defined statistical structure permitting a concrete information theoretic analysis.
However, biology does not operate at equilibrium.
While the result is still a lower bound, a higher bound could be obtained by characterizing the statistical features relevant to the process being studied.

There may also be other design principles which are not covered by our bounds.
We do not consider the cost of building and maintaining the protein machinery required to run these communication channels.
Certain strategies also permit multiplexing. For example, in diffusive signaling, multiple signal networks can run in parallel by using different second messenger molecules.
In contrast, there is only one electrical potential.

For sending information over longer ranges biology often uses relays, where information is sent through an excitable medium, often as a traveling wave, a mechanism of information transfer not discussed in this work.
For example, while action potentials are certainly electrical, the signal is not sent from the cell body all the way to the end of an axon directly, but instead via a relay of ion channels triggering successive channels.
However, it is possible our approach may be extended to consider signaling in excitable media, but the present analysis does not consider this.

The driving motivation behind this work is to understand the energetic cost of biological computations constrained by the physical reality of biological environments.
In conjunction with a range of recent efforts to quantify information transfer across biological scales

;

8.1
Time-series mutual information rate and cost per bit

We work with the framework used by Tostevin and ten Wolde in ref.
tostevin_mutual_2009
, which requires us to adapt some of our previous notation.
In the main text, we denoted
S^{O}(\omega)
to mean the intrinsic noise in the output signal arising from equilibrium fluctuation in the absence of an input signal.
Here we have to be more precise.
We now denote
S^{O}_{0}(\omega)
(i.e. the noise,
N(\omega)
in
tostevin_mutual_2009
) to mean the intrinsic noise in
O(t)
.
We denote
S^{O}_{I}(\omega)
(i.e. the power,
P(\omega)
in
tostevin_mutual_2009
) to mean the contribution to the power spectrum in
O(t)
arising as a result of the input signal. And we denote
S^{O}_{T}(\omega)
to mean their combined contributions.
As in ref
tostevin_mutual_2009
, the spectral addition rule holds:

S^{O}_{T}(\omega)=S^{O}_{0}(\omega)+S^{O}_{I}(\omega)

(8)

In the linear response regime, the output of the system can be written in terms of a response function and the input:

\langle O(\omega)\rangle=\chi^{OI}(\omega)\,I(\omega)

(9)

From this, we can rewrite
S^{O}_{I}(\omega)
in terms of the response kernel:

S^{O}_{I}(\omega)=|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}S^{I}(\omega)

(10)

where
S^{I}(\omega)
is the power spectrum of the input signal.
The mutual information rate between
I
and
O
can then be written:

R(I,O)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\ln\left(1+\frac{|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}S^{I}(\omega)}{S_{0}^{O}(\omega)}\right)

(11)

In the weak signal regime, which is a key assumption of this analysis, we can reasonably approximate the previous expression using the normal
\log
expansion:

R(I,O)=\frac{1}{4\pi\log 2}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\frac{|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}S^{I}(\omega)}{S_{0}^{O}(\omega)}\quad(\textnormal{bits/s})

(12)

where the factor of
\log 2
converts from nats to bits.
This is eq. (1) from the main text.

In our model, each physical is characterized by its intrinsic noise
S^{O}_{0}(\omega)
, describing the output’s behavior when no signal is present, and its gain
\chi^{OI}(\omega)
which describes how the output signal responds to the input signal.
The specific signal
I(t)
which the sender chooses to send is not a property of the channel itself.
Since we care about the dissipation incurred by the physical channel used to manifest the network, we also characterize each network by its dissipation kernel:

\textnormal{Dissipation rate}=\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathcal{D}(\omega)S^{I}(\omega)

(13)

which specifies the total energy dissipation rate required to run a process
I(t)
in terms of the cost at each frequency.

For a generalized input spectrum
S^{I}(\omega)
, the cost to send information is the dissipation rate divided by the information rate:

\hat{\mathcal{C}}=4\pi\log 2\frac{\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathcal{D}(\omega)S^{I}(\omega)}{\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}S^{I}(\omega)/S_{0}^{O}(\omega)}

(14)

The energy cost of sending information can be more concisely written in terms of a cost per bit at a particular frequency
\omega
,

\mathcal{C}(\omega)=4\pi\log 2\frac{\mathcal{D}(\omega)S_{0}^{O}(\omega)}{|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}},

(15)

which is the form given by eq. (2) in the main text.
This cost can be interpreted as a cost per bit for signals localized in frequency (e.g. a radio signal).
Alternatively, for a signal with power at many frequencies, the total power can be written as an integral over frequency space of
\mathcal{C}(\omega)
multiplying the differential information content at that frequency.

8.2
Diffusive signaling

Here we present a sketch of the calculation performed in section
4
for diffusive signaling in
d
dimensions.
A thorough derivation can be found in appendix
C
.

We suppose there is a sender
I
and receiver
O
separated by a distance
r
as depicted in fig.
1
d.
The fluctuation in particle density is given by
\lambda(\bm{x},t)=\rho(\bm{x},t)-\rho_{0}
and is subject to an equilibrium diffusive process
\partial_{t}\lambda=D\nabla^{2}\lambda
.
In a half-period, the distance traveled due to diffusion is
\Delta x^{2}=2dD(T/2)=2\pi dD/\omega
.
Thus we can characterize this system by lengthscale
\ell(\omega)=\sqrt{D/\omega}
.

Analogously to electrical signaling, the sender creates/destroys particles with localized rate
J(\bm{x},t)
, and the receiver measures the localized particle excess
O(t)
(fig.
1
a).
The free energy is purely entropic and is given by density (up to a constant):

\beta\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}V}=(\lambda(\bm{x})+\rho_{0})\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda(\bm{x})}{\rho_{0}}\right)-\beta\lambda(\bm{x})h(\bm{x})

(16)

Where
h
is an artificial external field which couples to
\lambda
in the free energy, introduced for the purpose of computing the susceptibility and then the noise.
From this, we can derive chemical potential
\mu=\delta F/\delta\rho=k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\left(1+\log(1+\lambda/\rho_{0}\right)-h(\bm{x})
, and potential current
J_{\mu}=-\beta D\rho\nabla\mu
which is subject to the conservation equation:
\partial_{t}\lambda=-\nabla J_{\mu}
.
Solving this yields the dynamics for the system:

\partial_{t}\lambda=D\nabla^{2}(\lambda-\beta\rho_{0}h)-D\beta\left[(\nabla\lambda)(\nabla h)+\lambda\nabla^{2}h\right]+J

(17)

where we have added the contribution
J
from the sender.
By Fourier transforming to momentum-frequency space and linearizing the dynamics, we obtain response functions:

\displaystyle\chi^{h}=\frac{\beta\rho_{0}k^{2}}{k^{2}+i\omega/D}

\displaystyle\chi^{J}=\frac{1}{Dk^{2}+i\omega}

(18)

Then following the same logic as section
4
, we can use
\chi^{h}
to compute the noise
S^{O}
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textnormal{($d=3$)}\qquad\quad S^{O}(\omega)&=\frac{4\pi^{3/2}\rho_{0}\sigma_{O}^{5}}{D}U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{N}(\sigma_{O}/\ell(\omega))\\
\textnormal{($d=2$)}\qquad\quad S^{O}(\omega)&=\frac{2\pi\rho_{0}\sigma_{O}^{4}}{D}U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{N}(\sigma_{O}/\ell(\omega))\end{split}

(19)

with scaling behavior
U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{N}(y\to 0)\sim 1
,
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{N}(y\to 0)\sim-2\log(y)
, with the latter diverging as
y\to 0
.
This divergence is why the scaling form of the energy,
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{D2}}
, depends explicitly on
\ell(\omega)
.
We use
\chi^{I}
to compute the gain
|\chi^{OI}|^{2}
:

\begin{split}\textnormal{($d=3$)}\qquad\quad\left|\chi^{OI}(\omega)\right|^{2}&=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{6}}{D^{2}r^{2}}U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{S}(r/\ell(\omega))\\
\textnormal{($d=2$)}\qquad\quad\left|\chi^{OI}(\omega)\right|^{2}&=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{4}}{D^{2}}U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}(r/\ell(\omega))\end{split}

(20)

with scaling behavior
U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{S}(z\to 0)\sim 1
,
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}(z\to 0)\sim\log^{2}(r/\ell)
.
Finally we can compute the dissipation by looking at the change in free energy induced by the signal
\langle\textnormal{diss}\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda}J\right\rangle_{t}
. This yields a dissipation kernel:

\begin{split}\textnormal{($d=3$)}\qquad\quad\mathcal{D}(\omega)&=\frac{k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{8\pi^{5/2}\rho_{0}D\sigma_{I}}U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{I}}{\ell(\omega)}\right)\\
\textnormal{($d=2$)}\qquad\quad\mathcal{D}(\omega)&=\frac{k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{8\pi^{2}\rho_{0}D}U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{I}}{\ell(\omega)}\right)\end{split}

(21)

with scaling behavior
U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{D}(x\to 0)\sim 1
,
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{D}(x\to 0)\to-2\log(x)
.
Then plugging equations (
19
-
21
) into the energetic cost (
2
), we find the energetic cost of sending a diffusive signal over a distance
r
in units of
k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT/
bit to be:

\begin{split}\textnormal{($d=3$)}\qquad\quad\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{D3}}&=\frac{4\log 2}{\pi}\frac{r^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}}U^{\textnormal{D3}}\left(\frac{r}{\ell(\omega)}\right)\\
\textnormal{($d=3$)}\qquad\quad\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{D2}}&\approx 4\log 2\frac{\log\left(\frac{\ell(\omega)}{\sigma_{I}}\right)\log\left(\frac{\ell(\omega)}{\sigma_{O}}\right)}{\log\left(\frac{\ell(\omega)}{r}\right)^{2}}\end{split}

(22)

we note that
U^{\textnormal{D3}}(r/\ell\to 0)\to 1
is a small correction to the scaling form of
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{D3}}
.
Diffusion in 2D behaves differently from all the other systems because the scaling form is given entirely by the asymptotic behavior of the integral functions
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{N}
,
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}
,
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{D}
.
For a discussion of these asymptotic relations, see appendices
C.4
-
C.6
.

8.3
Acoustic signaling

Here we present a sketch of the calculation performed in section
4
for acoustic signaling in 3 dimensions in a liquid.
A thorough derivation can be found in appendix
D
.

There is a sender
I
at the origin and a receiver
O
at a point
\bm{r}
embedded in a 3D media which supports coherent compression waves with viscous loss.
A physical wave may be characterized by its displacement vector
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
.
We use the fractional Kelvin-Voigt model to describe the background dynamics of
\bm{u}
which is the standard wave-equation with a fractional viscous loss term
wismer_finite_2006
;
holm_unifying_2010
;
holm_waves_2019
:

\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{u}-c^{2}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}-c^{2}\left[\tau\partial_{t}\right]^{\eta}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}=0

(23)

where
c
is the speed of sound,
\tau
is a timescale characterizing the strength of the relaxation modes,
0<\eta\leq 1
is a phenomenological fractional power reflecting the relaxation dependence on frequency, and
\partial_{t}^{\eta}
is a fractional derivative, which for our purposes is defined by its Fourier transform:

\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{\partial^{\eta}}{\partial t^{\eta}}f\right\}(\omega)=(i\omega)^{\eta}\mathcal{F}\left\{f\right\}(\omega)
.
This is an accurate model for low-frequency acoustics in liquids and gases
morse_theoretical_1968
;
holm_waves_2019
.
For ideal monatomic gases or distilled water,
\eta=1
, which leads to characteristic lengthscale
\ell=c/\omega^{2}\tau
, the classical
\omega^{2}
attenuation discovered by Stokes
stokes_theories_2009
.
We characterize this system in terms of the complex damping parameter:

\nu=\nu^{\prime}+i\nu^{\prime\prime}\equiv(i\tau\omega)^{\eta}

(24)

and a few (over-complete) lengthscales:

\displaystyle\ell_{c}\equiv\frac{c}{\omega}

\displaystyle\ell=\frac{\ell_{c}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}

\displaystyle\ell_{\sigma}\equiv\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}

(25)

where
\ell_{c}
is the radian wavelength and
\ell
is more commonly expressed as
\alpha=1/2\ell
, the acoustic attenuation coefficient
morse_theoretical_1968
;
dukhin_bulk_2009
;
holmes_temperature_2011
;
lin_bulk_2017
;
holm_waves_2019
.
The lengthscale
\ell_{\sigma}
pops out of our analysis as a limiting lengthscale for the sender size
\sigma_{I}
as well as term that appears in the final energetic cost.

We will describe the dynamics in terms of the density fluctuations
\lambda=-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}
(equivalently we could use the pressure fluctuations).
In this framing, the input signal
J
and output
O(t)
take on the form depicted in fig.
1
a.
The energy density is given by
morse_theoretical_1968
:

\frac{\mathrm{d}H}{\mathrm{d}V}=\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{0}+\lambda(x,t))\left(\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}\right)^{2}+\frac{(p_{0}+c^{2}\lambda)^{2}}{2\rho_{0}c^{2}}+h\lambda

(26)

where
p_{0}
is the equilibrium pressure and
h
is an artificial external field introduced to compute the susceptibility.
Solving the Euler-Lagrange equations then inserting the viscous loss term and signal term yields the full dynamics:

(\partial_{t}^{2}-c^{2}\nabla^{2}-c^{2}(\tau\partial_{t})^{\eta}\nabla^{2})\lambda=\rho_{0}\nabla^{2}h+\partial_{t}J

(27)

from which we can extract the response functions:

\displaystyle\chi^{h}=\frac{(\rho_{0}/c^{2})k^{2}}{k^{2}-\ell_{c}^{-2}+\nu k^{2}}

\displaystyle\chi^{J}=\frac{i}{\omega}\frac{\ell_{c}^{-2}}{k^{2}-\ell_{c}^{-2}+\nu k^{2}}

(28)

Then following through the calculation in section
4
we use
\chi^{h}
to obtain the noise
S^{O}(\omega)
via the fluctuation dissipation theorem
tong_lectures_2012
:

S^{O}(\omega)=2\pi^{3/2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}\nu^{\prime\prime}}{c^{2}\omega}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)

(29)

We discuss the scaling of
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}
after equation (
32
).
We obtain the gain
\left|\chi^{OI}\right|^{2}
using
\chi^{J}
:

\left|\chi^{OI}\right|^{2}=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{\omega\sigma_{O}^{3}}{rc^{2}}\right)^{2}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)

(30)

with scaling
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}(z\to 0,\nu\to 0)\sim 1
.
We can compute the dissipation by looking at the linearized change (i.e. ignoring the kinetic term) in energy induced by the signal:
\langle\textnormal{diss}\rangle=\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}J\rangle
, which yields dissipation kernel:

\mathcal{D}(\omega)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{5/2}\rho_{0}}\frac{\omega\nu^{\prime\prime}}{\sigma_{I}}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)

(31)

Plugging equations (
29
-
31
) into the energetic cost (
2
), we find the energetic cost of sending an acoustic signal over a distance
r
in units of
k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT/
bit to be:

\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{Ac}}=\frac{2\log 2}{\pi}\frac{r^{2}\ell_{\sigma}^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}^{3}}\frac{U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)}{U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)}

(32)

where
\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}
.
The asymptotic behavior of
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}
,
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}
, and
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}
for small arguments depends on the ratio of their first and second argument and is developed explicitly in appendices
D.5
-
D.7
.
The result is that in the regime where the characteristic damping is small (
\nu\ll 1
)
and where the sender and receiver sizes are small compared to
\ell_{\sigma}
, then the noise and dissipation functions may be neglected (
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\to 1
,
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\to 1
) and the signal function is given by
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}(r/\ell_{c},\nu)\sim e^{-r/\ell}
.
This latter scaling relation is commonly recognized as the defining feature of the attenuation coefficient
\alpha=1/2\ell

morse_theoretical_1968
.

Whether we are in the regime
\sigma_{I}
,
\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{\sigma}
depends on the damping parameters
\tau
and
\eta
which are, in general, difficult to compute.
For distilled water,
\eta=1
and we can compute the damping timescale using
\tau=(1/\rho_{0}c^{2})\left[4\eta/3+\mu_{B}\right]=1.6\cdot 10^{-12}
s where
\eta
and
\eta_{B}
are the dynamic and bulk viscosities
holm_waves_2019
;
holmes_temperature_2011
. This would mean that the scaling regime is only valid when
\sigma_{I}\ll\tau_{\ell}=2.4
nm.
However, biology does not operate in pure water.
As a proxy for relevant biological fluids, we can extract
\eta
and
\tau
from measurements of the acoustic attenuation coefficient in blood studied in the context of ultrasound experiments (Supplementary section 4.2).
The result of this procedure is that
\ell_{\sigma}=7
(
\mu
m) at the largest frequency shown in fig.
2
a and becomes even larger for smaller frequencies, and thus we may assume sub-cellular senders/receivers to be significantly smaller.

Appendix B
Electrical Signaling on a Membrane

Here we make explicit the derivation outlined in the main text.
This essentially requires two main steps.
The first is setting up the problem and solving for the dynamics of the system.
The second is analyzing the universal functions that the analysis produces.

B.1
Setup

As depicted in fig.
1
, we suppose we have a 2D membrane which behaves like a capacitor with capacitance per unit area
c
embedded in a bulk solution with conductivity
\alpha
. The surface charge density on the membrane is given by
\lambda(x,y)
, defined such that
\langle\lambda\rangle=0
(which simplifies the linear response calculation). No free charges are allowed to accumulate in the bulk.

The sender
I
, located at the origin, is the ion channel shown in blue which opens and closes, allowing the membrane to depolarize. This induces the input signal
I(t)
, the current flowing through the origin. The localized behavior of the sender is characterized by a current density
J(x,y,t)
which disperses the current
I(t)
in a Gaussian disk of radius
\sigma_{I}
:

\displaystyle\bm{J}(x,y,t)=\frac{e^{-\bm{x}^{2}/2\sigma_{I}^{2}}}{2\pi\sigma_{I}^{2}}I(t)\bm{\hat{z}}

(A/m
2
)

(33)

The receiver
O
, located at
\bm{r}
is the ion channel shown in yellow which detects the excess charge accumulated in its vicinity. Its localized detection is captured by a Gaussian disk of radius
\sigma_{O}
:

\displaystyle O(t)=\int\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,e^{-(\bm{x}-\bm{r})^{2}/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}\lambda(x,y,t)

(C)

(34)

B.2
Parameters

This system is described by a capacitance per area
c
and a conductance per unit length
\alpha
.
In analogy with a traditional RC circuit, which has timescale
\tau=RC
, we instead have here an RC
velocity
given by:

\nu_{RC}=\frac{\alpha}{c}\approx 10^{8}\,\textnormal{$\mu$m/s}

(35)

where the values of
\alpha
and
c
are taken to be
\alpha=10^{-2}

\Omega^{-1}\textnormal{cm}^{-1}

wang_role_2013
,
c=10^{-6}
F/cm
2

gentet_direct_2000
.
This means that at frequency
\omega
, the system has the characteristic lengthscale:

\ell(\omega)=\frac{\alpha}{\omega c}

(36)

Which we will show determines the limit of how far signals at frequency
\omega
may be sent.
The logic of this limitation is very similar to the sense in which the RC timescale limits the admissible frequencies which may pass unimpeded through an RC circuit.
For reference, for the frequency range
f\in\{10^{-}3,10^{4}\}
Hz used in fig 3 from the main text, this characteristic lengthscale is in the range:

\displaystyle f\in\{10^{-3},10^{4}\}\,\textnormal{Hz}

\displaystyle\ell(2\pi f)\in\{1.6\,\textnormal{mm},\,16\,\textnormal{km}\}

Thus, at sub
kHz
frequencies, this limiting lengthscale is quite large (
>5
mm).

B.3
Dynamics

To obtain the full dynamics of this system, we need two response functions.
First we need
\chi^{\lambda h}
, the susceptibility of the surface charge density which we can use with the fluctuation dissipation theorem to find the equilibrium noise
tong_lectures_2012
.
The second is
\chi^{\lambda I}
, the linear response function of the surface charge density to the input signal
tong_lectures_2012
.

Following the usual prescription in linear response theory (see
tong_lectures_2012
;
zwanzig_nonequilibrium_2001
), we write down the energy of the system and add a field
h
coupled to
\lambda
to find the response function
\chi^{\lambda h}
. In this case the energy is that of a capacitor:

H=\int\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\,\left[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\lambda^{2}(\bm{x})}{c}-h(\bm{x})\lambda(\bm{x})\right]

(37)

where the usual
H=Q^{2}/2C
can be expressed in differential form under the assumption that the membrane width is much smaller than variations in
\lambda
. Then the effective potential on the membrane is given by the functional derivative:

\phi(x,y,z=0^{+})\equiv\frac{\delta H}{\delta\lambda}=\frac{\lambda(\bm{x})}{c}-h(\bm{x})

In the bulk, the potential obeys Laplace’s equation, which we calculate in
xy
-momentum space:

\nabla^{2}\phi(x,y,z)=0\quad\to\quad\left[-k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}^{2}\right]\phi(\bm{k},z)=0

Yielding solution:

\phi(\bm{k},z\neq 0)=\phi(\bm{k})e^{-kz}\qquad\quad k=\sqrt{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}

Invoking continuity at
z=0
, we have:

\phi(\bm{k},z)=\left[\frac{\lambda(\bm{k})}{c}-h(\bm{k})\right]e^{-kz}

In the absence of a signal, the current in the bulk in given by the differential Ohm’s equation
\bm{J}_{0}(\bm{x},z\neq 0)=-\alpha\nabla\phi
. The current at
z=0
is zero since the membrane acts like a perfect insulator. Thus we can write the current using the Heaviside step function:

\bm{J}_{0}(\bm{x},z)=-\alpha\nabla\phi(\bm{x},z\neq 0)\Theta(z)

We plug this into the continuity equation on the 3D charge density
\partial_{t}\rho(x,y,z)=-\bm{\nabla}\cdot\bm{J}_{0}
:

\partial_{t}\rho=\alpha\nabla^{2}\phi(\bm{x},z\neq 0)\Theta(z)+\alpha\left[\nabla\phi(\bm{x},z\neq 0)\cdot\delta(z)\hat{z}\right]

By construction,
\nabla^{2}\phi(\bm{x},z\neq 0)=0
because it must satisfies Laplace’s equation.
We then plug in
\rho(x,y,z)=\lambda(\bm{x})\delta(z)
and drop the delta functions:

\partial_{t}\lambda(x,y)=\alpha\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}z}\phi(\bm{x},z\neq 0)

Moving to Fourier space and plugging in eq. (
B.3
) we are left with:

\partial_{t}\lambda(\bm{k})=-\frac{\alpha}{c}k\lambda(\bm{k})+\alpha kh(\bm{k})

Which is the basic dynamics in the absence of an input signal. When a weak input signal
J(\bm{k},t)
of the form given in eq. (
33
) is present, we have:

\partial_{t}\lambda(\bm{k})=-\frac{\alpha}{c}k\lambda(\bm{k})+\alpha kh(\bm{k})+J(\bm{k},t)

which is exactly eq. (3) from the main text.
Finally, we move to frequency space to obtain the full dynamics:

\left[i\omega+\frac{\alpha k}{c}\right]\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)=\alpha kh(\bm{k},\omega)+J(\bm{k},\omega)

(38)

From these dynamics we can extract the susceptibility
\chi^{\lambda h}
and input response
\chi^{\lambda I}
, defined to satisfy
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle=\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)h(\bm{k},\omega)
and
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle=\chi^{\lambda I}(\bm{k},\omega)I(\omega)

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{ck}{k+i\ell^{-1}}

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda I}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{c}{\alpha}\frac{e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}/2}}{k+i\ell^{-1}}

(39)

and the related response function of
\lambda
to
J
:

\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{c}{\alpha}\frac{1}{k+i\ell^{-1}}

It’s worth noting that this is where the dependence on the characteristic lengthscale
\ell=\alpha/c\omega
of the system comes from.

B.4
Signal

Here the goal is to compute
|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}
, the gain of the signaling network.
We start with the response function of
\lambda
to
J
:
\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{c}{\alpha}\frac{1}{k+i\ell^{-1}}

Invoking eq.
99
, we find:

\chi^{OI}(\omega)=\frac{c}{\alpha}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{uJ_{0}(u)}{u+ir/\ell}

The gain function is this squared, which we can write in terms of the scaling portion and a universal integral function:

\begin{split}|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}&=\frac{c^{2}\sigma_{O}^{4}}{\alpha^{2}r^{2}}\left|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}\left(\frac{{r}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)\right|^{2}\\
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(z)&\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{uJ_{0}(u)}{u+iz}\end{split}

(40)

The important fact here is that when
r\ll\ell(\omega)
, we have
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(r/\ell)=1
and can be completely ignored.
We can thus think of
U^{\textnormal{El}}(r/\ell)
as a small correction that only becomes relevant once the transmission distance approaches the characteristic lengthscale.
This can be demonstrated by breaking
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
into its real and imaginary components,
\left|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(z)\right|^{2}=U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Re}}(z)^{2}+U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Im}}(z)^{2}
, which each have analytic solutions:

\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Re}}(z)&\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{u^{2}J_{0}(u)}{u^{2}+z^{2}}=1+\frac{\pi z}{2}M_{0}(z)\\
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Im}}(z)&\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{uzJ_{0}(u)}{u^{2}+z^{2}}=zK_{0}(z)\end{split}

(41)

where
M_{0}
is a modified Struve function (see 11.2.6 from
olver_nist_2010
) and
K_{0}
is a modified Bessel function.
We can get the
z\to 0
behavior of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Re}}
using a power series expansion of
M_{0}(z)
(see 11.2.6, 11.2.2, 10.25.2 from
olver_nist_2010
),
and the behavior of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Im}}
using the asymptotic expression for
K_{0}
(see 10.30.3 from
olver_nist_2010
):

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Re}}(z)

\displaystyle=1+z\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\frac{z^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!!^{2}}-\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{z^{2k}}{2^{2k}k!^{2}}\right]\sim 1-\frac{\pi z}{2}

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Im}}(z)

\displaystyle=zK_{0}(z)\sim-z\log z

and therefore the approximation
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(z\ll 1)=1
is well-justified.
We can also obtain the
z\to\infty
behavior using the asymptotic expressions for
M_{0}
(see 11.6.2 from
olver_nist_2010
) and
K_{0}
(10.25.3 from
olver_nist_2010
):

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Re}}(z\to\infty)

\displaystyle\sim-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(1/2+k)}{\Gamma(1/2-k)}\frac{2^{2k}}{z^{2k}}\sim-\frac{1}{z^{2}}

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Im}}(z\to\infty)

\displaystyle\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi z}{2}}e^{-z}

Plugging these into
|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}|^{2}=|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Re}}|^{2}+|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S,\textnormal{Im}}|^{2}
, we obtain the
z\to 0
and
z\to\infty
asymptotic behavior of
|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}|^{2}
:

\displaystyle\left|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(z\to 0)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim 1-\pi z+\mathcal{O}(z^{2}\log(z)^{2})

(42)

\displaystyle\left|U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(z\to\infty)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{z^{4}}

(43)

In producing the plots in the main paper, this function is evaluated numerically.
We only derived these asymptotic limits to give the scaling behavior in the
z\ll 1
and
z\gg 1
regimes.

B.5
Noise

Here we compute
S^{O}(\omega)
, the fluctuations in the output signal in the absence of an input arising from thermal noise. The fluctuation dissipation theorem
tong_lectures_2012
tells us that since
h
is conjugate to
\lambda
in the energy, the fluctuations in
\lambda
are given by:

S^{\lambda}(\bm{k},\omega)=-\frac{2k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{\omega}\textnormal{Im}\left[\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)\right]=\frac{1}{\alpha}\frac{2c^{2}k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptTk}{k^{2}+(\omega c/\alpha)^{2}}

We then invoke eq. (
102
) to get
S^{O}(\omega)
:

S^{O}(\omega)=\frac{4\pi c^{2}\sigma_{O}^{3}k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{u^{2}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}+(\sigma_{O}/\ell(\omega))^{2}}

This integral has an analytic solution which permits us to write the noise in terms of a scaling portion and a universal function:

\begin{split}S^{O}(\omega)&=k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\frac{2\pi^{3/2}c^{2}\sigma_{O}^{3}}{\alpha}U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}\left(\frac{{\sigma_{O}}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)\\
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}(y)&\equiv 1-\sqrt{\pi}ye^{y^{2}}\textnormal{erfc}(y)\end{split}

(44)

When
\sigma_{O}\ll\ell
, the noise correction function
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}\to 1
and can be completely ignored.
This can be seen using the series expansion of
erfc
:

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}(y)

\displaystyle\equiv 1-\sqrt{\pi}ye^{y^{2}}\textnormal{erfc}(y)

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{\pi}ye^{y^{2}}\left[1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}y+\mathcal{O}(y^{3})\right]

\displaystyle=1-\sqrt{\pi}y+\mathcal{O}(y^{2})

And thus when the receiver radius
\sigma_{O}
is much smaller than the characteristic lengthscale
\ell
, we may ignore the contribution of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}
.

B.6
Dissipation

The dissipation in this system is Ohmic: we run current density
J(\bm{x},t)
through the membrane, which has some electrical potential density difference
\lambda(\bm{x})/c
.
Like an ordinary circuit, this requires power
\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{x}\,J(\bm{x})\lambda(\bm{x})/c\sim IV
.
However, it is useful to analyze this system explicitly in terms of the capacitive energy since there is a symmetry between dissipation in electrical signaling and the other systems under consideration.
The energy stored in the capacitor is given by:

H=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{x}\,\frac{1}{2c}\lambda^{2}(\bm{x})

And thus the energetic dissipation required to run the network is given by:

\langle\textnormal{Diss}(t)\rangle=\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\left(\frac{{\partial\lambda}}{{\partial t}}\right)_{J}\right\rangle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{x}}{c}\langle\lambda(\bm{x},t)J(\bm{x},t)\rangle

We then invoke eq. (
104
) with prefactor
\mathcal{C}=1/c
, giving us:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}\alpha\sigma_{I}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{u}\,\frac{ue^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}+(\sigma_{I}\omega c/\alpha)^{2}}

Which we can write in terms of a scaling portion and a universal integral function:

\begin{split}\mathcal{D}(\omega)&=\frac{1}{8\pi^{3/2}\alpha\sigma_{I}}U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}\left(\frac{{\sigma_{I}}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)\\
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}(x)&=U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}(x)\equiv\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{u^{2}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}+x^{2}}\end{split}

(45)

where
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}
is actually the same universal integral function as
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}
, the integral function found in the noise term.
Likewise, we have that
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}(\sigma_{I}/\ell)=1
when
\sigma_{I}\ll\ell
.

B.7
Energy Cost Per Bit

Here we combine our results for the electrical signal (eq. (
40
)), the noise (eq. (
44
)), and the dissipation (eq. (
45
)) into our expression for the energetic cost per bit (eq. (
2
)). We find that the energetic cost per bit to send information between ion channels in a 2D membrane at frequency
\omega
in
k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT
/bit is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{El}}

\displaystyle=\pi\log 2\,\frac{r^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}}\frac{U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}\left(\frac{{\sigma_{I}}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}\left(\frac{{\sigma_{O}}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)}{\left|U_{S}^{\textnormal{El}}\left(\frac{{r}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)\right|^{2}}

(46)

where
r
is the transmission distance,
\sigma_{I}
is the sender radius,
\sigma_{O}
is the receiver radius, and
\ell(\omega)=\alpha/c\omega
is the characteristic lengthscale.
The functions
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}
,
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}
, and
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
are the integral functions derived earlier which are all equal to
1
when their arguments are smaller than 1.

When the signaling lengthscales are smaller than the characteristic lengthscale (i.e.
\sigma_{O}
,
\sigma_{I}
,
r

\ll\ell(\omega)
), the universal functions can all be ignored since
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}=U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}=U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}=1
.
This condition can be reduced to requiring that
r\ll\ell(\omega)
since the concept of signaling is not well defined when the sender/receiver size are comparable or larger than the transmission distance (i.e. we always have
\sigma_{I}
,
\sigma_{O}

\ll r
).
This yields the expression for the energetic cost below the characteristic lengthscale:

when
r

\ll

\ell(\omega)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{El}}=\pi\log 2\,\frac{r^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}}\qquad\textnormal{($k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT$/bit)}

(47)

A surprisingly simple result given the complexity of its ingredients.
Note that it does not depend on the physical constants
\alpha
or
c
.
These constants only matter in determining the limiting behavior via the characteristic lengthscale.

Above the characteristic lengthscale, we need to handle the integral functions.
In this system as well as all the signaling systems, we need to use a mix of analytic, numerical, and asymptotic approaches to evaluate these.
We can view these integral functions as corrections to the energetic cost that reflect limiting physical behavior of the system near the characteristic lengthscale.

In each of the universal functions,
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}(r/\ell)
,
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{N}(\sigma_{O}/\ell)
,
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{D}(\sigma_{I}/\ell)
, the value only deviates from
1
once its argument begins to approach
1
. In a well-defined signaling process, the sender/receiver sizes should be significantly smaller than the transmission distance (
\sigma_{I}
,
\sigma_{O}

\ll

r
).
Thus the argument to
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
is always much larger than for the other two.
This means the first order corrections to eq. (
47
) are dominated by contributions from the universal function for the signal:
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
, which has the analytic expression given by eq.
41
.
A plot of the real and imaginary components of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
is shown below

Figure 3:

The integral function contribution to the electrical signal transmission coefficient. This plots
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
as a function of the transmission distance normalized by the characteristic lengthscale. In blue is the real component, in yellow is the imaginary component, and in red is the total value

From fig.
3
, we can develop a clearer picture of the underlying physics. The imaginary portion of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
indicates the phase difference between the sender and receiver.
Well below the characteristic lengthscale, the entire system is in phase and there is no loss in signal strength.
As the transmission distance approaches the characteristic lengthscale, the signal becomes highly attenuated due to the collapse in resolution between subsequent phases.
This is seen by the rapid falloff in the real portion of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
, which is the dominant contribution.
Just beyond the characteristic lengthscale, the real portion of
U^{\textnormal{El}}_{S}
becomes negative, but non-zero indicating that the communication is out of phase and highly attenuated.
Even further in the
r>\ell(\omega)
regime, the subsequent phases of the signal merge during transmission and the signal becomes fully attenuated.

As mentioned, the integral functions for the noise and dissipation are less relevant in evaluating eq. (
46
), the energetic cost per bit, but they are included in the calculations which produced the plots shown in the main text.

Appendix C
Diffusive Signaling in 2D and 3D

Here we do an explicit analysis of the energetic cost per bit to send information via diffusion in 2D and 3D.
We proceed in a similar structure as with the electrical analysis.

One caveat to keep in mind is that this setup assumes the sender injects/removes particles.
This is slightly different from more realistic modification-diffusive communication system where the sender consumes energy to modify/de-modify the state of the carrier molecules.
The system described here turns out to be the maximally efficient limit of modification-diffusive communication, which is briefly discussed in section
C.8
.

C.1
Setup

As depicted in fig.
1
d, we suppose we have a 2D membrane or 3D bulk with a particle density
\lambda(\bm{x},t)
, defined so that
\langle\lambda\rangle=0
.
The equilibrium dynamics are entirely produced by diffusion with some diffusion constant
D
.

The input and output signals are defined similarly as with electrical signaling.
We let
d=2
,
3
be the dimensionality of the system.
The sender, depicted in blue, injects/removes particles at the origin with rate
I(t)
, localized in a Gaussian disk/sphere of radius
\sigma_{I}
:

\displaystyle J=\frac{e^{-\bm{x}^{2}/2\sigma_{I}^{2}}}{(2\pi\sigma_{I}^{2})^{d/2}}I(t)

(particles/s/m
d
)

(48)

The receiver, shown in yellow, is a disk/sphere located at some position
\bm{r}
.
The output signal is the number of excess particles localized in a Gaussian disk/sphere of radius
\sigma_{O}
around the receiver:

\displaystyle O(t)=\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,e^{-(\bm{x}-\bm{r})^{2}/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}\lambda(\bm{x},t)

(particles)

(49)

C.2
Parameters

This system is described by
D
, the diffusion constant.
In time
t
, the typical distance traveled by a particle diffusing in
d
dimensions is given by:
\langle\Delta x^{2}\rangle=2dDt
.
Thus, for a signal sent at frequency
\omega
, particles may travel a distance:

\sqrt{\langle\Delta x^{2}\rangle}=\sqrt{2dD\frac{T}{2}}=\sqrt{2\pi dD/\omega}

in one phase (i.e. half-period) of the signal.
This means that the characteristic lengthscale of diffusive signaling at frequency
\omega
is given by:

\ell(\omega)=\sqrt{D/\omega}

(50)

Signals sent further than this lengthscale are strongly attenuated because of phase diffusion.
This fact will explicitly arise from the calculation of
\chi^{OI}
, the gain of the diffusive signaling process, in section
C.4
.

C.3
Dynamics

The dynamics of this system can be analytically solved in a straightforward manner.
One can write down the particle density in terms of each particle:

\lambda(\bm{x},t)=\sum_{i}\delta(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}(t))

and then use diffusive correlation functions to generate the response function and equilibrium fluctuations.
However, to keep a symmetry between the various systems under consideration, we take an alternative approach that uses the energy of the system and a chemical potential.

We start by denoting
\rho(\bm{x})
to be the true particle density such that
\lambda(\bm{x})=\rho(\bm{x})-\rho_{0}
, where
\rho_{0}
is the average density in the absence of a signal (everything done here is done in the absence of an input signal which is introduced later).
The entropy of the system is given by:

\displaystyle-\beta S

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,\rho\log\rho=\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,(\lambda+\rho_{0})\log(\lambda+\rho_{0})

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,\left[(\lambda+\rho_{0})\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{\rho_{0}}\right)+(\lambda+\rho_{0})\log\rho_{0}\right]

Next we note that
\int\lambda\log\rho_{0}=0
and that
\int\rho_{0}\log\rho_{0}
is just the constant zero-point entropy of the system.
This allows us to write down the free energy density of the system (relative to the zero-point free energy):

\displaystyle\beta\frac{\mathrm{d}F}{\mathrm{d}V}

\displaystyle\equiv-\beta\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}V}-\beta\lambda(\bm{x})h(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=(\lambda(\bm{x})+\rho_{0})\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda(\bm{x})}{\rho_{0}}\right)-\beta\lambda(\bm{x})h(\bm{x})

where
h(\bm{x})
is an artificial external field which couples to
\lambda(\bm{x})
, analogous to
h
in the electrical derivation, introduced solely to compute the susceptibility of the particle density.
The chemical potential
\mu
can be obtained via the functional derivative of
F
with respect to
\lambda
:

\beta\mu\equiv\frac{\delta(\beta F)}{\delta\lambda}=1+\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda(\bm{x})}{\rho_{0}}\right)-\beta h(\bm{x})

This chemical potential induces a current density
J_{0}

\displaystyle J_{0}=-\beta D\rho\nabla\mu=-D\left[\nabla\lambda-\beta\rho\nabla h\right]

which obeys a conservation equation
\partial_{t}\lambda=-\nabla J
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\lambda=D\nabla^{2}\lambda-D\beta(\nabla\lambda)(\nabla h)-D\beta(\rho_{0}+\lambda)\nabla^{2}h

Now we also introduce the (non-conservative) input signal
J(t)
:

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\lambda=&+D\nabla^{2}\left(\lambda-\beta\rho_{0}h\right)\\
&-D\beta\left[(\nabla\lambda)(\nabla h)+\lambda\nabla^{2}h\right]+J(\bm{x},t)\end{split}

(51)

Which gives the complete real-space dynamics of this system.
We then Fourier transform to frequency/momentum space:

\displaystyle i\omega\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)=

\displaystyle-Dk^{2}\left[\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)-\beta\rho_{0}h(\bm{k},\omega)\right]+J(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle-D\beta\mathcal{F}[(\nabla\lambda)(\nabla h)+\lambda\nabla^{2}h](\bm{k},\omega)

Reading off the linear terms, we can extract the susceptibility
\chi^{\lambda h}
and input response
\chi^{\lambda I}

tong_lectures_2012
, defined by
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle=\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)h(\bm{k},\omega)
and
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle=\chi^{\lambda I}(\bm{k},\omega)I(\omega)
:

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{\beta\rho_{0}k^{2}}{k^{2}+i\ell^{-2}}

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda I}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{1}{D}\frac{e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}/2}}{k^{2}+i\ell^{-2}}

(52)

where we have also exploited the Fourier transform of
J(\bm{x},t)
(eq. (
48
)) and used the characteristic lengthscale
\ell=\sqrt{D/\omega}
.

C.4
Signal

Here the goal is to compute
|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}
, the gain of the signaling network.
We start with the response function of
\lambda
to
J
:
\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{1}{D}\frac{1}{k^{2}+i\omega/D}
.

2D Result

Using eq. (
99
), we can show that in 2D,
\chi^{OI}(\omega)
is given by:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{D}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,\frac{uJ_{0}(u)}{u^{2}+i(r/\ell)^{2}}

Which allows us to write
|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}
in terms of a scaling term and an integral function which has a simple analytic form:

(2D)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}&=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{4}}{D^{2}}\left|U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}\left(\frac{{r}}{{\ell}}\right)\right|^{2}\\
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}(z)&\equiv K_{0}(\sqrt{i}z)\end{split}

(53)

where
K_{0}
is a Bessel function.
We note that unlike electrical signaling, 3D diffusion, and acoustic signaling, this function diverges for small arguments.
Therefore, we cannot ignore
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}(r/\ell)
in the
r\ll\ell
limit.
Thus, in order to obtain the leading behavior of the energetic cost, we have to look at the asymptotic behavior of this function.
For
z\to 0
,
K_{0}(\sqrt{i}z)
has the asymptotic form given by 10.30.3 from
olver_nist_2010
:

for
z\to 0

\displaystyle\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}(z)&\sim-\ln(z)-i\frac{\pi}{4}\\
\left|U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}\right|^{2}&\sim\ln(z)^{2}+\left(\frac{{\pi}}{{4}}\right)^{2}\end{split}

(54)

For
z\to\infty
,
K_{0}(\sqrt{i}z)
has the asymptotic form given by 10.25.3 from
olver_nist_2010
:

for
z\to\infty

\displaystyle\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}(z)&\sim\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{i}z}}e^{-\sqrt{i}z}\\
\left|U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}\right|^{2}&\sim\frac{\pi}{2z}e^{-\sqrt{2}z}\end{split}

(55)

The first relation gives us the scaling form of
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{S}
for transmission distances below the characteristic lengthscale
\ell
.
The second relation gives the
e^{-\sqrt{2}z}
exponential attenuation incurred for distances above
\ell
, which is found in all of the studied systems.

3D Result

Using eq. (
100
), the output response function in 3D is given by:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}}{Dr}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\frac{u\sin u}{u^{2}+i(r/\ell)^{2}}

This can be solved analytically using contour integration by rewriting
2i\sin u=e^{iu}-e^{-iu}
and then closing the two terms over the upper and lower semi-circles.
This allows us to write
|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}
in terms of a scaling term and a correction function:

(3D)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}&=\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{6}}{D^{2}r^{2}}\left|U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{S}\left(\frac{{r}}{{\ell}}\right)\right|^{2}\\
\left|U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{S}(z)\right|^{2}&\equiv e^{-\sqrt{2}z}\end{split}

(56)

where we can ignore the contribution from
U^{\textnormal{D3}}_{S}
when the transmission distance is smaller than the characteristic lengthscale (
z\ll 1
).

C.5
Noise

Here we compute
S^{O}(\omega)
, the fluctuations in the output signal in the absence of an input arising from thermal noise. Just as with electrical signaling, the fluctuation dissipation theorem
tong_lectures_2012
tells us that since
h
is conjugate to
\lambda
in the energy, the fluctuations in
\lambda

C.6
Dissipation

The dissipation in this system results from the entropic cost of creating particles at high concentration and degrading them at low concentration.
The dissipation is derived in much the same way as with electrical signaling.
The free energy of the system in the absence of an external field can be written:

F=k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,(\rho_{0}+\lambda)\log(\rho_{0}+\lambda)

Thus the change in free energy incurred by running the signal process is:

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{Diss}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial F}{\partial\lambda}J(x,t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\left\langle\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,\left[\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{\rho_{0}}\right)+1+\log(\rho_{0})\right]J(x,t)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\left\langle\log\left(1+\frac{\lambda}{\rho_{0}}\right)J(x,t)\right\rangle

where we have used the fact that
\langle J(t)\rangle=0
, i.e. the total particle count is conserved over long time periods.
Since we are in the weak signal regime, we expect the fluctuations in particle density to be small with respect to the average density.
We can therefore expand the log, yielding:

\langle\textnormal{Diss}\rangle\approx\frac{k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{\rho_{0}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,\left\langle\lambda(\bm{x},t)J(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle

We can now use eq. (
104
) with prefactor
\mathcal{C}=k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT/\rho_{0}
to get the dissipation kernel:

\mathcal{D}(\omega)=\frac{k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\sigma_{I}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{d+1}\rho_{0}D\sigma_{I}^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\frac{u^{2}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{4}+(\sigma_{I}/\ell)^{4}}

2D Result

In two dimensions the dissipation kernel becomes:

\mathcal{D}(\omega)=\frac{k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{4\pi^{2}\rho_{0}D}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\frac{u^{3}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{4}+(\sigma_{I}/\ell)^{4}}

which we write in terms of a scaling portion and a universal integral function, which is the same as the universal function for the noise in 2D:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{D}(\omega)&=\frac{k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{8\pi^{2}\rho_{0}D}U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{D}\left(\frac{{\sigma_{I}}}{{\ell(\omega)}}\right)\\
U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{D}(x)&=U^{\textnormal{D2}}_{N}(x)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\frac{u^{3}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{4}+x^{4}}\end{split}

C.8
Connection to Modification-Diffusive Communication

Here we briefly talk about the connection between the injection-diffusive system described above where the sender injects/removes signal molecules and modification-diffusive systems where the sender modifies the state of carrier molecules to communicate the signal.
A canonical example of the latter system is the CheY/CheY-P signaling pathway in E coli, where the sender, the kinase CheA, converts the protein CheY to CheY-P through the hydrolysis of ATP.
CheY-P then diffuses through the cytoplasm to the cellular motors, thus communicating the activity state of CheA.

In the injection-diffusive system discussed in this text, the dynamics in the presence of the input signal are given by:
\partial_{t}\lambda=D\nabla^{2}\lambda+J
.
We denote fields related to modified and unmodified carrier molecules using subscripts
a
and
b
.
Then the dynamics of modification-diffusive systems can be written as:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\lambda_{a}

\displaystyle=D\nabla^{2}\lambda_{a}+(\rho-\rho_{a})J_{a}-(\rho-\rho_{b})J_{b}

\displaystyle=D\nabla^{2}\lambda_{a}+(\langle\rho_{b}\rangle-\lambda_{a})J_{a}-(\langle\rho_{a}\rangle-\lambda_{b})J_{b}

where here,
\rho
describes the total density of modified+unmodified particles,
\rho_{a}
/
\rho_{b}
describe the density of modified/unmodified particles,
J_{a}
describes the signal’s activation process, and
J_{b}
describes the signal’s deactivation process.

In the weak-signal regime, the non-linear coupling
(\langle\rho_{b}\rangle-\lambda_{a})J_{a}(x,t)
can be linearized to
\langle\rho_{b}\rangle J_{a}
and likewise for the off-process.
If we then assume that there the process is symmetric in
a
and
b
and that there is no wasted energy in the modification-demodification cycle, we recover the original result found for injection-diffusion.
A more general treatment of modification-diffusive signaling is ongoing work the authors are currently pursuing.

Appendix D
Acoustic Signaling

Here we do an explicit analysis of the energetic cost per bit to send information via acoustic waves in 3D propagating either through water or air.
The dynamics are the same in both water and air, however, the damping strength

D.1
Setup

A physical wave may be characterized by the vector field
\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
describing its 3D displacement in time and space.
We can describe the low-frequency dynamics of compressive acoustic waves using the fractional Kelvin-Voigt equation, which is a standard model of acoustic waves with viscous relaxation
wismer_finite_2006
;
holm_unifying_2010
;
holm_waves_2019
:

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}\bm{u}-c^{2}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}-c^{2}\tau^{\eta}\frac{\partial^{\eta}}{\partial t^{\eta}}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}=0

(69)

where the timescale
\tau
controls the strength of viscous relaxation,
\eta\leq 1
is a phenomenological fractional power, and
\partial^{\eta}/\partial t^{\eta}
is a fractional derivative, which for our purposes is defined by its Fourier transform:

\mathcal{F}\left\{\frac{\partial^{\eta}}{\partial t^{\eta}}f(t)\right\}(\omega)=(i\omega)^{\eta}\mathcal{F}\left\{f\right\}(\omega)

When
\tau=0
, eq (
69
) is the regular undamped wave equation.
In distilled water or ideal monatomic gases,
\eta=1
, which is the classical result obtained by Stokes.
In impure media such as blood or seawater, we find that
\eta\in\{0.2,1.0\}

holm_waves_2019
.
Throughout all of the following analysis, we consider
\eta
in that range.
To first order, the local change in density and pressure are linearly related and given by:

\begin{split}\lambda&\equiv\rho(x,t)-\rho_{0}=-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}\\
p^{\prime}&\equiv p(x,t)-p_{0}=c^{2}\lambda\end{split}

(70)

We also note that we can define the local field velocity
\bm{v}
.
It obeys the usual continuity equation, but not momentum conservation due to the viscous damping:

\displaystyle\bm{v}\equiv\frac{\partial\bm{u}}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\lambda=-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{v}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho_{0}\bm{v})\neq-\nabla p

We choose
\lambda
, the density displacement, to play the role of the signal carrying medium to make the analysis of this system symmetric with the other signaling systems studied here.
However, we could just as easily choose
\lambda
to be the pressure fluctuations,
p^{\prime}
, and we would obtain the exact same results because pressure fluctuations and density fluctuations are equivalent up to a factor of
c^{2}
.
We will eventually take the divergence of both sides of eq. (
69
) to obtain the dynamics in terms of
\lambda
.
However, for now we keep it fully general.

The input and output are defined in terms of
\lambda
in much the same way as with diffusive and electrical signaling.
The sender,
I
, located at the origin injects a particle current
I(t)
spread out over a Gaussian sphere of radius
\sigma_{I}
:

\displaystyle J(\bm{x},t)=P(\bm{x})I(t)=\frac{e^{-\bm{x}^{2}/2\sigma_{I}^{2}}}{(2\pi\sigma_{I}^{2})^{3/2}}I(t)

(kg/s/m
3

(71)

The receiver,

\bm{r}
measures the excess particles in a Gaussian sphere of radius
\sigma_{O}

\displaystyle O(t)=\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\bm{x}\,e^{-(\bm{x}-\bm{r})^{2}/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}\lambda(\bm{x},t)

(kg)

(72)

D.2

Electrical signaling was characterized by the RC-velocity
\alpha/c

Diffusive signaling was characterized by the diffusion constant

This system is more complicated because it has two degrees of freedom, the wave velocity

For signals sent at frequency

This requires us to write down the energy of an acoustic wave, add the term
-h(x,t)\lambda(x,t)
, and then observe how this modifies the dynamics given by eq. (
69
).
The Lagrangian density for sound energy in 3D is given by (see eq. 6.17 from
morse_theoretical_1968
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\rho(x,t)\bm{v}(x,t)^{2}-\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}c^{2}}p(x,t)^{2}+h(x,t)\lambda(x,t)

where
h
is the artificial field described above.
Expanding about equilibrium and keeping only terms to 2nd order in the fluctuation fields:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\rho_{0}\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)^{2}-\frac{p_{0}^{2}}{2\rho_{0}c^{2}}-\frac{p_{0}}{\rho_{0}c^{2}}p^{\prime}(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\rho_{0}c^{2}}p^{\prime}(\bm{x},t)^{2}+h(x,t)\lambda(x,t)

We can ignore the second term since it’s constant and the third term since it integrates to zero. We now rewrite the Lagrangian in terms of the displacement field
\bm{u}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{\rho_{0}}{2}\left[\left(\frac{{\partial\bm{u}}}{{\partial t}}\right)^{2}-c^{2}(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})^{2}-2h(x,t)(\nabla\cdot\bm{u})\right]

(77)

We may ignore the
\rho_{0}
since it won’t affect the Euler-Lagrange equations, which produce:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{x}}=\partial_{t}\left(\frac{{\partial\mathcal{L}}}{{\partial(\partial_{t}u_{x})}}\right)+\bm{\nabla}\cdot\left(\frac{{\partial\mathcal{L}}}{{\partial(\bm{\nabla}u_{x})}}\right)

\displaystyle 0=\partial^{2}_{t}u_{x}-\partial_{x}\left(c^{2}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}+h(x,t)\right)

This gives us the lossless wave-equation with an additional dependence on the field
h
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{u}=c^{2}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}+\nabla h(x,t)

Adding back the viscous damping term from eq. (
69
), we have the lossy dynamics in the presence of an external field:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\bm{u}=c^{2}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}+c^{2}\tau^{\eta}\partial_{t}^{\eta}\nabla^{2}\bm{u}+\nabla h(x,t)

Then by applying
-\rho_{0}\bm{\nabla}\cdot
to both sides, we obtain the dynamics in terms of
\lambda=-\rho_{0}\nabla\cdot\bm{u}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\lambda=c^{2}\nabla^{2}\lambda+c^{2}\tau^{\eta}\partial_{t}^{\eta}\nabla^{2}\lambda-\rho_{0}\nabla^{2}h

(78)

Equation (
78
) represents the dynamics in the absence of an input signal.
The input signal
J(\bm{x},t)=P(\bm{x})I(t)
modifies the density fluctuations via:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\lambda(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}\lambda_{\textnormal{eq}}(\bm{x},t)+J(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\lambda(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle-\partial_{t}^{2}\lambda_{\textnormal{eq}}(\bm{x},t)=\partial_{t}J(\bm{x},t)

The linear contribution of this input signal to the dynamics is obtained by simply adding the missing term
\partial_{t}J(\bm{x},t)
to eq. (
78
):

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\lambda=c^{2}\nabla^{2}\lambda+c^{2}\tau^{\eta}\partial_{t}^{\eta}\nabla^{2}\lambda-\rho_{0}\nabla^{2}h+\partial_{t}J

(79)

In Fourier space this becomes:

\displaystyle\left[(ck)^{2}-\omega^{2}+(i\tau\omega)^{\eta}(ck)^{2}\right]\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)=\rho_{0}k^{2}h(\bm{k},\omega)+i\omega J(\bm{k},\omega)

From this we can obtain the response functions
\chi^{\lambda h}
and
\chi^{\lambda I}
, defined by
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle=\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)h(\bm{k},\omega)
and
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle=\chi^{\lambda I}(\bm{k},\omega)I(\omega)
:

\begin{split}\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{\rho_{0}}{c^{2}}\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}-\ell_{c}^{-2}+\nu k^{2}}\\
\chi^{\lambda I}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{i\omega}{c^{2}}\frac{e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}/2}}{k^{2}-\ell_{c}^{-2}+\nu k^{2}}\end{split}

(80)

where
\ell_{c}=c/\omega
is the radian wavelength and
\nu=(i\tau\omega)^{\eta}
is the complex damping parameter (eq. (
73
)).

D.5
Signal

Here we compute
|\chi^{OI}(\omega)|^{2}
, the gain of the signaling network.
We start with the response function of
\lambda
to
J
:
\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{i\omega}{c^{2}}\frac{1}{k^{2}-(\omega/c)^{2}+\nu k^{2}}
.
Plugging this into eq. (
100
) gives:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{i\omega}{c^{2}}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}}{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\frac{u\sin u}{u^{2}-(r/\ell_{c})^{2}+\nu u^{2}}

This can be analytically solved, allowing us to write the transmission coefficient in terms of a scaling portion and correction function:

\begin{split}\left|\chi^{OI}(\omega)\right|^{2}&=\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{{\omega\sigma_{O}^{3}}}{{c^{2}r}}\right)^{2}\left|U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}\left(\frac{r}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)\right|^{2}\\
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}\left(\tilde{z},\nu\right)&\equiv\frac{e^{-i\tilde{z}/\sqrt{1+\nu}}}{1+\nu}\end{split}

(81)

where we use notation
\tilde{z}=r/\ell_{c}
for the transmission distance normalized by the radian wavelength.
The function
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}
is a bit trickier than the universal functions for other systems because it is a function of two parameters.
In the regime
|\nu|=(\tau\omega)^{\eta}\ll 1
, we can expand the expression in the exponential of
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}
with
|\nu|
as a small parameter:

\displaystyle e^{\frac{-i\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{1+\nu}}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-i\tilde{z}+\frac{1}{2}i\tilde{z}\nu+\mathcal{O}(\nu^{2})\right]

\displaystyle\left|e^{\frac{-i\tilde{z}}{\sqrt{1+\nu}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{z}\nu^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{O}(\nu^{2})\right]

This gives us the approximate behavior of the correction function:

\left|U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}(\tilde{z},\nu)\right|^{2}\approx e^{-\tilde{z}\nu^{\prime\prime}}=e^{-r/\ell_{r}}

(82)

with
\ell_{r}
being defined from eq. (
75
).
This exponential falloff is precisely why we defined
\ell_{r}
as we did.
Plugging this into the transmission coefficient expression we obtain the
|\nu|\ll 1
expression for the gain of the signal:

for
|\nu|\ll 1

\displaystyle\left|\chi^{OI}(\omega)\right|^{2}\approx\frac{\pi}{2}\left(\frac{{\omega\sigma_{O}^{3}}}{{rc^{2}}}\right)^{2}e^{-r/\ell_{r}}

(83)

Thus when the damping parameter
|\nu|=(\omega\tau)^{\eta}
is small and the transmission distance is smaller than the characteristic lengthscale (
r\ll\ell_{r}
), we can ignore the contribution of the correction function
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}
.

D.6
Noise

The power spectrum of the equilibrium fluctuations in the density,
S^{\lambda}(\bm{k},\omega)
, is given by applying the fluctuation dissipation theorem
tong_lectures_2012
to
\chi^{\lambda h}
:

\displaystyle S^{\lambda}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT}{\omega}\textnormal{Im}\left[\chi^{\lambda h}(\bm{k},\omega)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{2k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT\rho_{0}}{\omega c^{2}}\textnormal{Im}\left[\frac{k^{2}}{k^{2}-\ell_{c}^{-2}+\nu k^{2}}\right]

By using eq. (
101
) we can then show that the equilibrium fluctuations in the output signal are given by:

\displaystyle S^{O}(\omega)=-\frac{\rho_{0}}{\beta}\frac{8\pi\sigma_{O}^{3}}{\omega c^{2}}\textnormal{Im}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{u^{4}e^{-u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u}{u^{2}-(\sigma_{O}/\ell_{c})^{2}+\nu u^{2}}\right]

which we write in terms of a scaling portion and an integral correction function:

\displaystyle\begin{split}S^{O}(\omega)&=2\pi^{3/2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}\nu^{\prime\prime}}{c^{2}\omega}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)\\
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}(\tilde{y},\nu)&\equiv-\frac{4}{\sqrt{\pi}\nu^{\prime\prime}}\textnormal{Im}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\frac{u^{4}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}-\tilde{y}^{2}+\nu u^{2}}\right]\end{split}

(84)

where
\nu^{\prime\prime}=\sin\left(\frac{{\eta\pi}}{{2}}\right)\nu
is the imaginary part of the damping parameter and
\tilde{y}=\sigma_{O}/\ell_{c}
denotes the receiver radius normalized by the radian wavelength.
We have chosen this normalization of
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}
so that we will find
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}=1
in the regime of interest.
The noise correction function
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}
has an analytic solution:

\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}(\tilde{y},\nu)=&-\frac{1}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\textnormal{Im}\left[\frac{1}{1+\nu}+\frac{2\tilde{y}^{2}}{(1+\nu)^{2}}\right.\\
&\left.-\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}i\tilde{y}^{3}}{(1+\nu)^{5/2}}w\left(-\frac{\tilde{y}}{\sqrt{1+\nu}}\right)\right]\end{split}

(85)

where
w(z)
is the Faddeeva function, defined by (see 7.6.3 from
olver_nist_2010
):

w(z)=e^{-z^{2}}\textnormal{erfc}(-iz)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(iz)^{n}}{\Gamma(n/2+1)}

(86)

We can expand
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}
using
|\nu|
,
\tilde{y}
as small parameters:

\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}(\tilde{y},\nu)\approx&\textnormal{Im}\left[i^{\eta}\frac{\nu}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}+2\sqrt{\pi}i\frac{y^{3}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\right.\\
&\left.+\mathcal{O}(\nu)+\mathcal{O}(\tilde{y}^{2})+\textnormal{real}\right]\\
\approx&1+2\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\tilde{y}^{3}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\end{split}

(87)

Thus for
0.2\leq\eta\leq 1
, we have two regimes for the noise based on the size of
\tilde{y}^{3}/\nu^{\prime\prime}
.
When the receiver is small (
\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{c}(\nu^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}\ll\ell_{c}
), the noise obeys:

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\sim 1

\displaystyle S^{O}(\omega)

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi^{3/2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}\nu^{\prime\prime}}{c^{2}\omega}

(88)

However, when the receiver is larger (
\ell_{c}(\nu^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}\ll\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{c}
), the noise obeys:

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\sim\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_{O}^{3}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}\ell_{c}^{3}}

\displaystyle S^{O}(\omega)

\displaystyle\sim 4\pi^{2}\frac{\rho_{0}}{\beta}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{6}\omega^{2}}{c^{5}}

(89)

In the parameter regime discussed in section
D.3
, the relevant scaling behavior is equation (
88
).

D.7
Dissipation

The linearized energy of an acoustic system relative to the zero point is given by (see eq. (
77
)):

H=\frac{1}{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{3}\bm{x}\,\left[\rho_{0}\bm{v}(\bm{x},t)^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{\rho_{0}}\lambda(\bm{x},t)^{2}\right]

Thus the dissipation induced by the input signal
J(\bm{x},t)
is given by:

\displaystyle\left\langle\textnormal{Diss}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\bm{v}}\left(\frac{{\partial\bm{v}}}{{\partial t}}\right)_{J}\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\left(\frac{{\partial\lambda}}{{\partial t}}\right)_{J}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\left(\frac{{\partial\lambda}}{{\partial t}}\right)_{J}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}}{\rho_{0}}\left\langle\lambda(\bm{x},t)J(\bm{x},t)\right\rangle

Then, by using eq. (
104
) with
\mathcal{C}=c^{2}/\rho_{0}
, we can show that the dissipation kernel is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\omega)=-\frac{\omega}{4\pi^{3}\rho_{0}\sigma_{I}}\textnormal{Im}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\frac{u^{2}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}-(\sigma_{I}/\ell_{c})^{2}+\nu u^{2}}\right]

which we can write in terms of a scaling portion and universal integral function:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{D}(\omega)&=\frac{\omega\nu^{\prime\prime}}{8\pi^{5/2}\rho_{0}\sigma_{I}}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{I}}{\ell_{c}},\nu^{\prime\prime}\right)\\
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\left(\tilde{x},\nu\right)&\equiv-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}\nu^{\prime\prime}}\textnormal{Im}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}u\,u^{2}e^{-u^{2}}}{u^{2}-\tilde{x}^{2}+\nu u^{2}}\right]\end{split}

(90)

where
\nu^{\prime\prime}=|\nu|\sin\left(\frac{{\eta\pi}}{{2}}\right)
is the imaginary part of the damping component and

\tilde{x}=\sigma_{I}/\ell_{c}
denotes the sender radius normalized by the radian wavelength.
We have chosen the normalization of
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}
so that we will find
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}=1
in the relevant regime.
The dissipation correction function
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}
has analytic solution:

\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}(\tilde{x},\nu)&=-\frac{1}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\textnormal{Im}\biggl[\\
&\left.\frac{1}{1+\nu}-i\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\tilde{x}}{(1+\nu)^{3/2}}w\left(-\frac{\tilde{x}}{\sqrt{1+\nu}}\right)\right]\end{split}

(91)

where
w(z)
is again the Faddeeva function (eq. (
86
)).
We can expand
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}
using
\nu
,
\tilde{x}
as small parameters (see section
D.3
):

\begin{split}U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}(\tilde{x},\nu)&\approx\textnormal{Im}\biggl[\\
i^{\eta}\frac{|\nu|}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}&+i\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\tilde{x}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}+\mathcal{O}(\nu)+\mathcal{O}(\tilde{x})+\textnormal{real}\\
\biggr]&\approx 1+\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\tilde{x}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}}\end{split}

(92)

Thus for
0.2\leq\eta\leq 1
, we have two regimes for the dissipation based on the size of
\tilde{x}/\nu^{\prime\prime}
.
When
\sigma_{I}
is small compared to
\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}
(
\sigma_{I}\ll\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}\ll\ell_{c}
), the dissipation obeys:

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\sim 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\omega)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\omega\nu^{\prime\prime}}{8\pi^{5/2}\rho_{0}\sigma_{I}}

(93)

When
\sigma_{I}
is large compared to
\ell_{\sigma}
(
\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}\ll\sigma_{I}\ll\ell_{c}
), the dissipation obeys

\displaystyle U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\sim\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\sigma_{I}}{\nu^{\prime\prime}\ell_{c}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\omega)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\omega^{2}}{8\pi^{2}\rho_{0}c}

(94)

where, as discussed in section
D.3
, the relevant regime for intracellular signaling is equation (
93
).

D.8
Energy Cost per Bit

Here we combine our results for the acoustic signal (eq. (
81
)), the noise (eq. (
84
)), and the dissipation (eq. (
90
)) into our expression for the cost (eq. (
2
)).
We find that the energetic cost of signaling, in
k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT
/bit, is given by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{Ac}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\log 2}{\pi}\frac{r^{2}\ell_{\sigma}^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}^{3}}\frac{U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}\left(\frac{\sigma_{O}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}\left(\frac{\sigma_{I}}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)}{\left|U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{S}\left(\frac{r}{\ell_{c}},\nu\right)\right|^{2}}

(95)

with
\ell_{\sigma}=\nu^{\prime\prime}\ell_{c}
,
\ell_{c}=c/\omega
,
\nu^{\prime\prime}=\textnormal{Im}\left[(i\tau\omega)^{\eta}\right]
.

D.8.1
Intracellular Environments

In section
D.3
, we argued that the regime relevant to intracellular signaling is the regime
\sigma_{I}\ll\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}
,
\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{c}(\nu^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}
.
In this regime, the noise and dissipation functions can be ignored (
U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{N}=U^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{D}=1
).
Thus the cost in this regime in
k_{B}\hskip-1.5ptT
/bit is given by:

(for
\sigma_{I},\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{\sigma}
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{Ac}}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2\log 2}{\pi}\frac{r^{2}\ell_{\sigma}^{2}}{\sigma_{I}\sigma_{O}^{3}}e^{r/\ell_{r}}

(96)

where
\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}
and
\ell_{r}=\ell_{c}/\nu^{\prime\prime}
come from eq. (
75
).

Just as with electrical signaling and diffusive signaling in 3D, we find that there is an exponential dependence of the cost on
r/\ell_{r}
, the ratio of the transmission distance and the characteristic attenuation lengthscale.
However, unlike these systems, we find that the scaling form of the energetic cost per bit (eq. (
96
)) depends on the system constants, which enter through the lengthscale parameter:

\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}=\frac{c}{\omega}(\tau\omega)^{\eta}\sin\left(\frac{{\eta\pi}}{{2}}\right)

where
0<\eta\leq 1
is a phenomenological fractional power dependent on the nature of viscous relaxation in the media under consideration.
For distilled water and monatomic gases,
\eta=1
and we obtain
\ell_{\sigma}=c\tau
.
In ocean water,
\eta\approx 0.37

ainslie_simplified_1998
.
In ultrasound experiments on blood,
\eta\approx 0.21

duck_physical_1990
;
holm_waves_2019
.

D.8.2
Distilled Water

In distilled water, we find
\eta=1
and
\tau\sim 10^{-12}
(s).
What happens to the energetic cost of signaling in this regime?
In section
D.3
, we argued that
\sigma_{I}\ll\ell_{\sigma}
,
\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{c}(\nu^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}
in intracellular environments.
In distilled water, this is no longer true.
Since
\eta=1
, we have
\nu^{\prime\prime}=\nu=\tau\omega
.
This means that the important lengthscales which dictate the scaling form of the noise and dissipation take on the values:

\displaystyle\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{c}\nu^{\prime\prime}=c\tau\approx 1.5\,\textnormal{nm}

\displaystyle\ell_{c}(\nu^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}\in\{94\,\textnormal{$\mu$m},4\,\textnormal{m}\}

This means that while we still have
\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{c}(\nu^{\prime\prime})^{1/3}
for sub-cellular receivers, but we no longer should expect that
\sigma_{I}\ll\ell_{\sigma}
.
This means we can obtain the energetic cost of acoustic signaling in this regime by
replacing the dissipation equation (
93
) with (
94
) in the energetic cost.
Doing this yields:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textnormal{(for $\sigma_{O}\ll\ell_{c}(\nu)^{1/3}$, $\ell_{\sigma}\ll\sigma_{I}$)}\\
\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{\textnormal{distilled}}\sim\frac{2\log 2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{r^{2}}{\sigma_{O}^{2}}\frac{\ell_{\sigma}}{\sigma_{O}}e^{r/\ell_{r}}\end{split}

(97)

In particular, note how this cost compares to the energetic cost of electrical signaling (eq.
47
) when
\sigma_{I}=\sigma_{O}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{Ac}}_{\textnormal{distilled}}}{\mathcal{C}^{\textnormal{El}}}=\frac{2}{\pi^{3/2}}\frac{\ell_{\sigma}}{\sigma_{O}}

This means that acoustic signaling will be more efficient in
pure
water whenever
\sigma_{O}>\ell_{\sigma}\approx 1.5
nm.
One may ask then why cells bother with constructing electrical transmission lines in neurons when acoustic transmission lines could hypothetically perform much better.
The answer lies in entropy.
It’s far easier to produce an electrochemical gradient by pumping charged ions across the membrane than it is to remove all ions from the aqueous environment.
The entropic cost of purifying water is very large at the cellular scale.

Appendix E
Lemmas

E.1
Output response from medium response

Lemma:
\chi^{OI}
formula

Consider a system in
d
dimensions with a density field
\lambda
with mean zero, an output signal defined as the density integrated over a Gaussian shell around point
\bm{r}
, and a time-dependent input signal that is smeared over a Gaussian shell around the origin.

\displaystyle O(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,e^{-(\bm{x}-\bm{r})^{2}/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}\lambda(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle J(\bm{x},t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{e^{-\bm{x}^{2}/2\sigma_{I}^{2}}}{(2\pi\sigma_{I}^{2})^{d/2}}I(t)

Suppose also that we have linearized response function
\chi^{\lambda J}
of
\lambda
to
I(t)
of the form:

\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle\equiv e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}/2}\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)I(\omega)

Then the response function of
O(t)
with respect to
I(t)
is given by:

\chi^{OI}(\omega)\approx\frac{\sigma_{O}^{d}}{(2\pi r^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\,e^{i\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\hat{r}}}\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{r},\omega\right)

(98)

where the approximation holds when
\sigma_{I}
,
\sigma_{O}\ll r
.
When
\chi^{\lambda J}
has rotational symmetry, the result for
d=2
becomes:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)\approx\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,uJ_{0}(u)\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{u}{r},\omega\right)

(99)

where
J_{0}
is a Bessel function.
The result for
d=3
becomes:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}}{r^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,u\sin u\,\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{u}{r},\omega\right)

(100)

Proof:

We start by writing down
O(\omega)
and expanding
\lambda(\bm{x},\omega)
into
k
modes:

\displaystyle\langle O(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,e^{-(\bm{x}-\bm{r})^{2}/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}\langle\lambda(\bm{x},\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\frac{e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{x}}}{e^{(\bm{x}-\bm{r})^{2}/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}}\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle

Solving the
\bm{x}
integral yields

\displaystyle\langle O(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_{O}^{2})^{d/2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\frac{e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}}{e^{k^{2}\sigma_{O}^{2}/2}}\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle

Then we insert the linear response formula for
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle
:

\displaystyle\langle O(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi\sigma_{O}^{2})^{d/2}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\frac{e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}}{e^{k^{2}(\sigma_{I}^{2}+\sigma_{O}^{2})/2}}\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)I(\omega)

We then change to the dimensionless integration variable
\bm{u}=|\bm{r}|\bm{k}
and find:

\displaystyle\langle O(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{d}}{(2\pi r^{2})^{d/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\frac{e^{i\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\hat{r}}}}{e^{\frac{u^{2}}{2}\left(\frac{\sigma_{I}^{2}}{r^{2}}+\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)}}\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{r},\omega\right)I(\omega)

where
r=|\bm{r}|
.
The linear response function
\chi^{OI}
of
O(\omega)
to
I(\omega)
, defined by
\langle O(\omega)\rangle=\chi^{OI}(\omega)I(\omega)
, is everything in the above expression except
I(\omega)
.

Whenever
\sigma_{I}
,
\sigma_{O}

\ll r
, we can drop the exponential term
e^{u^{2}\sigma^{2}/2r^{2}}
.
This is a very weak assumption:
since
\sigma_{I}
and
\sigma_{O}
are the sender and receiver sizes, the task of sending information is not well defined when
\sigma_{I}
,
\sigma_{O}

\geq r
.
From this approximation we obtain our desired result:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{d}}{(2\pi r^{2})^{d/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\,e^{i\bm{u}\cdot\bm{\hat{r}}}\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{r},\omega\right)

In
d=2
dimensions, when
\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)
is rotationally invariant, we can convert to polar coordinates and perform the
\theta
integral:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{2\pi r^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}u\,u\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{u}{r},\omega\right)\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,e^{iu\cos\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{r^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,uJ_{0}(u)\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{u}{r},\omega\right)

where
J_{0}(u)
is a Bessel function.
Likewise in
d=3
dimensions (with rotational invariance), the result is obtained by moving to spherical coordinates:

\displaystyle\chi^{OI}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi\sigma_{O}^{3}}{(2\pi r^{2})^{3/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,u^{2}\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{u}{r},\omega\right)\int_{0}^{\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\sin\theta e^{iu\cos\theta}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\sigma_{O}^{3}}{r^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,u\sin u\,\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{u}{r},\omega\right)

E.2
Output fluctuations from density fluctuations

Lemma:
S^{O}(\omega)
formula

Consider a system in
d
dimensions with a density field
\lambda
with mean zero and an output signal
O(t)
defined as the density integrated over a Gaussian shell around a point
\bm{r}
:

\displaystyle O(t)\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,e^{-(\bm{x}-\bm{r})/2\sigma_{O}^{2}}\lambda(\bm{x},t)

Then the power spectrum of
O(t)
is related to the power spectrum of
\lambda(\bm{x},t)
by:

S^{O}(\omega)=\sigma_{O}^{d}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\,e^{-u^{2}}S^{\lambda}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{\sigma_{O}},\omega\right)

(101)

When
S^{\lambda}
has rotational symmetry, the result for
d=2
trivially becomes:

S^{O}(\omega)=2\pi\sigma_{O}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,ue^{-u^{2}}S^{\lambda}\left(\frac{u}{\sigma_{O}},\omega\right)

(102)

For
d=3
it becomes

S^{O}(\omega)=4\pi\sigma_{O}^{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\,u^{2}e^{-u^{2}}S^{\lambda}\left(\frac{u}{\sigma_{O}},\omega\right)

(103)

Proof:

We start with step 2 from the proof of lemma (
98
):

\displaystyle O(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{d}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\frac{e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}}{e^{k^{2}\sigma_{O}^{2}/2}}\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)

Inserting this into the expectation value
\langle O(\omega)O(\omega^{\prime})\rangle
, we find:

\displaystyle\langle O(\omega)O(\omega^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}^{\prime}\frac{e^{i(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}}}{e^{\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{2}(k^{2}+k^{\prime 2})}}\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\lambda(\bm{k}^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\rangle

Next we insert the identity
\langle f(\omega)f(\omega^{\prime})\rangle=2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})S^{f}(\omega)
(see eq. 2.54 in
bialek_biophysics:_2012
) to both sides of the above expression
(where we apply it to both
\bm{k}
and
\omega
on the right hand side):

\displaystyle S^{O}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{O}^{2d}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}^{\prime}\frac{e^{i(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})\cdot\bm{r}}}{e^{\frac{\sigma_{O}^{2}}{2}(k^{2}+k^{\prime 2})}}\delta^{d}(\bm{k}+\bm{k}^{\prime})S^{\lambda}(\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{O}^{2d}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\,e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{O}^{2}}S^{\lambda}(\bm{k},\omega)

We then change to the dimensionless integration variable
\bm{u}=\sigma_{O}\bm{k}
, and find the desired result:

\displaystyle S^{O}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{O}^{d}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\,e^{-u^{2}}S^{\lambda}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{\sigma_{O}},\omega\right)

E.3
Dissipation kernel from response function

Lemma:
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
formula

This gives a formula for the dissipation kernel
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
, which is essentially a generalization of the real part of electrical impedance for arbitrary systems.
Consider a system in
d
dimensions with a density field
\lambda
with mean zero and an input signal
J(\bm{x},t)
defined via a Gaussian smeared density current:

J(\bm{x},t)=\frac{e^{-\bm{x}^{2}/2\sigma_{I}^{2}}}{(2\pi\sigma_{I}^{2})^{d/2}}I(t)

Suppose that we have linearized response function
\chi^{\lambda J}
of
\lambda
to
J
, defined by
\langle\lambda(\bm{k},\omega)\rangle\equiv\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega)J(\bm{k},\omega)
, which is even in
\bm{k}
and Hermitian in
\omega
:

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda J}(-u,\omega)=\chi^{\lambda J}(u,\omega)

\displaystyle\chi^{\lambda J}(u,-\omega)=\overline{\chi^{\lambda J}}(u,\omega)

And finally, suppose that we can write the dissipative work required to produce this input signal in terms of some factor
\mathcal{C}
, the input current, and the density field:

\langle\textnormal{Diss}(t)\rangle=\mathcal{C}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,\langle J(\bm{x},t)\lambda(\bm{x},t)\rangle

Then the dissipation kernel
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
of
S^{I}(\omega)
is given by

\begin{split}\langle\textnormal{Diss}\rangle&=\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathcal{D}(\omega)S^{I}(\omega)\\
\mathcal{D}(\omega)&=\frac{\mathcal{C}}{\sigma_{I}^{d}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}\,e^{-u^{2}}}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}\,\textnormal{Re}\left[\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{\sigma_{I}},\omega\right)\right]\end{split}

(104)

Proof:

We start by expanding in
\bm{k}
modes and using Parseval’s theorem:

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{Diss}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\int\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{x}\,\langle J(\bm{x},t)\lambda(\bm{x},t)\rangle=\mathcal{C}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\langle J(\bm{k},t)\lambda(-\bm{k},t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}\mathrm{d}\omega\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}}{(2\pi)^{d}(2\pi)^{2}}e^{i(\omega+\omega^{\prime})t}\langle J(\bm{k},\omega)\lambda(-\bm{k},\omega^{\prime})\rangle

We now focus on the Fourier space term, which we evaluate by inserting the linear response function:

\displaystyle\langle J(\bm{k},\omega)\lambda(-\bm{k},\omega^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle J(\bm{k},\omega)J(-\bm{k},\omega^{\prime})\rangle\chi^{\lambda J}(-\bm{k},\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\langle I(\omega)I(\omega^{\prime})\rangle e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}}\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},\omega^{\prime})

where we have used that
\chi
is even in
\bm{k}
and the Fourier transform of the Gaussian
J(\bm{k},\omega)=e^{-\bm{k}^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}/2}I(\omega)
.
Next we use the identity
\langle I(\omega)I(\omega^{\prime})\rangle=2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})S^{I}(\omega)
(see eq. 2.54 in
bialek_biophysics:_2012
):

\displaystyle\langle J(\bm{k},\omega)\lambda(-\bm{k},\omega^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle=2\pi\delta(\omega+\omega^{\prime})S^{I}(\omega)e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}}\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},-\omega)

Plugging this into our original expression, we find:

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{Diss}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,S^{I}(\omega)\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}e^{-k^{2}\sigma_{I}^{2}}\chi^{\lambda J}(\bm{k},-\omega)

To make the integral dimensionless we change variables to
\bm{u}=\sigma_{I}\bm{k}
,

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{Diss}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{C}}{\sigma_{I}^{d}}\int\mathrm{d}\omega\,S^{I}(\omega)\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}e^{-u^{2}}\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{\sigma_{I}},-\omega\right)

Finally, we make the observation that if
I(t)
is real, one can check that
S^{I}(\omega)
is even in
\omega
.
From this and the fact that
\chi^{\lambda J}
is Hermitian in
\omega
, we can see that only the real portion of
\chi^{\lambda J}
will contribute to the dissipation:

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,S^{I}(\omega)\chi^{\lambda J}(-\omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\,S^{I}(\omega)\textnormal{Re}\left[\chi^{\lambda J}(\omega)\right]

This allows us to write down the dissipation kernel
\mathcal{D}(\omega)
, which is analogous to the real part of electrical impedance:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{C}}{\sigma_{I}^{d}}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{d}\bm{u}}{(2\pi)^{d+1}}e^{-u^{2}}\textnormal{Re}\left[\chi^{\lambda J}\left(\frac{\bm{u}}{\sigma_{I}},\omega\right)\right]
