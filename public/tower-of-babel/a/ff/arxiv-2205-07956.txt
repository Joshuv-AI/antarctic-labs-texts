Quantum state inference from coarse-grained descriptions: analysis and an application to quantum thermodynamics
Raúl O. Vallejos, Pedro Silva Correia, Paola Concha Obando, Nina Machado O’Neill, Alexandre Baron Tacla, Fernando de Melo

Abstract
The characterization of physical systems relies on the observable properties which are measured, and how such measurements are performed. Here we analyze two ways of assigning a description to a quantum system assuming that we only have access to coarse-grained properties. More specifically, we compare the Maximum Entropy Principle method, with the Bayesian-inspired recently proposed Average Assignment Map method [P. S. Correia et al , Phys. Rev. A 103, 052210 (2021)]. Despite the fact that the assigned descriptions by both methods respect the measured constraints, and that they share the same conceptual foundations, the descriptions differ in scenarios that go beyond the traditional system-environment structure. The Average Assignment Map is thus shown to be a more sensible choice for the ever more prevalent scenario of complex quantum systems. We discuss the physics behind such a difference, and further exploit it in a quantum thermodynamics process.



I
Introduction

Whether considering our everyday perception of the surrounding environment or a sophisticated experimental setup, a characterization of a physical
system is given in terms of measurement results of its observable properties. The characterization of a physical system is thus not unique: besides considering
which
features are being observed, it is also necessary to take into account
how
these features are being observed.

For the first consideration, the “which features” part, given a set of observed properties – say a set
\mathcal{O}=\{o_{i}\}
of expected values
o_{i}
–, the objective of state inference is to assign to the system a description that abides by the known data. However, more often than not, the number of constraints is not sufficient to single out an unique state for the system, i.e., there are many states which are compatible with the known data. Let
\Omega(\mathcal{O})
be the set of all descriptions which are consistent with the knowledge about the system. Back all the way to Laplace’s Principle of Indifference, it is only reasonable to appoint the same probability for each description in
\Omega(\mathcal{O})
, and to assign the average description,
\overline{\Omega(\mathcal{O})}
, to the system. The use of the Maximum Entropy Principle (MEP)
Jaynes 1957a
;
Jaynes 1957b
, i.e., to assign to the system the description in
\Omega(\mathcal{O})
which has the maximum Shannon entropy, made this intuitive idea more mathematically concrete – allowing for the inclusion of constraints and symmetries, and extending its reach to continuous sets. The MEP has found applications in fields as diverse as biology, computer science, and financial markets
Banavar et al. 2010
;
Karmeshu 2003
;
Harte 2011
;
Boomsma et al. 2014
;
De Martino and De Martino 2018
;
Cesari et al. 2018
;
Berger et al. 1996
;
Bera and Park 2008
;
Caticha and Golan 2014
. Within Physics, the MEP received a great deal of attention as it recovers the ensembles of statistical physics
Pathria and Beale 2011
. The same success is observed in the quantum realm, where one now assigns to the system the quantum state that maximizes the von Neumann entropy, while respecting the known constraints
Bužek et al. 1998
;
Bužek 2004
.

Concerning “how” features are measured, most inference methods, and the MEP in particular, are somewhat reticent. For quantum systems – which are the subject of the present contribution –, the only point which is systematically taken care of is the locality of the observables.
Consider for instance the scenarios described by the theory of open quantum systems
Caldeira and Leggett 1981
;
Breuer et al. 2002
. In such a cases it is possible to split the total system into two parts: the system of interest
S
, which we assume to have access to; and the environment
E
, whose degrees of freedom we have no control of. Besides a possible restriction on the full system (like the total energy), when inferring the total system description, one usually only knows about local properties of the system.

In this case the effective description of the underlying microscopic system is given by effective spins, each one related to a coarse-grained detection of two underlying spins. Physically speaking, such a scenario can model a situation where we do not resolve any individual microscopic degree of freedom of the system, but we only have access to the effective description that emerges from a blurry detection of the whole system.

More concretely, associate to the total system a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
, and assume that we know the expected values for a set
\mathcal{O}
of subsystem
S
properties. Quantum mechanics tells us that there exists a set of observables
\{O_{i}\}
and a density matrix
{\varrho}_{S}
, acting on
\mathcal{H}_{S}
, such that
o_{i}={\rm tr}(O_{i}{\varrho}_{S})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|\}
. As the total system lives in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})
– with
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
representing the set of linear operators acting on
\mathcal{H}
– the reduced density matrix
{\varrho}_{S}
is obtained from the total one,
{\varrho}_{SE}
, by “tracing out” system
E
degrees of freedom, i.e.,
{\varrho}_{S}=\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}[{\varrho}_{SE}]
. Here, the map
\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
is the partial-trace map. If one wants to assign a description to the total system, the local constraints can be extended to the full system as follows:

\displaystyle o_{i}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(O_{i}\;{\varrho}_{S});

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(O_{i}\;\Lambda_{tr_{E}}[{\varrho}_{SE}]);

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(\Lambda^{*}_{tr_{E}}[O_{i}]\;{\varrho}_{SE});

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(O_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}\;{\varrho}_{SE}).

In the above,
\Lambda^{*}_{tr_{E}}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})
is the trace-dual map associated to the partial-trace operation, which only augments the observable with the identity matrix in the
E
sector. One can now use an inference method to assign a description the total system, which should abide by the constraints
o_{i}={\rm tr}(O_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}\;{\varrho}_{SE})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|\}
.

However, with the control of quantum systems reaching unprecedented levels, as driven by the development of modern quantum technologies, a new scenario comes forth. In this new picture, highly isolated complex quantum systems are produced whose microscopic description is experimentally challenging – requiring single particle addressing –, and theoretically hopeless – due to the exponential increase of system dimension with the number of particles.
In this new scenario, not only the observed data is local, but it also stems from an effective description of the system.

In order to deal with this new class of phenomena, quantum channels with the output dimension smaller than the input dimension, dubbed coarse-graining channels, were used to obtain a system’s effective description
Mermin 1980
;
Poulin 2005
;
Kofler and Brukner 2008
;
Raeisi et al. 2011
;
Wang et al. 2013
;
Jeong et al. 2014
;
Park et al. 2014
;
Duarte et al. 2017
;
Silva Correia and de Melo 2019
;
Kabernik 2018
;
Duarte 2020
;
Veeren and de Melo 2020
;
Carvalho and Correia 2020
;
Duarte et al. 2020
;
Pineda et al. 2021
. In this formalism, a coarse-graining map is thus a completely positive map
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
such that
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{D})>\dim(\mathcal{H}_{d})
. Very much like the partial trace map, the output description has fewer degrees of freedom than the total description. Differently from the partial trace, general coarse-graining maps do not require a clear-cut split between system and environment, being thus more appropriate to effectively describe complex highly interacting isolated quantum systems. See Fig.
1
for an example of effective description possible to be characterized by general coarse-graining maps, and how it differs from the usual partial-trace situation.

In the proposed framework, suppose that to a system’s effective description we associate a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
, and that we know the expected values for a set
\mathcal{O}
of effective properties. As before, there exists a set of observables
O_{i}
and a density matrix
{\varrho}_{d}
, in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, such that
o_{i}={\rm tr}(O_{i}{\varrho}_{d})
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|\}
. Since
{\varrho}_{d}
is an effective description, we can assume that it is the result of a coarse-graining map acting on a microscopic description
{\varrho}_{D}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
, i.e.,
{\varrho}_{d}=\Lambda[{\varrho}_{D}]
. If we want to infer the microscopic description, we can extend the known constraints as previously:

\displaystyle o_{i}

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(O_{i}\;{\varrho}_{d});

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(O_{i}\;\Lambda[{\varrho}_{D}]);

\displaystyle={\rm tr}(\Lambda^{*}[O_{i}]\;{\varrho}_{D}).

In the above,
\Lambda^{*}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
is the trace-dual map associated with the coarse-graining channel map
\Lambda
. These constraints can now be included in an inference method to obtain a microscopic description that abides by the coarse-grained data.

While in principle one could use the MEP to assign a description to the microscopic state, when studying the effective dynamics that might emerge from a coarse-grained dynamics
Correia et al. 2021
, an assignment map naturally surfaced. Consider the set of all states which are consistent with the coarse-grained data, i.e., the set

\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{O})=\{\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})\;|\;{\rm tr}(O_{i}\Lambda[\psi])=o_{i},\;\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|\}\}.

(1)

As in the foundations of the MEP, in Ref.
Correia et al. 2021
it was suggested to assign
to the underlying system the description given by the uniform average of the states,
\overline{\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{O})}^{\psi}
. The map
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}:\mathcal{O}\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
that takes the known coarse-grained data and assigns to the system a uniform average among all the fine-grained descriptions that agree with the data was named
Average Assignment Map
(AAM) – more details below.

Due to the undeniable importance of the Maximum Entropy Principle in the whole Physics, it is the aim of the present contribution to compare the assignments inferred by the MEP with those inferred by the AAM. Furthermore, we explore the difference between these two assignment maps in a simple thermodynamical process.

II
State Inference from Coarse-grained Data

In this section we give further details about the Average Assignment Map, and recall the Maximum Entropy Principle. For both inference methods, we will assume that we have access to an effective system description, living in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, for which we know the coarse-grained data
\mathcal{O}=\{o_{i}\}
coming from the expected values of a set
\{O_{i}\}
of corresponding effective observables. Moreover, we will use that the system effective description is generated by a coarse-graining map
\Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
.

In the scenario pictured above, notice that a fine-grained state
\psi
satisfying the coarse-grained constraints
\mathcal{O}
is in general not unique. The set of all fine-grained states in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
that abide by the coarse-grained constraints is then
\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{O})
– see Eq.(
1
).

II.1
Average Assignment Map (AAM)

From an operational perspective, when assembling an effective preparation
{\varrho}
, which is accessed through a coarse-graining map
\Lambda
, and with properties
\mathcal{O}
, microscopically we are in fact sampling from the set
\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{O})
. Due to the linearity of the expectation value, this perspective suggests an averaging map
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
that assigns the appropriate description to the microscopic ensemble
Correia et al. 2021
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}[\mathcal{O}]

\displaystyle\equiv\overline{\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{O})}^{\psi}={\int}d{\mu_{\psi}}{\rm Pr}_{\Lambda}(\psi|\mathcal{O})\,\psi

(2)

where
d{\mu_{\psi}}
is a prior uniform measure over states in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
, and
{\rm Pr}_{\Lambda}(\psi|\mathcal{O})
is a probability density of having the microscopic state
\psi
given the macroscopic constraints imposed by
\mathcal{O}
and the coarse-graining map
\Lambda
. Note that
{\rm Pr}_{\Lambda}(\psi|\mathcal{O})=0
for any
\psi\not\in\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{O})
.

In the particular case where the set
\mathcal{O}
is big enough as to allow for the full state reconstruction of
{\varrho}
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, that is
\mathcal{O}
is a tomographically complete set of values, then we can see
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
as a map between states,
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
.
In this case, that without loss of generality we hereafter concentrate on, the AAM map can be more directly written as

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]=\int d\mu_{\psi}\delta(\Lambda[\psi]-{\varrho})\,\psi\;.

(3)

The delta distribution makes clear that all states in
\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{{\varrho}})
are taken with the same weight in the convex sum.

It is important to discuss the role of the measure
\mu_{\psi}
. In a situation where the microscopic system is very well isolated, all the ignorance about the system state is classical. In this case, one can consider that the set
\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{{\varrho}})
contains only pure states, and the measure
d\mu_{\psi}
will be the Haar measure over pure states in
\mathcal{H}_{D}
. However, if the coupling to the environment cannot be neglected, then one needs to include mixed states in
\Omega_{\Lambda}(\mathcal{{\varrho}})
, and the choice of the measure
\mu_{\psi}
is no longer unique. Below we explore different measures, and see how they change the assigned description. The important point, nonetheless, is to realize that the measure
\mu_{\psi}
allows one to include prior knowledge about the way the system is being prepared.

We used two different methods for calculating the integral in (
3
) above, the choice of method depending on
\Lambda
and
\mu_{\psi}
. The first method consists in direct calculation of the integral using some representation of the
\delta
(to be described later).

An alternative method takes advantage of the symmetries of
\Lambda
, and we briefly introduce it in what follows.

Definition 1

(Channel symmetry)
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
. We say that
U
is a symmetry of
\Lambda
if

\Lambda[U\psi U^{\dagger}]=\Lambda[\psi]

(4)

for all
\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
.

It is sufficient to require that identity above holds for all pure states, for
its validity is extended to all linear operators by linearity. Moreover, it is easy to see that all the symmetries of
\Lambda
form a group.

Assume that
\mu_{\psi}
is unitarily invariant, i.e.,

\mu_{\psi}=\mu_{V\psi V^{\dagger}}

(5)

for all unitary
V
in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
. Then, let
U
be a symmetry of
\Lambda
, and make the change of variables
\psi\to U\psi U^{\dagger}
in Eq.(
3
), to obtain

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]=\int d\mu_{\psi}\delta(\Lambda[\psi]-{\varrho})\,U\psi U^{\dagger}\;.

(6)

As the assigned state does not change by the application of the symmetry on the fine-grained states, we can average – with an arbitrary measure – over a subset of symmetries and still have the same assignment.

Given that averages are linear functions, we can perform the average over the symmetries before the average over the micro-states. In some special cases (we exhibit two examples below) it turns out that such an average over some symmetries presents a remarkable property:

II.2
Maximum Entropy Principle (MEP) assignment

As mentioned in the introduction, the MEP allows for the inclusion of all sorts of constraints. Let
S({\varrho})=-{\rm tr}({\varrho}\ln{\varrho})
be the von Neumann entropy of a state
{\varrho}
. The MEP, taken into account the coarse-grained data
\mathcal{O}
, can be written as:

\displaystyle\psi_{\text{MEP}}=

\displaystyle\arg\max S(\psi)

(9)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\;{\rm tr}(O_{i}\Lambda[\psi])=o_{i}\;,\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|\}.

The above constraints can be equivalently written as
{\rm tr}(\Lambda^{*}[O_{i}]\psi)=o_{i}
, where we employed the trace-dual channel associated with the coarse-graining map
\Lambda
.

To solve the MEP optimization, it is the standard practice to use the Lagrangian:

L=-{\rm tr}(\psi\ln{\psi})-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}({\rm tr}(\Lambda^{*}[O_{i}]\psi)-o_{i}),

(10)

where
\lambda_{i}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the expected value
o_{i}
. The state that maximizes the entropy, while satisfying all the constraints, can then be written as:

\psi_{\text{MEP}}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp{(-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Lambda^{*}(O_{i}))},

(11)

where
Z
is the partition function, given by
Z={\rm tr}(\exp{(-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Lambda^{*}(O_{i}))})
, as required by the normalization condition of
\psi_{\text{MEP}}
. Finally, the Lagrange multipliers are related to the constraints via the equations:

o_{i}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda_{i}}\ln Z,

(12)

for all
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,|\mathcal{O}|\}
.

In the case where
\mathcal{O}
is sufficient to tomographically reconstruct the effective state
{\varrho}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{d})
, one can simply write
{\rm tr}(O_{i}{\varrho})
instead of
o_{i}
in the expressions above.

III
AAM vs. MEP: Three Physical Scenarios

In what follows we compare the AAM and the MEP assignments for three physical scenarios. The first one is the traditional case of an open quantum system with local constraints. For the other two instances the coarse-graining channel cannot be written simply as a partial trace of a subsystem.

III.1
Partial Trace

In this scenario the total system is described by a density operator in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})
, and the local constraints are described by the system’s state
{\varrho}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
. The full system’s description is thus related to the local one via the partial trace map
\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
. In such a situation, the set of fine-grained states that are consistent with the local constraints is then

\Omega_{\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}}({\varrho})=\{\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})\;|\;\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}[\psi]={\varrho}\}.

III.1.1
AAM assignment

U_{E}
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{E}
we have that
\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}[(\mathds{1}\otimes U_{E})\psi(\mathds{1}\otimes U_{E}^{\dagger})]=\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}[\psi]
. The unitary operators of the form
U=\mathds{1}\otimes U_{E}
thus form a symmetry group for
\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}
, and we can readily apply the symmetry method to obtain the average assignment for the partial-trace channel.

III.1.2
MEP assignment

Since we are assuming to completely know the system’s state,
{\varrho}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{S})
, we can consider to know the expectation value of a tomographic set of observables
\{\sigma_{i}\}
, with
i\in\{x,y,z\}
. For the partial trace map, we then have
\Lambda_{{\rm tr}_{E}}^{*}(\sigma_{i})=\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}
. As such, the state on the total space that maximizes von Neumann’s entropy is:

\displaystyle\psi_{\rm MEP}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z}e^{-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}\otimes\mathds{1}_{E}},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e^{-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}}}{Z_{S}}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{E}}{d_{E}},

(15)

with
Z_{S}={\rm tr}[\exp(-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i})]
. Nevertheless, as the local system state is fixed by the constraints, we have that the
\lambda_{i}
are such that

\psi_{\rm MEP}={\varrho}\otimes\frac{\mathds{1}_{E}}{d_{E}}.

(16)

We therefore see that for the partial trace case, where full knowledge about the system’ state is given, both the AAM and the MEP assign the same global description for the total system (system+environment).

III.2
Blurred and saturated detector

Now we introduce our first example of a coarse-graining map that cannot be reduced to the partial trace of a subsystem. The scenario described by the blurred and saturated coarse-graining map
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
, is suggested by optical-lattices experiments
Gross and Bloch 2017
;
Bloch 2008
;
Fukuhara et al. 2015
. In such experiments cold atoms are trapped in optical potential wells. Quantum information processing is done by encoding qubits in the atomic energy levels. The read-out of information is commonly carried out by a fluorescence technique
Sherson et al. 2010
, which for our illustrative purposes can be described, in the single atom case, as follows: a laser with a well-defined frequency is shone over the atom; if the atom is in the excited state, represented by
\lvert 1\rangle
, a rapidly decaying transition is resonantly stimulated and the atom scatters light in all directions; if the atom is the ground state, state
\lvert 0\rangle
, the laser is far from resonance, and no light is scattered.

Now imagine that two neighboring atoms are present in the optical lattice, but due to experimental issues it is not possible to distinguish from which atom the light scattered in the fluorescence measurement comes from. In this situation, the two atoms are seeing as an effective single two-level atom. The full coarse-graining map modelling this situation is given below (details can be found in
Duarte et al. 2017
;
Carvalho and Correia 2020
;
Correia et al. 2021
;
Duranthon and Di Molfetta 2021
):

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 00\rangle\langle 00\rvert]=\lvert 0\rangle\langle 0\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 10\rangle\langle 00\rvert]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lvert 1\rangle\langle 0\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 00\rangle\langle 01\rvert]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lvert 0\rangle\langle 1\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 10\rangle\langle 01\rvert]=0

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 00\rangle\langle 10\rvert]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lvert 0\rangle\langle 1\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 10\rangle\langle 10\rvert]=\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 00\rangle\langle 11\rvert]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lvert 0\rangle\langle 1\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 10\rangle\langle 11\rvert]=0

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 01\rangle\langle 00\rvert]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lvert 1\rangle\langle 0\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 11\rangle\langle 00\rvert]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\lvert 1\rangle\langle 0\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 01\rangle\langle 01\rvert]=\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 11\rangle\langle 01\rvert]=0

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 01\rangle\langle 10\rvert]=0

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 11\rangle\langle 10\rvert]=0

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 01\rangle\langle 11\rvert]=0

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\lvert 11\rangle\langle 11\rvert]=\lvert 1\rangle\langle 1\rvert

(17)

Although inspired by the resonance fluorescence measurement of cold atoms in optical lattices, the above map has no intention to fully capture all the experimental nuances. Nevertheless, it is worth stressing that
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
cannot be reduced to a simple partial trace of one of the subsystems. This can be immediately apprehended by observing the diagonal elements
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[|01\rangle\!\langle 01|]=\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[|10\rangle\!\langle 10|]=|1\rangle\!\langle 1|
– the equalities here are not compatible with the partial trace of either subsystem.

We then proceed to contrast the AAM and the MEP assignments for this, physically inspired, but not traditionally described situation. As for the partial trace case, we will assume to have a tomographic description of the effective system, i.e., we will assume that to know
{\varrho}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
, and we want to determine an assignment in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{4})
which abide by the constraints. The set of fine-grained states that are compatible with the effective system is now:

\Omega_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}({\varrho})=\{\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{4})\;|\;\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]={\varrho}\}.

III.2.1
AAM assignment

In what follows we obtain the AAM assignment considering both pure and mixed states in the set
\Omega_{\text{BnS}}({\varrho})
. We start by employing the symmetry method for pure states, and then continue with direct integration for both cases.

Symmetry method – pure-state measure.

Let
\psi=[\psi]_{ij}
, with
i,j\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, be the matrix representation in the computational basis of a generic state in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{4})
– where for the moment we ignore the trace, positivity, and purity constraints that a pure quantum state must fulfill. For the present coarse-graining map, we have

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{00}&\dfrac{\psi_{01}+\psi_{02}+\psi_{03}}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\dfrac{\psi_{10}+\psi_{20}+\psi_{30}}{\sqrt{3}}&\psi_{11}+\psi_{22}+\psi_{33}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(18)

Using the above expression in the definition of a channel symmetry Eq.(
4
), as we show in Appendix
A
, the symmetries of
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
can be written as

U=\mathds{1}\oplus\mathds{1}\oplus V,

where the operators on the RHS act on the following subspaces:
the first subspace is given by the
\text{span}\{\lvert 0\rangle\}
, the second one by
\text{span}\{(0,1,1,1)^{T}/\sqrt{3}\}
, and the third one is an arbitrary two-dimensional subspace orthogonal to the first two. In this way,
V
is an arbitrary unitary matrix in two dimensions, such that the group of symmetries of
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
is isomorphic to
U(2)
.

. This non-linearity was discussed in greater detail, and applied in an effective communication scenario, in Ref.

.

Explicit integration method – pure-state measure.

Before using direct integration to calculate the matrix elements corresponding to
\square

in Eq.(
19
) for the case of a mixed-state measure, first we reproduce the pure-state measure result. This will allow us to exhibit the method in simpler setting. After that, the generalization to a mixed-state measure will be relatively straightforward.

In what follows, in order to simplify the calculations’ presentation, we employ a hybrid notation for the effective states
{\varrho}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
: in part we use its computational basis elements, and in part its Bloch vector representation. Given a fixed effective state
{\varrho}
we write it as:

{\varrho}=\begin{pmatrix}{\varrho}_{00}&0\\
0&{\varrho}_{11}\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\big(x\;\sigma_{x}+y\;\sigma_{y}\big).

(28)

In the above expression,
\sigma_{i}
, with
i\in\{x,y,z\}
, are the usual Pauli matrices,
x={\rm tr}({\varrho}\,\sigma_{x})=\frac{1}{2}\text{Re}({\varrho}_{01})
, and
y={\rm tr}({\varrho}\,\sigma_{y})=\frac{1}{2}\text{Im}({\varrho}_{01})
.

As we are performing the integration over the pure-state measure (the uniform Haar measure), we directly make use of the pure state structure, as specified in Eq.(
25
). The action of the coarse-graining map
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
in a generic pure state can then be explicitly written as:

\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0}^{\ast}c_{0}&c_{0}(c_{1}^{\ast}+c_{2}^{\ast}+c_{3}^{\ast})/\sqrt{3}\\
c_{0}^{\ast}(c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3})/\sqrt{3}&c_{1}^{\ast}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}c_{2}+c_{3}^{\ast}c_{3}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(29)

With the above representations, the average assignment map, Eq.(
3
), can be expanded as follows for the present case:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}[{\varrho}]\propto\int d\mu_{\psi}

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{0}^{\ast}c_{0}-{\varrho}_{00}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{1}^{\ast}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}c_{2}+c_{3}^{\ast}c_{3}-{\varrho}_{11}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{x})-x\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{y})-y\big)\,\psi\equiv\mathcal{I}\;,

(30)

where
\propto
indicates that the average state given by the l.h.s, expression, named
\mathcal{I}
, may not be normalized. The normalization constant
\mathcal{N}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\equiv\int d\mu_{\psi}

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{0}^{\ast}c_{0}-{\varrho}_{00}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{1}^{\ast}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}c_{2}+c_{3}^{\ast}c_{3}-{\varrho}_{11}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{x})-x\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{y})-y\big)\;.

(31)

The average assignment will then be given as a quotient of two integrals

\mathcal{A}_{\text{BnS}}[{\varrho}]=\frac{\mathcal{I}}{\mathcal{N}}\;.

(32)

Both integrals will be evaluated using the same scheme we now show. The first step is to write the integration measure as a function of the coefficients of
\lvert\psi\rangle=(c_{0},c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})^{T}\equiv c
:

d\mu_{\psi}=\prod_{i}d\text{Re}(c_{i})d\text{Im}(c_{i})\equiv d(c^{\dagger},c).

(33)

To write the measure as
d(c^{\dagger},c)
is a reminder of its
explicit dependence on the complex coefficients
c_{i}
.
Note that the normalization of
\lvert\psi\rangle
is implicit in the first two deltas in (
30
), as long as
{\varrho}_{00}+{\varrho}_{11}=1
.

For the present scenario, the effective state
{\varrho}
is fixed, and therefore its components in the computational basis,
{\varrho}_{ij}
, are also fixed. Nevertheless, for the sake of calculation we will take them as independent variables. As we are going to perform integral transformations, more specifically Laplace (
\mathfrak{L}
) and Fourier (
\mathfrak{F}
) transformations
Laliena 1999
;
Lakshminarayan et al. 2008
, and then take their inverse transformations, their “temporary” independence will be just a calculation artifact. Within this perspective, as
{\varrho}_{00}
and
{\varrho}_{11}
are positive valued, we can write:

\displaystyle\delta(c_{0}^{*}c_{0}-

\displaystyle{\varrho}_{00})=

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\{\mathfrak{L}\{\delta(c_{0}^{*}c_{0}-{\varrho}_{00})\}(s_{0})\}({\varrho}_{00});

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left\{\int_{0}^{\infty}d{\varrho}_{00}\,e^{-s_{0}{\varrho}_{00}}\delta(c_{0}^{*}c_{0}-{\varrho}_{00})\right\}({\varrho}_{00});

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left\{e^{-s_{0}\,c_{0}^{*}c_{0}}\right\}({\varrho}_{00}).

(34)

Similarly, for
{\varrho}_{11}
:

\displaystyle\delta(c_{1}^{\ast}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}c_{2}

\displaystyle+c_{3}^{\ast}c_{3}-{\varrho}_{11})=

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}\left\{e^{-s_{1}\,(c_{1}^{\ast}c_{1}+c_{2}^{\ast}c_{2}+c_{3}^{\ast}c_{3})}\right\}({\varrho}_{11}).

(35)

For the remaining components,
x
and
y
, as they might assume negative values, we perform Fourier transformations:

\displaystyle\delta\big({\rm tr}(

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{x})-x\big)=

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\{\mathfrak{F}\{\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{x})-x\big)\}(k_{x})\}(x);

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left\{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-{\rm i}k_{x}\,x}\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{x})-x\big)\right\}(x);

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left\{e^{-{\rm i}k_{x}\,{\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{x})}\right\}(x);

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left\{e^{-{\rm i}k_{x}\,c\,\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{x}]\,c^{\dagger})}\right\}(x).

(36)

In the last line we employed the dual channel
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}
, the definition of
\psi=cc^{\dagger}
, and the ciclic property of the trace.
Proceeding exactly in the same fashion for the
y
component, we get:

\delta\big({\rm tr}(\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}[\psi]\,\sigma_{y})-y\big)=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left\{e^{-{\rm i}k_{y}\,c\,\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{y}]\,c^{\dagger})}\right\}(y).

(37)

After transforming all the deltas as shown above, and interchanging the order of integration between the inverse transformations and the
integral over states, we can define the the Laplace/Fourier transformed versions of both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{N}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{I}}[s_{0},s_{1},k_{x},k_{y}]

\displaystyle=\int d(c^{\dagger},c)\,e^{-c^{\dagger}Ac}\,c\,c^{\dagger}\;,

(38)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}[s_{0},s_{1},k_{x},k_{y}]

\displaystyle=\int d(c^{\dagger},c)\,e^{-c^{\dagger}Ac}\;,

(39)

where the matrix
A
is given by

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle={\rm diag}\,(s_{0},s_{1},s_{1},s_{1})+{\rm i}\Lambda^{\ast}[k_{x}\sigma_{x}+k_{y}\sigma_{y}]

(40)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}s_{0}&\frac{{\rm i}k_{x}+k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}&\frac{{\rm i}k_{x}+k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}&\frac{{\rm i}k_{x}+k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}\\
\frac{{\rm i}k_{x}-k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}&s_{1}&0&0\\
\frac{{\rm i}k_{x}-k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}&0&s_{1}&0\\
\frac{{\rm i}k_{x}-k_{y}}{\sqrt{3}}&0&0&s_{1}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

In this way, the integrals for
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
are well defined Gaussian integrals (as
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
are positive,
A+A^{\dagger}
is positive definite), which can be readily performed
Altland and Simons 2010
:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}[s_{0},s_{1},k_{x},k_{y}]

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\pi^{4}}{\det A}\;,

(42)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{ij}=\int d(c^{\dagger},c)\,e^{-c^{\dagger}Ac}c_{i}^{\ast}c_{j}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\pi^{4}}{\det A}A^{-1}_{ji}\;,

(43)

where
A^{-1}_{ji}
stands for the
ji
element of
A^{-1}
.

The next step is to perform the inverse transforms.
Although tedious, this step can be directly performed
with the aid of a mathematical software like Mathematica
Inc.
. Further details are to be found in the Appendix
B
.

With this final step, all the elements of
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}({\varrho})
, and in particular the one denoted by the
\square
, can be obtained. The result, as expected, is exactly the same as the one shown in Eq.(
27
).

Explicit integration method – mixed-state measure.

Up to now, when dealing with the
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
coarse-graining map, we assumed that the set
\Omega_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}({\varrho})
contained only pure states. Now we deal with the situation where the fine-grained system might be coupled to some other system that we do not have control of, some environment
E
, and thus all the preparations of our system of interest in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
are already intrinsically described by a mixed state. In such a situation, there exists no unique uniform measure over mixed states. One way to circumvent this is by employing the unique measure of pure states over the whole space, system and environment, and trace-out the environmental degrees of freedom
Zyczkowski and Sommers 2001
.

Within this mindset, we write:

\Omega_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}({\varrho})=\{{\rm tr}_{E}(\Psi)\;|\;\Psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}),\;\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}\circ{\rm tr}_{E}[\Psi]={\varrho}\},

with
\Psi
a pure state in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E})
. The Average Assignment Map is then expressed as:

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}[{\varrho}]=\int d\mu_{\Psi}\delta(\Lambda\circ{\rm tr}_{E}[\Psi]-{\varrho})\,{\rm tr}_{E}[\Psi],

(44)

with
d\mu_{\Psi}
the Haar measure over
\mathcal{H}_{D}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
. It is important to notice that different environment dimensions will lead to different induced measures of mixed states in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})

Zyczkowski and Sommers 2001
. This is physically related to “how” open our system of interest is, and its implication will be explored below.

Let
\{\lvert m\rangle\}
, with
m\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, be the computational basis in
\mathcal{H}_{D}
, and let
\{\lvert\phi_{k}\rangle\}
, with
k\in\{1,2,\ldots,d_{E}\}
, be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{E}
, whose dimension is
d_{E}
. Then, in order to follow the steps for the pure-measure case, we write:

|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{E}}\sum_{m=0}^{3}c_{m}^{k}\lvert m\rangle_{S}\otimes|\phi_{k}\rangle_{E}\;

(45)

and evaluate

\displaystyle{\rm tr}_{E}|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|=

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d_{E}}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{3}c_{m}^{k}\lvert m\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{m^{\prime}=0}^{3}c_{m^{\prime}}^{k*}\langle m^{\prime}\rvert\right)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{d_{E}}|\psi^{k}\rangle\!\langle\psi^{k}|.

(46)

In the above,

|\psi^{k}\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{3}c_{m}^{k}|m\rangle

(47)

are sub-normalized pure states in
\mathcal{H}_{D}
.
Associating to each pure state
|\psi^{k}\rangle
a coordinate vector, i.e.,
|\psi^{k}\rangle\to c^{k}=(c_{0}^{k},c_{1}^{k},c_{2}^{k},c_{3}^{k})^{T}
we can write
{\rm tr}_{E}|\Psi\rangle\!\langle\Psi|=c^{k}c^{k\dagger}
, where the summation over repeated indices is assumed whenever it is not explicitly written.

Like in the pure-state measure, we can split the deltas and define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}[{\varrho}]\propto\int d\mu_{\Psi}

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{0}^{k\ast}c_{0}^{k}-{\varrho}_{00}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{1}^{k\ast}c_{1}^{k}+c_{2}^{k\ast}c_{2}^{k}+c_{3}^{k\ast}c_{3}^{k}-{\varrho}_{11}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c^{k\dagger}\,\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{x}]c^{k}-x\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c^{k\dagger}\,\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{y}]c^{k}-y\big)\,c^{k^{\prime}}c^{k^{\prime}\dagger}\equiv\mathcal{I_{\text{mixed}}}\;,

(48)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\text{mixed}}\equiv\int d\mu_{\Psi}

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{0}^{k\ast}c_{0}^{k}-{\varrho}_{00}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c_{1}^{k\ast}c_{1}^{k}+c_{2}^{k\ast}c_{2}^{k}+c_{3}^{k\ast}c_{3}^{k}-{\varrho}_{11}\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c^{k\dagger}\,\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{x}]c^{k}-x\big)\times

\displaystyle\delta\big(c^{k\dagger}\,\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{y}]c^{k}-y\big),

(49)

where the integration measure now reads

d\mu_{\Psi}=\prod_{k=1}^{d_{E}}d(c^{k\dagger},c^{k}).

(50)

Following the pure-state measure recipe, we now perform the integral transformations to obtain:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{\rm mixed}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{d_{E}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d_{E}}\int d(c^{k\dagger},c^{k})\,e^{-c^{k\dagger}Ac^{k}}\right)\,c^{k^{\prime}}\,c^{k^{\prime}\dagger}

\displaystyle=d_{E}\,\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{d_{E}-1}\,\tilde{\mathcal{I}}[s_{0},s_{1},k_{x},k_{y}]\;,

(51)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{N}}_{\rm mixed}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d_{E}}\int d(c^{k\dagger},c^{k})\,e^{-c^{k\dagger}Ac^{k}}\right)

(52)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{N}}^{d_{E}}[s_{0},s_{1},k_{x},k_{y}]\;,

(53)

with
A
,
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{I}}
as in the previous section (pure case).

After doing the inverse transforms (see Appendix
B
), we arrive at the following result:

\square=\begin{cases}\dfrac{d_{E}}{3d_{E}-1}\dfrac{|{\varrho}_{01}|^{2}}{{\varrho}_{00}}-\dfrac{{\varrho}_{11}}{3(3d_{E}-1)}&\text{mixed states}\\[7.0pt]
\dfrac{|{\varrho}_{01}|^{2}}{2{\varrho}_{00}}-\dfrac{{\varrho}_{11}}{6}&\text{pure states}\end{cases}\;.

(54)

We included the expression for pure states for the sake of comparison.
The pure case corresponds to
d_{E}=1
.

III.2.2
MEP assignment

To obtain the traditional MEP assignment for the situation described by the Blurred and Saturated Detector coarse-graining map,
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
, we start from the general MEP assignment prescription:

\psi_{\rm MEP}^{\varrho}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp{\left(-\sum_{i\in\{x,y,z\}}\lambda_{i}\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}[\sigma_{i}]\right)},

(57)

with
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}^{*}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{4})
the trace-dual map related to
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
. The matrix in the exponent of Eq.(
57
) can be exactly diagonalized, and then exponentiated.

The
\psi_{\text{MEP}}
for the present scenario has the same structure shown in Eq.(
19
), but now its elements read:

\displaystyle\Circle

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{Z}\left(\cosh\lambda-\dfrac{\lambda_{z}\sinh{\lambda}}{\lambda}\right)\;,

(58)

\displaystyle\triangle

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{Z}\left(\frac{(\lambda_{x}-i\lambda_{y})\sinh\lambda}{\sqrt{3}\,\lambda}\right)\;,

(59)

\displaystyle\square

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{3Z}\left(\frac{\lambda_{z}\sinh\lambda}{\lambda}-e^{\lambda_{z}}+\cosh\lambda\right)\;,

(60)

\displaystyle\Diamond

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1-\Circle}{3}\;,

(61)

with the partition function given by

Z=2\left(\cosh{\lambda}+e^{\lambda_{z}}\right).

(62)

By imposing the constraint

\Lambda[\psi_{\rm MEP}^{\varrho}]={\varrho}\;,

(63)

we find that the elements denoted by
\Circle
,
\Diamond
, and
\triangle
have
the same expression as in the case of the average assignment [see Eq. (
24
)].
However, the elements expressed by a
\square
are different:

\square_{\rm MEP}=\square_{\rm pure}-\dfrac{1}{2Z^{2}{\varrho}_{00}}\;.

(64)

Despite the fact that
Z
is a function of only the effective properties, the effective state
{\varrho}
in the present case, we were not able to obtain an analytical expression
Z=Z({\varrho})
– the imposition in Eq.(
63
), or the equivalent set of equations in (
12
), lead to transcendental equations. We then resort to numerical evaluations in order to obtain the assigned state by the Maximum Entropy Principle for the situation described by the Blurred and Saturated detector.

III.3
SU(2) preserving coarse-graining map

In the previous section we saw that the MEP and the AAM, despite similar guiding principles, may lead to different assigned states. However, the size of the underlying system in the Blurred and Saturated detector scenario was small, a two spin-1/2 system. One may wonder whether such differences still prevail when the dimension of the fine-grained system is much larger than the coarse-grained description.

To address such issue, in this section we exploit the coarse-graining map
\Lambda_{J}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})\rightarrow\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
introduced in Ref.
Saideh et al. 2015
. The scenario described by
\Lambda_{J}
is that of a system (that can be a single system, or a collection of subsystems) with total angular momentum
J
, which is then perceived as an effective single spin-1/2 system. The coarse-graining map
\Lambda_{J}
is defined as

\Lambda_{J}[\psi]=\dfrac{1}{2}\bigg(\mathds{1}+\dfrac{1}{j}\sum_{i\in\{x,y,z\}}{\rm tr}({\psi}J_{i})\,\sigma_{i}\bigg),

(65)

where
J_{i}
are the angular momentum components, i.e., the generators of SU(2) rotations around the
x,y,z
axes in the
D
-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{D}
, and

\sigma_{i}
are the usual Pauli matrices.
In the above expression,
j
is the largest eigenvalue of
J_{i}
, thus fixing
D=2j+1
.

An important property of this coarse-graining channel is that it maps
a rotated microscopic state in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})

to a rotated qubit in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
around the same axis and by the same angle, i.e.,
\Lambda_{J}
preserves the
SU(2)
structure. Mathematically, this invariance is expressed by:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{J}[R_{\hat{n}}(\theta){\psi}R_{\hat{n}}(\theta)^{\dagger}]=R{{}^{\prime}}_{\hat{n}}(\theta){\Lambda_{J}[\psi]}R{{}^{\prime}}_{\hat{n}}(\theta)^{\dagger}\;,

(66)

where

R_{\hat{n}}(\theta)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
and

R_{\hat{n}}^{\prime}(\theta)\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})

are rotations by an angle
\theta
around a given axis
\hat{n}
:

\displaystyle R_{\hat{n}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\exp({-i{\theta}J_{\hat{n}}})\;,

(67)

\displaystyle R{{}^{\prime}}_{\hat{n}}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\exp({-i{\theta}\sigma_{\hat{n}}/2})\;,

(68)

with
J_{\hat{n}}=\vec{J}\cdot\hat{n}
,
\sigma_{\hat{n}}=\vec{\sigma}\cdot\hat{n}
,
and we are setting
\hbar=1
.

As before we assume that an effective state
{\varrho}
is prepared, and we want to determine which state we should assign to the underlying system. Microscopically, the set of fine-grained states that abide by the constraints is written as:

\Omega_{\Lambda_{J}}({\varrho})=\{\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H_{D}})\;|\;\Lambda_{J}(\psi)={\varrho}\}.

III.3.1
AAM assignment

Before we dive in the evaluation of the AAM assignments (pure and mixed priors), two general observations are in place. First, note that writing
{\varrho}
in its Bloch representation,

{\varrho}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathds{1}+\vec{r}.\vec{\sigma}\right),

with
\vec{r}.\vec{\sigma}\equiv r_{x}\sigma_{x}+r_{y}\sigma_{y}+r_{z}\sigma_{z}
, the coarse-graining constraint
\Lambda_{J}[\psi]={\varrho}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{j}{\rm tr}({\psi}\vec{J})=\vec{r}.

(69)

Second, notice that if
\psi\in\Omega_{\Lambda_{J}}({\varrho})
, then
R_{\hat{r}}(\theta){\psi}R_{\hat{r}}(\theta)^{\dagger}
also belongs to
\Omega_{\Lambda_{J}}({\varrho})
. This follows from the rotational invariance, Eq.(
66
), when applied to a rotation around the axis defined by the effective state Bloch vector:

\Lambda_{J}[R_{\hat{r}}(\theta){\psi}R_{\hat{r}}(\theta)^{\dagger}]=R{{}^{\prime}}_{\hat{r}}(\theta){\varrho}R{{}^{\prime}}_{\hat{r}}(\theta)^{\dagger}={\varrho}.

(70)

Even though
R_{\hat{r}}
is not a symmetry of
\Lambda_{J}
, as defined in (
4
) (because it is
\psi
-dependent), the above property simplifies the calculation of average assignments.

3

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{J}}[{\varrho}]=\int d\mu_{\psi}\delta(\Lambda_{J}[\psi]-{\varrho})\,R_{\hat{r}}(\theta){\psi}R_{\hat{r}}(\theta)^{\dagger}\;.

(71)

Explicit integration method – pure-state measure.

The evaluation of the AAM assigned state now proceeds very closely to the previous calculations. For the present coarse-graining map,
\Lambda_{J}
, using the observations above, we write

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{J}}[\vec{r}]\propto\int d\mu_{\psi}\delta\left(\frac{1}{j}{\rm tr}[\psi\vec{J}]-\vec{r}\right)\psi\;.

(72)

where for the moment,
d\mu_{\psi}
, is taken as the uniform measure over pure states acting in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
.

As the AAM assigned state is diagonal in the common basis of
J_{\hat{r}}
and
J^{2}
, i.e., the basis
\{\lvert m_{\hat{r}}\rangle\}
defined above, we employ this basis to make the correspondence
\lvert\psi\rangle\rightarrow c=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{D})^{T}
, and to write the angular-momentum matrices
\vec{\mathds{J}}
.
The integral above then becomes

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{J}}[\vec{r}]\propto\int d(c^{\dagger},c)\,\delta\left(c^{\dagger}c-E\right)\,\delta\left(\frac{1}{j}c^{\dagger}\vec{\mathds{J}}c-\vec{r}\right)c\,c^{\dagger}\equiv\mathcal{I}\;.

(73)

Given that the condition
{\rm tr}[\psi\vec{J}]=j\vec{r}
does not directly imposes the normalization of
\psi
, we explicitly included it with the first
\delta
. In the above, we introduced the variable
E>0
for mathematical convenience; at the end of calculations it will be set
E=1

Lakshminarayan et al. 2008
.

As before, we momentarily take
E
and
\vec{r}
as variables. We then perform a Laplace transform, and its inverse, in the normalization condition

\ \delta(c^{\dagger}c-E)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left\{e^{-s\;c^{\dagger}c}\right\}(E),

and a Fourier transformation, and its inverse, in the angular momentum constraints:

\delta\left(\frac{1}{j}c^{\dagger}\vec{\mathds{J}}c-\vec{r}\right)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left\{e^{-{\rm i}\,c^{\dagger}\,\vec{k}.\vec{\mathds{J}}\,c/j}\right\}(\vec{r}).

In the above,
s

\vec{k}=(k_{x},k_{y},k_{z})
are the transformations variables.

Exchanging the order of integration between the inverse transforms and the

, we obtain equations analogous to Eqs. (

\tilde{\mathcal{I}}_{ij}[s,\vec{k}]=\int d(c^{\dagger},c)\,e^{-c^{\dagger}Ac}\,c_{i}^{\ast}c_{j}=\dfrac{\pi^{D}}{\det A}A^{-1}_{ji}\;,

(74)

with the corresponding normalization

\tilde{\mathcal{N}}[s,\vec{k}]=\int d(c^{\dagger},c^{\dagger})\,e^{-c^{\dagger}Ac}=\dfrac{\pi^{D}}{\det A}\;.

(75)

For the present case, the matrix

\displaystyle A(s,\vec{k})=s\mathds{1}+{\rm i}\frac{k}{j}\mathds{J}_{\hat{k}}\;,

(76)

with

III.3.2
MEP assignment

Now we turn our attention to the determination of the state assigned by the Maximum Entropy Principle for the situation described by the coarse-graining map
\Lambda_{J}
. As for the AAM assignment, we assume knowledge of the effective state
{\varrho}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
. The MEP assignment for this case can then be expressed as the following optimization:

\displaystyle\psi_{\text{MEP}}^{\varrho}=

\displaystyle\arg\max S(\psi)

(79)

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }\;{\rm tr}(\sigma_{i}\Lambda_{J}[\psi])={\rm tr}(\sigma_{i}{\varrho})=r_{i}\;,\forall i\in\{x,y,z\}.

As explained in
II.2
, under the above constraints, the MEP assignment is given by:

\psi_{\rm MEP}^{\varrho}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp{\left(-\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\Lambda_{J}^{*}[\sigma_{i}]\right)}\,,

(80)

with the
\lambda_{i}
related to the accessible values via:

-\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{\lambda}}\ln{Z}=\vec{r}\,.

(81)

To determine the action of the dual coarse-graining channel on the Pauli matrices, it is sufficient to notice that for any
\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
, we have

{\rm tr}(\Lambda_{J}[\psi]\,\sigma_{i})=\frac{1}{j}{\rm tr}(\psi\,J_{i}).

(82)

It then follows that

\Lambda^{*}[\sigma_{i}]=\frac{J_{i}}{j}\;.

(83)

That, in fact, is an alternative way of stating the rotational symmetry of
\Lambda_{J}
. Hence, the microscopic state that maximizes the entropy is given by

\psi_{\rm MEP}^{\varrho}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{j}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}J_{i}\right)}=\frac{1}{Z}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{j}\lambda J_{\hat{\lambda}}\right)}\;,

(84)

where
J_{\hat{\lambda}}=\vec{J}\cdot\hat{\lambda}
, and
\lambda
is the modulus of
\vec{\lambda}

Imposing the constraint (
81

-\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{\lambda}}\ln{Z}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial\lambda}\left(\ln{Z}\right)\,\hat{\lambda}=\vec{r}\,.

(85)

From the above expression, we see that
\vec{\lambda}
and
\vec{r}
are parallel. Without loss of generality, we choose

(86)

At the formal level this state is identical to the canonical ensemble of the Brillouin paramagnet

(87)

IV
Application: Quantum Work

In the previous sections we have analyzed different schemes of quantum-state inference
from coarse-grained information. This lead to different fine-grained states: the Average Assignment Map (AAM) – both pure and mixed priors–, and the Maximum Entropy Principle (MEP) assigned states. The aim of this section is to evaluate the observational consequences of these alternative assignments. In what follows, we exploit a thermodynamical process for which we assume that the effective state is given by the
SU(2)
preserving
coarse-graining map, and inspect the impact of the different assignments in the amount of quantum work that can be extracted from the system.

Let us consider a microscopic quantum system composed of a particle, or system of particles, with total angular momentum
j
, which is thermally isolated. Furthermore, suppose that the underlying dynamics is given by the time-dependent Hamiltonian:

H(t)=\gamma\,\cos(\omega t)J^{2}_{z},

(90)

with
\gamma>0
, and
\omega
an external driving frequency.

Now assume that we have access to this system only via the coarse-graining map
\Lambda_{J}
, defined in (
65
), with effective initial state given by:

{\varrho}_{o}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathds{1}+0.7\sigma_{z}\right).

(91)

This initial state is chosen for two reasons. First, as we can see from Fig.(
7
), the difference between the AAM and MEP assignments is considerable for all values of
j
. Second, the AAM and MEP procedures will lead to assigned states which are diagonal in the
J_{z}
basis, and as such the assigned states will commute with the Hamiltonian. In this situation the quantum work is uncontroversially defined by
Binder et al. 2018
;
Campisi et al. 2011
;
Allahverdyan 2014
;
Talkner et al. 2007
:

\displaystyle W={\rm tr}\left(\overline{\psi}_{0}\,(H_{F}-H_{I})\right),

(92)

with
\overline{\psi}_{0}
an initial assigned state (AAM-pure, AAM-mixed, or MEP) and

H_{I}\equiv H(0),\>\>H_{F}=U^{\dagger}_{\tau}H(\tau)U_{\tau}\;,

(93)

with
U_{\tau}
the usual time-ordered evolution operator.

In such a scenario, the average quantum work reads

W=\gamma\,(\cos\omega\tau-1)\,{\rm tr}\left(\overline{\psi}_{0}J^{2}_{z}\right)\;.

(94)

From the expression above, it is clear that the difference between the assignments will translate into different values of average work.
The results for the quantum work are shown in Fig. (
9
).

Figure 9:
Average quantum work for different assignments.
The curves above shown the amount of work that can be extracted from the quantum system when only the effective state is known, for different choices of assignments and different system sizes. For each value of
j
(3/2, 5/2, 7/2) three assignements are considered:
the MEP state (dashed line), the AAM state for a mixed prior (
d_{E}=2j+1
; solid line),
and the AAM state for a pure prior (dot-dashed line). In all the cases, the AAM state over pure states is the one that allows for more work extraction.

It is clear from Fig. (
9
) that using the MEP assignment always leads to the least amount of work that can be extracted from the system. Moreover, among the AAM assignments, to use the average over pure states is always more beneficial, in terms of extracted work, than using the average over mixed states. This ordering can be understood by the knowledge of the physical scenario that each choice entails. While the MEP assignment is fixed given the constraints and the coarse-graining map, for the AAM assignments we still need to specify the measure (the prior) over the microscopic states that abide by the constraints. Choosing a pure state prior is consistent with an experimental setup where the microscopic system is fully isolated. A mixed state prior with a given environment dimension, on the other hand, assumes some lack of control about the microscopic system and some information about the environment. It is then reasonable to expect that from a situation where a pure state prior applies, one can extract more work from the quantum system, as it is the situation where more control about the system is present. Of course, using a prior which is not consistent with the experimental situation will lead to a mismatch between the amount of work predicted, and the one in fact extracted/produced.

V
Conclusions

The maximum entropy principle (MEP) is ubiquitous, and rightly so as it is a cornerstone of thermodynamics and, more generally, of quantum statistical mechanics. Nevertheless, with the raise of complex quantum systems, new scenarios come into play.

Here we analyzed how an assignment map, which shares some foundational ingredients with the MEP, namely the Average Assignment Map (AAM) behaves in various situations. As expected, in the scenario where only local access to a physical system is granted, the so called Open Quantum System picture, both assignments coincide.

However, in situations that go beyond the system-environment split, the two assignment strategies may differ. As we explicitly showed, in the case where two spin-1/2 particles, due to experimental constraints, are seen as an effective single spin-1/2, the two assignments differ – see Eq.
64
and Fig.
3
.

As the underlying system is small, a two spin-1/2 particles, one may expect that such a difference would disappear for larger systems. With the coarse-graining map introduced in
III.3
, we discredited this possibility. Even for increasing microscopic system dimensions, the difference between the MEP and the AAM assignments remains – see Fig.
7
. As it can be seen, the difference in fact increases with the underlying system dimension in this situation.

Where does the difference come from? Both assignments originate from the same idea: there are many microscopic states that abide by some few coarse-grained constraints. If no further constraints are present, the way we evaluate the expectation value of observable quantities in quantum mechanics suggests that an uniform average among all the states that satisfy the constraints is a sensible assignment. The MEP employs an entropic function to obtain such an assignment, with the idea that uniform mixtures maximize the entropy.

The AAM, on the other hand, makes this averaging process explicit. In doing so it becomes evident that an uniform measure over the microscopic states must be chosen. As there is no unique uniform measure among mixed states, the AAM allows for the introduction of some prior knowledge about the experimental condition – how isolated is the underlying system. If the microscopic system is very well isolated, one can take the average over pure states. If the environmental influence cannot be disregarded, an averaging over mixed states is more judicious. At this point the AAM connects to Bayesian inference methods
Caves et al. 2002
;
Blume-Kohout 2010
, and we show – see for instance Figures
2
and
8
– how the assignments differ for each prior knowledge situation.

Finally, when should one use the MEP or the AAM? We showed that depending on the access one has of the system, the assigned states may differ. Using the knowledge about how one access the system, and how the system interacts with its environment, if known, can only be beneficial. As different levels of knowledge about an experimental situation are often tied together with more efficient processes, we showed a thermodynamical process where the benefit of employing the AAM assignment is unmistakable (see Fig.
9
). The gain of using the AAM assignment, instead of the MEP one, is specially clear in the case where one knows that the system is highly isolated. It should be remarked that choosing an assignment which theoretically would allow for a greater extraction of work, in an experiment scenario which is not befitting with such a choice, will only lead to a poor description and no gain.

).
The difference between assignments has the following structure:

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}^{\text{mixed}}[{\varrho}]-\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}^{\text{pure}}[{\varrho}]=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&\mu&\mu\\
0&\mu&0&\mu\\
0&\mu&\mu&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(117)

From Eq. (
54
) we obtain

\mu=\dfrac{(d_{E}-1)(1-r^{2})}{4(3d_{E}-1)(1+z)}\;.

(118)

The eigenvalues of the difference matrix (
117
) are

\{2\,\mu,-\mu,-\mu,0\}
.
Then, the trace distance
\Delta
is

\Delta=2\,\mu=\dfrac{(d_{E}-1)(1-r^{2})}{2(3d_{E}-1)(1+z)}\;.

(119)

In order calculate the probability distribution of
\Delta
we must
compute the following integral over the Bloch ball:

{\rm Pr}(\Delta|d_{E})=\frac{3}{4\,\pi}\int dxdydz\,\delta\left(\Delta-2\mu(x,y,z;d_{E})\right)\;,

(120)

which leads to Eq. (
55
).

Appendix D
\Lambda_{J}
AAM assignments – Details

In the case of the present coarse graining it is not possible to obtain general formulae
of the average assignments for arbitrary
j
and
d_{E}
.
Each case must be handled separately.
In the following we show some details of the calculation of the inverse Fourier-
and Laplace-transforms of the quantities
\mathcal{\tilde{I}}
and
\mathcal{\tilde{N}}
,
whose quotient,
\mathcal{I}(\vec{r})/\mathcal{N}(\vec{r})
, determines the average assignment.

We start by calculating the norm
\mathcal{N}(\vec{r})
.
The determinant (
76
) reads

\det A=\prod_{m=-j}^{j}\left(s+\dfrac{ikm}{j}\right)\;.

(121)

Note that
\det A
is even function of
k
.
We do first the inverse Fourier transform of
\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(s,\vec{k})
.
As
\det A
only depends on
k=|\vec{k}|
, it is convenient to use spherical coordinates
in
\vec{k}
-space;
without loss of generality, we choose
\hat{z}=\hat{r}
.
Then, the Fourier transform is given by

\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(s,\vec{r})=\dfrac{4\,\pi^{Dd_{E}+1}}{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\,\dfrac{k\,\sin(k\,r)}{(\det A)^{d_{E}}}\;.

(122)

Next, we Laplace transform:

\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(\vec{r})=\mathfrak{L}^{-1}_{s}\left\{\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(s,\vec{r})\right\}(E)|_{E=1}\;.

(123)

By virtue of the SU(2) symmetry, the norm only depends on the modulus of
\vec{r}
,
for all
j
and
d_{E}
.
We show one example, for
j=3/2
and
d_{E}=2
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(s,r)

\displaystyle=\frac{27\pi^{10}e^{-3rs}}{128\,rs^{6}}\left[2rs+e^{2rs}(6rs-3)+3\right]\;,

(124)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{9\pi^{10}}{5120r}\left[3(1-3r)^{5}\theta(1-3r)+\right.

\displaystyle\mkern-48.0mu\left.10r(1-3r)^{4}\theta(1-3r)+3(r-1)^{5}+30(r-1)^{4}r\right]\;.

(125)

As
j
and/or
d_{E}
are increased, the expressions above grow rapidly, though preserving
the structure:
\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(r)
is a sum of Heaviside theta functions
multiplied by polinomials of
r
; the theta functions increase in number with
j
, the polinomials
increase in degree with
j
and
d_{E}
.

Now, let us turn to
\mathcal{\tilde{I}}_{\rm mixed}
.
Using Eqs. (
75
) and (
74
) into (
77
),
we arrive at

\mathcal{\tilde{I}}_{{\rm mixed},mm}(s,\vec{k})=d_{E}\,\pi^{d_{E}D}\dfrac{C_{mm}}{(\det A)^{d_{E}+1}}\;.

(126)

We have observed that
C_{mm}
, the diagonal cofactors of
A
,
do not depend on the azimuthal angle
\phi
; then, the inverse Fourier transform
reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{\tilde{I}}_{{\rm mixed},mm}(s,r)

\displaystyle=2d_{E}\,\pi^{d_{E}D+1}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk\dfrac{k^{2}}{(\det A)^{d_{E}+1}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{\pi}d\theta\sin{\theta}\exp{(ikr\cos\theta)}\,C_{mm}\;.

(127)

The last step consists in inverse-transforming Laplace (
s\to E
) the expression above,
and setting
E=1
.
The result,
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm mixed},mm}(r)
, has the same structure as the norm

\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(r)
, but appreciably more complex.
Finally, dividing
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm mixed},mm}(r)
by
\mathcal{N}_{\rm mixed}(r)

we obtain the probabilities
p_{m}(r)
plotted in the Figures
4
and
5
.

Appendix E
Further numerical analysis –
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
and
\Lambda_{J}

In the main text we showed that, for a fixed coarse-graining map
\Lambda
and fixed effective state
{\varrho}
, the assignments when employing pure or mixed priors are in general different – with the exception of the partial trace map. This conclusion can be visually apprehended, for instance, from Figs.
2
and
7
. For
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
the analytical evaluation of the trace distance between
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}^{\text{pure}}({\varrho})
and
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}^{\text{mixed}}({\varrho})
is presented in Appendix
C
.

In the above discussion it was, nevertheless, assumed that the effective state
{\varrho}
could be perfectly prepared, which of course it is not the case in a realistic scenario. Two points could then be raised:
i)
How close the error-free analytical assignment
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is from the one obtained when preparation errors are allowed?
ii)
Does the difference between the assigned states for pure and mixed priors prevail when errors in the preparation of
{\varrho}
are allowed?

To address these points, we extended the definition of the set of states that abide by the constraints, as to allow for a preparation error
\epsilon
:

\Omega^{\epsilon}_{\Lambda}({\varrho})=\{\psi\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})\;|\;||\Lambda(\psi)-{\varrho}||_{1}\leq\epsilon\}.

(128)

We then proceed by the way of a simple Rejection Sampling algorithm: for fixed
{\varrho}
,
\Lambda
and
\epsilon
, we uniformly sampled a large number (to be specified later) of states in
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{D})
and checked which ones belong to
\Omega^{\epsilon}_{\Lambda}({\varrho})
. With the ones that are selected, we estimate the assigned state with preparation error
\epsilon
as the average state,
\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]=\overline{\Omega^{\epsilon}_{\Lambda}({\varrho})}
.

Numerically, we generated a database of
10^{6}
pure 2 qubits states, and
10^{6}
pure 3 qubits states, sampled from their respective Haar measure. These databases were used both for
\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}
and
\Lambda_{\text{J=3/2}}
. With the two qubit sample we obtained
\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon,\text{pure}}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon,\text{pure}}_{\Lambda_{\text{J=3/2}}}
for selected effective states (see below). With the database of 3 qubits, after performing the partial trace on the third qubit, we estimated
\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon,\text{mixed}}_{\Lambda_{\text{BnS}}}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon,\text{mixed}}_{\Lambda_{\text{J=3/2}}}
. In all cases we set the preparation error as
\epsilon=0.025
.

Figures
10
and
11
summarize the obtained results. In both plots, the blue bars (left bar for each point in the horizontal axis) quantify the total difference between the analytical expression and the ones obtained numerically, i.e., for each
{\varrho}
it equals
||\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon,\text{pure}}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]-\mathcal{A}^{\text{pure}}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]||_{1}+||\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon,\text{mixed}}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]-\mathcal{A}^{\text{mixed}}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]||_{1}
. The orange bars (right bar for each point in the horizontal axis) quantify the difference between the pure and mixed prior analytical states for each
{\varrho}
, i.e.,
||\mathcal{A}^{\text{pure}}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]-\mathcal{A}^{\text{mixed}}_{\Lambda}[{\varrho}]||_{1}
.
