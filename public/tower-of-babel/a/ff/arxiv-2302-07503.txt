Abstract
:
This paper considers deep neural networks for learning weakly dependent processes in a general framework that includes, for instance, regression estimation, time series prediction, time series classification.
The
\psi
-weak dependence structure considered is quite large and covers other conditions such as mixing, association,
\ldots

Firstly, the approximation of smooth functions by deep neural networks with a broad class of activation functions is considered.
We derive the required depth, width and sparsity of a deep neural network to approximate any Hölder smooth function, defined on any compact set
{\mathcal{X}}
.
Secondly, we establish a bound of the excess risk for the learning of weakly dependent observations by deep neural networks.
When the target function is sufficiently smooth, this bound is close to the usual
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
.

Keywords:
Deep neural networks, weak dependence, function approximation, excess risk, ERM principle.

Introduction

We focus on the supervised learning framework and consider the training sample
D_{n}=\{Z_{1}=(X_{1},Y_{1}),\cdots,Z_{n}=(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
which is a trajectory of a stationary and ergodic process
\{Z_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{t}),~t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, taking values in
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathcal{X}
is the input space and
\mathcal{Y}
the output space.
In the sequel, we assume that
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
, with
d_{x},d_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Denote by
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
the set of measurable functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Y}
.
Consider a loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
and for a predictor
h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, define the risk,

R(h)=\mathbb{E}_{Z_{0}}\big[\ell\big(h(X_{0}),Y_{0}\big)\big].

(1
.
1)

For a learner
h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, a prediction of
Y_{t}
is
\widehat{Y}_{t}=h(X_{t})
for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The ”best” predictor
h^{*}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, when it exists, is the one that achieves the smallest risk, that is,

R(h^{*})=\underset{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}{\inf}R(h).

(1
.
2)

We would like to construct a learner
h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
such that, for any
t\in\mathbb{Z}
,
h(X_{t})
is average ”close” to
Y_{t}
; that is, a learner with the smallest risk.
But in general, this risk cannot be minimized in practice, because the distribution of
(X_{0},Y_{0})
is unknown.
Consider the empirical risk, defined for all
h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
by,

\widehat{R}_{n}(h)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\big(h(X_{i}),Y_{i}\big).

(1
.
3)

Thus, the aim is to build from the training sample
D_{n}
, a learner
\widehat{h}_{n}
, that minimizes the empirical risk.
In the sequel, we set
\ell(h,z)=\ell\big(h(x),y\big)
for all
z=(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
.

We focus on the class of deep neural networks (DNN) predictors, with a general activation function
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Recall that, a neural network architecture
(L,\mathbf{p})
stands for a positive integer
L
called the number of hidden layers or depth and a width vector
\mathbf{p}=(p_{0},p_{1},\cdots,p_{L+1})\in\mathbb{N}^{L+2}
. Therefore, a DNN with network architecture
(L,\mathbf{p})
is any function of the
form,

h:\mathbb{R}^{p_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p_{L+1}},~x\mapsto h(x)=A_{L+1}\circ\sigma_{L}\circ A_{L}\circ\sigma_{L-1}\circ A_{L-1}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma_{1}\circ A_{1}(x),

(1
.
4)

where for any
\ell=1,\cdots,L+1
,
A_{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell-1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p_{\ell}}
is an affine linear application defined by
A_{\ell}(x):=W_{\ell}x+b_{\ell}
for given
p_{\ell-1}\times p_{\ell}
weight matrix
W_{\ell}
, a shift vector

and

is an element-wise nonlinear activation map defined as
\sigma_{\ell}(z)=(\sigma(z_{1}),\cdots,\sigma(z_{p_{\ell}}))^{T}

denotes the transpose.
In the setting considered here,

(input dimension) and

(output dimension).
For a DNN
f
of the form (
1.4
), the vector of its parameters is denoted by

\theta(h)=\big(\text{vec}(W_{1})^{T},b_{1}^{T},\cdots,\text{vec}(W_{L+1})^{T},b_{L+1}^{T}\big),

denotes its depth and

For any

(1

where for any

•

For any function

Definition
2.1

An

\psi:[0,\infty)^{2}\times\mathbb{N}^{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty)

decreasing
to zero at infinity such that,
for any

with

,
g_{2}:E^{v}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(

)
and for any

(s_{1},\cdots,s_{u})
and any

-tuple
(t_{1},\cdots,t_{v})
with
s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{u}\leq s_{u}+r\leq t_{1}\leq\cdots\leq t_{v}

the following inequality is fulfilled:

\left|\mathrm{Cov}\left(g_{1}(Z_{s_{1}},\cdots,Z_{s_{u}}),g_{2}(Z_{t_{1}},\cdots,Z_{t_{v}})\right)\right|\leq\psi\left(\mathrm{Lip}(g_{1}),\mathrm{Lip}(g_{2}),u,v\right)\epsilon(r).

[
4
]

•

\psi\left(\mathrm{Lip}(g_{1}),\mathrm{Lip}(g_{2}),u,v\right)=v\mathrm{Lip}(g_{2})

3
Hölder smooth functions approximation by DNNs

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
.
For any
\beta=(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d_{x}})^{T}\in\mathbb{N}^{d_{x}}
and
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d_{x}})^{T}\in U
, we set

|\beta|=\sum_{i=i}^{d_{x}}\beta_{i}\text{ and }\partial^{\beta}=\dfrac{\partial^{|\beta|}}{\partial x_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d_{x}}^{\beta_{d_{x}}}}.

For any
s>0
, the Hölder space
\mathcal{C}^{s}(U)
is a set of vector valued functions
h:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{y}}
such that, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d_{x}}
with
|\beta|\leq[s]
,
\|\partial^{\beta}h\|_{\infty}<\infty
and for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d_{x}}
with
|\beta|=[s]
,
Lip_{s-[s]}(\partial^{\beta}h)<\infty
.
This space is equipped with the norm

\|h\|_{\mathcal{C}^{s}(U)}=\sum_{0\leq|\beta|\leq[s]}\|\partial^{\beta}h\|_{\infty}+\sum_{|\beta|=[s]}Lip_{s-[s]}(\partial^{\beta}h).

For
s>0
,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
,
\mathcal{C}^{s}(\overline{U})
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{C}^{s}(\overline{U})}
is a Banach space (see for example
[
24
]
,
[
9
]
).
For any
s>0
,
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
and
\mathcal{K}>0
, set

\mathcal{C}^{s,\mathcal{K}}(U)=\big\{h\in\mathcal{C}^{s}(U),~\|h\|_{\mathcal{C}^{s}(U)}\leq\mathcal{K}\big\}.

Let us consider the following definition, see also
[
18
]
,
[
19
]
.

Definition
3.1

Let a function
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

1.

g
is continuous piecewise linear (or ”piecewise linear” for notational simplicity) if it is continuous and there exists
K
(
K\in\mathbb{N}
) break points
a_{1},\cdots,a_{K}\in\mathbb{R}
with
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{K}
such that, for any

,
g^{\prime}(a_{k}-)\neq g^{\prime}(a_{k}+)
and
g
is linear on
(-\infty,a_{1}],[a_{1},a_{2}],\cdots[a_{K},\infty)
.

2.

g
is locally quadratic if there exits an interval
(a,b)
on which
g
is three times continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives and there exists
t\in(a,b)
such that
g^{\prime}(t)\neq 0
and
g^{\prime\prime}(t)\neq 0
.

The classical activation functions
\sigma(z)=\max(z,0)
(ReLU) is piecewise linear and
\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})
(sigmoid) is locally quadratic (see

\sigma
.

(
A4

There exists
\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}>0

\sigma\in\Lambda_{1,\mathcal{K}_{\sigma}}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover,
\sigma
is either piecewise linear or locally quadratic and fixes a segment

Recall that,

.

4
Excess risk bound

Consider the learning problem presented in the introduction, based on the observations
D_{n}=\{Z_{1}=(X_{1},Y_{1}),\cdots,Z_{n}=(X_{n},Y_{n})\}
which is a trajectory of a stationary and ergodic process
\{Z_{t}=(X_{t},Y_{t}),~t\in\mathbb{Z}\}
, taking values in
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, with
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
, that is,
d_{y}=1
.
We focus on the class of DNNs
\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,{d_{x}},1}(L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n})
(defined at (
1.6
)), for some
L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n}>0
.
Set for the sequel,
\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}:=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,{d_{x}},1}(L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n})
.
Also, denote,

M_{n}:=M_{L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n}},~G_{n}:=G_{L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n}}\text{ and }\widetilde{M}_{n}:=M_{F_{n}},

(4
.
1)

where
M_{L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n}}
,
G_{L_{n},N_{n},S_{n},B_{n},F_{n}}
and
M_{F_{n}}
are defined in (
2.2
) and (
2.3
).
The neural network obtained from the empirical risk minimization (ERM) algorithm is given by,

\widehat{h}_{n}=\underset{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}}{\argmin}\widehat{R}_{n}(h),

(4
.
2)

where
\widehat{R}_{n}
is defined at (
1.3
).
The excess risk is given by

R(\widehat{h}_{n})-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}R(h)
, where
R
is defined at (
1.1
).
This excess risk has the following well known decomposition,

R(\widehat{h}_{n})-\underset{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}{\inf}R(h)=\underset{\text{Estimation error}}{\underbrace{R(\widehat{h}_{n})-\underset{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}}{\inf}R(h)}}+\underset{\text{Approximation error}}{\underbrace{\underset{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}}{\inf}R(h)-\underset{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}{\inf}R(h)}}.

(4
.
3)

In the sequel, we consider the target neural network
h_{\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}}\in\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}
, assumed to exist and satisfies,

R(h_{\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}})=\underset{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}}{\inf}R(h).

(4
.
4)

A major concern in statistical learning theory is to solve the problem of the best trade-off between the estimation and approximation errors.
This is done by taking into account the complexity of the class of hypothesis functions, which must not be too large, nor too small.

In this section, we focus on the convergence rate of the excess risk, and set an additional assumption.

(
A5
):

There exists a predictor
h^{*}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, satisfying
R(h^{*})=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}R(h)
and
h^{*}\in\mathcal{C}^{s,\mathcal{K}}(\mathcal{X})
for some
s,{\mathcal{K}}>0
.

The following theorem provides a bound of the excess risk.

Theorem 4.1

Assume that (
A1
)-(
A5
) hold. Let
\eta,\nu\in(0,1)
and
\alpha>2+d_{x}/s
.
Then, there exist universal constants
L_{0},N_{0},S_{0},B_{0}>0
and

n_{0}\geq 1
, depending on
{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell},C_{\sigma},d_{x},\eta,\nu,\alpha,s,L_{0},N_{0},S_{0},B_{0}
, such that, for any
n\geq n_{0}
,
F_{n}>0
, if for the DNN’s class

\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,n}=\mathcal{H}_{\sigma,{d_{x}},1}\Big((L_{0}/\alpha)\log n,N_{0}n^{d_{x}/(s\alpha)},(S_{0}/\alpha)n^{d_{x}/(s\alpha)}\log n,n^{4(d_{x}/s+1)/\alpha},F_{n}\Big),

M_{n}<\infty
, then,
we have with probability at least
1-\eta
,

R(\widehat{h}_{n})-\underset{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}{\inf}R(h)\leq\dfrac{2M_{n}+{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}}{n^{1/\alpha}}+\Bigg(\frac{\log(2C_{1}\log n/\eta)}{C_{n,2}}\Bigg)^{1/2}

(4
.
5)

for some constant
C_{1}>0
, where
\widehat{h}_{n}
is defined in (
4.2
),
M_{n}
is given in (
4.1
) and (
2.2
), and
C_{n,2}
is given in (
5.21
).

Remark 4.2

If
{\mathcal{Y}}
is bounded, and by choosing a constant sequence
F_{n}=F>0
, then, from (
2.5
), (
4.1
) and (
4.5
), we get,

R(\widehat{h}_{n})-\underset{h\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})}{\inf}R(h)\leq\dfrac{2\max\big(\widetilde{M}_{F}+2{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}(\|{\mathcal{X}}\|+\|{\mathcal{Y}}\|),1\big)+{\mathcal{K}}_{\ell}}{n^{1/\alpha}}+\Bigg(\frac{\log(2C_{1}\log n/\eta)}{C_{n,2}}\Bigg)^{1/2}.

(4
.
6)

Which shows that, the convergence rate of the excess risk is less than
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/\alpha})
for all
\alpha>2+d_{x}/s
.
This rate is close to
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2})
when
s\gg d_{x}
.
Note that, the rate obtained in
[
18
]
is close to
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
when
s\gg d_{x}
. But, these findings are in specific cases of regression, classification, independent observations and with an input space
{\mathcal{X}}=[0,1]^{d_{x}}
.
The results obtained here are in a general setting and for a large class of dependent processes.
