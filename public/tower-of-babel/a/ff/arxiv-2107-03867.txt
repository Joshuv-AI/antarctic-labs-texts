A stochastic reconstruction theorem
Hannes Kern

Abstract
In a recent landmark paper, Khoa Lê (2020) established a stochastic sewing lemma which since has found many applications in stochastic analysis. He further conjectured that a similar result may hold in the context of the reconstruction theorem within Hairer’s regularity structures. The purpose of this article is to provide such a stochastic reconstruction theorem. We also discuss two variations of this theorem, motivated by different constructions of stochastic integration against white noise. Our formulation makes use of the distributional viewpoint of Caravenna–Zambotti (2021).



1
Introduction

1.1
Stochastic reconstruction in a nutshell

In recent years, the reconstruction theorem from Martin Hairer’s regularity structure theory
[
Hai14
]
has gained popularity in the mathematical community, not just within the said theory, but also as a standalone theorem (see
[
CZ20
]
,
[
ST18
]
).

It answers the following question: Given a family of distributions
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, is it possible to find a single distribution
f
, which around any point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
locally looks like
F_{x}
? The most simple example for such a situation is given by the Young product: Given an
0<\alpha
-Hölder continuous function
g
and a distribution
h
, which is
0>\beta
-Hölder continuous, with
\alpha+\beta>0
, it is possible to define a canonical product
g\cdot h
even for non-smooth
g
. E.g.
[
BCD11
]
and
[
RS96
]
have used paraproducts to construct this product.

As it turns out, there is a clear canonical local approximation of
h\cdot g
: We can approximate
g
locally with its Taylor polynomial
T_{x}g
around any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and set
F_{x}=h\cdot T_{x}g
, as
T_{x}g
is a smooth function. The only ingredient missing is a way to reconstruct the distribution from its local approximations.

To return to the general setting, let us consider a family of distributions
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and let us formalize what it means to “locally look like
F_{x}
” around
x
: For a smooth, compactly supported mollifier
\psi
, the rescaled function
\psi_{x}^{\epsilon}
approximates a Dirac distribution
\delta_{x}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Since we formally want “
F_{x}(x)=f(x)
” to hold, we will require that
(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})
vanishes as
\epsilon\to 0
. To be precise, the rigorous condition to “locally look like
f
” is given by

\left|(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\lesssim\epsilon^{\lambda}

(1.1)

for some
\lambda>0
. For this to be possible,
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
will need to fulfill some kind of continuity assumption: For example, it suffices that
\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}\left|x-y\right|^{\gamma-\alpha}
for some
\alpha<0
, which is the condition the germs in regularity structure fulfill
[
Hai14
]
. Caravenna and Zambotti managed to find the optimal condition called coherence in
[
CZ20
]
, which ensures
(
1.1
)
and is given as follows:
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is called coherent, if

\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\alpha}

(1.2)

for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
(typically negative) and a
\gamma>0
. Under this assumption, they proved the following theorem:

Theorem 1

(
[
CZ20
]
)
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a coherent germ for some
\gamma>0
. Then there exists a unique distribution
f
with

\left|(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\lesssim\epsilon^{\gamma}.

Remark 2
.

They also showed that it is possible to find such a distribution
f
for
\gamma<0
. However, this distribution is no longer unique. Also, note that for
\gamma<0
, the bound
\epsilon^{\gamma}
does explode, so the condition is much weaker. In this article, we will concentrate on the
\gamma>0
case.

At this point, the reconstruction theorem is a purely analytic tool. This is surprising, as it is often applied in SPDEs, see for example
[
Bay+20
]
,
[
Bra19
]
,
[
CFG17
]
,
[
Hai14
]
, and many more. In all those examples, the reconstruction theorem only uses the analytic properties of the stochastic processes, neglecting their stochastic properties.

This naturally leads to the question, of whether there exists a stochastic version of the reconstruction theorem, especially if one considers its close connection to the sewing lemma
[
FL06
]
,
[
Gub04
]
: In rough paths theory, rough integrals can be constructed with the sewing lemma (see
[
FH14
]
,
[
Lyo98
]
), whereas in regularity structure theory the reconstruction theorem is used to construct the corresponding products. And in
[
Lê20
]
, Khoa Lê showed the existence of a stochastic version of the sewing lemma.

It would be interesting to generalize his techniques to the multidimensional setting of the reconstruction theorem. Let us give two possible applications such a stochastic reconstruction theorem could have:

•

In the paper
[
FHL21
]
, Peter Fritz and Khoa Lê use the stochastic sewing lemma to generate a mixed theory of rough paths and stochastic differential equations to solve equations of the form

dY_{t}=b(t,Y_{t})dt+\sigma(t,Y_{t})dB_{t}+f(t,Y_{t})d\mathbb{X}_{t}\,,

where
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion and
\mathbb{X}_{t}
is a deterministic rough path. This is outside of the scope of both Itô-calculus as well as rough path theory since the construction of the integral
\int f(t,Y_{t})d\mathbb{X}_{t}
requires one to understand the integration of a random process against a rough path. One can imagine similar problems for SPDEs, for example, one can look at the multiplicative heat equation with common noise

du-\Delta udt=f(u)d\bar{W}+g(u)dW\,,

for a two independent Brownian sheets
\bar{W}
and
W
with spatial dimension
d=1
. One usually wants to treat the common noise
dW
as a deterministic distribution, so it would make sense to treat it with the help of regularity structure theory. However, if one wants to keep
d\bar{W}
as a stochastic integral, one would need a mixed theory of SPDEs and regularity structures. While we are not yet able to present such a theory, we hope that our stochastic reconstruction theorem provides the first step toward it.

•

The stochastic sewing lemma has shown tremendously useful in analyzing regularization by noise phenomena
[
HP21
]
. In recent years, there has been a growing interest in studying such phenomenons in multi-parameter settings, e.g.
[
BHR22
]
. This is currently done with a deterministic multi-parameter sewing lemma, and the authors themselves point out that many more interesting noises like fractional Brownian sheet
[
WX07
]
will most likely require a stochastic multi-parameter technique. We conjecture that using the stochastic reconstruction theorem instead of the deterministic multi-parameter sewing lemma would allow one to extend the results of
[
BHR22
]
to more general noises.

Remark 3
.

We need to point out that since the inception of this paper’s ideas, the regularization by noise community has moved towards favoring sewing techniques over reconstruction techniques. A stochastic multi-parameter sewing lemma is being researched as we are writing this paper, and while we believe that there is plenty enough reason to research both reconstruction and sewing techniques, it is at the moment not clear if stochastic reconstruction will hold a general advantage over stochastic multi-parameter sewing in the regularization by noise field, aside from being first.

In this paper we present such a generalization of Lê’s results to the multidimensional setting of the reconstruction theorem: Consider a family of random distributions
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
with
2\leq p<\infty
(see Definition
11
), which is adapted to a filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{y})_{y\in\mathbb{R}}
(see Definition
17
). The main idea of stochastic reconstruction is to enhance the coherence property
(
1.2
)
by an additional condition on the conditional expectation of
F_{x}
as follows:

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\alpha}

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{y_{1}}}(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\tilde{\gamma}-\alpha},

where
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{d}),x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
such that
y_{1}\leq x_{1}
and
\psi
has a positive support (i.e.
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\in[0,\infty)^{d}
) and for some
\tilde{\gamma}>\gamma
, so the conditional expectation is better behaved than the germ itself. We then hope, that
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
can “borrow regularity” from its conditional expectation.

In the case of stochastic sewing, this was achieved based on a Doob-Meyer decomposition. Khoa Lê managed to borrow up to
\frac{1}{2}
regularity from the conditional expectation of the terms corresponding to
(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})
, which indicates that we should assume
\tilde{\gamma}=\gamma+\frac{1}{2}
.

The interesting result of our analysis is that in the multidimensional setting, this technique can be used repeatedly for several directions to gain a higher improvement in regularity. We thus introduce the
stochastic dimension

e\leq d
of
(F_{x})
as the number of directions, such that for suitable filtrations
\mathcal{F}_{y_{i}}^{(i)}
for
i=1,\dots,e\leq d
,

\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y_{i}}}(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\tilde{\gamma}-\alpha}\,.

In this case, we can borrow
\frac{1}{2}
regularity from all of those directions, resulting in
\gamma=\tilde{\gamma}-\frac{e}{2}
, an improvement of
\frac{e}{2}
regularity. Thus, we say that
(F_{x})
is stochastically
\gamma
-coherent, if it fulfills certain adaptedness properties (see Definition
17
for details), and for
i=1,\dots,e\leq d

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\frac{e}{2}-\alpha}

(1.3)

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y_{i}}}(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\alpha}.

(1.4)

Under this condition, we can formulate our main result:

Theorem 4

(Stochastic reconstruction)
.

Assume the adaptedness condition of Definition
17
and that
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is stochastically
\gamma
-coherent for some
\gamma>0
with stochastic dimension
e\leq d
. Then, there exists a unique random distribution
f
, which fulfills

\displaystyle\left|\left|(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{\gamma-\frac{e}{2}}

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{x_{i}}}(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{\gamma}

for
i=1,\dots,e
.

We further discuss two more variations of Theorem
4
. In the above version of the reconstruction theorem, we assume
\psi
to have positive support and
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,e
in the stochastic coherence property. This is motivated by Itô calculus, where one needs to carefully separate between information “in the future” and “in the past”. In the deterministic theory however, this is not necessary and the classical reconstruction theorem
[
CZ20
]
,
[
Hai14
]
do not have similar assumptions. We show that as long as one carefully chooses the point
y_{i}
in the conditional expectation
E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y_{i}}}
, one can get rid of the assumptions
x_{i}\leq y_{i}
and that
\psi
has a positive support in Theorem
26
.

The second variation we are discussing is reconstruction in the covariance setting: If
p=2
, we can replace the additional condition in the coherence property
(
1.4
)
with the following property:

\left|E\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})((\psi_{i})_{y_{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}})\right)\right|\lesssim\prod_{i=1}^{2}\epsilon_{i}^{\alpha}(\left|x_{i}-y_{i}\right|+\epsilon_{i})^{\gamma-\alpha}\,,

(1.5)

for points
x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|,\left|y_{1}-y_{2}\right|
are big enough (see Section
3.3
for details), two test functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
and two parameters
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
. While this condition on the covariance of
(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{y}^{\epsilon})
looks rather technical, it allows one to entirely get rid of the filtrations
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y_{i}}
! Indeed,
(
1.3
)
and
(
1.5
)
together suffice to construct a reconstruction of the germ
F_{x}
without any adaptability assumptions. To our knowledge, this is a new idea, even in the one-dimensional setting of stochastic sewing. Let us stress that this is no technical detail: The filtrations we use in this paper need to have an orthogonality condition (also often called commuting condition in the literature, see
[
Kho06
]
, chapter 11 or
[
Wal86a
]
). While this is a standard assumption in the literature of multi-parameter martingales, in practice, it can be hard to verify: For example for anisotropic Gaussian fields
X(s)

[
BE03
]
, the covariance
E(X(s)X(t))
is easy to calculate, but the filtrations generated by
(X(t))_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
are not well understood and hard to work with. We conjecture that for noises in this class, the covariance setting will prove to be a useful tool.

Perhaps an even easier example can be found in Section
7
: In this section, we use stochastic reconstruction to construct the Walsh integral
\int X(s)\xi(ds)
against white noise. To use Theorem
4
, we would need to make an unusually strong assumption on the adaptedness on
X
: We would need
X(s)
to be
\mathcal{F}_{s_{i}}^{(i)}
-measurable for all
i=1\dots,d
, while it is well known that adaptedness in time, i.e.
X(s)
is
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{s_{1}}
-measurable suffices to construct the integral. The stochastic reconstruction in the covariance setting is to our knowledge the easiest way to construct this integral with reconstruction techniques.

Remark 5
.

It should be noted that the stochastic reconstruction theorem in the covariance setting is not strictly stronger than the classical one: In the covariance setting, we make no distinction whether
(x_{1})_{i}\leq(x_{2})_{i}
or
(x_{1})_{i}\geq(x_{2})_{i}
, i.e. we do not differentiate between the “past” and “future”. For white noise, this does not matter since
\xi(1_{[s,t]})
is both independent of the past before
s
and the future after
t
. However, if one only expects an improved regularity for conditioning on the past and no improvement for the future, it is highly likely that Theorem
4
is better suited for that situation.

Our last main result reads as follows:

Theorem 6
.

Let
p=2
. Assume that
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is stochastically
\gamma
-coherent for some
\gamma>0
in the covariance setting, in the sense that
(
1.3
)
and
(
1.5
)
hold. Then there exists a unique distribution, which fulfills

\displaystyle\left|\left|(f-F_{x_{1}})(\psi_{x_{1}}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\gamma-\frac{E}{2}}

\displaystyle\left|E\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}(f-F_{x_{i}})(\psi_{x_{i}}^{\lambda})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{2\gamma}\,.

The structure of this paper

This paper is structured as follows: In Section

, we will formally introduce distribution and scalings. The proofs of our main results make heavy use of wavelet techniques as well as a BDG-type inequality, but since they are not necessary for the presentation of the results themselves, we moved them to the appendix. Both of these parts mainly follow

[

Section

W_{t}(A)

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Distributions and Hölder continuity

A property that is typical for the theory of SPDEs is that most solutions are too irregular to be viewed as functions and need to be seen as distributions. Let us quickly introduce those: Let
C_{c}^{\infty}
be the space of smooth, compactly supported test functions, and let
C_{c}^{r}
be the space of compactly supported,
r
times continuously differentiable functions. We equip those spaces with the following
C_{c}^{r}
norms: For any multiindex
k=(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})
and
k_{1}+\dots+k_{n}
times continuously differentiable function
\psi
, let
\psi^{(k)}:=\frac{\partial^{k_{1}+\dots+k_{n}}}{\partial x_{1}^{k_{1}}\dots\partial x_{d}^{k_{d}}}\psi
be a partial derivative of
\psi
. Then, for some
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we set

\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}=\sum_{k_{1}+\dots+k_{d}\leq r}\left|\left|\psi^{(k)}\right|\right|_{\infty}.

A distribution on this space is defined as follows:

Definition 7
.

For any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, a
distribution
(of order at most
r
) is a linear map
f:C_{c}^{r}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that for any compact set
K
there is a constant
C(K)
, such that for any
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
with support in
K
, it holds that

\left|f(\psi)\right|\leq C(K)\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}.

Further, for any
p<\infty
, we call a linear map
f:C_{c}^{r}\to L_{p}(\Omega)
a
random distribution
, if it fulfills

\left|\left|f(\psi)\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\leq C(K)\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}.

We use the notation
\left|f(\psi)\right|\lesssim\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}
, where we allow constants hidden in
\lesssim
to depend on underlying compact sets.

We need to extend the notion of Hölder continuity to distributions. This requires us to measure the local behavior of distributions, which can be achieved with the help of localizations of test functions. To this end, we call the family of maps
(S^{\lambda})_{\lambda\geq 0}
a
scaling
associated to the vector
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
s_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,d
, if it is given by

\displaystyle S^{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})

\displaystyle\mapsto(\lambda^{s_{1}}x_{1},\dots,\lambda^{s_{d}}x_{d}).

The rank of
S
is given by
\left|S\right|=\left|s\right|:=s_{1}+\dots+s_{d}
. If
\mathbb{R}^{d}
has a scaling
S
assigned to it, we also equip the space with the seminorm

\left|x\right|=\left|x_{1}\right|^{\frac{1}{s_{1}}}+\dots+\left|x_{d}\right|^{\frac{1}{s_{d}}},

which is often referred to as a homogeneous norm, although it is not a norm in the strict sense. Note that it has the property, that
\left|S^{\lambda}x\right|=\lambda\left|x\right|
.

To give two short examples, the canonical scaling
x\mapsto\lambda x
is simply given by the map
S
associated to
s=(1,\dots,1)
, while the parabolic scaling is associated to
s=(2,1,\dots,1)
.

With this in mind, we can now take a look at the localization of a test function: Let
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
be a test function,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then, the
localized
test function
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
(under the scaling
S
) is given by

\psi_{x}^{\lambda}(y):=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\left|S\right|}}\psi\left(S^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}(y-x)\right).

In the case where
S
is the canonical scaling associated to
(1,\dots,1)
, this is simply given by the classical notion of localized test functions:

\psi_{x}^{\lambda}(y):=\frac{1}{\lambda^{d}}\psi\left(\frac{y-x}{\lambda}\right).

The localization has the following properties:

•

Assume
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset[-C,C]^{d}
. Then the support of
\psi^{\lambda}_{x}
lies in
[x-\lambda^{s_{1}}C,x+\lambda^{s_{1}}C]\times\dots\times[x-\lambda^{s_{d}}C,x+\lambda^{s_{d}}C]
, and

•

it holds that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\psi(y)\right|dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\psi_{x}^{\lambda}(y)\right|dy
.

These properties justify thinking of
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
as the density of a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is highly concentrated around
x
for small
\lambda
. Indeed, as
\lambda\to 0
, this converges to the Dirac measure
\delta_{x}
: For all continuous functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

\langle f,\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle\xrightarrow{\lambda\to 0}f(x),

(2.1)

where
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(x)dx
is simply the
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
scalar product.

For a true distribution
g
(i.e. there is no function
\tilde{g}
, such that
g(\psi)=\langle\tilde{g},\psi\rangle
for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
),
g(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
is bound to diverge as
\lambda\to 0
. (If it were to converge, we could set
\tilde{g}(x)=g(\delta_{x})=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}g(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
) The speed of divergence is going to give us a way to define the regularity of
g
, which will help us to define Hölder spaces of negative regularity.

Let us first introduce Hölder continuity for positive regularities: For an
\alpha\in(0,1]
, and a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

C^{\alpha}(K):=\{f\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\penalty\ |\penalty\ \exists C>0\forall x,y\in K:\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|\leq C\left|x-y\right|^{\alpha}\},

and we call
C^{\alpha}:=\bigcap_{K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}}C^{\alpha}(K)
the set of (locally)
\alpha
-Hölder continuous functions, where the intersection is taken over all compact sets
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We also refer to these spaces simply as the space of Hölder-continuous functions (and drop the “locally”), as every function and distribution in this paper will be only locally Hölder continuous. In consistence with the
\lesssim
notation, we write
\left|f(x)-f(y)\right|\lesssim\left|x-y\right|^{\alpha}
for
f\in C^{\alpha}
.

For random processes, we define the space of
\alpha
-Hölder continuous processes by

C^{\alpha}(L_{p}(\Omega),K):=\{X:K\to L_{P}(\Omega)\penalty\ |\penalty\ \left|\left|X(x)-X(y)\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\left|x-y\right|^{\alpha}\}\,,

where
C^{\alpha}(L_{p})
is defined similarly to before.

As mentioned above, for
\alpha<0
, we measure the Hölder regularity of a distribution
f
by measuring the speed of divergence of
f(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
for
\lambda\to 0
. More specifically, we define the space
C^{\alpha}
for negative
\alpha
as follows:

Definition 8
.

Let
\alpha<0
, and
r\in\mathbb{N}
with
r-1\leq\left|\alpha\right|<r
. A distribution
f:C_{c}^{r}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is in the space
C^{\alpha}
, if for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is some constant
C(K)
, such that for every distribution
\psi
with
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}=1
and compact support in
[-1,1]^{d}
and for all
x\in K
the following holds:

\left|f(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|\leq C(K)\lambda^{\alpha}.

(2.2)

For a random distribution, we say that
f\in C^{\alpha}(L_{p}(\Omega))
, if it holds that

\left|\left|f(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\leq C(K)\lambda^{\alpha}.

(2.3)

Remark 9
.

Note that any random
C_{c}^{r}
-distribution is in
C^{-\left|S\right|-r}(L_{p})
, since
\left|\left|\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}\lesssim\lambda^{-\left|S\right|-r}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}
.

Remark 10
.

In general, for a random distribution
f
and any
\omega\in\Omega
,
\psi\mapsto f(\psi)(\omega)
is not a distribution. However, it is possible to show a version of Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem for negative Hölder spaces: Given an
f\in C^{\alpha}(L_{p})
, it holds that for almost all
\omega\in\Omega
,
f(\cdot,\omega)\in C^{\hat{\alpha}}(L_{p})
for a small enough
\hat{\alpha}<\alpha
. By the previous remark, it follows that any random distribution
f
is a.s. a deterministic distribution on some space
C_{c}^{\hat{r}}
for a large enough
\hat{r}
. A detailed statement of this can be found in the appendix, Lemma
64
.

3
Main results

In this section, we are going to present our main result, the stochastic reconstruction theorem, as well as two variations of it. Since the proofs are rather technical, we postpone them to a separate section.

3.1
Stochastic Reconstruction

Let us start by rigorously defining the notion of random germ:

Definition 11
.

We call
(F_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
a
random germ
, if for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
F_{z}:C_{c}^{r}\to L_{p}(\Omega)
is a continuous, linear map for some fixed number
1\leq p<\infty
.

Recall that Martin Hairer showed in
[
Hai14
]
that the reconstructing sequence

f_{n}(\psi):=\sum_{y\in\Delta_{n}}F_{y}(\phi_{y}^{n})\langle\phi_{y}^{n},\psi\rangle

(3.1)

for a wavelet function
\phi
(see the appendix for details) converges to a limiting distribution, whenever the germ
(F_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
fulfills a property called
\gamma
-coherence for a
\gamma>0
. (Note that Hairer did not namely mention the coherence property. It was introduced by Caravenna and Zambotti in
[
CZ20
]
. However, Hairer’s proof works for
(
3.1
)
under the coherence assumption of Caravenna-Zambotti.)

If the germ
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is a random germ, it is very reasonable to assume random cancellations in the above sum and thus convergence for certain
\gamma<0
, if the germ has some form of martingale properties. As a motivation, we want to start with a short example: Consider space-time white noise
\xi(t,x)
(see Section
7
for a formal introduction) in
1+1
dimension and an
\alpha
-Hölder continuous deterministic function
X\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for some
\alpha>0
. We show in Section
7
that, for the germ
(F_{(t,x)})_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
defined by

F_{(t,x)}(\psi):=X(t,x)\xi(\psi)=X(t,x)\int\int\psi(s,y)\xi(s,y)dsdy,

(3.2)

the reconstructing sequence
f_{n}(\psi)
converges in
L_{p}(\Omega)
for
2\leq p<\infty
to

f_{n}(\psi)\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int\int X(s,y)\psi(s,y)\xi(s,y)dsdy,

where the right-hand side is a Walsh-type integral against white noise, see
[
Wal86
]
for reference. It is also not hard to see, that
F_{(t,x)}
is only
(-1+\alpha)^{-}
-coherent (which reflects
\xi
having regularity
(-1)^{-}
, if we use the canonical scaling
s=(1,1)
, and
X
having regularity
\alpha
). So this example shows a reconstruction with a
\gamma>-1
, an improvement of
+1
over classic reconstruction. If one compares this to the stochastic sewing lemma in which one usually gets an improvement of
+\frac{1}{2}
, it seems as if one can apply the sewing lemma twice in the above construction. This makes a lot of sense, as the white noise has martingale properties in both space and time directions.

However, since we also wish to cover more general noises, which might not have these properties in all directions (e.g. white noise in time, colored noise in space), we will split our dimensions
1,\dots,d
into two types of directions: Those, in which we have certain stochastic properties, and those, in which we lack such properties. We call the number of directions, which have such properties the
stochastic dimension
of the germ.

Before we formally introduce this concept, let us make two quick remarks:

Remark 12
.

The above construction also works for a stochastic process
X(t,x)
, which is adapted to the sigma-algebra
\mathcal{F}_{t}
defined in
(
3.4
)
, i.e.
X(t,x)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
measurable for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous in the following sense:

\left|\left|X(t,x)-X(s,y)\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\left|(t,x)-(s,y)\right|^{\alpha}.

Remark 13
.

It should be noted that for the above germ, Caravenna-Zambotti’s reconstructing sequence given by

f_{n}(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{z}(\rho_{z}^{\epsilon_{n}})\psi(z)dz

(3.3)

converges to the same limit for deterministic functions
X
. To see this, note that dominated convergence and the Itô-isometry (
[
Qua12
]
, equation (121)) imply
f_{n}(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\rho^{\epsilon_{n}}(-\cdot)*(X\psi))(y)\xi(y)dy
. One then shows
\rho^{\epsilon_{n}}(-\cdot)*(X\psi)\xrightarrow{\epsilon_{n}\to 0}X\psi
in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Using again the Itô-isometry, we get that
f_{n}(\psi)
converges to
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}X(y)\psi(y)\xi(y)dy
in
L_{2}
.

Because of this, we suspect that the main result of this paper can also be achieved using their mollification techniques.

Let us fix the stochastic setting for reconstruction: Using our example above as a motivation, we see that
(F_{(t,x)})_{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}}
is a random germ over the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
, where
\mathcal{F}=\sigma(\xi(\psi)|\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
is the sigma-algebra generated by white noise. This sigma-algebra comes with a natural filtration, which one can write informally as “
\mathcal{F}_{t}=\{\xi(s,x)|s\leq t\}
”, but is more formally given by

\mathcal{F}_{t}=\sigma(\xi(\psi)|\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset(-\infty,t]\times\mathbb{R}).

(3.4)

Let now
t\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi
with a support in
(-\infty,s]\times\mathbb{R}
be fixed. It then holds that
\xi(\psi)
is
\mathcal{F}_{s}
-measurable and we assume that
X(\cdot,x)
is adapted to
(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
, i.e.
X(t,x)
is
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. It immediately follows, that
F_{t,x}(\psi)=X(t,x)\xi(\psi)
is
\mathcal{F}_{\max(s,t)}
-measurable.

Of course, a similar construction can be made in the spatial direction
x
with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{x}^{\text{spatial}}:=\sigma(\xi(\psi)|\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}\times(-\infty,x])
. This gives us two different filtrations, and our germ is adapted to both in the sense that for
t,x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi
with support in
(-\infty,s]\times(-\infty,y]
,
F_{t,x}(\psi)
is
\mathcal{F}_{\max(s,t)}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\max(x,y)}^{\text{spatial}}
-measurable, as long as
X
is adapted to both filtrations.

We want to generalize this adaptedness property: Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a complete probability space. As in the example above, consider
e
many filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(i)})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
,
i=1,\dots,e
. We make the following assumptions about these filtrations:

•

Completeness:
We assume, that for each
i=1,\dots,e
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{t}
is complete.

•

Right-continuity:
We assume, that for
i=1,\dots,e
,
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(i)})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
is a right continuous filtration.

•

Orthogonality:
We assume that conditioning in one direction does not change measurability along the other directions. More precisely, let
X
be a
\mathcal{F}_{z_{i}}^{(i)}
-measurable random variable for some
1\leq i\leq e
and
z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. We assume that
E^{\mathcal{F}_{z_{j}}^{(j)}}X
is still
\mathcal{F}_{z_{i}}^{(i)}
-measurable for all
1\leq j\leq e,j\neq i
and all
z_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us give a short intuition, of what we need each of these properties for. The reconstructing sequence
(
3.1
)
will be a limit in
L_{p}
, so completeness is necessary to ensure that the limit, as well as modifications of the limit, are still
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{(i)}
measurable for all
i
and large enough
t
. A closer look at the reconstructing sequence reveals that
f_{n}(\psi)
is only
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y_{i}+\epsilon_{n}}
-measurable for
\psi
with support in
(-\infty,y_{1}]\times\dots\times(-\infty,y_{d}]
and a certain sequence
\epsilon_{n}\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0
. Right continuity then assures that the limit is
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y_{i}}
-measurable.

Orthogonality is used more subtly: The proof requires us to use the BDG-type inequality
(
B.1
)
inductively over all stochastic directions. However, without any assumption on how the filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}^{(i)})_{x_{i}}
,
i=1,\dots,e
interact with each other,
E^{\mathcal{F}_{x_{i}}^{(i)}}F_{x}(\psi)
will not have any adaptedness property with regards to the filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{x_{j}}^{(j)})_{x_{j}}
for
i\neq j
. So orthogonality ensures that we can use
(
B.1
)
several times with respect to different filtrations.

It should also be noted that if
e=1
, orthogonality is automatically fulfilled as one only has one filtration.

Remark 14
.

Another way of formulating orthogonality is requiring that conditioning on
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}
and
\mathcal{F}^{(j)}
commutes, i.e. for all
1\leq i,j\leq e
with
i\neq j
,
z_{i},z_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and for a random variable
X
, it holds that
E^{\mathcal{F}_{s_{j}}^{(j)}}E^{\mathcal{F}_{s_{i}}^{(i)}}X=E^{\mathcal{F}_{s_{i}}^{(i)}}E^{\mathcal{F}_{s_{j}}^{(j)}}X
.

Remark 15
.

This setting can be seen as a generalization of the setting used in
[
Imk85
]
,
[
Wal86a
]
to construct multiparameter martingales: The authors start from a multiparameter filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
and constructs

\mathcal{F}_{y}^{(i)}:=\sigma\left(\bigcup_{x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\dots,x_{d}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\dots,x_{d})}\right)\,.

In this case, orthogonality is equivalent to conditional independence of
\mathcal{F}_{y}^{(i)}
,
i=1\dots,d
, see hypothesis (F4) of
[
Wal86a
]
on page 348. We prefer to directly work with the filtrations
(\mathcal{F}_{x}^{(i)})
, as our proofs do not use any additional structure of the multiparameter setting.

Remark 16
.

We want to mention that the condition of orthogonality does not come for free: While it is a standard assumption in multiparameter martingale theory, e.g. the noises constructed in
[
BE03
]
do not generate such filtrations. For a rule of thumb, Brownian sheet and deterministic kernel integrated over Brownian sheet
B^{H}_{t}:=\int_{(-\infty,t]^{d}}K(t,s)dB_{s}
like fractional Brownian sheet (see
[
WX07
]
for the definition of
B^{H}
in dimension
d>1
and
[
Pic11
]
for the kernel
K
) will be adapted to orthogonal filtrations, while more general Gaussian noises like the spectral density construction of
[
BE03
]
will not generate such filtrations. Theorem
30
is better suited to deal with those cases.

We denote by
\pi_{i}
the projection onto the
i
-th coordinate. With this notation, we can now define the term stochastic dimension:

Definition 17
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a random germ in
d
dimensions, and let
e\leq d
. Let
(\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{z})_{z\in\mathbb{R}},i=1,\dots,e
be filtrations of some
\sigma
-Algebra
\mathcal{F}
, which fulfill the above three properties of completeness, right-continuity, and orthogonality.

We say that
(F_{x})
is of
stochastic dimension

e
, if for
i=1,\dots,e
:

For any test function
\psi
with support
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{i-1}\times(-\infty,y]\times\mathbb{R}^{d-i}
, it holds that

F_{x}(\psi)\text{ is }\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\max(\pi_{i}(x),y)}\text{-measurable}.

(3.5)

We further say that a random distribution
f
has stochastic dimension
e
, if for any test function
\psi
with support
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{i-1}\times(-\infty,y]\times\mathbb{R}^{d-i}

f(\psi)\text{ is }\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{y}\text{-measurable}.

Remark 18
.

As most readers have undoubtedly noticed, we use a two-directional setting in the sense that we always use
t\in\mathbb{R}
instead of
t\in[0,\infty)
. In one dimension, our example
(
3.2
)
becomes a two-sided Brownian motion, not a classical one.

Since this whole theory highly depends on recentering and scaling operations, the origin point does not have any special role, which makes this two-directional setting more natural. However, one can use the locality of this theory to achieve a one-sided setting, by restricting oneself to compact sets
K\subset[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
in Definition
19
and Theorem
22
.

Since we work with a scaling
S
associated to some
s\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
, we do not consider
e
to be the number of stochastic dimensions, but rather consider

E:=\sum_{i=1}^{e}s_{i}\leq\left|S\right|.

With the definition of the stochastic dimension in mind, we can now formulate a stochastic version of the coherence property. Since we aim to get
L_{p}
-convergence, let us fix now some
2\leq p<\infty
and define stochastic coherence as follows:

Definition 19
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a random germ with stochastic dimension
e\leq d
and let
E
be as above. We call
(F_{x})

stochastically
\gamma
-coherent
, if there is an
\alpha\leq\gamma
, such that the following holds for all test functions
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}=1
and
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset[0,\tilde{R}]^{e}\times[-\tilde{R},\tilde{R}]^{d-e}
for some
\tilde{R}>0
, for the stochastic directions
i=1,\dots,e
, for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for any
x,y\in K
with
\pi_{i}(x)\leq\pi_{i}(y)
:

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(x)}}(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{y}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\alpha}

(3.6)

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{y}^{\epsilon})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\epsilon^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\epsilon)^{\gamma-\frac{E}{2}-\alpha},

(3.7)

where the constant appearing in
\lesssim
is allowed to depend on the compact set
K
and the radius
\tilde{R}
.

We sometimes write
(\gamma,\alpha)-coherent
, if we need the explicit value of
\alpha
. Let us stress that the constant appearing in
\lesssim
(both in the above definition and the results throughout this paper) should not depend on
\psi
, as long as
\tilde{R}
and
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}
are fixed.

Remark 20
.

In the usual construction of stochastic integrals, it is far more natural to use left-side approximations than two-sided ones. Thus,
(
3.2
)
is only a useful approximation, if the support of
\psi
is in
[t,\infty)\times[x,\infty)
. We therefore only consider test functions with positive support in the stochastic directions in the definition above and Theorem
22
. It is however possible to get a two-sided stochastic reconstruction, which will be discussed in Section
3.2
.

Remark 21
.

It is possible to allow
\alpha=\alpha(K)
to depend on the compact set
K
. In this case, one simply has to replace
\alpha
with
\alpha(\tilde{K})
in the proofs, for some fattening
\tilde{K}
of
K
which depends on the support of the wavelet.

Note that each stochastic dimension reduces the coherence required by
(F_{x})
in
(
3.7
)
by
\frac{1}{2}
, resulting in a total reduction of
\frac{E}{2}
, under the corresponding scaling. In particular, if the scaling is just the canonical scaling
s=(1,\dots,1)
, we get a reduction by
\frac{e}{2}
. Further note that the support of
\psi_{y}^{\epsilon}
lies completely on the right-hand side of the time we condition on,
\pi_{i}(x)
, with respect to the direction
i
.

Recall that a random distribution
\tilde{f}
is a modification of
f
, if for all test functions
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
,
f(\psi)=\tilde{f}(\psi)
almost surely. With this in mind, we can formulate our main result:

Theorem 22

(Stochastic Reconstruction)
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a random germ for some
2\leq p<\infty
with stochastic dimension
e\leq d
, which is stochastically
(\gamma,\alpha)
-coherent for some
\gamma>0
and
\alpha<0
. Set
\tilde{r}:=r\cdot\min_{i=1,\dots,d}s_{i}
. Assume that our parameters fulfill
\alpha+\tilde{r}>0
and
\gamma-\frac{E}{2}+\tilde{r}>0
.

Then, there is a unique random distribution
f
(up to modifications) with stochastic dimension
e
with regard to the same filtrations
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}
as our germ, such that the following holds for
i=1,\dots,e
, any test function
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
with
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}=1
and whose support is in
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset[\tilde{C},\tilde{R}]^{e}\times[-\tilde{R},\tilde{R}]^{d-e}
for some
0\leq\tilde{C}\leq\tilde{R}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
:

\displaystyle\left|\left|(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\gamma-\frac{E}{2}}

(3.8)

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(x)}}(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\gamma},

(3.9)

where the constant in
\lesssim
again depends on the compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x\in K
and the constants
\tilde{C},\tilde{R}
.

Note that the support of
\psi
does have to be strictly positive. This is necessary to “fit in” a wavelet between the zero and the support of
\psi
. If we set
\tilde{C}=2R
(recall that the support of our wavelet is in
[C,R]^{d}
), the constant in
\lesssim
generated by
\tilde{C}
disappears.

Remark 23
.

The restrictions to
\psi
might seem strange to the reader. Indeed, it seems that this theorem only makes a statement about test functions with (strictly) positive supports in the stochastic directions. This is not true since the localizations
\psi_{y}^{\epsilon}
can have negative supports in all directions depending on
y
. The support does only serve to separate the point of localization of
\psi
and the index of the germ
F_{y}
. Another way to formulate the result is to use a
\psi
with any compact support, and take a look at
F_{(y-S^{\lambda}C)}(\psi_{y}^{\lambda})
for a certain vector
C
depending on the support of
\psi
and which directions are supposed to be stochastic.

Remark 24
.

If one is given a germ
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
such that
\alpha+\tilde{r}
or
\gamma-\frac{E}{2}+\tilde{r}
is smaller than
0
, one can always restrict
F_{x}
to a space
C_{c}^{\hat{r}}
for a large enough
\hat{r}
to fulfill the conditions on the parameters.

Before we prove this theorem, let us discuss two modifications of it:

3.2
Two-sided Reconstruction

The theorem we presented above is modeled after left-sided approximations of stochastic integrals. However, it is also possible (and closer to the original reconstruction Theorem) to have a two-sided reconstruction, i.e. to use test functions
\psi
with support in
[-\tilde{R},\tilde{R}]^{d}
for some
\tilde{R}>0
. In this setting, it no longer makes sense to condition
F_{x}(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
onto
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(x)}
, since the conditioning would not have any effect on test functions with purely negative support. Instead, we are going to condition it on the left boundary of the support of
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
, i.e. onto
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(x)-\lambda^{s_{i}}\tilde{R}}
.

Definition 25

(two-sided stochastic coherence)
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a stochastic germ with stochastic dimension
e\leq d
. We call
(F_{x})

two-sided stochastically
\gamma
-coherent
, if there is an
\alpha<\gamma
, such that the following holds for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
with
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}=1
and support in
[-\tilde{R},\tilde{R}]^{d}
for some
\tilde{R}>0
, for the stochastic directions
i=1,\dots,e
, for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for any
x,y\in K
:

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(y)-\lambda^{s_{i}}\tilde{R}}}(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{y}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\lambda)^{\gamma-\alpha}

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{y}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\alpha}(\left|x-y\right|+\lambda)^{\gamma-\frac{E}{2}-\alpha},

where the constant in
\lesssim
is allowed to depend on the compact set
K
and
\tilde{R}
.

Note that this definition also does not have the condition, that
\pi_{i}(x)\leq\pi_{i}(y)
, but rather uses arbitrary
x,y
. We want to add that there is no cheap way to get from the one-sided coherence property to the two-sided, or vice versa: If one replaces
\psi
with a shifted function
\psi_{\vec{r}}
to get a test function with a purely positive support, and substitute
y
with
\tilde{y}=y-S^{\lambda}\vec{r}
, such that
\psi_{y}^{\lambda}=(\psi_{\vec{r}})_{\tilde{y}}^{\lambda}
, one gets
F_{\tilde{y}+S^{\lambda}\vec{r}}
instead of
F_{\tilde{y}}
. This highlights the main difference between the two versions: In the one-sided reconstruction,
F_{y}
only gets tested against the future, while in the two-sided version,
F_{y}
sits “in the middle” of the support of the test function. This makes the two-sided version closely connected to Stratonovich integration. Since we are interested in the martingale properties, the one-sided version (which is closely connected to Itô integration) is more natural.

Using this property, one can show the following theorem:

Theorem 26

(two sided stochastic reconstruction)
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a random germ for some
2\leq p<\infty
with stochastic dimension
e\leq d
, which is two-sided stochastically
(\gamma,\alpha)
-coherent for some
\gamma>0
,
\alpha<0
. Let
\tilde{r}:=r\cdot\min_{i=1,\dots,d}(s_{i})
and assume
\alpha+\tilde{r}>0
and
\gamma-\frac{E}{2}+\tilde{r}>0
.

Then, where is a unique (up to modifications) random distribution
f
with stochastic dimension
e
with respect to the same filtrations as the germ
(F_{x})
, such that the following holds for
i=1,\dots,e
and any test function
\psi
with support in
[-\tilde{R},\tilde{R}]^{d}
for some
\tilde{R}>0
and
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}=1
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
:

\displaystyle\left|\left|(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\gamma-\frac{E}{2}}

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\check{S}_{i}(x)}}(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{\gamma},

where
\check{S}_{i}(x):=\pi_{i}(x)-\lambda^{s_{i}}(\tilde{R}+\tilde{C})
for some
\tilde{C}>0
, and the constant in
\lesssim
is allowed to depend on the compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
x\in K
and on
\tilde{C},\tilde{R}
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as for the one-sided version. However, one can use the more usual wavelets with supports fulfilling
\mathrm{supp}(\phi),\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\phi})\subset[-R,R]^{d}
.
∎

Remark 27
.

Note that this theorem has a similar fattening happening in the uniqueness-property: We do not condition on the left boundary
\pi_{i}(x)-\lambda^{s_{i}}\tilde{R}
, but we leave a “gap”, in which enough wavelets fit. If one sets
\tilde{C}=4R
, then
\tilde{C}
does not contribute a constant in the above inequalities.

3.3
The covariance setting in
L_{2}

Theorem
22
contains an assumption, which might be stronger than one would expect at first: Namely that
F_{x}(\psi)
is
\mathcal{F}_{\max(\pi_{i}(x),y)}^{(i)}
measurable for
i=1,\dots,d
for test functions
\psi
with the respective support, for a set of filtrations which have the orthogonality property. As mentioned in Remark
16
, this adaptedness condition does not come for free. Furthermore, one can find several interesting examples, in which stochastic integrals have been constructed, even if one does violate this property:

•

In the example
(
3.2
)
, we have to assume that
X
is adapted to all
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{x}
in the above mentioned way. However, it is well known that the construction of the Walsh integral should work for all processes
X
, which are adapted to the time-filtration
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{t}

[
Wal86
]
. A more throughout discussion of this can be found in Section

.

•

More generally, Malliavin calculus
[
Nua06
]
successfully constructs the Skorohod integral over white noise, even for non-adapted processes
X
.

•

The technique used in
[
HP21
]
to analyze regularization by noise only uses local non-determinism properties of the underlying noise for their construction, which only need the conditional covariances
\mathrm{cov}(X(t)\penalty\ |\penalty\ X(s_{1}),\dots,X(s_{n}))
to be known for a Gaussian noise
X
. These techniques should be possible for certain noises defined through their spectral densities
[
BE03
]
, even if they do not generate orthogonal filtrations.

All of the above examples can circumvent the use of conditional expectation by using the covariances of the underlying Gaussian processes in some way. This motivated us to consider the case, in which the germ
F_{x}(\psi)
is not adapted to orthogonal filtrations, but we instead know its covariance
E(F_{x}(\psi_{1})F_{y}(\psi_{2}))
.

Let us take a look at how the conditional expectation is used in the proof of Theorem
22
: We use it to establish an inequality of the form
\left|\left|\sum_{x\in\Delta_{n}}g_{x}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\left(\sum_{x\in\Delta_{n}}\left|\left|g_{x}\right|\right|^{2}_{L_{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
plus some remainder (see estimate
(
4.11
)
) for a technical random term
g_{x}
defined in
(
4.10
)
. The remainder gets controlled by the additional property
(
3.6
)
of the stochastic coherence property, which allows us to get a better bound for
\left|\left|\sum_{x\in\Delta_{n}}g_{x}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}
than simply pulling the norm into the sum.

If
p=2
, there is no need for such a bound, as one can explicitly calculate
\left|\left|\sum_{x\in\Delta_{n}}g_{x}\right|\right|_{L_{2}}^{2}=\sum_{x\in\Delta_{n}}\left|\left|g_{x}\right|\right|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\sum_{x\neq y}E(g_{x}g_{y})
. So what we need is an upper bound on
\sum_{x\neq y}E(g_{x}g_{y})
to replace the stochastic machinery used in Theorem
22
. So we need an additional property that allows us to bound
E(g_{x}g_{y})
for
x\neq y
.

In practice, this should be a bound on the term
\Pi_{i=1}^{2}(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})((\psi_{i})_{y_{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}})
for
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
“far enough” from each other, and two test functions
\psi_{1},\psi_{2}
in
C_{c}^{r}
. To quantify how far apart
(x_{1},y_{1})
and
(x_{2},y_{2})
need to be, we introduce the following concept:

Definition 28
.

We call the following intervals effective supports of the corresponding expressions:

•

For
(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi)
, the effective support is the smallest possible intervall
[a,b]:=[\pi_{1}(a),\pi_{1}(b)]\times\dots\times[\pi_{d}(a),\pi_{d}(b)]\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
x,y\in[a,b]
and
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset[a,b]
.

•

Given the reconstruction
f
of Theorem
(
30
)
, and a test function
\psi
with support in
[0,\tilde{R}]^{d}
, we say that the effective support of
(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
is given by
[\pi_{1}(x),\pi_{1}(x)+2\lambda^{s_{1}}\tilde{R}]\times\dots\times[\pi_{d}(x),\pi_{d}(x)+2\lambda^{s_{d}}\tilde{R}]
.

Furthermore, given an
e\leq d
, we call
[a,b]_{e}:=[\pi_{1}(a),\pi_{1}(b)]\times\dots\times[\pi_{e}(a),\pi_{e}(b)]
as well as
[\pi_{1}(x),\pi_{1}(x)+2\lambda^{s_{1}}\tilde{R}]\times\dots\times[\pi_{e}(x),\pi_{e}(x)+2\lambda^{s_{e}}\tilde{R}]
the stochastic effective support of the respective expressions.

Note that the effective support of
(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi)
is precisely the smallest interval that the expression sees. For
(f-F_{x})(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
however,
x
and
\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})
are contained in the smaller interval
[\pi_{1}(x),\pi_{1}(x)+\lambda^{s_{1}}\tilde{R}]\times\dots\times[\pi_{d}(x),\pi_{d}(x)+\lambda^{s_{d}}\tilde{R}]
without the factor
2
. The reason we use this factor is that the reconstruction theorem causes a “fattening” effect, where we need some extra distance between two test functions to put in another wavelet. The easiest way to do this is by extending the effective support to be slightly bigger. Instead of the factor
2
, one can use any factor
\eta\geq 1
, at the cost that the constants appearing in
\lesssim
will depend on
\eta
.

With these definitions, we can now give a precise meaning to points
(x_{1},y_{1})

(x_{2},y_{2})
being “far enough” from each other. We want them to be so far apart, that the (stochastic) effective supports of
(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})(\psi_{y_{i}}^{\epsilon_{i}})
are disjoint. This is motivated by two observations:

1.

If we think of the example of reconstructing the Walsh integral against white noise
(
3.2
)
, we have that white noise
\xi(\psi)
is independent to
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{x_{i}}
if
\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{i-1}\times[x_{i},\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d-i+1}
, and has expected value
E(\xi(\psi))=0
. This implies that
\left|E\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})(\psi_{i})\right)\right|=0
, where
F_{x}(\psi)=X(x)\xi(\psi)
and whenever the effective stochastic supports of this terms are disjoint. So in this example, disjoint effective stochastic supports give us a true improvement in the regularity over simply applying Hölder’s inequality to the product.

2.

Let
x_{i},y_{i},\psi_{i},i=1,2
be such that the effective stochastic supports of
(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})(\psi_{i})
are disjoint and assume w.l.o.g.
\pi_{1}(x_{1})\leq\pi_{1}(x_{2})
. Then the disjoint effective stochastic supports imply that
\pi_{1}(y_{1})\leq\pi_{1}(x_{2})
and
\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{1})\subset(-\infty,\pi_{1}(x_{2})]
which shows that
(F_{x_{1}}-F_{y_{1}})(\psi_{1})
is
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\pi_{1}(x_{2})}
-measurable. It follows that

\displaystyle\left|E\left(\prod_{i=1}^{2}(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})(\psi_{i})\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|E\left((F_{x_{1}}-F_{y_{1}})(\psi_{1})E^{\mathcal{F}_{\pi_{1}(x_{2})}^{(1)}}(F_{x_{2}}-F_{y_{2}})(\psi_{2})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left|(F_{x_{1}}-F_{y_{1}})(\psi_{1})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{\pi_{1}(x_{2})}^{(1)}}(F_{x_{2}}-F_{y_{2}})(\psi_{2})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\,.

So if we assume that inequality
(
3.6
)
from the classical setting holds, disjoint effective stochastic supports also give us better bounds on
E\left(\left|\prod_{i=1}^{2}(F_{x_{i}}-F_{y_{i}})(\psi_{i})\right|\right)
than simply applying Hölder’s inequality.

Let us formally write down the coherence property in this setting:

Definition 29

(stochastic coherence in the covariance setting)
.

Let
(F_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a stochastic germ with
p=2
and let
e\leq d
. We call
(F_{x})
stochastically

and

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{x_{1}}-F_{y_{1}})((\psi_{1})_{y_{1}}^{\epsilon_{1}})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

We want to stress that this condition is not a generalization of

5
Stochastic reconstruction is stochastic sewing in 1 dimension

As we discussed in the introduction, sewing and reconstruction are closely related to one another. It is well known
[
BZ22
]
,
[
Zor22
]
, that for a two-parameter process
(A(s,t))_{s,t\in[0,T]}
which fulfills the conditions of the classical sewing lemma, the germ given by

F_{s}(t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}A(s,t)

fulfills the conditions of the reconstruction theorem (as formulated in
[
CZ20
]
). Here,
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
should be seen as a distributional derivative, i.e.

F_{s}(\psi)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(s,t)\psi^{\prime}(t)dt

for any
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
.

Remark 42
.

We should note that the classical sewing lemma defines
A(s,t)
only on
\Delta:=\{(s,t)\in[0,T]^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ s\leq t\}
and assumes

for all
s
. In this case, we can extend
A
anti-symmetrically by setting
A(t,s):=-A(s,t)
for all
s\leq t
.

Using the above germ
(F_{s})_{s\in[0,T]}
and under the assumptions of the sewing lemma, it holds that
(F_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
can be reconstructed to get a distribution
f
and that

is the distributional derivative of the process

gained from the sewing lemma.

Thus, sewing can indeed be seen as the one-dimensional case of reconstruction. As a result of
[
Bra19
]
, Lemma 3.10, we can reverse the time derivative and regain

as “
f(1_{[0,t]})
”, which is of course only rigorous as the function

from
[
Bra19
]
, Lemma 3.10. This allows one to construct the process

from the reconstruction
f
.

Given all this, it should come as little surprise, that the statement stays true in the stochastic case: indeed, the stochastic reconstruction theorem is nothing else than the distributional version of Khoa Lê’s stochastic sewing Lemma in one dimension. We recall said stochastic sewing lemma
[
Lê20
]
:

Theorem 43

(stochastic sewing lemma)
.

Let
2\leq p<\infty
, and let
(A(s,t))_{s,t\in[0,T]}
be a two parameter process in
L_{p}(\Omega)
, which is adapted to some complete, right continuous filtration
\mathcal{F}_{t}
in the sense, that
A(s,t)\in\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
. Suppose that there is a
\gamma>0
, such that for all
0\leq s\leq u\leq t\leq T
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\left|A(s,t)-A(s,u)-A(u,t)\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|t-s\right|^{\frac{1}{2}+\gamma}

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(A(s,t)-A(s,u)-A(u,t))\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|t-s\right|^{1+\gamma}.

Then, there is a unique (up to modifications) process
(I(t))_{t\in[0,T]}
in
L_{p}(\Omega)
, which is adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{t}
, has
I(0)=0
and fulfills for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T

\displaystyle\left|\left|I(t)-I(s)-A(s,t)\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|t-s\right|^{\frac{1}{2}+\gamma}

\displaystyle\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(I(t)-I(s)-A(s,t))\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|t-s\right|^{1+\gamma}.

Note that we can easily extend
A(s,t)
to be a process over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, by setting
A(s,t)=A(0,t)
for
s\leq 0,t\in[0,T]
and
A(s,t)=A(s,T)
for
t\geq T,s\in[0,T]
. Should both parameter
s,t\notin[0,T]
, we simply set
A(s,t)=A(0,T)
if
s\leq 0
and
t\geq T
and
A(s,t)=0
, if both are less than
0
or greater than
T
. This leads to a process
I(t)
, which is the same for
t\in[0,T]
and constant outside of this interval.

We want to set
F_{s}(t)=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}A(s,t)
, as above. To do so, we need to assume that almost surely
A(s,\cdot)
is locally integrable. In practice, most candidates for
A
are almost surely continuous or at least piecewise continuous, so this additional assumption is reasonable to make. Under this assumption, we define the germ

F_{s}(\psi)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A(s,t)\psi^{\prime}(t)dt

as before. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{t}
fulfills our assumptions of completeness and right-continuity by assumption, and is trivially orthogonal as we only have one filtration. It follows that the germ
(F_{s})
has stochastic dimension
e=d=1
. We can now show the following:

Theorem 44
.

Let
A(s,t)
fulfill the assumptions of the stochastic sewing lemma, and assume
A(s,\cdot)
is in
L_{1}^{loc}(\mathbb{R})
almost surely. Then,
(F_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
is stochastically
\gamma
-coherent with stochastic dimension
1
. Let
I(t)
be the process we get from the sewing lemma from
A(s,t)
, and let
f
be the distribution gained from Theorem
22
applied to
(F_{x})
. It holds that

f(\psi)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}I(t)\psi^{\prime}(t)dt,

for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
a.s.

Proof.

Let us check the coherence property: Observe that

\displaystyle(F_{s}-F_{u})(\psi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(A(s,t)-A(s,u)-A(u,t))\psi^{\prime}(t)dt,

where we used that
A(s,u)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{\prime}(t)=0
since
\psi
is only compactly supported. It follows that for all
s\leq u
and
\psi
with compact, positive support

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{s}-F_{u})(\psi_{u}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{u}^{\lambda})}\left|\left|A(s,t)-A(s,u)-A(u,t)\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|(\psi_{u}^{\lambda})^{\prime}(t)\right|dt

\displaystyle\lesssim(\left|u-s\right|+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}+\gamma}\lambda^{-1},

and analogously

\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{s}}(F_{s}-F_{u})(\psi_{u}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\lesssim(\left|u-s\right|+\lambda)^{1+\gamma}\lambda^{-1}.

Thus,
(F_{s})_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
is indeed stochastically
\gamma
-coherent with
\alpha=-1
and there exists a reconstruction
f
. It remains to show that
f=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I
. To this end, we calculate

\displaystyle\left(F_{s}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I\right)(\psi)=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(A(s,t)-(I(t)-I(s)))\psi^{\prime}(t)dt,

where we again used that
I(s)\int\psi^{\prime}(t)dt=0
. It follows that for all test functions with strictly positive support

\displaystyle\left|\left|\left(F_{s}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I\right)(\psi_{s}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{t\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{s}^{\lambda})}\left|\left|A(s,t)-(I(t)-I(s))\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|(\psi_{s}^{\lambda})^{\prime}(t)\right|dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}+\gamma},

and analogously,

\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\left(F_{s}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}I\right)(\psi_{s}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{\gamma}.

By the uniqueness of the reconstruction, this concludes the proof.
∎

6
Gaussian Martingale Measure

As an application of stochastic reconstruction, we show that integration against Gaussian martingale measures can be seen as a product of a process in
C^{\alpha}
and a distribution in
C^{\beta}
, similar to the young product between distributions presented in the introduction. The martingale properties of the measure will allow us to do this reconstruction up to
\alpha+\beta>-\frac{1}{2}
, in comparison to the classical assumption
\alpha+\beta>0
.

We begin by introducing the notation of martingale measures, which can be found in
[
Dal+09
]
or
[
Wal86
]
.

6.1
Gaussian martingale measures and Walsh-type Integration

Loosely speaking, a Gaussian martingale measure is a Gaussian family
(W_{t}(A))_{t\geq 0,A\in\mathcal{A}_{K}}
for some subset
\mathcal{A}_{K}\subset B(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, such that

•

W_{t}(A)
is a martingale in
t
, and

•

W_{t}(A)
is a measure in
A
in the sense that
W_{t}(\emptyset)=0
and for all disjoint sets
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
W_{t}\left(\bigcup_{n}A_{n}\right)=\sum_{n}W_{t}(A_{n})
(where both equations hold almost surely).

To get a Gaussian measure (or a Gaussian family in general), we need to clarify its covariance. This is given through the notion of a covariance measure:

Definition 45
.

Let
K
be a symmetric, positive definite and
\sigma
-finite signed measure on
(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},B(\mathbb{R}^{d})\otimes B(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. Then,
K
is called a
covariance measure
, if there is a symmetric, positive definite, and
\sigma
-finite measure
\left|K\right|
, such that for all
A,B\in B(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\left|K(A\times B)\right|\leq\left|K\right|(A\times B).

We set

\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{K}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)f(y)K(dx,dy),

and define
\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{\left|K\right|}
analogously. We further set
\mathcal{A}_{K}:=\{A\in B(\mathbb{R}^{d})\penalty\ |\penalty\ K(A\times A)<\infty\}
. This allows us to formally define the Gaussian martingale measure
W
as follows:

Definition 46
.

Let
K
be a covariance measure. A
Gaussian martingale measure
is a family of centered Gaussian variables

(W_{t}(A)\penalty\ |\penalty\ t\geq 0,A\in\mathcal{A}_{k}),

such that

i)

W_{0}(A)=0
.

ii)

For all
A\in\mathcal{A}_{K}
,
(W_{t}(A))_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous martingale with respect to the filtration
\sigma(W_{s}(A)\penalty\ |\penalty\ 0\leq s\leq t,A\in\mathcal{A}_{K})
.

iii)

Almost surely,
W_{t}(\emptyset)=0
and
W_{t}\left(\bigcup_{n}A_{n}\right)=\sum_{n}W_{t}(A_{n})
holds for all disjoint sequences
(A_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

iv)

Its covariance is given by

E(W_{s}(A)W_{t}(B))=(s\land t)K(A\times B).

Remark 47
.

One can show that iii) is redundant in the sense that it can be derived from the other three properties. Since we decided not to present this result, we included the property in the definition.

We also denote the closure of the filtration of
W_{t}
as

\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\overline{\sigma(W_{s}(A)\penalty\ |\penalty\ 0\leq s\leq t,A\in\mathcal{A}_{K})}\,,

which is complete, right continuous, and, since we only have one filtration, fulfills the orthogonality assumption. The most common example of such a measure is space-time white noise, which is a Gaussian martingale measure with covariance measure
K^{WN}(dx,dy)=dx\,\delta_{x}(dy)
, which should be read as

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(y)K^{WN}(dx,dy)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)g(x)dx\,.

The construction of the integral over such martingale measure is rather straight-forward: We call processes of the form

H(s,x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=1}^{L_{k}}h_{k,l}1_{(t_{k},t_{k+1}]}(s)1_{A_{k,l}}(x)

for
\mathcal{F}_{t_{k}}
measurable random variables
h_{k,l}\in L_{\infty}(\Omega)
and sets
A_{k,l}\in\mathcal{A}_{K}

elementary processes
, and define the integral over such processes by

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H(s,x)W(ds,dx):=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=1}^{L_{k}}h_{k,l}(W_{t_{k+1}}(A_{k,l})-W_{t_{k}}(A_{k,l})).

We want to extend this definition to a certain
L_{2}
space. To do this, let
\mathcal{P}
be the predictable
\sigma
-algebra, which is generated by the elementary processes. We set

L_{2}(W):=\left\{H\text{ predictable},E\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|H(s,\cdot)\right|\right|_{\left|K\right|}^{2}ds\right)<\infty\right\}.

The following result can be found in
[
Wal86
]
:

Lemma 48
.

The set of elementary processes is dense in
L_{2}(W)
.

Our notion of an integral extends to the space
L_{2}(W)
, as the following theorem states:

Theorem 49

(Walsh Integral)
.

The above-given integral can be extended uniquely to a continuous, linear map

\displaystyle L_{2}(W)

\displaystyle\rightarrow M^{2}_{0}

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle\mapsto\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H(s,x)W(ds,dx),

where
M^{2}_{0}
is the set of square-integrable martingales starting from 0.

The proof can be found in
[
Wal86
]
.

By a simple approximation argument, one can show that the
Itô-isometry
holds for all
H\in L_{2}(W)
and
t\geq 0
:

E\left(\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H(s,x)W(ds,dx)\right)^{2}\right)=E\left(\int_{0}^{t}\left|\left|H(s,\cdot)\right|\right|_{K}^{2}ds\right).

With this, we can tackle the main result of this section:

6.2
Reconstruction of the Walsh-type Integral

Let
W
be a Gaussian martingale measure, and let
X(t,x)
be a predictable process. We want to show, that the distribution associated with the Walsh-type integral

\psi\mapsto\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(s,x)X(s,x)W(ds,dx)\,,

can be constructed with the stochastic reconstruction theorem. Note that the integral is a martingale in time
t
, making our stochastic dimension
e=1
.

We need a local approximation for the integral, which we get by the constant approximating
X(s,x)\approx X(t,y)
for
(s,x)
close to
(t,y)
. This motivates using the germ
F_{t,y}=X(t,y)dW
, i.e.

F_{t,y}(\psi):=X(t,y)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(s,x)W(ds,dx).

Remark 50
.

While this allows us to formally define the germ on any test function, it is only really useful on test functions with a support on the right-hand-side of
t
, so that
\tau\mapsto X(t,y)\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{R^{d}}\psi(s,x)W(ds,dx)
is a martingale. Our left-sided version of the reconstruction theorem mirrors this property.

For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to
L_{2}(\Omega)
, but one can always use Gaussianity to extend our result into
L_{p}
spaces for more general
p
. The regularity of
X
is governed by its Hölder-continuity, i.e. we assume
X\in C^{\alpha}(L_{2}(\Omega))
for some
\alpha>0
, where we use the classical scaling
s=(1,1,\dots,1)
. It is however not clear a priori how to measure the regularity of
W
. To this end, let
K
be the covariance measure of
W
. We say that
K
has a scaling
\delta>0
if it holds that

K(\lambda dx,\lambda dy)=\lambda^{\delta}K(dx,dy).

The following lemma shows, that
W
as a random distribution is
-d+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}
-Hölder continuous, if its covariance measure has the scaling
\delta
:

Lemma 51
.

Let
K
have scaling
\delta
, as above. It holds that:

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|\phi_{t,y}^{\lambda}(s,\cdot)\right|\right|_{K}^{2}ds\lesssim\lambda^{2\alpha}

for
\alpha=-d-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}
. We say that
K
is of homogeneity
\alpha.

Proof.

A straightforward calculation shows:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{t,y}^{\lambda}(s,x_{1})

\displaystyle\phi_{t,y}^{\lambda}(s,x_{2})K(dx_{1},dx_{2})ds

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2d-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{t,y}\left(\frac{s}{\lambda},\frac{x_{1}}{\lambda}\right)\phi_{t,y}\left(\frac{s}{\lambda},\frac{x_{2}}{\lambda}\right)K(dx_{1},dx_{2})ds

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2d-2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\frac{t}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda}}(r,v_{1})\phi_{\frac{t}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda}}(r,v_{2})K(\lambda dv_{1},\lambda dv_{2})\lambda dr

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2d-1+\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi_{\frac{t}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda}}(r,v_{1})\phi_{\frac{t}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda}}(r,v_{2})K(dv_{1},dv_{2})dr

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2d-1+\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|\phi_{\frac{t}{\lambda},\frac{y}{\lambda}}(r,\cdot)\right|\right|_{K}^{2}dr

\displaystyle=\lambda^{-2d-1+\delta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|\phi(s,\cdot)\right|\right|_{K}^{2}ds.

∎

Remark 52
.

Using the Itô-isometry, this implies that

\left|\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(s,x)W(ds,dx)\right|\right|_{L_{2}}=\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|\phi_{t,y}^{\lambda}(s,\cdot)\right|\right|_{K}^{2}ds\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{\alpha}.

Thus, the distribution
\psi\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(s,x)W(ds,dx)
is indeed in
C^{\alpha}(L_{2}(\Omega))
.

With this result, we can reconstruct the Walsh integral:

Theorem 53
.

Let
K
be of homogeneity
\alpha
, and let
X\in C^{\beta}(L_{2}(\Omega))
for some
\beta\in(0,1)
. Let
(F_{t,y})
be as above. If
\alpha+\beta>-\frac{1}{2}
, then
(F_{t,y})
fulfills the assumption of Theorem
22
, and thus there exists a distribution
f
fulfilling
(
3.8
)
and
(
3.9
)
. It further holds, that
f
is given by the Walsh-type integral, i.e.

f(\psi)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}X(s,x)\psi(s,x)W(ds,dx),

(6.1)

for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
a.s.,
r=\max(\lfloor-\alpha\rfloor+1,1)
.

Remark 54
.

Observe that by Lemma
51
, Theorem
53
automatically applies to all
K
with a scaling
\delta
, such that

\beta+\frac{\delta}{2}>d.

Remark 55
.

Looking at our example of white noise, we get that
K^{WN}(\lambda dx,\lambda dy)=(\lambda dx)\delta_{x}(\lambda dy)=\lambda^{d}K(dx,dy)
, which implies that
K^{WN}
has scaling
\delta=d
and thus, white noise has stochastic Hölder regularity
-\frac{d+1}{2}
. Therefore, we would need
\beta>\frac{d}{2}
to apply the above theorem, which is a highly sub-optimal requirement. Indeed, it is well known that
\beta>0
suffices to define integration against space-time white noise
[
Qua12
]
. The reason our result is much worse is that we only use the martingale properties in the time direction, i.e. we use stochastic dimension
e=1
, whereas white noise has additional martingale properties in all spatial directions and should have stochastic dimension
e=d+1
. Theorem
53
is much better suited to deal with cases of colored noise, which only have martingale properties in the time direction. The case of space-time white noise will be discussed at length in Section
7
.

Proof.

We first show that the assumptions of Theorem
22
are fulfilled: Let
t\leq s
, and let
\psi
be a test function with support in
[0,1]\times[-1,1]^{d}
. It holds that:

\displaystyle E^{\mathcal{F}_{t}}

\displaystyle(F_{t,y}-F_{s,x})(\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda})

\displaystyle=E^{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\left((X(t,y)-X(s,x))E^{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}(u,v)W(du,dv)\right)=0,

since
\int_{0}^{\tau}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}(u,v)W(du,dv)
is a martingale in
\tau
and
s
is on the edge of the support of
\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}
. It remains to show, that

\left|\left|(F_{t,y}-F_{s,x})(\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\left|(s,x)-(t,y)\right|+\lambda)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\tilde{\alpha}}

for some
\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R},\gamma>0
. We chose
\gamma=\alpha+\beta+\frac{1}{2}
and observe the following:

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{t,y}-F_{s,x})(\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|(X(t,y)-X(s,x))\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}(u,v)W(du,dv)\right|\right|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\underbrace{\left|\left|(X(t,y)-X(s,x))\right|\right|_{L_{2}}^{2}}_{\lesssim\left|(t,y)-(s,x)\right|^{2\beta}}\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}(u,v)\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}(u,w)K(dv,dw)du

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|(t,y)-(s,x)\right|^{2\beta}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}(u,\cdot)\right|\right|_{K}^{2}du

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|(t,y)-(s,x)\right|^{2\beta}\lambda^{2\alpha}.

(6.2)

Thus, using
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{t,y}-F_{s,x})(\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\lesssim\lambda^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\left|(t,y)-(s,x)\right|+\lambda)^{\alpha+\beta-\tilde{\alpha}}=\lambda^{\tilde{\alpha}}(\left|(t,y)-(s,x)\right|+\lambda)^{\gamma-\frac{1}{2}-\tilde{\alpha}}.

This shows that there is a unique reconstruction

. To find

, note that the proof of Theorem

requires
\tilde{r}=r
to be strictly greater than the absolute value of
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha
, and

. It follows that

.

It remains to show

)
. To this end, assume that the support of
\psi
is in
[\tilde{C},\tilde{R}]\times[-1,1]^{d}
for some

. We need to show that

\tilde{f}(\psi):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}X(s,x)\psi(s,x)W(ds,dx)

fulfills
(
3.8
)
and
(
3.9
)

(

, so it remains to show

\displaystyle\left|\left|(\tilde{f}-F_{t,y})(\psi_{t,y}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=\left|\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(X(s,x)-X(t,y))\psi_{t,y}^{\lambda}(s,x)W(ds,dx)\right|\right|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sup_{(s,x)\in\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{t,y}^{\lambda})}\underbrace{\left|\left|X(s,x)-X(t,y)\right|\right|_{L_{2}}}_{\lesssim\lambda^{\beta}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\left|\psi_{s,x}^{\lambda}\right|\right|_{K}^{2}ds

7
Integration against white noise

Let us take a closer look at space-time white noise, which is given by the Gaussian martingale measure
W^{WN}
characterized by its covariance measure
K^{WN}(dx,dy)=dx\,\delta_{x}(dy)
. As mentioned above, it has scaling
\delta=d
and does not fall into the setting of Theorem
53
. This is not at all surprising, as the previous section assumes a stochastic dimension of
e=1
, whereas space-time white noise has martingale properties in all of its directions and should be treated as
e=d+1
. This makes integration against white noise an easy example of a stochastic reconstruction of a non-trivial stochastic dimension, which is our main motivation for discussing it in this paper.

Let us first discuss the construction of space-time white noise: Treating it as a Gaussian martingale measure comes with the downside that we treat time and space differently in the construction, whereas white noise treats both the same. If we look at the space
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
containing vectors of the form
(t,x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
and look at a Gaussian family
(\tilde{W}(A))_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{k}}
for
\mathcal{A}_{k}\subset B(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
with covariance
E(\tilde{W}(A)\tilde{W}(B))=\tilde{K}(A,B)
and covariance measure
\tilde{K}(dx,dy)=dx\,\delta_{x}(dy)
in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, it is easy to see that
W^{WN}_{t}(A)=\tilde{W}([0,t]\times A)
in distribution. So it is far more natural to define space-time white noise as a Gaussian family over sets in
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, and forget that the first variable is given by time.

The most common notion of white noise takes this idea a bit further, and directly defines white noise as a random distribution: As shown in the literature (e.g.
[
Jan97
]
,
[
Nua06
]
) there exists a linear map
\xi:L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L_{2}(\Omega)
such that for each
\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\xi(\psi)
is a centered Gaussian random variable and the family
(\xi(\psi))_{\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
has the following covariance:

E(\xi(\psi)\xi(\phi))=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(z)\phi(z)dz\,,

(7.1)

which is also often referred to as
E(\xi(x)\xi(y))=\delta_{x}(y)
. This makes
\xi
a random
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
distribution (continuity follows from
\left|\left|\xi(\phi)\right|\right|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)^{2}dx=\left|\left|\phi\right|\right|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
by
(
7.1
)
). The connection to
\tilde{W}
is given by
\tilde{W}(A)=\xi(1_{A})
for all compact sets
A\in B(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and since
\xi:L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\mapsto L_{2}(\Omega)
is linear and continuous, defining it on the indicator functions uniquely determines it on all of
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. One can think of
\xi(\psi)
as the integration of white noise against deterministic test functions, i.e.
``\xi(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\tilde{W}(dx)
”.

In the remainder of this section, we will call
\xi
white noise and use the notation
\xi(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(x)\xi(dx)
.

Our goal in this section is to use stochastic reconstruction to construct the higher-order iterated integrals

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\xi(dz_{1})\dots\xi(dz_{n})\,,

(7.2)

for
\psi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times n})
, which make up the Wiener chaos
[
Jan97
]
,
[
Nua06
]
. To do so, we use the approach of
[
Qua12
]
: Set
X_{0}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n}):=\psi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})
and for
k=1,\dots,n
, we integrate one variable out:
X_{k+1}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k-1})=\int X_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k})\xi(dz_{n-k})
. This way,
X_{n}\in L_{2}(\Omega)
is the desired integral.

So the problem comes down to defining the integral

\int X(z)\xi(dz)

for a random process
X
, which we will do via stochastic reconstruction. Since
\xi
is defined by its covariance, Theorem
30
will be perfectly suited to deal with this, but let us shortly highlight what would happen if one wants to use Theorem
22
: To do so, one needs to fix filtrations

\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{t}=\overline{\sigma\left(\xi(\psi)\penalty\ \big|\penalty\ \mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{i-1}\times(-\infty,t]\times\mathbb{R}^{d-i}\right)}.

These filtrations are the same as the filtrations
\underline{\underline{F_{t}^{i}}}
generated by a Brownian sheet in
[
Wal86a
]
. As John B. Walsh shows in this paper, they fulfill our assumptions of completeness, right continuity, and orthogonality. (Orthogonality is equivalent to conditional independence, see Remark
15
.)

Note that by
(
7.1
)
, for
\psi,\phi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with disjoint support,
\xi(\psi)
and
\xi(\phi)
are uncorrelated. Since
\xi
is a Gaussian family, this implies that
\xi(\psi)
and
\xi(\phi)
are independent. Thus, for a test function with positive support and any
i=1,\dots,d
, it follows that
\xi(\psi_{z}^{\lambda})
is independent of
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(z)}
.

For a stochastic process
X
, which is adapted to all directions
i=1,\dots,d
in the sense that
X(z)\in\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(z)}
and under the assumption that
X\in C^{\gamma}(L_{2}(\Omega))
for some
\gamma>0
, one can now show that the germ
F_{z}(\psi):=X(z)\xi(\psi)
is indeed stochastically
\gamma
-coherent with stochastic dimension
e=d
and can be reconstructed. Moreover, it will turn out that the reconstruction is nothing else than the classical integral
f(\psi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}X(z)\psi(z)\xi(dz)
, which we define in
(
7.3
)
.

However, there is one subtlety hidden in this construction: If we want to use this to construct
(
7.2
)
, one notices that
X_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k})
is in general not
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(z_{n-k})}
adapted. We can save this by setting

X_{k+1}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k-1})=\int_{z_{n-k}\leq z_{n-k-1}}X_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k})\xi(dz_{n-k})\,,

where we say
x\leq y
if and only if
\pi_{i}(x)\leq\pi_{i}(y)
for
i=1,\dots,d
. This ensures that
X_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k})
is
\mathcal{F}^{(i)}_{\pi_{i}(z_{n-k})}
measurable. However, if one does this, one only succeeds in defining the iterated integral

\int_{z_{1}\leq\dots\leq z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\xi(dz_{1})\dots\xi(dz_{n})\,.

This somehow resembles the construction in
[
Qua12
]
, where one only manages to directly construct the integral on the set
t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}
, where
t_{i}=\pi_{1}(z_{i})
is the time variable of the vector
z_{i}
. In this case, the construction can be saved by using the symmetry of white noise: By assumption, it holds that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(z_{1},\dots,z_{n})\xi(dz_{1})\dots\xi(dz_{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\psi(z_{\sigma(1)},\dots,z_{\sigma(n)})\xi(dz_{1})\dots\xi(dz_{n})

for all permutations
\sigma
. Thus, if one can define the integral for
t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{n}
, one can use permutations to extend the integral to all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Unfortunately, this does no longer work if one is only able to intrinsically define
(
7.2
)
over the area
z_{1}\leq\dots\leq z_{n}
. For example for two points
z_{1},z_{2}
with
\pi_{1}(z_{1})<\pi_{1}(z_{2})
and
\pi_{2}(z_{1})>\pi_{2}(z_{2})
, there is no permutation
\sigma:\{1,2\}\to\{1,2\}
such that
z_{\sigma(1)}\leq z_{\sigma(2)}
. Thus, we can not extend the integral to the whole space by symmetry alone. We know of three ways, how one can get around this subtlety:

•

By using time-swapped filtrations

\tilde{\mathcal{F}}^{(i)}_{t}=\overline{\sigma\left(\xi(\psi)\big|\mathrm{supp}(\psi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{i-1}\times[t,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d-i}\right)}\,,

one can construct integrals of the form
\int_{z_{n-k}\geq z_{n-k-1}}X_{k}(z_{1},\dots,z_{n-k})\xi(dz_{n-k})
. By cleverly using combinations of time-swapped filtrations in some directions
\eta\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
, and the normal filtrations in the other directions
\{1,\dots,d\}\setminus\eta
, one can construct the integral over all areas individually. To our knowledge, this approach would work, although it is quite tedious.

•

One can construct sigma-algebras which more accurately describe the measurability of
F_{x}(\psi)
. Right now, we only consider sigma-algebras depending on
\max(\pi_{i}(x),\pi_{i}(y))
, where the support of
\psi
is contained in
(-\infty,\pi_{1}(y)]\times\dots\times(-\infty,\pi_{d}(y)]
. If one constructs sigma-algebras depending on
x
and the support of
\psi
in a more sophisticated manner, it is possible to show that the reconstructing sequence
f^{(n)}

(
4.1
)
converges to a limit

for all
X
such that
X(z)\in\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{\pi_{1}(z)}
, reducing the adaptedness assumptions on
X
to only requiring adaptedness in time. However, the problem with this approach is that it is highly unclear how the adaptedness assumption on
F_{x}(\psi)
should translate to some assumption on
f
since it no longer has an index
x
. So in this setting, it is not clear under which condition
f
is unique.

In Conclusion, this approach would allow one to show that the reconstructing sequence for the germ
F_{z}(\psi)
defined in Theorem
56
converges against the correct limit
(
7.4
)
, but we are currently unable to generalize this observation to a new version of the stochastic reconstruction theorem.

•

The easiest way to deal with this problem is to use Theorem
30
. It allows us to directly use the covariance of our germ and thus allows us to easily reconstruct the integral, even if
X
is only adapted in time. As mentioned above, one can use this together with the symmetry of white noise to construct
(
7.2
)
over all of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the remainder of this section, we will present this approach.

Let
(\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}

We need to show

)

\displaystyle\left|\left|(F_{x}-F_{y})(\psi_{y}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}=\left|\left|X(z)-X(y)\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\left|\left|\xi(\psi_{y}^{\lambda})\right|\right|_{L_{2}}\lesssim\left|\left|x-y\right|\right|^{\gamma}\lambda^{-\frac{d}{2}}\,.

We call the reconstruction of

Appendix A
Wavelet Bases

This section is dedicated to the introduction of the wavelet techniques used in Section
4
, and mainly follows Section 3.1 of
[
Hai14
]
. If one is interested in a more detailed discussion of the topic, it can be found in
[
Mey93
]
.

Intuitively, a wavelet basis is given by a smooth, compactly supported function
\phi
, such that any test function
\psi
can be decomposed in the form

\psi=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k\in\Delta_{n}}\langle\psi,\phi_{k}^{n}\rangle\phi_{k}^{n},

(A.1)

where the limit holds in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\phi_{k}^{n}
is a certain localized version of
\phi
around
k
,
\Delta_{n}=2^{-n}\mathbb{Z}
is a mesh, which gets increasingly fine as
n\to\infty
. This allows us to analyze arbitrary test functions
\psi
, using only the local properties of
\phi_{k}^{n}
. Such a wavelet can be constructed using the following theorem of Daubechies, which can be found in
[
Dau88
]
:

Theorem 59

(Daubechies)
.

For any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a function
\phi\in C_{c}^{r}(\mathbb{R})
, such that

i)

For all
z\in\mathbb{Z}
, it holds that

\langle\phi,\phi(\cdot-z)\rangle=\delta_{0,z}.

ii)

\phi
is self-replicable, i.e. there exist constants
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, such that

\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\phi(x/2)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}a_{k}\phi(x-k).

Due to the compact support of
\phi
, it holds that
a_{k}=0
for all but finitely many
k
. In Daubechies’ setting, it does not matter where the support of
\phi
lies, as long as it is compact. However, as we have seen several times in this paper, the stochastic theory requires us to separate the number line
\mathbb{R}
into the “future”
[0,\infty)
and the “past”
(-\infty,0]
. In particular, we are only interested in functions
\phi
with compact support in the future, i.e.
\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subset[0,R]
for some
R>0
. Such a wavelet can be easily constructed by replacing
\phi
with
\phi(\cdot-x)
for a large enough
x>0
. Further observe that by choosing
x
large enough, we can also assure that
a_{k}=0
for all
k<0
in property ii), and that
\phi
has support in
[C,R]
for some constants
0<C<R
.

Property
i)
ensures that the recentered wavelets
\phi_{z}
,
z\in\mathbb{Z}
form an orthonormal system in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. For
n\in\mathbb{N},k\in\Delta_{n}
, we set

\phi_{k}^{n}(x):=2^{\frac{n}{2}}\phi(2^{n}(x-k))=2^{-\frac{n}{2}}\phi_{k}^{2^{-n}}(x).

to be the
L_{2}
-normalization of
\phi
. It is shown in
[
Dau88
]
, that
(\phi_{k}^{n})_{k\in\Delta_{n}}
almost form an
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
basis in the sense that
(
A.1
)
holds in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Since
\phi
is self-replicable, it follows that

\phi_{k}^{n}=\sum_{l\in\Delta_{n+1}}a_{l}^{n+1}\phi_{k+l}^{n+1}\,,

is also self-replicable with
a_{l}^{n+1}:=a_{2^{n+1}l}
.

The second ingredient of a wavelet basis is a function
\hat{\phi}
, which is able to give one a description of the “missing part”
\psi-\sum_{k\in\Delta_{n}}\langle\psi,\phi_{k}^{n}\rangle\phi_{k}^{n}
in the sense that

\psi=\sum_{k\in\Delta_{n}}\langle\psi,\phi_{k}^{n}\rangle\phi_{k}^{n}+\sum_{m\geq n}\sum_{k\in\Delta_{m}}\langle\psi,\hat{\phi}_{k}^{m}\rangle\hat{\phi}^{m}_{k}

(A.2)

holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, where
\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}:=2^{\frac{n}{2}}\hat{\phi}(2^{n}(x-k))
as before. The existence of such a function is shown through the following theorem from
[
Mey93
]
:

Theorem 60

(Meyer)
.

Let

V_{n}:=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{k}^{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ k\in\Delta_{n}\}\,.

Then there is a function
\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\in C^{r}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, such that

i)

\hat{V}_{n}:=\mathrm{span}\{\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ k\in\Delta_{n}\}
is the orthorgonal complement of
V_{n}
in
V_{n+1}
, i.e.
\hat{V}_{n}=V_{n}^{\perp V_{n+1}}
.

ii)

(\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N},k\in\Delta_{n}}
is a family of orthonormal functions:

\langle\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n},\hat{\phi}_{l}^{m}\rangle=\delta_{n,m}\delta_{k,l}.

iii)

\hat{\phi}
eliminates all polynomials of degree less than
r
: For any monomial
x^{m}
,
m\leq r
, it holds that

\langle x^{m},\hat{\phi}\rangle=0.

Note that
V_{n+1}\subset V_{n}
by self-replicability.
(
A.1
)
implies that
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})=\overline{\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}V_{n}}
, which together with property i) automatically gives us
L_{2}(\mathbb{R})=\overline{V_{n}\cup\left(\bigcup_{m\geq n}\hat{V}_{m}\right)}
and thus shows
(
A.2
)
. Property iii) causes
\langle\psi,\hat{\phi}_{k}^{m}\rangle
to decay faster than one would expect as

, which is formalized in Lemma

.

Let us generalize this setting to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the scaling

\Delta_{n}^{S}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}2^{-ns_{i}}l_{i}\vec{e}_{i}\penalty\ \bigg|\penalty\ l_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},

to be the rescaled mesh on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\vec{e}_{i}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
is the

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We usually drop the index

and only write

, since the scaling is fixed within each section of this paper. The wavelet function in

\phi(y)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\phi(\pi_{i}(y))\,,

with its

\phi_{k}^{n}(x):=2^{n\frac{d}{2}}\phi(2^{n}(x-k))
as before. We define

\displaystyle V_{n}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{span}\{\phi_{k}^{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ k\in\Delta_{n}\}

(A.3)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{n}

\displaystyle:=V_{n}^{\perp V_{n+1}}

(A.4)

as before. As it turns out,

no longer gets generated by a single function
\hat{\phi}
, but by a finite set of functions

(for reference, see
[

). This construction immediately gives us that

have support in

for some

and that
\phi
is self-replicable with
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
such that
a_{k}\neq 0
only if
\pi_{1}(k),\dots,\pi_{d}(k)\geq 0
.

Since the one-dimensional wavelet
\hat{\phi}
eliminates polynomials of degree less or equal to
r
, this implies that for all
\hat{\phi}\in\Phi
,
\langle\hat{\phi},p\rangle=0
for all multivariate polynomials
p
of degree less or equal to
r
.

We use the following notations for the projections onto
V_{n}
and
\hat{V}_{n}
in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\displaystyle P_{n}\psi

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k\in\Delta_{n}}\langle\psi,\phi_{k}^{n}\rangle\phi_{k}^{n}

\displaystyle\hat{P}_{n}\psi

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\hat{\phi}\in\Phi}\sum_{k\in\Delta_{n}}\langle\psi,\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\rangle\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\,.

We will need to quantify the speed of convergence of
\langle\psi,\phi_{k}^{n}\rangle,\langle\psi,\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\rangle
for
n\to\infty
. This gets achieved by the following lemma:

Lemma 61
.

Let
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
. Then, for all
\hat{\phi}\in\Phi
,
n\in\mathbb{N},y\in\Delta_{n},z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
2^{-n}\leq\lambda\leq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\phi_{y}^{n},\psi_{z}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}

(A.5)

\displaystyle\left|\langle\hat{\phi}_{y}^{n},\psi_{z}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}-n\tilde{r}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|-\tilde{r}}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}.

(A.6)

where
\tilde{r}=r\cdot\min_{i=1,\dots,d}(s_{i})
.

Proof.

The proof consists mainly of straight-forward calculations. To show
(
A.5
)
, observe that

\displaystyle\left|\langle\phi_{y}^{n},\psi_{z}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\int\left|\phi\left(S^{2^{n}}(x-y)\right)\psi\left(S^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}(x-z)\right)\right|dx

\displaystyle=2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\int\left|\phi(u-S^{2^{n}}y)\psi\left(S{{}^{\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}}}u-S^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}z\right)\right|du

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\sup_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\psi(u)\right|\left|\left|\phi\right|\right|_{L_{1}}

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}.

To see
(
A.6
)
we will use that
\hat{\phi}
eliminates polynomials of order less or equal to
r
. Let
T^{r-1}_{x_{0}}
be the
(r-1)
-th Taylor approximation of
\psi
at the point
x_{0}
. Recall that

\left|\psi(x_{0}+u)-T^{r-1}_{x_{0}}(x_{0}+u)\right|\lesssim\left|\left|u\right|\right|_{1}^{r}\left|\left|\psi^{(r)}\right|\right|_{\infty},

where
\left|\left|u\right|\right|_{1}=\left|u_{1}\right|+\dots+\left|u_{d}\right|
is the 1-norm of the vector
u
. Let
\tilde{s}=\min_{i=1,\dots,d}(s_{i})
and observe that
\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}\leq 1
implies

\displaystyle\left|\left|S^{\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}}u\right|\right|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{1}{2^{ns_{1}}\lambda^{s_{1}}}u_{1}\right|+\dots+\left|\frac{1}{2^{ns_{d}}\lambda^{s_{d}}}u_{d}\right|

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}\right)^{\tilde{s}}\left(\left|\frac{1}{2^{ns_{1}-\tilde{s}}\lambda^{s_{1}-\tilde{s}}}u_{1}\right|+\dots+\left|\frac{1}{2^{ns_{d}-\tilde{s}}\lambda^{s_{d}-\tilde{s}}}u_{d}\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}\right)^{\tilde{s}}\left|\left|u\right|\right|_{1}.

With this in mind, we can use
\int\hat{\phi}(u)T^{r-1}_{x_{0}}(u)du=0
to get

\displaystyle\left|\langle\hat{\phi}_{y}^{n},\psi_{z}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\left|\int\hat{\phi}(u)\psi\Big(S^{\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}}u\underbrace{-S^{\frac{1}{\lambda}}z+S^{2^{n}}y}_{=:x_{0}}\Big)du\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|}\int\left|\hat{\phi}(u)\right|\underbrace{\left|\psi\left(S^{\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}}u+x_{0}\right)-T_{x_{0}}^{r-1}\left(S^{\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}}u+x_{0}\right)\right|}_{\lesssim\left|\left|S^{\frac{1}{2^{n}\lambda}}u\right|\right|_{1}^{r}\left|\left|\psi^{(r)}\right|\right|_{\infty}\lesssim 2^{-n\tilde{r}}\lambda^{-\tilde{r}}\left|\left|u\right|\right|_{1}^{r}\left|\left|\psi^{(r)}\right|\right|_{\infty}}du

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}-n\tilde{r}}\lambda^{-{\left|S\right|}-\tilde{r}}\int\left|\hat{\phi}(u)\right|\left|\left|u\right|\right|_{1}^{r}\left|\left|\psi^{(r)}\right|\right|_{\infty}du

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{-n\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}-n\tilde{r}}\lambda^{-\left|S\right|-\tilde{r}}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}.

∎

While this lemma is mostly technical, let us only remark that
(
A.6
)
shows that
\hat{\phi}
eliminating polynomials causes
\langle\psi,\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\rangle
to converge faster to zero than one would expect. In fact, by choosing
r
big enough, we can cause
\langle\psi,\hat{\phi}_{k}^{n}\rangle
to converge at any rate we want.

Throughout this paper, we have used that
(
A.1
)
and
(
A.2
)
hold in more topologies than just
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-convergence. To show this, we will use a wavelet basis in
C_{c}^{q}
for some
q>r
. Since
\phi,\hat{\phi}\in C_{c}^{q}\subset C_{c}^{r}
holds for all
\hat{\phi}\in\Phi
, it is easy to show that any
C_{c}^{q}
wavelet basis is automatically a
C_{c}^{r}
wavelet basis. So while we are technically using
C_{c}^{q}
wavelets to prove the main results of this paper, the test functions
\psi
lie in
C_{c}^{r}
and we treat
(\phi,\Phi)
as
C_{c}^{r}
wavelets.

Lemma 62
.

Let
q
be such that
\tilde{q}>r+\left|S\right|
and choose a wavelet basis
(\phi,\Phi)
of
C_{c}^{q}
. Then for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{q}
, it holds that

P_{n}\psi\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\psi

(A.7)

converges in
C_{c}^{r}
.

Furthermore, let
\alpha<0
such that
\tilde{r}+\alpha>0
and consider a distribution
f\in C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then for any test function
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
, it holds that

\displaystyle f(\psi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(P_{n}\psi)

(A.8)

\displaystyle=f(P_{n}(\psi))+\sum_{m\geq n}f(\hat{P}_{m}\psi)\,,

(A.9)

where
(
A.9
)
holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
f\in C^{\alpha}(L_{p})
for some
1\leq p<\infty
, both identities hold as limits in
L_{p}
.

Proof.

We begin by showing
(
A.7
)
. Let
k\geq l
be two natural numbers. Then it holds that

P_{k}\psi=P_{l}\psi+\sum_{m=l}^{k-1}\hat{P}_{m}\psi\,,

so to show that
P_{n}\psi
is Cauchy, it suffices to show
\left|\left|\hat{P}_{m}\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}\lesssim 2^{-m(\tilde{q}-r-\left|S\right|)}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{q}}
. By Remark
9
,
\left|\left|\hat{\phi}_{x}^{m}\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}\lesssim 2^{m\left(r+\frac{\left|S\right|}{2}\right)}
holds for all
m\in\mathbb{N},x\in\Delta_{m}
. Recall that
\psi\in C_{c}^{q}
, so we can use
(
A.6
)
with
q
to show

\displaystyle\left|\left|\hat{P}_{m}\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in\Delta_{m}}\left|\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{m},\psi\rangle\right|\left|\left|\hat{\phi}_{x}^{m}\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{x\in\Delta_{m},\langle\hat{\phi}_{x}^{m},\psi\rangle\neq 0}2^{-m(\tilde{q}-r)}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{q}}\,.

Since the number of non-zero terms is of order
2^{m\left|S\right|}
, the claim follows. Thus,
P_{n}\psi
is Cauchy and thus convergent in
C_{c}^{r}
. Since
P_{n}\psi\to\psi
in
L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
n\to\infty
, the limit can only be
\psi
. It follows that
P_{n}\psi\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\psi
holds in
C_{c}^{r}
.

For the second part of the lemma, we will only show the deterministic case, for the
L_{p}
case one simply replaces the absolute values with the
L_{p}
norms. Observe that for any
0\leq n\leq l
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\Delta_{l}}f(\phi_{k}^{l})\langle\phi_{k}^{l},\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=f(P_{l}\psi)

\displaystyle=f(P_{n}\psi)+\sum_{m=n}^{l}f(\hat{P}_{m}\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\Delta_{n}}f(\phi_{k}^{n})\langle\phi_{k}^{n},\psi\rangle+\sum_{m=n}^{l}\sum_{\hat{\phi}\in\Phi}\sum_{k\in\Delta_{m}}f(\hat{\phi}_{k}^{m})\langle\hat{\phi}_{k}^{m},\psi\rangle\,,

which shows that
(
A.8
)
and
(
A.9
)
are the same limit, and it suffices to show one of them. Using
(
A.6
)
, we get that

\sum_{k\in\Delta_{m}}\left|f(\hat{\phi}_{k}^{m})\langle\hat{\phi}_{k}^{m},\psi\rangle\right|\lesssim 2^{-m(\alpha+\tilde{r})}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}\,,

where we use that the number of non-zero terms in the sum is of order
2^{m\left|S\right|}
. It follows that
(
A.9
)
converges for
l\to\infty
, and
\left|f(P_{n}\psi)\right|\lesssim 2^{-n\alpha}\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{r}}
implies that the limit is a
C_{c}^{r}
distribution.

We call the limit
g
and it remains to show that
g=f
. By
(
A.7
)
,
f(P_{n}\tilde{\psi})\to f(\tilde{\psi})
as
n\to\infty
holds for
\tilde{\psi}\in C_{c}^{q}
for large enough
q
, which implies that
f(\tilde{\psi})=g(\tilde{\psi})
. Since
C_{c}^{q}\subset C_{c}^{r}
is dense, it follows that
f(\psi)=g(\psi)
for all
\psi\in C_{c}^{r}
.
∎

Appendix B
A BDG-type inequality

Let us introduce the inequality that allows us to “borrow regularity” from the conditional expectation of a process: Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a complete probability space. Given a process
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L_{p}(\Omega)
in discreet time that is adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{i+1}
for some filtration
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, we can use Doobs decomposition
[
DSC53
]
on the sum

\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}E^{\mathcal{F}_{i}}Z_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Z_{i}-E^{\mathcal{F}_{i}}Z_{i}\right)\,.

This allows us to bound
\left|\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}
by
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{i}}Z_{i}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}
plus
\left|\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(Z_{i}-E^{\mathcal{F}_{i}}Z_{i}\right)\right|\right|_{L_{p}}
. Since the last term is a sum over martingale differences, we can find a better bound than simply pulling the
L_{p}
-norm into the sum with BDG-type inequalities, see
[
BDG72
]
. So as long as the first sum (i.e. the conditional expectation of
Z_{i}
) is well-behaved, we can get a good bound on
\left|\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}
. Formally, this reads as:

Lemma 63
.

Let
p\geq 2
and for
i\in\mathbb{N}
let
Z_{i}\in L_{p}(\Omega)
. Let
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a filtration, such that
Z_{i}
is
\mathcal{F}_{i+1}
-measurable for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, the following inequality holds for all
N\geq 1
:

\left|\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\left|E^{\mathcal{F}_{i}}Z_{i}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\left|Z_{i}-E^{\mathcal{F}_{i}}Z_{i}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,.

(B.1)

The proof can be found in
[
Lê20
]
, at the beginning of Section 2.

Appendix C
Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem for negative Hölder regularity

We would like to present a version of Kolmogorov’s continuity theorem for distributions
f\in C^{\alpha}(L_{p})
with negative
\alpha
. To do so, we need the fattening of a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which for every
\epsilon>0
is defined to be

K_{\epsilon}:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \inf_{x\in K}\left|x-y\right|\leq\epsilon\right\}\,.

With this, we can formulate the following lemma:

Lemma 64
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact and
\alpha<0,1\leq p<\infty
. Assume that
f\in C^{\alpha}(L_{p}(\Omega),K_{\epsilon})
for some
\epsilon>0
. Let
\hat{\alpha}<\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}
and choose
\hat{r}
such that
\tilde{\hat{r}}>-\hat{\alpha}
, where
\tilde{\hat{r}}
is given as in Lemma
61
. Then there is a non-negative random variable
B\in L_{p}(\Omega)
such that for each
\psi\in C_{c}^{\hat{r}}
with
\left|\left|\psi\right|\right|_{C_{c}^{\hat{r}}}=1
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\lambda\in(0,1]
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\subset K
it holds that

\left|f(\psi_{x}^{\lambda},\omega)\right|\leq B(\omega)\lambda^{\hat{\alpha}}\,.

(C.1)

It follows that
f(\cdot,\omega)\in C^{\hat{\alpha}}(K)
a.s.

Proof.

We will assume throughout this proof that
\lambda<\tilde{\epsilon}:=\min_{i=1,\dots,d}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{R-C}\right)^{\frac{1}{s_{i}}}
, where
0<C<R
are given by a wavelet bases. Extending the result to
\lambda\in(0,1]
simply adds a factor only depending on
\epsilon,C,R
to all calculations. Let
(\phi,\Phi)
be
C_{c}^{\hat{r}}
wavelets and set

\displaystyle B_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{x}^{n})\subset K_{\epsilon}}\frac{\left|f(\phi_{x}^{n})\right|}{c_{n}}

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{n}

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\hat{\phi}\in\Phi,\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\phi}_{x}^{n})\subset K_{\epsilon}}\frac{\left|f(\hat{\phi}_{x}^{n})\right|}{c_{n}}\,,

with normalizing factor
c_{n}=2^{-n(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{p})\left|S\right|-n\alpha}
. We can calculate the
L_{p}
norm of these terms to be bound by the constant

\displaystyle\left|\left|B_{n}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{x}^{n})\subset K_{\epsilon}}\left|\left|f(\phi_{x}^{n})\right|\right|_{L_{p}}^{p}c_{n}^{-p}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{C^{\alpha}(L_{p})}^{p}\,,

where we use that the number of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{x}^{n})\subset K_{\epsilon}
is of order
2^{n\left|S\right|}
. Analogously,
\left|\left|\hat{B}_{n}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}\lesssim\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{C^{\alpha}(L_{p})}
is bound by a constant.

Let
\lambda\in(0,\tilde{\epsilon}]
,
\psi\in C_{c}^{\hat{r}}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\subset K
. Choose
n
in such a way, that
2^{-n}\leq\lambda<2^{-n+1}
. By our choice of
\tilde{\epsilon}
, it follows that for all
\phi_{y}^{n},\hat{\phi}_{y}^{n}
such that their supports overlap with the support of
\psi_{x}^{\lambda}
, we have
\mathrm{supp}(\phi_{y}^{n}),\mathrm{supp}(\hat{\phi}_{y}^{n})\subset K_{\epsilon}
. We calculate, that

\displaystyle\left|f(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\Delta_{n}}\left|f(\phi_{y}^{n})\langle\phi_{y}^{n},\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|+\sum_{\hat{\phi}\in\Phi}\sum_{m\geq n}\sum_{y\in\Delta_{m}}\left|f(\hat{\phi}_{y}^{m})\langle\hat{\phi}_{y}^{m},\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq B_{n}c_{n}\sum_{y\in\Delta_{n}}\left|\langle\phi_{y}^{n},\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|+\sum_{m\geq n}\hat{B}_{m}c_{m}\sum_{y\in\Delta_{m}}\left|\langle\hat{\phi}_{y}^{m},\psi_{x}^{\lambda}\rangle\right|\,.

Set
\kappa:=\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}-\hat{\alpha}>0
. By using
(
A.5
)
and
(
A.6
)
and carefully counting the number of non-zero terms, we get that

\displaystyle\left|f(\psi_{x}^{\lambda})\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(B_{n}+\sum_{m\geq n}\hat{B}_{m}2^{(-m-n)(\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}+\tilde{\hat{r}})}\right)\lambda^{\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}}

\displaystyle\approx\left(B_{n}+\sum_{m\geq n}\hat{B}_{m}2^{-(m-n)(\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}+\tilde{\hat{r}})}\right)2^{-n\kappa}\lambda^{\hat{\alpha}}\,,

so by choosing

B:=D\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\penalty\ 2^{-n\kappa}\left(B_{n}+\sum_{m\geq n}\hat{B}_{m}2^{-(m-n)(\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}+\tilde{\hat{r}})}\right)

for a smartly chosen constant
D>0
, we get
(
C.1
)
. It remains to show that
B\in L_{p}(\Omega)
, which can be easily seen from the calculation

\displaystyle\left|\left|B\right|\right|_{L_{p}}

\displaystyle\leq D\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\penalty\ 2^{-n\kappa}\left(\left|\left|B_{n}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}+\sum_{m\geq n}\left|\left|\hat{B}_{m}\right|\right|_{L_{p}}2^{-(m-n)(\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}+\tilde{\hat{r}})}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim D\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}2^{-n\kappa}\left(1+\sum_{m\geq n}2^{-(m-n)(\alpha-\frac{\left|S\right|}{p}+\tilde{\hat{r}})}\right)\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{C^{\alpha}(L_{p})}

\displaystyle\lesssim D\left|\left|f\right|\right|_{C^{\alpha}(L_{p})}\,.

∎
