Abstract
. In his classical paper [ 5 ] , Koornwinder studied a family of orthogonal polynomials of two variables, derived from symmetric polynomials. This family possesses a rare property that orthogonal polynomials of degree n n have n ⁡ ( n + 1 ) / 2 n(n+1)/2 real common zeros, which leads to important examples in the theory of minimal cubature rules. This paper aims to give an account of the minimal cubature rules of two variables and examples originating from Koornwinder polynomials, and we will also provide further examples.

33C50, 65D32

1.
Introduction

The two families of orthogonal polynomials of two variables introduced by Koornwinder in
[
5
,
6
]
,
derived via symmetric polynomials, are important for minimal cubature rules. We discuss the impact of the first
family in this context.

A cubature rule is a finite linear combination of point evaluations that gives an approximation to a multivariate integral.
It is called a quadrature rule if the integral is of one variable. We are interested in the cubature rules of two variables.
Let
\Omega
be a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and let
{\mathcal{I}}(f)
be an integral on
\Omega
,

{\mathcal{I}}f=\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{x}})W({\mathbf{x}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}},\qquad{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2}),

where
W
is a fixed nonnegative function on
\Omega
. Let
m
be a positive integer. A cubature rule,
{\mathsf{C}}(f)
,
of degree of precision
m
is given by

{\mathsf{C}}(f)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{\lambda}_{k}f({\mathbf{x}}_{k}),\quad\hbox{such that}\quad{\mathcal{I}}f={\mathsf{C}}(f),\quad\forall f\in\Pi_{m}^{2},

where
\Pi_{m}^{2}
denote the space of algebraic polynomials of degree
m
in two variables. The points
{\mathbf{x}}_{k}
are
called nodes and
{\lambda}_{k}
weights of the cubature rule. Cubature rules are essential for discretizing integrals and provide
accurate numerical approximation to integrals. The most useful one has a high degree of precision, equivalently,
has fewer nodes for a fixed degree of precision. A cubature rule is called
minimal
if the number of nodes
is the smallest among all cubature rules of the same degree for
{\mathcal{I}}f
.

For integral in one variable, the Gauss quadrature rule of degree
2n-1
is minimal with
n
nodes and exists
for every integral
{\mathcal{I}}f
on an interval. Moreover, its nodes are zeros of the orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
.
The same, however, does not hold for cubature rules. It is known that the number of nodes,
N
, of a cubature rule
of degree
2n-1
satisfies a lower bound (
[
12
,
16
]
)

(1.1)

N\geq\operatorname{dim}\Pi_{n-1}^{2}=\binom{n+1}{2}.

We call a cubature rule that attains the lower bound Gauss cubature rule. It is known that a Gauss cubature
rule of degree
2n-1
exists if, and only if, its nodes are common zeros of all orthogonal polynomials of degree
n

in two variables, which means that
n+1
polynomial curves of degree
n
intersects at
\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
real points.
Thus, it is not surprising that Gause cubature rules exist rarely, They do not exist, for example, if
W
and
\Omega

are symmetric with respect to the origin, called centrally symmetric. Moreover, only two families of Gauss cubature
rules are known to exist for integrals of two variables
[
7
,
14
]
. In both cases, the corresponding orthogonal
polynomials are first studied by Koornwinder in his classical papers
[
5
]
and
[
6
]
based on symmetric
functions.

For the centrally symmetric case, the nonexistence of the Gauss cubature rules can be seen from a lower bound for
the number of nodes stronger than that of (
1.1
). The bound is called Möller’s lower bound
[
9
]

and it states that if
W
and
\Omega
are centrally symmetric, then the number of nodes
N
of a cubature rule of
degree
2n-1
for
{\mathcal{I}}(f)
satisfies

(1.2)

N\geq\binom{n+1}{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor.

2.
Koornwinder polynomials and Gauss cubature rules

Let
W
be a nonnegative weight function on a compact domain
\Omega
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
. We consider orthogonal polynomials
with respect to the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{W}=\int_{\Omega}f(x_{1},x_{2})W(x_{1},x_{2}){\textnormal{d}}x_{1}{\textnormal{d}}x_{2}.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{2}
be the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
. Then
\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{2}=n+1
as shown by applying
the Gram-Schmidt process on
\{x_{1}^{n},x_{1}^{n-1}x_{2},\ldots,x_{1}x_{2}^{n-1},x_{2}^{n}\}
. We refer to properties of orthogonal
polynomials in two or more variables to
[
4
]
.

We first recall the definition of the Koornwinder polynomials. There are two families of these polynomials,
both coming from symmetric polynomials. We shall consider only one family, first studied in
[
5
]
.

2.1.
Koornwinder polynomials

Let
w
be a nonnegative weight function on
[-1,1]
. The mapping
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2})
, given by

(2.1)

u_{1}=x_{1}+x_{2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad u_{2}=x_{1}x_{2},

maps the triangle
\triangle=\{(x_{1},x_{2}):x_{1}\leq x_{2}\}
, which is half of
[-1,1]^{2}
, one-to-one and onto the domain

\Omega
give by

(2.2)

\Omega=\left\{(u_{1},u_{2}):u_{1}^{2}\geq 4u_{2},1+u_{2}\geq|u_{1}|,\quad-2\leq u_{1}\leq 2\right\},

which is bounded by two lines and a parabola. For
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
, let
W_{\gamma}
be the weight function defined
on
\Omega
by

(2.3)

{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}(u_{1},u_{2})=w(x_{1})w(x_{2})(u_{1}^{2}-4u_{2})^{\gamma},\quad(u_{1},u_{2})\in\Omega

under the mapping (
2.1
). Since the map
{\mathbf{u}}\mapsto{\mathbf{x}}
maps
\Omega
to
\triangle
that is half of the
product domain
[-1,1]^{2}
, we obtain

(2.4)

\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{u}}){\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}({\mathbf{u}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\triangle}f(x_{1}+x_{2},x_{1}x_{2})w(x_{1})w(x_{2})|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2{\gamma}+1}{\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}},

since the Jacobian of the map is
|x_{1}-x_{2}|=\sqrt{u_{1}^{2}-4u_{2}}
.

Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma})
denote the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
with respect to
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}
on
the domain
\Omega
. As shown in
[
5
]
, an orthogonal basis can be given explicitly when

{\gamma}=\pm\frac{1}{2}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
p_{n}=p_{n}(w)
be the orthonormal polynomial of degree
n
with respect to
w
. Under the mapping (
2.1
),
define

(2.5)

P_{k}^{n,-\frac{1}{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\begin{cases}p_{n}(x_{1})p_{k}(x_{2})+p_{n}(x_{2})p_{k}(x_{1}),&k<n,\\
\sqrt{2}p_{n}(x_{1})p_{n}(x_{2}),&k=n.\end{cases}

Then
\{P_{k}^{n,-\frac{1}{2}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathcal{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2}})
. Moreover, under the mapping
(
2.1
), define

(2.6)

P_{k}^{n,\frac{1}{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\frac{p_{n+1}(x_{1})p_{k}(x_{2})-p_{n+1}(x_{2})p_{k}(x_{1})}{x_{1}-x_{2}},\quad 0\leq k\leq n.

Then
\{P_{k}^{n,\frac{1}{2}}:0\leq k\leq n\}
is an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathcal{W}}_{\frac{1}{2}})
.

Example 2.2
.

If
w(t)=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}
is the Jacobi weight function on
[-1,1]
, then the weight
W_{\gamma}
becomes

{\mathcal{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}(u_{1},u_{2})=(1-u_{1}+u_{2})^{\alpha}(1+u_{1}+u_{2})^{\beta}(u_{1}^{2}-4u_{2})^{\gamma},\quad{\alpha},{\beta}>-1,

on the domain
\Omega
given in (
2.2
), since
(1\pm x_{1})(1\pm x_{2})=1\pm u_{1}+u_{2}
.

The above definition is also valid if the support of
w
is unbounded.

2.2.
Gauss cubature rules

Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=\{P_{1}^{n},\ldots,P_{n}^{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis for the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{2}
. A zero of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
is a
point
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
that is a zero of every element in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{2}
. The zeros of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
characterize the Gauss
cubature rule of degree
2n-1

[
12
]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

A Gauss cubature exists if, and only if,
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
has
\operatorname{dim}\Pi_{n-1}^{2}
real, distinct zeros.

If we regard
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
as a column vector, then the orthogonal polynomials satisfy a three-term relation in a vector-matrix form

[
4
, Chapter 3]

x_{i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}=A_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n+1}+B_{n,i}{\mathbb{P}}_{n}+A_{n-1,i}^{\mathsf{T}}{\mathbb{P}}_{n-1},\qquad i=1,2,

where
A_{n,i}:(n+1)\times(n+2)
and
B_{n,i}:(n+1)\times(n+1)
are called structural matrices. If
W
and
\Omega

are centrally symmetric, then
B_{n,i}=0
. The structural matrices can be used to study the zeros of
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}

[
18
]
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
be a basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{2}
. Then
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}
has
\operatorname{dim}\Pi_{n-1}^{2}
real zeros if and only if

(2.7)

A_{n-1,1}A_{n-1,2}^{\mathsf{T}}=A_{n-1,2}A_{n-1,1}^{\mathsf{T}}.

In particular, the Gauss cubature rule for
W
exists if and only if (
2.7
) holds.

Since matrices do not usually commute, the Gauss cubature rules exist rarely. The first families of such rules are given in

[
14
]
based on the zeros of Koornwinder polynomials. Let
P_{k}^{n,\pm\frac{1}{2}}
be the orthogonal polynomials
for
W_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}
given in Proposition
2.1
. We write

{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}=\left(P_{0}^{n,\pm\frac{1}{2}},\ldots P_{n}^{n,\pm\frac{1}{2}}\right).

Let
t_{k,n}
,
1\leq k\leq n
, be the zeros of
p_{n}(w)
and define

u_{j,k}^{(n)}=t_{j,n}+t_{k,n}\quad\hbox{and}\quad v_{j,k}^{(n)}=t_{j,n}t_{k,n},\quad 1\leq k,j\leq n.

Proposition 2.5
.

The polynomials in
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
have
\binom{n+1}{2}
common zeros given by

(2.8)

Z_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}:=\left\{\left(u_{j,k}^{(n)},v_{j,k}^{(n)}\right):1\leq j\leq k\leq n\right\}

and the polynomials in
{\mathbb{P}}_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}
have
\binom{n+1}{2}
common zeros given by

(2.9)

Z_{n}^{\frac{1}{2}}:=\left\{\left(u_{j,k}^{(n+1)},v_{j,k}^{(n+1)}\right):1\leq j\leq k-1,\,\,k\leq n\right\}.

This can be deduced from the explicit formulas of
P_{k}^{n,\pm\frac{1}{2}}
given in (
2.5
) and
(
2.6
). For example, in the case of
P_{k}^{n,-\frac{1}{2}}
, we see that every polynomial in the
right-hand side of (
2.5
) contains a
p_{n}(w;x_{1})
or
p_{n}(w_{2})
, so that it vanishes on

(t_{j,n},t_{k,n})
and, consequently, the left-hand side vanishes on
(u_{j,k}^{(n)},v_{j,k}^{(n)})
under (
2.1
).
By Theorem
2.3
, this proposition shows that the Gauss cubature
rules exist for
W_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}
on the domain
\Omega
. In
[
14
]
, the condition (
2.7
) is
verified. The Gauss cubature rules can be given explicitly. Let the Gauss quadrature rule for
w
be given by

\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)w(t){\textnormal{d}}t=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\lambda}_{k,n}f(t_{k,n}),\qquad\forall f\in\Pi_{2n-1}.

Theorem 2.6
.

For
{\mathcal{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2}}
on the domain
\Omega
, the Gaussian cubature rule of degree
2n-1
is given by

(2.10)

\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{u}}){\mathcal{W}}_{-\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbf{u}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{u}}=2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mathop{{\sum}^{\prime}}_{j=1}^{k}{\lambda}_{k,n}{\lambda}_{j,n}f\left(u_{j,k}^{(n)},v_{j,k}^{(n)}\right),\quad f\in\Pi_{2n-1}^{2},

where
{\sum}^{\prime}
means that the term for
j=k
is divided by 2. For
{\mathcal{W}}_{\frac{1}{2}}
on the domain

\Omega
, the Gaussian cubature rule of degree
2n-1
is given by

(2.11)

\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{u}}){\mathcal{W}}_{\frac{1}{2}}({\mathbf{u}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{u}}=2\sum_{k=2}^{n+1}\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}{\lambda}_{j,k}^{(n+1)}f\left(u_{j,k}^{(n+1)},v_{j,k}^{(n+1)}\right),\quad f\in\Pi_{2n-1}^{2},

where
\lambda_{j,k}^{(n+1)}={\lambda}_{j,n+1}{\lambda}_{k,n+1}(t_{j,n+1}-t_{k,n+1})^{2}
.

In the case that
w=w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}
is the Jacobi weight, the weight function
W_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}=W_{{\alpha},{\beta},\pm\frac{1}{2}}

is given in Example
2.2
. In particular, for
{\alpha}={\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}
, the weight function is

W_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}(u_{1},u_{2})=\left((1+u_{2})^{2}-u_{1}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}(u_{1}^{2}-u_{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

2n-1
with

3.
Minimal cubature rules and orthogonal polynomials

Recall that a cubature rule for the integral
{\mathcal{I}}f
is minimal if it has the smallest number of nodes among all cubature rules
of the same degree for
{\mathcal{I}}f
. We discuss minimal cubature rules and give a family of examples on the square, which are
associated with orthogonal polynomials originating from the Koornwinder polynomials

3.1.
Minimal cubature rules and Möller’s lower boound

We need to know a lower bound for the number of nodes to verify that a cubature rule is
minimal. If it exists, the Gauss cubature rule is minimal since its number of nodes attains the lower bound (
1.1
).

An important result by Möller
[
9
]
gave an improved lower bound for the number of nodes of a cubature rule
of degree
2n-1
, which can be made explicit in the centrally symmetric setting. The lower bound is formulated in

[
17
]
via the structural matrices of orthogonal polynomials and the result is given as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

The number of nodes of a cubature rule of degree
2n-1
satisfies

(3.1)

N\geq\operatorname{dim}\Pi_{n-1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{rank}\left[A_{n-1,1}A_{n-1,2}^{\mathsf{T}}-A_{n-1,2}A_{n-1,1}^{\mathsf{T}}\right].

In particular, if
W
and
\Omega
are centrally symmetric, then

(3.2)

N\geq\operatorname{dim}\Pi_{n-1}^{2}+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor=\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)+\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor.

The lower bound (
3.1
) can also be extended to several variables. There is another lower bound
that extends (
3.2
) to
d>2
in the centrally symmetric setting in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, also due to Möller but based
on a different approach. However, the most interesting case remains in two variables because
we do not know any family of cubature rules that attain Möller’s lower bound for
d>2
, whereas a large family
of examples is known for
d=2
on the square. To describe the result, we state a characterization of the cubature
rules that attain Möller’s lower bound in
[
10
]
when
d=2
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
W
and
\Omega
be centrally symmetric. A cubature rule of degree
2n-1
attains Möller’s lower
bound (
3.2
) if, and only if, its nodes are common zeros of
n+1-\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor

linearly independent orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
.

A characterization of when there are
n+1-\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
linearly independent orthogonal
polynomials of degree
n
that have
N_{\mathrm{min}}(n)
real nodes is given in
[
17
]
in terms of
a non-linear system of equations given via the structural matrices of orthogonal polynomials.

Based on the characterization in Theorem
3.2
, the first family of cubature rules of degree

2n-1
that attains the lower bound (
3.1
) appears in
[
11
]
, soon after
[
9
]
, for
the product Chebyshev weight on
[-1,1]^{2}
. Further examples centered around the product Chebyshev weight
functions have appeared in
[
1
,
3
,
13
,
17
]
, some based on different methods. However, the next group
of examples was obtained only recently in
[
19
]
, where a large collection of weight functions is initiated from
the orthogonality of the Koornwinder polynomials and proved to admit minimal cubature rules.

3.2.
A family of orthogonal polynomials on the square

Our examples of minimal cubature rules are established for the weight functions defined below. We consider
the relevant orthogonal polynomials in this subsection.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
w
be a weight function defined on
[-1,1]
. For
{\gamma}>-1
, we define
W_{\gamma}
on
[-1,1]^{2}
by

W_{\gamma}({\mathbf{x}})=w(\cos({\theta}_{1}-{\theta}_{2}))w(\cos({\theta}_{1}+{\theta}_{2}))\left(1-x_{1}^{2}\right)^{\gamma}\left(1-x_{2}^{2}\right)^{\gamma}\left|x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\right|,

where
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2})=(\cos{\theta}_{1},\cos{\theta}_{2})
with
0\leq{\theta}_{1},{\theta}_{2}\leq\pi
.

These weight functions are closely related to the weight functions
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}
defined on the curved domain
\Omega

in (
2.3
). As it is shown in Theorem
2.6
, the weight functions
{\mathcal{W}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}
admit
the Gaussian cubature rules of degree
2n-1
on the domain
\Omega
. The following observation was made in

[
20
]
, which connects the two weigh functions.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
w
is supported on
[-1,1]
, then

(3.3)

W_{\gamma}({\mathbf{x}}):=4^{\gamma}{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}\left(2x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-1\right)\left|x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}\right|,

so that
W_{\gamma}
is centrally symmetric. Moreover, for integrable function
f
on
\Omega
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\int_{[-1,1]^{2}}f({\mathbf{u}}){\mathcal{W}}_{{\gamma}}({\mathbf{u}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{u}}=4\int_{{\mathfrak{s}}(w)^{2}}f\left(2x_{1}x_{2},x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-1\right)W_{{\gamma}}({\mathbf{x}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{x}}.

Example 3.5
.

If
w
is the Jacobi weight function
w(t)=w_{{\alpha},{\beta}}(t)=(1-t)^{\alpha}(1+t)^{\beta}
,
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
, then, using the identity

(1\pm\cos({\theta}_{1}-{\theta}_{2}))(1\pm\cos({\theta}_{1}+{\theta}_{2}))=(x_{1}\pm x_{2})^{2}
, the weight function
W_{\gamma}
becomes

W_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}
defined by

W_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}({\mathbf{x}})=|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{2{\alpha}+1}|x_{1}+x_{2}|^{2{\beta}+1}\left(1-x_{1}^{2}\right)^{{\gamma}}\left(1-x_{2}^{2}\right)^{{\gamma}}

for
{\mathbf{x}}\in[-1,1]^{2}
, where
{\alpha},{\beta}>-1
and
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
. Correspondingly the weight
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}

becomes the weight function
{\mathcal{W}}_{{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}}
in (
3.3
).

The relation (
3.4
) allows us to derive orthogonal polynomials for
W_{\gamma}
through the Koornwinder polynomials.
Using the integral relation (
3.4
), the orthogonal polynomials associated with
W_{\gamma}
on
[-1,1]^{2}
can be
deduced in terms of those associated with
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}
and three other related weight functions on the domain
\Omega

[
20
]
. While the weight function
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}
in (
2.3
) is defined via

, the other weight functions are

(1-x^{2})w(x)
,
(1-x)w(x)
, and
(1+x)w(x)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}^{(1,1)}({\mathbf{u}})&:=(1-u_{1}+u_{2})(1+u_{1}+u_{2}){\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}({\mathbf{u}}),\\
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}^{(1,0)}({\mathbf{u}})&:=(1-u_{1}+u_{2}){\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}({\mathbf{u}}),\\
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}^{(0,1)}({\mathbf{u}})&:=(1+u_{1}+u_{2}){\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}({\mathbf{u}}),\end{split}\qquad{\mathbf{u}}\in\Omega,

since
1-u_{1}+u_{2}=(1-x_{1})(1-x_{2})
and
1+u_{1}+u_{2}=(1+x_{1})(1+x_{2})
under (

).
Let
\{P_{k,n}^{({\gamma})}:0\leq k\leq n\}
be an orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathcal{W}}_{{\gamma}})
under the inner product

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{{\mathcal{W}}_{{\gamma}}}=\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{u}})g({\mathbf{u}}){\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}({\mathbf{u}}){\textnormal{d}}{\mathbf{u}}.

We further denote by
P_{k,n}^{({\gamma}),1,1}
,
P_{k,n}^{({\gamma}),1,0,}
,
P_{k,n}^{({\gamma}),0,1}
the
orthogonal polynomials of degree
n
with respect to
{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathcal{W}}
for
{\mathcal{W}}={\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}^{(1,1)}
,

{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}^{(1,0)}
,
{\mathcal{W}}_{\gamma}^{(0,1)}
, respectively.

Proposition 3.6
.

An orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{2n}(W_{{\gamma}})
is given by

\left\{{}_{1}Q_{k,2n}^{({\gamma})}:0\leq k\leq n\right\}\bigcup\left\{{}_{2}Q_{k,2n}^{({\gamma})}:0\leq k\leq n-1\right\}
