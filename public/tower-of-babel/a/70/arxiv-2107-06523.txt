Abstract
. We show that any sequence ( x n ) n ∈ ℕ ⊆ [ 0 , 1 ] (x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1] that has Poissonian correlations of k k – th order is uniformly distributed, also providing a quantitative description of this phenomenon. Additionally, we extend connections between metric correlations and additive energy, already known for pair correlations, to higher orders. Furthermore, we examine how the property of Poissonian k k – th correlations is reflected in the asymptotic size of the moments of the function F ( t , s , N ) = # { n ⩽ N : ∥ x n − t ∥ ⩽ s / ( 2 N ) } , t ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] . F(t,s,N)=\#\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x_{n}-t\|\leqslant s/(2N)\},\,t\in[0,1].

1.
Introduction

1.1.
The main results

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
be a sequence and
k\geqslant 2
be an integer. Given a compactly supported test function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to[0,\infty)
we define the
k
– th order correlation function
of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with respect to
f
to be

(1)

R_{k}(f,N)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054ptf\left(N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{3}})\!),\ldots,N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)\right).

Here
(\!(x)\!)
denotes the signed distance of
x
from the origin modulo
1
(see Section
1.4
for a proper definition).

We say that the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has
Poissonian
k
– th order correlations
if

(2)

\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{k}(f,N)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\qquad\text{for any }f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{k-1}).

A discussion on equivalent definitions of Poissonian correlations appearing in the literature can be found in Appendix A. There we explain that a sequence has Poissonian
k
– th order correlations if and only if (
2
) holds for any
f
which is the characteristic function of some rectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{k-1}.

Having Poissonian
k
– th order correlations can be viewed as a pseudo-randomness property of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the following sense: when
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent, uniformly distributed random variables in
[0,1]
, then almost surely, the sequence
(Y_{n}(\omega))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian
k
– th order correlations (see Appendix B for a proof).

The
k
– th order correlations of a sequence are a local asymptotic statistics of the gaps of a sequence. Another closely related statistics is the asymptotic gap distribution of the sequence
[
14
]
. It is known that when a sequence has Poissonian correlations of all orders
k\geqslant 2,
then the asymptotic distribution of its gaps is also Poissonian; a proof can be found in
[
14
, Appendix A]
.

The term
Poissonian
comes from the fact that (
2
) is in accordance with the almost sure statistical behavior of gaps between random points coming from a Poisson process. Originally, the motivation for studying the gap statistics of point sequences came from theoretical physics, where the Berry – Tabor conjecture predicts that the spacings of the energy eigenvalues of generic integrable quantum systems follow the Poissonian model (see
[
17
]
for a survey in mathematical language). For some quantum systems the sequence of energy eigenvalues follows a simple arithmetic formula, but establishing the correlations to be Poissonian is usually a very substantial challenge (that often becomes more and more difficult as the order of the correlations increases). For some contributions concerning sequences of cognizable physical origin see for example
[
10
,
18
,
25
]
. However, the subject has also gained significant interest on a purely mathematical level, where the correlations of general sequences of arithmetic origin were studied; see for example
[
2
,
3
,
21
]
. Most results only concern the case of correlations of order
k=2
(known as
pair correlations
), while correlations of higher order are combinatorially and analytically more difficult to study and often out of reach; among the relatively few results in that direction are
[
23
]
and
[
29
]
.

A topic of particular interest has been the connection of Poissonian pair correlations with uniform distribution properties. Recall that a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
is called
uniformly distributed
if for any
0\leqslant a<b\leqslant 1
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant b\}=b-a.

To be more specific, it has been shown that when a sequence has Poissonian pair correlations it is also uniformly distributed.

Theorem A.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
be a sequence. If
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian pair correlations, then
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly distributed.

Theorem A was proved independently by Aistleitner, Lachmann and Pausinger
[
1
]
and by Grepstad and Larcher
[
9
]
. Additional proofs were given later by Steinerberger in
[
26
]

and, in a much more general setup, by Marklof
[
16
]
. These four proofs are all essentially different.

The authors of
[
1
]
also prove a quantitative version of Theorem A that is interesting in its own right. Before we present this version of Theorem A, recall that a function
G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is called
an asymptotic distribution function
of a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
if there exists a strictly increasing sequence of integers
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

(3)

G(x)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}0\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant x\},\qquad 0\leqslant x\leqslant 1.

By the Helly selection principle, every sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
has at least one asymptotic distribution function (see e.g.
[
12
, Ch. 1, Thm. 7.1]
). Whenever
G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is the unique asymptotic distribution function of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, that is, whenever

G(x)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}0\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant x\}

holds for all
x\in[0,1]
, we will simply refer to it as the asymptotic distribution function of
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

The quantitative version of Theorem A is the following statement.

Theorem B.

Assume that the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
has the unique asymptotic distribution function
G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}.
Assume also that there is a function
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,\infty]
such that

F(s)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\Big\{k\neq\ell\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x_{k}-x_{\ell}\|\leqslant\frac{s}{N}\Big\},\qquad s>0.

Then the following hold:

(i)

If
G
is not absolutely continuous, then
F(s)=\infty
for all
s>0
.

(ii)

If
G
is absolutely continuous, then

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{F(s)}{2s}\geqslant\int_{0}^{1}g(x)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x

where
g
is the density function of the corresponding measure (that is,
g=G^{\prime}
almost everywhere).

As a main result of this paper, we prove that Theorem A can be generalised to sequences with Poissonian correlations of order
k\geqslant 2
: having Poissonian correlations of any order
k\geqslant 2
is a stronger property than uniform distribution.

Theorem 1.1
.

If the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
has Poissonian
k
– correlations for some
k\geqslant 2
, then it is uniformly distributed.

In fact, with a little more effort than in the proof of Theorem
1.1
we are able to prove a stronger result that can be viewed as a generalisation of Theorem B in the context of
k
– th order correlations. In what follows, for
s>0
we shall write
R_{k}(s,N)=R_{k}(f_{s},N)
where
f_{s}
is the test function
f_{s}=\mathds{1}_{[-s,s]^{k-1}}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
,
G\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be an asymptotic distribution function of
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(N_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence as in (
3
). Then the following hold:

(i)

If
G
is not absolutely continuous, then
\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}R_{k}(s,N_{j})=\infty
for all
s>0
.

(ii)

If
G
is absolutely continuous, then

(4)

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\frac{\limsup\limits_{j\to\infty}R_{k}(s,N_{j})}{(2s)^{k-1}}\geqslant\int_{0}^{1}g(x)^{k}\,\mathrm{d}x,

where
g
is the density function of the corresponding measure.

To see why Theorem
1.2
is indeed a stronger version of Theorem
1.1
, observe that if
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian
k
– th correlations but is not uniformly distributed, it will have an asymptotic distribution function
G
whose density function
g
is not constantly equal to
1
(the existence of
g
follows from (i) of Theorem
1.2
). Therefore
\int_{0}^{1}g(x)^{k}\mathrm{d}x>1
and (
4
) leads to a contradiction.

The reader might spot two subtle differences between Theorem B and its generalisation, Theorem
1.2
. First, in Theorem
1.2
we assume that
G
is an asymptotic distribution function of
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, not necessarily unique as in Theorem B. Second, we do not require that the limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{k}(s,N)
exists, but instead, we work with the term
\limsup_{j\to\infty}R_{k}(s,N_{j})
. The additional assumptions in Theorem B are not essential, and the proof in
[
1
]
can be easily modified under the slightly weaker hypotheses of Theorem
1.2
. These minor modifications also make Theorem B an actually stronger result than Theorem A.

In the proof of both Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, we shall make use of several variants of the correlation function
R_{k}(f,N)
that was defined in (
1
). To be more specific, given some scales
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k-1}>0
we define the correlation function
R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
by

(5)

R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)=\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{\parbox{70.0001pt}{$i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N$ \\
$i_{j}\neq i_{\ell}\,\forall j\neq\ell$}\hskip-14.22636pt\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\,\|x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{r+1}}\|\leqslant\frac{s_{r}}{N}\,\,\,(1\leqslant r<k)\right\}

(where
\|x\|
denotes the distance of
x\in\mathbb{R}
to its nearest integer). Since we can write
R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
as
R_{k}(\mathds{1}_{B},N)
where
B
is the rectangle
[-s_{1},s_{1}]\times\ldots\times[-s_{k-1},s_{k-1}],
it follows from (
2
) and an approximation argument that sequences with Poissonian
k
– th correlations also satisfy

\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)=(2s_{1})\cdots(2s_{k-1})\quad\text{ for all }s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}>0

(see also Appendix A for a discussion).

Furthermore, we define the correlation function
R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
by

(6)

\displaystyle R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)=\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{r+1}}\|\leqslant\frac{s_{r}}{N}\,\,(1\leqslant r<k)\right\}.

That is, in the definition of
R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
we allow indices to be equal. We will also make use of the appropriate averages of
R_{k}
and
R_{k}^{*}
defined as

C_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)=\mathop{\iint\ldots\int}_{B(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1})}\hskip-2.84526ptR_{k}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k-1},N)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}\ldots\mathrm{d}\sigma_{k-1}

and

C_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)=\mathop{\iint\ldots\int}_{B(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1})}\hskip-2.84526ptR_{k}^{*}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k-1},N)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}\sigma_{2}\ldots\mathrm{d}\sigma_{k-1},

where
B(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1})
denotes the rectangle
[0,s_{1}]\times[0,s_{2}]\times\ldots\times[0,s_{k-1}].

Finally, when the scales
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}
are all equal to
s>0
we write for simplicity

\begin{gathered}R_{k}(s,N)=R_{k}(s,\ldots,s,N),\qquad R_{k}^{*}(s,N)=R_{k}^{*}(s,\ldots,s,N),\\
C_{k}(s,N)=C_{k}(s,\ldots,s,N),\qquad C_{k}^{*}(s,N)=C_{k}^{*}(s,\ldots,s,N).\end{gathered}

We note that the definition of
R_{k}(s,N)
here agrees with the definition given right before Theorem
1.2
.

1.2.
Some consequences of the main results

In the course of the proofs of the stated theorems, we examine the relations between the correlation functions of different orders for a given sequence. These relations enable us to prove that when a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
fails to have Poissonian
k
– th order correlations in a very strong sense, then it will also not have Poissonian correlations of any higher order.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
be a sequence and assume that for some scales
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}>0
the
k
– th order correlation function
R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
satisfies

\limsup_{N\to\infty}R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)=\infty.

Then the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not have Poissonian
p
– th order correlations for any
p\geqslant k.

Theorem
1.3
is one of the key ingredients we will use to derive new results on the
metric theory
of Poissonian correlations: an increasing sequence
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is fixed, and we study the Lebesgue measure of
x\in[0,1]
such that the sequence
(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian correlations. As with the correlations of fixed sequences, most of the metric results to date are concerned with the
k=2
case.

In the metric setup, the authors of
[
2
]
have established several statements on the connection of Poissonian correlations with the notion of
additive energy
. Recall that the additive energy of a finite set
A
is defined as

E(A)=\#\{a,b,c,d\in A\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a+b=c+d\}

(see
[
27
, Chapter 2]
for more details). Writing
\mathcal{A}_{N}=(a_{n})_{n\leqslant N}
for the set of the first
N
elements of
\mathcal{A},
Aistleitner, Larcher & Lewko
[
2
]

proved that whenever
E(\mathcal{A}_{N})=\mathcal{O}(N^{3-\varepsilon}),N\to\infty
for some
\varepsilon>0,
then the sequence
(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian pair correlations for almost all
x\in[0,1].
Examining to what extent this bound on the additive energy is optimal, Bourgain
[
2
, Appendix]
showed that if
E(\mathcal{A}_{N})=\Omega(N^{3}),N\to\infty
then
(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not have Poissonian pair correlations for all
x
in a subset of
[0,1]
of positive Lebesgue measure, while on the other hand there exists a sequence
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with
E(\mathcal{A}_{N})=o(N^{3}),N\to\infty
such that
(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not have Poissonian pair correlations for almost all
x\in[0,1].

In the present paper, we are able to deduce an analogue of the first – mentioned result of Bourgain, proving that when the additive energy of
\mathcal{A}
is of maximal order of magnitude, then the sequence
(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
fails to have Poissonian triple correlations (i.e. of order
k=3
) for Lebesgue almost all
x
. Further, as mentioned previously, we use Theorem
1.3
to generalise the second – mentioned result of Bourgain for
k
– th order correlations.

Theorem 1.4
.

(i) There exists a set
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
with additive energy
E(\mathcal{A}_{N})=o(N^{3}),N\to\infty
such that for Lebesgue almost all
x\in[0,1]
the sequence
(a_{n}x)_{n=1}^{\infty}
does not have Poissonian correlations of any order
k\geqslant 2.

(ii) Let
\mathcal{A}=(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a sequence such that
E(\mathcal{A}_{N})=\Omega(N^{3})
as
N\to\infty
. Then for all
x
in a set of positive Lebesgue measure,
(a_{n}x)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
does not have Poissonian triple correlations.

1.3.
Poissonian Correlations and the number of points in small intervals

As a final result of this paper, we seek to exhibit a connection of the property of Poissonian k – th correlations with the number of elements of a sequence in sufficiently small intervals. More formally, given
s>0
and
N\geqslant 1
we define

(7)

F(t,s,N)=\#\Big\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x_{n}-t\|\leqslant\frac{s}{2N}\Big\},\qquad 0\leqslant t\leqslant 1.

Heuristically, if
t
is seen as a random variable uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
, then
F(t)=F(t,s,N)
can be viewed as the number of points of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in a random interval of length
s/N
.

Our purpose is to establish a link between the property of Poissonian
k
– th correlations and the asymptotic size of the
k
– th moment of
F(t,s,N)
. For the
k=2
case, it is already known that a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian pair correlations if and only if

\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}F(t,s,N)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=s^{2}+s\qquad\text{ for all }s>0.

This is shown in
[
15
]
and also implicitly in
[
10
, Thm 3(i)]
. Regarding correlations of higher orders, some relevant results are shown in
[
28
]
for the triple correlations of sequences of the form
(n^{2}\alpha)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and for values of the length
s>0
that lie in a range that depends on
N
.

We hereby consider both the
k
– th moment of
F(t,s,N)
as well as its
k
– th factorial moment. To be more specific, given
s>0,N\geqslant 1
and
k\geqslant 2
we set

(8)

\displaystyle I_{k}(s,N)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}F(t,s,N)(F(t,s,N)-1)\cdots(F(t,s,N)-(k-1))\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle I_{k}^{*}(s,N)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}F(t,s,N)^{k}\,\mathrm{d}t.

We prove that the property of Poissonian correlations of
k
– th order is reflected in the asymptotic behaviour of
I_{k}(s,N)
and
I_{k}^{*}(s,N).

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
and
I_{k}(s,N),I_{k}^{*}(s,N)
be as in (
8
). Assume the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian
k
– th correlations. Then the following statements hold:

(i)
\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}I_{k}(s,N)=s^{k}
for all
s>0.

(ii)
\limsup\limits_{N\to\infty}I_{k}^{*}(s,N)=s^{k}+\mathcal{O}_{k}(s^{k-1}),\quad s\to\infty
.

(iii) If, in addition,
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian
\ell
– correlations for all
\ell\leqslant k
, then

(9)

\lim_{N\to\infty}I_{k}^{*}(s,N)=s^{k}+c_{k,k-1}s^{k-1}+\ldots+c_{k,1}s,

where
c_{k,i},i=1,\ldots,k-1
denote the Stirling numbers of the second kind.

Theorem
1.5
provides further evidence for the connection between sequences with Poissonian correlations on the one hand, and random variables that follow the Poisson distribution on the other. It is known
[
20
]
that the
k
– th factorial moment of a random variable following the Poisson distribution with parameter
s>0
is equal to
s^{k}
, while its
k
– th moment is equal to the polynomial on the right – hand side of (
9
) (this is called the Bell polynomial of degree
k,
see also
[
5
]
).

1.4.
Notation

Given two functions
f,g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},
we write
f(t)=\mathcal{O}(g(t)),t\to\infty,

f(t)=o(g(t)),t\to\infty
and
f(t)=\Omega(g(t)),t\to\infty
when

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{|f(t)|}{|g(t)|}<\infty,\quad\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{f(t)}{g(t)}=0\quad\text{ or }\quad\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{f(t)}{g(t)}>0

respectively. Any dependence of the value of the limsup above on potential parameters is denoted by the appropriate subscripts in the
\mathcal{O}
– symbol. Given a real number
x\in\mathbb{R},
we write
\{x\}
for the fractional part of
x
,
\|x\|=\min\{|x-k|\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}k\in\mathbb{N}\}
for the distance of
x
from its nearest integer, and

(\!(x)\!)=\begin{cases}\{x\},&\text{ if }0\leqslant\{x\}\leqslant\tfrac{1}{2}\\
\{x\}-1,&\text{ if }\tfrac{1}{2}<\{x\}<1\end{cases}

for the signed distance of
x
from the origin modulo
1
. Further, we use the symbol
\{\,\cdot\,\}^{+}
for the function

\{x\}^{+}=\begin{cases}x,&\text{ if }x\geqslant 0\\
0,&\text{ if }x<0.\end{cases}

Throughout the paper, we shall implicitly consider the unit interval
[0,1]
equipped with the topology induced by
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\,\cdot\,\right\rVert}
because we deal with distribution of sequences modulo
1
. This is homeomorphic to the interval
[0,1)
with the same topology, so for convenience, we will work interchangingly with
[0,1]
and
[0,1)
.

We use the standard notation
e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}.
We also write
B(x_{0},r)=\{x\in[0,1]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x-x_{0}\|\leqslant r\}
for the interval with center
x_{0}
and length
2r
modulo
1
. The characteristic function of a set
A
is denoted by
\mathds{1}_{A}.

1.5.
Directions for Further Research

We end this introductory part with some interesting questions that would shed more light on the properties of sequences with Poissonian correlations of
k
– th order.

\bullet
In Theorem
1.1
we proved that when a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian
k
– th correlations it is uniformly distributed, but we do not know whether the correlations of orders
m<k
also follow the Poissonian model. Are Poissonian correlations of order
k+1
a property stronger than Poissonian correlations of order
k
? In other words, does any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with Poissonian
k
– th correlations also have Poissonian correlations of all orders
2\leqslant m<k?

\bullet
We would like to know if some partial converse to Theorem
1.5
is true. Is it true, for example, that whenever (
9
) holds, the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian correlations of all orders up to
k
?

2.
Properties of the functions
R_{k},R_{k}^{*}
.

In the present section we prove several properties of the functions
R_{k}
and
R_{k}^{*}
defined in the introduction that will be used later in the proof of the main results.

We start by proving the inequality that will be the key ingredient in the proof of Proposition
2.2
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
m\geqslant 1.
For any
M\geqslant 1
and for all non-negative real numbers
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{M}\geqslant 0
we have

(10)

(x_{1}^{m+1}+x_{2}^{m+1}+\ldots+x_{M}^{m+1})\geqslant\frac{1}{M}(x_{1}+x_{2}+\ldots+x_{M})(x_{1}^{m}+x_{2}^{m}+\ldots+x_{M}^{m}).

Proof.

Applying the Hölder inequality with exponents
p=m+1
and
q=(m+1)/m
to the
M
–tuples
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{M})
and
(1,\ldots,1)
we get

(11)

x_{1}+x_{2}+\ldots+x_{M}\leqslant\left(x_{1}^{m+1}+\ldots+x_{M}^{m+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{m+1}}M^{\frac{m}{m+1}},

while the Hölder inequality with the same exponents applied to the
M
–tuples
(1,\ldots,1)
and
(x_{1}^{m},\ldots,x_{M}^{m})
yields

(12)

x_{1}^{m}+x_{2}^{m}+\ldots+x_{M}^{m}\leqslant\left(x_{1}^{m+1}+\ldots+x_{M}^{m+1}\right)^{\frac{m}{m+1}}M^{\frac{1}{m+1}}.

Multiplying (
11
) and (
12
), we obtain (
10
).∎

The following proposition provides a relation between correlation functions of different orders. This result will later have a key role in the proof of both Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, while it straightforwardly implies Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
be a sequence and
m\geqslant 2
. There exists a constant
s_{m}>0
such that for any
s>s_{m}
, the inequality

(13)

R_{m}\Big(\frac{s}{3},N\Big)\leqslant\dfrac{6}{s}R_{m+1}(s,N)

holds for all
N\geqslant N_{0}(s,m).
Moreover, the values of the constants
s_{m}
and
N_{0}
are independent of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

We partition the unit interval into pieces of size approximately
s/N
and count points in each interval: we set

K=K(s,N)=\left\lceil\frac{N}{s}\right\rceil

and for
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant K-1
we define

y_{\ell}=y_{\ell}(s,N)=\#\Big\{1\leqslant i\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i}\in\left[\tfrac{\ell s}{N},\tfrac{(\ell+1)s}{N}\right)\cap[0,1]\Big\}.

Observe that

\displaystyle R_{m+1}(s,N)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}y_{\ell}(y_{\ell}-1)\ldots(y_{\ell}-m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}\Big(y_{\ell}^{m+1}-c_{m}y_{\ell}^{m}+c_{m-1}y_{\ell}^{m-1}-\ldots+(-1)^{m}c_{1}y_{\ell}\Big)

with
c_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
.

First, we consider the case when
m
is odd.
Since
y_{0}+y_{1}+\ldots+y_{K-1}=N,
applying inequality (
10
) to (
2
) we obtain

R_{m+1}(s,N)\geqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}[(\tfrac{N}{K}-c_{m})y_{\ell}^{m}+(\tfrac{N}{K}c_{m-1}-c_{m-2})y_{\ell}^{m-2}+\ldots+(\tfrac{N}{K}c_{2}-c_{1})y_{\ell}].

Note that for any
\varepsilon>0
, we find that for
N
sufficiently large

\dfrac{N}{K}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)s
and therefore

R_{m+1}(s,N)\geqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}[((1-\varepsilon)s-c_{m})y_{\ell}^{m}+((1-\varepsilon)sc_{m-1}-c_{m-2})y_{\ell}^{m-2}+\ldots+((1-\varepsilon)sc_{2}-c_{1})y_{\ell}].

Similarly, for
m
even, we have

R_{m+1}(s,N)\geqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}[((1-\varepsilon)s-c_{m})y_{\ell}^{m}+((1-\varepsilon)sc_{m-1}-c_{m-2})y_{\ell}^{m-2}+\ldots+((1-\varepsilon)sc_{3}-c_{2})y_{\ell}^{2}+c_{1}y_{\ell}].

Now let
\varepsilon>0
be small enough such that
s(1-\varepsilon)>s_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=2\max\{c_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}1\leqslant i\leqslant m\}
. Then all non – leading terms are positive and hence we can estimate

R_{m+1}(s,N)\geqslant\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}((1-\varepsilon)s-c_{m})y_{\ell}^{m}\geqslant\frac{s}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}y_{\ell}^{m},

which implies that

(14)

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}y_{l}^{m}\leqslant\frac{2R_{m+1}(s,N)}{s}\cdot

We now seek an upper bound for
R_{m}(\frac{s}{3},N)
. For each
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant K-1
consider the sets

A_{\ell}=\left[\frac{s\ell}{N},\frac{s(\ell+1)}{N}\right),\qquad A_{\ell}^{\prime}=A_{\ell}+\frac{s}{3N},\qquad A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}=A_{\ell}+\frac{2s}{3N}\,

(where we understand the intervals modulo 1). Here we have essentially defined three different partitions of the unit interval: the partition
(A_{\ell})_{\ell=0}^{K-1}
we employed previously, and the partitions
(A_{\ell}^{\prime})_{\ell=0}^{K-1}
and
(A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime})_{\ell=0}^{K-1}
that we get by shifting the intervals of the first partition by
s/3N
and
2s/3N
respectively. Writing
y_{\ell}^{\prime},y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
for the number of points
x_{i}
in
A_{\ell}^{\prime}
and
A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
respectively, it is straightforward to show that an analogue of (
14
) holds for
(y_{\ell}^{\prime})_{\ell=0}^{K-1}
and
(y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime})_{\ell=0}^{K-1}
.

At this point, we need to employ the following Lemma, the proof of which we postpone for later in the text.

Lemma 2.3
.

If the
m
– tuple
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}})
contributes something to the sum

R_{m}(\frac{s}{3},N)
, then there exists some
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant K-1
such that the points
x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}}
belong all to
A_{\ell}
or to
A_{\ell}^{\prime}
or to
A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
.

Using Lemma
2.3
we finally obtain

\displaystyle R_{m}\Big(\frac{s}{3},N\Big)\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}y_{\ell}(y_{\ell}-1)\ldots(y_{\ell}-(m-1))+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}y_{\ell}^{\prime}(y_{\ell}^{\prime}-1)\ldots(y_{\ell}^{\prime}-(m-1))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}(y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}-1)\ldots(y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}-(m-1))

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell=0}^{K-1}\big(y_{\ell}^{m}+(y_{\ell}^{\prime})^{m}+(y_{\ell}^{\prime\prime})^{m}\big)\leqslant\frac{6}{s}R_{m+1}(s,N).

∎

We now provide the proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

Since the
m
– tuple
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}})
contributes to

R_{m}(\frac{s}{3},N)
, we have for all
j,k=1,2,\ldots,m
that

(15)

\lVert x_{i_{j}}-x_{i_{k}}\rVert\leqslant\lVert x_{i_{j}}-x_{i_{1}}\rVert+\lVert x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{k}}\rVert\leqslant\frac{2s}{3N}\cdot

For each
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant K-1
the set
A_{\ell}
can be written as a disjoint union

(16)

A_{\ell}=(A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime})\sqcup(A_{\ell}^{\prime}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime\prime})\sqcup(A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime})

where the three disjoint sets
A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime},A_{\ell}^{\prime}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime\prime}
and
A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
appearing are consecutive disjoint intervals of length
s/3N.

We may assume without loss of generality that the points
x_{i_{2}},x_{i_{3}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}}
are in increasing order; that is, the signed distances of differences of consecutive terms are
(\!(x_{i_{n+1}}-x_{i_{n}})\!)\geqslant 0
for
n=2,\ldots,m-1.

We consider two different cases regarding the relative position of
x_{i_{1}}
with respect to
x_{i_{2}}.
If
(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{1}})\!)\geqslant 0,
let
0\leqslant\ell\leqslant K-1
be such that
x_{i_{1}}\in A_{\ell}.
Then
x_{i_{1}}
lies in one of the three sets in the disjoint union in (
16
); assume without loss of generality
x_{i_{1}}\in A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime}.
Since
\|x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{m}}\|\leqslant 2s/(3N)
by (
15
) and the points
x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}}
are in increasing order, they will all lie in
A_{\ell}^{\prime}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime\prime}
or
A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
, and therefore they will all lie in
A_{\ell}.
Similarly, we see that if
x_{i_{1}}\in A_{\ell}^{\prime}\cap A_{\ell-1}^{\prime\prime}
then all points lie in
A_{\ell}^{\prime}
while if
x_{i_{1}}\in A_{\ell}\cap A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}
then they will all lie inside
A_{\ell}^{\prime\prime}.
If
(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!)>0
, we repeat the same argument with the point
x_{i_{2}}
in the place of
x_{i_{1}}.
The lemma is now proved.

∎

Remark
.

Proposition
2.2
provides an inequality involving the correlation functions
R_{k}(\tfrac{s}{3},N)
and
R_{k+1}(s,N)
only for values of the scale
s>0
that are large enough. This is not a restriction that comes from the method of proof followed, but rather a genuine obstruction, as can be seen by the following example. Define the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by

x_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{m}}\big(2\big\lceil\tfrac{k}{2}\big\rceil-1\big)\quad\text{whenever }n=2^{m}+k,\,m\geqslant 0\text{ and }1\leqslant k\leqslant 2^{m}.

That is,
x_{1}=x_{2}=0,

x_{3}=x_{4}=\frac{1}{2},

x_{5}=x_{6}=\frac{1}{4}
,
x_{7}=x_{8}=\frac{3}{4}
etc. Then for the choice of the scale
s<2
and for every integer of the form
N=2^{m}
we have

R_{2}(s,N)=1\quad\text{ but }\quad R_{3}(s,N)=0.

Using similar arguments we can define sequences for which
R_{m}(s,N)=1
and
R_{m+1}(s,N)=0
for some given
s>0
and for infinitely many
N\geqslant 1.
The upshot is that we cannot obtain an analogue of (
13
) that holds for all values of
s>0.

The next proposition connects the size of
R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
with the size of
R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
when the scales
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1}
are written in decreasing order.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
s_{1}\geqslant s_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant s_{k-1}>0
. Defining
R_{1}(s,N)=1
for all
s>0
and
N\geqslant 1
, we have

R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)\leqslant R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)+\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}b_{m}R_{m}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m-1},N),

where
b_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}\in\mathbb{N}
are constants depending only on
k
.

Proof.

Observe that if a
k
– tuple
(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})
consists of distinct indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N
then its contribution to
R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
defined in (
6
) is the same as its contribution to
R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N),
however the situation is different when the indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
are not pairwise distinct.

Any
k
– tuple
\mathbf{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})
gives rise to a uniquely determined partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{i}}=\{J_{1},J_{2},\ldots,J_{m}\}
of
[k]=\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
(where
m\leqslant k
) such that the following properties hold:

(i)

i_{j}=i_{\ell}\Leftrightarrow(j,\ell\in J_{t}\,\text{ for some }t\leqslant m)

(ii)

\min J_{i}<\min J_{i+1},\quad i=1,\ldots,m-1.

Conversely, given a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{J_{1},J_{2},\ldots,J_{m}\}
of
[k]
with the property that
\min J_{t}<\min J_{t+1}
for
t=1,2,\ldots,m-1
, we define the correlation counting function
R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}=R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)
by

R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}=\#\frac{1}{N}\left\{\mathbf{i}\in[N]^{k},\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{i}}=\mathcal{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lVert x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}}\rVert\leqslant\frac{s_{1}}{N},\ldots,\|x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{k}}\|\leqslant\frac{s_{k-1}}{N}\right\}.

That is,
R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}
is the variant of
R_{k}
that counts correlations only over indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N
with partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{i}}
equal to
\mathcal{P}.

Now if for a fixed partition
\mathcal{P}
as above we set

j_{r}=\min J_{r},\qquad r=1,2,\ldots,m

then we have that
j_{r}\geqslant r
. Write
J_{r}=\{t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{s}\}
for one of the sets comprising
\mathcal{P}.
In view of the hypothesis that
s_{1}\geqslant s_{2}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant s_{k-1}
, the inequalities

\|x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{t_{\ell}}}\|\leqslant\frac{s_{t_{\ell}-1}}{N},\qquad\ell=1,\ldots,s

appearing in the definition of
R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}},
altogether imply that

\|x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{t_{\ell}}}\|\leqslant\frac{s_{t_{\ell}-1}}{N}\leqslant\frac{s_{t_{1}-1}}{N}=\frac{s_{j_{r}-1}}{N}\leqslant\frac{s_{r-1}}{N},\quad\ell=1,\ldots,s.

Thus for the fixed partition
\mathcal{P}=\{J_{1},\ldots,J_{m}\}
as above, we have

\displaystyle R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)

\displaystyle\leqslant R_{m}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m-1},N).

Finally, for the counting function
R_{k}^{*}
, summing over all possible partitions
\mathcal{P}
of
[k]
we deduce that

\displaystyle R_{k}^{*}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}}R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|\mathcal{P}|=k}R_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)+\hskip-5.69054pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m\leqslant k-1\\
\mathcal{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|\mathcal{P}|=m\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054ptR_{k}^{\mathcal{P}}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)

\displaystyle\leqslant\,R_{k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1},N)+\sum_{m=1}^{k-1}b_{m}R_{m}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m-1},N),

with
b_{m}\in\mathbb{N},\,m=1,\ldots,k-1
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Here we prove that sequences with Poissonian correlations of
k
– th order are uniformly distributed.
We argue as in the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 2]
: if the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not uniformly distributed, then there exists some
a\in(0,1)
such that relation
\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i}\in[0,a]\}=a
fails, and by the Bolzano – Weierstrass theorem there exists a sequence
(N_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
and a number
b\neq a
such that

\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i}\in[0,a]\}=b.

We need the following lemma, which tells us that under the assumption of Poissonian correlations of
k
– th order, the proportion of points in a ball with sufficiently fast shrinking radius has to be asymptotically zero.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 2.
If
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence with Poissonian
k
– th correlations and
t\in[0,1],
then for any
s>0
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x_{n}-t\|\leqslant\frac{s}{2N}\Big\}=0.

Proof.

Assume for contradicition that there exist
t\in[0,1],s>0
and
\eta>0
such that

\frac{1}{N}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\|x_{n}-t\|\leqslant\frac{s}{2N}\Big\}\geqslant\eta\quad\text{ for inf. many }N\geqslant 1.

For such values of
N\geqslant 1
we have

\displaystyle R_{k}(s,N)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{N}\eta N\cdot(\eta N-1)\cdot\ldots\cdot(\eta N-(k-1))

\displaystyle=\eta^{k}N^{k-1}+\mathcal{O}(N^{k-2}),\qquad N\to\infty,

which contradicts the assumption of Poissonian
k
– th correlations.
∎

We can now apply Lemma
3.1
with
t=a
and
t=0
to deduce that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant a-\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i}\in[0,a]\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}0\leqslant x_{n}<\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a-\frac{s}{2N_{j}}<x_{n}\leqslant a\Big\}

\displaystyle\geqslant b-\varepsilon

and also

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{j}}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}a+\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant 1-\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-b-\varepsilon

for all
j\geqslant 1
sufficiently large.

For these values of
j,
if
F(t,s,N)
is the function defined in (
7
), we see that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}F(t,s,N_{j})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{a}\sum_{n\leqslant N}\mathds{1}_{B(x_{n},\frac{s}{2N})}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{n\leqslant N}\lambda\Big(B(x_{n},\frac{s}{2N})\cap[0,a]\Big)

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{s}{N_{j}}\#\Big\{n\leqslant N_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\leqslant x_{n}\leqslant a-\frac{s}{2N_{j}}\Big\}

\displaystyle\geqslant s(b-\varepsilon)

and similarly

\int_{a}^{1}F(t,s,N_{j})\mathrm{d}t\geqslant s(1-b-\varepsilon).

Applying the Cauchy – Schwarz inequality we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}F(t,s,N_{j})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a}F(t,s,N_{j})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t+\int_{a}^{1}F(t,s,N_{j})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}\left(\int_{0}^{a}F(t,s,N_{j})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}+\frac{1}{1-a}\left(\int_{a}^{1}F(t,s,N_{j})\,\mathrm{d}t\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\frac{s^{2}}{a}(b-\varepsilon)^{2}+\frac{s^{2}}{1-a}(1-b-\varepsilon)^{2}.

Since
a\neq b,
if we choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough we have

\frac{(b-\varepsilon)^{2}}{a}+\frac{(1-b-\varepsilon)^{2}}{1-a}=1+\delta\quad\text{ for some }\delta>0

and therefore

(22)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}F(t,s,N_{j})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t\geqslant(1+\delta)s^{2}

for all
j\geqslant 1
large enough.
At this point, we present a simple fact that connects the correlation functions
R_{2}^{*}(s,N)
with the function
F(t,s,N)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
s>0
, we have

(23)

\int_{0}^{1}F(t,s,N)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{s}R_{2}^{*}(\sigma,N)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Proof.

By definition of
F(t,s,N)

Appendix A: Equivalent Definitions of Poissonian Correlations

We discuss the different definitions of Poissonian
k
– th order correlations appearing in the literature.

The reader who is already familiar with the notion of Poissonian correlations might compare the definition given in (
2
) with another common definition, where in the correlation function
R_{k}(g,N)
the differences
(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}})\!),\ldots,(\!(x_{i_{k-1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)
appear instead of the differences
(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{3}})\!)\ldots(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)
as in (
1
).
This is the definition used, for example, in the papers
[
13
,
14
]
that deal with the
k
– th level correlations of quadratic residues modulo some integer
Q\geqslant 1
.

Here we explain that these two definitions are equivalent.

Proposition A.

Let
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq[0,1]
be a sequence.
The following are equivalent:

(i) The sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has Poissonian
k
– th order correlations.

(ii) For all test functions
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{k-1})
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054ptg\left(N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),N(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}})\!),\ldots,N(\!(x_{i_{k-1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)\right)=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(iii) For all rectangles
B=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]\times\ldots\times[a_{k-1},b_{k-1}],\;b_{i}>a_{i},\;1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1,
we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}R_{k}(\mathds{1}_{B},N)=\lambda(B)

where
\lambda
denotes the
(k-1)
– dimensional Lebesgue measure.

Proof.

The proof of the equivalence of (i) and (iii) uses a standard approximation argument from analysis and is omitted.
We show (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii), the direction (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) can be proven in a similar fashion. Let
g\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{k-1})
be an arbitrary test function, and define
f\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{k-1}\to\mathbb{R}
via

f(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k-1})=g(x_{1},x_{2}-x_{1},x_{3}-x_{2},\ldots,x_{k-1}-x_{k-2}).

This definition implies that
f\in C_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{k-1})
,

(49)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{k-1}}g(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

and furthermore

(50)

g(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k-1})=f\left(x_{1},x_{1}+x_{2},\ldots,x_{1}+x_{2}+\ldots+x_{k-1}\right).

Now when
N\geqslant 1
is so large that
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq\left[\frac{-N}{2k},\frac{N}{2k}\right]^{k-1}

and in addition

f\left(N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),N(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}})\!),\ldots,N(\!(x_{i_{k-1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)\right)\neq 0,

then for all
\ell\leqslant k
we have
\lVert x_{i_{\ell-1}}-x_{i_{\ell}}\rVert\leqslant\dfrac{1}{2k}.
Hence

\lVert x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}}\rVert+\lVert x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}}\rVert+\ldots+\lVert x_{i_{\ell-1}}-x_{i_{\ell}}\rVert\leqslant\frac{1}{2}

and thus

(51)

(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!)+(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}})\!)+\ldots+(\!(x_{i_{\ell-1}}-x_{i_{\ell}})\!)=(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{\ell}})\!).

Using these considerations, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054ptg\left(N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),N(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}})\!),\ldots,N(\!(x_{i_{k-1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{g_f_translate}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054ptf\big(N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),N\!\left((\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!)+(\!(x_{i_{2}}-x_{i_{3}})\!)\right),\ldots\big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{signed_distance_cond}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054ptf\left(N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{2}})\!),N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{3}})\!),\ldots,N(\!(x_{i_{1}}-x_{i_{k}})\!)\right)=R_{k}(f,N).

Combining this with (
49
), we see that under the hypothesis that the sequence has Poissonian

– th correlations, statement (ii) is true. As noted, the implication

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) can be shown in a similar way.

At this point, we should also mention that for the specific case

where

. Indeed, assume

) we have

\displaystyle R_{2}(\mathds{1}_{[0,s]},N)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}R_{2}(s,N)+\frac{1}{N}\#\big\{i\neq j\leqslant N\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}x_{i}=x_{j}\big\}.

We establish a fact that was alluded to in the introduction: whenever
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of independent and uniformly distributed random variables in
[0,1]
, then for all
k\geqslant 2
the sequence
(Y_{n}(\omega))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
almost surely has Poissonian correlations of
k
– th order. The method of proof we use is a standard mean – variance argument that appears very often in the relevant literature. We refer the reader to
[
11
]
for a proof in higher dimensions when
k=2
.

Indeed, given such a sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
a_{r}<b_{r}\,\,(1\leqslant r<k)
, let

R_{k}(\omega,N)=\frac{1}{N}\#\left\{\parbox{70.0001pt}{$i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N$ \\
$i_{j}\neq i_{\ell}\,\forall j\neq\ell$}\hskip-14.22636pt\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\frac{a_{r}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}(\omega)-Y_{i_{r+1}}(\omega)\leqslant\frac{b_{r}}{N}\quad(1\leqslant r<k)\right\}.

According to Proposition A it suffices to show that
R_{k}(\omega,N)\to(b_{1}-a_{1})\cdots(b_{k-1}-a_{k-1})
as
N\to\infty
almost surely.

We proceed to the calculation of the expectation and variance of
R_{k}(\cdot,N)
viewed as a random variable on
[0,1].
This will be done in several steps. In the estimates that follow, the implicit constants in the asymptotic notations depend on the scales
a_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}.

Step 1:
We claim that for any
m\geqslant 1
and for any distinct indices
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{m},\geqslant 1
the differences

\Delta_{1}=Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}},\quad\Delta_{2}=Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{3}},\quad\ldots\quad\Delta_{m+1}=Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{m+2}}

are independent.
For convenience, we only show this when
m=1
and for the random variables

\Delta_{1}=Y_{1}-Y_{2}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Delta_{2}=Y_{1}-Y_{3}

(the proof is similar for any other choice of indices and for any
m\geqslant 2
).
Writing
f_{X}
for the probability density function of a random variable
X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, we need to show that

(54)

f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})=f_{\Delta_{1}}(x_{1})f_{\Delta_{2}}(x_{2})\quad\text{ for all }x_{1},x_{2}\in[0,1].

Note that in our context, addition and subtraction of the random variables is always understood modulo
1
. Thus for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have
f_{Y_{i}}(y)=1,

0\leqslant y\leqslant 1
and the density functions of the differences
\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}
are

f_{\Delta_{1}}(\delta)=\int_{0}^{1}f_{Y_{1}}(y)f_{Y_{2}}(\delta+y)\mathrm{d}y=1

and similarly
f_{\Delta_{2}}(\delta)=1,
for all
\delta\in[0,1].

We start from the left – hand side of (
54
): the theorem of total probability and the independence of

\{Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3}\}
imply that

\displaystyle f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2}|Y_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2}|y)\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},Y_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2},y)}{f_{Y_{1}}(y)}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}f_{\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},Y_{1}}(x_{1},x_{2},y)\mathrm{d}y=\int_{0}^{1}f_{y-Y_{2}}(x_{1})f_{y-Y_{3}}(x_{2})\mathrm{d}y=1.

Step 2:
We now claim that whenever each of the sets
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\}
and
J=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
consists of pairwise distinct indices and
\#(I\cap J)\leqslant 1
, then the differences
Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}},Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{3}},\ldots,Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{m}},Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{2}},\ldots,Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{n}}

are independent random variables.
It follows by the previous step that the random variables
Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}},Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{3}},\ldots,Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{m}}
are independent, and so are
Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{2}},\ldots,Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{n}}
.
If the sets
I,J
are disjoint, all of these random variables are immediately independent, so we deal with the case when
\#(I\cap J)=1.
We need to distinguish the following cases:

Case 1:
i_{1}=j_{1}
. In this case, independence follows immediately from Step
1
.

Case 2:
i_{1}=j_{\ell}
or
j_{1}=i_{\ell}
with
\ell\geqslant 2
: Then we can assume without loss of generality that
i_{1}=j_{2}
. The random variables we are looking at are

Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{n}},Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{n-1}},\ldots,Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{2}}=Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{i_{1}},Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}},\ldots,Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{m}},

and these are independent by an argument similar to the one used in Step
1.

Case 3:
i_{r}=j_{\ell}
with
r,\ell\geqslant 2
: Then we assume without loss of generality
i_{2}=j_{2}
and we are in a similar situation as in Case 2.

Step 3:
Given
k
distinct indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N,
we write

X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}=\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}.

We calculate the covariance
\text{Cov}(X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}),
where
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N
and
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leqslant N
both consist of distinct indices. As seen above, when

\#\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}\leqslant 1
the variables
X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}
and
X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}
are independent and thus the covariance in question is
0
. We need to bound the covariance of
X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}
when

(55)

\#\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}=\ell\geqslant 2.

If
(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}),(j_{1},\ldots,j_{k})
fulfill (
55
), then

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\big(X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}]\big)\big(X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}-\mathbb{E}[X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}]\big)\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}\big[X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}}\cdot X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}}\big]

(56)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{j_{1}}-Y_{j_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}\Big].

To find an upper bound for the right – hand side, we take a maximal subset
\tilde{J}\subset\{j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\}
such that

\#\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap(\{j_{1}\}\cup\tilde{J})\leqslant 1
. By the discussion in Step
2
, this gives rise to
2(k-1)-(\ell-1)
independent random variables among the factors in the integral in (
56
), each of them having expectation
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
. The remaining differences appearing in (
56
) might not be independent, but can be trivially bounded from above by
1
since they are characteristic functions. We therefore conclude that

\text{Cov}(X_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}})=\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{N^{2(k-1)-(\ell-1)}}\Big)\cdot

Step 4:
We fix a value of
\ell\geqslant 2
and we estimate

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\\
\#\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}=\ell\end{subarray}}\hskip-22.76219pt\text{Cov}(X_{i_{1},...,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},...,j_{k}}).

We count how many choices of the sets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
and
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
there exist such that (
55
) holds. From a fixed set
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
we can chose
\ell
elements that will be in the intersection with
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
in
\binom{k}{\ell}
ways. The remaining
k-\ell
elements of
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
can be chosen in
\mathcal{O}(N^{k-\ell})
ways. Since the set
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
can be chosen in
\mathcal{O}_{k}(N^{k})
ways, we deduce that the different choices of the sets
\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}
and
\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}
with precisely
\ell
common elements is
\mathcal{O}_{k,\ell}(N^{2k-\ell})
. Combining with Step
3
, we obtain

(57)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\\
\#\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}=\ell\end{subarray}}\hskip-22.76219pt\text{Cov}(X_{i_{1},...,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},...,j_{k}})=\mathcal{O}_{k,\ell}\big(N^{2k-\ell}\frac{1}{N^{2(k-1)-(\ell-1)}}\big)=\mathcal{O}_{k,\ell}(N).

Step 5:
We are finally in place to calculate the expectation and variance of
R_{k}(\cdot,N).
The expectation is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[R_{k}(\cdot,N)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\big]\cdots\mathbb{E}\big[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}\big]

\displaystyle=(a_{1}-b_{1})\cdots(a_{k-1}-b_{k-1})+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{1}{N}\Big),\qquad N\to\infty

because
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent (here we used the result from Step
1
). For the variance we have

\displaystyle\text{Var}[R_{k}(\cdot,N)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\hskip-17.07164pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct},\;\,(i_{1},...,i_{k})\neq(j_{1},...,j_{k})\\
\#\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}\}=\ell\end{subarray}}\hskip-22.76219pt\text{Cov}(X_{i_{1},...,i_{k}},X_{j_{1},...,j_{k}})

(58)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Var}[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}].

In the first sum appearing in (
Appendix B: The
k
– th order correlations of random sequences are almost surely Poissonian
), the terms corresponding to
\ell=0
or
1
are equal to
0.
Applying (
57
) for the terms corresponding to
2\leqslant\ell\leqslant k
in (
Appendix B: The
k
– th order correlations of random sequences are almost surely Poissonian
), we obtain

\displaystyle\text{Var}[R_{k}(\cdot,N)]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Var}[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}]+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{N}).

Since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Var}[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}]-

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N}]}\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N}]}]^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\Big(\frac{a_{1}-b_{1}}{N}\cdot\ldots\cdot\frac{a_{k-1}-b_{k-1}}{N}-\prod_{r=1}^{k-1}\Big(\frac{a_{r}-b_{r}}{N}\Big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}_{k}\big(\frac{1}{N}\big),

we deduce that

\text{Var}[R_{k}(\cdot,N)]=\mathcal{O}_{k}\big(\frac{1}{N}\big),\qquad N\to\infty.

Consider now the sequence
N_{m}=\lfloor m^{1+\gamma}\rfloor,m\geqslant 1
where
\gamma>0
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
A_{N}=\{\omega\in[0,1]\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}|R_{k}(\omega,N)-\mathbb{E}[R_{k}(\cdot,N)]|\geqslant\varepsilon\},N\geqslant 1.
By Chebyshev’s inequality, the Lebesgue measure of
A_{N}
satisfies
\lambda(A_{N})=\mathcal{O}(1/N),N\to\infty.
Then the Borel – Cantelli lemma implies that

\lambda\big(\limsup\limits_{m\to\infty}A_{N_{m}}\big)=0,

and since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrarily chosen, we conclude that for almost all
\omega\in[0,1]
we have
\lim\limits_{m\to\infty}R_{k}(\omega,N_{m})=(b_{1}-a_{1})\cdots(b_{k-1}-a_{k-1}).

The fact that
R_{k}(\cdot,N_{m})
converges almost surely to this limit for any choice of the scalars follows by an intersection over a countable dense set of
(a_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}).

It remains to prove that for the same values of
\omega
,
R_{k}(\omega,N)
will converge to the value
(b_{1}-a_{1})\cdots(b_{k-1}-a_{k-1})
for any choice of
a_{1},\ldots,b_{k-1}
. This will follow from the fact that when
N_{m}\leqslant N<N_{m+1}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N_{m}\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69054pt\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}}{N_{m}}\frac{N_{m}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}}{N_{m}}\frac{N_{m}}{N}]}(\omega)\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}}{N_{m}}\frac{N_{m}}{N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}}{N_{m}}\frac{N_{m}}{N}]}(\omega)\leqslant R_{k}(\omega,N)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1}{N}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\leqslant N_{m+1}\\
\mathrm{distinct}\end{subarray}}\hskip-11.38109pt\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{1}N_{m+1}}{N_{m+1}N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{2}}\leqslant\frac{b_{1}N_{m+1}}{N_{m+1}N}]}(\omega)\cdots\mathds{1}_{[\frac{a_{k-1}N_{m+1}}{N_{m+1}N}\leqslant Y_{i_{1}}-Y_{i_{k}}\leqslant\frac{b_{k-1}N_{m+1}}{N_{m+1}N}]}(\omega)

and the fact that
N_{m}/N_{m+1}\to 1
as
m\to\infty
.
