A Comprehensive Definition of the Geometric Mean of Convex Bodies Based on Relations Between Their p p -Means
René Brandenberg, Florian Grundbacher

Abstract
. In light of the log-Brunn-Minkowski conjecture, various attempts have been made to define the geometric mean of convex bodies. Many of these constructions are fairly complex and/or fail to satisfy some natural properties one would expect of such a mean. We remedy this by providing a new geometric mean that is both technically simple and inherits all the natural properties expected. To improve our understanding of potential geometric mean definitions, we first study general p p -means of convex bodies, with the usual definition extended to two series ranging over all p p in the extended reals. We characterize their equality cases and obtain (in almost all instances tight) inequalities that quantify how well these means approximate each other. As a corollary, we establish that every Minkowski centered body is equidistant from all its p p -symmetrizations with respect to the Banach-Mazur distance. Finally, we show that our geometric mean satisfies all the properties considered in recent literature and extend this list with some properties regarding symmetrization and asymmetry.

The

-mean
of two positive real numbers

is for
p\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
defined as

m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\left(\frac{\alpha^{p}+\beta^{p}}{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}.

Moreover, the
(-\infty)
-
,

-

-means
are given by

m_{-\infty}(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\min\{\alpha,\beta\},~m_{0}(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\sqrt{\alpha\beta},\text{ and }m_{\infty}(\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq\max\{\alpha,\beta\}.

Remark 1.1
.

It is well-known
that
m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)
is increasing and continuous in all three arguments.
Furthermore, for
p<q
, it holds
m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)=m_{q}(\alpha,\beta)
if and only if
\alpha=\beta
.
If
p\neq\pm\infty
and
\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
,
\beta\leq\beta^{\prime}
,
then equality holds in
m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)\leq m_{p}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
if and only if
\alpha=\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta=\beta^{\prime}
.
Furthermore, it is easy to see that
m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)=1/m_{-p}(1/\alpha,1/\beta)

and for
\gamma>0
also
m_{p}(\gamma\alpha,\gamma\beta)=\gamma m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)
.

We further require some properties of the map
\phi_{p,q}\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\allowbreak\alpha\mapsto\frac{m_{q}(\alpha,1)}{m_{p}(\alpha,1)}

for
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]
.
For
\alpha,\beta>0
, we obtain as immediate consequences of the above properties

(1)

\phi_{p,q}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\right)=\frac{m_{q}(\alpha/\beta,1)}{m_{p}(\alpha/\beta,1)}=\frac{m_{q}(\alpha,\beta)}{m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)}=\frac{m_{q}(1,\beta/\alpha)}{m_{p}(1,\beta/\alpha)}=\phi_{p,q}\left(\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)

as well as

(2)

\phi_{-q,-p}(\alpha)=\frac{m_{-p}(\alpha,1)}{m_{-q}(\alpha,1)}=\frac{m_{-p}(1/\alpha,1)}{m_{-q}(1/\alpha,1)}=\frac{m_{q}(\alpha,1)}{m_{p}(\alpha,1)}=\phi_{p,q}(\alpha).

Moreover,
\phi_{p,q}
is clearly differentiable on
(1,\infty)
as the quotient of two differentiable maps
and if
p
and
q
do not lie in
\{-\infty,0,\infty\}
, its derivative is

\displaystyle\phi_{p,q}^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{\alpha^{q}+1}{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{q}}-1}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{q-1}}{2}\cdot m_{p}(\alpha,1)-\left(\frac{\alpha^{p}+1}{2}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}-1}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{p-1}}{2}\cdot m_{q}(\alpha,1)}{m_{p}(\alpha,1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{m_{q}(\alpha,1)\cdot m_{p}(\alpha,1)\cdot\left(\frac{\alpha^{q-1}}{\alpha^{q}+1}-\frac{\alpha^{p-1}}{\alpha^{p}+1}\right)}{m_{p}(\alpha,1)^{2}}=\frac{\phi_{p,q}(\alpha)}{\alpha}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{-q}}-\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{-p}}\right).

Defining
\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{-\infty}}\coloneqq 1
and
\frac{1}{1+\alpha^{\infty}}\coloneqq 0
for all
\alpha>1
,
it can easily be verified that the final formula above for
\phi_{p,q}^{\prime}(a)
is correct for all
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]
.
Hence,
\phi_{p,q}^{\prime}(\alpha)\geq 0
for
\alpha>1
if and only if
p\leq q
.
Together with (
1
),
\phi_{p,q}
is increasing on
[1,\infty)
and decreasing on
(0,1]
for
p\leq q
.

Notably, the geometric mean is missing.

The definition of
p
-means of convex bodies for general values of
p
requires two defining functions of convex bodies:

Definition 1.2
.

Let
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in C
be closed and convex.
Its
support function
is defined as

h_{C}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty],h_{C}(a)\coloneqq\sup\{a^{T}x:x\in C\}.

Its
gauge function
is given by

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty],\left\|x\right\|_{C}\coloneqq\inf\{\lambda\geq 0:x\in\lambda C\}.

These functions have many important properties (cf.
[
25
, Section 1.7]
):

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
C,K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in C\cap K
be closed and convex,
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\lambda\geq 0
, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
regular.
Then:

(i)

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C}=h_{C^{\circ}}
and
h_{C}=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C^{\circ}}
,

(ii)

h_{C}(\lambda v)=\lambda h_{C}(v)
,
h_{AC}(v)=h_{C}(A^{T}v)
, and
h_{-C}(v)=h_{C}(-v)
,

(iii)

h_{C}(v+w)\leq h_{C}(v)+h_{C}(w)
,

(iv)

h_{C}
is convex, and thus continuous if
C
is bounded, and

(v)

h_{\lambda C+K}=\lambda h_{C}+h_{K}
, and
h_{C}\leq h_{K}\iff C\subset K
.

Part (i) gives us results analogous to parts (ii) – (v) for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C}
,
so we do not state them explicitly.
In particular,
h_{C}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C}
characterize a closed convex set
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in C
in that
if
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is another such set, then its support/gauge function coincides with that of
C
if and only if
K=C
.

Using the above proposition, it can be shown that for any
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
,

\frac{K+L}{2}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a^{T}x\leq\frac{h_{K}(a)+h_{L}(a)}{2}\text{ for all }a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\right\}

and

\left(\frac{K^{\circ}+L^{\circ}}{2}\right)^{\circ}=\conv\left(\left\{\left(\frac{\left\|x\right\|_{K}+\left\|x\right\|_{L}}{2}\right)^{-1}\cdot x:x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\right\}\right).

This motivates the following generalization of
p
-means of convex bodies:

Definition 1.4
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and
p\in[-\infty,\infty]
.
The
upper
p
-mean
of
K
and
L
is defined as

\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a^{T}x\leq m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))\text{ for all }a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\}.

Their
lower
p
-mean
is given by

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\coloneqq\conv\left(\left\{(m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L}))^{-1}\cdot x:x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\right\}\right).

Theorem
3.1
below shows that
the upper and lower
p
-mean of
K
and
L
coincide only in some trivial cases.
This justifies giving these two sets distinct names and notation.

One can also define weighted
p
-means of convex bodies by replacing the
p
-means of numbers above with their weighted counterparts.
Since our focus lies on the symmetrization of convex bodies,
we do not do so here.
Nonetheless, let us point out that all results in Sections
2
–
4

remain valid for general weighted
p
-means with almost identical proofs,
and our methods in Section
5

can be adjusted to obtain similar results in this case, as well.

The upper
p
-means for
p\in[1,\infty]
were first introduced simply as
p
-means in
[
9
]
.
The lower
p
-means for
p\in[-\infty,-1]
are what is called dot-means in
[
10
]
.
There is no formal necessity to restrict these means to the above ranges of
p
-values.
This has mostly been done since they can be defined implicitly in these cases using the following property:

By definition, one has
h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\leq m_{p}(h_{K},h_{L})
and

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\leq m_{-p}(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{K},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L})
.
If
p\in[1,\infty]
, Minkowski’s inequality shows that
m_{p}(h_{K},h_{L})
is a sublinear function.
Thus, it is the support function of a unique convex body (cf.
[
25
, Theorem 1.7.1]
).
By the above definition, this body must be
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)

and therefore
h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=m_{p}(h_{K},h_{L})
.
For
p\in[-\infty,-1]
, we can argue analogously to obtain

\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=m_{-p}(\left\|\cdot\right\|_{K},\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L})
.

Evidently, we have

\displaystyle\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)

\displaystyle=K\cap L,

\displaystyle\underline{M}_{-1}(K,L)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{K^{\circ}+L^{\circ}}{2}\right)^{\circ},

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{1}(K,L)

\displaystyle=\frac{K+L}{2},

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)

\displaystyle=\conv(K\cup L).

Since we want the
p
-means of
K
and
L
to generalize these four special cases,
it is natural to define

(3)

M_{p}(K,L)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)&\text{if }p>0\\
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)&\text{if }p<0\end{cases}.

Nonetheless, we study upper and lower
p
-means for all values
p\in[-\infty,\infty]

and for brevity refer to the entirety of both series simply as
p
-means.

We will see later that
M_{p}(K,L)\subset M_{q}(K,L)
for
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]\setminus\{0\}
,
p<q
,
with equality if and only if
K=L
,
extending the behavior of
p
-means of real numbers.
It is further easy to see (and also a consequence of Theorem
5.6
below) that
M_{p}(K,L)
is continuous in
p\neq 0
.
However, Theorem
3.1
below shows that the set inclusion

(4)

\lim_{p\nearrow 0}M_{p}(K,L)=\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)=\lim_{p\searrow 0}M_{p}(K,L)

is in general strict, so
M_{p}(K,L)
cannot be extended to be continuous at
p=0
as well.

As we see in the following lemma,
this definition of
p
-means has the added advantage that

(M_{p}(K,L))^{\circ}\allowbreak=M_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
for all
p\in[-\infty,\infty]\setminus\{0\}
.
Again interpreting the polar as the inverse, this generalizes the behavior of
p
-means of real numbers.
Analogously, the geometric mean
M_{0}(K,L)
should satisfy the relation
(M_{0}(K,L))^{\circ}=M_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
.
Since
(\overline{M}_{0}(K,L))^{\circ}=\underline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
and
(\underline{M}_{0}(K,L))^{\circ}=\overline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})

do not coincide in general, neither
M_{0}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
nor
M_{0}(K,L)=\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)
are valid choices.
This is one motivation for finding a geometric mean
M_{0}(K,L)
that commutes with polarity
and satisfies
M_{-p}(K,L)\subset M_{0}(K,L)\subset M_{p}(K,L)
for all
p>0
,
i.e.
\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subset M_{0}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
.

2.
Technical Preliminaries

We begin with some basic properties of upper and lower
p
-means:

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
K,K^{\prime},L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
with
K\subset K^{\prime}
,
L\subset L^{\prime}
,
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]
with
p\leq q
, and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
regular.
Then:

(i)

(\overline{M}_{p}(K,L))^{\circ}=\underline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
and
(\underline{M}_{p}(K,L))^{\circ}=\overline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
,

(ii)

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
,

(iii)

\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{q}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})
and
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset\underline{M}_{q}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})
, and

(iv)

\overline{M}_{p}(AK,AL)=A\cdot\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and
\underline{M}_{p}(AK,AL)=A\cdot\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
.

Note that the regularity assumption for
A
is necessary since otherwise
\inter(AK)=\inter(AL)=\emptyset
,
in which case
\overline{M}_{p}(AK,AL)
and
\underline{M}_{p}(AK,AL)
are not defined.
Even if one would adjust the definitions to allow this,
it is not hard to construct an example where, e.g.,
A
is an orthogonal projection and the equality
A(K\cap L)=(AK)\cap(AL)
fails.

Proof.

For (i), we observe that

\displaystyle\left(\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\right)^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\left(\bigcap_{a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}H_{(a,m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a)))}^{\leq}\right)^{\circ}=\cl\left(\conv\left(\bigcup_{a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}\left(H_{(a,m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a)))}^{\leq}\right)^{\circ}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\cl\left(\conv\left(\left\{\frac{a}{m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))}:a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\right\}\right)\right)

The set that we take the closure and convex hull of in the last step is compact by compactness of
\mathbb{S}_{2}
and continuity of
m_{p}(h_{K},h_{L})
.
Thus, we may drop the closure in the last step.
Since
h_{K}=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{K^{\circ}}
and
h_{L}=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{\circ}}
by Proposition
1.3
(i),
we conclude that
(\overline{M}_{p}(K,L))^{\circ}=\underline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
.
Now, (i) follows from

(\underline{M}_{p}(K,L))^{\circ}=(\underline{M}_{-(-p)}((K^{\circ})^{\circ},(L^{\circ})^{\circ}))^{\circ}=((\overline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ}))^{\circ})^{\circ}=\overline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
.

For (ii), let
x,a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
.
Then
a^{T}x\leq 0
, or
a^{T}x>0
and

\displaystyle\frac{a^{T}x}{m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m_{-p}\left(\frac{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}{a^{T}x},\frac{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}{a^{T}x}\right)}=m_{p}\left(a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}},a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)\leq m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a)).

Since
a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
has been chosen arbitrarily, we obtain
x/m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})\in\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
.
Since
x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
has also been chosen arbitrarily and
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
is clearly convex, (ii) is proved.

(iii) is an immediate consequence of the monotonicity of
p
-means of real numbers.

Finally, for (iv), let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then
x
lies in
\overline{M}_{p}(AK,AL)
if and only if
a^{T}x\leq m_{p}(h_{AK}(a),h_{AL}(a))
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
This is equivalent to
(A^{T}a)^{T}(A^{-1}x)\leq m_{p}(h_{K}(A^{T}a),h_{L}(A^{T}a))
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
i.e.
A^{-1}x\in\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
.
Consequently,
\overline{M}_{p}(AK,AL)=A\cdot\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
.
Since for any
C\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
, it holds
(AC)^{\circ}=A^{-T}\cdot C^{\circ}
,
we also obtain the second equality in (iv) from (i).
∎

Characterizing under which conditions equality holds in (ii) and (iii) above is our next goal.
We first ask under which conditions the unique boundary points of two
p
-means on a ray
[0,\infty)x
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
coincide,
which is equivalent to the equality of the respective gauge function values.
Since two of these means are equal if and only if these boundary points are equal for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
,
we achieve our goal using the upcoming two lemmas.
Additionally, these lemmas yield by Proposition
1.3
(i)
and Lemma
2.1
(i) results on the equality of support functions, which we do not state explicitly.

We begin by comparing upper and lower
p
-means for the same finite
p
-value.
The cases for
p=\pm\infty
are covered by Theorem
3.1
below.
The containments from right to left and the implication from (iii) to (i) and (ii) in the following lemma
are for
p=0
also shown in
[
20
, Proposition 15]
.
Note that while the second set is clearly contained in the first one, the reverse inclusion is non-trivial for
p<1

since
h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}(a)<m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))
is possible for general
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
in that case.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
,
p\in\mathbb{R}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.
Then

\displaystyle N_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))\right\}

\displaystyle=N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right).

Moreover, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}
,

(ii)

\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})
, and

(iii)

N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)\supsetneq\{0\}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.1
and the definition of
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
, we have

\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\leq\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\leq m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L}).

This already yields the implication from (ii) to (i).
The definition of
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
now implies for any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that
a^{T}x\leq 0<m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))
, or
a^{T}x>0
and

(5)

\frac{a^{T}x}{m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})}\leq a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\leq a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\leq h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}(a)\leq m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a)).

If
a\in N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)
, we additionally obtain

m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))=m_{p}\left(a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}},a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)=\frac{a^{T}x}{m_{-p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})},

and therefore equality in the full chain in (
5
) as well.
Hence, the implication from (iii) to (ii) and the inclusions from right to left in the lemma follow.

Next, we assume
\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}
.
By the definition of
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
,
there exist some
v^{1},...,v^{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
and
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k}>0
such that

\sum_{i\in[k]}\lambda_{i}=1
and

(6)

y\coloneqq\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=\sum_{i\in[k]}\lambda_{i}\frac{v^{i}}{m_{-p}(\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K},\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L})}.

It follows directly from the definition of
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
that

\sup_{a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}\frac{a^{T}x}{m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}
,
where the supremum is attained for some
\bar{a}\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
by continuity in
a
.
Therefore, by the assumption
\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}
,
there exists
\bar{a}\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
with
\bar{a}^{T}y=m_{p}(h_{K}(\bar{a}),h_{L}(\bar{a}))
.
Since for all
i\in[k]

y^{i}\coloneqq\frac{v^{i}}{m_{-p}(\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K},\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L})}\in\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset H_{(\bar{a},m_{p}(h_{K}(\bar{a}),h_{L}(\bar{a})))}^{\leq}=H_{(\bar{a},\bar{a}^{T}y)}^{\leq},

all
y^{i}
must, because of (
6
), even belong to
H_{(\bar{a},\bar{a}^{T}y)}
.
Hence,

m_{p}(h_{K}(\bar{a}),h_{L}(\bar{a}))=\bar{a}^{T}y=\bar{a}^{T}\frac{v^{i}}{m_{-p}(\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K},\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L})}=m_{p}\left(\bar{a}^{T}\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K}},\bar{a}^{T}\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L}}\right).

Since
p\neq\pm\infty
, we obtain
\bar{a}^{T}\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K}}=h_{K}(\bar{a})
and
\bar{a}^{T}\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L}}=h_{L}(\bar{a})
.
With
\mu_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\lambda_{i}\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K}}{m_{-p}(\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K},\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L})}>0
, this yields

\frac{x}{\sum_{j\in[k]}\mu_{j}\cdot\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=\sum_{i\in[k]}\frac{\mu_{i}}{\sum_{j\in[k]}\mu_{j}}\cdot\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K}}\in\conv\left(\left\{\frac{v^{1}}{\left\|v^{1}\right\|_{K}},...,\frac{v^{k}}{\left\|v^{k}\right\|_{K}}\right\}\right)\subset K\cap H_{(\bar{a},h_{K}(\bar{a}))}.

It follows
\sum_{j\in[k]}\mu_{j}\cdot\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{K}
and
\bar{a}\in N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)
.
We can argue analogously for
\bar{a}\in N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)
.
This shows the implication from (i) to (iii) and that the second set in the lemma is contained in the third one,
as any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
with
a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))
can be rescaled to lie in
\mathbb{S}_{2}
and thus be a valid choice for
\bar{a}
.

Finally, assume
a\in N_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)
.
Then

\left\|a\right\|_{\underline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})}=h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}(a)=h_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}(a)=\left\|a\right\|_{\overline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})},

which by the already proven equivalence of (i) and (ii) yields

a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}(a)=\left\|a\right\|_{\overline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})}=m_{p}(\left\|a\right\|_{K^{\circ}},\left\|a\right\|_{L^{\circ}})=m_{p}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a)).

Thus, the first set in the lemma is contained in the second one, which completes the proof.
∎

Next, we compare
p
-means for different
p
-values.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
,
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]
,
p<q
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

(i)

\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
if and only if

\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
.

(ii)

\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
if and only if

N_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)\supsetneq\{0\}
, where

\displaystyle N_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=m_{q}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=h_{K}(a)=h_{L}(a)\right\}.

(iii)

If
\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
,
then
\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
and

N_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)=N_{\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)=N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)\supsetneq\{0\}.

Let us point out that while for
q\in[-\infty,-1]
the equality
\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}

always also implies
\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
,
this is not necessarily the case for
q>-1
(cf. Figure
1
).
Similarly, the first equality in (ii) is again non-trivial for
q<1
.
Furthermore, the second equality in (ii) does not imply that
\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}
lies in
K
or
L

(cf. Figure
1
).

Proof.

For (i), if
\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
,
then Lemma
2.1
and the definition of
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)

\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
.
Conversely, if we assume
\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
,
then there exist
v^{1},...,v^{k}\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
and
\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k}>0
such that
\sum_{i\in[k]}\lambda_{i}=1
and

y\coloneqq\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}}=\sum_{i\in[k]}\lambda_{i}\frac{v^{i}}{m_{-p}(\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K},\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L})}.

Since
y\in\bd(\underline{M}_{q}(K,L))
, there exists a hyperplane
H
that supports
\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)
at

.

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
is a subset of
\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)
by Lemma
2.1
,
so all

must also lie in

,

which by

implies
\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K}=\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L}
.
Hence, all

y^{i}\coloneqq\frac{v^{i}}{m_{-p}(\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K},\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L})}=\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{K}}=\frac{v^{i}}{\left\|v^{i}\right\|_{L}}

lie in

,
so
y=\sum_{i\in[k]}\lambda_{i}y^{i}
also lies in
K\cap L
.
This shows

\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\leq m_{-q}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})\leq\max\{\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L}\}\leq\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}.

Thus,
\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}
by

.

The equivalence in (ii) and the set inclusions from right to left are clear,

(i) implies
\left\|a\right\|_{K^{\circ}}=\left\|a\right\|_{L^{\circ}}=\left\|a\right\|_{\underline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})}
.
We conclude
h_{K}(a)=h_{L}(a)=h_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}(a)=a^{T}x
.

For (iii), we observe that Lemma

implies

\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\\
\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}\end{array}\right\}\leq\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}.

By assumption, equality holds from left to right.
We may therefore apply (i) to obtain

\left\|x\right\|_{K}=\left\|x\right\|_{L}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}.

Moreover, (ii) yields

\displaystyle\{0\}

\displaystyle\subsetneq N_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)=\left\{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:a^{T}\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}=h_{K}(a)=h_{L}(a)\right\}

\displaystyle=N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)\subset N_{\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right)\subset N_{\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\right).

Thus, the claimed equality of sets follows from
\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}=\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}
.

For general
p<q
, it is not necessarily true that
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)
.
This makes it both more difficult to handle common boundary points of these sets and less interesting to characterize them,
which is why Lemma
2.3
does not cover this case.
Fortunately, it is of the least interest in terms of equality of means:
As discussed, it is natural to define “the”
p
-mean of
K
and
L
for positive

as
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
, and for negative

as

.

The following corollary collects some important consequences of the above lemmas,
which are sometimes more convenient to use than the rather technical lemmas themselves.

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
and
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]
,
p<q
.
Then

(i)

x\in\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\cap\bd(\underline{M}_{q}(K,L))
if and only if
x\in\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)\cap\bd(\underline{M}_{q}(K,L))
.

(ii)

x\in\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\cap\bd(\overline{M}_{q}(K,L))
if and only if
x\in\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\cap\bd(\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L))
.

(iii)

x\in\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\cap\bd(\overline{M}_{q}(K,L))
if and only if
x\in\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)\cap\bd(\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L))
.

For
p\neq\pm\infty
, we have in addition:

(iv)

\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)}}\in\bd(\overline{M}_{p}(K,L))

if and only if
N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)\allowbreak\supsetneq\{0\}
.

Independently of
K
and
L
, this is always the case for at least one
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof.

The implications from right to left in (i) – (iii) are clear;
the converses follow from (i) – (iii) in Lemma
2.3
.
The equivalence in (iv) is already stated in Lemma
2.2
.
For the claim that both statements apply for some
x
,
let
R>0
such that
K\subset RL
and
x
is a common boundary point of
K
and
RL
.
Then there exists some
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
such that
H_{(a,h_{RL}(a))}^{\leq}
supports
K
and
RL
at
x
.
Thus,
a\in N_{K}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}\right)\cap N_{L}\left(\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}\right)
,
finishing the proof.
∎

3.
Equality Cases Between
p
-Means

We are now ready to analyze the equality cases of
p
-means,
beginning with upper and lower
p
-means for the same
p
-value.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and
p\in[-\infty,\infty]
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
,

(ii)

p=\pm\infty
, or
n=1
, or
K
and
L
are dilatates of each other.

Proof.

We first consider the cases
p=\pm\infty
.
By Lemma
2.1
(i) and (ii),
we only need to verify the inclusion
\overline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)\subset\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)=K\cap L
.
To this end, let
x\in\overline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)
and
a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
.
Then
a^{T}x\leq m_{-\infty}(h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a))=\min\{h_{K}(a),h_{L}(a)\}
.
Since
a
has been chosen arbitrarily, we obtain
x\in K\cap L
.

From now on, we assume
p\neq\pm\infty
.
By Corollary
2.4
(iv),
(i) is equivalent to the fact that for all
x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
,
K
and
L
can be supported
by parallel hyperplanes at
\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K}}
and
\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L}}
, respectively.
This in turn is equivalent to
n=1
or
K
and
L
being dilatates of each other,
which is shown in
[
12
]
.
∎

We turn to the equality cases for different values of
p
,
extending previously known results
[
9
,
10
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and
p,q\in[-\infty,\infty]
,
p<q
.
Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)=\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)
,

(ii)

\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
,

(iii)

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
, and

(iv)

K=L
.

Proof.

(iv) clearly implies (iii), which in turn implies (i) and (ii) by Lemma
2.1
(ii) and (iii).
Now we show that (ii) implies (iv):

\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
yields that
x/\left\|x\right\|_{\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)}

is a boundary point of
\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
for all
x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}
.
Corollary
2.4
now implies that
\overline{M}_{r}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)
for all
r\in[p,\infty]
.
In particular,
m_{r}(h_{K},h_{L})=m_{\infty}(h_{K},h_{L})
for all sufficiently large
r\in[1,\infty]
by
p<q\leq\infty
,
which is only possible for

, i.e.

.
For the implication from (i) to (iv), we use an analogous argument involving gauge functions.
∎

Next, we consider the monotonicity of

-means in

and

For

.
If

Theorem 3.3
.

Let

,

, and

Proof.

(iii) is clearly sufficient for (i) and (ii).

For the implication from (i) to (iii), we use an analogous argument involving gauge functions.
∎

4.
p
-Means of Polytopes

The goal of this section is to show that if

are polytopes,
then some of their

-means are guaranteed to be polytopes, too.
We obtain explicit representations of these polytopes, based on representations of
K
and
L
,
which allows for easier computation of the means in question.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
be polytopes and
p\in[-\infty,1]\cup\{\infty\}
.
Then
\underline{M}_{-p}(K,L)
and
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
are polytopes as well.

Besides the trivial case
p=\infty
, the interval
(0,1]
for

in the above proposition is of particular interest,
as this is the range of
p
-values where it applies to
M_{p}(K,L)
as defined in (

Proof.

We only need to show that
\underline{M}_{-p}(K,L)
is a polytope
since
K^{\circ}
and
L^{\circ}
are also polytopes and
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)=\left(\underline{M}_{-p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})\right)^{\circ}
.
As
\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)=K\cap L
is clearly a polytope, we may assume
p\in[-\infty,1]
.

Let
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F\coloneqq K\cap H_{(a,1)}
and
E\coloneqq L\cap H_{(b,1)}
are facets of the respective polytopes.
It is easy to see that for
v\in\text{pos}(F)

(7)

\left\|v\right\|_{K}=a^{T}v.

Now, let
v,w\in(\pos(F)\cap\pos(E))\setminus\{0\}
and write

\bar{v}\coloneqq\frac{v}{m_{p}(\left\|v\right\|_{K},\left\|v\right\|_{L})}
and
\bar{w}\coloneqq\frac{w}{m_{p}(\left\|w\right\|_{K},\left\|w\right\|_{L})}
.
For
\lambda\in[0,1]
, let

.
We claim that

\frac{x}{m_{p}(\left\|x\right\|_{K},\left\|x\right\|_{L})}\in[0,x],

which is equivalent to

(8)

1\leq m_{p}(\left\|\lambda\bar{v}+(1-\lambda)\bar{w}\right\|_{K},\left\|\lambda\bar{v}+(1-\lambda)\bar{w}\right\|_{L}).

By continuity in

, we may restrict to

.
Using (

the right-hand side of (

m_{p}\left(\frac{\lambda a^{T}v}{m_{p}(a^{T}v,b^{T}v)}+\frac{(1-\lambda)a^{T}w}{m_{p}(a^{T}w,b^{T}w)},\frac{\lambda b^{T}v}{m_{p}(a^{T}v,b^{T}v)}+\frac{(1-\lambda)b^{T}w}{m_{p}(a^{T}w,b^{T}w)}\right).

Defining for

\left\|(\alpha,\beta)\right\|_{p}\coloneqq(\alpha^{p}+\beta^{p})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}=2^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}m_{p}(\alpha,\beta)

we may re-express the right-hand side of (

\frac{\left\|\lambda\cdot\left\|(a^{T}w,b^{T}w)\right\|_{p}\cdot(a^{T}v,b^{T}v)+(1-\lambda)\cdot\left\|(a^{T}v,b^{T}v)\right\|_{p}\cdot(a^{T}w,b^{T}w)\right\|_{p}}{\left\|(a^{T}v,b^{T}v)\right\|_{p}\cdot\left\|(a^{T}w,b^{T}w)\right\|_{p}}

Using the reverse Minkowski inequality for

The above shows that if

\displaystyle\conv\left(\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{v}{m_{p}(\left\|v\right\|_{K},\left\|v\right\|_{L})}:v\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\cap\text{pos}(F)\cap\text{pos}(E)\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\conv\left(\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{v}{m_{p}(\left\|v\right\|_{K},\left\|v\right\|_{L})}:v\in\mathcal{R}(F,E)\right\}\right).

Any

For

.
Using

In Sections

Inspired by

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
K\in\mathcal{C}^{n}
and

.
Then

holds if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

, and

(ii)

There exist

, and
a^{1},...,a^{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

for every

and

.

We turn to the optimal containment between

-means:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and

.
Then

(i)

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset^{opt}\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)

(ii)

\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset^{opt}\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)

\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset^{opt}\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset^{opt}\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)

\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)\subset^{opt}\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)
.

\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset^{opt}\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
if both means are
0
-symmetric.
This applies in particular if
K
and
L
are
0
-symmetric, or

.

Proof.

The implications from right to left in the first three cases are direct consequences of Lemma
2.1
.
We turn to their converses:
For (i), Lemma
2.3
(i) shows that if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a common boundary point of
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and
\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)
,
then
x
also lies in
\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)
.
Hence, any halfspace that supports
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and
\underline{M}_{q}(K,L)
at
x
also supports
\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)
at
x
.
Now, Proposition
5.1
implies (i).

For (ii), Lemma
2.3
(ii) shows that if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is a common boundary point of
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and
\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
,
then any halfspace that supports
\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and
\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
at
x
also supports
\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)
at
x
.
Using Proposition
5.1
again implies (ii).

For (iii), Lemma
2.3
(iii) shows that
any common boundary point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and
\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
also lies in
\underline{M}_{-\infty}(K,L)
,
and that any hyperplane that supports
\overline{M}_{q}(K,L)
at
x
also supports
\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L)
at
x
.
A third time, Proposition

yields the desired result.

Finally, if
\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)
and

.

.
∎

The above theorem in particular implies that
\underline{M}_{0}(K,-K)\subset^{opt}\overline{M}_{0}(K,-K)
for any

.
Thus, no matter how the geometric mean

is defined,

,
then both set-inclusions are necessarily optimal.
The same holds true for general

instead of

and

are 0-symmetric
(independent of the symmetry of the geometric mean).

If we want to derive estimates on how well the

Definition 5.3
.

Let
K,L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any sets.
The

of

R_{0}(K,L)\coloneqq\inf\{\lambda\geq 0\mid K\subset\lambda L\}\in[0,\infty].

We also define
R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)\coloneqq\max\{R_{0}(K,L),R_{0}(L,K)\}
.

For
L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
, it clearly holds
R_{0}(K,L)=\max_{x\in K}\left\|x\right\|_{L}
.
An easy volume argument further shows for
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that
R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)\geq 1
with equality if and only if
K=L
.

Remark 5.4
.

Note that the covering radius

coincides with the circumradius

if and only if
0
is a circumcenter.
Unlike the latter, the covering radius depends on translations of

and

Furthermore,

shows that there always exist translates

and

, and either

or

.
It is not hard to see that this yields

\max\{R(K,L),R(L,K)\}=\min\{R_{0}^{\max}(K-x,L-y):x\in K,y\in L\},

where the minimum might not be attained for any pair of

and

If

and

In particular, in the latter case it holds

The assertion of the next lemma is clear from Proposition

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
f,g\colon\mathbb{S}_{2}\to[0,\infty]
be any maps.
Define

R_{0}(K_{f},K_{g})\leq\sup\left\{\frac{f(a)}{g(a)}:a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}\right\}

,
where we write

(11)

\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\coloneqq\inf\{\lambda>0:\alpha\leq\lambda\beta\}=\begin{cases}0&\text{, if $\alpha=0$ or $\beta=\infty$}\\
\infty&\text{, if $\alpha\neq 0$ and $\beta=0$, or $\alpha=\infty$ and $\beta\neq\infty$}\\
\frac{\alpha}{\beta}&\text{, else}\end{cases}

for any
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\infty]

Let

If

Otherwise, we have for any

\frac{f(a)}{g(a)}\leq\sigma<\infty

and thus with (

.
Hence,

K_{f}\subset H_{(a,f(a))}^{\leq}\subset H_{(a,(\sigma+\varepsilon)g(a))}^{\leq}=(\sigma+\varepsilon)H_{(a,g(a))}^{\leq},

by intersecting over all

.
Letting

.
Let

for all

\frac{f(a^{i})}{g(a^{i})}\nearrow\sigma

(i\to\infty)
.

(ii)

If

6.
Banach-Mazur Distance

Theorem
5.6
lets us estimate the Banach-Mazur distance between

(or

)
and the

The special case for

It extends the rather well-known result that for any

minimizes the Banach-Mazur distance to

among all symmetric convex bodies.
A generalization of this property to the four standard symmetrizations already appears in

.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let

and

.
Then

Moreover, if
K
is Minkowski centered,

, and

symmetric, then

Proof.

(
12
) implies

\displaystyle K\subset\conv(K\cup L)

\displaystyle\subset m_{-p}(R_{0}^{\max}(K,L),1)\underline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset m_{-p}(R_{0}^{\max}(K,L),1)C

\displaystyle\subset m_{-p}(R_{0}^{\max}(K,L),1)\overline{M}_{p}(K,L)\subset R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)\cdot(K\cap L)\subset R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)K

and therefore immediately the upper bound.

If

is Minkowski centered and

, then

.
Moreover, it is well-known that

]

7.
The Geometric Mean

One important motivation behind trying to find an appropriate definition of the geometric mean of convex bodies lies in the log-Brunn-Minkowski conjecture.
With our notation here, it states (in the case of equal weights, which would imply the general case) that

(28)

\forall K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\text{ 0-symmetric}\colon\vol(\overline{M}_{0}(K,L))\geq\sqrt{\vol(K)\cdot\vol(L)}.

One theoretically possible approach to prove this conjecture is to try to define a geometric mean
G\colon\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\times\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that satisfies
for
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}

(29)

G(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)

and

(30)

\text{$K,L$ 0-symmetric}\implies\vol(G(K,L))\geq\sqrt{\vol(K)\cdot\vol(L)}.

Such an approach is, for example,
used in
[
7
]
to re-establish the validity of (
28
)
for unconditional bodies and bodies with
n
symmetries.

The goal that we focus on is the extension of axiomatic descriptions of the geometric mean for real numbers to more general structures.
In
[
15
]
, the cases of matrices and metric spaces are considered among others.
There have also been multiple attempts to find a satisfying definition of the geometric mean of convex bodies in the past
(cf.
[
20
,
21
,
23
]
).
Unfortunately, as the authors of these papers note themselves,
most of these constructions either fail to satisfy some natural properties one would expect from such a geometric mean,
or only make sense for 0-symmetric bodies.
There exists one exception:
The geometric mean considered by Rotem in
[
23
]

can be shown to satisfy almost all properties considered in this section, except possibly
(P8)
below.
However, this construction is defined implicitly and involves rather technical tools such that it becomes difficult to work with.

The aim of this section is to present and analyze a more natural and technically rather simple construction that fulfills all those proposed properties.
Our suggestion is based on the idea of an iterative building of means, which has been pursued by Milman and Rotem in
[
20
]
.
It has also been considered already in
[
8
]
, and is one of the characterizations of the geometric mean in
[
15
]
.
For
p\in(0,\infty)
,
\alpha,\beta>0
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
, if one defines recursively

\displaystyle m_{p}^{1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq m_{p}(\alpha,\beta),

\displaystyle m_{-p}^{1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq m_{-p}(\alpha,\beta),

\displaystyle m_{p}^{i+1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq m_{p}(m_{p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta),m_{-p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta)),

\displaystyle m_{-p}^{i+1}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq m_{-p}(m_{p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta),m_{-p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta)),

it is not hard to show that
(m_{p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(m_{-p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are converging sequences with

(31)

\lim_{i\to\infty}m_{p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta)=\lim_{i\to\infty}m_{-p}^{i}(\alpha,\beta)=m_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=\sqrt{\alpha\beta},

independently of
p
.
In case of
p=1
and replacing
\alpha
and
\beta
with positive definite matrices,
the analogous process still yields a common limit that is known as the geometric mean of the initial matrices
(cf.
[
15
, Section 3]
).

In
[
20
]
, this idea is extended to convex bodies for
p\geq 1
, mainly focusing on
p=1
,
by mixing upper
p
-means and lower
(-p)
-means.
Unfortunately, as demonstrated by Magazinov’s example in
[
20
, Appendix]
,
the resulting geometric mean for
p=1
does not posses the so called
(geometric) scaling property
(
(P9)
, below).

To remedy this, we extend the construction to general
p\in\mathbb{R}
and show that this property is satisfied if we use
p=0
instead.
Recall that upper and lower
p
-means of convex bodies coincide only in special cases by Theorem
3.1
.
Thus, in contrast to real numbers, convergence of the sequences for
p=0
is non-trivial for convex bodies
and it is unclear whether the constructions for
p
and
-p
coincide.

The original proof by Milman and Rotem for convergence of the above process to a common limit for convex bodies
can be adapted for
p\in[0,1)
(cf. Remark
7.3
below), but not for
p<0
.
We therefore present a proof that works for general
p\in\mathbb{R}
.
It has the added advantage of proving a bound on the convergence speed of the construction.

Theorem 7.1
.

For
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and
p\in\mathbb{R}
,
we define recursively for
i\in\mathbb{N}\colon

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{p}^{1}(K,L)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{M}_{p}(K,L),

\displaystyle\underline{M}_{-p}^{1}(K,L)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\underline{M}_{-p}(K,L),

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{p}^{i+1}(K,L)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{M}_{p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L),\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)),

\displaystyle\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+1}(K,L)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\underline{M}_{-p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L),\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)).

Then the sequences
(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

converge to a common Hausdorff limit
G_{p}(K,L)\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
.

Proof.

For ease of notation, we drop the arguments
K
and
L
.
Our goal is to prove that
(\overline{M}_{p}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are Cauchy sequences with the same limit.
With the notation
\overline{M}_{p}^{0}\coloneqq K
and
\underline{M}_{-p}^{0}\coloneqq L
,
it follows for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
from induction on
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

\underline{M}_{-\infty}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i})=\overline{M}_{p}^{i}\cap\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}\subset\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\overline{M}_{p}^{i+j}\\
\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+j}\end{array}\right\}\subset\conv(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}\cup\underline{M}_{-p}^{i})=\overline{M}_{\infty}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}).

Theorems
5.6
and
5.7

now imply for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
h,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

\displaystyle R_{0}^{\max}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i+h},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+j})

\displaystyle\leq R_{0}(\overline{M}_{\infty}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}),\underline{M}_{-\infty}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}))=R_{0}^{\max}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i})

\displaystyle=R_{0}^{\max}(\overline{M}_{p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i-1},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i-1}),\underline{M}_{-p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i-1},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i-1}))\leq m_{|p|}(R_{0}^{\max}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i-1},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i-1}),1).

Induction on
i\in\mathbb{N}
thus shows

\displaystyle R_{0}^{\max}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i+h},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+j})\leq R_{p}(i)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\underbrace{m_{|p|}(m_{|p|}(...m_{|p|}}_{\text{$i$ times}}(R_{0}^{\max}(K,L),1)...,1),1)

\displaystyle{\thinspace}=\begin{cases}\left(1+\frac{R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)^{|p|}-1}{2^{i}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{|p|}}}&\text{if }p\neq 0\\
R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2^{i}}}}&\text{if }p=0\end{cases}.

The same holds true for the pairs
\overline{M}_{p}^{i+h}(K,L)
,
\overline{M}_{p}^{i+j}(K,L)

and
\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+h}(K,L)
,
\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+j}(K,L)
.
Since for any
K^{\prime},L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
and
R^{\prime}>0
with
K^{\prime},L^{\prime}\subset R^{\prime}\mathbb{B}_{2}
,

\displaystyle d_{H}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}\left\|\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{K^{\prime}}}-\frac{x}{\left\|x\right\|_{L^{\prime}}}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}\max\left\{\frac{1}{\left\|x\right\|_{K^{\prime}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{R_{0}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})}\right),\frac{1}{\left\|x\right\|_{L^{\prime}}}\left(1-\frac{1}{R_{0}(L^{\prime},K^{\prime})}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq R^{\prime}\left(1-\frac{1}{R_{0}^{\max}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})}\right)\leq R^{\prime}(R_{0}^{\max}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})-1),

we obtain for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
h,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that

(32)

\max\{d_{H}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i+h},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+j}),d_{H}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i+h},\overline{M}_{p}^{i+j}),d_{H}(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+h},\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+j})\}\leq R_{0}(\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L),\mathbb{B}_{2})(R_{p}(i)-1).

Now
R_{p}(i)\to 1
(
i\to\infty)
implies that

(\overline{M}_{p}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are Cauchy sequences
and converge to a common limit.
Since all
\overline{M}_{p}^{i}
contain
K\cap L
, the joint limit contains 0 as an interior point.
∎

Remark 7.2
.

The announced bound on the rate at which
(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and

(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L))_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
converge now follows from (
32
):

(33)

\max\{d_{H}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L),G_{p}(K,L)),d_{H}(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L),G_{p}(K,L))\}\leq R_{0}(\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L),\mathbb{B}_{2})(R_{p}(i)-1).

We can extend
R_{p}
to a function on
\mathbb{R}
, and see that it is differentiable.
If
R_{0}^{\max}(K,L)\neq 1
, i.e.
K\neq L
,
l’Hôpital’s rule shows

\lim_{i\to\infty}\frac{R_{0}(\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L),\mathbb{B}_{2})(R_{p}(i+1)-1)}{R_{0}(\overline{M}_{\infty}(K,L),\mathbb{B}_{2})(R_{p}(i)-1)}=\frac{1}{2}.

In particular, the upper bound in (
33
) converges to
0
Q-linearly with rate
1/2
.

Furthermore, it is easy to see from the definition of
R_{p}(i)
that it is increasing in
|p|
.
Thus, the convergence
\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)\to G_{p}(K,L)
(
i\to\infty
) is locally uniform in
p\in\mathbb{R}
.
For fixed
i\in\mathbb{N}
, it can additionally be shown using Theorem
5.6

that
\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)
is continuous in
p\in\mathbb{R}
.
The Moore-Osgood Theorem
[
26
, pp. 139–140]
therefore implies for
q\in\mathbb{R}
that

G_{q}(K,L)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\overline{M}_{q}^{i}(K,L)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\lim_{p\to q}\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)=\lim_{p\to q}\lim_{i\to\infty}\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)=\lim_{p\to q}G_{p}(K,L),

i.e. that
G_{p}(K,L)
is continuous in
p\in\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 7.3
.

If
p\in[0,\infty)
, then Theorem
7.1
can be obtained adjusting the proof of
[
20
, Theorem 6]
.
It has the added advantage of proving monotonicity of the sequences involved, which is needed later.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
and monotonicity of the sequences involved for
p\in[0,\infty)
.

We again drop the arguments
K
and
L
.
Induction on
i\in\mathbb{N}
and Lemma
2.1

yield
\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}\subset\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+1}\subset\overline{M}_{p}^{i+1}\subset\overline{M}_{p}^{i}
.
Thus,
(\overline{M}_{p}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
are monotonous and bounded sequences of convex bodies.
As such, they converge to some respective Hausdorff limits
\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty},\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}

with
\underline{M}_{-p}^{1}\subset\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty}\subset\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty}
.
In particular,
0\in\inter(\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty})\cap\inter(\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty})
is satisfied.
Moreover, for any
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty}}(a)=\lim_{i\to\infty}h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i+1}}(a)\leq\lim_{i\to\infty}m_{p}(h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i}}(a),h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}}(a))=m_{p}(h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty}}(a),h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty}}(a)),

which implies
h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty}}\leq h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty}}
by
p\in[0,\infty)

and consequently
\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty}\subset\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty}
.
Thus, the assertion is true for
G_{p}(K,L)\coloneqq\overline{M}_{p}^{\infty}=\underline{M}_{-p}^{\infty}
.
∎

The alternative proof does not necessarily work for
p<0

since
\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)

and
\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)\subset\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)
might both be violated.

We will see throughout this section that all the
G_{p}
satisfy a number of properties that one would expect of a geometric mean,
while
G_{0}
fulfills them all.
In contrast, it is known that some of the
G_{p}
for
p\neq 0
fail to satisfy
(P9)

(and therefore possibly also (
35
) below).
This, and the rather simple definition of
G_{0}
, lead us to suggest considering it as
the
geometric mean of convex bodies.

We turn to the properties of the geometric mean,
first considering those analyzed in
[
20
,
21
,
23
]
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Let
K,K^{\prime},L,L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
with
K\subset K^{\prime}
,
L\subset L^{\prime}
,

A,P\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
A
regular and
P
positive definite,
and
\alpha,\beta>0
.
Then for all
p\in\mathbb{R}\colon

(P1)

G_{p}(K,K)=K
.

(P2)

G_{p}
is symmetric in its arguments:
G_{p}(K,L)=G_{p}(L,K)
.

(P3)

G_{p}
is increasing in both arguments:
G_{p}(K,L)\subset G_{p}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})
.

(P4)

G_{p}
is continuous in both arguments w.r.t. the Hausdorff distance.

(P5)

G_{p}
is invariant under regular linear transformation:
G_{p}(AK,AL)=AG_{p}(K,L)
.

(P6)

G_{p}(PK,K^{\circ})=P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\cdot\mathbb{B}_{2}
, and in particular
G_{p}(K,K^{\circ})=\mathbb{B}_{2}
.

(P7)

G_{p}
commutes with polarity:
(G_{p}(K,L))^{\circ}=G_{p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
.

(P8)

\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subset G_{p}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
.

For
p=0
, we have in addition:

(P8)

\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subset G_{0}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
with equality on either side if and only if

n=1
or
K
and
L
are dilatates of each other.

(P9)

G_{0}
satisfies the (geometric) scaling property:
G_{0}(\alpha K,\beta L)=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\cdot G_{0}(K,L)
.

In the following, we first discuss why these properties are desirable for a geometric mean and then prove them.
For
(P2)
–
(P8)
, we reuse the methods employed in
[
20
,
23
]
for
G_{1}
.
For the convenience of the reader, we restate the proof ideas (adjusted for general
p\in\mathbb{R}
), but omit the details.

(P1)
–
(P5)
are often demanded for any kind of (unweighted) mean of convex bodies.

Proof of
(P1)
–
(P5)
.

(P1)
is an immediate consequence of
\underline{M}_{0}(K,K)=K=\overline{M}_{0}(K,K)

and
(P8)
once the latter property is proved.

By induction, we obtain for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
that

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(L,K)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)=\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(L,K),

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ and }\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)\subset\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(AK,AL)=A\cdot\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(AK,AL)=A\cdot\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L),

where the base cases are clear from definitions and Lemma
2.1
.
Using that Hausdorff limits carry over subset relations and are continuous w.r.t. regular linear maps,
we obtain
(P2)
,
(P3)
, and
(P5)
.

To see that
(P4)
follows from
(P3)
and
(P5)
,
let
(K^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(L^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be sequences in
\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that converge to
K
and
L
, respectively.
For
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, it holds
(1-\varepsilon)K\subset K^{i}\subset(1+\varepsilon)K
for all sufficiently large
i
,
and analogously for
L
.
Thus

(1-\varepsilon)G_{p}(K,L)=G_{p}((1-\varepsilon)K,(1-\varepsilon)L)\subset G_{p}(K^{i},L^{i})\subset G_{p}((1+\varepsilon)K,(1+\varepsilon)L)=(1+\varepsilon)G_{p}(K,L)

for all sufficiently large
i
, which shows
G_{p}(K^{i},L^{i})\to G_{p}(K,L)
(
i\to\infty
).
∎

For the properties involving polars, recall that for
\alpha,\beta>0
,
it holds
(\sqrt{\alpha\beta})^{-1}=\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}\cdot\beta^{-1}}
and
\sqrt{\alpha\cdot\alpha^{-1}}=1
.
If we interpret
C^{\circ}
as the inverse of a convex body
C\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
,
then
(P7)
is the direct analog of the first identity.
The “in particular”-part of
(P6)
extends the second identity with the number “1” replaced by the neutral element of polarization
\mathbb{B}_{2}
.
The first part of
(P6)
is a strengthening that has first been considered in
[
20
]
.
It lets us derive an explicit representation of
G_{p}(K,L)
if
K
and
L
are 0-symmetric ellipsoids (cf.
[
20
, Proposition 10]
).

Proof of
(P6)
and
(P7)
.

By induction, we obtain for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
that

(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L))^{\circ}=\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})\text{ and }(\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L))^{\circ}=\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ}),

where the base case is clear from Lemma
2.1
.
Then, we use that Hausdorff limits are continuous w.r.t. polarization on
\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
to obtain
(P7)
.

The “in particular”-part of
(P6)
follows from
(P2)
and
(P7)
by

(G_{p}(K,K^{\circ}))^{\circ}=G_{p}(K^{\circ},(K^{\circ})^{\circ})=G_{p}(K^{\circ},K)=G_{p}(K,K^{\circ}),

which implies
G_{p}(K,K^{\circ})=\mathbb{B}_{2}
.
Now,
(P5)
additionally implies

G_{p}(PK,K^{\circ})=G_{p}(P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K,P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}P^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K^{\circ})=P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}G_{p}(P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K,(P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}K)^{\circ})=P^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbb{B}_{2}.

∎

Since the geometric mean is the 0-mean for positive real numbers,
we expect the geometric mean of convex bodies to contain all
p
-means for
p<0
,
and to be contained in all
p
-means for
p>0
,
where we use the definition of
p
-mean in (
3
).
Thus, (
4
) shows that any geometric mean should satisfy
(P8)
,
or at the bare minimum the harmonic mean - geometric mean - arithmetic mean inequality

\underline{M}_{-1}(K,L)=\left(\frac{K^{\circ}+L^{\circ}}{2}\right)^{\circ}\subset G(K,L)\subset\frac{K+L}{2}=\overline{M}_{1}(K,L).

For
G_{0}
, we can even characterize the equality cases in
(P8)
, which leads us to
(P8)
.
Since we do not want a geometric mean to be biased toward
p
-means for positive or negative values of
p
,
it seems natural to demand that a geometric mean
G
that satisfies
(P8)

should only coincide with
\underline{M}_{0}
or
\overline{M}_{0}
if
\underline{M}_{0}
and
\overline{M}_{0}
themselves coincide.
This means that
G
should also satisfy
(P8)
by Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of
(P8)
and
(P8)
.

To prove
(P8)
, we define recursively for all
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\colon

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq h_{K},

\displaystyle\psi_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq h_{L},

\displaystyle\varphi_{i+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq m_{p}(\varphi_{i},\psi_{i}),

\displaystyle\psi_{i+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq m_{-p}(\varphi_{i},\psi_{i}).

With the notation
\overline{M}_{p}^{0}(K,L)\coloneqq K
and
\underline{M}_{-p}^{0}(K,L)\coloneqq L
,
it follows from induction on
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
that
h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)}\leq\varphi_{i}
and

h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)}\leq\psi_{i}
:
The case
i=0
is clear from definitions.
The induction step is obtained from

h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i+1}(K,L)}=h_{\overline{M}_{p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L),\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L))}\leq m_{p}(h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)},h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)})\leq m_{p}(\varphi_{i},\psi_{i})=\varphi_{i+1}

and by Lemma
2.1
also

\displaystyle h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{i+i}(K,L)}

\displaystyle=h_{\underline{M}_{-p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L),\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L))}\leq h_{\overline{M}_{-p}(\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L),\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L))}

\displaystyle\leq m_{-p}(h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)},h_{\underline{M}_{-p}^{i}(K,L)})\leq m_{-p}(\varphi_{i},\psi_{i})=\psi_{i+1}.

Therefore, by (
31
)

h_{G_{p}(K,L)}=\lim_{i\to\infty}h_{\overline{M}_{p}^{i}(K,L)}\leq\limsup_{i\to\infty}\varphi_{i}=m_{0}(h_{K},h_{L}),

which implies
G_{p}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
by the definition of the latter set.

(P7)
further implies

G_{p}(K,L)=(G_{p}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ}))^{\circ}\supset(\overline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ}))^{\circ}=\underline{M}_{0}(K,L).

For
(P8)
, we observe that Remark
7.3
shows

\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subset\underline{M}_{0}(\overline{M}_{0}(K,L),\underline{M}_{0}(K,L))\subset G_{0}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(\overline{M}_{0}(K,L),\underline{M}_{0}(K,L))\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L).

Now, equality on the left-hand side in
(P8)
yields equality in

\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)=\underline{M}_{-\infty}(\overline{M}_{0}(K,L),\underline{M}_{0}(K,L))\subset\underline{M}_{0}(\overline{M}_{0}(K,L),\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)),

which by Theorem
3.2
shows
\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)=\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
.
This in turn implies
n=1
or that
K
is a dilatate of
L
by Theorem
3.1
.
Equality on the right-hand side in
(P8)
can be handled analogously.
Lastly, if
n=1
or
K
is a dilatate of
L
,
then Theorem
3.1

shows equality on both sides in
(P8)
.
∎

Lastly,
(P9)
is again a direct extension of the behavior of the geometric mean for real numbers.
It is what mainly sets
G_{0}
apart from the other
G_{p}
,
p\neq 0
,
as for example
[
20
, Appendix]
shows that
G_{1}
violates this property.

Proof of
(P9)
.

We have

\overline{M}_{0}(\alpha K,\beta L)=\bigcap_{a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}H_{(a,\sqrt{\alpha\beta h_{K}(a)h_{L}(a)})}^{\leq}=\bigcap_{a\in\mathbb{S}_{2}}\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\cdot H_{(a,\sqrt{h_{K}(a)h_{L}(a)})}^{\leq}=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\cdot\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)

and by Lemma
2.1
also

\underline{M}_{0}(\alpha K,\beta L)=(\overline{M}_{0}(\alpha^{-1}\cdot K^{\circ},\beta^{-1}\cdot L^{\circ}))^{\circ}=(\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}\beta^{-1}}\cdot\overline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ}))^{\circ}=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\cdot\underline{M}_{0}(K,L).

Thus, it follows from induction that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}

(34)

\displaystyle\overline{M}_{0}^{i}(\alpha K,\beta L)=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\cdot\overline{M}_{0}^{i}(K,L)

\displaystyle\text{ and }\underline{M}_{0}^{i}(\alpha K,\beta L)=\sqrt{\alpha\beta}\cdot\underline{M}_{0}^{i}(K,L)

and therefore, by basic properties of Hausdorff limits, that
(P9)
holds true.
∎

Let us observe that this proof of
(P9)
cannot be reused for any
p\neq 0
.
As Magazinov’s example
[
20
, Appendix]
shows for
p=1
,
this cannot be remedied even with different proof ideas for general
p\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us further point out that the above proof shows that the properties
(P1)
,
(P4)
, and
(P6)

are automatically fulfilled for any function
G\colon\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\times\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}

that satisfies the other six properties in Theorem
7.4
.
To our knowledge, it is an open problem whether the remaining six properties already suffice to uniquely characterize
G_{0}
(cf.
[
19
]
)
or whether additional properties are needed to do so.

Next, we consider some additional properties that one would expect of a geometric mean.
They are concerned with various aspects of symmetrization and measuring (a)symmetry.
We again first state, then discuss, and finally proof them.

Corollary 7.5
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
.
Then for all
p\in\mathbb{R}\colon

(P10)

G_{p}(K,-K)
is
0
-symmetric.

(P11)

If
K
is Minkowski centered, then
d_{BM}(K,G_{p}(K,-K))=s(K)
.

(P12)

If
K
and
L
are Minkowski centered, then
s(G_{p}(K,L))\leq\max\{s(K),s(L)\}
,
where equality implies that
G_{p}(K,L)
is Minkowski centered.
In particular, if
K
and
L
are
0
-symmetric, then so is
G_{p}(K,L)
.

It is natural to expect
(P10)
of any kind of (unweighted) mean of a convex body and its negative
if it should not be biased toward either of them.

(P11)
is mostly expected due to the context given by Corollary
6.1
.
The latter in particular shows for a Minkowski centered
K\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that
its
p
-symmetrizations according to the definition in (
3
),
i.e.
p\neq 0
and
L=-K
, are equidistant from
K
w.r.t. the Banach-Mazur distance.
It seems appropriate to demand that the 0-symmetrization should also have the same distance from
K
in this case.

Lastly, it is natural to expect a “mean” of 0-symmetric convex bodies to also be 0-symmetric.

(P12)
generalizes this behavior to Minkowski centered bodies
in the sense that it would be somewhat counter-intuitive if a “mean” of
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
would be more asymmetric than both
K
and
L
.
It should rather be at a structural midpoint between
K
and
L
, and at least satisfy the inequality in
(P12)
.

Let us briefly discuss the assumption of Minkowski centeredness in
(P12)
:
All
G_{p}
depend on the translation of their arguments.
The example below shows that
G_{p}(K,L)
does not need to be symmetric if
K
and
L
are symmetric with their centers not at the origin,
even if their centers coincide.
We therefore need to fix an appropriate translation of the bodies
K
and
L
to obtain meaningful results even in this special case.
In the context of Minkowski asymmetry, requiring Minkowski centeredness is a rather intuitive choice.
It would, for example, restrict
K
and
L
to have their centers at the origin if they are symmetric.

Example 7.6
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
K\coloneqq 18\mathbb{B}_{1}+2u^{n}
and
L\coloneqq\conv(\{5u^{n},-u^{n}\}\cup\{\pm 18u^{i}+2u^{n}:i\in[n-1]\})
.
Then
K
and
L
are
(2u^{n})
-symmetric.
However,
G_{p}(K,L)
is not symmetric for any
p\in\mathbb{R}\colon

By Lemma
2.2
,
the points
\sqrt{20\cdot 5}u^{n}=10u^{n},-\sqrt{16\cdot 1}u^{n}=-4u^{n},\pm 18u^{i}+2u^{n}
,
i\in[n-1]
,
lie in the boundary of both
\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)
and
\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
.
By
\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subset G_{p}(K,L)\subset\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
,
these points are also boundary points of
G_{p}(K,L)
.
Moreover,
G_{p}(K,L)\subset\conv(K\cup L)=K
implies that

\pm 18u^{1}+2u^{n}
is the only point in
G_{p}(K,L)\cap H_{(\pm u^{1},h_{G_{p}(K,L)}(\pm u^{1}))}
.
Thus, the only possible symmetry center of
G_{p}(K,L)
is
2u^{n}
.
However,
(2u^{n}-(-4u^{n}))=6u^{n}\neq 8u^{n}=(10u^{n}-2u^{n})
implies that
G_{p}(K,L)
is not
(2u^{n})
-symmetric, either.
Hence,
G_{p}(K,L)
is asymmetric.

In the following, we essentially show that all three properties in Corollary
7.5
are automatically satisfied
if the properties in Theorem
7.4
(except possibly
(P8)
and
(P9)
) are fulfilled.

Proof of Corollary
7.5
.

(P10)
is a direct consequence of
(P2)
and
(P5)
by

-G_{p}(K,-K)=G_{p}(-K,-(-K))=G_{p}(-K,K)=G_{p}(K,-K).

(P11)
follows immediately from

(P8)
,
(P10)
and Corollary
6.1
.

Lastly,
(P3)
and
(P5)
yield

\displaystyle-G_{p}(K,L)

\displaystyle=G_{p}(-K,-L)\subset G_{p}(s(K)K,s(L)L)

\displaystyle\subset G_{p}(\max\{s(K),s(L)\}\cdot K,\max\{s(K),s(L)\}\cdot L)=\max\{s(K),s(L)\}\cdot G_{p}(K,L)

and thus the inequality in
(P12)
.
It also shows the Minkowski centeredness in case of equality.
The claims about the case of 0-symmetric
K
and
L
are immediate consequences of the above.
∎

The last property that we consider in this section is a strengthening of
(P12)
for
G_{0}
,
whose proof relies on
(P9)
.

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
be Minkowski centered.
Then

(35)

s(G_{0}(K,L))\leq\sqrt{s(K)s(L)},

where equality implies that
G_{0}(K,L)
is Minkowski centered.
Moreover, for all
s,t\in[1,n]
, there exist Minkowski centered bodies
K^{\prime},L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
with
s(K^{\prime})=s
and
s(L^{\prime})=t

such that
s(G_{0}(K^{\prime},L^{\prime}))=\sqrt{st}
.

Note that the following proof of tightness essentially shows that the upper bound in (
35
)
cannot be improved for any function
G\colon\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\times\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that satisfies
(P8)
.

Proof.

For the inequality, we use
(P3)
,
(P5)
, and
(P9)
:

-G_{0}(K,L)=G_{0}(-K,-L)\subset G_{0}(s(K)K,s(L)L)=\sqrt{s(K)s(L)}\cdot G_{0}(K,L).

We also see that
\sqrt{s(K)s(L)}=s(G_{0}(K,L))
implies that
G_{0}(K,L)
is Minkowski centered.

To show tightness of the inequality,
let
T^{n}\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
be a Minkowski centered
n
-simplex.
For any
r\in[1,n]
, let
K_{r}\coloneqq T^{n}\cap(-rT^{n})
.
Then

-\frac{r}{n}T^{n}\subset(rT^{n})\cap(-T^{n})=-K_{r}\subset(rT^{n})\cap(-r^{2}T^{n})=rK_{r}\subset rT^{n}

together with
s(T^{n})=n
shows that
K_{r}
is Minkowski centered with
s(K_{r})=r
.
Moreover, by Lemma
2.1
, (
34
),
(
35
) and
(P8)
, there exists
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\displaystyle-\frac{\sqrt{st}}{n}T^{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\sqrt{st}}{n}\cdot\underline{M}_{0}(T^{n},T^{n})=-\underline{M}_{0}\left(\frac{s}{n}T^{n},\frac{t}{n}T^{n}\right)\subset-\underline{M}_{0}(K_{s},K_{t})\subset-G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t})

\displaystyle\subset s(G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t}))\cdot G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t})+c\subset s(G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t}))\cdot\overline{M}_{0}(K_{s},K_{t})+c

\displaystyle\subset s(G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t}))\cdot\overline{M}_{0}(T^{n},T^{n})+c=s(G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t}))\cdot T^{n}+c.

Thus,
s(T^{n})=n
implies
s(G_{0}(K_{s},K_{t}))\geq\sqrt{st}
.
∎

Remark 7.8
.

In closing the paper, let us again consider the approach of proving (
28
)
via finding a geometric mean
G\colon\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\times\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}

that satisfies (
29
) and (
30
).
Equality in (
28
) for
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
would then require
G(K,L)=\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)
.
If
G
additionally satisfies
(P7)
,
then also
(P8)
by (
29
),
and equality in (
28
) for
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
would further imply
G(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})=\underline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})
.
Since equality is known to hold in (
28
) if
K
and
L
are
axis-parallel
0
-symmetric boxes,
we would obtain from Theorem
3.1

in the case of
n\geq 2
and
K,L
not being dilatates of each other that

(36)

\underline{M}_{0}(K,L)\subsetneq G(K,L)=\overline{M}_{0}(K,L)\quad\text{and}\quad\underline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})=G(K^{\circ},L^{\circ})\subsetneq\overline{M}_{0}(K^{\circ},L^{\circ}).

The equality case in
(P8)
thus shows that
G_{0}
must violate (
30
),
so
G=G_{0}
is not a valid choice.
In general, it might not be impossible to define a map
G
as described above,
but the behavior outlined in (
36
)
seems somewhat unnatural if we want to think of
G
as a geometric mean as discussed above.

It is worth mentioning in this context that the dual inequality to (
28
) is proved
in
[
24
, Theorem 6.1]
.
The inequality there in particular yields

(37)

\forall K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\colon\vol(\underline{M}_{0}(K,L))\leq\sqrt{\vol(K)\cdot\vol(L)}.

If a function
G\colon\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\times\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}\to\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}
that satisfies (
29
),
(
30
), and
(P7)
exists,
then the above argument would show that equality in (
28
) for
K,L\in\mathcal{C}^{n}_{0}

implies equality in (
37
) for
K^{\circ}
and
L^{\circ}
.
To our knowledge, the characterization of the equality case for either inequality is still open.
