Tailoring Dynamical Fermionization: Delta kick cooling of a Tonks-Girardeau gas
Léonce Dupays, Jing Yang, Adolfo del Campo

Abstract
In one spatial dimension, quantum exchange statistics and interactions are inextricably intertwined. As a manifestation, the expansion dynamics of a Tonks-Girardeau gas is characterized by dynamical fermionization (DF), whereby the momentum distribution approaches that of a spin-polarized Fermi gas. Using a phase-space analysis and the unitary evolution of the one-body reduced density matrix, we show that DF can be tailored and reversed, using a generalization of delta kick cooling (DKC) to interacting systems, establishing a simple protocol to rescale the initial momentum distribution. The protocol applies to both expansions and compressions and can be used for microscopy of quantum correlations.



In one spatial dimension, the interchange of particle coordinates inevitably leads to their interactions. In the process, the quantum state accumulates a phase shift that is the sum of two contributions, one stemming from quantum statistics, and the other from scattering.
This fact makes it possible to relate the physics of some strongly correlated quantum gases to that of noninteracting systems with different quantum statistics.
This is the basis of the Bose-Fermi duality introduced by Girardeau in 1960 to describe a gas of one-dimensional hard-core bosons, which is now known as the Tonks-Girardeau (TG) gas
Girardeau 1960
;
Cazalilla et al. 2011
. The latter can be described in terms of a spin-polarized one-dimensional Fermi gas with no interactions. Pauli exclusion principle in the Fermi gas makes the wavefunction vanish at contact, a feature shared by the TG gas due to the hard-core interactions.
The wavefunctions of the two systems are identical for a given particle ordering and differ only in their symmetrization. The bosonic TG wavefunction
\Psi_{\rm TG}
can be obtained from that of the Fermi gas
\Psi_{\rm F}
by explicit symmetrization according to the Bose-Fermi mapping
\Psi_{\rm TG}=\prod_{j<k}{\rm sgn}(x_{k}-x_{j})\Psi_{\rm F}
.
Similar relations exist in systems governed by strongly-attractive
p
-wave interactions
Granger and Blume 2004
;
Girardeau et al. 2004
;
Girardeau and Minguzzi 2006
.
One-dimensional dualities can be extended to general exchange statistics, in and out of equilibrium
Girardeau 2006
;
del Campo 2008
, paving the way to the experimental realization of hard-core anyons
Keilmann et al. 2011
. They can be further used to describe systems with finite-interaction strength
Buljan et al. 2008
;
Batchelor et al. 2008
;
Guan et al. 2013a
, mixtures
Girardeau and Minguzzi 2007
, and spinor systems
Deuretzbacher et al. 2008
, among other examples
Cazalilla et al. 2011
;
Guan et al. 2013b
.

The TG gas can be considered as the strong-coupling limit of the Lieb-Liniger (LL) gas, which describes one-dimensional bosons subject to contact interactions of finite-strength
c

Lieb and Liniger 1963
;
Lieb 1963
;
Olshanii 1998
.
This model is integrable and solvable by Bethe ansatz.
The relevance of the LL gas to ultracold atom physics was established by Olshanii, who showed that ultracold atoms in tight waveguides are described by the LL model with tunable coupling constant
c

Olshanii 1998
.
The strongly interacting limit
c\rightarrow+\infty
leads to the TG regime, realized experimentally by making use of an optical lattice
Kinoshita et al. 2004
;
Kinoshita et al. 2005
;
Cazalilla 2004
.
The connection between the continuum and lattice version of hard-core bosons is well-understood
Cazalilla 2004
, and dynamical correlations are in one-to-one correspondence at low densities.

Local correlations such as the density profile are indistinguishable between dual systems. By contrast, correlations depending on the off-diagonal elements of the density matrix exhibit clear signatures of quantum statistics
Penrose and Onsager 1956
;
Lenard 1964
. A prominent example is the momentum distribution. While that of a 1D Fermi gas in the ground state exhibits a characteristic flat profile, that of the TG gas is sharply peaked at
k=0
and has power-law tails decaying as
1/k^{4}
.
Out of equilibrium, it was predicted that a TG gas under free expansion in 1D exhibits dynamical fermionization (DF), with the asymptotic momentum distribution of the TG gas matching that of free fermions
Rigol and Muramatsu 2005
;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
.
This phenomenon has been recently observed in the laboratory for the first time
Wilson et al. 2020
.
DF also governs the asymptotic behavior of an expanding LL gas, which enters the TG regime
Jukić et al. 2008
;
Jukić et al. 2009
. While it is conveniently described using scale-invariance, which makes the density profile at different times self-similar, it does not rely on it, and occurs whether the initial confinement is harmonic or not
del Campo and Muga 2006
;
del Campo 2008
;
Campbell et al. 2015
. Generalizations of this phenomenon have been reported for a fermionic analog of the TG gas
Girardeau and Minguzzi 2006
, hardcore anyons
del Campo 2008
, and spinor quantum gases
Alam et al. 2021
.
DF is generally justified as a result of free expansion along the axial direction: as the particle density decreases, the asymptotic momentum distribution is that of the rapidities, which are the conserved quantities in a many-body integrable quantum system
Sutherland 1998
;
Rigol and Muramatsu 2005
;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
;
del Campo and Muga 2006
;
del Campo 2008
;
Bolech et al. 2012
;
Campbell et al. 2015
;
Mei et al. 2016
.

In this Letter, we analyze DF in phase space in arbitrary scale-invariant processes, showing that its appearance is not restricted to expansions but can occur as well in an implosion protocol leading to a density increase. While the momentum distribution and density profile of dual systems under DF become equal, the one-body reduced density matrix is shown to evolve unitarily, making the distinguishability of the corresponding quantum states independent of time. As a result, DF can be reversed, making use of a generalization of delta kick cooling (DKC) to interacting systems, pulsing an external potential.
This allows to engineer protocols that rescale the momentum distribution for microscopy of quantum correlations.

TG gas in a time-dependent trap.—

Consider a TG gas in a harmonic trap, dual to an ideal Fermi gas in the same confinement
Girardeau et al. 2001
. In the ground state, the TG wavefunction is the absolute value of the fermionic one, which is given by a Slater determinant, e.g.,
\Psi_{\rm F}(x_{1},\dots,x_{\rm N})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\rm N}!}}\det_{n=0,k=1}^{{\rm N}-1,{\rm N}}[\phi_{n}(x_{k})]
in terms of the single-particle harmonic oscillator eigenstates. Both systems are scale invariant with dimensionality
D=1
, and their time-dependent coherent states take the form
Sutherland 1998
;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
;
Gritsev et al. 2010
;
del Campo 2011

\displaystyle\Psi\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{\frac{{\rm N}}{2}}}\exp\left[i\frac{m\dot{b}}{2\hbar b}\sum_{i=1}^{\rm N}x_{i}\,^{2}-i\int_{0}^{t}\frac{E(0)}{\hbar b(t^{\prime})^{2}}dt^{\prime}\right]

(1)

\displaystyle\times\Psi\left(\frac{x_{1}}{b},\dots,\frac{x_{\rm N}}{b},t=0\!\right)\,,

where the scaling factor
b(t)>0
is the solution of the Ermakov equation
\ddot{b}+\omega(t)^{2}b=\omega_{0}^{2}/b^{3}
with the initial conditions
b(0)=1
,
\dot{b}(0)=0
.
Note that this scaling law is not restricted to the ground-state but it is shared by any many-body eigenstate
\Psi(0)
with energy eigenvalue
E(0)
.
Quantities derived from
|\Psi(t)|^{2}
are shared by dual systems related by the Bose-Fermi mapping, given that
\prod_{j<k}[{\rm sgn}(x_{k}-x_{j})]^{2}=1
. By contrast, those dependent on the coherence in real space generally differ.
We focus on the one-body reduced density matrix (OBRDM), that contains all the information required to analyze one-body observables. It is defined as

\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime},t)={\rm N}\int dx_{2}\cdots dx_{\rm N}\Psi\left(x,x_{2}\cdots x_{\rm N},t\right)\Psi^{*}\left(x^{\prime},x_{2}\cdots x_{\rm N},t\right)
. From it, one can determine the density profile

\rho(x,t)=\rho_{1}(x,x,t)
, as well as the momentum distribution, making use of the Fourier transform

n(p,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int dxdx^{\prime}e^{-ip(x-x^{\prime})/\hbar}\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime},t)
.
Using (
1
), the OBRDM evolves according to

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime},t)=\frac{1}{b}\exp\left[i\frac{m\dot{b}}{2\hbar b}(x^{2}-x^{\prime}\,{}^{2})\right]\rho_{1}\left(\frac{x}{b},\frac{x^{\prime}}{b},t=0\right).

(2)

Similar relations hold for the time-evolution of higher-order reduced density matrices.
In the limit of adiabatic driving
\dot{b}/b\to 0
, the OBRDM is rescaled as
\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime},t)=\rho_{1}(x/b,x^{\prime}/b,0)/b
.
The use of controlled expansions involving time-dependent traps and engineered by shortcuts to adiabaticity has been proposed for implementing such scaling without the requirement of slow driving, but generally involve time-dependent traps
del Campo 2011
;
del Campo and Boshier 2012
. Such protocols realize in essence a dynamical microscope zooming in on correlations in the OBRDM.

Shared unitary evolution of the OBRDMs and its consequences.—
Interestingly, Eq. (
2
) indicates that the evolution of OBRDMs for both the TG gas and the spin-polarized ideal Fermi gas is unitary. More precisely, we introduce a generic label
A=\{{\rm TG,F}\}
for any of the dual systems and define the corresponding quantum state
\sigma^{A}=\frac{1}{{\rm N}}\int dxdx^{\prime}\rho_{1}^{A}(x,x^{\prime})|x\rangle\langle x^{\prime}|
such that
{\rm Tr}(\sigma^{A})=1
. Then Eq. (
2
) implies
\sigma^{A}(t)=U(t)\sigma^{A}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)
, where

\displaystyle U(t)=\exp\left[i\frac{m\dot{b}}{2\hbar b}x^{2}\right]\exp\left[-i\frac{{\rm ln}b}{2\hbar}(xp+px)\right],

(3)

and the rightmost term is the dilatation operator implementing a scaling transformation in real space by a factor
b
. Note that
U(t)
is the same for both
A=\{{\rm TG,F}\}
. In fact, under scale invariant dynamics, the evolution of the quantum state associated with any
s
-body reduced density matrix is also unitary
SM
.

The identical unitary evolution of the OBRDMs has several consequences: (i) The spectral decomposition of the OBRDM is of the form
\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime},t)=\sum_{\mu}\lambda_{\mu}(0)\phi_{\mu}(x,t)\phi_{\mu}(x^{\prime},t)
, where
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{\mu}(0)
of the OBRDM, which correspond to the occupation numbers of the natural orbitals
\phi_{\mu}(x,t)
, are constant in time.
It follows that the dynamics is isentropic, i.e., it preserves the von Neumann entropy
S(\sigma^{A})=-{\rm Tr}[\sigma^{A}\log\sigma^{A}]
.
The situation in the continuum is thus in contrast with that reported for hard-core bosons in an optical lattice
Rigol and Muramatsu 2005
.
In addition, the natural orbitals fulfill the relation
\phi_{\mu}(x,t)=\exp\left[i\frac{m\dot{b}}{2\hbar b}x^{2}\right]\phi_{\mu}(x/b,0)/\sqrt{b}
. (ii) Consider the Ulhmann fidelity defined as
\mathcal{F}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})={\rm Tr}\left(\sqrt{\sqrt{\sigma}\sigma^{\prime}\sqrt{\sigma}}\right)

Uhlmann 1992
;
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
as a distinguishablity measure between the quantum states
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
.
Given that the Uhlmann fidelity is invariant under conjugation of its arguments by a common unitary, it follows that

\mathcal{F}[U(t)\sigma^{\rm TG}(0)U^{\dagger}(t),U(t)\sigma{{}^{\rm F}}(0)U^{\dagger}(t)]=\mathcal{F}[\sigma^{\rm TG}(0),\sigma^{\rm F}(0)].

(4)

As a result, even if the density profile, and the momentum distribution under DF, are shared by both dual systems, their quantum states remain equally distinguishable at all times. (iii) The unitary evolution in quantum mechanics can be represented by a kernel in the phase space, convoluted with the initial Wigner function of the system
Garcia-Calderon and Moshinsky 1980
. Under scale-invariant dynamics, the convolution simplifies to a linear canonical transformation on the initial Wigner function
SM
, which motivates the phase-space analysis of DF.
(iv) Finally, since the unitary evolution is invertible, this paves the way to reverse DF, which we shall discuss in what follows.

Phase-space analysis of DF.—

The Wigner function associated with the OBRDM can be represented as a function of the coordinate
x
and the canonically-conjugated momentum
p

Wigner 1932
;
Hillery et al. 1984
,

\displaystyle W(x,p,t)=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho_{1}(x-y,x+y,t)e^{2ipy/\hbar}dy\,.

(5)

The marginals of
W(x,p,t)
correspond to the density profile
\rho(x,t)=\rho_{1}(x,x,t)=\int W(x,p,t)dp
and the momentum distribution
n(p,t)=\rho_{1}(p,p,t)=\int W(x,p,t)dx
. From the dynamics (
2
) of the OBRDM, following an arbitrary modulation of the trapping frequency
\omega(t)
,
the exact time-evolution of the Wigner function reads
Shanahan et al. 2018
;
SM

\displaystyle W(x,p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W\left(\frac{x}{b},bp-m\dot{b}x,t=0\right),

(6)

where we note that
W
does not need to be positive, i.e., it can describe a non-classical state.
Note that this evolution is common to all scale-invariant systems, e.g., such as the single-particle time-dependent harmonic oscillator.
The Wigner function is rescaled, stretching (compressing) the density profile along the
x
-axis, and compressing (stretching) the momentum distribution along the
p
-axis if
b(t)>1
(
b(t)<1
). The supplementary term
-m\dot{b}x
involves a shift in phase-space, which induces DF.

Both for the TG and Fermi gas, the density profile exhibits explicitly the scale invariance, while the asymptotic momentum distribution can be related to the initial density profile
SM
:

\displaystyle\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{b}\rho\left(\frac{x}{b},0\right),n(p,t)\approx\frac{1}{m\dot{b}}\rho\left(\frac{p}{m\dot{b}},0\right).

(7)

Note that the relation between
n(p,t)
and
\rho(x,0)
is different from what is known in time-of-flight imaging, connecting
\rho(x,t)
to
n(p,0)
.
The results in (
7
) are consistent with previous studies limited to sudden expansions
Jukić et al. 2009
;
Campbell et al. 2015
. Note the first equation of Eq. (
7
) is exact while the second is approximate, which requires that the initial Wigner function decays over the characteristic spread
\Delta p
so that during the dynamics
\Delta p\ll x_{0}m\dot{b}b
with
x_{0}=\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega_{0})}
, which is equivalent to the condition
\dot{b}b\gg 2\omega_{0}
taking
\Delta p\approx 2p_{0}
, where
p_{0}=\hbar/x_{0}
. For a rigorous asymptotic analysis, see
SM
. In the special case of a harmonic trap, the initial density profile of the TG gas (and its dual system, the spin-polarized Fermi gas) can be expressed in terms of the rescaled momentum distribution of the Fermi gas

, making the term “DF” natural in this setting

;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
.
For the sake of demonstration, we consider the expansion of a TG gas initially confined in a harmonic trap with frequency
\omega_{0}
that is suddenly released in a wider trap with frequency

This process leads to periodic time-dependence of the scaling factor

As emphasized, the phase-space dynamics in Eq. (

The fact that the asymptotic momentum distribution can be also exactly related to that of the spin-polarized Fermi gas is a specific feature of the harmonic confinement, as single-particle eigenstates

Tailoring and reversing DF with kicks.—
The momentum shift in (
6
) is responsible for DF. Classically, one may expect to cancel it by applying a conservative force for a short period of time
\tau_{k}
inducing a momentum change
\delta p=-\tau_{k}\partial_{x}V(x)
, i.e., pulsing an external potential
V(x)
. This argument, limited to classical noninteracting systems, is the basis of delta-kick cooling (DKC)
Chu et al. 1986
;
Ammann and Christensen 1997
;
Morinaga et al. 1999
. In what follows, we make use of the extension of DKC for scale-invariant interacting systems. Under Eq. (
2
), excitations encoded in the phase factor proportional to
\dot{b}/b
, that are responsible for DF, can be explicitly canceled in an interacting system by applying a kick potential of appropriate strength. Canceling the phase allows to tailor the momentum distribution and reverse DF. Given that the phase oscillation in Eq. (
1
) is quadratic in the coordinates it can be canceled by pulsing an external harmonic trap with a given frequency
\omega_{k}
.
To this end, consider the
Hamiltonian with a
\delta
-kick applied at
t_{k}

\displaystyle H_{k}(t)=H(t)+\delta(t-t_{k})\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{k}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm N}}x_{i}\,^{2}.

(8)

The use of a delta function is justified when the duration of the pulse
\tau_{k}
is short with respect to other time scales
Dupays et al. 2021
.
The corresponding time-evolution operator admits the factorization

U_{\delta}(t_{F},0)=e^{-i\tau_{k}\frac{m\omega_{k}^{2}}{2\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm N}}x_{i}\,^{2}}U(t_{k},0)
,
where
U(t,t^{\prime})
is the propagator associated with
H(t)
, and
\tau_{k}
is a small time scale during which the kick is applied.
Considering the evolution from
t=0
to time
t_{k}+\tau_{k}
, one can choose the pulse parameters
\tau_{k}
and
\omega_{k}

such that

\displaystyle\tau_{k}\omega^{2}_{k}=\frac{\dot{b}(t_{k})}{b(t_{k})}\,.

(9)

This requires pulsing a harmonic trap with
\omega_{k}>0
in an expansion with
\dot{b}(t_{k})>0
and an inverted harmonic trap with purely imaginary frequency
i\omega_{k}
in a compression with
\dot{b}(t_{k})<0
.
In either case, the application of the kick cancels DF, and brings back the OBRDM to the initial one up to a scaling of the coordinates with respect to
b
,

\displaystyle\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime},t_{k}+\tau_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b(t_{k})}\rho_{1}\left(\frac{x}{b(t_{k})},\frac{x\,^{\prime}}{b(t_{k})},t=0\right).

(10)

Consequently, the momentum distribution after the kick is
n(p,t)=b\,n(pb,0)
, and similarly the Wigner function reduces to
W(x,p,t)=W_{0}(x/b,bp,0)
with
b=b(t_{k})
.
In turn, DKC prepares the same state that would have been obtained under adiabatic dynamics, without the requirement of slow driving.

In summary, we have established the unitary character of the dynamics of the OBRDM of a TG gas in a driven harmonic trap and point out its far-reaching consequences. The time evolution is exactly isentropic and preserves the occupation numbers of the natural orbitals.
As a result, DF does not affect the distinguishability between the OBRDMs of the dual systems, which is independent of time, e.g., as quantified by the Uhlmann fidelity.
For arbitrary driving of the trap frequency, the unitary character of the dynamics makes it possible to describe DF as a result of a canonical transformation in phase space, that relates the asymptotic momentum distribution to the initial density profile under rapid acceleration of the width of the atomic cloud. This relation holds for expansions as well as compressions leading to an increase of the interparticle density and is not restricted to the TG gas or systems with hard-core interactions.
Thanks to unitarity, DF can be further tailored and completely reversed by applying a kick with a pulsed external potential, generalizing DKC to interacting systems. This allows to rescale the initial momentum distribution without the requirement of slow driving. Our findings open the way to control nonequilibrium correlations in driven ultracold gases and can be tested in laboratory settings used in recent experiments
Wilson et al. 2020
. They should be generalizable to atomic mixtures, systems with
p
-wave interactions, finite coupling strength, fractional exchange statistics, and spinor gases, among other examples.

II
Representation of the scale-invariant unitary dynamics of the OBRDM by canonical transformation

As shown in Ref.
Garcia-Calderon and Moshinsky 1980
, any classical canonical transformation on the Wigner function corresponds to a unitary evolution in quantum mechanics, which is represented by a kernel function in the phase space. In particular, it was shown that for a linear canonical transformation,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\vec{r}\\
\vec{p}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{r}\,^{\prime}\\
\vec{p}\,^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},

(S12)

with
\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma=1
, the kernel function in the quantum mechanical evolution takes a very simple form,

\displaystyle K(\vec{r},\vec{p}|\vec{r}\,^{\prime},\vec{p}\,^{\prime})=\delta^{D}[\vec{r}\,^{\prime}-(\alpha\vec{r}+\beta\vec{p})]\delta^{D}[\vec{p}\,^{\prime}-(\gamma\vec{r}+\delta\vec{p})]\,,

where
\delta^{D}
the
D
-dimensional delta function. Thus, the evolution of the Wigner function is dictated by
Garcia-Calderon and Moshinsky 1980
;
Schuch and Moshinsky 2008

\displaystyle W(\vec{r},\vec{p},t)=\iint d^{D}\vec{r}\,^{\prime}d^{D}\vec{p}\,^{\prime}K(\vec{r},\vec{p}|\vec{r}\,^{\prime},\vec{p}\,^{\prime})W(\vec{r}\,^{\prime},\vec{p}\,^{\prime},0)=W(\alpha\vec{r}+\beta\vec{p},\gamma\vec{r}+\delta\vec{p},0).

(S14)

Under scale-invariance, the time-dependent Wigner function can be solely written in terms of the initial Wigner function:

\displaystyle W(\vec{r},\vec{p},t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\pi\hbar)^{D}}\int\frac{d^{D}\vec{y}}{b^{D}}\exp\left\{i\frac{m\dot{b}}{2\hbar b}[(\vec{r}-\vec{y})\,^{2}-(\vec{r}+\vec{y})\,^{2}]\right\}\rho_{1}\left(\frac{\vec{r}-\vec{y}}{b},\frac{\vec{r}+\vec{y}}{b},0\right)e^{2i\vec{p}\cdot\vec{y}/\hbar},

(S15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\pi\hbar)^{D}}\int\frac{d^{D}\vec{y}}{b^{D}}\rho_{1}\left(\frac{\vec{r}-\vec{y}}{b},\frac{\vec{r}+\vec{y}}{b},0\right)e^{2i\left(b\vec{p}-m\dot{b}\vec{r}\right)\cdot\vec{y}/(b\hbar)},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle W\left(\frac{\vec{r}}{b},b\vec{p}-m\dot{b}\vec{r},0\right).

Eqquation (
S15

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1/b&0\\
-m\dot{b}&b\end{pmatrix}.

(S16)

IV
Analysis of Dynamical Fermionization in time-dependent traps

As we have seen in the preceding section, the momentum distribution can be found in terms of the Fourier transform of the OBRDM
n(p,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi\hbar}\int dxdx\,^{\prime}\,e^{-ip(x-x\,^{\prime})/\hbar}\rho_{1}(x,x\,^{\prime};t)
. It is useful to make the change of variables
x/b\to x
in the distribution

\displaystyle n(p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{b}{2\pi\hbar}\int dxdx\,^{\prime}\,e^{i\phi(x,x^{\prime})}\rho_{1}\left(x,x\,^{\prime},t=0\right),

(S19)

\displaystyle\phi(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m\dot{b}b}{2\hbar}(x\,^{2}-x\,^{\prime}\,{}^{2})-\frac{pb}{\hbar}(x-x\,^{\prime}).

(S20)

Previous literature analyzed DF
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
making use of the Stationary Phase Approximation (SPA)
Bleistein and Handelsman 1986
, even in scenarios where the dynamics is not scale-invariant
Pedri 2005
;
del Campo 2008
. Here, we review this approach. The phase
\phi(x,x^{\prime})
(
S20
) has stationary points
x^{*}=x^{\prime*}=\frac{p}{m\dot{b}}
so that after Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\phi(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{m\dot{b}b}{\hbar}[(x-x^{*})^{2}-(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime*})^{2}].

(S21)

We introduce the reference length
x_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega_{0}}}
to perform the integration in dimensionless units. Note that the OBRDM
\rho_{1}(x,x^{\prime};t)
scales as
1/x_{0}
, while the momentum distribution scales as
x_{0}/\hbar
. One can insert the previous expression (
S21
) in the momentum distribution (
S19
). Provided that
\frac{1}{2}\frac{m\dot{b}b}{\hbar}\gg 1/x^{2}_{0}
(i.e.,
\dot{b}b\gg 2\omega_{0}
), one can make use of the SPA
Bleistein and Handelsman 1986
as proposed in
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005
to analyze DF. For instance, the free expansion gives
b(t)=\sqrt{1+(\omega_{0}t)^{2}}
,
\dot{b}=\frac{\omega^{2}_{0}t}{\sqrt{1+(\omega^{2}_{0}t)^{2}}}
, implying
\dot{b}b=\omega^{2}_{0}t
and
t_{DF}\gg\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}
, and leading to the asymptotic momentum distribution

\displaystyle n(p,t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{b}{2\pi\hbar}\int dxdx\,^{\prime}\,e^{i\frac{1}{2}\frac{m\dot{b}b}{\hbar}[(x-x^{*})^{2}-(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime*})^{2}]}\rho_{1}\left(x^{*},x^{\prime*},t=0\right),

(S22)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{b}{2\pi\hbar}x^{2}_{0}\int\frac{dx}{x_{0}}\frac{dx\,^{\prime}}{x_{0}}\,e^{i\frac{1}{2}\frac{\dot{b}b}{\omega_{0}}\frac{[(x-x^{*})^{2}-(x^{\prime}-x^{\prime*})^{2}]}{x^{2}_{0}}}\rho_{1}\left(x^{*},x^{\prime*},t=0\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{0}}{\dot{b}}\frac{x^{2}_{0}}{\hbar}\rho\left(x^{*},t=0\right)=\frac{1}{m\dot{b}}\rho\left(\frac{p}{m\dot{b}},t=0\right).

This equation can also be demonstrated without relying on scale-invariance when the dynamics is described by the single-particle free-propagator (no interactions)
Pedri 2005
.

For the harmonic trap, it is possible to go further and relate the density profile of the TG gas to the momentum distribution of the Fermi gas. This is possible as the OBRDM of the ideal Fermi gas can be directly expressed in terms of the single-particle harmonic-oscillator eigenstates

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm F}_{1}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{n=0}^{{\rm N}-1}\phi_{n}(x)\phi^{*}_{n}(x^{\prime}),

(S23)

whence it follows that the momentum distribution reads

\displaystyle n^{\rm F}(p,0)=\sum_{n=0}^{{\rm N}-1}\left|\tilde{\phi}_{n}(p)\right|^{2},

(S24)

in terms of the Fourier transform
\tilde{\phi}_{n}(p)
of the eigenstates
\phi_{n}(x)
of the Harmonic Oscillator
H=\frac{p^{2}}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^{2}_{0}x^{2}

Given that
\tilde{\phi}_{n}(p)=\frac{(-i)^{n}}{\sqrt{m\omega_{0}}}\phi_{n}\left(\frac{1}{m\omega_{0}}p\right)
,
we note that the density profile evaluated at the stationary points reads

\displaystyle\rho^{\rm F}(x^{*},t=0)=\sum_{n=0}^{{\rm N}-1}\left|\phi_{n}\left(\frac{p}{m\dot{b}},0\right)\right|^{2}=m\omega_{0}\ n^{\rm F}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\dot{b}}p,t=0\right).

(S25)

As the wavefunctions of the TG and Fermi gases only differ by the Bose-Fermi mapping, the density profile of the TG and ideal Fermi gas are equal at all times. In particular, they are equal at
t=0
, i.e.,
\rho^{\rm TG}(x,t=0)=\rho^{\rm F}(x,t=0)
. Thus, for the harmonic potential, the asymptotic momentum distribution of the TG gas is given in terms of that the Fermi gas, which is the central feature of DF
Rigol and Muramatsu 2005
;
Minguzzi and Gangardt 2005

\displaystyle n^{\rm TG}(p,t)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{0}}{\dot{b}}n^{\rm F}\left(\frac{\omega_{0}}{\dot{b}}p,t=0\right).

(S26)

The marginals of the Wigner function read

\displaystyle\rho(x,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dp\ W\left(\frac{x}{b},bp-m\dot{b}x,t=0\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{dp}{b}\ W\left(\frac{x}{b},p,t=0\right),

(S27)

\displaystyle n(p,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\ W\left(\frac{x}{b},bp-m\dot{b}x,t=0\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{dk}{m\dot{b}}\ W\left(\frac{p}{m\dot{b}}-\frac{k}{m\dot{b}b},k,t=0\right),

(S28)

where the last identity makes use of the change of variable

The first equation leads to

(S29)

We further assume

(S30)

we find

(S31)

where

denotes the
n
-th partial derivative
with respect to the first argument. Next, we show that when

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{p+q}\rho_{1}(x,\,y)}{\partial^{p}x\partial y^{q}}

(S32)

(S33)

(S34)

To show Eq. (
S32
) implies Eq. (
S33
),
we first resort to the definition of the Wigner transformation and

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dkW_{1}^{(n)}(x,k,\,0)k^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}}e^{2\text{i}ky/\hbar}k^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2i}\right)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}}\frac{\partial^{n}e^{2\text{i}ky/\hbar}}{\partial y^{n}}.

(S35)

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dkW_{1}^{(n)}(x,k,\,0)k^{n}=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2i}\right)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}}\frac{\partial^{n-1}e^{2\text{i}ky/\hbar}}{\partial y^{n-1}},

(S36)

where the boundary term vanishes since

\lim_{y\to\pm\infty}\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}}=0,

(S37)

according to Eq. (
S32
). Iterating this process
for
n-1

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dkW_{1}^{(n)}(x,k,\,0)k^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\left(\frac{\hbar}{2i}\right)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}\partial y^{n}}e^{2\text{i}ky/\hbar}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\hbar}{2i}\right)^{n}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}\partial y^{n}}\delta(y)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\hbar}{2i}\right)^{n}\frac{\partial^{n}\rho_{1}(x-y,\,x+y)}{\partial x^{n}\partial y^{n}}\bigg|_{y=0},

(S38)

which immediately implies Eq. (
S33
).

Finally, we note for an initial eigenstate of the TG gas and the spin-polarized

\rho_{1}(x,y,0)={\rm N}\int dx_{2}\dots dx_{{\rm N}}\ \Psi(x,x_{2},\dots,x_{{\rm N}},0)\Psi^{*}(y,x_{2},\dots,x_{{\rm N}},0).

VI.1

To compute the evolution of the momentum distribution (

(S47)

VI.3

The implosion can be engineered by a modulation of the trapping potential.
We proceed by reverse engineering. We first set a desired time evolution of the scaling factor. For the sake of illustration, we consider

\displaystyle b(t)=1+(b_{F}-1)\left(\frac{t}{t_{F}}\right)^{3}.

(S50)

Making use of the Ermakov equation, the time-dependent frequency
\omega^{2}(t)
that leads to thus time-dependence of
b(t)
can be determined, as shown in Fig.
7
.
Large values of
\dot{b}b
induce a high-frequency phase modulation in the coordinate representation.

One may wonder whether it is possible to cancel DF by DKC.
However, now the derivative of the scaling parameter
\dot{b}
is negative during the process as shown in Fig.
7
. Also, to cancel the DF it is necessary to apply a kick with an inverted potential. One can now consider the “kicked” Hamiltonian where the kick-pulse is repulsive
\omega_{k}=i\omega_{I}
, i.e.,

H_{k}(t)=H(t)-\delta(t-t_{k})\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{I}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm N}}x_{i}\,^{2}
.
The corresponding time-evolution operator reads
as

U_{\delta}(t_{k},0)=\exp\left(+i\frac{\tau_{k}m\omega_{I}^{2}}{2\hbar}\sum_{i=1}^{{\rm N}}x_{i}\,^{2}\right)U(t_{k},0)
,
where
U(t_{k},0)
is the propagator associated with
H(t)
, and
\tau_{k}
is a small time scale during which the kick is applied. As a result, the relation for the pulse parameters to reverse DF is still given by Eq. (
9
). For the prescribed trajectory (
S50
), applying the kick at the time
t_{k}
and duration
\tau_{k}
, the required pulse parameters to reverse DF are set by

\displaystyle\tau_{k}\omega^{2}_{k}=3\frac{(b_{F}-1)}{t_{k}b_{F}}.
