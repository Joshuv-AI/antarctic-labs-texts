Supercongruences using modular forms
Frits Beukers

Abstract
. Let F ⁡ ( t ) F(t) be the generating series of some combinatorially interesting numbers, like the Apéry numbers. For any prime p p we let F p ​ ( t ) F_{p}(t) be the sum of the terms of degree < p <p (the p p -truncation of F F ). It is a classical fact that for many p p and algebraic arguments α \alpha we have the congruence F p ​ ( α ) ≡ θ ⁡ ( mod ​ p ) F_{p}(\alpha)\equiv\theta({\rm mod}\ p) , where θ \theta is a zero of the characteristic p p ζ \zeta -function of an algebraic variety associated to F F and α \alpha . Surprisingly, very often these congruences turn out to hold modulo p 2 p^{2} or even p 3 p^{3} . We call such congruences supercongruences and in the past 15 years an abundance of them have been discovered. In this paper we show that a large proportion of them can be explained by the use of modular functions and forms.



1.
Introduction

A motivating example for this paper is the so-called
Legendre example
.
Let
p
be an odd prime and let
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
be the ring of
p
-adic integers. Define
the
Hasse polynomial

H_{p}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{-1/2}{k}^{2}t^{k}.

It is well-know, see
[
14
]
, that for any
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
we have
the congruence
H_{p}(t_{0})\equiv a_{p}({\rm mod}\ p)
where
a_{p}=p-N_{p}
and

N_{p}
is the number of solutions
x,y({\rm mod}\ p)
to the congruence

y^{2}\equiv x(x-1)(x-t_{0})({\rm mod}\ p).

The family of elliptic curves
y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-t)
parametrized by
t
is known as
the
Legendre family
. It is the toy example of many books on algebraic geometry.
When
t_{0}=0,1
we have
a_{p}=1
and
a_{p}=(-1)^{(p-1)/2}
respectively.
Let us assume
t_{0}\not\equiv 0,1({\rm mod}\ p)
.
A famous result of Hasse states that
|a_{p}|<2\sqrt{p}
, which implies that the
local
\zeta
-function
X^{2}-a_{p}X+p
has two complex zeros of absolute value
\sqrt{p}
.
Suppose
a_{p}\not\equiv 0({\rm mod}\ p)
. Then
X^{2}-a_{p}X+p
has a unique
p
-adic zero
\vartheta_{p}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

with
|\vartheta_{p}|_{p}=1
, which is called the
(
p
-adic) unit root
of
X^{2}-a_{p}X+p
. In this case
our congruence can be rephrased as
H_{p}(t_{0})\equiv\vartheta_{p}({\rm mod}\ p)
. To avoid cluttering of the notation
we have suppressed the dependence of
a_{p}
and
\vartheta_{p}
on
t_{0}
in our notation.

It turns out that for certain values of
t_{0}
we get
H_{p}(t_{0})\equiv\vartheta_{p}({\rm mod}\ p^{2})

for infinitely many primes
p
. This is a congruence modulo
p^{2}
instead of
p
. Here is an example,

Theorem 1.1
.

We have

–

For all odd primes
p
:
H_{p}(1)\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/2}({\rm mod}\ p^{2})
.

–

For all primes
p\equiv 1({\rm mod}\ 4)
:
H_{p}(-1)\equiv a+bi({\rm mod}\ p^{2})
, where
a,b
are integers such that

p=a^{2}+b^{2}
,
a\equiv(-1)^{(p-1)/4}({\rm mod}\ 4)
and the sign of
b
is such that
a+bi
is a
p
-adic unit.

The first statement was proven by Eric Mortenson,
[
30
]
,
the second essentially by L. van Hamme,
[
20
]
.
Note that in the latter result
i=\sqrt{-1}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
because
p\equiv 1({\rm mod}\ 4)
.
We call such congruences
supercongruences
, a name that was coined in
[
44
, p293]
.
Another example of a supercongruence is the following result,
[
22
]
.

Theorem 1.2

(Kilbourn, 2006)
.

For any odd prime
p
let

F_{p}(t):=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{-1/2}{k}^{4}t^{k}.

Then
F_{p}(1)\equiv a_{p}({\rm mod}\ p^{3})
, where
a_{p}
is the coefficient of
q^{p}
in the

q
-expansion of the product

q\prod_{n>0}(1-q^{2n})^{4}(1-q^{4n})^{4}.

The latter expansion is the
q
-series of the unique
\Gamma_{0}(8)
-modular cusp form of weight 4.

Note that this is a statement modulo
p^{3}
!
This theorem proved the first case of the famous list of 14, formulated as a conjecture
by F.Rodriguez-Villegas,
[
16
]
.
Each entry of this list is a congruence modulo
p^{3}
of an evaluation at
t=1

of a
p
-truncation of a
{}_{4}F_{3}
hypergeometric function. This conjecture has inspired many papers
on the subject and was recently proven entirely in the beautiful paper
[
25
]
by
Long, Tu, Yui and Zudilin.

It should be remarked here that the
a_{p}
in the last theorem comes from the local
\zeta
-function

X^{2}-a_{p}X+p^{3}
at
p
of an associated rigid Calabi-Yau threefold.
When
p
does not divide
a_{p}
the polynomial has a
p
-adic zero
\vartheta_{p}
which is a unit
root. Note that
a_{p}\equiv\vartheta_{p}({\rm mod}\ p^{3})
,
so in this case
a_{p}
and
\vartheta_{p}
are interchangeable in the congruence modulo
p^{3}
.

From about 2009 on, Zhi-Wei Sun presented an amazing list of a 100 or more conjectures for supercongruences
supported by extensive computer computations. See
[
38
]
, an updated version of
an arxiv preprint from 2009 (arXiv:0911.5665),
[
37
]
and many other papers.
More recently, Zhi-Wei Sun’s twin brother, Zhi-Hong Sun, joined in and published many more conjectures
and proofs of conjectures. See for example
[
35
]
and
[
33
]
.
The above citations are certainly not complete,
much more can be found by searching through their papers on arxiv.org. Also it is practically impossible
to give a comprehensive overview of these conjectures (proven or not) here.
But I recommend the interested reader to browse through
these papers to get an impression of the many forms of supercongruences that exist. The conjectures and
results of the Sun-brothers has unleashed a flood of papers, each proving
one or more of the conjectures.
When browsing through the above papers it becomes clear that supercongruences are a persistent
phenomenon and the question arises whether there exists a common mechanism by which they arise.
The present paper is an attempt to describe such a mechanism. We restrict ourselves to congruences
of the form
F_{p}(t_{0})\equiv\vartheta_{p}({\rm mod}\ p^{s})
with
s=2,3
. Here
F_{p}(t)
is the
p
-truncation
(sum of terms of order
<p
) of a
power series solution
F(t)
of a nice linear differential equation. In Sun’s list
one will find that many items are of this type but equally, many others are not.

We propose an approach which we sketch globally, leaving the details and proofs to the
remaining sections of this paper.
Let us start with a power series
F(t)\in 1+t{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket t\rrbracket
which satisfies
a linear differential equation of order
\geq 2
with coefficients in

{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
and such that it is of MUM-type (maximal unipotent monodromy) at
t=0
.
This means that
F(t)
satisfies a differential equation of the form

A_{n}(t)\theta^{n}y+A_{n-1}(t)\theta^{n-1}y+\cdots+A_{1}(t)\theta y+A_{0}(t)y=0,\quad\theta=t\frac{d}{dt},

where
A_{i}(t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}[t]
for all
i
,
A_{n}(0)\neq 0
and
A_{i}(0)=0
for all
i<n
.
One class of examples is given by hypergeometric series such as

{}_{2}F_{1}(1/2,1/2;1|16t),\quad{}_{4}F_{3}(1/5,2/5,3/5,4/5;1,1,1|5^{5}t).

Note that we normalized
t
so that the coefficients of
F
are integers.
The first series occurs in our starting example,
the second features as one instance of the conjectures of Rodriguez-Villegas.

Another construction is to take a Laurent polynomial
g({\bf x})\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1}^{\pm 1},\ldots,x_{n}^{\pm 1}]
in the
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, whose gcd of the coefficients is
1
.
Suppose that the Newton polytope
\Delta
has
{\bf 0}
as the unique lattice
point in its interior. Then consider

F(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}t^{k},\quad g_{k}=\text{constant term of }g({\bf x})^{k}.

Examples are
g(x,y)=(1+x)(1+y)(1+xy)/x/y
with

g_{k}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{k}{m}^{3}\quad\text{(Franel numbers)}

or
g(x,y)=(1+x)(1+y)(x+y)/x/y
with

g_{k}=\sum_{m=0}^{k}\binom{m}{k}^{2}\binom{m+k}{k}\quad\text{(Ap\'{e}ry numbers for $\zeta(2)$)}.

Since the differential equation is of MUM-type there exists a unique second solution of the form

F(t)\log t+G(t)
with
G(t)\in t{\mathbb{Q}}\llbracket t\rrbracket
. Our starting example has

F(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{2k}{k}^{2}t^{k}

which satisfies
\theta^{2}y=4t(2\theta+1)^{2}y
(note that we normalized
t
). One can compute that

G(t)=\sum_{k>0}\binom{2k}{k}^{2}\left(\sum_{j=k+1}^{2k}\frac{4}{j}\right)t^{k}.

Define the so-called
canonical coordinate
by

q(t):=\exp\left(\frac{G(t)}{F(t)}\right).

The inverse power series
t(q)
is called
the
mirror map
. In many cases of interest we have
t(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket q\rrbracket
.
Define
t^{\sigma}(q):=t(q^{p})
. This series can be written as a power series in
{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket t\rrbracket

and we call it the
excellent
p
-Frobenius lift
of
t
.
Then, in many cases of interest we have the supercongruence

(1)

\frac{F(t)}{F(t^{\sigma})}\equiv F_{p}(t)({\rm mod}\ p^{s})

as power series in
t
, for some
s\geq 2
(usually
s=2,3
). Note that modulo
p
this
congruence reads
F(t)\equiv F_{p}(t)F(t^{p})({\rm mod}\ p)
. This congruence is equivalent to the
statement that the coefficients of
F(t)
have the so-called
Lucas property

modulo
p
. This means that if
F(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}g_{k}t^{k}
, then for all
k\geq 0
we have

g_{k}\equiv g_{k_{0}}g_{k_{1}}\cdots g_{k_{m}}({\rm mod}\ p)
, where
k=k_{0}+k_{1}p+\cdots+k_{m}p^{m}
is the
base
p
-expansion of
k
. See for example
[
19
]
and
[
5
]
.

One goal of this paper is to
describe as many
F(t)
as possible which satisfy the stronger congruence
(
1
).

The other goal is to specialize
t
in (
1
) to
t_{0}\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
to get
supercongruences modulo
p^{s}
for
F_{p}(t_{0})
. Since
t^{\sigma}
is a power series in
t

that converges only when
|t|_{p}<1
we cannot simply set
t=t_{0}
in this power series.

However, the bulk of the examples given by the Sun-brothers concerns
F(t)
that satisfy a
differential equation of order
n=2
or
n=3
. In those cases, at least the ones that we
consider, it turns out that the mirror
map
t(q)
with
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
is a modular function with respect to a group
\Gamma

and
F(t(q))
is
a modular form of weight one (when
n=2
) or two (when
n=3
) with respect to
\Gamma

(with possible character on
\Gamma
). Here
\tau
is the complex coordinate of the
complex upper half plane
\mathcal{H}
. By abuse of notation we shall often denote

t(e^{2\pi i\tau})
by
t(\tau)
. We hope that this context dependent notation
will not cause too much confusion. The idea is now to use this modular parametrization to carry
out the specialization of
t
to
t_{0}
.

Let us start with
n=2
and illustrate this case via our initial
Legendre example. Using classical theory of the hypergeometric function
{}_{2}F_{1}(1/2,1/2,1|16t)

we get that the mirror map equals

t(q)=q-8q^{2}+44q^{3}-192q^{4}+\cdots=q\prod_{n>0}\frac{(1+q^{2n})^{16}}{(1+q^{n})^{8}}.

The series
t(q)
is connected with Felix Klein’s
\lambda
-invariant via the relation

16t(e^{2\pi i\tau})=\lambda(2\tau)
. It was also known to Klein that

{}_{2}F_{1}(1/2,1/2,1|16t(q))=\sum_{x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{x^{2}+y^{2}},

which, after setting
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
, is a modular form of weight one with respect
to
\Gamma_{0}(4)
. Let us denote this form by

\theta_{S}(q)
, where
S
is meant to refer to the square lattice
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
. Then we have

\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(1/2,1/2,1|16t(q))}{{}_{2}F_{1}(1/2,1/2,1|16t(q^{p}))}=\frac{\theta_{S}(q)}{\theta_{S}(q^{p})}\ \text{or, by abuse of notation, }\frac{\theta_{S}(\tau)}{\theta_{S}(p\tau)}.

Thus the quotient
F(t)/F(t^{\sigma})
is related to a quotient of modular forms.
In fact it is a modular function with respect to
\Gamma\cap\Gamma_{0}(p)
and hence an
algebraic function of
t
. We then specialize
t
to
t_{0}=t(\tau_{0})
, where

\tau_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
is such that
\tau_{0}=A(p\tau_{0})
for some
A\in\Gamma_{0}(4)
.
One sees from this that
\tau_{0}
is either imaginary quadratic or rational.
Suppose that
A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
, then

\frac{\theta_{S}(\tau_{0})}{\theta_{S}(p\tau_{0})}=\frac{\theta_{S}(A(p\tau_{0}))}{\theta_{S}(p\tau_{0})}=(-1)^{(d-1)/2}(cp\tau_{0}+d)

by the modularity of
\theta_{S}
. Roughly speaking we can now deduce statements like
Theorem
1.1
starting from (
1
) with
s=2
.
In this simple minded sketch it turns out that
\tau_{0}
depends on the choice of
p
.
In our later proofs we will make some more clever choices.

To make things a bit more precise we describe the general setup.

Assumption

(
I).

We make the following assumptions, where
q=e^{2\pi i\tau}
.

(1)

t(q)\in q+q^{2}{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket t\rrbracket
is a Hauptmodul for the modular group
\Gamma_{0}(N)
for
some integer
N
. Its poles are given by a single
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-orbit
denoted by
[\tau_{\infty}]
, where
\tau_{\infty}\in\mathcal{H}\cup{\mathbb{Q}}
.

(2)

\Theta(q)\in 1+q{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket q\rrbracket
is a modular form of weight 1 (with odd quadratic character
\chi

modulo
N
) with respect to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

(3)

The zero-set of
\Theta(q)
is given by
[\tau_{\infty}]
.
We assume that the zero-order is
r\leq 1
.

(4)

\Theta(q)
can be written as an
\eta
-product or
r<1/2
.

(5)

Let
F\in{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket t\rrbracket
be such that
\Theta(q)=F(t(q))
.

Remark 1.3
.

–

In a number of examples the zero-order
r
can be strictly less than
1
. This
happens at elliptic points of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
.

–

The number of zeros (counted with multiplicity)
of a weight 1 form in a fundamental domain of
\Gamma_{0}(N)

is
|SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}}):\Gamma_{0}(N)|/12
. Since
\Theta
has
a single zero of order
r\leq 1
we have
|SL(2,{\mathbb{Z}}):\Gamma_{0}(N)|\leq 12
, hence
N<10
or
N=11
.

–

We say that
\Theta(q)
can be written as
\eta
-product if it can be written
as
\Theta(q)=\prod_{m|N}\eta_{m}(q)^{e_{m}}
where
\eta_{m}(q)=\eta(q^{m})
and

\eta(q)
is the Dedekind
\eta
-function
q^{1/24}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})

are integers.

–

In Appendix A we have collected some data on the modular subgroups
\Gamma_{0}(N)

.

Let

It has been observed many times that the Van Hamme-congruences are very closely linked to the famous Ramanujan series
for
1/\pi
. Having the modular background at hand the connection is quite straightforward.

Theorem 1.25
.

Choose a differential equation from Appendix C. Suppose that the mirror map
t\in q+O(q^{2})
is a Hauptmodul
for the modular group
\Gamma
. Let
\Theta=F(t(\tau))
and suppose that
\Theta(\tau)^{r}
is a modular form
with respect to
\Gamma
of weight
2r
with trivial character for some integer
r\geq 1

Then, with the notation from Theorem
1.22
, we have

\sum_{k\geq 0}\left(1-\frac{k}{\delta(\tau)}\right)g_{k}t(\tau)^{k}=t(\tau)\frac{2\pi i\Theta(\tau)}{t^{\prime}(\tau)}\frac{1}{\delta(\tau)}\frac{1}{\pi i(\tau-\overline{\tau})}.

In particular
(2\pi i\Theta t/t^{\prime})^{r}\in{\mathbb{Q}}(t)
and
\delta
is a real analytic function
invariant under
\Gamma
.

If we choose the argument
\tau=\alpha
in a quadratic imaginary number field, then

and

\delta(\alpha)
will have algebraic values and we obtain fancy looking series expansions for

p
is a prime in
{\mathbb{Z}}
. Suppose, after embedding
\sqrt{-D}
in

{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, that
\wp
is a
p
-adic unit. Then,

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{6k}{3k}\binom{3k}{k}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{J(\omega_{D})^{k}}\equiv\pm\wp^{2}({\rm mod}\ p^{3})

and

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{\delta(\omega_{D})}\right)\binom{6k}{3k}\binom{3k}{k}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{J(\omega_{D})^{k}}\equiv\pm p({\rm mod}\ p^{3}).

We were unable to give an expression for the
\pm
sign. Note also that in the second congruence
the prime
\wp
does not occur. But we are able to prove it only for primes
p
that split
in
{\mathbb{Q}}(\sqrt{-D})
.

When
n\geq 4
the examples of interest do not have a modular background and the nature of the
mirror map is much more mysterious.
Recall the
excellent Frobenius lift
which we defined at the beginning of this introduction.
It was first introduced by B. Dwork in his famous paper

[
15
, §7]
, known as ’
p
-Adic Cycles’.
We speak of a Frobenius lift because
t^{\sigma}(q)=t(q^{p})\equiv t(q)^{p}\equiv t^{p}({\rm mod}\ p)
.
The substitution
A(t)\mapsto A(t^{\sigma})
gives an endomorphism
of the ring
{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket t\rrbracket
and modulo
p
it comes down to

A(t)\mapsto A(t^{\sigma})\equiv A(t^{p})\equiv A(t)^{p}({\rm mod}\ p)
.

The excellent Frobenius lift has a modular interpretation in the
case of the Legendre family of elliptic curves (as utilized by Dwork)
and more generally the equations of order 2 and 3 we dealt with until now.
Lacking such an interpretation in general we can still make a few observations.
For example, it turns out that
t^{\sigma}
is a
p
-adic limit of rational functions in
t
.
Dwork states in
[
15
]
that this is a consequence of ’Deligne’s theorem’
(without giving a reference) applied to the Legendre case.
For the case of constant term sequences with symmetric
g({\bf x})
it is proven in

[
8
, Thm 7.15]
. Consequently this allows us to compute the specialization of
t^{\sigma}
at
some
p
-adic point
t_{0}
by specializing the approximating rational functions at
t_{0}

and then take the limit. The image is denoted by
t_{0}^{\sigma}
. A condition is that
t_{0}
is not a
pole modulo
p
of the approximating rational functions which, in many cases, comes down to the condition

F_{p}(t_{0})\not\equiv 0({\rm mod}\ p)
. The second evaluation problem is to compute
F(t_{0})/F(t_{0}^{\sigma})

which, again, we cannot do by direct substitution into the power series. However it was Dwork’s
insight that
F(t)/F(t^{\sigma})
can be approximated
p
-adically by the functions

F_{p^{r}}(t)/F_{p^{r-1}}(t^{\sigma})
, see Theorem
1.29
for the case of
constant term sequences
F(t)
.
By specializing these rational functions at
t=t_{0}
and taking the limit one can compute

F(t_{0})/F(t_{0}^{\sigma})
in principle. Another insight of Dwork was that the limit

F(t_{0})/F(t_{0}^{\sigma})
equals
the unit root
\vartheta_{p}
of a certain
\zeta
-function if
t_{0}^{\sigma}=t_{0}
. It must be
said that the latter two statements are not contained in
[
15
]
in full generality.
We refer to Theorem
1.29
and the Appendix of
[
6
]
instead.

So we have argued that one may expect supercongruences at arguments
t_{0}
that are fixed under
the excellent
p
-Frobenius lift. Preferably one would like to have rational or
algebraic numbers
t_{0}
that are fixed for infinitely many primes
p
. In our modular setting
we looked for
\tau_{0}
such that
\tau_{0}
and
p\tau_{0}
are
\Gamma
-equivalent, and hence

t(p\tau_{0})=t(\tau_{0})
. But lacking such a background the nature of these fixed points is much less
clear.
We conjecture that the singularities of the differential equation are among
them, as long as they lie in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}
. The point
1
is a singularity of the hypergeometric equations
and the (former) conjectures by Rodriguez-Villegas are examples of supercongruences that correspond
to them. In
[
3
]
we find an extensive list of fourth order
equations of Calabi-Yau type, precisely the kind we are considering. It would be interesting to see
if Rodriguez-Villegas’s list extends to at least some of them.
Recently there has been some interest in special points called attractor points.
These are special values of
t
that are
related to the existence of black hole solutions of certain supergravity theories in theoretical
physics. Regrettably we lack the expertise to go into this subject more deeply.
What is relevant for us is that there are arithmetic methods to search for such

Finally we like to mention a much more general congruence which is a super version

2.
Central binomial coefficients, an example when
n=1

Before plunging into proofs announced in the introduction we like to point
out a very simple instance of our program when
n=1
. Consider the series

F(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}\binom{2k}{k}t^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}}.

Clearly it satisfies the differential equation
(1-4t)\theta y=2ty
, but we like
to consider the extended equation

\theta((1-4t)\theta-2t)y=0.

So, in a way we are back again in the case
n=2
, albeit a bit degenerate.
A basis of solutions of this differential equation is given by

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}},\quad\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-4t}}\times\log\left(\frac{2t}{1-2t+\sqrt{1-4t}}\right).

The
q
-coordinate is simply

q=\frac{2t}{1-2t+\sqrt{1-4t}}

and the mirror map

t=\frac{q}{(1+q)^{2}}.

Hence
t^{\sigma}=\frac{q^{p}}{(1+q^{p})^{2}}
, from which we see that
1/t^{\sigma}
is
a polynomial of degree
p
in
1/t
.
Furthermore,
F(t)=\frac{1+q}{1-q}
and hence, for any odd prime
p
,

\frac{F(t)}{F(t^{\sigma})}=\frac{(1+q)(1-q^{p})}{(1-q)(1+q^{p})}.

Let
pG(t)=(1+q)^{p}-1-q^{p}
and notice that by expansion in a
p
-adic geometrical series
modulo
p^{2}
,

\frac{1}{1+q^{p}}=\frac{1}{(1+q)^{p}-pG(t)}\equiv\frac{1}{(1+q)^{p}}+\frac{pG(t)}{(1+q)^{2p}}({\rm mod}\ p^{2}).

Hence

\frac{(1+q)(1-q^{p})}{(1-q)(1+q^{p})}\equiv\frac{1-q^{p}}{1-q}\left(\frac{1}{(1+q)^{p-1}}+\frac{pG(t)}{(1+q)^{2p-1}}\right)({\rm mod}\ p^{2}).

One easily verifies that the rational function on the right is invariant under
q\mapsto 1/q

and that the only pole is in
-1
with pole order
<2p
. Hence the right hand side is a
polynomial
A_{p}(t)
in
t
of degree
<p
. Hence
F(t)/F(t^{\sigma})\equiv A_{p}(t)({\rm mod}\ p^{2})
.
Since
F(t^{\sigma})
has the form
1+O(t^{p})
we conclude that
A_{p}(t)\equiv F_{p}(t)({\rm mod}\ p^{2})
.
So congruence (
1
) holds in this case and in particular we
see that

F_{p}(t)\equiv\frac{(1+q)(1-q^{p})}{(1-q)(1+q^{p})}({\rm mod}\ p^{2})\quad\text{with }t=\frac{q}{(1+q)^{2}}.

This is a rational function version of a result by Z.W.Sun,
[
36
]
with
a=1

and
m=q+2+1/q
. See also
[
39
]
.

We can specialize the identity at will. Some examples,

–

q=e^{2\pi i/3}
. Then
t=1
and we find

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{2k}{k}\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ p^{2})\quad\text{where}\ \epsilon=\pm 1\ \text{and}\ p\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ 3).

See
[
42
, (1.9)]

–

q=i
. Then
t=1/2
and we find

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ p^{2})\quad\text{where}\ \epsilon=\pm 1\ \text{and}\ p\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ 4)

–

q=e^{\pi i/3}
. Then
t=1/3
and we find

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{3^{k}}\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ p^{2})\quad\text{where}\ \epsilon=\pm 1\ \text{and}\ p\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ 6)

–

q=1
. Then
t=1/4
and we find

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{2k}{k}\frac{1}{4^{k}}\equiv p({\rm mod}\ p^{2})

–

q=e^{4\pi i/5}
. Then
t=\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}
and we find

\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\binom{2k}{k}\left(\frac{3+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right)^{k}\equiv\begin{cases}\epsilon({\rm mod}\ p^{2})&\text{when}\ p\equiv\epsilon({\rm mod}\ 5)\\
\epsilon(2-\sqrt{5})({\rm mod}\ p^{2})&\text{when}\ p\equiv 2\epsilon({\rm mod}\ 5)\end{cases}

different truncations of the series
F(t)
. Two more or less random references
are
[

]
and
[

]
.

3.
Modular preparations

In the two sections following this one we deal with differential equations of order
n=2,3
.
These are the cases where almost always the mirror map
t(q)
is a modular function.
We remind the reader that we often abuse our notation by using the notations
t(q)
and

t(\tau)
interchangebly, where
\tau
is the parameter of the complex upper half plane.
By the latter we actually mean
t(e^{2\pi i\tau})
, but
we trust that this context dependent notation will not present too many difficulties.
We start with a few simple lemmas

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
h(q)\in{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket q\rrbracket
. Then, for any prime
p
we have

\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(1+h(q^{1/p}\zeta_{p}^{k})x)\equiv 1+h(q)x^{p}({\rm mod}\ p),

where
\zeta_{p}=e^{2\pi i/p}
. In particular, when
0<s<p
, the
s
-th elementary function in

h(q^{1/p}\zeta^{k}),k=0,1,\ldots,p-1
is a power series in
{\mathbb{Z}}\llbracket q\rrbracket
whose coefficients are divisible by
p
.
The
p
-th elementary symmetric function equals
h(q)
modulo
p
.

Proof.

We prove our equality modulo
\zeta_{p}-1
. Since the coefficients of both sides of the equality are in
{\mathbb{Z}}

the congruence modulo
p
follows immediately. We see that the product equals

\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(1+h(q^{1/p}\zeta_{p}^{k})x)\equiv\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(1+h(q^{1/p})x)\equiv(1+h(q^{1/p})x)^{p}\equiv 1+h(q)x^{p}({\rm mod}\ \zeta_{p}-1).

Since
p
is unramified in
{\mathbb{Q}}(\zeta_{p})
this congruence also holds modulo
p
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
m
be a positive integer and
p
a prime that does not divide
m
.
Define
\eta_{m}(\tau):=\eta(m\tau)
where
\eta(\tau)=q^{1/24}\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})
.
Then

\eta_{m}(p\tau)\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}\eta_{m}((\tau+k)/p)=\exp(2\pi im(p-1)/48)\times\eta_{m}(\tau)^{p+1}.

Remark 3.3
.

A fortiori this lemma holds for any product of
\eta_{m}

with different
m
, all of them not divisible by
p
. We call a product of the
form
\prod_{m|N}\eta_{m}^{e_{m}}
with
e_{m}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
an
\eta
-product
.

Proof.

We prove our statement for
m=1
, the case
m>1
being completely similar.
Observe that

\exp(2\pi ip\tau/24)\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}\exp(2\pi i(\tau+k)/(24p))=\exp(2\pi i(p-1)/48)\exp(2\pi i(p+1)\tau/24),

which accounts for the factors coming from
q^{1/24}
. Next, for any
n\geq 1
,

\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(1-\zeta_{p}^{nk}q^{n/p})=\begin{cases}1-q^{n}&\text{if $p$ does not divide $n$}\\
(1-q^{1/p})^{p}&\text{if $p$ divides $n$}\end{cases}

Hence

\prod_{n\geq 1}\prod_{k=0}^{p-1}(1-\zeta^{nk}q^{n/p})=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})\times\prod_{n\equiv 0({\rm mod}\ p)}\frac{(1-q^{n/p})^{p}}{1-q^{n}}.

Replace
n
by
np
in the second product to get

\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})\times\prod_{n\equiv 0({\rm mod}\ p)}\frac{(1-q^{n/p})^{p}}{1-q^{n}}=\prod_{n\geq 1}\frac{(1-q^{n})^{p+1}}{1-q^{np}}.

This finishes the proof of our lemma.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Consider an
\eta
-product
E(\tau)=\prod_{m|N}\eta_{m}(\tau)^{e_{m}}
.
Let
r,s
be relatively prime integers. Then
r/s
is a zero of
E(\tau)

if and only if

\sum_{m|N}e_{m}\frac{\gcd(s,m)^{2}}{m}>0.

Proof.

Substitute
\tau=r/s+i\epsilon
in
E(\tau)
and let
\epsilon\downarrow 0
.
For each factor we have
\eta_{m}(\tau)=\eta(mr/s+im\epsilon)
. Let
m^{\prime}=m/\gcd(s,m)

and
s^{\prime}=s/\gcd(s,m)
. Choose
u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
m^{\prime}rv-us^{\prime}=1
. Then by modularity
of
\eta
we get

\eta\left(\frac{v\tau-u}{-s^{\prime}\tau+m^{\prime}r}\right)\sim(-s^{\prime}\tau+m^{\prime}r)^{1/2}\eta(\tau),

where
\sim
denotes equality up to a non-zero factor independent of
\epsilon
.
Set
\tau=mr/s+im\epsilon=m^{\prime}r/s^{\prime}+mi\epsilon
. Then after some rearranging,

\eta(mr/s+im\epsilon)\sim\epsilon^{-1/2}\eta\left(\frac{i}{m(s^{\prime})^{2}\epsilon}-\frac{v}{s^{\prime}}\right)\sim\epsilon^{-1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\gcd(s,m)^{2}}{24ms^{2}\epsilon}\right).

Hence
E(\tau)
behaves like

E(r/s+i\epsilon)\sim\epsilon^{-\sum_{m}e_{m}/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{24s^{2}\epsilon}\sum_{m\in N}e_{m}\frac{\gcd(s,m)^{2}}{m}\right)

and our lemma follows.
∎

The following lemma is a consequence of the standard theory of modular forms.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
e|N
be such that
\gcd(e,N/e)=1
or
2
. Then any point
r/s
with
\gcd(r,s)=1,r\neq 0,s>0

and
\gcd(s,N)=e
is
\Gamma_{0}(N)
-equivalent to
1/e
.

Let
f
be a modular form of weight
1
with respect to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
and character
\chi
.
Suppose that
f
has no zero in
1/e
, i.e.
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon f(1/e+i\epsilon)=c_{e}\neq 0
.
Let
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be such that
d\equiv r^{-1}({\rm mod}\ e)
and
d\equiv s/e({\rm mod}\ N/e)
.
Then
\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon f(r/s+i\epsilon)=\chi(d)ec_{e}/s
for any
r/s
as above.

Proof.

To prove the first statement choose
a,b,c,d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

d\equiv r^{-1}({\rm mod}\ s),\quad d\equiv s/e({\rm mod}\ N/e),\quad b=(rd-1)/s,\quad c=s-de,\quad a=r-be.

Note that
\gcd(s,N/e)=\gcd(e,N/e)
. If this gcd is
1
then the congruence equations
for
d
can be satisfied. If the gcd is
2
then
s
and
N/e
are both even. So

r,s/e
are both odd and the congruence equations can also be satisfied in that case.
One easily verifies that

A:=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma_{0}(N)\quad\text{and}\quad a+be=r,c+de=s.

Set
\tau=1/e+i\epsilon
in
f(A\tau)=\chi(d)(c\tau+d)f(\tau)
to obtain

f\left(\frac{r}{s}+i\epsilon\frac{1}{(c/e+d)^{2}}+O(\epsilon^{2})\right)=\chi(d)(c/e+d+ci\epsilon)f(1/e+i\epsilon).

Multiply by
\epsilon
and take the limit as
\epsilon\downarrow 0
. We find

\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\epsilon f\left(\frac{r}{s}+i\epsilon\frac{e^{2}}{s^{2}}\right)=\chi(d)\frac{s}{e}c_{e}.

Replace
\epsilon
by
\epsilon s^{2}/e^{2}
to find the second assertion of our lemma.
∎

Finally we introduce the
Atkin-Lehner extensions

\Gamma
of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
and
classify the orbits of
\Theta(p\tau),t(p\tau)
under the action of
\Gamma
on
\tau
.

Let
N
be a positive integer and consider
\Gamma_{0}(N)
.
Next, let
e
be a

of

; that is,

and
\gcd(e,N/e)=1.
We define the set of

(4)

c_{e}N&d_{e}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}^{+}(\mathbb{Z}),

where
a_{e},b_{e},c_{e},d_{e}
are integers so that

,

, and

\det(w_{e})=e
. In particular
W_{1}=\Gamma_{0}(N)
.
One easily verifies that

normalizes
\Gamma_{0}(N)
and the product of any two elements from

is

e

\Gamma_{0}(N)
.
Consequently the union
\Gamma_{0}(N)\cup W_{e}

\mathrm{PGL}_{2}({\mathbb{Q}})^{+}
.

When

, we call the matrix

w_{N}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
N&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}.

for
\Gamma_{0}(N)

.
In general

We note that the Hall divisors of

form a multiplicative set with the rule that

equals

For any multiplicative subset

\Gamma_{0}(N)+S:=\cup_{e\in S}W_{e}.

This group is called an

of
\Gamma_{0}(N)
by the involutions
corresponding to

\Gamma_{0}(N)
itself also a (trivial) Atkin-Lehner extension.
In this paper we shall specify the set

by its generators. For example, when

is denoted by
\left<2,3\right>

When

\Gamma_{0}(N)+
.

From the above properties it also follows that

Let

We now consider the action of

acts on

In the next lemma we need to extend

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
p
be a prime not dividing

and

with respect to

permutes the elements of the following set,

\left\{\chi(\gamma)^{-1}\frac{H|\gamma}{H}\right\}_{[\gamma]\in\Gamma_{0}(N)\backslash M(N,p)}.

One easily verifies that the elements of this set are independent of the choice of the
\gamma

in their equivalence class

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\Gamma_{0}(N)

Here we used

∎

Lemma 3.7

Let

.
By modularity we have

-adic unit

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.22
.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let us start with the identity

Proof of Theorem
1.11
.

We are in the case hypergeometric

from Appendix B. In that case the modular

\Gamma_{0}(4)
but we extend it to
\Gamma_{0}(2)
, which normalizes
\Gamma_{0}(4)

with index

. In particular we need the identy

for any integers

with

and

We will not fight for the determination of the

We copy the proof Theorem
1.7
.
Write

, where
c^{\prime}=-c,d^{\prime}=d+c(\alpha+\overline{\alpha})
.
Choose

such that

Using

.
∎
