Computation of q-Binomial Coefficients with the P ⁡ ( n , m ) P(n,m) Integer Partition Function
M.J. Kronenburg

Abstract
Using P ⁡ ( n , m ) P(n,m) , the number of integer partitions of n n into exactly m m parts, which was the subject of an earlier paper, P ⁡ ( n , m , p ) P(n,m,p) , the number of integer partitions of n n into exactly m m parts with each part at most p p , can be computed in O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) , and the q-binomial coefficient can be computed in O ⁡ ( n 3 ) O(n^{3}) . Using the definition of the q-binomial coefficient, some properties of the q-binomial coefficient and P ⁡ ( n , m , p ) P(n,m,p) are derived. The q-multinomial coefficient can be computed as a product of q-binomial coefficients. A formula for Q ⁡ ( n , m , p ) Q(n,m,p) , the number of integer partitions of n n into exactly m m distinct parts with each part at most p p , is given. Some formulas for the number of integer partitions with each part between a minimum and a maximum are derived. A computer algebra program is listed implementing these algorithms using the computer algebra program of the earlier paper.



Keywords
: q-binomial coefficient, integer partition function.

2
Properties of q-Binomial Coefficients and
P(n,m,p)

Some identities of the q-binomial coefficient are proved from its definition,
and from these some properties of
P^{*}(n,m,p)
and
P(n,m,p)
are derived.

Theorem 2.1
.

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{m}_{\textstyle q}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m-1}_{\textstyle q}+q^{m}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}

(2.1)

Proof.

\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j}}{1-q^{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1-q^{p+j}}{1-q^{j}}+q^{m}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j-1}}{1-q^{j}}

(2.2)

\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{p+j})=(1-q^{m})\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}(1-q^{p+j})+q^{m}\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(1-q^{p+j})

(2.3)

1=\frac{1-q^{m}}{1-q^{m+p}}+q^{m}\frac{1-q^{p}}{1-q^{m+p}}

(2.4)

1-q^{m+p}=1-q^{m}+q^{m}(1-q^{p})

(2.5)

∎

Theorem 2.2
.

P^{*}(n,m,p)=P^{*}(n,m-1,p)+P^{*}(n-m,m,p-1)

(2.6)

Proof.

Using the previous theorem:

\begin{split}P^{*}(n,m,p)&=[q^{n}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{m}_{\textstyle q}=[q^{n}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m-1}_{\textstyle q}+[q^{n-m}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}\\
&=P^{*}(n,m-1,p)+P^{*}(n-m,m,p-1)\\
\end{split}

(2.7)

∎

From this theorem and identity (
1.2
) follows:

P^{*}(n,m,p)-P^{*}(n,m-1,p)=P(n,m,p)=P^{*}(n-m,m,p-1)

(2.8)

or equivalently:

P^{*}(n,m,p)=P(n+m,m,p+1)

(2.9)

From this theorem and this identity follows:

P(n,m,p)=P(n-1,m-1,p)+P(n-m,m,p-1)

(2.10)

Theorem 2.3
.

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{m}_{\textstyle q}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}+q^{p}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m-1}_{\textstyle q}

(2.11)

Proof.

\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j}}{1-q^{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j-1}}{1-q^{j}}+q^{p}\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}\frac{1-q^{p+j}}{1-q^{j}}

(2.12)

\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{p+j})=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(1-q^{p+j})+q^{p}(1-q^{m})\prod_{j=1}^{m-1}(1-q^{p+j})

(2.13)

1=\frac{1-q^{p}}{1-q^{m+p}}+q^{p}\frac{1-q^{m}}{1-q^{m+p}}

(2.14)

1-q^{m+p}=1-q^{p}+q^{p}(1-q^{m})

(2.15)

∎

Theorem 2.4
.

P^{*}(n,m,p)=P^{*}(n,m,p-1)+P^{*}(n-p,m-1,p)

(2.16)

Proof.

Using the previous theorem:

\begin{split}P^{*}(n,m,p)&=[q^{n}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{m}_{\textstyle q}=[q^{n}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}+[q^{n-p}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m-1}_{\textstyle q}\\
&=P^{*}(n,m,p-1)+P^{*}(n-p,m-1,p)\\
\end{split}

(2.17)

∎

Using (
2.9
):

P(n,m,p)=P(n,m,p-1)+P(n-p,m-1,p)

(2.18)

The following theorem is a symmetry identity:

Theorem 2.5
.

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{m}_{\textstyle q}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{p}_{\textstyle q}

(2.19)

Proof.

\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j}}{1-q^{j}}=\prod_{j=1}^{p}\frac{1-q^{m+j}}{1-q^{j}}

(2.20)

Using cross multiplication:

\prod_{j=1}^{p}(1-q^{j})\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{p+j})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})\prod_{j=1}^{p}(1-q^{m+j})=\prod_{j=1}^{m+p}(1-q^{j})

(2.21)

∎

From this theorem follows:

P^{*}(n,m,p)=P^{*}(n,p,m)

(2.22)

and using (
2.9
):

P(n,m,p)=P(n-m+p-1,p-1,m+1)

(2.23)

Using (
1.2
) and (
2.8
):

P^{*}(n,m,p)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}P^{*}(n-k,k,p-1)

(2.24)

Combining this identity with (
1.4
) and using theorem
2.5

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p}{p}_{\textstyle q}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}q^{k}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{p+k-1}{p-1}_{\textstyle q}

(2.25)

which is identity (3.3.9) in
[
2
]
.
Taking
m=n
in (
1.2
) and (
2.9
) and conjugation of Ferrer diagrams:

P(2n,n,p+1)=P(n+p,p)

(2.26)

and taking

P(n)=P(2n,n)=P(2n,n,n+1)

(2.27)

The partitions of

and therefore by conjugation of Ferrer diagrams:

P(n,m,p)-P(n,m,p-1)=P(n,p,m)-P(n,p,m-1)

This identity can also be derived from the other identities.

Using (

\sum_{m=0}^{n}P(n,m,p)=P^{*}(n,n,p)=[q^{n}]\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{p}(1-q^{j})}

(2.29)

Theorem 2.6
.

\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}P(n,m,p)=[q^{n}]\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{p}(1+q^{j})}

(2.30)

Proof.

Using (

\begin{split}&\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}P(n,m,p)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}P^{*}(n-m,m,p-1)\\
&=\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}[q^{n-m}]\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}=[q^{n}]\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{m}q^{m}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}\\
\end{split}

(2.31)

Using the negative q-binomial theorem

\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+p-1}{m}_{\textstyle q}t^{m}=\frac{1}{\prod_{j=0}^{p-1}(1-q^{j}t)}

Taking

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m_1\cdots m_s}_{\textstyle q}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{\sum_{j=1}^i m_j}{m_i}_{\textstyle q}=\prod_{i=1}^{s}\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{n-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}m_j}{m_i}_{\textstyle q}

4
Computation of
P(n,m,p)
with
P(n,m)

Let the coefficient
a_{k}^{(m,p)}
be defined as:

a_{k}^{(m,p)}=[q^{k}]\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{p+j})

(4.1)

These coefficients can be computed by multiplying out the product,
which up to
k=n-m
is
O(m(n-m))=O(n^{2})
.
Using (
1.4
) and (
2.8
):

\begin{split}P(n,m,p)&=P^{*}(n-m,m,p-1)=[q^{n-m}]\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j-1}}{1-q^{j}}=[q^{n-m}]\sum_{k=0}^{n-m}a_{k}^{(m,p-1)}\frac{q^{k}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n-m}a_{k}^{(m,p-1)}[q^{n-k}]\frac{q^{m}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-m}a_{k}^{(m,p-1)}P(n-k,m)\\
\end{split}

(4.2)

The list of the
n-m+1
values of
P(m,m)
to
P(n,m)
can be computed
using the algorithm in
[
7
]
which is also
O(n^{2})
,
and therefore this algorithm computes
P(n,m,p)
in
O(n^{2})
.
For computing
P^{*}(n,m,p)
(
2.9
) can be used.

5
Computation of q-Binomial Coefficients

From definition (
1.3
) the q-binomial coefficients are:

\begin{split}[q^{n}]\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{1-q^{p+j}}{1-q^{j}}&=[q^{n}]\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}^{(m,p)}\frac{q^{k}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}^{(m,p)}[q^{n+m-k}]\frac{q^{m}}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})}\\
&=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k}^{(m,p)}P(n+m-k,m)\\
\end{split}

(5.1)

Because
P^{*}(n,m,p)=0
when
n>mp
and (
1.4
), the coefficients
[q^{n}]

are nonzero if and only if
0\leq n\leq mp
.
The product coefficients
a_{k}^{(m,p)}
can therefore be computed in
O(m^{2}p)
,
and the list of
mp+1
values of
P(m,m)
to
P(mp+m,m)
can also be computed
in
O(m^{2}p)

[
7
]
.
The sums are convolutions which can be done with
ListConvolve
,
and therefore this algorithm computes the q-binomial coefficients in
O(m^{2}p)
.
Because of symmetry theorem
2.5
,
m
and
p
can be interchanged when
m>p
,
which makes the algorithm
O(\textrm{min}(m^{2}p,p^{2}m))
.
Using a change of variables:

\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{n}{m}_{\textstyle q}=\genfrac{(}{)}{0.0pt}{}{m+n-m}{m}_{\textstyle q}

(5.2)

The algorithm for computing this q-binomial coefficient is
O(\textrm{min}(m^{2}(n-m),(n-m)^{2}m))
.
From this follows that when
m
or
n-m
is constant, then the algorithm is
O(n)
, and when

m=cn
for some constant
c
, then the algorithm is
O(n^{3})
. Because
P^{*}(n,m,p)=P^{*}(mp-n,m,p)

only
P^{*}(n,m,p)
for
0\leq n\leq\lceil mp/2\rceil
needs to be computed,
which makes the algorithm about two times faster.
For comparison of results with the computer algebra program below
an alternative algorithm using cyclotomic polynomials is given.

QBinomialAlternative[n_,m_]:=Block[{result={1},temp},
Do[Which[Floor[n/k]-Floor[m/k]-Floor[(n-m)/k]==1,
temp=CoefficientList[Cyclotomic[k,q],q];
result=ListConvolve[result,temp,{1,-1},0]],{k,n}];
result]

Computations show that this alternative algorithm is
O(n^{4})
.

6
A Formula for
Q(n,m,p)

Let
Q(n,m,p)
be the number of integer partitions of
n
into exactly
m

distinct parts with each part at most
p
.

Theorem 6.1
.

Q(n,m,p)=P(n-m(m-1)/2,m,p-m+1)

(6.1)

Proof.

The proof is with Ferrer diagrams and the ”staircase” argument.
Let a normal partition be a partition into
m
parts,
and let a distinct partition be a partition into
m
distinct parts.
Let the parts of a Ferrer diagram with
m
parts be indexed from small to large by
s=1\cdots m
.
Each distinct partition of
n
contains a ”staircase” partition
with parts
s-1
and a total size of
m(m-1)/2
, and subtracting this from such a
partition gives a normal partition of
n-m(m-1)/2
, and the largest part
is decreased by
m-1
.
Vice versa adding the ”staircase” partition to a normal partition of
n

gives a distinct partition of
n+m(m-1)/2
,
and the largest part is increased by
m-1
.
When the parts of the distinct partition are at most
p
,
then the parts of the corresponding normal partition
are at most
p-(m-1)
.
Because of this
1-1
correspondence between the Ferrer diagrams of these two types
of partitions the identity is valid.
∎

Theorem 7.1

P(n,m,p_{\min},p_{\max})=\sum_{l=0}^{\min(m,p_{\min})}(-1)^{l}\sum_{k=l(l+1)/2}^{n-m+l}P(k-l(l-1)/2,l,p_{\min}-l)P(n-k,m-l,p_{\max})

\sum_{k=1}^{m}q^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1-q^{j})=1-\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})

(7.22)

Proof.

The lemma is true for
m=0
, and using induction on
m
,
when it is true for
m
, then for
m+1
:

\begin{split}&\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}q^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1-q^{j})=q^{m+1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})+\sum_{k=1}^{m}q^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}(1-q^{j})\\
&=q^{m+1}\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})+1-\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})=1-(1-q^{m+1})\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})=1-\prod_{j=1}^{m+1}(1-q^{j})\\
\end{split}

(7.23)

∎

Dividing this lemma by
\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})
and taking the coefficient
[q^{n}]
:

[q^{n}]\sum_{k=1}^{m}q^{k}\frac{1}{\prod_{j=k}^{m}(1-q^{j})}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}[q^{n-k}]\frac{1}{\prod_{j=k}^{m}(1-q^{j})}=[q^{n}]\frac{1}{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(1-q^{j})}-[q^{n}]1

(7.24)

and using
P(0,p,p)=1

For integer
m\leq n

\sum_{k=1}^{\min(m,\lfloor n/2\rfloor)}P^{\#}(n-k,k,m)=P^{\#}(n,1,m)-\delta_{n,m}

(7.25)

Taking
m=n
and using
P^{\#}(n,1,n)=P(n)

\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}P^{\#}(n-k,k,n)=P(n)-1

(7.26)

(7.27)

Dividing this lemma by
