Convergence and Near-optimal Sampling for Multivariate Function Approximations in Irregular Domains via Vandermonde with Arnoldi
Wenqi Zhu, and, Yuji Nakatsukasa

Abstract
Vandermonde matrices are usually exponentially ill-conditioned and often result in unstable approximations. In this paper, we introduce and analyze the multivariate Vandermonde with Arnoldi (V+A) method , which is based on least-squares approximation together with a Stieltjes orthogonalization process, for approximating continuous, multivariate functions on d d -dimensional irregular domains. The V+A method addresses the ill-conditioning of the Vandermonde approximation by creating a set of discrete orthogonal bases with respect to a discrete measure. The V+A method is simple and general, relying only on the domain’s sample points. This paper analyzes the sample complexity of the least-squares approximation that uses the V+A method. We show that, for a large class of domains, this approximation gives a well-conditioned and near-optimal N N -dimensional least-squares approximation using M = 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 2 ) M={\cal O}(N^{2}) equispaced sample points or M = 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 2 ​ log ⁡ N ) M={\cal O}(N^{2}\log N) random sample points, independently of d d . We provide a comprehensive analysis of the error estimates and the rate of convergence of the least-squares approximation that uses the V+A method. Based on the multivariate V+A techniques, we propose a new variant of the weighted V+A least-squares algorithm that uses only M = 𝒪 ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) M={\cal O}(N\log N) sample points to achieve a near-optimal approximation. Our initial numerical results validate that the V+A least-squares approximation method provides well-conditioned and near-optimal approximations for multivariate functions on (irregular) domains. Additionally, the (weighted) least-squares approximation that uses the V+A method performs competitively with state-of-the-art orthogonalization techniques and can serve as a practical tool for selecting near-optimal distributions of sample points in irregular domains.



Keywords: least-squares, Vandermonde matrix, Arnoldi, polyval, polyfit, ill-conditioning, sample complexity, near-optimal sampling

Introduction and Overview of the Paper

Many problems in computational science call for the approximation of smooth, multivariate functions. In this paper, we consider the problem of approximating a multivariate continuous function
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
d\geq 1
variables, where the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
may be irregular. Using Vandermonde matrices to fit polynomials is one of the most straightforward approaches. However, the Vandermonde matrix is usually exponentially ill-conditioned, even on standard domains such as an interval, unless the sample points are very carefully chosen
[
9
,
27
,
31
]
. Recently, in
[
11
]
, the authors developed an orthogonalization framework that couples Vandermonde matrices with Arnoldi orthogonalization for univariate function approximations, known as the univariate Vandermonde with Arnoldi method (V+A).

In this paper, we extend the univariate V+A method to a multivariate version that can be used for
d
-dimensional function approximations (
d\geq 2
).
The multivariate V+A method is based on least-squares approximation coupled with a Stieltjes orthogonalization process, aimed at approximating continuous, multivariate functions on irregular
d
-dimensional domains. This method establishes a set of discrete multivariate orthogonal bases with respect to a discrete measure.

There is extensive literature on
d
-dimensional polynomial approximation algorithms that assume the function
f
is defined over a hypercube domain containing the irregular domain
\Omega

[
4
,
5
,
15
]
. These algorithms, commonly known as polynomial frame approximations, create an orthogonal basis in the hypercube domain. On the other hand, the V+A algorithm creates a discrete orthogonal basis directly in
\Omega
and effectively constructs a well-conditioned basis for the irregular domain
\Omega
.
It is well-known that, even if we have a well-conditioned basis, least-squares approximations can still become inaccurate when the number of sample points
M
(from a suboptimal distribution, e.g., equispaced points) is insufficient, such as when
M
is close to the dimension of the approximation space
N
. Poorly distributed sample points can also affect the quality of the solution. In some domains, polynomial frame approximations have provable bounds on the sample complexity; namely, the scaling between the dimension of the approximation space
N
and the number of samples
M
, which is sufficient to guarantee a well-conditioned and accurate approximation
[
2
,
5
,
15
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there appears to be no literature on the sample complexity of the V+A procedure.

A key theoretical contribution of this paper is to investigate how
M
behaves as a function of
N
such that the least-squares approximant converges to
f
as the total degree of freedom in approximation
N\rightarrow\infty
. We show that, in a large number of domains (i.e., real intervals, convex domains, or finite unions of convex domains), the least-squares approximation using the V+A method gives a well-conditioned and accurate
N
-dimensional approximation using
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
equispaced sample points or
M={\cal O}(N^{2}\log N)
random sample points. The sample complexity of the least-squares approximation using the V+A method is
comparable to that of
polynomial frame approximation
[
2
,
4
,
15
]
. However, since V+A only requires enough sample points such that the discrete orthogonal polynomials are nearly orthogonal in the whole/continuous domain of interest, it can, in some cases, provide an approximation of similar accuracy using fewer sample points. In addition, since the least-squares problem to be solved becomes well-conditioned, it requires no sophisticated analysis or algorithm to compute an accurate solution (in frame approximation, a remarkable result is that despite the ill-conditioning, one can find a near-optimal solution by finding a small-norm solution using the truncated SVD
[
2
,
4
]
).
Using results on sample complexity, we further prove that, under suitable sample distributions and domains, the least-squares approximation that uses the multivariate V+A method is near-optimal. Specifically, the
N
th polynomial approximation obtained (referred to as the V+A approximant) converges to
f
at a spectral rate with respect to
N
for the total degree polynomial space, contingent upon the smoothness of
f
in
\Omega.

In addition to theory, we provide numerical studies for the multivariate V+A method. Our preliminary numerical results indicate that the multivariate V+A method effectively tackles the ill-conditioning associated with the Vandermonde approximation. To our knowledge, the multivariate V+A algorithm introduced in
[
24
]
is implemented only for rational approximations on standard 1D or 2D domains. We extend the numerical applications of the multivariate V+A algorithm to include polynomial approximation on 2D (irregular domains) and higher-dimensional domains (
d=3,5
). Our initial numerical results indicate that as
N
increases, the V+A least-squares approximation provides a significantly more accurate approximation compared to the Vandermonde least-squares method for
2\leq d\leq 5
. In several papers
[
2
,
3
,
16
,
30
]
, the authors proved that an effective weighting can lead to the near-optimal scaling of
M={\cal O}(N\log(N))
. However, in past approaches
[
2
]
, the QR factorization was used to orthogonalize the least-squares system.
A key numerical contribution of this paper is that we propose a variant of the weighted least-squares algorithm that uses the multivariate V+A as the orthogonalization method (
VA+Weight
). This algorithm is stable with high probability and only takes
M={\cal O}(N\log N)
sample points to give a near-optimal approximation. Due to the reduced sample density,
VA+Weight
also gives a lower online computational cost than the unweighted least-squares using the V+A method. Our initial numerical results confirm that
VA+Weight
performs competitively with state-of-the-art orthogonalization techniques for the Vandermonde matrix. Finding the optimal distribution of sample points in a high-dimensional irregular domain is an open question in the literature
[
14
]
. Our preliminary numerical results highlight that
VA+Weight
is a practical tool for selecting the near-optimal distribution of sample points for the irregular domains considered in this paper.

The paper is arranged as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the Arnoldi orthogonalization procedure in the least squares approximation setup, presenting the univariate V+A algorithm along with three examples of its numerical applications. In Section
3
, we extend the univariate V+A algorithm to higher dimensions. We compare the multivariate V+A method with other techniques and showcase its effectiveness through five examples of numerical applications in function approximation within
d
-dimensional (irregular) domains (
d\geq 2
).
Section
4
gives our main theoretical result on sample complexity and convergence rate of least squares approximation using the V+A algorithm on general
d
-dimensional domains. In Section
5

, which takes only
M={\cal O}(N\log N)
sample points to give a near-optimal approximation.

Related Work

The idea of orthogonalizing monomials and combining Vandermonde with Arnoldi is not completely new. Past emphasis has been on constructing continuous orthogonal polynomials on a continuous domain. The purpose of the orthogonalization is to obtain the orthogonal polynomial itself
[
21
,
22

[

The multivariate version of V+A was first discussed by Hokanson

]

[

. In these studies, the multivariate V+A is used for rational approximations on standard domains. Applying the V+A algorithm to approximate multivariate functions on irregular domains appears not to have been considered in the literature.

It is worth noting that V+A also has numerous applications beyond polynomial approximation. For instance, it can be used in the ‘lightning’ solver
[
20
]
, a state-of-the-art PDE solver that solves Laplace’s equation on an irregular domain using rational functions with fixed poles. We also combine V+A with Lawson’s algorithm
[
28
]
to improve the accuracy of the approximation. We refer to this algorithm as
VA+Lawson

attempts to find the best polynomial approximation

2
Univariate Vandermonde with Arnoldi

Polynomial approximation of an unknown function by fitting a polynomial to a set of sample points from the domain is a classic problem. The interpolation and least-squares methods are two methods to solve this type of problem
[
30
,
32
]
. We start with the simplest 1D polynomial approximation problem in this section.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}
be a bounded domain,
\boldsymbol{X}:=\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq M}\subset\Omega
a set of
M
distinct sample points and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a continuous function which gives a value at each sample point. Let
{\cal P}_{1,n}
represent the space of univariate (
d=1
) polynomials with the highest order
n
, and
N:=n+1
denotes the total degrees of freedom.
We aim to find a
N
th degree polynomial approximation,
\mathcal{L}(f)
, such that

\mathcal{L}(f)=\argmin_{p\in{\cal P}_{1,n}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}|f({\bf x}_{i})-p({\bf x}_{i})|^{2}.

(1)

We can write
\mathcal{L}(f)({\bf x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}{\bf x}^{j-1}
where
\{c_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
are the monomial coefficients to be determined. The equation (
1
) can be formulated as a Vandermonde least-squares problem

\boldsymbol{c}=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\boldsymbol{Ac}-\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}\|_{2}.

(2)

Using the pseudoinverse, we can write the solution as
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{A}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}=(\boldsymbol{A}^{*}\boldsymbol{A})^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}^{*}{\tilde{f}}

where
\boldsymbol{A}
is an
M\times N
Vandermonde matrix with the
(i,j)
th entry
x_{i}^{j-1}
for
1\leq i\leq M
,
1\leq j\leq N
,
\boldsymbol{c}:=[c_{1},\dotsc,c_{N}]^{T}
, and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}:=[f({{\bf x}}_{1}),\dotsc,f({{\bf x}}_{M})]^{T}
. Since the sample points are distinct,
\boldsymbol{A}
is full rank and thus the solution
\boldsymbol{c}
exists and is unique. If
N=M
, we have an interpolation problem, and
\boldsymbol{A}
is a square matrix. The solution is given by
\boldsymbol{c}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}
. If
N<M
,
\boldsymbol{A}
is a tall rectangular matrix and we have a least-squares problem with normal equation
\boldsymbol{A^{*}Ac}=\boldsymbol{A^{*}\tilde{f}}
.

The least-squares problem (
2
) has the same solution as the normal equation

\boldsymbol{A^{*}Ac}=\boldsymbol{A^{*}\tilde{f}}.
If
A
is well-conditioned, the least squares problem can be solved by the normal equation. However, if
A
is moderately (or highly) ill-conditioned, since the condition number of
\mathbf{A^{*}A}
is the square of the condition number of
\mathbf{A}
, the conditioning of the normal equation

\boldsymbol{A^{*}Ac}=\boldsymbol{A^{*}\tilde{f}}
significantly worsens.
In this paper, unless otherwise stated, we focus on the least-squares problem (
2
) and assume that
N<M
.

Ideally, we can find the coefficients of the polynomial approximation by solving the least-squares problem (
2
). However, the Vandermonde matrices are well known to be exponentially ill-conditioned
[
27
]
(unless the nodes are uniformly distributed on the unit circle). This creates instability in solving the Vandermonde system and makes a moderately high degree Vandermonde approximation inaccurate. The ill-conditioning of the Vandermonde matrix is due to the non-orthogonal nature of the monomial basis. A potential solution to the ill-conditioning is to treat the monomial basis as a Krylov subspace sequence, such that

\spn\{1,\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{2},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{z}^{n}\}=\spn\{\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{Z}^{2}\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{Z}^{n}\boldsymbol{q}_{1}\}=\mathcal{K}_{N}{(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{q}_{1})},

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{z}=[{\bf x}_{1},\dotsc,{\bf x}_{M}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}=\diag({\bf x}_{1},\dotsc,{\bf x}_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{j}=\diag({\bf x}_{1}^{j},\dotsc,{\bf x}_{M}^{j})
and
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}=[1,\dotsc,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.
Based on this observation, in V+A we apply Arnoldi orthogonalization to the Krylov space
\mathcal{K}_{N}{(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{q}_{1})}
. The Krylov space
\mathcal{K}_{N}{(\boldsymbol{Z},\boldsymbol{q}_{1})}
is orthogonalized by the decomposition
\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{Q}_{-}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{H}
where
\boldsymbol{Q}
is the matrix with columns
[\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{q}_{N}]
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{-}
is the same matrix
without the final column. By the Arnoldi process, we transform the ill-conditioned Vandermonde system into an optimally conditioned system
2
2

2

The condition number of
\boldsymbol{Q}
is
1
.
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\boldsymbol{Qd}=\boldsymbol{{Q^{*}\tilde{f}}},\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{Q}=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{1}({{\bf x}}_{1})&\dots&\phi_{N}({{\bf x}}_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_{1}({{\bf x}}_{M})&\dots&\phi_{N}({{\bf x}}_{M})\end{bmatrix}{\quad\text{is orthonormal}\quad\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\boldsymbol{Q}=M\boldsymbol{I}_{N}}

(4)

and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}
is defined as before.

\boldsymbol{\phi}:=\{\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dotsc,\phi_{N}\}

(Discrete Orthogonal Polynomials)

is known as a set of
discrete orthogonal polynomials
or a discrete orthogonal basis.
\boldsymbol{d}:=[d_{1},\dots,d_{N}]^{T}
denotes the coefficient vector related to the discrete orthogonal polynomials, such that
\mathcal{L}(f)({\bf x})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}d_{j}\phi_{j}({\bf x})
. By construction, the discrete orthogonal basis
\boldsymbol{\phi}
spans the polynomial space
{\cal P}_{1,n}

\phi_{j},\phi_{k}\in{\cal P}_{1,n}
satisfies

\boldsymbol{X}=\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq M}

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\phi_{j}({\bf x}_{i})\phi_{k}({\bf x}_{i})=\delta_{j,k},\qquad 1\leq j,k\leq N

where
\delta_{j,k}
denotes the Kronecker delta. On real intervals, the discrete orthogonal polynomials are known to satisfy many algebraic properties analogous to those of the continuous orthogonal polynomials
[

40
]

2.1
Algorithm for Vandermonde with Arnoldi

The Arnoldi algorithm, originally applied to finding eigenvalues, uses the modified Gram-Schmidt process to produce a sequence of orthogonal vectors. The orthogonal columns
\{\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2},

\dotsc,\boldsymbol{q}_{N}\}
are obtained by the following recurrence formula

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}_{1,1}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{q}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{q}_{1}}{M}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}{\bf x}_{i},\qquad\boldsymbol{H}_{k+1,k}\boldsymbol{q}_{k+1}:=\boldsymbol{Z}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{H}_{j,k}\boldsymbol{q}_{j}\quad

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}=[1,\dotsc,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
and
\boldsymbol{Z}=\diag({\bf x}_{1},\dotsc,{\bf x}_{M})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
.
Note that
\boldsymbol{q}_{j}^{T}\boldsymbol{q}_{k}=0
for all
j,k=1,\dotsc,N
with
j\neq k
and
\|\boldsymbol{q}_{k}\|_{2}=\sqrt{M}
for
k=1,\dotsc,N
. The scaling of
\sqrt{M}
ensures that the Euclidean norm of the V+A solution,
\boldsymbol{d}
, is relatively constant as the number of sample points
M
increases.

\boldsymbol{H}_{j,k}
are the coefficients of the recurrence formula and also denote the
(j,k)
th entries of the matrix
\boldsymbol{H}
.
By orthogonality,
\boldsymbol{H}
is a
N\times(N-1)
lower Hessenberg matrix. For real sample points,
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq M}
,
\boldsymbol{H}
is tridiagonal and the Arnoldi algorithm is equivalent to the Lanczos algorithm.

In the Vandermonde least-squares system (
2
), we construct the full matrix
\mathbf{A}
and solve the poorly conditioned linear system. In V+A, however, we form the matrix
\mathbf{Q}
column by column, orthogonalizing each new column against all previous columns using the Arnoldi algorithm. Thus, the Arnoldi process gives us an optimally-conditioned least-squares problem (i.e., the condition number of
\boldsymbol{Q}
,
\kappa_{2}(\boldsymbol{Q})=1
)
\min_{\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\|\boldsymbol{Qd}-\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}\|_{2}
. The solution for the least-squares system exists,

\boldsymbol{d}=\frac{1}{M}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\boldsymbol{\tilde{f}}

(6)

where the
\frac{1}{M}
factor comes from the column scaling of
\boldsymbol{Q}
such that
\boldsymbol{Q^{*}Q}=M{\boldsymbol{I}_{N}}
. Note that the solution of the Vandermonde system,
\boldsymbol{c}
, and the solution of the V+A system,
\boldsymbol{d}
, are related by
\boldsymbol{d}=\frac{1}{M}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\boldsymbol{Ac}
. In MATLAB,
\boldsymbol{d}
can be obtained either by (
6
) or by the
backslash
command. The
backslash
command invokes the QR factorization for the least-squares problem and the Gaussian elimination for the interpolation problem (when
M=N
). For improved accuracy, we choose to obtain
\boldsymbol{d}
using the
backslash
command for the extra round of orthogonalization
[
11
]
.

Once the vector of coefficients
\boldsymbol{d}
is obtained, the least-squares approximant
p
can be evaluated at a different set of points
\boldsymbol{Y}=\{{\bf y}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq K}
. The entries of the
(k+1)
th column of
\boldsymbol{H}
are the coefficients used in the recursion formula of the discrete orthogonal polynomial, such that

\boldsymbol{H}_{k+1,k}\phi_{k+1}({\bf x})={\bf x}\phi_{k}({\bf x})-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{H}_{j,k}\phi_{j}({\bf x}),\qquad 1\leq k\leq N-1.

(7)

In the polynomial evaluation process, we use the same recursion formula as in (
7
) but apply it on a different set of points,
\boldsymbol{S}=\diag({\bf y}_{1},\dotsc,{\bf y}_{M})
, such that

\boldsymbol{U}_{k+1}:=\frac{1}{\boldsymbol{H}_{k+1,k}}\left(\boldsymbol{S}\boldsymbol{U}_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{H}_{j,k}\boldsymbol{U}_{j}\right),\quad 1\leq k\leq N-1,

(8)

with
\boldsymbol{U}_{1}:=[1,\dotsc,1]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and
\boldsymbol{H}
is given a priori by the Arnoldi process. The polynomials are evaluated at
\boldsymbol{Y}
by
\boldsymbol{p}:=\boldsymbol{Ud}
,
where
\boldsymbol{U}:=[\boldsymbol{U}_{1},\dotsc,\boldsymbol{U}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}
and
\boldsymbol{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is obtained a priori by the Arnoldi process. The
i
th entry of
\boldsymbol{p}
represents
\mathcal{L}f({\bf y}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}d_{j}\phi_{j}({\bf y}_{i})
for
1\leq i\leq K
. Note that the columns of
\boldsymbol{U}
are in general approximately orthogonal, but not orthogonal.
To test the validity of the least-squares approximant, we usually evaluate the polynomial approximant on a much finer mesh with
K\gg M
and compare the evaluated values with
f
. We assess the error of the approximant using this method by computing the maximum absolute value at the evaluation points,
\max_{\mathbf{y}_{i}\in\mathbf{Y}}|f(\mathbf{y}_{i})-\mathcal{L}f(\mathbf{y}_{i})|
.

Algorithm and Costs:
The univariate V+A fitting and evaluation is implemented in
[
11
]
using less than 15 lines of MATLAB code. We made slight variations to the code to improve their efficiency. We provide the algorithm in Algorithms

and

Doing CGS twice creates a discrete orthogonal basis that satisfies

, where
\boldsymbol{u}
is the unit roundoff. This is better than MGS, and a good enough bound for polynomial approximation problems which usually have dimensions of
N\lesssim 10^{3}
and
M\lesssim 10^{6}
. More details on the univariate V+A algorithm can be found in
[
11
]
.

In the first paper on V+A

(Disjoint Domain)

The key distinction between multivariate V+A and univariate V+A is that the multivariate monomial basis does not correspond to a Krylov subspace. Consequently, the multivariate monomial basis lacks a canonical ordering, meaning there is no universal order to list the columns of a multivariate Vandermonde matrix.
Therefore, in the multivariate V+A algorithm, we employ specific basis-ordering strategies. A new column is created by carefully selecting one coordinate from the sample points

\mathcal{I}=\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(2,0),(1,1),(0,2),(3,0),(2,1),(1,2),(0,3)\}.

4
Sample Complexity and Convergence of Multivariate V+A

In this section, we establish the sample complexity and convergence properties of the least squares approximation using the V+A algorithm for the total degree space. Note that the analysis follows naturally for the maximum degree of polynomial space, up to
d
-dependent constants. We demonstrate that, across many domains, utilizing either
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
equispaced points or
M={\cal O}(N^{2}\log N)
random sample points, the V+A approximant with
N
degrees of freedom is near-optimal. Namely, the error of the approximation is within a polynomial factor of that of the best approximation, for instance,
\|f-\mathcal{L}(f)\|_{\Omega}={\cal O}(N)\|f-p^{*}\|_{\Omega}
where the
{\cal O}(\cdot)
notation involves constants that depend on
d
.
Furthermore, we prove that, when the sample complexity condition is satisfied, the error of the least squares approximation using the V+A algorithm converges at a spectral rate. Specifically,

\|f-\mathcal{L}(f)\|_{\Omega}={\cal O}(n^{d-k})

(11)

where
d
is the dimension of the variables (i.e.,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
), and
k
is the highest order of the derivative of
f
that exists and is continuous (i.e., smoothness).

The proofs and key contributions for this section are structured as follows.

1.

We first prove that convex bodies or finite unions of convex bodies satisfy the Markov property for total degree polynomial space (and also maximum degree space), such that

\|\nabla p({\bf x})\|_{\Omega}:=\sup_{{\bf x}\in\Omega}\bigg(\max_{1\leq r\leq d}\bigg|\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{(r)}}({\bf x})\bigg|\bigg)\leq{\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}\|p\|_{\Omega}

(Markov Condition)

where
{\cal M}(\Omega)
is a domain-dependent constant. We highlight that this Markov property for the domain and the polynomial space is crucial for proving the sample complexity.
Note that the constant for the Markov property (for total degree polynomial space and maximum degree space) differs by a factor depending on
d
(Section
4.1
).

2.

Under the Markov property condition, we can form a set of
M={\cal O}(n^{2d})={\cal O}(N^{2})
equispaced sample points, such that the maximum absolute value of the polynomial in
\Omega
is bounded by a constant multiplying the maximum absolute value of the polynomial at sample points
\boldsymbol{X}
,
\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|p\|_{{\bf X}}
(Section
4.2
).
To the best of our knowledge, the Markov property and complexity results of this kind have only been established for total polynomial spaces in the literature
[
14
,
25
,
26
,
38
]
, but not specifically for maximum degree space.

3.

Then, we demonstrate V+A gives a near-optimal convergence rate (Section
4.3
) for the total degree polynomial spaces
7
7

7

In exact arithmetic, the V+A approximation is the same as that of a least-squares solution based on any basis, as long as the span of the bases is the same. The convergence results that therefore apply to any method based on least-squares, without the V+A orthogonalization. Of course, in finite precision, V+A can make a dramatic difference. For this reason, while the convergence results would hold for a least-squares method employing any basis, we often specify the use of V+A, as otherwise the theory may not be reflected in actual computations.

4.

At the end of the section, we provide a comparison between the sample complexity of the least squares approximation using the V+A Algorithm and the sample complexity of polynomial frame approximation

P_{{\bf u}}=\{{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},({\bf x}-{\bf x}^{*})^{T}{\bf u}=0\}

and
P_{{\bf u}}
contains no interior points of
\Omega
.
Now, assume

is an outer normal for
\Omega
, and let

be the supporting hyperplane with outer normal

lying a distance
\lambda_{\bf u}

from

. Let
{\bf x}^{0}\in P_{-{\bf u}}\cap\Omega
. The line segment
[{\bf x}^{0},{\bf x}^{*}]
lies in
\Omega
by convexity, and

p({\bf x}^{*}+\lambda{\bf w})
, where
{\bf w}=\frac{({\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*})}{\|{\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*}\|_{2}}
, is a polynomial in
\lambda
with order
\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}
.

(12)

This polynomial is bounded by
1
for

. Since polynomials are translation invariant, Lemma

yields

\displaystyle\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}^{2}}{\|{\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*}\|_{2}}\geq\bigg|\frac{d}{d\lambda}p({\bf x}^{*}+\lambda{\bf w})\bigg|=\bigg|\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})^{T}{\bf w}\bigg|=\frac{\lambda_{\bf u}}{\|{\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*}\|_{2}}\|\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})\|_{2}

where the last equality comes from
\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})^{T}({\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*})=\lambda_{\bf u}\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})^{T}{\bf u}=\lambda_{\bf u}\|\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})\|_{2}
. Also, since
\omega(\Omega)
is the minimal distance between two parallel supporting hyperplanes for
\Omega
, we have
\lambda_{\bf u}\geq\omega(\Omega)>0
. Therefore,

\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}^{2}}{\|{\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*}\|_{2}}\geq\frac{\omega(\Omega)}{\|{\bf x}^{0}-{\bf x}^{*}\|_{2}}\|\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})\|_{2}.

By the definitions of
\Omega
-norm and
{\bf x}^{*}
, we deduce that

\|\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})\|_{\Omega}\leq\|\nabla p({\bf x}^{*})\|_{\Omega,2}\leq\frac{2\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_{1}^{2}}{\omega(\Omega)}
. Extending the first result to the second result follows the same proof as in
[

, Thm 3.1]
.
∎

4.2
Sampling

We prove in this section that if
M={\cal O}(n^{2d})={\cal O}(N^{2})
equispaced sample points are chosen from a domain of a convex body or finite unions of convex bodies, then the maximum absolute value of the polynomial in
\Omega
is bounded by a constant multiplying the maximum absolute value of the polynomial at sample points
{\boldsymbol{X}}
,
\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|p\|_{{\bf X}}.

Since the constant for the Markov property
{\cal M}(\Omega)
only differs by a factor depending on
d
(Theorem
4.1
), the same order of sample complexity results apply to both the maximum degree and total degree spaces up to a
d
-dependent constant.
In a high-dimensional domain, it is sometimes easier to generate sample points randomly from the domain by rejection sampling. Therefore, in Section
4.2.2
, we prove that with
{\cal O}(N^{2}\log N)
random sample points
\tilde{{\bf X}}
generated i.i.d. from a uniform measure,
\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|p\|_{\tilde{{\bf X}}}
also holds with high probability for the maximum degree and total degree spaces up to a
d
-dependent constant.

4.2.1
Deterministic Sample Points

We define equispaced discretization in Definition
4.1
and the sample complexity for equispaced sample points is given in Theorem
4.2
.

Definition 4.1
.

(Equispaced Discretization)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a compact domain and
\Omega_{cube}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounding box containing
\Omega
. An equispaced discretization of
\Omega
is constructed by first defining an equispaced grid of points on
\Omega_{cube}
, and subsequently restricting this grid to the points contained within
\Omega
. We write
\{{\bf x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
for the set of equispaced points in
\Omega_{cube}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>1
be a convex body (i.e., a convex compact set with a non-empty interior) or finite unions of convex bodies. Let
0<c\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}
be a fixed constant.
Construct a set of
M
sample points
{\boldsymbol{X}}=\big\{{\bf x}_{i}|{\bf x}_{i}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\big\}_{1\leq i\leq M}
such that for all
{\bf x}\in\Omega
, we have

\inf_{{\bf x}_{i}\in{\bf X}}\|{\bf x}_{i}-{\bf x}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c}{{\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}}

(Mesh Condition)

where
{\cal M}(\Omega)=\frac{4d^{2}}{\omega(\Omega)}
for the maximum degree space, and
{\cal M}(\Omega)=\frac{4}{\omega(\Omega)}
for the total degree space. Then,

\displaystyle\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|p\|_{{\bf X}}

(13)

for all multivariate polynomials. Using equispaced discretization as in Definition
4.1
, the cardinality of
|{\bf X}|
satisfies
|{\bf X}|={\cal O}(n^{2d})={\cal O}(N^{2})
, where
N
is the number of basis functions. Note that the
{\cal O}(\cdot)
notation involves constants that depend on
d
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to
[
14
]
(3.13)–(3.18). We have sharpened the upper bound for
c
in (
Mesh Condition
) by a factor of
\sqrt{d}
. For any fixed
p\in{\cal P}_{d,n}
(i.e., maximum degree, total degree), let
{\bf x}_{*}=[{(x_{*})}_{1},\dotsc,{(x_{*})}_{d}]^{T}\in\Omega
such that the maximum value is attached at this point
\|p\|_{\Omega}=|p({\bf x}_{*})|
and for all
1\leq i\leq M
we have
{\bf x}_{i}\in{\bf X}
such that
\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{c}{{\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}}
. Then, using the mean value theorem, there exists
\boldsymbol{c}
on the line connecting
{\bf x}_{i}
to
{\bf x}_{*}
such that

\displaystyle\big|p({\bf x}_{*})-p({\bf x}_{i})\big|=|\nabla p(\boldsymbol{c})^{T}({\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{i})|\leq\big\|\nabla p\big\|_{[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]}\big\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{i}\big\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}\big\|\nabla p\big\|_{[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]}\big\|{\bf x}_{*}-{\bf x}_{i}\big\|_{\infty}

(14)

where
[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]
represents the line connecting
{\bf x}_{i}
to
{\bf x}_{*}
and
\big\|\nabla p\big\|_{[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]}=\sup_{{\bf x}\in[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]}\bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{d}\big|\frac{\partial p}{\partial x_{(r)}}({\bf x})\big|^{2}\bigg)^{1/2}
.

Since
\Omega
is convex, we have
[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]\subset\Omega
. Theorem
4.1
gives that
\big\|\nabla p\big\|_{[{\bf x}_{i},{\bf x}_{*}]}\leq{\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}\|p\|_{\Omega}
. Together with (
Mesh Condition
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\big|p({\bf x}_{*})-p({\bf x}_{i})\big|\leq\sqrt{d}\bigg({\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}\|p\|_{\Omega}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{c}{{\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}}\bigg)\leq\sqrt{d}c\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|p\|_{\Omega}.

where the last inequality comes from
0<c\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d}}
. Hence,

\big\|p\big\|_{\Omega}-\big|p({\bf x}_{i})\big|=\big|p({\bf x}_{*})\big|-\big|p({\bf x}_{i})\big|\leq\big|p({\bf x})-p({\bf x}_{i})\big|\leq\frac{1}{2}\|p\|_{\Omega}.

We deduce that
\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|p\|_{{\bf X}}
.
To prove the second argument regarding the cardinality of the mesh, we construct the mesh
{\bf X}
using a standard
discretization process. For
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we enclose
\Omega
in a
d
-dimensional hypercube
\Omega_{cube}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We then cover
\Omega_{cube}
with equispaced grids dense enough such that (
Mesh Condition
) is satisfied. This gives us
{\cal O}(n^{2d})={\cal O}(N^{2})
number of nodes for the mesh.
Lastly, the extension from a convex domain to a domain with the finite union of convex bodies is a direct application of
[
14
, Lemma 4(i)]
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

(Equispaced Sampling Method)

Definition
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
provide us with a way to discretize the domain and choose sample points that can control the size of the discrete orthogonal polynomial. For
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we enclose
\Omega
in a
d

\Omega_{cube}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Omega_{cube}
with equispaced grids dense enough such that (

) is satisfied which gives us

Note that the deterministic sampling method can be further improved in two ways. Firstly, instead of sampling in the hypercube domain

, one could sample directly on

using rejection sampling. Details on the randomized sampling method are discussed in Section

. Secondly, instead of taking equispaced samples, we can choose a set of sample points with a ‘near-optimal weight’. Details on near-optimal sampling strategy are discussed in Section
5
.

4.2.2
Randomized Sample Points

Theorem
8
gives the sample complexity for random sample points.

Theorem 4.3
.

(Randomized Sampling Method)

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>1
be a convex body or finite union of convex bodies. Let
\tilde{{\bf X}}=\{\tilde{{\bf x}}_{1},\tilde{{\bf x}}_{2},\dots,\tilde{{\bf x}}_{\tilde{M}}\}
be a set of points independently and uniformly sampled from
\Omega
. Let
\tilde{c}>1
, and set
{\tilde{M}}\geq\tilde{c}M\log(M)
. Then,
\|p\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|p\|_{\tilde{{\bf X}}}
with probability at least
1-M^{-\tilde{c}}
for the total degree polynomial spaces
8
8

8

The same order of result is true for the maximum degree polynomial spaces, up to a
d
-dependent constant.
.

Proof.

We prove this theorem using a similar approach to
[
39
, Thm 4.1]
. For the set of random sample points
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}
to satisfy (
Mesh Condition
), we need to prove that with high probability for every
{\bf x}\in\Omega
, one can find a point in
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}=\{\tilde{{\bf x}}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\tilde{M}}
such that their distance is at most
E:=\frac{c}{{\cal M}(\Omega)n^{2}}
. Specifically, for every
{\bf x}\in\Omega
,
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}\cap\bar{B}_{E}({\bf x})\neq\varnothing
is satisfied with high probability where
\bar{B}_{E}({\bf x}_{i})
denotes the closed ball with center
{\bf x}_{i}
and radius
E
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}\cap\bar{B}_{E}({\bf x})\neq\varnothing\right]=1-\big(\mathbb{P}\left[\tilde{{\bf x}}_{i}\cap\bar{B}_{E}({\bf x})=\varnothing\right]\big)^{\tilde{M}}\geq 1-\left(1-|{\bf X}|^{-1}\right)^{\tilde{M}}\geq 1-\left(1-M^{-1}\right)^{\tilde{M}}\geq 1-e^{-\tilde{M}/M}.

The first equality follows from the i.i.d sampling nature of the random sample points. Note that
|{\bf X}|=M
denotes the cardinality of
{\bf X}
. The first inequality is given in the proof of
[
39
, Thm 4.1]
. By taking
\tilde{M}\geq\tilde{c}M\log(M)
with
\tilde{c}>1
, we achieve
\boldsymbol{\tilde{X}}\cap\bar{B}_{E}({\bf x})\neq\varnothing
with probability at least
1-M^{-\tilde{c}}
. Applying Theorem
4.2
completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Convergence of Multivariate Vandermonde with Arnoldi

Theorem
4.2
and Theorem

4.3.1
Error Bound

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
f:\Omega\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
be a continuous multivariate function. Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>1
be a convex body or finite unions of convex bodies. Let
p^{*}\in{\cal P}^{T}_{d,n}
be the best polynomial approximation of
f
in the total degree polynomial space, such that
\|f-p^{*}\|_{\Omega}\leq\|f-p\|_{\Omega}
for all
p\in{\cal P}^{T}_{d,n}
. Construct a set of equispaced sample points as described in Remark
4.2
,
{\boldsymbol{X}}=\big\{{\bf x}_{i}\big\}_{1\leq i\leq M}\subset\Omega
with
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
.
Applying Algorithm
3
using the sample points
{\bf X}
, the error of the least squares approximation satisfies
9
9

9

The bound for the maximum degree space can be deduced similarly up to a
d
-dependent constant.

\|f-\mathcal{L}(f)\|_{\Omega}\leq\left(1+\frac{2}{M}\|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\|_{\infty}\right)\|f-p^{*}\|_{\Omega}\leq\bigg(1+{\cal O}(N)\bigg)\|f-p^{*}\|_{\Omega}

(15)

where the columns of
\boldsymbol{Q}
are the values of the discrete orthogonal basis generated by V+A at the sample points
{\bf X}
. The matrix
\infty
-norm is defined as the largest absolute value of the matrix.

Proof.

Denote
g:=f-p^{*}
. We have

\|f-\mathcal{L}(f)\|_{\Omega}\leq\|\underbrace{f-p^{*}}_{:={g}}\|_{\Omega}+\|\underbrace{\mathcal{L}(f-p^{*})}_{={\mathcal{L}(g)}\in{{\cal P}^{T}_{d,n}}}\|_{\Omega}.

(16)

Since
\mathcal{L}(g)\in{{\cal P}^{T}_{d,n}}
, we write it as a linear combination of discrete orthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{L}(g):=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\beta_{j}\phi_{j}=\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\beta}
where
\boldsymbol{\beta}:=[\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{N}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.
According to (
6
),
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\frac{1}{M}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}{\tilde{g}}
where
\tilde{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
is a vector with entries as
\{f({\bf x}_{i})-p({\bf x}_{i})\}_{1\leq i\leq M}
. Using Theorem
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}(g)\|_{\Omega}\leq 2\|\mathcal{L}(g)\|_{\boldsymbol{X}}=2\|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{\beta}\|_{\infty}=\frac{2}{M}\|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\tilde{g}\|_{\infty}.

(17)

Substituting (
17
) into (
16
) gives the first inequality in (
15
). Using the matrix property
\frac{1}{M}\|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\|_{\infty}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{M}}\|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\|_{2}=\sqrt{M}
and the sample complexity assumption
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
, we arrive at the second inequality in (
15
).
∎

Remark 4.3
.

A similar bound was derived by Calvi and Levenberg in

using Hermitian inner product norm,

\|f-\mathcal{L}(f)\|_{\Omega}\leq\bigg(1+2(1+\sqrt{M})\bigg)\|f-p^{*}\|_{\Omega}.

gives a sharper approximation than the bound in (

Example 4.5
.

(Improved Numerical Error Bound on Certain Domains)
Due to the special properties of the V+A basis (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{Q}
), for the intervals, 2D tensor product domain, and other domains (as indicated in Figure

\|f-\mathcal{L}(f)\|_{\Omega}\approx(1+\sqrt{2N})\|f-p^{*}\|_{\Omega}.

In other words, with
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
equispaced sample points,
\frac{1}{M}\|\boldsymbol{Q}\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\|_{\infty}
scales like
\sqrt{2N}
in the domains that we tested.

For intervals and 2D tensor product domains the discrete orthogonal basis generated by

equispaced sample points resembles the scaled Legendre polynomials, such that

for

The scaled Legendre polynomials for

Definition 4.2
.

(Univariate Scaled Legendre Polynomials) For any interval

with

.

(Multivariate Scaled Legendre Polynomials) For tensor–product domain

4.4
Related Work on Sample Complexity

In this subsection, we discuss the difference in construction between the sample complexity of the least squares approximation using the V+A Algorithm and the sample complexity of polynomial frame approximation.

There are many existing proofs for the sample complexity of polynomial approximations
[
4
,
5
,
15
,
16
,
30
]
.
For instance, in
[
15
,
16
]
, the proof by Cohen et al. first constructs a
\mathcal{L}^{2}
-continuous orthogonal basis
\{J_{1},J_{2},\dotsc,J_{N}\}
, such that
\int_{\Omega}J_{i}({\bf x})J_{j}({\bf x})dx=\delta_{i,j}.
Then, the solution of their least-squares problem can be computed by solving the
M\times M
Gram matrix system,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}d_{1}=f
where the
(i,j)
th entries of
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}
is
J_{j}({\bf x}_{i})
for
1\leq j\leq N
and
1\leq i\leq M
.
The purpose of their analysis is to find how many sample points we need such that the discrete measure inherits the orthogonality of
\{J_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq N}
. Namely, how to choose
M
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{M}{J_{j}({\bf x}_{i})J_{k}({\bf x}_{i})}\approx\delta_{j,k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1})\approx\boldsymbol{I}_{n}.

Using the exponentially decreasing bounds on tail distributions
\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}-I\|\geq\frac{1}{2})
, Cohen et al. proved that
M={\cal O}(N^{2}\log N)
random sample points is enough to obtain a stable least-squares approximation.

In
[
5
]
, Adcock and Huybrechs give different proof for the sample complexity of frame approximation on irregular domains. The key step of the proof uses the Nikolskii inequality
[
29
]
on the bounding tensor-product domain

\|p\|_{\Omega_{cube}}\leq\mathcal{N}\|p\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega_{cube})}

(21)

where
\mathcal{N}
is a constant that depends on the domain and the sample complexity. They proved that the least squares are near-optimal if the domain satisfies the
\lambda
-rectangle property. Namely, the bounding domain can be written as a (possibly overlapping and uncountable) union of hyperrectangles
\Omega_{cube}
where
\lambda
is the value of
\text{Volume}(\Omega)/\text{Volume}(\Omega_{cube})
. The sample complexity required scales by
N^{2}\lambda^{-1}
where
N
is the total degree of freedom. Similar to the Markov constant
{\cal M}(\Omega)
introduced in (
Markov Condition
), the parameter
\lambda
in
[
5
]
is also independent of the bounding domain and is a constant that depends on
\Omega
.

Although sample complexity results obtained for the least squares approximation using the frame approximation and using the V+A method are similar, the idea and the construction of the proof are different. Firstly, in polynomial frame approximation, the proof starts with a continuous orthogonal polynomial and explores the distribution of sample points such that the discrete least-squares matrix is approximately orthogonal (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}^{*}\boldsymbol{Q}_{1}\approx I_{n}
). By construction in V+A, we start with a discrete orthogonal basis (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{Q}^{*}\boldsymbol{Q}=I_{n}
) and examine the behaviours of these discrete orthogonal polynomials in the domain. Another difference is that these two approaches build the bound using different norm spaces in different domains. In V+A, we use the suprema of discrete orthogonal polynomials,
\|p\|_{\boldsymbol{X}}
, while in the polynomial frame approach, the Nikolskii inequality uses the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
norm,
\|p\|_{\mathcal{L}^{2}(\Omega_{cube})}
.
The analysis for the least squares approximation using V+A method applies to the maximum degree or total degree polynomial spaces. In contrast, the analysis in
[
5
]
provides a more general result regarding polynomial spaces, extending to any polynomial space defined by a lower set. This includes maximum degree, total degree, and hyperbolic cross polynomial spaces. Current sample complexity analysis (derived via Lemma (
4.2
)) results in a scaling of
M={\cal O}\left(\frac{N}{\log^{d-1}(N)}\right)^{2d}
for the hyperbolic cross index set. This scaling is less favorable compared to the
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
scaling reported in
[
5
]
, although the domain assumptions differ, and the error bound in that case is measured in the
L_{2}
norm rather than the
L_{\infty}
norm. Further investigation into improving the sample complexity analysis for hyperbolic cross polynomial spaces remains an open area of research.
In terms of the domain, the analysis in
[
5
]
requires the bounding domain can be written as a (possibly overlapping and uncountable) union of hyperrectangles
\Omega_{cube}
, whereas the analysis for the least squares approximation using V+A method holds for all domain satisfying (
Markov Condition
) (Remark
4.7
).

More recently, Adcock and Shadrin
[
6
]
demonstrated that with linear oversampling of equispaced sample points and polynomial extensions over an extended interval, the equispaced samples become near-optimal for polynomial frame approximation in 1D. They propose a well-conditioned method that ensures exponential decay of the error, reaching a finite, user-controlled tolerance.
In the following section, we will explore the use of the weighted least-squares algorithm and the multivariate V+A algorithm to give a well-conditioned and near-optimal polynomial approximation.

5
Near-Optimal Sampling Strategy for V+A

In this section, we propose a new variant of the weighted least-squares algorithm that uses the multivariate V+A to create the discrete orthogonal basis. We refer to our V+A weighted least-squares algorithm as
VA+Weight
.

In
[
2
,
3
,
16
,
30
]
, the authors gave comprehensive analyses on this weighted sampling strategy and proved that only
M={\cal O}(N\log N)
sample points are needed for a well-conditioned and accurate approximation. A recent work
[
1
]
provides a comprehensive review on optimal sampling for (weighted) least-squares approximation in arbitrary linear spaces, introducing the Christoffel function as a key quantity in the analysis of (weighted) least-squares approximation from random samples. In
VA+Weight
, we use the same weighting measure as in
[
2
]
.
But instead of creating the discrete orthogonal basis with QR factorization, we use V+A as the orthogonalization strategy for the Vandermonde basis. Since the Vandermonde matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
is usually highly ill-conditioned, and computing its
Q
factor incurs errors proportional to the condition number of
\boldsymbol{A}

[
23
, Ch. 19]
. Even though the QR factorization is a stable orthogonalization technique, the discrete orthogonal basis generated from
\boldsymbol{A}
could still be inaccurate. We refer to the weighted least-squares approximation using the QR factorization as
QR+Weight
.
Along with multiple numerical examples, we illustrate that

VA+Weight
gives more accurate approximations than
QR+Weight
for high-degree polynomial approximations. Due to the reduced sample density,
VA+Weight
also gives a lower online computational cost than the unweighted V+A least-squares method. Moreover,
VA+Weight
acts as a practical tool for selecting the near-optimal distribution of sample points in a high-dimensional irregular domain.

The section is arranged as follows. In Section
5.1
, we explain the algorithm and the numerical setup for the weighted least-squares approximation. We provide proof of the stability of the weighting measure for sample points. In Section
5.2
, we give numerical examples to compare the
VA+Weight
and
QR+Weight
algorithms.

Many sampling measures, such as Chebyshev points in real intervals and Padua points

.

\mathbb{P}(\|\boldsymbol{G}-\boldsymbol{I}\|_{2}<\hat{\delta}+\epsilon_{m})\geq\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\{\|\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{G})-\boldsymbol{I}\|_{2}<\epsilon_{m}\})}_{:=P_{1}}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}(\{\|\boldsymbol{G}-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{G})\|_{2}<\hat{\delta}\})}_{:=P_{2}}.

Finding the best distribution of sample points in a high-dimensional irregular domain is an open question in the literature. We highlight that the weighting method in Algorithm
5
is a practical tool for finding the near-optimal distribution of the sample points. We plot the
\hat{M}=N\log N

VA+Weight
for different domains in Figure
15
. Notice that the selected points are clustered near corners and boundaries. This distribution pattern matches the pattern in Padua points in tensor-product domains. This behaviour occurs because the measure we use to select sample points assigns a higher probability to points where the discrete orthogonal polynomials have larger absolute values. Typically, these points cluster near the boundaries. A simple example is the set of Chebyshev polynomials or Legendre polynomials in the interval
[-1,1]
, where the polynomials exhibit larger absolute values at the endpoints.

6
Conclusions and Future Works

In this paper, we analyzed the multivariate Vandermonde with the Arnoldi method to approximate
d
-dimensional functions on irregular domains. The V+A technique resolves the ill-conditioning issue of the Vandermonde matrix and builds the discrete orthogonal polynomials with respect to the domain. Our main theoretical result is the convergence of the multivariate V+A least-squares approximation for a large class of domains. The sample complexity required for convergence is quadratic in the degree of freedom of the approximation space, up to a logarithmic factor polynomial approximations in total degree and maximum degree polynomial spaces. For hyperbolic cross polynomial spaces,
[
5
]
proved a sample complexity scaling of
M={\cal O}(N^{2})
under the
L_{2}
norm, the study of hyperbolic cross polynomial spaces under the
L_{\infty}
norm remains a topic for our future research.
Using a suitable weighting measure, we showed that the sample complexity can be further improved. The weighted V+A least-squares method requires only log-linear sample complexity
M={\cal O}(N\log(N))
.

Our preliminary numerical results validate that the least squares approximation using the V+A method provides well-conditioned and near-optimal approximations for multivariate functions on (irregular) domains with the dimension
d
ranging from
1
to
5
. Additionally, the (weighted) least squares approximation using the V+A method performs competitively with state-of-the-art orthogonalization techniques and can serve as a practical tool for selecting near-optimal distributions of sample points in irregular domains.
It is important to note that Algorithm
3
and the theories we prove in this paper for multivariate V+A are applicable for
d\geq 2
. However, due to the growth of the polynomial basis with the dimensionality of functional approximation (i.e.,
N={\cal O}(n^{d})
for total degree and maximum degree polynomial spaces), the CPU time required for computations for even higher dimensions will soon become infeasible due to the curse of dimensionality.
The development of practical and scalable algorithms for high-dimensional functional approximation is also deferred to future work.

V+A has many applications beyond least-squares fitting. Our numerical experiments showed that the
VA+Lawson
algorithm improves the approximation accuracy and generates an equioscillating error curve. Yet, the convergence profile for
VA+Lawson
still seems to be unknown and could be an objective of future work. Another extension under consideration involves using multivariate V+A in vector- and matrix-valued rational approximations
[
17
, Subsec. 2.4]
. Generally speaking, in any application that involves matrix operations of Vandermonde matrix (or its related form), it seems likely that the V+A procedure could be an effective idea to apply.
