Lions’ representation theorem and applications
W. Arendt, I. Chalendar, R. Eymard

Abstract
. The Representation Theorem of Lions (RTL) is a version of the Lax–Milgram Theorem where completeness of one of the spaces is not complete. In this paper, RTL is deduced from an operator-theoretical version on normed space. The main point of the paper is a theory of derivations, based on RTL, for which well-posedness is proved. One application concerns non-autonomous evolution equations with a new initial-value and a periodic boundary condition for the time variable.

1.
Introduction

A most elegant way to prove well-posedness of a non-autonomous evolutionary problem has been given by J. L. Lions
[
14
, Chap. III, Théorème 1.1, p. 37]
using a surprising version of the Lax–Milgram Theorem, where one of the spaces is not complete (see e.g. Theorem
2.1
below). We call it the Representation Theorem of Lions, RTL. The purpose of this article is to study a derivation problem with the help of Lions’ result. A Hilbert space
V
is given, which we choose real for this introduction, as well as a derivation
{\mathcal{D}}:R\to V^{*}
, where
R
is a dense subspace of
V
; i.e.
\mathcal{D}
satisfies

\langle{\mathcal{D}}r_{1},r_{2}\rangle_{V^{*},V}+\langle{\mathcal{D}}r_{2},r_{1}\rangle_{V^{*},V}=0\mbox{ for all }r_{1},r_{2}\in R.

It turns out that this simple setting leads to an interesting structure and to interesting results on boundary conditions. In fact,
\mathcal{D}
can be extended naturally to a Hilbert space
W
which embeds continuously into
V
. Given a coercive operator
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{*})
,
we study the problem

(1.1)

{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=f

where
f\in V^{*}
is given and
u\in W
is a solution to be found. We obtain well-posedness results if we impose boundary conditions which are expressed in terms of (strongly) admissible subspaces
{Z}
of
W
(see Section
3
and Section
4.2
). Our main application concerns the evolution equations also considered by Lions (and which have seen a remarkable revival recently; see e.g. Ouhabaz and Spina
[
17
]
, Haak and Ouhabaz
[
13
]
, Ouhabaz
[
16
]
, Auscher and Egert
[
9
]
, Dier and Zacher
[
11
]
, Achache and Ouhabaz
[
1
,
2
]
as well as
[
7
,
8
]
). For these
evolution equations, we obtain a new result whose originality lies in the very general boundary conditions.

It looks as follows. Let
U,H
be Hilbert spaces where
U
is continuously and densely embedded in
H
so that we have the Gelfand triple

{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\hookrightarrow}}H\hookrightarrow{U}^{\prime}.

Let
W=H^{1}(0,T;{U}^{\prime})\cap L^{2}(0,T;{U})
. Then, as is well-known,
W\subset{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],H)
. Let
V=L^{2}(0,T;{U})
and let
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{*})
be coercive. Typically, one chooses for
{\mathcal{A}}
a non-autonomous differential operator. The following is proved in Section
5
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Phi\in{\mathcal{L}}(H)
be a contraction,
f\in L^{2}(0,T;{U}^{\prime})
,
y_{0}\in H
. Then there exists a unique

such that

(1.2)

u^{\prime}+{\mathcal{A}}u=f\mbox{ in }L^{2}(0,T;V^{\prime})

(1.3)

u(0)=\Phi^{*}u(T)+y_{0}\mbox{ in }H.

Theorem
1.1
is known if the form
a:V\times V\to{\mathbb{R}}
, given by
a(u,v)=\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,v\rangle_{V^{*},V}
is symmetric (see

[
20
, III. Proposition 2.4]
) but the estimates given here for the non-symmetric case are more demanding.

In Theorem
1.1
, not only well-posedness but also the

of the solution are remarkable: all three terms of the evolution equation
u^{\prime}
,
{\mathcal{A}}u
and
f
are in the space

(see
[

for more information on such regularity properties).

The theory of derivations as developed in the present article has applications for completely different subjects. If we identify

and
V^{\prime}
by the Riesz Theorem, then a densely defined operator

2.
Representation theorems for continuous linear or antilinear forms and dissipative operators

In this section we describe (generalizations of) the representation theorems of Lions and give an application to dissipative operators.

2.1.
Representation theorems of Lions

The representation theorems of Lions (abbreviated by RTL)
may be expressed in terms of operators (which seems to be new), or in terms of bilinear forms or sesquilinear forms. They depend also heavily on the topological properties (completeness, reflexivity) of the involved normed vector spaces over
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
. The antidual of a normed space
X
is denoted by
X^{\prime}
,
whereas the dual space is denoted by
X^{*}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Representation Theorem of Lions, real version)
.

Let
X
be a reflexive Banach space and
Y
be a normed space over
{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
a:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
be bilinear such that, for each
y\in Y
,
x\mapsto a(x,y)
is continuous on
X
. Let
\beta>0
. The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}a(x,y)\geq\beta\|y\|_{Y}
;

(ii)

for all
L\in Y^{*}
, the dual of
Y
, there exists
x_{L}\in X
such that

\|x_{L}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|L\|_{Y^{*}}\mbox{ and }\langle L,y\rangle_{Y^{*},Y}=a(x_{L},y\rangle,\,\,\,y\in Y

Next we consider the complex version.

Theorem 2.2

(Representation Theorem of Lions, complex version)
.

Let
X
be a reflexive Banach space and
Y
be a normed space over
{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
a:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{C}}
be sesquilinear such that, for each
y\in Y
,
x\mapsto a(x,y)
is continuous on
X
. Let
\beta>0
. The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits(a(x,y))=\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}|a(x,y)|\geq\beta\|y\|_{Y}
;

(ii)

for all
L\in Y^{\prime}
, the antidual of
Y
, there exists
x_{L}\in X
such that

\|x_{L}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|L\|_{Y^{\prime}}\mbox{ and }\langle L,y\rangle_{Y^{\prime},Y}=a(x_{L},y\rangle,\,\,\,y\in Y

In Theorem
2.2
, it is remarkable that
Y
does not need to be complete and
a
does not need to be continuous.

This theorem is a consequence of the following result involving operators instead of sesquilinear forms.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
E
and
F
be normed spaces over
{\mathbb{K}}
,
T:E\to F
linear and
\beta>0
.
The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\|Tx\|_{F}\geq\beta\|x\|_{E}
for all
x\in E
;

(ii)

for all
x^{*}\in E^{*}
, there exists
y^{*}\in F^{*}
such that

x^{*}=y^{*}\circ T\mbox{ and }\|y^{*}\|\leq\frac{\|x^{*}\|}{\beta}.

(iii)

for all
x^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}
, there exists
y^{\prime}\in F^{\prime}
such that

x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\circ T\mbox{ and }\|y^{\prime}\|\leq\frac{\|x^{\prime}\|}{\beta}.

Note that, in Theorem
2.3
,
T
is not necessarily continuous. On the other side, if
T\in{\mathcal{L}}(E,F)
, then
x^{*}=T^{*}y^{*}
and (ii) (resp. (iii)) means that
T^{*}
(resp.
T^{\prime}
) is surjective and
\|T^{*}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
(resp.
\|T^{\prime}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
).

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): Let
Z=TE\subset F
. Then
T:E\to Z
is bijective and

\|T^{-1}z\|_{E}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|z\|_{F},\,\,z\in Z.

Let
x^{*}\in E^{*}
. Then
x^{*}\circ T^{-1}\in Z^{*}
. From the Hahn-Banach theorem, there exists
y^{*}\in F^{*}
such that
y^{*}_{|Z}=x^{*}\circ T^{-1}
and

\|y^{*}\|_{F^{*}}=\|x^{*}\circ T^{-1}\|_{Z^{*}}\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\|x^{*}\|_{E^{*}}.

Now, for
x\in E
,

\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{E^{*},E}=\langle x^{*},T^{-1}Tx\rangle_{E^{*},E}=\langle y^{*},Tx\rangle_{Y^{*},Y}.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Let
x\in E
. Then, by the Hahn-Banach theorem, there exists
x^{*}\in E^{*}
,
\|x^{*}\|\leq 1
with
\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*},X}=\|x\|
. By (ii) there exists
y^{*}\in F^{*}
with
\|y^{*}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta}
and
x^{*}=y^{*}\circ T
. It follows that

\|Tx\|_{F}\geq\beta\langle y^{*},Tx\rangle_{Y^{*},Y}=\beta\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*},X}=\beta\|x\|_{E}.

The equivalence with (iii) follows from similar arguments.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

First note that

\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits a(x,y)=\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}|a(x,y)|.

Indeed, for each
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, there exists
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that

a(x,y)=e^{i\theta}|a(x,y)|
. Then
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits a(e^{-i\theta}x,y)=|a(x,y)|
with
\|e^{-i\theta}x\|=\|x\|
.

Let
E=Y
and
F=X^{\prime}
, the antidual of
X
. Define
T:E\to F
by
Tw=\overline{a(.,w)}
. Then
T
is linear and

\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits{a(x,y)}\geq\beta\|y\|_{Y}\Longleftrightarrow\|Ty\|_{X^{\prime}}\geq\beta\|y\|_{Y},

since
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\overline{a(x,y)}=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits a(x,y)
. By Theorem
2.3
, Condition (ii) of Theorem
2.2
holds if and only if, for all
L\in Y^{\prime}
, there exists
x_{L}\in X^{\prime\prime}\simeq X
(
X
is reflexive) such that
\|x_{L}\|\leq\frac{\|L\|_{Y^{\prime}}}{\beta}
and

\langle L,y\rangle_{Y^{\prime},Y}=\langle Ty,x_{L}\rangle_{X^{\prime},X}=a(x_{L},y),\,\,\,y\in Y.

∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
. Let
X
be a reflexive Banach space and
Y
be a normed space such that
Y
embeds continuously in
X
.
Let
\beta>0
and let
{a}
be a sesquilinear form on
X\times Y
such that

a)

|{a}(y,y)|\geq\beta\|y\|_{Y}^{2}
for all

y\in Y
;

b)

for all
y\in Y
,
x\mapsto{a}(x,y)
is continuous on
X
.

Let
L\in Y^{\prime}
.
Then there exists
x_{L}\in X
such that

a(x_{L},y)=\langle L,y\rangle_{Y^{\prime},Y}\mbox{ for all }y\in Y.

Proof.

There exists
c_{Y}>0
such that
\|y\|_{X}\leq c_{Y}\|y\|_{Y}
for all
y\in Y
. Thus for
y\in Y\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X,\|x\|_{X}\leq 1}|a(x,y)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|y\|_{X}}|a(y,y)|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{\|y\|_{X}}\|y\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{c_{Y}}\|y\|_{Y}.

Now the claim follows from Theorem
2.2
.
∎

Lions proved Theorem
2.1

[
14
, III, Théorème 1.1]
(see also
[
20
, III.2 Theorem 2.1]
) considering merely Hilbert (and pre-Hilbert) spaces. Our proof via Theorem
2.3
seems to be new.

We refer to the circle of results from Theorem
2.1
to Corollary
2.4
as RTL, that is,
representation theorems of Lions
.

2.2.
Dissipative operators

The following considerations are somehow a side order in this paper. Its aim is to show that Theorem
2.3
is useful also in a non-Hilbert space situation. We will give a new characterization of dissipativity (Theorem
2.6
), a notion which will also be used in Section
3
.

An
operator

B
on a Banach space
X
is a linear mapping from
\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
to
X
, where the
domain

\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
of
B
is a subspace of
X
. By

\|x\|_{B}:=\|x\|_{X}+\|Bx\|_{X}

we denote the
graph norm
of
B
. The operator
B
is called
closed
if its graph

G(B):=\{(x,Bx):x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)\}

is closed in
X\times X
. This means that for
x_{n}\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)

(2.1)

x_{n}\to x\mbox{ and }Bx_{n}\to y\mbox{ in }X\mbox{ implies }x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)\mbox{ and }Bx=y.

In other words,
B
is closed if and only if
(\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B),\|\cdot\|_{B})
is complete.

An operator
B
is called
closable
if
(0,y)\in\overline{G(B)}\subset X\times X
implies
y=0
. Then there exists a unique operator
\overline{B}
on
X
, the
closure
of
B
such that
\overline{G(B)}=G(\overline{B})
.

We call
B

dissipative
if

(2.2)

\|x-tBx\|_{X}\geq\|x\|_{X}\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B),\,\,t>0.

If
X
is a Hilbert space, this is equivalent to

(2.3)

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Bx,x\rangle_{X}\leq 0\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B).

Returning to the Banach space context, we say that
B
is
m
-dissipative
if
B
is dissipative and
(\mathop{\rm Id}\nolimits-tB)
is surjective for one (equivalently all)
t>0
. Then
(Id-tB)^{-1}\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
and
\|(\mathop{\rm Id}\nolimits-tB)^{-1}\|\leq 1
for all
t>0
. A densely-defined dissipative operator is closable and its closure

\overline{B}
is dissipative
[
3
, Lemma 3.4.4]
.

Remark 2.5
.

The operator
B
generates a contractive
C_{0}
-semigroup on
X
if and only if
B
is
m
-dissipative and has dense domain.

If
B
is
densely defined
(i.e., if
\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
is dense in
X
), then the adjoint
B^{*}

of
B
is defined by its domain
\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B^{*})
equal to the set

\{y^{*}\in X^{*}:\exists x^{*}\in X^{*}\mbox{ such that }\langle y^{*},Bx\rangle_{X^{*},X}=\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*},X},x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(A)\}

and

B^{*}y^{*}=x^{*},

where
y^{*}\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B^{*})
and
x^{*}\in X^{*}
such that
\langle y^{*},Bx\rangle_{X^{*},X}=\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*},X}
for all
x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
. Note that
x^{*}
is unique since
\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
is dense in
X
.

Theorem
2.3
allows us to give the following dual characterization of dissipativity, which seems to be new.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
B
be a densely defined operator on a Banach space
X
. The following assertions are equivalent.

(i)

B
is dissipative;

(ii)

for all
t>0
and for all
y^{*}\in X^{*}
there exists
x^{*}\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B^{*})
such that

x^{*}-tB^{*}x^{*}=y^{*}\mbox{ and }\|x^{*}\|_{X^{*}}\leq\|y^{*}\|_{X^{*}}.

Proof.

Consider the spaces
E=\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
with norm
\|x\|_{E}=\|x\|_{B}
and
F=X
with
\|x\|_{F}=\|x\|_{X}
. Let
t>0
and consider the linear mapping
T:E\to F
given by
Tx=x-tBx
. Note that
T
is not continuous unless
B
is continuous. Since
\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
is dense in
X
, one has
E^{*}=X^{*}
. Theorem
2.3
tells us that

\|x-tBx\|_{X}=\|Tx\|_{X}\geq\|x\|_{X}
for all
x\in E
if and only if for all
x^{*}\in X^{*}
there exists
y^{*}\in X^{*}
such that
\|y^{*}\|\leq\|x^{*}\|
and
x^{*}=y^{*}\circ T
, i.e.
\langle x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*},X}=\langle y^{*},x-tBx\rangle_{X^{*},X}
, or equivalently

\langle y^{*},tBx\rangle_{X^{*},X}=\langle y^{*}-x^{*},x\rangle_{X^{*},X},\,x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B).

This in turn is equivalent to
y^{*}\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B^{*})
and
tB^{*}y^{*}=y^{*}-x^{*}
, i.e.
y^{*}-tB^{*}y^{*}=x^{*}
.
∎

A densely defined operator
B
is
m
-dissipative if and only if
B
is closed, dissipative and
\mathop{\rm Id}\nolimits-tB^{*}
is injective for some (equivalently all)
t>0
. This is true since
\mathop{\rm Id}\nolimits-tB
has dense range if and only if
(\mathop{\rm Id}\nolimits-tB^{*})
is injective. We illustrate Theorem
2.6
by the following example.

Example 2.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be open and bounded,
X={\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Omega):=\{u\in{\mathcal{C}}(\overline{\Omega}):u_{|\partial\Omega}=0\}
. Let
Bu=\Delta u
,
\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B):=\{u\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Omega):\Delta u\in{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Omega)\}
. Then
B
is dissipative, closed and densely defined. Moreover
B
is
m
-dissipative if and only if
\Omega
is Wiener regular (see
[
4
]
). Note that
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}(\Omega)^{\prime}={\mathcal{M}}(\Omega):=\{\mu:\mu\mbox{ is a signed measure on }\Omega\}
with
\|\mu\|
the total variation of
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}(\Omega)
.

For
\mu,\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}(\Omega)
, one has

\mu\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B^{*})\mbox{ and }B^{*}\mu=\nu\Longleftrightarrow\int_{\Omega}\Delta ud\mu=\int_{\Omega}ud\nu,u\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B).

In particular,
\nu=\Delta\mu
in the sense of distributions.

Since
B
is dissipative, we see from Theorem
2.6
that for each
t>0
and each

\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}(\Omega)
, there exist
\mu\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B^{*})
such that

(2.4)

\mu-t\Delta\mu=\nu\mbox{ and }\|\mu\|\leq\|\nu\|.

Moreover,
\Omega
is Wiener regular if and only if the solution of (
2.4
) is unique for some (equivalently all)
t>0
.

3.
When Lions’ Representation Theorem meets derivations

We start introducing the notion of derivation acting on a Hilbert space. Our aim is to study perturbations of a coercive operator by a derivation, which leads to define Weak and Strong Derivation Problems.
In the remaining of the paper,
V
is a Hilbert space over
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
. By
V^{\prime}
we denote the antidual of
V
, that is the space of all continuous antilinear forms on
V
. In the case
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
,
V^{\prime}=V^{*}
where
V^{*}
is the dual space.

3.1.
Derivations

The following definition is central for the entire article.

Definition 3.1
.

A
derivation
(or
V
-derivation
if we want to specify the Hilbert space) is a linear mapping

{\mathcal{D}}_{0}:R\to V^{\prime},

whose domain
R
is a dense subspace of
V
satisfying

\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{0}u,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{0}v,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}=0,

for all
u,v\in R
.

Here, as previously, we denote by
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}
the duality between
V^{\prime}
and
V
, whereas
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{V}
is the scalar product in
V
.

Remark 3.2
.

In general we do not want to identify
V^{\prime}
and
V
. But if we do so, then
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is a derivation if and only if
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is
antisymmetric
, i.e.

\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{0}u,v\rangle_{V}=-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{0}v,u\rangle_{V}}\mbox{ for all }u,v\in R.

If
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{C}}
this is the same as saying that
i{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is symmetric.

In the remain of this entire section,
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is a derivation according to Definition
3.1
. We associate to
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
a new Hilbert space
W
in the following way.

(3.1)

W:=\{v\in V:\exists f\in V^{\prime}\mbox{ such that }\langle f,r\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}_{0}r,v\rangle}_{V^{\prime},V}=0\mbox{ for all }r\in R\}.

Since
R
is dense in
V
, given
v\in W
, there exists at most one
f\in V^{\prime}
satisfying the above requirement. We then denote
{\mathcal{D}}v:=f
.

The following is easy to see.

Lemma 3.3
.

a)

R\subset W
and
{\mathcal{D}}r={\mathcal{D}}_{0}r
for all
r\in R
.

b)

W
is a Hilbert space endowed with the scalar product

\langle v,w\rangle_{W}:=\langle v,w\rangle_{V}+\langle{\mathcal{D}}v,{\mathcal{D}}w\rangle_{V^{\prime}},

where
V^{\prime}
carries the scalar product coming from the Riesz isomorphism between
V
and
V^{\prime}
.

It follows from this definition that

(3.2)

\langle{\mathcal{D}}v,r\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}r,v\rangle}_{V^{\prime},V}=0\mbox{ for all }v\in W,r\in R.

Moreover we set

(3.3)

b(v,w):=\langle{\mathcal{D}}v,w\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}w,v\rangle}_{V^{\prime},V}\mbox{ for all }v,w\in W.

Then
b:W\times W\to{\mathbb{K}}
is sesquilinear and symmetric, i.e.

b(v,w)=\overline{b(w,v)}\mbox{ for all }v,w\in W.

We call
W
the
extended domain
of
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
,
{\mathcal{D}}
the extension of
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
, and
b
the
associated boundary form
.

If
R
is dense in
W
, then
b
is identically equal to
0
. But in general
b
is different from
0
and will be used to define boundary conditions for a Derivation Problem associated with
\mathcal{D}
.

Note that
b(w,w)\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
w\in W
.

Definition 3.4
.

For
E\subset W
its
orthogonal with respect to
b
is defined by

E^{b}:=\{u\in W:b(u,v)=0\,\,\forall v\in E\}.

3.2.
Admissible subspaces

Another notion of great importance in the sequel is the notion of
admissible space
and
strongly admissible space
.

Definition 3.5
.

A subspace
{Z}
of
W
is called
admissible
if

a)

R\subset{Z}
,

b)

b(w,w)\leq 0
for all
w\in{Z}
.

We say that
{Z}
is
strongly admissible
if, in addition,

c)

b(u,u)\geq 0
for all
u\in{Z}^{b}
.

Since
b:W\times W\to{\mathbb{K}}
is continuous, the closure of an admissible subspace
Z
is admissible, and, if
Z
is strongly admissible, then so is its closure
\overline{Z}
.

3.3.
The Weak Derivation Problem

Next we consider the
weak derivation problem
. Let
Z\subset W
be an admissible and closed subspace. We endow
Z
with the norm

\|v\|_{Z}^{2}:=\|v\|_{W}^{2}:=\|v\|_{V}^{2}+\|{\mathcal{D}}v\|^{2}_{V^{\prime}}.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{\prime})
be
coercive
, i.e. there exists
\alpha>0
such that

\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\geq\alpha\|u\|_{V}^{2}\mbox{ for all }u\in V.

Then the weak derivation problem is the following: given
L\in Z^{\prime}
find a vector
v
such that

(WDP)
v\in V
and
-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}v,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=\langle L,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z},\,\,\,z\in Z
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
Z\subset W
be a closed admissible subspace and let
L\in Z^{\prime}
. Then there exists a vector
u
such that (WDP) holds. If
Z
is strongly admissible, then the solution is unique.

We consider
{Z}
as a space of test functions. The solutions of

(WDP)
depend on the choice of this space. We will make this
dependance more transparent in Theorem
3.10
, Proposition
4.3
and Theorem
4.17
below.

For the proof we need some preparation.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
X
be a Banach space,
B
a closed operator on
X
and
A\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
. Assume that there exists
\alpha>0
such that

(3.4)

\|(A-B)x\|_{X}\geq\alpha\|x\|_{X}\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B).

Then there exists a constant
\beta>0
such that

(3.5)

\|(A-B)x\|_{X}\geq\beta(\|x\|_{X}+\|Bx\|_{X})\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B).

Proof.

By assumption the space
Z:=\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)
endowed with the norm
\|x\|_{Z}:=\|x\|_{X}+\|Bx\|_{X}
is complete. The mapping
T:=A-B:Z\to X
is continuous. In fact

\|Tz\|_{X}\leq\|A\|\|x\|_{X}+\|Bx\|_{X}\leq\max\{\|A\|,1\}(\|x\|_{X}+\|Bx\|_{X}).

Moreover,

(3.6)

\|Tx\|_{X}\geq\alpha\|x\|_{X}\mbox{ for all }x\in Z.

Thus
T
is injective and
Y:=TZ
is closed in
X
. To see this let
z_{n}\in Z
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|Tz_{n}-y\|_{X}=0
. It follows from (
3.6
) that

\alpha\|z_{n}-z_{m}\|_{X}\leq\|Tz_{n}-Tz_{m}\|_{X}.

Thus
x:=\lim_{n\to\infty}z_{n}
exists in
X
. Since
A\in{\mathcal{L}}(X)
, it follows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}Az_{n}=Ax
. Thus
\lim_{n\to\infty}Bz_{n}=Ax-y
. Since
B
is closed it follows that
x\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)=Z
and
Bx=Ax-y
. Hence
Tx=y
. We have proved that
Y
is closed in
X
and hence complete. By the Theorem of the Continuous Inverse,
T^{-1}\in{\mathcal{L}}(Y,Z)
. Thus

\displaystyle\|z\|_{X}+\|Bz\|_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|z\|_{Z}=\|T^{-1}Tz\|_{Z}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|T^{-1}\|\|Tz\|_{Z}=\|T^{-1}\|\|Az-Bz\|_{X}.

Choose
\beta=\frac{1}{\|T^{-1}\|}
.
∎

On a Hilbert space
V
we obtain the following more special version. Recall that an operator
B
on
V
is dissipative if and only if

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Bv,v\rangle_{V}\leq 0\mbox{ for all }v\in\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B).

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
B
be a densely defined dissipative operator on a Hilbert space
V
and let
A\in{\mathcal{L}}(V)
be
coercive
, i.e. there exists
\alpha>0
such that

(3.7)

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Av,v\rangle_{V}\geq\alpha\|v\|_{V}^{2}\mbox{ for all }v\in V.

Then there exists
\beta>0
such that

(3.8)

\|Av-Bv\|_{V}\geq\beta(\|v\|_{V}+\|Bv\|_{V})\mbox{ for all }v\in V.

Proof.

By
[
12
, II. Proposition 3.14 (iv)]
or
[
18
, I. Theorem 4.5 (c)]
,
B
is closable and the closure
\overline{B}
of
B
is dissipative again. Thus we can assume that
B
is closed. One has for
T=A-B:\mathop{\rm dom}\nolimits(B)\to V,

\alpha\|v\|_{V}^{2}\leq\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Av,v\rangle_{V}\leq\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Tv,v\rangle_{V}\leq\|Tv\|_{V}\|v\|_{V}.

Thus (
3.4
) is satisfied. Now the claim follows from Lemma
3.7
. ∎

Our short proof of Lemma
3.7
and Proposition
3.8
depends on the Theorem of the Continous Inverse. So we do not have a control of the constant
\beta
. For this reason we give a second (more involved) proof which shows that
\beta
can be chosen independently of
B
.

Second proof of Proposition
3.8
.

Consider
A_{+}:=\frac{A+A^{*}}{2}
and
A_{-}:=\frac{A-A^{*}}{2}
. Then

\langle A_{+}v,v\rangle_{V}=\frac{1}{2}\langle Av,v\rangle_{V}+\frac{1}{2}\langle A^{*}v,v\rangle_{V}=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Av,v\rangle_{V},\,\,v\in V,

and thus
\langle A_{+}v,v\rangle\geq\alpha\|v\|_{V}^{2},\,\,v\in V
. It follows that the selfadjoint operator
A_{+}
is positive and invertible. Let
S
be the (invertible and positive) square root of
A_{+}
.
Since
\|Sv\|_{V}^{2}=\langle A_{+}v,v\rangle_{V}
, we get

(3.9)

\|Sv\|_{V}\geq\sqrt{\alpha}\|v\|_{V},\,\,v\in V.

Since
\|Sv\|\leq\|A_{+}\|^{1/2}\|v\|
, we also get

(3.10)

\|S^{-1}v\|\geq\frac{1}{\|A_{+}\|^{1/2}}\|v\|,\,\,v\in V.

In addition, note that

\langle A_{-}v,v\rangle_{V}=\langle v,A^{*}_{-}v\rangle_{V}=-\langle v,A_{-}v\rangle_{V},\,\,v\in V,

and therefore

(3.11)

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle A_{-}v,v\rangle_{V}=0,\,\,v\in V.

Using (
3.9
) we first get, for all
v\in V
,

\displaystyle\|(A-B)v\|^{2}\

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha(\|-S^{-1}Bv+S^{-1}A_{+}v+S^{-1}A_{-}v\|_{V}^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(\|S^{-1}Bv\|_{V}^{2}+\|S^{-1}A_{+}v\|_{V}^{2}+\|S^{-1}A_{-}v\|_{V}^{2})

\displaystyle-2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}Bv,S^{-1}A_{+}v\rangle_{V}+2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}A_{+}v,S^{-1}A_{-}v\rangle_{V}

\displaystyle-2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}Bv,S^{-1}A_{-}v\rangle_{V}.

Note that

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}A_{+}v,S^{-1}A_{-}v\rangle_{V}=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-2}A_{+}v,A_{-}v\rangle_{V}=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle v,A_{-}v\rangle_{V}=0\mbox{ by }\eqref{eq:pf3}.

Moreover

-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}Bv,S^{-1}A_{+}v\rangle_{V}=-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Bv,S^{-2}A_{+}v\rangle_{V}=-\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle Bv,v\rangle_{V}\geq 0.

In addition, since
S^{-1}A_{+}=S
,

\|S^{-1}A_{+}v\|_{V}^{2}=\|Sv\|_{V}^{2}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\|(A-B)v\|^{2}\

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha(\|S^{-1}Bv\|_{V}^{2}+\|Sv\|_{V}^{2}+\|S^{-1}A_{-}v\|_{V}^{2})

\displaystyle-2\alpha\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}Bv,S^{-1}A_{-}v\rangle_{V}.

Now we use the celebrated Peter–Paul inequality combined with the Cauchy-Schwarz to estimate, for all
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle 2\left|\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle S^{-1}Bv,S^{-1}A_{-}v\rangle_{V}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|S^{-1}Bv\|_{V}\|S^{-1}A_{-}v\|_{V}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\gamma\|S^{-1}Bv\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\|S^{-1}A_{-}v\|_{V}^{2}.

We get then

\|(A-B)v\|^{2}\ \geq\alpha(1-\gamma)\|S^{-1}Bv\|_{V}^{2}+\alpha(1-1/\gamma)\|S^{-1}A_{-}v\|_{V}^{2}+\alpha\|Sv\|_{V}^{2}.

Let
\gamma=\frac{1}{1+s}
where
s>0
be be fixed later. Then
1-\gamma=\frac{s}{1+s}
and
1-1/\gamma=-s
. Then, by (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), it follows that

\|(A-B)v\|^{2}\geq\alpha\left(\frac{s}{1+s}\frac{1}{\|A_{+}\|}\|Bv\|_{V}^{2}+\alpha\|v\|_{V}^{2}-s\|S^{-1}A_{-}\|^{2}\|v\|_{V}^{2}\right).

Then we choose
s
such that
s\|S^{-1}A_{-}\|^{2}=\frac{\alpha}{2}
. Then we obtain

\|(A-B)v\|^{2}\geq\beta^{2}(\|Bv\|_{V}^{2}+\|v\|_{V}^{2}),

where

\beta^{2}=\min\left\{\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2},\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\alpha+2\|S^{-1}A_{-}\|^{2}}\frac{1}{\|A_{+}\|}\right\}
. In other words
\beta
does not depend on
B
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

By the Riesz Representation Theorem there exists an operator
A^{*}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V)
such that

\langle v,A^{*}w\rangle_{V}=\langle{\mathcal{A}}v,w\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\mbox{ for all }v,w\in V.

Similarly there exists
B:Z\to V
such that

\langle Bz,w\rangle_{V}=\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,w\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\mbox{ for all }z\in Z,w\in V.

The operator
B
is closed since
Z
is complete for the norm

\|u\|_{Z}=\left(\|z\|_{V}^{2}+\|{\mathcal{D}}z\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\|z\|_{V}^{2}+\|Bz\|_{V}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Since
Z
is admissible
B
is dissipative. The operator
A^{*}
is coercive. By Lemma
3.7
there exists
\beta>0
such that

\|A^{*}z-Bz\|_{V}\geq\beta(\|z\|_{V}+\|Bz\|_{V}).

Thus

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\sup_{\|v\|_{V}\leq 1}|\overline{\langle-{\mathcal{D}}z,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}v,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}|&=&\sup_{\|v\|_{V}\leq 1}|\langle-{\mathcal{D}}z,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\langle A^{*}z,v\rangle_{V}|\\
&=&\sup_{\|v\|_{V}\leq 1}|\langle-Bz+A^{*}z,v\rangle_{V}|\\
&=&\|-Bz+A^{*}z\|\\
&\geq&\beta(\|z\|_{V}+\|Bz\|_{V})=\beta\|z\|_{Z}.\end{array}

By RTL (Corollary
2.4
) there exists
v\in V
such that

\langle L,z\rangle=-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}v,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\mbox{ for all }z\in Z.

For the uniqueness, we now assume that
Z
is strongly admissible. Let
u\in V
be the difference of two solutions. Then

(3.12)

\overline{\langle-{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}+{\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}=0\mbox{ for all }z\in Z.

Taking
z\in R
we see that
u\in W
and

{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=0\mbox{ in }V^{\prime}.

In particular,

(3.13)

\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=0\mbox{ for all }z\in Z.

Substracting (
3.12
) from (
3.13
) we deduce that

b(u,z)=\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}=0\mbox{ for all }z\in Z.

Thus
u\in Z^{b}
. Since
Z
is strongly admissible, it follows that

2\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=b(u,u)\geq 0.

From (
3.13
) we deduce that

0=\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\geq\alpha\|u\|_{V}^{2}.

Thus
u=0
.
∎

3.4.
The Strong Derivation Problem

Next we consider the
strong derivation problem
. For that we have to assume that the antilinear form
L
on
Z
has a special form.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
Z
be a subspace of
W
endowed with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Z}=\|\cdot\|_{W}
such that
R\subset Z
, and let
L\in Z^{\prime}
. The following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

there exists a constant
c\geq 0
such that
|\langle L,r\rangle_{V}|\leq c\|r\|_{V}
for all
r\in R
;

(ii)

there exist
f\in V^{\prime}
and
g\in Z^{\prime}
such that
\langle g,r\rangle_{V}=0
for all
r\in R
and
L=f+g
.

Proof.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
This is obvious.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
Since
R
is dense in
V
, there exists
f\in V^{\prime}
such that
\langle L,r\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=\langle f,r\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}
for all
r\in R
. Let
g:=L-f
. Then
g\in Z^{\prime}
and
g(r)=0
for all
r\in R
.
∎

Let
Z\subset W
be an admissible subspace and let
L\in Z^{\prime}
be of the form
L=f+g
where
f\in V^{\prime}
and
g\in Z^{\prime}
such that
g(r)=0
for all
r\in R
. The
Strong Derivation Problem
consists in finding a vector
u
such that

\mbox{ (SDP) }u\in W,\,\,{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=f\mbox{ and }b(u,z)=-\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z},\,\,z\in Z.

Thus, the functional
g
expresses a kind of boundary condition. In the examples (Section
5
), this will become even more transparent.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
Z\subset W
be an admissible subspace. Let
L=f+g
with
f\in V^{\prime}
,
g\in Z^{\prime}
such that
\langle g,r\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z}=0
for all
r\in R
. For
u\in V
the following are equivalent:

(i)

u
is a solution of (WDP);

(ii)

u\in W
and
u
is a solution of (SDP).

Consequently, there exists a solution of (SDP). Moreover it is unique if
Z
is strongly admissible.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
Let
u
be a solution of (WDP). Then

(3.14)

-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=\langle f,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z},\,\,z\in Z.

Taking
z\in R
we deduce that
u\in W
and

(3.15)

{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=f\mbox{ in }V^{\prime}.

Evaluating at
z\in Z\subset V
, we obtain

\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=\langle f,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},\,\,z\in Z.

Substracting (
3.14
) from (
3.15
) yields

(3.16)

b(u,z)=\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}=-\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z},\,\,z\in Z.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
Let
u\in W
be a solution of (SDP), i.e. (
3.15
) and (
3.16
) hold. Evaluating (
3.15
) at
z\in Z
and using (
3.16
) we obtain

\displaystyle-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}+\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}-\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\langle f,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-b(u,z)+\langle f,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z}+\langle f,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle L,z\rangle_{V^{\prime},V},\,\,z\in Z.

Thus
u
is a solution of (WDP).
∎

Remark 3.11
.

Letting
g=0
, Theorem
3.10
proves that the operator
{\mathcal{D}}+{\mathcal{A}}~:~Z^{b}\to V^{\prime}
is invertible, i.e. it is injective with a closed and dense range. This means that there exists
\beta^{\prime}>0
such that, for all
u\in Z^{b}
,

(3.17)

\|{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u\|_{V^{\prime}}\geq\beta^{\prime}\left(\|u\|_{V}^{2}+\|{\mathcal{D}}u\|_{V^{\prime}}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

and that, for any
w\in V
,

(3.18)

\langle{\mathcal{D}}v+{\mathcal{A}}v,w\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=0,\,\,\,v\in Z^{b}\Rightarrow w=0.

Indeed (
3.17
) is equivalent to
{\mathcal{D}}+{\mathcal{A}}
is injective with closed range, whereas (
3.18
) is equivalent to
{\mathcal{D}}+{\mathcal{A}}
has dense range using the Hahn–Banach theorem combined with the reflexivity of our spaces.

Note that the existence of
\beta^{\prime}
follows directly from Lemma
3.7
. Indeed, for all
u\in Z^{b}
,
b(u,u)\geq 0
, and the second property follows along the same lines as the proof of
(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
above (first prove that
w\in W
, then that
w\in Z^{bb}=Z
, and finally that
\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits\langle{\mathcal{A}}^{\star}w,w\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}\leq 0
).

4.
Admissible subspaces and boundary operators

If we consider
\mathcal{D}
as a time derivative, then (SDP) may be interpreted as an evolution equation with a
boundary condition with respect to time
.

This can be made more concrete when we make further assumptions on the associated boundary form
b
and, of course, in the examples we give below.

4.1.
Maximal admissible subspaces

We start considering maximal admissible subspaces. This maximality is a handy criterion for proving strong admissibility.

Definition 4.1
.

A subspace
{Z}_{0}
of
W
is called
maximal admissible
if
{Z}_{0}
is admissible and if for any admissible subspace
{Z}_{1}
of
W
,
{Z}_{0}\subset{Z}_{1}
implies
{Z}_{0}={Z}_{1}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
{Z}_{0}
be an admissible subspace of
W
. Then there exists a maximal admissible subspace
{Z}_{1}
of
W
containing
{Z}_{0}
. Moreover, each maximal admissible subspace of
W
is strongly admissible.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{M}}:=\{{Z}_{1}\subset W:{Z}_{0}\subset{Z}_{1},{Z}_{1}\mbox{ admissible}\}
ordered by inclusion. If
({Z}_{i})_{i\in I}
is a chain in
{\mathcal{M}}
, then
{Z}:=\cup_{i\in I}{Z}_{i}
is a subspace of
W
containing
{Z}_{0}
. If
w\in{Z}
, then there exists
i\in I
such that
w\in{Z}_{i}
. Consequently
b(w,w)\leq 0
. Thus
{Z}
is admissible and hence an upper bound for the chain
({Z}_{i})_{i\in I}
. Now by Zorn’s Lemma
{\mathcal{M}}
has a maximal element.

Let
{Z}_{1}
be a maximal admissible subspace and let
u\in W
such that
b(u,w)=0
for all
w\in{Z}_{1}
. Suppose that
b(u,u)<0
. Then
u\not\in{Z}_{1}
. Thus
{Z}_{2}:={Z}_{1}\oplus{\mathbb{K}}u
is a subspace of
W
and for
w=w_{1}+\lambda u\in{Z}_{2}
,

b(w,w)=b(w_{1},w_{1})+|\lambda|^{2}b(u,u)\leq 0.

This contradicts the maximality of
{Z}_{1}
. We have proved that

b(u,u)\geq 0
for all
u\in{Z}_{1}^{b}
; i.e.
Z_{1}
is strongly admissible. ∎

Let
{Z}\subset W
be a strongly admissible subspace and
L\in{Z}^{\prime}
, where
{Z}
carries the norm of
W
. Then, by Theorem
3.6
, (WDP) has a unique solution
u\in V
. Let
\widetilde{{Z}}\subset W
be a maximal admissible subspace such that
{Z}\subset\widetilde{{Z}}
. By the Hahn-Banach Theorem there exists
\widetilde{L}\in\widetilde{{Z}}^{\prime}
such that
\widetilde{L}_{|{Z}}=L
. Since
\widetilde{{Z}}
is also strongly admissible, there exists a unique
\widetilde{u}\in V
solution for (WDP) with respect to
\widetilde{{Z}}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

One has
u=\widetilde{u}
.

Proof.

One has

\langle-{\mathcal{D}}z,u\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{A}}u,z\rangle=\langle L,z\rangle_{{Z}^{\prime},{Z}}\mbox{ for all }z\in{Z}

and

\langle-{\mathcal{D}}z,\widetilde{u}\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{A}}\widetilde{u},z\rangle=\langle L,z\rangle_{\widetilde{{Z}}^{\prime},\widetilde{{Z}}}\mbox{ for all }z\in\widetilde{{Z}}.

In particular

\langle-{\mathcal{D}}z,u-\widetilde{u}\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{A}}(u-\widetilde{u}),z\rangle=0\mbox{ for all }z\in{Z}.

Now it follows from Theorem
3.6
applied to
L=0
that
u-\widetilde{u}=0
.
∎

In view of the preceding abservations, our aim is now to study the maximal admissible subspaces in the next subsection.

4.2.
Boundary operators

In this section we continue to study the abstact framework from Section
3
. But we give more structure to the associated boundary form
b
.

Let
V,R,{\mathcal{D}},b
be given as in Section
3
and let
W
be defined by (
3.1
) and
b:W\times W\to{\mathbb{K}}
by (
3.3
). Recall that
b
is a continuous, symmetric sesquilinear form. Now we make the following assumption.

Assumption 4.4
.

There are given a Hilbert space
H
and operators

B_{0},B_{1}\in{\mathcal{L}}(W,H)
such that

(4.1)

b(v,w)=\langle B_{1}v,B_{1}w\rangle_{H}-\langle B_{0}v,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}\mbox{ for all }v,w\in W.

and

(4.2)

\ker B_{0}+\ker B_{1}=W.

Remark 4.5
.

Such operators
B_{0},B_{1}
and such a Hilbert space
H
do always exist. For example, we may choose
H=W
. There is a unique selfadjoint operator
B\in{\mathcal{L}}(W)
such that

b(v,w)=\langle Bv,w\rangle_{W}.

Then
B
has a canonical decomposition
B=B_{+}-B_{-}
where
B_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(B+|B|)
,
B_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(|B|-B)
are positive selfadjoint operators, satisfying
B_{+}B_{-}=B_{-}B_{+}=0
. One may choose
B_{0}=\sqrt{B_{+}}
,
B_{1}=\sqrt{B_{-}}
, which still satisfy
B_{1}B_{0}=B_{0}B_{1}=0
and therefore the image of one of them is included in the kernel of the other (this follows from the Spectral Theorem in
[
19
, Chapter VII]
). Since

Other choices of

By

Lemma 4.6
.

a)

\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}^{*}}\cap\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}^{*}}=\{0\}
.

b)

R\subset\ker B_{1}\cap\ker B_{0}
.

Proof.

a) Since by our assumption
W=\ker B_{0}+\ker B_{1}
, it follows that

\{0\}=(\ker B_{0}+\ker B_{1})^{\perp}=(\ker B_{0})^{\perp}\cap(\ker B_{1})^{\perp}=\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}^{*}}\cap\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}^{*}}.

b) Let
r\in R
. Then
b(r,w)=0
for all
w\in W
. Thus

0=\langle B_{1}r,B_{1}w\rangle_{H}-\langle B_{0}r,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}=\langle B_{1}^{*}B_{1}r-B_{0}^{*}B_{0}r,w\rangle_{W}

for all

. Hence
B_{1}^{*}B_{1}r=B_{0}^{*}B_{0}r
. It follows from a) that

B_{1}^{*}B_{1}r=B_{0}^{*}B_{0}r=0.

Hence
\|B_{1}r\|_{H}^{2}=\langle B_{1}^{*}B_{1}r,r\rangle_{W}=0
and so
B_{1}r=0
. Similarly, we obtain that
B_{0}r=0
.
∎

Now we can describe a first maximal admissible subspace.

Proposition 4.7
.

\ker B_{1}
is a maximal admissible subspace.

Proof.

a)
R\subset\ker B_{1}
by Lemma
4.6
.

b) Let
w\in\ker B_{1}
. Then

b(w,w)=-\|B_{0}w\|_{H}^{2}\leq 0.

c) Let
{Z}
be admissible and such that
\ker B_{1}\subset{Z}
. Let
w\in{Z}
. By our assumptions there exists a decomposition

Since
\ker B_{1}\subset{Z}
it follows that
w_{0}\in{Z}
. Thus

0\geq b(w_{0},w_{0})=\|B_{1}w_{0}\|_{H}^{2}-\|B_{0}w_{0}\|_{H}^{2}=\|B_{1}w_{0}\|_{H}^{2},

hence
w_{0}\in\ker B_{1}
. It follows that
w=w_{0}+w_{1}\in\ker B_{1}
. ∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let

,
x_{1}\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}

such that

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle w_{10}+w_{11}\in\ker B_{0}+\ker B_{1},

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=

Let

and
B_{1}w=B_{1}w_{20}=B_{1}w_{2}=x_{1}
.
∎

The following observation will be useful in the next proof .

Remark 4.9
.

The operator
-{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is also a derivation and its extended domain is
W
. The boundary form associated with
-{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
is
-b
and the extended operator
-{\mathcal{D}}
. In particular

-\langle{\mathcal{D}}u,v\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}-\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}v,u\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}=-b(u,v)=\langle B_{0}u,B_{0}v\rangle_{H}-\langle B_{1}u,B_{1}v\rangle_{H},

for all

We now show that

and

have closed images. This is surprising and due to the special construction of

. For the proof we will use the well-posedness of the Derivation problem with

Proposition 4.10
.

\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
and
\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
are closed subspaces of
H
.

Proof.

a) Let
x_{0}\in\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}}\subset H
. Choose
{Z}:=\ker B_{1}
. Then
{Z}
is strongly admissible and
g(w)=\langle x_{0},B_{0}w\rangle_{H}
defines a continuous antilinear form on
{Z}
, which vanishes on
R
.

Consider
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{\prime})
given by
\langle{\mathcal{A}}v,w\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}=\langle v,w\rangle_{V}
. By Theorem
3.10
there exists a unique
u\in W
such that

{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=0
and

b(u,w)=-g(w)\mbox{ for all }w\in\ker B_{1}.

Thus

-\langle B_{0}u,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}=-\langle x_{0},B_{0}w\rangle_{H}\mbox{ for all }w\in\ker B_{1}.

Since
\ker B_{1}+\ker B_{0}=W
, one has
B_{0}\ker B_{1}=B_{0}W
. It follows that

\langle B_{0}u-x_{0},B_{0}w\rangle_{H}=0\mbox{ for all }w\in W.

Consequently,
\langle B_{0}u-x_{0},y\rangle_{H}=0
for all
y\in\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}}
. Since
B_{0}u-x_{0}\in\overline{\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}}
, it follows that
B_{0}u-x_{0}=0
. Thus
x_{0}\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
.

b) Replacing
{\mathcal{D}}
by
-{\mathcal{D}}
and applying a), one obtains that
\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
is
closed.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
{Z}\subset W
be admissible. Then there exists a linear mapping
\Phi:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
such that

\|\Phi x\|_{H}\leq\|x\|_{H}\mbox{ for all }x\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}

and

{Z}\subset{Z}_{\Phi}:=\{w\in W:B_{1}w={\Phi}B_{0}w\}.

Proof.

Since
{Z}
is admissible,

\|B_{1}w\|_{H}\leq\|B_{0}w\|_{H}\mbox{ for all }w\in{Z}.

Thus, letting

{\Phi}_{0}B_{0}w:=B_{1}w\mbox{ for all }w\in{Z}

yields a well-defined mapping
{\Phi}_{0}:B_{0}{Z}\to B_{1}{Z}
. Then
{\Phi}_{0}
is linear and contractive. Hence
{\Phi}_{0}
has a unique continuous extension
{\Phi}_{1}:\overline{B_{0}{Z}}\to\overline{B_{1}{Z}}
and
\|{\Phi}_{1}x\|_{H}\leq\|x\|_{H}
for all
x\in\overline{B_{0}{Z}}
. Since
\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
is closed, one has

\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}=\overline{B_{0}{Z}}\oplus(B_{0}{Z})^{\perp}.

Let
x=x_{0}+x_{1}\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
with
x_{0}\in\overline{B_{0}{Z}}
,
x_{1}\in(B_{0}{Z})^{\perp}
. Defining
{\Phi}x:={\Phi}_{1}x_{0}
yields a linear contraction
{\Phi}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
satisfying the requirements of the lemma. ∎

Now we can describe all maximal admissible subspaces of
W
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Le
{\Phi}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
linear and contractive (i.e.
\|{\Phi}x\|_{H}\leq\|x\|_{H}
for all
x\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
). Then

{Z}_{\Phi}:=\{w\in W:B_{1}w={\Phi}B_{0}w\}

is maximal admissible. Moreover, each maximal admissible subspace of
W
is of the form
{Z}_{\Phi}
for some linear contraction
{\Phi}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
.

Proof.

a) Since
B_{1}r=B_{0}r=0
for all
r\in R
by Lemma
4.6
, one has
R\subset{Z}_{\Phi}
.

b) Let
w\in{Z}_{\Phi}
. Then

b(w,w)=\|B_{1}w\|^{2}_{H}-\|B_{0}w\|^{2}_{H}=\|{\Phi}B_{0}w\|^{2}_{H}-\|B_{0}w\|^{2}_{H}\leq 0.

c) Let
{Z}
be admissible and such that
{Z}_{\Phi}\subset{Z}
. We show that
{Z}_{\Phi}={Z}
. In fact, by Lemma
4.11
, there exists a contraction
{\Phi}_{1}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
such that
{Z}\subset{Z}_{{\Phi}_{1}}
. Hence
{Z}_{\Phi}\subset{Z}_{{\Phi}_{1}}
. Thus

(4.3)

{\Phi}B_{0}w={\Phi}_{1}B_{0}w\mbox{ for all }w\in{Z}_{\Phi}.

But

(4.4)

B_{0}{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}.

In fact, let
w\in W
. By Lemma
4.8
there exists
u\in W
such that
B_{0}u=B_{0}w
and
B_{1}u={\Phi}B_{0}w
. Thus
B_{1}u={\Phi}B_{0}u
; i.e.
u\in{Z}_{\Phi}
and
B_{0}w=B_{0}u
. Thus
B_{0}w\in B_{0}{Z}_{\Phi}
.
Now (
4.3
) implies that
{\Phi}={\Phi}_{1}
. hence
{Z}_{\Phi}\subset{Z}\subset{Z}_{{\Phi}_{1}}={Z}_{\Phi}
.

d) The fact that each maximal subspace of
W
is of the form
{Z}_{\Phi}
for some linear contraction
{\Phi}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
follows from Lemma
4.11
. ∎

Next we compute the initial value condition.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
\Phi:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
be a contraction. Then

(4.5)

{Z}_{\Phi}^{b}={Z}_{{\Phi}^{*}}:=\{w\in W:B_{0}w={\Phi}^{*}B_{1}w\}.

Proof.

Let
u\in{Z}_{\Phi}^{b}
. Then
\langle B_{1}u,B_{1}w\rangle_{H}=\langle B_{0}u,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}
for all

. Since
B_{1}w={\Phi}B_{0}w
for

it follows that

(4.6)

\langle{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u-B_{0}u,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}=0\mbox{ for all }w\in{Z}_{\Phi}.

Since by (
4.4
)
B_{0}{Z}_{\Phi}=\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
and since
{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u-B_{0}u\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
it follows from (
4.6

{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u-B_{0}u=0
, i.e.
B_{0}u={\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u
.

Conversely, if
B_{0}u={\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u
, then, for all
w\in{Z}_{\Phi}
,

\displaystyle b(u,w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle B_{1}u,B_{1}w\rangle_{H}-\langle B_{0}u,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle B_{1}u,{\Phi}B_{0}w\rangle_{H}-\langle{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u,B_{0}w\rangle_{H}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0.

This proves (
4.5
).
∎

Proposition
4.13
also shows that
{Z}_{\Phi}
is strongly admissible (without using

4.2

Lemma 4.14
.

If
Z
is maximal admissible, then
Z^{bb}=Z
.

Proof.

By Theorem

, any maximal admissible subspace of

is of the form

4.3.
Boundary conditions

Next we compare maximal and strongly admissible subspaces. We already know that maximal admissible subspaces are strongly admissible. Recall from Theorem
4.12
that the maximal admissible subspaces of
W
are exactly the spaces
{Z}_{\Phi}
where
\Phi:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
is a contraction.

Proposition 4.15
.

Let
{Z}\subset W
be strongly admissible. Then there exists exactly one maximal admissible subspace
{Z}_{\Phi}
such that
{Z}\subset{Z}_{\Phi}
. Moreover,

(4.7)

{Z}^{b}={Z}_{\Phi^{\star}}.

Proof.

Let
\Phi:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
be contraction such that
{Z}\subset{Z}_{\Phi}
. Then

{Z}_{\Phi^{*}}={Z}_{\Phi}^{b}\subset{Z}^{b},

where
{Z}_{\Phi^{*}}=\{w\in W:B_{0}w=\Phi^{*}B_{1}w\}
. Note also that
-{\mathcal{D}}
is a derivation with extended space
W
and associated with
-b
. Thus, by Theorem
4.12
,
{Z}_{\Phi^{*}}
is a maximal admissible subspace for
-b
. Since
{Z}
is strongly admissible, the space
{Z}^{b}
is admissible for
-{\mathcal{D}}
. Since
{Z}_{\phi^{*}}\subset{Z}^{b}
, it follows that
{Z}_{\Phi^{*}}={Z}^{b}
. We have shown the second assertion.

Newt we show uniqueness. Let
\Psi:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
be another contraction such that
{Z}\subset{Z}_{\Psi}
. Then

{Z}_{\Psi^{*}}={Z}^{b}={Z}_{\Phi^{*}}

by what we have just proved. We show that
\Psi^{*}=\Phi^{*}
(which implies that
\Psi=\Phi
). Let
w_{1}\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
,
w_{0}=\Phi^{*}w_{1}\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
. By Lemma
4.8
, there exists
w\in W
such that
w_{1}=B_{1}w
and
w_{0}=B_{0}w
. Thus

\Phi^{*}B_{1}w=\Phi^{*}w_{1}=w_{0}=B_{0}w.

Hence
w\in{Z}_{\Phi^{*}}={Z}_{\Psi^{*}}
. Consequently,

\Psi^{*}w_{1}=\Psi^{*}B_{1}w=B_{0}w=w_{0}=\Phi^{*}w_{1}.

∎

An obvious corollary is the following.

Corollary 4.16
.

Let
\Phi,\Psi\in{\mathcal{L}}(H)
be contractions. If
{Z}_{\Phi}={Z}_{\Psi}
then
\Phi=\Psi
.

Thus, in view of Proposition
4.3
, the solutions of (WDP) with respect to
{Z}
and to
{Z}_{\Phi}
are the same.

Now in view of Theorem
4.12
we obtain from Theorem
3.6
a theorem on existence and uniqueness of the solution to a Derivation Problem.

Let
{\Phi}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
be linear such that
\|{\Phi}\|\leq 1
. Consider the strongly admissible space

{Z}_{\Phi}=\{w\in W:B_{1}w={\Phi}B_{0}w\}.

Theorem 4.17
.

Let
{\Phi}:\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}\to\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{1}
be linear and contractive for the norm of
H
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{\prime})
be coercive,
f\in V^{\prime}
and let
y_{0}\in\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
. Then there exists a unique
u\in W
such that

{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=f\mbox{ and }B_{0}u-{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u=y_{0}.

Proof.

Let
Z={Z}_{\Phi}
and let us define
g\in Z^{\prime}
by
\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z}=\langle y_{0},B_{0}z\rangle_{H}
. Then
g
is such that
\langle g,r\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z}=0
for all
r\in R
. By Theorem
3.10
, there exists
u
such that (SDP) holds, that is

u\in W,\,\,{\mathcal{D}}u+{\mathcal{A}}u=f\mbox{ and }b(u,z)=-\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z},\,\,z\in Z.

Since
\langle B_{1}u,B_{1}z\rangle_{H}=\langle B_{1}u,\Phi B_{0}z\rangle_{H}=\langle{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u,B_{0}z\rangle_{H}
, it follows that

-b(u,z)-\langle g,z\rangle_{Z^{\prime},Z}=\langle B_{0}u-{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u-y_{0},B_{0}z\rangle_{H}=0,\,\,z\in{Z}_{\Phi}.

This yields
B_{0}u-{\Phi}^{*}B_{1}u-y_{0}=0
, since, by Lemma
4.8
,
\{B_{0}z,z\in{Z}_{\Phi}\}=\mathop{\rm Ran}\nolimits B_{0}
.

Theorem
3.10
also shows the uniqueness of

since
{Z}_{\Phi}
is strongly admissible.

∎

5.
Non-autonomous evolution equations

In this section we establish existence and uniqueness for an evolutionary problem with very general boundary conditions concerning the time variable which can prescribe as well an initial value problem or a periodicity condition.

Let
T>0
,
{U}
a Hilbert space over
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}}
or
{\mathbb{C}}
which is continuous and densely embedded into another Hilbert space
H
, i.e.
{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\hookrightarrow}}H
. As usual we identify
H
with a subspace of
{U}^{\prime}
which yields the Gelfand triple

{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\hookrightarrow}}H\hookrightarrow{U}^{\prime},

where for
u\in{U}
and
f\in H
,
\langle f,u\rangle_{{U}^{\prime},{U}}=\langle f,u\rangle_{H}
.

Let
V=L^{2}(0,T;{U})
. Thus
V^{\prime}=L^{2}(0,T;{U}^{\prime})
. Let
R={\mathcal{C}}_{c}^{\infty}(0,T;{U})
and define
{\mathcal{D}}:R\to V^{\prime}
by
{\mathcal{D}}r=r^{\prime}
. Then for

r_{1},r_{2}\in R
,

\langle{\mathcal{D}}r_{1},r_{2}\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}+\overline{\langle{\mathcal{D}}r_{2},r_{1}\rangle_{V^{\prime},V}}=\int_{0}^{T}(\langle r^{\prime}_{1}(t),r_{2}(t)\rangle_{H}+\overline{\langle r^{\prime}_{2}(t),r_{1}(t)\rangle_{H}})dt=0.

It is not difficult to see that
W
as defined in Section
3
is given by

W=H^{1}(0,T;{U}^{\prime})\cap L^{2}(0,T;{U}).

The following integration by parts formula (
[
20
, III Corollary 1.1 p.106]
) plays an important role.

Lemma 5.1
.

One has
W\subset{\mathcal{C}}([0,T],H)
and for all
v,w\in W,

\int_{0}^{T}\langle v^{\prime}(t),w(t)\rangle dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\langle w^{\prime}(t),v(t)\rangle dt+\langle v(T),w(T)\rangle_{H}-\langle v(0),w(0)\rangle_{H}.

Note that here
\langle{v^{\prime}}(t),w(t)\rangle:=\langle{v^{\prime}}(t),w(t)\rangle_{{U}^{\prime},{U}}
.

Thus, in this situation, the associated boundary form
b:W\times W\to{\mathbb{K}}
is given by

b(v,w)=\langle v(T),w(T)\rangle_{H}-\langle v(0),w(0)\rangle_{H}.

Thus it is possible to define
B_{1},B_{0}:W\to H
by
B_{0}w=w(0)
and
B_{1}w=w(T)
. Indeed, we have that for all

u\in W,u(t)=\frac{t}{T}u(t)+\frac{T-t}{T}u(t)
with
(t\mapsto\frac{t}{T}u(t))\in\ker B_{0}
and

(t\mapsto\frac{T-t}{T}u(t))\in\ker B_{1}
. So we have
W=\ker B_{0}+\ker B_{1}
where
B_{0}
and
B_{1}
are selfadjoint.

Now let
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{\prime})
be coercive.

Example 5.2
.

Let
a:[0,T]\times{U}\times{U}\to{\mathbb{K}}
be a function such that

a)

a(t,\cdot,\cdot):{U}\times{U}\to{\mathbb{K}}
is sesquilinear;

b)

|a(t,v,w)|\leq c\|v\|_{{U}}\|w\|_{{U}}
for all
t\in[0,T]
and for all
v,w\in{U}
;

c)

a(\cdot,v,w):[0,T]\to{\mathbb{K}}
is measurable for all
v,w\in{U}
;

d)

\mathop{\rm Re}\nolimits a(t,v,v)\geq\alpha\|v\|_{{U}}^{2}
for all
t\in[0,T]
,
v\in{U}
and some
\alpha>0
.

For
v,w\in V=L^{2}(0,T;{U})
, let

\langle{\mathcal{A}}v,w\rangle=\int_{0}^{T}a(t,u(t),v(t))dt.

Then
{\mathcal{A}}\in{\mathcal{L}}(V,V^{\prime})
is coercive.

In the following it is important to know the trace space of
W
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
x\in H
there exists
u\in W
such that
u(0)=x
.

Proof.

One has
H=[V^{\prime},V]_{1/2}
, the complex interpolation space, which coincides with the real interpolation space
[V^{\prime},V]_{1/2,1/2}
. The latter coincides with the trace space by
[
15
, Proposition 1.2.10]
. ∎

Now we obtain from Theorem
4.17
the following result on existence and uniqueness.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
{\Phi}\in{\mathcal{L}}(H)
be a contraction and let
y_{0}\in H
. Let
f\in L^{2}(0,T;{U}^{\prime})
. Then there exists a unique
u\in H^{1}(0,T;{U}^{\prime})\cap L^{2}(0,T;{U})
such that

(5.1)

u^{\prime}+{\mathcal{A}}u=f

and

(5.2)

u(0)-{\Phi}^{*}u(T)=y_{0}.

For
\Phi=0
, Theorem
5.4
establishes well-posedness for an initial-value problem. It is due to J. L. Lions
[
14
]
, see also
[
20
, III Proposition 2.3 p.112]
or
[
10
, XVIII.3 Théorème 2]
.

If
\Phi=\mathop{\rm Id}\nolimits
and
y_{0}=0
we obtain a periodic problem. If
\mathcal{A}
is selfadjoint then Theorem
5.4
is given in
[
20
, II. Proposition 2.4]
where selfadjointness is used in the proof on page 112. The general case given here is new and depends on our perturbation result Proposition
3.8
.
