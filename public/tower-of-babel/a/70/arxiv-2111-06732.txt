Dissecting axion and dark photon with a network of vector sensors
Yifan Chen a, Min Jiang b,c,d, Jing Shu a,e,f,g, Xiao Xue h, Yanjie Zeng a,e

Abstract
We develop formalisms for a network of vector sensors, sensitive to certain spatial components of the signals, to identify the properties of a light axion or a dark photon background. These bosonic fields contribute to vector-like signals in the detectors, including effective magnetic fields triggering the spin precession, effective electric currents in a shielded room, and forces on the matter. The interplay between a pair of vector sensors and a baseline that separates them can potentially uncover rich information of the bosons, including angular distribution, polarization modes, source localization, and macroscopic circular polarization. Using such a network, one can identify the microscopic nature of a potential signal, such as distinguishing between the axion-fermion coupling and the dipole couplings with the dark photon.

II
Vector Sensor Response to Axion Gradient and Dark Photon

II.1
Non-relativistic Dipole Coupling

We first consider the dipole couplings between light bosons to the spins of fermions.
In the non-relativistic limit of the fermions, these dipole couplings can be expressed universally as an effective vector-like coupling with the fermion’s spin operator
\displaystyle\vec{\sigma}
,

H=\alpha\,\vec{\mathcal{O}}\cdot\vec{\sigma}.

(3)

where
\displaystyle\alpha
is the coupling constant and
\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}
is the vector-like signals probed.

Up to dimension-five operators are considered, which become the form of Eq. (
3
) in the non-relativistic limit of the fermions, including axion-fermion coupling,

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{a}\partial_{\mu}a\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rightarrow\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{a}=\vec{\nabla}a,

(4)

and dipole interactions between dark photon and the fermions

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{A}V_{\mu}\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rightarrow\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{A}=\vec{V},

(5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\textrm{MDM}}V_{\mu\nu}\bar{\psi}\sigma^{\mu\nu}\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rightarrow\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{\textrm{MDM}}=\vec{\nabla}\times\vec{V},

(6)

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{\textrm{EDM}}V_{\mu\nu}\bar{\psi}\sigma^{\mu\nu}i\gamma^{5}\psi

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rightarrow\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{\textrm{EDM}}=\partial_{0}\vec{V}-\vec{\nabla}V^{0},

(7)

where
\displaystyle g_{a}
,
\displaystyle g_{A}
,
\displaystyle g_{\textrm{EDM}}
, and
\displaystyle g_{\textrm{MDM}}
are the coupling constants for the axion, axial-vector, electric dipole momentum (EDM) and magnetic dipole momentum (MDM) dark photon respectively.
\displaystyle a
is the axion field, and
\displaystyle V_{\mu}
and
\displaystyle V_{\mu\nu}
are the vector fields and their field strength, respectively.
\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu\nu}
is the anti-symmetric tensor constructed from Dirac matrix
\displaystyle\gamma^{\mu}
.
Notice that in the case of the axion gradient coupling (
4
),
\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}
behaves as a longitudinal mode in terms of the spatial momentum. In contrast, only transverse modes of the MDM dark photon contribute to the interaction in Eq. (
6
).
For the axial vector, all the polarization modes take part in the interaction in Eq. (
5
).
Interactions with the EDM dark photon differ between the relativistic and the non-relativistic limits of the dark photon in Eq. (
7
). In the former case, only the transverse modes interact, while all contribute to the interaction when being non-relativistic.

Operators with dimension higher than 5 always become one of the forms above in the non-relativistic limit. For example, an anapole dark photon, with interaction
\displaystyle g_{\textrm{Ana}}(\partial^{\nu}V_{\mu\nu})\bar{\psi}\gamma^{\mu}\gamma^{5}\psi
, takes the same form as the axial-vector in Eq. (
5
) when the dark photon is on-shell.

For more general cases of the dark photon, one can also use Eq. (
3
) to parametrize the detector response for a certain spatial direction. For example, the
\displaystyle U(1)_{B-L}/U(1)_{B}
dark photon leads to an additional force that accelerometers, optomechanical systems, or astrometry can sense
Graham et al. 2016
;
Pierce et al. 2018
;
Carney et al. 2021
;
Guo et al. 2019a
. The kinetic-mixing dark photon induces an effective current which can be captured by LC circuits in a shielded room
Chaudhuri et al. 2015
or read from the geomagnetic field
Fedderke et al. 2021a
;
Fedderke et al. 2021b
. In the former case, the force signal shares the same form as the one of the EDM in Eq. (
7
), while the effective current is proportional to the spatial component of the dark photon wave functions.

II.2
Vector-like Signals from Axion and Dark Photon Background

Bosonic fields can exist in the universe with different kinds of momentum distribution. For example, axion or dark photon with mass
\displaystyle m<\mathcal{O}(1)
eV can be cold dark matter candidates
Preskill et al. 1983
;
Abbott and Sikivie 1983
;
Dine and Fischler 1983
;
Nelson and Scholtz 2011

Baumann et al. 2016
;
Dror et al. 2021b
and a dark photon can also exist with the spectrum dependent on the production mechanism and the universe’s thermal history. Furthermore, one expects bosonic waves to come from a specific direction towards the Earth, such as non-virialized substructure of the dark matter

;
Foster et al. 2018
;
Knirck et al. 2018
, dipole radiations from binary systems charged by hidden
\displaystyle U(1)

Krause et al. 1994
;
Dror et al. 2021a
;
Hou et al. 2022
, and emissions from strongly self-interacting gravitational atoms
Baryakhtar et al. 2021
;
East 2022

Following
Foster et al. 2018
;
Derevianko 2018
;
Foster et al. 2021
;
Guo et al. 2019b
and the relativistic generalization in
Dror et al. 2021b
, one expands general scalar or vector fields with
\displaystyle N_{b}
non-interacting waves

\displaystyle\displaystyle a(t,\vec{x}\,)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{N_{b}\bar{\omega}\omega_{j}}}\cos\left[\omega_{j}t-\vec{p}_{j}\cdot\vec{x}+\alpha_{j}\right],

(8)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{V}(t,\vec{x}\,)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{N_{b}\bar{\omega}\omega_{j}}}\text{Re}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\left(\omega_{j}t-\vec{p}_{j}\cdot\vec{x}+\alpha_{j}\right)}\ \vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,j}\right],

(9)

where momentum
\displaystyle\vec{p}_{j}
are random variables drawn from the momentum distribution
\displaystyle f(\vec{p}\,)
,
\displaystyle\omega_{j}=\sqrt{m^{2}+p_{j}^{2}}
are the energy in which
\displaystyle p_{j}\equiv|\vec{p}_{j}|
,
\displaystyle\bar{\omega}\equiv\int d^{3}\vec{p}\,f(\vec{p})\,\omega
is the average energy, and
\displaystyle\rho
is the local energy density.

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}
are random phases uniformly distributed within
\displaystyle[0,2\pi)
for a stochastic background that we focus on in this study.
\displaystyle\beta
are discrete random variables following the probabilities of different polarization modes
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}
.

Generally, a massive vector field contains three degrees of freedom. In the unitary gauge, these include the longitudinal mode

\vec{\epsilon}_{0}=\frac{\omega}{m}\hat{e}_{p}\equiv\frac{\omega}{m}\left(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta\right)^{\textrm{T}},

(10)

and two transverse ones in the linear polarization basis,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{H}=\hat{e}_{\theta}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv\left(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta\right)^{\textrm{T}},

(11)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{V}=\hat{e}_{\phi}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\equiv\left(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0\right)^{\textrm{T}},

(12)

where
\displaystyle\hat{e}_{p},\hat{e}_{\theta}
and
\displaystyle\hat{e}_{\phi}
are three orthogonal unit directional vectors. Alternatively, one can express the transverse ones using the circular polarization basis

\vec{\epsilon}_{R/L}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{H}\pm\mathrm{i}\ \vec{\epsilon}_{V}\right).

(13)

Now the vector observables
\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}
in Eq. (
4
-
7
) can be written as a sum of different polarization modes depending on a specific type of coupling,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{a}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\,\frac{m\,p_{j}}{\omega_{j}}\,\Phi_{j}\cos\left[\Psi_{j}\right]\,\vec{\epsilon}_{0}^{\,j},

(14)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{A}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\,\Phi_{j}\,\text{Re}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Psi_{j}}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,j}\right],

(15)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{\textrm{MDM}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\,p_{j}\,\Phi_{j}\,\text{Re}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Psi_{j}}\ \vec{\epsilon}_{R/L}^{\,j}\right],

(16)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}_{\textrm{EDM}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N_{b}}\,\begin{cases}m\,\Phi_{j}\,\text{Re}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Psi_{j}}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,j}\right],&m\gg|p_{j}|,\\
\omega_{j}\,\Phi_{j}\,\text{Re}\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\Psi_{j}}\,\vec{\epsilon}_{R/L}^{\,j}\right],&m\ll|p_{j}|,\end{cases}{}

where one uses the constraint
\displaystyle\partial_{\mu}V^{\mu}=0
of the unitary gauge to express
\displaystyle V^{0}
in Eq. (
7

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}\equiv\sqrt{2\rho/N_{b}\bar{\omega}\omega_{j}}
and
\displaystyle\Psi_{j}\equiv\omega_{j}t-\vec{p}_{j}\cdot\vec{x}+\alpha_{j}
are defined for simplicity. One can thus treat the axion gradient effectively as the longitudinal-mode-only case of the dark photon.

II.3
Circular and Linear Polarization Basis of Vector Sensors

In this subsection, we start with a review on detecting effective magnetic fields from the spin precession and explain why the detectors are in the circular polarization basis, in analogy with the concept in radio astronomy. Thus one can transform it to the linear polarization basis with two systems polarized at opposite directions operating simultaneously, probing a specific spatial component of the vector-like signals on the transverse plane.

The couplings between the dark sectors and the spins of nucleons in Eq. (
3
) can be detected through nuclear magnetic resonance
Graham and Rajendran 2013
;
Budker et al. 2014
;
Garcon et al. 2019
;
Aybas et al. 2021
, Floquet masers
Jiang et al. 2021a
, or spin-based amplifiers
Jiang et al. 2021b
;
Su et al. 2021
.
In such cases, the nucleons are initially polarized along a specific direction, and the signals
\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}
on the transverse directions can be captured. Without loss of generality, taking the initial polarized spin
\displaystyle\vec{\sigma}_{0}
along
\displaystyle+\hat{z}
, one can describe the dynamics of the spin operators
\displaystyle\vec{\sigma}
using the Bloch equation

\frac{d\vec{\sigma}}{dt}=\vec{\sigma}\times\left(\gamma\vec{B}_{0}-\alpha\vec{\mathcal{O}}\right)-\frac{\vec{\sigma}-\vec{\sigma}_{0}}{T_{r}},

(18)

where
\displaystyle\gamma
is the gyromagnetic ratio,
\displaystyle\vec{B}_{0}=B_{0}\hat{z}
is the background magnetic field along
\displaystyle+\hat{z}
, and
\displaystyle T_{r}
is the relaxation time of the system. One way to see the response to the signal is to decompose the transverse directions in terms of
\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}\equiv\left(\sigma_{x}+\mathrm{i}\sigma_{y}\right)/\sqrt{2}
, which leads to

\left(\frac{d}{dt}+\mathrm{i}\gamma B_{0}+\frac{1}{T_{r}}\right)\sigma_{\perp}=-\mathrm{i}\alpha\sigma_{0}\left(\frac{\mathcal{O}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{O}_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}\right),

(19)

where
\displaystyle\gamma B_{0}
sets the resonant frequency, and
\displaystyle 1/T_{r}
denotes the dissipation.
\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{O}_{x}+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{O}_{y}\right)/\sqrt{2}
are the sensitive signals, which are clearly in the circular polarization basis. For transverse modes propagating along the
\displaystyle+\hat{z}
-axis, only the left-hand circular polarization mode
\displaystyle\epsilon_{L}
can excite the detector. For the longitudinal mode,
\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}
, only the projection to the transverse plane gets a response, and one cannot distinguish its component along
\displaystyle\hat{x}
and
\displaystyle\hat{y}
.

Two detectors with opposite initially polarized directions and the same magnetic field strength
\displaystyle B_{0}
can transform the circular polarization basis into the linear one. More explicitly, one sums up simultaneously the signals from the detectors polarized along
\displaystyle+\hat{z}
and
\displaystyle-\hat{z}
with relative phase shift
\displaystyle\pi/2
and gets the response to a specific spatial direction on the transverse plane,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}+\mathrm{i}\gamma B_{0}+\frac{1}{T}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{\perp}^{\uparrow}+\mathrm{i}\sigma_{\perp}^{\downarrow}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\mathrm{i}\alpha\sigma_{0}\mathcal{O}_{x},

(20)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left(\frac{d}{dt}+\mathrm{i}\gamma B_{0}+\frac{1}{T}\right)\left(\frac{\sigma_{\perp}^{\uparrow}-\mathrm{i}\sigma_{\perp}^{\downarrow}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha\sigma_{0}\mathcal{O}_{y},

(21)

where
\displaystyle\sigma_{\perp}^{\uparrow/\downarrow}
are the transverse responses for detectors polarized along
\displaystyle+\hat{z}
and
\displaystyle-\hat{z}
respectively.

This transformation of the response basis also applies to detectors using polarized electrons, such as the axion-magnon conversion
Barbieri et al. 2017
;
Mitridate et al. 2020
;
Chigusa et al. 2020
.
For the
\displaystyle U(1)_{B-L}/U(1)_{B}
dark photon or the kinetic-mixing dark photon, as mentioned in Sec.
II.1
, the detector responses to them are in the linear polarization basis.

III
From the Scalar Sensor Interferometry to Vector Sensor Network

This section starts with a review on the dark matter interferometry developed in
Foster et al. 2021
, where directional information can be extracted by correlating two detectors separated by a baseline whose length is comparable to the de Broglie wavelength.
We generalize the formalism to an array with multiple vector sensors, each of which is sensitive to a certain spatial component of the observables, as shown in Sec.
II
.
The covariance matrix, which contains the information of the correlations between each pair of sensors, is expanded with additional
\displaystyle 3\times 3
labels on the sensors’ sensitive directions.

III.1
Scalar Sensor Interferometry

One starts with
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{d}
detectors for scalar-like observables labeled with
\displaystyle I,J,\cdots
and operates them simultaneously over time
\displaystyle T
. A discrete Fourier transform to the frequency space, indexed by an integer
\displaystyle k
, gives the expected response of the
\displaystyle I
th detector,

\tilde{d}_{I}^{k}(\theta_{\rm sig})=A_{I}(\omega_{k})\,\tilde{a}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{I}\right)+\tilde{n}_{I}\left(\omega_{k}\right),

(22)

where
\displaystyle A_{I}
is the response functions of the detector that absorbs the coupling constant,
\displaystyle\tilde{a}
comes from the transformation of the axion field (
8
) to the frequency space, and
\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{I}
is the Gaussian noise at frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{k}=2\pi k/T
.
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm sig}
contains all the relevant signal parameters that characterize the axion contribution, for example, axion mass
\displaystyle m
and the momentum distribution
\displaystyle f(\vec{p}\,)
that we assume to be the same within the network of the detectors in this study. Since Eq. (
22
) is a Gaussian random variable with zero mean, one can construct the multi-detector covariance matrix

\Sigma_{k}(\theta_{\rm sig})=\langle\tilde{d}^{k}\tilde{d}^{k\dagger}\rangle

(23)

in terms of the
\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{d}
-dimensional vector

\tilde{d}^{k}\equiv\left(\tilde{d}_{1}^{k},\tilde{d}_{2}^{k},\cdots,\tilde{d}_{\mathcal{N}_{d}}^{k}\right)^{T},

(24)

where
\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle
denotes the ensemble average on the stochastic fields and the statistical average on the detector noise. We leave the discussion of the ensemble average on the stochastic fields to the Appendix.
Each component of the covariance matrix
\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}
characterizes the correlation between the
\displaystyle I
th and the
\displaystyle J
th detector,

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{d}_{I}^{k}\tilde{d}_{J}^{k*}\rangle=\delta_{IJ}\lambda_{I}\left(\omega_{k}\right)+\frac{A_{I}\left(\omega_{k}\right)A_{J}\left(\omega_{k}\right)\rho}{\bar{\omega}}F_{IJ}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ}\right),

(25)

where
\displaystyle\lambda_{I}=\langle\tilde{n}_{I}\tilde{n}_{I}^{*}\rangle
is the noise power spectral density of the
\displaystyle I
th detector, and

F_{IJ}(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ})=\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{p}}{\omega}\,f(\vec{p})\,e^{\mathrm{i}\,\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}_{IJ}}\,\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{k}\right).

(26)

represent the correlations of the axion field at the two locations separated by
\displaystyle\vec{x}_{IJ}\equiv\vec{x}_{I}-\vec{x}_{J}
, containing all the relevant signal parameters
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm sig}
in this study.

With the help of the covariance matrix, we next construct the likelihood for an array of detectors in terms of data
\displaystyle\tilde{D}
and the putative signal parameters
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm sig}

Foster et al. 2018
;
Foster et al. 2021

\mathcal{L}\left(\tilde{D}|\theta_{\rm sig}\right)=\prod_{k}\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\,\tilde{D}^{k\dagger}\cdot\Sigma_{k}^{-1}(\theta_{\rm sig})\cdot\tilde{D}^{k}\right]}{\sqrt{|2\pi\Sigma_{k}(\theta_{\rm sig})|}},

(27)

as well as the test statistic (TS)

\Theta=2\left[\,\ln\,\mathcal{L}\left(\tilde{D}|\theta_{\rm sig}\right)-\ln\,\mathcal{L}\left(\tilde{D}|\theta_{\rm sig}=0\right)\,\right]

(28)

that quantifies the significance of
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm sig}
.

To get an intuitive understanding of how to extract information from the TS in Eq. (
28
), we use the Asimov data set
Cowan et al. 2011
where the average on the covariance of data
\displaystyle\tilde{D}
converges to the truth value

\displaystyle\langle{\tilde{D}}_{I}^{k}{\tilde{D}}_{J}^{k*}\rangle=\Sigma_{k}(\theta_{\rm truth})

(29)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta_{IJ}\lambda_{I}\left(\omega_{k}\right)+\frac{A_{I}\left(\omega_{k}\right)A_{J}\left(\omega_{k}\right)\rho}{\bar{\omega}}F^{t}_{IJ}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ}\right),

where we use a superscript
\displaystyle t
to label functions containing the truth parameters within
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm truth}
.
Under the assumptions that the noise is much larger than the signal, and the frequency bin size
\displaystyle 2\pi/T
is small enough compared to the varying scales of both the signals and the noise, we take the expectation value of the likelihood in Eq. (
28
) with the average data and get the asymptotic TS
Foster et al. 2021

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Theta}(\theta_{\rm sig})\approx\sum_{I,J}\frac{\rho^{2}T}{2\pi\bar{\omega}^{2}}

\displaystyle\int d\omega\,\frac{A_{I}^{2}(\omega)A_{J}^{2}(\omega)}{\lambda_{I}(\omega)\lambda_{J}(\omega)}\,F_{JI}(\omega,\vec{x}_{JI})

(30)

\displaystyle\left(F^{t}_{IJ}(\omega,\vec{x}_{IJ})-\frac{1}{2}F_{IJ}(\omega,\vec{x}_{IJ})\right).

The maximization of the asymptotic TS (
30
) happens when
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm sig}
in
\displaystyle F_{IJ}
approaches
\displaystyle\theta_{\rm truth}
.

One can further estimate the Fisher information matrix in terms of different parameters
\displaystyle\theta_{i}\in\theta_{\rm sig}
, evaluated at the truth parameters

I_{ij}(\theta_{\rm sig})=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\Theta}}{\partial\theta_{i}\,\partial\theta_{j}}|_{\theta_{\rm sig}=\theta_{\rm truth}}.

(31)

For single parameter
\displaystyle\theta_{i}
, the inverse of Eq. (
31
) gives the uncertainty
\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\theta_{i}}
.

Take an infinitely cold stream with
\displaystyle f(\vec{p}\,)=\delta^{3}(\vec{p}-\vec{p}_{0})
as an example. With two detectors separated by a baseline
\displaystyle\vec{x}_{IJ}
, one can extract the information
\displaystyle\cos\theta_{\rm str}\equiv\hat{p}_{0}\cdot\hat{x}_{IJ}
from the relative phase factor in Eq. (
26
), with the uncertainty
\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{\theta_{\rm str}}\propto 1/\left(p_{0}\,x_{IJ}\,\sin\theta_{\rm str}\right)^{2}
derived from Eq. (
31
)
Foster et al. 2021
.

In the case in which the baselines
\displaystyle\vec{x}_{IJ}
between each pair of axion detectors cover a large volume in the coordinate space with a sufficient time resolution, we can take the continuous limit
\displaystyle\omega_{k}\rightarrow\omega
and
\displaystyle\vec{x}_{IJ}\rightarrow\vec{x}
, which leads to
\displaystyle F_{IJ}(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ})\rightarrow F(\omega,\vec{x})
.
Thus the three-dimensional Fourier transform

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{x}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\,\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}}\,\int d\omega\,F(\omega,\vec{x})=\frac{f(\vec{p}\,)}{\omega}

(32)

enables us to recover the complete information of the axion field in momentum space, namely,
\displaystyle f(\vec{p}\,)
.

III.2
Vector Sensor Array

In this subsection, we consider a network of vector sensors. For each detector, we replace the expected response in Eq. (
22
) with the vector-like signals
\displaystyle\vec{\mathcal{O}}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{I}\right)
,

\tilde{d}_{I}^{k}=A_{I}(\omega_{k})\,\vec{\mathcal{O}}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{I}\right)\cdot\hat{l}_{I}+\tilde{n}_{I}\left(\omega_{k}\right),

(33)

where
\displaystyle\hat{l}_{I}
is a unit vector representing the sensitive direction of the sensor. Similar to Eq. (
26
), the correlations of the vector-like signals are

F_{IJ}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ},\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J}\right)=\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{p}}{\omega}\,f(\vec{p}\,)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,\vec{p}\cdot\vec{x}_{IJ}}\,\delta\left(\omega-\omega_{k}\right)\,\zeta^{2}_{\mathcal{O}}(p)\,\sum_{\beta}h(\beta)\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}\cdot\hat{l}_{I}\right)\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,*}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}\right),

(34)

where
\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathcal{O}}(p)
is the factor before
\displaystyle\Phi_{j}
in Eq. (
14
-
), depending on different types of coupling
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
, and
\displaystyle h(\beta)
is the percentage of the polarization mode
\displaystyle\beta
.
Compared with the case of the scalar sensors in Eq. (
26
), the correlations of the vector-like signals (
34
) have additional polarization-dependent factors
\displaystyle h(\beta)\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}\cdot\hat{l}_{I}\right)\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,*}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}\right)
. Thus the product
\displaystyle f(\vec{p}\,)\times\zeta^{2}_{\mathcal{O}}(p)
can be extracted only when we know
\displaystyle h(\beta)
.

We next rewrite Eq. (
34
) as

\displaystyle F_{IJ}(\omega_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ},\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J})

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{k}\,\zeta^{2}_{\mathcal{O}}(p_{k})\,\sum_{\beta}h(\beta)\,\mathcal{F}^{\beta}_{IJ}(p_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ},\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J}),

where
\displaystyle p_{k}=\sqrt{\omega_{k}^{2}-m^{2}}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\beta}_{IJ}(p_{k},\vec{x}_{IJ},\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J})

(36)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\hat{\Omega}\,f(p_{k},\hat{\Omega})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,p_{k}\,\hat{\Omega}\cdot\vec{x}_{IJ}}\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}\cdot\hat{l}_{I}\right)\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,*}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}\right),

which characterizes the correlations between the two vector-like signals with a specific polarization mode
\displaystyle\beta
and energy
\displaystyle\omega_{k}
.

(37)

where
\displaystyle p=|\vec{p}\,|

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\,0}_{IJ}\left(p,\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J}\right)=

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\hat{\Omega}\,\frac{f_{\rm iso}(p)}{4\pi p^{2}}\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{0}\cdot\hat{l}_{I}\right)\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{0}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{2}f_{\rm iso}(p)}{3m^{2}p^{2}}\,\hat{l}_{I}\cdot\hat{l}_{J},

and the ones for each type of transverse modes are

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{R/L}_{IJ}\left(p,\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J}\right)=

\displaystyle\int d^{2}\hat{\Omega}\,\frac{f_{\rm iso}(p)}{4\pi p^{2}}\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{R/L}\cdot\hat{l}_{I}\right)\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{R/L}^{\,*}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}\right)

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\rm iso}(p)}{3p^{2}}\,\hat{l}_{I}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}.

Among all the polarization modes, the angular correlations are universally proportional to the dipole correlation
Jenet and Romano 2015

\mathcal{F}^{\beta}_{IJ}\propto\hat{l}_{I}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}=\cos\theta_{IJ},

(40)

where
\displaystyle\theta_{IJ}

\displaystyle\hat{l}_{I}

\displaystyle\hat{l}_{J}

and

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{f_{\rm str}(p)}{p^{2}}\left(\hat{l}_{I}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}-(\hat{\Omega}_{0}\cdot\hat{l}_{I})\,(\hat{\Omega}_{0}\cdot\hat{l}_{J})\right),

and
\displaystyle\hat{l}_{J}

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}_{0}

V

IV.3
Standard Halo Model of Dark Matter

The last example we consider is the Maxwellian distribution of the standard halo model of cold dark matter, whose velocity distribution is
Derevianko 2018

f_{\rm DM}(\vec{v}\,)=\left(2\pi v_{\textrm{vir}}^{2}\right)^{-3/2}\,\exp\left[-\frac{(\vec{v}-\vec{v}_{g})^{2}}{2v_{\textrm{vir}}^{2}}\right],

(48)

where
\displaystyle v_{\textrm{vir}}\approx 10^{-3}
and
\displaystyle\vec{v}_{g}
are the virial velocity and the Earth’s velocity in the galactic reference frame respectively. In the non-relativistic limit, Eq. (
34
) can be approximated as

\displaystyle F_{IJ}\left(\omega_{k},\vec{d},\hat{l}_{I},\hat{l}_{J}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{v}}{m^{2}\,v}\,f_{\rm DM}(\vec{v})\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\,m\,\vec{v}\cdot\vec{d}}\,\delta\left(v-v_{k}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\zeta^{2}_{\mathcal{O}}(m\,v)\,\sum_{\beta}h(\beta)\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}\cdot\hat{l}_{I}\right)\,\left(\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}^{\,*}\cdot\hat{l}_{J}\right),

where
\displaystyle v_{k}
satisfies
\displaystyle\omega_{k}/m=1+v_{k}^{2}/2
.
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}
reduce to unit directional vectors and
\displaystyle h(\beta)
contains an equal percentage among the allowed polarization modes in Eq. (
14
-
). With a preknowledge of the velocity distribution in Eq. (
48
), one can break the degeneracy in the product
\displaystyle f_{\rm DM}(\vec{v})\times\zeta^{2}_{\mathcal{O}}(m\,v)
and identify the exact type of the coupling
\displaystyle\mathcal{O}
listed in Sec.
II
.

Due to the complicated forms of Eq. (
49
) defined at each energy bin and the narrow bandwidth of the standard halo dark matter, we define the dark matter correlation matrix as

\displaystyle\Big(\mathscr{C}_{\mathcal{O}}(\vec{d}\,)\Big)_{ij}

(50)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int dv_{k}\,v_{k}\,\text{Re}\left[F_{IJ}(\omega_{k},\vec{d},\hat{i},\hat{j})\right]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{v}}{m^{2}}\,f_{\rm DM}(\vec{v}\,)\,\cos\left[m\,\vec{v}\cdot\vec{d}\,\right]\,\zeta^{2}_{\mathcal{O}}(m\,v)\,\sum_{\beta}h(\beta)\,\epsilon_{\beta}^{i}\,\epsilon_{\beta}^{j},

which comes from the integration of the real part of Eq. (
49
) in energy. Eq. (
50
) always leads to analytic expressions from the Gaussian integral.

\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
denote the index of the spatial components in Cartesian coordinates. For dark matter, we choose the linear polarization basis where
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}
are real.
Equation (
50
) is the vectorial generalization of the two-point spatial correlation function of the scalar-like signals from the dark matter in
Derevianko 2018
and the Appendix. The daily modulations of the vector-like signals were studied in
Lisanti et al. 2021
;
Gramolin et al. 2022
for the axion gradient and in
Caputo et al. 2021
for the kinetic-mixing dark photon.

IV.3.1
Axion Gradient

For the axion gradient, there is only longitudinal mode and
\displaystyle\zeta_{a}=m\,v
. To give an intuitive understanding, we can decompose Eq. (
50
) into three parts:

\mathscr{C}_{a}(\vec{d}\,)=\,\exp\left[-\frac{d^{2}}{2\lambda_{c}^{2}}\right]\,\left(C_{a}^{\textrm{vir}}+C_{a}^{g}+C_{a}^{g,\textrm{vir}}\right),

(51)

where
\displaystyle C_{a}^{\textrm{vir}}
contains a term proportional to the square of the virial velocity
\displaystyle v_{\rm vir}
,
\displaystyle C_{a}^{g}
comes from the square of
\displaystyle v_{g}
, and
\displaystyle C_{a}^{g,\textrm{vir}}
is a product of the two.

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}\equiv 1/\left(m\,v_{\textrm{vir}}\right)
is the correlation length of the virial dark matter.
The overall exponential factor indicates that the correlation decays at a length scale
\displaystyle\lambda_{c}
due to the virial fluctuations.

Without loss of generality, we assume that the separation between the detectors pair
\displaystyle\vec{d}
is along the
\displaystyle\hat{z}
-axis, where the virial part takes the form

C_{a}^{\textrm{vir}}=v_{\textrm{vir}}^{2}\,\textrm{diag}\left(1,1,1-\frac{d^{2}}{\lambda_{c}^{2}}\right)\,\cos\left[m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}\,\right].

(52)

In the absence of
\displaystyle\vec{v}_{g}
,
Eq. (
52
) is the only non-vanishing one. The anisotropic velocity leads to a spatial oscillation factor
\displaystyle m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}
. The second part of Eq. (
51
) is

\Big(C_{a}^{g}\Big)_{ij}=v_{g}^{i}\,v_{g}^{j}\cos\left[m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}\,\right],

(53)

which is an anisotropic term induced by
\displaystyle\vec{v}_{g}
. The last term in Eq. (
51
) is

\displaystyle C_{a}^{g,\textrm{vir}}=v_{\textrm{vir}}\,\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-v_{g}^{x}\\
0&0&-v_{g}^{y}\\
-v_{g}^{x}&-v_{g}^{y}&-2v_{g}^{z}\end{pmatrix}\,\frac{d}{\lambda_{c}}\,\sin\left[m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}\,\right],

(54)

which describes the mixing effect between the anisotropic velocity
\displaystyle\vec{v}_{g}
and the virial one.

Different cases of
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{a}(\vec{d}\,)
are shown in the left panels of Fig.
3
. For simplicity, we only consider the situation in which two detectors are co-aligned. In such a case, there are five different ways to arrange the directions of the baseline
\displaystyle\hat{d}
and the sensitive directions
\displaystyle\hat{l}
.

\displaystyle v_{\textrm{vir}}=v_{g}
is assumed in this work. When
\displaystyle\hat{v}_{g}\perp\hat{d}
, both the spatial oscillation factor
\displaystyle m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}
and the mixing term (
54

\displaystyle\hat{l}\perp\hat{v}_{g}
, Eq. (
53
) vanishes as well.
Then
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{a}(\vec{d}\,)
is influenced by the virial velocity only, so that it decreases to zero monotonically in terms of
\displaystyle d
.
On the other hand, when
\displaystyle\hat{l}\,\mathbin{\!/\mkern-5.0mu/\!}\,\hat{d}
,
\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{a}(\vec{d}\,)
becomes negative first before decaying to zero.
The impact of
\displaystyle\vec{v}_{g}
shows up in the rest cases where the spatial oscillation factor
\displaystyle m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}
can appear. However, since the correlation decays significantly when
\displaystyle d\simeq 2\pi/\left(m\,v_{g}\right)

Notice that if one defines Eq. (

\displaystyle\pi/2

We also calculate the angular correlations between two detectors at the same location with different sensitive directions

Dark Photon

For non-relativistic dark photons, the polarization vectors are equally distributed on the unit sphere in the galaxy frame. In the linear polarization basis, this is equivalent to taking the percentage of each polarization mode
\displaystyle\vec{\epsilon}_{\beta}

\displaystyle h(\beta)

\displaystyle 1/3

\displaystyle C_{\textrm{MDM}}^{\textrm{vir}}=v_{\textrm{vir}}^{2}\,\textrm{diag}\left(2-\frac{d^{2}}{\lambda_{c}^{2}},2-\frac{d^{2}}{\lambda_{c}^{2}},2\right)\,\cos\left[m\,\vec{v}_{g}\cdot\vec{d}\,\right].

VII
Conclusions

In this work, we explore several applications of an array of vector sensors in identifying the features of bosonic waves, including angular distribution, types of coupling, directions of the emission, and macroscopic circular polarization. Such information can be extracted from a pair of vector sensors with different optimal sensitive directions and a baseline that separates them for various purposes. These applications are based on two-point correlation functions of the vector fields with two spatial component labels.

We show that, for isotropic sources with a cosmological origin, one always gets the universal dipole angular correlation for all the polarization modes with two vector sensors at the same location. Any deviation from this relation is a signal of anisotropy. We find that a finite long baseline is necessary to break the degeneracy between the longitudinal and the transverse modes, leading to different correlation signals for the two polarization modes when the sensitive directions are parallel with the baseline or perpendicular to it. One can further identify a potential macroscopic circular polarization by making the two sensitive directions perpendicular to the baseline and each other.

Longitudinal and transverse polarization degrees of freedom contribute differently to the angular correlations for sources from a specific direction. However, we find that the optimal sensitive directions of the vector sensors, in terms of the angular resolution, are the same in both cases. With a short baseline, the sensitivity reaches an order of the inverse of the signal-to-noise ratio. In the long-baseline limit, one can resolve the polar angle with a resolution of the order
\displaystyle p_{0}d
, requiring the sensitive directions to overlap with the target vector signals as much as possible.

Finally, we show the spatial and angular correlations of the virialized dark matter with different vector-like couplings to the standard model, such as the axion-fermion couplings and the dipole couplings between dark photons and fermions. The axion and the dark photon coupled to the magnetic dipole momentum (MDM) of the fermions show completely different spatial correlations since the axion gradient signals are purely longitudinal while only the transverse modes interact with the fermions’ spins for the MDM dark photon. Due to the movement of the Earth in the galaxy frame, one expects to see an anisotropic part in their angular correlations, which have different signs between the axion and the MDM dark photon. The
\displaystyle U(1)_{B-L}/U(1)_{B}
dark photon, the kinetic-mixing dark photon, and the electric dipole momentum dark photon, on the other hand, contribute equally among all the polarization modes, showing spatial correlations similar to the correlations between two scalar signals and no anisotropic term in the angular correlations.

In practice, once a vector sensor sees a suspicious signal, the rest of the detectors in the world can either falsify it or identify the sources’ properties by correlating the signals of different detectors, in the way that we discuss in this study. Complete extraction of the data gives rich information on the microscopic nature and the origin of the sources.

A possible future extension of this work is to develop optimal data analysis strategies to increase the scan rate of the wave-like dark matter or other stochastic backgrounds, with vector-like couplings to the standard model, and to employ these in vector sensor networks, such as the GNOME.
