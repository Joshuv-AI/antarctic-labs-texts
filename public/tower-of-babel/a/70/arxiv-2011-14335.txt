Abstract
. We take advantage of the correspondence between pseudogroups and inverse quantal frames, and of the recent description of Morita equivalence for inverse quantal frames in terms of biprincipal bisheaves, to define Morita equivalence for pseudogroups and to investigate its applications. In particular, two pseudogroups are Morita equivalent if and only if their corresponding localic étale groupoids are. We explore the clear analogies between our definition of Morita equivalence for pseudogroups and the usual notion of strong Morita equivalence for C ∗ C^{\ast} -algebras and these lead to a number of concrete results.

1.
Introduction

A pseudogroup is a complete inverse semigroup in which multiplication distributes over compatible joins.
This is an abstract structure, whereas pseudogroups of transformations are concrete exemplars:
for example, the pseudogroup of all partial homeomorphisms between the open subsets of a topological space.
The path to the development of an
abstract
theory of pseudogroups has been a long and circuitous one;
see
[
15
]
, for further background.

The goal of this paper is to describe the Morita theory of pseudogroups.
Notice that there is a pre-existing theory of Morita equivalence for inverse semigroups
[
37
,
5
]
;
the theory we shall develop for pseudogroups is similar to but different from this one. In fact, both theories go
back to the Morita theory described by Rieffel for C*-algebras in
[
33
,
34
]
,
with ours being a much closer analogue of the latter.
Concretely, we shall rely on the fact that there are two distinct philosophies in handling pseudogroups:
they can be regarded as a class of inverse semigroups, as we have described above,
or they can be connected to quantales — specifically, inverse quantal frames
[
28
]
.
These two philosophies are related via a dictionary
[
28
, Th. 1.6]
in the form of
two functors that define an equivalence of categories: from the pseudogroup
S
one can construct the inverse quantal frame

\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
, and from the inverse quantal frame
Q
one can construct the pseudogroup
of partial units
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
. The goal of this paper is to describe the Morita
theory of pseudogroups within the inverse semigroup philosophy, for which we shall take advantage of the sheaf theory
[
29
]
of inverse quantal frames and their recently developed
[
24
]

Morita theory based on Hilsum–Skandalis maps.
We prove three main theorems, the first of which translates between the two philosophies:

Theorem A
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudgroups. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

S
and
T
are Morita equivalent pseudogroups.

(2)

are Morita equivalent inverse quantal frames.

(3)

\mathsf{G}(S)
and
\mathsf{G}(T)
are Morita equivalent localic étale groupoids.

The second is analogous to a similar result in ring theory:

Theorem B

Let

and

be pseudogroups.
Then
S
and

are Morita equivalent if and only if there is an

-equivalence bimodule.

The third is in the spirit of the characterization of Morita equivalence in inverse semigroup theory

Theorem C

Let

and

be pseudogroups.
Then there is an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule
if and only if

and

3.2.
Modules and Morita equivalence

Now, let
S
be a pseudogroup.
We say that

-module

(1)

There is a supported left action of
S
on the set

with support
p\colon X\rightarrow\mathsf{E}(S)
.

(2)

Every left compatible subset of
X
has a join; in particular, the set

has a smallest element, also denoted by
0
,
which is the join of the empty set.

(3)

The action distributes over left compatible joins. (This makes sense by part (1) of Lemma
3.2
).
Thus
s\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}\right)=\bigvee_{i\in I}sx_{i}
.

(4)

If
\bigvee_{i\in I}s_{i}
exists then
\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}s_{i}\right)x=\bigvee_{i\in I}s_{i}x
.
(This makes sense by part (2) of Lemma
3.2

Let us prove some important properties of left
S
-modules.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup and let
X
be a left
S
-module.
If
\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}
exists
then
p\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}\right)=\bigvee_{i\in I}p(x_{i})
.

Proof.

Put
x=\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}
.
Then
x_{i}\leq x
for all
i\in I
and so
\bigvee_{i\in I}p(x_{i})\leq p(x)
.
Suppose that
p(x_{i})\leq e
for all
i\in I

.
Then

Thus

It follows that
p(x)=\bigvee_{i\in I}p(x_{i})

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup and let

be a left
S
-module.
The poset

Proof.

Let

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup and let
X
be a left
S
-module.
Suppose that
\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}
exists and that

.
Then
\bigvee_{i\in I}x\wedge y_{i}

x\wedge\bigl(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}\bigr)=\bigvee_{i\in I}x\wedge y_{i}.

Proof.

We first obtain a more useful description of binary meets.
Let
u,v\in X
.
Define
f=\bigvee\{g\leq p(u)p(v)\colon gu=gv\}
.
By construction
fu=fv\leq u,v
.
Suppose that
z\leq u,v
.
Then
z=p(z)u=p(z)v
.
Thus
p(z)\leq f
and so
z\leq fu=fv
.
It follows that
u\wedge v=fu=fv
.

Observe that
x\wedge y_{i}
are all bounded above by
x
and so are left compatible.
It follows that

x\wedge\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}\right)

and

\bigvee_{i\in I}x\wedge y_{i}

both actually exist.
For each
j\in I
, we have that

x\wedge y_{j}\leq x\wedge\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}\right)
.
Thus

\bigvee_{i\in I}x\wedge y_{i}\leq x\wedge\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}\right)

It remains to prove the reverse inequality.
Put

.
Define

.
Thus
x\wedge y=gx=gy
.
Define
g_{i}=\bigvee\{g_{i}^{\prime}\leq p(x)p(y_{i})\colon g_{i}^{\prime}x=g_{i}^{\prime}y_{i}\}

Then
x\wedge y_{i}=g_{i}x=g_{i}y_{i}

It is easy to show that
p(y_{i})g\leq g_{i}
.
To show that

g_{i}\leq p(y_{i})
.
It follows that
g_{i}x=g_{i}y
.
We have therefore shown that
g_{i}=p(y_{i})g
.

We now calculate:

x\wedge y=gy=g\bigl(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i}\bigr)=\bigvee_{i\in I}gp(y_{i})y_{i}=\bigvee_{i\in I}g_{i}y_{i}=\bigvee_{i\in I}x\wedge y_{i}.

∎

(2)

S

(3)

\iota\colon X\rightarrow\mathsf{L}(X)

(1) We first of all define an action

.
Let

be a left compatible order-ideal.
We prove that
sA
is also a left compatible order-ideal.
Let

where

.
Then
s^{-1}x\leq s^{-1}sy\leq y

.
Thus

.
But

is an order-ideal.

We therefore have an action

Define

\widehat{p}(A)=\bigvee_{x\in A}p(x)

Let

Suppose that

4.
Characterizations of Morita equivalence

In this section, we connect the definition of Morita equivalence for pseudogroups described in section
3

with the Morita theory for inverse quantal frames and étale groupoids.

4.1.
Background on quantales and modules

Let us recall basic facts and definitions. General references on quantales are
[
4
,
35
]
. For monoids and actions in monoidal categories see
[
18
, Ch. VII]
.

First, following
[
11
, Ch. I]
, by a
sup-lattice
will be meant a complete lattice, and a
homomorphism
of sup-lattices
f:L\to M
is a map that preserves arbitrary joins:

f\bigl(\bigvee_{i}x_{i}\bigr)=\bigvee_{i}f(x_{i}).

The top element of a sup-lattice
L
is denoted by
1_{L}
, or simply
1
, and the bottom element by
0_{L}
, or simply
0
. The resulting category of sup-lattices
\mathsf{SL}
is monoidal closed with respect to the tensor product of sup-lattices.

The
category of unital involutive quantales
is the category of involutive monoids in
\mathsf{SL}
. In other words, a unital involutive quantale
Q
is a sup-lattice equipped with an involutive monoid structure whose multiplication and involution distribute over arbitrary joins,

a\bigl(\bigvee_{i}b_{i}\bigr)=\bigvee_{i}ab_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad\bigl(\bigvee_{i}a_{i}\bigr)^{*}=\bigvee_{i}a_{i}^{*},

(multiplication distributes on both sides due to the involution) and a homomorphism of unital quantales is a sup-lattice homomorphism which is also a homomorphism of involutive monoids.
The multiplicative unit of a unital quantale
Q
is denoted by
e_{Q}
, or simply
e
. The frames are exactly the unital involutive quantales with idempotent multiplication such that
e=1
(then, necessarily,
ab=a\wedge b
). Similarly to any commutative quantale, the involution on a frame can be taken to be trivial:
a^{*}=a
. If a frame carries an independent structure of unital involutive quantale it is referred to as a
quantal frame
.

The
category of left modules
of a unital involutive quantale
Q
is the category of left
Q
-actions in
\mathsf{SL}
(the involution plays no role in the definition). The action of an element
a\in Q
on an element
x
of a left
Q
-module
X
is denoted by
ax
.

All these structures can be presented by generators and relations. An exposition of this for sup-lattices and quantales in a form which is convenient for the purposes of this paper can be found in
[
26
, §II.1–2]
. Let us recall this. The free sup-lattice generated by a set
X
is the powerset
\mathcal{P}(X)
, and a presentation of a sup-lattice is a pair
(X,R)
where
X
is the set of generators and
R
is a subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(X)
; each pair
(S,T)\in R
is to be thought of as an equation
\bigvee S=\bigvee T
. Concretely, a construction of the sup-lattice presented by
(X,R)
is given by the following subset of
\mathcal{P}(X)
:

\langle X|R\rangle=\bigl\{Y\subset X:(S\subset Y\iff T\subset Y)\textrm{ for all }(S,T)\in R\bigr\}.

This is closed under arbitrary intersections in
\mathcal{P}(X)
, so it defines a closure operator, and the injection of generators
\eta:X\to\mathcal{\langle}X|R\rangle
assigns to each
x\in X
the closure of
\{x\}
. Then
\eta_{X}
is universal among the maps
f:X\to L
to sup-lattices
L
that
respect the relations
in
R
, i.e., such that
\bigvee f(S)=\bigvee f(T)
for all
(S,T)\in R
.

Unital involutive quantales can be presented in a similar way. Letting
M
be an involutive monoid, the free sup-lattice
\mathcal{P}(M)
is a unital involutive quantale under pointwise multiplication and involution, and the singleton assignment
M\to\mathcal{P}(M)
is universal among the homomorphisms of involutive monoids
f:M\to Q
to unital involutive quantales
Q
. Then, if
(M,R)
is a sup-lattice presentation and
R
is
stable
— i.e.,

(mS,mT)\in R
and
(S^{*},T^{*})\in R
for all
(S,T)\in R
and
m\in M
—,
the presented sup-lattice
\langle M|R\rangle
is a unital involutive quantale. The multiplication of two sets
U,V\in\langle M|R\rangle
is the closure of the pointwise product of
U
and
V
, and the involution is pointwise. This quantale has the expected universal property, namely every homomorphism of involutive monoids
f:M\to Q
to a unital involutive quantale
Q
such that
\bigvee f(S)=\bigvee f(T)
for all
(S,T)\in R
extends uniquely to a homomorphism of unital involutive quantales
f^{\sharp}:\langle M|R\rangle\to Q
.

Related to this, let
A
be a meet-semilattice and consider the set of relations

R=\bigl\{(\{a,b\},\{b\})\in\mathcal{P}(A)\times\mathcal{P}(A):a\leq b\bigr\}.

Then
\langle A|R\rangle
is the frame
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}}(A)
whose elements are the downwards closed sets of
A
, and it is universal among homomorphisms of meet semi-lattices
f:A\to F
to frames
F
. The injection of generators is the principal ideal mapping
a\mapsto a^{\downarrow}
. If we add further relations that are stable for
\wedge
we obtain again a frame, and a corresponding universal property. See
[
10
, §II.2.11]
.

The above facts are easily adapted to presentations of quantale modules. Let us describe this.

Definition
.

Let
M
be an involutive monoid acting on the left on a set
X
, and let

R_{M}\subset\mathcal{P}(M)\times\mathcal{P}(M)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad R_{X}\subset\mathcal{P}(X)\times\mathcal{P}(X).

We say that the pair
(R_{M},R_{X})
is
jointly stable
if the following conditions hold:

(1)

mR_{M}\subset R_{M}
and
R_{M}^{*}=R_{M}
for all
m\in M

(i.e.,
R_{M}
is stable as described above);

(2)

mR_{X}\subset R_{X}
for all
m\in M
;

(3)

R_{M}x\subset R_{X}
for all
x\in X
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
M
be an involutive monoid acting on the left on a set
X
, and let
(R_{M},R_{X})
be a jointly stable pair as in the above definition.

(1)

The sup-lattice
\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
is a left
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
-module whose action uniquely extends the action of
M
on
X
.

(2)

Given any left
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
-module
\Xi
and any map

that respects the relations in
R_{X}
and is
M
-equivariant (i.e.,
f(mx)=\eta_{M}(m)f(x)
for all

and
x\in X
), there is a unique homomorphism of left
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
-modules

such that the following diagram commutes:

\textstyle{X\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{\eta_{X}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\Xi.}

Explicitly,

is defined for all

by

A proof of this proposition is presented in the appendix.

4.2.
Background on inverse quantal frames

Recall that an inverse quantal frame
[
28
]
is a unital involutive quantale that is isomorphic to the quantale
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(G)
of a localic étale groupoid
G
. There is a concrete construction of an étale groupoid
\mathsf{G}(Q)
from an inverse quantal frame
Q
, and there are isomorphisms
G\cong\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(G))
and
Q\cong\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(\mathsf{G}(G))
.

Using the simpler characterization of
[
31
]
, an inverse quantal frame is the same as a unital involutive quantal frame
Q
that satisfies the following two conditions:

(1)

aa^{*}a\leq a\Longrightarrow a=aa^{*}a
for all
a\in Q
(i.e.,
Q
is
stably Gelfand
);

(2)

1=\bigvee Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
, where
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}=\{s\in Q\colon ss^{*}\leq e,\ s^{*}s\leq e\}

is the subset of
partial units
, which is necessarily a pseudogroup.

We borrow notation and terminology from
[
29
]
and denote the
base locale
of an arbitrary inverse quantal frame
Q
by
Q_{0}=\{a\in Q\colon a\leq e\}
.
This coincides with the locale of idempotents
\mathsf{E}(Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})
.

If
S
is a pseudogroup, then
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
is its associated inverse quantal frame, which consists of all order-ideals of
S
that are closed under joins of compatible sets.
We have
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}\cong S
and
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})\cong Q
for all pseudogroups
S
and inverse quantal frames
Q
.
Both
(-)_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
and
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}
extend to functors and show that the categories of inverse quantal frames and of pseudogroups are equivalent.

If
S
is a pseudogroup, we shall write
\mathsf{G}(S)
for the localic étale groupoid
\mathsf{G}(\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S))
, and call it the
groupoid of
S
.
If
S
is spatial then
\mathsf{G}(S)
can be regarded as a sober topological étale groupoid. Even if
S
is not spatial, we can always obtain a sober topological étale groupoid from
S
by taking the locale spectrum of
\mathsf{G}(S)
. This construction yields the groupoid of completely prime filters of
S

[
19
,
17
]
.

Remark 4.2
.

The inverse quantal frame
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
of a pseudogroup
S
coincides with the sup-lattice presented as

\langle S|R_{S}\rangle
, where
R_{S}
contains the relations
(C,\{\bigvee C\})
for all compatible sets
C\subset S
. The injection of generators
\eta_{S}:S\to\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
is the principal ideal mapping
s\mapsto s^{\downarrow}
. The distributivity properties of
S
imply that
R_{S}
is stable both with respect to the involutive monoid structure and the meets of
S
, and thus
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
has several universal properties, as explained in section
4.1
: as a sup-lattice; as a unital involutive quantale; and as a frame. This kind of reasoning will be useful below when dealing with pseudogroup modules.

4.3.
Sheaves and Morita equivalence for inverse quantal frames

An
equivariant sheaf
on a localic étale groupoid
G
, or a
G
-sheaf
, is a local homeomorphism
p\colon X\to G_{0}
, where
G_{0}
is the locale of objects of the groupoid,
together with a left action of
G
on
X
satisfying properties such as associativity, the details of which need not concern us here.
The category of such sheaves and equivariant maps between them is a Grothendieck topos known as the
classifying topos of
G
, usually written
\mathsf{B}G
.
Regarding this as the “category of left modules of
G
,” we formulate one of several equivalent definitions of Morita equivalence for étale groupoids:

Definition
.

Two étale groupoids
G
and
H
are
Morita equivalent
if
\mathsf{B}G
and
\mathsf{B}H
are equivalent categories.

Remark 4.3
.

For non-étale groupoids in general, Morita equivalence cannot be defined via classifying toposes.
For instance, Morita equivalence of Lie groupoids is the isomorphism relation in the category whose morphisms are the isomorphism classes of principal bi-bundles
[
20
]
, also known as Hilsum–Skandalis maps, and the classifying topos is only a Morita invariant. But the analogous definition for localic groupoids coincides with equivalence of classifying toposes
[
3
]
when restricted to the class of étale-complete groupoids, which contains the étale groupoids.

In this paper it will suffice to work with the quantale-theoretic characterizations of
\mathsf{B}G
given in
[
29
]
. There it is shown that the sheaves on an étale groupoid
G
can be precisely described in terms of modules on the inverse quantal frame
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(G)
, and that
\mathsf{B}G
can be represented as a category of
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(G)
-modules.
There are in fact two equivalent main definitions of such modules, the first of which we shall recall below and then use in section
4.4
. The second definition, in terms of Hilbert modules, will only be recalled and used in section
5.3
.

The first definition is scattered across several places in
[
29
]
, and here we present it in a way which is convenient for the purposes of this paper:

Definition
.

[
29
]

Let
Q
be an inverse quantal frame. By a
Q
-sheaf
is meant a left
Q
-module
X
which is also a locale, equipped with a (necessarily unique) monotone map
\varsigma:X\to Q_{0}
, called the
support
, such that

(1)

\varsigma(bx)=b\varsigma(x)
for all
b\in Q_{0}
and
x\in X
(
Q_{0}
-equivariance),

(2)

\varsigma(x)x=x
for all
x\in X
(support condition),

(3)

\bigvee\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}=1_{X}
(cover condition),

where
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is the set of
local sections
:

\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}=\bigl\{\gamma\in X\colon\varsigma(x\wedge\gamma)\gamma\leq x\text{ for all }x\in X\bigr\}.

Given
Q
-sheaves
X
and
Y
, a
sheaf homomorphism

\phi:X\to Y
is a homomorphism of left
Q
-modules that commutes with the supports and sends local sections to local sections:

\varsigma_{Y}\circ\phi=\varsigma_{X}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\phi(\mathit{\Gamma}_{X})\subset\mathit{\Gamma}_{Y}.

The
Q
-sheaves together with the sheaf homomorphisms form the
category of
Q
-sheaves
, which we denote by
Q
-
\mathsf{Sh}
.

From the definition of local section it immediately follows that the set
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is downwards closed in
X
, and that for all
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
we have (cf.
[
29
, Lemma 2.35]
)

(4.1)

\gamma\leq\gamma^{\prime}\iff\gamma=\varsigma(\gamma)\gamma^{\prime}.

We also remark that the support of a
Q
-sheaf is unique, and that it preserves arbitrary joins, because the conditions that it satisfies make it left adjoint to the mapping
(-)1_{X}:Q_{0}\to X
. Hence, being a
Q
-sheaf is a property of a left
Q
-module, rather than extra structure. In addition, a property that will be useful is the following
[
29
, Th. 3.25]
: for all
s\in Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
and
x\in X
we have

(4.2)

\varsigma(sx)=s\varsigma(x)s^{-1},

and thus also

(4.3)

s^{-1}\varsigma(sx)=\varsigma(x)s^{-1}.

Theorem 4.4

(
[
29
, Th. 3.33]
)
.

Let
G
be an étale groupoid. The category of
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(G)
-sheaves
\operatorname{\mathcal{O}}(G)
-
Sh
is isomorphic to the classifying topos
\mathsf{B}G
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
Q
and
R
be inverse quantal frames. The following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

The categories
Q
-
\mathsf{Sh}
and
R
-
\mathsf{Sh}
are equivalent;

(2)

The étale groupoids
\mathsf{G}(Q)
and
\mathsf{G}(R)
are Morita equivalent.

Accordingly, we adopt the following definition (cf.
[
24
, §5.6]
):

Definition
.

Two inverse quantal frames are
Morita equivalent
if their categories of sheaves are equivalent.

4.4.
Morita equivalence for pseudogroups

The purpose of this section is to prove that the category of modules of an arbitrary pseudogroup
S
is equivalent to the category of sheaves of its inverse quantal frame
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
. As a consequence, two pseudogroups are Morita equivalent if and only if their inverse quantal frames are, and this proves Theorem
A
of the Introduction.

The first step will be to provide a construction of pseudogroup modules from sheaves on inverse quantal frames:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
Q
be an inverse quantal frame, and
X
a
Q
-sheaf. Then
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is a
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-module whose action is the restriction of the action of
Q
to partial units and local sections, and whose support is the restriction of the support of
X
to local sections. Moreover, any homomorphism of
Q
-sheaves
\phi:X\to Y
restricts to a homomorphism of
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-modules
\phi|_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}}:\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}\to\mathit{\Gamma}_{Y}
, thus yielding a functor

\Theta:Q\textrm{-}\mathsf{Sh}\to Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}\textrm{-}\mathsf{Mod}.

Proof.

The action of
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
on
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is well defined due to
[
29
, Lemma 4.52]
. In order to make the paper more self-contained let us provide an explicit (and slightly shorter) proof of this fact here. Let
s\in Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
and
\gamma\in\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
. We need to show that
s\gamma
is a local section, i.e., that the inequality

\varsigma(x\wedge s\gamma)s\gamma\leq x

is satisfied for all
x\in X
. Indeed, multiplying by
s^{-1}
on the left in the inequality
x\wedge s\gamma\leq s\gamma
we obtain

s^{-1}(x\wedge s\gamma)\leq\gamma,

so due to (
4.1
) we have

\varsigma\bigl(s^{-1}(x\wedge s\gamma)\bigr)\gamma=s^{-1}(x\wedge s\gamma),

and, using (
4.3
),

\varsigma(x\wedge s\gamma)s\gamma=s\varsigma\bigl(s^{-1}(x\wedge s\gamma)\bigr)\gamma=ss^{-1}(x\wedge s\gamma)\leq x.

So there is a well defined left
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-action on
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
, which moreover is supported, with support
p:\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}\to E(Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})
equal to
\varsigma
restricted to
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
— the two defining conditions of the support of a supported left
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-action follow, respectively, from the support condition of the definition of
Q
-sheaf, and from (
4.2
).

Since
X
is a left
Q
-module, the distributivity conditions (3) and (4) of the definition of left
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-module (cf. section
3.2
) are automatically satisfied, so in order to show that
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is a
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-module it only remains to be proved that
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is closed under joins of left compatible sets. Let
Y\subset\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
be a left compatible set, and let
\gamma=\bigvee Y
in
X
. All we need is to show that
\gamma
is a local section, so let us pick an arbitrary element
x\in X
and prove that
\varsigma(x\wedge\gamma)\gamma\leq x
. Since both the action and the binary meets in
X
distribute over joins, and
\varsigma
preserves joins, we obtain

\varsigma(x\wedge\gamma)\gamma=\varsigma\bigl(x\wedge\bigvee_{y\in Y}y\bigr)\bigvee_{z\in Y}z=\bigvee_{y\in Y}\bigvee_{z\in Y}\varsigma(x\wedge y)z,

so the desired conclusion will follow from showing that
\varsigma(x\wedge y)z\leq x
for all left compatible elements
y,z\in\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
. First notice that for all
b\in Q_{0}
we have

b(x\wedge y)=x\wedge by,

by a routine calculation: from the properties of meets we obtain

b(x\wedge y)\leq bx\wedge by\leq x\wedge by,

and, conversely, since
x\wedge by
is a local section below
by
and
y
,

x\wedge by=\varsigma(x\wedge by)by=b\varsigma(x\wedge by)y=b(x\wedge by)\leq b(x\wedge y).

Therefore, writing

for the local section
\varsigma(z)y=\varsigma(y)z
, and using the

-equivariance of
\varsigma
, we obtain

\varsigma(x\wedge y)z=\varsigma\bigl(x\wedge\varsigma(y)y\bigr)\varsigma(z)z=\varsigma\bigl(x\wedge\varsigma(z)y\bigr)\varsigma(y)z=\varsigma(x\wedge w)w\leq x.

Hence,
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is a left
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-module.

Finally, if
\phi:X\to Y
is a sheaf homomorphism then by definition the restriction
\phi|_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}}:\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}\to\mathit{\Gamma}_{Y}
is well defined and

-equivariant, and it commutes with the supports, so it is a homomorphism of left
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-actions; and, being a homomorphism of
Q
-modules, it preserves all the existing joins, so it is a homomorphism of
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
-modules. The assignments
X\mapsto\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
and
\phi\mapsto\phi|_{\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}}
are respectively the object part and the morphism part of the required functor
\Theta:Q\textrm{-}\mathsf{Sh}\to Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}\textrm{-}\mathsf{Mod}
.
∎

Now let

be a pseudogroup.
Analogously to the definition of the inverse quantal frame

,
for each left
S
-module
X

we shall denote by

the set of all order-ideals of

that are closed under left compatible joins. This coincides with the sup-lattice presented as

\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
, where

contains the relations

(cf. Remark

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup, and

-module with support

. Then
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
is an

-sheaf whose local sections are the principal ideals. The left

-action is the unique extension of the

, and the support
\varsigma
is the unique extension of

UZ=\bigvee_{u\in U}\bigvee_{z\in Z}(uz)^{\downarrow}\quad\textrm{ and }\quad\varsigma(Z)=\bigvee_{z\in Z}p(z)^{\downarrow}.

Proof.

The distributivity properties of the pseudogroup action imply that the sets of defining relations of

\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
are jointly stable (cf. section

), so the conclusion that

are stable with respect to

, so

is a frame.

Now let us study the support of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
.
Since

preserves left compatible joins, the sup-lattice universal property of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
extends

to a unique sup-lattice homomorphism
\varsigma:\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)\to E(S)\cong\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)_{0}

Let us verify that this is
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)_{0}
-equivariant. If
J\in\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)_{0}
and

then, since

and

\displaystyle\varsigma(JI)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varsigma\bigl(\bigvee_{s\in J}\bigvee_{x\in I}(sx)^{\downarrow}\bigr)=\bigvee_{s\in J}\bigvee_{x\in I}\varsigma\bigl((sx)^{\downarrow}\bigr)=\bigvee_{s\in J}\bigvee_{x\in I}p(sx)^{\downarrow}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigvee_{s\in J}\bigvee_{x\in I}(sp(x))^{\downarrow}=J\varsigma(I).

Let us verify that
\varsigma
satisfies the support condition of a sheaf.
Let

.
Taking into account the inequality

In order to conclude that

is a local section.
Let

Note that

Therefore

with

\varsigma(J\cap x^{\downarrow})x^{\downarrow}=\varsigma(y^{\downarrow})x^{\downarrow}=(p(y)x)^{\downarrow}=y^{\downarrow}\subset J.

Finally, let us prove that every local section is a principal ideal.
Let

For all

.
Then

(p(x)y)^{\downarrow}=\varsigma(x^{\downarrow})y^{\downarrow}=\varsigma(x^{\downarrow})\varsigma(y^{\downarrow})\gamma=\varsigma(y^{\downarrow})\varsigma(x^{\downarrow})\gamma=\varsigma(y^{\downarrow})x^{\downarrow}=(p(y)x)^{\downarrow}.

This shows that

∎

Theorem 4.8
.

Let

5.
Bimodules

In this section, we develop further algebraic tools needed to show that two pseudogroups are Morita equivalent.

5.1.
Background on biactions

Let
S
and
T
be inverse semigroups.
By an
(S,T)
-biaction
we will mean a set
X
equipped with a left action
S\times X\rightarrow X
and a right action
X\times T\rightarrow X
,
such that
(sx)t=s(xt)
for all
s\in S
,
t\in T
and
x\in X
,
together with two functions, called
inner products
,
\langle-,-\rangle\colon X\times X\rightarrow S
and
[-,-]\colon X\times X\rightarrow T
such that the following axioms hold
for all
x,y,z\in X
,
s\in S
and
t\in T
:

(BA1):

\langle sx,y\rangle=s\langle x,y\rangle
.

(BA2):

\langle y,x\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle^{-1}
.

(BA3):

\langle x,x\rangle x=x
.

(BA4):

[x,yt]=[x,y]t
.

(BA5):

[y,x]=[x,y]^{-1}
.

(BA6):

x[x,x]=x
.

(BA7):

\langle x,y\rangle z=x[y,z]
.

The above definition is the same as Steinberg’s definition of a Morita context to be found in
[
37
, Definition 2.1]

except that we do not require the functions
\langle-,-\rangle
and
[-,-]
to be surjective
.
We shall need the following properties expressing how the above inner products interact with the action.
They are all proved in
[
37
, Prop. 2.3]
.

Proposition 5.1
.

In a biaction, the following properties hold:

(1)

\langle x,sy\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle s^{-1}
.

(2)

[xt,y]=t^{-1}[x,y]
.

(3)

\langle xt,y\rangle=\langle x,yt^{-1}\rangle
.

(4)

[x,sy]=[s^{-1}x,y]
.

Remark 5.2
.

Observe that
\mbox{\rm im}\langle-,-\rangle
is an ideal of
S
;
this follows by (BA1) above, that tells us it is a left ideal, and by Proposition
5.1
, that tells us it is a right ideal.
Similarly,
\mbox{\rm im}[-,-]
is an ideal of
T
.

Observing that both
[x,x]
and
\langle x,x\rangle
are idempotents, we obtain:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
S
and
T
be inverse semigroups.
Any
(S,T)
-biaction
X
is both a supported left
S
-action and a supported right
T
-action with supports
p\colon X\to\mathsf{E}(S)
and
q\colon X\to\mathsf{E}(T)
defined for all
x\in X
by

p(x)=\langle x,x\rangle\quad\quad\textrm{and}\quad\quad q(x)=[x,x]\;.

Moreover, the following hold:

(1)

\mbox{\rm im}\langle-,-\rangle=S
if and only if
p
is surjective.

(2)

\mbox{\rm im}[-,-]=T
if and only if
q
is surjective.

(3)

If
\langle x,y\rangle
and
[x,y]
are both idempotents then
x\sim y
.

(4)

q(x)[x,y]=[x,y]
, and dually.

Proof.

All these points are easily derived from
[
37
]
. Let us just address (3) and (4). In order to prove (3) let us assume that both
\langle x,y\rangle
and
[x,y]
are idempotents.
From the properties, it is easy to prove that

\langle x,y\rangle\langle x,y\rangle=\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle

and

[x,y][x,y]=[x,x][y,y].

Then

p(x)y=\langle x,x\rangle y=x[x,y]=x[y,x]

since
[x,y]
is an idempotent.
But

x[y,x]=\langle x,y\rangle x=\langle x,x\rangle\langle y,y\rangle x=\langle y,y\rangle x=p(y)x.

Thus
p(x)y=p(y)x
, so we have proved that
x\sim_{l}y
.
By symmetry,
x\sim_{r}y
, thus
x\sim y
. This proves (3), and (4) is a consequence of part (4) of Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

Steinberg’s theory
[
37
]
is developed for inverse semigroups.
In this paper, we are primarily interested in inverse semigroups with zero.
His theory can be extended to this case by using pointed actions.

5.2.
Pseudogroup bimodules

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups.
By an
(S,T)
-bimodule
we will mean an
(S,T)
-biaction
X
such that the following axioms hold:

(Completeness):

Every
compatible
subset of
X
(that is, a subset which is both left and right compatible) has a join.

(Distributivity I):

Both actions distribute over compatible joins in each variable.
That is, for all
x\in X
,
s\in S
, and
t\in T
, and all compatible subsets
\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}
of
X
,
\{s_{i}\colon i\in I\}
of
S
, and
\{t_{i}\colon i\in I\}
of
T
, we have
that the following conditions hold:

(1):

s\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}\right)=\bigvee_{i\in I}sx_{i}
.

(2):

\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}s_{i}\right)x=\bigvee_{i\in I}s_{i}x
.

(3):

\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}\right)t=\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}t
.

(4):

x\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}t_{i}\right)=\bigvee_{i\in I}xt_{i}
.

(Distributivity II):

For any compatible subset
\{x_{i}\colon i\in I\}
of
X
we have that

\bigl\langle\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i},y\bigr\rangle=\bigvee_{i\in I}\langle x_{i},y\rangle\text{ and }\bigl[y,\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}\bigr]=\bigvee_{i\in I}[y,x_{i}].

In particular,
\langle 0,y\rangle=0
and
[y,0]=0
.

Remark 5.5
.

The reader should be aware that if
X
is an
(S,T)
-bimodule it need be neither a
left
S
-module nor a right
T
-module. This is because we require that every compatible subset of
X
has a join
but we do not require that every left (respectively, right) compatible subset has a join. This leads to an unfortunate collision of terminology, but it is nothing more than that.

The two conditions in the righthand side of the equivalences in the following lemma
mean that the supports of the elements of
X
cover the pseudogroup units, so we refer to them as
covering conditions
(cf. Lemma
5.3
).

Lemma 5.6

(Covering conditions)
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups, with identities
e_{S}
and
e_{T}
, respectively, and
X
an
(S,T)
-bimodule.
The following hold:

(1)

\left(\mbox{\rm im}\langle-,-\rangle\right)^{\bigvee}=S
if and only if
\bigvee_{x\in X}p(x)=e_{S}
.

(2)

\left(\mbox{\rm im}[-,-]\right)^{\bigvee}=T
if and only if
\bigvee_{x\in X}q(x)=e_{T}
.

Proof.

We prove (1), the proof of (2) is similar.
Only one direction needs proving.
Suppose that
\bigvee_{x\in X}p(x)=e_{S}
.
Let
s\in S
.
Then

s=\bigvee_{x\in X}sp(x)=\bigvee_{x\in X}s\langle x,x\rangle=\bigvee_{x\in X}\langle sx,x\rangle,

as required.
∎

Definition
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups.
An
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule is an

-bimodule satisfying both the above covering conditions.

The following example shows that the notion of an equivalence bimodule arises naturally in the theory of transformation pseudogroups.

.

Let

5.3.
Morita equivalence for inverse quantal frames revisited

A characterization of Morita equivalence of inverse quantal frames in terms of bimodules has been obtained in
[

]
, based on a definition of sheaf for inverse quantal frames which is different from but equivalent to that of section

. The second definition of sheaf is formulated in terms of the Hilbert modules of
[

(a quantale version of Hilbert C*-modules), specifically the complete ones that were introduced in
[

,

Definition
.

Let

(1)

(2)

\bigl\langle\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i},y\bigr\rangle=\bigvee_{i\in I}\langle{x_{i}},y\rangle
,

(3)

\langle x,y\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle^{*}
,

and moreover is such that for some subset
\mathit{\Gamma}\subset X

(4)

By a

is meant an element
\gamma\in X

\langle x,\gamma\rangle\gamma\leq x
for all

is a Hilbert section
\gamma

\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle\gamma=\gamma

The following facts are easy consequences of the definition:

Proposition 5.9
.

Let

be an involutive quantale, and
X
a complete left Hilbert

-module.

(1)

For all

and any Hilbert basis

we have
\langle x,y\rangle=\bigvee_{\gamma\in\mathit{\Gamma}}\langle x,\gamma\rangle\langle\gamma,y\rangle
(Parseval’s identity).

(2)

If
\langle x,z\rangle=\langle y,z\rangle
for all

then

(the inner product is non-degenerate).

(3)

Any element of a Hilbert basis is a Hilbert section, and
\mathit{\Gamma}_{X}
is the greatest Hilbert basis.

(4)

Any join-dense set of regular sections is a Hilbert basis.

In
[

it has been proved that, for any modular quantal frame

(

(2)

(4)

Remark 5.11
.

Complete Hilbert modules provide a robust theory of sheaves whose applicability extends beyond quantales of étale groupoids. In particular, any Grothendieck topos is, up to equivalence, the category of sheaves on a Grothendieck quantale

whose objects have

(

]
)

Let

Based on this, Morita equivalence for inverse quantal frames can be characterized, much in the same way as for noncommutative rings, in terms of quantale bimodules, which are the unital bi-action objects in the category of sup-lattices.
The appropriate bimodules are those that define principal sheaves with respect to both actions — and thus correspond to Hilsum–Skandalis maps that are invertible 1-morphisms in the bicategory of localic étale groupoids (cf. Remark

.

(with

being right

Bisections:

There is

-bisheaf. Then
\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}
is a
(Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}},R_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})
-equivalence bimodule.

Proof.

The covering conditions in the definition of biprincipal bisheaf imply that that the bisections are principal for both sheaf structures, so by Proposition
5.12
the inner products restricted to
\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}
are valued in
Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
and
R_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
, respectively:

\langle-,-\rangle\colon\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}\times\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}\to Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad[-,-]\colon\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}\times\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}\to R_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}\;.

Then the covering conditions of the definition of pseudogroup bimodule follow from those of the definition of a biprincipal
Q
-
R
-bisheaf. Proving the remaining axioms for the inner products of a
(Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}},R_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})
-biaction is straightforward, only the axiom
\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle\gamma=\gamma
requires some justification: the support
\varsigma\colon\Xi\to Q_{0}
is defined by
\varsigma(x)=\langle x,x\rangle\wedge e_{Q}
for all
x\in\Xi
, and thus for
\gamma\in\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}
we have
\varsigma(\gamma)=\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle
, which implies
\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle\gamma=\varsigma(\gamma)\gamma=\gamma
. Finally, in order to conclude that
\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}
is a
(Q_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}},R_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})
-equivalence bimodule we only need to verify the completeness and distributivity conditions. The two distributivity conditions follow immediately from the distributivity of the actions and the inner products of sheaves on inverse quantal frames. In order to verify completeness, let
Z\subset\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}
be a compatible set. This means it satisfies both left compatibility and right compatibility. Hence,
\bigvee Z
is both a local section for the
Q
-sheaf structure and a local section for the
R
-sheaf structure, which means that it is a local bisection, thus concluding the proof.
∎

Now for each
(S,T)
-bimodule
X
we write
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
for the sup-lattice whose elements are the downwards closed subsets of
X
that are closed under the formation of joins of compatible sets. Notice that, despite the fact that the notation coincides with that of section
4.4
, here the meaning of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
is slightly different because the completion of the elements of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
is done with respect to compatibility rather than left compatibility — cf. Remark
5.5
. The technical details pertaining to the universal properties of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
are very similar to those based on Proposition
4.1
which were used in Lemma
4.7
, so we will slightly abbreviate the presentation.

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups, and
X
an equivalence
(S,T)
-bimodule.
Then
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
is a biprincipal
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-bisheaf.

Proof.

The general properties of module presentations allow us to extend the
(S,T)
-bimodule structure of
X
to an
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-bimodule structure on
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
, and checking the bi-action associativity properties is straightforward. Similarly, the inner products of
X
extend to Hilbert module inner products on
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
. Again, showing that these are Hermitian and left/right-linear is straightforward. Let us just verify the left
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-equivariance: let
I\in\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
and
J,J^{\prime}\in\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
; then we obtain

\langle IJ,J^{\prime}\rangle=\bigvee_{a\in I}\bigvee_{x\in J}\bigvee_{y\in J^{\prime}}\langle ax,y\rangle^{\downarrow}=\bigvee_{a\in I}\bigvee_{x\in J}\bigvee_{y\in J^{\prime}}a^{\downarrow}\langle x,y\rangle^{\downarrow}=I\langle J,J^{\prime}\rangle\;.

The associativity condition
\langle J,J^{\prime}\rangle J^{\prime\prime}=J[J^{\prime},J^{\prime\prime}]
is proved in the same way, so let us prove that there is a common Hilbert basis for the two inner products.
Write
\mathit{\Gamma}\subset\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
for the set of principal order-ideals. Let
J\in\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
and
x\in X
be such that
J\subset x^{\downarrow}
. Then
J
has an upper bound
x
, which implies that it is a compatible set and therefore must contain its own join
y=\bigvee J
. Hence
J=y^{\downarrow}
, and we conclude that
\mathit{\Gamma}
is downwards closed in
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
. Moreover,
\bigvee\mathit{\Gamma}=X
, so
\mathit{\Gamma}
is a downwards closed cover of
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
. Furthermore, each
\gamma=x^{\downarrow}\in\mathit{\Gamma}
is a Hilbert section with respect to both inner products, since for each
J\in\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
we have

\langle J,\gamma\rangle\gamma=\bigvee_{y\in J}\bigl(\langle y,x\rangle x\bigr)^{\downarrow}=\bigvee_{y\in J}\bigl(y[x,x]\bigr)^{\downarrow}\subset\bigvee_{y\in J}y^{\downarrow}=J\;,

and it is a regular Hilbert section:

\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle\gamma=\bigl(\langle x,x\rangle x\bigr)^{\downarrow}=x^{\downarrow}=\gamma\;.

From Proposition
5.9
it follows that
\mathit{\Gamma}
is a Hilbert basis for the
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-module structure, so
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
is an
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-sheaf. Analogously we see that it is a right
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-sheaf. Finally, we have

\bigvee_{\gamma\in\mathit{\Gamma}}\langle\gamma,\gamma\rangle=\bigvee_{x\in X}\langle x,x\rangle^{\downarrow}=(e_{S})^{\downarrow}=e_{\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)}\;,

and, similarly,

\bigvee_{\gamma\in\mathit{\Gamma}}[\gamma,\gamma]=e_{\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)}\;,

so
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
is a biprincipal
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-bisheaf.
∎

Finally we arrive at Theorem
B
:

Theorem 5.17
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups.
Then
S
and
T
are Morita equivalent if and only if there is an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule.

Proof.

Assume that
S
and
T
are Morita equivalent. Then by Theorem
A
the inverse quantal frames
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
and
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
are Morita equivalent, so there is a biprincipal
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-bisheaf
\Xi
due to Theorem
5.14
. Then Lemma
5.15
gives us an
(\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}},\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}})
-equivalence bimodule
\mathit{\Gamma}^{\mathrm{bi}}_{\Xi}
, which in turn can be made an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule due to the pseudogroup isomorphisms
S\cong\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
and
T\cong\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)_{\operatorname{\mathcal{I}}}
.

Now assume that
X
is an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule. Then Lemma
5.16
gives us a biprincipal
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-bisheaf
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)
, hence showing that
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
and
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
are Morita equivalent due to Theorem
5.14
. Then
S
and
T
are Morita equivalent, by Theorem
A
.
∎

Although irrelevant for the main conclusion that was the goal of this section, we remark that the above results can be strengthened by establishing isomorphisms
X\cong\mathit{\Gamma}_{\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(X)}
and
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(\mathit{\Gamma}_{\Xi})\cong\Xi
, indeed showing that there is an equivalence, analogous to the equivalence of categories of Theorem
4.8
, between appropriate categories of
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodules and of biprincipal
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(S)
-
\operatorname{{\mathcal{L}}^{\vee}}(T)
-bisheaves. This, in turn, can be placed in a wider bicategorical context (cf.
[
30
]
).

6.
Enlargements

In this section, we shall describe a purely algebraic characterization of when two pseudogroups are Morita equivalent.
It relies on the concept of an equivalence bimodule introduced in the previous section.

Let
T
be a pseudogroup and
e
an idempotent in
T
.
Then
eTe
is a pseudogroup in its own right with respect to the inherited operations of multiplication, inversion
and compatible joins that we call a
local pseudogroup
of
T
.
It is not a ‘subpseudogroup’ because the identity of
eTe
is
e
rather than the identity of
T
.
We say that
T
is a
sup-enlargement
of
S=eTe
if the following two conditions hold:

(E1):

S=STS
.

(E2):

T=(TST)^{\bigvee}
.

Enlargments were first defined in
[
14
]
and the above definition was motivated by the additive enlargements defined in
[
39
]
.
The following is actually just a consequence of (E1);
see
[
14
]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
T
be a sup-enlargement of
S=eTe
.

(1)

S
is an order-ideal of
T
.

(2)

If
t\in T
is such that
t^{-1}t,tt^{-1}\in S
then
t\in S
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups.
A
joint sup-enlargement
of
S
and
T
is a pseudogroup
U
which is a sup-enlargement of both
S
and
T
.
The following is the first part of Theorem
C
from the Introduction.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let the pseudogroups
S
and
T
have a joint sup-enlargment
U
.
Then there is an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule.

Proof.

Denote the identities of
S
and
T
by
e
and
f
, respectively.
Put
X=\mathsf{E}(S)U\mathsf{E}(T)=eUf
.
Define the action
S\times X\rightarrow X
by left multiplication and
X\times T\rightarrow X
by right multiplication.
Define
\langle-,-\rangle\colon X\times X\rightarrow S
by
\langle x,y\rangle=xy^{-1}
and

[-,-]\colon X\times X\rightarrow T
by
[x,y]=x^{-1}y
.
Then it is a routine, if somewhat laborious, business to check that with these definitions,
we have defined an equivalence bimodule and so by Theorem
5.17
we have proved that the pseudogroups

and

are Morita equivalent.
Observe that conditions (1)—(7) in the definition of a biaction are routine to check;
the covering conditions follow from condition (E2) in the definition of an enlargement;
the conditions in the definition of a bimodule follow from the fact that

is a pseudogroup.
∎

Example 6.3
.

Denote the finite symmetric inverse monoid on the letters
\{1,\ldots,n\}
by
I_{n}
.
These are all pseudogroups and they are all Morita equivalent as pseudogroups,
as we now show.
Denote the unique element of
I_{n}
that maps
\{x\}
to
\{y\}
by
f_{y,x}
.
Observe that
f_{1,1}I_{n}f_{1,1}\cong I_{1}
,
and that for any
1\leq x,y\leq n
, we have that
f_{y,x}=f_{y,1}f_{1,1}f_{1,x}
.
Thus
I_{n}=\left(I_{n}f_{1,1}I_{n}\right)^{\bigvee}
.
It follows that
I_{1}
is Morita equivalent to
I_{n}
for all finite
n
as a pseudogroup.
Thus, since Morita equivalence is an equivalence relation, all finite symmetric inverse monoids are Morita equivalent as pseudogroups.
However, by
[
37
, Corollary 5.2]
, Morita equivalent inverse semigroups have the same number of
\mathscr{D}
-classes.
It follows that two finite symmetric inverse monoids
I_{m}
and
I_{n}
are Morita equivalent as inverse semigroups
if and only if
m=n
.

The proof of the following lemma is routine.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(S,T,X,\langle-,-\rangle,[-,-])
be an equivalence bimodule.
Define
\overline{X}=\{\bar{x}\colon x\in X\}
.
Define a right action of
S
on
\overline{X}
by
\bar{x}s=\overline{s^{-1}x}
and define a left action of

T
on
\overline{X}
by
t\bar{x}=\overline{xt^{-1}}
.
Then
(T,S,\overline{X},[-,-],\langle-,-\rangle)
is an equivalence bimodule.
We call this the dual of
(S,T,X,\langle-,-\rangle,[-,-])
.

The next lemma will be useful in the proof of our main theorem.

The following is an adaptation of the main construction of
[
2
]
to this setting.
The generalized rook conditions are a slight extension of what is to be found in
[
8
,
38
,
12
]
.
It proves the second part of Theorem
C
in the Introduction.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups with an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule.
Then
S
and
T
have a joint sup-enlargement.

Proof.

We shall construct a pseudogroup
U
that contains copies of
S
and
T
and is a sup-enlargement
of both.
This is a sequence of laborious verifications.
We simply give the main stages here.
Denote the identity of
S
by
e_{S}
and the identity of
T
by
e_{T}
.
Let

(S,T,X,\langle-,-\rangle,[-,-])

be an
(S,T)
-equivalence bimodule.
By Lemma
6.4
, we can also construct the dual equivalence bimodule

(T,S,\overline{X},[-,-],\langle-,-\rangle).

Denote by

U=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}S&X\\
\overline{X}&T\end{array}\right)

the set of all
2\times 2
matrices

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)

satisfying what we shall term the
generalized rook conditions
:

•

s^{-1}x=0
.

•

yt=0
.

•

sy=0
.

•

xt^{-1}=0
.

Define a product in
U
by

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}&x_{1}\\
\overline{y_{1}}&t_{1}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{2}&x_{2}\\
\overline{y_{2}}&t_{2}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}s_{2}\vee\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle&s_{1}x_{2}\vee x_{1}t_{2}\\
\overline{s_{2}^{-1}y_{1}\vee y_{2}t_{1}^{-1}}&[y_{1},x_{2}]\vee t_{1}t_{2}\end{array}\right).

This is really a matrix product in which
\vee
replaces addition;
multiplication is treated in various ways:
as semigroup multiplication,
as a semigroup action,
and we treat a product of
x
and
\bar{y}
as
\langle x,y\rangle

and we treat a product of
\bar{x}
and
y
as
[x,y]
.

(1)
The product is well-defined.

We need to check that the joins as written are defined:

•

The fact that
s_{1}s_{2}\sim\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle
follows from the fact that
s_{1}^{-1}x_{1}=0
and
s_{2}y_{2}=0
.

•

To prove that
s_{1}x_{2}\sim x_{1}t_{2}
we show that
\langle s_{1}x_{2},x_{1}t_{2}\rangle=0

and
[s_{1}x_{2},x_{1}t_{2}]=0
using Lemma
5.3
.
To do this, requires us to use
x_{2}t_{2}^{-1}=0
and
s_{1}^{-1}x_{1}=0

and Proposition
5.1
.

•

To prove that
s_{2}^{-1}y_{1}\sim y_{2}t_{1}^{-1}
is similar to the above proof using the fact that
y_{1}t_{1}=0
and
s_{2}y_{2}=0
.

•

The fact that
[y_{1},x_{2}]\sim t_{1}t_{2}
follows from the fact that
y_{1}t_{1}=0
and
x_{2}t_{2}^{-1}=0
.

Thus all the joins that are claimed to exist, do really exist.
It is routine to check that the generalized rook conditions hold for the new matrix;
in checking these, you will find it useful to observe that if
ax=0
and
b\leq a
then
bx=0
.

(2)
U
is a monoid with zero.

We check that the multiplication is associative.
We therefore have to calculate the following product in two different ways and show that they are equal:

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}&x_{1}\\
\overline{y_{1}}&t_{1}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{2}&x_{2}\\
\overline{y_{2}}&t_{2}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{3}&x_{3}\\
\overline{y_{3}}&t_{3}\end{array}\right).

When we multiply the first two matrices together first, we get the following four elements:

(1)

s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\vee\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle s_{3}\vee\langle s_{1}x_{2}\vee x_{1}t_{2},y_{3}\rangle
.
We have used the fact that multiplication distributes over joins.
We can expand the final term to get

s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\vee\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle s_{3}\vee s_{1}\langle x_{2},y_{3}\rangle\vee\langle x_{1}t_{2},y_{3}\rangle
.

(2)

s_{1}s_{2}x_{3}\vee\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle x_{3}\vee s_{1}x_{2}t_{3}\vee x_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.
We have used the fact that the action distributes over joins.

(3)

s_{3}^{-1}s_{2}^{-1}y_{1}\vee s_{3}^{-1}y_{2}t_{1}^{-1}\vee y_{3}[x_{2},y_{1}]\vee y_{3}t_{2}^{-1}t_{1}^{-1}
.

(4)

[s_{2}^{-1}y_{1}\vee y_{2}t_{1}^{-1},x_{3}]\vee[y_{1},x_{2}]t_{3}\vee t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.
This expands to the following

[s_{2}^{-1}y_{1},x_{3}]\vee[y_{2}t_{1}^{-1},x_{3}]\vee[y_{1},x_{2}]t_{3}\vee t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.

When we multiply the second two matrices together first, we get the following four elements:

(1)

s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\vee s_{1}\langle x_{2},y_{3}\rangle\vee\langle x_{1},s_{3}^{-1}y_{2}\vee y_{3}t_{2}^{-1}\rangle
.
We can expand the final term to get

s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}\vee s_{1}\langle x_{2},y_{3}\rangle\vee\langle x_{1},s_{3}^{-1}y_{2}\rangle\vee\langle x_{1},y_{3}t_{2}^{-1}\rangle
.
By
[
37
, Prop. 2.3 part (3)]
,
we have that
\langle x_{1},s_{3}^{-1}y_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle s_{3}

and by
[
37
, Prop. 2.3 part (9)]
,
we have that
\langle x_{1},y_{3}t_{2}^{-1}\rangle=\langle x_{1}t_{2},y_{3}\rangle
.

(2)

s_{1}s_{2}x_{3}\vee s_{1}x_{2}t_{3}\vee x_{1}[y_{2},x_{3}]\vee x_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.
This is equal to

s_{1}s_{2}x_{3}\vee s_{1}x_{2}t_{3}\vee\langle x_{1},y_{2}\rangle x_{3}\vee x_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.

(3)

s_{3}^{-1}s_{2}^{-1}y_{1}\vee\langle y_{3},x_{2}\rangle y_{1}\vee s_{3}^{-1}y_{2}t_{1}^{-1}\vee y_{3}t_{2}^{-1}t_{1}^{-1}
.
This is equal to

s_{3}^{-1}s_{2}^{-1}y_{1}\vee y_{3}[x_{2},y_{1}]\vee s_{3}^{-1}y_{2}t_{1}^{-1}\vee y_{3}t_{2}^{-1}t_{1}^{-1}
.

(4)

[y_{1},s_{2}x_{3}\vee x_{2}t_{3}]\vee t_{1}[y_{2},x_{3}]\vee t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.
This expands to

[y_{1},s_{2}x_{3}]\vee[y_{1},x_{2}t_{3}]\vee t_{1}[y_{2},y_{3}]\vee t_{1}t_{2}t_{3}
.

We need to show that corresponding entries (these have the same number labelling) are equal.
These all check out, often using Proposition
5.1
.
It is easy to verify that
the identity is

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e_{S}&0\\
\bar{0}&e_{T}\end{array}\right)

and the zero is

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right).

(3)
U
is an inverse monoid.

Define

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)^{-1}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s^{-1}&y\\
\bar{x}&t^{-1}\end{array}\right).

It can be checked that this is a well-defined operation meaning that

Then

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s^{-1}&y\\
\bar{x}&t^{-1}\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)

and

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s^{-1}&y\\
\bar{x}&t^{-1}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}ss^{-1}\vee\langle x,x\rangle&0\\
\bar{0}&[y,y]\vee tt^{-1}\end{array}\right)

and

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s^{-1}&y\\

\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s^{-1}s\vee\langle y,y\rangle&0\\
\bar{0}&[x,x]\vee t^{-1}t\end{array}\right).

The idempotents of

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e&0\\
\bar{0}&f\end{array}\right)

where

and

.
It follows that the idempotents commute and so
U
is an inverse monoid with zero.

(3)
U
contains copies of
S
and
T
.

Define

\mathbf{e}_{S}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e_{S}&0\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)\text{ and }\mathbf{e}_{T}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\bar{0}&e_{T}\end{array}\right).

Then

S\cong\mathbf{e}_{S}U\mathbf{e}_{S}=S^{\prime}\text{ and }T\cong\mathbf{e}_{T}U\mathbf{e}_{T}=T^{\prime}.

It follows that
U
contains copies of
S
and
T
.

(4)
U
is a pseudogroup.
If

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}&x_{1}\\
\bar{y}_{1}&t_{1}\end{array}\right)\sim\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{2}&x_{2}\\
\bar{y}_{2}&t_{2}\end{array}\right)

then

s_{1}\sim s_{2}
,
x_{1}\sim x_{2}
,
y_{1}\sim y_{2}
and
t_{1}\sim t_{2}
.
It can now be checked that

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}&x_{1}\\
\bar{y}_{1}&t_{1}\end{array}\right)\vee\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{2}&x_{2}\\
\bar{y}_{2}&t_{2}\end{array}\right)

exists and is equal to

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}\vee s_{2}&x_{1}\vee x_{2}\\
\overline{y_{1}\vee y_{2}}&t_{1}\vee t_{2}\end{array}\right).

It is routine to check that this is a well-defined operation.
We now prove that
U
is a pseudogroup.
By symmetry, it is enough to check that multiplication distributes from the left over arbitrary joins.
We calculate

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)\left(\bigvee_{i\in I}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{i}&x_{i}\\
\bar{y_{i}}&t_{i}\end{array}\right)\right).

This is equal to

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bigvee_{i\in I}s_{i}&\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}\\
\overline{(\bigvee_{i\in I}y_{i})}&\bigvee_{i\in I}t_{i}\end{array}\right).

It is now routine to check the answer.

(5)
U
is an enlargement of the copy of
S
.

It remains to show that condition (E2) in the definition of an enlargement holds.
We shall do this with respect to
S
, since by symmetry the result will then be true for
T
.
We shall use the copy
S^{\prime}
of
S
.
Let

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)

be an arbitrary element of
U
.
Then

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s&x\\
\bar{y}&t\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\bigvee_{i\in I}\langle x_{i},y_{i}\rangle&x\\
\bar{y}&\bigvee_{j\in J}[u_{j},v_{j}]\end{array}\right)

where we have used Lemma
5.6
.
Consider now the following four kinds of element of
U
:

(1)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle x_{i},y_{i}\rangle&0\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)
.

(2)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\bar{y}&0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\bar{y}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle y,y\rangle&0\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)
.

(3)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&x\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle x,x\rangle&0\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&x\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)
.

(4)

\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\bar{0}&[u_{j},v_{j}]\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\bar{u}_{j}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\langle u_{j},u_{j}\rangle&0\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&v_{j}\\
\bar{0}&0\end{array}\right)
.

It follows that each of these four kinds of elements belongs to
US^{\prime}U
and so each element of
U
is a join of elements of
US^{\prime}U
.
∎

7.
Morita invariant properties

A property
\mathscr{P}
of pseudogroups is said to be
Morita invariant
if whenever a pseudogroup
S
has the property
\mathscr{P}

and
T
is Morita equivalent to
S
then
T
has the property
\mathscr{P}
.
In this section, we describe two Morita invariant properties.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup.
A
sup-ideal
of
S
is an ideal
I\subseteq S
which is closed under arbitrary compatible joins.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup.
Let
A
be an ideal.
Then
A^{\bigvee}
is a sup-ideal.

Proof.

The set
A^{\bigvee}
is sup-closed.
It remains only to check that it is an ideal.
But this follows from the fact that products distribute over arbitrary compatible joins.
∎

The following definition is motivated by
[
13
]
.
A pseudogroup
S
is said to be
0
-simplifying
if there are no non-trivial sup-ideals.
Let
e
and
f
be idempotents.
A subset
X\subseteq S
is called a
pencil
from
e
to
f
if

e=\bigvee_{x\in X}\mathbf{d}(x)
and
f\geq\bigvee_{x\in X}\mathbf{r}(x)
.
Define
e\preceq f
if there is a pencil from
e
to
f
.
The proof of the following is straightforward.

Lemma 7.2
.

The relation
\preceq
is a preorder on the set
\mathsf{E}(S)
.

Denote by
\equiv
the equivalence relation induced on
\mathsf{E}(S)
by
\preceq
.
Compare the following with
[
13
, Corollary, page 150]
; despite appearances, there is no number missing following the word ‘Corollary’
in this reference.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
S
be a pseudogroup.
The following are equivalent.

(1)

S
is
0
-simplifying.

(2)

\equiv
is the universal relation on the set of non-zero idempotents.

(3)

For any two non-zero idempotents
e
and
f

there is a subset
Z\subseteq S
such that
e=\bigvee_{z\in Z}\mathbf{d}(z)
and
f=\bigvee_{z\in Z}\mathbf{r}(z)
.

Proof.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2).
Let
e
and
f
be non-zero idempotents.
Then by assumption and Lemma
7.1
,
we have that

S=(SeS)^{\bigvee}=(SfS)^{\bigvee}
.
Now,
e\in(SfS)^{\bigvee}
.
Thus
e=\bigvee_{i\in I}e_{i}
where
e_{i}\in SfS
.
We can write
e_{i}=e_{i}a_{i}fb_{i}e_{i}
where
a_{i},b_{i}\in S
.
Put
x_{i}^{-1}=e_{i}a_{i}f
and
y_{i}=fb_{i}e_{i}
.
It is easy to check that
y_{i}=x_{i}^{-1}
(inverses are unique in inverse semigroups).
Thus
e=\bigvee_{i\in I}x_{i}^{-1}x_{i}
where

\mathbf{r}(x_{i})\leq f
.
It follows that
e\preceq f
.
Symmetry delivers the result.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3). Let
X
be a pencil from
e
to
f
and let
Y
be a pencil from
f
to
e
.
Put
Z=X\cup Y^{-1}
.
Then

\bigvee_{z\in Z}\mathbf{d}(z)=\left(\bigvee_{x\in X}\mathbf{d}(x)\right)\vee\left(\bigvee_{y\in Y}\mathbf{r}(y)\right)=e

and, by symmetry,

\bigvee_{z\in Z}\mathbf{r}(z)=f.

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1). Let
I\neq\{0\}
be a sup-ideal of
S
.
Since there exists
a\in I
with
a\neq 0
, it follows that there exists an idempotent
e\in I
where
e\neq 0
.
Let
f\in S
be any non-zero idempotent.
Then by (3), we can find a subset
Z\subseteq S
such that
e=\bigvee_{z\in Z}\mathbf{d}(z)
and
f=\bigvee_{z\in Z}\mathbf{r}(z)
.
Every ideal is an order-ideal so that
\mathbf{d}(z)\in I
for all
z\in Z
.
But
z=z\mathbf{d}(z)
and so
z\in I
.
Thus we have shown that
Z\subseteq I
.
But
I
is
\bigvee
-closed and so
f\in I
.
It follows that
\mathsf{E}(S)\subseteq I
.
But if
s\in S
is arbitrary, then
s=s(s^{-1}s)
.
It follows that
s\in I
and so we have proved that
S=I
, as required.
∎

The following lemma was actually proved in
[
14
]
but is so useful in
our calculation that we reprove it here.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
S
and
T
be pseudogroups such that
T
is a sup-enlargement of
S
.
Then each idempotent in
TST
is
\mathscr{D}
-related to an idempotent in
S
.

Proof.

Let
e\in TST
be an idempotent.
Then
e=(ets)(s^{-1}st^{\prime}e)
where
s\in S
and
t\in T
.
Put
x=ets
and
y=s^{-1}st^{\prime}e
.
Then
xyx=ex=x
and
yxy=ye=y
.
It follows that
x
and
y
are inverses of each other.
Thus
e=xx^{-1}
and
f=x^{-1}x\in STS=S
.
∎

We can now state our first Morita invariant property.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
S
and
T
be Morita equivalent pseudogroups.
Then
S
is
0
-simplifying if and only if
T
is
0
-simplifying.

Proof.

It is enough to prove the result under the assumption that
T
is a sup-enlargement of
S
.
Because
S
is an order-ideal of
T
by Lemma
6.1
, it is straightforward to check that if
T
is
0
-simplifying then
S
is
0
-simplifying.
We prove the converse.
Let
S
be
0
-simplifying.
We prove that
T
is
0
-simplifying.
We begin with a special case where
e
and
f
are idempotents in
TST
.
We use Lemma
7.4
.
If
e\in\mathsf{E}(TST)
then
e\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\rightarrow}}e^{\prime}

for some
a\in T
where
e^{\prime}\in\mathsf{E}(S)
,
and that if
f\in\mathsf{E}(TST)
then
f\stackrel{{\scriptstyle b}}{{\rightarrow}}f’
for some
b\in T
where
f’\in E(S)
.
By assumption,
e’\preceq f’

and so there is a pencil
X
from
e^{\prime}
to
f^{\prime}
.
It can now be checked that
b^{-1}Xa
is a pencil from
e
to
f
.
Thus
e\preceq f
.
Now let
e
and
f
be arbitrary idempotents in
T
.
The
e=\bigvee_{i\in I}e_{i}
and
f=\bigvee_{j\in J}f_{j}

where
e_{i}
and
f_{j}
are idempotents in
TST
.
Then
e_{i}\preceq f_{j}
, by our above result.
Let
X_{ij}
be a pencil from
e_{i}
to
f_{j}
.
Put
X=\bigcup_{i\in I,j\in J}X_{ij}
.
Then
X
is a pencil from
e
to
f
and so
e\preceq f
.
By symmetry,
f\preceq e
.
It follows that
T
is
0
-simplifying.
∎

Recall that an inverse semigroup
S
is said to be
fundamental
if the only elements that commute with
all the idempotents are the idempotents themselves.

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose that
T
is a sup-enlargement of
S
.
Then
S
is fundamental if and only if
T
is fundamental.

Proof.

Suppose that
T
is fundamental.
Then
S
is isomorphic to a local submonoid of
T
.
It is well-known (and easy to prove) that
S
must itself be fundamental.
We therefore need only prove the converse.
Suppose that
S
is fundamental;
we prove that
T
is fundamental.

We prove first that
TST
is fundamental.
Let
a\in TST
be an element that commutes with every idempotent in
TST
.
We prove that
a
is itself an idempotent.
Observe first that
\mathbf{d}(a)=\mathbf{r}(a)=e
where
e\in TST
.
By Lemma
7.4
,
there exists an idempotent
f\in S
such that
e\mathscr{D}f
.
Let
e\stackrel{{\scriptstyle u}}{{\rightarrow}}f
.
Then
s=uau^{-1}\in S
.
Let
e
be any idempotent in
S
.
Then

se=uau^{-1}e=ua(u^{-1}eu)u^{-1}=u(u^{-1}eu)au^{-1}=euau^{-1}=es.

It follows that
s
is an idempotent
and so
a
is an idempotent.

We can now prove that
T
is fundamental.
Let
a
be an element of
T
that commutes with all idempotents of
T
.
We prove that
a
is an idempotent.
By definition,
a=\bigvee_{i\in I}t_{i}
where
t_{i}\in TST
.
We shall prove that each
t_{i}
is an idempotent from which it follows that
a
is an idempotent.
Let
i\in I
.
Then
t_{i}\leq a
and so
t_{i}=a\mathbf{d}(t_{i})
.
Let
e\in\mathsf{E}(TST)
.
Then
t_{i}e=a\mathbf{d}(t_{i})e=ae\mathbf{d}(t_{i})=ea\mathbf{d}(t_{i})=et_{i}
.
But
TST
is fundamental and so
it follows that
t_{i}
is an idempotent and so
a
is an idempotent.
∎

Our second Morita invariant property is now immediate by Lemma
7.6
and Theorem
6.5
.

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
S
and
T
be Morita equivalent pseudogroups.
Then
S
is fundamental if and only if
T
is fundamental.

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

\mathcal{P}(X)
is a left
\mathcal{P}(M)
-module under the pointwise action.
For all
A\subset M
and
Y,Z\subset X
the two residuations for the action are defined as follows:

\displaystyle A\backslash Z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup\bigl\{W\in\mathcal{P}(X):AW\subset Z\bigr\}=\{x\in X:Ax\subset Z\},

\displaystyle Z/Y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigcup\bigl\{B\in\mathcal{P}(M):BY\subset Z\bigr\}=\{a\in M:aY\subset Z\}.

Take for instance
Z/Y
. This coincides with the intersection

\bigcap_{y\in Y}\{a\in M:ay\in Z\}.

If
Z\in\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
and
(S,T)\in R_{M}
then, using the third joint stability condition,

S\subset\{a\in M:ay\in Z\}\iff Sy\subset Z\iff Ty\subset Z\iff T\subset\{a\in M:ay\in Z\},

and thus

Z/Y\in\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
. Analogously we conclude that if
Z\in\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
then
A\backslash Z\in\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
for all
A\subset M
. Similar conclusions apply to the two residuations for the multiplication of
Q
.

Now let
j
and
k
be the closure operators on
\mathcal{P}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}(X)
which are determined by the intersection-closed subsets
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
and
\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
. The former is the set of fixed points of
j
, the latter is the set of fixed points of
k
, and we have two surjective sup-lattice homomorphisms

j:\mathcal{P}(M)\to\langle M|R_{M}\rangle\quad\textrm{ and }\quad k:\mathcal{P}(X)\to\langle X|R_{X}\rangle.

Let
A\subset M
and
Y\subset X
. Then

\displaystyle AY\subset k(AY)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle A\subset k(AY)/Y\iff j(A)\subset k(AY)/Y

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle j(A)Y\subset k(AY)\iff Y\subset j(A)\backslash k(AY)

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle k(Y)\subset j(A)\backslash k(AY)\iff j(A)k(Y)\subset j(AY),

so the condition
j(A)k(Y)\subset k(AY)
holds. Similarly,
j(A)j(B)\subset j(AB)
for all
A,B\subset M
(
j
is a
quantic nucleus

[
35
, §3.1]
), and
j
commutes with the involution. Hence,
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
has a well defined multiplication given by

(A,B)\mapsto A\mathop{\&}B:=j(AB),

and, similarly, it has a well defined action on
\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
given by

(A,Z)\mapsto A\mathop{\&}Z:=k(AZ).

So
j
and
k
preserve all the operations of the quantale and module structures,

j(AB)=j(A)\mathop{\&}j(B)\quad\textrm{ and }\quad k(AZ)=j(A)\mathop{\&}k(Z),

and, due to surjectivity, the required algebraic properties such as the associativity of the action of
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
on
\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
are inherited from the corresponding properties of
\mathcal{P}(M)
and
\mathcal{P}(X)
.

Now let
\Xi
be a left
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
-module, and
f:X\to\Xi
an
M
-equivariant map that respects
R_{X}
. Due to the sup-lattice universal property of
\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
there is a unique sup-lattice homomorphism
f^{\sharp}:\langle M|R_{X}\rangle\to\Xi
such that
f^{\sharp}\circ\eta_{X}=f
, defined by

f^{\sharp}(J)=\bigvee_{x\in J}f(x).

The
M
-equivariance of
f
translates to left equivariance of
f^{\sharp}
with respect to a set of generators of
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
and a set of generators of
\langle X|R_{X}\rangle
, and makes
f^{\sharp}
a homomorphism of
\langle M|R_{M}\rangle
-modules as required.
∎
