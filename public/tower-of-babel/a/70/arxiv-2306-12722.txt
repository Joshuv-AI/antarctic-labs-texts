Analysis of divergence-preserving unfitted finite element methods for the mixed Poisson problem
Christoph Lehrenfeld, Tim van Beeck, Igor Voulis

Abstract
. In this paper we present a new H ⁡ ( div ) H(\operatorname{div}) -conforming unfitted finite element method for the mixed Poisson problem which is robust in the cut configuration and preserves conservation properties of body-fitted finite element methods. The key is to formulate the divergence-constraint on the active mesh, instead of the physical domain, in order to obtain robustness with respect to cut configurations without the need for a stabilization that pollutes the mass balance. This change in the formulation results in a slight inconsistency, but does not affect the accuracy of the flux variable. By applying post-processings for the scalar variable, in virtue of classical local post-processings in body-fitted methods, we retain optimal convergence rates for both variables and even the superconvergence after post-processing of the scalar variable. We present the method and perform a rigorous a-priori error analysis of the method and discuss several variants and extensions. Numerical experiments confirm the theoretical results.

1.
Introduction

In the recent decades unfitted finite element methods have become a popular tool for the numerical approximation of partial differential equations (PDEs) on complex geometries. The separation of the geometry description from the computational mesh allows for a flexible handling of the geometry and avoids meshing and re-meshing issues of body-fitted discretizations.
Unfitted finite element methods go under different names such as

CutFEM

[
7
]
,
extended FEM
(X-FEM)
[
3
,
30
]
,
Finite Cell

[
32
]
and others.
Several issues that come from the construction of the unfitted methods, such as the enforcement of boundary and interface conditions, the numerical integration on cut cells and the conditioning of the resulting linear systems, have been addressed in the literature for a variety of PDE problems.

One crucial development in the field of unfitted finite element methods was the introduction of the
ghost penalty
(GP) stabilization
[
6
]
as a means of dealing with locally arbitrary cut configurations.
In these situations GP stabilization serves two purposes: ensuring stability of an underlying body-fitted discretization in exact arithmetics and robustness of linear systems in terms of conditioning.
In the following, we will distinguish these two aspects as
stability
and
robustness
, respectively.
An alternative approach with the same goals is the concept of cell agglomeration techniques, see e.g.
[
2
]
.

Despite these advances, there are still several situations where features of body-fitted discretization do not carry over to the unfitted setting. Mixed finite element formulations, which rely on
compatibility
of the finite element spaces involved, are one such situation. Compatibility here means that, compared to other methods, additional properties of the PDE solution carry over from the continuous to the discrete level. The most prominent example is local conservation.

In this paper we consider the mixed Poisson problem as a model problem for a mixed finite element formulation with additional
compatibility
. We expect that several of the ideas presented here can be extended to other mixed problems, such as the Stokes problem.

Several papers in the literature have considered the use of
H(\operatorname{div})
-conforming or divergence preserving finite elements in an unfitted setting for the mixed Poisson, the Darcy or the Stokes problem.
For the lowest order Raviart-Thomas elements an unfitted mixed finite element method has been proposed and analyzed for the Darcy interface problem in the 2D case in
[
12
]
. An immersed finite element method for a similar interface problem has recently been treated in
[
20
]
. Both formulations do not require ghost-penalty stabilization, but are restricted to the lowest order case. In
[
34
,
11
]
higher order Raviart-Thomas elements are considered, but a ghost-penalty stabilization is applied which pollutes the conservation properties of the method at least in the vicinity of cut elements.
In the context of Stokes problems, the stabilization of the pressure variable in unfitted finite element formulations has been considered in
[
29
,
21
,
17
]
. However, in these discretizations, the underlying body-fitted Stokes discretization is not locally mass-conservative in the first place.
Recently,
[
28
]
have considered a discretization for Stokes that is exactly divergence-free (and pressure robust) in the body-fitted case. For the unfitted case, they use a pressure stabilization and thus preserve the divergence-free property only in the vicinity of cut elements.

In
[
9
]
, a low order discretization of the Stokes problem is introduced which imposes the divergence-free constraint on the entire active mesh. Since the underlying velocity-pressure pair is
compatible
, the discrete solution is exactly divergence-free, stable and robust in the cut configuration without additional ghost-penalty-type stabilization that involves the pressure variable.
The use of ghost penalties to stabilize a discretization in a way that does not pollute the conservation balance has recently been achieved in
[
13
]
, where the authors consider a mixed unfitted finite element method for the Darcy problem.

In this work we use similar ideas as in
[
13
]
and
[
9
]
to achieve stability without the need for ghost penalties or cell agglomeration.

Main contributions

In this manuscript, we present a new unfitted mixed finite element method with an enforcement of the divergence constraint that avoids the need for ghost penalties or cell agglomeration on the scalar variable.
The resulting scheme introduces an inconsistency. We show that this inconsistency does not affect the accuracy of the flux variable which allows us to apply a post-processing to the scalar variable to obtain optimal convergence rates for both variables and even superconvergence after post-processing of the scalar variable.
We also discuss the use of hybridization and Neumann boundary conditions.

Structure of the paper

In
Section

2
, we introduce the problem, i.e. unfitted meshes and the PDE problem, as well as some further preliminary considerations.
The main method is presented in
Section

3
and arises from a seemingly inconsistent modification of a simple, but not stable, unfitted mixed formulation. To deal with the inconsistency, a simple post-processing, similar to that used in usual mixed methods for Poisson or Darcy problems, is applied.
In
Section

4
the method without post-processing is analysed in terms of a full a priori error analysis.
Section

5
then introduces and analyses two possible post-processing schemes which lead to optimal, i.e., superconvergent approximations of the primal unknown.

Further modifications of the main method with computational advantages or a broader range of applications are given in
Section

6
. Finally,

Section

7
discusses numerical results and computational aspects of the discussed methods.

2.1.
Unfitted geometry, computational meshes and patches

We consider a PDE problem posed on an unfitted open bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},~d=2,3
with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
.
Unfitted
means that
\Omega
is not parametrized by the computational mesh that is used for the finite element approximation of the PDE solution.

Let
\widetilde{\Omega}
be a background domain, sufficiently large such that
\overline{\Omega}\subseteq\widetilde{\Omega}
and let
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}=\{T\}
be a shape regular, simplicial and quasi-uniform triangulation of
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
The set of elements that overlap with
\Omega
is called
the active mesh
, while the set of elements that intersect with
\partial\Omega
is called
the cut mesh
:

{\mathcal{T}_{h}}=\{T\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\mid\meas_{d}(T\cap\Omega)>0\},\quad\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}=\{T\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}\mid\meas_{d-1}(T\cap\Gamma)>0\}.

We denote the domains corresponding to the active and cut mesh as
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
and
\Omega^{\Gamma}
, respectively, and by
\mathcal{T}_{h}^{i}={\mathcal{T}_{h}}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}
we denote the set of interior
uncut
elements with associated domain
\Omega^{\text{int}}
.
Given a subset
S_{h}=\{T\}\subseteq{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, we denote the set of interior element interfaces as
{\mathcal{F}_{h}}(S_{h})
and denote
{\mathcal{F}_{h}}={\mathcal{F}_{h}}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})
. A facet patch, consisting of the two adjacent elements of a facet
F
is denoted as
\omega_{F}
.

The local mesh size of a mesh element
T\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}
is defined as
h_{T}=\diam(T)\coloneqq\max_{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in T}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\|_{2}
and the global mesh size is
h=\max_{T\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}h_{T}
.

As trimmed cut elements
T\cap\Omega,~T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}
may become arbitrary small and may suffer from shape irregularity, we will group together sets of neighboring elements in
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
in the vicinity of
\partial\Omega
into disjoint patches. Each of these patches
\mathcal{T}_{\omega}
with associated domain
\omega
contains at least one
root element

T_{\omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{i}
. The interior facets of a patch are denoted as
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\omega}:={\mathcal{F}_{h}}(\mathcal{T}_{\omega})
. Loosely speaking, these patches will allow us to distribute “stability” from interior elements to cut elements (through stabilizations acting on its interior facets). We denote by
\mathcal{C}_{h}=\{\mathcal{T}_{\omega}\}
the set of disjoint patches and define the set of all patch-interior facets
\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\mathcal{C}}:=\bigcup_{\mathcal{T}_{\omega}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}}{\mathcal{F}_{h}}(\mathcal{T}_{\omega})
. All elements that are not cut by
\partial\Omega
or directly adjacent to cut elements form trivial patches
\mathcal{T}_{\omega}=\{T\}
.
We will make the following assumption on
\mathcal{C}_{h}
.

Assumption 1
.

The set of disjoint patches
\mathcal{C}_{h}=\{\mathcal{T}_{\omega}\}
ensures that for each element
T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}
there is a
patch
\mathcal{T}_{\omega}\in\mathcal{C}_{h}
so that
T\in\mathcal{T}_{\omega}
.
Further, the number of elements in a patch is uniformly bounded, s.t. from every element in a patch
\mathcal{T}_{\omega}
the number of
facets
F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\omega}
that needs to be crossed to approach
the root element
T_{\omega}
is also uniformly bounded (uniformly in
\mathcal{T}_{\omega}
, the
cut position and
h
).

For the construction of according patches we refer the interested reader to the
works of Badia and Verdugo on the
aggregated
finite element method, see
e.g.
[
2
]
. For a sketch of the resulting mesh and patch configurations
see also
Fig.

.

2.2.
Notation for finite element spaces

On a set of elements
\mathcal{S}_{h}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{h}
with associated
domain
S
we denote the space
of piecewise polynomials up to degree
k
on that submesh as

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\{v\in L^{2}(S)\mid v|_{T}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}(T),~T\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\},

where
\mathcal{P}^{k}(T)
is the space of scalar
polynomials up to degree
k
on
T
.
Similarly we denote the space of Raviart-Thomas functions up to degree
k
by

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})

\displaystyle:=\{v_{h}\in H(\operatorname{div};S)\mid v_{h}|_{T}\in\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}^{k}(T),T\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\},

where
\mathcal{R}\mathcal{T}^{k}(T)=[\mathcal{P}^{k}(T)]^{d}+\mathcal{P}^{k}(T)\cdot\mathbf{x}
is the local Raviart-Thomas space so that
\operatorname{div}\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})=\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})
. Note that
in
\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})
normal-continuity holds. Breaking up
normal-continuity leads to the broken Raviart-Thomas space that we denote by

\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}_{-}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})
.
We further define by
\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T},k}:[H^{1}(S)]^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})
and
\Pi^{Q,k}:L^{2}(S)\to\mathbb{P}^{k}(\mathcal{S}_{h})
the usual Raviart-Thomas and
L^{2}
interpolation operators and drop the index
k
if the polynomial degree is clear from the context.
The introduced spaces and operators are well-known, well-understood and can be found in standard literature, e.g.
[
4
]
.

2.3.
Notation for inner products, norms, inequalities up to constants

By
(\cdot,\cdot)_{S}
we denote the
L^{2}(S)
inner product on a domain
S
and by
\|\cdot\|_{S}
the corresponding
L^{2}(S)
norm.
We use the notation
a\lesssim b
if there exists a constant
c>0
, independent of the mesh size and mesh-interface cut position, such that
a\leq cb
. Similarly, we use
\gtrsim
if
a\geq cb
, and
a\simeq b
if both
a\lesssim b
and
b\lesssim a
holds.

2.4.
Model problem

We consider Poisson’s equation on
\Omega
with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
: For a given functions
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,
p_{D}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
, find
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
so that

(3a)

\displaystyle-\operatorname{div}(\nabla p)=\hphantom{-}f\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\qquad p=p_{D}\text{ on }\Gamma.

The problem can be rewritten as a system of first order equations: Find
{u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
so that

(3b)

\displaystyle u-\nabla p=0\text{ in }\Omega,\qquad\operatorname{div}u=-f\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\qquad p=p_{D}\text{ on }\Gamma.

For the numerical treatment in the following we will focus on (
3b
) as it allows to put an emphasis on the
conservation
of the (approximation of the) flux
u
.

We will focus on Dirichlet boundary conditions for simplicity in the main part of this manuscript and discuss the Neumann case in
Section

6.1
.

2.5.
Weak formulation

As a basis for a finite element discretization we consider a variational formulation of (
3b
).
With
\Sigma:=H(\operatorname{div},\Omega)
and
Q:=L^{2}(\Omega)
,
the weak formulation of (
3b
) reads:
For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
p_{D}\in H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
find
(u,p)\in\Sigma\times Q
s.t.

(C-a)

\displaystyle a(u,v)+b(v,p)

\displaystyle=\langle v\cdot n,p_{D}\rangle

\displaystyle\forall v\in\Sigma,

(C-b)

\displaystyle b(u,q)

\displaystyle=-(f,q)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall q\in Q.

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the duality pairing between
H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
and
H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma)
. The bilinear forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
and
b(\cdot,\cdot)
are defined as

(4)

\displaystyle a(u,v)

\displaystyle=(u,v)_{\Omega},\qquad b(u,q)=(\operatorname{div}u,q)_{\Omega},\quad u,v\in\Sigma,q\in Q.

We identify the bilinear forms
a(\cdot,\cdot)
and
b(\cdot,\cdot)
with the corresponding operators
a:\Sigma\to\Sigma^{\ast}
and
b:\Sigma\to Q^{\ast}
.

2.6.
Subproblems arising from a space decomposition

Defining the divergence-free subspace of
\Sigma
as

(5)

\Sigma^{0}:=\ker b=\{v\in\Sigma\mid b(v,q)=0~\forall q\in Q\}=\{v\in\Sigma\mid\operatorname{div}v=0\text{ in }\Omega\}

we obtain the orthogonal space decomposition
\Sigma=\Sigma^{0}\oplus_{a}\Sigma^{\perp}{\!}
where

\Sigma^{\perp}{\!}
is the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthogonal (i.e.
a
-orthogonal) complement of
\Sigma^{0}
.
We can now reformulate the continuous problem as the task of finding the three components
u^{0}\in\Sigma^{0}
,
u^{\perp}\in\Sigma^{\perp}{\!}
and
p\in Q
solving three subproblems, s.t.
(u^{0}+u^{\perp},p)
solves (
C-a
)–(
C-b
):

(C-1)

\displaystyle\text{Find }u^{0}\,\in\Sigma^{0}\,,\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle a(u^{0},v^{0})

\displaystyle=(v^{0}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\forall v^{0}\in\Sigma^{0},

(C-2)

\displaystyle\text{Find }u^{\perp}\in\Sigma^{\perp}{\!},\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle b(u^{\perp},q)

\displaystyle=-(f,q)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall q\in Q,

(C-3)

\displaystyle\text{Find }p^{\hphantom{\perp}}\in Q^{\hphantom{\perp}},\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle b(v^{\perp},p)

\displaystyle=(v^{\perp}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}-a(u^{\perp},v^{\perp})

\displaystyle\forall v^{\perp}\in\Sigma^{\perp}{\!}.

We will exploit this characterization of the mixed formulation in the remaining considerations of this work to investigate discrete variational formulations, especially with respect to their consistency.

We will then adapt the discrete variational formulation by replacing

,

and

by discrete versions

,

and

3.1.
Restricted mixed FEM

We set

\Sigma_{h}=\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h})\subset H(\operatorname{div},\Omega^{\mathcal{T}})

and

Q_{h}=\mathbb{P}^{k}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})\subset L^{2}(\Omega^{\mathcal{T}})

and start with a straight-forward application of mixed finite element methods in an unfitted setting. We take the finite element spaces w.r.t. the active mesh
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, but restrict the integrals to
\Omega
. Hence, we denote this method as
restricted
mixed FEM.
The discretization reads as: Find
(u_{h},p_{h})\in\Sigma_{h}\times Q_{h}
such that for all
(v_{h},q_{h})\in\Sigma_{h}\times Q_{h}
there holds

(R)

\displaystyle a(u_{h},v_{h})+b(v_{h},p_{h})=(v_{h}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma},\qquad b(u_{h},q_{h})=-(f,q_{h})_{\Omega}.

According to the orthogonal decomposition

\Sigma_{h}=\Sigma_{h}^{0}\oplus_{a}\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}

with the discrete subspaces

\Sigma_{h}^{0}:=\ker b=\{v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}\mid\operatorname{div}v_{h}=0\}
and
\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}:=\{v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}\mid a(v_{h},w_{h})=0~\forall w_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}^{0}\}
,
we can reinterpret problem (
R
) as the three subproblems:

(R-1)

\displaystyle\text{Find }u_{h}^{0\,}\in\Sigma_{h}^{0},\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle a(u_{h}^{0},v_{h}^{0})

\displaystyle=(v_{h}^{0}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}^{0}\in\Sigma_{h}^{0},

(R-2)

\displaystyle\text{Find }u_{h}^{\perp}\in\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!},\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle b(u_{h}^{\perp},q_{h})

\displaystyle=-(f,q_{h})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},

(R-3)

\displaystyle\text{Find }\,p_{h\,}\in\!Q_{h},\text{s.t. }

\displaystyle b(v_{h}^{\perp},p_{h})

\displaystyle=(v_{h}^{\perp}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}-a(u_{h}^{\perp},v_{h}^{\perp})

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}^{\perp}\in\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}.

Let us take a look at these three subproblems to characterize the stability of (
R
).
Stability of subproblem (
R-1
) follows directly from coercivity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
on
\Sigma_{h}^{0}
w.r.t.
\|\cdot\|_{H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)}
.
With
v_{h}^{0}=u_{h}^{0}
in (
R-1
) we have
\|u_{h}^{0}\|_{H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)}\leq\|p_{D}\|_{H^{1\!/\!2}(\Gamma)}
and with
q_{h}=\operatorname{div}u_{h}^{\perp}\in Q_{h}
in (
R-2
) we obtain
\|\operatorname{div}u_{h}^{\perp}\|_{\Omega}\leq\|f\|_{\Omega}
.
This yields control of
u_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
by the r.h.s. data only in the (unsatisfactorily) weak norm
\|u_{h}^{0}\|_{\Omega}+\|\operatorname{div}u_{h}\|_{\Omega}
. To obtain control (and hence finally error bounds) in
\|\cdot\|_{\Omega}
(or
\|\cdot\|_{H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)}
) we are missing an estimate
\|\operatorname{div}v_{h}^{\perp}\|_{\Omega}\gtrsim\|v_{h}^{\perp}\|_{\Omega}
for all
v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}
. More obviously problematic is the stability of (
R-3
) which requires a stability result of the form

(6)

\inf_{q_{h}\in Q_{h}\setminus\{0\}}\sup_{v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(v_{h},q_{h})}{\|v_{h}\|_{H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)}\|q_{h}\|_{\Omega}}>c_{R}

with a constant
c_{R}>0
. One can indeed easily show that (
6
) holds, cf.
Lemma

15
in
Appendix

A
. With standard saddle point theory, cf.
[
2
]
, this implies unique solvability of (
R
) and stability in
\|u_{h}\|_{H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)}+\|p_{h}\|_{\Omega}
for a constant
c_{R}
. However, the constant
c_{R}
will
not be robust w.r.t. the cut position
and may become arbitrary small, which renders the method practically useless.

In the next sections we derive a modified scheme tailored to fix the problem with the inf-sup constant.

3.2.
The discrete bilinear form
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)

Motivated by the insufficient robustness of
b(\cdot,\cdot)
w.r.t. the inf-sup condition, we modify the bilinear form
b(\cdot,\cdot)
by substituting the previous domain of integration
\Omega
with the domain of the active mesh
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
:

(7)

b_{h}(v_{h},p_{h}):=(\operatorname{div}v_{h},p_{h})_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}.

The same type of modification for the setting of a low-order Stokes discretization has been considered in
[
9
]
.
By construction, this form does not depend on the local cut configuration and will allow to provide control on the active mesh. Further, there holds
\Sigma_{h}^{0}=\ker b=\ker b_{h}
so that the reduced problem on
\Sigma_{h}^{0}
like (
R-1
) will not be affected by this change.
Subproblems (
R-2
) and (
R-3
) will obviously
be affected severely. For (
R-2
) we will change the r.h.s. to
adapt for the change in the integration domain below in
Section

3.3
.
For (
R-3
) we will accept the fact that the resulting discrete
approximation of the primal unknown
p
will be inconsistent. In

Section

3.6
we will make sense of the inconsistent discrete
approximation of
p
and will afterwards re-obtain a consistent approximation from post-processings discussed in
Section

5
.

3.3.
The extended r.h.s. data
f_{h}

In the discretization below we will replace
b(\cdot,\cdot)
by
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
which changes the integration domain for the discrete conservation equation. Correspondingly, we need to adjust the r.h.s. integral and require a proper integrand
f_{h}:\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we assume to be known.
This is reasonable, as we can typically consider to be in one of two situations:

•

In the first case the function
f
is given as a finite element function on the background mesh, because it stems from a coupling to a different (unfitted) discrete field or it is prescribed in closed form anyway.

•

In the second case
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is not given with a proper extension to
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
and the discrete extension has to be constructed explicitly, cf.
Section

3.4

Why an extension of
f
to
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
is necessary is further explained in
Remark

1
.

3.4.
Ghost penalty stabilization

For the restricted mixed FEM we have seen that the bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)
suffices for stability w.r.t.
L^{2}(\Omega)
, respectively
H(\operatorname{div};\Omega)
.
However, there are good reasons to ask for control on the whole active mesh, i.e. in
L^{2}(\Omega^{\mathcal{T}})
, respectively
H(\operatorname{div};\Omega^{\mathcal{T}})
. For instance, this can be crucial for conditioning or finite element post-processing. This control can be obtained by adding a proper stabilization term to the bilinear form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the
ghost penalty
(GP) stabilization, cf.
[
6
]
.
Another application of the GP stabilization in the context of this work is to obtain a proper extension of the r.h.s. data
f_{h}
to the whole active mesh
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
in the case where
f
is only given on
\Omega
, cf.
Section

3.3
.

We denote a GP stabilization, which is a symmetric non-negative bilinear form for a generic piecewise polynomial finite element space
R_{h}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{k}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})
(respectively
R_{h}\subseteq[\mathbb{P}^{k}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})]^{d}
) by
j_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
with corresponding semi-norm
|\cdot|_{j}
.
Although different GP formulations exist, we restrict to the set of methods that split into facet contributions, so that

j_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\ast}}j_{F}(u_{h},v_{h})
, where

\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\ast}
is a set of facets and
j_{F}(\cdot,\cdot)
is a

We will make use of the GP stabilization in the following in up to three occasions:
First, to potentially enlarge the domain of control. For

(8)

Note that we also allow

Second, for the generic construction of

Finally, in

3.5.
The unfitted mixed FEM

Bringing the previous motivation into play, we formulate the following unfitted mixed FEM problem: Find

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h},v_{h})+b_{h}(v_{h},\bar{p}_{h})

\displaystyle=(v_{h}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h},

(M-b)

\displaystyle b_{h}(u_{h},q_{h})

\displaystyle=-(f_{h},q_{h})_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}.

As before, we split the problem into three subproblems.
However, with

\displaystyle\text{Find }u_{h}^{0}\,\in\Sigma_{h}^{0}\text{, s.t. }a_{h}(u_{h}^{0},v_{h}^{0})

\displaystyle=(v_{h}^{0}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle\forall v_{h}^{0}\in\Sigma_{h}^{0},

(M-2)

\displaystyle\text{Find }u_{h}^{\perp}\in\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}\text{, s.t. }b_{h}(u_{h}^{\perp},q_{h})

\displaystyle=-(f_{h},q_{h})_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h},

(M-3)

\displaystyle\text{Find }\bar{p}_{h}^{\hphantom{\perp}}\in Q_{h}\text{, s.t. }b_{h}(v_{h}^{\perp},\bar{p}_{h})

\displaystyle=(v_{h}^{\perp}\cdot n,p_{D})_{\Gamma}-a_{h}(u_{h}^{\perp},v_{h}^{\perp})

The advantage of this discretization over the restricted mixed FEM (

providing proper control on the whole active mesh. This comes at the price of an inconsistency that is discussed in the next section.

Remark 1
.

From (

) it becomes clear that we need a proper extension of the r.h.s. data

to
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
to obtain a consistent discretization. If

is only extended trivially, i.e. by zero, outside of

with a discontinuous r.h.s. (unless

which would result in deteriorated convergence order of expected order

3.6.

We will analyze potential inconsistencies resulting from our modifications by sequentially reviewing problems (

While (

Remark 2

\|u_{h}\|_{a_{h}}\simeq\|u_{h}\|_{\Sigma}
(and
\|u_{h}\|_{\Omega,j}\simeq\|u_{h}\|_{\Omega_{\gamma}}
) on

,
follows directly from (

).

Remark 6
.

Often, for the analysis of the mixed Poisson problem in a geometrically fitted setting a stronger,
H^{1}
-type, norm on
Q_{h}
and a weaker,
L^{2}
-type, norm on
\Sigma_{h}
is used. To achieve this partial integration on the

-term is applied in the stability (and continuity) analysis. We will not do this here as after partial integration on

we would need control on
\Sigma_{h}
in

which we can only provide for
\gamma_{u}>0
which we don’t want to restrict the analysis to. We discuss consequence of this choice and potential improvements for the setting with stronger norms and
\gamma_{u}>0
in more detail in Remark
8
below.

4.3.
A-priori error analysis for
u-u_{h}

We start with the stability results that the method has been tailored for in the next two lemmas.

Lemma 3

(Kernel-coercivity)
.

a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive on
\Sigma_{h}^{0}
w.r.t.

\|\cdot\|_{a_{h}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\Sigma}
.

Proof.

For
u_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}^{0}

a_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})=a_{h}(u_{h},u_{h})+\|\operatorname{div}u_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}=\|u_{h}\|^{2}_{a_{h}}\simeq\|u_{h}\|^{2}_{\Sigma}.
∎

Lemma 4

(LBB-type inf-sup stability on
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
)
.

There holds the inf-sup condition

(16)

\inf_{\bar{p}_{h}\in Q_{h}}\sup_{u_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}}\frac{b_{h}(u_{h},\bar{p}_{h})}{\|u_{h}\|_{\Sigma}\|\bar{p}_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}}\geq c>0

for a constant
c
that is independent of
h
and local cut configurations. Hence, the saddle-point problem (
M-a
) – (
M-b
) is well-posed (in the corresponding norms).

Proof.

The proof follows standard arguments, cf.
[
4
, Section 7.1.2]
. Slight adaptations are necessary to account for the fact that the domain
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
depends on
h
. For completeness we add a complete proof in
Appendix

C
.
∎

Both stability results together with the approximation of the r.h.s. yield a first quasi-best approximation estimate for the discretization error.

Lemma 5

(Error estimate for
u_{h}
)
.

Let
(u,p)\in\Sigma\times Q
be the solution to (
C-1
) –
(
C-3
) and
(u_{h},\bar{p}_{h})\in\Sigma_{h}\times Q_{h}
the
discrete solution to (
M-1
) –
(
M-3
). Then, there holds

(17)

\displaystyle\!\!\|u\!-\!u_{h}\|_{\Omega}\!+\!\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u_{h}|_{j}\!+\!\|\bar{p}_{h}\!-\!\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\!\!\lesssim\!\!\inf_{v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}}\!\!\|u\!-\!v_{h}\|_{\Omega}\!+\!|v_{h}|_{j}\!+\!\|\operatorname{div}v_{h}\!\!-\!f_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\!.\!\!

If
\gamma_{u}>0
we further have

(18)

\displaystyle\!\!\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\!\!\!\!-\!u_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\!+\!\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u_{h}|_{j}\!+\!\|\bar{p}_{h}\!-\!\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\!\!\lesssim\!\!\inf_{v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}}\!\!\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\!\!\!\!-\!v_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\!+\!|v_{h}|_{j}\!+\!\|\operatorname{div}v_{h}\!\!-\!f_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\!,\!\!

where
u^{\mathcal{E}}
is the
Sobolev extension of
u
(with
u^{\mathcal{E}}=\nabla p^{\mathcal{E}}=\nabla(\mathcal{E}p)
).

Proof.

We will first bound the
p
-error in terms of the
u
-error:
Let
v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
be arbitrarily. From the inf-sup condition in the previous lemma we know that there is a
w_{h}^{\ast}\in\Sigma_{h}^{\perp}{\!}
so that

\displaystyle\|\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}{\|w_{h}^{\ast}\|_{\Sigma}}\lesssim{b_{h}(w_{h}^{\ast},\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{=}}{b_{h}(w_{h}^{\ast},\bar{p}_{h})-b(w_{h}^{\ast},p)}

\displaystyle\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:consistency}}}{{=}}{a(u,w_{h}^{\ast})-a_{h}(u_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})}\lesssim{a(u-u_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})-\gamma_{u}j_{h}(u_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})}

\displaystyle\qquad=\!{a(u-v_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})\!+\!a(v_{h}-u_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})+\!\gamma_{u}j_{h}(v_{h}-u_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})-\!\gamma_{u}j_{h}(v_{h},w_{h}^{\ast})}

(19a)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\|\bar{p}_{h}\!-\!\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\lesssim\|u-v_{h}\|_{\Omega}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|v_{h}|_{j}+\|v_{h}-u_{h}\|_{\Omega,j}

For the latter term, the discrete error, we have with
w_{h}\coloneqq v_{h}-u_{h}
that there holds

\displaystyle\|w_{h}

\displaystyle\|_{\Omega,j}^{2}=a(v_{h}-u_{h},w_{h})+\gamma_{u}j_{h}(v_{h}-u_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle=a(u-u_{h},w_{h})-\gamma_{u}j_{h}(u_{h},w_{h})+a(v_{h}-u,w_{h})+\gamma_{u}j_{h}(v_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:consistency}}}{{=}}b_{h}(w_{h},\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p)+a(v_{h}-u,w_{h})+\gamma_{u}j_{h}(v_{h},w_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\operatorname{div}w_{h}\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\|\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{\Omega,j}^{2}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}~(\left\|v_{h}-u\right\|_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma_{u}|v_{h}|_{j}^{2})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\leq}}\eta\|\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}+\eta^{-1}\left\|\operatorname{div}w_{h}\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}^{2}+\left\|v_{h}-u\right\|_{\Omega}^{2}+\gamma_{u}|v_{h}|_{j}^{2}

for any
\eta>0
, where we used a kickback argument and Young’s inequality in the last step. Now using (
19a
) and choosing
\eta>0
sufficiently small to absorb
\left\|w_{h}\right\|_{\Omega,j}
with another kickback argument, we obtain

(19b)

\displaystyle\left\|v_{h}-u_{h}\right\|_{\Omega,j}\lesssim\left\|u-v_{h}\right\|_{\Omega}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|v_{h}|_{j}+\left\|\operatorname{div}(v_{h}-u_{h})\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

Hence, we have

\displaystyle\left\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\!-\!u_{h}\right\|_{\Omega}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|u_{h}|_{j}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\!-\!v_{h}\right\|_{\Omega}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|v_{h}|_{j}+\left\|v_{h}\!-\!u_{h}\right\|_{\Omega,j}+\left\|\operatorname{div}(v_{h}\!-\!u_{h})\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\!\!\!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:intermediate:b}}}{{\lesssim}}\left\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\!-\!v_{h}\right\|_{\Omega}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|v_{h}|_{j}+\left\|\operatorname{div}v_{h}\!-\!f_{h}\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}},

where we made use of
\operatorname{div}u_{h}=f_{h}
.
Now, let’s turn to the case
\gamma_{u}>0
. We have

\displaystyle\|u^{\mathcal{E}}-u_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u^{\mathcal{E}}-v_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}+\|v_{h}-u_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\lesssim\|u^{\mathcal{E}}-v_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}+\|v_{h}-u_{h}\|_{\Omega,j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:intermediate:b}}}{{\lesssim}}\|u^{\mathcal{E}}-v_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}|v_{h}|_{j}+\|\operatorname{div}v_{h}-f_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}.

∎

Note that the last term in
Eq.

19b
vanishes in geometrically fitted methods when restricting the approximation space for
v_{h}
to those fulfilling
\operatorname{div}v_{h}=\Pi^{Q}\operatorname{div}u=\Pi^{Q}f
there, cf.
Remark

4
. Restricting
v_{h}\in\Sigma_{h}
in the infimum on the r.h.s. to comply to
\operatorname{div}v_{h}=\operatorname{div}u_{h}=f_{h}
would allow us to obtain a similar result here as well. However, the approximation error term
\left\|f-f_{h}\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}
would then enter the approximation problem, which would lead to the same overall convergence result.

Next, we take a look at the approximation problem.

Theorem 6
.

There holds for
u\in H^{m}(\Omega)
with integer
m\in\{0,..,k+1\}

(20a)

\displaystyle\!\|u-u_{h}\|_{\Omega}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle|u_{h}|_{j}+\|\bar{p}_{h}\!-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\lesssim h^{m}\|u\|_{\!H^{m}\!(\Omega)}\!+\left\|\Pi^{Q}f^{\mathcal{E}}\!-\!f_{h}\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\!\!.

For
\gamma_{u}>0
there further holds

(20b)

\displaystyle\!\|u^{\mathcal{E}}-u_{h}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}+\gamma_{u}^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle|u_{h}|_{j}+\|\bar{p}_{h}\!-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\lesssim h^{m}\|u\|_{\!H^{m}\!(\Omega)}\!+\left\|\Pi^{Q}f^{\mathcal{E}}\!-\!f_{h}\right\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\!\!.

Proof.

Starting from the quasi-best approximation result in
Lemma

5
we only need to consider the approximation problem. Here, we set
v_{h}=\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}}u^{\mathcal{E}}
and directly obtain the following standard bound from the literature, cf. e.g.
[
4
]
,

\|u-\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}}u^{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\Omega}\leq\|u^{\mathcal{E}}-\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}}u^{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\lesssim h^{m}\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega^{\mathcal{T}})}

For the GP part we recall (
GP2
) and we apply results from the literature, cf. e.g.
[

, for the Lagrange interpolation operator

of order

\displaystyle|\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}}u^{\mathcal{E}}|_{j}

\displaystyle\leq|\Pi^{\mathcal{L}}u^{\mathcal{E}}|_{j}+|(\Pi^{\mathcal{L}}-\id)u^{\mathcal{E}}|_{j}+|(\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}}-\id)u^{\mathcal{E}}|_{j}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\Pi^{\mathcal{L}}u^{\mathcal{E}}|_{j}+\|(\Pi^{\mathcal{L}}-\id)u^{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}+\|(\Pi^{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{T}}-\id)u^{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}\lesssim h^{m}\|u^{\mathcal{E}}\|_{H^{m}(\Omega^{\mathcal{T}})}.

Exploiting

∎

Lemma 7
.

Assume that the domain

The structure of the proof follows the standard strategy of exploiting

\Omega

Let us start with formulating the dual problem which is (
C-a
)–(
C-b
) with data
p_{D}=0
and

and we denote the solution as

(D1)

\displaystyle(w,w^{\prime})_{\Omega}+(\operatorname{div}w^{\prime},z)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\forall w^{\prime}\in\Sigma

(D2)

\displaystyle(\operatorname{div}w,z^{\prime})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=((\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p),z^{\prime})_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\forall z^{\prime}\in Q.

There holds

\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|z\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|(\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p)\|_{\Omega}

due to the

-

Further choosing

(21a)

We will need a proper extension of

which we construct next.
Let

be an

such that

\displaystyle-\Delta w_{e}+\nabla q_{e}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\operatorname{div}w_{e}

\displaystyle=\chi_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}(\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p)

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Omega_{e},

\displaystyle w_{e}|_{\Gamma}

\displaystyle=w|_{\Gamma},\text{ on }\Gamma

\displaystyle w_{e}|_{\Gamma_{e}\setminus\Gamma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{n}\frac{\int_{\Omega_{e}\setminus\Omega}\chi_{\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}}(\bar{p}_{h}-\mathcal{E}_{h}^{0}p)}{|\Gamma_{e}\setminus\Gamma|}

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{e}\setminus\Gamma.

With constants that are independent of

Remark 8
.

In
Remark

6
we commented on an alternative choice of norms for the error estimates that is often considered in the geometrically fitted case.
As a consequence of the choice of norms chosen here, we obtain results for the error in
\bar{p}_{h}
in a weaker norm than usual possible (in the geometrically fitted setting). However, we consider
\bar{p}_{h}
as an intermediate variable only and regard the post-processed pressure

, introduced in the next section,
Section

, as the more important approximation to

which does not suffer from this potential suboptimality. More interesting is the fact that the dependency on the approximation error of

could benefit from a change of norms. In the numerical examples in

we investigate the dependency of the solution accuracy on the approximation of

and observe a dependency on the approximation error of

that seems to be weaker than the one predicted by the previous error analysis. Whether the dependency on the approximation error of

in the error analysis can be improved by a change of norms is left for future work.

5.
Post-processings

A common approach to achieve higher order convergence in mixed finite element methods are post-processing schemes, e.g. as in
[
37
]
, which exploit the accuracy of
u_{h}
by making use of
\nabla p=u
.
For the unfitted mixed scheme (
M-a
)–(
M-b
), these schemes have the additional potential of repairing the inconsistency of
\bar{p}_{h}
on cut elements. We propose two of such schemes.

5.1.
Element-local post-processing

First, we introduce the following element-local post-processing scheme:
For each
T\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
, find
p_{h}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{k+1}(T)
such that

(
T
-PP-a)

\displaystyle(\nabla p_{h}^{*},\nabla q_{h}^{*})_{T}

\displaystyle=(u_{h},\nabla q_{h}^{*})_{T}

\displaystyle\forall q_{h}^{*}\in\mathcal{P}^{k+1}(T),

(
T
-PP-b)

\displaystyle(p_{h}^{*},1)_{T}

\displaystyle=(\bar{p}_{h},1)_{T}

\displaystyle\text{ if }T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{i},

(
T
-PP-c)

\displaystyle(p_{h}^{*},1)_{T\cap\Gamma}

\displaystyle=(p_{D},1)_{T\cap\Gamma}

\displaystyle\text{ if }T\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{\Gamma}.

This yields a post-processed field
p_{h}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{k+1}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})
.
Here, on each element
T
the equation
\nabla p=u
is used to reconstruct
p_{h}^{*}
. This requires
u_{h}
to be accurate on
T\in{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
(not only on
T\cap\Omega
) and hence
\gamma_{u}>0
in (
M-a
). Obviously (

T

-PP-a
) only determines
p_{h}^{\ast}
only up to a constant. On uncut elements the quality of
\bar{p}_{h}
can be exploited to fix that constant, leading to (

T

-PP-b
) while on cut elements we make use of the knowledge of the Dirichlet data leading to (

T

-PP-b
).
In the analysis below, we will show that we achieve higher order convergence for
p_{h}^{*}
with this scheme.

Despite its simplicity this post-processing has two disadvantages: First, it relies on
u_{h}
to be accurate on
\Omega^{\mathcal{T}}
instead of only
\Omega
which requires
\gamma_{u}>0
and second it relies on Dirichlet data. In the next section we discuss an alternative that does not have these disadvantages.

5.2.
Patchwise post-processing

Now, we consider another post-processing scheme that operates on suitable patches instead of on single elements.

Again, we use
u_{h}
to reconstruct
p_{h}^{*}
. However, this time we formulate corresponding patch-problems w.r.t. patches as in
Section

2.1
and add a proper GP stabilization on each patch that is cut by the boundary. To fix the constant on each patch, we demand the mean value on the uncut elements to match with the mean value of
\bar{p}_{h}
on the uncut elements, which is known to be accurate.

On each patch, we find
p_{h}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{\omega})
so that

(
\omega
-PP-a)

\displaystyle(\nabla p_{h}^{*},\nabla q_{h}^{*})_{\Omega\cap\omega}+j_{h}^{\omega}(p_{h}^{*},q_{h}^{*})

\displaystyle=(u_{h},\nabla q_{h}^{*})_{\Omega\cap\omega}\quad\forall q_{h}^{*}\in\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(\mathcal{T}_{\omega}),

(
\omega
-PP-b)

\displaystyle(p_{h}^{*},1)_{\omega\cap\Omega^{\text{int}}}

\displaystyle=(\bar{p}_{h},1)_{\omega\cap\Omega^{\text{int}}}.

Here, we set

j_{h}^{\omega}(u_{h},v_{h})=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{h}^{\omega}}h^{-2}j_{F}(u_{h},v_{h})
as
the patch-wise GP bilinear form
with
j_{F}(u_{h},v_{h})
a GP stabilization bilinear form as considered in
Section

3.4
, however for polynomials up to degree
k+1
instead of only
k
.
Note that we introduced a scaling with
h^{-2}
here due to the fact that we need to stabilize an

H^{1}

6.
Extensions

In this section we want to discuss two extensions of the proposed method. First, we want to discuss how Neumann-type boundary conditions can be prescribed in the given framework. This will be of specific interest when considering a generalization of this discretization approach for Dirichlet boundary conditions for the Stokes (and Stokes-type) problems. Afterwards, we will discuss an equivalent hybridized mixed formulation for the Dirichlet case that allows to improve the computational efficiency of the unfitted mixed formulation.

6.1.

6.3.
External force on the flow

The model problem (

7.1.
Geometry handling

In the analysis in this work we do not consider the influence of the geometry handling on the overall error. However, an accurate handling of the unfitted geometry is crucial for the overall accuracy of the method, especially for higher order accuracy. Here, we use the isoparametric unfitted finite element method, cf.
[
23
]
, where a piecewise linear approximation of the level set function
\phi(x)
is used as a starting point. The piecewise linear approximation allows for robust numerical integration on unfitted geometries, but is only second order accurate. A mesh deformation is then computed and performed to map certain near zero iso-lines (or iso-surfaces) of the piecewise linear level set function towards corresponding iso-lines (or iso-surfaces) of the exact (or sufficiently accurate approximation of the) level set function. For details we refer to the literature, see
[
23
,
26
]
. As a consequence of the mesh deformation
\Theta_{h}:\widetilde{\Omega}\to\widetilde{\Omega}
, which itself is a finite element function
\Theta_{h}\in[\mathbb{P}^{k+1}(\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{h})\cap H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})]^{d}
we will have that
{\mathcal{T}_{h}}
contains curved elements and will adjust the finite element spaces accordingly.
\Theta_{h}^{-1}({\mathcal{T}_{h}})
is the corresponding mesh consisting of straight simplicial elements on which the finite element spaces are defined as usual, which we denote as
\hat{\Sigma}_{h}
,
\hat{Q}_{h}
,
\hat{F}_{h}
and
\hat{\Lambda}_{h}
, respectively.
For
\Sigma_{h}
we will apply the Piola transformation, while functions in
Q_{h}
,
F_{h}
and
\Lambda_{h}
are directly mapped, i.e.

\displaystyle\Sigma_{h}

\displaystyle=\{v=(\det D\Theta_{h})^{-1}D\Theta_{h}\cdot\hat{v})\circ\Theta_{h}^{-1},\hat{v}\in\hat{\Sigma}_{h}\},

\displaystyle Q_{h}

\displaystyle=\hat{Q}_{h}\circ\Theta_{h}^{-1},F_{h}=\hat{F}_{h}\circ\Theta_{h}^{-1},\Lambda_{h}=\hat{\Lambda}_{h}\circ\Theta_{h}^{-1}.

The Piola mapping for
\Sigma_{h}
ensures that the bilinear forms
b_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
c_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
(on the corresponding discrete spaces) are geometry-independent, i.e. they do not depend on
\Theta_{h}
, cf.
[
31
]
. This implies especially that there still holds normal-continuity and
|\det D\Theta_{h}|\operatorname{div}u_{h}\circ\Theta_{h}=\Pi(|\det D\Theta_{h}|\cdot f_{h}\circ\Theta_{h})
pointwise where
\Pi
is the
L^{2}
-projection onto
\hat{Q}_{h}
. Hence,
\operatorname{div}u_{h}
is the suitably scaled
L^{2}
-projection of
f_{h}\circ\Theta_{h}
onto
\hat{Q}_{h}
which preserves the crucial property
\operatorname{div}u_{h}=0
pointwise for
f_{h}=0
.

9
we display the condition number of the system matrix for the two cases. We observe several poles in the condition number for the unstabilized case. These poles are removed by the GP stabilization which guarantees that the conditon numbers are bounded from above by a constant independent of the cut position.
In
Fig.

9
we also show the cut configurations for one case with a moderate condition number and three cut configurations corresponding to three of the poles in the condition number for the unstabilized case. We conclude that the condition number is indeed unbounded for the unstabilized case and ghost penalty stabilization is necessary to ensure a bounded condition number
if no further measures for solving the linear systems are taken
. Let us stress, however, that the unboundedness of the condition number does not imply a stability issue for the scenario of exact arithmetics. It is known that in other cases, see e.g.
[
25
]
, specifically tailored preconditioning strategies allowed to solve linear systems robustly and efficiently even if condition numbers got unbounded in bad cut configurations. We leave the investigation of preconditioning strategies for the (
a(\cdot,\cdot)
-)unstabilized unfitted mixed Poisson problem – especially in view of the hybridized variant – for future work.
