Sums of two square-zero selfadjoint or skew-selfadjoint endomorphisms
Clément de Seguins Pazzis

Abstract
Let V V be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} , equipped with a symmetric or alternating non-degenerate bilinear form b b . When the characteristic of 𝔽 \mathbb{F} is not 2 2 , we characterize the endomorphisms u u of V V that split into u = a 1 + a 2 u=a_{1}+a_{2} for some pair ( a 1 , a 2 ) (a_{1},a_{2}) of b b -selfadjoint (respectively, b b -skew-selfadjoint) endomorphisms of V V such that ( a 1 ) 2 = ( a 2 ) 2 = 0 (a_{1})^{2}=(a_{2})^{2}=0 . In the characteristic 2 2 case, we obtain a similar classification for the endomorphisms of V V that split into the sum of two square-zero b b -alternating endomorphisms of V V when b b is alternating (an endomorphism v v is called b b -alternating whenever b ⁡ ( x , v ⁡ ( x ) ) = 0 b(x,v(x))=0 for all x ∈ V x\in V ). Finally, if the field 𝔽 \mathbb{F} is equipped with a non-identity involution, we characterize the pairs ( h , u ) (h,u) in which h h is a Hermitian form on a finite-dimensional space over 𝔽 \mathbb{F} , and u u is the sum of two square-zero h h -selfadjoint endomorphisms.

Introduction

Throughout,
\mathbb{N}
denotes the set of all nonnegative integers, and
\mathbb{N}^{*}
the set of all positive ones.

1.1
The problem

The present article is set in the general context of decompositions of elements of an algebra into
quadratic objects. Given an associative algebra
\mathcal{A}
over a field
\mathbb{F}
, an element
x\in\mathcal{A}

is called quadratic whenever it is annihilated by a polynomial of degree
2
, i.e.
x^{2}\in\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{F}}(1_{\mathcal{A}},x)
.
In
[
12
,
13
]
, we have characterized, in algebras of endomorphisms of finite-dimensional vector spaces,
the elements that split into
a+b
where
a
and
b
are quadratic elements with prescribed annihilating polynomials
of degree
2
. For example, when both polynomials equal
t^{2}
, this describes the
endomorphisms that are the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms (see
[
15
]
and
[
1
]
), when both equal

t^{2}-t
this describes the endomorphisms that are the sum of two idempotents (see
[
6
]
for the seminal results).
The main innovation in
[
12
,
13
]
allowed to deal with the case where at least one of the prescribed annihilating polynomials is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
.

In the present work, we wish to start a similar study in the context of vector spaces equipped with non-degenerate
symmetric or alternating bilinear forms. This topic is not entirely new, yet to this day the
research has been mostly concentrated in the study of decomposing elements of orthogonal or symplectic groups into products of two quadratic elements of those groups. The oldest result is a theorem of M.J. Wonenburger
[
17
]
, which states that in an orthogonal group over a field with characteristic distinct from
2
, every element is the product of two involutions. This result was generalized by Gow
[
4
]
to fields of characteristic
2
for orthogonal groups of regular quadratic forms (but not for orthogonal groups of symmetric bilinear forms); see also
[
2
,
7
]
.

A few years ago, de La Cruz
[
3
]
considered the problem of sums of square-zero matrices:
among several results, he showed that over the complex field every Hamiltonian matrix is the sum of two square-zero Hamiltonian matrices,
whereas a skew-Hamiltonian matrix is the sum of two square-zero skew-Hamiltonian matrices if and only if it is similar to its opposite.

Due to the appeal of group theory, decompositions into products tend to be more popular,
but we view decompositions into sums as equally interesting.

Now, let us be more precise about our aims. Throughout, all the vector spaces under consideration are assumed to be
finite-dimensional

(we will only restate this assumption in the main theorems, for reference purpose). All those vector spaces are over a fixed field
\mathbb{F}

whose characteristic we denote by
\chi(\mathbb{F})
.
We denote by
\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
the set of all monic irreducible polynomials of
\mathbb{F}[t]
,
and by
\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
the set of all
even
monic irreducible polynomials of
\mathbb{F}[t]
, i.e.
\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})=\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{F}[t^{2}]
.

Let
V
be a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
, and

b:V\times V\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}

be a bilinear form. We assume that
b
is either
symmetric
(
\forall(x,y)\in V^{2},\;b(x,y)=b(y,x)
)
or
alternating
(
\forall x\in V,\;b(x,x)=0
).
We say that
b
is skew-symmetric whenever
\forall(x,y)\in V^{2},\;b(y,x)=-b(x,y)
.
The alternating bilinear forms are the skew-symmetric ones if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
,
whereas if
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
an alternating form is necessarily symmetric but the converse does not hold in general.

In general, the radical of
b
is defined as

\operatorname{Rad}(b):=\{x\in V:\;\forall y\in V,\;b(x,y)=0\}.

We shall systematically assume that this radical is zero, i.e.
b
is
non-degenerate
.
Given a linear subspace
W
of
V
, its orthogonal under
b
is denoted by
W^{\bot_{b}}
, or more simply by
W^{\bot}

in lack of a possible confusion. Remember that
\dim W^{\bot}=\dim V-\dim W
, that
(W^{\bot})^{\bot}=W
, and that
V=W\oplus W^{\bot}

if and only if the restriction of
b
to
W^{2}
is non-degenerate (in which case we say that
W
is a
b
-regular
subspace).

Given an endomorphism
u
of
V
, there is a unique endomorphism of
V
, denoted by
u^{\star}
and called the
b
-adjoint of
u
, such that

\forall(x,y)\in V^{2},\;b\bigl(u^{\star}(x),y\bigr)=b\bigl(x,u(y)\bigr).

We say that
u
is
b
-selfadjoint
whenever
u^{\star}=u
, and that
u
is
b
-skew-selfadjoint

whenever
u^{\star}=-u
(of course, these two notions are equivalent if
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
).
We say that
u
is
b
-alternating
whenever
\forall x\in V,\;b(x,u(x))=0
, i.e. the bilinear form

(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u(y))
is alternating. If
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
b
is symmetric (respectively,

\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
and
b
is alternating), then
u
is
b
-alternating if and only if it is
b
-skew-selfadjoint
(respectively,
b
-selfadjoint). If
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
, every
b
-alternating endomorphism
is
b
-selfadjoint but the converse does not hold in general.

The sets of all
b
-selfadjoint,
b
-skew-selfadjoint and
b
-alternating endomorphisms are respectively denoted by

\mathcal{S}_{b+}
,
\mathcal{S}_{b-}
and
\mathcal{A}_{b}
. Obviously, they are linear subspaces (but not subalgebras!) of the algebra
\operatorname{End}(V)
of all endomorphisms
of
V
.

Now, we can state our main problem:

In each subspace
\mathcal{S}_{b+}
,
\mathcal{S}_{b-}
and
\mathcal{A}_{b}
,
characterize the elements that split into the sum of two square-zero elements.

To this end, the following notation will be useful:

Notation 1.1
.

Given a linear subspace
\mathcal{W}
of
\operatorname{End}(V)
, we denote by
\mathcal{W}^{[2]}

the subset of
\mathcal{W}
consisting of the elements of the form
a_{1}+a_{2}
, where
a_{1},a_{2}
are elements of
\mathcal{W}

such that
a_{1}^{2}=a_{2}^{2}=0
.

1.2
A review of the case where
W=\operatorname{End}(V)

At this point, it is important to explain what is exactly meant by “characterize” in the above problem.

So, let us first go back to the classical case of
\operatorname{End}(V)
where
V
is a (finite-dimensional) vector space.
In that case, the problem is invariant under replacing
u
with a conjugate endomorphism:
indeed, if
\varphi
is an automorphism of
V
and
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
for square-zero endomorphisms of
V
,
then
\varphi u\varphi^{-1}=b_{1}+b_{2}
where
b_{1}:=\varphi a_{1}\varphi^{-1}
and
b_{2}:=\varphi a_{2}\varphi^{-1}

are square-zero endomorphisms of
V
. Hence,
\operatorname{End}(V)^{[2]}
is a union of conjugacy classes: if we have (computable) invariants for conjugacy classes
then, in theory, belonging to
\operatorname{End}(V)^{[2]}
could be expressed in terms of these invariants only.

So, let us recall the relevant invariants for conjugacy classes in the algebra
\operatorname{End}(V)
.
A module over a ring is called of rank
1
when it is non-zero and generated by a single element.
Every module of rank
1
over
\mathbb{F}[t]
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
for a unique zero or nonconstant monic polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, which we call the minimal
polynomial of the said module; the module under consideration is finite-dimensional as a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
if and only if
p
is nonzero.

Let
u
be an endomorphism of a vector space
V
. By putting
p(t).x:=p(u)[x]
,
we enrich the vector space
V
into an
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module
V^{u}
.
If
V^{u}
is of rank
1
, it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
where
p
denotes the minimal polynomial of
u
.

In general
V^{u}
splits into the direct sum
V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r}

of non-zero submodules of rank
1
whose respective minimal polynomials are so that

p_{i+1}
divides
p_{i}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,r-1\mathclose{]\!]}
.
The polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
are then uniquely determined by
u
: they are its
invariant factors
.
They characterize the similarity (i.e. conjugacy) class of
u
, i.e. the orbit of
u
under the action
of the general linear group
\operatorname{GL}(V)
by
a.u:=aua^{-1}
.

Now, we can reformulate the classical result of Wang-Wu
[
15
]
and Botha
[
1
]
(see also
the appendix of
[
11
]
for an alternative proof):

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
.
Then
u
belongs to
\operatorname{End}(V)^{[2]}
if and only if each invariant factor of
u
is even or odd.

Alternatively, we can characterize similarity classes in terms of primary invariants
and so-called Jordan numbers. An
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module of rank
1
is called
primary
whenever
its minimal polynomial is a power of some (monic) irreducible polynomial.
Using the Chinese remainder theorem, one can split each
module of the form
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
into a direct sum of primary submodules.
It follows that
V^{u}
is isomorphic to a direct sum of primary modules:
then, the non-ordered list of minimal polynomials of the summands in such a primary decomposition depends only on the similarity class of
u
,
and these minimal polynomials are called the
primary invariants
of
u
.
Finally, we can recover those invariants as follows: for each
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
,
and each positive integer
k
, we denote by
n_{p,k}(u)
the number of such invariants that equal
p^{k}
.
The mapping
p(u)
induce an injective homomorphism of
\mathbb{F}[t]
-modules

\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}/\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k-1}.

Each one of those modules has a natural structure of vector space over the residue field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
,
and one checks that the integer
n_{p,k}(u)
is the dimension over
\mathbb{L}
of the cokernel of that module.
In other words,

n_{p,k}(u)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)}\Bigl(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}/\bigl(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k-1}+(\operatorname{Im}p(u)\cap\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k})\bigr)\Bigr).

Notation 1.2
.

Given a non-zero polynomial
p\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, we define its opposite polynomial as

p^{\mathrm{op}}:=(-1)^{\deg p}\,p(-t).

If
p
is monic and irreducible, then so is
p^{\mathrm{op}}
. Note in any case that
(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{\mathrm{op}}=p
(because
p^{\mathrm{op}}
has the same degree as
p
).

Using Jordan numbers, the previous theorem can be reformulated as follows:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{F}
, and let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
. Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The endomorphism
u
belongs to
\operatorname{End}(V)^{[2]}
.

(ii)

For every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and every integer
k>0
, one has

n_{p,k}(u)=n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},k}(u)
.

(iii)

For every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus(\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cup\{t\})
and every integer
k>0
, one has

n_{p,k}(u)=n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},k}(u)
.

(iv)

u
is similar to
-u
.

Now, we turn to the characteristic
2
case.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{F}
, and let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
. Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

The endomorphism
u
belongs to
\operatorname{End}(V)^{[2]}
.

(ii)

For every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus(\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cup\{t\})
and every odd integer
k\geq 1
, one has

n_{p,k}(u)=0
.

(iii)

For every non-zero eigenvalue
\lambda

of
u
in an algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}
, the Jordan cells of
u
for the eigenvalue
\lambda
are all even-sized.

Notice that, according to these theorems, every nilpotent endomorphism of a finite-dimensional vector space is the sum of two square-zero ones!

1.3
The viewpoint of pairs

In the context of spaces equipped with a bilinear form (except in very special cases) there are known sets of invariants
that characterize the orbits of the elements of

\mathcal{S}_{b+}
,
\mathcal{S}_{b-}
and
\mathcal{A}_{b}
under the action of the isometry group
\operatorname{Isom}(b)

by conjugation. This is relevant because our problem is invariant under conjugation.
Actually, the relevant invariants are not associated to the endomorphism
u
itself but to the pair
(b,u)
.

The following terminology will be of help:

Definition 1.3
.

Let
(\varepsilon,\eta)\in\{-1,1\}^{2}
and
(b,u)
be a pair consisting of a non-degenerate bilinear form
b

on a vector space
V
, and of an endomorphism
u
of
V
.
We say that
(b,u)
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair when:

•

b
is symmetric and
u
is
b
-selfadjoint, if
\varepsilon=\eta=1
;

•

b
is symmetric and
u
is
b
-alternating (i.e.
b
-skew-selfadjoint if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
), if
\varepsilon=1
and
\eta=-1
;

•

b
is alternating and
u
is
b
-alternating (i.e.
b
-selfadjoint if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
) if
\varepsilon=-1
and
\eta=1
;

•

b
is alternating and
u
is
b
-skew-selfadjoint if
\varepsilon=-1
and
\eta=-1
.

Hence, in an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)
:

•

\varepsilon
is the parity of
b
;

•

\eta
is the parity of
u
with respect to
b
(i.e.
u^{\star}=\eta u
);

•

\varepsilon\eta
is the parity of the bilinear form
(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u(y))
.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
(\varepsilon,\eta)\in\{1,-1\}^{2}
.
An
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)
, with underlying vector space
V
,
is said to have the
square-zero splitting property
whenever there exist
endomorphisms
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
of
V
such that
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
,
a_{1}^{2}=a_{2}^{2}=0
and

(b,a_{1})
and
(b,a_{2})
are
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs.

Next, we consider the notion of an induced pair.
Let
(b,u)
be an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair with underlying vector space
V
.
Assume that we have a linear subspace
W
of
V
such that
u(W)\subset W
.
Since
u
is
b
-selfadjoint or
b
-skew-selfadjoint, this leads to
u(W^{\bot_{b}})\subset W^{\bot_{b}}
.
It follows that
u
induces an endomorphism
\overline{u}
of the quotient space
W/(W\cap W^{\bot_{b}})
.
Moreover, since
W\cap W^{\bot_{b}}
is the radical of the bilinear form induced by
b
on
W
,
it turns out that
b
induces a non-degenerate bilinear form
\overline{b}
on
W/(W\cap W^{\bot_{b}})
.
It is easily checked that the pair
(\overline{b},\overline{u})
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair.
Note that if
W
is already
b
-regular, then this new pair is defined on
W
itself (with the usual identification between
W
and
W/\{0\}
).

Definition 1.5
.

Let
(b,u)
be an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair with underlying vector space
V
, and let

W
be a linear subspace of
V
that is stable under
u
. We denote by
(b,u)^{W}
the pair induced by
(b,u)

on the quotient space
W/(W\cap W^{\bot_{b}})
. We call it the pair
induced by
(b,u)
for
W
.

Remark 1
.

The square-zero splitting property is not inherited by induced pairs in general, yet in specific instances it is:
assume indeed that
(b,u)
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair, with underlying vector space
V
,
and assume that there is a splitting
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
where each
a_{i}
has square zero and is such that
(b,a_{i})
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair.
Let
W
be a linear subspace of
V
that is not only stable under
u
, but also under
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.
Denote by
\overline{u},\overline{a_{1}},\overline{a_{2}}
the endomorphisms of
W/(W\cap W^{\bot_{b}})

induced by
u,a_{1},a_{2}
, respectively. Then,
\overline{a_{1}}
and
\overline{a_{2}}
have square zero and sum up to
\overline{u}
, and of course

(b,a_{1})^{W}
and
(b,a_{2})^{W}
are
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs. It follows that
(b,u)^{W}
has the square-zero splitting property.

Next, given two bilinear forms
b
and
c
on respective vector spaces
V
and
W
,
remember that an isometry from
b
to
c
is a vector space isomorphism
f:V\overset{\simeq}{\longrightarrow}W

such that
c(f(x),f(y))=b(x,y)
for all
(x,y)\in V^{2}
.

Definition 1.6
.

Given endomorphisms
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
and
v\in\operatorname{End}(W)
, the pairs
(b,u)
and
(c,v)

are called
isometric
if there exists an isometry
f
from
b
to
c
such that
v=f\circ u\circ f^{-1}
.

This defines an equivalence relation on the collection of all pairs
(b,u)
consisting of a bilinear form
b
on a vector space
and of an endomorphism
u
of it.
One checks that if
(b,u)
and
(c,v)
are isometric pairs, and
(\varepsilon,\eta)
is an arbitrary element of
\{-1,1\}^{2}
,
then
(b,u)
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair if and only if
(c,v)
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair; also, in that case

(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property if and only if
(c,v)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Shortly, we will recall a complete set of invariants for the
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs
(b,u)
under isometry.
Before we do so, it is important that we also introduce the notion of the orthogonal direct sum of pairs.

Definition 1.7

(Orthogonal direct sum of pairs)
.

Let
(b,u)
and
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
be two
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs, with respective underlying vector spaces
V
and
V^{\prime}
.
Their orthogonal direct sum is the pair
(B,U)=(b,u)\bot(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
defined
with

B:((x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime}))\in(V\times V^{\prime})^{2}\longmapsto b(x,y)+b^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

and

U:(x,x^{\prime})\in V\times V^{\prime}\longmapsto\bigl(u(x),u^{\prime}(x^{\prime})\bigr),

i.e.
B
is the orthogonal direct sum
b\bot b^{\prime}
and
U
is the (external) direct sum
u\oplus u^{\prime}
.
Note that
(B,U)
is itself an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair.

Remark 2
.

Let
(b,u)
and
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
be
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs, with underlying spaces
V
and
V^{\prime}
,
each with the square-zero splitting property.
Let us split
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
and
u^{\prime}=a^{\prime}_{1}+a^{\prime}_{2}
where the
a_{i}
’s and
a^{\prime}_{j}
’s have square zero, and
(b,a_{i})
and
(b^{\prime},a^{\prime}_{i})
are

(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs.
Then,
u\oplus u^{\prime}=(a_{1}\oplus a^{\prime}_{1})+(a_{2}\oplus a^{\prime}_{2})
, the endomorphisms
a_{1}\oplus a^{\prime}_{1}
and
a_{2}\oplus a^{\prime}_{2}

have square zero, and
(b,a_{1})\bot(b^{\prime},a^{\prime}_{1})
and
(b,a_{2})\bot(b^{\prime},a^{\prime}_{2})
are
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs.
This shows that
(b,u)\bot(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
has the square-zero splitting property.

Beware that the converse does not hold in general. It is possible for
(b,u)\bot(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
to have the square-zero splitting property
without
(b,u)
nor
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
having this property.

There is however an interesting special situation which we will sometimes use.
Assume that we have an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)
with the square-zero splitting property, and consider corresponding square-zero endomorphisms

a_{1}
and
a_{2}
. Denote the underlying space of
(b,u)
by
V
, and assume that we have a splitting
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
into

b
-orthogonal subspaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
that are both stable under
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.
Then the induced pairs
(b,u)^{V_{1}}
and
(b,u)^{V_{2}}
, which are respectively defined on
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
(because they are
b
-regular subspaces), have the square-zero splitting property. In that case, we also note that
(b,u)\simeq(b,u)^{V_{1}}\bot(b,u)^{V_{2}}
.

1.4
A review of the classification of selfadjoint and skew-selfadjoint endomorphisms

We are ready to review the relevant invariants for the
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs.
The simplest case is the one where
\eta=1
.

1.4.1
Extension of a bilinear form

Let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
, whose degree we denote by
d
.
We consider the residue field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)

and the
\mathbb{F}
-linear form
e_{p}
on
\mathbb{L}
that takes the class
\overline{1}
to
1
, and the class
\overline{t^{k}}
to
0

for all
k\in\mathopen{[\![}1,d-1\mathclose{]\!]}
. The
\mathbb{F}
-bilinear form
(x,y)\in\mathbb{L}^{2}\mapsto e_{p}(xy)
is then non-degenerate, and hence
every
\mathbb{F}
-linear form on
\mathbb{L}
reads
x\mapsto e_{p}(x\alpha)
for a unique
\alpha\in\mathbb{L}
.

Let
W
be an
\mathbb{L}
-vector space equipped with an
\mathbb{F}
-bilinear form
B:W^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}

such that

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{L},\;\forall(x,y)\in W^{2},\quad B(\lambda x,y)=B(x,\lambda y).

For all
x,y
in
W
, the
\mathbb{F}
-linear form
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}\mapsto B(x,\lambda y)
can be written
as
\lambda\mapsto e_{p}(\lambda B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y))
for a unique
B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)\in\mathbb{L}
.
It is then easily found that
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is
\mathbb{F}
-bilinear; one also checks that
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is right-
\mathbb{L}
-linear.
To sum up,
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is defined by the following property :

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{L},\;\forall(x,y)\in W^{2},\;B(x,\lambda y)=e_{p}(\lambda B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)).

If
B
is symmetric (respectively, skew-symmetric) then so is
B^{\mathbb{L}}
,
and in that case
B^{\mathbb{L}}
turns out to be
\mathbb{L}
-bilinear (because it is already right-
\mathbb{L}
-linear),
and better still
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is non-degenerate if in addition
b
is non-degenerate.

There is a specific difficulty related to the characteristic
2
case.
Assume that
B
is not only alternating but that

B(x,\lambda x)=0
for all
x\in W
and all
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
.
Then, by letting
x\in W
, one finds that
e_{p}(\lambda B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,x))=B(x,\lambda x)=0

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
, which yields
B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,x)=0
.
Hence,
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is alternating. It is not true however that if
B
is alternating
then so is
B^{\mathbb{L}}
(there is a special difficulty when
\mathbb{F}
has characteristic
2
and
p
is even).

We will also need a variation of the above construction in case
p
is even.
So, assume that
p
is even and
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
The mapping that takes
q(t)
to
q(-t)
induces a non-identity involution
x\mapsto x^{\bullet}
of
\mathbb{L}

(that takes the class of
t
to its opposite). The subfield
\mathbb{K}:=\{x\in\mathbb{L}:x^{\bullet}=x\}

of
\mathbb{L}
has index
2
in
\mathbb{L}
(and the extension
\mathbb{K}-\mathbb{L}
is separable). Note that
\mathbb{K}
is simply the subfield of
\mathbb{L}
generated by the class of
t^{2}
, and that

\forall x\in\mathbb{L},\;e_{p}(x^{\bullet})=e_{p}(x)
.
Now, let
W
be an
\mathbb{L}
-vector space and
B:W^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
be an

\mathbb{F}
-bilinear form such that

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{L},\;\forall(x,y)\in W^{2},\;B(x,\lambda y)=B(\lambda^{\bullet}x,y).

Like before, there is a unique
\mathbb{F}
-bilinear mapping
B^{\mathbb{L}}:W^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{L}

such that

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{L},\;\forall(x,y)\in W^{2},\;B(x,\lambda y)=e_{p}(\lambda B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)).

Again,
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is right-
\mathbb{L}
-linear.
Now, say that
B
is symmetric (in which case set
\varepsilon:=1
) or alternating (in which case
\varepsilon:=-1
).
Then, for all
(x,y,\lambda)\in W^{2}\times\mathbb{L}
,

B(y,\lambda x)=B(\lambda^{\bullet}y,x)=\varepsilon B(x,\lambda^{\bullet}y)=\varepsilon e_{p}(\lambda^{\bullet}B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y))=e_{p}(\lambda\varepsilon B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)^{\bullet}).

It follows that

\forall(x,y)\in W^{2},\;B^{\mathbb{L}}(y,x)=\varepsilon B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)^{\bullet}.

We conclude that
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is Hermitian if
B
is symmetric, and skew-Hermitian if
B
is alternating.
In any case,
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is left-
\mathbb{L}
-semilinear.
In any of these cases,
B
is non-degenerate if and only if
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is non-degenerate.

Note finally that skew-Hermitian forms over
\mathbb{L}
are in natural one-to-one correspondence with Hermitian forms:
indeed we can choose
\eta\in\mathbb{L}\smallsetminus\{0\}
such that
\eta^{\bullet}=-\eta
(it suffices to take
\eta
as the class of
t
) and then
C\mapsto\eta C
yields a bijection between Hermitian forms on
W
and skew-Hermitian forms on
W
, and this bijection and its inverse bijection turn equivalent forms into equivalent forms.

1.4.2
Symmetric endomorphisms

We are ready to recall the classification of
(\varepsilon,1)
-pairs.

Let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
, and set
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.
Let
P:=(b,u)
be an
(\varepsilon,1)
-pair for some
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.
There,
u
is
b
-selfadjoint so one sees that
q(u)
is
b
-selfadjoint for all
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
,
and it classically turns out that
(\operatorname{Ker}q(u))^{\bot_{b}}=\operatorname{Im}q(u)
.

Let
k
be a positive integer. Consider the
\mathbb{F}
-bilinear form

\begin{cases}\bigl(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}\bigr)^{2}&\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}\\
(x,y)&\longmapsto b(x,p(u)^{k-1}[y])\end{cases}

on the vector space
\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}
.
Note that is is symmetric (respectively, skew-symmetric) if

b
is symmetric (respectively, skew-symmetric).

Let us look at its radical: clearly it is the intersection of
\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}

with the inverse image of
(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k})^{\bot_{b}}=\operatorname{Im}p(u)^{k}
under
p(u)^{k-1}
, and one easily
checks that this inverse image equals
\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k-1}+(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}\cap\operatorname{Im}p(u))
.
Hence, the preceding bilinear form induces a non-degenerate
\mathbb{F}
-bilinear form
\overline{b_{p,k}}
on the quotient space
V_{p,k}:=\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}/(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k-1}+(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}\cap\operatorname{Im}p(u)))
, i.e. on the cokernel of the mapping from

\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}
to
\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}/\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k-1}
induced by
p(u)
.
Remember that these quotient spaces are naturally seen as vector spaces over
\mathbb{L}
, and hence so does the said cokernel.
Since
u
is
b
-selfadjoint it turns out that

\overline{b_{p,k}}(x,\lambda y)=\overline{b_{p,k}}(\lambda x,y)
for all

\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
and all
(x,y)\in(V_{p,k})^{2}
.
Hence, we can consider the extended non-degenerate
\mathbb{L}
-bilinear form

P_{p,k}:=\overline{b_{p,k}}^{\mathbb{L}}
, which is symmetric if
\varepsilon=1
.
If
\varepsilon=1
, we say that
P_{p,k}
is the
quadratic invariant
of
P
with respect to
(p,k)

(in fact, this quadratic invariant should be defined not as
P_{p,k}
but as its equivalence “class”; however, in practice we prefer to
deal with
P_{p,k}
directly).

Assuming that
\varepsilon=-1
, we also check that

P_{p,k}
is actually alternating. Indeed, we start from the observation that

b(x,x)=0=b(x,u(x))
for all
x\in W
, in that case.
Then, for every integer
k\geq 0
, we gather that
b(x,u^{2k}(x))=b(u^{k}(x),u^{k}(x))=0

and
b(x,u^{2k+1}(x))=b(u^{k}(x),u(u^{k}(x)))=0
for all
x\in V
, and it follows that

b(x,q(u)[x])=0
for all
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
and all
x\in V
.
Hence,
\overline{b_{p,k}}(x,\lambda x)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{L}
and all
x\in V_{p,k}
.
As explained in the previous section, it follows that
P_{p,k}
is alternating (and hence symplectic).

From the above construction, it is clear that the equivalence class of the
\mathbb{L}
-bilinear form
P_{p,k}

depends only on the equivalence class of the pair
P=(b,u)
and on the pair
(p,k)
.
It turns out that the family of those equivalence classes, when
p
ranges over
\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and
k
ranges over the positive integers, is sufficient to understand
P
up to equivalence: the following theorem can be retrieved from Section 2 of
[
16
]

(there, the definition of the quadratic invariants is slightly different, but it is not difficult to see that the invariants defined by
Waterhouse correspond to ours up to multiplication with fixed nonzero scalars).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
, and assume that
\varepsilon=1
if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
Let
(b,u)
and
(c,v)
be
(\varepsilon,1)
-pairs.

The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

(b,u)
is isometric to
(c,v)
;

(ii)

For every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and every integer
k\geq 1
, the forms
(b,u)_{p,k}
and
(c,v)_{p,k}
are equivalent as bilinear forms over the residue field
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.

Moreover, there is little limitation on the potential invariants
(b,u)_{p,k}
.
More precisely, let
(a_{p,k})_{p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F}),k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
be a family in which,
for all

and all

is a

.
Then the following conditions are equivalent:

•

There exists an
(\varepsilon,1)
-pair

P_{p,k}\simeq a_{p,k}
for all

•

All the

\varepsilon=1

\varepsilon=-1
,
and only finitely many of them are nonzero.

Note that the Jordan numbers of

are easily retrieved from the forms

since

is simply the dimension over the residue field
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
of the underlying vector space of
(b,u)_{p,k}
.
For a
(-1,1)
-pair
(b,u)
, the form
(b,u)_{p,k}
is symplectic and hence
its equivalence class is entirely determined by the dimension of the underlying space (which must be even). Hence, in that case the Jordan numbers of

are sufficient to classify the pair

: this is expressed in the next theorem:

Theorem 1.5

(Scharlau
[
9

)
.

Let
(b,u)
and
(c,v)
be
(-1,1)

(i)

The pairs

(ii)

The endomorphisms

and

(iii)

The endomorphisms

Remark 3
.

The quadratic invariants of a
(\varepsilon,1)

In other words, given such pairs
(b,u)
and
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
, and given

one has

1.4.3
Skew-selfadjoint endomorphisms

Here, we assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
, we take an
(\varepsilon,-1)
-pair
(b,u)

for some
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
, and we denote by
V
its underlying vector space. Note that
u
is
b
-skew-selfadjoint.

For all
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, we have

\forall(x,y)\in V^{2},\;b(x,q(u)[y])=b(q(-u)[x],y),

so that
q(u)^{\star}=q(-u)
.
In particular,
q(u)
is
b
-selfadjoint if
q
is even, and
b
-skew-selfadjoint if
q
is odd.

Classically, the endomorphism
u^{\star}
is similar to the (dual) transpose
u^{t}:\varphi\in V^{\star}\mapsto\varphi\circ u
of
u
, and hence to
u
.
Hence, the Jordan numbers of
-u
are the ones of
u
, leading to

n_{p,k}(u)=n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},k}(u)
for every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
, which is a non-trivial condition only when
p
is non-even and different from
t
.

Now, let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
. Consider the residue field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
and its non-identity involution
x\mapsto x^{\bullet}

that takes the class of
t
to the one of
-t
.

Let also
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Noting that
p(u)
is
b
-selfadjoint, we obtain, like in the previous section, that the bilinear form

(x,y)\mapsto b(x,p(u)^{k-1}(y))
induces a non-degenerate bilinear form on the quotient space

V_{p,k}:=\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}/(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k-1}+(\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{k}\cap\operatorname{Im}p(u)))
, which has a natural structure of vector space over
\mathbb{L}
.
This bilinear form
B
satisfies

\forall(x,y,\lambda)\in(V_{p,k})^{2}\times\mathbb{L},\;B(x,\lambda\,y)=B(\lambda^{\bullet}\,x,y).

Hence, we can consider its
\mathbb{L}
-extension
(b,u)_{p,k}:=B^{\mathbb{L}}
, which is
a Hermitian form over the
\mathbb{L}
-vector space
V_{p,k}
if
b
is symmetric, and a skew-Hermitian one if
b
is skew-symmetric.
The equivalence class of this Hermitian (respectively, skew-Hermitian) form is then uniquely determined by the isometry class of
(b,u)
and by the pair
(p,k)
.
The
(b,u)_{p,k}
forms, when
p
ranges over
\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and
k
ranges over the positive integers, are called the
Hermitian invariants
of

(b,u)
(in fact, the Hermitian invariants of
(b,u)
should be defined as the equivalence “classes” of the previous Hermitian forms).

There is one additional family of invariants in the present situation. Consider the polynomial
p:=t
, and
let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. The bilinear form

(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u^{k-1}(y))
induces a non-degenerate bilinear form
(b,u)_{t,k}
on

V_{t,k}:=\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}/(\operatorname{Ker}u^{k-1}+(\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}\cap\operatorname{Im}u))
.
Note that if
k
is odd, the
(b,u)_{t,k}
form has the same
parity
as
b

(i.e. it is symmetric if
b
is symmetric, and alternating if
b
is alternating);
if
k
is even then
(b,u)_{t,k}
has the inverse parity of
b
, i.e. it is symmetric if
b
is alternating, and it is alternating if
b
is symmetric.
As before, in case
(b,u)_{t,k}
is alternating its isometry class is entirely determined
by the corresponding Jordan number
n_{t,k}(u)
(which must be even).
In case
(b,u)_{t,k}
is symmetric, we call it the
quadratic invariant
of
(b,u)
attached to
(t,k)
.

Again, the following theorem can be derived, with some effort, from Theorem 4 of
[
14
]

or from
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.6

(Classification of
(\varepsilon,-1)
-pairs)
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.
Let
(b,u)
and
(c,v)
be
(\varepsilon,-1)
-pairs.
For
(b,u)
to be isometric to
(c,v)
, it is necessary and sufficient that all the following conditions hold:

(i)

For each positive integer
k>0
, the bilinear forms
(b,u)_{t,k}
and
(c,v)_{t,k}
are equivalent.

(ii)

For every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and every integer
k\geq 1
, the Hermitian forms (respectively, the skew-Hermitian forms)

(b,u)_{p,k}
and
(c,v)_{p,k}
over
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
are equivalent if
\varepsilon=1
(respectively, if
\varepsilon=-1
).

(iii)

For every

, one has

.

(respectively,

1.5
Main results

We are now ready to state our main results.

Unsurprisingly, the case of symplectic forms turns out to be the easier by far, and
the results obtained by de La Cruz in
[
3
]
over the complex field hold true in an arbitrary field of characteristic different from
2
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field with
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
, and
b
be a symplectic form over
\mathbb{F}
.
Then every
b
-skew-selfadjoint endomorphism is the sum of two square-zero ones.

In other words, over a field of characteristic different from
2
, every
(-1,-1)
-pair has the square-zero splitting property.

For the next theorem, in which there is no distinction of characteristic, remember that when
b
is symplectic and
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2

the
b
-selfadjoint endomorphisms are the
b
-alternating ones.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a field, and
b
be a symplectic form over
\mathbb{F}
.
Let
u
be a
b
-alternating endomorphism.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

u
is the sum of two square-zero
b
-alternating endomorphisms;

(ii)

u
is the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms;

(iii)

Every invariant factor of
u
is even or odd.

Moreover, if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
, then these conditions are equivalent to each one of the following additional conditions:

(iv)

u
is similar to
-u
;

(v)

u
is conjugated to
-u
through an element of the symplectic group of
b
.

For symmetric bilinear forms, the characterization is much more involving.
In that case it is necessary to separate the study into the one of pairs
(b,u)
with
u
nilpotent, and the one of pairs

(b,u)
with
u
bijective.

To see that such a reduction is relevant, we use two very simple lemmas:

Lemma 1.9

(Commutation lemma)
.

Let
u
be an endomorphism of a vector space
V
, and
a_{1},a_{2}
be square-zero endomorphisms of
V
such that
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
.
Then
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
commute with
u^{2}
.

Proof.

We expand
u^{2}=a_{1}a_{2}+a_{2}a_{1}
and we compute
a_{1}u^{2}=a_{1}a_{2}a_{1}=u^{2}a_{1}
and
a_{2}u^{2}=a_{2}a_{1}a_{2}=u^{2}a_{2}
.
∎

Lemma 1.10

(Stabilization lemma)
.

Let
u
be an endomorphism of a vector space
V
, and
a_{1},a_{2}
be square-zero endomorphisms of
V
such that
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
.
Then
\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}
and
\operatorname{Im}u^{k}
are stable under
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, for every integer
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

The commutation lemma readily shows that
\operatorname{Ker}u^{2k}
and
\operatorname{Im}u^{2k}
are stable under
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
, for every integer
k\geq 0
.

Next, note that
a_{1}u+ua_{1}=a_{1}a_{2}+a_{2}a_{1}=u^{2}
.
For every integer
k\geq 0
, using the commutation of
a_{1}
with
u^{2}
entails that

a_{1}u^{2k+1}+u^{2k+1}a_{1}=u^{2k+2}
, and one easily deduces that
\operatorname{Ker}u^{2k+1}
et
\operatorname{Im}u^{2k+1}
are stable under
a_{1}
.
And symmetrically they are also stable under
a_{2}
.
∎

Now, consider an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)
that has the square-zero splitting property.
Consider an associated pair
(a_{1},a_{2})
of square-zero endomorphisms. Denote by
V
the underlying vector space.
We introduce the Fitting decomposition of
u
, consisting of the core

\operatorname{Co}(u):=\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\bigcap}\operatorname{Im}u^{n}

and of the nilspace

\operatorname{Nil}(u):=\underset{n\in\mathbb{N}}{\bigcup}\operatorname{Ker}u^{n}

of
u
. Remember that
V=\operatorname{Co}(u)\oplus\operatorname{Nil}(u)
, that there exists an integer
n\geq 0
such that

\operatorname{Co}(u)=\operatorname{Im}u^{2n}
and
\operatorname{Nil}(u)=\operatorname{Ker}u^{2n}
, and that
u
induces an automorphism of
\operatorname{Co}(u)
and a nilpotent endomorphism of
\operatorname{Nil}(u)
.
Since
u^{2n}
is
b
-selfadjoint, we deduce that
\operatorname{Nil}(u)=\operatorname{Co}(u)^{\bot_{b}}
.
Using the Stabilization Lemma, we see that
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
stabilize both summands of the Fitting decomposition of
u
,
and by Remark
1
in Section
1.3
this shows that the induced pairs
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}
have the square-zero splitting property. Of course, if we start from an arbitrary
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)
such that both induced pairs
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}
have the square-zero splitting property,
then
(b,u)\simeq(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}\bot(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}
has the square-zero splitting property.

This leads to the following general result:

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
(b,u)
be an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair. For
(b,u)
to have the square-zero splitting property,
it is necessary and sufficient that both
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}
have the square-zero splitting property.

Now, this leaves us with four subproblems to solve: for each
\eta\in\{1,-1\}
,
characterize the
(1,\eta)
-pairs
(b,u)
, with
u
nilpotent or bijective,
that have the square-zero splitting property.

We start with the case of automorphisms, as it is substantially easier.

Theorem 1.12
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,-1)
-pair in which
u
is an automorphism.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(a)

Definition 1.9

In our examples, all the fields are assumed to be of characteristic different from

1.6.1
Algebraically closed fields

Assume that
\mathbb{F}

1.6.2
Real numbers

Here, the situation is already more complicated. For a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form

over the reals, the equivalence class of

is determined by the inertia

where

(respectively,

equals

, and

(0,|s_{+}(B)-s_{-}(B)|)

Now, for any irreducible polynomial

of

with degree more than

we actually have
\deg(p)=2
and the field
\mathbb{R}[t]/(p)
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
.
Let

for some positive real number

.
Since every non-zero complex number has a square root, it follows that every non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form over
\mathbb{R}[t]/(p)
is skew-representable (the associated quadratic form takes all possible nonzero values in
\mathbb{R}[t]/(p)
if its rank is nonzero).
Hence in Theorem

, condition (i) is automatically satisfied.
For condition (ii), we need to split the discussion, whether the irreducible

or

•

If

,

•

If

has degree

then the fields

and

Then, in Theorem

is even for all

Finally, condition (ii) in Theorem

•

The case where
\eta=1
. For all

,

The case where

1.6.3
Finite fields

Here, we assume that
\mathbb{F}
is finite with
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Then
\mathbb{F}^{*}/(\mathbb{F}^{*})^{[2]}
is a group of order
2

(where
(\mathbb{F}^{*})^{[2]}
denotes the set of all squares in
\mathbb{F}^{*}
).
Given a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
b
on an
n

\mathbb{L}
with characteristic different from
2
, we denote by
\det_{\mathbb{L}}(b)
the class in
\mathbb{L}^{*}/(\mathbb{L}^{*})^{[2]}
of

is an arbitrary matrix that represents

of

(using discriminants rather than determinants is a good idea because

It is known that two non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms on

are equivalent if and only if they have the same discriminant
and their underlying spaces have the same dimension (i.e. they have the same rank).

In contrast, over a finite field equipped with a non-identity involution, even possibly of characteristic
2

(i.e. a field of cardinality

even for all

.

Now, let

. Set

is the

to the one of

.
Then every element of

has a square-root in the field

\mathbb{L}
, or none is a square in
\mathbb{L}
.
Set

and

If

Finally, condition (ii) in Theorem

•

If

1.7
Structure of the article

The remainder of the article is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, we develop two main ways to construct interesting selfadjoint/skew-selfadjoint endomorphisms.
The simpler is the first, which we call the hyperbolic/symplectic extensions of an endomorphism, and it turns out that this method, combined
with the characterization of sums of two square-zero endomorphisms, is just enough to obtain Theorem
1.8
.
For the other theorems, a more subtle construction is required: the boxed sums of a pair of symmetric/alternating bilinear forms on the same vector space. It turns out (Proposition
2.5
) that pairs
(b,u)
with the square-zero splitting property in which
u
is bijective are systematically isometric to boxed sums. And conversely, boxed sums satisfy the square-zero splitting property. This pushes us to engage in a thorough study of boxed sums and their quadratic/Hermitian invariants (Section
2.5
).

Using these two constructions, we complete the study for symplectic forms (i.e.
(-1,\eta)
-pairs) in Section
3
, and the study of automorphisms for symmetric bilinear forms
in Section
4
.

However, for symmetric forms the case of nilpotent endomorphisms is difficult, and hyperbolic/symplectic extensions and boxed sums are not sufficient to completely account for that case.
The difficult case of
(1,\eta)
-pairs
(b,u)
in which
u
is nilpotent is dealt with in Section
5
.

The last section is devoted to the solution of the Hermitian version of the problem, in which we consider pairs

(h,u)
where
h
is a non-degenerate Hermitian form and
u
is an
h
-selfadjoint endomorphism.

2
Two key constructions

2.1
Review of duality theory

Here we recall some basic notation and facts on dual spaces. Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space
over
\mathbb{F}
. We denote by
V^{\star}:=\operatorname{Hom}(V,\mathbb{K})
its dual space. It has the same dimension as
V
.
Given a linear subspace
W
of
V
, we denote by
W^{o}:=\{\varphi\in V^{\star}:\;\forall x\in W,\;\varphi(x)=0\}

its orthogonal in
V^{\star}
. Given a linear subspace
H
of
V^{\star}
, we denote by
{}^{o}H:=\{x\in V:\;\forall\varphi\in H^{\star},\;\varphi(x)=0\}

its pre-orthogonal in
V
. One finds that
\dim W+\dim W^{o}=\dim V=\dim H+\dim{}^{o}H
, as well as the double-dual relations

{}^{o}(W^{o})=W
and
({}^{o}H)^{o}=H
.

Given an endomorphism
u
of
V
, the transpose of
u
is defined as

u^{t}:\varphi\in V^{\star}\mapsto\varphi\circ u\in V^{\star}.

A classical consequence of the Frobenius normal form is that
u^{t}
is similar to
u
.

2.2
Hyperbolic and symplectic extensions of an endomorphism

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.
On the product space
V\times V^{\star}
, we consider the bilinear form

H_{V}^{\varepsilon}:\begin{cases}(V\times V^{\star})^{2}&\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}\\
((x,\varphi),(y,\psi))&\longmapsto\varphi(y)+\varepsilon\psi(x).\end{cases}

Note that
H_{V}^{1}
is a non-degenerate hyperbolic symmetric bilinear form, and it can be seen as the
polar form
1
1

1

The polar form of a quadratic form
Q
is defined as
(x,y)\mapsto Q(x+y)-Q(x)-Q(y)
. We avoid dividing by
2
to take
fields of characteristic
2
into account.
of the canonical hyperbolic quadratic form.
In contrast,
H_{V}^{-1}
is a symplectic form.

Let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
and
\eta\in\{1,-1\}
.
We consider the endomorphism

h_{\eta}(u):\begin{cases}V\times V^{\star}&\longrightarrow V\times V^{\star}\\
(x,\varphi)&\longmapsto(u(x),\eta\,u^{t}(\varphi)).\end{cases}

Note that for all
(x,\varphi)
and
(y,\psi)
in
V\times V^{\star}
,

\displaystyle H_{V}^{\varepsilon}\bigl((x,\varphi),h_{\eta}(u)[y,\psi]\bigr)

\displaystyle=\varphi(u(y))+\varepsilon\,\eta\,u^{t}(\psi)(x)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\,\eta\,\psi(u(x))+u^{t}(\varphi)[y]

\displaystyle=\eta\,H_{V}^{\varepsilon}(h_{\eta}(u)[x,\varphi],(y,\psi)).

In particular, if
\varepsilon\eta=-1
then
h_{\eta}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-alternating.
We conclude that
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},h_{\eta}(u))
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair.

Moreover, the mapping
h_{\eta}:\operatorname{End}(V)\rightarrow\operatorname{End}(V\times V^{\star})
is linear and takes any square-zero
operator to a square-zero operator. Consequently, we have the following result:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
be the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms of
V
.
Then
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},h_{\eta}(u))
has the square-zero splitting property for all
(\varepsilon,\eta)\in\{-1,1\}^{2}
.

The similarity class of the endomorphism
h_{\eta}(u)
is easily deduced from the one of
u
because
u^{t}
is similar to
u
.
In particular, if the invariant factors of
u
are
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
, then the invariants factors of
h_{1}(u)
are
p_{1},p_{1},\dots,p_{r},p_{r}
.
For
h_{-1}(u)
, the invariant factors are harder to compute and we prefer to think in terms of Jordan numbers:
simply
n_{p,k}(h_{-1}(u))=n_{p,k}(u)+n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},k}(u)
for all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and all
k\geq 1
.

We shall further simplify the notation as follows:

Notation 2.1
.

For
(\varepsilon,\eta)\in\{-1,1\}^{2}
and an endomorphism
u
of a vector space
V
, we set

H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u):=(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},h_{\eta}(u)).

Next, we examine the behavior of the pair
H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)
with respect to similarity and direct sums.
Let
v\in\operatorname{End}(V)
and
w\in\operatorname{End}(W)
be similar endomorphisms, with an isomorphism
\varphi:V\overset{\simeq}{\rightarrow}W

such that
w=\varphi\circ u\circ\varphi^{-1}
. Then, for the isomorphism
\Phi:=\varphi\oplus(\varphi^{-1})^{t}

from
V\times V^{\star}
to
W\times W^{\star}
, we have, for all
(x,f)
and
(y,g)
in
V\times V^{\star}
,

\displaystyle H_{W}^{\varepsilon}(\Phi(x,f),\Phi(y,g))

\displaystyle=H_{W}^{\varepsilon}\bigl((\varphi(x),f\circ\varphi^{-1}),(\varphi(y),g\circ\varphi^{-1})\bigr)

\displaystyle=(f\circ\varphi^{-1})(\varphi(y))+\varepsilon(g\circ\varphi^{-1})(\varphi(x))

\displaystyle=f(y)+\varepsilon g(x)=H_{V}^{\varepsilon}\bigl((x,f),(y,g)\bigr).

Moreover, for all
(x,f)\in V\times V^{\star}
, we have

\displaystyle h_{\eta}(w)[\Phi(x,f)]

\displaystyle=\bigl((\varphi\circ v\circ\varphi^{-1})[\varphi(x)],(\eta\,f\circ\varphi^{-1})\circ(\varphi\circ v\circ\varphi^{-1})]\bigr)

\displaystyle=(\varphi(v(x)),\eta\,f\circ v\circ\varphi^{-1})=\Phi(h_{\eta}(v)[x,f]),

leading to
h_{\eta}(w)=\Phi\circ h_{\eta}(v)\circ\Phi^{-1}
.
Hence, we have proved that

H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(v)\simeq H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(w).

In other words, the isometry “class” of the pair
H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)
depends only on the pair

(\varepsilon,\eta)
and on the similarity “class” of
u
.

Finally, we examine the behavior of
H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)
with respect to direct sums.
So let
u_{1}\in\operatorname{End}(V_{1})
and
u_{2}\in\operatorname{End}(V_{2})
be endomorphisms.
We consider the direct sum
u:=u_{1}\oplus u_{2}\in\operatorname{End}(V_{1}\times V_{2})
.
We shall prove that

H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})\simeq H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u_{1})\bot H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u_{2}).

We introduce the canonical injections
i_{1}:V_{1}\hookrightarrow V_{1}\times V_{2}
and
i_{2}:V_{2}\hookrightarrow V_{1}\times V_{2}
,
and we consider the isomorphism

\Psi:\begin{cases}(V_{1}\times V_{2})\times(V_{1}\times V_{2})^{\star}&\longrightarrow(V_{1}\times V_{1}^{\star})\times(V_{2}\times V_{2}^{\star})\\
((x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi)&\longmapsto\bigl((x_{1},\varphi\circ i_{1}),(x_{2},\varphi\circ i_{2})\bigr).\end{cases}

Set
G:=H_{V_{1}}^{\varepsilon}\bot H_{V_{2}}^{\varepsilon}
.
For all
(x_{1},x_{2})
and
(y_{1},y_{2})
in
V_{1}\times V_{2}
and all
\varphi,\psi
in
(V_{1}\times V_{2})^{\star}
, we see that

\displaystyle G(\Psi((x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi),\Psi((y_{1},y_{2}),\psi))

\displaystyle=\varphi(y_{1},0)+\varepsilon\psi(x_{1},0)+\varphi(0,y_{2})+\varepsilon\psi(0,x_{2})

\displaystyle=\varphi(y_{1},y_{2})+\varepsilon\psi(x_{1},x_{2})

and hence
\Psi
is an isometry from
H_{V_{1}\times V_{2}}^{\varepsilon}

to
H_{V_{1}}^{\varepsilon}\bot H_{V_{2}}^{\varepsilon}
.
Finally, for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in V_{1}\times V_{2}
and all
\varphi\in(V_{1}\times V_{2})^{\star}
, we have

(h_{\eta}(u_{1})\oplus h_{\eta}(u_{2}))\bigl(\Psi((x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi)\bigr)=\Bigl(\bigl(u_{1}(x_{1}),\eta\,\varphi\circ i_{1}\circ u_{1}\bigr),\bigl(u_{2}(x_{2}),\eta\,\varphi\circ i_{2}\circ u_{2}\bigr)\Bigr)

whereas

h_{\eta}(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})[(x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi]=\bigl((u_{1}(x_{1}),u_{2}(x_{2})),\eta\,\varphi\circ(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})\bigr)

so that

\displaystyle\Psi(h_{\eta}(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})[(x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi])

\displaystyle=\bigl((u_{1}(x_{1}),\eta\,\varphi\circ(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})\circ i_{1}),(u_{2}(x_{2}),\eta\,\varphi\circ(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})\circ i_{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl((u_{1}(x_{1}),\eta\,\varphi\circ i_{1}\circ u_{1}),(u_{2}(x_{2}),\eta\,\varphi\circ i_{2}\circ u_{2})\bigr).

Hence, we have proved that
h_{\eta}(u_{1})\oplus h_{\eta}(u_{2})=\Psi\circ h_{\eta}(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})\circ\Psi^{-1}
,
which yields the claimed isometry.

We are now ready to investigate the invariants of the pair
H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
and
(\varepsilon,\eta)\in\{-1,1\}^{2}
.
Then the quadratic/Hermitian invariants of
H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)
are all hyperbolic.

Proof.

By the primary canonical form, we can split
u\simeq u_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus u_{r}
where every
u_{i}
is cyclic with
minimal polynomial a power of some monic irreducible polynomial.
Using the above two principles, we deduce that

H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u)\simeq H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u_{1})\bot\cdots\bot H_{\varepsilon,\eta}(u_{r}).

Remember that the quadratic/Hermitian invariants are turned to equivalent invariants if we replace a pair with an isometric pair,
and that they are compatible with orthogonal sums. Remember finally that the orthogonal sum of two hyperbolic quadratic or Hermitian forms
is hyperbolic.

Hence, in order to prove the result it suffices to consider the case where
u
is cyclic and its minimal polynomial equals
p^{r}

for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and some integer
r\geq 1
.

We start by proving the result for the quadratic invariants of
f:=H_{1,1}(u)
.
First of all, since the invariant factors of
h_{1}(u)
are
p^{r},p^{r}
, all the quadratic invariants of
f
vanish
with the exception of
f_{p,r}
, which is a
2
-dimensional non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form over the field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.
To see that
f_{p,r}
is hyperbolic, it suffices to exhibit a non-zero isotropic vector for this form.
Note that
q(h_{1}(u))=h_{1}(q(u))
for every
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.
For an element
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, we denote its class modulo
p
by
\overline{q}
.
We equip
V\times V^{\star}
with the structure of
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module induced by
h_{1}(u)
.

Here, we have
\operatorname{Ker}\bigl(p(h_{1}(u))^{r-1}\bigr)=\operatorname{Im}p(h_{1}(u))
and
V\times V^{\star}=\operatorname{Ker}p(h_{1}(u))^{r}=\operatorname{Ker}p(h_{1}(u))^{r+1}
, so the
starting space of
f_{p,r}
is the quotient space
(V\times V^{\star})/\operatorname{Im}p(h_{1}(u))
.
Obviously
\operatorname{Im}p(h_{1}(u))=\operatorname{Im}p(u)\times\operatorname{Im}p(u)^{t}
.
For all
x,y
in
V
, the vectors
(x,0)
and
(y,0)
are orthogonal for the
bilinear form

B:(X,Y)\mapsto H_{V}^{1}\bigl(X,h_{1}(u)^{r-1}(Y)\bigr)

on
V\times V^{\star}
.
Let us choose
x\in V\smallsetminus\operatorname{Im}p(u)
. Then for all
q(t)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
the vector

q(t)\,(x,0)=(q(u)[x],0)
is
B
-orthogonal to
(x,0)
.
Now, denote by
X
the class of
(x,0)
modulo
\operatorname{Im}p(h_{1}(u))
. Denote
by
\overline{B}
the non-degenerate bilinear form induced by
B
on the quotient
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module
(V\times V^{\star})/\operatorname{Im}p(h_{1}(u))
.
Then
q(t).X
is
\overline{B}
-orthogonal to
X
for all
q(t)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Considering
(V\times V^{\star})/\operatorname{Im}p(h_{1}(u))
with its structure of vector space over
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
,
this means that
\overline{B}(X,\lambda\,X)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
.
In other words, for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
,

0=\overline{B}(X,\lambda\,X)=e_{p}\bigl(\lambda f_{p,r}(X,X)\bigr),

which yields
f_{p,r}(X,X)=0
. Hence
f_{p,r}
is isotropic, and we conclude that it is hyperbolic.

There are no quadratic invariants for
H_{-1,1}(u)
, so we turn to
g:=H_{\varepsilon,-1}(u)
for a given
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.
For the quadratic invariants, we note that if
p=t
then
h_{-1}(u)
is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells,
both of size
r
. Thus, all the quadratic invariants of
g
vanish if
r=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{2}
mod 2,
otherwise the sole quadratic invariant of
g
is
g_{t,r}
, which is a
2
-dimensional non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form:
the proof that
g_{t,r}
is hyperbolic is then entirely similar to the previous proof.

Finally, if
p
is an even polynomial then one checks that
h_{-1}(u)
has exactly two invariant factors, namely
p^{r}
and
p^{r}
. Again, from there the proof that the
g_{p,r}
invariants are all hyperbolic is similar to the previous proof.
∎

We can immediately give two applications of that construction for our problem:

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\eta\in\{-1,1\}
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent.
Assume that all the quadratic invariants of
(b,u)
are hyperbolic.
Then
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Proof.

Since all the quadratic invariants of
(b,u)
are hyperbolic, all the corresponding Jordan numbers are even.
Moreover, if
\eta=-1
it is known that all the remaining Jordan numbers are even (because they are dimensions of spaces equipped with symplectic forms). Hence, all the Jordan numbers of
u
are even.

Thus, we can consider a vector space
V
together with a nilpotent endomorphism
v\in\operatorname{End}(V)

such that
n_{t,k}(v)=\frac{1}{2}n_{t,k}(u)
for every integer
k\geq 1
.
Then
h_{\eta}(v)
is nilpotent and has the same Jordan numbers as
u
.
Moreover, all the quadratic invariants of
H_{1,\eta}(v)
are hyperbolic.
Besides, two hyperbolic quadratic forms are equivalent whenever they have the same rank.
Hence,
(b,u)
and
H_{1,\eta}(v)
have the same quadratic invariants,
and we conclude that
(b,u)\simeq H_{1,\eta}(v)
.

Finally, since
v
is nilpotent Theorem
1.1
shows that it is the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms of
V
.
By Proposition
2.1
, the pair
H_{1,\eta}(v)
has the square-zero splitting property, and hence so does

(b,u)
.
∎

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(-1,-1)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent.
Assume that
u
has only Jordan cells of odd size. Then
u
is the sum of two square-zero
b
-skew-selfadjoint endomorphisms.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of Corollary
2.3
, but this time around we use the fact that
the Jordan number
n_{t,2k+1}(u)
is even for every integer
k\geq 0
, because the quadratic invariant
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}

is a symplectic form.
∎

2.3
Boxed sums: an introduction

Throughout this section and in the remainder of Section
2
, we will systematically assume that

\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.

Here, we let
V
be an arbitrary vector space, and we let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.

Let
b
be a bilinear form on
V
(at this point we do not assume that it is symmetric or alternating, and
b
can be degenerate as well).
We consider the associated linear map
L_{b}:x\in V\mapsto b(-,x)\in V^{\star}
, and we define the endomorphism

v_{b}:(x,\varphi)\in V\times V^{\star}\mapsto(0,L_{b}(x))\in V\times V^{\star},

which is obviously of square zero.
For all
(x,\varphi)
and
(y,\psi)
in
V\times V^{\star}
, we see that

H_{V}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(v_{b}(x,\varphi),(y,\psi)\bigr)=L_{b}(x)[y]=b(y,x).

Hence:

•

If
\varepsilon=1
then
v_{b}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-selfadjoint (respectively, skew-selfadjoint) if and only
b
is symmetric (respectively, skew-symmetric).

•

If
\varepsilon=-1
then
v_{b}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-selfadjoint (respectively, skew-selfadjoint) if and only
b
is skew-symmetric (respectively, symmetric).

•

In any case,
v_{b}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-alternating if and only if
b
is alternating.

Assume furthermore that
b
is non-degenerate, to the effect that
L_{b}
is an isomorphism. In that case we consider the endomorphism

w_{b}:(x,\varphi)\in V\times V^{\star}\mapsto(L_{b}^{-1}(\varphi),0)\in V\times V^{\star},

again obviously of square zero.
Let
(x,\varphi)
and
(y,\psi)
in
V\times V^{\star}
. Setting
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
in
V
such that
L_{b}(x^{\prime})=\varphi

and
L_{b}(y^{\prime})=\psi
, we find that

H_{V}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(w_{b}(x,\varphi),(y,\psi)\bigr)=\varepsilon\,\psi(x^{\prime})=\varepsilon\,b(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Again, we have the following equivalences:

•

If
\varepsilon=1
then
w_{b}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-selfadjoint (respectively, skew-selfadjoint) if and only
b
is symmetric (respectively, skew-symmetric).

•

If
\varepsilon=-1
then
w_{b}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-selfadjoint (respectively, skew-selfadjoint) if and only
b
is skew-symmetric (respectively, symmetric).

•

In any case,
w_{b}
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-alternating if and only if
b
is alternating.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
b,c
be two bilinear forms on a finite-dimensional vector space
V
, with
b
non-degenerate.
We define their
boxed sum
as the endomorphism

b\boxplus c:=v_{c}+w_{b}\in\operatorname{End}(V\times V^{\star}).

Note that
b\boxplus c
is an automorphism of
V\times V^{\star}
if and only if
c
is non-degenerate (in which case its inverse is simply
c\boxplus b
).
Again, one checks that for all
(x,\varphi)
and
(y,\psi)
in
V\times V^{\star}
,

H_{V}^{\varepsilon}\bigl((b\boxplus c)(x,\varphi),(y,\psi)\bigr)=c(y,x)+\varepsilon\,b(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

where
x^{\prime}:=L_{b}^{-1}(\varphi)
and
y^{\prime}:=L_{b}^{-1}(\psi)
.
The following equivalences are then easily obtained:

•

If
\varepsilon=1
then
b\boxplus c
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-selfadjoint (respectively, skew-selfadjoint) if and only both
b
and
c
are symmetric (respectively, skew-symmetric).

•

If
\varepsilon=-1
then
b\boxplus c
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-selfadjoint (respectively, skew-selfadjoint) if and only both
b
and
c
are skew-symmetric (respectively, symmetric).

•

In any case,
b\boxplus c
is
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
-alternating if and only if both
b
and
c
are alternating.

Moreover, from the above properties of
w_{b}
and
v_{c}
, we obtain:

•

If
b
and
c
are symmetric then
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)
has the square-zero splitting property as a
(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
-pair.

•

If
b
and
c
are alternating then
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)
has the square-zero splitting property as a
(\varepsilon,-\varepsilon)
-pair.

Better still, there is almost a converse result:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(\varepsilon,\eta)\in\{1,-1\}^{2}
, and let
(b,u)
be an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair with the square-zero splitting property in which
u
is an automorphism.

•

If
\varepsilon=\eta
then
(b,u)\simeq(H_{W}^{\varepsilon},B\boxplus C)
for some vector space
W

and some pair
(B,C)
of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms on
W
.

•

If
\varepsilon=-\eta
then
(b,u)\simeq(H_{W}^{\varepsilon},B\boxplus C)
for some vector space
W

and some pair
(B,C)
of symplectic forms on
W
.

Proof.

Denote by
V
the underlying vector space of the pair
(b,u)
.
There exist square-zero endomorphisms
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
of
V
such that each
(b,u_{i})
is an

(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair and
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
.

Let us consider the subspaces
V_{1}:=\operatorname{Im}u_{1}
and
V_{2}:=\operatorname{Im}u_{2}
. We have
V_{1}\subset\operatorname{Ker}u_{1}=V_{1}^{\bot_{b}}

and likewise
V_{2}\subset V_{2}^{\bot_{b}}
, i.e.
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
are totally
b
-singular, leading to

\dim V_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\dim V
and
\dim V_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\dim V
.
Moreover,
V_{1}+V_{2}=V
because
u
is surjective, whence
V=V_{1}\oplus V_{2}
,

V_{1}=V_{1}^{\bot_{b}}
and
V_{2}=V_{2}^{\bot_{b}}
. It follows that
u_{1}
maps
V_{2}
bijectively onto
V_{1}
,
and
u_{2}
maps
V_{1}
bijectively onto
V_{2}
.

Next, as
V_{2}
is totally
b
-singular, the isomorphism
L_{b}:x\mapsto b(-,x)
induces an
injective linear mapping
V_{2}\rightarrow(V_{2})^{\circ}
, which turns out to be an isomorphism because
2\dim V_{2}=\dim V
.
Composing it with the natural (restriction) isomorphism from
(V_{2})^{\circ}
to
V_{1}^{\star}
,
we obtain an isomorphism
f:V_{2}\rightarrow V_{1}^{\star}
that takes every
x\in V_{2}
to the linear form
y\in V_{1}\mapsto b(y,x)
.
Then, we consider the isomorphism

\Phi:\begin{cases}V&\longrightarrow V_{1}\times V_{1}^{\star}\\
x_{1}+x_{2}&\longmapsto\bigl(\varepsilon x_{1},f(x_{2})\bigr)\quad\text{with $x_{1}\in V_{1}$ and $x_{2}\in V_{2}$.}\end{cases}

Next, for all
x,y
in
V
, we split
x=x_{1}+x_{2}
and
y=y_{1}+y_{2}
with
x_{1},y_{1}
in
V_{1}
and
x_{2},y_{2}
in
V_{2}
,
and we see that

\displaystyle b(x,y)

\displaystyle=b(x_{1},y_{2})+b(x_{2},y_{1})

\displaystyle=b(x_{1},y_{2})+\varepsilon\,b(y_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=f(y_{2})[x_{1}]+\varepsilon\,f(x_{2})[y_{1}]

\displaystyle=H_{V_{1}}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(\Phi(x),\Phi(y)\bigr).

Now, set
u^{\prime}_{1}:=\Phi\circ u_{1}\circ\Phi^{-1}
and
u^{\prime}_{2}:=\Phi\circ u_{2}\circ\Phi^{-1}
.
As
u_{1}
vanishes everywhere on
V_{1}
and maps
V_{2}
bijectively onto
V_{1}
,
we find that
u^{\prime}_{1}
vanishes everywhere on
V_{1}\times\{0\}
and maps
\{0\}\times V_{1}^{\star}
bijectively onto
V_{1}\times\{0\}
.
The associated isomorphism from
V_{1}^{\star}
to
V_{1}
then reads
L_{B}^{-1}
for a unique non-degenerate bilinear form

on
V_{1}
,
and hence
u^{\prime}_{1}=w_{B}
.
Likewise,
u^{\prime}_{2}
vanishes everywhere on
\{0\}\times V_{1}^{\star}
and maps
V_{1}\times\{0\}
bijectively onto
\{0\}\times V_{1}^{\star}
: the associated linear mapping from
V_{1}
to
V_{1}^{\star}
reads
L_{C}
for some bilinear form
C
on
V_{1}
, and hence
u^{\prime}_{2}=v_{C}
.

Finally,
\Phi\circ u\circ\Phi^{-1}=B\boxplus C
, and we conclude that

(b,u)\simeq(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},B\boxplus C)
. It follows that
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},B\boxplus C)
is an
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pair.
From there, we deduce that if
\varepsilon=\eta
then
B
and
C
are symmetric, otherwise
B
and
C
are alternating.
∎

Hence, in order to solve our problem for automorphisms, it essentially
remains to compute the quadratic/Hermitian invariants of a boxed sum!

Before we can do that, additional general considerations are required on the dependency of

(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)
on the pair
(b,c)
.

2.4
Boxed sums as functions of pairs of forms

Let
(b,c)
and
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
be two pairs of bilinear forms, on respective vector spaces
V,V,V^{\prime},V^{\prime}
.

We say that
(b,c)
is isometric to
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
when there exists a vector space isomorphism

\varphi:V\overset{\simeq}{\rightarrow}V^{\prime}
such that

\forall(x,y)\in V^{2},\;b^{\prime}\bigl(\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\bigr)=b(x,y)\;\text{and}\;c^{\prime}\bigl(\varphi(x),\varphi(y)\bigr)=c(x,y).

This defines an equivalence relation on the collection of pairs of bilinear forms with the same underlying vector space.

Next, the orthogonal direct sum of two such pairs
(b_{1},c_{1})
and
(b_{2},c_{2})
is defined as

(b_{1},c_{1})\bot(b_{2},c_{2}):=(b_{1}\bot b_{2},c_{1}\bot c_{2})
, and one checks that it is compatible with isometry
(if we replace a summand with an isometric one, then the result is unchanged up to an isometry).

We shall say that a pair is indecomposable if it is non-trivial (i.e. defined on a non-zero vector space) and
not isometric to the orthogonal direct sum of two non-trivial pairs.

We shall now examine the effect, in boxed sums, of replacing a pair
(b,c)
with an isometric one.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
. Let
(b,c)
be a pair of bilinear forms on a vector space
V
, and

(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
be a pair of bilinear forms on a vector space
V^{\prime}
. Assume that
b
and
b^{\prime}
are non-degenerate and that

(b,c)\simeq(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
. Then

(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)\simeq(H_{V^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon},b^{\prime}\boxplus c^{\prime}).

Proof.

Let us consider an isometry
\varphi:V\overset{\simeq}{\rightarrow}V^{\prime}
from
(b,c)
to
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
.
Note in particular that
L_{c}=\varphi^{t}\circ L_{c^{\prime}}\circ\varphi
and
L_{b}=\varphi^{t}\circ L_{b^{\prime}}\circ\varphi
.
We have already seen in Section
2.2
that the vector space isomorphism

\Phi:=\varphi\oplus(\varphi^{-1})^{t}
yields an isometry from
H_{V}^{\varepsilon}
to
H_{V^{\prime}}^{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, for all
(y,g)
in
V^{\prime}\times(V^{\prime})^{\star}
,

v_{c}(\Phi^{-1}(y,g))=v_{c}(\varphi^{-1}(y),g\circ\varphi)=\bigl(0,L_{c}(\varphi^{-1}(y))\bigr)\\
=(0,(\varphi^{t}\circ L_{c^{\prime}})(y))=\Phi^{-1}(0,L_{c^{\prime}}(y))=\Phi^{-1}(v_{c^{\prime}}(y,g)),

that is

v_{c}=\Phi^{-1}\circ v_{c^{\prime}}\circ\Phi.

Likewise, one checks that
w_{b}=\Phi^{-1}\circ w_{b^{\prime}}\circ\Phi
.
Hence, by summing we obtain
b\boxplus c=\Phi^{-1}\circ(b^{\prime}\boxplus c^{\prime})\circ\Phi
, and the expected conclusion follows.
∎

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
. Let
(b_{1},c_{1})
and
(b_{2},c_{2})
be pairs of bilinear forms on respective vectors spaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
. Then,

\bigl(H_{V_{1}\times V_{2}}^{\varepsilon},(b_{1}\bot b_{2})\boxplus(c_{1}\bot c_{2})\bigr)\simeq(H_{V_{1}}^{\varepsilon},b_{1}\boxplus c_{1})\bot(H_{V_{2}}^{\varepsilon},b_{2}\boxplus c_{2}).

Proof.

We introduce the canonical injections
i_{1}:V_{1}\hookrightarrow V_{1}\times V_{2}
and
i_{2}:V_{2}\hookrightarrow V_{1}\times V_{2}
,
and we consider the isomorphism

\Phi:\begin{cases}(V_{1}\times V_{2})\times(V_{1}\times V_{2})^{\star}&\longrightarrow(V_{1}\times V_{1}^{\star})\times(V_{2}\times V_{2}^{\star})\\
((x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi)&\longmapsto\bigl((x_{1},\varphi\circ i_{1}),(x_{2},\varphi\circ i_{2})\bigr).\end{cases}

It was already proved in Section
2.2
that

\Phi
yields an isometry from
H_{V_{1}\times V_{2}}^{\varepsilon}
to
H_{V_{1}}^{\varepsilon}\bot H_{V_{2}}^{\varepsilon}
.
It remains to check that
(b_{1}\boxplus c_{1})\oplus(b_{2}\boxplus c_{2})=\Phi\circ((b_{1}\bot b_{2})\boxplus(c_{1}\bot c_{2}))\circ\Phi^{-1}
.
To see this, it suffices to check that
(v_{c_{1}}\oplus v_{c_{2}})\circ\Phi=\Phi\circ v_{(c_{1}\bot c_{2})}

and
(w_{b_{1}}\oplus w_{b_{2}})\circ\Phi=\Phi\circ w_{(b_{1}\bot b_{2})}
. We will only check the former relation, the proof of the latter being similar.
So, let
(x_{1},x_{2},\varphi)\in V_{1}\times V_{2}\times(V_{1}\times V_{2})^{\star}
.
Then,

(v_{c_{1}}\oplus v_{c_{2}})(\Phi((x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi))=\bigl(v_{c_{1}}(x_{1},\varphi\circ i_{1}),v_{c_{2}}(x_{2},\varphi\circ i_{2})\bigr)=\bigl((0,L_{c_{1}}(x_{1})),(0,L_{c_{2}}(x_{2}))\bigr).

Besides,

\Phi\bigl(v_{c_{1}\bot c_{2}}((x_{1},x_{2}),\varphi)\bigr)=\bigl((0,L_{c_{1}\bot c_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\circ i_{1}),(0,L_{c_{1}\bot c_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\circ i_{2})\bigr)

and we conclude by noting that
L_{c_{1}\bot c_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\circ i_{1}=L_{c_{1}}(x_{1})
and

L_{c_{1}\bot c_{2}}(x_{1},x_{2})\circ i_{2}=L_{c_{2}}(x_{2})
.
∎

Now, we need to better understand the isometry type of pairs
(b,c)
of bilinear forms with the same parity (both symmetric or both alternating),
with
b
non-degenerate. It turns out that this question is a reformulation of the classification of
(\varepsilon,1)
-pairs!
Indeed, let
\varepsilon\in\{1,-1\}
, and let
(b,c)
be a pair of bilinear forms on a vector space
V
, both symmetric if
\varepsilon=1
, and
both alternating if
\varepsilon=-1
. Then
u:=L_{b}^{-1}\circ L_{c}
is an endomorphism of
V
, and one checks that

(b,u)
is a
(\varepsilon,1)
-pair. Conversely, given an
(\varepsilon,1)
-pair
(b,u)
, one sees that

c:(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u(y))
is a bilinear form that is symmetric if
\varepsilon=1
, and alternating if
\varepsilon=-1
.
Moreover, one checks that, given pairs
(b,c)
and
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
of bilinear forms, any isometry from
(b,c)
to
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})

turns out to be an isometry from
(b,L_{b}^{-1}\circ L_{c})
to
(b^{\prime},L_{b^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ L_{c^{\prime}})
. Conversely, given

(\varepsilon,1)
-pairs
(b,u)
and
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
, any isometry from
(b,u)
to
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
turns out to be an isometry from

(b,c)
to
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
where
c:(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u(y))
and
c^{\prime}:(x,y)\mapsto b^{\prime}(x,u^{\prime}(y))
.

Finally, let
(b,c)
and
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
be two pairs of bilinear forms on respective vector spaces
V
and
V^{\prime}
, and assume that both
b

and
b^{\prime}
are non-degenerate. Then, one checks that
L_{b\bot b^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ L_{c\bot c^{\prime}}=(L_{b}^{-1}\circ L_{c})\oplus(L_{b^{\prime}}^{-1}\circ L_{c^{\prime}})
.
Hence, the previous correspondence literally takes orthogonal direct sums to orthogonal direct sums.

Hence, the classification of pairs of symmetric (respectively, alternating) bilinear forms (with the first form non-degenerate)
comes entirely down to the one of
(1,1)
-pairs (respectively, of
(-1,1)
-pairs).
In particular, pairs of symmetric bilinear forms with the first component non-degenerate are classified by quadratic invariants.

Remark 4
.

One checks that
(b\boxplus c)^{2}=h_{1}(L_{b}^{-1}\circ L_{c})
.

2.5
The invariants of a boxed sum

We start with an interesting observation which is especially relevant to the special case of
(1,1)
-pairs:

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
(b,c)
be a pair of bilinear forms on a vector space
V
, with
b
non-degenerate.
Then the pairs
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)
and
(-H_{V}^{1},-b\boxplus c)
are isometric.

Proof.

Consider the automorphism
\varphi:=h_{-1}(\operatorname{id}_{V})
of
V\times V^{\star}
.
It is skew-selfadjoint for
H_{V}^{1}
and satisfies
\varphi^{2}=\operatorname{id}
, whence

\varphi\varphi^{\star}=-\operatorname{id}
(where the adjoint is taken with respect to
H_{V}^{1}
).
This leads to
H_{V}^{1}\bigl(\varphi(x,f),\varphi(y,g)\bigr)=-H_{V}^{1}\bigl((x,f),(y,g)\bigr)

for all
x,y
in
V
and all
f,g
in
V^{\star}
.
Next, one easily checks that
\varphi\circ w_{b}\circ\varphi^{-1}=-w_{b}=w_{-b}
and that

\varphi\circ v_{c}\circ\varphi^{-1}=v_{-c}
, which leads to
\varphi\circ(b\boxplus c)\circ\varphi^{-1}=-b\boxplus c
.
Hence,
\varphi
is the required isometry.
∎

We are now ready to compute the invariants of
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)

when
(b,c)
is a pair of bilinear forms on the vector space
V
, both symmetric or both alternating, and with
b
non-degenerate.
Using the compatibility of boxed sums with orthogonal direct sums and with isometry, we will limit the computation to the
case where
(b,c)
is indecomposable.
There are only two special cases to consider:

•

If
b
and
c
are symmetric,
b
is non-degenerate and
(b,c)
is indecomposable, then
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
is cyclic and its minimal
polynomial equals
p^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and some
r\geq 1
. In that case, exactly one quadratic invariant of
(b,c)

is non-trivial, namely
(b,c)_{p,r}
, and it is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form on a
1
-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.

•

If
b
and
c
are symplectic,
b
is non-degenerate and
(b,c)
is indecomposable, then
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}

is the direct sum of two cyclic endomorphisms with minimal polynomial
p^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and some
r\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
b
and
c
be bilinear forms on a vector space
V
, with
b
non-degenerate.
Denote by
p_{1},\dots,p_{r}
the invariant factors of
L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
. Then
the invariant factors of
b\boxplus c
are
p_{1}(t^{2}),\dots,p_{r}(t^{2})
.

Proof.

Consider a basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
of
V
. Then,

\mathbf{B}:=((0,L_{b}(e_{1})),\dots,(0,L_{b}(e_{n})),(e_{1},0),\dots,(e_{n},0))

is a basis of
V\times V^{\star}
and one checks that the matrix of
b\boxplus c
in
\mathbf{B}
equals

M:=\begin{bmatrix}[0]_{n\times n}&A\\
I_{n}&[0]_{n\times n}\end{bmatrix},

where
A
stands for the matrix of
L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
in
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
. From there, it is known (see the proof of corollary
A.3 in
[
11
]
)
that the invariants factors of
M
are
p_{1}(t^{2}),\dots,p_{r}(t^{2})
.
∎

Now, we finally compute the relevant quadratic/Hermitian invariants of
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)
when

(b,c)
is indecomposable. We start with the case where
b,c
are symplectic forms.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
(b,c)
be an indecomposable pair of symplectic forms on a vector space
V
.
Then all the Hermitian invariants of
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)

are hyperbolic. Moreover, all the Jordan numbers of
b\boxplus c
are even.

Proof.

Here, there exists
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\{t\}
and an integer
r\geq 1
such that

u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
has exactly two primary invariants:
p_{0}^{r}
and
p_{0}^{r}
.
Lemma
2.9
shows that the invariant factors of
b\boxplus c
are
p_{0}(t^{2})^{r}
and
p_{0}(t^{2})^{r}
.
It easily follows that the Jordan numbers of
b\boxplus c
are all even.

Now, we have two cases: either
p:=p_{0}(t^{2})
is irreducible or
p_{0}(t^{2})=qq^{\mathrm{op}}
for some
q\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})

such that
q\neq q^{\mathrm{op}}
. In the second case, all the Hermitian invariants of
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)
vanish.
So, we assume that the first case holds. Then
p^{r}
and
p^{r}
are the primary invariants of
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)
,
and exactly one Hermitian invariant of
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)
is non-trivial, namely
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
:
it is a Hermitian form defined on a
2
-dimensional vector space over the field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
equipped with the involution that takes the class
\overline{t}
to its opposite.

In the remainder of the proof, we equip
V
with the
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module structure induced by
u
, and
V\times V^{\star}

with the one induced by
b\boxplus c
.

In order to show that
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
is hyperbolic, it suffices to exhibit a nonzero isotropic vector for it.
In our situation, the relevant quadratic form that is used to build
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
is the bilinear form

\overline{B}
induced by

B:(X,Y)\mapsto H_{V}^{1}(X,p^{r-1}\,Y)

on the quotient vector space
(V\times V^{\star})/\operatorname{Im}p(b\boxplus c)

(the details are the same as in the proof of Proposition
2.2
).
Remembering (see Remark
4
) that
(b\boxplus c)^{2}=h_{1}(u)
,
we find that
p(b\boxplus c)=h_{1}(p_{0}(u))
and hence

\operatorname{Im}p(b\boxplus c)=\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)\times\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)^{t}
.
Let us choose
x\in V\smallsetminus\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
, set
X:=(x,0)
and denote by
\overline{X}
the class of
X
modulo
\operatorname{Im}p(b\boxplus c)
.
Note that
\overline{X}\neq 0
. We shall prove that
X
is isotropic for
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
, which will complete the proof.

Noting that
(b\boxplus c)^{2}(x,0)=(u(x),0)
, we obtain

\forall q\in\mathbb{F}[t],\;q(t^{2})\,(x,0)=(q\,x,0)
and it follows that

\forall q\in\mathbb{F}[t],\;H_{V}^{1}\bigl((x,0),q(t^{2})\,(x,0)\bigr)=0.

Let
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
. Then,
tq(t^{2}).(x,0)=\bigl(0,L_{c}(q\,x)\bigr)
.
Hence
H_{V}^{1}\bigl((x,0),tq(t^{2}).(x,0)\bigr)=L_{c}(q(u)[x])[x]=c(x,q(u)[x])=b(x,uq(u)[x])
.
Since
b
and
c
are alternating and
u
is
b
-selfadjoint, we see that
b(x,u^{k}(x))=0
for every integer

k\geq 0
: indeed, if
k=2l
one writes
b(x,u^{k}(x))=b(u^{l}(x),u^{l}(x))=0
, if
k=2l+1
one writes
b(x,u^{k}(x))=b(u^{l}(x),u^{l+1}(x))=c(u^{l}(x),u^{l}(x))=0
.
Hence,

H_{V}^{1}\bigl((x,0),tq(t^{2}).(x,0)\bigr)=b(x,uq(u)[x])=0.

Combining the above two points yields
H_{V}^{1}((x,0),\lambda\,(x,0))=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.

It follows that, in the quotient
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module
V/\operatorname{Im}p(b\boxplus c)
,
the class
\overline{X}
generates a submodule that is orthogonal to the submodule
\mathbb{F}[t]\,\overline{X}

for the bilinear form induced by
(Y,Z)\mapsto H_{V}^{1}(Y,p^{r-1}\,Z)
.

By coming back to the definition of the Hermitian invariant of order
r
with respect to
p
(see Section
1.4
),
we get that
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}(\overline{X},\overline{X})=0
, which completes the proof.
∎

We turn to boxed sums of symmetric forms, for which the situation is more complicated.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
(b,c)
be a pair of symmetric bilinear forms on a vector space
V
, with
b
non-degenerate.
Assume that
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}^{r}
for some
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and some
r\geq 1
, and assume furthermore
that
p:=p_{0}(t^{2})
is irreducible. Assume finally that the quadratic invariant
(b,u)_{p_{0},r}
represents, for some polynomial
s(t)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
(not divisible by
p_{0}
), the class of
s(t)
modulo
p_{0}
.
Then, for all
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
, the pair
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)
has exactly one quadratic/Hermitian invariant, namely
(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
, which is defined on a

1
-dimensional vector space, and it represents the class of
\varepsilon ts(t^{2})
in the field
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.

Proof.

Again, the sole invariant factor of
b\boxplus c
is
p^{r}
because the sole one of
u
is
p_{0}^{r}
.

We consider the fields
\mathbb{M}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
and
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.
For a polynomial
a(t)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, we shall denote by
\overline{a(t)}^{\mathbb{L}}
its class modulo
p
, and by

\overline{a(t)}^{\mathbb{M}}
its class modulo
p_{0}
.

Let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
.
Like in the proofs of previous lemmas, the form
\overline{B}
that is used to construct the quadratic/Hermitian invariant

(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
is defined on the quotient space
(V\times V^{\star})/(\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)\times\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)^{t})
,
whereas the one that is used to construct the quadratic invariant
(b,u)_{p_{0},r}
is defined
on the quotient space
V/\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
. The assumption on
s(t)
shows that we can choose
a vector
x
of
V\smallsetminus\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
such that

\forall q\in\mathbb{F}[t],\;b(x,(qp_{0}^{r-1})(u)[x])=e_{p_{0}}(\overline{s(t)q(t)}^{\mathbb{M}}).

To simplify the notation, we now endow
V\times V^{\star}
with the structure of
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module induced by
b\boxplus c
,
whereas
V
is endowed with the structure of
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module associated with
u
.
Let us consider the vector
X:=(x,0)\in V\times V^{\star}
.
Note that
p^{r-1}\,X=(p_{0}^{r-1}\,x,0)

Let
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, and split
q=q_{1}(t^{2})+tq_{2}(t^{2})
for
q_{1}(t),q_{2}(t)
in
\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Remembering that
(b\boxplus c)^{2}=h_{1}(u)
, we see that

j(t^{2})\,X=(j\,x,0)
for all
j\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, and it follows that

(p^{r-1}q)\,X=q\,(p^{r-1}\,X)=\bigl(q_{1}p_{0}^{r-1}\,x,L_{c}(q_{2}p_{0}^{r-1}\,x)\bigr).

Hence,

H_{V}^{\varepsilon}(X,qp^{r-1}\,X)=\varepsilon\,c(x,q_{2}p_{0}^{r-1}\,x)=\varepsilon\,b(x,tq_{2}p_{0}^{r-1}\,x)=\varepsilon\,e_{p_{0}}(\overline{tq_{2}s}^{\mathbb{M}}).

Now, if we perform the Euclidean division
tq_{2}s=Qp_{0}+R
with
\deg R<\deg p_{0}
, then

t^{2}q_{2}(t^{2})s(t^{2})=Q(t^{2})p+R(t^{2})
and
\deg R(t^{2})<\deg p
, which leads to

e_{p}(\overline{t^{2}q_{2}(t^{2})s(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}})=R(0^{2})=R(0)=e_{p_{0}}\bigl(\overline{tq_{2}s}^{\mathbb{M}}\bigr).

Noting that
t\,q_{1}(t^{2})s(t^{2})
is an odd polynomial, we see that its remainder mod
p
is also odd (because
p
is even), and hence

e_{p}\bigl(\overline{tq_{1}(t^{2})s(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}}\bigr)=0.

By summing, we end up with

H_{V}^{\varepsilon}(X,p^{r-1}\,(q\,X))=\varepsilon\,e_{p_{0}}(\overline{tq_{2}s}^{\mathbb{M}})=\varepsilon\,e_{p}(\overline{tq(t)s(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}})=e_{p}\bigl(\overline{q}^{\mathbb{L}}\varepsilon\,\overline{ts(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}}\bigr).

Hence, by denoting by
\overline{X}
the class of
X
modulo
\operatorname{Im}p(b\boxplus c)
,
we have found that

(H_{V}^{\varepsilon},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}(\overline{X},\overline{X})=\varepsilon\,\overline{ts(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}},

which yields the claimed result because
\overline{X}\neq 0
.
∎

Corollary 2.12
.

Let
(b,c)
be a pair of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms on a vector space
V
.
Let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and
r\geq 1
be an integer. Then the quadratic invariant
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}

is skew-representable over the field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
equipped with the involution that takes the class of
t
to its opposite.

Proof.

Let us split the pair
(b,c)\simeq(b_{1},c_{1})\bot\cdots\bot(b_{n},c_{n})

into an orthogonal sum of irreducible pairs of symmetric bilinear forms (defined on respective vector spaces
V_{1},\dots,V_{n}
). Then we know that

(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)\simeq(H_{V_{1}}^{1},b_{1}\boxplus c_{1})\bot\cdots\bot(H_{V_{n}}^{1},b_{n}\boxplus c_{n})
, to the effect that

(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}\simeq(H_{V_{1}}^{1},b_{1}\boxplus c_{1})_{p,r}\bot\cdots\bot(H_{V_{n}}^{1},b_{n}\boxplus c_{n})_{p,r}
.
Hence, it suffices to prove the result when
(b,c)
is indecomposable as a pair of symmetric bilinear forms.

So, assume now that
(b,c)
is indecomposable. Then it is known that
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
q^{s}

for some
q\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and some
s>0
. If
p=q(t^{2})
, then Lemma
2.11
shows that

(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
is a skew-representable symmetric bilinear form (of rank

).
Otherwise

does not divide

, and

∎

Here is now a sort of converse statement:

Corollary 2.13

Let

be a skew-representable symmetric bilinear form over the field

(for the involution

Then there exists a vector space

) and a pair

The invariant factors of

, and

.

Let

. We can choose an indecomposable pair

of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms (with underlying space denoted by

) such that

, and the (rank

has a sole non-zero quadratic invariant, attached to the pair

∎

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus(\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cup\{t\})
and
r>0
.
Set
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p_{0}(t^{2})=p(t)\,p^{\mathrm{op}}(t)
.
Consider the field
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.
Let
\beta\in\mathbb{L}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.
Then there exists a vector space
V
(over
\mathbb{F}
) equipped with a pair
(b,c)
of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms such that:

(i)

b\boxplus c
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}(t^{2})^{r}=p(t)^{r}p^{\mathrm{op}}(t)^{r}
;

(ii)

The quadratic invariant
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
has dimension
1
and represents the value
\beta
.

Proof.

We consider the field
\mathbb{M}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
.
Let us start from an arbitrary
\alpha\in\mathbb{M}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.
By the classification of pairs of symmetric bilinear forms, there exists a vector space
V
equipped with a pair of non-degenerate
symmetric bilinear forms
(b,c)
such that
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}^{r}

and the quadratic invariant
(b,u)_{p_{0},r}
represents the value
\alpha
.
By Lemma
2.9
the endomorphism
b\boxplus c
has
p_{0}(t^{2})^{r}
as its sole invariant factor.
As
p\neq p^{\mathrm{op}}
, it follows that
b\boxplus c
has exactly two Jordan cells, both of size
r
, associated respectively with
p
and
p^{\mathrm{op}}
,
and in particular the bilinear form
(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
has rank
1
. We shall compute a specific nonzero value taken by the quadratic form attached to

(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
, a value that will happen to be an
\mathbb{F}
-linear function of
\alpha
.
Then, we will see that this function maps
\mathbb{M}\smallsetminus\{0\}
onto
\mathbb{L}\smallsetminus\{0\}
.

We set
W:=V\times V^{\star}
and
v:=b\boxplus c
.
Throughout, we equip
W
with the
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module structure induced by the endomorphism
v
, and

V
with the
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module structure induced by
u
.
We choose
x
in
V\smallsetminus\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
such that
\alpha=(b,u)_{p_{0},r}(\overline{x},\overline{x})
,
where
\overline{x}
denotes the class of
x
in the quotient space
V/\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
, and we set
X:=(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}\,(x,0)\in W
.
Hence
p^{r}\,X=0
, that is
X\in\operatorname{Ker}p^{r}(v)
.

We will prove first that
X\not\in\operatorname{Ker}p^{r-1}(v)
.
Let us write
(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}=r_{1}(t^{2})+tr_{2}(t^{2})
for polynomials
r_{1},r_{2}
in
\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Then
X=(r_{1}\,x,L_{c}(r_{2}\,x))
. Now, assume on the contrary that
p^{r-1}\,X=0
.
Noting that
\operatorname{Im}(pp^{\mathrm{op}})(v)=\operatorname{Ker}p^{r-1}(v)\oplus\operatorname{Ker}(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r-1}(v)

(because of the Jordan cells structure of
v
), we would deduce that
X\in\operatorname{Im}(pp^{\mathrm{op}})(v)
. Yet
(pp^{\mathrm{op}})(v)=p_{0}(v^{2})=h_{1}(p_{0}(u))
, and hence
we would find
X\in\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)\times V^{\star}
.
Therefore
r_{1}x\in\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
. Then,
p_{0}
must divide
r_{1}
because
x\not\in\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(u)
and
u
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}^{r}
.
Hence
pp^{\mathrm{op}}=p_{0}(t^{2})
must divide
r_{1}(t^{2})
, and we successively deduce that
p^{\mathrm{op}}
divides
tr_{2}(t^{2})
and that
p^{\mathrm{op}}
divides
r_{2}(t^{2})
.
Hence
p
divides
r_{2}(t^{2})^{\mathrm{op}}=r_{2}(t^{2})
, and finally
p
divides
r_{1}(t^{2})+t\,r_{2}(t^{2})=(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}
, which is absurd.
We conclude that
X\in\operatorname{Ker}p^{r}(v)\smallsetminus\operatorname{Ker}p^{r-1}(v)
.

In the next step, we obtain precious information on the non-zero scalar
\gamma:=(H_{V}^{1},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}(X,X)
of
\mathbb{L}
.
Let us start from an arbitrary polynomial
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.
We compute

\displaystyle e_{p}(\overline{q}^{\mathbb{L}}\gamma)

\displaystyle=H_{V}^{1}(X,p^{r-1}\,(q\,X))

\displaystyle=H_{V}^{1}\bigl((p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}q\,(x,0),p^{\mathrm{op}}(p_{0}^{r-1}(t^{2})\,(x,0))\bigr)

\displaystyle=H_{V}^{1}\bigl((p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}q\,(x,0),p^{\mathrm{op}}(p_{0}^{r-1}\,x,0)\bigr)

\displaystyle=H_{V}^{1}\bigl((p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}q\,(x,0),(p_{0}^{r-1}\,x,0)\bigr).

Now, split
(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}q=s_{1}(t^{2})+ts_{2}(t^{2})
with
s_{1},s_{2}
in
\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Then
(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}q\,(x,0)=(s_{1}\,x,L_{c}(s_{2}\,x))
and hence

e_{p}(\overline{q}^{\mathbb{L}}\gamma)=c(p_{0}^{r-1}\,x,s_{2}\,x)=c(s_{2}\,x,p_{0}^{r-1}\,x)\\
=b(s_{2}\,x,tp_{0}^{r-1}\,x)=b\bigl(x,p_{0}^{r-1}\,(ts_{2}\,x)\bigr)=e_{p_{0}}(\overline{ts_{2}}^{\mathbb{M}}\alpha).

In order to conclude, it suffices to prove that
\alpha
can be chosen so that
\gamma=\beta
.
To see this, we shall prove that
\gamma
can be obtained as an
\mathbb{F}
-linear function of
\alpha
(from
\mathbb{M}
to
\mathbb{L}
).

We consider the linear mapping
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]\mapsto\overline{ts_{2}}^{\mathbb{M}}\in\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
(with the above construction).
Let
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, and write
q(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}=s_{1}(t^{2})+ts_{2}(t^{2})
with
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
in
\mathbb{F}[t]
.

Assume that
p
divides
q
. Then
p_{0}(t^{2})
divides
q(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}
,
and one deduces that
p_{0}
divides
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
(to see this, we split the quotient
z
of
q(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}
by

into

for

and
r_{2}
in
\mathbb{F}[t]
, we recover that
q(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}=p_{0}(t^{2})r_{1}(t^{2})+tp_{0}(t^{2})r_{2}(t^{2})

and hence
s_{1}(t^{2})=p_{0}(t^{2})r_{1}(t^{2})
and
s_{2}(t^{2})=p_{0}(t^{2})r_{2}(t^{2})
, so that

and

Hence
\overline{ts_{2}}^{\mathbb{M}}=0
.

Conversely, assume that
p_{0}
divides

. Then
p_{0}
divides
s_{2}
and hence
pp^{\mathrm{op}}=p_{0}(t^{2})
divides

.
It follows that

divides
s_{1}(t^{2})=q(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r+1}-ts_{2}(t^{2})
, and since

is even it follows that

divides

. Finally

divides

and hence

divides

.
We conclude that the previous map induces an injective
\mathbb{F}
-linear mapping

\varphi:\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0}).

The
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces

and
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
have the same (finite) dimension over

, and we deduce that

is a vector space isomorphism.
Now, consider the transposed isomorphism

\varphi^{t}:f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{M},\mathbb{F})\overset{\simeq}{\longmapsto}(f\circ\varphi)\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{F}).

We know that the mappings

\Phi_{p}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{L}&\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{F})\\
a&\longmapsto[x\mapsto e_{p}(ax)]\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{p_{0}}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{M}&\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{M},\mathbb{F})\\

Hence, the composite

is an isomorphism of

-vector spaces from

to

to

.

The conclusion is finally obtained by taking

∎

(b,c)
of symmetric bilinear forms on a vector space

3
The case of a symplectic form

3.1
Alternating endomorphisms

Here, we will prove Theorem
1.8
by taking advantage of the symplectic extensions technique.

Throughout this part, we let
(b,u)
be a
(-1,1)
-pair.
In Theorem
1.8
, the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) is obvious.
Moreover, the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(iii) is known by Theorem
1.1
.

Let us prove that condition (iii) implies condition (i).
Assume that all the invariant factors of
u
are even or odd. The classification of
(-1,1)
-pairs (which holds regardlessly of the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}
)
shows that the invariant factors of
u
can be written
p_{1},p_{1},p_{2},p_{2},\dots,p_{r},p_{r},\dots
,
hence with all the
p_{i}
’s even or odd.
Now, let us choose an endomorphism
w
of a vector space
W
whose invariant factors are
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{r},\dots
.
As
h_{1}(w)=w\oplus w^{t}
and
w^{t}
is similar to
w
, we gather that

h_{1}(w)
and
u
have the same invariant factors.
Noting that
H_{-1,1}(w)
is a
(-1,1)
-pair, we gather from the characterization of
(-1,1)
-pairs
that
(b,u)\simeq H_{-1,1}(w)
.
And since every invariant factor of
w
is even or odd, we deduce from Theorem
1.1
that

w
is the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms of
W
, and we deduce
from Proposition
2.1
that
H_{-1,1}(w)
has the square-zero splitting property.
Hence
(b,u)\simeq H_{-1,1}(w)
also has the square-zero splitting property.

Assume furthermore that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
. Then, by Theorem
1.1
, there exists an automorphism
\varphi
of
W

such that
\varphi w\varphi^{-1}=-w
.
Set
\Phi:=\varphi\oplus(\varphi^{-1})^{t}
, which is an automorphism of
W\times W^{\star}
.
Note that

\Phi\,h_{1}(w)\,\Phi^{-1}=(\varphi\,w\,\varphi^{-1},(\varphi^{-1})^{t}\,w^{t}\,\varphi^{t})=h_{1}(\varphi\,w\,\varphi^{-1})=h_{1}(-w)=-h_{1}(w).

Finally, for all
(x,f)\in W\times W^{\star}
and all
(y,g)\in W\times W^{\star}
, we find

H_{W}^{-1}\bigl(\Phi(x,f),\Phi(y,g)\bigr)=H_{W}^{-1}\bigl((\varphi(x),f\circ\varphi^{-1}),(\varphi(y),g\circ\varphi^{-1})\bigr)\\
=f(y)-g(x)=H_{W}^{-1}\bigl((x,f),(y,g)\bigr).

Hence,
\Phi
belongs to the symplectic group of
H_{W}^{-1}
.
By the isometry
(b,u)\simeq H_{-1,1}(w)
, we deduce that
u
is conjugated to
-u
through an element of the symplectic group of
b
.

Hence, we have proved that, in Theorem
1.8
, condition (iii) implies condition (i) and that it implies condition (v) if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
Finally, condition (v) obviously implies condition (iv), and it known by Theorem
1.1
that
condition (iv) implies condition (iii) if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
.

Here, we prove Theorem

.
So, we assume that

and
we take a pair

-skew-selfadjoint endomorphism

We shall prove that

for some

Case 1:

is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells, both of size

of a vector space

.

Then

.

p_{0}
.

Next, by the classification of pairs of symmetric bilinear forms, we can choose such a pair
(B,C)

on a vector space
W
such that
B
is non-degenerate,
v:=L_{B}^{-1}L_{C}
is cyclic with minimal polynomial

p_{0}^{r}
, and the quadratic invariant
(B,v)_{p_{0},r}
represents the value
-\overline{s(t)}^{\mathbb{M}}
.

By Lemma
2.11
,
B\boxplus C
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p^{r}
,
and the Hermitian invariant
(H_{W}^{-1},B\boxplus C)_{p,r}
represents
\overline{ts(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}}

(and is defined on a
1
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{L}
).
By the classification of
(-1,-1)
-pairs, it follows that

(b,u)\simeq(H_{W}^{-1},B\boxplus C)
, and once more we conclude that

(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

4
Symmetric forms with automorphisms

Throughout this section, we assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.

4.1
Skew-selfadjoint automorphisms

Here, we shall prove Theorem
1.12
.
So, let
(b,u)
be a
(1,-1)
-pair in which
u
is bijective.

Assume first that
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.
By Proposition
2.5
, it is isometric to
(H^{1}_{W},B\boxplus C)
for some
vector space
W
and some pair
(B,C)
of symplectic forms on
W
.
We set
v:=L_{B}^{-1}L_{C}
. We know from the classification of pairs of alternating bilinear forms, with the first one non-degenerate, that
the invariant factors of
v
are of the form
p_{1},p_{1},p_{2},p_{2},\dots

Hence, by Lemma
2.9
the ones of
B\boxplus C
are
p_{1}(t^{2}),p_{1}(t^{2}),p_{2}(t^{2}),p_{2}(t^{2}),\dots

Hence,
B\boxplus C\simeq w\oplus w
for some endomorphism
w
with invariant factors
p_{1}(t^{2}),p_{2}(t^{2}),\dots
,
and we gather that the Jordan numbers of
B\boxplus C
are all even. Hence, so are the ones of
u
.

Finally, by Proposition
2.10
the Hermitian invariants of
(H^{1}_{W},B\boxplus C)
are all hyperbolic, and hence
so are the ones of
(b,u)
.

Conversely, assume that all the Jordan numbers of
(b,u)
are even and that all its Hermitian invariants are hyperbolic.
Remember that
n_{p,k}(u)=n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},k}(u)
for all
k\geq 1
and all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
, because
u
is
b
-skew-selfadjoint (and hence similar to its opposite).
Now, we can consider a vector space
W
equipped with an automorphism
w
such that:

•

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\{t\}
such that
p(t^{2})
splits into
qq^{\mathrm{op}}
for some
q\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
, and for all
k\geq 1
, one has
n_{p,k}(w)=n_{q,k}(u)
.

•

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\{t\}
such that
p(t^{2})
is irreducible, one has
n_{p,k}(w)=n_{p(t^{2}),k}(u)
.

Since all the Jordan numbers of
u
are even, all the ones of
w
are even and it follows from the classification of pairs of symplectic forms that there exists a pair

(B,C)
of symplectic forms on
W
such that
L_{B}^{-1}L_{C}=w
.
It follows from the definition of
w
that
B\boxplus C
has the same Jordan numbers as
u
.
Finally, for every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and every
k\geq 1
, the Hermitian forms
(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)_{p,k}

and
(b,u)_{p,k}
are hyperbolic (by Proposition
2.10
for the former, by assumption for the latter), and they have the same rank. Hence
(b,u)_{p,k}\simeq(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)_{p,k}
.
It follows that
(b,u)\simeq(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)
, and we conclude that

(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

The proof of Theorem
1.12
is now complete.

4.2
Selfadjoint automorphisms

In that case, we need an additional result to deal with the
(b,u)_{p,r}
invariant when
p
is neither even nor odd.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,1)
-pair.
Let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus(\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cup\{t\})
and
r\geq 1
. Denote by
d
the degree of
p
.
Then
(-b,-u)_{p,r}=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}\bigl((b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}\bigr)^{\mathrm{op}}
.

Proof.

We consider the fields
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
and
\mathbb{L}^{\prime}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p^{\mathrm{op}})
and
the isomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{L}\overset{\simeq}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{L}^{\prime}
that takes the class of
t
mod
p
to the one of
-t
mod
p^{\mathrm{op}}
.
For a polynomial
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}[t]
, we denote its class in
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
by
\overline{\lambda}^{\mathbb{L}}
, and its class in

\mathbb{F}[t]/(p^{\mathrm{op}})
by
\overline{\lambda}^{\mathbb{L}^{\prime}}
.

Note that
p(-u)=(-1)^{d}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)
. It follows that

\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{k}=\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{k}
for all
k\geq 1
, to the effect that the quotients

\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{k+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{k}
and
\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{k+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{k}
are equal for all
k\geq 1
.
Moreover, the cokernel of the linear map
\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{r+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{r}\rightarrow\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{r}/\operatorname{Ker}p(-u)^{r-1}

induced by
p(-u)
equals the cokernel of the linear map
\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{r+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{r}\rightarrow\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{r}/\operatorname{Ker}p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{r-1}
induced by
p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)
.

Hence, the quadratic invariants
(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}
and
(-b,-u)_{p,r}
are defined on the same
\mathbb{F}
-vector space
W
. We consider the induced bilinear forms

B:(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in W^{2}\mapsto b(x,p^{\mathrm{op}}(u)^{r-1}(y))

and

B^{\prime}:(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in W^{2}\mapsto(-b)(x,p(-u)^{r-1}(y)).

Note that
B^{\prime}=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}B
.
Let
x,y
in
W
. Let
\lambda(t)\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Then

\displaystyle B(x,\lambda(u)[y])

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}B^{\prime}(x,\lambda(u)[y])

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}B^{\prime}(x,\lambda(-t)(-u)[y])

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}e_{p}\bigl(\overline{\lambda(-t)}^{\mathbb{L}}(-b,-u)_{p,r}(x,y)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}e_{p^{\mathrm{op}}}\bigl(\varphi\bigl(\overline{\lambda(-t)}^{\mathbb{L}}(-b,-u)_{p,r}(x,y)\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}e_{p^{\mathrm{op}}}\Bigl(\overline{\lambda}^{\mathbb{L}^{\prime}}\varphi\bigl((-b,-u)_{p,r}(x,y)\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=e_{p^{\mathrm{op}}}\Bigl(\overline{\lambda}^{\mathbb{L}^{\prime}}\,(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}\varphi\bigl((-b,-u)_{p,r}(x,y)\bigr)\Bigr).

It follows that

(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}(x,y)=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}\varphi\bigl((-b,-u)_{p,r}(x,y)\bigr)=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}\bigl((-b,-u)_{p,r}\bigr)^{\mathrm{op}}(x,y).

∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.13
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,1)
-pair in which
u
is an automorphism.

Assume first that
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property. Then, by Proposition
2.5
it is isometric to

(H^{1}_{W},B\boxplus C)
for some vector space
W
and some pair
(B,C)
of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms on
W
.
By Proposition
2.8
, the pairs
(H^{1}_{W},B\boxplus C)
and
(-H^{1}_{W},-B\boxplus C)
are isometric, and hence

(b,u)
is isometric to
(-b,-u)
. It follows from Proposition
4.1
that

(b,u)_{p,r}\simeq(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}^{\mathrm{op}}
for every
r\geq 1
and every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus(\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cup\{t\})

of degree
d
.

Besides, Corollary
2.12
directly shows that, for all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
, the quadratic invariant

(H^{1}_{W},B\boxplus C)_{p,r}
is skew-representable over
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
equipped with the involution that takes the class of
t
to its opposite.

Conversely, assume that
(b,u)
satisfies condition (ii) from Theorem
1.13
.
First of all, we can split
(b,u)\simeq(b_{1},u_{1})\bot\cdots\bot(b_{n},u_{n})

so that, for each
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,n\mathclose{]\!]}
, one of the following conditions holds:

•

The invariant factors of
u_{i}
all equal
p^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and some
r\geq 1
, and further still

(b_{i},u_{i})_{p,r}\simeq(b,u)_{p,r}
; in that case this quadratic invariant is skew-representable.

•

The invariant factors of
u_{i}
all equal
p^{r}
or
p^{\mathrm{op}}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
, and further still

(b_{i},u_{i})_{p,r}\simeq(b,u)_{p,r}
and
(b_{i},u_{i})_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}\simeq(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}
.

It suffices to prove that each
(b_{i},u_{i})
has the square-zero splitting property.
Hence, in the remainder of the proof, we can reduce the situation to only two cases.

Case 1.
The pair
(b,u)
has a sole quadratic invariant, attached to
(p,r)
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and some
r\geq 1
,
and this invariant is skew-representable over
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
.

Then, we know from Proposition
2.13
that
(b,u)
has the same quadratic invariants as some
(1,1)
-boxed sum, the latter having the square-zero splitting property. Hence,
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Case 2.
The pair
(b,u)
has exactly two quadratic invariants, attached to
(p,r)
and
(p^{\mathrm{op}},r)
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus(\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cup\{t\})
and some

,
and these invariants satisfy
(b,u)_{p,r}\simeq(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}^{\mathrm{op}}
where
d:=\deg(p)
.

Then, we apply Corollary
2.15
to the bilinear form
(b,u)_{p,r}
.
We can find a triple
(W,B,C)
in which
W
is a vector space and
(B,C)
is a pair of symmetric bilinear forms on

, with
B
non-degenerate,
such that

has only two non-zero quadratic invariants, attached to

and
(p^{\mathrm{op}},r)
, and

(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)_{p,r}\simeq(b,u)_{p,r}
. Yet, by Proposition
2.8
, we know that

(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)\simeq(-H_{W}^{1},-B\boxplus C)
, and hence Proposition

yields

(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}\simeq(-1)^{1+(r-1)d}((H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)_{p,r})^{\mathrm{op}}\simeq(-1)^{1+(r-1)d}((b,u)_{p,r})^{\mathrm{op}}\simeq(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}.

Hence,
(b,u)
and
(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)
have equivalent quadratic invariants, and we conclude that they are isometric.
Finally,
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.13
.

5
Symmetric forms with nilpotent endomorphisms

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.14
. Throughout, we assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
The most difficult part is to prove the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii), so we will start with the implication (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).
One of the main keys for this implication is the ability to construct pairs
(b,u)

with the square-zero splitting property in which
u
has exactly two Jordan cells, one of size
2k+1
and one of size
2k-1
:
we shall say that such pairs are
twisted
(and it is a consequence of Theorem
1.14
that, in such a pair,
the bilinear form
b
must be hyperbolic). Before we study twisted pairs, we will start with a classical result that relates the
Witt type of
b
to the ones of the
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
invariants.

5.1
On quadratic invariants and the type of
b

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent. Here, we discuss the relationship between the Witt type of
b

and the quadratic invariants of
(b,u)
.

We start with the case where all the quadratic invariants of
(b,u)
but one vanish.
So, assume that, for some
k\geq 1
, the endomorphism
u
is nilpotent with only Jordan cells of size
k
.

Case 1:

k=2l
for some
l\geq 1
. Then
\operatorname{Ker}u^{i}=\operatorname{Im}u^{k-i}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}0,k\mathclose{]\!]}
(this is obvious on a Jordan cell of size
k
).
In particular
\operatorname{Ker}u^{l}=\operatorname{Im}u^{l}=(\operatorname{Ker}u^{l})^{\bot_{b}}
, and it follows that
b
is hyperbolic.

Case 2:

k=2l+1
for some
l\geq 0
. This time around, we find
(\operatorname{Ker}u^{l})^{\bot_{b}}=\operatorname{Im}u^{l}=\operatorname{Ker}u^{l+1}
.
Classically, this yields that
b
is Witt-equivalent to the non-degenerate bilinear form it induces on the quotient space

\operatorname{Ker}u^{l+1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{l}
. Now, the bilinear form
B:(x,y)\in V^{2}\mapsto b(x,u^{k-1}(y))
reads

(x,y)\in V^{2}\mapsto\eta^{l}b(u^{l}(x),u^{l}(y))

Hence, the form induced by
B
on
V/\operatorname{Im}u=\operatorname{Ker}u^{2l+1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{2l}

is equivalent to the form induced by
\eta^{l}b
on the quotient space
\operatorname{Ker}u^{l+1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{l}
through the isomorphism

\operatorname{Ker}u^{2l+1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{2l}\overset{\simeq}{\longrightarrow}\operatorname{Ker}u^{l+1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{l}
induced by
u^{l}
.
Hence,
b
is Witt-equivalent to
\eta^{l}(b,u)_{t,k}
.

In the general case of a
(1,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)
in which
u
is nilpotent, the classification theorem shows that for every integer
k\geq 1
,
there exists a
(1,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)^{(k)}
in which
u
is nilpotent, the quadratic invariant
(b,u)^{(k)}_{t,i}
vanishes
for every positive integer
i\neq k
, and
(b,u)^{(k)}_{t,k}
is equivalent to
(b,u)_{t,k}
.
It follows that
(b,u)\simeq\underset{k\geq 1}{\bot}(b,u)^{(k)}
.
Using the previous special cases, we conclude:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent.
Then
b
is Witt-equivalent to
\underset{l\geq 0}{\bot}\eta^{l}(b,u)_{t,2l+1}
.

5.2
Twisted pairs have the square-zero splitting property

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Let
V
be a
4k
-dimensional vector space
together with a hyperbolic symmetric bilinear form
b:V^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
.
Let
\eta\in\{-1,1\}
. There exists
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
such that
(b,u)
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair with the square-zero splitting property, and

u
is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells, one of size
2k+1
and one of size
2k-1
.

Proof.

Let us choose a
b
-hyperbolic basis
\mathbf{B}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{2k},f_{1},\dots,f_{2k})
of
V
.
The matrix of
b
in that basis is

S=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{2k}\\
I_{2k}&0\end{bmatrix}
.

Next, we define
a_{1}\in\operatorname{End}(V)
by
a_{1}(e_{2i})=e_{2i-1}
and

a_{1}(f_{2i-1})=\eta f_{2i}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,k\mathclose{]\!]}
, and
a_{1}
maps all the other vectors of
\mathbf{B}
to
0
.
We define
a_{2}\in\operatorname{End}(V)
by
a_{2}(e_{2i+1})=e_{2i}
and

a_{2}(f_{2i})=\eta f_{2i+1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
,

a_{2}(f_{1})=e_{2k}
and
a_{2}(f_{2k})=\eta e_{1}
, and
a_{2}
maps all the other vectors of
\mathbf{B}
to
0
.
It is easily checked on the vectors of
\mathbf{B}
that
a_{1}^{2}=0
and
a_{2}^{2}=0
.

The respective matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
in
\mathbf{B}
are of the form

M_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&\eta A_{1}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}&C\\
0&\eta A_{2}^{T}\end{bmatrix}

where
C^{T}=\eta C
.
One checks that both
SM_{1}
and
SM_{2}
are symmetric if
\eta=1
, and skew-symmetric otherwise.
Hence, both
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are
b
-symmetric if
\eta=1
, and
b
-skew-symmetric otherwise.

To conclude, it suffices to check that
u:=a_{1}+a_{2}
is nilpotent and that it has exactly two Jordan cells, one of size
2k+1

and one of size
2k-1
.
To see this, note first that
u(e_{i})=e_{i-1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}2,2k\mathclose{]\!]}
, whereas
u(e_{1})=0
,

u(f_{1})=\eta f_{2}+e_{2k}
,
u(f_{i})=\eta f_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}2,2k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
, and

u(f_{2k})=\eta e_{1}
.

From
u(f_{1})=\eta f_{2}+e_{2k}
, we gather that

u^{i}(f_{1})=\eta^{i}f_{i+1}+e_{2k-i+1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,2k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
,
in particular
u^{2k-1}(f_{1})=\eta f_{2k}+e_{2}
, hence
u^{2k}(f_{1})=2e_{1}
and finally
u^{2k+1}(f_{1})=0
.
Hence, the subspace
W_{1}:=\operatorname{span}(f_{1},e_{1})\oplus\operatorname{span}(\eta^{i}f_{i+1}+e_{2k-i+1})_{1\leq i\leq 2k-1}

is stable under
u
and the resulting endomorphism is a Jordan cell of size
2k+1
.

Besides, one checks that
u^{i}(\eta f_{2}-e_{2k})=\eta^{i+1}f_{i+2}-e_{2k-i}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,2k-2\mathclose{]\!]}
,
and in particular
u^{2k-2}(\eta f_{2}-e_{2k})=\eta f_{2k}-e_{2}
and then
u^{2k-1}(\eta f_{2}-e_{2k})=\eta^{2}e_{1}-e_{1}=0
.
Obviously,
W_{2}:=\operatorname{span}(\eta^{i}f_{i+1}-e_{2k-i+1})_{1\leq i\leq 2k-1}
is a
(2k-1)
-dimensional subspace, it is stable
under
u
and the resulting endomorphism is a Jordan cell of size
2k-1
.
Finally, one checks that
V=W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
(note that here the assumption that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
is critical), and the claimed result follows.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells, of respective sizes
2k+1
and

2k-1
. Assume that
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}\simeq-\eta(b,u)_{t,2k-1}
. Then

(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Proof.

By the previous lemma, there is a
(1,\eta)
-pair
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
in which
u
is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells, of respective sizes
2k+1
and
2k-1
, and
b^{\prime}
is hyperbolic.
Since
b^{\prime}
is Witt-equivalent to
\eta^{k}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}\bot\eta^{k-1}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}
,
it follows that
\eta^{k}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}\simeq-\eta^{k-1}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}
,
that is
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}\simeq-\eta(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}
.
Now, choose a nonzero value
\alpha
(respectively,
\beta
) represented by
(b,u)_{t,2k-1}
(respectively, by
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}
).
Then, for
b^{\prime\prime}:=\alpha\beta^{-1}b^{\prime}
, we see that
(b^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair, with sole non-trivial invariants
(b^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}=\alpha\beta^{-1}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}
and

(b^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}=\alpha\beta^{-1}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}
. The first one represents
\alpha
, and the second one
-\eta\alpha
.
It follows from our assumptions that
(b^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k-1}\simeq(b,u)_{t,2k-1}
and
(b^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}\simeq(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
,
and we deduce from the classification of
(1,\eta)
-pairs that
(b^{\prime\prime},u^{\prime})
is isometric to
(b,u)
.
Of course, the
b^{\prime}
-selfadjoint (respectively,
b^{\prime}
-alternating) endomorphisms are also
b^{\prime\prime}
-selfadjoint (respectively,
b^{\prime\prime}
-alternating) and hence
u^{\prime}
is the sum of two such square-zero endomorphisms. The conclusion then follows from the previous isometry.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with only Jordan cells of sizes
2k+1
and

2k-1
. Assume that
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}\simeq-\eta(b,u)_{t,2k-1}
. Then

(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Proof.

Let us take an orthogonal basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
for the form
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
, and denote by
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}

the values of the associated quadratic form at the vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
.
By assumption, we can find an orthogonal basis
(f_{1},\dots,f_{n})
for the form
(b,u)_{t,2k-1}

such that the corresponding values of the associated quadratic form are
-\eta\alpha_{1},\dots,-\eta\alpha_{n}
.
By the classification of
(1,\eta)
-pairs, we can find, for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,n\mathclose{]\!]}
,
such a pair
(b_{i},u_{i})
in which
u_{i}
is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells, one of size
2k+1
and one of size
2k-1
,
and
(b^{(i)},u^{(i)})_{t,2k+1}
represents
\alpha_{i}
and
(b_{i},u_{i})_{t,2k-1}
represents
-\eta\alpha_{i}
.
Then,
(b,u)\simeq(b^{(1)},u^{(1)})\bot\cdots\bot(b^{(n)},u^{(n)})
.
By Corollary
5.3
, each pair
(b^{(i)},u^{(i)})
has the square-zero splitting property, and we conclude
that so does
(b,u)
.
∎

5.3
Reconstructing pairs having the square-zero splitting property

We now have all the tools to prove the implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) in Theorem
1.14
.

We start with simple cases:

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\eta\in\{-1,1\}
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with only Jordan cells of even size.
Then
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Proof.

Assume first that
\eta=-1
. The assumptions yield that all the Jordan numbers of
u
are even.
We can consider a vector space
W
equipped with a nilpotent endomorphism
v
such that
n_{t,k}(v)=\frac{1}{2}n_{t,k}(u)

for all
k\geq 1
. Then the pair
H_{1,\eta}(v)
has the same Jordan numbers as
u
, with only Jordan cells of even size, all attached to the irreducible polynomial
t
. By the classification of
(1,-1)
-pairs, we deduce that
H_{1,\eta}(v)\simeq(b,u)
. Besides, since
v
is nilpotent it is the sum of two square-zero
endomorphisms of
W
, and hence
H_{1,\eta}(v)
has the square-zero splitting property. We conclude that so does
(b,u)
.

Assume now that
\eta=1
. Then, we can split
(b,u)\simeq(b_{1},u_{1})\bot\cdots\bot(b_{n},u_{n})

so that each
u_{i}
is a Jordan cell of even size
k_{i}
.
Let
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,n\mathclose{]\!]}
, and choose a non-zero value
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}\smallsetminus\{0\}

that is represented by the quadratic invariant
(b_{i},u_{i})_{t,k_{i}}
.
By Lemma
2.16
, there exists a triple
(W,B,C)
consisting of a vector space
W
and of a pair

(B,C)
of symmetric bilinear forms on
W
, with
B
non-degenerate, such that
B\boxplus C

is cyclic with minimal polynomial
t^{k_{i}}
, and the quadratic invariant
(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)_{t,k_{i}}
represents
\alpha_{i}
;
as this quadratic invariant is the sole non-trivial invariant of
(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)
, it is equivalent to

(b_{i},u_{i})_{t,k_{i}}
and we conclude that
(b_{i},u_{i})\simeq(H_{W}^{1},B\boxplus C)
.
Hence,
(b_{i},u_{i})
has the square-zero splitting property.
We conclude that so does
(b,u)
.
∎

In the next step, we tackle the pairs
(b,u)
, where
u
is nilpotent with only Jordan cells of odd size,
and whose quadratic invariants are all hyperbolic.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\eta\in\{-1,1\}
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with only Jordan cells of odd size.
Assume that
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
is hyperbolic for all
k\geq 0
. Then
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Proof.

The assumptions show that all the Jordan numbers of
u
are even.
Let us choose a nilpotent endomorphism
v
of a vector space such that
n_{t,k}(v)=\frac{1}{2}n_{t,k}(u)

for all
k\geq 1
(and in particular
v
has only Jordan cells of odd size).
Then the quadratic invariants of
H_{1,\eta}(v)
attached to the pairs
(t,2k+1)
are all hyperbolic, and all the other ones vanish.
Moreover
n_{t,k}(h_{\eta}(v))=2\,n_{t,k}(v)=n_{t,k}(u)
for all
k\geq 1
.
Since hyperbolic symmetric bilinear forms are characterized by their rank up to equivalence, it follows from the classification
of
(1,\eta)
-pairs that
(b,u)\simeq H_{1,\eta}(v)
. Finally,
H_{1,\eta}(v)
has the square-zero splitting property
because so does
v
by Botha’s theorem (see Proposition
2.1
).
We conclude that
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.
∎

Now, we extend our study to the situation where
u
has Jordan cells of odd size only.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\eta\in\{-1,1\}
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with only Jordan cells of odd size,
and
\eta^{k}(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
for every integer
k\geq 0
.
Then
(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Proof.

We shall say that a
(1,\eta)
-pair
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
satisfies condition
(\mathcal{C})
whenever

\eta^{k}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}\,(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2i+1}
for every integer
k\geq 0
.

We prove the result by induction on the dimension of the underlying space. The result is obvious for
0
-dimensional spaces, and more generally
when
u=0
(in that case it suffices to take the summands of
u
equal to zero).

So now we assume that
u\neq 0
. The nilindex of
u
reads
2\ell+1
for some
\ell\geq 1
.
Write
B_{i}:=(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
for all
i\geq 0
and note that
B_{\ell}
is non-zero, whereas
B_{k}=0
for all
k>\ell
.

Case 1:
B_{\ell}
is isotropic.

Then we can split
B_{\ell}=H\bot B^{\prime}
where
H
is a hyperbolic form on a
2
-dimensional space, and
B^{\prime}
is a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form. Using the classification of
(1,\eta)
-pairs, this helps us split
(b,u)\simeq(b_{1},u_{1})\bot(b_{2},u_{2})

in which:

•

(b_{1},u_{1})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair with exactly two Jordan cells, both of size
2\ell+1
, and

(b_{1},u_{1})_{t,2\ell+1}
is hyperbolic;

•

(b_{2},u_{2})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair with only Jordan cells of odd size,

(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2\ell+1}\simeq B^{\prime}
and
(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2k+1}\simeq(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
for all
k\neq\ell
.

By Proposition
5.6
,
(b_{1},u_{1})
has the square-zero splitting property.

Next, we show that
(b_{2},u_{2})
satisfies condition
(\mathcal{C})
: indeed, for all
k\neq\ell
,
the forms
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2i+1}
and
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}B_{i}

have the same non-isotropic parts up to equivalence, whereas
\eta^{k}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2k+1}\simeq\eta^{k}B_{k}
;
moreover
\underset{i>\ell}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2i+1}
equals zero, and hence its non-isotropic part is a subform of

\eta^{\ell}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2\ell+1}
. Using the fact that
(b,u)
satisfies condition
(\mathcal{C})
, we deduce that so does

(b_{2},u_{2})
.

Hence, by induction
(b_{2},u_{2})
has the square-zero splitting property, and we conclude that so does
(b,u)
.

Case 2:
B_{\ell}
is nonisotropic.

By condition
(\mathcal{C})
, we can split
B_{\ell-1}\simeq(-\eta B_{\ell})\bot\varphi
for some non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
\varphi
.
This helps us split
(b,u)\simeq(b_{1},u_{1})\bot(b_{2},u_{2})

in which:

•

(b_{1},u_{1})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair with only Jordan cells of size
2\ell-1
or
2\ell+1

(b_{1},u_{1})_{t,2\ell+1}\simeq B_{\ell}
and
(b_{1},u_{1})_{t,2\ell-1}\simeq-\eta B_{\ell}
;

•

(b_{2},u_{2})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair with only Jordan cells of odd size at most
2\ell-1
,

(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2\ell-1}\simeq\varphi
and

(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2k+1}\simeq B_{k}
for every
k\in\mathopen{[\![}0,\ell-2\mathclose{]\!]}
.

By Corollary
5.4
,
(b_{1},u_{1})
has the square-zero splitting property.

To check that
(b_{2},u_{2})
satisfies condition
(\mathcal{C})
, we note first that, for all

k\leq\ell-2
, we have

\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}B_{i}\simeq\bigl((-\eta^{\ell}B_{\ell})\bot(\eta^{\ell}B_{\ell})\bigr)\bot\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2i+1}
, and since the form

(-\eta^{\ell}B_{\ell})\bot(\eta^{\ell}B_{\ell})
is hyperbolic this shows that the nonisotropic parts of

\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}B_{i}
are equivalent to those of
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2i+1}
.
This is enough to check condition
(\mathcal{C})
for
(b_{2},u_{2})
at each integer
k\leq\ell-2
.
Besides, for
k\geq\ell-1
we have
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2i+1}=0
and hence condition

(\mathcal{C})
is trivially satisfied at
k
for
(b_{2},u_{2})
. We conclude that
(b_{2},u_{2})
satisfies condition
(\mathcal{C})
.
Hence, by induction
(b_{2},u_{2})
has the square-zero splitting property, and we conclude that so does
(b,u)
.

Hence, our inductive proof is complete.
∎

Now, we complete the proof of (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) in Theorem
1.14
.
So, let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent and, for all

k\geq 0
the bilinear form
\eta^{k}\,(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}\,(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
.
Using the classification of indecomposable
(1,\eta)
-pairs, we can split

(b,u)\simeq(b_{1},u_{1})\bot(b_{2},u_{2})
so that:

•

(b_{1},u_{1})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u_{1}
is nilpotent with all its Jordan cells of odd size;

•

(b_{2},u_{2})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u_{2}
is nilpotent with all its Jordan cells of even size.

By Proposition
5.5
, the pair
(b_{2},u_{2})
has the square-zero splitting property.
Next, we see that
(b_{2},u_{2})_{t,2k+1}\simeq(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
for every integer
k\geq 0
, and it follows that

(b_{2},u_{2})
satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
5.7
.
Hence,
(b_{2},u_{2})
has the square-zero splitting property, and we conclude that so does
(b,u)
.

Thus, it is now proved that in Theorem
1.14
condition (i) implies condition (ii).
The proof of the converse implication is spread over the next four sections.

5.4
Three reduction techniques to lower the nilindex

Here, we shall examine three techniques to reduce the nilindex in the analysis of a
(1,\eta)
-pair
(b,u)

in which
u
is nilpotent.

5.4.1
Descent

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with nilindex
\nu\geq 3
(and underlying vector space
V
).

Here, we compute the invariants of the induced pair
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Im}u}
, which we will locally denote by
(\overline{b},\overline{u})
.
Noting that
(\operatorname{Im}u)^{\bot_{b}}=\operatorname{Ker}u
, we see that the underlying vector space of the pair
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Im}u}
is

W:=\operatorname{Im}u/(\operatorname{Ker}u\cap\operatorname{Im}u)
.

Obviously
\overline{u}^{\nu-1}=0
. Let us look at the quadratic invariants of
(b,u)
(for pairs of type
(t,r)
with
r\geq 1
).
To this end, we start by examining the case where all the Jordan cells of
u
have the same size.

So, assume that, for some integer
r\geq 1
, all the Jordan cells of
u
have size
r
.
If
r=1
then
\operatorname{Im}u=\{0\}
and
(\overline{b},\overline{u})
is the zero pair.
If
r=2
then
\operatorname{Im}u=\operatorname{Ker}u
and again
(\overline{b},\overline{u})
is the zero pair.

Assume now that
r>2
.
We have
\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}=\operatorname{Im}u^{r-k}
for all
k\in\mathopen{[\![}0,r\mathclose{]\!]}
, and
\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}=V
for all
k\geq r
.
Hence, the source space of
\overline{u}
is
\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}/\operatorname{Ker}u
.
Then,
\operatorname{Ker}\overline{u}^{i}=\operatorname{Ker}u^{i+1}/\operatorname{Ker}u
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}0,r-2\mathclose{]\!]}
, and

\operatorname{Ker}\overline{u}^{i}=\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}/\operatorname{Ker}u
for all
i>r-2
.
It follows that all the Jordan cells of
\overline{u}
have size
r-2
(and there are as many as there are Jordan cells of
u
).
Hence,
(\overline{b},\overline{u})_{t,r-2}
is the sole non-zero quadratic invariant of
(\overline{b},\overline{u})
.
It is defined as the bilinear form induced by
(x,y)\mapsto\overline{b}(x,\overline{u}^{r-3}(y))
on the quotient space of
\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}/\operatorname{Ker}u
with
\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-2}/\operatorname{Ker}u
, and it is therefore naturally equivalent to the bilinear form
induced by
(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u^{r-3}(y))
on the quotient space
\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-2}
.

The linear map
u
induces an isomorphism from
V/\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}
to
\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-2}
.
Noting that
b(u(x),u^{r-3}(u(y)))=\eta\,b(x,u^{r-1}(y))
for all
x,y
in
V
, we find that this isomorphism defines an isometry from

V/\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}
equipped with
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\mapsto b(x,u^{r-1}(y))
to

\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-1}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-2}
equipped with
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\mapsto\eta\,\overline{b}(x,u^{r-3}(y))
.
Therefore,
(\overline{b},\overline{u})_{t,r-2}\simeq\eta\,(b,u)_{t,r}
.

Let us come back to the general case where
u
is nilpotent.
The pair
(b,u)
is known to split into
(b,u)\simeq\underset{i\geq 1}{\bot}(b_{i},u_{i})

where each
(b_{i},u_{i})
is a
(1,\eta)
-pair,
(b_{i},u_{i})_{t,k}
vanishes for all
k\neq i
, and
(b_{i},u_{i})_{t,i}\simeq(b,u)_{t,i}
.
Clearly, the previous reduction is compatible with isomorphisms and orthogonal direct sums. Piecing together the information we have gathered on
the above special case, we obtain the following conclusion.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with nilindex
\nu\geq 1
.
Then:

(i)

For all
r\geq 1
, one has
((b,u)^{\operatorname{Im}u})_{t,r}\simeq\eta\,(b,u)_{t,r+2}
.

(ii)

The nilindex of the endomorphism component of
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Im}u}
equals
\min(1,\nu-2)
.

5.4.2
Twisted descent

Here, we use a slightly different process.
Again, we let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent with nilindex
\nu\geq 3
(and underlying vector space
V
).
This time around, we consider the induced pair
(\overline{b},\overline{u}):=(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u}
.

Noting that
(\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u)^{\bot_{b}}=\operatorname{Im}u\cap\operatorname{Ker}u
is included in
\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u
,
we see that the underlying vector space of the induced pair is the quotient space

(\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u)/(\operatorname{Ker}u\cap\operatorname{Im}u)
.
From there, the results are very similar to the one of the previous section, with the notable exception
of the case where
u
has only Jordan cells of size
1
(i.e.
u=0
).
In that case indeed, we have
(\overline{b},\overline{u})=(b,u)
, leading to

(\overline{b},\overline{u})_{t,1}=(b,u)_{t,1}
(instead of
(\overline{b},\overline{u})_{t,1}=0
).

Using the same method as in the previous paragraph, we obtain:

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent.
Then:

(i)

For all
r\geq 2
,
((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u})_{t,r}\simeq\eta\,(b,u)_{t,r+2}
.

(ii)

((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u})_{t,1}\simeq(b,u)_{t,1}\,\bot\,\eta\,(b,u)_{t,3}
.

Note that if
u^{3}=0
then the endomorphism component of
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u}
vanishes,
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u}\simeq((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u})_{t,1}\simeq(b,u)_{t,1}\bot\eta(b,u)_{t,3}
.

5.4.3
Folding

Here, we use yet another type of induced pairs.
Again, we let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent.
Let
k\geq 1
.

Here, we consider the induced pair
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}
, which is defined on the quotient space

W:=\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}/(\operatorname{Im}u^{k}\cap\operatorname{Ker}u^{k})
because
(\operatorname{Ker}u^{k})^{\bot_{b}}=\operatorname{Im}u^{k}
.

Again, we compute the quadratic invariants of
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}
as functions of those
of
(b,u)
by considering the special case where
u
has only Jordan cells of size
r
for some
r\geq 1
.
If
k\geq r
then
\operatorname{Im}u^{k}\cap\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}=\{0\}
and
\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}=V
, and so

(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}=(b,u)
.

Assume now that
k<r
. Then
\operatorname{Im}u^{k}=\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-k}
and hence

\operatorname{Im}u^{k}\cap\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}=\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-k}\cap\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}=\operatorname{Ker}u^{\min(r-k,k)}
.

•

If
k\leq r-k
then
W=\{0\}
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}
vanishes.

•

Assume that
k>r-k
. Then
W=\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{r-k}
, and
\overline{u}
has only Jordan cells of size
2k-r
. The
((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}})_{t,2k-r}
invariant is the bilinear form induced by

(\overline{x},\overline{y})\mapsto b(x,u^{2k-r-1}(y))
on
\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}/\operatorname{Ker}u^{k-1}
.
Noting that
b(u^{r-k}(x),u^{2k-r-1}(u^{r-k}(y)))=\eta^{r-k}b(x,u^{r-1}(y))
for all
x,y
in
V
, we obtain that
the linear map
x\mapsto u^{r-k}(x)
induces an isometry
from
(b,u)_{t,r}
to
\eta^{r-k}((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}})_{t,2k-r}
.
Hence,
((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}})_{t,2k-r}\simeq\eta^{r-k}(b,u)_{t,r}
, and it is the sole possible non-vanishing quadratic invariant of
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}
.

As in Section
5.4.1
, we piece the previous results together to obtain the general form of the quadratic invariants of
(\overline{b},\overline{u})
:

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent. Let
k\geq 1
.
Then:

(i)

For all
r\in\mathopen{[\![}1,k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
,
\bigl((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}\bigr)_{t,r}\simeq(b,u)_{t,r}\bot\eta^{k+r}(b,u)_{t,2k-r}
.

(ii)

\bigl((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}\bigr)_{t,k}\simeq(b,u)_{t,k}
.

(iii)

For all
r>k
,
\bigl((b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}\bigr)_{t,r}=0
.

(iv)

The endomorphism component
\overline{u}
of
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Ker}u^{k}}
satisfies
\overline{u}^{k}=0
.

5.5
The key lemma: statement and consequences

We are ready for the main key in our study of nilpotent endomorphisms.
Let us first recall some notation and terminology. The rank of a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form is simply the
dimension of the underlying vector space. The Witt index of such a form
B
, denoted by
\nu(B)
,
is the greatest dimension for a totally
B
-isotropic subspace.
Remember that, given two such forms
B
and
B^{\prime}
, we say that
B
Witt-simplifies
B^{\prime}

whenever every nonisotropic part of
B^{\prime}
is equivalent to a subform of
-B
.

Here is our key result.

Lemma 5.11

(Key lemma)
.

Let
\eta\in\{1,-1\}
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair with the square-zero splitting property, in which
u^{3}=0
.
Then
(b,u)_{t,1}
Witt-simplifies
\eta\,(b,u)_{t,3}
.

The proof of this lemma is the most technical part of the present manuscript, so we wait until the next section to give it.
Here, we shall show how this lemma, combined with the processes described in the previous paragraphs,
helps one recover the necessary condition featured in Theorem
1.14
.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
(1,\eta)
-pair with the square-zero splitting property.
Then
\eta^{k}(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof of Proposition
5.12
, assuming the validity of Lemma

5.11
.

By induction, we deduce that

\underset{i>\ell}{\bot}\eta^{i+1}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}

Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>\ell}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}

It only remains to obtain the conclusion for

Witt-simplifies

Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>0}{\bot}\eta^{i}(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{t,2i+1}

Finally, remembering that

Witt-simplifies

Since
V_{3}\subset\operatorname{Ker}u^{2}
, it follows that the projection
\overline{\pi(\operatorname{Ker}f)}
in
V/(\operatorname{Ker}u^{2})

of the subspace
\pi(\operatorname{Ker}f)
is totally
B_{3}
-isotropic, yielding

\dim\overline{\pi(\operatorname{Ker}f)}\leq\nu(B_{3}).

Yet
\dim\overline{\pi(\operatorname{Ker}f)}=\dim\pi(\operatorname{Ker}f)
because
V_{2}\cap\operatorname{Ker}u^{2}=\{0\}
, whereas

\dim\pi(\operatorname{Ker}f)=\dim\operatorname{Ker}f
because
\operatorname{Ker}f\cap V_{3}=\{0\}
.
We conclude by the rank theorem that

\displaystyle\operatorname{rk}f

\displaystyle=\dim V_{2}+\dim V_{3}-\dim\operatorname{Ker}f

\displaystyle\geq\dim V_{2}+\dim V_{3}-\nu(B_{3})

\displaystyle\geq\dim V_{2}+\dim V_{1}-\nu(B_{3})=\dim(V/\operatorname{Ker}u^{2})-\nu(B_{3})=\operatorname{rk}(B_{3})-\nu(B_{3}).

Hence, the proof of the key lemma is finally complete, as well as the proof of Theorem
1.14
.

6
The Hermitian case

6.1
The problem in the Hermitian case

In this final section, we consider the problem in the Hermitian case.
Throughout, the field
\mathbb{F}
is equipped with a non-identity involution
x\mapsto x^{\bullet}
,
so that
\mathbb{F}
is a separable extension of degree
2
of the subfield
\mathbb{K}:=\{x\in\mathbb{F}:\;x^{\bullet}=x\}
.
The given involution is extended to an involution
p\mapsto p^{\bullet}
of
\mathbb{F}[t]
by applying it
coefficient-wise. Given a vector space
V
, we denote by
V^{\star 1/2}
the vector space of all semi-linear forms on
V
.

Remember our convention that a Hermitian form on a vector space over
\mathbb{F}
is right-linear and left-semi-linear.
Given such a Hermitian form
b
, on a vector space
V
, we obtain a linear mapping

L_{b}:x\in V\mapsto b(-,x)\in V^{\star 1/2},

and this mapping is an isomorphism if and only if
b
is non-degenerate.

A
1/2
-pair is a pair
(b,u)
consisting of a non-degenerate Hermitian form
b
on an
\mathbb{F}
-vector space
V

and of a
b
-selfadjoint endomorphism
u
of
V
, so that

\forall(x,y)\in V^{2},\;b(u(x),y)=b(x,u(y)).

In that case,
c:(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u(y))
is another Hermitian form on
V
.

A Hermitian pair is a pair
(b,c)
of Hermitian forms on a given vector space (over
\mathbb{F}
, of finite-dimension).
In such a pair, if
b
is non-degenerate then
c
reads
(x,y)\mapsto b(x,u(y))
for a unique
b
-selfadjoint endomorphism

u
. Hence,
1/2
-pairs naturally correspond to Hermitian pairs whose first component is non-degenerate,
and
1/2
-pairs whose second components are automorphisms naturally correspond to Hermitian pairs in which both components are non-degenerate.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair with underlying space
V
. We say that it has the
square-zero splitting property
when there are square-zero

b
-selfadjoint endomorphisms
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
such that
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
.

For
1/2
-pairs, we have the following notions, defined in the same way as for
(\varepsilon,\eta)
-pairs:

•

isometric pairs;

•

the induced pair
(b,u)^{W}
for a linear subspace
W
of the underlying space;

•

orthogonal sums of pairs.

Again, we have the following principles:

(i)

If two
1/2
-pairs are isometric and one has the square-zero splitting property, then so does the other one.

(ii)

If two
1/2
-pairs have the square-zero splitting property, then so does their orthogonal sum.

(iii)

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair, with underlying space
V
. Let
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
be square-zero
b
-selfadjoint endomorphisms of
V

such that
u=a_{1}+a_{2}
. Let finally
W
be a linear subspace of
V
that is stable under both
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.
Then the induced pair
(b,u)^{W}
has the square-zero splitting property.

Our aim is to classify, up to isometry, the
1/2
-pairs with the square-zero splitting property.
To do so, we need to recall first the relevant invariants of such a pair
(b,u)
under isometry, beyond the Jordan numbers
of
u
(which, in general, do not suffice to characterize the pair
(b,u)
up to isometry).

So, let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
be such that
p^{\bullet}=p
, i.e.
p
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
and has all its coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
.
Let
r\geq 1
be an integer. Since
p^{r-1}
has its coefficients in
\mathbb{K}
,

(x,y)\mapsto b(x,p(u)^{r-1}(y))
is a Hermitian form on
V^{2}
.
It induces a non-degenerate Hermitian form
B
on the cokernel
W_{p,r}
of the injective linear map

\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{r+1}/\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{r}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{r}/\operatorname{Ker}p(u)^{r-1}

induced by
p(u)
.
This cokernel has a naturally induced structure of vector space over
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
,
which is equipped with the involution induced by the one of
\mathbb{F}
(this works because
p^{\bullet}=p
),
and for this structure one uses the fact that
u
is
b
-selfadjoint to find that

B(q^{\bullet}\,x,y)=B(x,q\,y)
for all
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
and all
(x,y)\in(W_{p,r})^{2}
.

Now, we use the extension process of Section
1.4.1
.
Let
(x,y)\in(W_{p,r})^{2}
.
The mapping

\lambda\in\mathbb{L}\mapsto B(x,\lambda\,y)\in\mathbb{F}

is
\mathbb{F}
-linear, and hence there is a unique scalar
B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)\in\mathbb{L}
such that

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{L},\;B(x,\lambda\,y)=e_{p}\bigl(\lambda\,B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)\bigr).

Note that

\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{L},\;B(y,\lambda x)=B(\lambda x,y)^{\bullet}=B(x,\lambda^{\bullet}y)^{\bullet}=e_{p}\bigl(\lambda^{\bullet}B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)\bigr)^{\bullet}=e_{p}\bigl(\lambda B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)^{\bullet}\bigr)

and hence
B^{\mathbb{L}}(y,x)=B^{\mathbb{L}}(x,y)^{\bullet}
.
As
B
is right-
\mathbb{F}
-linear, it is clear that
B^{\mathbb{L}}
is right-
\mathbb{L}
-linear, and we conclude that
B^{\mathbb{L}}

is Hermitian. Finally, since
b
is non-degenerate it is clear that so is
B^{\mathbb{L}}
.
We set
(b,u)_{p,r}:=B^{\mathbb{L}}
and call it the
Hermitian invariant
of
(b,u)
attached to the pair
(p,r)
.

In contrast with the case of
(1,1)
-pairs, note that such invariants only appear with selfadjoint monic irreducible polynomials, not with all monic irreducible polynomials!

Now, we can state the classification theorem for
1/2
-pairs. It can be retrieved with some effort from Theorem 4 of
[
14
]

(where there is no need to assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
).

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(b,u)
and
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
be two
1/2
-pairs over
\mathbb{F}
.
These pairs are isometric if and only if both the following conditions hold:

(i)

n_{p,r}(u)=n_{p,r}(u^{\prime})
for every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p\neq p^{\bullet}
, and every integer
r\geq 1
.

(ii)

The Hermitian forms
(b,u)_{p,r}
and
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})_{p,r}
are equivalent for every
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p=p^{\bullet}
, and every
integer
r\geq 1
.

Moreover, there is little limitation on the possible Jordan numbers of
u
and the possible Hermitian invariants of
(b,u)
:
one limitation is that
n_{p,r}(u)=n_{p^{\bullet},r}(u)
for all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and all
r\geq 1
.
In other words, if we take any family
(m_{p,r})_{p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t],r\geq 1}
of natural numbers,
and any family
(H_{p,r})_{p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t],r\geq 1}
of non-degenerate Hermitian forms (where
H_{p,r}
is a Hermitian form over
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
),
and both those families have only finitely many non-zero terms and
m_{p,r}=m_{p^{\bullet},r}
for all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
,
then there exists a
1/2
-pair
(b,u)
such that
n_{p,r}(u)=m_{p,r}
for all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
,
and
(b,u)_{p,r}
is equivalent to
H_{p,r}
for all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
.

Consequently, indecomposable
1/2
-pairs are the ones of the following two types:

•

The pairs
(b,u)
in which
u
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and some
r\geq 1
;

•

The pairs
(b,u)
in which
u
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p^{r}(p^{\bullet})^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
and some
r\geq 1
.

6.2
The solution

As we shall see, our theorem for
1/2
-pairs is very reminiscient to the theorem on
(1,1)
-pairs.
First of all, we use the Fitting decomposition to split the problem into two subproblems.
Like in Section
1.3
, one proves that if a
1/2
-pair
(b,u)
has the square zero splitting property, then so
do
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}
, while in general
(b,u)\simeq(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}\bot(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}
.
This leads to the following principle:

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair. For
(b,u)
to have the square-zero splitting property, it is necessary and sufficient
that both
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Co}(u)}
and
(b,u)^{\operatorname{Nil}(u)}
have the square-zero splitting property.

For automorphisms, we need an additional notion:

Definition 6.2
.

Let
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
. A Hermitian form
\varphi
over
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)

is called
odd-representable
whenever there is a
\varphi
-orthogonal basis
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
such that, for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,n\mathclose{]\!]}
,
\varphi(e_{i},e_{i})
is the class of an odd polynomial with entries in
\mathbb{K}
modulo
p
(i.e. there exists
s_{i}\in\mathbb{K}[t]
such that
\varphi(e_{i},e_{i})=\overline{ts_{i}(t^{2})}^{\mathbb{L}}
).

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair where
u
is an automorphism. For
(b,u)
to have the square-zero splitting property, it is necessary and sufficient that
all the following conditions hold:

(i)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
, the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{p,r}
is odd-representable.

(ii)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
such that
p^{\mathrm{op}}\neq p
, and for all
r\geq 1
, one has

(b,u)_{p,r}\simeq(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}^{\mathrm{op}}
where
d:=\deg p
.

(iii)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
, the Jordan numbers
n_{p,r}(u)
and
n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}(u)
are equal.

Theorem 6.4
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair where
u
is an automorphism. For
(b,u)
to have the square-zero splitting property, it is necessary and sufficient that
all the following conditions hold:

(i)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and all odd
r\geq 1
, one has
n_{p,r}(u)=0
.

(ii)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
, the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{p,r}
is odd-representable.

Now, we turn to nilpotent endomorphisms. Remember that Witt theory applies in a similar way to Hermitian forms as to symmetric bilinear forms.
In particular, we have the Witt index
\nu(H)
of a non-degenerate Hermitian form
H
.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be two non-degenerate Hermitian forms (on finite-dimensional vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}
).
The following conditions are equivalent:

•

Each nonisotropic part of
H^{\prime}
is equivalent to a subform of
-H
;

•

One has
\nu(H^{\prime})+\nu(H^{\prime}\bot H)\geq\operatorname{rk}(H^{\prime})
.

When they are satisfied, we say that
H

Witt-simplifies

H^{\prime}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair in which
u
is nilpotent.
The following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

u
is the sum of two square-zero
b
-Hermitian endomorphisms.

(ii)

For every integer
k\geq 0
, the Hermitian form

(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
.

Note that here the result holds whatever the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}
.

Combining the above two theorems finally leads to the full classification:

Theorem 6.6
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair.
For
(b,u)
to have the square-zero splitting property, it is necessary and sufficient that all the following conditions hold:

(i)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
, the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{p,r}
is odd-representable.

(ii)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
such that
p^{\mathrm{op}}\neq p
, and for all
r\geq 1
, one has

(b,u)_{p,r}\simeq(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}^{\mathrm{op}}
where
d:=\deg p
.

(iii)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
, the Jordan numbers
n_{p,r}(u)
and
n_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}(u)
are equal.

(iv)

For every integer
k\geq 0
, the Hermitian form

(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
.

Theorem 6.7
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair.
For
(b,u)
to have the square-zero splitting property, it is necessary and sufficient that all the following conditions hold:

(i)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
and all odd
r\geq 1
, one has
n_{p,r}(u)=0
.

(ii)

For all
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and all
r\geq 1
, the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{p,r}
is odd-representable.

(iii)

For every integer
k\geq 0
, the Hermitian form

(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
Witt-simplifies
\underset{i>k}{\bot}(b,u)_{t,2i+1}
.

6.3
Examples

6.3.1
Complex numbers

We consider the complex field
\mathbb{C}
with the standard involution
z\mapsto\overline{z}
.
The only irreducible polynomials over
\mathbb{C}
are the ones of degree 1, so in Theorem
6.6

condition (i) is void.

Condition (iii) can be reformulated as saying that for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\mathbb{R}
and all
r\geq 1
, the Jordan numbers

n_{t-\lambda,r}(u)
and
n_{t+\lambda,r}(u)
are equal.

Now, remember that non-degenerate Hermitian forms
H
over
\mathbb{C}
(on finite-dimensional vector spaces)
are classified by their inertia
(s_{+}(H),s_{-}(H))
.

Hence, condition (ii) from Theorem
6.6
can be reformulated as follows: for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\smallsetminus\{0\}
and all
r\geq 1
,
either
r
is even and
s_{+}((b,u)_{t-\lambda,r})=s_{+}((b,u)_{t+\lambda,r})
and
s_{-}((b,u)_{t-\lambda,r})=s_{-}((b,u)_{t+\lambda,r})
,
or
r
is odd and
s_{+}((b,u)_{t-\lambda,r})=s_{-}((b,u)_{t+\lambda,r})
and
s_{-}((b,u)_{t-\lambda,r})=s_{+}((b,u)_{t+\lambda,r})
.

Finally, condition (iv) from Theorem
6.6
can be rephrased in terms of inertia of Hermitian invariants, just like
condition (iii) from Theorem
1.15
was rephrased over the field of real numbers in Section
1.6
(with
\eta=1
).

6.3.2
Finite fields

Over finite fields with a non-identity involution, non-degenerate Hermitian forms are classified by their rank.
Hence, in Theorem
6.6
, conditions (i) to (iii) can be simplified as
saying that
u
is similar to
-u
, and condition (iv) is simplified as follows:
for all
k\geq 0
,
n_{t,2k+1}(u)>0
if
\sum_{i=k+1}^{+\infty}n_{t,2i+1}(u)
is odd.

Likewise, in Theorem
6.7
, conditions (i) to (ii) can be simplified
as saying that all the invariant factors of
u
are even or odd, and condition (iii) is simplified just like for fields of characteristic different from
2
.

6.4
Hermitian extensions

Let
V
be a finite-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{F}
.
We equip the space
W:=V\times V^{\star 1/2}
with the hyperbolic Hermitian form

H_{V}^{1/2}:\bigl((x,\varphi),(y,\psi)\bigr)\longmapsto\varphi(y)^{\bullet}+\psi(x).

Let
u\in\operatorname{End}(V)
. We consider its half-transpose, denoted by
u^{t/2}
and defined as follows:

\varphi\in V^{\star 1/2}\longmapsto\varphi\circ u\in V^{\star 1/2}.

We set

h_{1/2}(u):=u\oplus u^{t/2}.

One checks that
h_{1/2}(u)
is
H_{V}^{1/2}
-selfadjoint, and we denote by

H_{1/2}(u)
the
1/2
-pair
(H_{V}^{1/2},h_{1/2}(u))
.

Now, assume that
u=u_{1}+u_{2}
for square-zero endomorphisms
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
of
V
.
Then, one checks that
h_{1/2}(u)=h_{1/2}(u_{1})+h_{1/2}(u_{2})
and that
\bigl(h_{1/2}(u_{1})\bigr)^{2}=0
and

\bigl(h_{1/2}(u_{2})\bigr)^{2}=0
. It follows that
H_{1/2}(u)
has the square-zero splitting property.

Besides, one checks that, for endomorphisms
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
of respective vector spaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, we have
an isometry

H_{1/2}(u_{1}\oplus u_{2})\simeq H_{1/2}(u_{1})\bot H_{1/2}(u_{2}).

We can now state an important result on the Hermitian invariants of
H_{1/2}(u)
.

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
u
be an endomorphism of a vector space.
Then all the Hermitian invariants of
H_{1/2}(u)
are hyperbolic.

The proof is essentially similar to the one of Proposition
2.2
, and the adaptation is effortless.

6.5
Hyperbolic boxed sums

Now, we turn to the adaptation of boxed sums.
Let
(b,c)
be a pair of sesquilinear forms on a vector space
V
, with
b
non-degenerate.
We obtain
linear
mappings

L_{c}:x\in V\mapsto c(-,x)\in V^{\star 1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad L_{b}:x\in V\mapsto b(-,x)\in V^{\star 1/2},

the latter of which is an isomorphism. This yields two endomorphisms

w_{b}:(x,\varphi)\in V\times V^{\star 1/2}\longmapsto(L_{b}^{-1}(\varphi),0)\in V\times V^{\star 1/2}

and

v_{c}:(x,\varphi)\in V\times V^{\star 1/2}\longmapsto(0,L_{c}(x))\in V\times V^{\star 1/2}.

The Hermitian boxed sum of
b
and
c
is defined as the endomorphism

b\boxplus c:=w_{b}+v_{c}:(x,\varphi)\mapsto\bigl(L_{b}^{-1}(\varphi),L_{c}(x)\bigr),

of
V\times V^{\star 1/2}
, and one checks that
b\boxplus c
is
H_{V}^{1/2}
-Hermitian if and only if both
b
and
c
are Hermitian.

One checks that, given two pairs
(b,c)
and
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
of Hermitian forms on respective vector spaces
V
and
V^{\prime}
, with the left
component non-degenerate, one has

(b,c)\simeq(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})\Rightarrow\bigl(H_{V}^{1/2},b\boxplus c\bigr)\simeq\bigl(H_{V^{\prime}}^{1/2},b^{\prime}\boxplus c^{\prime}\bigr)

and

\bigl(H_{V\oplus V^{\prime}}^{1/2},(b\bot b^{\prime})\boxplus(c\bot c^{\prime})\bigr)\simeq\bigl(H_{V}^{1/2},b\boxplus c\bigr)\bot\bigl(H_{V^{\prime}}^{1/2},b^{\prime}\boxplus c^{\prime}\bigr).

Moreover, just like in Section
2.3
, we obtain that Hermitian boxed sums entirely
account for
1/2
-pairs
(b,u)
with the square-zero splitting property in which
u
is an automorphism:

Proposition 6.9
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair in which
u
is an automorphism. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

(b,u)
has the square-zero splitting property;

(ii)

There exists a vector space
W
and a pair
(B,C)
of non-degenerate Hermitian forms on
W

such that
(b,u)\simeq\bigl(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C\bigr)
.

Now, we compute the invariants of boxed sums. Beware that in the non-degenerate case we need to discuss whether the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}

is
2
or not. We start with the case that most resembles the one of
(1,1)
-pairs:

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
(b,c)
be a pair of Hermitian forms on a vector space
V
, with
b
non-degenerate.
Assume that
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}^{r}
for some
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and some
r\geq 1
, and assume furthermore
that
p:=p_{0}(t^{2})
is irreducible. Assume finally that the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{p,r}
represents, for some polynomial
s(t)\in\mathbb{K}[t]
(not divisible by
p
), the class of
s(t)
modulo
p_{0}
.
Then
(H_{V}^{1/2},b\boxplus c)
has exactly one Hermitian invariant, namely
(H_{V}^{1/2},b\boxplus c)_{p,r}
, which is defined on a

1
-dimensional vector space, and it represents the class of

Lemma 6.17
.

Assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})=2
.
Let

be non-even and distinct from
t
.
Let

.
Let
\alpha
be a nonzero element of
\mathbb{L}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)

.
Then there exists a vector space
V
and a pair
(b,c)
of non-degenerate Hermitian forms on

such that:

•

b\boxplus c
is cyclic with minimal polynomial

;

•

The Hermitian invariant
\bigl(H_{V}^{1/2},b\boxplus c\bigr)_{p,2r}

1
and represents
\alpha

Proof.

We write
p^{2}=p_{0}(t^{2})
for some
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
. We also set
\mathbb{M}:=\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
.

We can start from a non-zero element
\beta\in\mathbb{M}
such that
\beta^{\bullet}=\beta
,
and find a pair
(b,c)
of non-degenerate Hermitian forms on a vector space
V
such that
u:=L_{b}^{-1}L_{c}

is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}
and the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{p_{0},r}
represents the value
\beta
.
Then, we know from Lemma
2.9
that
b\boxplus c

is cyclic with minimal polynomial
(p_{0})^{r}(t^{2})=p^{2r}
, which yields the first point and the first part of the second one.

Again, we see
V
as a
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module for the structure associated with the endomorphism
u
, and

W:=V\times V^{\star 1/2}
as a
\mathbb{F}[t]
-module for the structure associated with the endomorphism
v:=b\boxplus c
.

We can choose an element
x\in V
such that

b\bigl(x,p_{0}^{r-1}\,(q\,x)\bigr)=e_{p_{0}}(\overline{q}^{\mathbb{M}}\beta)
for all
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
.
We set
X:=(x,0)
and we denote by
\overline{X}
its class in
V/\operatorname{Im}p_{0}(v^{2})
(the space on which
the invariant
(H_{V}^{1/2},v)_{p,2r}
is defined).
So let
q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
. We split
pq=q_{1}(t^{2})+tq_{2}(t^{2})
with
q_{1},q_{2}
in
\mathbb{F}[t]
.
Then,

\displaystyle H_{V}^{1/2}\bigl(X,p^{2r-1}\,(q\,X)\bigr)

\displaystyle=H_{V}^{1/2}\bigl((x,0),(p_{0}^{r-1}(t^{2})\,q_{1}(t^{2})+tp_{0}^{r-1}(t^{2})\,q_{2}(t^{2})).(x,0)\bigr)

\displaystyle=L_{c}((p_{0}^{r-1}q_{2})\,x)[x]

\displaystyle=c\bigl(x,(p_{0}^{r-1}q_{2})\,x\bigr)

\displaystyle=b\bigl(x,(p_{0}^{r-1}tq_{2})\,x\bigr)

\displaystyle=e_{p_{0}}\bigl(\overline{tq_{2}}^{\mathbb{M}}\beta\bigr)

Now, we consider the
\mathbb{F}
-linear mapping that takes the polynomial

q\in\mathbb{F}[t]
to
\overline{tq_{2}}^{\mathbb{M}}\in\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
(where
q_{2}
is defined above with respect to
q
).

If
q
is a multiple of
p
, then we check that
tq_{2}
is a multiple of
p_{0}
: indeed we can then write

q=ps
for some
s\in\mathbb{F}[t]
which we split
s=s_{1}(t^{2})+ts_{2}(t^{2})
; then

pq=p^{2}s=s_{1}(t^{2})p_{0}(t^{2})+ts_{2}(t^{2})p_{0}(t^{2})
and hence
q_{2}(t^{2})=s_{2}(t^{2})p_{0}(t^{2})
, which leads to
q_{2}=s_{2}p_{0}
.

Conversely, assume that
tq_{2}
is a multiple of
p_{0}
. Then
q_{2}
is a multiple of
p_{0}
and hence
q_{2}(t^{2})
is a multiple of
p^{2}
,
and finally
q_{1}(t^{2})
is a multiple of
p
. Then
q_{1}
must be a multiple of
p_{0}

(otherwise it is coprime with
p_{0}
, and then applying the Bézout identity and specializing at
t^{2}
leads to
q_{1}(t^{2})
being coprime
with
p_{0}(t^{2})=p^{2}
and hence with
p
); therefore
q_{1}(t^{2})
is actually a multiple of
p^{2}
. We conclude that
pq
is a multiple of
p^{2}
and hence
p
divides
q
.

Hence, the above
\mathbb{F}
-linear mapping yields an injective homomorphism of
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces

\varphi:\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0}),

which turns out to be an isomorphism because
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p)
and
\mathbb{F}[t]/(p_{0})
have the same dimension as vector spaces over
\mathbb{F}
.
Finally, we consider the isomorphism of
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces

\Phi_{p}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{L}&\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{L},\mathbb{F})\\
a&\longmapsto[x\mapsto e_{p}(ax)]\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi_{p_{0}}:\begin{cases}\mathbb{M}&\longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{M},\mathbb{F})\\
b&\longmapsto[x\mapsto e_{p_{0}}(bx)].\end{cases}

The composite map
\Lambda:=\Phi_{p}^{-1}\circ\varphi^{t}\circ\Psi_{p_{0}}
is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces. In the above computation, it precisely maps

\beta
to
(H_{V}^{1/2},b\boxplus c)_{p,2r}(\overline{X},\overline{X})
.
In order to conclude, it suffices to prove that every Hermitian element of
\mathbb{L}

is the image under
\Lambda
of some Hermitian element of
\mathbb{M}
.
Yet, by varying
\beta
in the above construction, we see that
\Lambda
maps any Hermitian element to a Hermitian element. Hence
\Lambda
induces an injective
\mathbb{K}
-linear map from
\{q\in\mathbb{M}:q^{\bullet}=q\}
to

\{s\in\mathbb{L}:s^{\bullet}=s\}
, both of which are
\mathbb{K}
-vector spaces of dimension

;
hence
\Lambda
induces a surjection from the former to the latter. Finally we can take
\beta:=\Lambda^{-1}(\alpha)
, which completes the proof.
∎

First of all, given a pair

is such an isometry.

Besides, just like in the case of
(1,1)
-pairs, we obtain the following result on Hermitian invariants:

Proposition 6.19

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair.
Let

be non-even and different from

, and let

.
Denote by

.

(-b,-u)_{p,r}=(-1)^{1+d(r-1)}\bigl((b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}\bigr)^{\mathrm{op}}
.

The proof is, again, a word for word adaptation of the one of Proposition

.

Now, we are ready to prove Theorems
6.3
and
6.4
.

6.6.2
Fields of characteristic different from
2

Here, we assume that
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
.
Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair in which
u
is an automorphism.
Assume that this pair has the square-zero splitting property.
First of all, by Botha’s theorem we directly recover condition (iii) in Theorem
6.3
.

Next,
(b,u)\simeq(H_{V}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)
for some vector space
V
and some pair
(B,C)
of non-degenerate Hermitian forms on
V
.
Hence, by a remark in the preceding paragraph we obtain that
(b,u)
is isometric to
(-b,-u)
,
and by Proposition
6.19
we derive condition (ii) in Theorem
6.3
.

It remains to check condition (i). Noting that this condition is preserved in taking orthogonal direct sums of
1/2
-pairs,
we can reduce the situation to the one where the pair
(B,C)
is
indecomposable
.
Let us now assume that such is the case, and let us set
u:=L_{B}^{-1}L_{C}
.
We know that only three main situations can occur:

Case 1:

u
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
(pp^{\bullet})^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
and some
r\geq 1
. Then,
B\boxplus C
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
(pp^{\bullet})^{r}(t^{2})=(p(t^{2}))^{r}(p^{\bullet}(t^{2}))^{r}
.
We note that
p(t^{2})\not\in\mathbb{K}[t]
. Since
p
is irreducible, the only possible even
q\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
that divides
p(t^{2})

is
p(t^{2})
(the polynomial
p(t^{2})
might not be irreducible, by the way). Hence, condition (i) is trivially satisfied
(all the Hermitian invariants of
(b,u)
are trivial).

Case 2:

u
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]

such that
p(t^{2})
is irreducible, and some integer
r\geq 1
.

Then, by Lemma
2.9
,
B\boxplus C
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p(t^{2})^{r}
, and the Hermitian invariant

\bigl(H_{V}^{1/2},B\boxplus C\bigr)_{p(t^{2}),r}
is odd-representable. Hence
(b,u)_{p(t^{2}),r}
is the only non-trivial Hermitian invariant
of
(b,u)
, and it is odd-representable.

Case 3:

u
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p_{0}^{r}
for some
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]

such that
p_{0}(t^{2})
is not irreducible.
Then
p_{0}(t^{2})=pp^{\mathrm{op}}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p\neq p^{\mathrm{op}}
, and like in Case 1 we obtain
that condition (i) is trivially satisfied.

Now, we turn to the converse statement. We take a
1/2
-pair
(b,u)

that satisfies conditions (i) to (iii) in Theorem
6.3
, and we prove that
(b,u)
has the square-zero
splitting property.

We make the Klein group
(\mathbb{Z}/2)^{2}
act on
\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
as follows:

(a,b).p:=\varphi^{a}(\psi^{b}(p))
where
\varphi:p\mapsto p^{\bullet}
and
\psi:p\mapsto p^{\mathrm{op}}

(which obviously commute). The orbits of this group action yield a partition
\mathcal{P}

of
\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
. Using the classification of
1/2
-pairs, we are able to split

(b,u)\simeq\underset{\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{P},r\geq 1}{\bot}B_{\mathcal{O},r}

so that, for all
\mathcal{O}\in\mathcal{P}
and all
r\geq 1
:

•

The Jordan numbers of
B_{\mathcal{O},r}
attached to the polynomials
q\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathcal{O}
are all zero, whereas the ones attached to the polynomials
p\in\mathcal{O}
and to the integer
r
are equal to the corresponding ones for
(b,u)
.

•

The Jordan numbers of
B_{\mathcal{O},r}
attached to integers that are distinct from
r
are zero.

•

For all
p\in\mathcal{O}
such that
p=p^{\bullet}
, the Hermitian invariant of
B_{\mathcal{O},r}
attached to
(p,r)

is equivalent to
(b,u)_{p,r}
.

Considering the form of conditions (i) to (iii), it is then clear that each
B_{\mathcal{O},r}

satisfies them; and it suffices to prove that each
B_{\mathcal{O},r}
has the square-zero splitting property.

So, in the rest of the proof we only need to consider the case where, for some orbit
\mathcal{O}

and some
r\geq 1
, the only non-zero Jordan numbers of
(b,u)
are attached to
(p,r)
for polynomials

p
in
\mathcal{O}
(still assuming that conditions (i) to (iii) hold).
We split the discussion into several cases.

Case 1.

\mathcal{O}=\{p\}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
. Then
p\in\mathbb{K}[t]
and
p
is even.
We know from condition (i) that
(b,u)_{p,r}
is odd-representable. Applying Corollary
6.12
to the Hermitian form

(b,u)_{p,r}
, we deduce from the classification of

1/2
-pairs that
(b,u)
is isometric to a Hermitian boxed sum, and hence it has the square-zero splitting property.

Case 2.

\mathcal{O}=\{p,p^{\mathrm{op}}\}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p^{\bullet}=p
and
p\neq p^{\mathrm{op}}
.
Applying Corollary
6.14
to the Hermitian form

(b,u)_{p,r}
, we find a Hermitian boxed sum
(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)

in which
B\boxplus C
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p^{r}(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}
and

(b,u)_{p,r}\simeq(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)_{p,r}
. Then, by condition (ii) applied to both

(b,u)
and
(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)
, we deduce that

(b,u)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}\simeq(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)_{p^{\mathrm{op}},r}
. By the classification of
1/2
-pairs, we deduce
that
(b,u)\simeq(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)
. Hence
(b,u)
is isometric to a Hermitian boxed sum, and
we conclude that it has the square-zero splitting property.

Case 3.

\mathcal{O}=\{p,p^{\bullet}\}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p\neq p^{\bullet}
.

Subcase 3.1:
p=p^{\mathrm{op}}
, i.e.
p
is even.
We choose an endomorphism
v

whose invariant factors are all equal to
p^{r}
, and with
n_{p,r}(u)
such factors.
Then the pair
H_{1/2}(v)
has exactly two non-zero Jordan numbers, both equal to
n_{p,r}(u)
,
attached to the pairs
(p,r)
and
(p^{\bullet},r)
, and hence it is isometric to
(b,u)
.
By Botha’s theorem,
v
is the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms, and hence

H_{1/2}(v)
has the square-zero splitting property. Hence, so does
(b,u)
.

Subcase 3.2:
p^{\mathrm{op}}=p^{\bullet}
.

Then we can write
pp^{\mathrm{op}}=p_{0}(t^{2})
for some
p_{0}\in\mathbb{K}[t]
which is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
.
We can find a vector space
W^{\prime}
equipped with a pair
(B,C)
of non-degenerate Hermitian forms
such that all the invariant factors of
L_{B}^{-1}L_{C}
equal
p_{0}^{r}
, with
n_{p,r}(u)
such factors.
Then, by computing the Jordan numbers of
\bigl(H_{W^{\prime}}^{1/2},B\boxplus C\bigr)
we find that this pair
is isometric to
(b,u)
and we conclude as in the above cases.

Case 4.

\mathcal{O}=\{p,p^{\bullet},p^{\mathrm{op}},(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{\bullet}\}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p,p^{\bullet},p^{\mathrm{op}},(p^{\mathrm{op}})^{\bullet}

are pairwise distinct.
Then, as in Subcase 3.1, we choose a vector space
V^{\prime}
equipped with an endomorphism
v

whose invariant factors are all equal to
(pp^{\mathrm{op}})^{r}
, and with
n_{p,r}(u)
such factors.
Then, by computing the Jordan numbers we gather that

H_{1/2}(v)\simeq(b,u)
. Finally, Botha’s theorem shows that

v
is the sum of two square-zero endomorphisms, and hence
(b,u)
has the
square-zero splitting property.

Thus, the proof of Theorem
6.3
is now complete.

6.6.3

Here, we prove Theorem
6.4
.
The strategy is very similar to the one of the proof of Theorem
6.3
,
so we shall be brief.

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair with the square-zero splitting property, in which

is an automorphism.
Condition (i) in Theorem
6.4
is immediately satisfied thanks to
Botha’s theorem.
By Proposition
6.9
and the reduction of Hermitian pairs, we only need to prove condition (ii) when

(b,u)
is isometric to
(H_{V}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)
for some indecomposable pair
(B,C)
of non-degenerate Hermitian
forms on a vector space

over
\mathbb{F}
.

Then, there are three cases to consider, depending on

.

Case 1:

is cyclic with minimal polynomial
(pp^{\bullet})^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]

and some

.

Then

is cyclic with minimal polynomial

.
Either the polynomial

is irreducible or it is the square of an irreducible polynomial, but in any

, and ditto for

.
Hence, all the Hermitian invariants of

vanish, and condition (ii) is trivially satisfied.

Case 2:

is cyclic with minimal polynomial

for some

and some

,
and
p(t^{2})
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
.

Then
B\boxplus C
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p(t^{2})^{r}
,
and Corollary
6.11
shows that the sole non-zero Hermitian invariant of
(H_{V}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)
,
that is
(H_{V}^{1/2},B\boxplus C)_{p(t^{2}),r}
, is odd-representable.

Case 3:
v
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
p^{r}
for some
p\in\mathbb{K}[t]\cap\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
and some
r\geq 1
,
and
p(t^{2})
is not irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
.

Then
p(t^{2})=q^{2}
for some
q\in\mathbb{K}[t]\cap\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})
.
Then
B\boxplus C
is cyclic with minimal polynomial
q^{2r}
, and
q
is not even. Hence condition (ii) is trivially
satisfied by
\bigl(H_{V}^{1/2},B\boxplus C\bigr)
.

Conversely, let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair, where
u
is an automorphism, that satisfies conditions (i) and (ii)
in Theorem
6.4
. We seek to prove that
(b,u)

is isometric to an orthogonal sum of pairs that have the square-zero splitting property.
To do so, we can reduce the situation to the following cases:

Case 1: The invariant factors of
u
all equal
(p(t^{2})p^{\bullet}(t^{2}))^{r}
for some
p\in\mathbb{F}[t]\smallsetminus\mathbb{K}[t]
such that
p(t^{2})
is irreducible over
\mathbb{F}
, and some
r\geq 1
.

Then we choose a vector space automorphism
v
whose invariant factors all equal
p(t^{2})^{r}

and with as many invariant factors as
u
. Then, one sees that

(b,u)
and
H_{1/2}(v)
have exactly the same Jordan numbers and that all their Hermitian invariants are zero. It follows that they are isometric.

Case 2: The invariant factors of
u
all equal
(pp^{\bullet})^{2r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\smallsetminus\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})
such that
p\neq p^{\bullet}
, and some
r\geq 1
.

Then we choose a vector space automorphism
v
whose invariant factors all equal
p^{2r}
and with as many invariant factors as
u
. Then, one sees that
(b,u)
and
H_{1/2}(v)
have exactly the same Jordan numbers and that all their Hermitian invariants are zero. It follows that they are isometric.

Case 3: The invariant factors of
u
all equal
p^{r}
for some
p\in\operatorname{Irr}_{0}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
and some
r\geq 1
.

Then we write
p=p_{0}(t^{2})
for some
p_{0}\in\operatorname{Irr}(\mathbb{F})\cap\mathbb{K}[t]
.
In that case, condition (ii) yields that
(b,u)_{p,r}
is odd-representable.
By Corollary
6.12
, there exists a space
W
equipped with a pair
(B,C)
of bilinear forms such that
B\boxplus C
has all its invariant factors equal to
p^{r}
, and
the Hermitian invariant
\bigl(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C\bigr)_{p,r}
is equivalent to
(b,u)_{p,r}
.
It follows from the classification of
1/2
-pairs that

\bigl(H_{W}^{1/2},B\boxplus C\bigr)
is isometric to
(b,u)
.

In any case,
(b,u)
is isometric to a pair with the square-zero splitting property, and hence it has this property.

This completes the proof of Theorem
6.7
.

6.7
The nilpotent case

In this final section, we prove Theorem
6.5
.
Thankfully, the proof is very close to the one given in Section
5
, and we will
only focus on the main differences.

We start with the ways of constructing pairs
(b,u)
with the square-zero splitting property and
in which
u
is nilpotent. We have already constructed, for
r\geq 1
even, such pairs in which all the Jordan cells of
u
are of size
r
, and the Hermitian invariant
(b,u)_{t,r}
is equivalent to a given non-degenerate Hermitian form.

Just like in Section
5
, the next step is to tackle twisted pairs. Here, the proof is slightly different from the one given in the quadratic case because of the characteristic
2
case
(if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
the proof of Lemma
5.2
applies effortlessly to our case by taking
\eta:=1
).

Lemma 6.20
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Let
V
be a
4k
-dimensional vector space
together with a hyperbolic Hermitian form
h:V^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then there exists an
h
-selfadjoint endomorphism
u
of
V
that is nilpotent with exactly two Jordan cells, one of size
2k-1
and one of size
2k+1
.

Proof.

We can choose
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
such that
\alpha^{\bullet}\neq-\alpha

(if
\chi(\mathbb{F})\neq 2
it suffices to take
\alpha=1
, otherwise the existence follows from the fact that
x\mapsto x^{\bullet}
is not the identity of
\mathbb{F}
).

We choose an
h
-hyperbolic basis
\mathbf{B}=(e_{1},\dots,e_{2k},f_{1},\dots,f_{2k})
of
V
.
The matrix of
h
in that basis is

S=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{2k}\\
I_{2k}&0\end{bmatrix}
.
Now, we define
a_{1}\in\operatorname{End}(V)
by
a_{1}(e_{2i})=e_{2i-1}
and

a_{1}(f_{2i-1})=f_{2i}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,k\mathclose{]\!]}
, and
a_{1}
maps all the other vectors of
\mathbf{B}
to
0
.
We define
a_{2}\in\operatorname{End}(V)
by
a_{2}(e_{2i+1})=e_{2i}
and

a_{2}(f_{2i})=f_{2i+1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
,

a_{2}(f_{1})=\alpha e_{2k}
and
a_{2}(f_{2k})=\alpha^{\bullet}e_{1}
, and
a_{2}
maps all the other vectors of
\mathbf{B}
to
0
.
It is easily checked on the vectors of
\mathbf{B}
that
a_{1}^{2}=0
and
a_{2}^{2}=0
.

The respective matrices
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
of
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
in
\mathbf{B}
are of the form

M_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&A_{1}^{\star}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad M_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{2}&C\\
0&A_{2}^{\star}\end{bmatrix}

where
C^{\star}=C
, and
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
have their entries in
\mathbb{K}
.
One checks that both
SM_{1}
and
SM_{2}
are Hermitian, to the effect that both
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are
h
-selfadjoint.

To conclude, it suffices to check that
u:=a_{1}+a_{2}
is nilpotent and that it has exactly two Jordan cells, one of size
2k+1

and one of size
2k-1
.
To see this, note first that
u(e_{i})=e_{i-1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}2,2k\mathclose{]\!]}
, whereas
u(e_{1})=0
,

u(f_{1})=f_{2}+\alpha e_{2k}
,
u(f_{i})=f_{i+1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}2,2k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
, and

u(f_{2k})=\alpha^{\bullet}e_{1}
.

From
u(f_{1})=f_{2}+\alpha e_{2k}
, we gather that

u^{i}(f_{1})=f_{i+1}+\alpha e_{2k-i+1}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,2k-1\mathclose{]\!]}
,
in particular
u^{2k-1}(f_{1})=f_{2k}+\alpha e_{2}
. Hence,
u^{2k}(f_{1})=(\alpha+\alpha^{\bullet})e_{1}\neq 0
, and finally
u^{2k+1}(f_{1})=0
.
Hence, the subspace
W_{1}:=\operatorname{span}(e_{1},f_{1})\oplus\operatorname{span}(f_{i+1}+\alpha\,e_{2k-i+1})_{1\leq i\leq 2k-1}

is stable under
u
and the resulting endomorphism is a Jordan cell of size
2k+1
.

Besides, one checks that
u^{i}(\alpha^{\bullet}e_{2k}-f_{2})=\alpha^{\bullet}e_{2k-i}-f_{i+2}
for all
i\in\mathopen{[\![}1,2k-2\mathclose{]\!]}
,
and in particular
u^{2k-2}(\alpha^{\bullet}e_{2k}-f_{2})=\alpha^{\bullet}e_{2}-f_{2k}
and then
u^{2k-1}(\alpha^{\bullet}e_{2k}-f_{2})=0
.
Obviously,
W_{2}:=\operatorname{span}(\alpha^{\bullet}e_{2k-i+1}-f_{i+1})_{1\leq i\leq 2k-1}
is a
(2k-1)
-dimensional subspace, it is stable
under
u
and the resulting endomorphism is a Jordan cell of size
2k-1
.
Finally, using the fact that
\alpha^{\bullet}\neq-\alpha
, one checks that
V=W_{1}\oplus W_{2}
, and the claimed result follows.
∎

The same arguments as in Section
5
(with
\eta=1
) then show that,
in the previous lemma, the Hermitian invariants
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}
and
(b,u)_{t,2k-1}

satisfy
(b,u)_{t,2k+1}\simeq-(b,u)_{t,2k-1}
.

From there, the proof that, in Theorem
6.5
, condition (ii) implies condition (i)
is a word for word adaptation of the one given in Section
5
for Theorem
1.14
, where one takes
\eta:=1
.

It remains to prove the converse implication. Once again, the techniques with induced forms are adapted effortlessly
to the Hermitian case by taking
\eta:=1
, and one is entirely reduced to the following lemma:

Lemma 6.21
.

Let
(b,u)
be a
1/2
-pair in which
u^{3}=0
.
Assume that
u
is the sum of two square-zero
b
-selfadjoint endomorphisms.
Set
B_{1}:=(b,u)_{t,1}
and
B_{3}:=(b,u)_{t,3}
.
Then
B_{1}
Witt-simplifies
B_{3}
.

Proof.

Denote by
V
the underlying vector space of
(b,u)
.

The proof is globally similar to the one of Lemma
5.11
, with only subtle differences.
Step 1 is adapted effortlessly, and we are only left with the case where
B_{3}
is non-hyperbolic and the pair

(b,u)
is indecomposable as a
1/2
-pair with the square-zero splitting property.

In the rest of the proof, one takes a
b
-selfadjoint square-zero endomorphism
a
such that
u-a
has square zero.

Step 2 is slightly different: by taking advantage of the classification of
1/2
-pairs, one shows that if such a
1/2
-pair
(b^{\prime},u^{\prime})
, with underlying vector space
V^{\prime}
, satisfies
(u^{\prime})^{2}=0
and
u^{\prime}\neq 0
, then there exists a pair
(x,y)
of vectors of
V^{\prime}
such that
b(x,x)=b(y,y)=0
,
b(x,y)=b(y,x)=1
, and
u^{\prime}(x)=\alpha y
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{K}\smallsetminus\{0\}

(and consequently
u^{\prime}(y)=0
).

Now, for Step 3 one easily adapts the case where
\eta=1
. There, one proves that the assumption that
a
does not map
V
into
\operatorname{Ker}u^{2}

would lead to a contradiction with the assumption that
B_{3}
is non-hyperbolic, thanks to the assumption that
(b,u)
is indecomposable as a
1/2
-pair with the square-zero splitting property. We conclude at this point that
a
maps
V
into
\operatorname{Ker}u^{2}
.

In Step 4, we consider the endomorphism
\overline{a}
of
(\operatorname{Ker}u^{2})/(\operatorname{Im}u+\operatorname{Ker}u)
,
and we obtain that it is skew-selfadjoint for the Hermitian form
B_{2}:=(b,u)_{t,2}
.
Let us choose a nonzero
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}
such that
\alpha^{\bullet}=-\alpha
. Then
\alpha\overline{a}
is
B_{2}
-selfadjoint, and its square equals zero.
Assume now that
\overline{a}\neq 0
. Using the classification of
1/2
-pairs, we would obtain a pair

(x,y)
of vectors of
(\operatorname{Ker}u^{2})/(\operatorname{Im}u+\operatorname{Ker}u)
such that
B_{2}(x,x)=B_{2}(y,y)=0
,
B_{2}(x,y)=B_{2}(y,x)=1
, together with a non-zero scalar
\beta\in\mathbb{K}
such that
\alpha\overline{a}(x)=\beta y
, leading to
\overline{a}(x)=\lambda y
for some nonzero
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. From there, the rest of Step 4 is adapted to the present situation by lifting
x
to a vector
x^{\prime}
of
\operatorname{Ker}u^{2}
, and by showing that
U:=\operatorname{span}(x^{\prime},a(x^{\prime}),u(x^{\prime}),u(a(x^{\prime})))
is
b
-regular and stable under
a
and
u
.

It follows that
a
maps
\operatorname{Ker}u^{2}
into
\operatorname{Ker}u+\operatorname{Im}u
.

The remaining two steps can then be adapted word for word (with
\eta=1
), the only difference being that

(x,y)\in V_{3}\times a(V_{1})\mapsto b(x,u(y))
is sesquilinear, not bilinear, but this has no impact on the validity of the arguments.
This completes the adaptation of the proof of Lemma
5.11
.
∎
