Zeroth-Order Algorithms for Nonconvex-Strongly-Concave Minimax Problems with Improved Complexities
Zhongruo Wang, Krishnakumar Balasubramanian, Shiqian Ma, Meisam Razaviyayn

Abstract
In this paper, we study zeroth-order algorithms for minimax optimization problems that are nonconvex in one variable and strongly-concave in the other variable. Such minimax optimization problems have attracted significant attention lately due to their applications in modern machine learning tasks. We first consider a deterministic version of the problem. We design and analyze the Zeroth-Order Gradient Descent Ascent ( ZO-GDA ) algorithm, and provide improved results compared to existing works, in terms of oracle complexity. We also propose the Zeroth-Order Gradient Descent Multi-Step Ascent ( ZO-GDMSA ) algorithm that significantly improves the oracle complexity of ZO-GDA . We then consider stochastic versions of ZO-GDA and ZO-GDMSA , to handle stochastic nonconvex minimax problems. For this case, we provide oracle complexity results under two assumptions on the stochastic gradient: (i) the uniformly bounded variance assumption, which is common in traditional stochastic optimization, and (ii) the Strong Growth Condition (SGC), which has been known to be satisfied by modern over-parametrized machine learning models. We establish that under the SGC assumption, the complexities of the stochastic algorithms match that of deterministic algorithms. Numerical experiments are presented to support our theoretical results.

Assumption

Assumption 1

The objective function
f(x,y)
and the constraint set
\mathcal{Y}
have the following properties:

•

f(x,y)
is continuously differentiable in

and

, and

\|\nabla f(x_{1},y_{1})-\nabla f(x_{2},y_{2})\|_{2}\leq\ell\|(x_{1},y_{1})-(x_{2},y_{2})\|_{2},

(3)

•

The function

, this is indeed true with

•

The constraint set

The following assumption, which is standard in the literature

,

,

Assumption 2
(Uniformly Bounded Variance)

For any

and

, the stochastic zeroth-order oracle outputs an estimator

\textsf{\text{E}}_{\xi}[\nabla_{y}F\left(x,y,\xi\right)]=\nabla_{y}f\left(x,y\right)

\textsf{\text{E}}_{\xi}(\|\nabla_{x}F(x,y,\xi)-\nabla_{x}f(x,y)\|_{2}^{2})\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}

\textsf{\text{E}}_{\xi}(\|\nabla_{y}F(x,y,\xi)-\nabla_{y}f(x,y)\|_{2}^{2})\leq\sigma_{2}^{2}

2.1
Zeroth-order gradient estimator

We now discuss the idea of zeroth-order gradient estimator based on Gaussian smoothing technique
[
40
]
. For the deterministic case, we denote
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\sim N(0,\textbf{1}_{d_{1}})
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\sim N(0,\textbf{1}_{d_{2}})
, where
\textbf{1}_{d_{1}}
and
\textbf{1}_{d_{2}}
denote identity matrices with sizes
d_{1}\times d_{1}
and
d_{2}\times d_{2}
, respectively. The notion of the Gaussian smoothed functions is defined as follows:

\displaystyle f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y):

\displaystyle=\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}}f(x+\mu_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},y),

(4)

\displaystyle f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y):

\displaystyle=\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}f(x,y+\mu_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}),

and the zeroth-order gradient estimators
[
40
]
are defined as

\displaystyle G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x+\mu_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},y)-f(x,y)}{\mu_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},

(5)

\displaystyle H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{f(x,y+\mu_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2})-f(x,y)}{\mu_{2}}\boldsymbol{u}_{2},

where
\mu_{1}>0
and
\mu_{2}>0
are smoothing parameters.

As noted in
[
6
]
, the Gaussian smoothing technique proposed by
[
40
]
is based on the Stein’s identity
[
54
]
, for characterizing Gaussian random vectors. Specifically, Stein’s identity states that a random vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is standard Gaussian
if and only if
,
\textsf{\text{E}}\left[u~h(u)\right]=\textsf{\text{E}}\left[\nabla h(u)\right]
, for all absolutely continuous functions
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Note that Stein’s identity, naturally relates function queries to gradients and thus is naturally suited for zeroth-order optimization. If we let
h(u)
to be the Gaussian smoothed functions (as in (
4
)), it is easy to see that the zeroth-order gradients (as in (
5
)) follow by simply evaluating the Gaussian Stein’s identity.

It should be noted following the arguments in
[
40
,
7
]
that

\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1})=\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y)
, and
\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}G_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2})=\nabla_{y}f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y)
. Hence, the zeroth-order gradient estimators in (
5
) provide unbiased estimates of the gradient of Gaussian smoothed functions
f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1}):=f(x+\mu_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},y)
and
f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2}):=f(x,y+\mu_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2})
. Similarly, for the stochastic case, the Gaussian smoothed functions are defined as:

\displaystyle f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y):=\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\xi}F(x+\mu_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},y,\xi),

(6)

\displaystyle f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y):=\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\xi}F(x,y+\mu_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\xi),

and the zeroth-order stochastic gradient estimators are defined as:

\displaystyle G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\xi)=\frac{F(x+\mu_{1}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},y,\xi)-F(x,y,\xi)}{\mu_{1}}\boldsymbol{u}_{1},

(7)

\displaystyle H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\xi)=\frac{F(x,y+\mu_{2}\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\xi)-F(x,y,\xi)}{\mu_{2}}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}.

One can also show that the zeroth-order gradient estimators provide unbiased estimates to the gradients of the Gaussian smoothed functions, i.e.,
\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\xi}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\xi)=\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y),
and
\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\xi}H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\xi)=\nabla_{y}f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y).

In our algorithms, we also need to use mini-batch zeroth-order gradient estimators, which can reduce the variance of stochastic gradient estimators. To this end, we define the following notation. For integer
q>0
, we denote
[q]:=\{1,\ldots,q\}
. In the deterministic case, for integers
q_{1}>0
,
q_{2}>0
we denote

\displaystyle G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1,[q_{1}]})=\frac{1}{q_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{1}}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1,i}),

(8)

\displaystyle H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2,[q_{2}]})=\frac{1}{q_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{q_{2}}H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2,i}).

For indices sets
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, in the stochastic case we denote

\begin{array}[]{ll}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{1}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{1}}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1,i},\xi_{i}),\\
H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{2}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{2}})&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_{2}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2,i},\xi_{i}).\end{array}

(9)

It is easy to see that we have the following unbiasedness properties:

\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1,[q_{1}]}}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{1,[q_{1}]})=\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y)\mbox{ and }\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2,[q_{2}]}}H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{2,[q_{2}]})=\nabla_{y}f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y)

and

\displaystyle\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{1}}\textsf{\text{E}}_{\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}}G_{\mu_{1}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})=\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x,y)

\displaystyle\textsf{\text{E}}_{\boldsymbol{u}_{2}}\textsf{\text{E}}_{\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}}H_{\mu_{2}}(x,y,\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{2}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{2}})=\nabla_{y}f_{\mu_{2}}(x,y).

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we designed and analyzed zeroth-order algorithms for deterministic and stochastic nonconvex minimax problems. Specifically, we considered two types of algorithms: zeroth-order gradient descent ascent algorithm and a modified version of it with multiple ascent steps following each descent step. We obtained oracle complexities for both algorithms that match the performance of comparable first-order algorithms, up to unavoidable dimensionality factors. Our orcale complexities are better than that of existing methods under the same assumptions. Future works include to explore lower bounds for zeroth-order nonconvex minimax optimization problems, and to explore structural constraints to obtain improved dimensionality dependence in our results.

&+\|\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y^{*}(x_{s}))-\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|^{2}/L_{g}+\frac{L_{g}\eta_{1}^{2}}{4}\|G_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})\|^{2}\\
&-\eta_{1}\langle\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1}),G_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})\rangle+\frac{1}{2}L_{g}\eta_{1}^{2}\|G_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&g(x_{s})+\frac{\ell^{2}}{L_{g}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}-\eta_{1}\langle\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1}),G_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})\rangle\\
&+\eta_{1}^{2}L_{g}\|G_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{4L_{g}}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3},\end{array}

where the first inequality is due to Lemma
A.3
, the second inequality is due to Young’s inequality, and the last inequality is due to Lemmas
A.6
and
A.12
. Now take expectation with respect to
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}
to the above inequality, we get:

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\eta_{1}\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s})-\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s+1})+\frac{\ell^{2}}{L_{g}}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta_{1}^{2}L_{g}\textsf{\text{E}}\|G_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1},\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}},\xi_{\mathcal{M}_{1}})\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{4L_{g}}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}\\
\leq&\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s})-\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s+1})+\frac{\ell^{2}}{L_{g}}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta_{1}^{2}L_{g}\Bigl(3\|\nabla_{x}f(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}+\varrho_{1}(\epsilon,\mu_{1})\Bigr)+\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{4L_{g}}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3},\end{array}

(69)

where the second inequality is due to Lemma
A.11
. From Lemma
A.6
we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}f(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}f_{\mu_{1}}(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}/2.\end{array}

(70)

Combining (
69
) and (
70
), and noting
\eta_{1}=1/(12L_{g})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}f(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}\leq&48L_{g}\Bigl[\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s})-\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s+1})\Bigr]+48\ell^{2}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+13\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}+\varrho_{1}(\epsilon,\mu_{1})/12.\end{array}

(71)

It then follows that

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla g(x_{s})\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&2\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}g(x_{s})-\nabla_{x}f(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}+2\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}f(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&2\ell^{2}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}+2\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla_{x}f(x_{s},y_{s+1})\|_{2}^{2}\\
\leq&96L_{g}\Bigl[\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s})-\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s+1})\Bigr]+98\ell^{2}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+26\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}+\varrho_{1}(\epsilon,\mu_{1})/6,\end{array}

(72)

where the second inequality is due to Assumption
1
, and the last inequality is due to (
71
).

Take the sum over
s=0,\ldots,S
to both sides of (
72
), we get

\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{S+1}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla g(x_{s})\|_{2}^{2}\leq&\frac{96L_{g}}{S+1}\textsf{\text{E}}[g(x_{0})-g(x_{S+1})]+\frac{98\ell^{2}}{S+1}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+26\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}+\varrho_{1}(\epsilon,\mu_{1})/6.\end{array}

(73)

Denote
\Delta_{g}=g(x_{0})-\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}(g(x))
. From Lemma
E.1
, we know that when
T=\mathcal{O}(\kappa\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
, we have
\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}
(note that
y_{s+1}=y_{T}(x_{s})
). Therefore, choosing
parameters as in (
14
) guarantees that the right hand side of (
73
) is upper bounded by
O(\epsilon^{2})+O(\epsilon^{4})
. Hence, with
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we get the required result and thus an
\epsilon
-stationary point is found.
This completes the proof of Part 1.

We next prove Part 2. From Lemma
D.3
we have

\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s-1})-y_{s}\|^{2}\leq\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s-1})-y_{s-1}\|^{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{12\ell}\Bigr)+\bar{\varrho}(\mu_{2},\rho_{2}).

Choosing
\delta=\frac{12\ell}{\tau}\bar{\varrho}(\mu_{2},\rho_{2})
, we have:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{t}(x_{s})\|^{2}-\delta&\leq(\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{t-1}(x_{s})\|^{2}-\delta)\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{12\ell}\Bigr)\\
&\leq(\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{0}(x_{s})\|^{2}-\delta)\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{12\ell}\Bigr)^{t}\\
&\leq\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{0}(x_{s})\|^{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{12\ell}\Bigr)^{t}\leq D^{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\tau}{12\ell}\Bigr)^{t}.\end{array}

In order to ensure that

\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{T}(x_{s})\|^{2}\leq\epsilon^{2}
, we need
T=\mathcal{O}(\kappa\log(\epsilon^{-1}))
and
\mu_{2}=\mathcal{O}(\min(1,\rho_{2})\kappa^{-1/2}d_{2}^{-3/2}\epsilon)
. From (
72
) and (
20
) we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla g(x_{s})\|_{2}^{2}&\leq 96L_{g}\Bigl[\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s})-\textsf{\text{E}}g(x_{s+1})\Bigr]+98\ell^{2}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+26\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}+\varrho_{1}(\mu_{1},\rho_{1})/6.\end{array}

(74)

Taking the sum over
s=0,...,S
to both sides of (
74
), we get:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{1}{S+1}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\textsf{\text{E}}\|\nabla g(x_{s})\|_{2}^{2}\leq&\frac{96L_{g}}{S+1}\textsf{\text{E}}[g(x_{0})-g(x_{S+1})]+\frac{98\ell^{2}}{S+1}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\textsf{\text{E}}\|y^{*}(x_{s})-y_{s+1}\|_{2}^{2}\\
&+26\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}+\varrho_{1}(\mu_{1},\rho_{1})/6.\end{array}

(75)

Recall that
\varrho_{1}(\mu_{1},\rho_{1})=\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}}{\rho_{1}}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+6)+\mu_{1}^{2}\ell^{2}(d_{1}+3)^{3}/2
, choosing
\mu_{1}=\mathcal{O}\Bigl(\min(1,\rho_{1})\ell(d_{1})^{-3/2}\Bigr)
, we guarantee that the right hand side of (
75
) is upper bounded by
O(\epsilon^{2})+O(\epsilon^{4})
. Hence, with
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we get the required result and thus an
\epsilon
-stationary point is found. This completes the proof of Part 2.
