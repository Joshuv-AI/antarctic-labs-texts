Spectrally Constrained Optimization
Casey Garner Gilad Lerman Shuzhong Zhang

Abstract
We investigate how to solve smooth matrix optimization problems with general linear inequality constraints on the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix. We present solution methods to obtain exact global minima for linear objective functions, i.e., F ⁡ ( 𝑿 ) = ⟨ 𝑪 , 𝑿 ⟩ F(\bm{X})=\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}\rangle , and perform exact projections onto the eigenvalue constraint set. Two first-order algorithms are developed to obtain first-order stationary points for general non-convex objective functions. Both methods are proven to converge sublinearly when the constraint set is convex. Numerical experiments demonstrate the applicability of both the model and the methods. Keywords: eigenvalue optimization, constrained optimization, Frank-Wolfe algorithm, non-smooth analysis MSC codes: 90C26, 90C52, 65K10, 68W40

Introduction

Constrained matrix optimization is an essential aspect of machine learning

There are two types of constraints to consider in matrix optimization: coordinate constraints and spectral constraints.
Coordinate constraints impose prohibitions on the entries of the matrix, such as the diagonal elements must equal one or the row and column sums must be unity. Spectral constraints force the eigenvalues or singular values to satisfy certain conditions. For example, assuming a matrix is positive semidefinite enforces non-negativity on the eigenvalues. The literature is replete with a diverse array of coordinate constraints, but the variety of spectral constraints is limited. Given the power of constrained matrix optimization, the lack of general spectral constraints, with corresponding theory and algorithmic development, is a knowledge gap worth filling.

The goal of this paper is to explore spectrally constrained matrix optimization (SCO) and develop theory and algorithms to solve new models with general spectral constraints. We begin with the following spectrally constrained eigenvalue model

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;F(\bm{X})

(SCO-Eig)

\displaystyle\;\bm{A}\lambda(\bm{X})\leq\bm{b}

1.2
Contributions: A New Perspective

A common thread present in the eigenvalue optimization literature is minimizing objective functions solely dependent on the spectrum of the decision matrix. As a result, these papers fixate on exploring the non-smoothness of these objectives. This has led to beautiful mathematics and robust application; however, the success of these models has simultaneously limited the scope of the investigation. Constrained matrix optimization revolves around coordinate and spectral constraints, and the current eigenvalue optimization literature has not expanded our understanding of general spectral constraints.

Our work on (
SCO-Eig
) begins a novel study to understand eigenvalues being present functionally in the constraint set. Ours is a perspective shift, instead of focusing on eigenvalues in the objective, we focus on eigenvalues in the constraint, and, to our surprise, this perspective seems uncommon. Almost no papers consider general functional constraints on the eigenvalues, e.g.,
g(\lambda(\bm{X}))\leq 0
, and those that do restrict themselves to spectral functions. In the very recent work
[
33
]
, which appeared while we were finishing this paper, the authors allow spectral functions in the constraint set they consider; however, they do not go beyond this paradigm and they further require convexity of the spectral functions. We do not assume the constraint set in (
SCO-Eig
) is composed of spectral functions, and, unlike
[
33
]
, we develop approaches to solve our model without convexification. Another work which considers general functional eigenvalue constraints is
[
22
]
. In this paper, the authors investigate error bound conditions for constraint sets of the form
\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;f(\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}))\leq 0\right\}
where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, but they did not utilize their results to investigate matrix optimization.

The main contribution of this work is the first study of a matrix optimization model with general linear inequality constraints on the eigenvalues. We prove in the case of a linear objective function that a global minimum of (
SCO-Eig
) can be computed regardless of the non-convexity of the constraint set, and we prove how to perform optimal projections onto the constraint set. With these results, we develop two first-order algorithms to compute stationary points to (
SCO-Eig
) and provide a proof of their sublinear convergence rate. Numerical experiments demonstrate the applicability of our procedures.

The Eigenvalue Constraint Set

The paradigm shift we are pursuing rests on the eigenvalues being functionally present in the constraints. Due to the limited attention received by such settings, we begin by proving some general facts about the constraint set in (
SCO-Eig
), which we denote as

\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}):=\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})\leq\bm{b}\right\}.

(2)

We shall desire to take advantage of writing the constraint without ordering the eigenvalues. To this end, let
\bm{\lambda}_{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the unordered vector of eigenvalues for
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}

\bm{D}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times n}
to enforce the descending order of the eigenvalues, i.e., the
i

\bm{D}_{n}
is
\bm{e}_{i+1}-\bm{e}_{i}
where
\bm{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a one in the
i

2

\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})=\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}_{\bm{X}}\leq\bm{b},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}_{\bm{X}}\leq\bm{0}\right\}.\;
Our first fact demonstrates the unsurprising but key truth that
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
has the potential to be convex and non-convex as a function of the problem’s data.

Fact 1
.

\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
can be both convex and non-convex dependent on
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Proof.

If
\bm{A}=-\bm{I}
and
\bm{b}=\bm{0}
, then
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})=\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}_{+}
which is convex. Let
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
enforce the constraint
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{2\times 2}
with
\lambda_{1}(\bm{X})\geq 3
and
\lambda_{2}(\bm{X})\leq 1
. One can easily produce examples where convex combinations of matrices satisfying these constraints fail these conditions. For example, the matrices

\bm{X}_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}35&15\\
15&6\end{pmatrix}\;\;\text{ and }\;\;\bm{X}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}4&17\\
17&63\end{pmatrix}

satisfy the eigenvalue constraints; however,
\bm{Y}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{X}_{1}+\bm{X}_{2}\right)
fails the condition
\lambda_{2}(\bm{Y})\leq 1
.
∎

.

3
General Optimality Conditions

The non-smoothness of the eigenvalues necessitates a discussion of concepts from non-smooth analysis. Consider an open set
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
locally Lipschitz on
\Omega
, i.e., given
\bm{x}\in\Omega
there exists
L_{\bm{x}}
and
\delta_{\bm{x}}>0
such that if
\|\bm{y}-\bm{x}\|\leq\delta_{\bm{x}}
then

|f(\bm{x})-f(\bm{y})|\leq L_{\bm{x}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|
. The (
radial
)
directional derivative
of
f
at
\bm{x}
in the direction of
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is

f^{\prime}(\bm{x};\bm{d}):=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0_{+}}\frac{f(\bm{x}+t\bm{d})-f(\bm{x})}{t},

when the limit exists. A relaxed version of the directional derivative is the
Clarke directional derivative
.
The
Clarke directional derivative
of
f
at
\bm{x}
in the direction of
\bm{d}
is

f^{C}(\bm{x};\bm{d}):=\limsup_{(t,\bm{y})\rightarrow(0_{+},\bm{x})}\frac{f(\bm{y}+t\bm{d})-f(\bm{y})}{t}.

Unlike
\bm{d}\mapsto f^{\prime}(\bm{x};\bm{d})
,
\bm{d}\mapsto f^{C}(\bm{x};\bm{d})
is guaranteed to be a finite convex function for all
\bm{x}\in\Omega
(see Section 5.1 in
[
41
]
). The
Clarke subdifferential
of
f
at
\bm{x}
is

\partial_{C}f(\bm{x}):=\left\{\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;\langle\bm{s},\bm{d}\rangle\leq f^{C}(\bm{x};\bm{d}),\;\forall\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\right\}.

The Clarke subdifferential is a non-empty, compact and convex set, and these properties make it a frequently utilized generalization of differentiation for non-smooth functions. The definitions presented here for Clarke subdifferentials can be found in Section 2 of
[
19
]
. It is often necessary to assume a function is
regular
at a point for certain results to hold. Different definitions of regularity exist, but in this section we say
f
is
regular
at
\bm{x}\in\Omega
if

f^{C}(\bm{x};\bm{d})=\liminf_{(t,\bm{v})\rightarrow(0_{+},\bm{d})}\frac{f(\bm{x}+t\bm{v})-f(\bm{x})}{t}

for all
\bm{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(see Definition 5.46 and Proposition 4.3 in
[
41
]
).
This notion of regularity focuses on the equality of two slightly different generalizations of the directional derivative of
f
. One deals with perturbing the point
\bm{x}
while the other perturbs the direction
\bm{d}
. Convex functions are regular at all points in their domain and if
f
is continuously differentiable at
\bm{x}
then it is also regular at
\bm{x}
.
A thorough discourse on Clarke subdifferentials and regularity is presented in
[
41
]
.
The interested reader should consult this text and the references therein for further details.

Theorem 2
.

Assume

\mathcal{I}(\bm{X}^{*}):=\left\{i\in[p]:g_{i}(\bm{X}^{*})=0\right\}

\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+},\;t_{i}=0\;\forall i\in[p]\setminus\mathcal{I}(\bm{X}^{*}),\;0\in\partial_{C}g_{1}(\bm{X}^{*})+\ldots+\partial_{C}g_{p}(\bm{X}^{*})\implies\bm{t}=\bm{0}

and each

is regular at
\bm{X}^{*}
, then there exist multipliers
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}
such that,
\mu_{i}g_{i}(\bm{X}^{*})=0

0\in\nabla F(\bm{X}^{*})+\mu_{1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{a}_{1i}\text{conv}\left\{\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}\;|\;\bm{v}\in\mathbb{E}_{i}(\bm{X}^{*}),\;\|\bm{v}\|=1\right\}\\
+\ldots+\mu_{p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}{a}_{pi}\text{conv}\left\{\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}\;|\;\bm{v}\in\mathbb{E}_{i}(\bm{X}^{*}),\;\|\bm{v}\|=1\right\}

(3)

where
\mathbb{E}_{i}(\bm{X}^{*})
denotes the eigenspace of
\bm{X}^{*}
corresponding to the
i
-th largest eigenvalue of
\bm{X}^{*}
and
\text{conv}(S)
is the convex hull of a given set

.

Proof.

Using the framework of Corollary 5.54 in
[

, let
f=F
and

be as defined. Since

is globally Lipschitz continuous,

is locally Lipschitz for all
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}

for all
\bm{X}
. The proof is completed by computing the Clarke subdifferential of the

\displaystyle\partial_{C}g_{i}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\partial_{C}\left({a}_{ij}\bm{\lambda}_{j}(\cdot)\right)(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a}_{ij}\partial_{C}\bm{\lambda}_{j}(\bm{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}{a}_{ij}\text{conv}\left\{\bm{v}\bm{v}^{\top}\;|\;\bm{v}\in\mathbb{E}_{j}(\bm{X}),\;\|\bm{v}\|=1\right\}

(4)

where the first and second equalities follow by Theorem 5.51 and Proposition 5.9 in
[

respectively. The last equality is due to Theorem 5.3 in
[

]
.

4
Solving SCO-Eig with Linear Objective Functions

The simplest, non-trivial objective function to consider for (
SCO-Eig
) is a linear objective function,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}\rangle

(10)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})\leq\bm{b}

\displaystyle\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}}.

As we saw in Section
2
, the constraint set
\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
can be highly non-convex. Therefore, one may not expect to guarantee a global minimizer; however, Theorem
5
below shows a global minimizer is readily computable regardless of the constraint set. Moreover,
the solution to (
10

Theorem 5
.

Let
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with
\frac{1}{2}(\bm{C}+\bm{C}^{\top})=\bm{P}\bm{\Omega}\bm{P}^{\top}
for
\bm{P}\in\mathcal{O}(n)
and
\bm{\Omega}=\operatorname{Diag}([\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}])
with
\omega_{1}\geq\omega_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\omega_{n}

10

\bm{X}^{*}=\bm{P}\operatorname{Diag}([\lambda^{*}_{n},\lambda^{*}_{n-1},\ldots,\lambda^{*}_{1}])\bm{P}^{\top}

(11)

where

\bm{\lambda}^{*}\in\argmin\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\lambda_{n+1-i}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0}\right\}.

(12)

Proof.

We may assume without loss of generality
\bm{C}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}
. Observe,

\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}\rangle=\bigg\langle\frac{1}{2}(\bm{C}+\bm{C}^{\top}),\bm{X}\bigg\rangle

for all
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, so (
10

\bm{C}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}
and
\bm{C}=\bm{P}\bm{\Omega}\bm{P}^{\top}
for orthogonal
\bm{P}
and
\bm{\Omega}=\operatorname{Diag}([\omega_{1},\omega_{2},\ldots,\omega_{n}])
with
\omega_{1}\geq\omega_{2}\geq\ldots\geq\omega_{n}
. Utilizing the spectral decomposition of
\bm{X}
,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}\rangle\;|\;{\bm{A}}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.06749pt=\min\left\{\langle\bm{P}\bm{\Omega}\bm{P}^{\top},\bm{Q}\;\text{Diag}(\bm{\lambda})\bm{Q}^{\top}\rangle\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0},\;\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(n)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.06749pt=\min\left\{\langle\bm{\Omega},\bm{P}^{\top}\bm{Q}\;\text{Diag}(\bm{\lambda})\bm{Q}^{\top}\bm{P}\rangle\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0},\;\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{O}(n)\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 18.06749pt=\min\left\{\langle\bm{\Omega},\bar{\bm{Q}}^{\top}\;\text{Diag}(\bm{\lambda})\bar{\bm{Q}}\rangle\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0},\;\bar{\bm{Q}}\in\mathcal{O}(n)\right\}

By Theorem 2.1 in
[
34
]
, it follows for any
\bm{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying
\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0}

\min\left\{\text{Tr}\left(\bm{\Omega}\bar{\bm{Q}}^{\top}\;\text{Diag}(\bm{\lambda})\bar{\bm{Q}}\right)\;|\;\bar{\bm{Q}}\in\mathcal{O}(n)\right\}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\lambda_{n+1-i}.

\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}\rangle\;|\;{\bm{A}}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\right\}=\min\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\lambda_{n+1-i}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0}\right\}.

Let

\bm{\lambda}^{*}\in\argmin\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\lambda_{n+1-i}\;|\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}\leq{\bm{b}},\;\bm{D}_{n}\bm{\lambda}\leq\bm{0}\right\}.

In-order to compute
\bm{X}^{*}
such that the constraints are satisfied and

\text{Tr}(\bm{C}^{\top}\bm{X}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\lambda_{n+1-i}^{*},

we must determine

such that,

\text{Tr}\left(\bm{\Omega}(\bar{\bm{Q}}^{*})^{\top}\text{Diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})\bar{\bm{Q}}^{*}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\lambda_{n+1-i}^{*}.

With

4

\bm{X}^{*}=\bm{P}(\bar{\bm{Q}}^{*})^{\top}\text{Diag}(\bm{\lambda}^{*})\bar{\bm{Q}}^{*}\bm{P}^{\top}.

Since

solves (

∎

Remark 4.1
.

Though Theorem

\bm{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

6
Spectrally Constrained Solvers

We develop two first-order algorithms to compute stationary points for (
SCO-Eig
). Our capacity to solve the linear objective problem motivates the construction of a Frank-Wolfe algorithm and accessible projections make possible the development of a projected gradient method.

6.1
Inexact Projected Gradient Method

For
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
let
\text{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}}(\cdot)
be the operator which maps
\bm{Y}
to an element of the argmin set of (
15
), i.e.,

\text{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}}(\bm{Y})\in\argmin\left\{\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{X}-\bm{Y}\|_{F}^{2}\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}(\bm{A},\bm{b})\right\}.

For
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
convex, the projection is unique. If the set is non-convex multiple optimal projections are likely present.
Since inexact computations are the reality in implementation, we introduce a notion of inexact projections.

Definition 1
.

For convex
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
and parameter
\delta\geq 0
, the set of inexact projections of
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
onto
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
is

\text{P}_{\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}}^{\delta}(\bm{Y}):=\left\{\bm{Z}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})\;|\;\langle\bm{Z}-\bm{Y},\bm{X}-\bm{Z}\rangle\geq-\delta,\;\forall\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})\right\}.

(18)

The standard results for projections onto convex sets tell us
\bm{Z}^{*}
is the optimal projection of
\bm{Y}
onto convex
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
if and only if

\langle\bm{Z}^{*}-\bm{Y},\bm{X}-\bm{Z}^{*}\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}).

Therefore, the set of inexact projections are the points which inexactly satisfy this condition where the level of inexactness is controlled by
\delta
.
Clearly,
\text{P}_{\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}}^{0}(\bm{Y})=\text{Proj}_{\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}}(\bm{Y})
when
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
is a convex set.

For the sake of our analysis, we make the following assumption throughout Section
6

Assumption 1
.

\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})
is convex.

This assumption enables us to have a clear notion of first-order
\epsilon
-stationary points for (
SCO-Eig

Definition 2
.

\epsilon\geq 0
. The point

is a first-order

\min\left\{\langle\nabla F(\bm{X}^{*}),\bm{X}-\bm{X}^{*}\rangle\;|\;\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}^{*}\|_{F}\leq 1,\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b})\right\}\geq-\epsilon.

Algorithm

presents our inexact projected gradient method for solving (

).
The method can be neatly described. A point
\bm{X}
is selected from

and the traditional gradient update step is taken to compute
\bm{X}_{+}=\bm{X}-\alpha\nabla F(\bm{X})
where

is the stepsize. Since it is not necessarily true

, because
\alpha
could be too long of a step or
\nabla F(\bm{X})
might point out of the constraint set, feasibility is maintained by projecting
\bm{X}_{+}
onto the constraint. To save computational expenses, the projection is done inexactly. Line-search is performed at each iteration to ensure a sufficient decrease is obtained. The algorithm terminates when the gap between consecutive iterates decreases below a provided tolerance.

The approach is a fairly straightforward implementation of the traditional method in constrained optimization. The key novelty of Algorithm

comes from the fact we can solve the projection problem by Theorem

6.2
Inexact Frank-Wolfe Algorithm

We now present a Frank-Wolfe algorithm for solving (
SCO-Eig
).
The algorithm and analysis we present are an extension of the work in the concise technical report of Lacoste-Julien
[
24
]
.
The author in this paper presents the first Frank-Wolfe method for smooth non-convex functions over a compact and convex constraint.
Our approach extends the work in
[
24
]
by removing the compactness assumption. To accomplish this, we utilized a different notion of first-order stationarity, replaced the compactness assumption with the weaker assumption that the initial level set of the objective function is bounded, and introduced a modified subproblem for our model.
Traditional Frank-Wolfe approaches would require solving subproblems of the form

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle

(19)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\bm{A}\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})\leq\bm{b}

\displaystyle\;\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}\leq 1

\displaystyle\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}.

This model appears outside the scope of (
SCO-Eig
);
however, Theorem
8
below shows (
19
) can be bounded by an alternative optimization model which only contains linear constraints on the eigenvalues.

Theorem 8
.

Given any
\bm{X}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}
, we have the following upper bound to (
19
)

\bigg|\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}),\;\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}\leq 1\right\}\bigg|\\
\leq\bigg|\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}),\;\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}\bigg|.

(20)

Proof.

From Section 6.3 of
[
20
]
, we know for any
\bm{X},\bm{X}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}

\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{2}\text{ which implies }\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}.

Thus, given any
\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}
such that
\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}\leq 1
it follows
\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq 1
and
\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}\leq 1\right\}\subset\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}
. Hence

\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}),\;\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}\leq 1\right\}\\
\geq\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}),\;\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\},

(21)

and we know the optimal value of both models is non-positive since
\bm{X}_{0}
is a feasible solution for each model. Therefore, the right-hand side of (
21
) is greater in terms of absolute magnitude.
∎

Theorem
8
results in our ability to approximate the solution to (
19

Since Definition
2
is equivalent to the left-hand side of (

\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}),\;\|\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X})-\bm{\lambda}(\bm{X}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}

and use these solutions to bound the first-order stationarity condition of (
SCO-Eig
).

Theorem 9
.

Assume

Proof.

We first show

. If

\displaystyle F(\bm{X}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq F(\bm{X}_{0})+\gamma_{0}\langle\nabla F(\bm{X}_{0}),\bm{D}_{0}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle+\frac{L\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2}\|\bm{D}_{0}-\bm{X}_{0}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F(\bm{X}_{0})+\gamma_{0}\langle\nabla F(\bm{X}_{0}),\bm{D}_{0}-\bm{X}_{0}\rangle+\frac{\rho L\gamma_{0}^{2}}{2}

over all the iterates since

\bigg|\min\left\{\langle\bm{C},\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{t}\rangle\;|\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{A},\bm{b}),\;\|\bm{X}-\bm{X}_{t}\|_{F}\leq 1\right\}\bigg|\leq\epsilon.

Both Algorithms

The (

7.1
Preconditioning

One could argue solving systems of linear equations is the most important task in applied mathematics. Many iterative methods have been devised to solve
\bm{A}\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
full rank and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such as the Jacobi method
[
43
]
and the conjugate gradient method
[
18
]
. Methods for solving linear systems often converge linearly with the rate dependent upon the condition number of the matrix
\bm{A}
, i.e.,

\kappa(\bm{A}):=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})}

where
\sigma_{\max}(\bm{A})
and
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{A})
are the largest and smallest singular values of
\bm{A}
respectively. If
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}_{++}^{n\times n}
, then
\kappa(\bm{A})=\lambda_{1}(\bm{A})/\lambda_{n}(\bm{A})
. Table 1 in
[
42
]
compares how the convergence rates of different iterative methods depend on
\kappa(\bm{A})
. The art of matrix preconditioning is to form an equivalent linear system with an improved condition number which can be solved faster by iterative methods. This is accomplished by multiplying
\bm{A}
by simple matrices
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
such that
\bm{A}\bm{X}
,
\bm{Y}\bm{A}
or
\bm{Y}\bm{A}\bm{X}
have an improved condition number. Preconditioning is a well-studied aspect of numerical linear algebra
[
8
,
50
]
and numerous approaches have been proposed. For example, a recent paper proposes optimal diagonal preconditioners, i.e.,
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are diagonal matrices
[
42
]
. One popular preconditioning model proposed by Benson
[
2
]
is

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\|\bm{A}\bm{X}-\bm{I}\|_{F}

(27)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{M}

where
\mathcal{M}
is a special set of matrices. To reduce computation, Benson
[
2
]
assumed sufficiently sparse matrices in
\mathcal{M}
, e.g., positive diagonal matrices. We demonstrate the performance of our algorithms by solving (
27
) with various convex eigenvalue constraints, where we do not restrict to sparse matrices:

\mathcal{M}_{1}:=\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\lambda_{i}(\bm{X})\in[0.001,1],\;i=1,\ldots,n\right\},

(28)

\mathcal{M}_{2}:=\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\lambda_{1}(\bm{X})-\kappa\lambda_{n}(\bm{X})\leq 0,\;\lambda_{n}(\bm{X})\geq 0\right\},

(29)

and

\mathcal{M}_{3}:=\left\{\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}\;|\;\bm{c}_{i}^{\top}\lambda(\bm{X})\leq 1,\;i=1,\ldots,m\right\},

(30)

where
\kappa>0
and
\bm{c}_{i}=[i,i-1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots,0]^{\top}
for all
i
. The constraints
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
are reasonable choices for the constraint in (
27
) because they enforce the preconditioning matrix to be well-conditioned. The first ensures the condition number of the preconditioning matrix
\bm{X}
is bounded above by 1000 with bounded eigenvalues between 0.001 and 1; the second ensures the condition number is bounded above by
\kappa
while not enforcing an upper bound on the eigenvalues. Note,
\mathcal{M}_{2}
does admit the zero matrix as feasible which has an undefined condition number, but this is often of no consequence because often non-zero matrices are present in
\mathcal{M}_{2}
which yield better objective function values. The purpose of the last constraint is to showcase the algorithms applied to a general convex set as generated by Theorem
1
. The goal of these experiments is to evince the functionality of Algorithms
1
and
2
applied with different constraints on an objective function of practical import. Developing new specialized approaches for preconditioning is outside the scope of this paper.

We applied Algorithms
1
and
2
on three different instances of (
27
) with
\mathcal{M}_{1}
,
\mathcal{M}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{M}_{3}
serving as the constraint space
\mathcal{M}
. The matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathcal{S}^{250\times 250}
to be preconditioned was generated as
\bm{A}=\bm{V}\bm{V}^{\top}
where each element of
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{250\times 250}
was drawn from a standard normal. Figure

7.2
Solving Systems of Quadratic Equations

Solving systems of quadratic equations occurs regularly across scientific domains.
Two highly studied examples are phase retrieval and the algebraic Riccati equations. Phase retrieval is a problem of recovering a signal from the magnitude of its Fourier transform. Phase retrieval problems are important in imagining science with applications in X-ray crystallography
[
17
,
36
]
, X-ray tomography
[
10
]
and astronomy
[
14
]
, and all phase retrieval problems can be formulated as a system of quadratic equations
[
5
,
6
,
21
,
49
]
. The algebraic Riccati equations are crucial in the study of stochastic and optimal control
[
25
]
, and they also are equivalently expressed as a system of quadratic equations.

Here we demonstrate how to apply our methods to solve systems of quadratic equations. The general form of the problem we consider is:

\textbf{Find}\;\;\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\;\textbf{s.t.}\;\;\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{Q}_{i}\bm{x}=b_{i},\;i=1,\ldots m

(31)

where
\bm{Q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
. An equivalent matrix form of the problem is:

\textbf{Find}\;\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}_{+},\;\text{rank}(\bm{X})=1\;\;\textbf{s.t.}\;\;\langle\bm{Q}_{i},\bm{X}\rangle=b_{i},\;i=1,\ldots m

which naturally leads to the rank constrained model

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\langle\bm{Q}_{i},\bm{X}\rangle-b_{i}\right)^{2}

(32)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\text{rank}(\bm{X})=1

\displaystyle\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}_{+}.

If
\bm{X}^{*}
is computed yielding an objective value of zero to (
32
), then (
31
) has been solved. If no solution exists to (
31
), then (
32
) represents a least-squares approximate solution. A common relaxation of (
32
) is to drop the rank constraint to obtain a convex model. A global solution to the convex relaxation can be computed and projected onto the set of rank-1 matrices to obtain an approximate solution to (
31
). Another more precise relaxation of (
32
) is made possible with our framework. We instead consider the model

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(\langle\bm{Q}_{i},\bm{X}\rangle-b_{i}\right)^{2}

(33)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;\bm{\lambda}_{i}(\bm{X})\in[0,\delta],\;\;i=2,\ldots,n,

\displaystyle\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}

where
\delta>0
. For small
\delta
, the constraint closely approximates the rank-1 condition. It is easy to check this is a non-convex constraint on the eigenvalues which means the analysis in Section
6
does not guarantee convergence to stationary points; however, Theorem
6
guarantees we can compute optimal projections onto the constraint, so we can implement Algorithm
1
on (
33
).

We considered three methods for solving (
31
):

Newton:
Apply Newton’s method directly to (
31
). In our tests, we initialized and implemented Newton’s method ten times and took the best result as the solution.

Convex + Newton:
Solve the convex relaxation of (
32
), project the solution onto the set of rank-1 matrices and then run Newton’s method initialized from the projected solution. In our experiments, we utilized CVX to solve the convex relaxation
[
15
]
.

SCO + Newton:
Apply Algorithm
1
to approximately solve (
33
), project the solution onto the set of rank-1 matrices and then run Newton’s method initialized from the projected solution.

Our experiments were performed on synthetic data. We simulated random instances of (
31
) by drawing each entry in the
\bm{Q}_{i}
’s from a standard normal distribution and projecting the resulting matrix onto the set of symmetric matrices. The coefficients
b_{i}
were determined by randomly selecting a vector
\bm{y}\in R^{n}
with entries from a standard normal and computing
\bm{y}^{\top}\bm{Q}_{i}\bm{y}
for all
i
. In this way, every system admitted a solution. We conducted ten numerical experiments; five with
(n,m)=(75,75)
and five with

8
Conclusion

This paper investigates the first matrix optimization model with linear inequality constrained eigenvalues.
Theory was developed to understand the nature of the eigenvalue constraint set, and we presented KKT conditions for (
SCO-Eig
). We additionally verified the accuracy of a relaxation which ensured the differentiability of the eigenvalue operator over the feasible domain.
We proved global minima can be computed to (
SCO-Eig
) for linear objective problems, independent of the convexity of the constraint, and we proved how to compute exact projections. The computational complexity to obtain these global solutions is polynomial and only requires performing a spectral decomposition and solving a simple convex model, e.g., a linear program.
Using these results, we developed two algorithms which compute first-order
\epsilon
-stationary points for (
SCO-Eig
) with a sublinear convergence rate.
The practicality of our algorithms was accessed through numerical experimentation. Our example on solving systems of quadratic equations showcased a new and effective method for relaxing rank-constrained models.

Many future directions are open for investigation. First, we plan on extending the framework of (
SCO-Eig
) to include equality constraints on the coordinates of the matrix,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\;F(\bm{X})

(36)

s.t.

\displaystyle\;G_{i}(\bm{X})=0,i=1,\ldots,p

\displaystyle\;\bm{A}\lambda(\bm{X})\leq\bm{b}

\displaystyle\;\bm{X}\in\mathcal{S}^{n\times n}.

This paradigm will encompass most matrix optimization models studied over symmetric matrices and allow new spectral constraints never before considered. Additionally, we plan to develop approaches to solve models which constrain the singular values of non-square matrices and generalized singular values of tensors. As the applications of tensors expand in the burgeoning field of data science, the capability to manipulate the spectrum of these mathematical objects will become ever more important.

Proof for Section

The convexity of
f_{i}
in Theorem
1
follows from the following proposition.

Proposition 10
.

If
\alpha_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\alpha_{n}
, then
f(\bm{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\lambda_{i}(\bm{X})
is a convex function.

Proof.

It is well-known

and
h_{p}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{j=0}^{p}\lambda_{n-j}(\bm{X})
are convex and concave functions respectively for any
1\leq k\leq n
and
0\leq p\leq n-1

[
3
]
. From this and simple operations which preserve convexity it follows
g_{\alpha}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\lambda_{i}(\bm{X})
and
h_{\beta}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\beta_{n-j}\lambda_{n-j}(\bm{X})
with
\alpha_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\alpha_{n}\geq 0
and
\beta_{n}\leq\ldots\leq\beta_{1}\leq 0
are convex. Thus,

g_{\alpha}(\bm{X})+h_{\beta}(\bm{X})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i})\lambda_{i}(\bm{X})

is convex. The equivalence of the sets

\left\{\bm{y}+\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;y_{1}\geq\ldots\geq y_{n}\geq 0,\;z_{n}\leq\ldots\leq z_{1}\leq 0\right\}

and

\left\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;|\;x_{1}\geq\ldots\geq x_{n}\right\}
completes the argument.
