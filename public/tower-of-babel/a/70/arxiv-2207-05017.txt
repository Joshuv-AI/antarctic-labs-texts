Almost optimum ℓ \ell -covering of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n}
Ke Shi, Chao Xu

Abstract
A subset B B of the ring ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} is referred to as a ℓ \ell -covering set if { a b ( mod n ) ∣ 0 ≤ a ≤ ℓ , b ∈ B } = ℤ n \{ab\pmod{n}\mid 0\leq a\leq\ell,b\in B\}=\mathbb{Z}_{n} . We show that there exists a ℓ \ell -covering set of ℤ n \mathbb{Z}_{n} of size O ⁡ ( n ℓ ​ log ⁡ n ) O(\frac{n}{\ell}\log n) for all n n and ℓ \ell , and how to construct such a set. We also provide examples where any ℓ \ell -covering set must have a size of Ω ⁡ ( n ℓ ​ log ⁡ n log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\frac{n}{\ell}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}) . The proof employs a refined bound for the relative totient function obtained through sieve theory and the existence of a large divisor with a linear divisor sum. The result can be used to simplify a modular subset sum algorithm.

1.2
Technical overview

Our approach is similar to the one of Koiliaris and Xu
[
14
]
. We briefly describe their approach.
Recall
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
is the set of integers modulo
n
. We further define
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}=\left\{x\mid\gcd(x,n)=d,x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\right\}
, and
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}_{n,1}
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(X)
be the set of segments of length
\ell
and slope in
X
. Their main idea is to convert the covering problem over the
semigroup

(\mathbb{Z}_{n},\cdot)
to covering problems over the
group

(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n/d},\cdot)
for all
d\in\mathbb{D}_{n}
. Since
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}
forms a partition of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, one can reason about covering them individually. That is, covering
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}
by
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z}_{n,d})
. This is equivalent to covering
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n/d}
with
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n/d})
, and then lifting to a cover in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}
by multiplying everything by
d
. Hence, now we only have to work with covering problems over
(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n/d},\cdot)
for all
d
and
n\geq 2
, all of which are
groups
. The covering results for groups can be readily applied
[
3
]
. Once we find the covering for each individual
(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n/d},\cdot)
, we take their union, and obtain an
\ell
-covering.

The approach was sufficient to obtain
f(n,\ell)=O(\frac{n}{\ell}\log n\log\log n)
if
\ell
is neither
too small
nor
too large
. However, their result suffers when
\ell
is extreme in one of the two ways.

1.

\ell=n^{1-o(\frac{1}{\log\log n})}
: Any covering obtained would have size at least the number of divisors of
n
, which in the worst case can be
n^{\Omega(\frac{1}{\log\log n})}
, and dominates
\frac{n}{\ell}
.

2.

\ell=n^{o(\frac{1}{\log\log n})}
: If we are working on covering
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n}
, we need to know
|\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n}\cap[\ell]|
, also known as
\phi(n,\ell)
. Previously, the estimate for
\phi(n,\ell)
was insufficient when
\ell
is small.

Our approach can extend the applicable range to all
\ell
, and also eliminates the extra
\log\log n
factor. There are two steps: First, we improve the estimate for
\phi(n,\ell)
. This improvement alone is sufficient to handle the cases when
\ell
is relatively small compared to
n
. Second, we show that, roughly, a small
\ell^{\prime}
-covering of
\mathbb{D}_{n}
with some additional nice properties implies a small
\ell
-covering of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, where
\ell^{\prime}
is some number not too small compared to
\ell
. This change can shave off the
\log\log n
factor.

Preliminaries

This paper utilizes a few simple algorithmic concepts, but our methods are primarily analytical. Therefore, we have reserved some space in the preliminaries to set the scene.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a collection of subsets in some universe set
U
. A
set cover
of
U
is a subcollection of
\mathcal{X}
whose union covers
U
. Formally,
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a set cover of
U
if
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that
U=\bigcup_{X\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}}X
. The
set cover problem
is the computational problem of finding a set cover of minimum cardinality.

All multiplications in
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
are modulo
n
, and henceforth we will omit the "
\pmod{n}
" notation.
A set of the form
\left\{ix\mid x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cap[\ell]\right\}
is called a
segment
of length
\ell
with slope
i
. Note that a segment of length
\ell
might contain fewer than
\ell
elements. Recall that
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(X)
represents the collection of segments of length
\ell
with slopes in
X
, namely
\left\{\left\{ix\mid x\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\cap[\ell]\right\}\mid i\in X\right\}
. Thus, finding an
\ell
-covering is equivalent to the set cover problem where the universe is
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and the collection of subsets is
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z}_{n})
.

There are well-known bounds relating the size of a set cover to the frequency of each element in the cover.

Theorem 2.1
(
[
15
,
19
]
)

Let there be a collection of
t
sets each with size at most
a
, and each element of the universe is covered by at least
b
of the sets, then there exists a subcollection of
O(\frac{t}{b}\log a)
sets that covers the universe.

The above theorem serves as our primary combinatorial tool for bounding the size of a set cover. To achieve a cover of the desired size, we find the greedy algorithm to be sufficient. It is worth noting that the group covering theorem for finite groups, as presented in
[
3
]
, is a direct application of this principle.

In this context, the base of the
\log
is
e
. To avoid dealing with negative values, we define
\log(x)
as
\max(\log(x),1)
. We use
\tilde{O}(f(n))
, the soft
O
, as shorthand for
O(f(n)\operatorname{polylog}n)
.

3.1
Estimate for relative totient function

This section proves a good estimate of

Theorem 3.1

There exists positive constant

\phi(n,\ell)=\begin{cases}\Omega(\frac{\ell}{n}\phi(n))&\text{ if }\ell>c\log^{5}n\\
\Omega(\frac{\ell}{\log\ell})&\text{ if }\ell>c\log n\\
\end{cases}

The proof can be found in
Section

0.A.3
. As a corollary, we prove a density theorem.

Theorem 3.2

There exists a constant
c
, such that for any
n
, and a divisor
d
of
n
, if
\frac{\ell}{c\log^{5}n}\geq d
, then each element in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}
is covered
\Omega(\frac{n}{\ell}\phi(n))
times by
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n})
.

Proof

By
Theorem

2.2
, the number of segments in
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n})
covering some fixed element in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}
is
\frac{\phi(n/d,\ell/d)}{\phi(n/d)}\phi(n)
. As long as
\ell
is not too small,
\phi(n,\ell)=\Omega(\frac{\ell}{n}\phi(n))
. In particular, by

, if
\lfloor\ell/d\rfloor\geq c\log^{5}(n/d)
, we have
\phi(n/d,\ell/d)/\phi(n/d)=\Omega(\frac{\ell}{n})

\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}

times.

3.2
Large divisor with small divisor sum

Theorem 3.3

If
r=n^{O(\frac{1}{\log\log\log n})}
, then there exists
m|n
, such that
m\geq r
,

d(m)=r^{O(\frac{1}{\log\log r})}
and
\sigma(m)=O(m)
.

Proof

If there is a single prime
p
, such that
p^{e}|n
and
p^{e}\geq r
, then we pick
m=p^{e^{\prime}}
, where
e^{\prime}
is the smallest integer such that
p^{e^{\prime}}\geq r
. One can see

, also
\varphi(m)=m(1-\frac{1}{p})\geq\frac{m}{2}
, since
\varphi(m)\sigma(m)\leq m^{2}
we are done.

Otherwise, we write

, where each
p_{i}
is a distinct prime number. The prime

are ordered by the weight
w_{i}=e_{i}p_{i}\log p_{i}
in decreasing order. That is
w_{i}\geq w_{i+1}
for all
i
. Let
j
be the smallest number such that
\prod_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{e_{i}}\geq r
. Let
m=\prod_{i=1}^{j}p_{i}^{e_{i}}
.

First, we show
d(m)
is small.
Let
m^{\prime}=m/p_{j}^{e_{j}}
.
One can see that
m^{\prime}<r
and
p_{j}^{e_{j}}<r
. So
e_{j}=O(\log r)
, and

d(m)\leq(e_{j}+1)d(m^{\prime})=O(\log r)d(m^{\prime})=r^{O(\frac{1}{\log\log r})}.

To show that
\sigma(m)=O(m)
, we show
\phi(m)=\Theta(m)
. Indeed, by
\sigma(m)\leq\frac{m^{2}}{\phi(m)}
, we obtain
\sigma(m)=O(m)
. For simplicity, it is easier to work with sum instead of products, so we take logarithm of everything and define
t=\log n
. By definition,
\log r=O(\frac{\log n}{\log\log\log n})=O(\frac{t}{\log\log t})
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}e_{i}\log p_{i}=t
.

Note
j
is the smallest number such that
\sum_{i=1}^{j}e_{i}\log p_{i}\geq\log r
. Because there is no prime
p
such that
p^{e}|n
and
p^{e}\geq r
, we also have
\sum_{i=1}^{j}e_{i}\log p_{i}<2\log r=O(\frac{t}{\log\log t})
.

Now, consider
e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{k}
, such that the following holds.

•

\sum_{i=1}^{j}e_{i}\log p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}e_{i}^{\prime}\log p_{i}
, and
e_{i}^{\prime}p_{i}\log p_{i}=c_{1}
for some
c_{1}
, when
1\leq i\leq j
,

•

\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}e_{i}\log p_{i}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{n}e_{i}^{\prime}\log p_{i}
, and
e_{i}^{\prime}p_{i}\log p_{i}=c_{2}
for some
c_{2}
, where
j+1\leq i\leq k
.

Note

and
c_{2}
can be interpreted as weighted averages over
w_{i}
. Indeed, consider sequences
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
, such that
\sum_{i}x_{i}=\sum_{i}y_{i}
. If for some non-negative

, we have
a_{i}y_{i}=c
for all
i,j
, then
c\leq\max_{i}a_{i}x_{i}
. Indeed, there exists
x_{j}\geq y_{j}
, so
\max_{i}a_{i}x_{i}\geq a_{j}x_{j}\geq a_{j}y_{j}=c
. Similarly,
c\geq\min_{i}a_{i}x_{i}
. This shows
c_{1}\geq c_{2}
, because
c_{2}\leq\max_{i=j+1}^{k}w_{i}=w_{j+1}\leq w_{j}=\min_{i=1}^{j}w_{i}\leq c_{1}
.

We first give a lower bound of

.

\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}\frac{c_{2}}{p_{i}}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}e^{\prime}_{i}\log p_{i}=\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}e_{i}\log p_{i}\geq t-O(\frac{t}{\log\log t})=\Omega(t)
.

\sum_{i=j+1}^{k}\frac{c_{2}}{p_{i}}\leq c_{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\leq c_{2}\sum_{p\text{ prime},p=O(t)}\frac{1}{p}=c_{2}O(\log\log t)
.

This shows
c_{2}O(\log\log t)=\Omega(t)
, or

.

Since

,

\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{1}{p_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\frac{e_{i}^{\prime}\log p_{i}}{c_{1}}=\frac{O(\frac{t}{\log\log t})}{c_{1}}\leq\frac{O(\frac{t}{\log\log t})}{c_{2}}=\frac{O(\frac{t}{\log\log t})}{\Omega(\frac{t}{\log\log t})}=O(1)
.

Note

. Because

for

, so

.

3.3
Covering of
\mathbb{D}_{n}

Recall that
(\mathbb{D}_{n},\odot)
is the semigroup over the set of divisors of
n
, and the operation
\odot
is defined as
a\odot b=\gcd(ab,n)
. Throughout this section, we fix a
s\leq n
, and let
A:=\mathbb{D}_{n}\cap[s]
.
We are interested in finding
s
-coverings of
\mathbb{D}_{n}
, that is, finding
B\subseteq\mathbb{D}_{n}
such that
(\mathbb{D}_{n}\cap[s])\odot B=\mathbb{D}_{n}
.
As we mentioned previously, the main goal is to show that a good
s
-covering of
\mathbb{D}_{n}
lifts to a
\ell
-covering of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
of small size. The criteria for a good
s
-covering

is two folds: the size of

should be small (
O(\frac{n}{s}\frac{1}{\log^{c}n})
), and the reciprocal sum of
B
, namely
\sum_{d\in B}\frac{1}{d}
should also be small (
O(1)
). However, one can’t hope to optimize both at the same time. Fortunately, for our application, we only need the reciprocal sum to be small when
s
is small.

To obtain a
s
-covering of
\mathbb{D}_{n}
, there are two natural choices of
B
.

1.

Let
B=(\mathbb{D}_{n}\setminus[s])\cup\{1\}
. If
d\leq s

In this section, we prove our bounds in

4.1
Upper bound

The high-level idea is to divide the problem into sub-problems of covering multiple

\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}
for many distinct

to an
\ell
-covering of
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
. For the remainder of this section, we define

3.1
. Let

be any
s

\mathbb{D}_{n}
. For each

. We denote

as the size of the smallest set cover of
\bigcup_{d|n,d\leq s}\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}

We provide a bound for

Theorem 4.1

There exists a constant

g(n,\ell)=\begin{cases}O(\frac{n}{\ell}\log\ell)&\text{ if }\ell\geq c\log^{5}n,\\
O(\frac{\phi(n)}{\ell}\log^{2}\ell)&\text{ if }c\log^{5}n>\ell\geq c\log n.\end{cases}

Proof

By
Theorem

2.2
, The number of times an element in

get covered by a segment in

is
\frac{\phi(\frac{n}{d},\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\right\rfloor)}{\phi(\frac{n}{d})}\phi(n)
. We consider

Case 1.
\ell>c\log^{5}n
. Consider a

. Hence,
\phi(\frac{n}{d},\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\rfloor)=\Omega(\frac{\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\right\rfloor}{\frac{n}{d}}\phi(\frac{n}{d}))=\Omega(\frac{\ell}{n}\phi(\frac{n}{d}))

Therefore, each element in
\mathbb{Z}_{n,d}

\frac{\phi(\frac{n}{d},\left\lfloor\frac{\ell}{d}\right\rfloor)}{\phi(\frac{n}{d})}\phi(n)=\Omega(\frac{\ell}{n}\phi(n))

By

g(n,\ell)=O\left(\frac{\phi(n)\log\ell}{\frac{\ell}{n}\phi(n)}\right)=O\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\log\ell\right).

Case 2. If

, then

, and we try to cover
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n}
with
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}(\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n})
. Each element is covered by
\frac{\phi(n,\ell)}{\phi(n)}\phi(n)=\Omega(\frac{\ell}{\log\ell})
segments. By

g(n,\ell)=O\left(\frac{\phi(n)\log\ell}{\frac{\ell}{\log\ell}}\right)=O\left(\frac{\phi(n)}{\ell}\log^{2}\ell\right).

We are ready to prove our main theorem.

Theorem 4.2
(Main)

There exists an
\ell
-covering set of size

for all

where

Proof

If

\displaystyle f(n,\ell)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d\in B}g(\frac{n}{d},\ell)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{n}{\ell}(\log\log n)^{2}\right)+O\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\log n\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{n}{\ell}\log n\right)

\displaystyle f(n,\ell)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{d\in B}g(\frac{n}{d},\ell)

Let there be

Proof
(Sketch)

4.2
Lower bound

We note that our upper bound is optimal through the combinatorial set covering property (
Theorem

2.1
). The
\log n
factor cannot be avoided when
\ell=n^{\Omega(1)}
. To obtain a superior bound, stronger
number theoretical properties
must be leveraged, as was the case when
n
is a prime
[
5
]
.

We demonstrate that it is improbable to acquire significantly stronger bounds when
\ell
is small
. For an infinite number of
(n,\ell)
pairs, our bound is merely a
\log\log n
factor away from the lower bound.

Theorem 4.5

There exists a constant
c>0
, for which there are an infinite number of
n,\ell
pairs where

f(n,\ell)\geq c\frac{n}{\ell}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
.

Proof

Let
n
be the product of the smallest
k
prime numbers, then
k=\Theta(\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
. Let
\ell
be the smallest number where
\pi(\ell)=k
. Given that
\pi(\ell)=\Theta(\frac{\ell}{\log\ell})
, we know that
\ell=\Theta(\log n)
.

Note that
\phi(n,\ell)=1
. Indeed, every number
\leq\ell
except
1
has a common factor with
n
. To cover all elements in
\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{n}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{n}
, the
\ell
-covering size must be at least
\frac{\phi(n)}{\phi(n,\ell)}=\phi(n)=\Omega(\frac{n}{\log\log n})=\Omega(\frac{n}{\ell}\frac{\log n}{\log\log n})
.

4.3
Application: Simplifying modular subset sum computation

We demonstrate how our improved bound of
\ell
-covering can be advantageous in algorithm design.
\ell
-covering offers a natural divide-and-conquer algorithm; by partitioning elements into segments in the
\ell
-covering, solving the subproblem, and then combining them together. Such an approach was employed in modular subset sum computations. The modular subset sum problem is defined as follows: Given
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{n}
with
|S|=m
, output all values
i
such that
\sum_{x\in T}x=i
for some
T\subset S
.

To solve the modular subset sum, the following theorem was established:

Theorem 4.6
(
[
14
, Lemma 5.2]
)

Let
S\subset\mathbb{Z}_{n}
be a set of size
m
, and it can be covered by
k
segments of length
\ell
, then the subset sums of
S
can be computed in
O(kn\log n+m\ell\log(m\ell)\log m)
time.

Utilizing the previous
\ell
-covering bound of
O(\frac{n^{1+o(1)}}{\ell})
, a direct application would lead to an
O(\sqrt{m}n^{1+o(1)})
time algorithm. Instead, in
[
14
]
, using a much more intricate recursive partitioning, coupled with a second-level application of
Theorem

4.6
, Koiliaris and Xu obtained an

time algorithm.

Armed with our improved bound on
\ell
-covering, we know
k=O(\frac{n}{\ell}\log n)
. Therefore, setting
\ell=\frac{n}{\sqrt{m}}
, we directly obtain a running time of
O(\sqrt{m}n\log^{2}n)
from
Theorem

4.6
, matching the significantly more complicated algorithm.

It’s worth noting that

time algorithms that completely avoid
\ell
-covering have been discovered
[
4
,
10
,
2
,
1
,
16
]
. However, we continue to believe that
\ell
-covering can provide advantages in future algorithmic applications.
