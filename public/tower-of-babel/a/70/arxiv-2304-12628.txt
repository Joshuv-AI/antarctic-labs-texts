A Moment-SOS Hierarchy for Robust Polynomial Matrix Inequality Optimization with SOS-Convexity Thanks: Submitted to the editors DATE.
Feng Guo, Jie Wang

Abstract
We study a class of polynomial optimization problems with a robust polynomial matrix inequality (PMI) constraint where the uncertainty set itself is defined also by a PMI. These can be viewed as matrix generalizations of semi-infinite polynomial programs, since they involve actually infinitely many PMI constraints in general. Under certain SOS-convexity assumptions, we construct a hierarchy of increasingly tight moment-SOS relaxations for solving such problems. Most of the nice features of the moment-SOS hierarchy for the usual polynomial optimization are extended to this more complicated setting. In particular, asymptotic convergence of the hierarchy is guaranteed and finite convergence can be certified if some flat extension condition holds true. To extract global minimizers, we provide a linear algebra procedure for recovering a finitely atomic matrix-valued measure from truncated matrix-valued moments. As an application, we are able to solve the problem of minimizing the smallest eigenvalue of a polynomial matrix subject to a PMI constraint. If SOS-convexity is replaced by convexity, we can still approximate the optimal value as closely as desired by solving a sequence of semidefinite programs, and certify global optimality in case that certain flat extension conditions hold true. Finally, an extension to the non-convexity setting is provided under a rank one condition. To obtain the above-mentioned results, techniques from real algebraic geometry, matrix-valued measure theory, and convex optimization are employed.

polynomial optimization, polynomial matrix inequality, robust optimization, moment-SOS hierarchy, semidefinite programming

Introduction

Polynomial optimization problems with polynomial matrix inequality (PMI) constraints
have a wide range of applications in many fields
[
5
,
21
,
22
,
24
,
29
,
44
]
. In particular, as special cases of PMIs, linear or bilinear matrix inequality constrained problems appear frequently in many synthesis problems for linear systems in optimal control
[
49
]
.
Due to estimation errors or lack of information, the data of real-world optimization
problems often involve uncertainty. Hence, robust optimization is an appropriate
modeling paradigm for some safety-critical applications with little tolerance for failure

[
6
]
.

In this paper, we study the following robust PMI optimization problem
under data uncertainty in the PMI constraint:

\left\{\begin{aligned} f^{\star}\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}&\ f(\mathbf{y})\\
\text{s.t.}&\ \mathcal{Y}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell},\ P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\succeq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n},\\
\end{aligned}\right.

(RPMIO)

where
f(\mathbf{y})
is a polynomial function in
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})
which
is the decision variables constrained in a basic semialgebraic set

\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}\mid\theta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0,\ldots,\theta_{s}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0\},

(1)

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is the uncertain parameters belonging to some uncertainty set

\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid G(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0\}

(2)

defined by a
q\times q
symmetric polynomial matrix
G(\mathbf{x})
, and
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
is an
m\times m
symmetric polynomial matrix in
\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{x}
(i.e.,
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
depends polynomially on the decision variable
\mathbf{y}
and the uncertain parameter
\mathbf{x}
). This problem essentially has infinitely many PMI constraints corresponding to different points of
\mathcal{X}
.
We assume that the set of optimizers of (
RPMIO
) is nonempty and
make the following sum-of-squares (SOS)-convexity assumptions on (
RPMIO
).

Assumption 1
.

(i)
f(\mathbf{y}),-\theta_{1}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,-\theta_{s}(\mathbf{y})
are SOS-convex (Definition
2
);
(ii)
-P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
is PSD-SOS-convex (Definition
4
) in
\mathbf{y}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
;
(iii)
\mathcal{X}
is compact.

To highlight the modelling power of (
RPMIO
) under Assumption
1
, let us name a few problems from different fields which can be modelled as an instance of (
RPMIO
).
First of all, we note that if
\mathcal{Y}={\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
and
f(\mathbf{y})
,
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
depend affinely on
\mathbf{y}
, then we retrieve the robust polynomial semidefinite program (SDP) considered in
[
46
]
.
A basic problem in interval computations is to estimate intervals of confidence for the components of a given vector-valued function when its variables range in a product of intervals. Assume that the function is given by polynomials and we seek an ellipsoid of confidence for its components. Then this problem can be modelled as a robust polynomial SDP (hence an instance of (
RPMIO
)).
In optimal control, many problems for systems of ordinary differential equations can be posed as convex optimization problems with matrix inequality constraints which should hold on a prescribed portion of the state space
[
8
,
22
,
46
]
. If the involved functions in the differential equations are polynomials, these problems often take the form of robust polynomial SDPs. In the context of risk management, the robust correlation stress testing where data uncertainty arises due to untimely recording of portfolio holdings can be formulated as a robust least square SDP fitting into the form of (
RPMIO
)
[
35
]
. Moreover, the deterministic PMI optimization problem of maximizing a polynomial function
h(\mathbf{x})
subject to a PMI constraint
G(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0
(recall that multiple PMI constraints can be easily merged into one by diagonal augmentation) can be also formulated as an instance of (
RPMIO
):

\inf_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}}\ y\quad\text{s.t.}\ \ y-h(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

(3)

Since the deterministic PMI optimization problem (including the usual polynomial optimization problem as a special case) is already difficult to solve in general, solving (
RPMIO
) (even under Assumption
1
) is more challenging.

For deterministic PMI optimization problems, Kojima
[
30
]
proposed SOS
relaxations by utilizing a penalty function and a generalized Lagrangian dual, and subsequently Henrion and Lasserre
[
21
]
gave a hierarchy of moment relaxations allowing to detect finite convergence and to extract global minimizers.
Recently, there have been increasing interests in studying robust polynomial optimization
problems; see e.g.
[
9
,
23
,
25
,
34
]
. However, robust PMI constraints are not considered in these work. For robust polynomial SDPs,
Scherer and Hol
[
46
]
provided a hierarchy of matrix SOS relaxations by establishing a Putinar’s style Positivstellensätz for polynomial matrices. There are also approaches for solving other types of robust SDPs
[
35
,
37
,
43
]
. As far as the authors know, there is
little
work on how to solve or even approximate (
RPMIO
) in general.

Before introducing our main contributions, we would like to point out that the matrix SOS relaxations in
[
46
]
cannot be straightforwardly extended to handle (
RPMIO
) due to the
nonlinearity
of
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{y}
. Also, the dual
moment
facet of the matrix SOS relaxations for (
RPMIO
) was not investigated in
[
46
]
or elsewhere. This motivates us to establish a moment-SOS hierarchy for (
RPMIO
) (under Assumption
1
) in full generality.

Let us denote by
{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
the cone of
m\times m
symmetric real polynomial matrices
in
\mathbf{x}
and by
\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
its subcone consisting of polynomial matrices
which are positive semidefinite (PSD) on
\mathcal{X}
.
By Haviland’s theorem for polynomial matrices (Theorem
9
), the dual cone
of
\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
consists of tracial
\mathcal{X}
-moment functionals on
{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}

(Definition
8
), while justifying the membership of a linear functional on

{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
to the dual cone of
\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
amounts to the matrix-valued

\mathcal{X}
-moment problem (Definition
11
). Therefore, to explore the dual aspect of the
matrix SOS relaxations for (
RPMIO
), we need to invoke the results on
the matrix-valued moment problem. For a given multi-indexed sequence of real

m\times m
symmetric matrices
\mathbf{S}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
, the matrix-valued

\mathcal{X}
-moment problem asks if there exists a PSD matrix-valued representing
measure
\Phi
supported on
\mathcal{X}
such that
S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})
for all

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. We refer the reader to
[
28
]
for a thorough introduction on
the history and background about the matrix-valued moment problem. For the scalar moment
problem (
m=1
), due to Haviland’s theorem and Putinar’s Positivstellensätz,
the representing measure is guaranteed by the PSDness of the associated moment matrices and localizing matrices. Based on this, Lasserre
[
31
]

proposed the moment-SOS hierarchy for the scalar polynomial optimization and established
its asymptotic convergence. For the truncated scalar moment problem, Curto and Fialkow

[
12
]
gave the celebrated flat extension condition (FEC) on the moment matrix as a sufficient condition for the existence of a representing measure, which allows to detect finite convergence of Lasserre’s hierarchy and extract global minimizers
[
20
]
.
Recently, Kimsey and Trachana
[
28
]
obtained a flat extension theorem for the truncated matrix-valued moment problem.
Very recently, Nie
[
42
, Chapter 10.3.2]
investigated the truncated matrix-valued
K
-moment problem where
K
is defined by polynomial equalities and PMIs.
He gave a solution to this problem using an FEC on the

block rank
of the associated moment matrix. We would like to point
out that the FEC on the block rank is stronger than on the usual rank (see Remark
19
). In the rest of this paper, unless stated otherwise,
the abbreviation FEC means the flat extension condition on the usual rank of the associated moment matrix.
Unlike the scalar case, to the best of our knowledge, there is
little
work in the literature to link the theory of matrix-valued moments to PMI optimization.
In this paper, we aim to construct a moment-SOS hierarchy for (
RPMIO
) with SOS-convexity by employing Scherer-Hol’s Positivstellensätz and the matrix-valued measure theory.

Contributions.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

As the first contribution, we provide a solution to the truncated matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem using the FEC.
Furthermore, we develop a linear algebra procedure for retrieving a finitely atomic representing measure whenever the FEC holds, which extends Henrion-Lasserre’s algorithm to the matrix setting.
We remark that those extensions from the scalar setting to the matrix setting are not straightforward applications of existing results. Indeed, the matrix-valued moment matrix is a multi-indexed
block
matrix and the kernel of a truncated PSD moment matrix is a linear subspace rather than a real radical ideal, which make the matrix setting more involved.

2.

We establish a moment-SOS hierarchy for solving (
RPMIO
) with SOS-convexity.
Here, the main difficulty stems from the nonlinearity of
P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
in
\mathbf{y}
. To overcome this obstacle, we reformulate (
RPMIO
) to a conic optimization problem via the Lagrange dual theory, and then replace the conic constraints with more tractable matrix quadratic module constraints or matrix-valued pseudo-moment cone constraints.
This yields a sequence of upper bounds on the optimum of (
RPMIO
) with guaranteed asymptotic convergence.
More importantly, we show that if the FEC holds, then finite convergence occurs and we can extract a globally optimal solution
\mathbf{y}^{\star}
of (
RPMIO
) as well as the points
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta(\mathbf{y}^{\star})
and the corresponding vectors
\mathbf{v}
, where

\Delta(\mathbf{y}^{\star})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\mid\exists\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\,\ {\text{ s.t. }}\ P(\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}=0\}

(4)

is the index set of constraints active at
\mathbf{y}^{\star}
.

3.

For the linear case of (
RPMIO
), we show that the dual problem is exactly the generalized matrix-valued moment problem and our SOS relaxations recover the matrix SOS relaxations proposed by Scherer and Hol
[
46
]
.
As a complement to the matrix SOS relaxations in
[
46
]
,
the dual matrix-valued moment relaxation allows us to detect finite convergence and to extract optimal solutions.
As an application, we provide a solution to the problem of minimizing the smallest eigenvalue of a polynomial matrix over a set defined by a PMI.

4.

In case that the SOS-convexity assumption of (
RPMIO
) is weakened to convexity, we also provide a sequence of SDPs that can approximate the optimal value of (
RPMIO
) as closely as desired. Moreover, finite convergence can be detected via certain FECs. For the general non-convex case, we give respectively conditions under which a lower bound on the optimum, an upper bound on the optimum, or a globally optimal solution can be retrieved.

While the moment-SOS hierarchy provides a powerful framework for scalar polynomial optimization, extending it to handle robust PMI constraints is a non-trivial task even in the SOS-convex case. A straightforward idea would be scalarizing the PMI constraint via the equivalence relation: an
m\times m
polynomial matrix
P\succeq 0
if and only if
\mathbf{z}^{\intercal}P\mathbf{z}\geq 0
for all
\mathbf{z}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. However, this approach bears two drawbacks: 1) it necessitates
m
auxiliary variables which increases the problem size; 2) the information on the structure (e.g., sparsity) of the polynomial matrix becomes less transparent.
By contrast, our approach natively treats (
RPMIO
) at the matrix level, thus preserving its matrix structure and allowing the theory of matrix-valued measures to come into play. The information on matrix structures can be exploited to develop a structured moment-SOS hierarchy in order to tackle large-scale application problems (which will be addressed in a follow-up paper).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
We first recall some preliminaries in Section
2
.
Then, we consider the truncated matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem in Section
3
and propose a linear algebra procedure for retrieving the representing measure.
In Section
4
, we construct a moment-SOS hierarchy for solving (
RPMIO
) with SOS-convexity, and treat some special cases. Extensions of the results to the general convex case and the non-convex case are discussed in Section
5
.
Conclusions are given in Section
6
.

2.1
SOS polynomial matrices, SOS-convexity and positivstellensatz for polynomial matrices

For an
l_{1}\times l_{2}
polynomial matrix
T(\mathbf{x})=[T_{ij}(\mathbf{x})]
, define

\deg(T)\coloneqq\max\,\{\deg(T_{ij})\mid i=1,\ldots,l_{1},j=1,\ldots,l_{2}\}.

A polynomial matrix
\Sigma(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{q}
is said to be an
SOS matrix
if there exists an
l\times q
polynomial matrix
T(\mathbf{x})
for some

l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Sigma(\mathbf{x})=T(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}T(\mathbf{x})
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, denote by
u_{d}(\mathbf{x})

the canonical basis of
{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]_{d}
, i.e.,

u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq[1,\ x_{1},\ x_{2},\ \cdots,\ x_{n},\ x_{1}^{2},\ x_{1}x_{2},\ \cdots,\ x_{n}^{d}]^{\intercal},

(5)

whose cardinality is
|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}|=\binom{n+d}{d}
.
With
d=\deg(T)
,
we can write
T(\mathbf{x})
as

T(\mathbf{x})=Q(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{q})\text{ with }Q=[Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}|}],\quad Q_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l\times q},

where
Q
is the vector of coefficient matrices of
T(\mathbf{x})
with respect to

u_{d}(\mathbf{x})
. Hence,
\Sigma(\mathbf{x})
is an SOS matrix with respect to
u_{d}(\mathbf{x})
if there
exists some
Q\in{\mathbb{R}}^{l\times q|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}|}
satisfying

\Sigma(\mathbf{x})=T(\mathbf{x})^{\intercal}T(\mathbf{x})=(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{q})^{\intercal}(Q^{\intercal}Q)(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{q}).

We thus have the following result.

Proposition 1
.

[
46
, Lemma 1]

A polynomial matrix
\Sigma(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{q}
is an SOS matrix with
respect to the monomial basis
u_{d}(\mathbf{x})
if and only if there exists
Z\in\mathbb{S}_{+}^{q|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}|}
such that

\Sigma(\mathbf{x})=(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{q})^{\intercal}Z(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{q}).

Now let us recall some basic concepts about SOS-convexity.

Definition 2
.

[
19
]

A polynomial
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]
is
SOS-convex
if its Hessian
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}h(\mathbf{y})
is an SOS matrix.

While checking the convexity of a polynomial
is generally NP-hard
[
1
]
, the SOS-convexity can be justified numerically by solving an SDP by Proposition
1
. Recall that

Definition 3
.

A polynomial matrix
Q(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
is

PSD-convex
if

tQ(\mathbf{y}^{(1)})+(1-t)Q(\mathbf{y}^{(2)})\succeq Q(t\mathbf{y}^{(1)}+(1-t)\mathbf{y}^{(2)})

holds for any
\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\mathbf{y}^{(2)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
and
t\in(0,1)
.

Nie
[
40
]
gave an extension of SOS-convexity to polynomial matrices.

Definition 4
.

[
40
]

A polynomial matrix
Q(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
is

PSD-SOS-convex

if for every
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, there exists a polynomial matrix

F_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y})
in
\mathbf{y}
such that

\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v})=F_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y})^{\intercal}F_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{y}).

In other words,

is PSD-SOS-convex if and
only if
\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v}
is an SOS-convex polynomial for each

Clearly, if

is PSD-SOS-convex, then it is PSD-convex,
but not vice versa. The PSD-SOS-convexity condition requires checking the Hessian
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v})
for every

\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}

A

[

]

from the open subsets of
\mathcal{X}
and by
\mathfrak{m}(\mathcal{X})
the set of all finite Borel measures on
\mathcal{X}
. A measure
\phi\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathcal{X})
is
positive
if
\phi(\mathcal{A})\geq 0
for all
\mathcal{A}\in B(\mathcal{X})
. Denote by
\mathfrak{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
the set of all finite positive Borel measures on
\mathcal{X}
.
The support
\mbox{supp}(\phi)
of a Borel measure
\phi\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathcal{X})
is the (unique) smallest closed set
\mathcal{A}\in B(\mathcal{X})

such that
\phi(\mathcal{X}\setminus\mathcal{A})=0
.

Definition 6
.

Let
\phi_{ij}\in\mathfrak{m}(\mathcal{X})
,
i,j=1,\ldots,m
.
The
m\times m
matrix-valued measure
\Phi
on
\mathcal{X}
is defined as the matrix-valued function

\Phi\colon B(\mathcal{X})\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}
with

\Phi(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq[\phi_{ij}(\mathcal{A})]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m},\quad\forall\mathcal{A}\in B(\mathcal{X}).

If
\phi_{ij}=\phi_{ji}
for all
i,j=1,\ldots,m
, we call
\Phi
a symmetric matrix-valued measure.
If
\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\Phi(\mathcal{A})\mathbf{v}\geq 0
holds for all
\mathcal{A}\in B(\mathcal{X})
and for all column vectors
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we call
\Phi
a PSD matrix-valued measure. The set
\mbox{supp}(\Phi)\coloneqq\bigcup_{i,j=1}^{m}\mbox{supp}(\phi_{ij})

is called the support of the matrix-valued measure
\Phi
.
A function
h\colon\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called
\Phi
-measurable if
h
is
\phi_{ij}
-measurable for every
i,j=1,\ldots,m
. The matrix-valued integral of
h
with respect to the measure
\Phi
is defined by

\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\coloneqq\left[\int_{\mathcal{X}}h(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\phi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{i,j=1,\ldots,m}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times m}.

We denote by
\mathfrak{M}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
(resp.
\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
) the set of all
m\times m
(resp. PSD) symmetric matrix-valued measures on
\mathcal{X}
.

Definition 7
.

A
finitely atomic PSD
matrix-valued measure
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
is a matrix-valued measure of form
\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_{i}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}

where
W_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
,
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
’s are distinct points in
\mathcal{X}
, and
\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}
denotes the Dirac measure centered at
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
,
i=1,\ldots,r
.

Definition 8
.

A linear functional
\mathscr{L}\colon{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called a tracial
\mathcal{X}
-moment functional if there exists a matrix-valued measure
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}_{+}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
such that

\mbox{supp}(\Phi)\subseteq\mathcal{X},\ \mathscr{L}(F)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ \mbox{tr}\left(F(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right)=\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ F_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\phi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}),\ \ \forall F(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}.

(6)

The matrix-valued measure
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}_{+}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
is called a
representing measure
of
\mathscr{L}
and we write
\mathscr{L}_{\Phi}
for
\mathscr{L}
to indicate the associated measure.

Now we define the convex cones

\mathcal{L}^{m}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\{\mathscr{L}\colon{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}\mid\mathscr{L}\ \text{is a tracial $\mathcal{X}$-moment functional}\},

(7)

and

\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\left\{F(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\mid F(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0,\,\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\right\}.

(8)

The following theorem is a matrix version of Haviland’s theorem (
[
10
,
47
]
).

Theorem 9
(Haviland’s theorem for polynomial matrices).

[
10
, Theorem 3]

A linear functional
\mathscr{L}
is a tracial
\mathcal{X}
-moment functional
if and only if

\mathscr{L}(F)\geq 0
for all
F(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
.

Proposition 10
.

[
42
, Proposition 10.3.3]

The cones
\mathcal{L}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
and
\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
are dual to each other, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{m}(\mathcal{X})=\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})^{*}
and
\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})={\mathcal{L}^{m}(\mathcal{X})}^{*}
.

2.3
The matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem

Let
\mathbf{S}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
be a multi-indexed sequence of symmetric
matrices in
{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
.

Definition 11
.

[
27
]

For a non-empty closed set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the sequence

\mathbf{S}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
is called a matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment sequence if there exists a matrix-valued measure

\Phi=[\phi_{ij}]\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
such
that

\mbox{supp}(\Phi)\subseteq\mathcal{X}\quad\text{and}\quad S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\ \forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

(9)

The measure
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
satisfying (
9
) is
called a
representing measure
of
{\mathbf{S}}
.

For a given sequence
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
, we
can define a linear functional
\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\colon{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
in the
following way:

\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}(F)\coloneqq\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(F)}\mbox{tr}\left(F_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right),\ \forall F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(F)}F_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m},

where
F_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
is the coefficient matrix of
\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
in
F(\mathbf{x})
and

\mbox{supp}(F)\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\mid\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\text{ appears in some }F_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\}.

We call
\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
the
Riesz functional
associated to the sequence
{\mathbf{S}}
.
Clearly,
{\mathbf{S}}
is a matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment sequence if and only if
\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
is a tracial

\mathcal{X}
-moment functional.

Definition 12
.

Given a sequence
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
,
the associated
moment matrix

M({\mathbf{S}})
is the block matrix whose block row and block column are indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{n}
and the
({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
-th block entry is
S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. For
G\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{q}
,
the
localizing matrix

M(G{\mathbf{S}})
associated to
{\mathbf{S}}
and
G
is the block matrix whose block row and block column are indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
the
({\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}})
-th block entry is

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mbox{supp}(G)}S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\otimes G_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, the
d
-th order moment matrix
M_{d}({\mathbf{S}})
(resp. localizing matrix

M_{d}(G{\mathbf{S}})
) is the submatrix of
M({\mathbf{S}})
(resp.
M(G{\mathbf{S}})
) whose block row and block column are both indexed by
\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}
.

The following proposition can be easily verified from the definitions.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
and
\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{mq}
be SOS matrices such that

\Sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})=(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{m})^{\intercal}Z_{0}(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{m})\text{ and }\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{x})=(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{mq})^{\intercal}Z_{1}(u_{d}(\mathbf{x})\otimes I_{mq}),

with
Z_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}|}
and
Z_{1}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{mq|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{d}|}
. Then for a sequence
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
, it holds that

\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}(\Sigma_{0})=\mbox{tr}\left(Z_{0}M_{d}({\mathbf{S}})\right)\text{ and }\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}((\Sigma_{1},G)_{m})=\mbox{tr}\left(Z_{1}M_{d}(G{\mathbf{S}})\right).

Let
d_{G}\coloneqq\lceil\deg(G)/2\rceil
. For each integer
k\geq d_{G}
, we define the set

\mathcal{M}_{k}^{m}(G)\coloneqq\{{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}\mid M_{k}({\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0,\ M_{k-d_{G}}(G{\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0\},

and let
\mathcal{M}^{m}(G)\coloneqq\bigcap_{k\geq d_{G}}\mathcal{M}_{k}^{m}(G)
,
which are all convex cones. Checking membership in
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{m}(G)
can be accomplished with an SDP.
Moreover, by Proposition
13
,
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{m}(G)
is the dual cone of
\mathcal{Q}^{m}_{k}(G)
.

For a given
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
, the matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem
asks when there exists a matrix-valued measure
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
satisfying
(
9
). A necessary condition can be derived from Proposition
13
.

Corollary 14
.

If
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
has a matrix-valued representing measure

\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
, then
{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{M}^{m}(G).

Proof.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, PSD matrices
Z_{0}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{k}|}
, and
Z_{1}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{mq|\mathbb{N}^{n}_{k}|}
,
let
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{1}
be the SOS polynomial matrices defined in Proposition
13
.
Then, for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have
\Sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0
and
(\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{x}),G(\mathbf{x}))_{m}\succeq 0

(see
[
46
]
). Hence,

\mbox{tr}\left(Z_{0}M_{k}({\mathbf{S}})\right)=\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}(\Sigma_{0})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ \mbox{tr}\left(\Sigma_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right)\geq 0

and

\mbox{tr}\left(Z_{1}M_{k}(G{\mathbf{S}})\right)=\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}((\Sigma_{1},G)_{m})=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\ \mbox{tr}\left((\Sigma_{1}(\mathbf{x}),G(\mathbf{x}))_{m}\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right)\geq 0.

As
Z_{0}
and
Z_{1}
are arbitrary, we have
M_{k}({\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0
and
M_{k}(G{\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0
.

Moreover, the matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem is addressed in the following theorem.

Theorem 15
.

[
10
, Theorems 5 and 6]

Let Assumption
2
hold. Given a sequence

{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
,
{\mathbf{S}}
is a matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment sequence if and only if
{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{M}^{m}(G)
.

Remark 16
.

If
m=1
, we use the notation
\mathcal{Q}(G)
(resp.,
\mathcal{Q}_{k}(G)
,
\mathcal{M}(G)
,
\mathcal{M}_{k}(G)
) instead of
\mathcal{Q}^{1}(G)
(resp.,
\mathcal{Q}_{k}^{1}(G)
,
\mathcal{M}^{1}(G)
,
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{1}(G)
) for simplicity.
For a set of polynomials
H(\mathbf{x})=\{h_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,h_{s}(\mathbf{x})\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]
, by slightly abusing notation, we use
\mathcal{Q}^{m}(H)
,
\mathcal{Q}^{m}_{k}(H)
,
\mathcal{M}^{m}(H)
,
\mathcal{M}_{k}^{m}(H)
to denote the related sets associated with the diagonal matrix
\diag(h_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,h_{s}(\mathbf{x}))
.
Then, when
m=1
, Theorems
5
and
15
recover Putinar’s Positivstellensätz
[
45
]
and its dual aspect for the basic semi-algebraic set
\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid h_{1}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\ldots,h_{s}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}
.

3
The FEC and matrix-valued measure recovery

3.1
The truncated matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem

Recently, Kimsey and Trachana
[
28
]
obtained a flat extension theorem
which provides a solution to the truncated matrix-valued moment problem.

Theorem 17
.

[
28
, Theorem 6.2]
(flat extension)

For a truncated sequence
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
,
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathbf{S}}
admits an atomic representing measure
\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_{i}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}

with
W_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
,
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{rank}(W_{i})=\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))
;

(ii)

M_{k}({\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0
and
{\mathbf{S}}
admits an extension
\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}}=(\tilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k+2}}

such that
M_{k+1}(\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}})\succeq 0
and
\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))=\mbox{rank}(M_{k+1}(\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}}))
.

When
m=1
, Theorem
17
recovers the celebrated flat extension theorem
of Curto and Fialkow
[
12
]
.
There is also a version of the result by Curto and Fialkow
[
13
]
, which
characterizes a truncated real sequence having a representing measure supported on
a prescribed semi-algebraic subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
We next extend Theorem
17
to matrix-valued measures supported on
\mathcal{X}
, which provides a solution to the truncated matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem.

Theorem 18
.

Given a truncated sequence
{\mathbf{S}}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}\subseteq{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
,
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

{\mathbf{S}}
admits an atomic representing measure
\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_{i}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}

with
W_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
,
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{rank}(W_{i})=\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))
;

(ii)

M_{k}({\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0
and
{\mathbf{S}}
admits an extension
\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}}=(\tilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2(k+d_{G})}}

such that
M_{k+d_{G}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}})\succeq 0
,
M_{k}(G\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}})\succeq 0
and
\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))=\mbox{rank}(M_{k+d_{G}}(\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}}))
.

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii). It is implied by Corollary
14
and Theorem
17
.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i). By Theorem
17
,
{\mathbf{S}}
admits an atomic representing
measure
\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_{i}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}
with

W_{i}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
,
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{rank}(W_{i})=\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))
.
We need to prove
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{X}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
.
By Theorem
17
, we can extend
\tilde{{\mathbf{S}}}
to an infinite sequence

\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}=(\hat{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
such that

M(\hat{{\mathbf{S}}})\succeq 0
and
\mbox{rank}(M(\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}))=\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))
. For simplicity, in the following we will still use the symbol
{\mathbf{S}}
to denote
\hat{{\mathbf{S}}}
.

For a column vector of polynomials
H(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
, we write

H(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mbox{supp}(H)}H_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
with
H_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
We define a subspace
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
of
{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
associated with
{\mathbf{S}}
by

\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\coloneqq\left\{H(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\ \middle|\ \sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mbox{supp}(H)}S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}H_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=0,\ \forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\right\}.

For any
H(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
, we have
H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})^{\intercal}W_{i}H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})=0

for all
i=1,\ldots,r
. In fact, as
H(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
, it holds that

0=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(H)}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mbox{supp}(H)}H_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{\intercal}S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}H_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})^{\intercal}W_{i}H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}).

As
W_{i}
’s are PSD,
it implies that

H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})^{\intercal}W_{i}H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})=0\quad\text{and}\quad W_{i}H(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})=0.

(10)

Consider the quotient space
{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}/\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\coloneqq\{H+\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\mid H\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\}
over
{\mathbb{R}}

consisting of equivalence classes modulo
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
. Let
t\coloneqq\mbox{rank}(M({\mathbf{S}}))=\mbox{rank}(M_{k}({\mathbf{S}}))
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{k}
(not necessarily distinct) and standard basis (column) vectors
\mathbf{e}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{e}^{(t)}
(not necessarily distinct) of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be such that

\left\{\text{col}\left((S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\right)\mathbf{e}^{(1)},\ldots,\text{col}\left((S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\right)\mathbf{e}^{(t)}\right\}

(11)

is a set of
t
linearly independent column vectors of
M({\mathbf{S}})
and hence forms a basis of the column space of
M({\mathbf{S}})
.
Here,
\text{col}\left((S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\right)
denotes the block-column vector with
infinite
m\times m
block entries
(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(i)}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
. We claim that the set

\left\{\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)}}\mathbf{e}^{(1)}+\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}},\ \ldots,\ \mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}}\mathbf{e}^{(t)}+\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}\right\}

(12)

forms a basis of
{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}/\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
. To see this, first note that the elements in (
12
) are linearly independent as the elements in (
11
) are linearly independent. Then, it is sufficient to prove that for arbitrary
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
with
1\leq j\leq t
, the element
\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\mathbf{e}^{(j)}+\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
can be written as a linear combination of elements in (
12
). This is indeed true since the column vector
\text{col}\left((S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}}\right)\mathbf{e}^{(j)}
can be written as a linear combination of elements in (
11
). For any
H(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
, we write
H(\mathbf{x})=H^{(0)}(\mathbf{x})+H^{(1)}(\mathbf{x})
where
H^{(0)}
is the residue of
H
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
w.r.t. the basis (
12
) and
H^{(1)}\in\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
. Since
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(1)},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{k}
, we see that
\deg(H^{(0)})\leq k
.

Let
\{p^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})\}_{i=1}^{r}
be the Lagrange interpolation polynomials at the points
\{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{r}
such that
p^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})=1
and
p^{(i)}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})=0
for all
j\neq i
. Now we fix an
i
and prove
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}\in\mathcal{X}
. As
W_{i}\succeq 0
, there exists a vector
\mathbf{v}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that
(\mathbf{v}^{(i)})^{\intercal}W_{i}\mathbf{v}^{(i)}>0
. Let
H_{i}(\mathbf{x})=p^{(i)}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}^{(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
.
Let us write
H_{i}=H^{(0)}_{i}+H_{i}^{(1)}
and
H^{(0)}_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(H^{(0)}_{i})}H^{(0)}_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
with
\mbox{supp}(H^{(0)}_{i})\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}_{k}
. As
M_{k}(G{\mathbf{S}})\succeq 0
, we have

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(H_{i}^{(0)})}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mbox{supp}(H_{i}^{(0)})}\left((H_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{(0)})^{\intercal}\otimes I_{q}\right)[M_{k}(G{\mathbf{S}})]_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\left(H^{(0)}_{i,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\otimes I_{q}\right)\succeq 0.

By the definition of
M_{k}(G{\mathbf{S}})
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(H_{i}^{(0)})}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mbox{supp}(H_{i}^{(0)})}\left((H_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{(0)})^{\intercal}\otimes I_{q}\right)[M_{k}(G{\mathbf{S}})]_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\left(H^{(0)}_{i,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\otimes I_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(H_{i}^{(0)})}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mbox{supp}(H_{i}^{(0)})}\left((H_{i,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}^{(0)})^{\intercal}\otimes I_{q}\right)\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mbox{supp}(G)}S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+{\boldsymbol{\beta}}+{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\otimes G_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\right)\left(H^{(0)}_{i,{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\otimes I_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{r}\left(\left(H_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})-H_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})\right)^{\intercal}W_{j}\left(H_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})-H_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})\right)\right)G(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{v}^{(i)}-H_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\right)^{\intercal}W_{i}\left(\mathbf{v}^{(i)}-H_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\right)G(\mathbf{x}^{(i)})+\sum_{j\neq i}\left(H_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})\right)^{\intercal}W_{j}H_{i}^{(1)}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})G(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})

\displaystyle\left((\mathbf{v}^{(i)})^{\intercal}W_{i}{\mathbf{v}^{(i)}}\right)G(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}),

Remark 19

Remark 22
.

It is clear that if

\mbox{rank}([u_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(1)}),\ldots,u_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{(r)})])=r,

then

must have

by considering the Lagrange interpolation polynomials at

we know that (

and we may need to consider flat extensions of

to recover the weights

Inspired by

we propose the following heuristic method for finding such a flat extension. Consider the linear matrix-valued moment optimization problem:

\min_{\widetilde{{\mathbf{S}}}}\ \sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2d}}\mbox{tr}\left(R_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right)\ \text{ s.t. }\ \widetilde{{\mathbf{S}}}=(\widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2d}}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}^{m}(G),\ \widetilde{S}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}},\ \forall{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k},

for some

and

. By randomly choosing the matrices

, one may expect that the optimum of the above problem is achieved at an extreme point of the cone
\mathcal{M}_{d}^{m}(G)
which might admit a finitely atomic matrix-valued representing measure and hence provide a flat extension of

. We leave a detailed study on this issue in the future.

To conclude this section, we would like to remark that the results on the truncated matrix-valued
\mathcal{X}
-moment problem and the procedure for matrix-valued measure recovery are not direct generalizations of the scalar case. First, the matrix-valued moment problem cannot be reduced to the scalar case via certain scalarizing procedures as far as we know. For instance, it is not valid to solve the matrix-valued moment problem by separately considering the sequence of the entries at the same position in the matrices
\{S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}
. This is because the off-diagonal entries of a PSD matrix-valued measure are not necessarily positive scalar measures, making the theory of the scalar moment problem inapplicable here. One may also consider the scalar sequence
\{\mbox{tr}\left(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right)\}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}
which, however, contains only partial information on the matrix-valued moments. It is thus impossible to recover the matrix-valued measure in this way.
Second, extending the theory of the scalar moment problem to the matrix-valued case
requires carefully addressing additional complexities and intrinsic challenges present in the matrix-valued setting.
In particular, the proof of Theorem
18
is conducted in the quotient space
{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}/\mathcal{I}_{{\mathbf{S}}}
due to the matrix-valued nature, which involves
a subtle construction of the polynomial vectors
H_{i}
. In addition, the linear algebra procedure presented in Section
3.2
is the first efficient approach for extracting matrix-valued measures from the matrix-valued moment matrix satisfying FEC.
In comparison with the scalar counterpart in
[
20
]
, this procedure demands several delicate techniques tailored to the matrix-valued setting to handle the variables introduced by both the points
\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
and matrices
W_{i}
, making the justification of its correctness much more involved.

4
A moment-SOS hierarchy for (
RPMIO
) with SOS-convexity

In this section, we first reformulate (
RPMIO
) as a conic optimization problem, based on which we can then derive a moment-SOS hierarchy whose optima monotonically converge to the optimum of (
RPMIO
). Furthermore, the results in Section
3
enable us to detect finite convergence of the moment-SOS hierarchy and to extract optimal solutions.

4.1
A conic reformulation

For simplicity, we write

P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}P_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}},

where
P_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
(resp.

\mathbf{x}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
(resp.
\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}
) with
P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
being regarded as a polynomial matrix in
{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
(resp.
{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
).
For a linear functional
\mathscr{L}\colon{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we let

\mathscr{L}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\coloneqq\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}\mathscr{L}(P_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\mathbf{x}))\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}],

and for a linear functional
\mathscr{H}\colon{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we let

\mathscr{H}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\coloneqq\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}P_{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}}(\mathbf{x})\mathscr{H}(\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\beta}}})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}.

To obtain a conic reformulation of (
RPMIO

Assumption 3

Proposition 23

Proof.

For any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{V}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}^{2}=1\}
and
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, by Assumption
1
(ii), we have that the function
-\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}P(\,\cdot\,,\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}
is convex in
\mathbf{y}
. Then, (
RPMIO
) can be equivalently reformulated as the following convex semi-infinite program under Assumption
1
:

f^{\star}=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\ f(\mathbf{y})\quad\text{s.t.}\ \mathbf{v}^{\intercal}P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}\geq 0,\ \forall(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbf{V}.

(18)

Let
(\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\mathbf{v}^{(0)}),(\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\mathbf{v}^{(1)}),\ldots,(\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)},\mathbf{v}^{(\ell)})
be
\ell+1

arbitrary points in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbf{V}
. By Assumption
3
, there exists
\bar{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that

P(\bar{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\succ 0
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,\ell
. Hence, it holds

(\mathbf{v}^{(i)})^{\intercal}P(\bar{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\mathbf{v}^{(i)}>0
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,\ell
. As

\mathcal{X}\times\mathbf{V}
is compact in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
(Assumption
1
(iii)),
recall by
[
7
, Theorem 4.1]
that if for any arbitrary
\ell+1

points in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbf{V}
, the corresponding constraints in (
18
) are strictly satisfied
by some point from
\mathcal{Y}
, then (
18
) is reducible. In other words, there
exist
\ell
points
(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(1)},\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(1)}),\ldots,(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(\ell)},\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(\ell)})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbf{V}
such that

f^{\star}=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\ f(\mathbf{y})\quad\text{s.t.}\ (\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)})^{\intercal}P(\mathbf{y},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)})\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)}\geq 0,\ i=1,\ldots,\ell.

Then, by the Lagrange multiplier theorem,
we can find

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{\ell}>0
such that

f^{\star}=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\ f(\mathbf{y})-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)})^{\intercal}P(\mathbf{y},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)})\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)}.

Define a finitely atomic matrix-valued measure

\Phi^{\star}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_{i}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)}(\tilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(i)})^{\intercal}\delta_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{(i)}}\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X}).

Then, it holds that

f^{\star}=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\ f(\mathbf{y})-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})).

If
\mathbf{y}^{\star}
is an optimal solution to (
RPMIO
), then

P(\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{x})\succeq 0
on
\mathcal{X}
and hence

f^{\star}\leq f(\mathbf{y}^{\star})-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{x}))\leq f(\mathbf{y}^{\star})=f^{\star},

which implies
\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{x}))=0
.

Remark 24
.

From the above proof, one can see that Proposition
23
remains true if the Slater condition is weakened as: For any
\ell+1
points
\mathbf{x}^{(0)},\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(\ell)}\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists
\bar{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathcal{Y}
such that
P(\bar{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\succ 0
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,\ell
.

For any measure
\mu\in\mathfrak{m}_{+}(\mathcal{Y})
, we define an associated linear functional
\mathscr{H}_{\mu}\colon{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
\mathscr{H}_{\mu}(h)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}h(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\mu(\mathbf{y})
for all
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]
. Let us consider the following conic optimization problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{f}\coloneqq\sup_{\rho,\Phi}&\ \rho\\
\text{s.t.}&\ f(\mathbf{y})-\rho-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),\\
&\ \rho\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X}),\end{aligned}\right.

(19)

whose dual reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \hat{f}\coloneqq\inf_{\mu}\,&\ \mathscr{H}_{\mu}(f)\\
\text{s.t.}&\ \mu\in\mathfrak{m}_{+}(\mathcal{Y}),\ \mathscr{H}_{\mu}(1)=1,\\
&\ \mathscr{H}_{\mu}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\in\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X}),\end{aligned}\right.

(20)

with
\mathcal{P}^{m}(\mathcal{X})
being defined in (
8
).

Theorem 25
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
3
, it holds
\tilde{f}=\hat{f}=f^{\star}
.

Proof.

Let
\Phi^{\star}\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
be the finitely atomic matrix-valued measure given in
Proposition
23
. Then
(f^{\star},\Phi^{\star})
is feasible to (
19
) due to Proposition
23
. Thus,
\tilde{f}\geq f^{\star}
.
By the weak duality, we have
\hat{f}\geq\tilde{f}
. It remains to show
\hat{f}\leq f^{\star}
.

Let
(\mathbf{y}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a minimizing sequence of (
RPMIO
).
Then, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
k_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{y}^{(k_{\varepsilon})}
is feasible to (
RPMIO
)
and
f(\mathbf{y}^{(k_{\varepsilon})})\leq f^{\star}+\varepsilon
.
The Dirac measure
\delta_{\mathbf{y}^{(k_{\varepsilon})}}
centered at
\mathbf{y}^{(k_{\varepsilon})}
is feasible to (
20
).
Therefore,
\hat{f}\leq\mathscr{H}_{\delta_{\mathbf{y}^{(k_{\varepsilon})}}}(f)=f(\mathbf{y}^{(k_{\varepsilon})})\leq f^{\star}+\varepsilon.

As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we have
\hat{f}\leq f^{\star}
as desired.

Remark 26
.

It is easy to verify that the conic reformulation (

)–(

) for
(
RPMIO
) and the results in Proposition

and
Theorem

remain true
if “SOS-convex” in Assumption

(i)
is weakened to “convex” and “PSD-SOS-convex” in Assumption

(i.e., if Assumption

in Section

).

Remark 27
.

Before we proceed, let us highlight the significance of the conic reformulation (
19
)–(
20
)
and explain how it could provide insights in solving (
RPMIO
).
Essentially, through the reformulation (
19
)–(
20
), the complexity of
(
RPMIO
) is transferred to the conic constraints in (
19
) and (
20
),
which involve the cone
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
of nonnegative polynomials on the set
\mathcal{Y}
,
the cone
\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
of PSD matrix-valued measures supported on
\mathcal{X}
, and their dual cones.
While the exact representations of these cones are unavailable in general case,
there are various (matrix-valued) Positivstellensätze and the dual (matrix-valued) moment theories which can provide tractable approximations for these cones. Hence, by replacing these cones with suitable approximations,
the reformulation (
19
)–(
20
) offers us
a unified framework to derive tractable relaxations for (
RPMIO
).

4.2
A moment-SOS hierarchy

Let
\Theta\coloneqq\{\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{s}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]
collect the description polynomials of the semialgebraic set
\mathcal{Y}
.
Moreover, let

\displaystyle k_{\mathbf{y}}\coloneqq\max\,\{\deg(f),\ \deg(\theta_{1}),\ldots,\deg(\theta_{s}),\ \deg_{\mathbf{y}}(P_{ij}),\ i,j=1,\ldots,m\},

\displaystyle k_{\mathbf{x}}\coloneqq\max\,\{\deg_{\mathbf{x}}(P_{ij}),\ i,j=1,\ldots,m,\ \deg(G)\}.

For each
k\geq\lceil k_{\mathbf{x}}/2\rceil
, by replacing the cones
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and
\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
in (
19
) with the more tractable cones
\mathcal{Q}_{\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}(\Theta)
and
\mathcal{M}^{m}_{k}(G)
respectively, we obtain the following SDP relaxation for (
RPMIO
):

\left\{\begin{aligned} f^{\mbox{\tiny primal}}_{k}\coloneqq\sup_{\rho,{\mathbf{S}}}&\ \rho\\
\text{s.t.}&\ f(\mathbf{y})-\rho-\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\in\mathcal{Q}_{\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}(\Theta),\\
&\ \rho\in{\mathbb{R}},\ {\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{M}^{m}_{k}(G).\end{aligned}\right.

(21)

The dual of (
21
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} f^{\mbox{\tiny dual}}_{k}\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{s}}\,&\ \mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(f)\\
\text{s.t.}&\ \mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{M}_{\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}(\Theta),\ \mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(1)=1,\\
&\ \mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\in\mathcal{Q}^{m}_{k}(G),\end{aligned}\right.

(22)

where the linear functional
\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]_{2\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is defined by
\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(h)=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(h)}h_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}s_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
for
h\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]_{2\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}
.
We call (
21
)–(
22
) the
moment-SOS hierarchy
for (
RPMIO
) with SOS-convexity and call
k
the
relaxation order
.

4.2.1
Convergence analysis

Next we present the convergence analysis of the moment-SOS hierarchy (
21
)–(
22
).

Proposition 28
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
3
hold.
Then the sequences
(f_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny primal}})_{k\geq\lceil k_{\mathbf{x}}/2\rceil}
and
(f_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny dual}})_{k\geq\lceil k_{\mathbf{x}}/2\rceil}
are monotonically non-increasing and provide upper bounds on
f^{\star}
.

Proof.

By the weak duality
f_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny primal}}\leq f_{k}^{\mbox{\tiny dual}}
, we only need to prove that there exists a finitely atomic matrix-valued measure

\Phi^{\star}\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
such that

f(\mathbf{y})-f^{\star}-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\in\mathcal{Q}_{\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}(\Theta).

(23)

As Assumptions
1
and
3
hold, by Proposition
23
,
there exists a finitely atomic matrix-valued measure
\Phi^{\star}\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})
such that

f^{\star}=\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}\ f(\mathbf{y})-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\quad\text{s.t. }\theta_{1}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0,\ldots,\theta_{s}(\mathbf{y})\geq 0.

(24)

Suppose that
\Phi^{\star}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}W_{i}\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{(i)}}\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})

for some
\mathbf{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{x}^{(r)}\in\mathcal{X}
and

W_{1},\ldots,W_{r}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{+}^{m}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,r
, let

W_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}\mathbf{v}^{(i,k)}(\mathbf{v}^{(i,k)})^{\intercal}
for some

\mathbf{v}^{(i,k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Then,

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{y})-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))=f(\mathbf{y})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mbox{tr}\left(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})W_{i}\right)=f(\mathbf{y})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{k=1}^{m_{i}}(\mathbf{v}^{(i,k)})^{\intercal}P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}^{(i)})\mathbf{v}^{(i,k)}.

By Assumption
1
and the definition of PSD-SOS-convexity,

f(\mathbf{y})-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))
is SOS-convex.
By Proposition
23
, each optimal solution to (
RPMIO
) is
also optimal to (
24
).
Since the functions
f(\mathbf{y})-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))
,
-\theta_{i}(\mathbf{y})
’s
in (
24
) are all SOS-convex and the set of optimal solutions of (
24
)
is nonempty,
[
33
, Theorem 3.3]

states that there exists a convex SOS polynomial
\sigma(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{y}]
and scalars

\lambda_{1}\geq 0,\ldots,\lambda_{s}\geq 0
, such that

f(\mathbf{y})-f^{\star}-\mathscr{L}_{\Phi^{\star}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))=\sigma(\mathbf{y})+\sum_{j=1}^{s}\lambda_{j}\theta_{j}(\mathbf{y}).

Clearly, we have
\deg(\sigma)\leq 2\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil
which implies that
(
23
) holds true and the conclusion follows.

We next prove the
asymptotic convergence of this moment-SOS hierarchy.
We write
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{\ell})
for the standard basis of
{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
and let
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}}=(s_{\mathbf{e}_{1}},\ldots,s_{\mathbf{e}_{\ell}})
for any feasible
point
\mathbf{s}=(s_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}_{2\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}}
of (
22
).

Proposition 29
.

Suppose that
Q(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
is PSD-SOS-convex.
Let
\mathbf{s}=(s_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}_{2\lceil\deg(Q)/2\rceil}}

satisfy
s_{\mathbf{0}}=1
and
M_{\lceil\deg(Q)/2\rceil}(\mathbf{s})\succeq 0
. Then

\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(Q)\succeq Q(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}}).

Proof.

As
\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v}
is SOS-convex in
\mathbf{y}
for all
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, by

[
33
, Theorem 2.6]
, it holds

\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(Q(\mathbf{y}))\mathbf{v}=\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v})\geq\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}})\mathbf{v},

for all
\mathbf{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Hence we have
\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(Q)\succeq Q(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}}).

Corollary 30
.

Suppose that
-\theta_{1}(\mathbf{y}),\ldots,-\theta_{s}(\mathbf{y})
are SOS-convex and

-P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
is PSD-SOS-convex in
\mathbf{y}
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. If
\mathbf{s}
is feasible to (
22
), then
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{Y}
and it is feasible to (
RPMIO
).

Proof.

By the extended Jensen’s inequality for SOS-convex polynomials

[
33
, Theorem 2.6]
, it holds

\theta_{i}(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}})\geq\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(\theta_{i})\geq 0

for
i=1,\ldots,s
, which implies
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathcal{Y}
.
By Proposition
29
, for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

P(\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}},\mathbf{x})\succeq\mathscr{H}_{\mathbf{s}}(P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}))\succeq 0.

So
\mathbf{s}_{\mathbf{e}}
is feasible to (
RPMIO
).

Theorem 31
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, the following are true:

(i)

f^{\mbox{\tiny primal}}_{k}\searrow f^{\star}

and

(ii)

For any convergent subsequence

RPMIO
).

Proof.

(i). Let
\mathbf{y}^{\star}
be a minimizer of (
RPMIO
)
and
\bar{\mathbf{y}}
be the Slater point given in Assumption
3
.
Because

\mathcal{Y}
are convex by Assumption

for an arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
.
As
-P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
is PSD-SOS-convex in
\mathbf{y}
for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds

P(\mathbf{y}^{\prime},\mathbf{x})\succeq tP(\mathbf{y}^{\star},\mathbf{x})+(1-t)P(\bar{\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{x})\succ 0,

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
.
Let
\mathbf{s}^{\prime}=(s^{\prime}_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}_{{2\lceil k_{\mathbf{y}}/2\rceil}}}
with

. By Assumption

there exists

(ii).
Let

Remark 32

(i)

{\mathbf{S}}^{(k,\star)}

Suppose that Assumption

Proof.

Suppose on the contrary that the conclusion is false.

\mathcal{X}

{\mathbf{S}}^{\circ}=(S_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}
where each

\displaystyle 0

4.3.1
Robust polynomial semidefinite programming

Consider the robust polynomial semidefinite programming problem which is a special case of (
RPMIO

\displaystyle\tau^{\star}\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}}

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\ \sum_{i=1}^{\ell}P_{i}(\mathbf{x})y_{i}-P_{0}(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0,\ \forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

(RPSDP)

where
\mathbf{c}=[c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell}]^{\intercal}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}
and

,
i=0,1,\ldots,\ell

Applying the conic reformulation (
19
)
to (

) with
\mathcal{Y}={\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sup_{\rho,\Phi}&\ \rho\\
\text{s.t.}&\ \mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}-\rho-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathscr{L}_{\Phi}(P_{i}(\mathbf{x}))y_{i}+\mathscr{L}_{\Phi}(P_{0}(\mathbf{x}))\in\mathcal{P}({\mathbb{R}}^{\ell}),\\
&\ \rho\in{\mathbb{R}},\ \Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X}).\end{aligned}\right.

(28)

For any
\rho\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})

\mathbf{c}^{\intercal}\mathbf{y}-\rho-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathscr{L}_{\Phi}(P_{i}(\mathbf{x}))y_{i}+\mathscr{L}_{\Phi}(P_{0}(\mathbf{x}))=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(c_{i}-\int_{\mathcal{X}}P_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\right)y_{i}-\rho+\int_{\mathcal{X}}P_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x}).

Thus, if
(\rho,\Phi)
is feasible to (
28

c_{i}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}P_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x}),\ i=1,\ldots,\ell,\quad\text{and}\quad\rho\leq\int_{\mathcal{X}}P_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x}),

and (
28
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\sup_{\Phi\in\mathfrak{M}^{m}_{+}(\mathcal{X})}

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}P_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\ \int_{\mathcal{X}}P_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\Phi(\mathbf{x})=c_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(29)

Remark 38

Note that the generalized moment problem extensively studied by Lasserre in
[

4.3.2
An application: minimizing the smallest eigenvalue of a polynomial matrix

Consider the problem of minimizing the smallest eigenvalue of a polynomial matrix:

\lambda^{\star}\coloneqq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\,\lambda_{\min\,}(F(\mathbf{x}))\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid G(\mathbf{x})\succeq 0\},

(33)

where
F(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{m}
,
\lambda_{\min\,}(F(\mathbf{x}))
denotes the
smallest eigenvalue of
F(\mathbf{x})
and
G(\mathbf{x})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{x}]^{q}
.
The motivations for studying this problem come from many different fields.
For example, in the global optimization method
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
BB for general
constrained nonconvex problems, a convex relaxation of the original nonconvex problem
is constructed. To underestimate nonconvex terms of generic structure in the involved nonconvex functions, one needs to compute a parameter
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
which amounts to minimizing the
smallest eigenvalue of the corresponding Hessian matrix over a product of intervals. If the
involved functions are polynomials, then the problem can be formulated as (
33
)

[
4
,
39
]
. For another example from optimal control, the stabilisability
radius of a continuous-time system described by a state-space equation is defined as
the norm of the smallest perturbation that makes the system unstabilisable.
To compute such a radius, one needs to minimize the smallest eigenvalue of a bivariate
polynomial matrix over a half disc on a plane, which is a special case of the
problem (
33
)
[
16
]
.

Clearly, the problem (
33
) is equivalent to

\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}\,\lambda\quad\text{s.t.}\quad F(\mathbf{x})-\lambda I_{m}\succeq 0,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(34)

which is a special case of (
RPSDP
).

For each
k\geq\max\,\{\lceil\deg(F)/2\rceil,\lceil\deg(G)/2\rceil\}
, the
k
-th order primal moment relaxation of (
33
) reads as

\lambda^{\mbox{\tiny primal}}_{k}\coloneqq\inf_{{\mathbf{S}}\in\mathcal{M}^{m}_{k}(G)}\,\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}(F(\mathbf{x}))\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\mathscr{L}_{{\mathbf{S}}}(I_{m})=1,

(35)

with dual

\lambda^{\mbox{\tiny dual}}_{k}\coloneqq\sup_{\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}}\,\lambda\quad\text{s.t.}\quad F(\mathbf{x})-\lambda I_{m}\in\mathcal{Q}^{m}_{k}(G).

(36)

The dual problem (
36
) was studied in
[
48
]
where
\mathcal{X}
is set to the
n
-dimensional boolean hypercube
\{0,1\}^{n}
.

Clearly, the Slater condition holds for (
34
) whenever
\mathcal{X}
is compact.
Then, from Theorems
39
–
40
,
we deduce the following theorems.

Theorem 42
.

Let Assumption
2
hold.
Then
\lambda^{\mbox{\tiny primal}}_{k}\nearrow\lambda^{\star}
and
\lambda^{\mbox{\tiny dual}}_{k}\nearrow\lambda^{\star}

6
Conclusions

We have proposed a moment-SOS hierarchy for the robust PMI optimization problems with SOS-convexity
for which asymptotic convergence is established and the FEC is used to detect global optimality.
Extensions to the general convexity and non-convexity settings are also provided.
As this work generalizes most of the nice features of the moment-SOS hierarchy from the scalar polynomial
optimization to the robust PMI optimization, we would expect to stimulate more applications of
robust PMI optimization in different fields (e.g., robust optimization, control theory).
For the scalar polynomial optimization, various algebraic structures
(e.g., symmetry, sparsity
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
)
can be exploited to derive a structured moment-SOS hierarchy with lower computational complexity.
A recent work on exploiting sparsity for PMIs could be found in
[
53
]
.
As a line of further research, we intend to extend such techniques to the robust PMI optimization in future work.

Appendix A
Uniform PSD-SOS-convexity

Definition 53
.

[
40
]

A polynomial matrix
Q(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
is
uniformly

PSD-SOS-convex if there exists a polynomial matrix

F(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})
in
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})
such that

\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v})=F(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})^{\intercal}F(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y}).

(44)

Clearly, if
Q(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
is uniformly PSD-SOS-convex, then it is PSD-SOS-convex. Moreover, checking the existence of
F(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})
in (
44
) can be converted into an SDP feasibility problem.
For
Q(\mathbf{y})\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
, we write
Q(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
, where
Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
is the coefficient matrix of
\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}
in
Q(\mathbf{y})
and

\mbox{supp}(Q)\coloneqq\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}\mid\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\text{ appears in some }Q_{ij}(\mathbf{y})\}.

Then we have the following proposition.

Proposition 54
.

A polynomial matrix

Q(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m}
is uniformly PSD-SOS-convex if

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\otimes\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}

is an SOS matrix.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{v}^{\intercal}Q(\mathbf{y})\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}[Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}]_{ij}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right)v_{i}v_{j}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{v}\otimes I_{\ell})^{\intercal}\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\otimes\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right)(\mathbf{v}\otimes I_{\ell}).

If there exists a polynomial matrix
T(\mathbf{y})
such that

\left(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\otimes\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\right)=T(\mathbf{y})^{\intercal}T(\mathbf{y}),

we then obtain (
44
) by letting
F(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{y})=T(\mathbf{y})(\mathbf{v}\otimes I_{\ell})
.

Corollary 55
.

A quadratic polynomial matrix

Q(\mathbf{y})=C+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}L_{i}y_{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{\ell}Q_{ij}y_{i}y_{j}\in{\mathbb{S}}[\mathbf{y}]^{m},

where
C,L_{i},Q_{ij}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{m}
and

Q_{ij}=Q_{ji}
, is uniformly PSD-SOS-convex if the
m\ell\times m\ell
matrix

[Q_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}
is PSD.

Proof.

It is clear that
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\otimes Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}=2[Q_{ij}]_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell}
which implies that the matrix

\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mbox{supp}(Q)}Q_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\otimes\nabla_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{y}^{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{\ell m\times\ell m}
is PSD. Hence,
Q(\mathbf{y})
is uniformly PSD-SOS-convex by Proposition
54
.
