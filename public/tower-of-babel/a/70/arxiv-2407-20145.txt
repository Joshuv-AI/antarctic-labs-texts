A unified framework for N-phase Navier-Stokes Cahn-Hilliard Allen-Cahn mixture models with non-matching densities
M.F.P. ten Eikelder

Abstract
Over the past few decades, numerous N-phase incompressible diffuse-interface flow models with non-matching densities have been proposed. Despite aiming to describe the same physics, these models are generally distinct, and an overarching modeling framework is absent. This paper provides a unified framework for N-phase incompressible Navier-Stokes Cahn-Hilliard Allen-Cahn mixture models with a single momentum equation. The framework naturally emerges from continuum mixture theory, exhibits an energy-dissipative structure, and is invariant to the choice of fundamental variables. This opens the door to exploring connections between existing N-phase models and facilitates the computation of N-phase flow models rooted in continuum mixture theory.

The framework serves as a foundation for deriving governing equations for multiphase flows and provides a systematic approach to connect microscopic processes with macroscopic behavior.

2
Continuum mixture theory

The purpose of this section is to outline the continuum theory of mixtures for incompressible constituents, excluding thermal effects. This section aligns with
[
48
]
at several points.

The continuum theory of mixtures is grounded in three general principles introduced in the pioneering work of
[
47
]
:

1.

All properties of the mixture must be mathematical consequences of properties of the constituents.

2.

So as to describe the motion of a constituent, we may in imagination isolate it from the rest of the mixture, provided we allow properly for the actions of the other constituents upon it.

3.

The motion of the mixture is governed by the same equations as is a single body.

The first principle communicates that the mixture is made up of its constituent parts. The second principle asserts the connection of the different components of the physical model through interaction terms. Lastly, the latter principle states that one can not distinguish the motion of a mixture from that of a single fluid.

In

2.1
we introduce the fundamentals of the continuum theory of mixtures and the necessary kinematics. Then, in
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
, we provide balance laws of individual constituents and associated mixtures.

Remark 3.1

We finalize this section with a remark on the connection between the chemical potentials and the Lagrange multiplier.
Recalling the saturation constraint (

\displaystyle\Psi=

3.3
Alternative free energy classes

As mentioned in
Remark

3.4
, as an alternative for (

[

\displaystyle\Psi=

\displaystyle~\check{\Psi}\left(\left\{c_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla c_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(3.25)

subject to the summation constraint (
2.5a
). Alongside with free energy class (
3.25
) we introduce the chemical potential quantities (
\alpha=1,...,N
):

\displaystyle\check{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla c_{\alpha}}.

(3.26)

In
Appendix

C
we provide the derivation of the modeling restriction that emerges from the constitutive class (
3.25
). The modeling restriction takes the form:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{T}+\check{\lambda}\mathbf{I}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla c_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla c_{\alpha}}-\check{\Psi}\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\left(\rho^{-1}\check{\mu}_{\alpha}+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}\check{\lambda}\right)\cdot\mathbf{H}_{\alpha}-\sum_{\alpha}\left(\rho^{-1}\check{\mu}_{\alpha}+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}\check{\lambda}\right)\zeta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.27)

where
\check{\lambda}
is the Lagrange multiplier associated with the constraint (
2.29b
). Noting that the volume fractions and concentrations are connected via (
2.7
):

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\phi_{\alpha}\left(\left\{c_{\beta}\right\}\right),

(3.28a)

\displaystyle c_{\beta}=

\displaystyle~c_{\beta}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(3.28b)

the identification

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}\right)=\check{\Psi}\left(\left\{c_{\beta}\right\}\right)

(3.29)

reveals that the free energy classes coincide. Given that the initial modeling restriction is the same for both classes we conclude that the resulting modeling restrictions must coincide as well. In other words,
the modeling restriction is independent of the choice of order parameters
.

(Equivalence of modeling restrictions)
.

The modeling restrictions (
3.21
) and (
3.27
) are equivalent.

An alternative path to show equivalence of the modeling restrictions, one could apply the variable transformation (

3.21
).
We discuss this approach in
Appendix

.

Guided by

).

3.4
Selection of constitutive models

By means of the Colemann-Noll concept, we utilize (
3.21
) as a guiding principle to design constitutive models. Inspired by the specific form of the constraint (
3.21
), we restrict ourselves to mixture stress tensors
\mathbf{T}
, constituent peculiar velocities
\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}
, and constituent mass transfer terms
\mathbf{j}_{\alpha},\zeta_{\alpha}
that belong to the constitutive classes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{T}}\left(\nabla\mathbf{v},\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{g_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla g_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(3.30a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla g_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(3.30b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\alpha}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{\nabla g_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(3.30c)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\},\left\{g_{\alpha}\right\}\right),

(3.30d)

and define
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}=\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}+\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\alpha}
. Generally speaking, the introduction of the class (
3.30b
) deviates from continuum mixture theory. Arguably, a natural approximation is simply taking
\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}=0
, which, for instance, models the situation of matching velocities
\mathbf{v}_{\alpha}=\mathbf{v}_{\beta}
. We return to this case in
Section

4.2
.

We do not seek the most complete constitutive theory, rather our goal is to find a set of practical constitutive models compatible with (
3.21
). To this end, we aim to identify constitutive models (
3.30
) so that all three terms in (
3.21
) are positive, which occurs when:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbf{T}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}+\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha,\lambda}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.31a)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla g_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{J}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.31b)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla g_{\alpha}\cdot\mathbf{j}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0,

(3.31c)

\displaystyle-\sum_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}\zeta_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(3.31d)

Remark 3.10

(Onsager reciprocal relations)
.

As mentioned above, our objective is to find a set of practical constitutive models. A more complete theory follows from working with the original constraint (
3.21
), and extending the dependency of the classes (
3.30
). In particular, the classes may be interconnected. The well-known Onsager reciprocal relations take a central place in this framework. We refer to
[
51
,
52
]
.

In the following we provide constitutive models for the mixture stress tensor, constituent peculiar velocities, and constituent mass transfer, respectively.

Mixture stress tensor
. We select the following constitutive model for the stress tensor:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{T}}=-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}-\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha,\lambda}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}+\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}),

(3.32)

subject to the symmetry condition:

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}=\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\otimes\nabla\phi_{\alpha},

(3.33)

where the scalar field
\nu\geq 0
is the mixture dynamic viscosity,
\bar{\lambda}\geq-2/d
is a scalar, and
d
is the number of dimensions. Possible choices for the mixture viscosity include
\nu=\sum_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}
and
\nu=\sum_{\alpha}\nu_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}
, where
\nu_{\alpha}
are constituent viscosities. The condition (
3.33
) ensures compatibility with the angular momentum constraint (
2.25c
).

Lemma 3.11

(Compatibility mixture stress tensor)
.

The mixture stress tensor (
3.32
) adheres to the constraint (
3.31a
).

Proof.

An elementary calculation gives:

\displaystyle\left(\hat{\mathbf{T}}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}+\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha,\lambda}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}\right):\nabla\mathbf{v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\nu\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right):\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right)+\nu\left(\bar{\lambda}+\frac{2}{d}\right)\left({\rm div}\mathbf{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

(3.34)

∎

Constituent peculiar velocities
. We choose the peculiar velocities of the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta},

(3.35)

with mobility tensor
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\mathbf{M}_{{\beta}\alpha}
. The mobility tensor is positive definite (
\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{\alpha}^{T}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{\beta}\geq 0
for all
\mbox{\boldmath{$y$}}_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\alpha=1,...,N
), has the same dependencies as (
3.31b
), is compatible with
\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{M}_{{\beta}\alpha}=0
for all
{\beta}=1,..,N
, and vanishes in the single fluid region
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}|_{\phi_{\gamma}=1}=0,{\gamma}=1,...,N
(thus is degenerate). We note that the symmetry requirement follows from the Onsager reciprocal relations, the positive definiteness from (
3.31b
), and the zero sum of rows and columns from (
3.2a
). A possible choice for the mobility tensor is
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=-\mathbf{M}_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}
for
\alpha\neq{\beta}
, and
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\alpha}=\mathbf{M}_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\sum_{{\gamma}\neq\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\gamma}
for some
\mathbf{M}_{0}
that is not dependent on the constituent number.

Remark 3.12

(Lagrange multiplier in constituent peculiar velocities)
.

In most incompressible
N
-phase models, the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
does not explicitly appear in the constituent peculiar velocities
\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}
, whereas in the proposed framework, it appears as a component of
g_{\alpha}
. Notably, when all constituent densities are identical (
\rho_{\alpha}=\rho
for
\alpha=1,...,N
), the Lagrange multiplier vanishes, yielding the relation:
g_{\alpha}-g_{\beta}=\rho^{-1}(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\beta})
.

Lemma 3.13

(Compatibility constituent peculiar velocities)
.

The choice (
3.35
) aligns with both the balance (
2.13
), and the restriction (
3.31b
).

Constituent diffusive flux
.
Analogously to the constituent peculiar velocities, we select:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta},

(3.36)

for some positive definite constitutive tensor
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\mathbf{K}_{{\beta}\alpha}
compatible with
\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sum_{\alpha}\mathbf{K}_{{\beta}\alpha}=0
, with the same dependencies as (
3.30c
), and vanishes in the single fluid region
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}|_{\phi_{\gamma}=1}=0,{\gamma}=1,...,N
. Similarly as for the peculiar velocity, a possible choice for the mobility tensor is
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}=-\mathbf{K}_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}
for
\alpha\neq{\beta}
, and
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha\alpha}=\mathbf{K}_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\sum_{{\gamma}\neq\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\gamma}
for some
\mathbf{K}_{0}
that is not dependent on the constituent number.

Lemma 3.14

(Compatibility constituent diffusive fluxes)
.

The choice (
3.36
) aligns with both the balance (
2.28b
), and the restriction (
3.31c
).

Constituent mass transfer
. We select the constituent mass transfer terms analogously to the constituent peculiar velocities:

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}m_{\alpha{\beta}}g_{\beta},

(3.37)

where the positive definite scalar mobility
m_{\alpha{\beta}}=m_{{\beta}\alpha}
has the same dependencies as (
3.30d
), is compatible with
\sum_{\alpha}m_{\alpha{\beta}}=\sum_{\alpha}m_{{\beta}\alpha}=0
, vanishes in the single fluid region
m_{\alpha{\beta}}|_{\phi_{\gamma}=1}=0,{\gamma}=1,...,N
.

Lemma 3.15

(Compatibility mass transfer)
.

The choice (
3.37
) is compatible with the balance of mass supply (
2.27b
), and the constraint (
3.31d
).

Remark 3.16

(Related constitutive models)
.

In the case:

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\begin{cases}-\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha{\beta}}&\text{ if }\alpha\neq{\beta},\\
\sum_{{\gamma}\neq\alpha}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha{\gamma}}&\text{ if }\alpha={\beta},\end{cases}

(3.38)

for some symmetric
\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha{\beta}}
, we find:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~-\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}-\mathbf{M}_{\alpha\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{{\beta}\neq\alpha}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}-\sum_{{\gamma}\neq\alpha}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha{\gamma}}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~-\sum_{{\beta}}\hat{\mathbf{M}}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\beta}).

(3.39)

This model matches (for the isotropic case
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}=M_{\alpha{\beta}}\mathbf{I}
) that of
[
48
]
. It also closely resembles the form adopted in
[
26
]
. Both closure models involve the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
; a difference lies in fact that the model proposed by
[
26
]
depends on the numbering of the constituents. Finally, we note that forms similar to (
3.39
) may be adopted for the diffusive fluxes and the mass transfer terms.

This finalizes the construction of constitutive models compatible with the imposed energy-dissipative postulate. Substitution of the models (
3.32
), (
3.35
), and (
3.37
) yields the class of incompressible
N
-phase models:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla\lambda

\displaystyle+{\rm div}\left(\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.40a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\alpha}-\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.40b)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=~0,

(3.40c)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{J}}_{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}

\displaystyle~=0,

(3.40d)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{j}}_{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}

\displaystyle~=0,

(3.40e)

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}+\sum_{\beta}m_{\alpha{\beta}}g_{\beta}

\displaystyle~=0.

(3.40f)

Formulation (
3.40
) constitutes a class of models in the sense that particular closure relations (
\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}
,
\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}
and
m_{\alpha{\beta}}
) need to be specified. Given these relations, for each specification of the free energy
\hat{\Psi}
, (
3.40
) is a well-defined closed model; this model is invariant to the renumbering of the constituents, invariant to the set of independent variables, and reduction-consistent. Additionally, it exhibits energy-dissipation, which we explicitly state in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.17

(Compatibility energy dissipation)
.

The model (
3.40
) is compatible with the energy dissipation condition (
3.3
).

Proof.

This follows from
Lemma

3.11
,
Lemma

3.13
, and
Lemma

3.15
. In particular, the dissipation takes the form:

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}=

\displaystyle~\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{R}(t)}2\nu\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right):\left(\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}-\frac{1}{d}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I}\right)+\nu\left(\lambda+\frac{2}{d}\right)\left({\rm div}\mathbf{v}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle~\quad\quad+\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}\left(\nabla g_{\alpha}\right)^{T}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}+\sum_{\alpha,{\beta}}m_{\alpha{\beta}}g_{\alpha}g_{\beta}~{\rm d}v\geq 0,

(3.41)

with
\mathbf{B}_{\alpha{\beta}}=\mathbf{M}_{\alpha{\beta}}+\mathbf{K}_{\alpha{\beta}}
.
∎

4.1
Alternative formulations

As discussed in
Sections

2
and
3
, the unified modeling framework outlined in these sections is invariant to the choice of variables. However, it is worthwhile to discuss some of the formulations that are associated with particular variables.

First, we note that one can identify a pressure quantity in the model as:

\displaystyle p:=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}.

(4.1)

With this choice, the model takes the more compact form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla(\lambda+p)+{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\beta}\nabla\phi_{\beta}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\right)

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}-\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0.

(4.2b)

In accordance with the first metaphysical principle of continuum mixture theory, the mixture free energy is comprised of constituent free energies:

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}=\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\Psi}_{\alpha},

(4.3)

where
\hat{\Psi}_{\alpha}
are the volume-measure constituent free energies. Utilizing (
4.3
) we observe that the pressure satisfies Dalton’s law:

\displaystyle p=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha}p_{\alpha},

(4.4a)

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}_{\alpha},

(4.4b)

where
p_{\alpha}
is the partial pressure of constituent
\alpha
. Thus, the split (
4.3
) reveals that the system may be written as:

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta}\left(\partial_{t}(\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\phi_{\beta}\nabla\lambda+\nabla p_{\beta}-\tilde{\rho}_{\beta}\mathbf{g}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+~{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\gamma}\nabla\phi_{\gamma}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}_{\beta}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\gamma}}\right)\right)-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\lambda({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}-\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0.

(4.5b)

An alternative compact form is obtained with the aid of the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1

(Identity free energy)
.

The free energy contributions collapse into:

\displaystyle{\rm div}\left(\left(\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)\mathbf{I}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right)=\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}.

(4.6)

Proof.

See
Lemma

A.4
.
∎

Invoking
Lemma

4.1
, model (
3.40
) takes a more compact form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla\lambda+\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.7a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}-\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0.

(4.7b)

Considering the third and fourth term in the momentum equation in isolation, these can be written as:

\displaystyle\nabla\lambda+\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla(\lambda+\hat{\mu}_{\alpha})=\sum_{\alpha}\tilde{\rho}_{\alpha}\nabla g_{\alpha}.

(4.8)

Similarly, in the mass balance (
4.7b
), we observe that the chemical potentials
\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}
and the Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
appear solely as a sum via
g_{\alpha}
.

Additionally, we note that the model can alternatively be written in a form that more closely links to existing phase-field models:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\nabla\lambda+\sum_{{\beta}}\phi_{\beta}\nabla\hat{\mu}_{\beta}

\displaystyle-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{g}

\displaystyle=~0,

(4.9a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+{\rm div}(\rho\mathbf{v})

6
Conclusion and outlook

This paper presents a unified framework for
N
-phase Navier-Stokes Cahn-Hilliard Allen-Cahn mixture models with non-matching densities. The framework finds its roots in continuum mixture theory, which serves as a fundamental guiding principle for designing multi-physics models at large. The unified framework proposes a (phase-field) system of
N
mass balance laws, and
1
momentum balance law, that is invariant to the set of fundamental variables, has an energy-dissipative structure, is reduction-consistent, symmetric with respect to the numbering of the phases, and provides well-defined equilibrium solutions. More specifically, we draw the following conclusions:

•

The form of the balance laws is invariant to the set of fundamental variables; at both the constituent and mixture levels (
Sections

2.2
and
2.3
).

•

The free energy class depends on all volume fractions (and their gradients) (
Sections

3.1
and
3.2
); this provides symmetry with respect to the numbering of the constituents.

•

Chemical potentials are tightly connected to the Lagrange multiplier that enforces volume conservation; these quantities occur only as superposition (
Section

3.2
).

•

The unified framework is invariant to the set of independent variables; both before and after constitutive modeling (
Sections

3.3
and
3.4
).

•

Constitutive quantities are such that the resulting model exhibits energy-dissipation (
Section

3.4
).

•

Consistency with the single-phase equations requires mobility quantities to be degenerate (
Section

3.4
).

•

Equilibrium solutions are determined by a balance of (generalized) chemical potentials (see
Section

4.3
).

•

In the binary case, the framework does, in general, not coincide with existing two-phase models (see
Section

5.1
). Furthermore, the framework is closely connected to a class-II model (see
Section

5.3
), and the model of
[
28
]
does not fit into the framework (see
Section

5.2
).

While the proposed unified framework offers insight into the modeling of
N
-phase flows, we do not claim that it is complete. Therefore, we delineate potential future research directions. First, it is important to study the implications of the particular form of the free energy model, such as equilibrium characteristics, and Ostwald ripening phenomena (see e.g.
[
53
]
). To this purpose, we acknowledge the existence of numerous
N
-phase free energy closure models (see e.g.
[
38
]
. Second, it is essential to investigate the sharp interface asymptotic behavior (e.g. jump conditions at interfaces) for particular closure models.
The last point concerns the design of (property-preserving) numerical schemes. Details of
N
-phase computations will be presented elsewhere; however, we provide some considerations here. First, a numerical simulation requires specification of the free energy (as mentioned above). It is hereby important to take (
4.3
) into account to ensure applicability to a general number of constituents. A second consideration concerns the choice of fundamental variables. Although the framework remains invariant to the choice of variables (e.g., using a mass-averaged (
1.1
) or volume-averaged velocity (
1.2
)), certain selections may be more advantageous for designing property-preserving numerical methods. Next, although the proposed system is fully symmetric with respect to the set of variables, in the numerical solution there are at least two roads one can pursue; (i) work with
N-1
volume fractions and compute the
N
-th volume fraction from the others, or (ii) work with the full set of volume fractions and enforce the saturation constraint. In the second case, the saturation constraint could be enforced via (
2.29b
), so that the system of equations becomes:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}(\rho\mathbf{v})+{\rm div}\left(\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}\right)+\sum_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\nabla(\mu_{\beta}+\lambda)-{\rm div}\left(\nu(2\nabla^{s}\mathbf{v}+\bar{\lambda}({\rm div}\mathbf{v})\mathbf{I})\right)-\rho\mathbf{b}

\displaystyle=~0,

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{\alpha}+{\rm div}(\phi_{\alpha}\mathbf{v})+\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}{\rm div}(\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha})-\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=~0,

(6.1b)

\displaystyle{\rm div}\mathbf{v}+\displaystyle\sum_{{\beta}}\rho_{\beta}^{-1}\nabla\cdot\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\beta}-\displaystyle\sum_{{\beta}}\rho_{\beta}^{-1}\hat{\zeta}_{\beta}

\displaystyle~=0,

(6.1c)

\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}\mathbf{B}_{\alpha{\beta}}\nabla g_{\beta}
,
\hat{\zeta}_{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta}m_{\alpha{\beta}}g_{\beta}

\alpha=1,...,N
then provides a divergence-free velocity. Finally, the model can accommodate large differences in specific densities between constituents. Ensuring this property in the fully discrete case requires a robust numerical method.

Appendix A
Reduced free energy class, and proofs

We briefly discuss the free energy class with reduced dependency:

\displaystyle\Psi=

\displaystyle~\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\right),

(A.1)

where the constituent number
\beta\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
is fixed, and where both
\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}
and
\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}
consist of independent variables. The class (
A.1
) is connected to (
3.5
) via the identification:

\displaystyle\Psi=

\displaystyle~\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},1-\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\phi_{\alpha},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},-\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}\left(\left\{\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\right\}_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\right).

(A.2)

The associated chemical potentials take the form:

\displaystyle\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\mu}}}_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}=\dfrac{\partial\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}.

(A.3)

Lemma A.1

(Chemical potentials reduced class)
.

The chemical potentials of the reduced class may be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\mu}}}_{\alpha}^{(\beta)}=\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\beta}.

(A.4)

Proof.

Direct evaluation of the partial derivatives provides:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}=\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}},

(A.5a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}+\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}=\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}.

(A.5b)

The linearity of the divergence operator concludes the proof.

∎

Lemma A.2

(Derivative of the free energy)
.

The derivative of the free energy class (
3.5
) is given by:

\displaystyle{\rm d}\hat{\Psi}=\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha}),

(A.6)

where
{\rm d}
is the derivative operator.

Proof.

Inserting (
A.5
), the derivative of
\Psi
takes the form

\displaystyle{\rm d}\hat{\Psi}={\rm d}\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{6.36115pt}}\smash{\hat{\Psi}}}^{(\beta)}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}}\right){\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\right){\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle~-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}}\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}-\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})+\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\rm d}\phi_{\beta}+\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\beta})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha}),

(A.7)

where we have invoked
\sum_{\alpha}{\rm d}\phi_{\alpha}=0
and
\sum_{\alpha}{\rm d}(\nabla\phi_{\alpha})=0
. The latter expression matches the unconstrained derivative.

∎

Lemma A.3

(Well-defined free energy terms)
.

The following free energy terms in (

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}+\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\dot{\phi}_{\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle~\sum_{\alpha\neq{\beta}}\left(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\right)\dot{\phi}_{\alpha}

Lemma A.4

(Free energy identity)
.

The following identity holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\mu_{\alpha}=\nabla\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)+{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right).

(A.10)

Proof.

Expanding the derivatives of the right-hand size term yields:

\displaystyle\nabla\left(\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\mu_{\alpha}-\hat{\Psi}\right)+{\rm div}\left(\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\mu_{\alpha}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}-\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right)-\nabla\hat{\Psi}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}\right)+\sum_{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{H}\phi_{\alpha}\right)\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\nabla\mu_{\alpha}-\nabla\hat{\Psi}+\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}+\sum_{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{H}\phi_{\alpha}\right)\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}},

(A.11)

where
\mathbf{H}\phi_{\alpha}
is the hessian of
\phi_{\alpha}
. Observing that the sum of the latter three terms in the final expression in (
A.11
) vanishes completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Equivalence of modeling restrictions

This section discusses the equivalence of the restrictions (
3.21
) and (
3.27
) via variable transformation.

First, we recall the variable transformation (
2.7
):

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\rho_{\alpha}}\left(\sum_{\beta}\frac{c_{\beta}}{\rho_{\beta}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{c_{\alpha}}{\rho_{\alpha}}\rho,

(B.1a)

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\rho_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\left(\sum_{\beta}\rho_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\right)^{-1}=\rho_{\alpha}\phi_{\alpha}\rho^{-1},

(B.1b)

where we note:

\displaystyle\rho=\hat{\rho}\left(\left\{c_{\beta}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle~\left(\sum_{\beta}\frac{c_{\beta}}{\rho_{\beta}}\right)^{-1},

(B.2a)

\displaystyle\rho^{-1}=\check{\rho}^{-1}\left(\left\{\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle~=\left(\sum_{\beta}\rho_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\right)^{-1},

(B.2b)

for
\alpha=1,...,N
.

Lemma B.1

(Invertibility transformation maps)
.

The maps (
B.1
) are not invertible.

Proof.

A straightforward evaluation provides the elements of the Jacobian mappings:

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~\rho\rho_{\beta}^{-1}\left(\delta_{\alpha{\beta}}-\rho c_{\beta}\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}\right),

(B.3a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial c_{\beta}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~\rho^{-1}\rho_{\beta}\left(\delta_{\alpha{\beta}}-\rho^{-1}\phi_{\beta}\rho_{\alpha}\right),

(B.3b)

where
\delta_{\alpha{\beta}}
is the kroneckerdelta. Summation over
{\beta}=1,...,N
yields:

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~0,

(B.4a)

\displaystyle\sum_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial c_{\beta}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~0.

(B.4b)

Hence, each of the columns of the Jacobian sums to zero. Thus the columns are linearly dependent, and consequently the determinants of the both mappings vanish:

\displaystyle{\rm det}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~0,

(B.5a)

\displaystyle{\rm det}\dfrac{\partial c_{\beta}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}=

\displaystyle~0.

(B.5b)

∎

Next, we recall the chain rule for the chemical potential.

Lemma B.2

(Chain rule chemical potentials)
.

We have the chain rule for chemical potentials:

\displaystyle\check{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}},

(B.6a)

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\check{\mu}_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial c_{\beta}}{\partial\phi_{\alpha}}.

(B.6b)

Proof.

We show (
B.6a
) and note that (
B.6b
) follows similarly. A direct computation yields:

\displaystyle\check{\mu}_{\alpha}=

\displaystyle~\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\phi_{{\beta}}\left(\left\{c_{\gamma}\right\}\right),\sum_{\gamma}\frac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\gamma}}\nabla c_{\gamma}\right)}{\partial c_{\alpha}}-{\rm div}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\phi_{{\beta}}\left(\left\{c_{\gamma}\right\}\right),\sum_{\gamma}\frac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\gamma}}\nabla c_{\gamma}\right)}{\partial\nabla c_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{{\beta}}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)}{\partial\phi_{\beta}}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle~+\sum_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{{\beta}}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\cdot\left(\sum_{\gamma}\nabla c_{\gamma}~\frac{\partial^{2}\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}\partial c_{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle~-\sum_{\beta}{\rm div}\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{{\beta}}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)\frac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle~-\sum_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{{\beta}}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\cdot\nabla\left(\frac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{{\beta}}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)}{\partial\phi_{\beta}}-{\rm div}\left(\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}\left(\left\{\phi_{{\beta}}\right\},\left\{\nabla\phi_{\beta}\right\}\right)}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\beta}}\right)\right)\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\sum_{\beta}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\dfrac{\partial\phi_{\beta}}{\partial c_{\alpha}}.

(B.7)

∎

\displaystyle~\rho\rho_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(\hat{\mu}_{\alpha}-\sum_{\beta}\hat{\mu}_{\beta}\phi_{\beta}\right).

Proof.

This follows from substituting (
B.3
) into (
B.2
).

Lemma B.4

(Matching Korteweg tensors)
.

The Korteweg stress tensors of the both modeling choices are identical:

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\nabla\phi_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\hat{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla\phi_{\alpha}}=\sum_{\alpha}\nabla c_{\alpha}\otimes\dfrac{\partial\check{\Psi}}{\partial\nabla c_{\alpha}}.

(Equivalence modeling restrictions)
.

The modeling restrictions (
3.21
) and (

) are equivalent.

Proof.

We select the following relations between the Lagrange multipliers of the two modeling choices:

\displaystyle\check{\lambda}=

(B.11)
