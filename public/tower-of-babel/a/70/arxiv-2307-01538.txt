Introduction and main result

Let
\mathbb{M}
a smooth compact Riemannian manifold, and let
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
denote the space of finite signed Borel measures on
\mathbb{M}
. For any smooth function
V\colon\mathbb{M}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
and any
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
we write

V_{\mu}(x):=\mu(V(x,\cdot)):=\int_{\mathbb{M}}V(x,y)\mu(dy)\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{M}$.}

(1)

A stochastic process
X
is called a self-interacting diffusion on
\mathbb{M}
, if it satisfies an equation of the type

dX_{t}=\sqrt{2}dW_{t}(X_{t})-\beta(t)\nabla V_{\mu_{t}}(X_{t})dt,

(2)

where
V\colon\mathbb{M}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
is smooth,
\nabla
denotes the surface gradient on
\mathbb{M}
,
\beta\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function,

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}ds

is the normalized occupation measure of the process
X
up until time
t
, and
W
is a standard Brownian vector field on
\mathbb{M}
.

Here,
V
describes the type of self-interaction and
\beta
is the temporal weight that is given to the self-interaction mechanism. The asymptotic behavior of
X
has been studied for different choices of
V
and
\beta
, and we provide a summary of corresponding results and methods in Section
1.1
below.

Let us now describe the assumptions under which we work in the present article. We always write
|x|
for the euclidean norm of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by
x\cdot y
we denote the euclidean inner product of
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and we set

\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{M}}\left|f(x)\right|

for any function
f\colon\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Assumption 1.1
.

There is a smooth function
v\colon\mathbb{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
with
\left\|v\right\|_{\infty}=1
and

\int_{\mathbb{M}}v(x)dx=0

(3)

such that

V(x,y)=v(x)\cdot v(y)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{M}
.

With this choice of
V
, (
1
) yields

V_{\mu_{t}}(x)=\mu_{t}(v)\cdot v(x)=\left(\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}v(X_{s})ds\right)\cdot v(x).

Hence, the drift term
-\nabla V_{\mu_{t}}(X_{t})
is self-repelling in the sense that it tends to drive
v(X_{t})
away from the temporal mean of
(v(X_{s}))_{s\in[0,t]}
. This interpretation is particularly intuitive in the case where

\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{S}^{n}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\left|x\right|=1\}

and
v(x)=x
, so that

V(x,y)=x\cdot y=\cos(d(x,y))\quad\text{for all $x,y\in\mathbb{S}^{n}$,}

where
d
is the geodesic distance on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
.

Next, we explain our assumptions on
\beta
. Here and everywhere else in this article,
C
and
t_{0}
denote finite, positive, deterministic constants, the exact value of which is unimportant and may change from one step to the next with no indication.

Assumption 1.2
.

The function
\beta\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and there are
a\in(0,\infty)
and
\gamma\in(0,1]
such that

\left|\beta(t)\right|\leq a\log t\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\beta^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq Ct^{-\gamma}\quad\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}$.}

Assumption
1.2
allows the weight
\beta(t)
of the self-repelling drift
-\nabla V_{\mu_{t}}
to increase to infinity, but not fast enough to fully compensate the normalization
\frac{1}{t}
of the occupation measure. The generic case that we will usually have in mind is that of

\beta(t)=b\log(t+1)\quad\text{with $b>0$.}

Other valid choices include
\beta(t)=b\log(\log(t+e))
or simply
\beta\equiv b
. Note that we cap
\gamma
at 1 only for simplicity, as
\gamma>1
provides no meaningful improvement for the estimates that are relevant to our proofs and only results in awkward case distinctions. Also note that the normalization
\left\|v\right\|_{\infty}=1
in Assumption
1.1
makes sure that the parameter
a
is actually meaningful and cannot simply be hidden in
v
.

Assumption
1.2
does not require
\beta
to be non-negative, but we will assume a suitable lower bound in Assumption
1.3
below. Before we can state it precisely, we need to introduce some notation. Let
m\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Setting

Z(m)=\int_{\mathbb{M}}e^{-m\cdot v(x)}dx,

we define a probability measure
\Pi(m)
on
\mathbb{M}
via

\Pi(m)(dx)=\frac{e^{-m\cdot v(x)}}{Z(m)}dx.

(4)

We also interpret
\Pi
as a function on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
by setting

\Pi(\mu):=\Pi(\mu(v))\quad\text{for all $\mu\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})$.}

(5)

Furthermore, for any probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{M}
and any
f\in(\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mu))^{N}
we write

\operatorname{Cov}_{\mu}(f)=\big(\mu(f_{i}f_{j})-\mu(f_{i})\mu(f_{j})\big)_{i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}.

Assumption 1.3
.

There is a
\beta_{0}\geq 0
such that

\beta(t)\geq-\beta_{0}>-\frac{1}{\Lambda}\quad\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}$,}

(6)

where

\Lambda=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\,\left|x\right|=1}x^{T}\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(m)}(v)x\right).

Assumption
1.3
means that while the weight factor in front of the self-repelling drift
-\nabla V_{\mu_{t}}
is in fact allowed to turn negative, we do require a suitable lower bound that depends on
v
. In other words, we allow a limited amount of self-attraction. Clearly,

0<\Lambda\leq\left\|v\right\|_{\infty}^{2}=1,

(7)

so (
6
) makes sense. In particular,
\beta\equiv b
is covered in our setting if and only if
b>-1/\Lambda
.

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.4
.

There is a finite positive constant
\kappa
that depends only on the dimension of
\mathbb{M}
, such that the following holds: if Assumptions
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
hold and the constants
a
and
\gamma
from Assumption
1.2
satisfy

\gamma>2a\kappa,

then for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
we have

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\left|(\mu_{t}-\mathcal{U})(f)\right|}{\log t}\leq-\eta\quad\text{almost surely,}

(8)

where
\mathcal{U}
denotes the uniform distribution on
\mathbb{M}
and

\eta=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa,1-\Lambda\beta_{0}\right\}>0.

Remark 1.5
.

By density of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
in
C(\mathbb{M})
with respect to
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
, (
8
) implies that weak convergence of
\mu_{t}
to
\mathcal{U}
holds almost surely, i.e. we have the ergodicity property

\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}f(X_{s})ds=\mu_{t}(f)\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\mathcal{U}(f)\quad\text{almost surely for all $f\in C(\mathbb{M})$.}

Note, however, that the speed of convergence from (
8
) does not carry over to all
f\in C(\mathbb{M})
via a straight forward density argument (there is no uniformity in
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
or anything similar in (
8
)).

Remark 1.6
.

The constant
\kappa
that appears in Theorem
1.4
is the same one as in
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
, where its exact value is not given. However, one can go through its proof in
[
14
]
and check that it is possible to take
\kappa=2(n+3)
, with
n
the dimension of
\mathbb{M}
(note in particular, that the integral in the expression for
\kappa_{1}
on page 254 of
[
14
]
can be calculated, giving
\kappa_{1}=n
). Explaining the background to this constant requires the introduction of some objects that will be used in the proof of Theorem
1.4
, but we will provide some more insight to this in Lemma
2.2
and Remark
2.3
below.

Remark 1.7
.

It could be possible to relax Assumption
1.1
to
V
being a Mercer kernel, i.e.
V(x,y)=V(y,x)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{M}
and

\int_{\mathbb{M}}\int_{\mathbb{M}}V(x,y)f(x)f(y)dxdy\geq 0\quad\text{for all $f\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(dx)$},

where
dx
denotes integration with respect to the Riemannian measure (compare
[
3
, p. 2]
). Then, by Mercer’s Theorem (see
[
12
, Theorem 3.a.1]
), there are an orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
\mathbb{L}^{2}(dx)
and a sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset[0,\infty)
such that

V(x,y)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\lambda_{n}e_{n}(x)e_{n}(y)\quad\text{for all $x,y\in\mathbb{M}$}.

(9)

Notable examples of Mercer kernels can be found in
[
6
, Section 2.3]
). In particular, if
\mathbb{M}
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
v\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is completely monotonic, then
V(x,y)=v(|x-y|^{2})
,
x,y\in\mathbb{M}
, is a Mercer kernel.

Assumption
1.1
means that we restrict ourselves to such
V
where the expansion in (
9
) is finite (an assumption that is also used in
[
3
]
). This makes it possible to define
\Pi
as a function on
\mathbb{R}^{N}
in (
4
), and hence to argue as we do in Lemmas
2.4
to
2.6
.

Example 1.8

(Weak self-interaction)
.

Let

\beta\equiv b>-\frac{1}{\Lambda}.

Depending on the sign of
b
, this corresponds either to a self-repelling (
b>0
) or self-attracting (
b<0
) diffusion, and since
\beta
is constant in time, we speak of weak self-interaction.

Then
\beta^{\prime}\equiv 0
and for any
a>0
we have
|\beta(t)|\leq a\log t
for all
t\geq t_{0}=e^{b/a}
. Hence, Theorem
1.4
can be applied with
\gamma=1
,
\beta_{0}=\max\{0,-b\}
and any
a>0
, so for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
we get

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\left|(\mu_{t}-\mathcal{U})(f)\right|}{\log t}\leq-\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},1-\Lambda|b|\right\}\quad\text{almost surely.}

In the particular case
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{S}^{n}
and
v(x)=x
, this strengthens part (i) of
[
4
, Theorem 4.5]
. In Section
3
, we provide a more detailed investigation of this connection and also a proof that we can actually weaken Assumption
1.3
in this situation (see Proposition

).

Example 1.9

(Moderate self-repulsion)
.

Let

\beta(t)=b\log(t+1)\quad\text{with $b>0$.}

In this situation,
\beta
is positive and increases to infinity, but more slowly than the normalization factor
t
in
\mu_{t}
, so we speak of moderate self-repulsion.

Then
|\beta^{\prime}(t)|\leq Ct^{-1}
and for any
a>b
there is a
t_{0}>0
such that
|\beta(t)|\leq a\log t
for all
t\geq t_{0}
. Hence, Theorem
1.4
can be applied whenever
b<\frac{1}{2\kappa}
, and for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
we get

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\left|(\mu_{t}-\mathcal{U})(f)\right|}{\log t}\leq-\left(\frac{1}{2}-b\kappa\right)\quad\text{almost surely.}

1.1
Context

The asymptotic behavior of self-interacting diffusions has been studied with various degrees of generality concerning the state space
\mathbb{M}
and the type of self-interaction governed by
V
. The weight
\beta(t)
is usually chosen as either
\beta\equiv b
or
\beta(t)=bt
with
b>0
, which are sometimes referred to as weak and strong self-interaction respectively. In this sense, the prototypical case
\beta(t)=b\log(t+1)
of Assumption
1.2
corresponds to moderate self-interaction.

There are a number of case studies and some general results for
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
(e.g.
[
9
,
13
,
11
,
8
]
), but our focus lies on compact state spaces. Some of the results in the following summary are more general than others, but all of them are valid for
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{S}^{n}
and include the important case
V(x,y)=\cos(d(x,y))
(self-repulsion) or
V(x,y)=-\cos(d(x,y))
(self-attraction).

Of course, it is somewhat arbitrary to include the sign that distinguishes repulsion from attraction in
V
instead of
\beta
, but this choice reinforces the interpretation of
V
as the type of interaction and
\beta
as a weight (even though our Assumptions
1.2
and
1.3
do not require
\beta
to be non-negative).
The general expectation is that a diffusion with sufficient self-attraction will asymptotically be concentrated around (or even converge to) some limit random variable
X_{\infty}\in\mathbb{M}
, while a self-repelling diffusion (on a compact state space) will quite contrarily be uniformly distributed in the limit.

We will now give a brief overview of the methods that have been used in these different cases, the corresponding results are summarized in Table

The case of weak self-interaction has been studied the most thoroughly, in particular in the series of papers
[

to a measure-valued ordinary differential equation and use this to precisely describe its asymptotic behavior for some specific choices of

. The same approach is used in
[
14
]
to treat the case of moderate self-interaction. This turns out to be more delicate and only the self-attracting case is solved satisfyingly. The case of moderate self-repulsion is the content of the present paper, and we use a similar strategy. A detailed explanation of the general idea of this method is presented in Section
1.2
below, including a discussion of the differences between
[
4
]
,
[

is the uniform distribution. Almost sure convergence in the self-attracting case is proved in

1.2
Outline of proof

In order to get a grip of the long time behavior of

\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\delta_{X_{s}}ds,

we calculate its time evolution. First, we have

\partial_{t}\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{t}\left(-\mu_{t}+\delta_{X_{t}}\right),

where the derivative is to be understood as the derivative of a real function pointwise in all
f\in C(\mathbb{M})
. In order to eliminate the factor
\frac{1}{t}
, we look at the dynamics on an exponential time scale, i.e.

\partial_{t}\mu_{e^{t}}=-\mu_{e^{t}}+\delta_{X_{e^{t}}}.

If
t>0
is large, the distribution of
X_{t}
should be close to the equilibrium of the current drift potential
\beta(t)V_{\mu_{t}}(x)
, i.e. to the probability distribution
\Pi(\beta(t)\mu_{t})
as defined in (
5
). If our intuition of the process is correct,
\Pi(\beta(t)\mu_{t})
on the other hand should be close to the uniform distribution
\mathcal{U}
on
\mathbb{M}
. Therefore, we set

\varepsilon^{1}_{t}:=\delta_{X_{e^{t}}}-\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\mu_{e^{t}})

(10)

and

\varepsilon^{2}_{t}:=\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\mu_{e^{t}})-\mathcal{U},

(11)

so that

\partial_{t}\mu_{e^{t}}=-\mu_{e^{t}}+\mathcal{U}+\varepsilon^{1}_{t}+\varepsilon^{2}_{t}.

(12)

If
\varepsilon^{1}_{t}
and
\varepsilon^{2}_{t}
are asymptotically negligible in a suitable sense, then for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
the trajectory
t\mapsto\mu_{e^{t}}(f)
almost surely shadows the flow generated by the ordinary differential equation

\dot{\nu}_{t}(f)=-\nu_{t}(f)+\mathcal{U}(f),

(13)

so that in particular
\mu_{e^{t}}(f)
almost surely converges to
\mathcal{U}(f)
. The details of this argument are given in Section
2.3
below, but first we devote Sections
2.1
and
2.2
to the required analysis of the asymptotics of
\varepsilon^{1}
and
\varepsilon^{2}
.

Our proof strategy is inspired by
[
4
]
and
[
14
]
. In these works, the authors make use of the relation

\partial_{t}\mu_{e^{t}}=-\mu_{e^{t}}+\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\mu_{e^{t}})+\varepsilon^{1}_{t},

(14)

which will also be very useful for us in Section
2.2
. The formal limit equation corresponding to (
14
) is

\dot{\nu}_{t}=-\nu_{t}+\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\nu_{t}).

(15)

Note that under (
3
) we have
\Pi(\mathcal{U})=\mathcal{U}
, and so (
15
) can be viewed as a variant of the limit equation (
13
) that is less specific to a particular situation. For constant
\beta
as in
[
4
]
, the (measure-valued) equation (
15
) is homogeneous in time and hence after establishing that
\mu_{e^{t}}
shadows it, powerful results from the theory of dynamical systems can be used to study the long-time behavior of
\mu_{t}
for several different choices of
V
(cf.
[
4
, Sections 3 and 4]
). For non-constant
\beta
as in
[
14
]
, this link can still be established under certain conditions (cf.
[
14
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2]
), but it is not as fruitful, since (
15
) is no longer homogeneous in time. In particular,
[
14
, Theorems 2.1 and 2.2]
are not used in the proof of the convergence result for moderately self-attracting diffusions on the sphere (
[
14
, Theorem 3.1]
) which is instead proved "by hand".

The limit equation (
13
) is much simpler than (
15
), because it is tailor-made for cases in which we expect
\mu_{t}
to converge to the uniform distribution (while in other cases we might not expect a deterministic limit at all). Introducing the second error term
\varepsilon^{2}
allows us to move the explicit dependence of the problem on
\beta
entirely into the error terms.

While many techniques in the present article are extensions of the works in
[
14
]
and
[
4
]
, its main innovations lie in replacing (
15
) with (
13
) and in the arguments in Section
2.2
that are necessary to deal with the extra error term that is caused by this approach. This approach allows us to cover for the first time the case of moderate self-repulsion (
[
14
]
only deals with one particular example of moderate self-attraction), and it also comes with the benefit of providing a speed of convergence.

The aim of this section is the following result:

Proposition 2.1

If

f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
, then

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{t}^{t+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr\right|\right)\leq-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa\right)\quad\text{almost surely}.

While Proposition
2.1
is mostly just a reformulation of
[
14
, Theorem 2.1]
, the exact value on the right hand side is not provided there. Because of this, and since many of the intermediate results and the objects involved will also be crucial in Section
2.2
below, we will now give a detailed summary of Section 2 from
[
14
]
, including a streamlined proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let us fix
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
and consider the time-homogeneous stochastic differential equation with no self-interaction

dY_{t}=\sqrt{2}dW_{t}(Y_{t})-\nabla V_{\mu}(Y_{t})dt,

where we think of the dynamics as those arising from freezing the drift potential in (
2
) at some time
t_{0}
, so that formally
\mu=\beta(t_{0})\mu_{t_{0}}
. These dynamics can also be described via the infinitesimal generator

A_{\mu}f=\Delta f-\nabla V_{\mu}\cdot\nabla f\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{D}(A_{\mu})\supset C^{2}(\mathbb{M}),

(16)

and the corresponding equilibrium is given by
\Pi(\mu)
as defined in (
5
). Let
(P_{s}^{\mu})_{s\geq 0}
denote the transition semigroup on
\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Pi(\mu))=\mathbb{L}^{2}(dx)
generated by
A_{\mu}
. Then
A_{\mu}
satisfies a Poincaré inequality and therefore
P_{s}^{\mu}f
converges to
\Pi(\mu)(f)
exponentially fast with respect to the
\mathbb{L}^{2}
-norm for any
f\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(dx)
(see Sections 1.1 and 1.4.3 of
[
15
]
). Using this and basic semigroup theory, one can easily show that

Q_{\mu}f:=-\int_{0}^{\infty}P^{\mu}_{s}(f-\Pi(\mu)(f))ds\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(dx),

is well-defined and satisfies

A_{\mu}Q_{\mu}f=Q_{\mu}A_{\mu}f=f-\Pi(\mu)(f)\quad\text{for all }f\in\mathcal{D}(A_{\mu}),

so
Q_{\mu}
is "almost an inverse to
A_{\mu}
".

Now set

A_{t}:=A_{\beta(t)\mu_{t}},\quad Q_{t}:=Q_{\beta(t)\mu_{t}}.

Then for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
we can rewrite (
10
) as

\varepsilon^{1}_{t}(f)=f(X_{e^{t}})-\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\mu_{e^{t}})(f)=A_{e^{t}}Q_{e^{t}}f(X_{e^{t}}).

If we set

F^{f}_{t}(x):=\frac{1}{t}Q_{t}f(x),

(17)

applying the change of variables
r\mapsto\log r
and then Ito’s formula yields

\int_{s}^{t}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr=\int_{e^{s}}^{e^{t}}A_{r}F^{f}_{r}(X_{r})dr=F^{f}_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})-F^{f}_{e^{s}}(X_{e^{s}})-\int_{e^{s}}^{e^{t}}\dot{F}^{f}_{r}(X_{r})dr+M^{f,s}_{t},

(18)

where
(M^{f,s}_{t})_{t\geq s}
is a martingale with

\langle M^{f,s},M^{g,s}\rangle_{t}=\int_{s}^{t}e^{r}\nabla F^{f}_{e^{r}}(X_{e^{r}})\cdot\nabla F^{g}_{e^{r}}(X_{e^{r}})dr\quad\text{for all $f,g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})$}

(19)

(compare
[
14
, Section 3.3]
and
[
4
, Remark 2.2]
). Therefore, in order to estimate the integral over
\varepsilon^{1}
, we need to estimate
F^{f}_{t}
,
\nabla F^{f}_{t}
, and the time derivative
\dot{F}^{f}_{t}
, which can be done with the help of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

There is a constant

\kappa\in(0,\infty)

(20)

depending only on the dimension of
\mathbb{M}
such that for all
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
and
t\geq t_{0}
the following estimates hold.

1.

\left\|Q_{t}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ct^{a\kappa}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
,

2.

\left\|\nabla Q_{t}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq C(1+\log t)^{1/2}t^{a\kappa}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
,

3.

\left\|\partial_{t}Q_{t}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq C(\log t)^{3/2}t^{2a\kappa-\gamma}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

We can take
\kappa
as the constant of the same name from
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
, which then yields

\left\|Q_{t}f\right\|_{\infty}=\left\|Q_{\beta(t)\mu_{t}}f\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ce^{\kappa\left\|V_{\beta(t)\mu_{t}}\right\|_{\infty}}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}.

With Assumptions
1.1
and
1.2
, we get

\left\|V_{\beta(t)\mu_{t}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left|\beta(t)\right|\left|\mu_{t}(v)\right|\left\|v\right\|_{\infty}\leq a\log t,

and the first estimate follows. As shown in the proof of
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
,
\kappa
depends only on the dimension of
\mathbb{M}
. The second estimate also follows from
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
in a similar way, and the third estimate can be quoted straight from
[
14
, Lemma 2.8]
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

From now on,
\kappa
will always be the constant from (
20
). In particular, this is the same
\kappa
that we use in Theorem
1.4
. As seen in the proof of Lemma
2.2
above, its origin lies in
[
14
, Lemmas 2.3]
which is proved via classical results about
\log
-Sobolev and Poincaré inequalities for the operator
A_{t}
and an application of the Bakry-Emery criterion. The constant
\kappa
is derived from multiple different constants occurring in the process.

Again, consider
F^{f}_{t}
as in (
17
) with
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
. As mentioned above, Lemma
2.2
is the key to
estimating the terms
F^{f}_{t}
,
\nabla F^{f}_{t}
, and
\dot{F}^{f}_{t}
that occur in (
18
) and (
19
). Indeed, the first two estimates from Lemma
2.2
imply

\left\|F^{f}_{e^{t}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ce^{-(1-a\kappa)t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}

(21)

and

\left\|\nabla F^{f}_{e^{t}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq C(1+t)^{1/2}e^{-(1-a\kappa)t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}.

(22)

Noting that

\dot{F}^{f}_{t}=-\frac{1}{t^{2}}Q_{t}f+\frac{1}{t}\partial_{t}Q_{t}f,

the first and third estimates from Lemma
2.2
imply

\left\|\dot{F}^{f}_{e^{t}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq C\left(e^{(a\kappa-2)t}+t^{3/2}e^{(2a\kappa-\gamma-1)t}\right)\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq C(1+t)^{3/2}e^{(2a\kappa-\gamma-1)t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty},

(23)

where the last step used that
\gamma\in(0,1]
by Assumption
1.2
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Let
t\geq t_{0}
,
s\geq 0
, and let
F_{t}=F^{f}_{t}
with
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
as in (
17
). Writing
M=M^{f,t}
for short, we get from (
18
) that

\int_{t}^{t+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr=F_{e^{t+s}}(X_{e^{t+s}})-F_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})+\int_{t}^{t+s}e^{r}\dot{F}_{e^{r}}(X_{e^{r}})dr+M_{t+s},

(24)

and we will now estimate all of the terms on the right hand side. Let us write

\xi=\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa

for short. By (
21
), we get

\left|F_{e^{t+s}}(X_{e^{t+s}})-F_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})\right|\leq Ce^{-(1-a\kappa)t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ce^{-\xi t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}.

(25)

By (
23
),

\left|\int_{t}^{t+s}e^{r}\dot{F}_{e^{r}}(X_{e^{r}})dr\right|\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\int_{t}^{t+s}(1+r)^{3/2}e^{-2\xi r}dr\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-\xi r}dr\leq Ce^{-\xi t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}.

(26)

Now, let
\delta>0
. With the help of (
19
) and (
22
), we get

\langle M\rangle_{t+s}=\int_{t}^{t+s}e^{r}\left|\nabla F_{e^{r}}(X_{e^{r}})\right|^{2}dr\leq C\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}(1+r)e^{-(1-2a\kappa)t}dr\leq Ce^{-(2\xi-\delta)t}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}.

(27)

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Markov’s inequality, Burkholder’s inequality and (
27
) imply

\mathbb{P}\left[e^{(\xi-\delta)n}\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|M_{n+s}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right]\leq\varepsilon^{-2}e^{2(\xi-\delta)n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|M_{n+s}\right|^{2}\right]\leq C\varepsilon^{-2}e^{-\delta n}\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\quad\text{for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Since the right hand side is summable in
n\in\mathbb{N}
, Borel-Cantelli implies

e^{(\xi-\delta)n}\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|M_{n+s}\right|\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}0\quad\text{almost surely for any $\delta>0$.}

Combining this with (
24
), (
25
) and (
26
), we arrive at

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\left(\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{n}^{n+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr\right|\right)\leq-\xi\quad\text{almost surely.}

If
\lfloor t\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
t\in(0,\infty)
, then

\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{t}^{t+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)\right|dr=\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\left(\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{t+s}-\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{t}\right)\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr\right|\leq 2\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{\lfloor t\rfloor}^{{\lfloor t\rfloor}+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr\right|,

so we also get the continuous-time property as stated in the proposition.
∎

2.2
Dealing with
\varepsilon^{2}

The aim of this section is to find a suitable counterpart to Proposition
2.1
for
\varepsilon^{2}
. We start with some preparatory lemmas. Denote the total variation norm of
\mu\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
by

\left\|\mu\right\|=\sup\{\mu(f):f\in C(\mathbb{M}),\,\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1\},

and recall that

\Pi(m)(dx)=\frac{e^{-m\cdot v(x)}}{Z(m)}dx\quad\text{with}\quad Z(m)=\int_{\mathbb{M}}e^{-m\cdot v(x)}dx

(28)

for all
m\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

Lemma 2.4
.

The mapping
\Pi\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
is Lipschitz continuous, i.e.

\left\|\Pi(m)-\Pi(m^{\prime})\right\|\leq C\left|m-m^{\prime}\right|\quad\text{for all $m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$.}

Proof.

It is easy to check that the derivative of
\Pi
is bounded.
∎

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, we have

\Pi(m)(v)=-\nabla\log Z(m)

(29)

and

\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(m)}(v)=\operatorname{Hess}\log Z(m),

(30)

where
\operatorname{Hess}
denotes the Hessian matrix.

Proof.

This is a straight forward calculation.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

If we set

J_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R},\quad m\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\left|m\right|^{2}+1_{\beta(e^{t})\neq 0}\cdot\frac{1}{\beta(e^{t})}\log Z(\beta(e^{t})m),

for all
t\geq 0
, then

\nabla J_{t}(m)=m-\Pi(\beta(e^{t})m)(v)\quad\text{for all $m\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ and $t\geq 0$}

(31)

and

m\cdot\nabla J_{t}(m)\geq(1-\Lambda\beta_{0})\left|m\right|^{2}\quad\text{for all $m\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$ and $t\geq t_{0}$}

(32)

where
\Lambda
and
\beta_{0}
are the constants from Assumption
1.3
.

Proof.

Due to (
28
) and (
3
), we have
\Pi(0)(v)=0
. Using this fact and (
29
), we can easily check that (
31
) holds. Because of (
30
), we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hess}J_{t}(m)=1_{N\times N}+\beta(e^{t})\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(\beta(e^{t})m)}(v),

and thanks to Assumption
1.3
, this implies that the smallest eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Hess}J_{t}(m)
is at least
1-\Lambda\beta_{0}>0
. Therefore
J_{t}
is strongly convex, more precisely

(m-m^{\prime})\cdot\left(\nabla J_{t}(m)-\nabla J_{t}(m^{\prime})\right)\geq(1-\Lambda\beta_{0})\left|m-m^{\prime}\right|^{2}\quad\text{for all $m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}$.}

Since
\nabla J_{t}(0)=\Pi(0)(v)=0
, setting
m^{\prime}=0
yields (
32
).
∎

Remark 2.7
.

J_{t}(m)
,
\nabla J_{t}(m)
, and
\operatorname{Hess}J_{t}(m)
are all continuous with respect to
t\in[0,\infty)
.

The following lemma contains the essential step towards the main result of this section, Proposition
2.10
. In the sequel, we use the shorthand notation

m_{t}:=\mu_{e^{t}}(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\quad\text{for all $t\geq 0$,}

(33)

and
\eta
will always be the constant from Theorem
1.4
, i.e.

\eta=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa,1-\Lambda\beta_{0}\right\}>0.

Lemma 2.8
.

If
2a\kappa<\gamma
, then

e^{\delta t}m_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0\quad\text{almost surely for all $\delta<\eta$.}

Proof.

1.) Set

\tilde{m}_{t}:=m_{t}-F_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})

(34)

where

F_{t}(x):=(F_{t}^{v_{i}}(x))_{i=1,\ldots,N}=t^{-1}(Q_{t}v_{i}(x))_{i=1,\ldots,N}

(compare (
17
)). Let
t>t_{0}
. By (
34
) and (
21
), we have

|e^{\delta t}m_{t}|\leq e^{\delta t}|\tilde{m}_{t}|+Ce^{-(1-a\kappa-\delta)t}.

Since
\delta<\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa<1-a\kappa
, the second summand in the above bound vanishes for
t\to\infty
, and hence it suffices to show for this lemma that

e^{\delta t}\tilde{m}_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0\quad\text{almost surely for all $\delta<\eta$.}

(35)

Also note that for all
t>t_{0}
, both
m_{t}
and
\tilde{m}_{t}
have deterministic bounds, as

\left|m_{t}\right|=\left|\mu_{e^{t}}(v)\right|\leq\left\|v\right\|_{\infty}=1

and hence, by (
34
) and (
21
),

\left|\tilde{m}_{t}\right|\leq 1+Ce^{-(1-a\kappa)t}\leq C.

(36)

2.) In this step, we use a similar approach as in Sections 3.1 - 3.4 of
[
14
]
in order to find a stochastic differential equation that is fulfilled by
|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}
. Combining (
14
) and (
31
) (from Lemma
2.6
), we get

\dot{m}_{t}=-\nabla J_{t}(m_{t})+\varepsilon^{1}_{t}(v),

and hence, by (
34
),

d\tilde{m}_{t}=\left(-\nabla J_{t}(m_{t})+\varepsilon^{1}_{t}(v)\right)dt-dF_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}}).

By virtue of (
18
), this can be rewritten as

d\tilde{m}_{t}=\left(-\nabla J_{t}(m_{t})-e^{t}\dot{F}_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})\right)dt+dM^{v,t_{0}}_{t},

(37)

where we read
M^{v,t_{0}}_{t}
as the vector
(M^{v_{i},t_{0}}_{t})_{i=1,\ldots,N}
, and we will use the shorthand notations
M_{t}=M^{v,t_{0}}_{t}
and
M^{i}_{t}=M^{v_{i},t_{0}}_{t}
. Next, we set

H_{t}=-e^{t}\dot{F}_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})-\left(\nabla J_{t}(m_{t})-\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})\right)

(38)

and rewrite (
37
) as

d\tilde{m}_{t}=\big(-\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})+H_{t}\big)dt+dM_{t}.

(39)

Ito’s formula yields

d|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}=2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot d\tilde{m}_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}d\langle M^{i}\rangle_{t},

and by plugging in (
39
) and the expression for
\langle M^{i}\rangle_{t}
from (
19
), we get

d|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}=\left(-2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})+2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot H_{t}+e^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\nabla F_{e^{t}}^{v_{i}}(X_{e^{t}})\right|^{2}\right)dt+2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot dM_{t}.

(40)

3.) Our next goal is to find a suitable upper bound for the drift in (
40
). For the rest of the proof we assume that
t\geq t_{0}
and

0<\alpha<2\eta=\min\left\{\gamma-2a\kappa,2(1-\Lambda\beta_{0})\right\}.

By (
23
),

\left|e^{t}\dot{F}_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})\right|\leq C(1+t)^{3/2}e^{(2a\kappa-\gamma)t}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

(41)

By (
31
) and Lemma
2.4
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\nabla J_{t}(m_{t})-\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t}\right|+\left|\Pi(\beta(e^{t})m_{t})(v)-\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\tilde{m}_{t})(v)\right|

\displaystyle\leq(1+C|\beta(e^{t})|)\left|m_{t}-\tilde{m}_{t}\right|

\displaystyle=(1+C|\beta(e^{t})|)\left|F_{e^{t}}(X_{e^{t}})\right|.

and, with the help of (
21
) and the logarithmic bound for
\left|\beta\right|
from Assumption
1.2
, we arrive at

\left|\nabla J_{t}(m_{t})-\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})\right|\leq C(1+t)e^{(a\kappa-1)t}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

(42)

Combining (
41
), (
42
), and (
36
), we can conclude that with
H
from (
38
) we have

\left|\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot H_{t}\right|\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

(43)

For the third summand of the drift term in (
40
), we apply (
22
) and get

e^{t}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\nabla F_{e^{t}}^{v_{i}}(X_{e^{t}})\right|^{2}\leq C(1+t)e^{(2a\kappa-1)t}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}.

(44)

Finally, plugging (
43
) and (
44
) as well as (
32
) from Lemma
2.6
into (
40
) yields

d|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}\leq\left(-2(1-\Lambda\beta_{0})|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}+Ce^{-\alpha t}\right)dt+2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot dM_{t}.

(45)

4.) With (
45
) at hand, we can now investigate the asymptotics of
\tilde{m}_{t}
. Setting

\xi:=2(1-\Lambda\beta_{0})

and plugging (
45
) into

d\left(e^{\xi t}|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}\right)=\xi e^{\xi t}|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}dt+e^{\xi t}d|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2},

we get

\displaystyle|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\xi(t-t_{0})}|\tilde{m}_{t_{0}}|^{2}+C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\xi(t-r)-\alpha r}dr+2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\xi(t-r)}\tilde{m}_{r}\cdot dM_{r}\leq Ce^{-\alpha t}+2e^{-\xi t}N_{t},

(46)

where

N_{t}=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{\xi r}\tilde{m}_{r}\cdot dM_{r}.

By (
19
) and (
44
), we have

\langle N\rangle_{t}\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{2\xi r}\left|\tilde{m}_{r}\right|^{2}e^{r}\left|\nabla F_{e^{r}}(X_{e^{r}})\right|^{2}dr\leq C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{(2\xi-\alpha)r}\left|\tilde{m}_{r}\right|^{2}dr.

(47)

Since
\tilde{m}
is bounded (see (
36
)) and
\alpha<2\xi
, (
47
) yields

\langle N\rangle_{t}\leq Ce^{(2\xi-\alpha)t},

(48)

so the law of the iterated logarithm implies

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left|N_{t}\right|}{e^{\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)t}\log(t)}<\infty\quad\text{almost surely.}

(49)

In analogy to the way we treat constants
C
, we will now write
K
for an almost surely finite non-negative random variable, that may change from one step to the next with no indication. Using this notational convention, (
49
) is equivalent to

\left|N_{t}\right|\leq Ke^{\left(\xi-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)t}\log(t)\quad\text{for all $t>t_{0}$}.

(50)

Plugging (
50
) into (
46
) (and enlarging
t_{0}
, if necessary) yields

\displaystyle|\tilde{m}_{t}|\leq\left(Ce^{-\alpha t}+Ke^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}t}\log(t)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq Ke^{-\frac{\alpha}{4}t}(\log(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\quad\text{for all $t>t_{0}$},

and hence

e^{\delta t}\tilde{m}_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0\quad\text{almost surely for all $\delta<\frac{\alpha}{4}$.}

(51)

In particular, if
\delta<\frac{\alpha}{4}
, then
e^{\delta t}\tilde{m}_{t}
is almost surely bounded. We can use this fact in (
47
) in order to improve (
48
) to

\langle N\rangle_{t}\leq Ke^{(2\xi-(\alpha+2\delta))t}

for any
\delta<\frac{\alpha}{4}
. Then we use the same argument as from (
48
) to (
51
) in order to improve (
51
) to

e^{\delta t}\tilde{m}_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0\quad\text{almost surely for all $\delta<\frac{3\alpha}{8}$.}

Iterating this argument shows that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

e^{\delta t}\tilde{m}_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}0\quad\text{almost surely for all $\delta<\frac{(2^{n}-1)\alpha}{2^{n+1}}$.}

Since
n
can be arbitrarily large and
\alpha
arbitrarily close to
2\eta
, it follows that (
35
) holds, and thus the proof is completed.
∎

Remark 2.9
.

Note that Lemma
2.8
can be interpreted as a statement on the polynomial decay of the drift potential
\beta(t)V_{\mu_{t}}
of (
2
). More precisely, since
\left\|V_{\mu_{t}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left|m_{\log t}\right|
(by (
1
) and (
33
)), Lemma
2.8
and Assumption
1.2
imply

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\left\|\beta(t)V_{\mu_{t}}\right\|_{\infty}}{\log t}\leq-\eta\quad\text{almost surely},

provided that
2a\kappa<\gamma
.

We can now prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 2.10
.

If
2a\kappa<\gamma
, then almost surely

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left\|\varepsilon^{2}_{t}\right\|\leq-\eta.

Proof.

By (
11
) and (
28
),

\varepsilon^{2}_{t}=\Pi(\beta(e^{t})\mu_{e^{t}}(v))-\Pi(0),

so Lipschitz continuity (see Lemma
2.4
) and the logarithmic bound for
\left|\beta\right|
from Assumption
1.2
imply

\left\|\varepsilon^{2}_{t}\right\|\leq Ct\left|\mu_{e^{t}}(v)\right|=Ct\left|m_{t}\right|.

The claim now follows from Lemma
2.8
.
∎

2.3
Putting the pieces together

Let
f\in C(\mathbb{M})
. The unique solution to (
13
) at time
t\geq 0
with the initial value
x\in\mathbb{R}
is given by

\Phi_{f}(t,x)=e^{-t}x+(1-e^{-t})\mathcal{U}(f),

so the mapping
\Phi_{f}\colon[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the semiflow on
\mathbb{R}
that is generated by (
13
). Recall again the constant

\eta=\min\left\{\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa,1-\Lambda\beta_{0}\right\}>0

from Theorem
1.4
.

Proposition 2.11
.

If
2a\kappa<\gamma
and
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
, then almost surely
(\mu_{e^{t}}(f))_{t\geq 0}
is a
(-\eta)
-pseudotrajectory of the semiflow
\Phi_{f}
, i.e. almost surely

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\sup_{s\in[0,T]}\left|\mu_{e^{t+s}}(f)-\Phi_{f}(s,\mu_{e^{t}}(f))\right|\right)\leq-\eta\quad\text{for all $T>0$}.

(52)

Proof.

By (
12
) and (
13
) we have

\mu_{e^{t+s}}(f)-\Phi_{f}(s,\mu_{e^{t}}(f))=-\int_{0}^{s}\left(\mu_{e^{t+r}}(f)-\Phi_{f}(r,\mu_{e^{t}}(f))\right)(f)dr+\int_{0}^{s}\left(\varepsilon^{1}_{t+r}+\varepsilon^{2}_{t+r}\right)(f)dr,

so, by variation of constants,

\mu_{e^{t+s}}(f)-\Phi_{f}(s,\mu_{e^{t}}(f))=\int_{t}^{t+s}e^{-(t+s-r)}\left(\varepsilon^{1}_{r}+\varepsilon^{2}_{r}\right)(f)dr.

(53)

Since integration by parts yields

\int_{t}^{t+s}e^{-(t+s-r)}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr=\int_{t}^{t+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr-\int_{t}^{t+s}e^{-(t+s-r)}\left(\int_{t}^{r}\varepsilon^{1}_{u}(f)du\right)dr,

the claim follows from (
53
) and Propositions
2.1
and
2.10
.
∎

Intuitively, Proposition
2.11
says that
\mu_{e^{t}}
is exponentially close to the behavior of a solution of (
13
). Since any solution of (
13
) converges exponentially fast to
\mathcal{U}
, Theorem
1.4
now follows from a general result about pseudotrajectories.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Clearly,

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left|\Phi_{f}(t,x)-\mathcal{U}(f)\right|=-1\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$.}

Thanks to this and Proposition
2.11
, all of the conditions of part (i) of
[
1
, Lemma 8.7]
(with
B=\mathbb{R}
,
K=\{\mathcal{U}(f)\}
,
X(t)=\mu_{e^{t}}(f)
,
Y(t)=\mathcal{U}(f)
,
\alpha=-\eta>-1=\lambda
) are fulfilled, and hence we get

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left|\mu_{e^{t}}(f)-\mathcal{U}(f)\right|\leq-\eta\quad\text{almost surely,}

(54)

which is equivalent to (
8
).
∎

Remark 2.13
.

One can also use a more technical variant of the argument given in this section to get a slightly different version of Theorem
1.4
that provides a convergence rate with respect to a family of random metrics on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
. If
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{M})
is dense in the unit sphere of
C(\mathbb{M})
and
c=(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset(0,\infty)
is summable, then

d(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n}\left|\mu(f_{n})-\nu(f_{n})\right|\quad\text{for all $\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})$}

is a metric on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
that induces the weak convergence of measures, i.e. for
\mu,\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots\in\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})
we have

d_{c}(\mu_{k},\mu)\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}0\quad\iff\quad\mu_{k}(f)\xrightarrow{k\to\infty}\mu(f)\quad\text{for all $f\in C(\mathbb{M})$.}

By Proposition
2.1
, there exist finite random variables
K_{n}(\varepsilon)
such that almost surely

\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{t}^{t+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f_{n})dr\right|\leq K_{n}(\varepsilon)e^{-\left(\frac{\gamma}{2}-a\kappa-\varepsilon\right)t}\quad\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}$}.

With the random sequence
c(\varepsilon)=(c_{n}(\varepsilon))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by
c_{n}(\varepsilon)=(K_{n}(\varepsilon))^{-1}2^{-n}
, the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
2.11
yields that almost surely
(\mu_{e^{t}})_{t\geq 0}
is a
(-\eta+\varepsilon)
-pseudotrajectory of the semiflow

\Phi\colon[0,\infty)\times\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M})\to\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M}),\quad(t,\nu_{0})\mapsto e^{-t}\nu_{0}+(1-e^{-t})\mathcal{U},

on
(\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{M}),d_{c(\varepsilon)})
. Ultimately, this leads to the conclusion that under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1.4
we have

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log d_{c(\varepsilon)}(\mu_{t},\mathcal{U})}{\log t}\leq-(\eta-\varepsilon)\quad\text{almost surely for all $\varepsilon>0$.}

3.1
Calculating
\Lambda

In the following, for any
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we use the notation

\bar{m}=\begin{cases}\frac{m}{|m|},&\text{if $m\neq 0$,}\\
0,&\text{if $m=0$}.\end{cases}

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\varrho(r)=-\frac{\int_{0}^{\pi}\cos xe^{-r\cos x}(\sin x)^{n-1}dx}{\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-r\cos x}(\sin x)^{n-1}dx}\quad\text{for all $r\geq 0$}.

1.

For all
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we have

\Pi(m)(v)=-\varrho(|m|)\bar{m}.

(57)

2.

We have

\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{\varrho(r)}{r}=\frac{1}{n+1},

and we therefore interpret
\frac{\varrho(0)}{0}
as
\frac{1}{n+1}
in the following.

3.

For all
m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
we have

\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(m)}(v)=\left(\varrho^{\prime}(|m|)-\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}\right)\bar{m}\bar{m}^{T}+\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}1_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}

and its largest eigenvalue is given by
\lambda(|m|)
, where

\lambda(r)=\frac{2\varrho(r)}{r}-\varrho^{\prime}(r)\quad\text{for all $r\geq 0$}.

(58)

4.

We have

\Lambda=\max_{r\geq 0}\lambda(r)\in\left[\frac{1}{n+1},\frac{2}{n+1}\right).

(59)

Plots of
\varrho
and
\lambda
for
n\in\{1,2,3,4\}
.

Proof.

The first part of this lemma is just a reformulation of
[
4
, Lemma 4.7]
. Note that for all
r\geq 0

\displaystyle\varrho(r)=\frac{H^{\prime}(r)}{H(r)}\quad\text{with}\quad H(r)=\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{-r\cos x}(\sin x)^{n-1}dx

and hence

\varrho^{\prime}(r)=\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(r)}{H(r)}-\left(\frac{H^{\prime}(r)}{H(r)}\right)^{2}>0

(60)

where the inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz. As shown in the proof of
[
4
, Lemma 4.8]
,

\frac{d}{dr}\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(r)}{H(r)}>0,\quad\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(0)}{H(0)}=\frac{1}{n+1},

and since an integration by parts yields

H^{\prime}(r)=\frac{r}{n}(H(r)-H^{\prime\prime}(r))\quad\text{for all $r\geq 0$,}

(61)

we get

\frac{d}{dr}\frac{\varrho(r)}{r}=\frac{d}{dr}\left(\frac{1}{n}\left(1-\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(r)}{H(r)}\right)\right)<0,\quad\frac{\varrho(r)}{r}\xrightarrow{r\to 0}\frac{1}{n+1}.

(62)

In particular, we have

0>\frac{d}{dr}\frac{\varrho(r)}{r}=\frac{\varrho^{\prime}(r)}{r}-\frac{\varrho(r)}{r^{2}}\quad\text{for all $r>0$}

so, together with (
60
),

0<\varrho^{\prime}(r)<\frac{\varrho(r)}{r}\quad\text{for all $r>0$}.

(63)

Combining (
57
) with Lemma
2.5
, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(m)}(v)

\displaystyle=\left(\partial_{m_{i}}\left(\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}m_{j}\right)\right)_{i,j=1,\ldots,n+1}=\left(\varrho^{\prime}(|m|)-\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}\right)\bar{m}\bar{m}^{T}+\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}1_{(n+1)\times(n+1)}

and hence its largest eigenvalue is

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}x^{T}\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(m)}(v)x=\left|\varrho^{\prime}(|m|)-\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}\right|+\frac{\varrho(|m|)}{|m|}=\lambda(|m|),

where the last equality uses (
63
). Plugging (
63
) into (
58
) yields

\frac{\varrho(r)}{r}<\lambda(r)<\frac{2\varrho(r)}{r}\quad\text{for all $r>0$,}

which, in combination with (
62
), implies (
59
).
∎

3.2
Improving Theorem
1.4
in the case of weak self-attraction

The following proposition means that we can close the gap between Theorem
1.4
(for
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{S}^{n}
,
v(x)=x
and constant
\beta
) and
[
4
, Theorem 4.5 (i)]
for all cases in which the uniform distribution is the limit.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\beta\equiv b>-(n+1)
and
f\in C^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{S}^{n}\right)
. Then

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{\log\left|(\mu_{t}-\mathcal{U})(f)\right|}{\log t}\leq-\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},1+\frac{b}{n+1}\right\}\quad\text{almost surely.}

(64)

Remark 3.5
.

Note that we can technically also include the case
b=-(n+1)
in Proposition
3.4
, but then its conclusion is not useful. Indeed, the right hand side of (
64
) becomes 0 in this case, so this property is in fact weaker than what we already know from (
55
).

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

The case
b\geq 0
is already covered entirely in Example
1.8
, so we assume that

-(n+1)<b<0.

Since Proposition
2.1
implies

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left(\sup_{s\geq 0}\left|\int_{t}^{t+s}\varepsilon^{1}_{r}(f)dr\right|\right)\leq-\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{almost surely}

(see Example
1.8
), it only remains to show that

\limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\log\left\|\varepsilon^{2}_{t}\right\|\leq-\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},1-\frac{|b|}{n+1}\right\}\quad\text{almost surely},

(65)

since then the proof can be completed in the exact same way as in Section
2.3
. In order to prove (
65
), we will need to revisit and refine the arguments from Lemmas
2.6
and
2.8
(the notation of which we adapt in the sequel) and make explicit use of
[
4
, Theorem 4.5 (i)]
.

Since the second and third parts of Lemma
3.1
imply

\lim_{r\to 0}\lambda(r)=\lambda(0)=\frac{1}{n+1}<|b|^{-1},

we can choose
\vartheta>0
such that

\zeta:=\sup_{m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1},|m|\leq\vartheta}\left(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{S}^{n}}x^{T}\operatorname{Cov}_{\Pi(bm)}(v)x\right)=\sup_{r\in[0,\vartheta|b|]}\lambda(r)<|b|^{-1}.

Then with the same argument as in Lemma
2.6
we get

m\cdot\nabla J_{t}(m)\geq(1-|b|\zeta)\left|m\right|^{2}\quad\text{for all $m\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ with $|m|\leq\vartheta$.}

(66)

We already know from
[
4
, Theorem 4.5 (i)]
that almost surely
\mu_{t}\xrightarrow{w}\mathcal{U}
(see (
55
)), so
m_{t}\to 0
and hence also
\tilde{m}_{t}\to 0
(see (
34
) and Lemma
2.2
). Therefore, there is an almost surely finite random time
\tau=\tau(\vartheta)
such that
|\tilde{m}_{t}|\leq\vartheta
for all
t>\tau
, so with (
66
) we get

-\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})\leq-(1-|b|\zeta)\left|\tilde{m}_{t}\right|^{2}\quad\text{for all $t\geq\tau$.}

(67)

Also note that

-\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})\leq C\quad\text{for all $t\in[t_{0},\tau)$.}

(68)

With

0<\alpha<\min\left\{1,2-2|b|\zeta\right\},

we can show as in the proof of Lemma
2.8
that

d|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}\leq\left(-2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot\nabla J_{t}(\tilde{m}_{t})+Ce^{-\alpha t}\right)dt+2\tilde{m}_{t}\cdot dM_{t}\quad\text{for all $t\geq t_{0}$.}

(69)

If we set
\xi:=2-2|b|\zeta
, plug (
69
) into

d\left(e^{\xi t}|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}\right)=\xi e^{\xi t}|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}dt+e^{\xi t}d|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2},

and then apply (
67
) and (
68
), we can estimate

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\tilde{m}_{t}|^{2}&\leq e^{-\xi(t-t_{0})}|\tilde{m}_{t_{0}}|^{2}+C\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\xi(t-r)-\alpha r}dr+C\int_{t_{0}}^{\tau}e^{-\xi(t-r)}dr+2\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{-\xi(t-r)}\tilde{m}_{r}\cdot dM_{r}\\
&\leq Ke^{-\alpha t}+2e^{-\xi t}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}e^{\xi r}\tilde{m}_{r}\cdot dM_{r},\end{split}

where
K
is a positive, almost surely finite random number. Now, (
65
) follows from the same line of reasoning as in the last step of the proof of Lemma
2.8
and in the proof of Proposition
2.10
.
