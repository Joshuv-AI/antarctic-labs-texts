Abstract
. Weakly supervised named entity recognition methods train label models to aggregate the token annotations of multiple noisy labeling functions (LFs) without seeing any manually annotated labels. To work well, the label model needs to contextually identify and emphasize well-performed LFs while down-weighting the under-performers. However, evaluating the LFs is challenging due to the lack of ground truths. To address this issue, we propose the sparse conditional hidden Markov model (Sparse-CHMM). Instead of predicting the entire emission matrix as other HMM-based methods, Sparse-CHMM focuses on estimating its diagonal elements, which are considered as the reliability scores of the LFs. The sparse scores are then expanded to the full-fledged emission matrix with pre-defined expansion functions. We also augment the emission with weighted XOR scores, which track the probabilities of an LF observing incorrect entities. Sparse-CHMM is optimized through unsupervised learning with a three-stage training pipeline that reduces the training difficulty and prevents the model from falling into local optima. Compared with the baselines in the Wrench benchmark, Sparse-CHMM achieves a 3.01 3.01 average F 1 score improvement on 5 5 comprehensive datasets. Experiments show that each component of Sparse-CHMM is effective, and the estimated LF reliabilities strongly correlate with true LF F 1 scores.



1.
Introduction

Named entity recognition (NER), a task that identifies pre-defined named entities, is fundamental for extracting information from unstructured text data.
For example, to facilitate material synthesis, material scientists often need to extract entities such as materials, properties, and catalysts from research articles
(
Kim et al. 2017
)
.
NER also powers tasks such as question answering and knowledge graph construction.
Currently, prevailing NER methods are fully supervised models trained on a substantial amount of human-annotated data.
However, annotating sentences with named entity labels is difficult, expensive, and time-consuming
(
Boecking et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2021
)
.
The manually annotated dataset is also hard to expand—to insert new entity types or append new sentences, annotators must go over each sample, the effort of which is proportional to the data size.

Weak supervision
is proposed in recent works
(
Hoffmann et al. 2011
;
Ratner et al. 2017
)
to address this difficulty.
Instead of human annotations, weakly supervised methods generate multiple sets of
weak annotations
using labeling functions (LFs,
a.k.a.
weak supervision sources).
They can be knowledge bases, domain-specific dictionaries, pattern matching regular expressions or out-of-domain supervised models
(
Lison et al. 2020
)
.
Constructing LFs is significantly cheaper than annotating tokens manually.
And the weak annotations are expandable—the LFs can automatically annotate incoming sentences without any extra human labor.

Nonetheless, it is challenging to design
label models
which aggregate the LF annotations without seeing any ground-truth labels.
LFs are usually simple, and their annotations often suffer from incompleteness (low coverage/recall) and inaccuracy (low precision).
Moreover, the LFs can conflict with each other, generating contradictory results
(
Ratner et al. 2016
)
.
Without true labels, solving the conflicts and reducing the annotation noise become a tricky task.

Some weakly supervised NER methods such as majority voting (MV) and Snorkel
(
Ratner et al. 2017
)
classify each token in the sentence independently.
Such approaches neglect the complex inter-word relationship of natural language.
Recent works
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Safranchik et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Parker and Yu 2021
)
parameterize the label sequences with graphical models, regarding the unobserved “true labels” as latent variables and inferring them by optimizing the probabilities of the observed weak annotations through unsupervised learning.

Nguyen et al. 2017
,
Safranchik et al. 2020
and
Lison et al. 2020
apply the hidden Markov models (HMMs) to capture the token dependency with the transition matrix.
However, the Markov property constrains HMMs’ modeling of such dependency to only consecutive tokens meanwhile largely ignoring sentence semantics.
There are attempts to integrate neural networks (NNs) with graphical models
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Parker and Yu 2021
)
.

Li et al. 2021
propose the conditional HMM (CHMM) that uses BERT token embeddings
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
to predict HMMs’ transition and emission probabilities with NNs.
As each token embedding encodes the contextual semantics of the whole sentence, CHMM incorporates more comprehensive context information and alleviates the Markov constraint.
A similar approach is DWS
(
Parker and Yu 2021
)
, which substitutes HMM by the conditional random field (CRF) and predicts the latent variables instead of their transitions.
The problem with both CHMM and DWS is that they directly predict all elements in the emission matrices from the embeddings.
When the number of LFs or labels is large, doing so requires a myriad of network parameters.
As the optimization is non-convex, this creates many local optima, making the model hard to train.
Besides, the training and inference of a large NN are much more computationally demanding, which hurts model efficiency.

In this work, we propose Sparse-CHMM.
Maintaining the transition scheme, Sparse-CHMM enhances CHMM by restricting its emission process with the empirical structure of an emission matrix (Figure
1a
).
Generally, the diagonal of the emission matrix is its most essential component, which represents the reliability of the LF annotations.
Considering this fact, Sparse-CHMM first focuses on estimating the LF reliability scores instead of the entire emission matrix.
Then it expands the sparse scores to the full-fledged emission matrix by placing the scores at the diagonals and filling up other vacancies with our designed expansion functions (
section

3.2.1
).
Compared with CHMM, Sparse-CHMM features sparser emission prediction process with much fewer parameters to learn, making the model easier to optimize and more efficient to train.

To enhance Sparse-CHMM’s estimation of off-diagonal emission elements, we introduce another component—the weighted XOR (WXOR) scores (
section

3.2.2
).
The off-diagonal elements model LFs’ probabilities of generating incorrect entity annotations.
These probabilities are typically small but sometimes comparable with or larger than the diagonal elements.
In such cases, using the diagonal-oriented reliability expansion functions alone is biased and inflexible.
To address this issue, we leverage LF annotations and the predicted LF reliabilities to calculate LFs’ mutual disagreement frequencies as WXOR scores to refine off-diagonal emission elements.

Considering the amount of uncertainty in the emission, we treat the function-induced deterministic values as priors, with which a Dirichlet distribution is parameterized.
We then sample our final emission probabilities from the distribution (
section

3.2.3
).
Previous works
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
show that incorporating the probability model expands the search space, regularizes the gradient flow, and further prevents the model from being trapped by local optima.

In addition, to improve learning Sparse-CHMM’s inter-correlated components, we introduce a three-stage training strategy.
Each stage optimizes only a subset of model parameters (
section

3.3.3
) to reduce mutual interference and promote the training stability.

In summary, our contributions include:

•

proposing Sparse-CHMM to estimate LF reliability scores from sentence embeddings, and expand the scores to sparsely predicted emission matrices with our designed expansion functions;

•

modeling the probabilities of an LF generating incorrect annotations with the WXOR scores to augment the estimation of emission off-diagonal elements;

•

a three-stage training strategy and the Dirichlet sampling process to improve training stability and model robustness; and

•

thorough experiments on
5
comprehensive datasets with SOTA baselines that demonstrate the superiority of Sparse-CHMM, and ablation studies that justify the design of model components.

The code and data are available at
github.com/Yinghao-Li/Sparse-CHMM
for reproducibility.

For LF

With this concept established, we start by predicting the reliability logits

\bm{A}=\text{reshape}(\text{FC}(\bm{e}^{(0)})),

3.2.2.
Weighted XOR and Emission Addon Prior

In contrast to the diagonals
\Phi_{k,l,l}
, the off-diagonal values
\Phi_{k,i,j};i,j\geq 2;i\neq j
are the probabilities of LF
k

misclassifying
label
i
as
j
.
Such probabilities are generally insignificant.
But in some cases, they can be prominent (as the example shown in Figure
1a
) and affects the model performance.
The base prior
\bm{\Lambda}
assigns equal weights to all off-diagonals and fails to adjust to such cases.

Therefore, we develop the WXOR scores
\tilde{\bm{W}}\in[0,1]^{K\times L\times L}
to capture the misclassification probabilities.
Given the annotations of LF
k
and other LFs
k^{\prime}\in 1:K\backslash k
, a WXOR logit is specified between the query label
l^{\rm query}\in 2:L
and the target label
l^{\rm tgt}\in 2:L
:

(5)

\begin{gathered}W^{(t)}_{k,l^{\rm query},l^{\rm tgt}}=(1-\tilde{A}_{k,l^{\rm query}})x^{(t)}_{k,l^{\rm query}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{K}\tilde{A}_{k^{\prime},l^{\rm tgt}}x^{(t)}_{k^{\prime},l^{\rm tgt}};\\
\bm{W}^{(t)}_{k,1,:}=\bm{W}^{(t)}_{k,:,1}=\bm{0};\quad W^{(t)}_{k,l,l}=0,\ \forall l\in 1:L.\end{gathered}

It depicts the likelihood that LF
k
makes mistakes in its observation of
l^{\rm query}

w.r.t.
the other LFs at time-step
t
.

1-\tilde{A}_{k,l^{\rm query}}
is the
uncertainty level
of LF
k
to its observation of
l^{\rm query}
;

\tilde{A}_{k^{\prime},l^{\rm tgt}}
is the confidence of LF
k^{\prime}
to
l^{\rm tgt}
;
and the binary
x^{(t)}_{k,l}
decides whether
l
is observed.

W^{(t)}_{k,l^{\rm query},l^{\rm tgt}}
is large when LF
k
observes
l^{\rm query}
but is uncertain, while other LFs are confident about observing
l^{\rm tgt}
.
As
W^{(t)}_{k,l^{\rm query},l^{\rm tgt}}
is non-zero
i.f.f.
LF
k
does but other LFs do not observe

, it is similar to the XOR gate and hence gets its name.
Label

and the diagonal need no WXOR as these elements are well-defined in

, so the corresponding values in

are fixed to

\bm{W}^{(t)}

for
M

\hat{W}_{k,l^{\rm query},l^{\rm tgt}}=\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{m}}{W}^{(t)}_{m,k,l^{\rm query},l^{\rm tgt}}}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{m}}{x}^{(t)}_{m,k,l^{\rm query}}}.

Combining
\hat{\bm{W}}
with the emission base prior
\bm{\Lambda}
is also nontrivial.
Straightforward summation is unpractical since the relative scale of
\hat{\bm{W}}
and
\bm{\Lambda}
is obscure due to the summation in (

In regard of this, we leverage another scaling factor
\bm{C}\in(0,1)^{K\times L}
predicted from the BERT sentence embedding through one layer of NN:

\bm{C}=\text{reshape}(\sigma(\text{FC}(\bm{e}^{(0)}))).

Its domain
(0,1)
, however, enables
\bm{C}
to down-scale values but not up-scale them.
Consequently, we need to properly adjust the elements of
\hat{\bm{W}}
so that they have large enough pre-scaling values:

\tilde{\bm{W}}_{k,:,l^{\rm tgt}}=\text{SoftMax}(\hat{\bm{W}}_{k,:,l^{\rm tgt}}).

With the established matrices, we construct the
emission addon prior

\bm{\Delta}\in[0,1]^{K\times L\times L}
by re-scaling the rows of
\tilde{\bm{W}}_{k}
:

(6)

\bm{\Delta}_{k,:,l}=C_{k,l}\times\tilde{\bm{W}}_{k,l,:}.

Notice that in
\bm{\Delta}_{k}
, the target labels are at the first axis and the query labels second, different from
\tilde{\bm{W}}_{k}
.
As the target labels are essentially the latent states we want to predict, this unifies the physical meaning of
\tilde{\bm{W}}
and the emission matrix
\bm{\Phi}
.

3.2.3.
Emission Matrix Sampling

Instead of using the deterministic priors
\bm{\Lambda}
and
\bm{\Delta}
directly, we choose to sample the latent emission variables from the Dirichlet distribution parameterized by the priors.
This is a common practice in graphical models considering that proper latent distributions are more representative and robust to noise than the deterministic priors
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
;
Chen et al. 2021
)
.
The random sampling helps the model escape the saddle points or local optima.
In addition, Dirichlet distribution samples from probability simplex without requiring its concentration parameters to sum up to one, which makes the parameters selection more flexible.

To construct the concentration parameters
\bm{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K\times L\times L}
, we add the base prior and the addon prior together and scale the results:

\bm{\Omega}=\nu^{\rm expan}\times(\bm{\Lambda}+\bm{\Delta})+\nu^{\rm base}.

\nu^{\rm base}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\nu^{\rm expan}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
controls the minimum concentration value and the concentration range, respectively.
Larger
\nu_{\rm expan}
results in smaller sampling variance.
Each row of the emission matrix
\bm{\Phi}_{k}
of LF
k
is sampled independently from the distribution:

\bm{\Phi}_{k,l}\sim\text{Dir}(\bm{\Omega}_{k,l}).

We use pathwise derivative estimators developed by
Jankowiak and Obermeyer 2018
to push the gradient back through the Dirichlet.

Notice that we only apply Dirichlet sampling when it requires backpropagation.
On other occasions, such as validation and test, the samples are substituted by the mean of the Dirichlet distribution.

3.3.
Model Training

Sparse-CHMM is an unsupervised model whose optimization does not leverage any ground-truth labels
\bm{y}
but only the weak labels
\bm{x}
.
Section
3.3.1
–
3.3.3
describe Sparse-CHMM’s training strategy, whereas

3.3.4

3.3.1.
Model Pre-training

A good initialization is critical for HMMs to gain good performance.
However, as Sparse-CHMM contains several NNs, assigning proper initial values to model parameters is unrealistic.
Thus, we adopt the approach used by
Li et al. 2021
, which pre-trains the neural networks by minimizing the Euclidean distances between the predicted transitions and emissions and the initial matrices
\bm{\Psi}^{*}
and
\bm{\Phi}^{*}
that are from the observation statistics.
The objective is the mean squared error (MSE) loss:

(7)

\ell_{\rm MSE}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|\bm{\Phi}_{k}-\bm{\Phi}^{*}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\bm{\Psi}^{(t)}-\bm{\Psi}^{*}\|_{F}^{2},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.
Different from
(
Li et al. 2021
)
or
(
Lison et al. 2020
)
, we require no prior knowledge of LF performance.

3.3.2.
Training Objective

Same as CHMM
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, Sparse-CHMM is trained using the generalized EM algorithm.
Specifically, we leverage the gradient ascent method in the M step to optimize the expected complete data log likelihood computed in the E step:

(8)

Q(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\rm old})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{z}}[\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta})|\bm{x},\bm{\theta}^{\rm old}].

The expectation is defined over the hidden states given LF observations and the current model parameters
p(\bm{z}^{(1:T)}|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{\theta}^{\rm old})
.
The data log likelihood
\ell_{c}({\bm{\theta}})
is defined as:

(9)

\displaystyle\ell_{c}(\bm{\theta})\triangleq\log p(\bm{z}^{(0:T)},\bm{x}^{(1:T)}|\bm{\theta},\bm{e}^{(0:T)})=\log p(z^{(0)})+

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(z^{(t)}|z^{(t-1)},\bm{e}^{(t)})+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p(\bm{x}^{(t)}|z^{(t)},\bm{e}^{(0)}),

of which the conditional independency comes from the Markov assumption.
The computation of (
8
) largely aligns with CHMM.
One difference is the dependency of the emission evidence:

(10)

\varphi_{l}^{(t)}\triangleq p(\bm{x}^{(t)}|z^{(t)}=l,\bm{e}^{(0)})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{j=1}^{L}\Phi_{k,l,j}x_{k,j},

which in our case is sentence-level
\bm{e}^{(0)}
, whereas in CHMM is token-level
\bm{e}^{(t)}
.
This essentially is attributed to the different approaches to obtain the emission matrix
\bm{\Phi}
(
section

3.2
).
The computation of (
8
) and other forward inference details are in
appendix

A
.

3.3.3.
Training Procedure

As the computation of the emission is complicated and inter-dependent, we adopt a three-stage training strategy to allow sufficient training of each model component, as shown in Figure
5
.
It decouples the optimization of the model components while keeping the training efficient.

Stage 1 is focused on optimizing the transition matrix
\bm{\Psi}
and the emission base prior
\bm{\Lambda}
, excluding the addon prior by setting
\bm{\Delta}=\bm{0}
.
The reason is straightforward:
calculating the WXOR scores
\tilde{\bm{W}}
requires high-quality reliability scores
\tilde{\bm{A}}
, which are obtained by optimizing
\bm{\Lambda}
(
section

3.3
).
Stage 2 trains the addon prior
\bm{\Delta}
, leaving
\bm{\Psi}
and
\bm{\Lambda}
frozen.
This stage aims to search for the best scaling factors
\bm{C}
for
\tilde{\bm{W}}
, and freezing the irrelevant parameters relieves the training pressure.

\tilde{\bm{W}}
is calculated right after stage 1 with all training and validation instances and remains constant for stages 2 and 3.

Stage 3 is inspired by our empirical discovery about HMMs.
We find that if we only optimize the transition matrix
\bm{\Psi}
with the emission fixed, generally to the true values, the model performance can be improved.
The true emission is the statistics of the annotations
\bm{x}_{k}
of LF
k
given ground-truth labels
\bm{y}
:

\Phi^{\rm true}_{k,i,j}=p(\bm{x}_{k}|\bm{y})\triangleq\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{m}}\mathbb{I}(x^{(t)}_{m,k,j}=1,y^{(t)}_{m}=i)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{m}}\mathbb{I}(y^{(t)}_{m}=i)},

where
\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
is the indicator function.
Consequently, we believe that continuing training the transition of Sparse-CHMM for several more epochs with the emission frozen would lead to a similar performance improvement.

In addition, stages 1, 2, and 3 use different pre-training strategies.
The pre-training of stage 1 is the same as
section

3.3.1
.
Stage 2’s pre-training is to initialize the NN parameters associated with the addon prior
\bm{\Delta}
only, so we drop the transition part of the MSE loss and substitute the target emission by

\bm{\Phi}^{\prime}=\lambda\bm{\Phi}^{*}+\frac{(1-\lambda)}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\bm{\Phi}_{m}^{(I)}

to take advantage of stage 1’s results.
Here
\lambda\in[0,1]
is the weight of observation statistics, which is fixed to
0.2
in our experiments.

\bm{\Phi}_{m}^{(I)}
is the optimized emission from stage 1.
Stage 3 successes all model parameters from the previous stage and thus has no pre-training.

Related Works

Weak supervision is a widely-investigated approach for natural language processing tasks such as sentence classification
(
Ratner et al. 2016
;
Ratner et al. 2017
;
Ren et al. 2020
)
and NER
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Shang et al. 2018
;
Safranchik et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2020
;
Lan et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2021
;
Parker and Yu 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
)
.
It substitutes the laborious manual annotation process with multiple simple labeling functions, relieving the human effort to annotate large training data.
However, the LFs are generally noisy and controversial, which makes their utilization challenging.
Consequently, the primary track of weak supervision focuses on designing a robust label model that aggregates the weak annotations by de-noising them and resolving the conflict
(
Ratner et al. 2016
;
Ratner et al. 2017
;
Ratner et al. 2019
;
Ren et al. 2020
;
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Shang et al. 2018
;
Safranchik et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2020
;
Lan et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2021
;
Parker and Yu 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
)
.
The hidden Markov model with multiple independent observation sequences is principled and popular for this task
(
Nguyen et al. 2017
;
Safranchik et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2021
;
Parker and Yu 2021
;
Li et al. 2021
)
.
Transferring from sequence classification to sequence labeling (
a.k.a.
, token classification), it extends the naïve Bayes generative model proposed by
Ratner et al. 2016
by incorporating the chronological dependency relationship through the transition matrix.
The weak labels from different LFs are considered as multiple sets of observations that are conditionally independent given the latent variables.
Among these works,
Nguyen et al. 2017
and
Lison et al. 2020
;
Lison et al. 2021
use conventional HMMs.

Safranchik et al. 2020
design the linked HMM, which uses linking rules to model whether consecutive tokens belong to the same entity.
Other works
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Parker and Yu 2021
)
improve HMM’s context representation with BERT embeddings and alleviate the Markov assumption.

In the data programming framework, people also train
end models
, which are deep supervised neural networks, with the label model outputs in seek of refining the results with the power of deep networks.
One exception is the Consensus Network
(
Lan et al. 2020
)
that trains the label model and the end model jointly within a two-stage framework
(
Zhang et al. 2021
)
.
Another is ALT
(
Li et al. 2021
)
, which treats the end model as an additional LF and optimizes it alternately with the label model.

Zhang et al. 2021
, however, show that the end model does not necessarily outperform the label model.
Therefore, we focus on the label models here and leave end models to future works.

Our work is also related to the neuralized graphical models.
Many efforts seek to inject neural networks into graphical models, especially for variational inference, such as the variational autoencoder (VAE)
(
Kingma and Welling 2014
)
.
Other works include
(
Tran et al. 2016
)
, which neuralizes the HMM with one observation set for the unsupervised part-of-speech tagging.

Dai et al. 2017
and
Liu et al. 2018
incorporate recurrent units into the hidden semi-Markov model to segment and label high-dimensional time series;

Wiseman et al. 2018
learn discrete template structures for conditional text generation also with neuralized graphical models.
CHMM and DWS also fall into this category by predicting graphical models components through NNs.

6.
Conclusion

We presented Sparse-CHMM, a label model that aggregates the weak annotations from multiple noisy NER labeling functions.
In contrast to CHMM, Sparse-CHMM constructs the sentence-level sparse emission probabilities from the LF reliabilities predicted using the BERT sentence embeddings.
The off-diagonal emission elements are further augmented by the weighted XOR scores, which estimate the possibilities of an LF observing incorrect entity labels.
Wrapped in a three-stage training process with Dirichlet sampling, Sparse-CHMM outperforms all baseline label models on five representative datasets with different statistics and attributes.
In addition, the approximated LF reliability scores strongly correlate with the true LF performance, making it eligible to contribute to other tasks such as automated LF generation and evaluation.
In the future, we will consider leveraging prior heuristic knowledge to strengthen Sparse-CHMM’s ability to distill clean NER labels from noisy LF annotations.
In addition, we also plan to extend this technique to other sequence labeling tasks.

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle(\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\rm old})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}p(z^{(0)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)})\log p(z^{(0)}=i)+

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}p(z^{(t-1)}=i,z^{(t)}=j|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)})\log\Psi^{(t)}_{i,j}+

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=1}^{L}p(z^{(t)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)})\log\varphi^{(t)}_{i},

where
\bm{\Psi}
is the transition matrix and
\varphi_{i}^{(t)}
is defined in (
10
).

p(z^{(0)})
is the probability of the initial hidden state without any corresponding observations.
As we can predict the token-wise transition matrix from the embeddings, we can simply set it to Uniform or, as
Li et al. 2021
proposed, set
p(z^{(0)}=1)
to
1
and
p(z^{(0)}=i),\forall i\in 2:L
to
0
.

To calculate (
11
), we define the smoothed marginal
\bm{\gamma}^{(t)}\in[0,1]^{L}
as:

\gamma^{(t)}_{i}\triangleq p(z^{(t)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)}),

and the expected number of transitions
\bm{\xi}^{(t)}\in[0,1]^{L\times L}
as:

\xi^{(t)}_{i,j}\triangleq p(z^{(t-1)}=i,z^{(t)}=j|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)}).

These two variables are acquired using the
forward-backward
algorithm.

First, we define the filtered marginal
\bm{\alpha}\in[0,1]^{L}
as:

\alpha^{(t)}_{i}\triangleq p(z^{(t)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:t)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)}),

and the conditional future evidence
\bm{\beta}\in[0,1]^{L}
as:

\beta^{(t)}_{i}\triangleq p(\bm{x}^{(t+1:T)}|z^{(t)}=i,\bm{e}^{(0:T)}).

In the forward pass,
\alpha^{(t)}_{i}
is computed iteratively:

\displaystyle\alpha^{(t)}_{i}

\displaystyle\propto p(\bm{x}^{(t)}|z^{(t)}=i,\bm{e}^{(0)})p(z^{(t)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:t-1)},\bm{e}^{(0:t)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\varphi^{(t)}_{i}\Psi^{(t)}_{j,i}\alpha^{(t-1)}_{j},

which can be written in the matrix form:

\bm{\alpha}^{(t)}\propto\bm{\varphi}^{(t)}\odot({\bm{\Psi}^{(t)}}^{\sf T}\bm{\alpha}^{(t-1)}),

where
\odot
is the element-wise product.
We initialize
\bm{\alpha}
with
\alpha^{(0)}_{l}=p(z^{(0)}=l),\forall l\in 1:L
since we have no observation at time step
0
.

Similarly, we do the backward pass and compute
\bm{\beta}
:

\displaystyle\beta^{(t-1)}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}p(z^{(t)}=j,\bm{x}^{(t)},\bm{x}^{(t+1:T)}|z^{(t-1)}=i,\bm{e}^{(0,t:T)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\beta^{(t)}_{j}\varphi^{(t)}_{j}\Psi^{(t)}_{i,j}.

In the matrix form, it becomes:

\bm{\beta}^{(t-1)}=\bm{\Psi}^{(t)}(\bm{\varphi}^{(t)}\odot\bm{\beta}^{(t)}),

with base case:

\beta^{(T)}_{i}=p(\bm{x}^{(T+1:T)}|z^{(T)}=i)=1,\forall i\in 1:L.

With
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
calculated,
\gamma^{(t)}_{i}
and
\xi^{(t)}_{i,j}
can be written as:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \gamma^{(t)}_{i}&\propto p(z^{(t)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:t)},\bm{e}^{0:t})p(\bm{x}^{(t+1:T)}|z^{(t)}=i,\bm{e}^{(0,t+1:T)})\\
&=\alpha^{(t)}_{i}\beta^{(t)}_{i},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \xi^{(t)}_{i,j}&\propto p(z^{(t-1)}=i|\bm{x}^{(1:t-1)}\bm{e}^{(0:t-1)})p(\bm{x}^{(t)}|z^{(t)}=j,\bm{e}^{(0)})\\
&\quad\ p(\bm{x}^{(t+1:T)}|z^{(t)}=j,\bm{e}^{(0,t+1:T)})p(z^{(t)}=j|z^{(t-1)}=i,\bm{e}^{(t)})\\
&=\alpha^{(t-1)}_{i}\varphi^{(t)}_{j}\beta^{(t)}_{j}\Psi^{(t)}_{i,j}.\end{aligned}

Written in the matrix form, they become:

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}^{(t)}\propto\bm{\alpha}^{(t)}\odot\bm{\beta}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\bm{\xi}^{(t)}\propto\bm{\Psi}^{(t)}\odot(\bm{\alpha}^{(t-1)}(\bm{\varphi}^{(t)}\odot\bm{\beta}^{(t)})^{\sf T}).

Eventually, we insert
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\xi}
into (
11
) to compute the value of
Q
.
The training objective is to maximize
Q
, which can be readily done using the gradient ascend.
Please refer to
(
Li et al. 2021
)
for more details.

A.2.
Inference

Same as
Li et al. 2021
, we use the Viterbi algorithm to find the sequence of latent variables
\hat{\bm{z}}^{(1:T)}
that maximize the posterior:

\hat{\bm{z}}^{(1:T)}=\argmax_{\bm{z}^{(1:T)}}p(\bm{z}^{(1:T)}|\bm{x}^{(1:T)},\bm{e}^{(0:T)}).

This sequence of latent variables
\hat{\bm{z}}^{(1:T)}
is considered as Sparse-CHMM’s approximation of the true labels
\bm{y}
.

Appendix D
Function design criteria

Here we introduce the design criteria for the reliability scaling function (
2
) and the reliability expansion function (
4
).
There are infinite solutions that meet the criteria.
We select the simplest polynomials for better interpretability and calculation efficiency.

D.1.
Reliability Scaling Function

Function
f_{n,s,r}
, illustrated in Figure
3
, is 1) continuous, smooth and monotonic; 2) passing through coordinates
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
; and 3) having zero gradient at
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
.
It is designed such that its shape is controllable without making the function complicated.

D.2.
Reliability Expansion Function

As shown in Figure
4
,
g_{n,r}
is 1) continuous, smooth and monotonic; 2) passing through
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
; and 3)
\nabla_{a}g(a)|_{a=0}=\frac{1}{L-1}
.
We want the emission from non-
O
latent state to non-
O
observation
\bm{\Lambda}_{k,i,j\geq 2}
to be close to Uniform when
\tilde{A}_{k,i}
is small, which indicates that LF
k
may equally observe anything when the latent label is
i
.
This builds the constraint of
\nabla_{a}\frac{1-a-g(a)}{L-2}|_{a=0}=\nabla_{a}a|_{a=0}=1
, which leads to the third feature defined above.
The hyperparameter
r
controls the threshold where we trust LF
k
enough to stop increasing the off-diagonal emissions.
