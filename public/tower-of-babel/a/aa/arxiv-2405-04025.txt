A Unified Post-Processing Framework for Group Fairness in Classification
Ruicheng Xian, Han Zhao

Abstract
We present a post-processing algorithm for fair classification that covers group fairness criteria including statistical parity, equal opportunity, and equalized odds under a single framework, and is applicable to multiclass problems in both attribute-aware and attribute-blind settings. Our algorithm, called ‘‘LinearPost’’, achieves fairness post-hoc by linearly transforming the predictions of the (unfair) base predictor with a ‘‘fairness risk’’ according to a weighted combination of the (predicted) group memberships. It yields the Bayes optimal fair classifier if the base predictors being post-processed are Bayes optimal, otherwise, the resulting classifier may not be optimal, but fairness is guaranteed as long as the group membership predictor is multicalibrated. The parameters of the post-processing can be efficiently computed and estimated from solving an empirical linear program. Empirical evaluations demonstrate the advantage of our algorithm in the high fairness regime compared to existing post-processing and in-processing fair classification algorithms. 1 1 1 Our code is available at https://github.com/uiuctml/fair-classification .

Introduction

Algorithmic fairness is an important consideration in machine learning due to concerns that models may propagate social biases present in the training data against historically disadvantaged demographics
(
Barocas et al., 2023
)
, especially when involving sensitive domains such as criminal justice, healthcare, and finance
(
Barocas and Selbst, 2016
;
Berk et al., 2021
)
. Work on this topic includes evaluation of unfairness, design of fair learning algorithms, and analysis of the inherent limitations in achieving fairness
(
Kleinberg et al., 2017
;
zhao2022inherent
)
. To evaluate model unfairness,
group fairness
metrics group individuals by pre-specified attributes (called
sensitive attributes
, such as gender, race, etc.) and compute the difference between the aggregate statistics of the model output conditioned on the groups. Popular group fairness metrics include
statistical parity
(SP;
Calders et al., 2009
),
equal opportunity
(EOpp), and
equalized odds
(EO;
Hardt et al., 2016
); they differ in their specification of groups.

Fair learning algorithms can be broadly categorized into pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing approaches, depending on where bias mitigation takes place in the training pipeline. Pre-processing is applied to the (apparent) source of unfairness—the training data—by removing biased associations via data cleaning or reweighting techniques
(
Kamiran and Calders, 2012
;
Calmon et al., 2017
)
; however, debiasing training data does not generally guarantee fairness for models trained on them. In-processing modifies the model training objective to incorporate the fairness constraints, but necessitates specialized optimization procedures
(
zemel2013LearningFairRepresentations
;
zafar2017FairnessConstraintsMechanisms
;
Agarwal et al., 2018
)
. Post-processing
(
xian2023FairOptimalClassification
;
Chzhen et al., 2020
;
Le Gouic et al., 2020
)
is performed after the model is trained, by remapping model outputs to satisfy fairness (the remapping takes simpler form and is easier to train than the backbone model; in our case, it is a linear classifier on top of model outputs). These approaches are not mutually exclusive and could be applied altogether, but options may be limited by the computational budget, or when parts of the training pipeline cannot be modified.

Among the three approaches above, post-processing is the most flexible and computationally lightweight as it is post-hoc, model-agnostic, and incurs minimal overhead. It is applicable to scenarios where the decision to perform bias mitigation is to be made after training and evaluating an initial iteration of the model, or if (re-)training subject to fairness constraints is expensive or not possible, such as when operating under the “model as a service” paradigm that utilizes large foundation models
(
Sun et al., 2022
;
Gan et al., 2023
)
. Post-processing does not compromise on performance in the sense that it yields the Bayes optimal fair classifier when the predictors being post-processed are Bayes optimal score functions
(
zhao2022inherent
;
xian2023FairOptimalClassification
)
(which is not out of reach given the ability to train large models
(
Ji et al., 2021
)
).
2
2

2

We make the distinction between a score function and a classifier: the outputs of the former are probability estimates (scores, e.g., values in
[0,1]
for binary classification), and the latter are class labels (e.g.,
\{0,1\}
).
Access to Bayes scores may appear to be a strong requirement for good post-processing performance. In particular, it is shown that if the hypothesis class
\mathcal{F}
is restricted and does not contain the Bayes scores,
woodworth2017LearningNonDiscriminatoryPredictors
construct problem instances where post-processing underperforms in-processing. But the empirical study of
Cruz and Hardt (2024)
on real-world data shows post-processing achieving better accuracy-fairness tradeoffs, suggesting that predictors learned in practice using off-the-shelf algorithms are adequate for post-processing. Moreover,
Globus-Harris et al. (2023)
show that post-processing performance matches in-processing as long as the base predictor satisfies a notion of
multicalibration
with respect to
\mathcal{F}

(
Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018
;
Gopalan et al., 2022
)
.

There is already a rich literature on post-processing algorithms for fair classification, but they are often stylized to specific fairness criteria and simplified problem settings, such as binary instead of multiclass, and
attribute-aware
instead of
attribute-blind
(i.e., whether the sensitive attribute is observed at test time). We review some existing algorithms in
Section

1.2
; notably among them,
Chen et al. (2024)
recently propose a framework applicable to group fairness criteria covering SP, EOpp, and EO under both attribute-aware and blind settings, despite limited to binary classification.

Furthering this existing line of work, in this paper, we present a unified post-processing framework for fair classification that covers fairness criteria expressed as the difference of first-order group-conditional moments of the classifier output (
Definition

1.1
, which covers SP, EOpp, and EO), as well as all problem settings mentioned above (with attribute-blind and multiclass being the most general). Our proposed algorithm maps the outputs of the base predictor (score functions) to class labels satisfying the required fairness criteria.

The paper is organized as follows. We provide problem setup and definitions, and summarize our main results in
Section

1.1
, followed by related work in
Section

1.2
. Then in
Section

2
, we show that the optimal fair classifier can be expressed as a linear post-processing of Bayes optimal predictors (without fairness constraints). Based on this, we present a fair post-processing algorithm in
Section

3
, and discuss best practices and how to instantiate it to SP, EOpp, and EO in attribute-aware and blind settings. The sample complexity is analyzed, as well as the excess risk and unfairness of the classifier returned by our algorithm when the base predictors are not optimal. We close by evaluating our algorithm on several datasets in
Section

4
and comparing to existing in-processing and post-processing algorithms.

1.1
Preliminaries and Main Results

A classification problem is defined by a joint distribution over input features
X\in\mathcal{X}
, labels
Y\in\mathcal{Y}
(categorical), and group membership indicators
Z\in\{0,1\}^{K}
for a set of
K
groups, where
Z_{k}=1
represents membership to group
k
(the groups can be overlapping, that is, more than one group indicators can be active at a time). Uppercase letters refer to random variables.

Let
r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
denote the non-negative
pointwise risk
, where
r(x,y^{\prime})
is the risk of predicting class
y^{\prime}
on input
x
(we may occasionally override notation and write
r(x)\equiv r(x,\cdot)\in[0,\infty)^{\mathcal{Y}}
). The overall risk of a classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is then
R(h)=\E[r(X,h(X))]
. If the risk is computed with a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, then the pointwise risk is
r(x,y^{\prime})=\E[\ell(y^{\prime},Y)\mid X=x]
. For example, when
\ell
is the 0-1 loss, then
r(x,y^{\prime})
is the (average) classification error on input
x
from predicting
y^{\prime}
:

r(x,y^{\prime})=\E[\1[Y\neq y^{\prime}]\mid X=x]=\Pr(Y\neq y^{\prime}\mid X=x)=1-\Pr(Y=y^{\prime}\mid X=x).

(2)

Evaluating the pointwise risk requires the conditional distribution of
\Pr(Y\mid X)
—called the
Bayes optimal score function
—which is usually unknown and needs to be learned from the data, such as by fitting a predictor on labeled examples (without fairness constraints).

The Bayes optimal classifier, which achieves the minimum risk, is given by

x\mapsto\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r(x,y).

(3)

However, this classifier may be unfair when biases are present in the underlying distribution (or the training data on which an
\hat{r}
is learned), so instead we want to find the (potentially randomized) Bayes optimal fair classifier.
We consider fairness criteria that can be expressed as the difference of first-order group-conditional moments of the classifier output:

Definition 1.1

(Group Fairness)
.

Let the collection of fairness constraints be given in the form of
\mathcal{C}=\{(y_{1},\mathcal{I}_{1}),(y_{2},\mathcal{I}_{2}),\dots,(y_{C},\mathcal{I}_{C})\}
, where each set of constraints
(y_{c},\mathcal{I}_{c})
specifies a subset of (indices of) the group indicators
\mathcal{I}_{c}\subseteq[K]\coloneqq\{1,\dots,K\}
over which parity in output class
y_{c}\in\mathcal{Y}
is evaluated/to be required.

For tolerance
\alpha\in[0,1]
, a (randomized) classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
satisfies
\alpha
-group fairness with respect to
\mathcal{C}
if

\displaystyle\max_{k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\left|\Pr(h(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1)-\Pr(h(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k^{\prime}}=1)\right|\leq\alpha,\quad\forall c\in[C],

(4)

that is, the fraction of individuals belonging to group
k
on which
h
predicts
y_{c}
differs from that of group
k^{\prime}
by no more than
\alpha
, for all
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
.

.

Definition

1.1
covers the majority of commonly used group fairness criteria. Let
A\in\mathcal{A}
denote the (categorical) sensitive attribute, which may represent gender, or race, etc. (to be distinguished from the generalized notion of group
Z

For instance, statistical parity,

\displaystyle\max_{a,a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}\left|\Pr(h(X)=y\mid A=a)-\Pr(h(X)=y\mid A=a^{\prime})\right|\leq\alpha,\quad\forall y\in\mathcal{Y},

(5)

is recovered by choosing
\mathcal{C}=\{(y,\mathcal{A}):y\in\mathcal{Y}\}
with
Z_{a}=\1[A=a]
—indicators for

. Equal opportunity and equalized odds, which are more complex, are recovered with group indicators of the form
Z_{a,y}=\1[A=a,Y=y]
: see
Table

1
. However,
Definition

1.1
cannot recover the criterion of predictive parity (
Chouldechova, 2017
; also considered as group-wise calibration),
Y\perp A\mid h(X)
, because here the classifier output

is in the conditioning part.

Definition

1.1
naturally handles overlapping groups and allows combining different fairness criteria.

Although, some criteria may not be compatible with each other. For example, generally, exact SP and EO is only simultaneously achieved by constant classifiers
(
Kleinberg et al., 2017
)
.

Main Results.

Define the Bayes optimal group membership predictor
g:\mathcal{X}\times[K]\rightarrow[0,1]
by

g(x,k)=\Pr(Z_{k}=1\mid X=x);

(6)

we may occasionally override notation and write
g(x)\equiv g(x,\cdot)\in[0,1]^{K}
(or the
(K-1)
-dimensional simplex if each input belongs to exactly one group, which is the case for the fairness criteria in
Table

1
).

We show in
Theorem

2.1
that, under a uniqueness assumption that can be satisfied via random perturbation, the Bayes optimal fair classifier—which achieves the minimal risk while satisfying the fairness constraints—takes the form of

x\mapsto\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\!\Bigg(r(x,y)+\underbrace{\sum_{k\in[K]}g(x,k)w(y,k)}_{\text{fairness risk}}\Bigg)

(7)

for some weights
w
depending on
\mathcal{C}
and
\alpha
. This shows that the Bayes optimal fair classifier can be obtained from post-processing the Bayes optimal score: by offsetting the pointwise risk
r
(which is a function of the score) with an additive “fairness risk” (similar in concept to the
bias score
of
(
Chen et al., 2024
)
).
As a sanity check, when
w=0
, corresponding to not performing fair post-processing, it recovers the Bayes optimal classifier in
3
. With post-processing, the weights
w(y,k)
have values such that they discount the risk for individuals belonging to group
k
’s that would otherwise have a low rate of being labeled class
y
to increase the rate, and vice versa.

Equation

7
reveals two ingredients needed to perform fair post-processing: the group membership predictor
g
, and the appropriate weights
w
. For SP in the attribute-aware setting,
g
is given as the one-hot indicator for the explicitly provided sensitive attribute
A
, but in the attribute-blind setting, and for EOpp and EO where the group definition involves the label
Y
which is not observed during test time, the group membership predictor needs to be learned. As for the weights, we will show that they are given (and can be estimated from finite samples) by the optimal dual values of a linear program (
LP
) representing the (empirical) fair post-processing problem.

Using these results, in
Section

3
, we propose a fair classification algorithm based on post-processing. The algorithm first estimates the pointwise risk
\hat{r}
and group predictor
\hat{g}
without fairness constraints using off-the-shelf learning algorithms—as plugins of the optimal
r,g
—then the weights
\hat{w}
of the post-processing, and returns a classifier
\hat{h}
of the same form of
7
but in terms of
(\hat{r},\hat{g},\hat{w})
. The excess risk and unfairness of
\hat{h}
due to approximation and estimation error are analyzed in
Sections

3.2
and
3.3
: notably, its unfairness is upper bounded by the multicalibration error of the predictor, and its excess risk by the
L^{1}
distance of
\hat{r}
from the optimal
r
. The excess risk is also more sensitive to the distance of
\hat{r}
from
r
when the fairness tolerance
\alpha
is set to small values, providing another explanation for empirical observations that the post-processed classifier’s accuracy tends to drop more rapidly in the high fairness regime, in addition to the inherent tradeoffs.

1.2
Related Work

This work considers achieving group fairness on classification problems with respect to fairness criteria that can be expressed as the difference of first-order group-conditional moments of the classifier output (
Definition

1.1
), including SP, EOpp, and EO.
There are other group-level criteria such as predictive parity
(
Chouldechova, 2017
)
and ones defined using higher-order moments
(
woodworth2017LearningNonDiscriminatoryPredictors
)
, as well as individual-level metrics that look at how much model output varies on “similar” individuals
(
Dwork et al., 2012
)
. Further,
Hébert-Johnson et al. (2018)
and
Kearns et al. (2018)
consider the scenario where the groups are not pre-specified but only known to be generated from a class.
4
4

4

That is, the indicators (or, in the attribute-blind setting, the Bayes optimal predictors) for the (unknown) groups of interest are assumed to be contained in a known (structured/finite) hypothesis class.

In-Processing.

Among in-processing algorithms for fair classification, two commonly used are fair representation learning
(
zemel2013LearningFairRepresentations
;
zhao2020ConditionalLearningFair
)
and the reductions approach
(
Agarwal et al., 2018
;
yang2020FairnessOverlappingGroups
)
. By training multilayer neural networks, fair representation learning algorithms learn feature representations at an intermediate layer that are distributionally invariant when conditioned on the groups (e.g., via adversarial training;
zhang2018MitigatingUnwantedBiases
), which is sufficient for satisfying the fairness constraint at the output layer. The reduction approach analyzes the Lagrangian of the fair classification problem and turns it into a two-player game where the goal is to find the equilibrium; the algorithm returns a randomized ensemble of classifiers.

Post-Processing.

Most existing post-processing algorithms for fair classification are stylized to specific fairness criteria and problem settings. The algorithms of
Jiang et al. (2020)
,
Gaucher et al. (2023)
,
Denis et al. (2024)
, and
xian2023FairOptimalClassification
only support the SP criterion, whereas those of
Hardt et al. (2016)
and
Chzhen et al. (2019)
only support EOpp and EO in the attribute-aware setting. Attribute-awareness is also required by the algorithms of
Menon and Williamson (2018)
,
Li et al. (2023)
, and
Globus-Harris et al. (2023)
.
Chen et al. (2024)
and
zeng2024BayesOptimalFairClassificationa
only consider binary classification.

Fair post-processing algorithms typically map the outputs of the base predictor directly to class labels.
Alghamdi et al. (2022)
and
˙ (2024)
instead propose algorithms that output fair scores rather than class labels, via projecting the base predictor’s output scores to satisfy fairness while minimizing the divergence between the projected and the original scores (e.g., KL divergence). This objective can be shown to maximize the Brier score of the post-processed predictor
(
wei2020OptimizedScoreTransformation
)
, but it may not align with performance metrics such as accuracy.

2
Optimal Fair Classifier as a Post-Processing

Given pointwise risk
r:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
(e.g.,
2
), Bayes optimal group predictor
g:\mathcal{X}\times[K]\rightarrow[0,1]
(see
6
), fairness constraints
\mathcal{C}
and tolerance
\alpha
(
Definition

1.1
), we want to compute a (randomized) classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
that is optimal for the problem of

\min_{h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}}\E[r(X,h(X))]\quad\textrm{subject to}\quad\textrm{$\alpha$-group fairness w.r.t.\ $\mathcal{C}$}.

(8)

This problem always has a (trivial) solution: any constant classifier (
Proposition

B.1
).

We show that, under a problem-dependent condition that can always be satisfied by randomly perturbing the pointwise risk (
Proposition

2.3
; to be discussed in
Section

2.2
), the Bayes optimal fair classifier can be represented as a post-processing of
r
(
Assumption

2.2
and
Dual LP
will be presented in the next section):

Theorem 2.1

(Representation)
.

Under
Assumption

2.2
, a minimizer of
8
is given by

h(x)=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\!\Bigg(r(x,y)+\sum_{k\in[K]}g(x,k)w(y,k)\Bigg),\quad w(y,k)=-\sum_{c\in[C]}\1[y_{c}=y,k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}]\frac{\psi_{c,k}}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}

(9)

(break ties to the minimizing class with the smallest index
y
), where
\{\psi_{c,k}:c\in[C],k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}\}
is a maximizer of
\hyperlink{eq:dual}{\textnormal{Dual LP}}(r,g,\mathbb{P}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
.

As discussed earlier on
7
, this classifier adjusts the scores computed by
r(x)
with an offset that can be interpreted as a “fairness risk”, then outputs the class with the minimum risk. We can view the post-processing as a linear classifier on the outputs of
r
and
g
as features,
(r(x),g(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}\times K}
; this linear classifier has coefficients
(\mathbf{e}_{y^{\prime}},w(y^{\prime},\cdot))
for each output class
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{y^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
is the one-hot vector for
y^{\prime}
.

In subsequent
Sections

2.1
and
2.2
, we derive the form of the optimal fair classifier in
Theorem

2.1
, and discuss
Assumption

2.2
and how to satisfy it via random perturbation. Then in
Section

3
, using these results, we propose a fair classification algorithm based on post-processing, describe how to efficiently estimate the weights
\hat{w}
from finite samples, and analyze the sample complexity as well as the error from using inaccurate
\hat{r},\hat{g}
in practice as plugins for
r,g
.

2.1
Deriving the Optimal Fair Classifier

The representation result for the optimal fair classifier in
Theorem

2.1
follows from formulating and analyzing
8
as a linear program in terms of the pointwise risk
r
and the group predictor
g
.

First, we represent the randomized classifier
h:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
in tabular form by a lookup table (a row-stochastic matrix),
\pi\in[0,1]^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}}
, where row
x
is the output distribution of
h
given
x
, namely,

\pi(x,y)\equiv\Pr(h(X)=y\mid X=x).

(10)

Then, we can express both the objective and constraints of
8
as inner products between
\pi
and
r,g
, respectively, leading to a linear program with
|\mathcal{X}||\mathcal{Y}|+C
variables and
|\mathcal{X}|+\sum_{c}|\mathcal{I}_{c}|
constraints:

Primal LP:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathclap{\pi\geq 0,q}}\;\;

\displaystyle\E_{X}\!\Bigg[\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r(X,y)\pi(X,y)\Bigg]

subject to

\displaystyle\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi(x,y)=1,

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},

\displaystyle\left|\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\pi(X,y_{c})\right]-q_{c}\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2},

\displaystyle\forall c\in[C],\,k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}.

The first set of constraints is for the row-stochasticity of
\pi
, and the second is the fairness constraint for
\alpha
-group fairness with respect to
\mathcal{C}
.
The variable
q_{c}
’s represent the centroids of the group-conditional probabilities for predicting class
y_{c}
, and are introduced to replace the pairwise comparisons (polynomial in
|\mathcal{I}_{c}|
in total) with a linear number of comparisons to the centroid, since
|u_{i}-u_{j}|\leq\alpha
,
\forall i,j
, if and only if
\exists q
s.t.
|u_{i}-q|\leq\alpha/2
,
\forall i
.
Then the equivalence between the second constraint to group fairness follows directly from Bayes’ rule: by
6
and
10
,

\displaystyle\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\pi(X,y_{c})\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Pr(X=x\mid Z_{k}=1)\Pr(h(X)=y_{c}\mid X=x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Pr(X=x\mid Z_{k}=1)\Pr(h(X)=y_{c}\mid X=x,Z_{k}=1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\Pr(h(X)=y_{c},X=x\mid Z_{k}=1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

(13)

\displaystyle=\Pr(h(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1);

(14)

the second equality is because the distribution of
h(X)
is fully determined given
X
. The equivalence for the objective follows similarly.

The proof of
Theorem

2.1
requires analyzing the dual problem of
Primal LP
(derivation deferred to
Appendix

A
, which follows similar steps in deriving the dual of the Kantorovich optimal transport problem):

Dual LP:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathclap{\phi,\psi}}\;\;

\displaystyle\E_{X}[\phi(X)]-\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{c\in[C]}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}|\psi_{c,k}|

subject to

\displaystyle\phi(x)+\sum_{c:y_{c}=y}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}g(x,k)\frac{\psi_{c,k}}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\leq r(x,y),

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\,y\in\mathcal{Y},

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\psi_{c,k}=0,

\displaystyle\forall c\in[C].

Proof of
Theorem

2.1
.

Let
(\pi,q)
be a minimizer of
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{Primal LP}}(r,g,\mathbb{P}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
and
(\phi,\psi)
its corresponding dual values (maximizer of
Dual LP
).
By definition of
LP
and the problem
8
it represents, the randomized classifier that outputs
y
on each

with probability
\propto\pi(x,y)
is an optimal classifier satisfying
\alpha
-group fairness with respect to
\mathcal{C}
, and its risk is the optimal value of
LP
.

Define

r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)=r(x,y)+\sum_{k\in[K]}g(x,k)w(y,k),\quad w(y,k)=-\sum_{c\in[C]}\1[y_{c}=y,k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}]\frac{\psi_{c,k}}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)},

(15)

then the first constraint of
Dual LP
reads
\phi(x)-r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)\leq 0
for all
x,y
. By complementary slackness,
\pi(x,y)>0\iff\phi(x)-r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)=0

(
Papadimitriou and
Steiglitz, 1998
)
, and it follows that

\pi(x,y)>0\implies y\in\argmin_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y^{\prime}).

(16)

Suppose not, then
\exists y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
y^{\prime}\neq y
, such that
r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y^{\prime})-r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)<0
. By the first constraint in
Dual LP
, we know that
\phi(x)-r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y^{\prime})\leq 0
. Adding the two inequalities together, we get
\phi(x)-r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)<0
, which contradicts complementary slackness.

The function on the right-hand side of
16
, which has a simple form as a linear post-processing of
r
and
g
, is what we want to show to represent the optimal fair classifier.

Equation

16
says that if
\pi
—the minimizer of
Primal LP
that represents the (randomized) optimal fair classifier in tabular form—has a non-zero probability of predicting class
y
on input
x
, then
y
is among the “best” classes that minimize
r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,\cdot)
.
When
\pi
randomizes its prediction among multiple classes on
x
, then there will be multiple classes that simultaneously minimize
r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,\cdot)
, and in this case the
\argmin
should break ties randomly according to
\pi(x,\cdot)
. But because the values of
\pi
cannot be recovered from
r_{\mathrm{fair}}
alone, we do not know the distribution for randomizing the classes in cases of ties, so the r.h.s. of
16
does not generally contain enough information to represent
\pi
.

Yet, the r.h.s. of
16
is able to represent the optimal fair classifier
\pi
if the “best” class is always unique
\forall x
, since there will be no ambiguity in
\argmin
, and the relation “
\Longleftrightarrow
” will hold in
16
.
We state this as an assumption for now—whether it holds or not depends on the data distribution and the fairness constraints (
Remark

2.4
)—and in the next section we show that this assumption can always be satisfied by randomly perturbing the pointwise risk
r
(with incurring a small risk). This can be thought of as a way to inject randomness into an otherwise deterministic classifier, and is a standard practice as it is well-known that there are problem instances where (exact) group fairness is non-trivially achievable only by randomized classifiers.

Assumption 2.2

(Uniqueness of Best Class)
.

Given
r
and
g
, for all
w
, the set of minimizers
|\argmin_{y}\lparen r(X,y)-\sum_{k}g(X,k)w(y,k)\rparen|=1
almost surely with respect to
X\sim\Pr_{X}
(i.e., the set has cardinality one, which means that the minimizer is almost always unique).

By
Assumption

2.2
, we have
|\argmin_{y}r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)|=1
(almost surely), which together with
16
imply that
x\mapsto\argmin_{y}r_{\mathrm{fair}}(x,y)
(break any possible ties to the smallest index) agrees with
\pi
almost everywhere, and is therefore an optimal fair classifier.
∎

2.2
Satisfying Assumption
2.2
via Random Perturbation

Assumption

2.2

to represent the optimal fair classifier as a post-processing, can be satisfied by randomly perturbing the pointwise risk

(proof is deferred to

.

Let

denote an independent random variable with a continuous distribution and independent coordinates, then

is satisfied for

with respect to the joint distribution of

This random perturbation strategy doubles as a natural and straightforward way of injecting randomness into the classifier under our construction (and also appeared in
(
xian2023FairOptimalClassification

). Note that randomization is generally required to achieve (exact) group fairness with non-trivial classifier accuracy
(

;
woodworth2017LearningNonDiscriminatoryPredictors
)
; for instance, the reductions in-processing approach returns a randomized ensemble of classifiers
(

)
.

We use it in our algorithm in

and
4
to inject randomness into the otherwise deterministic post-processed classifiers

in the form of

2.1
, so that their outputs depend on the realization of the noise

upon each call to

:

h(x)=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\!\Bigg(r(x,y)+\xi_{y}+\sum_{k\in[K]}g(x,k)w(y,k)\Bigg).

(17)

Random perturbation does not affect fairness, but will increase the risk proportional to the magnitude of

(so the noise should be set as small as possibly allowed by machine precision). In our

. This choice incurs a risk of at most

, by combining the sensitivity analysis in

(to be discussed in the next section) and the following bound on the

distance between

\E_{X}\E_{\xi}\!\Bigg[\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left|r(X,y)-(r(X,y)+\xi_{y})\right|\Bigg]=|\mathcal{Y}|\E[|\mathcal{U}(-\sigma,\sigma)|]=\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|\sigma}{2}.

(18)

Remark 2.4

(Scenarios Where
Assumption

2.2
Fails)
.

Assumption

fails when the push-forward

contains atoms and the randomized optimal fair classifier splits their mass. This has been discussed in prior work on fair post-processing
(
Gaucher et al., 2023

, where it is typically handled by making a continuity assumption (

Assumption

xian2023efficient
)
.

Remark 2.5

(Impact of Randomization on Individual Fairness)
.

A potential issue of randomized classifiers in practice is that individuals with the same feature

may receive different predictions

, which could be perceived as unfair on the individual-level. To avoid this and obtain a deterministic classifier, one may resort to approximate fairness (e.g., under our framework, this could be done by setting

3.1
Instantiations of the Post-Processing Framework

We instantiate our framework and show how to learn fair classifiers using

1
with the objective of minimizing classification error and satisfying SP, EOpp, and EO (

1

0-1 Loss.

When the performance metric is classification error, i.e., using the 0-1 loss
\ell(y^{\prime},y)=\1[y^{\prime}\neq y]
, as discussed in
2
, the Bayes optimal pointwise risk is

r(x,y^{\prime})=1-\Pr(Y=y^{\prime}\mid X=x)=\Pr(Y\neq y^{\prime}\mid X=x),

(21)

so that
\E[r(x,h(x))]=\Pr(h(X)\neq Y)
.

This means that on
3
of
Algorithm

1
, learning
\hat{r}
amounts to training a predictor
\hat{f}_{Y}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta^{\mathcal{Y}}
for the label
Y
given
X

\hat{f}_{Y}(x)_{y^{\prime}}=\widehat{\Pr}(Y=y^{\prime}\mid X=x)
(called a score function;
\Delta^{\mathcal{Y}}
denotes the probability simplex over
\mathcal{Y}
), and set
\hat{r}(x,y^{\prime})=1-\hat{f}_{Y}(x)_{y^{\prime}}
.

Statistical Parity.

To recover statistical parity from our group fairness definition in
Definition

1.1
, we set
Z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{A}}
to be the one-hot indicator for the sensitive attribute
A\in\mathcal{A}
, and the constraints
\mathcal{C}
according to
Table

1
.

In attribute-aware setting, there is no need to learn the group predictor on
4
of
Algorithm

1
because
A
is always explicitly given:
g(x,a)=\1[A=a\mid X=x]
. In the attribute-blind setting, the Bayes optimal group predictor is
g(x,a)=\Pr(A=a\mid X=x)
; this entails learning a predictor for
A
given
X
,
\hat{f}_{A}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta^{\mathcal{A}}
, and set
\hat{g}(x,a)=\hat{f}_{A}(x)_{a}
.

With
\hat{r}
and
\hat{g}
learned, the algorithm proceeds to estimating the weights of the post-processing via solving for the optimal dual values of the empirical
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(\hat{r}+\xi,\hat{g},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
, returning a randomized classifier on
10
. In the binary class attribute-aware setting, the returned classifier is a (noisy) group-wise thresholding of the score function, which is known to represent the Bayes optimal fair classifier
(
Menon and Williamson, 2018
)
:

\displaystyle\hat{h}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\1\left[\widehat{\Pr}(Y=1\mid X=x)+\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen\xi_{0}-\xi_{1}\right\rparen\geq\hat{t}_{a}\right]\quad\text{where}\quad\hat{t}_{a}=\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen 1+\hat{w}(0,a)-\hat{w}(1,a)\right\rparen

(22)

(note that here, attribute-awareness is reflected by
\hat{h}
taking the sensitive attribute
a
as an explicit input).
In the multiclass setting, the notion of thresholding is generalized to class offsets
(
xian2023FairOptimalClassification
)
: the output is the class with minimum pointwise risk after being offset with the fairness risk as discussed in
7
. In fact, for attribute-aware SP, by grouping the inputs
X
according to the sensitive attribute
A
,
Primal LP
can be rewritten as a Wasserstein barycenter problem over the group-conditional distributions of the pointwise risk: see LP of
xian2023FairOptimalClassification
.

In the attribute-blind setting, since
A
is unknown, the threshold is dynamic according to the demographic makeup at each
x
according to
\hat{g}
:

\displaystyle\hat{h}(x)

\displaystyle=\1\left[\widehat{\Pr}(Y=1\mid X=x)+\frac{1}{2}\left\lparen\xi_{0}-\xi_{1}\right\rparen\geq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\widehat{\Pr}(A=a\mid X=x)\hat{t}_{a}\right].

(23)

Equalized Odds.

To recover equalized odds from
Definition

1.1
, we set
Z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}
to be the indicator for
(A,Y)
jointly, that is,
Z_{a,y}=\1[A=a,Y=y]
. Equal opportunity is not separately discussed because it can be seen as a special case of EO.

The Bayes optimal group predictor in this case is

g(x,(a,y))=\Pr(A=a,Y=y\mid X=x)=\Pr(Y=y\mid A=a,X=x)\Pr(A=a\mid X=x).

(24)

In the attribute-aware setting, because we already have a predictor
\hat{f}_{Y}
for
Y
from
3
(assuming that it makes use of
A
for prediction) and
\Pr(A=a\mid X=x)
is known thanks to attribute-awareness (as in attribute-aware SP), we do not need to learn any additional predictor for
\hat{g}
according to the decomposition. In the attribute-blind setting, we need to learn a predictor
\hat{f}_{A,Y}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta^{\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}}
for the joint
(A,Y)
. This can be done directly, or, one could use the above decomposition to break the problem down into learning two (simpler) predictors,
\hat{f}_{Y}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
and
\hat{f}_{A}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, then combining them into
\hat{f}_{A,Y}(a,y)=\hat{f}_{Y}(a,x)_{y}f_{A}(x)_{a}
. An alternative decomposition, used in
(
Alghamdi et al., 2022
)
, is
\Pr(A,Y\mid X)=\Pr(A\mid Y,X)\Pr(Y\mid X)
; the choice of one over the other (or neither) depends on whether

The classifier returned by
Algorithm

1
for EO, in the binary class attribute-aware setting, is a group-wise thresholding of the score function with a scalable noise:

\displaystyle\hat{h}(x,a)

\displaystyle=\1\left[\widehat{\Pr}(Y=1\mid X=x)+\hat{s}_{a}(\xi_{0}-\xi_{1})\geq\hat{t}_{a}\right],

(25)

where

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{a}

\displaystyle=\hat{s}_{a}\left\lparen 1+\hat{w}(0,(a,0))-\hat{w}(1,(a,0))\right\rparen,

(26)

\displaystyle\hat{s}_{a}

\displaystyle=\left\lparen 2+\hat{w}(0,(a,0))-\hat{w}(1,(a,0))+\hat{w}(1,(a,1))-\hat{w}(0,(a,1))\right\rparen^{-1}.

(27)

For this setting,
Hardt et al. (2016)
have shown that the optimal fair classifier is a group-wise thresholding with thresholds chosen so that the group-conditional TPRs and FPRs coincide across groups; if the conditional ROCs do not intersect except at the end points, randomization can be applied on the dominating group to worsen its ROC to intersect that of the other group. These properties are embodied in our returned classifier: if the ROCs do not originally intersect,
\hat{s}_{a}
amplifies the magnitude of the noise added to the score function on the dominating group thereby worsening its predictive power and its ROC.

Overlapping Groups

So far, we have only discussed the case where the groups are disjoint, but our framework naturally handles overlapping groups, that is,
Z_{k}
can be active for more than one
k\in[K]
at a time.

Going from disjoint to overlapping groups only changes how the group predictor
\hat{g}
should be learned: in the disjoint case,
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\Delta^{K-1}
is for predicting a single group label at a time, whereas in the overlapping case,
g:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]^{K}
becomes a multilabel predictor because
x
could belong to multiple groups. One may directly use the multilabel formulation, or alternatively, learn binary predictors
\hat{f}_{Z_{k}}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
for each group
k
separately.

Intersecting Groups

There are also cases where fairness is required across subgroups formed by taking the intersection of groups, such as the combinations of multiple sensitive attributes like gender and race
(
Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018
)
,
(A_{1},A_{2},\dots,A_{M})
. Our framework can handle intersecting groups—simply by representing each intersectional group by an indicator
Z_{k}=\1[A_{1}=a_{1},\dots,A_{M}=a_{M}]
, but the number of indicators and fairness constraints grow exponentially in
M
, as well as the parameters of the post-processing.

It is possible to get around this exponential complexity by using alternative algorithms to optimize the post-processing (rather than solve the
LP
directly). For example, the in-processing algorithm by
Kearns et al. (2018)
views (the Lagrangian of) the fair classification problem of
8
as a two-player zero-sum game between the learner
h
and the auditor (dual variables), and uses an iterative algorithm to find an approximate equilibrium of the game. It returns a randomized ensemble of classifiers, where the number of classifiers is equal to the iterations—polynomial in the optimality tolerance. Such algorithms could be adapted to optimize our post-processing problem (
LP
), which would build the post-processing up in an iterative manner (see, e.g.,
Globus-Harris et al., 2023
); we leave it as future work.

2.

For any

(32)

The first part considers the case where the pointwise risk

3.3
Finite Sample Estimation

Given pointwise risk

for post-processing—this is how finite sample estimation is performed in the post-processing stage of

Comparison with Existing Algorithms.

Among post-processing algorithms, ours algorithm achieves the best tradeoffs in most cases, especially on multiclass problems (ACSIncome5 and BiasBios). Comparing to MBS, because its post-processing parameters are obtained by performing grid search on labeled data, it could achieve higher fairness but at the expense of significantly longer running time. FairProjection encountered out-of-memory error when post-processing for EO on multiclass problems.

Between post-processing and in-processing, in-processing can attain higher accuracies (see discussions in
Section

1
), but in most case, particularly multiclass problems, it cannot achieve the same high levels of fairness as post-processing does. The main difficulty with in-processing at achieving high fairness is likely due to optimization. Reductions requires an oracle for learning cost-sensitive classifiers and involves repeatedly training and combining a large number of them to converge to a fair classifier, so performance may be affected if the oracle is suboptimal, or if the number of iterations is limited due to computational budget (Reductions already has the longest running time in our experiments). Adversarial training suffers from instability due to the conflicting objective of the predictor (whose goal is risk minimization) and the adversary (fairness). Indeed, ease of optimization is what distinguishes post-processing from in-processing: the ability to solve the post-processing problem to optimality enables post-processing to achieve better fairness.

High Fairness Regime.

In our algorithm, when the fairness tolerance
\alpha
is set to small values in hopes of achieving higher fairness, the improvement in fairness on the surrogate empirical problem
(\hat{r},\hat{g},\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X})
may not necessarily transfer to the true population problem
(r,g,\mathbb{P}_{X})
(see
Fig.

7
). Also, accuracy drops off more rapidly in the high fairness regime.

There are two main reasons for this. The first is sample variance: achieving high fairness (i.e., small
\alpha
) on the empirical problem requires fitting the weights
\hat{w}
to the training set more precisely, which would cause overfitting and therefore high variance; it could be alleviated with more samples.
The second is the error of the group predictor
\hat{g}
. As we have analyzed in
Theorem

3.1
, exact fairness cannot be guaranteed if
\hat{g}
is not optimal, and small
\alpha
settings causes accuracy to be more sensitive to its suboptimality hence leading to larger drops in performance (of course, this rapid drop may simply be due to the inherent tradeoffs). Solutions in this case include training better group predictors or performing calibration: indeed, as we show in our ablation studies in
Figs.

,
9
,
10
,
11
and
12
(discussed below), post-processing calibrated predictors yields fairer classifiers compared to their uncalibrated counterparts.

5
Conclusion and Limitations

Based on a representation result of the Bayes optimal fair classifier, in this paper, we present a post-processing fair classification learning algorithm that covers a variety of group fairness criteria (including SP, EOpp, and EO), and applicable to binary and multiclass problems in both attribute-aware and blind settings.

In addition to the limitations regarding randomization (although inherent to group fairness in classification) mentioned in
Remark

2.4
, and intersecting groups in
Section

3.1
, we discuss another limitation/future work below regarding threshold invariance.

Threshold-Invariance.

Some practical scenarios may occasionally require adjusting the threshold of the classifier after deployment, e.g., to change the acceptance rate in response to a
label shift
in the underlying distribution
(
Lipton et al., 2018
)
.
However, in our framework, fairness is not preserved under changes to the threshold of our post-processed scores
7
; changing the acceptance rate while maintaining fairness requires redoing post-processing under a class weighting.

Optimal and threshold-invariant fair scores can be obtained via fair representation learning
(
zemel2013LearningFairRepresentations
)
, which is an in-processing algorithm. By treating it as a fair regression problem, threshold-invariant fair scores could also be obtained using post-processing algorithms for fair regression
(
Chzhen et al., 2020
)
, but their objective (e.g., minimizing MSE) may not be aligned with performance metrics such as classification error. It remains an open problem on how to obtain optimal and threshold-invariant fair scores via post-processing—optimality here is in the sense that they are capable of exploring the Pareto front of the TPRs of each class from varying the threshold.

\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[L]
, parameterized by

\mathcal{H}_{L}=\bigg\{x\mapsto\min\!\bigg(\argmin_{i\in[L]}w^{\top}_{i}x\bigg):w_{1},\dots,w_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigg\}.

(106)

We bound the VC dimension of the above class of multiclass classifiers in one-versus-rest mode, i.e., the class of binary functions given by

\mathcal{H}_{L}^{\mathrm{ovr},\ell}=\left\{x\mapsto\1[h(x)=\ell]:h\in\mathcal{H}_{L}\right\}.

(107)

Lemma C.7
.

For all input dimension
d
, number of classes
L
, and
\ell\in[L]
,
d_{\mathrm{VC}}(\mathcal{H}^{\textnormal{ovr},\ell}_{L})\leq O(d\log L)
; see definition in
107
.

Proof.

The proof proceeds by showing that
\mathcal{H}_{L}^{\mathrm{ovr},\ell}
can be represented by
feed-forward linear threshold networks
, then cites an existing result on the VC dimension of this class of networks
(
Anthony and Bartlett, 1999
, Theorem 6.1)
.

Any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{L}^{\mathrm{ovr},\ell}
can be written for some
w_{1},\dots,w_{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\1[\min(\argmin\nolimits_{i}w_{i}^{\top}x)=\ell]

(108)

\displaystyle=\1\left[\sum_{i<\ell}\1[w_{\ell}^{\top}x>w_{i}^{\top}x]+\sum_{i>\ell}\1[w_{\ell}^{\top}x\geq w_{i}^{\top}x]\geq L-1\right]

(109)

\displaystyle=\1\left[(\ell-1)-\sum_{i<\ell}\1[w_{\ell}^{\top}x\leq w_{i}^{\top}x]+\sum_{i>\ell}\1[w_{\ell}^{\top}x\geq w_{i}^{\top}x]\geq L-1\right]

(110)

\displaystyle=\1\left[-\sum_{i<\ell}\1[(w_{i}-w_{\ell})^{\top}x\geq 0]+\sum_{i>\ell}\1[(w_{\ell}-w_{i})^{\top}x\geq 0]\geq L-\ell\right],

(111)

which is a two-layer feed-forward linear threshold network with

O(d)

variable weights and thresholds and

O(L)
computation units (a.k.a. perceptrons/nodes), so its VC dimension is
O(d\log L)
.
∎

Last but not least, note that the deterministic classifier
\hat{h}
constructed in
Theorem

3.3
with weights
\hat{w}
from solving the empirical problem
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
may not be optimal on the empirical problem nor satisfies its constraints. We want to bound the difference between the empirical loss of
\hat{h}
and the optimal value of
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
, as well as violation of the empirical constraints by
\hat{h}
.

Corollary C.8
.

Under the same conditions as
Theorem

3.3
, denote by
\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
the optimal value of
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
and
(\hat{\pi},\hat{q})
its minimizer. Then
\forall N\geq\max_{k}2\ln(2K/\delta)/\Pr(Z_{k}=1)^{2}
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}[r(X,\hat{h}(X))]-\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{Y}|^{2}}{N},

(112)

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\hat{q}_{c}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{N\Pr(Z_{k}=1)},\quad\forall c\in[C],\,k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}.

(113)

This follows from bounding the disagreements between the deterministic
\hat{h}
and the randomized classifier
\hat{\pi}
that minimizes the empirical problem on the samples
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
; disagreement occurs when
\hat{\pi}
needs to randomize its output.

Lemma C.9
.

Under the same conditions as
Theorem

3.3
, denote the minimizer of
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
by
(\hat{\pi},\hat{q})
. Then almost surely, for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\1[\hat{h}(x_{i})=y]-\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y)\right|\leq|\mathcal{Y}|-1.

(114)

Proof.

The binary classifier
x\mapsto\1[\hat{h}(x)=y]
is a linear multiclass classifier in one-versus-rest mode
107
on the features
u(x)\coloneqq(r(x),g(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}\times K}
:

\1[\hat{h}(x)=y]=\1\!\bigg[\min\!\bigg(\argmin_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}\beta_{y^{\prime}}^{\top}u(x)\bigg)=y\bigg],

(115)

where we defined
\beta_{y}=(\mathbf{e}_{y},\hat{w}_{y})
with
(\hat{w}_{y})_{k}=-(\sum_{c}\1[y_{c}=y,k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}]{\hat{\psi}_{c,k}}/{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)})
and
\hat{\psi}
is the optimal dual values.

As a consequence of complementary slackness in
16
,
\1[\hat{h}(x)=y]=\hat{\pi}(x,y)
when
y
is the unique “best” class that attains the
\argmin
in
115
, or when
y
is not a “best” class. This means that disagreement occurs when the feature
u(x)
lies on the decision boundary between
y
and some other class
y^{\prime}
, and therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\1[\hat{h}(x_{i})=y]-\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y)\right|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\1\left[\exists y^{\prime}\neq y,\,(\beta_{y}-\beta_{y^{\prime}})^{\top}u(x_{i})=0\right].

(116)

Because the features
u(x_{i})
are samples of the push-forward
(r,g)\sharp\mathbb{P}_{X}
, by
Assumption

A.1
, for each
y^{\prime}\neq y
, almost surely no more than one
u(x_{i})
can occupy the linear subspace given by
(\beta_{y}-\beta_{y^{\prime}})
, which has two non-zero coordinates in the
\mathcal{Y}
-component. So the number of disagreements is no more than
|\mathcal{Y}|-1
.
∎

Proof of
Corollary

C.8
.

By objective of
Primal LP
,

\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r(x_{i},y)\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y),

(117)

and by
Lemma

C.9
, almost surely,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}[r(X,\hat{h}(X))]-\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}r(x_{i},y)\left\lparen\1[\hat{h}(x_{i})=y]-\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y)\right\rparen\right|

(118)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\|r\|_{\infty}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\1[\hat{h}(x_{i})=y]-\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y)\right|

(119)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\|r\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{Y}|(|\mathcal{Y}|-1).

(120)

Similarly, by the constraint of
Primal LP
, for all
c\in[C]
and
k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
,

\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{g(x_{i},k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y_{c})-\hat{q}_{c}\right|\leq\frac{\alpha}{2},

(121)

and by
Lemma

C.9
, almost surely,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\hat{q}_{c}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{N}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{g(x_{i},k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\left\lparen\1[\hat{h}(x_{i})=y_{c}]-\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y_{c})\right\rparen\right|

(122)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{N\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|\1[\hat{h}(x_{i})=y_{c}]-\hat{\pi}(x_{i},y_{c})\right|

(123)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{1}{N\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}(|\mathcal{Y}|-1)

(124)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{2}{N\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}(|\mathcal{Y}|-1);

(125)

the last inequality holds for all
N\geq\max_{k}2\ln(2K/\delta)/\Pr(Z_{k}=1)^{2}
w.p. at least
1-\delta
, under which

\left|\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)-\Pr(Z_{k}=1)\right|\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2N}\ln\frac{2K}{\delta}}\leq\frac{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}{2}

(126)

by Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound, which implies
1/\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)\leq 2/\Pr(Z_{k}=1)
.
∎

With all the tools above, we can now prove
Theorem

3.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.3
.

We first bound the violation of fairness constraints, then the excess risk.

Constraint Violation.

Denote a minimizer of the empirical
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{Primal LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
by
(\hat{\pi},\hat{q})
, then for all
c\in[C]
and
k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
, w.p. at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\left|\Pr(\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1)-\hat{q}_{c}\right|

(127)

\displaystyle=\left|\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\hat{q}_{c}\right|

(128)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{multlined}\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\hat{q}_{c}\right|\\
+\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]\right|\\
+\left|\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\1[\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}]\right]\right|\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leq\begin{multlined}\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{N\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}+\widehat{\E}_{X}[g(X,k)]\left|\frac{1}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}-\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\right|\\
+O\left\lparen\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|K\log|\mathcal{Y}|+\ln K/\delta}{N}}\right\rparen\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|}{N\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}+O\left\lparen\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\sqrt{\frac{\ln K/\delta}{N}}+\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|K\log|\mathcal{Y}|+\ln K/\delta}{N}}\right\rparen,

(134)

where the second inequality is by
Corollary

C.8
and
Theorem

C.6
with the VC dimension in
Lemma

C.7
, and the last inequality follows from
126
. So, when
N\geq\Omega(|\mathcal{Y}|)
,

\displaystyle\max_{k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\left|\Pr(\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1)-\Pr(\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k^{\prime}}=1)\right|

(135)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}2\left|\Pr(\hat{h}(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1)-\hat{q}_{c}\right|

(136)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+O\left\lparen\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|K\log|\mathcal{Y}|+\ln K/\delta}{N}}\right\rparen,

(137)

and this concludes the bound on constraint violation.

Before continuing to bounding the excess risk, we similarly bound the constraint violation of the optimal fair classifier
h
on the empirical problem
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
, which will be used later (in the above, we have bounded that of the empirical fair classifier
\hat{h}
on the population problem
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\mathbb{P}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
). Let
(\pi,q)
and
(\phi,\psi)
denote the optimal primal and dual values of
\hyperlink{eq:primal}{\textnormal{LP}}(r,g,\widehat{\mathbb{P}}_{X},\mathcal{C},\alpha)
, respectively. Note that the optimal fair classifier
h
, given in
Theorem

2.1
, is equal to
\pi
almost everywhere under
Assumption

2.2
.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, for all
c\in[C]
and
k\in\mathcal{I}_{c}
,

\displaystyle\left|\widehat{\Pr}(h(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1)-q_{c}\right|

(138)

\displaystyle\leq\begin{multlined}\left|\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\1[h(X)=y_{c}]\right]-q_{c}\right|\\
+\left|\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\1[h(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[h(X)=y_{c}]\right]\right|\\
+\left|\E_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[h(X)=y_{c}]\right]-\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[\frac{g(X,k)}{\widehat{\Pr}(Z_{k}=1)}\1[h(X)=y_{c}]\right]\right|\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}+O\left\lparen\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\sqrt{\frac{\ln K/\delta}{N}}+\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\sqrt{\frac{\ln K/\delta}{N}}\right\rparen

(142)

by Hoeffding’s inequality, since
h
does not depend on the samples. So the constraint violation of
h
on the empirical problem is

\displaystyle\max_{k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{I}_{c}}\left|\widehat{\Pr}(h(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k}=1)-\widehat{\Pr}(h(X)=y_{c}\mid Z_{k^{\prime}}=1)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\alpha+O\left\lparen\max_{k\in[K]}\frac{1}{\Pr(Z_{k}=1)}\sqrt{\frac{\ln K/\delta}{N}}\right\rparen

(143)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\alpha+B_{1}.

(144)

Excess Risk.

We begin by constructing a randomized classifier
\tilde{h}
in terms of
h
that satisfies the fairness constraints on the empirical problem (similar to the proof of
Theorem

3.1
Part 2), given by

\tilde{h}=\begin{cases}h&\textrm{w.p. $1-\beta$}\\
1&\textrm{w.p. $\beta$},\\
\end{cases}\qquad\text{where}\quad\beta=\min\left\lparen 1-\alpha,\frac{B_{1}}{\alpha+B_{1}}\right\rparen\in[0,1].

(145)

Then we can bound the optimal value of the empirical problem
\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
by the value of
\tilde{h}
:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}\leq\widehat{\E}_{X}\E_{\tilde{h}}[r(X,\tilde{h}(X))]\leq(1-\beta)\widehat{\E}_{X}[r(X,h(X))]+\beta\|r\|_{\infty},

(146)

and we note that
\beta\leq B_{1}/\alpha
.

Next, note that

\displaystyle\left|\E_{X}[r(X,\hat{h}(X))]-\widehat{\E}_{X}[r(X,\hat{h}(X))]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left|\E_{X}\left[r(X,y)\1[\hat{h}(X)=y]\right]-\widehat{\E}_{X}\left[r(X,y)\1[\hat{h}(X)=y]\right]\right|

(147)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{Y}|\|r\|_{\infty}O\left\lparen\sqrt{\frac{|\mathcal{Y}|K\log|\mathcal{Y}|+\ln K/\delta}{N}}\right\rparen

(148)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon B_{2}

(149)

by
Theorem

C.6
with the VC dimension in
Lemma

C.7
.

Then, putting everything together,

\displaystyle\E_{X}[r(X,\hat{h}(X))]

(150)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\E}_{X}[r(X,\hat{h}(X))]+B_{2}

by
149

(151)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{Y}|^{2}}{N}+B_{2}

by
Corollary

C.8

(152)

\displaystyle\leq\widehat{\E}_{X}[r(X,h(X))]+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}B_{1}}{\alpha}+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{Y}|^{2}}{N}+B_{2}

by
146

(153)

\displaystyle\leq\E_{X}[r(X,h(X))]+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}B_{1}}{\alpha}+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{Y}|^{2}}{N}+2B_{2}

by
149

(154)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{OPT}+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}B_{1}}{\alpha}+\frac{\|r\|_{\infty}|\mathcal{Y}|^{2}}{N}+2B_{2}

by
Theorem

2.1
.

(155)

We may conclude by taking a final union bound over all events considered above.
