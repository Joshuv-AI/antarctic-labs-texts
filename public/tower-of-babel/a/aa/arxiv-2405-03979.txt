Dimension quotients as boundary limits
Roman Mikhailov

Abstract
. For a functor from the category of free presentations of a group to the category of all groups we define the boundary limit as an image of the natural map from limit to colimit. We show that the fourth dimension quotient of a group can be naturally described as the boundary limit of a simply-defined functor.

Introduction

Theory of limits was developed in the series of papers
[
7
]
,
[

]
,
[

,
[
13
]
. It gives a method or a point of view on construction of functors in algebraic categories. In
[
14
]
, the limits are mentioned as a part of a program called
Speculative theory of functors
.

Recall the main concepts of the theory of limits. Let
G
be a group. By
\textsf{Pres}(G)
we denote the
category of presentations
of
G
with objects being free groups
F
together with epimorphisms to
G
.
Morphisms are group homomorphisms over
G
. For a functor
\mathcal{F}:\textsf{Pres}(G)\to\textsf{Ab}
from the category
\textsf{Pres}(G)
to the category of abelian groups, one can
consider the (higher) limits
{\sf Lim}^{i},\mathcal{F},\ i\geq 0,
over the category of presentations. That is, we fix our group
G
, consider free presentations
R\hookrightarrow F\twoheadrightarrow G
and make functorial (on
F,R
) constructions
\mathcal{F}(F,R).
The limits
{\sf Lim}^{i}\,\mathcal{F}(F,R),\ i\geq 0,
will depend only on
G
. Moreover, the limits give a way to define functors from the category of all groups to abelian groups.

The category
\textsf{Pres}(G)
is strongly connected and has pair-wise coproducts. The limit
{\sf Lim}\mathcal{F}={\sf Lim}^{0}\mathcal{F}
has the following properties. For any
c\in\textsf{Pres}(G),

(1)

{\sf Lim}\ \mathcal{F}=\{x\in\mathcal{F}(c)|\forall\,c^{\prime}\in{\sf Pres}(G),\ \varphi,\psi:c\to c^{\prime},\ \mathcal{F}(\varphi)(x)=\mathcal{F}(\psi)(x)\}.

Moreover, it is known
[
13
]
that the limit of a functor from a strongly connected category with pair-wise coproducts is equal to the equalizer

(2)

{\sf Lim}\ \mathcal{F}=\textrm{eq}(\mathcal{F}(c)\rightrightarrows\mathcal{F}(c\sqcup c))

for any
c\in\textsf{Pres}(G)
.
In particular, this equalizer does not depend on
c
. That is, a limit is not just an abstractly defined object, but is the equalizer of a pair of explicitly described maps. One more property of the limit, which makes it intuitively clear, is that
{\sf Lim}\ \mathcal{F}
is the largest subgroup of
\mathcal{F}(c),
which depends only on
G
.

The theory of (nonderived) limits can rewritten without any serious changes to the case of non-commutative values. For example, for functors
\mathcal{F}:{\sf Pres}(G)\to{\sf Gr}
, from the category of presentations of
G
to the category of all groups, one can define the limit
{\sf Lim}\mathcal{F}
via universal properties and prove the main statements like mentioned above. The limit
{\sf Lim}\ \mathcal{F}
is the largest constant subgroup of
\mathcal{F}(c),
for any
c\in{\sf Pres}(G).
However, in the non-commutative case, there is a problem with definition of higher limits
{\sf Lim}^{i}\mathcal{F},\ i\geq 1
. The first derived functor
{\sf Lim}^{1}\mathcal{F}
can be defined not a a group, but as a pointed set only.

There is a large collection of examples of limits computed by various methods
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
13
]
. In particular, various combinations of derived functors and group homology appear as limits of simply defined functors in
{\sf Pres}(G)
. To illustrate briefly how it works, recall two examples from
[
7
]
and
[
11
]
. For a group
H
, the lower central series are defined inductively as follows

\gamma_{1}(H):=H,\ \gamma_{n+1}(H):=[\gamma_{n}(H),H]=\langle[x,y],\ x\in\gamma_{n}(H),y\in H\rangle^{H},\ n\geq 1.

There is the following description of the (derived) limits:

\displaystyle{\sf Lim}\ \gamma_{3}(F)/[\gamma_{2}(R),F]\gamma_{4}(F)=L_{1}{\sf S}^{3}(G_{ab}),

\displaystyle{\sf Lim}\ \gamma_{2}(R)/[\gamma_{2}(R),F]=H_{4}(G;\mathbb{Z}/2)\ \ \ (\text{provided}\ G\ \text{is 2-torsion free}),

\displaystyle{\sf Lim}^{1}\gamma_{2}(R)/[\gamma_{2}(R),F]=H_{3}(G;\mathbb{Z}/2)\ \ \ (\text{provided}\ G\ \text{is 2-torsion free}).

Here
L_{1}{\sf S}^{3}
is the first derived functor in the sense of Dold-Puppe of the symmetric cube,
G_{ab}:=G/\gamma_{2}(G)
is the abelianization of

.

It is easy to guess why the presented theory of limits belongs to the Speculative theory of functors. The symbols
F
and
R
play the role of building blocks or construction parts for design of tricky functors and natural transformations between them. Indeed, one can construct anything depending on
F
and
R
, take (derived) limits and obtain the functor on
G.

The theory of (derived) colimits in the categories of presentations is developed in
[
10
]
. It is shown in that paper that a lot of interesting functors like cyclic homology or certain K-functors can be described via derived colimits. For a presentation
\mathcal{F},
the colimit
{\sf Colim}\ \mathcal{F}
is the largest quotient of
\mathcal{F}(c),
which depends only on
G
.

In this paper, we will consider the
{\it boundary\ limits}
and denote them as
{\sf Blim}.
By definition, the boundary limit is the image of the limit in colimit, under the natural map, i.e. for a presentation
\mathcal{F},

{\sf Blim}\ \mathcal{F}:={\sf Im}\{{\sf Lim}\ \mathcal{F}\to{\sf Colim}\ \mathcal{F}\}.

That is, for any
c\in{\sf Pres}(G),
the boundary limit
{\sf Blim}\ \mathcal{F}
is the image of the largest subgroup of
\mathcal{F}(c)
which depends only on
G
in the largest quotient of
\mathcal{F}(c)
which depends only of
G
. Observe that, in the case of colimits, there is no analog of the formula (
2
), colimit clearly can be defined as a coequalizer of a set of maps, however, this set, in general, is infinite.

The dimension subgroups are fundamental objects in the theory of group rings. In this paper, we will study them in the context of limits. Recall the definition.
Let
g
be the augmentation ideal of

\mathbb{Z}[G]
. The subgroups
D_{n}(G):=G\cap(1+\textbf{g}^{n}),\ n\geq 1
are known as

dimension subgroups
. It is easy to see that, for any
G
and
n\geq 1
, the
dimension subgroups contain the terms of lower central series of
G
:

\gamma_{n}(G)\subseteq D_{n}(G)
. The quotients
D_{n}(G)/\gamma_{n}(G)
are called the
dimension quotients
. The structure of the dimension quotients in general is extremely complicated and, as it is shown recently in
[
1
]
, is related to the homotopy groups of spheres.

In this paper we show (see Proposition
2.1
) that, for every
n,
there exists a natural monomorphism

(3)

{\sf Blim}\frac{F}{R^{\prime}\gamma_{n}(F)}\hookrightarrow D_{n}(G)/\gamma_{n}(G).

Is it an isomorphism? For the moment we are not able to answer this question for general
n\geq 1.
Since the dimension quotient may be nontrivial for
n\geq 4,
the case
n=4
turns out to be the first interesting step. We prove that, for
n=4
, the above map is a natural isomorphism, i.e.

(4)

{\sf Blim}\frac{F}{R^{\prime}\gamma_{4}(F)}=D_{4}(G)/\gamma_{4}(G).

Observe that, this result follows from the results of the paper
[
13
]
, however the proofs from
[
13
]
are non-natural and based on a choose of basis in free groups and a routine analysis of forms of elements in free group rings, it is very complicated to generalize the arguments from
[
13
]
for higher
n
. In this paper we give a completely
functorial
proof of (
4
).

The fourth dimension quotient
D_{4}(G)/\gamma_{4}(G)
is extremely complicated functor, as an abelian group it always has exponent 2, however, it’s structure has relation to homotopy groups, in particular, the 2-torsion which appears in the fourth dimension quotient of some known examples is related to the homotopy group
\pi_{4}(S^{2})=\mathbb{Z}/2
(see
[
1
]
). Observe also that, the results of this paper can be easily reformulated for the case of Lie rings. In the Lie case, the dimension quotients are related to the derived functors of Lie functors (see
[
1
]
).

It is natural to make a conjecture that the inclusion (
3
) is an isomorphism for all
n
. What about boundary limits of the type
{\sf Blim}\frac{F}{\gamma_{k}(R)\gamma_{n}(F)},\ k<n?
These functors may have a complicated nature, and be related to homotopy theory.

\sf Lim^{n}\mathcal{F}\cong H^{n}(C^{*}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})).

3.3.
Abelian
1
-cocycles and
1
-coboundaries

We denote by
Z^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
the set of
n
-cocycles of the complex
C^{*}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
and by
B^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
the set of
n
-coboundaries. An element of the group
Z^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
is called
n
-cocycle of
\mathcal{C}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{F}
and an element of
B^{n}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
is called
n
-coboundary of
\mathcal{C}
with coefficients in
\mathcal{F}.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
a\in\prod_{\alpha\in{\sf Mor}(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{F}({\sf cod}(\alpha)).

(1)

a\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
if and only if

a(\alpha\beta)=a(\alpha)+\alpha\cdot a(\beta).

(2)

a\in B^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
if and only if there exists
x\in\prod_{c\in{\sf}\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{F}(c)
such that

a(\alpha)=x({\sf cod}(\alpha))-\alpha\cdot x({\sf dom}(\alpha)).

(3)

There is an isomorphism

\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}\cong\frac{Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})}{B^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})}

(4)

two
1
-cocycles
a,a^{\prime}\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
represent the same element in
\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}
if and only if there exists
x\in\prod_{c\in{\sf Ob}(\mathcal{C})}\mathcal{F}(c)
such that

a^{\prime}(\alpha)=-x({\sf cod}(\alpha))+a(\alpha)+\alpha\cdot x({\sf dom}(\alpha)).

Proof.

(1) and (2) can be proved by a direct computation using the definition of
C^{*}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
. (3) and (4) are obvious.
∎

3.4.
Non-abelian
\sf Lim^{1}

Denote by
\sf Gr
the category of groups and consider a functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{C}\to\sf Gr.
For an element
x\in\mathcal{F}(c)
and a morphism
\alpha:c\to c^{\prime}
we set

(21)

{}^{\alpha}x=\mathcal{F}(\alpha)(x).

It is well known that
\sf Lim\;\mathcal{F}
can be described as follows

(22)

\sf Lim\;\mathcal{F}=\{x\in\prod_{c}\mathcal{F}(c)\mid x({\sf cod}(\alpha))={}^{\alpha}x({\sf dom}(\alpha))\}.

Let us define
\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}
in a similar manner.

Denote by
Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
the set of elements
a\in\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}({\sf cod}(\alpha))
such that

(23)

a(\alpha\beta)=a(\alpha)\cdot{}^{\alpha}a(\beta).

We consider the set
Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
as a pointed set with the basepoint
a(\alpha)=1.

The group
\prod_{c}\mathcal{F}(c)
acts on the set
Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})
as follows

(24)

(a^{x})(\alpha)=x({\sf cod}(\alpha))^{-1}\cdot a(\alpha)\cdot{}^{\alpha}x({\sf dom}(\alpha))

Then we define
\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}
as the quotient by this action

(25)

\sf Lim^{1}\>\mathcal{F}:=Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F})/\prod_{c}\mathcal{F}(c).

Proposition
3.2
implies that this definition is a generalization of the definition for the functors to the category of abelian groups. Moreover, it is easy to see that
\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}
is natural in
\mathcal{F}
i.e. a natural transformation
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{F}
defines a map
\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{C}\to{\sf Gr}
be a functor and
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a subfunctor. Then the quotient
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}
defines a functor to the category of poited sets
\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{C}\to{\sf Sets}_{*},
where
(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G})(c)=\mathcal{F}(c)/\mathcal{G}(c)
consists of left cosets
x\cdot\mathcal{G}(c),x\in\mathcal{F}(c).

Lemma 3.3
.

There is a well defined map of pointed sets

\delta:\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}\longrightarrow\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{G}

such that for any
x\in\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}\subseteq\prod(\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G})(c)
and any its preimage
\bar{x}\in\prod\mathcal{F}(c)
the class
\delta(x)
is defined by
1
-cocycle
d(\bar{x})\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{G})
given by

d(\bar{x})(\alpha)=\bar{x}^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}({\sf dom}(\alpha)).

Moreover, if
\mathcal{G}
is a central subgroup of
\mathcal{F},
then
\delta
is a homomorphism.

Proof.

A straightforward computation shows that
d(\bar{x})
is
1
-cocycle. If
\bar{x},\tilde{x}
are two preimages of
x,
then
\bar{x}=\tilde{x}y
for some
y\in\prod_{c}\mathcal{G}(c)
and this implies that
d(\bar{x})
is equivalent to
d(\tilde{x}).

Now assume that
\mathcal{G}
is a central subgroup and
x_{1},x_{2}\in\sf Lim\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}.
Then

(26)

\begin{split}d(\bar{x}_{1}\bar{x}_{2})(\alpha)&=\bar{x}_{2}^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot\Big(\bar{x}_{1}^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}_{1}({\sf dom}(\alpha))\Big)\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}_{2}({\sf dom}(\alpha))\\
&=\Big(\bar{x}_{1}^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}_{1}({\sf dom}(\alpha))\Big)\cdot\Big(\bar{x}_{2}^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}_{2}({\sf dom}(\alpha))\Big)\\
&=d(\bar{x}_{1})(\alpha)\cdot d(\bar{x}_{2})(\alpha).\end{split}

It follows that
\delta
is a homomorphism.
∎

Proposition 3.4

(cf.
[
15
, Prop.36]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{C}\to\sf Gr
be a functor and let
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a subfunctor. Then the sequence of pointed sets

1\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{G}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{G}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}

is exact.

Proof.

Since the functor
\sf Lim
is right adjoint, it commutes with equializers, and hence the sequence
1\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{G}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}
is exact. The equation
{\sf Ker}(\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{G}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F})={\sf Im}(\delta)
follows obviously from the definition of
\delta
.

Prove that
{\sf Ker}(\delta)={\sf Im}(\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}).
Let
x\in\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{G}
and
\bar{x}\in\prod\mathcal{F}(c)
is its preimage. Then
\delta(x)
is trivial if and only if there exists
y\in\prod\mathcal{G}(c)
such that

\bar{x}^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}({\sf dom}(\alpha))=y^{-1}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot{}^{\alpha}y({\sf dom}(\alpha)).

The equation is equivalent to the following equation

(\bar{x}y^{-1})({\sf cod}(\alpha))={}^{\alpha}(\bar{x}y^{-1})({\sf dom}(\alpha))

which is equivalent to the fact that
\bar{x}y^{-1}\in\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}.
It follows that
\delta(x)
is trivial if an only if
x
has a preimage in
\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}.

∎

Proposition 3.5

(cf.
[
15
, Prop.38]
,
[
2
, Prop.IX.2.3]
)
.

For any short exact sequence

1\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}\longrightarrow\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\longrightarrow 1

of functors
\mathcal{C}\to\sf Gr
the sequence of pointed sets

1\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim\>\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}\to\sf Lim^{1}\>\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\sf Lim^{1}\>\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim^{1}\>\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}

is exact.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
we have the exactness in all terms except the last one. Prove that
{\sf Ker}(\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime})={\sf Im}(\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}).

Denote the epimorphism by
f:\mathcal{F}\to\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}
and assume that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}.
Take a
1
-cocycle
a\in Z^{1}(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{F}).
Then it corresponds to an element of the kernel if and only if

f(a)(\alpha)=x({\sf cod}(\alpha))^{-1}\cdot{}^{\alpha}x({\sf dom}(\alpha))

for some
x\in\prod_{c}\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime}(c).
Equivalently this can be rewritten as

x({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot g(a)(\alpha)={}^{\alpha}x({\sf dom}(\alpha)).

If we take a preimage
\bar{x}
of
x
we see that it is equivalent to the fact that

\bar{x}({\sf cod}(\alpha))\cdot a(\alpha)=b(\alpha)\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}({\sf dom}(\alpha))

for some
\bar{x}\in\prod_{c}\mathcal{F}(c)
and
b\in\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}({\sf cod}(\alpha)).
Hence the fact that the class
a
is in
{\sf Ker}(\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}^{\prime\prime})
is equivalent to the fact that there exists
\bar{x}\in\prod_{c}\mathcal{F}(c)
and
b\in\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}({\sf cod}(\alpha))
such that

a(\alpha)=\bar{x}({\sf cod}(\alpha))^{-1}\cdot b(\alpha)\cdot{}^{\alpha}\bar{x}({\sf dom}(\alpha)).

And it is easy to see that this is equiavalent to the fact that the class of
a
is in
{\sf Im}(\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\to\sf Lim^{1}\mathcal{F}).

∎
