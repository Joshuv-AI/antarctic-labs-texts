Optical properties of null geodesics emerging from dynamical systems
D. Batic, S. Chanda, P. Guha

Abstract
We study optical metrics via null geodesics as a central force system, deduce the related Binet equation and apply the analysis to certain solutions of Einstein’s equations with and without spherical symmetry. A general formula for the deflection angle in the weak lensing regime for the Schwarzschild-Tangherlini (ST) metric is derived. In addition, we obtain a new weak lensing formula for the deflection angle on the equatorial plane of a Kerr black hole (BH). We also explore the bending of light by considering the gravitational objects described by the Tomimatsu-Sato (TS) metric.

Optical metrics are essentially null geodesics studied by projecting them onto lower dimensional spatial surfaces. If a metric admits a timelike

Null curve geodesics are formulated according to Fermat’s principle, since they

II
Preliminaries: Null geodesics

P.L. Maupertuis speculated in
maup
that light passing through a medium was
refracted due to gravitational effects. Furthermore, null geodesics are unique since
the speed of a particle (photon) travelling along a null geodesic remains unchanged
under local Lorentz group transformations. In special relativity, for flat spaces, this
leads to Einstein’s postulate about the universality of the speed of light in all inertial
frames, which holds true locally, even in refracting media.
Here, starting with a null geodesic in an isotropic space-time, we will shortly remind the reader how refractive
phenomena can arise from a gravitational metric (see
br
for further details). Suppose
we have a space-time metric which in isotropic coordinates is described by the line element

ds^{2}=g_{\mu\nu}dx^{\mu}dx^{\nu}=A(r)dt^{2}-B(r)|d\bm{r}|^{2},\quad|d\bm{r}|^{2}=dr^{2}+r^{2}\left(d\vartheta^{2}+\sin^{2}{\vartheta}d\varphi^{2}\right),

(2.1)

where we adopted geometrized units such that
c=G_{N}=1
. Taking into account that
ds^{2}=0
for null particles and introducing an affine parameterization such that
x^{\mu}=x^{\mu}(\lambda)
yield

\left(\frac{ds}{d\lambda}\right)^{2}=A(r){\dot{t}}^{2}-B(r)|\dot{\bm{r}}|^{2}=0.

(2.2)

Here, a dot denotes differentiation with respect to the affine parameter. From the above equation and under the reasonable assumption that
t=t(\lambda)
is an increasing function of
\lambda
, it follows that the local refractive index
n(\bm{r})
with respect to vacuum is

n(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{v_{p,n}}=\sqrt{\frac{B(r)}{A(r)}},\quad v_{p,n}=\frac{|\dot{\bm{r}}|}{\dot{t}}=\left|\frac{d\bm{r}}{dt}\right|

(2.3)

with
v_{p,n}
denoting the phase velocity of a light ray as measured by a distant observer. Note that in the case of a conformally flat metric
v_{p,n}=1
. Furthermore, if we recast (
2.3
) into the form

1=v_{p,n}\sqrt{\frac{B(r)}{A(r)}},

(2.4)

we can immediately see that at any given position, the speed of light is universal in all inertial frames of reference. It is worth mentioning that in the case of spherically symmetric vacuum solutions to Einstein’s equations,
AB=1
and the refractive index (
2.3
) simplifies as follows

n(\bm{r})=\frac{1}{|A(r)|}.

(2.5)

The above scenario changes in the case of an anisotropic space-time because each spatial direction has its own refractive index
n_{i}(\bf{r})
. More precisely, if we consider the stationary metric with line element

ds^{2}=h_{00}(\bm{r})dt^{2}-2h_{i0}(\bm{r})dtdx^{i}-h_{ij}(\bm{r})dx^{i}dx^{j}

(2.6)

such that for all
i,j=1,2,3
the metric coefficients
h_{00}
and
h_{ij}
are both positive on some common domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, the refractive index along each spatial direction can be deduced from the following quadratic equation

h_{00}(\bm{r})c^{2}-2h_{i0}(\bm{r})v^{i}_{p,n}-h_{ii}(\bm{r})\left(v^{i}_{p,n}\right)^{2}=0,\quad v^{i}_{p,n}=\frac{\dot{x}^{i}}{\dot{t}}=\frac{dx^{i}}{dt},

(2.7)

which has been obtained from (
2.6
) with
i=j
and by setting
ds^{2}=0
followed by the introduction of an affine parameterization. If we define the refractive index in the
i
-th spatial direction as

n_{i}({\bf{r}})=\frac{1}{v^{i}_{p,n}},

(2.8)

equation (
2.7
) becomes

h_{00}(\bm{r})n_{i}^{2}-2h_{i0}(\bm{r})n_{i}-h_{ii}(\bm{r})=0

(2.9)

and the corresponding roots are given by

n_{i,\pm}(\bm{r})=\frac{h_{i0}(\bm{r})\pm\sqrt{h_{i0}^{2}(\bm{r})+h_{00}(\bm{r})h_{ii}(\bm{r})}}{h_{00}(\bm{r})}.

(2.10)

At this point a comment is in order. Since we initially assumed that for all
i,j=1,2,3

h_{00},h_{ij}>0
on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, it is evident that
h_{ii}
is also positive definite, and the inequality
\sqrt{h_{i0}^{2}(\bm{r})+h_{00}(\bm{r})h_{ii}(\bm{r})}>|h_{i0}(\bm{r})|
holds true on
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Thus, if
h_{i0}=-|h_{i0}|
, we have a negative refractive index (permissible when considering metamaterials
fnb
;
cs
;
csl
), technically signalizing that the group and phase velocities have opposite directions. This situation corresponds to a metamaterial with negative electric and magnetic permeability. Since in the present work we are not interested to model metamaterials that can be used to simulate general-relativity phenomena, we will consider the positive root in (
2.10
) which we denote as follows

n_{i}(\bm{r})=\frac{h_{i0}(\bm{r})+\sqrt{h_{i0}^{2}(\bm{r})+h_{00}(\bm{r})h_{ii}(\bm{r})}}{h_{00}(\bm{r})}.

(2.11)

Since the line element of a null geodesic vanishes, we need to vary the spatial part alone. This can be achieved by applying Fermat’s principle of light
Synge
stating that a null ray through the events
1
and
2
must obey the constraints

\delta\int_{1}^{2}dt=0,\quad ds=0,

(2.12)

which can be rewritten in equivalent form as

\delta\int_{1}^{2}\dot{t}\ d\lambda=0,\quad g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}=0.

(2.13)

Using (
2.3
) and assuming an isotropic space-time, the optical arc integral is

\ell=\int_{1}^{2}{\dot{t}}d\lambda=\int_{1}^{2}n(\bm{r})|\dot{\bm{r}}|d\lambda=\int_{1}^{2}n(\bm{r})|d{\bm{r}}|

(2.14)

with Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{n}=\dot{t}=n(\bm{r})|\dot{\bm{r}}|
. If we further impose that
\delta\ell=0
, the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation is

\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(n(\bm{r})\frac{\dot{\bm{r}}}{|\dot{\bm{r}}|}\right)=|\dot{\bm{r}}|\bm{\nabla}n(\bm{r}).

(2.15)

We will regard the arc length
\sigma
defined through the relation
d\sigma=|\dot{\bm{r}}|d\lambda
as a natural parameter along the curve. If we parameterize with respect to arc length, the reparameterized velocity can be written as a unit vector
\widehat{\bf{e}}=d\bm{r}/d\sigma=\dot{\bm{r}}/|\dot{\bm{r}}|
denoting the direction of the light ray, which in turn allows us to write the Maupertuis action for light-like curves (
2.14
) as

\ell=\int_{1}^{2}{\bf{p}}_{n}\cdot d\bm{r},\quad{\bf{p}}_{n}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{n}}{\partial\dot{\bm{r}}}=n(\bm{r})\widehat{{\bf{e}}}.

(2.16)

Taking into account that the time
\Delta\mathcal{T}
as measured by a distant observer for the light ray to go from
1
to
2
is given by

\Delta\mathcal{T}=\int_{1}^{2}\dot{t}d\lambda=\int_{1}^{2}dt=\frac{1}{c}\int_{1}^{2}d\ell,

(2.17)

and using (
2.16
), it is possible to derive as in
cgg
the following Eikonal equation

{\bm{\nabla}}\mathcal{T}={\bm{\nabla}}\ell={\bf{p}}_{n}=n({\bm{r}}){\widehat{\bm{e}}}

(2.18)

with
|{\bm{\nabla}}\mathcal{T}|^{2}=n^{2}
. Furthermore, we can use the arc length
\sigma
to rewrite the Euler-Lagrange
equation (
2.15
) as

\frac{d}{d\sigma}\left(n(\bm{r})\widehat{\bm{e}}\right)={\bm{\nabla}}n(\bm{r})

(2.19)

and derive from it the following equation for the light ray

\frac{d{\widehat{\bm{e}}}}{d\sigma}=\left({\widehat{\bm{e}}}\times{\bm{\nabla}}\ln{n}\right)\times{\widehat{\bm{e}}},

(2.20)

a result which is normally derived from the Eikonal equation (see
br
). These spatial geodesics are better analysed using a Frenet–Serret-like frame as pointed out in
bgj
. We will use this fact to demonstrate that Snell’s law is applicable to gravitational fields. To this purpose, we set up a basis
\left({\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\parallel},{\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\perp}\right)
such that
{\widehat{\bm{e}}}=\cos{\theta}\ {\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\parallel}+\sin{\theta}\ {\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\perp}
with
\widehat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}
lying along
\bm{\nabla}n
, more precisely
\widehat{\bm{e}}_{\parallel}={\bm{\nabla}}n/|{\bm{\nabla}}n|
, and
{\widehat{e}}_{\perp}
is orthogonal to
{\bm{\nabla}}n
. At this point, it is not difficult to verify that

\frac{dn}{d\sigma}=\frac{d{\bm{r}}}{d\sigma}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}n={\widehat{\bm{e}}}\cdot{\bm{\nabla}}n=|{\bm{\nabla}}n|\cos{\theta}.

(2.21)

Since the angle
\theta
is also parameterized in terms of the arc length
\sigma
, we find in the basis
\left({\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\parallel},{\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\perp}\right)

\frac{d{\widehat{\bm{e}}}}{d\sigma}=-\left(\sin{\theta}\ {\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\parallel}-\cos{\theta}\ {\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\perp}\right)\frac{d\theta}{d\sigma}.

(2.22)

Moreover, a lengthy but straightforward manipulation of (
2.20
) leads to the following equivalent representation for the rate of change of the unit vector
\widehat{\bm{e}}
, namely

\frac{d{\widehat{\bm{e}}}}{d\sigma}=\sin\theta\left(\sin{\theta}\ {\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\parallel}-\cos{\theta}\ {\widehat{\bm{e}}}_{\perp}\right)\frac{|{\bm{\nabla}}n|}{n}.

(2.23)

If we merge together (
2.22
) and (
2.23
), we end up with the following differential equation

\frac{d\theta}{d\sigma}+\frac{|{\bm{\nabla}}n|}{n}\sin{\theta}=0.

(2.24)

Finally, it is possible to apply (
2.21
) to (
2.24
) so that
|{\bm{\nabla}}n|
can be expressed in terms of the rate of change of the refractive index. In this way, we derive the differential equation

\frac{d}{d\sigma}\left(n\sin{\theta}\right)=0,

(2.25)

which once integrated leads to

n({\bm{r}})\sin\theta=const.,

(2.26)

i.e. the Snell law of refractive optics. This demonstrates that regions with
gravitational fields are comparable to refractive media. Now, we will study classical
particle mechanics in optical terms.

III
Dynamical solutions for null geodesics via Binet’s equation

In Section
II
, we deduced optical principles for null geodesics. Here, we study how Binet’s equation derives from null geodesics and in addition, we dynamically compare it to mechanical systems with a central force. In the following, we have also attempted to extend Casey’s work
casey1
on the ST metric
to other solutions. Without further ado let us recall that a spherically symmetric Lorentzian
(n+1)
-dimensional metric with
S^{n-1}
symmetry and asymptotically flat can be written as

\begin{split}ds^{2}=f(r)dt^{2}-\frac{dr^{2}}{g(r)}-r^{2}d\Omega_{n-1}^{2}\qquad\text{where }\end{split}\quad\begin{split}f(r)=1+F(r),\quad F(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}a_{i}r^{-i}\\
g(r)=1+G(r),\quad G(r)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}b_{i}r^{-i}\end{split}.

(3.1)

In the case
n=3
and assuming without loss of generality that
\vartheta=\pi/2
, the following directional refractive indices can be easily derived by means of (
2.11
)

n_{r}^{2}=\frac{1}{f(r)g(r)},\quad n_{\varphi}^{2}=\frac{r^{2}}{f(r)}.

(3.2)

The null geodesics are characterized by setting
ds^{2}=0
in the line element appearing in (
3.1
). Moreover, they can be determined by the extremising process we discussed in Section
II
, i.e. such geodesics can be found by extremising the spatial curve as shown in (
2.14
) to take the least time to traverse in accordance with Fermat’s principle, with respect to which they can be parameterized. For example, let us impose
ds^{2}=0
in (
3.1
) for the case
n=3
and
\vartheta=\pi/2
. The latter condition can always be imposed because of the spherical symmetry of the manifold. If we further set
ds_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}=dt^{2}
in (
3.1
), then the unparameterized geodesics are described in terms of the line element

ds_{\mathcal{O}}^{2}=\frac{dr^{2}}{f(r)g(r)}+\frac{r^{2}}{f(r)}d\varphi^{2}=\eta^{2}(\rho)\left(d\rho^{2}+\rho^{2}d\phi^{2}\right),

(3.3)

where in the last step we introduced isotropic coordinates defined in terms of a new radial variable
\rho
such that

\left(\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{d\rho}{dr}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{r^{2}g(r)},\quad\eta^{2}(\rho)=\left(\frac{r}{\rho}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{f(r)}.

(3.4)

In the case
\rho
is an increasing function of
r
, it follows from (
3.4
) that

\rho(r)=ke^{\int\frac{dr}{r\sqrt{g(r)}}},\quad\eta(\rho)=\frac{r}{\rho\sqrt{f(r)}}

(3.5)

with
k
an arbitrary integration constant. Moreover, the conformal factor
\eta(\rho)
plays the role of the isotropic refractive index. In case of the Schwarzschild solution where
f(r)=g(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}
, the isotropic coordinate in (
3.3
) is obtained from the first equation in (
3.4
) which leads to

\frac{dr}{\sqrt{r^{2}-2Mr}}=\pm\frac{d\rho}{\rho}.

(3.6)

Since we want
\rho\to\infty
as
r\to\infty
, we need to consider the plus sign in (
3.6
). As in
Buch
we require that
\rho=M/2
at
r=2M
so that the isotropic coordinate is

\rho=\frac{1}{2}\left(r-M+\sqrt{r^{2}-2Mr}\right),

(3.7)

which can be inverted to give

r=\rho\left(1+\frac{M}{2\rho}\right)^{2}.

(3.8)

As we will see below, the condition
\rho(2M)=M/2
ensures that asymptotically at space-like infinity the refractive index becomes one as we would expect for a vacuum in the absence of any gravitational field. At this point, by means of the second equation in (
3.4
) we conclude that the conformal factor takes the following form

\eta(\rho)=\frac{\left(1+\frac{M}{2\rho}\right)^{3}}{1-\frac{M}{2\rho}}.

(3.9)

At this point a comment is in order. If we stick to the fact that the refractive index is the ratio between the speed of light in vacuum and its phase velocity in a medium, then slowing down the phase velocity would result in an increase of the refractive index. The latter would diverge in the case of vanishing group velocity signalizing that light is not able to penetrate the medium. This is exactly what happens for the refractive index in (
3.9
) when the isotropic coordinates approaches the event horizon. Hence, to an observer at space-like infinity the event horizon acts like a medium with infinite refractive index. Such a behaviour is not surprising because it is well-known in General Relativity, that even though a light signal can reach the event horizon in a finite proper time, a distant observer would instead come to the conclusion that the same signal takes an infinite time (as measured by a clock attached to the observer) to cross the event horizon. We shall now discuss solutions to the null geodesic equations that will help to describe BH optics, and apply the formulation developed in Section
II
to other examples among which one that has been already discussed by Casey
casey1
but using a different approach from ours.

III.1
Central force mechanics and Binet’s equation

We can see that the metric (
3.1
) is spherically symmetric, which means that
we are dealing with mechanical systems governed by central forces. For various force
laws, the solutions to the equations will describe their respective orbits, allowing us to
use existing solutions from dynamics to describe null geodesic mechanics. More precisely, the central-force motion can be deduced from the null geodesics of (
3.1
). If we start with the null geodesics of the aforementioned metric upon restriction to motion in the plane
\vartheta=\pi/2
and we set
n=3
, the condition
g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}=0
gives rise to the equation

f(r)\dot{t}^{2}-\frac{\dot{r}^{2}}{g(r)}-r^{2}\dot{\varphi}^{2}=0,

(3.10)

where the dot means differentiation with respect to some affine parameter
\lambda
. Since the original metric admits two Killing vectors
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{\varphi}
, there are two integrals of motion

p_{t}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{t}}=f(r)\dot{t}=E,\quad p_{\varphi}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\varphi}}=r^{2}\dot{\varphi}=L,

(3.11)

where
2\mathcal{L}=g_{\mu\nu}\dot{x}^{\mu}\dot{x}^{\nu}
while
E
and
L
denote the energy and angular momentum of the massless particle, respectively. Replacing (
3.11
) into (
3.10
) gives rise to the following nonlinear ODE

\frac{f(r)}{g(r)}\left(\frac{dr}{d\lambda}\right)^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}f(r)=E^{2}.

(3.12)

If we introduce a tortoise coordinate
r_{*}=r_{*}(r)
defined through the equation

\frac{dr_{*}}{dr}=\sqrt{\frac{f(r)}{g(r)}},

(3.13)

then, (
3.12
) becomes

\left(\frac{dr_{*}}{d\lambda}\right)^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}f(r)=E^{2}

(3.14)

with
r=r(r_{*})
. Finally, differentiating the above equation with respect to
\lambda
yields

\frac{d^{2}r_{*}}{d\lambda^{2}}=-\frac{dV_{c}}{dr_{*}},\quad V_{c}(r_{*})=\frac{L^{2}}{2r^{2}(r_{*})}f(r(r_{*})).

(3.15)

Equation (
3.15
) describes a central force system with potential
V_{c}(r_{*})
for massless particles in spherically symmetric solutions to Einstein field equations. If we consider the tortoise coordinate
r_{*}
as a function of the azimuthal angle
\varphi
, then by means of the second relation in (
3.11
) we find that

\frac{dr_{*}}{d\lambda}=\frac{L}{r^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{f(r)}{g(r)}}\frac{dr}{d\varphi},

(3.16)

which in turn allows us to rewrite (
3.14
) as

\frac{L^{2}}{r^{4}}\frac{f(r)}{g(r)}\left(\frac{dr}{d\varphi}\right)^{2}+\frac{L^{2}}{r^{2}}f(r)=E^{2}.

(3.17)

Finally, setting
u=1/r
and introducing the impact parameter
1/b^{2}=E^{2}/L^{2}
leads to the following equation

\frac{\widetilde{f}(u)}{\widetilde{g}(u)}\left(\frac{du}{d\varphi}\right)^{2}+u^{2}\widetilde{f}(u)=\frac{1}{b^{2}},

(3.18)

which can be cast into the form

\left(\frac{du}{d\varphi}\right)^{2}+u^{2}=-u^{2}\widetilde{G}(u)+\frac{1}{b^{2}}\frac{\widetilde{g}(u)}{\widetilde{f}(u)}

(3.19)

whith
\widetilde{G}=G(1/u)
,
\widetilde{f}=f(1/u)
and
\widetilde{g}=g(1/u)
. Equation (
3.19
) describes the geometry of the null geodesics in the invariant plane. Taking into account that as a result of the transformation
u=1/r
on the metric (

The black-hole space-time known as the rotating (Kerr) BH is a stationary metric whose line element in geometrized units

IV
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper we have explored three space-times: the
ST
metric, the Kerr manifold and the
TS
geometry with a certain choice for the deformation parameter. We have derived new expressions for the refractive indices of the ST and Kerr metrics. Moreover, we obtained a general formula representing the deflection angle in the weak field approximation valid at first order in the perturbative parameter and for an arbitrary number of spatial dimensions in the case of the ST manifold. In the context of the Kerr metric, we were able to derive a remarkable formula for the deflection angle in the weak lensing regime for the light paths constrained to the equatorial plane of the BH with a low spin value. Regarding the TS metric, we have corrected and improved the accuracy of a weak lensing result previously obtained by
bosewang
. While working on the TS metric we discovered that the related literature is characterized by several mistakes and misprints starting from the derivation of the TS metric and extending until the most recent work on that metric by
Bambi
where the shadow of a TS gravitational object with
\delta=2
is studied. Instead of using the Boyer-Lindquist (BL) coordinates appropriate to the aforementioned case as given by
Yam02
, the author in
Bambi
adopted the BL coordinates for the case
\delta=1
which corresponds to the Kerr metric. In a forth-coming paper we will focus our attention on the study of circular null orbits, their (in)stability and the so-called Bose conjecture
bosewang
for the TS metric.
