2.1
Persistent homology and cohomology

Let

Definition 2.1

Let

to

.

The ring of integers

is an ordered set with the order given by

is a functor

to the category of
\mathbf{k}
-linear spaces. Let

be a persistence simplicial complex, that is, a filtration of simplicial complexes satisfies

(

For each

is a simplicial complex. For any integers

(
ii
)

For

f_{j,k}\circ f_{i,j}=f_{i,k}
.

There are many ways to obtain a persistence simplicial complex. The Vietoris-Rips complexes and Cěch complexes are the classical constructions from a data-set

By taking

Note that the homology

gives an isomorphism of rings.

A monoid

The Cayley-persistence module

3.1
Cayley grading and Cayley-persistence objects

Let
G
be a group. A ring

is
G
-graded

[
5
]
if there is a decomposition of

as an additive group into the direct sum of subgroups

R=\bigoplus_{g\in G}R_{g}

such that
R_{g}R_{h}\subseteq R_{gh}
for any
g,h\in G
. A (left)
R
-module
M
is
G
-graded if there is a direct sum decomposition

M=\bigoplus_{g\in G}M_{g}

of
M
such that
R_{g}M_{h}\subseteq M_{gh}
.

Example 3.1
.

(i)

The usual
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-graded ring is a group graded ring with group
G=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.

(ii)

A
\mathbb{Z}/2
-graded ring is a group graded ring with group
G=\mathbb{Z}/2
.

(iii)

Let
R
be a commutative ring with unit, then
R[G]
is a group ring. There is a natural
G
-grading on
R[G]
.

A digraph
D=(V,E)
can be regarded as a category with

(
i
)

objects: the vertices in
V
;

(
ii
)

morphisms: for every
x,y
in
V
,

\mathrm{Hom}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{f_{xy}:x\rightarrow y\},&\hbox{if there exists a directed path from $x$ to $y$;}\\
\emptyset,&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

We denote
\mathrm{cat}(D)
the category given by the digraph
D
. Let
G
be a group and
S
be a subset of
G
. We have a Cayley digraph
\mathrm{Cay(}G,S)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\langle S\rangle
be the semigroup generated by
S
in
G
. Then
\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))=\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,\langle S\rangle))
.

Proof.

Obviously,
\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))
is a subcategory of
\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,\langle S\rangle))
with objects given by
G
. For a morphism
f:a\rightarrow b
in
\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,\langle S\rangle))
, we have
ba^{-1}\in\langle S\rangle
. It follows that
ba^{-1}=s_{1}s_{2}\cdots s_{n}
for some
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\in S
. Thus we have a directed path

a\rightarrow s_{n}a\rightarrow s_{n-1}s_{n}a\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow s_{2}\cdots s_{n-1}s_{n}a\rightarrow b

in
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
. It follows that
f\in\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))
.
∎

From now on, we always assume that
S
is a monoid with the identity element

.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
be a Cayley digraph, and let
\mathfrak{C}
be a category. A
Cayley-persistence object
is a functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
from the category
\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))
to the category
\mathfrak{C}
. Dually, the
Cayley-copersistence object
is a contravariant functor
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
from the category
\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))
to the category
\mathfrak{C}
.

The Cayley-persistence object
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
can be also written as follows.

(
i
)

\mathcal{F}_{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
for
a\in G
. For
ba^{-1}\in S
, there is a morphism
f_{a,b}:\mathcal{F}_{a}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}_{b}
in
\mathfrak{C}
.

(
ii
)

For

, we have
f_{b,c}\circ f_{a,b}=f_{a,c}
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
be a Cayley-persistence object. Given

G
-graded morphism

for each

by the functorial property. Let

one has that

is a

-module. In a word, we can obtain a group graded module from a Cayley-persistence persistence object.

Considering the

This shows that

is a finite dimensional
\mathbf{k}
-linear space. Let

is a finite set. Then

, where
\mathbf{k}[E_{a}]
is the
\mathbf{k}
-linear space generated by

. For each element
\mathcal{F}(l_{ax_{i}^{-1}})m_{i}^{x_{i}}\in E_{a}
, one has
ax_{i}^{-1}\in S
and

\mathcal{F}(l_{z})\mathcal{F}(l_{ax_{i}^{-1}})m_{i}^{x_{i}}=\mathcal{F}(l_{zax_{i}^{-1}})m_{i}^{x_{i}}\in E_{za}

since
zax_{i}^{-1}\in S
. Let
\mathcal{L}_{S}
be the monoid given by the elements
\mathcal{F}(l_{x}),x\in S
.
Thus there is a monoid action of
\mathcal{L}_{S}
on
E

\mathcal{F}(l_{x})\cdot e_{a}=\mathcal{F}(l_{x})e_{a},\quad x\in S,e_{a}\in E.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}
be the set given by all the elements
\mathcal{F}(l_{x})
such that
x,x^{-1}\in S
. It can be verified
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}
is a group.
Let
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}
be the complementary set of
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}
in
\mathcal{L}_{S}
. It is obvious that
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}
is a semigroup. We assert that there is a group action of
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}
on
E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
. Indeed, if
e_{a}\in E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
and the element

is invertible, it suffices to show

\mathcal{F}(l_{x})e_{a}\in E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E.

Assume that
\mathcal{F}(l_{x})e_{a}\in\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
. Then
e_{a}=\mathcal{F}(l_{x^{-1}})\mathcal{F}(l_{x})e_{a}\in\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
, a contradiction. Moreover, each element in
E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
must be of the form
\mathcal{F}(l_{z})m_{i}^{x_{i}}
for some
m_{i}^{x_{i}}
and invertible
z
in
S
, otherwise,
z
would be not invertible in
S
and
\mathcal{F}(l_{z})m_{i}^{x_{i}}\in\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
.
Now, there is a decomposition of

into different orbits of
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}
-action. Each orbit is given by
\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}m_{i}^{x_{i}}
for some
m_{i}^{x_{i}}
.
For each orbit in
E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
, we choose an element
m_{i}^{x_{i}}
in
E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E
. Let

be the set of the chosen elements. Then

is finite with the number of elements

|B|\leq n.

In particular, if the identity element is the unique invertible element in

, then

. Let

B_{free}=\{b\in B|\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}b\cap\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}(B\backslash\{b\})=\emptyset\},\quad B_{tor}=B\backslash B_{free}.

We write

and

. Then we list the elements in

Consider the morphism

\pi:\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}R_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot e_{i}^{x_{i}}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{l}R_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}^{y_{j}}\longrightarrow M_{\mathcal{F}}

from a free
R_{\mathcal{F}}
-mudule to

given by

\pi(e_{i}^{x_{i}})=b_{i}^{x_{i}},\quad\pi(\varepsilon_{j}^{x_{j}})=\beta_{j}^{x_{j}},\quad i=1,\dots,k,j=1,\dots,l.

By the definition of orbit space, we have

. Note that

E\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{S}(E\backslash\mathcal{L}_{S}^{+}E)\subseteq E,

we obtain
\mathcal{L}_{S}\mathcal{L}_{S}^{\ast}B=E
, thus we have
\mathcal{L}_{S}B=E
. It follows that

M_{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathbf{k}[E]=\mathbf{k}[\mathcal{L}_{S}B]\subseteq R_{\mathcal{F}}[B].

Here,
R_{\mathcal{F}}[B]
denotes the
R_{\mathcal{F}}
-modules generated by the elements in

, that is,

. Hence, each element in

can be written in the form

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{F}(l_{u_{i}})e_{i}^{x_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\mathcal{F}(l_{v_{j}})\varepsilon_{j}^{y_{j}}

, which shows that

is a surjection. Next, we will show that
\mathcal{F}(l_{y})b_{t}^{x_{t}}
is different from all the elements of the following forms

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}(l_{y})b_{t}^{x_{t}}=\mathcal{F}(l_{z})c_{s}^{x_{s}}
, where

denotes

or

(

)

When one of

. We may assume

which implies that

(

\pi\left(\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}R_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot e_{i}^{x_{i}}\oplus\bigoplus_{j=1}^{l}R_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}^{y_{j}}\right)=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}R_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot b_{i}^{x_{i}}\oplus\pi\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{l}R_{\mathcal{F}}\cdot\varepsilon_{j}^{y_{j}}\right).

Remark 3.1
.

is a finitely generated monoid makes it possible for us to obtain the finiteness of the time of disappearance and meeting of generators, that is,

is a finitely generated
R_{\mathcal{F}}
-module.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that

3.3
The Cayley persistent homology

As an application of the module structure of
M_{\mathcal{F}}
, we introduce the Cayley persistent homology in this section. The simplicial complexes considered are always assumed to be finite.
Unless otherwise specified, we always use the reduced homology for convenience. We will first study when a Cayley persistent homology is a finitely generated module.

From now on, let
S\subseteq G
be a monoid such that the identity element
e
is the unique invertible element in
S
. Then the group
G
can be regarded as a poset with partial order given by
a\leq b
if
ba^{-1}\in S
.

Definition 3.2
.

A Cayley persistence object
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
is called
noetherian
if every ordered subset
X\subseteq G
has an element
x
such that
\mathcal{F}(l_{z}):\mathcal{F}_{x}\to\mathcal{F}_{zx}
is the identity morphism for all
z\in S
.

A Cayley persistence object
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
is called
lower bounded
if there exists an element
a\in G
such that
\mathcal{F}_{x}=\emptyset
unless
x\geq a
.

Generally,
\mathcal{F}
does not have to be a finitely generated
R_{\mathcal{F}}
-module even if
\mathcal{F}_{a}
is finite for all
a\in G
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}:\mathrm{cat}(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))\rightarrow\mathfrak{C}
be a noetherian and lower bounded Cayley-persistence simplicial complex. If
S
is a finitely generated commutative monoid, then
M_{\mathcal{F}}
is a finitely generated
R_{\mathcal{F}}
-module.

Proof.

Since
S
is a finitely generated commutative monoid, there exists a finite set
S_{gen}=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{p}\}
generating
S
.
As
\mathcal{F}
is lower bounded, there exists an element
a\in G
such that
\mathcal{F}_{x}=\emptyset
unless
a\leq x
. Let
X_{i}=\{a,s_{i}a,\dots,s_{i}^{k}a,\dots\}
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is noetherian, there exist integers
k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{p}
such that

\mathcal{F}(l_{z}):\mathcal{F}_{s_{i}^{k_{i}}a}\to\mathcal{F}_{zs_{i}^{k_{i}}a},\quad i=1,\dots,p

are the identity morphisms for all
z\in S
. Let

and

Then the morphism

\mathcal{F}_{s_{i}^{k_{i}}a}\to\mathcal{F}_{b},\quad i=1,\dots,p

is an identity map for all
1\leq i\leq p
. It follows that
\mathcal{F}_{s_{i}^{k_{i}}a}=\mathcal{F}_{s_{j}^{k_{j}}a}
for any

. This shows that

is either
\mathcal{F}_{b}
or
\emptyset

-module.

\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}
is nontrivial, then

\min(b_{i},b_{j})\leq\sup(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})),\quad\max(a_{i},a_{j})\leq\inf(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})).

Proof.

(i)
Suppose that

\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}=\sum\limits_{k\in J}\lambda_{k}\alpha^{k}\cdot L_{z_{k}},\quad\lambda_{k}\in\mathbf{k},z_{k}\in S

for some finite index set
J
. By Lemma
4.15
, we have
I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})=\bigcup\limits_{i\in J}I(\alpha^{i})
.
Note that the grading of
\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}
is

\deg(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})=b_{i}\times b_{j}=\min(b_{i},b_{j})=z_{k}^{-1}b_{k}

for any
k\in J
. We have that

\sup(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}))=\coprod_{k\in J}b_{k}=\coprod_{k\in J}\min(b_{i},b_{j})z_{k}=\min(b_{i},b_{j})\coprod_{k\in J}z_{k}.

Since
J
is finite, we have
\min(b_{i},b_{j})^{-1}\sup(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}))=\coprod\limits_{k\in J}z_{k}\in S
. It follows that

\min(b_{i},b_{j})\leq\sup(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})).

(ii)
Suppose that
\alpha^{i}\cdot L_{x}=0
. By the proof of Theorem
4.7
, we have

0=(\alpha^{i}\cdot L_{x})\cdot\alpha^{j}=(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})\cdot L_{(x^{-1}b_{i}\times b_{j})^{-1}(b_{i}\times b_{j})}.

It follows that
(x^{-1}b_{i}\times b_{j})^{-1}(b_{i}\times b_{j})>c_{k}^{-1}z_{k}^{-1}b_{k}=c_{k}^{-1}(b_{i}\times b_{j})
for any
c_{k}\in I(\alpha_{k})
. One has that
x^{-1}b_{i}\times b_{j}<c_{k}
for any
c_{k}\in I(\alpha_{k})
.

We first consider the case
I(\alpha^{i})=\{x\in G|a_{i}<x\leq b_{i}\}
. Then we have
\alpha^{i}\cdot L_{a_{i}^{-1}b_{i}}=0
.
It follows that
a_{i}\times b_{j}<c_{k}
for any
c_{k}\in I(\alpha^{k})
. Hence, we obtain
a_{i}\times b_{j}\leq a_{k}
for any
k\in J
. Note that
\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}
is nontrivial. We have
a_{i}\times b_{j}=a_{i}
, which shows that
a_{i}\leq a_{k}
for any
k\in J
. It follows that
a_{i}\leq\prod\limits_{k\in J}a_{k}=\inf(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}))
.
Now, we consider the case
I(\alpha^{i})=\{x\in G|a_{i}\leq x\leq b_{i}\}
. Then we have
\alpha^{i}\cdot L_{x}=0
for any
x>a_{i}^{-1}b_{i}
.
It follows that
x^{-1}b_{i}\times b_{j}<c_{k}
for any
c_{k}\in I(\alpha^{k})
. Since
x^{-1}b_{i}<a_{i}
, we have
x^{-1}b_{i}<c_{k}
for any
c_{k}\in I(\alpha^{k})
and
x>a_{i}^{-1}b_{i}
.
It follows that
a_{i}\leq c_{k}
for any
c_{k}\in I(\alpha^{k})
, which leads to
a_{i}\leq\inf(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}))
.
Similarly, we have
a_{j}\leq\inf(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j}))
. Hence, we obtain

\max(a_{i},a_{j})\leq\inf(I(\alpha^{i}\cdot\alpha^{j})).

The desired result follows.
∎
