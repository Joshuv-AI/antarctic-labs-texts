Bohr recurrence and density of non-lacunary semigroups of ℕ {\mathbb{N}} Thanks: NF was supported by the research grant ELIDEK HFRI-NextGenerationEU-15689 and BK was partially supported by the National Science Foundation grant DMS-2348315.
Nikos Frantzikinakis, Bernard Host, Bryna Kra

Abstract
. A subset R R of integers is a set of Bohr recurrence if every rotation on 𝕋 d {\mathbb{T}}^{d} returns arbitrarily close to zero under some non-zero multiple of R R . We show that the set { k ! 2 m 3 n : k , m , n ∈ ℕ } \{k!\,2^{m}3^{n}\colon k,m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\} is a set of Bohr recurrence. This is a particular case of a more general statement about images of such sets under any integer polynomial with zero constant term. We also show that if P P is a real polynomial with at least one non-constant irrational coefficient, then the set { P ( 2 m 3 n ) : m , n ∈ ℕ } \{P(2^{m}3^{n})\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\} is dense in 𝕋 {\mathbb{T}} , thus providing a joint generalization of two well-known results, one of Furstenberg and one of Weyl.

1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Notions of recurrence

The recurrence properties of a dynamical system are a classical way to study the system’s qualitative and quantitative properties. We focus on recurrence in a topological dynamical system
(X,T)
, meaning that
X
is a compact metric space and
T\colon X\to X
is a homeomorphism, and further assume that the system is minimal, meaning that no proper closed set in
X
is
T
-invariant. A set
R\subset{\mathbb{N}}
is a set of
(topological) recurrence
if for every minimal system
(X,T)
and non-empty open set
U\subset X
, there is some
r\in R
such that
U\cap T^{-r}U\neq\emptyset
. It follows quickly from the compactness of
X
that
{\mathbb{N}}
is a set of recurrence, and many sparser subsets have been shown to be sets of recurrence. These include, for example, the set of differences of an infinite set (see
[
6
]
for this example and others), the set
\{P(n):n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
where
P
is any integer valued polynomial with no constant term (see
[
5
,
13
]
), and the set of shifted primes
\{p-1:p\text{ is a prime}\}
(see
[
14
]
). It is also easy to check that parity obstructions give rise to examples, such as the odd numbers or any shift of the primes other than
\pm 1
, that cannot be sets of recurrence.

A natural question is if restricting the class of topological systems changes the possible sets of recurrence. The most basic nontrivial systems to consider are rotations on the
d
-dimensional torus
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. For

x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, write
\left\|x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}:=d(x,{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
, and we define sets of recurrence for these systems.

Definition
.

A subset
R
of the integers is a
set of Bohr recurrence
if for every integer
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, all
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists non-zero
r\in R
such that

\left\|r\alpha_{j}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon\quad\text{for }j=1,\dots,d.

Equivalently, the set
R
is a set of Bohr recurrence if for every integer
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and every
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, the
point
0
belongs to the closure of the set
\{rx\colon r\in R\setminus\{0\}\}
in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
.
By building an appropriate rotation, one can check that no lacunary set can be a set of Bohr recurrence, and therefore cannot be a set of recurrence.
An important open problem, popularized by Katznelson
[
10
]
, is whether a set of Bohr recurrence is necessarily a set of topological recurrence. See
[
16
,
3
,
9
,
7
,
8
]
for various equivalent formulations of this question and related results.

1.2.
Non-lacunary semigroups

Our objective is to derive recurrence and density properties of sets generated by thin non-lacunary semigroups of the integers, a prototypical example being the set

\{2^{m}3^{n}\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Again, parity reasons prevent this from being a set of Bohr recurrence, but after removing this obstruction we are led to the well-known question in dynamics
whether the set

\{k!\,2^{m}3^{n}\colon k,m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

is a set of topological recurrence (more generally one can ask if this is a set of measurable multiple recurrence). Although we are unable to answer these questions,
we derive some recurrence results of intermediate strength, which follow from Furstenberg’s Diophantine Theorem (see Section
1.3
), using elementary manipulations.

Two positive integers are
multiplicatively independent
if their only common power is
1
. The first main goal of this note is to establish the following result:

Theorem 1
.

Let
p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
be multiplicatively independent integers. Then the set
\{k!\,p^{m}q^{n}\colon k,m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is a set of Bohr recurrence.

More generally, it follows from our argument that
k!
can be replaced by any sequence that contains multiples of every positive integer.

On the other hand, considering rational multiples of elements of this set shows that the multiplicative factor
k!
cannot be removed, and, in fact, it is necessary for our argument to work even if the real numbers
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
have no rational relations.
As shown in
[
12
, Theorem 2]
, for
d\geq 4
, there exists
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
with rationally independent coordinates for which the set
\{2^{m}3^{n}x\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is not dense in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Although for fixed
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
this particular obstruction
can be removed if
more powers are included in the set (see
[
15
]
for a density statement on
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
that uses three powers),
[
12
, Theorem 2]
shows that the problem persists even when using
\ell
powers instead of two and taking
d
large enough; in this case we have non-density in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
for some
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
with rationally independent coordinates whenever
d\geq 2\ell
. Furthermore, by slightly modifying the example in
[
12
, Lemma 4.2]
, we obtain a point
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{4}
with rationally independent coordinates, such that for some
\varepsilon>0
we have
\left\|2^{m}3^{n}x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{4}}\geq\varepsilon
for every
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(see Section
3.2
).

We also remark that combining Theorem
1
with
[
9
, Theorem 4.1]
and
[
7
, Theorem A]
, we deduce that the sets in Theorem
1
(and in Theorem
2
below) are also good for topological recurrence for all nilsystems and for a large class of skew product systems.

In the proof of Theorem
1
, we consider a more general situation where sets of the form
\{p^{m}q^{n}\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
are replaced by more general non-lacunary semigroups of
{\mathbb{N}}
.

Definition
.

A
semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
is a sub-semigroup of
({\mathbb{N}},\cdot)
and it is
non-lacunary
if it contains two multiplicatively independent integers.

Equivalently, a semigroup is non-lacunary if it is not contained in the set of powers of a single integer.
It is shown in
[
4
, Lemma IV.1]
that a semigroup
\Sigma=\{s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots\}
of
{\mathbb{N}}
is non-lacunary if and only if
s_{n+1}/s_{n}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
.

We say that
P
is an
integer polynomial
if it has integer coefficients. We prove the following result, generalizing Theorem
1
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
,
K\subset{\mathbb{N}}
be a set that contains multiples of every positive integer,
and
P
be a non-constant integer polynomial with
P(0)=0
. Then the set
\{P(ks)\colon k\in K,s\in\Sigma\bigr\}
is a set of Bohr recurrence.

In particular, if
p,q\in{\mathbb{N}}
are multiplicatively independent and
P
is a non-constant integer polynomial with zero constant term, then the set

\{P(k!\,p^{m}q^{n})\colon k,m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\bigr\}

is a set of Bohr recurrence.
By taking
P(n)=n
, Theorem
1
follows.

The
argument used to prove Theorem
2
can also be used, essentially without change, to show that if
P_{1},\ldots,P_{\ell}
are integer polynomials with zero constant terms, then for every
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
k\in K
and
s\in\Sigma
such that
\left\|P_{j}(k\cdot s)\,\alpha_{j}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon
for
j=1,\ldots,\ell
.

We prove Theorem
2
in Section
3
. The main ingredient is Proposition
4
, which is of independent interest and shows the existence of a point with rational coordinates in the orbit closure of non-lacunary semigroup actions of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. A special case of this result is that if
\Sigma
is a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
and
A
is a non-empty closed subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, which is invariant under coordinate-wise multiplication by
elements of the form
(s,\ldots,s)
for every
s\in\Sigma
, then
A
contains a point with rational coordinates. Theorem
1
is a direct consequence of this result.

1.3.
A Weyl-type extension of Furstenberg’s result

We recall a celebrated theorem of Furstenberg
[
4
, Theorem IV.1]
(see also
[
2
]
for an elementary proof):

Theorem

(Furstenberg)
.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
and
\alpha
be irrational. Then the set

\{s\alpha\colon s\in\Sigma\}

is dense in
{\mathbb{T}}
.

This immediately implies that a closed
\Sigma
-invariant subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
is either dense or finite. In the latter case, it consists entirely of rational points.

Another celebrated theorem, this time of Weyl
[
17
]
, is as follows:

Theorem

(Weyl)
.

Let
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
be a polynomial with at least one non-constant irrational coefficient. Then the set

\{P(n)\colon n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}

is dense in
{\mathbb{T}}
.

We prove the following common generalization of these two density statements:

Theorem 3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup and
P\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
be a polynomial with at least one non-constant irrational coefficient. Then the set

\{P(s)\colon s\in\Sigma\}

is dense in
{\mathbb{T}}
.

It should be noted that Theorem
3
follows from
[
11
, Theorem 1.2]
, but the argument presented here is somewhat different and more detailed.

Taking
P(n):=n
gives Furstenberg’s result and taking
\Sigma:={\mathbb{N}}
gives Weyl’s result. On the other hand, even for the simplest non-linear cases, such as
P(n)=(n^{2}+n)\alpha
, where
\alpha
is irrational, it is not clear how to proceed and prove density, since the set
\{P(s)\colon s\in\Sigma\}

does not seem to satisfy any useful
\Sigma
-invariance property. A key maneuver in our argument is to instead work with the subset

\{(s\alpha,s^{2}\alpha)\colon s\in\Sigma\}
of
{\mathbb{T}}^{2}
, which is
invariant under multiplication by all elements of the set
\{(s,s^{2})\colon s\in\Sigma\}
.
In Proposition
5
, we show that this invariance implies that the closure of this set contains a line segment parallel to some coordinate axis. Theorem
3
follows easily from this fact.

Finally, we note that more general
d
-dimensional versions of Theorem
3
fail.
For example, if
P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}[t]
are such that every non-trivial integer combination of the polynomials is a polynomial with at least one irrational non-constant coefficient, and
\Sigma
is a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, it is not always true that
the set

\{(P_{1}(s),\ldots,P_{d}(s))\colon s\in\Sigma\}

is dense in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
(this holds for
\Sigma:={\mathbb{N}}
since equidistribution follows from Weyl’s criterion). To see this, take
d=4
and

\Sigma:=\{2^{m}3^{n}\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
,
P_{j}(n)=n\alpha_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,4
, for the rationally independent
reals
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{4}
constructed in
[
12
, Lemma 4.2]
, so that the above set is not dense.

1.4.
Notation

We denote the set of positive integers by
{\mathbb{N}}
. We let
{\mathbb{T}}

denote the one dimensional torus
{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
, and we often identify it with
[0,1)
.
We denote elements of
{\mathbb{T}}
by real numbers and we implicitly assume that these real numbers are taken modulo
1
.

If
(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
are two vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define their product by coordinate-wise multiplication as follows

(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})\cdot(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=(t_{1}x_{1},\ldots,t_{d}x_{d}).

Likewise, if
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, we write

(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\cdot(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=(k_{1}x_{1},\ldots,k_{d}x_{d}).

If
S
is a subset of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
A
a subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, we say that
A
is
S
-invariant
or
invariant under
S
if
s\cdot x\in A
for every
s\in S
,
x\in A
.

2.
Existence of rational points

Furstenberg’s Theorem implies that if
\Sigma
is a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, then a closed
\Sigma
-invariant subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
always contains a rational point. A natural question to consider is whether there are generalizations of this statement in higher dimensions. We prove that this is the case, and use this result in the proofs of both Theorems
2
and
3
.
To give the precise statement, we need another definition.

Definition
.

A point
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
is
rational
if its coordinates are rational. If
\Sigma
is a subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
, we say that the
denominators of a rational point
x
are relatively prime to
\Sigma
if its coordinates are rationals with denominators relatively prime to each element of
\Sigma
. By convention, the denominator of
0
is
1
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\in{\mathbb{N}}
(not necessarily distinct). Let
A
be a non-empty closed subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, invariant under

\Sigma^{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}}:=\{(s^{\ell_{1}},\ldots,s^{\ell_{d}}):s\in\Sigma\}.

Then
A
contains a rational point with denominators relatively prime to
\Sigma
.

Remark
.

For the proof of Theorem
1
, only the case
\ell_{1}=\cdots=\ell_{d}=1
is needed. The more general case is needed in the proofs of Theorems
2
and
3
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Throughout, for
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
we let

\Sigma^{\ell}:=\{s^{\ell}:s\in\Sigma\}.

The case
d=1

Since
\Sigma^{\ell_{1}}
is a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, by Furstenberg’s Theorem (see Section
1.3
) we have that
A
contains a rational point
x=a/b
with
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Write
b=b^{\prime}c
, where
b^{\prime}
divides some element
k
of
\Sigma
and
c
is relatively prime to every element of
\Sigma
. Then
kx=\frac{a(k/b^{\prime})}{c}
and thus this point of
A
is rational with denominator relatively prime to
\Sigma
.

Suppose that the result holds for some
d\in{\mathbb{N}}

We show that it holds for
d+1
. Let
A
be a non-empty closed subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}
, invariant under
\Sigma^{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d+1}}
, where
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d+1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
B
be the image of
A
under the coordinate projection

(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},x_{d+1})\mapsto x_{d+1}

of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d+1}
onto
{\mathbb{T}}
. Then
B
is a non-empty closed subset of
{\mathbb{T}}
, invariant under the non-lacunary semigroup
\Sigma^{\ell_{d+1}}
, and hence by the case
d=1
,
it contains a rational point
y
with denominator relatively prime to
\Sigma
.

Write

y=a/m
, where
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
m
is a positive integer relatively prime to every element of
\Sigma
, and let

\Sigma_{m}:=\Sigma\cap(m{\mathbb{Z}}+1).

Then
\Sigma_{m}
is a semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
with the property that

sy=y\quad\text{for every }s\in\Sigma_{m}.

We claim that
\Sigma_{m}
is non-lacunary. Indeed, if
p
and
q
are two multiplicatively independent integers belonging to
\Sigma
, then
p
and
q
are relatively prime to
m
(because the denominator of
y=a/m
is relatively prime to
\Sigma
).
Hence, by Euler’s Theorem, there exist integers
t
and
n
such that
p^{t}\equiv 1\pmod{m}
and
q^{n}\equiv 1\pmod{m}
. Then
p^{t}
and
q^{n}
are multiplicatively independent and belong to
\Sigma_{m}
and thus the semigroup
\Sigma_{m}
is non-lacunary.

We define the set

C:=\bigl\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\colon(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y)\in A\bigr\}.

Then
C
is a closed subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, and is non-empty since
y\in B
. We claim that
C
is invariant under the semigroup

\Sigma_{m}^{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}}:=\{(s^{\ell_{1}},\ldots,s^{\ell_{d}}):s\in\Sigma_{m}\}.

Indeed, if

(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in C
, using that
s^{\ell_{d+1}}y=y
for every
s\in\Sigma_{m}
and the invariance of
A

under
\Sigma^{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d+1}}
, we have that

(s^{\ell_{1}}x_{1},\dots,s^{\ell_{d}}x_{d},y)=(s^{\ell_{1}}x_{1},\dots,s^{\ell_{d}}x_{d},s^{\ell_{d+1}}y)\in A\quad\text{for every }s\in\Sigma_{m}.

Using the defining property of
C
, we deduce that
(s^{\ell_{1}}x_{1},\dots,s^{\ell_{d}}x_{d})\in C
.

Using the induction hypothesis with
C
substituted for
A
and
\Sigma_{m}^{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}}
substituted for
\Sigma^{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}}
, we deduce that
C
contains a rational point
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
with denominators relatively prime to
\Sigma
. Then the point
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d},y)
is rational with denominators relatively prime to
\Sigma
, and belongs to
A
, as desired.
∎

3.
Proof of the Bohr-recurrence result

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2

Suppose that

P(n)=c_{1}n+c_{2}n^{2}+\cdots+c_{r}n^{r}

for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

c_{1},\ldots,c_{r}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, not all of them zero. Let
\varepsilon>0
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Let
A
be the closure of the set

\{(sc_{1}x_{1},\dots,sc_{1}x_{d},s^{2}c_{2}x_{1},\dots,s^{2}c_{2}x_{d},\dots,s^{r}c_{r}x_{1},\dots,s^{r}c_{r}x_{d}):s\in\Sigma\}.

Then
A
is a non-empty closed subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{rd}
that is invariant under

\{(s,\ldots,s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{2},\ldots,s^{r},\ldots,s^{r}):s\in\Sigma\},

where each power of
s
is repeated
d
times. Therefore, by Proposition
4
,
A
contains a rational point
y\in{\mathbb{T}}^{rd}
.
Since
K
contains multiples of every positive integer, there exists
k\in K
such that all the coordinates of
ky
are integers. It follows that
0
(as an element of
{\mathbb{T}}^{rd}
) belongs to the closure of the set

\{(ksc_{1}x,(ks)^{2}c_{2}x,\ldots,(ks)^{r}c_{r}x):s\in\Sigma\}.

Since

P(ks)\,x=ksc_{1}x+(ks)^{2}c_{2}x+\cdots+(ks)^{r}c_{r}x,

we have that

\left\|P(ks)\,x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\leq\left\|ksc_{1}x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}+\left\|(ks)^{2}c_{2}x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}+\cdots+\left\|(ks)^{r}c_{r}x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}.

It follows that
0
(as an element of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
) belongs to the closure of the set

\{P(ks)\,x\colon k\in K:s\in\Sigma\bigr\}

and so

(1)

\left\|P(ks)\,x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\leq\varepsilon,\quad\text{for some }k\in K,s\in\Sigma.

The polynomial
P
has finitely many zeros, all bounded by some integer
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Estimate (
1
) also holds for the semigroup
\Sigma\cap(N,+\infty)
instead of
\Sigma
, and thus replacing
\Sigma
with this semigroup
we deduce that there exist
k\in K
and
s\in\Sigma
such that
P(ks)\neq 0
and

\left\|P(ks)\,x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{d}}\leq\varepsilon
. It follows that the set
\{P(ks)\colon k\in K,s\in\Sigma\bigr\}
is a set of Bohr recurrence.
∎

3.2.
Non Bohr recurrence of
2^{m}3^{n}
for totally ergodic rotations

Although the set
\{2^{m}3^{n}\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is not good for recurrence of rational rotations, it is reasonable to hope that it is good for recurrence for all
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
that have rationally independent coordinates.
An even more optimistic conjecture is that it is good for measurable recurrence for all totally ergodic systems. Unfortunately, we show that neither claim holds.

We claim that there exists
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{4}
with rationally independent coordinates and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\left\|2^{m}3^{n}x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{4}}\geq\varepsilon
for every
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Indeed, using
[
12
, Lemma 4.2]
, we have the existence of

y=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3},y_{4})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
with rationally independent coordinates such that for all but finitely many
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

\{2^{m}3^{n}y_{j}\}\leq 1/10

for some
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, where
\{t\}
denotes the fractional part of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Let
x_{j}:=y_{j}+1/5
,
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
, and
x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
; then obviously
x
also has rationally independent coordinates. Since
\{2^{m}3^{n}/5\}\in[1/5,4/5]

for all
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, it follows that for all but finitely many
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have
\{2^{m}3^{n}x_{j}\}\in[1/5,9/10]
for
some
j\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. Then
\left\|2^{m}3^{n}x\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{4}}\geq 1/10
for all but finitely many
m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
This immediately implies the claim.

The situation does not improve much if we consider sets of the form

\Sigma:=\{p_{1}^{n_{1}}\cdots p_{\ell}^{n_{\ell}}\colon n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{N}}\}.

Using
[
12
, Lemma 4.2]
, we can show in a similar fashion that there exists
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{2\ell}
with rationally independent coordinates and
\varepsilon>0
such that
\left\|sx\right\|_{{\mathbb{T}}^{2\ell}}\geq\varepsilon
for every
s\in\Sigma
.

However, the possibility that the set
\{2^{m}3^{n}\colon m,n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}
is good for measurable or topological recurrence for
systems without rotational factors (i.e. for weakly mixing systems) remains open. For measurable recurrence this problem is explicitly stated in
[
1
, Question 3]
.

4.
Proof of the density result

Our goal in this section is to prove Theorem
3
.

4.1.
A reduction

Theorem
3
is a direct consequence of the following higher dimensional density result. This higher dimensional setting has the advantage of some dilation invariance that the
1
-dimensional setting lacks.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}
denote the standard unit vectors of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
or
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, depending on the context.

Proposition 5
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, and

A
be a closed
infinite subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
that is invariant under

\{(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\colon s\in\Sigma\}.

Then

\{x+te_{j}\colon t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}\subset A

for some rational point
x\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Proof of Theorem
3
assuming Proposition
5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}
be arbitrary. It suffices to show that there exists
s\in\Sigma
such that
\left\|P(s)-\alpha\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon
.

We can assume that
P(0)=0
and write
P(n)=c_{1}n+\cdots+c_{d}n^{d}
for some
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and real numbers
c_{1},\ldots,c_{d}
, at least one of which is irrational.
Let
A
be the closure of the set

\{(c_{1}s,c_{2}s^{2},\ldots,c_{d}s^{d})\colon n\in\Sigma\},

considered as a subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Clearly
A
is invariant under the map
x\mapsto(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\cdot x
for every
s\in\Sigma
, and since at least one of the
c_{1},\ldots,c_{d}
is irrational the set
A
is infinite. It follows from Proposition
5
that there exist some rational point
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and
j_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
\{x+te_{j_{0}}\colon t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}\subset A
. Hence, for
t:=\alpha-\sum_{1\leq j\leq d}x_{j}\pmod{1}
there exists
s\in\Sigma
such that

\left\|c_{j}s^{j}-x_{j}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}\,\text{ for }\,j\neq j_{0}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left\|c_{j_{0}}s^{j_{0}}-\alpha+\sum_{1\leq j\leq d,\,j\neq j_{0}}x_{j}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{d}.

Then

\left\|P(s)-\alpha\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq d,\,j\neq j_{0}}\left\|c_{j}s^{j}-x_{j}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}+\left\|c_{j_{0}}s^{j_{0}}-\alpha+\sum_{1\leq j\leq d,\,j\neq j_{0}}x_{j}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon,

completing the proof.
∎

4.2.
A key density property on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}

The main goal in this subsection is to prove Proposition
7
. In its proof we use the following fact:

Lemma 6
.

For every
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{d}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, the set

\{(tu_{1}+k_{1},t^{2}u_{2},\ldots,t^{d}u_{d}+k_{d})\colon t\in(0,+\infty),k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}

is dense in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

Proof.

We can assume that
u_{1}>0
.
First, note that if we let
t^{\prime}:=tu_{1}
and
u_{j}^{\prime}:=u_{j}/u_{1}^{j}
, for
j=2,\ldots,d
, we see that it is sufficient to verify the stated property when
u_{1}=1
.
We work with this assumption from now on.

Let
x:=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. It suffices to show that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
t>0
such that

(2)

\left\|t-x_{1}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon,\,\left\|t^{2}u_{2}-x_{2}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon,\ldots,\,\left\|t^{d}u_{d}-x_{d}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon.

So let
\varepsilon>0
. First, we claim that there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that every non-trivial integer combination of the polynomials (in
k
)

(k+x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{2}u_{2},\ldots,\,(k+x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{d}u_{d}

has at least one irrational non-constant coefficient. Indeed, note that modulo rational multiples, the coefficients of the (degree
1
) monomial
k
in these polynomials are respectively

(x_{1}+\delta_{0})u_{2},\,(x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{2}u_{3},\ldots,\,(x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{d-1}u_{d}.

So it suffices to choose
\delta_{0}
such that all non-trivial integer combinations of these numbers are irrational. Such a choice of
\delta_{0}
exists, since for any choice of
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, not all of them
0
, the equation

\ell_{1}u_{2}\delta+\ell_{2}u_{3}\delta^{2}+\cdots+\ell_{d}u_{d}\delta^{d-1}=0

has finitely many solutions in the variable
\delta
, so there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon)
such that
x_{1}+\delta_{0}
avoids all these (countably many) solutions. This proves the claim.

For this choice of
\delta_{0}
, Weyl’s criterion
[
17
]
gives that the sequence

\big((k+x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{2}u_{2},\ldots,(k+x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{d}u_{d}\big)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is equidistributed in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
and thus dense in
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. It follows that there exists an integer
k_{0}>|x_{1}|+\delta_{0}
such that

\left\|(k_{0}+x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{2}-x_{2}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon,\ldots,\left\|(k_{0}+x_{1}+\delta_{0})^{d}-x_{d}\right\|_{\mathbb{T}}\leq\varepsilon.

Letting
t:=k_{0}+x_{1}+\delta_{0}
, and recalling that
\delta_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, we deduce that (
2
) holds, completing the proof.
∎

Proposition 7
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, and

A
be a closed
subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, invariant under

\{(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\colon s\in\Sigma\}.

If
0
is a non-isolated point of
A
, then
\{te_{j}\colon t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}\subset A

for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Proof.

For
d=1
, this follows from Furstenberg’s Theorem (see Section
1.3
).
For general
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we argue as follows using ideas from
[
4
]
and
[
15
]
, with the caveat that we are not working with the Euclidean norm but with the quantity defined in (
3
).

Let
\pi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
be the natural projection and let
B=\pi^{-1}(A)=A+{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. Then for every
s\in\Sigma
, the closed subset
B
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is invariant under the map
x\mapsto(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\cdot x
, and the point
0
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a non-isolated point of
B
.

For
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
let

(3)

\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert:=|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\cdots+|x_{d}|^{\frac{1}{d}}.

Note that
\lvert\!|\!|\cdot|\!|\!\rvert
satisfies the triangle inequality and we have the following identity

(4)

\lvert\!|\!|(tx_{1},t^{2}x_{2},\ldots,t^{d}x_{d})|\!|\!\rvert=|t|\lvert\!|\!|(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})|\!|\!\rvert.

Note also that a sequence of vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
converges to a vector
x
with respect to the Euclidean norm if and only if it converges to
x
with respect to the distance associated with
\lvert\!|\!|\cdot|\!|\!\rvert
; we use this fact without further reference.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, we let

(5)

D_{\varepsilon}:=\Bigl\{\Big(\frac{x_{1}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert},\frac{x_{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}},\ldots,\frac{x_{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}\Big)\colon x\in B,\ 0<\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert\leq\varepsilon\Bigr\}

and

(6)

D:=\bigcap_{\varepsilon>0}\overline{D_{\varepsilon}}.

Then
D
is a closed subset of the compact set
\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\colon\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert=1\}
.
Since, by assumption,
0
is a non-isolated point of
A
(this is the only point where this assumption is used), for every
\varepsilon>0
the compact set
\overline{D_{\varepsilon}}
is non-empty, so the set
D
is non-empty.

We claim that for every
u\in D
and every
t>0
we have
(t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{d})\cdot u\in B
(a similar statement holds for every
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
but we do not need this).
So let
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in D
,
t>0
, and
\varepsilon>0
.
It suffices to show that there exists
b\in B
with

(7)

\lvert\!|\!|(t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{d})\cdot u-b|\!|\!\rvert\leq\varepsilon.

Let
\Sigma=\{s_{1}<s_{2}<\cdots\}
. Since
\Sigma
is a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\infty
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s_{n+1}}{s_{n}}=1
.
Hence, for every
\delta>0
(to be chosen later, depending on
\varepsilon
and
t
only) there exists
M=M(\delta)\geq s_{1}
such that if
s_{n+1}>M
we have

|s_{n+1}^{j}-s_{n}^{j}|\leq\delta s_{n}^{j},\quad\text{for }j=1,\ldots,d.

It follows that for every real
\sigma\geq M
, there exists
s\in\Sigma
with

(8)

|s^{j}-\sigma^{j}|\leq\delta\cdot\sigma^{j},\quad\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,d.

Indeed, we can choose
s:=s_{n_{0}}
where
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
s_{n_{0}}\leq\sigma<s_{n_{0}+1}
, then
s_{n_{0}+1}>M
and we have
|\sigma^{j}-s_{n_{0}}^{j}|\leq|s_{n_{0}+1}^{j}-s_{n_{0}}^{j}|\leq\delta s_{n_{0}}^{j}\leq\delta\sigma^{j}
.

Since
u
belongs to
D
, by (
5
) and (
6
) there exists
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in B
, depending on
\varepsilon,t,u,M
, with

(9)

0<\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert\leq\frac{t}{M}\quad\text{and}\quad\lvert\!|\!|\big(\frac{x_{1}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert},\frac{x_{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}},\ldots,\frac{x_{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}\big)-u|\!|\!\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2t}.

Applying (
8
) with
\delta:=\frac{\varepsilon^{d}}{2^{d}(1+t^{d})}
and
\sigma:=\frac{t}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert}\geq M
(by (
9
)), it follows that there exists
s\in\Sigma
(depending on
\varepsilon,t,x
) such that

(10)

\Bigl|s^{j}-\frac{t^{j}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{j}}\Bigr|\leq\delta\cdot\frac{t^{j}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{j}}=\frac{\varepsilon^{d}}{2^{d}\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{j}},\quad\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,d.

If we add and subtract
\big(\frac{tx_{1}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert},\frac{t^{2}x_{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}},\ldots,\frac{t^{d}x_{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}\big)
in the expression below and use the triangle inequality, we get

(11)

\lvert\!|\!|(tu_{1},t^{2}u_{2},\ldots,t^{d}u_{d})-(sx_{1},s^{2}x_{2},\ldots,s^{d}x_{d}))|\!|\!\rvert\leq\\
\lvert\!|\!|\Big(t\big(\frac{x_{1}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert}-u_{1}\big),t^{2}\big(\frac{x_{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}}-u_{2}\big),\ldots,t^{d}\big(\frac{x_{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}-u_{d}\big)\Big)|\!|\!\rvert+\\
\lvert\!|\!|\big(x_{1}\big(\frac{t}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert}-s\big),x_{2}\big(\frac{t^{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}}-s^{2}\big),\ldots,x_{d}\big(\frac{t^{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}-s^{d}\big)\big)|\!|\!\rvert\leq\varepsilon,

where the last estimate follows from (
9
) and (
10
) as follows: By (
4
) the first term is equal to

t\,\large\lvert\!|\!|\big(\frac{x_{1}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert},\frac{x_{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}},\ldots,\frac{x_{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}\big)-u|\!|\!\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

where the last estimate follows from (
9
).
By (
3
) the second term is equal to

|x_{1}|\Big|\frac{t}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert}-s\Big|+|x_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big|\frac{t^{2}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{2}}-s^{2}\Big|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\cdots+|x_{d}|^{\frac{1}{d}}\Big|\frac{t^{d}}{\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert^{d}}-s^{d}\Big|^{\frac{1}{d}},

and using (
10
) we can bound this by

\frac{\varepsilon}{2\lvert\!|\!|x|\!|\!\rvert}(|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|^{\frac{1}{2}}+\cdots+|x_{d}|^{\frac{1}{d}})=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

It follows from (
11
) that (
7
) holds for
b:=(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\cdot x
, which is an element of
B
since
x\in B
and
B
is invariant under the map

x\mapsto(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\cdot x
. This completes the proof of the claim.

Let
u
now be any element in
D
.
We have just shown that the set
C:=\{(t,t^{2},\ldots,t^{d})\cdot u\colon t>0\}
is contained in
B
and thus the closure of its image
\pi(C)
under the natural projection
\pi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
is contained in
A=\pi(B)
.
Since
u\in D
we have
\lvert\!|\!|u|\!|\!\rvert=1
, hence there exists
j_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
u_{j_{0}}\neq 0
. We deduce from this and Lemma
6
that
\{te_{j_{0}}\colon t\in{\mathbb{R}}\}\subset C+{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\subset B
, hence
\{te_{j_{0}}\colon t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}\subset A
. This completes the proof.
∎

We deduce the following:

Corollary 8
.

Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\Sigma
be a non-lacunary semigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
, and

A
be a closed
subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
that is invariant under

\{(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\colon s\in\Sigma\}.

If
x
is a non-isolated rational point of
A
, with denominators relatively prime to
\Sigma
, then

\{x+te_{j}\colon t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}\subset A
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
4
we let
m
be an integer that is relatively prime to every element of
\Sigma
and such that the coordinates of
x
remain invariant when multiplied by any element of the non-lacunary semigroup
\Sigma_{m}:=\Sigma\cap(m{\mathbb{Z}}+1)
. Then the set
A-x
is closed, invariant under

\{(s,s^{2},\ldots,s^{d})\colon s\in\Sigma_{m}\},

and has
0
as a non-isolated point. Applying Proposition
7
with
\Sigma_{m}
instead of
\Sigma
, we get that
there exists
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
\{te_{j}\colon t\in{\mathbb{T}}\}\subset A-x
, completing the proof.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Proposition
5

Since
A
is an infinite subset of
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
, the set
A^{\prime}
of non-isolated points of
A
is non-empty. It is also closed and invariant under the set
\Sigma^{1,\ldots,d}
of Proposition
4
. Applying Proposition
4
for
\ell_{1}:=1,\ldots,\ell_{d}:=d
, we get that
A^{\prime}
contains a rational point with denominators relatively prime to
\Sigma
.
The result now follows from Corollary
8
and the fact that
A^{\prime}\subset A
(since
A
is closed).
