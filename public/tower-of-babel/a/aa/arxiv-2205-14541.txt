. 1.
Introduction

In this paper, we provide invariance principles, also called functional limit theorems, for Poisson weak limits for two classical results in probability theory but also for their recent generalizations. Let us begin by describing the central limit theorems for which functional laws have to be established.

1.1.
Poisson weak limits

The approximation of a sequence of binomial probability laws
\left(\mathcal{B}(n,p_{n})\right)_{n\geq 1}
associated to a sequence of random variables
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
[such that the sequence of probabilities
(p_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converges to zero and
np_{n}\rightarrow\lambda>0
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
] to a Poisson law
\mathcal{P}(\lambda)
is a classical and easy-to-prove result in probability theory. This approximation is very important in some real-life situations, especially in lack of powerful computers. In this simple case, the distribution of each
Z_{n}
is a convolution product of
n
independent and identically distributed (
iid
) Bernoulli laws
\mathcal{B}(p_{n})
-associated to the random variables

\left\{(X_{j,n})_{1\leq j\leq n},\ n\geq 1\right\}
,
i.e.
,
Z_{n}=X_{1,n}+X_{2,n}+\cdots+X_{n,n}
. A parallel theory also exists for sums of corrected geometric laws. When we depart from the
iid
assumption, the problem may get more and rapidly, even if the independence assumption is still required. The situation becomes more interesting if the random variables
X_{j,n}
are non-stationary and dependent. Generalizations of these results have been given recently by
Lo et al. 2021
. It happens that asymptotic laws of sums of random variables are closely related to invariance principles or functional weak limits which in turn may lead to Lévy stochastic processes which are so important in many areas of applications, in mathematical finance for example.

In that view, the aforementioned Asymptotic Poisson Weak Limits (
APWL
) should be associated with Invariance principles (
IP
) or Functional Limit Theorems (
FLT
) leading to scaled Lévy process, both for the classical cases and for the extended non-stationary approach. Among Lévy processes, two are iconic: Brownian motions and Poisson processes.

So our aim is to establish
FLT
’s for the extended Poisson weak limit laws in
Lo et al. 2021
and by the way to re-establish
FLT
’s for the two classical cases, since the statements of the later cases can be hardly found in the most common literature.

To have more details on our objectives, we recall the
APWL
under consideration in Subsection
1.2
. Next we make a quick introduction
to
FLT
’s in the space
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)
in Subsection
1.3
with the main tools to be used there.

1.2.
The Asymptotic Poisson Weak Laws

We recall the two results for stationary Bernoulli and corrected geometric sequences of random variables.

Proposition 1
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of random variables in some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
such that:

1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
X_{n}\sim\mathcal{B}(n,p_{n})
,
n\geq 1
;

2)
p_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
np_{n}\rightarrow\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Then

X_{n}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}(\lambda).

Next, we have:

Proposition 2
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of random variables in some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
such that:

1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
X_{n}\sim\mathcal{N}\mathcal{B}(n,p_{n})
,
n\geq 1
;

2)
(1-p_{n})\rightarrow 0
and
n(1-p_{n})\rightarrow\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Then

X_{n}-n\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}(\lambda).

We also recall the two generalizations of the above mentioned results for non-stationary Bernoulli and corrected geometric sequences of random variables (see
Lo et al. 2021
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
X=\biggr\{\{X_{k,n},\ 1\leq k\leq k_{n}=k(n)\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}
be an array by-row-independent Bernoulli random variables, that is:

(1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
\forall 1\leq k\leq k(n)
,
X_{k,n}\sim\mathcal{B}(p_{k,n})
, with
0<p_{k,n}<1
and:

(2)
\sup_{1\leq k\leq k(n)}p_{k,n}\rightarrow 0
;

(3)
\sum_{1\leq k\leq k(n)}p_{k,n}\rightarrow\lambda\in]0,\ +\infty[
.

Then we have

S_{n}[X]:=\sum_{k=1}^{k(n)}X_{k,n}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}(\lambda).

Next:

Theorem 2
.

Let
X=\biggr\{\{X_{k,n},\ 1\leq k\leq k_{n}=k(n)\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}
be an array by-row-independent corrected Geometric random variables, that is:

(1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
\forall 1\leq k\leq k(n)
,
X_{k,n}\sim\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(p_{k,n})
, with
0<p_{k,n}=1-q_{k,n}<1
and:

(2)
\sup_{1\leq k\leq k(n)}q_{k,n}\rightarrow 0
;

(3)
\sum_{1\leq k\leq k(n)}q_{k,n}\rightarrow\lambda\in]0,\ +\infty[
.

Then we have

S_{n}[X]:=\sum_{k=1}^{k(n)}X_{k,n}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}(\lambda).

Now, let us do a quick introduction on simple functional laws.

1.3.
A quick introduction to the
FLT
in
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)

The general principle of invariance principles is the following. Let
X_{1}
,
X_{2}
,
\cdots
be a sequence of real-valued centered random variables, defined on the same probability space, with finite variances, that is
\sigma_{i}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left|X_{i}\right|^{2}<\infty
. For each
n\geq 1
, set

s_{n}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}+\cdots+\sigma_{n}^{2}

and

S_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n}.

For
0\leq t\leq 1
and
n\geq 1
, put

Y_{n}(t)=\frac{S_{\left[nt\right]}}{s_{n}},

where, for any real
u
,
[u]
stands for the integer part of
u
, which is the greatest integer less or equal to
u
.

In the
iid
case with variance one,
s_{n}^{2}=n
and the sequence
\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}
is studied in the space
D(0,1)
of functions of the first kind endowed with the Skorohod metric. It may be proved that

(1.1)

\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}\rightsquigarrow\{W(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\},\ as\ n\rightarrow+\infty,

meaning that
\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}
weakly converges to a Wiener process (Brownian Motion)
\{W(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}
in the sense of the Skorohod topology.

This result is the starting point of a considerable research trying to have extensions of that result, labeled as
functional laws
or
invariance principles
.

In each part of this document
, the notation given in this introduction will be used and eventually adapted.

For a long time, the weak law (
1.1
) proved in the space
\mathcal{C}(0,1)
of continuous functions on (0,1) or in the space
\mathcal{D}(0,1)
of functions defined on (0,1) having left and right limits at each point and having at most a countable number of discontinuity points. The spaces
\mathcal{C}(0,1)
and
\mathcal{D}(0,1)
, when endowed with the supremum norm and the Skorohod metric are complete and separable spaces (Polish spaces). Most importantly, suprema of stochastic processes in them are measurable. However, handling such weak limits in them, specially in
\mathcal{D}(0,1)
is very hard. To find a less complicated way, the space
\ell^{\infty}(T)
of bounded functions equipped with the supremum norm is used where
T
is some set, and here we use
T=[a,b]
and frequently
T=[0,\ 1]
. To avoid the measurability problem, exterior and interior integrals with respect to a probability measure, and exterior and interior probabilities are introduced. A very advanced round up of weak convergence in
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)
is available in
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
.

Now, we can state our precise objective, which is to prove that for all four theorems above, we will have
FLT
’s in the form

(1.2)

\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\}\rightsquigarrow\{N(\lambda a(t)),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\},\ in\ \ell^{\infty}(0,1)\ as\ n\rightarrow+\infty,

where
N(\lambda a(\circ))
is scaled Poisson process of intensity
\lambda
and that
a(t)\equiv t
for the first two theorems.

Finally, before we give our results, let us describe the method of finding
FTL
’s in
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)
.

1.4.
General tools for weak laws in
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)

The invariance principle results are proved by first establishing the finite-dimensional convergence to a given stochastic process and second, by showing that the sequence of stochastic process is asymptotically tight. We will use the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
a
and
b
be two real numbers such that
a<b
and
T=[a,b]
. For the sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset\ell^{\infty}\left([a,b]\right)
converges to a tight stochastic process
W\in\ell^{\infty}\left([a,b]\right)
, it is sufficient that :

(a) the finite dimensional margins of
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
converge to those of
W

and

(b)
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is asymptotically tight.

The asymptotically tightness is characterized as follows: Given that each margin
(Y_{n}(t))_{n\geq 1}
,
t\in T
, is asymptotically tight, the stochastic process
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is tight if and only if there exists a semi-metric
\rho
on
T=[a,b]
such that
(T,\rho)
is totally bounded and for all
\eta>0
,

(1.3)

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{P}^{\ast}\left(\sup_{\rho(t,s)<\delta}\left|Y_{n}(t)-Y_{n}(s)\right|\geq\eta\right)=0.\ \

(See
van der Vaart and Wellner 1996
or
Lo et al. 2018
, Chapter 3).

However in most cases where
T
is a bounded interval of
\mathbb{R}
, we try to have that tightness in the simple case where
\rho(s,t)=|t-s|
.

2.
FLT
related
APWL

Let us consider the stationary case first. We have the two following theorems.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of random variables in some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
such that:

1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
X_{n}=X_{1,n}+\cdots+X_{n,n}
, with each
X_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{B}(p_{n})
,
1\leq i\leq n
;

2)
p_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
np_{n}\rightarrow\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Let us consider the sequence of stochastic processes

\biggr\{\left\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}=\biggr\{\left\{X_{[nt]},\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}

with
X_{[nt]}=0
for
0\leq nt<1
. Then we have

(2.1)

Y_{n}(\circ)\rightsquigarrow N(\circ)\ in\ \ell^{\infty}(0,1),

where
N(\circ)
is a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda
.

We also have:

Theorem 5
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a sequence of random variables in some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
such that:

1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
X_{n}=X_{1,n}+\cdots+X_{n,n}
, with each
X_{i,n}\sim\mathcal{G}(p_{n})
,
1\leq i\leq n
;

2)
(1-p_{n})\rightarrow 0
and
n(1-p_{n})\rightarrow\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
.

Let us denote
Z_{n}=X_{n}-n
,
n\geq 1
and consider the sequence of stochastic processes

\biggr\{\left\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}=\biggr\{\left\{Z_{[nt]},\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\},

with
Z_{[nt]}=0
for
0\leq nt<1
. Then we have

(2.2)

Y_{n}(\circ)\rightsquigarrow N(\circ)\ in\ \ell^{\infty}(0,1),

where
N(\circ)
is a Poisson process of intensity
\lambda
.

Proofs of theorems
.

Proof of Theorem
4
. We are going to apply Theorem
3
. Let us proceed to two steps.

Step 1
. Let us begin by the finite distribution convergences. Since, we have for any
0\leq t\leq 1
,

Y_{n}(t)=X_{[nt]}=X_{1,n}+\cdots+X_{[nt],n}\sim\mathcal{B}([nt],p_{n}),\ for\ t\geq 1/n,

we have, for
n\geq 1
big enough,

[nt]p_{n}=(np_{n})([nt]/n)\to\lambda t

and by Proposition
1
,
Y_{n}(t)\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}(\lambda t)
. Now let
k\geq 0
and
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{k}
. Let
n\geq 1
large enough (say
n\geq n_{0}
) to ensure that all
nt_{j}\geq 1
,
1\leq j\leq k
and
[nt_{1}]<\cdots<[nt_{k}]
. Let us define

Z_{n}=\left(Y_{n}(t_{1}),\ Y_{n}(t_{2})-Y_{n}(t_{1}),\ldots,Y_{n}(t_{k})-Y_{n}(t_{k-1})\right),\ n\geq n_{0}.

It is clear that:

(1)
Z_{n}
has independent components;

(2) For each
1\leq j\leq k
, we have

Z_{n}(t_{j})=Y_{n}(t_{j})-Y_{n}(t_{j-1})=X_{[nt_{j-1}]+1}+\cdots+X_{[nt_{j}]},

and the number of terms in
Z_{n}(t_{j})
is
m_{n}(j)=[nt_{j}]-[nt_{j-1}]
and satisfies
m_{n}(j)p_{n}\rightarrow\lambda(t_{j}-t_{j-1})
. Hence, by Proposition
1
,

Z_{n}(j)\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}\left(\lambda(t_{j}-t_{j-1})\right).\\

So,
Z_{n}=(Z_{n}(t_{1}),\cdots,Z_{n}(t_{k}))
has independent components which weakly converge to marginal laws. By the general Slutsky rule,
Z_{n}
weakly converges to the product law of those marginals laws, that is

Z_{n}\rightsquigarrow(Z_{t_{1}},\cdots,Z_{t_{k}}),

where the
Z_{t_{1}}
,
\cdots
,
Z_{t_{k}}
are independent and
Z_{t_{j}}\sim\mathcal{P}(\lambda(t_{j}-t_{j-1}))
. So, by decrementing, we obtain that

(Y_{n}(t_{1}),\cdots,Y_{n}(t_{k}))
converges to
(N(t_{1},\lambda),\cdots,N(t_{k},\lambda))
, where
N(\circ,\lambda)
is a simple Poisson process.
\square

Step 2
. Let
\delta>0
and let, for
n\geq 1

A_{n}(\delta)=\sup_{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2},\ |s-t|<\delta}|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|.

We take
\delta\in]0,1[
and so
m(n,\delta):=\left[n(\delta+1/n)\right]
is small against
n
. Here we will exploit that any
Y_{n}(t)
is a sum of constant-signed random variables. We have the following facts.

(a) For any
(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2}
such that
|s-t|<\delta
,
|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|
is of the form
X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+h,n}
, where
h\leq m(n,\delta)-1
.

(b) So for each
j\in\{1,\cdots,n-m(n,\delta)+1\}
, fixed,
X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+h,n}
, with
h\leq m(n,\delta)-1
, is bounded by

Z_{j,n}:=X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+m(n,\delta)-1},n.

Let us denote
N=n-m(n,\delta)+1
. For
j\leq N
, the quantity
X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+h,n}
, with
h\leq m(n,\delta)-1
, is still bounded by
Z_{N}:=\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}
.

(c) Finally, we have

A_{n}(\delta)\leq\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}.

We set
B_{h}=(\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}=Z_{h,n})
,
1\leq h\leq N
. It is clear that

\Omega=\bigcup_{1\leq h\leq N}B_{h}=\sum_{1\leq h\leq N}B^{\prime}_{h},

with
B^{\prime}_{1}=B_{1}
,
B^{\prime}_{2}=B_{1}^{c}B_{2}
,
B^{\prime}_{h}=B_{1}^{c}\cdots B_{h-1}^{c}B_{h}
for
h\geq 1
. We get, for any
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{n}>\eta)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}\}>\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}>\eta\right\}\cap B^{\prime}_{h}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}>\eta\right\}/B^{\prime}_{h}\right)\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(\{Z_{h,n}>\eta\})\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(Z_{h,n})\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m(n,\delta)p_{n}}{\eta}\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m(n,\delta)p_{n}}{\eta}=\frac{\delta(\lambda+o(1))}{\eta}.

We conclude that, for any
\eta>0
,

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{P}\biggr(\biggr(\sup_{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2},\ |s-t|<\delta}|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|\biggr)>\eta\biggr)=0.

\square

Proof of Theorem
5
. That proof closely follows that of Theorem
4
. There is no need to give the details.

\blacksquare

3.
FLT
for non-stationary Bernoulli or corrected geometrical laws

To have clues on how to extend the above results to a non-stationary scheme, we may draw some facts from hypotheses of Theorem
4
for example. There, we add
k_{n}(t)=[nt]
,
0\leq t\leq 1
,
n\geq 1
. For
0\leq s<t
,

Y_{n}(t)-Y_{n}(s)=X_{k_{n}(s)+1,n}+\cdots+X_{k_{n}(t),n}\sim\mathcal{B}(k_{n}(t)-k_{n}(s),p_{n}).

For
a(t)=t
,
0\leq t\leq 1
, by setting
\Delta a(s,t)=a(t)-a(s)
,

\mathbb{E}(Y_{n}(t)-Y_{n}(s))=(k_{n}(t)-k_{n}(s))p_{n}=:\Delta p_{n}(s,t)\rightarrow\lambda\Delta a(s,t)=\lambda\Delta(s,t),

uniformly in
(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2}
. This simple analysis suggests generalizing the above
FLT
in the following way: require a sequence of functions

(k_{n}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
,
n\geq 1
, which are non-decreasing with
k_{n}(0)=0
and
k_{n}(1)=n
and a uniformly right-continuous and non-decreasing function
a(t)
of
t\in[0,1]
, with
a(0)=1-a(1)=0
such that for
0\leq s<t
,

\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2}}\left|\biggr(p_{k_{n}(s)+1,n}+\cdots+p_{k_{n}(t),n}\biggr)-\lambda\Delta a(s,t)\right|=0

and

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2},|s-t|<\delta}\Delta a(s,t)=0.

Exploiting these ideas leads to the two
FLT
’s for non stationary data.

3.1.
Non-stationary sequences of Bernoulli random variables

Theorem 6
.

Let
X=\biggr\{\{X_{k,n},\ 1\leq k\leq k_{n}=k(n)\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}
(with
k_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
) be an array by-row-independent Bernoulli random variables, that is:

(1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
\forall 1\leq k\leq k(n)
,
X_{k,n}\sim\mathcal{B}(p_{k,n})
, with
0<p_{k,n}<1
.

Suppose that we have the following other assumptions.

(2)
\sup_{1\leq k\leq k(n)}p_{k,n}\rightarrow 0
;

(3) There exist:

(3a) a sequence of non-decreasing functions
(k_{n}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, such that
k_{n}(0)=0
and
k_{n}(1)=k_{n}
,
n\geq 1
, (satisfying : for any

0\leq s<t
, we have
k_{n}(s)<k_{n}(t)
for
n

and:

(3b) a uniformly right-continuous and non-decreasing function
a(t)
of
t\in[0,1]
with
a(0)=1-a(1)=0
, i.e,

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{t\in[0,1[}\Delta a(t,t+\delta)=0;\ \ (F3a)

with
\Delta a(s,t)=a(t)-a(s)
for
0\leq s<t
;

Moreover, for

\Delta p_{n}(s,t)=p_{k_{n}(s)+1,n}+\cdots+p_{k_{n}(t),n},

we have

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\Delta p_{n}(s,t)-\lambda\Delta a(s,t)\right|=0\ \ (F3b)

and, for
\delta>0
(small enough) and for
m(n,\delta)=k_{n}(t+\delta)-k_{n}(t)
,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}-m(n,\delta)+1}\left|p_{j,n}+\cdots+p_{j+m(n,\delta)-1,n}-\lambda\delta\right|=0.\ \ (F3c)

Then the stochastic process

\biggr\{\left\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}=\biggr\{\left\{X_{1,n}+\cdots+X_{k_{n}(t),n},\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}

weakly converges in
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)
to the compound Poisson process
N(a(\circ),\lambda)
of intensity
\lambda>0
.

Proof of Theorem of
6
. Let us proceed by steps.

Step 1
. Finite dimensional convergence. Let
r>1
and
0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{r}\leq 1
. Let for
1\leq j\leq r
,

Z_{j,n}=Y_{n}(t_{j})-Y_{n}(t_{j-1})=X_{k_{n}(t_{j-1})+1,n}+\cdots+X_{k_{n}(t_{j}),n}.

Since all the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold for each
Z_{j,n}
, we have

\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,r\},\ Z_{j,n}\rightsquigarrow\mathcal{P}(\lambda\Delta a(t_{j-1},t_{j})).

So,
Z_{n}=(Z_{1,n},\cdots,Z_{r,n})
has independent components which weakly converge to marginal laws. By the general Slutsky rule,
Z_{n}
weakly converges to the product law of those marginals laws, that is,

Z_{n}\rightsquigarrow(Z_{1},\cdots,Z_{r}),

where the
Z_{1}
,
\cdots
,
Z_{r}
are independent and
Z_{j}\sim\mathcal{P}(\lambda\Delta a(t_{j-1},t_{j}))
. So, by decrementing, we obtain that

(Y_{n}(t_{1}),\cdots,Y_{n}(t_{r}))
converges to the
(N(a(t_{1}),\lambda),\cdots,N(a(t_{r}),\lambda))
, where
N(a(\circ),\lambda)
is a scaled Poisson process by
(a(\circ))
.

Step 2
. We closely follow the proof of Theorem
4
and re-use its notations. Let
\delta>0
and let, for
n\geq 1

A_{n}(\delta)=\sup_{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2},\ |s-t|<\delta}|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|.

We have:

(a) For any
(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2}
, such that
|s-t|<\delta
,
|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|
is of the form
X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+h,n}
, where

h\leq m(n,\delta)-1,\ \ with\ \ m(n,\delta)=k_{n}(t+\delta)-k_{n}(\delta).

(b) So for each
j\in\{1,\cdots,k_{n}-m(n,\delta)+1\}
, fixed,
X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+h,n}
, with
h\leq m(n,\delta)-1
, is bounded by

Z_{j,n}=:X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+m(n,\delta)-1},n.

Let us denote
N=k_{n}-m(n,\delta)+1
. For
j\leq N
, the quantity
X_{j,n}+\cdots+X_{j+h,n}
, with
h\leq m(n,\delta)-1
, is still bounded by
Z_{N}=:\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}
.

(c) Finally, we have

A_{n}(\delta)\leq\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}.

We set
B_{h}=(\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j}=Z_{h})
,
1\leq h\leq N
. Surely, we have

\Omega=\bigcup_{1\leq h\leq N}B_{h}=\sum_{1\leq h\leq N}B^{\prime}_{h}

with
B^{\prime}_{1}=B_{1}
,
B^{\prime}_{2}=B_{1}^{c}B_{2}
,
B^{\prime}_{h}=B_{1}^{c}\cdots B_{h-1}^{c}B_{h}
for
h\geq 1
. We get, for any
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{n}(\delta)>\eta)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}\}>\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}>\eta\right\}\cap B^{\prime}_{h}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sup_{1\leq j\leq N}Z_{j,n}>\eta\right\}/B^{\prime}_{h}\right)\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{P}(\{Z_{h,n}>\eta\})\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{h=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}(Z_{h,n})\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\eta}\sum_{h=1}^{N}\biggr(p_{h,n}+\cdots+p_{h+m(n,\delta)-1,n}\biggr)\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})\ \ (L26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\Delta(t,t+\delta)}{\eta}\sum_{h=1}^{N}\ \mathbb{P}(B^{\prime}_{h})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda\Delta(t,t+\delta)}{\eta},

where we applied Assumption (F3c) in Line (L26). By applying Assumption (F3a), we get, for any
\eta>0
,

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{P}\biggr(\biggr(\sup_{(s,t)\in[0,1]^{2},\ |s-t|<\delta}|Y_{n}(s)-Y_{n}(t)|\biggr)>\eta\biggr)=0.

\square

3.2.
Non-stationary sequences of Corrected geometric variables

The following
FLT
is also valid for sums of corrected geometric random variables. The proof will be omitted because it is very similar of the proof of Theorem
6
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
X=\biggr\{\{X_{k,n},\ 1\leq k\leq k_{n}=k(n)\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}
(with
k_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
) be an array by-row-independent corrected geometric random variables, that is:

(1)
\forall n\geq 1
,
\forall 1\leq k\leq k(n)
,
X_{k,n}\sim\mathcal{G}^{\ast}(p_{k,n})
, with
0<p_{k,n}<1
.

Suppose that we have the following other assumptions.

(2)
\sup_{1\leq k\leq k(n)}q_{k,n}\rightarrow 0
.

(3) There exist:

(3a) a sequence of non-decreasing functions
(k_{n}(t))_{0\leq t\leq 1}
such that
k_{n}(0)=0
and
k_{n}(1)=k_{n}
,
n\geq 1
(satisfying : for any

0\leq s<t
, we have
k_{n}(s)<k_{n}(t)
for
n
large enough)

and:

(3b) a uniformly right-continuous and non-decreasing functions
a(t)
of
t\in[0,1]
with
a(0)=1-a(1)=0
, i.e.,

\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Delta a(t,t+\delta)=0\ \ (G3a)

with
\Delta a(s,t)=a(t)-a(s)
for
0\leq s<t
. Moreover, by setting

\Delta q_{n}(s,t)=q_{k_{n}(s)+1,n}+\cdots+q_{k_{n}(t),n},

we have

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\left|\Delta q_{n}(s,t)-\lambda\Delta a(s,t)\right|=0\ \ (G3b)

and, for

(small enough) and for
m(n,\delta)=k_{n}(t+\delta)-k_{n}(t)
,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\sup_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}-m(n,\delta)+1}\left|q_{j,n}+\cdots+q_{j+m(n,\delta)-1,n}-\lambda\delta\right|=0.\ \ (G3c)

Then the sequence of stochastic processes

\biggr\{\left\{Y_{n}(t),\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}=\biggr\{\left\{X_{1,n}+\cdots+X_{k_{n}(t),n},\ 0\leq t\leq 1\right\},\ n\geq 1\biggr\}

to the compound Poisson process
N(a(\circ),\lambda)
of intensity
\lambda>0
.

3.3.
Examples

Let us provide some examples for which the assumptions of Theorems
6
and
7
, specifically (F3b), (F3c), (G3b) and (G3c) hold. Actually, we illustrate conditions of the first theorem only.

1. Stationary case
. We already said that (F3b) and (F3c) hold in the stationary case with
k_{n}=n
,
k_{n}(t)=[tk_{n}]
,
p_{k,n}=p_{n}
and
a(t)=t
. This still hold even if
k_{n}
is an arbitrary sequence converging to
+\infty
.

2. Checking (F3c)
. It seems that (F3c) is more difficult to get. The example used to realize (F3b) in the next example seems not to make hold (F3c).

Actually, (F3c) broadly means the partial sums
p_{j,n}+\cdots+p_{j+\ell,n}
, for
\ell>0
, are stationary,
i.e.
, nearly stationary. They would be exactly stationary if they were all equal to
p_{1,n}+\cdots+p_{1+\ell,n}
. In other words, (F3c) means that the partial sums
p_{j,n}+\cdots+p_{j+\ell,n}
asymptotically behave like
p_{1,n}+\cdots+p_{1+\ell,n}
, independently of
j
, as
n\rightarrow+\infty
, meaning that they are asymptotically stationary.

Let us give some examples of such sequences.

3. Non trivial example for (F3b) holds
. Let us consider an array
\{\{X_{k,n,\ 1\leq k\leq\ell(n)}\},\ n\geq 1\}
with
\ell(n)\rightarrow+\infty
. Let
\varepsilon\in]0,1[
. Let
(k_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence converging to
+\infty
such that
[k_{n}^{1+\varepsilon}]\leq\ell(n)
. Let us define

k_{n}(t)=\left[k_{n}^{\varepsilon+t}\right],\ n\geq 1,\ t\in[0,1],

for
\gamma
standing for the Euler number,

b_{n}=\sum_{1\leq k\leq k_{n}}k^{-1}=\log k_{n}+\gamma+o(1).

Now, suppose that for any
1<k\leq K_{n}=\left[k_{n}^{1+\varepsilon}\right]
,

p_{k,n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{kb_{n}},

where
\lambda_{n}\leq k_{n}^{2}b_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\lambda
. For
0\leq s<t\leq 1
, for
n\geq 1
, we have

(3.1)

\Delta p_{n}(s,t)=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{b_{n}}\left\{\frac{1}{k_{n}(s)+1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{k_{n}(t)}\right\}=\frac{\lambda_{n}(\log k_{n})}{b_{n}}\frac{\log k_{n}(t)-\log k_{n}(s)+o(1)}{\log k_{n}}.

By setting
a_{n}=k_{n}^{\varepsilon}-1
(for
n
large enough), by direct computations, we may check

(3.2)

\sup_{(s,t)\in]0,1]^{2}}\left|\frac{\lambda_{n}\left(\log k_{n}(t)-\log k_{n}(s)\right)}{\log k_{n}}-\lambda(t-s)\right|\leq\log(1+a_{n}^{-1}),\ n\geq 1.

The combination of Formulas (
3.1
) and (
3.2
) leads to (F3b).

4. Non trivial examples for (F3c) holds
.

(a) Let
(\varepsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of real numbers converging to zero and bounded by
\lambda/2>0
. Let us set, for
n\geq 1
,
k_{n}(t)=[k_{n}t]
,
k_{n}<n
and

p_{k,n}=\frac{\lambda+(-1)^{k}\varepsilon_{n}}{n},\ 1\leq k\leq k_{n}.

For any
j\in[1,k_{n}]
, for any
\ell\in[1,\ n-k_{n}]
,

p_{j,n}+\cdots+p_{j+\ell,n}\in\left\{\frac{\lambda(\ell+1)-\varepsilon_{n}}{n},\frac{\lambda(\ell+1)}{n},\frac{\lambda(\ell+1)+\varepsilon_{n}}{n}\right\}

and hence the partial sums
p_{j+1,n}+\cdots+p_{j+\ell,n}
are stationary.

(b) Let us take
k_{n}=n
and

b_{n}=\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}+1}\right)^{-1}+\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}+2}\right)^{-1}+\cdots+\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}+k_{n}}\right)^{-1}

and

p_{k,n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{b_{n}\left(1+\frac{1}{k+k_{n}}\right)},\ 1\leq k\leq k_{n},

where
\lambda_{n}<b_{n}
and
\lambda_{n}\rightarrow\lambda
.

We will show later that
b_{n}=k_{n}(1+o(1))
. We have

p_{j,n}+\cdots+p_{j+m(n)-1,n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{b_{n}}\times\left\{\frac{k_{n}+j}{(k_{n}+1)+j}+\cdots+\frac{k_{n}+j+m(n)-1}{k_{n}+j+m(n)}\right\}.

We denote
\Delta p_{n}(j)=p_{j,n}+\cdots+p_{j+m(n)-1}
. Let us bound

A_{j,n}=\frac{k_{n}+j}{(k_{n}+1)+j}+\cdots+\frac{k_{n}+j+m(n)-1}{k_{n}+j+m(n)}.

The function
x\mapsto h(x)=(k_{n}+x)/(k_{n}+1+x)
is non-decreasing and concave and so, the comparison of the integral
\int_{j}^{j+m(n)-1}h(x)\ dx
and the series is as follows:

A_{j,n}-\frac{k_{n}+j+m(n)-1}{k_{n}+j+m(n)}\leq\int_{j}^{j+m(n)-1}\frac{k_{n}+x}{k_{n}+1+x}\ dx\leq A_{j,n}-\frac{k_{n}+j}{k_{n}+j+1}.

But

\int_{j}^{j+m(n)-1}\frac{k_{n}+1+x}{k_{n}+x}\ dx=\int_{j}^{j+m(n)-1}\left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}+x}\right)\ dx

and by using the primitive

\left(x+\log(k_{n}+x)\right)\ of\ \left(1+\frac{1}{k_{n}+x}\right),

we get

(3.3)

\displaystyle A_{j,n}-\frac{k_{n}+j+m(n)-1}{k_{n}+j+m(n)}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(m(n)-1)+\log\frac{k_{n}+j+m(n)-1}{k_{n}+j}\textbf{}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle A_{j,n}-\frac{k_{n}+j}{k_{n}+j+1}.

But all the terms, except
A_{j,n}
, when divided by
b_{n}\sim k_{n}
, go to zero uniformly in
j
. Let show this for one them, the middle term for example,

\frac{1}{k_{n}}\log\frac{k_{n}-1}{2k_{n}}\leq B_{j,n}=\frac{1}{k_{n}}\log\frac{k_{n}+j+m(n)-1}{k_{n}+j}\leq\frac{1}{k_{n}}\log\frac{3k_{n}-1}{k_{n}}

which shows that
\sup_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}}B_{j,n}\rightarrow 0
. By using the same remarks, ideas and methods, we have

b_{n}-\frac{2k_{n}}{2k_{n}+1}\leq\int_{1}^{k_{n}}\left(1-\frac{1}{k_{n}+x+1}\right)\ dx\leq b_{n}-\frac{k_{n}+1}{k_{n}+2}.

The integral is
(k_{n}-1)-\log((2k_{n}+1)/(k_{n}+2))
and so
b_{n}=k_{n}(1+o(1))
. Now, Formula (
3.3
) becomes

\frac{A_{j,n}}{b_{n}}+o(1)\leq\frac{m(n)}{k_{n}}(1+o(1))+o(1)\leq\frac{A_{j,n}}{b_{n}}+o(1),

where all the small
o^{\prime}s
are uniform in
j
. We already know that

\left|\frac{m(n)}{k_{n}}-(t-s)\right|\leq\frac{1}{k_{n}}.

The combination of all these facts gives

\sup_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}}\left|\Delta p_{n}(j)-\lambda(t-s)\right|\rightarrow 0

and so Condition (F3c) holds.
\blacksquare

4.
Conclusion

We saw how a very simple result in probability that can be proved by moment generating function can become sophisticated for sums of independent but non-stationary random variables. In this paper, we treated the question in the general frame of the weak laws in
\ell^{\infty}(0,1)
and learned how to deal with it in a general frame to have new results. The most important thing is the construction of the frame that will allow extensions for non-stationary and dependent data, for associated data in the first place.
