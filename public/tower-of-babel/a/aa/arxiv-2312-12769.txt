Wasserstein robust combinatorial optimization problems
Marcel Jackiewicz, Adam Kasperski, Paweł Zieliński

Abstract
This paper discusses a class of combinatorial optimization problems with uncertain costs in the objective function. It is assumed that a sample of the cost realizations is available, which defines an empirical probability distribution for the random cost vector. A Wasserstein ball, centered at the empirical distribution, is used to define an ambiguity set of probability distributions. A solution minimizing the Conditional Value at Risk for a worst probability distribution in the Wasserstein ball is computed. The complexity of the problem is investigated. Exact and approximate solution methods for various support sets are proposed. Some known results for the Wasserstein robust shortest path problem are generalized and refined.



Keywords:
uncertainty modelling; distributionally robust optimization; Wasserstein distance

1
Introduction

In a traditional combinatorial optimization problem, we seek an object composed of some elements of a ground set, which minimizes a linear objective function. Unfortunately, most combinatorial optimization problems are NP-hard, except for some special cases such as fundamental network problems (see, for example
[
1
,
20
]
). In many practical applications, the exact values of the element costs are unknown. The method of handling this uncertainty mainly depends on the knowledge available. In many situations, it is only possible to specify an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
which contains possible values of uncertain costs.
Then, some robust criteria, such as the min-max or the min-max regret, can be used to compute a solution
[
19
]
. However, additional knowledge in the scenario set is available in many situations. For example, some bounds on mean and covariance matrix
[
6
]
, or a family of confidence sets for the random cost vector can be specified
[
24
]
. Also, a sample of realizations of the random cost vector can be available. In all of these cases, one can define a set of admissible probability distributions for the random cost vector. Using a robust distributionally framework, we can minimize some risk measure for the worst probability distribution in this set. A risk measure commonly used in stochastic optimization is the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR)
[
21
,
22
]
. The CVaR is a convex coherent risk measure that has a nice interpretation and possesses many favorable computational properties.

When a sample of the random cost vector is available, then the Wasserstein metric
[
15
]
can be used to define a set of admissible probability distributions. The Wasserstein metric defines a distance between two probability distributions on the same support set. A Wasserstein ball contains then all probability distributions that are of some prescribed distance to the empirical distribution, which is defined by the available sample of the random cost vector. The Wasserstein ball contains all admissible probability distributions, and the robust distributionally framework can be used to compute a solution
[
8
,
10
]
. The approach based on the Wasserstein distance can be attractive when the sample size is small. In particular, it may outperform the approach based on the sample average approximation
[
8
]
.

Our paper is motivated by the paper by Wang et al.
[
23
]
, in which the Wasserstein robust approach has been applied to the shortest path problem. The authors provided in
[
23
]
various mathematical programming formulations and showed the results of some computational tests. In this paper, we generalize the results obtained in
[
23
]
to all combinatorial problems. In particular, we generalize the method of computing a worst probability distribution for a given solution and the mathematical programming formulation for the unrestricted support set. We also consider the problem with more restricted support sets. In the simplest case, lower and upper bounds for the costs are known, and the support set is a hyperrectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. We refine the results for such a support set shown in
[
23
]
. Generally, any closed, convex, and bounded support set (for example, an ellipsoid in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
) can be used. For this case, we propose a row generation algorithm, which can be applied when the sample size is not large. We also propose a more efficient method of constructing a solution consisting of solving the CVaR-optimization problem for an appropriately distorted sample. We show that this algorithm can provide an approximate solution to the robust Wasserstein problem. Finally, we also investigate the computational complexity of the problem. We show that it is strongly NP-hard for most basic problems, particularly for the shortest path problem discussed in
[
23
]
. We also present the results of some computational tests. This paper is a significantly extended version of the results presented in
[
13
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present the problem formulation. We also show how the problem can be reformulated in a more tractable way, based on a characterization of the worst probability distribution. In Section
3
, we investigate the complexity of the problem. We prove that the Wasserstein robust problem is strongly NP-hard for most basic problems (particularly for the shortest path), except when CVaR is the expectation. In Section
4
, we propose various methods of solving the problem. In Section
4.1
, we discuss the case of unrestricted support set, i.e. when any realization of cost in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
can occur. We show a mathematical programming formulation for this case and propose some exact and approximate methods of solving it. In Section
4.2
, we consider a simple case of restricted support being a hyperrectangle in
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. We refine the known results for this support set. In Section
4.3
, we consider the problem with an arbitrary closed, convex, and bounded support set, and we propose a row generation algorithm for that case. In Section
4.3.1
, we construct a more efficient approximation algorithm and prove its approximation guarantee. Finally, Section
5
contains the results of some computational tests for the 0-1 Knapsack problem.

2
Problem formulation

Consider the following combinatorial optimization problem:

\mathcal{P}:\;\;\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\;\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},

where
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}
is a set of feasible solutions and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is a given nonnegative cost vector. Typically,
\mathcal{X}
is described by a system of linear constraints of the form
\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b}
, for some fixed matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and vector
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, with
\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
.
In general, problem
\mathcal{P}
is NP-hard, and only some special cases are known to be polynomially solvable. One such a particular case is when the matrix
~\boldsymbol{A}
is totally unimodular, for example, when
\mathcal{P}
is the
Shortest Path
or the
Minimum Assignment
(see
[
1
,
20
]
).

In practice, the true realization of the cost vector
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is often unknown before the optimal solution
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\mathcal{P}
must be determined. It should be rather regarded as a random vector

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
with a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
supported on
\Xi\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
. In this paper, we assume that the support set
\Xi
is convex. If
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, then we say that the support set is unrestricted. However,
\Xi
can be restricted to a closed and bounded set in many practical situations. In this case,
\Xi
coincides with an uncertainty set commonly used in robust optimization
[
2
]
.
Vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
induces a random cost
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
of solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. To choose a solution, a risk measure that assigns a real value to random solution cost should be used
[
14
]
. In this paper, we will apply the following Conditional Value at Risk (Expected Shortfall)
[
21
,
22
]
, which is commonly used in stochastic optimization (see, e.g.
[
14
]
):

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]=\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t+\frac{1}{\alpha}{\rm E}_{\mathbb{P}}([\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-t]_{+})\right\}=\inf\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:t+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\Xi}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-t]_{+}\mathbb{P}({\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi})\right\},

(1)

where
\alpha\in(0,1]
is a fixed risk level,

{\rm E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\cdot]
is the expected value with respect to the probability distribution
\mathbb{P}

and
[x]_{+}=\max\{0,x\}
.
Note that for
\alpha=1
, the Conditional Value at Risk is the expected solution cost
{\rm E}_{\mathbb{P}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]
.
Given a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, we seek a solution minimizing the Conditional Value at Risk at a given risk level
\alpha\in(0,1]
, that is, we solve the following problem:

\text{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}:\;\;\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\\
&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}.\end{array}

(2)

In practical applications, the true probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
in problem (
2
) is rarely known exactly. Suppose that we have sample
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N}
of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, which induces the empirical probability distribution

\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}},

(3)

where
\delta_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}}
is a Dirac distribution concentrating unit mass at
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\Xi
. In this paper we will use a more convenient notation
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}:=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N})
, so we will identify
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
with a vector of realizations
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N})
. We will generally use the notation
\mathbb{P}^{N}:=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
to denote a discrete probability distributions in
\Xi
, defined analogously as (
3
). The indexed vectors
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
are realizations of the random vector
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. We point out that some realizations in
\mathbb{P}^{N}
can be repeated, which leads to a nonuniform discrete probability distribution.

Given two probability distribution
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{2}
supported on
\Xi
, we define the Wasserstein distance between them as follows
[
15
]
:

d_{W}(\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2})=\inf\left\{\int_{\Xi\times\Xi}||\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}||_{q}\Pi({\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}):\overset{\Pi\text{ is a joint probability distribution in }\Xi\times\Xi}{\footnotesize\text{with marginals }\tiny\mathbb{Q}_{1},\mathbb{Q}_{2}}\right\},

where
||\boldsymbol{v}||_{q}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|v_{i}|^{q})^{1/q}
is the
q
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
q\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 1}=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Using the Wasserstein distance, we can define the following ambiguity set of probability distributions (also called a Wasserstein ball)
[
8
]
:

\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})=\{\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{M}(\Xi):d_{W}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N},\mathbb{Q})\leq\epsilon\},

where
\mathcal{M}(\Xi)
is the set of all probability distributions in
\Xi
and
\epsilon\geq 0
is a given constant. The following theorem provides the foundations for establishing a finite sample guarantees:

Theorem 1

(
[
8
,
9
]
)
.

Assume that there exists
a>1
such that

A=\int_{\Xi}{\rm exp}(||\boldsymbol{\xi}||^{a})\mathbb{P}({\rm d}\boldsymbol{\xi})<\infty.

Then the true probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
belongs to
\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})
with the confidence
1-\beta
for a fixed
\beta\in(0,1)
, where

\epsilon=\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\left(\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}N}\right)^{1/\max\{n,2\}}&\text{if}&N\geq\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1}}{c_{2}}),\\
\left(\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1})}{c_{2}N}\right)^{1/a}&\text{if}&N<\frac{\log(c_{1}\beta^{-1}}{c_{2}}),\end{array}\right.

and
c_{1},c_{2}
are positive constants that depend on
A
,
a
and
n
.

Given an empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
and
\epsilon\geq 0
, we consider the following distributionally robust problem
[
8
]
:

\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}:\;\;\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\\
&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}.\end{array}

(4)

If the supremum in (
4
) is attained by some probability distribution
\mathbb{P}\in\mathbb{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})
, then
\mathbb{P}
is called a worst probability distribution for
\boldsymbol{x}
.
In this paper, we will use the following lemma, which was originally proven for the
Shortest Path
problem
in
[
23
, Lemma 2]
. We extend the result
[
23
]
for
a general combinatorial problem
\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 1
.

For each solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, there is a worst probability distribution which belongs to

\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})=\{\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N}):\sum_{i=1}^{N}||\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}||_{q}\leq N\epsilon,\;\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\Xi,i\in[N]\},

where
[N]
denotes the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A
.
∎

Lemma
1
shows that the worst probability distribution for a given solution
\boldsymbol{x}
is a discrete one and belongs to the closed, bounded and convex set
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})
. The set
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})
has a nice description, which we will exploit in the next part of the paper. Using Lemma
1
, we can represent (
4
) as the following optimization problem:

\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}:\;\;\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle\max_{\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\\
&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}.\end{array}

(5)

In the next part of this paper, we will investigate the properties of the
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
problem, using the formulation (
5
).

3
Minimizing the Conditional Value at Risk

Observe that
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
reduces to
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
for
\mathbb{P}=\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
when
\epsilon=0
.
In this section we will focus on solving
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
, i.e. on minimizing the Conditional Value at Risk for a given discrete probability distribution
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
. We will show some complexity and approximation results, which will be used to characterize the computational properties of the more general
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
problem. The
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
problem for
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
can be represented as the following mixed integer program:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle t+\frac{1}{\alpha N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\\
&\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-t\leq u_{i},&i\in[N],\\
&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},\\
&u_{i}\geq 0&i\in[N].\end{array}

(6)

Here and subsequently, we will make use of the fact that the Conditional Value at Risk is a special case of the Ordered Averaging Aggregation (OWA) criterion introduced in
[
25
]
. Let
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})
be a vector of nonnegative weights such that
w_{1}+\dots+w_{N}=1
. The OWA criterion for
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
is defined as follows:

{\rm Owa}^{\boldsymbol{w}}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\sigma(i)}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},

(7)

where
\sigma
is a permutation of
[N]
such that

\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{\sigma(1)}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{\sigma(2)}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\dots\geq\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{\sigma(N)}\boldsymbol{x}
.

Proposition 1
.

Assume that
\alpha\in(\frac{l-1}{N},\frac{l}{N}]
for
l\in[N]
. Then

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]={\rm Owa}^{\boldsymbol{w}}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}],

where

\boldsymbol{w}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}(1,0,\dots,0)&\text{if}&\alpha<\frac{1}{N},\\
(\underbrace{\frac{1}{\alpha N},\dots,\frac{1}{\alpha N},1-\frac{l-1}{\alpha N}}_{l\text{ positive weights}},\underbrace{0,\dots,0}_{N-l\text{ weights}})&\text{if}&\alpha\geq\frac{1}{N}.\end{array}\right.

(8)

Proof.

Fix
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{1}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{2}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\dots\geq\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{N}\boldsymbol{x}
.
Using (
6
), let us rewrite
{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]
as the following linear programming problem:

\begin{array}[]{llll}\min&\displaystyle t+\frac{1}{\alpha N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}\\
&u_{i}+t\geq\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},&i\in[N],\\
&u_{i}\geq 0,&i\in[N].\end{array}

(9)

The dual to (
9
) with dual variables
w_{i}
,
i\in[N]
, is

\begin{array}[]{llll}\max&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}w_{i}\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}=1,\\
&0\leq w_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\alpha N},&i\in[N].\end{array}

(10)

The dual (
10
) can be solved using the following greedy method.
If
\frac{1}{\alpha N}>1
(so
\alpha<\frac{1}{N}
), then we set
w_{1}=1
and
w_{i}=0
for
i=2,\dots,N
. If
\frac{1}{\alpha N}\leq 1
, then we set
w_{i}=\frac{1}{N\alpha}
for
i=1,\dots,l-1
,
w_{l}=1-\frac{l-1}{\alpha N}
, and
w_{i}=0
for
i=l+1,\dots,N
. Hence, the optimal objective value of the dual equals (
7
) with the weights defined as (
8
). By the linear programming strong duality, the proposition follows.
∎

Observe that if
\alpha=\frac{l}{N}
for some
l=1,\dots,N
, then
{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]
is just the average of the
l
largest solution costs under
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
.
Using Proposition
1
, we now characterize the complexity of
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
for some generic combinatorial optimization problem
\mathcal{P}
.
Namely,
assume that
\mathcal{P}
is the following
Representatives Selection
problem (
RS
for short), in which
we are given a set
\mathcal{T}
of
n
tools, numbered from
1
to
n
. This set is partitioned into
\ell
disjoint sets
T_{1},\ldots,T_{\ell}
.
We wish to choose a subset of the tools
\mathcal{T}
that contains exactly one tool from each set
T_{i}
,
i\in[\ell]
. In the deterministic case, each tool
j\in T
has a nonnegative cost
\xi_{j}
, and we seek a subset of the tools (a solution) whose total cost is minimum.
Clearly, the set of feaslible solutions
\mathcal{X}_{s}
can be described by
the constraints
\sum_{j\in T_{i}}x_{j}=1
for each
i\in[\ell]
.
It is easily seen that the
RS
problem, in this case
has a trivial solution, which can be constructed by setting
x_{j_{i}}=1
, where
j_{i}
is the index of the variable in
T_{i}
with the smallest cost under
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})
.
In the
Min-Max RS
problem, we are given a set
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
of realizations of the cost vector and we seek a solution minimizing the largest cost, i.e. we solve the following problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}_{s}}\max_{i\in[K]}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}.

The
Min-Max RS
problem has been investigated in
[
5
,
7
,
16
]
. In particular, it has been shown that it is strongly NP-hard when
K
is a part of the input. We consider now the
CVaR RS
problem, i.e. the problem (
6
) with
\mathcal{X}_{s}
and a fixed
\alpha\in(0,1]
.

Theorem 2
.

The
CVaR RS
problem is strongly NP-hard for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
when
K
is a part of the input.

Proof.

Given an instance
(n,\mathcal{T},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{K})
of
Min-Max RS
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we construct the corresponding instance
(n^{\prime},\mathcal{T}^{\prime},\mathbb{P}^{N},\alpha)
of
CVaR RS
as follows. Let
n^{\prime}=n+1
and
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}=\mathcal{T}\cup T_{0}
, where
T_{0}=\{n+1\}
. This forms a set
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{s}\in\{0,1\}^{n+1}
. Observe that
x_{n+1}=1
in any feasible solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{s}
.
Let
l=\lceil\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}\rceil
and fix
N=K+l-1
. Define the probability distribution

\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}_{K},\underbrace{\boldsymbol{\zeta},\dots,\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{l-1\text{ copies}}),

where the costs of the variables
x_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, under
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\prime}_{i}
are the same as under
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
and the cost of
x_{n+1}
is 0; the costs of the variables
x_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, under
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
are 0 and the cost of
x_{n+1}
is equal to a large constant
M
(we can choose, for example,
M=\sum_{i=1}^{K}||\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}||_{1}
). Using the equality
\lceil x\rceil=x+r
for
r\in[0,1)
, we get

\frac{l-1}{N}=\frac{\lceil\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}\rceil-1}{K+\lceil\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}\rceil-1}=\frac{\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}+r-1}{K+\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}+r-1}=\frac{K\alpha+(1-\alpha)(r-1)}{K+(1-\alpha)(r-1)}<\alpha,

where the inequality follows from the fact that
\alpha,r\in[0,1)
and thus
(1-\alpha)(r-1)\in[-1,0)
. We also obtain

\frac{l}{N}=\frac{\lceil\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}\rceil}{K+\lceil\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}\rceil-1}\geq\frac{\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}}{K+\frac{K\alpha}{1-\alpha}}=\alpha

Hence
\alpha\in(\frac{l-1}{N},\frac{l}{N}]
. Using Proposition
1
, we get for any solution
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}^{\prime}_{s}
:

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\alpha N}M+\dots+\frac{1}{\alpha N}M}_{l-1\text{ times }}+(1-\frac{l-1}{\alpha N})\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\sigma(1)}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},

where
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\sigma(1)}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=\max_{i\in[K]}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
. Note that
(1-\frac{l-1}{\alpha N})>0
, since there are
l
positive weights in (
8
) (see Proposition
1
).
Thus, there is a solution to
Min-Max RS
with the maximum cost at most
c
if and only if there is a solution to
CVaR RS
with the Conditional Value at Risk at most
\frac{(l-1)M}{\alpha N}+(1-\frac{l-1}{\alpha N})c
.
∎

Since for
\epsilon=0
,
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
is equivalent to
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
, we get the following corollary:

Corollary 1
.

The
Distr RS
problem is strongly NP-hard for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

The
RS
problem can be seen as a very special case of other basic combinatorial optimization problems. In particular, it is a special case of the
Shortest Path
problem. To see this, consider the
series-parallel
graph
G
shown in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
An instance of the
Shortest Path
problem, corresponding to
RS
.

The arcs belonging to the subsequent layers of
G
correspond to
T_{1},\dots,T_{\ell}
. The costs of the arcs are equal to the costs of the tools. It is easy to see that each
s-t
path corresponds to some solution of the
RS
problem and vice versa.
Since every
s-t
path in
G
is also a spanning tree of
G
, the same transformation applies to the
Minimum Spanning Tree
problem. This leads to the following corollary:

Corollary 2
.

The
Distr Shortest Path
and
Distr Spanning Tree
problems are strongly NP-hard
in series-parallel graphs
for each
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

The
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
problem for
\alpha=1
reduces to minimizing the expected solution cost. In this case, the problem with
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\xi}_{N})
easily reduces to solving the deterministic problem
\mathcal{P}
with the aggregated cost vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
. Hence, the complexity of
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
for
\alpha=1
is then the same as the complexity of
\mathcal{P}
. It has been shown in
[
17
]
that solving
\mathcal{P}
for the aggregated cost vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
yields a
w_{1}N
-approximate solution to the problem of minimizing OWA with nonincreasing weights, i.e.
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\dots\geq w_{N}
.
Using Proposition
1
we conclude that
w_{1}=\min\{1,\frac{1}{\alpha N}\}
, so the approximation ratio becomes
\min\{N,\frac{1}{\alpha}\}
. We have thus established the following result:

Proposition 2
.

If
\mathcal{P}
can be solved in polynomial time, then
\textsc{CVaR}~\mathcal{P}
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
is approximable within
\min\{N,\frac{1}{\alpha}\}
.

4
Solving the Wasserstein distributionally robust problem

In this section, we will propose some exact and approximate methods of solving the
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
problem. We will consider the problem with various

-norms,

q\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 1}
, and support sets
\Xi
. We start with the case when

. Then, we will consider a support
\Xi
being a hyperrectangle in

(such a model has been considered in
[
23
]
). Finally, we will show some results for any convex, closed, and bounded support
\Xi
.

4.1
The support set
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}

The following theorem is a generalization of the result obtained in
[
23
]
:

Theorem 3
.

If
\Xi=\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, then
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
can be expressed as

&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},\end{array}

(11)

where

,

if

, and

is the dual norm to

\frac{1}{q}+\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}=1

Proof.

See Appendix

∎

If
q=1
, then (

) reduces to minimizing the Conditional Value at Risk for the empirical distribution
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}
. Indeed, the dual norm
||\boldsymbol{x}||_{\infty}
is either 0 (when
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
) or 1 (when
\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{0})
and the second component in the objective function of (

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle t+\frac{1}{\alpha N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}+\lambda^{\frac{q-1}{q}}\gamma\epsilon\\

If
\alpha=1
, then model (

[
18
]
)

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle{\rm E}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\epsilon||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\epsilon||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}\\
&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},\end{array}

(13)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}

which can be represented equivalently as

\begin{array}[]{lll}\min&\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda^{\frac{q-1}{q}}\epsilon\\
&\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{b},\\
&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq\lambda,\\
&\boldsymbol{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n},\\
&\lambda\geq 0.\end{array}

(14)

Sometimes, (

\mathcal{P}
is polynomially solvable and

or

. In the former case the term

.
For example, when

is the

. In
[

and alternative method of solving (

deterministic problems

Proposition 3
.

If
(\ref{mod01})

\gamma=\min\{N,\frac{1}{\alpha}\}

Proof.

We will show first that for each

4.2
The support set
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}
and
q=1

In this section, we examine the model investigated in
[
23
]
, where the support set is defined as
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}=\{\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}:\boldsymbol{a}\leq\boldsymbol{\xi}\leq\boldsymbol{b}\}
, so lower
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and upper
\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
bounds on the costs are known. To solve
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
for this support set, some mixed integer programs have been constructed in
[
23
]
. In this section, we will show how the programs can be simplified. Furthermore, a special case with
q=1
can be solved more efficiently.

If
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}\in\Xi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}
for each
i\in[N]
, then an optimal solution to
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
will not change if we replace
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{a},\boldsymbol{b}}
with
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{b}}
, that is we fix the lower bounds
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{0}
. Indeed, a worst probability distribution
\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})
for
\boldsymbol{x}
is of the form

\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N})
,
where
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
for
i\in[N]
(all the components of
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}
are nonnegative). Therefore
\mathbb{P}^{N}
is still a worst probability distribution for
\boldsymbol{x}
if we fix
\boldsymbol{a}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

Proposition 4
.

If the support set is
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{b}}
,
\alpha=\frac{l}{N}
for some
l\in[N]
and
q=1
, then for each
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}

\max_{\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]=\min\left\{\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{l}N\epsilon\right\}.

(16)

Proof.

Fix
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
and assume that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\geq\dots\geq\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
.
We show first that

\max_{\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\leq\min\left\{\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{l}N\epsilon\right\}.

(17)

Indeed, the inequality
\max_{\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]\leq\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
follows from the fact that
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
is the maximum cost of
\boldsymbol{x}
in
\Xi_{\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{b}}
. Using Proposition
1
, we get for each
\mathbb{P}^{N}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N})\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]=\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\sigma(i)}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\sigma(i)})^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{l}\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{\sigma(i)}||_{1}\leq{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{l}N\epsilon

and (
17
) follows. Consider a probability distribution of the form

\mathbb{P}^{{}^{\prime}N}=(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{l}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{l},\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{l+1},\dots,\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{N})\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}),

where
\sum_{i=1}^{l}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{1}\leq N\epsilon
,
\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\geq\boldsymbol{0}
, and
\Delta_{ij}=0
if
x_{j}=0
for
i\in[N]
,
j\in[n]
. Using Proposition
1

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{{}^{\prime}N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]=\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i})^{T}\boldsymbol{x}={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^{l}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{1}.

Consider two cases:

1.

\sum_{i=1}^{l}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{1}<N\epsilon
. This means that either
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}+\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i})^{T}\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
for each

or we can further increase

so that

\sum_{i=1}^{l}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{1}=N\epsilon

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{{}^{\prime}N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{l}N\epsilon\leq\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x},

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}
is an upper bound on

The above cases show that equality in (

We can find an optimal solution to
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
for the problem satisfying the assumptions of Proposition
4
by solving two easier problems. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
minimize
\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}

, while

\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
be the solution among
\boldsymbol{x}_{1}

that has smaller value of (

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}
is an optimal solution to
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
. Then

\min\left\{\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}^{*},{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}^{*}]+\frac{1}{l}N\epsilon\right\}\geq\min\left\{\boldsymbol{b}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime},{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}]+\frac{1}{l}N\epsilon\right\}

\max_{\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}],

4.3.1
Approximation algorithm

In this section, we propose a method for computing approximate solutions to
\textsc{Distr}~\mathcal{P}
with a convex, closed, and bounded support set
\Xi
. Let
\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
maximize
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\boldsymbol{\xi}
over
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\Xi
, where
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,1,\dots,1)^{T}
. Suppose that
\alpha\in(\frac{l-1}{N},\frac{l}{N}]
for some
l\in[N]
.
Let
c\geq 1
be the smallest constant such that

||\overline{\boldsymbol{\xi}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{i}||_{q}\leq c\frac{\epsilon N}{l},\;\;i\in[N].

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we discussed the class of combinatorial optimization problems with uncertain costs in the objective function. Assuming that a sample of cost realizations is available, we used the Wasserstein distance to define a set of admissible probability distributions for the random costs vector. We then seek a solution minimizing the CVaR risk measure for the worst probability distribution. We showed that the problem is strongly NP-hard for basic combinatorial optimization problems (in particular for the shortest path problem), except for the case when CVaR becomes the expectation. We showed some exact and approximation methods of solving the problem. In particular, we proposed a row generation algorithm which can be applied to arbitrary convex support sets. We also constructed an approximation algorithm whose idea is to minimize CVaR for an appropriately distorted sample. Some computational tests indicated that the distributionally robust approach, based on the Wasserstein distance, may give better results than the sample average approximation.

An interesting open problem is the complexity of computing a worst probability distribution for a given solution. In this paper, we used a mixed integer programming formulation for this purpose, which, however, can be complex to solve for larger instances. More efficient algorithms for particular support sets (for example, ellipsoidal ones) should be constructed. Also, the proposed algorithms should be tested on other combinatorial problems, possibly considering real data.

\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{\sigma(i)}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]
,

\max_{\mathbb{P}^{N}\in\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N})}{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\max&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}\\
&\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q}\leq\epsilon,\\
&\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},&i\in[N].\end{array}\right.

(34)

Let us focus on the inner maximization problem in (
34
). The Lagrangian function with Lagrangian multiplier
v\geq 0
for this problem takes the following form:

L(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N},v)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-v(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q}-\epsilon)=

\sum_{i=1}^{l}(w_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-v\frac{1}{N}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q})-v\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=l+1}^{N}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q}+v\epsilon,

which follows from the fact that
w_{l+1}=\dots=w_{N}=0
.
We now show that

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}}(w_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-v\frac{1}{N}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0&\text{if}&Nw_{i}||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}\leq v,\\
\infty&\text{if}&Nw_{i}||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}>v.\end{array}\right.

(35)

Indeed, from the definition of the dual norm (see, e.g.,
[
4
]
), we get for each
t\geq 0

t||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}=\sup\{\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{T}_{i}\boldsymbol{x}:||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q}\leq t\}

and, because
v\geq 0

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}}(w_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-v\frac{1}{N}||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q})=\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{||\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}||_{q}\leq t}(w_{i}\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}-v\frac{1}{N}t)=

\sup_{t\geq 0}(w_{i}t||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}-v\frac{1}{N}t)=\sup_{t\geq 0}t(w_{i}||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}-v\frac{1}{N}),

which implies (
35
). Hence

\inf_{v}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N}}L(\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\Delta}_{N},v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}v\epsilon&\text{if}&Nw_{i}||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}\leq v,\;i\in[l],\\
\infty&\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The optimization problem in (
34
) is a convex one that satisfies the Slater condition for each
\epsilon>0
. Thus, by strong duality

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\max_{i\in[l]}Nw_{i}\epsilon||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}.

Finally, using the definition of the weights in Proposition
1
, we obtain

{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+N\epsilon||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}
if
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{N}
and
{\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\mathbb{P}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]={\rm CVaR}^{\alpha}_{\widehat{\mathbb{P}}^{N}}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{}^{T}\boldsymbol{x}]+\frac{1}{\alpha}\epsilon||\boldsymbol{x}||_{q^{\prime}}
, otherwise. This yields (
11
).

∎
