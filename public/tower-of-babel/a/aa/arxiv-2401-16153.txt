1.
Introduction

1.1.
Two martingale square functions

Let
(X,\mathscr{F},\mu)
be a probability space. We consider a discrete filtration, i.e. a sequence of
\sigma
-algebras
\mathscr{F}_{n}\subset\mathscr{F}_{n+1}\subset\mathscr{F}
,
n=0,1,2,\ldots
, such that each
\mathscr{F}_{n}
is generated by a finite or countable family
\mathscr{D}_{n}
of disjoint measurable sets called a partition. Namely, we suppose
\mathscr{D}_{0}=\{X\}
and each set
A\in\mathscr{D}_{n}
,
n\geq 0
, is a union of some elements of
\mathscr{D}_{n+1}
, which we call children of
A
. Given
x\in X
denote by
V_{n}(x)
the unique element of
\mathscr{D}_{n}
containing the point
x
. In this setting the probability conditional expectation of a random variable
f
with respect to the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathscr{F}_{n}
may be written by

(1.1)

\mathbf{E}(f|\mathscr{F}_{n})(x)=\frac{1}{\mu(V_{n}(x))}\int_{V_{n}(x)}f,\quad x\in X.

Recall that a finite sequence of random variables
f=\{f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
is said to be a martingale with respect to a filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{k}\}
(or a sequence of partitions
\{\mathscr{D}_{k}\}
), if
\mathbf{E}(f_{k}|\mathscr{F}_{k-1})=f_{k-1}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n
. Let
d_{k}=f_{k}-f_{k-1}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n
be the difference sequence of
f
.
Then the definition of the martingale may be equivalently written by
\mathbf{E}(d_{k}|\mathscr{F}_{k-1})=0
,
k\geq 1
, which in the case of discrete filtration means

(1.2)

\int_{V}d_{k}=0\hbox{ whenever }V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,n.

Consider the following non-classical and classical martingale square functions

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}f(x)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|D_{k}(x)|^{2}\right)^{1/2},\hbox{ where }D_{k}(x)=\sum_{V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}}\|\textbf{1}_{V}d_{k}\|_{\infty}\cdot\textbf{1}_{V}(x),

(1.4)

\displaystyle Sf(x)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}|d_{k}(x)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Clearly, we have
S(f)\leq\mathfrak{S}(f)
, since it is easy to check that
|d_{k}(x)|\leq D_{k}(x)
. In general, these square functions may differ depending on the martingale. We have an equality
S(f)=\mathfrak{S}(f)
whenever our filtration
\{\mathscr{F}_{k}\}
is dyadic, i.e. any element
V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
has exactly two children
V^{+},V^{-}\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
with
\mu(V^{+})=\mu(V^{-})=\mu(V)/2
. In the dyadic case we will simply have
D_{k}(x)=|d_{k}(x)|
. If the elements of
\mathscr{D}_{k}
are dyadic intervals of
[0,1)
, then we get a Haar martingale, i.e. we can write

f_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{2^{k+1}}a_{j}h_{j},\quad k=1,2,\ldots,n,

where
h_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots
, are the Haar functions, defined by

(1.5)

\displaystyle h_{1}(x)\equiv 1,\quad h_{n}(x)=\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{n}^{+}}(x)-\textbf{1}_{\Delta_{n}^{-}}(x),\quad n\geq 2,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{+}=\left[\frac{j-1}{2^{k}},\frac{2j-1}{2^{k+1}}\right),\quad\Delta_{n}^{-}=\left[\frac{2j-1}{2^{k+1}},\frac{j}{2^{k}}\right)

if
n=2^{k}+j
,
n\geq 0
,
1\leq j\leq 2^{k}
. In the case of Rademacher martingale, i.e. if
d_{k}(x)=a_{k}r_{k}(x)
, where
r_{k}(x)=\sin(2^{k}\pi x)
are the Rademacher independent random variables, we have
D_{k}(x)=|d_{k}(x)|\equiv|a_{k}|
. Thus both square functions are constant and we can write

(1.7)

\mathfrak{S}(f)=S(f)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

1.2.
Sub-Gaussian estimates

Square function
\mathfrak{S}
was first considered by Chang-Wilson-Wolff in
[
3
]
, proving a sub-Gaussian estimate for infinite sequences of martingales. The result of
[
3
]
can be equivalently stated for finite martingale sequences as follows.

Theorem A

(
[
3
]
)
.

If
f=\{f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
is a discrete martingale, then for any
\lambda>0
,

(1.8)

\mu\{f_{n}-f_{0}>\lambda\}\leq\exp\left(-\lambda^{2}/2\|\mathfrak{S}(f)\|_{\infty}^{2}\right).

In fact, this result was stated in
[
3
]
for the dyadic filtration in the unit cube
[0,1)^{n}
, but the proof works for an arbitrary discrete filtration (see
[
3
]
, Theorem 3.1). The proof of (
1.8
) stated in
[
3
]
was suggested by Herman Rubin, which replaces a much longer argument of the authors of
[
3
]
. Namely, the proof is adapted to the proof of classical Azuma-Hoeffding’s martingale inequality
[
2
,
7
]
. It is based on a fundamental identity of sequential analysis known in statistics and Hoeffding’s lemma
[
7
]
. Ivanishvili and Trail in
[
8
]
proved a version of inequality (
1.8
) for the classical martingale square function (
1.4
), considering homogeneous filtrations
\{\mathscr{F}_{k}\}
, that is if
U\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
is the children of an element
V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
, then
\mu(U)\geq\alpha\mu(V)
, where
0<\alpha\leq 1/2
is a common constant for all such choices.

Theorem B

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
f=\{f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
be a discrete martingale with respect to an
\alpha
-homogeneous filtration. Then

(1.9)

\mu\{x\in X:\,f_{n}-f_{0}>\lambda\}\leq\exp\left(-\alpha\lambda^{2}/\|S(f)\|_{\infty}^{2}\right),\quad\lambda>0.

A counterexample provided in
[
8
]
shows that a sub-Gaussian estimate like (
1.9
), involving the classical square function, fails for non-homogeneous filtrations.

1.3.
Khintchine type inequalities

One can state Theorems
A
and
B
in the terms of a martingale difference sequences

(1.10)

d=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\},

substituting
f_{n}-f_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}
in inequalities (
1.8
) and (
1.9
). For the sake of convenience in the sequel everything will be stated in terms of (
1.10
), denoting the square function (
1.3
) by
\mathfrak{S}(d)
. Denote

(1.11)

A_{p,n}=\sup_{d=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\}}\frac{\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}\right\|_{p}}{\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}},

where
\sup
is taken over all non-trivial martingale-difference sequences (
1.10
) with a fixed number of elements
n
. Obviously we have
A_{p,n}\leq A_{p,n+1}
. Taking
\sup
in (
1.11
) over all martingale-difference sequences (
1.10
), without restriction on the number of elements, we get another constant
A_{p}
, for which clearly we have
A_{p}=\lim_{n\to\infty}A_{p,n}
. Considering only Haar or Rademacher martingales in (
1.11
), the corresponding constants will be denoted by
A_{p,n}({\rm Haar})
and
A_{p,n}({\rm Rademacher})
respectively. Obviously,

(1.12)

A_{p,n}\geq A_{p,n}({\rm Haar})\geq A_{p,n}({\rm Rademacher}).

It is well known that

(1.13)

A_{p}({\rm Rademacher})=2^{1/2}\big(\Gamma((p+1)/2))/\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{1/p},\quad p>2,

and equivalently, every Rademacher sum satisfies the inequality

(1.14)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}r_{k}\right\|_{p}\leq 2^{1/2}\big(\Gamma((p+1)/2))/\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{1/p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where the constant on the right hand side is the best possible. For even integers
p\geq 4
the proof of this inequality goes back to Khintchine’s work
[
11
]
, which sharpness later was proved by Stechkin
[
17
]
. Later on Young
[
20
]
established (
1.14
) for all real numbers
p\geq 3
, and finally in 1982 Haagerup
[
6
]
introduced a new method, proving sharp bound (
1.14
) for all parameters
p>2
. Komorowski in
[
12
]
proved

(1.15)

A_{p,n}({\rm Rademacher})=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}\right\|_{p},\quad p\geq 3,

i.e. if
p\geq 3
and
n
are fixed, then the best constant in (
1.14
) is
\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}\right\|_{p}
.
For even
p>3
this relation earlier was established by Efron
[
5
]
and Eaton
[
4
]
. The proof of (
1.15
) for general parameters
p\geq 3
given in
[
12
]
is based on an inequality provided in
[
4
]
. See also
[
14
]
for a detailed review of the subject.

Remark 1.1
.

Recall that sub-Gaussian estimate (
1.8
) imply a Khintchine type inequality
A_{p}\leq c\sqrt{p}
, but this is a too rough approach to obtaining the exact value of
A_{p}
even if the constant in (
1.8
) is sharp.

1.4.
Main results

In this paper we prove that inequalities in (
1.12
) must actually be equalities whenever
p\geq 3
. This implies extensions of Khintchine’s inequality (
1.14
) and relation (
1.15
) for general discrete martingales. The main results of the paper are the following theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any
p>2
we have
A_{p,n}=A_{p,n}({\rm Haar})
.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
p\geq 3
, then

(1.16)

A_{p,n}=A_{p,n}({\rm Haar})=A_{p,n}({\rm Rademacher})=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}\right\|_{p},

where
r_{k}
are the Rademacher functions.

Theorem
1.2
is an extension of the results of
[
12
]
(see (
1.15
)) for general martingale-differences. Relation (
1.16
) implies the following Khintchine type inequality.

Corollary 1.1
.

If
p\geq 3
, then for any discrete martingale-difference (
1.10
) we have the bound

(1.17)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}\right\|_{p}\leq\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}\right\|_{p}\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty},

which is sharp, since for
d_{k}=r_{k}/\sqrt{n}
we have equality in (
1.17
).

It follows from the central limit theorem that

(1.18)

A_{p,n}=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}\right\|_{p}\to 2^{1/2}\big(\Gamma((p+1)/2))/\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{1/p}\hbox{ as }n\to\infty

and applying the sharpness of (
1.17
), we can say that
A_{p,n}
is increasing with respect to
n
if
p\geq 3
. Thus from (
1.17
) it follows that

(1.19)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}\right\|_{p}\leq 2^{1/2}\big(\Gamma((p+1)/2))/\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{1/p}\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty},\quad p\geq 3,

which is an extension of inequality (
1.14
) in the case
p\geq 3
for general discrete martingale-difference sequences. Also we note that in the case of dyadic martingale the square function
\mathfrak{S}(d)
in (
1.19
) can be replaced by the classical one
S(d)
. In particular,

Corollary 1.2
.

If
\{h_{k}\}
is the Haar system defined by (
1.6
) and
p\geq 3
, then the inequality

(1.20)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}h_{k}\right\|_{p}\leq 2^{1/2}\big(\Gamma((p+1)/2))/\sqrt{\pi}\big)^{1/p}\left\|\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}h_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right\|_{\infty},\quad p\geq 3,

holds for any coefficients
a_{k}
.

Let us also state the following sharp sub-Gaussian inequalities.

Corollary 1.3
.

For any discrete martingale-difference (
1.10
) and for any number
0<\lambda<1/2
we have

(1.21)

\mathbf{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\cdot\left(\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}}{\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}}\right)^{2}\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\lambda}},

where the constant on the right hand side is the best possible. Moreover, it is the best constant if we consider only dyadic or Rademacher martingales in (
1.21
) .

Consider the Orlicz space
L^{\psi}
of random variables, corresponding to the Young function
\psi(t)=e^{t^{2}}-1
, and equipped with the Luxembourg norm

(1.22)

\|f\|_{\psi}=\inf\{u>0:\,\mathbf{E}\left[\psi\left(f/u\right)\right]\leq 1\}.

Applying (
1.21
) we can immediately get the following sharp bound.

Corollary 1.4
.

If
\psi(t)=e^{x^{2}}-1
, then for any discrete martingale-difference (
1.10
) it holds the sharp bound

(1.23)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}\right\|_{\psi}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}\cdot\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}.

Remark 1.2
.

It was proved by Peskir
[
15
]
the inequality

(1.24)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}\right\|_{\psi}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}\cdot\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|X_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

for any sequence
X=\{X_{k}:\,k=1,2,\ldots,n\}
of independent symmetric random variables. Inequality (
1.24
) can be easily deduced from (
1.23
). Indeed, if
X
is a discrete sequence, then it becomes a martingale-difference on a discrete filtration. So (
1.23
) holds, since using independence we will have

(1.25)

\mathfrak{S}(X)=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|X_{k}\|_{\infty}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The general case of (
1.24
) may be reduced to the discrete case, applying a standard approximation argument.
We refer also related papers
[
19
,
16
]
, where authors prove Khintchine type inequality for independent symmetric random variables, with other square functions in the spirit of (
1.24
).

Remark 1.3
.

Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
show certain extreme properties of Haar and Rademacher random variables in a class of martingale difference sequences, implying extensions of some properties of Rademacher system to general martingales. Some extreme properties of Rademacher system in a class of uniformly bounded martingale differences were considered in
[
10
,
9
,
13
]
(see also
[
1
]
, chap. 9). Moreover, those papers consider more general sequences, namely multiplicative sequences of bounded random variables
\phi_{n}
,
n=1,2,\ldots
, i.e.
{\textbf{E}}\left[\prod_{j\in A}\phi_{j}\right]=0
for all nonempty finite subsets
A\subset\mathbb{N}
of positive integers. In particular, the result of
[
10
]
states that if
G:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
is a function convex with respect to each variables and
\phi=\{\phi_{k}:\,k=1,2,\ldots,n\}
is a multiplicative system of random variables, satisfying
A_{k}\leq\phi_{k}(x)\leq B_{k}
, then

(1.26)

\textbf{E}\left[G\left(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}\right)\right]\leq\textbf{E}\left[G\left(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\right)\right],

where
\xi_{k}
are the
\{A_{k},B_{k}\}
- valued independent mean zero random variables. The case of
A_{k}=-1
and
B_{k}=1
of inequality (
1.26
) was proved in
[
13
]
.

Remark 1.4
.

It is a remarkable result of Wang
[
18
]
that for any
p\geq 3
and any conditionally symmetric martingale-difference
d=\{d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}\}
we have the sharp bound

(1.27)

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}\right\|_{p}\leq R_{p}\|S(d)\|_{p},\quad p\geq 3

where
R_{p}
is the rightmost zero of the Hermite function
H_{p}(x)
. Here
H_{p}(x)
is the solution of
the Hermite differential equation

(1.28)

H^{\prime\prime}_{p}-xH^{\prime}_{p}+pH_{p}=0.

It is known that
R_{p}=O(\sqrt{p})
.

2.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Since a sequence of random variables may form a martingale difference with respect to different filtrations, the square function (
1.3
)
strongly depends on both random variables
d_{k}
and the partitions (or filtration)
\mathscr{D}_{k}
. So if a sequence of functions
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
form a martingale difference relative to an increasing sequence of partitions
\mathscr{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{D}_{n}
, then we say the collection

(2.1)

d=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}|\mathscr{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{D}_{n}\},\quad 1\leq k<n,

forms a MD-system (martingale difference system). Denote by
\mathrm{pr}(A)
the parent of a given element
A\in\mathscr{D}_{j}
,
j>1
, which is the unique element of
\mathscr{D}_{j-1}
, containing
A
. Set

(2.2)

\|d\|_{p}=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{k}\right\|_{p},\quad\mathbb{U}(d)=\|d\|_{p}/\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty},\quad p\geq 1,

where
\mathfrak{S}(d)
is the square function (
1.3
). Hence for the constant
A_{p,n}
we have

(2.3)

A_{p,n}=\sup_{d}\mathbb{U}(d),

where
\sup
is taken over all non-trivial MD-systems (
2.1
). One can take the
\sup
in (
2.3
) over MD-systems, satisfying certain property (P). In that case we will use the notation
A_{p,n}(P)
. We say that a MD-system (
2.1
) is
k
-dyadic with
2\leq k\leq n
, if each
V\in\mathscr{D}_{j}
with
1\leq j<k
has exactly two children in
\mathscr{D}_{j+1}
, and 1-dyadic if
d_{1}
takes two values. The property
n
-dyadic is the same as dyadic defined in the introduction. Observe that to prove Theorem
1.1
it is enough to prove

(2.4)

A_{p,n}((k-1)-{\rm dyadic})=A_{p,n}(k-{\rm dyadic})\hbox{ for all }1<k\leq n.

Introduce an intermediate property (IP) for MD-system (
2.1
) that requires

IP)
(k-1)
-dyadic &
|d_{k}(x)|=\|\textbf{1}_{V}\cdot d_{k}\|_{\infty},\quad x\in V\in\mathscr{B}_{k-1}
.

Hence, equality (
2.4
) and so the theorem will be proved if we prove following two relations:

R1:

A_{p,n}((k-1)-{\rm dyadic})=A_{p,n}({\rm IP})
,

R2:

A_{p,n}({\rm IP})=A_{p,n}(k-{\rm dyadic})
.

Proof of R1.

Without loss of generality we can suppose that
X=[0,1)
and each partition
\mathscr{D}_{k}
consists of intervals of the form
[a,b)
. It is enough to prove that for any
(k-1)
-dyadic MD-system (
2.1
) there exists a MD-system

\bar{d}
with the IP-property such that
\mathbb{U}(d)\leq\mathbb{U}(\bar{d})
. Let (
2.1
) be a
(k-1)
-dyadic MD-system. The desired MD-system will be obtained from
d
by reconstructing only the
k
’th function
d_{k}
and the partitions
\mathscr{D}_{k},\ldots,\mathscr{D}_{n}
. Namely, after these changes we will have a MD-system

(2.5)

\bar{d}=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k-1},\bar{d}_{k},d_{k+1},\ldots,d_{n}|\mathscr{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{D}_{k-1},\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{k},\ldots,\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{n}\},

which in any cases will be
(k-1)
-dyadic. The function
d_{k}
is constant on each interval
J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
, so we denote this constant by
\xi(J)
.
For
I=[a,b)\in\mathscr{D}_{n}
consider the unique interval
\bar{I}\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
such that
\bar{I}\supset I
. We have,
d_{k}(x)=\xi(\bar{I})
whenever
x\in\bar{I}
, and
|\xi(\bar{I})|\leq\|\textbf{1}_{\mathrm{pr}(\bar{I})}\cdot d_{k}\|_{\infty}
. Divide
I
into two intervals
I^{+}
and
I^{-}
, setting

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lambda=\lambda(\bar{I})=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\frac{\xi(\bar{I})}{\|\textbf{1}_{\mathrm{pr}(\bar{I})}\cdot d_{k}\|_{\infty}}\right)\in[0,1],

(2.7)

\displaystyle c=(1-\lambda)a+\lambda b\in[a,b],

(2.8)

\displaystyle I^{+}=[a,c),\quad I^{-}=[c,b).

Using these notations, denote

(2.9)

\bar{d}_{k}(x)=\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n}}\|\textbf{1}_{\mathrm{pr}(\bar{I})}\cdot d_{k}\|_{\infty}\left(\textbf{1}_{I^{+}}(x)-\textbf{1}_{I^{-}}(x)\right).

The required properties of
\bar{d}_{k}
are the followings:

(2.10)

\displaystyle|\bar{d}_{k}(x)|=\|\textbf{1}_{V}\cdot d_{k}\|_{\infty}\hbox{ whenever }x\in V\in\mathscr{B}_{k-1},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{I}\bar{d}_{k}=\int_{I}d_{k}\hbox{ for any }I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},

which easily follow from (
2.8
) and (
2.9
).
Then define the partition
\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{j}
,
k\leq j\leq n
, replacing each interval
V\in\mathscr{D}_{j}
by two sets

(2.12)

V^{+}=\bigcup_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},\,I\subset V}I^{+},\quad V^{-}=\bigcup_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},\,I\subset V}I^{-}.

Hence the construction of the elements of the MD-system (
2.5
) is complete. Clearly, the new MD-system
\bar{d}
is still
(k-1)
-dyadic. Thus, taking into account (
2.10
), we can say that
\bar{d}
satisfies IP-property. Let us prove that (
2.5
) is a MD-system, i.e. the functions in (
2.5
) form a martingale difference with respect to the partitions of (
2.5
). We need to check the martingale difference property only for the elements
\bar{d}_{k}
and
d_{j}
with
j>k
.
Applying (
2.11
), for any
V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
we obtain

(2.13)

\int_{V}\bar{d}_{k}=\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},\,I\subset V}\int_{I}\bar{d}_{k}=\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},\,I\subset V}\int_{I}d_{k}=\int_{V}d_{k}=0

that means
\mathbf{E}(\bar{d}_{k}|\mathscr{D}_{k-1})=0
. To show
\mathbf{E}(d_{j}|\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{j-1})=0
for
j>k
choose an element
V^{+}\in\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{j-1}
generated by a
V\in\mathscr{D}_{j-1}
(see (
2.12
)). If
I\in\mathscr{D}_{n}
and
I\subset V
, then we have
V\subseteq\bar{I}\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
. We have
V\subseteq J
for some
J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
. By (
2.6
) and (
2.7
), we have
|I^{+}|=\lambda|I|
with a common parameter
\lambda=\lambda(\bar{I})
. Thus, since the functions
d_{j}
are constant on each interval
I\in\mathscr{D}_{n}
, using (
2.12
), for
j>k
we can write

\int_{V^{+}}d_{j}=\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},\,I\subset V}\int_{I^{+}}d_{j}=\lambda\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n},\,I\subset V}\int_{I}d_{j}=\lambda\int_{V}d_{j}=0,

where the latter follows from the martingale-difference property of the initial MD-system
d
. Similarly,
\int_{V^{-}}d_{j}=0
. This implies
\mathbf{E}(d_{j}|\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{j-1})=0
and so (
2.5
) is a MD-system. By (
2.11
) we can write

(2.14)

\int_{I}\left(\bar{d}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}^{n}d_{j}\right)=\int_{I}\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j},\quad I\in\mathscr{D}_{n}.

Thus, since the functions
d_{j}
are constant on
I
, applying Jessen’s inequality, we get the bound

(2.15)

\|\bar{d}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n}}\int_{I}\left|\bar{d}_{k}+\sum_{j\neq k}d_{j}\right|^{p}\geq\sum_{I\in\mathscr{D}_{n}}\int_{I}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}\right|^{p}=\|d\|_{p}^{p},

By the definition of the square function, both
\mathfrak{S}\bar{d}(x)
and
\mathfrak{S}d(x)
are square roots of
n
-term sums. We will show that the corresponding terms in these sums coincide. The equality of first
k-1
terms in these sums is immediate, since the first
k-1
elements of MD-systems
d
and
\bar{d}
coincide. For the terms with indexes
j\geq k
we have the following. For any
x\in[0,1)
there is a unique sequence
V_{j}\in\mathscr{D}_{j}
,
j=1,2,\ldots,n
such that
x\in V_{j}
. For an appropriate choice of signs
\varepsilon_{j}=\pm
we will also have
x\in\cap_{k\leq j\leq n}V_{j}^{\varepsilon_{j}}
. Applying the definition of
\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{j}
,
j\geq k
, one can check,

(2.16)

\displaystyle\|\textbf{1}_{V_{j-1}}d_{j}\|_{\infty}=\|\textbf{1}_{V_{j-1}^{\varepsilon_{j}}}d_{j}\|_{\infty},\quad j>k,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\|\textbf{1}_{V_{k-1}}\bar{d}_{k}\|_{\infty}=\|\textbf{1}_{V_{k-1}}d_{k}\|_{\infty}(\hbox{see }\eqref{x67}).

Thus we obtain equality of all the terms in the sums of
\mathfrak{S}\bar{d}(x)
and
\mathfrak{S}d(x)
. So we get
\mathfrak{S}\bar{d}(x)=\mathfrak{S}d(x)
everywhere. Combining this with (
2.15
), we obtain
\mathbb{U}(d)\leq\mathbb{U}(\bar{d})
, completing the construction of (
2.5
) and the proof of relation R1.
∎

Proof of R2.

Now suppose that (
2.1
) satisfies the intermediate-property IP. We need to get a
k
-dyadic MD-system
\bar{d}
such that
\mathbb{U}(d)\leq\mathbb{U}(\bar{d})
. The only lack in the properties of
d
is that some sets in
\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
may have more that two children. Starting our construction, we will replace
d_{j}
by
\bar{d}_{j}
for
j>k
and
\mathscr{D}_{j}
by
\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{j}
for
j\geq k
. Thus our new MD-system will look like

(2.18)

\bar{d}=\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{k-1},d_{k},\bar{d}_{k+1}\ldots,\bar{d}_{n}|\mathscr{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{D}_{k-1},\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{k},\ldots,\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{n}\}.

For any
V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
consider two sets

(2.19)

V^{\pm}=\{x\in X:\,d_{k}(x)=\pm\|\textbf{1}_{V}\cdot d_{k}\|_{\infty}\},

which we define as child sets of the element
V
in
\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{k}
. Hence,

(2.20)

\bar{}\mathscr{D}_{k}=\{V^{\pm}:\,V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}\}.

If we stop our construction here the sets of
\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
would exactly have two children in
\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{k}
and we will have
\|d\|_{p}=\|\bar{d}\|_{p}
(that is good), but the square function of a new MD-system
\bar{d}
can be bigger than that of
d
(which is bad). So we have to continue the reconstruction to fix the problem. Suppose for a fixed
V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1}
the integrals

(2.21)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|J|}\int_{J}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}\right|^{p},\quad J\in\mathscr{D}_{k},\,J\subset V^{+},

(2.22)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|J|}\int_{J}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}\right|^{p},\quad J\in\mathscr{D}_{k},\,J\subset V^{-},

attains their maximum for the intervals
J=J_{V}^{+}
and
J=J_{V}^{-}
respectively. Let
\Lambda_{I\to J}
denote the dilation from
[0,1)
to
[0,1)
, which is a linear mapping from an interval
I\subset[0,1)
onto an interval
J\subset[0,1)
. We define the elements
\bar{d}_{j}(x)
and
\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{j}
for
j>k
on the intervals
J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}
,
J\subset V^{\pm}
making dilation of those elements from
J_{V}^{\pm}
onto each such
J
. Namely,

(2.23)

\displaystyle\bar{d}_{j}(x)=\sum_{V\in\mathscr{B}_{k-1}}\bigg(\sum_{J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}:\,J\subset V^{+}}d_{j}(\Lambda_{J\to J_{V}^{+}}(x))\cdot\textbf{1}_{J}(x)

(2.24)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sum_{J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}:\,J\subset V^{-}}d_{j}(\Lambda_{J\to J_{V}^{-}}(x))\cdot\textbf{1}_{J}(x)\bigg),\quad j>k,

(2.25)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{j}^{+}=\bigg\{\bigcup_{J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}:\,J\subset V^{+}}\Lambda_{J\to J_{V}^{+}}^{-1}(I):\,V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1},\,I\in\mathscr{D}_{j},\,I\subset J_{V}^{+}\bigg\},

(2.26)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{j}^{-}=\bigg\{\bigcup_{J\in\mathscr{D}_{k}:\,J\subset V^{-}}\Lambda_{J\to J_{V}^{-}}^{-1}(I):\,V\in\mathscr{D}_{k-1},\,I\in\mathscr{D}_{j},\,I\subset J_{V}^{-}\bigg\},

(2.27)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{j}=\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{j}^{+}\cup\bar{\mathscr{D}}_{j}^{-},\quad j>k.

Hence the elements of (
2.18
) have been already defined. One can check that

\bar{d}
is
k
-dyadic MD-system and satisfies
\mathfrak{S}(\bar{d})\leq\mathfrak{S}(d)
. Using the maximal property of the intervals
J_{V}^{\pm}
, we also have
\|d\|_{p}\leq\|\bar{d}\|_{p}
. Thus,
\mathbb{U}(d)\leq\mathbb{U}(\bar{d})
. This completes the proof of R2-relation and so the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
below were proved in
[
12
]
and
[
19
]
respectively. For the completeness we will sate the proofs of those lemmas. The proof of Lemma
3.1
is taken from
[
12
]
. For Lemma
3.2
an alternative proof is given.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
12
]
)
.

For a fixed
\xi>0
and
p>3
the function

(3.1)

u(t)=\frac{|t+\xi|^{p-1}\cdot{\rm sign\,}(t+\xi)+|t-\xi|^{p-1}\cdot{\rm sign\,}(t-\xi)}{t}

is increasing over
t>0
.

Proof.

For the derivative of function (
3.1
) we have

(3.2)

u^{\prime}(t)=\frac{|t+\xi|^{p-2}((p-2)t-\xi)+|t-\xi|^{p-2}((p-2)t+\xi)}{t^{2}}.

The derivative of the numerator of (
3.2
) is equal

t(p-1)(p-2)\left(|t+\xi|^{p-3}{\rm sign\,}(t+\xi)+|t-\xi|^{p-3}{\rm sign\,}(t-\xi)\right),

which is positive if
t>0
. Since this numerator is zero at
t=0
and its derivative is greater than zero we get
u^{\prime}(t)\geq 0
.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(
[
19
]
)
.

Let
p>3
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
be a fixed real number. Then the sum

(3.3)

|x+y+\xi|^{p}+|x+y-\xi|^{p}+|y-x+\xi|^{p}+|y-x-\xi|^{p}

where
x,y
satisfy
x^{2}+y^{2}=r^{2}
, attains its maximum if and only if
|x|=|y|=r/\sqrt{2}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can suppose that
\xi\geq 0
,
r=1
and
y\geq 1/\sqrt{2}\geq x>0
. Let
f(x)
denote the function (
3.3
) after substitution
y=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
. It is enough to prove that
f(x)
is increasing on the interval
(0,1/\sqrt{2})
. Applying Lemma
3.2
and

(|x|^{p})^{\prime}=p|x|^{p-1}{\rm sign\,}x,\quad p>1,

for the derivative of
f(x)
at a point
x\in(0,1/\sqrt{2})
we get

(3.4)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)

\displaystyle=p\left(1-\frac{x}{y}\right)\bigg(|x+y+\xi|^{p-1}{\rm sign\,}(x+y+\xi)

(3.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|x+y-\xi|^{p-1}{\rm sign\,}(x+y-\xi)\bigg)

(3.6)

\displaystyle+p\left(1+\frac{x}{y}\right)\bigg(|y-x+\xi|^{p-1}{\rm sign\,}(y-x+\xi)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|y-x-\xi|^{p-1}{\rm sign\,}(y-x-\xi)\bigg)

(3.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{p(y-x)(y+x)}{y}(u(y+x)-u(y-x))>0,

where
u(t)
is the function in (
3.1
). Thus, applying Lemma
3.1
, we get
f(x)
is increasing over
(0,1/\sqrt{2})
.
∎

Applying Lemmas
3.2
consecutively, we get

Lemma 3.3
.

If
p>3
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
are fixed, then the value

(3.9)

\left\|\xi+\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}r_{k}\right\|_{p}

where the Rademacher coefficients
a_{k}
satisfy
\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}=r^{2}
, attains its maximum if and only if
|a_{k}|=r/\sqrt{n}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
p\geq 1
and
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed. Then for any non-trivial function
g\in L^{1}(0,1)
, satisfying
\mathbf{E}(g)=0
, the function

(3.10)

h(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\xi+x\cdot g(t)\right|^{p}dt

is increasing over
(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality it is enough to prove that

(3.11)

\int_{0}^{1}\left|\xi+g(t)\right|^{p}dt<\int_{0}^{1}\left|\xi+r\cdot g(t)\right|^{p}dt\hbox{ for }r>1.

Applying an approximation, we can suppose that
g
is a step function on
[0,1)
with equal constancy intervals. Let

(3.12)

E^{+}=\{x\in[0,1):\,g(x)\geq 0\},\quad E^{-}=\{x\in[0,1):\,g(x)<0\}.

We will construct a sequence of step functions
g=g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{m}=r\cdot g
, with the same constancy intervals as
g
has, such that

(3.13)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(g_{k})=0,

(3.14)

\displaystyle 0\leq g_{k}\cdot\textbf{1}_{E^{+}}\leq g_{k+1}\cdot\textbf{1}_{E^{+}}\leq rg(x)\cdot\textbf{1}_{E^{+}},

(3.15)

\displaystyle 0\geq g_{k}\cdot\textbf{1}_{E^{-}}\geq g_{k+1}\cdot\textbf{1}_{E^{-}}\geq rg(x)\cdot\textbf{1}_{E^{-}},

(3.16)

\displaystyle\int_{X}\left|\xi+g_{k}(t)\right|^{p}dt<\int_{X}\left|\xi+g_{k+1}(t)\right|^{p}dt,\quad k=1,2,\ldots,m-1.

Suppose by induction we have already defined the functions
g_{1},\ldots,g_{l}
such that the above conditions hold for
k=1,\ldots,l-1
. If
g_{l}=rg
, then the process will stop. Otherwise, applying (
3.13
), one can say that
g_{l}
has at least two constancy intervals
I^{+}\subset E^{+}
and
I^{-}\subset E^{-}
such that

(3.17)

\displaystyle rg(x)=b_{+}>g_{l}(x)=a_{+}\geq 0,\,x\in I^{+},

(3.18)

\displaystyle rg(x)=b_{-}<g_{l}(x)=a_{-}<0,\,x\in I^{-}.

Denote

(3.19)

\lambda=\min\{b_{+}-a_{+},a_{-}-b_{-}\}>0.

We define
g_{l+1}
by changing the values of
g_{l}
, on the intervals
I^{+}
and
I^{-}
by
a_{+}+\lambda
and
a_{-}-\lambda
respectively. Clearly, the conditions (
3.13
), (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) are satisfied for
k=l
too. To show (
3.16
) it is enough to observe the inequality

\int_{I^{+}\cup I^{-}}\left|\xi+g_{l}(t)\right|^{p}dt\leq\int_{I^{+}\cup I^{-}}\left|\xi+g_{l+1}(t)\right|^{p}dt,

which is the same as the numerical inequality

|\xi+a_{+}|^{p}+|\xi+a_{-}|^{p}<|\xi+a_{+}+\lambda|^{p}+|\xi+a_{-}-\lambda|^{p}.

The latter follows from the fact that the function
t(x)=|c+x|^{p}+|c-x|^{p}
is increasing when
x>0
. After this step of induction we will get one more interval (either
I^{+}
or
I^{-}
), where
g_{l+1}
coincides with
rg
. Thus, continuing the induction we will finally get a function
g_{m}=rg
. Thus, we will have (
3.11
), completing the proof of lemma.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Recall that
\mathscr{D}_{0}=\{X\}
. We say a dyadic MD-system (
2.1
) is
m
-Rademacher,
1\leq m\leq n
, if for any
V\in\mathscr{D}_{m-1}
we have

(3.20)

|d_{m}(x)|=|d_{m+1}(x)|=\ldots=|d_{n}(x)|=c,\quad x\in V.

Here
n
-Rademacher is nothing but to be dyadic, while
1
-Rademacher property requires

(3.21)

|d_{1}(x)|=|d_{2}(x)|=\ldots=|d_{n}(x)|=c,\quad x\in X.

We will first prove that

(3.22)

A_{p,n}(m-{\rm Rademacher})=A_{p,n}((m-1)-{\rm Rademacher}),\quad 1<m\leq n.

To this end it is enough to prove that for any
m
-Rademacher system
d
there exists a
(m-1)
-Rademacher system
\bar{d}
such that
\mathbb{U}(d)\leq\mathbb{U}(\bar{d})
.
We apply two procedures inside arbitrary
V\in\mathscr{D}_{m-2}
, changing the values of some functions
d_{k}
on
V
.

Procedure 1:
Since our MD-system is dyadic,
V\in\mathscr{D}_{m-2}
has exactly two children
V^{+},V^{-}\in\mathscr{D}_{m-1}
with
\mu(V^{+})=\mu(V^{-})=\mu(V)/2
.
According to the property being
m
-Rademacher we have

(3.23)

\displaystyle|d_{m}(x)|=|d_{m+1}(x)|=\ldots=|d_{n}(x)|=c_{+},\quad x\in V^{+},

(3.24)

\displaystyle|d_{m}(x)|=|d_{m+1}(x)|=\ldots=|d_{n}(x)|=c_{-},\quad x\in V^{-},

(3.25)

\displaystyle|d_{k}(x)|=c_{k},\quad x\in V,\quad 1\leq k<m.

We have

(3.26)

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{S}d(x))^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}c_{k}^{2}+(n-m+1)(c_{+})^{2},\quad x\in V^{+},

(3.27)

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{S}d(x))^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{m-1}c_{k}^{2}+(n-m+1)(c_{-})^{2},\quad x\in V^{-}.

Without loss of generality we can suppose that
c_{-}\leq c_{+}
. In this case we change the values of functions
d_{m},d_{m+1},\ldots,d_{n}
on the set
V^{-}
just multiplying those by
c_{+}/c_{-}
. One can check that after this change the
L^{\infty}
-norm of the square function
\mathfrak{S}(d)
will not be changed, but
\|d\|_{p}
will increase because of Lemma
3.4
. Therefore
\mathbb{U}(d)
increases. After these changes we will get

(3.28)

|d_{m}(x)|=\ldots=|d_{n}(x)|=c_{V}\hbox{ for every }x\in V,

where
c_{V}
is either
c_{+}
or
c_{-}
.

Procedure 2:
So after Procedure-1 we will have (
3.28
) for any
V\in\mathscr{D}_{m-2}
. If along with (
3.28
) we also had

(3.29)

|d_{m-1}(x)|=c_{V}

with the same constant, then we could stop our procedure. Perhaps (
3.29
) may fail. One can define functions
\bar{d}_{m-1},\bar{d}_{m},\ldots,\bar{d}_{n}
such that the
\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-2},\bar{d}_{m-1},\bar{d}_{m},\ldots,\bar{d}_{n}\}
forms a dyadic MD-system with respect to the same partitions
\mathscr{D}_{j}
and on each
V\in\mathscr{D}_{m-2}
we have

(3.30)

\displaystyle|\bar{d}_{m-1}(x)|=|\bar{d}_{m}(x)|=\ldots=|\bar{d}_{n}(x)|={\rm constant}_{V},\quad x\in V,

(3.31)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m-1}^{n}|\bar{d}_{k}(x)|^{2}=\sum_{k=m-1}^{n}|d_{k}(x)|^{2},\quad x\in V.

Clearly, we will have
\|\mathfrak{S}(\bar{d})\|_{\infty}=\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}
. Applying Lemma
3.3
, we also get
\|d\|_{p}\leq\|\bar{d}\|_{p}
and therefore
\mathbb{U}(d)\leq\mathbb{U}(\bar{d})
. Completing Procedure-2, we will have proved required relation (
3.22
).
To finalize the proof of theorem it remains to apply (
3.22
) consecutively. Then we get

(3.32)

A_{p,n}({\rm diadic})=A_{p,n}(1-{\rm Rademacher})=\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}\right\|_{p},

where
r_{k}
are the Rademacher functions.
∎

Proofs of corollaries

Corollaries
1.1
and
1.2
immediately follow from Theorems
1.1
and

.

Proof of Corollary

.

Writing Taylor series of function

, we get

(4.1)

\mathbf{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda\cdot\left(\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}}{\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}}\right)^{2}\right)\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda^{k}}{k!}\left\|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}}{\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}}\right\|_{2k}^{2k}

, we can say that the quantities
\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}/\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}\right\|_{2k}^{2k}

Thus, using the Binomial series of
(1-x)^{-1/2}
, (
4.1
) can be estimated by

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2k)!}{2^{k}(k!)^{2}}\lambda^{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2\lambda}}.

∎

Proof of Corollary

.

Let
f=\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}/\|\mathfrak{S}(d)\|_{\infty}

and

. Applying (

), we can write

\mathbf{E}(\psi(f/u))\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-2/u^{2}}}-1.

Hence, according to the definition of Luxembourg norm we obtain

, since for

the right hand side of (

) is
1
.

5.
Open problems

The question whether inequality (
1.19
) holds for parameters
2<p<3
remains open. Note that according to Theorem
1.1
it is enough to consider Haar martingales insted of general discrete martingale-differences. Some standard calculations show that function (
1.17
) is not increasing if
2<p<3
: it decreases over
(0,c\xi)
and increases over
(c\xi,\infty)
, where
c\approx 1.2
is an absolute constant. This is the only issue that do not allow to apply the same technique in the case
2<p<3
. So let us state the following.

Problem 1
.

Prove inequality (
1.20
) for the parameters
2<p<3
.

In general, (
1.16
) and (
1.17
) fail for some parameters
2<p<3
, even for the Rademacher sums. Indeed, validity of (
1.17
) for some
p>2
implies increaseness of
\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}/\sqrt{n}\|_{p}
with respect to
n
, while for
p=2.5
a standard calculation shows

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(r_{1}+r_{2}\right)\right\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{2^{p/2}}{2}>\left\|\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(r_{1}+r_{2}+r_{3}\right)\right\|_{p}^{p}=\frac{3^{p/2}}{4}+\frac{3}{4\cdot 3^{p/2}}.

Problem 2
.

Find the values of
A_{p,n}({\rm Haar})
and
A_{p,n}({\rm Rademacher})
for
2<p<3
.

For a discussion of other problems, concerning Khintchine type inequalities we refer review paper
[
14
]
.
