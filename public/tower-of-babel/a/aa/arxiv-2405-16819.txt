Automatic Domain Adaptation by Transformers in In-Context Learning
Ryuichiro Hataya, Kota Matsui, Masaaki Imaizumi

Abstract
Selecting or designing an appropriate domain adaptation algorithm for a given problem remains challenging. This paper presents a Transformer model that can provably approximate and opt for domain adaptation methods for a given dataset in the in-context learning framework, where a foundation model performs new tasks without updating its parameters at test time. Specifically, we prove that Transformers can approximate instance-based and feature-based unsupervised domain adaptation algorithms and automatically select an algorithm suited for a given dataset. Numerical results indicate that in-context learning demonstrates an adaptive domain adaptation surpassing existing methods.



1
Introduction

Domain adaptation provides a methodology for transferring “knowledge” obtained in one domain to another related domain
(
Ben-David et al. 2010
)
.
In particular, the
unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA)
setting allows for training of the predictive model using the rich labeled data in the source domain, when there is no labeled data in the target domain.
Such methods are vital in the areas where sample sizes are inherently small, such as in the medical field, and numerous studies have been reported focusing on domain adaptation for medical image analysis, for example
(
Guan & Liu 2021
)
.
In this study, we focus in particular on instance-based and feature-based unsupervised domain adaptation methods.

One of the challenges associated with domain adaptation is selecting and differentiating effective methods.
There are many existing approaches in domain adaptation, such as the instance-based methods
(
Dai et al. 2007
;
Sugiyama et al. 2007
;
Kanamori et al. 2009
)
and the feature-based methods
(
Daumé III 2009
;
Ganin et al. 2016
)
.
However, when each method is effective differs based on data.
Specifically, the instance-based methods
(
Sugiyama et al. 2007
;
Kanamori et al. 2009
)
are effective when there exists a valid density ratio between covariates of the source and target domains, whilst the feature-based methods are effective if we can find a domain-invariant representation on a common feature space between the domains.
To properly select an appropriate approach to the given data, it is essential to assess whether they meet the specific conditions of each method.

There have been some attempts to automatically select appropriate UDA algorithms.
For example,
learning to transfer (L2T)

(
Ying et al. 2018
)
employs a meta-learning approach to predict appropriate features to transfer for a given pair of source and target domains.
In addition to L2T,
meta-transfer learning

(
Sun et al. 2019
)
and
CNAPS

(
Requeima et al. 2019
)
, which learn functions to adjust weights of trained neural nets in a task-adaptive manner, have been proposed, and thus algorithm selection based on meta-learning have achieved some success.
However, these existing methods have been developed only for selection within specific frameworks, such as feature-based domain adaptation.
That is, the possibility of cross-framework algorithm selection, such as “choosing which of the instances and features to transfer” was unknown.

As a method for developing versatile algorithms, the in-context learning ability of foundation models has garnered significant attention.
In-context learning, a form of meta-learning in foundational models, allows models to adapt to new tasks without updating their parameters.
In particular, the capabilities of Transformers in in-context learning have been intensively surveyed: for example,
Bai et al. 2023
showed that Transformers can learn algorithms such as gradient descent, as well as their optimal selection methods.
Such in-context learning abilities of Transformers have been validated from both experimental and theoretical aspects
(
Garg et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
;
Von Oswald et al. 2023
;
Akyürek et al. 2022
;
Xie et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Bai et al. 2023
;
Lin et al. 2023
;
Ahn et al. 2024
;
Raventós et al. 2024
)
.

In this study, we demonstrate that Transformer models effectively address the challenge in in-context learning.
Specifically, our analysis reveals that Transformers in the framework (i) solve domain adaptation problems by approximating the main UDA algorithms, and (ii) automatically select suitable methods adaptively to the dataset’s characteristics.
The results indicate that Transformers can, in context, not only implement various transfer learning methods but also possess the capability to appropriately select among them.
These findings suggest that by accurately choosing a combination of these methods based on the dataset, performance can be enhanced beyond what is achievable by applying the methods individually.

Our specific contributions are as follows:

1.

We demonstrate that the Transformer can approximate the instance-based transfer learning method based on importance weighting with the uLSIF estimator, which utilizes the density ratio to address covariate shift.
We prove that Transformers internally learn the density ratio, effectively implementing uLSIF and importance weighting.
A technical contribution of this proof is showing how Transformers can approximate the computation of inverse matrices multiplication required for uLSIF.

2.

We show that Transformers can approximate the feature-based transfer learning method DANN, which uses adversarial learning.
Within their architecture, Transformers solve the minimax optimization using a dual-loop mechanism. A significant technical contribution is developing the components that compute this dual-loop.

3.

We numerically demonstrate that Transformers trained with in-context learning perform better in domain adaptation tasks than separate existing methods. This empirically proves that Transformers can adaptively handle complex transfer learning tasks.

Preliminary

2.1
Unsupervised Domain Adaptation

Setup

We consider the
Unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA)
problem with two domains.
Let
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a compact input space and
\mathcal{Y}
be an output space.
Then, the source and the target domains,
P_{S}
and
P_{T}
, are distributions on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, let their density functions be
p_{S}
and
p_{T}
, and their marginal distributions on
\mathcal{X}
be
P_{S}^{X}
and
P_{T}^{X}
.
Denote
\mathcal{D}_{S}=\{({\bm{x}}^{S}_{i},y^{S}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{T}=\{{\bm{x}}^{T}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}
for the source labeled data and target unlabeled data, respectively and let
N\coloneqq n+n^{\prime}
.
Given a loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and a hypothesis space
\mathcal{F}
, UDA aims to minimize the target risk
\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}R_{T}(f)
, where
R_{T}(f)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim P_{T}}[\ell(f({\bm{x}}),y)]
is the target risk, without observing labels of the data from the target domain.
To this end, UDA methods aim to match the source and target distributions in the feature space, categorized into instance-based and feature-based approaches.

Instance-based Methods

Instance-based approaches reweight instances from the source domain to minimize the target risk, based on the following transferability assumption called
covariate shift
.

Assumption 1

(Covariate Shift,
Shimodaira 2000
)
.

Suppose that the distribution of
y
conditioned on the input
{\bm{x}}
is the same in the source and target domains, i.e.,

p_{S}(y|{\bm{x}})=p_{T}(y|{\bm{x}}).

(1)

Under this assumption,
R_{T}(f)=\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim P_{S}}(q({\bm{x}})\ell(f({\bm{x}}),y))
holds, where
q({\bm{x}})=p_{T}({\bm{x}})/p_{S}({\bm{x}})
is the density ratio between the marginal distributions of source and target.
Then,
Importance-weighted learning (IWL)

Sugiyama et al. 2012
;
Kimura & Hino 2024
obtains
\argmin_{f\in\mathcal{F}}R_{T}(f)
in two steps:

1.

Empirically learn an estimator of the density ratio
\widehat{q}
using
\{{\bm{x}}_{i}^{S}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{T}
, and then,

2.

minimize the weighted empirical risk
\widehat{R}_{T}(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{q}({\bm{x}}^{S}_{i})\ell(f({\bm{x}}^{S}_{i}),y^{S}_{i})
consists of data from the source domain.

Here, we introduce
unconstrained Least-Squares Importance Fitting (uLSIF)

Kanamori et al. 2009
, a method for density-ratio estimation.
Firstly, the density ratio is estimated by the linear basis function model

\widehat{q}_{\bm{\alpha}}({\bm{x}})={\bm{\alpha}}^{\top}{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}\phi_{j}({\bm{x}}),

(2)

where
{\bm{\alpha}}=[\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{J}]
and
{\bm{\phi}}=[\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{J}]
are parameter and feature vectors respectively, where
\phi_{j}:\mathcal{X}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
are feature maps for an input
{\bm{x}}
.
uLSIF estimates the weight
{\bm{\alpha}}
to directly minimize the following squared error:

L({\bm{\alpha}})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}(\widehat{q}_{\bm{\alpha}}({\bm{x}})-q({\bm{x}}))^{2}p_{S}({\bm{x}})\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\widehat{q}_{\bm{\alpha}}({\bm{x}})^{2}p_{S}({\bm{x}})-\widehat{q}_{\bm{\alpha}}p_{T}({\bm{x}})+\frac{1}{2}q({\bm{x}})^{2}p_{T}({\bm{x}})\right\}\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}.

(3)

As the third term of the RHS is constant to
{\bm{\alpha}}
, we can estimate
{\bm{\alpha}}
as
\widehat{{\bm{\alpha}}}=\argmin_{{\bm{\alpha}}\geq 0}\widehat{L}({\bm{\alpha}})
such that

\widehat{L}({\bm{\alpha}})=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{q}_{\bm{\alpha}}({\bm{x}}))^{2}-\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}\widehat{q}_{\bm{\alpha}}({\bm{x}})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert{\bm{\alpha}}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\alpha}}^{\top}{\bm{\Psi}}{\bm{\alpha}}-{\bm{\psi}}^{\top}{\bm{\alpha}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}{\bm{\alpha}}^{\top}{\bm{\alpha}},

(4)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter,
\Psi_{jj^{\prime}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{j}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{S})\phi_{j^{\prime}}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{S})
and
\psi_{j}\coloneqq\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}\phi_{j}({\bm{x}}_{i}^{T})
.
Then, if
\mathcal{F}
is a space of linear model, we can obtain a classifier with an empirical importance-weighted problem:

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{*}=\argmin_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}}\widehat{R}_{T}({\bm{w}}),~\widehat{R}_{T}({\bm{w}}):=\sum_{({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{S}}\widehat{q}_{\widehat{{\bm{\alpha}}}}({\bm{x}})\ell({\bm{w}}^{\top}{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}}),y).

(5)

Then, we obtain a classifier defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}):=({\bm{w}}^{*})^{\top}\phi({\bm{x}}).

(6)

Feature-based Methods

A feature-based method learns a domain-invariant feature map
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, where
\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
is a feature space, so that
R_{T}(f^{\prime})\approx\mathbb{E}_{({\bm{x}},y)\sim P_{S}}\ell(f^{\prime}(\phi({\bm{x}})),y)
, where
f^{\prime}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{Y}
.
The
domain adversarial neural network (DANN)

(
Ganin et al. 2016
)
is a typical method to achieve this by using adversarial learning.
DANN consists of three modules, a feature extractor
f_{F}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}^{\prime}
, a label classifier
f_{L}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to[0,1]
, and a domain discriminator
f_{D}:\mathcal{X}^{\prime}\to[0,1]
, parameterized by
{\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{L},{\bm{\theta}}_{D}
, respectively.
Then, a classifier
f_{L}
with an invariant feature extractor
f_{F}
can be obtained by solving the following minimax problem:

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{L}}\max_{{\bm{\theta}}_{D}}L({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{L})-\lambda\Omega({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{D}).

(7)

where
\lambda>0
is a regularization parameter,
L({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{L})\coloneqq\sum_{({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{S}}\gamma(f_{L}\circ f_{F}({\bm{x}}),y)
is label classification loss and
\Omega({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{D})\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{S}}\gamma(f_{D}\circ f_{F}({\bm{x}}),0)+\frac{1}{n^{\prime}}\sum_{({\bm{x}},y)\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}\gamma(f_{D}\circ f_{F}({\bm{x}}),1)
is domain classification loss.
Here,
\gamma(p,q)\coloneqq-q\log p-(1-q)\log(1-p)
for
p,q\in[0,1]
is a sigmoid cross entropy function.
The model parameters are updated by gradient descent with a learning rate of
\eta
as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{F}

\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{F}-\eta\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{F}}\left(L({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{L})-\lambda\Omega({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{D})\right),

(8)

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{L}

\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{L}-\eta\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{L}}L({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{L}),

(9)

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{D}

\displaystyle\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}_{D}-\eta\lambda\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}_{D}}\Omega({\bm{\theta}}_{F},{\bm{\theta}}_{D}).

(10)

2.2
In-context Learning

Setup.

In in-context learning, a fixed Transformer observes a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{({\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim(P)^{N}
with pairs of an input
{\bm{x}}_{i}
and its label
y_{i}
from a joint distribution
P
and a new query input
{\bm{x}}_{*}
then predicts
y_{*}
corresponding to
{\bm{x}}_{*}
.
Different from the standard supervised learning, in in-context learning, the Transformer is pre-trained on other datasets
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
from different distributions to learn an algorithm to predict
y_{*}
.
Unlike the standard meta learning, at the inference time of in-context learning, the parameters of a Transformer are fixed.
Our interest is to study the expressive power of a Transformer model for algorithms on a given dataset
\mathcal{D}
.

Transformer.

Define an
L
-layer Transformer consisting of
L
Transformer layers as follows.
The
l
th Transformer layer maps an input matrix
{\bm{H}}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
to
\widetilde{{\bm{H}}}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
and is composed of a self-attention block and a feed-forward block.
The self-attention block
\mathrm{Attn}^{(l)}:\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
is parameterized by
D\times D
matrices
\{({\bm{K}}^{(l)}_{m},{\bm{Q}}^{(l)}_{m},{\bm{V}}^{(l)}_{m})\}_{m=1}^{M}
, where
M
is the number of heads, and defined as

\mathrm{Attn}^{(l)}({\bm{X}})={\bm{X}}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\bm{V}}^{(l)}_{m}{\bm{X}}\sigma(({\bm{Q}}^{(l)}_{m}{\bm{X}})^{\top}{\bm{K}}^{(l)}_{m}{\bm{X}}).

(11)

\sigma
denotes an activation function applied elementwisely.

The feed-forward block
\mathrm{MLP}^{(l)}:\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}\to\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
is a multi-layer perceptron with a skip connection, parameterized by
({\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{1},{\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{D^{\prime}\times D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D\times D^{\prime}}
, such that

\mathrm{MLP}^{(l)}({\bm{X}})={\bm{X}}+{\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{2}\varsigma({\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{1}{\bm{X}}),

(12)

where
\varsigma
is an activation function applied elementwisely.
In this paper, we let both
\sigma
and
\varsigma
the ReLU function in this paper, following
Bai et al. 2023
.

In summary, an
L
-layer Transformer
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}
, parameterized by

{\bm{\theta}}=\{({\bm{K}}^{(l)}_{1},{\bm{Q}}^{(l)}_{1},{\bm{V}}^{(l)}_{1},\dots,{\bm{K}}^{(l)}_{M},{\bm{Q}}^{(l)}_{M},{\bm{V}}^{(l)}_{M},{\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{1},{\bm{W}}^{(l)}_{2})\}_{l=1}^{L},

(13)

is a composition of the abovementioned layers as

\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{X}})=\mathrm{MLP}^{(L)}\circ\mathrm{Attn}^{(L)}\circ\dots\circ\mathrm{MLP}^{(1)}\circ\mathrm{Attn}^{(1)}({\bm{X}}).

(14)

In the remaining text, the superscript to indicate the number of layer
(l)
is sometimes omitted for brevity, and the
n
th columns of
{\bm{H}}^{(l)},\widetilde{{\bm{H}}}^{(l)}
are denoted as
{\bm{h}}^{(l)}_{n},\widetilde{{\bm{h}}}^{(l)}_{n}
.
Following
Bai et al. 2023
, we define the following norm of a Transformer
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}
:

\left\lVert{\bm{\theta}}\right\rVert_{\mathrm{TF}}=\max_{l\in\{1,\dots,L\}}\left\{\max_{m\in\{1,\dots,M\}}\left\{\left\lVert{\bm{Q}}_{m}^{(l)}\right\rVert,\left\lVert{\bm{K}}_{m}^{(l)}\right\rVert\right\}+\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\lVert{\bm{V}}_{m}^{(l)}\right\rVert+\left\lVert{\bm{W}}_{1}^{(l)}\right\rVert+\left\lVert{\bm{W}}_{2}^{(l)}\right\rVert\right\},

(15)

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
for matrices indicates the operator norm in this equation.

3
Approximating UDA Algorithms by Transformers

This section demonstrates that transformers in in-context learning have the ability to approximate existing UDA algorithms. Specifically, we show that transformers can approximate uLSIF-based IWL, an instance-based method, and DANN, a feature-based method.

3.1
Setup and Notion

We consider in-context domain adaptation, where a fixed Transformer model is given a tuple
(\mathcal{D}_{S},\mathcal{D}_{T},{\bm{x}}_{*})
, where
{\bm{x}}_{*}\sim p_{T}^{X}({\bm{x}})
, and predicts
y_{*}
without updating model parameters.

We construct the input matrix using both source and target data.
Specifically, we suppose the input data, namely
({\bm{x}}_{i}^{S},y_{i}^{S})\in\mathcal{D}_{S}
and
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{T}\in\mathcal{D}_{T}
, are encoded into
{\bm{H}}^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times(N+1)}
as follows:

{\bm{H}}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{1}&\ldots&{\bm{x}}_{n}&{\bm{x}}_{n+1}&\ldots&{\bm{x}}_{N}&{\bm{x}}_{N+1}\\
y_{1}&\ldots&y_{n}&0&\ldots&0&0\\
t_{1}&\ldots&t_{n}&t_{n+1}&\ldots&t_{N}&t_{N+1}\\
s_{1}&\ldots&s_{n}&s_{n+1}&\ldots&s_{N}&s_{N+1}\\
1&\ldots&1&1&\ldots&1&1\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-(d+4)}&\ldots&{\bm{0}}_{D-(d+4)}&{\bm{0}}_{D-(d+4)}&\ldots&{\bm{0}}_{D-(d+4)}&{\bm{0}}_{D-(d+4)}\end{bmatrix},

(16)

where
{\bm{x}}_{i}={\bm{x}}_{i}^{S}
and
y_{i}=y_{i}^{S}
for
1\leq i\leq n
,
{\bm{x}}_{i}={\bm{x}}_{i-n}^{T}
for
n+1\leq i\leq N
,
{\bm{x}}_{N+1}={\bm{x}}_{*}
.

t_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq n
otherwise
0
is used to indicate which data point is from the source domain, and
s_{i}=1
for
1\leq i\leq N
otherwise
0
is used to mark training data.
We further define an output of a transformer for the prediction corresponding to
{\bm{x}}_{N+1}={\bm{x}}_{*}
.
For an input
{\bm{H}}^{(1)}
and its corresponding output matrix
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{H}}^{(1)})
, we write its
(2,N+1)
-th element of
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{H}}^{(1)})
as
\mathrm{TF}^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{H}}^{(1)})
.

We define the concepts necessary for our theoretical results.
To approximate smooth functions, such as loss functions
\ell
and
\gamma
, we use the following notion.

Definition 1

(
(\varepsilon,R,M,C)
-approximability by sum of ReLUs,
Bai et al. 2023
)
.

For
\varepsilon>0
and
R\geq 1
, a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
is
(\epsilon,R,M,C)
-approximabile by sum of ReLUs
if there exist a function

f({\bm{z}})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\sigma({\bm{a}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{z}}+b_{m})\quad\text{with}~\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left\lvert c_{m}\right\rvert\leq C,~\max_{m\in\{1,\dots,M\}}\left\lVert{\bm{a}}\right\rVert_{1}+b_{m}\leq 1,

(17)

where
{\bm{a}}_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},b_{m}\in\mathbb{R},c_{m}\in\mathbb{R},
, such that
\sup_{{\bm{z}}\in[-R,R]^{k}}\left\lvert g({\bm{z}})-f({\bm{z}})\right\rvert\leq\varepsilon
.

All functions approximated in this paper are included in this class.
To use this, we suppose
\left\lVert{\bm{x}}_{i}\right\rVert\leq B_{x}
and
\left\lVert y_{i}\right\rVert\leq B_{y}
for any
i\in\{1,\dots,N+1\}
, and
\left\lVert{\bm{w}}\right\rVert\leq B_{w}
for a model weight
{\bm{w}}
in in-context learning.
Additionally, for the feature map of IWL, we suppose
\left\lVert{\bm{\phi}}\right\rVert\leq 1
so that
\left\lVert{\bm{\phi}}({\bm{x}})\right\rVert\leq B_{x}
.

3.2
In-context IWL with uLSIF

We show a transformer in the ICL scheme can approximate the uLSIF estimator-based IWL algorithm, which we refer to as IWL in the following.

In this section, we consider the following setup:
Fix any
B_{w}>0
,
B_{\alpha}>0
,
L_{1},L_{2}>0
,
\eta_{1}
, and
\eta_{2}
.
Given a loss function
\ell
that is convex in the first argument, and
\nabla_{1}\ell
is
(\epsilon,R,M,C)
-approximable by the sum of ReLUs with
R=\max(B_{w}B_{x},B_{y},1)
.
We first state the main result of this section.
This theorem shows that the transformer has the performance to function as an algorithm almost equivalent to IWL for any input
{\bm{x}}_{*}
.
Note that the query
{\bm{x}}_{*}
is encoded in the input matrix
{\bm{H}}^{(1)}
defined in (
16
).

Theorem 1
.

Consider
\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}
with
{\bm{\phi}}
which is approximable by a sum of ReLUs.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
arbitrarily and set
L_{2}
as satisfying
0<\epsilon\leq B_{w}/2L_{2}
.
Suppose that an input
(\mathcal{D}_{S},\mathcal{D}_{T},{\bm{x}}_{*})
satisfies that
\sup_{{\bm{w}}:\left\lVert{\bm{w}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq B_{w}}\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}\widehat{R}_{T}({\bm{w}};\mathcal{D}_{S}))\leq\eta_{2}/2
and the minimizer
{\bm{w}}^{*}\in\argmin\widehat{R}_{T}({\bm{w}};\mathcal{D}_{S})
satisfies
\left\lVert{\bm{w}}^{*}\right\rVert\leq B_{w}/2
.
Then, there exists a transformer
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}
with
L_{1}+L_{2}+1
layers and
M
heads which satisfies the following:

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}^{*}({\bm{H}}^{(1)})-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*})\|\leq\varepsilon.

(18)

This result gives several implications.
First, while transformers with ICL have been shown to approximate algorithms for i.i.d. data
Bai et al. 2023
, this theorem extends it to show the capability of handling domain shifts.
This extension is nontrivial since it requires showing that the Transformer can express the algorithm’s ability of corrections for the domain shifts.
Second, this theorem shows that Transformers do not need to specify the feature map
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
.
Specifically, whatever feature map
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
is used for uLSIF, the transformer can approximate the uLSIF based on it.
In other words, pre-training a transformer induces a situation-specific feature map
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
.

Proof Outline

In our proof, we construct three types of sub-transformers and approximate IWL-uLSIF by combining them.
Specifically, the first sub-transformer with one layer to construct a feature map
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
, the second one calculates
{\bm{\alpha}}
with
L_{1}
layers, and this one optimizes the model weight
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
with
L_{2}
layers.
For simplicity, we define a notation
{\bm{z}}_{i}=[{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i},t_{i},s_{i},1]
.
In the following, we describe each approximation step by step.
The full proof is presented in
Section

A.1
.

Step 1:

Feature map approximation.

First, we construct a transformer that approximates the feature map
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
.
This construction is trivial under the assumption that the feature map
{\bm{\phi}}(\cdot)
is approximable by the sum of ReLUs.
For example, a self-attention block can construct a feature map with the RBF kernel.

Lemma 1
.

There exists a transformer with one layer which maps
{\bm{h}}_{i}^{(1)}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{0}}_{J},{\bm{0}}_{D-(d+4)}]
to
\widetilde{{\bm{h}}}_{i}^{(1)}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},\widehat{{\bm{\phi}}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),{\bm{0}}_{D-J-(d+4)}]
for
i=1,...,N+1
, such that
\|{\bm{\phi}}-\widehat{{\bm{\phi}}}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon
holds.

Step 2:

Density ratio parameter approximation.

Second, we construct a transformer that approximates
\widehat{{\bm{\alpha}}}
that minimizes the loss
\widehat{L}({\bm{\alpha}})
in
Eq.

4
, in which a technical difficulty lies.
For the approximation, we define a sequence of parameters
\{{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{GD}}^{(l)}\}_{l=1,2,...}
that converge to
\widehat{{\bm{\alpha}}}
, using an update equation by the gradient descent algorithm:

\displaystyle{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{GD}}^{(l+1)}={\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{GD}}^{(l)}-\eta_{1}\nabla\widehat{L}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{GD}}^{(l)}),~l=1,2,...,\mbox{~and~}{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{GD}}^{(1)}={\bm{0}}_{J}.

(19)

Then, we develop a Transformer layer that exactly implements a single update step of this equation.
We give the following statement.

Lemma 2
.

There exists a Transformer with
L_{1}

Here, we focus on the sub-problem that a pair of Transformer layers approximates a single step of update of DANN consisting of two-layer neural networks.
Specifically, the attention block at the first layer approximates the forward passes of networks, the succeeding MLP block computes the partial derivatives of the loss function, then the next attention block implements the minimax optimization step, and the last MLP block projects the weights.

The specific procedure is presented in the following steps.

4
Automatic Algorithm Selection by In-Context UDA

We demonstrate that Transformers can automatically select UDA algorithms based on data in context.
Specifically, we consider the selection of methods determined by whether the supports of the source distribution
p_{S}
and the target distribution
p_{T}
sufficiently overlap.
In cases where the support of the target distribution
p_{T}
overlaps with that of the source distribution
p_{S}
, the IWL algorithm, which operates using the density ratio
q({\bm{x}})=p_{T}({\bm{x}})/p_{S}({\bm{x}})
, is employed.
In other words, IWL should be employed when we have

\displaystyle p_{S}({\bm{x}})>0\mbox{~holds~for~all~}{\bm{x}}\mbox{~such~that~}p_{T}({\bm{x}})>0.

(30)

Conversely, when there is no such overlap, i.e.,
p_{S}({\bm{x}})=0
for some
{\bm{x}}
with
p_{T}({\bm{x}})>0
, one should select DANN, which does not rely on the density ratio.

We show that Transformers are capable of realizing the above design automatically.

\displaystyle\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{ICUDA}}({\bm{x}}):=\begin{cases}\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*})&\text{~if~}p_{S}({\bm{x}})>0,\forall{\bm{x}}\text{~such~that~}p_{T}({\bm{x}})>0,\\
\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{*})&\text{~otherwise},\end{cases}

(31)

The statement is as follows:

Theorem 3
.

Fix any
\epsilon>0
.
Suppose that
n^{\prime}\geq(1/\epsilon)^{3}\log n^{\prime}
holds and
p_{T}(\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous.
Then, there exists a Transformer
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}
with three layers and
M
heads satisfies the following with probability at least
1-1/n^{\prime}-O(\epsilon)
:
for any input
(\mathcal{D}_{S},\mathcal{D}_{T},{\bm{x}}_{*})
, a transformer
\mathrm{TF}_{\bm{\theta}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{TF}^{*}_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{H}}^{1})-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{ICUDA}}({\bm{x}}_{*})\|\leq\epsilon.

(32)

The results show that the Transformer can automatically check the condition (
30
) and use the appropriate algorithm without the user having to make a choice.
Note that the density ratio condition (
30
) is one of the options that are automatically learned, and in practice, Transformers can learn more complicated conditions.
Importantly, in this case, cross-validation cannot be used to make a selection since true labels of the target domain data cannot be observed.

Proof Outline

The key idea is to first implement kernel density estimation of
p_{S}({\bm{x}})
for
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}_{T}
and then select an algorithm with
\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{D}_{T}}\widehat{p}_{S}({\bm{x}})>\delta
, where
\delta>0
is a given threshold.
Here, we assume that each token is
{\bm{h}}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),{\bm{0}}]
, where
{\bm{\gamma}}
consists of weights of in-context IWL and DANN and is unused in this selection process.
Further details are discussed in
Section

A.3
.

Step 1:

Approximation of density estimation.
The first step is to approximate the density function
p_{S}
with kernel density estimation
\widehat{p}_{S}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\cdot,{\bm{x}}_{i})
, where
K
is a kernel, such as the RBF kernel, and evaluate it on each data point.

Lemma 7
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists an attention block that maps
{\bm{h}}_{i}
to
{\bm{h}}^{\prime}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},{\bm{0}}]

\left\lvert p_{i}-\bar{p}_{S}({\bm{x}}_{i})\right\rvert\leq\epsilon

Step 2:

Algorithm selection approximation.
Then, the Transformer decides which algorithm is appropriate based on
\softmin_{\beta,i}p_{i}>\delta
, where
\softmin_{\beta,i}p_{i}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\sum(-\beta p_{i})
is a relaxation of
\min
with an inverse temperature parameter
\beta>0

Lemma 8
.

For any
\epsilon>0

{\bm{h}}^{\prime}_{N+1}
to
{\bm{h}}^{\prime\prime}_{N+1}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{*}),p_{i},-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\sum_{i}\exp(-\beta p_{i}),\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}_{*})]
, where
|{\widetilde{f}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{ICUDA}}}|\leq\epsilon

6
Conclusion and Discussion

This paper proved that Transformers can approximate instance-based and feature-based domain adaptation algorithms and select an appropriate one for the given dataset in the in-context learning framework.
Technically, our results revealed that Transformers can approximately solve some types of linear equations and minimax problems.
Numerical experiments demonstrated that domain adaptation in in-context learning outperforms UDA methods tailored for specific assumptions.

Limitations

In this work, we presented that Transformer models can approximate popular UDA methods, namely, importance-weighted learning with the uLSIF estimator and domain adversarial neural networks.
However, the approximability of other domain adaptation techniques, such as parameter-based methods, is left unclear, and investigating it is an interesting direction.
Furthermore, investigating whether appropriate domain adaptation algorithms can be automatically selected from a larger pool of candidates remains a task for future work.

\frac{1}{\eta}\left\lVert\widehat{G}-G\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{K}B_{x}(\epsilon_{p}+B_{u}L_{r}(\epsilon_{\gamma}+B_{u}L_{\gamma}\epsilon_{r}))+2\sqrt{K}(B_{g}\epsilon_{r}+B_{r}(\epsilon_{\gamma}+B_{u}L_{\gamma}\epsilon_{r})).

(79)

By choosing
\epsilon_{p},\epsilon_{\gamma},\epsilon_{r}
appropriately, we can ensure
\left\lVert\widehat{G}-G\right\rVert\leq\eta\epsilon
.
Thus, we have an attention block
{\bm{h}}^{\prime\prime}\mapsto{\bm{h}}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}=[{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i},t_{i},s_{i},1,{\bm{u}}-\eta\nabla_{{\bm{u}}}(L-\lambda\Omega),{\bm{w}}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{w}}L,{\bm{v}}-\eta\nabla_{\bm{v}}\Omega,{\bm{0}},\bar{\Lambda}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\bar{\Delta}({\bm{x}}_{i}),g_{\Lambda,i},g_{\Delta,i}]
.

Step 4:

(The MLP block of the second layer)
This block projects the weights onto
\mathcal{W}
and fill the last four elements of
{\bm{h}}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}
with zeros, that is,
{\bm{h}}^{\prime\prime\prime}_{i}\mapsto[{\bm{x}}_{i},y_{i},t_{i},s_{i},1,\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}({\bm{u}}^{\prime}),\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}({\bm{w}}^{\prime}),\Pi_{\mathcal{W}}({\bm{v}}^{\prime}),{\bm{0}}_{4}]
.
By assumption, such a block exists.

∎

A.3
In-context UDA Algorithm Selection

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that the density
p_{S}
is Lipschitz continuous.
Define a density estimator
\widehat{p}_{S}(\cdot):=(nh)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}K^{\prime}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{i}^{T})
with a bandwidth
h>0
and some kernel function
K^{\prime}
, which is continuous and satisfying
\inf K^{\prime}d=1
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
For
n^{\prime}\geq\varepsilon^{-1/3}\log n
, with probability at least
1-1/n
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}|p_{S}({\bm{x}})-\widehat{p}_{S}({\bm{x}})|\leq\varepsilon.

(80)

Proof of Lemma
9
.

We regard the kernel function
K^{\prime}
as parameterized by the bandwidth parameter
h
as
K^{\prime}_{h}
, such that
\widehat{p}_{S}(\cdot)=(nh)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K^{\prime}_{h}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}_{i})
as a kernel function specified in
Jiang 2017
.
We apply Theorem 2 in
Jiang 2017
, then we obtain the statement.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

We show the theorem in the following steps.
We use a three-layer Transformer: the first self-attention block constructs a kernel density approximation
p_{i}=\widehat{p}_{S}({\bm{x}}_{i})
, the succeeding MLP block computes
\exp(-p_{i})
, then the next self-attention block computes
\sum_{i}\exp(-\beta p_{i})
, and then, the MLP layer computes
-\beta\sum_{i}\exp(-\beta p_{i})
, and the attention block of the third Transformer layer selects the final output from the outputs of UDA algorithms based on
-\beta\log\sum_{i}\exp(-\beta p_{i})>\delta
.

Step 1:

(The self-attention block of the first layer)

This block constructs kernel density estimation of the source domain
\widehat{p}_{S}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K(\cdot,{\bm{x}}_{i})
, where
K
is a kernel function, and obtains
p_{i}=\widehat{p}_{S}({\bm{x}}_{i})
for
i=1,\dots N
.
We adopt the RBF kernel as
K
, as it is universal.
As the RBF kernel
({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\mapsto K({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
is
(\epsilon_{1},B_{x},M_{1},C_{1})
-approximable, there exists a function
\bar{K}
such that

\bar{K}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{M_{1}}c_{m}\sigma({\bm{a}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}+d_{m}),\quad\text{with }\sum_{m=1}^{M_{1}}|c_{m}|\leq C_{1},|{\bm{a}}_{m}|+|{\bm{b}}_{m}|+d_{m}\leq 1~(\forall m),

(81)

where
\sup_{({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\in[-B_{x},B_{x}]^{d}\times[-B_{x},B_{x}]^{d}}|K({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})-\bar{K}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})|\leq\epsilon_{1}
.
A self-attention layer can represent
\bar{K}
with matrices

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{a}}_{m}\\
{\bm{x}}_{i}\\
d_{m}\\
-2B_{x}\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-2(d+1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{K}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{x}}_{j}\\
{\bm{b}}_{m}\\
1\\
1-t_{j}\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-2(d+1)}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{V}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{j}=c_{m}\frac{N+1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{0}}_{D-5}\\
1\\
{\bm{0}}_{4}\end{bmatrix}.

(82)

These matrices exist, and we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{1}}\sigma(\langle{\bm{Q}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{i},{\bm{K}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{j}\rangle){\bm{V}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{j}

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{1}}c_{m}\sigma({\bm{a}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{i}+{\bm{b}}_{m}^{\top}{\bm{x}}_{j}+d_{m}-2B_{x}(1-t_{j}))[{\bm{0}}_{D-5},1,{\bm{0}}_{4}]

(84)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})[{\bm{0}}_{D-5},1,{\bm{0}}_{4}].

(85)

Letting
p_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\bar{K}({\bm{x}}_{i},{\bm{x}}_{j})
, the output of this layer is
{\bm{h}}^{(1)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),{\bm{0}}_{5}]
to
{\bm{h}}^{\prime(1)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},{\bm{0}}_{4}]
.

Step 2:

(The MLP block of the first layer)

Because
p\mapsto\exp(-\beta p)
is
(\epsilon_{2},1,M_{2},C_{2})
-approximable, there exists a function

(86)

such that

{\bm{W}}_{1},{\bm{W}}_{2}

(87)

and
{\bm{W}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times M_{2}}

(88)

so that

{\bm{W}}_{2}\sigma({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{h}}^{\prime}_{i})=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{2}}\sigma(a_{m}p_{i}+b_{m})c_{m}{\bm{e}}_{D-4}.

(89)

Consequently, this block maps
{\bm{h}}^{\prime(1)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},{\bm{0}}_{4}]
to

.

Step 3:

(The Attention block of the second layer)

With a single-head self-attention block, we get
\sum_{i=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{I})
with the following matrices:

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\

(90)

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}\sigma(\langle{\bm{Q}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{i},{\bm{K}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{j}\rangle){\bm{V}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N+1}e(p_{i})\mathbbm{1}(n+1\leq j\leq n+n^{\prime})[{\bm{0}}_{D-3},1,{\bm{0}}_{2}]=[{\bm{0}}_{D-3},\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{i}),{\bm{0}}_{2}].

(91)

Thus, this block maps
{\bm{h}}^{(2)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},e(p_{i}),{\bm{0}}_{3}]
to
{\bm{h}}^{\prime(2)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},e(p_{i}),\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{j}),{\bm{0}}_{2}]

Step 4:

(The MLP block of the second layer)

Since
\log
is
(\epsilon_{4},n^{\prime},M_{4},C_{4})
-approximable by sum-of-ReLUs, there exists a function
\mu(t)
, where

\mu(t)=\sum_{m=1}^{M_{4}}c_{m}\sigma(a_{m}t+b_{m})\quad\text{with}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{4}}|c_{m}|\leq C_{4},|a_{m}|+|b_{m}|\leq 1~(\forall m),

(92)

such that
\sup_{t\in(0,n^{\prime})}|\mu(t)-\log(t)|\leq\epsilon_{4}
.
By selecting matrices
{\bm{W}}_{1},{\bm{W}}_{2}
such that
{\bm{W}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}
such that

{\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{i}=[a_{m}e+b_{m}]_{m=1}^{M_{4}},

(93)

and

consisting of elements of

(94)

so that

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}_{2}\sigma({\bm{W}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{i})

(95)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\sum_{m=1}^{M_{4}}c_{m}\sigma(a_{m}e+b_{m})[{\bm{0}},1,0]

(96)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[{\bm{0}},-\frac{1}{\beta}\mu(\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{i})),0].

(97)

As a result, by letting
q=-\frac{1}{\beta}\mu(\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{i}))
, this block maps
{\bm{h}}^{\prime(2)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},e(p_{i}),\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{i}),{\bm{0}}_{2}]
to
\widetilde{{\bm{h}}}^{(2)}_{i}={\bm{h}}^{(3)}_{i}=[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},e(p_{i}),\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{i}),q,0]
.

Step 5:

(The self-attention block of the third layer)

The outputs of UDA algorithms can be selected by
\mathbbm{1}(q\geq\delta)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*})+(1-\mathbbm{1}(q\geq\delta))\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{*})
.
This process can be approximated by
\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(t,s)=\sigma(a(t-s)+0.5)-\sigma(a(t-s)-0.5)
, where
a>0
, which approximates
\mathbbm{1}(t\geq s)
as
|\mathbbm{1}(t\geq s)-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(t,s)|=\frac{1}{2a}
.
This process can be represented by a self-attention layer with four heads consisting of the following matrices:

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{i}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}aq-a\delta+0.5\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{K}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{V}}_{1}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle(N+1)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*}){\bm{e}}_{D},

(98)

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{2}{\bm{h}}_{i}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}aq-a\delta-0.5\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{K}}_{2}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{V}}_{2}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle-(N+1)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*}){\bm{e}}_{D},

(99)

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{3}{\bm{h}}_{i}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}aq-a\delta+0.5\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{K}}_{3}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{V}}_{3}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle(N+1)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{*}){\bm{e}}_{D},

(100)

\displaystyle{\bm{Q}}_{4}{\bm{h}}_{i}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}aq-a\delta-0.5\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{K}}_{4}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1\\
{\bm{0}}_{D-1}\end{bmatrix},\quad{\bm{V}}_{4}{\bm{h}}_{j}=

\displaystyle-(N+1)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{*}){\bm{e}}_{D},

(101)

so that

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1,\dots,4}\sigma(\langle{\bm{Q}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{i},{\bm{K}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{j}\rangle){\bm{V}}_{m}{\bm{h}}_{j}

(102)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{\sigma(a(q-\delta)+0.5)-\sigma(a(q-\delta)-0.5)\}\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{*}){\bm{e}}_{D}

(103)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\{1-\sigma(a(q-\delta)+0.5)+\sigma(a(q-\delta)-0.5)\}\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{*}){\bm{e}}_{D}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}){\bm{e}}_{D}+(1-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta))\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}_{i})^{\mathrm{DANN}}{\bm{e}}_{D}.

(104)

Letting
\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}_{i})=\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}){\bm{e}}_{D}+(1-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta))\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}_{i})^{\mathrm{DANN}}{\bm{e}}_{D}
, this block finally outputs
[{\bm{z}}_{i},{\bm{\gamma}},\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}({\bm{x}}_{i}),p_{i},e(p_{i}),\sum_{j=n}^{n+n^{\prime}}e(p_{i}),q,\widetilde{f}({\bm{x}}_{i})]
.

We have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{f}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{ICUDA}}\right\rVert

(105)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\lVert(\widetilde{\zeta}\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}+(1-\widetilde{\zeta})\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}})-(\zeta\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}+(1-\zeta)\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}})\right\rVert

(106)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{\zeta}\right\rVert\left\lVert\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\widetilde{\zeta}-\zeta\right\rVert\left\lVert\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert 1-\widetilde{\zeta}\right\rVert\left\lVert\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}\right\rVert

(107)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{IWL}}\right\rVert+C\left\lVert\widetilde{\zeta}-\zeta\right\rVert+\left\lVert\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}\right\rVert

(108)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathrm{IWL}}+\epsilon_{\mathrm{DANN}}+C\left\lVert\widetilde{\zeta}-\zeta\right\rVert,

(109)

where
\widetilde{\zeta}=\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)
,
\zeta=\mathbbm{1}(\min_{i}\widehat{p}_{S}({\bm{x}}_{i})>\delta)
, and
C=\left\lVert\widetilde{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{DANN}}\right\rVert
.
Further, defining
r=\softmin_{\beta}\widehat{p}_{S}({\bm{x}}_{i})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{\zeta}-\zeta\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbbm{1}(r>\delta)-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)\right\rVert

(110)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbbm{1}(r>\delta)-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(r,\delta)\right\rVert+\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(r,\delta)-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)\right\rVert

(111)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2a}+\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(r,\delta)-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)\right\rVert

(112)

Assuming
r
is random, and its density is bounded, the second term is
\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(r,\delta)-\widehat{\mathbbm{1}}_{a}(q,\delta)\right\rVert=0
with probability of
1-O(|r-q|)
.
Here, we have

\displaystyle|r-q|

\displaystyle\leq|\softmin_{\beta}\widehat{p}_{S}-\softmin_{\beta}p_{S}|B_{x}+|\softmin_{\beta}p_{S}-\min p_{S}|B_{x}

(113)

\displaystyle\leq\|\softmin_{\beta}\|\|\widehat{p}_{S}-p_{S}\|B_{x}+\|\softmin_{\beta}-\min\|\|p_{S}\|B_{x}

(114)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\log n}{\beta}\epsilon^{\prime}B_{x}+\frac{\log n}{\beta}B_{x},

(115)

where
\epsilon^{\prime}=\|\widehat{p}_{S}-p_{S}\|
.
We used
\softmin_{\beta}p_{i}=-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\sum_{i}^{n}\exp(-\beta p_{i})\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\log n
and
|\softmin_{\beta}p_{i}-\min p_{i}|\leq\frac{\log n}{\beta}
for
(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
such that
p_{i}\geq 0
.
Hence, we can obtain
|r-q|<\epsilon
with sufficiently large
\beta
.
Thus, by properly selecting
a,\beta,\epsilon_{\mathrm{IWL}},\epsilon_{\mathrm{DANN}}
,
\left\lVert\widetilde{f}-\widehat{f}^{\mathrm{ICUDA}}\right\rVert
can be upper bounded by any
\epsilon>0
with probability at least
1-1/n^{\prime}-O(|r-q|)=1-1/n^{\prime}-O(\epsilon)
.
∎

Appendix B
Experimental Details

For the experiments, we used a machine equipped with an AMD EPYC 7543P 32-core CPU and an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPU with CUDA 12.4.
The Transformer model and the baselines were implemented with PyTorch v2.3
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
.

B.1
Two-moon 2D

In this problem, the source distribution is two-moon-shaped, and the target distribution is its random rotation, where the rotation is uniformly chosen between
-\pi/3
and
\pi/3
.
Gaussian noise of a standard deviation of 0.1 is added to each data point.
During the pre-training of the Transformer model, the source distribution is randomly rotated.

We used a Transformer model with eight layers, eight heads, and an embed dimension of 128 without Layer Normalization and trained it with an AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov & Hutter 2019
)
of a learning rate of
10^{-4}
and a weight decay of
10^{-2}
with a batch size of 64 for
10^{4}
iterations.
The baseline models were optimized for
10^{4}
iterations using an Adam optimizer
(
Kingma & Ba 2015
)
with the full batch.
uLSIF was trained with a learning rate of
0.1
, and the regularization term
\lambda
was set to
1.0
.
DANN was trained with a learning rate of
0.1
, and the regularization term
\lambda
was set to
1.0
.
The source-only neural network follows the DANN’s setup.

B.2
Colorized MNIST

In this setting, images of two digits are selected from the MNIST dataset to make a binary classification problem.
Images are downsampled to
8\times 8
pixels.
Their background colors depend on domains, where the color of the source domain is a given RGB color, and that of the target domain is its complement.
During the pertaining phase, digits and colors are randomly chosen.

We trained the Transformer model with the same setting as the Two-moon 2D problem, except for the embed dimension set to 256.
The baseline models were optimized for
10^{4}
iterations using an Adam optimizer with the full batch.
uLSIF was trained with a learning rate of
0.1
and the regularization term
\lambda
was set to
1.0
.
DANN was trained with a learning rate of
10^{-3}
, and the regularisation term
\lambda
was set to
10
.
The source-only neural network follows the DANN’s setup.
