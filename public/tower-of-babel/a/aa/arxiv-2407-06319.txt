Irreducible Unipotent Numerical Monoids
Mahir Bilen Can, Naufil Sakran

Abstract
In our earlier article [ 5 ] we initiated a study of the complement-finite submonoids of the group of integer points of a unipotent linear algebraic group. In the present article, we continue to develop tools and techniques for analyzing our monoids. In particular, we initiate a theory of ideals for unipotent numerical monoids. Keywords: Apéry set, pseudo-Frobenius set, Frobenius set, relative ideal, (pseudo-)symmetric unipotent numerical monoid, strongly (pseudo-)symmetric unipotent numerical monoid, irreducible unipotent numerical monoid MSC: 20G99, 20M14, 11B75

Introduction

This article builds on the observation that the theory of generalized numerical monoids is part of a broader family of finitely generated monoids that naturally emerge within algebraic groups.
Our primary objective here is to determine several structural invariants of these more general monoids, while shedding new light on generalized numerical semigroups, which form a fast developing area of research in semigroup theory.

The setup of our theory, which we will maintain throughout this article is as follows.

Let
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
denote the unipotent group consisting of all upper triangular unipotent matrices of size
n
with entries from
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Let
G
be a closed subgroup of
\mathbf{U}(n,\mathbb{C})
.
The group of integer points of
G
is denoted by
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We set

\displaystyle G({\mathbb{N}}):=\{A\in G_{{\mathbb{Z}}}:\text{all entries of $A$ are from ${\mathbb{N}}$}\}.

Then
G({\mathbb{N}})
is a submonoid of
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
.
We call a finitely generated submonoid
\mathcal{S}\subset G({\mathbb{N}})
, where the set
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
is finite, a

unipotent numerical monoid in
G
.
For example, if
G
is given by the unipotent algebraic group

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&a_{1}&a_{2}&\dots&a_{n-1}\\
0&1&0&\dots&0\\
0&0&1&\dots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\dots&1\end{bmatrix}:\ \{a_{1},\dots,a_{n-1}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\right\}\qquad(n\geq 2),

which we denote by
\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
, then we recover all generalized numerical monoids of
[
14
]
as unipotent numerical monoids.
If we employ semialgebraic topology and consider semialgebraic groups, then we recover the theory of affine
\mathcal{C}
-semigroups, introduced in
[
15
]
, as part of our theory as well.

A tool that we introduce in our paper is a notion of a semigroup of relative ideals.
Here, a

relative right ideal
of a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
is a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}.

A

relative left ideal
is defined similarly.
It is a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathcal{SI}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
Finally, we call a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
that is both a left and right relative ideal a

two-sided relative ideal
of
\mathcal{S}
.
For brevity, we will subsequently refer to a two-sided relative ideal simply as a ‘

relative ideal
.’
Before proceeding to articulate our results, we will state our rationale concerning the definitions of relative right (or relative left) ideals.
We could adopt a more generalized approach, considering subsets
\mathcal{I}\subset G_{\mathbb{Z}}
where
\mathcal{IS}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
holds, along with an additional condition, such as requiring the existence of an element
A\in\mathcal{S}
such that
A\mathcal{I}\subset\mathcal{S}
.
However, using this broader definition complicates our presentation, potentially obscuring the focus of our article.
Instead of pursuing this cumbersome route, which is unnecessary for our purposes, we have opted to work specifically with the subsets of
G({\mathbb{N}})
.

The set of all relative ideals of
\mathcal{S}
, denoted by
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
, forms a monoid under the product
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\mapsto\mathcal{IJ}:=\{AB\mid A\in\mathcal{I}\text{ and }B\in\mathcal{J}\}
, where
\{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\}\subset Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
.
The neutral element of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
is given by
\mathcal{S}
.
In the sequel, we will prove that
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
is closed under finite intersections and finite unions.
Furthermore, our monoid has two important submonoids, denoted
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\supseteq)
and
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
, which are sublattices.
The former submonoid consists of relative ideals of
\mathcal{S}
contained in
\mathcal{S}
.
The latter submonoid, which is finite, consists of relative ideals that contain
\mathcal{S}
.
It is worth mentioning that while we have both left and right-sided versions of these objects, our discussion in the introduction is limited to the two-sided version for the sake of simplicity in our presentation.
Our first main result is the following assertion.

Theorem 1.1
.

An element
\mathcal{T}
of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
is an idempotent if and only if
\mathcal{T}
is a unipotent numerical monoid.
Furthermore, if
\mathcal{S}
is commutative, then the idempotents of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
form a band.

By fixing a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
, we actually fix several interesting partial orders on the group of integer points of
G
.
For
A
and
B
from
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have

1.

A\leq_{\mathcal{S},r}B\iff BA^{-1}\in\mathcal{S}
,

2.

A\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}B\iff A^{-1}B\in\mathcal{S}
,

3.

A\leq_{\mathcal{S},t}B\iff\{A^{-1}B,BA^{-1}\}\subset\mathcal{S}
.

Let us call these partial orders collectively the

\mathcal{S}
-orders
on
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
\leq
is an
\mathcal{S}
-order on
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
, then the pair
(G({\mathbb{N}}),\leq)
is a locally finite poset.

Just as the torsion monoid of a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
has a strong order theoretic structure, the semigroups of ideals of
\mathcal{S}
also have a strong order theoretic property.
We observe that, for a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, the complement

\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
is a lower order ideal of the poset
(\mathcal{S},\leq_{\mathcal{S},l})
if and only if
\mathcal{I}
is a right ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{S}\backslash\mathcal{I}
is a lower order ideal of the poset
(\mathcal{S},\leq_{\mathcal{S},r})
if and only if
\mathcal{I}
is a left ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.

Two fundamental invariants of a numerical semigroup
S
are its Frobenius number and the set of pseudo-Frobenius elements.
For unipotent numerical monoids, there are various sets that serve similar purposes.
However, the Frobenius number is replaced by the Frobenius set.
We proceed to introduce these important gadgets.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
\mathcal{I}
be a subset of
\mathcal{S}
.
The

left Frobenius set of
\mathcal{I}
is defined by

\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{I}):=\{A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{S}\text{ and }A(G({\mathbb{N}})^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{I}\}
.
The

left pseudo-Frobenius set of
\mathcal{I}
is defined by

\mathtt{PF}_{l}(\mathcal{I}):=\{A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{S}\text{ and }AS^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\}
.
The right sided as well as the two-sided versions of these sets are denoted by
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{I}),\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{I}),\mathtt{PF}_{r}(\mathtt{I})
, and
\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{I})
.
The definitions of these sets are similar to those of
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
and
\mathtt{PF}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
.
Notice that the inclusions
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathtt{PF}_{l}(\mathcal{I})
,
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathtt{PF}_{r}(\mathcal{I})
, and
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{I})\subseteq\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{I})
always hold true. However, often these inclusions are strict inclusions.

We call a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}

irreducible
if there are no two unipotent numerical monoids
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, properly containing
\mathcal{S}
such that
\mathcal{T}_{1}\cap\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{S}
. The following theorems indicate that both Frobenius and the pseudo-Frobenius sets are critical notions for investigating the irreducibility.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
If one of the following conditions hold true, then
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible:

(1)

If
|\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
;

(2)

If
|\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
;

(3)

If
|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
.

In Theorem
1.3
, the assumptions
|\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})|=|\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})|=|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
If
\mathcal{S}

|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1

The converse of Theorem

Simple counter examples exist already for the commutative cases.

The set of special gaps of a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
is the subsets

\mathtt{SG}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
consisting of elements
A\in\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
such that
A^{2}\in\mathcal{S}

One of our main results is the following lattice theoretic characterization of the irreducible monoids.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid. Then
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible if and only if the torsion monoid
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
has a unique minimal nontrivial idempotent and the two-sided Frobenius set of
\mathcal{S}
equals the set of special gaps.

A key concept in the study of numerical semigroups is the notion of a symmetric numerical semigroup.
It has various equivalent descriptions, one of which is that for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}\setminus S
, the Frobenius number
|\mathtt{F}(S)|-x
is an element of
S
.
Then it is known that
S
is irreducible.
In our setup, an adaptation of this definition is suggested by Theorem
1.3
.
We call an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid

symmetric
if for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{or}\quad\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset.

Along similar lines, we call an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid

pseudo-symmetric
if the following two conditions are satisfied:

1.

there exists an element
B\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
B^{2}\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
, and

2.

for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus(\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{B\})
we have

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
or
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.

An interesting property of the symmetric unipotent numerical monoids is that their Frobenius sets overlap with their pseudo-Frobenius sets (Proposition
8.4
).
We subsequently show by an example that this property need not hold for every pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoid.

The definitions of symmetric and pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoids allow us to partition the set of all irreducible unipotent numerical monoids.
In this regard, we have the following result.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
If
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible, then
\mathcal{S}
is either symmetric or pseudo-symmetric.

In fact, to establish this theorem, we prove an even stronger result (Theorem
8.6
) within the main body of the text.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a subset of
G({\mathbb{N}})
.
Let
\leqslant
be a partial order on
G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is the unique minimal element with respect to
\leqslant
.
For
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})
and the poset
(\mathcal{X},\leqslant)
, we will use the following notation:

\displaystyle\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{X},A,\leqslant)

\displaystyle:=\{B\in\mathcal{X}\mid\mathbf{1}_{n}\leqslant B\leqslant A\}.

\displaystyle\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{X},A,\leqslant)

\displaystyle:=\{B\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{X}\mid\mathbf{1}_{n}\leqslant B\leqslant A\}.

Note that we always have
\mathtt{n}(G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{X},A,\leqslant)=\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{X},A,\leqslant)
.
Hereafter we use
\preceq
to denote the partial order
\leq_{G({\mathbb{N}}),t}
on
G({\mathbb{N}})

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a symmetric unipotent numerical monoid in
G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\{C\}
for some

If the additional condition
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
holds, then we have

\displaystyle|\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)|=|\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)|=\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S}).

Notation and Preliminaries

The set of positive integers (resp. nonnegative integers) is denoted by
{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
(resp.
{\mathbb{N}}
).
For
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, the
n\times n
identity matrix will be denoted by
\mathbf{1}_{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a monoid.
Let
*
denote the multiplication in
\mathcal{M}
.
Let
\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{M}
.
We denote by
\langle\mathcal{B}\rangle
the submonoid generated by
\mathcal{B}
and the unit
e
of
\mathcal{M}
, that is,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{B}\rangle:=\{a_{1}*a_{2}*\cdots*a_{k}\,:\,\{a_{1},\dots,a_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{B},\ k\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\cup\{e\}.

We call
\mathcal{M}
a

cancellative monoid
if for all
a,b,c\in\mathcal{M}
the following implications hold true:

•

Left cancellation law: if
a*b=a*c
, then
b=c
.

•

Right cancellation law: if
b*a=c*a
, then
b=c
.

In other words, a cancellative monoid is a monoid in which the cancellation property holds for both left and right multiplication.
For example, if
\mathcal{M}
is a submonoid of a group, then
\mathcal{M}
is automatically a cancellative monoid.
All unipotent numerical monoids that we consider in this article are cancellative monoids.

Let
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
Hereafter,
G
will denote either the full unipotent group
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
or a closed subgroup of
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
such that
G({\mathbb{N}})
is finitely generated.
In addition we require that
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
is the group completion of the monoid
G({\mathbb{N}})
.
This is indeed the case for the group
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
since both
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
and
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{Z}})

are generated by the same set of elements, which we will review below.

We observe that when
n=2
, the unipotent numerical monoids in
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
coincide with the numerical semigroups in
{\mathbb{N}}
.
Consequently, our results in this article can be regarded as extensions of some fundamental concepts of numerical semigroups into a noncommutative framework.
It is worth mentioning for motivational purposes that the theory of numerical semigroups is a classical subject in algebra, finding applications in algebraic geometry, coding theory, cryptography, number theory, and combinatorics. Comprehensive introductory material on numerical semigroups can be found in the monographs
[
1
]
and
[
20
]
. The natural extensions of numerical semigroups are termed ‘generalized numerical semigroups.’
These semigroups are finitely generated co-finite submonoids of
{\mathbb{N}}^{n-1}
.
In particular, they are commutative monoids.
For a deeper understanding of finitely generated cancellative commutative monoids, readers are directed to the monograph
[
19
]
.
These resources also offer comprehensive bibliographies, serving as valuable gateways to the commutative semigroup literature.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid in

.
Many important features of
\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}})_{k}:=\{(x_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\in\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}}):\ x_{ij}\in{\mathbb{N}}\ \text{ and }\ k\leq\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}x_{ij}\}\cup\{\mathbf{1}_{n}\},

k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}

The

\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})_{k}:=\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}})_{k}\cap\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}}).

Similarly to the monoid
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
, the commutative monoid
\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
is filtered by the fundamental monoids as well.
For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
M_{k}^{\prime}
denote
\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})_{k+1}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})=M_{0}^{\prime}\supset M_{1}^{\prime}\supset M_{2}^{\prime}\supset\cdots.

(2)

A partial order on
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{Z}})
that we make use of is the

entrywise order
, which is defined as follows:

\displaystyle A\leq_{e}B\iff a_{ij}\leq b_{ij}\quad\text{ for every $1\leq i,j\leq n$},

where
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
and
B=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
are from
G_{\mathbb{Z}}
.

One of our notational conventions regarding partial orders is as follows:
if
(P,\leq)
is a partially ordered set, then
\max_{\leq}(P)
denotes the set of maximal elements of
P
, not the maximum element of
P
.

We close this section by introducing one more notation.
For
\mathcal{I}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
, we denote by
\mathcal{I}^{*}
the set difference

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{*}:=\mathcal{I}\setminus\{\mathbf{1}_{n}\}.

3
Apéry Sets of Unipotent Numerical Monoids

A numerical semigroup is a complement-finite subsemigroup of
{\mathbb{N}}
.
Let
S
be a numerical semigroup.
The subsemigroup of nonzero elements of
S
is typically denoted by
S^{*}
.
Let
n\in S^{*}
.
The Apéry set, denoted by
\mathop{Ap}(n,S)
, is the set of elements
x\in S
such that
x
can not be decomposed as
x=n+s
for any
s\in S
.
Equivalently, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{Ap}(n,S):=\{x\in S:\ x-n\notin S\}.

(3)

These sets provide essential tools for the study of a numerical semigroup.
Our goal in this section is to generalize the Apéry sets to the setting of the unipotent numerical monoids.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
A\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
The

left Apéry set of
(\mathcal{S},A)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A):=\{B\in\mathcal{S}\mid A^{-1}B\notin\mathcal{S}\}.

The

right Apéry set of
(\mathcal{S},A)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathtt{Ap}_{r}(\mathcal{S},A):=\{B\in\mathcal{S}\mid BA^{-1}\notin\mathcal{S}\}.

The

two-sided Apéry set of
(\mathcal{S},A)
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathtt{Ap}_{t}(\mathcal{S},A):=\{B\in\mathcal{S}\mid\{A^{-1}B,BA^{-1}\}\cap\mathcal{S}=\emptyset\}.

Note that we always have

\displaystyle\mathtt{Ap}_{t}(\mathcal{S},A)=\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)\cap\mathtt{Ap}_{r}(\mathcal{S},A).

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
A\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Then we have

1.

L_{l}:=\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)\cup\{A\}
is a generating set for
\mathcal{S}
.

2.

L_{r}:=\mathtt{Ap}_{r}(\mathcal{S},A)\cup\{A\}
is a generating set for
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

The proof of (2) is very similar to the proof of (1).
We will prove (1) only.

Since
A^{-1}
has a negative entry, we see that
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
.
Now let
B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
. If
B\in L_{l}
, then we have nothing to prove.
We proceed with the assumption that
B\in\mathcal{S}\backslash L_{l}
.
Since
B
is not an element of
\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
, we have
A^{-1}B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
If
A^{-1}B
is an element of
\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
, then by writing
B
in the form
B=A(A^{-1}B)
we see that
B\in\langle L_{l}\rangle
.
Let us assume that
A^{-1}B
is not an element of
\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
.
Then we have
A^{-2}B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
If
A^{-2}B\in\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
, then the proof is finished since we can write
B=A^{2}(A^{-2}B)
.
We now assume that
A^{-2}B\notin\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
.
Then we have
A^{-3}B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Continuing inductively, we notice that this process must stop after a finite number of steps since at least one entry of
A^{-r}B
,
r=0,1,2,\dots
continues to decrease indefinitely.
Hence, we conclude that for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
the product
A^{-k}B
is an element of
\mathtt{Ap}_{l}(\mathcal{S},A)
.
But this means that
B=A^{k}\,(A^{-k}B)
, implying
B\in\langle L_{l}\rangle
.
This finishes the proof of our theorem.
∎

After proving the existence of a minimal generating set for
\mathcal{S}
we will show that the two-sided Apéry set is also a generating set.

In our earlier work, we proved that the unipotent numerical monoids have a unique minimal generating set.
Now we will write down a practical description of such a generating set.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid in
G({\mathbb{N}})
, where
G
is a closed subgroup of
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
.
Then the unique minimal generating set of
\mathcal{S}
is given by
\mathcal{S}^{*}\setminus(\mathcal{S}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
k\geq 2
, and define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{k}:=\{(x_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}\in\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}}):\ k\leq\max_{1\leq i,j\leq n}x_{ij}<2k\}.

We know from
[
5
, Lemma 2.9]
that
\mathcal{Q}_{k}\sqcup\{\textrm{sporadic elements}\}
, where
k
is the generating number of
\mathcal{S}
, is a generating set for
\mathcal{S}
despite the fact that it might be non-minimal.
Let
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{S}^{*}\setminus(\mathcal{S}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*})
and
\mathcal{B}
be another minimal generating set.
Let
B\in\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{A}
.
Then
B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Therefore, we have two elements
A,C\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
such that
B=AC
.
Since
A
and
C
are contained in
\langle\mathcal{B}\rangle
, we see that
\mathcal{B}\setminus\{B\}
is a generating set, contradicting the minimality of
\mathcal{B}
.
In conclusion, we have
\mathcal{B}\setminus\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
.
In other words,
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
holds.

For the reverse inclusion, let
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then we write
A
as a product of the elements of
\mathcal{B}
as follows:
A=\prod_{i}B_{i}^{n_{i}}
where
B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
for every
i
.
Since
A\in\mathcal{A}
, we have a unique index
i
such that
n_{i}=1
, and
n_{j}=0
for every
j\neq i
.
Hence,
A=B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
implying that
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
.
This finishes the proof of our theorem.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
A\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then
L_{t}:=\{A\}\cup\mathtt{Ap}_{t}(\mathcal{S},A)
is a generating set for
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

We already know that
L_{l}:=\mathtt{Ap}_{t}(\mathcal{S},A)\cup\{A\}
and
L_{r}:=\mathtt{Ap}_{t}(\mathcal{S},A)\{A\}
are generating sets for
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{S}^{*}\setminus(\mathcal{S}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*})
denote the unique minimal generating set of
\mathcal{S}
.
Then
\mathcal{E}
is a subset of both
L_{l}
and
L_{r}
.
Otherwise, for
A\in\mathcal{E}\setminus L_{l}
, the element
A
does not belong to the subset
\langle L_{l}\rangle
, which is absurd.
But it is evident from the definitions that
L_{t}=L_{l}\cap L_{r}
.
Hence,
L_{t}
contains
\mathcal{E}
.
This finishes the proof of our assertion.
∎

4
Semigroups of Relative Ideals

Throughout this section, we assume that
\mathcal{S}
is a unipotent numerical monoid of a unipotent algebraic subgroup
G\subset\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
.
The cases we have in mind are
G=\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
or
G=\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
.
The following notational conventions will be used throughout the paper.

Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be two subsets of
\mathcal{S}
.
The set
\{XY\mid X\in\mathcal{I},\ Y\in\mathcal{J}\}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{IJ}
.
We now proceed to define the notion of a relative ideal.

Recall that a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
is called a

relative right ideal
of
\mathcal{S}
if the inclusion
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
holds.

Example 4.1
.

Both
\mathcal{S}
and its maximal subsemigroup
\mathcal{S}^{*}
are automatically right relative ideals of
\mathcal{S}
.

We denote the set of all relative right ideals by
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
.
Now, if
T
is a semigroup, an element
e\in T
is said to be a

right identity element
of
T
if
xe=x
holds for all
x\in T
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The product
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\mapsto\mathcal{IJ}
endows
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
with a structure of a semigroup.
Furthermore, the relative right ideal
\mathcal{S}
is a right identity element of
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proof.

First of all, if
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\in Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})\times Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}:=\{XY\mid X\in\mathcal{I},\ Y\in\mathcal{J}\}

is a relative right ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.
This follows from the following simple observation:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J})\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{I}(\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}.

The associativity of the product
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\mapsto\mathcal{IJ}
follows from the associativity of the matrix multiplication.
We already pointed out in Example
4.1
that
\mathcal{S}\in Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
.
Since for every relative right ideal
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{S}
we have
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, and since
\mathcal{S}
contains the identity matrix
\mathbf{1}_{n}
, we see that
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{I}
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{S}
is a right identity element of
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
.
This finishes the proof of our proposition.
∎

Recall that a

relative left ideal of
\mathcal{S}
is a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathcal{S}\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
A subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is said to be a

relative (two-sided) ideal of
\mathcal{S}
if it is both a relative right ideal and a relative left ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.
A

right ideal
(resp.

left ideal
, resp.

ideal
) of
\mathcal{S}
is a relative right (resp. left, resp. two-sided) ideal
\mathcal{I}\subset G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.
We call a right (resp. left, resp. two-sided) ideal of
\mathcal{S}

proper
if
\mathcal{I}\neq\mathcal{S}
.

Example 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid in
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
.
Recall that
\mathtt{r}(\mathcal{S})
denotes the generating number of
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
k
be an integer such that
k\geq\mathtt{r}(\mathcal{S})
.
Then the
k
-th fundamental monoid
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{N}})_{k}
is an ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.

Similarly to how the semigroup
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
is constructed, we can analogously construct semigroups corresponding to relative left ideals and relative ideals.
These semigroups are denoted as
Rel_{L}(\mathcal{S})
and
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
, respectively.

Theorem 4.4
.

The set
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
is a monoid with the identity element
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

The semigroup property of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
is evident.
It is also evident that the relative ideal
\mathcal{S}
serves as both the right identity and the left identity element for the product
(\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J})\mapsto\mathcal{IJ}
, where
\{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\}\subset Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
.
∎

We have two remarks in order.

Remark 4.5
.

There are no nontrivial invertible elements in
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{I}
is an invertible element of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
such that
\mathcal{I}\neq\mathcal{S}
, then there exists
\mathcal{J}\in Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
such that
\mathcal{IJ}=\mathcal{S}
.
This means that both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
contains
\mathbf{1}_{n}
.
It follows that 1)
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, and 2)
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{IJ}=\mathcal{S}
.
Then we find that
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{S}
, which is absurd.

Remark 4.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a commutative unipotent numerical monoid. Then we have

\displaystyle Rel_{L}(S)=Rel_{R}(S)=Rel_{T}(S).

The elements
\mathcal{I}\in Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
such that
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
form a finite submonoid of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are two relative ideals containing
\mathbf{1}_{n}
, then
\mathbf{1}_{n}
is still an element of
\mathcal{IJ}
.
Furthermore, in this case we have
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{IJ}
.
Finally, if a relative ideal
\mathcal{I}
contains
\mathbf{1}_{n}
, then it contains the whole unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
.
It follows that there are only finitely many relative ideals
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{S}
such that
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
The

torsion monoid of
\mathcal{S}
is the finite submonoid

\displaystyle Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq):=\{\mathcal{I}\in Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})\mid\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}\}\subsetneq Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S}).

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be an element of
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
.
Then
\mathcal{I}
is a unipotent numerical monoid if and only if
\mathcal{I}
is an idempotent.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{I}^{2}=\mathcal{I}
holds, then
\mathcal{I}
is closed under multiplication.
Since
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
,
\mathcal{I}
is a completement finite submonoid of
G({\mathbb{N}})
.

Conversely, if
\mathcal{I}
is a unipotent numerical monoid, then
\mathcal{I}
is closed under products, that is,
\mathcal{I}^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.
In particular, since
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
, we see that
\mathcal{I}^{2}=\mathcal{I}
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid. Then the semigroups
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
,
Rel_{L}(\mathcal{S})
, and
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S})
are closed under finite intersections and finite unions.

Proof.

We will prove our theorem for
Rel_{R}(\mathcal{S})
only since the proofs of the other two cases are similar.

Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be two relative right ideals of
\mathcal{S}
.
Then we have

1.

(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{J})\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S})\cup(\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S})\subseteq(\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{J})
, and

2.

(\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J})\mathcal{S}\subseteq(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{S})\cap(\mathcal{J}\mathcal{S})\subseteq(\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J})
.

Hence, the proof of our theorem follows.
∎

Notation 4.10
.

Hereafter, the set of all idempotents of a semigroup
X
will be denoted by
E(X)
.
The neutral element of
X
will be referred to as the

trivial idempotent
of
X
.

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Then
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
is closed under unions and intersections.
In particular, the set of idempotents
E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
is a lattice.

Proof.

The fact that the monoid
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
is closed under finite unions and finite intersections is clear from Theorem
4.9
.
Since
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
is finite and contains
G({\mathbb{N}})
as the unique maximal element, to show that
E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
is a lattice, it suffices to show that

E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
is closed under intersections.
Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be two idempotents from
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
.
To this end, we observe that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J})^{2}\subseteq\mathcal{I}^{2}\cap\mathcal{J}^{2}=\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}.

The reverse inclusion automatically holds since
\mathbf{1}_{n}\in\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}
.
Hence, the intersection
\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}
is an idempotent as well.
This finishes the proof of our corollary.
∎

In general,
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
and its set of idempotents
E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
are not equal.

.

Let

.

An idempotent semigroup is a semigroup whose elements are idempotents.
Such semigroups are often referred to as

bands
.

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
If
\mathcal{S}
is commutative, then
E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
is a commutative band.

Proof.

It suffices to show that if
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
are two idempotent ideals from
Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq)
,

(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J})^{2}=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}

\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{I}

\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{J}\mathcal{I}
.

(\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J})^{2}=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{J}=\mathcal{I}\mathcal{J}.

This finishes the proof.

Remark 4.14
.

In the proof of Proposition

An element
\mathcal{T}

Furthermore, if
\mathcal{S}
is commutative, then the idempotents of

Proof of Theorem

.

The proof follows readily from Lemma

.
∎

Recall that a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
is called irreducible
if it cannot be written as an intersection of the form
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\cap\mathcal{T}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{i}
(

) is a unipotent numerical monoid properly containing
\mathcal{S}
. The torsion monoid of
\mathcal{S}
captures this property in a lattice theoretic way.
We will discuss this connection in a greater detail after we introduce the pseudo-Frobenius sets.

5
Generating Sets and Order Ideals

In this section, we discuss the generating sets of relative ideals in relation with the partial orders that we introduced in the introduction section.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a relative right ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.
A subset
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
is called

a set of generators for
\mathcal{I}
if
every element
D\in\mathcal{I}
can be written in the form
D=AB
for some
A\in\mathcal{E}
and
B\in\mathcal{S}
.
In other words, a subset
\mathcal{E}\subset\mathcal{I}
is a set of generators for
\mathcal{I}
if
\mathcal{I}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}\mathcal{S}:=\{AB\mid A\in\mathcal{E},\ B\in\mathcal{S}\}=\mathcal{I}.

A relative right ideal is said to be

finitely generated
if it has a finite set of generators.

Lemma 5.1
.

The set
\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
is the unique minimal generating set for
\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote
\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
The fact that
\mathcal{F}
must be a part of any generating set for
\mathcal{I}
does not require a proof.

Let
X_{0}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Then
X_{0}=X_{1}A_{1}
for some
X_{1}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
and
A_{1}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Assume that
X_{1}\notin\mathcal{F}
.
Then, there exists
X_{2}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
and
A_{2}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
such that
X_{1}=X_{2}A_{2}
.
Hence, we have
X_{0}=X_{2}A_{2}A_{1}
.
We proceed inductively to construct decompositions,

\displaystyle X_{0}

\displaystyle=X_{1}A_{1}

\displaystyle=X_{2}A_{2}A_{1}

\displaystyle=X_{m}A_{m}A_{m-1}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}

where
A_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
and
X_{i}\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
for
i=1,2,\dots
.
But since all matrices involved in these decompositions are from
G({\mathbb{N}})
, in each decomposition, the entries of the matrices get smaller.
Hence, this process terminates after finitely many iterations.
At the end, we arrive at a decomposition

\displaystyle X_{0}=X_{r}(A_{r}A_{r-1}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}),

where
X_{r}\in\mathcal{I}\setminus(\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*})
and
A_{r}A_{r-1}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
It follows that the set
\mathcal{I}\setminus(\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*})
is a minimal generating set for the relative right ideal
\mathcal{I}
.
∎

The generating set of a relative right ideal has a convenient interpretation via the partial orders that we described in the introduction.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a relative right ideal.
Then the unique minimal generating set of
\mathcal{I}
is given by the set of minimal elements of
\mathcal{I}^{*}
with respect to

\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}
, denoted
{\rm Minimals}_{\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}}\mathcal{I}^{*}
.

Proof.

Since every element
B\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
is greater than or equal to some minimal element
A\in{\rm Minimals}_{\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}}\mathcal{I}^{*}
, we have
B\in A\mathcal{S}
.
But this means that
{\rm Minimals}_{\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}}\mathcal{I}^{*}
generates
\mathcal{I}
.
Hence, the unique minimal generating set
\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
is contained in
{\rm Minimals}_{\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}}\mathcal{I}^{*}
.
Conversely, suppose
A
is an element of the minimum under
\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}
of
\mathcal{I}^{*}
, then
A
cannot be contained in
\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Otherwise, this would contradict the minimality of
A
.
Therefore,
A
is an element of
\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{I}^{*}\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
This finishes the proof of our assertion.
∎

In the rest of this section we establish an important combinatorial characterization of the ideals of a unipotent numerical monoid.
We continue with the discussion of a general order theoretic property.
Let
G
denote either
\mathbf{U}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
or
\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{C}})
.
We recall the statement of Theorem
1.2
from the introduction:

Let
\leq
denote one of the following partial orders:
\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}
,
\leq_{\mathcal{S},r}
, or
\leq_{\mathcal{S},t}
.
Then
(G({\mathbb{N}}),\leq)
is a locally finite poset.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Without loss of generality we will assume that
\leq
is the order
\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}
.
The proofs of the other cases are similar.

Let
[A,B]
be an interval in
(G({\mathbb{N}}),\leq)
.
Then for every
C\in[A,B]
we have
A\leq C
and
C\leq B
.
Equivalently, for every
C\in[A,B]
we have
A^{-1}C\in\mathcal{S}
and
C^{-1}B\in\mathcal{S}
.
Also, equivalently, for every
C\in[A,B]
we have
C\in A\mathcal{S}
and
B\in C\mathcal{S}
.
It follows that if
A,B
, and
C
are given by
A=(a_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
,
B=(b_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
, and

C=(c_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
, then
a_{ij}\leq c_{ij}\leq b_{ij}
for every
1\leq i,j\leq n
.
Now, let us assume towards a contradiction that the interval
[A,B]
has infinitely many elements.
Then there are infinitely many matrices
C(l):=(c_{ij}(l))_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
,
l=1,2,\dots
in
[A,B]
such that
C(l)\in A\mathcal{S}
and
B\in C(l)\mathcal{S}
.
But then there exists a matrix coordinate
(i,j)
, where
1\leq i<j\leq n
, such that
c_{ij}(l)\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
.
This means that
b_{ij}\to\infty
as
l\to\infty
.
Clearly, this is impossible since the entries of
B
are fixed integers.
Therefore,
[A,B]
has only finitely many elements.
∎

We proceed to characterize the ideals of
\mathcal{S}
by using
\mathcal{S}
-orders.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
(P,\leq)
be a poset.
A subset
L\subseteq P
is called a

lower order ideal
of
P
if for every
y\in L
and
x\in P
, the relation
x\leq y
implies that
x\in L
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a subset of
\mathcal{S}
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
is a lower order ideal of the poset
(\mathcal{S},\leq_{\mathcal{S},l})
.

(2)

\mathcal{I}
is a right ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
is a lower order ideal of
(\mathcal{S},\leq_{\mathcal{S},l})
.
This means that if
B\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
and
A\in\mathcal{S}
are two elements such that
A\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}B
, then
A
is an element of
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
as well.
Let
X\in\mathcal{I}
and
Y\in\mathcal{S}
.
We assume towards a contradiction that
XY\notin\mathcal{I}
.
Then
XY\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
as
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.
Since
Y\in\mathcal{S}
, the relation
X\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}XY
holds.
Then the lower order ideal property of
\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
implies that
X\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
, contradicting our initial assumption that
X\in\mathcal{I}
.
This shows that (1) implies (2).

Conversely, we assume that
\mathcal{I}
is a right ideal of
\mathcal{S}
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{S}
and
B\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
be two elements such that
A\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}B
.
We will show that
A\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
.
Now,
A\leq_{\mathcal{S},l}B
implies that
A^{-1}B\in\mathcal{S}
or that
B\in A\mathcal{S}
.
Of course, if
A
is an element of
\mathcal{I}
, then
A\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{I}
implies that
B\in\mathcal{I}
, contradicting our initial assumption that
B\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
.
Therefore, we have
A\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{I}
as claimed.
This shows that (2) implies (1).

Hence, the proof of our theorem is complete.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

The proof of our theorem extends naturally to left ideals and two-sided ideals of
\mathcal{S}
.
However, for brevity, we omit the statements and proofs here.

6
Frobenius Sets

As we mentioned in the introduction, a noncommutative unipotent numerical monoid has several Frobenius sets.
Recall that the Frobenius sets of a subset
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a unipotent numerical monoid, are defined by

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{I}^{*}\text{ and }A(G({\mathbb{N}})^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{I}\},

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{I}^{*}\text{ and }(G({\mathbb{N}})^{*})A\subseteq\mathcal{I}\},

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{I}^{*}\text{ and }A(G({\mathbb{N}})^{*})\cup(G({\mathbb{N}})^{*})A\subseteq\mathcal{I}\}.

In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{I})=\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{I})\cap\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{I}).

Notice that the Frobenius sets are always non-empty since every maximal element with respect to the entrywise order in
G(\mathbb{N})\backslash\mathcal{S}^{*}
is an element of the Frobenius set.
We proceed with another remark.

Remark 6.1
.

If
A
and
B
are two elements from
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
, then there is no
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
A=BC
.
Indeed, if we assume the existence of such an element
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
, then we find that
BC=A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
,
which is absurd since
B\in\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
.
Likewise, it is easy to see that for
\{A,B\}\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})
(resp.
\{A,B\}\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
)
there is no
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
A=CB
(resp. there is no
\{C,D\}\subset G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
s.t.
A=BC=DB
).

Example 6.2
.

We will show in this example that, in general, the left and the right Frobenius sets do not agree.
Let
\mathcal{S}
be the unipotent numerical monoid in
G({\mathbb{N}}):=\mathbf{U}(3,{\mathbb{N}})
defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\mathtt{N}(\mathcal{S})\sqcup\mathbf{U}(2,\mathbb{N})_{3},

where
\mathtt{N}(\mathcal{S})
consists of matrices

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&a&b\\
0&1&c\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbf{U}(3,{\mathbb{N}}),

where
(a,b,c)
is an element of the set

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{matrix}(0,0,0),&(1,1,1),&(1,2,1)&(1,2,2)\\
(2,0,1),&(2,0,2),&(2,1,1)&\end{matrix}\right\}.

It is straightforward to check that the right Frobenius set of
\mathcal{S}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\{A\in\mathbf{U}(3,{\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{S}\text{ and }\mathbf{U}(3,{\mathbb{N}})^{*}A\subset\mathcal{S}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&0&2\\
0&1&2\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\
0&1&2\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

The left Frobenius set of
\mathcal{S}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\{A\in\mathbf{U}(3,{\mathbb{N}})\mid A\notin\mathcal{S}\text{ and }A\mathbf{U}(3,{\mathbb{N}})^{*}\subset\mathcal{S}\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\
0&1&1\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\
0&1&2\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\right\}.

Clearly, we have
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})\neq\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
\mathtt{F}
denote one of
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
,
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})
, or
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Then the union
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
is a unipotent numerical monoid.
Furthermore, if
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
, then
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{C}
is a unipotent numerical monoid for any subset
\mathtt{C}\subseteq\mathtt{F}
.

Proof.

We will prove our claim for
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
only.
The proofs of the other two cases are similar.

Let
A
and
B
be two elements from
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
.
We will show that
AB\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
.
We have essentially four cases to consider:

1.

Both
A
and
B
are from
\mathcal{S}
. In this case, we always have
AB\in\mathcal{S}
.

2.

A\in\mathtt{F}
and
B\in\mathcal{S}
. In this case, since
A\in\mathtt{F}
, we have
AB\in\mathcal{S}
.

3.

A\in\mathcal{S}
and
B\in\mathtt{F}
. In this case, we will show below that
AB\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
.

4.

Both
A
and
B
are from
\mathtt{F}
. In this case, we will show below that
AB\in\mathcal{S}
.

Let us continue with the third case.
For every
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
we have
BC\in\mathcal{S}
.
At the same time, since
A\in\mathcal{S}
, the membership
BC\in\mathcal{S}
implies that
(AB)C=A(BC)\in\mathcal{S}
.
In other words, we have
AB\in\mathtt{F}
or
AB\in\mathcal{S}
.
Finally, let us handle the fourth case.
Let
\{A,B\}\subseteq\mathtt{F}
.
Then the assumption
A\in\mathtt{F}
implies that
AB\in\mathcal{S}
.
In conclusion, we see that if
\{A,B\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
, then
AB\in\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
.
For the last assertion, if
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
, then for every
A\in\mathcal{S}
and
B,B^{\prime}\in\mathtt{F}
, we have
\{AB,BA,BB^{\prime}\}\subset\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}
. Hence,
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{C}
is a unipotent numerical monoid for every subset
\mathtt{C}\subseteq\mathtt{F}
.
This finishes the proof of our proposition.
∎

Frobenius sets are quite useful for characterizing irreducibility.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
\mathtt{F}(\cdot)
denote one of
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\cdot)
,
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\cdot)
, or
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\cdot)
. Suppose
|\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})|=1
.
Then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{S}
is irreducible.

(2)

\mathcal{S}
is maximal with respect to set inclusion in the set of unipotent numerical monoids
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

We will prove our theorem for
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{l}
only. The other cases are proven similarly.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). Let
\mathcal{T}
be a unipotent numerical monoid of
G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})=\emptyset
, evidently,
\mathcal{S}
is given by the intersection
\mathcal{T}\cap(\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S}))
.
By Proposition
6.3
, the set
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})
is a unipotent numerical monoid.
Since
\mathcal{S}\cap\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})=\emptyset
, the irreducibility of
\mathcal{S}
implies that
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{S}
.
This finishes the proof of our assertion on the maximality of
\mathcal{S}
.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1). Let us assume that
\mathcal{S}
can be written as the intersection
\mathcal{T}_{1}\cap\mathcal{T}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{i}
(
i\in\{1,2\}
) is a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})=\{C\}
.
Since
CG({\mathbb{N}})^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\cap\mathcal{T}_{2}
, one of the following holds:

1.

C\in\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T}_{1})\cap\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
, or

2.

either
C\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
or
C\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
, but not both.

If
C\in\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T}_{1})\cap\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T}_{2})
holds, then
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{T}_{i}=\emptyset
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is maximal with respect to this property, we find that
\mathcal{T}_{i}=\mathcal{S}
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
.
In other words,
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible.
Now, if
C\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
then
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{T}_{2}=\emptyset
, which by hypothesis implies
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
We can apply a similar argument if
C\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
holds.
Hence, we see that
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible.
This finishes the proof of our theorem.
∎

Given the additional assumption
|\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})|=1
, we obtain a closely related characterization of irreducible unipotent numerical monoids.
In the next section, we will show, following the introduction of the ‘set of special gaps’ of a unipotent numerical monoid, that if
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible, then
|\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})|=1
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Let
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\in\{\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S}),\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S}),\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\}
.
If
|\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})|=1
holds, then the following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{S}
is irreducible.

(2)

\mathcal{S}
is maximal with respect to set inclusion in the set of unipotent numerical monoids
\mathcal{T}
such that

\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})=\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})
.

(3)

\mathcal{S}
is maximal with respect to set inclusion in the set of unipotent numerical monoids
\mathcal{T}
such that

\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

First, we prove our theorem for
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{l}
.
The proof of the case of
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{r}
is very similar, so, we omit it.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2). Let
\mathcal{T}
be a unipotent numerical monoid of
G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})=\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})
. Let us assume that
\mathcal{T}\supseteq\mathcal{S}
.
Since
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})=\emptyset
, evidently,
\mathcal{S}
is given by the intersection
\mathcal{T}\cap(\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S}))
.
By Proposition
6.3
, the set
\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})
is a unipotent numerical monoid.
But then the irreducibility of
\mathcal{S}
implies that
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{S}
.
This shows that
\mathcal{S}
is maximal.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(3). Let us assume that
\mathcal{S}
is contained in a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
. We will show that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
.

First, let us assume that there exists
A\in\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})
.
This means that
AG({\mathbb{N}})^{*}\subset\mathcal{S}
but there exists
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
AC\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{T}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is a subset of
\mathcal{T}
and since
AC
is an element of
\mathcal{S}
, the last statement is absurd.
In other words, we have
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})=\emptyset
, implying that
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})
.

Next, let us assume that
A\in\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})\setminus\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})
.
This means that
AG({\mathbb{N}})^{*}\subset\mathcal{T}
but there exists
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
AC\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
If for some
E\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
the element
ACE
is still not in
\mathcal{S}
, then we replace
C
by
CE
.
Then we repeat this procedure continually.
Since
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
is a finite set, our procedure will come to an end after a finite number of replacements.
The resulting element, which we still denote by
AC
has the property that
AC\notin\mathcal{S}
but
ACG(\mathbb{N})^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.
Said differently, we found an element
AC
of
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})
.
But we already know that
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})
.
By Remark
6.1
(2), this is absurd since the elements of
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})
can not be obtained from each other by multiplying by an element from
G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
.
Hence, we conclude that
\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}(\mathcal{T})
.
Now, our assumption on the maximality of
\mathcal{S}
implies that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
.
This finishes the proof of (2)
\Rightarrow
(3).

(3)
\Rightarrow
(1). We already know this implication from Theorem
6.4
.
Hence, the proof of our theorem is complete for
\mathtt{F}\in\{\mathtt{F}_{l},\mathtt{F}_{r}\}
.

We now proceed under the assumption that
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{t}
.
The proofs of the implications (1)
\Rightarrow
(2) and (3)
\Rightarrow
(1) remain valid in this case.
We are left with proving the assertion (2)
\Rightarrow
(3).
This part of the proof will be a subtle adaptation of the arguments used for the similar implication in the case of
\mathtt{F}=\mathtt{F}_{l}
.
To distinguish the Frobenius sets here, we will employ subscripts.

Let us assume that
\mathcal{S}
is contained in a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{T}
such that
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
.
We will show that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
.

First, let us assume that there exists
A\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{T})
.
Since
A\notin\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{T})
we know that either
A\notin\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{T})
or
A\notin\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{T})
.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
A\notin\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{T})
.
This means that there exists
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
AC\notin\mathcal{T}
.
Now, since
A\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\subset\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
, we know that
A
is an element of
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}^{*}
such that
AG({\mathbb{N}})^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
. In particular, we know that
AC\in\mathcal{S}
.
But this is absurd since we assumed that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
.
In conclusion, we have
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{T})
.
We emphasize our previous observation that the inclusion
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{T})
holds as well.
This reiteration will be useful in the next paragraph.

Next, let us assume that
A\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{T})\setminus\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
By using the argument as in the previous paragraph, we may assume without loss of generality that
A\in\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{T})\setminus\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
.
This means that 1)
AG({\mathbb{N}})^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{T}
, and 2) there exists
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
AC\notin\mathcal{S}
.
If for some
E\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
the element
ACE
is still not in
\mathcal{S}
, then we replace
C
by
CE
.
Then we repeat this procedure continually.
Since
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
is a finite set, our procedure will come to an end after a finite number of replacements.
The resulting element, which we still denote by
AC
has the property that
AC\notin\mathcal{S}
but
ACG(\mathbb{N})^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
.
Said differently, we found an element
AC
of
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
.
But we already know that
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{T})
.
By Remark
6.1
(2), this is absurd since the elements of
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{T})
can not be obtained from each other by multiplying by an element from
G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
.
Hence, we conclude that there is no such
A
.
In other words, we obtained the equality
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{T})
.
Now, our assumption on the maximality of
\mathcal{S}
implies that
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{T}
.
This finishes the proof of (2)
\Rightarrow
(3).
∎

We now present a sufficient condition for the irreducibility of a unipotent numerical monoid that will be useful when we investigate the symmetric and pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoids.
We note that this theorem was stated as Theorem
1.3
in the introduction.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
If one of the following conditions hold true, then
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible:

(1)

|\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have either
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
or

\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
;

(2)

|\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have either
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
or

\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
;

(3)

|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have either
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
or

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We will prove (1) only. The other assertions are proven similarly.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid such that
|\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})|=1
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}

we have either
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
or
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.
We assume towards a contradiction that
\mathcal{S}
is reducible.
Then, by Theorem
6.4
, there exists a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{T}
, properly containing
\mathcal{S}
, such that
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})=\emptyset
.
Let
A\in\mathcal{T}\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Then, by the hypothesis of our theorem, we have either
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
or

\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.
In other words, there exists
B\in\mathcal{S}
such that either
AB\in\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
or
BA\in\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
holds.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is a subset of
\mathcal{T}
we see that both
A
and
B
are from
\mathcal{T}
.
It follows that either
AB\in\mathcal{T}
or
BA\in\mathcal{T}
holds.
But this contradicts our assumption that
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})=\emptyset
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{S}
is an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid.
∎

.

.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let

. Then

if and only if

is a unipotent numerical monoid.

.

Conversely, if

.

(Theorem

)

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{SG}(\mathcal{S})

Proof.

We begin with the proof of

It follows from definitions that
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})

we have

.
In other words, every element of
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
is an element of
\mathtt{SG}(\mathcal{S})
.
For the reverse inclusion, for

, by Lemma

\mathcal{S}\cup\{D\}
is a unipotent numerical monoid such that

.
Then we have
\mathcal{S}=(\mathcal{S}\cup\{D\})\cap(\mathcal{S}\cup\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S}))
, which contradicts with the irreducibility of
\mathcal{S}
.

We proceed to show that
|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1
.
If
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
contains two distinct elements then we find two distinct unipotent numerical monoids

\mathcal{T}_{2}:=\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{A_{2}\}

We conclude this section by relating the irreducibility of
\mathcal{S}
to its torsion monoid and the set of special gaps.
We will prove our Theorem

from the introduction, which states the following:

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
Then
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible if and only if the following two conditions hold:

(1)

the torsion monoid

has a unique minimal nontrivial idempotent, and

(2)

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{SG}(\mathcal{S})

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Let
\mathcal{S}
be an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid.
By Theorem

, we know that
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{SG}(\mathcal{S})
.
We proceed to show (1).
Let
E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
denote the set of all idempotents of

We now assume that
\mathcal{S}
is an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid.
Towards a contradiction, let us assume that
E(Rel_{T}(\mathcal{S},\subseteq))
has two nontrivial distinct minimal idempotents, denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{2}
.
Since we have
\mathcal{T}_{2}\not\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}_{1}\not\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{2}

A_{1}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{2}

A_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{2}\setminus\mathcal{T}_{1}

A_{1}\notin A_{2}\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}A_{2}

A_{2}\notin A_{1}\mathcal{S}\cup\mathcal{S}A_{1}

For
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})

\mathcal{S}\cup A

Observe that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\cup\{A\}\subseteq\langle\mathcal{S},A\rangle

\langle\mathcal{S},A\rangle

\langle\mathcal{S},A\rangle

\langle\mathcal{S},A_{1}\rangle=\mathcal{T}_{1}

\mathcal{S}

In the rest of this section we discuss the refinements of the set of irreducible unipotent numerical monoids.
We begin with recalling the definitions of symmetric and pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoids from the introduction:

•

An irreducible unipotent numerical monoid is called

symmetric
if for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset\quad\text{or}\quad\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset.

•

An irreducible unipotent numerical monoid is called

pseudo-symmetric
if there exists
B\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
B^{2}\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
and for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus(\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{B\})
we have

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
or
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.

Remark 8.3
.

If
\mathcal{S}
is symmetric, then there is no
B\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
B^{2}\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Otherwise, it follows from the defining condition that we have either
B^{2}\in\{BD,DB\}
for some
D\in\mathcal{S}
.
This is absurd since
\mathcal{S}
is a cancellative monoid.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a symmetric unipotent numerical monoid. Then we have
|\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1
.

Proof.

Let
A\in\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Then
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
and
(\mathcal{S}^{*}A\cup A\mathcal{S}^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Since
\mathcal{S}
is symmetric, we have either
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\neq\emptyset
or
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.
Hence, unless
A\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
, we have
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}\neq\emptyset
.
But (the left, the right, or the two-sided) Frobenius subsets of
\mathcal{S}
are always disjoint from
\mathcal{S}
.
This contradiction shows that the membership
A\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
holds true.
It follows that
\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
is a subset of
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Since the converse inclusion holds automatically, we find that
\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Finally, since
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible, by Theorem
1.5
, we have
|\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})|=1
.
This finishes the proof of our proposition.
∎

In an earlier version of our paper, we had used more stringent definitions for symmetric and pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoids.
An anonymous referee suggested that these earlier definitions be referred to by a different name.
In light of the referee’s insightful suggestion, we now introduce the “strong” versions of symmetric and pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoids.

Definition 8.5
.

We call an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid

strongly symmetric
if for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
we have

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.
We call an irreducible unipotent numerical monoid

strongly pseudo-symmetric
if the following two conditions are satisfied:

1.

there exists an element
B\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
B^{2}\in\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
, and

2.

for every
A\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus(\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{B\})
we have

\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}A\neq\emptyset
.

B
with respect to
\leq_{e}
.

We now proceed with the assumption that
Z\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Note that
Z^{2}\neq C
. Otherwise we would have
ABEABE=ABAB
, implying
EABE=AB
, which is absurd.
Notice also that
C\notin Z\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}Z
.
Otherwise, we find that
C=ZL=ABEL
.
Then, by the cancellative property of
\mathcal{S}
, we have
AB=EL\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, which contradicts with our initial assumption that
AB\notin\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Hence,
C\notin Z\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}Z
.
Now all of these observations show that
Z\in\mathcal{D}
.
But, once again, this leads to a contradiction with the maximality of
B
with respect to
\leq_{e}
.
Hence, (1) is not possible also.

(d)

We assume that
A=B=H
.
We will show that
H^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Towards a contradiction, we assume that
H^{2}\notin\mathcal{S}
.
Parts (b) and (c) show that for every
A\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
we have
\{AH,HA\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
Hence, it follows from the definition of the two-sided pseudo-Frobenius sets that
H\in\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
At the same time, since
H
is a maximal element, we know that
H^{2}\notin\mathcal{D}
.
This means that either
H^{4}=C
or
C\in H^{2}\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H^{2}
.
If
H^{4}=C
holds, then we claim that
H^{3}\in\mathcal{D}
has to hold.
Let us assume that
H^{3}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Then
C=H^{4}
is an element of
H\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H
, which is absurd.
Hence, we proceed with the assumption that
H^{3}\in\mathtt{G}(\mathbb{N})\backslash\mathcal{S}
.
Now, if
H^{3}
is not an element of
\mathcal{D}
, then we must have that
C=H^{4}\in H^{3}\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H^{3}
.
Then it follows that
H\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
, which is a contradiction.
Hence, we proved our claim that
H^{3}
is contained in
\mathcal{D}
if
C=H^{4}
is assumed.
But this contradicts with the maximality of
H
in
\mathcal{D}\setminus\{C\}
.
Therefore, the remaining possibility, that is,
C\in H^{2}\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H^{2}
must be true.
In this case, let
A,B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
be two elements such that
C=AH^{2}=H^{2}B
.
By parts (b) and (c), we have
AH,HB\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
.
It follows that
C\in H\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H
, a contradiction.
In conclusion, we have
H^{2}\in\mathcal{S}
.

Hence, we showed that
\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{H\}
is a unipotent numerical monoid.
We now have two unipotent numerical monoids
\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{C\}
and
\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{H\}
, properly containing
\mathcal{S}
, such that we have

(\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{C\})\cap(\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{H\})=\mathcal{S}.

This contradicts with our hypothesis that
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible.
We proceed with the assumption that
\mathcal{D}=\{C\}
.
It follows from the definition of
\mathcal{D}
that for every
H\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}\sqcup\{C\}
such that
H^{2}\neq C
we have
C\in H\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H
.
Equivalently, for every such
H
, we have
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap H\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}H\neq\emptyset
.
Therefore, we have
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap H\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{S}H\neq\emptyset
for every
H\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
H^{2}\neq C
.
Now, on one hand, if there is no
H
in
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
such that
H^{2}=C
, then we readily see that
\mathcal{S}
is a symmetric unipotent numerical monoid.
On the other hand, if there is such an
H
in
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
, then we readily see that
\mathcal{S}
together with
B:=H
satisfy the conditions for
\mathcal{S}
being a pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoid.
Hence, we finished the proof of our theorem.
∎

We are now ready to prove our Theorem
1.7
which states the following:

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a unipotent numerical monoid.
If
\mathcal{S}
is irreducible, then
\mathcal{S}
is either symmetric or pseudo-symmetric.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

It is easy to check that every strongly symmetric unipotent numerical monoid is a symmetric unipotent numerical monoid.
Similarly, every strongly pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoid is a pseudo-symmetric unipotent numerical monoid.
The rest of the proof follows from Theorem
8.6
.
∎

The strong symmetry property imposes severe restrictions on the left and the right Frobenius sets.

Corollary 8.7
.

If
\mathcal{S}
is a strongly symmetric unipotent numerical monoid, then we have

\displaystyle\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S}).

Proof.

We already know the inclusions
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
and
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})
.
By Proposition
8.4
, we know that the two-sided Frobenius set has a unique element,
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\{C\}
.
Assume towards a contradiction that there is an element
A\in\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})\setminus\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Since
A
is from
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
, we know that
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap A\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}A\neq\emptyset
.
Hence, there exists
B\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
such that
C=AB
.
But, as we pointed in Remark
6.1
, if
A
and
C
are two elements from
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})
, then there is no element
B\in G({\mathbb{N}})^{*}
such that
AB=C
.
This contradiction shows that
\mathtt{F}_{l}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
The equality
\mathtt{F}_{r}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
is proven similarly.
Hence, our proof is complete.
∎

Recall our notation
\preceq
from the introduction. It denotes the partial order
\leq_{G({\mathbb{N}}),t}
on
G({\mathbb{N}})
.
We are now ready to prove the Theorem
1.8
.
We recall its statement for the convenience of the reader.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a strongly symmetric unipotent numerical monoid in
G({\mathbb{N}})
such that
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\{C\}
for some
C\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle|\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)|=|\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)|=\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S}).

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

By Propositions
8.4
and
7.4
, we know that
\{C\}=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
.
Let
X\in G({\mathbb{N}})\backslash\mathcal{S}
.
Then we have
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})\cap X\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathcal{S}^{*}X\neq\emptyset
, implying that
X\preceq C
.
This means that
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
is a subset of
\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
.
But by definition,
\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
is a subset of
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore we proved the following equalities of sets for symmetric numerical monoids:

\displaystyle G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}=\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)=\{X\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}\mid\{CX^{-1},X^{-1}C\}\subset\mathcal{S}\}.

It follows that
\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S})=|\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)|
.

Now, let us analyze the set
\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
.
This is precisely the set of all elements
A\in\mathcal{S}
such that
\{A^{-1}C,CA^{-1}\}\subseteq G({\mathbb{N}})
.
In addition, since
\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)\subset\mathcal{S}
, we must have that
\{A^{-1}C,CA^{-1}\}\cap\mathcal{S}=\emptyset
.
Otherwise, we would have
C\in\mathcal{S}
which is absurd.
In conclusion, the set
\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
is precisely the set of all elements
A\in\mathcal{S}
such that

CA^{-1}\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
and
A^{-1}C\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
We define a map
\Psi:\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)\to\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi(X):=CX^{-1}\qquad(X\in\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)).

It is evident from the fact that
\mathcal{S}
is a cancellative monoid that
\Psi
is an injective map.
We claim that this map is onto.
Let
Y\in\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
. We set
X:=Y^{-1}C
.
Then we know from the previous paragraph that
X\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
.
Hence, we see that
\Psi(X)=CX^{-1}=C(Y^{-1}C)^{-1}=Y
.
This finishes the proof of the fact that the sets
\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
and
\mathtt{n}(\mathcal{S},C,\preceq)
are in a bijection with each other.
∎

The last proposition of our paper shows how our previous theorem generalizes a well-known property of the symmetric generalized numerical semigroups.

Proposition 8.8
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a strongly symmetric unipotent numerical monoid.
Then we have

\displaystyle|\mathtt{n}(G({\mathbb{N}}),C,\leq_{e})|=\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S})+|\mathtt{n}(S,C,\leq_{e})|

In particular, if
G
is the commutative unipotent numerical monoid
\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
, then we have

\displaystyle\text{vol}(\text{Cube}(C))=2\mathtt{g}(\mathcal{S}),

where
\text{vol}(\text{Cube}(C))
stands for the volume of the convex hull of points
P\in{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
, where
P
is obtained from the vector
(1,c_{2},\dots,c_{n})
by setting some of the coordinates to 0. Here,
(1,c_{2},\dots,c_{n})
is the first row of the matrix
C
.

Proof.

Let
\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})
be given by
\{C\}
.
By Propositions
8.4
and
7.4
, we know that

\displaystyle\{C\}=\mathtt{F}_{t}(\mathcal{S})=\mathtt{PF}_{t}(\mathcal{S}).

This means that for every gap set element
X\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
, we have
X\leq_{e}C
.
In particular, we see that

\displaystyle\{X\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}\mid X\leq_{e}C\}=\{X\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}\mid X\leq_{\mathcal{S},t}C\}.

(7)

Since
C
is the unique maximal element of
G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}
with respect to
\leq_{\mathcal{S},t}
,
(
7
) implies that

\displaystyle G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}=\mathtt{n}(G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S},C,\leq_{e}).

(8)

Then we see that

\displaystyle|\mathtt{n}(G({\mathbb{N}}),C,\leq_{e})|

\displaystyle=|\{B\in G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}\mid B\leq_{e}C\}|+|\{B\in\mathcal{S}\mid B\leq_{e}C\}|

\displaystyle=|G({\mathbb{N}})\setminus\mathcal{S}|+|\mathtt{n}(S,C,\leq_{e})|.

This finishes the proof of our first assertion.

We now proceed with the assumption that
G=\mathbf{P}(n,{\mathbb{N}})
.
Then we notice that the orders
\leq_{e}
and
\preceq
on a unipotent numerical monoid
\mathcal{S}
in
G
are the same.
Indeed, an element
B\in\mathcal{S}
covers another element
A\in\mathcal{S}
in
\leq_{e}
if and only if the difference
B-A
has all nonnegative entries.
By using elementary matrices, we can express this as follows:

\displaystyle B=AE_{1,j}\quad\text{ for some $1\leq j\leq n$},

where
E_{1,j}
is the elementary unipotent matrix with 1 at its
(1,j)
-th entry.
Hence, by Theorem
1.8
, we see that
|\mathtt{n}(G({\mathbb{N}}),C,\leq_{e})|=2\mathtt{g}(S)
.
Let
(1,c_{2},\dots,c_{n})
denote the first row of
C
.
Then
|\mathtt{n}(G({\mathbb{N}}),C,\leq_{e})|
is given by the number of points in the hypercube whose vertices are obtained from
(1,c_{2},\dots,c_{n})
by setting some of the coordinates to 0.
But this proves our second assertion.
Hence, the proof of our proposition is complete.
∎
