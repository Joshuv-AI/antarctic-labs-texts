Abstract
. We provide a generalized definition for the quantized Clifford algebra introduced by Hayashi using another parameter k k that we call the twist. For a field of characteristic not equal to 2 2 , we provide a basis for our quantized Clifford algebra, show that it can be decomposed into rank 1 1 components, and compute its center to show it is a classical Clifford algebra over the group algebra of a product of cyclic groups of order 2 ​ k 2k . In addition, we characterize the semisimplicity of our quantum Clifford algebra in terms of the semisimplicity of a cyclic group of order 2 ​ k 2k and give a complete set of irreducible representations. We construct morphisms from quantum groups and explain various relationships between the classical and quantum Clifford algebras. By changing our generators, we provide a further generalization to allow k k to be a half integer, where we recover certain quantum Clifford algebras introduced by Fadeev, Reshetikhin, and Takhtajan as a special case.

Introduction

Clifford algebras (over
\mathbb{C}
) are a certain class of superalgebras defined by a universal property involving a symmetric bilinear form (equivalently a quadratic form) that appear naturally in the study of particle systems to describe fermionic creation and annihilation operators.
We build a Clifford algebra
\Cl(V\oplus V^{*})
by taking an
n

V
and its algebraic dual
V^{*}
to be maximal isotropic subspaces under the natural symmetric bilinear form defined by
\psi_{i}^{*}(\psi_{j})=\delta_{ij}
for dual bases
\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\{\psi_{i}^{*}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of
V
and
V^{*}
respectively.
We can also form the (s)pin group, which is the universal cover of the (special) orthogonal group and is used to describe the symmetries of fermions, from the Clifford algebra.
By taking
n\to\infty
in a suitable way, we arrive at the (fermionic) Fock space that is used to define representations of the infinite rank general linear Lie algebra
\mathfrak{gl}_{\infty}
and the affine general linear Lie algebra
\widehat{\mathfrak{gl}}_{n}
through the boson-fermion correspondence.
More precisely, this comes from the spinor representation of the Clifford algebra
\Cl(V\oplus V^{*})
that is constructed on the exterior algebra
\bigwedge V
, which is the unique (up to isomorphism) simple module of a Clifford algebra.
Taking
V
to be the natural
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
representation, the exterior power
\bigwedge^{k}V
is the irreducible fundamental representation
V(\Lambda_{k})
of
\mathfrak{gl}_{n}
.
Given the relation with the spin group, we also obtain representations of the special orthogonal Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}_{n}
, with a slight dependency on the parity of

.
See,

,
[
FH91
]

2.
Background: Classical Clifford algebras

In this section we define the classical Clifford algebra and recall some of its basic properties.

Every algebra considered here is unital and associative unless otherwise stated.
We fix a field
\mathbf{k}
of characteristic different from
2
and compute all tensor products over
\mathbf{k}
.
We denote by
\zeta_{r}
a primitive
r
-th root of unity in
\mathbf{k}
(assuming it exists); so
\zeta_{r}=e^{2\pi\mathbf{i}/r}
with
\mathbf{i}=\sqrt{-1}
in
\mathbb{C}
.
We use
\mathbb{Z}_{r}=\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}
to denote the (cyclic) group of integers modulo
r
under

, and let

denote the
j
th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
For any ring

denote its group of units.
We use
\{A,B\}
(resp.
[A,B]_{q}
) to denote the algebra anticommutator (resp.
q
-commutator) of

and

\{A,B\}=AB+BA,\qquad\qquad[A,B]_{q}=AB-q\,BA.

Definition 2.1

(
[
GW09
, Def. 6.1.1]
)
.

Let
W
be a vector space over
\mathbf{k}
equipped with a symmetric bilinear form
\beta
.
A
Clifford algebra
for
(W,\beta)
is an associative
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
\Cl(W,\beta)
and a linear map
\gamma\colon W\hookrightarrow\Cl(W,\beta)
satisfying the following conditions:

(i)

\{\gamma(v),\gamma(w)\}=\beta(v,w)
for every
v,w\in W
.

(ii)

\gamma(W)
generates
\Cl(W,\beta)
as an algebra.

(iii)

(Universal Property) Given any unital associative
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
with a linear map
\varphi\colon W\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\{\varphi(v),\varphi(w)\}=\beta(v,w)

\widetilde{\varphi}\colon\Cl(W,\beta)\to\mathcal{A}
such that
\varphi=\widetilde{\varphi}\circ\gamma
.
In other words, there is an algebra map
\widetilde{\varphi}
making the following diagram commute.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Cl(W,\beta)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widetilde{\varphi}}

From the universal property, Clifford algebras are unique (up to isomorphism) for the pair
(W,\beta)
, and so we call
\Cl(W,\beta)
the Clifford algebra of
(W,\beta)
.
We can explicitly construct it as a quotient of the tensor algebra
T(W)=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}W^{\otimes m}

.
Hence, the natural
\mathbb{Z}
-grading on

descends to a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-grading, making
\Cl(W,\beta)
a
superalgebra
.
The
exterior algebra
is when
\beta=0
and is denoted
\bigwedge(W)=\Cl(W,0)
, and it is supercommutative.

In this work, we mostly consider Clifford algebras for spaces of the form
V\oplus V^{*}
, where
V^{*}:=\Hom_{\mathbf{k}}(V,\mathbf{k})
is the usual algebraic dual of

.
We equip

with the symmetric bilinear form

(2.1)

\beta\big((v,f),(w,h)\big)=f(w)+h(v),\quad v,w\in V,\,\,f,h\in V^{*}.

Hence

are maximal isotropic subspaces with respect to

.
For simplicity, we write
\Cl(V\oplus V^{*})=\Cl(V\oplus V^{*},\,\beta)
.
We will work in a fixed basis
\{\psi_{a}\}_{a=1}^{n}
for

, and let
\{\psi_{b}^{*}\}_{b=1}^{n}
denote the dual basis in

.
Then the
\psi_{a}
and
\psi_{b}^{*}

canonical anticommutation relations

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}\psi_{a}\psi_{b}+\psi_{b}\psi_{a}=\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{b}^{*}+\psi_{b}^{*}\psi_{a}^{*}=0,\\
\psi_{a}\psi_{b}^{*}+\psi_{b}^{*}\psi_{a}=\delta_{ab}.\end{gathered}

Recall that
\Cl(V\oplus V^{*})
is a central simple algebra, and therefore it has a unique irreducible representation called the

, up to isomorphism.
See,

,
[
Bum04
, Thm. 31.1]
,
[

, Ch. IV]
,
[

, Ch. 5]

[

, Ch. V]

The irreducible

-module may be realized on the exterior algebra

\psi_{i}^{*}\cdot 1_{\bigwedge(V)}=0

\Cl(V\oplus V^{*})/J^{*}

.

We conclude this section by constructing an isomorphism

\Gamma_{V}\colon\Cl(V\oplus V^{*})^{\otimes m}\to\Cl\left(V\otimes W)\oplus(V\otimes W)^{*}\right),

For each

or

of the subalgebra

by

(2.4)

\displaystyle\overline{f}_{r}=[\psi_{1},\psi_{1}^{*}]\cdots[\psi_{r},\psi_{r}^{*}].

This name comes from rewriting

2.4

\overline{f}_{r}=\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{2r},

where

(2.5)

\epsilon_{2j-1}=\psi_{j}^{*}-\psi_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\epsilon_{2j}=\psi_{j}^{*}+\psi_{j}

(

The

correspond to the (standard) orthonormal basis of

with the bilinear form defined by (

), which becomes a diagonal bilinear form

(see

and

Hence,

is the volume element in the corresponding exterior algebra

.
Contrast this with
\psi_{j},\psi_{j}^{*}
, which define an isotropic basis of

In this context we use the volume element to construct a superalgebra isomorphism expressing a tensor product of Clifford algebras as another Clifford algebra.
This map is motivated by

.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose

and

are
\mathbf{k}
-vector spaces with

. Let
\phi_{a}

There is a superalgebra isomorphism

\displaystyle 1\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{a}\otimes\cdots\otimes 1\to\overline{f}_{(j-1)n}\phi_{a+(j-1)n},

where on the left

Remark 2.3
.

As the Clifford algebra is a superalgebra, clearly
\Cl(V\oplus W,\,\beta_{1}\oplus\beta_{2})\cong\Cl(V,\beta_{1})\,\widehat{\otimes}\,\Cl(W,\beta_{2})
, with

Furthermore, there is a natural superalgebra structure on

In particular, the volume elements

3.
Quantized Clifford algebras

In this section we prove structural properties of the quantum Clifford algebra.
To begin, we provide a generalized definition of the quantized Clifford algebra.
The first main result in this section is an explicit basis in
3.4
proven by giving a reduction algorithm.
Our other main result is computing the center in
3.12
.
This computation relies on the tensor product factorization of
3.10
, the quantum analog of
2.2
, and the construction a quantum volume element, which enjoys many of the same properties as its classical counterpart.

Definition 3.1
.

Choose
q\in\mathbf{k}^{\times}
and let
n,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
The
quantum Clifford algebra

\Cl_{q}(n,k)
of
rank

n
and
twist

k
is the associative
\mathbf{k}
-algebra generated by
\psi_{a}
,
\psi_{a}^{*}
,
\omega_{a}
, and
\omega_{a}^{-1}
, for
a\in\{1,\dotsc,n\}
, subject to the relations

\displaystyle\omega_{a}\omega_{b}

\displaystyle=\omega_{b}\omega_{a},

\displaystyle\omega_{a}\omega_{a}^{-1}

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\omega_{a}\psi_{b}

\displaystyle=q^{\delta_{ab}}\psi_{b}\omega_{a},

\displaystyle\omega_{a}\psi_{b}^{*}

\displaystyle=q^{-\delta_{ab}}\psi_{b}^{*}\omega_{a},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{b}

\displaystyle+\psi_{b}\psi_{a}=0,

\displaystyle\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{b}^{*}

\displaystyle+\psi_{b}^{*}\psi_{a}^{*}=0,

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}

\displaystyle+q^{k}\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}=\omega_{a}^{-k},

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}

\displaystyle+q^{-k}\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}=\omega_{a}^{k},

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{b}^{*}

\displaystyle+\psi_{b}^{*}\psi_{a}=0

\displaystyle\text{if }a\neq b.

The parameter
n
refers to the number of
pairs
of
(\psi_{j},\psi_{j}^{\dagger})
generators, so we have only defined a quantum analog for the
even
dimensional Clifford algebra
\Cl\left(\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\right)
.
The case
k=2
was given by Hayashi
[
Hay90
]
and the
k=1
version was utilized by Kwon
[
Kwo14
]
.

The defining relations imply that

(3.2)

(q\omega_{a})^{k}-(q\omega_{a})^{-k}=(q^{k}-q^{-k})\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}^{-k}-\omega_{a}^{k}=(q^{k}-q^{-k})\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}.

When

, we obtain an alternative presentation of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
using

3.2
by replacing the

-commutor relations for
\psi_{a}
and
\psi_{a}^{*}
with

(3.3)

\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}=\frac{(q\omega_{a})^{k}-(q\omega_{a})^{-k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}=-\frac{\omega_{a}^{k}-\omega_{a}^{-k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}.

Notice there is a
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-grading on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
defined by

(3.4)

\deg(\psi_{a})=e_{a},\qquad\qquad\deg(\psi_{a}^{*})=-e_{a},\qquad\qquad\deg(\omega_{a})=0.

This naturally yields a

-grading (by the linear map

-grading (by

\displaystyle=q^{2k}\omega_{a}^{k}-q^{2k}\omega_{a}^{3k}.

Solving for

.

The following relations hold in

\displaystyle=q^{k}\omega_{a}^{k}-q^{k}\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*},

Proof.

We obtain the last relation as follows:

The remaining identities follow immediately from the defining relations (

∎

We construct a basis of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
by considering a normal ordering of the generators analogous to the standard basis for the classical Clifford algebra.

Theorem 3.4
.

Consider the set
\mathcal{B}
of monomials

\prod_{a=1}^{n}\psi_{a}^{p_{a}}(\psi_{a}^{*})^{d_{a}}\omega_{a}^{v_{a}},

where

and

\Cl_{q}(n,k)

Proof.

Clearly
\mathcal{B}
contains

\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
Thus, we need to show that
\mathcal{B}
is linearly independent and closed under multiplication.
Together with Lemma

, the

\mathcal{B}
is closed under multiplication.
Indeed, these are a set of rewriting rules to reduce any product of generators into a linear combination of elements in
\mathcal{B}
.
Furthermore, note that we cannot apply the rewriting rules to any element of
\mathcal{B}
(after having chosen an order on the elements
\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*},\omega_{a}

Hence, we just need to show
\mathcal{B}

To show the linear independence, we need to show that the result of the rewriting rules is unique and that they undo the application of any of the defining relations.
The latter property is clear (the only non-trivial one is the

-commutor relations between
\psi_{a}
and
\psi_{a}^{*}

The uniqueness is a straightforward argument using the diamond lemma.
∎

Observe that the linear independence of the monomials in

and a sign with the exception of

For the replacement

We immediately obtain the dimension of

from

We have

Now notice that when

Conversely, when

Theorem 3.6

Suppose

Consider the set

Proof: Reduction rules.

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k}\omega_{a}^{3k}-q^{-k}\omega_{a}^{k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}=\frac{\omega_{a}^{k}(q^{2k}\omega_{a}^{2k}-1)}{q^{2k}-1},

\displaystyle\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{2k}\omega_{a}^{k}(1-\omega_{a}^{2k})}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}=\frac{q^{3k}\omega_{a}^{k}(1-\omega_{a}^{2k})}{q^{2k}-1},

which are obtained from

The remainder of the proof is similar to the proof of

Proof: Change-of-basis.

We will construct an invertible linear map from
\mathcal{B}
from Theorem

to
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}

Consider a monomial
\prod_{a=1}^{n}\psi_{a}^{p_{a}}(\psi_{a}^{*})^{d_{a}}\omega_{a}^{v_{a}}

\mathcal{B}

3.2.
Structure Theorem

We construct a quantum analog of the isomorphism
\Gamma_{V}
defined in

. The (anti)commutators

(3.13)

\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}=(1-q^{k})\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}+q^{k}\omega_{a}^{k},\qquad\qquad[\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}]=(1+q^{k})\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}-q^{k}\omega_{a}^{k},

Clearly
\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}\neq 0,1
for all

.
We first show that the anticommutators are nontrivial central elements.
This does not have a classical analog as the classical anticommutation relations state
\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}=1
.
Let
Z_{q}(n,k):=Z(\Cl_{q}(n,k))
denote the center of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
The next result follows from a straightforward computation, left as an exercise to the reader, using the reduction rules of
3.3
.

Lemma 3.7
.

For each
a=1,\ldots,n
, the anticommutator
\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}
belongs to
Z_{q}(n,k)
and
\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}^{2}=1

We need quantum analogs of the volume elements defined by
Equation

2.4
.
We proceed exactly as in the classical case.
For each

, the

or
chirality
element
f_{r}
of the subalgebra

is given by

(3.14)

\displaystyle f_{r}=[\psi_{1},\psi_{1}^{*}]\cdots[\psi_{r},\psi_{r}^{*}].

Like in the classical case, we can express each volume element as a product of standardized coordinates:

f_{r}=\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{2r},

with
\epsilon_{j}
given by the same formulae as in
Equation

2.5
.
The standardized coordinates in

are a quantum analog of a basis of
\mathbf{k}^{2n}\cong\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}
where the defining bilinear form is diagonal, but they do
not
satisfy
\{\epsilon_{a},\epsilon_{b}\}=\delta_{ab}

Nevertheless, the anticommutators

are still central; this follows from an easy calculation left as an exercise to the reader.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{2n}
denote the standardized coordinates of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
defined using formulae as in
Equation

2.5
. Then

\{\epsilon_{a},\epsilon_{b}\}=\epsilon_{a}\epsilon_{b}+\epsilon_{b}\epsilon_{a}=2\delta_{ab}\{\psi_{\lceil a/2\rceil},\psi_{\lceil a/2\rceil}^{*}\}

is central in
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.

Next we prove a quantum analog of
[
ML90
, Prop. 3.3]
, obtaining some relations involving
f_{r}
.
We note that many of the signs there vanish here because
f_{r}
is the product of an
even
number of factors.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
f_{r}
denote a quantum volume element in
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
as defined in (
3.14
).
Let
\phi_{a}
denote either
\psi_{a}
or
\psi_{a}^{*}
.

•

If
a>r
, then
\phi_{a}f_{r}=f_{r}\phi_{a}
and
\omega_{a}f_{r}=f_{r}\omega_{a}
.

•

If
a\leq r
, then
\phi_{a}f_{r}=-f_{r}\phi_{a}
.

•

For any
r,s\leq n
, we have
f_{r}f_{s}=f_{s}f_{r}
and
f_{r}^{2}=1
.

Proof.

The anticommutation relations imply
\phi_{a}
commutes with
[\psi_{b},\psi_{b}^{*}]
whenever
a\neq b
.
In addition, combining
Equation

3.13
with
3.3
implies

\displaystyle[\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}]\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=(1+q^{k})\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}-q^{2k}\psi_{a}\omega_{a}^{k}=q^{k}\psi_{a}\omega_{a}^{k}

\displaystyle=-\psi_{a}\left((1+q^{k})\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}-q^{k}\omega^{k}\right)=-\psi_{a}[\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}].

A similar calculation shows that
[\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}]\psi_{a}^{*}=-\psi_{a}^{*}[\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}]
.
This means
\phi_{a}f_{r}=f_{r}\phi_{a}
if

and
\phi_{a}f_{r}=-f_{r}\phi_{a}
if

.
The remaining relations follow from straightforward calculations.
∎

We are now ready to construct an isomorphism
\Gamma_{q}\colon\Cl_{q}(n,k)^{\otimes m}\to\Cl_{q}(nm,k)

e.g.
, as in
[

]

As in the classical case, our isomorphism relies on the volume elements

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\phi_{a}
denote either
\psi_{a}
or
\psi_{a}^{*}

Then the map
\Gamma_{q}\colon\Cl_{q}(n,k)^{\otimes m}\to\Cl_{q}(nm,k)

\displaystyle 1\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{a}\otimes\cdots\otimes 1

\displaystyle\mapsto f_{(j-1)n}\phi_{a+(j-1)n}\text{ and}

\displaystyle 1\otimes\cdots\otimes\omega_{a}\otimes\cdots\otimes 1

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega_{a+(j-1)n},

is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}^{nm}
-graded algebras.
The inverse
\Gamma_{q}^{-1}\colon\Cl_{q}(nm,k)\to\Cl_{q}(n,k)^{\otimes m}
is defined by

\displaystyle\phi_{a+(j-1)n}

\displaystyle\mapsto f_{n}\otimes\cdots\otimes f_{n}\otimes\phi_{a}\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1\text{ and}

\displaystyle\omega_{a+(j-1)n}

\displaystyle\mapsto 1\otimes\cdots\otimes\omega_{a}\otimes\cdots\otimes 1.

In particular,

\Gamma_{q}(1\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{a}\phi_{b}\otimes\cdots\otimes 1)=\phi_{a+(j-1)n}\phi_{b+(j-1)n}.

In all cases
\phi_{a}
,
\phi_{a}\phi_{b}
, and
\omega_{a}
appear in the
j
th tensor factor of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)^{\otimes m}

Proof.

Let
\phi_{a,j}
denote the tensor product

with
\phi_{a}
appearing in the
j
th tensor factor. First note that

maps each

\Cl_{q}(nm,k)

,

implies

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{a,j})\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{b,j})

\displaystyle=f_{(j-1)n}^{2}\phi_{a+(j-1)n}\phi_{b+(j-1)n}=-\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{b,j})\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{a,j}).

In addition, for any

,
3.9
implies

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(\omega_{a,j})\Gamma_{q}(\psi_{b,j})\Gamma_{q}(\omega_{a,j})^{-1}

\displaystyle=f_{(j-1)n}\omega_{a+(j-1)n}\psi_{b+(j-1)n}\omega_{a+(j-1)n}^{-1}=q^{\delta_{ab}}\Gamma_{q}(\psi_{b,j}),

and

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(\psi_{a,j})\Gamma_{q}(\psi_{a,j}^{*})+q^{\pm k}\Gamma_{q}(\psi_{a,j}^{*})\Gamma_{q}(\psi_{a,j})=\big\{\psi_{a+(j-1)n},\psi_{a+(j-1)n}^{*}\big\}_{q^{\pm k}}=w_{a+(j-1)n}^{\mp k}=\Gamma_{q}(\omega_{a,j})^{\mp k}.

Next notice

indeed defines an algebra map on

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{a,j})\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{b,k})

\displaystyle=-f_{(j-1)n}f_{(k-1)n}\phi_{a+(j-1)n}\phi_{b+(k-1)n}=\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{b,k})\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{a,k})\text{ and}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{a,j})\Gamma_{q}(\omega_{b,k})

\displaystyle=\omega_{b+(k-1)n}f_{(j-1)n}\phi_{a+(j-1)n}=\Gamma_{q}(\omega_{b,k})\Gamma_{q}(\phi_{a,j}).

We conclude by showing

coincide with appropriate identity maps on a set of generators. To this end, notice that if the commutator appears in the

th factor of

\displaystyle\Gamma_{q}(1\otimes\cdots\otimes[\psi_{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}},\psi_{a}^{*}]\otimes\cdots\otimes 1)=f_{(j-1)n}^{2}[\psi_{a+(j-1)n}^{\phantom{\dagger}},\psi_{a+(j-1)n}^{*}]=\big[\psi_{a+(j-1)n}^{\phantom{\dagger}},\psi_{a+(j-1)n}^{*}\big]

3.3.
The center of a quantum Clifford algebra

As we saw in

\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}
are nontrivial central elements that are square roots of unity.
We now construct their

and show that these roots generate the center

of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)

Theorem 3.12
.

We have

Z_{q}(n,k)\cong\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}]\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}]=\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}^{n}].

In particular,

is generated by the order

z_{a}=q\omega_{a}-(q-1)\psi_{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{a}^{*}\omega_{a}^{k+1},

for

.
Moreover,
\dim Z_{q}(n,k)=(2k)^{n}
and each

\psi_{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{a}^{*}+\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}=z_{a}^{k}.

We first show that the elements
z_{a}
are in the center, and we write their powers explicitly in terms of our basis from
3.4
.

Lemma 3.13
.

The elements
z_{a}
defined in
3.12
are central in
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
and they satisfy

(3.17)

\displaystyle z_{a}^{r}=q^{r}\omega_{a}^{r}-(q^{r}-1)\psi_{a}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{a}^{*}\omega_{a}^{k+r}.

In particular,
z_{a}^{k}=\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}
, so
z_{a}^{2k}=1
.

Proof.

That
z_{a}
is central is a straightforward computation showing it commutes with all of the generators of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
using the reduction rules.

Equation

3.17
is a straightforward calculation using induction on
r
.
That
z_{a}^{k}=\{\psi_{a},\psi_{a}^{*}\}
is immediate from comparing
Equation

3.13
with
Equation

3.17
with

.
The last claim is
3.7
.

Proof of
3.12
.

Using the decomposition of
3.11
, it is sufficient to show the claim for
\Cl_{q}(1,k)
; that is,
Z_{q}(1,k)\cong\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}]
.

We will show the central elements

constitute a basis of

.
The elements
z_{1},\ldots,z_{1}^{2k}
are linearly independent because the elements

are linearly independent by

.
Hence the subalgebra generated by

is central and isomorphic to
\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}]
by
3.13
.
To show there is nothing else in the center, consider an arbitrary central element
\zeta\in Z_{q}(1,k)
.
In what follows we omit the subscript

on
\Cl_{q}(1,k)
generators for brevity.
Using
3.4
, we write

\zeta=\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}a_{j}\omega^{j}+\psi\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}b_{j}\omega^{j}+\psi^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}c_{j}\omega^{j}+\psi\psi^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}d_{j}\omega^{j}

for some constants
a_{j},b_{j},c_{j},d_{j}\in\mathbf{k}
.
By direct computation using the reduction rules, we have

\displaystyle 0=[\zeta,\psi]

\displaystyle=\psi\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}\left((q^{j}-1)a_{j}+q^{j}d_{j+k}\right)\omega^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}q^{j+k}c_{j}\omega^{j}-\psi\psi^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(q^{j+k}+1)c_{j}\omega^{j},

\displaystyle 0=[\zeta,\psi^{*}]

\displaystyle=\psi^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}\left((q^{-j}-1)a_{j}+d_{j+k}\right)\omega^{j}-q^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}b_{j}\omega^{j+k}+q^{k}\psi\psi^{*}\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}(q^{-j-k}+1)b_{j}\omega^{j},

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2k-1}b_{j}\omega^{j+k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(b_{j}\omega^{k}+b_{j+k}q^{-2k})\omega^{j}+(1-q^{-2k})\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}b_{j+k}\psi\psi^{*}\omega^{j+k},

where we take the indices modulo

.
Therefore, by
3.2
and linear independence from

, we must have
b_{j}=c_{j}=0
for all

.
Furthermore, we must have
d_{j}=(q^{-j}-1)a_{k+j}
.
This means there are at most

degrees of freedom, which yields
\dim\left(Z_{q}(1,k)\right)\leq 2k
and the claim follows.

We comment on two consequences of

.

We may view

as a classical Clifford algebra
\Cl(R^{n}\oplus(R^{*})^{n})
, albeit with a slightly rescaled pairing, over the group ring
R=\mathbf{k}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]\cong\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}^{n}]
, once we relax the vector space requirement in
2.1
to admit more general (free) modules over commutative rings.
This interpretation elucidates the conclusion of
3.12
: the classical Clifford algebra is simple, so the only central elements in
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
can be the scalars
\mathbf{k}[\mathbb{Z}_{2k}^{n}]
in the base ring.
This also yields that
\mathcal{C}_{q}(n,k)
(see (
3.11
) and (
3.12
)) are isomorphic for any value of
q\in\mathbf{k}^{\times}
as
\mathcal{C}_{q}(n,k)\cong R\oplus R\langle\psi_{1}\psi_{1}^{*},\dotsc,\psi_{n}\psi_{n}^{*}\rangle
.
The second consequence is revealed by
Equation

3.15
: the center belongs to the degree
0
component of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
under the
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-grading given in
Equation

3.4
.

3.4.
Quantum Clifford (anti-)involutions

We define some (anti-)involutions on the quantum Clifford algebra
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
that are quantum analogs of (anti-)involutions of the classical Clifford algebra.
For example, we define quantum analogs of the transpose and conjugation maps.

First recall
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
is a superalgebra, and so there is the
grade involution

\alpha
on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
defined by

\psi_{a}\mapsto-\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto-\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto\omega_{a},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q.

When the twist
k
is even, we could also define a variant
\widetilde{\alpha}
by having
\omega_{a}\mapsto-\omega_{a}
instead.

Next, we define the
transpose
anti-involution on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
by

\psi_{a}\mapsto\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto q^{-1}\omega_{a}^{-1},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q.

This is the quantum analog of the transpose on the classical Clifford algebra, and we denote it by
x^{t}
.

Proposition 3.14
.

The transpose is an anti-involution on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
Moreover, the transpose commutes with the grade involution.

Proof.

A direct computation shows the transpose preserves the defining relations and that both
(x^{t})^{t}=x
and
\alpha(x^{t})=\alpha(x)^{t}
for all generators
x\in\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
∎

With the transpose and grade involution in hand, we may define the
Clifford conjugation
on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
:
for any
x\in\Cl_{q}(n,k)
, we set

\overline{x}=\alpha(x^{t}).

Next, we separate the anti-involution
\ast
defined in
[
Hay90
, Sec. 5]
depending on the value
q
into the product of two separate anti-involutions that do not depend on
q
.
Hayashi used the map
\ast
to make
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
into a
\ast
-algebra and to prove the unitarity of his
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
-modules
[
Hay90
]
.
The first is the anti-involution that we call the
dagger
map, which we define by

\psi_{a}\mapsto\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto\omega_{a},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q,

and denote by
x^{\dagger}
for
x\in\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
The second anti-involution is the
duality
map on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
, which we define by

\psi_{a}\mapsto\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto\omega_{a}^{-1},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q^{-1}

and denote by
x^{\vee}
for
x\in\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
Both the dagger and duality maps are different quantum analogs of the natural duality anti-involution
\ast
on
\Cl\left(\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\right)
sending
\psi_{a}^{*}\leftrightarrow\psi_{a}
, although arguably the dagger map is the most natural analog.
We justify calling the latter map the duality map by the relationship with the quantum group discussed below in
Section

3.5
.

Proposition 3.15
.

The grade involution, transpose, dagger, and duality maps all commute.

Proof.

This is a direct computation on the generators, such as
x^{\vee\dagger}=x^{\dagger\vee}
for any generator
x\in\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
∎

Now by composing the duality and dagger maps,we obtain the involution of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
given by

\psi_{a}\mapsto\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto\omega_{a}^{-1},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q^{-1}.

This could be said to not have a classical analog as it acts as the identity map on
\psi_{a}
and
\psi_{a}^{*}
.
The composition of the transpose and duality maps is the involution defined by

\psi_{a}\mapsto\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto q\omega_{a},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q^{-1}.

If
q^{2k}=1
, then we can construct another involution
\check{\kappa}
by

\psi_{a}\mapsto\psi_{a}^{*},\qquad\qquad\psi_{a}^{*}\mapsto\psi_{a},\qquad\qquad\omega_{a}\mapsto q^{-1}\omega_{a},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q^{-1}.

These arise from the isomorphism of
\Cl\left(\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\right)
induced from
\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\cong(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\oplus\mathbf{k}^{n}
.

3.5.
Quantum group morphisms

We provide the twist
k
version of
[
Hay90
, Thm. 3.2]
through a similar direct computation.

Let
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g},k)
denote a
twisted
form of the Drinfel’d–Jimbo
[
Dri85
,
Jim85
]
quantum group, defined as follows.
Let
\mathfrak{g}
denote a semisimple Lie algebra and let
A
denote its generalized Cartan matrix with the diagonal matrix of root lengths
D=\mathrm{diag}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
such that
DA
is symmetric.
Let
q\in\mathbf{k}
be transcendental and write
q_{i}=q^{d_{i}}
.
Then
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g},k)
is the associative
\mathbf{k}
-algebra generated by the elements
E_{i},F_{i},K_{i}
, and
K_{i}^{-1}
, for
i=1,\ldots,r
, subject to the relations:

\displaystyle K_{i}K_{j}=K_{j}K_{i},\quad K_{i}K_{i}^{-1}=K_{i}^{-1}K_{i}=1,

\displaystyle K_{i}E_{j}K_{i}^{-1}=q_{i}^{a_{ij}}E_{j},\quad K_{i}F_{j}K_{i}^{-1}=q_{i}^{-a_{ij}}F_{j},

(3.18)

\displaystyle E_{i}F_{j}-F_{j}E_{i}=\delta_{ij}\frac{K_{i}^{k}-K_{i}^{-k}}{q_{i}^{k}-q_{i}^{-k}},

together with the
q
-Serre relations for
i\neq j
,

(3.19)

\sum_{m=0}^{1-a_{ij}}(-1)^{m}\begin{bmatrix}1-a_{ij}\\
m\end{bmatrix}_{q_{i}^{k}}X_{i}^{k}X_{j}X_{i}^{1-a_{ij}-m}=0.

In the last equality every
X
is either an
E
or an
F
.
The
q
-integer is given by
[n]_{q_{i}}=\frac{q_{i}^{n}-q_{i}^{-n}}{q_{i}-q_{i}^{-1}}
, the
q
-factorial is
[n]_{q_{i}}!=[n]_{q_{i}}\cdots[1]_{q_{i}}
, and the
q
-binomial coefficient is defined analogously.
The following identity is often useful for verifying
q
-Serre relations when
a_{ij}=-1
:

(3.20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{m=0}^{1-a_{ij}}(-1)^{m}\begin{bmatrix}1-a_{ij}\\
m\end{bmatrix}_{q_{i}^{k}}X_{i}^{k}X_{j}X_{i}^{1-a_{ij}-m}&=X_{i}(X_{i}X_{j}-q_{i}^{k}X_{j}X_{i})-q_{i}^{-k}(X_{i}X_{j}-q_{i}^{k}X_{j}X_{i})X_{i}\\
&=\bigl[X_{i},[X_{i},X_{j}]_{q_{i}^{k}}\bigr]_{q_{i}^{-k}}.\end{split}

Note that
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g},k)
differs from the standard
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
by the exponents on
K_{i}
in
Equation

3.18
and the exponents on
q_{i}
in
Equations

3.18
and
3.19
.
In particular, we recover the standard presentation when
k=1
.
For more information on quantum groups, we refer the reader to one of the standard textbooks such as
[
CP94
,
Kas95
,
Jan96
]
.

We need the following twist
k
versions of
[
Hay90
, Lemma 3.1]
.

Lemma 3.16
.

Suppose
q^{2k}\neq 1
. Let
a,b,c
be distinct elements of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
and let
\phi_{a}
denote either
\psi_{a}
or
\psi_{a}^{*}
. The following identities hold in
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
:

(3.21)

\displaystyle[\psi_{a}\psi_{b}^{*},\psi_{b}\psi_{a}^{*}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(\omega_{a}\omega_{b}^{-1})^{k}-(\omega_{a}\omega_{b}^{-1})^{-k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}},

(3.22)

\displaystyle[\psi_{a}\psi_{b},\psi_{b}^{*}\psi_{a}^{*}]

\displaystyle=\frac{(q\omega_{a}\omega_{b})^{k}-(q\omega_{a}\omega_{b})^{-k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}},

(3.23)

\displaystyle[\phi_{a}\psi_{b},\psi_{b}^{*}\phi_{c}]_{q^{\pm k}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{b}^{\mp k}\phi_{a}\phi_{c},

(3.24)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}\pm\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k}\omega_{a}^{k}\pm\omega_{a}^{-k}}{q^{k}\pm 1}.

Proof.

Using the defining relations, we see that

[\psi_{a}\psi_{b}^{*},\psi_{b}\psi_{a}^{*}]=(\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*})(\psi_{b}^{*}\psi_{b})-(\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a})(\psi_{b}\psi_{b}^{*}).

Applying
Equation

3.3
then yields
Equation

3.21
:

\displaystyle[\psi_{a}\psi_{b}^{*},\psi_{b}\psi_{a}^{*}]

\displaystyle=\frac{-1}{(q^{k}-q^{-k})^{2}}\big(\bigl(q\omega_{a})^{k}-(q\omega_{a})^{-k}\bigr)(\omega_{b}^{k}-\omega_{b}^{-k})-(\omega_{a}-\omega_{a}^{-1})\bigl(q\omega_{b})^{k}-(q\omega_{b})^{-k}\bigr)\bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\omega_{a}\omega_{b}^{-1})^{k}-(\omega_{a}\omega_{b}^{-1})^{-k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}.

Equation

3.22
follows from a similar calculation. To establish
Equation

3.24
, we use
Equation

3.3
:

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}\pm\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}\big((q\omega_{a})^{k}-(q\omega_{a})^{-k}\bigr)\mp(\omega_{a}^{k}-\omega_{a}^{-k})\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k}\mp 1}{q^{2k}-1}\big(q^{k}\omega_{a}\pm\omega_{a}^{-k}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{k}\omega_{a}^{k}\pm\omega_{a}^{-k}}{q^{k}\pm 1}.

Equation

3.23
is similarly easy and we omit its proof.
∎

If
k
is even or
q
has a square root in
\mathbf{k}
, we may write
Equation

3.24
as the more symmetric

(3.25)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{*}\pm\psi_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{a})^{k}\pm(q^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{a})^{-k}}{q^{\frac{k}{2}}\pm q^{-\frac{k}{2}}}.

Proposition 3.17
.

Suppose
q^{2k}\neq 1
.
There is an algebra homomorphism
\Theta\colon U_{q}(\mathfrak{g},k)\to\Cl_{q}(n,k)
defined on generators as follows.

(1)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{sl}_{n}
then

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i+1}^{*},

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{i+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i}^{*},

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\omega_{i+1}^{-1},

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n-1.

(2)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{2n}
then

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i+1}^{*},

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{i+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i}^{*},

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\omega_{i+1}^{-1},

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle E_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{n-1}\psi_{n},

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{n}^{*}\psi_{n-1}^{*},

\displaystyle K_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto q\omega_{n-1}\omega_{n}.

(3)

If
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}
and
q^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbf{k}
, there is an algebra homomorphism
U_{q^{\frac{1}{2}}}(\mathfrak{g},k)\mapsto\Cl_{q}(n,k)
defined on generators by

\displaystyle E_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i+1}^{*},

\displaystyle F_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{i+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i}^{*},

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle\mapsto\omega_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\omega_{i+1}^{-1},

\displaystyle i=1,\ldots,n-1,

\displaystyle E_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{n},

\displaystyle F_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto\psi_{n}^{*},

\displaystyle K_{n}

\displaystyle\mapsto q^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{n}.

Proof.

The claim follows from direct calculation. We must verify that the images
\widetilde{E}_{i},\widetilde{F}_{i}
, and
\widetilde{K}_{i}
of
E_{i},F_{i}
, and
K_{i}
satisfy the relations defining
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g},k)
. For starters, if
i<n
then

\displaystyle[\widetilde{E}_{i},\widetilde{F}_{i}]=[\psi_{i}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i+1}^{*},\psi_{i+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i}^{*}]=\frac{(\omega_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{-1})^{k}-(\omega_{i}\omega_{i+1}^{-1})^{-t}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}=\frac{\widetilde{K}_{i}^{k}-\widetilde{K}_{i}^{-k}}{q^{k}-q^{-k}}.

If
i,j<n
and
\lvert i-j\rvert>1
, the canonical anticommutation relations imply
[E_{i},E_{j}]=0
. When
\lvert i-j\rvert=1
, each summand in the
q
-Serre
eq.

3.19
vanishes. Since
\widetilde{F}_{i}=\psi_{i+1}^{\phantom{\dagger}}\psi_{i}^{*}=\widetilde{E}_{i}^{\vee}
, this means the
\widetilde{F}_{i}
also satisfy the
q
-Serre relations.

To prove the theorem, it remains to check that
\widetilde{E}_{i},\widetilde{F}_{i}
, and
\widetilde{K}_{i}
satisfy the appropriate relations when
i=n
. For instance,
Equation

3.25
implies

[\widetilde{E}_{n},\widetilde{F}_{n}]=[\psi_{n},\psi_{n}^{*}]=\frac{(q^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{n})^{k}-(q^{\frac{1}{2}}\omega_{n})^{-k}}{q^{\frac{k}{2}}-q^{-\frac{k}{2}}}=\frac{\widetilde{K}_{i}^{k}-\widetilde{K}_{i}^{-k}}{q^{\frac{k}{2}}-q^{-\frac{k}{2}}}.

We leave the remaining verifications as an exercise to the reader.
∎

The formulas in
3.17
capture an underlying correspondence between the simple roots of
\mathfrak{g}
and
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
Using the grading from
Equation

3.4
, notice that

\deg(\Theta(E_{i}))=e_{i}-e_{i+1}\quad\text{and}\quad\deg(\Theta(F_{i}))=-(e_{i}-e_{i+1})

whenever
i<n
. Moreover, if
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{2n}
we have

\deg(\Theta(E_{n}))=e_{n-1}+e_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\deg(\Theta(F_{n}))=-(e_{n-1}+e_{n}),

and similarly if
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}
then

\deg(\Theta(E_{n}))=e_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\deg(\Theta(F_{n}))=-e_{n}.

To conclude, we compute the maps induced by the various (anti-)involutions defined in
Section

3.4
. These become algebra and coalgebra (anti-)automorphisms on the quantum group image. To begin, note that the dagger maps acts on
\Theta\left(U_{q}(\mathfrak{g},k)\right)
as the anti-automorphism
[
Hay90
, Eq. (5.6)]

E_{i}^{\dagger}=F_{i},\qquad\qquad F_{i}^{\dagger}=E_{i},\qquad\qquad K_{i}^{\dagger}=K_{i},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q.

This map becomes a coalgebra automorphism of the quantum group as indicated in
[
Hay90
, Sec. 5.3]
.
Next observe that the duality map induces the dual representation: the action on the quantum group image in
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
is
[
Hay90
, Eq. (5.8)]

E_{i}^{\vee}=F_{i},\qquad\qquad F_{i}^{\vee}=E_{i},\qquad\qquad K_{i}^{\vee}=K_{i}^{-1},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q^{-1}.

As noted by
[
Hay90
, Sec. 5.3]
, this is an anti-(co)algebra automorphism of the quantum group, which agrees with the fact that we need to also reverse the order of a tensor product of representations.
Finally, observe that the transpose on
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
becomes the star (anti-)involution map (see,
e.g.
,
[
Lus90
,
Kas91
,
Kas93
]
) on the quantum group image:

E_{i}^{t}=E_{i},\qquad\qquad F_{i}^{t}=F_{i},\qquad\qquad K_{i}^{t}=K_{i}^{-1},\qquad\qquad q\mapsto q,

which is important in Kashiwara’s grand loop argument for crystal bases
[
Kas91
]
.

4.
Representation theory

In this section we discuss the representation-theoretic aspects of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
.
In particular,
4.1
defines a collection of irreducible
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
-actions on the braided exterior algebra
\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(\mathbf{k}^{n})
, which is defined below, and
4.6
proves that
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
is semisimple exactly when its center
Z_{q}(n,k)
is semisimple.
We note that only one of the
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
-modules has a classical analog.
In addition, we discuss a representation-theoretic implication of the quantum volume elements defined in
Equation

3.14
.

Following
[
BZ05
]
, the
braided exterior algebra

\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(V)
is the associative algebra generated by a basis
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
of
V
subject to the following relations:

(4.1a)

\displaystyle v_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle=1,\ldots,n

(4.1b)

\displaystyle v_{j}v_{k}

\displaystyle=-qv_{k}v_{j},

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle<k.

The elements

(4.2)

\displaystyle v(\ell)=\begin{cases}v_{1}^{\ell_{1}}v_{2}^{\ell_{2}}\cdots v_{n}^{\ell_{n}}&\text{if }\ell_{j}\in\{0,1\},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for
\ell\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, form a basis of
\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(V)
.
Both the classical and braided exterior algebras can be understood as quotients of a tensor algebra modulo an ideal generated by “symmetric”
2
-tensors, which are “positive” eigenvectors of appropriate braiding operators.
In the classical case, the braiding operators are trivial flip maps that transpose tensor factors.
In the quantum case, braiding operators are induced by the universal
R
-matrix of some quantum group
[
BZ05
, Def. 2.1]
.

Recall that unlike its classical counterpart
\Cl\left(\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\right)
, the quantum Clifford algebra
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
has a non-trivial center, which is described in
3.12
.
This means there is some flexibility in defining an action of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
on
\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(\mathbf{k}^{n})
depending on which roots of unity are in
\mathbf{k}
.
There are
n
linearly independent central elements
z_{a}
such that
z_{a}^{2k}=1
, so the representations are naturally indexed by
\mathbb{Z}_{2k}^{n}
.
In other words, there is a unique irreducible representation for each element in the discrete subgroup
\mathbb{Z}_{2k}^{n}\subset Z_{q}(n,k)
.
Recall that
\zeta_{r}
denotes a primitive
r
-th root of unity.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
\zeta_{2k}\in\mathbf{k}
.
For each
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
in
\mathbb{Z}_{2k}^{n}
, there is an irreducible
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
representation
\pi_{p}
on
\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(\mathbf{k}^{n})
defined by

(4.3a)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}\rhd v(\ell)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell_{1}+\cdots\ell_{a-1}}v(\ell+e_{a}),

(4.3b)

\displaystyle\psi_{a}^{*}\rhd v(\ell)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\ell_{1}+\cdots\ell_{a-1}}\cdot\zeta_{2k}^{p_{a}}\cdot v(\ell-e_{a}),

(4.3c)

\displaystyle\omega_{a}\rhd v(\ell)

\displaystyle=\zeta_{2k}^{p_{a}}q^{\ell_{a}-1}v(\ell),

where
v(\ell\pm e_{a})=0
if
\ell\pm e_{a}\notin\{0,1\}^{n}
.
Moreover,
\pi_{p}
and
\pi_{p^{\prime}}
are not isomorphic whenever
p\neq p^{\prime}
.

Proof.

An easy calculation shows that
\pi_{p}
indeed defines an action of
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
. Each
\pi_{p}
is irreducible because we can obtain any basis vector
v(\ell)
from any other by first applying a sequence of lowering operators to obtain
v(0)
(up to a scalar), and then using an appropriate sequence of raising operators.
Using Relations (
4.3
), we see that

\displaystyle\pi_{p}(z_{a})v(\ell)=\zeta_{2k}^{p_{a}}\left(q^{\ell_{a}}-(q-1)\ell_{a}\right)v(\ell)=\zeta_{2k}^{p_{a}}v(\ell),

where
z_{a}
is the central element defined in
3.12
.
Hence
\pi_{p}
and
\pi_{p^{\prime}}
are distinct representations whenever
p\neq p^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

We have defined the
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
-action directly in terms of basis vectors without using
Equation

4.1b
.
As such, there is no difference between defining this on
\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(V)
,
\bigwedge(V)
, or
\mathbf{k}^{2n}
, as they are all isomorphic as
\mathbf{k}
-modules.
Hence, the formulae defining the
\Cl_{q}(n,2)
-module
\pi_{0}
have appeared previously in
[
Hay90
, Prop. 2.1]
, where the representation was defined on the usual exterior algebra
\bigwedge(V)
.
Yet, the braided exterior algebra is better behaved for taking tensor products of quantum group representations; see,
e.g.
,
[
Abo22b
,
Abo22a
]
.

Remark 4.3
.

The spinor representation considered in
[
Kwo14
]
is a “dual” version of the spinor representation considered here: it is obtained by interchanging
\ell_{a}\leftrightarrow 1-\ell_{a}
for all
a
.

Amongst the
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
-modules described in
4.1
, only
\pi_{0}
has a classical analog.
To see this, compare
Equation

4.3
with the
\Cl\left(\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\right)
-action on the exterior algebra.
In particular, the operators
\pi_{0}(\psi_{a})
and
\pi_{0}(\psi_{a}^{*})
satisfy the canonical anticommutation relations (
2
).

The action of classical Clifford algebra
\Cl\left(\mathbf{k}^{n}\oplus(\mathbf{k}^{n})^{*}\right)
on its spinor representation can be described in terms of inner and exterior multiplication.
However, in the quantum Clifford algebra, even under
\pi_{0}
, the generators
\psi_{a}^{*}
and
\psi_{a}
no longer act by inner and exterior multiplication because the algebra structure of
\bigwedge\nolimits_{q}(\mathbf{k}^{n})
is such that a product
v(\ell)
acquires powers of
\pm q
when re-arranging factors of
v_{k}
.
For instance, exterior multiplication of
v(e_{1})=v_{1}
by
v_{2}
results in
-q^{-1}v(e_{1}+e_{2})=-q^{-1}v_{1}v_{2}
.

In order to obtain quantum analogs of the inner and exterior multiplication operators that correctly account for these powers of
\pm q
, we must use the
\omega_{k}
.
Concretely, we let
\iota_{j}^{q}
and
\varepsilon_{j}^{q}
denote quantum analogs of the inner and exterior multiplication operators defined on any
v(\ell)

Theorem 4.6
.

The quantum Clifford algebra
\Cl_{q}(n,k)
is semisimple if and only if

Proof.

We consider a slightly more general problem.
Let
R
be a commutative ring, and fix some invertible
r\in R^{\times}
.
Let
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
be the Clifford algebra generated by
\psi,\psi^{*}
subject to
\psi\psi^{*}+\psi^{*}\psi=r
.
We show that if
R
is Artinian, then
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
is semisimple if and only if
R
is semisimple.
The claim then follows from
3.12
and
3.11
.

We first show there is a one-to-one correspondence between the two-sided ideals of
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
and
R
.
Clearly any ideal
J\subseteq R
can be used to construct an ideal of
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
.
Thus, we just need to show that for every two-sided ideal

,
we have the same form as above for some ideal
J\subseteq R
.
It is sufficient to show this for a principal ideal of
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
.

We start with a generic element

x=\alpha+\beta\psi+\gamma\psi^{*}+\delta\psi\psi^{*}

in
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
and we look at the two-sided ideal
I
generated by
x
.
We will show by direct computation that this is equivalent to the ideal
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)\subseteq R
.
The following elements belong to

\displaystyle\alpha\psi

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\psi x\psi^{*}\psi,

\displaystyle\alpha\psi^{*}

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\psi^{*}\psi x\psi^{*},

\displaystyle\alpha\psi\psi^{*}

\displaystyle=\psi x\psi^{*},

\displaystyle\beta\psi

\displaystyle=r^{-2}\psi\psi^{*}x\psi^{*}\psi,

\displaystyle\beta\psi^{*}

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\psi^{*}x\psi^{*},

\displaystyle\beta\psi\psi^{*}

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\psi\psi^{*}x\psi^{*},

\displaystyle\gamma\psi

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\psi x\psi,

\displaystyle\gamma\psi^{*}

\displaystyle=r^{-2}\psi^{*}\psi x\psi\psi^{*},

\displaystyle\gamma\psi\psi^{*}

\displaystyle=r^{-1}\psi x\psi\psi^{*}.

Consequently, we have that

, and since

\delta\psi\in I
.
It is easy to see that

.
Finally, we have

.

The Jacobson radical of a (not necessarily commutative) ring

is the intersection of all maximal left ideals of
S
.
As maximal two-sided ideals are necessarily maximal left ideals, we have that the Jacobson radical of

is contained in the intersection of all two-sided ideals of

Thus if the Jacobson radical of

,
[

Therefore, if

is semisimple then

is semisimple as well.

For the converse we assume

is not a semisimple ring, and so

is not a semisimple (rank

R
-module.
Consider the left
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})

\pi
, with basis

. Then
\pi\cong R+\psi R
with
\psi^{*}R=0
and
\psi^{*}\psi R=rR
.
Since

, we have

for every
R
-module

, and hence,
\Cl_{r}(R\oplus R^{*})
is not a semisimple ring.
∎

Indeed, we are effectively splitting the natural adjoint representation of the center into its irreducible (
1
dimensional) modules and taking the corresponding Clifford algebra
\Cl(\mathbf{k}\oplus\mathbf{k}^{*})
on each such module.
Compare this with
4.5
.

Example 6.2
.

Consider the case
n=1
and
k=1
.
The isomorphism
\vartheta\colon\Cl_{q}(1,1)\to\Cl_{(1)}\times\Cl_{(1)}
from
6.1
is given explicitly on basis elements by

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle\mapsto(1,1),

\displaystyle\omega_{1}^{m}

\displaystyle\mapsto\bigl((1-q^{-m})v_{1}v_{1}^{*}+q^{-m},(-1)^{m}(1-q^{-m})v_{1}v_{1}^{*}+(-1)^{m}q^{-m}\bigr),

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto(v_{1},-v_{1}),

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\omega_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto(q^{-1}v,q^{-1}v_{1}),

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{*}

\displaystyle\mapsto(v_{1}^{*},-v_{1}^{*}),

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1}

\displaystyle\mapsto(v_{1}^{*},v_{1}^{*}),

for
m=1,2,3
.
For fixed
x=\pm 1
, the morphism
\vartheta_{x}
is surjective but not injective since
\vartheta_{1}(\psi_{1}^{*}-x\psi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1})=0
.
However, looking at,
e.g.
,
(\psi_{1}^{*},\psi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1})
, we get the discrete Fourier transform matrix
\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}
.
We can see that

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}(v_{1},0)

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}-q\psi_{1}\omega_{1},

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}(v_{1}^{*},0)

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}^{*}-\psi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1},

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}(0,v_{1})

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}+q\psi_{1}\omega_{1},

\displaystyle\vartheta^{-1}(0,v_{1}^{*})

\displaystyle=\psi_{1}^{*}+\psi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1},

is coming from the inverse discrete Fourier transform matrix.

We continue to examine the case
n=1
and
k=1
in a bit more detail.
Let
\Cl_{a,b}
denote the Clifford algebra given by the quadratic form with signature
(a,b)
; that is, the form whose Hessian matrix is diagonal with
a
entries being
1
followed by
b
entries being
-1
.
We can change the basis in
\Cl_{(1)}:=\langle v,v^{*}\rangle
by writing

(6.2)

\xi=v+v^{*},\qquad\qquad\xi^{*}=v-v^{*},

which satisfy

\xi^{2}=1,\qquad(\xi^{*})^{2}=-1,\qquad\xi\xi^{*}=1-2vv^{*},\qquad\xi\xi^{*}+\xi^{*}\xi=0.

(Note
\xi=\epsilon_{2}
and
\xi^{*}=\epsilon_{1}
from
Equation

2.5
.)
Thus,
\Cl_{(1)}\cong\Cl_{1,1}
, where the inverse isomorphism is given by

(6.3)

v=\frac{\xi+\xi^{*}}{2},\qquad\qquad v^{*}=\frac{\xi-\xi^{*}}{2},\qquad\qquad vv^{*}=\frac{1-\xi\xi^{*}}{2}.

Next, we have
\Cl_{2,1}\cong\Cl_{1,1}\times\Cl_{1,1}
as
\mathbf{k}
-algebras by the isomorphism

e_{1}\mapsto(\xi,\xi),\qquad\qquad e_{2}\mapsto(\xi\xi^{*},-\xi\xi^{*}),\qquad\qquad e_{3}\mapsto(\xi^{*},\xi^{*}),

where
e_{1}^{2}=e_{2}^{2}=1
and
e_{3}^{2}=-1
.
Note that this is not a filtered
\mathbf{k}
-algebra isomorphism under the natural filtrations.
Hence, we have an isomorphism
\Cl_{q}(1,1)\cong\Cl_{2,1}
as
\mathbf{k}
-algebras.
We can write out this isomorphism explicitly:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{e_{1}+e_{3}}{2}&=(v,v)=q\psi_{1}\omega_{1},&\frac{e_{1}-e_{3}}{2}&=(v^{*},v^{*})=\psi_{1}^{*}\omega_{1},\\
\frac{e_{1}e_{2}+e_{2}e_{3}}{2}&=(-v^{*},v^{*})=-\psi_{1},&\frac{e_{1}e_{2}-e_{2}e_{3}}{2}&=(v,-v)=\psi_{1},\end{aligned}

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \omega_{1}^{m}&=\left(\frac{(q^{-m}-1)\xi\xi^{*}+q^{-m}+1}{2},(-1)^{m}\frac{(q^{-m}-1)\xi\xi^{*}+q^{-m}+1}{2}\right)\\
&=\begin{cases}\frac{q^{-m}-1}{2}e_{1}e_{3}+\frac{q^{-m}+1}{2}&\text{if }m=2,\\
\frac{q^{-m}-1}{2}e_{2}-\frac{q^{-m}+1}{2}e_{1}e_{2}e_{3}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{aligned}

For general
n
, with
k=1
, we note that all of the other relations are supercommuting relations with considering
\omega_{a}
as an even element and
\psi_{a}
and
\psi_{a}^{*}
as odd elements.
Therefore, we can form an isomorphism by taking the
n
-fold supersymmetric product of
\Cl_{2,1}
with
e_{2}
being an even element and
e_{1},e_{3}
being odd elements.
However, this is not an isomorphism of supercommutative superalgebras.
