Optical Emission from Light-like and Particle-like Excitons in Monolayer Transition Metal Dichalcogenides Preprint: APS/123-QED
Mikkel Ohm Sauer 1,2, Carl Emil Mørch Nielsen 1, Lars Merring-Mikkelsen 1, Thomas Garm Pedersen 1,2

Abstract
Several monolayer transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) are direct band gap semiconductors and potentially efficient emitters in light emitting devices. Photons are emitted when strongly bound excitons decay radiatively, and accurate models of such excitons are important for a full understanding of the emission. Importantly, photons are emitted in directions uniquely determined by the exciton center of mass momentum and with lifetimes determined by the exciton transition matrix element. The exciton band structures of two-dimensional hexagonal materials, including TMDs, are highly unusual with coexisting particle- and light-like bands. The latter is non-analytic with emission selection rules essentially opposite to the particle-like states, but has been ignored in analyses of TMD light emission so far. In the present work, we analyse the temperature and angular dependence of light emission from both exciton species and point out several important consequences of the unique exciton band structure. Within a first-principles Density-Functional-Theory+Bethe-Salpeter-Equation framework, we compute exciton band structures and optical matrix elements for the important TMDs MoS 2 , MoSe 2 , WS 2 , and WSe 2 . At low temperature, only the particle-like band is populated and our results agree with previous work. However, at slightly elevated temperatures, a significant population of the light-like band leads to modified angular emission patterns and lifetimes. Clear experimental fingerprints are predicted and explained by a simple four-state model incorporating spin-orbit as well as intervalley exchange coupling.

The distinct radiative properties of light- and particle-like TMD excitons are readily understood from simple physical principles. Primarily, whenever an exciton decays by emission of a photon, momentum conservation dictates that the in-plane exciton momentum
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
is transferred to the photon. In particular, slow and fast excitons emit photons in normal and oblique directions, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. It follows that the thermal population of states with different
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
is responsible for the emission pattern. The
K-K^{\prime}
symmetry of TMDs requires light- and particle-like excitons to be degenerate at
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}=0
. However, at finite
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
two distinct bands are formed as seen in the insets in Fig.
1
, with approximately parabolic and linear dispersion at small
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
. Accordingly, the kinetic energy of particle-like states with parabolic dispersions is much lower than that of light-like species at small
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
. In turn, a thermal population favouring particle-like states is expected at low temperature
T
while near equal populations should exist at elevated temperatures. The energy difference between the bands is due to exchange effects on the order of 10 meV. It follows that an estimate of the characteristic temperature is around 100 K. Accordingly, significantly different exciton populations are expected at low and room temperature.

A secondary effect is related to the different rates of spontaneous emission of the two exciton species. A high emission rate compared to competing non-radiative mechanisms is crucial for efficient emission. Fundamentally, the rate of spontaneous emission is determined by two factors: transition dipole moment and available phase space. The transition dipole moment is an intrinsically quantum mechanical quantity proportional to the exciton transition momentum matrix element
\bm{\mathrm{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{Q}})
. We note that the term ”momentum” appears in two distinct contexts: the center of mass momentum
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
that is simply a conserved quantum number and the momentum matrix element
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
that is a transition amplitude
\braket{0|\bm{\hat{\mathrm{P}}}|n}
taken between the ground state
0
and
n
’th exciton state. To avoid confusion, we will henceforth denote
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
by the transition matrix element. An accurate calculation of
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
requires inclusion of exciton Coulomb and exchange effects. In the present work, an
ab initio
Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE) approach combined with accurate density-functional theory (DFT) band structures are applied to this end. The available phase space refers to the result that photon momenta
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
are tightly restricted by geometrical effects. As mentioned above, the in-plane photon momentum is directly determined by the exciton momentum. In turn, under emission into free space, the out-of-plane component is uniquely specified by the photon dispersion relation
q=\omega/c
. Here, the photon frequency
\omega
follows from energy conservation, i.e.
\hbar\omega=E(\bm{\mathrm{Q}})
, where
E(\bm{\mathrm{Q}})
is the exciton energy. Together, these relations impose severe restrictions on the photon modes available for emission. For instance, no emission can take place if
Q>\omega/c
. In fact, phase space is restricted even further by the requirement of photon transversality. This requirement means that emission rates are proportional to
\sin^{2}\varphi
, where
\varphi
is the angle between
\bm{\mathrm{q}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
. We demonstrate in the present work that light-like and particle-like excitons are characterised by transition matrix element that are almost entirely parallel and perpendicular to
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
, respectively. Hence, different available phase spaces are found for the two exciton bands leading to differences in their emission rates. Strikingly, at grazing angles
Q\sim\omega/c
, emission from light-like excitons is forbidden while particle-like states remain fully bright. We evaluate and analyse all these effects below and provide concrete suggestions and predictions for experimental verification of our findings via the angular and thermal behaviour of emission spectra.

Exciton band structures in TMDs have previously been described within the DFT+BSE framework
Deilmann and Thygesen 2019
;
Qiu et al. 2015
and a similar approach is applied here. In particular, we use a strictly two-dimensional (2D) model of exciton screening incorporated via the Keldysh potential
Keldysh 1979
. Such a 2D approach has been found to successfully describe particle- and light-like bands
Qiu et al. 2015
. Note, though, that the approach by Deilmann
et al.

Deilmann and Thygesen 2019
adopts a 3D screening model, and consequently does not observe the light-like band. While the 3D screening approach is justifiable for TMDs in a 3D environment
article:Pedersen_2016
, it fails to describe intrinsic 2D properties because of the applied truncation of Coulomb interactions in the perpendicular direction to eliminate unphysical coupling between periodically repeated layers
Deilmann and Thygesen 2019
. We calculate
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
-dependent transition matrix elements that, in turn, yield the decay rate of emission via Fermi’s golden rule. Subsequently, we thermally average over the exciton bands to obtain average lifetimes, yielding the temperature dependence of both lifetime and emission profiles. A similar approach was applied by Palummo
et al.
for calculating the radiative lifetimes of the four TMDs
Palummo et al. 2015
. However, their model is based on an effective mass approximation for the exciton dispersion, meaning that only the particle-like band is included. These authors find room temperature radiative lifetimes in the few hundreds of ps range, significantly lower than experiments. Longer radiative lifetimes have been obtained by Wang
et al.

Wang et al. 2016
, modelling the radiative lifetimes of excitons and trions using a modified Wannier-Mott approach neglecting, however, long range exchange interaction between the
K
- and
K^{\prime}
-valleys
Wang et al. 2016
and, hence, only including the particle-like band. Still, these authors predict radiative exciton lifetimes for MoS
2
to be about one nanosecond at room temperature. In addition, the radiative exciton lifetimes follow a quasi-linear temperature dependence in agreement with the present results, provided we only consider particle-like states. In the present work, we apply a highly accurate DFT+BSE approach to determine the role of light-like bands in radiative exciton lifetimes. We show that a nonlinear temperature dependence of the emission is expected due to the different thermal populations of particle- and light-like bands. Moreover, the unique band structure produces clear fingerprints in the angular spectrum of radiated light. These, for instance, become evident when comparing spectra at low and high temperatures. Finally, our effective four-state model clarifies the important roles of SO splitting and the inter- and intravalley exchange couplings. In particular, emission is found to be highly sensitive to the competition between SO and exchange couplings.

II
Exciton Band Structures

First-principles BSE exciton band structures are numerically demanding because resolution corresponding to optical momenta is required. For this reason, we have developed a computationally efficient approach, in which a limited set of bands is retained and interpolation between
\vec{Q}
-points is applied to acquire exciton energies and transition matrix elements. Briefly, we solve the BSE based on DFT band structures obtained using the GPAW-package
Mortensen et al. 2005
;
Enkovaara et al. 2010
;
Yan et al. 2011
;
Larsen et al. 2017
;
Bahn and Jacobsen 2002
. We scissor-shift the DFT band gap, such that the lowest bright excitation matches the
A
-peak of the experimental spectrum measured by Hsu
et al.

Hsu et al. 2019
. The BSE is then constructed from four valence- and four conduction bands. Band states are two-component spinors due to SO coupling but we define the BSE matrix in a basis of definite spin projections
n_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}
with band index
n
, wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
and spin
\sigma
. The BSE matrix is constructed for a
60\times 60

k
-point grid covering the full Brillouin zone. We apply the Keldysh potential
Keldysh 1979
for the Coulomb interaction, such that the potential in Fourier
\vec{q}
-space is modelled as
w_{C}(\vec{q})=v_{\text{bare}}(\vec{q})/(\varepsilon+r_{0}q)
where
v_{\text{bare}}(\vec{q})
is the bare 2D Coulomb interaction in Fourier space,
\varepsilon
the external dielectric screening found by averaging sub- and superstrate dielectric constants, and
r_{0}
the screening length given as
r_{0}=2\pi\chi_{0,xy}
. Here,
\chi_{0,xy}
is the in-plane static sheet polarisability obtained directly from the DFT calculation. This approach has proven effective at describing the screening of the Coulomb interaction in TMDs
Trolle et al. 2017
;
Ridolfi et al. 2018
. Furthermore, only external charges screen the exchange term that is given as
w_{x}(\vec{q})=v_{\text{bare}}(\vec{q})/\varepsilon

Benedict 2002
;
article:Louie_2021_exchange
. Finally, the center of mass momentum
\vec{Q}
is handled by adding it to the
\vec{k}
-vector such that a conduction band state
c_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}
is replaced by
c_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\vec{Q},\sigma}
, while the valence band states
v_{\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}
are unchanged. However, momenta
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
and
\vec{Q}
will be restricted to the same grid unless additional DFT calculations for wave vectors
\vec{k}+\vec{Q}
are made for every
\vec{Q}
not in the
k
-point grid. Thus, to increase resolution to the optical momentum range, a
60\times 60
conduction band
k
-grid shifted by
\vec{Q}
is obtained by interpolation from a fixed
120\times 120
point dense grid, using a 4th-degree polynomial spline scheme. This approach ensures that any momentum
\vec{Q}
can be resolved, without the need for additional expensive DFT calculations. As a consequence of the added center of mass momentum, the transition matrix elements are calculated as

\bm{\mathrm{P}}=\frac{A}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{v,c,\sigma}\int\Psi_{v,\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}^{c,\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\vec{Q},\sigma}\braket{v_{\vec{k},\sigma}|\bm{\hat{\mathrm{p}}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})|c_{\vec{k}+\vec{Q},\sigma}}d^{2}\bm{\mathrm{k}},

(1)

where
A
is the TMD area,
\Psi_{v,\bm{\mathrm{k}},\sigma}^{c,\bm{\mathrm{k}}+\vec{Q},\sigma^{\prime}}
the exciton wave function in the basis of valence-conduction band transitions with definite spin projection
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
, and
\vec{q}
the photon momentum. Also,
\bm{\hat{\mathrm{p}}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})
is the momentum operator given as

\bm{\hat{\mathrm{p}}}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\left\{\nabla\text{e}^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}+\text{e}^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}\nabla\right\},

(2)

and we exploited the fact that matrix elements of
\bm{\hat{\mathrm{p}}}
are diagonal in spin.

Generally, exciton transition matrix elements depend on both the magnitude and direction of
\vec{Q}
. In Fig.
2
a and b, this dependence is shown for the particle- and light-like bands of MoS
2
, respectively. Fortunately, as demonstrated by Fig.
2
a and b, there is little angular dependence for both energy dispersion and transition matrix elements within the thermally allowed
Q
-range at room temperature. This is a consequence of the threefold symmetry of TMDs, which for low values of
Q
deviates only slightly from circular symmetry, allowing us to significantly simplify the calculations of the emission properties by only requiring
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
in one dimension. Furthermore, it can be observed that the transition matrix elements are always practically perpendicular to the
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
-vector in the lower energy band, and parallel to the
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
-vector in the upper energy band. This is a consequence of hexagonal symmetry in TMDs. In Sec.
IV
, we present a rigorous analysis of the symmetry properties including exchange and SO coupling. However, a very simple physical picture can be constructed if SO coupling is temporarily ignored. Firstly, at
\vec{Q}=0
, the
K/K^{\prime}
-valley excitons
\ket{K}
and
\ket{K^{\prime}}
are degenerate as required by time reversal symmetry. At finite
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
, the degeneracy is lifted by intervalley exchange
v_{x}
. To first-order in
\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}
perturbation theory, intervalley exchange is (see App. A)

\braket{K|v_{x}|K^{\prime}}\propto w_{x}(Q)(\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{K}}}^{*})(\bm{\mathrm{Q}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{K^{\prime}}}}),

(3)

where
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}=\braket{v_{\vec{k},\sigma}|\bm{\hat{\mathrm{p}}}(0)|c_{\vec{k},\sigma}}
is an interband transition matrix element. Because
w_{x}(Q)\propto 1/Q
, light-like energies increasing by
2|\braket{K|v_{x}|K^{\prime}}|
form a linear exciton band while particle-like energies are unperturbed to first order. This essentially explains the characteristic band structure. Next, the approximate circular symmetry around the
K/K^{\prime}
-points allows us to choose
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
along
x
. If we introduce the chiral unit vectors
\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{\pm}\equiv(\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}\pm i\bm{\hat{\mathrm{y}}})/\sqrt{2}
then, with an appropriate choice of phase,
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{K}}=p_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-}
and
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{K^{\prime}}}}=-p_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{+}
, where the minus sign is dictated by time reversal symmetry and
p_{0}
is real-valued
Taghizadeh and Pedersen 2019
. Note that choosing
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}=Q\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
makes intervalley exchange coupling real-valued as well, so that the coupled states are simply
\ket{p}=(\ket{K}+\ket{K^{\prime}})/\sqrt{2}
and
\ket{l}=(\ket{K}-\ket{K^{\prime}})/\sqrt{2}
for particle- and light-like states, respectively. It follows immediately that
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{p}\propto\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{K}}+\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{K^{\prime}}}
and
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{l}\propto\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{K}}-\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{K^{\prime}}}
, which are clearly parallel and perpendicular to
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
, accordingly. Thus, the distinct transition matrix elements for the two bands are explained by simple symmetry arguments. In Sec.
IV
, we demonstrate that identical conclusions are reached even if SO coupling is retained. This important conclusion has also been verified by direct comparison to the numerical BSE solution for MoS
2
. In addition, due to the identical parity of the valence band maximum (VBM) and conduction band minimum (CBM), the
z
-components of the transition matrix elements are approximately zero, only differing slightly due to the out-of-plane photon momentum.

In Fig.
2

\Gamma\rightarrow M
for the two lowest bright states in the four TMDs studied, including two cases of MoS
2
screened by a dielectric environment. The particle- and light-like bands are readily identified, and once again show either a parallel or perpendicular projection of the transition matrix elements on the
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
-vector. In addition, the difference in energy between the two bands increases linearly for small values of
Q
, and quickly settles at 10-20 meV around
Q\simeq 0.03

\text{Å}^{-1}
. The magnitude of the transition matrix elements for the light-like band decreases faster with increasing
Q
than the particle-like band. In particular, this happens when the energy of the light-like exciton approaches the energies of higher energy exciton states, due to the intervalley exchange. For the particle-like parabolic band, the exciton effective mass can be extracted from from a polynomial fit as seen in Table
1
. For free-standing MoS
2
, our results are in good agreement with previous values
Deilmann and Thygesen 2019
;
Qiu et al. 2015
. Figure
2

2
, namely with dielectric constants corresponding to quartz substrates and hBN encapsulation. Notably, it can be seen that the transition matrix elements increase slightly in a screened environment. This is counter-intuitive because a decreased electron-hole overlap is expected to reduce oscillator strength within a Wannier model. However, as demonstrated in the effective model presented in Sec.
IV

Radiative lifetimes are modelled using Fermi’s golden rule
Chen et al. 2019
, which yields a momentum-resolved radiative rate for 2D materials given by

\begin{split}\Gamma(\bm{\mathrm{Q}})&=\frac{e^{2}}{2m_{e}^{2}A\varepsilon_{0}\hbar\omega c}\sum_{q_{x},q_{y}}\delta_{q_{x},Q_{x}}\delta_{q_{y},Q_{y}}\\
&\times\int{\bm{\mathrm{P}}^{*}\cdot(\bm{\mathrm{I}}-\bm{\hat{\mathrm{q}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{q}}})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{P}}}\,\delta\left(q-\textstyle\frac{\omega}{c}\right)\,dq_{z}.\end{split}

(4)

Here, the Kronecker deltas ensure in-plane momentum conservation and the Dirac delta function
\delta\left(q-\textstyle\frac{\omega}{c}\right)
arises from the requirement of energy conservation. Defining the emission angle
\theta
as the angle to the surface normal, the exciton and photon momenta in the light cone are related via
Q=q\sin\theta
and
q_{z}=q\cos\theta
. Furthermore, Eq. (
4
) can be generalised to a 2D emitter encapsulated in a linear isotropic material, by replacing
\varepsilon_{0}\rightarrow n\varepsilon_{0}
and
\omega/c\rightarrow\omega n/c
in the Dirac delta function, with
n
being the refractive index of the medium. The exciton transition matrix elements
\bm{\mathrm{P}}
are calculated from the BSE eigenstates using Eq. (
1
). The integral is evaluated for exciton transition matrix elements either parallel
\bm{\mathrm{P}}\parallel\vec{Q}
or perpendicular
\bm{\mathrm{P}}\perp\vec{Q}
, thus yielding two cases

\begin{split}\Gamma(\vec{Q})&=\Gamma_{0}(\vec{Q})\begin{cases}\int_{Q}^{\infty}\frac{q_{z}}{q}\delta\left(q-\frac{\omega}{c}\right)dq&\bm{\mathrm{P}}\parallel\vec{Q}\\
\int_{Q}^{\infty}\frac{q}{q_{z}}\delta\left(q-\frac{\omega}{c}\right)dq&\bm{\mathrm{P}}\perp\vec{Q}\end{cases}\\
&=\Gamma_{0}(\vec{Q})\Theta\left(\textstyle\frac{\omega}{c}-Q\right)\begin{cases}\cos\theta&\bm{\mathrm{P}}\parallel\vec{Q}\\
1/\cos\theta&\bm{\mathrm{P}}\perp\vec{Q}\end{cases},\end{split}

(5)

where
\Gamma_{0}(\vec{Q})=\frac{e^{2}||\bm{\mathrm{P}}(\vec{Q})||^{2}}{m_{e}^{2}A\varepsilon_{0}\hbar\omega c}
and
\Theta(x)
is the Heaviside step function. The first and second of these cases apply to the light- and particle-like bands, respectively. Finally, by assuming a quasi-thermal distribution of excitons, and thus thermally averaging this result, one arrives at an expression for the intrinsic mean radiative rate

\left<\Gamma\right>=\frac{1}{\sum_{n}Z_{n}}\sum_{n}\sum_{|\bm{\mathrm{Q}}|<\omega/c}\Gamma_{n}(\vec{Q})\text{e}^{-E_{n}(\vec{Q})/k_{B}T},

(6)

where
Z_{n}
is the partition function and
E_{n}(\vec{Q})
the exciton energy of state
n
with center of mass momentum
\vec{Q}
. Here, we limit
n
to include only the bright particle- and light-like excitons (
n=p,l
) since radiation does not directly interact with the dark excitons, and thus they are not expected to be significantly populated. In the thermal average, the
Q
-dependent quantities are the exciton energy

IV
Analytical Model

The essence of the peculiar emission properties of TMDs is the presence of particle- and light-like excitons with transition matrix elements perpendicular and parallel to their motion, respectively. We now show that these features are captured by a simple analytical model. We include both exchange and SO coupling in the analysis. Furthermore, we show that a variational ansatz for the exciton allows for a quantitative estimate of the slope in linear light-like bands. To this end, we exploit the fact that states localized near the
K
- or
K^{\prime}
-valleys are responsible for the
A
and
B
resonances. Eight such states exist due to the presence of two valleys as well as two spins for both valence and conduction bands, i.e.
\sigma_{v},\sigma_{c}\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
. They can be grouped into spin-preserving, optically bright singlets
\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}},\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}},\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}},\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}
and spin-flipping, dark triplets
\ket{K_{\downarrow\uparrow}},\ket{K_{\uparrow\downarrow}},\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\uparrow}},\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\downarrow}}
. Here,
K_{\sigma_{v}\sigma_{c}}
is a Wannier-like exciton localized to the
K
-valley and similarly for the
K^{\prime}
-valley. The triplets can be omitted from the model since there is no SO-hybridisation in a two-band model with states of equal parity and, thus, no coupling to the bright singlets. At
Q=0
, the singlet
A
and
B
excitons have energies
E_{A}
and
E_{B}
, respectively, with
E_{B}-E_{A}=2\Delta_{SO}
the SO splitting. Without exchange effects, the center of mass motion simply adds a kinetic energy
\frac{\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{2M}
, where
M
is the sum of the hole and electron effective masses. In addition to SO coupling, we now include the exchange interaction leading to coupling between isolated exciton states. The intra- and intervalley exchange matrix elements are
V_{x}=V_{x}^{KK}=V_{x}^{K^{\prime}K^{\prime}}
and
v_{x}=V_{x}^{KK^{\prime}}=V_{x}^{K^{\prime}K*}
, respectively. Furthermore, by choosing the center of mass momentum along
x
so that
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}=Q\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
we ensure that
v_{x}
is real, as shown in App.
A
. Here, identities under interchange of valleys follow from time reversal symmetry. In this manner, the
4\times 4
Hamiltonian in the singlet basis including exchange coupling is
E_{0}(Q)\bm{\mathrm{I}}+\bm{\mathrm{H}}
with
E_{0}(Q)=\frac{1}{2}(E_{A}+E_{B})+\frac{\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{2M}
and

\bm{\mathrm{H}}=\begin{bmatrix}-\Delta_{SO}&V_{x}&v_{x}&v_{x}\\
V_{x}&\Delta_{SO}&v_{x}&v_{x}\\
v_{x}&v_{x}&\Delta_{SO}&V_{x}\\
v_{x}&v_{x}&V_{x}&-\Delta_{SO}\end{bmatrix}.

(8)

In App.
A
, we show that the intervalley exchange interaction is proportional to the center of mass momentum
v_{x}\propto Q
for low values of
Q
. In principle, an intervalley Coulomb coupling
v_{C}
also exists between the two valleys. However, as demonstrated in App.
A
,
v_{C}\propto Q^{2}
for small values of
Q
, and we shall approximate it as
v_{C}=0
. The energies found by diagonalising Eq.(
8
) are then

\begin{split}E_{A_{p}}(Q)&=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}+v_{x}-\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}},\\
E_{A_{l}}(Q)&=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}-v_{x}-\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}},\\
E_{B_{p}}(Q)&=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}+v_{x}+\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}},\\
E_{B_{l}}(Q)&=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}-v_{x}+\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}},\end{split}

(9)

where
\alpha=\frac{V_{x}-v_{x}}{2\Delta_{SO}}
and
\beta=\frac{V_{x}+v_{x}}{2\Delta_{SO}}
. The signs in front of
\Delta_{SO}
and
v_{x}
identify eigenvalues as belonging to the
A/B
-peaks and
p/l
-bands as indicated by the subscripts. In the
Q=0
limit, we see that
E_{A_{p}}(0)=E_{A_{l}}(0)=E_{A}
and
E_{B_{p}}(0)=E_{B_{l}}(0)=E_{B}
. In addition, as shown in App.
A
, for small
Q
we can write
v_{x}=-v_{0}Q
and
V_{x}=V_{0}+v_{0}Q
, where
v_{0}
and
V_{0}
are positive real-valued constants. Finally, in the case of large
\Delta_{SO}
, the exciton dispersions can be approximated as

\begin{split}E_{A_{p}}(Q)&=E_{A}+\frac{\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{2M},\\
E_{A_{l}}(Q)&=E_{A}+\hbar QV_{-}+\frac{\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{2M_{-}},\\
E_{B_{p}}(Q)&=E_{B}+\frac{\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{2M},\\
E_{B_{l}}(Q)&=E_{B}+\hbar QV_{+}+\frac{\hbar^{2}Q^{2}}{2M_{+}},\end{split}

(10)

where we introduce the effective velocities
V_{\pm}=2\frac{v_{0}}{\hbar}\left(1\pm\frac{V_{0}}{\Delta_{SO}}\right)
and masses
M_{\pm}=M\left(1\pm\frac{4v_{0}^{2}M}{\hbar^{2}\Delta_{SO}}\right)^{-1}
. This demonstrates that particle-like bands are parabolic while light-like bands contain a linear term. Note, also, that both effective masses and velocities of the light-like bands are affected by SO coupling such that the
A
and
B
light-like excitons have distinct band slopes and curvatures. Again, this finding agrees with our first-principles results.

The exact eigenvectors of Eq. (
8
) are given in App.
B
but, in the limit
\Delta_{SO}\gg V_{x}+|v_{x}|
, they can be approximated as

\begin{split}\Ket{A_{p}}&=2^{-1/2}\left(\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-\beta\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}-\beta\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}\right),\\
\Ket{A_{l}}&=2^{-1/2}\left(\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-\alpha\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}-\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+\alpha\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}\right),\\
\Ket{B_{p}}&=2^{-1/2}\left(\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+\beta\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}+\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}+\beta\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\right),\\
\Ket{B_{l}}&=2^{-1/2}\left(\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+\alpha\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-\alpha\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\right).\end{split}

(11)

The SO splitting
2\Delta_{SO}
is substantial (
\approx 0.6\,\text{eV}
) in tungsten-based TMDs and significantly smaller (
\approx 0.2\,\text{eV}
) in molybdenum compounds
Haastrup et al. 2018
. Thus, in the former case,
|\alpha|,|\beta|\ll 1
and eigenstates are approximately
\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}\pm\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}
or
\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\pm\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}
, all of which yield bright excitons, c.f. Fig.
6
. On the other hand, for the molybdenum based TMDs, where
\Delta_{SO}
is significantly smaller, the
A
-peak eigenstates will tend towards a triplet state, making them less bright. Thus, the exchange term implies that molybdenum based TMDs show a more pronounced hybridisation between the two spin-conserving transitions at the
K
-symmetry points. This is important since the exchange term is particularly affected by external screening. It follows that hybridisation will be reduced in a dielectric environment impacting, in turn, significantly excitonic states and transition matrix elements.

The appearance of strictly parallel- and perpendicular exciton transition matrix elements can now be demonstrated in the limit of small
Q
, where the time reversal symmetry is upheld. The transition matrix elements for the band transitions of TMDs at the
K
and
K^{\prime}
high symmetry points are
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{K}}=p_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-}
and
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\bm{\mathrm{K^{\prime}}}}=-p_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{+}
as derived by Taghizadeh
et al.

Taghizadeh and Pedersen 2019
. Here,
\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{\pm}\equiv(\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}\pm i\bm{\hat{\mathrm{y}}})/\sqrt{2}
are the chiral unit vectors introduced earlier, and the sign follows from time reversal symmetry. For
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
in the neighborhood of
\bm{\mathrm{K}}
, we write
\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\approx p_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-}
. The corresponding exciton matrix element for the Wannier-like excitons in the
K
-valley is then approximated as

\begin{split}\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\vec{K}}(\vec{Q})\approx\frac{A}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\bm{\mathrm{K}})\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}d^{2}\bm{\mathrm{k}}\approx P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-},\end{split}

(12)

where
\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K})
is the Wannier wave function for an isolated exciton in the
K
valley with center of mass momentum
Q=0
. For the other valley,
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{\vec{K^{\prime}}}(\vec{Q})\approx-P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{+}
. Now, by approximating the transition matrix elements for the Wannier-like excitons of different spin, we find
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\approx P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-}
and
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\approx-P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{+}
. Thus, combining the eigenvectors with the transition matrix elements yields the exciton transition matrix elements

\begin{split}\vec{P}_{A_{p}}&=-i(1-\beta)P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{y}}},\\
\vec{P}_{A_{l}}&=(1-\alpha)P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}},\\
\vec{P}_{B_{p}}&=-i(1+\beta)P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{y}}},\\
\vec{P}_{B_{l}}&=(1+\alpha)P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}.\end{split}

(13)

That is, the exciton transition matrix element of the light-like eigenstates will be parallel to
\vec{Q}=Q\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
, while for the particle-like eigenstates it will be perpendicular. This is illustrated in Fig.
6
, where the exciton wave functions are simple symmetric and anti-symmetric superposition of the
K
and
K^{\prime}
states. We stress that, under the assumption of circular symmetry, this is general for any direction of
\vec{Q}
. Furthermore, the exciton transition matrix elements will be reduced as
V_{x}+v_{x}
approaches
\Delta_{SO}
for the
A
-peak excitons, while they increase slightly for the
B
-peak excitons, due to the different signs in front of
\alpha
and
\beta
. In turn, this means that substrate screening can have a significant effect on the transition matrix elements. Our numerical calculations of the absolute squared transition matrix element
||\vec{P}||^{2}
for TMDs on a quartz substrate show a
30\%
increase for MoS
2
and MoSe
2
, while they show a mere
2\%
increase for the two tungsten based TMDs.

To compare this analytical model to the
ab initio
model, we shall apply a simple estimate using the variational ansatz for the Wannier-like exciton in position space

\psi_{0}(\vec{r})=N(\text{e}^{-ar}-b\text{e}^{-\gamma ar}),

(14)

which has proven effective at describing the ground exciton state in MoS
2

Pedersen 2016
. The parameter
N
is a normalisation constant, and the parameters
a
,
b
and
\gamma
are found by minimising the energy in a Wannier model including the Keldysh potential. In Fourier space, this wave function is

\psi_{0}(\vec{k})=N\left(\frac{a}{(a^{2}+k^{2})^{3/2}}-\frac{b\gamma a}{(\gamma^{2}a^{2}+k^{2})^{3/2}}\right).

(15)

To calculate the value of

, we need the transition matrix elements and energies for the band transitions, which can obtained from the DFT calculation. Applying these in equation (

), gives a value of

. To calculate the value of

, we need to include all the

-vectors of Eq. (

), and average it over our variational ansatz
\psi_{0}

V_{0}=\int\int\psi_{0}^{*}(\bm{\mathrm{k}})v_{x}(\vec{k},\vec{k}^{\prime},Q=0)\psi_{0}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}^{\prime})d^{2}\vec{k}d^{2}\vec{k}^{\prime}.

(16)

Applying our DFT calculation again, we obtain a value of
V_{0}=27.4\,\text{meV}
. Using these values to calculate the dispersion for MoS

then yields the plot of Fig.

, which shows good agreement between the two approaches in the limit of small perturbation

In fact, a quantitative description of exciton band structures and transition matrix elements can be obtained in this manner, without actually solving the BSE.

V
Summary

In summary, we have studied excitons and their optical emission in the monolayer TMDs MoS
2
, MoSe
2
, WS
2
, and WSe
2
using a first-principles DFT+BSE approach. Our emphasis is on excitons moving with a finite center of mass momentum and their effect on emission. In TMDs, exciton energies form characteristic particle- and light-like bands. The latter has previously been ignored but we demonstrate that clear experimental fingerprints are expected in both temperature and angular dependence of the emission. In particular, optical selection rules relating motion and emission directions are found to be essentially opposite for particle- and light-like excitons. Signatures of light-like excitons appear due the different thermal populations of the exciton bands combined with their selection rules. Moreover, we describe the effects of screening by a dielectric environment on both exciton band structure and optical emission. Our results are in good agreement with available experimental radiative lifetimes. Finally, a simple analytical model including exchange and spin-orbit coupling of valley excitons is shown to capture the essential physics.

Appendix A
Intervalley Exchange and Coulomb Interaction

To find an expression for the
K-K^{\prime}
exciton exchange interaction, we start from Bloch’s theorem to write the lattice-periodic part of the wave function
u_{n,\vec{k}}(\vec{r})=\text{e}^{-i\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}}\psi_{n,\vec{k}}(\vec{r})
, which is then expanded in 2D Fourier coefficients using the periodicity of the unit cell

u_{n,\vec{k}}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{A}}\sum_{\vec{G}}C_{n,\vec{k}}(\vec{G},z)\text{e}^{i\vec{G}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}},

(17)

where
\vec{\rho}
is a 2D spatial coordinate and
A
the TMD area. Next, products of these are Fourier decomposed, such that

u_{n,\vec{k}}^{*}(\vec{r})u_{m,\vec{k}^{\prime}}(\vec{r})=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{\vec{G}}i_{n\vec{k},m\vec{k}^{\prime}}(\vec{G},z)\text{e}^{i\vec{G}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}},

(18)

where the Fourier coefficients are given as

i_{n\vec{k},m\vec{k}^{\prime}}(\vec{G},z)=\int_{A}u_{n,\vec{k}}^{*}\text{e}^{-i\vec{G}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}}u_{m,\vec{k}^{\prime}}d^{2}\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}=\sum_{\vec{G}^{\prime}}C_{n\vec{k}}^{*}(\vec{G}^{\prime}-\vec{G},z)C_{m\vec{k}^{\prime}}(\vec{G}^{\prime},z).

(19)

Likewise, the Coulomb interaction
\hat{W}(\vec{r},\vec{r}^{\prime})
is Fourier decomposed in the entire 2D-plane

\hat{W}(\vec{r},\vec{r}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{\vec{q},\vec{G}}w(\vec{q}+\vec{G},z,z^{\prime})\text{e}^{i(\vec{q}+\vec{G})\cdot(\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}-\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}^{\prime})},

(20)

where
\vec{q}
is a 2D vector confined to the Brillouin zone, and
w(\vec{q}+\vec{G},z,z^{\prime})
is the Coulomb potential in 2D Fourier space. Taking the 2D limit of the screening, in accordance with the Keldysh approximation, we get

w(\vec{q}+\vec{G},z,z^{\prime})\approx w(\vec{q}+\vec{G},0,0),

(21)

where the TMD has been placed at
z=0
. The elements of the Coulomb interaction are

\begin{split}\braket{1,2|\hat{W}(\vec{r},\vec{r}^{\prime})|3,4}&=\frac{1}{A^{3}}\sum_{\vec{q},\vec{G},\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{1,3},\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{2,4}}w(\vec{q}+\vec{G},0,0)\int_{A}\text{e}^{i\left(\vec{G}_{W}+\vec{G}_{1,3}+\vec{q}+\vec{k}_{3}-\vec{k}_{1}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}}d^{2}\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}i_{1,3}(\vec{G}_{1,3},z)dz\\
&\times\int_{A}\text{e}^{-i\left(\vec{G}-\vec{G}_{2,4}+\vec{q}-\vec{k}_{4}+\vec{k}_{2}\right)\cdot\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}}d^{2}\bm{\mathrm{\rho}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}i_{2,4}(\vec{G}_{2,4},z)dz.\end{split}

(22)

Due to the integrals over the area, the non-zero terms in the sums will have vanishing exponents in the integrand. Furthermore, since
\vec{q}
and
\vec{k}
are restricted to the Brillouin zone, we get
\vec{q}=\vec{k}_{4}-\vec{k}_{2}=\vec{k}_{1}-\vec{k}_{3}
and
\vec{G}=-\vec{G}_{1,3}=\vec{G}_{2,4}
.
We now define
I_{1,2}(\vec{G})\equiv\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}i_{1,2}(\vec{G},z)dz
, such that the Coulomb (
C
) and exchange (
x
) kernels become

\displaystyle v_{C}(\vec{k},\vec{k}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{Q}})

\displaystyle=\braket{v\vec{k}^{\prime},c\vec{k}+\vec{Q}|\hat{W}_{C}(\vec{r},\vec{r}^{\prime})|v\vec{k},c\vec{k}^{\prime}+\vec{Q}}=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{\vec{G}}w_{C}(\vec{k}^{\prime}-\vec{k}+\bm{\mathrm{G}})I_{v\vec{k},v\vec{k}^{\prime}}^{*}(\vec{G})I_{c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},c\vec{k}^{\prime}+\vec{Q}}(\vec{G}),

(23)

\displaystyle v_{x}(\vec{k},\vec{k}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{Q}})

\displaystyle=\braket{v\vec{k},c\vec{k}^{\prime}+\vec{Q}|\hat{W}_{x}(\vec{r},\vec{r}^{\prime})|c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{A}\sum_{\vec{G}}w_{x}(\bm{\mathrm{G}}-\vec{Q})I_{c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}}^{*}(\vec{G})I_{c\vec{k}^{\prime}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}^{\prime}}(\vec{G}),

(24)

where Hermiticity
I_{1,2}(\vec{G})=I_{2,1}^{*}(-\vec{G})
has been applied. Furthermore, we expand Hamilton matrix elements to first order in
\vec{Q}
, such that

H_{\vec{G},\vec{G}^{\prime}}(\vec{k}+\vec{Q})\approx H_{\vec{G},\vec{G}^{\prime}}(\vec{k})+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m_{e}}\vec{Q}\cdot(\vec{k}+\vec{G})\delta_{\vec{G},\vec{G}^{\prime}}.

(25)

Applying the two-band approximation and using first-order perturbation theory for the perturbed conduction states at the
K
and
K^{\prime}
points then yields the plane wave coefficients

C_{c,\vec{k}+\vec{Q}}(\vec{G},z)\approx C_{c,\vec{k}}(\vec{G},z)+\frac{\hbar}{m_{e}}\frac{\vec{Q}\cdot\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}{\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}C_{v,\vec{k}}(\vec{G},z),

(26)

where
\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}=\epsilon_{c,\bm{\mathrm{k}}}-\epsilon_{v,\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
is the band-to-band transition energy and the
z
-component of
\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
is zero. As a consequence, the Coulomb kernel will vary approximately as
Q^{2}
, since the screening is independent of
\bm{\mathrm{Q}}
. In the so called slow-rapid approximation, we neglect all terms except
\vec{G}=0
. This is a valid approximation for the intervalley exchange since only the
\vec{G}=0
term will be proportional to
Q
for small
Q
, while
\vec{G}\neq 0
terms only contribute to order
Q^{2}
and higher as a constant
Q^{0}
contribution would break time reversal symmetry. Using Eq. (
26
) and the orthonormality of wave functions, we get

I_{c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}}(0)\approx\frac{\hbar}{m_{e}}\frac{\vec{Q}\cdot\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}{\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}},

(27)

and the exchange kernel in the slow-rapid approximation then becomes

v_{x}(\vec{k},\vec{k}^{\prime},\bm{\mathrm{Q}})=\frac{1}{A}w_{x}(Q)I_{c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}}^{*}(0)I_{c\vec{k}^{\prime}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}^{\prime}}(0).

(28)

The
K-K^{\prime}
exciton exchange interaction
v_{x}
is, thus, given by

\begin{split}v_{x}&=\frac{w_{x}(Q)}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int I^{*}_{c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}}(0)\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K})d^{2}\vec{k}\int I_{c\vec{k}+\vec{Q},v\vec{k}}(0)\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K}^{\prime})d^{2}\vec{k}\\
&\approx\frac{w_{x}(Q)}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\hbar^{2}}{m_{e}^{2}}\int\frac{\vec{Q}\cdot\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}^{*}}{\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K})d^{2}\vec{k}\int\frac{\vec{Q}\cdot\vec{p}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}{\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K}^{\prime})d^{2}\vec{k},\end{split}

(29)

where Eq. (
27
) has been applied. Here,
\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K})
is the unperturbed Wannier wave function centered at
\vec{K}
. Applying the center of mass momentum
\vec{Q}=Q\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
and transition matrix elements
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{k}}\approx p_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-}
in the vicinity of
K
and
\bm{\mathrm{p}}_{\vec{k}}\approx-p_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{+}
in the vicinity of
K^{\prime}
, used throughout Sec.
IV
, we get

v_{x}\approx-\frac{w_{x}(Q)}{2(2\pi)^{2}}\left|\frac{\hbar Q}{m_{e}}\int\frac{p_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}{\epsilon_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}}\psi_{0}(\vec{k}-\vec{K})d^{2}\vec{k}\right|^{2}.

(30)

Note that
p_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}
is real and any complex phase is handled by
\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{\pm}
. It follows that
v_{x}
is real-valued, due to the choice
\vec{Q}=Q\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
as allowed under full rotational symmetry. Choosing a center of mass momentum with a different orientation relative to the coordinate system would make
v_{x}
complex. The phase, however, would be precisely cancelled by an equal and opposite phase of the transition matrix element.

Appendix B
Arbitrary Spin-Orbit Coupling

In the main text, the limit of dominant SO coupling is discussed for simplicity. In fact, the general case can be handled analytically. The full Hamiltonian for the
K
-
K^{\prime}
interaction is given in Eq. (
8
). Solving the associated eigenvalue problem yields the eigenvalues

\begin{split}E_{A_{p}}(Q)&=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}+v_{x}-\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}},\quad E_{A_{l}}(Q)=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}-v_{x}-\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}},\\
E_{B_{p}}(Q)&=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}+v_{x}+\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}},\quad E_{B_{l}}(Q)=E_{0}(Q)+V_{x}-v_{x}+\Delta_{SO}\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}},\end{split}

(31)

where
\alpha=\frac{V_{x}-v_{x}}{2\Delta_{SO}}
and
\beta=\frac{V_{x}+v_{x}}{2\Delta_{SO}}
as in Sec.
IV
. The corresponding eigenvectors are

\begin{split}\Ket{A_{p}}&=\frac{\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-2\beta\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}-2\beta\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}}{\sqrt{2\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)^{2}+8\beta^{2}}},\\
\Ket{A_{l}}&=\frac{\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-2\alpha\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}-\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+2\alpha\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}}{\sqrt{2\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)^{2}+8\alpha^{2}}},\\
\Ket{B_{p}}&=\frac{\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-2\beta\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}-2\beta\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}}{\sqrt{2\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)^{2}+8\beta^{2}}},\\
\Ket{B_{l}}&=\frac{\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)\ket{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}-2\alpha\ket{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}-\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)\ket{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}+2\alpha\ket{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}}{\sqrt{2\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)^{2}+8\alpha^{2}}}.\end{split}

(32)

It should be noted that, in the limit of
\Delta_{SO}\rightarrow 0
, we get the usual superpositions of the
K
and
K^{\prime}
singlet- and triplet states. To show that the exciton transition matrix elements are parallel and perpendicular to
\vec{Q}
, we use
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K_{\uparrow\uparrow}}=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\approx P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{-}
and
\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K^{\prime}_{\uparrow\uparrow}}=\bm{\mathrm{P}}_{K^{\prime}_{\downarrow\downarrow}}\approx-P_{0}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{e}}}_{+}
similarly to Sec.
IV
.

\begin{split}\vec{P}_{A_{p}}&=-iP_{0}\frac{1+\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}-2\beta}{\sqrt{\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)^{2}+4\beta^{2}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{y}}},\\
\vec{P}_{A_{l}}&=P_{0}\frac{1+\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}-2\alpha}{\sqrt{\big(1+\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)^{2}+4\alpha^{2}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}},\\
\vec{P}_{B_{p}}&=-iP_{0}\frac{1-\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}-2\beta}{\sqrt{\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\beta)^{2}}\big)^{2}+4\beta^{2}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{y}}},\\
\vec{P}_{B_{l}}&=P_{0}\frac{1-\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}-2\alpha}{\sqrt{\big(1-\sqrt{1+(2\alpha)^{2}}\big)^{2}+4\alpha^{2}}}\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}.\end{split}

(33)

That is, the transition matrix elements are either parallel or perpendicular to the center of mass momentum
\vec{Q}=Q\bm{\hat{\mathrm{x}}}
