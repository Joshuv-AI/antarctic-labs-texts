Coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of the Schett polynomials
Bishal Deb \addressmark 1,2, Alan D. Sokal \addressmark 1,3

Abstract
We prove the coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of the even and odd subsequences of Schett polynomials X n ​ ( x , y , z ) X_{n}(x,y,z) .



keywords
Schett polynomials, Jacobian elliptic functions, total positivity,
production matrix, exponential Riordan array,
checkerboard exponential Riordan array

1
Introduction

A half-century ago, Schett
[
17
,
18
]
introduced implicitly,
and then Dumont
[
5
,
6
]
introduced explicitly,
the sequence of polynomials

X_{n}(x,y,z)\;=\;\biggl(yz{\partial\over\partial x}\>+\>xz{\partial\over\partial y}\>+\>xy{\partial\over\partial z}\biggr)^{\!n}\>x\;,

(1)

which we shall call the
Schett polynomials
initialized in
x
;
clearly they are homogeneous polynomials of degree
n+1

with nonnegative integer coefficients,
and are symmetric in
y\leftrightarrow z
.
Schett
[
17
,
18
]
showed that the polynomials
X_{n}(x,y,z)

unify and generalize
the Taylor coefficients of the Jacobian elliptic functions
{\rm sn},{\rm cn},{\rm dn}
.

It is easy to see that for even (resp. odd)
n
,
the polynomial
X_{n}(x,y,z)
is divisible by
x
(resp. by
yz
)
and that the quotient is a polynomial in
x^{2},y^{2},z^{2}
.
We therefore define the
reduced Schett polynomials

{\widehat{X}}_{n}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\;=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle{1\over x}\,X_{n}(x,y,z)&\textrm{for $n$ even}\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle{1\over yz}\,X_{n}(x,y,z)&\textrm{for $n$ odd}\end{cases}

(2)

Then
{\widehat{X}}_{2k}
and
{\widehat{X}}_{2k+1}
are homogeneous polynomials
of degree
k
in
x^{2},y^{2},z^{2}
.
It is also not difficult to show that these two polynomials coincide
when
x=0
: that is,
{\widehat{X}}_{2k}(0,y^{2},z^{2})={\widehat{X}}_{2k+1}(0,y^{2},z^{2})
.

1.1
Total positivity and production matrices

The main purpose of the present paper is to prove the

coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity

of the even and odd subsequences of Schett polynomials.
Recall first that a finite or infinite matrix of real numbers is called

totally positive
(TP) if all its minors are nonnegative.
Background information on totally positive matrices can be found
in
[
11
,
10
,
15
,
7
]
;
they have applications to many areas of pure and applied mathematics.
1

1

See
[
20
, footnote 4]
for many references.

In particular, it is known

[
9
, Théorème 9]

[
15
, section 4.6]

that an infinite Hankel matrix
(a_{i+j})_{i,j\geq 0}

of real numbers is totally positive if and only if the underlying sequence

(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a Stieltjes moment sequence,
i.e. the moments of a positive measure on
[0,\infty)
.

However, this is only the beginning of the story,
because our main interest
[
19
]

is not with sequences and matrices of real numbers,
but rather with sequences and matrices of
polynomials

(with integer or real coefficients) in one or more indeterminates
{\mathbf{x}}
:
in applications they will typically be generating polynomials that enumerate
some combinatorial objects with respect to one or more statistics.
We equip the polynomial ring
{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbf{x}}]
with the coefficientwise
partial order: that is, we say that
P
is nonnegative
(and write
P\succeq 0
)
in case
P
is a polynomial with nonnegative coefficients.
We then say that a matrix with entries in
{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbf{x}}]
is

coefficientwise totally positive

if all its minors are polynomials with nonnegative coefficients;
and we say that a sequence
{\bm{a}}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
with entries in
{\mathbb{R}}[{\mathbf{x}}]

is
coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive

if its associated infinite Hankel matrix

H_{\infty}({\bm{a}})=(a_{i+j})_{i,j\geq 0}

is coefficientwise totally positive.
Most generally, we can consider sequences and matrices
with entries in an arbitrary partially ordered commutative ring;
total positivity and Hankel-total positivity
are then defined in the obvious way.
Coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of a sequence of polynomials

(P_{n}({\mathbf{x}}))_{n\geq 0}

implies
the pointwise Hankel-total positivity
(i.e. the Stieltjes moment property) for all
{\mathbf{x}}\geq 0
,
but it is vastly stronger.

Our first main theorem is:

Theorem 1.1

(Coefficientwise Hankel-total positivity of the Schett polynomials)
.

(a)

The sequence
\bigl({\widehat{X}}_{2n}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\bigr)_{n\geq 0}

of even reduced Schett polynomials
is coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive in
x,y,z
.

(b)

The sequence
\bigl({\widehat{X}}_{2n+1}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\bigr)_{n\geq 0}

of odd reduced Schett polynomials
is coefficientwise Hankel-totally positive in
x,y,z
.

We remark that the
z=0
specialization of Theorem
1.1
(a)
follows from a continued fraction due to
Stieltjes
[
21
, p. H17]

and Rogers
[
16
, p. 77]

together with some general theory
[
19
]
.

1.1

Our proof of Theorem
1.1
will be based on the method of

production matrices

[
3

Let
P=(p_{ij})_{i,j\geq 0}
be an infinite matrix
with entries in a commutative ring
R
;
we assume that
P
is either row-finite
(i.e. has only finitely many nonzero entries in each row)
or column-finite.
Now define an infinite matrix
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
by

a_{nk}=(P^{n})_{0k}
.
We call
P
the
production matrix

and
A
the
output matrix
, and we write
A={\mathcal{O}}(P)
.
The two key facts here are the following
[
14
, section 9.2]
:
if
R
is a partially ordered commutative ring
and
P
is totally positive,
then
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
is totally positive

[
14
, Theorem 9.4]
,
and the zeroth column of
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
is Hankel-totally positive

[
14
, Theorem 9.7]
.

We will prove Theorem
1.1
by exhibiting production matrices
that generate the even or odd reduced Schett polynomials
as the zeroth column of their output matrix,
and then proving the total positivity of those production matrices.
The production matrices will be

quadridiagonal
unit-lower-Hessenberg matrices:
that is, they will have a superdiagonal with all entries equal to 1,
and then a diagonal, a subdiagonal, and a second subdiagonal;
all other entries are equal to 0.
In the language of
[
14
]
,
these are the production matrices for a 2-branched J-fraction.
Similar but simpler quadridiagonal production matrices
arose in
[
1
]
.
In particular, we will prove the following two results:

Proposition 1.2

(Production matrices for even and odd Schett polynomials)
.

(a)

The quadridiagonal production matrix

P(x,y,z)=(p_{ij})_{i,j\geq 0}
defined by

p_{n,n+1}&=1\\
p_{n,n}=(2n)^{2}x^{2}\,+\,(2n+1)^{2}(y^{2}+z^{2})\\
p_{n,n-1}=(2n)^{2}(2n-1)\,\bigl[(2n-1)x^{2}(y^{2}+z^{2})\,+\,(2n+1)y^{2}z^{2}\bigr]\qquad\\
p_{n,n-2}=(2n)^{2}(2n-2)^{2}(2n-1)(2n-3)x^{2}y^{2}z^{2}\\
p_{n,k}=0\qquad\textrm{if $k<n-2$ or $k>n+1$}

(3)

generates the even reduced Schett polynomials

\bigl({\widehat{X}}_{2n}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\bigr)_{n\geq 0}

as the zeroth column of its output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
.

(b)

The quadridiagonal production matrix

Q(x,y,z)=(q_{ij})_{i,j\geq 0}
defined by

q_{n,n+1}&=1\\
q_{n,n}=(2n+2)^{2}x^{2}\,+\,(2n+1)^{2}(y^{2}+z^{2})\\
q_{n,n-1}=(2n)^{2}(2n+1)\,\bigl[(2n+1)x^{2}(y^{2}+z^{2})\,+\,(2n-1)y^{2}z^{2}\bigr]\qquad\\
q_{n,n-2}=(2n)^{2}(2n-2)^{2}(2n+1)(2n-1)x^{2}y^{2}z^{2}\\
q_{n,k}=0\qquad\textrm{if $k<n-2$ or $k>n+1$}

(4)

generates the odd reduced Schett polynomials

\bigl({\widehat{X}}_{2n+1}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\bigr)_{n\geq 0}

as the zeroth column of its output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(Q)
.

Proposition 1.3

(Total positivity of the production matrices)
.

(a)

The quadridiagonal production matrix
P(x,y,z)

defined in (
3
)
is coefficientwise totally positive in
x,y,z
.

(b)

The quadridiagonal production matrix
Q(x,y,z)

defined in (
4
)
is coefficientwise totally positive in
x,y,z
.

We note that the production matrices
P(x,y,z)
and
Q(x,y,z)
coincide
when
x=0
; this is consistent with the fact that

{\widehat{X}}_{2n}(0,y^{2},z^{2})={\widehat{X}}_{2n+1}(0,y^{2},z^{2})
.

Combining Propositions
1.2
and
1.3

with the general theory of totally positive production matrices

[
14
, Theorem 9.7]

proves Theorem
1.1
.

1.2
Exponential generating functions of the output matrices

We can also give exponential generating functions for the
output matrices
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
and
{\mathcal{O}}(Q)
,
using the theory of exponential Riordan arrays.
Recall first that if
F(t)
and
G(t)
are formal power series
with coefficients in a commutative ring
R
, with
G(0)=0
,
then the
exponential Riordan array

{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]

is the infinite lower-triangular matrix with entries

{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]_{nk}\;=\;{n!\over k!}\>[t^{n}]\,F(t)G(t)^{k}\;\in\;R\;.

(5)

If the ring
R
contains the rationals (as it will here),
this says that the exponential generating function of
the
k
th column of
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]
is
F(t)G(t)^{k}/k!
.
Note also that if
F
is even and
G
is odd,
then
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]_{nk}
is nonvanishing
only when
n
and
k
have the same parity;
that is, only the even-even and odd-odd submatrices of
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]

are nonvanishing.
In this situation we call
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]

a
checkerboard exponential Riordan array
.

We will show that the output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
is the even-even submatrix
of a particular checkerboard exponential Riordan array.
Clearly
F
must be the exponential generating function
of the zeroth column of
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
,
which by Proposition
1.2
(a) are the
even reduced Schett polynomials
{\widehat{X}}_{2n}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})
,
alternated with zero entries.
Hence we must have

F(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\widehat{X}}_{2n}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\>{t^{2n}\over(2n)!}\;=\;{\rm Dn}(t;x,y,z)\,{\rm En}(t;x,y,z)\;,

(6)

where
{\rm Dn}
and
{\rm En}
are Dumont’s hyperelliptic functions

[
6
, section 3]
,
and the final equality is
[
6
, eq. (3.11)]
.
For
G
, it turns out that we will have

G(t)={\rm Sn}(t;x,y,z)
,
where
{\rm Sn}
is another of Dumont’s hyperelliptic functions.
We will therefore prove:

Proposition 1.4

(Exponential generating function for the output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
)
.

Let the matrix
P
be as in Proposition
1.2
(a),
and let
F(t)
and
G(t)
be as above.
Then the output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(P)
is the even-even submatrix
of the exponential Riordan array
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]
:

{\mathcal{O}}(P)_{nk}\;=\;{(2n)!\over(2k)!}\>[t^{2n}]\,F(t)G(t)^{2k}\;.

(7)

We can do something analogous for the output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(Q)

associated to the odd Schett polynomials,
but this time it will be most convenient to find it as the
odd-odd

submatrix of a checkerboard exponential Riordan array.
We will again have
G(t)={\rm Sn}(t;x,y,z)
.
Since the zeroth column of
{\mathcal{O}}(Q)
,
which by Proposition
1.2
(b) are the
odd reduced Schett polynomials
{\widehat{X}}_{2n+1}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})
,
will now appear in the
k=1
column of
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]

in odd rows starting at
n=1
, we must have

F(t)G(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}{\widehat{X}}_{2n+1}(x^{2},y^{2},z^{2})\>{t^{2n+1}\over(2n+1)!}\;=\;{\rm Sn}(t;x,y,z)\,{\rm Cn}(t;x,y,z)\;,

(8)

where
{\rm Sn}
and
{\rm Cn}
are Dumont’s hyperelliptic functions,
and the final equality is again
[
6
, eq. (3.11)]
.
Therefore
F(t)={\rm Cn}(t;x,y,z)
.
We will then prove:

Proposition 1.5

(Exponential generating function for the output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(Q)
)
.

Let the matrix
Q
be as in Proposition
1.2
(b),
and let
F(t)
and
G(t)
be as above.
Then the output matrix
{\mathcal{O}}(Q)
is the odd-odd submatrix
of the exponential Riordan array
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]
:

{\mathcal{O}}(Q)_{nk}\;=\;{(2n+1)!\over(2k+1)!}\>[t^{2n+1}]\,F(t)G(t)^{2k+1}\;.

(9)

We shall prove

The production matrix of an exponential Riordan array
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]

is well known
[

to be

p_{nk}=(n!/k!)\>(z_{n-k}\,+\,k\,a_{n-k+1})

where
A(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}s^{n}

and
Z(s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z_{n}s^{n}
satisfy

G^{\prime}(t)\;=\;A(G(t))\;,\qquad{F^{\prime}(t)\over F(t)}\;=\;Z(G(t))\;.

(10)

Here we will give the analogous result
for the even-even and odd-odd submatrices of a
checkerboard exponential Riordan array.

First we define some new series:

B(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;A(s)^{2}\,,\qquad C(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;2A(s)Z(s)\,,\qquad D(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;Z(s)^{2}+A(s)Z^{\prime}(s)\;.

(11)

Since
F
is even and
G
is odd,
it follows that
A
is even and
Z

D
are even, while

B(t)\;=\;\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}b_{m}\,t^{2m}\,,\qquad C(t)\;=\;\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}\,t^{2m+1}\,,\qquad D(t)\;=\;\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}d_{m}\,t^{2m}\;.

(12)

We then have:

Theorem 2.1

(Production matrices for submatrices of a checkerboard exponential Riordan array)
.

Let
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]
be a checkerboard exponential Riordan array
with invertible diagonal entries and
F(0)=1
,
and let
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]^{\rm ee}
and
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]^{\rm oo}

be its even-even and odd-odd submatrices.
Then:

(a)

The production matrix of
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]^{\rm ee}
has entries

p_{nk}\;=\;{(2n)!\over(2k)!}\,\Bigl[2k(n+k)\,b_{n-k+1}\>+\>2k\,c_{n-k}\>+\>d_{n-k}\Bigr]\;.

(13)

(b)

The production matrix of
{\mathcal{R}}[F,G]^{\rm oo}
has entries

p_{nk}\;=\;{(2n+1)!\over(2k+1)!}\,\Bigl[(2k+1)(n+k+1)\,b_{n-k+1}\>+\>(2k+1)\,c_{n-k}\>+\>d_{n-k}\Bigr]\;.

(14)

The proof will be based on the following
simple but powerful result, which we discovered very recently
and which to our surprise is apparently new;
it is inspired by the integration-by-parts arguments employed by

[

Proposition 2.2

(Exponential-generating-function method for production matrices)
.

Let
A=(a_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}
and
P=(p_{nk})_{n,k\geq 0}

be row-finite matrices with entries in
a commutative ring
R
containing the rationals,
with
a_{0k}=\delta_{k0}
.
Fix integers
r\geq 1
and
0\leq s<r
,
and define the column exponential generating functions of
A
:

A_{k}^{[r,s]}(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{nk}\,{t^{rn+s}\over(rn+s)!}\;.

(15)

Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

For all
k\geq 0
,

{d^{r}\over dt^{r}}\,A_{k}^{[r,s]}(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{nk}\,A_{n}^{[r,s]}(t)\;.

(16)

(b)

A={\mathcal{O}}(P)
.

Proof.
On both sides of (
16
),
the only powers of
t
that can occur are
t^{rm+s}
with
m\geq 0
;
for the left-hand side we have here used the hypothesis that
0\leq s<r
.
So take the coefficient of
t^{rm+s}/(rm+s)!

on both sides of (
16
):

\biggl[{t^{rm+s}\over(rm+s)!}\biggr]\>{d^{r}\over dt^{r}}\,A_{k}^{[r,s]}(t)\;=\;\biggl[{t^{r(m+1)+s}\over(r(m+1)+s)!}\biggr]\>A_{k}^{[r,s]}(t)\;=\;a_{m+1,k}

(17)

while

\biggl[{t^{rm+s}\over(rm+s)!}\biggr]\>\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{nk}\,A_{n}^{[r,s]}(t)\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{nk}\,a_{mn}\;.

(18)

But
a_{m+1,k}=\sum\limits_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{mn}\,p_{nk}

is precisely the recurrence stating that
A={\mathcal{O}}(P)
.

\square

Note that, to prove that
A={\mathcal{O}}(P)
,
it suffices to verify the identities (
16
)
for one pair
(r,s)
.
On the other hand, if
A={\mathcal{O}}(P)
,
then the identities (
16
)
hold for all pairs

satisfying

.

\implies

)
is unlikely to hold, because there are terms that will occur
on the left-hand side (unless they happen to vanish)
that cannot occur on the right-hand side,

with

.

4
Remarks

In a longer version of this work, we intend to treat the following
generalizations:

1) We can define Schett polynomials in any number of variables, not just three.
Fix an integer
m\geq 1
, and define the Schett operator
in
m+1
variables
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
by

{\mathcal{D}}_{m+1}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\sum_{i=0}^{m}\>\Biggl(\!\!\prod\limits_{00112233\begin{array}[]{c}0\leq j\leq m\\
j\neq i\end{array}}\!\!\!x_{j}\!\Biggr)\>{\partial\over\partial x_{i}}\;.

(30)

Then define the

Schett polynomials in
\bm{m+1}
variables

initialized in
x_{0}
by

X^{[m+1]}_{n}(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;({\mathcal{D}}_{m+1})^{n}\,x_{0}\;.

(31)

These polynomials are clearly symmetric in
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
.
Since
x_{0}
plays a special role, we write

x=x_{0}
and
{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
.
It turns out that if we define
a_{i}=1/y_{i}

and make an appropriate rescaling,
then the Schett polynomial in
m
variables
is obtained from the one in
m+1
variables
by specializing to
a_{m}=0
.
This allows us to take
m\to\infty

and define a symmetric-function generalization of the Schett polynomials.
For this generalization we have obtained analogues of
Propositions
1.2
,
1.4
and
1.5

for the case
x=0
,
but the coefficientwise total positivity of this production matrix
is at present a conjecture.
Furthermore, we have not yet been able to find a production matrix
for the case
x\neq 0
.

2) Schett
[
17
, Theorem III/1]
showed that
X_{n}(1,1,1)=n!
.
It was therefore natural to interpret
X_{n}(x,y,z)
as enumerating
permutations of
[n]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\{1,\ldots,n\}
with respect to some
bivariate statistic, and Dumont
[
5
]
identified the statistic:
he showed that

X_{n}(x,y,z)\;=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle x\,D_{n}(x,y,z)&\textrm{for $n$ even}\\
\displaystyle y\,D_{n}(x,y,z)&\textrm{for $n$ odd}\end{cases}

(32)

where

D_{n}(x,y,z)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rm def}}{{=}}\;\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}}(x^{2})^{{\rm cpeakodd}(\sigma)}(y^{2})^{{\rm cpeakeven}(\sigma)}z^{{\rm cdrise}(\sigma)+{\rm cdfall}(\sigma)+{\rm fix}(\sigma)}\;;

(33)

here these statistics count, respectively,
odd cycle peaks, even cycle peaks, cycle double rises,
cycle double falls, and fixed points in the permutation
\sigma
.
(We remark that the
y\leftrightarrow z
symmetry
is quite mysterious in this combinatorial interpretation.)
A different combinatorial interpretation of the Schett polynomials,
in terms of vertices of even and odd degree at even and odd levels
of an increasing tree,
was given by Lin and Ma
[
12
, Theorem 1.6]
.
It will be interesting to find combinatorial interpretations
for the higher-order Schett polynomials.

)/(

)

,
