Introduction

A
string C-group representation
, or
string C-group
for short, is a pair
(G,S)
where
G
is a group and
S:=\{\rho_{0},\ldots,\rho_{n-1}\}
is a finite ordered set of
n
involutions of
G
such that

(1)

\langle S\rangle=G
;

(2)

For each
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
with
|i-j|>1
, we have
\rho_{i}\rho_{j}=\rho_{j}\rho_{i}
;

(3)

For each
I,J\subseteq\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
, we have that

\langle\rho_{i}\;|\;i\in I\rangle\cap\langle\rho_{j}\;|\;j\in J\rangle=\langle\rho_{k}\;|\;k\in I\cap J\rangle
.

Property 2 above is usually called the
string property
while property 3 is usually called the
intersection property
.
A pair
(G,S)
satisfying 1 and 2 above is also called a
string group generated by involutions
or
sggi
for short.
The cardinality of
G
is called the
order
of
(G,S)
while the cardinality of
S
is called the
rank
of
(G,S)
.
The
Schläfli type
of
(G,S)
is the ordered set
\{o(\rho_{i}\rho_{i+1})\;|\;i=0,\ldots,n-2\}
where
o(g)
denoted the order of the element
g\in G
.
If
|G|=2\Pi_{i=0}^{n-2}o(\rho_{i}\rho_{i+1})
then
(G,S)
is called
tight
.

Proposition 2.2

Let

[xy,z]=[x,z]^{y}[y,z]

Let

The

, denoted by

, is the intersection of all maximal subgroups of

. Obviously,
\Phi(G)
is a characteristic subgroup of

. The following theorem is the well-known Burnside Basis Theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

[

Let

be a

|G:\Phi(G)|=p^{d}

(1)

G/\Phi(G)\cong{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{d}
. Moreover, if

.

(2)

Every minimal generating set of

The unique cardinality of all minimal generating sets of a

is called the

of

.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be an abelian group of odd order and
\alpha\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
. If
o(\alpha)=2
, then

G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
, where
G_{1}=\{g\in G\;|\;g^{\alpha}=g\}
and
G_{2}=\{g\in G\;|\;g^{\alpha}=g^{-1}\}
.

Proof.

Since
(|G|,2)=1
, we have
s|G|+2t=1
for some integers
s
and
t
.
For
g\in G
, we have

g=g^{1}=g^{2t}=g^{t}(g^{t})^{\alpha}(g^{-t})^{\alpha}g^{t}.

Since
G
is abelian and
\alpha^{2}=1
, we have
(g^{t}(g^{t})^{\alpha})^{\alpha}=g^{t}(g^{t})^{\alpha}
,
((g^{-t})^{\alpha}g^{t})^{\alpha}=((g^{-t})^{\alpha}g^{t})^{-1}
.
It means that
g^{t}(g^{t})^{\alpha}\in G_{1}
and
(g^{-t})^{\alpha}g^{t}\in G_{2}
.
Hence
G=G_{1}G_{2}
.

On the other hand, it is easy to see that
G_{1}
and

are both normal subgroups of

.
Hence
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Let
m\geq 2
and
p
be an odd prime. Let
(G,\{\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\})
be a string C-group of rank three and order
4p^{m}
with Schläfli type
\{k_{1},k_{2}\}
.

Let us first prove (1) of Theorem
1.1
.
Let
n_{p}(G)
be the number of Sylow
p
-subgroup subgroups of
G
and let
P
be a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
.
By Theorem
2.5
(1),

n_{p}(G)\mid 4
and
n_{p}(G)\equiv 1\ \hbox{\rm mod }p
. It follows that
n_{p}(G)=1
or
4
in our case as
|G|=4p^{m}
.
If
n_{p}(G)=1
, then
P\unlhd G
. By Theorem
2.5
(2), if
n_{p}(G)=4
, then
p=3
and hence
|G|=4\cdot 3^{m}
.
Set
\Omega=\{P^{g}\;|\;g\in G\}
. Recall that
|\Omega|=4
.
Consider the action
\phi
of
G
on
\Omega
by conjugation and let
K=\hbox{\rm Ker}\phi
.
As
|\Omega|=4
, we have that
G/K\leq S_{4}
. In fact, since
G
is transitive on
\Omega
and
K<P
, we have
|K|=3^{m-1}
.
Then
G/K\cong A_{4}
.
However, the subgroups generated by all involutions in
A_{4}
is
{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{2}
.
Since
\rho_{0}^{2}=\rho_{1}^{2}=\rho_{2}^{2}=1
, we have
|G/K|=|\langle\rho_{0}K,\rho_{1}K,\rho_{2}K\rangle|\leq 4
.
Note that
|K|=p^{m-1}
. Thus
|G|=4p^{m-1}
, a contradiction as
|G|=4p^{m}
. Thus,
n_{p}(G)=1
, that is,
P\unlhd G
.

We thus have
|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle|=4
and
|P|=p^{m}
. Also
|P\cap\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle|=1
.
It follows that
|P\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle|=4p^{m}=|G|
, that is,
G=P\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle
.
In face, since
P\unlhd G
, we have
G=P\rtimes\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle
, finishing the proof of (1).

Let us now prove (2) of Theorem
1.1
.
If, say,
k_{1}
is not divisible by
p
, then it is equal to 2 and
G
is degenerate.
The same holds for
k_{2}
.
We then only need to show that
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
cannot be both not divisible by 2.
Note that
G=\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{0}\rho_{1},\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\rangle
. Consider the quotient group
\overline{G}=G/P=\langle\rho_{0}P,\rho_{1}P,\rho_{2}P\rangle
.
If
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are both not divisible by 2, then
\rho_{0}\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{1}\rho_{2}
both have order a power of
p
. Thus
\rho_{0}\rho_{1}\in P
and
\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\in P
. It follows that
G/P=\langle\rho_{0}P\rangle
, and hence
|G/P|=2
, which is impossible because
|G/P|=4
. Therefore, one at least of
k_{1}
or
k_{2}
must be divisible by
2p
, finishing the proof of (2).

(3) Assume
\rho_{1}=u\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}
, where
u\in P
and
i,j\in\{0,1\}
. Then

1=\rho_{1}^{2}=u\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}u\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}=uu^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}}

and hence
u^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}}=u^{-1}
.
Since
u\in P
, we have
\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}\neq 1
. Then there exists
\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}
such that

\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle=\langle\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j},\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}\rangle
.

Now, let
H=\langle u,u^{\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}}\rangle
. Obviously,
\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}\in N_{G}(H)
. Moreover,

u^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}}=u^{-1}\in H,(u^{\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}})^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}}=(u^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}})^{\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}}=(u^{-1})^{\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}}=(u^{\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}})^{-1}\in H.

Then
\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}\in N_{G}(H)
. It follows that
H\langle\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j},\rho_{0}^{i^{\prime}}\rho_{2}^{j^{\prime}}\rangle=H\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle\leq G
.

On the other hand, it is easy to see that
\rho_{1}\in H\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle
. Thus
G=H\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle
, meaning that
P=H
and
d(P)=d(H)\leq 2
.

Now, if
d(P)=1
, then
P
is cyclic. Since
k_{1},k_{2}\geq 3
, we have
(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})^{2},(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})^{2}\in P
.
It follows that
\langle(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})^{2}\rangle\cap\langle(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})^{2}\rangle\neq\langle 1\rangle
, which is impossible
because
\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle\cap\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle=\langle\rho_{1}\rangle
.
Thus,
d(P)=2
. This finishes the proof of (3) and thus the proof of Theorem
1.1
. ∎

, where

A=\{g\in P\;|\;g^{\rho_{0}}=g,g^{\rho_{2}}=g\},B=\{g\in P\;|\;g^{\rho_{0}}=g,g^{\rho_{2}}=g^{-1}\},

C=\{g\in P\;|\;g^{\rho_{0}}=g^{-1},g^{\rho_{2}}=g\},D=\{g\in P\;|\;g^{\rho_{0}}=g^{-1},g^{\rho_{2}}=g^{-1}\}.

Moreover at least two of the above subgroups are the identity subgroup, and if two of them are not the identity subgroups, they must be cyclic by Theorem
1.1
(3).

If
P=A
, then
\rho_{0}\in Z(G)
. Consider the quotient group
\overline{G}=G/\langle\rho_{0}\rangle
.
Then
\overline{G}=\langle\overline{\rho_{1}},\overline{\rho_{2}}\rangle
and has order
2p^{m}
.
Since
o(\overline{\rho_{1}})=o(\overline{\rho_{2}})=2
, we have
\overline{G}\cong D_{2p^{m}}
.
It follows that
o(\overline{\rho_{1}}\overline{\rho_{2}})=p^{m}
and
d(P)=1
,
which is a contradiction with Theorem
1.1
(3).

If
P=B
,
C
or
D
, then
\rho_{0},\rho_{2}
or
\rho_{0}\rho_{2}\in Z(G)
. Using arguments similar as in the case
P=A
, we get
d(P)=1
, a contradiction. In fact, if
P\cap A\neq\{1\}
, then
\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle\cap Z(G)\neq\{1\}
. It follows that
d(P)=1
, again contradicting Theorem
1.1
(1).

Since we proved that
P
cannot be one of
A
,
B
,
C
or
D
and since
d(P)=2
, we have
P=K\times L
with
K,L\in\{A,B,C,D\}
and
K\neq L
.

If
K=A
, then
\rho_{1}=xy\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}
for some
x\in A
and
y\in L
.
Since
\rho_{1}^{2}=1
, we have that

xy(xy)^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}}=x^{2}yy^{\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}}=1

which is impossible as
x^{2}\neq 1
.
So
P
must be one of
B\times C
,
B\times D
or
C\times D
.

If
P=B\times C
, then

G=(\langle x\rangle\times\langle y\rangle)\rtimes\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2})\rangle

=\langle x,y,\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\;|\;x^{p^{l_{1}}},y^{p^{l_{2}}},\rho_{0}^{2},\rho_{2}^{2},[x,y],[\rho_{0},\rho_{2}],x^{\rho_{0}}=x,x^{\rho_{2}}=x^{-1},y^{\rho_{0}}=y^{-1},y^{\rho_{2}}=y\rangle.

Let
\rho_{1}=x^{t_{1}}y^{t_{2}}\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}
.
Then
G=\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle=\langle\rho_{0},x^{t_{1}}y^{t_{2}},\rho_{2}\rangle=\langle x^{t_{1}},y^{t_{2}},\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle=\langle x^{t_{1}},y^{t_{2}}\rangle\rtimes\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\rangle
.
If
p\mid t_{1}
, then
x^{t_{1}}\in\Phi(P)
and
P=\langle y^{t_{2}}\rangle
which is impossible because
d(P)=2
. Thus,
(t_{1},p)=1
. By the same reason, we have
(t_{2},p)=1
.
Without loss of generality, one can assume
\rho_{1}=xy\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}
. Since
\rho_{1}^{2}=1
,
it is easy to check that if one of
i
or
j
equals 0 we get either that
x^{2}=1
or
y^{2}=1
, a contradiction. Hence we have
i=j=1
and
\rho_{1}=xy\rho_{0}\rho_{2}
.

On the other hand, since
(\rho_{1}\rho_{0})^{2}=(xy\rho_{2})^{2}=y^{2}
, we have
o(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})=2o(y)
.
Since
(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})^{2}=(xy\rho_{0})^{2}=x^{2}
, we have
o(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})=2o(x)
. It follows that

|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle|=\frac{|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle|\cdot|\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle|}{|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle\cap\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle|}=\frac{2\cdot 2o(y)\cdot 2\cdot 2o(x)}{2}=8o(x)o(y),

which is impossible because
|G|=4o(x)o(y)
.

If
P=B\times D
, then

G=(\langle x\rangle\times\langle y\rangle)\rtimes\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{2})\rangle

=\langle x,y,\rho_{0},\rho_{2}\;|\;x^{p^{l_{1}}},y^{p^{l_{2}}},\rho_{0}^{2},\rho_{2}^{2},[x,y],[\rho_{0},\rho_{2}],x^{\rho_{0}}=x,x^{\rho_{2}}=x^{-1},y^{\rho_{0}}=y^{-1},y^{\rho_{2}}=y^{-1}\rangle.

Without loss of generality, one can assume
\rho_{1}=xy\rho_{0}^{i}\rho_{2}^{j}
. Since
\rho_{1}^{2}=1
,
we have
i=0
and
j=1
. Then
\rho_{1}=xy\rho_{2}
.

Since
(\rho_{1}\rho_{0})^{2}=(xy\rho_{2}\rho_{0})^{2}=y^{2}
, we have
o(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})=2o(y)
.
Since
\rho_{1}\rho_{2}=xy
, we have
o(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})=max\{o(x),o(y)\}
.
However, if
o(y)>o(x)
, then

|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle|=\frac{|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle|\cdot|\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle|}{|\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\rangle\cap\langle\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle|}=\frac{2\cdot 2o(y)\cdot 2\cdot o(y)}{2}=4o(y)^{2}>4o(x)o(y)=|G|.

Then
o(y)\leq o(x)
. Thus,
2\cdot o(\rho_{0}\rho_{1})\cdot o(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})=2\cdot 2o(y)\cdot o(x)=|G|
.
It follows that
G
is tight.

If
P=C\times D
, we get a case similar to the previous one, with
\rho_{1}=xy\rho_{0}
and we arrive to the same conclusion. This is the dual case to the previous one.

Here we have proved that if
P
is abelian then
(G,\{\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\})
is tight.

From
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
, up to duality, there is a unique string C-group of order
4p^{m}
with type
\{p^{k},2p^{l}\}
with
m=l+k
and
l\leq k
.
From the proof above, we have that
l\leq k
. Moreover, since we constructed the unique example,
P
has to be abelian. Therefore, if
G
is tight,
P
has to be abelian.
∎

5.
Construction of string C-groups of rank three with a group of order
4p^{m}
and a Sylow
p
-subgroup that is not abelian

The main purpose of this section is to prove Theorem
1.3
.
In order to do so, we will give a constructive proof. To do that, we need to know some more information about groups of order
p^{m}
.
Let
P
be a
p
-group and let
H_{p}(P)
be the
subgroup of
P
generated by all the elements of order different from
p
.
The subgroup
H_{p}(P)
is called the
Hughes
subgroup
of
P
(see
[
25
,
26
,
27
]
).
A group
P
of order
p^{m}
and nilpotency class
m-1
is said to be of maximal class if
m\geq 3
.
The basic
material about these groups can be found in Blackburn
[
4
, pages 83–84]
or Huppert
[
28
, Chapter 3]
.
If
P
has an abelian maximal subgroup
A
, and
A=H_{p}(P)
, then, by
[
39
, Chapter 8, Example 8.3.3]
),

P=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{r},s_{r+1},\cdots,s_{p-1},\beta\rangle\ \mbox{with}\ \mbox{the}\ \mbox{following}\ \mbox{relations}

s_{1}^{p^{e}},s_{2}^{p^{e}},\cdots,s_{r}^{p^{e}},s_{r+1}^{p^{e-1}},\cdots s_{p-1}^{p^{e-1}},\beta^{p},

[s_{i},s_{j}],\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq i,j\leq p-1,\ \mbox{and}\ s_{k+1}=[s_{k},\beta],\ {\rm for}\ 1\leq k\leq p-2,

[s_{p-1},\beta]=s_{1}^{-\tbinom{p}{1}}s_{2}^{-\tbinom{p}{2}}\cdots s_{p-2}^{-\tbinom{p}{p-2}}s_{p-1}^{-\tbinom{p}{p-1}},

where

1\leq e,1\leq r\leq p-1,\ m=er+(e-1)(p-r-1)+1\geq 3.

Let
A:=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{p-1}\rangle
. We have that
A
is abelian and
A\unlhd P
.

Moreover, any
x\in P\backslash A
has order
p
.

With the relations given above, it is clear that
P=\langle s_{1},\beta\rangle
. Define

\sigma:

s_{1}\mapsto s_{1}

and
\beta\mapsto\beta^{-1}
,

\tau:

s_{1}\mapsto s_{1}^{-1}

and
\beta\mapsto\beta
.

In this section we will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G=P\rtimes\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle
.
Let
\rho_{0}:=s_{1}\tau\sigma
,
\rho_{1}:=\beta\tau\sigma
and
\rho_{2}:=\sigma
with
s_{1},\sigma
and
\tau
defined as above.
Then
(G,\{\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\})
is a string C-group of order
4p^{m}
with Schläfli type
\{p,2p\}
, where
m\geq 3
and
p
is an odd prime.

Obviously, Theorem
1.3
is a corollary of the theorem above.

Let
s_{k+1}:=[s_{k},\beta]
for
k=p-1
and
p
. Then

s_{p}=[s_{p-1},\beta]=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{p}{i}},

s_{p+1}=[s_{p},\beta]=s_{p}^{-1}s_{p}^{\beta}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{-1})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}\cdot\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{\beta})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}

=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{-1}s_{i}^{\beta})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}[s_{i},\beta]^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}s_{i+1}^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}.

In order to prove Theorem
1.3
, we first need to prove the following two lemmas.

Lemma 5.2
.

For any
1\leq k\leq p
, we have

(1)

s_{k}^{\beta^{1-k}}=\prod\limits_{i=k}^{p+1}s_{i}^{\tbinom{p+1-k}{i-k}}
;

(2)

s_{k}^{\sigma}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s_{k}^{\beta^{1-k}},&k\mbox{ is odd,}\\
(s_{k}^{\beta^{1-k}})^{-1},&k\mbox{ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Proof.

(1) Since
s_{k+1}=[s_{k},\beta]
, we have
s_{k}^{\beta}=s_{k}s_{k+1}
.
Since
o(\beta)=p
and
k-1\leq p
, we have
p-k+1\geq 0
.
It follows that

s_{k}^{\beta^{1-k}}=s_{k}^{\beta^{p+1-k}}=(s_{k}s_{k+1})^{\beta^{p-k}}=(s_{k}s_{k+1}^{2}s_{k+2})^{\beta^{p-k-1}}=\cdots

=s_{k}^{\tbinom{p+1-k}{0}}s_{k+1}^{\tbinom{p+1-k}{1}}s_{k+2}^{\tbinom{p+1-k}{2}}\cdots s_{p}^{\tbinom{p+1-k}{p-k}}s_{p+1}^{\tbinom{p+1-k}{p+1-k}}.

(2) The proof uses an induction on
k
. For
k=1
, we have
s_{1}^{\sigma}=s_{1}
. For
k=2
, by Proposition
2.2
, we have

s_{2}^{\sigma}=[s_{1},\beta]^{\sigma}=[s_{1},\beta^{-1}]=([s_{1},\beta]^{-1})^{\beta^{-1}}=(s_{2}^{-1})^{\beta^{1-2}}.

Hence this lemma is true for
k=1
and
2
. We now use induction on
k
and Proposition
2.2
, splitting the analysis in two cases, namely the case where
k
is odd and the case where
k
is even.

If
k
is odd, then

s_{k}^{\sigma}=[s_{k-1}^{\sigma},\beta^{\sigma}]=[(s_{k-1}^{\beta^{2-k}})^{-1},\beta^{-1}]=[s_{k-1}^{-1},\beta^{-1}]^{\beta^{2-k}}=([s_{k-1},\beta]^{\beta^{-1}s_{k-1}^{-1}})^{\beta^{2-k}}.

Recall that
A=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{p-1}\rangle
is an abelian maximal subgroup of
G
and
A\unlhd G
.

It follows that
s_{k}\in A
and
s_{k}^{\beta}\in A
for any
k\geq 1
. Thus,

s_{k}^{\sigma}=([s_{k-1},\beta]^{\beta^{-1}s_{k-1}^{-1}})^{\beta^{2-k}}=s_{k}^{(s_{k-1}^{-1})^{\beta}\beta^{1-k}}=s_{k}^{\beta^{1-k}}.

If
k
is even, then

s_{k}^{\sigma}=[s_{k-1}^{\sigma},\beta^{\sigma}]=[s_{k-1}^{\beta^{2-k}},\beta^{-1}]=[s_{k-1},\beta^{-1}]^{\beta^{2-k}}=(([s_{k-1},\beta]^{-1})^{\beta^{-1}})^{\beta^{2-k}}=(s_{k}^{\beta^{1-k}})^{-1}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

\sigma,\tau\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(P)
, and
\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Proof.

Obviously,
\sigma^{2}=\tau^{2}=1
and
\sigma\tau=\tau\sigma
. Therefore
\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
It remains to prove that
\sigma,\tau\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(P)
.

We first prove that
\sigma\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(P)
. Obviously,
\langle s_{1}^{\sigma},\beta^{\sigma}\rangle=\langle s_{1},\beta^{-1}\rangle=P
. Thus, we only need to show that the generating set
\{s_{1}^{\sigma},s_{2}^{\sigma},\cdots,s_{p-1}^{\sigma},\beta^{\sigma}\}

of
P
satisfies
the same relations as
\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{p-1},\beta\}
in order to conclude that
\sigma\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(P)
.

Recall that
A=\langle s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{p-1}\rangle
is abelian and
A\unlhd P
. By Lemma
5.2
(2),

s_{i}^{\sigma}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}s_{i}^{\beta^{1-i}},&i\mbox{ is odd,}\\
(s_{i}^{\beta^{1-i}})^{-1},&i\mbox{ is even.}\end{array}\right.

Now
o(s_{i})=o(s_{i}^{\sigma})
,
[s_{i}^{\sigma},s_{j}^{\sigma}]=1
for
1\leq i,j\leq p-1
.
Moreover, it is easy to see that
s_{k+1}^{\sigma}=[s_{k}^{\sigma},\beta^{\sigma}]
for
1\leq k\leq p-2
. Thus we only need to show that

[s_{p-1}^{\sigma},\beta^{\sigma}]=(s_{1}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{1}}(s_{2}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{2}}(s_{3}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{3}}\cdots(s_{p-1}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{p-1}},

that is,
s_{p}^{\sigma}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}.

By Lemma
5.2
(1), we have

s_{1}^{\sigma}=s_{1},s_{2}^{\sigma}=\prod\limits_{i=2}^{p+1}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{p-1}{i-2}},s_{3}^{\sigma}=\prod\limits_{i=3}^{p+1}s_{i}^{\tbinom{p-2}{i-3}},\cdots,s_{p-1}^{\sigma}=\prod\limits_{i=p-1}^{p+1}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{2}{i-(p-1)}}.

Then
\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}=s_{1}^{u_{1}}s_{2}^{u_{2}}\cdots s_{p+1}^{u_{p+1}}
, where
u_{i}
comes from Table
1
.

\begin{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{c | c|cccccc}*&(s_{1}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{1}}&(s_{2}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{2}}&(s_{3}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{3}}&(s_{4}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{4}}&\cdots&\cdots&(s_{p-1}^{\sigma})^{-\tbinom{p}{p-1}}\\

\hline\cr u_{2}&0&\tbinom{p-1}{0}\tbinom{p}{2}&0&0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\

.

Obviously,
u_{1}=-\tbinom{p}{1}
. By Proposition
2.6
(1), we have

u_{p+1}=\sum\limits_{j=2}^{p-1}(-1)^{j}\tbinom{p}{j}=\tbinom{p}{p-1}+\sum\limits_{j=2}^{\frac{p-3}{2}}\left[(-1)^{j}\tbinom{p}{j}+(-1)^{p-j}\tbinom{p}{p-j}\right]=\tbinom{p}{p-1}=p.

For

, we have
u_{i}=\sum\limits_{j=2}^{i}(-1)^{j}\tbinom{p+1-j}{i-j}\tbinom{p}{j}
.
For
i=p

u_{p}-\tbinom{1}{0}\tbinom{p}{p}=u_{p}-1=\sum\limits_{j=2}^{p}(-1)^{j}\tbinom{p+1-j}{p-j}\tbinom{p}{j}.

By Proposition
2.6
(2)
(3)

\tbinom{p+1-j}{i-j}\tbinom{p}{j}=\left[\tbinom{p-j}{i-j}+\tbinom{p-j}{i-j-1}\right]\tbinom{p}{j}=\tbinom{p-j}{i-j}\tbinom{p}{j}+\tbinom{p-j}{i-j-1}\tbinom{p}{j}=\tbinom{p}{i}\tbinom{i}{j}+\tbinom{p}{i-1}\tbinom{i-1}{j}.

Together with Proposition

\sum\limits_{j=2}^{i}(-1)^{j}\left[\tbinom{p}{i}\tbinom{i}{j}+\tbinom{p}{i-1}\tbinom{i-1}{j}\right]

=\tbinom{p}{i}\left[i-1+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{j}\tbinom{i}{j}\right]+\tbinom{p}{i-1}\left[i-2+\sum\limits_{j=0}^{i}(-1)^{j}\tbinom{i-1}{j}\right]

=\tbinom{p}{i}\left[i-1+(-1)^{i}\tbinom{i-1}{i}\right]+\tbinom{p}{i-1}\left[i-2+(-1)^{i}\tbinom{i-2}{i}\right]

=\tbinom{p}{i}(i-1)+\tbinom{p}{i-1}(i-2)

=-\tbinom{p}{i}+(p-i+1)\tbinom{p}{i-1}+(i-2)\tbinom{p}{i-1}

=-\tbinom{p}{i}+(p-1)\tbinom{p}{i-1},

and hence

u_{p}=-\tbinom{p}{p}+(p-1)\tbinom{p}{p-1}+1=(p-1)\tbinom{p}{p-1},

u_{i}=-\tbinom{p}{i}+(p-1)\tbinom{p}{i-1},\quad 2\leq i\leq p-1.

By Lemma
5.2

s_{p+1}=[s_{p},\beta]

s_{p}^{\sigma}=s_{p}^{\beta^{1-p}}=s_{p}^{\beta}=s_{p}s_{p+1}.

The result is now immediate since

s_{1}^{u_{1}}s_{2}^{u_{2}}\cdots s_{p}^{u_{p}}s_{p+1}^{u_{p+1}}

=s_{1}^{-\tbinom{p}{1}}\left[\prod\limits_{i=2}^{p-1}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{p}{i}+(p-1)\tbinom{p}{i-1}}\right]s_{p}^{(p-1)\tbinom{p}{p-1}}s_{p+1}^{p}

=\left[\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}\right]\left[(\prod\limits_{i=2}^{p}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{p}{i-1}})^{1-p}\right]s_{p+1}^{p}

=s_{p}s_{p+1}^{1-p}s_{p+1}^{p}=s_{p}s_{p+1}=s_{p}^{\sigma}.

Let us now prove that
\tau\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(P)
. Since
A
is abelian and
s_{i+1}=[s_{i},\beta]
for
1\leq i\leq p-2
, by Proposition
2.2
, we have

s_{i+1}^{\tau}=[s_{i},\beta]^{\tau}=[s_{i}^{-1},\beta]=(([s_{i},\beta])^{-1})^{s_{i}^{-1}}=(s_{i+1}^{-1})^{s_{i}^{-1}}=s_{i+1}^{-1}.

We need to show that the generating set
\{s_{1}^{-1},s_{2}^{-1},\cdots,s_{p-1}^{-1},\beta\}
of

satisfies
the same relations as
\{s_{1},s_{2},\cdots,s_{p-1},\beta\}
.
Obviously,
o(s_{i})=o(s_{i}^{\tau})
,
[s_{i}^{\tau},s_{j}^{\tau}]=1
for
1\leq i,j\leq p-1
.
Also, we have

[s_{k},\beta]^{\tau}=[s_{k}^{\tau},\beta^{\tau}]=[s_{k}^{-1},\ \beta]=([s_{k},\beta]^{-1})^{s_{k}^{-1}}=s_{k+1}^{-1}=s_{k+1}^{\tau}\ \mbox{for}\ 1\leq k\leq p-2.

Now we only need to show that

[s_{p-1}^{\tau},\beta^{\tau}]=(s_{1}^{\tau})^{-\tbinom{p}{1}}(s_{2}^{\tau})^{-\tbinom{p}{2}}(s_{3}^{\tau})^{-\tbinom{p}{3}}\cdots(s_{p-1}^{\tau})^{-\tbinom{p}{p-1}}.

It is easy to see that

[s_{p-1}^{\tau},\beta^{\tau}]=[s_{p-1}^{-1},\beta]=([s_{p-1},\beta]^{-1})^{s_{p-1}^{-1}}=(s_{p}^{-1})^{s_{p-1}^{-1}}=s_{p}^{-1}.

On the other hand,

\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{\tau})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}(s_{i}^{-1})^{-\tbinom{p}{i}}=(\prod\limits_{i=1}^{p-1}s_{i}^{-\tbinom{p}{i}})^{-1}=s_{p}^{-1}.

It follows that
\tau\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(P)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

Let
\rho_{0}:=s_{1}\tau\sigma
,
\rho_{1}:=\beta\tau\sigma
and
\rho_{2}:=\sigma
. Since
o(\beta)=p,o(\tau)=2
and
[\beta,\tau]=1
, we have

(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})^{p}=(\beta\tau)^{p}=\tau
, and hence
\beta=(\rho_{1}\rho_{2})^{p+1}
. It follows that
G=\langle\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\rho_{2}\rangle
