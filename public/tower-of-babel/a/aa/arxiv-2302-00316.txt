Accelerated First-Order Optimization under Nonlinear Constraints
Michael Muehlebach, Michael I. Jordan

Abstract
We exploit analogies between first-order algorithms for constrained optimization and non-smooth dynamical systems to design a new class of accelerated first-order algorithms for constrained optimization. Unlike Frank-Wolfe or projected gradients, these algorithms avoid optimization over the entire feasible set at each iteration. We prove convergence to stationary points even in a nonconvex setting and we derive accelerated rates for the convex setting both in continuous time, as well as in discrete time. An important property of these algorithms is that constraints are expressed in terms of velocities instead of positions, which naturally leads to sparse, local and convex approximations of the feasible set (even if the feasible set is nonconvex). Thus, the complexity tends to grow mildly in the number of decision variables and in the number of constraints, which makes the algorithms suitable for machine learning applications. We apply our algorithms to a compressed sensing and a sparse regression problem, showing that we can treat nonconvex ℓ p \ell^{p} constraints ( p < 1 p<1 ) efficiently, while recovering state-of-the-art performance for p = 1 p=1 .

2
Velocity Constraints

The fundamental idea of this work is to express constraints in terms of the forward increment or velocity of our algorithms instead of constraining the iterates or positions directly. As we will see shortly, this naturally leads to local, sparse and convex approximations of the feasible set. Our treatment builds upon
Muehlebach and Jordan 2022
, which focused on gradient descent and gradient flow, whereas this work focuses on accelerated gradient algorithms. The section briefly summarizes the results from
Muehlebach and Jordan 2022
in order to set the stage for deriving accelerated algorithms in Sec.
3
and quantify their convergence rates in Sec.
4
.

We model an optimization algorithm as a continuous-time or discrete-time dynamical system, whose equilibria correspond to the stationary points of (
1
). In continuous time, the configuration of the system will be denoted by a function
x:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}

A fundamental observation, lying at the heart of the current research, is that the constraint
x(t)\in C
, for all

\dot{x}(t)^{+}\in T_{C}(x(t))

t\geq 0

\dot{x}(t)^{+}:=\lim_{\mathrm{d}t\downarrow 0}(x(t+\mathrm{d}t)-x(t))/\mathrm{d}t

,

T_{C}(x)=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \nabla g_{i}(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}v\geq 0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall i\in I_{x}\},

where

denotes the set of active inequality constraints at

We therefore conclude that the constraint

Accelerated Gradient Flow

We begin our presentation with a derivation in continuous time. The corresponding discrete-time algorithms will be stated subsequently. A natural starting point is the work of authors such as
Polyak 1964
,
Su et al. 2016
,
and
Muehlebach and Jordan 2019
, who argued that in the
unconstrained
case, accelerated optimization algorithms can be viewed as dynamical systems described by second-order differential equations. A canonical example is the following:

\dot{u}(t)+2\delta u(t)+\nabla f(x(t)+\beta u(t))=0,

(6)

where we use the variable
u(t)=\dot{x}(t)
to denote the velocity (or momentum), and where
\delta\geq 0
and
\beta\geq 0
are damping parameters.
5
5

5

The variables
\delta
,
\beta
may also depend on time. For ease of presentation we focus on the case where
\delta
and
\beta
are fixed, but also state corresponding results for time-varying parameters.

In the presence of constraints,
u(t)
is allowed to be discontinuous, which is in sharp contrast to (
6
). For example, if the trajectory
x(t)
approaches the boundary of the feasible set, an instantaneous jump in
u(t)
might be required to ensure that
x(t)
remains feasible. Thus, compared to (
4
), where the state
x(t)
is absolutely continuous, we are now in a position where we allow for the state
(x(t),u(t))
(which includes the velocity
u
) to be discontinuous. This means that in addition to a differential equation of the type (
4
), which characterizes the smooth motion, we also prescribe how the discontinuities in
u
can arise. If we regard
(x(t),u(t))
as the position and velocity of a mechanical system, discontinuities in

have a mechanical meaning as impacts, which are described by a corresponding impact law. The mathematical formalism, which enables discontinuities in
u

We regard the state
z:=(x,u)
to be the result of an integration process:

z(t)=z(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}z,\quad\forall t\geq t_{0}.

However, instead of the usual Lebesgue density
\mathrm{d}z=\dot{z}(t)\mathrm{d}t
,
\mathrm{d}z
now represents a differential measure
(
Leine and van de Wouw 2008
)
, and admits both a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure (denoted by
\mathrm{d}t
), as well as a density with respect to an atomic measure (denoted by
\mathrm{d}\eta
). As is common in non-smooth mechanics, we assume that
z(t)
is of locally bounded variation and does not contain any singular terms. This means that
z(t)
can be decomposed as an absolutely continuous function and a piecewise constant step function
(
Leine and van de Wouw 2008
)
. At every time

z(t)
has well-defined left and right limits,
z(t)^{-}
and
z(t)^{+}
, even though
z(t)
might not exist or might not be of interest. We can express the differential measure
\mathrm{d}z
as

\mathrm{d}z=\dot{z}(t)\mathrm{d}t+(z(t)^{+}-z(t)^{-})\mathrm{d}\eta,

and the integration over an interval
[t_{0},t]
, which contains the time instants
t_{\text{d}i}
,
i=1,2,\dots
, where
z(t)
is discontinuous, yields

z(t)^{+}=z(t_{0})^{-}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\dot{z}(t)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i\geq 1}z(t_{\text{d}i})^{+}-z(t_{\text{d}i})^{-}.

As a consequence of allowing the state to be discontinuous, we need to delineate both the density
\dot{z}(t)
with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}t
(which describes the smooth part of the motion) as well as the density
z(t)^{+}-z(t)^{-}
(which describes the non-smooth motion) for fully determining the state trajectory
z(t)
. By analogy to non-smooth mechanics
(
Studer 2009
, see, e.g.,)

\mathrm{d}u+2\delta u\mathrm{d}t+\nabla f(x+\beta u)\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i\in I_{x}}\nabla g_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i},\quad\gamma_{i}^{+}+\epsilon\gamma_{i}^{-}\in N_{\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}}(-\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}),\quad i\in I_{x},

(7)

where
\epsilon\in[0,1)
is a constant,

is the velocity associated with the

\gamma_{i}(x,u):=\nabla g_{i}(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u+\alpha g_{i}(x),

and where we have omitted the dependence on

I_{x}
(or

in full notation) is time-dependent.
The normal cone inclusion in (
7

1

f(x(t))-f(x^{*})\leq\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}|x(0)-x^{*}|^{2}+f(x(0))-f(x^{*})\right)\rho(t),

where
x^{*}
is a minimizer of (
1
) and
\lambda^{*}
is a multiplier that satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions.

Proof.

i) Heavy ball (
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu}
):

We start by analyzing the heavy ball variant (
\alpha=\sqrt{\mu}
) and consider the evolution of the function

W(t)=\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(x(t)-x^{*})+u(t)|^{2}+f(x(t))-f(x^{*}),

along the trajectories
(x(t),u(t))
. The corresponding differential measure
\mathrm{d}W
is given by
(
Leine and van de Wouw 2008
)
:

(\alpha(x-x^{*})+(u^{+}+u^{-})/2)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha u\penalty\ \mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}u)+\nabla f(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u\penalty\ \mathrm{d}t.

By following the same steps as in the proof of Thm.
1

(u^{+}+u^{-})/2^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\mathrm{d}u+\nabla f(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u\penalty\ \mathrm{d}t\leq-2\delta|u|^{2}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i\in I_{x}}-\alpha g_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}.

However, in contrast to Thm.
1

-\alpha g_{i}(x)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}
remains. This yields the following upper bound on
\mathrm{d}W

\mathrm{d}W\leq-(2\delta-\alpha)|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\alpha(x-x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla f(x)\mathrm{d}t+(\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\delta)(x-x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u\mathrm{d}t\\
+\sum_{i\in I_{x}}(\alpha\nabla g_{i}(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x-x^{*})-\alpha g_{i}(x))\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}.

(15)

The fact that
f
is strongly convex means that the following holds:

\displaystyle-(x-x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla f(x)\leq

\displaystyle-(f(x)-f(x^{*}))-\frac{\mu}{2}|x-x^{*}|^{2}.

In addition, each
g_{i}
is concave, and therefore

\nabla g_{i}(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x^{*}-x)\geq g_{i}(x^{*})-g_{i}(x)\geq-g_{i}(x),

where we used the fact that
x^{*}
is feasible for the last inequality. This concludes that the summand in (
15

\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}\geq 0
. Thus, after some elementary manipulations, we obtain the following upper bound on

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W\leq

\displaystyle-\alpha W\mathrm{d}t-(2\delta-3\alpha/2)|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq-\alpha W\mathrm{d}t.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality then implies the desired upper bound on
f(x(t))-f(x^{*})
.

The lower bound is obtained from a perturbation analysis, using an argument similar to
Muehlebach and Jordan 2022
. We define

f^{*}(t):=\min_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(\xi),\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g(\xi)\geq\min\{0,g(x(0))\}e^{-\alpha t},

which is of the form (
1

satisfies

and is therefore a feasible candidate for the above minimization, which implies
f(x(t))\geq f^{*}(t)
. The minimum is clearly attained, since
f
is bounded below,
f(x)\rightarrow\infty
for

, and the modified set of feasible points is closed. The multiplier
\lambda^{*}
satisfying the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions of (
1

\lambda^{*}
is guaranteed to satisfy the following inequality
(see, e.g.,
Rockafellar 1970

f^{*}(t)-f^{*}\geq\min\{0,g(x(0))\}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\lambda^{*}e^{-\alpha t},

which combined with
f(x(t))\geq f^{*}(t)
implies the desired lower bound.

\alpha=\sqrt{\mu}-\mu/2

We consider again the evolution of the function

(see i)) along the trajectories
(x(t),u(t))
. The corresponding differential measure is given by

\mathrm{d}W=(\alpha(x-x^{*})+(u^{+}+u^{-})/2)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\\

From the proof of Thm.

(16)

Including the remaining terms yields the following upper bound on

(17)

where we introduced the variable

-(x-x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla f(y)\leq-(f(x)-f(x^{*}))-\beta(\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y))^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u-\mu\beta^{2}|u|^{2}\\

In addition,
C
is convex, which implies that each

where we used the fact that

is feasible for the last inequality. The summand in (

since, by definition
\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}\geq 0

\mathrm{d}W\leq-\alpha W\mathrm{d}t-(2\delta-3\alpha/2+\mu\beta)|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\mu-\alpha^{3})|x-x^{*}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\\

where we have used the fact that

iii) Nesterov - varying parameters (
\alpha(t)=2/(t+3)

The proof follows the same steps. We consider again the evolution of the function

W(t)=\frac{1}{2}|\alpha(t)(x(t)-x^{*})+u(t)|^{2}+f(x(t))-f(x^{*}),

(x(t),u(t))

\mathrm{d}W=(\alpha(x-x^{*})+(u^{+}+u^{-})/2)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha u\mathrm{d}t+\dot{\alpha}(x-x^{*})\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}u)+\nabla f(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u\mathrm{d}t,

where according to the chain rule the derivative of

\alpha\dot{\alpha}|x-x^{*}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{\alpha}u^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x-x^{*})\mathrm{d}t.

As a result, by following the same steps as in the variant ii) (see previous section), we obtain

\mathrm{d}W\leq-\alpha W\mathrm{d}t-(2\delta-3\alpha/2+\mu\beta)|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}(\alpha\mu-\alpha^{3}-2\alpha\dot{\alpha})|x-x^{*}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t\\

We note that

vanishes. The same applies to

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}W\leq-\alpha W\mathrm{d}t-\mu\beta|u|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\frac{1}{2}\alpha\mu|x-x^{*}|^{2}\mathrm{d}t-\alpha\mu\beta(x-x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u\mathrm{d}t.

Applying Young’s inequality to the cross-term

\displaystyle W(t)\leq W(0)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\alpha(s)\mathrm{d}s\right)=W(0)\frac{9}{(t+3)^{2}},

and concludes the proof.
∎
We also demonstrate convergence of the discrete algorithm (

Theorem 3
.

Let
T_{k}=T_{0}/k^{s}

converges to zero.

Proof.

The update (
8

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{k}

\displaystyle=x_{k},\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ x_{k+1}=\bar{x}_{k}+u_{k+1}T_{k},

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{k}

\displaystyle=u_{k}-T_{k}f_{\text{d}}(x_{k},u_{k})+R_{k},\qquad u_{k+1}=\bar{u}_{k}-T_{k}\nabla f(\bar{x}_{k}),

(19)

where

contains the dissipative terms and

denotes the constraint forces. The first step, which maps
(x_{k},u_{k})

(\bar{x}_{k},\bar{u}_{k})
is an update of the velocity with the dissipative terms and the constraint forces
R_{k}

(\bar{x}_{k},\bar{u}_{k})

is a symplectic Euler discretization that captures the conservative parts of the underlying dynamics. In the following we will exploit the fact that the second step is a symplectic map. More precisely, the fact that the second step is symplectic implies that an energy function
V_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(bounded below, uniformly in

V_{k+1}(x_{k+1},u_{k+1})-V_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})\leq-c_{\text{V}1}T_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}-c_{\text{V}2}|R_{k}|^{2}+c_{\text{V}3}T_{k}^{2}-\alpha\sum_{i\in I_{x_{k}}}\lambda_{k}^{i}g_{i}(x_{k})

for
k\geq k_{0}
and for some constants
k_{0}>0
,

. A detailed derivation of this step is included in App.
10
(see Lemma
12
). Furthermore, as a result of the diminishing step-size, we infer that
g_{i}(x_{k})\geq-c_{\text{g}}T_{k}
holds for all sufficiently large

and a constant
c_{\text{g}}>0
(see Lemma
9
in App.
10
). We can therefore bound the terms
\lambda_{k}^{i}g_{i}(x_{k})

|-\alpha\sum_{i\in I_{x_{k}}}\lambda_{k}^{i}g_{i}(x_{k})|\leq\frac{\alpha}{c_{\lambda}}c_{\text{g}}T_{k}|R_{k}|n_{\text{g}}\leq\frac{c_{\text{V}2}}{2}|R_{k}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2c_{\text{V}2}}(n_{\text{g}}c_{\text{g}}\frac{\alpha}{c_{\lambda}})^{2}T_{k}^{2},

(20)

where we have exploited constraint qualification to conclude that

(see Lemma
10
in App.
10
) in the first step, and we have used Young’s inequality in the second step. We therefore obtain

V_{k+1}(x_{k+1},u_{k+1})-V_{k}(x_{k},u_{k})\leq-c_{\text{V}1}T_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}-\frac{c_{\text{V}2}}{2}|R_{k}|^{2}+\bar{c}_{\text{V}3}T_{k}^{2},

(21)

for large enough
k
and a modified constant

. We further introduce the constants

-c_{\text{V}0}=\inf_{k}V_{k}(x_{k},u_{k}),\qquad c_{\text{T}1}=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}T_{k}^{2},

and make the following claim: There exists a subsequence

-c_{\text{V}1}T_{k(j)}|u_{k(j)}|^{2}-\frac{c_{\text{V}2}}{2}|R_{k(j)}|^{2}\geq\underbrace{\left(-\bar{c}_{\text{V}3}-\frac{V_{k_{0}}(x_{k_{0}},u_{k_{0}})+c_{\text{V}0}}{c_{\text{T}1}}-\frac{1}{c_{\text{T}1}}\right)}_{:=-c_{\text{V}4}}T_{k(j)}^{2},

For the sake of contradiction we assume that the claim is not true, which means that

-c_{\text{V}1}T_{k}|u_{k}|^{2}-\frac{c_{\text{V}2}}{2}|R_{k}|^{2}\leq-{c}_{\text{V}4}T_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle-c_{\text{V}0}-V_{k_{0}}(x_{k_{0}},u_{k_{0}})

which leads to the desired contradiction since the right-hand side approaches
-c_{\text{V}_{0}}-V_{k_{0}}(x_{k_{0}},u_{k_{0}})-1
for

Upon passing to another subsequence we infer that
u_{k(j)}\rightarrow 0

R_{k(j)}/T_{k(j)}\rightarrow\bar{R}
, and

we conclude that

\bar{x}

\bar{R}

11

We now prove that the entire sequence converges. We infer from (

where
\bar{x}
is the limit of

(see above). We further introduce the collection of all accumulation points of

, that is,

where

is connected (since

|u_{k+1}-u_{k}|\rightarrow 0

|u|^{2}/2+f(x)=f(\bar{x})
.

We claim that
\omega=\{(\bar{x},0)\}
. For the sake of contradiction, we assume the existence of a sequence
(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{u}_{k})\rightarrow(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})
,
(\tilde{x}_{k},\tilde{u}_{k})\in\omega

(\tilde{x},\tilde{u})\neq(\bar{x},0)
(
\omega

\bar{x}
lies in the interior of
C
(constraints are not active). In that case

lies likewise in the interior of
C
for large

. However, in the absence of constraints the dynamics in (
8
) are smooth, which means that
\tilde{x}_{k}
and
\tilde{u}_{k}
are guaranteed to be equilibria and therefore satisfy

Thus, we conclude

where we have used the fact that
T_{k}(1-2\delta T_{k})=\beta
to simplify the objective function. We note that
x=\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})+x_{k}
is a feasible solution candidate in the minimization (
24
). This follows from the following reasoning:

\displaystyle\nabla g_{i}(y_{k})(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})+x_{k}-y_{k})

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\nabla g_{i}(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k}-y_{k})+\alpha\nabla g_{i}(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x^{*}-y_{k})

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha)(g_{i}(x_{k})-g_{i}(y_{k}))+\alpha(g_{i}(x^{*})-g_{i}(y_{k})),

where concavity of
g
is used in the second step together with the fact that
T_{k}=1
and
g_{i}(x^{*})\geq 0
. As a result, the stationarity condition of (
24
) implies

(x_{k+1}-y_{k}+\nabla f(y_{k}))^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})+x_{k}-x_{k+1})\geq 0.

This inequality can be restated as follows:

(x_{k+1}-y_{k}+\nabla f(y_{k}))^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k}-(x_{k+1}-y_{k}))\\
=-|\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})|^{2}-\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})\\
+\nabla f(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})-\nabla f(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k})\geq 0.

Rearranging this inequality yields the following bound:

\nabla l(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k})\leq-|\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})|^{2}-\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})\\
+\nabla f(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla g(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k}).

(25)

As a result of this inequality and the smoothness of
l
, we can bound the evolution of
l(x_{k+1})
as

\displaystyle l(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq l(y_{k})+\nabla l(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k})+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq l(y_{k})-\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})|^{2}-\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ +\nabla f(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla g(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k}).

(26)

By combining (
26
) with (
23
) we obtain

\displaystyle V_{k+1}-V_{k}\leq-\delta(1-\frac{3\delta}{2})|u_{k}|^{2}+\nabla f(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\alpha(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})+l(y_{k})-l(x_{k})

\displaystyle-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla g(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k})+\delta^{2}u_{k}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x^{*}-x_{k}),

(27)

where we have exploited that
\alpha=\delta
and the fact that all the terms containing
\nabla\bar{l}(y_{k})
cancel out (in fact the Lyapunov function and the algorithm (
22
) are specifically engineered in this way). The strong convexity of
f
implies that

\nabla f(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\delta(x^{*}-x_{k})-\beta u_{k})\leq-\delta(f(x_{k})-f(x^{*}))-\frac{\delta\mu}{2}|x^{*}-x_{k}|^{2}-\delta\beta\mu(x_{k}-x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u_{k}\\
-\frac{\mu}{2}\beta^{2}|u_{k}|^{2}+f(x_{k})-f(y_{k});

(28)

see
Muehlebach and Jordan 2019
, which yields, combined with (
27
),

V_{k+1}-V_{k}\leq-\delta(1-\frac{3\delta}{2}+\frac{\beta^{2}\mu}{2\delta})|u_{k}|^{2}-\frac{\delta\mu}{2}|x^{*}-x_{k}|^{2}+\delta(\beta\mu+\delta)(x^{*}-x_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u_{k}\\
-\delta(f(x_{k})-f(x^{*}))-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}g(y_{k})+{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}g(x_{k})-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla g(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k}).

This can be rearranged as follows:

\!\!\!V_{k+1}-V_{k}\leq-\delta V_{k}-\frac{1}{2}(\delta-\delta^{2}-\delta^{3}+\beta^{2}\mu)|u_{k}|^{2}-\frac{\delta\mu-\delta^{3}}{2}|x^{*}-x_{k}|^{2}+\delta(\beta\mu+\delta^{2})(x^{*}-x_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u_{k}\\
+(1-\delta){\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}g(x_{k})-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}g(y_{k})-{\lambda^{*}}^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\nabla g(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k}).

We further note that the inequality constraint in (
24
) implies

-\nabla g_{i}(y_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(x_{k+1}-y_{k})+(1-\delta)g(x_{k})-g(y_{k})\leq 0,

and therefore we obtain

V_{k+1}-V_{k}\leq-\delta V_{k}-\frac{1}{2}(\delta-\delta^{2}-\delta^{3}+\beta^{2}\mu)|u_{k}|^{2}-\frac{\delta\mu-\delta^{3}}{2}|x^{*}-x_{k}|^{2}+\delta(\beta\mu+\delta^{2})(x^{*}-x_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u_{k}.

We note that
\beta\mu=(1-\mu)\delta^{2}
and therefore
\beta\mu+\delta^{2}=\delta^{2}(2-\mu)
. We now apply Young’s inequality to the term
(x^{*}-x_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u_{k}
and conclude

-\frac{1}{2}(\delta-\delta^{2}-\delta^{3}+\beta^{2}\mu)|u_{k}|^{2}-\frac{\delta\mu-\delta^{3}}{2}|x^{*}-x_{k}|^{2}+\delta^{3}(2-\mu)(x^{*}-x_{k})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}u_{k}\leq 0,

for all
\kappa_{l}\geq 1
,
x^{*}-x_{k}
, and
u_{k}
. This yields
V_{k+1}-V_{k}\leq-\delta V_{k}
and concludes the proof.
∎

4
requires knowledge of the smoothness constant of

, which can be restrictive for certain applications. However, by following the same argument as in App.

we obtain the following corollary that proves a
\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\varepsilon})
convergence rate if

is unknown.

Corollary 5
.

Let the function
f
be
\mu
-strongly convex and
L
-smooth, let
g
be concave and
L_{\text{g}}
-smooth, and let

B:=\max_{x\in C}|\nabla f(x)|^{2}/(2\mu).

Then, the iterate
x_{N}
of (
22
) with

,
\delta=\sqrt{L_{l}}/(\sqrt{\kappa_{l}}+1)
,
\beta=T_{k}(1-2\delta T_{k})
,
\alpha=\delta
, and
L_{l}=L+BL_{\text{g}}/\varepsilon
satisfies

, where

N\!\geq\!2\sqrt{\frac{L\!+\!BL_{\text{g}}/\varepsilon}{\mu}}\!\left(\!\!2\log(1/\varepsilon)\!+\!\log\left(\frac{5L\!+\!5BL_{\text{g}}/\varepsilon}{4\mu}\right)\!+\!2\log(|x^{*}-x_{0}|)\!\right)\!=\!\mathcal{O}\!\left(\!\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\!\right),

and
x^{*}
denotes the minimizer of

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\penalty\ \text{s.t.}\penalty\ g(x)\geq-\varepsilon
.

Thm.
4
further requires strong convexity of the objective function. We also obtain an accelerated rate when
f
is convex and smooth, but not strongly convex. This is summarized in the following corollary, which adds a small, but well-chosen, regularization to
f
to render the problem strongly convex.

Corollary 6
.

Let the objective function
f
be convex and
L
-smooth, let
g
be concave and
L_{g}
-smooth, and let
\nabla^{2}f(x^{*})-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}\lambda^{*}_{i}\nabla^{2}g_{i}(x^{*})
be positive definite, where
\lambda^{*}
denotes an optimal multiplier of (
1
). Let
L_{l}
denote the smoothness constant of the Lagrangian
l(x)=f(x)-g(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\lambda^{*}
and consider the modified objective function

f_{\mu}(x)=f(x)+\mu|x-x_{0}|^{2}/2\quad\text{with}\quad\mu=\frac{16L_{l}\mathrm{log}(N)^{2}}{N^{2}}.

Then, the iterate
x_{N}
of (
22
), when applied to
f_{\mu}
with
\kappa_{l}=L_{l}/\mu
,
T_{k}=1/\sqrt{2L_{l}}
,
\delta=\sqrt{2L_{l}}/(\sqrt{2\kappa_{l}}+1)
,
\beta=T_{k}(1-2\delta T_{k})
,
\alpha=\delta
, and
u_{0}=0
satisfies

l(x_{N})-l(x^{*})\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mathrm{log}(N)^{2}}{N^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We consider the regularized Lagrangian
l_{\mu}(x):=f_{\mu}(x)-g(x)^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\lambda_{\mu}^{*}
, where
\lambda_{\mu}^{*}
denotes the optimal multiplier of (
1
) with
f
replaced by
f_{\mu}
, and define
\lambda^{*}:=\lim_{\mu\downarrow 0}\lambda_{\mu}^{*}
. We further introduce the function
d(\lambda,\mu):=\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{\mu}(x)-\lambda^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}g(x)
and note that
\nabla_{\lambda}d(\lambda,\mu)
is given by

\nabla_{\lambda}d(\lambda,\mu)=-g(x_{\mu}^{*}),\quad\text{where\penalty\ $x_{\mu}^{*}$\penalty\ is defined via}\penalty\ \nabla f_{\mu}(x_{\mu}^{*})-\nabla g(x_{\mu}^{*})\lambda=0.

Due to the fact that
H:=\nabla^{2}f(x^{*})-\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}\lambda_{i}^{*}\nabla^{2}g_{i}(x^{*})
is positive definite, we conclude from the implicit function theorem that
x^{*}_{\mu}
is a continuously differentiable function of
\mu
and
\lambda
in a neighborhood of
\mu=0
,
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
, and has derivatives

\frac{\partial x^{*}_{\mu}}{\partial\lambda}=H^{-1}\nabla g(x^{*}),\qquad\frac{\partial x^{*}_{\mu}}{\partial\mu}=-H^{-1}(x^{*}-x_{0})

at
\mu=0,\lambda=\lambda^{*}
. This means that
\nabla_{\lambda}d(\lambda,\mu)
is continuously differentiable in both arguments and that
\nabla^{2}_{\lambda}d(\lambda,\mu)
is strictly negative definite in a neighborhood around
\mu=0
,
\lambda=\lambda^{*}
. We now apply Robinson’s implicit function theorem, see, e.g.,
(
Dontchev and Rockafellar 2014

which yields

|\lambda_{\mu}^{*}-\lambda^{*}|\leq|x^{*}-x_{0}|c_{1}\mu,

for all
\mu
in a neighborhood of the origin and where
c_{1}\geq 0
is constant (the scaling with
|x^{*}-x_{0}|
arises from the derivative
\partial x_{\mu}^{*}/\partial\mu
). This means that for large enough
N
, the modified Lagrangian
l_{\mu}
is
2L_{l}
-smooth. Hence, as a result of Thm.
4
, we conclude

l_{\mu}(x_{N})-l_{\mu}(\tilde{x}^{*})\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{1+\sqrt{2\kappa_{l}}}N\right)\left(\frac{5L_{l}}{4}|x_{0}-\tilde{x}^{*}|^{2}\right),

where we have used the fact that
e^{-\xi}\geq 1-\xi
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, as well as the
2L_{l}
-smoothness of
l_{\mu}
. In addition,
\tilde{x}^{*}
denotes the minimizer of (
1
) with
f
replaced by
f_{\mu}
. As a result of the choice of
\mu
we note that
\sqrt{2\kappa_{l}}=N/(2\mathrm{log}(N)\sqrt{2})
and
1+\sqrt{2\kappa_{l}}\leq N/(2\mathrm{log}(N))
for large
N
. Hence, we can simplify the right-hand side of the previous inequality for large
N
as follows

l_{\mu}(x_{N})-l_{\mu}(\tilde{x}^{*})\leq\exp\left(-2\mathrm{log}(N)\right)\left(\frac{5L_{l}}{4}|x_{0}-\tilde{x}^{*}|^{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\frac{5L_{l}}{4}|x_{0}-\tilde{x}^{*}|^{2}\right).

We now relate
l_{\mu}
to
l
in the following way:

\displaystyle(f(x_{N})-g(x_{N})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\lambda_{\mu}^{*})-(f(x^{*})-g(x^{*})^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}\lambda_{\mu}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq l_{\mu}(x_{N})-l_{\mu}(x^{*})+\frac{\mu}{2}|x^{*}-x_{0}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq l_{\mu}(x_{N})-l_{\mu}(\tilde{x}^{*})+\frac{\mu}{2}|x^{*}-x_{0}|^{2},

where we have used the fact that
l_{\mu}(\tilde{x}^{*})\leq l_{\mu}(x^{*})
in the second step. From the construction of
f_{\mu}
we note that
|\tilde{x}^{*}-x_{0}|\leq|x^{*}-x_{0}|
, which yields, when combining the previous two inequalities,

l(x_{N})-l(x^{*})\leq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left(\frac{5L_{l}}{4}|x_{0}-x^{*}|^{2}\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}|x^{*}-x_{0}|^{2}+(g(x_{N})-g(x^{*}))^{\mathsf{\tiny{T}}}(\lambda_{\mu}^{*}-\lambda^{*}).

The result follows from the choice of
\mu
(i.e.,
\mu=\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{log}(N)^{2}/N^{2})
), and the fact that
|\lambda_{\mu}^{*}-\lambda^{*}|\leq|x^{*}-x_{0}|c_{1}\mu
.
∎

5
Numerical Examples

The following section is divided into two parts. The first part illustrates the dynamics of (
7
) and the discretization via (
8
) and (
22
) on a one-dimensional example and is intended to provide insights concerning the non-smooth dynamics, as well as the discretization. The second part applies (
8
) and (
22
) to (nonconvex) compressed sensing and large-scale sparse regression problems. As we will see, our algorithm recovers state-of-the-art performance for convex relaxations, while also handling nonconvex sparsity constraints in a seamless manner (traditional projection-based methods cannot be easily extended to this setting).

5.1
Illustrative example

In order to plot trajectories in the phase space we choose
f(x)=(x+2)^{2}/2
and
g(x)=(x,-x+2)
, where
x
is scalar. Each constraint
g_{i}(x)\geq 0
and its corresponding velocity constraint
\gamma_{i}(x)\geq 0
induces a region,

\mathcal{R}_{i}:=\{(x,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\penalty\ |\penalty\ g_{i}(x)\leq 0,\gamma_{i}(x,u)\leq 0\},

in the phase space,
i=1,2
, where trajectories are either non-smooth or slide along the boundary of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
. Outside of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
, the trajectories follow the smooth motion (
6
). The first panel in Fig.
2
shows the continuous-time trajectories (
7
) along with
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
. For a given
(x(t_{0}),u(t_{0})^{-})
an impact happens if
g_{i}(x(t_{0}))\leq 0
and
\gamma_{i}(x(t_{0}),u(t_{0}))^{-}<0
, which ensures that
\gamma_{i}(x(t_{0}),u(t_{0}))^{+}\geq-\epsilon\gamma_{i}(x(t_{0}),u(t_{0}))^{-}
. In our example only the case
\gamma_{i}^{+}=-\epsilon\gamma_{i}^{-}
occurs, as there are no impacts where more than one constraint participates (
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
are disjoint). The coefficient of restitution
\epsilon
therefore determines the velocity after impact. For
\epsilon=0
trajectories end up at the boundary of the set
\mathcal{R}_{i}
, whereas for
\epsilon>0
they will leave
\mathcal{R}_{i}
(in case of impact). If
g_{i}(x(t_{0}))\leq 0
,
\gamma_{i}(x(t_{0}),u(t_{0}))^{-}=0
, no impact occurs, (
u(t_{0})=u(t_{0})^{-}=u(t_{0})^{+}
), and trajectories either leave
\mathcal{R}_{i}
or slide along its boundary. This depends on the contribution of the unconstrained dynamics, that is, on the vector

v_{\text{uc}}(t_{0}):=(u(t_{0}),-2\delta u(t_{0})-\nabla f(x(t_{0})+\beta u(t_{0}))).

If
v_{\text{uc}}(t_{0})
points outwards, trajectories will leave
\mathcal{R}_{i}
and follow the unconstrained motion (
\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}=0
). If
v_{\text{uc}}(t_{0})
points inwards, there will be a contribution from
\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{i}(t_{0})\mathrm{d}t
, which ensures that trajectories slide along the boundary of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

The second panel in Fig.
2
shows the trajectories resulting from a discretization according to (
8
) with
T_{k}=T=0.1
. We can clearly see the consequences of including constraints on the velocity level: Trajectories may become infeasible, since constraints enter (
8
) only once they are violated. Nevertheless, even for large time steps
T_{k}=T
(up to
T\approx 1.8
), trajectories converge to the unique minimizer of our problem. The third panel in Fig.
2
shows trajectories from the discretization according to (
22
) with
T=0.1
. We note that in (
22
) each constraint is permanently active (that is,
I=[n_{\text{g}}]
in (
2
)), which explains the contrast between the trajectories in the second and third panel. In this example the dynamics (
22
) are more robust with respect to large time steps, and in fact, convergence can still be observed for
T=3
.

5.2
Nonconvex compressed sensing and image reconstruction

We consider the following
\ell^{p}
-regularized inverse problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}|Ax-b|^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad|x|_{p}^{p}\leq\nu,

(29)

where
|x|_{p}
refers to the
\ell^{p}
“norm” (we explicitly allow for
0<p\leq 1
). This has numerous applications in machine learning, statistics, and signal processing
(
Hastie et al. 2009
, see, e.g.,)
. The traditional convex approach for solving such an inverse problem is to set
p=1
and to leverage the fact that projections onto the
\ell^{1}
ball have closed-form solutions. This yields both accelerated and non-accelerated gradient descent schemes, which in the setting of (
29
) are also known under the name of iterative shrinkage-thresholding (ISTA) and fast iterative shrinkage-thresholding (FISTA)
(
Beck and Teboulle 2009
, see, e.g.,)
. However, when
p<1
, projections onto the
\ell^{p}
“norm” ball no longer have closed-form solutions and it is unclear how to generalize projected gradient algorithms to this setting. Nonetheless the setting
p<1
can be handled easily with our algorithms, as we highlight next.

In order to handle the absolute value and the
p
th power, we add slack variables,
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and reformulate (
29
) as

\min_{(x,\bar{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}}\frac{1}{2}|Ax-b|^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad-\bar{x}\leq x\leq\bar{x},\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\bar{x}_{i})_{\Delta}^{p}\leq\nu,

(30)

where
(\cdot)_{\Delta}^{p}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable and approximates
x^{p}
for
x>0
(
x^{p}
,
p<1
is nondifferentiable at the origin). The approximation
(\cdot)_{\Delta}^{p}
depends on the approximation parameter
\Delta>0
and is defined as

(x)_{\Delta}^{p}:=\begin{cases}x^{p}-\Delta^{p}(1-p)&x\geq\Delta,\\
p\Delta^{p-1}x,&x<\Delta.\end{cases}

For
p=1
our approximation recovers the constraint
|x|_{1}\leq\nu
exactly for any
\Delta>0
. The approximation is depicted in Fig.
1
for
p=0.6
and
\Delta=0.01
and highlights that
x^{p}
is approximated well even for modest values of
\Delta
. In the numerical examples
\Delta
is typically set to
10^{-6}
, which yields an excellent agreement between
x^{p}
and
(x)^{p}_{\Delta}
.

Despite the fact that the constraint in (
30
) is nonlinear and nonconvex for
p<1
, the optimization in (
8
) and (
22
) can be carried out in closed form, which yields the two algorithms Alg.
3
and Alg.
4
stated in App.
9
.

Nonconvex compressed sensing example:

In the first example, each element of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{100\times 1000}
is sampled from a standard normal distribution. The vector
b
is set to
Ax^{*}+n/2
, where the components of
n\in\mathbb{R}^{100}
are sampled from a standard normal and
x^{*}
is a vector that contains zeros everywhere except for 13 randomly chosen entries that are set to one. This gives rise to a challenging and ill-conditioned optimization problem that includes 1000 decision variables. The left panel in Fig.
3
compares the results computed by our algorithm for
p=1
and
p=0.8
, whereas the right panel (solid lines) compares our approach to projected gradient descent and accelerated projected gradient descent for
p=1
. We note: i) the quality of the reconstruction for
p=1
is significantly worse compared to
p=0.8
(the parameter
\nu
was tuned with five-fold cross validation, which yielded
\nu\approx 13
in both cases) and ii) our algorithms decrease the objective function at a similar rate as accelerated projected gradient for
p=1
. Aside from the computation of the gradient, all algorithms have the same
\mathcal{O}(n\text{log}(n))
complexity per iteration, which is determined by a sorting operation that is used for projecting the iterates onto the
\ell^{1}
ball or solving the minimization in (
8
) or (
22
), respectively. It is important to note that for both our algorithms the per-iteration complexity is independent of
p
. Fig.
4
shows the trajectories of Alg.
3
and Alg.

for

and highlights a convergence rate, both in terms of function value and in terms of constraint violation, on the order of
1/k^{2}
also in the nonconvex case.

The second example consists of an image reconstruction problem taken from

6
Conclusion

We have introduced a new type of accelerated optimization algorithm for constrained optimization problems. By imposing constraints on velocities, rather than on positions, the algorithms avoid projections or optimizations over the entire feasible set at each iteration. This not only has the potential to reduce execution time compared to Frank-Wolfe or projected gradient schemes, but more importantly, expands the range of potential applications, as constraints are not necessarily required to be convex or to have a simple structure. We have highlighted important analogies to non-smooth dynamical systems, and characterized the algorithm’s behavior in continuous and discrete time.
