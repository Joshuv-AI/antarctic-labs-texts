Steered discrete-time quantum walks for engineering of quantum states Preprint: APS/123-pED
Gururaj Kadiri

Abstract
We analyze the strengths and limitations of steered discrete time quantum walks in generating quantum states of bipartite quantum systems comprising of a qubit coupled to a qudit system. We demonstrate that not all quantum states in the composite space are accessible through quantum walks, even under the most generalized definition of a quantum step, leading to a bifurcation of the composite Hilbert space into “walk-accessible states” and the “walk-inaccessible states”. We give an algorithm for generating any walk-accessible state from a simple-to-realize product state, in a minimal number of walk steps, all of unit step size. We further give a prescription towards constructing minimal quantum walks between any pair of such walk-accessible states. Linear optics has been a popular physical system for implementing coin-based quantum walks, where the composite space is built up of spin and orbital angular momenta of light beams. We establish that in such an implementation, all normalized quantum states are “walk-accessible”. Furthermore, any generalized quantum step can be implemented upto a global phase using a single q-plate and a pair of homogeneous waveplates. We then give a quantum walk based scheme for realizing arbitrary vector beams, using only q-plates and waveplates.



I
Introduction to quantum walks

The theory of classical random walks has been an indispensable tool to understand statistical phenomena, and has been ubiquitous in modeling of various stochastic processes
Kac 1947
;
Berg 2018
;
Codling et al. 2008
;
Scalas 2006
;
Ibe 2013
. In one-dimensional random walks, the walker tosses a coin and moves forward or backward by one unit, depending upon the outcome of the toss.
The quantum mechanical counterpart of this are the discrete time quantum walks (DTQWs), where the walker and the coin obey the principles of quantum mechanics, and the motion of the walker and the toss of the coin are implemented as unitary transformations. Owing to quantum superposition effects, these walks display distinct statistical properties compared to their classical counterparts.
Quantum walks have proved to be of immense utility in varied domains, like in quantum computation
Aharonov et al. 1993
;
Singh et al. 2021
;
Childs 2009
, quantum search
Portugal 2013
, quantum algorithms
Kendon 2006
;
Campos et al. 2021
, generating random numbers
Sarkar and Chandrashekar 2019
, modeling topological phenomena
Panahiyan and Fritzsche 2021
;
Panahiyan and Fritzsche 2020
;
Cardano et al. 2016
;
Cardano et al. 2017
;
Barkhofen et al. 2017
etc.
In the DTQWs, the walker is modeled as a qudit, belonging to a potentially infinite-dimensional space called the “walk space”, while the coin is modeled as a qubit, belonging to two-dimensional space called the “coin-space”. In this context, another application of such quantum walks explored in recent years has been quantum state engineering (QSE) of high-dimensional quantum systems, which refers to constructing a desired quantum state starting from some simple-to-realize initial state. QSE is realized using DTQWs by carefully steering the quantum walk towards a certain state in the composite space and then projecting out the coin, such that the walker collapses into the desired state on the walk-space
Innocenti et al. 2017
;
Giordani et al. 2019
;
Suprano et al. 2021a
;
Suprano et al. 2021b
;
Zhang et al. 2022
. Such high-dimensional states have myriad applications, since, in general more can be accomplished in a qudit quantum system than in a qubit quantum system
Lanyon et al. 2009
;
Gedik et al. 2015
;
Cozzolino et al. 2019
. For instance, qudits are shown to be more powerful than qubits in tests exploring the foundations of quantum theory
Kaszlikowski et al. 2000
;
Rungta et al. 2001
;
Collins et al. 2002
, in quantum machine learning
Schuld et al. 2015
etc. In quantum cryptography, such states are shown to be more resilient to noise, and also offer a larger secret key rate
Sheridan and Scarani 2010
;
Islam et al. 2017
;
Etcheverry et al. 2013
;
Bechmann-Pasquinucci and Peres 2000
;
Durt et al. 2004
.

Quantum walks have been implemented in various physical systems

: NMR systems
Ryan et al. 2005
;
Du et al. 2003
, on ion traps
Xue et al. 2009
;
Matjeschk et al. 2012
, in circuit QED
Zhou et al. 2019

Karski et al. 2009
, to name a few.
In addition to these, photonic systems have also proved to be a very viable platform for implementing DTQWs, in that they have been demonstrated under various settings like, time-bin encoding

, interferometers

In this paper, we examine the viability of DTQWs in engineering of the quantum states of the composite-space itself instead of those of walk-space alone. In SAM-OAM optical implementation of the quantum walks, these states physically correspond to what are called vector beams, which are light beams having spatially varying polarization distribution across their transverse planes. Such vector light beams have found numerous applications in varied fields

;

;
Zhan 2009
;
Woerdemann et al. 2013
;

The paper is organized as follows: In section

We establish that given a quantum walk it is possible to construct another “simplified” quantum walk that is completely equivalent to it, but consisting of quantum steps of identical step size. Given a pair of walk-accessible states, we also provide here method for obtaining a “minimal quantum walk” that transforms one to the other. In Section

VII

VIII

II
Theoretical background

For carrying out a DTQW, we identity a quantum system with two different degrees of freedom (DOFs) : a qubit DOF called the “coin-space” spanning the Hilbert space
H_{c}
and a qudit DOF called the “walk space” whose states belong to a Hilbert space
H_{w}
.
An arbitrary state
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle\in H_{c}
in the standard basis is given by

\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle=w_{0}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+w_{1}\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle

(1)

where
w_{0}
and
w_{1}
are complex numbers. We associate with every state a unique state
\left|\bm{w}_{\perp}\right\rangle
which is orthogonal to
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
defined in terms of components of
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
in the standard basis as:

\left|\bm{w}_{\perp}\right\rangle=-\bar{w_{1}}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle+\bar{w_{0}}\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle

(2)

where the overbar indicates complex conjugation. Any state orthogonal to
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
will differ
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
only by an overall phase factor. It is to be noted that
\left|(\bm{s}_{\perp})_{\perp}\right\rangle
, the orthogonal state of
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, is not
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
but -
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
.
We use the symbol
\left|\bm{s};m\right\rangle
to represent the product state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\otimes\left|m\right\rangle
, where
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
is a unit vector in
H_{c}
, and
\left|m\right\rangle
is the
m^{th}
standard basis vector in
H_{w}
.

An arbitrary pure state in the composite space
H_{c}\otimes H_{w}
is given by
Ekert and Knight 1995
;
Pathak 2013

\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle=\sum_{m=b}^{m=e}p_{m}\left|\bm{p}_{m};m\right\rangle

(3)

where
\left|\bm{p}_{m}\right\rangle
are unit vectors in the coin-space
H_{c}
, and
p_{m}
are non-negative real numbers. Given a composite state of the form Eq. (
3
), we define the integer interval
[b,e]
as its “position span”, by which we mean that the probability amplitudes at all positions greater than
e
or less than
b
are all zero.

II.1
Coin-based quantum walks

A single step of the quantum walk is a product of these two operators:

\hat{T}=\hat{S}\left(\hat{C}\otimes\hat{I}_{w}\right),

(4)

where
\hat{C}
is an SU(2) operator called the “coin toss operator”:

\hat{C}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\alpha}\cos\beta&-e^{-i\gamma}\sin\beta\\
e^{i\gamma}\sin\beta&e^{-i\alpha}\cos\beta\\
\end{pmatrix}

(5)

in the
\{\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\}
basis of the coin-space.
The shift operator is given by:

\hat{S}=\sum_{m}\left(\left|\bm{0};m-1\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{0};m\right|+\left|\bm{1};m+1\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{1};m\right|\right)

(6)

The initial state, that is, the state at the beginning of the quantum walk is generally taken to be at position
m=0
, in some arbitrary coin-state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
. A quantum walk constitutes
N
such steps starting from this initial state leads to a composite state of the form Eq. (
3
):

\hat{T}^{N}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle

(7)

The state on the RHS
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
is a composite state of the form Eq. (
3
). The role of coin operator leading to different composite states is studied in ref.
Chandrashekar et al. 2008
.

II.2
Quantum State Engineering using quantum walks

We refer to the product state located at the position
0
, that is states of the form
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
, as the “home-states”.
The problem of quantum state engineering is to generate a composite state, like Eq. (
3
), starting from some home-state.
The state on the position space alone, after tracing out the coin degree of freedom from
\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle
is a mixed state whose density matrix is given by

\rho=\textbf{tr}_{coin}(\left|\bm{S}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{S}\right|)=\sum_{m=b}^{m=e}s_{m}^{2}\left|\bm{m}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{m}\right|

(8)

In this manuscript, we intend to address some of the following questions: (i) can all quantum states on the composite space, that is, states of the form Eq. (
3
), be generated by a quantum walk of the form Eq. (
7
) or some reasonable generalization of it, starting from some home-state? (ii) If two composite states can be generated by quantum walks, can those two be connected by a quantum walk? (iii) If the answer to the previous question is in affirmative, can a minimal walk be found that accomplishes it in least number of unit-sized steps?

III
Generalized coin-based quantum walks

Speaking of generalizing the quantum walks, one method has been to employ different coin operator at each step, so that in place of Eq. (
7
), we have:

\hat{T}_{N}\cdots\hat{T}_{1}\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle

(9)

where
\hat{T}_{i}
represents the
i^{th}
quantum step, differing in their coin-flip operators, Eq. (
5
). Another generalization of Eq. (
7
) has been to have different step sizes for forward and backward motion. For instance, in refs.
Montero 2013
;
Montero 2015
;
Innocenti et al. 2017
;
Giordani et al. 2019
, the conditional shift operator employed was the one of moving forward or staying still, depending upon the state of the coin. Owing to the translational symmetry of the problem, this shift operator is shown to be equivalent to the standard shift operator, in the sense that the resulting composite states in the both cases are identical, up to a relabeling of the position states.

It is to be noted that replacing an SU(2) coin with a unitary one will not give access to any different set of states: a composite state accessible with a unitary coin is also accessible with an SU(2) coin. Therefore in the rest of the manuscript, we shall restrict our attention to only SU(2) coins. Further, there are generalizations of the discrete time quantum walks where the coins are site-dependent
Suzuki 2016
;
Panahiyan and Fritzsche 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2022
or history-dependent
Flitney et al. 2004
;
Rohde et al. 2013
;
Di Molfetta et al. 2018
. Such treatment are also beyond the scope of this manuscript, as we intend to consider only those systems which can be readily implemented in the linear optics setting.

III.1
Generalized quantum steps

A single step of the quantum walk is an SU(2) operator
\hat{T}
on the composite system
H_{c}\otimes H_{w}
of the form:

\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})=\hat{S}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s})\left(\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})\otimes I_{w}\right),

(10)

where
\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})
and
\hat{S}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s})
are given by:

\displaystyle\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{c}\right|+\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{c}_{\perp}\right|,

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s})

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}I_{c}\otimes I_{w}+\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\sum_{m}\left(e^{i\delta}\left|\bm{s}_{\perp};m+p\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s};m\right|-e^{-i\delta}\left|\bm{s};m-p\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{s}_{\perp};m\right|\right)

where
\Gamma
and
\delta
are real numbers between
0
and
2\pi
,
p
is an nonzero integer and
I_{c}
and
I_{w}
are identity operators in the coin and walk space respectively. The coin-operator
\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})
is completely equivalent to the coin operator
\hat{C}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
defined in Eq. (
5
), only parameterized differently. Here we have parameterized it by two unit vectors
\bm{c}
and
\bm{s}
of the coin-space. The action of coin-toss operator
\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})
is to unitarily transform the orthogonal pair of states
\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{c}_{\perp}\right\rangle
to another orthogonal pair of states
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, respectively.
The shift operator
\hat{S}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s})
leaves a fraction of the state unaltered, and with the remaining fraction, it moves forward in the position space by
p
units if the coin-state is
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
, and moves backwards by the same number of units if the coin-state is
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
. Simultaneously, it also transforms the coin-states
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
into
e^{i\delta}\left|\bm{s}_{\perp}\right\rangle
and
-e^{-i\delta}\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
respectively.
The action of
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
on the orthogonal states
\left|\bm{c};m\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};m\right\rangle
is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})\left|\bm{c};m\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left|\bm{s};m\right\rangle+\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}e^{i\delta}\left|\bm{s}_{\perp};m+p\right\rangle,

(12)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})\left|\bm{c}_{\perp};m\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left|\bm{s}_{\perp};m\right\rangle-\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}e^{-i\delta}\left|\bm{s};m-p\right\rangle

We shall regard Eq. (
12
) as the definition of the quantum step
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
, being completely equivalent to Eq. (
11
). Its action of
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
on any other product state
\left|\bm{w};m\right\rangle
can obtained by expressing
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
in the basis
(\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{c}_{\perp}\right\rangle)
and acting it linearly on them. Its action on an arbitrary composite state can be found by acting it on each of the constituent terms. In the quantum step
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
, we call the state
\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
as its coin-state and
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
as its shift state. Here,
p
is an integer indicating the step size of the walk. The presence of
\Gamma
makes the quantum step very different from that of Eq. (
4
) as only a fraction of the state now undergoes the quantum walk, with
\Gamma
determining that fraction:
\Gamma=\pi
leading to complete state transfer, while
\Gamma=0
corresponds to no change in the walk space. Such walks have been termed “hybrid walks” in literature
Cardano et al. 2015
. While
\Gamma
can range from
[0,4\pi)
, it is easy to see that
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})=\hat{T}_{4\pi-\Gamma}(\pi+\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
and therefore can be restricted to
\Gamma
to
[0,2\pi]
.
The quantum steps
\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{s},\bm{c})
, i.e., the steps with
\Gamma=0
refer only to SU(2) transformation from
\bm{c}
to
\bm{s}
, acting only on the coin-space:

\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{s},\bm{c})=\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})\otimes I_{w}

(13)

where
\hat{C}(\bm{s},\bm{c})
is the operator defined in the first of Eq. (
11
).
The step-size
p
and the relative phase
\delta
in this case are immaterial, and hence they are suppressed in Eq. (
13
). We call these steps as the “improper steps”, as against the steps with
\Gamma>0
and
p\neq 0
, which we call as the “proper steps”.
The quantum step Eq. (
12
) is such that, in one proper step at most only two other states at a fixed distance (
\pm p
) from the current state, are accessed. We shall regard the step defined in Eq. (
12
) as the most generalized quantum walk step. This claim requires some justification, since we still have the same step-size
p
in both forward and backward directions. This definition of a quantum step is the most general, in the sense that any further relaxation in the definition will be inconsistent with the requirement of translational invariance.
It is essential, for instance, that the coin-states at the
m^{th}
and
(m\pm p)^{th}
positions appearing in the right hand side of Eq. (
12
) be orthogonal to each other. Likewise, having distinct step sizes in the forward and backward directions is not consistent with translational invariance, unless
\Gamma
is fixed to
\pi
. In this manuscript, we have made a choice to force identical step size in the forward and backward directions, retaining the facility to have a variable
\Gamma
instead. This choice is motivated by the linear optics implementation, to be introduced in Section
VII
.
It must be noted that the second relation in Eq. (
12
) is not independent, but follows directly from the first, by requiring that
\hat{T}
act as an SU(2) operator in the composite space.

We represent a collection of
M
such steps by the symbol
\hat{W}

\hat{W}=\hat{T}_{\Gamma_{M}}(\delta_{M},p_{m},\bm{s}_{m},\bm{c}_{m})\cdots\hat{T}_{\Gamma_{1}}(\delta_{1},p_{1},\bm{s}_{1},\bm{c}_{1})

(14)

We call these sequences of such steps
\hat{W}
as the “quantum walk”. The walk
\hat{W}
acts on the states from the left. We shall consider the dimension of walk-space to be larger than
N=\textbf{max}(|b|,|e|)
of all the involved composite states, and also larger than
|p_{1}|+|p_{2}|+\cdots|p_{M}|
of all the involved steps.

III.2
Identifying states obtained by quantum walks

A quantum walk of
M
-steps, starting from an arbitrary home-state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
, can be described as:

\hat{W}\left|\bm{u},0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle=\sum_{m=b}^{m=e}u_{m}\left|\bm{u}_{m};m\right\rangle

(15)

Note that not all steps in
\hat{W}
are of equal step size. The position span
[b,e]
of
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
depends on the details of the steps, and also on the initial coin-state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
. All that can be said is that
\textbf{max}(|b|,|e|)\leq(|p_{1}|+|p_{2}|+\cdots|p_{M}|)
, where
p_{i}
is the step size of the
i^{th}
step.
The state on the RHS is a unit vector of the form Eq. (
3
). We now derive a condition that such states have to satisfy.
From translation symmetry, if
\hat{W}\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
then
\hat{W}\left|\bm{u};d\right\rangle=\left|\bm{U}_{+d}\right\rangle
,
where
d
is an integer and
\left|\bm{U}_{+d}\right\rangle
is
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
shifted on the walk forward en-mass by
d
units:

\left|\bm{U}_{+d}\right\rangle=\sum_{n=b}^{n=e}u_{n}\left|\bm{u}_{n};n+d\right\rangle

(16)

Since
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{u};d\right\rangle
are orthogonal, and since the walk operator
\hat{W}
is an SU(2) operator, we demand the resulting states
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{U}_{+d}\right\rangle
to also be orthogonal,
\langle{\bm{U}}|{\bm{U}_{+d}}\rangle=0
, for all
d\neq 0
. The non-trivial
d
being
d=1,\cdots,e-b
, leading to
e-b
constraints:

\langle{\bm{U}}|{\bm{U}_{d}}\rangle=\sum_{m=b}^{m=e-d}u_{m}u_{m+d}\langle{\bm{u_{m}}}|{\bm{u_{m+d}}}\rangle=0,\forall\>d=1,\cdots,e-b

(17)

This result limits the composite states that can be obtained by quantum walks: not all states of the composite space are accessible by quantum walks. We shall refer to the composite states satisfying the constraints of Eq. (
17
) as the “walk-states”, and those composite states not satisfying them as the “non-walk-states”.
For instance, consider the product states in the composite space
H
. They are of the form
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle\otimes\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
, where
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle\in\textbf{span}(\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle)
and
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle\in\textbf{span}(\cdots,\left|\bm{-1}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle,\cdots)
. Of these, the only possible translation invariant product states are of the form
\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle
, where
\left|m\right\rangle\in(\cdots,\left|\bm{-1}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle,\cdots)
.
Likewise, a composite state with only two non-zero OAM components,
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle=w_{b}\left|\bm{w}_{b};b\right\rangle+w_{e}\left|\bm{w}_{e};e\right\rangle
is a walk-state only if the two constituent coin-states are orthogonal:
\langle{\bm{w}_{b}}|{\bm{w_{e}}}\rangle=0
.
Likewise, a composite state
\left|\bm{X}\right\rangle
consisting of three constituent terms:
\left|\bm{X}\right\rangle=x_{b}\left|\bm{x}_{b};b\right\rangle+x_{m}\left|\bm{x}_{m};m\right\rangle+x_{e}\left|\bm{x}_{e};e\right\rangle

,

and

,

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\frac{b+e}{2},

\displaystyle\left|\bm{x}_{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x_{e}^{2}+x_{b}^{2}}}\left(e^{i\delta}x_{e}\left|\bm{x}_{b}\right\rangle-e^{-i\delta}x_{b}\left|\bm{x}_{e}\right\rangle\right),

\displaystyle\langle{\bm{x}_{b}}|{\bm{x_{e}}}\rangle

\displaystyle=0

where
\delta
is an arbitrary phase. This is an interesting result, with respect to the state engineering aspect of the quantum walks. The first of these restricts the possible occupied positions of a three-term walk-state. It is not possible via quantum walks to access composite states where, for instance, only the
\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle
,
\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{3}\right\rangle

x_{0}
,
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
and the coin-states
\left|\bm{x}_{0}\right\rangle
,
\left|\bm{x}_{1}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{x}_{3}\right\rangle

\left|\bm{Y}\right\rangle=y_{b}\left|\bm{y}_{b};b\right\rangle+y_{m}\left|\bm{y}_{m};m\right\rangle+y_{n}\left|\bm{y}_{n};n\right\rangle+y_{e}\left|\bm{y}_{e};e\right\rangle,

\frac{y_{b}}{y_{m}}=\frac{y_{e}}{y_{n}}
, and the coin-states satisfy the relations
\langle{\bm{y}_{b}}|{\bm{y}_{m}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{y}_{m}}|{\bm{y}_{n}}\rangle=\langle{\bm{y}_{n}}|{\bm{y}_{e}}\rangle=0
and
\langle{\bm{y}_{b}}|{\bm{y}_{n}}\rangle+\langle{\bm{y}_{m}}|{\bm{y}_{e}}\rangle=0
. A few illustrations of walk and non-walk states will be given in sub-section (
VI.2
).

III.3
Generating new walk-states from existing ones

It must be noted that a linear combination of two walk-states need not be a walk-state. The set of walk-states, therefore do not form a subspace. This can be easily seen by noting that all the states belonging to the standard product basis
\{\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle\}\otimes\{\cdots,\left|\bm{-1}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle,\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle,\cdots\}
are walk-states, and therefore if the linear combination of walk-states also yielded a walk-state, then every state in the composite space would have been a walk-state.

Given a walk-state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
with position span
[b,e]
, one can generate another walk-state
\left|\bm{U}_{+d}\right\rangle
with span
[b+d,e+d]
by shifting each position
m
in
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
by
d
units. Likewise, we define another walk-state
\left|\bm{U}_{\times d}\right\rangle
as:

\left|\bm{U}_{\times d}\right\rangle=\sum_{n=b}^{n=e}u_{n}\left|\bm{u}_{n};nd\right\rangle

(18)

It is easy to confirm that
\left|\bm{U}_{\times d}\right\rangle
is also a walk-state. If a walk
\hat{W}
generates
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
, then
\left|\bm{U}_{\times d}\right\rangle
can be generated by changing the step size
p_{i}
of each step
\hat{T}_{\Gamma_{i}}(\delta_{i},p_{i},\bm{u}_{i},\bm{c}_{i})
of
\hat{W}
by
p_{i}\times d
.

Recall that the action of a linear operator on an
N
-dimensional space gets fixed only by specifying its action on
N
linearly independent vectors. In case of the walk operator, however, once the action of
\hat{W}
on any state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
is specified, as in Eq. (
15
), then its action on any other state, be it a home-state, a product-state or a composite-state, gets fixed, owing to its being SU(2) and translationally invariant.
Particularly, between orthogonal home-states we have the following relation:

\displaystyle\text{if }

\displaystyle\hat{W}\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle,

(19)

\displaystyle\text{ then }

\displaystyle\hat{W}\left|\bm{u_{\perp}};0\right\rangle=\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle

where, for a given
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
, the composite state
\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle
is defined as

\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle=\sum_{m=-e}^{m=-b}u_{-m}\left|(\bm{u}_{-m})_{\perp};m\right\rangle

(20)

where
\left|(\bm{u}_{-m})_{\perp}\right\rangle
is the state orthogonal to
\left|\bm{u}_{-m}\right\rangle
. It is easy to see that the walk-states
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle
are orthogonal to each other. Further, they share the same characteristic vector,
\overrightarrow{p}(\bm{U})=\overrightarrow{p}(\bm{U}_{\perp})
.
Given a walk-state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
, we can construct another walk-state
\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, and two other families of walk-states
\left|\bm{U}_{+d}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{U}_{\times d}\right\rangle
by shifting and scaling the walk position labels respectively. Furthermore, any complex linear combination of the walk-states
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
and
\left|{({\bm{U}}_{\perp})}_{+d}\right\rangle
is also a legitimate walk-state for any
d
. This facility enables us to construct new walk-states of larger span from smaller ones.

III.4
Characterizing the walk-states

Given two walk-states, we are interested in knowing whether they are a result of same walk
\hat{W}
, but with different home-states or not. Let
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
be the two walk-states originating from home with coin-states
\left|\bm{p}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{q}\right\rangle
respectively, through a walk
\hat{W}
, that is,
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle=\hat{W}\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle=\hat{W}\left|\bm{q};0\right\rangle
. We now discuss the conditions that these walks states must satisfy if they are so related. The position-spans of
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
are not of much help here: two composite states may well share the same position span, but might not have originated from the same walk, or they might have different position-spans but have originated from the same walk. For instance, the first and second states on the RHS of Eq. (
12
) have different position spans,
[b,b+m]
and
[b-m,b]
respectively, but they have originated from the same walk,
\hat{T}
. One necessary condition that can be derived on the basis of position-span is that
N=\textbf{max}(|b|,|e|)
of both the states be identical.
We now derive another necessary condition. For this, we first recall that the states
\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{q};d\right\rangle
are orthogonal in the composite state, for any
d\neq 0
, independent of the relation between the coin-states
\left|\bm{p}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{q}\right\rangle
. Since
\hat{W}
is an SU(2) operator, it preserves this orthogonality, leading to the constraints
\langle{\bm{P}}|{\bm{Q}_{d}}\rangle=0,\forall d\neq 0
, where
\left|\bm{Q}_{d}\right\rangle
is
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
shifted by
d
steps, as in Eq. (
16
).

For obtaining another necessary condition, given a composite state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
, with the span
[b,e]
, we an define an
N+1
-dimensional probability mass function
\overrightarrow{p}(\bm{U})
, where
N=\textbf{max}(|b|,|e|)
as:

\overrightarrow{p}(\bm{U})=(f_{0},f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{N})

(21)

where
f_{m}={u_{m}}^{2}+u_{-m}^{2}
, where
u_{m}
are amplitudes at the position
m
in the composite state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
. From this definition, it is easy to verify that

\displaystyle\text{if }\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle=\hat{W}\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle\text{ and }\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle=\hat{W}\left|\bm{q};0\right\rangle\text{ then }\overrightarrow{p}(\bm{P})=\overrightarrow{p}(\bm{Q})

(22)

The three conditions offered above are only necessary for two walk-states to have emerged from same quantum walk, not sufficient. A necessary and also sufficient condition for two walk-states
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
being output of the same walk is that
\left|\langle{\bm{P}}|{\bm{Q}}\rangle\right|^{2}+\left|\langle{\bm{P}}|{\bm{Q}_{\perp}}\rangle\right|^{2}=1
.

III.5
Characterizing the quantum walks

Having defined a “characteristic vector” for a walk-state, we could, likewise, define it for a quantum walk. Given a
\hat{W}
, we define its characteristic vector
\overrightarrow{p}(\hat{W})
as

\overrightarrow{p}(\hat{W})=\overrightarrow{p}(\hat{W}\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle)

(23)

for some arbitrary coin-state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
. Observe that the
\overrightarrow{p}
appearing on the LHS is that of a walk, while the one appearing on the RHS is that of a walk-state, defined in Eq. (
21
). That this quantity is identical for any
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
follows from Eq. (
22
). The import of characteristic vector of a walk is that it gives the description of its action in terms of walk space alone, without regard to the coin-space. Given an arbitrary product state
\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle
as input, the walk
\hat{W}
leaves a fraction
f_{0}
of it unaltered, alters the position of a fraction
f_{1}
of it by one unit, alters the position of another fraction
f_{2}
of it by two units and so on.
All
\Gamma=0
steps have the characteristic vector
(1)
and those with non-zero
\Gamma
has the characteristic vector of length
p+1
:
(\cos^{2}\frac{\Gamma}{2},\underbrace{0,\cdots,0}_{p-1},\sin^{2}\frac{\Gamma}{2})
.

IV
Targeted quantum walks

It is to be noted that a quantum walk
\hat{W}
preserves the translational invariance of composite states: it takes walk-states to walk-states and non-walk-states to non-walk-states.
Given a step
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
, we define its inverse
[\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})]^{-1}
as the operator that undoes the action of
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})
. The inverse of a quantum step is also a quantum step. It is easy to see that:

[\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})]^{-1}=\hat{T}_{\Gamma}(\pi+\delta,p,\bm{c},\bm{s})

(24)

Since each step of this walk is invertible, the walk
\hat{W}
itself is invertible. So, given any walk-state there always exist quantum walks that takes it to any of the home-states. Further, we show that it is always possible to do it using exactly
N
number of
p=1
quantum steps, where
N=\textbf{max}(|b|,|e|)
.

IV.1
The shrinking algorithm

Here we propose an algorithm to find the a walk of
p=1
quantum steps that can convert the given composite state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
to a home-state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
. We start with the simplest case first: when the composite state is of a single term
\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle
. A quantum step
\hat{T}_{\pi}(0,1,\bm{s},\bm{u}_{\perp})
on
\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle
yields
\left|\bm{s};m-1\right\rangle
. Now, from here, taking
m-1
steps of
\hat{T}_{\pi}(0,1,\bm{s},\bm{s}_{\perp})
, we land on the home-state
\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle
:

\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle={\hat{T}_{\pi}(0,1,\bm{s},\bm{s}_{\perp})}^{m-1}\hat{T}_{\pi}(0,1,\bm{s},\bm{u}_{\perp})\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle

(25)

We now consider the case of reducing an arbitrary walk-state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
to some home-state
\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle
. We achieve using a recursive method: we find a walk step
\hat{T_{1}}
, that can convert the given state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
of walk-spread
[b,e]
into a composite beam
\left|\bm{U}_{1}\right\rangle
of a shorter spread
[b+1,e-1]
. This is always possible if
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
is a walk-state. One set of parameters of
\hat{T}_{1}
that accomplishes this is given by (see appendix):

\displaystyle\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|{\bm{u_{e}}}_{\perp}\right\rangle,

(26)

\displaystyle\Gamma

\displaystyle=2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{u_{e}}{u_{e-1}|\langle{{\bm{u}_{e}}_{\perp}}|{\bm{u}_{e-1}}\rangle|}\right),

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle=\pi-\Phi(\langle{{\bm{u}_{e}}_{\perp}}|{\bm{u}_{e-1}}\rangle).

Now, note that the ensuing state
\left|\bm{U}_{1}\right\rangle
is also a walk-state, having obtained through a quantum step on a walk-state. It can also be reduced to another composite state
\left|\bm{U}_{2}\right\rangle
whose spread is
[b+2,e-2]
, by using another walk step
\hat{T}_{2}
, whose parameters can be computed in a similar manner as in Eq. (
26
). Proceeding this way for a total
\frac{e-b}{2}
steps if
b
and
e
are of same parity, or for
\frac{e-b+1}{2}
steps if they are of different parity, one lands in a separable state
\left|\bm{u};p\right\rangle
, where
p
is equal to
\frac{b+e}{2}
in the former case and
\frac{b+e-1}{2}
in the latter case. Now, if
p=0
, we are at home, and therefore have accomplished what we have set out to. Otherwise, transform
\left|\bm{u};p\right\rangle
into
\left|\bm{s};0\right\rangle
in
p
steps as in Eq. (
25
).
Therefore, in either case, in a total of
N=\textbf{max}(|b|,|e|)
steps it is possible to reduce a composite state of spread
[b,e]
into a home-state:

\hat{T}_{N}\cdots\hat{T}_{2}\hat{T}_{1}\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle=\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle

(27)

The composite state
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
can therefore be constructed from a home-state
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
by taking the inverse of those
N
steps, in the opposite order:

\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle=\hat{\bm{W}}_{min}\left|\bm{u};0\right\rangle

(28)

where

\hat{\bm{W}}_{min}={\hat{T}_{1}}^{-1}\cdots{\hat{T}_{N}}^{-1},

(29)

with the inverse of a walk step
\hat{T}
given by Eq. (
24
). This completes the algorithm to deterministically reach a given walk-state from some home-state.

V
Simplifying the quantum walks

V.1
Simplifying a quantum walk

In Section
IV
, given a walk-state, an algorithm was prescribed for obtaining a walk and a home-state which together generate the given walk-state. Here we discuss a case where the walk
\hat{W}
of the form Eq. (
14
) is given instead of a walk-state. Recall that our definition of the quantum walk allows it to be composed of steps of unequal step-sizes, different coin, shift states etc. Given such a walk, we intend to find another walk
\hat{W}_{min}
, that functions like
\hat{W}
, but consisting steps of identical step-size
p=1
, and all with the same shift state
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
.

It is always possible to absorb improper steps
\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{s},\bm{c})
into a proper quantum step
\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c}\right)
that follows it, as:

\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c}\right)\hat{T}_{0}\left(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{p},\bm{q}\right)=\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{w}\right)

(30)

where the coin-state
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
of the step on the RHS is given by
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle={\hat{T}^{-1}}_{0}\left(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{p},\bm{q}\right)\left|\bm{c}\right\rangle
. We also have these relations:

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(\delta+2\Delta,p,e^{i\Delta}\bm{s},e^{i\Delta}\bm{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c}\right),

(31)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(-\delta,-p,\bm{s}_{\perp},\bm{c}_{\perp}\right)

\displaystyle=\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c}\right)

We have the following relation between two quantum steps:

\hat{T}_{\Gamma_{2}}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{w}\right)\hat{T}_{\Gamma_{1}}\left(\delta,p,\bm{w},\bm{c}\right)\equiv\hat{T}_{\Gamma_{1}+\Gamma_{2}}\left(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c}\right)

(32)

Given a
\hat{W}
, pick up an arbitrary home-state
\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle
, and let
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
be the outcome of quantum walk
\hat{W}
starting with
\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle
. Now, ignoring
\hat{W}
, we could start from
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
and, from the shrinking algorithm of section
IV
, get a walk
\hat{W}_{s}
, and a home-state
\left|\bm{p}_{s}\right\rangle
. Now, how are
\hat{W}_{s}
and
\hat{W}
related? Both lead to the same walk-state, but for different home-states
\hat{W}\left|\bm{p}\right\rangle=\hat{W}_{s}\left|\bm{p}_{s}\right\rangle
. Both the walks have identical characteristic vector:
\overrightarrow{p}(\hat{W})=\overrightarrow{p}(\hat{W}_{s})
. Indeed, they are only related by an SU(2) operation in the coin-space:

\hat{W}=\hat{W}_{s}\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{p}_{s},\bm{p})

(33)

We say the walk on the RHS,
\hat{W}_{s}\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{p}_{s},\bm{p})
, as the walk that “minimizes the walk
\hat{W}
”, and represent it as
\textbf{min}\left(\hat{W}\right)
. The improper step
\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{p}_{s},\bm{p})
can be absorbed into the first step of
\hat{W}_{s}
, as in Eq. (
30
), so that the minimal walk
\textbf{min}\left(\hat{W}\right)
contains only as many steps as are in
\hat{W}_{s}
.

V.2
Quantum walks between two walk-states

While the paper till now discussed the cases of a quantum walk starting from home-states to the composite walk-states, in this subsection we discuss about going from one composite walk-state to another composite walk-state through a minimal quantum walk.
That is, given two walk-states
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
, we seek the minimal quantum walk
\hat{W}_{min}
such that
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle=\hat{W}_{min}\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
. Note that, by virtue of being
SU(2)
, the walk
\hat{W}_{min}
must take
\left|\bm{P}_{\perp}\right\rangle
to
\left|\bm{Q}_{\perp}\right\rangle
. One way to generate
\hat{W}_{min}
is to first obtain some walk
\hat{W}
that accomplishes this, and then get
\hat{W}_{min}
as
\textbf{min}\left(\hat{W}\right)
, using the minimization method disccused above. One such walk that takes
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
to
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
can be generated by making use of the shrinking algorithm as:

\hat{W}=\hat{W}_{Q}\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{q},\bm{p})\hat{W}^{-1}_{P}

(34)

where the walks
\hat{W}_{P}
and
\hat{W}_{Q}
are the minimal walks that take the walk-states
\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
to the home-states
\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{q};0\right\rangle
respectively, and
\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{q},\bm{p})
is the improper step that takes
\left|\bm{p}\right\rangle
to
\left|\bm{q}\right\rangle
in the coin-space:

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{P}\left|\bm{p};0\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{Q}\left|\bm{q};0\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle,

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{0}(\cdot,\cdot,\bm{q},\bm{p})

\displaystyle=\left(\left|\bm{q}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{p}\right|+\left|\bm{q}_{\perp}\right\rangle\left\langle\bm{p}_{\perp}\right|\right)\otimes I_{w}

From these definitions, it is straightforward to verify that
\hat{W}\left|\bm{P}\right\rangle=\left|\bm{Q}\right\rangle
and
\hat{W}\left|\bm{P}_{\perp}\right\rangle=\left|\bm{Q}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, as desired. Note that the quantities appearing on the left-hand side of the above equation,
\hat{W}_{P}
,
\hat{W}_{Q}
,
\left|\bm{p}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{q}\right\rangle
, are not available before hand, and must be computed through the shrinking algorithm of Sec (
IV
).
The minimal quantum walk
\hat{W}_{min}
to accomplish this can then be found by minimizing the above walk
\hat{W}
by the method discussed in subsection
V.1
.

Representing the composite states

\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
of the form Eq. (

\theta_{0}

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
with a single white-colored arrow making an angle
\theta
with the x-axis. The rest of the states, we indicate in terms of the two complex numbers,
w_{0}
and
w_{1}
, the component of
\left|\bm{w}\right\rangle
along
\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle
unit vectors respectively. We represent these complex numbers as two concentric green and red circles of radii proportional to
\left|w_{0}\right|
and
\left|w_{1}\right|
respectively. The phase of these complex numbers are indicated by yellow and cyan colored arrows over these circles, the angles they make with the horizontal indicative of their phases.

This representation can be carried over to the composite states as well. We depict them as follows: on the y-axis we mark the walk-space basis states
\left|m\right\rangle
from
m\in[b,e]
. At every
m
with non-zero
u_{m}
, the corresponding coin-state
\left|\bm{u}_{m}\right\rangle
is depicted as described above, except that the radii of these circles is scaled by the amplitude
u_{m}
.
This representation of composite states, while by no means as versatile as that of Poincare Sphere, will nevertheless serve us well to better illustrate the results derived earlier in the manuscript.

VI.7
Transformation between walk-states

As an illustration of transforming one walk-state to another as detailed in subsection

to

\left|\bm{u}_{n}\right\rangle
to
e^{i\Delta}\left|\bm{u}_{n}\right\rangle
, unless
\left|\bm{u}_{m}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{u}_{n}\right\rangle
themselves differ by an overall phase. One, therefore, has to take recourse to a multi-step quantum walk only.

\left|\bm{D}\right\rangle

VII
Optical implementation

We shall now provide a means of realizing the aforementioned ideas on an actual physical hardware over which such quantum walks are being implemented. In the linear optical implementation of DTQWs, the spin angular momentum (SAM) of the light beam as the coin-space
H_{c}
, and its orbital angular momentum (OAM) acts as the walk space,
H_{w}
. The state of polarization (SoP) of a light beam characterizes the SAM of light, with left circular polarization identified with
+1
units of SAM and right-circular polarization identified with
-1
units of it. OAM, on the other hand, is independent of the SAM of the light beam and characterizes the helicity of its wavefront. An arbitrary composite state of the light beam is the one which exists in a superposition of combinations of spin and orbital angular momenta, and a definite SoP or OAM cannot be assigned to them. Such beams are termed vector beams, and they can be identified by the varying phase and SoP distribution across their transverse plane.

VII.1
Scalar and vector beams

An arbitrary SoP of a light beam can be represented as a two-component unit vector,
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
called the “Jones vector”, in some orthogonal basis, as in Eq. (
1
).
We identify the states
\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{1}\right\rangle
with
\left|\bm{l}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{r}\right\rangle
, the left and right circular polarization states of light respectively. The states
\left|\bm{h}\right\rangle
,
\left|\bm{v}\right\rangle
,
\left|\bm{d}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{a}\right\rangle
, defined following Eq. (
35
), will then correspond to horizontal, vertical, diagonal and anti-diagonal SoPs of light, respectively.
In our numerical simulations, the
\left|\bm{h}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{v}\right\rangle
states correspond to the standard basis vectors
[1,0]^{T}
and
[0,1]^{T}
respectively. The left and right circular SoPs
\left|\bm{l}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{r}\right\rangle
would then correspond to the column vectors
[1,i]^{T}
and
[i,1]^{T}
respectively.

Unitary transformations between pairs of SoPs is affected using waveplates. In this paper, we represent them by the symbol
\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)
, where
\Gamma
is its retardance, and
\alpha
is the angle its fast-axis makes with the x-axis. The action of this waveplate on an arbitrary state of polarization
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
is given by:

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle-\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\langle{\bm{r}}|{\bm{u}}\rangle e^{-2i\alpha}\left|\bm{l}\right\rangle

(38)

\displaystyle+\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle+\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\langle{\bm{l}}|{\bm{u}}\rangle e^{2i\alpha}\left|\bm{r}\right\rangle

Here
\langle{\bm{l}}|{\bm{u}}\rangle
and
\langle{\bm{r}}|{\bm{u}}\rangle
stand for the left and right circular polarization components of
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
.
It is important to note it is always possible to transform one SOP to another SOP “up to a phase” using a single waveplate. That is: given a pair of SoPs
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
and
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
, there exists a waveplate
\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)
such that
\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle=e^{i\delta}\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
where
\delta
is an extra phase factor. The
\Gamma,\alpha
of the waveplate needed for this transformation, and the additional phase factor
\delta
generated in this process, can be derived from Eq. (
38
). Furthermore, it always possible to transform
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
to
\left|\bm{s}\right\rangle
, without any additional phase factors, by using a three-plate gadget consisting of a half-wave plate inserted in between two quarter-wave plates (
Simon and Mukunda 1989
;
Simon and Mukunda 1990
).

A vector beam is a light beam whose SoP varies across its transverse plane. In this work, we shall confine our attention to only cases where the variation is only along the azimuthal direction. We therefore ignore the radial co-ordinate in the rest of our discussion. The SoP of a vector beam will be represented as
\left|\bm{u}(\varphi)\right\rangle
where
\varphi
is the azimuthal angle, measured from some reference axis, say the x-axis. It can be represented as in Eq. (
1
):

\left|\bm{u}(\varphi)\right\rangle=u_{l}(\varphi)\left|\bm{l}\right\rangle+u_{r}(\varphi)\left|\bm{r}\right\rangle

(39)

where
u_{l}(\varphi)
and
u_{r}(\varphi)
are complex functions of the azimuthal angle
\varphi
, satisfying the normalization requirement
|u_{l}(\varphi)|^{2}+|u_{r}(\varphi)|^{2}=1
.
If the coefficients
u_{l}(\varphi)
and
u_{r}(\varphi)
depend identically on
\varphi
as
e^{im\varphi}
, for some integer
m
, we say the light beam carries an OAM of
m
units. We represent such states by the symbol
\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle
:

\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle=\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle\otimes\left|m\right\rangle=e^{im\varphi}\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle

(40)

where
m
is an integer and
\left|\bm{u}\right\rangle
is an SoP of the form Eq. (
1
), having no azimuthal dependence. We refer to such light beams as scalar light beams. The SoPs along two different azimuthal angles on the transverse plane of such scalar light beams differ only in their overall global phase. An arbitrary vector beam of the form Eq. (
39
) can be decomposed as a superposition of a collection of such scalar beams, resulting in a composite state
\left|\bm{W}\right\rangle
of the form Eq. (
3
). The Jones vector of its
m^{th}
OAM component and its amplitude can be extracted from
\left|\bm{u}(\varphi)\right\rangle
as

u_{m}\left|\bm{u}_{m}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{-im\varphi}\left|\bm{u}(\varphi)\right\rangle d\varphi,\forall m\in\left[b,e\right]

(41)

The limits
b
and
e
in Eq. (
41
) are the smallest and largest
m
that yield non-zero
u_{m}
. We shall confine our attention to only those
\bm{u}(\varphi)
for which the
b
and
e
are finite, i.e., only those vector beams which are constructed out of a superposition of a finite number of scalar beams. In such a case, there is a one-to-one correspondence between vector beams having a spatially SoP of the form Eq. (
39
) and composite quantum state of the form Eq. (
3
),
\left|\bm{u}(\varphi)\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
, with Eq. (
41
) establishing the mapping. The corresponding orthogonal states also follow the same mapping
\left|{\bm{u}(\varphi)}_{\perp}\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle
, where
\left|{\bm{u}(\varphi)}_{\perp}\right\rangle
is obtained through
\left|{\bm{u}(\varphi)}\right\rangle
from Eq. (
2
), and
\left|\bm{U}_{\perp}\right\rangle
is obtained through
\left|\bm{U}\right\rangle
using Eq. (
20
).

A prominent method of generating such vector light beams has been using optical interferometers
Liu et al. 2012
;
Li et al. 2016

Rosales-Guzmán et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2015

García-Martínez et al. 2020

\hat{q}_{\Gamma}\left(q,\alpha_{0}\right)
, where
\Gamma
is called the retardance,
q

\alpha_{0}
is the offset-angle
Kadiri et al. 2019

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\Gamma}\left(q,\alpha_{0}\right)\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle=

\displaystyle-\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\langle{\bm{r}}|{\bm{u}}\rangle e^{-2i\alpha_{0}}\left|\bm{l};m-2q\right\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle+\cos\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left|\bm{u};m\right\rangle+\sin\frac{\Gamma}{2}\langle{\bm{l}}|{\bm{u}}\rangle e^{2i\alpha_{0}}\left|\bm{r};m+2q\right\rangle

A q-plate of topological charge
q
and retardance
\pi

2q
units and simultaneously reduces the OAM of its right circular component by
2q
units. The inverses of
\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)
and
\hat{q}_{\Gamma}\left(q,\alpha_{0}\right)
are given by

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\hat{p}_{\Gamma}(\alpha+\frac{\pi}{2}),

(43)

\displaystyle\hat{q}_{\Gamma}\left(q,\alpha_{0}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\hat{q}_{\Gamma}\left(q,\alpha_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\phi

In the SAM-OAM implementation of quantum walks, the “coin-toss operation” is implemented using waveplates and the “shift operation” is implemented using q-plates

To make connection between the q-plate and the quantum step of Eq. (

\hat{q}_{\Gamma}\left(q,\alpha_{0}\right)=\hat{T}_{\Gamma}\left(2\alpha_{0},2q,\bm{l},\bm{l}\right)
.
It is possible to implement the quantum step

, that

of Eq. (

\textstyle{\hat{T}_{\pi}(\delta,p,\bm{s},\bm{c})}\equiv\hat{p}_{{}_{\Gamma_{2}}}(\alpha_{2})\hat{q}_{{}_{\pi}}(\frac{p}{2},\alpha_{0})\hat{p}_{{}_{\Gamma_{1}}}(\alpha_{1})

VIII
Conclusion

In this work we have considered a bipartite quantum system comprising of a qudit degree of freedom, coupled with a qubit degree of freedom. In such a composite system, we have examined the set of quantum states that are accessible by quantum walks starting from some easy-to-prepare product state.
A major result coming out of the current work is that such broadest generalization of the definition of quantum walks does not give access to all the states on the composite Hilbert space. A simple criterion for identifying whether a given quantum state can be accessible through a quantum walk is provided based on the notion of translational invariance of a quantum state. Given such a walk-accessible state, a clear and deterministic algorithm for realizing it through discrete time quantum walks is also provided. The given construction is “minimal”, in that the given composite state cannot be obtained using fewer quantum steps of unit step size than what is given by the algorithm. To give a practical context to our theoretical results, in this paper, we have interpreted them in the context of quantum walks on the Spin-and-Orbital angular momenta of light beams. We have demonstrated that, any azimuthally varying vector beam can be generated deterministically in such a quantum walk comprising of alternating waveplates and
q=\frac{1}{2}
q-plates. We believe that the results presented here will aid in designing better quantum-walk based algorithms for quantum state engineering, particularly in the linear optical implementation of discrete-time quantum walks.
