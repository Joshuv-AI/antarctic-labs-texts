Substitutions on Compact Alphabets
Neil Mañibo, Dan Rust, James J. Walton

Abstract
. We develop a systematic approach to continuous substitutions on compact Hausdorff alphabets. Focussing on implications of irreducibility and primitivity, we highlight important features of the topological dynamics of their (generalised) subshifts. We then reframe questions from ergodic theory in terms of spectral properties of a corresponding substitution operator. This requires an extension of standard Perron–Frobenius theory to the setting of Banach lattices. As an application, we identify computable criteria that guarantee quasi-compactness of the substitution operator. This allows unique ergodicity to be verified for several classes of examples. For instance, it follows that every primitive and constant length substitution on an alphabet with an isolated point is uniquely ergodic, a result which fails when there are no isolated points.

1.
Introduction

Substitutions on infinite alphabets and tilings with infinite local complexity (ILC) have been steadily gaining attention in the study of symbolic dynamics and aperiodic order
[
14
,
20
,
24
,
44
,
36
,
43
]
. Indeed, infinite alphabets must naturally be considered when recoding non-uniformly recurrent sequences by return words
[
15
]
, or when performing the balanced-pair and overlap algorithms for a non-Pisot substitution
[
26
]
. In the context of automatic sequences, constant length substitutions on infinite alphabets are the natural setting for regular sequences—sequences admitting a finitely-generated
k
-kernel
[
4
, Thm. 11]
—as well as profinite automatic sequences e.g., those defined over the
p
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}

[
36
, Sec. 4]
. It has also been shown that
(X,T)
is a self-induced minimal Cantor system if and only if it is conjugate to a substitution subshift on a zero-dimensional alphabet whose substitution is primitive, recognisable and aperiodic
[
14
, Thms. 24 and 25]
.

Given their ubiquity, it is important to develop a systematic approach to study such substitutions and their subshifts in terms of their dynamics, ergodic theory and spectral theory. Notable studies in this direction include those by Queffélec
[
34
]
, Ferenczi
[
17
]
, Frank and Sadun
[
20
]
, and Durand, Ormes and Petite
[
14
]
. The aim of this work is both to establish a framework for further study and to better understand in which ways the infinite alphabet landscape differs from the familiar world of finite alphabets. One novelty of our approach is the incorporation of techniques from the theory of positive operators on Banach lattices.

To obtain more general results in the infinite alphabet setting, it is necessary to impose some standing assumptions. Our alphabets will be equipped with a compact Hausdorff topology with the substitution being a continuous map. These assumptions are natural ones to impose so as to retain some of the structure enjoyed in the finite alphabet setting. Interestingly, most of our results do not require the alphabet to be equipped with a metric, generalising the results and often making the proofs conceptually simpler.

Alphabets, or labels, with compact Hausdorff topologies are also prevalent in the setting of higher dimensional ILC tiling substitutions. These include the Conway–Radin pinwheel substitution
[
35
]
, where infinitely many tiles appear up to translation, but up to rigid motion there is just one tile, so that the prototile space is homeomorphic to a disjoint union of two circles.

Our main focus will be on symbolic substitutions, which generate bi-infinite words.
Such symbolic substitutions can be made geometric, as so-called
stone inflations
of
{\mathbb{R}}
, by associating to each letter
a
an interval tile of length
\ell(a)\geq 0
(although we often require that each
\ell(a)>0
). A stone inflation first inflates the support of a tile by a fixed inflation factor
\lambda>1
and then decomposes the inflated support exactly into the substituted tiles, meaning here that
\lambda\ell(a)=\ell(a_{1})+\ell(a_{2})+\cdots+\ell(a_{n})
, where the substitution of
a
is
\varrho(a)=a_{1}a_{2}\cdots a_{n}
. If such a function
\ell
exists, and is continuous on the alphabet, we call it a natural length function. We find conditions on the substitution that imply existence (and uniqueness, up to scaling) of a natural length function but also examples that do not permit one (Examples
4.7
and
4.8
). This is different to the finite alphabet setting, where substitutions always permit natural length functions.

Many of the above cited works mention the difficult problem of establishing unique ergodicity for substitution dynamical systems in the infinite alphabet setting. It is sometimes possible to show this for a particular example, but finding general sufficient criteria is another matter. Beyond the constant-length setting
[
34
,
36
]
, little is known.

One of the principal aims of this work is to establish the first general criterion for unique ergodicity without requiring the substitution to have constant length. This entails considering the substitution operator
M
on the space
C(\mathcal{A})
of continuous real-valued functions on the alphabet. The space
C(\mathcal{A})
is a Banach lattice, allowing us to benefit from known results on positive, mean ergodic and power convergent operators on Banach lattices
[
1
,
16
,
27
,
41
,
39
]
. Even with the abundance of literature on such operators, we emphasise that the infinite-dimensional situation is far from straightforward. In particular, primitivity no longer guarantees unique ergodicity; compare
[
14
, Prop. 31]
. Moreover,
M
is a compact operator essentially only when the alphabet is finite (Proposition
6.2
), and hence the classical Kreĭn–Rutman theorem
[
30
]
for compact operators is not applicable. We mention this specific theorem as it is one of the simplest generalisations of Perron–Frobenius theory in the infinite dimensional setting.

However, similar results generalise to quasi-compact operators, where the spectral radius
r
is a pole of the resolvent and outside of the essential spectrum (see
[
32
, Thm. 4.1.4]
and the following remark). More generally, unique ergodicity follows from the weaker condition of strong power convergence of the operator
T=M/r
, meaning that
T^{n}f
converges strongly for each
f\in C(\mathcal{A})
; the result below follows from Theorem
5.22
and Corollary
5.23
(see also Theorem
6.7
):

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varrho
be a primitive substitution on a compact Hausdorff alphabet with corresponding subshift
X_{\varrho}
and substitution operator
M
. Let
r
be the spectral radius of
M
. If
T=M/r
is strongly power convergent, then
X_{\varrho}
is uniquely ergodic.

We show how strong power convergence of
T
follows from certain conditions on the substitution; see, for example, Theorem
6.18
in the constant length case. In the non-constant length case, the stronger condition of quasi-compactness can be verified for large classes of examples by applying a computable criterion detailed in Theorem
6.9
. This condition appears to be typically satisfied when the substitution is primitive and the alphabet has at least one isolated point. For example, it easily follows from Theorem
6.9
that every primitive and constant length substitution on an alphabet with an isolated point defines a quasi-compact operator and thus has uniquely ergodic hull, see Example
6.11
. Examples from
[
14
]
show that there are counter-examples to this in alphabets with no isolated points. In the non-compact setting of substitutions on countable alphabets, sufficient conditions for unique ergodicity are provided in
[
13
]
.

Our examples show that a subtle range of behaviours can occur in the infinite alphabet setting: whilst some have
T
quasi-compact and uniformly power convergent, others demonstrate that it is not uncommon for
T
to fail to be uniformly power convergent (Example
6.19
), even when
T
is strongly power convergent so that the associated subshift is uniquely ergodic. Moreover, the spectral radius
r
of
M
, which is the infinite-dimensional analogue of the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue for finite matrices, need not be an algebraic number; see Remark
6.16
.

The structure of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
we present general properties satisfied by subshifts over compact alphabets. In Section
3
we define notions related to substitutions and we present immediate consequences of continuity. We also discuss the associated substitution subshift and investigate its language. The substitution operator is introduced in Section
4
, and its operator-theoretic and spectral properties are discussed. Section
5
deals with invariant measures on the subshift and its relation to strong power convergence of the (normalised) substitution operator
T
. Here, we exploit a correspondence established in
[
20
]
in the fusion tiling formalism. Applications of our results, including sufficient conditions for unique ergodicity of the subshift and representative examples are discussed in Section
6
. Section
7
deals with asymptotic behaviour of discrepancy estimates for certain substitutions whose corresponding operator has a spectral gap. To simplify the exposition, and because several results consider the existence of natural length functions, we mostly work in the setting of one-dimensional and symbolic substitutions. However, in Section
8
, we briefly outline which results here generalise to higher dimensions. In particular, Theorem
6.18
on a criterion for constant length substitutions to have unique ergodicity naturally extends to higher dimensions, which provides a straightforward proof of unique ergodicity of the dynamical system associated with the Conway–Radin pinewheel tilings. Numerous examples are provided throughout the text to demonstrate our results.

2.
Topology and dynamics

Most of the results in this section are routine or simple exercises, and are likely already known in the wider literature, for instance in works such as
[
7
]
. Nevertheless, these results may be unfamiliar to the reader in the general setting of infinite alphabets and lay the groundwork for our main focus, substitutions on compact Hausdorff alphabets. We therefore include them, often without proof, in order to keep our work as self-contained as possible.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact Hausdorff space that we call an
alphabet
and whose elements we call
letters
. Let
\mathcal{A}^{+}=\bigsqcup_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{A}^{n}
denote the set of all finite (non-empty)
words
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{A}^{n}
has the product topology and
\mathcal{A}^{+}
is topologised as the disjoint union. Hence
\mathcal{A}^{n}
is a clopen subset of
\mathcal{A}^{+}
for each
n\geq 1
. For ease of notation we write

u_{1}u_{2}\cdots u_{n}\coloneqq(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{k}).

Let
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}=\mathcal{A}^{+}\sqcup\mathcal{A}^{0}=\mathcal{A}^{+}\sqcup\{\varepsilon\}
, where
\varepsilon
is the
empty word
.

Concatenation
is a binary operation
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\times\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
given by
(\varepsilon,u)\mapsto u
,
(u,\varepsilon)\mapsto u
and

(u_{1}\cdots u_{n},v_{1}\cdots v_{m})\mapsto u_{1}\cdots u_{n}v_{1}\cdots v_{m},

where
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
and
v=v_{1}\cdots v_{m}\in\mathcal{A}^{m}
. We write
uv
as shorthand for the concatenation of
u
and
v
, which is a continuous operation. If there is a
j\geq 0
such that
u_{i}=v_{j+i}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, then we call
u
a
subword
of
v
and write
u\triangleleft v
. We have the continuous projection map
p_{i}\colon\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathcal{A}
to the
i
th letter of a word.

Let
\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
denote the set of bi-infinite sequences over
\mathcal{A}
with the product topology, which is compact by Tychonoff’s theorem. We use a vertical line
|
to denote the position between the
-1
st and
0
th element of a bi-infinite sequence, and so we write
w=\cdots w_{-2}w_{-1}|w_{0}w_{1}\cdots
. For
j\leq k
, we let
w_{[j,k]}
denote the subword
w_{j}\cdots w_{k}
. We define the projection
p_{[j,k]}\colon\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathcal{A}^{k-j+1}
by
p_{[j,k]}(w)=w_{[j,k]}
. This is also clearly a continuous function.

The function
\sigma\colon\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}\to\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}}
given by
\sigma(x)_{i}=x_{i+1}
is a homeomorphism called the
(left) shift
map. The pair
(\mathcal{A}^{{\mathbb{Z}}},\sigma)
is called the
full shift
over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. Let
|\cdot|\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
be the
word-length function
mapping
u\mapsto n
for every
u\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
. The word-length function is continuous because
|\cdot|^{-1}(\{n\})=\left\{u\in\mathcal{A}^{*}\mid|u|=n\right\}=\mathcal{A}^{n}
is open in
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
for every
n
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. If
\sigma(X)=X
, we say that
X
is
shift-invariant
or
\sigma
-invariant
. If
X
is a non-empty, closed, shift-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
then we call
X
a
subshift
. We call
X
a
minimal
subshift if
X
contains no proper subshift
\varnothing\neq Y\subsetneq X
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, we let
\mathcal{L}^{n}=\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}
. We call
\mathcal{L}
a
language
if each
\mathcal{L}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{n}
is non-empty, closed (equivalently,
\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
is closed), and
\mathcal{L}
is closed under taking subwords. The
subshift associated with
\mathcal{L}
is the subset

X_{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq\{w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}\mid w_{[j,k]}\in\mathcal{L}\text{ for all }j\leq k\}.

Proposition 2.3
.

For any language
\mathcal{L}
, we have that
X_{\mathcal{L}}
is a subshift.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
and let
\operatorname{orb}(w)\coloneqq\{\sigma^{n}(w)\mid n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
denote the orbit of
w
under the shift map
\sigma
. We let
X_{w}\coloneqq\overline{\operatorname{orb}(w)}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
denote the
orbit closure
of
w
. Let
\mathcal{W}^{n}(w)
denote the set of all
n
-letter subwords appearing in
w
. We write
\mathcal{W}(w)\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{W}^{n}(w)
, seen as a subspace of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
. We define
\mathcal{L}^{n}(w)=\overline{\mathcal{W}^{n}(w)}
and similarly the
language
of
w
as
\mathcal{L}(w)=\overline{\mathcal{W}(w)}
.

Remark 2.5
.

Of course, we have that

\mathcal{L}(w)=\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{L}^{n}(w)

and
\mathcal{L}^{n}(w)=\mathcal{L}(w)\cap\mathcal{A}^{n}
, since each of the
\mathcal{A}^{n}
are disjoint clopen subsets of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
.

\Diamond

Definition 2.6
.

For a subshift
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
we let
\mathcal{L}^{n}(X)
denote the set of all
n
-letter subwords appearing in elements of
X
, that is,
\mathcal{L}^{0}(X)=\{\varepsilon\}
and

\mathcal{L}^{n}(X)\coloneqq\left\{u\in\mathcal{A}^{n}\mid\exists w\in X,\>\>j\leq k\ \text{ such that }\ u=w_{[j,k]}\right\}.

We write
\mathcal{L}(X)\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{L}^{n}(X)
, seen as a subspace of
\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
, and call
\mathcal{L}(X)
the
language
of
X
.

The following results are useful and left as exercises to the reader.

Proposition 2.7
.

If
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
then
\mathcal{L}(w)
is a language and, for a subshift
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have that
\mathcal{L}(X)
is a language.

Proposition 2.8
.

For
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
we have that
X_{w}
is a subshift with
\mathcal{L}(X_{w})=\mathcal{L}(w)
.

Remark 2.9
.

We assume in this section, without loss of generality, that all letters of
\mathcal{A}
appear in some word of the subshift
X
. Indeed, by shift-invariance, the set of all letters that appear is given by
\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)=p_{[0,0]}(X)\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. Since
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is closed, we have that
X
is compact and thus so is
\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
by continuity of
p_{[0,0]}
. So we may assume that all letters appear by restricting the alphabet to
\mathcal{L}^{1}(X)
, which is still compact and Hausdorff.

\Diamond

Recall that a topological dynamical system
(X,f)
is called
topologically transitive
if for all non-empty open sets
U,V\subseteq X
, there exists an
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
f^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\varnothing
.

The following lemmas are routine.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a subshift. If there exists an element
w\in X
with a dense orbit, then the subshift
X
is topologically transitive.

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a subshift. If there exists an element
w\in X
with dense orbit then
\mathcal{A}
is separable.

Since
X
is compact Hausdorff, by the Baire category theorem we have that
X
is Baire and thus of second category. Hence, (by an identical proof to
[
42
, Prop. 1.1]
) we have a converse to Lemma
2.10
:

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a subshift. There exists an element
w\in X
with a dense orbit if and only if the subshift
X
is topologically transitive and
\mathcal{A}
is separable.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a bi-infinite sequence over
\mathcal{A}
. We say that
w
is
repetitive
if for all
n\geq 1
and every non-empty open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}(w)
, there exists
N=N(n,U)\geq 1
such that every
v\in\mathcal{L}^{N}(w)
contains a subword in
U
.

The above definition may be interpreted as saying that any given finite word appears, up to some specified tolerance (determined by the open set
U
), with uniformly bounded gaps (within any word of size
N
). The lemma below (properties 2 and 3) says that this is equivalent, for any given tolerance, to
all

n
-letter words being found in all
N
-letter words, for some
N
depending on the tolerance.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a bi-infinite sequence over
\mathcal{A}
and let us write
\mathcal{L}^{n}=\mathcal{L}^{n}(w)
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

w
is repetitive;

(2)

for all
n\geq 1
and every finite collection
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
of non-empty open subsets
U_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}
, there exists an
N=N(n,\mathcal{U})\geq 1
such that for all
v\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
and for every
1\leq i\leq\ell
, there is a subword of
v
in
U_{i}
;

(3)

for all
n\geq 1
and every open subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}\times\mathcal{L}^{n}
containing the diagonal, there exists some
N=N(n,U)\geq 1
so that, for every
u\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
and
v\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
, there is a subword
u^{\prime}\triangleleft v
with
(u,u^{\prime})\in U
.

Proof.

The implications (
1\implies 2
) and (
3\implies 1
) are trivial.

(
2\implies 3
):
Let
U\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}\times\mathcal{L}^{n}
be open and contain the diagonal. It is well known (and not hard to prove) that, by compactness, we may find
V\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}\times\mathcal{L}^{n}
satisfying the same, with
V=V^{-1}
(where
V^{-1}
consists of pairs
(x,y)
, for
(y,x)\in V
) and
V\circ V\subseteq U
(where
(u,w)\in V\circ V
if there is some
(u,v)\in V
and
(v,w)\in V
).

For
u\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
let
V_{u}\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}
denote the set of
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
with
(u,u^{\prime})\in V
. Then let
\{V_{u_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
be a finite subcover of
\{V_{u}\}_{u\in\mathcal{A}}
. Take
N\geq 1
according to property 2. Let
u\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
and
v\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
be arbitrary and
i
such that
u\in V_{u_{i}}
. Hence,
(u,u_{i})\in V
. By property 2, we may find some
u^{\prime}\in V_{u_{i}}
with
u^{\prime}\triangleleft v
. Then
(u_{i},u^{\prime})\in V
so
(u,u_{i})\circ(u_{i},u^{\prime})=(u,u^{\prime})\in V\circ V\subseteq U
, as required.
∎

Remark 2.15
.

An alternative characterisation to the above is via open subsets of
\mathcal{A}
(rather than of
\mathcal{L}^{n}
), or open sets containing the diagonal in
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
, by comparing words letter-by-letter to define the ‘tolerance’.

In this case, property 3 is easy to interpret when
\mathcal{A}
has a metric
d
. Then we can take as
U
the pairs of words
(u,u^{\prime})
so that
d(u_{i},u_{i}^{\prime})<\epsilon
for each pair of letters
u_{i}\triangleleft u
and
u_{i}^{\prime}\triangleleft u^{\prime}
. The property then says: for every
n\geq 1
and
\epsilon>0
there is some
N=N(n,\epsilon)\geq 1
so that, for every
u\in\mathcal{L}^{n}
and
v\in\mathcal{L}^{N}
, there is some subword
u^{\prime}\triangleleft v
with

. This definition in the metric setting has appeared previously, for instance in the work of Frettlöh and Richard
[
24
]
.

\Diamond

The following is well known for general invertible topological dynamical systems
[
25
, Rem. 2.12]
and so we state it without proof.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a subshift. The subshift
X
is minimal if and only if every element
w\in X
has a dense orbit.

Likewise, the following lemmas are simple exercises.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
be a subshift. If
X
is minimal, every
w\in X
has the same language and is repetitive, with the same value of
N=N(n,U)
for each
n\geq 1
and non-empty open set

.

Lemma 2.18
.

If
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is repetitive, then

is minimal.

Putting together Proposition
2.16
, Lemma
2.17
and Lemma
2.18
gives us the following.

Corollary 2.19
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The following are equivalent:

(1)

X_{w}
is minimal;

(2)

every element
x\in X_{w}
has a dense orbit;

(3)

w
is repetitive;

(4)

every element
x\in X_{w}
is repetitive.

3.
Substitutions

Our main focus will be on subshifts coming from substitutions, to which we now turn. The theory of substitutions on finite alphabets is well developed
[
8
,
18
,
34
]
, and the reader is encouraged to familiarise themselves with that setting in order to compare and contrast our results with the classical theory. Where appropriate, we highlight these differences and subtleties.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a continuous function.
We call such a function a
substitution
on
\mathcal{A}
.
We say
\varrho
is a substitution of
constant length

n
if
\varrho(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{n}
.

Note that continuity of
\varrho
is automatically satisfied when
\mathcal{A}
is finite (and thus given the discrete topology). To give an idea of the variety of substitutions that satisfy this general definition, consider the following examples on infinite alphabets:

Example 3.2
.

Take
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}={\mathbb{N}}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
, the one-point compactification of the natural numbers
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, with the substitution
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
given by

\varrho\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}0\ \mapsto&0\ 0\ 0\ 1\\
n\ \mapsto&0\ n\!-\!1\ n\!+\!1\\
\infty\ \mapsto&0\ \infty\ \infty.\end{array}\right.

\Diamond

Example 3.3
.

Take
\mathcal{A}=S^{1}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid|z|=1\}
as the unit circle in the complex numbers. For fixed
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
we have the substitution
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}

\varrho\colon z\mapsto z\ \alpha z,

where the image of
z
contains itself and
\alpha z
, which is the product of
z
with
\alpha
.

\Diamond

Example 3.4
.

For an iterated function system
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{d}\}
on a compact space
X
with attractor
Y
, let
\mathcal{A}=Y
, with the substitution
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
given by

\varrho\colon a\mapsto f_{w_{1}(a)}(a)\ \cdots\ f_{w_{k}(a)}(a),

where the word
w_{1}(a)\cdots w_{k}(a)\in\{1,\ldots,d\}^{+}
is locally constant on
Y
.

\Diamond

Example
3.3
above is a constant length substitution because
|\varrho(z)|=2
for all
z\in\mathcal{A}
.
In fact, we will see that we have no choice but to make the substitution constant length when the alphabet is
\mathcal{A}=S^{1}
.
The next result shows that topological properties of the alphabet, namely connectedness, can heavily restrict properties of substitutions on that alphabet.
We see also that, even though our alphabets are potentially infinite, continuity of the substitution and compactness of the alphabet ensure that the images of letters under substitution are uniformly bounded in length.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution.
There exists
k\geq 1
such that for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
|\varrho(a)|\leq k
.
If the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is connected, then
\varrho
is constant length.

Proof.

The function
|\cdot|\circ\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to{\mathbb{N}}
is continuous as it is the composition of two continuous functions.
It follows that
|\varrho(\mathcal{A})|
is compact by the compactness of
\mathcal{A}
and so is bounded.
If
\mathcal{A}
is connected, then so is
|\varrho(\mathcal{A})|
and hence there exists an
n\geq 1
such that for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
|\varrho(a)|=n
.
∎

Similarly, a substitution is of course constant length on any connected subset of the alphabet. Given Proposition
3.5
, we may define the
length
of a substitution by
\displaystyle|\varrho|\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\varrho(a)|\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

It is often the case that checking continuity of a substitution is easier if one can do it component-wise. Indeed, Durand, Ormes and Petite gave an alternative equivalent definition of a substitution
[
14
]
(there called a
generalised substitution
). Recall that
p_{i}\colon\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathcal{A}
is the canonical projection function onto the
i
th component of the product
\mathcal{A}^{n}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

A function
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
is continuous if and only if for every
n\geq 1
the subspace
\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})\subseteq\mathcal{A}
is open, and for every
1\leq i\leq n
, the composition
p_{i}\circ\varrho\colon\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})\to\mathcal{A}
is continuous.

Proof.

Suppose that
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
is continuous.
As
\mathcal{A}^{n}
is an open subset of
\mathcal{A}^{+}
, and
\varrho
is continuous, it follows that
\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})
is open in
\mathcal{A}
.
Then, as
p_{i}
is continuous for every
1\leq i\leq n
, and restricting continuous functions to subspaces preserves continuity, it follows that
p_{i}\circ\varrho\colon\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})\to\mathcal{A}
is continuous.

For the other direction, it is enough to show that

\varrho|_{\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})}\colon\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})\to\mathcal{A}^{+}

is continuous for every
n\geq 1
, as the (disjoint) sets
\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})
are open in
\mathcal{A}
. For any particular
n
, the universal property of products says that
\varrho|_{\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})}
is continuous if and only if
p_{i}\circ\varrho\colon\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})\to\mathcal{A}
is continuous for every
1\leq i\leq n
and so we are done.
∎

By Proposition
3.5
, one is then only required to check the compositions
p_{i}\circ\varrho
for a finite number of the subalphabets
\varrho^{-1}(\mathcal{A}^{n})
in order to confirm continuity of
\varrho
.

A substitution naturally extends to a continuous function
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\to\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
. We define
\varrho(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\varepsilon
and for
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{n}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{+}
we define

\varrho(u)\coloneqq\varrho(u_{1})\cdots\varrho(u_{n})

by concatenation. Since
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}^{*}\to\mathcal{A}^{*}
has the same domain and codomain it may be iterated. By continuity of
\varrho
, all iterates
\varrho^{k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
are also continuous and so are also substitutions. Words of the form
\varrho^{k}(a)
are called
k
-
superwords
.

Definition 3.7
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution. We say a word
u\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
is
generated
by
\varrho
if there exist
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
k\geq 0
such that
\varrho^{k}(a)
contains
u
as a subword. We say that
\varrho

generates arbitrarily long words
if, for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a word of length
n
generated by
\varrho
. In this case, the
language
of
\varrho
is

\mathcal{L}(\varrho)=\overline{\{u\in\mathcal{A}^{\ast}\mid u\text{ is generated by }\varrho\}}

and we call words in
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)

legal
. The legal words of length
n
are denoted by
\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)
. The
subshift associated with
\varrho
is
X_{\varrho}\coloneqq X_{\mathcal{L}(\varrho)}
.

Note that the language includes closure points of words generated by
\varrho
. If closure points were not included then the following lemma, stating that
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
is a language, would fail and thus
X_{\varrho}
would also not be guaranteed to be a subshift.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
. Then
\varrho(v)\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
and
u\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
for any
u\triangleleft v
. In particular,
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
is a language if
\varrho
generates arbitrarily long words.

Proof.

Let
Y
be the set of generated words, so that
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)=\overline{Y}
. By continuity of
\varrho
we have that
\varrho(\mathcal{L}(\varrho))=\varrho\left(\overline{Y}\right)\subseteq\overline{\varrho(Y)}
. If
u\in Y
then
u\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(a)
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and thus
\varrho(u)\triangleleft\varrho^{n+1}(a)
so that
\varrho(Y)\subseteq Y
. Hence
\overline{\varrho(Y)}\subseteq\overline{Y}=\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
and thus
\varrho(\mathcal{L}(\varrho))\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
, as required.

The proof for closure under subwords is similar. Let
Y_{n}
and
Y_{m}
denote the sets of generated words of length
n>m
. Given some
0\leq j\leq n-m
, let
p\colon\mathcal{A}^{n}\to\mathcal{A}^{m}
be the continuous map that takes the
j
th subword of length
m
. Then
p(\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho))=p\left(\overline{Y_{n}}\right)\subseteq\overline{p(Y_{n})}
by continuity. Clearly a subword of a generated word is generated, so
\overline{p(Y_{n})}\subseteq\overline{Y_{m}}=\mathcal{L}^{m}(\varrho)
and thus
p(\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho))\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{m}(\varrho)
. Since
n>m
and
j
were arbitrary, we see that
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
is closed under taking subwords.

By definition,
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
is closed, and by the above is closed under taking subwords. If
\varrho
generates arbitrarily long words then each
\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)\neq\varnothing
and thus is a language.
∎

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution. Then
\varrho(X_{\varrho})\subseteq X_{\varrho}
.

Proof.

Let
w\in X_{\varrho}
, so every subword of
w
is legal. Let
u\triangleleft\varrho(w)
. Then
u\triangleleft\varrho(v)
for some
v\triangleleft w
, the latter implying
v\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
. By Lemma
3.8
we have
\varrho(v)\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
. By the same lemma, since
u\triangleleft\varrho(v)
, we have
u\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
. Since
u\triangleleft\varrho(w)
was arbitrary, we see that
\varrho(w)\in X_{\varrho}
, as required.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution that generates arbitrarily long words. The associated space
X_{\varrho}\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a subshift (in particular,
X_{\varrho}\neq\varnothing
). Conversely, if there exists some
k\geq 1
so that
|\varrho^{n}(a)|<k
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
, then
X_{\varrho}=\varnothing
.

Proof.

If
|\varrho^{n}(a)|<k
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
then
\mathcal{L}^{k}(\varrho)=\varnothing
and hence
X_{\varrho}=\varnothing
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
is a language by Lemma
3.8
and
X_{\varrho}
is a subshift by Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Corollary 3.11
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution with associated subshift
X_{\varrho}
.
If there exists a letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}|\varrho^{n}(a)|=\infty
, then
X_{\varrho}\neq\varnothing
is a subshift.

One might expect the converse of the above to hold by some compactness argument; it of course holds for finite or connected alphabets
\mathcal{A}
. However, it is possible to engineer an example where this is not the case:

Example 3.12
.

Let
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}
, where
{\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}={\mathbb{N}}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
is the one-point compactification of
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Define a substitution
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
by

\varrho(n,m)=\begin{cases}(0,0),&\text{if }n=0,m=0,\\
(n,m-1),&\text{if }m>0,\\
(n-1,n)(0,n),&\text{if }n>0\text{ and }m=0,\\
\end{cases}

where
\infty-1\coloneqq\infty
. By Proposition
3.6
,
\varrho
is continuous. One quickly sees that

\begin{array}[]{lcll}{\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho^{i}(n,m)}&=&(0,0)^{n+1}&\text{ if }n,m\neq\infty;\\
{\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho^{i}(\infty,m)}&=&(\infty,\infty)(0,\infty)&\text{ if }m\neq\infty;\\
{\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\varrho^{i}(n,\infty)}&=&(n,\infty).&\end{array}

For every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the letter
(k,0)
eventually grows to length
k+1
under iterated substitution, meaning that
X_{\varrho}
is non-empty by Corollary
3.10
. In fact,

X_{\varrho}=\{\cdots(0,0)(0,0)|(0,0)(0,0)\cdots\}.

However, every letter is eventually constant under substitution, so no letter grows without bound.

\Diamond

Example 3.13
.

It may be that not all letters of
\mathcal{A}
appear in
X_{\varrho}=X(\mathcal{L}(\varrho))
, even if
\#\mathcal{A}<\infty
and the alphabet cannot be reduced to give the same subshift. Indeed, consider the substitution

\varrho\colon\begin{cases}a\mapsto a,\\
b\mapsto ab.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)=\{a^{n},a^{n}b\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
. The only bi-infinite element admitted by this language is the periodic word containing only
a
, and the substitution cannot be restricted to a smaller alphabet that gives the same.

We see that
\mathcal{L}(X_{\varrho})=\{a^{n}\mid n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\}
and (as also for the previous example) it is possible for
\mathcal{L}(X_{\varrho})\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
to be a strict inclusion. However, for the sufficiently well behaved substitutions of main interest here (such as primitive substitutions, but also see Proposition
3.16
) the full language, and in particular the full alphabet, is realised by
X_{\varrho}
.

\Diamond

Remark 3.14
.

For
C\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, let
s(C)
be the set of letters in substitutes of elements of
C
i.e.,

s(C)\coloneqq\{a\triangleleft\varrho(c)\mid c\in C,a\in\mathcal{A}\}.

One may always restrict to a
letter surjective
substitution, in the sense that
\mathcal{A}=s(\mathcal{A})
. Indeed, consider the eventual range

\mathcal{B}=\bigcap_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}s^{n}(\mathcal{A}).

This is a nested intersection of non-empty compact sets and thus
\mathcal{B}\neq\varnothing
. Since
s(\mathcal{B})=\mathcal{B}
, the substitution is well defined and letter surjective on
\mathcal{B}
. All elements of the shift
X_{\varrho}
contain only letters in
\mathcal{B}
. Indeed, let
a\triangleleft w\in X_{\varrho}
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
and let
Y_{n}
be the set of words of length
n
generated by
\varrho
. Since
a\triangleleft v
for
v\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
and
v
arbitrarily long, we have that
a\triangleleft v\in\overline{Y_{n}}
for
n
arbitrarily large. Since the length of the substitution is bounded (Proposition
3.5
) we have that all letters of
Y_{n}
are in
s^{k}(\mathcal{A})
, where
k
can be made arbitrarily large by choosing
n
sufficiently large. It follows that
a\in\overline{s^{k}(\mathcal{A})}=s^{k}(\mathcal{A})
for
k
arbitrarily large and hence
a\in\mathcal{B}
, so all elements of the shift contain only letters of
\mathcal{B}
. This does not mean, however, that restricting the substitution to
\mathcal{B}
does not give a smaller subshift, as seen in the next example.

\Diamond

Example 3.15
.

Consider the finite substitution

\varrho\colon\begin{cases}a\mapsto bc,\\
b\mapsto bb,\\
c\mapsto cc.\end{cases}

One may restrict the substitution to its eventual range
\mathcal{B}=\{b,c\}
, which generates the subshift of two periodic elements of all
b
s and all
c
s. However,
\varrho^{n+1}(a)=b^{2^{n}}c^{2^{n}}
and thus we have the non-periodic element
\cdots bbbccc\cdots\in X_{\varrho}
in the subshift of the original substitution.

\Diamond

The following result identifies those substitutions whose languages (and in particular full alphabets) are fully realised in the subshift. A result of this form is only of interest when
\varrho
generates arbitrarily long words since, otherwise, (1) and (2) fail whilst the subshifts in the equalities of (3) and (4) are not defined in this case.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
\varrho
a substitution that generates arbitrarily long words.
The following are equivalent:

(1)

for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and non-empty open subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, there is some word generated by
\varrho
of length
2n+1
whose central letter is in
U
;

(2)

for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there is a legal word of length
2n+1
whose central letter is
a
;

(3)

\mathcal{L}^{1}(X_{\varrho})=\mathcal{L}^{1}(\varrho)=\mathcal{A}
;

(4)

\mathcal{L}(X_{\varrho})=\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
.

Proof.

(
1\implies 2
): Let
a\in\mathcal{A}
be arbitrary. By assumption, for each open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
containing
a
we may construct a word
v_{U}
of length
2n+1
that is generated by
\varrho
and has a letter of
U
at its centre. By compactness of
\mathcal{A}^{2n+1}
there is a subnet of
(v_{U})
that converges to some word
v
. By construction
v
has
2n+1
letters, contains
a
at its centre and is the limit of a net of words generated by
\varrho
. Thus
v
is in the closure of the generated words, so is legal, as required.

(
2\implies 3
): By definition,
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\varrho)=\mathcal{A}
(since
a\triangleleft\varrho^{0}(a)=a
). To see that every letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
appears in
X_{\varrho}
, by using the assumption we may find a sequence
(v_{n})
of legal words of length
2n+1
whose central letters are all
a
. By compactness of
\mathcal{A}
we may choose a subsequence
S_{1}
of
(v_{n})
that converges at positions
-1
and
+1
, say to
a_{-1}
and
a_{1}
, respectively. Similarly, we may find a subsequence
S_{2}
of
S_{1}
that also converges at positions
a_{-2}
and
a_{2}
. Continuing in this way, we construct an infinite word
w=\{a_{i}\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and subsequences
S_{n}
of legal words that converge to
w_{[-n,n]}
when restricted to the word of length
2n+1
about the origin. Since
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
is closed we see that
w_{[-n,n]}\in\mathcal{L}(\varrho)
. As these cover all of
w
, it follows that
w\in X_{\varrho}
, since then any finite subword of
w
is a subword of some
w_{[-n,n]}
, which is legal. As
a=w_{0}
we have that
a\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(X_{\varrho})
, as required.

(
3\implies 4
): We show that
\varrho^{n}(a)\in\mathcal{L}(X_{\varrho})
for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. By assumption, for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
there is some
w\in X_{\varrho}
so that
a\triangleleft w
. Then
\varrho^{n}(a)\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(w)
, and
\varrho^{n}(w)\in X_{\varrho}
by Corollary
3.9
. We see that
\mathcal{L}(X_{\varrho})
contains all generated words. Since languages are always closed (Proposition
2.7
) we see that
\mathcal{L}(\varrho)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(X_{\varrho})
. The reverse inclusion is trivial, by definition of
X_{\varrho}
.

(
4\implies 1
): Let
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be non-empty and open and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
arbitrary. Take any
a\in U
. Choose any
w\in X_{\varrho}
with
w_{0}=a
, which we may do using shift-invariance and the fact that, in particular, our assumption implies that
\mathcal{L}^{1}(X_{\varrho})=\mathcal{L}^{1}(\varrho)=\mathcal{A}
. Then
w_{[-n,n]}
is legal, of length
2n+1
and has
w_{0}=a
. Since the legal words are in the closure of the generated words, it follows that we may find a generated word
v
with
v_{0}\in U
.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

If the equivalent conditions of the above result are satisfied then
\varrho
is letter surjective. Indeed, in this case any given
a\in\mathcal{A}
is legal, so for arbitrary open
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
containing
a
we can find some superword
\varrho^{k}(b)=\varrho(\varrho^{k-1}(b))
containing a letter of
U
. In particular, there is some
b_{U}\in\mathcal{A}
with
\varrho(b_{U})_{i}\in U
. By compactness of
\mathcal{A}
, there is a convergent subnet of
(b_{U})
, which by continuity has limit
b
satisfying
a\triangleleft\varrho(b)
. However, being letter surjective is not sufficient, as demonstrated by Example
3.13
.

\Diamond

Remark 3.18
.

In the case that
\mathcal{A}
is infinite, it is not necessarily true that we may find any given
a\in\mathcal{A}
in the interior of a word generated by
\varrho
in the above result. For example, consider
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}
and

\varrho\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}n\ \mapsto&0\ n\!+\!1\\
\infty\ \mapsto&0\ \infty.\end{array}\right.

It is not hard to see that every letter of
\mathcal{A}
appears in some word of
X_{\varrho}
. In particular, we have the fixed point
x=\varrho^{\infty}(\infty\,|\,0)\in X_{\varrho}
; that is, we can build the point
x
as the limit of nested subwords
(u^{(n)})_{n\geq 0}
centred at the origin by defining
u^{(0)}=\infty\,|\,0
and
u^{(n)}=\varrho^{n}(\infty)\,|\,\varrho^{n}(0)
for
n\geq 0
. However,
\infty
appears only as the final term of superwords of the form
\varrho^{n}(\infty)
.

\Diamond

The above proposition gives necessary and sufficient conditions for the language of the subshift to agree with the legal words. The next result concerns this set of legal words, which, a priori, requires taking a closure of the generated words for each
\mathcal{L}^{n}
. Fortunately, as long as all letters grow without bound under substitution, then, once we know the 2-letter legal words, we can construct all other legal words by substituting these and the letters
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution such that
|\varrho^{n}(a)|\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
. For a subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{\ast}
, let
S_{n}(U)\subseteq\mathcal{A}^{n}
denote the subwords of length
n
of words from
U
. Then

(1)

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)=\bigcup_{j=0}^{P}S_{n}(\varrho^{j}(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho)))

where
P
may be taken as any number with
|\varrho^{P}(a)|\geq n
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Firstly, there exists some
P\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
|\varrho^{P}(a)|\geq n
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Indeed, consider

\mathcal{A}_{m}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid|\varrho^{m}(a)|\leq n-1\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}.

Then
\mathcal{A}_{m}=(|\,\cdot\,|\circ\varrho^{m})^{-1}\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
and hence is compact. Since words grow under application of
\varrho
, we have that
\mathcal{A}_{m}\supseteq\mathcal{A}_{m+1}
. If each
\mathcal{A}_{m}\neq\varnothing
, then by Cantor’s intersection theorem there is some
x\in\mathcal{A}_{m}
for each
m
. This contradicts the substitution growing on
x
. It follows that some
\mathcal{A}_{P}=\varnothing
or, equivalently,
|\varrho^{P}(a)|\geq n
for each
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

We define

Z=\bigcup_{j=0}^{\infty}S_{n}(\varrho^{j}(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho))),

which agrees with the union of the lemma except that we take an infinite union. Clearly
Z
contains all length
n
words generated by
\varrho
. Moreover, since the substitution of a legal word is legal (Lemma
3.8
), we have that
Z\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}
. It follows that
\overline{Z}=\mathcal{L}^{n}
.

We claim that the above union defining
Z
does not change by truncating at
j=P
. Indeed, suppose that
v\in\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)
with
v\triangleleft\varrho^{k}(u)
for
k>P
and
u\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho)
. Then
v\triangleleft\varrho^{P}(\varrho^{k-P}(u))
. As any word of the form
\varrho^{P}(a)
has length at least
n
, it follows that there is some
1
or
2
-letter subword
u^{\prime}\triangleleft\varrho^{k-P}(u)
with
v\triangleleft\varrho^{P}(u^{\prime})
. Since substitutions and subwords of legal words are legal, we have that
u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho)
and thus
v\in S_{n}(\varrho^{P}(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho)))
.

It follows that
Z
is the finite union in the statement of the lemma. Since
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho)
is compact, the same follows for
Z
, which is thus closed. Hence
\mathcal{L}^{n}=\overline{Z}=Z
, as required.
∎

Remark 3.20
.

Technically the
\mathcal{A}
term of Equation
1
needs to be included in general, since it is possible that a letter does not appear in any
2
-letter legal word. However, in examples where the substitution grows without bound, if every legal letter also appears in the subshift (i.e., it satisfies the equivalent conditions of Proposition
3.16
) then it must appear in a legal
2
-letter word and thus we may simplify the statement to

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)=\bigcup_{j=0}^{P}S_{n}(\varrho^{j}(\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho))).

In fact, it may be simplified further in this case. In Proposition
3.21
we will see that every element of
X_{\varrho}
is the image under substitution of another, up to a shift. It easily follows that every legal
1
or
2
-letter word is a subword of the substitute of another. Hence the above is a nested union and so we may write instead:

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)=S_{n}(\varrho^{P}(\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho))).

\Diamond

The following result shows that for every element of
X_{\varrho}
, there is a corresponding ‘superword’ decomposition (substitutive preimage up to an appropriate shift):

Proposition 3.21
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution with associated subshift
X_{\varrho}
.
For every
w\in X_{\varrho}
, there exists an element
x\in X_{\varrho}
and an integer
0\leq i\leq|\varrho(x_{0})|-1
such that
\sigma^{i}(\varrho(x))=w
.

Proof.

We present an analogue of the usual proof from the finite alphabet setting. Let
w\in X_{\varrho}
and
v_{n}=w_{[-n,n]}
. Since
v_{n}
is legal there is a net of generated words converging to
v_{n}
. Thus, for each
n
we have a directed set
\varLambda_{n}
, superwords
\varrho^{k(n,m)}(t(n,m))
for
k(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
t(n,m)\in\mathcal{A}
and subwords
u(n,m)\triangleleft\varrho^{k(n,m)}(t(n,m))
so that
u(n,m)\to v_{n}
as
m\to\infty
in
\varLambda_{n}
.

We may consider the subwords
u(n,m)
as centred over the origin, so that convergence
u(n,m)\to v_{n}
as
m\to\infty
holds letter-wise, at each position. Similarly, we may position the subwords
q(n,m)\coloneqq\varrho^{k(n,m)-1}(t(n,m))
over the origin so that
u(n,m)\triangleleft\sigma^{i(n,m)}\varrho(q(n,m))
, for
0\leq i(n,m)<\ell
, where
\ell
is the number of letters in the substitute of the origin letter of
q(n,m)
. Indeed, we may shift
q(n,m)
so that
u(n,m)_{0}
is in the image of the origin letter of
q(n,m)
after substitution. We may pass to subnets of each
\varLambda_{n}
so that
i(n,m)=i_{n}
is constant in
m
, and then restrict to values of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
so that all remaining
i_{n}=i
are constant.

We thus construct subwords
q(n,m)
so that letters at positions
[-n,n]
of
\sigma^{i}\varrho(q(n,m))
converge to those of
w
. Using compactness, choose a subnet
S_{0}
of
q(n,m)
that converges at position
0
, say to
x_{0}
. By continuity we have that
0\leq i\leq|\varrho(x_{0})|-1
. Similarly, we may choose a subnet
S_{1}
of
S_{0}
that also converges at positions
-1
and
1
, say to letters
x_{-1}
and
x_{1}
, respectively. Inductively define subnets in this way so that
S_{n}
converges at all positions

to letters

.

By construction (and continuity), the bi-infinite element
w^{\prime}=\{x_{j}\}_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
satisfies
\sigma^{i}(\varrho(w^{\prime}))=w
. We now need to show that every subword of
w^{\prime}
is legal. Since, by Lemma
3.8
, subwords of legal words are legal, it suffices to show that
w^{\prime}_{[-n,n]}
is legal for each
n
. By construction we have the net
S_{n}
, which converges letter-wise on positions
[-n,n]
to
w_{[-n,n]}
. This net consists of subwords of superwords
\varrho^{k(n,m)-1}(t(n,m))
, so it follows that
w_{[-n,n]}
is in the closure of the generated words and thus is legal, as required.
∎

3.1.
Primitivity

The following definition is adapted from the work of Durand, Ormes and Petite
[
14
]
, modified so that the condition only needs to be checked for a single power
p
. This definition is essentially the same as the one given by Frank and Sadun
[
20
]
, just in a slightly different setting.

Definition 3.22
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution.
We say
\varrho
is
primitive
if, for every non-empty open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
p=p(U)\geq 0
such that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, some letter of
\varrho^{p}(a)
is in
U
.

This is easily seen to be equivalent to the definition in
[
14
]
:

Lemma 3.23
.

The substitution
\varrho
is primitive if and only if, for every non-empty open set
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
p=p(U)\geq 0
such that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and for all
j\geq p
, some letter of
\varrho^{j}(a)
is in
U
.

Proof.

Suppose
\varrho
is primitive and let
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be a given open set. Let
p
be such that for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, some letter of
\varrho^{p}(a)
is in
U
and let
j\geq p
. Let
a
be a letter in
\mathcal{A}
and let
b
be the first letter of
\varrho^{j-p}(a)
. Then
\varrho^{p}(b)\triangleleft\varrho^{p}(\varrho^{j-p}(a))=\varrho^{j}(a)
. By primitivity,
\varrho^{p}(b)\triangleleft\varrho^{j}(a)
contains a letter in
U
. The other direction is trivial.
∎

The following is an analogue of Proposition
2.14
on repetitivity, in the sense that it gives an equivalent primitivity condition described by finding
all
letters of
\mathcal{A}
within
all

p
-superwords, up to some given tolerance:

Proposition 3.24
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

\varrho
is primitive;

(2)

for every finite collection
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
of open subsets
U_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
p=p(\mathcal{U})\geq 0
such that for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
1\leq i\leq\ell
, some letter of
\varrho^{p}(a)
is in
U_{i}
;

(3)

for every open
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
containing the diagonal, there is some
p\geq 0
so that, for all
a
,
b\in\mathcal{A}
, we have that
b^{\prime}\triangleleft\varrho^{p}(a)
for some
b^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
with
(b,b^{\prime})\in U
.

Proof.

(1
\implies
2):
This follows trivially from Lemma
3.23
.

(2
\implies
3):
Let
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
be open and contain the diagonal and take
V\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
with
V=V^{-1}
and
V\circ V\subseteq U
. Similar to before we take the open cover
\{V_{a}\}_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
of
\mathcal{A}
, where
a^{\prime}\in V_{a}
if
(a,a^{\prime})\in V
. By compactness we may find a finite subcover
\{V_{a_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
. Take
p\geq 0
according to property 2 and let
a
,
b\in\mathcal{A}
. We have that
a\in V_{a_{i}}
for some
i
, so
(a_{i},a)\in V
and thus also
(a,a_{i})\in V
. By property 2, we have that
a^{\prime}\triangleleft\varrho^{p}(b)
for some
a^{\prime}\in V_{a_{i}}
so that
(a_{i},a^{\prime})\in V
. We have that
(a,a^{\prime})=(a,a_{i})\circ(a_{i},a^{\prime})\in V\circ V\subseteq U
, as required.

(3
\implies
1):
Let
U\subseteq\mathcal{A}
be an arbitrary, non-empty open subset and let
V=(\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A})\setminus(\{a\}\times(\mathcal{A}\setminus U))
, where
a\in U
is arbitrary. Then
V\subseteq\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}
is open and contains the diagonal. Take
p\geq 0
according to property 3 and let
b\in\mathcal{A}
be arbitrary. Then there is some
a^{\prime}\triangleleft\varrho^{p}(b)
with
(a,a^{\prime})\in V
. The latter implies that
a^{\prime}\in U
, as required.
∎

Remark 3.25
.

Similar to Remark
2.15
, Property 3 above has an intuitive interpretation when
\mathcal{A}
is a metric space: for all
\epsilon>0
there is some
p=p(\epsilon)\geq 0
so that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, every letter

is
\epsilon
-close to a letter of
\varrho^{p}(a)
.

\Diamond

In the following result (and elsewhere), to avoid trivialities, we assume that
\varrho
is not the trivial substitution
\varrho(a)=a
on a one-letter alphabet, which technically would be primitive according to Definition
3.22
.

Proposition 3.26
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a primitive substitution. Then for all
n\geq 1
, there exists a
p\geq 0
such that for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
|\varrho^{p}(a)|\geq n
.
Consequently, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, one has
{\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}|\varrho^{i}(a)|=\infty}
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{A}
is a singleton then, since
\varrho
is not the trivial substitution of length

,
\varrho(a)
is given by
n
copies of
a
for the unique

, so that
|\varrho^{k}(a)|=2^{k}
. Otherwise,
\mathcal{A}
contains at least two distinct points and thus, since
\mathcal{A}
is Hausdorff, two non-empty disjoint open subsets

,

. By Proposition

, there exists

so that

contains a letter in both

and

for all

, in particular

.
∎

As a consequence of Corollary
3.11
,

must then also be non-empty.

Corollary 3.27
.

If
\varrho
is primitive, then

is a subshift.

It is clear that Property 1 of Proposition
3.16
holds for primitive substitutions. Indeed, take an arbitrary non-empty open subset

and

. For some

|\varrho^{n}|\geq 3
, so
\varrho^{n}(a)=xvy
for

,

and

. Consider

and
\varrho^{n+j}(a)=\varrho^{j}(x)\varrho^{j}(v)\varrho^{j}(y)
. By primitivity,
\varrho^{j}(v)
contains a letter

. By taking

sufficiently large, we may ensure that
|\varrho^{j}(x)|
,

, so that there is a word
v^{\prime}\triangleleft\varrho^{j}(a)
of length

centred around

and is generated by

, as required. Since all letters grow without bound, Remark

has the following consequence.

Corollary 3.28
.

Let
\varrho
be a primitive substitution. Then for all

,

\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)=\mathcal{L}^{n}(X_{\varrho})=S_{n}(\varrho^{P}(\mathcal{L}^{2}(\varrho))),

where

denotes the

-letter subwords of words from

and

is any number satisfying
|\varrho^{P}(a)|\geq n
for all

.

For any non-empty open set of legal
words

U
(rather than just a set of letters), it will be useful to find a power

such that every
p
-superword
\varrho^{p}(a)
contains a word in
U
as a subword.

Proposition 3.29
.

Let
\varrho
be primitive and
U\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)
be non-empty and open. Then there exists a power
p\geq 0
such that, for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
, there is a subword of
\varrho^{p}(a)
in
U
.

Proof.

Since
U
is open, we can find a generated word
u\in U
so that
u=p_{i}(\varrho^{k}(b))
, where
p_{i}
is the projection to the length
n
subword beginning at the index
i
. Again using that
U
is open, and continuity of
p_{i}\circ\varrho^{k}
(which is well defined on an open subset of
\mathcal{A}
), we have an open subset

for which
p_{i}\circ\varrho^{k}(v)\in U
for all

. By primitivity, there is some

so that
\varrho^{p}(a)
contains an element of

for all

. Then, for arbitrary

, we have
\varrho^{p+k}(a)
contains a word of
\varrho^{k}(V)
, whose

belongs to

, as required.
∎

Primitivity of
\varrho
is a strong condition and gives us that
X_{\varrho}
is minimal, hence every element has a dense orbit and is repetitive.

Theorem 3.30
.

Let
\varrho\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
be a substitution with associated subshift
X_{\varrho}
. If
\varrho
is primitive, then
X_{\varrho}
is a minimal subshift.

Proof.

Let
U\subseteq\mathcal{L}^{n}(\varrho)
be any open subset. By Proposition
3.29
, there is some
p\geq 0
such that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, there is a subword of
\varrho^{p}(a)
in
U
. Take any

. By Proposition

, there exists some

such that
\varrho^{p}(w^{\prime})=\sigma^{i}(w)
, where

. So the word

, and hence a word in

, appears within a uniformly bounded distance of the origin in

and hence

is repetitive. Thus, by Corollary

,

is minimal. It is clear that

, since the above shows that any non-empty open

contains a subword of any

.
∎

It is interesting to note, therefore, that by the above and Propositions

and

, if the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is non-separable, then all substitutions on

are non-primitive.

.

If
\varrho
is primitive, then
\mathcal{A}
is separable.

Remark 3.32
.

In the case when

is zero-dimensional and metrisable, Theorem

is already known under an equivalent assumption

. In this work, it was shown that the family of primitive substitution subshifts is in one-to-one correspondence with minimal self-induced Cantor systems.
The self-induced property implies the dichotomy result that, for this family, the topological entropy

.

\Diamond

3.2.
Realising set substitutions with compact alphabets

Suppose that
S
is a set, and we have a function
\varrho\colon S\to S^{+}
. It is natural to ask whether we may extend
\varrho
to a continuous substitution on a compact Hausdorff alphabet. In the finite case this is clear, by using the discrete topology on
S
. Combinatorial substitutions with
S
countable have already been studied, for example in the work of Ferenczi
[
17
]
. As we have seen, such a substitution on a compact alphabet would necessarily be of bounded length. Given this necessary restriction, we may in fact always find such a compactification:

Theorem 3.33
.

Let
\varrho\colon S\to S^{+}
with
|\varrho|<\infty
. Then there is a compact Hausdorff alphabet
\mathcal{A}
, a dense inclusion
\iota\colon S\hookrightarrow\mathcal{A}
and a substitution
\overline{\varrho}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}^{+}
so that
\overline{\varrho}(\iota a)=\iota\varrho(a)
for all
a\in S
. Moreover, we may choose
\iota
so that
\iota(S)
is the set of isolated points of
\mathcal{A}
.

Proof.

Since
L\coloneqq|\varrho|<\infty
, we may write
\varrho\colon S\to S\sqcup S^{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup S^{L}
. Equip
S
with the discrete topology and let
\iota\colon S\hookrightarrow\beta S
be its Stone–Čech compactification. Then
\iota
naturally embeds
S^{n}
into
(\beta S)^{n}
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, so we may regard
\varrho
as a map

\varrho\colon S\to\beta S\sqcup(\beta S)^{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup(\beta S)^{L}.

This is continuous, since all maps from discrete spaces are. As a finite disjoint union of compact Hausdorff spaces, we have that
\beta S\sqcup(\beta S)^{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup(\beta S)^{L}
is compact Hausdorff. Hence, by the universal property of the Stone–Čech compactification, we may extend
\varrho
to a continuous map

\overline{\varrho}\colon\beta S\to\beta S\sqcup(\beta S)^{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup(\beta S)^{L}.

This is nothing other than a continuous substitution of maximal length
L
on the compact, Hausdorff alphabet
\beta S
. This compactification makes
\iota(S)\subseteq\beta S
a dense subspace of isolated points, as required.
∎

Note that the approach in the above proof, using the Stone–Čech compactification, allows us to compactify any continuous substitution on a topologised alphabet that is Hausdorff (which will contain the original alphabet as a dense subspace). Although the above answers our question on realising arbitrary (infinite) set substitutions with continuous, compact substitutions, it is far from ideal for typical examples, since the Stone–Čech compactification is usually unwieldy. For example,
\beta{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
has cardinality
2^{\mathfrak{c}}
, where
\mathfrak{c}
is the cardinality of the continuum. In practice there are often more obvious compactifications. For example, the substitution
\varrho\colon n\mapsto 0\ n\!+\!1
on
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
may be clearly extended to the one-point compactification, by defining
\infty\mapsto 0\ \infty
, and it is easily checked that this is primitive; see
[
17
,
20
]
.

4.
The substitution operator and natural length functions

For a substitution
\varrho
, a continuous and non-zero
\ell\colon\mathcal{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
is called a
natural length function
for
\varrho
if, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

(2)

\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho(a)}\ell(b)=\lambda\cdot\ell(a),

where the notation
\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho(a)}
enumerates over each
b
-entry of
\varrho(a)
, including multiplicities. Here,
\lambda\geq 0
is called the
inflation factor
. In practice, we really want
\lambda>1
in order to represent an inflation, and
\ell(a)>0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
in order to represent tiles as closed intervals with positive length. For a primitive (or, more generally, irreducible) substitution, both of these properties derive from Equation
2
(in addition to non-negativity and non-triviality of
\ell
), see Theorem
4.26
. However, the results will be easier to develop in the above slightly generalised setting.

Geometrically, we thus have an associated inflation rule where all letters
a\in\mathcal{A}
are assigned to a tile of length
\ell(a)
, depending continuously on their location in
\mathcal{A}
, so that under substitution these tiles are inflated by a factor of
\lambda
and then perfectly dissected into other tiles, each with original lengths defined by
\ell
. Fixed points of the substitution
\varrho
(if they exist) then give rise to self-similar tilings of
{\mathbb{R}}
.

4.1.
The substitution operator

Definition 4.1
.

We let
E=C(\mathcal{A})
denote the Banach space of continuous functions
f\colon\mathcal{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which is equipped with norm
\|f\|\coloneqq\sup\{|f(a)|\mid a\in\mathcal{A}\}
. Call an element
f\in E

positive
(resp.
strictly positive
) if
f(a)\geq 0
(resp.
f(a)>0
) for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Let
K
denote the
positive cone
of positive elements, and
K_{>0}
denote the set of strictly positive elements. Given
f
,
g\in E
, we write
f\leq g
if
f(a)\leq g(a)
for all
a\in A
i.e.,
g-f\in K
. Given this partial order,
E
is a Banach lattice; see
[
41
]
.
We have the
order interval

[f,g]=\{z\in E\mid f\leq z\leq g\}
.

The dual of
E
is the Banach lattice
E^{\prime}
of continuous homomorphisms
\phi\colon E\to{\mathbb{R}}
, which has norm
\|\phi\|\coloneqq\sup_{\|f\|\leq 1}|\phi(f)|
. We sometimes write
\langle\phi,f\rangle\coloneqq\phi(f)
. For an operator
M\colon E\to E
, the dual operator
M^{\prime}
is defined by
(M^{\prime}\phi)(f)\coloneqq\langle\phi,Mf\rangle
. The dual cone
K^{\prime}\subset E^{\prime}
is defined as the set of
\phi\in E^{\prime}
for which
\phi(f)\geq 0
whenever
f\in K
.

By the Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem, there is a bijection between continuous linear functionals
\phi
on
E=C(\mathcal{A})
and regular signed finite Borel measures
\mu
on
\mathcal{A}
, where we identify

\langle\phi,f\rangle\longleftrightarrow\int_{\mathcal{A}}f\mathrm{d}\mu,

for continuous ‘test functions’
f\in E
. Positive functionals on
E
(which are necessarily continuous) i.e., the elements of
K^{\prime}
, may be identified with the (unsigned) regular finite Borel measures on
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 4.2
.

Because of the above correspondence, all measures here will be assumed to be regular. For example, when we speak of
unique ergodicity
in Section
6.1
, it is meant that there is a unique
regular
Borel probability measure. Note that a Borel probability measure on a compact Hausdorff alphabet
\mathcal{A}
is automatically regular in most cases of interest, including when
\mathcal{A}
has a countable base for its topology, or when
\mathcal{A}
is metrisable.

\Diamond

Given any ‘length function’
f\in E
(not necessarily one that satisfies Equation (
2
)), it is still meaningful to talk of the ‘length’ of the substitute of a tile
a\in\mathcal{A}
, by summing the lengths of the letters of
\varrho(a)
. So, we define

(3)

M_{\varrho}:=M\colon E\to E,\qquad(Mf)(a)\coloneqq\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho(a)}f(b),

where
\varrho(a)
is seen as a multiset (so we sum including multiplicities as in Equation (
2
)).

To find a natural length function is thus to find a positive eigenvector of
M
, which we call the
substitution operator
. Note that
M
only depends on the substitution considered as a multi-valued map, i.e., it does not depend on the order of letters of each superword.

Remark 4.3
.

We note that an analogous operator called the
transition map
was introduced in
[
19
,
20
]
in the geometric setting for fusion tilings. Before this, such an operator has already been used to determine statistical properties of generalised pinwheel tilings in
[
37
]
.

\Diamond

Example 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be finite and with the discrete topology. A basis for
E
is given by the indicator functions
\mathds{1}_{a}
for
a\in\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathds{1}_{a}(a)=1
and
\mathds{1}_{a}(b)=0
for
a\neq b\in\mathcal{A}
. Then

(M\mathds{1}_{a})(b)=\sum_{c\triangleleft\varrho(b)}\mathds{1}_{a}(c)=\sum_{a\triangleleft\varrho(b)}\mathds{1}_{a}(a)=M_{ab}

where
M_{ab}
is the number of occurrences of
a
in
\varrho(b)
. So we may write

M\mathds{1}_{a}=\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}M_{ab}\cdot\mathds{1}_{b}

and hence, with respect to this basis,
M
is represented by the matrix
(M)_{ba}
. It follows that the substitution operator
M
is represented by the transpose of the
Abelianisation
or
substitution matrix
of the substitution in the finite letter setting.

\Diamond

It is easy to see that
M^{n}
is the operator that replaces a length function
f
with its sum over
n
-superwords:

Lemma 4.5
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have
\displaystyle(M^{n}f)(a)=\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(a)}f(b)
. That is,
M_{\varrho^{n}}=\big(M_{\varrho}\big)^{n}
.

Proof.

The above is true for
n=1
by definition of
M
. Suppose that it holds for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then

(M^{n+1}f)(a)=M\left(b\mapsto\sum_{c\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(b)}f(c)\right)(a)=\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho(a)}\sum_{c\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(b)}f(c)=\sum_{c\triangleleft\varrho^{n+1}(a)}f(c),

so the result also holds for
n+1
and thus for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
by induction.
∎

Clearly
M
is linear and
positive
, which is to say that
M(K)\subseteq K
. Moreover
M
is a bounded operator, since, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
|M(f)(a)|=|\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho(a)}f(b)|\leq\max|\varrho(a)|\cdot\max|f|=|\varrho|\cdot\|f\|
, where the length
|\varrho|
of the substitution is bounded by Proposition
3.5
. In fact, we have the following formula for the operator norm of
M^{n}
. Let
\mathds{1}
be the constant function
a\mapsto 1
.

Corollary 4.6
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the operator norm of
M^{n}
is given by

\|M^{n}\|=\|M^{n}(\mathds{1})\|=|\varrho^{n}|.

Proof.

As
M^{n}
is a positive operator, for
\|f\|\leq 1
, the norm
\|M^{n}f\|
is maximised by the constant function
\mathds{1}
, for which
(M^{n}\mathds{1})(a)=|\varrho^{n}(a)|
by Lemma
4.5
.
∎

In the finite setting, the existence of a positive non-zero length function follows from Perron–Frobenius theory. Surprisingly, this does not hold for all substitutions on compact alphabets:

Example 4.7
.

Consider again the substitution of Example
3.12
on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}
, for which
(n,m)\mapsto(n,m-1)
for
m>0
,
(n,0)\mapsto(n-1,n)(0,n)
for
n>0
and
(0,0)\mapsto(0,0)
. It is not hard to show that this substitution has no continuous, positive, non-zero length function
\ell
. Dropping continuity of
\ell
, one finds that
M\ell=\lambda\ell
implies that
\lambda=1
and

\ell(n,m)=\begin{cases}(n+1)\alpha_{1},&\mbox{if }n,m\neq\infty,\\
\alpha_{2},&\mbox{if }n=\infty,m=0,\\
\alpha_{3},&\mbox{if }n=0,m=\infty,\\
\alpha_{2}-\alpha_{3},&\mbox{if }n=m=\infty,\\
\beta_{n},&\mbox{if }m=\infty,\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{1}
,
\alpha_{2}\geq\alpha_{3}
and
\beta_{n}
are arbitrary non-negative numbers, with at least one strictly positive. Note that
\ell
is unbounded if
\alpha_{1}\neq 0
, and if
\alpha_{1}=0
then
\ell=0
on a dense subset of
\mathcal{A}
.

\Diamond

Example 4.8
.

The substitution in the above example was primarily motivated by its pathological non-growth property, perhaps making it less surprising that it does not have a continuous length function. However, it can be easily modified to be growing in length on all letters but still admitting no continuous length function. We double up the substitution on
(0,0)
and triple it on all other letters: on
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
we define

\varrho\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{rll}(0,0)\ \mapsto&(0,0)(0,0),&\\
(n,m)\ \mapsto&(n,m-1)(n,m-1)(n,m-1)&\text{ for }m>0,\\
(n,0)\ \mapsto&(n-1,n)(n-1,n)(n-1,n)(0,n)(0,n)(0,n)&\text{ otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

which uniquely defines the substitution
\varrho
on
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}
by continuity. Since all letters in
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
eventually map to
(0,0)
, it is clear that
\ell(0,0)\neq 0
, otherwise
\ell=0
by continuity, so also
\ell(n,m)\neq 0
for all
(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Since
(0,0)\mapsto(0,0)(0,0)
, the inflation constant must be
\lambda=2
. But for
(n,m)\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}\times{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, with
m>0
, we have

2\ell(n,m+1)=\lambda\ell(n,m+1)=\ell(n,m)+\ell(n,m)+\ell(n,m)\implies\ell(n,m+1)=\frac{3}{2}\ell(n,m)\neq 0,

so
\ell
is unbounded, contradicting continuity. Thus, this substitution admits no continuous and non-zero length function.

\Diamond

Although the above shows that not all substitutions have a natural length function, it seems reasonable to conjecture this for primitive substitutions:

Question 4.9
.

Let
\varrho
be a primitive substitution on a compact, Hausdorff alphabet. Does
\varrho
admit a continuous natural length function?

We are not currently able to resolve the above question in full generality. However, we will find some conditions under which this holds. In the remainder of this subsection, we will consider general properties of the Banach space
E
, the operator
M
and implications of primitivity.

Remark 4.10
.

Because
\mathcal{A}
is compact Hausdorff, it is Tychonoff, i.e., Hausdorff and completely regular. This means that for any closed set
C\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and
a\notin C
, there exists a continuous function
f\colon\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
with
f(a)=1
and
f(C)=\{0\}
, which will be used in several proofs below.

\Diamond

Proposition 4.11
.

The substitution
\varrho
is primitive if and only if, for all non-zero
f\in K
, there exists some
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
M^{p}(f)\in K_{>0}
.

Proof.

Let
\varrho
be primitive and
0\neq f\geq 0
. Let
U=f^{-1}(0,\infty)
, which is open and non-empty. By primitivity, there exists some
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that for any
a\in\mathcal{A}
we have that
\varrho^{p}(a)
contains some
b\in U
, which is to say that
f(b)>0
. Since
f\geq 0
and
f(b)>0
for some
b\in\varrho^{n}(a)
, it follows from Lemma
4.5
that
(M^{p}f)(a)>0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Conversely, suppose the given condition on
M
holds and that
U\subset\mathcal{A}
is open and non-empty. Let
C=\mathcal{A}\setminus U
,
a_{0}\in U
be arbitrary and
f\colon\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
be continuous with
f(a_{0})=1
and
f(C)=\{0\}
(Remark
4.10
). By assumption there exists some
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that
M^{p}(f)>0
. By Lemma
4.5
, for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
we have that

\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{p}(a)}f(b)>0.

Since
f\geq 0
and
f(b)=0
for all
b\notin U
, this implies that
b\in U
for some
b\triangleleft\varrho^{p}(a)
, as required.
∎

We recall properties of positive cones for general Banach lattices from
[
39
]
. A positive cone
K\subset E
is called
proper
if
K\cap(-K)=\{0\}
,
generating
if
E=K-K
and
normal
if
\|x+y\|\geq\|y\|
for all
x
,
y\in K
. The ordered Banach space
(E,K)
is said to have the
decomposition property

[
1
]
if, for all
x
,
y
and
z\in K
with
z\leq x+y
, there exist
b_{1}
,
b_{2}\in K
with
z=b_{1}+b_{2}
and
b_{1}\leq x
,
b_{2}\leq y
.

Definition 4.12
.

A subset
A\subset E
is called
total
if the linear span of
A
is dense in
E
.

Definition 4.13
.

A point
f\in E
is called
quasi-interior
to
K
if the order interval
[0,f]
is a total subset of
E
.

For the next results, we go back to our setting where
E=C(\mathcal{A})
and
K
is the positive cone of non-negative functions. The proof of the following is routine, so we omit it.

Lemma 4.14
.

We have that
K\subset E
is a closed, proper, generating and normal cone, with interior
\mathrm{int}(K)=K_{>0}
. The Banach space
(E,K)
has the decomposition property.

Every interior point of
K
is quasi-interior
[
39
]
, and the converse holds if
\mathrm{int}(K)\neq 0
, which is clearly the case here by Lemma
4.14
. For a proof of the following lemma, we refer the reader to
[
41
]
.

Lemma 4.15
.

A point
f\in E
is quasi-interior to
K
if and only if
f\in\mathrm{int}(K)
.

Corollary 4.16
.

The substitution
\varrho
is primitive if and only if, for every non-zero
f\in K
, one has that
M^{p}(f)
is quasi-interior to
K
for some
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We recall from
[
39
]
that an operator
M
is
strongly positive
if for each
0\neq x\in K
, there is some
n=n(x)\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
M^{n}(x)
is quasi-interior to
K
. Then Proposition
4.11
may be restated as follows.

Corollary 4.17
.

The substitution
\varrho
is primitive if and only if
M
is strongly positive.

The strongly positive condition implies the weaker property of the operator
M
(and every power
M^{n}
) being
(ideal) irreducible
, meaning that, for every ideal
I
of
E
with
M(I)\subseteq I
, we have that
I=\{0\}
or
I=E
. For the notion of an ideal of a Banach lattice, we refer the reader to
[
41
, II.2]
, although note
[
41
, III.1 Exp. 1]
that for
E=C(X)
, with
X
a compact Hausdorff space, we have a bijective correspondence

\text{closed subsets }C\text{ of }X\longleftrightarrow\text{ideals }I_{C}=\{f\in E\mid f(C)=\{0\}\}.

As we will see shortly, irreducibility is a powerful property for ensuring essential uniqueness and positivity of natural length functions.

Definition 4.18
.

Let
\varrho
be a substitution on a compact alphabet
\mathcal{A}
. We say that
\varrho
is
irreducible
if it cannot be restricted to a strictly smaller closed and non-empty subset of
\mathcal{A}
, that is, there does not exist a non-empty closed
C\subsetneq\mathcal{A}
such that
\varrho(c)
contains only letters in
C
, for all
c\in C
.

Proposition 4.19
.

A substitution
\varrho
is irreducible if and only if the substitution operator
M
is irreducible.

Proof.

Suppose that
\varrho(c)\subseteq C^{+}
for all
c\in C
, for some closed subset
C\subseteq\mathcal{A}
. Let
f\in I_{C}
, so that
f(c)=0
for all
c\in C
. Then
(Mf)(c)=f(c_{1})+\cdots+f(c_{n})=0
, where
c_{1}\cdots c_{n}=\varrho(c)\in C^{+}
for
c\in C
. Thus
M(I_{C})\subseteq I_{C}
. If
M
is irreducible, we must have that
I_{C}=\{0\}
or
E
, so that
C=\mathcal{A}
or
\varnothing
, as required.

Conversely, suppose that
M(I_{C})\subseteq I_{C}
. We claim that
\varrho
may be restricted to
C
. Indeed, if we have
b\triangleleft M^{n}(c)
, for
c\in C
but
b\in\mathcal{A}\setminus C
, then consider a function
f\colon\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
with
f(c)=0
for all
c\in C
and
f(b)=1
(Remark
4.10
). Then
f\in I_{C}
but
M(f)\notin I_{C}
, since
(Mf)(c)=f(c_{1})+\cdots+f(c_{n})\geq 1
, where
c_{1}\cdots c_{n}=\varrho(c)
contains a copy of
b
. It follows that
\varrho
restricts to
C
. If
\varrho
is irreducible, it then follows that
I_{C}=\{0\}
or
E
, as required.
∎

Remark 4.20
.

If
\varrho
is primitive, then clearly
\varrho
is irreducible. Likewise, since powers of primitive substitutions are primitive, we see that each
\varrho^{k}
is irreducible. Note that Proposition
4.19
is consistent with the finite alphabet setting where a substitution is sometimes called irreducible if for every pair of letters
a,b\in\mathcal{A}
, there exists a power
k
such that
b
is in
\varrho^{k}(a)
, which is equivalent to the substitution matrix being irreducible.

\Diamond

Proposition 4.21
.

Every substitution
\varrho
restricts to an irreducible substitution on a non-empty, closed sub-alphabet.

Proof.

This is analogous the the proof that every dynamical system has a minimal subsystem. Consider the family
\Pi
of non-empty closed sub-alphabets
C\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, which are also closed under substitution. Then
\Pi\neq\varnothing
(since
\mathcal{A}\in\Pi
) and partially ordered under set inclusion. Moreover, every chain
(C_{i})
has a lower bound, namely
C=\bigcap C_{i}
. Indeed,
C
is closed and non-empty by Cantor’s intersection theorem. If
c\in C
then
c
belongs to each
C_{i}
; since these are each closed under substitution, it must hold that
\varrho(c)=c_{1}\cdots c_{n}
with each
c_{j}
belonging to every
C_{i}
and thus to
C
, so
C
is also closed under substitution. Hence,
\Pi
has a minimal element
C\subseteq\mathcal{A}
by Zorn’s Lemma. But this means that
C
is a closed alphabet and
\varrho
is irreducible over
C
, as required.
∎

4.2.
The spectrum of
M

The spectrum of the operator
M
is the set

\sigma(M)=\left\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\lambda\mathbb{I}-M\text{ is not invertible}\right\}.

We call
R(\lambda)=(\lambda\mathbb{I}-M)^{-1}
the resolvent operator, which is a holomorphic (operator-valued) function on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma(M)
. We call
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(M)
a
pole of the resolvent
(of order
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
) if there exists a punctured neighbourhood of
\lambda_{0}
such that the coefficient corresponding to
-p
of the Laurent expansion of
R(\lambda)
is non-zero and all other coefficients for
n<-p
are zero
[
45
, Sec. 8]
. A pole is called simple if it is of order
p=1
. It is often very useful to know that
\lambda\in\sigma(M)
is a pole of the resolvent; in particular, this implies that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue (see, for instance
[
45
, Thm. 5.8-A]
):

Lemma 4.22
.

If
\lambda\in\sigma(M)
is a pole of the resolvent then
\lambda
is isolated in
\sigma(M)
and is an eigenvalue of
M
.

For the rest of the paper, we let
r=r(M)
denote the
spectral radius
of
M
, defined as

r=\sup\{|\lambda|\mid\lambda\in\sigma(M)\}
. This is the natural generalisation of the Perron–Frobenius leading eigenvalue in the finite alphabet setting. Gelfand’s formula gives us that

(4)

r=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{\|M^{n}\|}\ \text{ and }\ r\leq\sqrt[n]{\|M^{n}\|}\ \text{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Lemma 4.23
.

We have the following for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\varrho^{n}(a)|\leq r^{n}\leq\max_{b\in\mathcal{A}}|\varrho^{n}(b)|.

Proof.

The upper bound follows from Equation (
4
) and Corollary
4.6
. For the lower bound, suppose that
c\coloneqq\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}|\varrho^{n}(a)|
. We claim that
c^{k}\leq\|M^{kn}\|
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, which is to say that each
kn
-superword contains at least
c^{k}
letters. By definition this holds for
k=1
. Suppose it holds for some given
k
. We have
|\varrho^{(k+1)n}(a)|=|\varrho^{n}(\varrho^{kn}(a))|
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
so, since
\varrho^{kn}
contains at least
c^{k}
letters, each of which substitutes under
\varrho^{n}
to at least
c
letters (by the definition of
c
), we obtain the claimed upper bound for all
k
by induction. Applying Equation
4
:

r=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sqrt[k]{\|M^{k}\|}=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sqrt[kn]{\|M^{kn}\|}\geq\sqrt[kn]{c^{k}}=\sqrt[n]{c}.

∎

Note, in particular, that
r\geq 1
for any substitution. The
peripheral spectrum
of
M
is the subset of the spectrum of maximum modulus:

\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}(M)=\{\lambda\in\sigma(M)\mid|\lambda|=r\}.

We say that
\lambda\in\sigma(M)
is in the
point spectrum
if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
M
and let
\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{per}}(M)
denote the set of elements in the peripheral point spectrum. Since
M
is a positive operator, the spectral radius
r
will always belong to the peripheral spectrum
[
39
, Prop. 1]
, although it is not always in the peripheral point spectrum (Examples
4.7
and
4.8
). For the rest of the paper, we let
T
denote the operator

T\coloneqq M/r.

Likewise, its dual operator is denoted by
T^{\prime}=M^{\prime}/r\colon E^{\prime}\to E^{\prime}
. Such normalisation is typical in operator theory, as many spectral and convergence results require
r(T)=1
.

Definition 4.24
.

We call
\phi\in E^{\prime}
an
eigenmeasure
if
T^{\prime}\phi=\phi
with
\phi\in K^{\prime}
and
\|\phi\|=1
.

Note that eigenmeasures always exist (see Remark
5.9
).

4.3.
Uniqueness and consequences of a natural length function

Natural length functions will be of most use when strictly positive. These always have as inflation factor the spectral radius:

Proposition 4.25
.

For any substitution
\varrho
, if
\ell\in K_{>0}
is a strictly positive eigenvector of
M
then its eigenvalue is the spectral radius
\lambda=r\geq 1
.

Proof.

Since
\ell>0
, by compactness there are
c
,
C>0
with
c\mathds{1}\leq\ell\leq C\mathds{1}
. Iteratively applying the substitution operator,
cM^{n}(\mathds{1})\leq\lambda^{n}\ell\leq CM^{n}(\mathds{1})
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Taking norms and applying Corollary
4.6
,

c\|M^{n}\|\leq\lambda^{n}\|\ell\|\leq C\|M^{n}\|,\text{ thus }c^{1/n}\sqrt[n]{\|M^{n}\|}\leq\lambda\|\ell\|^{1/n}\leq C^{1/n}\sqrt[n]{\|M^{n}\|}.

Since
c
,
C
and
\|\ell\|>0
, the lower and upper bounds have limits equal to
r
by Gelfand’s formula and
\lambda=r
.
∎

We now turn to irreducible substitutions, where we see that natural length functions must be strictly positive and essentially unique:

Theorem 4.26
.

Suppose that
\varrho
is irreducible and has a natural length function
\ell\in K
. Then
\ell\in K_{>0}
, has inflation factor
\lambda=r
the spectral radius and every other natural length function is a scalar multiple of
\ell
. Moreover,
\ell
is the only eigenvector of
M
with eigenvalue
r
, up to scalar multiplication.

Proof.

First we prove that every positive eigenvector of
M
, for an irreducible substitution, is strictly positive. Indeed, let
C\coloneqq\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid\ell(a)=0\}
. Then
C
is closed, by continuity of
\ell
. Moreover, for
c\in C
with
\varrho(c)=c_{1}c_{2}\cdots c_{n}
,

0=\lambda\ell(c)=\ell(c_{1})+\ell(c_{2})+\cdots+\ell(c_{n}),

so that each
\ell(c_{i})=0
, as
\ell\in K
. It follows that
\varrho
maps letters of
C
into words over
C
. Since
\ell\neq 0
, we have that
C\neq\mathcal{A}
and thus
C=\varnothing
, by irreducibility of
\varrho
, so that
\ell(a)>0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Then every natural length function for
\varrho
is strictly positive and thus, by Proposition
4.25
, has inflation factor
\lambda=r
. Suppose that
\ell
and
\ell^{\prime}
are two such eigenvectors of
M
. By strict positivity, compactness and scaling
\ell
if necessary, we may assume without loss of generality that
\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell
. We define

c\coloneqq\sup\left\{\xi\geq 0\mid\ell-\xi\cdot\ell^{\prime}\geq 0\right\}.

Again by compactness and continuity, we have that
\ell^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\ell-c\cdot\ell^{\prime}\in K
and
\ell^{\prime\prime}(a)=0
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}
. We also have
M\ell^{\prime\prime}=r\ell^{\prime\prime}
. Since, by the above, all eigenvectors are strictly positive, we must have that
\ell^{\prime\prime}=0
, that is
\ell=c\ell^{\prime}
, as required.

Finally, suppose that
\ell^{\prime}
is another (not necessarily positive) eigenvector of
M
, with eigenvalue
r
. For sufficiently large
c>0
we have that
\ell^{\prime}+c\ell
is strictly positive. Since this is still an eigenvector with eigenvalue
r
, by the above
\ell^{\prime}+c\ell=C\ell
for some constant
C
. But then
\ell^{\prime}=(C-c)\ell
, as required.
∎

As well as essential uniqueness and strict positivity of natural length functions for irreducible substitutions, we are also guaranteed that their inflation factors have
\lambda=r>1
, unless the substitution is non-growing for trivial reasons:

Proposition 4.27
.

Let
\varrho
be an irreducible substitution and suppose that
|\varrho(b)|\geq 2
for some
b\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have that
|\varrho^{n}(a)|\geq 2
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and, in particular,
r>1
.

Proof.

We first show that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
|\varrho^{n}(a)|\geq 2
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
(but perhaps with
n
depending on
a
). If not, then we have a sequence
(a_{n})_{n}
in
\mathcal{A}
, with
a_{1}=a
and
a_{n+1}=\varrho(a_{n})
. Consider the closure
C
of the set
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Since
|\varrho(a_{n})|=1
for any
n
, the same holds for all
c\in C
, by continuity. By construction
C\neq\varnothing
and because
|\varrho(b)|\geq 2
we have that
C\neq\mathcal{A}
. Finally, again by continuity,
\varrho
maps letters of
C
to other letters in
C
, contradicting irreducibility.

Let
X_{n}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mid|\varrho^{n}(a)|=1\}
. By continuity of
\varrho^{n}
, each
X_{n}
is closed, and clearly
X_{n+1}\subseteq X_{n}
. Suppose that
X_{n}\neq\varnothing
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then by Cantor’s intersection theorem, there exists some
a\in\mathcal{A}
that is a member of each
X_{n}
. But this contradicts
|\varrho^{n}(a)|\geq 2
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, for sufficiently large
n
depending on
a
. Thus,
X_{n}=\varnothing
for some
n
, so that
|\varrho^{n}(a)|\geq 2
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n
not depending on
a
. By Lemma
4.23
we have that
r>1
.
∎

Remark 4.28
.

Henceforth, we will always assume that our substitutions satisfy
|\varrho(a)|\geq 2
for some
a\in\mathcal{A}
, and hence
r>1
for any irreducible substitution.

\Diamond

Next we see that existence of a natural length function ensures certain bounds for letter growth of superwords, and occurrences of letters in superwords belonging to open subsets:

Lemma 4.29
.

Suppose that
\varrho
is irreducible and admits some natural length function
\ell
(necessarily with inflation constant
\lambda=r>1
). There are constants
\alpha
,
\beta>0
so that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\alpha\lambda^{n}\leq|\varrho^{n}(a)|\leq\beta\lambda^{n}.

If
\varrho
is primitive, then for any open subset
U\subset\mathcal{A}
, there exist
\alpha(U)
,
\beta(U)>0
and
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
so that, for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

(5)

\alpha(U)\lambda^{n}\leq\#\left\{b\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(a)\mid b\in U\right\}\leq\beta(U)\lambda^{n}\ \text{ for all }n>p.

Note that
\#\left\{b\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(a)\mid b\in U\right\}
counts the occurrences of
b
in
\varrho^{n}(a)
from the set
U
with multiplicities.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.26
, we have that
\ell>0
has inflation constant
\lambda=r
. We have that
r>1
by Proposition
4.27
(and Remark
4.28
).

By compactness we have constants
c
,
C>0
so that
c\mathds{1}\leq\ell\leq C\mathds{1}
. Thus,

5.
Existence of natural length functions and unique ergodicity

In this section we will find conditions that assure the existence of a natural length function. One can associate to this a
geometric inflation
rule
\varrho
and build the corresponding tiling dynamical system.

To motivate this, assume that
\varrho
admits a natural length function
\ell>0
. We then build the corresponding
geometric hull

\Omega
(also known as the tiling space) and equip it with the continuous translation action by
{\mathbb{R}}
. The resulting geometric self-similarity is a useful structure. For instance, one may then apply the general machinery of fusion tilings of Frank and Sadun
[
20
]
to relate translation-invariant measures on
(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
to sequences of measures on the alphabet
\mathcal{A}
that are compatible with the dual operator
T^{\prime}
. These can then be related (see Theorem
5.22
) to shift-invariant measures on
X_{\varrho}
by viewing
(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
as a suspension flow. In this way, we may find conditions for unique ergodicity in terms of the substitution operator. This will then be applied in Section
6
to also find sufficient conditions in terms of the substitution.

For completeness, we recall some of the notions above regarding the geometric realisation of tiling spaces from a subshift and length function; compare
[
8
, Sec. 5]
:

Definition 5.1
.

Let
(X,\sigma)
be a subshift over a compact alphabet
\mathcal{A}
and
\ell\colon\mathcal{A}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
be continuous. This defines
prototiles

(I_{a},a)
, given by intervals
I_{a}\coloneqq[0,\ell(a)]
also carrying the label
a\in\mathcal{A}
. A

is a translate
(I_{a}+x,a)
of a prototile. A
tiling

\mathcal{T}=\{\mathfrak{t}_{i}\mid i\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
is a set of tiles
\mathfrak{t}_{i}=(I_{a_{i}}+x_{i},a_{i}))
whose supports cover
{\mathbb{R}}
, where distinct tiles intersect at most on their boundaries and, given that the tiles are indexed in order (that is, each
\mathfrak{t}_{i}
is to the immediate left of
\mathfrak{t}_{i+1}
), the associated bi-infinite sequence
\cdots a_{-1}|a_{0}a_{1}\cdots\in\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{Z}}
of labels of tiles is an element of
X
(by shift-invariance, the choice of indexing giving the central letter is inconsequential). The
geometric hull
(or
tiling space
) is the set of all such tilings, which carries a natural (compact, Hausdorff) topology which, loosely speaking, considers two tilings to be ‘close’ if they have patches covering large intervals about the origin whose tiles may be paired to be close in support and with close labels in
\mathcal{A}
. More explicitly, there is a natural identification
\Omega\cong Y\coloneqq(X_{\rho}\times{\mathbb{R}})/\sim
with the equivalence relation generated by
(x,\ell(x_{0}))\sim(\sigma(x),0)
; elements of the form
[(x,0)]
are those tilings
\mathcal{T}
with (proto)tile
(I(x_{0}),x_{0})
containing the origin on its left endpoint and the other tiles following the sequence
x
in the obvious way. Any other
[(x,t)]
is identified with the shift
\mathcal{T}-t
of this tiling. We have a continuous
{\mathbb{R}}
-action on
\Omega
, by translating tilings, and the above identification defines a topological conjugacy with
Y
, with action
t\cdot[(x,s)]=[(x,s-t)]
. We call
(Y,{\mathbb{R}})
the
suspension flow
over
(X_{\varrho},\sigma)
with continuous
roof function

f(x)\coloneqq\ell(x_{0})
.

Remark 5.2
.

Note the importance of
\ell
being continuous in this definition. If
\ell
were not continuous, the identification

would not be continuous, for instance. One might imagine elements
x
,
y\in X
whose tiles are all pairwise close in
\mathcal{A}
but so that the tilings associated to
[(x,0)],

[(y,0)]\in Y
have tiles with close labels
x_{0}
and
y_{0}
yet very different supports.

\Diamond

Remark 5.3
.

In this more general setting, the
transversal

\Xi
of

consists precisely of the points identified with elements of the form
[(x,0)]
in

. This is unambiguous as the length function

is

away from 0.
Unlike in the FLC setting,

need not be totally disconnected.

1

(

Let

(

Let T be Cesàro bounded and suppose

Proposition 5.12

A substitution

with

.

Proof.

The operator
T
is necessarily bounded (since it comes from a substitution) hence mean ergodicity and Lemma

and that

is Cesàro bounded.
From Lemma

We now show that there must be a

Since

is mean ergodic,

by Proposition

5.2.
Fusion tilings, invariant measures, and power convergence

Frank and Sadun’s notion of

[
20
]
allows for a generalised setting in which we can see that invariant measures on the dynamical system

are in one-to-one correspondence with sequences of

and

measures on the sets
\mathcal{P}_{n}
of
n
-supertiles; see the discussion below for definitions. In this section, we recall this correspondence and we reformulate volume-normalisation and transition-consistency in our setting.
With this, one can show that invariant measures are in one-to-one correspondence with sequences

(\mu_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}

with

We show that a sufficient condition for this is strong power convergence of

and irreducibility (which also guarantees the existence, uniqueness and strict positivity of

). This extends the classical result for primitive substitutions over finite alphabets. We recall some definitions below regarding the general fusion framework in
[
19

20

.

We assume that our substitution has natural length function

with inflation factor
\lambda=r>1
. This defines geometric (labelled) prototiles, see Definition

. The set of these prototiles is denoted by

. Substitution naturally acts on prototiles; the

is a natural length function). Geometric substitution (also denoted by
\varrho

An

. We say that

For

The first statement follows trivially from Proposition

Our main result of this section is the following sufficient condition for unique ergodicity in terms of the normalised substitution operator
T
.

Theorem 5.22
.

Let
\varrho
be an irreducible substitution on a compact Hausdorff alphabet
\mathcal{A}
with
T
strongly power convergent. Then
\varrho
admits a natural length function
\ell\in K
with inflation factor
\lambda=r>1
. Moreover, this is an essentially unique natural length function and
\ell(a)>0
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. With respect to this length function,
(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
is uniquely ergodic.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.13
we have an essentially unique natural length function
\ell>0
, with inflation factor
\lambda=r
, and the projection operator
P
, to which
T^{n}
converges strongly (though not necessarily uniformly), must be of rank
1
.

Suppose that a volume normalised and transition consistent sequence
\{\mu_{n}\}
is given and let
f\in E=C(\mathcal{A})
be arbitrary. From transition consistency,

\mu_{0}(f)=(T^{\prime})^{n}(\mu_{n})(f)=\mu_{n}(T^{n}f).

Since
T
is strongly power convergent,
\lim_{n\to\infty}T^{n}f\to Pf=c\ell
for some

. It follows that

\mu_{0}(f)=\mu_{n}(T^{n}f)=\mu_{n}(c\ell+v_{n})=c\mu_{n}(\ell)+\mu_{n}(v_{n})=c+\mu_{n}(v_{n}),

where
v_{n}\to 0
uniformly over
\mathcal{A}
as
n\to\infty
. Since
\ell>0
we have that
\kappa\ell\geq\mathds{1}
for some
\kappa>0
, and since each
\mu_{n}
is positive we have that
\|\mu_{n}\|=\mu_{n}(\mathds{1})\leq\kappa\mu_{n}(\ell)=\kappa
for all

. Thus, since

uniformly as
n\to\infty
and the norms of the
\mu_{n}
are bounded, it follows that
\mu_{n}(v_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
and
\mu_{0}(f)=c
. Since the volume normalised and transition consistent sequence was arbitrary,
\Delta_{0}
is a singleton, so
\Delta_{\infty}
is too (Remark
5.20
), thus

is uniquely ergodic by Theorem

.
∎

There is an explicit form for the measures of cylinder sets in

5.3.
Invariant measures and suspension flows

To end this section, we go back to the relation between the

Let

Let

6.
Applications

Using the operator-theoretic perspective above, we will establish two classes of substitutions that are uniquely ergodic. We begin with examples that have a type of quantitative coincidence property, that appears to apply very generally to primitive substitutions on alphabets
\mathcal{A}
containing an isolated point. Afterwards, we will consider constant length substitutions whose columns generate uniformly equicontinuous semigroups.

6.1.
Quasi-compact substitutions and unique ergodicity

In this section we will give a simple condition on
\varrho
that ensures the operator
T
to be
quasi-compact
, allowing us to deduce strong properties of the substitution.

Definition 6.1
.

An operator
C\colon E\to E
is called
compact
if the image of the unit ball under
C
is relatively compact.

Proposition 6.2
.

If
\#\mathcal{A}=\infty
and
\varrho
is letter surjective then
M
is not compact.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is Hausdorff and
\#\mathcal{A}=\infty
there exists an infinite sequence
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
, …of non-empty, disjoint open subsets of
\mathcal{A}
. Since
\varrho
is letter surjective, for each
U_{i}
we may pick some
p_{i}\in U_{i}
and
a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}
with
p_{i}\triangleleft\varrho(a_{i})
. For each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
we may find a continuous map
g_{i}\colon\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
with
g_{i}(p_{i})=1
and
g_{i}(a)=0
for
a\notin U_{i}
(Remark
4.10
). For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, define
f_{n}\colon\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
by
f_{n}(a)\coloneqq g_{1}(a)+\cdots+g_{n}(a)
. Then each
f_{n}
belongs to the unit ball of
E
but we claim that
(M(f_{n}))_{n}
has no convergent subsequence. Indeed, given arbitrary
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we note that
\|M(f_{n})-M(f_{i})\|\geq 1
for
i<n
, since

(M(f_{n})-M(f_{i}))(a_{n})=M(f_{n}-f_{i})(a_{n})=M(g_{i+1})(a_{n})+\cdots+M(g_{n})(a_{n})\geq M(g_{n})(a_{n})\geq 1.

The last inequality follows from the fact that we have
p_{n}\triangleleft\varrho(a_{n})
, so that
M(g_{n})(a_{n})=g_{n}(x_{1})+\cdots+g_{n}(x_{k})\geq 1
, where
\varrho(a_{n})=x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{k}
and
g_{n}(x_{i})=1
for
x_{i}=p_{n}
.
∎

Recall from Remark
3.14
that any substitution can be restricted to a letter surjective substitution (although perhaps not without reducing the subshift), and from Remark
3.17
that any substitution that realises the whole alphabet in the subshift is letter surjective. So essentially all infinite substitutions of interest here have non-compact substitution operators. However, we are able to identify cases where
T
satisfies a weaker condition called
quasi-compactness
.
Quasi-compactness is a very powerful property here, since it ensures the existence of a continuous length function and unique ergodicity in the primitive case, as we will see below.

Definition 6.3
.

An operator
T
with
r(T)=1
is called
quasi-compact
if there exists some compact operator
C
and power
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which
\|T^{n}-C\|<1
.

The above definition can be reformulated as follows. Let
\mathcal{B}(E)
be the Banach algebra of bounded linear operators on
E
. We have the Banach subalgebra
\mathcal{K}(E)\leqslant\mathcal{B}(E)
of compact operators and the
Calkin algebra

\mathcal{B}(E)/\mathcal{K}(E)
. Now, there are several notions of ‘essential spectrum’ for the operator
T
, which are inequivalent (but nonetheless give the same notion of essential spectral radius). One is that
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
is in the essential spectrum if
T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is non-invertible in the Calkin algebra, equivalently
[
6
]
,
T-\lambda\mathrm{Id}
is not Fredholm (an operator is Fredholm if its range is closed and both its kernel and cokernel are finite-dimensional). An alternative (which gives a different essential spectrum in general) is to take the Browder spectrum
[
11
]
, that is, those
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
for which one of the following holds:
\text{range}(T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})
is not closed in
E
,
\text{dim}\left(\bigcup_{r\geqslant 0}\text{ker}\left((T-\lambda\mathrm{Id})^{r}\right)\right)=\infty
or
\lambda
is an accumulation point of
\sigma(T)
. In any case, the
essential spectral radius

r_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)
is then the supremum of moduli of elements in the chosen essential spectrum. Equivalently, there is a Gelfand formula: one may define the operator norm
\|[T]\|_{\mathrm{Cal}}
in the Calkin algebra as the infimum of operator norms
\|T-C\|
over all compact operators
C
. Then
r_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{\|[T^{n}]\|_{\mathrm{Cal}}}
, which is a decreasing sequence. Then clearly, for
r(T)=1
, we have that
T
is quasi-compact if and only if
r_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)<1
.

From
[
11
, Lem. 17]
(see also
[
31
, Thm. 1]
):

Lemma 6.4
.

We have that
\lambda\in\sigma(T)
lies outside the Browder spectrum if and only if
\lambda
is a pole of the resolvent
(\lambda\mathbb{I}-T)^{-1}
of finite rank.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
T
be a positive, quasi-compact operator with
r(T)=1
. Then
1\in\sigma(T)
is a pole of the resolvent of
T
of finite rank and thus
T
has a non-trivial fixed vector.

Proof.

Since
T
is positive,
r(T)=1\in\sigma(T)
, which cannot be in the Browder spectrum because
r_{\mathrm{ess}}(T)<1
by quasi-compactness. By Lemma
6.4
,
1
is a pole of the resolvent of finite rank, which is an eigenvalue by Lemma
4.22
.
∎

If
T
is additionally irreducible, then in our setting of
E=C(\mathcal{A})
and by Theorem
5.7
, the fixed subspace of
T
in Lemma
6.5
is one-dimensional and spanned by some
\ell>0
. If each power
\varrho^{k}
is irreducible, which includes the case that
\varrho
is primitive, then we have the following stronger result:

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose that
\varrho^{k}
is irreducible for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
(for example,
\varrho
is primitive) and that
T
is quasi-compact. Then
\{r(T)\}=\{1\}=\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}(T)=\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}^{p}(T)
and
T
is uniformly power convergent. If
\varrho
is primitive then
r(T)=1
is a simple pole of the resolvent.

Proof.

As discussed above,
T
admits a fixed vector in
K_{>0}
. Then
1
is the only element of the peripheral spectrum, and
T
is uniformly power convergent, by
[
1
, Prop. 5]
, whose results apply since

has the decomposition property (Lemma
4.14
). If
\varrho
is primitive then

is a simple pole of the resolvent, by
[
27

: the results of
[

are given for ‘strictly positive operators’, but all hold more generally for strongly positive operators (which holds for

∎

Theorem 6.7

Suppose that
\varrho
is primitive or, more generally, that

Remark 6.8
.

The previous theorem is reminiscent of the

for Markov shifts
\Sigma_{A}

. In this setting, the operator in question is the transfer operator

for a Hölder continuous potential

. In the case of topologically mixing Markov shifts over a finite alphabet, the unique equilibrium measure is given by

, where

and

, where

. Here,

(resp. of

The case of countable Markov shifts (CMS) is more involved, and the extension of the above result relies on recurrence properties of

(which is now an infinite

considered; see

]
for background. The main difficulty in the study of CMSs is the non-compactness of the shift space.

A family of substitutions over (parametrised) compactifications of

are studied in

of the substitution operator to the subspace

can be seen as a transition matrix

of a CMS. It would be interesting to see whether, in this subclass, there are connections between properties of the substitutive subshift with operator

and the CMS with transition matrix

. We suspect that

if, and only if,

satisfies a Ruelle–Perron–Frobenius theorem and admits a (unique) equilibrium measure. For results along this vein in the setting of substitutions on countable (discrete topology) alphabets, we refer the reader to

\Diamond

Note that primitivity and quasi-compactness are both
abelian
properties, whence
if the result above holds for
\varrho
, it also holds for any substitution derived from
\varrho
by rearranging letters in the images
\varrho(a)
. We now introduce the following combinatorial criteria (which are abelian in the sense of the description above) that guarantee
T
to be quasi-compact. For convenience, we let
r=r(M)
for the rest of the paper.

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
\varrho
be an arbitrary substitution. For
P\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define

C_{k}(P)\coloneqq\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\#\{b\triangleleft\varrho^{k}(a)\mid b\notin P\}.

For finite
P\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(1)

if
P
consists of only isolated points then
C_{k}(P)
is an upper bound for the essential spectral radius of
M^{k}
;

(2)

otherwise,
2C_{k}(P)
is an upper bound for the essential spectral radius of
M^{k}
.

In particular, if
C_{k}(P)<r^{k}
and
P
consists of only isolated points, or if
2C_{k}(P)<r^{k}
, then
T
is quasi-compact.

Proof.

We first consider the case that
P
is a finite set of isolated points. Consider the operator
V\colon E\to E
given by

(Vf)(a)=\begin{cases}f(a)&\text{ if }a\in P,\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly
V
is a compact operator, since it maps onto the subspace of functions supported on
P
, which is of finite dimension equal to
\#P
. We define
C\coloneqq T^{k}\circ V
. This is a compact operator, since the composition of any bounded operator with a compact operator is compact. Then

((T^{k}-C)(f))(a)=(T^{k}(I-V)(f))(a)=\frac{1}{r^{k}}\left(\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{k}(a)\text{ with }b\notin P}f(b)\right).

Then over all
\|f\|=1
, the norm of
(T^{k}-C)(f)
clearly maximised by the constant function
f=\mathds{1}
, for which

\|(T^{k}-C)(\mathds{1})\|=\frac{1}{r^{k}}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{k}(a)\text{ with }b\notin P}1\right)=\frac{C_{k}(P)}{r^{k}}.

Since
r_{\mathrm{ess}}(T^{k})
is bounded above by the spectral radius of
T^{k}-C
, which in turn is bounded above by
\|T^{k}-C\|
, we have that
C_{k}(P)/r^{k}
is an upper bound for the essential spectral radius of
T^{k}
, equivalently
C_{k}(P)
is an upper bound for the essential spectral radius of
M
. If
C_{k}(P)<r^{k}
, it follows that
T
is quasi-compact.

Now suppose that
P
is finite but contains non-isolated points. Then the operator
V
above is not continuous and needs to be adjusted. Choose open sets
U_{p}
, one for each
p\in P
, so that
p\in U_{p}
and
U_{p}\cap U_{q}=\varnothing
for
p\neq q
. For each
p\in P
, there exists a continuous function
\psi_{p}\colon\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
for which
\psi_{p}(p)=1
and
\psi_{p}(a)=0
for
a\notin U_{p}
(Remark
4.10
). We define
V\colon E\to E
by

(Vf)(a)=\sum_{p\in P}f(p)\cdot\psi_{p}(a)=\begin{cases}f(p)\cdot\psi_{p}(a)&\ \text{ for }\ a\in U_{p},\\
0&\ \text{ if }\ a\notin\bigcup_{p\in P}U_{p},\end{cases}

where in the latter equality we use that the
U_{p}
are disjoint. It is easy to see that
V
is continuous, and it is compact since
V(E)
is contained in the
\#P
-dimensional subspace spanned by the functions
\psi_{p}
. Thus,
C\coloneqq T^{k}\circ V
is also a compact operator and
T^{k}-C
is given by:

((T^{k}-C)(f))(a)=(T^{k}(I-V)(f))(a)=\frac{1}{r^{k}}\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{k}(a)}(f(b)-(Vf)(b)).

Notice that for
b\in P
we have
Vf(b)=f(b)
so that
f(b)-(Vf)(b)=0
and thus the above sum may be taken over
b\notin P
. For
b\notin P
and
\|f\|\leq 1
, we have that
|f(b)-Vf(b)|\leq|f(b)|+|Vf(b)|\leq 2
. Hence,

\|(T^{k}-C)(f)\|\leq\frac{1}{r^{k}}\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{k}(a)\text{ with }b\notin P}2\right)=\frac{2C_{k}(P)}{r^{k}}.

Then, analogously to the first case,
2C_{k}(P)
is an upper bound for the essential spectral radius of
M
and
T
is quasi-compact if
2C_{k}(P)<r^{k}
.
∎

Note that, in the finite alphabet case,
T
is always compact (i.e.,
r_{\text{ess}}(T)=0
), and hence always quasi-compact, so the above result is of interest only in the infinite alphabet case. The bound
r\geq\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sqrt[n]{|\varrho^{n}(a)|}
for all
n
from Lemma
4.23
makes the above checkable for many interesting examples:

Example 6.10
.

Consider the substitution

\varrho\colon\begin{cases}0\mapsto 0\ 1,\\
n\mapsto 0\ n\!-\!1\ n\!+\!1,\end{cases}

defined on
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}
. Let
P=\{0\}
. Then every
1
-supertile contains at most
2
letters not in
P
, so we need to show that
r=r^{1}>C_{1}(P)=2

4.23
that

, whereas we need a strict inequality. We therefore consider
\varrho^{2}

|\varrho^{2}(0)|=|01002|=5
,
|\varrho^{2}(1)|=|0101013|=7

.

Example 6.11
.

Let
\varrho
be any substitution of constant length

\varrho^{k}(a)

C_{k}(P)\leq r^{k}-1<r^{k}
.

In fact, the same criterion may be used even if

\varrho^{k}(a)
contains a letter of
P
, for some finite

P(N)=\left\{a\in\mathcal{A}\colon a\triangleleft v,\,v\in P\cup\varrho^{k}(P)\cup\varrho^{2k}(P)\cup\cdots\cup\varrho^{Nk}(P)\right\}.

Here,
\varrho^{n}(P)=\left\{\varrho^{n}(a)\colon a\in P\right\}

v\mapsto Av\ \text{ with }\ A=\begin{pmatrix}r&1\\

\Diamond

Question 6.14
.

Suppose that
\varrho
is primitive and that
\mathcal{A}
contains at least one isolated point. Is
T
power bounded? Is
T
quasi-compact?

One simplification of the above question is to restrict to primitive substitutions for which the isolated points are dense in
\mathcal{A}
, and
\varrho
sends isolated points to words of isolated points. Such substitutions are natural to consider: they arise, for instance, from some combinatorial substitutions on
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
that admit a compactification to a primitive substitution.

Below, we give an example of a primitive substitution that satisfies the conditions of Theorem
6.7
and compute the corresponding length function.

Example 6.15
.

Let
\mathcal{A}={\mathbb{N}}_{\infty}
and consider the substitution defined in Example
3.2
:

\varrho\colon\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}0\ \mapsto&0\ 0\ 0\ 1,\\
n\ \mapsto&0\ n\!-\!1\ n\!+\!1,\\
\infty\ \mapsto&0\ \infty\ \infty.\end{array}\right.

One can easily see that
\varrho
is primitive.
Using the same arguments as in Example
6.10
, one can show that the operator
T
is quasi-compact.
Here, we give closed formulae for the letter frequencies and for the natural length function (to the inflation factor
\lambda=r)
.
Let
\nu=(\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\ldots)^{T}
be the frequency vector.
Note that the letter frequencies satisfy the linear recurrence relation
\nu_{j}=\lambda\nu_{j-1}-\nu_{j-2}
, for
j\geqslant 2
with
\nu_{1}=(\lambda-2)\nu_{0}-1
. From the equation
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\nu_{j}=1
, one gets
\lambda=3+\nu_{0}
. This implies
\nu_{1}=\nu_{0}^{2}+\nu_{0}-1
.
The recurrence relation is homogeneous with characteristic polynomial

p(x)=x^{2}-\lambda x+1
. This means the solution of the recurrence relation is of the form

\nu_{j}=C_{+}\left(\frac{\lambda+\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4}}{2}\right)^{j}+C_{-}\left(\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4}}{2}\right)^{j}

for some constants
C_{-},C_{+}
. Since the frequencies satisfy
\nu_{j}\leqslant 1
, we immediately get that
C_{+}=0
. From the initial conditions, we get
C_{-}=\nu_{0}
and
C_{-}\left(\frac{\lambda-\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-4}}{2}\right)=\nu^{2}_{0}+\nu_{0}-1
. Solving these two equations simultaneously yields
\nu_{0}=-1
or
\nu_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, where the first is obviously an extraneous solution. This then yields
\lambda=3+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and

\nu_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{j}

for
j\geqslant 0
with
\nu_{\infty}=0
.

Next we solve for the lengths
\ell_{j}
. They satisfy the non-homogeneous linear recurrence relation
\ell_{j}=\lambda\ell_{j-1}-\ell_{j-2}-\ell_{0}
. If we fix
\ell_{\infty}=1
, we get that
\ell_{0}=\lambda-2=1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
. Note that the non-homogeneous term is
-\ell_{0}=-\ell_{0}\cdot 1^{j}
, and since
1
is not a root of
x^{2}-\lambda x-1
, and the two roots are distinct, we have a particular solution given by some constant
C_{p}
. Solving for
C_{p}
yields
C_{p}=\frac{\ell_{0}}{\lambda-2}=1
. Combining this to the solution of the associated homogeneous recurrence relation leads to

\ell_{j}=1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{j}\ \ \text{ for all }j\geq 0.

\Diamond

Remark 6.16
.

Recent work of Frettlöh, Garber and Mañibo

6.2.
Constant length substitutions

Queffélec has already considered substitutions of constant length in compact metrisable alphabets in
[
34
]
, which she called
compact automata
. In this subsection, we let
|\varrho(a)|=L
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
. This greatly simplifies matters, owing to the following:

(1)

\|M\|=r=L
and
\|T\|=1
, that is,
T
is a contraction;

(2)

M(\mathds{1})=L\mathds{1}
, so
\mathds{1}
is a natural length function of
\varrho
;

(3)

\varrho(a)=\varrho_{1}(a)\varrho_{2}(a)\cdots\varrho_{L}(a)
for continuous functions
\varrho_{i}\colon\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{A}
, which we call
columns
.

The last property means that the substitution operator is defined by

(12)

M(f)=f\circ\varrho_{1}+f\circ\varrho_{2}+\cdots+f\circ\varrho_{L}.

Let
\Phi
be the semigroup generated by the columns
\{\varrho_{i}\}
. We call
\Phi

equicontinuous
if this set of functions is uniformly equicontinuous (note that this notion does not require
\mathcal{A}
to be metrisable, since
\mathcal{A}
carries a uniformity, as a compact Hausdorff space). The following results are similar to those obtained in
[
34
, Thm. 12.1, Cor. 12.2]
.

Theorem 6.18
.

Suppose that
\varrho
is primitive, constant length and generates an equicontinuous semigroup
\Phi
. Then
T
is strongly power convergent and the tiling dynamical system associated with
\varrho
is uniquely ergodic.

Proof.

Let
f\in K
be arbitrary. First, note that

T^{n}(f)=\frac{1}{L^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{L^{n}}f\circ\phi^{(n)}_{i},

where the sum is over all possible
n
-fold compositions
\phi^{(n)}_{i}\in\Phi
of columns. Since
\mathcal{A}
is compact,
f
is uniformly continuous and thus the maps
\{f\circ\phi_{i}^{(n)}\}
are uniformly equicontinuous. Since
T^{n}(f)
is a convex combination of these maps, it follows that
\{T^{n}(f)\}
is a uniformly equicontinuous family. Since
\|T\|\leq 1
, it is also uniformly bounded, so
\{T^{n}(f)\}
is relatively compact in
E
by the Arzelá–Ascoli Theorem and thus

T^{n_{k}}(f)\to g\text{ as }k\to\infty

for some subsequence
(n_{k})_{k}
and
g\in K
. By primitivity, dismissing the trivial case of
f=0
, we must have that
g>0
. We will show that
g
is constant.

Observe that
g
is itself also a cluster point of iterates of
T
applied to
g
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|T^{n_{k+1}-n_{k}}(g)-g\|=\|T^{n_{k+1}-n_{k}}(T^{n_{k}}f-v_{k})-g\|=\|T^{n_{k+1}}f-T^{n_{k+1}-n_{k}}(v_{k})-g\|\leq

\displaystyle\|T^{n_{k+1}}f-g\|+\|T^{n_{k+1}-n_{k}}(v_{k})\|\leq\|T^{n_{k+1}}f-g\|+\|v_{k}\|\to 0\text{ as }k\to\infty,

where
v_{k}=T^{n_{k}}(f)-g\to 0
, so
T^{n_{k+1}-n_{k}}(g)\to g
as
k\to\infty
. Suppose that
n_{k+1}-n_{k}
is bounded. Then
T^{p}(g)=g
for some
p
so that
T^{np}(g)=g
for arbitrarily large
np
. Otherwise, we have that
T^{j_{k}}g\to g
as
k\to\infty
for
j_{k}\to\infty
. In either case, we see that
g
must be constant. Indeed,
\|T^{j}g\|
is monotonically decreasing in
j
, since
\|T\|\leq 1
. By primitivity,
\|T^{j}g\|<\|g\|
for sufficiently large
j
and
g
non-constant. To see this, suppose that
U\subset\mathcal{A}
is an open set small enough so that
g(a)<\|g\|
for all
a\in U
. By primitivity, for sufficiently large
j
we have that
\varrho^{j}(a)
contains a letter in
U
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, which implies that
T^{j}(g)(a)<\|g\|
for all
b\in\mathcal{A}
, that is,
\|T^{j}g\|<\|g\|
. But
T
is a contraction so then cannot converge to
g
, which is a contradiction, so
g
must be constant.

Recall that
T^{n_{k}}(f)\to g
as
k\to\infty
. On the other hand,

\|T^{n+1}(f)-g\|=\|T^{n+1}(f-g)\|\leq\|T^{n}(f-g)\|=\|T^{n}(f)-g\|,

since
T(g)=g
, so that
\|T^{n}(f)-g\|
is monotonically decreasing. Hence, the whole sequence
T^{n}f\to g
as
n\to\infty
, as required. If
f\notin K
then
f=f_{+}-f_{-}
, where
f_{+}
and
f_{-}\in K
, so by the above
T^{n}(f)=T^{n}(f_{+})-T^{n}(f_{-})\to g_{+}-g_{-}
as
n\to\infty
so that
T
is strongly power convergent on all of
E
. By Theorem
5.22
the tiling dynamical system
(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}})
or, by Corollary
5.23
, the subshift
(X_{\varrho},\sigma)
, is uniquely ergodic.
∎

The following substitution (considered in
[
34
, Exp. 12.1]
in the one-sided shift setting) is an example for which
T
cannot be quasi-compact, and
r
cannot be a pole of the resolvent, since
T
is
not
uniformly power convergent, but the subshift is still uniquely ergodic by the above result:

Example 6.19
.

Let
\mathcal{A}=S^{1}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
and recall the substitution from Example
3.3
:
\varrho(z)=z\ \alpha z
, where
\alpha\in S^{1}
is irrational (i.e.,
\alpha=e^{2\pi\beta i}
for
\beta
irrational). By Kronecker’s theorem, via the density of the orbit of
z\mapsto\alpha z
, it is clear that
\varrho
is primitive. Also note that
\Phi=\left\langle\text{id},\alpha\cdot\right\rangle
is an equicontinuous semigroup because it is generated by group translations.

We do not have that
T^{n}\mapsto P
uniformly as
n\mapsto\infty
, where
P
is a projection operator to the eigenline spanned by
\ell
. Indeed, first note that an eigenmeasure is clearly provided by the Lebesgue measure on the circle (since each letter map of the substitution is a rotation). Consider a function
f\in E
,
\|f\|=1
that has very small integral
c=c_{f}>0
and yet has
f(z)=1
for
z=1
,
\alpha
,
\alpha^{2}
, …,

(by taking a very spiky function this is of course possible; in fact, by taking a function with negative spikes too, we may choose such a function so that

,
(M^{n}(f))(1)=2^{n}
and hence

, since all elements of

for some non-negative

. Assuming uniform power convergence, we would have
T^{n}f\to c_{f}\cdot\ell
uniformly as

, that is
\|T^{n}(f)-c_{f}\ell\|<\epsilon
for sufficiently large

. However,

, so we do not have such uniform convergence and thus

cannot be a pole of the resolvent, and

cannot be quasi-compact, by Theorem

. However, whilst

is not uniformly power convergent, it is strongly power convergent by Theorem

.

7.
Discrepancy estimates from a spectral gap

Throughout this section, we make the following assumptions on
\varrho
:

(A1):

T
is uniformly power convergent;

(A2):

T
is irreducible.

The first assumption implies that
1
is a simple pole of the resolvent
[
29
, Thm. 2.5]
. It then follows from Theorem
5.7
that

•

\sigma_{\mathrm{per}}(T)=\{1\}
;

•

\varrho
admits a (unique) natural length function
0\neq\ell\in K
with stretching factor
\lambda=r
.

Since
\sigma(M)=r\sigma(T)
is compact,
r
is a pole of the resolvent (and thus isolated) and the only element of the peripheral spectrum of
M
, it follows that
M
has a
spectral gap
, that is,
\sigma(M)
has a ‘second largest element’:

r_{2}\coloneqq\sup\{|\lambda|\mid\lambda\in\sigma(M),\lambda\neq r\}<r.

We fix a (necessarily unique) eigenmeasure
\mu\in K^{\prime}
, which we normalise here with
\mu(\ell)=1
.

Remark 7.1
.

By Theorem
6.7
, the above conditions are satisfied whenever
\varrho
is primitive and
T
is quasi-compact. This includes any primitive substitution for which Theorem
6.9
applies, so the constructions to follow apply to those relevant examples from the last section.

\Diamond

The existence of a spectral gap will allow for some control on the discrepancy on the ‘expected weight’ of a weighted selection of tiles across a large supertile relative to the ‘actual’ sum of weights. Given
f\in E
(a ‘weight function’),
a\in\mathcal{A}
, and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we define

(13)

\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)=r^{n}\ell(a)\cdot\mu(f).

That is, we simply multiply the length of the
n
-supertile
\varrho^{n}(a)
by the ‘average weight’ of
f
. Such quantities are prevalent in recent works on bounded displacement equivalence
[
23
,
44
]
.

Example 7.2
.

For a primitive substitution on a finite alphabet,
\mu
is represented by the row vector
f=(f_{\alpha},f_{\beta},\ldots)
of frequencies
f_{a}>0
of each letter
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Then the expected number of tiles of type
b
in
\varrho^{n}(a)
is

\mathrm{Exp}(\mathds{1}_{b},a,n)=r^{n}\ell(a)\mu(\mathds{1}_{b})=r^{n}\ell(a)(v\cdot f)=r^{n}\ell(a)f_{b}
,
where
v
is the vector with entry corresponding to
b
equal to
1
and all other entries
0
. So we just multiply length of the
n
-supertile by the frequency of occurrence of
b
. More generally, given a weighted selection
v=(v_{\alpha},v_{\beta},\ldots)
of tiles, where we have one
v_{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for each tile
a\in\mathcal{A}
,

\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)=r^{n}\ell(a)(v\cdot f).

Here, we are identifying
v
with an arbitrary function
f\in E
, given by
f(a)=v_{a}
.

\Diamond

We define the
actual
weighted sum of
f
across an
n
-supertile
\varrho^{n}(a)
by

\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)=\sum_{b\triangleleft\varrho^{n}(a)}f(b)=(M^{n}f)(a),

where the second equality follows from Lemma
4.5
. The result below bounds the discrepancy between the expected and actual weight across all possible supertiles.
For a treatment for primitive substitutions on finite alphabets and applications to questions on balancedness, we refer to
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
, respectively.

Theorem 7.3
.

Suppose assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold. Then, there is some function
\theta\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
with
\sqrt[n]{\theta(n)}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
so that for all
f\in E
with
\|f\|\leq 1
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
we have

|\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)-\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)|\leq\theta(n)(r_{2})^{n}.

In particular, for any
\epsilon>0
, we have the following for sufficiently large
n
:

|\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)-\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)|\leq(r_{2}+\epsilon)^{n}.

In the above, recall that
r_{2}
denotes the maximum modulus of element of
\sigma(M)\setminus\{r\}
. Before proving this result, we require a few technical lemmas regarding the spectrum of the operator restricted to the subspace

\Pi\coloneqq\{f\in E\mid\mu(f)=0\}.

By uniform power convergence, we have
T^{n}\to P
uniformly as
n\to\infty
, where
P
is a projection to the eigenline
\Lambda=\langle\ell\rangle_{\mathbb{R}}
. Hence
\Pi=\ker(P)
is a closed, co-dimension one subspace and we have
E=\Lambda\oplus\Pi
. We let
T_{\Pi}
denote the restriction to
\Pi
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Suppose assumptions
(A1)
and
(A2)
hold. Then, we have the inclusion
\sigma(T_{\Pi})\subseteq\sigma(T)
.

Proof.

Since
P
is bounded (and thus continuous), its kernel is closed and so
T_{\Pi}
is a bounded operator on a Banach space. For such operators,
\tau\in\sigma(T)
if and only if
T-\tau\mathbb{I}
is not bijective. Suppose that
\tau\in\sigma(T_{\Pi})
. First assume that
T-\tau\mathbb{I}
is not injective, so that
T(f)=\tau f
for some
f\in\Pi
. The same
f
demonstrates that
\tau\in\sigma(T)
.

So suppose instead that
T-\tau\mathbb{I}
is not surjective, and thus there is some
g\in\Pi
with
Tf-\tau f\neq g
for any
f\in\Pi
. Assume, on the other hand, that
\tau\notin\sigma(T)
so that
Tf-\tau f=g
for some
f\in E
. We may write
f=a+b
for
a\in\Lambda
and
b\in\Pi
. Then

T(a+b)-\tau(a+b)=(Ta-\tau a)+(Tb-\tau b)=g\in\Pi.

But since
\Lambda
and
\Pi
are complementary this implies that
Ta-\tau a=0
. If
a\neq 0
it follows that
\tau=1
(since then
a
is a non-negative multiple of
\ell
), which we already know is in
\sigma(T)
. Otherwise, we have that
Tb-\tau b=g
, contradicting that
Tb-\tau b\neq g
for all
b\in\Pi
.
∎

We get the following consequence of uniform power convergence:

Lemma 7.5
.

We have that
1\notin\sigma(T_{\Pi})
.

Proof.

Let
f\in\Pi
be arbitrary; we have
T^{n}f\to P(f)=c\cdot\ell
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly in
f
. Clearly
c=0
, since
\mu(T^{n}f)=\mu(f)=0
for all
n
and
\mu(c\ell)=c
. Thus
T^{n}_{\Pi}\to 0
uniformly so
\|T_{\Pi}^{n}\|<1
for some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. It follows from Gelfand’s formula that
r(T_{\Pi})<1
, and thus
1\notin\sigma(T_{\Pi})
.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose that
\kappa\in\sigma(T)
with
\kappa\neq 1
. Then
\kappa\in\sigma(T_{\Pi})
.

Proof.

Let
\kappa\in\sigma(T)
with
\kappa\neq 1
. First assume that
Tf-\kappa f=0
for some
f\in E
and write
f=a+b
for
a=c\ell\in\Lambda
and
b\in\Pi
. Then

Tf-\kappa f=c(1-\kappa)\ell+(Tb-\kappa b)=0.

Since
c(1-\kappa)\ell\in\Lambda
,
Tb-\kappa b\in\Pi
, and the subspaces
\Lambda
and
\Pi
are complementary, it is necessary that
\kappa=1
or
c=0
. Since we assume that
\kappa\neq 1
, we have that
c=0
and thus
f\in\Pi
, so that
\kappa\in\sigma(T_{\Pi})
too.

So suppose instead that
Tf-\kappa f\neq g
for all
f\in E
and some
g\in E
. Write
g=a+b=c\ell+b
for
a\in\Lambda
and
b\in\Pi
. Suppose that
Tf-\kappa f=b
for some
f\in\Pi
. Then

T\left(\frac{c}{1-\kappa}\ell+f\right)-\kappa\left(\frac{c}{1-\kappa}\ell+f\right)=c\ell+(Tf-\kappa f)=c\ell+b=g,

contradicting that
Tx-\kappa x\neq g
for all
x\in E
. Hence
Tf-\kappa f\neq b
for all
f\in\Pi
and so
\kappa\in\sigma(T_{\Pi})
, as required.
∎

The three lemmas above imply the following result.

Corollary 7.7
.

We have
\sigma(T)=\sigma(T_{\Pi})\sqcup\{1\}
or, equivalently,
\sigma(M)=\sigma(M_{\Pi})\sqcup\{r\}
.

Proof of Theorem
7.3
.

We may write

|\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)-\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)|=|r^{n}\ell(a)\cdot\mu(f)-(M^{n}f)(a)|=|M^{n}(\mu(f)\ell-f)(a)|.

Since
M=rT
we thus have

|\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)-\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)|=r^{n}|T^{n}(\mu(f)\ell-f)(a)|=r^{n}|(T^{n}v)(a)|,

where
v=\mu(f)\ell-f
. We have that
\mu(v)=\mu(\mu(f)\ell-f)=\mu(f)(\mu(\ell))-\mu(f)=0
(since we normalise with
\mu(\ell)=1
) so
v\in T_{\Pi}
.

By Corollary
7.7
,
r(T_{\Pi})=r_{2}/r
. By Gelfand’s formula for the spectral radius,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{\|T_{\Pi}^{n}\|}=r(T_{\Pi}),\text{ hence }\sqrt[n]{\|T_{\Pi}^{n}\|}\leq\psi(n)(r_{2}/r),

where
\psi(n)\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Taking
n
th powers and substituting into the above,

|\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)-\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)|=r^{n}|(T^{n}v)(a)|=r^{n}|(T_{\Pi}^{n}v)(a)|\leq r^{n}\|T_{\Pi}^{n}\|\|v\|\leq\|v\|(\psi(n))^{n}(r_{2})^{n},

where
(\psi(n))^{n}
has
n
th roots converging to
1
as
n\to\infty
. Note that
\|v\|=\|\mu(f)\ell-f\|
is bounded above by a constant
c
, since
\mu
is bounded and
\|f\|\leq 1
, so the discrepancy estimate follows taking
\theta(n)=c(\psi(n))^{n}
.

Let
\delta>0
be arbitrary. Then
h(n)=(1+\delta)^{n}
is such that
\sqrt[n]{h(n)}=(1+\delta)>\sqrt[n]{\theta(n)}
, and thus
h(n)>\theta(n)
, for sufficiently large
n
. So for such
n
,

|\mathrm{Exp}(f,a,n)-\mathrm{Act}(f,a,n)|\leq\theta(n)(r_{2})^{n}\leq h(n)(r_{2})^{n}\leq(1+\delta)^{n}(r_{2})^{n}=((1+\delta)r_{2})^{n}.

Setting
0<\delta<\epsilon/r_{2}
establishes the second result.
∎

8.
Higher dimensions

Many constructions and results seen here, excluding those pertaining to natural length functions, also apply to the higher dimensional setting. We make some initial observations on these extensions in this final section.

8.1.
ILC tilings and substitutions in higher dimensions

In contrast to our symbolic setup in the
d=1
case, it makes sense to begin with the geometric substitution rule on a compact prototile set. Many of the following definitions are inspired by
[
20
]
and are extensions of those in Definition
5.1
. We let
\mathcal{A}
be a compact, Hausdorff space, now considered as a set of
labels
. We write

S({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\coloneqq\{X\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\mid X\text{ is non-empty and compact}\},

which we consider as a topological space, with topology induced by the Hausdorff distance (or equivalently the Vietoris topology). A
prototile
(with label in
\mathcal{A}
) is then a pair
p=(s,a)
, whose
support
is denoted by
\mathrm{supp}(p)\coloneqq s\in S({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. We assume that each
\mathrm{supp}(p)
is homeomorphic to a closed ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and that the origin lies in its interior. We further assume that the associated fusion rule is van Hove, in the sense of
[
20
]
. This does not seem too restrictive and holds, loosely speaking, so long as the boundaries of tiles are not too wild. Certain examples can easily be seen to be van Hove. This includes the pinwheel below, since in this example all supertiles are inflates of a single (up to rigid motion) polygonal shape. We let
\mathrm{label}(p)\coloneqq a
denote the
label
of prototile
p
.

A
prototile set

\mathcal{P}
is then a set of prototiles which is compact as a subspace of
S({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\mathcal{A}
. Without loss of generality,
\mathrm{label}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{A}
is a homeomorphism (see Remark
8.1
) i.e., all labels are realised and distinct prototiles have distinct labels (continuity of the inverse follows from compactness). Then
\mathrm{supp}\circ\mathrm{label}^{-1}\colon\mathcal{A}\to S({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is continuous, since both
\mathrm{supp}
and
\mathrm{label}^{-1}
are continuous. This condition may be interpreted as saying that, if a sequence of labels converges, then so do the supports of their tiles, a condition required in
[
20
]
.

Remark 8.1
.

Restricting
\mathcal{A}
to be compact causes no loss of generality given compactness of
\mathcal{P}
(as otherwise we may restrict labels to
\mathrm{label}(\mathcal{P})
). Similarly, the labelling being a homeomorphism causes no loss of generality. Indeed, otherwise, replace
\mathcal{A}
with
\mathcal{P}
; more precisely replace any prototile
p=(s,a)
with
(s,p)
(i.e., labelling the prototile with itself). Indeed, the map

f\colon\mathcal{P}\to S({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\times\mathcal{P},\quad p=(s,a)\mapsto(\mathrm{supp}(p),p)

is continuous and bijective onto the new prototile set
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{(\mathrm{supp}(p),p)\mid p\in\mathcal{P}\}
, with labels now in
\mathcal{P}
, so
\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
in a way preserving tile supports. An alternative is to drop the ‘labels’ and always work with
\mathcal{P}
in place of
\mathcal{A}
(or allowing different shapes of tiles with the same label). However, we will choose here to use the conventions in
[
20
]
.

\Diamond

For a prototile
p=(s,a)
and a vector
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define
p+x\coloneqq(s+x,a)
. Given a prototile set
\mathcal{P}
, a
tile
is a translate of a prototile. A
patch
is a finite set of tiles whose supports have mutually disjoint interiors and connected union. We let
\mathcal{P}^{*}
denote the set of patches. This can be topologised as follows. Consider an arbitrary patch
P\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
with tiles
t_{1}=p_{1}+x_{1}
, …,
t_{n}=p_{n}+x_{n}
, where
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
and
x_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For arbitrary open neighbourhoods
V_{i}\subset\mathcal{P}
of each
p_{i}
and
\epsilon>0
, we define an open neighbourhood
U(P,V_{1},\ldots,V_{n},\epsilon)
of
P
as all patches
P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
having a bijection
h
from the tiles of
P
to those of
P^{\prime}
so that, for each
i
and
h(p_{i}+x_{i})=p^{\prime}+x^{\prime}
, we have
p^{\prime}\in V_{i}
and
|x^{\prime}-x_{i}|<\epsilon
. A
tiling
is a covering of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by tiles with mutually disjoint interiors. The above topology on
\mathcal{P}^{*}
also topologises the space of all tilings, taking a basic open set for each open subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{*}
to consist of all tilings that contain a patch in
U
. So ‘small’ open sets are defined by basic open sets
U(P,V_{1},\ldots,V_{n},\epsilon)
for
P
covering a large ball about the origin, each
V_{i}
a small neighbourhood of each prototile
p_{i}
and
\epsilon>0
small; tilings in this basic open set thus contain a patch covering a large ball about the origin with tiles that may be paired with those of
P
in a way that only shifts tiles (translates and labels) a small amount.

A
substitution rule
(or
stone inflation
), with inflation factor
\lambda>1
, is defined to be a continuous map
\varrho\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}^{*}
so that, for each
p\in\mathcal{P}
, we have

\lambda\ \mathrm{supp}(p)=\bigcup_{t\in\varrho(p)}\mathrm{supp}(t).

An
n
-supertile
is a patch
\varrho^{n}(p)
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
p\in\mathcal{P}
, where
\varrho
is extended to patches in the obvious way. This again defines
generated patches
, the subpatches of supertiles, and
legal patches
, given as the closure of the collection of generated patches. We then obtain the
continuous hull
(or
tiling space
)
\Omega_{\varrho}
, consisting of all tilings whose finite patches are legal.

The space
\Omega:=\Omega_{\varrho}
is compact and Hausdorff, and translation by
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
acts continuously on it, making
(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
a topological dynamical system. By construction,
elements of
\Omega
are fusion tilings, and the results of
[
20
]
still apply to this slightly generalised setting, where
\mathcal{A}
is compact Hausdorff (but is not given a metric).

8.2.
Results on unique ergodicity in higher dimensions

Because our results on unique ergodicity mostly proceed in terms of the substitution operator, it turns out that essentially identical proofs of most results here follow also in the higher dimensional setting.

We still have the Banach space
E=C(\mathcal{P})
(recall that
\mathcal{P}\cong\mathcal{A}
) and the substitution operator
M
on it (Eq. (
3
)) where, for
f\colon\mathcal{P}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, we define
(Mf)(p)
, for a prototile
p\in\mathcal{P}
, to be

(Mf)(p)\coloneqq\sum_{t\in\varrho(p)}f(t),

summing with multiplicities. Here, we evaluate
f(p_{i}+x)=f(p_{i})
for all prototiles
p_{i}\in\mathcal{P}
and translates
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The notion of irreducibility and primitivity may be defined just as before, and results equating them to properties of the substitution operator (such as Propositions
4.19
and
4.11
) still hold, with identical proofs. The notion of a ‘natural length function’ no longer applies, but the function
\ell\in E
, given by setting
\ell(a)
as the volume of
a
, will be a positive eigenvector of
M
, with eigenvalue
\lambda^{d}
.

Most importantly here, Theorem
5.16
relating invariant measures of the tiling dynamical system to sequences of volume normalised and transition consistent measures still apply. Then Theorem
5.19
continues to hold in this higher dimensional setting, as does the part of Theorem
5.22
on unique ergodicity:

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
\varrho
be an irreducible substitution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, on a compact Hausdorff prototile set
\mathcal{P}
with
T=M/r
strongly power convergent. Then
(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is uniquely ergodic.

The higher dimensional analogue of Theorem
6.9
similarly follows by the same proofs. The important structure of constant length substitutions required for the proof of Theorem
6.18
is really that of having continuous columns. Thus, we define a
constant length substitution
(in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
) to be a substitution for which we have continuous maps
\varrho_{i}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}
for
i=1
, …,
L
, the
columns
, so that for each
p\in\mathcal{P}
there is a bijection between the tiles of
\varrho(p)
and
\{\varrho_{i}(p)\}_{i=1}^{L}
. This is exactly what is needed for Equation (
12
) to apply with each column continuous. Then, using an identical proof to that of Theorem
6.18
, we obtain:

Theorem 8.3
.

Suppose that
\varrho
is a primitive, constant length substitution in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
whose columns generate an equicontinuous semigroup
\Phi
. Then
T
is strongly power convergent and the tiling dynamical system associated with
\varrho
is uniquely ergodic.

Remark 8.4
.

For
d=1
the above notion of constant length coincides with the usual one i.e.,
|\varrho(p)|
is constant in
p\in\mathcal{P}
. It follows from the definition of the topology on
\mathcal{P}^{*}
that
|\varrho(p)|
is locally constant and thus globally constant if
\mathcal{P}
is connected. However, it is not clear to us that
|\varrho(p)|
being globally constant is sufficient for the constant length condition defined above for
d>1
, which requires a type of continuous indexing of subtiles.

\Diamond

We now demonstrate how unique ergodicity of the Conway–Radin pinwheel tilings
[
35
]
follows in a straightforward way from our results. Interestingly this approach seems quite distinct from others
[
21
,
33
]
, which make use of Weyl’s criterion at some stage to show statistical circular symmetry. Instead, this fact follows from unique ergodicity here because, from unique ergodicity, the unique eigenmeasure on the prototile space must correspond to Lebesgue measure.

Example 8.5
.

The pinwheel substitution has prototile set
\mathcal{P}
consisting of a
1
–
2
–
\sqrt{5}
distinguished triangle
p
and all linear isometries
gp
of it, for
g\in\mathrm{O}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. Thus,
\mathcal{P}
is homeomorphic to the disjoint union of two copies of
S^{1}
. The substitution
\varrho
is continuous and constant length: we may arbitrarily label the tiles of
\varrho(p)
with distinct labels from
1
to
5
to define
\varrho_{i}(p)
, and then we define
\varrho_{i}(gp)=g\,\varrho_{i}(p)
(note that this is well defined and continuous, since
gp=p
implies that
g=\mathrm{id}
).

Then each column
\varrho_{i}\colon\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}
is determined by some rigid motion
g_{i}\in\mathrm{O}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
, by setting
\varrho_{i}(gp)=g(g_{i}(p))
. Let
\alpha
be clockwise rotation by
\arctan\tfrac{1}{2}
,
\beta
be anticlockwise rotation by
\tfrac{\pi}{2}
and
\gamma
be reflection about the
y
-axis, all acting on the left. The columns (see Figure
1
) are then determined by

g_{1}=\alpha,\quad\quad\ g_{2}=\alpha\beta^{2},\quad\quad\ g_{3}=g_{4}=\alpha\beta^{2}\gamma\quad\quad\text{ and }\quad\quad g_{5}=\alpha\beta\gamma.

Figure 1.
The pinwheel substitution

Note that we have the relations
\alpha\beta=\beta\alpha
,
\gamma^{2}=\mathrm{id}
,
\alpha\gamma=\gamma\alpha^{-1}
and
\beta\gamma=\gamma\beta^{-1}
. Since
\mathrm{O}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
is a compact Lie group, the semigroup
\Phi
generated by the columns is uniformly equicontinuous. Primitivity of the substitution is easily established:
\varrho^{2}(p)
contains (up to translation) a copy of
p
(since
g_{3}^{2}(p)=p
), a reflection of
p
(since
g_{4}(g_{2}(p))=\gamma(p)
) and a rotation of
p
by the irrational angle
2\arctan\tfrac{1}{2}
(since
g_{1}^{2}(p)=\alpha^{2}(p)
), so that
\varrho^{n}(gp)
fills
\mathcal{P}
arbitrarily densely, uniformly in
g\in\mathrm{O}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
. Hence, unique ergodicity follows from Theorem
8.3
. In a similar way, any primitive generalised pinwheel tiling space
[
37
]
is uniquely ergodic.
