Traveling waves of an FKPP-type model for self-organized growth
Florian Kreten

Abstract
\vskip-12.0pt Abstract : We consider a reaction-diffusion system of densities of two types of particles, introduced by Edouard Hannezo et al. in the context of branching morphogenesis (Cell, 171(1):242–255.e27, 2017). It is a simple model for a growth process: active, branching particles form the growing boundary layer of an otherwise static tissue, represented by inactive particles. The active particles diffuse, branch and become irreversibly inactive upon collision with a particle of arbitrary type. In absence of active particles, this system is in a steady state, without any a priori restriction on the amount of remaining inactive particles. Thus, while related to the well-studied FKPP-equation, this system features a game-changing continuum of steady state solutions, where each corresponds to a possible outcome of the growth process. However, simulations indicate that this system self-organizes: traveling fronts with fixed shape arise under a wide range of initial data. In the present work, we describe all positive and bounded traveling wave solutions, and obtain necessary and sufficient conditions for their existence. We find a surprisingly simple symmetry in the pairs of steady states which are joined via heteroclinic wave orbits. Our approach is constructive: we first prove the existence of almost constant solutions and then extend our results via a continuity argument along the continuum of limiting points. Key words : Developmental biology, pattern formation, cellular organization, traveling wave, reaction-diffusion equation, continuum of fixed points. MSC2020 : 92C15, 35C07, 35K57, 34C14.

Motivation and result

The mechanics of tissue-growth have drawn the attention of the scientific community. A central question is, how the cells are organized, how they react to and communicate with their environment on the microscopic level, and how their behavior during the growth phase gives rise to distinct macroscopic structures. Mathematical models can help to understand these processes. Works regarding organoids, wound healing or tumor growth are abundant
[
26
,
25
,
12
,
11
,
19
]
. However, for most of these models, our numerical skills far predominate the possibility to analyze them rigorously. Hence, for understanding the basic mechanics of the underlying biological processes, the need for simplified models arises.

Especially when studying spatiotemporal effects and macroscopic pattern formation, reaction-diffusion systems and their traveling waves have proven insightful. One of the oldest and most studied models is the FKPP-equation
[
14
,
23
]
, describing the advance of an advantageous population. The arise of more complex spatial patterns due to the instability of a homogeneous state was first described in Turings groundbreaking paper
The Chemical basis of Morphogenesis

[
35
]
. More recently, systems of Keller-Segel type have been studied extensively, where growth, movement and self-organization of a population are driven by chemotactic guidance
[
20
,
31
,
30
]
. We want to highlight the works of Painter
[
30
]
and of Othmer
et al.

[
28
]
for an impression of mathematical modeling of pattern formation in developmental biology.

The group of Hannezo
et al.
proposed
A Unifying Theory of Branching Morphogenesis
in epithelial tissues
[
17
]
. They introduced a stochastic model, related to branching and annihilating random walks
[
9
]
. In this model, a branched structure is represented by a network. This network undergoes stochastic growth dynamics, where each branch of the network grows independently from the others and follows a set of simple, local rules. At its tip, each branch elongates or splits up at certain rates and these tips are called
active
. When an active tip comes too close to a different branch, it irreversibly ceases any activity and becomes
inactive
. The numerical results of Hannezo
et al.
reveal that this stochastic growth process self-organizes: the active tips are concentrated at the boundary of the network and form a rather sharp layer of growth. The center of the network is static and - rather surprisingly - exhibits a homogenous geometry, in particular a constant density of branches. Remarkably, as mentioned by the authors, this model self-organizes without any signaling gradients. Even a directional bias of the branches can be achieved, as the result of an appropriate spatial boundary. Moreover, the authors observed that their simulations were in good agreement with biological data from mammary glands, kidneys and the human prostate
[
17
]
.

The System (

1

For a wave speed

\displaystyle 0=a_{zz}+ca_{z}+a-a(a+i),

(1.2)

\displaystyle 0=ci_{z}+a(a+i)+ra.

The occurrence of these seemingly stable traveling waves is quite surprising, since the System (

\displaystyle A=0,\,I=K,\,K\in\mathds{R}^{+},

(1.3)

which is due to the fact that the inactive particles do not degrade. This continuum of steady states represents the difficulty when studying the system: we first need to find out which limiting states are chosen by the growth process.

Hannezo
et al.
presented a rich discussion of the Wave-Equation (
1.2

The goal of this paper is to give necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of such traveling wave solutions and to analyze the shape of the wave form. Our main result characterizes a family of pulled traveling waves:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

1.1
) and its traveling wave solutions given by (

\displaystyle i_{+\infty}\in[i_{c},1),\qquad i_{-\infty}=2-i_{+\infty},

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow\pm\infty}

\displaystyle a(z)=0,\qquad\lim_{z\rightarrow\pm\infty}i(z)=i_{\pm\infty}.

is exponentially fast. Convergence as

\displaystyle\mu_{\pm\infty}=-\frac{c}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+i_{\pm\infty}-1}.

(1.6)

Moreover, these are all bounded, non-negative, non-constant and twice differentiable solutions of Eq. (

).

) to self-organize and at the robustness of this mechanism, e.g. against errors of individual particles. Our theoretical analysis fortifies the numerical and biological findings of Hannezo

, where a simple set of local rules organizes the growth of a complex epithelial structure. The underlying assumption of a logistic growth is quite natural, so similar rules might drive and regulate other growth processes as well, without the need for guiding gradients.

2.1
Identifying the correct limits

We reformulate the System (
1.2
) for a traveling wave as an equivalent system of first-order ODEs. Denoting differention with respect to
z
by a prime, we introduce the auxiliary variable
a^{\prime}=b
, so that
(
1.2
) becomes

\displaystyle a^{\prime}

\displaystyle=b,

\displaystyle b^{\prime}

\displaystyle=a(a+i)-a-cb,

(2.1)

\displaystyle i^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{c}a\left(a+i+r\right),

for some
c>0,r\geq 0
. We call a solution of Eq. (
2.1
) non-negative if
a,i\geq 0
. If a solution is in
C^{1}(\mathds{R},\mathds{R}^{3})
, then it is also in
C^{\infty}
by a simple induction. This equation has a continuum of non-negative fixed points, similar to that of the PDE, cf. (
1.3
):

\displaystyle a=b=0,\quad i\in\mathds{R^{+}}.

(2.2)

Thus, in the first place, we need to find out which of these fixed points can be considered as limits of right-traveling waves. Any bounded and non-negative solution of System (
2.1
) can not be periodic and must converge since
ci^{\prime}=-a(a+i+r)\leq 0
. It is now evident that the limits at
z=\pm\infty
must be fixed points of Eq. (
2.1
), thus we denote them as
(a,b,i)=(0,0,i_{\pm\infty})
. Under mild assumptions regarding integrability, we can interrelate two different points on a given traveling wave, see Section
3
. Most importantly, this leads to the correspondence of the limits

\displaystyle i_{+\infty}+i_{-\infty}=2.

(2.3)

In view of this, monotonicity of
i
implies that
i_{-\infty}\in(1,2]
and
i_{+\infty}\in[0,1)
.

The fixed points of the ODE System (
2.1
) are not isolated, hence its Jacobian
D
is degenerate there. It is easily verified that
D
is given by

\displaystyle D_{(a,b,i)}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
2a+i-1&-c&a\\
-\frac{1}{c}(2a+i+r)&0&-\frac{a}{c}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.4)

At a fixed point
(a,b,i)=(0,0,K)
, the eigenvalues of
D_{(0,0,K)}
are

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\lambda_{\pm}=-\frac{c}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+K-1}.

(2.5)

Hence, we can not apply the classical Theorem of Grobmann-Hartmann to linearize the asymptotic behavior around the fixed points. We apply center manifold theory to work out the higher moments of the approximation, see Section
4
. The center manifold coincides with the continuum of fixed points. This implies that asymptotically, there is no flow along the direction of the eigenvector
(a,b,i)=(0,0,1)
with zero eigenvalue. Hence, the asymptotic flow around any fixed point is two-dimensional and the stability of the fixed point
(0,0,K)
is dictated by the two eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
. When
K>1
, the fixed point is unstable, while for
K<1
, it is stable.

At the same time, the analysis of the asymptotic behavior also yields a necessary condition on the speed
c
of a non-negative wave. A traveling wave can only be non-negative if
a(z)
does not spiral while converging to
0
. Therefore, the two eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
at the limiting fixed point must be real-valued. In view of (
2.5
), for a fixed point
(0,0,K)
, this is given if

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{4}+K-1\geq 0.

(2.6)

Thus, if the stable fixed point
(0,0,i_{+\infty})
is the limit of a non-negative traveling wave, where
i_{+\infty}\in[0,1)
, it must by (
2.6
) further hold that

\displaystyle\frac{c^{2}}{4}+i_{+\infty}-1\geq 0\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad i_{+\infty}\geq i_{c}=\max\{0,1-\frac{c^{2}}{4}\},

(2.7)

as in Theorem
1.1
. In other words,
i_{c}
is the minimal limiting density of inactive particles that is necessary for the existence of a non-negative traveling wave with speed
c
.

2.2
Construction of a traveling wave

We will explicitly construct a non-negative traveling wave such that the two necessary conditions
i_{+\infty}\geq i_{c}
and
i_{+\infty}+i_{-\infty}=2

We first verify global integrability of a non-negative solution:

Lemma 3.1

Let

Proof.

Let
a(z),b(z),i(z)
be a smooth, bounded and non-negative solution of Eq. (

and

. If not

At this point,

and the wave is constant, or

, denoted as

. Since

, we may assume that this be the first local minimum after passing through

\displaystyle\int_{-M}^{M}b^{\prime}(z)+cb(z)+a(z)\,dz=\int_{-M}^{M}a(z)\cdot\big[a(z)+i(z)\big]\,dz.

We know that the right-hand is integrable since

3.3
), (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) simplify to

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}(+\infty)

\displaystyle=i_{+\infty}\cdot\mathscr{A}(+\infty)+\frac{1+r}{2c}\mathscr{A}(+\infty)^{2},

(3.8)

\displaystyle\frac{(1+r)}{c}\mathscr{A}(+\infty)

\displaystyle=i_{-\infty}-i_{+\infty}.

(3.9)

Either
\mathscr{A}(+\infty)=0
implies
i_{+\infty}=i_{-\infty}
, or we can divide the first equation by
\mathscr{A}(+\infty)
. Solving the resulting linear system proves the claim.
∎

4
Asymptotics around the fixed points

Let us recall that the Jacobian
D_{(0,0,K)}
of the ODE System (
2.1
) at a fixed point
(0,0,K)
has eigenvalues

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=0,\hskip 28.45274pt\lambda_{\pm}=-\frac{c}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+K-1}.

(4.1)

The existence of
\lambda_{0}
implies the existence of a center manifold. In the present case, it locally coincides with the set of fixed points
a=b=0
. This implies that there is no flow along the center manifold, so the asymptotics are fully described by the remaining two linear terms. The calculations are standard and presented in Appendix
A
, along with a short review of the underlying theory. We only state the results here. First, regarding the unstable set
S_{-\infty}
, as defined in (
2.8
):

Theorem 4.1

(Unstable set)
.

For
i_{-\infty}>1
, the point
(a,b,i)=(0,0,i_{-\infty})
is an unstable fixed point of Dynamics (
2.1
). Locally, there exists a smooth unstable manifold of dimension one. Its restriction to
\{a\geq 0\}
is the unique trajectory that emerges from the fixed point such that
a(z),i(z)>0
as
z\rightarrow-\infty
. It has the following properties:

\displaystyle\bullet\lim_{z\rightarrow-\infty}a(z)=0,

(4.2)

\displaystyle\bullet\lim_{z\rightarrow-\infty}i(z)=i_{-\infty},

\displaystyle\bullet b(z)>0,\text{ }b^{\prime}(z)>0,\text{ }i^{\prime}(z)<0\quad\text{as }z\rightarrow-\infty.

Proof.

By choice of
i_{-\infty}>1
, the eigenvalue
\lambda_{+}
is positive, whereas
\lambda_{-}
is negative. Denote by
u,v,w
the coordinates in the system of eigenvectors
e_{0},e_{+},e_{-}
of the Jacobian at the fixed, where the fixed point is shifted to the origin. This transformation is done explicitly Lemma
A.11
. By Theorem
A.12
, the dynamics in an open neighborhood around the fixed point are equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u^{\prime}&=0,\\
v^{\prime}&=\lambda_{+}\,v,\\
w^{\prime}&=\lambda_{-}\,w.\end{aligned}

(4.3)

Hence, there is a stable and an unstable manifold, each of dimension one. The eigenvector
e_{+}
describes the asymptotic direction of the unstable manifold, in coordinates
a,b,i
it is given by

\displaystyle e_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda_{-}\\
i_{-\infty}-1\\
\frac{1}{c}(r+i_{-\infty})\cdot\frac{\lambda_{-}}{\lambda_{+}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(4.4)

Since
\lambda_{-}<0
and
\lambda_{+}>0
, asymptotically along the branch of the unstable manifold in direction
e_{+}
, where
a>0
and
b>0
, it also holds that
b^{\prime}=\lambda_{+}\,(i_{-\infty}-1)>0
and that
ci^{\prime}=\lambda_{-}\,(r+i_{-\infty})<0
.
∎

Next, we prove Lyapunov-stability of the points in
S_{+\infty}
, defined in (
2.9
). Figure
4
shows how the phase lines converge to
(0,0)
in the
(a,b)
-plane. For technical reasons, we require that
\lambda_{+}\neq\lambda_{-}
. Later, we deal with this degenerate case via a continuity argument.

(a,b)
of the Wave Eq. (

) if we impose a fixed value of

, see also Section

. The choices of

for

Theorem 4.2

(Stable set)
.

For all
c>0
and
i_{+\infty}\in[i_{c},1)
, such that
i_{+\infty}>c^{2}/4-1
, the point
(a,b,i)=(0,0,i_{+\infty})
is Lyapunov stable under Dynamics (
2.1
). In a small neighborhood,
(a,b)\rightarrow(0,0)
exponentially fast.

Proof.

By choices of
c
and
i_{+\infty}
, both non-zero eigenvalues (
4.1
) of the Jacobian are real-valued and negative and it holds that
\lambda_{+}\neq\lambda_{-}
. As before, denote by
u,v,w
the coordinates in the system of eigenvectors
e_{0},e_{+},e_{-}
of the Jacobian at the fixed point, see Lemma
A.11
. By Theorem
A.12
, the dynamics of the system in a neighborhood around the fixed point are equivalent to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u^{\prime}&=0,\\
v^{\prime}&=\lambda_{+}\,v,\\
w^{\prime}&=\lambda_{-}\,w.\end{aligned}

(4.5)

Take some small enough initial data
(\epsilon_{u},\epsilon_{v},\epsilon_{w})
: in view of Eq. (
4.5
),
\epsilon_{u}
does not vanish, but also does not propagate, whereas
v
and
w
converge to zero exponentially fast. Since
a
and
b
are represented in terms of
v
and
w
, see (
A.13
), they vanish exponentially fast.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
c>0
and
i_{+\infty}<i_{c}
. There is no non-negative and non-constant traveling wave that converges to
(a,b,i)=(0,0,i_{+\infty})
as
z\rightarrow+\infty
.

Proof.

As in the previous Theorem, the asymptotic behavior of Eq. (
2.1
) around the limiting fixed point
(a,b,i)=(0,0,i_{+\infty})
is described by the linear System (
4.5
). But now, since
i_{+\infty}<i_{c}=\max\{0,1-c^{2}/4\}
, either
i_{+\infty}<0
or both eigenvalues
\lambda_{\pm}
have a non-vanishing imaginary part and thus,

and
w
spiral. Since
a
and
b
are represented in terms of
v
and
w
, see (
A.13
), any trajectory that converges to
(0,0,i_{+\infty})
can not stay non-negative in its

-component.
∎

5
Attractor of a lower-dimensional sub-system

5.1
Construction and result

We begin our search for a non-negative attractor of

in an easier setting: we fix

and investigate the two-dimensional sub-system in the remaining coordinates. To separate it from the full system, we write it as

are drawn in Figure

Definition 5.1

(Two-dimensional sub-system)

For
c>0
and

, denote by
\bar{a}(z),\bar{b}(z)
the two-dimensional flow defined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bar{a}^{\prime}&=\bar{b},\\
\bar{b}^{\prime}&=\bar{a}(\bar{a}+i-1)-c\bar{b},\end{aligned}

(5.1)

which results from the Wave System (
2.1
) by fixing
i(z)=i
.

There are only two fixed points of (
5.1
),
(\bar{a},\bar{b})=(0,0)
and
(\bar{a},\bar{b})=(1-i,0)
. We denote the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Jacobian at
(0,0)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\pm}(i)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{c}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+i-1},\qquad l_{\pm}(i):=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\mp}\\
1-i\end{pmatrix}.

(5.2)

It holds that
\lambda_{-}(i)\leq\lambda_{+}(i)<0

\displaystyle:=-\frac{c}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+1-i},\qquad r_{\pm}(i):=\begin{pmatrix}-\beta_{\mp}\\
1-i\end{pmatrix},

(5.3)

and it holds that
\beta_{-}(i)<0<\beta_{+}(i)

We now define the region

. It is a triangle, spanned by the two fixed points
(0,0)
and
(1-i,0)
and two adjacent eigenvectors:

(The triangle

)
.

(Invariant region of the reduced system)
.

The set

(5.5)

It holds that

and
\bar{b}\leq 0
within

For a fixed

5.2
Invariance and monotonicity of
\boldsymbol{T_{c}(i)}

We analyze the
(\bar{a},\bar{b})

in detail. We prove that the Flow (
5.1
) at the boundary of

points inwards, and that the sets

are increasing in
-i
. For not getting lost in the following fiddly computations, always keep Figure
5
in mind. We first examine the eigenvector

at the fixed point

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
1>i>i_{c}
, and let
l_{+}(i)=(\lambda_{-}(i),1-i)
be defined as in (
5.2
). The quotient of the absolute values of the
\bar{b}

\bar{a}
-component of

is increasing in

.

Proof.

The claim is equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{di}\,\frac{|\lambda_{-}(i)|}{1-i}>0.

(5.6)

Recall that
\alpha_{-}(i)=-c/2-\sqrt{c^{2}/4+i-1}<0
. A computation reveals that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{di}\,\frac{|\lambda_{-}(i)|}{1-i}&=-\frac{d}{di}\,\frac{\lambda_{-}(i)}{1-i}\\

&=\frac{1-i+c\sqrt{c^{2}/4+i-1}+2(\frac{c^{2}}{4}+i-1)}{2(1-i)^{2}\sqrt{c^{2}/4+i-1}}>0,\end{aligned}

(5.7)

the last inequality holds since
i_{c}<i
implies
i>1-c^{2}/4
.
∎

Now, since
\gamma_{l}(i)
is the angle between the two vectors
(0,1)
and
-l_{+}(i)

.

Let
c>0
and

\gamma_{l}(i)

-i
.

For the invariance of
T_{c}(i)
, we need

Lemma 5.7
.

For any

, the Flow (
5.1
) at the point
(\bar{a},\bar{b})=-p\cdot l_{+}(i)

.

Proof.

Let
L_{inw}:=\big(1-i,-\lambda_{-}(i)\big)
be orthogonal to
l_{+}(i)
and point inwards
T_{c}(i)
. The claim of the Lemma is now equivalent to

\displaystyle\Big\langle\begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}^{\prime}\\
\bar{b}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},L_{inw}\Big\rangle>0.

(5.8)

Let
(\bar{a},\bar{b})=-p\cdot l_{+}(i)
. First compute the Flow (
5.1

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}^{\prime}\\
\bar{b}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}&=\begin{pmatrix}-p(1-i)\\

&=p\begin{pmatrix}-(1-i)\\
p\lambda_{-}^{2}+(1-i)(\lambda_{-}+c)\end{pmatrix}.\end{aligned}

(5.9)

Its part in direction

is given by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \Big\langle\begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}^{\prime}\\

(5.10)

∎

For the fixed point
(\bar{a},\bar{b})=(1-i,0)

.

Let

, and let

\displaystyle\frac{d}{di}\,\frac{1-i}{|\beta_{-}(i)|}<0.

(5.11)

Proof.

Recall that
\beta_{-}(i)=-c/2-\sqrt{c^{2}/4+1-i}
. A computation reveals that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{di}\,\frac{1-i}{|\beta_{-}(i)|}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{di}\frac{1-i}{\beta_{-}(i)}=-\frac{-\beta_{-}+\frac{1-i}{2\sqrt{c^{2}/4+1-i}}}{\beta_{-}^{2}}<0

(5.12)

∎

Now, since
\gamma_{r}(i)
is the angle between the two vectors
(0,-1)
and
-r_{+}(i)

Corollary 5.9

Let
c>0
and
i_{c}\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<1
. It holds that

, the angle
\gamma_{l}(i)
is increasing in
-i
.

For the invariance of
T_{c}(i)
, we need

Lemma 5.10
.

For any
p>0

5.1
) at the point

T_{c}(i)

Proof.

Let
R_{inw}:=\big(i-1,-\beta_{-}(i)\big)
be orthogonal to
r_{+}(i)

T_{c}(i)

\displaystyle\Big\langle\begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}^{\prime}\\

(5.13)

Let
p>0
. We compute the Flow (
5.1
) at

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\bar{a}\\
\bar{b}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1-0\\
0\end{pmatrix}-p\cdot r_{+}(i)=\begin{pmatrix}1-i+p\beta_{-}\\

6.1
Invariant region of the full system

Theorem 6.1

(Invariant region of the full system)

for all

\displaystyle a(z),b(z)\,\in\,T_{c}(i_{c})\qquad\forall z\in[0,\infty).

(6.1)

Within

,
a\geq 0
and

. Notice that while

Corollary 6.2
.

Under the assumption that

for all

:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} a(z)&\rightarrow 0,\\

i(z)&\rightarrow i_{+\infty}\in[i_{c},1).\end{aligned}

(6.2)

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

In the full System (
2.1
) with coordinates
(a,b,i)
, neither

nor

depend on

, but only on

and

. Thus, we can easily compare the full system to the two-dimensional System (

) in coordinates

for fixed value

are equal, compare (
2.1
) and (

).

By Proposition
5.4
, this implies that

points strictly inwards

. There are two irrelevant exceptions: for

(a,b)=(1-i_{0},0)
is a fixed point of the reduced, but not of the full system. Since

and

, a Taylor-expansion reveals that

lies in the interior of

for small times

.

Importantly, the set

is not decreasing as a function of

. Hence, at each phase-time
z\geq 0
, the two components
a(z),b(z)
can not escape
T_{c}\big(i(z)\big)
. In fact, since
i^{\prime}\leq 0
and
T_{c}(i)
is increasing in
-i
, the set
T_{c}\big(i(z)\big)
is increasing in
z
, at most up to
T_{c}(i_{c})
. Thus,
a(z),b(z)
remain within
T_{c}(i_{c})
for all
z\geq 0
.
∎

With a very similar argument, we can determine the rate of convergence:

Proposition 6.3
.

Assume that
i(z)\geq i_{c}
for all
z\in[0,\infty)
, such that
(a,b,i)\rightarrow(0,0,i_{+\infty})
as
z\rightarrow+\infty
for some
i_{+\infty}\in[i_{c},1)
. If
i_{+\infty}>c^{2}/4-1
, then convergence is exponentially fast with rate

\displaystyle\mu_{+\infty}=-\frac{c}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+i_{+\infty}-1}<0.

(6.3)

Further, if
(c/2)^{2}-i_{+\infty}-1=0
, which can only happen if
i_{+\infty}=i_{c}
, then the system converges sub-exponentially fast. As
z\rightarrow+\infty
, the distance to the limit is of order

\displaystyle z\cdot e^{-\frac{c}{2}z}.

(6.4)

Proof.

In the case
i_{+\infty}\in(i_{c},1)
, all eigenvalues of the limit are simple, we refer to Section
4
. The system converges exponentially fast, as shown in Theorem
4.2
. It remains to determine the rate of convergence. The two candidates are
\lambda_{\pm}=-\frac{c}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4}+i_{+\infty}-1}
. Corresponding to
\lambda_{\pm}
, the projections of the eigenvectors into the
(a,b)
-plane are given by

\displaystyle l_{\pm}:=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{\mp}\\
1-i_{+\infty}\end{pmatrix}.

(6.5)

We know that
a(z),b(z)\in T_{c}(i_{+\infty})
for all
z\geq 0
. At
(0,0)
, the triangle
T_{c}(i_{+\infty})
is bounded by the line
-l_{+}
, see Def.
5.2
and Figure
5
. Since
0>\lambda_{+}>\lambda_{-}
, the direction of
l_{-}
is steeper than that of
l_{+}
, such that the line
\{q\cdot l_{-}\text{ | }q\in\mathds{R}\}
lies outside
T_{c}(i_{+\infty})
for all
q\neq 0
. Thus, the two components
a(z),b(z)
cannot converge towards
(0,0)
along
-l_{-}
. But since they converge exponentially fast, the only possible remaining rate of convergence is
\lambda_{+}
.

For the case
\lambda_{+}=\lambda_{-}=-c/2
, we do not have a complete description of the asymptotics around the fixed point. However, under the assumption that the system stays non-negative and converges, it converges along a stable manifold along which
(a,b,i)^{\prime}=-\frac{c}{2}\cdot(a,b,i)
asymptotically. This will be proven in Theorem
6.10
(which does not rely on this Proposition). Thus, it suffices to describe the dynamics on this manifold. The eigenvalue
-c/2
has algebraic multiplicity
2
, but only a single linearly independent eigenvector. In this setting, the subspace that corresponds to the eigenvalue is spanned by one eigenvector and one generalized eigenvector. It is well-known that this results in sub-exponential convergence, cf. chapter 9 in
[
6
]
.
∎

6.2
A small attractor

In view of the previous paragraph, convergence and non-negativeness follow if we can show that
i(z)\geq i_{c}
for all
z\geq 0
. If we choose
a_{0}
small enough, some rough bounds do the trick. We control the total mass of active particles via

Lemma 6.4
.

Fix
c>0,\,i_{0}\in(i_{c},1)
and let
a_{0}\in[0,(1-i_{0})/2]
. Under the assumption that
i(s)\geq i_{c}
is true for all
s\in[0,z]
, there exists a finite constant
L(c,i_{0})\geq 0
, such that the following bound holds for all
s\in[0,z]
:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}a(t)\,dt\leq\frac{ca_{0}-b(z)}{1-(i_{0}+a_{0})}\leq L\cdot a_{0}.

(6.6)

Proof.

With the help of Theorem
6.1
, we can use that
a(s),b(s)\in T_{c}(i_{c})
for all
s\in[0,z]
. We integrate
a(s)\cdot\big[1-i(s)\big]=a^{2}(s)-b^{\prime}(s)-cb(s)
and use that
b(0)=0
:

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{z}a(s)\cdot\big[1-i(s)\big]\,ds=ca_{0}-ca(z)-b(z)+\int_{0}^{z}a^{2}(s)\,ds.

(6.7)

By monotonicity:
1-i_{0}\leq 1-i(s)
and
0\leq a(s)\leq a_{0}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (1-i_{0}-a_{0})\int_{0}^{z}a(s)\,ds&\leq ca_{0}-ca(z)-b(z)\\
&\leq ca_{0}-b(z)\\
\Leftrightarrow\int_{0}^{z}a(s)\,ds&\leq\frac{ca_{0}-b(z)}{1-(i_{0}+a_{0})},\end{aligned}

(6.8)

where we need
a_{0}+i_{0}<1
to avoid a blow-up, which is true by our choice of
a_{0}
. It remains to bound
-b(z)
. Take a look at the flow in the
(a,b)
-plane in Figure
5
. It holds that
a(z),b(z)
stay within
T_{c}(i_{c})
. Within the triangle
T_{c}(i_{c})
, it holds for the left inner angle
\gamma_{l}(i_{c})
that

\displaystyle\tan\big(\gamma_{l}(i_{c})\big)\geq\frac{|b|}{|a|}.

(6.9)

Thus, also
-b(z)\leq\tan\big(\gamma_{l}(i_{c})\big)\cdot a(z)\leq L_{2}\cdot a_{0}
.
∎

Since we can bound the total mass of active particles, we can also bound the change of
i(z)
:

Proposition 6.5

(Small attractor of
S_{+\infty}
)
.

Fix
c>0
and
i\in(i_{c},1)
. There exists a positive constant
0<M\ll 1
, such that for all
0\leq a_{0}\leq M
:

\displaystyle i(z)\geq i_{c}\qquad\forall z\geq 0.

(6.10)

Hence, also
a(z),b(z)\in T_{c}(i_{c})
for all
z\geq 0
. The trajectory is non-negative and converges to
S_{+\infty}
as
z\rightarrow+\infty
.

Proof.

As long as
i(z)\geq i_{c}
, it must be that
a(z),b(z)\in T_{c}(i_{c})
by Theorem
6.1
. Assume there exists finite phase-time
\tau:=\inf_{z\geq 0}\{i(z)<i_{c}\}
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} i(\tau)&=i_{0}+\int_{0}^{\tau}i^{\prime}(z)\,dz&&=i_{0}-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{\tau}a(s)\big[a(s)+i(s)+r\big]\,ds\\
&&&\geq i_{0}-\frac{1}{c}\int_{0}^{\tau}a(s)\big[1+r\big]\,ds,\end{aligned}

(6.11)

where we used
a(s)+i(s)\leq 1
. For
z\leq\tau
and
a_{0}
sufficiently small, we can apply Lemma
6.4
. This implies that there is a finite constant
L
, which does not depend on
a_{0}
, such that

\displaystyle i(\tau)\geq i_{0}-\frac{L}{c}(1+r)\cdot a_{0}.

(6.12)

The right-hand side is strictly larger than
i_{c}
for sufficiently small
a_{0}
, say
a_{0}\leq M
. Thus, there is no such phase-time
\tau
for
a_{0}\leq M
.
∎

6.3
Extending the attractor

The previous section ended with a condition of type
a_{0}\ll 1
, under which the system stays non-negative and converges. However, given
a_{0}
and
i_{0}
and under the assumption that the system converges, we can explicitly calculate its limit
i_{+\infty}
. For fixed
i_{0}
, we continuously deform the trajectory while increasing
a_{0}
up to some upper bound
a^{\ast}(i_{0})
, as sketched in Figure
6
.

In what follows, we first assume that the system stays non-negative and converges, analyze its behavior under this assumption, and then verify that this must be true for certain initial data, which leads to Theorem
6.10
.

Figure 6
:
Trajectories of
a(z),i(z)
of the Wave System (
2.1
) for
c=2,r=0
. Initial values are
b(0)=0,i_{0}=0.5
, and
a_{0}
such that
a_{0}\in[0,a^{\ast}(i_{0})\approx 0.42]
. The upper bound
a^{\ast}
is given in Def.
6.8
. Trajectories with such initial data converge and stay non-negative, since
i(z)\geq i_{c}
.

We apply Proposition
3.2
to interrelate the limit
(0,0,i_{+\infty})
of the trajectory to its initial data
(a_{0},0,i_{0})
:

Lemma 6.6
.

If
i(z)\geq i_{c}
, such that the system stays non-negative and converges to
(0,0,i_{+\infty})
as
z\rightarrow+\infty
, then
i_{+\infty}
can be written as a function of
a_{0}
and
i_{0}
:

\displaystyle i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})=1-\sqrt{(i_{0}+a_{0}-1)^{2}+\frac{1+r}{c^{2}}(a_{0}^{2}+2c^{2}a_{0})}.

(6.13)

The function
i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})
is decreasing in
a_{0}
, for
a_{0}\in[0,1-i_{0}]
.

Proof.

We apply Proposition
3.2
, and solve the resulting system for
i_{+\infty}
. In the present case, since
a_{0}\neq 0
, this results in a quadratic equation with two possible solutions. By positiveness of
a(z)
and
i(z)
,
i(z)
is decreasing, so it must be that
i_{+\infty}<1
, which uniquely determines (
6.13
). It can easily be verified that
\frac{d}{d\,a_{0}}i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})\leq 0
.
∎

We look for values of
a_{0}
that ensure
i_{+\infty}\geq i_{c}
. Thus, we rearrange (
6.13
) for
a_{0}
, set
i_{+\infty}=i_{c}
, and choose the only possible positive solution of the resulting quadratic equation:

Lemma 6.7
.

Given
i_{0}
and under the assumption that
i_{+\infty}=i_{c}
, the value of
a_{0}
is uniquely determined by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha(i_{0})&:=\frac{c^{2}}{1+c^{2}+r}\Bigg\{-(i_{0}+r)\\
&\quad+\sqrt{(i_{0}+r)^{2}+\frac{c^{2}+1+r}{c^{2}}\Big((1-i_{c})^{2}-(1-i_{0})^{2}\Big)}\Bigg\}.\end{aligned}

(6.14)

Equation (
6.14
) can be restated as
i_{+\infty}\big(\alpha(i_{0}),i_{0}\big)=i_{c}
, but keep in mind that still have to prove convergence. It can easily be seen that
\alpha(i_{c})=0
. Since we require that
a_{0}\in[0,1-i_{0}]
, such that
a_{0}\in T_{c}(i_{0})
, this leads to our

Definition 6.8

(Upper bound for
a_{0}
)
.

For fixed
c>0
and, we define

\displaystyle a^{\ast}(i_{0}):=\min\Big\{\alpha(i_{0}),1-i_{0}\Big\},\quad\text{for }i_{0}\in[i_{c},1).

(6.15)

This will hold as sharp upper bound for
a_{0}
, such that the trajectory stays non-negative and converges. Before we state the corresponding theorem, we perform a last check that we are in the correct setup:

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
i_{0}\in[i_{c},1)
and
a_{0}\in[0,a^{\ast}(i_{0})]
. If the system stays non-negative and converges to
(0,0,i_{+\infty})
, then

\displaystyle i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})\,\in\,[i_{c},i_{0}],

(6.16)

where
i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})
is given as in Lemma
6.6
.

Proof.

It holds that
i_{+\infty}(0,i_{0})=i_{0}
. The claim follows since
i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})
is decreasing in
a_{0}
and since
a^{\ast}(i_{0})\leq\alpha(i_{0})
, where
i_{+\infty}\big(\alpha(i_{0}),i_{0}\big)=i_{c}
.
∎

Finally, we can remove the training wheels and get rid of the assumption that the system converges and stays non-negative. We state

Theorem 6.10

(Attractor of
S_{+\infty}
)
.

For
r\geq 0,c>0
, let
i_{0}\in[i_{c},1)
and
a_{0}\in\big[0,a^{\ast}(i_{0})\big]
. Let
a(z),b(z),i(z)
be the solution of Eq. (
2.1
) with initial data
(a_{0},0,i_{0})
.

It then holds that
a(z),i(z)\geq 0
and
i^{\prime}(z),b(z)\leq 0
for all
z\geq 0
. As
z\rightarrow+\infty
,
a(z)
and
b(z)
converge to
0
, and
i(z)
converges to

\displaystyle i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})=1-\sqrt{(i_{0}+a_{0}-1)^{2}+\frac{1+r}{c^{2}}(a_{0}^{2}+2c^{2}a_{0})}\quad\in\,[i_{c},1).

(6.17)

The type of convergence is given in Proposition
6.3
.

Proof.

Notation
: We fix
r,c,i_{0}\in(i_{c},1)
and change only
a_{0}
. If
i_{0}=i_{c}
, we must choose
a_{0}=0
. For visual clarity,
\Phi_{z}(x)
is the state of the system at phase-time
z
, starting in
{x=(a,b,i)}
. If the limit of a trajectory exists, we denote

\displaystyle\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0}):=\lim_{z\rightarrow+\infty}\Phi_{z}(a_{0},0,i_{0})=(0,0,i_{+\infty}).

(6.18)

Step 1: starting interval

For
a_{0}
positive but small enough, Proposition
6.5
grants that for all
z\geq 0
:

\displaystyle i(z)\geq i_{c}\quad\text{ and }\quad a(z),b(z)\in T_{c}(i_{c}),

(6.19)

where
T_{c}(i_{c})
is a bounded invariant region that contains only points such that
a\geq 0,b\leq 0
. Thus,
a(z),i(z)\rightarrow(0,i_{+\infty})
monotone. With the help of Lemma
6.6
, we can explicitly calculate
i_{+\infty}
as stated, and Eq.
6.17
holds on some small non-empty interval
a_{0}\in[0,a_{u})
.

Step 2: neighborhood of existing trajectories

Pick some
a_{0}<a^{\ast}(i_{0})
for which the statement is already proven. By choice of
a_{0}
, it holds that
i_{+\infty}>i_{c}
. Thus, the limit
\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0})
is Lyapunov stable by our previous analysis of the asymptotics, see Theorem
4.2
: for every
\epsilon_{\infty}>0
, there exists a
\delta_{\infty}>0
, such that

\displaystyle||x-\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0})||<\delta_{\infty}\Rightarrow||\Phi_{z}(x)-\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0})||<\epsilon_{\infty}

(6.20)

for all
z\in[0,\infty)
. Choose
\epsilon_{\infty}\leq i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})-i_{c}
and
0<\delta_{\infty}\leq\epsilon_{\infty}
. This grants

after entering the
\delta_{\infty}
-neighborhood. Within this attractor, also

in view of Theorem
6.1
, since
i(z)\geq i_{c}
.

Starting in
(a_{0},0,i_{0})
, we follow the trajectory up to some finite time
\tau

\delta_{\infty}
-neighborhood:

\displaystyle||\Phi_{\tau}(a_{0},0,i_{0})-\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0})||\leq\frac{\delta_{\infty}}{2}.

(6.21)

The derivative of the system is locally Lipschitz continuous, and so trajectories
\Phi_{z}(x_{0})
are uniformly continuous in initial data
x_{0}
on finite time intervals. This is a classic result, we refer to Chapter 2 of the textbook of Hsieh and Yasutaka
[
18
]
. There exists some
\delta_{0}>0
, s.t. for all

with

\displaystyle||\Phi_{z}(x)-\Phi_{z}(a_{0},0,i_{0})||_{[0,\tau]}<\frac{\delta_{\infty}}{2}.

(6.22)

This implies for all such trajectories
\Phi_{z}
:

\displaystyle 1)\text{ }||\Phi_{\tau}(x)-\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0})||<\delta_{\infty},\text{ and}

(6.23)

\displaystyle 2)\text{ }i(z)\geq i_{c}\qquad\forall z\in[0,\tau].

(6.24)

In particular,
\Phi_{\tau}(x)
lies within the
\delta_{\infty}
-neighborhood. We conclude that for all initial data
x\in\mathds{R}^{3},||x-(a_{0},0,i_{0})||<\delta_{0}
:

\displaystyle 3)\text{ }i(z)\geq i_{c}\qquad\forall z\geq 0.

(6.25)

Again, Theorem
6.1
implies
a(z),i(z)\in T_{c}(i_{c})
for all
z\geq 0
. As before, the system is integrable and converges as
z\rightarrow+\infty
, so we can explicitly calculate
i_{+\infty}(a_{0},i_{0})
. Thus, our claim holds for all starting points
(a_{1},0,i_{0})
, where
a_{1}
lies in a small open interval around
a_{0}
.

Step 3: limits of trajectories

Assume that the claim holds for all
a_{0}\in[0,a_{u})
. For all trajectories starting in
(a_{0},0,i_{0})
, where
a_{0}\in[0,a_{u})
, it holds that
i(z)
is monotone and bounded from below by
i_{c}
, such that
a(z),b(z)
stay within
T_{c}(i_{c})
. These trajectories are uniformly continuous in initial data on finite time-intervals. Fix any finite time-horizon
[0,T]
. The trajectories
\Phi_{z}(a_{0},0,i_{0})
form a Cauchy-sequence on
||.||_{[0,T]}
as
a_{0}\rightarrow a_{u}
. Since
T
can be chosen arbitrarily large and since all trajectories converge towards
\Phi_{+\infty}(a_{0},0,i_{0})
, which continuous in
a_{0}
, the claim also holds for the limiting trajectory that starts in
a_{u}
.

Step 4: conclusion

By step 1, the claim holds for
a_{0}
in some small interval
[0,a_{u})
. By step 3, it then also holds for
a_{0}=a_{u}
. If now
a_{u}<a^{\ast}
, the claim holds for
a_{0}\in[0,a_{u}+\epsilon)
by step 2 for some

. Iterating these two steps, the claim ultimately holds for all
a_{0}\in\big[0,a^{\ast}(i_{0})\big]
. In particular, we have proven that the trajectories
\Phi_{z}(a_{0},0,i_{0})
are uniformly continuous with respect to initial data on
z\in[0,+\infty]
. This continuity allows us to finish the proof of Proposition
6.3
. In the non-critical cases where
c^{2}/4+i_{+\infty}-1>0
, the trajectories converge along a stable manifold with rate of convergence
-c/2+\sqrt{c^{2}/4+i_{+\infty}-1}
. As
c^{2}/4+i_{+\infty}-1\rightarrow 0
, the critical trajectory must converge along the limit of these manifolds.
∎

7
The complete trajectory

We follow the unstable manifold of points in
S_{-\infty}
, see (

), and show that it stays positive and enters the attractor of

from the previous section, cf. Theorem

.

Assumption
: We will use the following setup over the entire Section
7
: Let
i_{-\infty}>1
and let
a(z),b(z),i(z)
be the unique solution of the ODE-System (

) that emerges from
(0,0,i_{-\infty})
as

asymptotically as

For all

, existence and uniqueness of these trajectories have been proven in Section

. Moreover, we know their asymptotic behavior:

Lemma 7.1
.

The following holds as

:

\displaystyle a(z)

\displaystyle>0,

(7.1)

\displaystyle b(z)

\displaystyle>0,

\displaystyle(a+i)^{\prime}

\displaystyle<0.

The first two inequalities are given by Theorem

7.1
The maximum of active particles

For connecting these trajectories with
S_{+\infty}
, we need

Proposition 7.2

(The maximum of active particles)
.

There exists a finite phase-time
z_{0}
, such that
b(z_{0})=0
for the first time.

Again, a certain monotonicity of the system comes at help. We will show that the sum
a(z)+i(z)
decreases below
1
. Given this, the term
cb(z)+b^{\prime}(z)=a(z)\cdot[a(z)+i(z)-1]
becomes negative, so
b
must eventually reach
0
.

Lemma 7.3
.

As long as
b(s)>0
for all
s\in(-\infty,z]
, it holds that

\displaystyle b(z)+i^{\prime}(z)<0.

(7.2)

Proof.

In view of the asymptotic behavior of the trajectory, described in Lemma
7.1
, assume that there exists a finite time
z^{\ast}
, such that for the first time
b(z^{\ast})+i^{\prime}(z^{\ast})=0
, but still
b(z^{\ast})>0
. The wave-equations
0=b^{\prime}+cb+a-a(a+i)
and
0=ci^{\prime}+a(a+i)+ra
imply that

\displaystyle 0=cb(z^{\ast})+ci^{\prime}(z^{\ast})

\displaystyle=-a(z^{\ast})\cdot(1+r)-b^{\prime}(z^{\ast})

(7.3)

\displaystyle=c\cdot b(z^{\ast})-a(z^{\ast})\cdot\big[a(z^{\ast})+i(z^{\ast})+r\big].

(7.4)

Rearranging the second line yields
cb(z^{\ast})=a(z^{\ast})\cdot\big[a(z^{\ast})+i(z^{\ast})+r\big]
. As long as
z<z^{\ast}
, it holds that
b(z)+i^{\prime}(z)<0
, hence also

\displaystyle cb(z)

\displaystyle<a(z)\cdot\big[a(z)+i(z)+r\big].

(7.5)

However, equality at
z=z^{\ast}
implies that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}cb(z)\Big|_{z^{\ast}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{d}{dz}a(z)\cdot\big[a(z)+i(z)+r\big]\Big|_{z^{\ast}},

(7.6)

which we can rewrite, using both (
7.3
) and (
7.4
) :

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} c\cdot b^{\prime}(z^{\ast})&\geq b(z^{\ast})\cdot\big[a(z^{\ast})+i(z^{\ast})+r\big]+a(z^{\ast})\cdot\big[b(z^{\ast})+i^{\prime}(z^{\ast})\big]\\
&=\frac{a(z^{\ast})}{c}\cdot\big[a(z^{\ast})+i(z^{\ast})+r\big]^{2}+0\geq 0.\end{aligned}

(7.7)

But
a(z^{\ast})>0
, so Eq. (
7.3
) implies that
b^{\prime}(z^{\ast})=-(1+r)a(z^{\ast})<0
. This contradicts (
7.7
).
∎

Lemma 7.4
.

As long as
b(s)>0
for all
s\in(-\infty,z]
, it can not happen that
a(z)+i(z)
converges to some finite
L>0
.

Proof.

By the previous lemma:
(a+i)^{\prime}<0
while
b>0
. Assume that
a(z)+i(z)
converges to a finite value
L>0
from above, which we denote as
a(z)+i(z)\,\searrow\,L
. This implies that also
b+i^{\prime}\,\nearrow\,0
. By the Wave Equations (
1.2
), these two expressions are equivalent to

\displaystyle-a(1+r)-b^{\prime}

\displaystyle\,\nearrow\,0\quad\text{ and }

(7.8)

\displaystyle cb-a(a+i+r)

\displaystyle\,\nearrow\,0.

(7.9)

The first convergence indicates that
b^{\prime}\leq\delta<0
after some time
z_{\delta}
, since
a
is strictly increasing and hence positive. The second statement is equivalent to
cb-a\cdot(L+r)\,\nearrow\,0
. Thus, also
b
is increasing. But
b^{\prime}(z)<0
for all
z\geq z_{\delta}
and while
b>0
, a contradiction.
∎

We can now finish the

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

We now show that there exists a finite phase-time
z_{0}
such that
b(z_{0})=0
. By the previous lemma,

decreases below every positive value as long as

\displaystyle cb(z)+b^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=a(z)\cdot\big[a(z)+i(z)-1\big]

\displaystyle\leq a(z)\cdot(1-\epsilon-1)=-a(z)\epsilon<0.

Either

and the system has already passed a first local maximum of

. The bound

.
∎

7.2
Reaching the attractor of
\boldsymbol{S_{+\infty}}

We now prove that
(a_{z_{0}},0,i_{z_{0}})
lies in the attractor of the stable set

. Therefore, we show that

.

The following holds at phase-time

\displaystyle a_{z_{0}}

\displaystyle>0,\quad a_{z_{0}}+i_{z_{0}}\leq 1,

\displaystyle a_{z_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}}{c^{2}+1+r}\Bigg\{-(i_{z_{0}}+r)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{(i_{z_{0}}+r)^{2}+\frac{c^{2}+1+r}{c^{2}}\Big((i_{-\infty}-1)^{2}-(1-i_{z_{0}})^{2}\Big)}\Bigg\}.

(7.12)

In the case

, then additionally

\displaystyle i_{z_{0}}\in(i_{c},1),\qquad a_{z_{0}}\in(0,1),

(7.13)

and the trajectory is non-negative for

.

Proof.

As

In particular,

Finally, we connect the unstable manifold of

8
Concluding the proof of the main result

We bring together our results from the previous sections and complete the

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
i_{-\infty}\in(1,2-i_{c}]
. We consider the ODE System (
2.1
) in coordinates
a,b,i
. The unstable manifold of the fixed point
(a,b,i)=(0,0,i_{-\infty})
has dimension one. Its two branches are the only trajectories that leave the fixed point, which is stated in Theorem
4.1
. There is one branch of the unstable manifold such that
a(z)>0
as
z\rightarrow-\infty
, we follow this trajectory in positive direction of
z
. There is a finite phase-time
z_{0}
, such that for the first time

, see Proposition
7.2
. Up to time

,

and

. Denote the state of the system at

as
(a_{z_{0}},0,i_{z_{0}})
. Lemma
7.5
states that
i_{z_{0}}\in(i_{c},1)
, Proposition
7.6
states that
a_{z_{0}}\in(0,a^{\ast}(i_{z_{0}})]
, for
a^{\ast}
as in Definition
6.8
. By Theorem
6.10
, we then know that
(a_{z_{0}},0,i_{z_{0}})
lies in a non-negative attractor of the set

. Thus,
a(z),b(z),i(z)\rightarrow(0,0,i_{+\infty})
as

, where

, and ultimately

for all

.

For any non-negative and bounded solution, the identity

holds by Proposition

. For
c>0
and
i_{-\infty}\in(1,2-i_{c}]
, the previous paragraph proves existence and uniqueness of the claimed wave. For

, the constant solution can be the only non-negative and bounded one.

Now assume that there exists a non-constant, bounded and non-negative solution. By monotonicity of

, it must converge as

. If
i_{-\infty}\in(1,2-i_{c}]
, it is one of the above solutions. If

, then

. In this case, the trajectory can not stay non-negative as

∎

9
Discussion and outlook at stability

9.1
FKPP-waves

We have given a description of all bounded and non-negative traveling waves of the Reaction-Diffusion System (
1.1
). For the most related systems, the FKPP-equation
[
23
,
14
]
, the FitzHugh-Nagumo-equation
[
15
,
27
]
and combustion equations
[
4
]
, no such continuum of traveling waves has yet been constructed.

Apart from that, the traveling waves of System (
1.1
) are closely related to pulled FKPP-waves with only a single type of particles
[
14
,
23
]
. The equation for such a wave
w(z)
reads
0=cw^{\prime}+w^{\prime\prime}+F(w)
. For the purpose of a simple comparison, we let
F(w)=aw-w^{2}
, where
a>0
is the branching rate of the particles. In this case, Theorem
1.1
states that the convergence of System (
1.1
) as
z\rightarrow+\infty
is identical to that of
w
, if
a=1-i_{+\infty}
, see e.g.
[
36
]
. In words, the asymptotic growth speed of traveling waves of System (
1.1
) coincides with that of simple FKPP-waves in presence of a constant density
i_{+\infty}
of inhibiting particles. Moreover, Theorem
1.1
implies that
i_{c}=0
for all
c\geq 2
. Thus, the minimal speed of an invasive front, where
i_{+\infty}=0
, is given by
c_{\min}=2
. Again, this coincides with the minimal wave speed of the associated FKPP-equation,
i.e.
in the absence of inactive particles. It is this critical front which can be interpreted as the most natural one, our simulations indicate that it always arises under compact initial data. If we assume convergence, a technique of Berestycki, Brunet & Derrida
[
5
]
yields an upper bound for the speed of the traveling front, just by ignoring the dampening influence of the inactive particles. For compact initial data, the system always chooses the smallest possible wave speed, as suggested.

The emergence of traveling fronts is known for many reaction-diffusion systems. We suggest the
literature
[
8
,
37
,
28
]
for more examples with a biological motivation. Rigorous proofs of these phenomena are rare. Typically, only the form of the traveling waves is analyzed analytically. The FKPP-equation is one of the cases, where the convergence of the front of the PDE towards a traveling wave solution can be proved. The first rigorous proof was done by Kolmogorov, Petrovsky & Piscunov in 1937
[
23
]
. Extensions of this result to more general initial data and a more precise description of the speed of the front have been provided by Uchiyama
[
36
]
and M. Bramson
[
7
]
. The approach of Kolmogorov
et al.
and Uchiyama seems to be restricted to systems with only a single type of particles, as it relies on a maximum principle and monotonicity of the front. The approach of Bramson relies on a relationship between the FKPP-equation and branched Brownian motion, which can not be applied in the present case since the inactive particles do not diffuse. A singular perturbation of System (
1.1
) which introduces a small diffusion to the inactive particles will be subject to future investigations. This would also rule out some difficulties when analyzing the stability of the traveling waves against perturbations, discussed in the next section.

9.2
Stability of the traveling waves

We give a brief introduction to stability of traveling waves against small perturbations, in the spirit of the introduction in
[
16
]
. A good overview, where the following concepts are presented in depth, has been written by Sandstede
[
32
]
.

Consider a reaction-diffusion system

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=D\cdot Y_{xx}+R(y),

(9.1)

where
Y\in\mathds{R}^{n},x\in\mathds{R},t\geq 0,D=\text{diag}(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
with
d_{i}\geq 0
, and
R
a smooth reaction. In the moving frame
z=x-ct
, the System reads

\displaystyle Y_{t}

\displaystyle=D\cdot Y_{zz}+cY_{z}+R(y).

(9.2)

A traveling wave
w(z)
with speed
c
is a constant solution of Eq. (
9.2
). The wave
w
is called
non-linearly stable
in a space
\mathcal{X}
, if any solution of the PDE (
9.2
) which starts in
Y_{0}=w+\tilde{Y}
, where
\tilde{Y}\in\mathcal{X}
is a sufficiently small perturbation, converges to a shift of
w
. This type of stability is often encoded in the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{L}
, that is obtained by linearizing the equation for the perturbation
\tilde{Y}
in (
9.2
) around to the constant part
w
:

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{t}=D\cdot\tilde{Y}_{zz}+c\tilde{Y}_{z}+JR(w)\cdot\tilde{Y}:=L\tilde{Y},

(9.3)

where
JR
is the Jacobian of the reaction
R
. Let
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
be the operator given by
\tilde{Y}\rightarrow L\tilde{Y}
. We say that the wave
w
is
spectrally stable
in
\mathcal{X}
if the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
is contained in the half-plane
\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)<0
, except maybe a simple a simple eigenvalue at
0
(that corresponds to the traveling wave itself, if
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
). For diffusive systems, a quite general theory has been developed. If
\mathcal{X}
is appropriately chosen, spectral stability implies non-linear stability, we refer to the literature
[
32
,
16
]
. Classical results are e.g. given for subspaces of
\mathcal{X}=H^{1}
, the
L^{2}
-Sobolev space.

To cut a long story short, we are not aware of any rigorous framework for studying the non-linear stability of System (
1.1
). Two problems arise, that so far have been treated only separately
[
16
,
22
]
.

Most importantly, the traveling waves of System (
1.1
) can not be stable against perturbations in the classical sense, since the inactive particles neither react nor diffuse. Any initial deviation remains for all times, as shown in Figure
1
. However, the actual front of the system does converge to a traveling wave. For capturing this idea, we introduce the weighted space
\mathcal{X}=H^{1}_{\alpha}
with norm
||f||_{H^{1}_{\alpha}}=||f\cdot e^{\alpha z}||_{H^{1}}
for some
\alpha>0
. Non-linear stability in
H_{1}^{\alpha}
is referred to as
convective stability
. Convergence of the PDE in the moving frame (
9.2
) in
H^{1}_{\alpha}
means that the front of the system approaches the traveling wave, whereas any initial finite and local deviation is convected towards
z=-\infty
and vanishes due to the weighting. A first rigorous result regarding convective stability was obtained by Ghazaryan
et al.

[
16
]
. They could show that spectral stability in
H^{1}_{\alpha}
implies convective stability against small perturbations in
H^{1}_{\alpha}\cap H^{1}
. For their approach, the authors require that the weight
\alpha
can be chosen such that all eigenvalues
\gamma
of
\mathcal{L}
except zero fulfill
\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)\leq\nu<0
and such that the derivative
w^{\prime}\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
of the traveling wave is an eigenfunction that corresponds to a simple eigenvalue at zero. Unfortunately, this setting is not suited for studying pulled FKPP-fronts: the assumption
w^{\prime}\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
implies that the continuous spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
touches the origin, see e.g. chapter 6 in the work of Sattinger
[
33
]
.

Another difficulty arises when studying critical pulled fronts (with minimal possible speed) whose tail as
z\rightarrow+\infty
converges sub-exponentially, as in Theorem
1.1
. In this case, the requirement
w^{\prime}\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
is only fulfilled for rather small values of
\alpha
, which do not suffice for shifting the continuous spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
to the left half-plane. For diffusive systems, this more delicate case was first treated by Kirchgässner
[
22
]
, a recent overview is given in
[
13
]
. In contrast to non-critical waves, the type of convergence of the system to the critical traveling wave is not exponential, but algebraic. For the convergence of the FKPP-equation with compact initial data to its critical traveling wave, this is known since the pioneering work of Kolmogorov
et al.

[
23
]
. After introducing a small diffusion to the inactive particles, we could use the result of Kirchgässner, this seems like the next natural step.

For the most natural traveling wave solution, the critical one with speed
c=2
and
i_{+\infty}=i_{c}=0
, we performed a numerical analysis that strongly indicates that this wave is spectrally stable in
H^{1}_{\alpha}
, when we choose
\alpha=-\mu_{+\infty}=c/2
. The details are presented in Appendix
B
. Thus, based on the work of Ghazaryan
et al.
regarding convective stability
[
16
]
and the work of Kirchgässner regarding critical fronts
[
22
]
, we dare to make an educated guess: we expect that this traveling wave is convectively stable against small perturbations in
H^{1}_{c/2}\cap H^{1}
, with algebraic speed of convergence.

(Asymptotic behavior)
.

Let

and

, and let

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u^{\prime}\\

\lambda_{+}\,v\\
\lambda_{-}\,w\end{pmatrix},

where

are the coordinates in the system of eigenvectors

of the matrix

Proof.

In the normal form from Lemma

, the center manifold can be calculated as a function

with components

, we can estimate the error of the approximation
(HT)(u)
by Theorem
A.6
. Inserting the normal form from Lemma

results in

\displaystyle(HT)(u)=

\displaystyle-DT(u)\cdot P\big(u,T(u)\big)\cdot\frac{1}{c}(1+\frac{K-r}{1-K})-\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{+}\cdot T_{v}(u)\\
\lambda_{-}\cdot T_{w}(u)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle-P\big(u,T(u)\big)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{\lambda_{+}}{2\Delta(1-K)}\\
\frac{\lambda_{-}}{2\Delta(1-K)}\end{pmatrix}.

around the fixed point iteratively, by choosing better and better approximating polynomials

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(u^{3})-\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{+}\,\alpha u^{2}\\

Appendix B
Numerical evaluation of the spectrum of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{L}}

As announced in our discussion in Section
9
, we analyze the spectral stabilty of the critical traveling wave. The theoretical background is presented in
[
32
,
16
]
, the details about the computational approach are presented by Barker
et al.

[
2
]
, we only describe the application in the present setting.

Here and from now on,
c=2
and we denote as
a(z),i(z)
the critical traveling wave with speed
c=2
and
i_{+\infty}=0
. We denote the exponent of the weight-function as
\alpha>0
and analyze the spectral stability of the critical traveling wave in the weighted
L^{2}
-Sobolev space
H^{1}_{\alpha}
, with norm
||f||_{H^{1}_{\alpha}}=||f\cdot e^{\alpha z}||_{H^{1}}
.

We linearize the PDE around
a(z),i(z)
and analyze the non-negative spectrum of the resulting linear operator
\mathcal{L}
, as defined in (
9.3
). For System (
1.1
), this operator
\mathcal{L}:H^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathds{R})\times H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathds{R})\rightarrow H^{2}_{\alpha}(\mathds{R})\times H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathds{R})
acts on a pair of functions
u\in H^{2}_{\alpha},v\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
, which correspond to perturbations of
a
and
i
, respectively:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} u&\mapsto u^{\prime\prime}+cu^{\prime}+u(1-(2a+i))-va,\\
v&\mapsto cv^{\prime}+u(2a+i+r)+va.\end{aligned}

(B.1)

As will see later, we only need to consider the point-spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
. Thus, for
\gamma\in\mathds{C}
with
\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)\geq 0
, we look for a function
U\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
that solves
\mathcal{L}U=\gamma\cdot U
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}
is equivalent to a first-order operator
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}:H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathds{R}^{3})\rightarrow H^{1}_{\alpha}(\mathds{R}^{3})
, when we introduce an auxiliary variable for
u^{\prime}
. We will omit the tilde. Now,
\gamma\in\mathds{C}
lies in the point spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
if and only if there exists a function
U:\mathds{R}\rightarrow\mathds{C}^{3},U\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
, which solves

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dz}U=M(z,\gamma)\cdot U,

\displaystyle M(z,\gamma):=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
\gamma+2a(z)+i(z)-1&-c&a(z)\\
-\frac{2a(z)+i(z)+r}{c}&0&\frac{\gamma-a(z)}{c}\end{pmatrix}.

(B.2)

It can easily be seen that the matrix
M(+\infty,\gamma)
has eigenvalues

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{c},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\beta_{2}=-\frac{c}{2}+\sqrt{\gamma},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\beta_{3}=-\frac{c}{2}-\sqrt{\gamma},

(B.3)

and the matrix
M(-\infty,\gamma)
has eigenvalues

\displaystyle\beta_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{c},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\beta_{2}=-\frac{c}{2}+\sqrt{2+\gamma},\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle\beta_{3}=-\frac{c}{2}-\sqrt{2+\gamma}.

(B.4)

If
U\in H^{1}_{\alpha}
, then
W(z):=U(z)\cdot e^{\alpha z}
is bounded and vanishes. The function
W(z)
fulfills

\displaystyle W^{\prime}(z)=\big(M(z,\gamma)+\alpha\cdot\mathds{1}\big)\cdot W(z).

(B.5)

Remark that the matrix
M+\alpha\mathds{1}
has the same eigenvectors as
M
, and that its eigenvalues are shifted by
\alpha
when compared to
M
. If
M(\pm\infty)+\alpha\mathds{1}
has no eigenvalues with zero real-part, the theory of
exponential dichotomies
implies that any bounded solution
W(z)
must vanish exponentially fast as
z\rightarrow\pm\infty
, and that it asymptotically approaches the unstable (resp. stable) manifold of the constant matrix
M(-\infty,\gamma)
as
z\rightarrow-\infty
(resp.
M(+\infty,\gamma)
as
z\rightarrow+\infty
)
[
32
]
. Therefore, a bounded solution
W
exists if and only if the trajectories that emerge from these manifolds intersect. This allows us to compute the Evans-function: it is a determinant that evaluates to zero if and only if the solutions that decay at
-\infty
and those that decay at
+\infty
are somehow linearly dependent, and thus intersect.

We investigate the case
\alpha=\frac{c}{2}
, which is equal to the rate of convergence of the wave as
z\rightarrow+\infty
, up to a sub-exponential term, see Theorem
1.1
. For
\alpha=\frac{c}{2}
, then within the region
\{\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)\geq 0,\gamma\neq 0\}
the following holds: the dimension of the unstable space of
M(-\infty,\gamma)+\frac{c}{2}\cdot\mathds{1}
is given by
k_{-}=2
, and the dimension of the stable space of
M(+\infty,\gamma)+\frac{c}{2}\cdot\mathds{1}
is given by
k_{+}=1
. This can easily be deduced from the corresponding Eigenvalues (
B.3
), (
B.4
), which do not cross the imaginary axis. The values
k_{-}
and
k_{+}
add up to the dimension of the ODE (
B.2
). We say that
\{\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)\geq 0,\gamma\neq 0\}
is contained in the
region of consistent splitting
. This implies that the non-negative part of the spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
is contained in the point spectrum of the operator, which is a standard result
[
32
,
33
]
. Within the region of consistent splitting, we can define the Evans-function
E(\gamma)
.

Given
\gamma
with
\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)\geq 0,\gamma\neq 0
, we let
X(z)
be the unique solution of Eq. (
B.5
) that vanishes at
z=+\infty
, and let
Y_{1}(z),Y_{2}(z)
span the two-dimensional space of solutions of Eq. (
B.5
) that vanish at
z=-\infty
. The Evans-function is defined as

\displaystyle E(\gamma):=\text{det}\big(Y_{1}(z)\big|Y_{2}(z)\big|X(z)\big)\Big|_{z=0}.

(B.6)

:

It holds that

if and only if
\gamma
lies in the point spectrum of
\mathcal{L}
. Moreover,

is analytic if

are chosen such that they are analytic in

. Thus it suffices to calculate

along the boundary of a domain: the winding number along this contour then corresponds to the number of zeros inside the domain. We use this to verify that there are no zeros of

within the set

\displaystyle S:=\Big\{\gamma\in\mathds{C}\,\Big|\,\mathfrak{Re}(\gamma)\geq 0,\,10^{-3}\leq|\gamma|\leq 1000\Big\},

where we keep a small distance from the origin for numerical reasons and hope that there are no unexpectedly large eigenvalues. It may be possible to generalize the approach in

Appendix C
Rescaling the general system

Let
r_{S},r_{A},D>0
and
r_{I}\geq 0
, and consider the reaction-diffusion system

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=D\cdot A_{xx}+r_{A}A-r_{S}A(A+I),

(C.1)

\displaystyle I_{t}

\displaystyle=r_{I}A+r_{S}A(A+I),

which is the general form of System (
1.1
). There exists a linear one-to-one correspondence to the normalized form. Therefore, we rescale time and space,
s:=r_{A}\cdot t,\,y:=\sqrt{D/r_{A}}\cdot x
, and also the densities of the particles,
\bar{A}:=A\cdot r_{S}/r_{A},\,\bar{I}:=I\cdot r_{S}/r_{A}
. The rescaled dynamics of System (
C.1
) follow

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{s}

\displaystyle=\bar{A}_{yy}+\bar{A}-\bar{A}(\bar{A}+\bar{I}),

(C.2)

\displaystyle\bar{I}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{I}}{r_{A}}\bar{I}+\bar{A}(\bar{A}+\bar{I}),

which is equivalent to System (
1.1
) with
r=\frac{r_{I}}{r_{A}}
. In view of this, we can easily formulate a parameter-dependent version of Theorem
1.1
:

Theorem C.1
.

Let
r_{S},r_{A},D>0
and
r_{I}\geq 0
, and consider the System (
C.1
) and a wave-speed
c>0
. Set

\displaystyle i_{c}:=\max\big\{0,\frac{1}{r_{S}}\big(r_{A}-\frac{c^{2}}{4D}\big)\big\}.

(C.3)

For each pair
i_{-\infty},i_{+\infty}\in\mathds{R}^{+}
such that

\displaystyle i_{+\infty}\in[i_{c},\frac{r_{A}}{r_{S}}),\qquad i_{-\infty}=\frac{2\cdot r_{A}}{r_{S}}-i_{+\infty},

(C.4)

there exists a unique bounded and positive traveling wave
a,i
with speed
c
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow\pm\infty}

\displaystyle a(z)=0,\qquad\lim_{z\rightarrow\pm\infty}i(z)=i_{\pm\infty}.

(C.5)

If
\frac{c^{2}}{4D}+r_{S}\cdot i_{+\infty}-r_{A}=0
, then convergence as
z\rightarrow+\infty
is sub-exponentially fast and of order
z\cdot e^{-\frac{c}{2D}z}
. If
\frac{c^{2}}{4D}+r_{S}\cdot i_{+\infty}-r_{A}>0
, then convergence as
z\rightarrow+\infty
is exponentially fast. Convergence as
z\rightarrow-\infty
is exponentially fast in all cases. The corresponding rates are

\displaystyle\mu_{\pm\infty}=-\frac{c}{2D}+\sqrt{\frac{c^{2}}{4D^{2}}+\frac{r_{S}\cdot i_{\pm\infty}-r_{A}}{D}}.

(C.6)

Moreover, these are all bounded, non-negative, non-constant and twice differentiable solutions of Eq. (
1.2
).

In particular, for an invading front where
i\rightarrow 0
as
z\rightarrow+\infty
, the remaining density of particles at the back of the wave is given by
i_{-\infty}=2\cdot\frac{r_{A}}{r_{I}}
.
