Numerical analysis of a wave equation for lossy media obeying a frequency power law
Katherine Baker, Lehel Banjai

Abstract
We study a wave equation with a nonlocal time fractional damping term that models the effects of acoustic attenuation characterized by a frequency dependence power law. First we prove existence of a unique solution to this equation with particular attention paid to the handling of the fractional derivative. Then we derive an explicit time stepping scheme based on the finite element method in space and a combination of convolution quadrature and second order central differences in time. We conduct a full error analysis of the mixed time discretization and in turn the fully space time discretized scheme. Error estimates are given for both smooth solutions and solutions with a singularity at t = 0 t=0 of a type that is typical for equations involving fractional time-derivatives. A number of numerical results are presented to support the error analysis. Keywords: fractional calculus; wave equation; convergence; existence and uniqueness.

Introduction

We are interested in the initial boundary problem given by a wave equation with the addition of a time fractional damping term on a bounded Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with boundary
\partial\Omega

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c^{2}}\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta u+\frac{a_{\gamma}}{c}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u

\displaystyle=f,

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times[0,T]

(1)

\displaystyle u(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=u_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(\cdot,0)

\displaystyle=v_{0}

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\Omega\times[0,T],

Here
\gamma\in(-1,1)\setminus\{0\}
,
c(x)>0
is the wave speed, and

a_{\gamma}=-\alpha_{0}\frac{4}{\pi}\Gamma(-\gamma-1)\Gamma(\gamma+2)\cos((\gamma+1)\pi/2),

(2)

for some constant
\alpha_{0}>0
.
Note that
a_{\gamma}>0
for
\gamma\in(-1,1)
and that

. In this problem

denotes the Caputo fractional derivative; see Definition

The interest in this problem stems from the modelling of acoustic attenuation that occurs as a wave propagates through lossy media
[
30
, Chapter 4]
.
In applications, this is applied to modelling high intensity focused ultrasound therapy (HIFU) where the lossy media is biological tissue. HIFU is a noninvasive and nonionising medical treatment that focuses multiple high intensity acoustic pressure waves on a region, ablating it away
[
31
]
. It is known that acoustic attenuation obeys a frequency dependence characterized by the following power law,

S(\overrightarrow{x}+\Delta\overrightarrow{x})=S(\overrightarrow{x})e^{-\alpha(\omega)|\overrightarrow{x}|}

(3)

where

is the amplitude,
\Delta\overrightarrow{x}
is the wave propagation distance,
\omega
denotes frequency and
\alpha(\omega)
the attenuation coefficient which defined by

\alpha(\omega)=\alpha_{0}|\omega|^{\gamma}

for
\gamma
the frequency power exponent and
\alpha_{0}
a constant relating to the media
[
8
]
. Values of
\gamma
have been determined by many experiments and field measurements, their results conclude that for most media
\gamma\in(0,2)

[
11
]
. In this paper we restrict our attention to
\gamma\in(-1,1)
. For the range
\gamma\in(1,2)
(and potentially also
(0,1)
) we prefer changing the weak damping term
\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}
with the strong damping of the form
(-\Delta)\partial_{t}^{\gamma-1}

[
7
,
13
,
14
,
34
]
. The techniques developed in this paper, can be used to analyze the strongly damped case for the full range
\gamma\in(0,2)
; see
[
1
]
. Another model that we can consider is the nonlinear fractional Westervelt equation
[
13
]
, this contains the strong damping term mentioned above and describes the nonlinear behaviour that the high intensity focusing causes during HIFU. Similar models appear also in fractional order viscoelasticity, similar to both the strong damping case (for
\gamma\in(0,1)
)
[
15
,
25
]
and the weak damping case
[
24
]
.

[
29
]
derived a wave equation with a convolution type operator to incorporate the effects of acoustic attenuation and showed that it adheres to the frequency dependence power law (
3
).
[
8
]
praise this model for its simplicity, due to it only containing two parameters, but criticise it for being difficult to implement initial conditions. To overcome this they refined the model to include the Caputo fractional derivative as the damping term. Furthermore within their paper they show that even with this modification the solutions still obey the power law.

Issues that arise within this model largely stem from the convolution based fractional derivative that requires us to store the full history when numerically solving with a time stepping scheme, making it expensive to compute, particularly in 3D. In the literature this is countered by replacing the fractional time derivative with a fractional Laplacian
[
9
,
32
]
. However, using efficient quadrature methods
[
3
]
will also be sufficient in reducing memory requirements and allows us to remain using the simpler operator.

The paper is structured in the following way. Section
2
begins by outlining necessary definitions and lemmas required to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1

3
we develop a numerical scheme to approximate solutions of (
1

We first give the definition of the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral.

Definition 2.1
.

For
\beta>0
the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral of a function
f\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
is defined by

I_{t}^{\beta}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta-1}f(\tau)d\tau,\qquad t>0,

where
\Gamma
denotes the Gamma function.

The fractional integral
I_{t}^{\beta}f(t)
exists almost everywhere if
f\in L^{1}(0,T)
, see
[
26
,
10
]
, but we will not make use of this reduced smoothness requirement except when
f(t)=t^{\mu}
where for
\mu>-1
we have that

I_{t}^{\beta}t^{\mu}=\frac{\Gamma(\mu+1)}{\Gamma(\mu+\beta+1)}t^{\beta+\mu}.

(4)

The Caputo fractional derivative is obtained by applying the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral to a classical derivative of the function. In the following definition,
\lceil\gamma\rceil
denotes the ceiling of
\gamma
.

Definition 2.2
.

For
\gamma>-1
and
n=\lceil\gamma\rceil
the left-sided Caputo fractional derivative of a function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{(n)}[0,T]
is defined by

\partial_{t}^{\gamma}f(t):=\begin{cases}I_{t}^{-\gamma}f(t)&\gamma<0\\
\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{n-\gamma-1}f^{(n)}(\tau)\,d\tau=I_{t}^{n-\gamma}f^{(n)}(t)&\gamma\notin\mathbb{N},\,\gamma>0\\
\frac{d^{n}}{dt^{n}}f(t)&\gamma\in\mathbb{N},\end{cases}

for
t>0
.

Again, an explicit formula can be given in case where
f(t)=t^{\mu}
for
\mu>0
we have that

\partial_{t}^{\gamma}t^{\mu}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\gamma>\mu,\;\mu\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\\
\frac{\Gamma(\mu+1)}{\Gamma(\mu+1-\gamma)}t^{\mu-\gamma}&\gamma\leq\mu.\end{array}\right.

(5)

Lemma 2.1
.

(a)

For
\gamma>0
and any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}[0,T]
where
n=\lceil\gamma\rceil

\partial_{t}^{\gamma}f(t)=\partial_{t}^{\gamma-1}\partial_{t}f(t),\quad t\in[0,T].

(b)

For
\gamma>0
and any
f\in\mathcal{C}^{n}[0,T]
such that
f^{(n-1)}(0)=0
,
n=\lceil\gamma\rceil
,

\partial_{t}^{\gamma}f(t)=\partial_{t}\partial_{t}^{\gamma-1}f(t),\quad t\in[0,T].

(c)

The semi group property holds for fractional integrals: for any
f\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
and
\alpha,\beta\geq 0

I_{t}^{\alpha}I_{t}^{\beta}f(t)=I_{t}^{\alpha+\beta}f(t),\quad t\in[0,T].

From the definition of the fractional derivative we see that the model (
1
) is a Volterra integro-differential equation. To proceed with the analysis we will need a result on the corresponding ordinary differential equation.

Theorem 2.2
.

For
f\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
,
\gamma\in(-1,1)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
a_{\gamma}>0
, and
u_{0},\,v_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
there exists a unique solution to the following initial value problem: Find
u\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,T]
such that

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}+\lambda u+a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u=f

\displaystyle\quad\text{in }[0,T]

\displaystyle u(0)=

\displaystyle u_{0}

(6)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(0)=

\displaystyle v_{0}.

Furthermore, for
\gamma\in(-1,0)

u^{\prime\prime}(t)=f(t)-\lambda u_{0}-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}t^{-\gamma}v_{0}+O(t),

and for
\gamma\in(0,1)

u^{\prime\prime}(t)=f(t)-\lambda u_{0}-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}(f(0)-\lambda u_{0})t^{1-\gamma}+o(t^{1-\gamma})

as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

Proof.

For
\gamma=0
we omit the proof since in this case the result follows from standard ODE theory.

Using the definition of the Caputo derivative in (
6
) gives

u^{\prime\prime}+\lambda u+\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(n-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{n-\gamma-1}u^{(n+1)}(\tau)\,d\tau=f,

(7)

where
n=\lceil\gamma\rceil
.
Next, define
v=u^{\prime\prime}
, i.e.,

u(t)=u_{0}+tv_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)v(\tau)\,d\tau.

(8)

We first consider the case
\gamma\in(0,1)
, i.e.,
n=1
. By substituting
v
into (
7
) we obtain a Volterra integral equation for
v
:

v(t)=f-\lambda u_{0}-\lambda tv_{0}-\lambda\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)v(\tau)\,d\tau-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\gamma}v(\tau)\,d\tau.

This Volterra equation for
v
can be written in the form investigated
in
[
6
, Section 6.1.2]
, where the existence of a unique solution
v\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
is proved; see Theorem
A.2
in the appendix. The uniqueness of
u
follows by the smoothness assumption
u\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,T]
and the initial data requirements. Hence
u
defined by (
8
) is in
\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,T]
and solves the original equation. The asymptotic behaviour of
u^{\prime\prime}
follows from Theorem
A.2
, giving

\begin{split}u^{\prime\prime}(t)&=f(t)-\lambda u_{0}-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}v(0)t^{1-\gamma}+o(t^{1-\gamma})\\
&=f(t)-\lambda u_{0}-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}(f(0)-\lambda u_{0})t^{1-\gamma}+o(t^{1-\gamma}).\end{split}

If
\gamma\in(-1,0)
, the equation is given by

u^{\prime\prime}+\lambda u+\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\gamma-1}u^{\prime}(\tau)\,d\tau=f.

(9)

Again we wish to let
v=u^{\prime\prime}
but to do so directly we must write the integral in the above equation as

\frac{1}{\Gamma(-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\gamma-1}u^{\prime}(\tau)\,d\tau=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}t^{-\gamma}v_{0}+\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\gamma}v(\tau)\,d\tau.

Now we substitute the above equation and (
8
) into the ODE (
9
) and we have,

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=f-\lambda u_{0}-\lambda tv_{0}-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}t^{-\gamma}v_{0}-\int_{0}^{t}\lambda(t-\tau)v(\tau)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\gamma}v(\tau)\,d\tau.

This is now a Volterra integral equation with a continuous kernel. Hence, see
[
6
, Theorem 2.1.5]
and Theorem
A.2
in the appendix, a unique solution
v\in\mathcal{C}[0,T]
exists and consequently also the solution
u\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,T]
of the original equation. The asymptotic behaviour of
u^{\prime\prime}
again follows from Theorem
A.2
.
∎

Next we show that the time-fractional term satisfies a positivity result that will in turn imply its damping properties.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
,
g\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and let
\gamma\in(0,1)
. Then the following hold:

(a)

\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}I_{t}^{\gamma}g(t),g(t))dt\geq\frac{(T/2)^{-1+\gamma}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{T}\|g(t)\|^{2}dt.

(b)

\int_{0}^{T}(I_{t}^{\gamma}f(t),f(t))dt\geq\frac{(T/2)^{-\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}\int_{0}^{T}\|I_{t}^{\gamma}f(t)\|^{2}dt.

Proof.

The proof of an analogue of (
a
) for Caputo fractional derivatives is given in
[
4
]
. The result can also be deduced from Lemma 1.7.2 in
[
33
]
and Lemma 3.1 in
[

]
, which for our case gives

\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}I^{\gamma}g(t),g(t))\,dt\geq\frac{1}{2\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{T}\left((T-t)^{\gamma-1}+t^{\gamma-1}\right)\|g(t)\|^{2}\,dt.

Minimizing the kernel gives the required result

\int_{0}^{T}(\partial_{t}I_{t}^{\gamma}g(t),g(t))dt\geq\frac{(T/2)^{\gamma-1}}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{T}\|g(\tau)\|^{2}d\tau.

(10)

The second part of the lemma follows by using the semigroup property, see Lemma
2.1
(
c
),

\int_{0}^{T}(I_{t}^{\gamma}f(t),f(t))dt=\int_{0}^{T}(I_{t}^{\gamma}f(t),\partial_{t}I_{t}^{1-\gamma}I_{t}^{\gamma}f(t))dt

and then applying part (
a
).
∎

A numerical test indicates that

for all

Next we investigate the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the weak formulation of (
1
): Find
u(t)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

(\partial_{t}^{2}u,v)+(\nabla u,\nabla v)+(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u,v)=(f,v),\qquad\text{for all }v\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

(12)

Theorem 2.4
.

Given
u_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
v_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
and
\gamma\in(-1,0)
a unique weak solution
u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))
of (
12
) exists and we have that
\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
,
\partial_{t}^{2}u\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))
and
\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.

Proof.

We follow the standard proof for the wave equation as described, e.g., in
[
12
,
18
]
, with modifications required due to the fractional damping term.

Let
\{\omega_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
be an orthogonal basis of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
with corresponding eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
. We look for
u_{m}
of the form

u_{m}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{m}^{k}(t)\omega_{k}

satisfying

\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u_{m},\omega_{k}\right)+\left(\nabla u_{m},\nabla\omega_{k}\right)+\left(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u_{m},\omega_{k}\right)=\left(f,\omega_{k}\right),

(13)

and

d_{m}^{k}(0)=\left(u_{0},\omega_{k}\right)\;\text{ and }\;\frac{d}{dt}d_{m}^{k}(0)=\left(v_{0},\omega_{k}\right),

(14)

for
k=1,2,\dots,m
. As this problem is equivalent to

\partial_{t}^{2}{d_{m}^{k}}(t)+\lambda_{k}d_{m}^{k}(t)+a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}d_{m}^{k}(t)=(f,\omega_{k}),

Theorem
2.2
shows that a unique solution
d_{m}^{k}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}[0,T]
exists for all
k\leq m
.

Testing (
13
) with
\partial_{t}u_{m}
, integrating in time, and using Lemma
2.3
b
we obtain

E(T;u_{m})\leq E(0;u_{m})+\int_{0}^{T}(f,\partial_{t}u_{m})\,dt,

where the energy is given by
E(t;v)=\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}v\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla v\|^{2}
. Applying Cauchy Schwarz inequality, the definition of the energy and the Gronwall inequality in the usual way we obtain that the energy is bounded independently of
m

E(T;u_{m})\leq C\left(\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}+\|v_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\|f\|^{2}\,dt\right)

(15)

for a constant
C=C(T)
.

In the standard way, see
[
12
]
, we obtain a bound on
\partial_{t}^{2}u_{m}

\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\partial_{t}^{2}u_{m}\right\|_{-1}^{2}\,dt\leq C\left(\|\nabla u_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\|f\|^{2}+\left\|\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u_{m}\right\|^{2}\,dt\right).

(16)

It remains to bound the term containing the fractional derivative.

Recalling that
\gamma+1\in(0,1)
, then using the definition of the Caputo derivative, see Definition
2.2
, Young’s convolution inequality
[
5
]
and (
15
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\partial_{t}^{\gamma}\partial_{t}u_{m}\right\|^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}t^{-\gamma-1}dt\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\partial_{t}u_{m}\right\|^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\partial_{t}u_{m}\right\|^{2}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\left\|\nabla u_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|v_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\right).

(17)

Returning to (
16
) we obtain the bound

\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\partial_{t}^{2}u_{m}\right\|_{-1}^{2}\leq C\left(\left\|\nabla u_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|v_{0}\right\|^{2}+\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}^{2}\right).

Hence
u_{m}
has a subsequence that converges weakly to a
u
in the following spaces

\begin{split}u&\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\right),\;\partial_{t}u\in L^{\infty}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right),\;\\
\partial_{t}^{2}u&\in L^{2}\left(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega)\right),\;\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u\in L^{2}\left(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega)\right).\end{split}

Further, in the usual way
u
is a weak solution of the original problem (

).

Finally we will show that
u
is a unique weak solution. To do so we let
f=0
,
u_{0}=0
and
v_{0}=0
and show that the weak solution is

.
For a fixed

, let

v(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\int_{t}^{s}u(\tau)\,d\tau&0\leq t\leq s\\
0&s<t\leq T\end{array}\right..

Testing (
1
) with
v
we obtain

0=\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u,v\right)+\left(\nabla u,\nabla v\right)+\left(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u,v\right).

Then integrating with respect to time,

0=\int_{0}^{s}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u,v\right)+\left(\nabla u,\nabla v\right)+\left(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u,v\right)\,dt=-\int_{0}^{s}\left(\partial_{t}u,\partial_{t}v\right)-\left(\nabla u,\nabla v\right)+\left(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma}u,\partial_{t}v\right)\,dt

where we used Lemma

b
. Now by the definition of

,

\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\|u\|^{2}\,dt-\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\|\nabla v\|^{2}\,dt=-\int_{0}^{s}\left(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma}u,u\right)\,dt

and thus

\frac{1}{2}\|u(s)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla v(0)\|^{2}=-\int_{0}^{s}\left(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma}u,u\right)\,dt\leq 0

implying
u\equiv 0
as

.

The only difficulty in extending the proof of Theorem

is the bound on
\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}
in (

). To circumvent this problem we note that due to the additional smoothness assumption on

\partial_{t}^{3}u_{m}-\Delta\partial_{t}u_{m}+a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u_{m}=\partial_{t}f.

Now testing with

E(t;\partial_{t}u_{m})\leq C\left(E(0;\partial_{t}u_{m})+\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}f\|^{2}\,dt\right).

As

\begin{split}E(0;\partial_{t}u_{m})&=\frac{1}{2}\|\partial_{t}^{2}u(0)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\partial_{t}u(0)\|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\|\Delta u_{0}+f(0)\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla v_{0}\|^{2}\end{split}

we have obtained a bound on
\partial_{t}^{2}u
and consequently using again Young’s inequality

\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u\|^{2}dt\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}^{2}u\|^{2}dt\leq C\left(\|\Delta u_{0}\|^{2}+\|f(0)\|^{2}+\|\nabla v_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{t}\|\partial_{t}f\|^{2}\,dt\right).

Using this bound in the proof of Theorem
2.4
we obtain the result.

∎

Remark 2
.

The fractional Zener wave equation investigated in
[
24
]
is of a similar form to the Szabo equation with
\gamma\in(0,1)
; see equation (2.5) in
[
24
]
. With similar approach to ours, the authors prove uniqueness and existence of the Zener model. The solution in
[
24
]
is understood in a weaker sense, namely lower regularity of the data is required but bounds on the second derivative in time of the solution are not given.

Remark 3
.

As is usual in PDEs with time fractional derivatives, we expect a singularity at
t=0
even for smooth data. Namely, due to Theorem
2.2
we expect for smooth
f
and
\gamma\in(0,1)

u(x,t)=u_{0}(x)+tv_{0}(x)+t^{2}w_{0}(x)+t^{3-\gamma}z_{0}(x)+o(t^{3-\gamma}).

(18)

The right hand side is then

\begin{split}f(t)&=\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta u+a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u\\
&=2w_{0}+(3-\gamma)(2-\gamma)t^{1-\gamma}z_{0}-\Delta u_{0}+\frac{2a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}t^{1-\gamma}w_{0}+o(t^{1-\gamma})\end{split}

If
f
is to be smooth, we need to match the term
t^{1-\gamma}
by setting
z_{0}=-\frac{2a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(4-\gamma)}w_{0}
.

On the other hand if
\gamma\in(-1,0)
we have

u(x,t)=u_{0}(x)+tv_{0}(x)+t^{2}w_{0}(x)+t^{2-\gamma}z_{0}(x)+o(t^{2-\gamma}).

The right hand side is then

f(t)=2w_{0}+(2-\gamma)(1-\gamma)t^{-\gamma}z_{0}-\Delta u_{0}+\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}t^{-\gamma}v_{0}+o(t^{-\gamma~}).

If
f
is to be smooth, we need to match the term
t^{-\gamma}
by setting
z_{0}=-\frac{a_{\gamma}}{\Gamma(3-\gamma)}v_{0}
.

Alternatively, for
u
to be smooth we would need
f
to have a singularity of the type
t^{1-\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(0,1)
and
t^{-\gamma}
for
\gamma\in(-1,0)
with further weaker singularities at
t=0
.

3
Fully discrete system

To obtain the fully discrete system we will use a finite element method in space and a combination of leapfrog and BDF2 based convolution quadrature discretization in time. The motivation for using leapfrog is to obtain an explicit scheme, whereas the main motivation for using convolution quadrature are its excellent stability properties
[
20
,
19
]
and the ability to evaluate it very efficiently
[
3
,
27
]
. An alternative discretization of the fractional time derivative is the L1 scheme
[
28
,
17
]
. This scheme also has the required stability property, i.e., it preserves the positivity property of the fractional derivative; see
[
28
, Lemma 3.1]
. However, to the best of our knowledge correction terms for the L1 scheme are not available except for specific equations such as subdiffusion; see
[
35
]
.

3.1
Spatial semidiscretization

To discretize in space, we make use of a piecewise linear Galerkin finite element method. Namely let
V^{h}
be a family of finite dimensional subspaces of
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
parametrized by the meshwidth
h>0
. We assume that these spaces satisfy the following approximation property

\inf_{v^{h}\in V^{h}}\|v-v^{h}\|_{1}\leq Ch\|v\|_{2}

and

\inf_{v^{h}\in V^{h}}\|v-v^{h}\|\leq Ch^{2}\|v\|_{2}

for some constant
C>0
independent of
h
. Furthermore, we assume that an inverse inequality holds

\sup_{v\in V^{h}}\|\nabla v\|\leq C_{\text{inv}}h^{-1}\|v\|,

(19)

for some constant
C_{\text{inv}}
.

The semidiscrete problem then reads: Find
u^{h}(t)\in V^{h}
such that

(\partial_{t}^{2}u^{h},v)+(\nabla u^{h},\nabla v)+(a_{\gamma}\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u^{h},v)=(f,v),\qquad\text{for all }v\in V^{h}.

We denote projections of the initial data
u_{0}
and
v_{0}
onto
V^{h}
by
u^{h}_{0}
and
v^{h}_{0}
respectively; the projections will be specified later on.

3.2
Time discretization

In time we discretize using the explicit, leapfrog scheme

\tfrac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(u^{h}_{n+1}-2u^{h}_{n}+u^{h}_{n-1},v)+(\nabla u^{h}_{n},\nabla v)+(a_{\gamma}w^{h}_{n},v)=(f(t_{n}),v),

where
t_{n}=n{\kappa}
for some fixed time step
{\kappa}>0
,
u^{h}_{n}\in V^{h}
is an approximation of
u(t_{n})
and
w_{n}
is an approximation of
\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}u(t_{n})
which we describe next.

First of all we define the central difference operator

{\bar{\partial}}_{t}u^{h}(t_{n}):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}v^{h}_{0}&n=0\\
\frac{1}{2{\kappa}}(u^{h}_{n+1}-u^{h}_{n-1})&n\geq 1\end{array}\right..

We extend the definition also to continuous functions
g\in C[0,T]
with a given time derivative at
t=0
:

{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}g(0)&t=0\\
\frac{1}{2{\kappa}}(g(t+{\kappa})-g(t-{\kappa}))&t\geq{\kappa}\\
\frac{1}{{\kappa}}({\bar{\partial}}_{t}g({\kappa})-\partial_{t}g(0))t+\partial_{t}g(0)&t\in[0,{\kappa}]\end{array}\right.,

i.e.,
{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)
is exact at
t=0
, the central difference quotient for
t\geq{\kappa}
and is the linear interpolant of
{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g
for
t\in[0,{\kappa}]
.

It remains to discretize the fractional derivative
\partial_{t}^{\gamma}
. The formula should be computable efficiently and should retain the positivity property of the fractional derivative as described in Lemma
2.3
. All this is satisfied by convolution quadrature introduced by
[
19
]
, which we describe next.

Convolution quadrature (CQ) is based on an
A(\theta)
-stable linear multistep method. For a continuous function
g
, the CQ formula for
\partial_{t}^{\gamma}
is given by

\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}g(t_{n}):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n-j}(g(t_{j})-\chi_{\gamma}g(0))=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{j}(g(t_{n}-t_{j})-\chi_{\gamma}g(0)),

(20)

where

\chi_{\gamma}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}0&\gamma\in(-1,0]\\
1&\gamma\in(0,1)\end{array}\right.

and
\omega_{j}
are convolution weights defined below. The term
\chi_{\gamma}\omega_{n}g(0)
above is added to the standard definition of convolution quadrature in order to correct for the fact that we are using CQ to compute Caputo rather than Riemann-Liouville fractional derivatives.

Next, we define the convolution weights. As central differences are approximations of order 2, we restrict our discussion to second order, BDF2 based CQ. The corresponding convolution weights are then given by

\left(\frac{\delta(\zeta)}{{\kappa}}\right)^{\gamma}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\omega_{j}\zeta^{j},\quad\delta(\zeta)=\frac{3}{2}-2\zeta+\frac{1}{2}\zeta^{2}.

We can also define the approximation at intermediate values of
t
by

\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}g(t):=\sum_{j\geq 0;t_{j}\leq t}\omega_{j}(g(t-t_{j})-\chi_{\gamma}g(0))=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\omega_{j}(g(t-t_{j})-\chi_{\gamma}g(0)),

(21)

where we define
g(t)\equiv 0
for
t<0
. Further, it is clear that definition (
20
) extends to sequences by

\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}g(t_{n}):=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n-j}(g_{j}-\chi_{\gamma}g_{0})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{j}(g_{n-j}-\chi_{\gamma}g_{0}).

From
[
21
, Theorem 2.1]
we have the useful estimate

|\omega_{n}-{\kappa}t_{n}^{-\mu-1}|\leq Ct_{n}^{-\mu-1-p}{\kappa}^{p+1},\quad p=0,1,2,

(22)

for
t\in(0,T]
.

As mentioned above, most of the results in the literature analyse CQ as an approximation to Riemann-Liouville derivatives
[
19
]
.

However, as the Caputo and Riemann-Liouville derivatives of order
\gamma
are equivalent for
\gamma\in(-1,0)
and for
\gamma\in(0,1)
are equivalent if
g(0)=0
, we can deduce from
[
21
, Theorem 2.2]
the following result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\gamma\in(-1,1)
,
{\kappa}\in(0,\bar{\kappa})
be the time step for some sufficiently small
\bar{\kappa}>0
, and
g(t)=t^{\beta}
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Then for BDF2 based CQ it holds

\left|\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}g(t)-\partial_{t}^{\gamma}g(t)\right|\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\beta=0,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa},&\beta=0,\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-p}{\kappa}^{p},&\beta\geq 1,\end{array}\right.

(23)

and
p=1,2
.

Proof.

Note that for
\beta=0
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
we have
\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}g\equiv\partial_{t}^{\gamma}g\equiv 0
. The remaining cases follow directly from
[
21
, Theorem 2.2]
.
∎

Next we add correction terms that integrate lower order terms exactly and do not destroy the convergence for the higher order terms. For
\gamma\in(-1,0)
it is sufficient to correct for constant functions, whereas for
\gamma\in(0,1)
we will need to correct for linears as well.

Correction terms were introduced by Lubich in
[
19
]
and are of the form

\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}g(t)=\sum_{j\geq 0;t_{j}\leq t}\omega_{j}g(t-t_{j})+w_{0}(t)g(0)+w_{1}(t)g(t_{1}).

(24)

Here, the correction terms
w_{j}
are chosen so that

w_{0}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{t^{-\gamma}}{\Gamma(1-\gamma)}-\sum\limits_{j\geq 0;t_{j}\leq t}\omega_{j},&\text{for }\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
-\sum\limits_{j\geq 0;t_{j}\leq t}\omega_{j}-w_{1}(t),&\text{for }\gamma\in(0,1)\end{array}\right.

w_{1}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{for }\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
{\kappa}^{-1}\left(\frac{t^{1-\gamma}}{\Gamma(2-\gamma)}-\sum\limits_{j\geq 0;t_{j}\leq t}(t-t_{j})\omega_{j}\right),&\text{for }\gamma\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, denote
w_{nj}:=w_{j}(t_{n})
and hence

\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}g(t_{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\omega_{n-j}g(t_{j})+w_{n0}g(0)+w_{n1}g(t_{1}).

Note that by definition
\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}
already contains the correction for constants if
\gamma\in(0,1)
. In that case the above just adds the correction for linear functions.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under the conditions of Lemma
3.1
it holds that for
g(t)=t^{\beta}

\left|\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}g(t)-\partial_{t}^{\gamma}g(t)\right|\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\beta=0,\\
0&\beta=1,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-2}{\kappa}^{2}&\beta\geq 1,\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-2}{\kappa}^{2}+t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}^{\beta+1}&\beta>1,\gamma\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

(25)

with
C>0
independent of
{\kappa}\in(0,\bar{\kappa})
.

Proof.

For
\gamma<0
, the proof follows directly from Lemma
3.1
and definition of
\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}
. For
\gamma>0
, Lemma
3.1
and the definition of
w_{1}(t)
imply that
w_{1}(t)\leq Ct^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}
and hence
w_{n1}g(t_{1})=t^{-\gamma-1}O({\kappa}^{1+\beta})
for
g(t)=t^{\beta}
and
\beta>1
. This in turn implies the final missing result.
∎

Using the above shorthand for the CQ approximation, we can write the fully discretized systems as

\tfrac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(u^{h}_{n+1}-2u^{h}_{n}+u^{h}_{n-1},v)+(\nabla u^{h}_{n},\nabla v)+(a_{\gamma}\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}u^{h}(t_{n}),v)=(f(t_{n}),v)

(26)

or when including the correction

\tfrac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(u^{h}_{n+1}-2u^{h}_{n}+u^{h}_{n-1},v)+(\nabla u^{h}_{n},\nabla v)+(a_{\gamma}\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}u^{h}(t_{n}),v)=(f(t_{n}),v),

(27)

n=1,\dots,N-1
. The coupling of the two time discretizations is similar to the FEM-BEM coupling in
[
2
]
. As both of the above schemes are explicit, we will see during the course of the analysis that the following CFL condition is required

{\kappa}\leq\frac{\sqrt{2}h}{C_{\text{inv}}}.

(28)

It remains to describe the choice of initial data
u^{h}_{0},u^{h}_{1}
. To do this we require the Ritz projection, denoted by
R_{h}:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\rightarrow V^{h}
and defined by

(\nabla R_{h}u,\nabla v)=(\nabla u,\nabla v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V^{h}

and the
L^{2}
projection is denoted by
P_{h}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow V^{h}

(P_{h}u,v)=(u,v)\qquad\text{for all }v\in V^{h}.

We have the approximation property, see, e.g.,
[
16
]
,

\|R_{h}u-u\|\leq Ch^{s}\|u\|_{s}\quad\text{ for }s\in[1,2]\text{ and }\forall u\in H^{s}(\Omega)\cap H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

(29)

We then define the initial data by

u^{h}_{0}=R_{h}u_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad u^{h}_{1}=R_{h}\left(u_{0}+{\kappa}v_{0}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\kappa}^{2}P_{h}\partial_{t}^{2}u(0).

(30)

Using the PDE and the fact that
\partial_{t}^{2}u
is continuous we see that
P_{h}\partial_{t}^{2}u(0)\in V^{h}
is the solution of

(P_{h}\partial_{t}^{2}u(0),v)=(f(0),v)-(\nabla u_{0},\nabla v)\quad\text{for all }v\in V^{h}.

Note also that by definition

{\bar{\partial}}_{t}u^{h}(0)=R_{h}\partial_{t}u(0)=R_{h}v_{0}.

The reason for using the mixed approximation
\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}
instead of a fully CQ approximation
\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma+1}}
is to conserve the sign of the damping term.

Lemma 3.3
.

Given a sequence
v_{0},\dots,v_{N}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have

(a)

For
\gamma\in(-1,0)

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}v(t_{n}),v_{n})\geq 0

and

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}v(t_{n}),v_{n})\geq-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\omega_{n0}(v_{0},v_{n}).

(b)

For
\gamma\in(0,1)

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}v(t_{n}),v_{n})\geq-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\omega_{n}(v_{0},v_{n})

and

\sum_{n=0}^{N}(\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}v(t_{n}),v_{n})\geq-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\omega_{n0}(v_{0},v_{n})-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\omega_{n1}(v_{1},v_{n}).

Proof.

The proof follows directly from the frequency domain estimate

\operatorname{Re}(s^{\gamma}v,v)\geq 0

for
v\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and the Herglotz theorem
[
2
, Theorem 2.3]
.
∎

4
Convergence analysis

We start with analyzing the error of the mixed approximation
\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}
when applied to
t^{\beta}
. We will first need a simple technical lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
t_{n}=n{\kappa}
with
{\kappa}>0
and
t_{N}=T
. Given
\eta\neq-1
there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
T
and
{\kappa}
such that

{\kappa}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}^{\eta}\leq C\max(T^{\eta+1},{\kappa}^{\eta+1}).

Proof.

Consider first the case
\eta\leq 0
. Then
t^{\eta}
is a decreasing function and

{\kappa}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}^{\eta}\leq{\kappa}^{\eta+1}+\int_{{\kappa}}^{T}t^{\eta}dt=\frac{1}{\eta+1}\left(T^{\eta+1}+\eta{\kappa}^{\eta+1}\right).

If
\eta\geq 0
, then
t^{\eta}
is an increasing function and we have instead

{\kappa}\sum_{n=1}^{N}t_{n}^{\eta}\leq\int_{{\kappa}}^{T+{\kappa}}t^{\eta}dt=\frac{1}{\eta+1}\left((T+{\kappa})^{\eta+1}-{\kappa}^{\eta+1}\right).

Hence in both cases the sum is bounded by
C\max(T^{\eta+1},{\kappa}^{\eta+1})
with the constant depending on
\eta
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

For
g(t)=t^{\beta}
and
\beta=0,1
or
\beta\in[2,3]
we have

\left|\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}g(t)\right|\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\beta=0,\\
0&\beta=1,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}&\beta=1,\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}&\beta\in\{2,3\}\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}+t^{\beta-3}{\kappa}^{\min(2-\gamma,2)}&\beta\in(2,3),\end{array}\right.

and constant
C>0
independent of
{\kappa}\in(0,\bar{\kappa})
for some small enough
\bar{\kappa}>0
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof,
C>0
denotes a generic constant allowed to change from one step to another.

First note that the error is 0 for
\beta=0
since
\partial_{t}g\equiv{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g\equiv 0
. For
\beta>0
, split the error as

\left|\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}g(t)\right|\leq\underbrace{\left|\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}({\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}g(t))\right|}_{A_{\beta}}+\underbrace{\left|(\partial_{\kappa}^{{\gamma}}-\partial_{t}^{\gamma})\partial_{t}g(t)\right|}_{B_{\beta}}.

(31)

As
{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}g(t)=0
for
t\geq 0
if
\beta=1,2
, we have that
A_{1}=A_{2}=0
and for
\gamma>0
also
A_{3}=0
.

For
\beta\in(2,3]
we apply Newton’s generalized binomial theorem to
{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)=\frac{1}{2{\kappa}}((t+{\kappa})^{\beta}-(t-{\kappa})^{\beta})
and see that for
t>{\kappa}

\begin{split}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}g(t)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
k\end{pmatrix}t^{\beta-k}{\kappa}^{k-1}-\beta t^{\beta-1}\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=3\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
k\end{pmatrix}t^{\beta-k}{\kappa}^{k-1}\\
&={\kappa}^{2}t^{-3}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=3\\
k\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
k\end{pmatrix}t^{\beta-k+3}{\kappa}^{k-3}\\
&\leq{\kappa}^{2}t^{-3}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
k+3\end{pmatrix}t^{\beta-k}{\kappa}^{k}\\
&\leq C{\kappa}^{2}t^{-3}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\
k\end{pmatrix}t^{\beta-k}{\kappa}^{k}\\
&=C{\kappa}^{2}t^{-3}(t+{\kappa})^{\beta}\leq C{\kappa}^{2}t^{\beta-3}.\end{split}

For
t\in[0,{\kappa}]
we have instead

\begin{split}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}g(t)&=2^{\beta-1}{\kappa}^{\beta-2}t-\beta t^{\beta-1}=O({\kappa}^{\beta-1}).\end{split}

As the convolution weights satisfy, see (
22
),
|\omega_{j}|\leq C{\kappa}t_{j}^{-\gamma-1}
,
j\geq 1
, with
|\omega_{0}|\leq C{\kappa}^{-\gamma}

we have for
t\in[t_{n},t_{n+1})
,
n\geq 1
,

\begin{split}A_{\beta}&\leq C{\kappa}^{2}\left(|\omega_{0}|t^{\beta-3}+|\omega_{n}|{\kappa}^{\beta-3}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}|\omega_{j}|(t-t_{j})^{\beta-3}\right)\\
&\leq C{\kappa}^{2}\left({\kappa}^{-\gamma}t^{\beta-3}+t_{n}^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}^{\beta-2}+{\kappa}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}t_{j}^{-\gamma-1}(t-t_{j})^{\beta-3}\right)\\
&\leq C{\kappa}^{2}\left({\kappa}^{-\gamma}t^{\beta-3}+t_{n}^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}^{\beta-2}+t_{n}^{\beta-3}\max(1,{\kappa}^{-\gamma})\right)=C(t^{\beta-3}{\kappa}^{\min(2-\gamma,2)}),\quad t\geq{\kappa},\end{split}

where we have used
[
20
, Lemma 5.3]
to bound the discrete convolution in the following way:

\begin{split}{\kappa}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}t_{j}^{-\gamma-1}(t-t_{j})^{\beta-3}&\leq{\kappa}^{\beta-3-\gamma}\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}j^{-\gamma-1}(n-j)^{\beta-3}\\
&\leq C{\kappa}^{\beta-3-\gamma}n^{\max{(-\gamma-1,\beta-3,\beta-\gamma-3)}}\\
&\leq C{\kappa}^{-\gamma}t_{n}^{\beta-3}n^{\max{(-\gamma-\beta+2,0,-\gamma)}}\\
&\leq Ct_{n}^{\beta-3}\max(1,{\kappa}^{-\gamma}),\end{split}

where
C
as always is allowed to depend on
T
. For
t\in(0,{\kappa})
we obtain a similar bound as
A_{\beta}\leq C{\kappa}^{-\gamma+\beta-1}\leq Ct^{\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2-\gamma}
.

For
\beta=0
,
B_{0}=0
and for
\beta=1
or
\beta\geq 2
we have from (
23
) with
\partial_{t}g=\beta t^{\beta-1}
that

B_{\beta}\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\beta=1,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}&\beta=1,\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}&\beta\geq 2.\end{array}\right.

Combining all the cases gives the stated result.
∎

We now prove the corresponding lemma for the corrected quadrature.

Lemma 4.3
.

For
g(t)=t^{\beta}
and
\beta=0,1
or
\beta\in[2,3]
we have

\left|\partial_{{\kappa}\ast}^{{\gamma}}{\bar{\partial}}_{t}g(t)-\partial_{t}^{\gamma+1}g(t)\right|\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\beta=0,1\\
0&\beta=2,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}&\beta\in\{2,3\},\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma}{\kappa}^{2}+t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}^{3}&\beta=3,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}&\beta>2,\beta\neq 3,\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}+t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}^{\beta}+t^{\beta-3}{\kappa}^{\min(2,2-\gamma)}&\beta\in(2,3),\gamma\in(0,1)\\
\end{array}\right.

for
t\in(0,T]
,
C>0
independent of time step
{\kappa}\in(0,\bar{\kappa})
for some small enough
\bar{\kappa}>0
.

Proof.

The proof is along the same lines as the proof of Lemma
4.2
, where using Lemma
3.2
we note that

B_{\beta}\leq C\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\beta=1,\\
0&\beta=2,\gamma\in(0,1)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}&\beta\geq 2,\gamma\in(-1,0)\\
t^{-\gamma+\beta-3}{\kappa}^{2}+t^{-\gamma-1}{\kappa}^{\beta}&\beta>2,\gamma\in(0,1).\end{array}\right.

and as before
A_{\beta}=0
for
\beta=1,2,3
and
A_{\beta}=t^{\beta-3}O({\kappa}^{\min(2,2-\gamma)})
for
\beta\in(2,3)
.
∎

We next investigate the error for functions that are smooth for
t>0
, but may have a singularity at
t=0
.

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists a constant
C>0
independent of
{\kappa}\in(0,\bar{\kappa})
for some small enough
\bar{\kappa}>0

Proof.

Let

and

The first term is a polynomial to which we can directly apply Lemma

Theorem 4.6

Let

It remains to bound

where we used the smoothness assumption and the calculation (for

\begin{split}\left\|\frac{u_{n}-u_{n-1}}{{\kappa}}-u^{\prime}(t_{n}-\frac{1}{2}{\kappa})\right\|&\leq\left\|\frac{e^{h}_{n}-e^{h}_{n-1}}{{\kappa}}\right\|+\left\|R_{h}\frac{u(t_{n})-u(t_{n-1})}{{\kappa}}-u^{\prime}(t_{n}-\frac{1}{2}{\kappa})\right\|\\

Consider the linear Volterra equation of the second kind

v(t)=g(t)+\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{-\gamma}k(t-\tau)v(\tau)\,d\tau,

(46)

for
\gamma\in(-1,1)
and
k\in C[0,T]
. Before stating an existence result, we need a technical lemma the proof of which is elementary.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
g,k\in C[0,T]
. Then for
\beta>-1

\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta}k(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau=g(0)k(0)\frac{t^{\beta+1}}{\beta+1}+o(t^{\beta+1})\quad\text{as }t\rightarrow 0^{+}.

Proof.

As
k
and
g
are continuous, there exist constants
C_{k}>0
,
C_{g}>0
such that
|k(t)|\leq C_{k}
and
|g(t)|\leq C_{g}
. Further for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
\delta>0
such that

\max\{|k(t)-k(0)|,|g(t)-g(0)|\}\leq\varepsilon\qquad\text{for }t\in(0,\delta).

Hence

\left|\int_{0}^{t}(t-\tau)^{\beta}k(t-\tau)g(\tau)d\tau-g(0)k(0)\frac{t^{\beta+1}}{\beta+1}\right|\leq(C_{k}+C_{g})\frac{1}{\beta+1}t^{\beta+1}\varepsilon,

for any
t\in(0,\delta)
.
∎

Theorem A.2
.

If
g\in C[0,T]
, then (
46
) has a unique solution
v\in C[0,T]
. Furthermore,

v(t)=g(t)+g(0)k(0)\frac{t^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}+o(t^{1-\gamma})\quad\text{as }t\rightarrow 0^{+}.

Proof.

For
\gamma\in(-1,0)
, the kernel
(t-\tau)^{-\gamma}k(t-\tau)
is continuous and hence the existence of the continuous solution follows from
[
6
, Theorem 2.1.5]
. Whereas, for
\gamma\in(0,1)
,
[
6
, Theorem 6.1.2]
gives the existence of the continuous solution.

The technical lemma together with the fact that
v(0)=g(0)
and (
46
) gives the form of solution for
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
∎

The following technical lemma is needed to investigate the error for the nonsmooth solution.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
g\in\mathcal{C}^{4}(0,T]
with
g^{(3)}\in L^{1}[0,T]
. Then for
t\in[2{\kappa},T]

\left|g^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(g(t+{\kappa})-2g(t)+g(t-{\kappa}))\right|\leq C{\kappa}\int_{t-{\kappa}}^{t+{\kappa}}|g^{(4)}(\tau)|d\tau

and for
t={\kappa}

\left|g^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(g(t+{\kappa})-2g(t)+g(t-{\kappa}))\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{2{\kappa}}|g^{(3)}(\tau)|d\tau,

where
C>0
is a constant depending on
C_{k}
and
\alpha
.

Proof.

Let
t\geq 2{\kappa}
. Then using the integral form of the remainder

\left|g^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(g(t+{\kappa})-2g(t)+g(t-{\kappa}))\right|\leq C{\kappa}\int_{t-{\kappa}}^{t+{\kappa}}|g^{(4)}(\tau)|d\tau.

This gives the proof for
t\geq 2{\kappa}
.

Next, for
t>{\kappa}
and
s\in[t-{\kappa},t+{\kappa}]
we have the Taylor expansion

g(s)=g(t)+g^{\prime}(t)(s-t)+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(t)(s-t)^{2}+\int_{t}^{s}(s-\tau)^{2}g^{(3)}(\tau)d\tau.

Hence

|\frac{1}{{\kappa}^{2}}(g(t+{\kappa})-2g(t)+g(t-{\kappa}))-g^{\prime\prime}(t)|\leq C\int_{0}^{2{\kappa}}|g^{(3)}(\tau)|d\tau.

Letting
t\to{\kappa}
gives the result for
t={\kappa}
.
∎
