Jackknife Empirical Likelihood Method for U Statistics Based on Multivariate Samples and its Applications
Naresh Garg, Litty Mathew, Isha Dewan, Sudheesh Kumar Kattumannil

Abstract
Empirical likelihood (EL) and its extension via the jackknife empirical likelihood (JEL) method provide robust alternatives to parametric approaches, in the contexts with uncertain data distributions. This paper explores the theoretical foundations and practical applications of JEL in the context of multivariate sample-based U-statistics. In this study we develop the JEL method for multivariate U-statistics with three (or more) samples. This study enhance the JEL methods capability to handle complex data structures while preserving the computation efficiency of the empirical likelihood method. To demonstrate the applications of the JEL method, we compute confidence intervals for differences in VUS measurements which have potential applications in classification problems. Monte Carlo simulation studies are conducted to evaluate the efficiency of the JEL, Normal approximation and Kernel based confidence intervals. These studies validate the superior performance of the JEL approach in terms of coverage probability and computational efficiency compared to other two methods. Additionally, a real data application illustrates the practical utility of the approach. The JEL method developed here has potential applications in dealing with complex data structures.

Introduction

Empirical likelihood (EL) has emerged as a powerful tool in statistical inference, particularly valuable when traditional parametric assumptions about data distribution are either unknown or do not hold. EL operates on the principle of maximizing a nonparametric likelihood function constrained by observed data to perform hypothesis tests and construct confidence intervals. Owen’s papers (
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
) have been instrumental in the development and popularization of EL. Furthermore,
[
17
]
highlights EL’s ability to automatically shape confidence regions, integrate additional constraints, and preserve the range and transformations inherent in the data. This flexibility and robustness have positioned EL as a cornerstone in modern statistical methodology, offering advantages in scenarios ranging from incomplete data to complex multivariate analyses (
[
19
]
).

The jackknife empirical likelihood (JEL) method is an extension of Owen’s foundational work that combines the EL method with the jackknife resampling technique.
[
7
]
introduced the JEL method as a modification of EL to deal with non-linear statistics. They illustrated this method using one- and two-sample U-statistics, paving the way for its application in diverse settings.
[
26
]
considered testing the equality of two multivariate means using the JEL method.
[
11
]
proposed a JEL-based non-parametric inferential procedure for non-smooth estimating functions and demonstrated its application.
[
18
]
extended the EL method to general estimating equations based on U-statistics and proposed JEL for vector U-statistics. Using this approach, they developed EL ratio tests for moment-based distribution characteristics.
[
20
]
developed a consistent JEL-based test for diagonal symmetry based on energy statistics.

Motivated by these studies, we generalize the JEL method to accommodate
q
-dimensional three (or more)-samples U-statistics. This generalization is needed for a versatile statistical tool capable of handling complex data structures and multiple sample comparisons without relying on distributional assumptions. This extension broadens the applicability of EL and JEL beyond traditional univariate and two-sample scenarios. Additionally, we explain the adaptation of JEL to
q
-dimensional
k
-sample based U-statistics, contribute to a deeper understanding of its theoretical foundations and practical implications in multivariate statistical analysis.

To give an application of described JEL method for three sample based U statistics, we applied it to a practical problem by constructing confidence intervals for the difference in volumes under surfaces (VUS) for two dependent variables. The VUS, introduced by
[
14
]
, expands upon area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) by accommodating three-class diagnostic scenarios (a detailed discussion about VUS is provided in Section 2.1). For an application of the JEL method for
k
-sample-based U statistics, we construct confidence intervals for the difference of two dependent hypervolumes under the ROC manifolds (HUM). ROC curves are generalized to ROC manifolds for multi-category classification, and the analogous extension of the area under the ROC manifold is termed the HUM. For more details on HUM, refer to
[
10
]
and Chapter 3 of
[
25
]
(a detailed discussion about HUM is provided in Section 5.1).

The paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we generalize JEL to
q
-dimensional, three-sample-based U-statistics. To provide an application of the described method, we consider the problem of constructing confidence intervals for difference of two dependent VUSs. We use the methods: JEL, U-statistics with asymptotic normality, and the kernel bootstrap to obtain the confidence intervals. Section 3 conducts extensive simulation studies to evaluate effectiveness of the confidence intervals for the difference of the VUSs under the Marshall-Olkin bivariate exponential and Bivariate Pareto models. In Section 4, we consider a real data analysis to demonstrate the findings. In Section 5, we further generalize the JEL U-statistics-based approach to
k\,(>3)
multivariate sample U-statistics and illustrated the method using HUM.

2.1
Application: Volumes under ROC surfaces

This subsection presents an application of the results obtained above to the Volume Under the ROC Surface (VUS), introduced by

Traditionally confined to binary classifications, ROC curves and their associated Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics provide critical insights into diagnostic accuracy but are limited in their applicability to multi-class scenarios. The VUS extends AUC by accommodating three-class diagnostic scenarios. This evolution addresses the inherent limitations of binary classifications in medical diagnostics, where conditions often manifest along a spectrum rather than as discrete categories

]

[

Suppose

The difference of two correlated VUS’s can be defined as

\displaystyle=E\left[I\left(X_{11}<Y_{11}<Z_{11}\right)-I\left(X_{12}<Y_{12}<Z_{12}\right)\right],

Consider the symmetric kernel
h^{VUS}
given by

h^{VUS}\left(\left(X_{i1},X_{i2}\right),\left(Y_{j1},Y_{j2}\right),\left(Z_{k1},Z_{k2}\right)\right)=I\left(X_{i1}<Y_{j1}<Z_{k1}\right)-I\left(X_{i2}<Y_{j2}<Z_{k2}\right).

An estimator of
\theta
can be found as U-statistic with kernel
h
as

U_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}^{VUS}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}h^{VUS}\left(\left(X_{i1},X_{i2}\right),\left(Y_{j1},Y_{j2}\right),\left(Z_{k1},Z_{k2}\right)\right).

The U-statistic
U_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}^{VUS}
is an unbiased and consistent estimator of
\theta
(Lehmann, (1951)). Also, using Theorem
2.1
, a two sided
(1-\alpha)
level CI for
\theta
based on the asymptotic distribution of
U_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}^{VUS}
is

\left(U_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}^{VUS}-z_{\alpha/2}\hat{\sigma},U_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}^{VUS}+z_{\alpha/2}\hat{\sigma}\right),

where
\hat{\sigma}
is the jackknife pseudo-values based consistent estimator of
Var(U_{n_{1},n_{2},n_{3}}^{VUS})
(see
[
2
]
), as defined by (
2
).

JEL based Confidence Interval :

Now, we use the Theorem
2.2
to construct JEL based confidence intervals for
\theta
. Here with respect to Theorem
2.2
, we have
q=2
and
m_{1}=m_{2}=m_{2}=1
, and on the same lines as of Section 2, we can define
\log R(\theta)
. Therefore, using Theorem
2.2
, the JEL based confidence interval for
\theta
at level
(1-\alpha)
is given by

CI=\{\theta\;|\;-2\log R(\theta)\leq\chi^{2}_{1,1-\alpha}\}.

Kernel Based Confidence Interval :

To estimate the difference of two VUS’s smoothly
[
8
]
proposed the kernel-smoothed version of the difference of two VUS’s as follows

\hat{\theta}=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{3}}\left(\Phi\left(\frac{Y_{1j}-X_{1i}}{\sqrt{h_{1}^{2}+h_{2}^{2}}}\right)\Phi\left(\frac{Z_{1k}-Y_{1j}}{\sqrt{h_{2}^{2}+h_{3}^{2}}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{Y_{2j}-X_{2i}}{\sqrt{h_{4}^{2}+h_{5}^{2}}}\right)\Phi\left(\frac{Z_{2k}-Y_{2j}}{\sqrt{h_{5}^{2}+h_{6}^{2}}}\right)\right),

where
\Phi(\cdot)
represents the standard normal distribution function and the bandwidths are calculated by

h_{i}=0.9\min\left\{sd\left(x_{i}\right)\right.,\left.\operatorname{iqr}\left(x_{i}\right)/1.34\right\}n_{i}^{-0.2},\;\;\;\;i=1,2,\dots,6,

where
sd(\cdot)
and
\operatorname{iqr}(\cdot)
represent the standard deviation and the interquartile range.
[
22
]
introduced the bandwidth
h_{i}
, which regulates the degree of smoothing. This asymptotic bandwidth selection is effective for a broad range of density functions. Such bandwidths have been utilized by many researchers in diagnostic studies.

To construct the kernel based confidence interval for
\theta
, we employ the non-parametric bootstrap method to obtain the confidence interval. In each bootstrap replication, we find a point estimate
\hat{\theta}
of
\theta
, that allows us to construct the confidence intervals using the percentile method.

3
Simulation

In this section, we employ Monte Carlo simulation studies to evaluate the finite sample performance of CIs for difference of two VUS’s. The three methods used are the JEL, normal approximation with jackknife variance estimator and kernel based bootstrapping. We assess the performance of each method using two distinct criteria:

(a) Coverage Probability (CP): This represents the probability that the true parameter value falls within the constructed confidence interval (CI). Methods with lower coverage error—defined as the discrepancy between the actual coverage probability and the nominal value are preferred.

(b) Average Length (AL) of CIs: Methods that produce CIs with shorter average lengths are desirable, as shorter intervals provide more precise information about the location of the unknown parameter.

We conduct simulation studies with different sample sizes and simulation is repeated 1,000 time. We use R package “empilik” for finding JEL based confidence intervals. For constructing confidence intervals based on kernel based method we use 100 bootstrap replications. Two bivariate probability models are considered for the simulation studies.

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}

[

where
\lambda_{i}>0,\;i=1,2,3.

We generated sample sizes
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
, and
n_{3}
from three independent models MOBVE(
\lambda_{x_{1}},\lambda_{x_{2}},\lambda_{x_{3}}

\lambda_{y_{1}},\lambda_{y_{2}},\lambda_{y_{3}}

Bivariate Pareto Model :

F_{X_{i}}(x_{i})=1-\left(\frac{\lambda_{i}}{x_{i}}\right)^{\alpha_{i}},\;x_{i}\geq\lambda_{i}>0,\;\alpha_{i}>0,\;i=1,2.

-1\leq\theta\leq 1

\displaystyle C_{\theta}(F_{X_{1}}(x_{1}),F_{X_{2}}(x_{2}))

\displaystyle F_{X_{1}}(x_{1})F_{X_{2}}(x_{2})[1+\theta(1-F_{X_{1}}(x_{1}))(1-F_{X_{2}}(x_{2}))],

Data Analysis

In this section, we apply different methods discussed above to the Alzheimer’s disease dataset taken from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI,

The focus is on understanding how these biomarkers behave differently in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD), cognitively normal (CN) individuals, and those with late mild cognitive impairment (LMCI). This analysis aims to compare the differences in volumes under surfaces that represent probabilities involving the amyloid-beta (A
\beta
) and phosphorylated tau (p-tau) biomarkers across various cognitive states in Alzheimer’s disease.
Let random variables

represent p-tau levels in AD, CN, and LMCI, respectively. Here, our parameter of interest is

\theta=P(X_{1}<Y_{1}<Z_{1})-P(X_{2}<Y_{2}<Z_{2}),

where

is the probability that an individual with AD has lower A
\beta
levels compared to a CN individual, and the CN individual has lower A
\beta
levels compared to an individual with LMCI. If this probability is high, it indicates that the A
\beta
biomarker effectively distinguishes between the three cognitive states. The probability

can be interpreted similarly.

This comparison tells us which biomarker, A
\beta
or p-tau, is more effective in highlighting the changes or differences among the three cognitive conditions. A positive estimated value of
\theta
means A
\beta
levels give a clearer picture of the progression from normal cognition to late mild impairment to Alzheimer’s disease, while a negative
\theta
means p-tau levels do.

In total, 883 results were included in this analysis, with sample sizes for the three individual classes being 222, 122, and 539, respectively. We used the JEL, Normal approximation, and Kernel-based methods, as described in Section 2.1, to construct confidence intervals for the difference between two VUSs. The JEL method took 3.4 hours, the Normal approximation method took 4.7 hours, and the Kernel-based method (with 1000 bootstraps) took 52 hours to obtain results for this real data analysis using R software on the previously specified laptop.

Multivariate Sample U-statistics

Until now, the results developed in this paper have been restricted to three-sample-based U-statistics. However, in practical applications, there are many cases where U-statistics are based on more than three samples (
[

]
). To provide an alternative non-parametric method for estimation and testing purposes, in this section, we develop the JEL method for more than three multivariate samples based U statistics. Consider the following independent random samples

\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n_{1}}^{(1)}\right),\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n_{2}}^{(2)}\right),\dots,\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n_{k}}^{(k)}\right)

from
k
independent
q
-dimensional distribution functions
F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{k}
, respectively, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{i}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=(X_{11}^{(1)},X_{12}^{(1)},\dots,X_{1q}^{(1)})^{T},\;i=1,2,...,n_{1},

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{i}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=(X_{11}^{(2)},X_{12}^{(2)},\dots,X_{1q}^{(2)})^{T},\;i=1,2,...,n_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{i}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=(X_{11}^{(k)},X_{12}^{(k)},\dots,X_{1q}^{(k)})^{T},\;i=1,2,...,n_{k}.

Let
\theta=E\left[h\left(\mathbb{X}_{i_{1,1}}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{1,m_{1}}}^{(1)},\mathbb{X}_{i_{2,1}}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{2,m_{2}}}^{(2)},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{k,1}}^{(k)}\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{k,m_{k}}}^{(k)}\right)\right]\in\Re
be the parameter of interest. A
k
-sample U-statistics of degree
\left(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}\right)
with a symmetric kernel
h
is given by

\displaystyle U_{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}}=

\displaystyle d^{*}\hskip-7.22743pt\mathop{\sum\sum\cdots\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i_{1,1}<\ldots<i_{1,m_{1}}\leq n_{1}\\
1\leq i_{2,1}<\ldots<i_{2,m_{2}}\leq n_{2}\\
\vdots\;\;\;\\
1\leq i_{k,1}<\ldots<i_{k,m_{k}}\leq n_{k}\end{subarray}}\hskip-7.22743pth\left(\mathbb{X}_{i_{1,1}}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{1,m_{1}}}^{(1)},\mathbb{X}_{i_{2,1}}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{2,m_{2}}}^{(2)},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{k,1}}^{(k)}\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{i_{k,m_{k}}}^{(k)}\right),

where

d^{*}=\frac{1}{{n_{1}\choose m_{1}}{n_{2}\choose m_{2}}\cdots{n_{k}\choose m_{k}}},

and
\{i_{1,1},\ldots,i_{1,m_{1}}\}
,
\{i_{2,1},\ldots,i_{2,m_{2}}\},\dots,\{i_{k,1},\ldots,i_{k,m_{k}}\}
indicates
m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{k}
integers chosen without replacement from the sets
\{1,\ldots,n_{1}\},\{1,\ldots,n_{2}\},\dots,\{1,\ldots,n_{k}\}
, respectively.

Let
n=\sum_{l=1}^{k}n_{l}
and
m=\sum_{l=1}^{k}m_{l}
. Define
\mathbb{W}_{l},
as

\mathbb{W}_{l}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{X}_{l}^{(1)},&\text{ if }l=1,\dots,n_{1}\\
\mathbb{X}_{l-n_{1}}^{(2)},&\text{ if }l=n_{1}+1,\dots,n_{1}+n_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{X}_{l-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}n_{i}}^{(k)},&\text{ if }l=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}n_{i}+1,\dots,n\end{cases}

Therefore,

(\mathbb{W}_{1},\mathbb{W}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{W}_{n})=\left(\mathbb{X}_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n_{1}}^{(1)},\mathbb{X}_{1}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n_{2}}^{(2)},\dots,\mathbb{X}_{1}^{(k)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{n_{k}}^{(k)}\right).

In same lines as the three sample case (in Section 2), we define the jackknife pseudo-values for combined single sample
(\mathbb{W}_{1},\mathbb{W}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{W}_{n})
,
V_{l},
as

V_{l}=nU_{n}-(n-1)U_{n-1}^{(-l)},\;\;\forall\;\;l=1,2,\dots,n,

(17)

where

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper, we have described the JEL method for
k\,(\geq 3)
multivariate samples U-statistics. This study opens the door for a wider range of applications in multivariate scenarios. By formalizing the JEL approach for
q
-dimensional
k
-sample-based U-statistics, we have established the asymptotic distribution of the JEL ratio and successfully constructed confidence intervals for unknown parameters.

To demonstrate the applications of our findings, we constructed confidence intervals for differences in two volumes under ROC surfaces (VUSs) and differences in two hypervolumes under the ROC manifolds (HUMs). These applications highlight the practical utility of the JEL method in addressing complex statistical problems. To evaluate the performance of the JEL method in comparison with other non-parametric approaches, we conducted extensive simulation studies under various models, including the Marshall-Olkin bivariate exponential and Bivariate Pareto distributions. Our results showed the superior performance of the JEL method in terms of coverage probability and computational complexity when compared to the normal approximation using jackknife pseudo-values based variance estimator and kernel-based methods.

Additionally, we conducted a real data analysis using an Alzheimer’s disease dataset to illustrate the practical benefits of the JEL method in real-world scenarios. Overall, this study highlights the superior performance, versatility, lower computational cost and practical benefits of the JEL method, recommending its use in statistical practices that require reliable nonparametric inference for complex data structures.

Furthermore, the results of this paper facilitate hypothesis testing using the JEL method, based on the established asymptotic distribution of the JEL ratio. Previous studies, including
[
23
]
,
[
12
]
, and Chapter 8 of
[
3
]
, have explored testing problems based on U-statistics. The JEL method we developed in this paper can be effectively applied to these types of problems.
