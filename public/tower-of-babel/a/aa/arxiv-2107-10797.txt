Fourier growth of structured 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} -polynomials and applications
Jarosław Błasiok, Peter Ivanov, Yaonan Jin, Chin Ho Lee, Rocco A. Servedio, Emanuele Viola

Abstract
We analyze the Fourier growth , i.e. the L 1 L_{1} Fourier weight at level k k (denoted L 1 , k L_{1,k} ), of various well-studied classes of “structured” 𝔽 2 \mathbbm{F}_{2} -polynomials. This study is motivated by applications in pseudorandomness, in particular recent results and conjectures due to [ CHHL19 , CHLT19 , CGL+21 ] which show that upper bounds on Fourier growth (even at level k = 2 k=2 ) give unconditional pseudorandom generators. Our main results on Fourier growth are as follows: • We show that any symmetric degree- d d 𝔽 2 \mathbbm{F}_{2} -polynomial p p has L 1 , k ( p ) ≤ 𝐏𝐫 [ p = 1 ] ⋅ O ( d ) k L_{1,k}(p)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]\cdot O(d)^{k} , and this is tight for any constant k k . This quadratically strengthens an earlier bound that was implicit in [ RSV13 ] . • We show that any read- Δ \Delta degree- d d 𝔽 2 \mathbbm{F}_{2} -polynomial p p has L 1 , k ( p ) ≤ 𝐏𝐫 [ p = 1 ] ⋅ ( k Δ d ) O ⁡ ( k ) L_{1,k}(p)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]\cdot(k\Delta d)^{O(k)} . • We establish a composition theorem which gives L 1 , k L_{1,k} bounds on disjoint compositions of functions that are closed under restrictions and admit L 1 , k L_{1,k} bounds. Finally, we apply the above results to obtain new unconditional pseudorandom generators and new correlation bounds for various classes of 𝔽 2 \mathbbm{F}_{2} -polynomials.

Introduction

1.1
Background:
L_{1}
Fourier norms and Fourier growth

Over the past several decades, Fourier analysis of Boolean functions has emerged as a fundamental tool of great utility across many different areas within theoretical computer science and mathematics. Areas of application include (but are not limited to) combinatorics, the theory of random graphs and statistical physics, social choice theory, Gaussian geometry and the study of metric spaces, cryptography, learning theory, property testing, and many branches of computational complexity such as hardness of approximation, circuit complexity, and pseudorandomness. The excellent book of O’Donnell
[
O’D14
]
provides a broad introduction. In this paper we follow the notation of
[
O’D14
]
, and for a Boolean-valued function
f
on
n
Boolean variables and
S\subseteq[n]
, we write
\widehat{f}(S)
to denote the Fourier coefficient of
f
on
S
.

Given the wide range of different contexts within which the Fourier analysis of Boolean functions has been pursued, it is not surprising that many different quantitative parameters of Boolean functions have been analyzed in the literature. In this work we are chiefly interested in the
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k
:

Definition 1

(
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k
)
.

The
L_{1}
Fourier norm of a function
f\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
at level
k
is the quantity

\displaystyle L_{1,k}(f):=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]:\lvert S\rvert=k}\lvert\widehat{f}(S)\rvert.

For a function class
\mathcal{F}
, we write
L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F})
to denote
\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}L_{1,k}(f)
.

As we explain below, strong motivation for studying the
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k
(even for specific small values of
k
such as
k=2
) is given by exciting recent results in unconditional
pseudorandomness
.
More generally, the notion of
Fourier growth
is a convenient way of capturing the
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k
for every
k

Definition 2

(Fourier growth)
.

A function class
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{f\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}\}
has

if there exist constants
a

b
such that
L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F})\leq a\cdot b^{k}
for every
k
.

The notion of Fourier growth was explicitly introduced by Reingold, Steinke, and Vadhan in
[
RSV13
]
for the purpose of constructing pseudorandom generators for space-bounded computation (though we note that the Fourier growth of DNF formulas was already analyzed in
[
Man95
]
, motivated by applications in learning theory). In recent years there has been a surge of research interest in understanding the Fourier growth of different types of functions
[
Tal17
,
GSTW16
,
CHRT18
,
Lee19
,
GRZ21
,
Tal20
,
SSW21
,
GRT21
]
. One strand of motivation for this study has come from the study of quantum computing;
in particular, bounds on the Fourier growth of

[

]

[

]
which gave an oracle separation between the classes
\mathsf{BQP}
and
\mathsf{PH}

More recently, in order to achieve an optimal separation between quantum and randomized query complexity, several researchers
[
Tal20
,
BS21
,
SSW21
]
have studied the Fourier growth of decision trees, with the recent work of
[

]
obtaining optimal bounds. Analyzing the Fourier growth of other classes of functions has also led to separations between quantum and classical computation in other settings
[
GRT21
,
GRZ21
,
GTW21
]
.

Our chief interest in the current paper arises from a different line of work which has established powerful applications of Fourier growth bounds in pseudorandomness.
We describe the relevant background, which motivates a new conjecture that we propose on Fourier growth, in the next subsection.

1.3
Our results: Fourier bounds for structured
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials

Our main results show that
L_{1,2}
and
L_{1,k}
bounds of the flavor of
6

7
indeed hold for several well-studied classes of
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials, specifically
symmetric

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials and
read-
\Delta

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials. We additionally prove a composition theorem that allows us to combine such polynomials (or, more generally, any polynomials that satisfy certain
L_{1,k}
bounds) in a natural way and obtain
L_{1,k}
bounds on the resulting combined polynomials.

Before describing our results in detail, we pause to briefly explain why (beyond the fact that they are natural mathematical objects) such “highly structured” polynomials are attractive targets of study given known results. It has been known for more than ten years
[
BHL12
, Lemma 2]
that for any degree
d<(1-\epsilon)n
, a
random

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree
d
(constructed by independently including each monomial of degree at most
d
with probability
1/2
) is extremely unlikely to have bias larger than
\exp(-n/d)
.
It follows that as long as
d
is not too large, a random degree-
d

polynomial
p
is overwhelmingly likely to have
L_{1,k}(p)=o_{n}(1)
, which is much smaller than
d^{k}
.
(To verify this, consider the polynomials
p_{S}
obtained by XORing
p
with the parity function
\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}
.
Note that the bias of
p_{S}
is the Fourier coefficient of
(-1)^{p}
on
S
.
Now apply
[
BHL12
, Lemma 2]
to each polynomial
p_{S}
, and sum the terms.)

Since the conjectures hold true for random polynomials, it is natural
to investigate highly structured polynomials.

1.3.1
Symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials

A
symmetric

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial over
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is one whose output depends only on the Hamming weight of its input
x
. Such a polynomial of degree
d
can be written in the form

p(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{d}c_{k}\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k,S\subseteq[n]}\prod_{i\in S}x_{i},

where
c_{0},\ldots,c_{d}\in\{0,1\}
.
While symmetric polynomials may seem like simple objects, their study can sometimes lead to unexpected discoveries; for example, a symmetric, low-degree
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial provided a counterexample
to the “Inverse conjecture for the Gowers norm”
[
LMS11
,
GT09
]
.
For another example
[
IPV22
]
, symmetric polynomials have been conjectured to have maximal correlation with the mod 3 function. The conjecture has been verified for degree 2, and if true for higher degrees it would imply long-sought progress in circuit complexity.

We prove the following upper and lower bounds on the
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k
for any symmetric polynomial:

Theorem 8
.

Let
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
be a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree
d
.
Then
L_{1,k}(p)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]\cdot O(d)^{k}
for every
k
.

Theorem 9
.

For every
k
, there is a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of degree
d=\Theta(\sqrt{kn})
such that
L_{1,k}(p)\geq(e^{-k}/2)\cdot\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}=\Omega_{k}(1)\cdot d^{k}
.

Theorem

8
verifies the
[
CHLT19
]
conjecture (
6
), and even the generalized version
7
, for the class of symmetric polynomials.
Theorem

9
complements it by showing that the upper bounds in
Theorem

8
are tight for
k=O(1)
when
d=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.

Theorem

8
also provides a quadratic sharpening of an earlier bound that was implicit in
[
RSV13
]
(as well as providing the “correct” dependence on
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]
).
In
[
RSV13
]
Reingold, Steinke and Vadhan showed that any function
f
computed by an oblivious, read-once, regular branching program of width
w
has
L_{1,k}(f)\leq(2w^{2})^{k}
. It follows directly from a result of
[
BGL06
]
(
Lemma

17
below) that any symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial
p
of degree
d
can be computed by an oblivious, read-once, regular branching program of width at most
2d
, and hence the
[
RSV13
]
result implies that
L_{1,k}(p)\leq 8^{k}d^{2k}.

Subsequent to our work, Lee, Pyne, and Vadhan
[
LPV22
]
improved the
(2w^{2})^{k}
bound obtained in
[
RSV13
]
to
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1](w-1)^{k}

Theorem

8

1.3.2
Read-
\Delta

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials

For
\Delta\geq 1
, a
read-
\Delta

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial is one in which each input variable appears in at most
\Delta
monomials.
The case
\Delta=1
corresponds to the class of
read-once polynomials
, which are simply sums of monomials over disjoint sets of variables; for example, the polynomial
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{3}x_{4}
is read-once whereas
x_{1}x_{2}+x_{1}x_{4}
is read-twice. Read-once polynomials have been studied from the perspective of pseudorandomness
[
LV20
,
MRT19
,
Lee19
,
DHH20
]
as they capture several difficulties in improving Nisan’s generators
[
Nis92
]
for width-
4
read-once branching programs.

We show that the
L_{1,k}
Fourier norm of read-
\Delta
polynomials is polynomial in
d
and
\Delta
:

Theorem 10
.

Let
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a read-
\Delta
polynomial of degree
d
.
Then
L_{1,k}(p)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]\cdot O(k)^{k}\cdot(d\Delta)^{10k}
.

The work
[
Lee19
]
showed that read-once polynomials satisfy an

bound of
O(d)^{k}
and this is tight for every
k
, but we are not aware of previous bounds on even the
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k=2
for read-
\Delta
polynomials, even for
\Delta=2
.

As any monomial with degree
\Omega(\log n)
vanishes under a random restriction with high probability, we have the following corollary which applies to polynomials of any degree.

Corollary 11
.

Let
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a read-
\Delta
polynomial.
Then
L_{1,k}(p)\leq O(k)^{11k}\cdot(\Delta\log n)^{10k}

1.3.3
A composition theorem

The upper bounds of
Theorem

8
and
Theorem

10
both include a factor of
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]
. (We observe that negating
p
, i.e. adding 1 to it, does not change its

or
L_{1,k}
and keeps
p
symmetric (respectively, read-
\Delta
) if it was originally symmetric (respectively, read-
\Delta
), and hence in the context of those theorems we can assume that this
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]
factor is at most
1/2.
)
Level-
k
bounds that include this factor have appeared in earlier works for other classes of functions
[
OS07
,
BTW15
,
CHRT18
,
Tal20
,
GTW21
]
, and have been used to obtain high-level bounds for other classes of functions
[
CHRT18
,
Tal20
,
GTW21
]
and to extend level-

bounds to more general classes of functions
[
Lee19
]
.
Having these
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p=1]
factors in
Theorem

8
and
Theorem

10
is important for us in the context of our composition theorem, which we now describe. We begin by defining the notion of a
disjoint composition
of functions:

Definition 12
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions from
\{-1,1\}^{m}
to
\{-1,1\}
and let
\mathcal{G}
be a class of functions from
\{-1,1\}^{\ell}
to
\{-1,1\}
.
Define the class
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G}
of
disjoint compositions of
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
to be the class of all functions from
\{-1,1\}^{m\ell}
to
\{-1,1\}
of the form

h(x^{1},\ldots,x^{m})=f(g_{1}(x^{1}),\ldots,g_{m}(x^{m})),

where
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\mathcal{G}
are defined on
m
disjoint sets of variables and
f\in\mathcal{F}
.

As an example of this definition, the class of
block-symmetric
polynomials (i.e. polynomials whose variables are divided into blocks and are symmetric within each block but not overall) are a special case of disjoint compositions where
{\cal G}
is taken to be the class of symmetric polynomials. We remark that block-symmetric polynomials are known to correlate better with parities than symmetric polynomials in certain settings
[
GKV17
]
.

We prove a composition theorem for upper-bounding the
L_{1}
Fourier norm at level
k
of the disjoint composition of any classes of functions that are closed under restriction and admit a
L_{1,k}
bound of the form
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1]\cdot a\cdot b^{k}
:

Theorem 13
.

Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\mathcal{G}
and let
f\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\mathcal{F}
is closed under restrictions.
Suppose that for every
1\leq k\leq K
, we have

1.

L_{1,k}(f)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1]\cdot a_{\mathsf{out}}\cdot b_{\mathsf{out}}^{k}
for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
, and

2.

L_{1,k}(g)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[g=1]\cdot a_{\mathsf{in}}\cdot b_{\mathsf{in}}^{k}
for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
.

Then for every
\pm 1
-valued function
h\in\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{F}\circ\mathcal{G}
, we have that

L_{1,K}(h)\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[h=1]\cdot a_{\mathsf{out}}\cdot(a_{\mathsf{in}}b_{\mathsf{in}}b_{\mathsf{out}})^{K}.

See
Theorem

31
for a slightly sharper bound.
We remark that
Theorem

13
does not assume any
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial structure for the functions in
{\cal F}
or
{\cal G}
and thus may be of broader utility.

Applications of our results

Our structural results imply new pseudorandom generators and correlation bounds.

1.4.1
Pseudorandom generators

Combining our Fourier bounds with the polarizing framework, we obtain new PRGs for read-few polynomials.
The following theorem follows from applying

Theorem 14
.

There is an explicit pseudorandom generator that
\epsilon
-fools read-
\Delta

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials with seed length
\textnormal{poly}(\Delta,\log n,\log(1/\epsilon))
.

For constant

[

]

.
(Note that read-

We are not aware of any previous PRG for read-2 polynomials with

Note that the

By expressing a DNF in the Fourier expansion of

1.4.2
Correlation bounds

Exhibiting explicit Boolean functions that do not
correlate
with low-degree polynomials is a fundamental challenge in complexity. Perhaps surprisingly, this challenge stands in the way of progress on a striking variety of frontiers in complexity, including circuits, rigidity, and multiparty communication complexity. For a survey of correlation bounds and discussions of these connections we refer the reader to

[
Vio09a
,
Vio17
,
Vio21b
]
.

For polynomials of degree larger than
\log_{2}n
, the state-of-the-art remains the lower bound proved by Razborov and Smolensky in the 1980s’
[
Raz87
,
Smo87
]
, showing that for any degree-
d
polynomial
p
and an explicit function
h
(in fact, majority) we have:

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p(x)=h(x)]\leq 1/2+O(d/\sqrt{n}).

Viola
[
Vio21a
]
recently showed that upper bounds on
L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F})
imply correlation bounds between
\mathcal{F}
and an explicit function
h_{k}
that is related to majority and is defined as

h_{k}(x):=\textnormal{sgn}\Biggl(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr).

In particular, proving
6
or related conjectures implies new correlation bounds beating Razborov–Smolensky. The formal statement of the connection is given by the following theorem.

Theorem 15

(Theorem 1 in
[
Vio21a
]
)
.

For every
k\in[n]
and
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\{f\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}\}
, there is a distribution
D_{k}
on
\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{x\sim D_{k}}\left[f(x)=h_{k}(x)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{e^{k}}{2\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}}}L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F}).

For example, if
k=2
and we assume that the answer to
6
is positive, then the right-hand side above becomes
1/2+O(d^{2}/n)
, which is a quadratic improvement over the bound by Razborov and Smolensky.

Therefore,
Theorems

8
and
10
imply correlation bounds between these polynomials and an explicit function that are better than
O(d/\sqrt{n})
given in
[
Raz87
,
Smo87
]
.
We note that via a connection in
[
Vio09b
]
, existing PRGs for these polynomials already imply strong correlation bounds between these polynomials and the class of
\mathsf{NP}
.
Our results apply to more general classes via the composition theorem, where it is not clear if previous techniques applied. For a concrete example, consider the composition of a degree-
(n^{\alpha})
symmetric polynomial with degree-
(n^{\alpha})
read-
(n^{\alpha})
polynomials.
Theorem

13
shows that such polynomial has
L_{1,2}\leq n^{O(\alpha)}
. For a sufficiently small
\alpha=\Omega(1)
, we again obtain correlation bounds improving on Razborov–Smolensky.

1.5
Related work

We close this introduction by discussing a recent work of Girish, Tal and Wu
[
GTW21
]
on parity decision trees that is related to our results.

Parity decision trees are a generalization of decision trees in which each node queries a parity of some input bits rather than a single input bit.
The class of depth-
d
parity decision trees is a subclass of
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
degree-
d
polynomials, as such a parity decision tree can be expressed as a sum of products of sums over
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
, where each product corresponds to a path in the tree (and hence gives rise to
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-monomials of degree at most
d
).
The Fourier spectrum of parity decision trees was first studied in
[
BTW15
]
, which obtained
a level-
1
bound of
O(\sqrt{d})
.
This bound was recently extended to higher levels in
[
GTW21
]
, showing that any depth-
d
parity decision tree
T
over
n
variables has
L_{1,k}(T)\leq d^{k/2}\cdot O(k\log n)^{k}
.

2
Our techniques

We now briefly explain the approaches used to prove our results.
We note that each of these results is obtained using very different ingredients, and hence the results can be read independently of each other.

2.1
Symmetric polynomials (
Theorems

8
and
9
,
Section

4
)

The starting point of our proof is a result from
[
BGL06
]
, which says that degree-
d
symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials only depend on the Hamming weight of their input modulo
m
for some
m
(a power of two) which is
\Theta(d)
, and the converse is also true. (See
Lemma

17
for the exact statement.)
We now explain how to prove our upper and lower bounds given this.

For the upper bound (
Theorem

8
), since
p(x)
takes the same value for all strings
x
with the same weights
\ell\bmod m
, to analyze
L_{1,k}(p)
it suffices to analyze
\mathop{\bf E\/}[(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}]
conditioned on
x
having Hamming weight exactly
\ell\bmod m
.

We bound this conditional expectation by considering separately two cases depending on whether or not
k\leq n/m^{2}
.
For the case that
k\leq n/m^{2}
, we use a (slight sharpening of a) result from
[
BHLV19
]
, which gives a bound of
m^{-k}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})}
.
In the other case, that
k\geq n/m^{2}
, in
Lemma

19
we prove a bound of
O(km/n)^{k}
. This is established via a careful argument that gives a new bound on the Kravchuk polynomial in certain ranges (see
22
), extending and sharpening similar bounds that were recently established in
[
CPTS21
]
(the bounds of
[
CPTS21
]
would not suffice for our purposes).

In each of the above two cases, summing over all the
\binom{n}{k}
coefficients gives the desired bound of
O(m)^{k}=O(d)^{k}
.

For the lower bound (
Theorem

9
), we first consider the symmetric function
h_{k}(x):=\textnormal{sgn}(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}})
. (Note that
h_{1}
is the majority function.)
As
h_{k}
is the sign of a degree-
k
real polynomial (i.e. a degree-
k
polynomial threshold function), it follows from a consequence of the hypercontractive inequality that
L_{1,k}(h_{k})\geq e^{-k}\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}
.
We define our polynomial
p
so that on inputs
x
whose Hamming weight
\lvert x\rvert
is within
m=\Theta(\sqrt{kn})
from
n/2
, it agrees with
h_{k}
, and on other inputs its value is defined such that
p
only depends on
\lvert x\rvert\bmod 2m
. By the converse of
[
BGL06
]
,
p
can be computed by a symmetric polynomial of degree less than
2m
.
Our lower bound then follows from
L_{1,k}(f)\leq\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}
for any Boolean function
f
, and that by our choice of
m
, the two functions
p
and
h_{k}
disagree on
e^{-\Omega(k)}
fraction of the inputs.

2.2
Read-
\Delta
polynomials (
Theorem

10
,
Section

5
)

Writing
f:=(-1)^{p}
for an
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial
p
, we observe that the coefficient
\widehat{f}(S)
is simply the bias of
p_{S}(x):=p(x)+\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}
.
Our high-level approach is to decompose the read-few polynomial
p_{S}
into many disjoint components, then show that each component has small bias.
Since the components are disjoint, the product of these biases gives an upper bound on the bias of
p_{S}
.

In more detail, we first partition the variables according to the minimum degree
t_{i}
of the monomials containing each variable
x_{i}
.
Then we start decomposing
p_{S}
by collecting all the monomials in
p
containing
x_{i}
to form the polynomial
p_{i}
.
We observe that the larger
t_{i}
is, the more likely
p_{i}
is to vanish on a random input, and therefore the closer
p_{i}+x_{i}
is to being unbiased.
For most
S
, we can pick many such
p_{i}
’s (
i\in S
) from
p
so that they are disjoint.
For the remaining polynomial
r
, because
\Delta
and
d
are small, we can further decompose
r
into many disjoint polynomials
r_{i}
.
Finally, our upper bound on
|\widehat{f}(S)|
will be the magnitude of the product of the biases of the
p_{i}
’s and
r_{i}
’s.
We note that our decomposition of
p
uses the structure of
S
; and so the upper bound on
\widehat{f}(S)
depends on
S
(see
Lemma

27
).
Summing over each
\lvert\widehat{f}(S)\rvert
gives our upper bound.

2.3
Composition theorem (
Theorem

13
,
Section

6
)

As a warmup, let us first consider directly computing a degree-1 Fourier coefficient
\widehat{h}(\{(i,j)\})
of the composition.
Since the inner functions
g_{i}
depend on disjoint variables, by writing the outer function
f
in its Fourier expansion, it is not hard to see that

\widehat{h}(\{(i,j)\})=\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat{f}(S)\prod_{\ell\in S\setminus\{i\}}\mathop{\bf E\/}[g_{\ell}]\cdot\widehat{g}_{i}(\{j\}).

When the
g_{i}
’s are balanced, i.e.
\mathop{\bf E\/}[g_{i}]=0
, we have
\widehat{f}(\{(i,j)\})=\widehat{f}(\{i\})\widehat{g}_{i}(\{j\})
, and it follows that
L_{1,1}(h)\leq L_{1,1}(\mathcal{F})L_{1,1}(\mathcal{G})
.
To handle the unbalanced case, we apply an idea from
[
CHHL19
]
that lets us relate
\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat{f}(S)\prod_{\ell\in S\setminus\{i\}}\mathop{\bf E\/}[g_{\ell}]
to the average of
\widehat{f_{R}}(\{i\})
, for some suitably chosen random restriction
R
on
f
(see
33
).
As
\mathcal{F}
is closed under restrictions, we can apply the
L_{1,1}(\mathcal{F})
bound on
f_{R}
, which in turns gives a bound on
\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat{f}(S)\prod_{\ell\in S\setminus\{i\}}\mathop{\bf E\/}[g_{\ell}]
in terms of
L_{1,1}(\mathcal{F})
and
\mathop{\bf E\/}[g_{i}]
.

Bounding
L_{1,k}(h)
for
k\geq 2
is more complicated, as each
\widehat{h}(S)
involves
\widehat{f}(J)
and
\widehat{g}_{i}(T)
’s, where the sets
J
and
T
have different sizes.
We provide more details in
Section

6
.

3
Preliminaries

Notation.

For a string
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we write
\lvert x\rvert
to denote its Hamming weight
\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
.
We use
\mathcal{X}_{w}
to denote
\{x:\lvert x\rvert=w\}
, the set of
n
-bit strings with Hamming weight
w
, and
\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}=\bigcup_{w:w\equiv\ell\bmod m}\mathcal{X}_{w}=\{x:\lvert x\rvert\equiv\ell\bmod m\}
.

We recall that for an
n
-variable Boolean function
f
, the
level-
k
Fourier
L_{1}
norm of
f
is

L_{1,k}(f)=\sum_{S\subset[n]:\lvert S\rvert=k}\lvert\widehat{f}(S)\rvert.

We note that a function
f
and its negation have the same
L_{1,k}
for
k\geq 1
.
Hence we can often assume that
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1]\leq 1/2
, or replace the occurrence of
\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1]
in a bound by
\min\{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1],\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=0]\}
for a
\{0,1\}
-valued function
f
(or by
\min\{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1],\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=-1]\}
for a
\{-1,1\}
-valued function).
If
f
is a
\{-1,1\}
-valued function then
\frac{1-\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert}{2}
is equal to
\min\{\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=1],\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[f=-1]\}
, and we will often write
\frac{1-\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert}{2}
for convenience.

Unless otherwise indicated, we will use the letters
p,q,r
, etc. to denote
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials (with inputs in
\{0,1\}^{n}
and outputs in
\{0,1\}
) and the letters
f,g,h,
etc. to denote general Boolean functions (where the inputs may be
\{0,1\}^{n}
or
\{-1,1\}^{n}
and the outputs may be
\{0,1\}
or
\{-1,1\}
depending on convenience).
We note that changing from
\{0,1\}
outputs to
\{-1,1\}
outputs only changes
L_{1,k}
by a factor of
2
.

We use standard multilinear monomial notation as follows: given a vector
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})
and a subset
T\subseteq[n]
, we write
\beta^{T}
to denote
\prod_{j\in T}\beta_{j}
.

4
L_{1,k}
bounds for symmetric polynomials

The main result of this section is
Theorem

16
, which gives an upper bound on
L_{1,k}(p)
for any symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial
p
of degree
d
, covering the entire range of parameters
1\leq k,d\leq n
:

Theorem 16

(Restatement of
Theorem

8
)
.

Let
p\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree
d
.
For every
1\leq k,d\leq n
,

L_{1,k}(p):=\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}\lvert\widehat{p}(S)\rvert\leq\mathop{\bf Pr\/}[p(x)=1]\cdot O(d)^{k}.

Proof idea.

As the polynomial
p
is symmetric, its Fourier coefficient
\widehat{p}(S)
only depends on
|S|
, the size of
S
.
Hence to bound
L_{1,k}
it suffices to analyze the coefficient
\widehat{p}(\{1,\ldots,k\})=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[p(x)(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}]
.

Our proof uses a result from
[
BGL06
]
(
Lemma

17
below), which says that degree-
d
symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials only depend on the Hamming weight of their input modulo
m
for some
m=O(d)
.
Given this, since
p(x)
takes the same value for strings
x
with the same weights
\ell\bmod m
, we can in turn bound each
\mathop{\bf E\/}[(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}]
conditioned on
x
having Hamming weight exactly
\ell\bmod m
, i.e.
x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}
.
We consider two cases depending on whether or not
k\leq n/m^{2}
.
If
k\leq n/m^{2}
, we can apply a (slight sharpening of a) result from
[
BHLV19
]
, which gives a bound of
m^{-k}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})}
.
If
k\geq n/m^{2}
, in
Lemma

19
we prove a bound of
O(km/n)^{k}
.
In each case, summing over all the
\binom{n}{k}
coefficients gives the desired bound of
O(m)^{k}=O(d)^{k}
.

We now give some intuition for
Lemma

19
, which upper bounds the magnitude of the ratio

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}[(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}]=\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}

(2)

by
O(km/n)^{k}
.
Let us first consider
k=1
and
m=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.
As most strings
x
have Hamming weight within
[n/2-\Theta(\sqrt{n}),n/2+\Theta(\sqrt{n})]
, it is natural to think about the weight
\lvert x\rvert
in the form of
n/2+m\mathbbm{Z}+\ell^{\prime}
.
It is easy to see that the denominator is at least
\Omega(2^{n}/\sqrt{n})
,
so we focus on bounding the numerator.
Consider the quantity
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{n/2+s}}\mathop{\bf E\/}[(-1)^{x_{1}}]
for some
s
.
As we are summing over all strings of the same Hamming weight, we can instead consider
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{n/2+s}}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{i\sim[n]}[(-1)^{x_{i}}]
(see
Equation

5
).
For any string of weight
n/2+s
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf E\/}_{i\sim[n]}[(-1)^{x_{i}}]=(1/2-s/n)-(1/2+s/n)=-2s/n.

(3)

Therefore, in the
k=1
case we get that

\bigg\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}[(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}]\bigg\rvert\leq 2\sum_{c}\binom{n}{n/2+cm+\ell^{\prime}}\frac{\lvert cm+\ell^{\prime}\rvert}{n}.

Using the fact that
\binom{n}{n/2+cm+\ell^{\prime}}
is exponentially decreasing in
\lvert c\rvert
, in
20
we show that this is at most
O(2^{n}/n)
.
So the ratio in
eq.

2
is at most
O(1/\sqrt{n})
, as desired, when
k=1.

However, already for
k=2
, a direct (but tedious) calculation shows that

\displaystyle\mathop{\bf E\/}_{i<j}[(-1)^{x_{i}+x_{j}}]=\frac{4s^{2}-2ns+n}{n(n-1)},

(4)

which no longer decreases in
s
like in
eq.

3
.
Nevertheless, we observe that this is bounded by
O(1/n+(\lvert s\rvert/n)^{2})
, which is sufficient for bounding the ratio by
O(1/n)
.
Building on this, for any
k
we obtain a bound of
2^{O(k)}((k/n)^{k/2}+(\lvert s\rvert/n))^{k}
in
22
, and by a more careful calculation we are able to obtain the desired bound of
O(km/n)^{k}
on
Equation

2
.

4.1
Proof of
Theorem

16

We now prove the theorem.
We will use the following result from
[
BGL06
]
, which says that degree-
d
symmetric

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomials only depend on their input’s Hamming weight modulo

O(d)
.

Lemma 17

(Corollaries 2.5 and 2.7 in
[
BGL06
]
,
p=2
)
.

Let
p\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be any function and
m
be a power of two.
If
p
is a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree
d
, where
m/2\leq d<m
, then
p(x)
only depends on
\lvert x\rvert\bmod m
.
Conversely, if
p(x)
only depends on
\lvert x\rvert\bmod m
, then it can be computed by a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree less than
m
.

We will also use two bounds on the biases of parities under the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}
, one holds for
k\leq n/(2d)^{2}\leq n/m^{2}
(
18
) and the other for
k\geq n/(2d)^{2}\geq n/(4m^{2})
(
Lemma

19
).

18
is essentially taken from
[
BHLV19
]
.
However, the statement in
[
BHLV19
]
has a slightly worse bound, so for completeness we provide a self-contained proof below to give the bound of
18
.
The proof of
Lemma

19
involves bounding the magnitude of Kravchuk polynomials.
As it is somewhat technical we defer its proof to
Section

4.2
.

Claim 18

(Lemma 10 in
[
BHLV19
]
)
.

For every
1\leq k\leq n/m^{2}
and every integer
\ell
,

2^{-n}\Big\lvert\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}\Big\rvert\leq m^{-(k+1)}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})},

while for
k=0
,

\Big\lvert 2^{-n}\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert-1/m\Big\rvert\leq m^{-1}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})}.

Lemma 19
.

For
k\geq n/(4m^{2})
, we have

\binom{n}{k}\cdot\max_{\ell}\left\lvert\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}\right\rvert\leq O(m)^{k}.

We now use
18
and
Lemma

19
to prove
Theorem

16
.

Proof of
Theorem

16
.

As
p
is symmetric, all the level-
k
coefficients are the same, so
it suffices to give a bound on
\widehat{p}(\{1,2,\ldots,k\})
.
Let
\tilde{p}\colon\{0,\ldots,n\}\to\{0,1\}
be the function defined by
\tilde{p}(\lvert x\rvert):=p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
By
Lemma

17
, we have
\tilde{p}(\ell)=\tilde{p}(\ell\bmod m)
for some
d<m\leq 2d
where
m
is a power of
2
.
Using the definition of
\widehat{p}(\{1,\ldots,k\})
, we have

\displaystyle\lvert\widehat{p}(\{1,\ldots,k\})\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}\bigl[p(x)(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}\bigr]\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert\sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}\tilde{p}(\ell)\frac{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}{2^{n}}\cdot\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]\cdot\max_{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}\left\lvert\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}\right\rvert,

where we use the shorthand
\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x\sim\{0,1\}^{n}}[p(x)]
in the last step.

When
k\leq n/(2d)^{2}\leq n/m^{2}
, by
18
(using the first bound for the numerator and the second
k=0
bound for the denominator) we have

\max_{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}\left\lvert\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}\right\rvert\leq\frac{m^{-(k+1)}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})}}{m^{-1}(1-e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})})}\\
\leq O(1)\cdot m^{-k}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})},

where the last inequality holds because
1\leq k\leq n/m^{2}
and hence the
(1-e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})})
factor in the denominator of the left-hand side is
\Omega(1)
.
Hence, summing over all the
\binom{n}{k}
level-
k
coefficients, we get that

L_{1,k}(p)\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]\cdot\binom{n}{k}\cdot O(1)\cdot m^{-k}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})}\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]\cdot O(1)\cdot m^{k}\left(\frac{ne}{km^{2}}\right)^{k}e^{-\Omega(n/m^{2})}\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]\cdot O(m)^{k},

where the last inequality is because for constant
c
, the function
(x/k)^{k}e^{-cx}
is maximized when
x=k/c
, and is
O(1)^{k}
.

When
k\geq n/(2d)^{2}\geq n/(4m^{2})
, by
Lemma

19
we have

L_{1,k}(p)\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]\cdot\binom{n}{k}\max_{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}\left\lvert\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}\right\rvert\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}[p]\cdot O(m)^{k}.\qed

It remains to prove
18
and
Lemma

19
.

Proof of
18
.

Consider an expansion of

p(y)=(1-y)^{k}(1+y)^{n-k}

into
2^{n}
terms indexed by
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
where
x_{i}=0
indicates that we take the term 1 from the
i
-th factor.
It is easy to see that the coefficient of
y^{d}
is

\sum_{\lvert x\rvert=d}(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}}
, where
\lvert x\rvert
denotes the number of occurrences of
1
in
x
.
Denote
\zeta:=e^{2\pi i/m}
as an
m
-th root of unity.
Recall the identity

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\zeta^{j(d-\ell)}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $d\equiv\ell\pmod{m}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus the sum we want to bound is equal to

\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\zeta^{-j\ell}p(\zeta^{j}).

Note that for
j=0
we have
p(\zeta^{0})=p(1)=0
for
k\neq 0
while
p(\zeta^{0})=2^{n}
for
k=0
.
We now bound above
p(\zeta^{j})
for
j>0
.
As
\lvert 1+e^{i\theta}\rvert=2\lvert\cos(\theta/2)\rvert
and
\lvert 1-e^{i\theta}\rvert=2\lvert\sin(\theta/2)\rvert
we have

\lvert p(\zeta^{j})\rvert=\lvert 1-\zeta^{j}\rvert^{k}\lvert 1+\zeta^{j}\rvert^{n-k}=2^{n}\Bigl|\sin\frac{j\pi}{m}\Bigr|^{k}\Bigl|\cos\frac{j\pi}{m}\Bigr|^{n-k}.

Therefore, for
k\neq 0
we have

\left\lvert\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\lvert\zeta^{j\ell}\cdot p(\zeta^{j})\rvert\leq\frac{2^{n}}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big\lvert\cos\frac{j\pi}{m}\Big\rvert^{n-k}\Big\lvert\sin\frac{j\pi}{m}\Big\rvert^{k}.

For
0<\theta<\pi/2
, we have
\cos\theta\leq e^{-\theta^{2}/2}

and
\sin\theta\leq\theta
.
So

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big\lvert\cos\frac{j\pi}{m}\Big\rvert^{n-k}\Big\lvert\sin\frac{j\pi}{m}\Big\rvert^{k}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{0<j<m/2}\Bigl(\cos\frac{j\pi}{m}\Bigr)^{n-k}\Bigl(\sin\frac{j\pi}{m}\Bigr)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sum_{0<j<m/2}e^{-\frac{n-k}{2}\bigl(\frac{j\pi}{m}\bigr)^{2}}\Bigl(\frac{j\pi}{m}\Bigr)^{k}.

We now observe that each term in the above summation is at most half of the previous one. This is because for every
j\geq 1
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(n-k)(j+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}{2m^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{(j+1)\pi}{m}\Bigr)^{k}}{\exp\Bigl(-\frac{(n-k)j^{2}\pi^{2}}{2m^{2}}\Bigr)\Bigl(\frac{j\pi}{m}\Bigr)^{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\pi^{2}(n-k)}{m^{2}}j\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(1+\frac{1}{j}\Bigr)^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{\pi^{2}(n-k)}{m^{2}}\Bigr)\cdot 2^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{3\pi^{2}}{4}k\Bigr)\cdot 2^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\Bigl(-\frac{3\pi^{2}}{4}\Bigr)\cdot 2

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2},

where the third step holds because w.l.o.g.
m\geq 2
and
1\leq k\leq n/m^{2}\leq n/4
, and hence
(n-k)/m^{2}\geq 3k/4
.
For these reasons, we can deduce that

\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\Big\lvert\cos\frac{j\pi}{m}\Big\rvert^{n-k}\Big\lvert\sin\frac{j\pi}{m}\Big\rvert^{k}\leq 2e^{-\frac{\pi^{2}(n-k)}{2m^{2}}}\Bigl(\frac{\pi}{m}\Bigr)^{k}\sum_{1\leq j<m/2}2^{1-j}\leq m^{-k}\exp\bigl(-\Omega(n/m^{2})\bigr).

For
k=0
we also need to include the term
p(\zeta^{0})=p(1)=2^{n}
which divided by
m
gives the term
2^{n}/m
.
∎

4.2
Proof of
Lemma

19

In this section, we prove
Lemma

19
.
Let
\mathcal{K}(n,k,w):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{w}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}
be the
Kravchuk polynomial
.
We recall the “symmetry relation” for this polynomial (see e.g.
[

):

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\mathcal{K}(n,k,w)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{w}}(-1)^{\langle y,x\rangle}=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{w}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}(-1)^{\langle x,y\rangle}=\binom{n}{w}\mathcal{K}(n,w,k).

(5)

Using
Equation

, we have

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\max_{0\leq\ell\leq m-1}\left\lvert\frac{\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}}}{\lvert\mathcal{X}_{\ell\bmod m}\rvert}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\max_{\ell}\frac{\lvert\sum_{w\equiv\ell\bmod m}\binom{n}{k}\mathcal{K}(n,k,w)\rvert}{\sum_{w\equiv\ell\bmod m}\binom{n}{w}}

\displaystyle=\max_{\ell}\frac{\lvert\sum_{w\equiv\ell\bmod m}\binom{n}{w}\mathcal{K}(n,w,k)\rvert}{\sum_{w\equiv\ell\bmod m}\binom{n}{w}}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\ell}\frac{\sum_{c}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}\lvert\mathcal{K}(n,\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell,k)\rvert}{\sum_{c}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\max_{\ell}\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}\lvert\mathcal{K}(n,\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell,k)\rvert}{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}},

(6)

where the indices of
c
are integers that iterate over
\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
, and the last line uses the symmetry of
{n\choose w}\mathcal{K}(n,w,k)
around

Later in this section, we will prove (in

) that

\lvert\mathcal{K}(n,\lfloor n/2\rfloor+s,k)\rvert\leq 2^{O(k)}\left(\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}+\left(\frac{\lvert s\rvert}{k}\right)^{k}\right).

Plugging this bound into
Equation

6
, we have

eq.

6

\displaystyle\leq 2^{O(k)}\max_{\ell}\left(\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}\bigl((n/k)^{k/2}+((cm+\ell)/k)^{k}\bigr)}{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}\right)

\displaystyle=O(n/k)^{\frac{k}{2}}+\frac{2^{O(k)}}{k^{k}}\max_{\ell}\left(\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}(cm+\ell)^{k}}{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}\right).

(7)

We will prove the following claim which bounds the ratio of the two summations.

Claim 20
.

For any
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
and

, we have

\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}(cm+\ell)^{k}}{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}\leq O(mk)^{k}.

Therefore, as

, we have

\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:bias2}\leq O(n/k)^{\frac{k}{2}}+\frac{2^{O(k)}}{k^{k}}\cdot O(mk)^{k}=O(m)^{k}.

This completes the proof of
Lemma

. ∎

It remains to prove

and
22
. We begin with

.

Proof of
20
.

Fix any
\ell\in\{0,\ldots,m-1\}
, we first bound

\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}(cm+\ell)^{k}}{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}

with some quantity that does not depend on
\ell
.
First, observe that the denominator is at least
\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\ell}
.
Next, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\ell+cm}}{\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\ell}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{cm}\left(\frac{n-\lfloor n/2\rfloor-\ell-cm+i}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\ell+i}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{cm}\left(1-\frac{2\ell+cm+\lfloor n/2\rfloor-\lceil n/2\rceil}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\ell+i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{2\ell+cm-1}{n}\right)^{cm}

\displaystyle\leq e\cdot\exp(-(cm)^{2}/n)

\displaystyle(e^{cm/n}\leq e)

\displaystyle\leq e\cdot\exp(-(c^{2}/4k))

Finally, we have

Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}(cm+\ell)^{k}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}{\sum_{c\geq 0}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sum_{c\geq 0}(cm+\ell)^{k}\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+cm+\ell}}{\binom{n}{\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq m^{k}+e\sum_{c\geq 1}(2cm)^{k}e^{-\frac{c^{2}}{4k}}

\displaystyle=m^{k}+e(2m)^{k}\sum_{c\geq 1}c^{k}e^{-\frac{c^{2}}{4k}}.

In

.

Claim 21
.

\sum_{c\geq 1}c^{k}e^{-\frac{c^{2}}{4k}}\leq O(k^{k})
for any
k\geq 1
.

Proof.

Consider the function

\lambda_{a}(x):=x^{k}\cdot e^{-ax^{2}},

for some parameter
a>0
. Its derivative is
\lambda_{a}^{\prime}(x)=(k-2ax^{2})x^{k-1}e^{-ax^{2}}
; so for
x\geq 0
,
\lambda_{a}(x)
is increasing when
x\leq\sqrt{k/(2a)}
, and is decreasing when
x\geq\sqrt{k/(2a)}
. For such a (nonnegative)
first-increasing-then-decreasing
function, it can be seen that

\displaystyle\sum_{c\geq 1}\lambda_{a}(c)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\vphantom{\int_{0}^{\infty}}\max\{\lambda_{a}(x):x\geq 0\}}_{\textup{A}}+\underbrace{\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda_{a}(x)dx}_{\textup{B}}.

The first term
section

4.2
is exactly equal to

\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{term:T1}=\lambda_{a}\left(\sqrt{k/(2a)}\right)=\left(\frac{k}{2ea}\right)^{k/2}.

Using the substitution
u=ax^{2}
, the second term
section

4.2
is equal to

\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{term:T2}=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k}e^{-ax^{2}}dx=\frac{1}{2}a^{-\frac{k+1}{2}}\cdot\Gamma\left(\frac{k+1}{2}\right).

where
\Gamma(k):=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{k-1}e^{-x}dx
is the Gamma function.
It is known (see, for example,
[
OLBC10
, Equation (5.6.1)]
) that
\Gamma(\frac{k+1}{2})\leq\sqrt{2\pi}\cdot e^{\frac{1}{12}}\cdot(\frac{k}{2e})^{k/2}
for each integer
k\geq 1
. Plugging this upper bound into the above formula gives

\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{term:T2}\leq e^{\frac{1}{12}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2a}}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{2ea}\right)^{k/2}\leq\frac{2}{\sqrt{a}}\cdot\left(\frac{k}{2ea}\right)^{k/2}.

By setting
a=1/(4k)
, we deduce that

\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{term:T1}+\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{term:T2}\leq\bigl(1+4\sqrt{k}\bigr)\cdot\left(2k^{2}/e\right)^{k/2}=\frac{1+4\sqrt{k}}{(e/2)^{k/2}}\cdot k^{k}=O(1)\cdot k^{k},

proving the claim.
∎

It remains to prove
22
, which gives a bound on
\mathcal{K}(n,w,k):=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}(-1)^{x_{1}+\cdots+x_{w}}

Claim 22
.

\lvert\mathcal{K}(n,\lfloor n/2\rfloor+s,k)\rvert\leq 2^{O(k)}((n/k)^{k/2}+(\lvert s\rvert/k)^{k})
for any
k\geq 1.

A similar claim was also made in
[

, Claim 4.10]
, but the bounds we obtain here are more general and sharper for larger

We start with a preliminary claim.

Claim 23
.

For any positive integer
s
,

\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{n^{i}}{i!}\frac{s^{k-2i}}{(k-2i)!}\leq\begin{cases}(2e)^{k}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{k/2}&\text{if $s\leq\sqrt{nk}$}\\
(2e)^{k}\left(\frac{s}{k}\right)^{k}&\text{if $s\geq\sqrt{nk}$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Write
s=(cn)^{1/2}
for some

.
Then
n^{i}s^{k-2i}=(cn)^{k/2}/c^{i}
, and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{n^{i}}{i!}\frac{s^{k-2i}}{(k-2i)!}=(cn)^{\frac{k}{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{1}{c^{i}i!(k-2i)!}.

(8)

We can write

\frac{1}{i!(k-2i)!}=\frac{(2i)!}{k!i!}\frac{k!}{(2i)!(k-2i)!}=\frac{(2i)!}{k!i!}\binom{k}{2i}.

So

eq.

8

\displaystyle=\frac{(cn)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{k!}\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{(2i)!}{c^{i}i!}\binom{k}{2i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(cn)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{k!}\max_{0\leq i\leq\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\left(\frac{2i}{c}\right)^{i}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\binom{k}{2i}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{k}\cdot\frac{(cn)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{k!}\max_{0\leq i\leq\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\left(\frac{2i}{c}\right)^{i}.

If
c\leq k

2^{k}\cdot\frac{(cn)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{k!}\max_{0\leq i\leq\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor}\left(\frac{2i}{c}\right)^{i}\leq 2^{k}\frac{(cn)^{\frac{k}{2}}}{k!}\left(\frac{k}{c}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}\leq(2e)^{k}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{k/2}.

If
c\geq k

Proof of

.

Observe that

Our goal is to bound above its

-th coefficient.
We will assume

.
By the symmetry of

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{K}(n,n/2-s,k)x^{k}=(1-x)^{\frac{n}{2}-s}(1+x)^{\frac{n}{2}+s}=(1-x^{2})^{\frac{n}{2}}\left(\frac{1+x}{1-x}\right)^{s}.

The

otherwise (because the function is even).

Since

Therefore, the magnitude of the

\displaystyle\lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:kravchuk2}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}\frac{n^{i}}{i!}\frac{(\lvert s\rvert+k-2i-1)^{k-2i}}{(k-2i)!}2^{k-2i}.

4.3
Proof of
Theorem

9

In this subsection, we give examples of symmetric polynomials
p
with
L_{1,k}(p)=\Omega_{k}(1)\cdot d^{k}
for
d=\Theta(\sqrt{n})
, matching our upper bound up to a constant factor when
k=O(1)
.
We restate our theorem for convenience.

Theorem 24

(Restatement of
Theorem

9
)
.

For every
k
, there is a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of degree
d=\Theta(\sqrt{kn})
such that
L_{1,k}(p)\geq(e^{-k}/2)\cdot\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}=\Omega_{k}(1)\cdot d^{k}
.

Proof.

Let
m:=C\sqrt{kn}
for some constant
4\leq C\leq 8
such that
m
is a power of two.
Consider the function
h_{k}\colon\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
defined by

h_{k}(x)=\textnormal{sgn}\Bigg(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}(-1)^{\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}}\Bigg).

Let
\widetilde{h_{k}}\colon\{0,\ldots,n\}\to\{-1,1\}
be the symmetrization of
h_{k}
so that
\widetilde{h_{k}}(\lvert x\rvert):=h_{k}(x)
.
Now consider the periodic function
\widetilde{p}\colon\{0,\ldots,n\}\to\{-1,1\}
that on input
z\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
such that

z+2tm\in[n/2-m,n/2+m)\text{ for some $t\in\mathbbm{Z}$},

evaluates to
\widetilde{p}(z):=\widetilde{h}_{k}(z+2tm)
.
We define the
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial
p
by
p(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}):=\tilde{p}(\lvert x\rvert)
.
Since
p(x)
depends only on
\lvert x\rvert\bmod 2m
, by
Lemma

17
, it can be computed by a symmetric
\mathbbm{F}_{2}
-polynomial of degree
d<2m
.
Moreover,
p(x)
agrees with
h_{k}(x)
when
\lvert x\rvert\in[n/2-m,n/2+m)
.
Let
f=(-1)^{p}
.
We have (note that from here on we switch from
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to
x\in\{-1,1\}^{n}
)

\displaystyle L_{1,k}(f)\geq\Bigg\lvert\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}\widehat{f}(S)\Bigg\rvert

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lvert\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Bigl[f(x)x^{S}\Bigr]\Bigg\rvert

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lvert\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Bigl[h_{k}(x)x^{S}\Bigr]+\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Bigl[\bigl(f(x)-h_{k}(x)\bigr)x^{S}\Bigr]\Bigg\rvert

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[h_{k}(x)\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr]+\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[\bigl(f(x)-h_{k}(x)\bigr)\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr]\Bigg\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\left|\Bigg\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[h_{k}(x)\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr]\Bigg\rvert-\Bigg\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[\bigl(f(x)-h_{k}(x)\bigr)\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr]\Bigg\rvert\right|.

We proceed to lower bound the first term and upper bound the second term.
For the first term we use the same lower bound of
e^{-k}\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}
in the proof of
[
Vio21a
, Theorem 1]
;
for completeness we include the argument here.
One can verify that
\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}[(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S})^{2}]=\binom{n}{k}
.
Applying
[
O’D14
, Theorem 9.22]
, which states that
\mathop{\bf E\/}[\lvert h(x)\rvert]\geq e^{-k}\mathop{\bf E\/}[h(x)^{2}]^{1/2}
for any
h\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\mathbbm{R}
of degree
k
, we get

\displaystyle e^{-k}\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}=e^{-k}\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]^{1/2}\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[\Bigg\lvert\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Bigg\rvert\Biggr]\leq\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]^{1/2}=\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}.

So for the first term we have

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[h_{k}(x)\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr]=\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\left[\Bigg\lvert\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Bigg\rvert\right]\geq e^{-k}\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}.

We now bound above the second term.
As
\lvert f(x)-h_{k}(x)\rvert\leq 2
for every
x
, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we get

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\Biggl[\bigl(f(x)-h_{k}(x)\bigr)\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr]\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{x}\biggl[2\Big\lvert\sum_{i}x_{i}\Big\rvert\geq m\biggr]\mathop{\bf E\/}_{x}\left[\Biggl(\sum_{\lvert S\rvert=k}x^{S}\Biggr)^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle=2\cdot\mathop{\bf Pr\/}_{x}\biggl[2\Big\lvert\sum_{i}x_{i}\Big\rvert\geq m\biggr]\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq(e^{-k}/2)\cdot\binom{n}{k}^{1/2},

where the last inequality follows from the Chernoff bound recalling our choice of
m=\Theta(\sqrt{kn})
.
Therefore
L_{1,k}(f)\geq(e^{-k}/2)\cdot\binom{n}{k}^{1/2}
, proving the theorem.
∎

\Delta

Corollary 26

\Delta

For any

Proof.

Let
R

\rho
-random restriction that independently for each

with probability
1-\rho

x_{i}
to a uniform random bit and keeps
x_{i}
alive with the remaining
\rho

For any
f\colon\{-1,1\}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}

\mathop{\bf E\/}_{R}[\widehat{f_{R}}(S)]=\rho^{\lvert S\rvert}\widehat{f}(S)

Proof idea.

We first observe that for

, the Fourier coefficient
\widehat{f}(S)
is simply the bias of the

-polynomial

.
Assuming that

so that each of them depends on disjoint variables, and it is not hard to show that the product of the biases of these polynomials upper bounds the bias of
p_{S}
.
From this it is easy to see that any read-
\Delta
degree-
d
polynomial has bias
\exp(2^{-d}n/\textnormal{poly}(\Delta,d))

However, this quantity is too large to sum over

Our next idea (

) is to give a more refined decomposition of the polynomial

Suppose the variables

below), as must indeed be the case for most of the

size-

Then we can collect all the monomials containing each

Moreover, if every monomial in

has high degree (see the definition of

with high probability and therefore

is almost unbiased.
Therefore, we can first collect these

and

from

; then, for the remaining

Multiplying the biases of the

We now proceed to the actual proof.

Lemma 27

(Main lemma for read-

Let

Proof of

.

Using a reduction given in the proof of

, it suffices to prove the same bound without the acceptance probability factor, i.e. to prove that for every

As
[

]
did not provide an explicit statement of the reduction, for completeness we provide a self-contained statement and proof in

in

For every subset
S\subseteq[n]
of size
k
, there exists an
\ell\leq k
and
i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in S
such that their pairwise distances in

are at least
6
, each

for some

, and each of the remaining

indices in

is within distance at most

to some

.

Fix any

, and let us bound the number of subsets

of size

that can contain

. Because

can appear in at most

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{\ell}=k-\ell}\prod_{b\in[\ell]}\binom{6(\Delta d)^{5}}{j_{b}}

\displaystyle=\binom{6\ell(\Delta d)^{5}}{k-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq(6(\Delta d)^{5})^{k}\cdot e^{k-\ell}\left(\frac{\ell}{k-\ell}\right)^{k-\ell}

\displaystyle\leq(e\Delta d)^{5k}

different ways, where the equality uses the Vandermonde identity, the first inequality uses

, and the last one uses

Therefore, by

\displaystyle\sum_{S:\lvert S\rvert=k}\lvert\widehat{f}(S)\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{t\subseteq[d]^{\ell}}\left[\Biggl(\prod_{j\in[\ell]}\lvert V_{t_{j}}(p)\rvert\Biggr)\cdot(e\Delta d)^{5k}\cdot O(1)^{k}\Delta^{\ell}\prod_{j^{\prime}\in[\ell]}\left(2^{-t_{j^{\prime}}}\exp\left(-\frac{2^{-{t_{j^{\prime}}}}\lvert V_{t_{j^{\prime}}}(p)\rvert}{\ell(\Delta d)^{4}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq O(1)^{k}\cdot(\Delta d)^{5k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\Delta^{\ell}\cdot d^{\ell}\cdot(\ell(\Delta d)^{4})^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq O(k)^{k}\cdot(\Delta d)^{5k}\cdot(\Delta d)^{5k}

\displaystyle=O(k)^{k}\cdot(\Delta d)^{10k},

where the third inequality is because the function

is maximized when

.
This completes the proof.

Proof idea.

Before proving
Theorem

31

For a subset

, let

denote the

and

Appendix A
Reduction to bound without acceptance probability

In this section, we show that given any
L_{1,k}
Fourier norm bound on a class of functions that is closed under XOR on disjoint variables, such a bound can be automatically “upgraded” to a refined bound that depends on the acceptance probability:

Lemma 36
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of
\{-1,1\}
-valued functions such that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
, the XOR of disjoint copies of
f
(over disjoint sets of variables) also belongs to
\mathcal{F}
.
If
L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F})\leq b^{k}
, then for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
it holds that
L_{1,k}(f)\leq 2e\cdot\frac{1-\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert}{2}\cdot b^{k}
.

Proof.

Suppose not, and let
f\in\mathcal{F}
be such that
L_{1,k}(f)>2e\cdot\frac{1-\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert}{2}\cdot b^{k}
.
We first observe that since
L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F})\leq b^{k}
, it must be the case that
1-\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert\leq 1/e.

Let
\alpha:=\frac{1-\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert}{2}\in[0,\frac{1}{{2e}}]
so that
\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert=1-2\alpha\geq 1-1/e
.
Let
f^{\oplus t}
be the XOR of
t
disjoint copies of
f
on
tn
variables, where the integer
t
is to be determined below.
By our assumption, we have
f^{\oplus t}\in\mathcal{F}
and thus

\displaystyle L_{1,k}(f^{\oplus t})

\displaystyle\geq\binom{t}{1}\cdot L_{1,0}(f)^{t-1}\cdot L_{1,k}(f)

(by disjointness)

\displaystyle=t\cdot(1-2\alpha)^{t-1}\cdot L_{1,k}(f)

(
L_{1,0}(f)=\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]
)

\displaystyle>t\cdot(1-2\alpha)^{t-1}\cdot 2e\cdot\alpha\cdot b^{k}=:\Lambda(t).

We note that if
\alpha=0
then
\lvert\mathop{\bf E\/}[f]\rvert=1
, so all the Fourier weight of
f
is on the constant coefficient, and hence the claimed inequality holds trivially. So we subsequently assume that
0<\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2e}
.
Let
t^{*}:=\frac{1}{-\ln(1-2\alpha)}>0
.
It is easy to verify that
\Lambda(t)
is increasing when
t\leq t^{*}
, and is decreasing when
t\geq t^{*}
.

We choose
t=\lceil t^{*}\rceil
.
Since
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{2e}<\frac{e-1}{2e}\approx 0.3161
, we have
t^{*}>1
and thus

\displaystyle L_{1,k}(f^{\oplus t})>\Lambda(\lceil t^{*}\rceil)\geq\Lambda(t^{*}+1)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{-\ln(1-2\alpha)}+1\right)\cdot(1-2\alpha)^{\frac{1}{-\ln(1-2\alpha)}}\cdot 2e\cdot\alpha\cdot b^{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\alpha}{-\ln(1-2\alpha)}+2\alpha\right)\cdot b^{k}\geq b^{k},

where the last inequality holds for every
\alpha\in(0,\frac{e-1}{2e}]
and can be checked via elementary calculations.
This contradicts
L_{1,k}(\mathcal{F})\leq b^{k}
, and the lemma is proved.
∎
