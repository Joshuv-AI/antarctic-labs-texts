Mean values of multiplicative functions and applications to the distribution of the sum of divisors
Akash Singha Roy

Abstract
. We provide uniform bounds on mean values of multiplicative functions under very general hypotheses, detecting certain power savings missed in known results in the literature. As an application, we study the distribution of the sum-of-divisors function σ ⁡ ( n ) \sigma(n) in coprime residue classes to moduli q ≤ ( log ⁡ x ) K q\leq(\log x)^{K} , obtaining extensions of results of Śliwa that are uniform in a wide range of q q and optimal in various parameters. As a consequence of our results, we obtain that the values of σ ⁡ ( n ) \sigma(n) sampled over n ≤ x n\leq x with σ ⁡ ( n ) \sigma(n) coprime to q q are asymptotically equidistributed among the coprime residue classes mod q q , uniformly for odd q ≤ ( log ⁡ x ) K q\leq(\log x)^{K} . On the other hand, if q q is even, then equidistribution is restored provided we restrict to inputs n n having sufficiently many prime divisors exceeding q q .

1.
Introduction

Mean values of multiplicative functions have been a central topic of interest in analytic number theory. There is already a rich variety of literature on this subject, with the classical result of Halász
[
9
]
(see
[
20
, Corollary III.4.12]
for a precise version) providing a widely applicable general bound on the partial sums of any multiplicative function taking values in the (complex) unit disk, and with the analytic method of Landau–Selberg–Delange (see
[
20
, Chapter II.5]
) providing a highly precise asymptotic formula for the partial sums of multiplicative functions whose Dirichlet series behave like powers of the Riemann zeta function. However in applications, situations arise where Halász’s bound turns out to not be precise enough, while the Landau–Selberg–Delange method despite being applicable leads to additional error terms that become too large to yield useful information. Such situations occur, for instance, while studying the distribution of values of multiplicative functions in residue classes.

In an attempt to address this issue, in recent joint work with Pollack
[
14
]
, we gave an upper bound on the partial sum of a multiplicative function taking values in the unit disk, under some natural control on the average behavior of this function at the primes. Using this result, we studied the distribution of the values of the Alladi-Erdős function
A(n)\coloneqq\sum_{p^{k}\parallel n}k\cdot p
and Euler’s totient function
\varphi(n)\coloneqq\#\{1\leq d\leq n:~\gcd(d,n)=1\}
in residue classes to moduli varying uniformly in a wide range. Our results extended those of Goldfeld
[
8
]
and Narkiewicz
[
12
]
(who studied the corresponding questions for fixed moduli) and had error terms that (for concrete heuristic reasons) could be expected to be essentially optimal. A related function occurring commonly in number theory is the sum-of-divisors function
\sigma(n)\coloneqq\sum_{d\mid n}d
, and Śliwa
[
19
]
studies the distribution of the values of this function among the coprime residue classes to a fixed modulus
q
, obtaining a necessary and sufficient criterion for
\sigma(n)
to be equidistributed among the coprime residue classes mod
q
. Our methods in
[
14
]
, however, are unable to obtain a complete uniform version of Śliwa’s results, primarily because they are unable to detect the necessary power savings in certain naturally-arising character sums. Motivated by this problem, our first main result in this manuscript is one which provides a variant of Theorem 1.1 in
[
14
]
that can detect certain power savings missed in the referred result, and in fact also enables us to obtain a uniform version of Śliwa’s result.

In what follows,
\pi(y)
denotes (as usual) the number of primes up to
y
, and
\mathbb{U}
denotes the unit disk in the complex plane, namely, the set
\{s\in\mathbb{C}:|s|\leq 1\}
. For a given real number
z
, we say that a positive integer
n
is
z
-smooth
if it has no prime factor exceeding
z
. The number of
z
-smooth numbers up to
x
is denoted by
\Psi(x,z)
, a quantity which has been studied extensively in the literature.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{U}
be a multiplicative function and
x,y,z,M
be positive real numbers such that
M\geq 1
,
1<z<x
and
e^{11/2}\leq y\leq z^{1/(18\log\log z)^{2}}
. Assume that there exists a complex number
\varrho\in\mathbb{U}
such that for all
Y\geq y
,

(1)

\sum_{y<p\leq Y}f(p)=\varrho(\pi(Y)-\pi(y))+O(MY\mathcal{E}(y)),

for some decreasing function
\mathcal{E}\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
satisfying
\lim_{X\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}(X)=0
. Then

\sum_{n\leq x}f(n)\ll\frac{|\varrho|x}{\log z}\left(\frac{\log z}{\log y}\right)^{\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{|f(p)|}{p}\right)\\
+~\frac{x(\log y)^{1+|\varrho|}}{(\log z)^{2-\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}}\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{|f(p)|}{p}\right)+\Psi(x,z)+Mx(\log x)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}(y)+\frac{1}{y}\right),

where the implied constant depends at most on the implied constant in (
1
).

A few comments are in order. In applications, the terms involving
\Psi(x,z)
,
\mathcal{E}(y)
and
1/y
often become very small, so the usefulness of the bounds is dictated by the sizes of the first two terms. The crucial difference between this bound and the one in Theorem 1.1 of
[
14
]
is the factor
(\log z)^{\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}
in the first expression, whose analogue in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
contained the larger factor
(\log x)^{|\varrho|}
. These greater savings turn out to play a significant role in certain applications. However, our bound above cannot entirely subsume
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
because the additional term with
(\log y)^{1+|\varrho|}
(which has no analogue in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
) can limit the applicability of our bound. We shall witness these phenomena in our applications (see the remark following the proof of Proposition
5.4
).

Both Theorem
1.1
and the arguments used to prove it can be applied to study the asymptotic distribution of multiplicative functions like the sum-of-divisors function
\sigma(n)
in coprime residue classes to moduli varying uniformly in a wide range. Following Narkiewicz
[
12
]
, we say that an integer-valued arithmetic function
g
is
weakly uniformly distributed
(or
weakly equidistributed
) modulo
q
if
\gcd(g(n),q)=1
for infinitely many
n
and, for every
coprime
residue class
a\bmod{q}
,

(2)

\#\{n\leq x:g(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\}\sim\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}{\#\{n\leq x:\gcd(g(n),q)=1\}},\quad\text{as $x\to\infty$}.

In
[
12
, Theorem I]
, Narkiewicz gives a criterion for deciding weak equidistribution in a large class of multiplicative functions. As an application, we can show that
\sigma(n)
is weakly equidistributed mod
q
for any
q
coprime to
6
. This result was extended by Śliwa
[
19
]
(see also
[
13
, Proposition 7.9, p. 106]
), who showed that
\sigma(n)
is weakly equidistributed to a fixed modulus
q
precisely when
q
is not divisible by
6
.

As a natural analogue of the Siegel-Walfisz theorem for primes in arithmetic progressions, it seems of some interest to investigate analogues of these results where the modulus
q
itself is allowed to vary uniformly in a certain range depending on the stopping point
x
of inputs. To formalize this, we say that an integer-valued arithmetic function
g(n)
is
weakly equidistributed
mod
q
,
uniformly for
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
,
if:

(i)

For every such
q
,
g(n)
is coprime to
q
for infinitely many
n
, and

(ii)

The relation (
2
) holds uniformly in moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
and in coprime residue classes
a
mod
q
. Explicitly, this means that for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
X(\epsilon)>0
such that the ratio of the left hand side of (
2
) to the right hand side lies in
(1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)
for all
x>X(\epsilon)
,
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
and coprime residues
a
mod
q
.

The weak equidistribution of certain classes of arithmetic functions to uniformly varying moduli was investigated in
[
10
,
15
,
16
]
. A general theorem established in
[
16
]
yields the weak equidistribution of
\sigma(n)
uniformly to moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
coprime to
6
. However, the methods in
[
16
]
are unable to address the case when
q
is even but not divisible by
6
(a case in which Śliwa is able to show that
\sigma(n)
is weakly equdistributed mod
q
). Moreover, the error terms that arise from the arguments in
[
16
]
are quite weak. Our next three theorems address these defects. We first establish an effective estimate demonstrating the weak equidistribution of
\sigma(n)
to odd moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
and obtain a strong error term that can be expected to be (essentially) of the correct order of magnitude.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix
K>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We have

\#\{n\leq x:\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\}\\
=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{n\leq x:\gcd(\sigma(n),q)=1\}+O_{K,\epsilon}\left(\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-\alpha(q)(1/3+\epsilon)}}\right),

uniformly in odd moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
and coprime residue classes
a
mod
q
, where
\alpha(q)\coloneqq\prod_{\ell\mid q}\left(1-\frac{1}{\ell-1}\right)
. As a consequence,
\sigma(n)
is weakly equidistributed mod
q
, uniformly for odd moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
.

To obtain uniform analogues of Śliwa’s results, we still need to address the case when the modulus
q
is even. Here, the first crucial difference with the above theorem is that while studying the weak equidistribution of
\sigma(n)
to even moduli, the set of relevant inputs
n\leq x
is highly sparse. In fact, elementary number theory shows that if
\sigma(n)
is odd, then
n
must be of the form
2^{k}m^{2}
for some odd
m
, which means that there are
O(x^{1/2})
many
n\leq x
which have
\sigma(n)
coprime to a given even modulus
q
. Sparse sets like this can often present difficulties while studying arithmetic questions about them. However, we can work around this sparsity issue by looking at the behaviour of
\sigma(n)
at the squares of primes.

Another crucial difference between the situations for odd and even moduli is that in the latter case, the positive integers
n
with too few large prime factors present an obstruction to uniformity in the modulus. As such, in order to restore uniformity in the modulus
q
, it becomes necessary to restrict the set of inputs
n
to those having sufficiently many prime factors exceeding
q
. To make this precise, we write
P(n)
or
P^{+}(n)
for the largest prime divisor of
n
, with the convention that
P(1)=1
. We set
P_{1}(n)\coloneqq P(n)
and define, inductively,
P_{k}(n)\coloneqq P_{k-1}(n/P(n))
. Thus,
P_{k}(n)
is the
k
th largest prime factor of
n
(counted with multiplicity), with
P_{k}(n)=1
if
\Omega(n)<k
.

In what follows, we set

\widetilde{\alpha}(q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{v\bmod q:\gcd(v(v^{2}+v+1),q)=1\}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid q\\
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{2}{\ell-1}\right),

the last equality being true by the Chinese Remainder Theorem and the law of quadratic reciprocity.

Theorem 1.3
.

Fix
K>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We have

(3)

\#\{n\leq x:P_{6}(n)>q,~\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\}\\
=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{n\leq x:P_{6}(n)>q,~\gcd(\sigma(n),q)=1\}+O_{K,\epsilon}\left(\frac{x^{1/2}}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-\widetilde{\alpha}(q)(1/4+\epsilon)}}\right),

uniformly in even moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
not divisible by
3
, and in coprime residues
a
mod
q
.

In subsection
6.1
, we give an explicit counterexample showing that the restriction
P_{6}(n)>q
is optimal in the sense that it cannot be replaced by a condition of the form “
P_{k}(n)>q
” with
k<6
, while still retainining weak equidistribution among the corresponding set of
n
’s in the same range of uniformity in
q
. In fact, we will show that uniformity fails to moduli
q
of the form

for certain odd squarefree integers

having several prime factors. While the proof of Theorem
1.3
relies heavily on bounds on certain character sums modulo prime powers, the aforementioned counterexample works due to an excess in the number of lifts of solutions to a polynomial congruence from prime moduli to prime square moduli.

On the other hand, if we restrict our moduli
q
to be
squarefree
even integers, then we may enlarge the set of inputs
n
to those having four (as opposed to six) prime divisors exceeding
q
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Fix
K>0
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. We have

(4)

\#\{n\leq x:P_{4}(n)>q,~\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\}\\
=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{n\leq x:P_{4}(n)>q,~\gcd(\sigma(n),q)=1\}+O_{K,\epsilon}\left(\frac{x^{1/2}}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-\widetilde{\alpha}(q)(1/4+\epsilon)}}\right),

uniformly in squarefree even moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
not divisible by
3
, and in coprime residue classes
a
mod
q
.

In subsection
7.1
, we show that the restriction
P_{4}(n)>q
is optimal for squarefree even
q
in the same sense as mentioned above. The constants “
1/3
” (respectively, “
1/4
”) in the exponent of

in the error terms of Theorem

(resp., Theorems
1.3
and

), arise as the maximum real part of the averages

\gcd(v,q)=1\end{subarray}}~\chi(v+1)~~~\text{ \Bigg(resp. }\eta_{\chi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod q\\

taken over the nonprincipal Dirichlet characters

. These averages play the roles of the parameter

We conclude this introductory section with the remark that precise estimates for the main terms in Theorems

P_{2},\dots,P_{j-1}\text{ distinct}\\
P_{2}\cdots P_{j-1}\leq x/myP_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{P_{2}\cdots P_{j-1}}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\ll(\log x)^{2}\sum_{j\geq 2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,P_{1},\dots,P_{j-1}\\
mP_{1}\cdots P_{j-1}\leq x/y\\
P_{1}>z,~P^{+}(m)\leq y<P_{j-1}<\dots<P_{1}\end{subarray}}1~+~Mx(\log x)\mathcal{E}(y)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m,P_{1},\dots,P_{j-1}\\
mP_{1}\cdots P_{j-1}\leq x/y\\
P_{1}>z,~P^{+}(m)\leq y<P_{j-1}<\dots<P_{1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{mP_{1}\cdots P_{j-1}}

\displaystyle\ll(\log x)^{2}\sum_{n\leq x/y}1~+~Mx(\log x)\mathcal{E}(y)\sum_{n\leq x/y}\frac{1}{n}\ll Mx(\log x)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}(y)+\frac{1}{y}\right).

As a consequence, we obtain

(7)

\begin{split}\sum\nolimits_{n\leq x}^{*}f(n)&=\varrho\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\varrho^{j-1}}{(j-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},\dots,P_{j}\\
P_{1}>z,~P_{1}\cdots P_{j}\leq x/m\\
P_{2}\cdots P_{j}\in(y,P_{1})\text{ distinct}\end{subarray}}1+O\left(Mx(\log x)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}(y)+\frac{1}{y}\right)\right)\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\sum_{j\geq 1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},\dots,P_{j}\\
P_{1}>z,~P_{1}\cdots P_{j}\leq x/m\\
y<P_{j}<\dots<P_{1}\end{subarray}}\varrho^{j}~+~O\left(Mx(\log x)^{2}\left(\mathcal{E}(y)+\frac{1}{y}\right)\right).\end{split}

At this point, we observe that every squarefree positive integer
r\leq x/m
having
P^{+}(r)>z
and
P^{-}(r)>y
can be uniquely written in the form
P_{j}\cdots P_{1}
with
P_{1}>z
and
y<P_{j}<\dots<P_{1}
, and in this case
j=\Omega(r)
. As such, the main term in the final expression in (
7
) is equal to

(8)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq x/m\\
r\text{ squarefree}\\
P^{+}(r)>z,~P^{-}(r)>y\end{subarray}}\varrho^{\Omega(r)}~.

Ignoring the condition
P^{+}(r)>z
incurs a total error of size

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}~~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq x/m\\
P^{-}(r)>y,\,P^{+}(r)\leq z\end{subarray}}1\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P^{+}(n)\leq z\end{subarray}}1\leq\Psi(x,z),

which is absorbed in the bound claimed in the theorem statement. Moreover, since any non-squarefree
r
having
P^{-}(r)>y
is divisible by the square of a prime exceeding
y
, ignoring the squarefreeness condition in (
8
) incurs a total error

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}~\sum_{p>y}~\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\leq x/m\\
p^{2}\mid r\end{subarray}}1\ll x\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{p>y}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\ll\frac{x}{y\log y}\prod_{\ell\leq y}\left(1+\sum_{v\geq 1}\frac{1}{\ell^{v}}\right)\ll\frac{x}{y},

which is also absorbed in the claimed bound. Hence, up to a negligible error, the expression in (
8
) is equal to

(9)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}f(m)\sum_{r\leq x/m}\mathbbm{1}_{P^{-}(r)>y}~\varrho^{\Omega(r)}.

In order to estimate the innermost sum, we shall be making use of the lemma below, which we will establish in the next section.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X,Y,Z\geq e^{11/2}
be positive real numbers satisfying
Y\leq Z^{1/(18\log\log Z)^{2}}
and
Z\leq X
. We have for all
\beta\in\mathbb{U}
,

\sum_{n\leq X}\mathbbm{1}_{P^{-}(n)>Y}~\beta^{\Omega(n)}=\frac{X}{(\log X)^{1-\beta}}\left\{\frac{e^{-\gamma\beta}}{\Gamma(\beta)(\log Y)^{\beta}}\left(1+O(\exp(-C_{0}\sqrt{\log y}))\right)+O\left(\frac{(\log Y)^{1+|\beta|}}{\log Z}\right)\right\},

where
C_{0}>0
is an absolute constant. In particular,

\sum_{n\leq X}\mathbbm{1}_{P^{-}(n)>Y}~\beta^{\Omega(n)}\ll\frac{X}{(\log X)^{1-\mathrm{Re}(\beta)}}\left\{\frac{|\beta|}{(\log Y)^{\mathrm{Re}(\beta)}}+\frac{(\log Y)^{1+|\beta|}}{\log Z}\right\}.

The implied constants in the above estimates are absolute.

Applying the above lemma with
X\coloneqq x/m
,
Y\coloneqq y
,
Z\coloneqq z
and
\beta\coloneqq\varrho
, we find that the expression in (
9
) is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x/z\\
P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}|f(m)|\cdot\frac{x/m}{(\log(x/m))^{1-\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}}\left\{\frac{|\varrho|}{(\log y)^{\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}}+\frac{(\log y)^{1+|\varrho|}}{\log z}\right\}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{x}{(\log z)^{1-\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}}\left(\sum_{m:\,P^{+}(m)\leq y}\frac{|f(m)|}{m}\right)\left\{\frac{|\varrho|}{(\log y)^{\mathrm{Re}(\varrho)}}+\frac{(\log y)^{1+|\varrho|}}{\log z}\right\}.

Finally, since
|f(n)|\leq 1
for all
n
, the sum on
m
is
\ll\exp(\sum_{p\leq y}|f(p)|/p)
, yielding the estimate in the theorem. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
, up to the proof of Lemma
2.1
.

3.
Proof of Lemma
2.1
: the Landau–Selberg–Delange method

A comprehensive account of the method of Landau–Selberg–Delange may be found in Tenenbaum
[
20
, Chapter II.5]
. However, we shall be using a recent formulation of this method due to Chang and Martin
[
6
]
. This is based on Tenenbaum’s treatment but is more explicit in the dependence on certain parameters, a feature that shall be crucial in our current application.

3.1.
Setup

In what follows, we write complex numbers
s
as
s=\sigma+it
, where
\sigma\coloneqq\mathrm{Re}(s)
and
t\coloneqq\mathrm{Im}(s)
. (This convention is relevant only for this section and the use of
\sigma
will not create any confusion with the notation for the sum-of-divisors function.) For a non-negative
u
, we use
\log^{+}{u}
to denote the quantity
\max\{0,\log{u}\}
(the positive part of
\log u
), with the convention that
\log^{+}{0}=0
.

Given
\delta\in(0,1]
, a complex number
z
, and positive real numbers
c_{0}
and
M
, we say that the Dirichlet series
F(s)
has
property
\mathcal{P}(z;c_{0},\delta,M)
if the function

G(s;z):=F(s)\zeta(s)^{-z}

satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

G(s;z)
continues analytically into the region
\sigma\geq 1-c_{0}/(1+\log^{+}|t|)
, and

(ii)

|G(s;z)|\leq M(1+|t|)^{1-\delta}

for all
s
in this same region.

Given complex numbers
z
and
w
, along with positive numbers
c_{0}
,
M
and
\delta\in(0,1]
, we say that a Dirichlet series
F(s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}n^{-s}
has
type
\mathcal{T}(z,w;c_{0},\delta,M)
if:

(i)

F(s)
has property
\mathcal{P}(z;c_{0},\delta,M)
, and

(ii)

there is a sequence
\{b_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of nonnegative real numbers satisfying
|a_{n}|\leq b_{n}
for all
n
, such that the Dirichlet series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_{n}n^{-s}
has property
\mathcal{P}(w;c_{0},\delta,M)
.

The following is the special case of Theorem A.13 in
[
6
]
with
N=0
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Fix
A\geq 1
and let
z,w
be complex numbers satisfying
|z|,|w|\leq A
. Let
c_{0},\delta,M
be positive real numbers with
c_{0}\leq 2/11
and
\delta\leq 1
. Let
F(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}/n^{s}
be a Dirichlet series of type
\mathcal{T}(z,w;c_{0},\delta,M)
. Then, uniformly for
x\geq\exp(8^{1/A}\max\{\delta^{-1-1/A},2/c_{0}\})
,
we have

\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}=\frac{x}{(\log{x})^{1-z}}\left(\frac{G(1;z)}{\Gamma(z)}+O(MR(x))\right),

where

R(x)=\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{2A+3/2}}+\frac{1}{c_{0}^{2A+1}}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{c_{0}\delta\log{x}}\right)+\frac{1}{c_{0}\log{x}}

and the implied constant depends at most on
A
.

Here we have corrected some typos in
[
6
]
; the expression for
R(x)
there has an extra factor of
M
throughout as well as an extra factor of
x
in its first term.

3.2.
Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

We claim that the Dirichlet series
F(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathbbm{1}_{P^{-}(n)>Y}~\beta^{\Omega(n)}/n^{s}
is of type
\mathcal{T}(\beta,|\beta|;1/\log Y,1,C_{0}(\log Y)^{|\beta|})
for some absolute constant
C_{0}>0
. Indeed, in the half plane
\sigma>1
, we find that

(10)

G(s)\coloneqq F(s)\zeta(s)^{-\beta}=\prod_{p\leq Y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{\beta}\prod_{p>Y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)^{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{p^{s}}\right)^{-1},

and in the same half plane

(11)

\log G(s)=\beta\sum_{p\leq Y}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)+\sum_{p>Y}\left\{\beta\log\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s}}\right)-\log\left(1-\frac{\beta}{p^{s}}\right)\right\}.

Now since
Y\geq e^{11/2}
, we see that if
\sigma\geq 1-1/\log Y
, then
\sigma\geq 9/11
, so that
|\beta/p^{s}|\leq 2^{-\sigma}\leq 0.57
. Consequently for all such
s
, the second sum in (
11
) is

\sum_{p>Y}\left\{\beta\left(-\frac{1}{p^{s}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{2\sigma}}\right)\right)+\left(\frac{\beta}{p^{s}}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{2\sigma}}\right)\right)\right\}\ll\sum_{p>Y}\frac{1}{p^{2\sigma}}\leq\sum_{p>Y}\frac{1}{p^{18/11}}\ll 1.

This shows that the second sum in (
11
) converges absolutely and uniformly in the half plane
\sigma\geq 1-1/\log Y
, thus defining a holomorphic function in the same region. Furthermore, for all
s
in this half plane, we have

\displaystyle\log|G(s)|\leq|\log G(s)|

\displaystyle\leq|\beta|\sum_{p\leq Y}\frac{1}{p^{\sigma}}+O(1)\leq|\beta|\sum_{p\leq Y}\frac{1}{p}\exp\left(\frac{\log p}{\log Y}\right)+O(1)

\displaystyle=|\beta|\sum_{p\leq Y}\frac{1}{p}+O\left(1+\frac{1}{\log Y}\sum_{p\leq Y}\frac{\log p}{p}\right)=|\beta|\log_{2}Y+O(1).

We deduce that
|G(s)|\leq C_{0}(\log Y)^{|\beta|}
for all
\sigma\geq 1-1/\log Y
, which shows that
F(s)
has property
\mathcal{P}(\beta;1/\log Y,1,C_{0}(\log Y)^{|\beta|})
. By invoking this very observation with
|\beta|
in place of
\beta
, we find that the Dirichlet series
\sum_{n\geq 1}|\beta|^{\Omega(n)}/n^{s}
has property
\mathcal{P}(|\beta|;1/\log Y,1,C_{0}(\log Y)^{|\beta|})
, which establishes our claim.

An application of Proposition
3.1
with
A=1
now shows that for all
X\geq Y^{16}=\exp(16\log Y)
, we have

\sum_{n\leq X}\mathbbm{1}_{P^{-}(n)>Y}\beta^{\Omega(n)}=\frac{X}{(\log X)^{1-\beta}}\left\{\frac{G(1)}{\Gamma(\beta)}+O\left(\frac{(\log Y)^{1+|\beta|}}{\log X}+(\log Y)^{3+|\beta|}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{\frac{\log X}{\log Y}}\right)\right)\right\}.

For
Y\leq Z^{1/(18\log_{2}Z)^{2}}
, we have
2\log_{2}Y+\log_{2}Z\leq 3\log_{2}Z\leq\frac{1}{6}\sqrt{\frac{\log Z}{\log Y}}
, which shows that

(12)

\sum_{n\leq X}\mathbbm{1}_{P^{-}(n)>Y}\beta^{\Omega(n)}=\frac{X}{(\log X)^{1-\beta}}\left\{\frac{G(1)}{\Gamma(\beta)}+O\left(\frac{(\log Y)^{1+|\beta|}}{\log Z}\right)\right\}.

Finally,

\sum_{p>Y}\left\{\beta\log\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)-\log\left(1-\frac{\beta}{p}\right)\right\}\ll\sum_{p>Y}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{Y\log Y},

and by the prime number theorem (with the usual de la Vallée Poussin error term), we have

\prod_{p\leq Y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\gamma\beta}}{(\log Y)^{\beta}}\left(1+O(\exp(-C_{0}\sqrt{\log Y}))\right),

for some absolute constant
C_{0}>0
. This shows that

G(1)=\prod_{p\leq Y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\beta}\prod_{p>Y}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{\beta}\left(1-\frac{\beta}{p}\right)^{-1}=\frac{e^{-\gamma\beta}}{(\log Y)^{\beta}}\left(1+O(\exp(-C_{0}\sqrt{\log Y}))\right)

which from (
12
) yields the first of the claimed estimates in Lemma
2.1
. Since the Gamma function has no zeros in the complex plane, we have
|\Gamma(s)|\gg 1
for all
s
in a fixed compact region. As a consequence,
\beta\Gamma(\beta)=\Gamma(1+\beta)\gg 1
for all
|\beta|\leq 1
, yielding the second assertion of the lemma.

4.
Distribution of the sum-of-divisors to odd moduli: Proof of Theorem
1.2

In the rest of this section, we abbreviate
\alpha(q)
to
\alpha
. We shall make frequent use of the fact that
\alpha=\prod_{\ell\mid q}(1-1/(\ell-1))\gg 1/\log_{2}(3q)
for all odd
q
. The following proposition allows us to give a rough estimate on the count of
n\leq x
for which
\sigma(n)
is coprime to
q
, uniformly in odd moduli
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
K>0
and a multiplicative function
f
for which there exists a non-constant polynomial
F\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
satisfying
f(p)=F(p)
for all primes
p
. If
x
is sufficiently large and
q\leq(\log{x})^{K}
with
\alpha_{F}(q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\#\{u\bmod q:(uF(u),q)=1\}>0
, then

(13)

\#\{n\leq x:(f(n),q)=1\}=\frac{x}{(\log{x})^{1-\alpha_{F}(q)}}\exp(O((\log\log{(3q)})^{O(1)})).

This is Proposition 2.1 in
[
16
]
; more precise results appear in work of Scourfield
[
17
,
18
]
. By the above proposition, we obtain

(14)

\#\{n\leq x:(\sigma(n),q)=1\}=\frac{x}{(\log{x})^{1-\alpha}}\exp(O((\log\log{(3q)})^{O(1)})),

uniformly in odd
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
. Since
\alpha\gg 1/\log_{2}(3q)
, this shows that the second of the two assertions of Theorem
1.2
is an immediate consequence of the first, so we need to show the first assertion of the theorem. For this purpose, we shall also need the following estimate
[
16
, Lemma 2.4]
on the sum of reciprocals of the primes at which a given polynomial is coprime to a modulus
q
that varies in a wide range.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
F(T)\in\mathbb{Z}[T]
be a fixed nonconstant polynomial. For each positive integer
q
and each real number
x\geq 3q
,

\sum_{p\leq x}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{\gcd(F(p),q)=1}}{p}=\alpha_{F}(q)\log_{2}{x}+O((\log\log{(3q)})^{O(1)}),

where
\alpha_{F}(q)
is as defined in Proposition
4.1
.

To establish the first assertion of Theorem
1.2
, we set
y\coloneqq\exp((\log x)^{\epsilon/2})
and
z\coloneqq x^{1/\log_{2}x}
. From the count of
n\leq x
having
\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}
, we first remove those that are either
z
-smooth or have a repeated prime factor exceeding
y
. By well-known results on smooth numbers (for instance
[
20
, Theorem 5.13
and
Corollary 5.19, Chapter III.5]
), the total contribution of such
n
is
\ll\Psi(x,z)+x/y\ll x/(\log x)^{(1+o(1))\log_{3}x}
, which is negligible in comparison to the error term in the statement of Theorem
1.2
.

Among the surviving
n
, we now remove those that have
P_{2}(n)\leq y
. Any such
n
can be written as
n=mP
, where
P=P(n)>z
,
m
is
y
-smooth and
\sigma(n)=\sigma(m)\sigma(P)=\sigma(m)(P+1)
. The condition
\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}
forces
\sigma(m)
to be coprime to
q
and, for each choice of
m
, restricts
P\in(z,x/m]
to at most one coprime residue class modulo
q
. Hence for each choice of
m
, there are
\ll x/\varphi(q)m\log(z/q)\ll x\log_{2}x/\varphi(q)m\log x
many possible choices of
P
, by the Brun-Titchmarsh theorem. Consequently, the total contribution of the surviving
n\leq x
that have
P_{2}(n)\leq y
is

\begin{split}\ll\frac{x\log_{2}x}{\varphi(q)\log x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m:\,P^{+}(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{(\sigma(m),q)=1}}{m}&\ll\frac{x\log_{2}x}{\varphi(q)\log x}\exp\left(\sum_{p\leq y}\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{(p+1,\,q)=1}}{p}\right)\\
&\ll\frac{x\log_{2}x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-\alpha\epsilon/2}}\exp((\log_{2}(3q))^{O(1)})\ll\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-2\alpha\epsilon/3}}.\end{split}

Here we have estimated the sum
\sum_{p\leq y}\mathbbm{1}_{(p+1,\,q)=1}/p
using Lemma
4.2
(on the polynomial
F(T)\coloneqq T+1
) and recalled that
\alpha\gg 1/\log_{2}(3q)\gg 1/\log_{3}x
for all odd
q\leq(\log x)^{K}
. Collecting estimates, we have so far shown that

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,\,P_{2}(n)>y\\
p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}}+O\left(\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-2\alpha\epsilon/3}}\right)\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,\,P_{2}(n)>y\\
p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\bmod q}~\overline{\chi}(a)\chi(\sigma(n))+O\left(\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-2\alpha\epsilon/3}}\right),\end{split}

where in the second line above, we have used the orthogonality of the Dirichlet characters mod
q
to detect the congruence
\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}
. With
\chi_{0,q}
denoting the principal character modulo
q
, we may thus isolate the contribution of
\chi_{0,q}
to obtain

(15)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
(\sigma(n),q)=1\end{subarray}}1\\
&+\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\chi\neq\chi_{0,q}\bmod q}\overline{\chi}(a)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,\,P_{2}(n)>y\\
p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(n))+O\left(\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-2\alpha\epsilon/3}}\right).\end{split}

The second outer sum on the right hand side above is over the non-trivial characters
\chi
mod
q
, and we have adapted our previous arguments to observe that there are
O({x}/{(\log x)^{1-2\alpha\epsilon/3}})
many
n\leq x
satisfying
(\sigma(n),q)=1
but failing at least one of the three conditions below:

(i)

P(n)>z,

(ii)

p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n,

(iii)

P_{2}(n)>y
.

Indeed, any
n
satisfying conditions (i) and (ii) but failing condition (iii) is of the form
mP
, where
P^{+}(m)\leq y
,
P=P^{+}(n)\in(z,x/m]
and
\sigma(n)=\sigma(m)(P+1)
. As such, we must have
(\sigma(m),q)=1
, and the number of
P
given
m
is
\ll x/m\log z\ll x\log_{2}x/m\log x
. Summing this expression over all possible
m
yields the observed bound.

In order to estimate the inner sums of
\chi(\sigma(n))
occurring in (
15
), we start by modifying some of our initial arguments in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Any
n
with
P(n)>z
,
P_{2}(n)>y
and without any repeated prime factor exceeding
y
can be uniquely written in the form
mP_{j}\cdots P_{1}
for some
j\geq 2
, where
P_{1}=P(n)>z
and
P(m)\leq y<P_{j}<\dots<P_{1}
. As such, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,\,P_{2}(n)>y\\
p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(n))=\sum_{j\geq 2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
P(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(m))\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},\dots,P_{j}\\
P_{j}\cdots P_{1}\leq x/m\\
P_{1}>z,~y<P_{j}<\dots<P_{1}\end{subarray}}\chi(P_{1}+1)\cdots\chi(P_{j}+1).

Define
\rho_{\chi}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{v\bmod q}\chi_{0,q}(v)\chi(v+1)
. By the Siegel-Walfisz Theorem, we see that

(16)

\begin{split}\sum_{y<p\leq Y}\chi(p+1)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod q\\
(v,q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(v+1)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y<p\leq Y\\
p\equiv v\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod q\\
(v,q)=1\end{subarray}}\chi(v+1)\left\{\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y<p\leq Y\end{subarray}}1+O\left(Y\exp(-C_{0}\sqrt{\log y})\right)\right\}\\
&=\rho_{\chi}(\pi(Y)-\pi(y))+O(\varphi(q)Y\exp(-C_{0}\sqrt{\log y})),\end{split}

where
C_{0}
is a constant depending at most on
K
. Proceeding as in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we successively remove
\chi(P_{1}+1),\dots,\chi(P_{j}+1)
, with the input from (
1
) replaced by the above estimate. This leads us to

(17)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,\,P_{2}(n)>y\\
p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(n))=\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{(\rho_{\chi})^{j}}{(j-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
P(m)\leq y\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(m))&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{1},\dots,P_{j}\\
P_{1}>z,~P_{j}\cdots P_{1}\leq x/m\\
P_{2},\dots,P_{j}\in(y,P_{1})\text{ distinct }\end{subarray}}1\\
&+O(x\exp(-C_{1}\sqrt{\log y})),\end{split}

for some constant
C_{1}>0
depending at most on
K
.

Now the main term in the display above is absolutely bounded by

(18)

\displaystyle|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{|\rho_{\chi}|^{j-2}}{(j-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
P(m)\leq y\\
(\sigma(m),q)=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{2},\dots,P_{j}\in(y,x)\\
P_{2}\cdots P_{j}\leq x/mz\end{subarray}}~\sum_{z<P_{1}\leq x/mP_{2}\cdots P_{j}}1

\displaystyle\ll\frac{|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}x}{\log z}\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{|\rho_{\chi}|^{j-2}}{(j-1)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
P(m)\leq y\\
(\sigma(m),q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P_{2},\dots,P_{j}\in(y,x)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{P_{2}\cdots P_{j}}

\displaystyle\ll|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}\frac{x(\log_{2}x)^{2}}{\log x}\sum_{j\geq 2}\frac{(|\rho_{\chi}|\log_{2}x)^{j-2}}{(j-2)!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leq x\\
P(m)\leq y\\
(\sigma(m),q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{m}\ll|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}\frac{x(\log_{2}x)^{2}}{(\log x)^{1-|\rho_{\chi}|}}\exp\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leq y\\
(p+1,\,q)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{p}\Bigg).

Invoking Lemma
4.2
to estimate the last sum in the above display, we obtain

(19)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
P(n)>z,\,P_{2}(n)>y\\
p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(n))\ll|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-|\rho_{\chi}|-2\alpha\epsilon/3}}+x\exp(-C_{1}\sqrt{\log y}).

In order to be able to make use of this bound, we need to better understand the
|\rho_{\chi}|
. To this end, given a nontrivial character
\chi\bmod q
we let
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
denote its conductor, so that
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\mid q
and
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)>1
. We can write
\chi
uniquely in the form
\prod_{\ell^{e}\parallel q}\chi_{\ell}
, with
\chi_{\ell}
denoting a character mod
\ell^{e}
that is nontrivial precisely when
\ell\mid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
. Now
\varphi(q)\rho_{\chi}=\prod_{\ell^{e}\parallel q}S_{\chi,\ell}
, where for each prime power
\ell^{e}\parallel q
,

(20)

S_{\chi,\ell}\coloneqq\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e}}\chi_{0,\ell}(v)\chi_{\ell}(v+1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod{\ell^{e}}\\
(v,\ell)=1\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(v+1)=\sum_{u\bmod{\ell^{e}}}\chi_{\ell}(u)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\bmod{\ell^{e}}\\
u\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(u).

Here
\chi_{0,\ell}
denotes the trivial character mod
\ell^{e}
and we have noted that as

runs over all the coprime residues mod
\ell^{e}
, the expression
v+1
runs over all the residues mod
\ell^{e}
except for those congruent to
1
mod
\ell
. The first sum above is
\mathbbm{1}_{\chi_{\ell}=\chi_{0,\ell}}\varphi(\ell^{e})
. To evaluate the second sum, we consider a primitive root
g
mod
\ell^{e}
(which exists as
\ell
is odd), and observe that the residues
\{u\bmod{\ell^{e}}:u\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}\}
are a permutation of the residues
\{g^{(\ell-1)k}\bmod{\ell^{e}}:0\leq k<\ell^{e-1}\}
. Hence

(21)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\bmod{\ell^{e}}\\
u\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(u)=\sum_{0\leq k<\ell^{e-1}}\chi_{\ell}(g^{\ell-1})^{k}=\mathbbm{1}_{(\chi_{\ell})^{\ell-1}=\chi_{0,\ell}}~\ell^{e-1}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{f}(\chi_{\ell})\mid\ell}~\ell^{e-1},

with
\mathfrak{f}(\chi_{\ell})
denoting the conductor of
\chi_{\ell}
. Altogether, we find that

S_{\chi,\ell}=\mathbbm{1}_{\chi_{\ell}=\chi_{0,\ell}}\varphi(\ell^{e})-\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{f}(\chi_{\ell})\mid\ell}\ell^{e-1}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{f}(\chi_{\ell})\mid\ell}\ell^{e-1}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\ell\nmid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)}(\ell-1)-1\right),

leading to

(22)

\rho_{\chi}=\prod_{\ell^{e}\parallel q}\frac{S_{\chi,\ell}}{\varphi(\ell^{e})}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\text{ squarefree }}\prod_{\ell^{e}\parallel q}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\ell\nmid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)}-\frac{1}{\ell-1}\right)=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\text{ squarefree }}\frac{(-1)^{\omega(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))}\alpha}{\prod_{\ell\mid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)}(\ell-2)}.

If
3\mid q
, let
\psi
denote the unique character mod
q
induced by the nontrivial character mod
3
. Then for any nonprincipal character

mod

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\chi(\sigma(n))\ll|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}\frac{x}{(\log x)^{1-(1/3+2\epsilon/3)\alpha}}+x\exp(-C_{1}\sqrt{\log y}).

Since there are exactly

primitive characters modulo any squarefree integer

\begin{split}\sum_{\chi\bmod q}|\rho_{\chi}|^{2}\leq\alpha^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q\\

(This may be compared with the bound placed on the averages

in the proof of the analogous Theorem 1.3 in

.)

Summing the bound (

) over all nontrivial characters

(24)

\begin{split}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
\sigma(n)\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}1&=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
(\sigma(n),q)=1\end{subarray}}1+\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{3\mid q}\overline{\psi}(a)}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\

p>y\implies p^{2}\nmid n\end{subarray}}\psi(\sigma(n))+O\left(\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-\alpha(1/3+\epsilon)}}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x\\
(\sigma(n),q)=1\end{subarray}}1+\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{3\mid q}\overline{\psi}(a)}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{n\leq x}\psi(\sigma(n))+O\left(\frac{x}{\varphi(q)(\log x)^{1-\alpha(1/3+\epsilon)}}\right).\end{split}

Here in passing to the second line above, we have recalled our previous bound on the count of

having
\sigma(n)
coprime to

Note that the last equality in (

5.
Technical Preparation for Theorems
1.3
and
1.4

In order to establish Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
, we will need to study the averages

\eta_{\chi}\coloneqq\eta_{\chi}(q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{v\bmod q}\chi_{0,q}(v)\chi(v^{2}+v+1)

which will play the roles of the averages
\rho_{\chi}
that came up in the previous section. The following proposition will provide the key information on these averages that will prove to be crucial in our arguments.

Proposition 5.1
.

There exists a set
\mathcal{S}
of eighteen (fixed) squarefree positive integers coprime to
6
such that for all sufficiently large integers
q
and all nonprincipal characters
\chi
mod
q
, the following two properties hold true:

(i)

If
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\not\in\mathcal{S}
, then
|\eta_{\chi}|\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
.

(ii)

If
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\in\mathcal{S}
, then
\mathrm{Re}(\eta_{\chi})\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
.

In particular, we have
\mathrm{Re}(\eta_{\chi})\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
for all nontrivial characters
\chi
mod
q
, and
|\eta_{\chi}|\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
for all but a bounded number of characters
\chi
mod
q
.

The following character sum bound, a special case of
[
7
, Theoem 1.1]
, will be useful to give a proof of Proposition
5.1
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\ell
be a prime at least
5
. Then for any integer
e\geq 2
and any primitive character
\chi
mod
\ell^{e}
, we have

\left|\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e}}\chi(v^{2}+v+1)\right|\leq\ell^{e/2}.

Proof of Proposition
5.1
.

We start by factoring
\chi\eqqcolon\prod_{\ell^{e}\parallel q}\chi_{\ell}
, where each
\chi_{\ell}
is as usual a character mod
\ell^{e}
. This allows us to factor
\eta_{\chi}
as
\prod_{\ell^{e}\parallel q}\eta_{\chi,\ell}
, where

\eta_{\chi,\ell}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e})}\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e}}\chi_{0,\ell}(v)\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1).

Since
\widetilde{\alpha}(\ell^{e})=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e})}\#\{v\bmod\ell^{e}:(v(v^{2}+v+1),\ell)=1\}
, it is immediate that
|\eta_{\chi,\ell}|\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(\ell^{e})=\widetilde{\alpha}(\ell)
. Moreover, letting
e_{\ell}\coloneqq v_{\ell}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))
denote the exponent of the prime
\ell
in the integer
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
, we see that
\mathfrak{f}(\chi_{\ell})=\ell^{e_{\ell}}
, so if
e_{\ell}=0
(i.e.,
\ell\nmid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
), then
\chi_{\ell}=\chi_{0,\ell}
and
\eta_{\chi,\ell}=\widetilde{\alpha}(\ell)
.

Assume that
\chi_{2}
is nontrivial, so that
e_{2}\geq 2
. Letting
U_{2^{e_{2}}}
denote the multiplicative group mod
2^{e_{2}}
we observe that the map
U_{2^{e_{2}}}\rightarrow U_{2^{e_{2}}}:v\mapsto v^{2}+v+1
, being injective, is also bijective. As a consequence

\eta_{\chi,2}=\frac{1}{\varphi(2^{e_{2}})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod 2^{e_{2}}\\
(v,2)=1\end{subarray}}\chi_{2}(v^{2}+v+1)=\frac{1}{\varphi(2^{e_{2}})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\bmod 2^{e_{2}}\\
(u,2)=1\end{subarray}}\chi_{2}(u)=0,

leading to
\eta_{\chi}=\eta_{\chi,2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid q:~\ell>2\end{subarray}}\eta_{\chi,\ell}=0
. Hence in the rest of the argument, it suffices to consider only those characters
\chi
mod
q
for which
\chi_{2}
is trivial, that is, for which
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
is not a multiple of
4
. Since
3\nmid q
and
\chi
is nontrivial mod
q
, it must then be the case that
\chi_{\ell}\neq\chi_{0,\ell}
for some prime
\ell\geq 5
dividing
q
.

Consider a prime
\ell\geq 5
dividing
q
for which
\chi_{\ell}
is nontrivial. Letting
\chi_{\ell}
also denote the primitive chraracter mod
\ell^{e_{\ell}}
that induces
\chi_{\ell}
mod
\ell^{e}
, we find that

(25)

\displaystyle\eta_{\chi,\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e})}\cdot\ell^{e-e_{\ell}}\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}}\chi_{0,\ell}(v)\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e_{\ell}})}\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}}\chi_{0,\ell}(v)\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e_{\ell}})}\left\{\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}\\
v\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e_{\ell}})}\left\{\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)-\mathbbm{1}_{e_{\ell}=1}\right\}.

In the last equality above, we have observed that the map
\{v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}:v\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}\}\rightarrow\{u\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}:u\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}\}\colon v\mapsto v^{2}+v+1
being an injection from one set to another of the same cardinality is also a bijection. By (
21
), this led to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}\\
v\equiv 0\pmod{\ell}\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}\\
u\equiv 1\pmod{\ell}\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(u)=\mathbbm{1}_{e_{\ell}=1}~\ell^{e_{\ell}-1}=\mathbbm{1}_{e_{\ell}=1}.

If
\ell\geq 5
and
e_{\ell}\geq 2
, then by Lemma
5.2
, we have

(26)

|\eta_{\chi,\ell}|=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell^{e_{\ell}})}\left|\sum_{v\bmod\ell^{e_{\ell}}}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)\right|\leq\frac{\ell^{e_{\ell}/2}}{\varphi(\ell^{e_{\ell}})}\leq\frac{\ell^{1-e_{\ell}/2}}{\ell-1}.

Hence if
v_{\ell}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))=e_{\ell}\geq 2
for some prime
\ell\geq 5
dividing
q
, then

\frac{|\eta_{\chi}|}{\widetilde{\alpha}(q)}\leq\frac{|\eta_{\chi,\ell}|}{\widetilde{\alpha}(\ell)}\leq\frac{\ell^{1-e_{\ell}/2}}{\ell-1}\cdot\left(\frac{\ell-1}{\ell-3}\right)^{\mathbbm{1}_{\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}}}\leq\frac{1}{4}.

This shows that
|\eta_{\chi}|\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
for all characters
\chi
mod
q
whose conductor is not squarefree.

Consider now a nontrivial character
\chi
mod
q
whose conductor is squarefree. Then by (
25
) and the Weil bounds (see for instance,
[
21
, Corollary 2.3]
), we find that

(27)

|\eta_{\chi,\ell}|=\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\left|\sum_{v\bmod\ell}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)-1\right|\leq\frac{\ell^{1/2}+1}{\ell-1}.

Applying this bound for each prime
\ell\geq 5
dividing
q
, we find that

\frac{|\eta_{\chi}|}{\widetilde{\alpha}(q)}\leq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid q\\
\ell>2\end{subarray}}\mu_{\ell},~~\text{ where }\mu_{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\ell^{1/2}+1}{\ell-3}\right)+\mathbbm{1}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\geq 5,~\ell\equiv 2\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\ell^{1/2}+1}{\ell-1}\right).

Note that
\mu_{\ell}\in(0,1)
for all primes
\ell\geq 5
. Since the functions
(\ell^{1/2}+1)/(\ell-3)
and
(\ell^{1/2}+1)/(\ell-1)
are both strictly decreasing, we observe the following cases in which
|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}(q)\leq 1/4
. For
i\in\{1,2\}
, we let
\omega_{i}(r)
denote the number of distinct primes dividing an integer
r
that are congruent to
i
mod
3
.

•

If
P^{+}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))\geq 29
, then
|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}(q)\leq\frac{29^{1/2}+1}{29-3}<0.246
.

•

If
\omega(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))\geq 4
, then one of the following three possibilities must hold:

(i) Either
\omega_{1}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))\geq 3
, in which case
|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}\leq\mu_{7}\mu_{13}\mu_{19}<0.141
,
OR

(ii)
\omega_{2}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))\geq 3
, in which case
|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}\leq\mu_{5}\mu_{11}\mu_{17}<0.112
,
OR

(iii)
\omega_{1}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))=\omega_{2}(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))=2
, in which case
|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}\leq\mu_{7}\mu_{13}\cdot\mu_{5}\mu_{11}<0.147
.

•

If
\omega(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))=3
but
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
does not lie in the set
A_{0}\coloneqq\{5\cdot 7\cdot 11,5\cdot 7\cdot 13\}
, then

|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}\leq\max\{\mu_{5}\mu_{11}\mu_{13},~\mu_{5}\mu_{7}\mu_{17},~\mu_{7}\mu_{11}\mu_{13},~\mu_{5}\mu_{7}\mu_{19}\}<0.247.

•

If
\omega(\mathfrak{f}(\chi))=2
but
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
is not a member of the set
B_{0}\coloneqq\{7\cdot 13,7\cdot 19\}\cup\{5\cdot 11,5\cdot 17\}\cup\{5\cdot 7,5\cdot 13,5\cdot 19,7\cdot 11,7\cdot 17\}
, then

|\eta_{\chi}|/\widetilde{\alpha}\leq\max\{\mu_{13}\mu_{19},~\mu_{11}\mu_{17},~\mu_{5}\mu_{23},~\mu_{7}\mu_{23},~\mu_{11}\mu_{13}\}<0.241.

Hence, defining
\mathcal{S}
to be the set
\{5,7,11,13,17,19,23\}\cup A_{0}\cup B_{0}
, we have shown that
|\eta_{\chi}|\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
for all characters
\chi
mod
q
whose conductor
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
does not lie in the set
\mathcal{S}
. It thus only remains to show that for all
\chi
mod
q
with
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\in\mathcal{S}
, we have
\mathrm{Re}(\eta_{\chi})\leq\widetilde{\alpha}(q)/4
. For such characters, the identity (
25
) shows that

(28)

\begin{split}\eta_{\chi}&=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid q\\
\ell\nmid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\end{subarray}}\widetilde{\alpha}(\ell)\cdot\prod_{\ell\mid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(\ell)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod\ell\\
v\not\equiv 0\bmod\ell\end{subarray}}\chi_{\ell}(v^{2}+v+1)\right)\\
&=\frac{\widetilde{\alpha}(q)}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\\
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}(\ell-3)\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\\
\ell\equiv 2\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}(\ell-1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod\mathfrak{f}(\chi)\\
\gcd(v,\mathfrak{f}(\chi))=1\end{subarray}}\psi(v^{2}+v+1),\end{split}

where
\psi\coloneqq\prod_{\ell\mid\mathfrak{f}(\chi)}\chi_{\ell}
denotes the primitive character mod
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
inducing
\chi
. So we need only show that

(29)

\max_{Q\in\mathcal{S}}~\frac{1}{\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid Q\\
\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}(\ell-3)\cdot\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\mid Q\\
\ell\equiv 2\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}(\ell-1)}~\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\psi\bmod Q\\
\psi\text{ primitive}\end{subarray}}~\mathrm{Re}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\bmod Q\\
\gcd(v,Q)=1\end{subarray}}\psi(v^{2}+v+1)\right)\leq\frac{1}{4}.

But
\mathcal{S}
consists of only eighteen moduli, so this can be verified by a short Sage code. This completes the proof of Proposition
5.1
.
∎

Lemma 5.3

\ell\equiv 1\pmod{3}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\ell}+O(1)\right)<\frac{K_{2}}{\log Y}\end{split}

for some absolute constant
K_{2}>0
, where we have used the prime number theorem in arithmetic progressions to estimate the sum on
\ell
. As such, the desired condition
\pi(Y)>(1+\delta)\widetilde{\alpha}\log_{2}x/\log 2
holds as soon as
Y>2K_{2}(1+\delta)\log_{2}x/\log 2
, which is compatible with the only other condition
Y\ll\log_{2}x
needed on the parameter
Y
. Choosing
Y
accordingly, we have therefore established that the condition
P_{6}(n)>q
in Theorem
1.3
cannot be weakened to
P_{4}(n)>q
in the range of uniformity in
q
. Since the largest odd divisor of
n
is a perfect square, it follows that the restriction
P_{6}(n)>q
in Theorem
1.3
is indeed optimal.

In order to estimate the sum

7.1.
Optimality in the restriction
P_{4}(n)>q
.

The restriction
P_{4}(n)>q
is crucial and optimal in the sense that weak equidistribution fails (in the range of uniformity in
q
provided in the theorem) as soon as one enlarges the set of inputs
n
to those having fewer prime factors exceeding
q
. Indeed, let
q\coloneqq 2\prod_{5\leq\ell\leq Y}\ell
for some parameter
Y\ll\log_{2}x
to be chosen appropriately. For a prime
P\in(q,x^{1/2}]
, the congruence
\sigma(P^{2})\equiv 3\pmod{q}
holds for
P
lying in exactly
2^{\omega(q)-1}
distinct coprime residue classes modulo
q
(namely those lying in the residue classes
-2
or
1
modulo each of the odd prime divisors of
q
). As such, by the Siegel-Walfiz theorem, there are
\gg\frac{2^{\omega(q)}}{\varphi(q)}\frac{x^{1/2}}{\log x}
many integers
n\leq x
having
P_{2}(n)>q
and
\sigma(n)\equiv 3\pmod{q}
, coming only from the squares of the primes lying in the interval
(x^{1/4},x^{1/2}]
. By (
30
), the coprime residue class
3
mod
q
will be over-represented as soon as
2^{\omega(q)}>(\log{x})^{(1+\delta)\widetilde{\alpha}(q)}
for a fixed
\delta>0
. By the same computation as in (
58
), we have
\widetilde{\alpha}(q)\ll 1/\log Y
, whereas
\omega(q)\geq Y/2\log Y
. The inequality
2^{\omega(q)}>(\log{x})^{(1+\delta)\widetilde{\alpha}(q)}
is thus ensured as soon as we choose
Y>K_{1}\log_{2}x
for some appropriate constant
K_{1}>0
, a condition that is consistent with the only requirement
Y\ll\log_{2}x
on
Y
. This shows that the restriction
P_{2}(n)>q
is inadequate to get weak equidistribution to moduli varying up to a fixed arbitrary power of
\log x
. Since
n
is of the form
2^{k}m^{2}
for some odd
m
, it follows that the restriction
P_{4}(n)>q
in Theorem
1.3
is optimal.
